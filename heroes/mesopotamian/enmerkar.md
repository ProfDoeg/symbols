# Enmerkar: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal but divinely descended **epic king and culture hero** of Uruk/Kulaba; founder-builder, conqueror, judge, and—only in one literary tradition—inventor of writing. He is not securely attested as a deified king with an independent cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Enmerkar is securely named in Sumerian literary and historiographic manuscripts of the **Old Babylonian period, approximately 1900–1600 BCE**, especially the surviving tablets of the Aratta poems and the developed recensions of the *Sumerian King List*. A much earlier Uruk III/Jemdet Nasr lexical tradition, **AD-GI₄**, approximately 3200–3000 BCE, may contain his name, but that reading and its interpretation as a story about Enmerkar are scholarly reconstructions, not an unambiguous contemporary royal inscription. No inscription demonstrably issued by a historical Enmerkar is known.

## Evidence labels

- **[EE] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or early cuneiform evidence securely naming the figure.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** Old Babylonian or later literary development accepted within Mesopotamian learned tradition.
- **[RV] Regional or manuscript variant:** a variant confined to particular copies, cities, or recensions.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** king lists, catalogues, chronicles, sage lists, and scholarly genealogies.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation with a different named figure.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned modern inference, not an ancient statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Enmerkar is the legendary king of **Unug**—Sumerian Uruk, biblical Erech—and its sacred precinct **Kulaba**. In the major Sumerian poems he is “lord of Unug,” “lord of Kulaba,” and “son of Utu.” He competes with the rulers of the fabulous eastern mountain land Aratta for Inana’s favor, luxury materials, craftsmen, and ideological supremacy. In one poem he wins through riddles, material abundance, and the unprecedented inscription of speech on clay; in another his prestige is vindicated through a magical contest fought by proxies; in the Lugalbanda poems he is the commander of an unsuccessful expedition against Aratta, dependent upon Lugalbanda and Inana. The *Sumerian King List* instead makes him the human son of Meškiaĝgašer and credits him with building Uruk. These traditions cannot be reduced to a single biography.

### Name and spellings

The normalized Sumerian royal name is conventionally written **Enmerkar**, with attested Old Babylonian spellings including **en-me-er-kar₂** and **en-me-kar₂**. ORACC’s literary prosopography classifies it as a royal name and records the oblique form *Enmerkara(k)*. The actual ancient spelling varies, and the final element should not casually be separated as Akkadian or modern *kar*, “hunter.” [ORACC’s lexical record](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/literary/cbd/qpn-x-people/x000000630.html) supplies the secure orthographic evidence.

No consensus etymology exists. Proposed analyses include:

- **[MSR]** interpretations involving Sumerian *en*, “lord,” and a complex or fossilized remainder;
- **[MSR]** a Sumerian-Akkadian hybrid name, discussed by Catherine Mittermayer partly because the sequence resembles elements in historical Ur royal names;
- **[MI]** “Enmer the hunter,” based on treating final *-kar* as “hunter.” This segmentation is central to modern attempts to equate Enmerkar with Nimrod but is not an established Sumerological etymology.

### Titles and descriptive epithets

The divine determinative appears before his name in some first-millennium scholarly texts, including the Neo-Assyrian satire SAA 3 29 and the *Bīt mēseri* introduction. This is **late learned orthography**, not evidence that Enmerkar possessed a temple cult comparable to the cults of Lugalbanda or Gilgamesh. Dina Katz stresses precisely this contrast in “Ups and Downs in the Career of Enmerkar.”

### Genealogical contradictions

- **Aratta poetry:** Enmerkar is explicitly **son of Utu**. [LC]
- **Sumerian King List:** he is **son of Meškiaĝgašer**, predecessor in the First Dynasty of Uruk. The list describes Meškiaĝgašer himself as son of Utu. Enmerkar is therefore Utu’s grandson in this learned scheme. [LS]
- **No secure ancient source names Enmerkar’s mother.**
- **AD-GI₄, under Civil’s interpretation:** Enmerkar has a female partner called **Enmerkar-zi**, described with him as knowing how to build towns and construct irrigation works. Because the underlying signs belong to an archaic lexical text and their interpretation as personal names is debated, “wife of Enmerkar” is [MSR], not secure biographical evidence.
- **No secure Sumerian source calls Lugalbanda Enmerkar’s son.** The king list makes Lugalbanda his successor, while the poems make Lugalbanda a subordinate warrior. Later summaries that silently turn succession into biological descent are [MI].
- **No source makes Gilgamesh Enmerkar’s immediate son.** In the king list, Lugalbanda and Dumuzid the Fisherman intervene before Gilgamesh.

### Enmerkar and Nimrod

No ancient Mesopotamian, biblical, Jewish, Christian, or Islamic source identifies Enmerkar with **Nimrod**.

The equation is modern:

1. **[MSR, now marginal]** Joseph Poplicha’s 1929 study of the First Dynasty of Uruk has been cited in the history of the proposal.
2. **[MI/alternative chronology]** David Rohl popularized the equation in *Legend: The Genesis of Civilisation* (1998). His argument combines Erech/Uruk, monumental construction, the language episode in the Enmerkar poem, and the doubtful “Enmer the hunter” etymology.
3. The biblical Nimrod notice, Genesis 10:8–12, does connect Nimrod with Erech, but it does not name Enmerkar, Aratta, Inana, or the invention of writing.
4. Modern biblical scholarship has proposed many different backgrounds for Nimrod—Gilgamesh, Ninurta, Marduk, Tukulti-Ninurta I, Sargon, Naram-Sin, or a composite heroic king. Yigal Levin, for example, argued for a Sargon–Naram-Sin composite rather than Enmerkar. The variety itself demonstrates that no identification commands consensus. [Bar-Ilan record of Levin’s article](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/nimrod-the-mighty-king-of-kish-king-of-sumer-and-akkad-4/)

The equation Enmerkar = Nimrod must therefore be labeled **modern conjecture**, not inherited tradition.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 AD-GI₄: possible archaic precursor

**Language and witnesses:** archaic Sumerian lexical material, conventionally called AD-GI₄, “Archaic Word List C,” or “Tribute”; earliest exemplars belong to Uruk III, approximately 3200–3000 BCE, with Early Dynastic and later copies.

Miguel Civil reconstructed lines containing sign sequences read as Enmerkar and Enmerkar-zi:

> “Enmerkar and (his) wife Enmerkar-zi, who know how to build towns, made brick and brick pavements. When the yearly flood reached its proper level, they made irrigation canals…”

This would make Enmerkar a primordial builder, irrigator, and agricultural culture hero. But several limitations matter:

- the archaic entries are not ordinary narrative prose;
- the readings of the name-signs are reconstructed through much later forms;
- the relationship between the archaic list and the Old Babylonian hero is interpretive;
- Katz consequently says “if Civil’s interpretation is correct.”

The text is thus the **earliest possible attestation**, but not the earliest unambiguous, securely contextualized occurrence. Civil’s study is *Remarks on AD-GI₄ (A.K.A. “Archaic Word List C” or “Tribute”)*, JCS 65 (2013), pp. 13–64; Katz summarizes the evidence and its limits in [“Ups and Downs in the Career of Enmerkar”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347642159_Ups_and_Downs_in_the_Career_of_Enmerkar_King_of_Uruk).

### 2.2 Sumerian King List

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Formation:** probably begun in the Ur III period, late third millennium BCE, then repeatedly expanded and altered.  
**Principal complete witness:** the Weld-Blundell Prism, Old Babylonian, probably early second millennium BCE.

The list says:

> “Enmerkar, son of Meškiaĝgašer, king of Uruk, the one who built Uruk, became king; he ruled 420 years.”

Some copies or secondary reports give different reign totals, including 900 years. The decisive point is not the number but the tradition: Enmerkar succeeds Meškiaĝgašer, precedes Lugalbanda, and builds Uruk. The wording also varies between “who built Uruk” and “under whom Uruk was built.” [Jacobsen’s foundational edition is available through the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/as11.pdf); the [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.1.1) provides the composite recension.

The earliest known Ur III king-list fragment cannot securely supply this whole entry: its First Dynasty of Uruk section is damaged, and descriptive comments such as “who built Uruk” belong to later recensions. Peter Steinkeller dated the early manuscript to the latter part of Šulgi’s reign; Katz cautions that the building notice is a later addition, perhaps based on older tradition. [LS]

### 2.3 *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Length:** approximately 636 lines, incompletely preserved.  
**Witnesses:** numerous Old Babylonian school tablets, predominantly from Nippur, with manuscript variants and lacunae.  
**Probable form of extant composition:** Old Babylonian, although motifs or an earlier version may go back to Ur III.

This is the principal Enmerkar poem. It makes him protagonist, Inana’s chosen king, conqueror by wit, and inventor of writing. The [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1823.htm) and [Sumerian composite text](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/c1823.htm) document the line-by-line evidence.

Critical study proceeded through Samuel Noah Kramer’s 1952 edition, Sol Cohen’s 1973 dissertation, Herman Vanstiphout’s *Epics of Sumerian Kings* (2003), and Catherine Mittermayer’s *Enmerkara und der Herr von Arata* (2009). The [Reallexikon bibliography](https://rla.badw.de/reallexikon/abkuerzungslisten/literatur-und-koerperschaften.html) records these editions.

### 2.4 *Enmerkar and En-suhgir-ana*

Also transliterated *Enmerkar and Ensuhkešdanna*, *Ensuhgirana*, or *Ensuhgiranna*.

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Date of manuscripts:** Old Babylonian, approximately 1900–1600 BCE.  
**Length:** 283 lines plus a small separate fragment.  
**Role:** Enmerkar answers En-suhgir-ana’s claim to Inana and wins when the Arattan sorcerer Ur-girnuna is defeated and killed by the wise woman Sagburu.

Adele Berlin’s *Enmerkar and Ensuhkeshdanna: A Sumerian Narrative Poem* (1979) is the full monographic edition. ETCSL lists more than thirty cuneiform witnesses and fragments in its [bibliography](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/b1824.htm), and supplies a [translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1824.htm).

### 2.5 *Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave*

Also *Lugalbanda in the Wilderness* or *Lugalbanda in the Hurrum Mountains*.

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Date:** Old Babylonian manuscripts, probably drawing on late third-millennium royal ideology.  
**Role of Enmerkar:** he mobilizes Uruk against Aratta and leads the army; when Lugalbanda collapses, the army cannot save him and leaves him in a cave. Enmerkar then largely disappears while Lugalbanda becomes the story’s hero. [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1821.htm)

### 2.6 *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*

ETCSL labels it simply *Lugalbanda*; Vanstiphout calls it *The Return of Lugalbanda*.

**Language and date:** Sumerian; Old Babylonian witnesses. A Neo-Assyrian bilingual copy shows first-millennium transmission.  
**Role of Enmerkar:** besieging Aratta and afraid after a year-long stalemate, he sends Lugalbanda to Inana. His speech claims that Inana brought him from the mountains to Kulaba, Enki drained Uruk’s marshes for him, and he spent fifty years building and judging. [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1822.htm)

The two Lugalbanda poems may originally have constituted one long work; their division may reflect ancient pedagogical copying as much as narrative structure.

### 2.7 *Cuthean Legend of Naram-Sin*

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Recensions:** Old Babylonian fragments, a Middle Babylonian version, and the Standard Babylonian/first-millennium form.

Naram-Sin complains that Enmerkar once destroyed the same uncanny enemy army but did not record his experience on a stele:

> Enmerkar “whose wisdom and weapons paralyzed, caught and annihilated that army, did not write on a stele, did not leave it to me.”

This is an ironic reversal of the Sumerian inventor-of-writing tradition: Enmerkar knew writing but failed in a king’s duty to preserve knowledge for successors. Joan Goodnick Westenholz edited the text in *Legends of the Kings of Akkade* (1997); Benjamin Foster translates it in *Before the Muses*. Katz interprets the passage as an Akkadian scribal counterstatement to Enmerkar’s Sumerian prestige. [LC/RV]

### 2.8 Weidner Chronicle / Chronicle of Esagila

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Present form:** first-millennium manuscripts, probably preserving older scholarly traditions.

A damaged passage makes Enmerkar commit an unidentified offense associated with Babylon and Esagila and apparently be cursed by **Adapa**. Because the passage is fragmentary, neither the offense nor the exact action can be reconstructed. F. N. H. al-Rawi and A. R. George published the principal Sippar manuscript in 1990. [LS]

### 2.9 Fragmentary Enmerkar–Adapa narrative

First-millennium fragments mention Enmerkar and Adapa, lamentation, a deep ancient tomb, and the breaking of its metal door. The fragments do **not** establish whose tomb it is or whether the two figures cooperate or oppose one another. Claims that archaeologists have found “Enmerkar’s tomb” misuse this text. [LC]

### 2.10 Catalogues, sage lists, and scholarly texts

- The first-millennium **Catalogue of Texts and Authors** attributes at least three, perhaps four, Sumerian compositions to “King Enmerkar.” This turns the writing hero into an antediluvian-style author. W. G. Lambert published the catalogue in 1962. [LS]
- The introduction to the protective-incantation series **Bīt mēseri** pairs Enmerkar with the postdiluvian sage **Nungalpiriggal**, whom Ištar/Inana brought down from heaven to Eanna. [LS]
- The **Uruk List of Kings and Sages**, written in Seleucid Uruk in 165 BCE, likewise pairs Enmerkar with Nungalpiriggal. Enmerkar is the last king assigned an *apkallu*; later kings have human *ummânū*, “master-scholars.” The list constructs scholarly ancestry rather than preserving a contemporary court register. [Alan Lenzi’s study](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/cop-facarticles/170/) and the [published discussion of the Seleucid tablet](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/id/e1fa75e5-7f8b-4b18-8770-d539f10a735c/9789004520264.pdf) document this system.
- Neo-Assyrian SAA 3 29 uses the spelling EN.KAR in a satirical attack on Bēl-eṭir, punning on Enmerkar and the opening formula of the *Cuthean Legend*. [LS/RV]

### 2.11 Greek afterlife: Seuechoros

Aelian, writing in Greek around 200 CE, relates a story about a Babylonian king variously transmitted as **Seuechoros/Euechoros**, grandfather of **Gilgamos**. Warned by an omen that his daughter’s son will depose him, he confines her; guards throw the resulting infant from a tower, but an eagle rescues him. The child becomes king.

Some Assyriologists identify the distorted king-name with Enmerkar because of his place before Gilgamesh in the Uruk dynastic tradition. This is a **[MSR] philological identification**, not a transparent Greek transliteration, and Aelian’s exposure story is not found in surviving Sumerian Enmerkar poems.

### 2.12 Texts named in the brief that do not contain Enmerkar

The **Etana Epic**, **Sargon Birth Legend**, Old Akkadian royal inscriptions, first-millennium Sargon legends, and *King of Battle* do not document Enmerkar. They are relevant comparanda for legendary kingship, miraculous succession, heroic genealogy, and Nimrod hypotheses. Kinnier Wilson, Jamie Novotny, Brian Lewis, Joan Goodnick Westenholz, Benjamin Foster, and Andrew George are therefore comparative bibliography, not primary witnesses to Enmerkar.

That distinction matters: Sargon’s exposure in a reed basket belongs to the Akkadian Sargon legend; Etana’s eagle flight belongs to Etana; neither episode may be inserted into Enmerkar’s story.

## 3. The Story

## 3.1 Origins and accession

No surviving Enmerkar narrative tells his birth, childhood, or accession.

The *Sumerian King List* supplies a dynastic framework: kingship passes from Kish to Eanna; Meškiaĝgašer, son of Utu, rules first; Enmerkar, Meškiaĝgašer’s son, becomes king and builds Uruk. The poems replace or compress that genealogy by calling Enmerkar directly “son of Utu.” These are competing literary constructions.

Under Civil’s interpretation of AD-GI₄, Enmerkar and Enmerkar-zi make bricks, lay pavement, regulate irrigation, and introduce plows or yokes. The Lugalbanda poem preserves a related but not identical foundation memory: Enmerkar says Inana summoned him from the mountains into Kulaba; Enki cleared old reeds and drained the marsh; Enmerkar built and judged for fifty years.

There is no ancient account of his marriage ceremony, mortal parents beyond Meškiaĝgašer, or childhood upbringing.

## 3.2 Contest with the unnamed lord of Aratta

In *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*, Uruk’s Eanna already flourishes while Aratta possesses gold, silver, lapis lazuli, and mountain stone. Aratta’s ruler wears a golden crown for Inana, but the narrator immediately privileges Uruk: he has not pleased her as Enmerkar has.

Enmerkar asks “my sister” Inana to make Aratta submit and supply materials and labor for Eanna at Uruk and the Abzu sanctuary at Eridu. This is imperial extraction expressed as sacred construction. Inana endorses the mission and instructs him to send an eloquent, enduring messenger across seven mountains.

Enmerkar’s threat is explicit: unless Aratta delivers precious materials and temple labor, he will scatter its population and reduce the city to dust. The poem does not sanitize his program; it celebrates threatened destruction, deportable labor, and seizure of resources.

### The Spell of Nudimmud and languages

The messenger carries a difficult “incantation of Nudimmud,” lines 134–155. Its syntax and temporal direction remain disputed. In the ETCSL translation, it looks forward to a condition in which Šubur, Hamazi, Sumer, Akkad, and Martu will address Enlil “in a single language,” and Enki will “change the speech in their mouths.” Scholars disagree whether the passage remembers an original linguistic unity disrupted by Enki or anticipates the restoration of unity.

The text is therefore not simply “the Sumerian Tower of Babel.” It has no tower, no human attempt to reach heaven, no divine descent to inspect a city, and no named Babel. Comparison with Genesis 11 is legitimate comparative mythology; derivation or identity is unproved.

### Riddles and impossible demands

The lord of Aratta refuses submission, claiming that Inana installed him in the mountain land. The rivals then exchange challenges through the messenger:

1. **Grain:** Aratta demands barley despite its remoteness and famine. Enmerkar sends loaded pack animals and relieves the famine, displaying Uruk’s agricultural abundance.
2. **The impossible scepter:** Aratta asks for a scepter made of no known wood. Enki gives Enmerkar wisdom; Enmerkar cultivates or manufactures an enigmatic reed-like object over years and sends it.
3. **The champion of no color:** Aratta asks for a champion who is neither black, white, brown, red, yellow, nor multicolored. Enmerkar answers with a champion clothed in undyed or color-neutral material. Interpretation varies because the passage is damaged.
4. **Writing:** Enmerkar’s final message becomes too long for the messenger, whose “mouth is heavy.” Enmerkar pats clay flat and places words upon it. The poem declares that formerly putting words on clay had not existed.

The writing is not invented benevolently for bookkeeping or literature: its first narrative use is a royal ultimatum. The lord of Aratta sees the wedge-shaped marks—“nails”—and becomes angry. Jean-Jacques Glassner and Katz read the “nail” as a visual sign of submission; other scholars emphasize the etiological explanation of tablet writing.

### Outcome

Iškur/Adad produces a storm and miraculous grain for Aratta, temporarily rescuing the city from Enmerkar’s economic pressure. The ruler insists that Inana has not deserted Aratta. The conclusion is damaged: an old woman appears; Inana makes Enmerkar occupy a throne-dais with her; livestock multiply; and Aratta’s people work gold, silver, and lapis for deposition in Eanna.

It is unsafe to summarize this as simple military conquest. Enmerkar wins ideological primacy and tribute, but Aratta remains fertile and still speaks of Inana’s protection. Katz interprets the ending as cooperation or peace under Uruk’s intellectual superiority; other readings emphasize Enmerkar’s victory. The lacunae prevent certainty.

## 3.3 Contest with En-suhgir-ana

In *Enmerkar and En-suhgir-ana*, the named ruler of Aratta initiates the challenge. En-suhgir-ana claims a superior sexual and cultic relationship with Inana: Enmerkar may dream with her, but he converses with her awake; Enmerkar may lie with her in Uruk, but he lies with her in Aratta.

Enmerkar replies that he is Inana’s earthly companion, lies on her splendid bed, and accompanies her invisibly. He claims the crown and scepter from Enlil, nurturance from Aruru, and favor from Ninurta. His reply turns the dispute from sexual boasting into a comprehensive claim to legitimate kingship.

En-suhgir-ana’s assembly advises retreat, admitting that Uruk’s champions have already prevailed. He refuses. The refugee sorcerer **Ur-girnuna of Hamazi** promises to subdue Uruk and attacks Eresh, Nisaba’s city, magically depriving cows and goats of milk.

The wise woman **Sagburu** confronts him in a sequence of transformations or generated animals:

- Ur-girnuna produces a carp; Sagburu produces an eagle that seizes it.
- He produces a ewe and lamb; she produces a wolf.
- He produces a cow and calf; she produces a lion.
- He produces an ibex and wild sheep; she produces a leopard.
- He produces a gazelle; she produces a tiger or similar predator.

Ur-girnuna concedes defeat and begs for life. Sagburu refuses because his attack deprived the gods’ dining hall and the population of dairy produce. She throws him from the Euphrates’ bank and removes his life-force. This is a judicial killing, not a harmless riddle game.

After hearing the result, En-suhgir-ana submits:

> “You are the beloved lord of Inana… From the west to the east you are the great lord, and I am only second to you… you are the older brother.”

The poem concludes that Enmerkar proved superior and praises Nisaba. Enmerkar does not personally fight; the city, goddess, and wise woman validate him.

## 3.4 The Aratta expedition and Lugalbanda

In *Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave*, Enmerkar mobilizes Uruk and Kulaba to destroy rebellious Aratta. He leads the troops across the mountains. Lugalbanda, eighth among elite commanders, becomes deathly ill. Neither Enmerkar nor the army can transport him; his companions arrange food and weapons like funerary offerings and leave him in a cave.

Lugalbanda recovers through divine aid and rejoins the army. The narrative shifts heroism away from Enmerkar: the king is commander and cause of the expedition, but Lugalbanda survives through piety, endurance, and special favor.

In *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*, the siege has lasted a full year. Aratta’s missiles and supernatural defenses hold Uruk’s army at bay. Enmerkar is “afraid, troubled, disturbed” and cannot find a messenger until Lugalbanda volunteers.

Enmerkar dictates a reproach to Inana: she once summoned him from the mountains; Enki drained Uruk for him; he built and judged for fifty years; now she appears to have abandoned him. Lugalbanda carries the message to Kulaba. Inana prescribes a ritual strategy: Enmerkar must cut a tamarisk, make a bucket, clear reeds, catch and prepare a sacred fish, and sacrifice it to her *a-an-kara* weapon. Then his army will prevail and he will carry away Aratta’s metalwork, smiths, stonework, and stonemasons.

The text ends by praising Lugalbanda, not Enmerkar. Enmerkar’s promised result is forced transfer of skilled labor and material culture. The poem does not narrate his death.

## 3.5 Sin, defeat, and missing death tradition

The *Cuthean Legend* gives a hostile retrospective version. Enmerkar successfully destroys a supernatural enemy, but fails to leave an inscription; Naram-Sin therefore lacks precedent and suffers disaster. In the late logic of the text, Enmerkar is culpably negligent.

The Weidner Chronicle makes him sin against Babylon/Esagila and apparently receive Adapa’s curse. A further fragment involves Enmerkar, Adapa, a deep tomb, and a metal door, but is too damaged to reconstruct.

No surviving source securely states:

- how Enmerkar died;
- where he died;
- where he was buried;
- what happened to his body;
- that he was translated to heaven;
- that a tomb or relic was venerated.

Katz suggests that a first-millennium Babylonian reader might infer an improper death from the absence of deification and ancestral cult, perhaps death in wilderness or battle. She explicitly presents this as reconstruction, not recovered myth. [MSR]

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

## 4.2 Every documented named interaction

## 5. Cult, Tomb, Ritual and Images

### Cult

The current evidence is negative but significant.

Enmerkar is not found in the Early Dynastic god lists in the same way as Lugalbanda and Gilgamesh. Ur III offering records document offerings to the latter two, but no comparable offering series to Enmerkar has been identified. Katz concludes that he was remembered as Uruk’s founder and first king rather than becoming a regularly worshiped divine ancestor.

Consequently, there is no securely documented:

- temple dedicated to Enmerkar;
- priesthood of Enmerkar;
- recurring festival or games;
- sacrificial calendar;
- oracle;
- theophoric personal-name tradition;
- relic or cult statue;
- royal funerary chapel.

The divine determinative in some first-millennium scholarly spellings is not sufficient to establish public cult.

### Tomb

No excavated grave at Uruk, elsewhere in Iraq, or in Iran has been securely identified as Enmerkar’s. The fragment mentioning Enmerkar, Adapa, a deep old tomb, and a metal door neither calls the tomb Enmerkar’s nor locates it. Therefore:

- “Tomb of Enmerkar” is unattested;
- claims to have located it are [MI] unless supported by new inscriptional evidence;
- no politics of relic transfer or tomb possession comparable to Greek hero cult is documented.

### Ritual within the stories

Ritual is nevertheless central to his literary world:

- Enmerkar seeks materials for Eanna and Eridu’s Abzu.
- He and the ruler of Aratta compete through messages, grain, crafted objects, champions, and divine signs.
- Inana’s eventual military prescription requires clearing reeds, capturing a sacred fish, cooking and garnishing it, and sacrificing it to her battle weapon.
- The *jipar*, divine bed, crown, scepter, and throne-dais express sacred kingship and possible ritual intimacy with Inana.

These are rituals **inside narrative**, not evidence for an independent cult of Enmerkar.

### Images

No ancient image bears an inscription securely identifying Enmerkar. Generic Early Dynastic or Uruk-period ruler, combat, procession, or “priest-king” imagery cannot be assigned to him merely because it depicts a powerful man. Museum objects catalogued under the Enmerkar poems are normally tablets, not authenticated portraits. The [Penn Museum record](https://collections.penn.museum/collections/object/52324) illustrates the material transmission of the epic, not an identifiable likeness.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Phase I: possible archaic culture hero, approximately 3200–2600 BCE

If Civil’s interpretation of AD-GI₄ is correct, Enmerkar begins as a builder-irrigator paired with Enmerkar-zi. The Fara/Early Dynastic transmission of the list would show continued scribal preservation. Because the underlying text is lexical and difficult, this phase remains [MSR].

No contemporary royal inscription confirms a king Enmerkar. The absence is not proof that no such ruler existed, but it prevents historical identification.

### Phase II: Ur III historiography and royal memory, approximately 2100–2000 BCE

The *Sumerian King List* began taking recognizable form. Ur III rulers, whose dynasty had Urukean connections, cultivated Lugalbanda and Gilgamesh as divine ancestors. Enmerkar entered the constructed succession but, unlike them, does not occur in surviving Ur III royal hymns. The earliest SKL witness is damaged precisely where the Uruk dynasty would be expected, so the details known from later copies cannot automatically be retrojected.

Scholars often date the origins of the Aratta cycle to this ideological environment, but the texts as preserved are later.

### Phase III: Old Babylonian canonical formation, approximately 1900–1600 BCE

This is the decisive period. Scribal schools copied and reshaped:

- *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*;
- *Enmerkar and En-suhgir-ana*;
- the two Lugalbanda poems;
- developed recensions of the *Sumerian King List*.

Enmerkar becomes the first post-flood Sumerian culture king, intellectually superior to foreign rivals. Writing, riddle solving, irrigation, construction, and acquisition of exotic materials represent civilized power. The poems simultaneously expose his limits: he threatens annihilation, fails in direct siege warfare, becomes afraid, and depends on goddesses, a wise woman, and Lugalbanda.

Katz dates the extant ELA to the Old Babylonian period partly because its temporal use of the Flood is not earlier and because its world reflects Old Babylonian scribal concerns. She interprets the invention of writing as an “invented tradition” of schools preserving Sumerian prestige after Sumerian had ceased to be a vernacular.

### Phase IV: Akkadian counter-memory, second to first millennia BCE

The *Cuthean Legend* reverses the inventor’s prestige: Enmerkar defeats an enemy but fails to write for posterity. The Weidner Chronicle adds an offense against Babylon and Adapa’s curse. These texts relocate him from Sumerian triumph into an Akkadian and Babylon-centered moral history.

At the same time, his positive identity survives. A Neo-Assyrian bilingual Lugalbanda manuscript and catalogues demonstrate continued knowledge of the Aratta material.

### Phase V: first-millennium scholarly ancestor

Learned catalogues credit Enmerkar with authorship. *Bīt mēseri* and the Seleucid Uruk List attach Nungalpiriggal to him, making Enmerkar an anchor between primordial apkallu wisdom and human scholarship. The 165 BCE list is explicit learned systematization, not independent confirmation of a third-millennium court sage.

### Phase VI: Greek transformation

Aelian’s Seuechoros episode may preserve a remote transformation of the Uruk royal genealogy, but its infant-exposure plot has no surviving Enmerkar precedent. Here the ancient Mesopotamian founder becomes, if the name equation is right, the tyrannical grandfather of the miraculously saved Gilgamos.

### Phase VII: decipherment and modern reconstruction

The recovery of Sumerian literature in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries restored Enmerkar after more than a millennium of obscurity. Kramer’s edition made ELA widely known; Cohen, Berlin, Wilcke, Vanstiphout, Black and colleagues, Mittermayer, and Katz progressively reconstructed its manuscripts and intellectual setting. ETCSL and ORACC later made the texts and lexical data publicly searchable.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Enmerkar has not developed a modern artistic afterlife comparable to Gilgamesh. No securely documented canonical opera, major film cycle, or widely influential visual type centers on him. His modern reception is primarily scholarly, biblical-comparative, educational, and internet-based.

### Writing and the Tower of Babel

Samuel Noah Kramer’s presentations of the language spell and writing episode encouraged comparison with Genesis 11. The comparison remains useful at the level of Mesopotamian language ideology, but popular retellings often produce several modern inventions:

- Enmerkar builds the Tower of Babel—**not in the Sumerian poem**.
- Enmerkar confuses the languages—grammatically disputed; Enki is the acting god in the spell.
- The poem confirms Genesis as eyewitness history—unsupported.
- The tablet describes a single universal language in straightforward historical prose—it is an incantatory, grammatically difficult passage within fiction.

### Nimrod

Rohl’s Enmerkar–Nimrod equation is the most visible modern reinvention. It is repeated in biblical archaeology media, creationist publications, videos, and popular timelines. Its principal supports—Erech, building, language change, and “hunter”—are analogies or contested etymology, not an ancient chain of identification. Rohl’s wider New Chronology is not accepted in mainstream Egyptology or Assyriology.

The claim should be catalogued as **modern syncretistic identification**, not as either Sumerian or biblical tradition.

### National and political uses

The ancient poem itself has a strong cultural politics: Uruk/Sumer possesses grain, scribal intelligence, legitimate Inana worship, and the right to extract mountain resources. Katz reads its writing episode as a political and ethnic claim for Sumerian intellectual superiority.

Modern Iraqi and Iranian presentations sometimes call the poem an ancient “epic of Iraq and Iran,” following Kramer’s subtitle. That framing is a modern geographical translation of Uruk–Aratta relations; Aratta has not been securely located, and it cannot simply be equated with a modern nation.

No well-documented extremist movement or sustained state cult centered specifically on Enmerkar emerged from the searches undertaken for this dossier. Broader nationalist appropriations of “Sumerian civilization” should not be attributed to Enmerkar without case-specific evidence.

### Commercial and occult reinvention

Online mythological databases, fiction sites, AI-generated “ancient history,” and alternative-history material sometimes describe Enmerkar as:

- a god or demigod worshiped at Uruk;
- husband of Inana in a fixed monogamous theology;
- father of Lugalbanda or Gilgamesh;
- builder of Babel;
- historical emperor of a unified world;
- alien-assisted ruler or “Anunnaki king.”

None of these formulations is supported as stated. The poems call him divinely descended and Inana’s intimate, but cult, paternity, Babel, world empire, and extraterrestrials are modern extrapolations.

### Psychological and comparativist systems

Enmerkar was not a major exemplar for Otto Rank, Lord Raglan, Joseph Campbell, Georges Dumézil, or Gregory Nagy. Applying their hero-pattern systems to him is modern comparison. His tradition lacks several central “hero biography” elements—miraculous birth, childhood threat, exile, monster combat, death, and tomb—which makes forced monomythic classification especially distorting.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Enmerkar historical?

Three positions should be distinguished:

1. **Minimal historical possibility:** a very early Uruk ruler or remembered founder may stand behind the name. Uruk was real, urbanization and long-distance exchange were real, and later tradition consistently associated him with early Uruk.
2. **Historicity unproved:** no contemporary inscription, sealed administrative archive, tomb inscription, or securely identified image names him. The securely readable sources are many centuries later.
3. **Literary figure “fictitious to the core”:** scholars such as Piotr Michalowski emphasize that whatever remote historical stimulus existed, the Enmerkar of Old Babylonian epic belongs to a fully constructed literary past. An accessible discussion in *Epic and History* notes that historical existence has little bearing on analysis of the second-millennium figure. [Online text](https://dokumen.pub/epic-and-history-1nbsped-9781405193078-1405193077.html)

The evidence does not justify either “Enmerkar certainly never existed” or “Enmerkar reigned around 2750 BCE.” Absolute dates assigned to his supposed reign are modern placements of a legendary king-list sequence.

### Date and purpose of the Aratta cycle

The surviving tablets are overwhelmingly Old Babylonian school manuscripts. Possible Ur III composition is inferred from royal ideology, language, political geography, and the prominence of Uruk’s heroic kings. Katz argues that ELA as presently known is Old Babylonian and that the writing episode serves the advanced scribal curriculum.

The abundance of repetition, messenger speeches, formulae, and duplicated passages generated an oral-versus-written debate. Vanstiphout’s work treats the cycle as highly structured literary composition with performative or aural qualities; formulaic style does not demonstrate purely oral origin.

### Was Aratta real?

Earlier scholarship searched Iran and Afghanistan for Aratta and connected the poems with lapis-lazuli trade. Proposed locations have included areas around Anshan, Jiroft, the Iranian plateau, and routes toward Afghanistan.

Nicole Brisch summarizes the prevailing caution: Aratta appears only in literary texts and is best treated as a mythic geographical designation, though its descriptions draw on knowledge of eastern lands and luxury trade. [Encyclopedia of Ancient History entry](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781444338386.wbeah24025)

Thus:

- eastern geography and trade memory are plausible;
- identification with one excavated city is unproved;
- military details cannot be mined as a campaign chronicle;
- “Iran” is a modern geographical shorthand, not Aratta’s ancient ethnic identity.

### What exactly did Enmerkar invent?

ELA explicitly claims that before this occasion “putting words on clay” did not exist. That makes it an ancient etiological story of writing. It does not mean the poem preserves the actual fourth-millennium invention of writing.

Interpretations include:

- literal mythical invention of tablet writing;
- invention of written messages or epistolary communication;
- a pun in which the wedge/nail sign embodies submission;
- praise of logographic writing’s power;
- Old Babylonian scribal self-legitimation.

Since actual writing developed gradually in late fourth-millennium administrative practice, Enmerkar cannot historically have invented it by writing one threatening letter.

### Language-unity debate

The Spell of Nudimmud has been translated in opposite directions: Enki may confound a previously single language, or may restore one language from plurality. The verbal forms, broken context, and incantatory repetitions prevent treating either reading as effortless certainty. The “Sumerian Babel” label is therefore heuristic rather than textual identity.

### Enmerkar’s reputation

Katz traces a striking contest of traditions:

- ELA: inventor and ideal Sumerian intellectual king.
- *Cuthean Legend*: negligent predecessor who failed to write.
- Weidner Chronicle: sinner cursed in Babylonian sacred history.
- Author catalogue and sage lists: venerable author and possessor of a heavenly sage.

The variations are not corruption to be harmonized; they are evidence for changing scribal institutions and cultural politics.

### State of the manuscripts

The Enmerkar poems survive as reconstructions from many partial school tablets, principally excavated at Nippur and other Babylonian sites. No single tablet preserves the whole cycle. Repeated speeches assist reconstruction but also create variant orderings and readings. Major editions include:

- Samuel Noah Kramer, *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta* (1952);
- Sol Cohen, dissertation (1973);
- Adele Berlin, *Enmerkar and Ensuhkeshdanna* (1979);
- Claus Wilcke, *Das Lugalbandaepos* (1969);
- Herman Vanstiphout, *Epics of Sumerian Kings* (2003);
- Catherine Mittermayer, *Enmerkara und der Herr von Arata* (2009);
- ETCSL composite texts and translations;
- ORACC/ePSD lexical and prosopographic tools.

ETCSL’s bibliography lists the individual museum tablets for *Enmerkar and En-suhgir-ana*, including CBS, Nippur, Istanbul, Berlin, and Uruk-area fragments. That material basis is much stronger than a single “epic tablet” but leaves important lacunae.

### Discredited or weak theories still circulating

- Enmerkar securely ruled in a precise year such as 2750 BCE.
- The 420-year reign is a literal chronological datum.
- Aratta has been conclusively located.
- Enmerkar built Babel.
- Enmerkar is certainly Nimrod.
- *-kar* unquestionably means “hunter” in his name.
- The poem records the historical invention of cuneiform.
- Enmerkar was worshiped as a major god.
- A known statue or seal portrait depicts him.
- His grave has been excavated.
- The Etana or Sargon birth legends are episodes in his biography.
- The “Anunnaki” were extraterrestrials who sponsored his reign.

All are either unproved reconstruction or modern invention.
