# Naram-Sin (the Cuthean Legend): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** historical king transformed into an **epic king and royal exemplum**; during his own reign, a divinized mortal and “god of Akkad,” but not a demigod by birth. In the *Cuthean Legend* he is a fallible heroic king whose military presumption destroys his armies and whose eventual obedience supplies instruction for later rulers.

**Earliest secure attestation:** contemporary Old Akkadian administrative, dedicatory, and royal inscriptions of the reign of Narām-Sîn, conventionally placed around **2254–2218 BCE** under the Middle Chronology. These attest the king, his conquests, titulary, family, and eventual deification. The *Cuthean Legend* itself is first physically attested in **Old Babylonian Akkadian manuscripts, approximately the eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE**, notably MLC 1364 and BM 17215—not in Narām-Sîn’s lifetime.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary or near-contemporary Old Akkadian evidence.
- **[LE] Later canonical elaboration:** Old Babylonian or later literary reshaping.
- **[RV] Regional or manuscript variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** king lists, chronicles, omen traditions, or scholarly cataloguing.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction or interpretation.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or reception.**
- **[NEG] No documented evidence found in the surviving record.**

Dates attached to compositions distinguish, wherever possible, the period of composition or redaction from the dates of surviving copies.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

**[EA]** Narām-Sîn was a historical ruler of the Akkadian dynasty, son of Maništušu and grandson of Sargon according to the principal recension of the *Sumerian King List*. Contemporary inscriptions and monuments present him as a conqueror, “king of Akkad,” “king of the four quarters,” and, after a major revolt, the divinized “god of Akkad.” The Bassetki inscription says that the inhabitants of Akkad, after he defeated a coalition that had risen against him, petitioned the principal gods to make him their city’s god and built him a temple.

**[LE]** Later literature did not preserve a single portrait. It made him, variously:

1. the impious destroyer of Enlil’s Ekur whose act brings the Gutians against Akkad in the Sumerian *Curse of Agade*;
2. the rash but corrigible king who ignores unfavorable extispicy and loses three armies in the Akkadian *Cuthean Legend*;
3. a victorious warrior in narratives of Apišal, the Great Revolt, and association with Erra;
4. a dead king praised elegiacally;
5. a negative example in Babylon-centered chronicles that anachronistically make Marduk punish him.

These portraits share a historical name but not a unified plot.

### Name and writing

**[EA]** Akkadian **Narām-Sîn**, also normalized **Narām-Su’en**, means “Beloved of Sîn/Su’en,” from *narām*, “beloved,” and the moon-god’s name. Sumerological publications often use **Naram-Suen** because the divine name written with EN.ZU can be read Su’en; Assyriological publications commonly use **Narām-Sîn**. Modern unmarked spellings include **Naram-Sin**, **Naram Suen**, and **Naramsin**.

The name is commonly represented syllabically as *na-ra-am-dEN.ZU* or comparable spellings. After deification, a divine classifier could precede the royal name. That classifier is a scribal sign, not a spoken component. The moon-god within the name is not evidence that Narām-Sîn was believed to be Sîn’s biological son.

### Titles and epithets

| Form | Meaning and evidence |
|---|---|
| **šar Akkadê / lugal A-ga-de₃** | **[EA]** “king of Akkad,” the dynastic and territorial title. |
| **šar kibrāt arba’im** | **[EA]** “king of the four quarters,” a universalizing imperial title documented in his inscriptions. |
| **dINGIR A-ga-de₃ki** | **[EA]** “god of Akkad”; the Bassetki inscription associates this elevation with victory over the Great Revolt. It does not make him chief god of all Mesopotamia. |
| **dannum** | **[EA]** “the mighty,” occurring in royal and dedicatory formulae. |
| **“shepherd Narām-Suen”** | **[LE]** Sumerian royal-poetic characterization in the *Curse of Agade*. |
| **“sun/daylight of his people”** | **[LE]** Royal metaphor in the Old Babylonian *Elegy on the Death of Narām-Sîn*, particularly apt in a Larsa text shaped by the cultic language of Šamaš. |
| **“grandson of Sargon”** | **[LE]** The *Cuthean Legend* uses dynastic memory to authorize the purported first-person inscription. |

### Conflicting genealogy

**[EA/LS]** The *Sumerian King List* calls Narām-Suen the **son of Maništušu**, who is in turn a son of Sargon. It gives Narām-Suen a reign of 56 years in some manuscripts and names Šar-kali-šarrī as his son and successor. The figures are schematic and manuscript-dependent; they are not precise modern chronology.

**[LE]** Some later texts call Narām-Sîn **Sargon’s son** rather than grandson. This is not evidence for a second genealogy contemporary with the dynasty. It is a recurrent flattening of famous dynastic names in later literary memory. Joan Goodnick Westenholz specifically treated this phenomenon as the manipulation of famous names; modern historical reconstruction generally retains the contemporary/dynastic sequence Sargon → Maništušu → Narām-Sîn.

**[MI]** Internet genealogies assigning him a named wife such as “Summarrat, princess of Awan,” exact birth dates, or complete pedigrees reaching modern family trees are unsupported by the primary record.

### Identification with other figures

- **[SI/NEG]** Narām-Sîn was not identified in ancient sources with Sîn, Nergal, Erra, Gilgameš, or any other god or hero. His divinization made him a god of Akkad, not an avatar of another deity.
- **[MSR]** Yigal Levin proposed that biblical Nimrod may preserve a composite memory of Sargon and Narām-Sîn. This is a modern comparative hypothesis, not an ancient equation and not a consensus identification.
- **[MI]** Claims that Narām-Sîn was an incarnation of Nergal, an Anunnaki extraterrestrial, an immortal survivor of the Gutian catastrophe, or the builder of a subterranean alien temple are modern esoteric or entertainment inventions.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Contemporary evidence

**[EA] Royal inscriptions and administrative documents, c. twenty-third century BCE.** These record actual royal titulary, building work, appointments, campaigns, the Great Revolt, and members of the royal family. Their survival is uneven: originals coexist with Old Babylonian scribal copies of older monuments, so each witness must be evaluated individually.

**[EA] Bassetki inscription.** Inscribed on the copper-alloy base of a statue, it recounts a widespread revolt, Narām-Sîn’s victory, the people of Akkad’s petition to major gods that he become their city’s god, and the construction of his temple. The statue base is contemporary Old Akkadian evidence even though its findspot, Bassetki in northern Iraq, is far from the still-unlocated city of Akkad.

**[EA] Victory Stele.** The pink-limestone monument shows Narām-Sîn ascending a wooded mountain over defeated Lullubi, larger than all other human figures and wearing a horned helmet. The fragmentary original inscription identifies the campaign; an Elamite inscription added by Šutruk-Naḫḫunte in the twelfth century BCE records its removal to Susa. It is visual evidence for divine kingship and imperial victory, not an illustration of the *Cuthean Legend*. The Louvre dates it within Narām-Sîn’s reign and records its discovery at Susa.

**[EA] Great-Revolt inscriptions.** Old Babylonian copies of monuments originally erected in Enlil’s Ekur at Nippur report the coalition and Narām-Sîn’s victories. The literary traditions enlarge and rearrange this historical nucleus.

**[EA] Old Elamite treaty.** A treaty between Narām-Sîn and an unnamed or disputed Elamite ruler invokes an extensive Elamite pantheon and aligns the ruler’s enemies and friends with Narām-Sîn’s. It is diplomatic evidence, not epic narrative.

### Sumerian literary and historiographic corpus

#### *The Curse of Agade* / *The Cursing of Akkad*

**[LE]** Sumerian, probably composed in the Ur III or early Old Babylonian cultural environment; fragments of an earlier form come from Ur III Nippur, while the complete composite depends principally on Old Babylonian school copies. Approximately 280 lines survive.

Narām-Suen receives a dream that Enlil will not permit Akkad lasting prosperity. He mourns for seven years and twice seeks an omen concerning the Ekur. When no favorable answer comes, he mobilizes troops, attacks Enlil’s temple, exposes its treasures, and carries away its materials. Enlil responds by bringing the Gutians from the mountains. Other great gods then curse Akkad.

The composition is not a contemporary chronicle. Contemporary year names and inscriptions instead associate Narām-Sîn with building work for Enlil. The contradiction is historically important: later Sumerian memory converted the empire’s collapse into a moral narrative about the violation of Nippur.

#### *Sumerian King List*

**[LS]** The composition took shape after the Akkadian period and survives chiefly in Old Babylonian manuscripts. It places Narām-Suen after Maništušu and before Šar-kali-šarrī. It supplies dynastic ordering but contains variant and implausibly long regnal totals. It also places Gutian rule after Akkad, contributing to later schemes in which Narām-Sîn became adjacent to, or responsible for, imperial collapse even though several kings followed him.

### Akkadian Narām-Sîn literature

Joan Goodnick Westenholz’s *Legends of the Kings of Akkade* remains the central corpus edition.

#### *Narām-Sîn and the Lord of Apišal*

**[LE]** Old Babylonian Akkadian. Narām-Sîn confronts and defeats the ruler of Apišal. The work uses heroic battle language and apparently ends with royal clemency or restoration of the defeated ruler. Apišal’s location and whether the story preserves a historical campaign remain disputed. Later omen and chronicle traditions also remember an Apišal campaign, but repetition in later texts does not independently prove the episode.

#### *Erra and Narām-Sîn*

**[LE]** Old Babylonian Akkadian, fragmentary. Narām-Sîn receives weapons from Ištar and fights alongside the god Erra—possibly to be understood in this context as Nergal, the god of Kutha—against hostile forces associated with Enlil. The surviving text is too damaged to construct a complete plot. Laṣ, spouse of Erra/Nergal, is named, but her exact action in the fragmentary narrative is uncertain.

#### *Elegy on the Death of Narām-Sîn*

**[LE]** Old Babylonian, preserved on a prism from Larsa. It praises the king’s deeds and laments his death in a style related to royal hymnody. Unlike the Bassetki inscription and Victory Stele, it does not write him as a god; it calls him, metaphorically, the sun of his people. This is evidence for literary remembrance of his death, not for the manner of his historical death.

#### *The Great Revolt against Narām-Sîn*

**[EA/LE]** The tradition spans an Old Akkadian exercise tablet, Old Babylonian extracts, and copies or adaptations based on older royal inscriptions. Historical inscriptions identify major rebels including Iphur-Kiš of Kiš and Amar-girid of Uruk. Literary versions greatly expand the coalition, sometimes turning it into a fabulous alliance of many kings. “Gula-AN and the Seventeen Kings” belongs to this expanding legendary complex.

#### *Narām-Sîn and the Enemy Hordes* / *Cuthean Legend*

**[LE/RV]** Akkadian pseudo-autobiographical royal literature. Its ancient incipit was **ṭupšenna pitēma**, “Open the tablet box.” “Cuthean Legend” is a modern title derived from the work’s claim that Narām-Sîn deposited the account in E-meslam, Nergal’s temple at Kutha. “Narām-Sîn and the Enemy Hordes” is Westenholz’s descriptive title.

The manuscript history is unusually long:

- **Old Babylonian:** MLC 1364 and BM 17215, approximately eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE; the long version may have occupied two tablets and roughly 600 short lines.
- **Middle Babylonian:** Akkadian prism KBo 19.98 from Ḫattuša; another fragment, KBo 19.99, is uncertain.
- **Hittite reception:** fragmentary Hittite translation/manuscripts at Ḫattuša.
- **Standard Babylonian:** seven Neo-Assyrian Nineveh witnesses, one Neo-Assyrian Sultantepe/Ḫuzirina copy, and one Neo-Babylonian copy, perhaps from Kiš. The standardized recension has about 175–180 longer lines.
- The uniformity of the first-millennium form suggests standardization by or after the Kassite period, but the precise date cannot be demonstrated.

The Neo-Assyrian copies show that the story functioned within learned scribal collections, including the royal-library environment of Nineveh, as instruction about omens, self-restraint, and the obligation to preserve written precedent.

### Chronicles and omen traditions

**[LS] Chronicle of Early Kings.** A first-millennium Babylonian chronicle, preserved in later tablets, gives Narām-Sîn brief notices involving Apišal and Magan. Its form depends partly on omen traditions; it cannot simply be treated as a contemporary annal.

**[LS] Weidner Chronicle.** Probably formulated in the second millennium but preserved in first-millennium copies. It reorganizes early history around proper service to Marduk at Babylon. Narām-Sîn mistreats or destroys Babylon’s people, and Marduk summons Gutian forces against him. Babylon and Marduk had no such demonstrated supremacy in Narām-Sîn’s own period: the episode is a datable Babylonian theological rewriting.

**[LS] Historical omens.** Later omen compendia use Narām-Sîn’s campaigns as protases or explanatory historical exempla. Such notices show the persistence of his name within divinatory scholarship; they do not automatically preserve verbatim records from his reign.

### Works named in the brief that do not contain his story

- **[NEG] *Epic of Etana*.** Etana appears in the *Sumerian King List* as a much earlier king of Kiš. Standard editions by J. V. Kinnier Wilson and Jamie Novotny are important for Mesopotamian heroic kingship but do not provide an episode involving Narām-Sîn.
- **[NEG] *Sargon Birth Legend*.** The Neo-Assyrian birth autobiography concerns Sargon, not Narām-Sîn. Brian Lewis’s study is relevant to the dynasty’s later legendary reception, but Sargon’s exposed-child narrative must not be transferred to his grandson.
- **[NEG] *King of Battle* (*šar tamḫāri*).** This tells of Sargon’s Anatolian campaign to Purušḫanda. It is comparative evidence for the international transmission of Akkadian royal legend, not a Narām-Sîn episode.
- **[MSR] George’s work on *Gilgamesh*.** Relevant as comparison for transmission, tablet-box and monument motifs, heroic kingship, and scribal canonization; it does not make Gilgameš Narām-Sîn’s companion.

## 3. The Story

### Birth and upbringing

**[NEG]** No surviving ancient narrative gives Narām-Sîn a miraculous birth, childhood exposure, foster parent, youthful trial, or divine biological parent. Those motifs belong to Sargon or to generalized hero-pattern scholarship. Narām-Sîn enters his stories already as king.

### Dynastic accession

**[LS]** The *Sumerian King List* makes him Maništušu’s son and successor. Later texts emphasize descent from Sargon because the famous founder gives the narrator authority. No ancient source supplies a detailed accession story.

### The Great Revolt

**[EA]** Contemporary or copied royal inscriptions say that Kiš and Uruk headed a large coalition against Narām-Sîn. He defeated the revolt in a sequence summarized as nine victories in one year. The Bassetki inscription makes this salvation of Akkad the occasion for his elevation as the city’s god.

**[LE]** Literary versions transform the historical coalition into an enormous, sometimes fabulous alliance. Kings, cities, and numerical totals vary. Gula-AN appears as a Gutian leader in one branch; in expanded tradition he leads seventeen kings, is defeated and captured, released or paroled, and rebels again. The conclusion of the second conflict is fragmentary. The historically grounded revolt and the fabulous coalition must therefore be presented as related but non-identical traditions.

### Apišal

**[LE]** In *Narām-Sîn and the Lord of Apišal*, the enemy king is represented with leonine, fiery, or thunderous battle imagery. Narām-Sîn defeats him but behaves magnanimously toward him. Some scholars connect this to genuine campaign traditions; others observe that Apišal may be a literary substitution for Armanum or an otherwise minor place. The evidence permits a remembered-campaign hypothesis, not certainty.

### Erra’s ally

**[LE]** In *Erra and Narām-Sîn*, Ištar arms Narām-Sîn, and Narām-Sîn and Erra enter battle together. The fragment reverses the later image of the isolated sinner: here he is a successful divine ally. Because the text is broken, the identity of all enemies and the ending remain uncertain.

### The *Curse of Agade*

**[LE]** Inana first establishes prosperity at Akkad. Narām-Suen rises on its throne while tribute and luxury fill the city. A troubling pronouncement from Enlil’s Ekur causes Inana and other gods to withdraw divine qualities from Akkad.

Narām-Suen dreams that Enlil will deny Akkad a stable future. He mourns for seven years, lays aside royal splendor, and performs extispicy twice. The oracle does not authorize work on the Ekur. Trying to alter Enlil’s decision, he mobilizes troops and attacks the temple. He sets ladders and axes against it, exposes its dark chambers and treasure chest, burns or removes sacred fittings, and ships away gold, silver, copper, timber, and other materials.

Enlil brings the Gutians down from the mountains. The poem describes them in xenophobic, dehumanizing language: human intelligence combined with canine instincts and simian features. Roads close; livestock and people are seized; agriculture and trade collapse; famine becomes so severe that the dead remain unburied and dogs consume travelers.

Suen, Enki, Inana, Ninurta, Iškur, Utu, Nuska, and Nisaba placate Enlil by redirecting destruction onto Akkad itself. Their curse includes famine, murder, social disintegration, women’s deaths, a butcher killing his child, abortion or miscarriage, prostitution ending in suicide, economic collapse, and the city’s conversion into a haunted ruin. The poem closes by praising Inana for Akkad’s destruction.

**Historical control:** no contemporary evidence says Narām-Sîn destroyed Ekur. His inscriptions instead claim building and dedication for Enlil. The story is therefore a later theological explanation of catastrophe, not an eyewitness account.

### The *Cuthean Legend*

#### Prologue and Enmerkar precedent

**[LE]** The Standard Babylonian recension opens by directing a future reader to open the tablet box and read Narām-Sîn’s stele. Narām-Sîn invokes Enmerkar as a failed predecessor: Enmerkar confronted danger but did not leave an adequate written record from which a later king could learn. The point is as much archival as military—royal experience must be preserved.

#### Creation and character of the enemy

**[LE/RV]** The gods generate a monstrous mountain host. Bēlet-ilī is called their progenitress and Tiāmat their wet nurse. The enemy is variously characterized across recensions as the **Ummān-manda**, Hurrian or northern forces, cave-dwellers, birdlike or demonic beings, and the clan of Anubanini. These labels cannot be reduced confidently to one historical ethnicity.

In the Standard Babylonian version the royal family of the enemy comprises:

- King **Anubanini**;
- Queen **Melili**;
- their sons **Memanduḫ/Memandaḫ**, **Medudu/Midudu**, **Tartadada**, **Baldaḫdaḫ**, **Aḫudanadiḫ**, and **Ḫurrukidû**.

The seven kings command 360,000 troops. Modern analysis connects some names with Lullubian, Hurrian, or constructed “barbarian” onomastics, but the list’s literary function is to give monstrous chaos an exotic genealogy.

#### Testing the enemy

**[LE]** Narām-Sîn sends a scout to pierce or prick a captured enemy. The resulting blood proves that the creatures are mortal. Physical mortality, however, does not mean that war is divinely authorized.

#### Rejected oracle and three disasters

**[LE]** Narām-Sîn sacrifices seven lambs and performs extispicy, one animal corresponding to each enemy king. The omen is unfavorable. He rejects it and declares, in effect, that he will disregard the gods’ judgment and control his own course.

He dispatches three immense armies in successive years. Each disappears or is annihilated:

1. the first army, given as 180,000 in one reconstruction;
2. a second enormous force;
3. a third force.

The precise totals vary with recension and restoration. The narrative point is repetition: empirical confidence and manpower cannot compensate for rejected divine knowledge.

#### Despair and reform

**[LE]** Narām-Sîn becomes bewildered, exhausted, and sunk in grief. He questions what memorial he can leave. At the New Year festival he resolves not to act without divine approval. This is the decisive conversion: not from warrior to pacifist in an absolute sense, but from autonomous command to divinely constrained kingship.

#### Captives and Ištar’s warning

**[LE]** After receiving permission to pursue the enemy, Narām-Sîn captures twelve. He again consults divination rather than killing them immediately. Ištar warns him not to destroy the “brood of destruction,” because Enlil will summon or use them for future evil. The enemy belongs to a divine timetable beyond the king’s control.

#### Testament to future kings

**[LE]** Narām-Sîn deposits his account at E-meslam in Kutha. He tells future rulers to respect omens, preserve inscriptions, restrain military pride, and, when confronted by such a divinely appointed enemy, put weapons aside rather than repeat his catastrophe. Blessings attach to preserving the inscription and curses to destroying it. The work imitates a royal monument while turning royal defeat into usable wisdom.

### Death and body

- **[LE]** The *Elegy* assumes and laments Narām-Sîn’s death but supplies no securely reconstructible cause.
- **[LS]** The King List merely passes kingship to Šar-kali-šarrī.
- **[NEG]** No securely identified tomb, corpse, burial narrative, relic, or ancient claim about the fate of his body survives.
- **[MI]** Stories that the king remained alive beneath a cursed temple are products of modern horror fiction.

### Every documented named divine or heroic interaction

“Interaction” here includes direct action, a divinatory relationship, a jointly narrated event, or explicit genealogical/dynastic positioning. Mere appearance elsewhere in the same corpus is excluded.

- **Sargon of Akkad —** dynastic grandfather and model whose fame authorizes Narām-Sîn’s first-person testament. *Sumerian King List* and *Cuthean Legend*, Old Babylonian onward. **[LS/LE]**
- **Maništušu —** father and predecessor. *Sumerian King List*, Old Babylonian manuscripts preserving the dynasty. **[LS]**
- **Šar-kali-šarrī —** son and successor. King List and contemporary inscriptions. **[EA/LS]**
- **Enmerkar —** failed predecessor whose omission of a useful monument leaves Narām-Sîn without guidance; Narām-Sîn cites him as a warning about archival memory. Standard Babylonian *Cuthean Legend*, first-millennium copies of an older tradition. **[LE]**
- **Enlil —** gives kingship to Sargon; denies Akkad a lasting future; Narām-Sîn tries to reverse his pronouncement and destroys Ekur; Enlil releases the Gutians. *Curse of Agade*, Ur III/Old Babylonian witnesses. In the *Cuthean Legend*, Enlil preserves the monster host for future destructive use. **[LE]**
- **Inana/Ištar —** Inana establishes Akkad’s prosperity, supports its arms, then withdraws and ultimately participates in its curse in the *Curse of Agade*. Ištar arms Narām-Sîn in *Erra and Narām-Sîn* and warns him not to kill the captured enemy in the *Cuthean Legend*. **[LE]**
- **Sîn/Su’en/Nanna —** the god within Narām-Sîn’s theophoric name; Suen joins the gods who redirect Enlil’s anger against Akkad. Narām-Sîn also installs his daughter En-men-ana in the moon-god’s cult at Ur. Name and administrative evidence are contemporary; the curse episode is later. **[EA/LE]**
- **Erra —** fights alongside Narām-Sîn in *Erra and Narām-Sîn*. Whether “Erra” here should be understood as Nergal is debated. Old Babylonian fragment. **[LE/SI]**
- **Nergal —** a contemporary votive statue was dedicated to Erra/Nergal for Narām-Sîn’s life; in the late *Cuthean Legend*, Narām-Sîn deposits his stele in E-meslam, Nergal’s temple at Kutha. **[EA/LE]**
- **Laṣ —** named in the fragmentary *Erra and Narām-Sîn* tradition as the divine spouse associated with Erra/Nergal; no securely reconstructible direct dialogue with Narām-Sîn survives. **[LE]**
- **Bēlet-ilī —** progenitress of Narām-Sîn’s monstrous opponents. *Cuthean Legend*, Standard Babylonian recension. **[LE]**
- **Tiāmat —** wet nurse of the monstrous host. *Cuthean Legend*, Standard Babylonian recension. This does not make Narām-Sîn a participant in *Enūma eliš*. **[LE]**
- **Enki/Ea —** withdraws Akkad’s wisdom and mooring pole, helps placate Enlil, and joins the curse. *Curse of Agade*. **[LE]**
- **An/Anu —** removes Akkad’s heaven-reaching awesomeness in the *Curse of Agade*. In the Bassetki account, An is among the great gods petitioned concerning Narām-Sîn’s deification, depending on restoration and enumeration. **[EA/LE]**
- **Ninurta —** retrieves the regalia bestowed upon Akkad and joins the curse. *Curse of Agade*. **[LE]**
- **Utu/Šamaš —** removes Akkad’s eloquence and witnesses fulfillment of the curse; Narām-Sîn’s daughter Šumšani serves in Šamaš’s cult. *Curse of Agade* and contemporary administrative evidence. **[EA/LE]**
- **Iškur/Adad —** appears in similes of destructive force and joins the divine curse against Akkad. *Curse of Agade*. **[LE]**
- **Nuska —** joins the gods who calm Enlil and curse Akkad. *Curse of Agade*. **[LE]**
- **Nisaba —** joins the gods who calm Enlil and curse Akkad. *Curse of Agade*. **[LE]**
- **Ezina/Ašnan —** Akkad’s returned grain is cursed by Ezina in the divine malediction. *Curse of Agade*. **[LE]**
- **Ninilduma —** Akkad’s timber is cursed by Ninilduma. *Curse of Agade*. **[LE]**
- **Dagan —** a royal inscription credits Dagan with giving Narām-Sîn Armanum and Ebla and enabling the capture of their ruler. The Bassetki narrative includes Dagan among the gods approached about deification. **[EA]**
- **Ninḫursag/Ninmaḫ —** among the great city gods invoked in the Bassetki account of Narām-Sîn’s elevation. **[EA]**
- **Marduk —** punishes Narām-Sîn with Gutian forces because of his treatment of Babylon in the *Weidner Chronicle*. This is an anachronistic Babylon-centered reinterpretation preserved in first-millennium copies, not contemporary evidence. **[LS]**
- **Lugal-Apišal/Rīš-Adad —** enemy king defeated and apparently treated mercifully in *Narām-Sîn and the Lord of Apišal*. Old Babylonian. **[LE/RV]**
- **Iphur-Kiš —** leader of Kiš in the Great Revolt, defeated by Narām-Sîn. Royal-inscription tradition and later literary versions. **[EA/LE]**
- **Amar-girid —** ruler of Uruk and principal rebel, defeated in the Great Revolt. Royal-inscription tradition. **[EA]**
- **Gula-AN —** Gutian leader in a later Great-Revolt branch; defeated, captured, released or spared, and attacks again. **[LE/RV]**
- **Anubanini —** king of the monster clan opposing Narām-Sîn. Standard Babylonian *Cuthean Legend*. The name resembles that of a historical Lullubian ruler, but identity is unproved. **[LE/MSR]**
- **Melili —** Anubanini’s queen and member of the hostile monster dynasty. Standard Babylonian *Cuthean Legend*. **[LE]**
- **Memanduḫ/Memandaḫ, Medudu/Midudu, Tartadada, Baldaḫdaḫ, Aḫudanadiḫ, Ḫurrukidû —** sons of Anubanini and Melili, collectively belonging to the enemy leadership opposed to Narām-Sîn. Standard Babylonian *Cuthean Legend*. **[LE/RV]**
- **Lullubi ruler Satuni** — defeated in the mountain campaign represented on the Victory Stele, according to the inscriptional tradition. **[EA]**
- **Šutruk-Naḫḫunte —** later Elamite king who removed Narām-Sîn’s Victory Stele to Susa and added his own inscription, transforming the Akkadian monument into Elamite war booty. Twelfth century BCE. **[EA for the later reuse]**

**[NEG]** No ancient interaction is documented between Narām-Sîn and Etana, Gilgameš, Lugalbanda, Dumuzid, Sargon’s gardener Akki, Moses, Nimrod, or the heroes of later Greek and Iranian epic.

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

### Deification and temple

**[EA]** The Bassetki inscription is direct evidence that Narām-Sîn was made **god of Akkad** during his lifetime and that a temple was built for him in the city. The divine classifier before his name, the title “god of Akkad,” seals belonging to servants of the divine king, and his horned helmet on the Victory Stele corroborate the ideology.

The evidence supports a geographically and politically bounded royal cult, not the claim that all Mesopotamia accepted him as a universal god. His son Šar-kali-šarrī did not generally inherit this divinity. Modern scholarship accordingly distinguishes:

- royal self-presentation;
- the formal city cult at Akkad;
- divine notation used by officials;
- the much broader theological status of long-established gods.

### Ritual integration of the family

**[EA]** Narām-Sîn placed daughters in major priestly offices:

- En-men-ana in the cult of Nanna/Sîn at Ur;
- Tūta-napšum in Enlil’s cult at Nippur;
- Šumšani in Šamaš’s cult at Sippar.

These appointments embedded imperial family members in major cult centers. They are political-religious institutions, not evidence for legends about the daughters.

### Votive images

**[EA]** A Louvre statue fragment carries a dedication by the official Šu’astakkal to Erra “for the life of Narām-Sîn, the mighty, his companion, king of the four regions.” Such objects document prayers for the living king and the placement of elite images in temples.

**[EA/MSR]** The Victory Stele’s horned crown is the clearest visual assertion of divinity. Its ascending composition, hieratic scale, celestial disks, disciplined soldiers, and defeated Lullubi make imperial conquest appear cosmically sanctioned. It should not be read as evidence of the *Curse* or the *Cuthean Legend*.

### Kutha and the alleged stele

**[LE]** The *Cuthean Legend* says its text was deposited in a tablet box or on a stele in E-meslam at Kutha. No such original Narām-Sîn stele has been excavated there. The framing claim is part of pseudo-royal inscriptional fiction.

### Tomb and relics

**[NEG]**

- No tomb securely identified as Narām-Sîn’s is known.
- No ancient text describes pilgrimages to his grave.
- No relic tradition, bones, funerary games, healing oracle, or later hero-shrine comparable to a Greek *heroon* is attested.
- Akkad itself has not been securely located, preventing archaeological examination of the reported temple and any royal cemetery.

The absence of a tomb tradition is especially important: his documented sacralization is a royal cult centered on kingship and the city, not a known cult of the heroic dead.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### c. 2254–2218 BCE: victorious and divine king

**[EA]** Contemporary representation is overwhelmingly affirmative. Narām-Sîn defeats rebels, receives territories from gods, builds for temples, installs priestesses, assumes universal titles, and becomes the god of Akkad. The Victory Stele embodies the successful god-king.

### Late third millennium BCE: collapse reframed

**[LE]** Akkadian authority weakened after his reign, but he was not its final king. Šar-kali-šarrī and several lesser kings followed. The *Curse of Agade* compresses the political collapse into a moral causal chain: Narām-Suen violates Ekur; Enlil releases the Gutians; the gods curse Akkad. This is the decisive creation of the hubristic Narām-Sîn.

### Old Babylonian period, c. 2000–1600 BCE: proliferation

**[LE]** School and court literature developed multiple, incompatible Narām-Sîns:

- temple destroyer;
- conqueror of Apišal;
- ally of Erra;
- hero of the Great Revolt;
- lamented dead king;
- rash commander of the *Cuthean Legend*.

The *Cuthean Legend*’s earliest manuscripts belong here. Its king is culpable but teachable, unlike the largely condemned ruler of the *Curse*.

### Middle Babylonian and Hittite worlds

**[RV]** Copies and translations at Ḫattuša demonstrate that Akkadian royal legend traveled beyond Babylonia. The *Cuthean Legend* joined Sargon and Gilgameš materials in the international scribal culture of Late Bronze Age Anatolia. Fragmentary Hittite “Travails of Narām-Sîn” material may represent related reception, but damaged manuscripts prevent a seamless reconstruction.

### First millennium BCE: canonical instruction and Babylonian rewriting

**[LE/LS]** Neo-Assyrian libraries copied the Standard Babylonian *Cuthean Legend*. Seven Nineveh witnesses and the relatively complete Sultantepe manuscript demonstrate its pedagogical longevity. Its lesson was useful to kings: heed extispicy, recognize divinely appointed limits, and leave documentary precedent.

Babylonian chronicles separately made Marduk the arbiter of Narām-Sîn’s fate. This was not preservation without change; it was learned ideological appropriation.

### Twelfth to sixth centuries BCE: monuments as antiquities

**[EA/LS]** Šutruk-Naḫḫunte carried the Victory Stele to Susa in the twelfth century BCE and added an inscription celebrating its capture. Centuries later Nabonidus sought old foundation deposits and invoked Sargon and Narām-Sîn as ancient royal predecessors. This antiquarian interest helped preserve their authority while greatly exaggerating their chronological remoteness.

### Post-cuneiform antiquity through the early modern period

**[NEG]** No demonstrable continuous medieval narrative tradition about the *Cuthean Legend* has been identified. Classical and medieval writers do not transmit a recognizable full Narām-Sîn cycle. Proposed equations with Nimrod are modern reconstructions, not a traceable medieval tradition.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries: decipherment and reconstruction

**[MSR]** Excavation, museum acquisition, and cuneiform decipherment recovered the historical king before the literary corpus was fully assembled. The Sultantepe discovery was particularly important because its relatively complete tablet clarified fragments from Nineveh and Ḫattuša. Al-Fouadi’s 1976 publication of the Bassetki statue supplied decisive contemporary evidence for the king’s deification.

Westenholz’s 1997 edition brought the disparate royal legends into a single critically controlled corpus. ETCSL subsequently made the Sumerian *Curse* and King List widely accessible; CDLI, ORACC, SEAL, and the electronic Babylonian Library continue manuscript-level digital work.

### Twenty-first century

**[MSR/MI]** Scholarship increasingly treats Narām-Sîn’s image as a history of cultural memory: contemporary divine conqueror, Sumerian temple violator, Babylonian cautionary king, and modern emblem of imperial imagery. Popular entertainment selectively merges these layers.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Art history

**[MSR]** The Victory Stele is now one of the canonical objects in global art-history surveys. Its diagonal ascent, bodily hierarchy, landscape, celestial symbols, and divine horned crown have shaped the modern image of Narām-Sîn far more than the fragmentary texts.

Recent art-historical criticism questions the easy claim that it directly created a continuous later tradition of victory monuments. Its exceptional modern status partly reflects museum display, aesthetic preference, and modern admiration for centralized imperial power.

### Literature, opera, and film

**[NEG]** No major securely documented opera, medieval romance, national epic, or canonical modern novel centered on Narām-Sîn was found in the reviewed sources. Modern summaries sometimes inflate the breadth of his reception by listing works about Sargon or Mesopotamia generally.

### Games

**[MI] *The Dark Pictures Anthology: House of Ashes* (2021).** Narām-Sîn appears in the prologue as a cursed “God King” fighting Gutians after violating Enlil’s temple. The game deliberately fuses the *Curse of Agade* with the historical king’s divinity, relocates him into a vast underground temple, and introduces extraterrestrial vampire-like creatures. The subterranean complex, immortality implications, and alien threat are inventions, not ancient variants.

**[MI] *Blue Archive* (2021; later story content).** “Throne of Naram-Sin” names an inner chamber associated with the Ark of Atra-Hasis. This is allusive reuse of Mesopotamian names, not an adaptation of the *Cuthean Legend*.

### National and political reception

**[MSR]** Akkad, Sumer, Babylon, and Assyria have been employed broadly in modern Iraqi territorial nationalism and Baʿthist heritage politics. The best-documented personal exemplars, however, are often Hammurabi, Nebuchadnezzar II, and Saladin. Narām-Sîn participates in the larger appropriation of Mesopotamian antiquity, especially through museum imagery and the language of the first empire, but the reviewed evidence does not establish a comparably distinct state cult or extremist appropriation centered specifically on him.

**[NEG]** No well-documented modern extremist movement organized specifically around Narām-Sîn was found. Claims of one should not be inferred from generalized neo-Mesopotamian nationalism.

### Comparative psychology and hero-pattern theory

**[MSR]** Otto Rank and Joseph Campbell are relevant principally through the birth legend of **Sargon**, not Narām-Sîn. Narām-Sîn lacks the exposed-child sequence needed for Rank’s birth-of-the-hero pattern. Lord Raglan’s and Campbell’s schemes can be imposed retrospectively only by mixing Sargon’s childhood, Narām-Sîn’s reign, and later disaster tales. Such a composite is modern analysis, not the ancient tradition.

Dumézilian Indo-European tripartition has no established explanatory standing for this Akkadian king. Structural comparison with other failed kings may illuminate motifs, but it cannot establish borrowing without historical transmission evidence.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

There is no serious doubt that a historical Narām-Sîn ruled Akkad: contemporary inscriptions, year names, administrative records, seals, architecture, and monuments establish him independently of later literature.

The disputed question is not whether he existed but how much history particular legends preserve.

- **Great Revolt:** strong historical core because royal inscriptions and contemporary material attest rebellion; literary coalitions and numbers are elaborated.
- **Lullubi campaign:** secured by the Victory Stele and inscriptional context.
- **Apišal:** possible historical memory, but location, ruler, and relationship to Armanum remain disputed.
- **Cuthean monster war:** no independent evidence for the supernatural host or three annihilated armies; the story uses historical geography and ethnic labels in a didactic fiction.
- **Destruction of Ekur:** contradicted or at least strongly challenged by contemporary claims that Narām-Sîn built for Enlil.
- **Gutian overthrow during his reign:** an oversimplification; Akkadian rulers followed him, and the historical Gutian role in collapse was more complex than the literary invasion.

### When was the *Cuthean Legend* composed?

**[MSR]** The earliest surviving copies are Old Babylonian, and older scholars sometimes treated the work broadly as early-second-millennium *narû* literature. Because copies are not originals, the story may draw on earlier oral or scribal traditions. However, there is no surviving Old Akkadian exemplar. Its safe documentary starting point is therefore the Old Babylonian manuscripts.

The older form was longer—perhaps two tablets and about 600 short lines—while the Standard Babylonian edition is a compressed one-tablet composition of approximately 175–180 long lines. “Growth” is therefore not a simple progression from short to long: standardization involved abbreviation, recomposition, and selective preservation.

### Is *narû literature* an ancient genre?

**[MSR]** Gurney described the work as *narû* literature because it imitates a royal stele: first-person royal introduction, narrative, future-facing admonition, and blessing/curse. More recent scholarship cautions that “*narû literature*” is a modern analytical category, not necessarily an emic genre name. “Pseudo-autobiographical royal inscription” describes the formal strategy without assuming ancient librarians grouped all such works together.

### Who are the enemy hordes?

Competing identifications include:

- Gutians remembered through the ideology of Akkad’s fall;
- Lullubians, encouraged by the name Anubanini;
- Hurrian or northern populations;
- the flexible **Ummān-manda**, a label later applied to different threatening outsiders;
- wholly mythologized agents of divine destruction.

The variants themselves rule out a single ethnic identification. The Standard Babylonian recension’s monstrous genealogy and artificial numbers serve literary theology; geographical names may nevertheless preserve knowledge of northern and eastern campaigning.

### Deification: royal cult or rhetorical self-inflation?

The Bassetki inscription and divine orthography establish more than metaphor. A temple was reportedly built for the king in Akkad, and officials used divine notation. Nevertheless:

- his divinity was localized;
- the exact chronology within his reign is uncertain;
- divine spelling is not consistent in every document;
- later copyists sometimes added classifiers not demonstrably present in originals;
- the practice did not automatically pass to his successor.

Thus “first living Mesopotamian king securely attested as deified” is defensible, while “first god-king in world history” is an expansive modern slogan.

### The Victory Stele

The stele’s horned helmet, hieratic scale, mountain ascent, weapons, and solar emblems support divine royal imagery. Disputed interpretations concern the number and meaning of the celestial disks, the original height of the broken monument, where it first stood, and how directly it influenced later art.

The stele was found at Susa because it had been taken there as booty. Its findspot is not evidence that the Lullubi battle occurred at Susa.

### Oral tradition

**[MSR]** Recurring campaigns, speeches, and royal names may have circulated orally alongside written monuments. The great temporal gaps and manuscript variants make oral transmission plausible but difficult to isolate. The evidence does not permit reconstruction of a verbatim Old Akkadian oral epic.

### Discoveries that changed the record

- discovery and publication of the Victory Stele at Susa: secured the divine conqueror’s visual image;
- Sultantepe tablet: supplied a substantially complete Standard Babylonian *Cuthean Legend*;
- Bassetki statue inscription, published 1976: provided direct evidence for deification and a temple;
- Urkeš seal of Tar’am-Agade: expanded the documented royal family and northern political network;
- digital joins and fragment identification at Nineveh: increased the known Standard Babylonian witnesses.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- that the *Curse of Agade* is a contemporary eyewitness chronicle;
- that Narām-Sîn personally ended the Akkadian dynasty;
- that his horned helmet proves extraterrestrial ancestry;
- that the Bronze Head from Nineveh can be securely called Narām-Sîn or Sargon—the identification remains uncertain;
- that Sargon’s river-basket birth belongs to Narām-Sîn;
- that his tomb or underground temple has been found;
- that the *Cuthean Legend* records literal nonhuman invaders;
- that he is securely identical with Nimrod;
- that all later Mesopotamian divine kingship descends institutionally and continuously from his cult.

### Assessment

The documentary history does not yield one Narām-Sîn. It yields a sequence of strategically incompatible figures:

1. a contemporary victorious king and localized living god;
2. an early-second-millennium memory of imperial grandeur and rebellion;
3. the impious destroyer of Ekur in Sumerian catastrophe literature;
4. the rash but educable king of the *Cuthean Legend*;
5. a royal warrior allied with Ištar and Erra;
6. a subject of international Hittite scribal culture;
7. a Babylonian negative exemplum subordinated to Marduk;
8. an antiquarian predecessor recovered by later kings and modern archaeologists;
9. a modern icon of divine empire and, in popular horror, a cursed god-king.

The tradition’s center is not a recoverable birth-to-death biography. It is the repeated use of a famous historical ruler to think about kingship, divine authorization, failed divination, imperial catastrophe, written memory, and the limits of force.
