# Midas: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Mortal Phrygian king who became a mythic exemplum and culture-hero-like figure; not a Greek demigod and, on present evidence, not a recipient of securely demonstrated Greek hero cult. Greek and Roman authors treat him as an ancient foreign king whose proverbial wealth, divine encounters, bodily transformation, and foolish judgments belong to legend.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest contemporary written evidence is Neo-Assyrian: in 717 BCE Sargon II’s records name **Mitâ/Mita, king of Muški**, an Anatolian ruler commonly—but inferentially, not demonstrably—identified with the Phrygian Midas. The earliest securely datable Greek literary reference is Tyrtaeus fr. 12 West, probably later seventh century BCE, where “Midas’ wealth” is already proverbial. An Old Phrygian monumental inscription, *midai lavagtaei vanaktei*, names a Midas as ruler, but its date remains disputed between the late eighth and early/mid-seventh century BCE.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary inscriptions, archaeology, or early Greek testimony.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** the classical or Roman form that became standard.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically localized form.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, genealogical, rationalizing, or handbook synthesis.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** an explicit identification or fusion of traditions.
- **[MEA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** orally circulating or locally adapted narrative, documented late.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than a primary attestation.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** demonstrably post-antique material presented in modern retellings.

## 1. Identification, names and epithets

Midas is simultaneously:

1. a name in the political history of Iron Age Anatolia;
2. the Greek memory of one or more Phrygian kings;
3. an Archaic and Classical story-king associated with wealth and the capture of a satyr;
4. the Roman canonical protagonist of the golden touch and the judgment between rustic and Apollonian music.

The records cannot prove that every “Midas” is the same person. Greek sources themselves move him between Phrygia, Lydia, Mygdonia/Macedonia, and the remote past. “Midas” may have been borne by more than one ruler or remembered as a dynastic name. [MSR]

### Names and scripts

| Form | Language/script | Attestation and status |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitâ / Mita** | Akkadian cuneiform, conventionally written *mi-ta-a* or *mi-ta-a-a* | Sargon II’s inscriptions and correspondence, 717–709 BCE: “king of Muški.” [SEA] |
| **Midai** | Old Phrygian alphabet; dative singular | M-01a on the so-called Midas Monument: *Ates … midai lavagtaei vanaktei edaes*, approximately “Ates … made/dedicated [this] for Midas, leader of the people and ruler.” [SEA, interpretation disputed] |
| **Μίδας**, genitive **Μίδου** | Ancient Greek | Tyrtaeus fr. 12; Herodotus 1.14 and 8.138; Xenophon, *Anabasis* 1.2.13. |
| **Midas** | Latin | Cicero, Ovid, Valerius Maximus, Hyginus, Pliny and other Roman-period authors. |

The final Greek **-s** is the normal Greek nominative ending and need not have existed in Phrygian. The correspondence *Mita*–*Midas* is phonetically plausible, but an etymology of the root is not securely established. “Midas means X” claims circulating without a Phrygian derivation should therefore be labeled speculative. [MSR]

### Titles, patronymics and geographical labels

- **“King of Muški”** (*šar māt Muški*): Assyrian political title for Mitâ. [SEA]
- **“King of Phrygia/the Phrygians”**: Herodotus 1.14; Xenophon 1.2.13. [SEA as Greek testimony]
- **“Son of Gordias”** (*Μίδην τὸν Γορδίεω*): Herodotus 1.14 and 8.138. This is the earliest surviving explicit patronymic. [SEA]
- **“Midas, leader of the people and ruler”**: a common interpretation of Old Phrygian *lavagtaei vanaktei*. Both words resemble Greek *lāwāgetās* and *wanax*, but their exact institutional force and the monument’s date remain debated. [SEA/MSR]
- **“Mygdonian king”** (*rex Mygdonius*): Hyginus, *Fabulae* 191, probably first or second century CE in its extant form. This relocates or labels him through the Mygdonian/Macedonian-Phrygian tradition. [LCE/RV]
- **“Berecyntian hero”**: Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 11.106, a poetic Phrygian geographical epithet. It does not show hero cult. [LCE]

### Parentage labels

- **Gordias’ son:** Herodotus, fifth century BCE. [SEA]
- **Son of the Mother Goddess/Cybele and Tmolus:** Hyginus 191’s difficult Latin, conventionally construed as *filius Matris deae a Timolo*, “son of the Mother Goddess by Tmolus.” [LCE/LS]
- **Son of Gordias and Cybele:** familiar later mythographic synthesis, but not stated by Herodotus and not a secure early genealogy. [LS]

The equation of the anonymous Phrygian **Matar/Mother** with Greek **Cybele/Rhea** is itself a Greek and Roman interpretive history. It must not be projected automatically into every Phrygian monument. [SI]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### A. Neo-Assyrian record

Sargon II’s fifth regnal year, 717 BCE, supplies the earliest securely dated written occurrence of Mitâ. Pisiri of Carchemish, an Assyrian treaty-vassal, had corresponded with “Mitâ, king of Muški”; Sargon destroyed Pisiri’s regime. In 715 BCE Sargon says that Assyrian forces recovered Cilician/Quean strongholds taken by Mitâ’s forces. By 709 BCE Mitâ returned captured Assyrian subjects through the governor of Que and sought peaceful relations; Sargon’s correspondence presents this as submission by a ruler who had not previously bowed to Assyria. [SEA]

These texts establish:

- a powerful western or northwestern Anatolian ruler named Mitâ;
- expansion toward the Cilician gates and Neo-Hittite states;
- conflict, diplomacy, and eventual accommodation with Assyria;
- activity from at least 717 through 709 BCE.

They do **not** establish:

- a golden touch;
- ass’s ears;
- Gordias as his father;
- kingship at Gordion;
- his death in a Cimmerian invasion.

The identification **Mitâ = the historical Midas of Phrygia** was popularized after Hugo Winckler’s publication of Sargon’s records in the late nineteenth century and is now widely accepted because of the matching name, date, region, and scale of power. Nevertheless, “Muški” is an Assyrian ethnogeographical term whose relation to Phrygians is not perfectly transparent; identity remains a strong historical inference. [MSR]

### B. Old Phrygian inscription M-01a

The façade popularly called the “Midas Monument” at Yazılıkaya/Midas City bears:

> *Ates : arkiaevais : akenanogavos : midai : lavagtaei : vanaktei : edaes*

A cautious translation is:

> “Ates, son of Arkias, the *akenanogavos*, made/dedicated [it] for Midas, the *lavagta-* and *vanak-*.”

“People’s leader and ruler” is plausible but not philologically certain. The façade is a cult monument, probably associated with the Phrygian Mother, not Midas’ excavated tomb. Its dating has ranged from the late eighth century to the early/mid-seventh; a recent defense places it in the late eighth century, during the historically documented Midas’ reign. [SEA, disputed chronology]

### C. Earliest Greek evidence

**Tyrtaeus fr. 12 West**, commonly dated to the later seventh century BCE, says that even a man “richer than Midas and Cinyras” is of no account without martial excellence. Midas requires no narrative explanation: his wealth is already proverbial. The poem’s precise date and attribution have occasionally been questioned, but it remains the earliest generally accepted Greek literary reference. [SEA]

**Herodotus, *Histories* 1.14**, composed in the later fifth century BCE, reports that Midas son of Gordias dedicated at Delphi the royal judgment-seat on which he sat. Herodotus says Gyges was the first non-Greek known to dedicate there “after Midas,” and that the throne remained visible in his day. This is valuable testimony to a displayed object and Delphic memory, but the throne is lost and was not independently inscribed in the surviving record. [SEA as fifth-century testimony]

**Herodotus 8.138** locates the fragrant, self-growing sixty-petaled “gardens of Midas son of Gordias” beneath Mount Bermion in Macedonia/Mygdonia. The passage belongs to the migration tradition according to which Phrygians had earlier lived in Europe as Bryges. [SEA/RV]

### E. Named corpora in which the Phrygian Midas is absent or not securely present

Absence is material here:

- **Homer, *Iliad* and *Odyssey*:** no Midas. Homer knows Phrygians and Phrygian figures, but does not name him.
- **Epic Cycle fragments:** no secure Midas episode.
- **Hesiod, including the surviving *Catalogue of Women* fragments:** no secure Phrygian Midas.
- **Pindar:** “Midas of Acragas” is an historical flute-player celebrated in *Pythian* 12, not the Phrygian king.
- **Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides:** no surviving play or fragment securely gives a Midas cycle.
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica*:** Phrygian religious geography appears, but no Midas episode.
- **Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*:** no substantial Midas narrative in the surviving text.
- **Pausanias:** preserves local monuments and traditions but not the golden touch or ear narrative.
- No surviving evidence places Midas in the Trojan War, Argonautic expedition, Theban cycle, or Homeric heroic genealogies.

All ancient literary texts survive through later manuscript traditions, usually medieval; no authorial autograph survives. The Old Phrygian and Assyrian inscriptions are therefore uniquely contemporary material.

## 3. The story

### 3.1 Birth, childhood and accession

Herodotus calls Midas “son of Gordias” but narrates neither his birth nor accession. [SEA]

Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.78, and Valerius Maximus 1.6 ext. 2 report that while the infant slept, ants filled his mouth with grains of wheat. Interpreters predicted extraordinary wealth, later fulfilled. This is a Roman-period prodigy story, not early biography. [LCE]

Later accounts merge Midas into the Gordian foundation cycle: Gordias, an obscure farmer, arrives in a wagon in fulfillment of an oracle and is chosen king; the wagon’s fastening becomes the Gordian knot. Some versions make Midas the son who accompanies his parents, but ancient narratives differ about whether Gordias or Midas is the oracle-selected king. Alexander’s cutting or unfastening of the knot belongs to Alexander historiography, not originally to the golden-touch cycle. [LS]

### 3.2 Wealth before the golden touch

Midas’ wealth is older in the evidence than the tale explaining it:

- Tyrtaeus: “richer than Midas.” [SEA]
- Herodotus: a king able to send a remarkable throne to Delphi. [SEA as testimony]
- Phrygian elite archaeology: monumental architecture, bronze vessels, inlaid wooden furniture, textiles, and enormous tumuli show genuine royal expenditure, though none proves a golden-touch myth. [SEA]
- Lydia’s Pactolus carried natural electrum; later narrative linked this real resource to Midas’ cleansing. [LCE/MSR]

Thus wealth was the early attribute; the transmuting touch is a later explanatory tale.

### 3.3 Silenus captured with wine

The earliest surviving representations are sixth-century Attic black-figure vases. A c. 550–540 BCE amphora attributed to the Painter of Berlin 1686 shows Midas enthroned while a bound Silenus is brought by Hermes; another black-figure pelike attributed to the Acheloös Painter shows Midas’ henchmen lying in wait. The images precede any surviving full literary telling. [SEA]

Herodotus’ Macedonian rose garden does not explicitly mention Silenus, but later tradition connects beautiful gardens with the capture. [SEA versus later synthesis]

Xenophon, *Anabasis* 1.2.13, reports beside the road at Thymbrium a spring “called Midas’,” where Midas allegedly caught “the Satyr” by mixing wine into the water. Pausanias 1.4.1 reports a comparable local tradition at Ancyra, together with a surviving anchor allegedly found by Midas. Neither author says that Midas imprisoned Silenus in order to obtain a golden wish. [SEA/RV]

In Aristotle’s lost *Eudemus*, as quoted by Plutarch, Midas pursues and questions the captured Silenus. Silenus reluctantly delivers the pessimistic wisdom:

> For mortals, the best is not to be born; next best is to die as soon as possible.

This philosophical Midas seeks forbidden wisdom rather than gold. [LCE philosophical adaptation]

Theopompus’ fourth-century *Philippica*, preserved in Aelian, similarly makes Silenus answer Midas but substitutes a fantastic geography: beyond the known world lie enormous continents and the cities Eusebes and Machimos. [LS]

Ovid finally joins the captive to Dionysus’ reward. Phrygian peasants find the aged, drunken Silenus; Midas recognizes him because Orpheus and Eumolpus had initiated the king into Dionysiac rites. Midas entertains him for ten days and returns him to Dionysus on the eleventh. [LCE]

### 3.4 The golden touch

Aristotle, *Politics* 1.9, supplies the earliest extant literary statement of the motif. Discussing the difference between money and usable wealth, he says of the “famous Midas” that because of his insatiable prayer, everything set before him turned to gold. Aristotle gives no Silenus, Dionysus, starvation sequence, or Pactolus cure. [SEA/LCE]

Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 11.100–145, provides the canonical sequence:

1. Dionysus, grateful for Silenus’ return, offers a wish.
2. Midas asks that whatever his body touches become yellow gold.
3. Dionysus grants it but recognizes that the wish is disastrous.
4. Twig, stone, earth, grain, fruit, doorposts, food, wine and water become gold.
5. Unable to eat or drink, Midas acknowledges his error and prays for release.
6. Dionysus tells him to follow the Pactolus to its source and wash.
7. The power passes from his body into the riverbed, accounting for its gold-bearing sands.

Ovid never says that Midas touches a daughter. [LCE]

Hyginus 191 does not preserve the golden touch at all; his Midas story concerns the musical contest and ears. The divergence warns against treating “the Midas myth” as a single ancient fixed text.

### 3.5 Pactolus

The Pactolus flows down Mount Tmolus near Sardis and historically carried electrum. Ovid’s tale turns geology and Lydian wealth into divine aetiology: before Midas’ bath the river was not golden; afterward its soil hardened into gold-veined clods. [LCE]

The episode also relocates a Phrygian king into Lydian sacred-economic geography. This may reflect long interaction among Phrygia, Lydia, Aeolis, and Greek storytelling, but a specific political agenda cannot be proved. [MSR]

### 3.6 Judgment of Pan and Apollo

After renouncing wealth, Ovid’s Midas lives in the countryside around Tmolus and follows Pan. Pan challenges Apollo’s music; the mountain god Tmolus judges:

- Pan plays the rustic reed pipe.
- Apollo plays the gemmed ivory lyre.
- Tmolus awards Apollo victory.
- Everyone accepts the decision except Midas.
- Midas declares it unjust.
- Apollo changes the offending ears into gray-haired, mobile ass’s ears. [LCE]

This is not the same contest as Apollo versus Marsyas. Hyginus 191 explicitly preserves instability: Apollo competed with **Marsyas—or, some say, Pan**; Tmolus judged for Apollo, while Midas preferred Marsyas. Hyginus then relates Marsyas’ flaying. [LCE/variant]

Ass’s ears existed independently of Ovid’s explanation. Aristophanes alludes to them in 388 BCE, and red-figure vases show an ass-eared Midas from the mid-fifth century BCE. Earlier black-figure Silenus-capture scenes generally do not. The musical judgment is therefore an explanation of an already circulating bodily feature, not demonstrably its origin. [SEA/MSR]

Possible scholarly interpretations include:

- foreign royal headgear misunderstood or caricatured as ears;
- affinity with Silenus and satyrs, frequently equine-eared;
- the ass as a sign of stupidity and bad musical judgment;
- Greek caricature of the wealthy “barbarian” ruler;
- royal omniscience—long ears that “hear everything”—in ancient rationalization.

None is established as the single original meaning. [MSR]

### 3.7 Barber and speaking reeds

Ovid gives the earliest surviving complete barber-and-reeds form:

1. Midas hides his ears beneath a purple turban.
2. His unnamed hair-cutter discovers them.
3. Unable either to tell or keep the secret, the servant digs a hole.
4. He whispers the secret into the earth and fills it in.
5. Reeds grow there.
6. Stirred by the wind, they repeat the buried words and expose the king.

The story makes secrecy materially impossible: royal shame enters earth, vegetation, breath, and public speech. Ovid does not say that Midas kills his barbers. [LCE]

Later versions name or transform the barber, substitute Midas’ wife, or have successive barbers executed. These are medieval, folkloric, or modern adaptations unless tied to an earlier text. [MEA/F]

### 3.8 Silenus’ wisdom

The philosophical Silenus story is not a moral about greed. Midas asks what is best for humanity; the answer negates human life. Aristotle’s *Eudemus* made the episode part of a dialogue concerning soul and mortality. Plutarch and later authors preserved it, and Nietzsche famously reused “the wisdom of Silenus” in *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872). [LCE; modern philosophical reception]

### 3.9 Anchurus and the chasm

Pseudo-Plutarch, *Parallel Stories* 5, says that during Midas’ reign at Celaenae a rain-accompanied chasm swallowed houses. An oracle required the city’s most precious possession. Midas threw in gold and silver, unsuccessfully. His son **Anchurus**, recognizing human life as most precious, embraced Midas and his wife **Timothea**, then rode his horse into the abyss; it closed. An altar of Zeus Idaios was erected there, and the place was called Anchurus. [LCE/LS]

The text deliberately parallels Rome’s Marcus Curtius. Its alleged Greek authority is Callisthenes’ *Metamorphoses* Book 2, a lost or dubious citation. This late parallel narrative is not evidence for an early Phrygian rite of human sacrifice. [LS]

### 3.10 Foundation of Ancyra

Pausanias 1.4.5 reports that Midas founded Ancyra (“Anchor”) and that an anchor he found was still kept in a sanctuary of Zeus. This is a local foundation aetiology built on the city-name *ankyra*, “anchor.” It is not independently confirmed by archaeology. [RV]

### 3.11 Wife and coinage

A fragment attributed to Aristotle calls **Hermodice**, wife of Midas, beautiful, wise, and ingenious, and credits her with first striking coinage for Cyme. Pollux 9.83 calls her **Demodice**, daughter of **Agamemnon, king of Cyme**, and lists her among several rival inventors of money. [LS]

Because coinage began after the historical Midas’ floruit in many conventional chronologies, scholars have proposed:

- a later Midas bearing the dynastic name;
- corrupted chronology;
- a learned fiction linking Phrygian/Lydian metal wealth to Aeolian Cyme;
- a loose use of “invented” for introducing coinage locally.

No option is proven. [MSR]

### 3.12 Death

Competing records must remain separate:

- **Strabo 1.3.21:** Cimmerians overran Phrygia, and Midas drank bull’s blood and died. [LCE historical tradition]
- **Plutarchan tradition:** nightmares or visions drove Midas to suicide by bull’s blood. [LCE]
- **Eusebius/Jerome chronography:** places Midas’ end around 696 BCE.
- **Julius Africanus:** a different chronological tradition places the end around 676 BCE.
- **Assyrian records:** Mita is still active in 709 BCE but do not report his death.
- **Gordion archaeology:** the main citadel destruction once assigned to c. 700 BCE and the Cimmerians has been redated substantially earlier, undermining the simple equation “destruction layer = Cimmerian conquest that killed Midas.” [SEA/MSR]

Ancient “bull’s blood” was conventionally imagined as poisonous, but the tale is a recurrent literary mode of royal suicide; it is not a medically secure cause of death. No identified body or inscribed tomb of Midas exists.

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Was Midas worshipped as a hero?

No securely identified shrine, altar, priesthood, sacrifice, festival, games, or votive corpus dedicated to **Midas as a dead hero** is presently known. Farnell-style Greek hero-cult categories therefore should not be imposed on him. He is a mythic king and religious actor, but “hero” in the literary sense does not prove hero cult. [SEA: absence]

The Delphic throne reported by Herodotus was a royal dedication to Apollo, not a cult object offered to Midas. The Spring of Midas, gardens, anchor, and place-names are localized memory and aetiology, not evidence of worship. [SEA/RV]

### The Midas Monument

The Yazılıkaya façade is a monumental Phrygian religious installation bearing Midas’ name and royal titles. Its niche and architectural imitation suggest cultic display, probably connected with the Phrygian Mother. It may document royal patronage, sacral kingship, or posthumous commemoration, but it is not an excavated burial chamber and does not establish cult **of** Midas. [SEA/MSR]

### Tumulus MM, the “Midas Mound”

The University of Pennsylvania expedition opened Tumulus MM at Gordion in 1957. It contains a remarkably preserved log-built chamber, the remains of an older man, bronze vessels, fibulae, carved and inlaid wooden furniture, textiles, and traces of a large funerary banquet. The mound is approximately 53 metres high and 300 metres across. [SEA]

Earlier chronology made the tomb appear compatible with the famous late-eighth-century Midas. Dendrochronological and radiocarbon reassessment dates the chamber’s timbers around **740 BCE**, before Mita’s documented activity through 709. The leading reconstruction identifies the occupant as Midas’ predecessor—often conjecturally his father Gordias—and the mound as an accession-era monument built by Midas. No inscription identifies the dead man. [MSR]

Therefore:

- “Midas Mound” is a modern archaeological nickname.
- “The skull of King Midas” is an overstatement.
- The museum facial reconstruction is of the unidentified MM occupant.
- Midas’ own tomb has not been found or securely identified.

The banquet residue—meat stew, mixed fermented drink involving grape, barley beer, and honey mead—documents elite Phrygian funerary practice, not a named ritual of Midas. [SEA]

### Political and cultural memory

The royal tumuli and citadel monumentalized Phrygian authority. Later Greek claims about a Delphic throne placed Midas inside pan-Hellenic sacred memory, while the Gordian knot made his dynasty a retrospective prelude to Alexander’s Asian kingship. Modern Turkish museums and tourism use “King Midas,” “Midas Mound,” and “Midas City” as accessible heritage names. These labels preserve memory but can blur object, ruler, and legend. [MSR/modern reception]

## 6. Historical evolution

### Late eighth century BCE: Anatolian ruler

Mitâ is an expansionist king in Assyrian geopolitical records, while Old Phrygian monumental writing names a royally titled Midas. Gordion displays centralized wealth, literacy, long-distance trade, and elaborate funerary monumentality. [SEA]

### Seventh century BCE: proverbial wealth and death tradition

Greek poetry already treats Midas as the measure of riches. Greek contact with Phrygia helps explain his early incorporation into Greek poetic memory. Later chronographers place his death amid Cimmerian incursions, but the exact date and archaeological correlate are insecure. [SEA/MSR]

### Sixth century BCE: Silenus in images

Attic vase painters depict the capture and delivery of Silenus to an enthroned eastern king. The visual tradition gives Midas a narrative role before any surviving prose account. [SEA]

### Fifth–fourth centuries BCE: localization and philosophical use

Herodotus attaches Midas to Delphi and Macedonia. Red-figure vase painters emphasize ass’s ears and eastern royal costume. Aristophanes assumes that the ears are familiar. Xenophon localizes the Silenus trap at a named spring. Aristotle turns the king into two philosophical examples: insatiable monetary desire and Silenus’ pessimistic wisdom. [SEA/LCE]

### Hellenistic period: systematization and geographical blending

Lost mythographers and local historians probably developed genealogies, foundations, and etiologies. Phrygia, Lydia, Mygdonia, Tmolus, Sardis, Cyme, Celaenae and Ancyra accumulate around the name. The fragmentary state of the sources prevents a secure stemma of versions. [LS/MSR]

### Roman period: canonical synthesis

Ovid combines Silenus, Dionysiac initiation, the fatal wish, Pactolus, rural conversion, Pan’s contest, Apollo’s punishment, the barber, and the reeds into one artfully continuous metamorphic narrative. Hyginus preserves the Pan/Marsyas instability and a different parentage. Strabo, Pausanias, Plutarchan texts, Pollux, Aelian and Philostratus retain competing historical, local, philosophical, and rationalized Midases. [LCE]

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Chronographers date Midas among ancient kings; scholia and compilers preserve excerpts from otherwise lost authors. Euhemerism increasingly treats strange features as distorted history—for example, ears as inherited satyr physiognomy or as a metaphor for spies. [LS]

### Medieval Europe

Ovid remains the main conduit. Moralized Ovids interpret Midas as avarice, bad judgment, tyrannical secrecy, and the inevitable disclosure of rulers’ faults. [MEA]

Chaucer’s *Wife of Bath’s Tale*, late fourteenth century, transfers the secret from Midas’ barber to **Midas’ wife**: she whispers it into marsh water. Chaucer signals Ovid as authority while deliberately changing the secret-keeper, using the story in a comic argument about whether women can keep secrets. [MEA]

### Early modern Europe

John Lyly’s *Midas*, performed around 1589–1590 and printed in 1592, turns the king into an allegory of avaricious, expansionist tyranny, commonly read against Philip II of Spain. George Sandys’ 1632 Ovid commentary rationalizes the ears as royal espionage and the reeds as historians exposing concealed vice. Paintings repeatedly merge Pan’s contest with Marsyas’ flaying. [MEA]

### Nineteenth century

Mary Shelley’s *Midas* (written 1820, published posthumously in 1922), with lyrics by Percy Bysshe Shelley, adapts both the musical contest and golden touch for juvenile/educational drama. Nathaniel Hawthorne’s “The Golden Touch,” first published in *A Wonder-Book for Girls and Boys* in 1851, invents Midas’ beloved daughter **Marygold** and has him turn her into a statue. This became one of the most influential modern forms. [MI]

Nietzsche’s *Birth of Tragedy* (1872) makes Silenus’ answer emblematic of Greek tragic pessimism. [Modern philosophical reception]

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Archaeology, Assyriology and dendrochronology split the composite figure back into evidence layers:

- Assyrian Mitâ;
- Old Phrygian ruler;
- Greek story-king;
- Roman moral exemplum;
- modern children’s character.

At the same time “Midas touch” changed semantically: in English it normally means an unfailing capacity to make money or succeed, omitting the ancient/Ovidian catastrophe that makes the gift unusable.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **John Lyly, *Midas* (c. 1589–1590; printed 1592):** political-allegorical drama of greed and bad kingship. [MEA]
- **Chaucer, *Wife of Bath’s Tale* (late 14th c.):** wife replaces barber. [MEA]
- **Kane O’Hara, *Midas* (1760s; second edition 1766):** extraordinarily successful English burletta/comic opera drawing on Ovid and contemporary musical satire.
- **Mary Shelley, *Midas* (1820):** educational verse drama; Percy Shelley supplied songs for Pan and Apollo.
- **Nathaniel Hawthorne, “The Golden Touch” (1851):** invents Marygold, the golden daughter. [MI]
- **Patrick Skene Catling, *The Chocolate Touch* (1952):** children’s transposition from gold to chocolate. [MI]

### Visual art

Representative, not exhaustive:

- Attic black-figure Silenus-capture vases, c. 550–530 BCE.
- British Museum stamnos E447 by the Midas Painter, mid-fifth century BCE: ass-eared Midas and Silenus.
- Titian, *The Flaying of Marsyas*, c. 1570–1576: an introspective seated king often identified as Midas appears in the Marsyas complex, although the identification and self-portrait reading remain debated.
- Nicolas Poussin, *Midas and Bacchus*, c. 1629–1630: the king petitions to lose the touch.
- Hendrick de Clerck and related Flemish treatments of *The Judgment of Midas*, late sixteenth/early seventeenth century.
- Jan van den Hoecke, *The Judgment of Midas*, seventeenth century.
- English and continental decorative porcelain repeatedly uses the Apollo–Pan–Midas contest.

### Music and performance

- O’Hara’s *Midas* helped form early English musical theatre and puppet/burletta traditions.
- André-Ernest-Modeste Grétry’s *Le jugement de Midas* (1778) used the myth for satire of French musical institutions.
- Richard Strauss’ *Die Liebe der Danae* (composed 1937–1940; public premiere 1952) radically revises Midas as a figure whose divine golden wealth is relinquished for human love.
- Numerous school plays, ballets and children’s operas use Hawthorne rather than Ovid as their narrative source.

### Film, television, comics and games

Modern fantasy frequently detaches the name from Phrygia:

- characters named Midas become alchemists, gilded villains, financiers, casino owners, or weaponized “gold touch” users;
- games render touch as a combat or resource-production mechanic;
- Fortnite’s Midas, introduced in 2020, is an original crime-boss/agent character with a gilding power, not an adaptation of the ancient narrative beyond name and motif. [MI]
- “King Midas” episodes in animated myth series usually incorporate the Hawthorne daughter. [MI]

### Commercial and technical language

“Midas touch” is used for profitable investors, successful producers, athletes, coaches and entrepreneurs. Commercial names include banks, jewelry businesses, automotive services and software. In human-computer interaction, the **“Midas touch problem”** means that a gaze-controlled system may activate whatever the user merely looks at: an apt restoration of the unwanted-gift dimension.

### Politics, Orientalism and nationalism

Greek art and literature can present Midas as the magnificently clothed but foolish “eastern” monarch. Modern scholarship has accordingly examined him as part of Greek construction of Anatolian otherness. [MSR]

Modern Turkish heritage presentation legitimately emphasizes Midas as part of Phrygia’s Anatolian past, but popular claims that a particular skull, mound, palace, or treasure definitively belonged to him exceed the evidence. Conversely, Balkan migration traditions have sometimes been pressed into modern ethnic claims. Herodotus’ Brygian/Macedonian geography documents an ancient migration story, not a modern national title deed. No well-documented extremist cult of Midas comparable to appropriations of some Norse or Spartan figures is evident; isolated nationalist claims should not be inflated into an organized reception tradition. [MSR]

### Documented modern inventions

- Midas’ daughter **Marygold/Marigold** and her transformation: Hawthorne, 1851. [MI]
- A daughter named **Zoe**: no ancient source located; modern circulation. [MI]
- Midas killing every barber: later folklore/retelling, not Ovid. [F/MI]
- Tumulus MM as certainly Midas’ grave: obsolete or sensationalized modern identification. [MSR rejected]
- Its reconstructed face as certainly “King Midas”: modern overclaim.
- Midas participating in the Trojan War or Argonautic voyage: modern conflation.
- The touch as an uncomplicated superpower: modern inversion of Aristotle’s and Ovid’s point.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historical Midas versus Mita

The identification is strong because:

- *Mita* and *Midas* are closely corresponding names;
- both belong to the late-eighth-century Anatolian political world;
- Assyria depicts Mitâ as a ruler of exceptional regional power;
- Phrygian archaeology shows a polity capable of that role;
- Old Phrygian independently attests *Midai*.

Objections and cautions:

- Muški is not simply and consistently synonymous with Phrygia across all Near Eastern sources;
- Assyrian texts do not name Gordion or call Mitâ “Phrygian”;
- more than one Midas may have ruled;
- Greek legend telescopes geography and centuries.

The responsible formula is therefore: **Mitâ is very probably, but not demonstrably, the late-eighth-century Phrygian Midas.**

### One Midas or several

Herodotus treats Midas as more ancient than Gyges yet attaches him to traditions whose geography may precede Phrygian migration. Pollux’s coinage-wife may require a later chronology. Greek sources often reuse royal names. Scholars consequently distinguish:

- the late-eighth-century historical king;
- an ancestral or legendary Midas;
- later rulers bearing the same name;
- a composite literary Midas.

No surviving Phrygian king list resolves the problem.

### Tumulus MM

Rodney Young excavated it in 1957 when conventional chronology allowed identification with Midas. Improved tree-ring dating shifted it to around 740 BCE. Since Assyrian Mitâ remained active in 709, MM cannot plausibly be his burial if the identification is correct. The “father Gordias” attribution is plausible dynastic reconstruction, not epigraphic fact.

### Gordion’s destruction and the Cimmerians

An older historical scheme dated Gordion’s great fire to about 700 BCE and tied it to Midas’ suicide and Cimmerian invasion. Revised radiocarbon and dendrochronology moved the major Early Phrygian destruction toward c. 800 BCE, before the historical Midas. That discovery removed what had seemed the archaeological confirmation of Strabo. A later Cimmerian impact remains historically possible, but the famous destruction layer no longer proves it.

### Origin of the golden touch

The evidentiary sequence is:

1. proverbial wealth;
2. Aristotle’s compressed impossible-prayer example;
3. Ovid’s full Dionysus–Silenus–Pactolus narrative.

Whether Ovid inherited a lost Hellenistic poem or substantially created the surviving sequence is unresolved. It is safer to call Ovid the earliest complete extant narrative, not necessarily its inventor.

### Ass’s ears

The ears appear in fifth-century art and Aristophanes before the surviving Pan–Apollo narrative. This has generated competing explanations—ritual, satyric, ethnic caricature, royal costume, stupidity, or omniscient hearing. Vase chronology proves only that Ovid’s explanation is later than the image motif; it does not identify the motif’s original meaning.

### Silenus: myth, ritual and philosophy

The capture with wine occurs in art and localized springs; the pessimistic answer belongs to philosophical prose; the golden reward belongs to Ovid’s synthesis. Scholars debate whether these descended from one ritual narrative or were independent clusters attached to Midas. The lost intermediaries make a confident reconstruction impossible.

### Cult debate

The Midas Monument is cultic architecture with a royal dedication; it does not necessarily make Midas the cult recipient. The lack of a named heroon, sacrifices, priesthood, or festival distinguishes him from figures such as Achilles or Orestes. Claims of a “cult of Midas” generally infer too much from the monument or royal funerary display.

### Sources, finds and losses

Major losses include:

- Aristotle’s *Eudemus*;
- Theopompus’ *Philippica*;
- local Phrygian and Lydian histories;
- the Delphic throne;
- most of the context behind later scholia;
- any securely identified tomb of Midas.

Major advances include:

- decipherment and publication of Sargon II’s archives in the nineteenth century;
- collection of Old Phrygian inscriptions;
- the 1957 opening of Tumulus MM;
- residue analysis of its funerary vessels;
- revised Gordion dendrochronology and radiocarbon dating;
- systematic vase cataloguing by Beazley and the *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae*.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- Tumulus MM certainly contains Midas.
- Gordion’s main destruction layer proves the Cimmerians killed him.
- Greek sources prove every Midas was one individual.
- The golden daughter is ancient Greek.
- Ovid’s Pan contest is the earliest attestation of the ears.
- Midas was worshipped throughout Greece as a hero.
- The Phrygian inscription can be translated in every detail with certainty.
- Bull’s blood supplies a medically verified account of his death.
