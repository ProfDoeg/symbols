# Menelaus: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Achaean hero and epic king of Lacedaemon/Sparta; after death or translation, a **cult-receiving hero or divinized consort of Helen** at Therapne. Isocrates explicitly says Spartans sacrificed to the pair “not as heroes but as gods,” whereas modern scholarship usually describes the archaeological complex as a hero shrine.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Menelaus is securely named in the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, poems substantially shaped in the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, although their textual fixation was gradual. The cult site later called the Menelaion began receiving offerings in the later eighth century BCE. Its earliest surviving named dedication, probably c.650–600 BCE, is principally to Helen and identifies her through Menelaus; the first unambiguous dedication addressed directly to Menelaus dates around the late sixth or early fifth century BCE. No accepted Linear B tablet names the legendary Menelaus.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[E1] Secure early:** Homeric or other Archaic evidence, or securely excavated Archaic material.
- **[E2] Classical elaboration:** fifth–fourth-century BCE drama, history, art, or testimony.
- **[E3] Later canonical/systematic:** Hellenistic and Roman mythography, poetry, scholia, or antiquarian synthesis.
- **[E4] Regional variant:** a locally situated and source-dated alternative.
- **[E5] Late antique/medieval accretion:** Dictys, Dares, Byzantine material, medieval romance.
- **[E6] Modern reconstruction:** scholarly inference not directly stated by ancient evidence.
- **[E7] Modern invention/reception:** modern art, fiction, cinema, commerce, or unsourced popular lore.

Dates assigned to lost works concern probable composition; the evidence may survive only as a quotation or summary centuries later.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Orientation

Menelaus is the fair-haired, warlike but comparatively restrained younger Atreid: husband of Helen, brother of Agamemnon, king of “hollow Lacedaemon,” commander of sixty ships, and the injured host whose marriage and household Paris violates. The *Iliad* makes him a capable warrior but not its supreme fighter; the *Odyssey* makes him a wealthy returned wanderer, teller of Egyptian marvels, host of Telemachus, and prospective inhabitant of Elysium. Cyclic epic and tragedy developed his roles in Helen’s wooing, the oath of the suitors, the embassy to Troy, the Wooden Horse, Troy’s capture, his recovery or attempted killing of Helen, and his daughter Hermione’s disputed marriage. At Therapne, material and literary evidence shows that he and Helen became recipients of continuing cult.

### Name and scripts

- Greek: **Μενέλαος**, *Menélaos*; epic genitive **Μενελάου**, dative **Μενελάῳ/Μενελάϊ**.
- Doric/Laconian inscriptions preserve local orthography, including **ΜΕΝΕΛΑΙ** in the dative.
- Latin: **Menelaus**; medieval vernacular forms include Old French **Menelaus/Menelans**, Italian **Menelao**, French **Ménélas**, Spanish **Menelao**, and Modern Greek **Μενέλαος**.
- English *Menelaus* comes through Latin.

The best-supported linguistic analysis treats the name as a compound of a verbal element related to **μένω**, “remain, stand fast,” and **λαός**, “people/host,” hence approximately “he who stands fast against/for the host.” The alternative popular derivation from **μένος**, “might, spirit, rage,” plus *laos*—“might of the people”—is morphologically less favored. Because no secure Mycenaean spelling of the name exists, reconstructions such as hypothetical Linear B *me-ne-ra-wo* are **[E6]**, not attestations. Anna Stelow emphasizes the thematic fitness of “stands fast against the hostile host” for the Iliadic character [S1].

### Patronymics and epithets

Homer repeatedly calls him:

- **Ἀτρεΐδης / Ἀτρείδης**, “son/descendant of Atreus” [E1].
- **ξανθὸς Μενέλαος**, “fair-/golden-haired Menelaus” [E1]. *Xanthos* denotes a bright, yellow, tawny, or fair color; “blond” is conventional but more precise than the popular modern certainty that he was red-haired.
- **βοὴν ἀγαθός**, “good at the battle cry” [E1].
- **ἀρηΐφιλος**, “dear to Ares” or “warlike” [E1].
- **ἀρήϊος**, “Ares-like/warlike” [E1].
- **διοτρεφής**, “Zeus-nurtured,” a royal epithet [E1].
- **κῦδος Ἀχαιῶν** or language of *kudos*, “glory of the Achaeans,” in contextual rather than uniquely personal formulas [E1].

These expressions belong to an inherited oral-formulaic system and should not all be read as individualized psychological descriptions. Menelaus is particularly saturated with Ares-derived formulas even though the plot repeatedly places stronger warriors around him; the tension is literary, not evidence for a lost war god [S2].

### Genealogical identity

The dominant Homeric formulation makes Menelaus and Agamemnon sons of Atreus. Later genealogy disagrees:

1. **Atreus + Aerope → Agamemnon, Menelaus**: the ordinary Homeric and later canonical scheme [E1/E3].
2. **Pleisthenes + Aerope → Agamemnon, Menelaus; adopted or reared by Atreus**: Hesiodic fragments, scholia, Dictys, and mythographic material [E1 fragment/E3].
3. **Pleisthenes + Cleolla, daughter of Dias → Agamemnon, Menelaus, Anaxibia**: another genealogical arrangement attributed through the Hesiodic tradition [E1 fragment].
4. **Atreus → Pleisthenes → the Atreids**: a compromise allowing *Atreidai* to mean descendants rather than immediate sons [E3].

The instability is genuine. Pleisthenes is a genealogical “buffer” inserted at different generations; it should not be harmonized into a single family tree.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

The archaeological and poetic evidence are nearly contemporary but answer different questions:

- The *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, conventionally late eighth/early seventh century BCE, securely attest an already elaborate Menelaus tradition [E1].
- Late-Geometric offerings at Therapne establish ritual activity in the later eighth century BCE, but anonymous offerings cannot by themselves name the recipient [E1].
- A bronze aryballos from the Menelaion, object and inscription variously dated between the mid-seventh and around 600 BCE, reads approximately **Δεινὶς ἀνέθεε … Ἑλέναι … Μενελάϊ**, “Deinis dedicated [this] to Helen, [wife] of Menelaus.” Restoration and exact syntax remain debated; it names Menelaus but is addressed to Helen [E1].
- A bronze implement or *harpax*, c.570 BCE, bears **ΤΑΙ ϜΕΛΕΝΑΙ**, “to Helen” [E1].
- A limestone base or stele of the late sixth/early fifth century bears **Εὐθυκρένης ἔθηκε τοῖ Μενελάϊ**, “Euthycrenes dedicated [this] to Menelaus”: the earliest unambiguous direct dedication [E1]. The excavation report stresses that the site was unoccupied between the destruction/abandonment of the Late Helladic settlement around 1200 BCE and renewed cult activity in the later eighth century [S3–S5].

Thus neither the Bronze Age architecture nor the Archaic shrine proves that a historical Menelaus occupied the earlier complex.

### Primary corpus

#### Archaic and early Classical

- **Homer, *Iliad***; Greek, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. Menelaus commands Lacedaemonians, fights Paris, is wounded by Pandarus, participates in battle and councils, kills several Trojans, competes in Patroclus’ funeral games, and leads the defense of Patroclus’ corpse [E1].
- **Homer, *Odyssey***; Greek, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. Books 3–4 and 15 present his delayed return, Egyptian wanderings, capture of Proteus, hospitality to Telemachus, recovered domestic life with Helen, and promised translation to Elysium [E1; S6].
- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women***; Greek, probably seventh–sixth century BCE, surviving in papyri and quotations. It treated Helen’s suitors, Menelaus’ marriage and children, and variants of Atreid genealogy [E1, fragmentary].
- **Stesichorus, *Helen* and *Palinode***; Greek lyric, early–mid sixth century BCE, fragmentary. The *Palinode* denied that the true Helen went to Troy, substituting an image or phantom; Plato is a major witness [E1 fragment].
- **Epic Cycle**:
  - *Cypria*, probably seventh or sixth century BCE: wooing, oath, Paris’ visit during Menelaus’ absence, abduction/elopement, mustering, embassy, and opening war [E1 fragment through later summaries].
  - *Little Iliad*, probably seventh–sixth century BCE: Wooden Horse and preparations for Troy’s capture; Menelaus was among the warriors inside in later lists.
  - *Iliou Persis*, attributed to Arctinus: Menelaus kills Deiphobus and recovers Helen [E1 fragment].
  - *Nostoi*, seventh–sixth century BCE: the divided returns and Menelaus’ eventual arrival from Egypt [E1 fragment].
  
  The surviving Proclean summaries are late witnesses and must not be mistaken for intact Archaic texts [S7].
- **Alcman**, seventh-century BCE Spartan lyric, contains Therapnean religious contexts, but no extant line securely narrates a Menelaus cult. Claims that Alcman directly attests a “Menelaeia” require caution [E1/uncertain].
- **Sappho 16**, late seventh/early sixth century BCE, makes Helen’s abandonment of husband, child, and parents an illustration of desire; Menelaus is implicit rather than named [E1].
- **Pindar**, early fifth century BCE, alludes to Helen, the Atreids, and Trojan-war traditions but provides no continuous Menelaus biography [E2].
- **Simonides, lyric fragments**, fifth century BCE: Trojan-war allusions; fragmentary [E2].

#### Tragedy and comedy

- **Aeschylus, *Agamemnon***, 458 BCE: Menelaus is the injured husband and joint commander, but the surviving play concentrates on Agamemnon; a storm separates the brothers after Troy [E2].
- **Sophocles, *Ajax***, probably 440s BCE: Menelaus forbids burial of Ajax and is answered by Teucer; he appears as a severe coalition commander [E2].
- **Sophocles, lost *Hermione* and other fragments**: marriage dispute involving Hermione, Orestes, and Neoptolemus [E2 fragment].
- **Euripides, *Andromache***, probably 420s BCE: hostile anti-Spartan representation; Menelaus supports Hermione against Andromache and is humiliated by Peleus [E2].
- **Euripides, *Trojan Women***, 415 BCE: Menelaus intends to take Helen home for execution; Hecuba prosecutes and Helen defends herself [E2; S8].
- **Euripides, *Helen***, 412 BCE: the real Helen has remained chaste in Egypt while a divine phantom went to Troy; shipwrecked Menelaus discovers her and escapes with her [E2; S9].
- **Euripides, *Orestes***, 408 BCE: Menelaus refuses decisive aid to Orestes; Apollo finally ordains marriages and Helen’s apotheosis [E2].
- **Euripides, *Iphigenia at Aulis***, produced posthumously c.405 BCE: Menelaus first insists on the expedition and sacrifice, then recoils, while Agamemnon reverses in the opposite direction [E2].
- **Aristophanes, *Lysistrata***, 411 BCE: comic reference to Menelaus dropping his sword before Helen’s exposed beauty, showing that the recovery image was widely recognizable [E2].

#### Historians, orators, and mythographers

- **Herodotus 2.112–120**, c.430s BCE: Egyptian priests tell him that the real Helen and Menelaus’ wealth remained with the Egyptian king Proteus; after Troy’s fall Menelaus retrieves them, then sacrilegiously sacrifices two Egyptian children to obtain favorable winds [E2]. This is the dossier’s clearest human-sacrifice allegation. Herodotus reports it as Egyptian tradition, not established history [S10].
- **Isocrates, *Helen* 61–64**, c.370s BCE: Helen grants Menelaus immortality; Spartans at Therapne sacrifice to both as gods [E2; S11].
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica***, third century BCE: Menelaus is not an Argonaut in the canonical expedition, but his ancestry and the later Trojan generation enter genealogical comparisons [E3].
- **Apollodoran *Library* and *Epitome***, probably first–second century CE: systematic synthesis of genealogy, wooing, oath, abduction, war, sack, returns, children, and competing Helen traditions [E3; S12].
- **Diodorus Siculus**, first century BCE: rationalizing and synthetic Trojan genealogies [E3].
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae***, Latin, probably second century CE in surviving form: compressed lists of suitors, Horse occupants, killings, and genealogies [E3].
- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 2 and 6**, 29–19 BCE: Menelaus is peripheral to the Trojan-centered sack; Aeneas’ aborted desire to punish Helen reworks material elsewhere attached to Menelaus [E3].
- **Ovid, *Heroides* 5, 16–17; *Metamorphoses* 12–13**, c.20 BCE–8 CE: Paris/Helen correspondence and Romanized Trojan-war narration [E3].
- **Strabo**, Augustan/Tiberian era: Canobus, Menelaus’ pilot, gives his name to Canopus after dying in Egypt [E3].
- **Pausanias**, second century CE: temple and alleged joint tomb at Therapne; the Chest of Cypselus showed Menelaus approaching Helen with a sword; other regional memories [E3/E4; S13].
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica***, probably third century CE: full late-antique narration of Troy’s final battles and Menelaus’ recovery of Helen under Aphrodite’s restraint [E3].
- **Ptolemy Chennus/Hephaestion**, first–second century CE, preserved by Photius: Menelaus and Helen instead reach the Taurians and are sacrificed by Iphigenia to Artemis [E3, paradoxographic variant].
- **Scholia to Homer, Euripides, and Lycophron**, compiled over centuries: preserve otherwise lost genealogies, children, cult notices, and local variants [E3].
- **Dictys Cretensis**, Greek original probably first–second century CE, surviving chiefly through a fourth-century Latin version: euhemerized Greek “eyewitness” chronicle [E5].
- **Dares Phrygius**, Latin text probably fifth–sixth century CE: pseudo-eyewitness Trojan account stripped of active Olympian machinery [E5].
- **John Malalas and Byzantine chronicles**, sixth century onward: chronographic and often physicalized portraits of Trojan heroes [E5].
- **Tzetzes**, twelfth century: learned Byzantine compilation preserving scholia and variants [E5].

The Homeric manuscripts are mostly medieval; papyri from the Hellenistic and Roman periods show that the poems circulated with textual variation. The Cyclic poems survive only through quotations and prose summaries.

## 3. The Story

### Ancestry, exile, and accession

The Homeric poems call Menelaus an Atreid and brother of Agamemnon but do not narrate his childhood. Later mythography makes Atreus and Aerope his parents; the rival Hesiodic/scholiastic construction inserts Pleisthenes as biological father. After Atreus is killed or displaced by Thyestes and Aegisthus, Menelaus and Agamemnon flee, in varying accounts to Sicyon, Aetolia, or the Spartan court of Tyndareus. These exile details are **[E3]** systematization.

Menelaus becomes king through marriage to Helen, Tyndareus’ daughter or stepdaughter. Homer assumes but does not narrate the succession. The idea that Menelaus ruled a Mycenaean state with its capital at historical Sparta is a poetic geography, not a recovered Bronze Age constitutional fact.

### Wooing and oath

In the Hesiodic *Catalogue* and *Cypria* tradition, Helen attracts suitors from across Greece. Later sources credit Odysseus with advising Tyndareus to require every suitor to swear to defend the chosen husband against injury. Tyndareus then selects Menelaus, or Helen chooses him, or Menelaus wins through Agamemnon’s gifts and standing. These alternatives should remain separate:

- Choice by Tyndareus: mythographic norm [E3].
- Choice by Helen: later literary emphasis [E3].
- Victory through wealth or Agamemnon’s advocacy: Hesiodic/cyclic reconstruction [E1 fragment].
- Oath invented by Odysseus in return for help winning Penelope: Apollodoran synthesis [E3].

The oath is not narrated in the extant Homeric epics; treating it as “Homer’s oath” is a common modern error.

### Marriage and children

Homer’s *Odyssey* gives Helen and Menelaus one daughter, **Hermione**, and gives Menelaus a son **Megapenthes** by an unnamed enslaved woman. Later sources name the mother as Pieris or Tereis. Variants add:

- **Nicostratus**, son of Helen according to some Hesiodic/mythographic evidence, but son of an enslaved woman in Pausanias.
- **Aethiolas**, worshipped with Nicostratus according to an Iliadic scholion.
- **Maraphius**, a genealogical ancestor of a Persian clan in Ariaethus.
- **Pleistolaus** or other poorly attested sons in late genealogies.

Hermione’s marriage is also contradictory. In *Odyssey* 4 Menelaus is sending her to Neoptolemus. Euripides’ *Andromache* makes her Neoptolemus’ jealous wife and Orestes’ eventual partner. Apollodorus reports the attempted reconciliation that Tyndareus had already promised her to Orestes while Menelaus, unaware or overriding him, promised her to Neoptolemus [S14].

### Paris’ visit and Helen’s departure

In the *Cypria* and Apollodorus, Paris/Alexander is entertained at Sparta for nine days. Menelaus leaves for Crete to attend Catreus’ funeral and instructs Helen to care for the guest. Paris then leaves with her and substantial household wealth. Sources differ on agency:

- Abduction or rape is emphasized by Greek speakers and later summaries.
- Helen’s erotic choice under Aphrodite’s compulsion appears in lyric and epic traditions.
- Sappho treats her as abandoning husband and child under desire.
- Herodotus’ Egyptians treat Paris as a criminal violator of hospitality but say Proteus confiscated Helen and the stolen wealth.
- Euripides’ *Helen* says Hermes removed the real Helen to Egypt and Hera manufactured a cloud-image.
- Stesichorus’ *Palinode* is the earliest surviving witness to the proposition that Helen did not go to Troy, although the exact location of the real Helen in his poem is uncertain.

“Kidnapping” and “elopement” are therefore interpretive choices within an ancient dispute, not interchangeable facts.

### Muster and embassy

Menelaus appeals to Agamemnon, who invokes the suitors’ oath in the cyclic/mythographic tradition. Menelaus and Odysseus then go as ambassadors to Troy seeking Helen and the property. *Iliad* 3 has Antenor remember this embassy: Menelaus speaks briefly and clearly, Odysseus with a more overwhelming rhetorical style. Antimachus, bribed by Paris according to *Iliad* 11, had urged the Trojans to kill the envoys. The embassy fails.

Menelaus’ motive remains recovery of wife, property, and honor; Agamemnon commands the coalition. Interpretations that reduce the expedition entirely to Menelaus’ sexual jealousy are modern simplifications.

### The war

The Catalogue of Ships gives Menelaus sixty vessels from Lacedaemon, Pharis, Sparta, Messe, Bryseae, Augeiae, Amyclae, Helos, Laas, and Oetylus (*Iliad* 2.581–590). Agamemnon commands separately.

#### Duel with Paris

In *Iliad* 3 Paris proposes single combat for Helen and her possessions. Menelaus accepts and overcomes him: his spear pierces Paris’ shield and armor, his sword breaks on Paris’ helmet, and he drags him by the helmet strap. Aphrodite snaps the strap and removes Paris in mist to his bedroom. The result is therefore:

- **Human contest:** Menelaus wins decisively.
- **Narrative outcome:** no settlement, because Aphrodite saves Paris.
- **Treaty:** broken when Athena, executing Zeus’ plan, induces Pandarus to shoot Menelaus in Book 4.

The arrow only wounds Menelaus because Athena deflects it from a fatal path. Machaon treats him. Agamemnon’s fear and lament reveal the political and fraternal consequences of a possible death.

#### Battlefield record

Menelaus kills or helps kill, among others:

- **Scamandrius** (*Iliad* 5.49–58).
- **Pylaemenes**, though Book 13 later appears to place Pylaemenes alive at his son’s funeral; either two namesakes or a compositional inconsistency (*Iliad* 5.576–579; 13.658–659).
- **Dolops** (*Iliad* 15.525–545).
- **Peisander** after savage close combat (*Iliad* 13.601–619).
- **Euphorbus**, who had first wounded Patroclus (*Iliad* 17.1–60).
- **Podes**, Hector’s companion (*Iliad* 17.575–581).
- **Thoas** and several minor warriors in contested or shared kill lists.

He wounds **Helenus** through the hand (*Iliad* 13.581–600). Homeric duplicated names make numerical “kill counts” uncertain [S15].

Menelaus’ fullest martial episode is Book 17. He guards Patroclus’ corpse, kills Euphorbus, summons Ajax, and carries the body with Meriones while the Ajaxes defend them. He is brave but repeatedly recognizes when heavier fighters are needed.

At Patroclus’ funeral games, Antilochus blocks him dangerously during the chariot race. Menelaus demands an oath, accepts Antilochus’ apology, and voluntarily yields the disputed prize—a characteristic Homeric display of anger moderated through public procedure.

### The Wooden Horse and Troy’s capture

*Odyssey* 4 supplies two memories:

1. Helen walks around the Wooden Horse imitating the voices of the hidden warriors’ wives; Menelaus and Diomedes nearly answer, but Odysseus restrains them.
2. Menelaus praises Odysseus’ conduct inside the Horse.

The Cyclic and later lists count Menelaus among the hidden warriors. During the sack, the *Iliou Persis* summary says Menelaus kills **Deiphobus**, Helen’s Trojan husband after Paris’ death, and leads Helen to the ships. Other accounts make Odysseus assist him; in the *Odyssey*, Helen claims she helped the Greeks, whereas Menelaus’ Horse story casts suspicion on her.

### Recovery of Helen

The traditions diverge:

- *Iliou Persis*: Menelaus kills Deiphobus and recovers Helen [E1 fragment].
- Archaic Chest of Cypselus, known from Pausanias: Menelaus advances toward Helen with a sword [E1 object described by E3 witness].
- Fifth-century vase painting: he pursues her, then drops his sword, sometimes under Aphrodite or Eros’ influence [E2; S16–S18].
- Euripides, *Andromache* 627–631: Peleus mocks him for failing to kill Helen after seeing her breast [E2].
- *Trojan Women*: Menelaus still professes an intention to execute Helen after returning to Greece; the ending leaves the threat unresolved [E2].
- Quintus Smyrnaeus: Aphrodite directly restrains his anger [E3].
- Euripides’ *Helen*: there is no guilty Helen at Troy; Menelaus reunites with the chaste original in Egypt [E2].

The familiar tableau in which Helen deliberately bares her breasts and Menelaus drops his sword is ancient, but no single surviving early text supplies every detail seen in modern retellings.

### Wanderings and Proteus

After the sack, Athena’s anger over Ajax’s assault on Cassandra divides the returning Greeks. Menelaus separates from Agamemnon and wanders for eight years. *Odyssey* 3–4 names Cyprus, Phoenicia, Egypt, Ethiopians, Sidonians, Erembians, and Libya. These places form an epic geography, not a historically documented itinerary.

At Pharos, adverse gods detain him until provisions fail. **Eidothea**, daughter of the Old Man of the Sea, instructs him and three companions to hide beneath sealskins and seize **Proteus** at noon. They hold the shape-changing god until he resumes his form. Proteus directs Menelaus to return to the Egyptian river and sacrifice to the gods; he reports the fates of Ajax, Agamemnon, and Odysseus. Menelaus performs hecatombs, erects a mound for Agamemnon, and returns home.

Herodotus radically recasts Egypt: Proteus is a righteous mortal Egyptian king, not a sea god. Menelaus retrieves the real Helen and his treasure from him, but then seizes and sacrifices two local boys to obtain favorable winds before fleeing Egyptian pursuit. That atrocity is **[E2 regional Egyptian testimony]**, absent from Homer [S10].

### Return and household

Menelaus reaches home on the day Orestes buries Clytemnestra and Aegisthus. In *Odyssey* 4, Telemachus and Peisistratus find him celebrating two marriages: Hermione’s departure to Neoptolemus and Megapenthes’ marriage to Alector’s daughter. His palace is dazzlingly rich. Helen administers an Egyptian drug, *nepenthes*, and both hosts tell morally incompatible Trojan memories without open rupture.

### Fate and death

There is no single death tradition:

1. **Translation to Elysium:** Proteus says Menelaus will not die in horse-pasturing Argos; the gods will convey him to Elysium because he is Helen’s husband and Zeus’ son-in-law (*Odyssey* 4.561–569) [E1; S6].
2. **Immortality granted through Helen:** Isocrates says Helen made him immortal and her eternal companion [E2].
3. **Burial with Helen at Therapne:** Pausanias reports a temple and local claim that both were buried there [E3 reporting E4].
4. **Sacrifice in Tauris:** Ptolemy Chennus says Iphigenia sacrificed Menelaus and Helen to Artemis [E3, sensational variant].
5. **No narrated ordinary death:** the canonical tradition otherwise leaves his end unstated.

Elysian translation and a cult tomb need not be harmonized; Greek traditions routinely located tombs for figures also said to have become immortal.

| Mother | Aerope | Tragedy and mythography [E2/E3] |
| Brother | Agamemnon | Universal early tradition [E1] |

### Documented interactions: gods and named heroes

The list records narratively direct dealings or explicit joint action. Mere kinship without a scene is marked as such.

#### Divine beings

- **Aphrodite** — Rescues Paris from Menelaus’ grip; later traditions and images make her or Eros disarm Menelaus before Helen. *Iliad* 3; fifth-century vases; Quintus 13. [E1/E2/E3].
- **Athena** — Supports Menelaus generally; deflects Pandarus’ arrow from a fatal course but also engineers the treaty-breaking shot under Zeus’ plan. *Iliad* 4. [E1].
- **Hera** — Named with Athena as Menelaus’ divine supporter; opposes Paris and Troy. *Iliad* 4. [E1].
- **Zeus** — Oversees the duel and broken truce; Menelaus is “Zeus-nurtured” and Zeus’ son-in-law; Zeus’ family connection explains his Elysian fate. *Iliad* 3–4; *Odyssey* 4. [E1].
- **Ares** — No sustained personal encounter; Menelaus is repeatedly “dear to Ares/Ares-like.” *Iliad*. [E1, formulaic].
- **Hermes** — In Euripides’ phantom tradition Hermes transports the real Helen to Egypt; Menelaus later recovers her. *Helen* 44–48. [E2, indirect interaction].
- **Proteus, sea god** — Menelaus captures and interrogates him at Pharos; Proteus gives ritual instructions and prophecy. *Odyssey* 4.351–570. [E1].
- **Eidothea** — Pities and guides Menelaus, provides sealskins and the plan to seize Proteus. *Odyssey* 4.363–446. [E1].
- **Thetis** — Menelaus participates in Patroclus’ funeral events shaped by Thetis’ aid to Achilles, but no direct exchange is preserved. [Absence of direct interaction].
- **Eris** — Her apple ultimately causes the judgment leading to Helen’s removal and Menelaus’ war; no face-to-face scene. *Cypria* summary. [E1 fragment, causal only].
- **Artemis** — In Ptolemy Chennus’ variant Iphigenia sacrifices Menelaus and Helen to Artemis in Tauris. [E3].
- **Apollo** — Protects Trojan interests; no secure personal dialogue with Menelaus. Helen’s refuge at an altar of Apollo occurs in some recovery iconography. [E2 visual/indirect].
- **Poseidon** — The returning fleet is affected by divine sea hostility, but Homer gives Menelaus no major personal confrontation with Poseidon. [E1, indirect].
- **Rhadamanthys** — Already dwells in the Elysian region promised to Menelaus; no encounter is narrated. *Odyssey* 4.563–568. [E1, prospective].
- **Eros** — Appears above Menelaus in Classical recovery scenes, causing or symbolizing the dropped sword. Vase painting, fifth century BCE. [E2 visual].

#### Family and Greek allies

- **Helen** — Husband and wife; separated by Paris, recovered after Troy, reunited in Sparta or Egypt, jointly worshipped at Therapne. Homer onward. [E1–E4].
- **Agamemnon** — Brother and coalition commander; musters forces with him, fears for him after Pandarus’ shot, separates from him during the return. *Iliad* 2–4; *Odyssey* 3–4; *Nostoi*. [E1].
- **Tyndareus** — Gives or permits Helen in marriage and, in later sources, administers the suitors’ oath. Hesiodic/cyclic fragments; Apollodorus. [E1 fragment/E3].
- **Castor and Polydeuces** — Helen’s brothers and Menelaus’ in-laws; no surviving early direct encounter. *Iliad* 3 notes their absence/death. [E1, kinship only].
- **Odysseus** — Fellow suitor and ally; later devises the oath; accompanies Menelaus on the embassy and into Deiphobus’ house; restrains men inside the Horse; his fate is revealed by Proteus. *Iliad* 3; *Odyssey* 4; Cycle; Apollodorus. [E1/E3].
- **Nestor** — Advises the coalition; Menelaus visits him while mustering and Telemachus travels from Nestor to Menelaus. *Cypria*; *Odyssey* 3–4. [E1].
- **Diomedes** — Fellow warrior; sits with Menelaus in the Horse and nearly responds to Helen’s imitation. *Odyssey* 4. [E1].
- **Ajax the Greater** — Menelaus summons him to defend Patroclus’ corpse; they fight jointly. *Iliad* 17. [E1].
- **Ajax the Lesser** — Fellow Greek whose assault on Cassandra helps provoke Athena’s wrath and the disastrous returns; no major direct Menelaus scene. *Iliou Persis/Nostoi*. [E1 fragment].
- **Patroclus** — Menelaus guards and helps recover his corpse and competes at his funeral games. *Iliad* 17, 23. [E1].
- **Achilles** — Coalition ally; Menelaus attends Patroclus’ games and later traditions place him in the Horse after Achilles’ death. *Iliad* 23; Cycle. [E1].
- **Antilochus** — Competes unfairly against Menelaus in the chariot race, apologizes, and is forgiven. *Iliad* 23. [E1].
- **Meriones** — Helps Menelaus carry Patroclus’ corpse. *Iliad* 17. [E1].
- **Idomeneus** — Fellow commander; Menelaus leaves for Crete in traditions about Catreus, but no developed personal myth survives. *Iliad*; *Cypria*. [E1].
- **Calchas** — Coalition seer; connected to the sacrifice and departure traditions but no major individual exchange with Menelaus survives. *Cypria*; *Iphigenia at Aulis*. [E1 fragment/E2].
- **Iphigenia** — Menelaus initially insists upon, then opposes, her sacrifice at Aulis; in the Taurian variant she later sacrifices him. Euripides, *Iphigenia at Aulis*; Ptolemy Chennus. [E2/E3].
- **Orestes** — Menelaus returns as Orestes buries the usurpers; in Euripides refuses him effective help; disputes Hermione’s marriage. *Odyssey* 4; *Orestes*; *Andromache*. [E1/E2].
- **Electra** — Menelaus is her uncle; in *Orestes* he declines to rescue her household. [E2].
- **Hermione** — Daughter whom he promises to Neoptolemus; Euripides makes him her protector against Andromache. *Odyssey* 4; *Andromache*. [E1/E2].
- **Neoptolemus** — Receives Hermione from Menelaus; Horse companion and fellow sack participant. *Odyssey* 4; Cycle; Euripides. [E1/E2].
- **Peleus** — Publicly humiliates and expels Menelaus in *Andromache*, condemning his conduct toward Helen and Andromache. [E2].
- **Andromache** — Menelaus threatens her and her child while supporting Hermione’s plot; Peleus intervenes. Euripides, *Andromache*. [E2].
- **Telemachus** — Guest at Sparta; Menelaus tells him about Odysseus and offers gifts. *Odyssey* 4, 15. [E1].
- **Peisistratus** — Accompanies Telemachus and is entertained by Menelaus. *Odyssey* 4, 15. [E1].
- **Machaon** — Treats Menelaus’ arrow wound. *Iliad* 4. [E1].
- **Teucer** — Opposes Menelaus’ ban on Ajax’s burial in Sophocles’ *Ajax*. [E2].

#### Trojans and opponents

- **Paris/Alexander** — Violates Menelaus’ hospitality, takes Helen and treasure, loses their duel but is rescued by Aphrodite. Homer; Cycle. [E1].
- **Priam** — Recognizes Menelaus from Helen’s account and sanctions the duel; no direct conversation between them survives. *Iliad* 3. [E1].
- **Hector** — Menelaus contemplates answering Hector’s challenge but is restrained; later they contest Patroclus’ body. *Iliad* 7, 17. [E1].
- **Antenor** — Hosts or recalls Menelaus and Odysseus’ embassy and compares their speaking styles. *Iliad* 3. [E1].
- **Antimachus** — Urges killing the envoys after accepting Paris’ gold. *Iliad* 11. [E1, remembered event].
- **Pandarus** — Shoots and wounds Menelaus, breaking the truce. *Iliad* 4. [E1].
- **Helenus** — Menelaus wounds his bow hand. *Iliad* 13. [E1].
- **Euphorbus** — Menelaus kills him over Patroclus’ body. *Iliad* 17. [E1].
- **Deiphobus** — Menelaus kills or mutilates Helen’s later Trojan husband during the sack. *Iliou Persis*; *Odyssey* 8 indirectly; later epic. [E1 fragment/E3].

- **Theoclymenus** — Egyptian king who tries to marry Helen and whom Menelaus deceives in Euripides’ *Helen*. [E2].
- **Theonoe** — Conceals Menelaus’ presence and enables the couple’s escape. Euripides, *Helen*. [E2].
- **Proteus, Egyptian king** — In Herodotus, safeguards Helen and restores her to Menelaus. [E2 regional rationalization].

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

### The Menelaion at Therapne

The sanctuary occupies a ridge east of the Eurotas, roughly five kilometers from modern Sparta, above substantial Middle and Late Helladic remains. Excavation identified several Bronze Age “mansions,” major construction in LH IIB–IIIA, and abandonment or destruction by the end of LH IIIB. There is no demonstrable continuous use down to the Archaic cult [S3–S5].

The cult sequence is approximately:

1. Later eighth century BCE: renewed deposition and possible altar/enclosure.
2. Seventh century BCE: substantial votive activity; pottery, terracottas, bronzes, lead figures.
3. Sixth century BCE: architectural rebuilding and named Helen dedications.
4. Early fifth century BCE: monumental naiskos/platform and direct dedication to Menelaus.
5. Classical–Hellenistic periods: continued cult and literary testimony.
6. Roman period: Pausanias still knows the temple and tomb tradition.

Offerings include lead warriors, armed men, women, animals, winged figures, terracotta figurines, and bronzes. Warrior votives suit Menelaus, but without inscriptions individual pieces cannot securely be assigned to him rather than Helen or the pair.

### Nature of the cult

- The inscriptions establish cult of Helen and Menelaus, not the identities of the Bronze Age occupants.
- The earliest named dedication privileges Helen. Older scholarship sometimes proposed an originally independent goddess Helen to whom Menelaus was later attached; that remains **[E6]**, not a demonstrated sequence.
- Isocrates says sacrifices were made to both “as gods”; Pausanias calls the structure a temple and reports their tomb. Modern “hero cult” is therefore a useful category but does not reproduce every ancient classification.
- Claims that men prayed to Menelaus for courage while women asked Helen for beauty derive from late lexicographic and antiquarian reconstruction, not a securely preserved ritual calendar.
- A festival name **Menelaeia/Menelaia** is reported in lexicographic tradition, but its ceremonies and date are not recoverable.
- Polybius 5.18, describing events in 218 BCE, uses **Menelaion** as a recognized topographic name.

### Tomb and relics

Pausanias 3.19.9 reports: at Therapne “there is a temple of Menelaus; they say that Menelaus and Helen were buried here.” Archaeology has not identified human remains that can be linked to either figure. “Tomb of Menelaus” is therefore **[E3 literary/local tradition]**, not an excavated authenticated burial.

No securely documented ancient relic cult of Menelaus’ bones parallels the politically transferred bones of Orestes. No oracle of Menelaus is securely attested.

### Political meaning

The shrine gave Dorian Sparta a ritual relationship with the pre-Dorian/Achaean royal couple of epic. Scholars variously interpret this as:

- incorporation of epic into a developing Spartan civic identity;
- appropriation of conspicuous Bronze Age remains as a heroic past;
- legitimation of Spartan territorial power through an Achaean predecessor;
- a cult centered first on Helen, subsequently represented as a divine royal couple.

All are **[E6]** historical interpretations. None proves that an organized program founded the sanctuary for a known Bronze Age king.

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### c.700 BCE: epic and local cult

The earliest recoverable Menelaus is already dual: the Panhellenic epic husband-warrior and the locally worshipped Spartan royal dead. Homer portrays him neither as coward nor tyrant. He is less formidable than Achilles, Ajax, or Diomedes but consistently brave, courteous, rhetorically concise, emotionally attached to Agamemnon, and unusually willing to accept reconciliation.

### Sixth century BCE: cyclic completion and visual myth

The *Cypria*, *Iliou Persis*, and *Nostoi* supplied what the Homeric epics leave outside their plots: wooing, oath, abduction, sack, Helen’s recovery, and returns. Vase painters made his duel with Paris and recovery of Helen legible scenes. The earliest recovery images can show forcible seizure or armed confrontation; fifth-century images increasingly emphasize the dropped sword.

### Fifth century BCE: contested ethics

Athenian drama fractures the character:

- Sophocles makes him an authoritarian general in *Ajax*.
- Euripides’ *Andromache* makes the Spartan king treacherous and ineffective.
- *Trojan Women* makes him Helen’s would-be executioner.
- *Helen* reverses the adultery story and turns him into a shipwrecked recognition-play hero.
- *Iphigenia at Aulis* dramatizes his oscillation over human sacrifice.
- Aristophanes reduces the recovery to erotic comedy.

It is possible that Athenian-Spartan hostility sharpened Euripidean caricature, especially during the Peloponnesian War, but direct one-to-one political allegory is **[E6]**, not an ancient statement.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Mythographers regularized genealogy, suitors, children, and Horse lists. Roman poets shifted sympathy toward Trojan ancestors and made Menelaus less central. Isocrates’ divinized pair coexisted with Pausanias’ tomb-bearing cult heroes. Herodotus’ Egyptian rationalization and Euripides’ phantom remained influential alternatives.

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Dictys and Dares recast divine epic as military history, presenting purported eyewitness records. Their euhemerized Menelaus is a human commander rather than a man visibly aided or obstructed by gods. Byzantine compilers preserved otherwise lost details but also multiplied reconciliation and genealogical variants.

### Medieval western tradition

Because complete Homer was unavailable in Latin western Europe, Dares and Dictys, then Benoît de Sainte-Maure’s *Roman de Troie* (c.1155–1165), Guido delle Colonne’s *Historia destructionis Troiae* (1287), and vernacular adaptations dominated. Menelaus became a feudal monarch and chivalric claimant. This is not survival of an independent folk biography but literary refashioning through pseudo-historical chronicles [S19–S21].

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Recovery of Greek Homer restored the epic Menelaus, but Trojan material continued through medieval and Virgilian lenses. Shakespeare’s *Troilus and Cressida* (c.1601–1602) turns “cuckolded Menelaus” into a repeated joke. Offenbach’s *La belle Hélène* (1864) makes Ménélas a comic bourgeois husband; this enormously influential characterization is **[E7]**, not Homeric.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Screen versions commonly convert Menelaus into one of three types:

1. noble wronged husband;
2. comic cuckold;
3. brutal possessive antagonist whose death permits Paris and Helen’s romance.

The third type dominates Wolfgang Petersen’s *Troy* (2004), where Hector kills Menelaus during the duel—an explicit modern invention contrary to every principal ancient return tradition. Modern games, comics, and novels frequently import the militarized stereotype of historical Classical Sparta into Menelaus’ much earlier epic kingdom; no ancient source depicts him within the later Spartan agoge or hoplite constitution.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- Shakespeare, *Troilus and Cressida*, c.1601–1602: comic sexual humiliation [E7].
- Goethe, *Faust II*, 1832: Helen’s classical afterlife centers more on Faust and Paris than Menelaus, who functions as returning husband and threat [E7].
- Offenbach, Meilhac, and Halévy, *La belle Hélène*, 1864: operatic caricature of an obtuse cuckolded king [E7].
- Jean Giraudoux, *La guerre de Troie n’aura pas lieu*, 1935: diplomatic Menelaus participates in an antiwar reconfiguration [E7].
- Modern Helen-centered novels frequently make him abusive to enable a consensual Paris–Helen romance. That characterization is a legitimate adaptation but not a securely Homeric trait.

### Visual art

The strongest ancient visual sequence is Helen’s recovery:

- black-figure confrontation, c.550 BCE;
- Attic red-figure pursuit and dropped sword, c.490–430 BCE;
- Douris’ cup, c.485–480 BCE, showing the duel with Paris under divine observation [S22];
- British Museum amphora 1837,0609.71: Menelaus visibly drops his sword [S17];
- Pausanias’ description of the seventh/sixth-century Chest of Cypselus: armed approach to Helen.

Renaissance and Baroque art usually centers Helen’s abduction rather than Menelaus’ subjectivity; examples include Giuseppe Salviati’s *Abduction of Helen* and Maerten van Heemskerck’s 1535 panoramic treatment [S23–S24].

The “Pasquino” sculptural type traditionally labeled **Menelaus supporting the body of Patroclus** is a Roman marble group after a Hellenistic composition. The identification is conventional rather than inscriptionally secure; Ajax is another candidate. Calling every copy “Menelaus and Patroclus” overstates certainty.

### Film and television

Representative portrayals include:

- *The Private Life of Helen of Troy* (1927): Lewis Stone.
- *Helen of Troy* (1956): Niall MacGinnis.
- *The Trojan Women* (1971): Patrick Magee, adapting Euripides.
- *Helen of Troy* television miniseries (2003): James Callis.
- *Troy* (2004): Brendan Gleeson; abusive, politically useful husband killed by Hector—**modern invention**.
- *Troy: Fall of a City* (2018): Jonas Armstrong; another strongly antagonistic reworking.

### Political and nationalist appropriation

Spartan and Homeric imagery has been used in Greek state heritage, tourism, education, and nationalist discourse. Menelaus, however, is rarely an independently important modern nationalist or extremist emblem; Leonidas and the “300” dominate those uses. No well-documented Menelaus-specific extremist cult or political movement emerged in the dedicated search. Claims that modern extremists systematically venerate Menelaus should therefore be labeled **absence of evidence**, not silently inferred from generic Spartan symbolism.

### Psychological and comparative interpretation

- Freudian and post-Freudian readings often treat the duel, cuckoldry, and dropped sword as castration anxiety or the defeat of aggression by desire; these are twentieth-century interpretations [E7].
- Structuralist readings place Menelaus in exchanges of women, violated hospitality, and reciprocal alliance.
- Rank and Raglan’s hero-pattern schemes fit Menelaus poorly: he lacks a securely narrated miraculous birth, exposure, victory over a father, and singular ritual death.
- Campbell’s monomyth is likewise a poor documentary map; Menelaus’ return resembles a wandering-initiation sequence but no ancient text presents it as a universal “hero’s journey.”
- Dumézilian Indo-European trifunctional readings have not produced a widely accepted Menelaus interpretation.
- Nagy’s work illuminates epic competition, heroic *kleos*, and cult, but Menelaus is not simply “the best of the Achaeans.” Stelow’s description “not quite the best” better captures the poem’s deliberately intermediate warrior.

### Commercial and scientific name use

The blue morpho butterfly **Morpho menelaus**, named by Linnaeus in 1758, is a scientific commemoration, not ancient symbolism. “Menelaus” also names astronomical objects and ships. Menelaus’ theorem belongs to the Alexandrian mathematician Menelaus, not the hero; merging them is a modern category error.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was there a historical Menelaus?

No inscription, administrative tablet, contemporary foreign record, or securely identified grave attests the epic individual. Late Bronze Age elite buildings at Therapne show that the region supported significant centers, not that one belonged to Menelaus. Homeric geography may preserve inherited place knowledge, but using it to reconstruct an individual biography exceeds the evidence.

The responsible conclusions are:

- A Bronze Age ruler or remembered dynasty may lie behind some Lacedaemonian epic traditions: **possible, unverified [E6]**.
- Menelaus himself has been historically identified: **false**.
- The Menelaion proves continuous worship from his lifetime: **false**; the excavated occupation gap argues against demonstrated continuity.
- Later worshippers associated impressive prehistoric remains with Menelaus and Helen: **strong inference**, supported by the shrine’s placement and inscriptions.

### Epic versus cult

Older “epic created cult” and “cult created epic” models are too simple. The Menelaion’s eighth–seventh-century chronology coincides with the formation and dissemination of monumental epic. The available evidence cannot determine whether worshippers first identified an existing local power with epic Helen and Menelaus, whether poetry stimulated the cult, or whether both developed through reciprocal exchange.

Farnell’s model often treated heroes as epic names attached to older graves. Later archaeology, including work by Antonaccio, Ekroth, and others, stresses that tomb cult, ancestor cult, and named hero cult must not automatically be equated. An offering beside a prehistoric building is not by itself an offering to a known epic hero.

### Was Helen primary?

The earliest named offerings address Helen; Menelaus’ first unequivocal direct dedication is later. This supports, but does not prove, a cult initially centered on Helen. The theory that Helen was an old vegetation, tree, solar, or initiatory goddess and Menelaus a secondary husband is influential but composite and disputed. Isocrates’ fourth-century testimony treats them as a divine pair; Pausanias’ second-century testimony gives Menelaus the temple’s name while burying both there.

### Oral-formulaic scholarship

Milman Parry and Albert Lord demonstrated that Homeric epithets operate metrically and formulaically. Menelaus’ “fair-haired,” “good at the war cry,” and Ares-derived epithets cannot be treated as independent eyewitness descriptors. More recent work asks how formulas acquire contextual force: Menelaus’ exceptionally warlike vocabulary contrasts meaningfully with his subordinate rank and repeated need for assistance.

### Phantom Helen

The sequence is:

1. Stesichorus, sixth century BCE: earliest attested retraction denying Helen’s Trojan guilt.
2. Herodotus, fifth century BCE: real Helen detained in Egypt; Troy possesses neither woman nor wealth; no phantom is required.
3. Euripides, *Helen*, 412 BCE: Hermes brings real Helen to Egypt; Hera makes a cloud phantom for Paris.
4. Apollodorus and later commentators synthesize variants.

The claim that “Herodotus invented the phantom” is wrong: his rationalized Egyptian narrative has the real Helen, while the phantom belongs to the Stesichorean/Euripidean line.

### Image and text

The dropped-sword motif appears in vase painting before its clearest surviving literary formulations. Scholars debate whether painters illustrated lost poetry, poets borrowed a visual convention, or both drew on performance traditions. The evidence does not permit a single direction of borrowing. Museum descriptions that present the breast exposure as the one canonical account simplify a variable iconographic field [S16–S18].

### State of the evidence

- Homer: complete poems, but preserved through a long manuscript and editorial history.
- Epic Cycle: tiny verbatim fragments plus late summaries.
- Hesiodic catalogue: fragmentary papyri and quotations.
- Tragedy: several complete plays plus many lost works known only by fragments.
- Menelaion: securely excavated architecture and votives, but early excavation records and disturbed deposits limit contextual precision.
- Iconography: numerous vases, but unlabeled pursuit scenes cannot always be identified securely.
- Ritual: inscriptions name recipients, while liturgical procedure is poorly preserved.
- “Palaces of Menelaus” at Therapne or Pellana: modern labels, not ancient ownership records.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Menelaus appears in Linear B”: unsupported.
- “Archaeologists found Menelaus’ palace/tomb”: unsupported identification.
- “He was a historical king of Classical Sparta”: chronological conflation.
- “Helen certainly chose Paris freely” or “was certainly raped”: the sources disagree.
- “Paris defeated Menelaus”: contradicted by *Iliad* 3; Aphrodite extracts Paris after Menelaus wins.
- “Menelaus died in the duel”: modern cinema.
- “Homer tells the oath of Tyndareus or the Wooden Horse’s construction”: neither episode is narrated there; the *Odyssey* presupposes the Horse.
- “The red-haired Menelaus is Homerically exact”: *xanthos* does not establish that narrow modern color category.
- “The Menelaion demonstrates uninterrupted Bronze Age cult”: contradicted by the excavated gap.
