# Perseus: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** divine-born mortal hero (*hērōs*), Argive dynastic ancestor and founder-king; after death, a locally worshipped hero with attested sanctuaries but no securely identified body or tomb. “Demigod” is a useful modern shorthand for a mortal son of Zeus, not his normal ancient cult-title.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Homer, *Iliad* 14.319–320, conventionally dated to the later eighth or early seventh century BCE: Zeus recalls Danaë and “Perseus, pre-eminent among all men.” This establishes divine paternity and fame, but none of the Gorgon, Andromeda, or Seriphos narrative. The earliest surviving narrative image is the Proto-Attic Eleusis amphora, approximately 675–650 BCE, showing Athena, Perseus, the decapitated Medusa, and the pursuing Gorgons.  
> **Historical status:** no contemporary Bronze Age record, securely deciphered Linear B occurrence, grave, body, or independently verifiable human original is known. Perseus is historically observable as a figure in Iron Age poetry, art, genealogy, local religion, and political discourse—not as a documented Bronze Age king.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Homeric, archaic poetic, visual, or epigraphic evidence, ordinarily before approximately 480 BCE.
- **[CE] Classical elaboration:** fifth- and fourth-century BCE literature, art, or religious testimony.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a tradition localized to a particular city, island, sanctuary, or ethnic genealogy.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Hellenistic or Roman mythography organizing older, sometimes incompatible material.
- **[SR] Syncretistic or interpretative identification:** equation with another god, hero, people, or religious figure.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion/reception.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation, not direct ancient testimony.
- **[MI] Modern invention or deliberate modern adaptation.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** something often claimed for which the relevant early evidence is absent.

Dates are dates of composition or production unless otherwise stated. Most archaic works survive in medieval manuscripts or quotations centuries later; therefore “early” describes the reconstructed work, not the physical witness.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Perseus is the Argive hero whom the developed tradition makes the son of Zeus and Danaë, grandson of Acrisius, survivor of attempted infanticide by exposure at sea, killer of the mortal Gorgon Medusa, rescuer and husband of Andromeda, involuntary killer of Acrisius, and founder or fortifier of Mycenae, Midea, and Tiryns. Through his sons he becomes ancestor of the Perseid kings and of Heracles. These elements did not enter the record together. Homer knows only the divine birth and extraordinary fame; archaic art and the pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield* securely add Medusa and the escape; Pindar supplies the golden conception, Danaë’s coerced sexual servitude under Polydectes, Medusa’s death, and vengeance on Seriphos; the most seamless surviving itinerary is the much later *Bibliotheca*.

### Name and forms

- **Ancient Greek:** Περσεύς, *Perseús*; genitive Περσέως, epic Περσῆος. **[EA]** Homer, *Iliad* 14.320.
- **Patronymic:** Περσηϊάδης/*Persēïadēs*, “son/descendant of Perseus,” used especially of Heracles; *Iliad* 19.116, 123. **[EA]**
- **Dynastic plural:** Περσεῖδαι/*Perseidai*, “Perseids,” the Argive-Mycenaean lineage. **[CE/LS]**
- **Latin:** *Perseus*, inherited directly from Greek. **[LS]**
- **Modern Greek:** Περσέας, *Perséas*; learned and astronomical Περσεύς, conventionally pronounced *Perséfs*. **[MA/modern linguistic continuation]**
- **English:** Perseus; Italian *Perseo*; French *Persée*; Spanish *Perseo*; German *Perseus*. **[MA]**

### Etymology

The name’s origin is uncertain. Comparison with Greek πέρθω, *pérthō*, “sack, ravage, destroy,” permits an agent interpretation “destroyer/sacker,” advanced in older etymological scholarship and accepted cautiously by Frisk. The formation is not demonstrably transparent, however, and a pre-Greek personal name remains possible. “Perseus means destroyer” should therefore be labeled **[MSR: disputed]**, not translated as an ancient fact. Standard lexical summaries correctly mark the origin as unknown or uncertain.

The resemblance between Περσεύς and Πέρσαι, “Persians,” produced an ancient genealogical etymology: Perseus and Andromeda begot Perses, ancestor and namesake of the Persians. Herodotus 7.61 records it, while 7.150 reports a story that Xerxes invoked this Argive kinship to encourage Argive neutrality in 480 BCE. This is **[CE/RV: political ethnogony]**, not Iranian etymology. Old Persian *Pārsa* is independent of the Greek hero.

### Descriptors and status terms

Ancient texts more often identify Perseus by genealogy than by fixed cult epithet:

- “son of Danaë” and “son of Zeus”: Homer, *Iliad* 14.319–20; Pindar, *Pythian* 12. **[EA]**
- “most distinguished/famous of all men”: Homer, *Iliad* 14.320. **[EA]**
- “horseman Perseus”: pseudo-Hesiod, *Shield* 216. **[EA]**
- “Gorgon-slayer” is a descriptive modern and ancient-derived designation, but not a stable Homeric formula. **[NEG]**
- “Founder of Mycenae” belongs to Argive local history recorded by Pausanias, not Homer. **[RV/LS]**
- “Constellation Perseus” belongs to Hellenistic catasterism and astronomical tradition. **[LS]**

### Identifications

No securely attested Greek source identifies Perseus outright with another Greek hero. Later eastern civic traditions, especially in Cilicia and Asia Minor, associated or syncretized an ancestral Perseus with local foundation figures and, in some scholarship, with Mithras on coinage; individual cases remain contested and must not be generalized into “Perseus was Mithras.” **[SR/MSR]**

Medieval astronomical manuscripts transmitted Perseus as a constellation figure, sometimes altering his classical dress and weaponry, but did not turn him into a Christian saint. **[NEG/MA]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual and visual corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **Homer, *Iliad* 14.319–20; Greek; late eighth/early seventh century BCE.** **[EA]** Zeus names Danaë, daughter of Acrisius, and their son Perseus. Nothing is said about imprisonment, golden rain, the chest, Medusa, Andromeda, Seriphos, or Mycenae. The physical manuscripts are medieval, but the poem’s archaic linguistic and compositional history is independently established.

2. **Proto-Attic Eleusis amphora, approximately 675–650 BCE, Eleusis Archaeological Museum 2630.** **[EA: visual]** The lower register shows Athena interposed as Perseus escapes the Gorgon sisters, with Medusa’s headless body behind. Damage leaves part of Perseus incomplete, but the scene is securely identified. It proves that the Gorgon expedition and Athena’s aid were established by the seventh century BCE. It does not prove the later Graeae, reflective shield, or Polydectes framework. The vessel was reused for a child’s burial, but that context does not make its Perseus scene a hero cult.

3. **Thermon terracotta metope, approximately 630–600 BCE.** **[EA: visual]** Perseus runs carrying Medusa’s head; it demonstrates the story’s place in monumental temple decoration.

4. **Pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* 216–37; Greek; probably sixth century BCE.** **[EA]** A representation of Perseus appears on Heracles’ shield: he flies or runs without touching the ground, wears winged sandals and Hades’ cap, carries a sword and the Gorgon’s head in a silver *kibisis*, and is pursued by Stheno and Euryale. Athena, Hermes, Graeae, Polydectes, and the reflected gaze are absent from this passage.

5. **Corinthian black-figure amphora, Berlin F 1652, approximately 575–550 BCE.** **[EA: visual]** Perseus and Andromeda jointly attack the sea creature with stones. This is the earliest secure surviving Andromeda image and differs materially from later versions in which she hangs helplessly while Perseus fights alone.

### Principal literary corpus

- **Hesiod, *Theogony* 270–86; Greek; approximately 700 BCE.** **[EA]** Genealogy of the Graeae and Gorgons; Medusa alone is mortal; Poseidon lies with her; Pegasus and Chrysaor spring out when Perseus cuts off her head. It does not narrate why Perseus came or how he succeeded.
- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fragments 129–35 Most/23a Merkelbach–West and testimonia; Greek; probably sixth century BCE in its monumental form.** **[EA, fragmentary]** Danaë’s union with Zeus, exposure in the chest, and the Perseid genealogy were included, but reconstruction depends on papyri and later citations.
- **Pherecydes of Athens, *Histories/Genealogies* fr. 10–12 Fowler; Greek; early fifth century BCE.** **[CE, fragmentary]** An important early prose synthesis behind later accounts: Polydectes’ contrivance, Graeae, nymphs, equipment, and the Medusa expedition. Survives only through later writers and scholia.
- **Pindar, *Pythian* 10 and 12; Greek; 498 and 490 BCE.** **[EA]** *Pythian* 12 connects Athena’s invention of the aulos mode to the Gorgons’ lament, mentions Medusa’s “fair cheek,” Danaë’s conception by self-flowing gold, Polydectes’ forced bed, and Perseus’ lethal return. It is the earliest securely dated text for several of those motifs.
- **Aeschylus, lost *Phorcides*, *Polydectes*, and satyr-play *Dictyulci/Net-Haulers*; Greek; first half of fifth century BCE.** **[CE, fragments]** *Net-Haulers* dramatized Dictys finding the chest and the infant; *Phorcides* treated the Graeae/Gorgon expedition; *Polydectes* probably the return and vengeance. Their arrangement as a connected tetralogy is plausible but not absolutely proved.
- **Sophocles, lost *Acrisius*, *Danaë*, *Larisaioi*, and *Andromeda*; Greek; fifth century BCE.** **[CE, fragments/testimonia]** Birth, exposure, Acrisius’ death, and the Ethiopian rescue were dramatized. Whether *Acrisius* and *Danaë* were alternative titles or separate plays remains disputed.
- **Euripides, lost *Danaë*, *Dictys* (431 BCE), and *Andromeda* (412 BCE); Greek.** **[CE, fragments]** *Andromeda* was particularly influential: the heroine chained and lamenting, Echo answering her, Perseus’ aerial entrance and sudden love, and likely the conflict over marriage. Aristophanes parodied the play in *Thesmophoriazusae* 1015–69.
- **Herodotus, *Histories* 2.91, 6.53–54, 7.61, 7.150; Greek; approximately 440–420 BCE.** **[CE/RV]** Perseus’ Egyptian shrine and games at Chemmis; Heraclid genealogy; Perses as Persian ancestor; alleged diplomatic use by Xerxes.
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1.747–51, 4.1091 and scholia; Greek; third century BCE.** **[LS]** Perseid and Andromeda material appears mainly as genealogy, art-description, and geographical allusion rather than a continuous Perseus narrative.
- **Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterisms* 15–17, 22, 36; Greek; Hellenistic material in a later epitome.** **[LS]** Cepheus, Cassiopeia, Andromeda, Perseus, and Cetus are placed among the stars.
- **Conon, *Narratives* 40, epitomized by Photius; Greek; first century BCE composition, ninth-century CE epitome.** **[LS: rationalization]** Cetus becomes the name of a Phoenician ship; Perseus abducts/rescues Andromeda amid human conflict rather than killing a biological monster.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4.604–5.249; Latin; completed approximately 8 CE.** **[LS: Roman poetic elaboration]** Atlas’ petrifaction, the aerial sighting and rescue of Andromeda, battle with Phineus’ faction, mirror-like shield, sleeping Medusa, and Medusa’s transformation after Poseidon’s sexual assault in Minerva’s temple.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.2.1–2.4.5; Greek; usually first or second century CE.** **[LS]** The fullest surviving Greek synthesis: oracle, bronze chamber, chest, Dictys, Polydectes’ banquet ruse, Graeae, nymphs’ gifts, divine guidance, decapitation, Andromeda, Phineus, Seriphos, Acrisius, kingdom exchange, and descendants.
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 63–64, 151, 244; *Astronomica* 2.9–12; Latin; material compiled in the early Roman imperial period, surviving in late manuscripts.** **[LS]** Compressed variants and constellation lore; *Fabula* 244 uniquely lists Megapenthes as Perseus’ killer.
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 2.15–25, 3.17.3, 5.18.5, 10.10.5; Greek; approximately 150–175 CE.** **[RV/LS]** Argive foundations, local etymologies, the shrine between Mycenae and Argos, honors in Seriphos and Athens, the battle with Dionysus’ followers, and surviving representations.
- **Lucian, Philostratus, Achilles Tatius, later scholia, and Tzetzes; Greek; second–twelfth centuries CE.** **[LS/MA]** Rhetorical descriptions, geographical lore, rationalizations, and preservation of otherwise lost variants.
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 25 and 47; Greek; fifth century CE.** **[LS: late-antique expansion]** Expands Perseus’ armed resistance to Dionysus at Argos and the petrifaction of Ariadne in an extravagantly learned epic setting.

### Archaeological and iconographic corpus

LIMC VII, “Perseus,” catalogues hundreds of Greek, Etruscan, and Roman objects. Major stages include:

- seventh-century flight/pursuit compositions;
- archaic and early classical decapitations, often with Athena and Hermes;
- the earliest Andromeda rescue, where Andromeda participates;
- fifth-century shifts from a grotesque frontal Gorgon to a sleeping or beautiful Medusa;
- fourth-century South Italian scenes influenced by Euripidean theater, including Andromeda bound to posts or rock;
- Hellenistic and Roman mosaics, gems, sarcophagi, frescoes, and silver showing the rescue, wedding, or triumphant head;
- coins of Seriphos, Argos, Tarsus, and other eastern cities using Perseus or the gorgoneion as civic imagery.

Iconography is independent evidence for what viewers could recognize; it is not automatically an illustration of a surviving text.

## 3. The story and its variants

### 3.1 Argive ancestry and the oracle

Acrisius and Proetus, sons of Abas, contend for Argos. In the developed genealogy, Acrisius fathers Danaë, while an oracle says Danaë’s son will kill him. He confines her in a bronze underground chamber. Zeus enters as golden rain, and Perseus is conceived.

- **Earliest secure core:** Zeus, Danaë, Acrisius, and Perseus are linked in *Iliad* 14.319–20. **[EA]**
- **Golden rain:** Pindar, *Pythian* 12.17–18 (490 BCE), the first securely dated explicit text. **[EA]**
- **Bronze chamber and oracle:** Pherecydes fragments and later *Bibliotheca* 2.4.1. **[CE/LS]**
- **Alternative mortal father:** later mythographic traditions sometimes name Proetus as Danaë’s seducer and explain his quarrel with Acrisius thereby. **[LS: minority variant]**
- **Sexual language:** Zeus’ access is a divine impregnation, not a courtship in the evidence. Modern children’s retellings often euphemize it. Ancient texts do not preserve Danaë’s consent. **[EA/modern reception distinction]**

### 3.2 The chest and attempted infanticide

Acrisius, unwilling to kill Zeus’ child directly, encloses mother and infant in a chest (*larnax*) and casts it into the sea. They reach Seriphos, where the fisherman Dictys hauls them ashore.

- The chest appears in the Hesiodic *Catalogue* and Aeschylus’ *Net-Haulers*. **[EA/CE]**
- Aeschylus preserves Danaë’s terror at sea and Dictys’ discovery; these fragments are earlier than the complete prose account.  
- *Bibliotheca* 2.4.1 supplies the coherent later sequence. **[LS]**
- This is attempted murder of a woman and infant by exposure. “A voyage in a box” is a sanitized modern formulation.

### 3.3 Polydectes and the impossible gift

Polydectes, king of Seriphos and Dictys’ brother, desires Danaë. To remove her adult son, he announces or pretends a marriage with Hippodamia and demands horses as gifts. Perseus, lacking a horse, rashly promises Medusa’s head. Polydectes holds him to it.

- Pindar says Danaë endured Polydectes’ “forced bridal bed” or coerced union and that Perseus exacted repayment. **[EA]**
- Pherecydes and *Bibliotheca* frame the expedition as a banquet-gift trap. **[CE/LS]**
- In *Bibliotheca*, Danaë and Dictys later take refuge at an altar from Polydectes’ violence. Pindar’s harsher language may imply completed sexual coercion rather than merely attempted marriage. These should not be harmonized.

### 3.4 The Graeae and the nymphs’ equipment

Athena and Hermes guide Perseus to the Graeae—Enyo, Pemphredo, and, in some lists, Deino—who share one eye and one tooth. Perseus intercepts the eye and compels them to reveal the nymphs’ location. The nymphs provide the *kibisis* or Gorgon-bag, winged sandals, and Hades’ cap of invisibility; Hermes supplies an adamantine sickle or curved sword.

- The Graeae exist in Hesiod’s genealogy, but the eye-theft episode is first recoverable through Pherecydes and later prose. **[CE/LS]**
- The pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield* already knows winged sandals, cap, sword, and bag, proving that the equipment is archaic even though its donors are not named there. **[EA]**
- In *Bibliotheca*, Athena and Hermes are guides; the nymphs own the sandals, cap, and bag. **[LS]**
- In later art and retelling, Athena often gives a polished shield. The famous claim that Perseus watched the entire killing “in Athena’s mirror-shield” is explicit in Ovid’s Roman account and related later tradition, not in Homer or the *Shield*. **[LS]**

### 3.5 Medusa beheaded

Perseus finds the Gorgons asleep. Looking at Medusa through a reflective shield, or turning away under Athena’s direction, he cuts off her head. Pegasus and Chrysaor spring from her severed neck; Stheno and Euryale pursue, but Hades’ cap conceals him.

- Hesiod, *Theogony* 276–86: Medusa alone is mortal; Poseidon lies with her in a meadow; Pegasus and Chrysaor emerge when Perseus cuts her head off. **[EA]**
- *Shield* 216–37: escape, bag, cap, winged sandals, and pursuit. **[EA]**
- Eleusis amphora: Athena’s support and pursuit. **[EA: visual]**
- Pindar: Athena hears and converts Euryale’s lament into flute music; Medusa is “fair-cheeked.” **[EA]**
- *Bibliotheca*: Athena guides Perseus’ hand as he looks away at a bronze shield; Medusa was targeted because she was mortal. **[LS]**
- Ovid: Medusa had once been a beautiful woman whose hair Minerva transformed after Neptune sexually violated—or, in older translations, “attained” or “ravished”—her in Minerva’s temple. **[LS: Roman innovation or unattested antecedent]** No extant archaic Greek text makes Athena transform a formerly human Medusa for temple sex. Presenting that as “the original Greek myth” is **[MI: false antiquity]**.
- In no ancient canonical account does Perseus negotiate with Medusa, recruit her against the gods, or discover that she was falsely maligned and spare her. Those are modern revisions. **[MI]**

The decapitation is an ambush of a sleeping mortal female monster in several versions, aided by gods and magical equipment. Ancient narrative celebrates it; modern retellings increasingly interrogate its gendered violence.

### 3.6 Uses of the severed head

The head remains lethal:

- Perseus petrifies Atlas after refusal of hospitality: Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4.627–62. **[LS: Roman poetic elaboration]** This conflicts chronologically with traditions in which Atlas later encounters Heracles; ancient mythographers did not maintain a single universal chronology.
- Drops of blood falling in Libya generate snakes: Apollonius, *Argonautica* 4.1505–17, elaborated by Ovid. **[LS]**
- Coral forms when seaweed hardens on contact with the head: Ovid 4.740–52. **[LS]**
- Phineus and his armed supporters are petrified: Ovid 5.1–235; *Bibliotheca* 2.4.3 gives Agenor, not Phineus, as the rival in some manuscript traditions. **[LS: conflicting rival]**
- Polydectes and his court are petrified: Pindar already knows lethal vengeance; the staged assembly and display appear in later mythography. **[EA core/LS scene]**
- Perseus returns the equipment and gives the head to Athena, who fixes it to her shield or breastplate: *Bibliotheca* 2.4.3; artistic gorgoneia are much earlier than this surviving etiological account. **[LS]**

### 3.7 Andromeda and the sea creature

In Aethiopia, Cassiopeia boasts that she or Andromeda surpasses the Nereids. Poseidon floods the land and sends a *kētos*, a sea creature. An oracle requires Andromeda’s exposure. Perseus sees her, bargains with Cepheus for marriage, kills the creature, and claims her.

- Berlin F 1652, 575–550 BCE, is the earliest secure evidence: Andromeda stands free and supplies stones while Perseus attacks the monster. **[EA: substantially different from later passive-victim imagery]**
- Sophocles’ and Euripides’ lost *Andromeda* plays shaped the fifth-century scene. Euripides’ heroine is bound and lamenting; Perseus falls in love on seeing her. **[CE]**
- *Bibliotheca* 2.4.3: Cassiopeia’s boast, Nereids’ anger, Poseidon’s monster, oracle, exposure, and marriage bargain. **[LS]**
- Ovid 4.663–752: Perseus initially mistakes her for marble, negotiates with her parents, kills the creature with sword strokes, and sacrifices to Jupiter, Minerva, and Mercury. **[LS]**
- Conon 40 rationalizes “Cetus” into a ship carrying the Phoenician suitor; the episode becomes conflict among human abductors. **[LS: euhemerism]**
- Joppa/Jaffa claimed the location and displayed Andromeda’s chains and the monster’s bones: Strabo 16.2.28, Josephus, *Jewish War* 3.420, and Pausanias 4.35.9. **[RV: local relic tradition]**
- “Kraken” is Scandinavian vocabulary popularized for Perseus by the 1981 film *Clash of the Titans*. No Greek or Roman source calls Andromeda’s monster a Kraken. **[MI]**

### 3.8 Wedding conflict

Andromeda had an earlier claimant:

- *Bibliotheca* names Agenor in its surviving text; other traditions name Phineus, brother of Cepheus.
- Ovid’s long Book 5 battle makes Phineus attack the wedding feast. Perseus kills numerous named opponents conventionally, then petrifies the survivors.
- This large martial catalogue is Roman poetic expansion, not securely archaic narrative. **[LS]**

### 3.9 Return to Seriphos

Perseus finds Danaë and Dictys at an altar. He summons Polydectes and his supporters, displays Medusa’s head, and turns them to stone; Dictys becomes king.

- Pindar’s “deadly wedding gift” supplies the early core. **[EA]**
- *Bibliotheca* provides altar refuge, public assembly, petrifaction, and Dictys’ installation. **[LS]**
- Seriphos’ rocky landscape was later explained as the petrified inhabitants. **[RV: aetiology]**
- Perseus does not massacre every Seriphian in all versions; Pindar’s compressed “death to sea-girt Seriphos and its people” and later selective court scene are not identical.

### 3.10 Acrisius killed by the discus

Perseus returns toward Argos. Acrisius flees the oracle’s fulfillment. At funeral games at Larisa in Thessaly, Perseus throws a discus; wind or accident carries it into Acrisius, killing him.

- The episode was dramatized in Sophocles’ *Larisaioi*. **[CE, fragmentary]**
- *Bibliotheca* 2.4.4 provides the fullest surviving account. **[LS]**
- In some accounts the object strikes Acrisius’ foot; in others his head. **[LS variants]**
- Perseus’ ignorance does not prevent fulfillment of the oracle. Ancient emphasis is on inescapable fate, not legal innocence.
- Acrisius’ grave was variously placed outside Larisa and within Athena’s sanctuary on the Larisa acropolis. **[RV]**

### 3.11 Kingdom exchange and foundation of Mycenae

Ashamed to inherit through accidental killing, Perseus exchanges Argos for Tiryns with Megapenthes, son of Proetus. He fortifies Tiryns, Midea, and Mycenae, or founds Mycenae outright.

- *Bibliotheca* 2.4.4: kingdom exchange and fortification. **[LS]**
- Pausanias 2.16.2–4: foundation of Mycenae and rival name-aetiologies. Perseus drops the cap or pommel (*mykēs*) of his sword, or pulls a mushroom (*mykēs*) and discovers the spring Persea. Pausanias also knows the alternative that Mycene, daughter of Inachus, gave the city its name. **[RV: mutually competing local explanations]**
- Cyclopes build the Argive walls in Pausanias and related traditions. **[RV/LS]**
- Archaeology establishes Mycenae as a major Bronze Age center centuries before the earliest attestation of Perseus. It does not establish Perseus as its historical builder. **[NEG]**

### 3.12 Dionysus at Argos

Pausanias 2.20.4 and 2.22.1 records graves of women who followed Dionysus and were killed when Perseus fought the god’s force at Argos. A tomb or memorial of Chorea was shown. Nonnus later greatly expands the conflict and has Hera encourage resistance; Hermes eventually mediates, and Ariadne is petrified in some late versions.

This is an Argive local and late-antique branch, not part of the familiar Medusa-Andromeda cycle. **[RV/LS]**

### 3.13 Death

Most surviving narratives end with Perseus reigning and establishing a dynasty; they give no death scene.

- Hyginus, *Fabula* 244, lists Megapenthes killing Perseus in revenge for Proetus’ death. **[LS: isolated and chronologically troublesome variant]**
- No secure ancient source says Perseus died peacefully “at a ripe old age”; that is a later harmonizing retelling. **[MI/modern literary smoothing]**
- No securely excavated “tomb of Perseus” is known. Pausanias reports a roadside hero-shrine, not an identified burial containing his remains. **[NEG]**
- Catasterism places his image among the stars without necessarily describing bodily apotheosis. **[LS]**

### Documented interactions: one line per named divine being or hero

“Interaction” here requires an event, direct assistance/opposition, marriage, killing, or explicit genealogical relation—not merely appearance in a modern chart.

- **Zeus** — impregnates Danaë in golden form/rain and fathers Perseus; *Iliad* 14.319–20, Pindar *Pythian* 12; **[EA]**.
- **Danaë** — mother; shares the chest and Seriphian exile; Perseus later protects or avenges her against Polydectes; Hesiodic *Catalogue*, Aeschylus, Pindar, *Bibliotheca*; **[EA–LS]**.
- **Acrisius** — maternal grandfather; exposes infant Perseus and is later accidentally killed by his discus; Sophocles’ *Larisaioi*, *Bibliotheca* 2.4.4; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Proetus** — great-uncle and, in a minority variant, Danaë’s mortal seducer; his son exchanges kingdoms with Perseus; later mythography; **[LS]**.
- **Dictys** — rescues Danaë and Perseus in his net, shelters them, and receives Seriphos’ kingship; Aeschylus’ *Net-Haulers*, *Bibliotheca*; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Polydectes** — coerces Danaë, sends Perseus after Medusa, and is killed or petrified on his return; Pindar *Pythian* 12, Pherecydes, *Bibliotheca*; **[EA–LS]**.
- **Hippodamia** — named as the bride whose supposed wedding generates Polydectes’ gift demand; Pherecydes/*Bibliotheca*; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Athena/Pallas** — protects Perseus in early art, guides the decapitation, receives Medusa’s head, and uses the Gorgons’ lament to invent an aulos mode; Eleusis amphora, Pindar, *Bibliotheca*; **[EA–LS]**.
- **Hermes/Mercury** — guides Perseus and supplies or facilitates the curved blade; Pherecydes/*Bibliotheca*; Perseus sacrifices to Mercury after Andromeda’s rescue in Ovid; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Hades/Pluto** — owner or source of the invisibility cap worn by Perseus; pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield*, later mythography; **[EA/LS]**.
- **Hephaestus** — fashions the image of Perseus on Heracles’ shield; pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield* 216–37. This is interaction at the level of represented divine craftsmanship, not an episode of Perseus’ life; **[EA]**.
- **Enyo (Graea)** — eye taken or intercepted to force directions to the nymphs; Pherecydes/*Bibliotheca*; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Pemphredo (Graea)** — same interaction; Pherecydes/*Bibliotheca*; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Deino (Graea)** — third Graea in the expanded list; compelled with her sisters; later mythography; **[LS]**.
- **The unnamed guardian nymphs** — give the *kibisis*, winged sandals, and cap; Pherecydes/*Bibliotheca*; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Medusa** — Perseus decapitates her while she sleeps or while looking away; her head becomes his weapon; Hesiod, archaic art, Pindar, *Bibliotheca*, Ovid; **[EA–LS]**.
- **Stheno** — pursues Perseus after Medusa’s death; pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield*, Pindar; **[EA]**.
- **Euryale** — pursues and laments Medusa; Athena imitates her cry in music; *Shield*, Pindar *Pythian* 12; **[EA]**.
- **Poseidon/Neptune** — no early direct meeting with Perseus; he fathers Pegasus and Chrysaor through Medusa and later sends the sea monster against Cepheus’ land; Hesiod, *Bibliotheca*, Ovid; **[EA/LS, indirect]**.
- **Pegasus** — springs from Medusa’s severed neck at Perseus’ stroke; Hesiod *Theogony*; **[EA]**. Perseus does **not** ride Pegasus in the standard early literary account; winged sandals provide his flight. Pegasus-riding Perseus is a visual or modern conflation with Bellerophon, though isolated images invite debate.
- **Chrysaor** — born from Medusa’s neck when Perseus decapitates her; Hesiod; **[EA]**.
- **Andromeda** — Perseus fights beside or rescues her, marries her, and fathers the Perseids; Berlin F 1652, Euripides, Herodotus, *Bibliotheca*, Ovid; **[EA–LS]**.
- **Cepheus** — promises Andromeda to Perseus in return for rescue; becomes his father-in-law; Herodotus, *Bibliotheca*, Ovid; **[CE/LS]**.
- **Cassiopeia** — her boast precipitates Andromeda’s exposure; Perseus becomes her son-in-law; *Bibliotheca*, constellation tradition; **[LS]**.
- **The Nereids** — offended by Cassiopeia’s comparison and demand punishment; *Bibliotheca* and later tradition; **[LS]**.
- **Ammon/Zeus Ammon** — his oracle orders or confirms Andromeda’s sacrifice in Ovid’s version; *Metamorphoses* 4.670–71; **[LS]**.
- **Cetus/the sea creature** — Perseus kills it, in early art with Andromeda’s active help; Berlin F 1652, Euripidean tradition, *Bibliotheca*, Ovid; **[EA–LS]**.
- **Phineus** — rival claimant who attacks the wedding and is petrified; Ovid *Metamorphoses* 5; **[LS]**.
- **Agenor** — alternative rival claimant whom Perseus defeats; *Bibliotheca* 2.4.3; **[LS: variant]**.
- **Atlas** — refuses Perseus hospitality and is transformed into a mountain by Medusa’s head; Ovid 4.627–62; **[LS: Roman elaboration]**.
- **Themis** — her prophecy to Atlas motivates his refusal of Perseus; Ovid 4.642–47; **[LS, indirect]**.
- **Dionysus** — leads or is associated with forces opposed by Perseus at Argos; Pausanias 2.20.4, 2.22.1; expanded by Nonnus; **[RV/LS]**.
- **Chorea** — maenad or Dionysian follower killed in Perseus’ victory; her tomb was shown at Argos; Pausanias 2.20.4; **[RV]**.
- **Ariadne** — petrified by Perseus during the Dionysian conflict in late tradition; Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 47; **[LS: late antique]**.
- **Hera** — encourages Perseus’ opposition to Dionysus in Nonnus; **[LS: late antique]**.
- **Megapenthes** — exchanges Argos for Tiryns with Perseus; in Hyginus alone, later kills him; Pausanias/*Bibliotheca* versus Hyginus *Fabula* 244; **[LS: conflicting versions]**.
- **Heracles** — descendant of Perseus through both Amphitryon’s and Alcmena’s lines; called Perseid in Homer; no surviving early narrative stages a meeting because they belong to different generations; *Iliad* 19.116–24, Herodotus 6.53–54; **[EA/NEG for direct interaction]**.
- **Bellerophon** — no ancient text securely makes him Perseus’ companion; the modern association chiefly comes from confusing Pegasus’ birth in the Perseus cycle with Bellerophon’s later mastery of the horse; **[NEG/MI]**.
- **Calibos** — invented antagonist of *Clash of the Titans* (1981), not an ancient hero encountered by Perseus; **[MI]**.
- **Bubo** — mechanical owl invented for *Clash of the Titans* (1981); no ancient source; **[MI]**.
- **Hades as principal enemy of Perseus** — premise of *Clash of the Titans* (2010), not an ancient episode; **[MI]**.

## 5. Cult, shrine, tomb, and ritual

### Mycenae–Argos

Pausanias 2.18.1 reports a *hērōon* of Perseus on the left of the road from Mycenae to Argos and says local people honored him. This is explicit second-century CE testimony for hero cult. Pausanias does not call it Perseus’ tomb and reports no bones. **[RV: literary cult testimony]**

A damaged Mycenaean inscription, generally placed around the late sixth or early fifth century BCE, has been restored to refer to “hieromnemones of Perseus.” The restoration would make it the earliest epigraphic evidence for an organized cult personnel, but the stone is fragmentary and the reading must remain **[EA/MSR: probable, not certain]**.

The fountain Persea at ruined Mycenae and the city’s foundation stories attached the hero to the landscape. Neither proves cult continuity from the Bronze Age. Archaeologically observed Iron Age activity at Mycenaean tombs is evidence for later engagement with ancient remains; assigning every deposit to “Perseus worship” would exceed the evidence.

### Seriphos

Pausanias says Perseus received his “greatest honors” on Seriphos and among the Athenians. The island’s Perseus traditions explained its stones and connected places with Dictys’ net and the petrified court. Coins and civic imagery reinforce the hero’s local importance. Details of a named annual festival or securely excavated Perseus temple are much less certain than general cult testimony. **[RV]**

### Athens

The grammar of Pausanias 2.18.1 has generated confusion in translations, but he reports a sacred precinct of Perseus and an altar of Dictys and Clymene, called Perseus’ saviors, in the discussion of Athenian honors. This is literary evidence for a cultic grouping, not an excavated tomb. **[RV]**

### Chemmis in Egypt

Herodotus 2.91 says the Egyptian city Chemmis possessed a rectangular sanctuary of Perseus and held Greek-style games when the hero appeared; inhabitants claimed Perseus was native to their city and had visited before killing Medusa. Herodotus reports local testimony, not proof that an archaic Greek cult founded the site. The account is a fifth-century example of cross-cultural localization and interpretatio. **[CE/RV/SR]**

### Joppa

Hellenistic and Roman writers localized Andromeda’s chains and the monster’s bones at Joppa. These were displayed relic traditions and tourist geography, not a tomb or securely attributable biological remains. **[RV/LS]**

### Cilicia and the East

Tarsus and other Cilician cities claimed Perseus as founder; imperial-period coins show him, often carrying the harpe or gorgoneion. These claims participated in civic competition, Hellenization, and imperial self-representation. Proposed identifications with local gods or Mithras are scholarly interpretations of particular monuments, not a universal ancient equation. **[RV/SR/MSR]**

### Tomb and body

No ancient source securely locates Perseus’ corpse, and no archaeological burial has been identified as his. The Mycenae–Argos *hērōon* is not automatically a grave. Hyginus’ murder notice supplies a death but no burial. **[NEG]**

### Sacrifice and games

- Local “honors” imply ritual but Pausanias does not specify the sacrificial animal, calendar, or exact procedure.
- Herodotus explicitly mentions games at Chemmis.
- Modern reconstruction of Perseus’ cult from general Greek hero-cult patterns—blood offerings, nighttime rites, meals at tombs—cannot substitute for Perseus-specific testimony. Ekroth’s work cautions that heroic sacrifice was more varied than the old opposition “Olympian altar versus chthonic pit” suggests. **[MSR]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### Phase I: archaic emergence, approximately 750–500 BCE

Homer supplies divine birth and fame. Hesiodic genealogy anchors Medusa among primordial monsters. Seventh-century art already knows Athena’s aid, decapitation, and pursuit. By the sixth century, the *Shield* supplies the magical equipment, the *Catalogue* Danaë’s exposure and descendants, and vase painting links Perseus to Andromeda. Pindar joins these motifs to Polydectes and the golden conception. There is no surviving archaic continuous “life of Perseus.” **[EA]**

### Phase II: classical dramatization, approximately 500–323 BCE

Pherecydes organizes prose genealogy and adventure. Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides dramatize nearly the entire cycle in separate lost works. Euripides turns Andromeda’s exposure into an emotionally elaborate rescue-and-love plot; vase painting subsequently favors theatrical binding structures and romantic encounter. Herodotus embeds Perseus in Egyptian ethnography, Spartan/Argive genealogy, Persian origins, and wartime diplomacy. **[CE]**

### Phase III: Hellenistic cataloguing and celestial system, 323–31 BCE

Alexandrian scholarship coordinates genealogies, star myths, local claims, and poetic allusion. Perseus and the Ethiopian royal family become a constellation group. Rationalizers such as Conon translate monsters and miracles into ships, abductions, and political events. **[LS]**

### Phase IV: Roman synthesis, first century BCE–fourth century CE

Ovid creates the most influential continuous literary spectacle: Atlas, airborne romance, elaborate monster fight, wedding massacre, sleeping Medusa, reflective shield, and Medusa’s prior beauty and violation. The *Bibliotheca* supplies the standard handbook narrative. Hyginus compresses stories for mythographic and astronomical use. Pausanias documents regional cult and topography. Roman frescoes, mosaics, gems, and sarcophagi favor the erotic rescue and victorious head. **[LS/RV]**

### Phase V: late antiquity and Byzantium

Nonnus expands the Dionysian conflict. Scholiasts, epitomes, astronomical manuscripts, and Tzetzes preserve fragments and harmonize variants. Christian authors sometimes treat Perseus as pagan fiction, euhemerized history, or an astronomical figure; there is no stable Christian cult of him. **[LS/MA]**

### Phase VI: medieval astronomical survival

Arabic and Latin astronomical traditions transmit the constellation, usually through Ptolemaic star catalogues and illustrated manuscripts. The star Algol—β Persei, the “demon” star in Arabic nomenclature—becomes associated with the Gorgon’s head. These astronomical continuities should not be mistaken for continuous hero worship. **[MA]**

### Phase VII: Renaissance and early modern political art

Ovidian translations, mythographic handbooks, opera, painting, and sculpture revive the full adventure. Benvenuto Cellini’s bronze *Perseus with the Head of Medusa*, commissioned by Cosimo I de’ Medici and executed 1545–1554, stands in Florence’s Loggia dei Lanzi. Its placement opposite republican and Medicean political monuments made the triumphant decapitation a manifesto of ducal control: the Uffizi explicitly interprets Medusa’s snakes as the factional discord subdued by Cosimo. **[MA: political appropriation]**

### Phase VIII: nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Comparative mythology, psychoanalysis, folktale classification, and “hero-pattern” theory detach the tale from its local sources and treat it as a universal plot. Painters and sculptors increasingly aestheticize Medusa or place sympathy with her. Cinema fuses Perseus with Bellerophon, Scandinavian monster terminology, and newly invented villains. **[MSR/MI]**

### Phase IX: contemporary revision

Recent novels, comics, games, sculpture, and feminist reception frequently recenter Medusa as a survivor of sexual violence and Perseus as agent of patriarchal power. This is a conscious ethical reinterpretation built especially from Ovid, not recovery of a suppressed complete archaic narrative. Conversely, popular “Medusa was Athena’s consensually empowered priestess” accounts often lack any ancient source. **[MI]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theater

- **Giambattista Marino, *La Galeria* and baroque treatments:** poetic engagement with the visual triumph of Perseus. **[MA]**
- **Pierre Corneille, *Andromède* (1650):** machine-play combining classical tragedy, spectacle, and opera-like music. **[MA]**
- **Pedro Calderón de la Barca, *Fortunas de Andrómeda y Perseo* (1653):** court spectacle. **[MA]**
- **Sri Aurobindo, *Perseus the Deliverer* (written 1906–07; published later):** deploys the liberating hero within an anticolonial and nationalist intellectual context. Its heroic masculinity has been read as part of Indian nationalist discourse. **[modern political reception]**
- **Rick Riordan, *Percy Jackson* series (from 2005):** names Percy after Perseus and recasts Greek divine descent in young-adult fantasy; the character is not the ancient Perseus. **[MI]**
- **Natalie Haynes, *Stone Blind* (2022):** makes Medusa and the women around the myth central and treats heroic entitlement critically. **[MI/revision]**

### Visual art

- **Cellini, *Perseus with the Head of Medusa*, 1545–1554:** bronze, Loggia dei Lanzi; political image of Medicean victory and one of the decisive modern formulations of the triumphant nude hero.
- **Titian, *Perseus and Andromeda*, approximately 1554–1556:** Ovidian rescue emphasizing erotic exposure and aerial motion.
- **Antonio Canova, *Perseus Triumphant*, 1797–1801:** neoclassical assimilation of Perseus to an Apollo-like ideal.
- **Edward Burne-Jones, *The Perseus Series*, begun 1875:** extensive Pre-Raphaelite cycle, especially influential in modern fantasy imagery.
- **Camille Claudel, *Perseus and the Gorgon*, 1897–1902:** psychologically charged reworking.
- **Luciano Garbati, *Medusa with the Head of Perseus* (2008):** reverses Cellini’s composition; a copy installed in New York in 2020 became associated with the #MeToo movement. Its use as a feminist emblem is modern, not ancient cult continuity.

### Opera and music

- **Jean-Baptiste Lully and Philippe Quinault, *Persée* (1682):** court opera emphasizing royal order and spectacle.
- **Michael Haydn, *Andromeda e Perseo* (1787):** operatic reception.
- Numerous seventeenth- and eighteenth-century *Andromeda* operas derive more from Ovid, Euripidean reconstruction, and court convention than from a single Greek source.

### Film and television

- ** *Perseus the Invincible* (1963):** Italian sword-and-sandal reinvention.
- ** *Clash of the Titans* (1981):** Ray Harryhausen’s influential synthesis. Calibos, Bubo, the “Kraken,” Pegasus as Perseus’ mount, and much of the divine chess-game are modern constructions.
- ** *Clash of the Titans* (2010):** makes Hades the principal antagonist and gives Perseus an anti-divine motivation absent from the main ancient sources.
- ** *Wrath of the Titans* (2012):** almost wholly modern sequel mythology.

### Comics and games

Perseus appears in DC and Marvel adaptations, *God of War II* (2007), *Age of Mythology*, *Smite*, and many mobile games. These generally standardize helmet, winged sandals, reflective shield, curved sword, Medusa’s head, and Pegasus—equipment and associations drawn from different ancient heroes and different chronological layers. **[MI]**

### Psychology and comparative theory

- **Otto Rank, *The Myth of the Birth of the Hero* (1909):** Perseus exemplifies the exposed royal child whose threatened father attempts infanticide. **[MSR]**
- **Sigmund Freud and psychoanalytic successors:** Medusa’s head interpreted through castration anxiety; a theory about reception and psychic symbolism, not an ancient explanation. **[MSR]**
- **Lord Raglan, *The Hero* (1936):** incorporates Perseus-like motifs into a ritual hero-pattern. The scoring system depends heavily on selecting and harmonizing variants. **[MSR]**
- **Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949):** the quest can be mapped onto the monomyth, but that framework suppresses local cult, variant chronology, and gendered violence. **[MSR]**
- **Structuralist readings:** oppose human/monster, culture/wilderness, seeing/not-seeing, male initiation/female danger. Their value is analytical, not evidentiary proof of origin.
- **Walter Burkert and Near Eastern comparativists:** compare Gorgon imagery and heroic monster combat with Lamashtu, Humbaba, and broader eastern Mediterranean motifs. Burkert’s “creative misunderstanding” model is influential, but no surviving Near Eastern text is simply “the original Perseus story.” **[MSR]**
- **Gregory Nagy:** places Perseus within the semantics of heroic lineage, kleos, and local cult, while emphasizing that Panhellenic poetry and local traditions interact rather than descend from one canonical biography. **[MSR]**

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

- Herodotus already records political use: Xerxes’ alleged appeal to common descent from Perseus and Argos. **[CE]**
- Cellini’s Perseus served Medicean autocratic symbolism. **[MA]**
- Aurobindo’s *Perseus the Deliverer* has been read through anticolonial nationalism and heroic masculinity. **[modern reception]**
- Cellini’s figure has appeared in nationalist imagery, including reported use by the Polish neopagan-nationalist milieu associated with Zadruga. Such use is modern appropriation, not evidence of an ancient ethnic doctrine.
- Online claims that Herodotus proves Persians were “really Greek,” or that modern Greeks possess genealogical rights over Iran through Perseus, are **[MI/pseudohistory]**. Herodotus is documenting a Greek ethnogony and an alleged diplomatic argument, not population genetics.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was there a historical Perseus?

No positive historical case presently rests on contemporary evidence. Mycenae was real; Perseus’ traditional setting is Bronze Age; later Greeks treated him genealogically as an ancestral king. None of those statements demonstrates an individual founder. The first securely datable record is Iron Age poetry and art, separated from palatial Mycenae by several centuries. A historical chieftain behind the myth remains logically possible but evidentially untestable. **[NEG/MSR]**

### Did the myth exist in Mycenaean Greece?

No securely accepted Linear B tablet names Perseus or narrates his cult. Attempts to connect him with *pe-re-* forms or with the possible goddess *pe-re-*82 are speculative and linguistically uncertain. The apparent Bronze Age geography of the story may reflect inherited tradition, later engagement with ruins, or both. Claims that Linear B tablets “record rituals of Perseus” are unsupported. **[NEG]**

### Text versus image

The Eleusis amphora is approximately contemporary with the formation of the Homeric/Hesiodic corpus but contains details absent from Homer. This shows that visual artists had access to narrative traditions not preserved in surviving poems. Scholars therefore reject a simple model in which every vase illustrates a known literary text.

The direction of influence between the apotropaic gorgoneion and the Perseus narrative remains contested:

1. the story produced the protective head-symbol;
2. the symbol generated an aetiological beheading story;
3. both developed partly independently and converged.

The approximately contemporary emergence of narrative and gorgoneion prevents certainty.

### Near Eastern comparisons

Gorgon bodies and faces have been compared with Lamashtu and Humbaba imagery, while the heroic beheading resembles broader eastern Mediterranean monster-combat patterns. Formal resemblance and cultural contact make influence plausible; direct derivation of the entire Perseus cycle is unproved. **[MSR]**

### Dating the *Catalogue of Women*

Older scholarship often spoke loosely of “Hesiod” as an eighth-century witness to every *Catalogue* episode. M. L. West and later scholars date the poem’s monumental form substantially later, commonly in the sixth century BCE, while allowing inherited material. Fragment assignment and ordering remain debated. Consequently, reconstructed Danaë lines cannot always be quoted with the confidence appropriate to a complete poem.

### Lost tragedy

The fifth century was central to the cycle, but almost every Perseus tragedy is lost. Reconstructions depend on quotations, hypotheses, vase imagery, parody, and later narratives. Assigning a particular Ovidian detail directly to Euripides may be plausible without being demonstrable. The proposed Aeschylean connected tetralogy *Phorcides–Polydectes–[lost play]–Net-Haulers* is a scholarly reconstruction, not a surviving ancient program.

### Hero cult

Older models, especially in the tradition of Rohde and Farnell, often treated hero worship as intrinsically tomb-centered and sharply “chthonic.” Archaeological work by Antonaccio and ritual studies by Ekroth show greater diversity: epic heroes could acquire cult at old tombs, civic shrines, battle sites, or places without a demonstrable burial. Perseus’ roadside *hērōon* and multiple regional honors fit that diversity. They do not certify possession of his bones.

### Medusa’s transformation

A common modern claim runs: “Medusa was originally Athena’s beautiful priestess; Poseidon raped her; Athena punished her by making her a monster.” The only extant ancient source close to that sequence is Ovid, approximately 8 CE; he calls her formerly beautiful and locates Neptune’s sexual act in Minerva’s temple but does not identify her as Minerva’s priestess. Archaic Hesiod already makes her a Gorgon by birth and places sex with Poseidon in a meadow. “Priestess” is a modern addition; “original myth” is false. **[MI falsely presented as ancient]**

### Mirror shield

Ancient art often shows Perseus averting his face, and later sources describe reflection. The fully polished “mirror shield” is most explicit in Ovid and later retelling. It should not be retrojected into Homer or Hesiod. **[LS versus EA]**

### Perseus and Pegasus

Pegasus’ birth from Medusa is archaic. Bellerophon’s riding of Pegasus is also ancient. Perseus’ habitual use of Pegasus as his mount is largely postclassical and especially cinematic, though isolated ancient images have been interpreted as mounting or association. Winged sandals are Perseus’ normal early means of flight. **[contested iconography/MI standardization]**

### The Persian genealogy

Herodotus 7.61 reports Perses as ancestor of the Persians; 7.150 attributes the claim to Xerxes’ diplomacy. Iranological evidence derives Persian identity from Old Persian *Pārsa*, not Perseus. Herodotus’ story is significant evidence for Greek political genealogy, not Persian ethnic origin. **[CE, not historical ethnogenesis]**

### State of the evidence

- Homeric and Hesiodic texts: reconstructed from medieval manuscripts, ancient quotations, papyri, and comparative textual criticism.
- *Catalogue* and tragedies: fragmentary; new papyri can alter reconstruction.
- Pherecydes: survives in quotations and scholia.
- *Bibliotheca*: relatively complete but late and compilatory.
- Hyginus: corrupt late manuscript transmission; isolated notices require caution.
- Pausanias: invaluable eyewitness to second-century landscapes but records local claims centuries after their supposed events.
- Art: abundant but identification can depend on attributes, inscriptions, and comparison.
- Cult archaeology: sparse and rarely names Perseus securely.
- LIMC remains the fundamental iconographic catalogue; Gantz and Fowler are major guides to early textual stratification; Ogden provides the principal modern single-hero synthesis.
