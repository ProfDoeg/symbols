# Pelops: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal epic king and culture-founding conqueror; beloved of Poseidon; ancestor of the Pelopids/Atreids; after death, a heroized dead with major chthonic cult at Olympia. He is not normally called a god or demigod in early Greek evidence, although divine ancestry is assigned to him through Tantalus.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** *Iliad* 2.100–108, in the form transmitted from the late eighth or early seventh century BCE: Zeus gives a royal sceptre through Hermes to “Pelops the driver of horses,” who transmits it to Atreus, Thyestes, and Agamemnon. This establishes Pelops as an ancestral king and charioteer but says nothing about his dismemberment, ivory shoulder, Poseidon, Hippodamia, Myrtilus, or the Olympic Games. The earliest securely identifiable material evidence for his myth is the decorated Chest of Cypselus, conventionally dated around 600 BCE and described by Pausanias; the earliest surviving extended narrative is Pindar’s *Olympian* 1 of 476 BCE.

## Evidentiary key

Claims are marked by the character of their evidence:

- **[E1: securely attested early]** Archaic textual or securely identified material evidence, approximately eighth–sixth centuries BCE.
- **[E2: classical canonical elaboration]** Fifth–fourth-century BCE poetry, drama, historiography, or monumental art.
- **[E3: regional or cultic variant]** A local tradition, ritual, shrine, relic, or regional genealogy, whether recorded early or late.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** Hellenistic or Roman mythography, scholia, genealogical synthesis, or antiquarian harmonization.
- **[E5: medieval/early-modern accretion]** Byzantine commentary or post-antique literary transmission.
- **[E6: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A proposal based on comparative, archaeological, ritual, linguistic, or historical analysis.
- **[E7: modern reception or invention]** A demonstrably post-antique creative adaptation, unsupported popular detail, or modern ideological use.

Dates assigned to fragmentary works refer to probable composition, not to the much later manuscripts, papyri, quotations, or scholia through which they survive.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Pelops is the eponymous hero of the **Peloponnese**, the “island of Pelops,” and the dynastic ancestor through whom Greek tradition linked Elis and Olympia with Mycenae, Sparta, Troezen, Corinth, and other cities. Homer knows him principally as the bearer of a divinely authorized sceptre and progenitor of Atreus. Pindar makes him a Lydian prince, the beautiful beloved of Poseidon, victor over Oenomaus, husband of Hippodamia, father of six sons, and recipient of blood-sacrifice beside the Alpheus. Classical tragedy instead foregrounds the treacherous death of Myrtilus and the inherited ruin of Pelops’s house. Roman mythographers combine these strands with the gruesome banquet of Tantalus, Pelops’s restoration from the cauldron, his ivory shoulder, and extensive genealogical lists. Pausanias records the hero as a continuing object of Elean cult. **[E1–E4]**

### Name and scripts

- Ancient Greek: **Πέλοψ**, *Pélops*; genitive **Πέλοπος**, *Pélopos*. An Ionic or personal-name variant **Πέλωψ**, *Pélōps*, is epigraphically recorded. The related dynastic adjective and noun are **Πελοπίδης**, “son/descendant of Pelops,” and plural **Πελοπίδαι**, “Pelopids.” **[E1; E4]**
- Latin: **Pelops**, genitive **Pelopis**; adjectival **Pelopeius/Pelopius**, frequently meaning “Pelopian,” “Pelopid,” or more broadly “Greek.” **[E4]**
- Modern Greek: **Πέλοπας**, *Pélopas*; French *Pélops*; Italian and Spanish *Pelope*; German *Pelops*. **[E7: modern linguistic reception]**

No secure Linear B spelling naming the hero has been identified. Reconstructions such as hypothetical Mycenaean **\*Pelokʷs** are linguistic back-formations, not tablet attestations. **[E6]**

### Etymology

The old transparent analysis joins **πελός/πελιός**, “dusky, livid, dark,” to **ὄψ**, “eye, face,” yielding “dark-faced” or “dark-eyed.” Liddell and Scott’s traditional entry accepts this segmentation. It remains possible but is not demonstrable as the historical origin of the name. Alternative substrate or pre-Greek explanations have been proposed, especially because heroic names ending in *-ops* may not always be synchronically transparent. No consensus etymology proves either a Greek or Anatolian ethnic origin for the figure. **[E6]**

**Πελοπόννησος**, *Pelopónnēsos*, is understood in Greek as **Πέλοπος νῆσος**, “Pelops’s island.” The name is already archaic: “Peloponnese” appears in the Homeric Hymn to Apollo. Ancient authors converted the toponym into narrative history by saying that the wealthy immigrant Pelops conquered or dominated the region and gave it his name—explicitly in Thucydides 1.9, Diodorus 4.74, and Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.9. Whether the personal name genuinely generated the place-name, or the eponym was fashioned from an older toponym, cannot be decided. **[E1; E2; E4; E6]**

### Epithets and descriptive titles

- **ἱπποδάμος / “horse-tamer,” “charioteer”**: Homer calls him “Pelops the driver of horses” in *Iliad* 2.104. This is the oldest securely attested characterization. **[E1]**
- **Λυδός / “Lydian”**: Pindar, *Olympian* 1.24, identifies him as Lydian. Other traditions call him Phrygian, Paphlagonian/Enetian, Arcadian, or Achaean. **[E2; E3]**
- **Κρόνιος / *Kronios***: Pindar, *Olympian* 3.23–24, uses an epithet connecting him with Cronus, explicable genealogically through versions in which Tantalus’s mother is Plouto, daughter of Cronus. **[E2; E4]**
- **Tantalides**, “son/descendant of Tantalus”: a poetic patronymic, especially in Latin and later Greek writing. **[E2–E4]**
- **Pelopid/Pelopeian**: applied both to Pelops’s descendants and, metonymically, to Mycenae, Argos, or the cursed royal house. **[E1–E4]**
- **Founder of the Olympic Games**: not an invariant epithet. Pausanias 5.8.2 says Pelops “established the contest for Olympian Zeus” more splendidly than earlier founders; other sources give the foundation to Idaean Heracles, Zeus’s son Heracles, Clymenus, Endymion, Amythaon, or Iphitus. **[E3; E4]**

### Geographic identities

Ancient tradition never settled Pelops’s birthplace:

| Origin | Witness | Evidentiary character |
|---|---|---|
| Lydia/Sipylus | Pindar, *Olympian* 1; Sophocles, *Ajax* 1291–1292; Thucydides 1.9; Pausanias 5.13.7 | **[E2; E3]** |

| Paphlagonia/Enete | Apollonius, *Argonautica* 2.358, 790 with scholia; Diodorus 4.74 | **[E4; regional variant]** |
| Olenus in Achaea | Scholia to Pindar, *Olympian* 1 | **[E4; regional variant]** |
| Arcadia | Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.12.6, in the Stymphalus episode | **[E4; regional variant]** |

These are competing traditions, not stages of a recoverable historical migration.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Homer

In *Iliad* 2.100–108, Zeus has Hephaestus make a sceptre; Zeus gives it to Hermes, Hermes to “Pelops the horse-driver,” Pelops to Atreus, Atreus to Thyestes, and Thyestes to Agamemnon. The passage makes Pelops indispensable to the legitimacy of Agamemnon’s kingship. It does **not** call Pelops an Asian immigrant, son of Tantalus, lover of Poseidon, husband of Hippodamia, killer of Myrtilus, or founder of games. **[E1]**

The *Odyssey* does not narrate Pelops’s life. Its stories of Agamemnon, Aegisthus, and Orestes presuppose the Pelopid dynasty, but Pelops himself is not an active character. **[E1: absence is material]**

#### Archaic visual evidence

Pausanias 5.17.5–7 describes the **Chest of Cypselus**, a richly decorated cedar chest traditionally associated with the Corinthian tyrant Cypselus and conventionally dated around 600 BCE. One register showed Oenomaus pursuing Pelops, who carried Hippodamia in his chariot; inscriptions identified the figures. The chest is lost, and the evidence is Pausanias’s second-century CE autopsy and reading of an archaic object. If the dating and identification are correct, this is among the earliest material witnesses to the race/flight story. **[E1 object; E4 description]**

The earliest unmistakable surviving monumental image is the east pediment of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia, about 470–456 BCE: Zeus stands centrally between the parties before the race, with Pelops, Hippodamia, Oenomaus, Sterope, chariots, attendants, and river gods. Pausanias 5.10.6–7 identifies the subject. The surviving composition shows tense preparation, not the crash itself, and cannot independently establish the bribery of Myrtilus. **[E2]**

## 3. The story

### 3.1 Parentage

The stable paternal relationship is **Pelops son of Tantalus**. It is explicit by the fifth century BCE and standard thereafter. Tantalus’s own divine parentage makes Pelops a descendant of Zeus in genealogical systematization, but early Homer calls him neither son of a god nor demigod. **[E2; E4]**

His mother varies:

- **Dione**, daughter of Atlas: Hyginus, *Fabula* 82/83. **[E4]**
- **Euryanassa**, daughter of the river-god Pactolus: scholia to Euripides, *Orestes* 4. **[E4]**
- **Clytia/Clytie**: scholia to *Orestes* 11 and Byzantine sources. **[E4/E5]**
- **Plouto**, daughter of Cronus: a genealogy sometimes made Tantalus’s mother rather than Pelops’s; later summaries frequently confuse the generation. **[E4; transmission warning]**
- **Plastene**: Pausanias 5.13.7 reports a sanctuary of “Pelops’s mother Plastene” below the so-called throne of Pelops on Sipylus. This is a specifically Anatolian local tradition and should not be harmonized with the mythographers. **[E3]**

Niobe and Broteas are commonly his siblings in later genealogy. Ovid makes Pelops mourn Niobe as his sister. **[E4]**

### 3.2 Tantalus’s banquet, death, and restoration

The familiar account says that Tantalus killed Pelops, cut him into pieces, boiled or cooked him, and served him to the gods. The gods recognized the outrage and did not eat; one portion of the shoulder was lost, in later explanations because grieving Demeter ate it. The gods reassembled Pelops, and an ivory piece replaced the missing shoulder. **[E4 as fully preserved; an earlier version is presupposed by Pindar]**

Pindar’s *Olympian* 1 is crucial because it both attests and rejects an earlier tale. He acknowledges the cauldron and Pelops’s ivory shoulder but refuses to say that a god ate human flesh. His preferred version is that Poseidon abducted Pelops from Tantalus’s feast because he desired him; envious neighbors then invented the story that the gods cut and ate the boy. Pindar’s moral correction proves that the cannibal-banquet story circulated by 476 BCE, but the poem is not evidence that Pindar’s revision was universally accepted. **[E2]**

The fully explicit dismemberment-restoration account survives in Pseudo-Apollodorus, Hyginus, Ovid, scholia, Servius, and later mythographers. Demeter’s responsibility for the missing shoulder is common in scholia and later summaries, but Ovid merely says that one part could not be found. Clotho is said by Pindar to draw Pelops from the cleansing cauldron equipped with an ivory shoulder; later authors distribute the restoration among Rhea, the Fates, Hermes, or “the gods.” **[E4; variant]**

The episode contains child-murder, dismemberment, attempted theophagic deception, and—in the rejected version—divine anthropophagy. Interpretations as a relic of actual human sacrifice, an initiation ritual, a cooking-and-resurrection rite, or a myth explaining ivory-colored birthmarks are modern hypotheses. None is directly demonstrated by the ritual archaeology at Olympia. **[E6]**

### 3.3 Pelops and Poseidon

In Pindar, Poseidon falls in love with Pelops, carries him in a golden chariot to Zeus’s dwelling, and makes him his intimate attendant. Pindar compares this removal to Zeus’s later taking of Ganymede. The erotic language is not a modern inference: Pelops later invokes the *charis* of Aphrodite and asks Poseidon to repay their loving relationship. **[E2]**

Later authors sometimes soften Pelops into Poseidon’s “favorite” or “cupbearer,” but Pindar and Pseudo-Apollodorus explicitly make him the god’s beloved. The relationship is structured like elite Greek pederastic myth, though applying a single institutional model to an archaic divine narrative remains interpretation. **[E2/E6]**

When Tantalus’s offense causes Pelops to return to mortal society, Poseidon does not abandon him. In Pindar he answers Pelops’s prayer and supplies a golden chariot and tireless winged horses. Pseudo-Apollodorus says the vehicle could run across the sea without wetting its axles. **[E2; E4 elaboration]**

### 3.4 Wooing Hippodamia

Oenomaus, king of Pisa, refuses to permit his daughter Hippodamia to marry. Two explanations coexist:

1. An oracle warned that his daughter’s husband would kill him. **[E4, Pseudo-Apollodorus and later mythographers]**
2. Oenomaus desired Hippodamia himself and tried to prevent her marriage. Pseudo-Apollodorus preserves this incestuous motive as an alternative, not as the sole or earliest version. **[E4: variant]**

Oenomaus challenges each suitor to a chariot race. The suitor takes Hippodamia toward the Isthmus while Oenomaus sacrifices and then pursues with horses and weapons given by Ares. If caught, the suitor is killed. Pindar says thirteen suitors had died; Pseudo-Apollodorus gives a variant total of twelve. Later accounts say their severed heads were displayed on Oenomaus’s house, a detail represented in Philostratus’s ekphrasis. **[E2; E4]**

Pelops prays to Poseidon rather than relying only on human fraud in Pindar’s version. Poseidon gives him the divine team; Pelops wins, Oenomaus dies, and Pelops marries Hippodamia. Pindar does not introduce Myrtilus or sabotaged linchpins. **[E2]**

### 3.5 Myrtilus and the sabotaged chariot

In the influential tragic and mythographic strand, victory is gained through **Myrtilus**, Oenomaus’s charioteer and son of Hermes.

Who initiates the plot varies:

- Hippodamia sees Pelops, desires him, and persuades Myrtilus to help: Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.6. **[E4]**
- Pelops bribes Myrtilus: scholia and later prose accounts. **[E4]**
- Pelops promises half the kingdom, a night with Hippodamia, or her first sexual intercourse: different scholia and mythographic witnesses. **[E4; variant]**
- Myrtilus himself loves Hippodamia and acts to please her: Pseudo-Apollodorus. **[E4]**

The mechanism likewise varies:

- Myrtilus omits the linchpins from Oenomaus’s wheel-boxes: Pseudo-Apollodorus. **[E4]**
- He replaces bronze linchpins with wax ones: a widespread late explanatory form, preserved in scholia and Roman/Byzantine retellings. It should not be projected into Pindar. **[E4/E5]**
- Oenomaus becomes entangled in the reins and is dragged to death; in another version Pelops kills him directly. **[E4]**

Dying, Oenomaus discovers the betrayal and curses Myrtilus to die at Pelops’s hands. **[E4]**

### 3.6 Pelops kills Myrtilus

After the race, Pelops throws Myrtilus into the sea near Cape Geraestus; the Myrtoan Sea is explained from his name. Myrtilus curses Pelops and his descendants as he falls. Sophocles’s *Electra* and Euripides’s *Orestes* make this treachery the dynastic starting point of the Atreids’ disasters. **[E2]**

Pelops’s motive varies sharply:

- Myrtilus attempts to rape Hippodamia while Pelops fetches water; she reports him, and Pelops kills him: Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.8. **[E4]**
- Myrtilus asks for the sexual payment Pelops had promised; Pelops refuses and murders him. **[E4]**
- Pelops kills him to avoid dividing the kingdom or paying the promised reward. **[E4]**
- Hippodamia falsely accuses Myrtilus after he refuses or after a sexual confrontation. **[E4; scholiastic variant]**

The attempted-rape version does not acquit Pelops of bad faith in traditions where he first promised Hippodamia as payment. The variants alternately make Pelops a protector, oath-breaker, calculating murderer, or all three.

### 3.7 Purification and rule

Pseudo-Apollodorus says Pelops traveled to Ocean and was purified by Hephaestus before returning to Pisa. Other sources associate the cleansing with the river-god Oceanus or with cultic sites. **[E4]**

Pelops succeeds Oenomaus as king of Pisa and Olympia. Thucydides rationalizes the conquest: Pelops brought vast wealth from Asia and gained power among an impoverished population. Diodorus similarly credits courage, wisdom, and political expansion. Pseudo-Apollodorus says he subdued the land formerly called Apia or Pelasgiotis and renamed it Peloponnese. These are ancient political interpretations, not independent evidence of a Bronze Age conqueror. **[E2; E4]**

An isolated Arcadian story says Pelops pretended friendship with King Stymphalus, killed him, cut up his body, and scattered the pieces. Demeter then afflicted Greece with barrenness. The story appears in Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.12.6 and is a regional doublet of treacherous dismemberment. **[E3/E4]**

### 3.8 Marriage and children

Pindar says Pelops and Hippodamia had six sons, without preserving a universally agreed list. Later sources supply incompatible catalogues. Securely recurrent children include:

- **Atreus** and **Thyestes**, rivals for Mycenae.
- **Pittheus**, king of Troezen and grandfather of Theseus.
- **Troezen**, eponym of the city in one genealogy.
- **Alcathous**, associated with Megara.
- **Hippalcimus/Hippalcmus**, **Dias**, **Hippasus**, and **Copreus** in various lists.
- Daughters **Lysidice**, **Nicippe**, **Astydameia**, and others who connect the Pelopids to Heracles, Perseids, and regional dynasties.
- **Chrysippus**, usually Pelops’s son by Axioche, Danais, Astyoche, or another woman; some scholia instead make him Hippodamia’s son. **[E2–E4]**

The extraordinarily long lists are genealogical colonization: cities and royal houses claim Pelopid descent through locally useful sons and daughters. They should not be combined into a single historical family. **[E6 based on E3/E4 evidence]**

### 3.9 Chrysippus, Laius, and the second curse

In Euripides’s lost *Chrysippus*, reconstructed from fragments and testimonia, Laius becomes sexually obsessed with the beautiful Chrysippus, abducts him during the Nemean Games, and rapes or seduces him. Later moralizing sources call this the first pederastic crime among mortals, but that universal claim is ideological, not historical. Pelops curses Laius; this becomes one explanation for Laius’s death at the hands of his son Oedipus and for the ruin of the Theban house. **[E2; E4 elaboration]**

Chrysippus’s death has incompatible versions:

- At Hippodamia’s instigation, Atreus and Thyestes throw him into a well; Pelops curses and exiles them. **[E4]**
- Hippodamia kills Chrysippus with Laius’s sword and attempts to blame Laius; the dying Chrysippus identifies her. **[E4]**
- Chrysippus kills himself in shame after the abduction: a scholiastic version attributed to a Peisander of uncertain identity. **[E4]**
- Hyginus says Hippodamia later kills herself because she caused Chrysippus’s death. Pausanias says Pelops became angry with her; she withdrew to Midea, and her bones were eventually returned to Olympia by oracle. **[E3/E4]**

Thus two curses intersect but must be distinguished: Myrtilus curses the Pelopids after Pelops kills him; Pelops curses Laius or his own sons following Chrysippus’s violation or murder.

### 3.10 Death, tomb, and body

No early source gives Pelops a dramatic death. Pindar says he died and was enclosed by the Alpheus at a well-attended tomb where he receives blood-offerings. The lack of a narrative death is significant: his cultic presence matters more than a biographical ending. **[E2]**

Pausanias records multiple bodily claims:

- The **Pelopion at Olympia** was treated as Pelops’s precinct and tomb. **[E3]**
- A chest containing Pelops’s bones was shown near the sanctuary of Artemis Kordax in Elis. **[E3]**
- Pelops’s gigantic shoulder-blade was taken to Troy because an oracle required it for victory. On its return it was lost at sea; the fisherman Damarmenus later recovered it, and Delphi authenticated both the relic and the fisherman’s role. Pausanias says the bone had disappeared by his own day, probably through decay in seawater. **[E3/E4]**
- A “throne of Pelops” stood on Mount Sipylus above Plastene’s sanctuary. It was a landscape monument, not proof of Pelops’s residence. **[E3]**

The shoulder relic may have been an unusually large animal fossil or bone, but identifying it as mammoth or another species is modern conjecture because the object is lost and was never scientifically described. **[E6]**

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical trees

#### Common mythographic tree

```text
Zeus
└── Tantalus + Dione / Euryanassa / Clytie / another mother
    └── Pelops
        ├── with Hippodamia
        │   ├── Atreus
        │   │   └── Agamemnon and Menelaus
        │   ├── Thyestes
        │   │   └── Aegisthus (in the dominant genealogy)
        │   ├── Pittheus
        │   │   └── Aethra
        │   │       └── Theseus
        │   ├── Troezen, Alcathous, Dias, Hippalcimus, etc.
        │   └── Lysidice, Nicippe, Astydameia, etc.
        └── with Axioche / Danais / Astyoche
            └── Chrysippus
```

**[E4: composite synthesis, not one ancient source]**

#### Cultic and regional corrections

- Pausanias’s Sipylus tradition names **Plastene** as Pelops’s mother. **[E3]**
- Chrysippus is sometimes assigned to Hippodamia rather than a separate consort. **[E4]**
- The descent of Heracles from Pelops depends on variant marriages of Pelops’s daughters; Pausanias nevertheless treats Heracles as Pelops’s fourth-generation descendant when explaining the Pelopion. **[E3/E4]**
- Homer certifies only the political succession Pelops → Atreus → Thyestes → Agamemnon, not the full later family tree. **[E1]**

### Documented named interactions

Each entry identifies an actual textual, visual, cultic, or genealogical relationship. Mere co-membership in the heroic age is excluded.

- **Zeus** — Ultimate donor of the royal sceptre later transmitted to Pelops through Hermes; central divine witness before the Olympia race in the temple pediment; host of the divine realm to which Poseidon takes Pelops. *Iliad* 2.100–108, c. 700 BCE; Olympia east pediment, c. 470–456 BCE; Pindar, *Olympian* 1, 476 BCE. **[E1/E2]**
- **Hephaestus** — Manufactures Zeus’s sceptre in Homer; later purifies Pelops after Myrtilus’s murder. *Iliad* 2.100–108; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.9. **[E1/E4]**
- **Hermes** — Hands the sceptre to Pelops in Homer; father of Myrtilus in the later race tradition and enforcer, through the curse or golden-lamb episode, of vengeance on Pelops’s house. *Iliad* 2.103–104; Euripides, *Orestes*; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.6–12. **[E1/E2/E4]**
- **Poseidon** — Falls in love with and abducts Pelops; receives him as beloved; later answers his prayer and gives him a golden chariot and tireless or winged horses. Pindar, *Olympian* 1, 476 BCE; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.3–5. **[E2/E4]**
- **Aphrodite** — Pelops invokes the reciprocal *charis* of Cyprian Aphrodite when asking Poseidon to remember their erotic relationship. Pindar, *Olympian* 1.75–78. **[E2]**
- **Clotho** — Draws restored Pelops from the cleansing cauldron with his ivory shoulder. Pindar, *Olympian* 1.25–27. **[E2]**
- **Demeter** — In the rejected banquet tradition, eats or consumes part of Pelops’s shoulder while distracted by grief for Persephone; in the Stymphalus story, punishes Pelops’s crime with barrenness. Scholia to Pindar; Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.12.6. **[E4]**
- **Rhea** — In some later mythographic accounts, restores or reconstitutes Pelops after the banquet. Roman and scholiastic mythography. **[E4]**
- **Tantalus** — Father; kills and serves Pelops in the cannibal-banquet tradition, or loses him when Poseidon abducts him in Pindar’s corrective version. Pindar, *Olympian* 1; Hyginus 82–83; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.3. **[E2/E4]**
- **Dione** — Mother in Hyginus. *Fabulae* 82–83. **[E4]**
- **Euryanassa** — Mother in scholia to Euripides. Scholia to *Orestes* 4. **[E4]**
- **Clytie/Clytia** — Mother in another scholiastic genealogy. Scholia to *Orestes* 11. **[E4]**
- **Plastene** — Mother in the local Sipylus landscape tradition. Pausanias 5.13.7. **[E3]**
- **Niobe** — Sister; Pelops mourns her dead children and reveals his ivory shoulder. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 6.401–411, c. 8 CE. **[E4]**
- **Broteas** — Brother in later Tantalid genealogy; no surviving narrative interaction with Pelops. Mythographic genealogies. **[E4; absence noted]**
- **Ganymede** — Pindar compares Zeus’s later abduction of Ganymede to Poseidon’s taking of Pelops; they do not interact directly. *Olympian* 1.43–45. **[E2; explicit parallel, not encounter]**
- **Oenomaus** — Pelops challenges him for Hippodamia; defeats him through divine aid and/or Myrtilus’s treachery; Oenomaus dies in the race or at Pelops’s hand. Pindar, *Olympian* 1; Sophocles and Euripides, lost *Oenomaus* plays; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.4–8. **[E2/E4]**
- **Ares** — Gives Oenomaus the horses and weapons used against Hippodamia’s suitors; Pelops overcomes this divine equipment. Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.5. No direct conversation with Pelops is attested. **[E4]**
- **Hippodamia** — Desired bride, collaborator or beneficiary in the race, wife, mother of Pelops’s children, and later object of his anger after Chrysippus’s death. Pindar, *Olympian* 1; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.4–10; Pausanias 6.20.7. **[E2/E3/E4]**
- **Myrtilus** — Conspires in the sabotage; Pelops kills him by throwing him into the sea; Myrtilus curses Pelops’s house. Sophocles, *Electra* 504–515; Euripides, *Orestes*; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.6–9. **[E2/E4]**
- **Cillus/Cillas** — Pelops’s charioteer, who dies on Lesbos while Pelops travels to Pisa; his ghost requests burial, and Pelops establishes a sanctuary of Cillaean Apollo. Theopompus as preserved in Pseudo-Apollodorus’s scholia/notes. **[E4, preserving a lost earlier historian]**
- **Apollo Cillaeus** — Receives a sanctuary established by Pelops after Cillus’s burial. Lost local account preserved in later commentary. **[E3/E4]**
- **Stymphalus** — Arcadian king murdered, dismembered, and scattered by Pelops after a false friendship. Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.12.6. **[E3/E4]**
- **Heracles, son of Zeus** — Pausanias says Heracles, Pelops’s descendant in the fourth generation, assigned the Pelopion to him and sacrificed into a pit; Pindar makes Heracles establish the Olympic precinct near Pelops’s tomb. Pindar, *Olympian* 10; Pausanias 5.13.2. **[E2/E3]**
- **Heracles of Ida** — One of the alternative founders of the Olympic contest; no direct Pelops interaction is stated. Pausanias 5.7–8. **[E3/E4; separate from Zeus’s son]**
- **Atreus** — Son and successor; receives the sceptre from Pelops in Homer; in later tradition is cursed and expelled after Chrysippus’s murder. *Iliad* 2.104–107; scholia, Hyginus 85. **[E1/E4]**
- **Thyestes** — Son; receives the sceptre after Atreus in Homer; later cursed or expelled with Atreus over Chrysippus. *Iliad* 2.106–108; scholia and Hyginus. **[E1/E4]**
- **Chrysippus** — Favored son; Pelops pursues or curses Laius after his abduction and curses or expels Chrysippus’s killers. Euripides’s lost *Chrysippus*; Hyginus 85; scholia. **[E2/E4]**
- **Laius** — Abducts and sexually violates or seduces Chrysippus; Pelops curses him and thereby helps initiate the Theban dynastic disaster. Euripides’s lost *Chrysippus* and later testimonia. **[E2/E4]**
- **Pittheus** — Son in the dominant genealogy; receives a regional kingdom and becomes grandfather of Theseus. Euripides, *Medea* 683; Pseudo-Apollodorus; Pausanias 2.30.8. No surviving direct dialogue with Pelops. **[E2/E4]**
- **Theseus** — Great-grandson through Pittheus and Aethra; no direct meeting with Pelops. Pausanias and Plutarch, *Theseus* 3. **[E4]**
- **Alcathous** — Son in Megarian genealogy; no direct narrative encounter survives. Pausanias 1.39–43. **[E3]**
- **Troezen** — Son or descendant and civic eponym; no direct episode survives. Pausanias 2.30.8. **[E3]**
- **Agamemnon** — Great-grandson and final bearer of the sceptre that passed through Pelops’s line; no direct meeting because the genealogical chronology separates them. *Iliad* 2.100–108. **[E1]**
- **Helenus** — In late accounts, reveals that Pelops’s shoulder-bone is required for Troy’s fall; no direct encounter with the dead Pelops. Pausanias 5.13.4–6 and Byzantine elaboration. **[E3/E5]**
- **Damarmenus** — Fisherman who recovers Pelops’s shoulder relic from the sea and receives hereditary honors after Delphic authentication. Pausanias 5.13.5–6. **[E3]**

No ancient source makes Pelops an Argonaut, participant in the Trojan War, Olympian deity, resurrected Christ figure, or literal immortal. Such descriptions are modern conflations or comparative interpretations. **[E7]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, relics, and ritual

### The Pelopion at Olympia

Pindar’s *Olympian* 1 gives the earliest literary evidence for Pelops as a cult hero at Olympia: dead, he lies beside the Alpheus at a much-visited tomb and “has a share in splendid blood-sacrifices.” This is direct fifth-century evidence that the tomb and offerings were integral to the Olympic landscape by 476 BCE. **[E2]**

Pausanias 5.13.1–2 describes the Pelopion north of the Temple of Zeus: a stone-walled enclosure containing trees, statues, and dedications, entered from the west. The Eleans, he says, honored Pelops above Olympia’s other heroes as they honored Zeus above other gods. Annual officials sacrificed a **black ram** into a pit. The seer received no share; the neck went to the “woodman.” Pausanias does not say in this passage that the animal was wholly burned or that the sacrifice occurred at night. Those details arise from broader reconstructions of chthonic ritual and should not be silently added to his testimony. **[E3/E4]**

The classical Pelopion occupied and monumentalized an earlier mound. Archaeology distinguishes several phases:

- A large Early Helladic mound or tumulus belongs to the third millennium BCE. **[archaeologically secure]**
- Later prehistoric occupation and deposits occur around it, but continuous worship of a named hero Pelops from the Bronze Age is not proved. **[E6: disputed]**
- The identifiable hero precinct and cult configuration emerge in the Archaic period, commonly in the sixth century BCE; a pentagonal or irregular stone enclosure was added and later monumentalized. **[archaeological E1/E2]**
- Votive pottery, terracottas, and bronzes attest ritual use, but objects without inscriptions cannot always be assigned exclusively to Pelops rather than to the wider Altis. **[archaeological caution]**

Older scholarship sometimes treated the prehistoric mound as proof of uninterrupted Pelops worship from the Early Bronze Age. Current hero-cult methodology rejects that inference: an old mound could be reidentified in the Archaic period. The monument’s antiquity and the named cult’s antiquity are different questions. **[E6]**

### Pelops and the Olympic Games

Pausanias 5.8 preserves a deliberately plural foundation history. Clymenus, Endymion, Pelops, Amythaon, Pelias and Neleus, Heracles, and Iphitus all establish, renew, or reorganize the games at different mythical moments. Pelops’s version says he established the contest for Olympian Zeus more splendidly than his predecessors. Another tradition makes his victory over Oenomaus—or funeral games held for Oenomaus—the origin of chariot competition. Pindar instead gives Heracles a central role in creating the Olympic festival while placing Pelops’s tomb and cult at its heart. **[E2–E4]**

Accordingly, “Pelops founded the Olympic Games” is ancient but not uniquely authoritative. The historical Olympic victor list was conventionally reckoned from 776 BCE, centuries after any imagined heroic generation; it does not verify Pelops’s activity. **[E3/E6]**

### Hero sacrifice and Olympian sacrifice

Older models sharply opposed “chthonic” hero ritual—black animals, pits, night, complete destruction—to “Olympian” ritual—white animals, raised altars, daylight, shared meat. Pelops’s black ram and pit encouraged this binary. Ekroth’s work shows that Greek hero cults were more heterogeneous: heroes could receive meals and shared sacrifices, and vocabulary such as *thyein* did not mechanically encode a separate chthonic system. Pelops’s ritual changed over time and operated inside Zeus’s festival rather than as its simple opposite. **[E6]**

A prohibition reported in later discussion—that those eating Pelops’s sacrificial meat could not enter Zeus’s temple—belongs to interpretations of the ritual dossier and must not be confused with the exact wording of Pausanias 5.13.1–2. **[E4/E6 caution]**

### Hippodamia’s associated cult

Pausanias 6.20.7 describes Hippodamia’s precinct at Olympia. Women entered once a year to sacrifice and perform other rites. The Eleans said Hippodamia had withdrawn to Midea because Pelops was angry over Chrysippus’s death, but an oracle ordered her bones returned. This is a distinct female cult linked to, but not identical with, the Pelopion and the Heraia. **[E3]**

### Relics and political authority

The shoulder-blade story made Pelops’s body a Panhellenic instrument: Troy could be taken only with the relic, and Elis could reclaim it through Delphi. Its authentication by the Pythia and hereditary rewards to Damarmenus’s family show how relic stories authorized local privilege. **[E3/E4]**

The east pediment of the Temple of Zeus monumentalized the transfer of rule from Oenomaus to Pelops at the sanctuary itself. Whether read as a warning against deceit, a model of divinely sanctioned marriage, an image of civic succession, or an Elean claim over Pisa, the sculpture politicalized local myth. The exact ancient interpretation is not preserved. **[E2/E6]**

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Eighth–sixth centuries BCE: dynastic ancestor and emerging local hero

Homer’s Pelops is a horse-driving ancestral king whose possession of Zeus’s sceptre validates Agamemnon. Genealogical epic expanded his descendants. By around 600 BCE the Chest of Cypselus apparently displayed the race and abduction/flight of Hippodamia. The Olympia cult becomes archaeologically identifiable in the Archaic period. **[E1]**

### Fifth century BCE: canonical shaping

Pindar integrates three domains: erotic divine favor, athletic victory, and hero cult. His explicit rejection of divine cannibalism reveals an already contested myth. The Temple of Zeus pediment makes the pre-race moment one of Olympia’s principal public images. Tragedy then reorients Pelops from victorious founder toward criminal ancestor: the murdered Myrtilus and inherited curse explain the Atreids’ cycle of adultery, fratricide, cannibalism, and kin-murder. **[E2]**

Thucydides strips away marvels and turns Pelops into a wealthy Asian dynast. This is ancient rationalization, not modern historical confirmation. **[E2]**

### Hellenistic period: cataloguing and regional reconciliation

Alexandrian scholarship, Apollonius, mythographers, and scholia collected maternal variants, regional origins, sons, daughters, cults, and alternate mechanisms of the race. Pelops becomes a junction in a comprehensive mythological chronology. **[E4]**

### Roman Imperial period: encyclopedic synthesis and antiquarian cult

Ovid highlights bodily metamorphosis through the ivory shoulder. Pseudo-Apollodorus supplies the fullest continuous biography-like synthesis. Pausanias documents living ritual, monuments, local guides’ stories, relics, and incompatible Olympic foundations. Philostratus visualizes Pelops as an exotically dressed Lydian prince. Roman terracotta plaques, sarcophagi, and mosaics continue the race and bridal imagery. **[E3/E4]**

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Christian polemicists used the banquet, Poseidon’s love, and the crimes of the Pelopids as evidence of pagan immorality. Mythographers and commentators euhemerized, moralized, and preserved otherwise lost variants. Tzetzes assembled much of this antiquarian material in the twelfth century. Cult at Olympia ceased as the ancient sanctuary and games disappeared in late antiquity; there is no evidence of a continuous medieval religious cult of Pelops. **[E5; absence noted]**

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Latin mythography restored the Pelopids to European education, but Pelops remained less independent than Tantalus, Orestes, or Agamemnon. Richard Lateware’s lost or unfinished Oxford tragedy conventionally called *Pelops* or *The House of Pelops* belongs to about 1587–1588; the title itself is a modern conjecture. Antiquarian travelers identified the Pelopion and Sipylus monuments through Pausanias. German excavation at Olympia after 1875 recovered the sanctuary’s topography and the Temple of Zeus sculptures. **[E5/E7]**

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Ritualist, psychoanalytic, structuralist, fossil-historical, and hero-cult studies made Pelops a test case for sacrifice, initiation, pederasty, kingship, chariot ritual, and the relation between epic and cult. Recent work emphasizes diachronic change and refuses to infer a named Bronze Age cult from a prehistoric mound. Pelops survives principally in classical scholarship, museum display, Olympic-origin narratives, contemporary Hellenic polytheist discussion, and retellings of the House of Atreus. **[E6/E7]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature, drama, and art

- **Richard Lateware, “Pelops” or “The House of Pelops,” c. 1587–1588**: an Oxford tragedy known only from references; its conventional title is modern, and completion or performance is uncertain. **[E5/E7]**
- **Archaeological and neoclassical reception after 1875**: publication and reconstruction of Olympia’s east pediment made the poised, youthful charioteer a dominant modern visual image of Pelops. **[E7]**
- **Modern retellings of the Atreids** often mention Pelops as the origin of the curse but begin their dramatic action with Atreus or Agamemnon. Claims that a modern *Oresteia* adaptation “tells the full story of Pelops” should be checked work by work. **[E7]**
- **Museum and heritage displays** at Olympia, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the British Museum, and elsewhere present Pelops through the race, sacrifice, and wedding. Roman plaques dated 27 BCE–68 CE and later mosaics show that what appears “modernly romantic” already had ancient visual precedents. **[E4 objects; E7 display]**

Pelops has not generated a stable modern operatic, cinematic, comic-book, or video-game canon comparable with Achilles, Heracles, or Perseus. Namesakes and incidental appearances exist, but no famous ancient pairing should be inferred from modern fantasy databases. **[E7; negative finding]**

### Interpretive traditions

- **Cambridge ritualism and sacrifice theory**, especially the early twentieth century, connected Pelops’s cauldron, dismemberment, chariot race, and tomb to hypothetical seasonal kingship and human sacrifice. The comparisons are historically influential but exceed the direct evidence. **[E6]**
- **Walter Burkert** treated the myth and Olympia cult in relation to sacrifice, death, competition, and ritual violence. His reconstructions remain influential but are not primary evidence for actual prehistoric sacrifice. **[E6]**
- **Structuralist readings** oppose raw/cooked, mortal/divine, foreign/native, honest contest/treacherous victory, and marriage/death. These illuminate narrative relations but do not prove a single origin. **[E6]**
- **Psychoanalytic readings** emphasize paternal filicide, bodily repair, homoerotic favor, marital rivalry, and hereditary guilt. No ancient source supplies a psychological diagnosis. **[E6]**
- **Gregory Nagy’s hero theory** treats epic kleos, death, athletic competition, and cult as mutually constitutive. Pelops is particularly useful because Pindar locates his tomb and blood-offerings inside the Olympic victory song. **[E6]**
- **Gunnel Ekroth’s cult studies** challenge rigid Olympian/chthonic classifications and reconstruct changes in Pelops’s sacrificial role across Archaic, Classical, and Roman Olympia. **[E6]**
- **Adrienne Mayor’s fossil interpretation** makes large fossil bones a plausible context for Greek heroic relics. Applying this specifically to Pelops’s shoulder is possible, not demonstrable. **[E6]**
- **Rank, Raglan, Campbell, and generic hero-pattern theory** can fit selected elements—threatened child, death and return, divine aid, royal marriage—but Pelops does not neatly follow a universal monomyth, and pattern matching does not establish borrowing or historicity. **[E6]**
- **Dumézilian Indo-European reconstruction** has not produced a broadly accepted Pelops-specific trifunctional model. **[E6: negative finding]**

### Political and nationalist use

Ancient Elean and Pisan claims over Olympia likely shaped the representation of Pelops as local conqueror and founder, although the exact political program of individual monuments remains disputed. Thucydides already deploys him in a historical argument about wealth, migration, and hegemony. **[E2/E6]**

Modern Greek national discourse uses Olympia and the Peloponnese as emblems of continuity with antiquity, but Pelops himself has not become a major extremist icon. Searches for documented Pelops-specific fascist, racial-nationalist, or white-supremacist appropriation yield no stable movement comparable to modern abuses of Sparta or “Aryan” Indo-European mythology. Racial claims that Pelops proves a historical “Lydian,” “Nordic,” “Pelasgian,” or Anatolian conquest confuse competing myths with population history. **[E7: contested-material finding]**

### Documented modern inventions and recurrent errors

- “Pelops was a historical Lydian king whose migration has been archaeologically proven.” **Modern historicizing invention.**
- “Linear B tablets name Pelops.” **Unsupported.**
- “The Early Helladic mound proves continuous worship of Pelops from c. 2500 BCE.” **Unsupported inference from a prehistoric monument later used by the cult.**
- “Demeter certainly ate the shoulder in the earliest version.” **Unproved; Pindar attests and rejects cannibalism but does not name Demeter as eater.**
- “Wax linchpins occur in Pindar.” **False; Pindar has Poseidon’s divine team and no Myrtilus.**
- “Pelops founded the Olympics in the only ancient account.” **False; ancient foundation traditions are multiple.**
- “The black ram was certainly a human-sacrifice substitute.” **Modern ritualist hypothesis.**
- “Pelops became an immortal god.” **Not the normal ancient classification; he dies and receives hero cult.**
- “Pelops’s love affair with Poseidon is a modern sexualization.” **False; Pindar makes the erotic reciprocity explicit.**
- “Hippodamia is merely a passive prize in every source.” **False; Pseudo-Apollodorus makes her desire Pelops and recruit Myrtilus.**
- “Myrtilus always attempts rape.” **False; that is one of several competing motives for his death.**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was there a historical Pelops?

Ancient rationalizers treated Pelops as a real immigrant dynast. Thucydides’s account—wealth from Asia enabling domination of a poor country—is the earliest surviving historicizing theory. It is evidence for fifth-century historical thought, not for a Bronze Age individual. No contemporary inscription, administrative tablet, grave inscription, or datable Anatolian record identifies such a king. **[E2/E6]**

Attempts to recover Pelops as a Mycenaean warlord, Lydian colonizer, Hittite prince, or memory of population movement rest on combinations of myth, place-name, and later genealogy. The traditions disagree about Lydia, Phrygia, Paphlagonia, Arcadia, and Achaea. That instability weighs against treating any one origin as biographical fact. **[E6]**

### Did cult precede myth, or myth create cult?

The prehistoric mound at Olympia predates Greek alphabetic literature by more than a millennium. One model supposes long-lived tomb veneration later articulated as Pelops’s cult. Another holds that Archaic Greeks reidentified a visible ancient mound after heroic poetry and local political needs made Pelops useful. The archaeological evidence establishes reuse and monumentalization, not the prehistoric worshipper’s name. **[E6]**

### Pindar: preservation or innovation?

Pindar plainly knows the cannibal story and replaces it with Poseidon’s erotic abduction. Scholars debate how much of the replacement is his invention. The relationship between Poseidon and Pelops may be older than Pindar because it coheres with Pelops’s horsemanship and the god’s equine sphere, but no surviving pre-Pindaric text states it. “Pindar invented Poseidon’s love” and “Pindar faithfully preserves an age-old cult myth” are both stronger than the evidence allows. **[E6]**

### Divine horses versus sabotage

Pindar’s victory depends on Poseidon’s chariot; tragic and prose traditions emphasize Myrtilus’s treachery. These may be alternate story-types rather than components of one original narrative. Later handbooks commonly combine them—Pelops receives divine horses and also bribes Myrtilus—but no need for both exists in Pindar. **[E2/E4/E6]**

### Meaning of the cauldron

Proposed explanations include:

- memory of human sacrifice;
- initiation through symbolic death and rebirth;
- ritual cooking preceding sacrificial distribution;
- rejuvenation magic;
- an etiological tale for an ivory relic or bodily mark;
- narrative doubling of the later Thyestean feast.

Each comparison is possible. No excavated deposit demonstrates that a child was sacrificed as “Pelops,” and later Atreid cannibalism may echo Pelops’s story without revealing which tale came first. **[E6]**

### Hero-cult categories

Farnell and older historians often classified Pelops as a strongly chthonic hero because of the tomb, pit, black ram, and blood. Burkert integrated violence and sacrifice into the Olympic festival’s origin. Ekroth and subsequent scholarship stress that hero cult was not a single ritual type and that Pelops’s place within communal feasting changed over time. **[E6]**

### Iconographic disputes

The central subject of the Olympia east pediment is secure from Pausanias, but the identification and original placement of several individual figures remain debated. The sculpture represents the charged moment before the race; interpreting specific gestures as proof that Zeus condemns Pelops’s deceit or guarantees his victory exceeds what the figures themselves establish. **[E2/E6]**

Vase paintings catalogued in the *LIMC* show pre-race sacrifice, divine spectators, abduction, chariots, and Myrtilus. Names painted beside figures provide the strongest identifications; uninscribed scenes require comparison and remain probabilistic. **[E2/E4]**

### State of the evidence

The central losses are severe:

- the *Catalogue of Women* survives only in papyri and quotations;
- Stesichorus’s *Oresteia* is fragmentary;
- Sophocles’s and Euripides’s *Oenomaus* and Euripides’s *Chrysippus* are lost;
- Pherecydes, Hellanicus, Theopompus, and other mythographers survive through excerpts or scholia;
- the Chest of Cypselus is lost;
- Pelops’s shoulder relic had disappeared before Pausanias;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus’s *Epitome* survives through late manuscript recovery;
- Hyginus’s text is corrupt and transmitted through a precarious medieval witness.

Consequently, absence from an extant work does not prove that an episode was unknown in its period, but neither does a late scholiast permit an undated detail to be called “the original myth.”

## 10. Sources

### Primary texts and material evidence

- Homer, *Iliad* 2.100–108: earliest literary attestation and sceptre genealogy.
- Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fragments: Pelopid genealogies.
- Pindar, *Olympian* 1: Poseidon, cauldron controversy, race, tomb, cult.
- Sophocles, *Electra* 504–515 and fragments of *Oenomaus*.
- Euripides, *Orestes* and fragments/testimonia of *Oenomaus* and *Chrysippus*.
- Thucydides 1.9: rationalized Asian migration and wealth.
- Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica*, with ancient scholia.
- Diodorus Siculus 4.73–74.
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 6.401–411.
- Hyginus, *Fabulae* 82–85, 243.
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*, *Epitome* 2.3–12.
- Pausanias 5.8, 5.10, 5.13, 5.17, 6.20–22, and regional passages.
- Philostratus the Younger, *Imagines* 9.
- Olympia east pediment, c. 470–456 BCE.
- Archaic Pelopion enclosure and associated archaeological deposits.
- Greek and South Italian vase paintings assembled in *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae*, s.v. “Pelops.”
- Roman terracotta reliefs and mosaics of Pelops and Hippodamia.

- Timothy Gantz, *Early Greek Myth: A Guide to Literary and Artistic Sources*.
- M. L. West and Glenn W. Most, editions of Hesiodic and epic fragments.
- Robert L. Fowler, *Early Greek Mythography*.
- Gregory Nagy, *The Best of the Achaeans* and studies of Greek hero cult.
- Lewis Richard Farnell, *Greek Hero Cults and Ideas of Immortality*.
- Walter Burkert, studies of ancient Greek religion, sacrifice, and Olympia.
- Gunnel Ekroth, studies of hero cult and “Pelops Joins the Party.”
- William J. Slater, “Pelops at Olympia.”
- Judith Barringer, work on Olympia, athletics, gender, and the Temple of Zeus.
- Pierre Amandry, “La légende de Pélops et son iconographie.”
- Adrienne Mayor, *The First Fossil Hunters*, for the comparative interpretation of heroic bones.
- *LIMC*, “Pelops,” for the iconographic corpus.
