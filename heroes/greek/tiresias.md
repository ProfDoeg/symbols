# Tiresias: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Theban seer and culture-hero; after death, a uniquely conscious prophetic shade. Literary sources occasionally attach him to local oracular and funerary sites, but the surviving evidence does **not** establish a substantial, independently excavated Tiresias hero-cult comparable to those of Achilles, Orestes, or Amphiaraus.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Odyssey*, especially 10.490–540 and 11.90–151, composed in its received monumental form about 700 BCE, perhaps in the late eighth or early seventh century. There Tiresias is already the famous “blind Theban seer”: Persephone has preserved his intelligence after death, and Odysseus must consult him. This is earlier than every surviving narrative of his sex changes, blinding, parentage, or service to Oedipus.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early]** Archaic Greek evidence, approximately eighth–sixth centuries BCE.
- **[E2: classical canonical]** Fifth–fourth-century BCE tragedy, lyric, mythography, or art.
- **[E3: later canonical elaboration]** Hellenistic or Roman literary development.
- **[E4: regional/local variant]** A geographically localized tradition, whether early or late.
- **[E5: learned systematization]** Mythographic, scholastic, antiquarian, or genealogical harmonization.
- **[E6: medieval/early-modern accretion]** Post-antique reception presented in inherited mythic terms.
- **[E7: folklore or living belief]** Popular or devotional practice not demonstrably continuous from antiquity.
- **[E8: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern explanatory hypothesis rather than an ancient assertion.
- **[E9: modern invention/reception]** Modern creative, political, commercial, or technological reuse.

The labels classify the evidence, not its artistic worth or the ancient audience’s degree of belief.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Tiresias is the paradigmatic blind seer of Thebes. Homer knows him as a dead prophet whose mind alone remains intact in Hades; tragedy makes him the politically dangerous truth-teller who confronts rulers; Hellenistic and Roman authors explain his blindness and supernatural knowledge through competing tales of divine transgression, sex change, and compensation. His mythic chronology spans successive Theban generations—from Cadmus and Pentheus through Laius, Oedipus, the Seven, and the Epigoni—but that longevity is a narrative device, later rationalized as a life equal to seven ordinary lives. **[E1–E5: *Odyssey* 10–11; Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus* and *Antigone*; Euripides, *Bacchae* and *Phoenissae*; Callimachus, *Bath of Pallas*; “Apollodorus,” *Bibliotheca* 3.6.7]**

### Names and forms

- **Greek:** Τειρεσίας, *Teiresías*; epic-Ionic genitive Τειρεσίᾱο, *Teiresíāo*, in *Odyssey* 10.492, 537, 565. Another poetic nominative is Τειρεσίης, *Teiresíēs*. **[E1]**
- **Latin:** *Tiresias*; genitive *Tiresiae*. This is the normal form in Ovid, Hyginus, Statius, and later Latin literature. **[E3]**
- **English:** *Tiresias*; scholarly transliteration often *Teiresias*.
- **French:** *Tirésias*; Italian and Spanish: *Tiresia*; German: *Teiresias*. **[E6–E9]**
- **Etruscan:** Treccani’s iconographic entry records an Etruscan form, but no secure body of independent Etruscan narrative about him survives. **[E3; iconographic/onomastic evidence]**

### Etymology

The etymology is uncertain. Ancient Greek supplies no accepted transparent derivation. Connections proposed in popular sources with τεῖρος, “heavenly sign,” or with verbs of seeing, marking, or wearing out are speculative; compounds such as “he who sees signs” are modern folk-etymologies, not established historical linguistics. A pre-Greek or otherwise opaque heroic name remains possible but unproved. **[E8: modern linguistic uncertainty; E9 for confident internet etymologies]**

### Descriptions and quasi-epithets

Tiresias has no stable cult-title comparable to “Pythian Apollo.” Recurring descriptions perform that function:

- “The Theban seer,” μάντις Θηβαῖος in later prose descriptions. **[E1–E5]**
- “Blind seer/prophet,” conventional in tragedy and later reception. **[E2 onward]**
- In Homer, “the spirit of the Theban Tiresias,” whose understanding Persephone alone preserves; the other dead are “flitting shadows.” *Odyssey* 10.492–495. **[E1]**
- “Son of Everes,” Εὐηρείδης, is the patronymic implied by the standard genealogy. **[E2–E5]**
- “Old man” and staff-bearing prophet characterize his tragic stage persona rather than functioning as formal epithets. **[E2]**

### Class and identifications

He is consistently mortal, not a god or demigod. His survival as a conscious shade and the reporting of a tomb or oracle make “heroized dead” a defensible descriptive category, but an organized cult is only weakly documented. **[E1, E3–E4]**

He is not securely identified with another ancient deity or hero. Comparisons with Calchas, Melampus, Phineus, Helenus, Cassandra, or the Sibyl concern function, not identity. The assimilation of his daughter Manto to a Sibyl is a later learned development. Tiresias’s placement among diviners in Dante does not equate him with another figure. **[E5–E6]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

The *Odyssey* is the earliest secure evidence. Circe directs Odysseus to consult Tiresias in *Odyssey* 10.490–540; in Book 11 the seer drinks sacrificial blood and foretells the danger posed by Helios’s cattle, Odysseus’s solitary return, vengeance on the suitors, a later inland journey carrying an oar, sacrifice to Poseidon, and a gentle death “from/out of the sea,” depending on interpretation of ἐξ ἁλός. **[E1: approximately 700 BCE]**

What Homer does **not** say is equally important: he gives no parentage, no snake episode, no former female life, no judgment between Zeus and Hera, no cause of blindness, no association with Oedipus, and no account of the seer’s death. The *Iliad* does not securely feature Tiresias as an acting character. Later references attributed to “Homer” or found in Iliadic scholia must not be retrojected into the *Iliad*. **[E1; absence in the extant text]**

### Archaic epic and lyric

- **Hesiodic *Melampodia***, probably archaic but lost: a fragment transmitted by a scholion on *Odyssey* 10 recounts Tiresias encountering mating snakes, changing sex after killing or striking one, and recovering his original sex at a later encounter. The poem’s wording and date are uncertain because the evidence is an indirect, much later quotation or summary. **[E1 source tradition; E5 transmission]**
- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*:** sometimes loosely cited for Tiresias, but the principal sex-change testimony belongs to the lost *Melampodia*, not securely to the *Catalogue*. **[Corrective finding]**
- **Epic Cycle:** surviving summaries and fragments of the *Oedipodea*, *Thebaid*, *Epigoni*, and *Telegony* provide contexts into which later mythographers place Tiresias, but the extant fragments do not permit a complete Tiresian narrative. Martin West’s *Greek Epic Fragments* is the appropriate modern edition. **[E1 fragmentary evidence; E8 editorial reconstruction]**
- **Pindar, *Nemean* 1.60–72**, 470s BCE: Tiresias prophesies the infant Heracles’ future labours and final divine peace after the child strangles Hera’s snakes. **[E2]**
- **Pindar, *Pythian* 11:** evokes Theban prophetic space, but it is not a full Tiresias narrative. **[E2]**

### Athenian tragedy

- **Sophocles, *Antigone* 988–1114**, about 442–441 BCE: Tiresias, led by a boy, reports corrupt sacrificial signs caused by the unburied Polyneices. Creon accuses seers of greed; Tiresias foretells the death of Creon’s child. **[E2]**
- **Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus* 300–462**, usually dated to the 420s BCE: compelled by Oedipus, Tiresias identifies him as Laius’s killer and predicts discovery of his incestuous identity. The play does not narrate Tiresias’s sex changes or explain his blindness. **[E2]**
- **Euripides, *Bacchae* 170–369**, produced 405 BCE: Tiresias and Cadmus put on Bacchic dress and defend Dionysus’s rites against Pentheus. **[E2]**
- **Euripides, *Phoenissae* 834–959**, about 410–408 BCE: Tiresias tells Creon that Thebes can be saved only if Creon’s son Menoeceus is sacrificed to Ares, recompensing the dragon slain by Cadmus. **[E2]**
- Tiresias probably appeared in other lost Theban dramas, but roles reconstructed solely from fragments or later summaries remain conjectural. **[E2/E8]**

### Hellenistic Greek literature

- **Callimachus, Hymn 5, *The Bath of Pallas* 57–136**, third century BCE: the youth Tiresias accidentally sees Athena bathing with his mother Chariclo. Athena cannot revoke the divinely fixed blindness but gives him bird-divination, an honoured staff, authority among the dead, and posthumous mental distinction. **[E3]**
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica*:** Tiresias is not a principal Argonautic character. References in general indexes should not be treated as evidence that he accompanied Jason. **[Negative finding]**
- **Theocritus, *Idyll* 24.70–102**, third century BCE: after the infant Heracles kills the snakes, Tiresias predicts his heroic career to Alcmena. **[E3, elaborating the Pindaric scene]**

### Roman and imperial corpus

- **“Apollodorus,” *Bibliotheca* 2.4.8; 3.6.7; 3.7.3–4**, compiled in the first or second century CE from earlier sources: Tiresias explains Zeus’s impersonation of Amphitryon; catalogs rival causes of blindness; gives the seven-lifetimes tradition; advises Thebes during the wars of the Seven and Epigoni; dies at Tilphussa; and is father of Manto. **[E5]**
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 3.316–510**, completed about 8 CE: canonical Roman synthesis of the snakes, seven years as a woman, judgment of Jupiter and Juno’s sexual dispute, Juno’s blinding, Jupiter’s gift of prophecy, and the prediction concerning Narcissus. **[E3]**
- **Hyginus, *Fabula* 75 and 77**, probably first or second century CE in the transmitted handbook: offers compressed mythographic versions of the Theban story and Tiresias’s transformations. **[E5]**
- **Diodorus Siculus, *Library* 4**, first century BCE: preserves rationalizing and genealogical material about Theban seers and Manto’s post-conquest fate. **[E3/E5]**
- **Strabo, *Geography* 9.2**, Augustan/Tiberian period: places a sanctuary or memorial of Tiresias near Tilphussa and the temple of Tilphoussian Apollo. **[E4: geographical testimony]**
- **Pausanias 9.11.7; 9.33.1**, second century CE: reports a Theban oracle of Tiresias and a grave beside the Tilphusan spring, while distinguishing what local Greeks “say” from what he observes. **[E4/E5]**
- **Pliny, *Natural History* 7.203**, first century CE, associates Tiresias with legendary longevity; Pliny also transmits a competing Macedonian burial tradition in some readings or later notices. **[E3/E5; text and localization require caution]**
- **Lucian, *Dialogues of the Dead* 28**, second century CE: Menippus questions Tiresias in Hades about having been both man and woman. **[E3, comic reception]**
- **Statius, *Thebaid* 4**, about 92 CE: Tiresias conducts elaborate necromancy to summon Laius, greatly expanding the ritual and horrific elements. **[E3]**
- **Seneca, *Oedipus* 288–402**, first century CE: Tiresias and Manto interpret a failed sacrifice; because the blind prophet cannot inspect the entrails, Manto describes them. They then proceed toward necromancy. **[E3]**
- **Sostratus, *Tiresias*, summarized by Eustathius on *Odyssey* 10.492:** a sensational sequence of repeated transformations by Apollo, Hera, Zeus, the Muses, and Aphrodite, finally into a mouse. The original’s date and wording are lost; this is not the source of the ordinary “seven years as a woman” version. **[E5; extreme learned variant]**
- **Phlegon of Tralles, *Marvels***, second century CE: preserves paradoxographical transformation material. **[E3/E5]**

### Medieval transmission

Greek texts survived unevenly through Byzantine manuscripts and scholia; Latin Europe principally knew Tiresias through Ovid, Statius, mythographic handbooks, and later through recovered Greek tragedy. **[E6]**

- **Dante, *Inferno* 20.40–45**, about 1308–1320: Tiresias is among diviners punished by having their heads twisted backward. Dante retells the snakes and sex changes while condemning divination under Christian theology. **[E6]**
- **Boccaccio, *Genealogia deorum gentilium* and *De mulieribus claris***, fourteenth century: systematizes Manto and Tiresias from Latin authorities. **[E6]**
- **Christine de Pizan, *The Book of the City of Ladies***, 1405, and later mythographers draw upon Ovidian gender transformation in moralized frameworks, though not always centrally. **[E6]**

## 3. The story: episodes and variants

### Parentage and youth

The standard later genealogy makes Tiresias the son of **Everes** and the nymph **Chariclo**, descended from Udaeus, one of the Spartoi born from the dragon’s teeth. “Apollodorus” 3.6.7 and Callimachus’s identification of Chariclo carry the clearest evidence; Homer supplies none. **[E3/E5]**

Callimachus presents Tiresias as a beardless youth hunting with dogs on Helicon. He accidentally enters the sacred place where Athena and Chariclo bathe at noon. **[E3: *Bath of Pallas* 70–82]**

### Why he was blinded

The sources preserve irreconcilable accounts:

1. **Athena’s bath:** Tiresias involuntarily sees Athena naked. The sight of an immortal under forbidden conditions blinds him. Chariclo protests; Athena says she did not choose the punishment and cannot reverse what divine law has accomplished. She compensates him with prophecy and other gifts. **[E3: Callimachus, Hymn 5.70–130]**

2. **Judgment between Zeus and Hera:** because Tiresias has experienced sex as both man and woman, Zeus and Hera ask which sex receives more pleasure. He answers that the woman receives the greater share—Hyginus gives the famous ratio of nine parts to one. Hera blinds him; Zeus cannot undo another god’s act, so grants prophecy and extended life. **[E1 tradition possibly underlying later accounts; securely extant E3/E5: Ovid 3.316–338; Hyginus]**

3. **Revelation of divine secrets:** “Apollodorus” notes a version in which the gods blind him because he reveals their secrets to mortals. **[E5]**

These versions should not be combined into a single sequence. Callimachus makes Athena central and does not require the sex-judgment episode. Ovid makes Juno responsible and does not use the bathing story.

### The snakes and seven years as a woman

In the fragmentary Hesiodic *Melampodia* tradition, Tiresias sees two snakes mating on Mount Cithaeron. After injuring or killing one—some witnesses specify the female—he becomes a woman. At a second encounter years later, he strikes the other snake and becomes a man again. The precise action varies among the scholion, Ovid, Hyginus, and Phlegon: striking, wounding, killing, or trampling; some versions imply that he should spare rather than attack the snakes. **[E1 indirect fragment; E3/E5 variants]**

Ovid says he lived as a woman for seven autumns and changed back in the eighth year. **[E3: *Metamorphoses* 3.324–331]**

Claims that, as a woman, Tiresias was formally **Hera’s priestess**, married, worked as a prostitute, and bore Manto circulate widely. Some depend on late scholia or paradoxographical material, but they are frequently repeated without precise ancient citation. Manto is securely his daughter in later antiquity; that Tiresias personally bore her is not securely stated by the major early texts. **[E5 for late possibilities; E9 when asserted as settled ancient fact]**

### The sexual-pleasure judgment

Zeus says women enjoy intercourse more; Hera says men do. Tiresias supports Zeus because of his experience in both sexes. Ovid leaves the answer qualitative; Hyginus supplies a numerical division, usually nine-tenths to women. Hera’s retaliatory blinding and Zeus’s compensation transform embodied sexual knowledge into prophetic knowledge. **[E3/E5]**

The modern claim that this makes Tiresias straightforwardly “transgender,” “intersex,” “bisexual,” or “hermaphroditic” is interpretive, not ancient terminology. Ancient sources describe bodily transformation and socially gendered lives; they do not give Tiresias a stable modern identity category. Conversely, treating the female life as irrelevant also suppresses a central ancient tradition. **[E8: contemporary gender/classical-reception debate]**

### Seven lifetimes or seven generations

“Apollodorus” 3.6.7 says Zeus granted Tiresias a lifespan of seven generations or lives. This explains his appearance across the long Theban sequence. The tradition does **not** narrate seven reincarnations in the ordinary sense; “seven lifetimes” is longevity, not seven complete biographies. **[E5]**

### Cadmus, Pentheus, and Dionysus

In Euripides’ *Bacchae*, the elderly Tiresias visits Cadmus wearing fawnskin and ivy. They prepare to dance for Dionysus. Tiresias argues that no clever rationalization should override ancestral divine tradition. Pentheus mocks their age and dress and threatens the seer; Tiresias predicts Pentheus’s disastrous collision with the god. **[E2: *Bacchae* 170–369]**

This episode is dramatic invention or adaptation, not attested in Homer. It makes Tiresias an advocate of prudent ritual recognition rather than merely a clairvoyant. **[E2; E8 for functional interpretation]**

### Heracles’ infancy

Pindar’s *Nemean* 1 has Tiresias interpret the infant Heracles’ strangling of the snakes sent by Hera. He foretells the hero’s labours, destruction of monsters and unjust men, participation in the gods’ defeat of the Giants, and eventual immortal marriage to Hebe. Theocritus later expands the domestic setting. **[E2: Pindar; E3: Theocritus]**

“Apollodorus” also has Amphitryon consult Tiresias after Zeus, disguised as Amphitryon, sleeps with Alcmena. Tiresias reveals the divine deception. **[E5: *Bibliotheca* 2.4.8]**

### Laius and Oedipus

Later Theban tradition sometimes credits Tiresias with warning Laius about fathering a child, but surviving canonical drama places primary emphasis elsewhere. In Sophocles’ *Oedipus Tyrannus*, Tiresias is summoned during the plague. He initially refuses to speak, then tells Oedipus that he himself is the pollution, Laius’s killer, and both father and brother to his children. Oedipus accuses Tiresias and Creon of conspiracy. **[E2]**

Tiresias neither causes Oedipus’s self-blinding nor appears at its enactment. Similar blindness creates a tragic contrast: Tiresias lacks physical sight but knows; sighted Oedipus cannot recognize himself until too late. This contrast is strongly grounded in Sophocles, although its philosophical formulation belongs to interpretation. **[E2/E8]**

Ovid gives Tiresias a separate youthful prophecy about Narcissus—he will live long “if he does not know himself.” This verbal paradox later resonates with Oedipus but is Ovidian narrative construction, not evidence that the myths were originally one cycle. **[E3]**

### The Seven against Thebes

In Euripides’ *Phoenissae*, Tiresias declares that Menoeceus, Creon’s son, must die for Thebes because Ares demands recompense for Cadmus’s killing of the dragon. Creon tries to arrange his son’s escape, but Menoeceus voluntarily sacrifices himself. **[E2]**

The sacrifice is not in Sophocles’ *Antigone*. It is one of several incompatible traditions about Creon’s children and the salvation of Thebes. **[E2; variant history]**

### Creon and Antigone

In *Antigone*, birds tear at Polyneices’ unburied corpse and contaminate altars with carrion. Sacrificial fire will not burn properly. Tiresias warns Creon that refusing burial and entombing Antigone alive invert the proper placement of living and dead. After Creon accuses him of corruption, Tiresias foretells payment of “corpse for corpse,” fulfilled in Haemon’s and Eurydice’s deaths. **[E2]**

### Fall of Thebes, death, and body

After the Epigoni capture Thebes, the defeated Thebans flee under Tiresias’s guidance. According to “Apollodorus,” he drinks from the spring Tilphussa and dies; Pausanias similarly says the Argives were taking him and other captives toward Delphi when thirst led him to the spring, where he died. His grave was shown beside it. **[E4/E5: *Bibliotheca* 3.7.3–4; Pausanias 9.33.1]**

His daughter Manto is dedicated to Apollo at Delphi and, in later genealogies, helps establish the Claros/Colophon prophetic line. **[E3–E5]**

Competing burial notices exist, including a Macedonian localization in the Plinian tradition. These cannot both describe an archaeologically verified burial; they are rival claims to legendary remains. **[E3–E4]**

### Tiresias after death

Persephone grants Tiresias consciousness after death; Homer does not say Zeus did so. Odysseus digs a trench, pours libations, sacrifices sheep, and lets Tiresias drink blood. The prophet then identifies Poseidon’s hostility and prescribes Odysseus’s future inland sacrifice. **[E1: *Odyssey* 10–11]**

Callimachus later says Athena will make Tiresias honoured in Hades and that Hades himself will recognize him. This adapts the Homeric exceptional shade to Callimachus’s Athena-centered explanation. **[E3]**

### Documented interactions: divine beings and heroes

Each line records a textual or visual interaction, not mere coexistence in the same mythology.

- **Persephone** — Preserves Tiresias’s intelligence after death. *Odyssey* 10.492–495, ca. 700 BCE. **[E1]**
- **Odysseus** — Receives sacrificial blood from him and foretells his return, revenge, inland journey, and death. *Odyssey* 11.90–151, ca. 700 BCE. **[E1]**
- **Circe** — Directs Odysseus to consult Tiresias; no face-to-face meeting between Circe and Tiresias is narrated. *Odyssey* 10.487–540. **[E1; indirect interaction]**
- **Poseidon** — Tiresias explains Poseidon’s anger and prescribes a distant sacrifice to him. *Odyssey* 11.101–134. **[E1; indirect]**
- **Helios** — Tiresias warns Odysseus against harming Helios’s cattle. *Odyssey* 11.104–117. **[E1; indirect]**
- **Hades** — Callimachus says Hades will recognize and honour Tiresias among the dead. *Bath of Pallas* 129–130, third century BCE. **[E3]**
- **Athena** — Tiresias sees her bathing and is blinded; she compensates him with prophetic gifts, bird-language, staff, honour, and posthumous distinction. Callimachus, Hymn 5.57–136. **[E3]**
- **Chariclo** — His mother protests his blinding to Athena and obtains compensatory gifts for him. Callimachus, Hymn 5.85–130. **[E3]**
- **Zeus** — Asks him to judge sexual pleasure; after Hera blinds him, grants prophecy and prolonged life. Ovid 3.316–338; “Apollodorus” 3.6.7. **[E3/E5]**
- **Hera/Juno** — Disputes Zeus’s claim, receives Tiresias’s adverse verdict, and blinds him; the snakes are sometimes connected with her. Ovid 3.316–338; Hyginus. **[E3/E5]**
- **Apollo** — Later tradition associates Tiresias with Apolline divination; “Apollodorus” has Tiresias reveal Zeus’s deception to Amphitryon, while Sostratus makes Apollo one agent in repeated sex transformations. **[E5]**
- **Dionysus** — Tiresias accepts his divinity, dons Bacchic dress, and prepares to worship him. Euripides, *Bacchae* 170–369, 405 BCE. **[E2]**
- **Cadmus** — Tiresias’s companion in Bacchic worship; the two defend Dionysus before Pentheus. Euripides, *Bacchae*. **[E2]**
- **Pentheus** — Tiresias rebukes his impiety and anticipates its disastrous consequences. Euripides, *Bacchae*. **[E2]**
- **Heracles** — Prophesies his labours and apotheosis after the infant kills the snakes. Pindar, *Nemean* 1; Theocritus, *Idyll* 24. **[E2/E3]**
- **Alcmena** — Receives Tiresias’s prophecy about her infant son. Pindar and Theocritus. **[E2/E3]**
- **Amphitryon** — Consults Tiresias, who explains that Zeus assumed Amphitryon’s shape and slept with Alcmena. “Apollodorus” 2.4.8. **[E5]**
- **Oedipus** — Identifies him as Laius’s murderer and foretells his exposure. Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus* 300–462. **[E2]**
- **Laius** — Tiresias reveals or summons his truth: indirectly in Sophocles; his ghost is explicitly summoned in Statius. **[E2/E3]**
- **Creon** — Warns him in both *Antigone* and *Phoenissae*; predicts catastrophe when Creon rejects prophetic advice. **[E2]**
- **Menoeceus** — Names his sacrifice as the price for saving Thebes. Euripides, *Phoenissae* 834–959. **[E2]**
- **Ares** — Explains that Ares requires payment for Cadmus’s killing of the dragon. *Phoenissae*. **[E2; indirect]**
- **Polyneices** — Interprets pollution arising from his unburied corpse. Sophocles, *Antigone*. **[E2; posthumous/indirect]**
- **Antigone** — His warning requires that she be released from her living tomb, though the two do not converse. Sophocles, *Antigone*. **[E2; indirect]**
- **Haemon** — Foretells the death of Creon’s child; Haemon fulfills the prophecy. Sophocles, *Antigone*. **[E2; indirect]**
- **Narcissus** — Prophesies that he will live long only if he does not know himself. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 3.339–350. **[E3]**
- **Manto** — His prophetic daughter and guide; she describes sacrificial signs to her blind father in Seneca. Seneca, *Oedipus* 288–402. **[E3]**
- **The Epigoni** — Foresees Thebes’s fall and is taken as captive or dedicated spoil after the city falls. “Apollodorus”; Pausanias. **[E4/E5]**
- **Apollo at Delphi** — The conquerors intend Tiresias and/or Manto as offerings or dedications to the god; Tiresias dies en route. Pausanias 9.33.1; “Apollodorus” 3.7.4. **[E4/E5]**
- **Aphrodite, Muses, Apollo, Hera, and Zeus in Sostratus** — Successively alter Tiresias’s sex or form in a late, extravagant transformation cycle. Sostratus as summarized by Eustathius. **[E5; not early canon]**

No ancient source securely makes Tiresias an Argonaut, lover of Odysseus, or biological child of Apollo. Such pairings are errors or modern invention. **[Negative finding]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, oracle, and ritual

### Theban oracle

Pausanias 9.11.7 reports at Thebes a place called the “Oracle of Tiresias” near the sanctuary of Ammon and a shrine of Tyche. His wording establishes second-century CE local identification, not the date of the oracle’s foundation or continuous operation. There is no surviving Tiresian oracle archive comparable to the lead tablets from Dodona. **[E4]**

### Tilphussa

Strabo and Pausanias associate Tiresias with Tilphussa/Tilphousa in Boeotia. Pausanias says his grave was beside the spring where he died; nearby stood a sanctuary of Praxidike. Strabo locates a sanctuary or memorial of Tiresias and the temple of Tilphoussian Apollo in the same landscape. **[E4: literary topography]**

This is evidence that Roman-period visitors and informants recognized a localized sacred landscape. It is not, by itself, archaeological proof that a Bronze Age or Archaic individual named Tiresias was buried there. No securely published excavated tomb with an identifying inscription has established such a burial. **[E4/E8]**

### Orchomenian oracle and Pelops

Later antiquarian tradition knew an oracle of Tiresias in Boeotia, reportedly consulted by Pelops. The notice is isolated and does not demonstrate a long-running historical institution. **[E4/E5]**

### Ritual dimensions of the *Odyssey*

Odysseus’s trench, libations of milk and honey, wine and water, barley, promises of future sacrifice, and slaughter of a black ewe and ram constitute literary necromancy. Tiresias drinks blood to speak. The episode strongly influenced later representations of summoning the dead, but it is not an instruction manual proven to reflect one particular excavated sanctuary. **[E1 text; E8 ritual interpretation]**

### Hero cult assessment

Ancient literary geography supports:

- a reported grave at Tilphussa;
- a reported Tiresias oracle at Thebes or in Boeotia;
- posthumous prophetic power in epic;
- possible sanctuary language in Strabo.

It does **not** presently support:

- a securely excavated, inscribed heroon;
- a known annual festival exclusively for Tiresias;
- securely attested games in his honour;
- an epigraphically documented priesthood;
- an independently verified relic cult.

Farnell’s older category of “hero cult” can accommodate prophetic dead and claimed graves, but later work by Burkert and Ekroth warns against treating every literary tomb or heroic figure as evidence for a uniform cult institution. **[E8: historiographic classification]**

## 6. Images and material record

The surviving iconography is far thinner than the literary reception.

### Odysseus consulting Tiresias

A Lucanian red-figure calyx-krater attributed to the **Dolon Painter**, about 390–380 BCE, now Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Cabinet des Médailles 422, shows Odysseus at the underworld consultation. Tiresias’s head or shade emerges near the sacrificial pit. The identification rests upon the close correspondence to *Odyssey* 11 and the iconographic catalogues. **[E2: securely ancient object; interpretation well established]**

A small number of other classical underworld scenes have been connected with the episode, but not every bearded head beside Odysseus can be identified as Tiresias without inscription or distinctive context. LIMC’s *Teiresias* entry remains the specialist catalogue to consult. **[E2/E8]**

### Manto and Tiresias

A Hellenistic relief bowl, conventionally dated to the third or second century BCE, depicts a scene derived from Euripides’ *Phoenissae*: Creon supplicates the blind Tiresias, who is guided by Manto. Treccani’s iconographic survey treats this as among the clearest identifications of Manto. **[E3]**

### Visual attributes

Ancient art generally marks Tiresias through age, staff, guidance by a child or Manto, closed or impaired eyes, prophetic context, or emergence from the ground. A blindfold is common in modern design but is not his necessary ancient attribute. **[E2–E3 ancient conventions; E9 modern blindfold]**

## 7. Historical evolution

### About 700 BCE: the privileged dead prophet

Homer presupposes Tiresias’s fame but supplies almost no biography. His defining trait is knowledge that survives death. His role serves the *Odyssey*’s return narrative and extends Odysseus’s story beyond the poem’s conclusion. **[E1]**

### Archaic period: sex transformation and expanding prophecy

The lost *Melampodia* apparently supplied the snake transformation, while Pindar attached Tiresias to Heracles’ infancy. These traditions expand him from underworld consultant into a long-lived Theban interpreter of divine signs. **[E1–E2]**

### Fifth century BCE: civic truth-teller

Athenian tragedy makes Tiresias a recurrent opponent of rulers who mistake authority for knowledge. In Sophocles he confronts Oedipus and Creon; in Euripides he counsels Creon and defends Dionysus before Pentheus. Each confrontation places correct ritual or prophetic knowledge against political anger. **[E2]**

### Hellenistic period: theological biography

Callimachus gives Tiresias childhood, mother, precise occasion of blindness, and a package of Athena’s compensatory gifts. The learned poet explicitly reworks the problem of why a goddess would blind an innocent youth. **[E3]**

### Roman period: metamorphic and encyclopedic Tiresias

Ovid’s concise sequence—snakes, womanhood, sexual arbitration, blindness, prophecy—became the most influential account in Western Europe. Mythographers assembled incompatible traditions rather than erasing them. Seneca and Statius intensified sacrifice and necromancy. **[E3/E5]**

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Scholiasts and paradoxographers preserved lost variants, sometimes turning Tiresias into a catalogue of marvels. Christian authors treated pagan divination as error or demonic practice while still transmitting the stories. **[E5–E6]**

### Medieval West

Dante preserved Tiresias’s fame while reversing the ancient valuation of prophecy: the seer’s attempt to see too far ahead earns grotesquely backward sight in Hell. Ovidian transformation survived through moralized mythography. **[E6]**

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Printed Ovid, restored Greek tragedy, opera, painting, mythographic dictionaries, and classical education circulated two dominant Tiresiases: the sexually transformed arbiter and the blind rebuker of kings. Paintings such as René-Antoine Houasse’s *Minerva and Tiresias* belong to this post-antique visual reconstruction, not ancient iconography. **[E6/E9]**

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Modernism, psychoanalysis, feminism, queer and trans studies, theatre, opera, film, and popular fantasy detached Tiresias from a single Theban plot. The figure became a vehicle for divided consciousness, gender crossing, traumatic witness, and the authority or failure of interpretation. **[E9]**

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **Guillaume Apollinaire, *Les Mamelles de Tirésias***: written initially in 1903, staged and published in 1917. Thérèse becomes Tirésias in a surrealist treatment of gender, reproduction, war, and theatrical experiment. It is a modern transformation, not a retelling of any one ancient source. **[E9]**
- **T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land* (1922):** “I Tiresias, though blind, throbbing between two lives” observes the typist and the “young man carbuncular.” Eliot’s own note calls Tiresias the poem’s most important personage, “uniting all the rest,” and cites Ovid’s sex-change episode. Eliot’s Tiresias combines voyeur, sufferer, prophet, male/female memory, and modern urban witness. **[E9]**
- Eliot’s phrase “old man with wrinkled female breasts” derives its authority from Ovidian dual experience, but the precise modern consciousness that absorbs the poem’s other speakers is Eliot’s invention. **[E9]**
- **Virginia Woolf, *Orlando* (1928):** often compared with Tiresias because its protagonist changes sex, but Tiresias is an interpretive parallel, not a direct character. **[E8/E9]**
- **George Barker, *The True Confession of George Barker* and related modernist poetry:** uses Tiresian prophetic personae within the broader post-Eliot tradition. **[E9]**
- **Kate Tempest, *Hold Your Own* (2014):** a poetry sequence retelling Tiresias through youth, womanhood, manhood, age, and prophecy, explicitly reframing the myth through contemporary gender and desire. **[E9]**

### Opera, ballet, and performance

- **Francis Poulenc, *Les Mamelles de Tirésias***, premiered 1947, adapts Apollinaire’s play. **[E9]**
- **Constant Lambert/Frederick Ashton, *Tiresias***, Festival of Britain, 1951: a ballet centered on Tiresias’s sex transformation; its initial reception was notably hostile. **[E9]**
- **Cassils, *Tiresias* (2010–2013 performances):** uses the myth in durational body art exploring transformation, endurance, visibility, and trans embodiment. **[E9]**

### Film and television

Screen adaptations of the *Odyssey*, *Oedipus*, *Antigone*, and *Hercules* regularly include a blind prophet called Tiresias. Costuming—blindfold, white eyes, hood, staff, disembodied voice—largely follows modern visual shorthand rather than a fixed ancient model. Christopher Lee and other performers have played the role in modern mythological productions. **[E9]**

Bertrand Bonello’s film *Tiresia* (2003) transposes the myth to a contemporary transgender protagonist and a violent modern setting. Its plot is reception, not recovered antiquity. **[E9]**

### Gender and sexuality

Luc Brisson’s *Le mythe de Tirésias* (1976, later revised) assembled the ancient transformation testimonia and became foundational for modern interpretation. Structuralist studies read Tiresias as mediating male/female, sight/blindness, human/divine, and living/dead. **[E8]**

Later feminist and queer criticism objects that ancient male-authored texts often appropriate supposed “female experience” to authorize male pronouncements about women’s pleasure. Trans studies, meanwhile, treats Tiresias as a resource for gender variance while warning against assigning a modern fixed identity retrospectively. These approaches are dated interpretive traditions, not discoveries of an ancient diagnosis. **[E8]**

Freudian psychoanalysis frequently invokes blindness, forbidden sight, castration, sexual difference, and Oedipal knowledge. Tiresias is not central to Freud’s original formulation of the Oedipus complex, however; modern claims that Freud made Tiresias its essential analyst are exaggerations. **[E8/E9]**

### Political and nationalist use

No major, securely documented ancient nationalist cult centered on Tiresias. The figure’s political function in tragedy is internal to civic questions of rule, dissent, ritual pollution, and expert authority. **[E2]**

Modern Greek cultural discourse sometimes treats Tiresias as part of the national classical inheritance, while disability, feminist, queer, and trans communities have reclaimed the figure for different political purposes. These are modern receptions, not evidence of an unbroken identity tradition. The contested-material search found no well-documented extremist appropriation in which Tiresias functions as a major nationalist emblem. **[E9; negative finding]**

### Commercial and technological naming

“Tiresias” is used for typefaces designed for legibility, forecasting systems, cybersecurity software, anomaly detection, artificial-intelligence projects, fictional characters, and commercial divination services. The name signals “seeing despite blindness” or prediction; none of these uses preserves cult or ancient belief. **[E9]**

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Tiresias historical?

No ancient document identifies a verifiable historical Theban diviner behind the stories. No contemporary inscription, administrative record, securely identified grave, or datable personal archive survives. Attempts to extract a Bronze Age priest, transgender ritual specialist, shaman, or historical oracle-founder are modern reconstructions. They may illuminate comparative patterns but cannot be stated as biography. **[E8]**

### Dating and priority

The *Odyssey* is the earliest secure attestation, but it presents Tiresias as already famous. That proves antecedent tradition, not the content of a lost “original myth.” The snake story’s attribution to the *Melampodia* makes it plausibly archaic, but its late scholastic transmission prevents confident reconstruction of its earliest wording. **[E1/E5]**

The Athena-bathing story is first fully extant in Callimachus. It may use older local material, but claims that it is demonstrably the primordial version exceed the evidence. The Zeus-Hera judgment may likewise be older than Ovid because earlier fragments and mythographic testimonia point toward it; Ovid remains the earliest complete surviving literary narrative. **[E3/E8]**

### Unity versus multiple traditions

Older handbooks often harmonized all episodes into one “life”: youth, bath, snakes, womanhood, judgment, tragic service, death, Homeric afterlife. The sources do not provide that seamless sequence. Different authors give incompatible causes of blindness and different divine patrons. Modern source criticism therefore treats “Tiresias” as a traditional role and name repeatedly recomposed across genres. **[E8]**

### Blindness and prophecy

Comparativists have noted blind or physically marked seers across cultures. Burkert and others relate Greek mantics to broader Mediterranean patterns; structuralists analyze blindness as inverse sight. These are explanatory models, not evidence that all blind seers derive from one prehistoric prototype. **[E8]**

### Shamanism

Tiresias’s animal-language, altered sex, underworld knowledge, and posthumous consciousness have encouraged “shamanic” comparisons. The category is disputed: it can obscure the specifically Greek literary and ritual contexts and project a loosely defined pan-cultural institution onto fragmentary evidence. **[E8]**

### Hero cult

Farnell’s early twentieth-century surveys tended to collect literary graves, shrines, and ritual notices within a broad hero-cult category. Later archaeological work, represented by Ekroth and others, distinguishes funerary commemoration, sacrifice to heroes, oracle cult, literary tomb claims, and local topography. For Tiresias, literary testimony is considerably stronger than excavation or epigraphy. **[E8]**

### Oral-formulaic and epic questions

Milman Parry and Albert Lord’s oral-formulaic model changed how Homeric figures are understood: the *Odyssey* is not necessarily the first invention of Tiresias but the earliest surviving monumental crystallization of an oral tradition. The model cannot recover unattested episodes merely because they would “fit” an oral cycle. **[E8]**

Gregory Nagy’s work on heroic poetry and cult emphasizes the evolving multiformity of Greek heroes and the interaction of local traditions with Panhellenic poetry. Applied to Tiresias, this supports attention to variation but does not prove a specific cult behind every Homeric appearance. **[E8]**

### Textual losses

The largest evidentiary gaps are the loss of:

- most archaic Theban epic;
- the *Melampodia*;
- many tragedies about Laius, Oedipus, the Seven, and the Epigoni;
- Hellenistic mythographic works used by “Apollodorus”;
- Sostratus’s original *Tiresias*;
- local inscriptions or structures, if any, from the reported Boeotian sites.

Consequently, late scholia sometimes preserve genuinely old material, but their age does not automatically become the age of every detail they report. **[E5/E8]**

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- That Homer tells the snake transformation: **false**; Homer does not. **[E9]**
- That the *Iliad* presents Tiresias as Achilles’ or Agamemnon’s adviser: **false** in the extant epic. **[E9]**
- That Tiresias lived exactly 175 years: a later arithmetic rationalization of seven generations, not early evidence. **[E5/E9]**
- That he was certainly Manto’s biological mother: unsupported inference. **[E9]**
- That the snakes were a specified modern zoological species: no ancient source establishes one. **[E9]**
- That an excavated grave proves he existed: no such accepted identification exists. **[E9]**
- That ancient Greeks unanimously regarded him as transgender in the modern sense: anachronistic; modern trans readings are legitimate receptions but not ancient terminology. **[E8/E9]**
- That Athena and Hera jointly blinded him on separate occasions in one canonical biography: harmonization of alternatives. **[E9]**
- That “Tiresias” transparently means “he who sees signs”: disputed folk-etymology. **[E8/E9]**
- That continuous modern worship descends demonstrably from an ancient cult: no evidence establishes continuity. Contemporary Hellenic devotional use is modern religious reception. **[E7/E9]**

### Assessment

The securely early Tiresias is narrower than the familiar composite: a dead Theban prophet who retains understanding and advises Odysseus. The archaic transformation tradition is probably old but survives indirectly. Classical tragedy turns him into the authoritative opponent of rulers; Callimachus and Ovid supply the rival biographies that explain blindness and sexual knowledge. The tomb, oracle, and sanctuary belong to documented ancient local tradition, but archaeology and epigraphy do not substantiate a major organized cult. Modern Tiresias—especially Eliot’s divided witness and contemporary gender-variant readings—is a further historical layer, not a corruption to discard and not evidence to project backward.
