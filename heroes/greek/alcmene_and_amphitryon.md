# Alcmene and Amphitryon: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Alcmene is a mortal Argive/Theban heroine, mother of the demigod Heracles, and—at Athens at least—a cult recipient with an altar. Amphitryon is a mortal Perseid hero, exile, war-leader, and dynastic king or prince; some traditions make him the social or putative father of Heracles, but no independent cult for him is securely documented.  
> **Earliest secure attestations:** the Homeric epics, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE: *Iliad* 5.392–397 calls Heracles “the mighty son of Amphitryon,” while *Iliad* 14.323–324 identifies him as Zeus’s son by Alcmene; *Odyssey* 11.266–268 calls Alcmene Amphitryon’s wife and says she bore Heracles after intercourse with Zeus. The contradiction is functional, not necessarily accidental: “son of Amphitryon” can express household and heroic identity while divine paternity supplies exceptional status.

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[Early]** Securely attested Archaic material, principally Homer, Hesiodic poetry, early lyric, and securely dated Archaic art.
- **[Classical]** Fifth- or fourth-century BCE literary or visual evidence.
- **[Regional]** A locally located tradition, tomb, cult, genealogy, or place-memory.
- **[Later canonical]** Hellenistic or Roman-period elaboration that became standard in handbooks and later reception.
- **[Learned systematization]** Mythographic reconciliation, genealogy, scholium, or antiquarian synthesis.
- **[Medieval/early-modern]** Post-antique adaptation before modern scholarship.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent claim unattested in ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Alcmene

Greek **Ἀλκμήνη**, *Alkmḗnē*; Latin **Alcmena**, **Alcmena/Alcumena** in manuscripts of Plautus; French **Alcmène**; German **Alkmene**; Italian **Alcmena**; Spanish **Alcmena**; Portuguese **Alcmena**.

The first element is securely connected with Greek **ἀλκή**, “strength, fighting spirit, defensive prowess.” The second is uncertain. Ancient and modern proposals associate it with **μένος**, “force/spirit,” **μῆνις**, “wrath,” or a suffix rather than an independently meaningful “moon” element. Translations such as “strong in wrath” are plausible interpretive etymologies, not an established ancient gloss. **[Modern reconstruction]**

Her recurrent descriptions are relational rather than cultic epithets:

- “wife of Amphitryon,” *Odyssey* 11.266. **[Early]**
- “daughter of Electryon,” Hesiodic *Shield* 1–4 and the mainstream genealogical tradition. **[Early]**
- mother of Heracles by Zeus, *Iliad* 14.323–324; *Odyssey* 11.266–268. **[Early]**
- “Argive Alcmene,” emphasizing Perseid origin, in Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.276. **[Later canonical]**

She is not a goddess in early poetry. Her altar at Cynosarges and localized tomb or memorial traditions show later **heroic commemoration**, but the evidence does not justify promoting her to a universally worshipped goddess.

### Amphitryon

Greek **Ἀμφιτρύων**, *Amphitrýōn*, genitive **Ἀμφιτρύωνος**; Latin **Amphitruo**; French and English **Amphitryon**; German **Amphitryon**; Italian **Anfitrione**; Spanish **Anfitrión**; Portuguese **Anfitrião**.

The etymology is disputed. The initial **ἀμφί-** is transparent (“on both sides,” “around”), but the second member is not. Proposals from verbs meaning “wear out,” “trouble,” or “rub” have produced glosses such as “harassing on both sides” or “much-troubled”; none is certain. The name is not securely shown to encode his cuckoldry. **[Modern reconstruction]**

His ordinary designations are:

- “son of Alcaeus,” Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.5. **[Later canonical]**
- “Perseid,” by genealogy through Alcaeus and Perseus. **[Learned systematization]**
- “warlike” or “host-gathering,” in the diction surrounding him in the Hesiodic *Shield*. **[Early poetic description, not fixed cult titles]**
- social father of Heracles: Homer’s “son of Amphitryon,” followed by the conventional patronymic **Ἀμφιτρυωνιάδης**, “Amphitryonid,” for Heracles. **[Early]**

After Molière’s *Amphitryon* (1668), French **amphitryon**, Spanish **anfitrión**, and Portuguese **anfitrião** acquired the meaning “host,” from the joke that the “true Amphitryon” is the one at whose house one dines. This is an early-modern lexical afterlife, not an ancient epithet.

### Identity and non-identifications

Neither figure was securely identified through *interpretatio* with a foreign god or historical ruler. Late euhemerizing authors could rationalize Zeus as a mortal king, but that does not amount to a stable identification of Alcmene or Amphitryon with other cult figures. No credible ancient evidence identifies either with a Christian saint. Allegorical equations of Alcmene with the Virgin Mary or Amphitryon with Joseph belong to medieval or later rewriting, not Greek religion.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **Homer, *Iliad* 5.392–397** — late eighth/early seventh century BCE. Heracles is called Amphitryon’s powerful son. It does not narrate the impersonation, twin conception, or marriage. **[Early]**
2. **Homer, *Iliad* 14.323–324** — Zeus lists Alcmene among his mortal partners and calls Heracles their son. No disguise or triple night appears. **[Early]**
3. **Homer, *Iliad* 19.95–133** — Hera delays the birth of Heracles and causes Eurystheus to be born first, defeating Zeus’s promise that the day’s Perseid child would rule. Amphitryon is not central. **[Early]**
4. **Homer, *Odyssey* 11.266–268** — Odysseus sees Alcmene among the women of the dead; she is Amphitryon’s wife and Heracles’ mother by Zeus. This is the earliest explicit conjunction of the marriage and divine paternity. **[Early]**

These passages survive through a long manuscript and papyrus transmission, not in authorial manuscripts. Their composition dates must not be confused with the dates of surviving copies.

### Archaic and Classical Greek corpus

- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fr. 195 Most / fr. 195 MW, partly incorporated into *Shield of Heracles* 1–56** — probably sixth century BCE in its transmitted form. Alcmene leaves home for Thebes with Amphitryon; he may not consummate the marriage until he avenges her brothers; Zeus desires a son who will defend gods and men and lies with her; Amphitryon returns the same night. The surviving proem calls Alcmene exceptionally beautiful and wise. **[Early]** The text says Zeus made the night long, but the fully explicit “three nights” formulation is later.
- **Hesiodic *Shield* 1–56** — sixth-century BCE poem or redaction, anciently attributed to Hesiod. It preserves the fullest early account of Alcmene’s lineage, Amphitryon’s expedition, and the double union. [Greek and translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0128).
- **Pherecydes of Athens, fragments** — fifth century BCE, preserved chiefly in Homeric scholia and Pseudo-Apollodorus. He treated the expedition, Zeus’s deception, tokens from the war, and—in a distinctive version—Amphitryon placing the snakes near the infants to discover which was his child. **[Classical fragmentary mythography]**
- **Pindar, *Nemean* 1.33–72** — c. 470s BCE. Hera sends two serpents against the infant twins; Heracles strangles them; Alcmene and attendants panic; Amphitryon arrives armed; Teiresias foretells the boy’s career and apotheosis. [Text and translation](https://kosmossociety.org/pindar-nemean-1/). **[Classical]**
- **Pindar, *Nemean* 10.13–18** — 444 BCE or thereabouts. Alcmene’s lineage helps glorify Argos. **[Classical political-genealogical use]**
- **Aeschylus, *Alcmene*** — lost, known only from the title and later lexical testimony; plot unknown. **[Classical, fragmentary]**
- **Sophocles, *Amphitryon*** — lost; only a few fragments survive, TrGF 4 F122–124. The surviving evidence does not permit a secure plot reconstruction. **[Classical, fragmentary]**
- **Euripides, *Alcmene*** — lost, fragments TrGF 5 F87b–104; date unknown. The familiar reconstruction in which Amphitryon tries to burn Alcmene and Zeus saves her with rain is supported by fourth-century South Italian images but is not explicitly preserved in the literary fragments. The reconstruction remains debated. [Discussion of the dispute](https://iris.univr.it/handle/11562/996687). **[Classical evidence; modern reconstruction of plot]**
- **Euripides, *Heracles*** — c. 416 BCE. Amphitryon is an aged resident of Thebes, father in social terms and defender of Heracles’ family against Lycus. He survives long after the period at which other traditions kill him in war. [Loeb record](https://doi.org/10.4159/DLCL.euripides-heracles.1998). **[Classical dramatic variant]**
- **Greek comedy:** Archippus, Plato Comicus, Rhinthon, and others appear to have treated Amphitryonic material, but fragmentary titles and testimonia rarely establish exact plots. **[Classical/Hellenistic fragmentary]**

### Hellenistic and Roman corpus

- **Theocritus, *Idyll* 24, “The Infant Heracles”** — third century BCE. Expands the serpent episode, Alcmene’s fear, Teiresias’s prophecy, and Amphitryon’s paternal education of Heracles; Teiresias predicts that Argive women will honor Alcmene in song or cultic commemoration. [Text](https://www.theoi.com/Text/TheocritusIdylls4.html). **[Hellenistic elaboration]**
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1.118–121, 3.1226 and related allusions** — third century BCE. Iphicles and Heracles are embedded in Argonautic genealogy; Alcmene and Amphitryon are background ancestors rather than protagonists. **[Hellenistic]**
- **Plautus, *Amphitruo*** — c. 190–185 BCE, Latin. The earliest substantially complete dramatic treatment. Jupiter impersonates Amphitruo; Mercury impersonates the slave Sosia; Alcumena is subjected to contradictory evidence about her own sexual and marital experience; Jupiter finally reveals himself and announces Hercules’ birth. Mercury calls the hybrid play a **tragicomoedia**. Plautus’ exact Greek model is unknown; derivation directly from Euripides’ *Alcmene* or from a lost Greek comedy is possible, not demonstrated. [Background and sources](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/plautus-amphitruo/background-and-sources/537F33C0631C3834A7AB2DC6506DE0B2).
- **Diodorus Siculus 4.9** — first century BCE. Gives the triple-length night and a providential account of Zeus begetting a benefactor for humankind. **[Later canonical/rationalizing]**
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 29** — probably first or second century CE in surviving form, though drawing on earlier handbooks. Compresses Amphitryon’s exile, campaign, Zeus’s deception, and the double paternity. Hyginus sometimes makes the hostile people Oechalians rather than Taphians/Teleboans. **[Later canonical variant]**
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.273–323** — 8 CE. An aged Alcmene tells Iole how Juno/Lucina obstructed her labor for seven days until the servant Galanthis deceived the birth-goddess and was transformed into a weasel. [Latin and translation](https://ovid.lib.virginia.edu/trans/Metamorph9.htm). **[Roman elaboration]**
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.4.5–11** — first or second century CE. The standard surviving synthesis: genealogies, killing of Electryon, exile, purification, Taphian expedition, Comaetho’s betrayal, triple night, twins, snakes, and Amphitryon’s death. [2.4.8](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Apollod.+2.4.8&fromdoc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022). **[Learned systematization]**
- **Plutarch, *Life of Lysander* 28; *On the Sign of Socrates* 577e–578b** — early second century CE. Records Haliartian claims about Alcmene’s memorial, Spartan excavation of her alleged tomb, a substitute stone, vessels of compacted earth, and a bronze tablet bearing unreadable or archaic signs. **[Regional antiquarian testimony]**
- **Pausanias 1.19.3; 1.41.1; 5.18.3; 9.11.1; 9.16.7** — c. 150–175 CE. Records Alcmene’s altar at Cynosarges, her Megarian tomb, the Chest of Cypselus, Amphitryon’s alleged Theban house, and the Theban denial of an ordinary tomb for Alcmene. **[Regional antiquarian evidence]**
- **Antoninus Liberalis 29** — second or third century CE, citing Nicander. Calls Alcmene’s helper Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, and makes the Moirai and Eileithyia the obstructing powers; Hecate turns Galinthias into a weasel but later makes her sacred attendant. **[Later canonical variant]**
- **Philostratus the Younger, *Imagines* 5** — third century CE. Describes a painting of the infant Heracles and snakes, following Pindaric motifs: Alcmene rushes forward, Amphitryon comes armed, and Teiresias interprets the event. [Text](https://www.theoi.com/Text/PhilostratusYounger.html). **[Late antique ekphrasis]**
- **Scholia to *Iliad* 14.323 and *Odyssey* 11.266** — compiled over centuries, substantially Byzantine in surviving manuscripts. Preserve otherwise lost Pherecydean and other variants, including Zeus ordering Helios not to rise and the differing war-tokens carried by the disguised god. **[Learned systematization preserving earlier fragments]**
- **Tzetzes, *Chiliades* 2.36** — twelfth century CE. Euhemerizes Zeus as a mortal king or astrologer. [Text](https://www.theoi.com/Text/TzetzesChiliades2.html). **[Medieval learned rationalization]**

## 3. The story and its variants

### 3.1 Genealogy and marriage

The mainstream genealogy makes Alcmene daughter of **Electryon**, son of Perseus, and therefore a Perseid princess of Mycenae. Her mother is variously:

- **Anaxo**, daughter of Alcaeus: Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.5. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Lysidice**, daughter of Pelops: scholia and later genealogical sources. **[Variant]**
- **Eurydice**, daughter of Pelops: Diodorus 4.9.1. **[Later canonical variant]**

The early epic opening preserved in the *Shield* names Electryon but not her mother.

Amphitryon is normally son of **Alcaeus**, another son of Perseus. His mother is variously **Astydameia**, **Laonome**, or **Hipponome** in mythographic genealogies. Alcmene and Amphitryon are therefore cousins in the common tree. **[Learned systematization]**

A striking alternative attached to the Chest of Cypselus and attributed to the epic poet **Asius** apparently made an Alcmene daughter of **Amphiaraus and Eriphyle**. Whether this is the same heroine, an artistic misunderstanding, or a homonymous figure cannot be resolved; it should not be silently merged with the Electryon genealogy. **[Archaic regional/genealogical variant]**

### 3.2 Electryon’s death and exile

Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.5–6 gives the canonical sequence:

1. Taphians or Teleboans raid Electryon’s cattle and kill most of his sons.
2. Amphitryon recovers the cattle.
3. Electryon entrusts kingdom and daughter to Amphitryon while preparing vengeance, requiring that Alcmene remain untouched.
4. Amphitryon accidentally kills Electryon when a thrown club rebounds from a cow.
5. Sthenelus expels Amphitryon, Alcmene, and her surviving half-brother Licymnius.
6. They take refuge at Thebes, where Creon purifies Amphitryon.

The *Shield* begins after the exile and emphasizes Alcmene’s refusal of consummation until her brothers are avenged. Later mythographers differ over whether she was already legally married or only betrothed. **[Early core plus later harmonization]**

Some lost or fragmentary traditions apparently made Electryon’s killing intentional or the result of anger rather than accident. Because the witnesses are scholia and late compilations, the intentional version cannot be dated confidently.

### 3.3 The Teumessian fox and the Taphian war

To obtain Theban help, Amphitryon first assists Creon against the **Teumessian fox**, a creature fated never to be caught. He recruits **Cephalus**, owner of **Laelaps**, the hound fated always to catch its prey. Zeus resolves the logical contradiction by petrifying both animals: Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.6–7, with fuller metamorphic treatment in Antoninus Liberalis 41. **[Later canonical synthesis from earlier local myth]**

Amphitryon then attacks the Taphians/Teleboans with allies including Cephalus, Panopeus, Heleus, and Creon. Their king **Pterelaus** is invulnerable while a golden hair given by Poseidon remains on his head. His daughter **Comaetho**, in love with Amphitryon, plucks the hair; Pterelaus dies, and Amphitryon executes Comaetho for betraying her father. Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.7 is the fullest source. The episode combines divinely guaranteed invulnerability, erotic betrayal, and punitive killing. **[Later canonical elaboration; likely based on earlier mythography]**

Hyginus’ substitution of Oechalians for Taphians/Teleboans is a genuine variant or textual confusion, not evidence for an additional securely located campaign.

### 3.4 Zeus’s impersonation and the “triple night”

Early Homer knows intercourse between Zeus and Alcmene but does not say that Zeus impersonated Amphitryon. The *Shield* supplies the structural setting: Zeus joins Alcmene while Amphitryon is away and Amphitryon returns to her afterward. Fifth-century mythography, scholia, and later Pseudo-Apollodorus make the deception explicit.

In Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.8, Zeus:

- assumes Amphitryon’s exact appearance;
- makes one night three times its ordinary length;
- reports the military victory as though he had won it;
- sleeps with Alcmene shortly before the real Amphitryon returns.

Scholia variously say Zeus ordered **Helios** not to rise for one or three days and sometimes mention **Selene** or Night as cooperating. The full cosmic pause is therefore later than Homer. **[Classical/later elaboration]**

The disguised Zeus reportedly presented a captured cup as proof of identity. Lost authors disagreed on its form: **δέπας**, *depas*; **σκύφος**, *skyphos*; or **καρχήσιον**, *karchesion*. The divergent vessel terminology survives through scholia and fragment collections. **[Fragmentary variant]**

Modern language should call the encounter sexual deception or rape by impersonation: Alcmene consents to intercourse with the person she believes to be her husband, not knowingly to Zeus. Ancient sources often celebrate Zeus’s plan or treat the event comically, but they do not supply informed consent to the disguised god. Plautus intensifies the injury by making Alcumena defend her fidelity while divine power controls the evidence.

### 3.5 Recognition, trial, and the pyre

In Pseudo-Apollodorus, Amphitryon notices Alcmene’s cool welcome; she says he returned the previous night, and **Teiresias** explains that Zeus visited her. No trial or attempted execution occurs. **[Later canonical version]**

Plautus dramatizes prolonged accusation, contradictory testimony, divine doubles, and threatened repudiation. Jupiter ultimately discloses the truth. **[Roman comic transformation]**

Two fourth-century BCE South Italian vases show a more violent episode:

- A Paestan red-figure bell-krater signed by **Python**, c. 350–325 BCE, British Museum 1890,0210.1: Alcmene sits on an altar or pyre while Amphitryon and Antenor bring fire; Zeus and a female divine figure appear above; personified clouds or Hyades pour rain beneath a rainbow. [Object description](https://www.theoi.com/Gallery/O2.1.html).
- A Campanian amphora, British Museum F193, depicts a comparable attempted burning.

These images are commonly connected with Euripides’ lost *Alcmene*: Amphitryon condemns his apparently adulterous wife, and Zeus proves her innocence by thunder and rain. Yet the reconstruction depends principally on the vases; surviving Euripidean fragments do not narrate the pyre. R. Engelmann and later scholars accepted a theatrical connection, while R. Aélion and others stressed the silence of the literary witnesses. The correct classification is **[Classical iconographic attestation; disputed dramatic reconstruction]**, not “known plot of Euripides.”

Calling it a formal “trial at Thebes” is likewise partly reconstructive. Thebes is the narrative setting, and accusation and attempted punishment are clear in reception, but no surviving source supplies courtroom procedure.

### 3.6 Twins and delayed birth

Alcmene bears:

- **Heracles**, son of Zeus;
- **Iphicles**, son of Amphitryon.

Pseudo-Apollodorus calls Heracles one night older; Plautus makes the birth climactic; Pindar presents them together in swaddling clothes. This is mythic double paternity, often compared by modern writers with heteropaternal superfecundation, but the medical comparison is explanatory analogy, not ancient evidence.

In *Iliad* 19, Zeus boasts that the Perseid born that day will rule. Hera extracts an oath, hastens the seven-month birth of **Eurystheus**, and restrains Alcmene’s labor, transferring sovereignty to Eurystheus. This is the earliest birth-obstruction version. **[Early]**

Pausanias 9.11.3 names **Historis**, daughter of Teiresias, as the woman who falsely announces the birth and tricks Hera’s agents, called the **Pharmacides**. Ovid instead names **Galanthis**, has Lucina physically bind the birth by crossed limbs and fingers, and makes Alcmene labor seven days and nights. Antoninus Liberalis calls the helper **Galinthias**, makes her a daughter of Proetus, and adds the Moirai and Hecate. These are related but non-identical traditions. **[Regional and Roman variants]**

### 3.7 The serpents

Pindar *Nemean* 1 supplies the earliest full surviving account: Hera sends two serpents; infant Heracles strangles them while Iphicles is present; Alcmene rushes from bed; Amphitryon arrives with a sword; Teiresias prophesies.

Pseudo-Apollodorus follows the Hera version but also preserves Pherecydes’ alternative: **Amphitryon himself** put the snakes in the infants’ bed as a paternity test; Iphicles fled and Heracles stood firm. **[Classical mythographic variant]**

This difference changes the ethical meaning sharply. In Pindar, Amphitryon is a terrified protector; in Pherecydes, he deliberately endangers both infants to establish biological identity.

### 3.8 Parenting and Heracles’ adulthood

Theocritus 24 makes Amphitryon a benevolent instructor who teaches Heracles chariot driving. Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.9 distributes Heracles’ education among Amphitryon, Autolycus, Eurytus, Castor, and Linus. **[Hellenistic/later systematization]**

Euripides’ *Heracles* gives Amphitryon his most substantial tragic role. During Heracles’ absence, the usurper **Lycus** prepares to kill Amphitryon, Megara, and the children. Heracles returns and kills Lycus but, driven mad by Hera and Lyssa, kills his own family. Amphitryon survives because Athena strikes Heracles with a stone and renders him unconscious. Amphitryon then reveals the killings to him. **[Classical dramatic tradition]**

### 3.9 Amphitryon’s death

Competing traditions cannot be harmonized:

- **Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.11:** Amphitryon dies fighting beside Heracles against the Minyans of Orchomenus. **[Later canonical]**
- **Pausanias 1.41.1:** Amphitryon has a tomb at Thebes. This locates the dead hero but does not narrate the death. **[Regional]**
- **Euripides, *Heracles*:** he remains alive and elderly after Heracles’ labors and family catastrophe. **[Classical dramatic variant]**
- Later genealogies allow Alcmene to marry Rhadamanthys “after Amphitryon’s death,” without specifying how he died.

No ancient source provides a securely historical burial or skeleton identifiable as Amphitryon.

### 3.10 Alcmene after Heracles

The tradition supplies several mutually exclusive endings:

1. **Megarian death and burial:** she dies while traveling from Argos to Thebes. Heracleidae dispute whether to bury her at Argos or Thebes; Delphi commands burial at Megara. Pausanias 1.41.1 reports seeing the claimed tomb. [Text](https://periegesis.abm.uu.se/en/reports.php?reportid=41). **[Regional]**
2. **Theban transformation/substitution:** Pausanias 9.16.7 says Thebans had no tomb because she was transformed from a woman into stone; he explicitly notes Megarian disagreement. [Greek and translation](https://pausanias.symmachus.org/skepticism/9_16.html). **[Regional]**
3. **Haliartian burial:** Plutarch, *Lysander* 28, locates a memorial near the tomb of Rhadamanthys at Haliartus and says she was buried there after living with Rhadamanthys. [Text](https://lexundria.com/plut_lys/28/prr). **[Regional antiquarian]**
4. **Removal to the Isles of the Blessed:** later mythographic and scholial traditions say Hermes, at Zeus’s command, substituted a stone for her body and transported her to the Blessed Isles, where she married Rhadamanthys. The Heracleidae discover the stone and establish it in a sacred grove. **[Later canonical/apotheosis-like variant]**
5. **Marriage during mortal life:** Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.11 places Rhadamanthys in Boeotia and has Alcmene marry him after Amphitryon dies. **[Learned systematization]**
6. **Eurystheus’ severed head:** Euripides’ *Heracleidae* and later tradition place Alcmene among the Heracleidae after Eurystheus’ defeat. In Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.8.1, Hyllus sends Eurystheus’ head to her and she gouges out its eyes with weaving pins. The mutilation is vengeance for the persecution of Heracles and his descendants. **[Classical/later canonical]**

## 4. Relations with gods and heroes

### Genealogical schemes

**Mainstream Alcmene line**

Perseus + Andromeda  
→ Electryon + Anaxo/Lysidice/Eurydice  
→ Alcmene  
→ by Zeus: Heracles  
→ by Amphitryon: Iphicles  
→ in some late sources: Laonome, daughter of Amphitryon and Alcmene

**Mainstream Amphitryon line**

Perseus + Andromeda  
→ Alcaeus + Astydameia/Laonome/Hipponome  
→ Amphitryon  
→ by Alcmene: Iphicles; socially Heracles

**Asius-related alternative**

Amphiaraus + Eriphyle  
→ “Alcmene”  
The identification with Heracles’ mother is uncertain.

## 5. Images, cult, tomb, and ritual

### Images

The earliest visual history is less certain than the textual history because many archaic scenes lack labels.

- **Chest of Cypselus**, traditionally early sixth century BCE, known only through Pausanias 5.17–19: its genealogical women included an Alcmene associated with Asius’s unusual genealogy. The monument itself is lost. **[Archaic monument known from Roman-period description]**
- **Infant Heracles and the snakes:** numerous late-sixth- and fifth-century BCE vases show Alcmene, Amphitryon, Athena, attendants, or Teiresias around the children. Attribution of unlabeled figures must be made scene by scene. **[Archaic/Classical iconography]**
- **Alcmene hydriai:** late-sixth-century Attic black-figure works helped establish the infant-serpent scene. Modern reassessment of one important hydria has revised painter attributions and workshop relationships. [Hesperia study](https://www.ascsa.edu.gr/publications/hesperia/article/73/3/427-463).
- **Python krater:** secure because figures are named and the painter signed **ΠΥΘΩΝ ΕΓΡΑΦΕ**, “Python painted [it].” It documents a pyre-and-rain story by c. 350–325 BCE, even if its dependence on Euripides remains uncertain. [Gantz image and discussion](https://open.online.uga.edu/gantzmythsources/chapter/p-377/).
- **Campanian amphora F193:** fourth century BCE; comparable rescue at the pyre.
- **Comic phlyax-type imagery:** South Italian images of Zeus and Hermes approaching Alcmene in theatrical costume may attest parody or stage reception rather than direct illustration of Plautus.

The relevant LIMC entries are **“Alkmene”** and **“Amphitryon.”** Their catalogue numbers should be checked against the physical museum records and Beazley Archive rather than treated as self-explanatory evidence.

### Alcmene’s cult

#### Cynosarges, Athens

Pausanias 1.19.3 saw a sanctuary of Heracles at Cynosarges with altars to Heracles, Hebe, Alcmene, and Iolaus. [Text](https://pausanias.symmachus.org/sentences/1_19.html). This is the clearest direct evidence that Alcmene received cultic honor. It does not establish a separate temple, priesthood, festival, or sacrificial calendar devoted exclusively to her.

The arrangement integrates Heracles’ mother into his cult-family. The archaeological identification and chronology of each altar remain uncertain. **[Literarily attested cult; limited archaeological confirmation]**

Evidence from Attic priesthoods and calendars also links **Hebe and Alcmene** within Heraclean cult, but it does not demonstrate a panhellenic Alcmene cult.

#### Thebes

Pausanias 9.11.1 saw ruins said to be Amphitryon’s house and a bridal chamber supposedly built by Trophonius and Agamedes, complete with an inscribed hexameter. This is place-memory and antiquarian attribution, not proof of a Bronze Age owner. [Passage](https://pausanias.symmachus.org/mythic/9_11.html).

At 9.16.7 he says Thebes possessed **no tomb of Alcmene**: Thebans claimed she became a stone. This absence is an important finding. A Theban heroon is mentioned in later accounts of the substitute stone, but archaeology has not securely identified it.

Pausanias 1.41.1 nevertheless says Amphitryon was buried at Thebes. No independently verified tomb or cult installation is known.

#### Megara

Pausanias 1.41.1 saw or was shown Alcmene’s tomb near the Olympieum. The Megarian story included a Delphic oracle selecting Megara over Argos and Thebes. This is a typical political localization of heroic remains: possessing the tomb inserted Megara into the Heraclid landscape. **[Regional literary attestation; archaeology not securely identified]**

#### Haliartus

Plutarch records a competing Alcmene monument near Rhadamanthys’ tomb. In *On the Sign of Socrates*, the Spartan king Agesilaus is said to have opened it in the fourth century BCE. No bones were present. The excavators reportedly found:

- a large stone;
- a small bronze bracelet;
- two clay vessels containing earth hardened into a solid mass;
- a bronze tablet bearing strange, archaic-looking characters.

The tablet was allegedly sent to Egypt for decipherment. [Plutarch passage](https://viachrista.org/Library/Plutarch_SocratesGenius.html).

This is a second-century CE literary report about an alleged fourth-century BCE excavation, not a surviving archaeological dossier. The stone coheres with the body-substitution myth, but one cannot infer that the grave truly belonged to Alcmene. The story also says the Haliartians associated subsequent crop failure and flooding with disturbance of the tomb—evidence for belief in the dangerous agency of heroic remains, if the report is accepted.

### Amphitryon’s cult

No secure sanctuary, altar, priesthood, festival, or sacrifice dedicated specifically to Amphitryon has been identified. His Theban tomb and house are memorial claims, not proof of continuing sacrificial cult. Farnell’s broad category of heroic cult should therefore not be applied automatically: **tomb tradition is not synonymous with archaeologically demonstrated cult**.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Late eighth–sixth centuries BCE: genealogical emergence

Homer already holds together two identities for Heracles: son of Zeus and “son of Amphitryon.” Alcmene is the hinge between divine paternity and a legitimate Perseid household. The *Shield/Catalogue* expands this into an ethical and dynastic story: the exceptionally worthy woman, the avenging husband, Zeus’s plan, and the nearly simultaneous unions. **[Early]**

### Fifth century BCE: prediction, danger, and dramatic conflict

Pindar turns the household into the first arena of Heracles’ heroism. Teiresias reads the infant’s victory as a compressed biography of future monsters, rewards, and immortality. Tragedians made Alcmene and Amphitryon title figures, but their plays are almost wholly lost. Euripides’ extant *Heracles* transforms Amphitryon into the vulnerable elderly father who preserves memory while divine violence destroys the household. **[Classical]**

### Fourth century BCE: violent visual reworking

The South Italian pyre vases present Alcmene as a threatened suppliant and Amphitryon as would-be executioner. Divine rain replaces human adjudication. Whether Euripides invented this plot remains disputed, but the visual story itself is secure. **[Classical iconography]**

### Hellenistic period: domestic and pedagogical elaboration

Theocritus develops the household—nurses, terror, prophecy, and paternal education. Alcmene becomes a woman whose name will be sung by later women, while Amphitryon becomes a trainer and socially meaningful father. **[Hellenistic]**

### Roman Republic and Empire: comedy, metamorphosis, and systematization

Plautus makes divine impersonation a comedy of unstable identity, but the humor depends on coercion, threatened divorce, and the incapacity of mortals to establish truth against gods. Ovid shifts attention to female memory, obstructed childbirth, and Galanthis’ bodily punishment. Diodorus, Hyginus, and Pseudo-Apollodorus stabilize the campaign, triple night, twins, and genealogy into the form most later handbooks reproduce. Pausanias and Plutarch document incompatible local tombs and relic traditions. **[Later canonical/regional]**

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Mythographers, scholiasts, lexica, and Tzetzes preserve fragments while rationalizing divine events. Zeus may become a mortal king or astrologer; the supernatural story is not erased but reclassified as concealed history. **[Learned/medieval]**

### Medieval Western Europe

Vitalis of Blois’s Latin elegiac comedy **Geta**, c. 1150–1160, loosely adapts Plautus and makes the servant-double increasingly central. [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/vitale-di-blois_%28Enciclopedia-Italiana%29/). Christianizing analogies sometimes recast the miraculous conception through Joseph-and-Mary imagery; these are adaptations, not ancient variants.

### Early modern Europe

The Amphitryon plot becomes a major vehicle for court comedy, marital skepticism, sovereignty, and theatrical doubling:

- Jean de Rotrou, *Les Sosies*, 1636.
- Molière, *Amphitryon*, first performed 13 January 1668.
- John Dryden, *Amphitryon; or, The Two Sosias*, 1690, with music by Henry Purcell.
- António José da Silva, *Anfitrião ou Júpiter e Alcmena*, 1736, adding Juno and Iris to multiply the identity plot. [Study](https://revistas.usp.br/desassossego/en/article/view/176343).

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

- Heinrich von Kleist, *Amphitryon*, published and performed in 1807: a tragicomic reworking of Molière concerned with religious experience, identity, and Alcmene’s inability to distinguish husband from god.
- Jean Giraudoux, *Amphitryon 38*, first performed 8 November 1929 at the Comédie des Champs-Élysées. The “38” is a playful approximation of prior dramatic versions, not a verified census. [BnF record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb123662455.public).
- Georg Kaiser, *Zweimal Amphitryon*, 1944.
- Cole Porter, *Out of This World*, 1950, a Broadway musical derived from the Amphitryon complex.
- Peter Hacks, *Amphitryon*, 1968, a socialist-era German reworking.

### Present

The pair persists principally through theatrical adaptation, Heracles retellings, opera, television, comics, games, and modern discussion of reproductive deception. Alcmene is increasingly interpreted as a victim whose experience ancient male-authored comedy subordinates to divine and marital authority. This ethical framing is modern, but the deception and her lack of knowledge are explicit in the ancient narratives.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Theatre, opera, and literature

Plautus’ version remains the generative center of the European tradition. Molière adds **Cléanthis**, Sosie’s wife, creating a lower-status marital mirror of Alcmene and Amphitryon. The familiar Cléanthis–Sosie subplot is therefore **[early-modern invention]**, not Greek myth.

Kleist intensifies Alcmene’s spiritual bewilderment; the famous final “Ach!” resists comic closure. Giraudoux makes Alcmene more independent and turns divine desire into a negotiation with mortal fidelity. His title’s “thirty-eighth” is deliberately witty, not bibliographically exact: scholarship has counted more than thirty-seven predecessors. [Survey of European reception](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bude_0004-5527_1954_num_1_3_4625).

Operatic and musical treatments include:

- John Eccles, *The Judgment of Paris*–era incidental settings associated with Dryden’s *Amphitryon* tradition, late seventeenth century.
- André Destouches, *Amphitryon*, 1712.
- Various nineteenth- and twentieth-century operettas and stage scores.
- Cole Porter’s *Out of This World*, 1950.

### Painting and print

Renaissance and Baroque artists generally favored “Jupiter and Alcmene,” often eroticizing the encounter while minimizing impersonation. Illustrated Ovid editions frequently depict Alcmene’s labor and Galanthis’ transformation. Such images derive from Ovid, not from the Homeric account.

### Psychology and comparative mythology

- **Otto Rank**, *The Myth of the Birth of the Hero* (1909), placed Heracles within a comparative heroic-birth pattern. Alcmene and Amphitryon become parental roles in a schema; this is a psychoanalytic construction, not evidence for historical ritual.
- **Lord Raglan**, *The Hero* (1936), scored Heracles against a ritual-hero pattern. Numerical patterning is an analytic device and has been criticized for selective coding.
- **Joseph Campbell**, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), popularized a monomyth. The Alcmene–Amphitryon material does not itself supply Campbell’s complete sequence, and using it as proof of a universal archetype is circular.
- **Georges Dumézil’s** trifunctional Indo-European model has occasionally been brought to Heraclean genealogy, but no persuasive consensus makes Alcmene or Amphitryon survivals of a specific Proto-Indo-European deity.
- **Structuralist readings** emphasize doubles, legitimate/illegitimate paternity, household/outsider, mortal/divine, and truth controlled by hierarchy.
- **Gender and performance scholarship** examines how Alcumena’s fidelity becomes comic material and how divine and male authority override her testimony. Recent work rejects a simple choice between “tragic Alcmene” and “comic Alcmene”; the play simultaneously produces humiliation, sympathy, and laughter. [Reassessment](https://www.aup-online.com/content/journals/10.5117/LAM2019.2.005.MARC).

### Political and nationalist appropriation

Heracles has been widely appropriated by dynasties, states, nationalist movements, and extremist imagery, but a dedicated search found **no comparably substantial, documented nationalist or extremist cult of Alcmene and Amphitryon as a pair**. Molière’s play has sometimes been read as flattering Louis XIV or alluding to his relationship with Madame de Montespan, but that “key” interpretation remains disputed and can flatten the play’s broader politics of identity and power. [Discussion](https://earlymodernfrance.org/journal/2015-volume-xvi-1/ruse-nom-machination-rhetorique-amphitryon-moliere).

Absence of evidence here should not be replaced with generic claims about “Greek values” or modern ethnic ancestry.

### Modern inventions and recurrent errors

- “Alcmene was a moon goddess”: not securely attested; generally an etymological or comparativist speculation.
- “The triple night is in Homer”: false. Homer names the union; the prolonged or tripled night is later.
- “Euripides’ surviving play says Amphitryon burned Alcmene”: false. *Alcmene* is lost, and the pyre plot is reconstructed largely from vases.
- “Archaeologists found Alcmene’s body at Haliartus”: false. Plutarch reports an ancient opening in which no body was found; the objects themselves are not extant or securely excavated.
- “Alcmene knowingly chose Zeus over Amphitryon”: contradicted by the impersonation plot.
- “Amphitryon is Heracles’ biological father in Homer, while Zeus’s paternity is later”: false. Both formulations already occur in Homer.
- “Giraudoux proved there were exactly thirty-seven earlier Amphitryons”: false; the numeral is playful.
- “Alcmene and Amphitryon were historical Mycenaean rulers”: unsupported. No Linear B document securely names either as a historical individual.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

No inscription, administrative tablet, burial, or contemporary record verifies historical individuals behind either figure. Their Perseid geography—Mycenae, Tiryns, Midea, Thebes—preserves memories of important Bronze Age centers, but converting that geography into personal historicity is methodologically invalid. **[Modern reconstruction rejected as unproved]**

Euhemeristic readings from antiquity through early modern mythography treated Zeus as a king or famous man. They are evidence for reception, not independent evidence for an actual seducer, warlord, or dynastic marriage.

### Dating and formation of the texts

The Homeric references establish that the divine/mortal double paternity was known by the period when the epics took their transmitted monumental form. They do not prove that every later element—triple night, military cup, Sosia, pyre, Galanthis—belonged to an eighth-century story.

The first 56 lines of the *Shield* overlap the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*. Scholars debate their original position, textual reuse, and the date of the *Shield*. The safe conclusion is that they preserve an Archaic poetic treatment, not that “Hesiod” in a biographical sense wrote every line.

### Plautus’ source

The lost Greek model of *Amphitruo* is one of the central controversies:

- direct or indirect dependence on Euripides’ *Alcmene*;
- adaptation of a lost Middle or New Comedy;
- combination of tragic and comic sources;
- substantial Plautine invention.

Because neither Euripides’ play nor a Greek comic model survives, confident source reconstruction exceeds the evidence. Plautus’ own prologue foregrounds generic mixture, but “tragicomedy” does not identify his source.

### The pyre controversy

The Python krater securely proves circulation of the rescue-from-burning story in the fourth century BCE. What remains uncertain is whether:

1. Euripides invented it;
2. his play used an existing tradition;
3. the vases reflect another tragedy;
4. the images combine theatrical and independent iconographic conventions.

The absence of explicit literary narration is significant but cannot erase the visual evidence.

### Hero cult

Older scholarship, including Farnell, tended to gather tombs, altars, relics, and heroic legends into broad cult dossiers. More recent ritual studies, including work by Gunnel Ekroth, insist on separating:

- a claimed tomb;
- sacrifice at a tomb;
- an altar with a named recipient;
- a sanctuary;
- literary commemoration;
- archaeological remains.

Under that stricter standard, Alcmene has secure literary evidence for an altar at Cynosarges, but her tomb cult at Megara or Haliartus is not archaeologically demonstrated. Amphitryon has memorial geography but no securely documented independent cult.

### Oral tradition

The contradictions between Homeric patronymic, divine paternity, regional burials, and later myths are compatible with a long oral and performative tradition, but oral transmission cannot be invoked to date unattested details. “It must have existed earlier” is a hypothesis, not evidence.

### Manuscripts and losses

The most consequential losses are the tragedies named *Alcmene* and *Amphitryon* and the mythographic works of Pherecydes. Their remains survive through quotations in scholia, lexica, Stobaeus, Pollux, Hesychius, and papyrus scraps. Every reconstruction is therefore vulnerable to:

- decontextualized quotations;
- mistaken title assignment;
- contamination among similarly named plays;
- late paraphrase;
- circular use of vase painting to reconstruct drama and reconstructed drama to interpret the vase.

### Discoveries that changed interpretation

- Systematic South Italian vase study made the pyre episode central to reconstruction of Euripides’ *Alcmene*.
- LIMC and the Beazley Archive enabled corpus-level comparison rather than reliance on one famous vase.
- Papyrus fragments improved the text of some lost tragedies, though no discovery has restored a narrative section of *Alcmene*.
- Gender-conscious readings of Plautus shifted attention from Jupiter’s cleverness to Alcumena’s epistemic and sexual violation.
