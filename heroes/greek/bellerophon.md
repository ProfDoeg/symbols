# Bellerophon: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early]** securely attested Archaic material: Homeric/Hesiodic poetry or Archaic visual evidence.
- **[Classical]** fifth–fourth-century BCE literary, artistic, or historical evidence.
- **[Regional]** a geographically local tradition, not necessarily accepted throughout Greece.
- **[Later]** Hellenistic, Roman, or late-antique elaboration.
- **[Systematization]** mythographic synthesis assembled from earlier traditions.
- **[Cult-literary]** an ancient author reports a sanctuary, image, rite, or sacred geography, but archaeology has not independently identified it.
- **[Cult-archaeological]** material evidence independently supports cult activity.
- **[Medieval/early modern]** post-antique reception or allegorization.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** a scholarly inference rather than an ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention]** a popular detail lacking an ancient source, or a modern creative transformation.

## 1. Identification, names, and class

**Class:** Bellerophon is primarily a **mortal heroic prince and monster-slayer**, regarded in genealogy as either the mortal son of Glaucus or the divine son of Poseidon. He becomes a Lycian dynastic ancestor and, in Corinthian evidence, a locally honored hero associated with Pegasus, Athena Chalinitis, and the spring Peirene. Ancient sources do not consistently describe him as an immortal or a god. His posthumous cult is much less securely documented than his presence in local sanctuary imagery.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the extended Bellerophon narrative in *Iliad* 6.152–211, conventionally assigned to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, is the earliest surviving literary account. It already contains Anteia’s false accusation, the “death-bearing signs,” the Chimera, Solymi, Amazons, Lycian ambush, royal marriage and land-grant, three children, and Bellerophon’s final isolation. It does **not** name Pegasus, Athena’s bridle, a flight to Olympus, a fall, a gadfly, or Bellerophon’s death. The approximately contemporary *Theogony* 319–325 explicitly associates Pegasus with “noble Bellerophon” in the killing of the Chimera. Protocorinthian representations of a mounted hero attacking the Chimera appear during the seventh century BCE; a Late Protocorinthian vessel in Boston, approximately 650–625 BCE, shows Bellerophon on winged Pegasus confronting the monster ([Homer, *Iliad* 6](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0134%3Abook%3D6); [Boston 95.10](https://perseus-aa.github.io/items/aa_4934.html)).

### Names and forms

- **Ancient Greek:** Βελλεροφόντης, *Bellerophóntēs*; Doric Βελλεροφόντας, *Bellerophóntas*, in Pindar; shortened Βελλεροφῶν, *Bellerophôn*, is attested in later Greek. The Latin form is *Bellerophon* or *Bellerophontes*. The lexical attestations and dialect forms are collected by [Logeion](https://logeion.uchicago.edu/%CE%92%CE%B5%CE%BB%CE%BB%CE%B5%CF%81%CE%BF%CF%86%CF%8C%CE%BD%CF%84%CE%B7%CF%82).
- **Hippónous/Hipponoos:** Ἱππόνοος, “horse-minded,” “horse-knowing,” or “horse-intelligent,” is presented by later scholiastic tradition as his original name before he acquired “Bellerophon.” **[Later etiological variant]**
- **“Slayer of Belleros”:** ancient and Byzantine etymologizing analyzes the name as Βέλλερος + -φόντης, “killer of Belleros.” The alleged Belleros is otherwise obscure. Scholia, Eustathius, and Tzetzes make Belleros a Corinthian whom the hero killed, but no securely Archaic source preserves this episode. **[Later learned etymology, not secure history]**
- Attempts to derive the first element from Greek words for a missile, dart, or evil are speculative; modern Homeric commentators commonly call the transmitted name’s etymology uncertain. **[Modern reconstruction]**
- The spelling **Bellerophontes** more closely represents the Greek nominative; **Bellerophon** reflects the stem familiar through Latin and English.

### Patronymics and dynastic descriptions

- **Glaucus’ son; Sisyphus’ grandson:** *Iliad* 6.152–155. **[Early]**
- **Poseidon’s son, born to Eurynome in Glaucus’ household:** the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fragment 7 (Berlin Papyrus 7497 + P.Oxy. 421), probably composed in the sixth century BCE though transmitted on later papyri. **[Archaic variant]**
- **“Divine offspring”:** the Lycian king infers in *Iliad* 6.191 that Bellerophon is “the valiant offspring of a god,” without identifying that god. **[Early, deliberately nonspecific]**
- **Corinthian/Ephyrean hero:** Homer locates Sisyphus’ house at Ephyre “in a nook of horse-pasturing Argos,” conventionally understood here as Corinth. Corinth subsequently made Pegasus, Bellerophon, Athena’s bridle, and Peirene prominent civic symbols.
- **Lycian ruler and dynastic ancestor:** after his victories, the Lycian king gives him a daughter, half the royal honor, and an exceptional agricultural estate; his descendant Glaucus fights at Troy. **[Early]**

There is no secure ancient identification of Bellerophon with Perseus, Saint George, or another named hero. Comparisons with Perseus and mounted Christian dragon-slayers are typological or iconographic, not ancient statements that the figures were identical.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Archaic evidence

1. **Homer, *Iliad* 6.119–236, especially 152–211**  
   **Language:** epic Greek.  
   **Composition:** commonly late eighth or early seventh century BCE, with continuing debate over oral and textual formation.  
   **Surviving witnesses:** principally medieval manuscripts and papyri many centuries later.  
   **Role:** Glaucus tells Diomedes his lineage. Bellerophon is a virtuous ancestor victimized by Anteia, sent to Lycia with lethal written signs, victorious over the Chimera, Solymi, Amazons, and an ambush, then honored by the Lycian king. Later, “hated by all the gods,” he wanders alone on the Aleian plain.  
   **Limits:** no Pegasus; no named murder by Bellerophon; no Athena; no Olympus flight; no cause for divine hatred; no death.

2. **Homer, *Iliad* 16.327–329**  
   Glaucus son of Hippolochus remains part of Bellerophon’s Lycian line. This does not add another episode from Bellerophon’s own story.

3. **Hesiod, *Theogony* 319–325**  
   **Composition:** approximately 700 BCE.  
   Echidna and Typhon produce the fire-breathing Chimera; Pegasus and “noble Bellerophon” kill it. This is the earliest surviving explicit literary conjunction of Bellerophon, Pegasus, and the Chimera.

4. **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fr. 7 M–W/Most**  
   **Composition:** probably sixth century BCE; anciently attributed to Hesiod.  
   **Witnesses:** Berlin Papyrus 7497 and P.Oxy. 421.  
   Eurynome, daughter of Nisus, is sought by Glaucus, but Zeus ordains that Sisyphus’ son will not have children by one father; Eurynome bears Bellerophon to Poseidon in Glaucus’ house. Athena had instructed Eurynome in skill and wisdom. This establishes the divine-paternity variant independently of later mythographers ([Hesiodic fragments](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodCatalogues.html)).

5. **Archaic visual corpus**  
   Protocorinthian pottery of the seventh century BCE depicts the mounted hero attacking the Chimera. The Boston vessel is securely assigned to Late Protocorinthian. Other Archaic representations show Bellerophon fighting on foot, demonstrating that Pegasus was not iconographically compulsory in every local or compositional version. J. L. Benson’s study treats the Chimera combat as a major Protocorinthian mythic theme and cautiously relates its exploitation to Corinthian elite or Cypselid identity ([Benson](https://www.ascsa.edu.gr/uploads/media/hesperia/148052.pdf); [Getty vase study](https://www.getty.edu/publications/resources/virtuallibrary/0892361840.pdf)).

### Classical corpus

6. **Pindar, *Olympian* 13.63–92**  
   **Date:** 464 BCE, for Xenophon of Corinth.  
   This is the principal Corinthian version of Pegasus’ taming. Bellerophon longs to capture Pegasus at Peirene. The seer Polyeidus tells him to sleep at Athena’s altar; Athena appears in a dream and gives him the golden bridle. He establishes an altar to Athena Hippia and sacrifices to Poseidon. He then defeats the Amazons, Chimera, and Solymi from Pegasus’ back. Pindar refuses to narrate his doom, while Pegasus gains Zeus’ Olympian stalls ([Pindar, *Olympian* 13](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pindar_and_Anacreon/Pindar/Olympic_Odes/13)).

7. **Pindar, *Isthmian* 7.44–48**  
   **Date:** probably mid-fifth century BCE, often placed around 454 BCE.  
   Pegasus throws Bellerophon to earth when he seeks the heavenly dwellings and Zeus’ company. This is the earliest surviving explicit testimony to the attempted ascent and fall. It does not mention a gadfly.

8. **Euripides, *Stheneboea***  
   **Date:** before 429 BCE has been proposed; certainly a fifth-century play, produced no later than about 422 on common reconstructions.  
   **State:** lost except for fragments, testimonia, and a Byzantine-preserved hypothesis.  
   Stheneboea falsely accuses Bellerophon after he rejects her. Following his return from Lycia, he feigns compliance, carries her away on Pegasus, and throws her into the sea near Melos; fishermen return her body to Tiryns. This revenge and her mode of death are not in Homer. The reconstructed plot is unusually well supported by the hypothesis, although details of staging remain debated ([Euripides, *Selected Fragmentary Plays*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1228h21)).

9. **Euripides, *Bellerophon***  
   **Date:** approximately 430 BCE.  
   **State:** about thirty fragments plus very damaged P.Oxy. 3651 and 4017.  
   The hero, ruined and bitter, questions divine justice and attempts to fly upward on Pegasus. The surviving evidence supports the fall as a staged or reported event, but complete plot summaries offered by modern writers remain conjectural. The notorious “there are no gods” reasoning belongs to a dramatic character in a fragmentary tragedy, not automatically to Euripides’ own convictions.

10. **Aristophanes, *Acharnians* 426–429; *Peace* 135–154**  
    **Dates:** 425 and 421 BCE.  
    Aristophanes parodies Euripidean theatrical Bellerophon: Dicaeopolis seeks the hero’s rags, and Trygaeus’ comic airborne journey on a dung beetle evokes the tragic Pegasus ascent. These parodies show that Euripides’ version was recognizable to an Athenian audience.

### Hellenistic and Roman corpus

11. **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica***  
    **Third century BCE.** Bellerophon is peripheral, entering through genealogical and geographic allusion rather than receiving a fresh continuous narrative. Later scholia to Apollonius preserve genealogical mythography, but the poem is not a principal source for his exploits.

12. **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.3.1–2**  
    **Date:** usually first or second century CE; falsely attributed to Apollodorus of Athens.  
    **[Systematization]** Bellerophon, after killing his brother Deliades—or, according to another version, Peiren—is purified by Proetus. Stheneboea propositions him and accuses him when refused. Proetus sends him to Iobates with a written death order. Iobates assigns the Chimera, Solymi, Amazons, and an ambush. Bellerophon kills the Chimera from Pegasus, marries Iobates’ daughter Philonoe, and inherits the kingdom. This combines several previously separate strands ([Apollodoran text](https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0548/tlg001/perseus-eng2/1.9.26-2.3.2)).

13. **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 2.2–4**  
    **Date:** approximately 150–180 CE.  
    At Corinth Pausanias reports a Bellerophon sanctuary near the Craneion, an image of Bellerophon on Pegasus at Peirene, and the sanctuary of Athena Chalinitis, “Bridler,” whose bridle enabled the capture of Pegasus. His account is crucial for Roman-period Corinthian sacred topography, but the particular sanctuary’s archaeological footprint remains unidentified.

14. **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 57**  
    **Date:** text of uncertain authorship and transmission, normally placed under the early Roman Empire, though the surviving compilation is textually unstable.  
    Stheneboea falsely says Bellerophon forced her; he kills the Chimera on Pegasus. Hyginus then says he fell on the Aleian plain and dislocated his hip. Iobates gives him another daughter; Stheneboea kills herself. This blends Homeric wandering, the fall, and a physical injury absent from Homer ([Latin text](https://lingualatina.github.io/texts/browsable/hyginus/57pr/)).

15. **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4.785–786; 5.250–268; 9.646–648**  
    **Date:** completed by 8 CE.  
    Ovid tells of Pegasus’ birth from Medusa’s blood, the Hippocrene spring opened by Pegasus’ hoof, and Pegasus once captured at Peirene. Bellerophon is not the center of an Ovidian continuous narrative. Modern retellings sometimes wrongly transfer Perseus’ aerial adventures or Medusa material to him ([Ovid, book 4](https://ovid.lib.virginia.edu/trans/Metamorph4.htm); [Ovid, book 5](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses5.html)).

16. **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 10.162–166**  
    **Date:** probably third century CE.  
    Refers to a sacred precinct or honor of Bellerophon in Lycia. This is late literary testimony for a Lycian heroic localization, not by itself archaeological proof of a continuously functioning cult.

17. **Scholia, Eustathius, and Tzetzes**  
    **Dates:** scholia are multilayered; Eustathius and Tzetzes are twelfth-century Byzantine authors.  
    They preserve alternative names, a murdered Belleros, explanations of the fall, and other material whose original dates cannot always be recovered. Such notices must not be retrojected into Homer without independent evidence.

### What the *Odyssey* and Epic Cycle do not supply

The *Odyssey* knows Sisyphus but gives no Bellerophon dossier. No surviving secure fragment or Proclean summary of the Epic Cycle supplies a substantial independent Bellerophon cycle comparable to *Iliad* 6. Lost cyclic or genealogical poetry may once have treated him, but reconstruction from silence is unsafe. West’s editions remain important because they establish precisely how little survives.

## 3. The story: episodes and variants

### 3.1 Parentage and birth

**Homeric version [Early]:** Bellerophon is the son of Glaucus, son of Sisyphus. Homer calls him beautiful and manly, and says the gods gave him courage and desirable qualities.

**Hesiodic version [Archaic variant]:** Glaucus seeks Eurynome daughter of Nisus as wife, but Poseidon fathers Bellerophon on her. He is born in Glaucus’ house. This produces a double genealogy: socially Glaucus’ son, biologically Poseidon’s.

**Later synthesis:** mythographers commonly retain both levels, making Glaucus the mortal or putative father and Poseidon the divine father.

There is no early birth narrative involving exposure, miraculous infancy, or Pegasus appearing at his birth.

### 3.2 The killing that precedes exile

Homer says only that Proetus “devised evil” against Bellerophon. He does not explain why the hero was at Proetus’ court.

Pseudo-Apollodorus supplies the blood-guilt:

- Bellerophon killed his brother **Deliades**.  
- Variant: the victim was **Peiren**.  
- Scholiastic and Byzantine versions make the victim **Belleros**, sometimes a Corinthian ruler or citizen, and interpret the hero’s name as “Belleros-killer.”

The sources differ over whether the killing was accidental. No one named Belleros is securely attested in an Archaic account. The proposition that “Bellerophon’s real name was Hipponoos and he was renamed after accidentally killing Belleros” is therefore **[Later learned systematization]**, not an established early myth.

### 3.3 Proetus, Anteia/Stheneboea, and the false accusation

**Earliest account:** *Iliad* 6.157–170. Proetus’ wife is **Anteia**. She desires Bellerophon and invites him to secret intercourse. He refuses because he is “good-minded.” She tells Proetus: “May you die, Proetus, or kill Bellerophon, who tried to lie with me against my will.” Homer does not say she was raped; he states that her accusation was false.

**Later name:** **Stheneboea**, the form used in tragedy and mythography. The equation Anteia = Stheneboea is ancient but represents a name variant.

**Proetus’ response:** unwilling or religiously unable to kill a guest directly, he sends Bellerophon to Lycia carrying folded tablets inscribed with “many life-destroying signs.” The king in Lycia is not named by Homer. Later sources call him **Iobates**.

The tablets are often described as “the first letter” in Greek literature. More precisely, this is one of the earliest Greek poetic references to meaningful written marks; what kind of writing the poet imagined remains debated. It is a death-message motif, not evidence for a historical diplomatic letter.

The narrative belongs to the widespread “Potiphar’s wife” story pattern: a powerful woman propositions a younger man, then falsely accuses him. Similarity does not establish direct borrowing from Genesis or any single Near Eastern tale.

### 3.4 Reception in Lycia and the lethal commission

In Homer, the Lycian king entertains Bellerophon for nine days and sacrifices nine oxen. Only on the tenth day does he ask to see Proetus’ signs. This dramatizes the conflict between hospitality and the message’s command.

The king therefore chooses indirect execution: he orders Bellerophon to kill the Chimera, believing the monster will kill him.

Later mythographers call the king Iobates and make him Stheneboea’s father. Euripidean tragedy appears to have used the same relationship.

### 3.5 Chimera

**Homeric description [Early]:** the Chimera is of divine stock, lion in front, snake behind, goat in the middle, and breathes fire. Bellerophon kills it “trusting in signs of the gods.” Homer neither names Pegasus nor specifies the weapon.

**Hesiodic version [Early]:** Pegasus and Bellerophon kill the Chimera.

**Pindar [Classical]:** Bellerophon fights from Pegasus after Athena supplies the bridle.

**Pseudo-Apollodorus [Systematization]:** Bellerophon rides winged Pegasus and shoots the monster from above.

**Visual variants:** Archaic art normally favors the spear-wielding mounted hero, but some representations put Bellerophon on foot. The popular story that he fixed lead to his spear and melted it in the Chimera’s fire is a late explanatory elaboration, transmitted in scholia and later retellings rather than Homer or Hesiod.

The Chimera’s localization in Lycia, especially the eternally burning terrain later associated with Yanartaş/Chimaera, has encouraged naturalizing interpretations. These are **[Ancient rationalization or modern reconstruction]**, not proof that volcanic gas flames generated the myth.

### 3.6 Solymi

After the Chimera, the Lycian king sends Bellerophon against the **Solymi**. Homer calls this the fiercest battle Bellerophon ever fought. No named Solymian opponent appears in the earliest account. Pindar retains the victory.

Ancient geography associated the Solymi with southwestern Anatolia. Attempts to identify the episode with a particular Bronze Age war or ethnic migration are **[Modern historical reconstruction]**; there is no contemporary Bronze Age document naming Bellerophon or recording this campaign.

### 3.7 Amazons

Homer next says that Bellerophon killed the Amazons, “women equal to men.” No individual Amazon is named. Pindar depicts an aerial attack on the female army from Pegasus.

The episode links a Corinthian-Lycian hero to the broader Greek tradition of eastern heroic campaigns. It is not evidence for a historical war against a documented Amazon polity.

### 3.8 The Lycian ambush

The king’s final attempt is an ambush of the bravest Lycian men. Bellerophon kills every one. The king recognizes divine ancestry or protection and abandons the plot.

This episode appears in Homer and is not merely a late mythographic addition.

### 3.9 Marriage, kingship, and land

**Homer:** the king gives Bellerophon his daughter, half the royal honor, and a privileged **témenos**, an estate of orchard and ploughland. The daughter is unnamed.

**Pseudo-Apollodorus:** she is **Philonoe**; Bellerophon eventually inherits the kingdom.

**Other names:** later genealogical traditions sometimes call the bride Cassandra or Anticleia. These are regional or learned variants, not Homeric names.

The Lycian settlement functions genealogically: Bellerophon becomes ancestor of the Lycian Glaucus who fights at Troy. Homer thus embeds an originally Corinthian/Ephyrean hero in Lycian aristocratic memory.

### 3.10 Children

Homer gives three:

- **Isander**, killed by Ares while fighting the Solymi.
- **Hippolochus**, father of the Trojan-war Glaucus.
- **Laodameia**, who lies with Zeus and bears Sarpedon; Artemis later kills her in anger.

Later genealogies vary the names, especially of the daughter, and redistribute descendants. Homer’s version is the earliest and should not be silently harmonized with later tables.

### 3.11 Guest-friendship with Oeneus

After Bellerophon’s arrival in Greece or before his final exile—Homer does not narrate the occasion—Bellerophon and **Oeneus** of Calydon exchanged guest-gifts: Oeneus gave a purple belt, Bellerophon a double-handled golden cup. Their grandsons Diomedes and Glaucus renew this inherited *xenia* in *Iliad* 6.

This is an indirect but important relation: Bellerophon does not meet Diomedes in the text; their descendants recognize the bond.

### 3.12 Hatred of the gods and the Aleian plain

Homer abruptly says that when Bellerophon became hateful to all the gods, he wandered alone over the Aleian plain, “eating his own heart” and avoiding human paths. No offense is stated. The passage may portray alienation, madness, grief, ritual pollution, or ruined status; the text does not decide.

His son and daughter are then killed by Ares and Artemis. Homer never says Bellerophon died in the plain.

The name *Aleian* was anciently linked with wandering, but geographic identifications ranged across Cilicia and Lycia. Treating any particular site as his historical grave is unjustified.

### 3.13 Attempted flight to Olympus and fall

**Earliest explicit source:** Pindar, *Isthmian* 7.44–48. Pegasus throws Bellerophon when he seeks heaven and Zeus’ company.

**Euripides:** the fragmentary *Bellerophon* dramatized an ascent after the hero’s despair over divine injustice. Its exact ending is uncertain.

**Hyginus:** Bellerophon falls in the Aleian plain and dislocates his hip.

**Later moralization:** pride or *hybris* becomes the standard cause. This interpretation is compatible with Pindar’s warning against excess but is more explicit in later retelling.

**Gadfly:** the widely repeated account that Zeus sent a gadfly to sting Pegasus is not stated by Homer, Hesiod, Pindar’s surviving text, or Apollodorus. It is associated with later scholia and modern myth handbooks. Presenting it as “the ancient Greek version” without qualification is misleading.

**Blindness and thorn bushes:** common modern retellings say the fallen hero became blind after landing in thorns. This is not part of the secure early corpus. It is **[Late or modern accretion]** unless a specific late source is supplied.

### 3.14 Stheneboea’s death

Competing versions are irreconcilable:

- **Euripidean *Stheneboea* [Classical]:** Bellerophon pretends to accept her, carries her on Pegasus, and drops her into the sea near Melos. Her corpse is recovered and returned to Proetus.
- **Hyginus [Later]:** she kills herself upon hearing that Bellerophon has married her sister.
- **Homer [Early]:** says nothing about her fate.

### 3.15 Bellerophon’s death and body

No early source supplies a normal death, burial, relic translation, or securely identifiable tomb. Homer leaves him wandering alive. Pindar gives the fall but not an explicit death. Hyginus gives injury, not death.

Consequently:

- “Bellerophon died from the fall” is an inference or later retelling.
- “He lived blind and crippled until death” is not securely early.
- No archaeologically verified grave contains his body.
- Monuments called the “Tomb of Bellerophon” in Lycia are modern archaeological names based on imagery or local association, not authenticated heroic relics.

## 5. Cult, tomb, sacred landscape, and ritual

### Corinth

#### Sanctuary near the Craneion

Pausanias 2.2.4 reports a **sanctuary of Bellerophon** near Corinth’s Craneion. This is the clearest explicit ancient cult-topographical testimony. Pausanias does not describe its foundation, priesthood, festival, sacrificial calendar, or relics.

**Assessment:** **[Cult-literary]**. The sanctuary is ancient testimony, but no securely identified archaeological building bears an inscription establishing it as Bellerophon’s.

#### Athena Chalinitis

Pausanias 2.4.1 describes a sanctuary of **Athena Chalinitis**, “Athena the Bridler,” explaining that Athena gave Pegasus to Bellerophon after bringing the horse under the bridle. Pindar’s *Olympian* 13 already has Bellerophon establish an altar to Athena Hippia after receiving the bridle.

The sanctuary integrates Bellerophon into Athena’s control of horses, civic technique, and Corinthian prestige. Its archaeological location remains uncertain. A modern study of Corinthian numismatics emphasizes that the sanctuary described by Pausanias has not been confidently identified ([Athena, Peirene and Pegasi](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371686391_Athena_Peirene_and_Pegasi_Myth_and_Identity_in_Corinthian_Numismatics)).

#### Peirene

Pindar localizes Pegasus’ capture at **Peirene**, and Pausanias reports an image of Bellerophon riding Pegasus near the fountain. Peirene was a real and monumentalized Corinthian water source; the myth makes it a place where divine animal, hero, goddess, and civic landscape converge.

The physical fountain is archaeologically attested. The capture of Pegasus there is **[Classical mythic localization]**, not an archaeological event.

#### Coins and civic imagery

Corinth’s silver staters commonly bear Pegasus on the obverse and Athena on the reverse from the Archaic period onward. Bellerophon himself is not required on the coin for the mythic complex to operate: Pegasus and Athena advertised Corinthian identity across the Mediterranean.

The numismatic pairing supports a strong Corinthian Pegasus–Athena tradition. It does not prove that every such coin depicts a specific Bellerophon ritual.

### Lycia

Homer’s narrative gives Bellerophon land and royal status in Lycia; Quintus Smyrnaeus later mentions his sacred precinct there. Lycian funerary monuments, including the Heroon of Trysa and the so-called Bellerophon tomb at Tlos, employ Bellerophon/Chimera imagery.

At Trysa, the imagery helped assimilate a Lycian ruler to Greek heroic prestige; it does not make the buried ruler Bellerophon. The monument dates to approximately 380 BCE and combines Greek mythic scenes with Lycian elite funerary self-presentation ([Heroon of Trysa](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1584/the-heroon-of-trysa-a-lycian-tomb-reappears/)).

The label **“Bellerophon Tomb”** at Tlos derives from its relief of Bellerophon and Pegasus. It is not Bellerophon’s authenticated burial. This is a central contested-material finding.

### No secure bones or relics

Unlike the traditions of Orestes’ bones or Theseus’ translation, no reliable ancient source describes Bellerophon’s remains being discovered, transported, or used politically. No verified grave goods or inscription identify a corpse as his.

### Ritual limits

There is no securely attested panhellenic festival, athletic contest, mystery initiation, oracle, or standardized sacrifice “of Bellerophon.” Farnell’s willingness to include him among cult heroes rests chiefly on Pausanias and local sacred associations. Modern hero-cult scholarship, including work by Antonaccio and Ekroth, warns that an old tomb, a heroic image, a sanctuary report, and actual recurring sacrificial worship are not interchangeable categories.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Phase I: Archaic formation, approximately 750–600 BCE

The earliest evidence already joins two geographies:

- Corinth/Ephyre: Sisyphus, Glaucus, horse culture.
- Lycia: Chimera, Solymi, Amazons, marriage, and dynasty.

Homer presents the moral and dynastic story but omits Pegasus. Hesiod and visual art make Pegasus central to the Chimera combat. The difference may reflect genre, local tradition, or Homeric narrative economy; it should not be converted into a simple chronological claim that “Pegasus was invented later.”

Corinthian pottery exports spread the hero-and-monster image. The myth could serve civic self-definition, but linking particular scenes directly to Cypselid propaganda remains a scholarly hypothesis.

### Phase II: Classical Corinthian and Athenian reshaping, 500–323 BCE

Pindar creates the fullest surviving sacred Corinthian version: desire for Pegasus, Polyeidus, incubation, Athena’s dream epiphany, golden bridle, sacrifice to Poseidon, aerial victories, and a morally dangerous ascent.

Athenian tragedy moves attention from monster-slaying to sexual accusation, revenge, suffering, divine justice, and failed transcendence. Euripides’ *Stheneboea* makes Bellerophon an avenger who kills his accuser; *Bellerophon* turns the broken hero into a critic of divine order. Aristophanes’ parodies show the theatrical Bellerophon was culturally familiar.

### Phase III: Hellenistic and Roman systematization

Mythographers combine homicide, purification, Stheneboea, Iobates, Pegasus, the three tasks, marriage, inheritance, and the fall into a coherent biography-like sequence. This apparent coherence is secondary.

Roman-period art greatly expands Bellerophon-and-Chimera imagery in mosaics, funerary decoration, gems, and luxury objects. The triumphant mounted hunter could signify victory, protection, status, or conquest over evil.

### Phase IV: Late-antique Christian adjacency, third–sixth centuries CE

Bellerophon survives in Christian environments. A fourth-century belt fitting places Bellerophon and the Chimera opposite a Christian marriage image and Christogram ([Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/466253)). Fifth-century ivory panels retain the mounted combat ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1856-0623-2)).

Methodius of Olympus interpreted the Chimera in relation to the apocalyptic dragon and Christ’s victory. Modern scholars disagree over whether particular objects Christianize Bellerophon, preserve a prestigious classical hunter, or simply juxtapose inherited and Christian visual languages. “Bellerophon became Christ” is too strong; the evidence is one of analogy and adaptation, not formal syncretistic identity ([Metropolitan Museum symposium](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Age_of_Spirituality_A_Symposium.pdf)).

### Phase V: Byzantine and medieval survival

Byzantine commentators preserve philological and mythographic details, including the Belleros etymology. Mounted hero-versus-monster compositions overlap with the visual development of Christian rider saints. The proposition that Saint George derives directly and exclusively from Bellerophon is unprovable; Bellerophon is one strand in a larger Mediterranean mounted-victor tradition.

### Phase VI: Renaissance and early-modern revival

Humanist mythography and restored classical art revived Bellerophon as a figure of virtuous conquest and, especially, overreaching ambition. Bertoldo di Giovanni’s bronze *Bellerophon Taming Pegasus*, approximately 1481–1482, makes mastery of the horse a Renaissance meditation on heroic power and arrogance ([Kunsthistorisches Museum](https://www.khm.at/en/artworks/bellerophon-taming-pegasus-91603)).

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century operas transformed the dossier into courtly spectacle:

- Francesco Sacrati, *Il Bellerofonte*, Venice, 1642.
- Jean-Baptiste Lully, *Bellérophon*, Paris, 31 January 1679.
- Domènec Terradellas, *Bellerofonte*, London, 1747.
- Francesco Araja, *Bellerofonte*, St Petersburg, 1750.
- Josef Mysliveček, *Il Bellerofonte*, Naples, 1767.

These works freely alter characters, romances, political allegory, and endings. They are reception, not lost ancient variants.

### Phase VII: Nineteenth century to present

The name became a symbol of martial power and high ambition. HMS *Bellerophon*, launched in 1786, fought in major British naval engagements and received Napoleon’s surrender on 15 July 1815. That historical association made “Bellerophon” part of British imperial and Napoleonic memory rather than Greek cult ([Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-14700)).

William Morris’s unfinished or fragmentary *Bellerophon at Argos* reworked the Stheneboea/Potiphar’s-wife pattern in a Victorian literary setting ([William Morris Archive](https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/introduction-bellephronatargos)). Odilon Redon repeatedly treated Pegasus and Bellerophon; his approximately 1888 charcoal makes Pegasus psychologically dominant over the hero ([Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/459400)).

Modern fantasy often transfers Bellerophon’s attributes to Pegasus, Perseus, or an unrelated rider. In the Japanese *Fate* franchise, “Bellerophon” is the name of Medusa/Rider’s magical attack rather than an appearance by the historical Greek mythic hero. This is a **[Modern invention]** produced through associative recombination of Medusa, Pegasus, and Bellerophon.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature, theatre, and music

- Euripides’ fragments strongly influenced modern reconstructions of the hero as an atheist or existential rebel. Because the play is lost, modern complete plots are interpretive creations.
- Early-modern opera makes him a courtly lover and dynastic champion.
- Victorian and later poetry frequently emphasizes either chastity before Stheneboea or *hybris* in the ascent.
- Modern myth handbooks tend to combine Homer, Pindar, Apollodorus, Hyginus, and scholiastic details into one seamless story. This produces the familiar sequence: accidental killing → purification → false accusation → letter → Pegasus at Peirene → Chimera → Amazons → marriage → Olympus → gadfly → blindness. No ancient source contains that entire sequence.

### Visual art

- Archaic pottery: heroic combat and Corinthian identity.
- Classical/Lycian funerary monuments: heroic prestige.
- Roman mosaics: triumph, protection, hunting imagery, elite display.
- Late-antique Christian settings: ambiguous continuity and Christian reinterpretation.
- Renaissance bronze and painting: mastery, ambition, and downfall.
- Rubens and associated seventeenth-century festival imagery: triumph over monstrosity.
- Redon: Pegasus as dream, imagination, and psychic force.

### Psychological and comparative interpretations

- **Otto Rank:** the exposed or endangered hero pattern is only imperfectly applicable because Bellerophon lacks a secure infancy narrative.
- **Lord Raglan:** Bellerophon can be fitted to portions of the ritual-hero schema, but the fit results from selecting among variants.
- **Joseph Campbell:** the Chimera expedition resembles a “road of trials,” but the failed ascent resists a simple triumphant monomyth. Campbell’s schema is a modern comparative abstraction, not an ancient interpretation.
- **Structuralist readings:** commonly oppose Corinth/Lycia, horse/monster, civilization/wilderness, earth/sky, legitimate hospitality/sexual betrayal, and mortal/divine boundaries.
- **Walter Burkert and comparativists:** the false-accusation episode belongs to an internationally distributed tale pattern; motif correspondence alone cannot determine borrowing direction.
- **Gregory Nagy:** reads Bellerophon within Homeric systems of heroic lineage, kleos, social reciprocity, and estrangement. The inherited guest-friendship of Oeneus and Bellerophon is essential to the Diomedes–Glaucus scene.
- Psychoanalytic readings of Pegasus as sublimation, libido, or imaginative power are modern interpretive traditions without ancient evidentiary authority.

### Nationalist and political appropriation

No major, sustained modern nationalist ideology appears to have made Bellerophon its central founding hero in the manner of Heracles, Arminius, or Vercingetorix. His most documented political uses are more limited:

- Corinthian civic identity in antiquity through Pegasus, Athena, and heroic myth.
- Lycian elite funerary appropriation of Greek heroic imagery.
- Court spectacle in early-modern monarchy.
- British naval naming, particularly HMS *Bellerophon* and Napoleon’s surrender.
- General modern Greek heritage and tourism centered on Corinth, Peirene, Lycia, Pegasus, and Chimera.

Claims that Bellerophon was a historical ethnic conqueror, a coded record of Greek colonization, or proof of a national claim over Lycia are modern reconstructions unless independently documented. No well-attested extremist appropriation comparable to modern uses of Spartan or Norse symbols emerged in the reviewed record.

### Common modern inventions or distortions

- **“Zeus’ gadfly is in Pindar.”** False: the surviving Pindaric passage says Pegasus threw him.
- **“He certainly died in the fall.”** Not stated by the early sources.
- **“He was blinded by thorns.”** Late or modern accretion, not Homeric.
- **“Pegasus was given to Perseus and later passed to Bellerophon.”** Ancient accounts of Perseus and Pegasus vary; no principal source narrates a handover between the heroes.
- **“Bellerophon was a demigod in every version.”** Homer calls Glaucus his father; Poseidon’s paternity is a variant.
- **“The Lycian Bellerophon Tomb is his grave.”** Modern archaeological naming, not authentication.
- **“Saint George is Bellerophon under a Christian name.”** Overstated iconographic diffusion theory.
- **“Bellerophon means ‘arrow-slayer’ with certainty.”** The etymology is disputed.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was there a historical Bellerophon?

No contemporary Bronze Age inscription, administrative tablet, royal monument, or datable burial identifies a historical Bellerophon. Linear B records do not securely attest him. The Homeric placement in Lycia and fighting against the Solymi has encouraged attempts to locate a historical conflict behind the myth, but these remain speculative.

Older diffusionist scholarship proposed Semitic, Anatolian, solar, storm-god, or volcanic origins. Such theories often depended on insecure etymologies or one-to-one equations between monsters and natural phenomena. Modern work tends to separate:

1. the circulation of motifs;
2. the construction of a Greek poetic hero;
3. Corinthian civic localization;
4. Lycian adoption of the imagery;
5. the possibility of older oral materials.

The fifth proposition cannot be demonstrated as a historical biography.

### Homer versus Pegasus

Because Homer’s account omits Pegasus, scholars have proposed:

- Homer did not know the horse tradition.
- Homer knew it but omitted it because Glaucus’ genealogy emphasizes human ethics and lineage.
- Pegasus belonged to a Corinthian version that ran alongside an eastern/Lycian version.
- The winged horse entered the story before the surviving texts, as seventh-century iconography and Hesiod imply.

The evidence favors coexistence by the seventh century; it does not prove which version was “original.”

### Date and unity of the Hesiodic *Catalogue*

The *Catalogue of Women* was attributed to Hesiod in antiquity but is commonly dated later than the *Theogony*, often to the sixth century BCE. The Bellerophon fragment’s papyrus witnesses establish genuine ancient poetic material but not eighth-century authorship. Its Poseidon paternity must therefore be called an Archaic variant, not automatically “Hesiod’s older truth.”

### The letter and literacy

The *Iliad*’s “baneful signs” have generated debate about whether the poet imagined alphabetic writing, a sealed pictographic message, or a traditional story motif. The passage proves that meaningful inscribed signs were intelligible to the poem’s audience. It does not securely reconstruct Bronze Age diplomatic literacy.

### Hero cult

Older scholarship often treated every hero’s sanctuary, tomb, or mythic localization as evidence of a standard “hero cult.” Archaeological approaches emphasize that:

- tomb cult is not automatically named hero cult;
- an image of a hero need not be an object of worship;
- Pausanias’ sanctuary reports are valuable but centuries later than the Archaic poems;
- inscriptions, altars, sacrificial remains, and cult regulations are preferable identifiers.

For Bellerophon, the literary case for Corinthian sacred honor is good; the archaeological case for a specifically identified Bellerophon cult installation is limited. The broader archaeology of Corinthian hero cult demonstrates real hero worship in the city but does not automatically assign every heroon to Bellerophon ([Corinthian hero cult](https://shs.cairn.info/journal-etudes-sur-la-mort-2023-1-page-223?lang=en)).

### The *Stheneboea* problem

The lost Euripidean play is better known than many lost tragedies because a hypothesis preserves its broad plot. Yet modern scholars still dispute staging, motive, chronology, and how closely later South Italian vase scenes depend on Euripides. Vase-painting should not be treated as a literal illustrated script unless iconography establishes the connection.

### Euripides’ supposed atheism

Fragments in which Bellerophon questions the gods were already controversial in antiquity. Extracting them as Euripides’ personal creed ignores dramatic context. Conversely, dismissing them as meaningless character speech understates tragedy’s capacity to stage genuine intellectual challenges. The responsible conclusion is that *Bellerophon* dramatized a crisis of theodicy.

### The fall and the gadfly

The ascent is securely Classical. The gadfly is not. It is a paradigmatic example of a late explanatory detail becoming dominant in modern summaries. The secure sequence is:

1. Homer: unexplained divine hatred and wandering.
2. Pindar: attempted ascent; Pegasus throws him.
3. Euripides: despair, divine criticism, ascent/fall.
4. Hyginus: fall and injured hip.
5. Scholia and modern manuals: elaborated divine mechanism, commonly the gadfly.

### Bellerophon and Saint George

Late-antique Bellerophon imagery demonstrably appears in Christian settings. Mounted saints later kill dragons. Formal influence is plausible and sometimes likely, but a single uninterrupted genealogical line cannot be demonstrated for every image. Shared Roman imperial victory iconography, mounted hunting imagery, and biblical dragon symbolism also participate. Getty’s claim that Saint George imagery “probably” originated partly in Bellerophon’s formula should remain a probability, not identity ([Getty, *Chimaera of Arezzo*](https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/chimaera/english.html)).

### State of the record

- **Lost:** the complete Euripidean tragedies, early local poems, most mythographic intermediaries, original cult furnishings.
- **Preserved indirectly:** fragments quoted by grammarians, scholiasts, lexicographers, and papyri.
- **Strongest evidence:** Homeric narrative, Hesiodic references, Pindar, Archaic pottery, Pausanias’ topography, extant Roman and late-antique objects.
- **Weakest recurring claims:** a historical prototype, a verified tomb, the precise etymology, and detailed post-fall biography.
