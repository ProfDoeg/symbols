# Penelope: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

Every substantive item below is marked by evidence class:

- **[E1 securely attested early material]** Archaic Greek evidence, principally the *Odyssey*, or an early independently dated witness.
- **[E2 later canonical elaboration]** Classical, Hellenistic, or Roman literary development that became influential.
- **[E3 regional variant]** A locally attributed genealogy, tomb, ritual, or narrative.
- **[E4 learned systematization]** Mythographers, scholiasts, genealogists, and antiquarians arranging earlier traditions.
- **[E5 syncretistic identification]** Identification or genealogical combination of figures from different traditions.
- **[E6 medieval/early-modern accretion]** Byzantine, medieval, Renaissance, or early-modern reception.
- **[E7 folklore]** Vernacular tradition not demonstrably continuous from antiquity.
- **[E8 modern scholarly reconstruction]** An interpretation or historical model proposed by modern scholars.
- **[E9 modern invention/reinvention]** A modern narrative, artistic, ideological, or commercial construction.

Dates are BCE/CE. “Homer” denotes the poet or poetic tradition responsible for the received epics, not a historically verified individual author.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

**Class:** **mortal epic queen and heroic-age woman; not securely a cult-receiving heroine.** Penelope is the wife of Odysseus, mother of Telemachus, daughter of Icarius, and mistress of the Ithacan household in the *Odyssey*. Her characteristic heroic capacities are endurance, guarded intelligence, testing speech, textile craft, and the preservation of the household during Odysseus’s absence. The later tradition makes her, incompatibly, an adulteress, exile, mother of Pan, second wife of Telegonus, immortalized mortal, or exemplary embodiment of marital fidelity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **the Greek *Odyssey*, conventionally dated to the late eighth or seventh century BCE, with substantial disagreement about composition and textual fixation.** **[E1]** It is the first extant text to name and characterize Penelope. No securely identified Mycenaean Linear B occurrence, Bronze Age inscription, or eighth-century dedication names her. The *Iliad* does not develop or securely attest her as a speaking character. The oldest physical manuscripts of Homer are much later than composition: surviving Homeric papyri begin in the third century BCE, when Alexandrian scholarship was stabilizing the text; roughly 300 medieval manuscripts of the two epics survive from the ninth–fifteenth centuries CE. [University of Chicago Library](https://www.lib.uchicago.edu/collex/exhibits/homer-print-transmission-and-reception-homers-works/homer-print/)

### Names and forms

- **Πηνελόπεια — Pēnelópeia.** Homeric/epic Greek expanded form. **[E1]**
- **Πηνελόπη — Pēnelópē.** Standard Attic and later Greek form. **[E2]**
- **Πενελόπη — Penelópē.** Ancient spelling variant recorded in lexicographical transmission. **[E4]**
- **Penelope.** Latin form, adopted unchanged in English. **[E2/E6]**
- **Pénélope, Penélope, Penelope, Penelopi.** French, Spanish/Portuguese, Italian/German/English, and modern Greek transliterated forms. **[E6/E9]**
- **Uxor Ulixis / coniunx Ulixis**, “wife of Ulysses,” is a recurrent Latin identifying description rather than a proper epithet. **[E2]**

### Etymology

The name’s etymology is unresolved.

1. **Textile explanation:** comparison with Greek **πήνη, pēnē**, “weft/thread on the bobbin,” encouraged interpretations such as “weaver” or “one associated with the web.” **[E4/E8]** It is attractively congruent with the shroud story but may be a secondary folk etymology created from her narrative role.

2. **Bird explanation:** comparison with **πηνέλοψ, pēnélops**, a water bird, conventionally “duck,” “teal,” or “wigeon.” Robert Beekes and other modern etymologists prefer or take seriously this connection; the suffix may be Pre-Greek, while the semantic connection remains uncertain. **[E8]** [Etymonline summary of the dispute](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Penelope)

3. **Ancient etiological legend:** Didymus, preserved through later lexicographical and Byzantine sources, reportedly said that her earlier name was **Ameirákē, Arnákia, or Arnaía**; after Nauplius or her parents threw her into the sea, water birds called *pēnelopes* saved or fed her, producing the name Penelope. **[E4; transmitted late]** This is an etymological birth tale, not early evidence for her childhood. [Smith/Perseus source index](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dpenelope-bio-1)

The often-repeated claim that *Penelope literally means “faithful wife”* is **[E9 modern invention]**. “Faithful wife” is a reception-history meaning, not a linguistic translation.

### Homeric epithets and descriptions

- **περίφρων Πηνελόπεια — períphrōn Pēnelópeia**, “circumspect,” “very thoughtful,” or “prudent Penelope.” **[E1]**
- **ἐχέφρων — ekhéphrōn**, “possessing sense,” “self-controlled,” “prudent.” **[E1]**
- **δῖα γυναικῶν — dîa gynaikôn**, “divine/noble among women.” **[E1]**
- **κούρη Ἰκαρίοιο — koúrē Ikaríoio**, “daughter of Icarius.” **[E1]**
- **ὁμόφρονα**, in the thematic language of **ὁμοφροσύνη, homophrosýnē**, “like-mindedness,” characterizes the ideal concord of husband and wife; its application to Penelope and Odysseus is partly textual and partly a major modern interpretive formulation. **[E1/E8]**

These formulae belong to oral-poetic diction as well as characterization. A repeated epithet is not automatically a psychologically exact description in every occurrence.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The *Odyssey*

**Greek; monumental oral-derived epic, probably late eighth or seventh century BCE; surviving papyri from the third century BCE onward. [E1]**

Penelope appears throughout the Ithacan narrative:

- **Book 1:** descends to the hall, hears Phemius sing the Achaeans’ returns, asks for another song, and is ordered upstairs by Telemachus; Athena begins directing events.
- **Book 2:** Antinous publicly recounts the shroud deception: by day Penelope wove Laertes’s burial cloth, by night she undid it, until a maid betrayed her in the fourth year.
- **Book 4:** grieving for Telemachus, she receives an encouraging dream-image of her sister Iphthime sent by Athena.
- **Books 16–18:** becomes aware that Telemachus has returned; rebukes Antinous for the plot against her son; appears before the suitors after Athena beautifies her and elicits costly gifts from them.
- **Book 19:** interviews the disguised Odysseus, recounts the shroud, describes her dream of twenty geese killed by an eagle, discusses remarriage, and announces the bow contest. Odysseus gives a false Cretan identity but accurately describes her absent husband.
- **Book 21:** fetches Odysseus’s bow and declares the contest: the man who can string it and shoot through twelve axe heads will become her husband. Telemachus orders her from the hall before the slaughter.
- **Book 23:** initially refuses Eurycleia’s claim that Odysseus has returned; observes him cautiously; tests him by ordering their bed moved; recognizes him when he explains that it was built around a living olive trunk.
- **Book 24:** Agamemnon’s shade contrasts Penelope’s fidelity with Clytemnestra’s murder of her husband and predicts enduring poetic fame for Penelope.

The *Odyssey* itself says that Odysseus fought at Troy for ten years and wandered for ten more. Penelope’s “twenty years” therefore means the whole absence, not twenty years spent weaving or resisting suitors. The suitors’ occupation belongs to the closing portion of the absence, while the shroud trick lasted “three years,” being exposed in the fourth. **[E1]**

A convenient public-domain Greek/English text is available through [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136). The bed recognition is at [*Odyssey* 23](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D23).

### The Epic Cycle: *Telegony* or *Thesprotis*

**Lost Greek epic, probably sixth century BCE or later; attributed to Eugammon of Cyrene; known through fragments and Proclus’s late-antique/Byzantine-transmitted summary. [E2]**

After the events of the *Odyssey*, Odysseus travels again, returns, and is unwittingly killed by Telegonus, his son by Circe. Telegonus takes Penelope, Telemachus, and Odysseus’s body to Circe. Circe makes the survivors immortal; **Penelope marries Telegonus**, while Telemachus marries Circe. This is not Homer’s ending and should not be retrojected into the *Odyssey*. [Epic Cycle fragments](https://www.theoi.com/Text/EpicCycle.html)

Pausanias calls a poem **Thesprotis** and says it gave Odysseus and Penelope a further son, **Ptoliporthes**, contradicting the Mantineian story of her expulsion and death. Whether *Thesprotis* was a separate poem or a title/section associated with the *Telegony* is disputed. **[E2/E8]**

### Hesiodic and genealogical poetry

The fragmentary **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women***, assembled between the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, organized heroic genealogies but survives only in papyri and quotations. Material about Icarius’s family is fragmentary and cannot supply a continuous Penelope biography. **[E1/E4]** [Catalogue fragments](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodCatalogues.html)

### Classical lyric, drama, and historiography

- **Pindar and Hecataeus:** later testimonia associate a Penelope with Pan’s parentage, sometimes naming Apollo rather than Hermes. The fragmentary transmission and possible existence of more than one Penelope make confident identification with Odysseus’s wife unsafe. **[E1/E4, disputed]**
- **Herodotus 2.145, c. 430 BCE:** says that, “according to the Greeks,” Pan was the son of Penelope and Hermes, and places him after the Trojan War. This is the earliest securely extant explicit witness to that genealogy. **[E1, but incompatible with Homeric characterization]** [Herodotus 2.145](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hdt.+2.145&fromdoc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126)
- **Attic tragedy:** Penelope and the homecoming were used in lost plays and alluded to in surviving tragedy, but the fragmentary evidence generally does not permit reconstruction of a full independent Penelope drama. Euripides’ *Orestes* 588–90 invokes her fidelity as a moral contrast. **[E2]**
- **Apollonius, *Argonautica*, third century BCE:** belongs to the genealogical world connecting Odysseus’s and Penelope’s families, but Penelope is not an active participant in the Argonautic action. **[E2]**

### Mythography and antiquarian prose

- **Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 3.10.6 and Epitome 7, first–second century CE in received form:** systematizes her parentage, marriage, suitors, and divergent endings. The Epitome records seduction by Antinous, banishment, and the birth of Pan to Hermes at Mantinea. **[E4]** [Apollodorus, Epitome 7.38](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DEpitome%3Abook%3DE%3Achapter%3D7%3Asection%3D38)
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 3.12 and 8.12, c. 150–180 CE:** records a Spartan footrace for Penelope and a Mantineian grave and exile story. **[E3/E4]**
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae*, Roman mythographic handbook, probably compiled in the first centuries CE:** preserves Latin genealogical lists and the Hermes/Mercury–Penelope parentage of Pan. **[E4]**
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 3.56, 45 BCE:** lists one Pan as the child of Mercury and Penelope. **[E4]**
- **Scholia on Homer, Theocritus, and Lycophron; Servius on Virgil; Eustathius and Tzetzes:** preserve otherwise lost variants, including Penelope’s alternate names, Pan’s conception by all the suitors, and competing genealogies. Their testimony is valuable but often centuries later than the works they cite. **[E4/E6]**

### Roman poetry

- **Ovid, *Heroides* 1, c. 20–10 BCE:** Penelope writes to the absent Ulysses, fearing war, shipwreck, and another woman; she asks him not to answer but to come. Ovid turns an epic character into an elegiac first-person speaker and emphasizes aging, loneliness, rumor, and the unequal burdens of war. **[E2]** [Text of *Heroides* 1](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/library/passage/urn%3Acts%3AlatinLit%3Aphi0959.phi002.perseus-eng2%3A1.1-1.53/)
- **Propertius, Ovid’s *Ars amatoria* and *Tristia*, and later Latin poets:** use Penelope as shorthand for chastity, waiting, weaving, or—sometimes satirically—weariness with prolonged fidelity. **[E2]**

## 3. The story and its variants

### Parentage and childhood

**Homeric minimum:** Penelope is the daughter of **Icarius** and has a sister **Iphthime**; Homer does not name her mother. **[E1]**

Competing genealogies:

| Source/tradition | Father | Mother | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Odyssey* | Icarius | Unnamed | **[E1]** |
| Apollodorus 3.10.6 | Icarius | Periboea, a naiad | **[E4]** |
| Strabo’s genealogical tradition | Icarius | Polycaste, daughter of Lygaeus | **[E4/E3]** |
| Odyssey scholia | Icarius | Asterodia/Asterodeia | **[E4]** |
| Odyssey scholia | Icarius | Dorodoche | **[E4]** |

The drowning-and-birds infancy story occurs only in learned late transmission and is an etymological legend. **[E4/E6]**

### Marriage to Odysseus

Two major traditions coexist:

1. **Tyndareus bargain:** Odysseus solves the dangerous competition for Helen by proposing the oath of her suitors; in exchange Tyndareus helps him obtain Penelope. Apollodorus 3.10.9 provides the familiar systematized form. **[E4]**
2. **Spartan footrace:** Icarius holds a race for Penelope’s suitors; Odysseus wins. Pausanias 3.12.2 records the race and connects it with Sparta’s Aphetaid Road. **[E3]** [Pausanias 3.12](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0160%3Abook%3D3%3Achapter%3D12)

Pausanias elsewhere reports the story that, when Icarius urged his daughter to remain in Sparta, Odysseus made her choose; Penelope silently veiled herself, signifying that she would go with her husband. Icarius dedicated an image of **Aidos**, Modesty. **[E3/E4]** The tale is a later Spartan aetiology, not a Homeric wedding scene.

### Telemachus and the departure for Troy

Penelope and Odysseus have the infant **Telemachus** before the Trojan expedition. In later myth, Odysseus feigns madness to avoid service; Palamedes exposes him by threatening the infant, but Penelope’s participation varies and is not Homeric. **[E2/E4]**

### The absence: ten years at Troy, ten years’ return

The *Odyssey* represents Penelope as inhabiting a crisis of incomplete information. She knows neither that Odysseus is dead nor that he is alive. The suitors consume the household’s livestock and wine, sleep with some enslaved women, pressure Penelope to marry, and plot Telemachus’s murder. Her delay protects the house but cannot continue indefinitely. **[E1]**

### The shroud of Laertes

Antinous tells the assembly that Penelope promised to choose a husband after completing the burial shroud of the still-living **Laertes**, so that he would not lack proper funerary honor. For three years she wove by day and secretly unwove by torchlight; in the fourth, one of her women betrayed the scheme. Penelope repeats the account to the disguised Odysseus. **[E1: *Odyssey* 2.93–110; 19.137–56]**

The object is a **funeral cloth for Laertes**, not Odysseus’s shroud. Calling it “the shroud of Odysseus” is **[E9 modern error]**.

The episode does more than symbolize patience: weaving gives Penelope controlled temporal power inside the household. Modern readings that make weaving a metaphor for oral composition, narrative revision, or female authorship are **[E8]**, insightful but not explicit ancient doctrine.

### Her appearance before the suitors

Athena sends sleep over Penelope and beautifies her. Penelope then rebukes Telemachus and tells the suitors that proper courtship requires gifts rather than consumption of another man’s estate. They give clothing and jewelry; Odysseus, still disguised, is pleased at her success. **[E1: *Odyssey* 18]**

This scene has generated the “Penelope question”: is she knowingly helping Odysseus, testing the suitors, preparing reluctantly to remarry, or acting under incomplete knowledge? The text gives her intelligence but withholds decisive access to her recognition state. **[E8]**

### Interview with the stranger and the dream

In Book 19, Penelope questions the disguised Odysseus. His false autobiography embeds accurate knowledge of Odysseus’s clothing and companions, making her weep. She recounts a dream: twenty geese feed in her court; an eagle kills them and declares itself her husband, the geese the suitors. Though the dream interprets itself, Penelope invokes the gates of horn and ivory and refuses certainty. **[E1]**

Claims that she fully recognizes Odysseus during this interview divide scholarship:

- **Late/full recognition:** she remains uncertain until the bed test in Book 23; this follows the narrative’s explicit surface. **[E8, majority position]**
- **Early or gradual recognition:** she begins to recognize him in Book 19 and coordinates the bow test indirectly. **[E8]**
- **Analytic/interpolation theories:** apparent inconsistencies arose through combination of variant compositional layers. **[E8]**

The debate began in antiquity and remains unresolved; modern narratological analysis warns against turning textual ambiguity into unreported subconscious certainty. [Cambridge discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-classical-journal/article/homeric-motivation-and-modern-narratology-the-case-of-penelope/CFF77EABE334691D70FDC9FACB997955), [recognition study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8A2CBCC3FF8DADFFA62F59F884080F1E/S0075426922000040a.pdf/recognizing-odysseus-reading-penelope-the-anagnorisis-in-the-23rd-book-of-the-odyssey.pdf)

### The bow and twelve axes

Penelope retrieves Odysseus’s bow and proposes marriage to whoever can string it and send an arrow through twelve axe heads. Telemachus nearly strings it before stopping; the suitors fail; the disguised Odysseus succeeds and begins the killing. **[E1: *Odyssey* 21–22]**

Penelope does **not** witness the massacre: Telemachus has sent her upstairs, and Athena keeps her asleep. **[E1]** Modern films and retellings that make her an archer or active participant in the slaughter are **[E9]** unless explicitly presented as adaptation.

### The olive-tree bed and recognition

After the suitors’ deaths, Penelope remains cautious. She instructs Eurycleia to move the marital bed outside the chamber. Odysseus protests: he built the room around a living olive tree, trimmed its trunk into a bedpost, and constructed the bed from it; it cannot be moved unless someone has cut the trunk. Knowledge of this secret proves his identity. **[E1: *Odyssey* 23.173–206]**

The episode is simultaneously recognition token, test of identity, and image of rooted marriage. Its interpretation as a universal fertility ritual, relic of sacred tree marriage, or cryptogram for cult is **[E8 speculative]**; no independent ritual evidence demonstrates those theories.

### The maids

Twelve women of the household who slept with the suitors are forced to remove the corpses and are then hanged by Telemachus. Penelope neither orders nor witnesses the execution in Homer. **[E1]** Ancient and many modern translations morally label them “whores” or “sluts,” but those terms add modern judgment not present in the corresponding Greek passage. [Emily Wilson on translation bias](https://time.com/5008920/odyssey-translation-gender-history/)

Atwood’s transformation of these women into a murdered chorus indicting Odysseus, Telemachus, and Penelope is **[E9]**, a deliberate ethical rewriting rather than recovery of a lost ancient version.

### Endings

1. **Homeric:** Penelope and Odysseus reunite; the poem closes after Athena stops conflict with the suitors’ relatives. Tiresias has foretold Odysseus’s later inland journey and peaceful death, but Penelope’s death is not narrated. **[E1]**
2. **Telegony:** after Odysseus’s accidental death, Circe immortalizes Penelope, who marries Telegonus. **[E2]**
3. **Thesprotis reported by Pausanias:** Penelope bears Odysseus a further son, **Ptoliporthes**. **[E2]**
4. **Mantineian:** Odysseus finds her unfaithful or accuses her of admitting lovers; he expels her. She goes first to Sparta, then Mantinea, where she dies and is buried. **[E3]**
5. **Antinous variant:** Penelope is seduced by Antinous and dismissed by Odysseus. **[E4]**
6. **Hermes/Pan:** in Apollodorus’s version she reaches Mantinea and bears Pan to Hermes. **[E4, combining Arcadian and Pan genealogies]**
7. **All-suitors/Pan:** Duris of Samos, preserved through the scholia and Tzetzes, reportedly made Pan the child of Penelope and all the suitors—an apparent play on **Pan/pân**, “all.” **[E4]**
8. **Odysseus/Pan:** Servian and scholastic traditions also preserve Odysseus as Pan’s father. **[E4]**

These are competing traditions, not chapters in one continuous biography.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Mantineian tomb

Pausanias 8.12.5–6 saw or was shown a high earthen mound beside the road from Mantinea to Orchomenus, locally called **Penelope’s grave**. He explicitly notes conflict between the local account and the *Thesprotis*: the poem gave Penelope another son by Odysseus, whereas Mantineans said Odysseus expelled her for admitting lovers; she went from Ithaca to Sparta and then Mantinea, where she died. **[E3]** [Pausanias 8.12](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias8A.html)

What the evidence establishes:

- A mound near Roman-period Mantinea was identified to Pausanias as Penelope’s grave. **[E3]**
- A local explanatory narrative accompanied it. **[E3]**

What it does **not** establish:

- That the mound contained a historical Penelope.
- That it was originally built for her.
- That sacrifices, priesthood, games, inscriptions, or a recurring festival for Penelope occurred there.
- That Pausanias witnessed active worship.

No published inscription securely identifies a Mantineian **heroon of Penelope**, and the dossier has found no securely excavated votive assemblage tied to her name. The equation “traditional tomb = demonstrated hero cult” is methodologically unsafe: archaeological studies distinguish tomb reuse, ancestor veneration, and named hero cult. **[E8]** [Antonaccio, “Contesting the Past”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdfplus/10.2307/506436)

### Sparta and marriage aetiology

Pausanias connects Penelope with:

- the **Aphetaid Road**, where Icarius’s suitors allegedly ran;
- sanctuaries of **Athena Keleuthea** supposedly founded by Odysseus after winning;
- the image of **Aidos** associated with Penelope’s veiling decision.

These are local Spartan landscape traditions, not evidence that Penelope herself received sacrifice. **[E3]**

### Ithaca

The *Odyssey* makes Penelope queen of Ithaca, but no securely identified Bronze or Iron Age sanctuary, grave, inscription, or household cult can be assigned to her. Archaeological attempts to match Ithacan sites with Homeric palaces illuminate regional occupation, not the historicity of Penelope. **[E8]**

### Names and commemoration

Personal naming after Penelope became widespread in post-classical Europe and modern Greece. This is literary commemoration, not evidence for ancient theophoric naming: Penelope was mortal, and “Penelope” is not a theophoric formation comparable to Dionysodoros or Apollonios. **[E6/E9]**

**Cultic conclusion:** Penelope is securely an epic figure and later moral exemplar. A locally claimed tomb is attested at Mantinea, but **active ancient cult is unproven**. Absence of a cult dossier is itself a material finding.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### Oral-epic formation: before c. 700 BCE

**[E8 reconstruction]** Penelope probably emerged within orally transmitted homecoming traditions. The received *Odyssey* makes her integral to the *nostos*: Odysseus cannot recover identity and kingship without recovering the household and being recognized by its guarded mistress. No recoverable “original Penelope myth” predating Homer can be isolated.

### Archaic canonical shaping: late eighth–sixth centuries BCE

**[E1]** The *Odyssey* establishes the decisive complex: prudent queen, mother, delayed remarriage, shroud, bow, stranger-interview, dream, and bed test.

**[E2]** Cyclic epic extends the story beyond Homer, giving further children, Odysseus’s death, crossed marriages, and immortality.

### Classical divergence: fifth–fourth centuries BCE

**[E1/E2]** Herodotus records the Hermes–Penelope–Pan genealogy by c. 430 BCE, proving that the “unchaste” or alternative Penelope was not merely a very late Roman invention. Classical art gives the seated, veiled, pensive Penelope at her loom. Tragedy and moral discourse contrast her with Clytemnestra.

### Hellenistic systematization: third–first centuries BCE

**[E4]** Historians and mythographers collect contradictory parentages and endings. Duris’s collective-suitor parentage of Pan belongs to this learned and possibly comic/etymological environment. Alexandrian textual scholarship stabilizes Homer while scholia accumulate explanations of her motives.

### Roman moral and elegiac Penelope: first century BCE–second century CE

**[E2/E4]** Ovid gives her a first-person letter and emotional interiority. Roman writers make her a portable exemplum of **pudicitia**, chastity, while also joking about the strain of waiting. Cicero and Hyginus transmit the Pan genealogy; Pausanias records local Spartan and Mantineian variants.

### Late antique and Byzantine preservation

**[E4/E6]** Scholiasts, Servius, Eustathius, and Tzetzes preserve lost variants, etymologies, and mythographic contradictions. Christian moralizing could assimilate her to the faithful wife, but she did not become a saint or acquire Christian cult.

### Medieval and Renaissance reception

**[E6]** Latin Ovidian tradition often transmitted Penelope more accessibly than Greek Homer. Christine de Pizan included classical women within moral and political arguments about women’s capacities. Renaissance readers treated Penelope both as obedient wife and skillful household governor.

### Early-modern and nineteenth-century visual canon

**[E6/E9]** European art increasingly isolates the nocturnal unweaving scene and the melancholy woman at the loom. Joseph Wright of Derby’s *Penelope Unravelling Her Web by Lamp Light* (1785) makes clandestine labor and artificial light the drama. John Roddam Spencer Stanhope and John William Waterhouse’s *Penelope and the Suitors* (1912) helped fix the modern Pre-Raphaelite image of the besieged but self-possessed weaver.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

**[E9]** Modernism, feminism, postcolonial reception, novels, theater, opera, film, games, and popular music detach Penelope from the single virtue of fidelity. She becomes narrator, artist, strategist, resentful military spouse, political ruler, survivor, accomplice in class violence, or critic of the epic that contains her.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature

- **James Joyce, *Ulysses* (1922):** Molly Bloom corresponds structurally to Penelope; the final “Penelope” episode replaces the static faithful wife with an embodied, sexually complex modern consciousness. **[E9]**
- **H.D., “At Ithaca” (1924):** Penelope’s weaving becomes control over Odysseus’s fate. **[E9]**
- **Dorothy Parker, “Penelope” (1928):** compresses the gendered imbalance between the publicly celebrated adventurer and the waiting woman. **[E9]**
- **Edna St. Vincent Millay, “An Ancient Gesture” (1954/1956 publication context):** connects domestic grief to Penelope’s tears. **[E9]**
- **Carolyn Heilbrun, “What Was Penelope Unweaving?” (1979):** influential feminist interrogation of plots allotted to women. **[E8]**
- **Inge Merkel, *Odysseus und Penelope: Eine ganz gewöhnliche Ehe* (1987):** reframes the epic as marriage history. **[E9]**
- **Louise Glück, *Meadowlands* (1996):** polyphonic poems destabilize heroic marriage, desire, and fidelity. **[E9]**
- **Margaret Atwood, *The Penelopiad* (2005):** dead Penelope narrates from Hades while the twelve maids form a genre-shifting chorus. It foregrounds class, sexual coercion, judicial injustice, and Penelope’s unreliable self-defense. **[E9]** It is not an ancient “maids’ version,” though it deliberately exploits Homeric silences and variants. [Oxford reception study](https://academic.oup.com/crj/article/10/2/109/4746660?guestAccessKey=97f612ff-0541-42f3-a221-050d151a919b)

### Opera and theater

- **Claudio Monteverdi, *Il ritorno d’Ulisse in patria* (Venice, 1640):** Penelope’s constancy structures the opera; her long lament and delayed recognition dominate the emotional action. **[E6]**
- **Gabriel Fauré, *Pénélope* (1913):** centers the queen’s resistance and recognition. **[E9]**
- **Rolf Liebermann, *Penelope* (1954):** postwar opera focused on Penelope’s perspective. **[E9]**
- **Mary Zimmerman, *The Odyssey* (1999):** theatrical adaptation that participates in the late-twentieth-century recovery of women’s perspectives. **[E9]**
- Stage versions of *The Penelopiad* from 2007 onward turn the maids into a visible collective chorus. **[E9]**

### Visual art

The most important ancient object is the **Attic red-figure skyphos attributed to the Penelope Painter, c. 440 BCE**, found at Chiusi. Penelope sits veiled and pensive before a loom; Telemachus stands nearby, and both are named by inscriptions. The reverse shows Eurycleia’s recognition of Odysseus, though the nurse is inscribed **Antiphata**. It is frequently called the earliest securely identified image of Penelope. **[E1 material evidence]** [Object description](https://www.wikimuseo.it/w/en/museochiusi/959f12cc-b645-47eb-b2d8-967f44870ce2/p/2968b53b-bf61-44c8-bf0c-829231ec930f?tour=7794c9c3-3578-49b9-b940-2ac75bf58581)

Later images include:

- the widely copied “Penelope” seated-mourner type in Classical and Roman art, though unlabeled replicas require caution;
- Pinturicchio’s *Penelope with the Suitors* (c. 1509);
- Joseph Wright’s nocturnal unweaving (1785);
- Waterhouse’s *Penelope and the Suitors* (1912).

The identification of an unlabeled, pensive woman as Penelope merely because she sits at a loom is **[E8 attribution]**, not direct inscriptional evidence.

### Film, television, and games

Screen adaptations regularly compress the poem and alter Penelope’s knowledge, age, sexuality, and participation in revenge. Making her secretly recognize Odysseus much earlier, shoot the bow herself, fight the suitors, or leave Ithaca is **[E9]**, even when inspired by genuine scholarly ambiguity.

Popular games and retellings sometimes treat the *Telegony* as the mandatory “canonical sequel.” That is misleading: it is an ancient competing continuation, not a surviving Homeric book.

### Political and social uses

Penelope has repeatedly served as an emblem of the wife waiting for a soldier, sailor, emigrant, prisoner, or missing man. **[E9]** Such use can honor unpaid endurance but can also prescribe female chastity and passivity while normalizing male sexual freedom.

Modern feminist reception reverses that prescription, reading her:

- as an intelligence parallel to Odysseus;
- as an artist who controls time by making and undoing;
- as a woman constrained by property transfer and patriarchal remarriage;
- as a ruler preserving an estate;
- or, in Atwood, as an elite mistress unable or unwilling to save enslaved women.

No well-documented, durable nationalist or extremist cult of Penelope comparable to modern appropriations of Sparta, Achilles, or Leonidas emerged from the material reviewed. Claims that she was an ancient emblem of “racial purity,” ethnonational motherhood, or a historically documented Spartan feminine military ideal are **[E9 modern ideological projections]** unless supported by a specific modern source.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debates

### The “Penelope question”

The central interpretive controversy asks what Penelope knows, when she knows it, and what she intends in Books 18–23.

- **Traditional/unitarian reading:** she is intelligent but does not securely recognize Odysseus until the bed test.
- **Early-recognition reading:** Book 19’s coded conversation and immediate bow contest imply tacit recognition.
- **Gradual-recognition reading:** suspicion grows through the interview, dream, bow, appearance, and bed test.
- **Analytic reading:** inconsistencies result from compositional layers or combination of once-independent recognition stories.

The text’s restricted narration makes complete certainty impossible. Ancient scholia already debated her intention in instituting the bow contest. [Society for Classical Studies overview](https://classicalstudies.org/penelopes-recognition-odysseus-importance-simile-in-odyssey-23)

### Oral-formulaic composition

Milman Parry and Albert Lord’s oral-formulaic work changed interpretation of repeated phrases such as *periphrōn Penelopeia*. **[E8]** Epithets are inherited compositional resources, not necessarily authorial diagnoses freshly chosen at every occurrence. Later scholarship nevertheless shows that formula and characterization can operate together.

Gregory Nagy’s evolutionary model treats epic texts and hero traditions as developing through repeated performance before relative stabilization. Applied to Penelope, it cautions against reconstructing a single fixed prehistoric biography from the received poem. **[E8]**

### Female agency and *mētis*

Nancy Felson, Sheila Murnaghan, Marilyn Katz, Barbara Clayton, Lillian Doherty, and other scholars have shown that Penelope’s uncertainty is narratively productive and that her intelligence parallels Odysseus’s. **[E8]** The label “female Odysseus” can illuminate their shared testing and concealment, but it can also obscure her distinct constraints: she cannot travel, openly deploy violence, or control the remarriage crisis indefinitely.

### Weaving and poetics

Interpretations of the shroud as a figure for poetic composition, deferred closure, or female counter-narrative are modern scholarly constructions grounded in real textual parallels. **[E8]** They should not be presented as an ancient cult doctrine or authorially declared allegory.

### Historicity

There is no contemporary Bronze Age record of Penelope, Odysseus’s household, the suitors, or the marriage. A historical elite woman may lie somewhere behind generations of oral tradition, but no method can recover her identity. **[E8]** Archaeology can establish settlement, exchange, burial, and palace history in the Ionian islands; it cannot verify the olive bed, shroud, or individual queen.

The question “Was Penelope real?” therefore has a narrower answer than “Was the epic world informed by real social institutions?” Aristocratic marriage, household textile production, guest-feasting, inheritance anxiety, slavery, and competition over estates are historically intelligible; the named dossier remains legendary.

### Hero cult

Older scholarship sometimes inferred cult from every heroic tomb named by Pausanias. Farnell, Rohde, and later hero-cult studies provided major typologies; Burkert, Antonaccio, Ekroth, and others demanded stricter separation of literary tomb claims, tomb visitation, ancestor practice, and sacrificial cult. **[E8]** For Penelope, that stricter method leaves a Mantineian grave tradition but no demonstrable ritual complex.

### Pan’s mother: one Penelope or two?

Three positions recur:

1. Herodotus means Odysseus’s Penelope, demonstrating a sharply anti-Homeric tradition by the fifth century BCE.
2. An originally distinct Arcadian nymph or woman named Penelope became confused with the epic queen.
3. Learned mythographers intentionally exploited the famous wife and the *Pan/pân* etymological joke.

The first is textually straightforward; the second and third are **[E8 reconstructions]**. Nonnus’s late-antique **Pēnelópeia**, an Arcadian nymph and mother of a Pan by Hermes, may reflect differentiation rather than proof that Herodotus meant someone else.

### Textual state and losses

The evidentiary imbalance is severe:

- the *Odyssey* survives substantially complete;
- the *Telegony*, *Thesprotis*, early mythography, and most relevant tragedy survive only through fragments or summaries;
- scholia may preserve genuine early material but are transmitted in medieval manuscripts;
- vase imagery rarely explains which textual variant it follows;
- cultic evidence is largely absent.

Consequently, Homer’s Penelope appears culturally dominant partly because her poem survived. The alternative Penelopes are not necessarily “later inventions” in origin merely because their witnesses are later; they are, however, later **attestations** unless independent dating is possible.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Penelope means faithful wife.” **Modern semantic myth.**
- “She wove Odysseus’s shroud for twenty years.” **Contradicts Homer: Laertes’s shroud; three years, exposed in the fourth.**
- “She waited alone for twenty years while 108 suitors occupied the palace throughout.” **Compression of distinct periods; the poem does not say the courtship lasted the full twenty years.**
- “All ancient sources considered her perfectly faithful.” **False by Herodotus, Duris, Pausanias, and Apollodorus.**
- “All ancient sources considered her unfaithful.” **False; Homeric and much Roman reception make fidelity fundamental.**
- “Her grave proves she was historical.” **False inference.**
- “The Mantineian mound proves an active cult.” **Unproven.**
- “The *Telegony* is Homer’s lost final book.” **False; it was a separate cyclic poem.**
- “Atwood recovered the original suppressed maids’ narrative.” **Modern reinvention, though based on Homer’s execution scene.**
- “Penelope personally devised or conducted the suitors’ slaughter.” **Not in Homer.**
