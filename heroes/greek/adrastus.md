# Adrastus: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal heroic king; epic leader of the Argive expedition against Thebes; heroized dead with documented cult at Sicyon and reported honors at Megara and Colonus Hippius.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Homer’s *Iliad*, conventionally dated to the later eighth or early seventh century BCE, remembers “Sicyon, where Adrastus ruled at first” (*Iliad* 2.572) and “swift Arion, the horse of Adrastus, of divine stock” (23.346–347). Homer presupposes the figure and his famous horse but does not narrate the Seven’s expedition or identify Adrastus’s parents. Tyrtaeus fr. 12 West, mid-seventh century BCE, treats Adrastus’s persuasive speech as proverbial. These are the earliest securely datable literary witnesses. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548060349); [Homer, *Iliad* 23](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad23.html)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EE] Secure early evidence:** Homeric or archaic evidence, normally before 500 BCE.
- **[CE] Classical elaboration:** fifth- or fourth-century BCE poetry, drama, historiography, or art.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a locally situated genealogy, tomb, rite, or narrative.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Hellenistic or imperial mythography reconciling earlier traditions.
- **[SR] Syncretistic or interpretive identification:** explicit equation with another being or concept.
- **[MA] Medieval/early-modern accretion or transmission.**
- **[F] Folklore:** post-antique vernacular tradition demonstrably independent of learned retelling.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference rather than ancient testimony.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern claim.**

Dates below are dates of composition or testimony, not dates assigned to the hero’s supposed “life.”

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Adrastus is the Greek heroic king attached simultaneously to Argos and Sicyon, organizer or leader of the unsuccessful expedition of the Seven against Thebes and participant in the successful war of their sons, the Epigoni. In the mature narrative he receives the exiles Polynices and Tydeus, interprets an oracle that his daughters will marry a lion and a boar, makes the marriages, and undertakes to restore both men. The first expedition destroys its champions; Adrastus alone escapes on Arion. In the Athenian version dramatized by Euripides, he then supplicates Theseus at Eleusis for recovery and burial of the Argive dead. A generation later he accompanies the Epigoni, loses his son Aegialeus, and either dies from age and grief at Megara or, in a divergent Latin mythographic notice, enters fire with a son.

### Name and forms

- **Ancient Greek:** Ἄδραστος, *Ádrastos*; Ionic and epic Ἄδρηστος, *Ádrēstos*.
- **Latin:** *Adrastus*, genitive *Adrasti*.
- **Modern Greek:** Άδραστος, *Ádrastos*.
- **Common English forms:** Adrastus; occasionally Adrestus where the Ionic form is reproduced.
- **Croatian and some Slavic-language classical reference works:** Adrast, derived through Latin or modern European usage.

The ordinary Greek adjective ἄδραστος means “not running away” or “not inclined to flee,” from privative ἀ- plus a form related to διδράσκω, “run away.” LSJ records this lexical meaning and notes that in Homer the form occurs only as a proper name. “He who does not flee” is therefore linguistically defensible; “the inescapable” is a secondary interpretive rendering, particularly attractive through association with Adrasteia/Nemesis. It should not be treated as a securely documented archaic theology of the Argive hero. [LSJ/Scaife](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n1479/)

**[SR] Adrasteia/Nemesis.** Hellenistic and later writers connected the divine title Ἀδράστεια, “the inescapable,” either with an otherwise distinct Phrygian or Troadic king Adrastus who established her cult or, in some sources, with Talaus’s son. This is a learned etymological identification, not evidence that the Argive king was originally Nemesis or her consort. The popular derivation of Adrasteia as the power “from whom no one escapes” is not securely attested before the Hellenistic period.

### Titles and descriptions

No stable cult epithet peculiar to Adrastus is preserved.

- **“King of Sicyon”** is the earliest territorial designation: Homer, *Iliad* 2.572 [EE].
- **“Son of Talaus”** is secure by Bacchylides 9.15–19 and Pindar, *Nemean* 9, early fifth century BCE [EE/CE].
- **King of Argos / leader of the Argives** becomes standard in tragedy and mythography [CE/LS].
- **“Hero”** is appropriate both to Greek poetic usage and to his cult status. Herodotus 5.67 explicitly calls his Sicyonian installation a ἡρῷον, hero-shrine [CE].
- **“Demigod”** occurs in loose modern translations of Herodotus but is potentially misleading: no secure source makes either parent divine.

### Homonyms requiring separation

The following are not the Argive king unless a source expressly equates them:

1. Adrastus, son of Merops of Percote, a Trojan ally killed in the *Iliad*.
2. Adrastus, son of Gordias, the Phrygian exile in Herodotus 1.35–45.
3. Adrastus, son of Polynices and Argia, the king’s grandson in later genealogies.
4. A leader named Adrastus among later lists of Epigoni or Trojan contingents.
5. The king associated with Adrasteia in the Troad.
6. Adrastus of Aphrodisias, the imperial-period Peripatetic philosopher.

Conflating these produces false claims that the Argive Adrastus fought at Troy, killed Croesus’s son, or founded the Troadic cult of Nemesis.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaic evidence

**Homer, *Iliad* 2.572; 23.346–347, late eighth/early seventh century BCE [EE].** The Catalogue of Ships identifies Sicyon as the place where Adrastus formerly reigned. Nestor uses Adrastus’s divine-bred Arion as the measure of an exceptionally fast racehorse. Neither passage supplies parentage, the marriages, the Seven, or Adrastus’s fate. The unrelated Trojan Adrastus in *Iliad* 6 must be excluded. [Homer, *Iliad* 23](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad23.html)

**Homer, *Odyssey* 11.326 and 15.243–247, late eighth/early seventh century BCE [EE, indirect].** Adrastus is not named, but the poem knows the Theban expedition’s network: Eriphyle, bribed with gold, and Amphiaraus’s fatal departure. This establishes that major components of the cycle circulated early, without proving the later complete Adrastus narrative.

**Tyrtaeus fr. 12.8–9 West, mid-seventh century BCE [EE].** In a catalogue of qualities insufficient without martial courage, even a man possessing speech “more persuasive than Adrastus’s” is inadequate. Adrastus’s eloquence was therefore proverbial by this date, although the poem does not specify the occasion on which he displayed it.

**Cyclic *Thebaid* and *Epigoni*, probably seventh or sixth century BCE, surviving only in fragments and testimonia [EE, fragmentary].** A line quoted by Pindar and attributed by his scholia to the cyclic *Thebaid* has Adrastus lament Amphiaraus as both the army’s eye and its spear-fighter. The attribution is ancient but indirect. The lost *Epigoni* treated the second expedition; its first line reportedly announced a song of “younger men.” Martin L. West’s *Greek Epic Fragments* is the standard Loeb collection; fragmentary transmission prevents reconstruction of a complete plot.

**Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* fr. 99 Merkelbach–West/Most, probably sixth century BCE in its received form [EE].** It gives Eriphyle and Amphiaraus’s son Alcmaeon and mentions the Danaans following Polynices to Thebes and the earth swallowing Amphiaraus with horses and chariot. It illuminates Adrastus’s family-cycle but does not preserve a connected biography of him. [Hesiodic fragments](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodCatalogues.html)

### Fifth- and fourth-century BCE corpus

**Herodotus, *Histories* 5.67–68, c. 430s–420s BCE [CE; cult testimony].** This is the fundamental cultic document. Herodotus says Adrastus son of Talaus had a hero-shrine in Sicyon’s agora “then and still,” had ruled the country through descent from Polybus, and was honored with sacrifices and “tragic choruses” recounting his sufferings. Cleisthenes of Sicyon tried to expel him because he was Argive, imported the Theban enemy Melanippus, reassigned Adrastus’s sacrifices to Melanippus and the choruses to Dionysus, and attacked Argive cultural prestige by stopping Homeric recitations. [Herodotus 5.67](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Herodotus/5c%2A.html)

**Pindar, *Nemean* 9.8–27, *Nemean* 8.50–51, *Nemean* 10.7–9, *Olympian* 6.13–17, *Pythian* 8.39–56; 474–446 BCE [CE].** Pindar links Adrastus with Sicyon, flight from civil conflict and Amphiaraus, institution of contests on the Asopus, the doomed expedition, the lament for Amphiaraus, and the Epigoni. In *Pythian* 8, the dead Amphiaraus predicts Adrastus’s second return shadowed by his son’s death. [Pindar translation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8b/The_odes_of_Pindar_%28IA_odesofpind00pind%29.pdf)

**Bacchylides 9.10–24, early fifth century BCE [CE].** Names Talaus as Adrastus’s father and associates Adrastus with the Nemean/Opheltes tradition.

**Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes*, produced 467 BCE [CE].** Adrastus remains offstage. Aeschylus’s attacking seven are Tydeus, Capaneus, Eteoclus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Amphiaraus, and Polynices: Adrastus leads the expedition but is not one of the seven gate champions. This contradicts later lists that replace Eteoclus with Adrastus. [Aeschylus, *Seven*](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AeschylusSeven.html)

**Aeschylus’s lost *Eleusinians*, *Argive Women*, *Nemea*, and *Epigoni*, fifth century BCE [CE, fragmentary].** Adrastus probably appeared in several; reconstruction depends on titles, fragments, and later narrative parallels and must remain tentative.

**Sophocles, *Oedipus at Colonus* 1301–1325, produced posthumously in 401 BCE [CE].** Polynices names his Argive allies and his marriage into Adrastus’s house. Sophocles follows the gate-champion list that excludes Adrastus.

**Euripides, *Suppliants*, usually c. 424–420 BCE [CE].** Adrastus is a principal speaking character at Eleusis. He admits that he launched the war against divine and prophetic advice, asks Theseus to retrieve the unburied bodies, and later delivers ethical portraits of the dead. Athena’s concluding intervention orders an Argive-Athenian oath and predicts the Epigoni’s victory. [Complete translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Euripides/suppliants.html)

**Euripides, *Phoenician Women*, c. 410–408 BCE [CE].** Supplies the oracle-and-marriages narrative and makes Adrastus one of the seven gate champions, replacing Aeschylus’s Eteoclus.

**Euripides, fragmentary *Hypsipyle*, late fifth century BCE [CE].** Adrastus and the Argive leaders stop at Nemea; the infant Opheltes dies after Hypsipyle leaves him while guiding them to water. Funeral games and the renaming of Opheltes as Archemorus follow.

**Hellanicus, Pherecydes, and other fifth-century mythographers [CE, fragmentary].** Their notices helped articulate the genealogies, including Aegialeus as Adrastus’s son, but survive mainly through later quotation.

**Plato, *Phaedrus* 269a, c. 370 BCE [CE, allusive].** Adrastus appears with Pericles in a discussion of persuasive speech, probably exploiting the same proverbial eloquence known from Tyrtaeus.

### Hellenistic and imperial corpus

**Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1, third century BCE [LS, genealogical].** Talaus, Adrastus’s father in the usual genealogy, is an Argonaut. Adrastus himself is not an Argonaut in Apollonius; lists that include him confuse generations.

**Diodorus Siculus 4.65–66, first century BCE [LS].** Gives a prose synthesis of the Seven and Epigoni, including Adrastus among the seven leaders.

**Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.480, 29–19 BCE [LS, reception].** Aeneas sees Adrastus among the Greek heroic dead in the underworld; Virgil neither narrates his death nor reconciles this with Greek variants.

**Ovid, *Ibis* 427–428 and related Theban allusions, early first century CE [LS, allusive].** Adrastus belongs to Ovid’s learned catalogue of mythic precedents, not to a sustained retelling.

**Statius, *Thebaid*, c. 80–92 CE [LS; major Roman re-creation].** The fullest surviving epic construction of Adrastus: aged ruler, host and father-in-law, coalition leader, mourner, participant in Nemea’s games, and sole returning chief. Statius greatly expands scenes, psychology, divine machinery, and battlefield action; it is not evidence for an otherwise lost verbatim archaic epic. [Statius, Book 3](https://www.theoi.com/Text/StatiusThebaid3.html); [Book 4](https://www.theoi.com/Text/StatiusThebaid4.html); [Book 5](https://www.theoi.com/Text/StatiusThebaid5.html)

**Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.13; 3.6–7, probably first or second century CE [LS].** The most convenient Greek prose synthesis: genealogy, feud with Amphiaraus, shield emblems, marriages, Eriphyle’s judgment, Nemea, Seven, Arion’s rescue, burial dispute, Epigoni, and Aegialeus’s death. It must not be projected wholesale back into Homer. [*Bibliotheca* 3.6](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DLibrary%3Abook%3D3%3Achapter%3D6)

**Hyginus, *Fabulae* 69, 70, 71, 242, text probably compiled in the early imperial period but preserved through a damaged late-antique/medieval tradition [LS].** Hyginus gives the animal pelts rather than shield emblems and names Apollo as giver of the oracle. Fabula 242 uniquely says Adrastus and his son Hipponous threw themselves into fire following Apollo’s response. [Hyginus 69](https://archive.ph/rf2f); [Hyginus 242](https://www.lateinlex.de/?call=Puc&permalink=Hyg_fab_242)

**Pausanias, *Description of Greece*, c. 150–175 CE [LS/RV; topographical testimony].** Records Adrastus’s Sicyonian genealogy; monuments and representations at Amyclae, Argos, Delphi, Thebes, Colonus, and Megara; Megarian honors; and the local death account. Pausanias is evidence that these claims and monuments existed in his period, not automatic proof that each was prehistoric.

**Scholia on Pindar, Euripides, and Homer, principally preserved in Byzantine manuscripts [LS/MA].** These quote otherwise lost historians and poets, including the attribution of Adrastus’s lament to the cyclic *Thebaid*. Each scholium’s source and manuscript date must be distinguished from the archaic work it purports to quote.

### Manuscript gap

No authorial manuscript of any relevant archaic or classical text survives. Homer, Pindar, and tragedy are preserved through Hellenistic editorial activity, papyri, and medieval codices. Pseudo-Apollodorus’s principal text is incomplete; its later books depend partly on epitomes. Hyginus’s text is particularly corrupt. The evidentiary date of an underlying work and the physical date of its surviving witness are therefore different.

## 3. The Story

### Parentage and dynastic position

**[EE/CE] Father:** Talaus is first securely attested as Adrastus’s father by Bacchylides and Pindar. Pseudo-Apollodorus makes Talaus son of Bias and Pero, joining Adrastus to the Amythaonid houses of Argos.

**Mothers, irreconcilable late variants:**

- **Lysimache**, daughter of Abas: Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.13 [LS].
- **Lysianassa**, daughter of Polybus: Pausanias 2.6.3 [RV/LS].
- **Eurynome:** Hyginus 69 [LS].

The Lysianassa version directly explains Adrastus’s succession to his maternal grandfather Polybus at Sicyon. Other accounts require a different relationship or leave the succession unexplained.

Siblings vary by system. Pseudo-Apollodorus gives Parthenopaeus, Pronax, Mecisteus, Aristomachus, and Eriphyle among Talaus’s children; other genealogies alter the list. Eriphyle is consistently central as Adrastus’s sister and Amphiaraus’s wife.

### Exile to Sicyon and return

Homer merely says Adrastus once ruled Sicyon [EE]. Pindar, *Nemean* 9, says the sons of Talaus were overcome by civil discord and Adrastus fled Amphiaraus [CE]. Later prose narratives explain that Amphiaraus killed Talaus or that rival branches of the Melampodid and Biantid houses struggled for power. Adrastus fled to Polybus, inherited Sicyon, and eventually reconciled with Amphiaraus by giving him Eriphyle in marriage and agreeing that she would arbitrate future disputes [LS].

This pact explains both peace and catastrophe: Polynices later bribes Eriphyle with Harmonia’s necklace, compelling Amphiaraus to join the expedition he knows will fail.

### Sicyonian games

Pindar says Adrastus established contests by the Asopus during his Sicyonian rule [CE]. Some later summaries conflate these with the Nemean Games founded after Opheltes’s death. The sources preserve overlapping aetiologies rather than one secure institutional history.

### The lion and the boar

The episode first survives fully in Euripides:

1. Polynices, expelled from Thebes by Eteocles, and Tydeus, exiled from Calydon after a killing, arrive at Adrastus’s house.
2. They fight, compared to wild beasts.
3. Adrastus recalls an oracle that his daughters will marry a lion and a boar.
4. He identifies the men with the animals, gives Argia to Polynices and Deipyle to Tydeus, and promises to restore both.

**Variants:**

- **Wild-beast behavior:** Euripides, *Suppliants* 131–154 and *Phoenician Women* 408–429 [CE].
- **Animal devices on shields:** Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.6.1 [LS].
- **Lion and boar pelts worn as clothing:** Hyginus 69 and Statius, *Thebaid* 1–2 [LS].
- **Oracle source:** Euripides leaves its divine source less explicit in the relevant narration; Hyginus names Apollo [LS].

The pelts are not the earliest recoverable form. Modern illustrations that present them as an invariant ancient fact silently privilege Roman-period tellings.

### The decision for war

Adrastus undertakes first to restore Polynices; Tydeus’s restoration to Calydon is postponed and never accomplished in the dominant cycle. Amphiaraus foresees the expedition’s destruction and opposes it. Polynices gives Eriphyle Harmonia’s necklace; because Adrastus and Amphiaraus agreed to accept her arbitration, Amphiaraus must go.

Euripides’s Adrastus accepts moral responsibility: he ignored Amphiaraus and went to war without divine approval. This is a Classical dramatic judgment, not an objective statement that every earlier version made Adrastus culpably reckless.

### Who were “the Seven”?

No single canonical list exists.

- **Aeschylus, *Seven*; Euripides, *Suppliants*; Sophocles, *Oedipus at Colonus* [CE]:** Tydeus, Capaneus, Eteoclus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Amphiaraus, Polynices. Adrastus is commander but not one of the seven gate attackers.
- **Euripides, *Phoenician Women*; Diodorus; Pseudo-Apollodorus; Hyginus; Statius [CE/LS]:** Adrastus replaces Eteoclus.
- Fragmentary and iconographic witnesses yield still other combinations.

The hypothesis that seven champions were devised to match Thebes’s seven gates is **[MSR]**, plausible but unproved. The Oxford Classical Dictionary notes that Homer knows several associated heroes but no canonical seven and suggests the tradition developed after the Mycenaean period but before Corinth’s rise. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548060349)

### Nemea and Opheltes

On the march, the army needs water. Hypsipyle, enslaved nurse of the infant Opheltes, leaves the child on the ground while guiding the leaders. A serpent kills him. Amphiaraus interprets the death as an omen and renames him Archemorus, “beginning of doom.” Adrastus and the leaders bury him and institute funeral games.

Pindar already links Adrastus with Nemean contests; Euripides’s fragmentary *Hypsipyle* gives the developed dramatic episode; Pseudo-Apollodorus and Statius systematize and expand it. Statius makes Adrastus preside and compete: his arrow returns ominously to his feet, forecasting that he alone will return from Thebes. This particular portent is Roman poetic elaboration.

### The first war and escape

The assault fails. Capaneus is struck by Zeus’s thunderbolt after boasting that even Zeus cannot stop him. Tydeus dies despite Athena’s intended immortality when she recoils from his savage consumption of Melanippus’s brain. Polynices and Eteocles kill each other. Amphiaraus is swallowed by the earth with his chariot when Zeus opens it, preventing a dishonorable spear-strike from behind. Other champions die according to variant gate traditions.

Adrastus alone escapes. Homer already knows his supernaturally bred horse Arion but does not narrate the escape. Pindar and later sources connect rider, horse, and survival. Pseudo-Apollodorus says Heracles gave Arion to Adrastus; Pausanias reports traditions in which Poseidon fathered Arion on Demeter, who had taken mare-form. The genealogy belongs to the horse, not Adrastus. [Arion source dossier](https://www.theoi.com/Ther/HipposAreion.html)

### Recovery and burial of the dead

Three major versions must not be harmonized:

1. **Athenian force, battle, and recovery:** Euripides’s *Suppliants* has Adrastus and the Argive mothers supplicate Aethra and Theseus at Eleusis. Theseus initially rebukes Adrastus, then leads Athens against Thebes, recovers the bodies, and arranges cremation and burial [CE].
2. **Athenian diplomatic recovery:** other Athenian commemorative traditions could emphasize persuasion rather than battle; Theseus’s recovery became an Athenian exemplum of respect for pan-Hellenic burial law [CE/RV].
3. **Argive or non-Athenian handling:** earlier epic evidence is too fragmentary to prove the Athenian role belonged to the archaic form [EE absence].

The story became Athenian civic ideology: Athens represented itself as the city willing to enforce universal burial norms and protect suppliants. That political function is securely Classical, whatever the narrative’s earlier shape. [Study of Adrastus in Athenian ideology](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/ideology-of-democratic-athens/combining-hybris-and-philanthropia-the-myth-of-adra​​stus/5C36D163FFAB8F9238BCD426C85C6AD2)

### The Epigoni

Ten years later in the usual chronology, the dead chiefs’ sons march against Thebes. Adrastus accompanies them. Amphiaraus, now dead and oracular, predicts success but also Adrastus’s domestic grief. The Epigoni capture Thebes; the Thebans withdraw under prophetic direction.

Adrastus’s son Aegialeus kills or is killed by Laodamas depending on the source’s arrangement; the dominant point is that Aegialeus alone of the Epigoni dies, reversing the first expedition, where Adrastus alone of the senior leaders survived. In Pindar’s formulation Adrastus must gather his son’s bones and return with the otherwise intact army.

### Death

- **Megarian version:** Pausanias 1.43.1 says the Megarians honored Adrastus and claimed that, while returning after Thebes’s capture, he died in their country from old age and grief at Aegialeus’s death [RV/LS]. [Pausanias 1.43](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias1C.html)
- **Self-immolation:** Hyginus 242 says Adrastus and his son Hipponous cast themselves into fire in obedience to Apollo [LS]. This conflicts with the more widespread genealogy and death account and may reflect textual corruption or a separate local story; it cannot be treated as the standard ending.
- **Underworld:** Virgil places Adrastus among the heroic dead [LS], without specifying how he died.
- **No secure archaic death:** Homer and surviving cyclic fragments do not establish his fate [EE absence].

### Documented interactions, one named figure per entry

“Interaction” here means an explicit narrative, cultic, or dramatic relation—not mere shared membership in a genealogy.

- **Aegialeus** — Adrastus leads the Epigoni with his son; Aegialeus dies and Adrastus carries or mourns his remains. Pindar, *Pythian* 8, 446 BCE; Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.7 [CE/LS].
- **Aethra** — Adrastus and the Argive mothers supplicate Theseus’s mother at Eleusis; she persuades Theseus to hear them. Euripides, *Suppliants*, c. 424–420 BCE [CE].
- **Amphiaraus** — dynastic enemy, later brother-in-law and reluctant ally; Adrastus accepts Eriphyle’s arbitration, ignores Amphiaraus’s warning, and laments his disappearance. Pindar, *Nemean* 9 and *Olympian* 6; Euripides, *Suppliants*; later mythographers [CE/LS].
- **Apollo** — gives or is credited with the lion-and-boar oracle in Hyginus 69; Hyginus 242 also makes his oracle command the fatal fire. Imperial-period Latin mythography [LS].
- **Arion** — Adrastus’s divine horse and rescuer from Thebes. Homer, *Iliad* 23, gives ownership; survival is explicit in Pindaric and later traditions [EE/CE/LS].
- **Argia** — Adrastus gives his daughter in marriage to Polynices. Euripides and Pseudo-Apollodorus [CE/LS].
- **Athena** — in Euripides’s *Suppliants*, appears after the recovery of the bodies and orders Theseus to exact an Argive oath; Adrastus is present and bound by this settlement. c. 424–420 BCE [CE].
- **Capaneus** — member of Adrastus’s expedition; Adrastus later praises him in the funeral oration. Euripides, *Suppliants* 857–917 [CE].
- **Creon** — Theban ruler whose refusal to release the bodies drives Adrastus’s supplication of Theseus. Euripides, *Suppliants* [CE]. No direct face-to-face encounter is required by the play.
- **Deipyle** — Adrastus gives his daughter to Tydeus. Euripides and Pseudo-Apollodorus [CE/LS].
- **Demeter** — no direct interaction with Adrastus is stated. She is Arion’s mother in one genealogy reported by Pausanias; any relationship to Adrastus is mediated through the horse [LS].
- **Dionysus** — Cleisthenes transfers to Dionysus the tragic choruses previously performed for Adrastus. Herodotus 5.67, reporting early-sixth-century Sicyon [CE testimony about cult politics].
- **Diomedes** — Adrastus’s grandson through Deipyle and Tydeus in the standard genealogy; he is one of the Epigoni in later systematization. Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.7 [LS].
- **Eriphyle** — Adrastus’s sister, whom he marries to Amphiaraus and accepts as arbitrator; her bribed decision forces Amphiaraus into the war. *Odyssey* 11 and 15 supply the early betrayal framework; full relation in later sources [EE/LS].
- **Eteocles** — opposing Theban king whose exclusion of Polynices motivates Adrastus’s war. Euripides, *Suppliants* and *Phoenician Women* [CE].
- **Eteoclus** — one of Adrastus’s champions in Aeschylus and Euripides’s *Suppliants*; replaced by Adrastus in other seven-lists. Fifth century BCE [CE].
- **Evadne** — widow of Capaneus; during the funerals organized after Adrastus’s appeal, she leaps onto Capaneus’s pyre. Euripides, *Suppliants* 980–1071 [CE]. They do not converse directly.
- **Heracles** — gives Arion to Adrastus in Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.6.8. Imperial Greek mythography [LS]; Homer knows horse and owner but not the gift.
- **Hippomedon** — expedition member and subject of Adrastus’s funeral praise. Aeschylus and Euripides [CE].
- **Hypsipyle** — guides Adrastus and his army to water at Nemea; her absence permits Opheltes’s death. Euripides, fragmentary *Hypsipyle*; expanded by Statius [CE/LS].
- **Laodamas** — leader of the Theban defense against the Epigoni and killer or opponent of Aegialeus in differing accounts; interaction with Adrastus is military rather than personal. Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.7 [LS].
- **Melanippus** — Theban hero installed at Sicyon by Cleisthenes as Adrastus’s cultic enemy and recipient of Adrastus’s former sacrifices. Herodotus 5.67 [CE testimony/RV].
- **Mecisteus** — Adrastus’s brother or kinsman in later genealogy and, in some early reconstructions, a participant in the Theban cycle. Pseudo-Apollodorus and Homeric allusion [LS; relation reconstructed].
- **Nemesis/Adrasteia** — some late sources derive her title from Adrastus or an identically named king; Pausanias also gives Adrastus a Nemesis foundation after the Epigoni. No archaic personal encounter is securely attested [SR/LS].
- **Opheltes/Archemorus** — Adrastus helps bury the child and institute funeral games. Pindar, Bacchylides, Euripides’s *Hypsipyle*, later mythographers [CE/LS].
- **Parthenopaeus** — member of Adrastus’s expedition and subject of his funeral praise. Aeschylus and Euripides [CE].
- **Periclymenus** — pursues Amphiaraus during the rout; Zeus removes Amphiaraus before his spear can strike. Adrastus’s relation is indirect, as opposing combatant. Pindar, *Nemean* 9 [CE].
- **Polynices** — Adrastus receives the exile, marries Argia to him, promises restoration, and leads war on his behalf. Euripides; Pseudo-Apollodorus; Statius [CE/LS].
- **Poseidon** — father of Arion in a genealogy reported by Pausanias; no direct meeting with Adrastus is recorded [LS].
- **Polybus** — maternal grandfather or host and predecessor at Sicyon; Adrastus inherits his throne. Herodotus 5.67; Pausanias 2.6.3 [CE testimony/RV].
- **Talaus** — father in the earliest secure genealogy. Bacchylides and Pindar [CE].
- **Theseus** — Adrastus supplicates him for recovery of the dead; Theseus rebukes, then aids him and receives Athena’s instructions for an Argive oath. Euripides, *Suppliants* [CE].
- **Thersander** — Adrastus’s grandson through Polynices and Argia and an Epigonus in later genealogical synthesis. Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.7 [LS].
- **Tydeus** — Adrastus receives him, marries Deipyle to him, promises restoration, and makes him a champion. Euripides and Pseudo-Apollodorus [CE/LS].
- **Zeus** — sends signs discouraging the expedition in Pindar, destroys Capaneus, and opens the earth to preserve Amphiaraus from a dishonorable death. Pindar and tragedy [CE]. No text states that Zeus saves Adrastus personally.
- **The Argive mothers** — collectively supplicate with Adrastus and receive their sons’ bodies. Euripides, *Suppliants* [CE].
- **The Epigoni** — Adrastus accompanies or leads the sons in the successful second expedition. Cyclic *Epigoni*, Pindar, and later mythography [EE fragmentary/CE/LS].

**Absences worth stating:** no secure source gives Adrastus a divine parent, apotheosis, encounter with Achilles or Heracles in person, participation in the Argonaut voyage, or action in the Trojan War. Those claims normally result from homonyms or genealogical compression.

## 5. Cult, Tomb, and Ritual

### Sicyon: securely literary-attested cult

Herodotus 5.67 is the strongest evidence:

- Adrastus had a **hero-shrine in the agora**.
- Herodotus says it existed in Cleisthenes’s day and still existed when he wrote.
- Sicyonians honored Adrastus “very greatly.”
- They celebrated his sufferings with **tragic choruses**, which Herodotus contrasts with choruses for Dionysus.
- They offered **sacrifices** that Cleisthenes reassigned to Melanippus.
- Cleisthenes, at war with Argos, asked Delphi for permission to expel Adrastus.
- The Pythia refused and called Adrastus king of Sicyon while insulting Cleisthenes.
- Cleisthenes therefore installed Melanippus, Adrastus’s Theban enemy, and transferred the ritual honors indirectly.

This is not literally a “transfer of Adrastus” or his bones. It is the political displacement of his cult and ritual prerogatives. No excavated structure has been securely identified as Adrastus’s agora heroon. The evidence is textual, specific, and contemporary only in Herodotus’s fifth-century report about events placed around 600–570 BCE. [Herodotus 5.67](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Herodotus/5c%2A.html)

Herodotus’s notice is central to histories of tragedy because the πάθη, “sufferings,” of a hero were enacted by choruses called “tragic.” It does not prove that Attic tragedy directly evolved from Adrastus’s cult; that larger claim is a modern reconstruction.

### Megara

Pausanias 1.43.1 says Adrastus “has honors” among the Megarians, who claimed he died there after the Epigoni’s victory from old age and grief. The text does not clearly describe an excavated tomb or enumerate rites. Calling the site a securely known tomb overstates the evidence. [Pausanias 1.43](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias1C.html)

### Colonus Hippius, Attica

Pausanias 1.30.4 reports a precinct or shrine shared by heroes including Adrastus at Colonus. This is imperial-period topographical testimony. Its relation to Sophocles’s Theban-Athenian mythic geography is plausible, but archaeology has not securely isolated “Adrastus’s shrine” as an independently datable installation.

### Eleusis and the burial landscape

Pausanias records the Athenian tradition that Theseus recovered the Seven’s bodies and buried them at Eleusis. Euripides makes Eleusis the location of Adrastus’s supplication and the funerals. These are important cultic-political landscapes but do not demonstrate an independent Adrastus cult at Eleusis equivalent to Sicyon’s.

### Games

Pindar associates Adrastus with:

- contests instituted during his Sicyonian rule along the Asopus;
- the funeral games for Opheltes/Archemorus at Nemea.

The Nemean festival’s historical organization cannot be recovered simply from these aetiological myths. Adrastus’s “foundation” is mythic charter, not an archival founding date.

### Images and monuments

- A late-sixth-century BCE Chalcidian calyx-krater shows Adrastus, identified by inscription, reclining in a scene associated with the Theban expedition [CE, material evidence].
- Classical vases depict the arming or departure of the Seven; identifications without inscriptions remain probabilistic.
- Pausanias 3.18.7 describes the Amyclae throne’s decoration involving Adrastus and Tydeus.
- Pausanias 10.10.2–4 describes an Argive monument at Delphi with figures from the Seven and Epigoni traditions.
- Pausanias 9.9.1–3 saw Theban monuments and explicitly remarked on the abundant epic and tragic treatment of the wars.
- The *LIMC* entry “Adrastos” remains the specialist iconographic catalogue; web-accessible object descriptions should not replace its object-by-object attributions. A vase dealer’s or auction catalogue’s “perhaps Adrastus” is not equivalent to an inscriptional identification. [Treccani, ancient-art entry](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/adrasto_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Antica%29/)

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Presupposed epic hero, c. 750–650 BCE

Homer assumes knowledge of Adrastus as former Sicyonian ruler and owner of Arion. Tyrtaeus assumes proverbial eloquence. The *Odyssey* knows Eriphyle’s betrayal and Amphiaraus’s doom. The surviving evidence is allusive: the complete later biography cannot be assigned to this period.

### II. Theban-cycle formation, seventh–sixth centuries BCE

The cyclic *Thebaid* and *Epigoni* organized the failed and successful expeditions. Hesiodic genealogy incorporated Amphiaraus, Eriphyle, and Alcmaeon into a Panhellenic family structure. At Sicyon, Adrastus had a politically potent cult by Cleisthenes’s time.

### III. Cultic conflict and interstate identity, c. 600–500 BCE

Cleisthenes’s treatment of Adrastus demonstrates that hero cult, epic performance, ethnicity, and war were politically entangled. An Argive hero could be treated as Sicyon’s true king by Delphi and yet as an enemy-symbol by Sicyon’s ruler. The response was ritual reassignment, not skeptical abolition.

### IV. Canonical dramatic differentiation, 467–400 BCE

Aeschylus fixed one influential list of attackers while keeping Adrastus offstage. Euripides converted him into a self-critical political speaker, supplicant, mourner, and moral evaluator. *Phoenician Women* made him one of the seven; *Hypsipyle* integrated him into Nemean aetiology. Sophocles used his Argive alliance in Polynices’s final appeal. Variants multiplied rather than converged.

### V. Hellenistic organization, third–first centuries BCE

Genealogists and poets systematized the houses of Bias, Melampus, and Oedipus. Local explanations were fitted into larger chronologies. Associations between Adrastus, Adrasteia, and Nemesis acquired learned etymological force. Most direct sources from this phase survive only through later quotation.

### VI. Roman epic and mythography, first century BCE–second century CE

Diodorus, Hyginus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, and Pausanias supplied prose syntheses. Statius transformed Adrastus into an aged Roman epic king negotiating hospitality, pietas, war leadership, and grief. These accounts dominate modern handbooks because they survive continuously, not because they are earliest.

### VII. Late antique and Byzantine preservation

The Greek epics, scholia, mythographic handbooks, and Statius remained educational texts in different degrees. Byzantine scholia preserved valuable quotations but also late explanatory harmonizations. No independent medieval folk cult of Adrastus is securely documented.

### VIII. Early-modern learned revival

Printed editions of Homer, Pindar, tragedy, Pausanias, and Statius restored Adrastus to European classical education. Retellings generally followed Statius or mythographic handbooks and therefore privileged the lion-and-boar emblems, the canonical “seven,” and Arion’s rescue.

### IX. Modern philology and archaeology

Nineteenth-century source criticism separated Homeric allusion from later mythography. Farnell treated Adrastus within hero cult; twentieth-century fragment editions reconstructed the cyclic and Hesiodic evidence; Gantz and Fowler mapped variants without forcing unity. Modern iconographic corpora and papyrus studies improved control of the evidence but have not produced a contemporary inscription that verifies Adrastus as a historical king.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, theater, opera, and music

- **Statius’s *Thebaid*** remained the chief postclassical literary mediator, especially through medieval school and manuscript culture [MA].
- **Medieval Theban narratives**, including the Old French *Roman de Thèbes* (c. 1150), reworked Statius extensively. Their Adrastus belongs to medieval romance conventions rather than independent Greek folklore [MA].
- **Renaissance and early-modern Theban drama** usually centered on Eteocles, Polynices, or Antigone; Adrastus frequently became a supporting ruler rather than protagonist [MA].
- **Tommaso Traetta’s opera *Antigona* (1772)** includes a tenor character Adrasto. Its plot and dramaturgy are an eighteenth-century operatic reconstruction, not an ancient variant. [Traetta’s *Antigona*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigona_%28Traetta%29)
- Nineteenth- and twentieth-century prose mythologies reproduced him mainly as the king who misunderstood or fulfilled the lion-and-boar oracle and survived through Arion.
- Modern performances of Euripides’s *Suppliants* restore his politically uncomfortable role as failed commander, refugee-supplicant, and funeral orator.

### Visual arts

Modern illustrations commonly show:

1. Adrastus separating a lion-clad Polynices and boar-clad Tydeus;
2. the departure of the Seven;
3. his escape on Arion;
4. his supplication of Theseus.

Only the underlying episodes are ancient. Exact armor, pelts, heraldry, facial types, and scenes of Adrastus dramatically mounted on a white “immortal horse” are modern visual choices unless derived from an identified ancient object.

### Psychological and comparativist readings

Adrastus is not a major independent case in Rank’s birth-hero pattern, Raglan’s hero schema, or Campbell’s monomyth. Applying those universalizing templates to him is **[MSR]**, not ancient evidence. He lacks the paradigmatic miraculous birth and solitary quest required by many popular “hero’s journey” diagrams.

Structuralist readings have treated the paired expeditions as reversal:

- fathers fail / sons succeed;
- all senior champions die except Adrastus / all Epigoni survive except Aegialeus;
- failed restoration / successful conquest;
- denial of burial / recovery and civic commemoration.

This pattern is textually well grounded, but interpretation of its Indo-European or Near Eastern origin remains debated.

Walter Burkert and others have compared the seven-against-seven or seven-gates configuration with Near Eastern narrative structures. Such comparisons demonstrate possible shared motifs, not direct proof that Adrastus derives from a named Near Eastern king.

Gregory Nagy’s work on hero cult and epic emphasizes the reciprocal shaping of local cult and Panhellenic poetry. Applied to Adrastus, it helps explain why Sicyonian ritual identity and Argive epic identity conflict in Herodotus. It does not prove a single chronological progression from “real king” to cult hero to poem.

### Political appropriation

The strongest documented political appropriation is ancient:

- Cleisthenes of Sicyon attacked Adrastus’s cult because of war with Argos.
- Athenian versions made Adrastus’s appeal evidence of Athens’s defense of burial law and distressed suppliants.
- Argive monuments at Delphi represented the city through the Seven and Epigoni.

The dedicated search found **no well-documented modern nationalist or extremist movement for which Adrastus himself is a central emblem**. General online claims that he represents “Greek national unity,” “Aryan warrior kingship,” or a timeless military federation are **[MI]** unless tied to a dated publication or organization. Absence of found evidence is not proof that no isolated use has occurred, but it is a reason not to manufacture a reception history.

### Commercial and astronomical reuse

“Adrastus” appears as a personal name, taxonomic name, and the name of a crater on Saturn’s moon Dione. These are modern commemorative reuse of the classical name, not survivals of worship. Online role-playing profiles and commercial mythology pages routinely harmonize variants and sometimes call him immortal, a demigod, or an Argonaut; those assertions are **[MI]** when no ancient source is supplied.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Adrastus historical?

No contemporary Bronze Age document, Linear B tablet, securely identified tomb inscription, or royal archive names this Adrastus. Efforts to recover a historical Mycenaean war behind the Theban cycle are **[MSR]**. Archaeology shows Bronze Age destruction and conflict at Thebes, but it cannot identify the attackers as Adrastus and the Seven.

Some scholars have proposed that memories of Mycenaean warfare lie behind the cycle. Others emphasize that the tradition’s political geography—strong Argos, absent or subordinate Mycenae, no Corinthian champion—fits early Iron Age interstate conditions better. The Oxford Classical Dictionary accordingly suggests development after the Mycenaean period but before Corinth’s rise. This is a relative literary-historical argument, not proof of an eighth-century historical Adrastus. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548060349)

### Is Homer’s Adrastus the later Argive king?

“Iliad 2.572” plainly connects the name with Sicyon, while the later figure is both Sicyonian and Argive. Most scholarship accepts continuity. Homer nevertheless says nothing of Talaus, Argos, or the Seven in that passage. Identification is highly probable but its narrative implications should not be inflated.

### Did the Seven originate from seven gates?

The correspondence is obvious in Aeschylus, but early lists are unstable. It is possible that a pre-existing war tale was reshaped into seven pairings; possible also that gate topography generated the canonical number. The surviving evidence cannot establish direction of influence.

### Hero cult and epic priority

Older scholarship often sought a linear sequence: historical grave → local cult → epic legend. Adrastus resists that simplicity. Sicyon’s cult is securely reported, yet his Attic and Megarian connections may reflect epic diffusion rather than primordial tomb worship. Theories treating every reported tomb as the grave of a historical chieftain are now methodologically discredited.

Farnell’s *Greek Hero Cults and Ideas of Immortality* remains useful as a collection of testimonia but reflects early-twentieth-century evolutionary assumptions. Ekroth’s ritual studies stress that “hero cult” covers diverse sacrificial practices and cannot be reconstructed from literary labels alone. Herodotus gives concrete ritual transfers; Pausanias’s bare “honors” is much thinner evidence.

### Origins of tragedy

Herodotus’s statement that Sicyonian choruses sang Adrastus’s sufferings “not Dionysus’s” was once made a cornerstone of theories deriving tragedy from hero lament. It proves the existence of tragic choruses in Adrastus’s cult as Herodotus understood it. It does not by itself prove the origin of Attic tragedy, a direct institutional line to Athens, or the form of those performances.

### Oral-formulaic and cyclic questions

The Homeric allusions show that listeners were expected to know more than the poems narrate. Whether that knowledge came from a stable *Thebaid*, multiple oral songs, local performance traditions, or an evolving cycle remains disputed. Later scholia that quote “the *Thebaid*” attest an attributed text in antiquity, but do not let scholars reconstruct a unitary seventh-century edition.

### Genealogy and kingship

M. L. West, Jonathan Hall, Margalit Finkelberg, Fowler, and others have examined how Argive genealogies encode competing dynastic and territorial claims. One reconstruction interprets Adrastus’s insertion into Sicyonian and Argive lines as negotiation between patrilineal heroic genealogy and succession through Polybus’s daughter. This is **[MSR]**, useful but not an ancient explanation.

### Major modern works

- L. R. Farnell, *Greek Hero Cults and Ideas of Immortality* (1921): collection and interpretation of cult testimony.
- Timothy Gantz, *Early Greek Myth* (1993): source-by-source reconstruction of archaic and classical variants.
- Robert L. Fowler, *Early Greek Mythography*, vols. 1–2 (2000, 2013): fragments and analysis of genealogical traditions.
- M. L. West, *Greek Epic Fragments* (Loeb, 2003): critical text and translation of cyclic fragments.
- Gregory Nagy, *The Best of the Achaeans* and work on Greek hero cult: performance, kleos, and cultic poetics.
- Walter Burkert, comparative studies of Greek and Near Eastern myth and ritual.
- Gunnel Ekroth, studies of sacrificial ritual and the categories of hero cult.
- *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae* (LIMC), “Adrastos”: principal iconographic catalogue.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Adrastus was a securely historical Mycenaean king [MI/MSR presented as fact].
- His name appears in Linear B [MI; no accepted attestation found].
- He was a demigod by birth [MI].
- He was one of the Argonauts [MI; this confuses him with Talaus].
- Homer narrates him as sole survivor of the Seven [MI; Homer only names Sicyon and Arion].
- The lion and boar were always literal pelts [MI; this is a late variant].
- Cleisthenes physically transported Adrastus’s bones out of Sicyon [MI; Herodotus reports ritual displacement, not relic translation].
- His Sicyonian heroon has been securely excavated and identified [MI].
- Adrastus and Nemesis/Adrasteia were originally one deity [unsupported SR].
- Every ancient list includes Adrastus among the seven gate champions [false; Aeschylus excludes him].
- Hyginus’s self-immolation and Pausanias’s death at Megara describe successive events [false harmonization].

## 10. Conclusions on the State of the Evidence

1. Adrastus is securely an archaic figure, not a Classical invention: Homer and Tyrtaeus presuppose him.
2. The earliest record is extremely compressed. The full familiar biography is assembled from Pindar, tragedy, lost-epic fragments, Herodotus, and much later mythography.
3. The Sicyonian cult is unusually well documented in literary terms but not securely matched to excavated remains.
4. His status as sole survivor through Arion is ancient, though Homer only establishes the celebrated horse-owner relationship.
5. The lion-and-boar marriages are Classical; literal pelts are a later elaboration.
6. “The Seven” never had one uncontested membership. Whether Adrastus counts among them depends on the source.
7. Euripides’s Eleusinian supplication is both mythic narrative and Athenian civic ideology.
8. The successful Epigoni campaign reverses the first expedition’s mortality pattern and culminates in Adrastus’s loss of Aegialeus.
9. Adrastus’s death is not uniform: Megarian old age and grief, Hyginian self-immolation, and simple placement among the dead coexist.
10. No sound evidence identifies a historical Adrastus, a Bronze Age inscription, a divine parent, or an unbroken post-antique cult.
