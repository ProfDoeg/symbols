# Paris (Alexandros): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Trojan prince, archer and epic warrior; neither demigod nor securely attested recipient of hero cult. Later mythographers make him an exposed-and-restored royal child and disastrous judge; Homer treats him as a living mortal, not a cult hero.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Iliad*, composed in monumental form probably in the later eighth or early seventh century BCE, where he is called both **Alexandros** and **Paris**, abducts Helen, fights Menelaus, is rescued by Aphrodite, and is blamed for Troy’s ruin. The earliest surviving image is the Judgment of Alexandros on the Corinthian **Chigi olpe**, about 650–640 BCE. A Bronze Age ruler **Alaksandu of Wilusa**, known from a Hittite treaty of about 1280 BCE, is earlier evidence for a similar name at a place often identified with Homeric Ilios, but not an attestation of the legendary Paris.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early]** Archaic Greek text or image, approximately eighth–sixth century BCE.
- **[E2: classical elaboration]** Fifth–fourth-century BCE literature or art.
- **[E3: Hellenistic/Roman variant]** Hellenistic or imperial-era testimony, including summaries of lost early works.
- **[E4: learned systematization]** Mythographic, scholastic or antiquarian harmonization.
- **[E5: medieval/early-modern accretion]**
- **[E6: modern reception/invention]**
- **[R: modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **[N: negative finding]** No secure evidence presently known.

Dates refer to composition or production, not the often much later manuscript transmitting a text.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

Paris is the son of Priam and Hecuba, brother of Hector, and husband or lover successively of Oenone and Helen. In the surviving *Iliad* he is beautiful, sexually attractive, musically accomplished and sometimes effective as an archer, but Hector, Helen and the narrator associate him with irresponsibility and disaster. His cowardice is not absolute: he accepts combat with Menelaus and kills or wounds several Achaeans. His defining acts—judging the goddesses and taking Helen—cause the divine and human hostility that destroys Troy. **[E1: *Iliad* 3, 6, 7, 11, 13, 24]**

### Names

- **Πάρις, Páris; dialectal/poetic Πάρης, Párēs.** The name has no convincing Greek etymology. Comparison with Anatolian names, especially Hittite/Luwian **Pari-ziti**, “foremost man” or “man in front,” is plausible but unproved. Treating *Paris* as certainly Luwian is a scholarly hypothesis, not an ancient explanation. **[R]**
- **Ἀλέξανδρος, Aléxandros.** Transparently interpretable in Greek as “defender of men” (*alex-* “ward off, defend” + *anēr/andros* “man”). Homer uses Alexandros more frequently than Paris. **[E1]**
- Pseudo-Apollodorus explains that the shepherd-prince acquired “Alexandros” because he defended the flocks from robbers. This is a Greek etiological pun, not proof of the name’s historical origin. **[E4: *Bibliotheca* 3.12.5, probably first–second century CE]**
- Hittite **Alakšanduš/Alaksandu**, ruler of Wilusa and vassal of Muwatalli II, appears in a treaty conventionally dated about 1280 BCE. Paul Kretschmer connected this name with Greek *Alexandros* in 1924. The resemblance is striking and may show circulation of the name between Greek and Anatolian speakers, but the treaty does not mention Helen, Priam, a judgment or a Trojan War. Equating Alaksandu with Paris is therefore **modern reconstruction**, not historical identification. Some scholars also dispute whether Wilusa must be Troy. **[R]** [University of Chicago treatment of Wilusa and Hittite history](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/as26.pdf); [critical reassessment](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/gephyra/article/623455)

### Homeric descriptions and epithets

- **δῖος Ἀλέξανδρος**, “divine/noble Alexandros”: a conventional heroic epithet, not a statement that Paris is a god. **[E1: *Iliad* 3.16 and elsewhere]**
- **θεοειδής**, “godlike in appearance”: stresses beauty. **[E1: *Iliad* 3]**
- **Δύσπαρις**, “accursed Paris,” used by Hector: a hostile punning compound. **[E1: *Iliad* 3.39; 13.769]**
- **Ἑλένης πόσις ἠϋκόμοιο**, “husband of fair-haired Helen.” **[E1]**
- Son of Priam/Priamid; the Greeks also call him the cause or origin of the quarrel. **[E1]**

### Etruscan and Latin forms

- Etruscan mirror inscriptions render Alexandros as **Elχsntre, Alcsentre, Aleχsantre** and related spellings. These identify the Trojan judge within imported Greek iconography; they do not establish an independent Etruscan hero. **[E3: chiefly fourth–third centuries BCE]**
- Latin normally uses **Paris** and **Alexander**. Virgil, Ovid, Hyginus and later Latin authors inherit and reshape Greek traditions. **[E3]**

### Status and identifications

Paris was consistently classified as a mortal Trojan prince. No ancient source securely identifies him with a god, saint or culture hero. Euphranor’s famous fourth-century BCE bronze reportedly showed Paris simultaneously as judge of the goddesses, lover of Helen and killer of Achilles; this condensed his narrative identities without deifying him. **[E2/E3: Pliny, *Natural History* 34.77]**

Claims that Paris began as an Anatolian vegetation, beauty or solar god belong mainly to nineteenth- and early twentieth-century comparativism and lack independent cultic evidence. **[R, now generally disfavored]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest literary evidence

#### Homer, *Iliad*

**Greek; monumental composition usually dated about 750–650 BCE; earliest substantial manuscripts are Hellenistic papyri, while the complete medieval tradition is much later. [E1]**

Paris/Alexandros:

- advances as a champion and then recoils from Menelaus; Hector rebukes him (*Iliad* 3.15–57);
- proposes a duel for Helen and her property (3.67–75);
- is defeated and nearly dragged into the Greek lines until Aphrodite breaks his helmet-strap and transports him to his bedroom (3.340–382);
- has sex with Helen after Aphrodite threatens her into joining him (3.383–448);
- is rebuked by Hector for remaining away from battle (6.312–369);
- returns to combat with Hector (7.1–16);
- wounds Diomedes in the foot and boasts, but Diomedes belittles archery (11.369–400);
- kills Euchenor (13.660–672) and wounds or kills other Achaeans in the poem’s battle catalogues;
- is blamed in the final book for insulting Hera and Athena and praising Aphrodite, who gave him “grievous lust” (24.25–30).

The poem thus securely knows the judgment, the taking of Helen and Aphrodite’s patronage. It does **not** narrate Paris’s exposure, Oenone, the golden apple, Achilles’ death, Paris’s own death or a cult. The Judgment passage has occasionally been suspected as interpolation, but it belongs to the transmitted text and is also consistent with early iconography; individual modern editors have athetized only part of the wording. **[E1/R]** [Greek and translation of *Iliad* 3](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad3.html); [discussion of *Iliad* 24.25–30](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/iliad-24-and-the-judgement-of-paris1/45F7669651D636F1F0B3063F17648D82)

#### Homer, *Odyssey*

Paris never appears alive in the *Odyssey*. Menelaus and Helen recall the war and Helen’s eventual return, but the poem does not supply Paris’s death. **[E1; N]**

### Early visual evidence

The Corinthian **Chigi olpe**, about 650–640 BCE, labels the judge **ΑΛΕΞΑΝΔΡΟΣ**. Hermes leads Hera, Athena and Aphrodite toward him. It is the earliest securely surviving representation of the Judgment and roughly contemporary with the formation of the Epic Cycle. No golden apple is visible or required. **[E1]** [study of the Chigi vase](https://www.ascsa.edu.gr/uploads/media/hesperia/3182058.pdf)

Other archaic monuments known through Pausanias include:

- the Judgment on the **Chest of Cypselus** at Olympia, probably early sixth century BCE; Paris is accompanied by Hermes and the goddesses; **[E1 image lost; Pausanias 5.19.5]**
- the Judgment on the **Throne of Apollo at Amyclae**, made by Bathycles, conventionally sixth century BCE. **[E1 image lost; Pausanias 3.18.12]**

### Epic Cycle

#### *Cypria*

**Greek epic, probably seventh or sixth century BCE; lost. Its plot survives principally in Proclus’s summary, preserved through medieval manuscripts of the *Chrestomathy*, and in quotations by later writers. [E1 content through E3/E4 witnesses]**

The poem began with Zeus and Themis planning the war, the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, and Eris producing a quarrel among Athena, Hera and Aphrodite. Zeus ordered Hermes to conduct the goddesses to Alexandros on Ida. Promised marriage with Helen, he selected Aphrodite. Aphrodite instructed him in shipbuilding or the expedition; Helenus and Cassandra prophesied disaster; Paris reached Sparta; Menelaus departed for Crete; Paris and Helen left with property; Hera sent a storm; they reached Sidon before Troy.

Important limits:

- Proclus mentions Eris but **not a golden apple**.
- The goddesses’ differentiated bribes—Hera’s kingship, Athena’s victory, Aphrodite’s Helen—are supplied by later testimonia and may preserve early material, but cannot all be demonstrated verbatim from the lost poem.
- Whether Helen left willingly, under Aphrodite’s compulsion or by violent abduction varies with source and genre.

A translation of Proclus’s summary is available in the public-domain *Hesiod, Homeric Hymns and Homerica*. [Proclus’s *Cypria* summary](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Hesiod%2C_The_Homeric_Hymns%2C_and_Homerica.djvu/545)

#### *Aethiopis* and *Little Iliad*

The *Aethiopis*, attributed to Arctinus and usually dated to the seventh or sixth century BCE, narrated Achilles’ death after he killed Memnon. Proclus’s surviving summary says Apollo and Paris killed Achilles. The *Little Iliad*, probably later archaic, proceeded after Achilles’ funeral and included Philoctetes’ return and Paris’s death. The precise allocation of the fatal shot among Paris and Apollo varied. **[E1 material through later summaries]**

### Hesiodic corpus

The fragmentary *Catalogue of Women*, composed probably in the sixth century BCE though transmitted on Hellenistic and Roman papyri and by quotations, treated Helen’s genealogy and suitors. It is important background to her marriage and the oath of the suitors but preserves no continuous Paris narrative. Claims that “Hesiod tells the complete story of Paris” overstate the remains. **[E1/N]** [surviving *Catalogue* fragments](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodCatalogues.html); [Berlin papyrus of Helen’s suitors](https://berlpap.smb.museum/02696/)

### Lyric poetry and early mythography

- **Sappho fr. 16**, about 600 BCE, presents Helen leaving husband, child and parents for Troy under Aphrodite’s power; Paris is implicit rather than the poem’s emotional center. **[E1]**
- **Alcaeus fr. 42**, about 600 BCE, contrasts Helen and Thetis and makes Helen’s marriage destructive to Troy. **[E1]**
- **Ibycus** and **Stesichorus**, sixth century BCE, treated Trojan material; Stesichorus’s *Palinode* generated the variant that only Helen’s phantom went to Troy. Paris’s precise role survives incompletely. **[E1 fragments]**
- **Pindar, Paean 8a**, early fifth century BCE, provides the earliest surviving testimony to Hecuba’s ominous prenatal vision and Cassandra’s warning. The fragment does not securely preserve every later detail of exposure. **[E2]** [Pindar’s principal fragments](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/ekh150b2445809.pdf)
- **Hellanicus, Pherecydes, Acusilaus and other early mythographers** are fragmentary and must be recovered through later quotations; Fowler’s *Early Greek Mythography* is the standard collection. **[E1/E2 through later witnesses]**

### Tragedy

- **Sophocles, *Alexandros* or *Krisis***: lost and of uncertain date. The fragments do not permit a secure full plot. It probably concerned Paris or the Judgment, but modern reconstructions vary. **[E2 fragments]**
- **Euripides, *Alexandros*, 415 BCE**: first play of the Trojan trilogy completed by *Palamedes* and *Trojan Women*. Fragments and Hyginus support a plot in which the exposed Paris returned as a shepherd, won funeral games held in his own memory, faced murder by Hecuba and Deiphobus, and was recognized and restored despite Cassandra’s warning. Details such as the identifying rattle or bag belong to reconstruction from later mythography and Roman adaptation. **[E2/E4]** [review of the fragmentary play and its reconstruction](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2018/2018.09.56)
- **Euripides, *Andromache* 274–308**, about 425 BCE: blames the divine beauty contest and says Cassandra had urged Paris’s death as an infant. **[E2]**
- **Euripides, *Trojan Women* 919–950**, 415 BCE: Helen invokes the Judgment and Aphrodite in her defense; Hecuba rationalizes the goddesses as Paris’s own desire and ambition. **[E2]**
- **Euripides, *Helen* 17–48**, 412 BCE: Hera substitutes a phantom Helen; Hermes carries the real Helen to Egypt. Paris receives only an image. **[E2: radical tragic variant]**
- **Euripides, *Iphigenia at Aulis* 573–589, 1284–1310**, about 405 BCE: the Judgment is embedded in wedding and war causation. **[E2]**

### Hellenistic and imperial Greek texts

- **Lycophron, *Alexandra***, early third century BCE or later in its transmitted form: Cassandra’s riddling prophecy includes Paris, Oenone and the war; scholia preserve further variants. **[E3]**
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 4.148–150**, third century BCE: in a simile, Paris is still herding on Ida when Hera, Athena and Aphrodite contend, a chronological allusion rather than a full narrative. **[E3]**
- **Parthenius, *Erotica Pathemata* 4 and 34**, first century BCE: records Oenone traditions, including Corythus, a son connected with Paris and Helen. **[E3]**
- **Diodorus Siculus, *Library* 4**, first century BCE: embeds Trojan genealogy in universal history. **[E3/E4]**
- **Conon, *Narrations***, Augustan age, survives in Photius’s ninth-century epitome; it preserves an Oenone version. **[E3 through medieval epitome]**
- **Strabo, *Geography* 13.1**, about 7 BCE–23 CE: localizes the Judgment on Mount Alexandreia above Antandrus, reporting a Troad landscape tradition rather than cult. **[E3]** [Strabo 13.1](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/13A3%2A.html)
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 3.12.5 and *Epitome* 3–5**, probably first or second century CE: systematic birth, exposure, Oenone, Judgment, Helen, Achilles and death narrative. **[E4]** [*Bibliotheca* 3.12.5](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DLibrary%3Abook%3D3%3Achapter%3D12%3Asection%3D5)
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece***, second century CE: describes archaic Judgment monuments and other artistic representations. **[E3 antiquarian witness]**
- **Lucian, *Dialogues of the Gods* 20**, second century CE: comic dramatization of the Judgment and explicit apple. **[E3]**
- **Aelian, *Varia Historia* 9.38**, early third century CE: reports that Alexander the Great saw Paris’s lyre at Ilium but was unimpressed, preferring Achilles’ lyre. This is relic lore, not proof that the instrument was ancient. **[E3]**
- **Philostratus, *Heroicus***, third century CE: participates in the wider Trojan hero tradition but supplies no secure Paris cult. **[E3/N]**
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 10**, probably third century CE: fullest surviving account of Paris’s wounding by Philoctetes, Oenone’s refusal and remorse, Paris’s death and Oenone’s self-immolation. **[E3]** [*Posthomerica* 10](https://www.theoi.com/Text/QuintusSmyrnaeus10.html)
- **Colluthus, *Rape of Helen***, late fifth or early sixth century CE: surviving miniature epic of the Judgment, Paris’s voyage and Helen’s departure. **[E3, late antique elaboration]**

### Latin corpus

- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 1 and 6**, 29–19 BCE: Juno’s resentment at Paris’s judgment helps explain her hostility to the Trojans; *Aeneid* 5.370 identifies Paris as a boxer in later recollection. **[E3]**
- **Ovid, *Heroides* 5**, about 20–10 BCE: Oenone addresses Paris; she recalls their pastoral marriage, his carved declarations of love, Cassandra’s warning and his abandonment. **[E3 literary elaboration]**
- **Ovid, *Heroides* 16–17**, of disputed Ovidian authenticity but ancient: Paris and Helen exchange seductive letters; Paris narrates the Judgment in detail. **[E3; authorship disputed]**
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 12.580–628**, about 8 CE: Apollo directs Paris’s arrow against Achilles. **[E3]**
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 90–92, 107, 110**, compiled in its surviving form probably first–second century CE: systematic exposure, recognition, Judgment and war narratives; *Fabula* 107 makes Apollo assume Paris’s appearance when Achilles is killed. **[E4]**
- **Seneca, *Troades***, first century CE: Paris is dead but remains causally present in Troy’s catastrophe. **[E3]**
- **Dictys Cretensis** and **Dares Phrygius**, surviving in Latin versions of late antiquity: pseudohistorical “eyewitness” chronicles that suppress or rationalize divine machinery and profoundly influenced medieval Europe. Their claimed eyewitness status is fictitious. **[E3/E5]**
- **Fulgentius and the Vatican Mythographers**, late antique to medieval: allegorize the Judgment and systematize its moral meaning. **[E5]**

## 3. The Story

### Parentage, dream and exposure

**[E1]** Homer knows Paris as Priam’s son and Hector’s brother but says nothing about an exposure.

**[E2]** Pindar’s *Paean* 8a and Euripidean allusions attest the ominous prenatal sign by the fifth century BCE: Hecuba’s child will burn or destroy Troy, and Cassandra urges that he be killed.

**[E2/E4]** The reconstructed *Alexandros* of Euripides and the continuous account of Pseudo-Apollodorus say that Hecuba dreamed she bore a firebrand whose flames consumed Troy. Priam consulted his son Aesacus, an interpreter of dreams; the infant was exposed on Ida. A herdsman—usually Agelaus—found and raised him. In some late forms a she-bear suckled the infant for several days before the shepherd accepted the survival as a divine sign.

The firebrand, exposure, shepherd and animal-nursing motifs should not be projected wholesale back into Homer. The securely datable development is:

1. Paris is Priam’s son in Homer;
2. an ominous dream and demand for infant death are attested in fifth-century poetry;
3. the detailed exposure and fosterage survive in imperial mythography, possibly drawing on Euripidean drama.

### Shepherd and recognition

Paris grew up among shepherds on Mount Ida. Pseudo-Apollodorus derives “Alexandros” from his defense of the herds. **[E4]**

In the reconstruction of Euripides’ *Alexandros*, Priam and Hecuba held games in memory of the supposedly dead prince. Paris’s best bull was taken as a prize; he entered the games, defeated his royal brothers and aroused Deiphobus’s hostility. Hecuba nearly killed him before recognition restored him to the royal house. Cassandra continued to predict ruin. **[E2 reconstructed from fragments; E4 fuller forms]**

Hyginus supplies much of the continuous story. It is unsafe to assign every Hyginian detail to Euripides.

### Oenone

Oenone is a nymph of Mount Ida and daughter of the river-god Cebren. Paris married or lived with her during his pastoral youth. She possessed prophecy and healing knowledge, often attributed to Apollo’s instruction. Their union is securely visible only in later literary evidence, although it may be older. **[E3: Parthenius; Ovid, *Heroides* 5; E4: Apollodorus]**

Variants:

- Ovid makes Oenone the abandoned rustic wife and Paris a perjurer who carved their names into trees. **[E3]**
- Some sources give them a son **Corythus**. In Parthenius 34, Corythus goes to Troy, Helen falls in love with him, and Paris kills him without recognizing him—or kills him out of jealousy, depending on reconstruction. **[E3]**
- Oenone foretells that Paris will be wounded and instructs him to return to her for healing. **[E3/E4]**

### Judgment of the goddesses

At the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, Eris initiated a dispute between Hera, Athena and Aphrodite. Zeus declined to judge and sent them with Hermes to Paris on Ida. Paris awarded victory to Aphrodite in exchange for Helen. **[E1: *Cypria* summary; *Iliad* 24 presupposes the judgment]**

Development and variants:

- The *Iliad* says the goddesses entered Paris’s farm enclosure and that he praised the one who gave him destructive lust. It does not mention Hermes, bribes or an apple. **[E1]**
- The *Cypria* included Eris, Hermes, the three goddesses and Aphrodite’s promise of marriage with Helen. **[E1 through Proclus]**
- The differentiated offers—Hera: kingship; Athena: military victory or wisdom; Aphrodite: Helen—are clearly stated in classical and later treatments. **[E2/E3]**
- The **golden apple inscribed “for the fairest”** is late: explicit in Lucian and Hyginus, not in Homer or Proclus’s *Cypria* summary. Calling it a securely Homeric object is a modern popular conflation. **[E3; modern misattribution]** [source-index and texts for the Judgment](https://www.theoi.com/Olympios/JudgementParis.html)
- Some vase paintings show Paris trying to flee or being physically conducted by Hermes, emphasizing an imposed task; others make him an assured judge. **[E1/E2 iconographic variants]**

The Judgment functions in archaic epic as an explanation for Aphrodite’s protection and Hera’s and Athena’s hostility to Troy. Later moralizations turn it into a choice among power, military glory and erotic desire.

### Voyage to Sparta and Helen

The *Cypria* made Aphrodite encourage Paris’s expedition. Phereclus built the ships; Helenus and Cassandra warned of disaster. Paris was received by Menelaus at Sparta; when Menelaus left for Crete, Paris departed with Helen and property. Hera sent a storm, and Paris sacked Sidon before reaching Troy. **[E1 through Proclus]**

The moral and legal characterization varies:

- **Abduction and theft:** Homer repeatedly calls Paris’s act a taking and emphasizes stolen property and violated guest-friendship. Hector says Paris should never have been born; Helen condemns herself and Paris. **[E1]**
- **Erotic flight under Aphrodite:** Sappho stresses the power that made Helen abandon husband, child and parents. **[E1]**
- **Willing elopement:** some lyric, Cyclic and later romantic treatments make Helen consent. **[E1/E3]**
- **Divinely compelled departure:** Aphrodite’s coercive power, vividly displayed over Helen in *Iliad* 3, complicates consent. **[E1]**
- **Rape/violent seizure:** Greek and Latin titles frequently use *harpagē/raptus*, terms covering abduction and seizure, not necessarily a single uniform account of sexual violence. Sources must be read separately.
- **Phantom Helen:** Stesichorus’s *Palinode*, Herodotus 2.112–120 and Euripides’ *Helen* detach the real Helen from Troy. Herodotus says Paris was driven to Egypt, where Proteus detained Helen and her property; Paris reached Troy without her. Euripides substitutes a divinely created phantom. **[E1/E2 regional or literary alternatives]**

In every principal ancient version, taking Helen while enjoying Menelaus’s hospitality constitutes a grave breach of *xenia*. Modern treatments that present an uncomplicated Romeo-and-Juliet romance suppress much of the ancient ethical and coercive framework. **[E6]**

### War and duel with Menelaus

Paris proposes single combat in *Iliad* 3 after Hector shames him. The terms are Helen and her possessions; the loser’s side should recognize the outcome. Paris’s spear fails to penetrate Menelaus’s shield. Menelaus breaks his sword but seizes Paris by the helmet and drags him. Aphrodite breaks the chinstrap, envelops Paris in mist and deposits him in his perfumed bedroom. She then compels Helen—threatening abandonment and death—to join him. **[E1]**

The truce fails not because Paris legitimately wins but because Athena, acting under Zeus’s decision and Hera’s pressure, induces Pandarus to shoot Menelaus. **[E1: *Iliad* 4]**

The episode establishes several simultaneous traditions:

- Paris can accept formal combat;
- Menelaus is physically superior;
- Aphrodite’s intervention, not Paris’s prowess, saves him;
- Helen expresses contempt for him;
- erotic power immediately replaces martial failure.

Paris later rejoins battle. He wounds Diomedes in the foot, provoking the reply that an archer’s shot is insignificant; he kills **Euchenor**, son of Polyidus, who knowingly chose battle over death by disease. Ancient and modern summaries that call him wholly inactive overlook these episodes. **[E1: *Iliad* 11.369–400; 13.660–672]**

### Achilles’ death

The *Iliad* predicts Achilles’ death but does not narrate it. Hector’s dying prophecy says Paris and Apollo will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates (*Iliad* 22.359–360). **[E1]**

Variants:

- **Paris and Apollo jointly:** *Aethiopis* summary and Homeric prophecy. **[E1]**
- **Paris fires; Apollo guides the arrow:** Virgil and Ovid. **[E3]**
- **Apollo disguised as Paris fires:** Hyginus *Fabula* 107. **[E4]**
- Later art and literature place the wound in Achilles’ heel. The early epic evidence does not say Achilles was physically invulnerable everywhere except the heel; that familiar formulation is a later synthesis. **[E3/E4]**
- A romance variant makes Achilles enter Apollo’s sanctuary to marry Polyxena and be ambushed by Paris and Deiphobus. This is later than the early epic account and especially prominent in late antique and medieval traditions. **[E3/E5]**

### Death of Paris

The *Little Iliad* included Philoctetes’ killing of Paris; Pseudo-Apollodorus and Quintus Smyrnaeus provide fuller narratives. **[E1 content through later witnesses; E3/E4 narration]**

Philoctetes, returned from Lemnos with Heracles’ poisoned bow, wounds Paris. Paris seeks Oenone, whose healing arts alone might save him. She refuses because he abandoned her. He dies while being carried back or shortly afterward. Oenone repents too late.

Oenone’s death varies:

- she hangs herself: Pseudo-Apollodorus; **[E4]**
- she leaps from a height: Lycophron/scholia; **[E3/E4]**
- she throws herself onto Paris’s funeral pyre: Quintus Smyrnaeus; **[E3]**
- in Conon, she kills the messenger who brings the news before killing herself or dying in grief, depending on the epitomized version. **[E3 through medieval epitome]**

Quintus gives Paris a Trojan funeral and makes Oenone the principal sincere mourner. Helen’s position after Paris’s death passes to Deiphobus. **[E3]**

### Body and alleged grave

Quintus describes cremation and burial in literary terms. Later geographical traditions located Paris and Oenone in the Cebrenian/Idaean landscape, but no excavated tomb can be securely identified as theirs. The supposed “tomb of Paris” is therefore a literary-local tradition, not an archaeologically authenticated heroic burial. **[E3/N]**

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

The stable genealogy is:

```text
Dardanus
└── Erichthonius
    └── Tros
        └── Ilus
            └── Laomedon
                └── Priam + Hecuba
                    ├── Hector
                    ├── Paris/Alexandros
                    ├── Deiphobus
                    ├── Helenus
                    ├── Cassandra
                    ├── Polyxena
                    └── numerous other Priamids
```

Paris is mortal on both sides. Hecuba’s father varies:

- **Dymas of Phrygia**: Homeric and common tradition; **[E1/E4]**
- **Cisseus of Thrace**: Euripidean and later genealogy; **[E2/E4]**
- **Sangarius**, a river-god: a later alternative. **[E3/E4]**

Consorts:

- **Oenone**, Idaean nymph, daughter of Cebren; **[E3]**
- **Helen**, daughter of Zeus and Leda or Zeus and Nemesis in competing genealogies, legally wife of Menelaus. **[E1 onward]**

Children are unstable and late:

- **Corythus**, usually by Oenone, sometimes entangled with Helen; **[E3]**
- **Bunomus/Bunicus, Aganus, Idaeus** and a daughter **Helen**, attributed to Helen and Paris in Dictys, Tzetzes or Ptolemy Chennus; **[E3/E4]**
- No child of Paris is important in Homer. **[N]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Cult

No securely documented sanctuary, priesthood, festival, sacrifice or oracle dedicated specifically to Paris/Alexandros is known. He is therefore not demonstrably a **heroized dead with cult** in the manner of Achilles, Ajax, Helen, Menelaus or Orestes. **[N]**

Evidence sometimes mistaken for cult includes:

- Mount **Alexandreia** above Antandrus, where Strabo says the Judgment occurred. This is mythic landscape localization, not proof of sacrifice to Paris. **[E3]**
- artistic representations in sanctuaries, including the Chest of Cypselus at Olympia and Apollo’s throne at Amyclae. These honor or narrate a myth within larger decorative programs; they are not Paris shrines. **[E1/N]**
- Paris’s alleged lyre shown at Ilium to Alexander the Great. This is a relic-display anecdote; no associated ritual is reported. **[E3/N]**
- literary graves of Paris and Oenone in the Ida/Cebren region. No securely identified archaeological *heroon* or inscribed dedication confirms worship. **[E3/N]**

Farnell’s early survey of Greek hero cult and later work by Burkert, Nagy and Ekroth clarify that a figure’s epic fame, grave tradition or sanctuary image does not by itself establish cult. Paris is a useful negative case.

### Tomb and body

Quintus Smyrnaeus narrates a funeral pyre and shared death imagery with Oenone. Later geographical and scholastic traditions place their burial in the Troad. There is no excavated grave with an ancient inscription naming Paris, no identifiable bones and no record of politically transferring his relics. **[E3/N]**

### Ritual and names

The personal name **Alexandros** was widely used historically, but ordinary use cannot be assumed to commemorate Paris. “Paris” also became a personal name in Greek and Roman contexts. Neither usage constitutes a securely attested Paris cult. **[N]**

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### 1. Archaic formation, c. 750–500 BCE

The *Iliad*, early lyric, the *Cypria*, *Aethiopis*, *Little Iliad* and vase painting establish the durable core:

- two names, Paris and Alexandros;
- Trojan prince and husband of Helen;
- judgment of Hera, Athena and Aphrodite;
- Aphrodite’s patronage;
- taking of Helen;
- defeat by Menelaus;
- archer-warrior;
- shared responsibility with Apollo for Achilles’ death;
- death at Philoctetes’ hands in the post-*Iliad* cycle.

The Chigi vase demonstrates that the Judgment circulated visually by the mid-seventh century BCE. **[E1]**

### 2. Classical moral and dramatic reinterpretation, c. 500–323 BCE

Pindar and tragedy develop the ominous birth, Cassandra’s warning, exposure and recognition. Euripides repeatedly uses Paris to examine causation and blame:

- *Andromache*: the child should have been destroyed;
- *Trojan Women*: Helen blames Aphrodite, while Hecuba interprets “Aphrodite” as lust;
- *Helen*: the divine quarrel produces only a phantom;
- *Alexandros*: social status, innate nobility and recognition stand against prophecy.

Visual art expands the Judgment into a favorite scene. Paris may be a shepherd, prince, judge, reluctant participant or erotically charged spectator. **[E2]**

### 3. Hellenistic and Roman consolidation, 323 BCE–third century CE

Mythographers join formerly discrete episodes into a life-like sequence. Ovid gives Oenone and Paris subjective erotic voices; Virgil makes the Judgment part of Rome’s inherited Trojan past; Hyginus and Apollodorus standardize exposure, recognition and death. Imperial art increasingly presents the goddesses nude and Paris in Phrygian dress. **[E3/E4]**

Roman wall painting at Pompeii and mosaics throughout the empire show Paris, Helen and the Judgment. The scene becomes suitable for domestic display because it combines learned mythology, erotic spectacle and a consequential act of choice.

### 4. Late antiquity

Quintus reconstructs the missing post-Homeric war and turns Paris’s death into a long romantic tragedy with Oenone. Colluthus makes the abduction of Helen the center of a compact epic. Allegorists interpret the goddesses as competing forms of life—power, wisdom or pleasure—and Paris as the chooser of sensuality. **[E3]**

### 5. Medieval transformation

Western medieval readers often knew Troy through Dares, Dictys, Virgil, Ovid and late Latin mythography rather than Greek Homer. Paris consequently became a historical-seeming prince and courtly lover.

- Benoît de Sainte-Maure’s Old French *Roman de Troie*, c. 1155–1165, recast Trojan narrative for an aristocratic court. **[E5]**
- Guido delle Colonne’s Latin *Historia destructionis Troiae*, completed 1287, presented the material as history and circulated exceptionally widely. **[E5]**
- Translations and adaptations appeared in French, Italian, Iberian, German, English, Norse, Byzantine Greek and Slavic traditions. [Medieval transmission overview](https://medievalfrancophone.ac.uk/textual-traditions-and-segments/troie/index.html)
- Medieval artists often depict Paris as an armored knight who experiences the Judgment in a dream. This derives from medieval Troy books and allegorical conventions, not ancient iconography. **[E5]**
- Trojan-origin legends served dynastic and national genealogies, but Paris himself was rarely the putative founder; Aeneas, Brutus, Francus and Antenor were more useful. **[E5]**

### 6. Renaissance and Baroque

Recovery of Greek texts and sustained use of Ovid made the Judgment a major vehicle for the nude, court allegory and the aesthetics of choice.

- Marcantonio Raimondi’s engraving after Raphael, c. 1510–1520, arranged the Judgment as a grand assembly of gods; its lower-right reclining figures later contributed to Manet’s composition for *Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe*. **[E5/E6]** [Metropolitan Museum entry](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/337058)
- Lucas Cranach the Elder repeatedly placed an armored, sleeping or seated Paris in a northern forest; about a dozen painted versions and two woodcuts survive from his workshop tradition. **[E5]** [National Gallery of Art, 1508 woodcut](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/6053-judgment-paris)
- Peter Paul Rubens painted the Judgment repeatedly. His monumental version of about 1638 was commissioned by Philip IV of Spain in 1637–1638, transforming the myth into a display of flesh, textiles, animals and political magnificence. **[E5]** [Prado catalogue](https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-judgement-of-paris/f8b061e1-8248-42ae-81f8-6acb5b1d5a0a)

### 7. Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

The Judgment remained a formal pretext for comparing female beauty, while Paris and Helen became increasingly romantic.

- Jacques-Louis David, *The Loves of Paris and Helen*, 1788, depicts an idealized musical and erotic pair rather than abduction, coercion and war. **[E6]** [Louvre catalogue](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010063647)
- Goethe’s *Faust II* uses Paris and Helen within a classicizing pageant, 1832. **[E6]**
- Tennyson’s “Oenone,” 1829 and revised 1842, shifts sympathy to the abandoned nymph; “The Death of Oenone,” 1892, returns to her fatal reunion with Paris. **[E6]**
- Offenbach’s operetta *La belle Hélène*, 1864, turns Paris’s divinely promised seduction into satire of bourgeois sexual morality and imperial society. **[E6]**
- Academic and Symbolist painting alternately eroticized the Judgment or moralized Paris’s choice. **[E6]**

### 8. Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

- Film versions include *Helena* (1924), *Helen of Troy* (1956), *The Trojan Women* (1971), the television miniseries *Helen of Troy* (2003), Wolfgang Petersen’s *Troy* (2004), and *Troy: Fall of a City* (2018). **[E6]**
- *Troy* gives Paris survival and escape with Helen, kills Menelaus at Troy and removes the gods: all are deliberate modern inventions, not ancient consensus. [Film overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy_%28film%29)
- Novels increasingly restore Helen’s, Oenone’s or Trojan women’s perspectives. Examples include Margaret George’s *Helen of Troy* (2006), Pat Barker’s Trojan novels and Claire Heywood’s *Daughters of Sparta* (2021). **[E6]**
- Paris appears in comics, television, strategy games and role-playing games, usually reduced to “handsome prince,” “cowardly lover” or “archer who killed Achilles.” These are selective receptions rather than complete ancient characterizations.
- “The Judgment of Paris” also became the name for the 1976 blind wine tasting organized by Steven Spurrier. That commercial-cultural phrase borrows the choice motif but has no connection to ancient ritual. **[E6]**

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Dominant modern images

Three modern Parises coexist:

1. **The culpable seducer:** inherited from Homeric blame, Christian moralization and histories of adultery.
2. **The beautiful but weak lover:** strengthened by neoclassical painting, Romantic poetry and cinema.
3. **The reluctant chooser caught by divine politics:** encouraged by modern readings of vase scenes in which Hermes drives or restrains him.

None alone exhausts the ancient evidence.

### Gender and sexuality

Ancient texts contrast Paris’s beauty, music, luxurious clothing and archery with Hector’s spear-fighting masculinity. Nineteenth-century scholarship sometimes converted this contrast into racialized claims about “effeminate Asia” versus “virile Greece.” Those formulations reflect modern Orientalism and should not be treated as recovered Bronze Age ideology. **[R, historically important but discredited in racial form]**

Modern feminist interpretation emphasizes that:

- the Judgment makes women objects of comparison;
- Aphrodite’s “gift” is another person already married;
- Helen’s consent is unstable and sometimes overridden;
- *Iliad* 3 explicitly represents Aphrodite threatening Helen;
- Oenone bears the consequences of Paris’s abandonment.

These are interpretations anchored in ancient narrative, but their theoretical formulation belongs chiefly to twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship. **[R]**

### Psychological and structural readings

- **Freudian and psychoanalytic readings**, from the early twentieth century onward, interpret the three goddesses as competing desires or maternal/sexual figures. These are modern hermeneutics, not ancient doctrine. **[R]**
- **Otto Rank’s exposed-hero pattern** and **Lord Raglan’s hero pattern** illuminate Paris’s ominous birth, exposure, fosterage, return and recognition. Paris does not fit every stage, particularly divine parentage, accession and cultic death. **[R]**
- **Structuralist readings** treat the Judgment as mediation among sovereignty, war and sexuality, or among alternative forms of social power. Comparisons with Dumézil’s trifunctional model are suggestive but disputed: Hera, Athena and Aphrodite do not map neatly onto a historically demonstrated Indo-European triad in every source. **[R]**
- **Campbell’s monomyth** poorly fits Paris. His exposure and return resemble an initiation, but his central choice produces catastrophe and he never completes a redemptive return. Claims that Paris is a standard “hero’s journey” protagonist are modern schematization. **[R]**
- **Nagy’s work on epic heroism** helps explain Paris as a foil within a competitive heroic system, but Paris lacks the securely attested cultic afterlife central to many Greek heroes. **[R]**

### Politics and nationalism

Paris is less frequently appropriated as a national founder than Aeneas or Hector. Nevertheless:

- medieval European dynasties claimed Trojan descent through other survivors, embedding Paris’s act within providential “history”; **[E5]**
- early-modern court paintings of the Judgment could flatter, warn or invite comparison among royal women; **[E5]**
- nineteenth-century discussions often racialized Greeks and Trojans as Europe versus Asia; **[E6]**
- modern Turkish tourism and popular historical writing sometimes identify Paris directly with Alaksandu and Troy directly with Wilusa. The Wilusa–Ilios connection is strong but debated at the margins; Alaksandu is not demonstrably the individual Paris. **[R/modern nationalist simplification]**
- No major, sustained extremist cult of Paris comparable to modern appropriations of Sparta, Achilles or Norse heroes is securely documented. **[N]**

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

No evidence establishes a historical individual who performed Paris’s legendary deeds. Archaeology demonstrates that Hisarlık was a major Bronze Age settlement repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt; Hittite texts probably refer to a western Anatolian polity called Wilusa. These facts provide a historical environment, not a biography of Paris.

The **Alaksandu hypothesis** has several levels that must remain separate:

1. Alaksandu was a real ruler named in a Hittite treaty: secure.
2. Wilusa is Homeric Wilios/Ilios: widely accepted but not logically certain.
3. Alaksandu’s name corresponds linguistically to Alexandros: plausible and important.
4. The legendary Paris preserves that ruler’s biography: unsupported.
5. Alaksandu and Paris were the same individual: chronologically and evidentially unproved.

The treaty is commonly dated several generations before the destruction horizon Troy VIIa often associated with Trojan War traditions. Even if the name survived orally, nothing connects the treaty king with Helen or the Judgment. **[R]**

### Dating and priority of the Judgment

The combination of *Iliad* 24 and the Chigi vase makes the Judgment securely archaic. Debate concerns whether the *Iliad* passage is integral and how much of the full story it presupposes. Aristarchus and some modern editors questioned lines or diction; most interpretation accepts at least an early form of the episode. The absence of the apple from both Homer and Proclus is decisive against treating it as securely archaic.

### Paris and Alexandros as originally separate figures

Because Homer alternates two names and because Alexandros is more frequent, scholars have proposed that:

- one name was Greek and one Anatolian;
- “Paris” belonged to a local Trojan figure later merged with a Greek Alexandros;
- Alexandros was once a more formidable warrior;
- the two-name usage reflects oral formula rather than separate origins.

No surviving text presents Paris and Alexandros as two people. Separation is therefore reconstruction, not attested mythology. The name pair may preserve cultural layering, but the merger cannot be dated. **[R]**

### Epic character

Older summaries exaggerated Paris’s cowardice. The *Iliad* deliberately opposes his beauty and erotic gifts to Hector’s discipline, yet Paris accepts combat, returns to battle and successfully uses the bow. The issue is not simple incapacity but unstable heroic value: he causes the conflict, fails in the decisive duel and relies on divine rescue, while remaining lethal at range.

Nagy’s contrast between heroic excellence and social consequence is relevant, but Paris’s lack of known cult distinguishes him from Achilles, Heracles and many locally worshipped heroes.

### Oral-formulaic and cyclic questions

The *Iliad* assumes stories it does not narrate: the Judgment, the abduction and Achilles’ coming death. Oral-traditional scholarship cautions against treating the lost *Cypria* as merely a written “prequel.” Its poet drew on a shared, evolving story-world. Proclus’s late summary cannot establish the original wording or every detail of an archaic performance tradition.

Martin L. West’s *Greek Epic Fragments* and Malcolm Davies’s editions remain fundamental for separating quotations, summaries and conjectural reconstruction. Gantz’s *Early Greek Myth* is especially useful because it organizes variants chronologically instead of producing a seamless synthetic biography.

### Exposure narrative

The first secure surviving evidence for Hecuba’s dream is fifth century BCE. The detailed bear-and-shepherd story is later. Scholars debate whether tragedy invented the exposure narrative or dramatized older oral material. Absence from Homer cannot prove invention; conversely, motif antiquity cannot be assumed without evidence. **[R]**

### Oenone

Oenone is absent from Homer and the surviving *Cypria* summary. Her story may derive from local Idaean tradition, Hellenistic erotic narrative or lost archaic poetry, but current evidence does not securely date it before the Hellenistic record. Ovid’s *Heroides* made her abandonment central to later reception.

### Hero cult

Earlier scholarship sometimes inferred cult wherever epic described a tomb or honored dead. Modern studies by Ekroth and others demand ritual, archaeological or epigraphic evidence. By that standard Paris has no securely established cult. A localized grave story or place named Alexandreia is insufficient.

### Art history

LIMC, under **Alexandros**, catalogues the extensive iconography. The major developmental questions include:

- whether Corinthian representations preceded Attic types;
- whether Paris is willing, hesitant or fleeing;
- how attributes identify goddesses before inscriptions become unnecessary;
- when the apple becomes visually standard;
- how the Phrygian cap constructs “Trojan” easternness;
- how post-antique art changes an adjudication scene into a display of nude female bodies.

The Chigi vase is the indispensable terminus: the subject and the name Alexandros were recognizable by about 650–640 BCE. [Getty discussion of early vase traditions and LIMC classifications](https://www.getty.edu/publications/resources/virtuallibrary/0892361506.pdf)

### Manuscripts and losses

- The *Iliad* survives abundantly, with papyri beginning in the Hellenistic period and a rich medieval manuscript tradition.
- The *Cypria*, *Aethiopis* and *Little Iliad* are lost except for quotations and summaries.
- Euripides’ *Alexandros* survives in fragments and adaptations; reconstructions remain provisional.
- Pindar’s relevant paean is papyrological and incomplete.
- Sophocles’ Paris plays are too fragmentary for confident plots.
- The *Catalogue of Women* is reconstructed from papyri and quotations.
- Conon survives through Photius; Dares and Dictys through complicated late Latin transmission.
- Images often supply evidence independent of surviving texts, particularly the Chigi vase and lost monuments described by Pausanias.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **“The golden apple is in Homer.”** False; Homer does not mention it.
- **“Paris was definitely Alaksandu.”** Unsupported historical identification.
- **“Homer says Achilles was shot in his only vulnerable heel.”** False; the *Iliad* does not narrate the shot or bodily invulnerability.
- **“Helen freely chose Paris in every ancient version.”** False.
- **“Paris was a coward who never fought.”** False; reductive reading of Homer.
- **“A tomb of Paris has been archaeologically discovered.”** No secure evidence.
- **“Paris was worshipped throughout Troy as a god.”** No secure cult evidence.
- **“Oenone appears in Homer.”** False.
- **“The Judgment represents an established Indo-European three-function ritual.”** A modern comparativist proposal, not demonstrated ancient belief.
