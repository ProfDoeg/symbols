# Neoptolemus (Pyrrhus): Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal epic hero; son of Achilles and Deidameia; after death, a **heroized dead with an attested cult at Delphi**; in Molossian dynastic tradition, an ancestral king and founder.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the Homeric poems. *Iliad* 19.326–337, conventionally dated around 700 BCE, knows Achilles’ unnamed son growing up on Scyros. *Odyssey* 4.5–9 and 11.505–537, probably late eighth or early seventh century BCE in substantially its transmitted form, names **Neoptolemus**, makes him Hermione’s husband, and describes his excellence at Troy. The alternative name **Pyrrhus**, Πύρρος, is independently early—apparently already in Archilochus fr. 304 West, seventh century BCE—but explanations that it was his “birth name” derived from Achilles’ disguise as “Pyrrha” are later constructions.
>
> **Evidence labels:** **[EE]** securely attested early material; **[LC]** later canonical elaboration; **[RV]** regional variant; **[LS]** learned systematization; **[SI]** syncretistic identification; **[MA]** medieval/early-modern accretion; **[F]** folklore; **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction; **[MI]** modern invention. Dates refer to composition or production, not to the much later manuscripts through which most texts survive.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

### Orientation

**[EE]** Neoptolemus is the son whom Achilles fathered on Scyros before—or in traditions elaborated after Homer, during—his departure for Troy. After Achilles’ death the Greeks fetch the youth to Troy. He proves his father’s formidable successor: he helps obtain Philoctetes and Heracles’ bow, fights Eurypylus, enters the wooden horse, kills Priam, receives Andromache as captive, and returns from Troy. Yet the record divides sharply over his conduct and fate. The *Odyssey* presents an exemplary, handsome, eloquent and successful warrior. Archaic sack narratives and images turn him into the principal instrument of dynastic extermination: Priam is killed at Zeus’s altar; Astyanax is killed in one important version; Polyxena is sacrificed at Achilles’ grave. Sophocles’ *Philoctetes* makes him a youth whose inherited nobility struggles against Odyssean deceit. Euripides makes him an absent slave-owner and husband whose quarrel with Orestes ends at Delphi. Pindar and Delphic tradition transform his violent death there into the foundation of a hero cult.

### Names

- **Νεοπτόλεμος — Neoptólemos; Latin Neoptolemus. [EE]** A transparent compound of νέος, “new/young,” and πόλεμος, “war”: approximately **“new to war,” “young warrior,” or “new war.”** Ancient explanations vary. Pausanias 10.26.4 reports that Lycomedes named him Pyrrhus and Phoenix gave him *Neoptolemus* because Achilles had begun warfare while young. Other ancient glosses instead apply the “new to war” idea directly to the son. These are explanatory etymologies, not proof of an original naming ceremony.

- **Πύρρος — Pýrrhos; Latin Pyrrhus. [EE]** The adjective πυρρός means fiery-red, flame-colored, tawny or reddish-blond. Its relation to πῦρ, “fire,” is linguistically natural. The ancient personal name is therefore approximately **“Red” or “Fiery-haired.”** Later sources often treat *Pyrrhus* as his first or childhood name and *Neoptolemus* as a war-name, but the early evidence does not establish that sequence.

- **“Son of Achilles,” Ἀχιλλέως παῖς/υἱός; “Aeacid,” Αἰακίδης. [EE]** These patronymic and dynastic descriptions connect him through Achilles and Peleus to Aeacus. Pindar especially situates him among the Aeacids of Aegina.

- **Pelides/Peleiades. [LC]** Latin poets can describe him through Peleus, his paternal great-grandfather, or simply call him *Achillides*, “son/descendant of Achilles.”

- **“Island-born/islander.” [LC]** Euripides’ *Andromache* 14 calls him νησιώτης, emphasizing Scyros.

No securely attested ancient cult-title comparable to a divine epithet is known. Pindar instead describes a cultic office: the dead hero dwells in Apollo’s precinct and oversees heroic processions or sacrificial observances.

### Equations and confusions

- **Neoptolemus = Pyrrhus. [EE]** This is an ancient double name, not a modern identification.
- **Pyrrhus of Epirus ≠ the hero. [LS/political genealogy]** The historical king Pyrrhus, 319/318–272 BCE, bore the ancestral name and belonged to the Aeacid royal house that claimed descent from Neoptolemus. Ancient and modern popular accounts sometimes blur the two.
- **Neoptolemus I and II of Epirus ≠ the hero. [Historical namesake]** These were historical Molossian rulers.
- **Achilles as “Pyrrha.” [LC]** The story that Achilles assumed the feminine name Pyrrha while disguised on Scyros is a later explanation linking father and son by red or blond hair. It is not present in Homer and cannot securely explain the independently early name Pyrrhus.
- No ancient evidence identifies Neoptolemus syncretistically with a foreign god, saint, or another Trojan hero. **Absence of evidence is significant:** Delphic cult made him an honored heroic dead, not Apollo under another name.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **Homer, *Iliad* 19.326–337; Greek; approximately 700 BCE. [EE]** Achilles grieves that he had expected his son, being reared on Scyros, to survive him and inherit his property. The passage does **not** name the son, identify his mother, or describe his participation at Troy.

2. **Homer, *Odyssey* 4.1–9; 11.505–537; Greek; late eighth–early seventh century BCE. [EE]** Menelaus celebrates the marriage of Hermione to Neoptolemus, whom he had promised at Troy. In the Underworld, Odysseus tells Achilles that Neoptolemus came from Scyros, spoke excellently in council, fought with distinction, never suffered a wound in the horse, and departed with his prize. Homer knows no murder of Priam, Astyanax or Polyxena by him and no Delphic death.

3. **Archilochus fr. 304 West; Greek; seventh century BCE. [EE, fragmentary]** Apparently attests the name Pyrrhus for Achilles’ son. Its fragmentary survival prevents reconstruction of a narrative.

4. **Early visual evidence. [EE, identification sometimes inferential]** The large relief pithos from Mykonos, approximately 675–650 BCE, depicts the sack of Troy and killings of women and children, but its individual warriors are not inscribed; assigning a particular scene to Neoptolemus is therefore interpretive. Securely recognizable sixth-century black-figure scenes show Priam at an altar attacked by Neoptolemus, sometimes with Astyanax used as a weapon. An Attic Tyrrhenian amphora of approximately 570–550 BCE in the British Museum explicitly represents the sacrifice of Polyxena and identifies principal figures by inscriptions.

### Archaic epic and lyric

- **The *Little Iliad*, attributed to Lesches; Greek; probably seventh or sixth century BCE, lost except for quotations and ancient summaries. [EE]** Odysseus fetches Neoptolemus from Scyros; the hero receives Achilles’ armor, kills Eurypylus, participates in the final operations at Troy, takes Andromache and, in a quoted fragment, throws Astyanax from the wall. Pausanias 10.26.9 attributes the latter deed to Lesches.

- **The *Iliou Persis* or *Sack of Ilium*, attributed to Arctinus; Greek; conventionally seventh century BCE, lost. [EE]** In Proclus’ summary Neoptolemus kills Priam at the altar of Zeus Herkeios, but **Odysseus**, not Neoptolemus, kills Astyanax. Polyxena is sacrificed at Achilles’ tomb, although the surviving summary does not explicitly name her executioner.

- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*; Greek; principally seventh–sixth centuries BCE, fragmentary. [EE/fragmentary]** The genealogical tradition connected Achilles, Deidameia and Neoptolemus. Because the poem survives through papyri and quotations, details supplied by later mythographers must not automatically be assigned to Hesiod.

- **Pindar, *Paean* 6, approximately 490 BCE. [EE]** Apollo’s hostility follows Neoptolemus’ killing of Priam at Zeus’s altar. The hero goes to Delphi and dies there. The surviving papyrus is damaged, making ancient scholia important for reconstruction.

- **Pindar, *Nemean* 7, probably 467 BCE. [EE]** Neoptolemus sails to the Molossians after Troy, eventually reaches Delphi with first-fruits, becomes involved in a quarrel over sacrificial meat, is killed, and remains in the sanctuary as a cult hero overseeing heroic rites. The poem is an early secure witness both to the Delphic death tradition and to a connection with Molossia.

- **Pherecydes of Athens, early fifth century BCE, FGrHist/BNJ 3 F64, preserved in scholia. [EE/RV]** Neoptolemus visits Delphi to ask about children by Hermione; during a scramble for sacrificial meat, Apollo’s priest Machaereus kills him and buries him beneath the temple threshold. This is a localizing aetiology, not independent archaeological proof.

### Tragedy

- **Sophocles, *Philoctetes*, performed 409 BCE; Greek. [LC]** Neoptolemus, recruited by Odysseus, must deceive Philoctetes into surrendering Heracles’ bow. He lies, recoils from the deception, restores the bow and offers to take Philoctetes home. Heracles’ epiphany commands both men to go to Troy. This is the most sustained surviving ethical portrait of the young hero.

- **Sophocles, lost *Scyrians*, *Hermione*, *Polyxena* and possibly related plays; fifth century BCE. [LC/fragmentary]** Titles and fragments show that several parts of the tradition received tragic treatment; plots cannot safely be reconstructed beyond the surviving evidence.

- **Euripides, *Hecuba*, usually dated about 424 BCE. [LC]** Achilles’ ghost demands Polyxena; Neoptolemus officiates at her human sacrifice and cuts her throat after she asserts her freedom and bares her breast.

- **Euripides, *Andromache*, probably 420s BCE. [LC]** Andromache is Neoptolemus’ enslaved concubine and mother of his son; Hermione is his childless wife. Orestes rescues Hermione from the household and arranges Neoptolemus’ death at Delphi. The victim is first attacked with stones and then stabbed by a Delphian at Apollo’s altar.

- **Euripides, *Trojan Women*, 415 BCE. [LC]** Astyanax’s killing is a collective Greek decision communicated and executed through Talthybius; Neoptolemus is not made the killer. Andromache has already been allotted to him.

- **Euripides, *Orestes*, 408 BCE. [LC/inconsistent tragic tradition]** Apollo predicts that Hermione will marry Orestes and says Neoptolemus will never marry her—a contradiction if read as continuous biography with *Andromache*, but tragedy did not require a single canonical continuity.

### Mythography, epic continuations and Roman literature

- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 4; Greek; third century BCE. [LS/allusive]** The poem supplies background about Thetis, Achilles and Scyros-related traditions but gives Neoptolemus no active role. Later scholia attach fuller biographical explanations.

- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 2–3; Latin; 29–19 BCE. [LC]** “Pyrrhus” is a terrifying sack-warrior. He kills Polites before Priam’s eyes and then kills Priam at the household altar. In Book 3 Andromache recounts her captivity, the sharing of Pyrrhus’ bed, his transfer of her to Helenus, his marriage to Hermione and his murder by Orestes. Virgil’s version profoundly shaped later European reception.

- **Ovid, *Heroides* 8; Latin; late first century BCE or early first century CE. [LC]** Hermione writes to Orestes after Pyrrhus has taken her, defining the marriage as abduction and invoking the hereditary violence of Achilles’ house. *Metamorphoses* 13 relates Astyanax’s fall and Polyxena’s sacrifice; the latter is performed by Neoptolemus.

- **Strabo 9.3.9; Greek; Augustan/Tiberian period. [LS]** Records that Neoptolemus was killed at Delphi, either for demanding compensation from Apollo for Achilles’ death or, more probably in Strabo’s view, for attacking the sanctuary.

- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 97, 108–110, 123; Latin handbook transmitted in a corrupt late-antique/medieval text, perhaps ultimately first–second century CE. [LS]** Systematizes the fetching from Scyros, sack episodes, Polyxena and Orestes’ killing of Neoptolemus.

- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*, Epitome 5–6; Greek; first or second century CE. [LS]** Combines cyclic and tragic material: Neoptolemus kills Priam; “the Greeks” kill Astyanax and Polyxena; Andromache is allotted to Neoptolemus; Orestes kills him because of Hermione. It also preserves the alternative that Machaereus killed him after an assault on Delphi.

- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 1.11, 1.13.9, 10.7.1, 10.24.4–6, 10.25–26; Greek; approximately 150–175 CE. [LC/RV/material testimony]** Reports the Epirot genealogy, the Delphic hearth of the killing, the enclosed grave, annual sacrifice, Polygnotus’ painting and divergent death traditions.

- **Philostratus, *Heroicus* 19–20 and *Life of Apollonius* 4.16; Greek; early third century CE. [LC/RV]** Romanticizes Achilles and Polyxena: she voluntarily kills herself at Achilles’ tomb rather than being slaughtered by Neoptolemus. This is an explicit alternative to the dominant sacrifice tradition.

- **Quintus of Smyrna, *Posthomerica* 7–14; Greek; probably third century CE. [LC]** Supplies the fullest surviving continuous account: Odysseus and Diomedes fetch Neoptolemus; he kills Eurypylus and many Trojans; Priam dies by his hand; Achilles’ ghost demands Polyxena, whom Neoptolemus sacrifices.

- **Tryphiodorus, *Taking of Ilios*; Greek; probably third or fourth century CE. [LC]** Neoptolemus enters the horse and kills Priam, but Odysseus throws Astyanax from the tower.

- **Heliodorus, *Aethiopica* 2.34–3.6; Greek; third or fourth century CE. [LC/cult testimony in fictional form]** Describes an elaborate quadrennial Aenian procession and hecatomb at Delphi for Neoptolemus. The novel is evidence for late-antique cultural memory and ritual imagination; it cannot by itself prove that every described detail was historically performed.

- **Dictys Cretensis, *Ephemeris belli Troiani*; Latin; probably fourth century CE, claiming a Greek eyewitness source. [LC/pseudohistory]** Rationalizes the story as war chronicle and includes Neoptolemus in the post-Homeric campaigns. Its alleged eyewitness tablet is a literary fiction.

- **Dares Phrygius, *De excidio Troiae historia*; Latin; late antiquity. [LC/pseudohistory]** Another fabricated eyewitness account influential in medieval Europe.

- **Scholia on Homer, Pindar and Euripides; Greek; compiled over centuries in manuscripts principally medieval. [LS]** Preserve otherwise lost fragments of Pherecydes, Asclepiades, the cyclic epics and local explanations. A scholium’s manuscript date is not the date of the source it quotes.

The Homeric and tragic texts survive chiefly in Byzantine manuscripts of the tenth century and later, supplemented by ancient papyri. The Epic Cycle survives only through quotations, scholia and Proclus’ summary, the latter transmitted with the ninth- or tenth-century *Venetus A* manuscript of the *Iliad*. Consequently, “what the *Little Iliad* said” is sometimes secure quotation and sometimes only later synopsis.

## 3. The Story

### Conception and upbringing on Scyros

**[EE]** Homer knows Achilles’ son being raised by a friend on Scyros but gives neither mother nor conception story (*Iliad* 19.326–337). *Odyssey* 11 says Odysseus brought Neoptolemus from Scyros.

**[LC]** Later tradition names the mother **Deidameia**, daughter of **Lycomedes**. Achilles, concealed among Lycomedes’ daughters to prevent his recruitment, fathers the child. The disguise narrative is post-Homeric; the detail that Achilles was called **Pyrrha** and the son consequently **Pyrrhus** is later still. It must not be retrojected into Homer.

No early source supplies a securely calculable age. Modern claims that Neoptolemus was exactly ten, twelve or thirteen at Troy derive from literal synchronization of incompatible legendary chronologies, not an ancient birth record.

### Recruitment from Scyros

**[EE]** The *Little Iliad* and *Odyssey* make Odysseus the essential recruiter. The motive in later summaries is an oracle or information extracted from Helenus: Troy cannot fall without an Aeacid, Achilles’ son, and/or without Heracles’ bow.

**[RV]**

- Odysseus alone fetches him in some summaries.
- Sophocles’ *Philoctetes* makes Odysseus his commander and manipulator.
- Quintus sends **Odysseus and Diomedes**.
- Some later accounts add **Phoenix**.
- Claims that Phoenix universally raised or fetched him harmonize distinct sources.

### Philoctetes and the bow

**[LC]** In Sophocles, Neoptolemus accepts Odysseus’ plan to gain Philoctetes’ trust by pretending hostility toward the Atreids and Odysseus. He obtains Heracles’ bow but becomes ashamed, confesses, and returns it. When Odysseus threatens force, Neoptolemus sides with Philoctetes. Heracles appears and orders the journey to Troy. The play thus invents or radically develops an ethical conflict absent from the Homeric portrait: inherited *physis* and truth contend with expedient deception.

Other lost tragic treatments differed. There is no warrant for treating Sophocles’ repentant Neoptolemus as the single “original” character.

### Arrival and warfare

**[EE]** *Odyssey* 11 gives the earliest surviving account: Neoptolemus is foremost in council after Odysseus; kills many men, including **Eurypylus son of Telephus**; enters the wooden horse; remains disciplined and unwounded; and returns with his allotted prize.

**[EE/LC]** The *Little Iliad* and Quintus expand his aristeia. He receives Achilles’ armor, defeats Eurypylus, participates in the horse stratagem and becomes Achilles’ replacement. Quintus repeatedly models son upon father, often intensifying the son’s youth and violence.

### Priam

**[EE]** The cyclic *Iliou Persis* makes Neoptolemus kill **Priam**, who has taken refuge at the altar of **Zeus Herkeios**, protector of the household enclosure.

**[EE visual tradition]** Sixth-century vases repeatedly depict this sacrilege. Some fuse it with Astyanax’s death: Neoptolemus swings the child’s corpse or living body toward Priam. This fusion is not preserved as such in surviving early poetry and may be a distinctive pictorial construction.

**[LC]** Virgil intensifies the scene. Pyrrhus kills Priam’s son **Polites** before him; Priam contrasts the son’s brutality with Achilles’ former respect for supplication; Pyrrhus drags the old king to the altar and kills him. The speech makes Pyrrhus the “degenerate Neoptolemus,” a deliberate reversal of Achilles’ conduct in *Iliad* 24.

### Astyanax

The killer is genuinely disputed:

- **Neoptolemus kills him. [EE]** A quoted *Little Iliad* fragment says he snatches Astyanax from the nurse and throws him from a tower; Pausanias attributes the act to Lesches.
- **Odysseus kills him. [EE]** Proclus’ summary of the *Iliou Persis* assigns the killing to Odysseus; Tryphiodorus follows this form.
- **The assembled Greeks decree his death. [LC]** Euripides’ *Trojan Women* and Seneca’s *Troades* treat the murder as collective policy, with Talthybius or Odysseus administering it.
- **Unspecified collective execution. [LS]** Apollodorus says “they” threw him from the walls.
- **Astyanax survives. [MA/MI in relation to Greek antiquity]** Racine’s *Andromaque* preserves Astyanax as the hostage over whom Pyrrhus and Andromache struggle. Racine openly changes the ancient death tradition. This survivor is a seventeenth-century dramatic counterfactual, not a recovered ancient variant.

### Polyxena

**[EE, archaic iconography and lost epic]** The *Iliou Persis* already contained a sacrifice of Polyxena at Achilles’ tomb. The British Museum’s inscribed Tyrrhenian amphora, approximately 570–550 BCE, shows Neoptolemus cutting her throat over the grave while other Greek heroes restrain or attend her. The image predates Euripides by more than a century.

**[LC]** In Euripides’ *Hecuba*, Achilles’ ghost demands the victim. Polyxena rejects enslavement, voluntarily presents herself, and Neoptolemus performs the killing. Her consent within the scene does not make the institutional act non-coercive: she is a captive selected for human sacrifice.

**[RV]**

- Quintus: Achilles appears in Neoptolemus’ dream and threatens to detain the fleet; Neoptolemus sacrifices her.
- Ovid: Achilles’ ghost appears to the Greeks or Agamemnon; Pyrrhus kills her.
- Seneca: she is dressed as Achilles’ posthumous bride and sacrificed.
- Philostratus: Polyxena, in love with Achilles, secretly comes to the tomb and kills herself. Neoptolemus is therefore not her killer. This romantic late-antique variant contradicts the archaic iconography and dominant literary record.

### Andromache: enslavement and sexual possession

**[EE]** The *Little Iliad* says the Greek leaders award Hector’s widow **Andromache** to Neoptolemus. This is captive enslavement, not a freely contracted marriage.

**[LC]** Euripides’ *Andromache* says she was selected as his prize, taken to Phthia and compelled into his bed. She bears a son. The play’s language of slavery and ownership must not be sanitized into a consensual romance.

**[LC/RV]** Virgil relocates the aftermath to Epirus: Pyrrhus has Andromache as concubine, later gives her to Helenus, and marries Hermione. Medieval and early-modern works increasingly romanticize Pyrrhus’ desire for Andromache.

### Hermione and Orestes

**[EE]** *Odyssey* 4 says Menelaus promised Hermione to Achilles’ son at Troy and later celebrated the marriage. Homer knows no competing marriage to Orestes.

**[LC/RV]** Later sources make Hermione a disputed bride:

- Euripides’ *Andromache*: Menelaus had given her to Orestes, later promised her to Neoptolemus, and Neoptolemus took her while Orestes was incapacitated by madness. Childlessness and jealousy of Andromache drive the household plot.
- Ovid’s *Heroides* 8: Hermione describes herself as seized by Pyrrhus and appeals to Orestes.
- Apollodorus and Hyginus: Orestes kills Neoptolemus because he took Hermione.
- Euripides’ *Orestes*: Apollo says Neoptolemus will never marry Hermione, showing that Euripides himself used incompatible versions in different plays.

### Return and Molossia

**[EE]** Pindar’s *Nemean* 7 sends Neoptolemus to the Molossians after Troy.

**[LC/RV]** Euripides places his household in the borderland between Phthia and Pharsalus. Virgil and Pausanias emphasize Epirus. Later genealogies make his sons by Andromache—usually **Molossus, Pielus and Pergamus**—eponymous or dynastic founders. The divergence reflects Thessalian, Delphic and Epirot claims, not a recoverable itinerary.

### Death at Delphi

At least four distinguishable explanations survive:

1. **Sacrificial-meat quarrel. [EE/RV]** Pindar’s *Nemean* 7: while bringing Trojan first-fruits, Neoptolemus becomes involved in a dispute over meat and is stabbed. His death establishes his permanent ritual presence.

2. **Punishment for Priam’s murder. [EE/RV]** Pindar’s earlier *Paean* 6 connects Apollo’s hostility with the killing of Priam at Zeus’s altar.

3. **Demand for satisfaction or attack on Apollo. [LC/RV]** Euripides says Neoptolemus had previously demanded justice from Apollo for killing Achilles and later returned to seek reconciliation. Strabo, Pausanias and Apollodorus preserve variants in which he attacks, burns or attempts to rob the sanctuary.

4. **Orestes’ conspiracy. [LC]** Euripides’ *Andromache* has Orestes spread the claim that Neoptolemus intends to plunder Delphi. Delphians stone and attack him; an unnamed Delphian delivers the fatal blow. Apollodorus and Hyginus compress responsibility into “Orestes killed him.”

5. **Machaereus the priest. [EE/RV]** Pherecydes and later scholia name **Machaereus**, son of Daetas, as killer during the meat dispute. Pausanias shows visitors the hearth where Apollo’s priest killed him.

These are not stages of a single forensic event. They are competing aetiologies explaining why an enemy or violator of Apollo lies honored inside Apollo’s precinct.

### Body and burial

**[EE/RV]** Pherecydes says he was initially buried under the temple threshold. Asclepiades, quoted by a scholiast, says Menelaus later transferred the body into the precinct. Pindar locates him in the sanctuary. Pausanias saw an enclosed “grave” to the left of Apollo’s temple.

**[Material limit]** Excavation has not produced a universally accepted archaeological identification of this heroon or an authenticated body. The literary testimony securely proves that imperial-period visitors were shown a site and that Pausanias understood annual sacrifice to occur; it does not authenticate Bronze Age remains.

## 4. Relations with Gods and Other Heroes

### Genealogical structures

**Paternal line [EE/LS]:**

```text
Zeus + Aegina
└── Aeacus
    └── Peleus + Thetis
        └── Achilles + Deidameia
            └── Neoptolemus/Pyrrhus
```

Homer securely supplies Peleus → Achilles → son; later epic and mythography name Deidameia and extend the Aeacid tree.

**Children [RV]:**

```text
Neoptolemus + Andromache
├── Molossus — principal Molossian ancestor
├── Pielus — Pausanias' Epirot succession
└── Pergamus — migrates to Asia and gives Pergamum an eponymous genealogy

Neoptolemus + Hermione
└── no child in Euripides and Pausanias
```

Euripides concentrates on one son of Andromache, identified as Molossus by the play’s end and later tradition. Pausanias 1.11.1 gives Molossus, Pielus and Pergamus. Genealogists disagree over which son transmitted Molossian kingship. These are political genealogies, not biological records.

### Documented interactions

Each line states the interaction, primary witness and evidence tier.

- **Achilles:** father; Neoptolemus inherits his armor and martial role; sacrifices Polyxena at his tomb; *Iliad* 19, *Odyssey* 11, *Little Iliad*, Euripides’ *Hecuba*, seventh–fifth centuries BCE. **[EE]**
- **Deidameia:** mother on Scyros; named in post-Homeric cyclic/mythographic tradition and Quintus 7. **[LC]**
- **Lycomedes:** maternal grandfather and fosterer on Scyros; Pausanias 10.26.4 and later mythography. **[LC/LS]**
- **Peleus:** great-grandfather; Neoptolemus returns to or succeeds within Peleus’ Thessalian house; Euripides’ *Andromache*. **[LC]**
- **Thetis:** divine grandmother; her altar shelters Andromache; in cyclic return tradition she warns Neoptolemus to travel overland; Euripides and Proclus’ Cycle summary. **[EE/LC]**
- **Aeacus:** heroic ancestor grounding his necessity for Troy and the Molossian Aeacid claim; Pindar and later genealogists. **[EE/LS]**
- **Zeus Herkeios:** Neoptolemus kills Priam at the god’s household altar; *Iliou Persis*, vase painting, Virgil. **[EE]**
- **Apollo:** indirect killer of Achilles; Neoptolemus challenges or seeks reconciliation with him and is killed in his sanctuary; Pindar, Pherecydes, Euripides, Pausanias. **[EE/RV]**
- **Heracles:** owner of the indispensable bow; appears as deus ex machina directing Neoptolemus and Philoctetes; Sophocles’ *Philoctetes* 1409–1444. **[LC]**
- **Athena:** Neoptolemus participates in the horse operation undertaken under divine protection; no surviving early text gives a direct personal exchange. **[EE; indirect]**
- **Odysseus:** brings him from Scyros; counsels or compels deception of Philoctetes; fellow occupant of the horse; *Odyssey* 11, *Little Iliad*, Sophocles. **[EE/LC]**
- **Diomedes:** helps fetch him in Quintus’ version; *Posthomerica* 7. **[LC/RV]**
- **Phoenix:** accompanies or guides him in some cyclic summaries; dies on the overland return and is buried by him; Proclus’ summary. **[EE, summary]**
- **Philoctetes:** deceived and befriended; Neoptolemus takes and restores his bow and escorts him toward Troy; Sophocles, 409 BCE. **[LC]**
- **Helenus:** his prophecy makes Neoptolemus necessary; later becomes Neoptolemus’ captive/adviser and receives Andromache or part of his realm; cyclic tradition, Euripides, Virgil. **[EE/LC]**
- **Eurypylus:** killed in single combat; *Odyssey* 11 and *Little Iliad*. **[EE]**
- **Epeius:** fellow participant in the wooden horse; *Odyssey* 11 and later sack traditions. **[EE; joint scene]**
- **Priam:** killed by Neoptolemus at Zeus’s altar; *Iliou Persis*, sixth-century vases, Virgil. **[EE]**
- **Polites:** pursued and killed before Priam; Virgil, *Aeneid* 2. **[LC/Roman elaboration]**
- **Astyanax:** killed by Neoptolemus in the *Little Iliad* but by Odysseus or collective decree elsewhere; seventh/sixth century BCE onward. **[EE/RV]**
- **Andromache:** awarded as captive, enslaved concubine and mother of his children; *Little Iliad*, Euripides, Virgil. **[EE/LC]**
- **Hector:** no living encounter; Neoptolemus enslaves Hector’s widow and, in one tradition, kills his son; early epic. **[EE; posthumous relation]**
- **Polyxena:** sacrificed by him in archaic art and Euripides; not killed by him in Philostratus’ suicide version. **[EE/RV]**
- **Hecuba:** her daughter Polyxena is killed by him; Hecuba narratively confronts the result rather than Neoptolemus at length; Euripides’ *Hecuba*. **[LC]**
- **Hermione:** wife or forcibly appropriated bride; *Odyssey* 4, Euripides, Ovid. **[EE/RV]**
- **Orestes:** rival for Hermione and principal organizer or direct agent of his death; Euripides’ *Andromache*, Apollodorus, Hyginus. **[LC]**
- **Menelaus:** promises him Hermione; sometimes later transfers or buries his corpse; *Odyssey* 4, Asclepiades through scholia. **[EE/LS]**
- **Agamemnon:** commander under whom he fights; in later versions receives Achilles’ demand for Polyxena; Cyclic epic, Ovid. **[EE/LC]**
- **Calchas:** prophecy or ritual counsel helps define the conditions for Troy’s fall and post-sack sacrifices; later mythography. **[LC/LS]**
- **Machaereus:** Delphic priest who kills and first buries him; Pherecydes and scholia. **[EE/RV]**
- **Molossus:** son by Andromache and ancestral link to Molossian kings; Euripides and Pausanias. **[LC/RV]**
- **Pielus:** son and Epirot successor in Pausanias 1.11.1. **[LC/RV]**
- **Pergamus:** son who defeats Areius in Asia and becomes an eponym; Pausanias 1.11.1. **[LC/RV]**
- **Aeneas:** a *Little Iliad* fragment unusually says Neoptolemus puts Aeneas aboard his ships as a prize; most other traditions let Aeneas escape. **[EE/RV]**
- **Glaucus son of Antenor:** Apollodorus has Odysseus and Menelaus, not Neoptolemus, rescue him; a joint sack context but no direct interaction. **[LS; absence noted]**
- **Pyrrhus of Epirus:** no narrative interaction; the historical king claims or inherits genealogical identity from him and bears his name; Plutarch and Molossian tradition. **[LS/political appropriation]**
- **Alexander the Great:** no mythic encounter; through Olympias, later historical genealogy traced Alexander to Aeacus via Neoptolemus; Plutarch, *Alexander* 2. **[LS/political genealogy]**

No securely attested ancient text gives Neoptolemus a brother named **Oneiros** in the central tradition. Internet genealogies that list such a sibling without a precise ancient citation should be treated as **[MI or unsupported harmonization]**.

## 5. Cult, Tomb and Ritual

### Delphi

**[EE]** Pindar’s *Nemean* 7 is the earliest secure literary evidence that Neoptolemus’ grave and continuing function were recognized inside the Delphic sanctuary. The dead hero was to remain near Apollo’s temple as an overseer of heroic processions and sacrifice. The account is a cult aetiology: the violent outsider becomes permanently useful to the sanctuary.

**[Material/literary testimony]** Pausanias 10.24.4–6, approximately 160 CE, says:

- the temple hearth was where Apollo’s priest killed Neoptolemus;
- outside and to the left lay an enclosure containing his grave;
- the Delphians sacrificed to him annually “as to a hero.”

Pausanias’ exact spatial testimony is important, but no excavated structure has been unanimously matched to the enclosure. Modern maps that label a particular foundation “the tomb of Neoptolemus” usually follow literary topography rather than an identifying inscription.

**[Epigraphic evidence]** An Amphictyonic inscription apparently regulates or identifies a bull destined for sacrifice to Neoptolemus. This supports institutional sacrifice beyond Pausanias’ narrative, although its precise reconstruction and date require consultation of the published inscription rather than nineteenth-century paraphrase.

**[LC]** Heliodorus describes a spectacular Aenian embassy every four years during the Pythian festival: cattle, maidens, armed youths, horsemen, triple circuits around the tomb, lament and war cry. The Aenianes claim kinship through Achilles. The episode may preserve ritual knowledge, but its choreography occurs inside a romance and cannot all be accepted as documentary reportage.

### Change in Delphic status

**[LC/RV]** Pausanias 1.13.9 says the Delphians once treated Neoptolemus’ grave dishonorably because he was their enemy. During the Celtic/Galatian attack on Delphi in 279 BCE, the hero appeared or aided the defense; thereafter they honored him more highly. This is a classic protective-hero narrative: a dangerous dead enemy becomes guardian of the community that killed him.

### Other cult claims

No securely documented independent sanctuary devoted specifically to Neoptolemus is comparable to the Delphic cult. Aeginetan poetry, Thessalian kinship claims and Epirot genealogy honor him, but literary prominence is not itself proof of recurrent sacrifice.

The claim that Delphi held an **enneateric or eight-year “festival of Neoptolemus”** is repeated in older reference works, but Pausanias specifies annual sacrifice and Heliodorus a quadrennial Aenian mission. Without a primary citation, an independent eight-year festival should be marked **[unsupported/possibly modern conflation]**.

## 6. Historical Evolution

### Homeric and early archaic period, approximately 700–600 BCE

**[EE]** The figure begins as Achilles’ successful surviving son. The *Odyssey* emphasizes continuity without moral censure: eloquence, discipline, courage and safe return. Lost cyclic epic already contained the more violent sack tradition. The alternative names Neoptolemus and Pyrrhus circulated early.

### Archaic visual and cultic crystallization, approximately 650–480 BCE

**[EE]** Vase painters foreground atrocities omitted by Homer: Priam at the altar, Astyanax, and Polyxena. Images can combine episodes more radically than preserved poetry. By the early fifth century, Pherecydes and Pindar attest a Delphic death-and-cult complex and an Epirot/Molossian connection.

### Classical tragedy, 480–400 BCE

**[LC]** Tragedy divides the character:

- Sophocles makes him ethically educable and capable of rejecting deceit.
- Euripides treats the consequences of his household power: enslavement, sexual coercion, dynastic rivalry and sacrilegious death.
- The hero frequently remains absent while captive women describe what his victory means.

The contrast is not evidence that one playwright knew the “real Neoptolemus.” Each reorganizes inherited material for a different drama.

### Fourth-century Molossian state formation

**[LS/political genealogy]** The Aeacid kings of Molossia claimed descent from Aeacus, Achilles and Neoptolemus. Historical rulers bore the names Neoptolemus and Pyrrhus. Olympias’ ancestry made the genealogy available to Alexander the Great; Plutarch presents the maternal descent from Aeacus through Neoptolemus as accepted dynastic tradition.

Modern scholarship dates the fully political form of this genealogy variously. Some argue that the Neoptolemus–Molossian linkage developed around the later fifth century BCE, when Molossian rulers sought recognized Hellenic heroic ancestry. Pindar proves that a connection existed by the 460s, but not that every later family branch was already fixed.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

**[LC/LS]** Hellenistic rulers, Roman poets and mythographers stabilize “Pyrrhus” as the terrifying killer of Priam and possessor of Andromache. Virgil makes him an anti-Achilles whose impiety embodies Troy’s destruction. Ovid gives speech and subjectivity to Hermione and Polyxena. Systematic handbooks reconcile or juxtapose incompatible traditions.

### Late antiquity

**[LC]** Quintus constructs a continuous epic sequel; Tryphiodorus abbreviates the sack; Philostratus replaces sacrifice with romantic suicide; Heliodorus turns the cult into spectacular novelistic ritual. Dictys and Dares repackage myth as purported eyewitness history.

### Medieval period

**[MA]** Western Europe knew “Pyrrhus” especially through Virgil, Ovid, Dares and the medieval *Roman de Troie* tradition. The story was euhemerized: gods receded, courtly motives grew, and Trojan-war actors became quasi-historical knights and princes. Medieval claims based on Dares or Dictys are reception evidence, not independent Bronze Age testimony.

### Early modern and modern periods

**[MA/MI]** French tragedy makes Pyrrhus principally the ruler torn between Hermione and Andromache. Racine’s preservation of Astyanax and romantic love for Andromache changes the ancient record deliberately. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship then reverses direction, reconstructing the fragmentary early traditions and distinguishing epic, cult and local aetiology.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Drama, opera and visual art

- **Luc Percheron, *Pyrrhe*, approximately 1590s. [MA]** One of several late-sixteenth-century French treatments of Pyrrhus’ death.
- **Jean Racine, *Andromaque*, first performed at the Louvre, 17 November 1667; first edition 1668. [MA]** The decisive modern reinvention. Oreste loves Hermione, Hermione loves Pyrrhus, Pyrrhus loves Andromaque, and Andromaque remains faithful to Hector while trying to save Astyanax. Pyrrhus is murdered after choosing Andromaque. Racine cites Virgil but also transforms Euripides. Astyanax’s survival is knowingly unhistorical within the ancient mythic record.
- **Ambrose Philips, *The Distrest Mother*, 1712. [MA]** An English adaptation of Racine that helped transmit the romanticized Pyrrhus.
- **André-Ernest-Modeste Grétry, *Andromaque*, 1780. [MA]** Operatic adaptation of Racine.
- **Gioachino Rossini, *Ermione*, Naples, 1819. [MA]** Concentrates on Hermione, Pyrrhus, Andromache and Orestes; its Pyrrhus belongs to the Racine/Virgil reception line rather than Homer’s exemplary warrior.
- **Pierre-Narcisse Guérin, *Andromache and Pyrrhus*, 1810. [MA]** Neoclassical painting synthesizing moments from Racine and fixes the captive woman/royal suitor image.

### Modern reinterpretation

**[MSR]** Modern performances of *Philoctetes* often read Neoptolemus through moral injury, indoctrination and the ethics of military obedience. Those are modern interpretive frameworks, though grounded in Sophocles’ explicit conflict between deceit and pity.

**[MSR]** Psychoanalytic, structuralist and “hero-pattern” approaches sometimes classify him as the son who repeats and degrades the father’s violence, the youthful initiate, or the doomed dynastic successor. Rank, Raglan and Campbell did not establish a historical universal template for Neoptolemus; applying their patterns is comparative interpretation. Dumézilian Indo-European readings likewise require independent linguistic and institutional evidence. Nagy’s influential account is more specifically Greek: Neoptolemus becomes Apollo’s ritual antagonist, whose violent death enables cultic integration.

**[MI]** Modern retellings frequently assign him:

- an exact childhood age;
- “red hair” as a universally attested physical trait;
- a simple birth-name/war-name sequence;
- a love marriage with Andromache;
- remorse for all sack atrocities;
- or, conversely, uncomplicated psychopathy.

Each may be a legitimate fictional choice, but none represents the entire ancient record.

### Political appropriation

**[Ancient political use]** Molossian kings used Aeacid descent to position their dynasty within prestigious Greek heroic genealogy. Through Olympias the lineage enhanced Alexander’s Achilles-centered self-presentation. Historical King Pyrrhus’ name reinforced the same ancestral program.

**[Modern contested use]** Neoptolemus sometimes enters Greek–Albanian arguments over the ancient ethnicity and modern ownership of Epirus. The ancient evidence establishes that Molossian rulers claimed descent from a Greek epic hero and used Greek names and genealogies. It does **not** convert a legendary genealogy into biological evidence for modern populations or settle modern territorial identity. No substantial, independently documented extremist cult centered on Neoptolemus was located; claims of such appropriation should not be manufactured from general online nationalist rhetoric.

### Commercial and popular culture

The names “Pyrrhus” and “Neoptolemus” appear in historical games and Trojan-war adaptations, but the better-known commercial “Pyrrhus” is usually the historical Epirot king. Confusing that king with Achilles’ son—especially explaining “Pyrrhic victory” through the mythic hero—is a **modern error**.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Historicity

**[MSR]** No contemporary Bronze Age inscription, securely identified tomb occupant, or independent historical record verifies Neoptolemus as a person. The safest historical statement is that Greek poetic traditions by approximately 700 BCE knew Achilles’ son and soon developed him into a major sack hero.

Euhemerizing proposals that a migrating Mycenaean war-leader underlies the legend remain hypotheses. Similarity between legendary movements and later Greek settlement patterns is not identification.

### Dating the tradition

The Homeric passages, cyclic fragments and early art do not form a simple sequence of borrowing. The Sack of Troy existed in oral traditions before surviving texts. A vase image may witness a lost song, an independent oral variant, or pictorial invention. Gantz’s source-critical method remains important precisely because it resists treating Apollodorus’ late synthesis as the earliest story.

### Delphic cult debate

Scholarly interpretations include:

- **L. R. Farnell:** treated the Delphic hero as a significant cultic problem and considered whether a hieratic figure lay behind epic Neoptolemus.
- **Joseph Fontenrose, “The Cult and Myth of Pyrros at Delphi” (1960): [MSR, disputed]** argued for a pre-Apollonian fertility or vegetation figure and ritual conflict.
- **Gregory Nagy, *The Best of the Achaeans* (1979): [MSR]** reads Pyrrhus as Apollo’s ritual antagonist: the hero’s excess leads to death at the god’s sanctuary, followed by cultic incorporation.
- **Robert Fowler:** emphasizes the aetiological logic of Pherecydes’ narrative rather than extracting a historical killing.
- **Gunnel Ekroth and later hero-cult scholarship:** warns that literary tombs, archaeological installations and recurrent sacrificial practice do not automatically coincide. The lack of a securely identified excavated heroon at Delphi matters.

The older claim that Neoptolemus was certainly a fertility god displaced by Apollo goes beyond the demonstrable evidence. The secure minimum is: Pindar knew a Delphic grave-and-cult tradition; later sources knew sacrifices; Pausanias was shown a grave enclosure.

### Pindar’s supposed recantation

Ancient scholia and much modern scholarship interpret *Nemean* 7 as an apology for Pindar’s harsher treatment of Neoptolemus in *Paean* 6. Other scholars regard the ode’s rhetoric as more complex than a simple recantation. The two poems certainly frame the death differently, but authorial embarrassment cannot be directly documented.

### Epic versus tragedy

A persistent error is to combine:

- Homer’s successful warrior,
- the cyclic killer of Priam and sometimes Astyanax,
- Sophocles’ truth-seeking youth,
- Euripides’ absent slave-owner,
- and Virgil’s monstrous Pyrrhus

into a continuous psychological development. Source criticism treats them as historically situated constructions.

### Molossian genealogy

The genealogy is ancient and politically real **as a claim**. Debate concerns when and how it was formalized. Pindar gives a fifth-century terminus ante quem for the Molossian association. The naming of historical rulers Neoptolemus and Pyrrhus shows active dynastic adoption. It does not verify descent from a Trojan-war individual.

### State of the evidence

- The Homeric poems are relatively well transmitted, with papyri and medieval codices.
- The *Little Iliad* and *Iliou Persis* are almost wholly lost.
- Pherecydes survives through later quotation.
- Pindar’s *Paean* 6 is papyrologically damaged.
- Lost tragedies are represented only by titles and fragments.
- Vase evidence is abundant but often unlabeled.
- The Delphic monument remains archaeologically uncertain.
- LIMC’s catalogue remains indispensable for separating object, date, provenance, attribution and iconographic interpretation.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **“Pyrrhus was certainly his birth name and Neoptolemus was bestowed at Troy.” [Unsupported harmonization]**
- **“Achilles named him Pyrrhus because Achilles’ own female alias was Pyrrha.” [Late explanatory tale]**
- **“He was exactly twelve at Troy.” [Modern chronological calculation]**
- **“He killed Astyanax in every ancient version.” [False; Odysseus and collective-decree variants exist]**
- **“Orestes personally stabbed him in every version.” [False; Machaereus or an unnamed Delphian often delivers the blow]**
- **“His Delphic tomb has been archaeologically identified beyond dispute.” [False]**
- **“Racine preserves an ancient version in which Astyanax survived.” [Modern dramatic invention]**
- **“Pyrrhic victory is named after Neoptolemus.” [False; it refers to historical King Pyrrhus of Epirus]**

## 10. Sources

### Primary editions and corpora

For lost epic, Martin L. West’s *Greek Epic Fragments* (Loeb Classical Library 497, 2003) is the preferred modern Greek text and translation; older public-domain translations reproduced online require checking against West or Bernabé. For early mythography, Fowler’s *Early Greek Mythography* supersedes casual extraction from late handbooks. LIMC II’s entries on Astyanax and related sack iconography, together with its Neoptolemus/Pyrrhus material, should be consulted object by object.

The online source collections used below are gateways and translations, not substitutes for apparatus-bearing critical editions. Theoi’s texts frequently reproduce older Loeb translations; Perseus’ editions vary in age; museum catalogues are strongest for object data.

### Assessment

The evidentiary core is unusually clear despite the absence of a biography:

1. Homer securely knows Achilles’ son and names Neoptolemus.
2. Lost archaic epic and sixth-century art already associate him with Troy’s final violence.
3. The sources genuinely disagree over Astyanax, Polyxena’s agency and the cause and agent of Neoptolemus’ death.
4. Pindar securely attests the Delphic cult complex by the early fifth century BCE.
5. Pausanias documents the sanctuary’s imperial-period presentation and annual sacrifice, but archaeology has not authenticated the hero’s remains.
6. The Molossian genealogy is historically important as dynastic ideology, not demonstrable heredity.
7. Virgil and Racine, rather than Homer alone, created the dominant European “Pyrrhus.”
8. No evidence warrants reconstructing a single historical Neoptolemus behind these traditions.
