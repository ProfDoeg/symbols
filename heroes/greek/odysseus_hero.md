# Odysseus (the hero and his cult): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal epic king of Ithaca and leader of the Cephallenians; Trojan-War hero; culture hero of cunning, navigation, endurance, and successful return; after death, a locally worshipped hero, most securely attested on Ithaca.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Odysseus is already a fully developed character in the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, composed through oral tradition and conventionally placed around the late eighth or early seventh century BCE. No securely readable Mycenaean Linear B occurrence of his name is known. Ninth–eighth-century BCE bronze tripods from the Polis cave on Ithaca attest an important sanctuary but do **not by themselves** identify its recipient as Odysseus; explicit epigraphic identification appears only in the Hellenistic period.

## Evidentiary key

- **[E] Secure early attestation:** Archaic evidence, approximately eighth–sixth centuries BCE.
- **[C] Later canonical elaboration:** Classical or later literature developing an established myth.
- **[R] Regional variant:** A locally situated genealogy, cult, foundation, death, or return.
- **[L] Learned systematization:** Mythographic, scholastic, antiquarian, or genealogical compilation.
- **[S] Syncretistic identification:** Deliberate equation with another cultural figure.
- **[M] Medieval/early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or folktale-comparative material.**
- **[SR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference, not ancient testimony.
- **[MI] Modern invention or creative reinvention.**

Dates assigned to ancient works are approximate dates of composition or final monumental form, not dates of the surviving medieval manuscripts.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The figure

[E] In the *Iliad*, Odysseus is Laertes’ son, king of Ithaca and the Cephallenians, an effective speaker, counselor, diplomat, fighter, wrestler, scout, and raider. He is not the poem’s principal wanderer because the *Iliad* ends before Troy falls.

[E] In the *Odyssey*, he is the absent and returning husband of Penelope, father of Telemachus, favorite of Athena, enemy of Poseidon, destroyer of Troy, narrator of his own wanderings, beggar in disguise, and killer of Penelope’s suitors. The poem repeatedly balances *mētis*—intelligence, stratagem, verbal adaptability—against endurance, kingship, violence, and the restoration of the household.

[E] Homer calls him mortal. “Divine,” “Zeus-born,” or “godlike” are honorific epic epithets, not assertions that Zeus biologically fathered him. [The *Odyssey*’s full Greek and translated text is available through Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136).

[C/R] From the Classical period onward, traditions increasingly made him a treacherous sophist, colonial founder, ancestor of Italian peoples, or victim of his unrecognized son. None displaces the Homeric figure; each is a dated reception of it.

### Names and scripts

- **Greek:** Ὀδυσσεύς, *Odysseús*; Homeric metrical variants include Ὀδυσεύς. Vase inscriptions preserve such non-epic forms as Ὀλυτεύς, Ὀλυττεύς, and Ὀλυσσεύς. A modern linguistic study emphasizes that the literary form *Odysseus* does not become uniform in vase inscriptions before the fifth century BCE. [Discussion and bibliographic record](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/handle/10807/174631). **[E/SR]**
- **Attic dramatic plural:** Ὀδυσσέας; Sophocles also permits Οὑδυσσεύς by crasis. [LSJ entry](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n72122/). **[C]**
- **Latin:** *Ulixēs*, *Ulyxēs*, later *Ulysses*. The Latin forms probably entered through a western Greek form with *l* rather than through direct phonetic descent from canonical Ὀδυσσεύς. **[C/SR]**
- **Italian:** *Ulisse*; Dante’s form. **[M]**
- **Modern Greek:** Οδυσσέας, *Odysseas*. **[M]**
- **English and French:** *Odysseus/Ulysses*; *Ulysse*. **[M]**

### Etymology

[E] The *Odyssey* supplies an explicit poetic etymology. At 19.407–409 Autolycus says that, because he has become hateful or incurred anger among many—*odyssamenos*—the child shall be called Odysseus. Elsewhere Athena asks Zeus why he is so angry with Odysseus (*ōdysao*, 1.62). The name is thus woven into ὀδύσσομαι, “be angry with, hate,” and perhaps ὀδύνη, “pain.” [Greek lexical evidence and Homeric passages](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/nodes/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0012.tlg002.perseus-grc2%3A19.407/).

[SR] This is secure as Homeric wordplay but not as historical etymology. Modern proposals include a pre-Greek name, an Anatolian connection, and derivation from Greek roots concerning anger or pain; none commands consensus. The variation between *d*, *l*, *ss*, *t(t)*, and *x* forms is part of the difficulty. Claims that the name proves Odysseus was a Luwian or other identifiable historical person exceed the evidence.

- **δῖος, διογενής, διίφιλος, ἀντίθεος** — “radiant/divine,” “Zeus-born,” “dear to Zeus,” “godlike”; conventional heroic praise, not genealogical claims. **[E]**

### Parentage and status conflicts

| Tradition | Parentage/status | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homeric | Laertes + Anticleia; grandson of Arcesius and Autolycus | *Iliad*; *Odyssey*, late 8th/early 7th c. BCE | **[E]** |
| Sisyphid | Sisyphus impregnates Anticleia before her marriage to Laertes | implied insult in Sophocles’ *Ajax* 189–190 and *Philoctetes* 417; scholia; explicit later authors | **[C/L]** |
| Heroized king | dead mortal receiving local honor and invocation | Hellenistic Ithacan inscription and later testimony | **[R]** |

The Sisyphus story is not Homeric and should not be retrojected into Homer. Its dramatic utility is polemical: it makes Odysseus congenitally fraudulent.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **Homeric poems, late eighth or early seventh century BCE.** **[E]** Odysseus is already integral to two highly developed epic traditions. Their language reflects prolonged oral composition; the exact dates and relationship of monumental composition, performance, dictation, and textual fixation remain disputed.

2. **Early iconography, seventh century BCE.** **[E/uncertain identification]** The Mykonos relief pithos, about 675 BCE, depicts the wooden horse and warriors but does not label Odysseus. It proves early circulation of the sack narrative, not the identity of every warrior.

3. **Vase inscriptions, seventh–sixth centuries BCE.** **[E]** Forms such as *Olyteus/Olytteus* label Odyssean scenes. These are independent evidence for regional name-forms and for visual traditions, though some unlabeled figures remain disputable.

4. **Polis cave tripods, principally ninth–eighth centuries BCE.** **[E for sanctuary; SR for Odyssean dedication]** Thirteen or more bronze tripods and other votives show a wealthy maritime sanctuary. Their resemblance to the thirteen tripod-cauldron gifts of the Phaeacians in *Odyssey* 8 and 13 is striking, but Odysseus does not dedicate the poem’s gifts; he stores them. The cave’s original cult-recipient is therefore unresolved. A modern reassessment warns that early excavation interpreted material through Homeric expectations. [Archaeological review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2009/2009.11.22/); [scholarly reassessment](https://olympias.lib.uoi.gr/jspui/bitstream/123456789/38663/1/The%20Heroon%20of%20Odysseus%20in%20Ithaca%20reconsidered.pdf).

5. **Explicit cult inscription, Hellenistic period.** **[R]** A terracotta mask or plaque from the Polis cave carries ΕΥΧΗΝ ΟΔΥΣΣΕΙ, “a vow/thank-offering to Odysseus.” This securely identifies Odysseus as a recipient by that period; it cannot automatically date the identification back to the ninth century.

### Principal primary corpus

- **Homer, *Iliad*** — Greek; late eighth/early seventh century BCE. Commander of twelve ships, diplomat to Achilles, assembly speaker, night-raider with Diomedes, fighter and wrestler. **[E]**
- **Homer, *Odyssey*** — Greek; late eighth/early seventh century BCE. Principal hero; Troy’s destroyer, wanderer, disguised returner, husband, father, storyteller, and avenger. **[E]**
- **Epic Cycle: *Cypria*, *Little Iliad*, *Iliou Persis*, *Nostoi*, *Telegony*** — Greek; conventionally seventh–sixth centuries BCE, but relative chronology remains debated; surviving through quotations and late summaries. Recruitment, Palamedes, judgment of the arms, Helenus, Philoctetes, Neoptolemus, Palladium, horse, sack, later travels, and death. Proclus’ epitomes survive principally through Photius and *Iliad* manuscripts. [Primary summaries](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/epic-cycle-sb/); [account of transmission](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811084/20082/excerpt/9781108420082_excerpt.pdf). **[E/L transmission]**
- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* and *Theogony* ending** — Greek; seventh–sixth centuries BCE, composite and textually difficult. Genealogical placements and children by Circe; early references to Calypso. [Fragments](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodCatalogues.html). **[E]**
- **Stesichorus and early lyric fragments** — Greek; seventh–sixth centuries BCE. Trojan and return traditions, mostly fragmentary. **[E]**
- **Pindar** — Greek; early fifth century BCE. The judgment of Achilles’ arms becomes an example of persuasive falsehood defeating Ajax’s valor (*Nemean* 7 and 8). **[C]**
- **Aeschylus** — Greek; early fifth century BCE. Lost plays treated Palamedes and Philoctetes; fragments make reconstruction hazardous. **[C]**
- **Sophocles, *Ajax*** — 440s BCE, approximately. Odysseus wins Achilles’ arms but refuses to dishonor dead Ajax. **[C]**
- **Sophocles, *Philoctetes*** — 409 BCE. Odysseus directs Neoptolemus to deceive Philoctetes; stage antagonist and hard political pragmatist. **[C]**
- **Sophoclean fragments:** *The Mad Odysseus*, *Palamedes*, *Euryalus*, and related plays. **[C]**
- **Euripides, *Hecuba*, *Trojan Women*, *Cyclops*, and lost *Palamedes*, *Philoctetes*, *Sisyphus*** — fifth century BCE. Odysseus appears as calculating diplomat, advocate or executor of cruel policy, comic-satyr hero, and enemy of Palamedes. **[C]**
- **Thucydides 3.92** — fifth century BCE. Reports a sanctuary of Zeus at Acharnae said to have been founded by Odysseus during a storm-bound return: antiquarian etiological tradition, not evidence for a historical visit. **[R]**
- **Plato, *Republic* 10** — fourth century BCE. Odysseus’ soul chooses the quiet life of a private man after remembering ambition’s suffering. **[C/philosophical reuse]**
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1.** — third century BCE. Odysseus is not an Argonaut; Laertes is, separating generations. **[L]**
- **Lycophron, *Alexandra*** — third/second century BCE. Dense prophetic allusions connect Odysseus with western Mediterranean and death traditions. **[L/R]**
- **Theocritus and Hellenistic epigram** — third century BCE onward. Allusive reuse of the wanderer and rustic Cyclops. **[C]**
- **Apollodorus, *Library/Epitome*** — probably first or second century CE. Systematic synthesis of recruitment, Palamedes, war stratagems, sack, return, and aftermath. [Perseus text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DEpitome%3Abook%3DE%3Achapter%3D3%3Asection%3D7). **[L]**
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece*** — second century CE. Local artworks, rival relics, Palladium traditions, and episodes differing from the Cycle. **[L/R]**
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae*** — Latin; probably second century CE in transmitted form. Concise systematization of recruitment, horse, return, and Telegonus. **[L]**
- **Virgil, *Aeneid*** — Latin; 29–19 BCE. *Dirus Ulixes*, “dreadful Ulysses,” embodies Greek cunning from a Trojan/Roman perspective. **[C/Roman]**
- **Horace, Ovid, Seneca, Statius, Silius Italicus** — Latin; first century BCE–first century CE. Ethical exemplar, lover, fraudulent Greek, and Italian genealogical ancestor. **[C/L]**
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica*** — Greek; third century CE. Extant imperial synthesis of events between *Iliad* and *Odyssey*. **[L]**
- **Dictys Cretensis and Dares Phrygius** — Latin imperial/late-antique pseudo-histories. Euhemerizing Trojan chronicles that profoundly affected medieval tradition. **[L]**
- **Scholia on Homer, Sophocles, Euripides, and Lycophron; Eustathius and Tzetzes** — ancient through twelfth century CE. Preserve otherwise lost variants but frequently systematize or rationalize them. **[L/M]**

The medieval manuscripts of Homer postdate the poems by more than a millennium, although papyri, quotations, and the Alexandrian scholarly tradition supply much earlier textual witnesses.

## 3. The story and its variants

### Birth, ancestry, and scar

[E] Homer makes Odysseus the son of Laertes and Anticleia. His maternal grandfather Autolycus, a master thief favored by Hermes, names him. As a young man Odysseus visits Autolycus on Parnassus, is wounded above the knee by a boar, and receives the scar by which Eurycleia later recognizes him (*Odyssey* 19.386–475).

[C] The scar is both bodily evidence and narrative authentication: the disguised storyteller is tied to a remembered childhood event. Modern claims that it is a ritual initiation scar are comparative hypotheses, not stated ancient facts.

[C] Sophoclean drama and scholia make Sisyphus his biological father. This gives hereditary form to a hostile interpretation of cunning. Homer nowhere suggests it.

### Marriage and the oath of Helen’s suitors

[L] In Apollodorus, Odysseus seeks Penelope after recognizing that he cannot win Helen. He advises Tyndareus to bind Helen’s suitors by oath to defend her chosen husband; in exchange, Tyndareus helps him win Penelope. This politically elegant invention is absent from Homer and belongs to the later cyclic/mythographic complex.

[L] Some accounts have Odysseus win Penelope in a footrace arranged by Icarius. After marriage she follows Odysseus and veils herself, choosing husband over father.

### Avoiding the war and Palamedes

[C/L] Odysseus feigns madness to avoid Troy, yoking mismatched animals and sowing salt. Palamedes exposes him by placing infant Telemachus in the plough’s path; Odysseus turns aside. Apollodorus instead says Palamedes threatened the child with a sword, while recognizing the ploughing tradition in parallel testimony. [Primary passage and variants](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DEpitome%3Abook%3DE%3Achapter%3D3%3Asection%3D7).

[C/L] Odysseus later destroys Palamedes. Variants include forged correspondence and planted Trojan gold followed by stoning for treason; drowning while fishing; or descent into a well for supposed treasure followed by stoning. These are mutually incompatible. The revenge story belongs to the Cycle and tragedy, not securely to Homer.

### Recruitment and pre-Trojan operations

[C/L] Odysseus discovers Achilles hidden among Lycomedes’ daughters on Scyros by displaying weapons among feminine goods and arranging a trumpet alarm. Achilles reaches for the arms. Sources include the *Cypria* tradition, later mythography, and Roman poetry.

[C/L] He joins embassies to obtain cooperation or supplies from Cinyra, Telephus, and others. Attribution varies by source.

### In the *Iliad*

[E] Odysseus restores order after Agamemnon’s deceptive test, beating common soldiers and rebuking Thersites (*Iliad* 2). He participates with Menelaus in the prewar embassy remembered by Antenor (*Iliad* 3), leads Ithacan/Cephallenian forces, joins the embassy to Achilles (*Iliad* 9), conducts the night raid with Diomedes (*Iliad* 10), kills Trojan allies, is wounded, wrestles Ajax, and competes in the footrace at Patroclus’ games.

[E] His speech is characterized as initially still and unprepossessing, then like winter snow; the comparison shows that eloquence, not mere deception, is central to the early figure.

### Judgment of Achilles’ arms

[E/C] After Achilles’ death, Ajax and Odysseus compete for his divine armor. The lost *Little Iliad* began with the judgment. One version has Trojan girls, overheard at Nestor’s suggestion, judge which Greek harmed Troy more; another has Trojan prisoners decide; Athena’s favor is decisive in later drama.

[C] Pindar treats the result as a victory of deceptive speech over Ajax’s true excellence.

[C] Sophocles’ *Ajax* makes the award the trigger for Ajax’s madness and suicide but gives Odysseus moral generosity at the end: he argues that Ajax deserves burial despite their enmity. Odysseus is therefore not simply the play’s villain.

[C] Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 13 expands the dispute into rival forensic speeches by Ajax and Ulysses, making rhetoric the instrument of victory.

### Helenus, Philoctetes, Neoptolemus, and the Palladium

[C/L] When Troy cannot fall, Odysseus captures or receives the seer Helenus and learns the necessary conditions: Philoctetes and Heracles’ bow, Achilles’ son Neoptolemus, and, in some versions, removal of the Palladium.

[C] Odysseus and Diomedes retrieve Philoctetes from Lemnos in the cyclic/mythographic tradition. Aeschylus, Euripides, and Sophocles dramatized the episode differently. Sophocles’ version has Odysseus instruct Neoptolemus to obtain the bow through deception; Heracles finally commands Philoctetes to sail.

[C/L] Odysseus brings Neoptolemus from Scyros and gives him Achilles’ arms.

[C/L] Odysseus enters Troy disguised as a whipped beggar. Helen recognizes him but does not betray him; he kills Trojans and returns with intelligence.

[C/L] Odysseus and Diomedes steal the Palladium. A later hostile variant says Odysseus tries to murder Diomedes and monopolize the credit; Diomedes sees the drawn sword’s shadow, disarms him, binds him, and drives him back. Rival Greek and Roman communities later claimed the authentic image: Argos, Athens, Rome, and Aeneas-related traditions could not all possess the same original. [Ancient testimonia and conflicts](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DLibrary%3Abook%3D3&force=y).

### The wooden horse and sack

[E] The *Odyssey* already knows the horse: Demodocus sings of it in Book 8; Helen and Menelaus recount conduct inside it in Book 4. Odysseus is its central commander and, in Homer, its maker or organizer in association with Epeius.

[C/L] The *Little Iliad* attributes construction to Epeius under Athena’s direction; Odysseus persuades leading warriors to enter. Proclus’ summary lists his central role. [Cycle text](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/epic-cycle/little-iliad/).

[E] Helen circles the horse imitating the voices of the warriors’ wives. Anticlus nearly answers; Odysseus clamps his mouth shut, in later accounts until Anticlus dies. The killing detail is later than Homer’s concise version.

[E/C] After the horse enters Troy, Odysseus and Menelaus go to Deiphobus’ house. Accounts differ over who kills Deiphobus.

[E/C] Menelaus and Odysseus protect the Trojan Antenor or mark his house for preservation because of earlier hospitality.

[E/C] In the *Iliou Persis*, Odysseus kills the child Astyanax by throwing him from Troy’s wall; the *Little Iliad* assigns the killing to Neoptolemus. Euripidean tragedy turns the act into a Greek collective decision delivered by Talthybius. The variant must not be harmonized.

[C] Polyxena is sacrificed at Achilles’ tomb in cyclic and tragic tradition. Euripides’ *Hecuba* makes Odysseus the successful advocate and escort of the human sacrifice, despite Hecuba’s reminder that she once spared him. The play presents no clear divine necessity; the army votes for it.

[C] Hecuba becomes Odysseus’ allotted captive in several sources, although her subsequent fate varies.

### Nostos and wanderings

[E] After leaving Troy, Odysseus raids the Cicones at Ismarus; his men’s refusal to withdraw produces losses. The raid includes enslavement and plunder.

[E] The Lotus-eaters threaten memory and return; Odysseus forcibly drags affected crewmen back to the ships.

[E] In the Cyclops episode, Odysseus enters Polyphemus’ cave, blinds him after Polyphemus eats several crewmen, escapes beneath sheep, and reveals his name. Polyphemus invokes Poseidon, his father, producing the return’s governing curse. The “Nobody” trick belongs to a widespread one-eyed-ogre folktale complex; whether the Homeric poet borrowed a pre-existing international tale or contributed to its diffusion cannot be proved.

[E] Aeolus gives Odysseus a bag containing the winds; the crew opens it near Ithaca, believing it treasure.

[E] The Laestrygonians destroy every ship except Odysseus’ own.

[E] Circe turns part of the crew into swine. Hermes supplies *moly* and instructions. Odysseus compels Circe to restore them and remains for a year. Their sexual relationship is consensual only after an armed confrontation and oath; the poem does not treat the power imbalance in modern terms.

[E] At Circe’s direction, he performs a blood ritual and questions the dead. He speaks with Elpenor, Anticleia, Tiresias, Agamemnon, Achilles, Ajax, and others. Ajax refuses reconciliation because of the armor judgment.

[E] Odysseus hears the Sirens while bound to the mast; he blocks his crew’s ears with wax.

[E] He chooses passage by Scylla rather than risk the whole ship at Charybdis, withholding Circe’s warning from his men; Scylla kills six.

[E] On Thrinacia, the starving crew slaughter Helios’ cattle while Odysseus sleeps. Zeus destroys the ship; Odysseus alone survives.

[E] Calypso keeps Odysseus on Ogygia for seven years and offers immortality. The poem calls her relationship with him compulsory: he sleeps with her unwillingly while longing for Penelope.

[E] Poseidon wrecks his raft; Ino-Leucothea supplies a saving veil. The Phaeacian Nausicaa finds him, Arete and Alcinous receive him, and Demodocus’ songs cause him to reveal his identity. The Phaeacians return him with treasure and tripods; Poseidon petrifies their ship.

### Return, recognition, and suitors

[E] Athena disguises Odysseus as an old beggar. Eumaeus shelters him; Telemachus recognizes him after Athena reverses the disguise. Argos, his neglected dog, recognizes him and dies.

[E] The beggar contests the real beggar Irus, is abused by the suitors, tests servants, and speaks with Penelope without revealing himself.

[E] Eurycleia recognizes the boar scar while washing his feet; Odysseus threatens her into silence.

[E] Penelope announces the bow contest. Odysseus alone strings the bow and shoots through the axe-heads. He kills Antinous first, then Eurymachus, Amphinomus, and the other suitors with Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius.

[E] The poem depicts the slaughter as restoration of violated household and kingship, but it is also mass killing. Leodes’ plea is rejected. Melanthius is mutilated and killed. Telemachus hangs twelve enslaved women accused of sexual relations with the suitors; Odysseus had ordered death by sword. The text does not establish equal freedom of consent for enslaved women.

[E] Penelope tests Odysseus with the immovable marriage bed built around a living olive tree. Their recognition is intellectual and material, not merely emotional.

[E] Athena stops renewed civil violence between Odysseus’ party and the suitors’ relatives.

### Death and post-*Odyssey* endings

[E] Tiresias predicts that Odysseus must travel inland carrying an oar until someone mistakes it for a winnowing fan, sacrifice to Poseidon, return home, and receive a gentle death “from the sea” or “away from the sea” in prosperous old age. The Greek phrase is ambiguous.

[C/L] The *Telegony*, attributed to Eugammon of Cyrene and usually placed in the sixth century BCE, narrates a different completion. Odysseus performs the inland journey, visits Elis, returns to Ithaca, then travels to Thesprotia. He marries Queen Callidice, fathers Polypoetes, fights the Brygians, and returns after her death. Telegonus, his son by Circe, raids Ithaca unknowingly and kills him with a spear tipped by a stingray spine. Recognition follows too late. Telegonus takes the body, Penelope, and Telemachus to Circe; Circe makes them immortal, Telegonus marries Penelope, and Telemachus marries Circe. [Proclus’ transmitted summary](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/epic-cycle/telegony/).

[R/L] Aetolian tradition instead has Odysseus exiled after the suitors’ deaths by Neoptolemus’ arbitration. He goes to Thoas in Aetolia, marries Thoas’ daughter, fathers Leontophonus, and dies in old age. This aligns more closely with the peaceful Homeric prophecy but is a later regional version.

[R/L] In Parthenius’ *Erotica Pathemata*, Odysseus fathers Euryalus by Euippe in Epirus. Euryalus later comes to Ithaca; Penelope, learning his identity, induces Odysseus to kill him unknowingly. This is an ironic filial-death doublet of the Telegonus story.

[L] Later genealogies assign him still more children: Telemachus, Acusilaus or Ptoliporthes by Penelope; Agrius, Latinus, Telegonus, and Cassiphone by Circe; Nausithous, Nausinous, Auson, Telegonus, or Teledamus by Calypso; Polypoetes by Callidice; Euryalus by Euippe; Leontophonus by Thoas’ daughter. These traditions cannot be made into one consistent family.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical outline

```text
Arcesius
└── Laertes ── Anticleia, daughter of Autolycus
    ├── Odysseus ── Penelope
    │   ├── Telemachus [Homer]
    │   └── Acusilaus/Ptoliporthes [later variants]
    └── Ctimene

Competing paternity:
Sisyphus ── Anticleia → Odysseus [Classical/later hostile tradition]

Odysseus ── Circe → Agrius, Latinus, Telegonus, Cassiphone [varying sources]
Odysseus ── Calypso → Nausithous, Nausinous, Auson, Telegonus/Teledamus
Odysseus ── Callidice → Polypoetes
Odysseus ── Euippe → Euryalus
Odysseus ── daughter of Thoas → Leontophonus
```

### Individually documented interactions

The following catalog lists named beings with direct narrative interaction, not merely shared genealogy.

- **Achilles** — embassy speaker, comrade, rival in rhetoric; later defends his corpse and receives his armor. *Iliad* 9, 23; *Little Iliad*; fifth-century lyric and drama. **[E/C]**
- **Aeolus** — hosts Odysseus and gives him the winds, then rejects his second appeal. *Odyssey* 10. **[E]**
- **Agamemnon** — commander, council partner, quarrel mediator; Odysseus later speaks with his shade. *Iliad*; *Odyssey* 11. **[E]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Ithaca: Polis cave

[E] The cave above Polis Bay contained Mycenaean material and substantial Early Iron Age through Hellenistic/Roman votives. Its bronze tripod-cauldron series, dated chiefly to the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, indicates a sanctuary connected to elite and maritime movement.

[SR] The exact relation between cave and poem admits at least four models:

1. the poet knew a pre-existing tripod sanctuary and transformed it into the cave where Phaeacian gifts were stored;
2. worshippers familiar with the poem dedicated tripods in imitation of it;
3. cult and song shaped one another over time;
4. the numerical resemblance is partly coincidental and later interpretation Homerized an older sanctuary.

The archaeological evidence does not decide among them. [Discussion in *The Cambridge Guide to Homer*](https://eclass.upatras.gr/modules/document/file.php/LIT2211/%CE%8C%CE%BC%CE%B7%CF%81%CE%BF%CF%82%2C%20%CE%9F%CE%B4.%20%CE%99%CE%A7-%CE%A7%CE%99%CE%99/2%20The%20Cambridge%20Guide%20to%20Homer-Cambridge%20University%20Press%20%282020%29.pdf).

[R] A fourth-century BCE temenos wall appears to have divided the cave’s cult area. The explicit ΕΥΧΗΝ ΟΔΥΣΣΕΙ dedication securely identifies Odysseus in the Hellenistic cult phase. Other finds include masks, pottery, coins, and dedications.

[R] An inscription from Magnesia on the Maeander mentions Ithacan contests called the **Odysseia**, apparently instituted or reorganized in the Hellenistic period. This supports festival honors but does not prove that ninth-century tripods were athletic prizes.

[R] The term **Odysseion** could designate an Odysseus sanctuary. Recent claims that structures at “Homer’s School” or Agios Athanasios constitute Odysseus’ palace or definitive heroon remain dependent on excavation publication and epigraphic context. No excavated building can be identified as the historical palace of a verified individual Odysseus.

### Aetolia and Epirus

[R] The evidence here is principally genealogical and literary, not a comparably secure excavated sanctuary.

- In the Aetolian variant, Odysseus marries the daughter of Thoas and dies in old age. This embeds him in Aetolian dynastic geography.
- In Epirus/Thesprotia, the *Telegony* gives him Callidice and Polypoetes; Parthenius gives him Euippe and Euryalus.
- His fictive Cretan tales in the *Odyssey* send him to Dodona to learn Zeus’ will. These are stories Odysseus tells in disguise, not unqualified narrator testimony.
- Modern localization of the *Nekyia* at the Acheron “Necromanteion” is controversial. The visible Hellenistic structure was once identified as an oracle of the dead but has also been reinterpreted as a fortified farmhouse or storage complex. Homer does not name Ephyra as the ritual’s site.

[SR] Malkin argues that Odyssean return myths helped articulate Greek–Epirote and Greek–Italian encounters, producing genealogies before or alongside formal colonization. This explains political use of the hero better than treating the stories as records of one traveler. [Book description and chapter structure](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Returns_of_Odysseus.html?id=8eORbgLB6a4C).

### Western Mediterranean and Italy

[R/L] Communities and antiquarians connected Odysseus with Epirus, Sicily, southern Italy, Etruria, Latium, Iberia, Lisbon, and numerous coastal sites. Strabo reports foundation and place-name traditions; Roman authors make his descendants founders or eponyms.

[R/S] Etruscan **Uthuze** is the visual-cultural adaptation of Greek Odysseus, appearing in Etruscan art, notably the blinding of the Cyclops and underworld scenes. This is not evidence that an Etruscan historical king was Odysseus.

[R/L] Cortona and other Italian sites claimed tombs, ashes, relics, or descent. Such competitive possession is a normal form of local mythography; none constitutes a verified burial.

### Tomb

[E] Homer does not locate Odysseus’ tomb.

[C] The *Telegony* transports his corpse to Circe’s island for burial.

[R] Aetolian tradition lets him die in Aetolia.

[R/L] Lycophronian and scholastic traditions connect his ashes or burial with western locations, including Etruria.

**Finding:** no archaeologically authenticated tomb or bodily relic of Odysseus exists.

## 6. Historical evolution

### c. 800–650 BCE: epic formation

[E] Odysseus emerges in surviving evidence already bifocal: in the *Iliad*, a disciplined warrior-counselor serving the coalition; in the *Odyssey*, a returning king whose intelligence passes through folktale, seafaring, household, and succession plots. His complexity is therefore early, not a late correction to a simple “trickster.”

### Seventh–sixth centuries BCE: cycle and images

[E/C] The Trojan Cycle filled narrative gaps—recruitment, Palamedes, arms, Palladium, horse, sack, Telegonus—and often intensified Odysseus’ morally compromising acts. Visual art selected recognizable moments: Cyclops, Circe, Sirens, underworld, and sack.

### Fifth century BCE: Athenian contestation

[C] Tragedy converted epic cunning into a political and ethical problem. Sophocles could make Odysseus the humane opponent in *Ajax* and coercive pragmatist in *Philoctetes*. Euripides associated him with rhetoric, collective violence, human sacrifice, enslavement, and the slaughter of Trojan children.

[SR] It is tempting to equate this directly with anti-democratic or anti-Athenian propaganda. The secure conclusion is narrower: fifth-century drama used Odysseus to test whether expediency, persuasion, and civic necessity justify deceit and cruelty. [Modern study of his Athenian transformation](https://etheses.durham.ac.uk/id/eprint/15519/).

### Hellenistic period: genealogy, localization, and cult

[R/L] Mythographers and poets extended Odysseus into Thesprotia, Aetolia, Italy, and dynastic genealogies. Ithaca supplies explicit cult evidence and festival terminology. Learned geography increasingly sought physical counterparts for poetic places.

### Roman period: Ulysses from Trojan eyes

[C/S] Roman literature inherited both admiration and hostility. Virgil’s Trojans call him cruel and deceitful, yet Horace can make him a Stoic-like model of endurance and self-command. Italian genealogies domesticated him as ancestor even while Trojan national narrative vilified him.

### Late antiquity and Middle Ages

[L/M] Dictys and Dares recast the Trojan War as prose history, strengthening the image of Ulysses as fraudulent Greek. Western medieval authors generally knew Homer indirectly.

[M] Dante’s *Inferno* 26, composed in the early fourteenth century, places **Ulisse** with Diomedes among fraudulent counselors. Dante invents a last voyage beyond the limits set for humanity: Ulisse abandons home again, urges his aged companions to pursue virtue and knowledge, sails beyond the Pillars of Hercules, and dies in a divinely sent whirlpool near the mountain of Purgatory. This is not the Homeric or *Telegony* ending. [Text and commentary](https://digitaldante.columbia.edu/dante/divine-comedy/inferno/inferno-26/inferno-26-mandelbaum/).

### Renaissance to Romanticism

[M] Renewed Greek learning restored Homer’s more affirmative figure, while Dante’s transgressive explorer remained influential. Odysseus became a symbol of navigation, knowledge, political prudence, and sometimes Machiavellian fraud.

[M] Operatic treatments included Monteverdi’s *Il ritorno d’Ulisse in patria* (1640), centered on recognition and recovered rule.

[M/MI] Tennyson’s “Ulysses” (published 1842) transforms the old king into a restless Victorian speaker determined “to strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.” This owes more to Dante and nineteenth-century ideals than to Homeric closure.

### Twentieth century to present

[MI] James Joyce’s *Ulysses* (published 1922) maps one Dublin day, 16 June 1904, onto the *Odyssey*: Leopold Bloom corresponds broadly to Odysseus, Molly to Penelope, Stephen Dedalus to Telemachus. It universalizes the returning hero while replacing martial aristocracy with urban domestic life.

[MI] Nikos Kazantzakis’ *Odyssey: A Modern Sequel* (Greek, 1938) sends Odysseus away again through revolution, exploration, and metaphysical quest. It is an avowedly modern continuation, not recovered tradition.

[MI] Major modern rewritings include:

- Giraudoux, *La guerre de Troie n’aura pas lieu* (1935).
- Horkheimer and Adorno, *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944/1947), reading Odysseus as a prototype of instrumental bourgeois reason.
- Jean Cocteau and related modernist theatrical adaptations.
- Derek Walcott, *The Odyssey* (1993), a Caribbean theatrical transformation.
- Margaret Atwood, *The Penelopiad* (2005), giving Penelope and the hanged maids counter-voices.
- Madeline Miller, *Circe* (2018), recentering Circe.
- The Coen brothers’ *O Brother, Where Art Thou?* (2000), a loose Depression-era comic analogue.
- The films *Ulysses* (1954), *L’Odissea* (1968 television), *The Odyssey* (1997), and *The Return* (2024).

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and scholarship-shaped images

[MI] Joyce’s Bloom is the most influential modern Ulysses: Jewish, domestic, non-martial, sexually displaced, and humane. Joyce’s episode names were supplied chiefly through schemas rather than printed as headings in the first edition; claims that the novel is a scene-by-scene retelling oversimplify its method. [Oxford research on its Greek reception](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A44ec50f9-0141-489e-9978-b4d069e12724).

[MI] Modern feminist reception interrogates the enslaved women, Penelope’s constrained agency, Calypso’s detention of Odysseus, Circe’s transformation, and the epic’s sexual double standard.

[MI] “The Odyssey” has become a generic commercial label for travel, endurance, exploration, vehicles, software, games, and space missions. Such uses draw on modern cultural shorthand and should not be treated as survivals of cult.

### Psychology and comparative theory

[SR] Freud used classical myth extensively but did not establish an “Odysseus complex” parallel to the Oedipus complex.

[SR] Otto Rank’s hero-birth pattern only partially fits Odysseus; Homer lacks infant exposure or threatened royal birth.

[SR] Lord Raglan’s ritual-hero pattern also fits him poorly because Odysseus survives the central epic and restores his kingdom rather than dying sacrificially within it.

[SR] Joseph Campbell’s *Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949) helped popularize a generalized “hero’s journey.” Applying its monomyth rigidly to the *Odyssey* can suppress the poem’s household, succession, oral-poetic, and gendered structures. Campbell’s model is a twentieth-century synthesis, not an ancient Greek interpretation.

[SR] Structuralist readings analyze oppositions such as raw/cooked, human/monster, home/wilderness, identity/anonymity, and guest/host. These are explanatory models rather than recovered authorial intentions.

[SR] Gregory Nagy’s work treats Odysseus within evolving performance traditions, competition for heroic prestige, and the semantics of cunning and poetic identity. Oral-traditional scholarship generally rejects the assumption that every variant derives from one fixed original biography.

### Nationalism and politics

[R/M] Ancient western foundation myths used Odysseus to mediate colonial and ethnic claims. He could provide local elites with heroic Greek ancestry without proving mass Greek descent.

[M/MI] Nineteenth-century European Hellenism used Homeric heroes within narratives of classical inheritance; modern Greek education and tourism likewise make Ithaca and Odysseus national-cultural symbols.

[MI] Joyce’s “Cyclops” episode caricatures exclusionary nationalism through a one-eyed Citizen. That political use is deliberately anti-monolithic, not a nationalist consecration of Odysseus. [Study of Joyce’s classical engagement and nationalism](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3991BF4288B9F404CAA4050CCD6F393C/9781108844864c2_85-122.pdf/hellenise_it.pdf).

[MI] Claims that Odysseus is inherently a modern racial, European, colonial, liberal, fascist, or anti-fascist emblem are acts of reception. The ancient corpus itself supplies material for opposed appropriations: conqueror and displaced veteran, slave-owner and beggar, colonizing ancestor and homesick island king, ingenious survivor and fraudulent destroyer.

No well-documented continuous extremist cult of Odysseus comparable to modern extremist use of Spartan symbols was found. His political appropriation is broader and more literary than organizational.

### Recurrent modern inventions falsely presented as ancient

- **“Odysseus was a verified Mycenaean king whose palace has been found.”** **[MI]** Archaeology can illuminate Ithaca’s settlement and cult but has not identified a historical individual.
- **“The Polis tripods prove the *Odyssey* is a factual travel diary.”** **[MI]** They prove a sanctuary; literary-historical direction of influence remains debated.
- **“Homer says Odysseus invented the horse entirely by himself.”** **[MI/oversimplification]** Homer centers him, while cyclic tradition makes Epeius the builder under Athena.
- **“Homer narrates Telegonus killing Odysseus.”** **[MI/error]** That ending belongs to the *Telegony* and later witnesses.
- **“Odysseus was an Argonaut.”** **[MI/error]** Standard generational tradition makes his father Laertes an Argonaut.
- **“Nausicaa marries Odysseus.”** **[MI/error]** The *Odyssey* entertains the possibility but returns him to Penelope.
- **“The Sirens were mermaids in Homer.”** **[MI]** Homer gives no physical description; bird-women are early visual tradition, fish-tailed sirens are later.
- **“The Trojan horse appears in the *Iliad*.”** **[MI/error]** It appears in the *Odyssey* and cyclic sack traditions; the *Iliad* ends before Achilles’ death and Troy’s fall.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

[SR] Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship sometimes sought a Bronze Age chieftain behind Odysseus. Archaeological evidence for Late Bronze Age settlement in the Ionian Islands makes a local ruling elite plausible in general, but no inscription, tomb, palace archive, or synchronizable foreign record names Odysseus.

[SR] The disciplined position is agnostic: oral traditions may preserve transformed memories, but the documented object is the evolving character. Treating topographical resemblance or a heroic name as proof of one historical biography is circular.

### Homeric Ithaca

[SR] Ithaca’s Homeric geography has been located on modern Ithaki, Leukas, Cephalonia, Paliki, and elsewhere. Ancient identification strongly favors modern Ithaki, but detailed textual correspondences are imperfect. Landscape may be selectively remembered, poetic, formulaic, or composite.

[SR] Claims of exact route reconstruction belong to the history of Homeric geography. Since the present dossier concerns the figure rather than an atlas, they matter chiefly because communities used location claims to appropriate the hero.

### Oral-formulaic composition

[SR] Milman Parry and Albert Lord demonstrated that Homeric diction is deeply formulaic and compatible with oral composition-in-performance. This displaced older assumptions that a single literary author invented Odysseus’ complete character at one moment.

[SR] Analysts once treated inconsistencies as seams between authors; Unitarians emphasized artistic design. Current approaches often combine oral tradition, diachronic development, and recognition of large-scale composition. The poems can be artistically organized without being modern novels written from scratch.

### Folktale and Near Eastern comparison

[SR/F] The Cyclops has extensive international one-eyed-ogre analogues; the homecoming husband, recognition scar, magical women, descent to the dead, and impossible contest likewise have comparanda.

[SR] Parallel does not establish direct borrowing, date, or ethnic origin. Individual motifs may be ancient and mobile, while their combination into Odysseus’ return is specifically Greek epic work. A recent compositional study situates trickster, ogre, and returning-husband materials within a late-seventh-century poetic construction. [Oxford overview](https://academic.oup.com/book/3033).

### The Epic Cycle

[SR] The non-Homeric cyclic poems are not simply late fan fiction, nor can their contents be confidently treated as earlier than Homer. Some episodes may preserve old traditions; their attested poetic forms may be later.

[L] Proclus’ summaries are invaluable but drastically compressed and transmitted many centuries after the poems. Apollodorus adds material from multiple sources without marking every transition. Reconstruction must distinguish direct fragment, ancient testimony, and summary.

### Hero cult

[SR] Older scholarship often assumed epic automatically generated hero cult or that cult automatically generated epic. Archaeological work shows more complex, site-specific interaction.

[SR] For Ithaca, the secure sequence is: early sanctuary and tripods; later explicit Odysseus dedication and festival evidence. Whether Odysseus was the original recipient remains open. Jonathan Burgess, Carla Antonaccio, Irad Malkin, Bruno Currie, and others differ over the priority and interaction of epic, travel, landscape, and cult.

### Iconography

[SR] Labeled vases provide stronger identifications than unlabeled scenes. Attributes such as the *pilos*, short beard, traveler’s cloak, or association with Circe and Polyphemus become conventional, but resemblance alone cannot identify every isolated warrior.

The *LIMC* entry remains the essential systematic catalog, but much of it is not freely accessible online. Museum catalogues should be checked object by object; for example, the British Museum explicitly marks uncertainty in an early attempted identification. [British Museum object record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1888-0208-106-e).

### Discredited or weakly supported theories

- A solar or lunar Odysseus whose adventures encode one complete celestial cycle: historically important comparative speculation, not established evidence.
- A single real voyage whose every monster maps onto an exact Mediterranean landmark: underdetermined by the text.
- A proven Luwian biography inferred from a disputed name etymology: unsupported.
- Direct continuity from a Mycenaean ruler’s domestic cult to the Hellenistic Odysseion: possible but unproved.
- The excavated Ephyra structure as unquestionably Homer’s functioning necromanteion: disputed both archaeologically and textually.
- All negative portrayals as late anti-Odyssean propaganda: false; moral ambiguity is already present in early epic, although tragedy intensifies it.

## 10. Sources and source criticism

### Primary editions and corpora

The indispensable documentary base is:

- Homer, *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, with the Oxford Classical Text, Teubner, and major commentaries.
- Martin L. West, ed. and trans., *Greek Epic Fragments* and *The Epic Cycle*.
- Merkelbach–West and subsequent editions of Hesiodic fragments.
- Tragic texts in Oxford Classical Texts and *Tragicorum Graecorum Fragmenta*.
- Apollodorus, *Library and Epitome*.
- Pausanias, *Description of Greece*.
- Hyginus, *Fabulae*.
- Lycophron, *Alexandra*, with scholia.
- Homeric and dramatic scholia; Eustathius; Tzetzes.
- *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae*—“Odysseus/Uthuze”—for visual evidence.
- *Inscriptiones Graecae*, especially the Ithacan series, for cult inscriptions.

### Major modern scholarship

- Timothy Gantz, *Early Greek Myth* (1993): source-by-source reconstruction without forced harmonization.
- M. L. West, *The Epic Cycle* (2013): fragments, summaries, and lost-epic reconstruction.
- Robert Fowler, *Early Greek Mythography*, vols. 1–2: fragmentary genealogical and mythographic traditions.
- Gregory Nagy, *The Best of the Achaeans* and work on hero cult and performance traditions.
- Irad Malkin, *The Returns of Odysseus* (1998): western nostoi, colonization, ethnicity, Ithaca, and Epirus.
- L. R. Farnell, *Greek Hero Cults and Ideas of Immortality* (1921): foundational but requiring archaeological updating.
- Walter Burkert, *Greek Religion*: ritual and religious framework.
- Gunnel Ekroth’s studies of hero cult and sacrificial ritual: correction of overly uniform models.
- Carla Antonaccio, *An Archaeology of Ancestors*: archaeological differentiation of tomb cult and hero cult.
- Bruno Currie, *Pindar and the Cult of Heroes*: interaction between poetry and cult.
- W. B. Stanford, *The Ulysses Theme*: influential reception history, now supplemented by more recent work.
- Jonathan Burgess, *The Tradition of the Trojan War in Homer and the Epic Cycle*: cyclic and iconographic traditions.
- Denys Page, Alfred Heubeck, Stephanie West, J. B. Hainsworth, A. Hoekstra, Joseph Russo, Manuel Fernández-Galiano, and Alfred Heubeck: major *Odyssey* commentary traditions.

### Final assessment

The securely documented Odysseus is not a recoverable Bronze Age biography but an evolving record:

- an already complex warrior and counselor in early epic;
- the intelligent, violent, suffering returner of the *Odyssey*;
- the increasingly compromised strategist of Cycle and tragedy;
- the recipient of explicitly attested Hellenistic cult on Ithaca;
- a regional ancestor in Epirus, Aetolia, and Italy;
- Rome’s admirable survivor and hateful Greek deceiver;
- Dante’s damned explorer;
- Joyce’s ordinary urban wanderer;
- and a continuing instrument for arguments about intelligence, homecoming, colonialism, masculinity, war, migration, and the moral cost of survival.

The most important negative conclusions are equally documentary: no Linear B Odysseus is securely known; no historical palace, tomb, skeleton, or biography has been authenticated; the earliest Polis cave tripods do not name him; and neither the *Telegony* nor Dante’s final voyage is Homeric. Those absences define the limits of the record.
