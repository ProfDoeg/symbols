# The Seven against Thebes: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A collective of mortal epic warriors; several members were subsequently heroized, while Amphiaraus developed an especially important oracular and healing cult and was sometimes treated as a god. Adrastus alone survives the expedition in the dominant tradition.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The tradition of an unsuccessful Argive expedition against Thebes is securely attested in the *Iliad*, especially 4.365–421 and 14.113–127, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE. Homer names Tydeus, Polynices, and Adrastus and presupposes a well-known earlier Theban war, but neither supplies a canonical group of seven nor assigns seven attackers to seven gates. The first completely preserved, securely dated list of seven champions is Aeschylus’s *Seven against Thebes*, performed at Athens in 467 BCE.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Securely attested archaic material, approximately eighth–sixth centuries BCE.
- **[CLASSICAL]** Canonical or substantially preserved fifth–fourth-century BCE formulation.
- **[VARIANT]** Regional, authorial, or otherwise competing ancient version.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Hellenistic or Roman mythographic organization of earlier traditions.
- **[ROMAN]** Roman-period literary elaboration, especially Statius.
- **[CULT]** Literary, epigraphic, or archaeological evidence for worship, ritual, tomb, oracle, or shrine.
- **[MEDIEVAL/EARLY MODERN]** Post-antique reception or accretion.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern scholarly inference, not an ancient assertion.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A recent retelling, conflation, or claim without ancient authority.
- **[ABSENCE]** A proposition for which the extant evidence is silent.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### 1.1 The collective

The Seven are not a stable primordial fellowship but a changing literary roster attached to Polynices’ attempt to recover Thebes from his brother Eteocles. In the most influential Greek arrangement they are Polynices, Adrastus, Tydeus, Capaneus, Amphiaraus, Hippomedon, and Parthenopaeus. Aeschylus instead assigns the seven attacking positions to Tydeus, Capaneus, Eteoclus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Amphiaraus, and Polynices; Adrastus commands the expedition but is not one of his seven gate champions. Later lists variously introduce Eteoclus, Mecisteus, or Adrastus and omit Polynices or Tydeus.

The defining ancient paradox is therefore important: “the Seven” may mean seven gate-champions, seven principal expeditionary leaders, or a traditional numerical title imposed on a larger command. There were many other Argive combatants.

### 1.2 Collective names

- **Ἑπτὰ ἐπὶ Θήβας**, *Heptà epì Thḗbas*, “Seven against Thebes” or “Seven at/against Thebes.” The preposition *epi* with the accusative conveys hostile movement toward a place. **[CLASSICAL]** The title is securely attached to Aeschylus’s play, *Hepta epi Thēbas*, in the dramatic tradition.
- **οἱ ἑπτὰ ἐπὶ Θήβας**, “the seven [who went] against Thebes,” becomes the ordinary learned designation. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Latin: **Septem contra Thebas** or **Septem adversus Thebas**. These are conventional Latin renderings, not the title of Statius’s epic.
- Statius calls his subject the **Thebais/Thebaid**, “Theban poem,” and writes of a *septemplex ... exercitus*, a “sevenfold army” (*Thebaid* 12). **[ROMAN]**
- French **Les Sept contre Thèbes**, Italian **I sette contro Tebe**, German **Sieben gegen Theben**, Spanish **Los siete contra Tebas** are modern vernacular titles.

“Argive,” “Danaans,” and “Inachians” are geographical or poetic designations for the expeditionary side, although several leaders are outsiders who have come under Adrastus’s protection. “Cadmeans” and “Thebans” designate their opponents.

### 1.4 Status and identifications

- Adrastus: mortal king of Argos/Sicyon and later recipient of hero honors at Sicyon, Megara, and Attica. **[EARLY/CULT]**
- Tydeus: mortal Aetolian exile; Athena intends to immortalize him in one tradition but withdraws the gift after he consumes Melanippus’s brain. **[EARLY/CLASSICAL VARIANT]**
- Capaneus: mortal warrior destroyed directly by Zeus; later honored with a tomb and heroic memory, but no evidence makes him a thunder god. **[CLASSICAL/CULT/ABSENCE]**
- Amphiaraus: mortal seer in epic genealogy, swallowed alive by the earth and thereafter an oracular hero or god. Herodotus 1.46–49 already treats his oracle as one tested by Croesus. **[EARLY/CULT]**
- Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Eteoclus, Mecisteus, Polynices: mortals; tombs or heroic commemoration are reported, but continuous individual cult is unevenly documented. **[CULT]**
- There is no secure ancient syncretistic identification of the collective Seven with seven planets, seven demons, the Seven Sages, or a Near Eastern divine heptad. Such equations are modern speculation. **[ABSENCE/MODERN INVENTION]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 Homer

#### *Iliad* 4.365–421

**[EARLY: late eighth/early seventh century BCE in conventional dating]** Agamemnon rebukes Diomedes by recalling Tydeus. Tydeus accompanied Polynices to Mycenae while they recruited allies against “holy-walled Thebes.” At Thebes he challenged and defeated the Cadmeans, then killed forty-nine of fifty men sent to ambush him, sparing Maion because of divine signs.

What it establishes:

- Tydeus and Polynices joined an Argive-supported campaign against Thebes.
- The expedition required recruitment beyond Argos.
- Tydeus undertook an embassy or mission and survived an ambush.
- The story was sufficiently familiar to function as ancestral exemplum.

What it does **not** establish:

- A company of exactly seven.
- Seven gates and seven formal duels.
- The alternating-kingship agreement.
- Capaneus’s lightning death or the complete later roster.

#### *Iliad* 5.800–813; 10.284–291; 14.113–127

Athena recalls assisting Tydeus at Thebes; Diomedes invokes his father; Agamemnon names Tydeus as Adrastus’s son-in-law, says that he lies buried at Thebes, and contrasts the successful Epigoni with their fathers. **[EARLY]** The failed first expedition and successful second expedition are already a paired tradition.

#### *Odyssey* 11.326–327; 15.243–255

Eriphyle is condemned for accepting precious gold in exchange for her husband; Amphiaraus is remembered as a prophet loved by Zeus and Apollo who died because of “women’s gifts.” **[EARLY]** The necklace-bribery tradition is presupposed, though the complete later account is not narrated.

### 2.2 Lost archaic epic

#### Cyclic *Thebaid*

A lost Greek hexameter epic, probably composed in the seventh or sixth century BCE, traditionally assigned in antiquity to Homer and elsewhere to an uncertain poet. Ancient notices credited it with about 7,000 verses. Only fragments and testimonia survive. **[EARLY, fragmentary]**

Among the most consequential fragments:

- Oedipus curses his sons when Polynices sets before him Laius’s silver table and golden cup.
- In another episode he curses them over an inadequate portion of sacrificial meat.
- Amphiaraus, hostile to Tydeus for forcing the war, cuts off Melanippus’s head; Tydeus eats the brain, causing Athena to recoil from granting immortality.

Because quotations are isolated and mediated by scholiasts, reconstruction of the poem’s plot and roster remains uncertain.

#### *Epigoni*

A lost archaic epic concerning the sons of the defeated champions and their successful return against Thebes. Herodotus 4.32 knew verses attributed to “Homer” about the Epigoni but doubted or discussed authorship. **[EARLY, fragmentary]**

#### Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*

Fragments preserve genealogical material concerning figures around the Theban and Argive cycles, including Atalanta and her descendants. The surviving fragments do not permit a continuous Seven narrative. **[EARLY, fragmentary]**

### 2.3 Lyric poetry

- Pindar, *Nemean* 9, approximately 474 BCE: Adrastus recalls Amphiaraus being swallowed by the earth after the expedition; the poem ties the Sicyonian context to Adrastus. **[CLASSICAL, preserving earlier traditions]**
- Pindar, *Olympian* 6, 472 BCE: Amphiaraus praises the young Alcmaeon during the Epigoni’s attack. **[CLASSICAL]**
- Pindar, *Pythian* 8, 446 BCE: the Epigoni and Alcmaeon exemplify inherited prowess and altered fortune. **[CLASSICAL]**
- Bacchylidean fragments and victory poetry contain related Argive genealogical material but no complete expedition.

### 2.4 Tragedy

#### Aeschylus

- *Laius*, lost.
- *Oedipus*, lost.
- *Seven against Thebes*, preserved; performed in 467 BCE as the third tragedy of a tetralogy, followed by the satyr play *Sphinx*. The production won first prize. **[CLASSICAL]**
- *Eleusinians*, lost: apparently dramatized Theseus’s recovery of the Argive dead. **[CLASSICAL, fragmentary]**

The preserved *Seven* is the earliest complete articulation of seven named attackers at seven gates. Its final Antigone episode, beginning approximately at line 1005 in the transmitted text, is widely considered a later addition or substantial reworking designed to connect the play with the familiar burial prohibition. That remains a textual-critical conclusion, not a unanimously proven fact.

#### Sophocles

- *Antigone*, conventionally dated around 442 BCE: begins after the brothers’ deaths; Creon permits Eteocles’ burial and prohibits Polynices’.
- *Oedipus at Colonus*, performed posthumously in 401 BCE: Polynices attempts to recruit Oedipus’s support and receives a curse forecasting mutual fratricide.
- Lost *Epigoni*, *Eriphyle*, and possibly related plays survive only in fragments and titles.

#### Euripides

- *Suppliant Women*, approximately 423 BCE: Adrastus and the mothers of the dead ask Theseus to recover the unburied bodies; Theseus defeats Thebes, and Evadne leaps onto Capaneus’s pyre.
- *Phoenician Women*, produced approximately 411–409 BCE: gives an extended prehistory, embassy, gate-array, Menoeceus’s sacrifice, brothers’ duel, Jocasta’s suicide, and Antigone’s response.
- *Hypsipyle*, late fifth century BCE and fragmentary: the expedition encounters Hypsipyle at Nemea; Opheltes dies and the Nemean funeral games are founded.
- Lost *Alcmaeon* plays treated the Epigoni and Eriphyle’s death.

### 2.5 Historians and mythographers

- Herodotus, *Histories* 1.46–49, approximately 430s–420s BCE: Croesus tests the oracle of Amphiaraus; 5.61 names a descendant of Adrastus. **[CLASSICAL/CULT]**
- Hellanicus, Pherecydes, Acusilaus: fragments preserve genealogical and narrative variants. **[EARLY/CLASSICAL MYTHOGRAPHY]**
- Diodorus Siculus 4.65–66, first century BCE: rationalized consecutive account of the Seven and Epigoni. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- “Apollodorus,” *Bibliotheca* 1.8 and 3.5–7, probably first or second century CE: the fullest compact Greek mythographic synthesis, expressly preserving alternative rosters. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Hyginus, *Fabulae* 68–73, transmitted in a corrupt late Latin text whose compilation is conventionally placed around the first–second centuries CE: roster, expedition, deaths, and Epigoni, often with divergent names. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Pausanias, *Description of Greece*, approximately 150–175 CE: tombs, dedications, local traditions, gates, and cult sites. **[CULT/SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### 2.6 Latin poetry

- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.404–417, approximately 8 CE: Themis prophesies the expedition and Capaneus as the only attacker whom Jupiter himself will kill. **[ROMAN]**
- Ovid, *Ibis* and *Tristia*: allusive uses of Theban exempla rather than a continuous narrative.
- Statius, *Thebaid*, composed approximately 80–92 CE: twelve-book Latin epic and the most extensive surviving version. Its book sequence proceeds from Polynices’ exile and alliance with Tydeus through the Nemean delay, deaths of the leaders, fratricide, Creon’s burial prohibition, and Theseus’s intervention. **[ROMAN]**
- Valerius Flaccus, *Argonautica*, late first century CE: includes figures related to the Seven and later Argonautic genealogies, but is not a Seven narrative.

### 2.7 Manuscript survival

Aeschylus’s play survives through the medieval manuscript tradition of the seven selected tragedies, not through a contemporary script. Statius’s *Thebaid* survives extraordinarily well, in more than 160 medieval manuscripts; principal textual families include the ninth-century *Puteaneus* and a large vulgate tradition. The abundance of Statian witnesses contrasts with the fragments of the archaic Greek *Thebaid*.

## 3. The story

### 3.1 The dynastic cause

Oedipus’s sons Eteocles and Polynices inherit the cursed house of Laius. The sources disagree about their political arrangement:

1. **Alternating annual rule:** Eteocles rules first and refuses to surrender power when his year ends. This is explicit in later tragedy and mythography, notably Euripides’s *Phoenician Women* and Apollodorus 3.6.1. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
2. **Division of kingship and property:** In some accounts one brother receives the kingship and the other movable wealth, including the necklace and robe of Harmonia; the settlement fails. **[VARIANT]**
3. **Expulsion without a detailed compact:** Aeschylus’s surviving play presupposes Polynices’ hostile return but does not narrate a full alternating-rule contract. **[CLASSICAL/ABSENCE]**
4. **Oedipus’s curse as primary cause:** The lost *Thebaid* fragments make a paternal curse over the table/cup or sacrificial portion central. Aeschylus repeatedly presents the inherited curse as ripening through the brothers. **[EARLY/CLASSICAL]**

No source establishes that such a dynasty or succession crisis was historical.

### 3.2 Polynices and Tydeus at Argos

Polynices goes into exile and reaches Adrastus’s palace at Argos. Tydeus, exiled from Calydon after a disputed killing, arrives independently. They fight outside or within the palace. Adrastus recognizes an oracle that he must marry his daughters to a lion and a boar: the animal imagery is variously supplied by shields, skins, or the men’s temperaments. He gives Argia to Polynices and Deipyle to Tydeus. Apollodorus 3.6.1 is the standard surviving synthesis. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**

Homer knows both men as connected with Adrastus but does not tell the lion-and-boar episode. **[EARLY/ABSENCE]**

### 3.3 Amphiaraus compelled to join

Amphiaraus foresees that the campaign will fail and that all the principal attackers but Adrastus will die. He refuses to join or hides. Polynices or Adrastus bribes Eriphyle, Amphiaraus’s wife and Adrastus’s sister, with Harmonia’s necklace. Earlier, Amphiaraus and Adrastus had agreed that Eriphyle would arbitrate their disputes; she rules for participation.

- The fatal “gifts to a woman” are already presupposed in *Odyssey* 11 and 15. **[EARLY]**
- The fully articulated arbitration and necklace plot appears in tragedy, mythography, and later epic. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Some authors add Harmonia’s robe in the later bribery connected with the Epigoni. **[VARIANT]**

Before departing, Amphiaraus commands his sons Alcmaeon and Amphilochus to avenge him by killing Eriphyle and to conduct the second expedition. This command becomes the basis of Alcmaeon’s matricide and pollution.

### 3.4 The march and Nemea

At Nemea the army lacks water. Hypsipyle, enslaved nurse of the infant Opheltes/Archemorus, leaves the child on the ground while guiding the heroes to a spring. A serpent kills him. Amphiaraus interprets the death as an omen—“beginning of doom”—and the heroes conduct funeral games, conventionally explaining the foundation of the Nemean Games.

- The episode was dramatized in Euripides’s fragmentary *Hypsipyle*. **[CLASSICAL]**
- Apollodorus 3.6.4 and Statius *Thebaid* 4–6 supply fuller accounts. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/ROMAN]**
- Statius greatly expands Hypsipyle’s Lemnian history, the serpent, lamentation, funeral, and games. **[ROMAN]**
- Historical Nemean Games are real; derivation from Opheltes’ funeral is a cult aetiology, not documentation of an actual Bronze Age founding. **[CULT/MYTHIC AETIOLOGY]**

### 3.5 Tydeus’s embassy

Tydeus goes to Thebes to demand Polynices’ restoration. He challenges Thebans to athletic or martial contests and defeats them. On his return he is ambushed by fifty men and kills forty-nine, sparing Maion.

This is one of the oldest securely attested episodes, already detailed in *Iliad* 4. **[EARLY]** Later sources make the embassy a formal negotiation and sometimes intensify Tydeus’s savagery.

### 3.6 The sevenfold oath

In Aeschylus *Seven* 42–56, the seven captains slaughter a bull over a black-rimmed shield, touch the blood, and swear either to sack Thebes or die and redden its soil. **[CLASSICAL]** No independent evidence shows that historical Argive commanders performed such a rite; it is a tragic representation of a terrible martial oath.

### 3.7 Rosters

| Source | Roster |
|---|---|
| Aeschylus, *Seven* (467 BCE) | Tydeus, Capaneus, Eteoclus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Amphiaraus, Polynices; Adrastus commands outside the gate catalogue. |
| Euripides, *Suppliant Women* (c. 423 BCE) | The dead praised include Capaneus, Eteoclus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Tydeus; Amphiaraus has vanished into the earth; Polynices is integral to the cause; Adrastus survives. |
| Euripides, *Phoenician Women* (c. 411–409 BCE) | Parthenopaeus, Tydeus, Polynices, Capaneus, Hippomedon, Adrastus, Amphiaraus. Eteoclus is not one of the principal seven in the same arrangement. |
| Apollodorus 3.6.3 | Adrastus, Amphiaraus, Capaneus, Hippomedon, Polynices, Tydeus, Parthenopaeus; “some” exclude Tydeus and Polynices and substitute Eteoclus and Mecisteus. |
| Diodorus 4.65 | Adrastus, Polynices, Tydeus, Amphiaraus, Capaneus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus. |
| Hyginus, *Fabulae* 70 | A corrupt and unstable Latin list, broadly aligned with the later canonical seven but with genealogical divergences. |
| Statius, *Thebaid* | Adrastus, Polynices, Tydeus, Hippomedon, Capaneus, Amphiaraus, Parthenopaeus, while other commanders, notably Eteoclus, also lead contingents. |

**Finding:** there is no recoverable original roster. Aeschylus provides the earliest complete surviving list, not proof of the first list ever composed.

### 3.9 The shield scenes

Aeschylus makes heraldic imagery the dramatic core:

- Tydeus: night sky with full moon and stars.
- Capaneus: a naked fire-bearing man with the declaration “I will burn the city.”
- Eteoclus: a warrior scaling a ladder while Ares himself cannot throw him down.
- Hippomedon: Typhon breathing fire.
- Parthenopaeus: the Sphinx carrying off a Theban.
- Amphiaraus: no boastful device; he wishes to be, not merely seem, the best.
- Polynices: Dike, Justice, leading a warrior home.

Eteocles verbally reinterprets each emblem against its bearer. **[CLASSICAL]** These are Aeschylus’s attested stage-poetic signs; they should not automatically be assigned to the lost epic or to historical shields.

### 3.10 Deaths

#### Tydeus

Melanippus mortally wounds Tydeus. Tydeus or Amphiaraus kills Melanippus. Amphiaraus, hating Tydeus, brings him the severed head; Tydeus splits it and eats the brain. Athena, approaching with immortality from Zeus, recoils.

- The cannibalism occurs in a fragment assigned to the cyclic *Thebaid*. **[EARLY]**
- Apollodorus 3.6.8 preserves the developed account. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Statius *Thebaid* 8 elaborates the act into a horrific climax. **[ROMAN]**

#### Capaneus

Capaneus boasts that not even Zeus can stop him, mounts the wall, and is struck by Zeus’s thunderbolt.

- Aeschylus *Seven* makes his shield and speech anticipate divine punishment but reports the deaths only collectively at the end. **[CLASSICAL]**
- Euripides *Suppliant Women* and *Phoenician Women* explicitly present the thunderbolt.
- Apollodorus 3.6.7–8, Ovid *Metamorphoses* 9, and Statius *Thebaid* 10 canonicalize and amplify it. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/ROMAN]**

In Euripides’s *Suppliant Women*, Capaneus’s wife Evadne appears above his pyre and leaps into the flames. This is suicide on her husband’s funeral pyre, not evidence for an ordinary Greek requirement of widow immolation. **[CLASSICAL]**

#### Amphiaraus

As Periclymenus pursues him, Zeus opens the earth with a thunderbolt; Amphiaraus, chariot, charioteer, and horses descend alive. Zeus renders him immortal in some formulations.

- Pindar *Nemean* 9 gives an early full poetic attestation. **[CLASSICAL]**
- Sophocles *Electra* 836–841, Euripides *Suppliant Women* 925–927, Apollodorus 3.6.8, and Pausanias 1.34 preserve variants.
- The disappearance supplies the charter for his underground oracle. **[CULT AETIOLOGY]**

#### Hippomedon

Aeschylus does not narrate an individual death. Later accounts make him die fighting near the Ismenus, sometimes overwhelmed in the river or killed by Ismarus. Statius *Thebaid* 9 gives the grandest river battle and death. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/ROMAN VARIANT]**

#### Parthenopaeus

- Euripides *Phoenician Women*: Periclymenus kills him with a massive stone. **[CLASSICAL]**
- Apollodorus: Amphidicus kills him; the account notes another attribution to Periclymenus. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/VARIANT]**
- Statius: Dryas kills him after an extensive Arcadian aristeia; his mother Atalanta senses the death far away. **[ROMAN]**

#### Eteoclus

Aeschylus assigns him to the Neistan Gate but does not individualize his death. Euripides’s *Suppliant Women* praises his modest, incorruptible civic virtue. Later traditions variously make Leades or Megareus his killer. **[CLASSICAL/VARIANT]**

#### Polynices and Eteocles

The brothers meet and kill one another.

- The mutual death is fundamental to Aeschylus’s catastrophe. **[CLASSICAL]**
- Euripides *Phoenician Women* stages a formal duel: Eteocles inflicts the first fatal wound but Polynices kills him during a final embrace or moment of apparent triumph; Jocasta kills herself over the bodies. **[CLASSICAL VARIANT]**
- Apollodorus gives mutual killing compactly.
- Statius *Thebaid* 11 intensifies infernal influence, hatred, and the later supernatural hostility of their funeral flames. **[ROMAN]**

### 3.11 Outcome and burial

The expedition fails. Adrastus escapes on the divine horse Arion.

The burial traditions conflict:

1. **Athenian military recovery:** In Euripides’s *Suppliant Women*, Theseus defeats Creon’s Thebans and retrieves the bodies after persuasion fails. **[CLASSICAL, Athenian version]**
2. **Voluntary Theban surrender:** Pausanias 1.39.2 records that Thebans denied a battle and said they freely released the corpses. **[REGIONAL VARIANT, reported second century CE]**
3. **Persuasion rather than conquest:** Other Athenian traditions credited Theseus with recovering the bodies by agreement, a version consistent with Plutarch’s later rationalizing discussion. **[VARIANT]**
4. **Aeschylean version:** The lost *Eleusinians* apparently treated the recovery; fragments do not permit certainty over whether force or negotiation predominated.

At Eleusis Pausanias saw collective graves “of those who went against Thebes.” He distinguishes these from the separate tombs of individual figures elsewhere. The visible monuments document classical/Roman-period commemoration, not the interment of demonstrable Bronze Age warriors.

### 3.12 The Epigoni

Ten years later, conventionally, the sons of the Seven consult Delphi and renew the war:

- Alcmaeon, son of Amphiaraus;
- Amphilochus, also son of Amphiaraus, in some lists;
- Aegialeus, son of Adrastus;
- Diomedes, son of Tydeus;
- Sthenelus, son of Capaneus;
- Promachus, son of Parthenopaeus;
- Polydorus, son of Hippomedon;
- Thersander, son of Polynices;
- sometimes Euryalus, son of Mecisteus.

They capture Thebes. Aegialeus dies, and Adrastus dies of grief after hearing of the death of the only son among the Epigoni to be lost. Laodamas and the Thebans either flee or are defeated; Thersander receives the throne.

The successful sons reversing their fathers’ failure are already known to *Iliad* 4 and 14. **[EARLY]** The full roster and details are later systematizations.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### 4.1 Genealogical alternatives

| Figure | Parentage and immediate family |
|---|---|
| Adrastus | Usually son of Talaus and Lysimache/Lysianassa; brother of Eriphyle and sometimes Mecisteus. Husband of Amphithea; father of Argia, Deipyle, Aegialeus, and others. Variants alter his mother and Sicyonian relations. |
| Polynices | Son of Oedipus and either Jocasta/Epicasta or, in some genealogies, Euryganeia. Brother of Eteocles, Antigone, and Ismene. Husband of Argia; father of Thersander. |
| Tydeus | Son of Oeneus of Calydon and Periboea, Gorge, or Althaea in competing sources. Husband of Deipyle; father of Diomedes. |
| Capaneus | Son of Hipponous; wife Evadne, usually daughter of Iphis; father of Sthenelus. Some sources give his father as Astynous. |
| Amphiaraus | Son of Oicles/Oecleus and Hypermestra; in some genealogies descended from the seer Melampus and given Apollo as more remote or divine ancestor. Husband of Eriphyle; father of Alcmaeon, Amphilochus, and in expanded lists other children. |
| Hippomedon | Aeschylus calls him a giant-like warrior but does not give a stable genealogy. Apollodorus makes him son of Aristomachus or of Talaus; other traditions make him son of Mnesimachus and Metidice, Adrastus’s sister. |
| Parthenopaeus | Commonly son of Atalanta by Melanion/Hippomenes or by Ares; alternatively son of Talaus and Lysimache, making him Argive rather than Arcadian. Father of Promachus in Epigoni lists. |
| Eteoclus | Son of Iphis; later genealogies supply a son Medon. |
| Mecisteus | Son of Talaus and brother of Adrastus; father of Euryalus. |

### 4.2 Documented interactions: gods and personified powers

Each line records an interaction, not merely shared genealogy.

- **Apollo — Amphiaraus:** Apollo loves or favors the prophet; *Odyssey* 15.245–247, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. **[EARLY]**
- **Zeus — Amphiaraus:** Zeus opens the earth to save him from death and, in Pindar and Apollodorus, grants continuing or immortal status; Pindar *Nemean* 9.24–27, c. 474 BCE; Apollodorus 3.6.8, first/second century CE. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Athena — Tydeus:** Athena supports him at Thebes; *Iliad* 5.800–813, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. **[EARLY]**
- **Athena — Tydeus:** She approaches with immortality but withdraws it after his cannibalism; cyclic *Thebaid* fragment, probably seventh/sixth century BCE, preserved by later scholia; Apollodorus 3.6.8. **[EARLY, fragmentary/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Zeus — Capaneus:** Zeus kills him with a thunderbolt for his impious boast; Euripides *Phoenician Women* 1172–1186 and *Suppliant Women* 496–505, fifth century BCE; later Apollodorus and Statius. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Ares — Eteoclus:** His shield claims that not even Ares will cast him from the wall; Aeschylus *Seven* 461–469, 467 BCE. This is an emblematic claim, not an enacted meeting. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Typhon — Hippomedon:** Typhon appears as the fire-breathing shield device; Aeschylus *Seven* 486–520. No personal encounter occurs. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Sphinx — Parthenopaeus:** The Sphinx appears on his shield carrying a Theban; Aeschylus *Seven* 529–558. No personal encounter occurs. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Dike — Polynices:** His shield depicts Justice leading him home; Aeschylus *Seven* 642–648. Eteocles rejects the emblem’s claim. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Ares — the expedition:** The warriors invoke martial destruction and their emblems repeatedly claim Ares’ favor; Aeschylus *Seven*. This is ritual and symbolic alignment, not an epiphany. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Dionysus — the Theban side:** Aeschylus’s chorus invokes Thebes’ local gods, including the city’s Dionysiac inheritance, against the attackers; *Seven* 128–180. The Seven do not personally meet Dionysus. **[CLASSICAL/ABSENCE]**
- **Tisiphone — Polynices and Eteocles:** Statius has Oedipus summon the Fury, who inflames the brothers and drives the war; *Thebaid* 1 and 7–11, c. 80–92 CE. **[ROMAN]**
- **Jupiter — Capaneus:** Roman form of Zeus; Jupiter directly annihilates him; Statius *Thebaid* 10. **[ROMAN SYNCHRONISTIC NOMENCLATURE]**
- **Mars, Minerva, Bacchus, Juno, Venus — the armies:** Statius repeatedly introduces divine interventions and partisan alignments absent or less developed in the Greek synopsis; *Thebaid* 3–12. **[ROMAN ELABORATION]**

### 4.3 Documented interactions: heroes and mortals

- **Polynices — Eteocles:** Rival brothers dispute Thebes and kill each other; Aeschylus *Seven*, 467 BCE; Euripides *Phoenician Women*, c. 411–409 BCE. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Polynices — Oedipus:** Son is cursed by his father over disrespect, succession, or appeals for support; cyclic *Thebaid* fragments; Sophocles *Oedipus at Colonus* 1254–1446. **[EARLY/CLASSICAL VARIANTS]**
- **Polynices — Adrastus:** Adrastus shelters him, marries him to Argia, and sponsors his restoration; Homer *Iliad* 4 presupposes the alliance; developed in Apollodorus 3.6.1. **[EARLY/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Polynices — Argia:** Marriage creates his Argive alliance; tragedy and mythography, securely classical and later. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Polynices — Tydeus:** The two exiles fight, reconcile, become brothers-in-law, recruit forces, and campaign together; Apollodorus 3.6.1; Statius *Thebaid* 1–2. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/ROMAN]**
- **Polynices — Eriphyle:** He bribes her with Harmonia’s necklace to compel Amphiaraus; *Odyssey* 11 and 15 presuppose the fatal gift, with fuller later accounts. **[EARLY/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Adrastus — Tydeus:** Adrastus shelters and purifies him, gives him Deipyle, and leads him to Thebes; Apollodorus 3.6.1. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Adrastus — Amphiaraus:** They are former rivals and brothers-in-law; their arbitration compact gives Eriphyle decisive power; mythographic tradition. **[SYSTEMATIZATION preserving earlier material]**
- **Adrastus — Theseus:** Adrastus supplicates Theseus for recovery of the corpses; Euripides *Suppliant Women*, c. 423 BCE; Pausanias 1.39.2. **[CLASSICAL/REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Adrastus — Aegialeus:** Father survives the first war but dies of grief when his son alone among the Epigoni dies; Apollodorus 3.7.3. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tydeus — Eteocles:** Tydeus brings the restoration demand to Thebes; later epic and tragedy. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tydeus — Maion:** Tydeus spares him, the lone survivor of the fifty-man ambush, because of divine signs; *Iliad* 4.391–398. **[EARLY]**
- **Tydeus — Polyphontes:** Tydeus kills or helps kill one of the ambush leaders; *Iliad* 4.395 and later accounts. **[EARLY]**
- **Tydeus — Melanippus:** They mortally wound or kill one another; cyclic *Thebaid* fragment, Aeschylean gate pairing, Apollodorus 3.6.8. **[EARLY/CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tydeus — Amphiaraus:** Amphiaraus restrains or reproaches him at the Ismenus in Aeschylus; later he brings Melanippus’s head to the dying Tydeus out of hatred; Aeschylus *Seven* 377–394; cyclic fragment/Apollodorus. **[CLASSICAL plus EARLY fragment]**
- **Tydeus — Diomedes:** Father and son never interact in a surviving scene; Homeric heroes repeatedly compare Diomedes with his dead father. *Iliad* 4, 5, 10, 14. **[EARLY/ABSENCE of meeting]**
- **Capaneus — Polyphontes:** Gate opponents in Aeschylus; *Seven* 422–451. The play does not state that one kills the other. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Capaneus — Evadne:** Husband and wife; she immolates herself on his pyre; Euripides *Suppliant Women* 980–1071. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Capaneus — Sthenelus:** Father and Epigonus son; genealogical sources. No surviving ancient scene presents them together. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/ABSENCE]**
- **Amphiaraus — Eriphyle:** Wife betrays his opposition to the expedition for Harmonia’s necklace; *Odyssey* 11 and 15, fuller in later accounts. **[EARLY/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Amphiaraus — Alcmaeon:** Father orders his son to avenge him and lead the second expedition; Pindar and mythography. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Amphiaraus — Periclymenus:** The Theban pursues the seer until Zeus opens the earth; Pindar *Nemean* 9; Euripides *Suppliant Women*; Apollodorus 3.6.8. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Hippomedon — Hyperbius:** Gate opponents; Aeschylus *Seven* 486–520. No narrated duel outcome. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Hippomedon — Ismarus:** Later sources make Ismarus his killer; Apollodoran and scholastic variants. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/VARIANT]**
- **Parthenopaeus — Atalanta:** Mother and son in the dominant Arcadian genealogy; Euripides and later sources; Statius dramatizes her distant apprehension of his death. **[CLASSICAL/ROMAN]**
- **Parthenopaeus — Actor:** Gate opponents in Aeschylus *Seven* 526–562. No narrated killing. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Parthenopaeus — Periclymenus:** Periclymenus kills him with a stone in Euripides *Phoenician Women*. **[CLASSICAL VARIANT]**
- **Parthenopaeus — Amphidicus:** Killer in Apollodorus 3.6.8. **[SYSTEMATIZATION VARIANT]**
- **Eteoclus — Megareus:** Gate opponents in Aeschylus *Seven* 457–485. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Mecisteus — the Thebans:** He belongs to the alternative all-Argive roster reported by Apollodorus; no securely surviving early narrative assigns him Aeschylus’s gate role. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/ABSENCE]**
- **The Seven — Hypsipyle:** She guides the thirsty army to water; Euripides *Hypsipyle*, Apollodorus 3.6.4, Statius *Thebaid* 4–6. **[CLASSICAL/SYSTEMATIZATION/ROMAN]**
- **The Seven — Opheltes:** They conduct his funeral and games; same sources. **[CLASSICAL and later]**
- **The Seven — Theseus:** With the exception of Adrastus, the fallen interact only posthumously: Theseus recovers their bodies; Euripides *Suppliant Women*. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **The Seven — Heracles:** No expeditionary interaction is securely narrated. Heracles belongs to overlapping genealogical time and Theban geography, but familiar modern joint-adventure presentations are unsupported. **[ABSENCE]**
- **The Seven — the Argonauts:** Adrastus and several related figures occur in expanded Argonautic catalogues, but no ancient source makes “the Seven” collectively an Argonautic unit. **[ABSENCE]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### 5.1 The graves at Eleusis

Pausanias 1.39.2 reports, on the road from Eleusis toward Megara, graves of those who marched against Thebes. He says Adrastus supplicated Theseus, who defeated the Boeotians and buried the dead in Eleusinian territory; he immediately records the Theban denial that a battle occurred.

**Assessment:**

- The monument or monuments were visible and locally identified in the second century CE. **[CULT/TOPOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**
- Euripides’s *Suppliant Women* already attaches the burial to Eleusis in the fifth century BCE. **[CLASSICAL]**
- No excavated remains have securely established that the named legendary warriors occupied these graves. **[ABSENCE]**
- The competing Athenian and Theban accounts expose political ownership of the myth: Athens advertises panhellenic enforcement of burial law; Thebes rejects military humiliation. **[CLASSICAL POLITICAL USE]**

### 5.2 The Theban tombs

Pausanias 9.18 describes monuments near Thebes associated with combatants, including Tydeus and other Argive dead, while distinguishing Amphiaraus, whom the earth received. Pausanias also records the seven gates at 9.8.4–7 but notes disagreements in their names.

Such monuments are evidence that Roman-period visitors were shown a mapped heroic landscape. They are not independent confirmation of the war.

### 5.3 Adrastus

- Herodotus 5.67 reports that the tyrant Cleisthenes of Sicyon, hostile to Argos, tried to expel the hero Adrastus. When Delphi refused, he transferred tragic choruses formerly performed for Adrastus to Dionysus and introduced Melanippus, Adrastus’s enemy, into the civic cult landscape. **[EARLY CULT/POLITICAL APPROPRIATION, sixth-century event reported in fifth century BCE]**
- Pausanias reports monuments or honors for Adrastus at Sicyon, Megara, and in Attica. **[CULT]**
- The Sicyonian evidence proves a real civic cult and its political manipulation, not Adrastus’s historicity.

### 5.4 Amphiaraus

Amphiaraus has the clearest material cultic record.

- Herodotus 1.46–49 says Croesus tested the oracle and, judging it truthful, made dedications. **[EARLY CULT]**
- The principal excavated sanctuary at Oropos was established or monumentalized in the later fifth century BCE. It developed a temple, altar, sacred spring, incubation facilities, stoa, theater, baths, and stadium.
- Consultants purified themselves, sacrificed, and slept in the sanctuary to receive dreams. Pausanias 1.34.2 describes sacrifice, incubation, and throwing gold and silver coins into the spring after healing.
- Inscriptions document dedications, healing, regulation, interstate administration, and Athenian ephebic visits.
- Strabo 9.2.10 says the cult was transferred from Theban Knopia to Oropos according to an oracle. This is a literary tradition; the location of Knopia remains uncertain.
- The sanctuary’s frontier location made control of it politically valuable to Thebes, Athens, and Oropos. **[CULT/POLITICAL USE]**
- It remained active into late antiquity, declining by approximately the fourth century CE.

Ancient language varies between *hērōs* and *theos* for Amphiaraus. That variation should not be normalized into a rigid modern category: Greek cult could accord divine-style honors to a formerly mortal underground figure.

### 5.5 Capaneus and Evadne

Euripides makes Capaneus’s funeral pyre and Evadne’s leap a public ritual spectacle. Pausanias saw monuments assigned to them. There is, however, no comparably documented widespread Capanean cult with its own sanctuary and priesthood. **[CLASSICAL/CULT/ABSENCE]**

### 5.6 Opheltes and the Nemean Games

The myth treats the games as funerary honors founded by the Seven:

- judges wear dark clothing;
- the crown is wild celery;
- the infant is renamed Archemorus, “beginning of doom.”

These ritual explanations are ancient, especially in Euripidean fragments, Apollodorus, scholia, and Statius. The historical games are archaeologically and epigraphically attested, but the heroes’ foundation is mythic aetiology.

### 5.7 Collective cult

No evidence supports a single panhellenic sanctuary where “the Seven” were universally worshipped as a corporate divine heptad. What exists is a network of:

- collective graves and memorial claims;
- local cults of Adrastus and Amphiaraus;
- individual tombs;
- Nemean aetiology;
- tragic and civic commemoration.

**[ABSENCE]** Literary fame must not be converted automatically into corporate cult.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Archaic period, approximately 750–500 BCE

The *Iliad* presupposes a failed fathers’ campaign and a successful sons’ return. The *Odyssey* knows Eriphyle’s betrayal and Amphiaraus’s fate. Lost *Thebaid* and *Epigoni* poems organized the inherited saga but survive only in fragments. At Sicyon, Adrastus received cult and tragic choruses; Cleisthenes’ intervention demonstrates political reuse by the early sixth century BCE.

The number seven may already have belonged to lost epic, but the surviving archaic evidence does not prove it. **[ABSENCE]**

### Classical period, 499–323 BCE

Aeschylus fixes the best-known seven-at-seven-gates configuration in 467 BCE and makes the siege an exploration of civic defense, boastful signs, divine justice, and inherited curse. Sophocles develops the burial conflict and Oedipus’s curse. Euripides reworks the story toward democratic Athens, panhellenic burial ethics, maternal lament, skeptical debate over courage, and an elaborate duel narrative.

The fifth century also sees:

- the monumental rise of the Amphiareion at Oropos;
- funerary and heroic iconography;
- Athenian claims to have recovered the dead;
- Theban counterclaims.

### Hellenistic period, 323–31 BCE

Much Hellenistic literature is lost. Antimachus’s earlier classical *Thebaid*—a learned epic already known in the fourth century BCE—helped establish the Theban war as a field for extended literary refinement. Alexandrian scholars edited tragedy and classified cyclic epic. Mythographers increasingly reconciled genealogies while preserving variants.

### Roman period, 31 BCE–fourth century CE

Diodorus, Apollodorus, Hyginus, and Pausanias systematized narrative and topography. Ovid made Capaneus a compact example of a mortal whom only Jupiter could defeat. Statius transformed the cycle into a twelve-book epic of civil war, tyranny, pietas, nefas, divine violence, and dynastic repetition under the Flavian principate.

Roman-period viewers continued to visit tombs and Amphiaraus’s oracle. The story’s language of fratricidal civil conflict gave it special resonance in Roman literary culture, but identifying any one character as a coded portrait of Domitian is interpretive rather than documented fact. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY DEBATE]**

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

The Amphiaraus cult disappeared with the contraction and Christianization of traditional cult. Aeschylus’s play survived because it entered the Byzantine selection of seven tragedies. Scholia transmitted lost mythographic and cyclic material. Statius remained a school author in Latin Europe.

There is no securely documented Christian cult that directly transformed any of the Seven into a saint. Apparent “saint-like” parallels are comparative interpretations, not historical syncretism. **[ABSENCE]**

### Medieval western Europe

The twelfth-century *Roman de Thèbes* recast Statius’s war in the idiom of chivalric romance, adding medieval warfare, courtliness, love, and political institutions. **[MEDIEVAL ACCRETION]** It is a reinvention, not evidence for Greek myth.

Statius profoundly influenced Dante, who places the poet in *Purgatorio* and evokes Amphiaraus’s descent in *Inferno* 20. Dante’s Christian Statius is a poetic construction; there is no historical evidence that the Flavian poet secretly converted to Christianity. Boccaccio’s *Teseida* and Chaucer’s *Knight’s Tale* inherit Theban material mainly through Statius and medieval transformations.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Printed editions restored Aeschylus and Statius to humanist scholarship. Painters and illustrators favored the brothers’ duel, Capaneus on the wall, Evadne’s leap, and the burial of the dead. German Romantic admiration elevated Aeschylus’s archaic severity, although *Seven* generally remained less frequently staged than the *Oresteia* or *Antigone*.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Modern productions have repeatedly interpreted the play through contemporary siege warfare, civil conflict, masculinity, propaganda, and the psychology of enemy images. The 1924 Syracuse production used seven towers and shield imagery in a modern Hellenizing spectacle; later American adaptations translated the siege into experiences of modern war and urban violence.

The collective is now also present in novels, online games, visual art, music, and mythology databases. Such works are reception, not newly discovered tradition.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### 7.1 Scholarship and performance

- The 1924 Greek-theater production at Syracuse, with designs by Duilio Cambellotti, made the seven towers and shields central visual motifs. **[MODERN RECEPTION]**
- Jean Anouilh’s and other twentieth-century Antigone traditions generally emphasize the burial aftermath rather than dramatizing the Seven themselves.
- Modern American translations and adaptations by Anthony Hecht and Helen Bacon, David Grene, and others reframe the martial rhetoric for contemporary audiences.
- Recent productions often cast Eteocles as a wartime political leader engaged in counter-propaganda: each enemy emblem is verbally neutralized before battle. This interpretation rests closely on Aeschylus but remains theatrical analysis.

### 7.2 Medieval-to-modern literary afterlife

- *Roman de Thèbes*, approximately 1150–1165: French chivalric transformation of Statius. **[MEDIEVAL ACCRETION]**
- Dante, *Commedia*, early fourteenth century: Amphiaraus in *Inferno* 20; Statius as guide in *Purgatorio*. **[MEDIEVAL RECEPTION]**
- Boccaccio, *Teseida*, c. 1339–1341, and Chaucer, *Knight’s Tale*, late fourteenth century: aftermath traditions mediated principally through Statius. **[MEDIEVAL RECEPTION]**
- Modern historical and fantasy novels frequently collapse the expedition into the Oedipus–Antigone story or use “seven champions” as a quest-party template. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**

### 7.3 Popular and commercial uses

A 2013 browser game titled *Seven against Thebes* offered a modern mini-game treatment. The title and characters also appear in tabletop supplements, independent games, online myth retellings, and visual fan art. These have no evidentiary standing for ancient variants. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

The Mountain Goats’ 2023 album *Jenny from Thebes* visually and textually alludes to Aeschylus; the use is a modern artistic citation, not a retelling of the entire expedition. **[MODERN RECEPTION]**

### 7.4 Political appropriation

Ancient political use is much better documented than modern nationalist appropriation:

- Cleisthenes of Sicyon altered Adrastus’s cult because of anti-Argive policy.
- Athens used Theseus’s recovery of the corpses to represent itself as guardian of panhellenic burial norms.
- Thebes preserved a counternarrative denying defeat by Theseus.
- Control of the Amphiareion conveyed territorial and religious prestige.

Modern Greek performances have participated in cultural projects that claim continuity with classical theater. The 1924 Syracuse production similarly combined modernism with Italian claims upon the classical Mediterranean. That is documented cultural politics, but there is no evidence that the Seven have become a stable emblem of a major contemporary nationalist or extremist movement. Claims that the seven attackers encode a modern racial, fascist, occult, or nationalist “brotherhood” are unsupported unless tied to a specific modern work. **[ABSENCE/MODERN INVENTION]**

### 7.5 Psychological and structural interpretations

- Freudian and post-Freudian readings commonly subordinate the war to the Oedipal family, sibling rivalry, paternal curse, and repetition of inherited violence. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY TRADITION]**
- Otto Rank and Lord Raglan developed general heroic-pattern models, but neither proves that the Seven derive from a historical initiation fraternity.
- Campbell’s monomyth fits the material poorly: this is a collective failed expedition followed by the sons’ successful repetition, not a single hero’s departure-initiation-return. Applications of the monomyth are modern analytical choices. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**
- Structuralist readings emphasize paired oppositions: insider/outsider, city/wilderness, speech/deed, sign/referent, father/son, first expedition/second expedition, failed Seven/successful Epigoni.
- Dumézilian or Indo-European tripartite readings have occasionally distributed seer, warrior, and king functions among the leaders. The unstable roster and lack of ancient recognition make any exact seven-part prehistoric system unprovable.
- Nagy’s work on heroic kleos, death, cult, and epic tradition helps explain why a figure may die in poetry yet remain locally present in cult. It does not establish one original canonical text.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### 8.1 Was there a historical war?

Thebes was a major Mycenaean center. Its palace produced Linear B tablets and was destroyed during the later Bronze Age. Archaeology therefore establishes:

- a powerful Bronze Age Thebes;
- palace administration;
- destructive episodes;
- later Greek memory attached to the site.

It does **not** identify Polynices, Adrastus, or seven Argive commanders, or connect a specific destruction layer with the legendary siege. Converting a destruction horizon into “the war of the Seven” is circular unless an independent inscription names the event, and none does. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION rejected as proof]**

An older scholarly model treated the myth as substantially preserved Mycenaean history. Current, more cautious approaches examine how memories of ruined palace centers, later interstate rivalries, cult sites, and oral poetry accumulated. Historicity remains possible only in the minimal sense that stories of war may preserve distant social memory; the dossier’s named narrative is not historically verified.

### 8.2 Did the seven gates generate the Seven, or vice versa?

Wilamowitz and others proposed that seven gates were invented or regularized to match seven attackers; other scholars have argued that actual topography generated the number. Pausanias already knew disagreement about the gates’ names, and the literary assignments conflict heavily.

Evidence permits three possibilities:

1. a traditional seven-gated Thebes generated seven champions;
2. a traditional group of seven generated seven gates;
3. poets reciprocally regularized unstable local names and rosters.

No excavated circuit securely matches Aeschylus’s full table. The third model is often the most economical but remains reconstruction.

### 8.3 Aeschylus’s ending

The transmitted ending introduces Antigone and Ismene and announces that Polynices must remain unburied. Many scholars judge all or part of this scene non-Aeschylean because:

- its dramatic structure changes abruptly;
- it anticipates Sophocles’ *Antigone*;
- linguistic and staging features appear secondary;
- the preceding lament supplies a plausible close.

Others defend substantial authenticity or posit a fourth-century theatrical adaptation rather than wholesale interpolation. The exact boundary of addition remains disputed.

### 8.4 Aeschylus and contemporary war

Aeschylus wrote within living memory of the Persian invasions. The play’s alarms, walls, civic discipline, and monstrous foreign shield-devices invite comparison with contemporary siege and invasion. Yet equating the Argives straightforwardly with Persians is reductive: both sides are Greek heroic kin, and the final conflict is internal to Oedipus’s household. Scholarship increasingly treats the play as simultaneously civic defense and civil war.

### 8.5 Athenian burial ideology

Euripides’s *Suppliant Women* presents Athens and Theseus as defenders of the universal obligation to bury the dead. Debate concerns whether this primarily reflects:

- Athenian democratic ideology;
- contemporary interstate warfare;
- funeral oratory;
- critique as well as praise of military intervention.

Pausanias’s Theban counterclaim is important evidence that the Athenian version was not uncontested.

### 8.6 Hero cult and epic

Older scholarship sometimes assumed that epic heroes first existed in poetry and later acquired cult, or conversely that every epic hero originated as a local cult dead. Archaeology and regional evidence resist either universal rule. In this cycle:

- Adrastus has early civic cult evidence.
- Amphiaraus has an excavated sanctuary and oracle.
- the Eleusinian graves anchor collective memory.
- several other attackers have literary tombs but weak evidence for organized cult.

The data support multiple routes from story to ritual and ritual to story.

### 8.7 Oral-formulaic composition and the lost epics

The Homeric allusions show that audiences could recognize Tydeus and the Epigoni without exposition. This supports a broad oral-traditional background, not the reconstruction of a single fixed proto-*Thebaid*. The cyclic fragments may derive from one monumental poem, successive recensions, or fluid material later assigned to a title. Martin L. West’s edition is fundamental precisely because it prints fragments and testimonia rather than fabricating a continuous epic.

### 8.8 Iconography

Secure visual subjects include:

- Eteocles and Polynices fighting;
- Capaneus scaling the wall and receiving the thunderbolt;
- Amphiaraus departing in his chariot, often with Eriphyle and the necklace;
- Opheltes’ death and funeral games;
- the Epigoni.

A red-figure cup associated with the Kleophrades Painter, approximately 490–480 BCE, has been connected with the brothers’ duel. South Italian vases frequently illustrate elaborate tragic scenes in the fourth century BCE. Identification depends on inscriptions, distinctive attributes, or close narrative configuration. An armed group of seven is not automatically “the Seven”; LIMC attributions sometimes remain disputed.

### 8.9 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulated

- That Homer gives the canonical seven: false; he does not.
- That Aeschylus’s roster is universal: false.
- That all seven were sons of gods: false.
- That Athena actually immortalized Tydeus: in the cannibalism version she explicitly refuses.
- That Capaneus survives the thunderbolt: unsupported in the dominant ancient record.
- That Evadne’s death documents a standard Greek widow-sacrifice rite: false.
- That the Eleusinian graves authenticate Bronze Age bodies: unproven.
- That archaeologists have identified each of the seven Theban gates exactly: false.
- That the Seven are a Greek version of the Seven Sages, seven planets, or Christian seven archangels: unsupported.
- That Statius’s Christian conversion is historical: a medieval/Dantean invention.
- That “Adrastus was not one of the Seven” or “Adrastus was always one of the Seven”: each is true only for particular roster conventions.

| First century BCE | Diodorus supplies a rationalized mythographic account. |
| c. 8 CE | Ovid alludes to Capaneus and the expedition. |
| c. 80–92 CE | Statius composes the twelve-book *Thebaid*. |
| First–second century CE | Apollodoran *Bibliotheca* and Hyginian compilation systematize rosters, deaths, and Epigoni. |
| c. 150–175 CE | Pausanias records Eleusinian graves, Theban counterclaims, gates, tombs, and the Amphiareion. |
| Fourth century CE | Approximate terminal phase of active traditional cult at Oropos. |
| Ninth century CE onward | Major medieval manuscripts transmit Statius; Byzantine manuscripts preserve Aeschylus. |
| c. 1150–1165 | *Roman de Thèbes* converts the Statian story into French chivalric romance. |
| c. 1308–1321 | Dante uses Amphiaraus and makes Statius a Christian guide in the *Commedia*. |
| Fourteenth century | Boccaccio and Chaucer reshape Statian Thebes. |
| Renaissance–nineteenth century | Printed editions, philology, painting, and Romantic criticism recover Aeschylus and Statius. |
| 1924 | Monumental modern production of *Seven against Thebes* at Syracuse. |
| Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries | War-centered stage adaptations, new translations, games, music, online retellings, and renewed scholarship on cult, gender, civil war, and reception. |

- Aeschylus. *Septem quae supersunt tragoediae*. Especially *Seven against Thebes*, performed 467 BCE. Editions by Denys Page and Martin L. West; translations by Herbert Weir Smyth, David Grene, Anthony Hecht and Helen Bacon, and others.

- West, Martin L., ed. and trans. *Greek Epic Fragments: From the Seventh to the Fifth Centuries BC*. Loeb Classical Library 497. Harvard University Press, 2003.

### Major scholarship and reference works

- Bethe, Erich. *Thebanische Heldenlieder*. Foundational but often overly confident reconstruction of lost epic.
- Burkert, Walter. *Greek Religion*. Evidence-based treatment of hero cult, sacrifice, oracle, and festival.
- Ekroth, Gunnel. Studies of Greek hero-cult ritual and sacrificial practice.
- Farnell, Lewis Richard. *Greek Hero Cults and Ideas of Immortality*. Important older synthesis, to be corrected by later archaeology.
- Fowler, Robert L. *Early Greek Mythography*, 2 vols. Collection and analysis of fragmentary mythographers.
- Gantz, Timothy. *Early Greek Myth: A Guide to Literary and Artistic Sources*. Essential source-by-source comparison.
- Hall, Edith, and others. Studies of Aeschylus in performance and political reception.
- Hardie, Philip; Dominik, William; and Newlands, Carole. Studies of Statius and Roman epic.
- LIMC, *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae*, entries for Adrastos, Amphiaraos, Eteokles, Polyneikes, Kapaneus, Tydeus, Parthenopaios, Hippomedon, and the Epigonoi.
- Nagy, Gregory. *The Best of the Achaeans* and studies of Greek hero cult, poetic immortality, and local/Panhellenic traditions.
- Pache, Corinne Ondine. *Baby and Child Heroes in Ancient Greece*, relevant to Opheltes.
- Parke, H. W. Studies of Greek oracles, including Amphiaraus.
- Sineux, Pierre. Studies of Amphiaraus, Oropos, incubation, and healing.
- Snodgrass, Anthony, and Boardman, John. Studies of early Greek iconography and heroic imagery.
- Sommerstein, Alan H. Edition and commentary on Aeschylus.
- Torrance, Isabelle. *Aeschylus: Seven Against Thebes*. Literary, textual, and reception study.
- Zeitlin, Froma I. Studies of Thebes as tragic space and of signs, gender, and civic identity in Aeschylus.
- *Aeschylus and War: Comparative Perspectives on Seven against Thebes*. Essays on warfare, civic ideology, and reception.
