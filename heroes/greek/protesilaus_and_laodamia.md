# Protesilaus and Laodamia: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Protesilaus is a mortal Thessalian epic warrior and leader, subsequently a heroized dead with tomb, sanctuary, oracle, games, and—at Elaeus—honours that Pausanias describes as divine. Laodamia is a mortal legendary princess and exemplary tragic wife; no independent cult of her is securely documented.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Protesilaus appears in the *Iliad*, especially 2.695–710, whose monumental written form is conventionally dated to the later eighth or seventh century BCE. Homer says that he led forty ships from Thessalian communities, was already dead and buried, had been the first Achaean killed after leaping from his ship, and left behind an unfinished house and a wife tearing her cheeks. Homer does **not** name that wife, mention an oracle, identify Protesilaus’s killer, describe a temporary resurrection, or mention a wax image. Laodamia’s earliest surviving name is later and textually unstable: the cyclic *Cypria* called the wife **Polydora**, while classical tragedy and later mythography developed the name Laodamia.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EE] Securely attested early material:** Archaic or Classical primary evidence, though not necessarily historical fact.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** Hellenistic or Roman literary development that became standard.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** A locally anchored or mutually incompatible tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Mythographic, antiquarian, scholastic, or genealogical ordering of earlier material.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** Identification or assimilation with another figure or religious scheme.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or continuing popular belief.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An inference, not direct ancient testimony.
- **[MI] Modern invention or demonstrably modern embellishment.**

Dates below are dates of composition or evidence, not dates when the mythical events supposedly occurred.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[EE]** In *Iliad* 2.695–710, Protesilaus is the dead former commander of the forty-ship contingent from Phylace, Pyrasus, Iton, Antron, and Pteleos. “Black earth” already holds him; his troops miss him, though his younger brother Podarces has taken command. The poet calls him the first Achaean to leap from a ship and the first killed by a Trojan. The bereaved woman at Phylace remains unnamed. The passage is a compressed memorial rather than a biography: homeland, command, marriage, death, burial, succession, and communal longing are all present, but the landing and death are not narrated as a scene. The text is available through the [Homer Multitext/CHS folio](https://beyond-transl-pr-173.herokuapp.com/reader/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0012.tlg001.perseus-eng3%3A2.695?toc=urn%3Acite%3Ascaife-viewer%3Atoc.iliad-folio-ref-23).

**[LC]** By the Roman period, Protesilaus and Laodamia formed a paired myth of newlywed separation, foreknown death, excessive or exemplary marital fidelity, temporary return from the dead, the artificial image, and suicide. Ovid’s *Heroides* 13 and Hyginus’s *Fabulae* 103–104 preserve the fullest surviving ancient versions ([Ovid](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidHeroides3.html); [Hyginus](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HyginusFabulae3.html)).

### Names and forms

- **[EE] Πρωτεσίλαος — *Prōtesílaos*.** The regular Greek form; Latin **Protesilaus**. Ionic and Attic oblique forms include Πρωτεσίλεως; Pindar has the Doric form Πρωτεσίλας. Homer’s association between the name and πρῶτος, “first,” is explicit in the narrative: he was πρώτιστος, “first of all,” to leap ashore and die.

- **[MSR] “First of the people/host.”** The transparent ancient and lexicographical analysis takes Πρωτεσίλαος as πρῶτος, “first,” plus λαός, “people” or “host.” LSJ nevertheless cautions that the name may be a remodeled or corrupted **Πορθεσίλαος**, with Cretan forms Πορθεσίλας/Πορτεσίλας as comparanda. The “first” interpretation may therefore be an old speaking-name or folk etymology that helped generate or stabilize the landing story, rather than the historical origin of the name. See the [LSJ entry](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n90930/).

- **[LC] Iolaus.** Hyginus, *Fabula* 103, says that the hero’s original name was **Iolaus**, son of Iphiclus and Diomede, and that everyone called him Protesilaus because he was the first to die. This is Roman-period mythographic etymologizing, not early evidence, and must not be confused with Heracles’ companion Iolaus.

- **[LC] Protosilaus / Protosileus.** Latin and later Greek manuscript spellings sometimes render the name with *Proto-* or *-sileus*. These are orthographic or transliteration variants, not independently attested heroes.

- **[LC] Λαοδάμεια — *Laodámeia*; Latin *Laodamia*.** Conventionally analyzed from λαός, “people/host,” and δαμάζω/δάμνημι, “tame, master, subdue”: “she who subdues the people/host,” the feminine counterpart of Laodamas. This is linguistically plausible but does not establish a narrative function.

- **[RV] Πολυδώρη / Πολύδωρα — *Polydōrē/Polydora*.** According to Pausanias 4.2.7, quoting the lost *Cypria*, Protesilaus’s wife was Polydora, daughter of Meleager. The name means approximately “many-gifted” or “bearing many gifts.” This is not merely a nickname for Laodamia in the surviving early evidence; it belongs to a competing genealogy.

### Descriptors and cult titles

- **[EE] μεγάθυμος Πρωτεσίλαος — “great-hearted Protesilaus.”** Homeric heroic descriptor, *Iliad* 2.706.
- **[EE] πολέμαρχος / leader of the Phylacians.** A functional description inferred from his command of the contingent, not a securely transmitted cult epithet.
- **[LC] ἥρως, “hero.”** Explicitly appropriate in Herodotus’s and Philostratus’s cultic settings.
- **[LC] θεός, “god.”** Pausanias 1.34.2 reports that at Elaeus Protesilaus was honoured “as a god.” This records elevated local terminology, not a universal reclassification of him as an Olympian.
- **[LC] “husband of Laodamia.”** In Philostratus’s *Heroicus* 2.11, Protesilaus reportedly likes to be identified this way—an imperial-period reversal of the epic pattern in which the wife is unnamed.

### Identifications and non-identifications

**[LS]** Ancient authors never securely identify Protesilaus with another pan-Hellenic hero. Hyginus’s Iolaus is an alleged former name, not syncretism with Heracles’ nephew. Protesilaus’s resurrection-like return led later Christian and comparative writers to discuss him beside Alcestis, Orpheus, and resurrection traditions, but no ancient cult equation among them is attested.

**[MI]** Claims that Protesilaus was literally a historical Mycenaean prince, a pre-Greek vegetation god, or a dying-and-rising god are modern hypotheses unless tied to a specific ancient witness. No Linear B tablet, contemporary Bronze Age inscription, or excavated burial identifies him.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[EE] Homer, *Iliad* 2.695–710, 13.681, 15.705; Greek; monumental form c. 700 BCE.** Book 2 supplies the foundational record. Book 13 locates the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus near the hardest fighting; Book 15 again remembers the dead hero’s ships and men. These references confirm the durability of his contingent within the poem but add no Laodamia narrative.

**[EE: absence]** Protesilaus and Laodamia have no narrative role in the surviving *Odyssey*. Later works sometimes place Protesilaus among the heroic dead, but the extant Homeric *Nekyia* does not give him a speaking appearance.

### Archaic and Classical corpus

- **[EE/RV] *Cypria*, Greek epic, probably late seventh or sixth century BCE; lost, surviving through Proclus and quotations.** Proclus’s summary says that when the Achaeans disembarked, the Trojans resisted and Hector killed Protesilaus. Pausanias 4.2.7 preserves the variant wife Polydora, daughter of Meleager. The *Cypria* therefore supplied both the first continuous landing episode and a genealogy incompatible with Laodamia daughter of Acastus. The surviving fragments are collected in M. L. West’s *Greek Epic Fragments*; an older public translation is available in the [Epic Cycle fragments](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Homerica/Epic_Cycle/Fragments%2A.html).

- **[EE/RV] Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, Greek, probably sixth century BCE in its surviving form.** Fragmentary material makes Protesilaus and Podarces suitors connected with Phylace; in this genealogy they can appear as cousins rather than the brothers of the *Iliad*. The fragment therefore warns against treating “Homeric genealogy” as universal. See the [Hesiodic fragments](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodCatalogues.html).

- **[EE] Pindar, *Isthmian* 1.58–59, Greek, c. 470s BCE.** Pindar invokes Protesilaus in connection with Phylace. Ancient scholia understood the passage against a local shrine and the **Protesilaia**, games for the hero. The scholia are later witnesses, but Pindar confirms his Classical Thessalian prominence.

- **[EE] Herodotus, *Histories* 7.33 and 9.116–120, Greek, c. 430s BCE.** This is the earliest explicit, securely datable description of Protesilaus’s tomb-sanctuary and accumulated votives at Elaeus. It records Persian appropriation and desecration, an omen attributed to the dead hero, and the execution of Artayctes. The relevant Greek and translation are available at [Herodotus 9.116](https://lexundria.com/hdt/9.116.1/mcly) and [the Greek text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/H/Roman/Texts/Herodotus/9C%2A.html).

- **[EE] Sophocles, *Poimenes* (“The Shepherds”), fifth century BCE; lost tragedy or possibly satyr/pro-satyric drama.** Fragment 497 associates Hector’s killing of Protesilaus with the first battle, followed by Achilles’ killing of Cycnus. Genre and plot reconstruction remain disputed; the surviving evidence is surveyed in *Sophocles: Selected Fragmentary Plays* ([JSTOR record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.38238619)).

- **[EE/LC] Euripides, *Protesilaus*, fifth century BCE; lost tragedy.** Fragments and later testimonia indicate that the play treated Laodamia’s erotic mourning and an image of her husband, probably together with his return. The complete plot cannot be recovered securely. Assertions that Euripides invented either the image or the return are possible but unprovable.

- **[EE] Polygnotus’s *Nekyia* in the Cnidian Lesche at Delphi, c. 460–440 BCE, known through Pausanias 10.30.3.** Protesilaus sat in Hades looking toward Achilles, with Patroclus behind Achilles. This is visual evidence for his integration among the heroic dead, although Pausanias wrote about six centuries after the painting. [Pausanias’s description](https://pausanias.symmachus.org/pausanias.pdf) is the only detailed witness.

### Hellenistic and Roman corpus

- **[LC] Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica*, third century BCE.** Protesilaus is not an Argonaut in the surviving poem; Iphiclus is. Scholia to Apollonius preserve Hesiodic and genealogical material relevant to Protesilaus. Popular lists that make Protesilaus himself an Argonaut conflate generations.

- **[LC] Conon, *Narrations* 13, Greek, Augustan period, surviving through Photius.** In the Scione foundation story Protesilaus survives the Trojan expedition: Trojan captive women led by Aethilla burn the returning Greeks’ ships on Pallene, forcing settlement and the foundation of Scione. This directly contradicts the pan-Hellenic first-death story and belongs to local foundation mythology.

- **[LC] Catullus 68.73–86, Latin, c. 50s BCE.** Catullus compares his own love and bereavement with Laodamia’s marriage, whose house was founded without proper sacrificial blood and whose joy was cut short by Troy. The myth becomes a vehicle for private elegy.

- **[LC] Propertius 1.19.7–10, Latin, c. 20s BCE.** Laodamia’s love for the shade of Protesilaus exemplifies affection surviving death.

- **[LC] Ovid, *Heroides* 13, Latin, c. 20 BCE–10 CE.** Laodamia writes to Protesilaus while the fleet is delayed at Aulis. She fears Hector, asks her husband to fight cautiously, reports ominous dreams, and embraces a wax image already during his absence. Ovid shifts the story before the death and makes dramatic irony its governing device.

- **[LC] Ovid, *Ars amatoria* 3.17–18; *Remedia amoris* 723–724; *Tristia* 1.6.19–20; *Epistulae ex Ponto* 3.1.109–110.** These shorter references establish Laodamia as a proverbial faithful or dangerously image-fixated wife. The *Remedia* explicitly says the wax image was how she perished.

- **[LS] Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*, *Epitome* 3.30, Greek, first or second century CE.** Protesilaus is first ashore and killed by Hector; Achilles then lands and kills Cycnus. The attached ancient and modern notes collect variants naming Aeneas, Achates, or Euphorbus as killer. See [Apollodorus, *Epitome* 3](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DEpitome%3Abook%3DE%3Achapter%3D3) and the [annotated text](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApEb.html).

- **[LS] Hyginus, *Fabulae* 103–104, Latin, probably first or second century CE in transmitted form.** An oracle says the first Greek ashore will die. “Iolaus,” son of Iphiclus and Diomede, leaps down and Hector kills him; he is renamed Protesilaus. Laodamia, daughter of Acastus, obtains three hours with him after Hermes leads him back. *Fabula* 104 gives the image story: after his second death she keeps a wax likeness, her father orders it burned, and she throws herself into the fire.

- **[LC] Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.447, Latin, 20s BCE, with Servius’s late-antique commentary.** Virgil places “Laodamia” among women destroyed by harsh love in the underworld. Servius supplies the explanatory narrative of Protesilaus’s temporary return and her death.

- **[LC] Lucian, *Dialogues of the Dead* 23 and *Assembly of the Gods* 12, Greek, second century CE.** The first treats the bereaved wife’s desire to follow her husband; the latter mocks proliferating heroic oracles, including Protesilaus’s. Lucian shows both the myth’s literary currency and scepticism toward contemporary cult claims.

- **[LC] Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 1.34.2, 4.2.7, 4.36.3, 10.30.3, Greek, c. 150–180 CE.** He records Elaean worship, the *Cypria*’s Polydora, Iphiclus’s Thessalian cattle, and Polygnotus’s underworld Protesilaus.

- **[LC] Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 4.49, 16.238, 34.76, Latin, 70s CE.** Pliny locates the shrine at Elaeus, tells of the elms that repeatedly wither when they grow high enough to “see” Troy, and lists a Protesilaus statue by the sculptor Dinomenes. See [*Natural History* 4](https://www.attalus.org/pliny/hn4a.html) and the [ancient-source index](https://www.attalus.org/names/p/protesilaus.html).

- **[LC] Greek Anthology 7.141, Antiphilus of Byzantium, probably first century BCE/CE.** The epitaph addresses “Thessalian Protesilaus,” connects his first blow and death with the Trojan shore, and invokes the elm-shaded tomb. [Text and translation](https://anthologiagraeca.org/passages/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg7000.tlg001.ag%3A7.141/).

- **[LC] Greek Anthology 7.385, Philip of Thessalonica, first century CE.** A further funerary epigram associates the hero with his first landing and Hellespontine tomb.

- **[LC] Philostratus, *Heroicus*, Greek, early third century CE.** Protesilaus is the dialogue’s presiding dead hero. A vinedresser near Elaeus claims personal encounters with him and transmits his corrections of Homer. The work describes his mound, grove, statue on a prow-shaped base, beauty, changing size, appearances, agricultural benefits, cures, athletic advice, and oracular speech. It is evidence for imperial-period imagination and perhaps real cult practice, not an eyewitness history of Troy. A public translation is available in [*On Heroes*](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Philostratus.pdf).

- **[LC] Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 7.408–423, Greek, probably third century CE.** Neoptolemus’s voyage passes Protesilaus’s shrine and tall elms; branches facing Troy wither. [Text and translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/QuintusSmyrnaeus7.html).

- **[LS] Dares Phrygius, *De excidio Troiae historia*, Latin, probably fifth century CE.** Recasts the war as prose “history”; Protesilaus fights successfully before Hector kills him. The work’s claim to eyewitness authority is pseudonymous and not evidence for Bronze Age events.

### Medieval transmission

**[MA]** Eustathius on *Iliad* 2.701, Tzetzes’s *Chiliades* 2.763–780, scholia on Homer and Aristides, and the Vatican Mythographers preserve or recombine the three-hour return, image, fire, and genealogical variants. Their value is principally as witnesses to lost ancient sources and Byzantine teaching traditions; they do not independently prove an archaic form of the story.

## 3. The story and its variants

### Parentage and homeland

**[EE]** Homer calls Protesilaus son of Iphiclus and makes him leader of the communities around Phylace in Thessaly. Podarces is his younger brother and successor.

**[RV]** The Hesiodic *Catalogue* can make Podarces and Protesilaus members of adjacent branches—apparently cousins rather than brothers. Later mythographers usually harmonize them as sons of Iphiclus.

**[LS]** Hyginus names the mother **Diomede**. Other genealogical compilations name **Astyochia** or relate the family differently. These maternal names are late and mutually unsecured.

**[LC]** Laodamia is normally daughter of **Acastus**, king of Iolcus, and hence granddaughter of Pelias. The *Cypria*, however, called the wife **Polydora**, daughter of **Meleager**; some later genealogies add Meleager’s wife Cleopatra as her mother.

### Suitors and marriage

**[EE/RV]** Hesiodic material places Protesilaus among Helen’s suitors. This would make him bound by Tyndareus’s oath in later systematizations, but no surviving early passage explicitly applies that oath to him.

**[EE]** Homer says he left a wife tearing her cheeks and a house only half-built. The wording suggests a recent marriage but gives no wedding duration, wife’s name, curse, oracle, or reason for the unfinished house.

**[LC]** Catullus 68 says the marriage-house lacked the proper sacrificial blood and therefore began under a religious defect. This is a Roman poetic elaboration, not Homeric ritual history.

### The voyage and oracle

**[LC]** Hyginus first explicitly supplies the oracle that the first Achaean to touch Trojan soil would die. Later retellings make the Greeks hesitate and Protesilaus knowingly leap. Homer says only that he was first to leap and was killed.

**[MI]** The common modern scene in which Odysseus tricks the prophecy by throwing his shield ashore and stepping on it before Protesilaus lands is not part of the securely attested ancient Protesilaus dossier. Unless tied to a demonstrable late source, it should be treated as modern popular elaboration.

### Landing and death

- **[EE] Homer:** first Achaean from the ships; killed by “a Dardanian man,” unnamed.
- **[EE] *Cypria* and Sophoclean tradition:** killed by **Hector**.
- **[LS] Apollodorus and Hyginus:** Hector, now canonical.
- **[RV/LS] Variant killers:** ancient scholarly traditions reported **Aeneas**, **Achates**, or **Euphorbus**. The evidence survives chiefly in scholia and mythographic notes; none displaces Hector in the dominant tradition.
- **[RV] Cycnus:** some late compilations confuse the sequence and name Cycnus, but the better-attested cyclic sequence has Hector kill Protesilaus and Achilles then kill Cycnus.
- **[LC] Dares:** Protesilaus kills several Trojans before Hector kills him, expanding the otherwise instantaneous casualty into a martial aristeia.
- **[MI] Deliberate patriotic self-sacrifice:** Wordsworth makes the leap a philosophically conscious sacrifice for the Greek cause. Ancient oracle traditions permit that interpretation, but Homer does not state it.

### Burial

**[EE]** Homer says Protesilaus lies under the earth but not where.

**[EE/LC]** Herodotus locates his tomb and sacred precinct at Elaeus, on the European side of the Hellespont opposite the Troad. The displacement is narratively meaningful: the first Greek to die in Asia is memorialized across the strait. Whether this cultic tomb was imagined to contain a transported body, a cenotaph, or relics is not explained in Herodotus.

**[MSR]** Attempts to identify an excavated mound as Protesilaus’s actual grave establish ancient and later monumental use, not the identity of its occupant.

### Laodamia’s mourning and the return

**[EE]** Homer’s nameless wife tears her cheeks, a conventional but severe mourning act.

**[LC]** Propertius presupposes love for Protesilaus’s shade. Hyginus gives the canonical miracle: Laodamia asks the gods to speak with her husband for three hours; Hermes conducts him back; when the allotted time ends, she cannot endure the second loss.

**[LC]** Lucian uses the story as a dialogue about love stronger than life. Servius relates that the gods allowed Protesilaus to return because of Laodamia’s devotion.

**[LS]** The identity of the divine authority granting the return varies or is left collective. Modern retellings that assign the decision specifically to Hades, Persephone, Zeus, or “the Fates” often sharpen what the sources leave vague. Hermes is securely the conductor in Hyginus.

### The image

- **[LC] Ovid, *Heroides* 13:** Laodamia possesses a wax image while Protesilaus is still alive but absent. She embraces and speaks to it. Here the image anticipates death.
- **[LC] Ovid, *Remedia* 723–724:** the wax image is directly associated with her destruction.
- **[LC] Hyginus 104:** after Protesilaus’s death, Laodamia makes or keeps a wax image and performs rites or embraces it secretly. A servant sees her, mistakenly reports a lover, and Acastus orders the image burned. Laodamia throws herself into the fire.
- **[EE/LC?] Euripides:** fragments strongly associate the lost *Protesilaus* with a husband-image, perhaps a statue rather than Ovid’s small wax figure. Its material and precise dramatic use cannot safely be reconstructed.
- **[MI] “Three hours and then the wax image”:** Many summaries fuse two partly independent narrative mechanisms into one seamless sequence. Hyginus places them in consecutive fables, but their literary histories need not have begun together.

### Laodamia’s death

- **[LC] Reunion version:** she dies—sometimes in Protesilaus’s arms, sometimes simply from grief—when he returns below.
- **[LC] Image-fire version:** she throws herself onto the burning wax likeness.
- **[LC] Virgilian paradigm:** she belongs among women destroyed by harsh or excessive love.
- **[MI] Moral diagnosis:** “necrophilia,” “psychosis,” or a particular modern psychiatric disorder is not ancient testimony. Such terminology belongs to modern interpretation.

### Scione: a contradictory survival

**[RV]** Conon’s Scione story keeps Protesilaus alive through Troy and makes him founder or leader of settlers stranded when Trojan women burn the ships. This cannot be harmonized with the first-death tradition. It is valuable precisely as a local claim through which Scione attached its foundation to the Trojan cycle.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Protesilaus | Wife | Wife’s descent | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Homeric | Son of Iphiclus; elder brother of Podarces | Unnamed | Unstated | *Iliad* 2.695–710, c. 700 BCE **[EE]** |
| Hesiodic | Phylacid, relationship with Podarces apparently different from Homer | Not securely preserved in the relevant fragment | — | *Catalogue of Women*, sixth century BCE **[EE/RV]** |
| Cyclic | Son of Iphiclus by later agreement | Polydora | Daughter of Meleager | *Cypria* fr. via Pausanias 4.2.7 **[EE/RV]** |
| Tragic/later Greek | Son of Iphiclus | Laodamia | Daughter of Acastus | Euripidean testimonia and later sources **[LC]** |
| Hyginian | Originally Iolaus, son of Iphiclus and Diomede | Laodamia | Daughter of Acastus | *Fabulae* 103–104 **[LS]** |

### Dedicated interaction register

“Interaction” here means an expressly stated kinship, encounter, combat, divine intervention, command relationship, or paired scene—not mere membership in the same mythological world.

- **Iphiclus** — Father of Protesilaus; Homeric patronymic and later genealogy. *Iliad* 2.705; Herodotus 9.116; c. 700 and c. 430 BCE. **[EE]**
- **Diomede** — Mother in Hyginus. *Fabula* 103, Roman imperial mythography. **[LS]**
- **Phylacus** — Paternal grandfather/eponymous ancestor in later genealogical systematization. Scholia and mythography. **[LS]**
- **Podarces** — Younger brother and successor as commander after Protesilaus’s death; in Hesiodic material possibly a cousin. *Iliad* 2.704–710; *Catalogue of Women* fragment. **[EE/RV]**
- **Laodamia** — Wife; mourns him, petitions for his return, receives him temporarily, preserves his image, and dies after or with him. Euripides’ lost *Protesilaus*; Propertius 1.19; Ovid, *Heroides* 13; Hyginus 103–104. **[LC]**
- **Polydora** — Competing wife, daughter of Meleager. *Cypria* fragment quoted by Pausanias 4.2.7. **[EE/RV]**
- **Acastus** — Laodamia’s father; in Hyginus orders the suspected lover-image burned, precipitating her suicide. *Fabula* 104. **[LC/LS]**
- **Meleager** — Father of Polydora in the cyclic genealogy; no direct narrative encounter with Protesilaus survives. *Cypria* fragment. **[EE/RV]**
- **Helen** — Protesilaus appears among her suitors in Hesiodic genealogical material; later systems would bind him to the expedition through the suitors’ oath. *Catalogue of Women* fragments and later mythography. **[EE/LS]**
- **Agamemnon** — Protesilaus serves in the Achaean expedition under the overall high command; Hyginus’s nomenclature calls him one of the Achaeans assembled for Troy. Homer and later mythography. **[EE/LS]**
- **Hector** — Dominant killer: first explicit in the *Cypria*, then Sophocles, Apollodorus, and Hyginus. Late seventh/sixth century BCE onward. **[EE/LC]**
- **Aeneas** — Variant killer preserved in learned commentary. Scholia/mythographic notes to the Trojan landing. **[RV/LS]**
- **Achates** — Variant killer preserved in learned commentary. Scholia/mythographic notes. **[RV/LS]**
- **Euphorbus** — Variant killer preserved in learned commentary. Scholia/mythographic notes. **[RV/LS]**
- **Cycnus** — Protesilaus’s death immediately precedes Achilles’ combat with Cycnus in the cyclic/Sophoclean sequence; direct combat between Protesilaus and Cycnus is not securely attested. *Cypria* summary; Sophocles, *Poimenes*. **[EE; negative finding]**
- **Achilles** — Lands after Protesilaus’s death and kills Cycnus in Apollodorus; Protesilaus looks toward Achilles in Polygnotus’s underworld painting. *Epitome* 3.30; Pausanias 10.30.3 describing a fifth-century BCE painting. **[EE/LC]**
- **Patroclus** — Joint underworld grouping: Patroclus stands behind Achilles while Protesilaus looks toward Achilles. Polygnotus as described by Pausanias 10.30.3. **[EE visual testimony]**
- **Antilochus** — Appears near Protesilaus in Polygnotus’s *Nekyia*, but no narrative interaction is stated. Pausanias 10.30.3. **[EE; joint appearance only]**
- **Hermes/Mercury** — Psychopomp who leads Protesilaus back for the three-hour reunion and presumably returns him below. Hyginus 103; Roman imperial period. **[LC]**
- **The gods collectively** — Grant Laodamia’s request for a temporary reunion. Hyginus 103 and related testimonia. **[LC]**
- **Hades/Pluto** — The realm and ruler from whom Protesilaus returns in later literary and visual treatment; no early personal dialogue between Hades and Protesilaus survives. Roman sarcophagus iconography and later accounts. **[LC; indirect]**
- **Persephone/Proserpina** — Sometimes implied as underworld authority in reception, but no securely surviving early text gives her a discrete exchange with the couple. **[Negative finding]**
- **Zeus/Jupiter** — Wordsworth makes “Great Jove” the granting god; ancient sources generally say “the gods.” The individualized role is modern poetic specification. Wordsworth, “Laodamia,” 1815. **[MI]**
- **Apollo** — Modern accounts frequently call the landing warning “the Delphic oracle.” Hyginus says only that an oracle warned the Achaeans; an explicit encounter with Apollo is not securely attested in the principal ancient account. **[LC/negative finding]**
- **Nymphs** — Later tradition says nymphs planted elms around Protesilaus’s tomb. Pliny-associated and later antiquarian transmission. **[LC]**
- **Xerxes** — Grants Artayctes the hero’s property after being deceived into treating the sanctuary as the “house” of an enemy Greek. Herodotus 9.116. The relationship is political appropriation, not a personal encounter. **[EE historical narrative]**
- **Artayctes** — Plunders and profanes the sanctuary; later recognizes an omen as Protesilaus’s punishment and is executed after the Elaeans demand vengeance. Herodotus 9.116–120. **[EE historical narrative/cult belief]**
- **Xanthippus** — Athenian commander who refuses Artayctes’s ransom and permits or orders punishment in response to Elaean demands. Herodotus 9.120. **[EE historical narrative]**
- **Alexander the Great** — Sacrifices at Protesilaus’s tomb before crossing into Asia so that his own landing will be more fortunate. Arrian, *Anabasis* 1.11.5, second century CE account of 334 BCE. [Arrian translation](https://elfinspell.com/ClassicalTexts/Rooke/Arrian-Alexander/Book1.html). **[LC historical tradition]**
- **The Elaean vinedresser** — Claims repeated personal encounters, revelations, cures, and agricultural assistance from Protesilaus. Philostratus, *Heroicus*, early third century CE. **[LC literary representation of belief]**
- **Palamedes, Odysseus, Nestor, Ajax, and other Trojan heroes** — Protesilaus functions as Philostratus’s posthumous authority about them, correcting Homer. These are reported testimonial relations, not evidence that he met all of them alive. *Heroicus*. **[LC metafictional/hero-cult discourse]**
- **Aethilla** — Trojan captive who persuades women to burn the ships, forcing Protesilaus and companions to settle at Scione. Conon, *Narrations* 13. **[RV]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Elaeus in the Thracian Chersonese

**[EE]** Herodotus 9.116 supplies the strongest early documentary description: at Elaeus there was a **τάφος**, tomb, and a surrounding **τέμενος**, sacred precinct, containing gold and silver bowls, bronze, clothing, and other dedications. The quantity of portable wealth demonstrates sustained votive activity before Xerxes’ invasion; it does not date the sanctuary’s foundation.

**[EE]** Artayctes persuaded Xerxes to give him the property by describing Protesilaus as a Greek who invaded the king’s land and deserved death. He removed the dedications to Sestus, cultivated the sacred precinct, and had sex with women in the sanctuary’s inner chamber. Herodotus presents each act as sacrilege.

**[EE]** During the siege of Sestus, salted fish jumped or quivered on the fire. Artayctes interpreted the sign as Protesilaus showing that, although “dead and dried,” he retained divine power to punish wrongdoing. He offered restitution and ransom; the Elaeans demanded punishment. He was executed near the Hellespont, and the Greeks recovered the sanctuary’s property. The episode closes Herodotus’s Persian-War narrative with a reversal: the Asian ruler’s agent who mocked a Greek invasion is punished by the first Greek invader of Trojan Asia. A scholarly discussion is available in [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/herodotus-x/).

**[LC]** Arrian says Alexander sacrificed at the tomb in 334 BCE before crossing into Asia, hoping for a more fortunate landing than Protesilaus. The act transformed the hero into a ritual precedent for Greek/Macedonian invasion of Asia and answered the Persian desecration remembered by Herodotus.

**[LC]** Pliny still knows a shrine and tower at Elaeus. Pausanias 1.34.2 says Protesilaus was honoured there “as a god.” Philostratus describes a mound, sacred grove, temple or shrine, and statue standing on a prow-shaped base; the hero sends dreams and oracles, heals, advises athletes, protects crops, and converses with a local cultivator.

**[MSR]** Philostratus’s topographical setting corresponds sufficiently with other evidence to preserve real knowledge of Elaeus, but his giant, mobile, fragrant, conversational hero is literary sacred fiction. The distinction is developed in the scholarly study [“Philostratus’ *Heroikos* and its Setting in Reality”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/abs/philostratus-heroikos-and-its-setting-in-reality/3F1EEF336ED0B8678A4560B9D2A369CA).

### The elms

**[LC]** Pliny, *Natural History* 16.238, says that trees around the tomb repeatedly died when their growth allowed them to see Troy, then grew again. Antiphilus, Philip, Quintus Smyrnaeus, and Philostratus elaborate the motif.

**[LC]** The usual developed form distinguishes branches facing Troy, which leaf early and wither, from those facing away, which remain healthy. The trees reproduce Protesilaus’s own abbreviated flourishing: first to emerge, first to die.

**[MSR]** This may encode observed salt-wind damage, pruning, or asymmetrical exposure, but no ancient botanical investigation establishes that explanation. The ancient evidence presents the phenomenon as the hero’s continuing hostility or sorrow.

### Phylace

**[EE]** Pindar’s *Isthmian* 1 anchors Protesilaus in Phylace. Ancient scholia report a shrine and games, the **Protesilaia**, in his honour.

**[MSR]** The verb of communal longing in *Iliad* 2.703 and 709 may resonate with hero cult, as Gregory Nagy argues, but Homer does not explicitly describe sacrifice or a sanctuary. This is a modern interpretation of epic diction, not direct ritual documentation. See [Nagy’s discussion of the hero’s sign](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/curated-article/gregory-nagy-the-sign-of-the-hero-a-prologue-to-the-heroikos-of-philostratus/).

### Scione

**[RV]** Conon’s story makes Protesilaus a founder. Coinage from Scione has sometimes been connected with this local claim, but the identification of individual types and their political message requires numismatic argument rather than assumption. The foundation tradition is incompatible with his burial at Elaeus unless treated as a local rival myth.

### Archaeology

**[MSR]** Schliemann searched for the Elaean tomb. French military and archaeological work in 1915 and 1920–1923 excavated the so-called Tumulus of Protesilaus and the necropolis of ancient Elaeus. The mound’s association with the literary tomb is topographically plausible, but no inscription identifies a burial as the mythical hero. The excavation history is summarized in [“La ville grecque d’Éléonte en Chersonèse de Thrace et sa nécropole”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/crai_0065-0536_1978_num_122_4_13550).

**[MSR]** Archaeology therefore confirms ancient occupation, cemeteries, monuments, and a landscape in which a hero cult could operate; it does not verify Protesilaus as a historical individual.

### Coins and sculpture

- **[EE material evidence]** A bronze coin of Phthiotic Thebes, 302–286 BCE, shows Protesilaus armed and leaping from a ship’s prow. It is a securely dated civic visualization of the landing myth: [British Museum 1933,0214.813](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1933-0214-813).

- **[LC material evidence]** Imperial coinage of Elaeus, including issues under Commodus, depicts an armed Protesilaus on a ship and probably reflects the local cult statue.

- **[LC/MSR]** The Metropolitan Museum’s wounded warrior, Roman Antonine, c. 138–181 CE, copies a Greek bronze of c. 460–450 BCE and is conventionally identified as Protesilaus. The identification is plausible because a related British Museum copy stands on a sloping, wave-marked or prow-like base, but neither surviving statue is inscribed. The museum itself retains a question mark: [Met educational catalogue](https://www.metmuseum.org/ru/-/media/files/learn/for-educators/publications-for-educators/greek.pdf).

- **[LC]** Pliny 34.76 lists a statue of Protesilaus by Dinomenes. No surviving sculpture can securely be identified as that original.

### Funerary art

**[LC material evidence]** Roman sarcophagi appropriate the story for elite commemoration. The major Vatican sarcophagus, Galleria dei Candelabri inv. 2465, probably Antonine rather than first-century despite older catalogue dating, shows Protesilaus’s death, Hermes leading him back, reunion with Laodamia through the marital gesture *dextrarum iunctio*, her mourning, and his return to Hades. Underworld punishments appear on a side. Its unfinished portrait heads allowed the deceased couple to be assimilated to the mythic lovers. See [Zahra Newby’s discussion](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110216783.189/pdf?licenseType=open-access) and the [Fondazione Zeri record](https://catalogo.fondazionezeri.unibo.it/entry/work/107202/Anonimo%20romano%20sec.%20I%2C%20Protesilao%20e%20Laodamia).

**[LC material evidence]** Another sarcophagus at Santa Chiara, Naples, presents a different reunion composition. Claims that the Vatican example is the sole ancient representation are therefore obsolete.

**[EE/LC uncertainty]** An Italiote vase inscribed with the name Laodamia and a Pompeian painting have been associated with the myth, but their interpretation is uncertain. The *Enciclopedia dell’Arte Antica* explicitly cautions against firm identification: [Treccani, “Laodamia”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/laodamia_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Antica%29/).

## 6. Historical evolution of the figures

### c. 700–500 BCE: epic memorial and competing genealogy

**[EE]** The *Iliad* defines Protesilaus through priority, loss, and succession. He is already dead before the poem’s action. The *Cypria* supplies Hector and the landing battle; cyclic and Hesiodic genealogies preserve a wife named Polydora and divergent relations within the Phylacid family.

### Fifth century BCE: cult and tragedy

**[EE]** Herodotus proves that the Elaean tomb-sanctuary was wealthy and politically significant by 480–479 BCE. Pindar anchors the hero in Thessaly. Polygnotus places him among the dead at Delphi. Sophocles stages the first Trojan battle; Euripides shifts attention toward conjugal longing, the image, and perhaps return.

### Hellenistic period: civic image and local foundation

**[LC/RV]** Coins make the leap visually recognizable. Scione’s founder story preserves a living Protesilaus, demonstrating that local civic memory could reject the epic death when a founder was needed.

### Late Republic and Augustan Rome: erotic-elegiac Laodamia

**[LC]** Catullus and Propertius turn her into a language for bereavement and love beyond death. Ovid gives her a sustained female voice before the catastrophe while also making her attachment to the wax image ominous.

### First–third centuries CE: mythographic canon and flourishing cult

**[LC/LS]** Hyginus joins the oracle, renamed hero, three-hour return, image, paternal intervention, and suicide. Apollodorus fixes Hector as the standard killer while scholia preserve rivals. Pliny, Pausanias, Lucian, coins, sarcophagi, and Philostratus show an active field of belief, tourism, scepticism, art, and literary elaboration.

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

**[MA]** Servius, mythographers, Eustathius, and Tzetzes preserve the story for schools and learned readers. There is no secure evidence that Protesilaus’s pagan cult survived Christianization as an identifiable saint cult, or that Laodamia became a folk saint.

### Renaissance to eighteenth century

**[MA]** Printed Ovid, Virgil, Hyginus, and mythographic handbooks made the pair available to European poets, painters, and opera librettists. Their afterlife remained learned and courtly rather than a widespread folk tradition.

### Nineteenth century to present

**[MI/MSR]** Romanticism emphasized the returned shade and the conflict between passionate attachment and spiritual duty. Archaeology sought the physical tomb. Modern novels, handbooks, websites, and games generally reduce Protesilaus to “the first Greek to die” and Laodamia to “the faithful wife with the wax statue,” often merging incompatible versions.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Wordsworth

**[MI]** William Wordsworth’s “Laodamia,” composed in 1814 and published in 1815, is the most influential English adaptation. Laodamia prays for the dead hero; Protesilaus returns under divine permission, says that a Delphic oracle foretold the first Greek ashore would die, and describes himself as a self-devoted victim slain by Hector. He urges philosophical submission; Hermes recalls him; she dies when he departs.

**[MI]** Wordsworth’s moral architecture is not ancient documentary evidence. He makes Protesilaus the spokesman for self-command and condemns or disciplines Laodamia’s “passionate” inability to release him. Revisions softened and then partially restored the punitive ending. The poem combines Homer, Virgil, Ovidian tradition, later mythography, and the elm legend. A contemporary text is reproduced in this [public-domain collection](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/10/Narrative_and_lyric_poems%2C_for_students_%28IA_narrativelyricpo00sewa%29.pdf); its composition and gender politics are discussed in [*Wordsworth and the Cultivation of Women*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/wordsworth-and-the-cultivation-of-women.html).

### Drama and opera

- **[MA]** Eighteenth-century operatic treatments include libretti such as Mattia Verazi’s *Protesilao* (1780). These belong to learned neoclassical reception, not transmitted Greek ritual.
- **[MI]** Stanisław Wyspiański wrote *Protesilas i Laodamia* in February 1899 and published it that year in Kraków. His symbolist tragedy turns the myth into a drama of desire, vision, wedding, and death; it is not a reconstruction of Euripides. The Polish text and publication record are available through [Wikisource](https://pl.wikisource.org/wiki/Protesilas_i_Laodamia) and the [Polish Academy repository](https://rcin.org.pl/dlibra/publication/278314/edition/242132).
- **[MI]** Martinus Nijhoff’s *Een idylle* (1940) is a twentieth-century Dutch dramatic reworking.
- **[MI]** Louis N. Parker and other modern dramatists produced English-language versions; titles alone should not be used as evidence for ancient variants.

### Painting and sculpture

**[MA/MI]** Neoclassical and Romantic illustrations typically show the farewell, supernatural reunion, or Laodamia embracing the image. Modern works often depend more on Ovid and Wordsworth than on Homer. They should be catalogued as reception objects, not evidence for the appearance of an ancient cult statue.

### Modern fiction and popular media

**[MI]** Trojan-War novels commonly give Protesilaus a short landing scene. Modern adaptations may add shield tricks, speeches of voluntary sacrifice, prolonged duels, or a detailed romance before departure. These are narrative inventions unless sourced to an ancient text.

**[MI]** The online use of “Protesilaus” as a screen name, commercial identity, or fictional allusion draws on “first to fall” or sacrificial-first-mover symbolism. It is reception, not survival of cult.

### Political appropriation

**[EE ancient political use]** The clearest political appropriations are ancient:

1. Artayctes recasts Protesilaus as an enemy invader whose property a Persian king may confiscate.
2. Herodotus recasts the hero’s revenge as closure for the Persian Wars.
3. Alexander sacrifices to him before invading Asia, using the Trojan expedition as a precedent for Macedonian conquest.
4. Scione uses him as a founder.

**[MSR/negative finding]** The dedicated search located no well-documented, sustained modern nationalist or extremist cult centered specifically on Protesilaus or Laodamia. General Hellenic, Trojan, Polish-national, or archaeological symbolism should not be attributed to these figures without direct evidence. Wyspiański’s place in Polish national culture does not by itself make *Protesilas i Laodamia* a nationalist appropriation.

### Psychological, feminist, and comparative readings

- **[MSR]** Psychoanalytic readings treat the image as fetish, substitute body, denial of loss, or mediator of mourning. These are modern explanatory models.
- **[MSR]** Feminist criticism notes that Laodamia’s voice is repeatedly authorized only to be disciplined: Ovid lets her speak but fixes her within dramatic irony; Wordsworth gives Protesilaus the moral last word.
- **[MSR]** Ritualist readings connect the first-fallen warrior with hero cult and the tomb as a *sēma*, both grave and sign.
- **[MSR]** Structural comparison with Alcestis, Orpheus and Eurydice, Adonis, or “dying and rising gods” can clarify recurring patterns, but no common origin follows merely from resemblance.
- **[MSR/negative finding]** Protesilaus is not a central example in the canonical systems of Rank, Raglan, Campbell, or Dumézil. Applying the monomyth retrospectively would be a modern analytic exercise, not an attested tradition.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Protesilaus historical?

**[MSR]** No independent evidence identifies a historical Protesilaus. Homeric geography may preserve Bronze Age memories, but neither that possibility nor the archaeology of Troy establishes an individual first casualty.

**[MSR]** The cult tomb does not solve the problem. Greek communities frequently identified old or empty monuments with epic heroes. Conversely, the lack of an inscribed body does not make the cult unreal: Herodotus securely attests ritual property and belief.

### Name before story, or story before name?

**[MSR]** One model takes *Prōtesilaos* as “first of the host” and sees the first-landing death as an etiological expansion of a speaking name. The competing linguistic possibility—an earlier *Porthesilaos* remodeled under the influence of πρῶτος—would make the narrative association secondary. Homer already knows the association, so any remodeling predates the extant text.

### Epic and cult

**[MSR]** Older scholarship often assumed that Homeric poetry created hero cults around epic tombs. Archaeological work shows a more complex landscape: ancestor cult, tomb cult, and named hero cult have different distributions and chronologies. Carla Antonaccio’s review argues that the limited eighth-century archaeological record does not support a simple model of Homer directly generating hero cult everywhere: [“Contesting the Past”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.2307/506436).

**[MSR]** Nagy instead emphasizes reciprocal shaping between epic poetics and cult. The *Iliad*’s repeated “longing” for Protesilaus may evoke the emotional economy of hero worship, but that remains interpretation rather than an explicit liturgical notice.

### Did Euripides invent the return or image?

**[MSR]** The lost *Protesilaus* is earlier than surviving full accounts, making Euripidean innovation possible. Yet fragments are inadequate to establish priority. Ovid and Hyginus may draw on Euripides, other tragedies, Hellenistic mythography, or several channels. Statements that Euripides definitely invented the resurrection or wax image exceed the evidence.

### What exactly returned?

**[MSR]** Ancient vocabulary can describe Protesilaus as returning to life without specifying a permanently reanimated corpse. The three-hour visitation resembles temporary restoration, apparition, or licensed return from Hades. Later theological comparison with bodily resurrection risks imposing categories the pagan texts do not settle. A modern semantic discussion appears in [“Resurrection in Paganism and the Question of an Empty Tomb”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/resurrection-in-paganism-and-the-question-of-an-empty-tomb-in-1-corinthians-15/EF4DE640BE9104A454C7847ECF899313).

### How reliable is Philostratus?

**[MSR]** The *Heroicus* combines recognizable Elaean topography and cult objects with sophisticated fiction. Its Protesilaus is a source who corrects Homer despite dying at the war’s beginning; the paradox is deliberate. James Porter argues that the work repeatedly mediates heroes through statues, imagined visibility, and second-hand testimony: [“Statue Fever in the *Heroicus*”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-classical-journal/article/statue-fever-in-the-heroicus/9E92BB289B751FF5D2D8DF3D11CA156F).

### The “tomb of Protesilaus”

**[MSR]** Excavated tumuli at Elaeus must be separated from literary identification. Archaeology may show that a mound was ancient and ritually used; attribution to Protesilaus can still be a historical act of naming rather than proof of its occupant. Schliemann’s confidence belongs to nineteenth-century heroic archaeology.

### Iconographic uncertainty

**[MSR]** An inscribed vase or coin provides firmer identification than a generic wounded warrior. The Metropolitan and British Museum statues fit the landing type but remain attributed, not proved. Likewise, the Italiote “Laodamia” vase need not depict this Laodamia: several mythical women share the name.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **[MI]** Protesilaus has a securely excavated Bronze Age grave containing his remains.
- **[MI]** Homer names Laodamia and narrates the three-hour return.
- **[MI]** The oracle is securely Delphic and spoken personally by Apollo in the earliest version.
- **[MI]** Odysseus’s shield trick is part of Homer.
- **[MI]** All ancient sources agree Hector killed Protesilaus.
- **[MI]** The wax image and three-hour reunion are a single fully attested archaic story.
- **[MI]** Laodamia had an independently documented ancient cult.
- **[MI]** Protesilaus was an Argonaut.
- **[MI]** Philostratus records objective eyewitness conversations with an immortalized Trojan-War combatant.
- **[MI]** Similarity to Christian resurrection proves borrowing in either direction.

### Assessment

The documented record does not yield a recoverable biography. It yields several historically distinguishable Protesilai and Laodamias:

1. Homer’s already-dead Thessalian leader and unnamed mourning wife.
2. The cyclic Protesilaus killed by Hector and married to Polydora.
3. The tragic couple organized around image, reunion, and unbearable mourning.
4. The Elaean cult hero with tomb, treasure, punitive power, dreams, cures, elms, and political usefulness.
5. The Roman emblem of conjugal fidelity and funerary reunion.
6. The Romantic and modern pair through whom artists debate sacrifice, grief, erotic attachment, and the authority to command the bereaved to let go.

The variants are not corrupt debris around one recoverable original. They are the history of the figures.
