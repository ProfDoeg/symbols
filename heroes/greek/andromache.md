# Andromache: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Trojan/Cilician princess and epic heroine; wife and widow of Hector, enslaved war captive of Neoptolemus, mother of Astyanax and—under post-Homeric traditions—Molossus and other dynastic sons. Pausanias reports a *hērōion* of Andromache at Pergamum, making her locally a heroized dead, but no evidence establishes a widespread cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Iliad*, especially 6.369–502, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE after a long period of oral formation. No Bronze Age tablet, contemporary Trojan inscription, or securely earlier image names Andromache. The name and figure therefore first enter the surviving record through Greek epic poetry.

## Evidence labels

- **[EARLY]** securely attested Archaic material, principally Homer and surviving early epic.
- **[CLASSICAL]** fifth–fourth-century BCE canonical elaboration.
- **[REGIONAL]** locally situated or politically useful variant.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Hellenistic or Roman mythographic reconciliation/genealogy.
- **[ROMAN]** Roman literary reworking.
- **[MEDIEVAL]** medieval transformation or accretion.
- **[EARLY MODERN]** Renaissance–eighteenth-century reinvention.
- **[MODERN RECEPTION]** nineteenth century onward.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** modern scholarly inference, not primary testimony.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** claim or story presented as ancient without adequate ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Andromache is already fully formed in the *Iliad* as Ἀνδρομάχη, daughter of Eëtion, ruler of Thebe beneath Mount Placus; wife of Hector; mother of Scamandrius/Astyanax; and survivor of Achilles’ destruction of her natal family. Her defining Homeric action is not combat but speech: she identifies the tactical weakness in Troy’s wall, begs Hector not to return to battle, and imagines her own enslavement. Books 22 and 24 then make her the principal wife-lamenter over Hector. Post-Homeric epic turns Hector’s predicted death and her predicted slavery into accomplished events. Classical tragedy expands the captive’s history: Neoptolemus takes her as a sexual slave or concubine, fathers Molossus, and exposes mother and child to Hermione’s attempted murder. Later genealogical traditions make her the ancestress of Molossian and Pergamene rulers. Virgil makes her queen beside Helenus in an expatriate “little Troy” at Buthrotum.

### Name

- Greek: **Ἀνδρομάχη**, *Andromáchē*.
- Latin: **Andromache**; poetic inflections include *Andromacha*.
- French: **Andromaque**.
- Italian/Spanish/Portuguese: **Andromaca/Andrómaca**.
- Modern Greek: **Ανδρομάχη**, *Andromáchi*.

The transparent Greek components are ἀνδρ-/ἀνήρ, “man, husband, warrior,” and μάχη, “battle.” Possible semantic renderings include “man-battle,” “she who fights men,” “battle of men,” or “she who fights for/over a husband.” Ancient poetry does not furnish an authoritative etymology. “Man-fighter” is lexically possible but narratively paradoxical: Homer does not make her an Amazon. Interpretations such as “fighter for her husband” are contextual glosses, not established historical etymologies. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

No Linear B form has been securely identified as her name. Claims that the mythical Andromache herself appears in Mycenaean records are unsupported. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

### Descriptive formulas and statuses

- **Ἀνδρομάχη, θυγάτηρ Ἠετίωνος**, daughter of Eëtion: *Iliad* 6.395–398. **[EARLY]**
- **ἄλοχος Ἕκτορος / Ἑκτορέη ἄλοχος**, Hector’s wife: Homer and the *Little Iliad* fragment. **[EARLY]**
- **λευκώλενος**, “white-armed”: Homeric feminine formula applied to her in *Iliad* 6.371. **[EARLY]**
- **πολύδωρος**, “richly dowered/bounteous,” in Homeric description: evidence of elite marriage, not an independent cult epithet. **[EARLY]**
- **δορίκτητος**, effectively “won by the spear,” is the social condition emphasized by Euripides even when the exact formula varies: captive slave and concubine. **[CLASSICAL]**
- “Queen” at Buthrotum is a Virgilian narrative status, not a separately attested Greek title. **[ROMAN]**

There is no securely attested divine epithet, priesthood, mystery title, or identification of Andromache with a goddess. The occasional confusion of her with an Amazon named Andromache belongs to later name-listing and should not merge two separate mythographic figures. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/MODERN CONFUSION]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

The oldest secure evidence is *Iliad* 6.369–502. Its poetry is conventionally placed around 700 BCE, with dates ranging across the later eighth and early seventh centuries. The surviving text is much later: fragments occur in Hellenistic and Roman papyri; the earliest complete *Iliad* manuscript is the tenth-century CE Venetus A. That transmission gap does not make Andromache a late Byzantine creation, because she is independently present in Archaic cyclic epic, fifth-century drama, art, and Classical quotation.

What Homer establishes:

- Her father is Eëtion, lord of Cilician Thebe under Placus.
- Achilles killed Eëtion and her seven brothers.
- Artemis killed her ransomed mother in Priam’s house.
- She married Hector with a rich dowry.
- Their infant is called Scamandrius by Hector and Astyanax by the Trojans.
- She understands the military vulnerability near Troy’s fig tree.
- Hector foresees her enslavement, specifically imagining her weaving and carrying water for an Argive mistress.
- She foretells or anticipates Hector’s death and, in Book 24, leads a formal lament over his corpse.

It does **not** say that she married Helenus, bore Molossus, went to Epirus, or received cult. Astyanax’s murder is foreshadowed in her lament at *Iliad* 24.734–738 but not narrated.

### Ancient corpus

1. **Homer, *Iliad***, Homeric Greek, ca. 700 BCE. Books 6, 22 and 24 contain the meeting at the Scaean Gate, her discovery of Hector’s death, and her funeral lament. Passing references elsewhere establish Hector’s household. **[EARLY]**

2. **Homer, *Odyssey***, ca. late eighth–seventh century BCE. Andromache does not participate. Odyssey 4 knows Hermione’s marriage to Neoptolemus but does not mention the rival captive or Molossus. This silence is important: it cannot establish that the postwar Andromache tradition was either known or unknown to the poet. **[EARLY/ABSENCE]**

3. **The *Little Iliad*, attributed to Lesches**, probably seventh or sixth century BCE, surviving only through fragments and later testimonia. A quoted passage says Achilles’ son dragged Hector’s wife to the ships, seized the child from his nurse, threw him from the tower, and received Andromache as his prize. Pausanias’ account of Polygnotus and a Homeric scholion preserve associated testimony. **[EARLY]**

4. **The *Iliou Persis* or *Sack of Ilion*, attributed to Arctinus**, probably seventh century BCE, surviving in Proclus’ summary and fragments. Here **Odysseus**, not Neoptolemus, kills Astyanax, while Neoptolemus receives Andromache. **[EARLY VARIANT]**

5. **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women***, genealogical poem assembled in the Archaic period, fragmentary and transmitted through papyri and quotations. The securely assignable surviving Andromache material is slight; later references to “Hesiod” must not be used to fill her biography without fragment numbers and transmitting witnesses. **[EARLY/FRAGMENTARY]**

6. **Pindar, Paean 6 and Nemean 7**, early fifth century BCE. These treat Neoptolemus’ route and death at Delphi and support the Epirote/Delphic matrix in which his union with Andromache and Molossian genealogy developed, although Pindar’s main subject is Neoptolemus rather than a continuous Andromache narrative. **[CLASSICAL/REGIONAL]**

7. **Euripides, *Andromache***, generally dated ca. 425–417 BCE. The play is set at Phthia/Thetidean territory. Andromache is Neoptolemus’ enslaved concubine and mother of Molossus. Hermione, barren and jealous, accuses her of drugs and plots to kill her and the child with Menelaus. Peleus rescues them; Orestes removes Hermione and engineers Neoptolemus’ death at Delphi; Thetis predicts Andromache’s marriage to Helenus and Molossus’ dynastic future. The ancient hypothesis’s claim of a performance outside Athens is uncertain. **[CLASSICAL]**

8. **Euripides, *Trojan Women***, performed 415 BCE. Andromache is allocated to Neoptolemus and learns from Talthybius that the Greek council, persuaded by Odysseus, will kill Astyanax lest he avenge Troy. His body returns on Hector’s shield for burial. **[CLASSICAL VARIANT]**

9. **Lost tragedies and fragmentary dramas.** Sophocles wrote a lost *Hermione*; other Trojan plays may have treated Andromache, but fragmentary titles and vase conjectures cannot be converted into full plots. **[CLASSICAL/LOST]**

10. **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica***, third century BCE. Andromache is not a participant in the Argonautic action; references to the earlier generation or to Peleus do not document interactions with her. **[HELLENISTIC/ABSENCE]**

11. **Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca/Epitome***, first or second century CE in its present form. The *Epitome* systematizes her allocation to Neoptolemus, Astyanax’s death, the journey with Neoptolemus and Helenus, and Molossus’ birth. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**

12. **Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.294–505**, composed 29–19 BCE. Aeneas finds Andromache and Helenus ruling at Buthrotum. She performs offerings at an empty mound for Hector beside a counterfeit Simois. She recounts enslavement to Pyrrhus, being handed to Helenus when Pyrrhus married Hermione, and Pyrrhus’ death at Orestes’ hands. She gives Ascanius garments as gifts to the “sole surviving image” of Astyanax. **[ROMAN]**

13. **Ovid**, late first century BCE–early first century CE. *Ars Amatoria* 3.777–778 invokes Andromache as a tall, dignified Homeric matron unsuitable as a model for erotic delicacy. The *Metamorphoses* presupposes the Trojan aftermath but does not supply a continuous Andromache biography. **[ROMAN]**

14. **Seneca, *Troades***, first century CE. Andromache hides Astyanax in Hector’s tomb after Hector appears in a dream warning her. Ulysses discovers the deception; in Seneca’s distinctive version Astyanax ultimately leaps from the tower himself, though under Greek compulsion. **[ROMAN VARIANT]**

15. **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 1.11 and 10.25–27**, ca. 150–180 CE. He records three sons by Neoptolemus—Molossus, Pielus and Pergamus—a son Cestrinus by Helenus, migration with Pergamus to Teuthrania, and an extant *hērōion* at Pergamum. His description of Polygnotus’ fifth-century BCE Delphic mural preserves otherwise lost details from Lesches. **[SYSTEMATIZATION/REGIONAL TESTIMONY]**

16. **Hyginus, *Fabulae***, surviving in a defective Latin manuscript tradition, probably incorporating Imperial material. It supplies compressed genealogical and Trojan-war catalogues; individual readings require caution because the text is corrupt and frequently harmonizing. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**

17. **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica***, probably third century CE. The poem supplies a late Greek epic bridge between Homer and the sack, dramatizing Andromache’s grief, Astyanax’s fate, and the captive women. **[LATE ANTIQUE EPIC]**

18. **Dictys Cretensis and Dares Phrygius**, Latin recensions ca. fourth–sixth centuries CE claiming eyewitness authority. Their pseudo-documentary Trojan histories profoundly influenced medieval Europe but are not Bronze Age chronicles. **[LATE ANTIQUE FICTION]**

19. **Byzantine scholia, Eustathius and Tzetzes**, principally tenth–twelfth centuries, preserve ancient variants and learned harmonizations. Their statements must be dated to the source they quote when identifiable, otherwise to their own Byzantine context. **[MEDIEVAL LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**

20. **Benoît de Sainte-Maure, *Roman de Troie***, ca. 1160; Guido delle Colonne, *Historia destructionis Troiae*, 1287; and descendant Trojan chronicles. These inherit the Dictys–Dares tradition and refashion the Trojan women according to courtly and Christian moral frameworks. **[MEDIEVAL]**

## 3. The story: cycles and variants

### Cilician birth and destroyed natal family

In the earliest account Andromache is Eëtion’s daughter, from Thebe under wooded Placus, a Cilician community in the Troadic myth-geography. Homer does not name her mother or seven brothers. Achilles kills Eëtion but respectfully burns him in his armor and raises a mound; mountain nymphs plant elms. He kills all seven brothers among their livestock. He ransoms the captured mother, after which Artemis kills her in Priam’s house (*Iliad* 6.414–428). **[EARLY]**

No surviving early source narrates Andromache’s birth, childhood, or an independent premarital adventure. Modern novels that supply these are inventions or acknowledged historical fiction.

### Marriage to Hector and the gate scene

At *Iliad* 6.369–502 Hector finds Andromache, Astyanax and an unnamed nurse at the Scaean Gate. Andromache asks him to defend the vulnerable fig-tree section of the wall rather than seek open combat. She identifies him as her remaining father, mother, brother and husband. Hector refuses because shame before Trojans and Trojan women requires him to fight. He predicts Troy’s fall and says her enslavement pains him more than the deaths of Priam or his brothers.

Astyanax recoils from Hector’s horsehair crest; Hector removes the helmet, and both parents laugh. He prays that the child will become a greater warrior than himself. The scene joins tenderness to catastrophe: the audience knows that Hector’s heroic code cannot preserve the household it is supposed to defend. **[EARLY]**

Later art frequently turns this into “Hector’s departure,” although Homer stages it during the fighting, not necessarily as their final chronological farewell. **[RECEPTIONAL CONVENTION]**

### Hector’s death and lament

In *Iliad* 22 Andromache is weaving and preparing a bath when she hears lamentation, runs to the wall, and sees Achilles dragging Hector’s corpse. She collapses, casts off the marriage veil given by Aphrodite, and laments Astyanax’s future. In Book 24 she leads the first of three formal laments over the restored body, followed by Hecuba and Helen. She praises Hector as husband and protector and anticipates that Astyanax may be thrown from Troy’s wall. **[EARLY]**

The prediction probably provided a generative cue for later sack poetry; the Homeric scholion explicitly says post-Homeric poets developed the child’s fall from *Iliad* 24.735. That is ancient literary criticism, not proof that Homer invented the episode. **[ANCIENT SCHOLARLY CLAIM]**

### Sack, Astyanax and enslavement

The sources disagree:

- *Little Iliad*: Neoptolemus personally throws Astyanax from the wall and receives Andromache. Pausanias says Lesches made it Neoptolemus’ private act, not a collective Greek decree. **[EARLY VARIANT]**
- *Iliou Persis*: Odysseus kills Astyanax; Neoptolemus receives Andromache. **[EARLY VARIANT]**
- Euripides’ *Trojan Women*: Odysseus persuades the Greek council to decree the death; Talthybius takes the child away. **[CLASSICAL POLITICAL VARIANT]**
- Seneca: Calchas requires the death for the fleet’s departure; Astyanax leaps from the tower. **[ROMAN VARIANT]**

These cannot be harmonized into one event. They successively assign responsibility to Achilles’ brutal son, Odyssean policy, the Greek collective, religious necessity and the victim’s final agency.

Andromache’s assignment to Neoptolemus is common to the cyclic summaries and tragedy. The relationship is enslavement and coerced sexual possession, irrespective of older translations that call her “bride” or “wife.” Euripides explicitly makes her booty and a slave in Neoptolemus’ bed.

### Molossus, Hermione and attempted murder

Euripides’ *Andromache* provides the fullest Classical version. Andromache has borne **Molossus** to Neoptolemus. Hermione, Neoptolemus’ legitimate Spartan wife, remains childless and accuses Andromache of using drugs to make her barren and alienate her husband. With Menelaus she threatens the captive and her hidden son.

Andromache takes sanctuary at Thetis’ altar. Menelaus uses Molossus as leverage, promising that surrender might save him; after she leaves sanctuary, he admits the promise does not bind him to spare both. Peleus intervenes and humiliates Menelaus, saving mother and child. Hermione then fears punishment. Orestes, to whom she had previously been betrothed, carries her away and reveals his plot against Neoptolemus at Delphi. A messenger reports Neoptolemus’ death.

Thetis’ epilogue orders:

- Neoptolemus’ burial at Delphi;
- Peleus’ immortalization;
- Andromache’s marriage to Helenus in Molossia;
- Molossus’ dynastic continuation.

The play’s dating and immediate politics are disputed. Anti-Spartan language is unmistakable, but interpretations that reduce the play to a single piece of Athenian wartime propaganda exceed the evidence. **[CLASSICAL + DISPUTED INTERPRETATION]**

### Sons and dynastic variants

- **Astyanax/Scamandrius**, by Hector: Homer. **[EARLY]**
- **Molossus**, by Neoptolemus: Euripides and later sources; eponymous ancestor of the Molossians. **[CLASSICAL/REGIONAL GENEALOGY]**
- **Pielus** and **Pergamus**, by Neoptolemus: Pausanias 1.11.1. **[ROMAN-PERIOD SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Cestrinus**, by Helenus: Pausanias 1.11.1–2. **[REGIONAL SYSTEMATIZATION]**

Some later lists offer Pyrrhus, Aeacides or alternative sons. These are genealogical variants, duplications or dynastic remodeling, not evidence for additional historical children.

### Helenus, Epirus and Buthrotum

Euripides’ divine epilogue makes Helenus Andromache’s future husband in Molossia. Pausanias says she lived with Helenus after Neoptolemus’ death and bore Cestrinus. Virgil relocates their literary center to Buthrotum in Chaonia.

In *Aeneid* 3 Andromache performs rites at Hector’s empty mound beside a *falsus Simois*, “counterfeit Simois.” The settlement reproduces Troy’s names and architecture. It is not archaeological proof that historical Trojan refugees founded Buthrotum; it is a Roman literary landscape of exile, memory and failed repetition. Aeneas must leave this static replica to found a future people. **[ROMAN]**

### Pergamum and death

Pausanias says Pergamus went to Teuthrania, killed its ruler Areius in single combat, renamed the city after himself, and was accompanied by Andromache. A *hērōion* of Andromache still stood there in Pausanias’ day. This is the only clear ancient report of her final place and heroized monument. No surviving earlier narrative specifies her death, age, burial circumstances, or apotheosis. **[REGIONAL]**

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical outline

```text
Eëtion + unnamed mother
└── Andromache
    ├── with Hector
    │   └── Scamandrius/Astyanax
    ├── with Neoptolemus/Pyrrhus
    │   ├── Molossus
    │   ├── Pielus         [Pausanias]
    │   └── Pergamus       [Pausanias]
    └── with Helenus
        └── Cestrinus      [Pausanias]
```

Neoptolemus’ marriage to Hermione overlaps with his possession of Andromache; ancient sources present a hierarchy between legitimate Greek wife and enslaved Trojan concubine, not an equal romantic triangle.

### Documented interactions and relationships

- **Eëtion** — father; his death and burial by Achilles are recounted by Andromache. *Iliad* 6.395–428, ca. 700 BCE. **[EARLY]**
- **Unnamed mother of Andromache** — captured and ransomed by Achilles, then killed by Artemis; known only through Andromache’s speech. *Iliad* 6.425–428. **[EARLY]**
- **Seven unnamed brothers** — Achilles kills them; Andromache reports their deaths. *Iliad* 6.421–424. **[EARLY]**
- **Hector** — husband; gate conversation, shared laughter over Astyanax, predicted separation and funeral lament. *Iliad* 6, 22, 24. **[EARLY]**
- **Astyanax/Scamandrius** — son; carried at the gate, mourned and later taken to execution. Homer; cyclic epic; Euripides; Seneca. **[EARLY onward]**
- **Unnamed nurse** — carries Astyanax at the gate and, in the *Little Iliad* fragment, loses him to Neoptolemus. *Iliad* 6; *Little Iliad* fr. 29/30 West. **[EARLY]**
- **Achilles** — destroys Andromache’s natal family, kills Hector, and makes her threatened enslavement possible; they have no surviving face-to-face encounter. *Iliad* 6, 22, 24. **[EARLY]**
- **Priam** — father-in-law and royal household head; Andromache mourns within his family. Homer. No direct extended dialogue survives. **[EARLY]**
- **Hecuba** — mother-in-law and fellow mourner/captive; they lament Hector and converse in *Trojan Women*. Homer; Euripides, 415 BCE. **[EARLY/CLASSICAL]**
- **Helen** — fellow Trojan royal woman; both deliver funeral laments over Hector, but Homer gives no direct dialogue between them. *Iliad* 24. **[EARLY]**
- **Paris/Alexander** — Hector’s brother; kin by marriage, but no securely attested direct encounter with Andromache. **[ABSENCE]**
- **Aphrodite** — gave the marriage veil that falls from Andromache’s head when she sees Hector’s corpse. *Iliad* 22.468–472. **[EARLY GIFT/INDIRECT]**
- **Artemis** — kills Andromache’s mother; no direct meeting with Andromache. *Iliad* 6.427–428. **[EARLY/INDIRECT]**
- **Zeus’ mountain nymphs** — plant elms around Eëtion’s burial mound; Andromache reports it. *Iliad* 6.419–420. **[EARLY/INDIRECT]**
- **Athena** — Trojan women visit her temple, but Andromache goes instead to the wall; no direct divine response or meeting occurs. *Iliad* 6. **[EARLY/NEGATIVE INTERACTION]**
- **Neoptolemus/Pyrrhus** — captor, enslaver and father of Molossus; receives Andromache as booty. *Little Iliad*, *Iliou Persis*, Euripides and later texts. **[EARLY onward]**
- **Molossus** — son by Neoptolemus; threatened with execution and rescued by Peleus. Euripides’ *Andromache*. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Pielus** — son by Neoptolemus in Pausanias. No narrative interaction survives. Pausanias 1.11.1, second century CE. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Pergamus** — son by Neoptolemus; Andromache accompanies him to Asia. Pausanias 1.11.2. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Helenus** — Hector’s brother; becomes Andromache’s husband and ruler with her in Epirus/Buthrotum. Euripides; Virgil; Pausanias. **[CLASSICAL/ROMAN]**
- **Cestrinus** — son by Helenus in Pausanias; no direct episode survives. **[SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Hermione** — legitimate wife of Neoptolemus and hostile rival; accuses Andromache of sorcery and plots her death. Euripides’ *Andromache*. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Menelaus** — assists Hermione, deceives Andromache with an equivocal promise, and attempts to execute her and Molossus. Euripides’ *Andromache*. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Peleus** — rescues Andromache and Molossus from Menelaus. Euripides’ *Andromache*. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Thetis** — her altar gives Andromache sanctuary; in the epilogue she guarantees Andromache’s future with Helenus and Molossus’ dynasty. Euripides’ *Andromache*. **[CLASSICAL DIVINE PATRONAGE]**
- **Orestes** — removes Hermione and arranges Neoptolemus’ death; his action indirectly determines Andromache’s future. Euripides’ *Andromache*; Virgil *Aeneid* 3. **[CLASSICAL/ROMAN]**
- **Apollo** — Neoptolemus dies at Apollo’s Delphic sanctuary; no direct meeting with Andromache. Pindar, Euripides and later variants differ over responsibility. **[CLASSICAL/INDIRECT]**
- **Odysseus/Ulysses** — in the *Iliou Persis* kills Astyanax; in Euripides persuades the council; in Seneca exposes Andromache’s concealment of the child. **[EARLY/CLASSICAL/ROMAN VARIANTS]**
- **Talthybius** — announces Astyanax’s sentence and removes him in *Trojan Women*. Euripides, 415 BCE. **[CLASSICAL]**
- **Calchas** — in Seneca declares Astyanax’s death necessary for Greek departure; no such encounter belongs to Homer. **[ROMAN VARIANT]**
- **Aeneas** — visits Andromache at Buthrotum, exchanges memories and gifts, and hears her story. Virgil *Aeneid* 3. **[ROMAN]**
- **Ascanius/Iulus** — receives Andromache’s woven gifts because he recalls Astyanax to her. Virgil *Aeneid* 3. **[ROMAN]**
- **Anchises** — present with Aeneas’ expedition at Buthrotum, but Virgil gives no substantial private exchange with Andromache. **[ROMAN/INCIDENTAL]**
- **Areius** — killed by Pergamus; Andromache accompanies the expedition but does not herself interact with him in Pausanias. **[REGIONAL/INDIRECT]**
- **Dionysus, Heracles, Jason and the Argonauts** — no securely documented personal interaction. References to their generations or to Peleus do not create an Andromache episode. **[ABSENCE]**

## 5. Cult, tomb and ritual

### Pergamene *hērōion*

Pausanias 1.11.2 states: after Pergamus conquered Teuthrania and Andromache accompanied him, “even now there is a *hērōion* of Andromache in the city.” This is explicit second-century CE literary testimony to a hero shrine or heroic monument at Pergamum. It establishes that Pausanias or his source identified a local monument as hers. It does not by itself tell us:

- when it was founded;
- whether it contained a burial;
- what archaeology belonged to it;
- whether sacrifices, libations, priests or annual games occurred there;
- whether its identification predated Attalid dynastic mythmaking.

No securely published inscription naming Andromache as recipient of sacrifice has been identified here. Accordingly, “Andromache had a hero cult at Pergamum” is plausible but should be stated narrowly: Pausanias saw or reported a *hērōion*; its ritual program is unknown. **[REGIONAL/LITERARY CULT EVIDENCE]**

### Hector’s empty tomb at Buthrotum

Virgil’s green mound and paired altars are a cenotaph or ritual replica created by Andromache for Hector. This is fictional Roman epic testimony, not archaeological documentation of an Andromache cult. The rites—offerings, invocation and anniversary mourning—characterize her as the custodian of Hector’s memory. **[ROMAN LITERARY RITUAL]**

### Delphi and Epirus

Neoptolemus possessed an important Delphic tomb and heroic tradition, and the Molossian royal house claimed Aeacid descent. Andromache’s position as Molossus’ mother connects her genealogically to those traditions, but surviving evidence does not show her independently worshipped at Delphi or at the Epirote royal centers. **[ABSENCE]**

Claims that a modern grave, ruin or church in Epirus or the Troad is “Andromache’s tomb” require inscriptions or ancient testimony. None has displaced Pausanias’ Pergamene report. **[CONTESTED/MODERN LOCALIZATION]**

## 6. Historical evolution

### ca. 700 BCE: Homeric household and lament

The earliest Andromache is a Cilician-born Trojan wife defined through elite marriage, military intelligence, bereavement and the expected fate of conquered women. Homer gives her neither a postwar rescue nor a heroic death.

### Seventh–sixth centuries BCE: sack traditions

Cyclic epic supplies the murder of Astyanax and Andromache’s allocation to Neoptolemus. Competing poems assign the child’s murder to Neoptolemus or Odysseus. Early art develops related sack imagery.

### Fifth century BCE: tragedy and regional genealogy

Euripides relocates her from ruined Troy to Greece, makes the sexual coercion and legal vulnerability of enslavement central, and attaches her son Molossus to an Epirote future. The dynastic ending can speak simultaneously to mythic genealogy and contemporary Greek politics.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Mythographers enlarge the family tree. Pausanias connects it to Epirus and Pergamum. Virgil makes Andromache the emblem of a survivor trapped in ritualized recollection, a foil to Aeneas’ future-directed migration. Seneca intensifies concealment, psychological terror and the spectacle of Astyanax’s death.

### Late antiquity and Middle Ages

Dictys and Dares replace openly poetic authority with fictitious eyewitness history. Their Latin availability, when direct knowledge of Greek epic was limited in western Europe, makes their Trojan chronology foundational for Benoît, Guido and vernacular Troy books. Andromache becomes increasingly exemplary: loyal wife, chaste widow, noble captive.

### Early modern Europe

Humanist recovery of Greek tragedy and sustained use of Virgil and Seneca restore the postwar conflict. Robert Garnier’s *La Troade* (1581) mediates Senecan material. Racine’s *Andromaque* (1667) transforms the tradition into an enclosed chain of unreciprocated desire and dynastic coercion.

### Nineteenth century to present

Baudelaire converts Virgil’s exiled mourner into an allegorical companion for displaced beings in modern Paris. Theatre, painting, opera, cinema, feminist criticism, trauma studies and refugee discourse increasingly foreground captivity, sexual violence, forced migration and maternal bereavement.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Racine

Jean Racine’s *Andromaque* premiered in 1667. Racine says he drew on Virgil, but radically alters the tradition: Astyanax is alive; Pyrrhus uses the boy’s survival to pressure Andromaque into marriage; Hermione desires Pyrrhus; Orestes desires Hermione. Andromaque agrees to marry Pyrrhus while planning suicide afterward, preserving fidelity to Hector and protection for her son. Hermione orders Orestes to kill Pyrrhus, then rejects him and kills herself; Orestes descends into madness.

Astyanax’s survival is an acknowledged dramatic alteration, not an ancient lost fact. Racine’s Andromaque became the dominant French image of heroic widowhood and maternal fidelity. **[EARLY MODERN]**

Ambrose Philips’ *The Distrest Mother* (1712) adapted Racine for the English stage and helped recast Andromache as a sentimental model of wife, widow and mother, while participating in contemporary political and humanitarian discourse.

### Opera and theatre

- André Grétry, *Andromaque*, 1780, libretto after Racine.
- Gioachino Rossini, *Ermione*, 1819, derived principally from Racine’s configuration.
- Herbert Windt, *Andromache*, 1931. Windt’s later career under National Socialism gives this reception a political context, but it does not make the ancient Andromache a nationalist figure.
- Jean Giraudoux’s *La guerre de Troie n’aura pas lieu* (1935) and numerous productions of *Trojan Women* invoke Andromache in interwar and antiwar settings.
- Jacques Rivette’s film *L’Amour fou* (1969) places rehearsals of Racine’s *Andromaque* within the collapse of a modern relationship.
- Michael Cacoyannis’ *The Trojan Women* (1971), with Vanessa Redgrave as Andromache, presents her through Euripides in a modern antiwar film.

### Baudelaire’s “Le Cygne”

Charles Baudelaire’s “Le Cygne,” first published in 1860 and incorporated into the 1861 *Fleurs du mal*, begins “Andromaque, je pense à vous!” It evokes Virgil’s *falsi Simoentis ad undam*, the counterfeit Simois at Buthrotum. Andromache’s immense grief and empty ritual landscape become analogues for the swan displaced in rebuilt Paris, the exiled Black woman, orphans, sailors and all who have lost what cannot be recovered.

The poem’s primary ancient intertext is demonstrably Virgil: the false Simois, captivity to Pyrrhus, and transfer to Helenus all come from *Aeneid* 3. Racine remains part of the French reader’s reception horizon but cannot alone explain those details. Scholarly readings connect the poem to Haussmannization, the failed Second Republic and criticism of Napoleon III’s pseudo-continuity with the past. **[MODERN RECEPTION]**

### Visual art

Ancient artists generally represent Andromache through three iconographic clusters:

1. Hector’s departure or the family group with Astyanax.
2. The sack, often with Andromache reaching toward Astyanax while Neoptolemus attacks Priam.
3. Captivity and mourning.

The famous image of Neoptolemus wielding Astyanax’s body against Priam is an artistic compression of atrocities. Identification of every adjacent woman as Andromache is not automatic; inscriptions, composition and LIMC comparanda are necessary. Polygnotus’ lost fifth-century mural at Delphi, described by Pausanias, showed Andromache with the child grasping her breast.

Major modern treatments include Jacques-Louis David’s *Andromache Mourning Hector* (1783), which turns Homeric lament into a neoclassical tableau of conjugal and maternal virtue, and Frederic Leighton’s *Captive Andromache* (1888), which monumentalizes isolation among women performing domestic labor.

### Political uses

The Molossian genealogy made Molossus, Neoptolemus and ultimately Achilles useful to Epirote rulers claiming Aeacid descent. Andromache participates as dynastic mother, but evidence that she herself was the principal object of a state ideology is weak.

Modern Greek, Albanian and Epirote arguments about ancient ethnicity sometimes enlist Buthrotum, Epirus, Molossians and Trojan migration. Virgil’s “little Troy” and Pausanias’ genealogy are mythic-political traditions, not genetic or archaeological proof of a modern nation’s descent. No documented, stable extremist cult centered on Andromache emerged from the contested-material search. General invocations of “traditional womanhood” or bereaved motherhood should not be mislabeled as organized appropriation without a specific source.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

No independent evidence verifies a historical Andromache. Excavations at Hisarlık establish successive Bronze Age settlements and destruction horizons, not Homeric individuals. Cilician Thebe and the geography under Placus may preserve regional knowledge, but matching a place does not authenticate the character. Treating Andromache as a recoverable Bronze Age princess is speculative. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Oral poetry and dating

Oral-formulaic scholarship explains the *Iliad* as the product of a long-performing tradition crystallized into a monumental composition. The gate scene contains formulas but is also intricately structured around household, city and heroic obligation. It is methodologically unsafe to assign individual lines to a Bronze Age “original Andromache” without linguistic or textual evidence.

Neoanalytic scholarship asks whether Homer drew on pre-existing sack traditions later represented by the Cycle. The Homeric prediction of Astyanax’s fall may presuppose such tradition; alternatively, later poets may have expanded Homer’s prediction. The surviving evidence cannot decide direction with certainty.

### The postwar genealogy

The Molossus tradition can be interpreted as an Epirote genealogy that links Molossian rulers to Troy and the Aeacids. Its appearance in fifth-century poetry and tragedy is early enough to be historically significant, but not proof that Euripides invented it. Political usefulness explains transmission and reshaping more securely than origin.

### Euripidean politics

Interpretations include:

- anti-Spartan satire through the cowardice and duplicity assigned to Menelaus and Hermione;
- criticism of imperial violence and enslavement;
- a conflict between legitimate marriage and enslaved concubinage;
- an Epirote or Molossian dynastic aetiology;
- an experiment in fragmented tragic structure.

The play’s production date and venue remain debated, so precise equation with one campaign or diplomatic event is hazardous.

### Hero cult

Older syntheses often converted any mythical tomb into proof of cult. Modern work by Antonaccio, Ekroth and others distinguishes archaeological burial practice, literary tomb narratives, *hērōa*, sacrifices and civic foundation myths. For Andromache the evidence is unusually narrow: Pausanias’ Pergamene *hērōion*, without surviving ritual detail. Farnell-style cataloguing remains useful but cannot supply missing archaeology.

### Iconography

LIMC cataloguing demonstrates the breadth of Hector–Andromache and sack imagery, while also exposing uncertainty. A mother and child in a Trojan scene may be Andromache and Astyanax; without an inscription or decisive narrative attributes, the identification remains probable rather than secure. Images can preserve traditions absent from extant texts, but they do not automatically illustrate a particular lost tragedy.

### Manuscripts and losses

- The *Iliad* survives abundantly in papyri and medieval manuscripts.
- Euripides’ *Andromache* belongs to the medievally selected plays and has a substantial manuscript tradition, but its original performance record is lost.
- The Cycle survives almost entirely in quotations, summaries and testimonia.
- Polygnotus’ Delphic paintings are lost and known through Pausanias.
- The Pergamene *hērōion* has not yielded a universally accepted archaeological identification.
- Hyginus and the pseudo-histories of Dictys and Dares survive in textually difficult recensions.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Andromache is recorded in Linear B.” Unsupported.
- “Archaeology proves she lived at Troy.” Unsupported.
- “Her name means unquestionably ‘she who fights men’ and therefore she was originally an Amazon.” Etymological overreach and conflation.
- “She voluntarily married Neoptolemus.” Sanitizes a tradition that explicitly makes her war booty and slave.
- “Racine preserves an ancient version in which Astyanax survived.” Racine’s survival plot is an early-modern alteration.
- “Virgil’s Buthrotum episode proves a historical Trojan colony.” Literary and political foundation narrative, not direct proof.
- “A known grave contains Andromache’s remains.” No authenticated remains exist.
