# Patroclus: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal epic hero; after death, a heroized companion sharing Achilles’ tomb and, in some evidence, his cultic honors. He is not a demigod in Homer and undergoes no canonical apotheosis.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the *Iliad*, whose monumental composition is conventionally placed in the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, following a long oral tradition. No securely identified Linear B occurrence names this hero. His earliest securely identifiable visual record is the early-sixth-century BCE Attic representation of the funeral games; the inscribed Sosias cup showing Achilles tending Patroclus dates about 500 BCE.

## Evidence labels

- **[E1: early]** Securely attested Archaic material: Homeric epic or securely identified Archaic art.
- **[E2: classical]** Fifth- or fourth-century BCE literary, artistic, or cultic reception.
- **[E3: later]** Hellenistic or Roman elaboration.
- **[RV]** Regional or otherwise competing ancient variant.
- **[LS]** Learned systematization by mythographers, scholiasts, or antiquarians.
- **[MA]** Medieval or early-modern accretion.
- **[MSR]** Modern scholarly reconstruction or interpretation.
- **[MI]** Modern artistic invention or adaptation.

Dates below are dates of composition or manufacture, not the legendary date of the Trojan War.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Patroclus is the mortal son of Menoetius, an Opuntian Locrian reared in Peleus’ household at Phthia after an accidental boyhood killing. In the *Iliad* he is Achilles’ older, exceptionally beloved companion, attendant, counselor, healer, and battlefield surrogate. He induces Achilles to let him lead the Myrmidons in Achilles’ armor, repels the Trojans from the ships, kills Sarpedon and numerous other warriors, and is then successively disabled by Apollo, wounded by Euphorbus, and killed by Hector. His death ends Achilles’ withdrawal and generates the poem’s final movement: Achilles’ return, Hector’s death, Patroclus’ funeral, and the funeral games. In *Odyssey* 24 the bones of Achilles and Patroclus share a golden urn and a conspicuous Hellespontine mound. Homer therefore makes Patroclus simultaneously a heroic warrior and the indispensable “other self” through whom Achilles’ social identity, mortality, grief, and violence are revealed. [E1]

### Name and forms

- **Ancient Greek:** Πάτροκλος, *Pátroklos*; epic inflections include Πατρόκλοιο, *Patrókloio*. The longer underlying form Πατροκλέης/*Patrokléēs* is reflected in oblique forms and later Πατροκλῆς, *Patroklês*. [E1/LS]
- **Latin:** *Patroclus*. [E3]
- **Modern Greek:** Πάτροκλος, *Pátroklos*.
- Common vernacular descendants include French *Patrocle*, Italian and Spanish *Patroclo*, German *Patroklos*, and English *Patroclus*.

The transparent Greek compound joins πατρ-/πατρο-, “father,” with κλέος, “glory, fame”: “glory of the father” or “paternal glory.” It is structurally comparable to Κλεόπατρος, *Kleópatros*, with the elements reversed. Nothing supports the popular Internet etymology “glory of the fatherland”; that would require a different first member. [LS]

A metrical study observes that the *Iliad* avoids an expected nominative Πατροκλέης and ordinarily uses the shorter Πάτροκλος, suggesting tension between the name and inherited epic phonology. That observation has been used in arguments that the character’s Iliadic prominence may be comparatively late, but it does not prove that “Homer invented Patroclus.” [MSR]

### Homeric descriptions and functional epithets

Patroclus does not possess a unique, rigid epithet system comparable to Achilles’ “swift-footed.” Homer applies recurring heroic formulas:

- **Μενοιτιάδης / Μενοιτίου υἱός**, “son of Menoetius.” [E1]
- **διογενής**, “Zeus-born/Zeus-nurtured,” a conventional honorific rather than literal divine parentage. [E1]
- **ἱππεῦ / ἱπποκέλευθε**, “horseman/master of horses,” in apostrophe. [E1]
- **θεόφιν μήστωρ ἀτάλαντος**, approximately “counselor equal to a god.” [E1]
- **ἤπιος**, “gentle/kindly,” especially in mourning and retrospective characterization. [E1]
- **φίλος**, **φίλτατος**, and phrases such as “dearest by far,” relational language marking his singular place for Achilles. These words can express affection of many kinds and, by themselves, do not specify sexual relations. [E1]

### Status and identifications

Patroclus remains mortal in the early tradition. His ghost is excluded from the company of the dead until burial, and afterward he dwells in Hades. *Odyssey* 24 places his shade with Achilles, Antilochus, and Ajax. [E1]

He was never generally identified with another deity or hero. Later analogy, however, repeatedly created paired equations:

- **Alexander–Achilles / Hephaestion–Patroclus:** reported by Arrian in the second century CE as a story about Alexander’s visit to Troy in 334 BCE. This is political and affective emulation, not syncretism. [E3]
- **Modern “Greek David and Jonathan” or “Greek Gilgamesh and Enkidu”:** comparative literary constructions, not ancient identifications. [MSR]
- **617 Patroclus:** the Jupiter Trojan asteroid discovered in 1906 and later found to be a binary, its companion being named Menoetius. This is astronomical commemoration, not survival of ancient belief. [MI]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

No tablet, Mycenaean inscription, or Bronze Age image securely names the hero. The first evidence is therefore literary: the received *Iliad*. References to Patroclus already in Book 1, where he receives Briseis from the heralds, and the unglossed introduction of him as “Menoetius’ son” show that the poem expects its audience to recognize him. This expectation argues against a simple claim that the extant poet invented him outright, though it cannot establish how large his role was in earlier song. [E1/MSR]

The Homeric poems survive principally through medieval manuscripts, supplemented by ancient papyri and quotations. The gap between monumental composition and complete medieval codices must not be confused with a lack of ancient circulation: Alexandrian editions, papyri, scholia, and extensive quotation document a continuous textual history.

### Primary corpus

1. **Homer, *Iliad*** — Greek; monumental composition conventionally late eighth/early seventh century BCE.

   - Book 1: Patroclus receives Briseis from Agamemnon’s heralds.
   - Book 9: he sits opposite Achilles as Achilles sings; he prepares wine and food for the embassy; Iphis sleeps beside him.
   - Book 11: Achilles sends him to identify Nestor’s wounded passenger; Nestor urges him to persuade Achilles or borrow his armor; Patroclus treats Eurypylus’ wound with medicines learned through Achilles from Chiron.
   - Book 15: Zeus predicts the sequence ending in Patroclus’ death.
   - Book 16: borrowing of the armor; command of the Myrmidons; killing of Sarpedon and many Trojans; Apollo–Euphorbus–Hector death sequence.
   - Book 17: struggle for his body and armor.
   - Books 18–22: Achilles’ lament and vengeance, with Patroclus’ corpse repeatedly present.
   - Book 23: ghost, burial request, shared-bones request, cremation, human and animal victims, funeral games.
   - Book 24: Achilles drags Hector around Patroclus’ tomb, then finally ransoms Hector.

   The principal English text cited here is A. T. Murray’s public-domain Loeb translation, with Greek available through Perseus. [E1]

2. **Homer, *Odyssey* 3.109–110; 24.15–97** — Greek; usually dated somewhat later than, or near the end of the formative period of, the *Iliad*. Nestor remembers Patroclus among the dead at Troy; Book 24 places his shade beside Achilles and says their bones were mingled in the golden urn made by Hephaestus and given by Dionysus to Thetis. Antilochus’ bones lie separately. A great tomb was built on a Hellespontine headland, visible to future sailors. [E1]

3. **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fr. 61 Merkelbach–West** — fragmentary Greek genealogical epic, probably sixth century BCE in its received form, though incorporating older traditions. Eustathius preserves the assertion that Menoetius and Peleus were brothers, making Patroclus and Achilles first cousins. This conflicts with the more familiar genealogy in which Menoetius is Actor’s son and Peleus Aeacus’ son. [E1/RV]

4. **Pindar, *Olympian* 9.69–79** — Greek, 466 BCE. Opuntian-local genealogy makes Menoetius a guest of Opus and recalls Patroclus standing with Achilles when Telephus routed the Greeks. This preserves a pre-Iliadic-war episode absent from Homer. [E2/RV]

5. **Pindar, *Olympian* 10.16–21** — Greek, 476 BCE. The victorious boxer Hagesidamus is to repay his trainer Ilas “as Patroclus did Achilles.” The allusion presupposes a reciprocal benefaction not clearly narrated in Homer. Whether its athletic trainer–youth analogy carries pederastic coloring is debated. [E2/MSR]

6. **Aeschylus, *Myrmidons*** — Greek tragedy, lost, first half of the fifth century BCE; fragments survive in quotations and papyri. Achilles mourns Patroclus in explicitly erotic language, including reference to kisses and to “reverent companionship of the thighs.” Aeschylus apparently cast Achilles as *erastēs*, the desiring lover, and Patroclus as beloved. The play is the earliest surviving explicit sexual interpretation, though the fragmentary state forbids reconstruction of every detail. [E2]

7. **Aeschylus, *Nereids* and *Phrygians/Ransom of Hector*** — companion tragedies in the Achillean trilogy, fifth century BCE, fragmentary. The trilogy treated the new armor, Hector’s death, dragging around Patroclus’ tomb, and ransom. The fourth-century CE Ketton mosaic may ultimately reflect this non-Homeric Aeschylean arrangement. [E2/E3]

8. **Plato, *Symposium* 179e–180b** — Greek, approximately 385–370 BCE. Phaedrus treats Achilles’ self-sacrifice for his lover Patroclus as divinely rewarded, but rebukes Aeschylus for making Achilles the lover: Achilles, younger and beardless, should be the beloved (*erōmenos*), Patroclus the older lover (*erastēs*). This is evidence for Classical Athenian reception, not proof of Homer’s intention. [E2]

9. **Xenophon, *Symposium* 8.31** — Greek, fourth century BCE. Xenophon’s Socrates expressly counters the erotic reading: Homer made Achilles avenge Patroclus “not as his favorite, but as his comrade.” Ancient disagreement is therefore explicit. [E2]

10. **Aeschines, *Against Timarchus* 133–142** — Greek forensic oratory, 346/345 BCE. Aeschines says Homer “hid” the name of their love while making its greatness plain to educated hearers, and cites Achilles’ wish that only he and Patroclus might survive to sack Troy. [E2]

11. **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1 and ancient scholia** — Greek, third century BCE; the poem includes Menoetius among relevant heroic genealogies, while the scholia preserve competing maternal genealogies for Patroclus. Patroclus himself is not securely an Argonaut in Apollonius’ narrative. Claims that he sailed on the *Argo* usually confuse him with his father. [E3/LS]

12. **Apollodoran *Bibliotheca* 3.13.8 and *Epitome* 3–5** — Greek mythographic handbook, first or second century CE. It systematizes his parents, exile, fostering, Trojan service, death, and burial. It names three candidate mothers and identifies the slain child as Clysonymus, son of Amphidamas. [E3/LS]

13. **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 97 and Trojan-War summaries** — Latin mythography in a text of uncertain date and heavily corrupt transmission, broadly early Imperial in content. *Fabula* 97 calls him son of Menoetius and “Philomela” and assigns him ten ships from Phthia—an allocation not found in Homer, where he belongs to Achilles’ contingent. The transmitted maternal name may reflect correction of corrupt *Pilomella*. [E3/LS]

14. **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 12–13** — Latin, completed about 8 CE. Patroclus is chiefly the causal absence behind Achilles’ renewed war and the later contest for Achilles’ arms; Ovid does not give an independent Patroclus biography. [E3]

15. **Pausanias, *Description of Greece*** — Greek, approximately 150–180 CE. It records:

   - an alternative Laconian report that Patroclus, rather than Achilles, killed Las, connected with the wooing of Helen (*3.24.10*); [RV]
   - an Amyclaean throne scene interpreted as events around Patroclus’ death (*3.19.13*);
   - Calliphon’s painting at Ephesus of women fitting Patroclus’ cuirass (*10.26.6*);
   - Patroclus among the dead in Polygnotus’ Delphic *Nekyia* (*10.30.6*). [E3]

16. **Philostratus, *Heroicus* 49–53** — Greek, early third century CE. The dialogue revises Homer through putative testimony of heroic ghosts. It describes Achilles and Patroclus’ shared tomb and extensive rites for Achilles; it is valuable evidence for Imperial hero-cult discourse but cannot be treated as straightforward reportage of Bronze Age practice. [E3]

17. **Philostratus, *Life of Apollonius* 4.16** — Greek, early third century CE. The ghost of Achilles says that one golden vessel holds himself and Patroclus “as if we were one.” [E3]

18. **Dictys Cretensis, *Ephemeris belli Troiani*** — surviving Latin recension probably fourth century CE from a lost Greek Imperial original. It rationalizes the war as eyewitness history, supplies Patroclus with a beloved woman, Diomedea, and transfers or recasts Homeric details. [E3]

19. **Dares Phrygius, *De excidio Troiae historia*** — Latin, probably fifth or sixth century CE. Like Dictys, it presents pseudo-eyewitness history and became more influential than Homer in much medieval Europe. [E3/MA]

20. **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica*** — Greek, probably third century CE. Beginning after Hector’s funeral, it repeatedly recalls Patroclus and culminates in Achilles’ funeral and the disposition of his remains with Patroclus. [E3]

21. **Tryphiodorus, *Taking of Ilios*** — Greek, probably third or fourth century CE. Patroclus survives as a retrospective marker: Neoptolemus resembles Achilles, Sarpedon’s death is recalled, and the poem assumes the Homeric sequence. [E3]

22. **Byzantine scholia, Eustathius, and Tzetzes** — Greek, preserving much earlier material but composed or compiled principally from late antiquity through the twelfth century. They are essential witnesses for the lost genealogies, tragedy, and mythography, but a scholiast’s attribution must be distinguished from a surviving Archaic text. [LS/MA]

## 3. The story

### Parentage and birthplace

In Homer, Patroclus is consistently Menoetius’ son. *Iliad* 11.765 and 16.14 make Menoetius son of Actor; Patroclus’ home is Opus/Opoeis in Locris. His mother is unnamed. [E1]

Later sources offer incompatible mothers:

| Mother | Parentage | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sthenele | daughter of Acastus | Apollodorus 3.13.8 | [E3/LS] |
| Periopis | daughter of Pheres | Apollodorus 3.13.8 | [E3/LS] |
| Polymele | daughter of Peleus, attributed to Philocrates | Apollodorus 3.13.8 | [E3/LS/RV] |
| Philomela | no secure parentage in this context | Hyginus 97; Homeric scholia and Eustathius | [E3/LS] |

The Hesiodic fragment making Menoetius Peleus’ brother would make Achilles and Patroclus first cousins. The standard Aeacid and Actorid genealogies do not. These trees cannot be harmonized without discarding evidence. [E1/RV]

### Boyhood killing, exile, and fostering

Patroclus’ ghost supplies the earliest account. As a little boy in Opoeis, he killed the unnamed son of Amphidamas “unwittingly,” in anger during a dice game. Menoetius brought him to Peleus, who received and reared him and made him Achilles’ attendant or *therapōn*. The Greek combines lack of premeditation with culpable anger; later retellings that make the event calculated murder or pure accident simplify Homer. [*Iliad* 23.82–90; E1]

Apollodorus names the victim **Clysonymus**. The name is later mythographic supplementation, not in Homer. [E3/LS]

“Fostering at Phthia” is secure. The frequent modern story that Patroclus studied with Achilles under Chiron is not directly Homeric. Homer says Patroclus learned medical remedies from Achilles, who learned them from Chiron; this does not state that Patroclus himself lived or trained on Pelion. [*Iliad* 11.828–832; E1 versus MI]

### Before Troy

Pindar says Patroclus stood with Achilles against Telephus when the latter routed the Achaeans in Mysia. This non-Homeric episode is securely Classical but may preserve cyclic material. [*Olympian* 9.70–79; E2/RV]

Pausanias records the Laconian claim that Patroclus, not Achilles, killed Las, implying his presence among Helen’s suitors. No early epic source makes him a suitor, and the variant probably served a local Laconian monument or genealogy. [Pausanias 3.24.10; E3/RV]

Hyginus gives Patroclus ten ships from Phthia. Homer does not list him as an independent contingent commander. [Hyginus 97; E3/LS]

### Patroclus in Achilles’ camp

He is neither a passive dependent nor merely a double:

- He obeys Achilles in receiving Briseis, but does not speak. [*Iliad* 1.337–347; E1]
- In Book 9, he silently listens while Achilles sings heroic deeds, prepares meat and wine, and signals Phoenix’s departure at Achilles’ instruction. Iphis, a captive woman Achilles allotted to him, sleeps beside him. [E1]
- In Book 11, Nestor uses Patroclus’ compassion to reopen communication with the withdrawn Achilles. Patroclus then treats Eurypylus, combining battlefield service and healing. [E1]
- Briseis’ lament later says Patroclus promised to make her Achilles’ lawful wife and was always gentle. The promised marriage never occurs, and the statement is hers, not Achilles’. [*Iliad* 19.287–300; E1]

### Borrowing the armor

With the Achaean ships threatened, Patroclus weeps before Achilles. Achilles allows him to wear the armor and lead the Myrmidons so the Trojans will mistake him for Achilles. He orders Patroclus to drive them from the ships and return, not to seek Troy or deprive Achilles of honor. [*Iliad* 16.1–100; E1]

Patroclus himself proposes the disguise after Nestor’s prompting in Book 11. Achilles authorizes and limits it. Versions making the substitution wholly Achilles’ plan, or Patroclus’ secret deception, are adaptations. [E1 versus MI]

Menoetius’ earlier instruction, recalled by Nestor, was that Achilles surpassed Patroclus in birth and strength but Patroclus was older and should give good counsel. This age difference later drives Plato’s reversal of Aeschylus’ erotic roles. [*Iliad* 11.786–789; E1]

### Aristeia

Patroclus leads five Myrmidon companies under Menesthius, Eudorus, Peisander, Phoenix, and Alcimedon. His appearance causes panic because the Trojans think Achilles has returned. He extinguishes the fire at Protesilaus’ ship and drives the Trojans from the camp. [*Iliad* 16.124–357; E1]

His named kills include Pyraechmes, Areilycus, Thoas, Amphiclus, Atalanta’s son Echeclus, Tlepolemus son of Damastor, Echius, Pyris, Ipheus, Euippus, Polymelus son of Argeas, Thestor son of Enops, Erymas, Amphoterus, Epaltes, Tlepolemus son of Damastor, Echius, Pyris, Ipheus, Euippus, Polymelus, Sthenelaus, Thrasymelus, Sarpedon, Adrastus, Autonous, Echeclus, Perimus, Epistor, Melanippus, Elasus, Mulius, Pylartes, and Cebriones. Some names occur in rapid catalogues and manuscript spellings vary; duplicated-looking modern lists often arise by combining the several phases of Book 16 carelessly. [E1]

Against **Sarpedon**, Zeus considers rescuing his son but Hera warns that other gods would demand the same privilege. Patroclus kills Sarpedon; Apollo later cleans the corpse and entrusts it to Sleep and Death for transport to Lycia. Patroclus therefore defeats a son of Zeus but does not personally interact with Hypnos or Thanatos. [*Iliad* 16.419–683; E1]

### Death: Apollo, Euphorbus, and Hector

The *Iliad* carefully divides responsibility:

1. Patroclus attacks Troy’s wall three times.
2. Apollo repels him; on the fourth attempt the god verbally warns that Troy is not fated to fall to him or even to Achilles. [16.698–711]
3. Apollo, disguised as Asius, urges Hector back into battle. [16.712–725]
4. After Patroclus kills Cebriones and fights over his body, Apollo approaches unseen from behind, strikes Patroclus, knocks off Achilles’ helmet, breaks his spear, removes his shield and corselet, and bewilders him. [16.788–817]
5. Euphorbus wounds him between the shoulders and withdraws. [16.806–829]
6. Hector thrusts a spear into his lower belly and boasts over him. Patroclus predicts Hector’s imminent death at Achilles’ hands. [16.830–867]

Thus “Hector killed Patroclus” is true but incomplete. Apollo is the decisive supernatural agent, Euphorbus the first mortal wounder, Hector the final killer. Menelaus kills Euphorbus in Book 17; Hector takes Achilles’ armor; Ajax, Menelaus, Meriones, and others recover the naked corpse. [E1]

Later rationalizing versions reduce divine action, redistribute wounds, or amplify Hector’s cruelty. They must not replace the Homeric sequence. [E3]

### The corpse and lamentation

Antilochus tells Achilles. Achilles pours ash over himself, tears his hair, and lies in the dust; captive women, Briseis, the Myrmidons, Thetis, and the Nereids lament. Thetis preserves Patroclus’ body with ambrosia and nectar while Achilles pursues vengeance. [*Iliad* 18–19; E1]

Briseis’ lament gives Patroclus an ethical identity: he comforted a captive whose husband and brothers Achilles had killed. It neither erases the system of enslavement nor proves that Patroclus opposed it; Iphis is also his allotted captive and probable concubine. [E1]

### Ghost, burial, sacrifice, and games

Patroclus’ ghost appears in Achilles’ sleep and complains that other shades exclude him until cremation. He requests immediate burial and that their bones ultimately share one vessel. Achilles tries unsuccessfully to embrace him. The scene supplies the childhood-exile story and treats funeral rites as necessary for integration among the dead. [*Iliad* 23.62–107; E1]

The funeral includes:

- hair offerings from the Myrmidons and Achilles;
- sheep and cattle;
- honey and oil;
- four horses;
- two of Achilles’ nine dogs;
- **twelve Trojan youths killed by Achilles at the pyre**.

The last act is human sacrifice within the poem’s narrative. Homer calls Achilles’ intention grim; nothing says Patroclus’ ghost requested it. It is Achilles’ retaliatory violence, not a regular rite securely documented for Patroclus’ historical cult. [*Iliad* 23.138–183; E1]

The pyre is lit with the help of Boreas and Zephyrus after Iris carries Achilles’ prayer. The bones are gathered into a golden urn and a provisional mound erected. Achilles then sponsors contests in chariot racing, boxing, wrestling, foot racing, armed combat, discus, archery, and spear casting. The contests redistribute wealth and status and partially reintegrate the Achaean community after Achilles’ destructive withdrawal. [E1/MSR]

### Shared urn and fate after death

*Odyssey* 24 fulfills the request: Achilles’ bones are mingled with Patroclus’ in the golden urn, while Antilochus lies separately. A great mound rises on a Hellespontine promontory. Their shades appear together in Hades. [E1]

Philostratus later intensifies the union: Achilles’ ghost says the single vessel contains them “as if we were one.” This is Imperial reception. [E3]

No early source makes Patroclus immortal on Leuke. Later descriptions of Achilles’ island sometimes include Patroclus among Achilles’ companions; this is participation in Achilles’ posthumous retinue, not an independently attested Patroclean apotheosis. [E3]

### The relationship with Achilles

The evidence supports several historically distinct conclusions:

- **Homer:** extraordinary mutual affection, domestic intimacy, age asymmetry, a wish for shared burial, and grief exceeding ordinary comradeship; no explicit statement of sexual intercourse or of *erastēs/erōmenos* roles. Each man also has a woman in his sleeping quarters in Book 9. This establishes neither heterosexual exclusivity nor a sexual relation between the men. [E1]
- **Aeschylus:** explicitly erotic and apparently sexual; Achilles is lover, Patroclus beloved. [E2]
- **Plato’s Phaedrus:** accepts the erotic pair but reverses the roles on age and beauty grounds. [E2]
- **Xenophon’s Socrates:** explicitly rejects Patroclus as Achilles’ erotic favorite and calls him comrade. [E2]
- **Aeschines:** says Homer deliberately left the love unnamed because educated hearers could recognize it. [E2]
- **Modern scholarship:** divided between nonsexual heroic companionship, erotic potential left deliberately unclassified, and readings in which Homeric social categories do not map cleanly onto “gay,” “straight,” or the institutional pederasty of Classical Athens. [MSR]
- **Modern popular culture:** often either erases erotic reception (“cousins” in *Troy*) or makes the pair an explicitly monogamous modern couple (*The Song of Achilles*, *Hades*). Both are adaptations. [MI]

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical alternatives

**Homeric/Actorid tree [E1]**

```text
Actor
└── Menoetius
    └── Patroclus
```

**Hesiodic kinship claim [RV]**

```text
Unnamed common parent
├── Peleus ── Thetis
│   └── Achilles
└── Menoetius
    └── Patroclus
```

This makes Achilles and Patroclus first cousins, but it conflicts with the familiar Aeacid genealogy of Peleus.

**Apollodoran alternatives [LS]**

```text
Menoetius + Sthenele, daughter of Acastus
          or Periopis, daughter of Pheres
          or Polymele, daughter of Peleus
                    └── Patroclus
```

**Hyginus [LS]**

```text
Menoetius + Philomela
          └── Patroclus
```

No secure ancient tradition gives Patroclus a child. Myrto is sometimes made his sister and mother of Eucleia by Heracles in late genealogical compilations, but this is peripheral, poorly anchored material rather than Homeric family history.

### Documented interactions: gods and named heroes

The following treats “interaction” strictly. Merely sharing a genealogy, catalogue, or scene without action is noted only where it materially affects the tradition.

#### Gods and supernatural beings

- **Apollo** — Repels Patroclus from Troy, warns him, disguises himself as Asius to incite Hector, then strikes and strips Patroclus before Euphorbus’ wound. *Iliad* 16.698–817, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. **[E1]**
- **Zeus** — Hears Achilles’ prayer but grants only part of it; weighs events around Sarpedon and Patroclus; directs the sequence leading to Patroclus’ death and protects his corpse from dishonor. *Iliad* 16–17. **[E1]**
- **Hera** — Persuades Zeus not to rescue Sarpedon, indirectly leaving him to Patroclus. No direct conversation with Patroclus. *Iliad* 16.431–461. **[E1]**
- **Thetis** — Receives the fugitive into her household in the parallel story of Epeigeus and, more importantly, mourns Patroclus with Achilles, preserves his corpse, and prompts the funeral lament. *Iliad* 18–19, 23. **[E1]**
- **Chiron** — No direct meeting is attested in Homer; Patroclus knows remedies through Achilles, who learned them from Chiron. *Iliad* 11.828–832. Direct tutelage of Patroclus is a later inference. **[E1/MI distinction]**
- **Athena** — No direct personal exchange with Patroclus. She acts in the surrounding war and funeral games; claims that she guided his aristeia are not stated. **[absence of evidence]**
- **Aphrodite** — Protects Hector’s corpse while Achilles abuses it in Patroclus’ honor; she does not address Patroclus. *Iliad* 23.184–191. **[E1, indirect]**
- **Iris** — Carries Achilles’ request to the winds so Patroclus’ pyre will burn. *Iliad* 23.192–225. **[E1, funerary]**
- **Boreas** — Answers the prayer and fans Patroclus’ pyre. *Iliad* 23.192–225. **[E1]**
- **Zephyrus** — Acts with Boreas at the pyre. Same source. **[E1]**
- **Hephaestus** — No living interaction; makes the golden urn later holding the bones of Achilles and Patroclus. *Odyssey* 24.73–84. **[E1]**
- **Dionysus** — Gives Thetis the golden urn made by Hephaestus; no personal meeting with Patroclus. *Odyssey* 24.73–84. **[E1]**
- **Hades** — Patroclus’ ghost cannot pass fully among Hades’ dead until cremated; Hades does not appear as an acting character. *Iliad* 23.69–76. **[E1]**
- **Hypnos and Thanatos** — Carry Sarpedon away only after Patroclus has killed him; they do not meet Patroclus. *Iliad* 16.666–683. **[E1, indirect]**
- **Spercheus** — Achilles redirects to Patroclus the lock of hair once vowed to this river. *Iliad* 23.141–151. **[E1, funerary displacement]**

#### Family, allies, companions, and captives

- **Menoetius** — Father; brings the boy to Peleus after the killing and had earlier instructed him to counsel Achilles. *Iliad* 11.765–789; 23.82–90. **[E1]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### The epic tomb

The *Iliad* gives Patroclus a provisional mound on the Hellespontine shore, to be enlarged after Achilles’ death. *Odyssey* 24 describes the completed common mound on a projecting headland, visible from the sea. These are literary attestations, not archaeological identifications. [E1]

The visible, maritime mound functions as memorial and landmark. Its promised future audience—“men now living and those to be born hereafter”—makes the monument a mechanism of *kleos*. [E1]

### Historical localization

Ancient visitors associated a Troad tumulus with Achilles, and the literary tradition held that Patroclus shared it. Philostratus’ *Heroicus* says the Achaeans erected a hill when Achilles was mingled with Patroclus in the tomb. [E3]

Modern travelers assigned “tomb of Patroclus” to more than one Troad mound. Nineteenth-century reports explicitly admit uncertainty over which tumulus, if any, ancient people identified separately with Patroclus. The modern label on a small mound is therefore not archaeological proof of his grave. [MSR]

No excavated burial has been demonstrated to contain a historical Patroclus. Nor would a heroic-age burial, without a contemporary identifying inscription, establish the epic individual.

### Ritual and cult

The strongest ritual evidence attaches to the **joint tomb and Achilles’ cult**, not to a securely independent sanctuary of Patroclus:

- Achilles’ funeral narrative superimposes his future burial upon Patroclus’ rites. Some scholars consequently read *Iliad* 23 as poetic anticipation of a joined hero cult. [MSR]
- Philostratus describes annual Thessalian rites at Achilles’ Troad tomb, including sacrifice and holocaust. Because the tomb was understood to contain Patroclus too, he was spatially included, but the named recipient is principally Achilles. [E3]
- Gunnel Ekroth notes textual references grouping sacrificial honors to Achilles, Ajax, Antilochus, and Patroclus under *enagizein/enagismata*. This is evidence that Patroclus could be counted among honored Trojan-war dead, but it does not locate a flourishing independent Patroclus cult. [E2/E3 scholarly synthesis]
- Arrian’s story that Hephaestion crowned Patroclus’ tomb shows that by the Roman period a separately recognizable Patroclean memorial could be imagined or visited at Troy. Arrian marks it as one reported version, not uncontested autopsy. [E3]
- The twelve captives at the pyre are literary human sacrifice. There is no archaeological evidence that worshippers historically repeated human sacrifice to Patroclus. [E1 versus absence]

**Finding:** Patroclus is securely a heroized dead man in literary and ritual discourse, and his burial is inseparable from Achilles’. Evidence for a separate, institutionally continuous cult, priesthood, festival, oracle, or dedicated *heroon* is slight to absent.

### Visual ritual record

- **Sophilos’ Attic black-figure lebes fragment**, about 580–570 BCE, names or depicts the funeral games for Patroclus. It is among the earliest identifiable Iliadic scenes in Attic art. [E1]
- **François Vase**, about 570 BCE, includes the funeral games. [E1]
- **Euphorbus plate**, East Greek/Rhodian, about 600 BCE, depicts Menelaus and Hector over Euphorbus after the wounding of Patroclus; the precise presence of Patroclus’ corpse in the surviving composition is debated. [E1]
- **Sosias cup, Berlin F 2278**, about 500 BCE: Achilles bandages Patroclus’ arm; both figures are inscribed. Homer never narrates this episode, making it independent visual invention or nonextant tradition, not an illustration of a specific Homeric passage. [E2]
- **Priam Painter amphora**, about 520–510 BCE: Achilles drags Hector around Patroclus’ tomb; Patroclus’ armed shade appears above the mound. The composition differs from the *Iliad*, where the dragging circuit is described around the tomb in Book 24 but elsewhere associated with the city and camp. [E1/E2]
- **Polygnotus’ Delphic *Nekyia***, mid-fifth century BCE, known from Pausanias: Patroclus appeared among the underworld dead. The painting is lost. [E2/E3 testimony]
- **Calliphon at Ephesus**, date uncertain but pre-Pausanian: women fit Patroclus’ armor. Lost, known only from Pausanias. [E3 testimony]
- **Ketton/Rutland mosaic**, Roman Britain, fourth century CE: Hector and Achilles combat, dragging around Patroclus’ tomb, and a ransom by weighing. Its departures from Homer have been plausibly connected with Aeschylus’ *Phrygians*. [E3/MSR]

## 6. Historical evolution

### Oral and Archaic formation, before approximately 700 BCE

Patroclus’ prehistory cannot be directly documented. The *Iliad* presupposes him, gives him formulaic language and an Opuntian background, and makes his death structurally indispensable. The absence of Bronze Age evidence means that assertions about a historical warrior behind him are speculation. [E1/MSR]

### Monumental Homeric shaping, approximately 750–650 BCE

The extant epic establishes the durable sequence: exile, Phthian fostering, service to Achilles, compassionate intervention, borrowed armor, aristeia, tripartite death, corpse struggle, ghost, funeral, and shared burial. The *Odyssey* completes the joint tomb. [E1]

### Archaic visual and genealogical expansion, sixth century BCE

Attic and East Greek art selects wounds, funeral games, the corpse struggle, and the tomb. The Hesiodic genealogy turns the pair into cousins. These are not merely illustrations of a fixed text; several show non-Homeric details. [E1/RV]

### Classical erotic, civic, and tragic rereading, fifth–fourth centuries BCE

Pindar localizes Patroclus in Opuntian civic genealogy and connects him with Telephus and trainer–athlete reciprocity. Aeschylus eroticizes the bond. Plato accepts the erotic tradition but debates its roles; Xenophon rejects the sexual reading; Aeschines finds Homeric reticence intentional. Patroclus had thus become a test case for education, male love, courage, and proper reciprocity. [E2]

### Hellenistic scholarship and mythography, third–first centuries BCE

Genealogists and scholars collected incompatible mothers, local episodes, and textual explanations. Apollonius’ scholia preserve much that the poem itself does not narrate. Artistic repertories increasingly detach scenes from any single poem. [E3/LS]

### Roman Imperial systematization, first–third centuries CE

Apollodorus and Hyginus turn scattered traditions into dossier-like biographies. Pausanias documents regional stories and lost artworks. Philostratus treats heroic ghosts and tomb worship as living religious discourse and strengthens the language of Achilles and Patroclus as posthumously one. [E3]

### Late antique rationalization

Dictys and Dares present the war as eyewitness history, reducing gods and adding courtly or sexual details. Patroclus acquires Diomedea in Dictys. These works later displace Homer as medieval Europe’s supposed historical authorities. [E3]

### Medieval transmission

Benoît de Sainte-Maure’s Old French *Roman de Troie*, about 1155–1160, derives chiefly from Dictys and Dares and turns Troy into chivalric history. Guido delle Colonne’s Latin *Historia destructionis Troiae*, completed in 1287, recasts Benoît as sober history and circulated extremely widely. The explicit Classical debate over Achilles and Patroclus’ erotic roles was muted or removed, while Achilles’ grief remained. [MA]

Byzantine Tzetzes and Eustathius preserved Greek scholia and variants rather than creating a single canonical Patroclus. Their value is greatest where their named sources can be checked. [LS/MA]

### Early modern recovery

Printed Homer, Plato, Aeschines, and fragments of tragedy returned the erotic controversy to learned Europe. Poliziano’s philological discussion of the pair is an early post-Classical engagement with the role dispute. Shakespeare’s *Troilus and Cressida*, about 1601–1602, makes Patroclus Achilles’ intimate companion and an object of Thersites’ sexualized abuse; its cynical military world is neither Homeric biography nor neutral antiquarian recovery. [MA]

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Neoclassical art privileged grief and the beautiful male body: Jacques-Louis David’s *The Funeral of Patroclus* (1778) compresses funeral, corpse, and vengeance into a panoramic tableau. John Flaxman’s Homeric illustrations and Ingres’ *Achilles Receiving the Ambassadors of Agamemnon* (1801) reinforced the domestic pairing. Nineteenth-century translations often used “friend” or “comrade,” while scholarship on Greek sexuality and emerging homosexual identities increasingly reclaimed the pair as exemplary lovers. [MI/MSR]

### Twentieth century to present

The pair became a cultural index of whether an adaptation would suppress, imply, or affirm same-sex love. This history culminates in sharply different Patrocluses: Hollywood cousin, pacifist healer, warrior lover, comic sidekick, or underworld partner. Contemporary reconstructionist worship sometimes honors Patroclus with Achilles, but online practice is modern religious revival, not evidence for an unbroken cult. [MI]

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and drama

- **William Shakespeare, *Troilus and Cressida***, approximately 1601–1602 — Patroclus is inseparable from Achilles and participates in theatrical imitation; Thersites weaponizes allegations of sexual passivity. [MA]
- **Goethe, *Achilleis***, 1799, fragment — centers Achilles’ mortality and memorial world; Patroclus is chiefly an absent cause. [MI]
- **Christopher Logue, *War Music***, 1959–2005 — radically modernizes Homeric diction and violence; Patroclus remains the hinge of Achilles’ return. [MI]
- **Pat Barker, *The Silence of the Girls***, 2018, and *The Women of Troy*, 2021 — recenter enslaved women and expose the violence underlying heroic grief; Patroclus is remembered through Briseis’ compromised experience. [MI]
- **Madeline Miller, *The Song of Achilles***, 2011 — first-person Patroclus narrates an explicitly loving and sexual relationship. Miller makes him a poor fighter and committed healer, follows Achilles to Chiron, and allows Thetis eventually to secure his posthumous reunion. These are purposeful modern inventions or expansions; Homer’s Patroclus is a highly effective killer and is not directly taught by Chiron. [MI]

### Opera and music

- **Jean-Baptiste Lully/Pascal Collasse, *Achille et Polyxène***, 1687 — calls Patroclus Achilles’ tender friend while directing Achilles’ erotic desire toward Briseis and Polyxena. [MA]
- Numerous musical settings of the *Iliad* or Trojan cycle use Patroclus’ death as the motive for Achilles’ return, but no single “Patroclus opera” has dominated the repertory.

### Painting and sculpture

- **Jacques-Louis David, *The Funeral of Patroclus***, 1778 — monumental neoclassical synthesis, exhibited in Rome and now in the National Gallery of Ireland. [MI]
- **David, *Patroclus***, approximately 1780 — an academic male nude traditionally given the hero’s name; the identification supplies heroic narrative to a studio exercise. [MI]
- **Sculptural “Pasquino/Menelaus supporting Patroclus” group** — Roman copies of a Hellenistic composition are conventionally identified as Menelaus carrying Patroclus. The identification is strong but not inscribed, and other fallen-hero identifications have been proposed. [E3/MSR]

### Film and television

- ** *Helen of Troy* **, 1956 — uses Patroclus within a conventional heterosexualized sword-and-sandal narrative. [MI]
- ** *Troy* **, 2004 — makes Patroclus Achilles’ younger cousin and trainee. Hector kills him believing him to be Achilles. The kinship borrows an ancient variant but the younger-trainee characterization and suppression of the Classical erotic tradition are modern screenplay choices. [MI]
- **BBC/Netflix, *Troy: Fall of a City***, 2018 — explicitly depicts Achilles and Patroclus as lovers, while also reshaping the wider Trojan cycle. [MI]

### Comics and games

- **Marvel and other mythological comics** generally subordinate Patroclus to Achilles’ origin or vengeance plot; portrayals are modern fantasy rather than transmission of a distinct ancient variant. [MI]
- **Supergiant Games, *Hades***, full release 2020 — Achilles and Patroclus are explicitly former lovers separated in the underworld. Zagreus can reconcile them. Their contract with Hades, separation between the House and Elysium, and interactive reunion are inventions, though the shared afterlife develops *Odyssey* 24 and later erotic reception. [MI]
- **Fate/Grand Order and fan-derived “Patroclus” concepts** frequently circulate with spurious epithets or biographies. Unless tied to an official released character or ancient citation, these are fandom inventions. [MI]

### Political and identity uses

Alexander’s emulation of Achilles and the story of Hephaestion honoring Patroclus are the principal ancient political appropriation: the legendary pair furnished a language for Macedonian friendship, conquest, and heroic self-fashioning. [E3]

From the late nineteenth century onward, Achilles and Patroclus became resources in the history of homosexual identity and later queer reception. Modern advocates have sometimes claimed that Homer “clearly says they had sex”; opponents have sometimes claimed that erotic interpretation is wholly recent. Both statements are false. Homer is not explicit, but erotic interpretation is securely Classical and contested already in antiquity. [E2/MSR]

No comparably substantial, documented nationalist or extremist cult of **Patroclus specifically** emerges from the evidence examined. Modern militarist invocations concentrate on Achilles, Sparta, or the Homeric warrior generally. Patroclus is more often used in antiwar, grief, companionship, or LGBTQ contexts. Absence of specific evidence should not be replaced by a generic claim about “Greek nationalist appropriation.”

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Patroclus invented for the *Iliad*?

One analytic line treats him as a comparatively late creation or expansion required to bring Achilles back into battle without immediately resolving the quarrel. His story resembles a narrative substitute: he wears Achilles’ armor, performs an Achillean aristeia, and dies in a sequence that anticipates Achilles’ own death. Metrical awkwardness in the name and scarcity of independent early myths support a late-expansion hypothesis. [MSR]

Against this, oral-traditional scholars emphasize:

- his unglossed introduction as Menoetius’ son;
- formulaic language and a coherent fugitive-murderer pattern;
- independent Opuntian genealogy;
- Pindar’s Telephus episode;
- early art not confined to the received Iliadic text;
- the conservative nature of oral traditions.

Jonathan Burgess accordingly argues that “Homeric invention” is much harder to prove than older source-analytic models assumed. The responsible conclusion is that the extant *Iliad* enormously shaped Patroclus, but the evidence cannot decide whether it created him, enlarged a minor traditional figure, or reorganized several older motifs. [MSR]

### Neoanalysis and substitution

Neoanalytic scholarship compares Patroclus’ death with traditions about Achilles and Antilochus in the *Aethiopis*: a beloved companion dies, Achilles avenges him, and Achilles’ own death follows. One approach sees the *Iliad* adapting an older Memnon–Antilochus–Achilles pattern; another regards the surviving cyclic tradition as equally capable of being shaped by Iliadic prestige. Because the *Aethiopis* is lost and survives through fragments and Proclus’ summary, direction of borrowing remains disputed. [MSR]

### Oral-formulaic interpretation

Milman Parry and Albert Lord’s work made it untenable to treat every repeated formula or scene as an individual author’s mechanical borrowing. Gregory Nagy reads Patroclus within an evolving oral tradition in which Achilles’ *kleos* and social identity are realized through the death of his *therapōn*. Homeric Multitext scholarship similarly emphasizes multiformity rather than a single lost original. [MSR]

### What does *therapōn* mean?

In ordinary context it can mean attendant, comrade, or squire. Comparative linguistic arguments connect it with ritual substitution or a “ritual stand-in,” leading Nagy to interpret Patroclus as Achilles’ heroic substitute. This is a scholarly semantic reconstruction, not a translation that every occurrence must bear. [MSR]

### Sexuality

The debate must keep three questions separate:

1. What does Homer explicitly narrate?
2. How did later Greeks interpret it?
3. Which modern sexual categories are appropriate?

Homer provides intimacy and singular love but no explicit sexual act. Aeschylus, Plato, and Aeschines supply erotic readings; Xenophon supplies explicit dissent. Modern scholarship by W. M. Clarke, David Halperin, Michel Foucault, James Davidson, Marco Fantuzzi, Shane Butler, and others has debated whether heroic friendship is compatible with, prior to, or categorically unlike Classical pederasty and modern homosexuality. The ancient role dispute itself proves that interpretation was culturally conditioned. [MSR]

Thomas Hubbard argues that Pindar’s trainer–athlete comparison, especially when Hagesidamus is later compared with Ganymede, may carry pederastic resonance. That is a contextual inference, not an explicit statement in Pindar. [MSR]

### Hero cult

Earlier scholars, especially Erwin Rohde and L. R. Farnell, sometimes read unusual funerary bloodshed in *Iliad* 23 as a fossil of pre-Homeric chthonic cult. Later archaeology and ritual scholarship complicate any simple progression from “tomb cult” to “hero cult.” Carla Antonaccio, Gunnel Ekroth, Walter Burkert, James Whitley, Gregory Nagy, and others distinguish:

- one-time funerals from recurring public worship;
- ordinary sacrifice (*thysia*) from total burning or *enagismata*;
- epic narrative from performed ritual;
- cult at an actual ancient tomb from retrospective identification of prehistoric mounds.

The joint mound is textually secure; recurring independent cult of Patroclus is not. [MSR]

### Historicity

No inscription, grave good, or Bronze Age archive verifies a historical Patroclus. Excavated Troy makes a Late Bronze Age conflict-zone historically plausible but does not validate named Homeric persons. Attempts to identify a particular Troad mound as “his actual tomb” move from ancient localization to unwarranted biography. [MSR]

### Textual losses

Major losses include:

- most of the Epic Cycle;
- Aeschylus’ Achillean trilogy except fragments;
- early local mythographers cited by Apollodorus and scholia;
- Polygnotus’ and Calliphon’s paintings;
- contexts for many vases removed through ancient trade or modern excavation.

These losses explain why later mythographers sometimes preserve genuinely old material, but “preserved by a late source” is not the same as “securely Archaic.”

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Patroclus is securely attested in Linear B — **unsupported**.
- Homer explicitly calls Achilles and Patroclus sexual lovers — **false**.
- The lovers interpretation was invented in the twenty-first century — **false; Aeschylus, Plato, and Aeschines attest it**.
- Patroclus was simply an unskilled pacifist healer — **modern invention contradicted by his Iliadic aristeia**.
- Patroclus personally studied under Chiron — **not stated by Homer**.
- Hector alone killed him — **incomplete; Apollo and Euphorbus are indispensable**.
- Patroclus asked for the twelve Trojan youths to be sacrificed — **false; he asks only for burial and shared bones**.
- Archaeologists have identified his historical remains — **false**.
- Alexander and Hephaestion’s tomb offerings prove their sexual relationship — **false inference; Arrian reports heroic emulation but not a sexual confession**.
- Patroclus had a securely attested independent pan-Hellenic cult — **not supported**.
