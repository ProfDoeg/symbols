# Arjuna: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: early attestation]** Evidence preceding, or plausibly preceding, the received Sanskrit epic.
- **[E2: Sanskrit epic]** Material in the critically reconstructed *Mahābhārata* or its early appendix, the *Harivaṃśa*. “Critical edition” means the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute edition, not an autograph or recoverable authorial original.
- **[E3: classical elaboration]** Sanskrit drama, *mahākāvya*, Purāṇic retelling, commentary, or iconography after the epic’s principal redaction.
- **[E4: regional tradition]** Vernacular epic, ritual, theatre, oral narrative, or localized cult.
- **[E5: learned reconstruction]** Modern philological, archaeological, comparative, or historical interpretation.
- **[E6: modern reception/invention]** Modern literature, film, television, nationalism, popular religion, games, or an unattested claim circulated as ancient.

Dates of ancient Indian texts are normally ranges, not publication dates.

## 1. Identification, names, epithets, and class

**Class:** Arjuna is principally a **mortal Kṣatriya epic hero of divine paternity**, the third of the five Pāṇḍavas, and—in the epic’s developed theology—the earthly manifestation or rebirth of the primordial sage **Nara**, paired with Kṛṣṇa-Nārāyaṇa. He is not consistently an independently worshipped god. He is, however, incorporated into temple ensembles, regional ritual, theatrical repertoires, and the devotional reception of the *Bhagavad Gītā*. **[E2; E3; E4]**

**Earliest secure attestation:** The safest early evidence is not a biography but the grammatical pairing **Vāsudeva–Arjuna** in Pāṇini, *Aṣṭādhyāyī* 4.3.98, usually dated around the fourth century BCE, though Pāṇini’s date remains debated. The rule forms derivatives from *Vāsudeva* and *Arjuna*; later commentators understand such derivatives as adherents or devotees. It probably establishes that a named Arjuna was associated with Vāsudeva by Pāṇini’s or the early commentarial period, but it does **not by itself securely establish every epic deed, the complete Kṛṣṇa-Arjuna theology, or even beyond dispute that the referent is the fully developed Pāṇḍava**. **[E1; disputed]**

The oldest direct material witness to a text organized as a *Mahābhārata* is the fragmentary Sanskrit **Spitzer manuscript**, recovered from Central Asia and now often palaeographically dated around 200–230 CE. Its list of books demonstrates an epic already divided into *parvans*, but it neither preserves Arjuna’s continuous story nor proves that every episode in the medieval manuscript tradition was then present. **[E1]**

### Principal names

- **अर्जुन — Arjuna.** Sanskrit *arjuna* means “white,” “bright,” “clear,” “silvery,” and names the pale-barked *Terminalia arjuna* tree. The epic itself exploits the name as an epithet of purity or brightness but does not preserve a single historical act of naming from which an etymology can be recovered. **[E2; learned etymology]**
- **पार्थ — Pārtha, “son/descendant of Pṛthā.”** Pṛthā is Kuntī’s birth-name. Especially prominent in Kṛṣṇa’s addresses to him in the *Bhagavad Gītā*. **[E2]**
- **कौन्तेय — Kaunteya, “son of Kuntī.”** Also applicable to Kuntī’s other sons and, genealogically, Karṇa. **[E2]**
- **पाण्डव — Pāṇḍava, “son/descendant of Pāṇḍu.”** Social and dynastic rather than biological in Arjuna’s case: the epic makes Indra his divine progenitor. **[E2]**
- **इन्द्रपुत्र / शक्रसूनु — Indraputra/Śakrasūnu, “son of Indra/Śakra.”** **[E2]**
- **धनञ्जय — Dhanañjaya, conventionally “winner of wealth.”** Connected by the epic with his conquests and collection of tribute for Yudhiṣṭhira’s sacrifice. **[E2]**
- **सव्यसाचिन् — Savyasācin, “ambidextrous,” literally one who can use the left hand.** Marks his ability to shoot with either hand. **[E2]**
- **गुडाकेश — Guḍākeśa.** Conventionally interpreted as “conqueror of sleep”; alternative lexical analysis connects it with thick or curly hair. The devotional translation “lord of sleep” is interpretive, not an independently attested biography. **[E2; disputed etymology]**
- **किरीटिन् — Kirīṭin, “diademed.”** Associated with the celestial crown obtained in the Indra cycle. **[E2]**
- **फाल्गुन — Phālguna.** Explained through his birth under the constellation Uttarā/Phālgunī or on the slopes of Himavat; explanations vary with recension and commentary. **[E2]**
- **जिष्णु — Jiṣṇu, “victorious.”** **[E2]**
- **विजय — Vijaya, “victory/victorious.”** Both an epithet and, in some enumerations, one of his ten names. **[E2]**
- **बीभत्सु — Bībhatsu, usually “one who shuns the disgusting/dishonourable.”** The epic explains it through his claimed refusal to perform a repellent act in battle, although his killing of Karṇa while Karṇa struggles with his chariot produces a deliberate ethical contradiction. **[E2]**
- **श्वेतवाहन — Śvetavāhana, “he whose horses are white.”** **[E2]**
- **कपिध्वज — Kapidhvaja, “monkey-bannered,”** after Hanumān takes his place upon Arjuna’s standard. **[E2]**
- **गाण्डीवधन्वन् / गाण्डीविन् — Gāṇḍīvadhanvan/Gāṇḍīvin, “bearer of the Gāṇḍīva bow.”** **[E2]**
- **नर — Nara.** The human sage paired with Nārāyaṇa; the epic’s theological strata identify Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa with that primordial pair. This is a theological equation, not evidence for a historical reincarnation. **[E2; learned systematization]**
- **Bṛhannalā / बृहन्नला.** Arjuna’s assumed identity during the incognito year: a dance-and-music teacher represented as wearing women’s clothing and describing himself in gender-ambiguous or eunuch-like terms. Translations such as “eunuch,” “transvestite,” or “third-gender person” impose somewhat different modern categories on the Sanskrit scene. **[E2]**
- **Tamil:** அருச்சுனன்/அர்ஜுனன், *Aruccuṉaṉ/Arjuṉaṉ*.  
- **Kannada:** ಅರ್ಜುನ, *Arjuna*.  
- **Malayalam:** അർജുനൻ, *Arjunan*.  
- **Old Javanese/Javanese/Balinese:** Arjuna; wayang honorific and alternate names include **Janaka**, **Permadi/Pamadi**, and **Parta**. These belong to the Indonesian reception and should not automatically be read back into the Sanskrit text. **[E4]**

Arjuna must not be confused with **Kārtavīrya Arjuna/Sahasrārjuna**, the thousand-armed Haihaya king killed by Paraśurāma. Cults and mantras specifically directed to Kārtavīrya are not evidence for a cult of the Pāṇḍava. **[E2; E3]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Early external evidence

1. **Pāṇini, *Aṣṭādhyāyī* 4.3.98, probably fourth century BCE:** *vāsudevārjunābhyāṃ vun*. Possible evidence for a Vāsudeva-and-Arjuna association and, under the commentators’ interpretation, adherents of each. It says nothing about Draupadī, the war, Gāṇḍīva, or the *Gītā*. **[E1; identification debated]**
2. **Patañjali, *Mahābhāṣya*, c. 150 BCE:** preserves evidence that stories and dramatic representations concerning early epic heroes circulated by his period. It strengthens the general case for an established heroic narrative culture, although not every commonly quoted passage names the Pāṇḍava Arjuna directly. **[E1]**
3. **Spitzer manuscript, approximately 200–230 CE:** a damaged Sanskrit manuscript containing a list of *Mahābhārata* books. It establishes a structured epic but not the verbatim antiquity of every present episode. **[E1]**
4. Claims that Painted Grey Ware, submerged remains at Dvārakā, astronomical calculations, or modern villages named after Pāṇḍavas “prove Arjuna” are not accepted identifications of an individual in mainstream archaeology. Archaeology may illuminate settlements and social worlds later drawn into epic geography; it has produced no inscription, grave, weapon, or body securely identifiable as Arjuna’s. **[E5]**

### The Sanskrit epic

The Sanskrit *Mahābhārata* is a stratified work. A broad scholarly chronology places substantial composition and redaction between roughly **400 BCE and 400 CE**. John Brockington’s developmental model treats it as the product of long growth; Alf Hiltebeitel has argued for a shorter, more purposive written formation around the last centuries BCE. Neither model supplies a date for a historical Arjuna. [A review of the dating problem notes the general absence of evidence for a Sanskrit *Mahābhārata* before 400 BCE](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Aeef9d82e-859c-40f1-afc5-c0a9041c011b/files/m0f9844ae53a7c7af0d04ae0c6f19eb63). **[E5]**

The BORI Critical Edition was prepared from **1,259 manuscripts**, published in nineteen volumes after work extending from 1919 to 1966. It reconstructs the latest common ancestor that editorial comparison can plausibly reach; it is not “Vyāsa’s original.” Its rejected passages remain evidence for regional and later reception. [BORI describes the project, editors, manuscripts, and publication history](https://bori.ac.in/department/mahabharata/). The Sanskrit constituted text is also available electronically through [GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/2_epic/mbh/sas/) and the [electronic critical-edition project](https://bombay.indology.info/mahabharata/welcome.html).

Arjuna’s principal roles by book are:

1. **Ādiparvan:** divine birth; training by Droṇa; rivalry with Karṇa; defeat and capture of Drupada; Draupadī’s contest; shared marriage; exile after entering Yudhiṣṭhira and Draupadī’s chamber; encounters with Ulūpī, Citrāṅgadā, and Subhadrā; Khāṇḍava conflagration.
2. **Sabhāparvan:** northern conquest for Yudhiṣṭhira’s rājasūya; presence at the dice catastrophe, though he does not prevent Draupadī’s humiliation.
3. **Āraṇyakaparvan:** exile; ascent and austerities; combat with Śiva as Kirāta; acquisition of the Pāśupata; journey to Indra’s heaven; Urvaśī’s proposition and curse; combat with the Nivātakavacas and Kālakeyas.
4. **Virāṭaparvan:** Bṛhannalā; teacher of Uttarā; retrieval of weapons; defeat of the Kuru raid.
5. **Udyogaparvan:** negotiations and preparations; Arjuna chooses unarmed Kṛṣṇa rather than Kṛṣṇa’s army.
6. **Bhīṣmaparvan:** despair before battle; the *Bhagavad Gītā*; attack on Bhīṣma under Śikhaṇḍin’s cover.
7. **Droṇaparvan:** death of Abhimanyu; vow to kill Jayadratha; killing of Jayadratha; confrontations with Droṇa, Aśvatthāman, and others.
8. **Karṇaparvan:** climactic duel and killing of Karṇa, prompted by Kṛṣṇa while Karṇa’s chariot wheel is trapped.
9. **Śalya- and Sauptikaparvans:** closing battles and aftermath.
10. **Strī-, Śānti-, and Anuśāsanaparvans:** grief and postwar instruction; Arjuna is no longer the sole narrative center.
11. **Aśvamedhikaparvan:** guards the sacrificial horse; campaigns; is defeated and temporarily killed by Babhruvāhana; revived through Ulūpī’s intervention; hears Kṛṣṇa’s *Anugītā* after admitting that he has forgotten the earlier revelation.
12. **Mausalaparvan:** witnesses the destruction of the Yādavas; Kṛṣṇa’s death; escorts survivors but finds his former power and command of celestial weapons failing; bandits defeat him.
13. **Mahāprasthānika- and Svargārohaṇaparvans:** relinquishes Gāṇḍīva; falls on the Himalayan journey; ultimately belongs among the restored heroes in heaven.

### Other primary corpora

- **Bhagavad Gītā**, *Bhīṣmaparvan* 23–40 in the Critical Edition: eighteen chapters and 700 verses in the standard text. Arjuna is interlocutor, objector, visionary, and finally the warrior who declares his confusion removed. Its independent date remains disputed within the larger epic’s compositional range. **[E2]**
- **Harivaṃśa**, Sanskrit, principally early centuries CE with later additions; critically edited by P. L. Vaidya, 1969. Arjuna appears mainly within Kṛṣṇa’s extended dynastic world. **[E2]**
- **Bhāsa’s plays**, dating disputed, commonly placed in the early centuries CE but known only through manuscripts recovered in the twentieth century. Plays such as *Madhyamavyāyoga*, *Dūtaghaṭotkaca*, *Karṇabhāra*, *Ūrubhaṅga*, and *Pañcarātra* reshape the epic dramatic world; Arjuna is sometimes invoked or peripheral rather than protagonist. **[E3; date disputed]**
- **Bhāravi, *Kirātārjunīya***, Sanskrit *mahākāvya*, probably sixth century CE and certainly known before the Aihole inscription of 634. It magnifies the Kirāta encounter into eighteen cantos of political counsel, ascetic heroism, battle, and verbal virtuosity. [The Aihole terminus and sixth-century placement are summarized here](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp04/chapter/types-of-literature-kavyas-poetry-2/). **[E3]**
- **Purāṇas**, especially the *Bhāgavata*, *Viṣṇu*, *Brahma*, *Skanda*, and *Padma Purāṇas*: variously retell his association with Kṛṣṇa, pilgrimages, and descendants. Their passages require individual dating and cannot simply be treated as “Vedic.” **[E3]**
- **Jaina Mahābhārata traditions:** Jinasena’s ninth-century *Harivaṃśapurāṇa* and later *Pāṇḍavacaritas* reconfigure genealogy, ethics, and marriage. In Jinasena’s account Draupadī is associated solely with Arjuna rather than married to all five brothers. **[E4: sectarian retelling]**
- **Old Javanese *Arjunawiwāha*** by Mpu Kanwa, c. 1030 under Airlangga. It is an original Javanese court poem using Indian materials, not a translation of the Sanskrit *Kirātārjunīya*. It makes Arjuna an ascetic champion who defeats Niwātakawaca and marries heavenly women; scholars have long read him as an idealizing parallel for Airlangga. [The historical edition and study are available through the Library of Congress](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/66/65/82/2019666582/2019666582.pdf), while the interpretation of Arjuna as Airlangga’s exemplar is documented in scholarship on early Javanese historiography. **[E4]**
- **Regional Mahābhāratas:** Tamil *Villibhāratam*; Telugu works beginning with Nannaya’s eleventh-century *Āndhra Mahābhāratamu* and continued by Tikkana and Errāpragaḍa; Kannada traditions including Kumāravyāsa’s fifteenth-century *Karṇāṭa Bhārata Kathāmañjarī*; Bengali Kāśīrām Dās; Malayalam *Bhāratam Pāṭṭu* and theatrical literature; Marathi narrative, devotional, and performance traditions. These alter emphasis, speeches, family relationships, and especially the moral weight of Karṇa and Draupadī. **[E4]**
- **Performance corpora:** Tamil *terukkūttu/Bhārata-kūttu*; Malayalam Kathakali plays including *Kirātam*, *Kālakeyavadham*, and *Subhadrāharaṇam*; Kannada/Tulu Yakshagana plays such as *Kṛṣṇārjuna Kāḷaga*; Javanese and Balinese wayang. **[E4]**

English translations differ materially. K. M. Ganguli’s 1883–1896 translation follows the nineteenth-century Calcutta/Bombay vulgate; J. A. B. van Buitenen and the Clay Sanskrit Library translate substantial portions against modern scholarship; Bibek Debroy translates the BORI constituted text. A claim found in Ganguli but absent from a critical-edition translation may represent a real manuscript variant rather than a translator’s fabrication.

## 3. The story and its variants

### Birth and divine paternity

Pāṇḍu is cursed to die if he has sexual intercourse. Kuntī employs a divine-invocation formula learned from Durvāsas. After Dharma fathers Yudhiṣṭhira and Vāyu fathers Bhīma, **Indra fathers Arjuna**. Celestial voices and portents foretell a supreme archer. Socially he is Pāṇḍu’s son; biologically and theologically the Sanskrit epic calls him Indra’s. **[E2]**

The epic also identifies Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna as **Nārāyaṇa and Nara**. This is a developed theological interpretation within the text, layered over the human genealogy; it does not cancel the Indra paternity story. **[E2: learned theological systematization]**

### Training, Ekalavya, and Drupada

Droṇa recognizes Arjuna’s concentration and promises to make him the foremost archer. When the Niṣāda prince Ekalavya, self-trained before an image of Droṇa, proves exceptionally skilled, Droṇa demands his right thumb as *guru-dakṣiṇā*, protecting his promise to Arjuna. Arjuna benefits from this mutilation even though Droṇa orders it. The episode is not a modern anti-caste invention; it is epic material, while its interpretation as a paradigmatic instance of caste exclusion is modern and regional as well as textual. **[E2; E5 reception]**

In Droṇa’s revenge campaign, Arjuna captures King Drupada. Droṇa divides or returns part of the kingdom after humiliating him. Drupada later seeks a son able to kill Droṇa and a daughter connected to Arjuna. **[E2]**

### Karṇa’s emergence

Karṇa challenges Arjuna at the martial exhibition. His uncertain social identity becomes decisive: he is crowned king of Aṅga through Duryodhana’s intervention. The audience does not yet know openly that both are Kuntī’s sons. Their rivalry henceforth combines skill, status, concealed fraternity, patronage, and incompatible loyalties. **[E2]**

Modern popular debate often treats “Arjuna versus Karṇa” as if the epic supplied an objective power ranking. It does not: the narrative repeatedly changes circumstances, weapons, curses, divine support, and ethical conditions. **[E6: modern competitive framing]**

### Draupadī’s contest: the “fish’s eye”

At Draupadī’s *svayaṃvara*, Arjuna, disguised as a Brahmin, strings the difficult bow and strikes the target, winning her. Kuntī, not seeing what the brothers have brought, tells them to share it; the extraordinary fivefold marriage is then justified through Vyāsa’s narratives of prior destiny. **[E2]**

The universally familiar version in which Arjuna must look downward at a fish’s reflection in oil or water and pierce its rotating eye is **not uniform across the manuscript tradition**. The Critical Edition’s constituted narrative describes a bow and elevated mechanical target; reflection details are prominent in later and regional retellings. “Archer of the fish’s eye” is therefore a valid reception-history title but not a securely invariant detail of the earliest recoverable Sanskrit text. **[E2 variant; E4; E6]**

Draupadī becomes wife to all five brothers, each having an allotted period with her. The story does not make her merely Arjuna’s wife, although some Jaina and regional traditions do. **[E2 versus E4]**

### Pilgrimage, marriages, and coercive contexts

Arjuna enters the chamber where Yudhiṣṭhira is with Draupadī in order to retrieve weapons needed to aid a petitioner. Bound by the brothers’ rule, he accepts exile/pilgrimage. **[E2]**

- **Ulūpī:** the Nāga princess seizes or draws Arjuna into the waters and demands intercourse. Arjuna invokes his celibacy; Ulūpī argues that his obligation applies specifically to Draupadī and that refusing a woman who seeks him would be wrong. The resulting sex is obtained after pressure and argumentative override of his stated reluctance. Calling it a simple romance suppresses the scene’s coercive structure. Their son is Irāvat/Arāvān. **[E2]**
- **Citrāṅgadā:** Arjuna desires the princess of Maṇalūra/Manipura. Her father permits marriage on condition that their son remain heir to the maternal kingdom. Their son is Babhruvāhana. This “Manipura” cannot simply be equated without argument with the modern northeastern state of Manipur; that identification is a later geographical and political claim. **[E2; E5 disputed geography]**
- **Subhadrā:** with Kṛṣṇa’s counsel, Arjuna carries away Kṛṣṇa’s sister. Some tellings emphasize mutual attraction and Subhadrā’s agency; the formal idiom remains *rākṣasa* marriage or abduction. Balarāma is angered and then reconciled. Their son is Abhimanyu. **[E2; recensionally elaborated]**

### Khāṇḍava: alliance and mass killing

Agni seeks to consume the Khāṇḍava forest but is repeatedly prevented by Indra, protector of the serpent Takṣaka. Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna assist Agni. Varuṇa supplies Arjuna with Gāṇḍīva, inexhaustible quivers, and a chariot. Arjuna creates an arrow barrier preventing escape while Kṛṣṇa kills fugitives; animals, Nāgas, and other inhabitants are slaughtered. Maya survives through Arjuna’s protection and later builds the Pāṇḍavas’ assembly hall. **[E2]**

The episode is conquest and mass destruction, not a sanitized demonstration of archery. Modern ecological and postcolonial readings regard it as a myth of violent clearance and sovereignty; that is an interpretive tradition, not an explicit ancient confession of settler colonialism. **[E5]**

### Dice, humiliation, and exile

Arjuna is present when Yudhiṣṭhira loses kingdom, brothers, himself, and Draupadī. He does not prevent Duḥśāsana from dragging and attempting to disrobe her. His restraint is framed through bondage to the wager and hierarchy, but the epic preserves Draupadī’s challenge to that supposed legality. **[E2]**

During forest exile Arjuna undertakes austerities to obtain weapons.

### Śiva as Kirāta and the Pāśupata

A boar/demon named Mūka charges Arjuna. Arjuna and a Kirāta hunter shoot it and dispute whose arrow killed it. Arjuna fights the hunter, is defeated, fashions or worships a liṅga, and recognizes Śiva. Śiva grants him the terrifying **Pāśupata weapon**, which must not be used casually against human beings. **[E2]**

Bhāravi’s sixth-century *Kirātārjunīya* vastly expands this brief epic complex, intensifying Arjuna’s asceticism, political self-command, and duel with Śiva. The poem is an elaboration, not proof that every detail in it belonged to an early oral epic. **[E3]**

### Heaven, Urvaśī, and celestial wars

Indra brings Arjuna to heaven, gives him weapons and a crown, and has him instructed in music and dance by the Gandharva Citrasena. Urvaśī propositions him. Arjuna refuses on the ground that, as an ancestress or former partner in the Lunar lineage, she deserves maternal reverence. She curses him to lose masculinity or live among women; Indra limits the curse to one year, making it useful during the incognito exile. **[E2]**

Arjuna defeats the Nivātakavacas and Kālakeyas for Indra. These battles become especially important in Kathakali’s *Kālakeyavadham* and the Old Javanese *Arjunawiwāha*. **[E2; E4]**

### Bṛhannalā

In Virāṭa’s court Arjuna becomes **Bṛhannalā**, teacher of dance and music to Princess Uttarā. The episode exploits his celestial arts and Urvaśī’s curse. When Kuru forces raid Virāṭa’s cattle, he becomes charioteer and then warrior for the frightened prince Uttara, retrieves the Pāṇḍava weapons from a śamī tree, and defeats the Kuru champions. **[E2]**

When Virāṭa offers Uttarā to Arjuna, he refuses because he has lived as her teacher and regards her as a daughter; she instead marries Abhimanyu. Later devotional claims that Arjuna personally married Uttarā are false. **[E2 versus E6]**

### Kṛṣṇa’s choice and the *Bhagavad Gītā*

Kṛṣṇa offers one side his vast Nārāyaṇī army and the other Kṛṣṇa himself, unarmed. Duryodhana chooses the army; Arjuna chooses Kṛṣṇa, who becomes his charioteer. **[E2]**

At Kurukṣetra Arjuna asks to see those assembled, recognizes teachers, kin, and friends, drops his bow, and rejects a victory purchased by their deaths. Kṛṣṇa answers with teachings on the imperishable self, disciplined action without attachment to its fruits, knowledge, devotion, divine embodiment, and duty. He reveals the cosmic form. Arjuna eventually says that his delusion has been destroyed and that he will act. **[E2]**

Interpretations conflict:

- Śaṅkara, eighth century, integrates the dialogue into non-dualist renunciation through knowledge. **[E3]**
- Rāmānuja, eleventh–twelfth century, reads it through devotion and qualified non-dualism. **[E3]**
- Tilak’s *Gītā Rahasya* (written 1910–1911; published 1915) makes Arjuna emblematic of activist duty. **[E6: nationalist-era exegesis]**
- Gandhi’s *Anāsaktiyoga* reads the battlefield allegorically and emphasizes nonattachment and nonviolence, despite the narrative’s literal war. **[E6]**
- Aurobindo reads Arjuna as the human instrument of divine action. **[E6]**

No single one exhausts the ancient text.

### Bhīṣma’s fall

Bhīṣma refuses fully to fight Śikhaṇḍin, whom he treats as having been female. Kṛṣṇa urges use of this vulnerability. Arjuna shoots from behind or in conjunction with Śikhaṇḍin until Bhīṣma falls onto a bed of arrows. Arjuna supplies an arrow-pillow and shoots the earth to produce water for him. **[E2]**

Arjuna is therefore Bhīṣma’s effective killer, but Śikhaṇḍin is indispensable; accounts crediting only one erase the tactic’s structure.

### Abhimanyu and Jayadratha

After Abhimanyu is trapped and killed by several warriors, Arjuna vows to kill Jayadratha before sunset the next day or enter fire. Kṛṣṇa guides the chariot, manages the crisis, and the narrative uses obscured light or the appearance of sunset to expose Jayadratha. Arjuna decapitates him with a specialized shot that carries the head to Jayadratha’s father. **[E2]**

### Karṇa’s death

In the seventeenth-day duel, Karṇa’s prior curses mature: his chariot wheel sinks and knowledge fails him. Karṇa asks for the warrior’s rule that combat pause. Kṛṣṇa answers by listing Karṇa’s complicity in Draupadī’s humiliation, Abhimanyu’s killing, and other wrongs, and orders Arjuna to shoot. Arjuna kills Karṇa while he is impeded, using the Añjalika weapon. **[E2]**

The epic presents the act simultaneously as vengeance, fulfillment of destiny, and breach or suspension of ordinary battle ethics. Versions in which Karṇa survives to give a final golden tooth, dies in Kuntī’s lap, or undergoes a prolonged final charity scene are regional, theatrical, cinematic, or devotional expansions, not the Critical Edition’s account. **[E4; E6]**

### Aśvamedha, Babhruvāhana, and temporary death

After the war, Arjuna follows Yudhiṣṭhira’s sacrificial horse and fights rulers who detain it. At Manipura, he rebukes Babhruvāhana’s peaceful welcome and insists on combat. Babhruvāhana defeats and kills or renders him lifeless. Citrāṅgadā laments; Ulūpī revives him using a Nāga jewel or life-restoring means. She explains that the event expiates Arjuna’s role in Bhīṣma’s death, following a curse or judgment by the Vasus. **[E2]**

This is Arjuna’s first narrative “death,” explicitly reversed. It must not be harmonized with the final Himalayan fall.

### Loss of power

After Kṛṣṇa’s death and the Yādava civil destruction, Arjuna escorts women and survivors from Dvārakā. Ābhīra bandits attack. He cannot recall celestial weapon formulas or use Gāṇḍīva effectively, and survivors are abducted. Vyāsa explains that the allotted time of Kṛṣṇa, Arjuna, and their divine weapons has ended. **[E2]**

The episode deliberately dismantles the invincible-warrior image: power belonged to a cosmic period and alliance, not to an autonomous permanent ability.

### Final journey and fall

The Pāṇḍavas renounce rule and walk north with Draupadī and a dog. Arjuna falls after Draupadī, Sahadeva, and Nakula. Bhīma asks why; Yudhiṣṭhira answers that Arjuna had boasted he could destroy all enemies in a single day and had disparaged other archers. Pride and unfulfilled boast cause his fall. No secure early source identifies a tomb, relic, or historically recovered body. **[E2]**

The heavenly conclusion reunites the heroes in transformed divine identities. It is not a terrestrial burial report.

## 5. Cult, tomb, image, and ritual

### Independent cult

There is **no securely excavated ancient heroon, tomb, reliquary, or inscription identifying a burial of the Pāṇḍava Arjuna**. Locations advertised as Arjuna’s tomb, cave, footprint, or place of penance are sacred-geographical claims whose earliest documentation must be assessed individually. Place-name continuity does not authenticate the epic event. **[E5]**

Pāṇini’s rule may imply early adherents of an Arjuna paired with Vāsudeva, but the exact religious status is disputed. Later Vaiṣṇava tradition overwhelmingly centers Kṛṣṇa rather than an autonomous Arjuna cult. **[E1; disputed]**

### Temple and festival incorporation

Arjuna appears as a member of the Pāṇḍava ensemble in Draupadī Amman temples and regional shrines. A bronze Arjuna in the Draupadī temple at Kumbakonam reportedly bears a pedestal inscription dated **1873**; this is firm evidence for a nineteenth-century cult image, not for an ancient shrine founded by Arjuna. [The relevant iconographic report describes the bronze and inscription](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/33185.pdf). **[E4]**

The **Draupadī cult of northern Tamil Nadu** uses weeks-long epic recitation, *terukkūttu*, fire-walking, battlefield construction, and possession. Arjuna’s penance is enacted through climbing the **tapas tree** to obtain Śiva’s weapon. The ritual cycle belongs to the Draupadī-centered temple complex rather than proving a separate pan-Indian Arjuna cult. [A documentary account describes the *tapas* tree and Pāśupata performance](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-18-issue-3-oct-dec-2022-theemithi-firewalking-festival/). **[E4]**

Arjuna’s son **Arāvān/Kūttāṇṭavar** has a more sharply individualized Tamil cult. At Koovagam and related sites, ritual traditions stage his prewar sacrifice and marriage to Kṛṣṇa as Mohinī; transgender women participate as ritual brides and mourners. These forms substantially expand the comparatively brief Sanskrit Irāvat narrative. They are genuine regional religious history, not corruptions to be discarded or automatic evidence for the episode’s prehistoric antiquity. **[E4]**

### Kerala

- **Arjuna Nṛttam/Mayilpīli Tūkkam:** a ritual dance associated with Bhagavatī/Kālī temples, performed in peacock-feather costume. Its origin is mythically attributed to Arjuna dancing in devotion after battle; this is ritual aetiology, not an episode securely found in the Critical Edition. [Kerala Tourism documents the living form and its traditional origin claim](https://www.keralatourism.org/artforms/arjuna-nritham/8/). **[E4]**
- Kathakali *Kirātam* stages Arjuna’s pride, duel, and recognition of Śiva. *Kālakeyavadham* treats his celestial visit and demon war; *Subhadrāharaṇam* dramatizes the marriage/abduction. **[E4]**

### Visual evidence

The seventh-century Pallava “Great Relief” at Māmallapuram is traditionally called **Arjuna’s Penance**, but specialists have long disputed whether it represents Arjuna obtaining the Pāśupata, Bhagīratha bringing down the Gaṅgā, or a deliberate visual fusion of both. The natural cleft, Nāgas, ascetic figure, and Śiva permit more than one reading. It must not be cited as an uncontested portrait of Arjuna. [The ANU catalogue records the competing identification](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/entities/anuarchivesitem/40d98b95-eb3a-4337-acf9-61ad5d1ac6ef). **[E3; disputed iconography]**

Later South and Southeast Asian reliefs more securely identify scenes through narrative sequence: Kirāta combat, the *Gītā* chariot, Abhimanyu’s death, or Arjuna’s marriages. Javanese temple reliefs and Balinese Kamasan paintings use local costume and physiognomy and document regional reception, not Iron Age Indian dress.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Possible pre-epic heroic association: fourth–second centuries BCE

Pāṇini’s Vāsudeva-Arjuna pairing supplies the earliest plausible named trace. Whether the two were already understood exactly as Kṛṣṇa and the Pāṇḍava is probable to many scholars but not demonstrable from the rule alone. **[E1]**

### Epic formation: c. 400 BCE–200 CE

A martial Kuru-Pāñcāla narrative developed into a dynastic and didactic epic. Arjuna became its premier archer, Indra’s son, Kṛṣṇa’s intimate, husband within an anomalous polyandrous marriage, recipient of divine weapons, and principal battlefield agent. **[E2/E5]**

Scholars disagree over how to model that development. The older “heroic core plus successive Brahmanical accretions” model remains useful but can become circular when every theological passage is simply declared late. Hiltebeitel instead emphasizes a more unified authorial-redactional design. **[E5]**

### Theological consolidation: early centuries CE

The *Bhagavad Gītā* makes Arjuna the exemplary human recipient of divine instruction. Nara-Nārāyaṇa identification transforms friendship with Kṛṣṇa into cosmic complementarity. The *Harivaṃśa* embeds him further in Kṛṣṇa’s extended sacred history. **[E2]**

### Classical Sanskrit literature: c. second–seventh centuries

Drama redistributes sympathy among defeated characters; Bhāravi turns the Kirāta episode into a monument of courtly poetics. Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava readings coexist: Arjuna is Kṛṣṇa’s chosen companion yet must submit to Śiva for the Pāśupata. **[E3]**

### Temple and transregional epic culture: seventh–fifteenth centuries

Pallava, Cāḷukya, Khmer, Javanese, and Balinese visual cultures localized the epic. The Old Javanese *Arjunawiwāha* made Arjuna an ascetic royal ideal and possible analogue for Airlangga. Wayang developed the refined, slender, self-controlled Arjuna, often more erotically prolific and genealogically expansive than his Sanskrit counterpart. Studies of Javanese puppetry note that his visual masculinity is deliberately “halus”—soft-spoken and refined rather than physically massive. **[E4]**

Vernacular Mahābhāratas made him available to new courts, sects, performance communities, and castes. Some centered Karṇa more strongly, thereby making Arjuna morally less secure.

### Early-modern ritual and theatre

Tamil Draupadī festivals, Kerala Kathakali, Karnataka Yakshagana, shadow theatre, ballads, and pilgrimage legends reorganized the story as annually repeatable ritual. Arjuna might be hero, flawed husband, ritual ascetic, dancer, or foil to Karṇa. **[E4]**

### Colonial philology and print: 1785–1947

Charles Wilkins’s 1785 English *Bhagvat-Geeta* detached the Kṛṣṇa-Arjuna dialogue from the epic for a global readership. European philosophers frequently treated Arjuna as a universal moral subject while minimizing the dialogue’s caste, kinship, and war setting. Sanskrit print editions and Ganguli’s English translation made a broad vulgate Arjuna widely accessible. **[E6]**

Indian nationalist readers recast him as the citizen paralyzed before necessary action. Tilak emphasized activist *karma-yoga*; Gandhi allegorized the conflict and internalized its violence; Aurobindo made Arjuna an instrument of divine transformation. These are historically consequential readings, not neutral recoveries of one original meaning.

### Critical-edition era: 1919–1966

BORI’s project exposed extensive northern and southern variation. It made it impossible to use “the original Mahābhārata” casually: many famous details occur only in limited manuscript branches, while the constituted text is itself an editorial hypothesis. **[E5]**

### Broadcast and digital era: 1988–present

B. R. Chopra’s Hindi *Mahabharat*, first broadcast from 1988 to 1990, gave Arjuna—played by Firoz Khan, subsequently professionally known as Arjun—a nationally standardized face. Its ninety-plus episodes turned the epic into synchronized mass viewing. Scholarship argues that the serial circulated a Hindi-speaking, upper-caste-coded Hindu national image; this concerns the effect of the representation, not proof that every viewer accepted a single ideology. [Ananda Mitra’s study describes the serial’s hegemonic national image](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01296612.1993.11726404), while later work examines its visual coding of Pāṇḍava purity and morally authorized violence ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10304312.2016.1231791)). **[E6]**

Digital fandom has intensified competitive Arjuna-versus-Karṇa debates, often flattening manuscript variation into claims about “power levels.” Assertions that the Critical Edition simply removed all supernatural or devotional content are false; it retains both extensively.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **Rabindranath Tagore, *Chitrā*/*Chitrāṅgadā*:** poetic drama in 1892 and dance-drama revision in 1935. Tagore transforms Citrāṅgadā into a meditation on gender, beauty, autonomy, and recognition. These psychological developments are modern, though rooted in the epic marriage. [Oxford scholarship dates and compares the two versions](https://academic.oup.com/book/10799/chapter/158937713). **[E6]**
- **Sri Aurobindo:** poems and essays use Arjuna in evolutionary and spiritual-nationalist interpretation. **[E6]**
- **Peter Brook and Jean-Claude Carrière, *The Mahabharata*:** stage production premiered in 1985; film/television version 1989. Arjuna became part of an intentionally international ensemble, provoking both admiration and criticism concerning universalism and cultural appropriation. **[E6]**
- Numerous modern novels retell the war from Draupadī’s, Karṇa’s, Bhīma’s, or marginal characters’ viewpoints; these often deliberately demote or criticize Arjuna.

### Film and television

Important visual reinventions include:

- *Bhakta Vidur* (1921) and early Indian silent mythological cinema, within which epic characters acquired nationalist resonance.
- *Mayabazar* (Telugu/Tamil, 1957), centered on the Abhimanyu–Śaśirekha regional story; Arjuna belongs to its larger dynastic world, while the romance is not the Critical Edition’s plot.
- B. R. Chopra’s *Mahabharat* (1988–1990).
- Peter Brook’s screen *Mahabharata* (1989).
- Star Plus *Mahabharat* (2013–2014), with Shaheer Sheikh as Arjuna, emphasizing youth, romance, and Kṛṣṇa-guided destiny.
- Animated films, devotional serials, and streaming adaptations frequently import later fish-reflection, final-charity Karṇa, and romantic material while presenting it as a single ancient canon. **[E6]**

### Political uses

The *Gītā’s* Arjuna became a flexible political emblem:

- For activist nationalists, the hesitant patriot awakened to necessary action.
- For Gandhi, the human conscience confronting an internal moral battlefield.
- For Hindu nationalist media culture, one member of a righteous Hindu civilizational army.
- For critics of militarism, the figure through whom sacred discourse legitimates kin-killing and caste duty.

The 1988 serial was broadcast amid the expansion of Hindu-nationalist mass politics. Causation should not be reduced to “the serial created Hindutva,” but television helped produce a shared visual religious public. **[E6]**

### State and commercial naming

- India’s **Arjuna Award**, instituted in 1961, invokes the archer as an emblem of discipline and sporting excellence. It is a modern state commemoration, not continuation of an ancient athletic festival.
- “Arjun” is widely used for military hardware, sports teams, businesses, films, and given names. Such uses selectively invoke precision, virility, or victory.
- Modern missiles are sometimes described online as literal equivalents of divine *astras*. This is analogy or nationalist pseudohistory; no ancient text documents nuclear, guided, or aerospace technology. **[E6]**
- Games and anime franchises, notably *Fate/Grand Order*, detach Arjuna and Karṇa from epic sequence and recast them as summonable superhuman rivals. **[E6]**

### Psychological and comparative readings

- **Otto Rank/Raglan hero-pattern approaches:** Arjuna fits some heroic-birth motifs but not a clean universal pattern; selecting only matching incidents produces circularity.
- **Joseph Campbell:** the “hero’s journey” can be imposed on exile, descent, weapon acquisition, and return, but the *Mahābhārata* has multiple centers and does not reduce to Arjuna’s individual self-realization.
- **Georges Dumézil:** read the Pāṇḍavas through Indo-European trifunctionality, with Arjuna associated with warrior sovereignty/Indra. The system illuminates divine fathers but strains over Arjuna’s many functions. A later scholarly discussion calls Arjuna a particular “Dumézilian crux” ([Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/arjuna-and-the-second-function-adumezilian-crux/A6CE9135E18B188F6F190689724C03F7)).
- **Alf Hiltebeitel:** emphasizes the epic’s ritual architecture, Draupadī, sacrificial war, and purposeful literary design rather than only a mechanically accreted heroic core.
- **Madeleine Biardeau:** interprets epic narrative through sacrifice, kingship, and Hindu theological structure.
- **John Brockington:** reconstructs linguistic and compositional development across the Sanskrit epics.
- **Wendy Doniger and gender-focused scholarship:** attend to ambiguity, sexuality, disguise, and the contradictions of dharma.
- **Dalit and anti-caste readings:** foreground Arjuna’s benefit from Ekalavya’s mutilation and the epic order’s exclusionary hierarchy.
- **Queer and transgender readings:** treat Bṛhannalā, Urvaśī’s curse, and Arāvān ritual as resources for non-binary histories. Responsible scholarship distinguishes ancient categories from modern identity terminology.

## 8. Historiography and contested material

### Was Arjuna historical?

No contemporary inscription, document, securely identified grave, or datable portrait confirms an individual matching epic Arjuna. A historical Kuru conflict or remembered warrior traditions may lie behind parts of the epic, but moving from “the epic preserves historical geography or social memory” to “Arjuna lived in year X” is unjustified. Archaeological scholarship describes epic historicity as a continuing, heated debate rather than a solved identification ([*Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/two-indian-epics-visavis-archaeology/1FF65AC988B8C4D3391920BF562B2A98)). **[E5]**

Astronomical dates extending from the fourth millennium BCE to the first millennium BCE depend on selecting among contradictory celestial notices and assuming that passages are contemporaneous observations rather than redactional literary data. They do not independently authenticate Arjuna.

### “Jaya, Bhārata, Mahābhārata”

A popular model speaks of an 8,800-verse *Jaya*, 24,000-verse *Bhārata*, and 100,000-verse *Mahābhārata*. The epic itself supplies these numbers, but interpreting them as three securely recoverable historical editions is contested; Brockington and others warn that the relevant verses do not straightforwardly document three surviving textual stages. **[E2 tradition; E5 disputed reconstruction]**

### Oral versus written formation

The epic represents bardic recitation and contains formulaic diction, repetition, and variation compatible with oral performance. Older scholarship therefore envisioned a long oral heroic core. Hiltebeitel argues that the large-scale design points to literary composition and redaction over a more concentrated period. The evidence supports interaction between performance and writing better than an absolute oral/written dichotomy.

### The Critical Edition’s limits

The BORI editors did not identify “what really happened.” They compared manuscript families and constituted readings judged ancestral. A passage excluded from the main text can still be old; a passage included can still be composite. The oldest complete or nearly complete manuscripts are many centuries later than the main compositional period. Climatic loss, repeated copying, sectarian use, and regional rewriting created the record now available.

### Fish-eye controversy

- **Secure:** Arjuna wins the difficult archery contest.
- **Variable:** exact apparatus, revolving fish, reflection, water/oil, and instruction to look only at the reflection.
- **Finding:** the elaborate reflected-fish-eye test is regional and receptionally dominant but not uniform early evidence.

### Karṇa’s morality

The Critical Edition does not sustain either modern extreme: Karṇa is neither merely a flawless victim murdered by a jealous Arjuna nor merely an unambiguous villain. He suffers concealment and status humiliation, gives lavishly, remains loyal to Duryodhana, participates in Draupadī’s abuse and Abhimanyu’s killing, and dies under ethically compromised conditions. Marathi and other performance traditions often heighten his generosity and tragedy, shifting audience sympathy away from Arjuna. **[E2; E4]**

### Arjuna and “Manipur”

The epic place-name Maṇalūra/Manipura has been connected with several regions. Identifying Citrāṅgadā straightforwardly as a princess of the modern northeastern Manipur state is important in modern regional identity but geographically disputed by philologists and historians. The epic’s pilgrimage itinerary does not settle the question unambiguously. **[E5/E6]**

### Tombs and relics

Dedicated searches disclose no archaeologically authenticated Arjuna tomb or bodily relic. “Arjuna caves,” “Arjuna rock,” “Arjuna’s footprint,” the “Arjuna Ratha,” and similarly named sites generally reflect later naming. The monolithic “Arjuna Ratha” at Māmallapuram is a modern conventional monument-name and was not constructed as Arjuna’s burial place. **[E3/E6]**

### Cult

Possible Pāṇinian devotees, later Pāṇḍava ensemble worship, and ritual impersonation are documented. A continuous, autonomous, pan-Indian Arjuna cult from the fourth century BCE to the present is not. Evidence is discontinuous and often centered on Kṛṣṇa, Draupadī, Śiva, or Arāvān.

### Nationalism

Arjuna’s usefulness to nationalism comes especially through the *Gītā*: hesitation can be overcome and violent duty sacralized. Yet nationalist readings range from revolutionary activism to Gandhi’s internalized nonviolence. It is inaccurate to label the ancient figure inherently nationalist; nationalism is a modern political framework. **[E6]**

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “NASA proved the date of Arjuna’s war.” **Modern invention.**
- “Gāṇḍīva was a nuclear weapon” or *astras* were guided missiles. **Modern technological euhemerism.**
- “The fish-reflection contest is word-for-word identical in every manuscript.” **False.**
- “Arjuna married Uttarā.” **Contradicted by the epic; she marries Abhimanyu.**
- “The BORI edition is Vyāsa’s autograph.” **False.**
- “Arjuna founded Java’s historical royal dynasties.” **Courtly myth and later genealogical legitimation, not established political history.**
- “Māmallapuram’s relief unquestionably depicts Arjuna.” **Iconographically disputed.**
- “A located tomb proves Arjuna’s remains.” **No authenticated archaeological evidence.**
- “Bṛhannalā maps without remainder onto one modern gender identity.” **Anachronistically certain; modern communities may legitimately receive the figure while historians preserve categorical difference.**

The documentary conclusion is firm but limited: **Arjuna is securely recoverable as a named heroic figure by the late first millennium BCE or early classical period, fully elaborated in the Sanskrit epic compiled across several centuries, and continuously re-created in theology, court poetry, temple performance, regional epic, nationalism, television, and digital culture. No evidence presently verifies his individual historical life, tomb, body, exact war date, or modern technological arsenal. The absence of those verifications does not diminish the exceptionally rich history of the figure as story, image, ritual presence, and moral problem.**
