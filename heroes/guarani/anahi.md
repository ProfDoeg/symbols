# Anahí: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal Indigenous heroine and etiological plant-transformee; in modern Argentine civic folklore, a martyr/culture-symbol of Guaraní resistance. She is **not securely attested as a deity, demigod, saint, historical cacica, or recipient of cult**.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Argentina’s Decree 13,847 of 23 December 1942 says that the ceibo had been “evoked in Indigenous legends” and celebrated by poets and musicians, but does **not** name Anahí or narrate her story. The earliest securely datable, named public form established in the accessible record is Osvaldo Sosa Cordero’s song **“Anahí,” incorporated into Argentina’s school repertory in 1943**. A demonstrably earlier Indigenous or colonial text naming Anahí has not been located.
>
> **Central evidentiary finding:** The familiar story is best documented as a twentieth-century Argentine littoral legend, disseminated through song, school readers, folklore books, government culture, and tourism. Its regular attribution to an immemorial “Guaraní legend” is not corroborated by the early Guaraní dictionaries, Jesuit records, or Indigenous religious texts examined here. That does not invalidate it as folklore; it dates the form in which it is presently known.

## Evidentiary key

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary documentary evidence.
- **[LCE] Later canonical elaboration:** the stabilized literary/school form.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically differentiated retelling.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** folkloristic, lexicographic, educational, or institutional classification.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation with another religious or legendary figure.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally transmitted or represented as oral tradition, with collection circumstances stated where known.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an academic inference, not a primary tradition.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern attribution:** a recent element presented as old without early evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Anahí is the heroine of an Argentine littoral legend explaining the ceibo’s red flower. In its canonical version she is a young Guaraní woman living beside the Paraná: physically “ugly” or coarse-featured but possessed of an exceptionally beautiful singing voice. Spanish invaders capture her; she escapes after killing or wounding a guard, is recaptured, and is burned at a tree or stake. At dawn her body—or the tree to which she was tied—has become the flowering ceibo. **[LCE: securely documented in twentieth- and twenty-first-century school, literary, and governmental transmission.]**

The story’s historical setting is the Spanish conquest, but no date, expedition, settlement, named Spaniard, or identifiable Guaraní community belongs consistently to the tradition. It is therefore an etiological legend placed in a generalized colonial past, not a recoverable episode of conquest history.

### Name and orthography

Forms include **Anahí**, **Anahi**, and occasionally **Anaí/Anai**. Modern Guaraní-oriented renderings may call the tale *Anai mombe’upy*, “the story/account of Anai,” but that title is itself a modern translation or pedagogical adaptation rather than an early textual witness.

The spelling **Anahí** is Spanish-oriented. A Corrientes museum notice observes that it would conventionally be pronounced approximately *Anají* in Spanish phonetics, with an aspirated Guaraní *h*. That observation concerns pronunciation, not proof of ancient origin.

Published popular etymologies include:

- “the smallest/youngest in the family,” sometimes analyzed as **anã**, “relative/kinswoman,” plus diminutive **-’i**;
- “little one” or “little relative”;
- “flower of the ceibo,” “princess,” “beautiful like the ceibo,” or “song of heaven.”

Only the first group even attempts a Guaraní morphological analysis, and the analysis remains uncertain. **[LS/MSR]** “Flower of the ceibo” is a back-formation from the legend, not a demonstrated lexical meaning. Modern Guaraní sources identify the ceibo by plant names such as **syiñandy/zuinandý**, not *Anahí*. **[MI]** “Princess” and “flower of ceibo” should not be repeated as dictionary definitions without qualification.

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639), the foundational Classical Guaraní dictionary, provides an essential control: no evidence located in its digitized record establishes **Anahí** as this heroine, as a ceibo-name, or as an ancient divine name. The same negative result applies to the accessible descriptions of Montoya’s related lexicographic work. Absence from a dictionary cannot prove that no person bore a similar name, but it defeats claims that the legend is documented there. **[SEA: negative evidence.]**

### Titles and epithets

The following are modern narrative labels, not ancient epithets:

- **“la indiecita fea”** — “the ugly little Indian girl”; common in school and civic retellings. **[LCE]**
- **“la de la dulce voz”** — “she of the sweet voice.” **[LCE]**
- **“reina india” / “princesa guaraní”** — “Indian queen” or “Guaraní princess.” These appear in later popular retellings; most versions furnish no royal genealogy. **[MI/LCE]**
- **“guerrera”** — warrior woman; explicit in the song and later nationalist readings, but not independently documented as a precolonial title. **[LCE]**
- **“flor del ceibo”** — metonymic identification of woman and flower. **[LCE]**

No patronymic, clan-name, father’s name, mother’s name, or historically attested title is stable.

### Identifications

Anahí is identified with:

1. **the ceibo tree or its flower**—the defining metamorphosis; **[LCE/F]**;
2. **the soul inhabiting the flower**—some versions say the flower is her soul rather than her transformed body; **[RV]**;
3. **the “soul of the Guaraní race/people” or of the Argentine nation**—a civic-national allegory especially strong after the ceibo became the national flower; **[LS/MI]**;
4. **a “queen” or “princess”**—later romantic elevation; **[LCE/MI]**.

She is not securely identified with a Guaraní goddess, saint, or historically named colonial woman.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The 1942 decree

Decree 13,847/42, signed on 23 December 1942, declared the ceibo Argentina’s national flower. Its preamble says the flower had been evoked in *“leyendas aborígenes”* and celebrated by poets and in musical pieces. It also cites popular polls, the plant’s distribution and resilience, the red color’s perceived relation to the national arms, and the practice of planting ceibos beside flagpoles. **[SEA]**

What the decree does **not** say is equally important: it does not mention Anahí, Spaniards, execution, metamorphosis, Guaraní authorship, Corrientes, or Misiones. Consequently, it attests the governmental recognition of unspecified “Indigenous legends” about the ceibo, not this precise narrative.

### Osvaldo Sosa Cordero’s “Anahí”

José Osvaldo Sosa Cordero (1906–1986), a Corrientes poet, composer, performer, and illustrator, gave the legend its nationally influential verbal and musical form. Documentary accounts agree that **“Anahí” entered the repertory transmitted in Argentine schools in 1943**. Sosa Cordero recorded for Odeón from 1942, although the precise matrix and first commercial issue of “Anahí” require discographical verification. **[SEA/LCE]**

The song establishes or popularizes the compact canonical portrait: Anahí as a woman of the Guaraní people, remembered in the ceibo, with red flowers figured as blood and resistance. Because lyrics are copyrighted, they are not reproduced here. The song’s institutional adoption explains why generations remember a nearly uniform “oral” story: school and mass media repeatedly returned it to oral circulation.

Ramona Galarza’s performance in the 1960s became one of the best-known recorded versions. Revista *Folklore* printed Sosa Cordero material in issue 35 (8 January 1963), discussed the genesis of his work in issue 37 (5 February 1963), and printed “Anahí” with an extensive discography in issue 272 (August 1977). **[LCE]**

### Folklore documentation

Guillermo Perkins Hidalgo’s **“Leyendas y supersticiones del Iberá,”** published in volume 4 of the *Cuadernos del Instituto Nacional de Antropología* in 1963, is repeatedly cited as a field-based source involving local informants. **[F/LS]** It postdates the 1942–43 nationalization of the ceibo and song, so an Anahí account recorded then cannot by itself establish pre-song antiquity. Its value is evidence that the story circulated in the Iberá region by the mid-twentieth century.

Berta Elena Vidal de Battini’s work is methodologically important. Her ten-volume *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina* preserves more than 3,000 oral narratives gathered over decades and attempts faithful transcription rather than literary beautification. Her plant-legend work and collections are appropriate comparanda for tracing regional transmission. **[F/LS]** However, the accessible catalogue and searchable material consulted here did not establish a pre-1942 Anahí text or a securely proven Anahí item in the way often implied by unspecific modern citations.

Susana Chertudi’s *Cuentos folklóricos de la Argentina* (1960) and later folklore studies are similarly important to the history of Argentine collection and classification. The accessible bibliographic and indexed records did not reveal an Anahí text that predates Sosa Cordero. **[LS: negative finding.]**

Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s writings on Misiones preserve such figures as Kaá-Yarí, Caá-Porá, Ahó-Ahó, Yasí-Yateré, Mboi-Tatá, and associated beliefs. The accessible table of contents and bibliographic record do not document Anahí. Citing Ambrosetti as though he collected her story is therefore presently unsupported. **[SEA: negative evidence.]**

Javier Villafañe is sometimes named in generic bibliographies of Argentine legends, but the searches conducted here did not locate a primary Villafañe Anahí text. Such attribution should remain unverified unless a title, edition, and page are supplied.

### Cadogan and Indigenous Guaraní religious texts

León Cadogan’s Mbyá-Guaraní research is indispensable for Indigenous cosmology, sacred speech, and myth. Yet no Anahí-ceibo narrative was located in the accessible records of his corpus. The absence matters because casual summaries sometimes class Anahí beside documented Guaraní divine beings. She should instead be classified as a littoral colonial-contact legend unless an Indigenous-language field text is produced. **[SEA/MSR: negative finding.]**

### Jesuit and colonial records

Montoya and the Jesuit mission corpus document Guaraní language and many features of seventeenth-century life. No securely identified Jesuit account of a captive woman Anahí burned by Spaniards and transformed into a ceibo was found. Nor was a colonial trial, execution record, martyr narrative, place-name, or eyewitness chronicle located. **[SEA: absence of corroboration.]**

### School-reader corpus

The most durable prose form appears in school readers and instructional materials. A representative Argentine educational version calls Anahí an *“indiecita fea, de rasgos toscos”* with a voice that delighted her Guaraní community; Spaniards seize the people and their land; she kills a guard during escape; the Spaniards hunt her down and burn her; the ceibo flowers embody bravery and resistance. **[LCE]**

Modern readers may add:

- that she is a chief’s daughter or queen;
- that the guard dies rather than merely being struck;
- that she is accused of witchcraft;
- that she sings during the burning;
- that the fire refuses at first to touch her;
- that the tree appears only at dawn;
- that the flower is her soul;
- that Tupã directly effects the transformation.

These are variant elaborations, not components of a single recoverable original.

## 3. The story

### Origins and family

No early source gives Anahí’s parents. **[F/LCE]** She belongs to a Guaraní community along the Paraná. Later versions make her the chief’s daughter, a princess, or queen. **[RV/MI]** These royal titles romanticize Indigenous political organization and should not be backdated.

The name is sometimes explained within the narrative as “the youngest of the family.” **[LCE/MSR]** No siblings are normally named.

### Appearance and voice

The canonical contrast is between an “ugly,” small, or coarse-featured body and a beautiful voice. **[LCE]** This device makes inward or artistic beauty defeat colonial contempt; it also repeats racialized schoolbook language about Indigenous women. Some recent adaptations omit “ugly” or recast it as the Spaniards’ prejudiced judgment. **[MI: modern ethical revision.]**

Her songs concern her people, gods, warriors, homeland, forest, and freedom. No early version preserves the supposed song in Guaraní. Spanish-language poetry and prose supply its content retrospectively.

### Invasion and capture

White, bearded, armored strangers arrive by river or invade the forest. In a Misiones retelling, their vessel is perceived as a huge white bird, their firearms as thunder, and mounted men as two-headed or four-legged monsters. **[RV/LCE]** This is a modern literary focalization imagining first contact.

Anahí either fights as a warrior or is simply captured when her community is defeated. **[RV]** The Spaniards take Indigenous land, idols, and liberty in explicitly anticolonial school versions.

### Escape and the guard

At night she loosens her bonds or takes advantage of a sleeping sentry. The variants are:

- she strikes or wounds him and escapes;
- she stabs him in the chest;
- she kills him outright;
- he cries out while dying and alerts the camp.

She is pursued and recaptured. **[LCE/RV]** The tradition does not name the guard. Versions describing a trial, a captain, or a formal accusation of witchcraft are later narrative elaborations.

### Execution

The Spaniards condemn her to burning, usually in vengeance for the guard’s death. She is tied to a tree or stake and surrounded with wood. **[LCE]** This is killing by colonial captors, not an Indigenous sacrifice.

Her conduct varies:

- she remains silent, head inclined;
- she sings as the flames rise;
- she utters a threatening or funerary murmur;
- she prays to Tupã for her people and land;
- the flames appear reluctant to touch her;
- she actively changes into the tree before death;
- her burned body disappears and a ceibo is discovered at dawn.

These mutually incompatible details should not be harmonized.

### Metamorphosis

By morning, a ceibo stands where she died, with glossy green leaves and red velvety flowers. The red signifies one or more of:

- the flames;
- Anahí’s blood;
- courage;
- her people’s suffering;
- the persistence of Guaraní identity;
- Argentina’s patriotic red.

**[LCE/LS]**

Some versions say Anahí’s **body became the tree**; others that her **soul entered the flower**; still others that a merciful god transformed her or caused the ceibo to grow. **[RV]**

### Function

The tale functions as:

1. a plant-origin legend explaining the red ceibo blossom;
2. a colonial-resistance martyr story;
3. a national allegory after 1942;
4. a school lesson about bravery, cultural identity, and respect for nature;
5. a vehicle for littoral regional identity.

The first function is presented as traditional; the latter three are demonstrably modern institutional uses.

### Every documented interaction with another named god or hero

- **Tupã/Tupá** — In some modern pedagogical and devotional variants Anahí sings or prays to Tupã during her execution, and Tupã hears her or transforms her into the ceibo. Modern school and religious retellings, chiefly late twentieth or twenty-first century. **Evidence tier: [RV/MI].** Tupã is absent from many canonical tellings; the interaction must not be projected backward.

No other named god or hero interacts with Anahí in the sources examined.

In particular:

- **Morotí and Pitá**, heroes of an irupé-origin tale, do not interact with Anahí. Their story is a separate plant-metamorphosis narrative set by the Paraná. Juxtaposition in flower-legend anthologies is not narrative contact.
- **Kaá-Yarí, Pombero, Yasí-Yateré, Kurupí, Mboi-Tatá, and other named Guaraní beings** do not interact with her in the documented story.
- Claims that Anahí was one of the children, priestesses, or chosen humans of a Guaraní deity are unsupported modern expansions.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

No shrine, tomb, relic, burial, annual sacrifice, oracle, feast, pilgrimage, or votive practice devoted to Anahí is securely documented. **[SEA: negative finding.]** There is no archaeological “heroon” and no identified execution site.

The ceibo itself has civic-symbolic uses. The 1942 decree records that institutions planted ceibos beside flagpoles and treated the tree as a national emblem. That is **civic commemoration of the plant**, not evidence of an Anahí cult.

Modern commemorative manifestations include:

- school recitations and musical performances;
- sculptures and public-art presentations, including Roberto Svanotti’s wooden work for Corrientes’ 2021 meeting of sculptors on local myths and legends;
- naming of neighborhoods, associations, cultural groups, businesses, and comparsas;
- theatrical and ballet personifications in Corrientes;
- government tourism presentations of the legend.

These constitute reception and civic folklore, not worship. The Asociación Anahí explicitly links the legendary girl with Clara Anahí Mariani, a child abducted during Argentina’s dictatorship; this is a modern memorial-political reuse of the name and its associations with violated childhood and resistance.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Before 1639: no textual Anahí

No precolonial inscription, image, manuscript, or oral recording exists. No archaeological object is securely associated with the heroine. **[SEA]**

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: lexical and Jesuit silence

Montoya’s seventeenth-century lexicography and the accessible Jesuit record do not attest the heroine. This does not rule out undocumented local storytelling, but it makes claims of colonial textual antiquity unsubstantiated.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: the collection problem

Ambrosetti and other collectors published substantial Guaraní-region folklore without providing a presently identified Anahí-ceibo witness. **[SEA]** The legend could have circulated orally without reaching them; alternatively, it could have taken shape later from widespread American plant-transformation motifs, conquest memory, and local symbolism. The evidence does not decide between those possibilities.

### 1920s–1943: literary-musical fixing

Sosa Cordero developed a large littoral repertory from the 1920s onward. “Anahí” became its best-known legendary song. The 1942 national-flower decree created a powerful civic frame, and the song’s 1943 entry into school repertory supplied a memorable narrative to accompany the new emblem. **[SEA/LCE]**

Whether Sosa Cordero invented the story, reshaped a local oral account, or selected one version from existing circulation cannot presently be proved. Calling him simply “the author of the legend” goes beyond the evidence; calling his song a decisive **literary fixing** is justified.

### 1950s–1970s: folklore, recording, and print

Folklorists documented littoral oral traditions after the story had entered national education and broadcasting. Perkins Hidalgo’s 1963 Iberá work provides field-context evidence. Ramona Galarza’s recordings carried the song nationally. Sosa Cordero’s *Romancero guaraní* appeared in 1962, while *Revista Folklore* helped establish his canonical standing.

This circulation complicates the oral/literary division: an informant in the 1950s or 1960s might know the story through family narration, school, radio, song, or all four.

### Late twentieth century: children’s literature and provincial canon

Collections such as Elsa Leonor Pasteknik’s *Misiones y sus leyendas* and numerous school anthologies standardized the tale as “Guaraní.” Anahí became variously a child, princess, queen, warrior, or tribal singer. **[LCE/RV]**

### Twenty-first century: anticolonial and ecological heroine

Government, educational, museum, tourism, and activist retellings increasingly frame her as a symbol of Indigenous resistance, women’s courage, ecological continuity, and Argentina’s plural identity. These readings are compatible with the canonical plot but are historically modern emphases.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Music

Sosa Cordero’s “Anahí” is the single most influential reception object. Its school adoption in 1943 and later recordings—especially Ramona Galarza’s—helped convert a regional legend into national cultural memory. It is variously labeled guarania, canción del litoral, or folk song; calling it a **chamamé** without checking the particular musical arrangement can be imprecise.

### Education and children’s literature

Anahí is now a standard example in lessons on:

- the legend as a narrative genre;
- Indigenous cultures;
- plant origins;
- the ceibo as national flower;
- colonial violence;
- courage and identity.

Ana María Shua’s *La flor del ceibo* in the *Leyendas latinoamericanas* collection (2012) is one identifiable modern literary adaptation. **[MI/LCE]**

### Visual and performing arts

Illustrations generally show a young Indigenous woman, flames, a red-flowering ceibo, armored Spaniards, or a metamorphic fusion of woman and tree. These images are modern iconography: no premodern portrait or ritual image of Anahí exists.

Corrientes’ official mythological public-art itinerary includes the 2021 Svanotti sculpture; regional ballets and festivals personify her alongside figures such as Pombero and Yasí-Yateré. Joint billing does not create mythological interactions.

### Nationalism and politics

The 1942 decree belongs to a period of intense Argentine cultural nationalism. It made the ceibo a state emblem and explicitly invoked “aboriginal legends,” poetry, music, flagpole plantings, territory, and the colors of the national arms. **[SEA/LS]** The decree did not adopt Anahí officially, but it supplied the institutional conditions in which her story could become a national allegory.

Later versions sometimes speak of the “Guaraní race,” an idiom now recognized as historically racializing. Others mobilize her as an anticolonial heroine whose body is destroyed but whose land and culture flower again. No significant extremist appropriation specific to Anahí was located; generic nationalist uses should not be inflated into evidence of organized extremist cult.

### Documented modern inventions

The following circulate as ancient facts but remain unsupported:

- that Anahí was a historically identifiable princess or queen;
- that her execution occurred at a known site or date;
- that Spanish records document her burning;
- that her tomb or relics exist;
- that *Anahí* literally means “ceibo flower”;
- that she belonged specifically to the Mbyá in the earliest version;
- that Tupã appears in every traditional telling;
- that Cadogan, Montoya, or Ambrosetti recorded the canonical story;
- that the 1942 decree itself narrates or names Anahí.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Historicity

There is no independent evidence for a historical Anahí. The narrative supplies no testable date, place, Spanish commander, legal record, kin group, or burial. **[MSR]** A real episode of colonial violence may stand behind any oral legend, but identifying this heroine with a historical victim would be speculation.

The execution method is narratively plausible within colonial violence, yet plausibility is not attestation. The accusation of witchcraft found in some versions is likewise uncorroborated.

### Antiquity versus modern literary fixation

The central debate is not “true or false” but **how old the documented form is**. Three models remain possible:

1. **Old oral-tradition model:** Sosa Cordero versified a long-standing regional story.
2. **Recent folklore model:** the story arose in the late nineteenth or early twentieth century and circulated before publication.
3. **Literary-diffusion model:** Sosa Cordero or another modern writer substantially created the canonical form, which schools and radio subsequently traditionalized.

The 1942 decree’s generic reference to Indigenous ceibo legends modestly supports the existence of some antecedent legend, but it does not identify its plot. The absence of Anahí from earlier searchable corpora favors caution. No presently available evidence conclusively selects one model.

### “Guaraní” attribution

The tale contains Guaraní geography, a Guaraní-named heroine, Indigenous song, Spanish invasion, and the ceibo. That makes “Guaraní-region legend” a defensible cultural-geographic classification. It does not establish that the story derives from precontact Guaraní religion or from all Guaraní peoples.

The canonical moral universe—martyrdom by fire, bodily transfiguration, inward beauty, national sacrifice—also resonates with Christian hagiography and romantic nationalism. Recognizing that resemblance is comparative interpretation, not proof of direct borrowing.

### Folklore-collection problem

Later oral testimony is real folklore even if mass media helped create it. The scholarly error is to treat a twentieth-century oral collection automatically as proof of precolonial age. Field date, informant biography, location, language, exposure to schooling, and publication history are essential.

### Gender and colonial representation

Modern criticism can read Anahí as an Indigenous woman transformed from colonial victim into enduring territorial presence. It must also acknowledge that many canonical texts call her ugly, childlike, primitive, or racially “other.” The tale both condemns Spanish violence and reproduces twentieth-century stereotypes about Indigenous appearance.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Anahí is an ancient Guaraní goddess”: unsupported.
- “The Jesuits recorded her myth”: unsupported.
- “Anahí means ceibo”: lexically unsupported.
- “The tree was named for her”: unsupported; *ceibo* and Guaraní plant-names have independent histories.
- “She was executed during a particular named expedition”: no source found.
- “The national-flower decree was issued because of Anahí”: the decree invokes legends among several reasons but does not name her.
- “The story proves a specific Guaraní rite of human sacrifice”: false; the killers in the canonical narrative are Spaniards.

## 10. Sources and source assessment

### Primary and near-primary documentary sources

- **Decreto 13.847/42, 23 December 1942.** The controlling source for the ceibo’s designation as national flower. It mentions Indigenous legends but not Anahí.
- **Sosa Cordero, Osvaldo, “Anahí.”** The decisive literary-musical fixing; nationally taught from 1943. Early sheet music, school circulars, and Odeón matrices deserve further archival examination.
- **Perkins Hidalgo, Guillermo, “Leyendas y supersticiones del Iberá,” 1963.** An important regional folklore witness, but later than national school dissemination.
- **Modern Argentine school readers and government cultural pages.** Primary evidence for the canonical late form, not for precolonial belief.

### Comparative and control corpora

- **Ruiz de Montoya, Antonio, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639).** Lexical and ethnolinguistic control; does not presently support the popular etymologies or an early heroine.
- **Ambrosetti, Juan Bautista, writings on Misiones beliefs and legends.** Control corpus demonstrating which regional beings were actually recorded around 1900.
- **Cadogan, León, Mbyá-Guaraní texts.** Control corpus for Indigenous sacred tradition; no Anahí interaction or myth located.
- **Chertudi, Susana, *Cuentos folklóricos de la Argentina* (1960).**
- **Vidal de Battini, Berta Elena, *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina* and studies of plant legends.** Essential for field methodology and variant comparison.
- **Irupé narratives of Morotí and Pitá.** Comparative modern plant-transformation legends; related by genre, not by shared characters.

### Final assessment

Anahí’s documented history is not that of an ancient heroine traceable continuously from precontact Guaraní religion. It is the history of a powerful littoral legend whose **secure named record begins in the cultural-nationalist environment of 1942–43**, when the ceibo became Argentina’s national flower and Sosa Cordero’s song entered the schools. Regional oral circulation may predate that moment, and the 1942 decree’s generic language leaves room for antecedent ceibo legends, but no located Jesuit, colonial, nineteenth-century, or early ethnographic witness establishes the canonical plot.

The legend itself is nevertheless fully historical as folklore: it documents how twentieth- and twenty-first-century Argentines have narrated conquest, Indigenous resistance, female courage, bodily difference, song, landscape, and national identity. Its variants—silent or singing, commoner or princess, murderer or resister, bodily metamorphosis or survival of the soul—are not errors to eliminate. They are the record.
