# Irupé: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** Irupé is not securely attested as an ancient Guaraní hero, demigod, or heroized dead. In the documented tradition, *irupé* is first the Guaraní name of a giant water-lily and, in twentieth-century etiological legends, either the transformed form of one or two mortal lovers or the personal name subsequently given to a drowned maiden. The most accurate classification is therefore **folkloric transformation figure / flower heroine**, sometimes a mortal maiden and sometimes a paired hero and heroine.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The plant and its vernacular world are older, but the earliest securely dated narrative presently located is Ernesto Morales’s Spanish-language “El irupé,” printed in *Leyendas guaraníes* in **1925**. It tells the story of **Morotí and Pitá**, not the now-familiar moon-loving maiden. The moon-lover form is securely documented in Argentina by **1939**, when Luis Perlotti made his bronze *Flor de Irupé*; it is printed at length, alongside the Morotí–Pitá story and a Brazilian cognate, in Fernando Hugo Casullo’s “Voces de supervivencia indígena: Irupé” in **1957**. No Irupé heroine or corresponding narrative has been verified in Ruiz de Montoya’s seventeenth-century Jesuit works, in León Cadogan’s Mbyá sacred corpus, or in Juan B. Ambrosetti’s published Misionero folklore. Those absences do not prove that no oral antecedent existed, but they prevent describing the presently documented story as securely precolonial.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

“Irupé” names several overlapping but historically separable things:

1. **[Secure botanical and lexical record]** A Guaraní noun, conventionally written **yrupe** or **yrupẽ**, meaning a sieve or strainer and, by visual extension, the giant floating water-lily of the Paraná–Paraguay system.
2. **[Later literary-folkloric elaboration]** The flower created from Morotí and Pitá after their deaths in the Paraná.
3. **[Regional/literary variant]** The transformed body of a nameless maiden who drowned trying to embrace the Moon’s reflection.
4. **[Later regional elaboration]** A maiden called **Jasy Ratá / Yasí-Ratá / Rasí-Ratá**, or occasionally a youth of that name, who loves the Moon and becomes the flower.
5. **[Modern personification]** A princess explicitly named Irupé, transformed after the death of her warrior fiancé.
6. **[Modern invention]** A Paraguayan romance in which a woman named Irupé interacts with Panambí and Akãhatã; no early collector or dated indigenous source has been found for it.

Accordingly, “Irupé” is not one consistently individuated protagonist. It is a mobile narrative position attached to a plant: the flower may embody a woman, a man, two lovers, or the memory of a dead fiancé.

### Forms and meanings

- **Guaraní:** *yrupe*; standardized modern form also **yrupẽ**.
- **Spanish:** *irupé*, *irupe*, *yrupe*, occasionally *flor del irupé*.
- **Portuguese:** *irupé* occurs regionally, while the Amazonian cognate is normally called *vitória-régia*.
- **Scientific name relevant to Corrientes, Misiones, Paraguay, and the Paraná basin:** ***Victoria cruziana* A.D.Orb.** It was first validly published in 1840. Kew gives its native range as west-central Brazil through Paraguay to northeastern Argentina. “Victoria regia,” common in older folklore books, is an obsolete or misapplied botanical designation in this regional context; *Victoria amazonica* is the Amazonian species. [Kew’s taxonomic record](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn%3Alsid%3Aipni.org%3Anames%3A605778-1) dates *V. cruziana* to *Annales des Sciences Naturelles*, 1840.

The current Paraguayan Guaraní dictionary defines **yrupe** first as “cedazo, tamiz” and second as the aquatic plant *Victoria regia*. This supports a comparison with a round sieve, not the often repeated literal segmentation “plato sobre el agua.” [The Paraguayan dictionary entry](https://www.mec.gob.ar/descargas/Bibliograf%C3%ADa/Educaci%C3%B3n%20Intercultural%20Biling%C3%BCe/GUARANI/avane-Diccionario-Guarani-Esp-Esp-Guarani.pdf) is explicit about both meanings.

Competing popular etymologies include:

- **“Plato sobre el agua” / “plato que lleva el agua.”** **[Folklore/modern pedagogical gloss]** Common in school texts and tourism pages, but morphologically doubtful as a literal derivation.
- **“Lo chato que trae el agua,” from *pe* ‘flat’.** **[Learned popular etymology]** Reported by modern myth dictionaries without a demonstrated historical derivation.
- **“Sieve/strainer.”** **[Secure lexicographic sense]** The safest explanation: the plant’s broad, ribbed leaf resembles an indigenous sieve called *yrupe*.

The personal names in the stories are usually transparent color or celestial words:

- **Morotí / Morotĩ:** “white.”
- **Pitá / Pytã:** “red.”
- **Jasy / Yasy:** “Moon.”
- **Mbyja / mbyja-ratá:** “star” or “fiery star” would be more regular modern Guaraní; the widespread claim that **Jasy Ratá** simply means “star” is linguistically unstable. Taken literally, *jasy* means “moon/month” and *tata* means “fire,” while orthographies and folk segmentation vary.
- **Tupã / Tupá:** a divine name transformed substantially by missionary usage; treating Tupã straightforwardly as the unitary “Supreme God of all Guaraní” is a learned Christianizing systematization.
- **Panambí / Panambi:** “butterfly.”
- **Akãhatã:** literally “hard-headed,” hence “stubborn” or “obstinate.”

There are no securely traditional epithets, patronymics, dynastic titles, or genealogical designations for Irupé. “Princess” and “daughter of the cacique” are generic romance formulas in later Spanish retellings.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Before the legend: word, plant, and image

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* was printed in Madrid in **1639**. It is indispensable for testing claims of ancient vocabulary and religious concepts, but no Irupé heroine or water-lily transformation narrative has been located in it. Its importance here is negative and methodological: it gives an extensive seventeenth-century Guaraní lexicon yet does not establish the modern legend. [The Misiones repository describes the 1639 work and its 1876 facsimile](https://repositorio.bpm.parquedelconocimiento.com/handle/bpm/266).

The regional plant was scientifically described by Alcide d’Orbigny as ***Victoria cruziana*** in **1840**, based on the giant water-lily he encountered at Corrientes. William Hooker’s 1847 account quotes d’Orbigny’s proposal and shows how European botany initially confused or combined it with *Victoria regia*. [The contemporary botanical account](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Curtis%27s_Botanical_Magazine/Volume_73/4275) supplies the historical nomenclature.

Marcos Sastre’s *El Tempe argentino*—first published in the nineteenth century and repeatedly reissued—describes the plant and its nocturnal “sleep,” but the accessible passage does not supply the heroine’s story. It belongs to the environmental and literary prehistory of the legend, not its first narrative attestation. [Sastre’s plant description](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/P%C3%A1gina%3AEl_Tempe_Argentino.djvu/191).

### The first securely dated story

**Ernesto Morales, *Leyendas guaraníes* (Buenos Aires: El Ateneo, 1925), “El irupé.”**  
Language: Spanish. Genre: authored literary legend presented as Guaraní tradition. Role: the earliest securely located complete narrative.

Morotí, a beautiful and coquettish maiden, throws her bracelet into the Paraná and challenges her beloved Pitá to recover it. Pitá dives and does not return. A diviner says the water-woman **I-Cuñá-Payé** has captured him. Morotí dives after him. At dawn a new flower appears: its inner petals are white like Morotí and its outer petals red like Pitá. The lovers have been joined in the flower. A digitized copy of Morales’s book confirms the 1925 edition and the Irupé chapter. [*Leyendas guaraníes*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/45/Leyendas_guaranies_-_Ernesto_Morales.pdf).

Morales is an important witness to early twentieth-century regional legend-making, but he does not identify an individual informant, collection date, Guaraní original, or community provenance for “El irupé.” His text is therefore **[literary folklorization]**, not a verbatim indigenous-language field record.

### Brazilian parallel

João Barbosa Rodrigues’s *Poranduba amazonense* (**1890**) contains Nheengatu narratives collected between 1872 and 1887, but the presently searchable text does not establish the familiar Naiá–Jaci–vitória-régia story. The book is relevant because later writers frequently cite it as though it did. Its documented contents include lunar and river myths, but attributing the full Naiá story to Barbosa Rodrigues requires a page-level citation that modern summaries rarely provide. [The National Library record](https://cpbn.bn.gov.br/planor/handle/20.500.12156.6/31585?locale-attribute=en&show=full) describes the 1890 bilingual collection.

By **1934**, however, the Brazilian story is explicitly in print: a newspaper comparison recounts **Nayá**, daughter of an old chief, who hopes that the Moon will turn her into a star, embraces its reflection, drowns, and is changed into the “star of the waters.” The same article places this beside Morales’s Morotí–Pitá version. This proves circulation by 1934, not antiquity.

### The Argentine moon-lover version

**Luis Perlotti, *Flor de Irupé*, bronze, 1939.**  
This is the earliest firmly dated visual monument found for the moon-lover form. The Museo de Esculturas Luis Perlotti identifies the work and reproduces the accompanying narrative: a maiden climbs trees and mountains seeking the Moon, mistakes its image in a lake for the celestial body, drowns, and is changed by Tupá into the irupé. [Buenos Aires museum material](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/sites/default/files/media/document/2022/06/29/89d07282237609b465d331c601974ce6cda5dbb1.pdf).

**Bienvenido Velmiro Ayala Gauna, *La selva y su hombre* (1944).**  
Spanish; regional folklore and literary ethnography. The book contains an Irupé treatment and helped circulate the moon-lover legend in the Argentine littoral. Like Morales, Ayala Gauna writes in an authorial Spanish folklore mode rather than supplying a recorded Guaraní performance. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/La_selva_y_su_hombre.html?id=EXNsAAAAMAAJ).

**Fernando Hugo Casullo, “Voces de supervivencia indígena: ‘Irupé,’” *Boletín de la Academia Argentina de Letras* 22 (1957), pp. 41–49.**  
This is the pivotal comparative dossier. Casullo presents:

- the Morotí–Pitá story;
- the nameless maiden in love with the Moon;
- a Brazilian version in which the Moon is an alluring youth or hermaphroditic divinity who makes maidens into stars;
- botanical observations;
- the plant’s artistic reception, including a 1927 ballet.

Casullo’s presentation is valuable for documenting mid-century circulation, but its generalized statements about what “the Guaraní imagined” do not substitute for named indigenous informants. [The digitized BAAL article](https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/91125/files/b123174302_%24B677384.pdf).

### Vidal de Battini

Berta Elena Vidal de Battini’s national survey is the essential control corpus for Argentine oral narrative. Her *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina*, especially volumes VII–VIII, classifies legends by places, waters, plants, animals, and celestial phenomena and preserves informants and geographical variants. Volume VIII was issued by Ediciones Culturales Argentinas in 1980/1984 and digitized in 2010. [Bibliographic record for volume VIII](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/al_tall/obra/cuentos-y-leyendas-populares-de-la-argentina-tomo-8--0/).

However, an Irupé narrative with a clearly identified informant has not been verified in the searchable digital text. Vidal de Battini’s importance should not be converted into a fictitious citation. Her corpus demonstrates the appropriate documentary standard and the larger Argentine plant-legend genre; it does not, on present evidence, move Irupé’s first secure attestation earlier than Morales.

Her separate study *La narrativa popular de la Argentina: Leyendas de plantas* (1972) belongs in the bibliography of the genre, but no page-specific Irupé record was recovered.

### Chertudi, Ambrosetti, Villafañe, and Cadogan

- **Juan B. Ambrosetti:** his late nineteenth-century work records Misionero beliefs including the payé, Caá-Yarí, Mboi-Tatá, Yasí-Yateré, Pombero, Curupí, and other forest beings. No Irupé heroine has been verified there. **Finding: absence from the located record.**
- **Susana Chertudi:** her Argentine folktale cataloguing and collections are relevant methodological comparanda, but no securely indexed Irupé text was located. **Finding: unverified attribution.**
- **Javier Villafañe:** later bibliographies and educational lists associate him with regional children’s literature, but no primary Irupé version by him was verified. **Finding: unverified attribution.**
- **León Cadogan:** *Ayvu Rapyta*, his mid-twentieth-century Mbyá-Guaraní sacred corpus, is genuine indigenous-language religious testimony, but Irupé is not a documented actor in its cosmogony. **Finding: absence is significant.** A modern review usefully warns that anthologies of the “Guaraní region” combine genuine Mbyá texts with Hispanicized regional stories and should not be treated as a unitary ancient Guaraní canon. [Review of the regional anthology](https://www.xn--eepora-vwa.com.ar/p/normal-0-21-false-false-false-es-ar-x.html).

### Later corpus

- Félix Coluccio, *Diccionario folklórico argentino* (editions from 1948/1950 onward): prints both Morotí–Pitá and the moon-loving maiden, consolidating them as parallel national folklore.
- Guillermo Cabanellas and numerous school anthologies: simplify Morales’s story, often omitting I-Cuñá-Payé.
- Ramón Ayala, “Irupé,” **canción-leyenda**, recorded at least by Ramona Galarza on *Alma guaraní* (**1972**): the Paraná itself loves and courts an “indiecita,” who becomes a “novia vegetal.” [Lyrics and attribution](https://www.portalguarani.com/2397_ramon_ayala_cidade/17348_irupe__letra_y_musica_ramon_ayala.html).
- Provincial tourism and school resources, twenty-first century: circulate several incompatible stories under one title.
- Zulma Prina’s 2025 version makes the lover a boy called **Rasí-Ratá** and adds “maloca,” an Amazonian communal-house term alien to the usual Corrientes setting. This is **[modern literary adaptation]**, not early evidence.

## 3. The story: cycles and variants

### A. Morotí and Pitá

**Earliest source:** Morales, 1925.  
**Evidence tier:** later literary-folkloric elaboration, possibly based on regional oral material but without a documented informant.

Morotí and Pitá are young lovers. Her name means “white”; his means “red.” Morotí’s friends provoke her into testing Pitá’s devotion. She removes a bracelet and casts it into the Paraná. Pitá dives after it but fails to return. The community waits through evening and performs divination. A ritual specialist explains that **I-Cuñá-Payé**, a female supernatural owner or enchantress of the water, has taken Pitá into her jeweled palace and made him forget Morotí.

Morotí, remorseful, enters the river to recover him. In some versions she ties a stone to her feet; in others she simply dives. Neither lover returns. At dawn the people see an unknown flower. Its center is white for Morotí and its outer petals red for Pitá. Their love has defeated the water-being’s possessive magic by combining both bodies in one plant.

Variants alter the moral:

- Morales emphasizes coquettishness, repentance, courage, and love overcoming enchantment.
- School versions make Morotí’s act a simple “proof of love,” reducing the supernatural episode.
- Some modern dramatizations allow the lovers to meet underwater and consciously flee I-Cuñá-Payé before becoming the flower.
- Retellings commonly call Morotí a chief’s daughter and Pitá the tribe’s greatest warrior; these are generic embellishments.
- The real flower’s white-to-pink change supplies the names and climax. *Victoria cruziana* opens creamy white and turns pink on its second night, a biological sequence converted into human moral symbolism. [Kew’s species account](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn%3Alsid%3Aipni.org%3Anames%3A605778-1/general-information).

### B. The maiden who loved the Moon

**Earliest secure Argentine evidence:** Perlotti’s 1939 sculpture and its associated narrative; printed comparative exposition in Casullo, 1957.  
**Evidence tier:** twentieth-century Argentine regional legend with a Brazilian cognate; precolonial antiquity unproved.

A beautiful maiden falls in love with the Moon. She climbs the tallest trees and then a mountain, extending her arms toward the unattainable beloved. She walks toward the horizon until stones and thorns cut her feet. At a still lake she sees the Moon reflected close beneath her. Believing it reachable, she leaps into the water and drowns. Tupá takes pity and transforms her into the irupé, whose circular leaf resembles the lunar disk and whose flower looks upward.

The tale explains several observable features:

- circular floating leaves → image of the full Moon;
- nocturnal opening → continued contemplation of the beloved;
- white petals → moonlight or purity;
- later pink/red petals → passion or the maiden’s bloodied feet;
- submergence → the drowned body;
- the plant’s position between sky and reflected sky → union of mortal lover and celestial object.

Older Argentine printings leave the maiden nameless. Later versions call her **Yasí Ratá/Jasy Ratá**, often glossed “star.” Naming her is therefore **[later canonical elaboration]**, not part of the earliest recoverable form.

Her sex is unstable. Many tellings make her a girl; water-agency educational materials make Yasí-Ratá a young man, with the flower’s red color embodying his passion. Gender reversal shows that the narrative role—not a fixed heroic identity—is primary.

### C. Naiá and Jaci: the Brazilian cognate

**Secure circulation:** Brazilian print by 1934; exact earlier source disputed.  
**Evidence tier:** Brazilian/Amazonian regional variant, subsequently blended with the Río de la Plata story.

Naiá hears that the Moon, called **Jaci**, descends to kiss beautiful maidens and turns them into stars. She rejects mortal suitors and searches forests and mountains for the divine lover. Seeing the Moon in a lake, she dives and drowns. Jaci transforms her not into a star in the sky but into the “star of the waters,” the vitória-régia.

This shares the decisive motif chain with the Argentine Irupé story: female lunar desire → reflection mistaken for presence → drowning → aquatic flower. The direction of borrowing is not proved. The safest conclusion is that the Argentine and Brazilian literary-folklore systems converged during the early twentieth century. Claims that both independently preserve an immemorial pan-Guaraní myth go beyond the dated evidence.

### D. The bereaved princess

**Source:** current Misiones tourism tradition.  
**Evidence tier:** modern regional variant.

Irupé is a Guaraní princess promised to a powerful chief whom she loves. He dies in intertribal war before their marriage. Grief-stricken, she wanders until she walks unconsciously into a stream and drowns. Her floating body becomes the plant, which receives her name. This story lacks the Moon, Tupá, the reflection, Morotí, and Pitá. [Misiones’s official tourism presentation](https://misiones.tur.ar/mitos-y-leyendas-misioneras/).

### E. The river’s bride

**Source:** Ramón Ayala, “Irupé,” circulating by the 1960s–1972 recording.  
**Evidence tier:** modern authored song.

The Paraná is anthropomorphized as the suitor of an Indigenous girl. Its voice, fish, water-arms, mud-bed, current, and foam form the imagery of courtship and transformation. At dawn the “flower-woman” travels as the river’s “vegetable bride.” This is not merely a sung copy of Morales: it transfers agency from Tupá or I-Cuñá-Payé to the river itself.

### F. Panambí and Akãhatã

**Source:** twenty-first-century internet retelling by Susana C. Otero.  
**Evidence tier:** modern invention or freely authored adaptation.

Irupé and Panambí are lovers. She wishes to be the Moon; he says he would become a butterfly to caress her. A rival warrior, Akãhatã, enters the plot, and Tupá eventually transforms Irupé and her canoe into the flower. No pre-internet primary source, collector, or dated Guaraní-language analogue has been found. It must not be backdated as Paraguayan antiquity.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogy and social relations

No fixed genealogy exists. The moon-lover is variously:

- an unnamed maiden of an unnamed community;
- a chief’s daughter;
- the daughter of ordinary parents;
- a boy or young man;
- the fiancée of an unnamed chief;
- Morotí, beloved of Pitá;
- Irupé, beloved of Panambí.

No source supplies a securely traditional mother, father, sibling, child, dynasty, or descendants. She is never a demigod by birth. Transformation, when a god causes it, occurs only after drowning.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

There is **no evidence of an Irupé cult** in the technical sense:

- no shrine or *heroon*;
- no identified tomb;
- no relics;
- no priesthood or initiatory society;
- no sacrifice;
- no festival addressed to the transformed heroine;
- no oracle;
- no votive inscriptions;
- no archaeological icon securely identified as Irupé;
- no indigenous ritual text invoking her.

The lack of a body is intrinsic to every major version: the corpse disappears beneath the water and is replaced by a plant. That is a narrative metamorphosis, not evidence for a relic cult.

Casullo asserted in 1957 that local people admired the plant, left it to grow, and considered picking its flowers an offense. This is **[mid-twentieth-century ethnographic assertion]**, but he did not provide a named informant or ritual description. It may represent conservation custom, regional reverence, or literary idealization; it does not establish organized worship.

The plant itself had practical and symbolic uses. Its seeds led to the name “water-maize,” and its immense leaves became icons of the littoral landscape. Personal names, streets, neighborhoods, schools, cultural groups, and businesses called Irupé are commemorative or commercial uses of the plant-name, not survival of a heroine cult.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Seventeenth century: lexical and missionary control

Jesuit grammars, dictionaries, and catechisms establish Classical Guaraní vocabulary and show the intensity of Christian reinterpretation. They do not presently attest the Irupé heroine. Tupã’s later role as a benevolent creator who rewards lovers must therefore be treated cautiously: missionary Guaraní often uses divine terminology through Christian categories.

### Nineteenth century: botanical discovery and romantic landscape

D’Orbigny’s 1840 description at Corrientes and subsequent European fascination with giant *Victoria* lilies supplied a spectacular visual object. The flower became associated with Queen Victoria through *Victoria regia*, while Río de la Plata writers such as Marcos Sastre incorporated the plant into national landscape description. No dated nineteenth-century Irupé heroine has yet been secured.

### 1920s: literary fixation

Morales’s 1925 *Leyendas guaraníes* converts regional nature, Guaraní vocabulary, and romantic morality into a literary cycle. His Morotí–Pitá story is the first secure Irupé narrative in the present record.

A ballet titled *La Flor del Irupé*, based on a choreographic poem by Víctor Mercante, was staged at Buenos Aires’s Teatro Colón in **1927**, with music associated in Casullo’s account with Constantino Gaito and choreography directed by Boris Romanoff. This is early evidence that the story had entered elite national performance culture.

### 1930s–1950s: lunar heroine and comparative canon

The Naiá lunar story was in Brazilian print by 1934. Perlotti monumentalized the Argentine moon-maiden in bronze in 1939. Ayala Gauna’s 1944 regional writing and Casullo’s 1957 academic article then set multiple versions beside one another.

This is the period in which “Guaraní legend” became a broad cultural label covering:

- indigenous vocabulary;
- regional Spanish oral tradition;
- literary compositions by non-indigenous writers;
- Brazilian Tupi/Nheengatu parallels;
- school nationalism;
- Christianized divine machinery.

### 1960s–1980s: film, song, folklore dictionaries, and school circulation

The film *La flor del irupé* premiered on **4 October 1962**, directed by Alberto Dubois. It used the moonlit water-maiden as an erotic and supernatural frame for a crime melodrama. Its violence and sexual exploitation are modern cinematic material, not part of Morales’s legend.

Ramón Ayala’s song transformed Irupé into the Paraná’s bride. Ramona Galarza’s 1972 recording on *Alma guaraní* helped circulate it beyond Misiones and Corrientes.

Coluccio’s dictionaries, classroom anthologies, and folklore compilations regularized both the Morotí–Pitá and moon-lover versions. Names and details became increasingly interchangeable.

### Late twentieth century to present: tourism and children’s literature

Provincial tourism presents Irupé as Misionero heritage, while Corrientes educational texts localize the moon-lover at Iberá. Children’s versions increasingly name the protagonist, moralize her desire, soften death, or reverse her gender. Internet texts add Panambí, Akãhatã, canoes, ñandutí, and explicitly Paraguayan scenery.

This is living folklore in the broad sense of repeated, variable regional narrative. It should not be confused with evidence that every detail is old or indigenous.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Visual art

- **Luis Perlotti, *Flor de Irupé*, 1939, bronze.** The maiden’s upward-reaching body embodies longing for the Moon. Securely dated modern artistic reception.
- Botanical illustration from the nineteenth century onward made the giant leaf and opening flower nationally recognizable, even where the heroine was absent.

### Stage and music

- ***La Flor del Irupé*, Teatro Colón, 1927.** Early ballet reception.
- **Ramón Ayala, “Irupé,” canción-leyenda**, circulating by the 1960s and recorded by Ramona Galarza in 1972.
- Later school songs and dramatizations commonly use the Morotí–Pitá colors as speaking roles.

### Film

- ***La flor del irupé* (1962), directed by Alberto Dubois.** The legend frames the apparition of a beautiful woman in the Iberá/Paraná environment. The film adds robbery, murder, sexual threat, rape, and erotic spectacle. These are cinematic inventions and must not be folded back into the traditional narrative.

### Children’s literature and education

The story is now a standard Argentine classroom etiological legend. Simplification commonly produces the following false impressions:

- that there is one authoritative version;
- that the protagonist was always named Irupé or Jasy Ratá;
- that “plato sobre el agua” is a settled literal etymology;
- that Tupá’s action proves a uniform ancient Guaraní theology;
- that the story was recorded unchanged from precolonial times.

Recent versions openly adapt the tale. A 2025 children’s anthology makes Rasí-Ratá a boy and describes his home as a “maloca”; that Amazonian vocabulary is evidence of modern pan-indigenous blending, not of Corrientes ethnography.

### The flower-legend genre: Anahí and mburucuyá

Irupé is one member of an Argentine literary-pedagogical genre in which an Indigenous woman’s death or suffering becomes a regional flower.

**Anahí and the ceibo:** The familiar story makes Anahí a gifted singer and warrior captured by Spaniards, tied to a tree, and burned; at dawn the ceibo’s red flowers embody her blood, courage, or song. The ceibo was declared Argentina’s national flower by decree in **1942**. Osvaldo Sosa Cordero’s song “Anahí” was recorded on **6 December 1943** and entered the school repertory that year, powerfully fixing the legend’s national form. [Recording documentation](https://www.fundacionmemoriadelchamame.com/discoteca/277/) and [the official decree summary](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/modernizacion/sustentabilidad/ceibo).

**Mburucuyá:** In Morales’s 1925 literary corpus, passionflower legends similarly convert love, death, and color into botany. Later Christian interpretations read the flower as the instruments of Christ’s Passion; those are colonial or learned Christian associations, not automatically Guaraní cosmology.

As a genre, these stories served school regionalism: local plants became memorials to idealized Indigenous heroines. They preserved Guaraní words and landscapes while often replacing historically specific indigenous societies with the generic figures “princess,” “cacique,” “tribe,” “warrior,” and “payé.”

### Political and nationalist use

No extremist appropriation of Irupé specifically was documented. Its politics are softer but real:

- It was used to naturalize Argentine territorial identity in the littoral.
- “Guaraní legend” supplied cultural antiquity to a modern national and provincial folklore canon.
- School circulation celebrated an Indigenous past while frequently excluding living Guaraní narrators and precise community attribution.
- The 1927 Colón staging elevated regional material into metropolitan high culture.
- Pairing Irupé with Anahí after the ceibo’s 1942 national designation made flower heroines vehicles of Argentine civic education.

This is not proof of cynical fabrication. It is the documented institutional setting in which the versions became canonical.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is the legend ancient?

**Claim:** Irupé is an immemorial precolonial Guaraní myth.  
**Evidence offered:** Guaraní names, Guaraní territory, Tupá, the payé, and the native plant.  
**Problem:** No seventeenth- to nineteenth-century Guaraní text or named indigenous informant has been found for the complete story. The first secure narrative is Morales, 1925.

**Assessment:** Ancient oral antecedents remain possible but unproved. “Traditional Guaraní legend” is defensible as a history-of-circulation label; “documented precolonial myth” is not.

### Argentine invention or Brazilian borrowing?

The moon-lover form closely resembles Naiá and Jaci. A Brazilian version is securely printed by 1934, while the Argentine image is securely dated to 1939. This chronology suggests possible Brazilian influence, but it does not settle priority because oral or ephemeral Argentine versions could be earlier.

Morales’s 1925 version is certainly earlier than the located 1934 Naiá printing, but it has a different plot. The likely historical process is recombination: an Argentine flower legend and a Brazilian lunar-drowning legend became mutually assimilated.

### One heroine or several?

Popular summaries often collapse Morotí, the unnamed moon-lover, Yasí Ratá, Naiá, and “Princess Irupé” into a single character. The sources do not warrant this. They are variant protagonists occupying the same etiological role.

### The problem of Tupá

In later stories Tupá behaves like the benevolent monotheistic God of children’s literature. Guaraní religious traditions are much more diverse. Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus, for example, contains a complex hierarchy and vocabulary that cannot be reduced to this schoolbook Tupá. Tupá’s role in Irupé is best labelled **Christianized regional systematization** unless an indigenous-language performance demonstrates otherwise.

### Folklore collection versus authored folklore

Morales, Ayala Gauna, Casullo, Coluccio, Perlotti, and Ramón Ayala are witnesses to tradition, but also artists and editors. Their texts have dates and individual styles. Calling their work folklore does not make it anonymous, verbatim, or prehistoric.

Vidal de Battini’s practice—recording narrator, place, and variants—shows why provenance matters. The lack of such information for early Irupé texts is the dossier’s central evidentiary limitation.

### Scientific nomenclature

“Victoria regia” is entrenched in the stories, but Corrientes and Paraguay chiefly concern *Victoria cruziana*. The distinction matters historically:

- *Victoria regia* evokes nineteenth-century British royal naming and the Amazonian species now called *V. amazonica*.
- *V. cruziana*, published in 1840 from the southern region, is the relevant Paraná–Paraguay taxon.
- The flower’s genuine color transition from white to pink supplied an unusually strong natural basis for the Morotí/Pitá symbolism.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Irupé literally means plate on water.” Popular but not the secure dictionary analysis.
- “Jasy Ratá simply means star.” Orthographically and morphologically doubtful.
- “The legend appears in the Jesuit chronicles.” Not verified.
- “Cadogan collected the Irupé story from the Mbyá.” Not verified.
- “Vidal de Battini’s national corpus proves this precise version is old.” No page-level Irupé record has been established.
- “The heroine was worshipped.” No cult evidence.
- “There is an Irupé tomb.” No evidence.
- “All Guaraní believed the Moon was a male lover called Jasy.” Guaraní-speaking societies and texts are too diverse for that generalization.
- “Naiá’s story is demonstrably in Barbosa Rodrigues’s 1890 *Poranduba*.” Often asserted, but a matching page in the primary text has not been verified here.

## 9. Chronology of attestation and reception

| Date | Event and evidence |
|---|---|
| **1639** | Ruiz de Montoya publishes *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní*. Essential early lexical witness, but no verified Irupé heroine or transformation tale. |
| **1840** | D’Orbigny validly publishes ***Victoria cruziana*** from the southern South American plant tradition, including Corrientes material. |
| **Mid-19th century** | Marcos Sastre describes the giant water-lily in *El Tempe argentino*; no heroine appears in the verified passage. |
| **1890** | Barbosa Rodrigues publishes *Poranduba amazonense*, a Nheengatu/Portuguese collection. Frequently invoked in later discussions, but not securely verified as the source of the full Naiá story. |
| **1925** | Ernesto Morales publishes “El irupé” in *Leyendas guaraníes*: Morotí, Pitá, bracelet, water enchantress, double transformation. Earliest secure narrative. |
| **1927** | *La Flor del Irupé* staged as a ballet at the Teatro Colón, according to Casullo’s 1957 historical notice. |
| **1934** | Brazilian newspaper prints Naiá’s lunar drowning and compares it with Morales’s Morotí–Pitá story. |
| **1939** | Luis Perlotti creates the bronze *Flor de Irupé*, documenting the Argentine moon-lover form. |
| **1942** | Ceibo declared Argentina’s national flower, strengthening the civic setting for regional flower legends. |
| **1943** | Sosa Cordero’s “Anahí” recorded and introduced into Argentine school repertory. |
| **1944** | Ayala Gauna publishes *La selva y su hombre*, including Irupé in a regional folklore-literary synthesis. |
| **1957** | Casullo publishes the first located comparative study devoted to Irupé, setting Morotí–Pitá, the moon-maiden, and the Brazilian version side by side. |
| **1962** | Alberto Dubois’s film *La flor del irupé* premieres, turning the legend into erotic-supernatural cinema. |
| **1972** | Vidal de Battini publishes *La narrativa popular de la Argentina: Leyendas de plantas*. Ramona Galarza’s *Alma guaraní* includes Ramón Ayala’s “Irupé.” |
| **1980–1984** | Vidal de Battini’s ten-volume *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina* provides the major national comparison corpus. |
| **Late 20th century** | Coluccio, schoolbooks, songbooks, and children’s anthologies standardize both principal plots. |
| **2000s–present** | Tourism sites and internet adaptations proliferate: Yasí Ratá changes sex; Irupé becomes a princess; Panambí and Akãhatã enter a purported Paraguayan version; Amazonian and Paraguayan vocabulary is freely blended. |
| **2025** | A new children’s adaptation calls the lover Rasí-Ratá, makes him male, and uses “maloca,” transparently demonstrating ongoing reinvention. |

## 10. Sources and evidentiary conclusion

The documentary history does not support a biography of a single ancient heroine. It supports a layered tradition centered on a real and visually extraordinary plant.

The firm sequence is:

1. a Guaraní plant/tool word and a southern giant water-lily;
2. nineteenth-century botanical and landscape description;
3. Morales’s 1925 Morotí–Pitá literary legend;
4. early twentieth-century convergence with the Brazilian maiden-and-Moon narrative;
5. visual, theatrical, musical, cinematic, school, and tourism elaboration.

The legend’s instability is not a defect to be corrected. It is its documented history. The lover can be Morotí, a nameless girl, Jasy Ratá, a boy, Naiá, or Princess Irupé. The transforming power can be Tupá, the Moon, the Paraná, or an unexplained metamorphosis. The flower can contain one body or two. These differences mark the movement of a regional nature story between Guaraní vocabulary, Brazilian and Argentine folklore, literary nationalism, children’s education, and contemporary cultural commerce.

What remains absent is equally important: no ancient manuscript, Jesuit description, archaeological image, indigenous shrine, tomb, ritual, or securely transcribed nineteenth-century oral performance establishes Irupé as a precolonial Guaraní divine or heroic person. The oldest securely documented “heroic” actors are Morotí and Pitá in 1925. Everything earlier belongs to the history of the plant and the word unless further primary evidence emerges.
