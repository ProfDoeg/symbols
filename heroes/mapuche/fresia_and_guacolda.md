# Fresia and Guacolda: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary orientation

**Class:** mortal legendary heroines of colonial epic; neither is a demigoddess, culture hero, heroized dead, or recipient of documented cult. In literary and national reception, Fresia becomes the proud, martial wife who repudiates a captured husband; Guacolda becomes the faithful lover and prophetic mourner.

**Earliest secure attestations:**

- **Fresia:** the evidentiary situation is unusually complicated. A woman associated with Caupolicán and the violent rejection of their child is reported in Jerónimo de Vivar’s *Crónica y relación copiosa y verdadera de los reinos de Chile*, completed in 1558 but unpublished and effectively unavailable until its twentieth-century recovery. Modern scholarship identifies this woman with Ercilla’s Fresia, although the exact form and independence of the name in Vivar’s manuscript tradition require caution. The first **public, securely datable appearance under the name Fresia** is Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana*, canto XXXIII, in the third part printed at Madrid in **1589**.  
- **Guacolda:** Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana*, canto XIII, in the first part printed at Madrid in **1569**. No securely earlier Mapuche-language, administrative, or archaeological source naming her is known.

The women belong first to a Spanish colonial textual tradition about the sixteenth-century Arauco War. Subsequent chronicles, drama, painting, opera, school history, place-names, and military nomenclature transformed them into Chilean national figures. No evidence establishes a pre-Hispanic tradition about either woman.

### Evidence labels used below

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** sixteenth-century text or object demonstrably early, though not necessarily eyewitness truth.
- **[CE] Canonical epic elaboration:** Ercilla or a direct continuation/adaptation of his epic.
- **[EC] Early-modern chronicle/accretion:** colonial historiography whose dependence must be assessed.
- **[RV] Regional or literary variant.**
- **[FO] Recorded folklore/oral tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[NR] National reception or nationalist reuse.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern claim.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** the relevant evidence has been sought but is absent.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Fresia

Fresia is the wife—or, in later summaries, “principal wife”—of the Mapuche toqui Caupolicán. In *La Araucana* she appears only at the moment of his capture. Carrying their fifteen-month-old son, she sees Caupolicán bound among ordinary prisoners, condemns him for surviving dishonorably, rejects the title of mother to “the infamous son of an infamous father,” throws the child before him, and refuses to return. The Spaniards find the child another nurse. This is a literary scene about martial honor, gender, maternity, and defeat rather than a recoverable biography. Ercilla calls her a **palla**, a colonial Andean title for a high-status Indigenous woman, and “Fresia mujer del gran Caupolicano.” [*La Araucana*, XXXIII, 1589; **CE**] [The primary text gives the child’s age and survival explicitly.](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-araucana--1/html/014721b2-82b2-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_39.html)

**Name forms and variants**

- **Fresia** — Spanish orthography in Ercilla, 1589.
- **Fresa** — Gaspar de Ávila’s *El gobernador prudente*, an early-seventeenth-century dramatic variant. José Toribio Medina proposed that this form might expose a Spanish rather than Mapudungun origin. [**RV/MSR**]
- **Güeden / Guedén / Guden** — assigned to Caupolicán’s wife by later authors, especially Cristóbal Suárez de Figueroa and subsequent historians; not demonstrated to be an early alternative name for Ercilla’s woman. Medina reports speculative derivations from Mapudungun *üden*, “hated/abhorrent,” or constructions involving *we*, “new,” but presents these as conjectures. [**EC/MSR**]
- **Paca** — sometimes circulated as an alternative name, but no early independent primary attestation securely linking it to Fresia was located. [**MI or late learned accretion**]

**Etymology**

No reliable Mapudungun derivation is established. Medina’s linguistic correspondents observed that historical Mapudungun did not naturally contain the Spanish clusters and sounds represented by *fr* and *s* in *Fresia*. Rodolfo Lenz therefore regarded it as non-Indigenous; Félix de Augusta similarly found it unrecognizable. Proposed derivations from “Frisia,” Spanish *fresa*, or *preciada* are learned speculation, not traditional Mapuche etymology. Medina ultimately treated free poetic invention as a serious possibility. [**MSR**] [Medina’s collected linguistic discussion records both the objections and the speculative alternatives.](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-araucana-ilustraciones--0/html/016456e2-82b2-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_58.html)

### Guacolda

Guacolda is Lautaro’s beloved in *La Araucana*. Ercilla does not supply parents, birthplace, childhood, or a Christian name. She and Lautaro sleep together in his fortified camp on the eve of the Spanish surprise attack. Both have foreboding dreams; she interprets them as announcing his death and promises to die when he does. Ercilla repeatedly calls her **“la bella Guacolda”** and **“Guacolda enamorada.”** [*La Araucana*, XIII, 1569; **EA/CE**]

Later sources commonly call her Lautaro’s wife, but Ercilla’s language is that of reciprocal lovers and does not document a Mapuche marriage ceremony. “Wife” is therefore a later conventional systematization.

**Name forms and proposed etymology**

- **Guacolda** — Ercilla’s form, 1569.
- **Wa-kelü / wa-koli**, frequently glossed in popular sources as “red corn” or “maize-colored,” is a modern etymological proposal used to imagine reddish or blond hair. It is not established by an early Mapudungun text and should not be treated as the historical meaning of a recorded personal name.
- **Teresa**, alleged Christian or household name: a popular modern biographical accretion. Claims that she was raised in Pedro or Francisco de Villagra’s household under this name were not located in a securely independent sixteenth-century source. [**MI/late systematization**]

Medina’s early-twentieth-century inquiry found that several female names in *La Araucana* sounded foreign to Mapudungun speakers and could be poetic creations. Unlike *Fresia*, *Guacolda* is not so plainly incompatible with possible Indigenous phonology, but its derivation remains uncertain. [**MSR**]

### Identification with other figures

Neither woman was syncretized with a Mapuche deity. Comparisons are literary:

- Fresia is repeatedly read through **Dido**, **Medea**, Roman exemplary women, and the early-modern *virago*. These are classical analogies, not Indigenous identifications.
- Guacolda is cast as a faithful epic beloved, prophetic dreamer, mourner, and—by Fray Diego de Ocaña—as an American **Amazon**. Ocaña’s image updates a European classical type rather than recording a Mapuche divine or cultic identity. [**EC**] [The Amazon comparison is explicit in analysis of Ocaña’s manuscript image.](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0719-26812016000200011&script=sci_arttext)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The problem of Vivar

Jerónimo de Vivar completed his *Crónica* in 1558. A modern scholarly discussion states that Vivar recounts a more brutal version of the child episode: Caupolicán’s wife kills the child against a tree. Because the manuscript was not published in the sixteenth century, it did not generate the early printed reception that Ercilla did. It is nevertheless potentially the earliest textual witness to the underlying anecdote. [**EA, with transmission caution**]

This matters historically: if the passage is accepted as independent, Ercilla did not invent the entire incident, though he transformed it and spared the child. It does **not** prove that the woman’s historical name was Fresia, nor that every detail happened.

## 3. The story

### Guacolda and Lautaro

#### The Ercillan episode

In canto XIII, Lautaro occupies a fortified camp accessible by a single guarded path. He sleeps with Guacolda, whom he loves as intensely as she loves him. Lautaro awakens distressed: he has dreamed that a proud Spaniard overpowered him. Guacolda replies that she too has dreamed what she most feared—his death and the beginning of her grief. She vows that fortune will not separate them because she will die as his body falls. Lautaro embraces and reassures her, boasting of his liberation of the Araucanians and dismissing dreams as omens. The Spanish attack then fulfills the lovers’ foreboding. [**CE, 1569**] [Ercilla’s text gives both dreams and Guacolda’s death vow.](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-araucana--1/html/014721b2-82b2-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_20.html)

Important correction to modern summaries: Guacolda does not alone “dream Lautaro’s death.” Lautaro first dreams of being overcome; Guacolda says that she has also dreamed the feared outcome. The paired dream binds erotic intimacy to martial catastrophe.

Ercilla does not narrate Guacolda’s death, capture, removal by Villagra, upbringing in a Spanish house, or later life. Those are later additions.

#### Ballad variants

The 1604 romances detach the scene from Ercilla’s controlling narration:

- One has Guacolda warn Lautaro immediately before Villagrán’s assault and makes her an honored captive.
- Another depicts her mourning over Lautaro’s body, kissing him and addressing the fatal arrow.
- Another makes her expire in grief, thereby fulfilling the suicide/death promise that Ercilla leaves unresolved.

These are **literary variants**, not independent testimony to her fate.

#### Pedro de Oña’s reversal

In *Arauco domado*, canto XIII, the dead Lautaro appears within a supernatural or visionary framework and reproaches Guacolda for surviving him and accepting a new bond under Spanish domination. Oña turns Ercilla’s uncompleted vow into a charge of female inconstancy. The poem itself acknowledges the gender stereotype—“¿para qué, Guacolda, te condeno / si acudes a tu sexo femenino?”—while imagining political submission as erotic betrayal. [**CE/RV, 1596**] [Modern analysis documents the passage and its Virgilian structure.](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?lng=es&nrm=iso&pid=S2448-65582021000100079&script=sci_arttext_plus&tlng=es)

#### Modern pseudo-biography

The following claims circulate widely but lack secure independent early documentation:

- Guacolda was baptized **Teresa**.
- She was raised in Pedro or Francisco de Villagra’s household.
- She met Lautaro at the capture of Concepción and accompanied every campaign.
- She died at Mataquito, was carried away by Villagra, or died shortly afterward “of sorrow.”
- Her hair was red or blond because her name meant “red maize.”

These belong to **later systematization or modern invention**, unless a specific early manuscript is produced for each claim.

### Fresia and Caupolicán

#### Ercilla’s version

After Caupolicán’s capture, Spaniards overtake a woman fleeing rough terrain with a poorly wrapped fifteen-month-old child. She is brought to the prisoners and recognizes her disarmed, bound husband. Rather than weeping, she attacks his honor: a brief honorable death would have made his life immortal; captivity has transformed his “membrudo cuerpo” into that of a woman. She declares that their child had been the bond of lawful love, casts him before Caupolicán, and leaves. The Spaniards’ pleas and threats do not bring her back; they secure another nursing mother. [**CE, 1589**]

Thus Ercilla’s Fresia does **not** kill the child. She abandons him, and the Spanish party preserves him. Later retellings that say Ercilla describes infanticide conflate his poem with the harsher chronicle or dramatic variants.

#### Vivar and Mariño de Lobera/Escobar

The chronicle tradition gives a lethal ending: the woman smashes or casts the infant against a tree or rock. If Vivar’s 1558 account is textually secure, the violent anecdote antedates the printed Fresia episode. Mariño de Lobera’s surviving revised chronicle also says she dashed the child against a rock, but its late Escobar redaction makes dependence on Ercilla or another circulating source possible. The surviving *Crónica del Reino de Chile* expressly announces that Escobar recast Mariño’s work; the original is lost. [**EA/EC, competing variants**] [The revision and lost-original problem are explicit in the transmitted work.](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/cronica-del-reino-de-chile--0/html/feec70e8-82b1-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_1.html)

Góngora Marmolejo, whose account was completed in 1575 and whose author knew Ercilla’s first part, does not report Fresia’s intervention. Nineteenth-century historian Diego Barros Arana consequently suspected poetic invention. Omission is not proof, but it weakens claims of universal contemporary knowledge. [**NEG/MSR**]

#### Oña’s courtly lovers

Pedro de Oña does not merely repeat the repudiation. In cantos IV–V he constructs an elaborate pastoral-erotic relationship: Caupolicán and Fresia bathe together in a woodland fountain, speak in refined courtly language, and embody a Renaissance amorous pair. This is a conspicuous **later canonical elaboration**, shaped by classical and Italianate epic. Oña was born in Chile but did not witness the events, and his poem was commissioned to magnify García Hurtado de Mendoza. [The Biblioteca Nacional characterizes the commission and the famous bath scene.](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-3650.html)

#### Lope’s dramatic Fresia

Lope converts Fresia into an active political and emotional counterweight to Caupolicán. She participates in the resistance-versus-submission debate; in the terminal scene, the child’s death dramatizes the absolute consequences of lost honor. This is theater built on the epic cycle, not new historical evidence.

### Parentage, childhood, death, and body

For both women:

- No parents are named in the earliest texts.
- No childhood or securely attested birthplace is recorded.
- No human remains, grave, tomb, or relic are identified.
- Fresia’s fate after leaving Caupolicán is unknown.
- Guacolda’s fate after Lautaro’s death is unknown in Ercilla; capture, suicide, death beside him, and death from grief are later variants.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogies

No divine descent is attributed to either woman.

**Fresia**

```text
[parents unrecorded]
        |
      Fresia ─── Caupolicán
                    |
        unnamed 15-month-old son in Ercilla
```

The later identification of the infant with **Lemucaguin/Caupolicán the Younger** is a learned harmonization. Ercilla does not name the child, and the equation is not secure.

**Guacolda**

```text
[parents unrecorded]
        |
     Guacolda ─── Lautaro
             no child recorded
```

The earliest text establishes reciprocal love, not a documented ceremonial marriage.

### Documented named interactions

Each entry gives the named figure, interaction, source/date, and tier.

#### Fresia

- **Caupolicán** — wife or beloved; condemns him for capture, rejects their maternal bond, throws their child before him; Ercilla, *La Araucana* XXXIII, 1589. **[CE]**
- **Caupolicán** — courtly lover in the woodland bath and other amorous scenes; Pedro de Oña, *Arauco domado* IV–V, 1596. **[CE/RV]**
- **Caupolicán** — political and marital antagonist over surrender; later kills or abandons the child during his captivity; Lope de Vega, *Arauco domado*, composed around the turn of the seventeenth century and printed 1625. **[CE/RV]**
- **García Hurtado de Mendoza** — belongs to the opposing Spanish power in Oña and Lope, but no secure personal meeting between him and Fresia should be inferred merely because they inhabit the same dramatic cycle. **[NEG]**
- **Lemucaguin/Caupolicán the Younger** — often called her son in later histories; the identification with Ercilla’s unnamed infant is not made by Ercilla. **[later learned systematization]**
- **Medea** — no narrative meeting; a nineteenth-century visual analogy created by Raymond Monvoisin’s composition. **[NR, not an interaction within the legend]**

#### Guacolda

- **Lautaro** — mutual lovers; sleep together, exchange foreboding dreams, embrace, and discuss his coming death; Ercilla, *La Araucana* XIII, 1569. **[EA/CE]**
- **Lautaro’s ghost or posthumous image** — reproaches her for surviving and taking another bond; Pedro de Oña, *Arauco domado* XIII, 1596. **[CE/RV]**
- **Francisco de Villagra/Villagrán** — commands the Spanish surprise attack that kills Lautaro; Ercilla does not stage a direct personal exchange with Guacolda. A 1604 romance makes her Villagrán’s honored captive. **[CE background; RV direct relation]**
- **Pillán** — Lautaro is called “hijo de Pillán” in Ercilla’s elevated poetic diction. Guacolda does not meet, worship, invoke, or receive a message from Pillán. **[NEG: epithet, not divine interaction]**
- **Mars/Marte, Cupid/Cupido, Fortune/Fortuna, Fate/Hado, and Parca** — occur as classical personifications or metaphors in the epic and ballads. They are not literal actors interacting with Guacolda. **[learned poetic apparatus]**
- **Colocolo, Caupolicán, Fresia, Galvarino, Tegualda, Glaura, and Lauca** — famous co-members of the Arauco cycle, but no direct Guacolda interaction with them is stated in her Ercillan episode. Later theater and opera may place some in the same cast; joint casting alone is not a narrative interaction. **[NEG/late adaptation]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

**[NEG]** No shrine, *rehue*, temple, chapel, heroon, dedicated sacrifice, oracle, festival, votive deposit, or ritual officiant for Fresia or Guacolda has been documented. They are not known as Mapuche supernatural beings and should not be inserted into a “pantheon.”

### Tombs and relics

**[NEG]** No ancient or colonial tomb, body, relic, or claimed burial place is securely associated with either woman. The lack is especially important for Guacolda because popular biographies sometimes narrate a definite death at Mataquito without producing a burial tradition.

### Commemorative naming

Commemoration exists, but it is civic and national rather than cultic:

- **Fresia**, the commune in Llanquihue Province, bears the name. Its municipal heraldry explicitly invokes “the sacrifice of Fresia,” showing the legend’s conversion into local identity. [**NR**] [Municipal account.](https://mail.munifresia.cl/escudo)
- Streets, schools, neighborhoods, organizations, and personal names preserve both names throughout Chile.
- The Chilean Navy applied the names **Guacolda** and **Fresia** to vessels. Four torpedo boats named *Fresia*, *Guacolda*, *Quidora*, and *Tegualda* served from the 1960s; a preserved boat became part of Punta Arenas’s Plaza de la Soberanía in 2007. The memorial joins Ercilla’s heroines to military sovereignty, especially the 1978 Beagle conflict. [**NR**] [Chilean Ministry of Education monument record.](https://www.curriculumnacional.cl/estudiantes/Aprendo-en-mi-territorio/Provincia-de-Magallanes/Punta-Arenas/259791%3APlaza-de-La-Soberania)

These are acts of secular national commemoration, not evidence of ancient belief.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figures

### 1558–1589: formation in conquest writing

The possible historical kernel of Fresia’s act appears in the near-contemporary chronicle tradition. Ercilla transforms such material into epic exemplarity: Guacolda embodies mutual love confronted by fate; Fresia embodies a severe martial code stronger than maternity.

Ercilla’s gendered language is crucial. Fresia humiliates Caupolicán by declaring his masculine body transformed into female sex. The poem gives a woman moral authority by using femininity itself as the defeated man’s stigma. Modern celebration of her as simply “empowered” can therefore obscure the scene’s misogynistic machinery.

### 1590s–seventeenth century: canonical expansion

Oña domesticates and eroticizes Fresia, turning her into a refined courtly beloved. He also transforms Guacolda’s fidelity into a posthumous dispute over survival and remarriage. Romances make Guacolda more visibly sentimental: captive, corpse-mourner, or lover dying of grief.

Lope’s theater enlarges Fresia into a dramatic, political character. Mariño/Escobar and Rosales help move epic episodes into “history,” but their dependence on the literary cycle prevents them from functioning as straightforward corroboration.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: history absorbs epic

Historians such as Juan Ignacio Molina incorporated Ercillan scenes into national history, sometimes nearly verbatim. The boundary between chronicle and poem became blurred. The women were assigned exemplary qualities: Guacolda was faithful and loving; Fresia heroic, proud, and fierce.

### Nineteenth-century national iconography

Chile’s post-independence culture could honor Indigenous resistance as a remote foundation of national courage while the state was conquering autonomous Mapuche territory. Fresia’s imagery therefore carried a contradiction: an Indigenous woman was celebrated as a national ancestor during the “Pacification of Araucanía.”

Raymond Monvoisin painted two major versions of **Caupolicán prisoner and Fresia**:

- a Chilean version dated **1854**, now associated with the Museo Histórico Nacional;
- a Paris version exhibited at the **Salon of 1859**, where Fresia dominates the action and visually recalls Delacroix’s furious Medea.

The Paris version places bound Caupolicán below Fresia, making her the agent of his moral defeat. It received a third-class medal. [**NR**] [The MNBA’s catalogue traces both versions, the Salon exhibition, and the Medea analogy.](https://quinsac.mnba.gob.cl/pdf?idObra=97)

### 1880–1920: pedagogical and operatic nationalism

Illustrated editions of *La Araucana* fixed Guacolda visually beside Lautaro; the Biblioteca Nacional preserves an 1884 “Lautaro y Guacolda” plate. [**NR**] [Collection record.](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-70150.html)

Two operas made the epic cycle national music:

- Eliodoro Ortiz de Zárate, *Lautaro*, **1902**, includes Guacolda, Lautaro, Valdivia, Colocolo, and Fray Rodrigo González.
- Remigio Acevedo Gajardo, *Caupolicán*: first act performed **1902**, complete opera premiered **11 December 1942**; Fresia is part of the dramatic world.

The operas combined European grand-opera form with stylized “Araucanian” material and a national historical program. [**NR**] [Modern musicological study situates both works in the nationalist reception of *La Araucana*.](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-22012022000200061&script=sci_arttext)

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Schoolbooks condensed the women into complementary national virtues: Guacolda as loving fidelity and Fresia as heroic pride. A 1927 school history stated the formula explicitly. Later literature, theater, pageants, murals, place-names, naval vessels, and popular biography repeated it. National imagery commonly called them historical “Mapuche heroines” without acknowledging that most individual detail comes from colonial Spanish literature.

The 2011–2013 musical *Corazón mestizo* included Guacolda and Lautaro as protagonists and gave Fresia the number “Del infame padre.” This is a modern theatrical reassembly of the Ercillan tradition, not recovered Indigenous memory.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theater

- Anonymous Guacolda romances, *Romancero general*, 1604.
- Oña’s *Arauco domado*, 1596.
- Santisteban Osorio’s continuation, 1597.
- Lope de Vega’s *Arauco domado*, approximately 1599–1604 or by 1618; printed 1625.
- Gaspar de Ávila, *El gobernador prudente*, early seventeenth century.
- Numerous nineteenth-century school and patriotic adaptations.
- Isidora Aguirre, *¡Lautaro! Epopeya del pueblo mapuche*, 1982, modern political theater centered on popular resistance.
- *Corazón mestizo*, premiered in the 2011–2013 period, a bicentennial-era musical adaptation.

### Visual art

- Fray Diego de Ocaña’s “La bella Guacolda,” c. 1600–1607: earliest important named image, but an ethnographic/classical type rather than a portrait.
- Illustrated *La Araucana* editions, especially the nineteenth century, made Guacolda an idealized companion of Lautaro.
- Raymond Monvoisin, *Caupolicán prisoner and Fresia*, 1854 and 1859.
- Mario Toral’s 2002 “Árbol de próceres” includes Fresia among national founders and heroes, demonstrating her continuing insertion into official memory. [**NR**] [A Chilean heritage study discusses Fresia and Guacolda as figures of official and female national mythology.](https://www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/sites/www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/files/2023-10/libro_sombras_del_lente_vf_baja.pdf)

### Nationalist appropriation

Neither figure belongs exclusively to a single political tendency. They have been used to signify:

- Indigenous resistance to Spanish invasion;
- the martial origin of Chile;
- loyal or sacrificial womanhood;
- motherhood subordinated to patriotic honor;
- female entry into public and political action;
- military sovereignty, through naval naming;
- a domesticated Indigenous ancestry compatible with a state that historically dispossessed Mapuche communities.

This is appropriation through selective admiration: the epic Mapuche is honored as a national ancestor while living Mapuche sovereignty and land claims can be marginalized.

### Psychological and comparativist readings

No influential Rank, Raglan, Campbell, Dumézil, or Nagy school has produced a major figure-specific theory of Fresia or Guacolda. Applying a generic “hero’s journey” to them would be a modern imposed scheme. Relevant scholarship instead emphasizes:

- classical epic imitation;
- Renaissance conventions of faithful lovers and prophetic dreams;
- the *virago* and Amazon;
- Dido and Medea analogies;
- colonial gender ideology;
- the conversion of epic characters into national history.

### Demonstrably modern or unsupported inventions

- definite birthplaces and birth dates;
- Guacolda’s Christian name Teresa;
- red or blond hair inferred as biographical fact;
- lifelong service in Villagra’s household;
- attendance at every Lautaro campaign;
- definite death at Mataquito or death “of sadness”;
- Fresia’s securely known descent, grave, later life, or named child;
- ancient worship of either woman;
- treating naval, municipal, or school commemoration as continuity of Mapuche cult.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Were they historical women?

Three positions recur.

1. **Historical-core position:** Vivar’s near-contemporary child episode suggests a real woman and incident behind Fresia. Ercilla had personal experience in Chile, and some of his war material can be checked against chronicles.
2. **Literary-invention position:** the women’s names, speeches, private dreams, and courtly dialogue are inaccessible to the Spanish narrators and closely follow European epic conventions. Góngora Marmolejo’s silence and the linguistic difficulty of *Fresia* strengthen suspicion.
3. **Layered position:** an anecdotal or oral report may underlie Fresia, but Ercilla created the named, rhetorically polished heroine; Guacolda is even more securely rooted in epic convention because no comparably early independent attestation has been established.

The layered position best fits the surviving evidence. It neither discards the legend nor mistakes literary detail for documentary biography.

### Did Ercilla invent the names?

For **Fresia**, linguistic evidence strongly favors a Spanish or literary formation. Medina collected opinions from Lenz, Augusta, and others noting that its phonology is anomalous for Mapudungun. For **Guacolda**, an Indigenous origin remains possible, but published derivations are conjectural.

### Are the chronicles independent?

- Góngora Marmolejo is early and independent enough to matter, but omits the women’s episodes.
- Vivar is early but poses manuscript and exact-wording questions.
- Mariño de Lobera’s original is lost; Bartolomé de Escobar’s surviving redaction was produced after Ercilla and under Hurtado de Mendoza’s patronage.
- Rosales writes a century later and openly draws on Ercilla for Fresia.
- Núñez de Pineda offers no independent cycle for either heroine.

Accordingly, the number of colonial authors who repeat an episode cannot simply be counted as multiple independent witnesses.

### Epic truth versus historical truth

Ercilla presents *La Araucana* as truthful history while using classical epic, prophecy, set speeches, erotic digressions, and allegory. Modern scholarship treats this not as fraud but as Renaissance historiographical poetics: moral and political truth could be expressed through crafted scenes. The women’s private dialogue is consequently evidence for what Ercilla wanted the episode to mean, not a transcript.

### Indigenous oral tradition

The absence of the women from major early Mapudungun collections such as Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* is a significant negative finding. It does not prove that no Mapuche community ever adopted or retold them; colonial print could re-enter oral circulation. But claims of an ancient, independently continuous Mapuche oral tradition require a dated informant, location, language text, and collection history. None was located for these specific narratives.

Koessler-Ilg and Augusta remain essential controls precisely because they preserve named Indigenous narrators and regional contexts. Their general importance cannot be converted into evidence that they recorded Fresia or Guacolda when no such item is cited.

### Major discoveries and editions

- Vivar’s manuscript recovery changed the assessment of possible historical material behind Fresia.
- Góngora Marmolejo’s chronicle, completed in 1575 but printed only in the nineteenth century, provided an independent check on Ercilla.
- Modern critical work on Mariño de Lobera exposed the extent of Escobar’s rewriting.
- José Toribio Medina’s documentary editions and *La Araucana: ilustraciones* assembled names, variants, ballads, theater, and linguistic opinions, but some of Medina’s own etymologies remain conjectural.
- Digital access to Ercilla, Oña, colonial chronicles, Ocaña’s images, and museum catalogues now permits claims about text and iconography to be checked directly.

### Discredited or weak theories still circulating

- The inference from *Fresia* to ancient Frisian discovery or settlement in Chile is antiquarian speculation.
- A transparent *wa-kelü* etymology proving Guacolda’s red hair is unestablished.
- Calling Mariño de Lobera an entirely independent eyewitness corroboration ignores Escobar’s post-Ercillan redaction.
- Stating that Ercilla’s Fresia killed her child is textually false: his infant survives.
- Presenting the women as members of Mapuche religion confuses literary heroes with deities.

## 9. Chronology of attestation and reception

| Date | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| **1558** | Jerónimo de Vivar completes his *Crónica*; a child-killing episode associated by modern scholars with Caupolicán’s wife may be the earliest textual kernel of Fresia. The manuscript remains unpublished for centuries. |
| **1569** | Part I of Ercilla’s *La Araucana* names Guacolda in canto XIII; paired dreams with Lautaro and her death vow establish her canonical story. |
| **1575** | Góngora Marmolejo completes his chronicle; it supplies an early independent history of the war but not the famous women’s narratives. |
| **1589** | Part III of *La Araucana* names Fresia in canto XXXIII; she repudiates captive Caupolicán and abandons their child, who survives. |
| **c. 1593–1595** | Mariño de Lobera’s chronicle is rewritten by Bartolomé de Escobar; the lethal child-on-rock variant enters the surviving chronicle text. |
| **1596** | Pedro de Oña publishes *Arauco domado*: Fresia becomes a courtly beloved; Guacolda’s fidelity is posthumously questioned. |
| **1597** | Santisteban Osorio publishes his continuation of *La Araucana*. |
| **c. 1600–1607** | Fray Diego de Ocaña draws and describes “la bella Guacolda” as an Amazon-like Chilean woman. |
| **1604** | Guacolda-and-Lautaro romances circulate in the printed *Romancero general*. |
| **c. 1599–1604 / by 1618** | Likely composition or documented existence of Lope de Vega’s *Arauco domado*. |
| **1625** | Lope’s play is printed; Fresia becomes a major dramatic heroine. |
| **1670s** | Diego de Rosales incorporates Fresia and Guacolda into colonial history, substantially through the prior epic tradition. |
| **1776–1787** | Juan Ignacio Molina’s historical synthesis further transfers Ercillan material into learned history. |
| **1854** | Monvoisin paints the Chilean version of *Caupolicán prisoner and Fresia*. |
| **1859** | Monvoisin exhibits the Paris version at the Salon; Fresia is monumentalized in European academic painting. |
| **1884** | An illustrated Madrid edition of *La Araucana* includes “Lautaro y Guacolda.” |
| **1902** | Ortiz de Zárate’s opera *Lautaro*, with Guacolda, premieres; the first act of Acevedo Gajardo’s *Caupolicán* is performed. |
| **1910** | Augusta and Fraunhäusl publish *Lecturas araucanas*; no independent Fresia or Guacolda cycle is securely identified in the collection. |
| **1916–1917** | Augusta’s dictionary and Medina’s linguistic investigations sharpen doubts about allegedly Mapudungun epic names. |
| **1927** | Vicente Bustos Pérez’s school history epitomizes Guacolda as loving and Fresia as heroic and proud. |
| **1942** | Complete premiere of Acevedo Gajardo’s *Caupolicán*. |
| **1965** | Chilean torpedo boats *Guacolda* and *Fresia* enter the modern military commemorative tradition. |
| **1978** | The vessels participate in sovereignty patrols during the Beagle conflict. |
| **1982** | Isidora Aguirre’s *¡Lautaro! Epopeya del pueblo mapuche* reworks the cycle for modern political theater. |
| **1985** | Bengoa’s *Historia del pueblo mapuche* reframes modern Mapuche history around Indigenous political experience rather than colonial epic heroes. |
| **2002** | Mario Toral’s “Árbol de próceres” includes Fresia in a visual genealogy of Chile. |
| **2007** | A preserved *Fresia* torpedo boat becomes a monument in Punta Arenas’s Plaza de la Soberanía. |
| **2011–2013** | *Corazón mestizo* restages Guacolda and Fresia for bicentennial-era musical theater. |
| **Present** | Both remain common personal, civic, school, street, neighborhood, and cultural names; their public image continues to oscillate between “historical Mapuche women,” colonial epic characters, feminist icons, and national symbols. No cult, grave, or new early independent attestation has emerged. |

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary sources

- Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana*, Parts I–III, 1569, 1578, 1589. Principal named source for both figures.
- Jerónimo de Vivar, *Crónica y relación copiosa y verdadera de los reinos de Chile*, completed 1558. Potential early kernel for Fresia; manuscript-transmission caution required.
- Alonso de Góngora Marmolejo, *Historia de todas las cosas que han acaecido en el Reino de Chile*, completed 1575. Crucial independent control and notable omission.
- Pedro Mariño de Lobera/Bartolomé de Escobar, *Crónica del Reino de Chile*, late-sixteenth-century redaction. Important violent variant, but not securely independent.
- Pedro de Oña, *Arauco domado*, 1596. Major literary expansion.
- Diego de Santisteban Osorio, *Quarta y quinta parte de La Araucana*, 1597.
- Fray Diego de Ocaña, travel manuscript and images, c. 1600–1607.
- *Romancero general*, 1604 Guacolda ballads.
- Lope de Vega, *Arauco domado*, composed around 1599–1604 or by 1618; printed 1625.
- Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del Reino de Chile, Flandes Indiano*, seventeenth century.
- Félix de Augusta and Sigifredo de Fraunhäusl, *Lecturas araucanas*, 1910.

### Critical and documentary scholarship

José Toribio Medina remains indispensable for variant names, early theater, romances, reception, and the history of etymological speculation. Modern philological studies of Oña and Lope clarify literary dependence and classical imitation. Museum and archival catalogues are the strongest evidence for the visual record. Bengoa is essential for later Mapuche political history but should not be cited as an independent witness to the two women’s sixteenth-century existence.

### Final evidentiary finding

Fresia and Guacolda are not recoverable as complete historical persons. Fresia may preserve a near-contemporary anecdotal core, but her name, speech, motivation, and later biography were shaped by epic and chronicle transmission. Guacolda is first securely visible as an Ercillan beloved whose paired dream with Lautaro condenses erotic fidelity and national catastrophe. Their transformation from colonial literary women into supposedly historical Mapuche heroines is itself the central documented history: epic generated chronicle-like authority; drama and painting intensified the images; schools and the state converted them into national ancestors; modern popular biography supplied names, hair color, households, campaigns, and deaths that the early record does not securely contain.
