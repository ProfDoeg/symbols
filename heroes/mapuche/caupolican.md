# Caupolicán: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Historically attested sixteenth-century Mapuche war leader (*toqui* in later and literary terminology); in colonial epic, a mortal heroic captain and Christianized martyr-like epic hero; in modern Chilean and Mapuche memory, an Indigenous resistance hero. There is no evidence that he was regarded as a demigod, supernatural culture hero, saint, or cult-receiving hero.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** García Hurtado de Mendoza’s letter-relación of **24 January 1558**, written at Cañete, which calls Caupolicán an “indio muy belicoso, desasosegado y cruel con sus indios.” This contemporary administrative testimony establishes a real Indigenous commander of that name or a closely related form, but does not attest the log contest, Fresia, Leocán, a universal Mapuche kingship, or most of the deeds later assigned to him. The next crucial witnesses are Jerónimo de Vivar’s chronicle, completed in **1558**, and Alonso de Ercilla’s epic *La Araucana*, published in three parts in **1569, 1578, and 1589**. [Faúndez Carreño, “Muerte y resurrección”](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/muerte-y-resurreccion-el-retrato-aureo-de-caupolican/html/b66d58e6-09a1-4e71-b4fb-7800dabc027d_2.html)

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA—early attestation]** Contemporary or near-contemporary documentary or chronicle evidence, independent insofar as surviving transmission permits.
- **[CE—canonical epic]** Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (1569–1589), the foundational literary construction.
- **[LE—later early-modern elaboration]** Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century epic, drama, chronicle, or learned history dependent on, reshaping, or supplementing Ercilla.
- **[RV—regional variant]** A geographically or textually localized variant distinguishable from the canonical version.
- **[FO—folklore/oral tradition]** A version documented from oral testimony; this label is not applied merely because a modern retelling calls something “tradition.”
- **[MSR—modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern historical or philological conclusion.
- **[MI—modern invention/reinvention]** A modern literary, artistic, nationalist, educational, or commercial creation.
- **[NEG—negative evidence]** No securely documented evidence in the examined corpus.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[EA]** The recoverable historical core is a Mapuche leader active in the Arauco region during the campaign of Governor García Hurtado de Mendoza in 1557–1558. The 24 January 1558 letter confirms that a commander called Caupolicán was then sufficiently important to threaten or challenge the governor. Jerónimo de Vivar’s chronicle uses forms such as **Teopolicán** and once **Otopolicán**, describes him as one-eyed, associates him with **Pelmayquén/Pilmaiquén**, and gives him command of a substantial but not uniquely sovereign force. Alonso de Góngora Marmolejo, writing his history through 1575, independently records his execution and explicitly distinguishes the historical man from the hero whom Ercilla “so greatly exalts”: “Este es aquel Queupulicán que don Alonso de Ercilla en su *Araucana* tanto levanta sus cosas.” [Góngora Marmolejo, digital edition](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/biblioteca_americana/obra/historia-de-chile-desde-su-descubrimiento-hasta-el-ano-de-1575--0/); [Medina’s historical annotations](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-araucana-ilustraciones--0/html/016456e2-82b2-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_57.html)

**[CE]** Ercilla converts this regional commander into the supreme “general” or effective ruler of an epic Araucanian confederacy: a figure of *sapientia et fortitudo*, combining extraordinary bodily force, prudence, justice, severity, and self-command. In the poem he is the son of Leocán, lord of Pilmaiquén, victor of the log-bearing contest, commander after the first Araucanian council, claimant to responsibility for Valdivia’s death and Spanish defeats, husband of Fresia, and finally a baptized, impaled martyr-like enemy. This is the canonical Caupolicán.

### Written forms

Documented spellings include:

- **Caupolicán / Caupolican** — the standard Spanish literary and modern form, fixed above all by Ercilla.
- **Caupolicano** — Ercilla’s metrically useful expanded form.
- **Queupulicán / Queupolicán** — used by Góngora Marmolejo and later writers and philologists; often regarded as closer to a Mapudungun form.
- **Teopolicán / Otopolicán** — forms in Vivar’s mid-sixteenth-century chronicle.
- **Kallfülikan / Kallfúlikan** — a widespread modern Mapudungun respelling or reinterpretation.
- **Kewpulikan / Keupulikan** — modern phonological reconstructions based on *kewpü/keupu* and the early *Queupulicán* spellings.
- **Caupolicán II / Caupolicán the Younger** — later historiographic designation for a supposed son or successor; not an independently attested personal name in the earliest sources.

Mapudungun had no Indigenous alphabetic convention in the sixteenth century. Accordingly, modern spellings in Mapudungun orthographies are reconstructions, not preserved autographs.

### Etymology

**[MSR—disputed]** No single etymology is securely established.

1. **Queupu/Kewpü + -likan interpretation.** Older Chilean philology preferred **Queupùlican/Queupulicán**, connecting the first element with a hard stone, flint, or polished stone. Popular translations include “polished flint,” “hard stone,” or “quartz stone.” The exact segmentation and the meaning of the second element vary between authors.

2. **Kallfü-likan interpretation.** Modern public usage often writes **Kallfülikan**, taking *kallfü* as “blue” and sometimes translating the whole as “blue quartz stone.” This is a modern normalization and semantic reconstruction, not the form in the sixteenth-century Spanish witnesses.

3. **“New chief of stone.”** José Toribio Medina’s collected annotations preserve older competing analyses, including a segmentation *Caapo-licán* glossed “nuevo jefe de roca.” The same annotations object that **Queupùlican** is linguistically preferable. The disagreement itself is the secure finding; confident internet translations that present one English gloss as certain exceed the evidence. [Medina, *La Araucana: ilustraciones*](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-araucana-ilustraciones--0/html/016456e2-82b2-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_57.html)

### Titles and descriptive epithets

- **Toqui.** The standard modern title. *Toki* denotes an axe and, historically, a war leader or office associated with military command. Ercilla more often uses Spanish political and military vocabulary—*general*, *capitán*, *cacique*, *señorío*, *mando*—than a consistently ethnographic constitutional title. Treating Caupolicán as an absolute monarch is therefore **[CE/LE]**, not a transparent description of Mapuche political organization.
- **“General Caupolicano,” “famoso capitán,” “gran guerrero.”** **[CE]** Ercilla, especially canto XXXIV.
- **“Hijo de Leocán.”** **[CE]** Patronymic first securely attested in *La Araucana* II and repeated by Oña, Santisteban Osorio, and Golden Age dramatists.
- **“Señor de hombres y fieras.”** **[LE]** Lope de Vega’s theatrical hyperbole.
- **“Héroe más caracterizado de la raza araucana.”** **[MI]** Language used in the institutional debate over placing Nicanor Plaza’s sculpture in Santiago, reflecting nineteenth-/early-twentieth-century racial-national discourse rather than a colonial epithet. [Zamorano, “Nicanor Plaza y Francisco Gazitúa”](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-71812011000100005&script=sci_arttext)

### Identifications

- **Caupolicán–Christ. [LE, allegorical identification]** In the anonymous/contested-author auto sacramental *La Araucana*, probably early seventeenth century, the log becomes an image of the Cross and Caupolicán competes against allegorical representatives of the Devil, Judaism, and Idolatry. This is Christian theatrical allegory, not Mapuche syncretism or Indigenous belief. [Study of the auto’s authorship and sources](https://insulabaranaria.com/2017/12/03/autoria-y-fuentes-del-auto-sacramental-de-la-araucana/)
- **Hercules, Samson, Atlas. [MI/learned literary comparison]** Rubén Darío’s 1888 sonnet likens him to Hercules and Samson and gives him an Atlas-like burden. These are comparisons, not genealogical or cultic identifications.
- **“The Last of the Mohicans.” [MI/contested art-historical genealogy]** Nicanor Plaza’s statue employs a generalized North American “Indian” visual vocabulary and was later repeatedly said to have originated as a proposed *Last of the Mohicans*. Scholarship securely establishes its European academic and “Indianist” visual conventions; the strongest version—that an already completed Mohican was simply renamed—should be treated cautiously because contemporary documentation of the sculpture’s successive conception is incomplete. [MNBA dossier](https://www.mnba.gob.cl/sites/www.mnba.gob.cl/files/images/articles-8694_archivo_03.pdf)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### García Hurtado de Mendoza, letter-relación, 24 January 1558

**[EA]** This is the earliest presently identified securely dated notice. Written from the newly re-established Cañete, it describes Caupolicán as warlike, restless, and cruel toward his own people. Its rhetoric is hostile colonial military reporting, but it establishes contemporaneous recognition of the name and commander.

It does **not** mention:

- the log test;
- three days of burden-bearing;
- Leocán;
- Fresia or her child;
- Caupolicán’s conversion and execution;
- a divine or hereditary genealogy;
- a hero cult.

#### Jerónimo de Vivar, *Crónica y relación copiosa y verdadera de los reinos de Chile*, completed 1558

**[EA with transmission caution]** Vivar’s chronicle was completed in the year of Caupolicán’s death but survived in a manuscript recovered only in the twentieth century. It uses the forms **Teopolicán/Otopolicán**, describes the man as one-eyed and lord of Pelmayquén, and assigns him a force of about 4,000, fewer than the contingents assigned to some other leaders. Vivar’s naming of a Teopolicán in connection with Valdivia’s death is important but does not reproduce Ercilla’s mature epic hierarchy.

#### Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana*

**[CE]** Spanish epic in ottava rima:

- **First Part, 1569**, cantos I–XV: election, log contest, early war, death of Valdivia, and Lautaro’s rise.
- **Second Part, 1578**, cantos XVI–XXIX: Hurtado de Mendoza’s campaign, battles including Lagunillas and Millarapue, and Galvarino.
- **Third Part, 1589**, cantos XXX–XXXVII: the Andresillo plot, Caupolicán’s capture, Fresia’s denunciation, attempted negotiation, baptism, impalement, and shooting with arrows.

The complete text was printed together in 1589–1590 under Ercilla’s supervision. The poem repeatedly claims historical truth and incorporates the author as participant, but its speeches, classical architecture, council scenes, ethnographic generalizations, chronology, and characterization are literary constructions. Ercilla expressly admits he was absent from Caupolicán’s execution. [Publication history](https://cvc.cervantes.es/obref/fortuna/expo/literatura/lite012.htm); [digitized complete edition](https://cervantesvirtual.com/obra/primera-segunda-y-tercera-partes-de-la-araucana--0/)

### Independent or partly independent colonial chronicles

#### Alonso de Góngora Marmolejo, *Historia de todas las cosas que han acaecido en el Reino de Chile*, completed by 1575

**[EA]** Góngora was a contemporary soldier. His restrained notice says Alonso de Reinoso ordered Caupolicán impaled: “y así murió.” His comment that this is the same man whom Ercilla exalts is decisive historiographically: it confirms a historical prisoner and execution while warning, in effect, that Ercilla’s magnitude is poetic. The original manuscript tradition is complicated; a modern critical edition appeared in 2010. [Critical-edition record](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.31819/9783964560384/html)

#### Pedro Mariño de Lobera/Bartolomé de Escobar, *Crónica del Reino de Chile*

**[EA/LE with serious transmission qualification]** Mariño was a soldier in Chile, but his lost manuscript was extensively recast by the Jesuit Bartolomé de Escobar around 1593; the surviving text was first printed in 1865. It calls Caupolicán noble, rich, and extraordinarily forceful, and incorporates an election by log-bearing. Because the surviving wording is post-Ercillan and editorially mediated, it cannot automatically be used as independent proof of Ercilla’s scene. [Digitized 1865 edition](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Archivo%3ACr%C3%B3nica_del_reino_de_Chile_-_MC0008848.pdf)

### Literary continuations and adaptations

#### Pedro de Oña, *Arauco domado*, 1596

**[LE]** A Lima epic written within the Hurtado de Mendoza family’s apologetic campaign. It counterbalances Ercilla’s marginalization of Governor García Hurtado. Oña makes Caupolicán and Fresia an erotic pair; Caupolicán is distracted from war in a Virgilian/Ovidian love episode and later falls headfirst into a defensive pit during a failed assault. This is not independent biography. [Imogen Choi’s study](https://www.uhu.es/publicaciones/ojs/index.php/etiopicas/article/view/7952)

#### Diego de Santisteban Osorio, *Cuarta y quinta parte de La Araucana*, 1597

**[LE]** A continuation of Ercilla’s epic cycle, repeating the patronymic “son of Leocán” and extending the Araucanian heroic world.

#### Lope de Vega, *Arauco domado*, written around 1598–1603, printed 1625

**[LE]** A stage tragedy/tragicomedy serving the Hurtado de Mendoza apologetic tradition. It dramatizes Caupolicán, Fresia, Tucapel, Rengo, Pillán, and García; makes García Caupolicán’s captor, godfather, evangelizer, and effective execution authority; kills Fresia’s thrown child in some readings/stagings; and ends with the impaled Caupolicán’s Christian sonnet. Its theology and political redistribution of responsibility are theatrical inventions.

#### *La Araucana*, auto sacramental, early seventeenth century

**[LE]** Traditionally attributed to Lope, but modern scholarship has proposed Andrés de Claramonte. It transforms Caupolicán into a Christ figure bearing the Cross/log. Its invented women Fidelfa and Glitelda function chorally.

#### *Algunas hazañas de las muchas de don García Hurtado de Mendoza*, nine playwrights, performed/published 1622

**[LE]** Courtly propaganda. Caupolicán displays chivalric restraint toward Don Felipe. Reinoso again performs the execution, while García becomes baptismal protector rather than cruel judge.

#### Gaspar de Ávila, *El gobernador prudente*, printed 1652/1663

**[LE]** Presents a markedly more “barbaric” Caupolicán: he performs a blood rite, kills the magician Fitón for an unfavorable prophecy, suffers defeat, and is executed by Reinoso. The play’s distribution of blame protects García.

#### Francisco González de Bustos, *Los españoles en Chile*, later seventeenth century

**[LE]** Returns Caupolicán to a dignified, authoritative role and reports his death “puesto en un palo” with exemplary constancy, omitting controversy over conversion.

### Later colonial histories

#### Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz*, composed 1670s

**[LE]** A major source for seventeenth-century Mapuche-Spanish relations and Mapuche political speech, but not an independent eyewitness source for Caupolicán’s 1550s career. Its historical reminiscences belong to a literary-ethnographic work composed more than a century later.

#### Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del reino de Chile, Flandes indiano*, completed 1674; printed 1877–1878

**[LE/learned systematization]** Rosales provides a developed election and death narrative, including Fresia’s reproach, negotiation, conversion, and impalement. The Chilean National Archive explicitly notes that Rosales based this episode on Ercilla. Rosales’s surviving autograph is valuable evidence for seventeenth-century reception, not an independent confirmation of every sixteenth-century incident. [Memoria Chilena on the manuscript and publication](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-3356.html); [Archivo Nacional presentation](https://www.archivonacional.gob.cl/la-muerte-inmortal-de-caupolican)

#### Juan Ignacio Molina, *Compendio de la historia civil del reino de Chile*, 1787 Spanish/Italian-era publication history

**[LE/learned systematization]** Molina organizes the epic-chronicle material into Enlightenment civil history. His account helps fix “Caupolicán the Younger,” treachery, and the succession at Quiapo in later historiography. [Molina, Book III](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Compendio_de_la_historia_civil_del_reyno_de_Chile/Libro_III)

### Mapuche oral corpora

**[NEG with methodological caution]** Published collections associated with Félix de Augusta and Bertha Koessler-Ilg are indispensable records of Mapuche language, narrative, religious knowledge, and oral literature. The searches conducted for this dossier did not identify in their indexed contents an independently collected narrative of the sixteenth-century Caupolicán that can be shown to precede or remain uninfluenced by Chilean schooling, Ercilla, chronicles, or nationalist commemoration.

This does **not** prove that Mapuche communities did not remember Caupolicán. It means that the familiar log-Fresia-impalement cycle cannot presently be cited as securely transcribed pre-literary Mapuche oral tradition. Modern community retellings are genuine acts of Mapuche memory, but their relationship to the national literary canon must be examined case by case. Studies of *ül/ülkantun* demonstrate the vitality of Mapuche oral historical memory, particularly for the nineteenth-century occupation and dispossession, without supplying an independent sixteenth-century Caupolicán cycle. [Pozo, Canio, and Velásquez, oral memory study](https://resonancias.uc.cl/n-45/memoria-oral-mapuche-a-traves-de-cantos-tradicionales-uelkantun-recordando-la-epoca-de-ocupacion-siglos-xix-y-xx/)

## 3. The story: episodes, sources, and variants

### Parentage, territory, and appearance

- **Leocán as father; Pilmaiquén as patrimony. [CE]** Ercilla calls Caupolicán the son of Leocán and associates the family with Pilmaiquén.
- **Lord of Pelmayquén/Pilmaiquén. [EA]** Vivar independently situates Teopolicán there, giving the geographic association more weight than the named genealogy.
- **One-eyed. [EA/CE]** Vivar calls him *tuerto*; Ercilla describes one eye as sightless, red like a garnet. Because both early texts preserve the trait, a real ocular impairment is plausible but not certain.
- **Ideal epic body. [CE]** Ercilla’s canto II presents him as large-bodied, broad-chested, strong, agile, wise, severe, just, astute, and self-controlled. The conjunction of wisdom and force follows a classical heroic topos rather than neutral description. [Faúndez Carreño](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/muerte-y-resurreccion-el-retrato-aureo-de-caupolican/html/b66d58e6-09a1-4e71-b4fb-7800dabc027d_2.html)

### The council and log-bearing election

**[CE, 1569]** After the Araucanians learn that the Spaniards are mortal, their chiefs assemble to choose a commander. Rival claims threaten disorder. The elderly Colo Colo proposes a measurable test: whoever can carry an immense tree trunk longest will command.

Ercilla names successive competitors and escalating durations:

- Paicabí, Angol, and Purén: roughly six hours;
- Ongolmo, Elicura, and Tucapel: more than half a day;
- Lincoya: a full day;
- Caupolicán: **three days and two nights/moons** in the poem’s temporal imagery.

He therefore wins command. The often-repeated formulation “three days and three nights” is a simplifying modern retelling; Ercilla’s poetic chronology is more precisely summarized as three days and two intervening nights. [Canto and scholarship summarized here](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/muerte-y-resurreccion-el-retrato-aureo-de-caupolican/html/b66d58e6-09a1-4e71-b4fb-7800dabc027d_2.html)

**Historicity dispute:**

- **Invention thesis [MSR].** The scene is absent from García’s letter, Vivar, and Góngora’s independent historical notices; its catalogue of warriors and competitive trial resemble Homeric and Virgilian epic construction. Literary critics therefore long treated it as Ercilla’s invention.
- **Possible ethnographic basis [MSR, contested].** Miguel Zugasti’s 2006 study examines Pedro Ordóñez de Ceballos’s *Viaje del mundo* (1614), which reports an analogous American log-bearing leadership test. Zugasti argues that the episode may preserve or adapt an Indigenous selection practice rather than arise solely from classical imagination.
- **Limitation.** Ordóñez’s account is later than Ercilla and circulated in the same Hispanic literary environment. It proves that the motif was not confined to one stanza, but cannot by itself establish that Caupolicán personally underwent the contest. [Zugasti, “El toqui Caupolicán y la prueba del tronco”](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/monsenor_romero/obra/el-toqui-caupolican-y-la-prueba-del-tronco-a-la-luz-de-un-nuevo-texto-entre-etnohistoria-y-literatura-848046/)

### War after Valdivia

**[EA]** Caupolicán’s firmly recoverable activity belongs chiefly to the period after Lautaro’s death in 1557 and during García Hurtado de Mendoza’s offensive. Contemporary evidence makes him one important commander; it does not demonstrate uninterrupted supreme command from Valdivia’s death in 1553.

**[CE]** Ercilla retrojects Caupolicán’s authority into the council before Tucapel and places him over the sequence of Mapuche resistance. In canto XXXIV, the captive boasts:

- “Soy quien mató a Valdivia en Tucapelo”;
- he dismantled Purén;
- he brought Penco down;
- he won many battles.

This is a dramatic self-account within the poem, not a deposition. Other early sources foreground Lautaro in the victories after Tucapel. Góngora Marmolejo treats Lautaro, not Caupolicán, as the central leader of the first rising. Thus:

- **Caupolicán personally killed or ordered Valdivia’s death:** **[CE/variant in Vivar; contested].**
- **Lautaro as principal organizer of the post-Valdivia war:** **[EA]** stronger in Góngora.
- **Caupolicán as supreme leader throughout 1553–1558:** **[CE/LE]**, not securely documented.

### Lagunillas and Millarapue, 1557

**[CE and colonial campaign tradition]** Caupolicán commands or participates in resistance to García’s army. Ercilla’s episodes connect him with Galvarino, whose hands the Spaniards amputate after Lagunillas and who returns to urge resistance. At Millarapue the Mapuche forces are defeated; later retellings frequently make Galvarino Caupolicán’s lieutenant.

The existence of fighting and Spanish mutilation of captives belongs to the documented violence of the campaign. Exact speeches and the neat heroic chain Galvarino → Caupolicán → renewed battle are literary.

### Assault on Cañete and Andresillo’s betrayal

**[CE, Third Part]** Caupolicán plans to take the Spanish fort at Cañete through the assistance of **Andresillo**, an Indigenous servant or auxiliary inside the fort. Andresillo reveals the plan to Alonso de Reinoso. The Spaniards turn the apparent conspiracy into an ambush; the attackers are defeated.

**[LE]** Molina and later national histories convert this into a straightforward story of a treacherous Indigenous informer. Modern retellings vary over whether Andresillo betrayed Caupolicán voluntarily, whether another unnamed Indigenous informant exposed his refuge, and whether the failed fort operation and later capture were one continuous event.

### Capture

**[EA]** Góngora confirms capture and execution but supplies little heroic narrative. Later colonial documentation associates the capture with Alonso de Reinoso’s officers, especially Pedro de Avendaño, near Pilmaiquén.

**[CE]** Ercilla says Caupolicán is surprised at night, hides among his people, is identified, seized, and bound. His captors take him toward Cañete. The poem uses this humiliation to prepare both Fresia’s denunciation and the hero’s recovery of dignity at death.

**Date problem.** Modern institutional accounts commonly give **5 February 1558** for capture after the failed attack and **27 June 1558** for execution. These precise dates have become conventional, but surviving narrative sources do not all present the sequence consistently. They should not be treated like entries in a modern civil register.

### Fresia and the child

**[CE, 1589]** In *La Araucana* XXXIII, **Fresia**, carrying an infant, confronts the bound Caupolicán. She reviles him for allowing himself to be captured alive, refuses to remain his wife or raise a coward’s child, and throws the infant at his feet.

Crucial distinctions:

- Ercilla does **not securely establish that the child dies** from the throw.
- Fresia is not independently attested in the 1558 letter, Vivar, or Góngora.
- The scene first survives in the Third Part of *La Araucana* in 1589, three decades after the event.
- Her alternative names **Güeden** and **Paca** occur in later authors, not in an independent contemporary record.
- The episode is structurally comparable to classical and early-modern exempla in which heroic women shame defeated men; it therefore cannot be read as unmediated Mapuche domestic ethnography.

**[LE]** Rosales repeats the denunciation substantially through Ercilla. Lope heightens the violence and can make the infant’s death explicit. Nineteenth-century painters select the moment because it joins Indigenous “savagery,” eroticized motherhood, captivity, and tragic resistance.

**Finding:** Fresia is a securely attested **literary character from 1589**, but her historical existence is unverified. Calling her simply Caupolicán’s documented wife collapses epic into biography.

### Negotiation, sentence, and baptism

**[CE]** In canto XXXIV, Caupolicán offers Reinoso peace, Spanish royal obedience, and establishment of Christianity if spared. He claims authority over the whole land and asks for time to perform the settlement. Reinoso rejects the offer and condemns him to be impaled and shot.

Ercilla then says God changes him “in a moment”; Caupolicán requests baptism and receives brief instruction before execution. This Christianized ending serves several functions:

- it saves the admired pagan enemy within Catholic theology;
- it intensifies Spanish cruelty because the now-Christian captive is still tortured;
- it assimilates the hero to the “noble enemy” and martyr-like death;
- it permits later court dramatists to redistribute moral blame between García and Reinoso.

**[EA]** Góngora confirms that Reinoso ordered him impaled but does not supply the elaborate conversion scene. Therefore the baptism is possible but only securely documented in Ercilla’s literary account and its dependants.

### Impalement and death

**[EA]** Góngora’s terse report is the strongest independent evidence: Reinoso ordered Cristóbal de Arévalo, camp alguacil, to impale Caupolicán, “y así murió.”

**[CE]** Ercilla gives the canonical sequence:

1. Caupolicán walks barefoot, partly naked and chained to the scaffold.
2. He accepts death calmly.
3. Seeing that the appointed executioner is a Black man—described by the poem with the racialized term *negro gelofo*—he protests that death at such a hand is an added dishonor.
4. He kicks the executioner off the platform.
5. He is seated on the sharpened stake.
6. Six archers shoot deliberately; the poem magnifies this into a body pierced by one hundred arrows.
7. He dies without outwardly yielding to pain.
8. Ercilla states explicitly that he **was not present**, adding that he would have suspended the cruel execution had he been there. [*La Araucana* XXXIV](https://www.profesorenlinea.cl/Biblioteca/Ercilla_Alonso_de/La_araucana/canto_XXXIV.htm)

The execution was torture and exemplary colonial terror. Ercilla simultaneously condemns its cruelty and reproduces racial hierarchy: Caupolicán regards execution by a Black man as worse than execution by an honorable Spaniard. Modern heroic abridgments often omit this scene; evidentiary completeness requires retaining it.

### The body and burial

**[NEG]** No securely identified grave, preserved body, relic, exhumation, or archaeological remains of Caupolicán are documented in the examined primary and institutional records. Ercilla leaves the displayed, arrow-pierced corpse as an image of terror and resistance, not a cult relic. Claims to know a precise tomb should be regarded as unsupported unless accompanied by new archaeological documentation.

### Successor and children

- **Unnamed infant thrown by Fresia. [CE]**
- **Caupolicán the Younger/Lemucaguin. [LE]** Rosales and especially Molina’s later historical synthesis identify a son or successor involved at Quiapo in 1558. The equation “Lemucaguin = Caupolicán II” is learned retrospective systematization, not securely demonstrated by the earliest records.
- **Engol. [LE]** In Oña’s and Lope’s literary world, Engol is associated with Fresia and Caupolicán, but dramatically boasts that only the Sun could be his true father. This is poetic genealogy, not historical evidence.

## 4. Relations with gods, heroes, and named figures

### Genealogies and household variants

| Relation | Version | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|

- **Galvarino** — Mutilated warrior returns to the Mapuche forces, exhorts resistance, and participates in the campaign associated with Caupolicán. Ercilla, Second Part, **1578**. **[CE]**
- **Fresia** — Wife/lover; condemns his capture and throws their infant at his feet. Ercilla XXXIII, **1589**; Oña **1596** turns them into an elaborate erotic couple; Lope dramatizes the domestic tragedy. **[CE/LE]**

- **Alonso de Reinoso/Reynoso** — Captor/judge in the dominant chronicle-epic version; rejects negotiation and orders the impalement. Góngora, by **1575**; Ercilla XXXIV, **1589**. **[EA/CE]**

- **Andresillo** — Indigenous insider whose promised assistance in taking Cañete becomes betrayal and Spanish ambush. Ercilla, Third Part, **1589**. **[CE]**
- **Don Felipe de Mendoza** — In *Algunas hazañas*, Caupolicán stops Tucapel and others from dishonorably killing the isolated Spaniard. Nine-author play, **1622**. **[LE]**

- **Christian God/Christ** — Ercilla says God illuminates the condemned man and he accepts baptism; Lope makes the impaled hero address Christ directly. Ercilla XXXIV, **1589**; Lope, early seventeenth century. **[CE/LE]**
- **King Philip II** — Caupolicán offers to bring the land into obedience to Philip if spared; later theatre places royal imagery beside his death. Ercilla XXXIV, **1589**; Lope, early seventeenth century. **[CE/LE]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

**[NEG]** There is no evidence of an Indigenous or colonial **hero cult** of Caupolicán in the strict sense: no securely attested shrine, priesthood, periodic sacrifice, oracle, sacred relic, invocation in healing, or archaeological *heroon*. He is commemorated, not worshipped.

The term “martyr,” used in modern political language, is metaphorical. Ercilla’s baptism and steadfast impalement produce a martyr-like literary structure, but the Catholic Church did not canonize him and no documented Mapuche ritual makes him a divine being.

### Tomb and relics

**[NEG]** No authenticated tomb, bones, preserved stake, weapons, or bodily relics are known. Cañete is the conventional place of death, but the exact execution and burial site has not been archaeologically established. Later monuments are memorials, not tombs.

### Commemorative ritual and naming

**[MI]** Civic commemoration is extensive:

- statues and replicas;
- streets, districts, schools, clubs, and public venues bearing his name;
- a historical Department of Caupolicán;
- Teatro Caupolicán in Santiago;
- personal naming in Chile and Argentina;
- political organizations, especially the **Sociedad Caupolicán Defensora de la Araucanía**, founded on **3 July 1910**.

These practices amount to civic and political heroization, not ancient cult.

### Public monuments

#### Nicanor Plaza’s *Caupolicán*

Plaza modeled the work while studying in Paris in the 1860s; the sculpture is conventionally dated **1863**. A plaster version and bronze casts followed. The famous bronze on Santiago’s Cerro Santa Lucía was installed in connection with the **1910 centenary**, not securely in 1873 as some popular accounts state. The National Library’s digital record identifies the Santa Lucía bronze as a 1910 placement donated by the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes. [Biblioteca Nacional Digital](https://www.bibliotecanacionaldigital.gob.cl/visor/BND%3A612249)

**[MI]** The figure is semi-nude, muscular, feathered, and carries a club or log: an academic-European composite “Indian,” not an ethnographically accurate sixteenth-century Mapuche commander. Its generalized Indigenous physiognomy made it transferable among national, Pan-American, and “Last of the Mohicans” stories.

Replicas or related casts appeared in Lota, Rengo, Tomé, and elsewhere. The image entered banknotes, coins, textbooks, postcards, and municipal iconography.

#### Temuco monuments

In the 1930s Mapuche organizations pressed for a Caupolicán monument as a means of occupying civic space and monumentalizing an Indigenous heroic past. A replica/variant of Plaza’s figure was installed in Temuco, later becoming a focus of Mapuche appropriation. The **Monumento a la Araucanía**, completed in 1990, also includes Kallfúlikan/Caupolicán beside a machi, Ercilla, a soldier, and a settler—an explicit attempt to stage competing regional histories.

During the protests of **2019**, monuments in Temuco were re-signified: the severed head from a statue associated with the Chilean state order was placed on or near the Caupolicán figure and the Wenufoye displayed. Scholarship treats this as a new decolonizing use of an older, itself colonial-national monument. [Study of Temuco’s civic monuments](https://scielo.conicyt.cl/article_plus.php?lng=es&pid=S0719-64662020000200024&tlng=es)

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### I. Contemporary war record, 1558–1575

**[EA]** García’s letter, Vivar, and Góngora disclose a regional Mapuche war leader, probably associated with Pilmaiquén, active against García Hurtado’s campaign and eventually captured and impaled under Reinoso. His precise rank and responsibility for earlier victories remain uncertain.

### II. Ercilla’s canonical construction, 1569–1589

**[CE]** *La Araucana* supplies nearly every component of the durable legend:

- ideal heroic body and one blind eye;
- Leocán and Pilmaiquén;
- council of chiefs;
- log contest;
- supreme command;
- association with Valdivia’s fall;
- Fresia and the infant;
- dignified negotiation;
- baptism;
- protest against execution by a Black man;
- impalement and arrows;
- posthumous renewal of resistance.

Ercilla’s decisive innovation is ethical ambiguity. The enemy is “barbarian” in imperial vocabulary but often more constant and honorable than the conquerors. Spanish victory becomes morally stained by torture.

### III. Viceregal and courtly revision, 1596–1660s

**[LE]** Oña and the Hurtado-sponsored dramatists seek to repair García’s reputation. Caupolicán remains indispensable because only a magnificent adversary can magnify the governor.

Three recurrent transformations appear:

1. **Erotic:** Oña enlarges the love of Caupolicán and Fresia.
2. **Christian:** Lope and the auto sacramental make him a convert or Christ figure.
3. **Propagandistic:** responsibility for his death moves between García and Reinoso according to the patron’s interests.

### IV. Chronicle consolidation, seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

**[LE]** Rosales, Núñez de Pineda, Córdoba y Figueroa, and Molina recast epic material as civil history. Once Ercilla’s details enter chronicles, later readers mistake repetition for independent corroboration. Alternative name forms, sons, succession narratives, and exact dates accumulate.

### V. Republican national hero, nineteenth century

**[MI]** Independence-era and republican Chile appropriated *La Araucana* as a proto-national epic. The “Araucanian” became an ancestor of Chilean courage even while the Chilean state dispossessed and militarily occupied autonomous Mapuche territory.

This produced a central contradiction:

- the dead sixteenth-century Mapuche warrior was celebrated as an ancestor of the nation;
- living Mapuche people were represented as obstacles to civilization and subjected to territorial seizure.

Monvoisin’s paintings, Plaza’s sculpture, histories by Barros Arana and literary historians, and school editions of Ercilla made Caupolicán nationally visible.

### VI. Modernismo and continental Indianism, 1888 onward

Rubén Darío published the sonnet **“El Toqui”** in Santiago’s *La Época* on **11 November 1888**. Retitled **“Caupolicán,”** it entered the second edition of *Azul…* in **1890**. Its famous opening—“Es algo formidable que vio la vieja raza”—compresses Ercilla’s multi-day contest into a sculptural, sonorous image: Caupolicán bears the log like a mythic superman, compared to Hercules and Samson, and emerges from the forest.

**[MI]** Darío does not preserve an independent tradition; he monumentalizes Ercilla through modernista rhythm, classical-biblical comparison, and racial vocabulary. [Publication history and text](https://poemario.com/caupolican/)

### VII. Twentieth-century Chilean and Mapuche reappropriation

- **1910:** Sociedad Caupolicán Defensora de la Araucanía founded. Its adoption of the name marks an Indigenous political appropriation, initially compatible with integration into Chilean civic life while demanding rights.
- **1902/1942:** Remigio Acevedo Gajardo’s opera *Caupolicán* had its first act performed in 1902; the complete work was staged in 1942.
- **1950:** Pablo Neruda’s “Toqui Caupolicán” in *Canto general* remakes him as body, tree, earth, storm, and anti-colonial resistance. Neruda’s figure belongs to a left-national and continental history rather than colonial Catholic epic. [Fundación Pablo Neruda](https://cultura.fundacionneruda.org/2023/10/articulo-sobre-el-poema-toqui-caupolican-del-canto-general-1950-de-pablo-neruda/)
- Throughout the century, textbooks, children’s histories, municipal names, coins, stamps, plays, and statues standardized the log scene as if it were documented biography.

### VIII. Contemporary decolonizing memory

**[MI/current reception]** Caupolicán now operates in several overlapping registers:

- Chilean national ancestor;
- Mapuche resistance leader;
- anti-colonial martyr;
- schoolbook strongman;
- emblem used by Indigenous organizations;
- commercial and municipal name;
- object of critique because older images dress him as a generic Plains/North American “Indian.”

The plurality is historically significant: neither the nineteenth-century state nor contemporary Mapuche activists possess the image in exactly the same sense.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature

- **Rubén Darío, “El Toqui”/“Caupolicán,” 1888/1890. [MI]** The most internationally influential short literary portrait; it makes the log the entire emblem of the man.
- **Pablo Neruda, “Toqui Caupolicán,” *Canto general*, 1950. [MI]** Re-embeds the hero in landscape, wood, mineral, bodily impairment, and continental resistance.
- Numerous Chilean school anthologies reproduce either Ercilla’s contest or Darío’s sonnet, often without distinguishing sixteenth-century evidence from literary imagination.

### Painting

- **Raymond Quinsac Monvoisin, *La captura de Caupolicán*, c. 1854. [MI]** Depicts the captive leader within nineteenth-century history painting.
- **Monvoisin, *Caupolicán prisionero y Fresia*, shown at the Paris Salon of 1859. [MI]** Centers Fresia’s reproach and infant, synthesizing Ercilla’s literary scene for a European audience. The Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes’ catalogue records two versions and the work’s workshop complications. [MNBA Quinsac catalogue](https://quinsac.mnba.gob.cl/obra?Sec=Obras&idCat=null&idObra=74)

### Sculpture

- **Nicanor Plaza, *Caupolicán*, conventionally 1863. [MI]** The dominant bodily image.
- **Santa Lucía bronze, installed 1910. [MI]**
- **Temuco and regional replicas/variants, twentieth century. [MI]**
- **Monumento a la Araucanía, Temuco, 1990. [MI]**

The Plaza type has often been mistaken for a documentary likeness. No portrait from Caupolicán’s lifetime exists.

### Theatre and opera

- Golden Age plays listed above established the converted, chivalric, or barbarized variants.
- **Remigio Acevedo Gajardo, *Caupolicán*, opera, first act 1902; complete staging 1942. [MI]** Part of the early-twentieth-century “Chileanization” of *La Araucana* and the search for a national art music.
- Teatro Caupolicán, a modern Santiago entertainment venue, carries the name commercially; it is not a ritual theatre dedicated to the hero.

### Nationalist appropriation

Caupolicán became useful to Chilean nationalism because he could symbolize:

- courage supposedly inherited by the Chilean “race”;
- territorial rootedness before Spain;
- resistance that could paradoxically be declared completed and absorbed into the republic;
- a heroic Indigenous past separated from present Indigenous sovereignty claims.

The racial-organic idea of a “Chilean race,” developed around 1880–1920, lacked scientific foundation but materially shaped editions, opera, art, and education. [Study of *La Araucana* in the national context](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-22012022000200061&script=sci_arttext)

Mapuche organizations then reappropriated the same hero for Indigenous representation and rights. This is not a simple opposition between “false Chilean” and “authentic Mapuche” meanings: the modern image developed through conflict, translation, schooling, and political reuse.

### Argentine and trans-Andean reception

**[MI]** The name Caupolicán circulated as a personal name and symbol in Argentina, especially in regions connected to Mapuche and Araucanian history and in modernista literary culture through Darío. Koessler-Ilg’s Patagonian collections belong to the broader preservation of Mapuche oral culture, but no securely independent Argentine Caupolicán epic emerged from the searched corpus.

### Commercial and popular uses

- Teatro Caupolicán;
- streets and avenues;
- schools and sporting clubs;
- banknotes and commemorative coinage using Plaza’s statue;
- postcards and tourist imagery;
- given names throughout Chile and neighboring countries.

These attest reception, not ancient tradition.

### Documented modern inventions and recurring errors

1. **“A photograph/portrait of Caupolicán.”** Impossible; no life portrait survives. Images are modern inventions.
2. **“The log contest is confirmed fact.”** Not independently confirmed; first securely attested in Ercilla.
3. **“Fresia certainly killed the baby.”** Ercilla has her throw the child but does not securely state its death; explicit infanticide belongs to later dramatization.
4. **“Caupolicán was king of all Mapuche.”** An epic and colonial simplification of decentralized leadership.
5. **“He personally executed Valdivia” as uncontested fact.** Conflicting early narrative allocations make this uncertain.
6. **“His exact Mapudungun name and meaning are known.”** Competing reconstructions exist.
7. **“Plaza sculpted an ethnographically accurate Mapuche.”** False; the statue is a European academic composite.
8. **“The Santa Lucía statue was installed in 1873.”** Frequently repeated, but the institutional photographic record identifies the present bronze’s centenary placement in 1910.
9. **“There is a known tomb or relic.”** No verified archaeological evidence was found.
10. **“The story is simply ancient Mapuche oral tradition.”** The documented canonical cycle is primarily traceable to Ercilla and later colonial writing.

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Caupolicán historical?

**Current evidentiary conclusion: yes, but only a restricted core is secure.**

The 1558 gubernatorial letter, Vivar, and Góngora are mutually sufficient to reject the older suspicion that Ercilla invented the man wholesale. The historical core is:

- an Indigenous commander with a name rendered Caupolicán/Queupulicán/Teopolicán;
- active in the Arauco-Pilmaiquén region;
- an enemy of García Hurtado’s forces;
- captured and executed by impalement under Reinoso in 1558.

The following are not equally secure:

- his paternity;
- the log test;
- his absolute supremacy;
- responsibility for all victories after 1553;
- Fresia;
- exact words before death;
- baptism;
- one hundred arrows;
- sons and succession.

Diego Barros Arana emphasized this distinction in nineteenth-century critical historiography: documents before and apart from Ercilla make Lautaro central, while Ercilla created Caupolicán’s towering traditional personality. [Barros Arana, *Historia general de Chile*, vol. II](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/historia-general-de-chile-tomo-segundo--0/html/ff2db864-82b1-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_61.html)

### Did Ercilla invent the log test?

The dispute remains open at two levels:

- **Biographical level:** there is no independent contemporary proof that Caupolicán carried a log for three days.
- **Ethnohistorical level:** analogous descriptions suggest that strength contests or burden-bearing could have had an Indigenous basis.

Zugasti’s comparative evidence weakens the claim that Ercilla necessarily invented the motif ex nihilo. It does not transform the episode into established personal history.

### Epic truth versus eyewitness truth

Ercilla participated in García’s campaign and witnessed some events but arrived in Chile after Valdivia’s death. He explicitly missed Caupolicán’s execution. Modern criticism consequently rejects both extremes:

- *La Araucana* is not a neutral chronicle;
- it is not arbitrary fantasy detached from events.

Its historical names, geography, violence, and campaign framework coexist with invented speeches, classical topoi, telescoped command structures, providential theology, and aesthetic design.

### The source-dependence problem

Rosales, Mariño/Escobar, Molina, and nineteenth-century histories frequently appear in popular writing as separate witnesses. Philological chronology shows that many repeat or rewrite Ercilla. Five later tellings do not equal five independent attestations.

The Mariño problem is especially serious: the soldier’s lost original was “reduced to new method and style” by Escobar, and the surviving edition is post-Ercillan. It must be cited with that qualification.

### Fresia and Guacolda

Both women are central to the emotional architecture of *La Araucana*, yet neither has secure independent contemporary attestation. Scholarship therefore treats them first as epic characters. Their possible basis in unnamed historical women cannot be excluded, but possibility is not evidence.

### Christian conversion

Catholic epic convention favors the noble pagan’s last-minute conversion. Góngora’s silence does not disprove baptism, but the theological shaping is clear. Later dramatists intensified it until Caupolicán became an explicit Christological emblem.

### Ercilla and colonial criticism

Scholars disagree over whether Ercilla is:

- an imperial poet who ultimately naturalizes conquest;
- an internal critic who exposes Spanish cruelty;
- or a deliberately ambivalent author whose admiration for Mapuche resistance destabilizes, without escaping, imperial and racial categories.

Caupolicán’s death best supports the third reading. Ercilla condemns the execution as cruel, imagines that he would have stopped it, and ennobles the victim; yet he Christianizes the hero and reproduces contempt for a Black executioner.

### Monument controversy

Art historians reject naïve use of Plaza’s statue as a Mapuche likeness. Debate concerns how strongly it should be tied to *The Last of the Mohicans*. Securely, it belongs to a transatlantic academic repertoire that collapsed diverse Indigenous peoples into the muscular, feathered “Indian.” Later Chilean display renamed and nationalized that repertoire. [Zamorano study](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-71812011000100005&script=sci_arttext)

### Oral tradition and archival imbalance

Nearly all sixteenth-century written evidence came through Spaniards. Absence from surviving written records is therefore not absence from Mapuche memory. Conversely, a modern oral narration collected after centuries of printed, ecclesiastical, and school circulation cannot automatically be treated as uncontaminated evidence of a pre-contact or sixteenth-century tradition.

Responsible reconstruction must preserve both facts:

- the archive is colonial and radically incomplete;
- source criticism cannot solve that incompleteness by labeling Ercilla-derived material “ancestral oral tradition.”

### Archaeological state

No archaeological discovery has identified Caupolicán’s grave, execution stake, or remains. The places associated with Cañete, Antihuala, and Pilmaiquén retain historical and commemorative importance, but material attribution to the individual is absent.

### Critical conclusions

The documented figure is not reducible either to “real chief” or “fictional hero.” The sources establish a real commander and a real execution, but the culturally consequential Caupolicán is Ercilla’s creation around that core. The log, Fresia, conversion, death tableau, Christological allegory, modernista giant, bronze “Indian,” Chilean racial ancestor, and anti-colonial Mapuche emblem belong to successive, datable acts of representation.

The dossier’s strongest negative findings are equally important:

- no securely documented pre-Ercillan log contest;
- no independent contemporary evidence for Fresia;
- no verified divine genealogy;
- no ancient or colonial hero cult;
- no authenticated tomb or relic;
- no surviving life portrait;
- no demonstrated independent oral version of the complete canonical cycle in the cited Mapuche collections.
