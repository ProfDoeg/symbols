# Fernán González: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: contemporary/near-contemporary]** Tenth- or early-eleventh-century diplomas, annals, and chronicles.
- **[E2: early legendary]** Twelfth-century narrative testimony, especially the *Chronica Naierensis*.
- **[E3: medieval canonical elaboration]** The thirteenth-century *Poema de Fernán González* and its Alfonsine prose adaptation.
- **[E4: later medieval/early-modern accretion]** Late chronicles, romances, monastic histories, drama, and antiquarian reconstructions.
- **[E5: folklore/local tradition]** Claims maintained in monuments, relic traditions, festivals, and place-lore without early corroboration.
- **[E6: modern scholarly reconstruction]** Conclusions inferred critically from documents or literary transmission.
- **[E7: modern invention or appropriation]** Demonstrably modern representations, political uses, and anachronisms.

## 1. Identification, names, and class

**Traditional class:** mortal historical count transformed into an **epic founder and Christian culture hero**. He is not a demigod and was never formally canonized. At San Pedro de Arlanza he was commemorated as founder, benefactor, victorious Christian warrior, and privileged dead; this approaches heroization but does not establish a liturgical cult equivalent to that of a saint.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **[E1]** a Cardeña diploma dated 932 names *comite Fredinando Gundissalbiz in Castella*—“Count Ferdinand González in Castile.” Documents earlier than 932 that call him count, notably supposed acts of 929, survive in later or disputed forms. Consequently 932, not a legendary birth date, is the proper documentary terminus.

Fernán González was a tenth-century magnate who governed Castile and associated eastern territories within the kingdom of León. The historical record supports his accumulation and hereditary transmission of comital power; it does **not** call him an independent king. The figure encountered in literature is larger: providential defender of Castile, conqueror of Muslims and rival Christian rulers, protégé of saints, twice-imprisoned husband rescued by Sancha, and liberator who obtains Castile by exploiting an exponentially increasing debt.

### Names

- **Fernando / Ferdinandus.** **[E1]** The underlying personal name is represented in Latin documents by such forms as *Fredinandus* and *Ferdinandus*. Medieval Castilian **Fernán** is an apocopated vernacular form of Fernando.
- **Gundisalviz / Gundissalviz / González / Gonçález.** **[E1–E3]** The Latin patronymic means “son of Gonzalo”; medieval Castilian writes *Gonçález*, modern Spanish *González*. The father identified by the documentary-genealogical tradition is Gonzalo Fernández.
- **Fernán Gonçález.** **[E3]** Normal medieval vernacular form in the poem and chronicle tradition.
- **Portuguese Fernão Gonçalves** and modern non-Iberian equivalents such as Ferdinand Gonzales are translations, not separate indigenous heroes.
- **“Buen conde,” “buen conde castellano,” “conde de Castiella.”** **[E3–E4]** Formulaic epic and romance epithets stressing legitimate lordship, valor, and solicitude for Castile.
- **“Primer conde independiente de Castilla.”** **[E4–E7]** A retrospective political title, still inscribed on modern monuments, but absent from tenth-century evidence. The Madrid municipal catalogue itself preserves this designation while acknowledging the uncertainty of his alleged independent deeds.
- **“Founder of Castile” or “father of Castilian independence.”** **[E2–E7]** This develops from the *Chronica Naierensis* and later epic-historiographical tradition, not from contemporary titulature.

The name **Fernando** is Germanic, but proposed decompositions vary; popular translations such as “bold traveller” should not be treated as securely recoverable medieval meanings. *González* is transparently patronymic in context; speculative “battle-elf” translations of the remote Germanic compound *Gundisalvus* are modern etymological reconstructions, not medieval epithets.

There is no ancient syncretism with a pagan god or foreign hero. Later comparisons with El Cid, Bernardo del Carpio, Pelayo, or other national heroes are literary and political analogies, not identity equations.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Documentary substrate

**[E1] Tenth-century charters.** Diplomas from Cardeña, Arlanza, Silos, San Millán, and related archives name Fernán as count and date acts by the reigning Leonese king and the count governing Castile. Their evidentiary value is uneven: many survive only in cartulary copies, and monastic archives sometimes interpolated or fabricated privileges. Only one act attributed to Fernán is commonly reported as surviving in an original tenth-century form. Diplomas must therefore be assessed individually, not treated as a homogeneous archive.

The 932 Cardeña formula—*Regnante Ranimiro in Obeto et comite Fredinando Gundissalbiz in Castella*—places Ramiro II above the count. Later documents continue to date by kings of León. **[E6]** This is strong evidence against full juridical independence, though it does not exclude extensive practical autonomy or hereditary consolidation.

**[E1] *Anales Castellanos Primeros*.** Completed around 939, these terse annals belong to Fernán’s own period but supply no developed heroic biography.

**[E1] Sampiro’s chronicle.** Written around 1000 and incorporated into later historiography, it records tenth-century Leonese politics, including comital rebellion and royal authority. It does not tell the horse-and-hawk liberation story.

**[E1] Ibn Ḥayyān, *al-Muqtabis*.** The eleventh-century Andalusi historian, using earlier records, supplies an external Islamic narrative for campaigns involving Ramiro II, Castile, and Córdoba. This evidence confirms warfare but not the poem’s battles against “Almanzor”: the famous chamberlain al-Manṣūr was born around 938 and did not command the campaigns attributed to him in the poem.

### Emergence of the legend

**[E2] *Chronica Naierensis*, late twelfth century.** This is the earliest surviving narrative source to credit Fernán, with a distancing formula equivalent to “as is said,” with freeing the Castilians from Leonese domination. It also contains the liberation-by-Sancha tradition. The chronological problem was already visible to later critics: the historical Sancha had married Fernán decades before the imprisonment that the legend turns into courtship.

**[E2/E6] Lost *Cantar de Fernán González*.** Menéndez Pidal and later scholars reconstructed a vernacular heroic song behind parts of the surviving traditions. Its existence is an inference from chronicle prose, romances, and divergences between the *Poema* and the 1344 chronicle; no manuscript of it survives. Deyermond’s catalogue distinguishes hypothetical versions rather than treating one reconstructed *Cantar* as an extant work.

### Canonical poem

**[E3] *Poema* or *Libro de Fernán González*, approximately 1250–1270, perhaps around 1258–1260.** Written in Castilian *cuaderna vía*, it is a learned clerical poem, not a surviving juglar epic. It reworks historical notices, presumed heroic material, biblical and clerical models, folklore, and monastic memory.

Its association with San Pedro de Arlanza is strong but not absolute proof that the author was a monk there. The work makes Arlanza the spiritual centre of the hero’s victories and burial program; conversely, geographical mistakes and the absence of an explicit authorial identification have led scholars to prefer “a cleric familiar with Arlanza” over “certainly an Arlanza monk.”

The sole substantial manuscript is El Escorial **b-IV-21**, fols. 136r–190v, a paper miscellany copied approximately 1460–1480 in several cursive hands. It is damaged, corrupt, and incomplete, ending during the conflict with Navarre. The large gap between composition and copy is fundamental. The Alfonsine prose tradition preserves material corresponding to the lost ending. Some scholars blame careless copyists; others argue that the exemplar had already been reshaped by oral or performative transmission. [Philobiblon’s manuscript record](https://philobiblon.upf.edu/xtf/servlet/org.cdlib.xtf.dynaXML.DynaXML?source=%2Funified%2FDisplay%2F4788BETA.MsEd.xml&style=MsEd.xsl) dates the copy and describes its hands and provenance.

### Chronicle transmission

- **[E3] Alfonso X’s *Estoria de España*, later thirteenth century.** It prosifies the *Poema*, but also alters its political meaning. The Alfonsine maxim “no one can make a count unless the king makes him” reinscribes Fernán within royal hierarchy. The prose is indispensable for reconstructing the poem’s missing conclusion, although it is an adaptation, not a transparent transcript.
- **[E3/E4] *Crónica de 1344*.** Its Fernán material sometimes follows a line closer to the presumed lost heroic tradition rather than the clerical *Poema*. This is one basis for reconstructing more than one stage of the legend.
- **[E4] Late medieval chronicles and printed histories.** Diego de Valera’s *Crónica abreviada de España* (composed 1479–1481; printed 1482) helped transmit the story into print. Sixteenth-century *Crónicas de Fernán González* often derive from Valera or the general chronicle tradition rather than from independent ancient witnesses.
- **[E4] Gonzalo de Arredondo, abbot of Arlanza, early sixteenth century.** His *Crónica del santo y valeroso caballero el conde Fernán González* intensified the monastic, providential, and nearly saintly construction of the count.

### Romancero and drama

**[E4] Romances.** The Fernán cycle includes:

- “Preso está Fernán González,” concerning captivity and Sancha’s intervention;
- romances of the horse and hawk;
- narratives of the treacherous queen, the Navarrese prison, Sancha disguised as a pilgrim, and the lustful archpriest;
- sixteenth-century learned or “chronicle” romances by Alonso de Fuentes, Lorenzo de Sepúlveda, and the anonymous “Caballero Cesáreo.”

Menéndez Pidal stressed that not every apparently old romance is an independent survival of medieval oral epic: several were deliberately versified from printed chronicles. Sepúlveda’s collection was printed at Antwerp in 1551; the enlarged edition followed in 1566.

**[E4] Lope de Vega, *El conde Fernán González* / *La libertad de Castilla*.** Listed by 1618 and printed in *Parte XIX* in 1624, it dramatizes the canonical battles, prophecies, imprisonment, and liberation. A performance bearing the title is recorded at El Escorial in 1676.

Other seventeenth- and eighteenth-century plays include *La libertad de Castilla*, Mira de Amescua’s *Lo que puede el oír misa*, *La más hidalga hermosura*, *Favores que hizo Dios al señor conde Fernán González*, and *La antigüedad de Segovia*. They are reworkings, not witnesses to tenth-century belief.

### Limits of the item-specific comparative corpus

The *Cantar de mio Cid*, *Historia Roderici*, *Chanson de Roland*, Einhard’s account of Roncevaux, the *Nota Emilianense*, the cycles of Bernardo del Carpio, the Infantes de Lara and Don Rodrigo, the *Crónica de Alfonso III*, and Lope García de Salazar’s *Bienandanzas e fortunas* are essential to a wider Iberian epic dossier. They are **not primary attestations of Fernán González**. Likewise, the Basque Jaun Zuria tradition does not document an interaction with Fernán. Conflating these independent cycles would manufacture a pantheon that the texts do not contain.

## 3. The story

### Ancestry, dispossession, and childhood

**[E3]** The *Poema* embeds Fernán in a providential history of Spain extending from the Visigoths, the Islamic conquest, and early Christian resistance to Castile’s rise. His lineage legitimates inherited lordship.

One version makes the child secretly carried away and raised by a charcoal-burner in the mountains. **[E3: clerical/folkloric elaboration]** This humble concealment magnifies his eventual elevation. A different reconstructed heroic version has him raised by an old warrior who trains him in arms. The versions should not be harmonized: the contrast itself reveals competing models—the hidden noble child of folklore and the precocious epic warrior.

### Lara

**[E3]** “Almanzor” invades with overwhelming numbers. Fernán’s men hesitate; Gonzalo Díaz speaks, and the count rallies them. While pursuing a boar, Fernán enters the hermitage associated with Pelayo near Arlanza. Pelayo prophesies victory. Fernán promises a fifth of the booty and the establishment or enrichment of a monastery, with provision for one hundred monks, and wishes to be buried there.

The Christians win at Lara; Fernán pursues the enemy and rewards Arlanza.

**Historical assessment:** **[E6]** no contemporary source records this battle in the poem’s form. The enemy’s identification as Almanzor is anachronistic. Scholars have compared the episode with historically attested campaigns around Osma and the upper Duero, but that is source criticism, not recovery of a certain event.

### Navarre and Toulouse

**[E3]** A Navarrese king raids Castile. Fernán challenges and defeats him at the Era Degollada; the king dies and Fernán is wounded. A count of Toulouse then advances against Castile. Against counsellors urging accommodation, Fernán fights him at an Ebro ford and kills him; nevertheless, the enemy receives honourable funeral treatment.

These antagonists are epic constructions whose names and roles do not map securely onto documented rulers. Their function is geographical and ideological: Castile must withstand Christian neighbours as well as Muslims.

### Hacinas

**[E3]** Almanzor returns with a greater host. Fernán goes to Arlanza. The deceased Pelayo appears in a dream; San Millán also announces divine assistance and tactical instructions. The Castilians are divided into three formations, associated in the poem with Gustio González, Lope “the Biscayan,” and Fernán.

A fiery serpent or dragon-like prodigy terrifies the army at night. Fernán interprets it so as to restore courage. Fighting lasts several days. Gustio González dies. At the crisis, Santiago and San Millán appear with a celestial host, panic the Muslims, and secure victory. The Castilians pursue the defeated enemy and seize rich booty.

**Development:** Berceo’s *Vida de San Millán* associates Fernán and San Millán with victory and with the saint’s tribute, while the *Poema* relocates the spiritual centre toward Arlanza and Pelayo. Scholarship disagrees over direct borrowing. The safest conclusion is competition among monastic memory systems: San Millán retained a role, but Arlanza became the count’s privileged sanctuary.

### Leonese politics, horse and hawk

**[E3]** The Leonese king covets Fernán’s excellent horse and hunting hawk. The count agrees to sell them for a modest sum provided that, if payment is delayed, the debt doubles at fixed intervals—the *gallarín* arrangement. The king neglects payment; exponential accumulation makes the debt impossible. Fernán ultimately accepts Castile’s freedom in settlement.

This is the legend’s most economical charter myth: freedom is acquired by contractual intelligence, not simply rebellion. **[E2/E6]** No tenth-century document records the transaction. Some antiquarians related it to the genuine practice of giving a horse as confirmation or *robra* of an agreement; one modern reconstruction interprets a possible horse-and-hawk gift as homage after reconciliation. Neither explanation verifies the multiplying debt.

### Two imprisonments and Sancha’s rescues

The tradition distributes captivity in different ways:

1. **Navarrese captivity. [E2–E3]** Fernán travels into Navarre and is treacherously imprisoned. Princess Sancha learns of him, secures access, and releases him after he promises marriage. They flee together. Chronology contradicts a literal reading because the historical Sancha was already his wife long before the later imprisonment usually associated with the story.
2. **Leonese captivity. [E3–E4]** The Leonese king holds Fernán. Sancha, sometimes disguised as a pilgrim to Santiago, exchanges clothes or places with him so he can escape. In expanded versions, an archpriest tries to rape or coerce Sancha during the flight; she kills or defeats him. The episode makes her active loyalty and bodily risk indispensable to Castile’s liberation.
3. **Romance variants. [E4]** Some romances condense the two captivities, shift the captor, or make his desire for Sancha a motive. These are variants, not historical clarification.

### Fate and burial

**[E1]** Fernán’s final documentary appearance is in August 969. By 20 February 970 a diploma names his son García Fernández as count, so Fernán probably died between those dates.

**[E3–E5]** Arlanza claimed his burial beside Sancha. The extant sarcophagi are reused late-antique stone objects with later medieval and modern components, not securely contemporary portrait tombs. Following the monastery’s dissolution, the supposed remains and tombs were transferred to the collegiate church at Covarrubias in 1841. The transfer preserved a powerful local relic claim; it did not establish osteological identity.

## 4. Relations with divine beings and other heroes

### Genealogies

**Historical-documentary reconstruction [E1/E6]:**

- Father: Gonzalo Fernández.
- Mother: usually reconstructed as Muniadona or Munia.
- First wife: Sancha Sánchez of Pamplona, daughter of Sancho Garcés I and Toda Aznárez; previously married to Ordoño II and Álvaro Herraméliz.
- Second wife: Urraca Garcés; descent from this marriage is uncertain.
- Children attributed to Fernán and Sancha include García Fernández and Urraca Fernández; documentary genealogies also name Gonzalo, Sancho, Munio, and Muniadona, but lists vary with source criticism.

**Legendary genealogy [E2–E4]:** Chronicle, poem, ballad, and later antiquarian witnesses rearrange wives and kin. Some later ballad scholarship reports **Constanza** where the dominant tradition has Sancha; early-modern retellings sometimes reverse Urraca and Sancha. These are not alternate historical discoveries.

## 5. Cult, tomb, ritual, and images

### Cult status

No securely attested liturgical office, feast, altar dedication, votive corpus, miracle dossier, or canonization establishes a formal cult of Fernán González. **Absence of evidence is significant:** unlike Santiago or San Millán, Fernán is the beneficiary of sacred aid, not a saint who dispenses it.

Arlanza nevertheless created what may cautiously be called a **monastic memorial cult**:

- it claimed Fernán as founder or principal refounder and benefactor;
- displayed his and Sancha’s tombs;
- linked its site to Pelayo’s prophecy;
- represented burial there as Fernán’s own vow;
- produced or fostered texts that made Arlanza the spiritual birthplace of Castile.

This is institutional commemoration and political memoria, not proof of popular worship.

### Foundation dispute

**[E1/E6]** The monastery possessed early charters connecting Gonzalo Fernández and Fernán with the house, but “Fernán founded Arlanza in 912” is chronologically impossible if taken literally: 912 belongs to his father’s generation. Some records support later endowment or patronage by Fernán. The *Poema* converts benefaction into heroic foundation following Pelayo’s prophecy.

### Tomb and remains

The sarcophagus traditionally assigned to Fernán is a reused late-antique stone coffin; Sancha’s is likewise a Roman or late-antique object mounted in a medieval display. Their reuse reveals high-status memorial practice but does not authenticate the bodies. After the suppression and dispersal of Arlanza, the tombs were moved to Covarrubias in 1841. The modern lid and labels contribute additional layers. No published scientific identification of the remains establishes that they are Fernán and Sancha.

### Relics and attributed objects

The Limoges enamel known as the **Virgen de las Batallas** or Virgin of Arlanza dates approximately 1225–1235—more than two centuries after Fernán. Tradition says he carried it on his horse in battle. The object’s date makes that claim impossible. It is a clear case of **[E7] retrospective relic attribution**, though the legend itself is historically important. The Prado acquired it in 1998 and deposited it in the Museo de Burgos.

### Visual record

- **Arlanza Romanesque frescoes, c. 1200 or early thirteenth century.** Lions, a dragon, griffin, birds, and other fantastic figures decorated a chamber near the chapter house. They do not securely portray Fernán, but formed the visual environment in which his monastic legend matured. Portions are now in the Metropolitan Museum’s Cloisters, the Museu Nacional d’Art de Catalunya, Harvard, and other collections.
- **Arco de Santa María, Burgos, sixteenth century.** Fernán appears among monumental founders and rulers in an urban genealogy of Castile.
- **Royal Palace series, 1743–1748; Fernán statue customarily dated around 1750.** Juan de Villanueva Barbales’ colossal figure gives him cape and sceptre, effectively turning a count into a king. Removed from the palace roof ensemble and later placed in Madrid’s Plaza de Oriente, it bears the modern claim “1 Conde Independiente de Castilla.”
- Later monuments, street names, schools, the Institución Fernán González, and heritage routes continue civic commemoration without constituting religious cult.

### Ritualized modern remembrance

Since 2013, Covarrubias has held the “Fiesta de la Cereza y de la Cuna de Castilla,” and Lara de los Infantes has staged “El Conde Fernán González.” These costumed festivals are **[E7] modern historical reenactments**, not survivals of medieval rites.

## 6. Historical evolution

### Tenth to early eleventh century: magnate, rebel, dynast

**[E1]** Fernán is a count operating within Leonese royal structures. He consolidates several eastern counties, participates in warfare against Córdoba, rebels or intervenes in Leonese succession, suffers temporary displacement, returns to office, and transmits Castile to his son. Nothing contemporary calls him king or records a legal declaration of independence.

### Late twelfth century: liberation memory

**[E2]** The *Chronica Naierensis* first makes him the man who, “as is said,” removed Castile from Leonese subjection. Sancha’s liberation of the prisoner is already attached to him. The distancing phrase suggests circulating story rather than archival knowledge.

### Mid-thirteenth century: clerical canonization of the epic

**[E3]** The *Poema* integrates Fernán into salvation history. His battles become tests of faith; Arlanza becomes oracle, sanctuary, beneficiary, and future tomb; Castile’s political destiny receives divine approval. The work incorporates anti-Muslim crusading rhetoric, rivalry with León and Navarre, and clerical exemplarity.

### Later thirteenth to fourteenth century: royal domestication and variant prose

The Alfonsine *Estoria* absorbs the hero into a monarchy-centred history: Fernán can be exceptional, but the crown remains the source of legitimate comital authority. The 1344 chronicle preserves divergent heroic material, demonstrating that the story was not fixed.

### Fifteenth to sixteenth century: genealogy, print, and Arlanza’s campaign

Chronicles, noble histories, and romances expand the cycle. Arlanza’s Gonzalo de Arredondo presents Fernán as a “holy and valiant knight.” Printing spreads the horse-and-hawk and rescue stories. Learned romances written from chronicles begin to masquerade, in later reception, as ancient oral survivals.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: national theatre and royal iconography

Lope’s drama and successor plays convert the cycle into dynastic and Catholic theatre. Palace statuary inserts Fernán into a continuous gallery of Spanish sovereigns, visually resolving the historical ambiguity in favour of kingship.

### Nineteenth century: romantic nationalism and heritage

Romantic antiquarianism, historical novels, translated ballads, monumental sculpture, and philological collection treat Fernán as a national founder. Sancha becomes romantic heroine; the count becomes a precursor of unified Spain. The movement of the tombs to Covarrubias in 1841 supplies the legend with an accessible shrine-like destination.

### Twentieth century to present: philology, dictatorship, tourism

Critical editions separated the unique manuscript, chronicle prose, and hypothesized lost epic. At the same time, nationalist culture continued to literalize the legend. Under Franco, the count served, with the Cid and “Reconquest,” as an archetype of military, Catholic, and national unity. Contemporary scholarship is markedly more cautious; local festivals, routes, performances, and museums continue the legend as heritage.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship shaping the modern hero

Ramón Menéndez Pidal’s work on the *Romancero de Fernán González* and *La epopeya castellana* placed the count within a theory of a persistent Castilian epic tradition. This recovered neglected texts but encouraged confidence in reconstructing lost oral poems.

C. Carroll Marden’s 1904 critical edition established the Escorial manuscript as the central textual witness. Later editors—Juan Victorio, Miguel Ángel Muro, Itzíar López Guil, Peter Such and Richard Rabone—have revised text, dating, transmission, and interpretation.

Alan Deyermond treated the lost *Cantar* as a carefully qualified reconstruction and emphasized how the *Estoria de España* helps recover the *Poema*’s lost ending. María Eugenia Lacarra and later gender-focused scholarship shifted attention toward Sancha, Teresa, marriage, coercion, and the political work assigned to women.

### Literature and theatre

- Lope de Vega, *El conde Fernán González*, printed 1624.
- Manuel Fernández y González, *Doña Sancha de Navarra*, 1860s: historical-romance expansion of captivity, love, and the impossible debt.
- Mariano José de Larra’s dramatic treatment, *El conde Fernán González y la exención de Castilla*, belongs to nineteenth-century Romantic nationalist recovery.
- Modern stage adaptations often personify “History” and “Legend,” explicitly dramatizing the divide between record and myth.

No major film, television, comic, or game has fixed an internationally dominant Fernán image comparable with modern versions of the Cid. Claims of a large cinematic tradition are unsupported.

### Political appropriation

**[E7] Francoism.** The 1943 “Milenario de Castilla,” built around the year 943, presented Castile as the nucleus of Spain and Fernán as its providential political architect. Official rhetoric described Castile as “base,” “root,” or “marrow” of the nation. This reframed a rebellious regional count as defender of unity and analogue of the *caudillo*. Menéndez Pidal lectured at Burgos on 7 September 1943, while clerical-national histories such as Justo Pérez de Urbel’s *Fernán González* appeared in the same ideological climate.

The appropriation is internally paradoxical: the medieval legend celebrates release from León, whereas Francoist rhetoric uses Fernán to authorize centralized Spanish unity. The contradiction was managed by identifying Castilian expansion with Spain’s destined formation.

### Modern inventions that circulate as ancient

- “First independent count” as a formal tenth-century title.
- The 912 foundation of Arlanza personally by Fernán.
- Battles against the historical Almanzor.
- The exponential horse-and-hawk debt as documentary fact.
- The Limoges Virgin carried by Fernán.
- The present tomb and its contents as archaeologically verified.
- Treating learned sixteenth-century chronicle romances as verbatim survivals of a tenth-century song.
- Treating the *Poema* as a contemporary biography.
- Pairing Fernán with Jaun Zuria, the Cid, Bernardo, or Roland as though they shared one narrative world.

## 8. Historiography and contested-material audit

### Was Fernán the first independent count?

**Traditional position:** the *Chronica Naierensis*, *Poema*, later chronicles, horse-and-hawk episode, hereditary succession, and diminished effective royal control collectively depict liberation.

**Critical position:** all tenth-century dating clauses place Castile within a regnal framework; Fernán was appointed or recognized as count, participated in Leonese dynastic politics, was imprisoned or displaced after rebellion, and never used a royal title. The first explicit liberation claim is approximately two centuries late.

**Measured conclusion:** **[E6]** Fernán consolidated an unusually autonomous, increasingly hereditary principality within the Leonese polity. “Independent” describes later memory better than a securely attested constitutional act.

### Did an oral epic precede the poem?

**For:** the *Naierensis*’ “as is said,” divergent chronicle narratives, epic formulas, romance survivals, and material in the 1344 chronicle point to pre-existing heroic narration.

**Against strong reconstruction:** no *Cantar* manuscript survives; prose chroniclers may reshape one another; romances may derive from chronicles; scholars cannot securely reconstruct one original poem.

**Conclusion:** an earlier legendary or performative tradition is highly probable; the contents and date of a single “original *Cantar*” remain hypothetical.

### Was the poet an Arlanza monk?

**For:** extraordinary promotion of Arlanza, Pelayo, its foundation, endowment, and burial claims.

**Against:** no signature or monastic self-identification; geographical errors unlikely for a resident; an external cleric could know or serve the monastery’s interests.

**Conclusion:** Arlanza institutional ideology is secure; precise authorship is not.

### Are Lara and Hacinas historical battles?

No contemporary annal names them as the poem does. Hacinas against Almanzor is chronologically impossible. Attempts to identify these stories with Osma, Simancas, or other campaigns are **[E6] modern source harmonizations**. The legendary battles may preserve generalized memory of frontier war, but their narrated form is thirteenth-century evidence.

### Was Sancha’s rescue historical?

The *Naierensis* is late, and the courtship chronology conflicts with the historical marriage. The second, Leonese rescue has still later and more elaborate forms. Sancha’s role is therefore securely attested as medieval legend, not as a tenth-century event.

### Tomb and cult

Arlanza’s burial claim is old and institutionally coherent, but the sarcophagi are reused objects and the contents lack secure archaeological identification. “Cult” should be limited to monastic memoria unless new liturgical or votive evidence appears.

### Nationalism

Romantic, Francoist, and regional heritage uses do not merely repeat the medieval story. Each selects a different Fernán:

- medieval Arlanza: divinely favoured patron;
- Alfonsine court: great but subordinate count;
- Romantic nationalism: father of liberty;
- Francoism: Catholic warrior and architect of Spanish unity;
- modern local heritage: founder of Castilian identity and tourism.

### Related Iberian scholarship

Menéndez Pidal’s epic model, Deyermond’s lost-literature catalogue, Lacarra’s ideological and gender analysis, and Richard Fletcher’s sceptical treatment of comparable Cidian legend provide relevant methods. Fletcher’s *The Quest for El Cid* is comparative historiography, not evidence about Fernán. The same distinction applies to research on Roncevaux, Covadonga, the Infantes de Lara, and Jaun Zuria.

## 9. Chronology of attestation and remembrance

|---|---|
| **932** | Secure diploma names *Fredinando Gundissalbiz* as count in Castile under Ramiro II. |
| **930s–960s** | Diplomas and annalistic material document comital government, warfare, rebellion, and restoration; authenticity varies by act. |
| **August 969** | Last documentary appearances of Fernán. |
| **20 February 970** | García Fernández appears as count; Fernán was probably dead. |
| **c. 1000** | Sampiro’s chronicle preserves the Leonese political context, not the full legend. |

| **late 12th century** | *Chronica Naierensis* gives the earliest surviving liberation and Sancha-rescue traditions. |
| **c. 1230s** | Berceo’s *Vida de San Millán* links Fernán’s warfare to Saint Millán’s heavenly patronage. |
| **c. 1250–1270** | Composition of the *Poema de Fernán González*, probably near the beginning of Alfonso X’s reign. |

| **1344** | The *Crónica de 1344* preserves divergent heroic material. |
| **c. 1460–1480** | Escorial b-IV-21, the unique substantial verse manuscript, is copied. |

| **2013** | Modern Fernán/Castile reenactment festivals begin at Covarrubias and Lara de los Infantes. |
| **Present** | The figure persists in scholarship, civic nomenclature, monuments, performance, tourism, and debate over Castilian and Spanish identity. |

## 10. Sources and assessment

### Primary and near-primary evidence

- Tenth-century monastic diplomas, especially Cardeña, Arlanza, Silos, and San Millán cartularies. Each requires diplomatic criticism because original, copy, interpolation, and forgery cannot be conflated.
- *Anales Castellanos Primeros*, c. 939.
- Sampiro, chronicle, c. 1000.
- Ibn Ḥayyān, *al-Muqtabis*, eleventh century using earlier Andalusi material.
- *Chronica Naierensis*, late twelfth century.
- Gonzalo de Berceo, *Vida de San Millán*, thirteenth century.
- *Poema de Fernán González*, c. 1250–1270; Escorial b-IV-21, copied c. 1460–1480.
- Alfonso X and collaborators, *Estoria de España*, later thirteenth century.
- *Crónica de 1344*.
- Diego de Valera, *Crónica abreviada de España*, 1479–1481; printed 1482.
- Gonzalo de Arredondo, *Crónica del santo y valeroso caballero el conde Fernán González*, early sixteenth century.
- Lorenzo de Sepúlveda and the chronicle-romance tradition, 1551 onward.
- Lope de Vega, *El conde Fernán González*, printed 1624.

### Major modern scholarship

- C. Carroll Marden, *Poema de Fernán González: texto crítico* (1904).
- Ramón Menéndez Pidal, *Romancero de Fernán González* and *La epopeya castellana*.
- Alan Deyermond, studies of the poem’s biblical usage, lost Castilian epic, and the relation between the manuscript and the *Estoria de España*.
- María Eugenia Lacarra, ideological and gender-focused readings of medieval epic.
- Juan Victorio, Miguel Ángel Muro, Itzíar López Guil, and Peter Such with Richard Rabone, modern editions and translations.
- Studies of Arlanza monastic memory and the poem’s supernatural structure.
- Francisco Javier Peña Pérez, work on Castilian medieval myths and their Francoist appropriation.

The surviving record is asymmetrical: administrative evidence establishes a historical count, while the complete heroic personality appears only centuries later. That is not a defect to be repaired by harmonization. It is the central fact of Fernán González’s tradition.
