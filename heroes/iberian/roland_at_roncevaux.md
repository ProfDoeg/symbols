# Roland and Roncevaux: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early record]** securely attested material close to the Carolingian event.
- **[Canonical epic]** the Oxford *Chanson de Roland*, normally dated in composition to c. 1075–1100, preserved in a manuscript copied in the later twelfth or early thirteenth century.
- **[Medieval variant]** a medieval textual or visual witness that differs from the Oxford poem.
- **[Learned accretion]** clerical, chronicle, genealogical, or antiquarian elaboration.
- **[Regional tradition]** Iberian, Basque, Italian, German, Scandinavian, or other local adaptation.
- **[Folklore]** orally collected or topographically attached legend whose antiquity cannot automatically be inferred from the age of the place.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** a scholarly inference rather than an express statement of a primary source.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** demonstrably modern adaptation, political use, or popular-culture transformation.

Dates refer to composition or attestation; manuscript dates are supplied separately where materially different.

## 1. Identification, Names and Epithets

**Class:** In the only early historical notice, Roland is a mortal Frankish office-holder and warrior, *Hruodlandus Brittannici limitis praefectus*, “Hruodland, prefect of the Breton frontier.” In medieval epic he becomes Charlemagne’s nephew, foremost of the Twelve Peers, Christian warrior and martyr-like heroic dead. He was attached to alleged tombs and relics and sometimes treated with quasi-sacral reverence, but no securely documented liturgical cult, feast, priesthood, canonical sainthood, or archaeologically verified *heroon* is known.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Einhard’s Latin *Vita Karoli Magni* 9, written probably in the 820s–830s, names Hruodland among the Frankish magnates killed when *Wascones* attacked the rear guard during Charlemagne’s return across the Pyrenees in 778. Einhard supplies no parentage, age, deeds, weapon, horse, companion, horn, speech, burial, or exact named pass. [The Latin text reads](https://www.documentacatholicaomnia.eu/03d/0770-0840%2C_Eginhardus_%5BEinhardus%5D%2C_Vita_Karoli_Magni%2C_LT.pdf): “*Eggihardus … Anshelmus … et Hruodlandus Brittannici limitis praefectus … interficiuntur*.”

### Names

- **Hruodlandus/Hroudlandus/Rotlandus** — Latinized manuscript spellings of a Frankish Germanic personal name. Rita Lejeune analyzes it as *hruod/hroth-* “fame, glory” + *land* “land, country”; “glory of the land” is a possible name-gloss, not an ancient epithet applied to the hero. The name was independently current in the eighth century, so not every historical Hruodland is the Roncevaux commander. **[Early record/onomastic reconstruction]** [Lejeune](https://www.persee.fr/doc/barb_0001-4133_1979_num_65_1_55399).
- **Rollant/Rolant** — Old French forms in the epic tradition.
- **Roland** — modern French, English, and German literary form.
- **Roldán** — Spanish.
- **Rotllà/Rotllan** — Catalan.
- **Erroldan/Errolan** — Basque forms associated particularly with giant-lore, stones, footprints, and Pyrenean place-legends.
- **Orlando** — Italian form, dominant in Pulci, Boiardo, Ariosto, and opera.
- **Roelant** — Middle Dutch, notably in the *Roelantslied* tradition.
- **Rólant/Roland** — Scandinavian ballad and saga forms.
- **Rodlane** — accusative or vernacularly influenced Latin form in the *Nota Emilianense*, c. 1065–1070.

### Titles, relationships, and epithets

- **“Prefect of the Breton frontier”** — Einhard, c. 820s–830s; the only early title. “Count of the Breton March” is a reasonable modern normalization, but Einhard says *praefectus*, not “paladin.” **[Early record]**
- **“Nephew” (*nepos*) of Charlemagne** — already implied by the *Nota Emilianense*, whose Twelve are called the king’s *neptes*, c. 1065–1070; explicit and fundamental in the later epic. **[Early legendary attestation]**
- **“Count” (*quens*) and “baron”** — recurrent in the Oxford poem. **[Canonical epic]**
- **One of the Twelve Peers** — the *Nota Emilianense* already knows twelve named or collectively conceived companions; the Oxford poem systematizes the peer group. **[Early legendary attestation/canonical epic]**
- **“Prince/lord of Blaye”** — Pseudo-Turpin and related burial tradition, twelfth century. No eighth-century document makes Roland lord of Blaye. **[Learned accretion]**
- **Paladin** — later conventional class-name for Charlemagne’s champions, especially after Italian romance; not Einhard’s terminology.
- **Christian martyr-like hero** — Roland dies praying, confessing, offering his glove to God, and receiving angels in the Oxford poem. This is literary sacralization, not proof of canonization. **[Canonical epic]**
- **Giant** — in Basque and Pyrenean folklore Errolan/Roldán often becomes a superhuman stone-thrower whose horse and body mark the landscape. Julio Caro Baroja regarded this as Roland’s schematic assimilation into Basque giant-lore, not evidence for an ancient Basque deity. **[Folklore; modern analysis]** [Caro Baroja](https://www.fundacioncajanavarra.es/cultura-y-educacion/archivo/etnografia-historica-de-navarra-volumen-II.pdf).

### The place-name

Modern forms are French **Roncevaux**, Spanish **Roncesvalles**, and Basque **Orreaga**. Medieval forms include *Rozaballes*, *Rencesvals*, and *Runcievallis*. The derivation of modern “Roncesvaux/Roncesvalles” from a Latinized “valley of thorns” is conventional; the proposed derivation from Basque *Erro-zabal* is modern and disputed. Neither Einhard nor the revised Royal Frankish Annals names Roncesvaux: they say only the Pyrenees or Pyrenean pass. The exact battlefield therefore remains a historical question, not a datum furnished by the ninth-century texts.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 The Carolingian record

1. **Royal Frankish Annals, entry for 778.**  
   The earliest annalistic recension records Charlemagne’s Spanish expedition, hostages received from Muslim magnates, the destruction of Pamplona, and his return, but suppresses or omits the defeat. A revised recension produced around the turn of the ninth century adds a Vascon ambush on the rear guard and says the Franks were disadvantaged by terrain and mode of fighting. It does not name Roland. **[Early record]** [Latin evidence](https://saraceni.uni-koeln.de/wiki/Annales_regni_Francorum_0002).

2. **Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni* 9, c. 820s–830s.**  
   Names Eggihard, Anselm, and Hruodland; identifies Hruodland’s office; says the enemy attacked the baggage and rear guard from wooded heights, killed the detachment, looted the baggage, and dispersed under night’s cover. It neither dates the battle explicitly to 15 August nor calls the place Roncesvalles. **[Early record]** [Latin](https://www.documentacatholicaomnia.eu/03d/0770-0840%2C_Eginhardus_%5BEinhardus%5D%2C_Vita_Karoli_Magni%2C_LT.pdf); [English translation](https://resources.saylor.org/wwwresources/archived/site/wp-content/uploads/2010/12/Einhard-The-Life-of-Charlemagne.pdf).

3. **The Astronomer, *Vita Hludovici*, c. 840.**  
   Remembers the Pyrenean disaster while narrating Louis the Pious’s career, demonstrating continued courtly knowledge. It does not independently create the developed Roland cycle. **[Early record]**

No eighth- or ninth-century archaeological object securely identifies Roland, Durendal, the olifant, or the battlefield.

### 2.2 Before the Oxford poem

4. **The *Nota Emilianense*, c. 1065–1070.**  
   This Latin marginal note in Codex Aemilianensis 39, fol. 245r, from San Millán de la Cogolla, is the earliest compact witness to a developed Roland story. It names *Rodlane, Bertlane, Oggero Spatacurta, Ghigelmo Alcorbitunas, Olibero,* and Bishop Turpin; brings Charles to Zaragoza; leaves Roland in the rear; and says Saracen peoples kill him at *Rozaballes*. It proves that a recognizable Carolingian-Roncevaux legend circulated south of the Pyrenees before the surviving Oxford manuscript. It does **not** prove that the Oxford text already existed in its present form. **[Secure early legendary attestation]** [text and bibliography](https://arlima.net/mp/nota_emilianense.html).

5. **“Roland at Hastings,” 1066 — disputed.**  
   William of Malmesbury, writing in the twelfth century, says a *cantilena Rollandi* was sung before the Norman battle line at Hastings. This is evidence for what a later historian believed about 1066, not contemporary proof that the Oxford poem was performed there. The identity and wording of the alleged song are unrecoverable. **[Learned testimony; contested]**

### 2.3 The French textual tradition

6. **The Oxford *Chanson de Roland*.**  
   Old French/Anglo-Norman, 4,002 decasyllabic lines in 219 assonanced *laisses*. Most scholars place the poetic form around 1075–1100; Bodleian MS Digby 23, part II, is materially dated to the late twelfth or early thirteenth century, though older scholarship often dated it c. 1125–1150. Its concluding “*Ci falt la geste que Turoldus declinet*” does not securely identify Turold as author: *declinet* can imply tells, copies, renders, or concludes. The manuscript has no narrative illuminations. **[Canonical epic]** [Bodleian manuscript notice](https://www.arlima.net/mss/united_kingdom/oxford/bodleian_library/digby/23.html); [Old French transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/La_Chanson_de_Roland/Manuscrit_d%E2%80%99Oxford/Transcription_Wikisource); [English translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/roland-ohag.asp).

7. **The broader *Roncevaux* family.**  
   The Châteauroux–Venice 7, Venice 4, Paris, Cambridge, Lyon, and fragmentary versions are not mere scribal copies. They alter and expand episodes: Aude’s thirty Oxford lines can grow to hundreds; Venice 4 adds the capture of Narbonne; Lyon omits the Baligant episode; rhymed versions add miracles, Ganelon’s attempted flights, and a reworked trial. **[Medieval variants]** [comparative corpus description](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503518275-1).

8. **Pseudo-Turpin, *Historia Karoli Magni et Rotholandi*, c. 1130–1140.**  
   Latin clerical pseudo-history incorporated into the *Codex Calixtinus*. It makes Archbishop Turpin the narrator, embeds Roland in an apostolic and Compostelan geography, supplies the combat with the Muslim giant Ferracutus/Ferragut, and distributes heroic graves and relics along pilgrimage routes. It was received as historical testimony for centuries but is pseudepigraphic. **[Learned accretion]** [edition](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.medievalacademy.org/resource/resmgr/maa_books_online/smyser_0030.htm).

9. **Konrad’s *Rolandslied*, c. 1170.**  
   Middle High German adaptation commissioned in the orbit of Henry the Lion; intensifies crusading, penitential, and imperial-Christian ideology. Roland is even more explicitly God’s warrior. **[Regional adaptation]**

10. **Middle Dutch *Roelantslied*, thirteenth-century tradition.**  
    A vernacular reworking concentrating on the Roncevaux episode, now surviving in fragments and later witnesses. **[Regional adaptation]** [manuscript survey](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kien001hand01_01/kien001hand01_01_0051.php).

11. **Old Norse *Karlamagnús saga*, principally thirteenth century.**  
    Translates and compiles several branches of Charlemagne material; its Roncevaux branch regionalizes the French tradition. **[Regional adaptation]**

12. **Scandinavian ballad tradition, including “Roland og Magnus kongen.”**  
    Roland fights heathens and blows his horn in a compressed ballad narrative. Surviving collections are early modern or modern, so an unchanged medieval oral pedigree cannot simply be assumed. **[Folklore/late textual attestation]**

### 2.4 Iberian texts

13. **The Navarrese *Cantar de Roncesvalles*.**  
    Probably composed in the early thirteenth century; surviving only in two parchment folios copied c. 1310 in Navarre, now Archivo Real y General de Navarra MS 212. Its hundred verses show Charlemagne finding and lamenting Turpin, Oliveros, and Roldán. Oliver’s mutilated corpse differs from Oxford; Roland’s body is described without an obvious wound; Durendal’s marks remain in stone. Discovered in 1916 and first edited by Ramón Menéndez Pidal in 1917. **[Regional medieval variant]** [official archival account](https://www.navarra.es/es/noticias/2019/08/14/el-archivo-de-navarra-presta-el-manuscrito-del-cantar-de-roncesvalles-para-una-exposicion-en-la-biblioteca-nacional); [text](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Cantar_de_Roncesvalles).

14. **Spanish chronicle and *romancero* tradition of Bernardo del Carpio.**  
    Thirteenth-century chronicles combine incompatible genealogies and stories: Bernardo is variously tied to Alfonso II or Alfonso III and to a secretly married noble couple; at Roncevaux he becomes the Spanish defender who defeats or kills Roland. Later ballads famously make him crush the supposedly invulnerable Roland in his arms. This is a Spanish counter-memory, not evidence about 778. **[Regional medieval and early-modern accretion]** [Pattison’s chronicle analysis](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/olifant/article/download/13907/20079/36131); [Lejeune on the “anti-Charlemagne” formation](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hispa_0007-4640_1943_num_45_2_2953).

15. **Later Iberian ballads.**  
    The *romancero* retained Roncesvaux, Bernardo, Roland’s death, and Charlemagne’s grief. Dating must be attached to the surviving printed or manuscript ballad, often fifteenth–sixteenth century, not to a conjectural eighth-century song.

### 2.5 Texts named in the brief that are contextual, not Roland sources

- The ***Cantar de mio Cid*** and ***Historia Roderici*** do not narrate Roland or Roncevaux. They are comparative evidence for Castilian heroic discourse and for the contrast between a relatively documentable Rodrigo Díaz and a legendary epic image.
- The ***Poema de Fernán González***, the **Infantes de Lara**, and ballads of **Don Rodrigo** belong to other Spanish heroic cycles.
- The ***Crónica de Alfonso III*** supplies the earliest extended Asturian court version of Pelayo and Covadonga, not Roland.
- Lope García de Salazar’s fifteenth-century ***Bienandanzas e fortunas*** records the **Jaun Zuria** foundation legend of Biscay, not a Roland episode. Its value here is comparative: it shows how late-medieval Basque-Iberian writing fashioned legitimating heroes. Conflating Jaun Zuria with Roland would be a modern error.
- Francoist deployment of **El Cid**, Pelayo, and the “Reconquista” provides comparative evidence for twentieth-century heroic politics, but does not establish a comparably central Francoist cult of Roland.

## 3. The Story, Cycle by Cycle

### 3.1 What the early record says

In 778 Charlemagne entered Iberia after receiving overtures from Muslim opponents of the Umayyad emir of Córdoba. Zaragoza did not yield as expected. On the return, the Franks destroyed Pamplona’s defenses. Somewhere in the Pyrenees, *Wascones* attacked the baggage and rear guard from advantageous wooded heights. Roland, Eggihard, Anselm, and others were killed; the attackers plundered the baggage and escaped.

That is the recoverable Carolingian narrative. The often-repeated date 15 August 778 derives from chronological reconstruction, not Einhard’s sentence. The conventional identification with the western pass at Roncesvalles is strong but not textually explicit in the earliest sources; eastern-Pyrenean locations proposed by Rita Lejeune and others remain minority hypotheses. No excavated weapon, grave, or battle layer resolves the location. **[Early record/modern reconstruction]** [review of the geographical controversy](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/frrec/article/view/89153/83705).

### 3.2 The *Nota Emilianense*

Charles comes to Zaragoza with twelve nephews or champions, each commanding troops. Persuaded to accept gifts and retreat rather than let the army starve, he leaves the strong warrior Roland in the rear. Saracen peoples kill Roland at Rozaballes. Already present are Oliver, Turpin, Ogier, William, and Bertram. Absent are the Oxford poem’s Ganelon plot, horn debate, detailed death scene, trial, and Baligant. **[Early legendary attestation]**

### 3.3 The Oxford *Chanson de Roland*

#### The embassy and betrayal

After seven years in Spain, Charlemagne has conquered everything except Zaragoza, held by King Marsile. Marsile’s adviser Blancandrin proposes a fraudulent submission. Roland nominates his stepfather Ganelon as Frankish envoy. Ganelon interprets this as a lethal insult, conspires with Marsile and Blancandrin, and identifies Roland as the man whose death will break Charlemagne’s strength. Returning, he recommends accepting Marsile’s terms and then nominates Roland to command the rear guard. **[Canonical epic]**

#### The rear guard

Roland commands the Twelve Peers and 20,000 Franks. Marsile’s enormous Saracen host attacks. The historical Vascons have disappeared; the narrative instead constructs a world war between Christians and polytheistic “Saracens.” Its alleged Muslim theology—Mahound, Apollin, Tervagant—is Christian fantasy, not an accurate description of Islam. **[Canonical epic]**

#### The horn dispute

Oliver sees the enemy and urges Roland to sound his ivory horn, the **olifant**, while Charlemagne is still within reach. Roland refuses three times because calling prematurely would bring shame upon his lineage and “sweet France.” After catastrophic losses, Roland wishes to blow; Oliver now says the act is too late and condemns Roland’s reckless valor. Archbishop Turpin mediates: the horn cannot save them, but it may bring Charlemagne back to avenge them and recover their bodies. Roland blows so violently that his temples burst and blood runs from his mouth and ears. Charlemagne hears; Ganelon repeatedly dismisses the sound until Duke Naimes recognizes that Roland is in distress. **[Canonical epic]**

The poem therefore does not present refusal as unambiguously admirable. Roland’s *prouesse* and Oliver’s *sapience* form a moral argument about courage, prudence, reputation, and feudal responsibility.

#### Durendal

Roland’s sword is **Durendal/Durindana**. In the poem an angel caused Charlemagne to give it to Roland. Its golden hilt contains relics: a tooth of St Peter, blood of St Basil, hair of St Denis, and part of the Virgin Mary’s garment. Mortally wounded, Roland attempts to break it against stone so it cannot fall to pagans, but the stone splits and the sword remains intact. Later traditions convert these blows into clefts and mountain landmarks, including the Brèche de Roland. **[Canonical epic; later folklore]**

#### Companions and death

Oliver fights with **Halteclere** and, blinded by wounds, accidentally strikes Roland; they reconcile before Oliver dies. Turpin continues fighting despite grave wounds, blesses the dead, and collapses. Roland gathers the bodies of the peers, attempts to destroy Durendal, then lies beneath a pine facing Spain. He confesses his sins, raises his glove to God, and dies. Angels—Gabriel, Michael “of the Peril,” and a cherub—carry his soul to paradise. The posture facing Spain asserts that he dies unconquered. **[Canonical epic]**

#### Revenge and judgment

God halts the sun for Charlemagne, enabling pursuit and vengeance. Marsile dies after losing his hand; Queen Bramimonde eventually converts and is baptized Juliana. Emir Baligant arrives with a universal pagan host and is killed by Charlemagne. At Aachen, Roland’s betrothed Aude dies on hearing of him. Ganelon argues that he took lawful private vengeance, not treason; Thierry defeats Pinabel in judicial combat, and Ganelon is torn apart by four horses. His relatives who stood surety are hanged. **[Canonical epic]**

These executions are narrated approvingly by the poem. The work’s religious violence, coerced mass baptism after Zaragoza’s fall, and imagined annihilation of non-Christians should not be softened into generic chivalry.

### 3.4 The Spanish counter-cycle: Bernardo del Carpio

In later Spanish chronicle and ballad traditions, Charlemagne is the foreign invader rather than Christendom’s emperor. Bernardo del Carpio joins Spanish and sometimes Muslim forces against him and kills Roland. Versions disagree about Bernardo’s king, parentage, motive, and Roland’s manner of death. One famous ballad solution makes Roland magically or naturally invulnerable to weapons; Bernardo squeezes him to death. This reversal is the ideological point: Roncevaux becomes a Spanish victory over French imperial pretension. **[Regional medieval/early-modern accretion]**

No contemporary Asturian, Frankish, or Arabic source places Bernardo at the 778 battle. His historicity is unsupported.

### 3.5 Pseudo-Turpin and Ferragut

Pseudo-Turpin moves Roland through a Christianized Spanish campaign. At Nájera he duels the giant Ferracutus/Ferragut for several days. During pauses they debate theology; Ferragut reveals that his navel is vulnerable, and Roland kills him there. The combat appears monumentally on the late-twelfth-century façade capital of the so-called Palace of the Kings of Navarre at Estella, signed or associated with Martín de Logroño. **[Learned accretion; medieval visual attestation]** [Estella museum](https://www.museogustavodemaeztu.com/en/the-building/).

### 3.6 Italian transformation: Orlando

- **Luigi Pulci, *Morgante*, 1478–1483:** turns Carolingian epic toward comic, grotesque, and courtly adventure.
- **Matteo Maria Boiardo, *Orlando innamorato*, first books 1482–1483; unfinished third book printed 1495:** Orlando falls in love with Angelica. Carolingian war merges with Arthurian-style wandering, enchantment, and erotic pursuit. **[Renaissance reinvention]**
- **Ludovico Ariosto, *Orlando furioso*, 1516; revised 1521 and 1532:** Angelica loves the Muslim soldier Medoro; discovering their union drives Orlando mad. He abandons armor and identity and devastates the landscape until Astolfo retrieves his lost wits from the Moon. Restored, he returns to Christian service. This Orlando is recognizably descended from Roland but is no longer principally the dying hero of Roncevaux. **[Renaissance reinvention]** [text and edition history](https://www.sacred-texts.com/neu/orl/index.htm); [Metropolitan Museum account](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Fragonard_Drawing_Triumphant.pdf).

### 3.7 Basque and Pyrenean giant-lore

In Basque-Navarrese and Pyrenean traditions, Errolan/Roldán throws stones, cleaves rock, leaps abysses, and leaves hand-, foot-, knee-, or hoof-prints. “Roland stones” can be prehistoric menhirs retrospectively attributed to him. Such stories include:

- **Erroldan-arriya/Roldán’s stone** at Uharte-Arakil and other megalithic stones.
- **Pasos de Roldán** between Roncesvalles and Mezkiritz.
- **Brèche de Roland** near Gavarnie/Ordesa.
- **Salto de Roldán** north of Huesca, where his horse leaps between cliffs and leaves hoofprints.
- Rocks near Hendaye supposedly hurled from Peñas de Aya.
- Springs or split rocks produced by sword, lance, or horse.

The earliest publication or ethnographic collection must be distinguished from the supposed antiquity of the belief. Jean-François Cerquand collected Basque Roland legends in the nineteenth century; José Miguel de Barandiaran, Julio Caro Baroja, and later ethnographers recorded further examples. These traditions may reuse older giant and footprint motifs, but the claim that Roland replaced a specific prehistoric Basque god is unproved. **[Folklore/modern reconstruction]**

## 5. Cult, Tomb, Relics, and Ritual

### No securely attested hero cult

There is no eighth- or ninth-century shrine, sacrifice, annual rite, votive inscription, or burial securely attributable to Roland. He is not a canonized saint. Language calling him a “martyr” describes epic form and reception, not an established liturgical cult.

### Blaye

The Oxford poem says Roland, Oliver, and Turpin are placed in white marble sarcophagi and associates Roland’s burial with Saint-Romain at Blaye. Pseudo-Turpin further systematizes the burial itinerary. By the twelfth century Blaye thus functioned as a claimed heroic tomb on the road from Spain.

Camille Jullian’s documentary study concluded that there is no historical evidence that Charlemagne buried Roland at Blaye; the tradition is explicable as medieval localization. François I reportedly ordered the supposed tomb opened in 1526, showing royal belief or antiquarian curiosity, not authentication. **[Medieval shrine-claim; early-modern reception]** [Jullian](https://www.persee.fr/doc/roma_0035-8029_1896_num_25_98_5445).

### Bordeaux and the olifant

The poem says pilgrims can see Roland’s split horn at Bordeaux. Medieval churches and treasuries possessed prestigious ivory olifants, often assigned to famous rulers or heroes. No surviving horn has a demonstrable chain of custody to 778. **[Medieval relic-claim]**

### Roncesvalles and Ibañeta

The hospital and collegiate complex at Roncesvalles developed as a pilgrimage institution, not as an excavated Carolingian battlefield shrine. The Chapel of the Holy Spirit or *Silo de Carlomagno* served as an ossuary; its identification with bones of the 778 dead is recorded only much later, prominently from the seventeenth century. Excavations have not produced a securely attributable mass grave or artifact assemblage from Roland’s rear guard. **[Later localization; archaeology negative]** [survey of burial traditions](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccmed_0007-9731_1981_num_24_94_2172).

### Durendal at Rocamadour

A sword fixed high in the rock at Rocamadour was presented in local tradition as Durendal, sometimes with a fertility practice in which women touched or approached the sword while praying. The Oxford poem does not send the sword to Rocamadour: Roland tries unsuccessfully to break it and then keeps it beneath or beside him. The story that he threw it hundreds of kilometers to Rocamadour is later folklore. The displayed object was not authenticated as an eighth-century weapon and disappeared, apparently stolen, in 2024. **[Folklore/modern heritage object]**

### Topographic “relics”

Clefts, footprints, hoofprints, stones, and springs attributed to Roland are best understood as legendary claims on natural or prehistoric features. Their physical reality is not evidence for the attributed deed. Conversely, their distribution is genuine evidence for the geographical spread of belief.

### Civic Roland statues

From the later Middle Ages, knightly Roland figures were erected in north and east German and Central European towns. They are political-legal monuments, not cult statues. The 1404 stone Roland at Bremen replaced an earlier wooden figure and represents civic autonomy, jurisdiction, market rights, and privileges claimed under imperial authority. It was erected after Bremen joined the Hanseatic League, but Roland statues are not exclusively Hanseatic; their distribution is strongly connected with Saxon and Magdeburg-law regions.

The Bremen Roland is 5.5 meters high, more than 10 meters with pedestal and canopy, and entered the UNESCO World Heritage List with the town hall in 2004. **[Late-medieval civic appropriation]** [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1087); [art-historical analysis](https://academic.oup.com/arthistory/article-pdf/38/2/324/53989523/ahis12152.pdf).

## 6. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### 778–c. 840: court memory

A failed rear-guard action is first omitted or minimized, then acknowledged. Einhard names three elite dead. Roland is important enough to name but receives no special biography.

### c. 840–1065: poorly documented transformation

The record between Einhard and the *Nota Emilianense* is fragmentary. Later claims of an uninterrupted, textually stable heroic song are hypotheses. Personal-name evidence and indirect literary allusions may show growing fame, but they do not reconstruct a lost poem word for word.

### c. 1065–1100: international epic formation

The *Nota* already has Zaragoza, twelve champions, Roland in the rear, Oliver, Turpin, and Saracen killers. The Oxford poem then produces a highly organized tragedy of betrayal, heroic excess, Christian martyrdom, and imperial vengeance.

### Twelfth–thirteenth centuries: clerical, visual, and regional expansion

Pseudo-Turpin binds the story to Compostela and relic geography. Konrad Christianizes it for a German imperial audience. Estella represents Roland and Ferragut in stone. Chartres’s Charlemagne window and other church programs visualize the Carolingian sacred-war cycle. The Navarrese *Roncesvalles* creates an Iberian lament tradition. French textual versions expand Aude, miracles, and Ganelon’s fate.

### Thirteenth–fifteenth centuries: counter-memory and civic hero

Spanish historiography gives Roncevaux to Bernardo del Carpio. Basque and Pyrenean landscapes accumulate Roland marks. German towns detach Roland from battlefield narrative and make his upright sword-bearing body an emblem of law and civic freedom.

### Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: Orlando

Pulci, Boiardo, and Ariosto shift the center from heroic death to romance, enchantment, erotic obsession, madness, and recovery. Orlando becomes one of Renaissance Europe’s most reusable fictional protagonists.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: opera and court spectacle

Lully’s *Roland* (Versailles and Paris, 1685) turns Ariosto into a French *tragédie en musique*. Later examples include Steffani’s *Orlando generoso* (1691), Ristori/Vivaldi’s *Orlando furioso* (1713–1714), Vivaldi’s *Orlando furioso* (1727), Handel’s *Orlando* (1733), and Haydn’s *Orlando paladino* (1782). Their Orlando is primarily the lover driven mad, not the historical prefect. **[Early-modern reception]**

### Nineteenth century: rediscovery and national epic

Francisque Michel published the Oxford manuscript in 1837. After France’s defeat in 1870, Gaston Paris lectured on *La Chanson de Roland et la nationalité française*, presenting the epic as a resource for national recovery. School editions and literary histories made Roland an embodiment of sacrificial Frenchness. This was not merely the neutral recovery of a medieval classic: philology and national pedagogy developed together. [CNRS account](https://lejournal.cnrs.fr/articles/les-ecrivains-au-coeur-du-recit-national); [nationalism study](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/81yy4-q3r10).

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Roland remained a school-text, monument, tourist figure, operatic hero, and name for fictional knights and magical swords. Frank Cassenti’s film *La Chanson de Roland* (1978) frames the epic as a performance by medieval travelers and subjects heroic ideology to a Marxist-inflected critique. Modern fantasy, comics, anime, and games freely detach “Roland,” “Orlando,” “Durandal,” and the “paladin” from the medieval plots; those uses are reception, not variant medieval evidence.

## 7. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and criticism

- Alfred de Vigny’s “Le Cor” (1825) helped make the horn and last stand central to French Romantic memory.
- Robert Browning’s “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came” (1855) takes its title immediately from Shakespeare’s *King Lear*; any connection to the Carolingian Roland is atmospheric and onomastic, not a retelling.
- Ariosto’s Orlando, rather than the Oxford Roland, dominates much later painting and opera.
- Tolkien’s Boromir—horn-blowing, surrounded, and killed while defending companions—has often been compared with Roland. The comparison is interpretive; Boromir is not a stated medieval variant.

### Visual arts

- Late-twelfth-century Estella: Roland fighting Ferragut.
- Thirteenth-century Chartres Charlemagne window: Roncevaux within a sacred royal cycle.
- Fifteenth-century illuminated chronicles, including Simon Marmion’s multi-scene Roncevaux imagery.
- Tiepolo’s 1757 Villa Valmarana frescoes and Boucher’s *Angelica and Medoro* (Salon of 1765) visualize Ariosto’s romance.
- Nineteenth-century historicist painting repeatedly shows Roland sounding the horn or dying beneath a tree, fixing the modern iconic image.

### Opera

- Lully, *Roland* (1685).
- Vivaldi, *Orlando finto pazzo* (1714) and *Orlando furioso* (1727).
- Handel, *Orlando* (1733).
- Haydn, *Orlando paladino* (1782).

These adapt the Boiardo–Ariosto love-and-madness cycle rather than Roncevaux directly.

### Film

- Frank Cassenti, *La Chanson de Roland* (1978), 110 minutes in its theatrical listing, embeds performances of the epic among pilgrims and players traveling toward Compostela. **[Modern critical reinvention]** [film record](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/film/la_Chanson_de_Roland/1928).

### National and political use

- **French nationalism:** After 1870 Roland became a model of military sacrifice, loyalty, and refusal to flee. The epic was taught as an early manifestation of the French nation, although its language, manuscript setting, feudal geography, and crusading ideology resist simple modern nationality.
- **Spanish counter-nationalism:** Bernardo del Carpio made Roncevaux a victory over foreign French domination. Early-modern epics and drama repeatedly used the opposition.
- **Basque memory:** Modern Basque accounts restore the Vascon attackers erased by the French poem, sometimes treating the battle as an ancestral victory against Frankish invasion. This corrects a central epic distortion but can itself become a modern national allegory.
- **German civic and later nationalist use:** Roland statues originally served varied urban legal and political meanings. Later imperial and National Socialist readings could recode them as symbols of obedience, Germanness, or military virtue. Such readings are not the single original meaning of every statue. [Magdeburg-law project](https://magdeburg-law.com/de/magdeburger-recht/glossar/altmaerkischer-roland/).
- **Francoist Spain:** The regime’s primary medieval heroes were El Cid, Pelayo, Ferdinand III, and the Catholic Monarchs. Roland, a defeated Frankish invader in Spanish counter-tradition, was not an equivalent central Francoist icon. Using “Franco’s Cid” as evidence for “Franco’s Roland” would be false equivalence.
- **Extremist appropriation:** Modern European far-right discourse sometimes incorporates crusading and Carolingian imagery into a generalized “Christian Europe” mythology. A specific claim that Roland is central to any organization requires a dated document; resemblance to the poem’s Christian–Muslim war is not itself proof of appropriation.

### Psychological and structural readings

Rank, Raglan, Campbell, Dumézil, and Nagy did not establish a single scholarly explanation of Roland.

- **Rank/Raglan:** Hero-pattern schemes can map noble birth, exile, testing, and violent death, but Roland lacks a stable birth-and-childhood narrative in the early corpus. Applying the full pattern requires selecting later accretions.
- **Campbell:** The “monomyth” poorly fits the Oxford poem: Roland neither departs voluntarily on an initiatory quest nor returns transformed. The poem is a last-stand tragedy embedded in imperial war.
- **Dumézil:** Trifunctional Indo-European models have sometimes been applied to heroic groups, but no consensus reconstructs Roland as a transformed Indo-European god.
- **Nagy/oral poetics:** Comparative epic theory helps explain formula, performance, and heroic fame, but cannot by itself demonstrate a continuous oral text from 778 to Oxford.

## 8. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 8.1 Was there a historical Roland?

The answer is narrowly yes: Einhard names an office-holder killed in the Pyrenean ambush. Beyond name, office, allegiance, and death in the engagement, biography becomes conjecture. No independent contemporary genealogy makes him Charlemagne’s nephew. The epic Roland is therefore based on a historical name and death, not a recoverable historical personality.

Claims that a coin bearing a similar name proves Roland’s governorship or biography remain disputed: the name was not unique, attribution is difficult, and numismatic identity cannot be assumed from spelling alone.

### 8.2 Who attacked?

The revised Royal Frankish Annals and Einhard say *Wascones*. The Oxford tradition replaces them with Saracens. “Basques” is the normal modern translation, although the ethnic and geographical range of Carolingian *Wascones* can include populations north and south of the Pyrenees. What is secure is that the early sources do not blame an Islamic army for the ambush.

### 8.3 Where did it occur?

Roncesvalles/Valcarlos is the traditional and still dominant identification. The early texts only say the Pyrenees. Alternatives have included Cerdanya, Andorra, and the eastern passes. Topography in a poem composed three centuries later cannot conclusively locate the battle. No decisive battlefield archaeology has been found.

### 8.4 When and how did the epic arise?

Two classic models dominated:

1. **Gaston Paris and traditionalism:** short heroic songs arose soon after 778, circulated orally, accumulated material, and eventually produced the surviving epic.
2. **Joseph Bédier:** chansons de geste were largely composed much later around pilgrimage roads and shrines, with clerics and jongleurs converting localized memorial claims into epic.

The *Nota Emilianense* weakened any theory that made the complete legend a sudden invention of the surviving Oxford manuscript, because it proves a developed pre-Oxford story. It does not prove three centuries of unchanged oral transmission. Ramón Menéndez Pidal’s “neo-traditionalism” argued for a long, variable oral-written continuum. Current work more often emphasizes **mouvance**: performances and manuscripts repeatedly re-created the material rather than transmitting one recoverable original verbatim. [Summary of the “Roland question”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccmed_0007-9731_1961_num_4_15_1196).

### 8.5 Dating the Oxford poem and manuscript

Composition near 1100 is conventional; proposals range across the later eleventh and early twelfth centuries. The Oxford codex is later than the presumed composition. The Norman Conquest, crusading ideology, Anglo-Norman language, and allusions to contemporary institutions have all been used in dating arguments. None makes “written by Turold in 1066” a demonstrated fact.

### 8.6 The First Crusade question

The poem’s holy-war language is compatible with crusading culture, but this does not prove it was composed as direct First Crusade propaganda. Christian warfare, conversion rhetoric, and conflict in Iberia predate 1095. Conversely, the Oxford poem’s mature polarization of Christian and “pagan” worlds makes its eleventh–twelfth-century ideological setting essential.

### 8.7 Oral-formulaic composition

Repetition, parallel *laisses*, formulaic epithets, and performance markers support an oral or oral-derived dimension. They do not establish that Digby 23 is a stenographic record of a singer or that every repeated unit is ancient. Written composition and performance were not mutually exclusive.

### 8.8 Historicity of Bernardo del Carpio

No contemporary evidence verifies Bernardo. The chronicle versions disagree on dynasty, parentage, and chronology because separate narrative complexes were combined. He is historically important as a literary answer to French Carolingian prestige, not as a proven victor of 778.

### 8.9 Tombs and relics

Blaye’s tomb, Bordeaux’s horn, Roncesvalles’s bones, and Rocamadour’s Durendal are attestations of belief, pilgrimage economy, and localization. None has an eighth-century provenance. The old argument that the Oxford poet’s phrase “pilgrims see it” proves the prior authenticity of a relic mistakes the existence of a display for the authenticity of its attribution.

### 8.10 Basque giant or pre-Christian god?

Recorded Errolan folklore securely shows assimilation to giant and landscape-forming motifs. A stronger theory—that Errolan is the Christian name laid over a specific indigenous god—has no securely identified ancient inscription, image, or mythic text. It remains comparative speculation.

### 8.11 Major modern scholarship

- Gaston Paris, *Histoire poétique de Charlemagne* (1865): long-development and national-literary model.
- Joseph Bédier, *Les Légendes épiques* (1908–1913): roads, shrines, and clerical-jongleur creation.
- Ramón Menéndez Pidal, especially *La Chanson de Roland et la tradition épique des Francs* and *La epopeya castellana*: neo-traditionalism and Romance epic continuity.
- Dámaso Alonso, 1953: publication and analysis of the *Nota Emilianense*.
- Jules Horrent, *Roncesvalles* (1951): major study of the Spanish fragment.
- Rita Lejeune and Jacques Stiennon, *La légende de Roland dans l’art du Moyen Âge* (1966): foundational iconographical corpus.
- Cesare Segre and Ian Short: influential critical editorial work on the French tradition.
- Modern *mouvance* and reception studies treat each manuscript version as a historically situated realization rather than debris from a single ideal original.

## 10. Conclusions on the State of the Evidence

1. A Frankish official named Hruodland was killed in the Pyrenean ambush of 778; this is the firm historical nucleus.
2. Nothing securely contemporary makes him Charlemagne’s nephew, one of twelve peers, Ganelon’s stepson, Oliver’s companion, Aude’s fiancé, owner of Durendal or an olifant, or a Christian martyr.
3. By c. 1065–1070, the *Nota Emilianense* proves that Zaragoza, Roland’s rear-guard role, Rozaballes, Saracen killers, twelve companions, Oliver, and Turpin already belonged to a trans-Pyrenean legend.
4. The Oxford poem is one brilliant realization, not the entire tradition and not a chronicle of 778.
5. The substitution of Saracens for Vascons is medieval ideological transformation. It converts a politically complicated Frankish defeat into holy-war tragedy and eventual Christian victory.
6. Spanish tradition answered with Bernardo del Carpio, transforming the same defeat into native resistance against foreign empire.
7. Basque and Pyrenean folklore transformed the warrior into a giant whose body, horse, and sword explain the landscape.
8. Alleged tombs, horns, bones, and swords document medieval and later belief; none is authenticated to Roland.
9. Roland statues document civic law and political symbolism, not worship.
10. The modern “national hero” was substantially constructed through nineteenth-century editing, education, commemoration, and political reading. That reception is as historically real as the medieval legend, but it must not be projected back into 778.
