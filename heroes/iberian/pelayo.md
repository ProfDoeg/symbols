# Pelayo: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** mortal Christian war-leader (*princeps*), first ruler of the Asturian dynastic sequence, and subsequently an epic-national founder; not a demigod, canonized saint, or securely attested recipient of an independent hero cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the Latin *Chronica Albeldensia* or *Crónica Albeldense*, compiled under Alfonso III and completed in its first form in 881–883, roughly 160 years after the conventionally dated revolt. Its notice says that Pelayo “first took up rebellion against” the Saracens in Asturias and assigns him a nineteen-year reign. No securely contemporary text, inscription, coin, charter, image, or archaeological deposit names him. The *Chronicle of 754*, the fullest near-contemporary narrative of the conquest, does not mention Pelayo or Covadonga. [S1, S2]

## Evidentiary key

- **[E1: securely attested early material]** Evidence contemporary or nearly contemporary with the alleged events.
- **[E2: early retrospective tradition]** Ninth- or early-tenth-century Asturian material, already separated from the alleged events by about 160–200 years.
- **[E3: later canonical elaboration]** High-medieval chronicles and derivative Arabic accounts.
- **[E4: medieval/early-modern accretion]** Post-thirteenth-century legend, antiquarian reconstruction, drama, shrine history, and genealogy.
- **[E5: folklore or devotional tradition]** Belief and practice not independently demonstrated for the eighth century.
- **[E6: modern scholarly reconstruction]** A reasoned historical hypothesis, not a primary attestation.
- **[E7: modern invention or reinvention]** Modern literary, visual, political, or commercial creation.

“Securely attested” below describes the existence and content of a source—not necessarily the historical truth of what that source narrates.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

Pelayo is the name attached by late ninth-century Asturian historiography to the leader of an otherwise poorly documented northern revolt following the Islamic conquest of 711. The *Albeldense* makes him the first insurgent against Muslim government in Asturias. The two recensions of the *Crónica de Alfonso III* turn that compressed notice into a foundation story: the election of a leader, resistance in a Marian cave, divine reversal of an immense Muslim army, restoration of the church, and dynastic succession through Pelayo’s daughter Ermesinda. [E2; *Albeldense*, c. 881–883; *Crónica de Alfonso III*, late ninth/early tenth century; S1, S2]

What can responsibly be reconstructed is much narrower. A northern leader named Pelayo probably stood near the beginning of the Asturian ruling genealogy; García de Castro considers a revolt in or around 718 the best historical reconstruction. Pedro Chalmeta argues much more radically that the entire Pelayo–Covadonga localization may be a late Asturian transposition of a conflict that the contemporary *Chronicle of 754* places in the Pyrenees. Neither position converts the detailed Alfonsine story into contemporary testimony. [E6; S1, S3]

### Forms of the name

- **Latin:** *Pelagius*, accusative *Pelagium*. This is the form in the *Albeldense* and Alfonsine chronicles. [E2; S1, S2]
- **Castilian/Spanish:** *Pelayo*; honorific modern-historical usage, *don Pelayo*.
- **Asturian:** *Pelayu*.
- **Galician-Portuguese:** *Paio*; medieval variants include *Payo* and *Pelaio*.
- **French:** *Pélage*.
- **Italian:** *Pelagio*, notably the title of Mercadante’s 1857 opera.
- **Arabic historiography:** usually rendered in modern transcription as **Balāy/Belāy**; medieval Arabic authors also use expressions translated as “the Rock of Pelayo.” The forms occur only in considerably later texts. [E3; S4]

The name is ordinarily derived from Latin *Pelagius*, from Greek Πελάγιος (*Pelágios*), “of the sea” or “marine,” from πέλαγος, “open sea.” That is a normal personal-name etymology, not evidence that Pelayo was a sea deity or mythic water-hero. [E6; S5]

Chalmeta has proposed, expressly tentatively, that a common Latin expression involving *pelagium* (“the sea”) might have been misunderstood or transformed into the personal name *Pelagius*. This is an ingenious modern conjecture, not an accepted derivation and not supported by an early manuscript that documents the transformation. [E6, disputed; S1]

### Titles and classifications

- **Princeps:** the Alfonsine Latin says that the gathered Asturians elected Pelayo *principem*. This can mean leader or ruler and need not imply the developed institution later conveyed by “King of Asturias.” [E2; S2]
- **Rex:** the *Albeldense* places him at the head of a regnal list; subsequent historiography routinely calls him king. [E2–E3]
- **Spatarius:** the Rotense recension calls him a *spatarius* of the Visigothic kings Wittiza and Roderic—probably a court or military office. The Sebastianense suppresses that notice and instead makes him the royal-blooded son of Duke Favila. [E2, competing versions; S6]
- **First king of Asturias:** canonical medieval and modern regnal classification, not an eighth-century self-description.
- **First king of Spain / founder of Spain:** early-modern and modern ideological titles. No early medieval source calls him king of a Spanish nation-state. [E4–E7; S7, S8]
- **Initiator of the Reconquista:** retrospective designation. The late medieval chronicles constructed a restoration of Gothic Spain; the historiographical noun “Reconquista” acquired its familiar national-period meaning much later, especially between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. [E4–E7; S7]

### Parentage and origin: irreconcilable versions

1. **The Rotense version:** Pelayo was a *spatarius* of Wittiza and Roderic who entered Asturias with his unnamed sister under Muslim domination. It does not supply noble parents. [E2; late ninth/early tenth century; S6]

2. **The Sebastianense version:** Pelayo was the son of Duke Favila/Fáfila and “of royal stock”; surviving Goths in Asturias elected him. This recension more explicitly makes the Asturian monarchy the legitimate continuation of Toledo. [E2; S2]

3. **An Albeldensian genealogical interpolation or manuscript tradition:** “Pelagius, son of Vermudo, grandson of King Roderic of Toledo.” The wording is textually and chronologically problematic and conflicts with the Favila genealogy. It should not be harmonized with it. [E2/E3, variant; S1]

4. **Later genealogies:** medieval and early-modern writers made him a close kinsman, nephew, or descendant of various Visigothic kings. These are learned attempts to perfect dynastic continuity, not independent evidence. [E3–E4]

5. **Local-Asturian reconstruction:** some modern historians consider him more plausibly a member of a regional aristocracy, perhaps connected with refugees or with an indigenous power network. This explains his ability to lead without accepting the detailed Gothic pedigree. [E6; S3]

No early source identifies Pelayo with a Basque hero, Jaun Zuria, El Cid, Bernardo del Carpio, Fernán González, or Roland. Such figures belong to neighboring but separate heroic dossiers.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Contemporary silence

The *Chronicle of 754*, written by a Christian author in al-Andalus and unusually well informed about the governors and wars of the conquest generation, records neither Pelayo nor a battle at Covadonga. It describes an unsuccessful expedition under ʿAbd al-Malik against inhabitants of the Pyrenean heights. [E1; 754; S1]

This silence proves that Pelayo and Covadonga made no detectable impact on this particular surviving narrative. It does not logically prove that no local revolt occurred. It does, however, prevent treating the later battle speeches, names, numbers, and miracles as contemporary evidence.

There is likewise no accepted eighth-century Pelayo coinage, victory inscription, royal diploma, grave inscription, or securely excavated Covadonga battle layer. [E1: absence of evidence; S1]

### The Asturian chronicles

#### *Chronica Albeldensia*

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Composition:** first completion 881–883, later continued.
- **Survival:** principally in later codices, including the tenth-century Codex Vigilanus/Albeldensis.
- **Role:** earliest securely datable named notice; Pelayo initiates rebellion in Asturias and heads the dynasty.
- **Limits:** already separated from the alleged events by roughly five generations; it is court historiography from Alfonso III’s reign. [E2; S1]

#### *Crónica de Alfonso III*: Rotense

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Composition:** normally placed in the late ninth or opening tenth century; the relation and priority of its recensions remain debated.
- **Role:** fullest adventurous version. Pelayo is a former royal *spatarius*; Munuza’s coercive marriage to Pelayo’s sister supplies a personal grievance; Pelayo escapes, is elected by the Asturians, debates Bishop Oppa, wins at the cave through divine intervention, defeats Munuza, and rules nineteen years. [E2; S2, S6]

#### *Crónica de Alfonso III*: Sebastianense or *ad Sebastianum*

- **Language:** polished Latin.
- **Composition:** generally treated as an ideological revision of related material around the same courtly period.
- **Manuscripts:** its textual transmission is late; the surviving witnesses do not place an autograph in Alfonso III’s hands.
- **Role:** strengthens neo-Gothic legitimacy. Pelayo becomes the son of Duke Favila, of royal descent; the more novelistic Munuza-and-sister episode is removed. [E2; S2, S6]

The convenient phrase “Alfonso III wrote the chronicle” is itself a traditional attribution. Modern criticism distinguishes court sponsorship, redaction, and manuscript recensions.

### Later Christian chronicles

- **The so-called *Historia Silense* or *Chronica Hispana*, early twelfth century:** transmits and integrates the Asturian royal foundation into Leonese history. [E3]
- **Lucas of Tuy, *Chronicon Mundi*, completed c. 1236:** renews the providential and dynastic account and helps connect Pelayo’s victory with the Cross later known as the Cruz de la Victoria. [E3]
- **Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada, *De rebus Hispaniae*, completed 1243:** places Pelayo firmly in a universal history of the Spanish monarchy and the recovery of Gothic territory. [E3]
- **Alfonso X’s *Estoria de España*/*Primera Crónica General*, begun in the 1270s:** vernacularizes and canonizes the narrative for later Castilian historiography. [E3]
- **Late medieval and early-modern chronicles:** progressively convert a northern Christian ruler into “first king of Spain” and Covadonga into the first act of a continuous national recovery. [E3–E4; S7]

### Arabic corpus

There is no contemporary Arabic account of Pelayo. Later materials include traditions attributed to:

- ʿĪsā ibn Aḥmad al-Rāzī, late tenth century;
- Ibn Ḥayyān, eleventh century;
- *Akhbār majmūʿa*, conventionally dated to the eleventh century in its received form;
- *Fatḥ al-Andalus*, early twelfth century;
- Ibn al-Athīr, thirteenth century;
- Ibn ʿIdhārī, late thirteenth/early fourteenth century;
- al-Nuwayrī, fourteenth century;
- al-Maqqarī, seventeenth century.

The best-known Arabic-side story says that Pelayo began with about 300 followers in a cave; hunger reduced them to thirty men and ten women, living on honey found in rock fissures; the Muslims withdrew contemptuously, asking what harm “thirty wild asses” could do. [E3; S4]

This is not contemporary Muslim confirmation of the Alfonsine account. David Arbesú’s textual comparison argues that the extant Arabic notices share a textual ancestry and probably depend, directly or indirectly, upon a tradition already shaped by the Asturian story. The late Arabic sources therefore cannot simply be counted as multiple independent witnesses. [E6; S4]

### Corpus-boundary finding

The *Cantar de mio Cid*, *Historia Roderici*, *Chanson de Roland*, Einhard’s Roncesvalles notice, *Nota Emilianense*, *Poema de Fernán González*, Infantes de Lara cycle, Bernardo del Carpio romances, Don Rodrigo romances, and Lope García de Salazar’s *Bienandanzas e fortunas* are indispensable to the broader history of Iberian hero-making. They contain no primary Pelayo cycle and document no encounter between Pelayo and the Cid, Roland, Bernardo, Fernán González, the Lara princes, or Jaun Zuria. Menéndez Pidal’s synthetic history of Spanish epic places these traditions in a common national-epic construction; it does not make their heroes contemporaries. **Absence of an interaction is the evidentiary result.**

## 3. The story

### Before Asturias

**Rotense story.** Pelayo had served Wittiza and Roderic as *spatarius*. Oppressed after the conquest, he entered Asturias with a sister whose name the chronicle does not give. Munuza, Muslim governor at Gijón, sent Pelayo to Córdoba on a pretext and in his absence joined himself to the sister by deception. Pelayo rejected the union and pursued his plan for “the salvation of the Church.” [E2; S6]

The nature of the sister’s experience should not be softened: the text describes an exploitative or coerced marriage arranged while her male protector was absent. Later retellings often romanticize Munuza as an enamored suitor or make the sister “Hormesinda/Ermesinda,” but her name is not supplied by this episode’s early witness. [E2/E4]

**Sebastianense story.** The personal episode disappears. Surviving Goths of royal stock enter Asturias and elect Pelayo, son of Duke Favila and himself of royal lineage. [E2; S2]

These are competing ideological plots: private dishonor and resistance in the Rotense; legitimate dynastic restoration in the Sebastianense.

### Escape and election

In the Rotense, Muslim troops pursue Pelayo after Munuza reports him. He escapes by crossing a river and reaching Mount Auseva. He sends messages through the Asturians; they assemble and elect him *princeps*. [E2; S2]

The conventional date **718** for the election or outbreak and **722** for Covadonga is a scholarly chronology assembled from inconsistent regnal and gubernatorial sequences, not a date supplied by an eyewitness. García de Castro favors a revolt in 718; older national historiography often fixes the climactic battle in 722. [E6; S3]

### The cave and Oppa’s temptation

The Alfonsine narrative places Pelayo in a great cave on Mount Auseva, from which the river Enna flows. Oppa, a bishop and son of Wittiza, calls to him from a mound. Oppa argues that if all the Gothic army failed against the Ishmaelites, Pelayo cannot succeed from one mountain. He offers peace and material advantage under Muslim rule. [E2; S2]

Pelayo answers with an ecclesiological prophecy. The Rotense compares the reduced Church to the mustard seed that will grow again; the Sebastianense version uses the moon’s diminution and renewal. Pelayo declares that through the little mountain will come *salus Spaniae*—“the salvation of Spain”—and restoration of the Gothic army. [E2, variant rhetoric; S2]

The speech is evidence for late ninth-century Asturian political theology. It cannot be treated as a verbatim utterance from the eighth century.

### The battle miracle

The Muslim commander Alqama orders the attack. Slings, swords, spears, and arrows are deployed. Projectiles reaching the “house of holy Mary ever Virgin” in the cave rebound upon the attackers. Pelayo’s people charge; Alqama is killed and Oppa captured. [E2; S2]

The chronicle then claims:

- 124,000 Muslims killed at the cave;
- 63,000 survivors crushed when a mountain collapses into the River Deva;
- bones and arms still exposed by the winter river in the author’s day.

The narrator explicitly compares the disaster to the drowning of Pharaoh’s army in the Red Sea and tells readers not to call it fabulous. The figures and biblical framing expose the account as providential historiography, not plausible military reporting. Scholarship has identified biblical number-patterns behind the totals. [E2 text; E6 analysis; S2, S9]

### Mary: presence, apparition, or later visualization?

The Alfonsine text calls the cave the house of Saint Mary and assigns the projectile miracle to divine power. It does **not** narrate a visible apparition in which Mary personally speaks to Pelayo. Paintings and popular accounts that show “the apparition of the Virgin to Pelayo” are later visual and devotional elaborations. [E2 versus E4–E7; S2, S10]

The modern formula that “the Virgin caused a landslide” compresses two episodes: the missiles rebound at the Marian cave, while the subsequent mountain collapse is attributed to God’s judgment. [E2; S2]

### Munuza’s death and restoration

After hearing of the defeat, Munuza flees Gijón. Asturians catch and kill him and his company at an uncertainly identified place called Olalies/Clacliensem in the recensions. The land is then repopulated, churches restored, and thanks given to God. [E2; S2]

This total expulsion is part of the chronicle’s triumphal ending. Archaeology does not independently document the named pursuit or massacre.

### Dynasty and death

The chronicle gives Pelayo a nineteen-year reign and says that he died naturally at Cangas in Hispanic Era 775, corresponding to 737. His son Favila succeeds him and is killed by a bear two years later. His daughter Ermesinda marries Alfonso, son of Duke Peter of Cantabria; Alfonso fights alongside Pelayo and later becomes Alfonso I. [E2; S2]

A prose translation of the Sebastianense says Pelayo was buried with his wife Gaudiosa at Santa Eulalia de Velanio/Abamia. The Latin Rotense passage merely says he ended his life at Cangas; burial details vary with recension and later transmission. [E2; S2]

### Later body tradition

By the sixteenth century Ambrosio de Morales found or reported a tradition that Pelayo and Gaudiosa had first lain at Santa Eulalia de Abamia and had later been translated to the Santa Cueva. Accounts disagree over whether Alfonso II, Alfonso X, or another royal authority ordered the translation. The supposed medieval documentary basis is lost or unverified. [E4; S11]

The stone monument now presented in the cave as Pelayo’s tomb is an object of memorial belief. No published scientific identification establishes the enclosed remains as those of an eighth-century Pelayo. The empty monuments at Abamia and the late or altered inscriptions are equally incapable of authenticating an individual. [E4–E5; S11]

No divine parentage is claimed. No source makes Pelayo the father of a supernatural lineage.

### Every documented interaction with a named divine being or heroic/historical figure

- **Jesus Christ** — Pelayo invokes Christ as advocate with the Father and source of rescue in his speech to Oppa. *Crónica de Alfonso III*, Rotense, late ninth/early tenth century. **E2.**
- **God/the Lord** — God reverses the missiles, grants victory, collapses the mountain, and restores the Church; Pelayo professes trust in divine mercy. *Crónica de Alfonso III*. **E2.**
- **Virgin Mary** — Pelayo shelters in her cave or “house”; the attacking stones rebound from it. She neither speaks nor visibly appears in the early text. *Crónica de Alfonso III*. **E2.** A personal apparition is **E4–E7**.
- **King David** — Pelayo quotes or paraphrases Psalm 88/89 in the debate with Oppa. This is scriptural invocation, not a physical meeting. *Crónica de Alfonso III*. **E2.**
- **Wittiza** — Rotense makes Pelayo his *spatarius*; Sebastianense instead makes Wittiza the father of the traitorous Oppa. No direct scene between Pelayo and Wittiza survives. **E2.**
- **Roderic/Don Rodrigo** — Rotense makes Pelayo his *spatarius*; another genealogy calls Pelayo his grandson. The claims conflict and no narrated meeting survives. **E2/E3.**
- **Favila/Fáfila the duke** — Sebastianense calls him Pelayo’s father; there is no narrated interaction. **E2.**
- **Vermudo** — an Albeldensian genealogical variant calls him Pelayo’s father; no interaction is narrated. **E2/E3.**
- **Munuza** — Muslim governor and personal antagonist in the Rotense: he sends Pelayo to Córdoba, takes Pelayo’s sister, reports his rebellion, and later flees and is killed by Asturians. **E2.**
- **Pelayo’s unnamed sister** — travels with him to Asturias; her coerced/deceptive union with Munuza becomes Pelayo’s personal grievance. Rotense. **E2.**
- **Bishop Oppa** — attempts to persuade Pelayo to submit; Pelayo refuses; Oppa urges battle and is captured. *Crónica de Alfonso III*. **E2.**
- **Alqama/Alkama** — commands the attacking army and is killed in its rout. *Crónica de Alfonso III*. **E2.**
- **Favila, Pelayo’s son** — succeeds him; no joint exploit is narrated. Asturian chronicles. **E2.**
- **Ermesinda** — Pelayo’s daughter; marries Alfonso. *Crónica de Alfonso III*. **E2.**
- **Alfonso I** — arrives in Asturias, marries Ermesinda, and according to the chronicle wins victories with his father-in-law and afterward. **E2.**
- **Peter of Cantabria** — named as Alfonso’s father; no direct meeting with Pelayo is narrated. **E2.**
- **Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād** — Alqama and/or Munuza are connected to Ṭāriq in different passages; the chronicles narrate no Pelayo–Ṭāriq meeting. **E2.**
- **The Pharaoh and Israelites of Exodus** — the chronicler typologically compares Pelayo’s victory to Israel’s deliverance. Pelayo does not interact with them. **E2 learned biblical systematization.**
- **Judas Maccabaeus** — modern scholarship identifies a broad biblical heroic analogy, but the chronicle does not stage a meeting or explicitly identify Pelayo as Judas. **E6.**
- **El Cid/Rodrigo Díaz** — no documented interaction. Any joint “Spanish heroes” grouping is modern national canon formation. **E7.**
- **Bernardo del Carpio** — no documented interaction. **Absence.**
- **Fernán González** — no documented interaction. **Absence.**
- **Roland and Charlemagne** — no documented interaction. **Absence.**
- **The Infantes de Lara** — no documented interaction. **Absence.**
- **Jaun Zuria** — no medieval interaction or identification with Pelayo. Parallel treatment as a Basque founder-hero is modern comparative framing. **E7 if equated.**
- **Saint James/Santiago Matamoros** — no interaction in Pelayo’s medieval story. Their later juxtaposition belongs to national-Catholic iconography, not the early tradition. **E7.**
- **Hernán Cortés** — a 1967 Mexican comic imagines Cortés carrying the Virgin of Covadonga’s banner and singing of Pelayo. This is a demonstrably modern invention. **E7; S10.**

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Was Pelayo worshipped?

There is no evidence of sacrifices, a pagan-style *heroon*, an oracle, liturgical canonization, or an independent feast of Saint Pelayo the king. The medieval child-martyr Saint Pelagius/San Pelayo is a different person; observance of San Pelayo on 26 June must not be transferred to the Asturian ruler. **The absence of a Pelayo hero cult is a positive finding.**

Pelayo’s memorial presence is instead embedded in:

1. the Marian cult of Covadonga;
2. royal and national commemoration;
3. tomb veneration and tourism;
4. statues, civic festivals, military units, and personal naming.

### The cave and Marian sanctuary

The earliest literary evidence is the Alfonsine description of a *cova dominica* and *domus sanctae semper virginis Mariae*. That proves that the late ninth-century author associated the cave with Mary; it does not establish an eighth-century cult installation or surviving building. [E2; S2]

Claims that Alfonso I immediately erected a chapel or that an organized monastery existed there from the 720s are traditional reconstructions. Evidence for Covadonga’s medieval religious establishment is fragmentary, and the surviving monumental ensemble is much later. [E4–E6; S10, S12]

By the sixteenth century Ambrosio de Morales described a poorly maintained but functioning pilgrimage site, with a Marian image and large silver cross. The medieval wooden church and associated structures were destroyed by fire in 1777. The present monumental landscape, especially the basilica, is substantially a nineteenth- and twentieth-century creation. [E4–E7; S10]

### Tombs and relics

- **Abamia:** a medieval/early-modern burial tradition placed Pelayo and Gaudiosa at Santa Eulalia. Surviving stone monuments are not contemporary grave markers; Gaudiosa’s inscription appears to have been added or altered after Jovellanos observed a plain slab in 1782. [E4; S11]
- **Santa Cueva:** the present tomb claims Pelayo, Gaudiosa, and sometimes Ermesinda. The alleged royal translation is variously assigned to Alfonso II or Alfonso X and lacks secure contemporary documentation. [E4–E5; S11]
- **Cruz de la Victoria:** the surviving jeweled cross was donated by Alfonso III in 908. Its association with a wooden cross carried by Pelayo is a later tradition, prominently developed in thirteenth-century historiography. It is not an eighth-century authenticated weapon or standard. [E3–E5]
- **Bones and weapons in the Deva:** the Alfonsine chronicler says the river still exposed relics of the crushed Muslim army. No controlled archaeological assemblage confirms this claim. [E2 narrative; archaeological absence]

### Ritual and pilgrimage

Devotion is directed principally to the Virgin of Covadonga, “la Santina.” The canonical coronation of the image in 1918 coincided with the officially imagined twelve-hundredth anniversary of Pelayo’s proclamation and the Covadonga cycle. A special jubilee reinforced the fusion of shrine, monarchy, Asturias, and Spain. [E7 institutional commemoration; S13, S14]

Popular practices include the pilgrimage to the cave, ascent of the “Stairway of Promises,” Marian hymns, drinking at the fountain below the cave, and the marriage divination associated with its seven spouts. These belong to the shrine’s modern devotional folklore, not to Pelayo’s early dossier. [E5; S10]

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### c. 711–754: unattested historical horizon

The Islamic conquest and resistance in northern mountains are historical. Pelayo is absent from the only substantial near-contemporary Christian chronicle and from contemporary material evidence. [E1; S1]

### c. 881–920: Asturian dynastic foundation

The *Albeldense* supplies the short founder: Pelayo was first to rebel in Asturias. The Alfonsine recensions create two richer identities:

- a court officer driven by Munuza’s violation of his household;
- a royal-blooded Goth elected to restore the lost kingdom.

Both culminate in providential victory and legitimate succession. The narrative serves the late ninth-century Oviedan court’s claims to be heir and restorer of Christian Gothic Hispania. [E2; S1, S2]

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: canonization of the restoration story

Leonese and Castilian universal chronicles absorb Pelayo into a continuous national and sacred history. Lucas of Tuy, Jiménez de Rada, and the Alfonsine historical workshop strengthen the teleology: 711 is loss; Pelayo is restoration; later conquests recover an inherited realm. [E3; S7]

This is where the legend becomes much larger than the uncertain scale of any eighth-century encounter.

### Fourteenth–seventeenth centuries: antiquarian and monarchic consolidation

Late chronicles and antiquaries identify tombs, perfect genealogies, locate relics, and increasingly call Pelayo the first king of Spain. From the seventeenth century royal commissions combine four motifs in art: cave, Pelayo, battle, and Marian apparition/intercession. The Habsburg monarchy uses Covadonga to express providential Catholic kingship. [E4; S10]

### Eighteenth century: sacred monarchy and Enlightenment tragedy

The Royal Academy’s 1753 competition subject, “The election of Don Pelayo as King of Spain,” already treats the national title as self-evident. Martín Rufo and other artists visualize the election in a landscape resembling the later sanctuary, not eighth-century Asturias. [E7; S15]

Gaspar Melchor de Jovellanos wrote *El Pelayo* around 1769–1773; an altered version appeared as *Munuza* in 1792 and a fuller *Pelayo* was published posthumously in 1832. The drama turns the chronicle into a neoclassical conflict of tyranny, honor, sexual coercion, and patriotic liberty. [E7; S16]

### Nineteenth century: national-liberal and Romantic hero

Manuel José Quintana’s *Pelayo*, staged in 1805, became a liberal-patriotic drama: Pelayo’s martial masculinity and appeal to descendants made resistance to foreign rule usable during the Napoleonic crisis. [E7; S17]

The Romantic state gave the story monumental form. Luis de Madrazo’s *Don Pelayo en Covadonga*, rewarded and purchased by royal order in 1856, presents the event as the beginning of Spain’s recovered Christian and political unity. [E7; S18]

The vocabulary of an eight-century “Reconquista” reached full national-historical force in this period. Conservative Catholic and liberal traditions could both claim Pelayo: one as defender of faith and monarchy, the other as a free leader elected against despotism. [E7; S7]

### 1918: centenary, regionalism, and national Catholicism

The 1,200th-anniversary celebrations, canonical coronation of the Virgin, and establishment of the Montaña de Covadonga national park transformed the landscape into a modern national lieu de mémoire. Carolyn Boyd shows that the celebrations marked the victory of a national-Catholic interpretation over attempts to make Covadonga a broader regional symbol. [E7; S13, S14]

### Carlism and the Civil War

Carlist political culture could read Covadonga through the triad religion–fatherland–king, close to “Dios, Patria, Rey.” During the Civil War an Asturian Requeté unit bore the name **Tercio de Nuestra Señora de Covadonga**. Carlist usage centered more directly on the Marian shrine and crusading restoration than on a distinctive alternative biography of Pelayo. [E7; S19]

Claims that “the Carlists invented Pelayo” are therefore false. They appropriated a medieval and early-modern symbol already deeply nationalized.

### Francoism

Francoist discourse styled the Civil War as a new Reconquista against the “red” anti-Spain and made Franco a new Pelayo or successor to Pelayo’s work. Schoolbooks transmitted Pelayo as an uncomplicated national-Catholic hero. The regime embraced Covadonga as the origin of Spain and Christianity’s restoration but handled its monarchical implications selectively, since Franco—not the Bourbon claimant—was to occupy the providential role. [E7; S10]

The dictatorship did not invent the national-Catholic reading; it enlarged, simplified, and institutionalized motifs developed under earlier monarchies and during the nineteenth century.

### Democracy to the present

Covadonga remains simultaneously a Marian sanctuary, Asturian regional symbol, dynastic site, tourist destination, cycling landmark, and political stage. Since 1983 the Vuelta a España’s finishes at the Lagos de Covadonga have secularized and commercialized the sacred-national landscape as a “temple of cycling.” [E7; S10]

Conservative and far-right politicians continue to invoke Pelayo and launch campaigns at Covadonga. The slogan “Asturias is Spain and the rest is conquered territory” is modern political folklore, not a medieval quotation. Critics in turn use Pelayo to expose the construction of nationalist memory. [E7; S20]

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theater

- **Jovellanos, *El Pelayo*:** written c. 1769–1773; altered *Munuza* printed in 1792; authoritative form published in 1832. Munuza’s desire for Pelayo’s sister, here dramatically individualized, supplies the central tyranny-and-honor plot. [E7; S16]
- **Manuel José Quintana, *Pelayo*:** performed in 1805; recasts the founder as a liberal-patriotic hero warning descendants to defend the nascent polity. [E7; S17]
- **Ángel de Saavedra, Duke of Rivas, *El moro expósito* (1834):** invokes and magnifies Covadonga within Romantic national epic.
- **Nineteenth-century historical romances titled *Pelayo en Covadonga*:** popularize the cave, cross, Virgin, and vastly unequal armies. [E7]
- **José Ángel Mañas, *¡Pelayo!* (2021):** openly supplies features missing from the medieval record, including an Arthurian-style mentor and sacred-relic analogue. These are acknowledged modern inventions. [E7; S21]
- **José Soto Chica, *Pelayo* (2025):** a historical novel offering a maximal reconstruction favorable to the substantial historical importance of Pelayo’s revolt. It remains fiction informed by scholarship, not new primary evidence. [E7; S22]

### Opera and music

Saverio Mercadante’s Italian opera *Pelagio*, with libretto by Marco D’Arienzo, premiered in Naples in 1857. It belongs to the Risorgimento-era language of national liberation and heroic resistance. Its plot and characters cannot be used as evidence for the medieval legend. [E7; S23]

The hymn *Reina de nuestra montaña*, associated with the Virgin’s 1918 coronation, explicitly fuses Marian devotion and patriotism. [E7; S10]

### Painting and sculpture

- Royal Academy competition paintings of 1753 depict “Pelayo elected King of Spain,” an anachronistic national title. [E7; S15]
- Luis de Madrazo’s *Don Pelayo en Covadonga* (1855/1856) gives the hero monumental Romantic-Christian form and was understood by the Prado as symbolizing the beginning of recovered Christian and political unity. [E7; S18]
- Gijón’s monument to Pelayo was inaugurated in 1891. [E7]
- Gerardo Zaragoza’s monumental bronze at Covadonga was inaugurated in 1964; its heroic body, sword, and cross belong to Franco-era public iconography rather than early evidence. [E7]
- Modern artists alternately dress him as a Visigothic “barbarian,” an Astur mountain warrior, or an anachronistic high-medieval knight. None is based on a contemporary portrait. [E7]

There is no known authentic likeness of Pelayo.

### Film, comics, and popular media

- *Altar Mayor* (1944), set at Covadonga, uses the shrine’s landscape and sacred associations in a melodramatic love story rather than presenting a documentary life of Pelayo. [E7; S10]
- Mexican comics *Epopeya* no. 105, “La gesta de Don Pelayo,” and *Patronos y Santuarios* no. 10, “La Virgen de Covadonga” (1967), present a blond, blue-eyed Pelayo and even connect Covadonga to Hernán Cortés. This racialized transatlantic connection is modern invention. [E7; S10]
- Raúl Balén and Pedro Segade, *Covadonga: Historia de España en viñetas* (2020), gives a modern graphic reconstruction. [E7]
- Online memes, games, and political social media commonly turn Pelayo into a generic “first reconqueror,” collapse eight centuries into one campaign, and picture him with high-medieval or fantasy equipment. [E7]

### Commercial and commemorative use

Pelayo names hotels, streets, schools, civic organizations, military formations, historical novels, tourism products, and an insurance company. The personal names Pelayo and Covadonga retain particularly strong Asturian and sometimes conservative social associations. These uses show the endurance of the founder-symbol; they do not preserve independent oral evidence from the early Middle Ages. [E7]

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

Spanish nationalism has repeatedly used Pelayo to assert:

- uninterrupted national existence from Visigothic Hispania;
- an essentially Catholic Spanish identity;
- an eight-century war between fixed national or civilizational blocs;
- a providential mandate for political unity.

Each proposition goes beyond the early evidence. Modern far-right uses often reproduce the Franco-era simplification of Pelayo as the first warrior in an unbroken ethnic-religious reconquest. Conversely, declaring Pelayo “pure invention” as a political retort can exceed what the evidence establishes: the responsible conclusion is that his existence is plausible, while nearly every detailed episode is late and ideologically shaped. [E6–E7; S3, S20]

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Did Pelayo exist?

**Minimal-historicity position:** the recurrence of Pelayo at the head of the Asturian genealogy, the dynasty’s interest in remembering its founder, later property traditions, and the political existence of an early Asturian power make a historical leader probable. García de Castro reconstructs an uprising around 718 and treats Covadonga as a small but consequential encounter. [E6; S3]

**Strong-skeptical position:** Chalmeta emphasizes the complete contemporary silence, late manuscripts, biblical architecture, invented numbers, uncertain place-names, and possible displacement of the *Chronicle of 754*’s Pyrenean episode to Asturias. On this reading the “Pelayo–Covadonga construct” cannot safely be treated as an eighth-century event at that location. [E6; S1]

**Balanced finding:** Pelayo’s probability as an ancestral ruler is greater than the probability that the Alfonsine battle narrative is literal. “Pelayo probably existed” and “the received Covadonga story is a late construction” are compatible conclusions.

### Goth or Asturian?

Sánchez-Albornoz and traditional neo-Gothic historiography accepted, in varying degrees, a Visigothic noble refugee who established legitimate resistance. Other historians emphasize the *Albeldense*’s northern setting and the practical necessity of local kinship and aristocratic support. The sources permit several models:

- Gothic court refugee;
- local Asturian magnate;
- mixed refugee–local coalition;
- dynastic ancestor retroactively Gothicized.

No surviving evidence resolves the choice. [E6]

### Battle or skirmish—or no Covadonga battle?

The canonical military figures are impossible. A small clash or failed punitive expedition is historically conceivable and could have become dynastic memory. García de Castro regards a historical confrontation as probable. Corral and Chalmeta represent stronger skeptical positions. Archaeology has not isolated a battle deposit at the cave. [E6; S1, S3]

### Christian and Arabic testimony

Older apologetic histories counted Christian and Arabic accounts as mutually confirming. Textual criticism shows that all extant Arabic Pelayo narratives are late and may depend on the northern Christian complex. “The Arabs admitted the defeat” is therefore too strong. Conversely, “Arabic sources are completely silent” is true only for contemporary Arabic evidence, not for the whole later Arabic corpus. [E3/E6; S4]

### The biblical construction

The Red Sea parallel is explicit in the chronicle. The rebounding missiles, remnant community, sacred mountain, miraculous destruction, and enormous symbolic totals make the story salvation history. This does not prove that no encounter lay behind it; it shows that the surviving form is theological literature. [E2/E6; S2, S9]

### “Reconquista”

Medieval Asturian and Castilian authors certainly articulated restoration of church, Goths, and *Spania*. The exact modern national master-concept “the Reconquista,” however, was systematized much later. Martín Ríos Saloma traces the transition from “restoration” language to the modern historiographical category between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. Pelayo as conscious initiator of an eight-century national program is therefore an anachronism. [E6; S7]

### Menéndez Pidal and epic origins

Ramón Menéndez Pidal treated chronicle episodes—including the Rotense’s Munuza, sister, flight, and dialogue—as possible residue of lost heroic song. This fits his larger reconstruction of a continuous Castilian epic tradition. Later scholarship has been more cautious: narrative vividness can arise from clerical rhetoric, biblical typology, oral report, or literary invention and does not by itself prove a lost *cantar de Pelayo*. [E6]

### Tomb criticism

Antiquarians treated Abamia and Covadonga as mutually explanatory: an original grave and later royal translation. Examination of the contradictory dates, late inscriptions, lost documentary claims, and changed monuments leaves both the translation and identification unverified. “Pelayo’s tomb” is secure as a commemorative designation, not as a scientifically authenticated burial. [E4–E6; S11]

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Pelayo was indisputably a close blood relative of Roderic: **conflicting late genealogy**.
- Pelayo was crowned “king of Spain” in 718: **modernized title and uncertain date**.
- Exactly 187,000 Muslims attacked: **biblical-symbolic chronicle number**.
- Mary visibly appeared and personally redirected every missile: **later visualization of a less specific chronicle miracle**.
- The Cruz de la Victoria is demonstrably the cross Pelayo carried: **later relic legend**.
- Arabic chronicles independently confirm every Christian detail: **false; late and textually related evidence**.
- His authenticated bones lie in the Santa Cueva: **unverified**.
- Pelayo founded a continuous, self-conscious eight-century national “Reconquista”: **retrospective systematization**.
- Pelayo and Jaun Zuria were the same person, allies, or rivals: **no medieval evidence**.
- The child martyr San Pelayo and the Asturian ruler were one person: **false conflation**.

## 10. Sources

### Primary and critical foundations

The foundational critical edition remains Juan Gil Fernández, José L. Moralejo, and Juan Ignacio Ruiz de la Peña, *Crónicas asturianas* (Oviedo, 1985), containing Latin texts of the *Albeldense* and the Alfonsine recensions. The online bilingual compilation used here reproduces Gil’s Latin while warning that its Spanish translation derives from Nicolás Castor de Caunedo’s nineteenth-century rendering. Primary wording has therefore been treated cautiously. [S2]

For the Arabic material, David Arbesú’s textual study is essential because it compares the witnesses rather than treating “the Muslim version” as a single contemporary account. [S4]

For the historical dispute, García de Castro offers a comparatively affirmative reconstruction, while Chalmeta supplies a sustained skeptical challenge based upon the *Chronicle of 754*, the late source sequence, and geographical problems. [S1, S3]

For modern political and artistic reception, the official Covadonga memory study, Carolyn Boyd, the Prado catalogue, and Ríos Saloma provide the principal documentation used above. [S7, S10, S14, S18]
