# Bernardo del Carpio: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** mortal epic hero; royal bastard or secretly legitimate royal nephew; champion of León/Castile and, in the dominant Spanish tradition, defender of Spain against Frankish domination. He was never securely worshipped as a divine or heroized being.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Lucas of Tuy’s Latin *Chronicon mundi*, completed about 1236. Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada’s *De rebus Hispaniae* followed in 1243. Earlier Asturian, Frankish, and Roland sources describe Alfonso II’s reign or the defeat of 778 without naming Bernardo.
>
> **Historical verdict:** no contemporary or near-contemporary evidence establishes Bernardo’s existence. His documentary existence begins more than four centuries after the historical battle of Roncevaux. The most economical explanation remains that he is an epic construction assembled from Leonese dynastic traditions, aristocratic rebellion stories, Carolingian narrative, and an Iberian counter-memory of Roncevaux.
>
> **Evidence labels used below:** **[EARLY]** securely attested medieval evidence; **[CANONICAL]** later medieval consolidation; **[VARIANT]** regional or competing narrative; **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** learned harmonization; **[ACCRETION]** medieval or early-modern addition; **[FOLKLORE]** orally collected or localized tradition; **[RECONSTRUCTION]** modern scholarly hypothesis; **[INVENTION]** demonstrably modern or anachronistic construction; **[NEGATIVE]** absence from a relevant source.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Bernardo del Carpio—medieval spellings include **Bernaldo**, **Bernardus**, *Bernaldus del Carpio*, and later ballad corruptions such as Arnaldo, Belardo, Abelardo, or Leonardo—is a legendary Iberian warrior retrospectively placed in the reigns of Alfonso II “the Chaste” (791–842) and, in some narratives, Alfonso III (866–910). The dominant consolidated story makes him the son of Jimena, Alfonso II’s sister, and Sancho Díaz, count or lord of Saldaña. Alfonso punishes the parents’ unauthorized union by confining Jimena to a religious house and imprisoning, sometimes blinding, Sancho Díaz. Raised at court, Bernardo repeatedly saves the king but cannot obtain his father’s release. He opposes Alfonso’s alleged plan to make Charlemagne his successor, helps defeat the Franks at Roncevaux, rebels from the fortress of El Carpio, and ultimately receives his father only after the latter has died. **[CANONICAL: Alfonsine *Estoria de España*, c. 1270–84; amplified chronicles and romances]**

This compact “biography” is composite. The thirteenth-century record preserves incompatible genealogies and regnal settings instead of one stable primitive narrative.

### Name

- **Bernardo/Bernaldo** derives from continental Germanic *Bern-hard*, conventionally “bear-strong” or “strong/brave as a bear.” That etymology belongs to the personal name in general; no medieval text explains Bernardo’s character through it. **[RECONSTRUCTION: historical onomastics]**
- Latin chroniclers use forms of **Bernardus**. Old Castilian prose and verse commonly use **Bernaldo**, while **Bernardo** becomes standard in later Spanish. **[EARLY/CANONICAL]**
- Oral transmission produced **Arnaldo, Belardo, Abelardo, Leonardo**, particularly in late and contaminated ballad versions. These are phonetic or mnemonic substitutions, not separately attested ancient heroes. **[FOLKLORE: modern collections summarized in the Pan-Hispanic ballad tradition]**
- **Del Carpio** is a locative epithet. In the Alfonsine account Bernardo builds a fortress on a height near Salamanca/Alba de Tormes and is thereafter called “of the Carpio”: *“pusol nombre Carpio; et dalli adelante llamaron a el Bernaldo del Carpio.”* **[CANONICAL: *Estoria de España*, c. 1270s]**
- Attempts to derive *Carpio* specifically from Latin *carpinus* “hornbeam” or to turn it into a hereditary surname are not explanations supplied by the medieval legend. Several Iberian places bear *Carpio*, and the narrative itself treats the name as toponymic. **[RECONSTRUCTION; disputed etymology]**

### Titles and descriptors

- **“Sobrino” of Alfonso the Chaste:** dominant Spanish genealogy. **[EARLY: Lucas of Tuy, c. 1236; Jiménez de Rada, 1243]**
- **Son of the count of Saldaña:** dominant Leonese genealogy. **[EARLY/CANONICAL]**
- **Royal bastard:** common later characterization, although some texts make the parents secretly married and thus transform the issue from illegitimacy into marriage without royal consent. **[VARIANT]**
- **Defender/liberator of Spain:** implicit in medieval resistance to Frankish succession, explicit in Juan de la Cueva’s *La libertad de España por Bernardo del Carpio* and subsequent patriotic reception. **[ACCRETION: late sixteenth century onward]**
- **Victor of Roncevaux; slayer of Roland:** Bernardo is associated with the Frankish defeat in the thirteenth-century chronicles, but the famous killing of invulnerable Roland by crushing him in Bernardo’s arms is securely visible only in the sixteenth-century elaboration later cited by Cervantes. **[ACCRETION]**
- **“Archetype of the Hispanic hero”:** a modern critical description of his Golden Age reception, not a medieval epithet. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Father | Mother | Royal relationship | Principal evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leonese family tragedy | Sancho Díaz, count/lord of Saldaña | Jimena/Ximena, sister of Alfonso II | Nephew of Alfonso II | Lucas of Tuy c. 1236; Jiménez de Rada 1243; Alfonsine chronicle |
| Carolingian genealogy | Sancho Díaz | Timbor, sister of Charlemagne | Nephew of Charlemagne | Reported as what “some say in their songs and tales” in the *Estoria de España*, c. 1270s |
| Later harmonized cycle | Usually Sancho Díaz and Jimena, but with adventures borrowed from the Timbor/Carolingian cycle | Jimena | Nephew of Alfonso, later ally or opponent of Charlemagne | Later chronicles, romances, learned epic and drama |
| Folk variants | A count of “Zardaña,” Saldaña, or another corrupted name | Jimena or a French princess | Variable | Modern oral collections |

The Alfonsine compiler does not conceal the Timbor account but marks it as a report from *cantares* and *fablas*. That is invaluable evidence that more than one genealogy circulated by the later thirteenth century. **[CANONICAL evidence for an earlier VARIANT]**

There is no evidence that Bernardo was identified with a pagan god, Christian saint, the Cid, Fernán González, Jaun Zuria, or another Iberian hero. Literary comparison and national-pantheon grouping are not syncretistic identity. **[NEGATIVE]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The decisive negative evidence

The following sources are indispensable because they show what is known before Bernardo enters the record:

- **Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, c. 817–833:** reports that the Basques/Gascons ambushed Charlemagne’s rear guard in 778 and names Roland among the Frankish dead in the fuller textual tradition. It knows no Bernardo, Alfonso II, Spanish Christian national army, or duel between champions. **[EARLY historical context; NEGATIVE for Bernardo]**
- **Royal Frankish Annals, late eighth–early ninth century:** record Charlemagne’s 778 Spanish expedition and the Pyrenean disaster; no Bernardo. **[EARLY; NEGATIVE]**
- **Chronicle of Alfonso III**, Rotense and *ad Sebastianum* versions, late ninth century: major Asturian dynastic source. It contains no Bernardo and supplies no story of Alfonso II offering his kingdom to Charlemagne. **[EARLY; NEGATIVE]**
- **Chronica Albeldensis**, completed 881 with continuations: no Bernardo. **[EARLY; NEGATIVE]**
- **Nota Emilianense**, c. 1065–70: a Latin marginal summary from San Millán that attests an Iberian circulation of Roland material, including Charlemagne, Roland, Oliver, and the Roncevaux catastrophe, but no Bernardo. It demonstrates that Carolingian narrative was available in Iberia before Bernardo’s first appearance. **[EARLY context; NEGATIVE]**
- **Old French *Chanson de Roland***, conventionally composed around 1100, earliest major witness Oxford, Bodleian Library MS Digby 23, probably second quarter of the twelfth century: Roland dies fighting Saracens after Ganelon’s betrayal; Bernardo does not appear. **[EARLY comparative corpus; NEGATIVE]**
- **Historia Silense/Legionense**, early twelfth century: invokes Einhard against expansive French claims but does not know Bernardo. **[EARLY context; NEGATIVE]**

The silence is consequential. It does not logically prove that no person named Bernardo lived, but it excludes the claim that the surviving ninth- to early-twelfth-century records already document the hero or his deeds.

### Direct medieval corpus

1. **Lucas of Tuy, *Chronicon mundi*, Latin, c. 1236.**  
   The oldest secure textual witness. Lucas places Bernardo in Asturian-Leonese dynastic history and associates him with resistance to Charlemagne and the Roncevaux victory. Its appearance inside a universal chronicle gives legend historiographical authority. **[EARLY]**

2. **Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada, *De rebus Hispaniae*/*Historia Gothica*, Latin, 1243.**  
   Alfonso II, lacking an heir, secretly approaches Charlemagne; the Asturian magnates threaten to depose him rather than accept Frankish domination. Bernardo is the foremost opponent and participates in defeating the Franks at Roncevaux. Jiménez de Rada’s formula that the nobles prefer to “die free rather than live under the rule of the Franks” makes the episode an explicit story of political liberty. **[EARLY]**

3. **Anonymous *Poema de Fernán González*, Castilian cuaderna vía, c. 1250, surviving in a fifteenth-century manuscript.**  
   Mentions Bernardo retrospectively among defenders of Spain and predecessors of Fernán González. It treats him as an exemplary national hero rather than narrating the full family tragedy. Its survival gap matters: the poem is thirteenth-century, but the principal manuscript is much later. **[EARLY]**

4. **Alfonsine *Estoria de España*, Castilian, first redaction c. 1270–74; critical redaction c. 1282–84; transmitted in a complicated manuscript tradition.**  
   The principal medieval repository, especially chapters conventionally numbered 617, 619, 621, 623, 648–52, and 654–56 in Menéndez Pidal’s mixed *Primera Crónica General*. It narrates Jimena and Sancho Díaz’s union; Bernardo’s upbringing; the French succession crisis and Roncevaux; battles against Muslim rulers; demands for his father; rebellion; construction of El Carpio; and the dead father’s delivery. It also records the competing Timbor genealogy. Modern textual scholarship warns that Menéndez Pidal’s printed *Primera Crónica General* combines Alfonsine and post-Alfonsine strata. **[CANONICAL]**

5. **Lost *Cantar de Bernardo del Carpio*, reconstructed from chronicle prosification.**  
   No verse manuscript survives. Its existence is inferred from formulaic and narrative material embedded in chronicles and from their explicit references to songs and tales. Dating ranges from the later twelfth to early thirteenth century. Whether there were two independent songs—a Leonese family tragedy and a Carolingian adventure—or one evolving cycle is disputed. **[RECONSTRUCTION based on CANONICAL evidence]**

6. **Pero López de Ayala, *Historia de la casa de Ayala*, c. 1398.**  
   Testifies that a song concerning Bernardo was still heard, useful evidence for continued performance but not a surviving copy of the supposed primitive poem. **[LATE MEDIEVAL attestation]**

7. **Pero Ferrús, verse, later fourteenth century.**  
   Includes Bernardo among celebrated heroic names. **[LATE MEDIEVAL reception]**

8. **Lope García de Salazar, *Bienandanzas e fortunas*, Castilian/Biscayan prose, c. 1471–76.**  
   This enormous Basque-authored universal and Iberian chronicle includes legendary history and demonstrates the circulation of common peninsular epic-historical matter in late-medieval Biscay. It is not evidence that Bernardo and the separately narrated Biscayan Jaun Zuria were the same figure or interacted. **[LATE MEDIEVAL reception; NEGATIVE for identification]**

### The ballad corpus

The **Romancero viejo** preserves discrete episodes rather than a continuous epic. Written witnesses are principally fifteenth- and sixteenth-century, although individual narratives may derive from older materials.

Important types include:

- **“En los reinos de León el casto Alfonso reinaba”**: Jimena and the count of Saldaña love one another; she becomes pregnant; Bernardo is born; Jimena enters a convent; the count is imprisoned. Printed in the *Cancionero de romances* of 1550 and later collections. **[ACCRETION/CANONICAL BALLAD]**
- **“En corte del casto Alfonso”**: Bernardo learns his father is imprisoned, dresses in mourning, and petitions the king. Its close verbal agreement with the chronicle may indicate derivation from chronicle prose rather than preservation of an untouched primitive song. **[ACCRETION; textual dependence disputed]**
- **“Por las riberas de Arlanza”**: an armed Bernardo confronts the king. Survives in several sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Iberian manuscripts and print witnesses. **[BALLAD VARIANT]**
- **The imprisoned count’s lament:** Sancho Díaz reproaches his absent son and counts the years by his gray hair. **[BALLAD ACCRETION]**
- **The dead father/casamiento en la muerte episode:** Bernardo is deceived into receiving or ceremonially greeting his father’s corpse; some versions stage a grotesque posthumous reunion or “marriage in death.” **[CANONICAL BALLAD/DRAMATIC DEVELOPMENT]**
- **Departure for France:** after losing his father and surrendering El Carpio, Bernardo goes to Charlemagne’s court, where a maternal half-brother denies him recognition. This belongs to the Carolingian contamination or second cycle. **[VARIANT]**
- **Modern oral versions:** collected among Andalusian and northern Iberian Romani communities, Sephardic singers, and regional Spanish traditions; episodes, names, and sequence vary sharply. **[FOLKLORE]**

The RAE’s modern scholarly selection properly describes Bernardo as a mythical hero with no demonstrable historical basis and identifies the likely twelfth-century anti-French impetus proposed by Menéndez Pidal. Its presentation is editorial scholarship, not new primary evidence.

## 3. The story: cycles, episodes, and development

### A. Birth and punishment

**Dominant Leonese cycle.** Jimena/Ximena, sister of Alfonso II, and Sancho Díaz of Saldaña form a secret sexual union. Some witnesses make it a secret marriage; others emphasize pregnancy outside a publicly sanctioned marriage. Bernardo is born. Alfonso places Jimena in religious confinement and imprisons Sancho, later localized at the castle of Luna. Some later versions add blinding. Bernardo is raised honorably at the king’s court without initially knowing the truth. **[EARLY outline: thirteenth-century chronicles; CANONICAL detail: Alfonsine prose; BALLAD elaboration: sixteenth century]**

The story’s violence is dynastic and sexual-political: Alfonso controls his sister’s marriage and succession, confines her, and makes the father’s imprisonment effectively lifelong. The sources do not describe rape; the best-known ballad explicitly says Jimena loved the count. Claims that Bernardo is simply the product of rape contradict that branch of the corpus. **[NEGATIVE/VARIANT distinction]**

**Carolingian genealogy.** The Alfonsine text reports that “some say in their songs and tales” that Bernardo was born to Timbor, Charlemagne’s sister, while she was traveling on pilgrimage to Santiago, after Sancho Díaz took her to Saldaña. Bernardo can therefore become Charlemagne’s nephew. **[CANONICAL report of VARIANT]**

### B. Formation at court

Bernardo grows into a beautiful, intelligent, eloquent, and exceptionally capable knight, excelling at arms and courtly counsel. Alfonso loves and raises him because he has no son. This creates the central contradiction: the king cherishes the child while destroying his parents’ household. **[CANONICAL: *Estoria de España*]**

No independent childhood narrative predating the thirteenth-century chronicles survives. “Mocedades” episodes in Lope and later drama are literary expansions. **[NEGATIVE/ACCRETION]**

### C. Alfonso’s supposed agreement with Charlemagne

In Jiménez de Rada and the chronicle tradition, the childless Alfonso proposes that Charlemagne or his house inherit Spain. The magnates threaten to depose Alfonso, declaring that they prefer death in freedom to subjection to the Franks; Bernardo is their principal spokesman or champion. Alfonso retracts or cannot fulfill the agreement, and the Franks invade or march into Spain. **[EARLY/CANONICAL]**

No ninth-century source records this succession offer. It is best treated as a thirteenth-century constitutional-national legend about the limits of royal power, not as Carolingian diplomacy. **[NEGATIVE; RECONSTRUCTION]**

### D. Roncevaux

The Spanish cycle relocates or repeats the Roncevaux catastrophe in Alfonso II’s time—often effectively around 808 rather than the historically attested 778 expedition—and gives Bernardo a leading role. Depending on the witness:

- Bernardo and Alfonso’s forces defeat Charlemagne. **[EARLY/CANONICAL]**
- Bernardo cooperates with the Muslim king Marsilio against the Franks. **[VARIANT]**
- Bernardo kills Roland/Roldán personally. **[LATER ELABORATION]**
- Because Roland is invulnerable to weapons, Bernardo seizes and crushes or suffocates him in his arms, an episode likened by Cervantes to Hercules killing Antaeus. **[SIXTEENTH-CENTURY ACCRETION, not the earliest Bernardo record]**
- Bernardo takes Roland’s sword Durandarte. Later tomb legends place it beside Bernardo’s body. **[ACCRETION]**
- In a contrary Carolingian branch, Bernardo subsequently reconciles with Charlemagne and assists him against Marsilio or other enemies. **[VARIANT]**

Historically, Einhard attributes the 778 ambush to Basques/Gascons exploiting wooded mountain terrain. Neither Einhard nor the *Chanson de Roland* knows Bernardo; the *Chanson* turns the attackers into Muslims and makes Ganelon the traitor. The Spanish Bernardo cycle is therefore not independent contemporary testimony about the battle. **[EARLY comparative evidence; NEGATIVE]**

### E. Wars against Muslim rulers

The Alfonsine cycle credits Bernardo with victories and rescues in campaigns variously involving named Muslim rulers:

- **Ores of Mérida:** Bernardo saves Alfonso when the king is unhorsed or surrounded at Benavente. **[CANONICAL]**
- **Alzaman/Almanzor-like figures:** Bernardo reminds Alfonso of prior service in battle. The names and chronology vary and should not be converted into verified ninth-century campaigns. **[VARIANT]**
- **Marsilio of Zaragoza:** enemy, ally, or enemy of Charlemagne depending on whether the narrative follows Spanish, French, or reconciled Carolingian logic. **[VARIANT]**
- Later learned epic greatly multiplies Muslim kings, giants, magicians, and campaigns. **[ACCRETION]**

The name “Almanzor” in popular retellings is chronologically dangerous: the historical al-Mansur Ibn Abi Amir lived in the late tenth century. Its use in a Bernardo narrative indicates epic anachronism or genericization, not contemporary record. **[NEGATIVE/ACCRETION]**

### F. Discovery and petitions for Sancho Díaz

Courtiers or women at court disclose Bernardo’s parentage. He puts on mourning clothes and asks Alfonso to release his father. Alfonso angrily refuses and sometimes swears that Sancho will remain imprisoned as long as the king lives. Bernardo protests that such treatment is poor recompense for loyal military service but initially continues serving. **[CANONICAL; BALLAD elaboration]**

Repeated petitions structure the family cycle. Each service raises an expectation of royal reciprocity; each refusal converts exemplary vassalage into justified rebellion. Alfonsine redactions differ in how sympathetically they present Bernardo’s private warfare against his lord. Inés Fernández-Ordóñez has shown that the critical redaction suppresses passages emphasizing his profitable raiding and thereby remodels the politically awkward rebel. **[RECONSTRUCTION based on textual comparison]**

### G. Rebellion and El Carpio

Bernardo “denaturalizes” himself from the king, attacks royal lands around Salamanca and Alba de Tormes, and establishes a stronghold on a height called El Carpio. In one chronicle formulation, founding the castle supplies his epithet. From it he wages war until Alfonso negotiates. **[CANONICAL]**

This is not a simple story of national obedience. The same defender of Spain becomes a rebel who raids his natural lord’s territory over a family grievance. Some Alfonsine material frankly depicts booty-taking; later redaction softens it. **[CANONICAL; editorial reshaping]**

The ruined site called Carpio Bernardo near Alba de Tormes is a material locus of remembrance, but its association with the hero does not archaeologically prove that he built or occupied it. **[LOCAL TRADITION; NEGATIVE archaeological finding]**

### H. The father returned dead

Alfonso eventually agrees to exchange Sancho Díaz for El Carpio or promises release. Sancho, however, has died in prison. In the most memorable version, the corpse is prepared and presented to Bernardo as if alive—mounted on a horse or seated—and the son kisses or takes the dead father’s hand before discovering the deception. Bernardo has surrendered his fortress and received only a corpse. **[CANONICAL/LATE MEDIEVAL-BALLAD DEVELOPMENT]**

The “casamiento en la muerte” theme becomes especially elaborate in drama, where dynastic honor, legitimacy, and the parents’ union may be ceremonially repaired only at death. **[EARLY-MODERN ACCRETION]**

### I. Exile, France, marriage, descendants, and death

No single ending governs the cycle:

- Bernardo leaves Alfonso’s realm after burying his father. **[CANONICAL]**
- He travels to France and seeks recognition from Charlemagne or a maternal half-brother, who refuses him. **[CAROLINGIAN VARIANT]**
- He reconciles with Charlemagne and campaigns for him. **[VARIANT]**
- He marries **Galinda** and fathers **Galín Galíndez**, connecting the hero with Pyrenean/Ribagorzan genealogical legend. **[LEARNED/REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- Portuguese prose makes him renounce the world and become a monk at Aguilar de Campoo. **[EIGHTEENTH-CENTURY ACCRETION]**
- Aguilar tradition says he was buried with his sword beneath or near Santa María la Real/Peña Longa. **[LOCAL TOMBSTONE TRADITION]**
- Northern Romani tradition says he is not dead but enchanted in a cave on the plains of Calahorra, awaiting Spain’s final peril. **[FOLKLORE, collected in the modern period]**

These endings cannot be harmonized into a historical death account.

## 4. Relations with gods and other heroes

### Genealogical map

```text
Leonese version:
unnamed Asturian royal parents
├── Alfonso II "the Chaste" — childless king
└── Jimena/Ximena ── secret union/marriage ── Sancho Díaz of Saldaña
                                            │
                                      Bernardo del Carpio
                                            │
                                  [later: Galinda]
                                            │
                                      Galín Galíndez

Carolingian version:
Carolingian family
├── Charlemagne
└── Timbor ── Sancho Díaz
              │
        Bernardo del Carpio
```

The Alfonsine corpus preserves rather than resolves the incompatibility. **[CANONICAL]**

## 5. Cult, tomb, relics, and ritual

### Cult

No securely documented medieval cult of Bernardo is known:

- no altar dedication;
- no liturgical office or feast;
- no canonization tradition;
- no archaeologically identified *heroon*;
- no securely medieval votive inscriptions;
- no documented sacrifice, pilgrimage obligation, oracle, or games;
- no reliable theophoric naming practice based on him.

**[NEGATIVE]**

Bernardo is therefore not a “heroized dead with cult” in the technical sense. Civic commemoration, literary veneration, reenactment, and nationalist praise must not be redescribed as cult without evidence.

### Claimed tomb at Aguilar de Campoo

A long-standing local tradition locates Bernardo’s grave in a cave or rocky recess at Peña Longa, near Santa María la Real de Aguilar de Campoo. A reported inscription identifies him as “the noble and valiant knight Bernardo del Carpio, defender of Spain,” son of Sancho Díaz and Jimena. Later accounts say that his sword lay with him. **[LOCAL LATE TRADITION]**

Lope García de Salazar’s late-medieval tradition says Bernardo lay at San Francisco of Aguilar de Campoo. Later accounts shift or conflate institutions and cave locations. The alleged grave has not produced securely dated human remains, weapon, inscription, or stratigraphic context capable of identifying a ninth-century Bernardo. The inscription’s developed genealogy and “defender of Spain” rhetoric reflect the received legend rather than independent proof of its subject. **[NEGATIVE archaeological finding]**

Claims that the buried sword was Roland’s Durandarte are still later accretions. In Carolingian literature the weapon belongs to Roland; assigning it to Bernardo materializes Spain’s literary victory over the French champion. **[ACCRETION]**

### Castle of Luna

The castle of Luna is traditionally identified as Sancho Díaz’s prison. The site is historically real, but the attribution of a specific confinement there comes from the legend. No prison record documents Sancho Díaz. **[LOCALIZATION; NEGATIVE]**

### El Carpio/Carpio Bernardo

The landscape near Alba de Tormes contains ruins and a place called Carpio Bernardo. The name and visible fortification supported localization of Bernardo’s rebellious stronghold. A modern iron sculpture by Víctor Pozo was installed in the early twenty-first century with the hero facing the ruins. Archaeological presence of a fortress does not by itself establish its legendary founder. **[MATERIAL RECEPTION; MODERN COMMEMORATION]**

### Salamanca medallion and heraldic memorialization

Bernardo appears among heroes and conquerors in a medallion program in Salamanca’s Plaza Mayor. A purported Bernardo tomb or heraldic monument elsewhere in the Salamanca tradition has incorporated a recumbent knight transferred from another context and late-medieval heraldry. Lope de Vega adopted an attributed “Bernardo” coat of arms with the motto *“De Bernardo es el blasón, las desdichas mías son”* (“Bernardo’s is the blazon; mine are the misfortunes”). Such retrospective heraldry is not contemporary evidence for the hero. **[EARLY-MODERN/MODERN COMMEMORATION]**

## 6. Historical evolution of the figure

### I. 778–c. 1100: the battle and Carolingian memory without Bernardo

A real Frankish defeat occurred in 778. Einhard attributes the attack to Basques/Gascons; the developing Roland legend transformed the encounter into Christian warfare against Muslims. The *Nota Emilianense* proves Roland material circulated in Iberia by c. 1065–70. Bernardo is absent. **[EARLY/NEGATIVE]**

### II. Twelfth century: probable gestation

The *Historia Silense* resists French epic claims, and Carolingian stories travel along Iberian routes, including the pilgrimage road to Santiago. Forged or problematic Ribagorzan genealogical materials connect a Bernardo of Ribagorza with Charlemagne’s family. Scholars have proposed that these materials helped generate the Timbor genealogy. No complete Bernardo poem survives from this period. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

Menéndez Pidal and Defourneaux interpreted Bernardo as an “anti-Roland” or “anti-Charlemagne,” created by Spanish resistance to French epic prestige. That remains plausible but is a theory about formation, not a dated authorial declaration. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

### III. 1230s–1280s: historiographical creation and canonization

Lucas of Tuy and Jiménez de Rada insert Bernardo into national history. The *Poema de Fernán González* makes him a predecessor of Castilian independence. The Alfonsine workshop then expands and reconciles disparate material in vernacular prose, preserving both Jimena and Timbor genealogies. This is the decisive canonical phase. **[EARLY/CANONICAL]**

Bernardo’s political meanings now include:

- resistance to foreign succession;
- aristocratic power to constrain a king;
- loyalty owed only reciprocally;
- family honor against royal coercion;
- León and Castile as inheritors of an independent Spain.

### IV. Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries: continued performance and diffusion

Ayala testifies to continued song; Ferrús lists Bernardo among great heroes; chronicles proliferate; ballad episodes become detachable. The late-medieval Biscayan *Bienandanzas e fortunas* belongs to the broader peninsular process of compiling history, epic, genealogy, and local foundation legend. **[LATE MEDIEVAL DEVELOPMENT]**

### V. Sixteenth century: print, theater, and the explicit national champion

Printed romanceros stabilize some ballads while generating new ones. Historians such as Florián de Ocampo, Ambrosio de Morales, Esteban de Garibay, and Juan de Mariana repeat or debate chronicle material, often allowing Bernardo historical standing. Learned epics and Juan de la Cueva turn him explicitly into Spain’s liberator. The personal crushing of Roland becomes famous. **[EARLY-MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION/ACCRETION]**

Humanist source criticism also begins to expose chronological contradictions. Bernardo’s historical existence, previously naturalized by chronicles, becomes contestable. **[EARLY-MODERN SCHOLARLY DEVELOPMENT]**

### VI. Seventeenth century: theatrical and imperial apogee

Lope’s plays focus on youth, honor, filial duty, and the horrifying delivery of the dead father. Cubillo rewrites the cycle. Balbuena expands it into a transatlantic Catholic-imperial epic, mixing Roncevaux with Ariostan fantasy, classical mythology, prophecy, and the conquest of America. Bernardo becomes both medieval hero and vehicle for Habsburg imperial imagination. **[REINVENTION]**

### VII. Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: popular history and Romanticism

Portuguese Charlemagne books assimilate Bernardo into Iberian popular prose. “True history” chapbooks and melodrama continue to present legend as fact. Romantic historical novels favor the persecuted bastard, ruined family, dungeon, and national resistance. Nineteenth-century philology begins systematic collection of the romances and reconstruction of lost epic. **[POPULAR/ROMANTIC RECEPTION; SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**

### VIII. Twentieth century: philological controversy and folklore

Menéndez Pidal’s school reconstructed the lost Spanish epic and emphasized oral continuity and national reaction to French epic. Heinermann argued for two source cycles. Entwistle developed a two-*cantar* model; Cirot and Luis Rubio García objected to aspects of it. Defourneaux restated the anti-Charlemagne interpretation. Later textual critics shifted attention from a hypothetical pristine poem to the differences among chronicles and manuscript redactions. **[MODERN SCHOLARSHIP]**

Oral researchers documented Bernardo among Romani and other regional singers. In one northern Romani conclusion he remains enchanted in a cave and will return when Spain must be saved. **[FOLKLORE]**

### IX. Twenty-first century: local heritage and renewed ideological use

Associations, heritage sites, congresses, sculptures, digital ballad archives, and local historical tourism keep the figure visible. Contemporary Spanish-nationalist organizations have revived him as a symbol of an ancient unified Spain defeating foreign empire. Such usage reproduces the medieval anti-Frankish theme but often presents the battle, Bernardo, and a unified national consciousness as literal ninth-century history. **[MODERN POLITICAL REINVENTION]**

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and scholarship

The strongest modern image remains the tragic son who performs heroic service for a king who refuses justice. This is substantially a product of the chronicle-ballad-drama continuum, not simply the supposed lost *Cantar*.

Cervantes both preserves and destabilizes Bernardo. In *Don Quixote* the hero’s crushing of the invulnerable Roland appears among the extravagant “truths” of chivalric reading. Cervantes thereby documents the episode’s currency while placing it inside a comedy of unreliable books. **[EARLY-MODERN IRONIC RECEPTION]**

Balbuena’s *El Bernardo* has recently attracted renewed attention as a major New Spanish/transatlantic epic rather than merely a derivative monument. Its visions of America, Spanish genealogy, gods, demons, enchanted castles, and Ariostan heroes should never be projected backward into the medieval legend. **[SCHOLARLY REASSESSMENT]**

### Visual culture

Compared with Roland or the Cid, Bernardo has a thin and late iconographic record. Surviving public images are chiefly:

- printed book illustrations and theatrical imagery;
- Salamanca’s heroic medallion;
- retrospective recumbent-knight and heraldic associations;
- local statues and monuments at Carpio Bernardo and proposed commemorative projects;
- heritage representations of the cave, prison, castle, and battlefield.

No securely medieval image can presently be identified as Bernardo. **[NEGATIVE]**

### Music and performance

Romances remained sung or recited in Hispanic, Sephardic, and Romani communities. Flamenco performers retained fragments such as the prisoner/father material into the twentieth century. Modern performances are evidence for continuity and recomposition, not for the exact wording of a medieval song. **[FOLKLORE]**

The eighteenth-century *Bernardo del Carpio en el castillo de Luna* is a melodramatic treatment. There is no comparably canonical international Bernardo opera equivalent to Wagner’s treatments of Germanic legend. **[NEGATIVE/RECEPTION]**

### Film, comics, and games

Bernardo never acquired the sustained international film industry enjoyed by the Cid, Roland, Arthur, or Hercules. Scattered popular illustrations, educational comics, internet character designs, and role-playing adaptations exist, but they have not established a stable modern canon. Online “servant,” fantasy-statistics, or game-character profiles are modern fan inventions unless licensed to a particular franchise. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

### Nationalism

Bernardo’s nationalism has several historically distinct layers:

1. **Thirteenth-century political community:** the magnates’ resistance to Frankish rule in Jiménez de Rada. This is medieval regnal liberty, not automatically modern nation-state nationalism. **[EARLY]**
2. **Golden Age Spain:** Juan de la Cueva, Suárez de Figueroa, Lope, and Balbuena make Bernardo a defender of Spain within imperial-Catholic historical imagination. **[ACCRETION]**
3. **Nineteenth-century nation building:** literary histories canonize him as an indigenous answer to French Roland. **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
4. **Franco-era context:** the dictatorship heavily privileged the Cid, Catholic monarchy, Reconquest, and imperial unity. Bernardo fit the same usable heroic genealogy, but claims that Franco personally made Bernardo an equally central official icon require specific evidence and should not be inferred from the much better documented Francoist Cid. **[CAUTION/NEGATIVE]**
5. **Contemporary nationalist appropriation:** organizations such as DENAES explicitly hail Bernardo as the “glory of Spain” and treat his victory as the expression of a primordial Spanish political unity. This is documented current appropriation, not medieval fact. **[MODERN POLITICAL REINVENTION]**

### Psychological and comparativist readings

Bernardo readily fits later “hero pattern” analysis: hidden or irregular birth, separation from parents, hostile king, extraordinary prowess, quest for paternal recognition, betrayal, exile. Applying Rank, Raglan, Campbell, or structuralist templates can describe similarities but does not establish common origin or historicity. **[MODERN INTERPRETATION]**

Dumézilian or Indo-European trifunctional readings are not central to Bernardo scholarship and lack an early ritual or divine corpus against which to test them. Nagy’s work on hero poetry and performance can illuminate multiformity in general, but Bernardo’s evidence must be handled through Iberian manuscripts, chronicles, and ballad field records rather than imported Greek cult models. **[NEGATIVE/COMPARATIVE CAUTION]**

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Did Bernardo exist?

**Mainstream position:** no documentary basis confirms a historical Bernardo del Carpio. The ninth-century chronicles omit him, the earliest direct witness is c. 1236, and his story contains incompatible reigns, genealogies, and versions of Roncevaux. **[SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS]**

**Historicity defenses:** Vicente José González García and some regional writers have argued that Bernardo participated in a second Roncevaux battle around 808 and that documents associated with Bishop Pelayo of Oviedo preserve genuine traces. Critics answer that the relevant materials are late, interpolated, forged, or circularly interpreted through the developed legend. González García’s vehement defense is a datable modern thesis, not settled history. **[CONTESTED RECONSTRUCTION]**

A more productive historical question is not “Was every deed true?” but “What circumstances made the figure meaningful?” Matthew Bailey has proposed that Bernardo’s uncle-nephew conflict responds to changes from matrilineal toward patrilineal royal succession in Asturias-León and that his rebellion resembles those of the Banu Gómez counts of Saldaña against Vermudo II in the late tenth century. This is a contextual reconstruction, not identification of Bernardo with a particular count. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**

### One poem or two cycles?

- **Two-cycle hypothesis:** Heinermann and Entwistle distinguished a Leonese *Cantar* centered on Jimena, Sancho Díaz, imprisonment, and filial tragedy from a Carolingian *Cantar* centered on Timbor, Charlemagne, France, and reconciliation. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Objections:** Cirot and Rubio García questioned whether the evidence requires two separate poems rather than one changing legend with chronicle contamination. No manuscript of either poem survives. **[CRITIQUE]**
- **Current caution:** “The lost *Cantar de Bernardo*” is convenient shorthand, but it can imply a recoverable unitary original that the evidence does not warrant. The prose witnesses preserve redacted narrative, not a mechanical transcription. **[TEXTUAL-CRITICAL POSITION]**

### Was Bernardo invented “against Roland”?

Gaston Paris, Menéndez Pidal, and Defourneaux variously treated Bernardo as a Spanish nationalist counter-hero to Roland and Charlemagne. Supporting evidence includes:

- early Iberian knowledge of Roland;
- the *Historia Silense*’s resistance to French heroic claims;
- Bernardo’s absence from earlier records;
- his first appearance as opponent of Frankish political control;
- his eventual replacement of anonymous/Basque victors with a Leonese champion.

Against a simplistic formulation:

- the Timbor genealogy makes Bernardo part-Carolingian;
- some branches reconcile him with Charlemagne;
- Iberian Christian and Muslim alliances vary;
- regional Ribagorzan genealogical material may have contributed independently;
- “nationalism” must be defined historically rather than assumed to mean its nineteenth-century form.

Thus “invented against Roland” is a powerful account of function, not proof of a single moment of invention by a known author. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Manuscript and edition problems

Menéndez Pidal’s *Primera Crónica General* remains influential but prints a composite text. Fernández-Ordóñez’s work distinguishes the Alfonsine primitive and critical redactions from later amplified chronicles. Narrative differences—especially the treatment of rebellion and plunder—can reflect editorial politics rather than separate oral originals. **[TEXTUAL CRITICISM]**

The romances pose the reverse difficulty. Some may descend from epic matter; others demonstrably versify or closely imitate chronicle prose; still others are sixteenth-century authored “new romances.” Age of subject is not age of wording. **[TEXTUAL CRITICISM]**

### Tomb and relic claims

No published archaeological demonstration identifies Bernardo’s grave, bones, or sword. Tomb inscriptions and local narratives depend on the mature genealogy and therefore cannot serve as independent ninth-century corroboration. **[NEGATIVE]**

### Relationship to other Iberian corpora

The *Cantar de mio Cid* and *Historia Roderici* are methodological comparanda: unlike Bernardo, Rodrigo Díaz is independently documented, while his literary image still accumulated legend. Bernardo’s dossier therefore cannot be modeled as a poorly documented biography of the same kind. **[COMPARATIVE FINDING]**

The Infantes de Lara, Don Rodrigo, Fernán González, Pelayo/Covadonga, and Jaun Zuria show how chronicles and ballads convert dynastic, regional, and political memory into heroic narrative. They are not missing chapters of Bernardo’s story. **[NEGATIVE]**

Richard Fletcher’s *The Quest for El Cid*, Alan Deyermond’s literary histories, María Eugenia Lacarra’s work on epic and ideology, and Alberto Montaner’s critical Cid scholarship sharpen this distinction between historical documentation and the development of heroic narrative. They should not be cited as direct Bernardo witnesses unless they actually discuss him. **[HISTORIOGRAPHICAL CAUTION]**

- Lope de Vega/Lopean texts in the ARTELOPE and EMOTHE digital editions.

### Principal modern scholarship represented

- Ramón Menéndez Pidal: epic reconstruction, oral continuity, national counter-tradition.
- Theodor Heinermann: two-source/two-cycle analysis.
- W. J. Entwistle: distinct reconstructed *cantares*.
- Georges Cirot and Luis Rubio García: objections to overly rigid two-poem reconstruction.
- Marcelin Defourneaux: French-Spanish epic relations and Bernardo as counter-Charlemagne.
- Philippe Lauer: development of the Bernardo legend and the late arrival of the Roland-crushing episode.
- Inés Fernández-Ordóñez: Alfonsine manuscript transmission and ideological revision.
- Matthew Bailey: uncle-nephew succession structures and possible Banu Gómez historical context.
- Marjorie Ratcliffe: foundational myth and collective memory.
- Gloria Chicote: comparative analysis across Latin historiography, vernacular chronicle, clerical verse, and ballad.
- Paloma Díaz-Mas and associated researchers: survival and transformation in northern Iberian Romani tradition.

### Findings from the contested-material pass

- **Historicity:** defended by a minority of modern regional scholars; unsupported by contemporary evidence.  
- **Tomb:** locally attested tradition at Aguilar de Campoo, but no identifying archaeological proof.  
- **Cult:** none securely documented.  
- **Variants:** two genealogies and several incompatible political alignments are securely documented.  
- **Etymology:** *Bernardo* is Germanic; *del Carpio* is narratively toponymic; more exact derivations remain uncertain.  
- **Nationalism:** present first as medieval resistance to foreign lordship, then repeatedly reconfigured by Golden Age imperial, nineteenth-century national, Franco-era heroic, and present-day Spanish-nationalist discourses.  
- **Modern invention:** claims that the medieval sources already document Bernardo crushing Roland, owning Durandarte, sleeping enchanted until Spain’s final peril, or embodying a ninth-century unified Spanish nation confuse later layers with early evidence.

The resulting figure is not an unsuccessful historical biography. He is a highly successful documentary legend: created late enough for its formation to be visible, authoritative enough to enter national chronicles, flexible enough to be simultaneously Alfonso’s loyal vassal and rebel, Charlemagne’s enemy and nephew, Roland’s Spanish answer and Carolingian relative, and durable enough to survive in print, theater, oral performance, local landscape, and modern politics.
