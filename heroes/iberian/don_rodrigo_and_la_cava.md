# Don Rodrigo and La Cava: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** Don Rodrigo is a mortal, failed epic king and penitential legendary hero—the “last Goth”—not a demigod or cult hero. La Cava is a mortal legendary court woman, alternately represented as rape victim, seduced lover, temptress, traitor, national scapegoat, and modern survivor. Neither received a securely documented religious cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestations:** Rodrigo is historically attested by gold tremisses struck in his name at Toledo and Egitania during his reign, conventionally dated 710–711, and textually by the Latin *Chronicle of 754*. A probable portrait labeled **Rodorikos/Ludhrīq** appears among the “Six Kings” at the Umayyad palace of Quṣayr ʿAmra, painted probably in the 730s or 740s. La Cava is absent from all eighth-century evidence. The causative story about Count Julian’s violated daughter first survives in Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam’s ninth-century Arabic conquest history; she remains unnamed there. Her familiar names—La Cava and Florinda—are fourteenth- and sixteenth-century developments respectively.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Secure or near-contemporary evidence, eighth century.
- **[LATER CHRONICLE]** Medieval historiographical elaboration presented as history.
- **[ARABIC TRADITION]** Arabic conquest narrative compiled substantially after 711.
- **[ROMANCE]** Romancero or related poetic tradition.
- **[EARLY MODERN]** Sixteenth- or seventeenth-century literary accretion.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Local place legend or orally transmitted form.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** A scholarly inference, not something stated by early evidence.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A demonstrably recent creation, misidentification, or artistic restructuring.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The paired figures

**Rodrigo** was a historical Visigothic ruler whose short, disputed kingship ended during the Muslim invasion of Iberia. The eighth-century evidence establishes his kingship, defeat, and the catastrophic collapse of the Gothic army; it does not establish his alleged rape of Julian’s daughter, his opening of a forbidden tower, a battle specifically on the Guadalete, survival as a penitent, or death in a serpent-filled tomb. Those constitute the evolving legendary Rodrigo.

**La Cava** has no independent contemporary attestation. She enters the record as an unnamed daughter—occasionally, in variant transmission, a wife—of the ruler conventionally called Count Julian. Her violation by Rodrigo is made the private cause of Julian’s assistance to the invaders. Medieval and early-modern authors progressively give her a name, speech, letters, moral responsibility, and an afterlife. Modern writers frequently call her “Florinda la Cava,” but no source of 711 knows that composite name.

### Rodrigo’s names

- Latin: **Rudericus, Rodericus, Ruderigus**, with orthographic variation.
- Gothic reconstruction: usually *Hrōþareiks* or a related compound, “fame/glory” + “ruler.” This is a linguistic reconstruction, not an attested Gothic spelling for this king.
- Spanish and Portuguese: **Rodrigo**.
- English: **Roderic, Roderick**.
- French: **Rodrigue**.
- Italian: **Roderico**.
- Arabic: **لذريق**, commonly transliterated **Ludhrīq, Ladhriq, Lūdhriq**. The initial *l-* probably reflects reception of Romance *el Rodrigo* or phonological adaptation; details remain disputed.
- Epithets: **el último godo**, “the last Goth”; **el último rey de los godos**, “the last king of the Goths”; **postrimero rey de los godos**, especially in Pedro de Corral’s title. These are retrospective literary-historiographical designations. They obscure the rival or successor kings Achila II and Ardo, attested in northeastern Visigothic territories.

The personal name *Rodrigo* also belongs to Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar, El Cid. The two must not be conflated. A Cervantine study appropriately calls the Cid and Rodrigo de Narváez part of an “extended family of homonymous heroes,” not versions of the last Gothic king.

### La Cava’s names

1. **Unnamed daughter of Julian** — [ARABIC TRADITION], Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam, ninth century.
2. **Unnamed daughter** — [LATER CHRONICLE], *Historia Silense*, early twelfth century.
3. **Oliba** — [LATER CHRONICLE], *Chronica Gothorum Pseudo-Isidoriana*, twelfth century. This form did not become canonical.
4. **Alataba / Alacaba / La Taba** — [LATER CHRONICLE], Portuguese *Crónica Geral de Espanha de 1344*, surviving through Castilian transmission.
5. **La Caba / La Cava** — [LATER CHRONICLE], especially the fourteenth-century rewriting of the *Crónica de 1344* and Pedro de Corral’s c.1430 *Crónica sarracina*.
6. **Florinda** — [EARLY MODERN], introduced and popularized by Miguel de Luna’s pseudo-translation *Historia verdadera del rey don Rodrigo*, part I, 1592.
7. **Florinda la Cava** — later composite usage.

### Disputed etymologies

- The common claim that *Cava* straightforwardly “means prostitute in Arabic” is too categorical. Scholars connect it with Arabic **qaḥba**, “prostitute,” particularly because the later *Crónica de 1344* both fixes a Caba-like form and increases her culpability. That may be a hostile pun rather than the name’s sole origin.
- Patricia Grieve and others note a possible association with Hebrew **Ḥawwāh** and Arabic **Ḥawwāʾ**, Eve. The equation is explicit only in Gabriel Esteban de Salazar’s 1574 statement that “Chava” was the woman who occasioned Spain’s loss. It is therefore a learned sixteenth-century typology, not an ancient etymology.
- North African *Qabr al-Rūmiyya*, “tomb of the Roman/Christian woman,” and similar place-names were later confused with *qaḥba rūmiyya*, “Christian prostitute,” and attached to La Cava. Luis del Mármol Carvajal already rejected that identification in the sixteenth century. It is a syncretistic place-legend, not evidence for her burial.
- *Florinda* is not recovered from an Arabic original. Miguel de Luna placed it in a work falsely presented as a translation of an ancient Arabic manuscript. Modern scholarship treats the attribution to “Abulcacim Tarif Abentarique” as literary pseudohistory.

### Class and identification

Rodrigo is an **epic king and heroized penitent**, but not a heroized dead person with documented cult. La Cava is a **legendary woman of national catastrophe**, structurally compared with Eve, Helen, and Lucretia by later writers and scholars. Those are typological comparisons, not ancient identifications or cultic syncretisms.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Eighth-century record

#### Coins

**[EARLY]** Gold tremisses naming Rodrigo were struck at Toledo and Egitania, modern Idanha-a-Velha. They prove that a ruler bearing his name exercised royal minting authority in parts of the kingdom. They do not prove the later genealogy, the Julian story, or a realm uniformly governed from Toledo.

Some purported Rodrigo coins are modern or early-modern forgeries. Authentication must rest on die, weight, metal, provenance, and comparison with the Visigothic series—not merely the legend *RVDRICVS*.

#### *Chronicle of 754*

**[EARLY]** The anonymous Latin *Chronicle of 754*, composed in al-Andalus approximately forty years after the conquest, is the closest substantial narrative source. Its surviving textual transmission is later: the earliest manuscript material is ninth century, and the work’s manuscript history is difficult. It says that:

- Rodrigo seized or tumultuously obtained the kingdom.
- Mūsā’s forces were devastating Iberia.
- Rodrigo confronted the invaders at the **Transductine promontories**.
- The Gothic army was ruined amid aristocratic rivalry and ambition.
- Rodrigo lost both kingdom and homeland.

It does **not** name La Cava, Count Julian, a locked tower, Pelayo, Guadalete, Rodrigo’s penitence, or his tomb. Carmen Cardelle de Hartmann calls it an exceptionally valuable near-contemporary witness while emphasizing the problems of its transmission.

#### Quṣayr ʿAmra

**[EARLY IMAGE / contested interpretation]** The Umayyad bath-palace at Quṣayr ʿAmra in Jordan contains the “Six Kings” fresco. Greek and Arabic labels were recorded as identifying the Byzantine emperor, the Sasanian ruler, the Ethiopian negus, and Rodrigo of Hispania. The complex is now generally placed in the second quarter of the eighth century, probably under the future al-Walīd II. The Rodrigo figure is badly damaged, and parts of the inscription were harmed after the site’s modern rediscovery.

This is probably the earliest surviving image connected with Rodrigo. It depicts him as one of several foreign sovereigns in an Umayyad program of universal victory or royal precedence, not as La Cava’s lover or Christian penitent. The precise ideological meaning—defeated kings, peers, supplicants, or exemplars of world sovereignty—remains debated. [UNESCO describes the monument and its early-eighth-century murals](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/327); the specialized debate is summarized in Elizabeth Drayson’s study of the first Islamic and Christian depictions.

### Arabic conquest narratives

#### Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam, *Futūḥ Miṣr wa-akhbāruhā*

**[ARABIC TRADITION]** Composed in ninth-century Egypt, roughly a century and a half after 711. This is the earliest surviving text usually credited with the causal chain:

1. Julian sends his daughter to Rodrigo’s court.
2. Rodrigo violates or impregnates her.
3. Julian seeks revenge.
4. Julian helps the Muslim crossing.

It is vital as the first attested form of the daughter story, but late as evidence for 711. It gives her no canonical personal name.

#### Ibn al-Qūṭiyya, *Taʾrīkh iftitāḥ al-Andalus*

**[ARABIC TRADITION]** Late tenth century, drawing on Andalusi traditions. It transmits the Toledo locked-house story: successive kings add locks to a forbidden chamber; Rodrigo opens it and finds images or a prophecy of the people who will conquer Spain. Versions associate the chamber with Solomon’s table and with riders dressed like the invading Arabs.

This etiological wonder tale is independent in origin from the violated-daughter plot, although later compilations join them.

#### *Akhbār majmūʿa*

**[ARABIC TRADITION]** Anonymous Arabic compilation, probably assembled in the eleventh century from earlier materials. It elaborates the invasion, Julian’s mediation, Gothic factionalism, and Rodrigo’s defeat. Its stories cannot simply be projected back to 711; David James’s modern edition and translation presents its composite character.

#### Al-Maqqarī, *Nafḥ al-ṭīb*

**[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** Seventeenth century, completed before al-Maqqarī’s death in 1632. It is not an early conquest witness. It preserves and organizes quotations from earlier Andalusi writers, including Ibn Ḥayyān and Ibn Khaldūn. Its full version of Julian sending his beautiful daughter to court, Rodrigo forcing and deflowering her, her secret letter, Julian’s oath of revenge, and his aid to Ṭāriq is immensely influential but represents a late compilation.

Al-Maqqarī also reports Rodrigo fighting in the Basque region when the invasion began and gives the large, conventional army figures of later Arabic historiography. Those figures are not securely recoverable statistics.

### Latin and Romance chronicles

- ***Chronicle of Alfonso III***, late ninth century, in Rotense and *ad Sebastianum* recensions — [LATER CHRONICLE]. It attributes treason to the sons of Witiza, says Rodrigo’s exact death was unknown, and reports discovery at Viseu of a monument inscribed “Here rests Rodrigo, king of the Goths.” It still has no La Cava.
- ***Historia Silense***, early twelfth century — [LATER CHRONICLE]. First surviving Christian-Latin adoption of Julian’s violated daughter, still unnamed.
- ***Chronica Gothorum Pseudo-Isidoriana***, twelfth century — [LATER CHRONICLE]. Names the daughter **Oliba**. Its single manuscript is BnF lat. 6113; its text passed through Arabic and Latin transformations and is poor evidence for 711 but important evidence for the legend’s transmission.
- Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada, ***De rebus Hispaniae***, completed 1243 — [LATER CHRONICLE]. Integrates the fall into a providential Christian national history.
- Alfonso X’s ***Estoria de España***, begun in the 1270s, with later Sancho IV and fourteenth-century strata — [LATER CANONICAL ELABORATION]. Makes Rodrigo’s palace, passion, political failure, and Spain’s loss part of the vernacular historical canon.
- Portuguese ***Crónica Geral de Espanha de 1344*** — [LATER CHRONICLE]. Supplies Alataba/Alacaba and gives the woman a more active but morally constrained role.
- Pedro de Corral, ***Crónica del rey don Rodrigo* / *Crónica sarracina***, c.1430; first printed 1499 — [LATE-MEDIEVAL FICTIONALIZATION]. Spain’s early historical romance expands the court, war, exile, temptations, and serpent-tomb penance. Eleven manuscripts survive and preserve substantial variation.
- Miguel de Luna, ***Historia verdadera del rey don Rodrigo***, 1592 and 1600 — [EARLY-MODERN PSEUDOHISTORY]. Introduces Florinda and retells the conquest from a pro-Arab, Morisco-inflected perspective while falsely claiming translation of an ancient Arabic witness.
- Lope de Vega, ***El último godo* / *El postrer godo de España***, composed after 1604, printed 1617 — [EARLY-MODERN DRAMA]. Dramatizes accession, desire, Julian’s revenge, conquest, and penitential closure.

### Romancero

The Rodrigo cycle survives principally through sixteenth-century printing and later oral collection; claims for a complete eighth-century epic are reconstruction, not manuscript fact. Important romances include:

- “En Ceuta está don Julián” — Julian and his daughter.
- “Amores trata Rodrigo” and related seduction material.
- “Folgaba el rey Rodrigo / con la hermosa Cava” — the lovers by the Tagus.
- “Cartas escribe la Cava” — her denunciatory letter to Julian.
- “Los vientos eran contrarios” — Julian’s passage and treason.
- “Las huestes de don Rodrigo” — battle and defeat.
- “Ayer era rey de España” — Rodrigo’s lament.
- “Después que el rey don Rodrigo” — penitence.
- “Dentro de una ermita estaba” — the tomb and serpent.

Lorenzo de Sepúlveda’s *Romances nuevamente sacados de historias antiguas* appeared in 1551. Some “old romances” may preserve earlier traditional matter, but individual printed forms must be dated to their witnesses rather than assigned wholesale to an undocumented primitive epic.

### Corpora named in the brief but not belonging to this cycle

The following are essential to the study of Iberian epic, but they contain no documented interaction with Don Rodrigo or La Cava:

- ***Cantar de mio Cid***, c.1200 manuscript tradition; Rodrigo Díaz is a different Rodrigo.
- ***Historia Roderici***, late twelfth century; concerns the Cid.
- ***Chanson de Roland***, late eleventh-century Oxford text; concerns Charlemagne and Roland.
- **Einhard’s *Vita Karoli Magni***, early ninth century, and the **Nota Emilianense**, c.1065–1075; Roncevaux witnesses, not Rodrigo–Cava texts.
- ***Poema de Fernán González***, thirteenth century.
- **Infantes de Lara** and **Bernardo del Carpio** cycles.
- ***Crónica de Alfonso III*** and Covadonga material intersect Rodrigo only through the providential succession from Gothic loss to Asturian restoration; La Cava is absent.
- Lope García de Salazar’s ***Bienandanzas e fortunas***, fifteenth century, and **Jaun Zuria** traditions belong to Basque-Biscayan genealogical legend. They do not document a meeting with Rodrigo or La Cava.

## 3. The story: episodes and variants

### Accession and disputed kingship

**[EARLY]** The *Chronicle of 754* depicts Rodrigo as obtaining rule amid upheaval. Coins of Achila II from northeastern mints indicate simultaneous or rival authority. Later Asturian chronicles give Rodrigo a royal genealogy, making him son of Theodefred and descendant of Chindaswinth. This ancestry is [LATER CHRONICLE], not contemporary proof.

Later narratives make Rodrigo either:

- lawful heir overcoming Witiza’s tyranny;
- elected king after Witiza;
- usurper who displaced Witiza’s sons;
- factional king whose enemies helped cause his destruction.

The variants reflect later arguments about legitimate kingship and treason.

### The forbidden tower or palace of Toledo

**[ARABIC TRADITION]** In Ibn al-Qūṭiyya and subsequent Arabic narratives, earlier kings sealed a chamber and every successor added a lock. Rodrigo, imagining treasure within, breaks the seals. He discovers painted Arab horsemen and a warning that when the chamber is opened, those people will conquer the country.

Later Christian retellings enlarge the site into the **Tower of Hercules**, an enchanted palace, cave, or house. Some include Solomon’s table; others place a prophetic cloth or parchment in a casket. Rodrigo’s act becomes one of illicit curiosity—an analogue to opening Pandora’s vessel or violating a taboo—but those classical comparisons are modern interpretation unless explicitly made by an author.

There is no archaeological identification of Rodrigo’s locked tower. Toledo’s “Cueva de Hércules” and related tourist sites preserve the city’s legendary geography, not an excavated Visigothic prophecy chamber.

### La Cava at court

**[ARABIC TRADITION]** Julian sends his daughter to Rodrigo’s court, conventionally at Toledo, to be educated according to noble custom. Later versions make her a hostage-like pledge, lady-in-waiting, or royal ward.

**Variants:**

- daughter of Julian: dominant tradition;
- wife of Julian: minority confusion in some transmission;
- named Oliba, Alataba/Alacaba, Caba/Cava, then Florinda;
- raised at court with other noble children;
- tending Rodrigo’s skin ailment with a gold pin: late picturesque accretion;
- bathing or washing her hair in the Tagus when Rodrigo sees her: romancero and early-modern visual-literary elaboration.

The “Baño de la Cava” at Toledo is a surviving medieval bridge-tower to which the bathing episode became attached. The building is not evidence that such a woman bathed there in 711.

### Rape, coercion, or seduction

The central conflict is not stable.

- **Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam:** Rodrigo violates or impregnates Julian’s daughter. [ARABIC TRADITION]
- **Al-Maqqarī’s compilation:** Rodrigo is overcome by desire and **forces** her; the Arabic says he compelled her and deflowered her. [LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION preserving earlier traditions]
- ***Crónica de 1344*:** she resists verbally but eventually yields under pressure. Medieval gender ideology can convert coercion into alleged female weakness.
- **Pedro de Corral:** Rodrigo’s pressure is unmistakable, yet the narrator repeatedly blames Cava’s body, conduct, silence, and womanhood. James Fogelquist and Elizabeth Drayson show that the text simultaneously records coercion and constructs her as another Eve.
- **Romances:** range from “Rodrigo viola a la Cava” to mutual pleasure—“Folgaba el rey Rodrigo / con la hermosa Cava”—or ambiguous seduction.
- **Fray Luis de León, “Profecía del Tajo,” c.1570s:** the prophetic river calls Rodrigo an “injusto forzador,” an unjust violator, while the poem also treats the woman as the disastrous object of temptation.
- **Lope:** dramatizes desire through courtly and theatrical conventions.
- **Modern retellings:** frequently convert rape into a love triangle or consensual romance.

The dossier therefore cannot choose a single version. **Rape is the dominant explicit form in several major Arabic and Christian witnesses; consensual seduction is a documented competing literary version.**

### The message to Julian

La Cava secretly tells her father what happened.

- In some Arabic narratives, she writes directly.
- In later folklore she sends symbolic gifts, notably a rotten egg, whose spoiled interior signals violated virginity.
- “Cartas escribe la Cava” makes the letter itself a set-piece of complaint and summons.
- Julian obtains leave to take her home by claiming her mother is ill.

The rotten egg is not in the earliest record and should be labeled [FOLKLORE / LATE ACCRETION].

### Julian’s revenge and the invasion

Julian vows to destroy Rodrigo’s sovereignty and aids Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād, sometimes first testing Iberia with Ṭarīf’s raid. He supplies ships, intelligence, guides, or political invitation.

Historically:

- **[EARLY]** The *Chronicle of 754* knows an **Urbanus**, a noble associated with North Africa, but does not make him the father of a violated daughter.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Some historians identify Urbanus with the Julian of Arabic tradition.
- Roger Collins and other skeptics treat the literary Julian as fictional or at least unrecoverable.
- Later Arabic narratives agree that Julian facilitated passage but disagree about his office, ethnicity, allegiance, and motives.
- No contemporary evidence connects an actual invasion facilitator with Rodrigo’s sexual misconduct.

The story changes an entangled civil war and military conquest into a morally intelligible private revenge: a king violates one household; that household destroys his kingdom.

### The battle

**[EARLY]** The *Chronicle of 754* locates the encounter at the **Transductine promontories**, an uncertain designation. It describes defeat amid aristocratic treachery or ambition.

**[LATER TRADITION]** Arabic sources place the battle near Sidonia, a lake, Wādī Lakka, La Janda, Barbate, or a river later identified with Guadalete. “Battle of Guadalete,” usually dated July 711, is the conventional modern name, not the wording of the earliest witness. The exact location and date remain disputed; 711 is probable, while the *Chronicle of 754*’s sequencing creates chronological difficulties.

Later accounts add:

- Rodrigo traveling from war against the Vascones;
- a magnificent chariot or throne-like carriage;
- betrayal by Witiza’s sons or relatives;
- immense armies;
- Rodrigo’s horse, sandals, crown, or robe found by the water;
- drowning, battlefield death, disappearance, or escape.

### Rodrigo’s fate

Four principal versions must remain separate:

1. **Death in defeat:** inferred from the eighth-century collapse and his disappearance. [EARLY inference]
2. **Unknown death; Viseu tomb:** the *Chronicle of Alfonso III* says no one knew his end but reports a discovered epitaph, *Hic requiescit Rudericus, rex Gothorum*. [LATER CHRONICLE]
3. **Survival and penitential wandering:** Pedro de Corral makes Rodrigo flee, confess, live with a holy hermit, undergo temptations, and seek divinely appointed punishment. [LATE-MEDIEVAL FICTIONALIZATION]
4. **Serpent tomb:** Rodrigo enters a sealed tomb with a two-headed serpent; it consumes the organs with which he sinned—often his genitals—and his heart. At his death bells ring without human hands. Romancero variants give the serpent seven heads and the famous cry that it eats him “por do más pecado había.” [ROMANCE / CORRAL DERIVATION]

Menéndez Pidal observed that the traditional romances contain details also found in Corral—multiple heads, self-ringing bells, the heard voice—raising a transmission problem. The evidence does not justify simply declaring the romance either wholly earlier or wholly copied from Corral; oral and written recirculation interacted.

### La Cava’s fate

The early narratives do not give her a death. Later variants make her:

- return to Julian;
- remain a condemned or shamed woman;
- drown or kill herself in the Tagus;
- die mad with grief;
- survive as Florinda in dramatic reconciliation;
- become Rodrigo’s lover and mother of a child in Handel’s opera;
- acquire posthumous association with North African monumental tombs.

None is early or historically verifiable.

## 5. Cult, tomb, and ritual

### Cult

There is **no secure evidence** for:

- a shrine or *heroon* dedicated to Rodrigo or La Cava;
- sacrifices, votive deposits, feast days, games, priesthood, oracle, or healing cult;
- canonization of either figure;
- authenticated relics;
- medieval liturgical commemoration.

Saint Roderick of Córdoba, martyred in 857, is a different person. His cult must not be transferred to King Rodrigo.

### Rodrigo’s alleged tomb

The *Chronicle of Alfonso III* reports that during Asturian activity around Viseu a tomb was found in a church with the epitaph:

> *Hic requiescit Rudericus, rex Gothorum*  
> “Here rests Rodrigo, king of the Goths.”

This is evidence that a late ninth-century Asturian author claimed such a discovery. No securely proven tomb, skeleton, or surviving early-eighth-century inscription establishes Rodrigo’s burial there. The tradition was politically useful: it brought the last Gothic king’s body into territory reclaimed by an Asturian monarchy presenting itself as Gothic successor.

Later Portuguese accounts move Rodrigo through Cauliniana, Pederneira, a cave, the hermitage of São Miguel, and Viseu. Washington Irving reproduced these claims while acknowledging their dependence on late Portuguese chroniclers.

The serpent tomb belongs to moral romance, not archaeology.

### La Cava’s places

- **Baño de la Cava, Toledo:** an actual medieval tower of the former bridge of San Martín, retrospectively attached to the bathing story.
- **Cueva or Torre de Hércules, Toledo:** an archaeological and legendary complex associated with the forbidden chamber but not demonstrably Rodrigo’s building.
- **North African “tomb of the Christian woman”:** an ancient monument or place-name later associated with La Cava through folk etymology.
- Local “Huerto de la Cava,” towers, fountains, and bathing sites elsewhere are [FOLKLORE], evidence of the legend’s diffusion rather than of her life.

These are commemorative landscapes, not cult sites.

## 6. Historical evolution of the figures

### 710–754: ruler, conquest, and military catastrophe

Coins establish Rodrigo’s authority; the *Chronicle of 754* establishes a disputed ruler destroyed during invasion and civil discord. The record is political and military. La Cava is absent.

### c.730s–740s: Umayyad imperial image

At Quṣayr ʿAmra Rodrigo becomes one of the world’s labeled sovereigns in an Umayyad visual program. His conquered kingship, not sexual sin, defines the image.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: moral causation develops

Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam supplies Julian’s violated daughter and revenge. The Asturian chronicle instead blames Witiza’s heirs and the sins of rulers and clergy, then supplies a Viseu tomb. Ibn al-Qūṭiyya supplies the locked-house prophecy. Distinct explanatory traditions coexist.

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: synthesis

The *Akhbār majmūʿa*, *Historia Silense*, Pseudo-Isidore, Jiménez de Rada, and Alfonso X’s historical workshop combine Arabic, Latin, providential, dynastic, and vernacular elements. The daughter gains a name in Pseudo-Isidore; the fall becomes the negative beginning of Christian restoration.

### Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries: Cava and the historical romance

The *Crónica de 1344* produces Alataba/Alacaba/Caba forms and sharpens gendered blame. Pedro de Corral turns chronicle into expansive fiction: Rodrigo becomes a psychologically and bodily punished sinner; Cava becomes both coerced woman and misogynistically constructed cause of catastrophe. The penitential serpent gives the fallen king possible salvation.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: romancero and national theater

Printed romances fragment the story into memorable scenes: tower, bath, letter, invasion, lament, hermit, tomb. Fray Luis’s “Profecía del Tajo” makes the river prophesy national disaster while Rodrigo is violating Cava. Miguel de Luna names her Florinda and rewrites conquest pseudohistorically from a Morisco perspective. Lope turns the material into national tragicomedy. Al-Maqqarī systematizes Arabic accounts for a seventeenth-century readership.

### Eighteenth century: opera and tragedy

Handel’s Italian opera *Rodrigo* (Florence, 1707) radically restructures the plot: Rodrigo has a wife, Esilena; Florinda is Giuliano’s sister and has borne Rodrigo a son; dynastic and erotic reconciliation replace the conquest legend.

Spanish neoclassical dramatists—Eusebio Vela, Cándido María Trigueros, Antonio Valladares de Sotomayor, and María Rosa Gálvez—used Rodrigo, Florinda, and Egilona to debate tyranny, honor, female agency, and patriotic duty.

### Nineteenth century: Romantic internationalization

The Peninsular War made Rodrigo’s lost kingdom newly usable:

- Walter Scott, *The Vision of Don Roderick* (1811), links the old invasion with resistance to Napoleon.
- Walter Savage Landor, *Count Julian* (1812), reworks tyranny, revenge, and moral ambiguity.
- Robert Southey, *Roderick, the Last of the Goths* (1814), elaborates penitence and national redemption.
- Washington Irving, *Legends of the Conquest of Spain* (1835), popularizes tower, conquest, and tomb narratives in English.
- Alexandre Herculano, *Eurico, o Presbítero* (1844), incorporates the fall into Portuguese Romantic historical fiction.
- Isidoro Lozano’s oil painting *La Cava saliendo del baño / Florinda y Don Rodrigo* (1854) fixes the voyeuristic bath scene in Spanish history painting.
- Eduardo Rosales sketched *Muerte de la Cava* around 1860–61.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

- Alberto Ginastera’s *Don Rodrigo*, libretto by Alejandro Casona, premiered at the Teatro Colón in 1964; the New York production of 1966 helped establish Plácido Domingo internationally.
- Dana Broccoli’s *Florinda* became the West End musical *La Cava* in 2000, recasting the legend as romantic spectacle.
- Academic and feminist reinterpretation increasingly restores La Cava’s status as a coerced or silenced subject and examines the cultural mechanism by which a raped woman was blamed for conquest.
- Toledo continues to market the Baño de la Cava and associated sites; murals installed near the bridge in 2025 demonstrate continued local visual reinvention. This is civic commemoration, not cult.

## 7. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and drama

| Date | Work | Reinvention |
|---|---|---|
| 1707 | Handel, *Rodrigo* | Florinda becomes Rodrigo’s lover and mother of his son; conquest history largely displaced by operatic intrigue. |
| 1760 | Trigueros, *La Egilona* | Focus on Rodrigo’s widow. |
| c.1802–04 | María Rosa Gálvez, *Florinda* | Neoclassical tragedy foregrounding coercion, gender, and political ruin. |
| 1811 | Scott, *The Vision of Don Roderick* | Uses Visigothic loss to address the Peninsular War. |
| 1812 | Landor, *Count Julian* | Rehabilitates moral and psychological ambiguity in Julian’s revenge. |
| 1814 | Southey, *Roderick, the Last of the Goths* | Expansive Christian penitential epic. |
| 1835 | Irving, *Legends of the Conquest of Spain* | English-language synthesis of tower, Cava, conquest, and tomb traditions. |
| 1844 | Herculano, *Eurico, o Presbítero* | Portuguese national-historical framing. |
| 1964 | Ginastera/Casona, *Don Rodrigo* | Modernist opera; rape, kingship, curse, and fall treated monumentally. |
| 2000 | *La Cava* musical | Love-triangle spectacle derived from Dana Broccoli’s *Florinda*. |

Handel’s Rodrigo should not be used as evidence for an ancient wife–mistress–child triangle. Ginastera’s score and Casona’s libretto likewise interpret the myth rather than transmit a medieval text unchanged.

### Images

The visual record is discontinuous:

1. Quṣayr ʿAmra’s labeled sovereign: probable eighth-century political image.
2. Medieval chronicle imagery: rulers and battles shaped by manuscript conventions rather than eyewitness likeness.
3. Nineteenth-century history painting: La Cava becomes a nude or semi-nude bathing beauty observed by Rodrigo.
4. Modern public mural and illustration: frequently converts rape into tragic romance.

Isidoro Lozano’s 1854 painting won a second medal at Spain’s first National Exhibition of Fine Arts. Its bath scene is a nineteenth-century visualization of a late legend, not an image of an attested event.

### National and political uses

The legend’s durable formula is **loss followed by restoration**:

```text
Gothic unity → royal sexual/political sin → treason → Muslim conquest
             → punishment and repentance → Pelayo/Covadonga → “restoration”
```

Medieval Asturian and Castilian historiography used this sequence to connect successor kingdoms to the Goths. Nineteenth-century nationalism naturalized it as the fall and rebirth of a timeless Spanish nation. Patricia Grieve describes the Cava–Rodrigo–Pelayo complex as a founding myth through which Christian, Muslim, and Jewish relations and Spanish identity were narrated.

The figure is less straightforwardly celebratory than El Cid: Rodrigo is the bad or failed king whose loss validates later restoration. Under National-Catholic and Francoist narratives, the broader Visigothic-unity/Reconquest scheme was politically useful, while the Cid, Catholic Monarchs, and Pelayo were more suitable positive icons. Assertions that Franco personally maintained a distinctive “cult of Rodrigo” are not supported by the evidence reviewed here. The proper finding is broader ideological incorporation, not a specific Rodrigo cult.

La Cava has served opposed politics:

- misogynistic scapegoat whose sexuality “lost Spain”;
- victim whose rape exposes royal tyranny;
- Morisco figure through whom conquest can be represented positively;
- feminist emblem of silencing and victim-blaming;
- commercial romantic heroine.

### Modern theoretical readings

Rank, Raglan, Campbell, Dumézil, and Nagy did not establish a canonical Rodrigo-specific theory comparable to their work on Oedipus, Indo-European heroic structures, or Greek oral tradition. Applying a generic monomyth mechanically—call, trial, descent, return—would obscure the actual transmission.

More relevant documented approaches are:

- Menéndez Pidal’s reconstruction of lost epic and interaction between chronicle and oral romance;
- oral-traditional and neo-traditionalist study of the romancero;
- chronicle-source criticism;
- national-myth analysis;
- gender and feminist criticism;
- postcolonial and Morisco readings of Miguel de Luna;
- memory studies focused on “loss of Spain.”

## 8. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Rodrigo historical?

Yes, to the limited degree established by coins and the *Chronicle of 754*. His reign, geographical authority, accession, and death remain less certain than traditional biographies imply. Calling him simply “the last Visigothic king of all Spain” conceals Achila II and Ardo.

### Was La Cava historical?

No independent evidence establishes her existence. The earliest source for her story is ninth-century and written in Egypt. That does not make the tradition unimportant; it means her securely documentable history begins as a literary figure.

### Was Julian historical?

Three positions recur:

1. Julian was a real governor of Ceuta who aided the invaders.
2. He preserves memory of the *Urbanus* known to the *Chronicle of 754*.
3. He is a literary concentration of several intermediaries or wholly fictional.

The resemblance to Urbanus is suggestive but not proof. His title—count, Byzantine commander, Berber ruler, Gothic governor—and political allegiance vary by source.

### Was there a Battle of Guadalete?

A decisive defeat of Rodrigo’s forces is secure. Its modern name and exact site are not. Competing locations include the Transductine promontories, Sidonia, Wādī Lakka, the Guadalete valley, Barbate, and the La Janda basin. The review headline “Guadalete did not exist” is best understood as rejecting the conventional localization, not denying the military collapse.

### What caused the conquest?

Early evidence points to civil conflict, military invasion, and aristocratic disunity. Sexual revenge is a later moral narrative. Later sources successively blame:

- Rodrigo’s disputed accession;
- Witiza’s sons;
- clerical and royal sin;
- Rodrigo’s rape or lust;
- La Cava’s sexuality;
- Julian’s treason;
- providential punishment;
- the opening of the tower.

These are explanations produced by traditions, not interchangeable historical facts.

### Did Rodrigo survive?

No contemporary source records survival. The Viseu epitaph is first reported about 180 years later; the hermitage and serpent narratives are later still. A penitent survivor is nevertheless central to the legend because he converts an irredeemable political catastrophe into personal salvation.

### Menéndez Pidal and the lost epic

Ramón Menéndez Pidal’s *El rey Rodrigo en la literatura* and three-volume *Floresta de leyendas heroicas españolas: Rodrigo, el último godo* collected the medieval and early-modern corpus and reconstructed relations among lost heroic poetry, chronicles, and ballads. His work remains foundational.

The caution is methodological: a chronicle passage resembling epic may reflect oral song, but it does not by itself prove the date, extent, or exact wording of a lost *cantar de gesta*. Deyermond’s histories of medieval Spanish literature and later romancero scholarship have treated the evidence more cautiously.

### Pedro de Corral: chronicle or novel?

Earlier readers sometimes mined the *Crónica sarracina* as history. Modern scholarship treats it as a hybrid historical romance or early historical novel. Its use of al-Rāzī, the Alfonsine *Estoria*, classical tragedy, chivalric material, and moral exempla explains both its authority and fantasy. Elizabeth Drayson calls attention to the erotic violence of Rodrigo’s final punishment; Fogelquist demonstrates how Corral reconfigures Cava through medieval misogynistic discourse.

### Miguel de Luna’s “true history”

Luna’s claimed ancient Arabic original is not accepted as genuine. His work belongs beside the Sacromonte Lead Books and Morisco pseudohistory: an early-modern intervention in who could author Spain’s past. Calling it a forgery identifies its false documentary claim; it should not erase its literary and political significance.

### Images and losses

- The Quṣayr ʿAmra fresco was damaged before modern conservation, including during early attempts to clean or detach portions.
- The Rodrigo chronicle corpus survives in recensions and late manuscripts rather than authorial originals.
- The traditional romancero was collected after centuries of oral and print circulation.
- No verified tomb assemblage or La Cava artifact exists.
- The actual battle site has not been securely identified.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- La Cava’s full name “Florinda la Cava” is contemporary with 711: false.
- *Cava* unquestionably was her Arabic personal name: unsupported.
- The Toledo bridge tower was where Rodrigo saw her bathing: folklore.
- Julian’s revenge is the established historical cause of the invasion: unsupported.
- Rodrigo opened a real magical Tower of Hercules: legend.
- His corpse and serpent tomb have been found: false.
- The Viseu epitaph survives as an authenticated eighth-century object: unproven.
- Rodrigo is a Catholic saint: confusion with Saint Roderick of Córdoba.
- The Cid, Roland, Bernardo del Carpio, Fernán González, and Jaun Zuria interacted with him: no textual evidence.
- Quṣayr ʿAmra preserves a realistic portrait from life: the label may identify Rodrigo, but the image belongs to an ideological royal series.
