# The Armenian and Georgian alphabets: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier treats Armenian and Georgian together because both arose in the Christian Caucasus in late antiquity, share important structural affinities, and repeatedly enter the same historical controversy. They are nevertheless independent writing traditions for languages from different families: Armenian is Indo-European; Georgian is Kartvelian.

Claims are marked as follows:

- **[Artefact]** Surviving object, inscription, manuscript, printed book, or securely documented digital standard.
- **[Text]** Claim made by an identifiable historical text; this label does not certify that the text’s narrative is factually correct.
- **[Reconstruction]** Scholarly inference from palaeography, linguistic structure, letter order, or historical circumstances.
- **[Tradition]** Long-standing communal or ecclesiastical account.
- **[Disputed]** Materially contested claim or interpretation.
- **[Legend]** Narrative involving anachronism, divine intervention, or an inventor for whom adequate contemporary evidence is lacking.
- **[Modern invention]** Recent redesign, reform, encoding decision, nationalist elaboration, or unsupported modern attribution.

Dates before 600 should normally be read as approximate unless an inscription contains an independently interpretable chronological formula.

# The scripts in detail

## 1. Armenian

#### Western Armenian

The same letters are retained, but a historical restructuring merged or reassigned much of the three-way Classical stop-and-affricate system. For example, Բ and Փ may both be realized approximately /pʰ/, while Պ is voiced /b/ in standard Western Armenian; analogous developments affect dental, velar, and affricate series. Consequently, a table pretending that every Armenian letter has one modern sound would be misleading.

### 1.2 Numbers

**[Artefact/Text]** Armenian letters served as alphabetic numerals in manuscripts, inscriptions, calendars, page numbering, chapter numbers, dates, and calculations. The arrangement follows four decimal series:

- Ա–Թ = 1–9
- Ժ–Ղ = 10–90
- Ճ–Ջ = 100–900
- Ռ–Ք = 1,000–9,000
- later Օ and Ֆ = 10,000 and 20,000

Numbers are additive: letters are combined, generally in descending order. A horizontal line or other scribal marking can distinguish numerals from words, but conventions vary by period and centre.

**[Finding]** Armenian alphabetic numeration is comparable to Greek isopsephy, but evidence for a single institutionalized Armenian “gematria” system equivalent in historical prominence to rabbinic Hebrew gematria is limited. Numerical wordplay and chronograms occur; claims of a primordial esoteric numerical code normally exceed the dated evidence.

### 1.3 Letter names and the acrophonic question

“Aybuben,” the Armenian word for alphabet, combines the first two names, *ayb* and *ben*, just as Greek *alphabet* combines alpha and beta and Georgian *anbani* combines *an* and *ban*.

**[Reconstruction]** Several Armenian names echo the phonological shape of Greek or Semitic letter-name traditions, but most are not transparent Armenian nouns. They do not form a demonstrable semantic series like the traditional West Semitic interpretations “ox,” “house,” and “camel” for *ʾālep*, *bēt*, and *gīml*. Armenian letter names primarily identify sounds; assigning each an ancient pictorial meaning is unsupported.

**[Modern invention]** Internet charts giving all Armenian letters elaborate primordial meanings, cosmic meanings, or pictograms should not be projected back to the fifth century unless an early Armenian text is cited.

### 1.4 Vowels

Armenian writes vowels alphabetically: Ա /a/, Ե and Է /e/, Ը /ə/, Ի /i/, Ո and later Օ /o/. Classical ու, written ՈՒ/ու, represents /u/ and developed from the sequence *o + wiwn*. It is not an abjad-style *mater lectionis* system.

The distinction between Ե/Է and Ո/Օ is partly historical and morphological in modern spelling. Soviet reform redistributed some of these spellings.

### 1.5 Capitals, minuscules, and hands

Armenian is now bicameral, but its uppercase/lowercase relationship is historically layered rather than an exact replay of Latin development.

#### Erkatʿagir

**[Artefact/Reconstruction]** Երկաթագիր *erkatʿagir*, “iron writing,” is the monumental majuscule or uncial tradition. Rounded erkatʿagir dominates the oldest formal inscriptions and manuscripts; angular or rectilinear varieties become conspicuous later. The usual explanation of “iron” is writing cut with an iron stylus or chisel, though Armenian scribal discussions also evoke biblical “iron pen” language.

It remained important from late antiquity through the high Middle Ages and continued afterward in titles, initials, monumental texts, and deliberately archaizing work.

#### Bolorgir

**[Artefact]** Բոլորգիր *bolorgir*, “round writing,” is a smaller book hand. Elements ancestral to it appear early, but fully developed and extensively attested bolorgir belongs especially to the later medieval manuscript tradition. The earliest securely dated witnesses are much later than Mashtots.

**[Disputed]** Older palaeographers sometimes treated each named hand as a sharply bounded chronological stage. The *Album of Armenian Paleography* showed more continuous, regional, and mixed development, including transitional erkatʿagir–bolorgir hands.

#### Nōtrgir

**[Artefact]** Նոտրգիր *nōtrgir*, “notarial writing,” is a compact documentary and cursive hand, especially prominent in early modern legal, commercial, colophon, and administrative writing. It draws on bolorgir and cursive tendencies and is especially common in the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries.

#### Šłagir or shghagir

**[Artefact]** Շղագիր, literally “linked writing,” is the flowing cursive tradition and an ancestor of much modern handwriting. Fully cursive forms were less often translated into metal type than erkatʿagir and bolorgir.

The [*Album of Armenian Paleography*](https://en.unipress.dk/udgivelser/a/album-of-armenian-paleography/) remains the central comparative palaeographical resource.

### 1.6 Ligatures and abbreviations

Armenian manuscripts use numerous contextual ligatures. Particularly important are:

- և, historically ե + ւ, now an ordinary high-frequency form;
- ﬓ men-now;
- ﬔ men-ečʿ;
- ﬕ men-ini;
- ﬖ vev-now;
- ﬗ men-xē.

Unicode encoded the last five as presentation forms at U+FB13–U+FB17. Modern text is generally better stored as base-letter sequences unless the compatibility character itself is historically required. Unicode treats U+0587 և as an Armenian letter/ligature with special orthographic history.

**[Artefact]** The *patiw* abbreviation mark was written above contracted sacred or common words. Manuscripts contain many more abbreviation systems than the modern encoded repertoire can convey typographically.

### 1.7 Punctuation and diacritics

Distinctive Armenian marks include:

| Mark | Name/function |
|---|---|
| ։ | վերջակետ *verǰaket*, full stop |
| ՝ | բութ *butʿ*, pause/comma-like mark |
| , | ստորակետ *storaket*, comma |
| ֊ | միության գծիկ, Armenian hyphen |
| ՜ | exclamation mark, placed over the stressed vowel |
| ՞ | question mark, placed over or just after the relevant vowel |
| ՛ | emphasis mark |
| ՟ | *patiw*, abbreviation mark |
| ՚ | apostrophe; Unicode now recommends U+2019 in ordinary modern use |

The tonal punctuation marks are unusual from a European typographic perspective: the question or exclamation sign attaches to the vowel bearing the interrogative or emphatic contour, while the sentence may still end in a full stop. See [Unicode Standard, Chapter 7](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-7/).

### 1.8 Unicode

**[Artefact]**

- Armenian block: U+0530–U+058F.
- Alphabetic Presentation Forms: Armenian ligatures U+FB13–U+FB17.
- Armenian dram sign: U+058F.
- Armenian was present in Unicode 1.0.
- Modern standard: uppercase at U+0531–U+0556, lowercase at U+0561–U+0586, with punctuation and ligature և.

**[Documented controversy]** Armenian encoding discussions have included:

- whether և should decompose into ե + ւ;
- whether an uppercase counterpart of և should be encoded;
- treatment of historical punctuation and superscript abbreviation letters;
- the difference between encoded semantic characters and merely stylistic ligatures.

The Unicode Script Ad Hoc group declined to treat every typographic uppercase rendering of և as requiring a new character. Relevant documents are indexed by [Writing Systems Technical Resources](https://writingsystems.info/scrlang/unicode/armn-unicode/).

## 2. Georgian

### 2.1 One alphabet, three historical scripts

UNESCO’s phrase “three writing systems of the Georgian alphabet” reflects Georgian cultural terminology. Historically and structurally:

- **Asomtavruli/Mrgvlovani** is the old monumental majuscule.
- **Nuskhuri** is a clerical manuscript minuscule derived from the older tradition.
- **Mkhedruli** is the secular/civil hand from which modern Georgian developed.
- **Khutsuri**, “ecclesiastical,” normally means the combined use of Asomtavruli initials or capitals with Nuskhuri body text.
- **Mtavruli** is the modern upright capital/display counterpart to Mkhedruli.

They are best understood as related historical forms of one Georgian alphabet, not three unrelated inventions.

### 2.3 Vowels and phonological design

Georgian directly represents its five modern vowels:

- ა /a/
- ე /e/
- ი /i/
- ო /o/
- უ /u/

There is no inherent vowel and no consonantal skeleton requiring optional vocalization. Spelling is relatively phonemic, although morphology, historical forms, dialects, and foreign names complicate the popular claim that every word is written exactly as pronounced.

The alphabet handles the Georgian stop and affricate series particularly efficiently:

- voiced: ბ /b/, დ /d/, გ /g/, ძ /dz/, ჯ /dʒ/
- aspirated: ფ /pʰ/, თ /tʰ/, ქ /kʰ/, ც /tsʰ/, ჩ /tʃʰ/
- ejective: პ /pʼ/, ტ /tʼ/, კ /kʼ/, წ /tsʼ/, ჭ /tʃʼ/, plus ყ /qʼ/

This systematic coverage is one reason scholars infer deliberate creation or close conscious reorganization rather than undirected evolution.

### 2.4 Letter order and names

*Anbani* combines *ani* and *bani*. The early sequence follows Greek strongly enough to be historically diagnostic. Georgian-only consonants were placed so as to preserve a Greek-compatible numerical structure, with additional values continuing into thousands.

**[Reconstruction]** This is strong evidence that the organizer knew Greek alphabetic order and Greek numerals.

**[Finding]** Georgian letter names are mainly conventional phonetic names. There is no securely attested Georgian semantic-acrophonic system equivalent to “ox, house, camel.” Elaborate claims that each circular letter encodes a cosmological picture are modern speculation unless tied to a dated text.

### 2.5 Asomtavruli or Mrgvlovani

*Asomtavruli* means approximately “capital-letter [writing]”; *mrgvlovani* means “rounded.”

**[Artefact]** It is the script of the Bir el Qutt mosaics and Bolnisi inscriptions. Early forms are largely constructed from circles, arcs, straight strokes, and symmetrical bodies. It is monumental and originally unicameral: calling it “uppercase” does not mean that a paired lowercase alphabet already existed in 430.

As Nuskhuri developed, Asomtavruli acquired a secondary capital, initial, heading, and decorative role.

### 2.6 Nuskhuri and Khutsuri

*Nuskhuri* derives from a word for “written copy” or “manuscript hand,” ultimately connected with Arabic/Semitic *nuskha* terminology.

**[Artefact]** Early recognizable Nuskhuri appears by the ninth century; inscriptions at Ateni dated to 835 are frequently cited. It became a principal Georgian ecclesiastical book hand during the tenth and eleventh centuries.

**[Artefact/Tradition]** In Khutsuri, “priestly writing,” Nuskhuri body text is paired with Asomtavruli initials and headings. The functional relationship resembles majuscule/minuscule pairing, though it does not have the same history as Latin uppercase and lowercase.

### 2.7 Mkhedruli

*Mkhedruli* literally means “military” or, by extension, lay/civil writing. The label contrasts it with priestly Khutsuri.

**[Artefact]** Precursors appear in medieval documents; a recognizably Mkhedruli civil hand becomes increasingly common in royal, legal, commercial, and secular writing from roughly the eleventh century onward. It eventually displaced Nuskhuri for most non-ecclesiastical purposes and became the modern national script.

It was historically unicameral. Normal Georgian does not capitalize sentence beginnings or proper nouns.

### 2.8 Mtavruli

Modern Mtavruli consists of upright, equal-height display forms corresponding to Mkhedruli letters. Its name relates to *mtavari*, “principal” or “capital.”

**[Artefact]** Such forms occur in headings, monuments, advertising, book design, and all-capital emphasis. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century typography sometimes used them in capitalization-like patterns.

**[Documented Unicode dispute]** An earlier Unicode position treated Mtavruli as styling comparable to small capitals. Georgian designers and Michael Everson’s later proposal documented use requiring plain-text representation. Unicode 11.0 encoded Mtavruli at U+1C90–U+1CBF in 2018 and established uppercase mappings to Mkhedruli.

Opponents argued that:

- Georgian remained orthographically caseless;
- Mtavruli normally occurred as whole words or headings;
- encoding a typographic style contradicted the character–glyph distinction.

Proponents answered that:

- historical title-case usage existed;
- all-capital text is still character-level casing even if a language’s capitalization rules differ from English;
- plain-text interchange, searching, and case mapping required stable encoded forms.

The disagreement is preserved in the [Unicode mailing-list exchange](https://www.unicode.org/mail-arch/unicode-ml/y2018-m07/0089.html) and the [Mtavruli proposal N4712](https://www.unicode.org/wg2/docs/n4712-georgian.pdf).

### 2.9 Punctuation, abbreviations, and ligatures

Old Georgian manuscripts use:

- points at different heights;
- colon-like and triple-dot separators;
- rubrication;
- enlarged or decorated initials;
- overlines and contractions for sacred names;
- manuscript ligatures and scribal abbreviations.

Modern Georgian uses largely international punctuation: period, comma, colon, semicolon, question mark, exclamation mark, quotation marks, parentheses, and dash. It has no obligatory joining or contextual shaping.

Ligatures exist in calligraphy and type design but are not fundamental encoded letters of ordinary modern Georgian.

### 2.10 Numerical notation

**[Artefact]** Georgian letters functioned as numerals in manuscripts, inscriptions, chronicles, ecclesiastical tables, dates, and pagination. The system is additive and Greek-inspired:

- first nine letters: units
- next nine: tens
- next nine: hundreds
- later letters: thousands up to 10,000

Marks such as bars or dots may identify numeral strings. Arabic numerals displaced this system in ordinary modern writing.

# Origins

## 3. The late-antique Caucasian context

Before the native alphabets, Armenia and Iberia lay among several literate powers.

**[Artefact]**

- Greek inscriptions and texts circulated through Hellenistic and Roman political, ecclesiastical, and commercial networks.
- Imperial Aramaic and its Iranian descendants were used in administration.
- Middle Persian/Pahlavi was important under Arsacid and especially Sasanian power.
- Syriac was a major Christian language east of the Roman frontier.
- The Armazi bilingual inscriptions from Iberia use Greek and a local Aramaic variety, not Georgian script.

**[Reconstruction]** This multilingual environment explains why both alphabets can be structurally Greek while displaying graphic or scribal affinities with eastern scripts. “Parent script” is therefore less simple than in a continuously evolved line such as Phoenician → Greek.

## 4. Armenian origins

### 4.1 Political and ecclesiastical setting

Armenia adopted Christianity in the early fourth century according to Armenian ecclesiastical tradition, although the exact date and character of that process remain debated. In 387 the kingdom was divided between Roman and Sasanian spheres.

**[Reconstruction]** Greek and Syriac scripture could serve clergy, but neither provided an ideal national written standard. A native alphabet supported:

- translation and preaching;
- training of Armenian-speaking clergy;
- institutional independence;
- preservation of Christian affiliation under Sasanian pressure;
- standardization of Armenian literature.

### 4.2 Mesrop Mashtots

Mesrop Mashtots was born c. 360–362 and died in 440. He had experience in royal or administrative service, became a monk and missionary, and worked with Catholicos Sahak Partev/Isaac the Great and King Vramshapuh.

The principal early narrative is Koriwn/Koriun’s *Life of Mashtots*. Koriun describes himself as a pupil and participant in the translation movement.

**[Text]** Koriun says Mesrop, distressed that scripture and worship were not adequately available in Armenian, obtained royal and catholical backing to devise letters.

**[Text]** King Vramshapuh reported that Bishop Daniel in Syria possessed Armenian letters. These “Danielian letters” were obtained and taught for a time, but proved insufficient to represent Armenian.

**[Disputed]** Their nature is unknown. Suggestions include:

- an older Armenian alphabet;
- a modified Syriac or Aramaic consonantal script;
- a local shorthand;
- letters devised by Daniel;
- a legendary or literary preliminary episode.

No securely identified Danielian inscription survives. The conjecture that their defect was failure to represent vowels adequately is attractive if they derived from an abjad, but it remains a reconstruction.

### 4.3 Edessa and Samosata

**[Text]** Koriun places Mesrop’s work in the Syriac scholarly world, especially Edessa, followed by Samosata. A Greek scribe or calligrapher named Rufinus/Rophanos is said to have helped give the letters their final graphical form.

**[Reconstruction]** This itinerary plausibly accounts for combined Greek and Syriac contacts. The alphabet’s left-to-right direction, full vowel notation, alphabetic ordering, and decimal numerical design point strongly toward Greek. Shapes are sufficiently original that letter-by-letter derivation from one cursive Greek, Syriac, or Pahlavi hand remains controversial.

### 4.4 Date: 405 or 406?

**[Tradition/Reconstruction]** Armenian convention gives 405 CE. Some chronological reconstructions yield 404, 405, or 406 depending on synchronisms and how the Armenian era is interpreted. “405” is the conventional and reasonable dossier date, not a date carved on a surviving foundation document.

### 4.5 The first translated sentence

**[Text/Tradition]** The first exercise is said to have been Proverbs 1:2:

> Ճանաչել զիմաստութիւն եւ զխրատ, իմանալ զբանս հանճարոյ  
> “To know wisdom and instruction; to perceive the words of understanding.”

The wording comes from the Armenian biblical tradition and Koriun’s account. No autograph written by Mesrop survives.

### 4.6 Original 36-letter design

**[Reconstruction]** The original alphabet was carefully matched to Classical Armenian’s vowels, consonants, aspirated stops, affricates, and two rhotics. Its numerical order adapts Greek decimal structure while inserting Armenian phonemes.

The script therefore represents deliberate phonological analysis, whether Mesrop worked alone or with Sahak, Rufinus, Daniel’s circle, translators, and other assistants.

### 4.7 Earliest Armenian artefacts

There is a striking material gap between the conventional invention date and securely dated surviving Armenian inscriptions.

#### Tekor inscription

**[Artefact, formerly extant]** The inscription of the church at Tekor named Prince Smbat Kamsarakan and Catholicos Yovhannēs Mandakuni, dating it to the latter’s tenure, 478–490. It was long treated as the earliest securely datable Armenian lapidary inscription.

The church, in present-day eastern Turkey, was damaged and ultimately destroyed; photographs, scholarly copies, and casts preserve the inscription. It does not have a widely used museum accession number comparable to a portable antiquity.

The dating is summarized in the French scholarly notice: “La première inscription connue est celle de Tekor,” tied to Mandakuni’s catholicate, at [Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0001_1995_num_11_1_8086).

#### Fifth-century fragments and graffiti

**[Artefact/Disputed dating]** Some manuscript fragments and graffiti have been assigned to the fifth or sixth century palaeographically. Because they lack explicit dates, precise placement depends on comparison with later material.

#### Hripsime inscriptions

**[Artefact]** Inscriptions connected with the church of St Hripsime at Vagharshapat are conventionally dated 618 and 628. They are early secure demonstrations of rounded erkatʿagir.

#### Earliest dated substantial manuscripts

- **[Artefact]** Gospel of Queen Mlkē, Venice, San Lazzaro MS 1144/86, dated within 851–862.
- **[Artefact]** Lazarian Gospel, Matenadaran MS 6200, dated 887.
- **[Artefact]** Echmiadzin Gospel, Matenadaran MS 2374, copied in 989; it incorporates older leaves usually assigned around the sixth–seventh century and has a sixth-century ivory binding.

The [Matenadaran manuscript catalogue](https://matenadaran.am/en/category/manuscripts/) and the [Echmiadzin manuscript record](https://utpictura18.univ-amu.fr/serie/evangile-detchmiadzine-989-erevan-matenadaran-ndeg-2374-armenie) distinguish the 989 codex from its older inserted materials.

### 4.8 Was there pre-Mashtots Armenian writing?

#### What is documented

Armenians participated in literate states before 405, and Greek, Aramaic, and Iranian scripts were present in Armenian territories. Armenian personal names and words appear in texts written in other languages.

#### Traditions and arguments

**[Text/Tradition]**

- Movsēs Xorenacʿi attributes literary or archival activity to pre-Christian Armenia.
- Tir was remembered as a deity associated with writing and scribal knowledge.
- The Danielian episode implies that “Armenian letters” of some kind were claimed before Mesrop completed his system.
- Later writers sometimes transformed this into a lost national alphabet suppressed after conversion.

#### Evidentiary conclusion

**[Finding]** No accepted corpus of pre-405 inscriptions in an identifiable alphabet used consistently to write the Armenian language has been found. Literacy in Armenia is not the same proposition as a pre-Mashtots Armenian alphabet. Claims that Urartian, “Armeno-Phoenician,” petroglyphic, or otherwise undeciphered symbols are the direct Armenian alphabet require a demonstrable phonetic decipherment and archaeological sequence; none has gained specialist consensus.

## 5. Georgian origins

### 5.1 The archaeological terminus

Unlike Armenian, Georgian lacks a universally accepted contemporary inventor narrative. The earliest securely dated Georgian-script artefacts are Christian inscriptions of the fifth century.

This supplies:

- a **terminus ante quem**: the script existed by c. 430;
- a likely cultural setting: Christian translation and monasticism;
- but not the inventor, exact place, or exact year.

### 5.2 Bir el Qutt

**[Artefact]** In 1952 the Franciscan archaeologist Virgilio Canio Corbo excavated a Georgian monastery dedicated to St Theodore Tiron at Bir el Qutt in the Judaean Desert, between Jerusalem and Bethlehem.

Four mosaic inscriptions in Asomtavruli were found:

- two conventionally dated to c. 430;
- two to 532.

They contain Christian invocations and commemorations, including the name Bakur and prayers for founders or patrons.

Repository: Studium Biblicum Franciscanum/Terra Sancta Museum, Jerusalem. The available public catalogues identify them as Bir el Qutt inscriptions rather than by stable, universally cited museum accession numbers. That absence should not be filled by inventing one.

Corbo published the excavation; Georgian scholars subsequently analysed the language and letterforms. They were not “deciphered” like an unknown script: Asomtavruli remained readable through the Georgian tradition.

A concise archaeological record is provided by the [Bir el Qutt object summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bir_el_Qutt_inscriptions); the original excavation publication should be preferred for measurements and stratigraphy.

### 5.3 Bolnisi Sioni

**[Artefact]** Georgian inscriptions at Bolnisi Sioni in Kvemo Kartli date the construction to 478/479–493/494. One records the building over a span of years and refers to the reigning Persian monarch.

The original inscriptional stones are in Tbilisi museum collections; replicas stand at the church. One is catalogued as no. 8 in Nodar Shoshiashvili’s corpus; see the [Epigraphic Corpus of Georgia record](https://tamarae.github.io/ECG_static/inscriptions/ECG056.html).

Bolnisi supplies the earliest securely dated Georgian inscriptions within Georgia itself. The architectural study available through the Library of Congress confirms the 493 completion date and notes that the original is in the Shalva Amiranashvili Museum of Fine Arts: [Bolnisi Sioni study](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/18/02/74/35/2018027435/2018027435.pdf).

### 5.4 Other early witnesses

**[Artefact]**

- fifth- and sixth-century inscriptions occur in Palestine and Georgia;
- the Mtskheta/Jvari and other church inscriptions document the consolidation of monumental Georgian;
- Khanmeti linguistic features survive in early palimpsests;
- Sinai preserves an exceptionally important Georgian manuscript tradition.

The oldest explicitly dated substantial Georgian manuscript is commonly identified as the Sinai Mravaltavi, Sin. georg. 32–57–33 with associated new finds, dated 864. Its undertexts and related pieces are catalogued in the [Sinai Manuscripts Data Portal](https://sinaiportal.library.ucla.edu/textunits/s17t2w).

### 5.5 Greek structural model

**[Reconstruction]** The strongest broadly accepted conclusions are:

1. Georgian was intentionally organized as a full alphabet.
2. Its direction and complete vowel notation align with Greek.
3. Its order and numerical series closely track Greek.
4. Special Georgian consonants were integrated deliberately.
5. Its graphic shapes are not simple copies of contemporary Greek capitals.

This allows “Greek model” to be stated with more confidence than “direct graphic descent from a particular Greek hand.”

### 5.6 Christian creation hypothesis

**[Reconstruction]** Many specialists place creation after the conversion of Iberia, conventionally c. 337, and before Bir el Qutt c. 430. Translation of scripture and liturgy is the most economical context.

The conspicuous absence of Georgian-script texts from well-excavated pre-Christian sites, despite Greek and Aramaic inscriptions, supports but cannot mathematically prove this conclusion.

### 5.7 The Pharnavaz account

The Georgian chronicle tradition, particularly the *Life of the Kings* incorporated in *Kartlis Tskhovreba*, says that King Pharnavaz “spread the Georgian language” and is commonly interpreted as saying he created or established Georgian writing. Pharnavaz is placed traditionally in the third century BCE.

- **[Text]** The medieval Georgian chronicle contains the statement.
- **[Tradition]** Georgian national memory treats Pharnavaz as founder of state, language, and alphabet.
- **[Legend/Disputed]** No contemporary Pharnavaz inscription or Georgian-script object from the third century BCE verifies the invention.
- **[Alternative reconstruction]** Some scholars interpret the passage as language policy, administrative literacy, or later projection of medieval cultural institutions onto a founding king.
- **[Finding]** Even if a historical Pharnavaz promoted scribal administration, the evidence does not identify that writing with surviving Asomtavruli.

### 5.8 Koriun and the Mashtots question

Versions of Koriun’s *Life* say that Mashtots went to Iberia, worked with an interpreter named Jaghay, and made Georgian letters. Later Armenian historical writers repeat or elaborate his creation of Georgian and Caucasian Albanian scripts.

#### Case for Mashtotsian participation

- **[Text]** The attribution occurs in the transmitted text of Koriun, an early source close to Mashtots’s circle.
- **[Text]** Later Armenian histories repeat it.
- **[Reconstruction]** Armenian and Georgian alphabet creation belongs to the same Christian translation environment.
- **[Reconstruction]** A scholar capable of organizing Armenian and Albanian writing could have collaborated with Georgian speakers through interpreters.
- **[Reconstruction]** The probable date fits Mashtots’s lifetime.

Hrachia Acharian defended Koriun’s essential reliability. Older international handbooks often repeated “Mashtots invented Armenian and Georgian” more confidently than current scholarship does.

#### Case against or for caution

- **[Textual criticism/Disputed]** Georgian scholars including Ivane Javakhishvili argued that the Georgian section was a later interpolation, perhaps after the Armenian–Georgian ecclesiastical rupture of the early seventh century. No autograph or fifth-century manuscript of Koriun survives, so the interpolation claim cannot be settled by direct manuscript comparison.
- **[Silence]** Łazar Pʿarpecʿi praises Mashtots’s Armenian work but does not independently confirm Georgian invention.
- **[Linguistic reconstruction]** Tamaz Gamkrelidze argued that Georgian’s design required close knowledge of Georgian phonology not demonstrated for Mashtots.
- **[Graphic reconstruction]** Georgian order and numerical organization are at least as directly Greek-oriented as Armenian and do not require derivation from Armenian letterforms.
- **[Archaeological finding]** Bir el Qutt demonstrates Georgian monastic writing in Palestine but names no inventor.
- **[Historiographical concern]** Medieval saints’ lives characteristically expand missionary achievements.

#### Balanced conclusion

**[Finding]** Mashtots’s invention of Armenian is supported by a near-contemporary biographical tradition, multiple early Armenian historians, a coherent political context, and uninterrupted Armenian memory. His personal invention of Georgian is possible but unproved. Koriun’s transmitted testimony cannot simply be erased, yet archaeology supplies neither confirmation nor refutation of personal involvement.

The most defensible formulation is:

> Georgian was deliberately organized in a Greek-oriented Christian environment by c. 430. Armenian experience and Armenian clerical networks may have influenced the project; Mashtotsian participation remains a disputed historical claim rather than an established fact.

### 5.9 Caucasian Albanian comparison

Koriun and Movsēs Kałankatuacʿi also associate Mashtots with an alphabet for Caucasian Albanian. Unlike the supposed Danielian alphabet, Caucasian Albanian has been materially recovered in inscriptions and Sinai palimpsests.

Its existence shows that several indigenous alphabets were indeed created or standardized in the Christian Caucasus. It does not by itself prove that one person designed all three.

# Spread and change

## 6. Fifth–seventh centuries: translation cultures

### Armenian

**[Text/Artefact]** Armenian literary culture began with biblical and liturgical translation from Syriac and Greek, followed by patristics, theology, history, philosophy, grammar, science, and hagiography.

Koriun says the prophets and apostles became “Armenian-speaking.” This is a theological description of translation, not evidence that a complete surviving fifth-century Bible is autograph.

Sahak Partev, Eznik of Kołb, Koriun, Łazar Pʿarpecʿi, Ełišē, and the tradition attached to Movsēs Xorenacʿi stand near the formation of Classical Armenian prose.

### Georgian

**[Artefact/Text]** Georgian literacy also spread through scripture, lectionaries, saints’ lives, and monasteries. Georgian monks worked in:

- Kartli and Tao-Klarjeti;
- Palestine, including Jerusalem and the Judaean Desert;
- Sinai;
- the Black Mountain near Antioch;
- Mount Athos.

The fifth-century *Martyrdom of Shushanik*, attributed to Iakob Tsurtaveli, is conventionally treated as the earliest surviving original work of Georgian literature, though its manuscript witnesses are later.

## 7. Manuscript centres

### Matenadaran

The Mesrop Mashtots Matenadaran in Yerevan holds one of the world’s greatest Armenian manuscript collections. Its institutional roots lie in the manuscript treasury of Echmiadzin; it became a distinct research institution in 1959.

**[Artefact]** Holdings include biblical codices, lectionaries, histories, legal works, medicine, astronomy, calendars, music, poetry, dictionaries, colophons, archival fragments, and illuminated manuscripts. The precise total changes through acquisition and cataloguing, so current figures should be taken from the [Matenadaran](https://matenadaran.am/), not frozen from older encyclopedias.

Other major Armenian repositories include:

- the Armenian Patriarchate of Jerusalem;
- the Mekhitarist Congregation, San Lazzaro, Venice;
- the Mekhitarist library in Vienna;
- the British Library;
- Bibliothèque nationale de France;
- Library of Congress;
- Walters Art Museum;
- New Julfa and Echmiadzin collections.

### Georgian manuscript collections

Major repositories include:

- Korneli Kekelidze Georgian National Centre of Manuscripts, Tbilisi;
- National Archives of Georgia;
- National Parliamentary Library of Georgia;
- St Catherine’s Monastery, Sinai;
- Iviron Monastery, Athos;
- Jerusalem and Vatican collections.

Palimpsest recovery has materially expanded the corpus of early Georgian and Caucasian Albanian.

## 8. Scribal arts and illumination

### Armenian

Armenian manuscripts developed:

- canon tables under architectural arches;
- zoomorphic and anthropomorphic initials;
- birds, fish, serpents, and vegetal letter bodies;
- marginal signs;
- red rubrics;
- donor portraits;
- elaborate Gospel cycles;
- densely informative colophons.

Prominent artistic centres include Ani, Cilicia, Vaspurakan, Siwnikʿ, Artsakh, Crimea, Constantinople, and New Julfa. Named artists include Tʿoros Roslin, active in Cilicia in the thirteenth century, celebrated for signed and dated Gospel illumination.

### Georgian

Georgian manuscripts likewise employ:

- monumental Asomtavruli initials;
- Nuskhuri body text;
- rubrication;
- decorated headpieces and canon tables;
- geometric, vegetal, and figural ornament;
- colophons identifying scribes, patrons, and monasteries.

The Khutsuri pairing visually marks ecclesiastical hierarchy: Asomtavruli for sacred emphasis, Nuskhuri for continuous text.

## 9. Medieval secularization and documentary use

### Armenian

Bolorgir and nōtrgir facilitated smaller codices, correspondence, legal deeds, commercial records, tax material, and extensive colophons. Armenian merchant networks carried the script from the Mediterranean to Poland, Crimea, Iran, India, and Southeast Asia.

### Georgian

Mkhedruli emerged in royal charters, legal documents, correspondence, courtly literature, and administration. By the early modern period it was the dominant civil script.

The word “military” should not be interpreted as proof that soldiers invented it. It expresses a social contrast with priestly writing.

## 10. Printing

### Armenian printing

**[Artefact]** Hakob Meghapart established the first Armenian press in Venice. In 1512 he printed *Urbatagirkʿ*, the “Book of Fridays,” followed by several other books in 1512–1513.

The printed forms imitate manuscript bolorgir and retain abbreviations, ligatures, rubrics, and woodcut ornament. The [Library of Congress exhibition](https://loc.gov/exhibits/armenian-literary-tradition/overview.html) documents the press and its cultural consequences.

Important later centres included:

- Constantinople;
- Rome;
- Amsterdam;
- Marseille;
- New Julfa;
- Madras;
- Calcutta;
- Venice’s Mekhitarist press;
- Vienna;
- Tbilisi;
- Moscow and St Petersburg.

Oskan Erevantsʿi’s Armenian Bible, Amsterdam, 1666–1668, was a milestone in standardizing printed biblical Armenian.

Type design often shared erkatʿagir-like capitals with bolorgir-derived lowercase. European punchcutters sometimes regularized Armenian according to Latin proportions, a process intensified in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Modern designers debate how much stroke contrast, baseline regularity, and Latin typographic structure Armenian can absorb without losing native rhythm.

### Georgian printing

**[Artefact]** In 1629 the Propaganda Fide press in Rome printed:

- *Alphabetum Ibericum sive Georgianum cum Oratione*;
- Stefano Paolini and Niceforo Irbachi/Nikoloz Irubakidze-Cholokashvili’s Georgian–Italian dictionary.

The Georgian diplomat and cleric supplied linguistic and scribal expertise. Type was based in part on Georgian royal correspondence. See the [Georgian Encyclopedia biography](https://www.georgianencyclopedia.ge/ka/form/31358) and [Typotheque’s history](https://www.typotheque.com/articles/georgian-alphabet-writing-and-typography).

The first press in Georgia was established under Vakhtang VI at Tbilisi in 1709, assisted by expertise connected with Anthim the Iberian and his printing network. Its publications included the Psalter and, in 1712, an influential edition of Shota Rustaveli’s *The Knight in the Panther’s Skin*.

Georgian type passed through Europeanizing, Russian imperial, Soviet, and post-Soviet stages. Metal type sometimes simplified or distorted manuscript proportions; digital revival has encouraged closer study of historic ductus.

## 11. Orthographic reforms

### Armenian

#### Medieval additions

Օ and Ֆ were added for sounds increasingly needed in loanwords. The older sequence Ո and Ւ/ու continued in inherited vocabulary.

#### Soviet Armenian reform

**[Modern invention/documented reform]** Soviet Armenia introduced a reformed orthography in 1922, modified in 1940. It altered the distribution of ե/է, ո/օ, յ, and ւ, and elevated և in practical alphabetization.

The reform is used in the Republic of Armenia and much Eastern Armenian publishing derived from Soviet practice.

**[Documented controversy]** The Armenian diaspora, Western Armenian institutions, the Armenian Apostolic sees outside Soviet Armenia, and Eastern Armenian in Iran largely retained traditional spelling. Critics saw the reform as etymologically destructive or politically imposed; supporters emphasized regularity, mass literacy, and correspondence with Eastern Armenian pronunciation.

Both systems remain living. Calling either simply “correct Armenian” erases an institutional and diasporic division.

### Georgian

In the nineteenth century, Ilia Chavchavadze and associates of the national revival promoted removal of five letters no longer needed by contemporary Georgian phonology. Printing and schooling consolidated the 33-letter standard.

**[Modern reform]** This was not the invention of modern Georgian writing but a rationalization of its inventory.

Soviet rule did not replace Georgian with Cyrillic, though Russian political dominance affected publishing, education, and terminology. Georgian remained the principal script of the Georgian language.

## 12. Other languages

### Armenian script

Armenian letters have occasionally represented Ottoman Turkish, Kurdish, Kipchak/Armeno-Kipchak, and other languages in Armenian communities. Armeno-Turkish printing is especially substantial.

### Georgian script

Georgian has been adapted for:

- Mingrelian;
- Svan;
- Laz;
- Abkhaz during certain nineteenth- and twentieth-century phases;
- Ossetian in some early publications;
- other Caucasian languages in scholarly or temporary orthographies.

Extra encoded letters such as ჶ, ჷ, ჸ, ჹ, and ჺ belong to some of these adaptations, not the standard Georgian 33.

## 13. Digital transition

Both scripts suffered from incompatible pre-Unicode encodings and Latin-font hacks. Unicode enabled interoperable text, but implementation problems persisted:

- inadequate fonts;
- wrong normalization of ligatures;
- confused legacy encodings;
- poor keyboard support;
- incorrect Armenian punctuation placement;
- conflation of Asomtavruli with Mtavruli;
- missing Nuskhuri or historic Georgian glyphs;
- search failures across traditional and reformed Armenian spelling.

Unicode solved character identity, not palaeographic transcription. Manuscripts still require specialist fonts, markup, abbreviational expansion, and often image–text linkage.

# The letters as numbers and signs

## 14. Armenian symbolic uses

### Alphabetic numerals and chronograms

**[Artefact]** Armenian letters date buildings, tombstones, manuscripts, and printed books. Chronograms may embed a date in words whose letters are summed.

### Sacred abbreviation

**[Artefact]** Names such as God, Jesus, Christ, Lord, and Saint can be contracted and marked by a superscript line or *patiw*, comparable in function—not necessarily direct descent—to Greek *nomina sacra*.

### Alphabet hymns and acrostics

**[Text/Artefact]** Armenian religious poetry includes alphabetic acrostics in which successive lines begin with Ա through Ք. Alphabet order provided mnemonic and theological architecture.

### Alphabet mysticism

**[Tradition]** Mesrop’s letters acquired sacred-national importance. Medieval and modern Armenian authors treated the alphabet as providential, a defense of Christianity and national continuity.

**[Finding]** There is no Armenian equivalent, with comparable early canonical standing, to the Hebrew *Sefer Yetzirah*’s cosmology of letters. Modern numerological systems assigning universal metaphysical essences to each Armenian letter should be labeled modern esotericism unless an early manuscript witness is produced.

### Talismans and amulets

**[Artefact]** Armenian manuscript culture contains prayers, protective scrolls, magical diagrams, sacred names, crosses, letter squares, and amuletic texts. Letters function there as written sacred power, but this is broader Christian manuscript magic rather than proof that the alphabet was invented as a magical code.

## 15. Georgian symbolic uses

### Numerals

**[Artefact]** Georgian letters appear as dates and numbers in inscriptions, manuscripts, ecclesiastical tables, chapter divisions, and colophons.

### Sacred display

Asomtavruli became a sign of antiquity and sanctity. Monumental inscriptions, icons, church walls, Gospel headings, and embroidered objects use it long after Mkhedruli became the civil norm.

### Acrostics

**[Text]** Georgian hymnography and poetry employ alphabetic acrostics and name acrostics. The ordered sequence becomes a compositional frame and a way for authors or scribes to encode names.

### “Alphabet prayers”

**[Tradition/Text]** Georgian schools and ecclesiastical practice transmit alphabetic prayers and pedagogical verses. Their existence does not prove that the original letter shapes were cryptographic theology.

### Modern symbolic politics

**[Modern use]** Georgian letterforms appear in state branding, currency, monuments, public art, tourism, and national demonstrations. Armenian letters likewise appear in monuments, jewelry, diasporic identity, and public memorial art.

# People

## 16. Armenian

### Mesrop Mashtots, c. 360/362–440

- **[Text/Tradition]** Inventor or chief organizer of Armenian writing.
- Missionary, teacher, translator, and saint.
- Traditionally also associated with Caucasian Albanian and, disputably, Georgian.
- Buried at Oshakan, where his tomb became a national shrine.

### Sahak Partev, c. 354–439

Catholicos and senior ecclesiastical partner of the translation project. Armenian tradition sometimes compresses the achievement into “Mesrop invented the alphabet,” but Koriun’s account makes royal, clerical, scribal, and translation collaboration essential.

### Vramshapuh, ruled c. 389–414

Arsacid king who authorized the project and introduced the Danielian-letter report.

### Bishop Daniel

**[Text]** Custodian or source of the insufficient preliminary letters. His alphabet is unattested materially.

### Rufinus/Rophanos

**[Text]** Greek calligrapher or scribe at Samosata who helped shape or order the new signs.

### Koriun/Koriwn, fifth century

Pupil of Mashtots and author of the earliest surviving biography. His proximity is invaluable, but the work is hagiography with biblical typology, not a bureaucratic invention report.

### Łazar Pʿarpecʿi, late fifth century

Early historian who independently reinforces Mashtots’s Armenian achievement but does not clearly corroborate the Georgian episode.

### Movsēs Xorenacʿi

Traditionally fifth century, though the date and textual layers of his *History of Armenia* have long been disputed. Important for later Armenian historical memory, including pre-Mashtots literacy traditions.

### Tʿoros Roslin, active 1256–1268 and later

Cilician Armenian illuminator and scribe whose signed manuscripts demonstrate the integration of writing, painting, patronage, and liturgy.

### Hakob Meghapart, active 1512–1513

First Armenian printer. “Meghapart” means “the sinner,” a devotional epithet rather than a criminal designation.

### Oskan Erevantsʿi, 1614–1674

Printer of the first complete Armenian Bible in print, Amsterdam, 1666–1668.

### Mekhitar of Sebaste, 1676–1749

Founder of the Mekhitarist Congregation, whose Venetian and Viennese scholarship, dictionaries, editions, type, and printing profoundly shaped modern Armenian philology.

### Hrachia Acharian, 1876–1953

Linguist and historian of the Armenian alphabet; defended the credibility of Koriun’s account, including its Caucasian components more strongly than many Georgian scholars.

### Modern palaeographers

Frederick Macler, Yakov Tashjian, Garegin Hovsepian, Hrachia Acharian, Michael E. Stone, Dickran Kouymjian, and Henning Lehmann helped establish comparative Armenian palaeography. Their work replaced overly rigid stage schemes with manuscript-based analysis.

## 17. Georgian

### Pharnavaz I

Traditional founding king, conventionally third century BCE.

- **[Text/Legend]** Creator or promulgator of Georgian writing in medieval Georgian historiography.
- **[Finding]** No contemporary artefact connects him with Asomtavruli.

### Anonymous late-antique organizer or organizers

**[Reconstruction]** The secure evidence requires at least one highly competent bilingual or multilingual organizer familiar with Georgian phonology, Greek alphabetic order, and Christian textual needs. No name is securely recoverable.

### Mesrop Mashtots

**[Disputed]** Candidate participant or inventor according to transmitted Armenian sources. The evidence and objections are treated above.

### Jaghay/Jagha

**[Text]** Interpreter named in the Mashtots tradition as assisting work among Georgians. No independent contemporary biography survives.

### Virgilio Canio Corbo, 1918–1991

Franciscan archaeologist who excavated Bir el Qutt in 1952, bringing the early Palestinian evidence to scholarly attention.

### Ivane Javakhishvili, 1876–1940

Historian who studied the origins and palaeography of Georgian writing and argued against taking the Mashtots attribution at face value.

### Akaki Shanidze, 1887–1987

Philologist central to Old Georgian grammar, Khanmeti texts, Sinai manuscripts, and the history of Georgian writing.

### Tamaz Gamkrelidze, 1929–2021

Linguist who developed the Greek-structural analysis of the Georgian alphabet and argued for a Christian-era creation.

### Ilia Chavchavadze, 1837–1907

Writer, editor, and national leader associated with the modern 33-letter reform and standardization of Georgian literary culture.

### Shota Rustaveli, conventionally c. 1160–after 1220

Author of *The Knight in the Panther’s Skin*. Not a script inventor, but the reception and printing of his poem were central to Mkhedruli’s prestige.

### Nikoloz Irubakidze-Cholokashvili/Niceforo Irbachi, c. 1585–1658/59

Cleric, diplomat, and collaborator in the first Georgian movable-type publications in Rome, 1629.

### Stefano Paolini

Italian printer and co-compiler of the 1629 Georgian–Italian dictionary.

### Vakhtang VI, 1675–1737

King, lawgiver, editor, and patron of the first Tbilisi printing press.

### Anthim the Iberian, c. 1650–1716

Georgian-born Orthodox hierarch and master printer in Wallachia; his printing network helped transmit expertise to Tbilisi.

### Michael Everson and contemporary Georgian designers

Participants in modern Unicode proposals, including the contentious encoding of Mtavruli. Their work belongs to character standardization and type technology, not the ancient invention of the alphabet.

# Culture

## 18. Scripture and church

The alphabets became durable partly because each was tied to scripture in a national church.

### Armenian

- Bible translation and revision;
- lectionaries and Gospel books;
- patristic translation;
- hymnals and ritual books;
- khachkar inscriptions;
- church foundations and donor memorials;
- sacred-name contractions.

Classical Armenian, *grabar*, remained a liturgical and learned language after spoken Armenian changed substantially.

### Georgian

- Old Georgian Bible translations;
- lectionaries;
- saints’ lives;
- hymnography;
- Sinai, Jerusalem, Antioch, and Athos monastic books;
- Asomtavruli inscriptions and Khutsuri liturgical manuscripts.

Nuskhuri and Asomtavruli remain visible in Georgian Orthodox ecclesiastical culture even though Mkhedruli dominates daily writing.

## 19. Law and administration

### Armenian

Armenian letters appear in:

- princely and ecclesiastical inscriptions;
- canon law;
- contracts and commercial correspondence;
- community registers;
- merchant documentation;
- legal manuscripts such as Mkhitar Gosh’s law code;
- seals and ownership notes.

### Georgian

Georgian appears in:

- royal charters;
- monastic grants;
- legal codes;
- boundary inscriptions;
- diplomatic correspondence;
- seals;
- coin legends alongside Greek, Arabic, Persian, or Cyrillic in multilingual political environments.

Vakhtang VI’s law code and editorial projects linked Mkhedruli print with state reform.

## 20. Monuments

### Armenian

Erkatʿagir appears on churches, tombstones, fortifications, bridges, dedicatory blocks, and khachkars. Letterforms often merge with interlace and vegetal ornament.

### Georgian

Asomtavruli appears on church façades, mosaic floors, crosses, donor reliefs, and royal monuments. The Bolnisi cross and inscriptional style became major symbols of Georgian Christian antiquity.

## 21. Coins and seals

Neither script monopolized the coinage of its region. Political sovereignty often required Greek, Arabic, Persian, Mongol, Russian, or bilingual legends.

**[Artefact]** Armenian and Georgian letters nevertheless occur on medieval and modern coins, seals, medals, banknotes, and national currencies. Their presence or absence reflects political authority and audience rather than merely literacy.

## 22. Calendars and computation

Both alphabets supplied numerals for:

- regnal and ecclesiastical dates;
- Easter tables;
- manuscript foliation;
- zodiacal and astronomical texts;
- tables of months and feast days;
- chronologies.

Armenian dating may use the Armenian Era beginning in 552 CE. Georgian manuscripts employ several chronological systems, including Creation eras, regnal years, indictive cycles, and Christian dating. Conversion requires attention to the particular scribal convention.

## 23. Literature and alphabet art

### Armenian

Alphabetic acrostics appear in hymns, laments, moral verse, and pedagogical poetry. Decorated initials may become birds, dragons, human figures, architecture, or vines. The alphabet itself is a frequent subject of poems and monuments.

### Georgian

Georgian poetry uses alphabetic and name acrostics. Calligraphy emphasizes the circular geometry of Asomtavruli and the flowing rhythm of Mkhedruli. Modern artists enlarge letters into sculpture, concrete poetry, logos, and abstract composition.

### Modern monuments

- The Armenian Alphabet Monument near Artashavan, created in 2005 for the conventional 1,600th anniversary, is **[Modern invention/commemoration]**, not an archaeological site.
- Georgian alphabet sculptures and national typography similarly turn writing into public heritage.

## 24. Identity and diaspora

For Armenians, the alphabet survived the loss of political sovereignty and became a portable institution across monastic and merchant diasporas. For Georgians, continued use of Georgian script under Persian, Ottoman, Russian imperial, and Soviet pressure became a marker of linguistic sovereignty.

This symbolic force explains why origin debates become politically charged. Cultural importance, however, does not decide archaeological chronology.

# Controversies and disputes

## 25. Was Armenian derived from Greek, Syriac, Pahlavi, or an earlier Armenian script?

### Greek case

Evidence:

- left-to-right direction;
- explicit vowel letters;
- alphabetic order;
- alphabetic numeral system;
- Christian Greek scholarly setting;
- some possible shape correspondences.

This is the strongest structural model.

### Syriac case

Evidence:

- Mesrop’s reported activity at Edessa;
- Syriac Christian scripture and schools;
- possible shape and ductus parallels;
- early Armenian translation from Syriac.

It is plausible as a contributing scribal environment, less convincing as the sole parent.

### Pahlavi/Iranian case

Evidence:

- Pahlavi was the administrative language of Sasanian Armenia;
- some letter shapes have been compared with Iranian cursive forms;
- Armenian scribes knew Iranian documentary culture.

Objections include Pahlavi’s right-to-left, consonantally oriented, highly ambiguous system—the opposite of several deliberate Armenian design choices.

### Lost native alphabet case

Evidence offered:

- Danielian letters;
- pre-Christian Armenian literacy traditions;
- the deity Tir;
- later reports of older books.

Counterevidence:

- no accepted pre-405 corpus;
- literary references do not identify letterforms;
- “writing in Armenia” may mean Greek, Aramaic, or Iranian writing.

### Conclusion

**[Reconstruction]** Armenian is best classified as an original fifth-century alphabet created under dominant Greek structural influence in a Syriac–Iranian contact zone. A neat family-tree arrow from one graphic parent overstates the evidence.

## 26. Did Mashtots alone invent Armenian?

**[Tradition]** Mesrop is the named inventor.

**[Textual nuance]** Koriun depicts a collective enterprise involving Sahak, Vramshapuh, Daniel’s letters, Syrian scholars, Greek calligraphic assistance, teachers, and translators.

**[Finding]** “Mashtots invented the Armenian alphabet” is a defensible shorthand if “invented” means led and completed its design. It should not erase collaborators or imply that a complete font emerged in one solitary vision.

## 27. Divine vision

Later Armenian tradition sometimes describes the letters as divinely revealed, including stories that Mesrop saw them traced by the hand of God.

- **[Tradition/Legend]** Expresses sacred authorization.
- **[Textual history]** Later narratives intensify providential elements already compatible with Koriun’s hagiographical frame.
- **[Finding]** This is theological tradition, not independently testable design documentation.

## 28. The Danielian letters

They are among the most important genuine unknowns.

- Their existence is reported by Koriun.
- No specimen is securely identified.
- They may have been incomplete because they lacked vowels, failed to distinguish Armenian consonants, or were not designed for Armenian at all.
- Later claims that archaeologists have “found the Danielian alphabet” remain unaccepted without a decipherable corpus and provenance.

## 29. Georgian: Pharnavaz versus Christian-era creation

### Pharnavaz position

Support:

- medieval Georgian chronicle;
- national tradition;
- arguments that a pre-Christian state required native writing;
- proposed early readings of disputed inscriptions.

Problems:

- source written many centuries after the alleged event;
- no secure third-century-BCE Georgian-script artefact;
- Greek and Aramaic, not Georgian, dominate surviving ancient epigraphy;
- state administration does not require a vernacular alphabet.

### Christian-era position

Support:

- first secure evidence is Christian;
- Greek structural model;
- script well suited to Bible translation;
- absence from pre-Christian archaeological contexts;
- parallel creation of Armenian and Caucasian Albanian scripts.

Limit:

- the first surviving object need not equal the date of invention;
- Christian use does not prove that no earlier experimental phase existed.

### Conclusion

A fourth- or early-fifth-century Christian creation remains the strongest reconstruction; Pharnavaz belongs to documented medieval tradition and national legend unless earlier secure evidence appears.

## 30. Was the Koriun Georgian passage interpolated?

### Interpolation argument

Javakhishvili and other Georgian scholars proposed that the passage entered Koriun’s text after ecclesiastical conflict, perhaps in the sixth or seventh century. They cite:

- lack of independent early corroboration;
- narrative and linguistic issues;
- absence in Łazar’s account;
- possible polemical motive.

### Authenticity argument

Acharian and defenders respond:

- all known manuscript traditions contain the material;
- Koriun was close to Mashtots;
- absence in another author is not proof of interpolation;
- use of an interpreter answers the language-objection;
- no manuscript preserves a shorter demonstrably earlier text.

### Finding

The passage is **textually transmitted but historically disputed**. “Later interpolation” is not a palaeographically proven fact; “therefore Mashtots certainly invented Georgian” also goes beyond what transmission alone proves.

## 31. Nekresi inscriptions

Levan Chilashvili proposed pre-Christian dates, in some cases second or first century BCE, for Asomtavruli-like inscriptions excavated around Nekresi.

### Arguments for early dating

- claimed archaeological association with pre-Christian structures and burials;
- letterforms read as archaic Georgian;
- apparent non-Christian or Zoroastrian contexts.

### Criticism

- stratigraphic associations were considered insufficiently secure;
- some stones may be reused or intrusive;
- letterforms do not compel the proposed date;
- the historical reconstruction depends heavily on the desired palaeographic conclusion;
- specialists such as Stephen Rapp described some datings as circumstantial.

A recent Georgian-language reassessment notes that pre-fourth-century sites have yielded abundant Greek and Aramaic material but no generally accepted Georgian-script text: [“Concerning the Nekresi Inscriptions”](https://chronos.ihe.tsu.ge/index.php/journal/article/view/83).

**Conclusion:** The inscriptions are real artefacts; their very early dates and use as proof of a Pharnavaz-era alphabet are disputed.

## 32. Grakliani Hill inscription

An inscription discovered at Grakliani Hill was publicly announced as extremely ancient and potentially transformative.

- **[Artefact]** Marks exist on an excavated object or architectural surface.
- **[Disputed]** The sign system has not received a generally accepted decipherment as Georgian.
- **[Finding]** An undeciphered inscription in Georgia is not automatically an early Georgian alphabet.
- **[Modern media problem]** Headlines often converted “unknown script found in Georgia” into “oldest Georgian writing,” which is not the same claim.

## 33. Claims of Georgian antiquity in classical authors

Passages about Colchian writing, records, or inscribed objects are sometimes cited.

**[Finding]** They show that inhabitants of the region participated in literacy or were believed to possess written records. Without letterforms or language identification, they cannot establish the use of Asomtavruli.

## 34. Armenian–Georgian graphic resemblance

Some letters look similar, and both alphabets employ rounded monumental geometry.

Possible explanations include:

- direct influence of one on the other;
- common Greek design principles;
- shared late-antique calligraphic environment;
- deliberate differentiation from Greek and Syriac;
- coincidence within a limited repertoire of circles, stems, and crossbars.

**[Finding]** Visual resemblance alone cannot identify inventor or direction of borrowing. Order, phonological mapping, chronology, and actual ductus are more probative than isolated look-alikes.

## 35. “Three Georgian alphabets”

- **[Tradition/UNESCO terminology]** Three writing systems remain culturally recognized.
- **[Palaeographic reconstruction]** They form an evolutionary and functional continuum.
- **[Finding]** Saying “Georgia has three alphabets” is culturally legitimate but can mislead if it implies three separate sign inventories created independently.

## 36. UNESCO 2016 listing

**[Artefact/institutional act]** In 2016 UNESCO inscribed “Living culture of three writing systems of the Georgian alphabet” on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

UNESCO’s decision says Mrgvlovani was followed by Nuskhuri and Mkhedruli and that all remain in use. The listing recognizes living practice and identity; it does not adjudicate whether Pharnavaz or Mashtots invented the script. See the [UNESCO decision 11.COM 10.b.13](https://ich.unesco.org/en/Decisions/11.COM/10.b.13).

## 37. Unicode as a historical authority

Unicode code points establish digital identities, not ancient genealogy. Terms such as “Georgian Capital Letter An” reflect modern encoding architecture. They do not prove that Asomtavruli functioned as an uppercase partner of Mkhedruli in antiquity.

Likewise, encoding Armenian ligatures does not prove that every encoded presentation form was alphabetically independent.

## 38. Forgeries and misreadings

No single Armenian or Georgian forgery plays the same canonical role as the Praeneste fibula controversy in Latin studies. The recurring problems are instead:

- unprovenanceanced stones offered as ancient inscriptions;
- medieval or modern carvings assigned pre-Christian dates;
- decorative marks read as letters;
- Greek, Aramaic, Pahlavi, or Caucasian Albanian texts relabeled Armenian or Georgian;
- damaged dates reconstructed circularly;
- modern alphabet monuments photographed as though archaeological;
- machine-generated charts inventing letter meanings;
- nationalist websites promoting disputed readings without excavation reports.

A responsible authentication requires:

1. secure provenance and stratigraphy;
2. material and tool-mark analysis;
3. letterform comparison without circular dating;
4. linguistically coherent reading;
5. publication of photographs and competing readings;
6. independent specialist review.

# Open questions

1. What exactly were the Danielian letters: earlier Armenian, modified Syriac, an abjad, shorthand, or a hagiographical preliminary device?

2. Which individual graphic sources did Mesrop and his collaborators use? Greek structure is clear, but the contribution of Syriac and Iranian hands remains difficult to quantify.

3. How much did Rufinus/Rophanos determine the final Armenian shapes?

4. How large was the gap between the creation of Armenian c. 405 and the lost earliest manuscript corpus?

5. When precisely did forms ancestral to bolorgir emerge, and should they be treated as a separate hand in the earliest fragments?

6. Who created Georgian writing, and was the work done in Kartli, Palestine, Syria, or through a transregional monastic network?

7. Is Koriun’s Georgian passage original to the earliest recoverable text? Earlier manuscripts or new textual witnesses could materially alter the debate.

8. Did Mesrop participate directly, advise Georgian clergy, or merely become attached to the story through the prestige of his Armenian achievement?

9. Can any Nekresi inscription be dated independently of palaeographic assumptions to before the fourth century CE?

10. What is the Grakliani sign system, and is it linguistic writing at all?

11. How should the earliest Georgian palimpsests be dated as imaging reveals more undertext?

12. How much did Armenian, Georgian, and Caucasian Albanian designers influence one another, as opposed to independently adapting Greek organizational principles?

13. What are the precise histories of Georgian ჳ /w/ and უ /u/ in the numerical sequence?

14. How should Unicode represent manuscript abbreviations without confusing diplomatic transcription with ordinary normalized text?

15. Will Armenian traditional and reformed orthographies continue as stable parallel standards, or converge in particular digital domains?
