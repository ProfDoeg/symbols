# Ogham: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| **Name** | **Ogham**, also **ogam** and historically *ogom*. Medieval Irish often calls the alphabet **Beithe-luis-nin**, from letter names conventionally associated with its opening sequence. |
| **Type** | **Alphabet**: consonants and vowels have independent letters. Because its signs are systematically differentiated by the number and position of strokes, it may also be described morphologically as **partly featural**, but “alphabet” is the secure functional classification. |
| **Core inventory** | **20 letters**, arranged in four groups (*aicmí*, singular *aicme*) of five: 15 consonantal signs and 5 vowels. |
| **Extensions** | Five principal supplementary signs, the **forfeda** (“additional letters”), developed in the manuscript/scholastic tradition; further local and manuscript variants exist. |
| **Direction** | Monumental Ogham normally begins at the bottom of a stone’s left-hand edge and ascends; it may continue over the top and descend the other edge. Manuscript Ogham is commonly horizontal, left-to-right, along a drawn stemline. No established boustrophedon tradition is known. |
| **Period** | Invention uncertain, probably **late Roman period**; securely attested from approximately the **late fourth or fifth century CE**. The principal “orthodox” monumental phase belongs approximately to **400–600/700**. Scholastic, manuscript and antiquarian uses continued through the Middle Ages and early modern period; revived uses continue today. |
| **Region** | Ireland, especially Munster and overwhelmingly southwest Ireland; Wales, Cornwall, Devon, the Isle of Man, western and northern Scotland, Orkney and Shetland; one important find at Silchester in southern England. |
| **Languages** | Primarily Primitive/Archaic Irish, later Old and Middle Irish; Latin in some learned uses; some Scottish inscriptions probably represent Pictish or otherwise unidentified language, and later Manx/Scottish examples occur in multilingual settings. |
| **Parent** | **No demonstrable graphic parent.** Scholarly consensus treats Ogham as a deliberate creation by people acquainted with Latin grammatical/alphabetic learning. Its *ordering* and phonological analysis have been compared with Latin and Greek grammatical traditions, while its strokes may derive from tally notation. Direct descent from Latin, Greek, runes or a prehistoric “tree alphabet” is unproved. |
| **Daughters** | No major daughter script. “Scholastic Ogham,” cryptographic *ogams*, modern adaptations, jewellery alphabets and Neo-pagan divination systems are continuations or reinventions rather than independent daughter scripts. |
| **Decipherment** | Ogham was **not lost and rediscovered** in the manner of Egyptian or Linear B. Medieval alphabet keys remained available. Bilingual inscriptions and comparative Irish linguistics subsequently corrected and refined readings. |

**Evidence labels used below**

- **[A] Documented artefact:** a surviving stone, object or manuscript.
- **[T] Documented text/tradition:** a statement actually present in a historical literary or learned source.
- **[R] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from linguistic, epigraphic or archaeological evidence.
- **[D] Disputed claim:** a position with material scholarly disagreement or inadequate evidence.
- **[L] Legend:** a mythic or pseudohistorical narrative, reported as such.
- **[M] Modern invention/reception:** a demonstrably modern construction or practice.

## The script in detail

### Graphic principle

**[A]** Monumental Ogham is built around a **stemline**. On standing stones the stemline is usually the natural arris or edge. Letters consist of one to five strokes:

1. to one side of the stemline;
2. to the opposite side;
3. crossing or straddling it;
4. notches or strokes directly upon it.

This produces four graphic families of five signs each. A letter’s identity depends jointly on:

- its family;
- the side or relationship to the stemline;
- the number of strokes.

This is why an inverted, rotated or incorrectly segmented copy can change its reading. Weathering, broken edges and natural fissures create corresponding epigraphic hazards.

**[R]** The economy of the system—four positions multiplied by one through five strokes—strongly suggests conscious construction. Its numerical architecture resembles tallying, but resemblance is not proof that literal tally marks were its immediate source.

### The forfeda

**[A/T]** The *forfeda* are additional signs outside the original four-by-five structure. Their forms, names and assignments vary more than those of the core alphabet. Their existence is especially associated with scholastic Ogham and the need to accommodate changed Irish phonology, diphthongs and Latin loans.

| ᚖ | U+1696 | OGHAM LETTER OR | **Ór** | OI | Name means “gold”; commonly a diphthongal sign. |

**[R/D]** It is misleading to print one forfeda table as though all five signs simultaneously possessed one immutable fifth-century sound system. Some values are late; some were reassigned; the stones attest them unevenly. The lack of a native /p/ phoneme in Primitive Irish helps explain why P was absent from the core inventory and why supplementary P notation became useful for loans.

### Order

**[T/R]** The canonical grouping is:

> BLFSN · HDTCQ · MGNZR · AOUEI

This is a transliteration mnemonic, not ordinary Latin alphabetical order. Manuscript spellings and modern displays often obscure that the value of ᚃ was earlier closer to W/V than later F.

The name **Beithe-luis-nin** is anomalous if understood simply as “the first three signs,” because the displayed order is B-L-F-S-N. Possible explanations include an earlier ordering, use of selected family-initial or salient names, or later reinterpretation. No proposed solution is universally demonstrable.

### Vowels

**[A]** Ogham has five independent vowel letters. It is therefore neither an abjad nor a vowelless cipher. Vowels are routinely written in authentic Primitive Irish inscriptions, including final inflectional vowels.

There are:

- no *matres lectionis*;
- no vowel-pointing system;
- no parallel to the Greek conversion of Semitic consonant signs into vowels, except in the very general sense that an alphabet with full vowel notation was known to its learned inventors;
- no evidence for an early “consonantal Ogham” preceding the five-vowel aicme.

Claims that American petroglyphs represent “vowelless Ogham” remove one of the actual system’s defining features and make arbitrary scratches much easier to manipulate into desired readings.

### Word division, punctuation and layout

**[A]** Early monumental inscriptions generally use little or no systematic punctuation. Formula and proper-name morphology often identify word boundaries. In manuscript and modern typesetting, spaces may be shown by:

- blank intervals;
- an uninterrupted stemline;
- U+1680 **OGHAM SPACE MARK**: ` `;
- opening and closing feather marks: `᚛` and `᚜`.

| Sign | Code point | Unicode name | Function |
|---:|---|---|---|
|   | U+1680 | OGHAM SPACE MARK | Space maintaining, in some typefaces, a visible stemline |
| ᚛ | U+169B | OGHAM FEATHER MARK | Modern/editorial opening mark |
| ᚜ | U+169C | OGHAM REVERSED FEATHER MARK | Modern/editorial closing mark |

Unicode’s handling of U+1680 produced a small but genuine design dispute: whether its reference glyph should visibly preserve a stemline while still behaving as whitespace. UTC minutes and later proposals document the revisions. [UTC minutes](https://www.unicode.org/consortium/utc-minutes/UTC-113-200710.html); [Unicode core specification](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/UnicodeStandard-17.0.pdf).

### Capitals, minuscules, ligatures and diacritics

**Absence of evidence is important here.**

- **Capitals/minuscules:** no indigenous bicameral distinction comparable to Latin capitals and minuscule.
- **Case:** Unicode Ogham is unicameral.
- **Ligatures:** no general ligature system comparable to Latin scribal ligatures. Joined or compound scholastic forms occur, notably *emancholl*, but should not be projected backward as ordinary monumental ligatures.
- **Diacritics:** no native system for length, lenition, aspiration or palatalization.
- **Long vowels:** not consistently distinguished graphically from short vowels.
- **Consonant mutation:** later Irish Latin orthography’s dotting and *h*-digraph conventions do not belong to original Ogham.
- **Decoration:** crosses and Christian signs sometimes accompany inscriptions, but are not alphabetic diacritics.

### Letters as numbers

**[A/T]** Ogham’s stroke counts are inherently ordinal—one through five within each *aicme*—and medieval descriptions exploit this structure in finger, nose, leg, shield and other cryptographic *ogams*.

**Absence:** there is no securely attested Ogham numeral system analogous to Greek Milesian numerals, Hebrew gematria or Arabic abjad numerals. Modern charts that assign every letter a divinatory number usually count its position in a group or in the full series. That is a convenient modern index, not proof that fifth-century writers used letters as numerical signs.

## Origins: dated and placed

### What can actually be dated

There is no securely excavated “first Ogham inscription” accompanied by a dated inventor’s statement. Most Irish stones were:

- found standing without datable deposits;
- moved to churches, graveyards or collections;
- reused as lintels, building stones or grave markers;
- dated mainly through language and letter forms.

Consequently “Ogham was invented in 400 CE” is a shorthand, not an artefact date.

### Late fourth to early fifth century: the earliest archaeological horizon

#### Silchester, Hampshire: E-HAM-001 / CIIC 496

**[A]** A fragmentary Ogham inscription was cut on a reused baluster or architectural stone from Roman Calleva Atrebatum, excavated in 1893 in Insula IX. Re-examination of its context and associated radiocarbon evidence places deposition in the later Roman/sub-Roman sequence; the inscription is commonly assigned to the **fourth or early fifth century**, arguably after c. 325.

The surviving text is damaged and its personal names are debated. Its importance is chronological and cultural: a person familiar with Irish writing was present in, or connected to, a Romanized town. The University of Reading summarizes the excavation and two AMS dates from associated wood. [Silchester Archaeology](https://research.reading.ac.uk/silchester/the-ogham-stone/).

**Qualification:** the radiocarbon samples date the archaeological context, not the moment the strokes were cut.

#### Broch of Gurness, Orkney: inscribed knife handle

**[A]** An Ogham-inscribed organic object from the Broch of Gurness produced a radiocarbon range of approximately **cal CE 340–540 at 95% probability**. This demonstrates that knowledge of Ogham reached northern Scotland surprisingly early. It does not establish that Ogham originated there. [Antiquity](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2018.68).

#### Newgrange and Raffin

**[A/R]** Ogham objects or stones have been associated with late Roman material at Newgrange and with the royal site at Raffin, County Meath. Proposed fourth-century dates are important but context-dependent. They strengthen the possibility of pre-400 use without providing an incontrovertible dated invention.

### Fifth and sixth centuries: the principal monumental corpus

**[A]** The overwhelming body of “orthodox” inscriptions belongs to approximately the fifth and sixth centuries. Their language preserves Primitive Irish before major developments visible in Old Irish:

- final syllables still present;
- inherited case endings;
- /kʷ/ represented by *cert*;
- formulaic genitives;
- earlier consonant values.

Typical formulae include:

- `X MAQI Y` — “of X, son of Y”;
- `X MAQI MUCOI Y` — approximately “of X, son/member of the kin or people of Y”;
- `ANM X` — “name of X,” more characteristic in later contexts.

#### CIIC 81, Garranes, County Cork

**[A]** `C[A]SSITT[A]S MAQI MUCOI CALLITI`, now among the UCC Stone Corridor collection, exemplifies a personal name plus descent/kin formula. Its broken or uncertain vowels also illustrate why editions must distinguish visible strokes from restoration. [Digital humanities edition discussion](https://zfdg.de/sb005_010).

#### CIIC 58

**[A/R]** `CATTUBUTTAS` preserves a final ending later lost and is frequently used to illustrate the linguistic distance between Primitive and Old Irish.

#### CIIC 243 and 244

**[A/R]** The inscriptions name related generations and illustrate genealogical commemoration:

- CIIC 244: `COILLABBOTAS MAQI CORBBI MAQI MOCOI QERAI`;
- CIIC 243: `MAQI-RITTE MAQI COLABOT MAQI MOCO QERAI`.

Their juxtaposition is powerful evidence that the stones record real social lineages, although proposed historical identifications remain reconstructions.

#### Ballintaggart, County Kerry

**[A]** The concentration of nine stones at Ballintaggart near Dingle is one of the clearest demonstrations that inscription was a regional social practice rather than an isolated curiosity. CIIC 157 reads approximately `DOVETI MAQQI CATTINI`.

#### Arraglen, County Kerry: CIIC 145

**[A]** `QRIMITIR RONANN MAQ COMOGANN` commemorates the priest Rónán, son of Comgán. *Qrimitir* reflects Latin *presbyter* through Irish phonology. Crosses and a chi-rho place the monument in a Christian environment.

### Wales and southwest Britain

#### Bridell, Pembrokeshire

**[A]** The fifth-century inscription `NETTASAGRI MAQI MUCOI BRIACI` commemorates Nettasagri, “son/member of the kin of Briaci.” A cross in a circle was added later. It is a strong artefactual witness to Irish language and settlement in southwest Wales. [Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales](https://rcahmw.gov.uk/early-medieval-carved-stones-in-wales-by-professor-nancy-edwards-chair-of-the-commissioners-rcahmw/).

#### Castell Dwyran / Voteporix stone

**[A]** This fifth- or sixth-century bilingual stone carries:

- Latin: `MEMORIA VOTEPORIGIS PROTICTORIS`;
- Ogham: `VOTECORIGAS`.

**[T/D]** It was long identified with Vortiporius, the ruler attacked by Gildas. Current linguistic evaluation does not require—and may oppose—that identification: the inscription can commemorate another person of a similar name. The change from Latin/Brittonic P to Gaelic C/K is deliberate adaptation to Irish phonology. [OG(H)AM object study](https://ogham.glasgow.ac.uk/index.php/ogham-of-the-month/).

#### Fardel Stone, Devon: British Museum 1861,0209.1 / CIIC 489

**[A]** A red-sandstone memorial, dated to the fifth–sixth centuries, bears Latin inscriptions including `FANONI MAQVTRINI` and `SAGRANVI` and Ogham along its angles. It materially joins Roman epigraphic custom, Irish linguistic influence and post-Roman Dumnonia. It is preserved in the British Museum under registration **1861,0209.1**. [British Museum collection record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1861-0209-1).

### Seventh and eighth centuries: regional transformations

**[A/R]** Monumental Ogham declined in Ireland but remained active or was transformed in Scotland, the Northern Isles and the Isle of Man. Scottish examples may use:

- the face rather than edge of a stone;
- drawn stemlines;
- unusual signs;
- crosses and Pictish symbols;
- linguistic material not securely interpretable as Irish.

#### Broch of Burrian, North Ronaldsay

**[A/D]** A seventh- or eighth-century cross-slab from a secondary occupation layer bears Ogham. Katherine Forsyth described its reading as the only extant sentence in Pictish, but both segmentation and linguistic interpretation require caution. The object is in National Museums Scotland, formerly catalogued **NMAS/NMS GB.1** in the cited record. [Canmore](https://canmore.org.uk/site/3679/north-ronaldsay-broch-of-burrian).

### Ninth century: Ogham in manuscripts

#### St Gallen, Stiftsbibliothek MS 904, p. 204

**[A]** A ninth-century copy of Priscian contains the Irish word `LATHEIRT`, “excessive drunkenness/hangover,” written in Ogham in its upper margin. This is among the earliest surviving manuscript uses and proves that the script served informal scribal play, identity and concealment, not merely funerary monuments. [OG(H)AM Project](https://ogham.glasgow.ac.uk/index.php/2022/01/05/the-origins-of-manuscript-ogam-and-medieval-irish-grammatical-tradition/).

This manuscript evidence marks no sudden “decipherment.” Literate Irish scholars still knew the script.

### Fourteenth century: Book of Ballymote

**[A]** The fullest surviving medieval display is in the **Book of Ballymote**, Royal Irish Academy **MS 23 P 12**, compiled approximately **1383–1397**. Its Ogham material appears around fols. 169r–170v and includes *In Lebor Ogaim* alongside the *Auraicept na nÉces*. Digital images and manuscript history are available from [Irish Script on Screen](https://www.isos.dias.ie/RIA/RIA_MS_23_P_12.html).

**[T]** *In Lebor Ogaim* preserves:

- the alphabet and its divisions;
- letter names;
- *bríatharogaim*, short kennings explaining letters;
- the forfeda;
- many alternative “ogams” or cryptographic scales;
- competing origin stories;
- grammatical classifications.

The Book of Ballymote copy is late, but parts of the learning it transmits are substantially earlier. Exact dating of individual strata is difficult: a fourteenth-century manuscript is evidence that a passage existed by the fourteenth century, not automatically that it existed unchanged in pagan antiquity.

## The borrowing or invention event

### Latin grammatical inspiration

**[R; currently the strongest general model]** Ogham was probably devised by a learned person or small community familiar with Latin literacy and grammatical classification during the late Roman period.

Evidence includes:

- systematic separation of vowels and consonants;
- an inventory designed around the phonology of early Irish;
- awareness of phonemes and alphabetic order;
- signs for sounds important in Primitive Irish but awkward in contemporary Latin spelling;
- contact across the Irish Sea with Roman Britain;
- early Christian and pre-Patrician Christian communities in Ireland by the early fifth century;
- the Silchester context.

McManus proposed that Christians or Christian-connected scholars could have created it partly to give Irish a distinct written form. This is plausible but not directly documented by an inventor’s colophon.

### Cipher hypothesis

**[R/D]** Ogham can be analyzed as a transformation or cipher of alphabetic knowledge: letters are reassigned to numbered strokes rather than borrowed graphically.

Supporting evidence:

- artificial four-by-five construction;
- later medieval use as cryptography;
- the *Ogam Tract’s* claim that it belonged to learned people and excluded “rustics and herdsmen”;
- ease of communicating letters by fingers or body positions.

Against a narrowly “secret code” origin:

- the monumental inscriptions are conspicuous public objects;
- their texts are formulaic names and genealogies, not hidden political messages;
- later cryptographic enthusiasm need not reproduce original purpose.

### Tally hypothesis

**[R/D]** Rudolf Thurneysen, Joseph Vendryes and others connected the shapes with tally notation. The one-to-five strokes in four positions make this mechanically attractive.

Against treating this as proved:

- tally systems are widespread;
- no intermediate artefact shows Irish tallies being converted letter by letter into Ogham;
- alphabetic ordering and phonological choices still require learned design.

The likely synthesis is that alphabetic/grammatical knowledge supplied the linguistic system while tally-like marks supplied a practical graphic mechanism.

### Runic, Greek and other proposed parents

**[D]** Graphic comparison with runes or North Italic alphabets has been proposed. Chronology permits awareness of runes, but Ogham’s structural logic differs sharply from the *futhark*. No sequence of intermediate forms establishes descent.

Direct derivation from Greek is likewise unsupported. Greek grammatical concepts could have reached its inventor through Latin education without Greek letter shapes being copied.

### Prehistoric or druidic survival

**[D/L/M]** Claims that Ogham descends from a Bronze Age Celtic tree script or from an immemorial Druidic alphabet have no securely dated inscriptions, manuscripts or abecedaria to support them. Perishable wooden writing is possible, but possibility is not evidence. The oldest recoverable linguistic system fits Late Antique Irish much better than a remote prehistoric stage.

## Spread and change

### Phase 1: formation and Irish Sea mobility, approximately 350–450

**[R]** Ogham probably arose within a network joining Ireland and Roman Britain. Soldiers, traders, captives, clergy and settlers could all have carried alphabetic knowledge. Silchester shows movement into southern Britain; southwest Irish concentration may indicate either invention there or especially intensive adoption there.

### Phase 2: orthodox monumental culture, approximately 400–600

**[A/R]** The script was carved mainly on standing stones. Southwest Ireland—especially the territory associated with the **Corcu Duibne** in County Kerry—contains the densest cluster.

Most texts record:

- a man’s name in the genitive;
- his father;
- occasionally grandfather or kin group;
- sometimes status or ecclesiastical association.

The stones have been interpreted as:

- **memorials or grave markers**;
- **territorial/property assertions**;
- lineage monuments;
- boundary markers;
- public statements of elite identity.

No single function accounts for every stone. Many are not in original positions, making a universal “boundary-stone” theory unsustainable.

### Phase 3: Irish settlement and bilingual epigraphy in Britain

**[A/R]** Ogham travelled with Irish-speaking communities to Wales, Cornwall and Devon. Bilingual Latin–Ogham stones do not merely provide a decipherment crib; they display two cultural registers:

- Ogham/Irish ancestry;
- Latin and post-Roman Christian memorial practice.

The spellings are not always literal translations. Names can be adapted between Goidelic, Brittonic and Latin phonologies.

### Phase 4: Scotland, Pictland and the Northern Isles

**[A/D]** Ogham was adapted more freely in Scotland. Some inscriptions are linguistically obscure, partly because:

- the underlying language may be Pictish rather than Irish;
- supplementary and regional characters occur;
- surfaces are damaged;
- texts may run on stone faces;
- letter orientation and stemlines vary.

Richard Cox proposed Old Norse readings for some Scottish inscriptions. Others regard them as Pictish or multilingual products. There is no consensus that all Scottish Ogham represents one language.

### Phase 5: scholastic Ogham

**[A/R]** From roughly the seventh or eighth century onward, especially in Ireland, Ogham became a learned secondary script alongside the Latin alphabet. Scholars distinguish this from “orthodox” Ogham because its spellings and forms can be mediated by manuscript teaching rather than inherited monumental practice.

Uses included:

- marginal notes;
- colophons and signatures;
- grammatical examples;
- cryptography;
- learned games;
- headings and display;
- antiquarian monuments.

A legal manuscript scribe, for example, signed himself `GILLA NA NAEM O DEORAIN` in standard Ogham. Irish legal manuscripts also use deliberately obscure scripts and anagrams, embedding Ogham in professional scribal culture. [OG(H)AM legal manuscripts study](https://ogham.glasgow.ac.uk/index.php/2023/08/22/ogam-script-and-cryptography-in-the-irish-legal-manuscript-tradition-guest-blog-by-dr-chantal-kobel/).

### The many manuscript “ogams”

**[T]** *In Lebor Ogaim* preserves approximately one hundred named or illustrated scales; the Book of Ballymote contains about ninety-two in its particular presentation. These include methods described through:

- fingers;
- noses or legs;
- shields;
- colours;
- trees or plants;
- animals;
- occupational or semantic classes;
- substitutions and rearrangements.

These are not one hundred historically independent alphabets. Some are:

- graphic variants;
- substitution ciphers;
- mnemonic taxonomies;
- learned exercises;
- literary conceits.

The tradition that a *file* learned many such scales reflects the script’s role in elite grammatical training, though curriculum statements in later sources should not automatically be projected into the fifth century.

### Printing and type

Ogham never developed an indigenous manuscript calligraphy comparable in scale to uncial, Carolingian minuscule, Kufic or naskh. Manuscript scribes normally drew it with pen strokes along a horizontal stemline.

Early modern printers faced two approaches:

1. cut specialized sorts or engraved plates;
2. reproduce Ogham with rules and ordinary type approximations.

Antiquarian works such as Roderic O’Flaherty’s *Ogygia* (1685), Edward Lhuyd’s *Archaeologia Britannica* (1707), Charles Vallancey’s publications, and later Brash and Macalister disseminated printed alphabets and stone drawings. Engraving was often more practical than movable type.

Victorian Celtic Revival design placed Ogham on jewellery. The British Museum owns an 1849 silver copy of a medieval Irish brooch marketed as an “Ogham pin,” demonstrating how antiquarian script became national decorative art. [British Museum 1978,1002.529](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1978-1002-529).

### Digital encoding

**[A/M]** Ogham occupies **U+1680–U+169F**. Encoded characters comprise:

- U+1680 space mark;
- 20 core letters;
- six supplementary characters;
- two feather marks;
- three reserved positions at U+169D–U+169F.

The block entered Unicode/ISO 10646 during the development culminating in Unicode 3.0-era support. Michael Everson played a major role in standardizing Irish script names and glyph practice. The official names intentionally accommodate historical spelling variation imperfectly; character names cannot later be changed merely to adopt a preferred modern spelling.

The most visible technical controversy concerned whether OGHAM SPACE MARK should appear as blank space or carry a stemline. Unicode now explains that a stemmed font may display the connecting line while a stemless implementation may leave it blank. [Unicode chart](https://unicode.org/charts/PDF/U1680.pdf); [Ogham names history](https://www.evertype.com/standards/og/ognames.html).

## The letters as numbers and signs

### Letter names and the acrophonic question

The Ogham names are lexical words, but the relationship between name and sound is uneven.

- **[R]** Many conform approximately to acrophony: *beith* begins B, *luis* L, *dair* D.
- **[D]** Some names may have been altered after their original values changed.
- **[T]** Medieval scholars freely etymologized names through puns, glosses and kennings.
- **[R]** The manuscript key remains broadly authentic, but it is not an untouched fifth-century handbook.

McManus’s analysis of archaizing spellings argues that the learned tradition preserved genuine early information even while misunderstanding some signs. Patrick Sims-Williams accepted parts of that reconstruction while emphasizing unresolved values for Úath, Gétal and Straif. [Sims-Williams abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-968X.1993.tb01067.x); [McManus article](https://www.cip.ifi.lmu.de/~langeh/test/1986%20-%20McManus%20-%20Ogam%20-%20Archaizing%2C%20Orthography%20and%20the%20Authenticity%20of%20the%20Manuscript%20Key%20to%20the%20Alphabet.pdf).

### Bríatharogaim

**[T]** Three sets of “word Oghams” or kennings are traditionally associated with:

- Bríatharogam Morainn mic Moín;
- Bríatharogam Maic ind Óc;
- Bríatharogam Con Culainn.

Each supplies compressed poetic clues for the letter names. They are important evidence for medieval interpretation and sometimes for original etymology. They are not equivalent to a standardized ancient divination manual.

### Ogham and trees

The evidence has four distinguishable layers:

1. **[R] Original names:** about six are securely or probably trees—Beith, Fern, Sail, Dair, Coll and Onn; Ailm and Idad are possible.
2. **[T] Medieval systematization:** the *Auraicept* and associated tracts assign trees or plants to the full alphabet, sometimes by strained glossing.
3. **[M] Graves’s calendar:** a reordered set of tree letters becomes thirteen lunar months.
4. **[M] Neo-pagan practice:** letters become divinatory symbols with keywords, woods, colours, deities and seasonal meanings.

The fact that *fid* can mean both “wood/tree” and “letter,” and that strokes resemble branches on a stem, encouraged arboreal classification. It does not establish that every original letter was named for a tree.

### Divination in medieval literature

**[T]** Irish narratives describe messages or magical knowledge mediated by Ogham-cut rods or wands.

In *Tochmarc Étaíne*, the druid Dalan uses rods and Ogham in divination to discover where Étaín has been taken. This proves that medieval Irish literature associated Ogham with divinatory expertise. It does **not** by itself prove that surviving fifth-century stones were divination tools or that modern casting layouts reproduce a pagan rite. [CELT edition of *Tochmarc Étaíne*](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/G300012/).

Ulster Cycle narratives associate Ogham messages on rods, hoops or branches with Cú Chulainn and warnings placed in landscapes. These scenes represent the imagined heroic past of medieval authors. Their historical content cannot be dated simply to the age in which their heroes supposedly lived.

### Magic

**[T/R]** Ogham’s secrecy, compressed learned associations and bodily/finger variants made it suitable for:

- charms;
- concealed authorship;
- medical or healing texts;
- mnemonic classifications;
- protective inscriptions.

Directly dated pre-Christian magical Ogham objects are scarce. “Ogham was a Druidic magical alphabet” therefore exceeds the artefactual record, although medieval tradition undeniably made it a script of occult or specialist knowledge.

### Comparison with gematria and runes

Ogham has no documented equivalent to:

- Hebrew gematria;
- Greek isopsephy;
- Arabic abjad numerals;
- the extensive runic rune-poem tradition as a primary witness to letter names.

The *bríatharogaim* are the closest formal analogy to rune poems: both attach compact verbal lore to named letters. Historical dependence between them has not been demonstrated.

## People

### Ogma

**[L/T]** *In Lebor Ogaim* says:

> “Ogma, a man well skilled in speech and poetry, invented the Ogham.”

The text explains its creation as proof of ingenuity and as speech reserved for learned people. Ogma belongs to the Tuatha Dé Danann and is associated with eloquence and martial strength.

**[D]** Ogma has often been connected with Lucian’s Gaulish **Ogmios**, an aged Herakles-like deity whose eloquence binds listeners by chains from tongue to ears. Similarity of name and eloquence supports comparison, but Thurneysen, van Hamel and others questioned direct identity. Neither figure is historical evidence for a named Late Antique inventor.

### Fénius Farsaid

**[L/T]** The *Auraicept* also places alphabet creation within the Babel legend. Fénius Farsaid and his scholars select the best elements of the confused languages to create Irish and, in some recensions, Ogham. One commentary says the 25 letters were named after 25 scholars—an anachronistic number reflecting the expanded alphabet rather than the original 20.

This story embeds Irish grammar in universal biblical history and asserts Irish intellectual parity with Hebrew, Greek and Latin.

### Medieval scribes and grammarians

- **Anonymous early grammarians:** assembled material that became the *Auraicept* and *Ogam Tract*.
- **Book of Ballymote scribes, late fourteenth century:** Solamh Ó Droma, Robertus Mac Síthigh and Maghnus Ó Duibhgeannáin are associated with the manuscript’s production.
- **Irish legal and poetic-school scribes:** maintained Ogham as cryptography, technical learning and professional identity.
- **The St Gallen annotator:** anonymously left `LATHEIRT`, a rare personal glimpse of Ogham in everyday scribal life.

### Antiquarians and editors

- **Roderic O’Flaherty (1629–1716/18):** published *Ogygia* in 1685 and helped transmit an all-tree interpretation to later antiquarians.
- **Edward Lhuyd (1660–1709):** travelled in Ireland and Britain, recorded inscriptions and reportedly encountered the Emlagh East stone between 1702 and 1707; published *Archaeologia Britannica* in 1707.
- **Charles Vallancey (1721–1812):** spread Ogham studies but also speculative comparisons characteristic of eighteenth-century Celtic antiquarianism.
- **Samuel Ferguson (1810–1886):** documented monuments and Irish antiquities.
- **Richard Rolt Brash (1817–1876):** produced *The Ogam Inscribed Monuments of the Gaedhil in the British Islands* (posthumously published 1879), with drawings still useful where stones have deteriorated.
- **George Calder (1859–1941):** edited and translated *Auraicept na n-Éces* in 1917.
- **R. A. Stewart Macalister (1870–1950):** compiled *Corpus Inscriptionum Insularum Celticarum*, vol. I in 1945 and vol. II in 1949. His numbering remains standard, although readings and the corpus require revision.
- **Kenneth Jackson (1909–1991):** integrated inscriptions with the historical phonology of Brittonic and Goidelic.
- **Damian McManus:** published *A Guide to Ogam* in 1991 and the Ogham chapter in Daniels and Bright, pp. 340–344, in 1996; his work is the modern standard synthesis.
- **Sabine Ziegler:** catalogued Gaelic Ogham inscriptions and language in 1994.
- **Katherine Forsyth:** transformed study of Pictish and Scottish Ogham through contextual and linguistic analysis.
- **David Stifter:** produced a recent compact specialist survey and contributes to digital-corpus work.
- **Michael Everson:** central to Ogham’s Unicode character names and typographic implementation.

### Robert Graves

**[M]** Poet and novelist **Robert Graves (1895–1985)** published *The White Goddess* in 1948. He rearranged Ogham tree associations into a thirteen-month calendar and treated them as the remnant of a Goddess-centered poetic religion.

Graves presented his work as “poetic myth” and intuitive reconstruction, but its learned apparatus encouraged readers to receive it as recovered history. He drew partly on O’Flaherty and Victorian/early twentieth-century comparative mythology. His system profoundly influenced Wicca, modern Druidry, occultism, card decks and popular Celtic spirituality. [Reception study](https://robertgravesreview.org/pdfs/issue_35.pdf).

### Barry Fell

**[D/M]** Zoologist Barry Fell (1917–1994), especially in *America B.C.* (1976), claimed that petroglyphs in the Americas contained Ogham, including a vowelless variety, and used them to argue for pre-Columbian Celtic visitors.

Specialists objected that:

- authentic Ogham writes vowels;
- his stemlines and letter divisions were selected inconsistently;
- he relied on chalk-enhanced photographs;
- proposed languages and sign systems changed to fit the desired reading;
- secure archaeological context was absent.

The Council for West Virginia Archaeology preserves both the claims and detailed methodological criticism. [Council overview](https://cwva.org/pmwiki.php?n=Ogam.HowScienceWorksAndHowItDoesnt); [Oppenheimer and Wirtz analysis](https://archive.cwva.org/wirtz.html).

## Culture

### Law, status and land

**[A/R]** Monumental inscriptions publicly asserted names, descent and group affiliation. These facts had potential relevance to inheritance, landholding and political legitimacy.

**Caution:** no surviving stone explicitly states a boundary law or land transaction in the manner of a legal charter. “Territorial marker” remains a contextual interpretation, not a translation of every inscription.

**[T]** In later Irish legal manuscripts, Ogham served scribal display and cryptography. Its presence marked learned authority rather than replacing the Latin script in ordinary legal documentation.

### Religion and scripture

The corpus does not support a simple pagan-to-Christian divide.

- Early formulaic stones often lack crosses or Christian titles.
- Some stones were later Christianized with crosses.
- Some inscriptions are clearly Christian, naming priests or using Christian symbols.
- Ogham appears in manuscripts of Christian grammatical learning.
- The *Auraicept* relates Irish letters to Babel and compares Irish learning with the *tres linguae sacrae*—Hebrew, Greek and Latin.

Ogham never became the normal script of an Irish Bible or liturgy. Latin and Insular Latin script filled those roles.

### Monuments

Standing stones are the defining medium. Their edge-writing integrates text with three-dimensional form: the reader follows a physical corner rather than a flat line.

This had consequences:

- carving could proceed with simple tools;
- the edge supplied the stemline;
- weathering could erase whole letter-series;
- stones could be rotated, inverted or reused;
- a text might require walking around the monument.

### Portable objects

Ogham also occurs on:

- bone and antler;
- knife handles;
- gaming pieces and dice;
- brooches;
- small stones;
- manuscript margins.

The relative rarity of portable survivals warns against assuming that stone was its only original medium. Wood is repeatedly mentioned in literature and would preserve poorly. But the hypothetical loss of wooden archives cannot be used to invent their contents.

### Coins, seals and calendars

- **Coins:** no standard Ogham coinage tradition is known.
- **Seals:** no extensive administrative sealing system comparable to Roman or Near Eastern practices.
- **Calendars:** no ancient or medieval Ogham calendar survives.
- **Graves’s tree calendar:** explicitly modern reconstruction.

These absences help define Ogham as a restricted epigraphic and learned system rather than a full bureaucratic script.

### Literature

In medieval tales, Ogham functions as:

- a warrior’s warning;
- an ownership or prohibition notice;
- a grave identification;
- a magical or divinatory device;
- a token requiring interpretation by a learned person.

It therefore carries narrative associations of authority, danger, compressed knowledge and access to the heroic past.

### Acrostics and alphabetic art

The *bríatharogaim* and semantic “ogams” are closer to alphabet poems and learned word games than to ordinary orthography. The Book of Ballymote’s pages are themselves works of visual scholarship: scripts, alphabets and diagrams organize knowledge graphically.

In modern Ireland Ogham appears in:

- public sculpture;
- memorials;
- logos;
- tattoos;
- wedding rings and pendants;
- language-learning material;
- digital art;
- national and diasporic identity.

Modern inscriptions often write English or Modern Irish by one-to-one transliteration. Since Ogham lacks native signs for several modern contrasts and has no conventional mutation notation, such texts are adaptations, not necessarily historical orthography.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Date of invention

#### Early-date position

**[D/R]** James Carney argued that Ogham could have been invented as early as the first century BCE or early Roman period as a cipher based on Latin alphabetic knowledge.

Evidence offered:

- archaic phonological distinctions implied by the inventory;
- the elaborate organization may precede the surviving stones;
- writing on wood could have vanished;
- Roman–Irish contact predates Christianization.

Weaknesses:

- no securely dated BCE inscription;
- no intermediate palaeographic sequence;
- reconstructed sound values can sometimes be explained by learned archaizing.

#### Late Roman/early Christian position

**[R]** McManus and much current scholarship place invention closer to the fourth century CE, in an Irish milieu acquainted with Latin grammar and perhaps Christianity.

Evidence:

- earliest archaeological horizons cluster in the fourth–fifth centuries;
- Silchester and Irish Sea Roman contact;
- compatibility with Late Antique grammatical learning;
- no earlier material corpus.

Weakness:

- invention necessarily predates its earliest surviving use, but the interval is unknowable.

**Finding:** late Roman invention is the economical reconstruction; a substantially earlier origin remains possible but unsupported by direct artefacts.

### 2. Exact place of invention

Possible centers include:

- southwest Ireland;
- southern/eastern Ireland with Roman-British contact;
- Irish communities in western Britain;
- a transmarine learned network.

Southwest concentration favors intensive early use there, not necessarily invention. Silchester shows early mobility but is an isolated eastern British find. No workshop, school or inventor’s archive identifies a birthplace.

### 3. Original purpose

#### Monument-first theory

The script’s strokes are ideal for stone edges, and the earliest large corpus is monumental. It may have been designed specifically for memorial and lineage inscriptions.

#### Wood/cipher-first theory

Medieval narratives consistently associate Ogham with wooden rods, and the system suits tally-like cutting. Stone inscriptions may be the durable residue of a broader wooden practice.

#### Christian intellectual theory

It may have been devised to write Irish while differentiating indigenous literacy from Latin, perhaps by Christians before or during the conversion period.

#### Anti-Roman or secret communication theory

A cipher could permit messages unintelligible to Latin-literate authorities.

**Finding:** memorial, identity and learned display are documented uses. Wood and cryptography are plausible and later textually attested. Anti-Roman secrecy is speculative.

### 4. Authenticity of the manuscript key

**[D]** Because surviving complete alphabet descriptions are centuries later than the earliest stones, scholars once suspected serious corruption.

McManus argued that:

- archaic spellings in the manuscript key cannot be easy late inventions;
- inscriptional distributions confirm many assignments;
- sound changes explain apparent mismatches such as Fern’s V/W-to-F development.

Sims-Williams agreed that real early information survived but emphasized continuing uncertainty over Úath, Gétal and Straif.

**Finding:** the core key is authentic in structure and most values, but individual names and supplementary assignments underwent learned alteration.

### 5. Is it really a tree alphabet?

#### Medieval case

The *Auraicept*, *Ogam Tract* and tree lists explicitly interpret letters through trees. Tree association is therefore authentic medieval Irish tradition.

#### Historical-linguistic case

Most names do not originally mean trees. Medieval commentators transformed words such as “fear,” “field,” “neck,” “redness” and “ingot” into arboreal names or epithets.

**Finding:** “tree alphabet” is accurate for an important medieval interpretive system, misleading as a description of the original twenty letter names, and false if taken to imply a prehistoric tree calendar.

### 6. Graves’s calendar

**[M]** Graves’s thirteen-month arrangement first appears in *The White Goddess* (1948). No ancient stone or medieval manuscript presents the alphabet as thirteen lunar months.

The calendar nevertheless has genuine cultural history:

- Graves spread it through a major literary work;
- occult and Neo-pagan authors adopted it after the 1950s;
- practitioners developed stable rituals, keywords and decks;
- it is now a living modern tradition.

Scholarly rejection concerns its claim to ancient continuity, not the sincerity or contemporary reality of its use.

### 7. Divination

Evidence for a historical relationship:

- medieval tales describe Ogham rods and divination;
- semantic and cryptographic Oghams provide rich associative material;
- learned poets memorized letter lore.

Evidence against a reconstructed ancient casting oracle:

- no excavated set securely identified as a complete Ogham divination kit;
- no medieval text supplies the widely marketed modern spreads;
- standardized upright/reversed meanings resemble modern Tarot and rune practice.

**Finding:** literary divination is traditional; most operational manuals are modern.

### 8. Pictish and Scottish readings

Scottish inscriptions are difficult because the underlying language and sign repertoire vary. Proposed readings include:

- Pictish;
- Gaelic;
- Old Norse;
- personal names from several languages;
- meaningless or miscopied sequences.

A successful reading must explain every visible stroke, direction and linguistic form without freely rotating or suppressing signs. Many published readings remain provisional.

### 9. North American “Ogham”

Barry Fell and successors identify Ogham in West Virginia, Oklahoma and elsewhere.

Evidence cited:

- parallel strokes;
- apparent stemlines;
- proposed Old Irish or related translations;
- astronomical alignments.

Counterevidence:

- scratches and natural cracks allow many segmentations;
- vowels are omitted despite authentic Ogham’s vowel series;
- archaeological contexts do not establish Irish presence;
- translations often begin with a desired English message;
- proposed readings mix chronological stages and unrelated languages.

**Finding:** the claims are modern disputed interpretations, not accepted additions to the Ogham corpus. They should still be documented because of their large popular afterlife.

### 10. Forgery, over-restoration and misreading

Not every error is a deliberate forgery. Common causes include:

- chalking grooves before photography;
- mistaking grain or erosion for strokes;
- reading displaced stones in the wrong orientation;
- expanding damaged formulae too confidently;
- copying antiquarian drawings after further weathering;
- carving modern “Ogham stones” without marking them as replicas.

CIIC 180, Emlagh East, is instructive. Macalister restored material after `CALIACI`; O’Kelly and McManus rejected most of the continuation as nonexistent or natural marks. The stone was found by Lhuyd around 1702–07, and successive editions show how an inscription’s printed text can grow or shrink with autopsy. [TITUS record](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/ogam/h/og180.htm).

### 11. Corpus numbers are not dates

Macalister’s CIIC numbers are catalogue identifiers, not chronological sequence. CIIC also included Latin and runic inscriptions, so “CIIC 507” does not mean there were exactly 507 Ogham texts.

Macalister knew approximately 344 Gaelic Ogham inscriptions; later discoveries increased the total. McManus counted 382 orthodox inscriptions in the material available to him. “About 400” remains a useful approximation, but totals vary according to:

- authenticity criteria;
- inclusion of Pictish material;
- fragments;
- portable objects;
- manuscript or antiquarian inscriptions;
- duplicate records.

## Decipherment and scholarly recovery

Ogham’s history differs fundamentally from scripts that became unreadable.

### Continuous knowledge

**[A]** Ninth-century manuscript use and later grammatical keys demonstrate continuity. Medieval scribes could transliterate Ogham, though some original values had become obscure.

### Bilingual confirmation

**[A/R]** Latin–Ogham stones in Wales and southwest Britain confirmed that Ogham texts chiefly contained personal names and genealogies. They also showed predictable phonological adaptation rather than simple letter substitution.

### Antiquarian collection

From the seventeenth century onward, antiquarians copied stones and compared them with manuscript alphabets. Their work contained both valuable observation and speculative etymology.

### Linguistic decipherment

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Celtic philology turned simple transliteration into historical interpretation:

- recognition of genitive endings;
- identification of *maqi* with later Irish *maic*;
- interpretation of *mocoi/mucoi* as kin affiliation;
- reconstruction of sound changes;
- separation of orthodox from scholastic spelling.

Thus Ogham had no single decipherer. Lhuyd, Brash, Ferguson, Macalister, Jackson, McManus, Sims-Williams, Forsyth and others successively refined a continuously transmitted key.

## Open questions

1. **When was Ogham invented?**  
   The late fourth century is the earliest increasingly firm archaeological horizon, but invention could be earlier.

2. **Where was it invented?**  
   Southwest Ireland, another Irish center, Roman Britain or a cross-channel community remain possible.

3. **Who invented it?**  
   Ogma and Fénius are literary inventors. The historical inventor or group is unknown.

4. **Was the inventor Christian?**  
   Plausible, especially under McManus’s model, but not documented.

5. **What was its first medium?**  
   Stone survives; wood is textually prominent but archaeologically elusive.

6. **Was secrecy primary or secondary?**  
   The design supports cipher-like use, but the earliest surviving texts are public monuments.

7. **What were the original values of Úath, Gétal and Straif?**  
   Each remains partly unresolved because inscriptional occurrences are limited or ambiguous.

8. **How early are the letter names?**  
   Some are linguistically archaic, but the complete named system is first directly preserved in later manuscripts.

9. **Why is the alphabet called Beithe-luis-nin?**  
   No explanation fully reconciles the title with the canonical B-L-F-S-N order.

10. **How should *mocoi/mucoi* be translated?**  
    “Of the tribe,” “of the people,” “of the descendants” and related formulations capture different aspects; social meaning may have changed.

11. **Were the stones graves, boundaries or status monuments?**  
    All are plausible in particular cases. Reuse and loss of original context prevent a universal answer.

12. **What languages underlie Scottish Ogham?**  
    Some sequences may be Pictish, Gaelic, Norse-influenced or multilingual; many remain undeciphered.

13. **How many authentic inscriptions exist?**  
    The corpus continues to change through new finds, rediscoveries, 3-D recording and reassessment of doubtful marks.

14. **How much of *In Lebor Ogaim* is early?**  
    Its contents are layered. Individual sections require linguistic and manuscript analysis rather than one date for the whole tract.

15. **Did Ogham ever support extended prose on perishable materials?**  
    Possible, but no such archive survives. The existing evidence favors short texts.
