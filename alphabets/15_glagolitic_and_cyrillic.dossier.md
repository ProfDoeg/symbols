# Glagolitic and Cyrillic: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier treats Glagolitic and Cyrillic as related but distinct alphabets and distinguishes six kinds of claim:

- **[Artefact/text]**: directly documented by a surviving inscription, manuscript, printed book, decree, or contemporary record.
- **[Reconstruction]**: the prevailing or a significant scholarly inference from palaeography, phonology, textual transmission, archaeology, or historical context.
- **[Tradition]**: a premodern attribution or belief transmitted by communities or texts but not independently demonstrated.
- **[Disputed]**: a claim for which substantial competing interpretations remain.
- **[Legend]**: supernatural or overtly legendary narrative.
- **[Modern invention]**: recent symbolic, occult, nationalist, commercial, or pseudohistorical elaboration not documented in the medieval evidence.

Transliterations are conventional scholarly Old Church Slavonic forms. Exact pronunciation varied by date and region; sound values in the tables are phonemic approximations, not claims about every manuscript or modern language.

# 2. The scripts in detail

## 2.1 Alphabetic structure and the “vowel question”

Both scripts are **true alphabets**: vowels and consonants receive independent letters.

**[Artefact/text]** Old Church Slavonic writing distinguishes a substantial vowel inventory, including ordinary oral vowels, reduced vowels or *yers* (*ъ, ь*), *yat* (*ě*), and nasal vowels represented by the “yuses.” It therefore differs fundamentally from an abjad. There are no *matres lectionis* in the Semitic sense and no optional vocalic pointing comparable to Hebrew or Arabic.

**[Reconstruction]** The deeper conceptual precedent is Greek: the Greek alphabet’s systematic treatment of vowels had long been inherited by Coptic and other Christian manuscript cultures. Constantine’s problem was not whether to write vowels but how to represent Slavonic phonology—including /ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, ts, dz/, palatal consonants, reduced vowels and nasal vowels—without forcing it into the inadequate inventories of contemporary Greek or Latin.

Chernorizets Hrabar’s *On the Letters* explicitly illustrates Slavonic sounds difficult to represent “without contrivance” in Greek and Latin letters. Its surviving manuscripts are late, the oldest being a 1348 copy, but the work itself is generally placed around the end of the ninth or beginning of the tenth century. It says Constantine made 38 letters, “some according to Greek letters and some according to Slavonic speech.” **[Text transmitted in later copies]** [Hrabar overview and textual problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernorizets_Hrabar)

## 2.4 Letter names and acrophony

The Slavonic letter names are mostly ordinary Slavonic words beginning with the relevant sound: *azъ* “I,” *buky* “letters/books,” *vědě* “know,” *glagoli* “speak,” *dobro* “good,” *živěte* “live,” *zemlja* “earth,” *ljudije* “people,” *myslite* “think,” *slovo* “word.”

That is an **acrophonic** system: the initial sound cues the letter. It resembles the mnemonic principle of Semitic *aleph*, *beth* and Greek *alpha*, *beta*, but most Slavonic names are translations or newly selected Slavonic words, not simple phonetic descendants of Phoenician names.

A frequently circulated reading turns the complete sequence into a hidden sermon: “I know the letters; speak, it is good to live…” The opening clusters genuinely permit phrases such as *azъ buky vědě* and *glagoli dobro*, and alphabetic poetry encouraged semantic play. But:

- **[Artefact/text]** alphabetic acrostics exist;
- **[Reconstruction]** mnemonic semantics probably influenced naming;
- **[Modern invention/disputed]** no early source presents the entire canonical list as the polished esoteric message now circulated online. The uninterrupted “secret sentence” is largely retrospective. A useful critique notes that no early text explicitly supplies it as one composition. [Letter-name evidence](https://dev-divinity.interactiveatlas.org/alphabet-letters/vedi)

## 2.5 Numerals and the titlo

### Glagolitic

**[Artefact/text]** The first nine letters represent 1–9, the next series 10–90, and then 100–900. The values follow native Glagolitic alphabetic order more closely than Cyrillic does. A mark, dotting, or titlo-like stroke may distinguish a numeral from a word, though practice varies.

### Cyrillic

**[Artefact/text]** Cyrillic numerals reproduce the Greek alphabetic system:

- units: А̄ 1, В̄ 2, Г̄ 3 … Ѳ̄ 9;
- tens: І̄ 10, К̄ 20 … Ҁ̄ 90;
- hundreds: Р̄ 100 … Ц̄ 900.

U+0483 COMBINING CYRILLIC TITLO marks numeric use: for example, А҃ = 1. Thousands could be marked by a special thousands sign before a letter; larger-number systems became increasingly elaborate.

The titlo also marks abbreviations and *nomina sacra*: sacred names such as “God,” “Lord,” “Jesus,” “Christ” could be contracted with a superscript stroke. Superscript letters sometimes identify omitted internal letters.

Unicode Technical Note 41 gives the actual historic character combinations and warns that Church Slavonic typography cannot be represented adequately by treating every visible superscript as an ordinary accent. [Church Slavonic Typography in Unicode](https://www.unicode.org/notes/tn41/tn41-1.pdf)

## 2.6 Ligatures, diacritics and punctuation

**[Artefact/text]**

- Glagolitic and Cyrillic manuscripts employ ligatures, contractions and superscript letters.
- Early Cyrillic inherited Greek scribal accents and breathing marks in liturgical and learned usage, especially in later standardized Church Slavonic.
- The titlo identifies abbreviations, sacred contractions and numerals.
- Other marks include stress accents, breathings, paerok/yer-related signs, combining superscript letters and punctuation points at varying heights.
- Punctuation is not uniform: points, multiple-dot arrangements, crosses, paragraph signs and ornamental dividers coexist.
- Red initials and headings—hence “rubric” in the literal color sense—structure many manuscripts.
- *Vyaz’* is a compact decorative Cyrillic display style in which letters share strokes and interlock, prominent in titles, inscriptions and ornamental writing.
- Angular Croatian Glagolitic developed very dense ligatures and abbreviations, particularly in liturgical and administrative hands.

No stable modern punctuation system should be projected backward onto the earliest manuscripts.

## 2.7 Capitals, lowercase and hands

The modern opposition between uppercase and lowercase is partly an editorial and typographic normalization.

### Glagolitic

- **Round Glagolitic** characterizes most early canonical manuscripts.
- **Angular or Croatian Glagolitic** emerged through changes in pen movement and regional book practice; its rectangular, compact forms became characteristic of Croatian liturgical writing and print.
- Cursive forms were used for legal, notarial and administrative writing.
- Large initials existed, but a complete bicameral alphabet in the modern typographic sense is later. Unicode provides paired cases because modern text processing requires them and because historical display/text distinctions support the model.

### Cyrillic

- **Ustav**: formal, deliberate book majuscule based strongly on Byzantine Greek uncial/majuscule.
- **Poluustav**: more compact “semi-uncial” book hand, increasingly common in later medieval and early printed books.
- **Skoropis’**: rapid chancery and documentary cursive.
- **Vyaz’**: ornamental ligatured display lettering.
- **Civil type, 1708–1710**: Peter I’s reform recast non-ecclesiastical Russian printing in forms derived from Western roman type and Russian civil cursive.
- Modern Cyrillic lowercase partly consists of reduced capital-like forms, but cursive and italic forms can differ sharply: Russian italic *т* resembles *m*, *д* may resemble Latin *g*, and regional Bulgarian and Serbian forms have their own traditions.

Modern OpenType fonts may use language-sensitive `locl` substitutions for Serbian, Macedonian, Bulgarian and other preferred glyph forms. [Unicode discussion](https://unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-7/)

# 3. Origins

## 3.1 Political and missionary setting

In the ninth century, Great Moravia lay between Frankish-Latin ecclesiastical power and Byzantium. Prince Rastislav sought teachers capable of explaining Christianity in the people’s language.

**[Text transmitted later]** The *Life of Constantine*, chapter 14, reports that Rastislav sent to Emperor Michael III because his people lacked a teacher who could set forth the faith in their own language. Constantine prayed, received or devised letters, and began translating with the opening of John’s Gospel. Its wording presents the alphabet as divinely revealed, but the text is hagiography rather than a workshop memorandum. [Slavonic text and commentary](https://www.moraviamagna.cz/legendy/l_zk14.htm)

**[Historical reconstruction]** Constantine—born in Thessalonica about 826/827, later monastically named Cyril—was an educated Byzantine diplomat, philosopher and linguist. His brother Methodius, born about 815, had administrative and monastic experience. Their background near a heavily Slavic-speaking region made them unusually suitable for the mission.

**[Artefact/text]** The mission to Moravia is securely associated with 863 in the Slavonic lives and Byzantine/Latin historical framework.

The initial translation corpus probably included a Gospel lectionary, liturgical texts and essential ecclesiastical material. It expanded into biblical, canonical and patristic translations.

## 3.2 Did Cyril invent Glagolitic?

The best-supported answer is yes, with qualifications.

Evidence:

1. **[Text]** The *Life of Constantine* attributes Slavic letters to Constantine.
2. **[Text]** Hrabar attributes 38 letters to “Saint Constantine the Philosopher, called Cyril.”
3. **[Reconstruction]** The Glagolitic alphabet’s internally organized numerical order and close fit to ninth-century South Slavic phonology look like deliberate construction, not gradual casual borrowing.
4. **[Reconstruction]** Glagolitic underlies the earliest layer of translated Slavonic texts; some Cyrillic copies preserve errors or features best explained by Glagolitic exemplars.
5. **[Reconstruction]** Constantine of Preslav’s alphabetic acrostic follows Glagolitic order even though transmitted in later Cyrillic copies.

The qualification is that no autograph by Cyril survives, and the first extant Glagolitic manuscripts are roughly a century later. Therefore “Cyril invented Glagolitic around 862/863” is a strong historical reconstruction supported by early textual tradition—not a surviving signed artefact.

## 3.3 Graphic models for Glagolitic

There is no consensus that one pre-existing alphabet supplies all the shapes.

### Greek cursive or minuscule

**[Reconstruction]** Greek was Constantine’s principal learned script and supplied alphabetic order, number-system concepts, spelling culture and probably elements of construction. Individual Glagolitic forms have been derived from rotated, combined or stylized Greek cursive forms.

**Problem:** many resemblances require several transformations, and Glagolitic’s total appearance is deliberately unlike ordinary Greek.

### Hebrew

The common comparisons include:

- Glagolitic *sha* and Hebrew *shin* ש for /ʃ/;
- *tsi* and Hebrew *tsadi* צ for an affricate;
- other proposed formal correspondences.

**[Reconstruction/disputed]** Constantine knew Hebrew well enough for polemical and scholarly work, so borrowing is historically possible. But similarity of one or two appropriately valued signs does not prove that Hebrew was the matrix of the alphabet.

### Samaritan

**[Disputed reconstruction]** Some forms have been compared with Samaritan letters, including proposed models for *buky*. Constantine’s eastern diplomatic and scholarly milieu makes awareness possible, but direct transmission is undocumented and the proposed matches are selective.

### Coptic

Glagolitic *živěte* has often been compared with Coptic janja, a sign used for a non-Greek affricate. Coptic is also relevant as a Christian adaptation of Greek supplemented for local sounds.

**[Reconstruction]** Coptic may have supplied both a specific model and, more importantly, a conceptual precedent. Direct dependence of the entire alphabet is not demonstrated.

### Armenian and Georgian

Various scholars have proposed Armenian or Georgian comparisons. Horace Lunt and others treated Glagolitic as a consciously designed system drawing from the multilingual Christian world, while some authors have proposed Armenian as the closest single visual analogue.

**[Disputed]** No single Armenian or Georgian derivation explains the repertoire, ordering and sound assignment decisively.

### Cross–circle–triangle theory

Georg Tschernochvostoff proposed that Glagolitic letters were systematically constructed from three Christian geometric elements:

- cross: Christ and redemption;
- circle: divine perfection or eternity;
- triangle: Trinity.

**[Modern scholarly hypothesis, disputed]** The theory elegantly explains the script’s visual unity and the cross-like form of *azъ*, but not every sign reduces uniquely to the three elements. It is best understood as a theory of graphic design principles, not an established decipherment of encoded theology.

### Synthetic conclusion

**[Reconstruction]** Constantine probably designed a visibly autonomous Christian alphabet within Greek writing culture, borrowing or recalling individual forms from several scripts he knew. A mixed model better fits the evidence than derivation from one parent alphabet. The theological geometry may have influenced design, but proof letter by letter is unavailable.

## 3.4 Why not simply Greek?

Hrabar supplies the medieval answer: ordinary Greek letters could not unambiguously write words beginning with Slavonic /ʒ, dz, ts, tʃ, ʃ/ and nasal vowels.

A modified Greek system was nevertheless possible—as Cyrillic soon demonstrated. Glagolitic’s graphic independence may therefore also have served:

- liturgical dignity for Slavonic;
- freedom from forcing Slavonic into Greek orthographic conventions;
- political and missionary neutrality between Greek and Latin clerical systems;
- the Christian ideal that a people receiving scripture should possess adequate letters.

These motives are **[reconstructions]**. No contemporary design brief states them.

## 3.5 Rome, Cyril’s death and Methodius’s archbishopric

Cyril and Methodius traveled to Rome, where Pope Hadrian II approved Slavonic books and ordained their followers. Constantine entered monastic life as Cyril and died in Rome in 869.

Methodius returned as archbishop of Sirmium and papal missionary. Conflict with Frankish clergy led to imprisonment and continuing controversy over Slavonic liturgy. Methodius died in 885.

**[Text and historical reconstruction]** After his death, opponents expelled or enslaved many disciples. Clement, Naum, Angelarius and others reached Bulgaria, where Boris I’s court received them. This displacement is the crucial route by which Cyrillo-Methodian literacy became a Bulgarian and eventually pan-Orthodox-Slavic institution.

## 3.6 Was Cyrillic made at Preslav in 893?

The familiar statement—“Clement of Ohrid invented Cyrillic at Preslav under Simeon in 893”—compresses several different propositions.

### What is documented

- **[Artefact/text]** Boris’s Bulgaria received disciples expelled from Moravia after 885.
- **[Historical reconstruction]** Literary centers developed in northeastern Bulgaria and in the Ohrid–Devol region.
- **[Artefact]** The earliest geographically concentrated Cyrillic epigraphy belongs to northeastern Bulgaria.
- **[Artefact]** The Krepcha monastery inscription is explicitly dated 6430 from Creation, corresponding to 921.
- **[Artefact]** A Preslav ceramic inscription is dated 931.
- **[Artefact/text]** Tsar Simeon reigned 893–927 and sponsored an extraordinary translation and literary culture.

### What is reconstructed

**[Prevailing reconstruction]** Cyrillic was systematized in the Pliska–Preslav cultural zone around the late ninth or early tenth century. It adapted Greek uncial/majuscule and added signs—often related to Glagolitic—for Slavonic sounds.

### What is disputed

- No contemporary source says, “At the Council of Preslav in 893, Cyrillic was invented or proclaimed.”
- No securely dated 893 Cyrillic alphabet specimen survives.
- Early works linked to Constantine of Preslav were originally composed with Glagolitic alphabetic structure.
- The precise institution, designer and year are unknown.

Angel Nikolov and later studies criticize the modern ideological narrative of an official 893 decree as unverified. [Savić, “On the issue of the formation of the Cyrillic alphabet”](https://doiserbia.nb.rs/Article.aspx?id=0350-185X2202175S) and [Micheva](https://www.papersofbas.eu/images/papers/papers-1-2018/Micheva.pdf)

### Clement of Ohrid

**[Tradition]** The short *Life of Clement*, often called the Ohrid Legend, was interpreted as saying that Clement devised clearer letters than Cyril’s. This became the principal basis for crediting Clement with Cyrillic.

**[Disputed]** The wording may refer to calligraphic reform, teaching, or improved Glagolitic rather than invention of Cyrillic. Clement worked chiefly in the southwestern Ohrid region, while the earliest Cyrillic concentration is northeastern. His authorship is possible but not demonstrated.

### Constantine of Preslav or Naum

Both have been proposed.

- Constantine’s *Alphabetic Prayer* is culturally central, but its Glagolitic acrostic can count against his authorship of Cyrillic.
- Naum’s association with the northeastern school makes him a possible participant, but there is no direct attribution.

The most cautious conclusion is: **Cyrillic was probably a collective or anonymous Bulgarian scholarly adaptation made in the generation of Cyril and Methodius’s disciples, most plausibly in or around the Pliska–Preslav centers.**

## 3.8 “Decipherment”

Neither script underwent a modern decipherment comparable with Egyptian hieroglyphs or Linear B.

- They wrote a known liturgical language.
- Their descendants remained in use.
- Letter names, bilingual material, alphabet tables and Church Slavonic continuity preserved their identities.

What did require scholarly reconstruction was:

- determining which script was older;
- reconstructing original Glagolitic order;
- separating manuscript recensions;
- recognizing copied Glagolitic errors beneath Cyrillic texts;
- editing palimpsests and fragmented Sinai manuscripts.

Josef Dobrovský initially regarded Glagolitic as relatively late. Jernej Kopitar, Pavel Jozef Šafárik, Franc Miklošič and Vatroslav Jagić helped establish its antiquity and Cyrillo-Methodian significance during nineteenth-century Slavic philology. The *Abecenarium Bulgaricum*, published by Tassin and Toustain in 1750, translated by Adelung and republished by Kopitar in 1836, played a major role in the debate. [Reception history](https://weltderslaven.de/index.php/wds/article/view/65-1-142)

# 4. Spread and change

## 4.1 Great Moravia and Pannonia, 863–885

Glagolitic was used for mission, translation, teaching and liturgy. Constantine and Methodius defended Slavonic worship against the claim that only Hebrew, Greek and Latin were proper sacred languages—the so-called “three-language heresy” in the Slavonic hagiographical presentation.

After Methodius’s death, Latin-aligned clergy prevailed politically. Some disciples were expelled, sold or driven south. Glagolitic largely disappeared from Moravia, although linguistic and liturgical traces survived in later Czech traditions.

## 4.2 Bulgaria, 886–tenth century

Boris I received the exiles and sponsored Slavonic ecclesiastical education.

Two linked centers dominate the later narrative:

- northeastern Pliska/Preslav;
- southwestern Ohrid/Devol, associated especially with Clement and later Naum.

Glagolitic initially carried the translated corpus. Cyrillic then gained ground in the northeast because Greek-trained scribes could read most of it immediately and because Byzantine book culture supplied models for page design and majuscule forms.

By the mid-tenth century, Bulgarian books were increasingly recopied in Cyrillic. Glagolitic did not vanish instantly: mixed alphabets, Glagolitic marginalia and Glagolitic-derived characters reveal a prolonged transition.

## 4.3 Rus’

Christianization under Vladimir in 988 brought South Slavic clergy, books and Church Slavonic literacy to Kievan Rus’.

Cyrillic became the principal script of:

- scripture and liturgy;
- chronicles and saints’ lives;
- princely administration;
- legal texts;
- seals and inscriptions;
- graffiti, including ordinary writing in Novgorod.

Birch-bark letters show that Cyrillic was not confined to luxury Gospel books. It served debts, orders, family communication, jokes, school exercises and petitions.

Glagolitic was known but marginal. East Slavic scribes sometimes used it for cryptography or special signs, demonstrating learned familiarity rather than ordinary use.

## 4.4 Serbia, Bosnia and the Adriatic

Cyrillic entered medieval Serbian church and state culture. Regional documentary forms developed, including the script commonly called *bosančica* or Bosnian/Western Cyrillic. Its classification and national naming are politically contested; palaeographically it belongs to the western South Slavic Cyrillic continuum.

Major reforms include:

- **Saint Sava’s era:** Serbian Church Slavonic and ecclesiastical institutions.
- **Resava school, fourteenth–fifteenth centuries:** learned orthographic correction associated with Constantine of Kostenets.
- **Vuk Stefanović Karadžić, 1787–1864:** vernacular Serbian reform, commonly summarized as “write as you speak and read as it is written.” He removed historical letters no longer needed and established Ј, Љ, Њ, Ђ, Ћ and Џ in the modern repertoire. Jernej Kopitar encouraged and supported his philological work.

## 4.5 Croatia: the long survival of Glagolitic

Croatia is the exceptional region where Glagolitic became a durable local institution.

### Early inscriptions

The Old Church Slavonic Institute lists:

- Plomin inscription, tenth/eleventh century;
- Valun Tablet, eleventh century;
- Krk inscription, eleventh century;
- Baška Tablet, about 1100;
- Jurandvor and other fragments, eleventh–twelfth centuries.

[Institute catalogue](https://stin.hr/en/glagolitic-monuments/)

### Valun Tablet

**[Artefact]** Bilingual and digraphic: Croatian/Slavonic in Glagolitic and Latin in Carolingian script. It records three generations’ names and demonstrates coexistence rather than isolation of script cultures.

### Baška Tablet

- **Date:** approximately 1100.
- **Material:** limestone, about 199 × 99.5 cm.
- **Found:** Church of St Lucy, Jurandvor near Baška, Krk; brought to scholarly attention in 1851 by Petar Dorčić.
- **Repository:** Croatian Academy of Sciences and Arts, Zagreb, since 1934.
- **Content:** records a donation connected with King Zvonimir and contains an early Croatian ethnonym.
- **Script:** transitional between round and angular Glagolitic.
- **Status:** **[Artefact; approximate palaeographic date]**. It is not the oldest Croatian Glagolitic object, despite national shorthand calling it a “birth certificate.” [HAZU record](https://www.info.hazu.hr/en/povijest/bascanska-ploca/)

### Papal permission and the Jerome tradition

The councils of Split opposed or constrained Slavonic clergy in the tenth–eleventh centuries. In 1248 Pope Innocent IV responded to Bishop Philip of Senj and permitted continuation of Slavonic liturgy in Glagolitic books.

The rescript refers to a belief that Saint Jerome devised the letters.

- **[Artefact/text]** the 1248 rescript exists.
- **[Tradition]** Jerome was credited with the script.
- **[Historical reconstruction]** the attribution protected Croatian Glagolitic practice by giving it an unimpeachably Latin saint and Dalmatian pedigree.
- **[False as literal history]** Jerome died in 420, centuries before Slavonic literacy.

The 1248 document is the first known explicit witness to the Jerome attribution. It remained influential into the nineteenth century. [Study of the rescript](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/clanak/259888)

Croatian Glagolitic liturgy continued—with regional contraction—until the twentieth century. Its exceptional permission effectively lasted until vernacular liturgy became general after the Second Vatican Council. [Old Church Slavonic Institute](https://stin.hr/en/glagolitic-liturgy/)

## 4.6 Romania and Moldova

Cyrillic was used for Romanian in Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania through Church Slavonic ecclesiastical and chancery influence. Transitional alphabets mixed Cyrillic and Latin in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The United Principalities adopted Latin officially during nineteenth-century modernization.

Soviet Moldavian Cyrillic, based substantially on Russian Cyrillic, was introduced in the Moldavian ASSR and later the Moldavian SSR. The Republic of Moldova returned to Latin script in 1989–1991. Cyrillic remains official for “Moldovan” in Transnistria.

## 4.7 Printing

### Glagolitic printing

The *Missale Romanum Glagolitice*, dated 22 February 1483, is the first printed Croatian book and the first securely dated Glagolitic incunabulum. Its place of printing is not stated and remains disputed.

Subsequent centers included:

- Kosinj;
- Senj, whose press printed a missal in 1494 and ritual in 1507/1508;
- Rijeka;
- Venice;
- Urach, where Protestant reformers printed South Slavic books in Glagolitic, Cyrillic and Latin scripts;
- Rome.

The Croatian Glagolitic type tradition continued intermittently into the twentieth century. A major Roman Glagolitic missal appeared in 1893. [National and University Library survey](https://www.nsk.hr/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Hrvatska-glagoljica-Croatian-Glagolitic-script.pdf)

### Cyrillic printing

Schweipolt Fiol printed the *Octoechos* and *Book of Hours* in Kraków in 1491; these are the earliest substantial Cyrillic incunabula. The types were cut with the assistance of Rudolf Borsdorf, while merchant Johann Thurzo financed the enterprise.

Printing spread to Cetinje in the 1490s and Târgoviște in 1508–1512. [National Electronic Library overview](https://old.kp.rusneb.ru/item/thematicsection/cyrillic-books-15-19)

Francysk Skaryna printed biblical books in Prague beginning in 1517, using a learned East Slavic recension.

Ivan Fedorov and Petr Mstislavets printed the Moscow *Apostol* of 1564, often called the first precisely dated Russian printed book. Fedorov later worked in Zabludów, Lviv and Ostroh. His 1581 Ostrog Bible was the first complete printed Bible in Church Slavonic.

## 4.8 Peter the Great’s civil type, 1708–1710

**[Artefact/text and surviving proofs]** Peter I personally directed the reform of Russian secular type.

Chronology:

- 1707: the military draftsman Kuhlenbach prepared designs from sketches supplied or approved by Peter.
- Punches and matrices were produced in Moscow and Amsterdam.
- 1708: the first books appeared in the new civil type.
- 18 January 1710: Peter approved a corrected alphabet proof, crossing out unwanted forms.
- Church printing retained traditional poluustav; secular, scientific, administrative and educational printing used *grazhdanskii shrift*.

Changes included:

- reduction of the working inventory;
- removal of many Greek-derived or duplicate letters from ordinary secular type;
- abandonment of most liturgical superscripts;
- adoption of Western-style Arabic numerals and punctuation;
- systematic sentence-initial and proper-name capitalization;
- clearer differentiation of upper- and lowercase;
- letter proportions influenced by Dutch baroque roman types, engraving and Russian civil cursive.

Peter was therefore patron, editor and active design director, not necessarily the sole draughtsman. [Russian Design Museum](https://russiandesignmuseum.ru/objects/grazhdanskiy-shrift-petra-i/) and [detailed type history](https://typejournal.ru/en/articles/Civil-Type)

## 4.9 The Russian spelling reform of 1917–1918

This is often called the “Bolshevik alphabet,” but its preparation preceded the Bolshevik seizure of power.

**[Documented policy history]**

- Russian Academy committees had debated reform for years.
- The Provisional Government’s education authorities approved the essential program in May 1917.
- The People’s Commissariat for Education issued its decree on 23 December 1917 Old Style/5 January 1918 New Style.
- A Council of People’s Commissars decree of October 1918 enforced it in Soviet printing.

The reform:

- replaced Ѣ with Е;
- replaced Ѳ with Ф;
- replaced І with И;
- ended obligatory final Ъ after hard consonants while preserving it as a separator;
- simplified several inflectional and prefix spellings;
- made Ё desirable but not obligatory.

The Soviet state enforced an inherited expert reform and turned it into a visible symbol of the new order. Printing offices were reportedly deprived of old sorts, helping produce transitional apostrophe spellings.

[Text of the December decree](https://base.garant.ru/187916/) and [University of Kansas documentary explanation](https://olrc.ku.edu/russian_orthography)

## 4.10 Soviet alphabet engineering

The Soviet period involved two contrary campaigns.

### Latinization, 1920s–early 1930s

The state promoted alphabets for previously unwritten or Arabic-writing languages. The New Turkic Alphabet/Yañalif was intended to aid literacy, secularization and cross-language standardization.

Motives included:

- *korenizatsiia*, supporting national languages in government and schools;
- easier mass literacy;
- separation from Arabic-script Islamic education;
- internationalist symbolism;
- technological standardization.

A 1929 decree gave union-wide authority to Latinization for peoples using Arabic writing. A commission under Nikolai Yakovlev even examined Latinization of Russian, but Stalin stopped that project in 1930.

### Cyrillicization, late 1930s–1940s

From approximately 1935–1938, policy reversed. Most Latinized Soviet languages were ordered onto Cyrillic alphabets, usually based on Russian plus added letters or digraphs.

**[Scholarly interpretation]** Explanations emphasize state centralization, compulsory Russian, administrative integration and isolation of Soviet Turkic peoples from Turkey and other external communities. Literacy considerations were still invoked, but the political direction had changed. [“Scripts and Politics in the USSR”](https://oaj.fupress.net/index.php/ss/article/view/2396) and [Soviet Latinization study](https://edizionicafoscari.unive.it/media/pdf/books/978-88-6969-667-1/978-88-6969-667-1-ch-01.pdf)

The result was a large Cyrillic ecology: Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Tajik, Mongolian, Tatar, Bashkir, Chuvash, Komi, Udmurt, Mari, Sakha, Buryat, Chechen, Abkhaz and many others acquired distinct additions.

## 4.11 Post-1991 script changes

### Azerbaijan

Azerbaijani adopted a Latin alphabet after independence; official implementation accelerated around 2001. Cyrillic remains readable and appears in older publications and communities, while South Azerbaijani in Iran is normally Arabic-script.

### Turkmenistan

Turkmen adopted a Latin alphabet in the 1990s, after brief and unstable experimental designs.

### Uzbekistan

Uzbek adopted Latin officially in 1993, revised it in 1995, but Cyrillic remains widely used. Repeated proposals to revise the Latin alphabet show that legal adoption and social replacement are different processes.

### Moldova

Romanian/Moldovan returned to Latin officially in 1989; Cyrillic continues in Transnistria.

### Kazakhstan and “2025”

The item “Kazakhstan 2025” requires correction.

- **[Documented announcement]** President Nursultan Nazarbayev proposed beginning transition in 2025.
- Several Latin drafts were promulgated from 2017 onward.
- **[Documented current position]** by the Kazakh government’s November 2025 update, practical transition remained under technical and scholarly preparation; keyboards, spelling and common-Turkic comparisons were still being studied.
- **Finding:** Kazakhstan did not simply replace Cyrillic nationwide in 2025. The deadline evolved into a gradual, unresolved program. Cyrillic remained dominant in ordinary public use. [Kazakhstan e-government, updated 20 November 2025](https://egov.kz/cms/en/articles/Alfavit-kazahskogo-yazyka-na-latinice)

### Mongolia

Mongolia planned to expand traditional Mongolian script to parity with Cyrillic in government work by 2025. This is a dual-script policy, not straightforward abolition of Cyrillic.

# 5. The letters as numbers and signs

## 5.1 Alphabetic numeration in documents

Glagolitic and Cyrillic numbers occur in:

- manuscript foliation;
- chapter and canon numbers;
- dates;
- regnal and creation-era chronologies;
- tables and calendars;
- inscriptions;
- accounts;
- printer’s signatures.

The Byzantine era “from Creation” required conversion rather than direct reading as Anno Domini. Krepcha’s 6430 corresponds to 921/922 depending on month and chronological convention; its October date supports 921.

Peter’s civil reform displaced letter numerals from ordinary scientific and secular typography, but Church Slavonic retained them.

## 5.2 Sacred abbreviation and visual theology

The titlo is both practical and symbolic. In *nomina sacra*, contraction visually sets sacred words apart. This practice derives from Greek Christian manuscripts, where abbreviated forms of God, Lord, Jesus, Christ, Spirit and related terms were marked by a bar.

The visual contrast between red headings, ornamental initials, black text and superscript sacred forms made the alphabet part of liturgical performance.

## 5.3 Amulets and magic

**[Artefact]** Medieval Bulgarian lead amulets survive with prayers, adjurations, angel names, crosses, biblical phrases and protective formulae. Some mix Cyrillic and Glagolitic. A tenth-century lead amulet published from Bulgaria uses both alphabets; another Glagolitic prayer amulet from Varna has been compared with Novgorod parallels. [Mixed-script amulet](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=413671) and [Varna study](https://oaj.fupress.net/index.php/ss/article/view/14493)

Croatian Glagolitic amulets preserve the Sisin/Sisinnios and Saint Michael protective traditions. Their texts belong to wider Greek, Hebrew-Christian, Latin and Slavonic apotropaic networks. [Croatian amulet study](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/clanak/21648)

This is not evidence that Glagolitic itself was invented as a magical alphabet. Sacred or unfamiliar script can acquire apotropaic force after its creation.

## 5.4 Modern esoteric alphabets

Modern “Slavic runes,” “Bukvitsa,” “all-light letters,” letter-by-letter cosmic doctrines and claims that each Cyrillic word encodes a theological sentence are generally:

- **[Modern inventions]** when their inventories include unattested signs or modern graphic hybrids;
- **[Pseudohistorical claims]** when presented as prehistoric Slavic writing;
- sometimes inspired by genuine medieval letter names, numerals, acrostics, titlos and amulets.

The strongest medieval statement is Christian and philological: letters are a divine gift enabling scripture. It does not provide the elaborate neopagan cosmologies now sold as ancient knowledge.

# 6. People

## Constantine-Cyril, c. 826/827–869

**[Text/reconstruction]** Byzantine scholar, missionary and probable designer of Glagolitic. Educated in Constantinople; associated with philosophy, diplomacy, Hebrew study and missions before Moravia. Translated with Methodius and disciples. Died in Rome.

## Methodius, c. 815–885

Mission leader, translator, administrator and archbishop. He did not merely assist a younger brother: after Cyril’s death he sustained the Slavonic ecclesiastical project under intense political pressure.

## Prince Rastislav of Moravia, ruled 846–870

Requested Byzantine teachers according to the *Life of Constantine*. His political goal was greater ecclesiastical autonomy from East Frankish bishops.

## Emperor Michael III, 840–867

Sent the brothers. Byzantine political and missionary patron.

## Patriarch Photios, c. 810–893

Central intellectual and ecclesiastical figure in Constantine’s Constantinopolitan environment, though direct supervision of alphabet design is not documented.

## Pope Hadrian II, pope 867–872

Approved Slavonic books and the brothers’ Roman mission according to the hagiographical and papal context.

## Boris I of Bulgaria, ruled 852–889

Received the expelled disciples and supplied institutional support. His conversion and state-building made Bulgaria the durable center of Slavonic literacy.

## Simeon I, 864/865–927; ruler from 893

Educated in Constantinople; patron of Preslav’s translation and literary culture. **[Disputed]** often called the ruler who officially introduced Cyrillic in 893, though no surviving council act says so.

## Clement of Ohrid, c. 840–916

Disciple, teacher, bishop and prolific figure of the Ohrid school. **[Tradition/disputed]** inventor of Cyrillic. Securely important in transmitting Slavonic literacy; individual authorship of Cyrillic is unproven.

## Naum of Preslav/Ohrid, c. 830–910

Disciple and teacher associated with Bulgaria and Ohrid. Sometimes proposed as Cyrillic’s designer; evidence is circumstantial.

## Constantine of Preslav, late ninth–early tenth century

Author associated with the *Didactic Gospel* and *Alphabetic Prayer*. His acrostic is crucial evidence for original Glagolitic order. Attribution of every work bearing his name is a textual question.

## Chernorizets Hrabar, floruit c. 900

Name means approximately “Brave Black-robed Monk” and may be a pseudonym. *On the Letters* is the earliest sustained Slavonic defense of the alphabet. Its original text is reconstructed from much later copies.

## Deacon Grigory, eleventh century

Named scribe of the Ostromir Gospel, copied in 1056–1057. One of the earliest Slavic scribes whose work, patron and exact dates survive together.

## Saint Jerome, c. 347–420

**[Tradition, historically impossible attribution]** credited from 1248 onward with Glagolitic invention. His actual role was as translator of the Latin Vulgate; his Dalmatian origin made him an effective protector-saint for Croatian Slavonic liturgy.

## Schweipolt Fiol, c. 1460–c. 1525/1526

German-born Kraków printer of the 1491 Cyrillic *Octoechos* and *Book of Hours*. Johann Thurzo financed the press; Rudolf Borsdorf is associated with the type punches.

## Ivan Fedorov, c. 1525–1583

Printer, deacon and publisher active in Moscow, Zabludów, Lviv and Ostroh. His books stabilized influential printed Church Slavonic forms.

## Francysk Skaryna, before 1490–after 1541

Humanist, physician, translator/editor and printer from Polotsk. Printed biblical books in Prague from 1517 and later in Vilnius.

## Peter I, 1672–1725

Patron and hands-on editor of civil Cyrillic. Surviving type proofs bearing his cancellations document unusually direct royal intervention in typography.

## Kuhlenbach, active 1707

Military draftsman who prepared letter drawings for the civil type from Peter’s sketches or instructions. Amsterdam and Moscow punchcutters translated them into metal type.

## Vuk Stefanović Karadžić, 1787–1864

Reformer of Serbian language and alphabet. His phonemic program made Serbian Cyrillic one of the most systematically sound-oriented modern Cyrillic alphabets.

## Jernej Kopitar, 1780–1844

Slovene philologist and supporter of Karadžić; important in comparative Slavic manuscript study and the reassessment of Glagolitic antiquity.

## Josef Dobrovský, 1753–1829

Foundational Slavist. His early view that Glagolitic was a later Croatian development was influential but eventually overturned.

## Pavel Jozef Šafárik, 1795–1861

Helped argue for Glagolitic priority and connect it with the Cyrillo-Methodian mission.

## Vatroslav Jagić, 1838–1923

Major editor and historian of Old Church Slavonic and Glagolitic manuscripts.

## Branko Fučić, 1920–1999

Cataloguer and interpreter of Croatian Glagolitic epigraphy; his *Glagoljski natpisi* remains foundational.

## Vjekoslav Štefanić, 1900–1975

Catalogued major Croatian Glagolitic manuscript collections on Krk and at the Yugoslav/Croatian Academy.

## Michael Everson and Ralph Cleminson

Prepared the final Unicode proposal that led to the Glagolitic block. Their work translated a complex manuscript repertoire into a modern character standard. [Final proposal](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2003/03282r-glagolitic.pdf)

# 7. Culture

## 7.1 Scripture and liturgy

The alphabets were created and institutionalized through Christian translation. Their foundational corpus included:

- Gospel lectionaries;
- Psalters;
- missals and euchologia;
- apostolic readings;
- homilies;
- saints’ lives;
- canon law;
- hymnography.

Old Church Slavonic was not simply a transcription of spoken Moravian. It was a literary language based substantially on South Slavic speech familiar to the Thessalonian mission, then reshaped in Moravian, Bulgarian, Macedonian, Serbian, Croatian and East Slavic transmission.

## 7.2 Law and administration

Cyrillic served:

- princely charters;
- land grants;
- monastic records;
- the *Russkaya Pravda* textual tradition;
- Serbian royal and ecclesiastical charters;
- Bosnian and Ragusan diplomacy;
- Wallachian and Moldavian chancery;
- Muscovite administrative books.

Croatian Glagolitic served statutes, boundary records, confraternity books, legal instruments and notarial records. The Vinodol Law Code tradition and Istrian documents demonstrate that it was not confined to worship.

## 7.3 Monuments, graffiti, seals and coins

Cyrillic and Glagolitic occur on:

- tombstones;
- church walls;
- donor inscriptions;
- ceramic vessels;
- bells;
- icons;
- reliquaries;
- seals;
- coins;
- pilgrim graffiti.

Graffiti from Preslav, Ravna, Novgorod, Kyiv and other centers complicate the elite-book model of literacy. Alphabet exercises and informal names reveal teaching and personal use.

## 7.4 Calendars

Church books include menologia and calendars of saints. Alphabetic numerals recorded dates from Creation, feast cycles, Gospel sections and tables for calculating Easter. The script thus organized sacred time as well as language.

## 7.5 Alphabet poems and acrostics

Constantine of Preslav’s *Alphabetic Prayer* begins successive lines according to alphabetic order. It combines:

- mnemonic sequence;
- Christian prayer;
- poetic demonstration that Slavonic can support learned literature;
- evidence for the alphabet’s early order.

Alphabet acrostics also occur in hymnography. The alphabet was therefore a compositional structure, not merely a code.

## 7.6 National symbolism and art

In modern Croatia, Glagolitic appears on monuments, logos, jewelry, public art and the euro coin design. In Bulgaria and North Macedonia, Cyril and Methodius, Clement and Naum are central to national educational memory. Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Serbia and other states use early Cyrillic imagery to invoke medieval continuity.

These appropriations are historically consequential even when their claims of exclusive ownership exceed the mixed Byzantine, Moravian and Bulgarian evidence.

# 8. Controversies and disputes

## 8.1 Which alphabet came first?

### Cyrillic-first theory

Dobrovský and some early scholars treated Glagolitic as a later, perhaps cryptographic Croatian alteration of Cyrillic.

Evidence once cited:

- Cyrillic’s obvious Greek ancestry seemed older and more natural.
- Glagolitic’s strange forms looked artificially disguised.
- many known manuscripts were relatively late.

### Glagolitic-first consensus

Evidence:

- early textual tradition attributes Constantine’s distinctive letters to him;
- Glagolitic numerical order is independent, while Cyrillic numerals follow Greek;
- Cyrillic transcriptions preserve mistakes explicable from Glagolitic originals;
- the *Alphabetic Prayer* follows Glagolitic order;
- earliest canonical texts include archaic Glagolitic witnesses;
- Cyrillic’s special Slavonic signs often correspond to Glagolitic.

**Present assessment:** Glagolitic priority is the strong scholarly consensus. Cyrillic-first claims survive mainly in outdated scholarship or nationalist/pseudohistorical writing.

## 8.2 Exact date of Glagolitic’s invention

Possible dates range around 855–863.

- 863 is the conventional mission date.
- Some reconstructions place design and preliminary translation in 862.
- A date of 855 occurs through chronological interpretation of Hrabar and later traditions.

**Finding:** no contemporary dated alphabet sheet survives. “Around 862/863, before departure for Moravia” is safer than a precise day or year.

## 8.3 Exact date and creator of Cyrillic

Competing positions:

1. Preslav collective, around 890s—prevailing.
2. Clement of Ohrid—traditional and still widely repeated.
3. Naum or Constantine of Preslav—possible but unsupported directly.
4. Constantine-Cyril created both alphabets—minority theory.
5. Cyrillic arose gradually in Greek–Slavic border writing before formal codification—associated with Horace Lunt and others.

Evidence favors northeastern Bulgarian standardization but does not identify an individual. The “Council of 893 officially adopted Cyrillic” claim is not documented by surviving acts.

## 8.4 The name “Cyrillic”

The script’s name honors Cyril even if he designed Glagolitic. This is not paradoxical if “Cyrillic” originally meant the writing of the Cyrillo-Methodian school rather than an autograph design.

The names *Glagolitic* and *Cyrillic* stabilized later; early witnesses often call both simply Slavonic letters. A recent Italian study argues that deriving *glagolitic* straightforwardly from the fourth letter name *glagol* may be a later para-etymology rather than the original naming event. [Naming study](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/1386257)

## 8.5 “Glagolitic is encrypted Greek”

Some nineteenth-century theories treated it as transformed Greek used to disguise texts.

**Against:** its ordering, number system and special phonological design are too systematic to be mere substitution cipher. Greek influence is real, but “cipher” does not explain the evidence.

## 8.6 Pre-Christian Slavic writing

Hrabar says pagan Slavs used *črъty i rězy*, “strokes and incisions/notches,” for reading/counting or divination.

Interpretations:

- **[Text]** the phrase is genuinely transmitted in *On the Letters*.
- **[Reconstruction]** it may mean tally marks, ownership marks, divination signs or limited mnemonic notation.
- **[Disputed]** it does not demonstrate a full pre-Christian Slavic alphabet.
- **[Modern invention]** elaborate “Slavic rune” alphabets presented without datable inscriptions are not established by Hrabar’s sentence.

No securely contextualized pre-863 Slavonic text in a native alphabet has been found.

## 8.7 The “Rus’ letters” in the Life of Constantine

The Life reports Constantine finding Gospel and Psalter books written in “Rus’ letters” at Cherson.

Interpretations include:

- an anachronistic reference to Russian;
- Syriac letters, through textual corruption;
- Gothic or another Germanic script;
- a later interpolation or scribal misunderstanding.

**[Disputed textual problem]** Since this episode predates conventional East Slavic Christian literacy and the manuscript transmission is late, it cannot securely prove a pre-Cyrillic Russian alphabet.

## 8.8 Jerome as inventor

**Tradition:** Jerome created Glagolitic.

**Evidence for the tradition:** 1248 papal rescript and later Croatian ecclesiastical writing.

**Evidence against literal fact:**

- chronological impossibility;
- no late antique Glagolitic artefacts;
- no mention in Jerome’s works;
- linguistic fit to ninth-century Slavonic;
- early sources credit Constantine.

The Jerome account remains historically important as medieval institutional memory and legal strategy.

## 8.9 Cross–circle–triangle symbolism

The theory is neither pure legend nor demonstrated fact. Repeated geometry is visible, and a Christian designer could intentionally privilege those forms. But assignments of Trinity, eternity and cross to every letter are interpretive. Modern diagrams often tidy irregular medieval shapes to make the system appear more exact than manuscripts warrant.

## 8.10 Competing national claims

Modern narratives variously label the scripts Greek, Macedonian, Bulgarian, Moravian, Russian or Croatian inventions.

A source-sensitive allocation is more precise:

- Constantine and Methodius were Byzantine brothers from Thessalonica.
- Glagolitic was designed for a mission requested by Great Moravia.
- its language base was South Slavic.
- expelled disciples institutionalized the tradition in Bulgaria.
- Cyrillic was most probably systematized in medieval Bulgaria.
- Ohrid, now in North Macedonia, was a major Bulgarian imperial and Slavonic literary center.
- Croatia preserved and transformed Glagolitic longer than any other region.
- East Slavic cultures made Cyrillic one of the world’s major scripts.

Modern national borders do not map cleanly onto ninth-century identities.

## 8.11 Forgery and invented antiquity

### Psalter of Nikola of Rab

A manuscript promoted by Tomko Mrnavić as evidence for very ancient Croatian Glagolitic tradition was eventually judged a forgery or fabricated witness through philological and historical analysis. The episode shows how Jerome-based antiquity could motivate early-modern textual manufacture. [Recent reassessment](https://hrcak.srce.hr/clanak/494836)

### Modern “Veles Book”

The supposed ancient Slavic text on wooden boards uses a Cyrillic-like script and is widely regarded by specialists as a modern forgery. Its language is anachronistic and inconsistent; its physical originals are unavailable for independent examination.

### “Ancient Slavic runes” and expanded Bukvitsa charts

Charts containing 49, 64 or more “primordial” letters often combine:

- genuine early Cyrillic characters;
- Glagolitic signs;
- later scribal variants;
- mirrored or invented forms;
- modern esoteric meanings.

They are useful evidence for modern religious imagination, not for early medieval palaeography.

## 8.12 “Deciphered inscriptions” without context

Short scratched marks on ceramics, stones and idols are highly susceptible to alphabet matching. A credible reading requires:

- secure archaeological provenance;
- dating independent of the desired interpretation;
- repeated sign forms;
- linguistically plausible segmentation;
- comparison with contemporary hands;
- publication of photographs and alternative readings.

Absence of those controls is a substantive negative finding.

## 8.13 Unicode “fights”

Glagolitic entered Unicode 4.1 in 2005 after proposals by Joe Becker and later Michael Everson and Ralph Cleminson. Difficult questions included:

- whether round and angular Glagolitic are separate characters or font styles;
- whether glyph variants deserve code points;
- how to represent numeral marks;
- how to encode superscript letters and combining abbreviations;
- whether ligatures are characters or sequences.

Unicode generally encodes abstract characters, treating round/angular forms as typographic variants unless textual behavior establishes a character distinction.

Church Slavonic Cyrillic posed even harder problems because superscript letters can form ligatures, abbreviation marks interact with base letters, and historic glyphs differ from modern Cyrillic shapes. Extended-A and Extended-B encode many necessary combining letters and historic forms, while fonts and shaping engines must do substantial work. [Unicode Technical Note 41](https://www.unicode.org/notes/tn41/tn41-1.pdf)

# 9. Open questions

1. What exact forms did Constantine’s original alphabet contain before the surviving tenth–eleventh-century normalization?
2. Were *djerv*, the i-series and both yus series all original, or did some arise during early regional adaptation?
3. Which Glagolitic shapes are genuine borrowings from Hebrew, Samaritan, Coptic, Armenian or Greek cursive, and which are accidental resemblance?
4. Did Constantine deliberately construct the entire script from Christian geometry, or only emphasize symbolic forms in selected letters?
5. What was the precise sequence and institutional setting of Cyrillic standardization?
6. Was one identifiable designer responsible for Cyrillic, or did it emerge through cumulative Greek–Slavonic scribal practice?
7. How should the short *Life of Clement* passage about “clearer letters” be interpreted?
8. How early are undated Preslav, Ravna and Murfatlar inscriptions relative to Krepcha?
9. How much of the surviving Old Church Slavonic corpus reproduces Moravian translation language, and how much reflects Bulgarian revision?
10. Can multispectral imaging recover more of the Sinai palimpsests and clarify the earliest letter shapes?
11. How consistently can Unicode separate characters from contextual glyph variants across Glagolitic and Old Cyrillic manuscripts?
12. Will Kazakhstan implement a stable Latin standard, retain long-term digraphia, or continue primarily in Cyrillic?
13. How rapidly will Uzbekistan’s formal Latin policy displace everyday Cyrillic use?
14. How should digital editions preserve the manuscript distinction between base letters, superscripts, abbreviations, ligatures and purely graphical allographs?
15. How many alleged early inscriptions or amulets remain unpublished or lack adequate archaeological documentation?

Absence of Cyril’s autograph, of a contemporary 893 Cyrillic foundation act, and of securely dated pre-Christian Slavonic alphabetic texts remains central—not a gap that later national or occult tradition can fill by assertion.
