# Cuneiform (Sumerian, Akkadian and the Ugaritic alphabet): Research Dossier

## Basic identification

| Field | Sumero-Akkadian cuneiform | Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform |
|---|---|---|
| **Name** | Cuneiform, from Latin *cuneus*, “wedge”; the ancient languages normally called a written document a “tablet,” not “cuneiform” | Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform |
| **Type** | **Logo-syllabic**: logograms/word-signs, syllabograms, determinatives, phonetic complements and numerals | Principally a **consonantal alphabet or abjad**, with 30 letters; three varieties of *ʾaleph* partly indicate a following vowel |
| **Inventory** | Historically variable. Mature scholarly signaries normally distinguish roughly **600–900 signs**, many polyvalent; Unicode encodes more than a thousand diachronically distinguished signs and compounds | **30 letters plus one word divider** |
| **Direction** | Earliest tablets were organized in boxes and columns, probably read downwards with columns progressing right-to-left; by the mid-third millennium the normal orientation was horizontal, **left-to-right, top-to-bottom** | Normally **left-to-right, top-to-bottom** |
| **Boustrophedon** | No established regular cuneiform boustrophedon tradition | None established |
| **Period** | Proto-cuneiform from approximately **3350/3300 BCE**; readable Sumerian increasingly evident in the early third millennium; conventional cuneiform into the first century CE and perhaps later | Approximately the fifteenth/fourteenth century to the destruction of Ugarit, conventionally about **1190–1180 BCE** |
| **Region** | Southern Mesopotamia; subsequently Iraq, Syria, Anatolia, western Iran, Armenia, the Levant and diplomatic centers including Egypt | Principally Ugarit/Ras Shamra and nearby Syrian sites; isolated finds elsewhere in the Levant |
| **Immediate parent** | Late Uruk accounting notations, numerical tablets, seals and related administrative devices. A direct derivation of every sign from clay tokens is disputed | A local alphabetic tradition related in order and consonantal analysis to the early West Semitic alphabet, graphically recast with wedge-and-stylus technology; not a genealogical reduction of the Sumero-Akkadian signary |
| **Principal daughters/adaptations** | Akkadian/Babylonian/Assyrian, Eblaite, Elamite cuneiform, Hurrian, Hittite, Luwian in cuneiform, Urartian and several minor language traditions. Old Persian is a newly designed, strongly simplified wedge script rather than an ordinary linear daughter | The script itself died with Late Bronze Age Ugarit. Its alphabetic order documents a tradition also ancestral or collateral to Phoenician and its descendants; the actual Ugaritic wedge-shaped letterforms did not become the forms of Greek, Latin, Hebrew or Arabic |
| **Present status** | Extinct as a community script; actively used in Assyriology, digital corpora, fonts, teaching, replicas and modern artistic or recreational compositions | Extinct; used in Ugaritic philology, biblical and Northwest Semitic studies, fonts and digital editions |

### Evidentiary labels

The dossier uses the following labels:

- **[A—Artefact]**: physically documented object with archaeological or museum context.
- **[T—Text]**: statement or practice documented in an ancient text.
- **[R—Reconstruction]**: prevailing or substantial scholarly inference.
- **[D—Disputed]**: competing scholarly readings, chronologies or genealogies.
- **[Tradition]**: ancient or later attribution preserved as cultural tradition, not independently demonstrated history.
- **[Legend]**: mythic or supernatural narrative.
- **[M—Modern]**: modern terminology, encoding, revival, popular claim or invention.
- **[Absence]**: a requested feature for which dependable evidence is absent.

“Cuneiform” must not be treated as a single language. Sumerian, Akkadian, Hittite and Elamite are languages; cuneiform is a family of writing practices. Nor does every wedge-written system belong to the same genealogical script. Ugaritic and Old Persian are conspicuous qualifications.

# The script in detail

## 1. Materials and the wedge

**[A]** The characteristic signs were impressed into moist clay with a cut reed stylus. Its triangular end made a wedge-shaped head and tapering stroke. Scribal hands combined horizontal, vertical, diagonal and corner wedges. Tablets were dried, accidentally fired in conflagrations, or deliberately baked; monumental cuneiform was also cut into stone, metal, glazed brick, ivory and rock faces.

**[R]** Proto-cuneiform was initially drawn and impressed rather than constructed entirely from standardized wedges. During the third millennium, curvilinear pictographs became increasingly abstract and rectilinear. This was partly a consequence of the stylus and clay medium, partly conventionalization through repeated scribal teaching.

**[M]** Thomas Hyde used Latin *cuneiformis* in 1700 for wedge-shaped Persepolitan characters, although he did not accept that they were ordinary writing. Engelbert Kaempfer’s 1712 publication helped circulate the designation. “Cuneiform” is therefore an early-modern classificatory name, not an indigenous one. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cuneiform-script/)

## 2. Structural principles

### Logograms

A sign could represent a whole word. Thus 𒀭, conventionally **DINGIR**, could mean Sumerian *diĝir*, “god,” and in Akkadian text stand logographically for *ilu*, “god.” The reader supplied the target language’s word.

Uppercase transliteration normally signals a Sumerian logogram used inside another language: for example, Akkadian texts may write LUGAL for Akkadian *šarru*, “king.”

### Syllabograms

Signs could express syllables, normally:

- V: *a, i, u*;
- CV: *ba, bi, bu*;
- VC: *ab, ib, ub*;
- CVC: *ban, šar, kur*, and so forth.

Akkadian orthography often divided a word into overlapping or sequential syllables. Cuneiform was not a consonantal alphabet: vowels were normally written as constituents of syllable signs.

### Polyvalence and homophony

**[A/T]** One sign commonly had several logographic and phonetic readings; conversely, several signs could share one pronunciation. Modern Assyriology distinguishes homophones with accents or subscripts:

- *u*, *ú* or *u₂*, *ù* or *u₃*;
- *du*, *du₃*, *du₆*, etc.

Unicode’s technical report gives the example of phonetic /u/ written with distinct signs 𒌑 U₂ and 𒌋 U; the former can also mean “grass,” while the latter can represent 10. [Unicode Technical Report 56](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr56/)

### Determinatives

Determinatives classified a following or preceding expression but were ordinarily not pronounced:

- 𒀭 **DINGIR** before a divine name;
- 𒆠 **KI** after a place-name;
- 𒄑 **GIŠ** before wooden objects or trees;
- 𒇽 **LÚ** before persons or occupations;
- 𒁹 **DIŠ** before a masculine personal name in many periods.

This principle made meaning easier to identify while increasing the distance between visible spelling and spoken language.

### Phonetic complements

A syllabogram could follow a logogram to indicate which reading or grammatical ending was intended. This resembles neither alphabetic spelling nor a detachable system of vowel pointing; it is an integral logo-syllabic device.

## 3. Vowels

### Sumero-Akkadian cuneiform

**[A/T]** Vowels are written within syllabograms. Akkadian conventionally distinguishes *a, i, u*, with *e* also represented; vowel length could be indicated inconsistently through plene spelling, such as an additional vowel sign. Consonant quality and vowel length are not always recoverable with certainty.

**[Absence]** There are no *matres lectionis* in the alphabetic sense, no medieval-style pointing, and no Greek-type historical transformation of unused consonant letters into a full independent vowel series.

### Ugaritic

**[A/T]** Ugaritic is predominantly consonantal. Most vowels are unmarked. Three *aleph* signs conventionally transcribed **ʾa, ʾi, ʾu** indicate the glottal stop in relation to a vowel, although their exact orthographic operation varies and remains debated.

**[R]** The common model is /ʾa/, /ʾi/, /ʾu/, not three general-purpose vowel letters. Signs ʾi and ʾu occur late in the alphabetic school order, suggesting that an older omnivalent ʾaleph was supplemented. Hurrian use complicates the description.

Ugaritic vocalization is reconstructed from:

- alphabetic spellings containing the three aleph signs;
- Ugaritic words and names written in ordinary syllabic cuneiform;
- comparative Northwest Semitic, Akkadian and Arabic evidence;
- grammar, poetic parallelism and internal morphology.

The Harvard Museum description properly calls the Ugaritic alphabetic system “essentially unvocalized.” [Harvard Museum of the Ancient Near East](https://hmane.harvard.edu/publications/ugaritic-vocabulary-syllabic-transcription)

## 4. Direction and orientation

**[A/R]** Archaic tablets comprise fields and boxes rather than continuous running lines. Their pictographs appear sideways when displayed in the later conventional orientation. The likely early system was downward through short columns, with columns ordered right-to-left.

**[D]** The chronology and universality of the rotation remain debated. The familiar account says that by the middle of the third millennium tablets and signs had been rotated about 90 degrees, producing horizontal left-to-right lines. Giovanni Picchioni emphasized monuments whose physical orientation preserves vertical writing considerably later, into the second millennium. Dominique Charpin and other scholars caution that “rotation” need not have been a single event. The evidence is laid out in CDLI’s discussion of *pisan dub-ba*. [CDLI, “The Direction of Cuneiform Script”](https://cdli.earth/articles/cdlb/2003-2)

**[Absence]** No sustained alternating-line boustrophedon convention comparable to archaic Greek has been demonstrated.

**[A]** Ugaritic runs left-to-right, an unusual direction among early West Semitic alphabets but consonant with the local cuneiform writing environment.

## 5. Sign joining, ligatures and graphic modification

Cuneiform signs do not join cursively like Arabic letters. Nevertheless:

- compound signs place one sign inside, across or beside another;
- repeated wedges produce “gunû” and related modified forms;
- **tēnû** forms incline or otherwise transform a sign;
- signs may fuse into conventional clusters;
- some “ligatures” became sufficiently lexicalized to be treated as distinct signs.

**[M]** Unicode’s original encoding discussions debated a “dynamic” model—about 300 primitives plus modifiers—from which complex signs could be composed, versus a “static” model encoding roughly a thousand established signs. The static model largely prevailed because it corresponds more reliably to Assyriological transliteration and searching. [Unicode mailing-list record](https://www.unicode.org/mail-arch/unicode-ml/y2004-m01/0208.html)

## 6. Punctuation, spacing and layout

There was no universal punctuation system equivalent to modern commas and periods.

Documented devices include:

- ruled lines and columns;
- blank space;
- dividing lines between sections;
- repeated wedges or special marks;
- the **Glossenkeil**, a wedge or paired wedge used in some traditions to mark a gloss, foreign word or separation;
- colophons naming tablet, series, owner, scribe, source or copying circumstances;
- catch-lines identifying the next tablet in a series;
- in Ugaritic, the explicit word divider **𐎟 U+1039F**.

Words in Akkadian cuneiform are often separated by spaces, but spacing is neither universal nor mechanically dependable.

## 7. Capitals, minuscules and diacritics

**[Absence]** Ancient cuneiform had no capital/minuscule opposition. There is no counterpart to Greek uncial and ninth-century minuscule, Latin Carolingian minuscule, or Arabic Kufic versus naskh.

There were, however, distinct:

- period styles: Ur III, Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian;
- regional schools;
- monumental and tablet hands;
- formal, rapid and pedagogical execution;
- archaizing hands used to evoke antiquity.

Modern romanization supplies distinctions absent from the ancient writing:

- uppercase for logograms;
- lowercase for phonetic readings;
- superscript determinatives;
- subscript homophone numbers;
- acute/grave accents as alternatives to subscripts;
- *š, ṣ, ṭ, ḫ, ĝ, ŋ* and other philological characters;
- brackets for restorations, half-brackets for damaged signs, and *x* for unreadable material.

These are **[M] modern editorial conventions**, not cuneiform diacritics.

1. it was not alphabetically ordered;
2. sign repertoires changed over more than three millennia;
3. one sign could have many names, readings and functions;
4. ancient sign lists followed pedagogical, graphic or lexical principles;

### Letter names, meanings and acrophony

**[A/T]** A fragmentary Ugaritic–Akkadian syllabic school text preserves the opening syllable of a number of letter names: A, BE, GA, ḪA and so forth. It supports continuity with West Semitic names such as *ʾalp*, *bet*, *gaml*, *dalt*.

**[R]** The conventional semantic series—ox, house, throwing-stick/camel, door, and so forth—belongs to the reconstructed history of the early West Semitic alphabet. It is not fully documented by Ugaritic explanatory texts, and the wedge-shaped Ugaritic forms no longer pictorially resemble those objects.

**[R]** Acrophony means that a pictured object named *ʾalp*, “ox,” supplied the initial consonant /ʾ/. The principle plausibly belongs to the alphabet’s pre-Ugaritic linear ancestry or collateral tradition. Ugaritic scribes adopted the consonantal inventory and order but constructed wedge combinations suitable for clay.

**[D]** Individual name meanings are not equally secure. “Gimel = camel,” for example, is traditional but philologically more difficult than *aleph = ox* or *beth = house*. A responsible Ugaritic table therefore separates documented phonetic order from reconstructed pictorial etymology.

# Origins

## 1. Before writing: seals, tokens and numerical tablets

### Neolithic tokens

**[A]** Clay counters of many shapes occur across Southwest Asia long before Uruk writing.

**[R]** Denise Schmandt-Besserat argued that plain and complex tokens represented counted commodities; tokens placed in clay envelopes, with impressions on the envelope, led to impressed numerical notation and ultimately writing.

**[D]** A restricted connection between tokens and some numerical/metrological signs has wide support. The stronger thesis—that the whole cuneiform signary directly evolved from a continuous token system—is contested:

- many proposed token-to-sign matches are not unique;
- complex tokens are not always demonstrably earlier than corresponding written signs;
- pictorial and linguistic signs cannot all be derived from accounting counters;
- administrative practice could have contained several interacting technologies rather than one linear sequence.

The evidence therefore supports an administrative prehistory but not a proved token ancestor for every sign. See Englund’s [proto-cuneiform account-books](https://cdli.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/files-up/publications/englund2004a.pdf) and Schmandt-Besserat’s statement of the model, [“The Evolution of Writing”](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/the-evolution-of-writing/).

## 2. Uruk: the earliest surviving corpus

**[A]** The oldest substantial corpus comes from the Eanna precinct at Uruk/Warka, especially archaeological level IV, conventionally around **3350–3200 BCE**, followed by Uruk III/Jemdet Nasr material. Dating differs slightly among chronological schemes.

**[A]** British Museum tablet **1989,0130.2 / BM 140853**, dated by the museum to 3300–3100 BCE, records barley with a stalk-like commodity sign and three numerical systems. Its archaeological provenance is incomplete because it reached the museum through the antiquities market, but its typology is Late Uruk. [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1989-0130-2)

**[A]** British Museum **1851,0101.217**, dated 3300–3100 BCE, is a gypsum tablet with archaic numerals, reportedly from the White Temple area at Warka and excavated by William Kennett Loftus. [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1851-0101-217)

**[A]** The “Blau Monuments,” including BM **86261 / 1899,0418.3**, were initially suspected of being forgeries. Later excavated parallels established them as authentic early objects, now dated broadly to the Late Uruk/early third millennium. This is a documented example in which a forgery judgment was reversed by contextual evidence. [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1899-0418-3)

### What the earliest tablets say

**[A/R]** Most concern quantities of grain, livestock, labor, land, containers, textiles and institutional personnel. Numerical notation is sophisticated and metrologically contextual: the same-looking quantity cannot always be converted without knowing whether grain, area, time or another commodity is being counted.

**[R]** These tablets represent a threshold between restricted administrative notation and glottographic writing. They contain named commodities, offices and probably personal names but little continuous syntax.

### Was the language Sumerian?

**[D]** Calling the Uruk IV corpus “Sumerian writing” is convenient but not demonstrated.

Robert Englund stresses that no definitive linguistic evidence identifies the language of the earliest proto-cuneiform scribes. Even apparent personal-name sequences have resisted secure Sumerian grammatical analysis. [Englund, “The State of Decipherment of Proto-Cuneiform”](https://cdli.earth/files-up/publications/englund2004c.pdf)

Evidence favoring a Sumerian environment includes later continuity, signs whose later values can be explained through Sumerian, and unmistakable Sumerian in early-third-millennium texts. Evidence against certainty is the earliest corpus’s severe semantic and syntactic restriction.

Accordingly:

- **earliest administrative script at Uruk**: documented;
- **earliest tablets written in fully expressed Sumerian**: not documented;
- **Sumerian participation in the tradition that developed from them**: overwhelmingly probable;
- **the ethnicity or language of every Uruk inventor**: unknown.

## 3. From proto-cuneiform to written Sumerian

During approximately 3200–2600 BCE:

- signs became wedge-based and standardized;
- graphic order increasingly represented linguistic order;
- rebus use allowed a sign’s spoken name to represent the same sound in another word;
- phonetic complements and grammatical writing expanded;
- lexical lists stabilized sign values and trained scribes;
- writing spread to sites including Jemdet Nasr, Ur, Kish, Fara/Šuruppak, Abu Salabikh and Ebla.

**[R]** Rebus writing was decisive: a sign ceased to be only a picture or concept and could record sound. This made it possible to represent otherwise unpicturable grammatical elements and proper names.

## 4. Sumerian traditions of invention

### Enmerkar

**[T/Legend]** The Old Babylonian copies of *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta* describe a message too difficult for a herald to remember. Enmerkar shapes clay and sets the words upon it. The poem treats this as an unprecedented act.

It is invaluable evidence for how second-millennium Mesopotamians imagined writing’s origin, but not a contemporary fourth-millennium report. The historical Enmerkar, if any, cannot be shown to have invented writing. [ETCSL, *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1823.htm)

### Nisaba and Nabû

**[T/Tradition]** Nisaba/Nidaba, originally associated with grain and accounting, is documented as patron of scribes and writing from Early Dynastic through Old Babylonian sources. Later Babylonian and Assyrian culture increasingly placed writing, wisdom and the “Tablet of Destinies” under Nabû.

This divine succession mirrors cultural change but is not an inventor biography. [Oracc, Nidaba](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/nidaba/)

## 5. Adaptation to Akkadian

**[A/R]** Semitic personal names and words occur in third-millennium texts. By the Old Akkadian period, especially under the dynasty of Akkad, scribes were systematically using the inherited Sumerian signary for Akkadian.

The borrowing required:

- selecting syllabic readings useful for Semitic morphology;
- retaining Sumerian logograms;
- using phonetic complements to disambiguate them;
- tolerating mismatches between the signary and Akkadian consonants;
- exploiting CV, VC and CVC spellings.

This was not the invention of a new signary. It was the reinterpretation of a prestigious system by bilingual scribes.

## 6. Ebla

**[A]** At Tell Mardikh/Ebla in Syria, Paolo Matthiae’s Sapienza expedition found the palace archives principally in 1974–76. The climactic 1975 discovery comprised rooms with thousands of tablets and fragments, now represented by more than 17,000 inventory numbers, approximately 5,000 tablets.

**[A/R]** The texts, around the twenty-fourth century BCE, include administration, diplomacy, ritual, literature and bilingual Sumerian–Eblaite lexical material. Giovanni Pettinato served as the expedition’s first epigrapher and identified Eblaite as Semitic; its exact place within East Semitic remains debated.

The discovery is documented by [Sapienza University](https://news.uniroma1.it/node/13228) and the [University of Bologna’s Ebla Chora project](https://www.unibo.it/en/research/projects-and-initiatives/Unibo-Projects-under-7th-Framework-Programme/ideas/ebla-chora).

**[D]** Early popular claims that the tablets confirmed biblical patriarchs, Sodom and Gomorrah, or names from Genesis proved methodologically unstable. Similar-sounding names in a large Semitic corpus do not establish identity. These claims belong to the modern reception of Ebla, not the secure decipherment of its archives.

## 7. Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform

**[A]** Ras Shamra was recognized as ancient Ugarit after excavations begun in 1929 by Claude Schaeffer and Georges Chenet, following the accidental 1928 discovery of a tomb. Alphabetic tablets appeared in the first excavation season.

**[R]** The script was probably devised by scribes already familiar with syllabic Akkadian cuneiform and a West Semitic consonantal alphabet. Its signs are wedge-built and written left-to-right, but the inventory and order belong to alphabetic culture.

**[A]** Ugaritic school tablets preserve the order:

> ʾ b g ḫ d h w z ḥ ṭ y k š l m ḏ n ẓ s ʿ p ṣ q r ṯ ġ t ʾi ʾu s₂

More than a dozen northern-order abecedaries are known. Examples cited in the standard corpus include KTU 5.4, 5.5, 5.6, 5.8, 5.9, 5.12–5.14, 5.16–5.17, 5.19–5.21 and 5.25. Tablet **RS 12.063**, held in Damascus, is a prominent complete example.

**[A]** A South-Semitic-order abecedary found at Ugarit in 1988 demonstrated that Ugaritic scribes also knew an order beginning *h l ḥ m*. [Bordreuil and Pardee’s preliminary publication](https://www.persee.fr/doc/crai_0065-0536_1995_num_139_3_15526)

### “First attested alphabetical order”

**[A]** Ugaritic abecedaries are the earliest presently known securely contextualized tablets to record extended conventional alphabetic sequences in the northern and southern orders.

**[R]** The order must be older than the surviving tablets because it is already conventional and occurs in separate traditions.

**[D]** “Ugarit invented alphabetical order” is not demonstrated. Earlier alphabetic inscriptions from Egypt and Sinai do not preserve a full securely identified order; a Middle Kingdom Egyptian ostracon proposed as a mnemonic for early Semitic order remains debated. Ugarit supplies the earliest unambiguous material witnesses, not necessarily the invention.

## 1. Sumerian and Akkadian coexistence

Akkadian gradually displaced Sumerian as the principal spoken language of southern Mesopotamia, probably during the later third and early second millennia BCE. The exact chronology was regional and is disputed.

Sumerian nevertheless remained:

- a liturgical and learned language;
- a subject of scribal education;
- a source of logograms in Akkadian;
- a language of hymns, royal praise, incantations and lexical scholarship.

The relationship is sometimes compared to Latin in medieval Europe, but the comparison is incomplete: Sumerian logograms were embedded directly in Akkadian orthography, and bilingual lexical lists maintained a sign-by-sign interpretive science.

## 2. Old Babylonian scribal culture

The Sumerian term **é-dub-ba-a**, “tablet house,” supplies modern *edubba*.

**[A/T]** School tablets show progressive exercises:

- clay preparation and wedge practice;
- personal names and simple sign forms;
- syllabaries;
- thematic lexical lists;
- proverbs and model contracts;
- extracts from literary works;
- advanced copying and commentary.

**[D]** Literary descriptions of a formal “tablet house” should not automatically be mapped onto every archaeological school. Much instruction probably occurred in scribes’ houses, temples, palaces and workplaces. [Oracc, Old Babylonian School](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/obmc/OldBabylonianSchool/index.html)

## 3. Law and administration

Cuneiform documented:

- land, labor and ration accounts;
- loans, sales, adoptions, marriages and inheritances;
- tax and tribute;
- court judgments and witness lists;
- international treaties;
- royal decrees;
- law collections.

**[A]** Hammurabi’s famous basalt monument, now Louvre **Sb 8**, uses monumental Old Babylonian Akkadian. It is conventionally called a “code,” but scholars debate whether it functioned as binding statute, exemplary royal justice, learned jurisprudence, propaganda, or some combination.

Seals rolled across tablets or clay envelopes authenticated parties and protected the contents. Witnesses and oath formulae made writing part of a larger oral, social and divine legal procedure.

## 4. Trade routes

**[A]** At Kültepe/Kaneš, Old Assyrian merchants maintained private family and commercial archives during the early second millennium. UNESCO describes the site as the administrative center of a trade network linking Aššur and Anatolia. [UNESCO Kültepe dossier](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5905)

Writing traveled with merchants, officials, diplomatic envoys and specialist scribes rather than through mass literacy.

## 5. Hittite and Hurrian

**[R]** Hittite cuneiform was transmitted through a North Syrian scribal channel ultimately derived from Old Babylonian practice. The Hittites adapted it to an Indo-European language whose phonology it represented imperfectly. Sumerograms and Akkadograms continued to function as learned shorthand inside Hittite texts.

Hurrian was written in several cuneiform conventions, including syllabic cuneiform and, at Ugarit, alphabetic cuneiform. Ugaritic’s ʾi, ʾu and s₂ signs have special Hurrian functions in Unicode’s description.

The Ḫattuša archives preserve treaties, rituals, myths, laws, letters and multilingual lexical work. Their decipherment became possible after Bedřich Hrozný recognized Hittite as Indo-European in 1915, famously interpreting a sentence containing *watar*, “water,” and *eku-/aku-*, “drink.” His result was a language decipherment built on an already substantially readable script.

## 6. Diplomatic Akkadian and the Amarna archive

**[A]** The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna letters, found at Akhetaten in Egypt, show Akkadian cuneiform as the principal language of international diplomacy. Egyptian, Babylonian, Assyrian, Mitannian, Hittite and Levantine courts employed scribes trained in related conventions.

Peripheral Akkadian contains local interference and Canaanite glosses. The letters demonstrate that script spread did not imply Akkadian mother-tongue communities.

## 7. Ugarit’s bilingual scribes

Ugaritic administrative and literary culture employed both:

- ordinary syllabic cuneiform, especially Akkadian for diplomacy and much legal work;
- the local alphabet for Ugaritic literature, letters, ritual and administration.

The alphabet reduced hundreds of learned signs to 30, but it did not simply replace the older system. Each had institutional functions. This coexistence is direct evidence against an inevitable evolutionary ladder from “primitive” logograms to “superior” alphabets.

## 8. Elamite

Elam had earlier Proto-Elamite and Linear Elamite traditions whose exact relationship to Mesopotamian writing and to one another remains disputed. Later Elamite adopted Mesopotamian cuneiform, progressively reducing and regularizing its inventory.

**[A]** The treaty associated with Naram-Sin is generally treated as the earliest known Elamite-language text in cuneiform, though difficult to interpret. Achaemenid administrations continued Elamite in the Persepolis Fortification and Treasury archives. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Elam”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/elam-i/)

## 9. Urartian

In the first millennium BCE, the kingdom of Urartu adapted Neo-Assyrian cuneiform to Urartian. Its monumental use marks both cultural borrowing and rivalry with Assyria. The system was largely royal and monumental rather than a broad everyday script.

## 10. Old Persian

Old Persian cuneiform contains a small set of signs, principally consonant-vowel values, several logograms, numerals and a word divider. It is often called a syllabary or semi-alphabetic syllabary.

**[A/T]** Behistun DB 4.88–92 contains Darius’s statement that this was “my script, which I made.” The inscription’s construction sequence indicates that the Old Persian version was added during the monument’s development, about 520 BCE.

**[R]** The dominant interpretation is that the script was designed under Darius for royal monumental use, inspired by the prestige and appearance of Babylonian or Elamite cuneiform but structurally new.

**[D]** “Darius personally invented it” goes beyond the text: royal first-person statements claim projects performed by a king’s staff. Whether an earlier prototype existed remains open. Alleged pre-Darius Old Persian inscriptions of Ariaramnes and Arsames are widely considered later products or forgeries because their language and sign forms resemble later Achaemenid usage. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Cuneiform Script”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cuneiform-script/)

## 11. Ashurbanipal’s collections

**[A]** Excavations at Kuyunjik/Nineveh by Austen Henry Layard, Hormuzd Rassam and later excavators recovered tens of thousands of fragments conventionally called the Library of Ashurbanipal. They include omen series, lexical lists, medical and ritual texts, epics, prayers, letters and royal inscriptions.

The term “library” covers several palace and temple deposits and manuscripts of different dates; it was not a single modern public library.

**[A]** British Museum **K.3375**, the Flood Tablet, is Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*. Hormuzd Rassam excavated it at Kuyunjik. George Smith recognized and publicly announced its flood narrative in 1872. [British Museum K.3375](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_K-3375)

**[M]** The collection’s museum catalogue history is itself a major scholarly undertaking: Carl Bezold’s 1891 volume alone links to thousands of objects. [British Museum catalogue record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/BIB902)

## 12. Decline

Aramaic alphabetic writing increasingly handled administration during the first millennium BCE. Parchment, leather and papyrus rarely survived Mesopotamian soil, so the archaeological record overrepresents durable clay.

Cuneiform remained prestigious in:

- Babylonian temples;
- astronomy and astrology;
- ritual and omen scholarship;
- legal and economic documentation;
- archaizing royal display.

After the Macedonian conquest, Greek joined Aramaic and local languages. The shrinking temple-based scholarly community maintained cuneiform for centuries.

**[A]** The last currently datable tablet is an astronomical text for **75 CE** from southern Iraq/Babylonia. [Oracc DCCMT](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dccmt/)

**[R]** The script may have survived into the second or even third century CE. That possibility rests on undated or indirectly dated texts and the likelihood of continuing apprenticeship.

**[Absence]** No securely dated post-75 CE cuneiform tablet presently displaces the 75 CE endpoint.

# Decipherment

## 1. Early European observation

**[A/T]** Travelers copied Persepolis inscriptions from the seventeenth century. García de Silva Figueroa identified the ruins as Persepolis in 1620. Pietro della Valle sent copies to Europe and correctly recognized the left-to-right direction.

Carsten Niebuhr’s exceptionally accurate copies, made in 1765 and published later, distinguished three adjacent systems in Achaemenid trilingual inscriptions. This supplied dependable material for decipherment. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Epigraphy”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/epigraphy-i/)

## 2. Grotefend

**[R, later confirmed]** In 1802 Georg Friedrich Grotefend reasoned from repeated royal formulas and known Achaemenid genealogy. He proposed that groups contained “king,” “king of kings,” and the names Darius and Xerxes, with patronymics.

His work was partial and contained wrong values, but its structural identifications were decisive. It was not a miraculous reading of an unknown language from nothing: he exploited repetitive formulae, historical names and the relatively simple Old Persian system.

## 3. Burnouf, Lassen and Rawlinson

Eugène Burnouf and Christian Lassen expanded sign values using Avestan and known Iranian names.

Henry Creswicke Rawlinson copied the Behistun inscription, working on the cliff in the 1830s and 1840s. He published the Old Persian text and translation in the *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1846–47.

**[D]** Popular accounts sometimes cast Rawlinson as the single “Champollion of cuneiform.” The evidence instead shows cumulative work by Niebuhr, Grotefend, Rask, Burnouf, Lassen, Rawlinson, Hincks, Oppert and others.

## 4. Elamite and Babylonian

Once Old Persian proper names and clauses were understood, parallel Elamite and Babylonian versions supplied anchors.

Niels Ludvig Westergaard made major early progress on Elamite. Edward Hincks recognized crucial properties of Babylonian/Assyrian cuneiform:

- the system was syllabic rather than alphabetic;
- signs were polyvalent;
- some were logograms inherited from an earlier language;
- grammatical patterns revealed a Semitic language.

Rawlinson, Hincks and Jules Oppert progressively established the Babylonian reading.

## 5. The 1857 test

**[A/T]** William Henry Fox Talbot sent the Royal Asiatic Society a sealed translation of a newly published Tiglath-pileser I inscription and proposed comparison with translations by Rawlinson, Hincks and Oppert.

On 29 May 1857 a committee compared the four. Rawlinson’s and Hincks’s versions were especially close; Talbot’s and Oppert’s were less complete but recognizably translations of the same text. The Society judged the decipherment valid. The translations were published in 1861. [Original comparative publication](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/art-iv-comparative-translations/515A3FBDA0D8603D82800835246749A2)

**[Qualification]** The test was not perfectly blind by modern experimental standards. The participants shared a developing scholarly literature, some communicated with one another, and Hincks received the text late. It nevertheless demonstrated reproducible reading rather than free fantasy.

## 6. Sumerian

Bilingual sign lists and translations showed that the inherited non-Semitic language behind many logograms was distinct from Akkadian. Oppert promoted the name “Sumerian.” François Lenormant and others defended its linguistic reality.

**[D]** Joseph Halévy argued for decades that “Sumerian” was an artificial priestly cryptography or special mode of writing Semitic Babylonian. The existence of coherent Sumerian grammar across early texts, its productive morphology, bilingual materials and historical distribution eventually defeated this view.

Modern pronunciation remains reconstructed and incomplete. Akkadian syllabic spellings, loanwords and lexical lists transmit much, but some Sumerian consonantal distinctions were poorly represented by Akkadian scribes.

## 7. Ugaritic decipherment

The first texts were published rapidly by Charles Virolleaud.

**[A/T]** Hans Bauer received photographs/transcriptions in April 1930. Using frequency, word division and Semitic linguistic expectations, he assigned most values within days. Édouard Dhorme and Virolleaud independently or concurrently identified additional words and corrected values.

Virolleaud’s 1930 notice reported recognizable Semitic forms such as *mlk*, “king,” *bʿl*, “lord/Baal,” *bn*, “son,” *bt*, “house,” and *ṯlṯ*, “three.” [Virolleaud’s announcement](https://www.persee.fr/doc/crai_0065-0536_1930_num_74_3_75946)

**[D]** Priority among Bauer, Dhorme and Virolleaud became contentious because results circulated quickly and publication dates did not perfectly correspond to moments of discovery. Modern histories properly credit all three while recognizing Bauer’s exceptionally rapid systematic breakthrough.

No bilingual “Rosetta Stone” initiated the decipherment. Word dividers, the small inventory, recurring names and a recognizable Semitic language made the problem tractable. Later abecedaries confirmed the order after much of the script had already been read.

# The signs as numbers and signs

## 1. Mesopotamian numeration

Cuneiform numerical notation was not alphabetic. It developed alongside or before the earliest lexical signs.

### Sexagesimal place value

Babylonian mathematical and astronomical texts used a base-60 place-value notation built principally from unit and ten signs. Context distinguished:

- 1 from 60 or 1/60;
- 10 from 600 or 1/6;
- integral from fractional magnitude.

A place-holder sign developed, but for much of the tradition there was no fully functioning zero as an independently valued number.

The system survives indirectly in modern:

- 60 minutes per hour;
- 60 seconds per minute;
- 360 degrees in a circle.

These modern continuities are historical inheritances mediated through ancient astronomy, Greek and later scientific traditions, not evidence that the modern symbols themselves are cuneiform.

### Metrological systems

Early texts used distinct systems for:

- discrete objects;
- grain capacity;
- area;
- length;
- weight;
- time.

The “numerical value” of a wedge cluster therefore depends on its metrological context. Unicode encodes many numerical signs separately even when their later shape merges with a lexical sign. The original encoding committee explicitly chose this because early number signs were historically distinct. [Unicode cuneiform proposal](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2003/03415-cuneiform.pdf)

## 2. Ugaritic numbers

**[A]** Ugaritic alphabetic texts use separate cuneiform numerical signs.

**[Absence]** There is no evidence that 𐎀–𐎝 formed a numerical alphabet comparable to Greek isopsephy, Hebrew gematria or Arabic *abjad* reckoning.

**[M]** Assigning later *abjad* values—1, 2, 3, 4, etc.—to Ugaritic letters is a modern extrapolation. It may be useful for comparison but is not documented Ugaritic practice.

## 3. Divination

Cuneiform was fundamental to Mesopotamian divinatory scholarship:

- **extispicy** interpreted features of sacrificed animals;
- **celestial omens** correlated astronomical phenomena with political events;
- **terrestrial omens** interpreted unusual births, behavior and accidents;
- **dream texts** classified dreams;
- **physiognomic texts** interpreted bodily features.

Large series such as *Enūma Anu Enlil* organized celestial omens in conditional form: “If X, then Y.”

**[T]** The texts document a systematic learned tradition, but their predictions are not modern empirical science in the narrow sense. They combined observation, analogy, textual authority, wordplay and theological causation. [Oxford Handbook of Cuneiform Culture](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/27992/chapter-abstract/211705103)

## 4. Sign hermeneutics and esoteric readings

Mesopotamian scholars exploited:

- polyvalent sign readings;
- graphic decomposition;
- numerical equivalence;
- homophony;
- alternative spellings;
- etymological and pseudo-etymological association.

A divine or royal name could be “explained” by decomposing its written signs into other values. This is comparable in function—but not genealogically identical—to later gematria.

**[D]** Modern descriptions sometimes label every such interpretation “cryptography” or “mysticism.” Some writings are deliberately esoteric; others are ordinary learned commentary. Genre and context must decide.

## 5. Protective and magical writing

Cuneiform appears on:

- incantation tablets;
- amulets;
- foundation deposits;
- boundary stones;
- figurines buried under buildings;
- ritual tablets;
- curse formulae.

A written divine name or ritual series possessed institutional and religious authority. Damaging or erasing a name could be political and magical violence.

## 6. The Tablet of Destinies

**[T/Legend]** Mesopotamian myths imagine cosmic authority embodied in a tablet determining destinies. In *Anzû*, the bird Anzû steals the Tablet of Destinies; the recovery restores divine order.

This is ancient letter/tablet symbolism, not proof that historical scribes possessed a magical master text.

## 7. Later alphabetic mysticism

**[Absence]** Sefer Yetzirah, Greek isopsephy, the Greek magical papyri, Marcosian letter speculation, Arabic *ʿilm al-ḥurūf*, rune magic, Ogham tree alphabets and Enochian do not belong to cuneiform’s demonstrated transmission history.

Ugaritic shares an ancient consonantal order with later Semitic alphabets, but no continuous evidence carries Ugaritic letter magic into Jewish or Arabic systems. Similarity of order alone cannot establish continuity of esoteric doctrine.

# Culture

## 1. Government and economy

The overwhelming majority of surviving tablets are not epics. They are accounts, receipts, lists, letters, contracts and records of institutions and households.

Clay’s durability produces a paradox: routine discarded paperwork survives better than prestigious writing on perishable materials.

## 2. Royal monuments

Cuneiform proclaimed kingship on:

- stelae;
- statues;
- palace reliefs;
- rock inscriptions;
- foundation cylinders;
- bricks;
- boundary stones;
- weapons and vessels.

Many monumental inscriptions were inaccessible or too high to be read easily. Their audience included gods and posterity as much as passers-by.

Behistun is the supreme example: relief and trilingual inscription jointly construct Darius’s legitimacy after civil war. Its content is political testimony, not a neutral chronicle.

## 3. Scripture and religion

Cuneiform preserved no single closed “Bible.” It transmitted changing corpora:

- temple hymns;
- laments;
- prayers;
- ritual instructions;
- myths;
- incantations;
- divination;
- theological lists.

Ugaritic tablets preserve the Baal Cycle, *Aqhat*, *Kirta*, ritual texts and divine lists. These illuminate Late Bronze Age Northwest Semitic religion but do not provide a direct draft of the Hebrew Bible.

## 4. Literature

Genres include:

- epics and heroic tales;
- hymns;
- laments;
- wisdom literature;
- disputations or debates;
- proverbs;
- humorous school compositions;
- royal praise;
- letters and fictional letters;
- historiographic chronicles.

The *Epic of Gilgamesh* exists in multiple languages and versions; “the text” is a history of composition and copying, not one authorial manuscript.

## 5. Lexical art and learned play

Lexical lists arranged:

- professions;
- animals;
- trees and wooden objects;
- stones;
- cities;
- stars;
- sign forms and readings;
- synonyms;
- bilingual equivalents.

They were dictionaries, curricula, archives of obsolete knowledge and sometimes speculative scholarship. The list tradition extends from Uruk to the last centuries of cuneiform. [DCCLT introduction](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/intro/lexical_intro.html)

## 6. Acrostics and constrained composition

Alphabetic acrostics in the later Hebrew sense are not native to the non-alphabetic Sumero-Akkadian signary.

Mesopotamian authors nevertheless used:

- repeated initial signs;
- sign-name puns;
- concealed acrostic names;
- line-count structures;
- thematic and lexical ordering;
- elaborate colophons.

**[D]** Proposed acrostics require caution because sign polyvalence lets interpreters select among several readings. Secure cases depend on consistent patterning and manuscript evidence.

Ugaritic abecedaries are direct alphabet exercises, but no securely established corpus of long Ugaritic alphabetic acrostic poems is comparable to biblical alphabetic psalms.

## 7. Seals

Cylinder seals combined image and inscription. Rolled on clay, they:

- authenticated documents;
- secured containers and doors;
- identified officials;
- presented owners before gods;
- carried protective imagery.

Seal legends could be alphabetically short yet socially potent.

## 8. Calendars and astronomy

Cuneiform tablets recorded:

- lunar months;
- intercalations;
- eclipse observations;
- planetary phenomena;
- star catalogues;
- mathematical astronomy;
- horoscopes.

Late Babylonian astronomical diaries are among the most continuous observational records from antiquity. Their institutional continuity helps explain why cuneiform survived long after ordinary administration shifted to alphabetic scripts.

## 9. Modern literature, art and popular culture

**[M]** Cuneiform now appears in museum typography, jewelry, tattoos, reconstructed tablets, science fiction and internet culture.

The complaint tablet to Ea-naṣir, British Museum **BM 131236**, became a modern meme about poor-quality copper. Its ancient status as an Old Babylonian complaint is genuine; its persona as “history’s worst customer-service review” is modern comic reframing. A scholarly reception study is available through [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10220181/).

**[M]** Modern people compose English or other languages with cuneiform-looking signs. Unless a historically appropriate language and orthography are used, these are substitutions or neographies, not revived ancient cuneiform literacy.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was cuneiform the world’s first writing?

**Position A:** Uruk proto-cuneiform, about 3350/3300 BCE, is conventionally called the earliest writing.

**Evidence:** a large stratified corpus and visible administrative development.

**Position B:** Egyptian writing may be contemporary or slightly earlier, depending on the chronology of Tomb U-j tags and definitions of full writing.

**Evidence:** late-fourth-millennium Egyptian inscriptions and radiocarbon/archaeological chronologies.

**Finding:** Exact priority is unresolved within dating margins. Mesopotamian and Egyptian systems are structurally different, and direct borrowing has not been demonstrated. “One of the earliest independently developed writing systems” is more defensible than an absolute first-place claim.

## 2. Is proto-cuneiform full writing?

**Position A:** It is writing because it conventionally records institutional information, names, quantities and classifications.

**Position B:** Its weak representation of syntax and speech makes “proto-writing” or “restricted notation” more appropriate.

The disagreement partly concerns definition. It is not a dispute over whether the objects are real or information-bearing.

## 3. Were the inventors Sumerian?

The later script unmistakably writes Sumerian; the earliest Uruk corpus does not securely expose a language. Claims about a pre-Sumerian “Proto-Euphratean” population are reconstructions based largely on place-names and loanwords, not deciphered Uruk prose.

## 4. Token theory

The strongest surviving conclusion is that numerical and administrative technologies contributed materially to writing. The claim that a single token sequence mechanically generated the entire signary is not established.

## 5. Pictograph-to-wedge evolution

Older textbooks sometimes draw clean linear chains from a detailed picture through successive rotations to a late sign. Some chains are plausible, but many combine forms from different cities, centuries and scribal traditions. A chart may be pedagogically useful without documenting a continuous genealogy.

## 6. Directional rotation

The standard single-rotation story explains much of the evidence. Monumental inscriptions preserving vertical orientation show that practice was neither instantaneous nor uniform. Cuneiform’s history is better described as gradual reformatting with regional conservatism.

## 7. Ugaritic’s parentage

### Mesopotamian-reduction hypothesis

Ugaritic was created by simplifying cuneiform because scribes used wedges, clay and left-to-right direction.

### West-Semitic alphabet hypothesis

Its phonemic inventory, order and probable letter names link it to early alphabetic tradition; wedge shapes are a local material translation.

### Synthesis

Most scholars accept the synthesis: **alphabetic structure/order from West Semitic tradition; graphic technique from cuneiform scribal practice**. Individual signs occasionally invite comparison with Mesopotamian signs, but no systematic reduction of hundreds of Sumerian signs yields the 30-letter set.

## 8. Date of Ugaritic invention

Dates range from the fifteenth century to about 1300 BCE depending on archaeological phase, palaeography and historical reconstruction. The abundant surviving corpus is mainly thirteenth century. “Circa 1400 BCE” is a useful approximation, not a dated invention event.

## 9. The 30th sign, s₂

Its phonetic identity and origin remain debated. Transcriptions include *s₂* and *ś*. Proposals associate it with a lateral sibilant, a foreign-language requirement, or a sign related to later West Semitic *samekh* or *shin*. Distribution varies between Ugaritic and Hurrian writing.

Unicode’s choice **UGARITIC LETTER SSU** is a standardized label, not a final phonological verdict.

## 10. The three aleph signs

One model treats ʾa, ʾi and ʾu as glottal-stop-plus-vowel signs. Another emphasizes morphophonemic rules in which the spelling reflects the vowel before or after aleph depending on position and historical change.

The signs do not transform Ugaritic into a fully vowel-writing alphabet.

## 11. First alphabetic order

Ugaritic preserves the earliest secure extended ordered alphabets. Whether the northern order originated at Ugarit, was borrowed from a linear alphabet, or reflects a still older oral mnemonic remains unknown.

The presence of both northern and southern orders at Ugarit shows contact among scribal traditions, not that all later alphabets descend graphically from Ugaritic wedges.

## 12. Old Persian invention

Darius’s inscription claims creation of “my script,” and the construction history of Behistun favors introduction under him. Yet the staff who designed it are unnamed, and its sources—Elamite, Babylonian, Aramaic alphabetic analysis or deliberate mixture—remain debated.

## 13. The “Sumerian problem” in nineteenth-century decipherment

Halévy’s artificial-language theory is historically important because it exposed a real methodological problem: polyvalent logograms might look like a separate language. It ultimately failed because Sumerian morphology and early monolingual texts cannot be reduced to a Semitic cryptographic register.

## 14. Independence of the 1857 test

The four translations were not isolated from all shared knowledge, and the participants had corresponded. The test established convergence within a common research tradition, not four independent rediscoveries. That limitation does not invalidate the decipherment; subsequent bilingual, lexical and archaeological discoveries overwhelmingly confirm it.

## 15. Forgeries

### Blau Monuments

Initially condemned as modern forgeries because nothing comparable was known; later excavated parallels established authenticity. This is a warning against arguments solely from unfamiliarity.

### Achaemenid gold tablets

Gold inscriptions in the names of Ariaramnes and Arsames are widely regarded as inauthentic ancient royal inscriptions or later creations because their language resembles later Old Persian and their historical implications conflict with the script’s Behistun chronology.

### Modern moulded tablets

**[M/A]** Museums acquired modern mould-made and freehand cuneiform tablets, particularly through poorly documented markets. Diagnostic clues include:

- identical surfaces from moulds;
- halves joined before baking;
- impossible combinations of obverse and reverse;
- incorrect orientation;
- copied damage;
- incoherent signs;
- anachronistic palaeography.

A British Museum review describes a group of 74 detected forgeries and the methods used to make them. [British Museum Middle East Newsletter 8](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2024-05/Middle_East_Newsletter_Issue_8.pdf)

### Market provenance

A grammatically coherent text is not automatically authentic. Conversely, an authentic ancient tablet may lack archaeological context because of early collecting or illicit excavation. Material analysis, joins, palaeography, archive reconstruction and provenance must be considered together.

## 16. Sensational readings

Recurring unsupported claims include:

- extraterrestrial technology encoded in Sumerian;
- a planet “Nibiru” predicted to collide with Earth;
- modern electrical or aerospace devices described in tablets;
- exact biblical persons “proved” by every similar Eblaite name;
- Ugaritic letters carrying later gematria values;
- a direct graphic line from Ugaritic wedges to Greek or Latin letters.

These are **[M] modern inventions or disputed popular readings**, not positions supported by standard sign lists, grammars or archaeological editions. Their circulation comes primarily through occult, pseudoarchaeological and internet literature rather than primary tablet publication.

# Digital encoding and the “fights”

## 1. Why encoding is difficult

An alphabet maps a relatively small number of abstract letters. Cuneiform presents:

- thousands of historical glyphs;
- signs that merge or split over time;
- visually similar but semantically distinct signs;
- one abstract sign with radically different Old Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian forms;
- compounds made by containment, crossing, repetition and adjacency;
- disagreement among printed sign lists;
- damaged or unique forms;
- scholarly transliteration that records readings rather than only shapes.

## 2. Static versus dynamic encoding

The early Unicode discussion contrasted:

- **dynamic encoding**: encode primitives and modifiers, then compose signs;
- **static encoding**: encode attested signs and conventional complexes individually.

Dynamic encoding promised extensibility but required complex rendering and could make searching unreliable. Static encoding aligned better with conventional transliteration but risked freezing a contested sign list and consuming many code points.

The adopted repertoire is principally static, supplemented by scholarly sign-list data and fonts.

## 3. Character versus glyph

Unicode encodes characters, not every palaeographic variant. A single code point may display differently in Ur III, Old Babylonian or Neo-Assyrian fonts.

This creates legitimate tension:

- historians want period-accurate form;
- text processing wants stable character identity;
- palaeographers sometimes need distinctions Unicode deliberately treats as glyph variation.

## 4. Exclusion and later additions

The 2003 proposal deliberately excluded systematic proto-cuneiform because its classification was fluid. It also encoded Old Persian and Ugaritic separately because they are distinct systems despite their wedges.

Early Dynastic signs later received their own block. A major 2025 proposal revisited proto-cuneiform; Unicode 18’s draft includes archaic numerals first while leaving most lexical proto-cuneiform for later work. [Proto-cuneiform proposal](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2025/25211-proto-cuneiform.pdf)

## 5. Fonts and input

Fonts must choose a historical style. A Unicode cuneiform string therefore does not by itself guarantee that the displayed wedges resemble the source tablet.

Oracc’s sign list links:

- Unicode code point;
- transliteration;
- sign-list numbers;
- readings;
- period glyph forms.

This ancillary data is essential. Unicode alone is an encoding, not a grammar, dictionary or palaeographic edition.

## 6. Modern corpora

Major infrastructures include:

- CDLI, the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative;
- Oracc, the Open Richly Annotated Cuneiform Corpus;
- ETCSL, the Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature;
- DCCLT, the Digital Corpus of Cuneiform Lexical Texts;
- Hethitologie Portal Mainz;
- Ras Shamra Tablet Inventory;
- museum collection databases.

**[M]** OCR, machine transliteration and language identification are active research areas. Claims of automated “decipherment” should be distinguished from recognition or translation of already deciphered languages. A recent dataset, for example, maps existing scholarly transliterations back to Unicode signs; it does not independently decipher Sumerian.

# Open questions

1. What language or languages underlie the earliest Uruk IV personal names and administrative entries?

2. How many proto-cuneiform signs represent spoken language rather than commodities, offices or metrological categories?

3. Which parts of the token system are direct precursors, and which are parallel accounting technologies?

4. Was Egyptian writing wholly independent of Mesopotamian writing, or did contact stimulate the general idea without transferring signs?

5. When and where did the decisive rotation from vertical columns to horizontal lines occur, and how differently did tablets and monuments behave?

6. Through which Syrian center did cuneiform reach Anatolia and become the basis of Hittite practice?

7. Who designed Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform, and was there an earlier perishable linear Ugaritic alphabet?

8. Do northern- and southern-order Ugaritic abecedaries reflect one curriculum, competing schools, multilingual scribes or imported exercises?

9. What was the precise phonetic value of Ugaritic s₂ and the historical operation of the three aleph signs?

10. Can every claimed Ugaritic letter name be separated into genuinely attested, recoverable from syllabic transcription, and merely reconstructed categories?

11. How much cuneiform literature on waxed boards, wood, leather and papyrus has vanished?

12. Did temple communities continue cuneiform after 75 CE? No securely later dated artefact presently answers the question.

13. How should Unicode distinguish diachronic characters from font-level glyph variants without imposing one modern sign theory?

14. Will full proto-cuneiform encoding improve scholarship, or prematurely solidify readings that remain uncertain?

15. How many tablets remain unexcavated, unpublished, unjoined or unidentified in museum stores?
