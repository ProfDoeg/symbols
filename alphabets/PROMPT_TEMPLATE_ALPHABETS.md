# The alphabet dossier prompt (writing systems and their histories)

Anthony, 2026-09-15: "And different alphabets." One dossier per script. `[NAME]` is the only
substitution point; the per-script brief follows.

---

[NAME]
Please conduct a comprehensive, source-driven research dossier on this writing system, its
history and its use. I want the fullest reconstructable account, from the earliest attestation
to the present, chronologically and in detail. Include, wherever available:

- THE SCRIPT ITSELF: its type (logographic, syllabary, abjad, abugida, alphabet, featural),
  its inventory of signs with their names, order, sound values and numerical values where
  the letters count, given as a table in Unicode where possible; the direction of writing
  (and boustrophedon where it occurred); the vowel question (how vowels are marked or not:
  matres lectionis, pointing, the Greek innovation); ligatures, diacritics, punctuation,
  capitals and minuscules and where they came from; the letter names and their meanings
  (aleph the ox, beth the house) and the acrophonic principle
- ORIGINS: the earliest surviving inscriptions with dates, places, museum numbers and the
  scholars who read them (Serabit el-Khadim and Wadi el-Hol for Proto-Sinaitic, the Ugaritic
  abecedaries, the Ahiram sarcophagus, the Izbet Sartah ostracon, the Dipylon oinochoe and
  the Nestor cup for Greek, the Praeneste fibula and the Lapis Niger for Latin, the Kylver
  stone for the runes, the Ashokan edicts for Brahmi, the Hunmin Jeongeum for Hangul, the
  Nabataean inscriptions for Arabic); the parent script and the borrowing event; the
  decipherment story if there was one (Champollion, Ventris, Rawlinson, Knorozov)
- SPREAD AND CHANGE: the routes, dates and peoples by which the script travelled, split
  into its daughters, was reformed or replaced; the scribal and calligraphic traditions
  (uncial, Carolingian minuscule, Kufic and naskh, the Torah scroll rules, the Greek
  minuscule of the ninth century); printing and the type designers; the digital encoding
  (Unicode blocks and the fights over them)
- THE LETTERS AS NUMBERS AND SIGNS: alphabetic numerals, gematria and isopsephy, the
  letters in magic and divination (the Sefer Yetzirah, the Greek magical papyri, the
  Marcosian letter-mysticism in Irenaeus, the Arabic ilm al-huruf and the abjad, rune
  magic and the rune poems, the Ogham tree alphabet and Graves's White Goddess and its
  critics, Enochian), letter symbolism in religion (alpha and omega, the tetragrammaton,
  the bismillah)
- PEOPLE: inventors, reformers, scribes, calligraphers, grammarians, decipherers, type
  designers, with dates; the legends of invention (Cadmus, Thoth, Palamedes, Odin's
  hanging for the runes, Ogma, Sejong and his scholars, Mesrop Mashtots, Cyril and
  Methodius, Sequoyah)
- CULTURE: the script in law, scripture, monuments, coins, seals, calendars, and in
  literature and art (the alphabet poems, abecedaria, acrostics, concrete poetry)
- CONTROVERSIES: contested origins and datings, the Greek-alphabet date debate (Naveh vs
  the eighth-century consensus), the vowel priority, the runic origin (Latin, Etruscan or
  Greek), Ogham's date and purpose, the Hangul-Phagspa question, forgeries and
  misreadings

Include the good and the bad without judgment, and legend as fully as fact.
For every significant claim distinguish documented artefact or text, scholarly
reconstruction, tradition, disputed claim, legend and modern invention, and say which sources
carry it, when the reading first appears, who spread it, and what evidence exists.
Do not use fact-checking summaries as substitutes for the sources; trace to the texts and
the objects (Daniels and Bright, The World's Writing Systems 1996; Diringer; Naveh, Early
History of the Alphabet; Jeffery, The Local Scripts of Archaic Greece; Sass; Rollston;
Powell; Page for the runes; McManus for Ogham; Salomon for Indic; the Unicode charts; the
museum records).

Structure the dossier as: Basic identification (name, type, inventory, direction, period,
region, parent and daughters); The script in detail (with the table); Origins (dated,
placed); Spread and change; The letters as numbers and signs; People; Culture; Controversies
and disputes; Open questions; Sources (full list of URLs and editions consulted).
Length: as long as the material requires; for Phoenician, Greek, Latin or Arabic a thousand
lines is not too many.
