# The runes (Elder Futhark, Younger Futhark, Anglo-Saxon futhorc): Research Dossier

> **Scope and method.** “Runes” denotes a family of related Germanic alphabets, not one timeless alphabet. This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:
>
> - **[Artefact]** directly documented by an archaeological object or inscription.
> - **[Text]** directly documented by a manuscript or historical text.
> - **[Reconstruction]** a comparative linguistic or historical inference.
> - **[Tradition]** a premodern report whose historical accuracy is uncertain.
> - **[Disputed]** substantially contested in scholarship.
> - **[Legend] / [Modern invention]** mythology, occult reinterpretation, forgery, fiction, or modern design.

## Basic identification

Runes are **full alphabets**, not logograms, syllabaries, abjads, or abugidas. Consonants and vowels are written as independent letters. There are no *matres lectionis* or vowel points and no analogue of the Greek “vowel innovation”: their immediate Mediterranean models already possessed vowel letters. Because rune names were ordinary words beginning with—or otherwise phonologically associated with—the rune’s sound, individual runes could also be used **acrophonically or ideographically**, rather as “I,” “X,” or “&” can function beyond ordinary spelling. That secondary use does not make the system logographic.

“Rune” is cognate with Germanic words meaning “secret,” “mystery,” “counsel,” or whispered communication: Gothic *rūna* occurs in the sense “mystery/counsel,” Old Norse *rún* and Old English *rūn* have overlapping semantic ranges. **[Reconstruction]** The name therefore predates modern romantic identification of every rune with occultism, but it does not prove that the alphabet was invented for magic.

## The script in detail

### Graphic principles

The classic rune is made from a vertical or diagonal **stave** and diagonal **branches**. Horizontal strokes are comparatively uncommon. **[Reconstruction]** This has often been explained as adaptation to carving across wood grain, although the survival corpus is biased toward durable metal, bone, antler, and stone. Shapes vary substantially: there was no centralized academy, typeface, or universal orthography.

A rune may be:

- written normally;
- reversed or mirrored;
- inverted;
- combined with another rune as a **bind-rune**;
- placed on a common stave;
- repeated;
- abbreviated by standing for its rune-name;
- disguised through “cipher runes,” especially branch or tent systems that encode an *ætt* and a rune’s position within it.

Punctuation was optional. Single dots, multiple dots, crosses, vertical strokes, and colon-like separators occur; many texts have no word division. There are no native capitals or minuscules. Changes such as long-branch versus short-twig runes are alphabetic hands or regional graphic traditions, not case distinctions.

#### Qualifications to the table

- **[Reconstruction]** Names beginning with sounds lost or transformed in later languages had to be reconstructed backward. The proposed meanings of *kaunan/kenaz*, *perþō*, and *algiz* are particularly unsettled.
- **[Artefact]** Glyphs are variable. The double-barred ᚻ, multiple forms of ᛊ, open and closed *ingwaz*, and mirrored runes are not separate alphabetic “cases.”
- **[Disputed]** Calling the three groups “Freyr’s ætt,” “Heimdall’s ætt,” and “Týr’s ætt” is familiar in modern literature, but these divine group names are not securely documented for the Elder Futhark period. The division into three groups of eight is ancient; those expanded mythological labels are not equally secure.
- **[Modern invention]** Assigning the values 1–24 to the row and performing “runic gematria” is a modern convention unless a particular inscription independently demonstrates counting. The runes did not operate like Greek alphabetic numerals or Hebrew gematria.

From about the late tenth or eleventh century, **dotted or “stung” runes** disambiguated values: a point could distinguish /g/ from /k/, /e/ from /i/, and /d/ from /t/. Medieval Scandinavian rows eventually restored enough distinctions to approximate the Latin alphabet and were sometimes arranged in ABC order.

### Anglo-Saxon and Frisian futhorc

Anglo-Frisian sound changes, particularly the splitting of Proto-Germanic *a*, encouraged additions. The fourth rune changed from *ansuz* /a/ to *ōs* /o/, while new runes represented /a/, /æ/, and later additional sounds.

### Orthographic habits

Runic orthography normally records sounds more directly than modern historical spelling, but it is not uniformly phonemic:

- vowel length generally lacks a distinct mark;
- geminate consonants are often written once;
- nasal consonants can be omitted before homorganic stops;
- Younger Futhark systematically merges voiced/voiceless pairs;
- word division is inconsistent;
- spelling varies by region, date, carver, and available space;
- some inscriptions transliterate Latin words or shift between Latin and runic scripts.

The Franks Casket is a striking example: its Old English and Latin captions employ ordinary and cryptic runes, Roman letters, and several writing directions. The British Museum records it as **1867,0120.1** and dates its language and art to early eighth-century Northumbria or northern Mercia ([British Museum collection record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1867-0120-1)).

## Origins: dated objects, places, and readings

### Before the secure runic corpus

#### Negau helmet B

**[Artefact; interpretation partly disputed]** A bronze helmet found at Ženjak/Negau, now Negova in Slovenia, bears a North Italic inscription commonly transliterated *harigasti teiva*. The object and lettering belong before the general spread of Roman script in the area; dates around the second or first century BCE are proposed. *Harigastiz* looks Germanic, making the inscription evidence that a Germanic-named person could participate in North Italic literacy before runes.

It is not itself an Elder Futhark inscription. Its relevance is contextual: it supplies a plausible contact zone for a North Italic borrowing theory. The second word and the precise dating remain debated.

#### Meldorf fibula

**[Artefact; disputed classification]** A fibula excavated at Meldorf, Schleswig-Holstein, in 1979 carries four punched signs and is typologically dated to the first half of the first century CE. Readings have included runic *hiwi*, *iþih*, or related sequences; Latin *IDIN* or *NIDI*; and non-writing decoration. Because orientation and alphabet are uncertain, it cannot safely displace Vimose as the earliest generally accepted runic inscription. Klaus Düwel’s 1981 study and Bernard Mees’s 1997 reinterpretation are milestones in the dispute; the alternatives are summarized in the [Meldorf object dossier](https://norserunes.com/inscriptions/meldorf-fibula/).

#### Tacitus, *Germania* 10

**[Text; disputed relevance]** Around 98 CE Tacitus described Germanic divination by cutting a fruit-bearing branch into slips, marking them with *notae*, scattering them on a white cloth, and selecting three. *Notae* means signs or marks, not specifically alphabetic runes. Tacitus did not say *litterae*, provide shapes, or name the futhark.

The passage documents a Roman report of Germanic marked-lot divination. Interpreting those marks as runes is possible only if one assumes runes already existed; it is not independent proof of a runic divination system.

### The earliest secure inscriptions

#### Vimose comb

**[Artefact]** A bone/antler comb from the Vimose weapon-deposit site on Funen, Denmark, carries:

> ᚺᚨᚱᛃᚨ — *harja*

Usually interpreted as a masculine personal name or a word connected with “warrior/army.” Archaeological dating is approximately **140–160 CE**. The RuneS database lists the object, reading, context, and date; Denmark’s National Museum displays it in Copenhagen and identifies *HARJA* as its inscription ([RuneS entry](https://www.runesdb.de/en/find/20); [National Museum](https://natmus.dk/museer-og-slottenationalmuseet/undervisning-paa-nationalmuseet/undervisningsmaterialer/grundskolen/danmarks-oldtid/arkaeologi/runer/)).

It is frequently called the oldest securely datable runic object. “Vimose comb proves the complete Elder Futhark existed in 160” goes too far: the object has only five signs.

#### Øvre Stabu spearhead and other early weapon finds

**[Artefact]** A spearhead from Øvre Stabu in Norway, commonly dated near the later second century, reads *raunijaR* or a closely related form, often interpreted “tester.” Other early inscriptions from Vimose, Illerup, Thorsberg, and related weapon-deposit contexts show that runic literacy existed across military and prestige networks.

**[Reconstruction]** Roman-frontier service, returning warriors, gift exchange, and elite military networks are plausible channels by which knowledge of Mediterranean writing reached Germanic speakers. No surviving text describes the borrowing lesson.

#### Kylver stone

**[Artefact]** The Kylver stone was found in 1903 closing a grave at Kylver farm, Stånga parish, Gotland. It is catalogued **G 88** and is held by Sweden’s Historical Museum in Stockholm. Its archaeological context is usually dated around **400 CE**. On its inward-facing surface is the earliest surviving substantially complete ordered 24-rune row:

> ᚠᚢᚦᚨᚱᚲᚷᚹ ᚺᚾᛁᛃᛇᛈᛉᛊ ᛏᛒᛖᛗᛚᛜᛞᛟ

followed by a tree-like sign and the palindrome-like sequence commonly read *sueus*. Some characters and their segmentation have been discussed, but the futhark order is unmistakable.

Kylver is therefore:

- not the first rune inscription;
- not the first known use of every individual sign;
- but the earliest surviving complete ordered Elder Futhark row and the principal direct witness for its canonical order.

### No “decipherment” event

Runes did not undergo a single modern decipherment comparable to Egyptian hieroglyphs or Linear B. Younger and medieval runes remained known locally; runes appeared in Latin-script manuscripts; rune poems preserved names; antiquarians collected inscriptions. The older language and some signs nevertheless became progressively difficult to understand.

Important stages include:

- **Johannes Bureus** (1568–1652), Swedish antiquarian, surveyed runestones and developed a mystical Gothicist interpretation.
- **Ole Worm** (1588–1654), Danish physician and antiquarian, published *Danicorum Monumentorum libri sex* (1643) and preserved the Norwegian Rune Poem through an earlier copy.
- **Jón Ólafsson of Grunnavík** (1705–1779) and other Icelandic scholars copied and explained manuscript rune lore.
- **Wilhelm Grimm** (1786–1859) systematized comparative discussion in *Über deutsche Runen* (1821).
- **Ludvig F. A. Wimmer** (1839–1920) developed historical accounts of the rune rows.
- **Sophus Bugge** (1833–1907) made major philological advances in older inscriptions. Calling 1865 a single “decipherment of Elder Futhark” is a misleading popular shorthand: readings accumulated through comparative Germanic philology and expanding archaeological evidence.

## The parent-script debate

No ancient source names the inventor, place, date, or parent. The borrowing must therefore be reconstructed from shape, sound, chronology, and contact.

### Latin hypothesis

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Runes were created during intense contact with Roman literacy in the first or early second century CE.

Evidence:

- obvious formal similarities: ᚠ/F, ᚱ/R, ᚺ/H, ᛁ/I, ᛏ/T, ᛒ/B;
- Roman military, commercial, and diplomatic contact along the Rhine and Danube;
- Latin was the dominant literate system near Germanic communities at the date of the secure inscriptions;
- several runes can be interpreted as Roman capital forms stripped of curves and horizontals.

Difficulties:

- several rune forms and sound assignments do not derive neatly from contemporary monumental Latin;
- the highly non-alphabetic *futhark* order is unexplained;
- characters for Germanic sounds absent from Latin required innovation or borrowing elsewhere.

Recent work continues to defend a solely Latin model; the 2023–24 *Futhark* article “De äldre runornas bildning” explicitly develops that assumption ([journal record](https://journals.uu.se/futhark/article/view/1074)).

### North Italic, Etruscan, Venetic, or Raetic hypotheses

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Germanic speakers adapted one or more Alpine/North Italic alphabets before or during Roman expansion.

Evidence:

- closer formal parallels for some difficult runes;
- North Italic scripts frequently used angular carving;
- the Negau helmet demonstrates Germanic linguistic contact with such literacy;
- Raetic and Venetic areas offer plausible routes through the Alps.

Difficulties:

- the relevant alphabets are internally diverse;
- chronological gaps separate much North Italic writing from the secure second-century runic corpus;
- no single North Italic row explains every shape, value, and the futhark order;
- a theory can become “eclectic,” choosing a different parent whenever convenient.

Carl Marstrander’s 1928 work was formative for North Italic arguments. Corinna Salomon’s modern reassessment stresses that the epigraphic evidence must be compared at the level of actual regional corpora, not a synthetic “North Etruscan alphabet” ([Salomon, *Raetic and Runes*](https://utheses.univie.ac.at/detail/48069)).

### Greek hypothesis

**[Historical scholarly hypothesis; now generally disfavored in its classic form]** Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century theories connected rune invention with Goths on the Black Sea and Greek writing.

Its central chronological problem is that secure Vimose material predates sustained Gothic contact with Greek culture in the third century. A much earlier Greek or Greek-derived intermediary cannot be logically excluded, since Old Italic and Latin are themselves Greek-derived, but “the Goths borrowed runes directly from Greek” does not fit the current chronology.

### Punic and other proposals

**[Disputed/minority reconstruction]** Theo Vennemann and others have proposed a Punic model, arguing that the principal Latin, Greek, and Etruscan theories leave structural questions unanswered. The proposal depends on otherwise poorly documented direct transmission between Punic writers and early Germanic speakers and has not replaced the Latin/North Italic debate. The argument should be read as a minority reconstruction, not an established parentage ([published chapter text](https://www.asau.ru/files/pdf/2294308.pdf)).

### Who invented the row?

**Absence of evidence:** no historically named “Germanic Cadmus” survives. The systematic order, the careful provision for Germanic phonology, and the rapid appearance of a recognizable tradition make deliberate adaptation by one person or a small literate circle plausible. That remains inference. Dates proposed range from the first century BCE to the early second century CE; only the later limit supplied by secure objects is firm.

## Spread and change

### c. 150–400: portable objects and elite networks

The first inscriptions are overwhelmingly short: personal names, ownership or maker formulae, weapon words, and opaque sequences on combs, spearheads, shield fittings, brooches, belt equipment, and amulets.

This distribution suggests limited, specialized literacy rather than mass public reading. Yet “secret priestly alphabet” is not proved. Mundane labels and names coexist with probable ritual deposits and formulaic texts.

### c. 400–700: migration-period expansion

Runes spread through Scandinavia and continental Germanic-speaking regions. Characteristic media include:

- gold bracteates modeled in part on Roman medallions;
- weapons and scabbard fittings;
- brooches and jewelry;
- memorial stones;
- amulets;
- complete or partial rune-rows.

Formulae such as *alu*, *laþu*, *laukaz*, *gibu auja*, and the title *erilaz* have generated extensive debate. Some occur in contexts suggesting ritual or performative force; some remain linguistically opaque. “Untranslated therefore magical” is not a valid inference, but neither is it sound to deny magic where intelligible charm language, repetition, or ritual deposition occurs.

### Anglo-Frisian development

By approximately the fifth century the Elder *a* system no longer suited Anglo-Frisian vowels. New *ac* and *æsc* runes were added, *ansuz* became *os*, and the row expanded.

Important objects include:

- **The Undley bracteate**, with an early Anglo-Frisian inscription and enigmatic *gægogæ* sequence.
- **The Franks/Auzon Casket**, early eighth century, combining classical, biblical, Germanic, and Christian scenes with Old English and Latin inscriptions. Museum no. **British Museum 1867,0120.1**.
- **The Ruthwell Cross**, monumental Christian sculpture carrying runic lines related to *The Dream of the Rood*. Its present form and dating have been repeatedly reassessed; a broad eighth-century attribution is common, while restoration history complicates the monument.
- **The Thames scramasax**, British Museum **1857,0623.1**, whose full futhorc and personal name *Beagnoth* are inlaid in the blade. The museum dates English runic use generally from the fifth to eleventh centuries and notes that this particular row may have been copied imperfectly from a manuscript model ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1857-0623-1)).
- coins of kings and churchmen, including names or titles in runes;
- rings and reliquaries carrying Christian formulae.

Christianization did not instantly abolish runes. The futhorc was used for crosses, sacred poetry, personal names, and Latin words. Latin script ultimately displaced it because church, documentary, and book institutions were Latin-literate, not because runes were intrinsically pagan.

### The transition to Younger Futhark

Between roughly 650 and 800, the Scandinavian row changed from 24 to 16 signs. Transitional inscriptions show that this was not one royal reform issued on a known date. Several Elder signs disappeared or merged while sound changes altered the language.

Why reduce the inventory just when the vowel system was becoming more complex?

**[Open scholarly problem]** Proposed explanations include:

- economical carving and reliance on contextual reading;
- a regularization already underway in spelling;
- sociolinguistic signaling of a new Scandinavian norm;
- deliberate preservation of a 16-sign traditional system;
- gradual merger and redistribution rather than a single reform.

The Rök stone, around the early ninth century, illustrates the intellectual complexity of this tradition: long inscription, multiple cipher techniques, allusive poetry, and contested interpretation.

### Viking-Age expansion

Younger Futhark traveled with Scandinavian migration, trade, raiding, colonization, and royal/state formation:

- west to the British Isles, Ireland, Isle of Man, Faroe Islands, Iceland, and Greenland;
- south through Denmark and northern Germany;
- east across the Baltic and along river routes into Rus’ and Byzantium.

Runestones marked inheritance, bridges, roads, conversion, deaths abroad, retinues, and family relationships. They were public legal and memorial acts as much as grave markers. Many were erected by women. Christian crosses and prayers appear beside traditional serpentine ornament.

### Medieval runes

From c. 1050 onward Scandinavian runes coexisted extensively with Latin script. Dots restored distinctions:

- ᚴ /k/ → dotted form for /g/;
- ᛁ /i/ → dotted form for /e/;
- ᛏ /t/ → dotted form for /d/.

Additional vowel and consonant forms eventually made a near one-to-one Latin correspondence possible. Medieval runes appear on:

- merchant sticks from Bergen;
- ownership tags;
- prayers in Latin and vernacular languages;
- love messages and sexual boasts;
- church graffiti;
- legal notices;
- wax tablets, wood, bone, and stone;
- calendar staffs.

The hundreds of Bryggen inscriptions from Bergen decisively refuted any simple picture of runes as only monumental, pagan, or magical.

### Dalecarlian runes

**[Artefact]** In Älvdalen and surrounding areas of Dalarna, Sweden, a mixed runic–Latin local script is attested from the sixteenth century. Its letterforms changed under continuing Latin influence. It was used for names, ownership, farm and herding records, buildings, and local memorial inscriptions.

Sweden’s National Heritage Board register lists more than 350 known inscriptions and identifies **D Rv317**, dated 1900 in a shelter at Gryvleån, as the youngest certainly genuine traditional example ([Riksarkivet/Runverket summary](https://riksarkivet.x-ref.se/lang/en-GB/Webpage?id=193)).

**[Disputed]** Claims of completely unbroken vernacular use later in the twentieth century are harder to prove because revival learned from books can resemble inherited practice. The safest finding is continuous local development into the modern era, with a securely genuine inscription in 1900—not a demonstrable mass living script throughout the twentieth century.

### Printing, typography, and digital encoding

Runes entered antiquarian print through specially cut sorts, woodcuts, engravings, and facsimiles. Bureus and Worm helped stabilize early-modern visual conventions. George Hickes’s *Thesaurus* of 1705 is indispensable because its printed reproduction of the Old English Rune Poem predates the destruction of the sole manuscript witness in the Cotton fire of 1731.

Unicode encodes runes by abstract character identity rather than every glyph variation. The **Runic block U+16A0–U+16FF** was introduced with Unicode 3.0 in 1999 and later expanded. Its formation involved national bodies, Michael Everson, the ISO Runes Project, and debates over:

- whether historically related shapes should be unified;
- how to order characters;
- which rune-name tradition should control the English names;
- whether graphic variants were characters or glyphs;
- additions for Tolkienian or modern symbols;
- whether existing encoding adequately serves runological databases.

The documented proposal history begins with Rick McGowan’s 1992 “Northern Runes,” continues through Everson’s ordering and naming proposals and the 1997 ISO Runes work, and includes later additions proposed by Everson and Andrew West. Sustainability and scholarly-encoding problems were again raised in Unicode document L2/24-129 in 2024 ([proposal history](https://writingsystems.info/scrlang/unicode/runr-unicode/); [current chart](https://www.unicode.org/charts/PDF/U16A0.pdf)).

Fonts cannot display all epigraphic variation through ordinary Unicode text. Scholarly editions therefore use photographs, drawings, variant databases, transliteration conventions, and sometimes private-use glyphs.

## Rune names and the three rune poems

No surviving poem belongs to the Elder Futhark period. The Elder names are reconstructed by comparing later traditions.

### Old English Rune Poem

**[Text, preserved indirectly]** The poem probably originated in the eighth or ninth century and treats 29 runes. Its sole manuscript witness was Cotton Otho B X. The manuscript burned in 1731, but George Hickes printed the text in 1705; every modern edition ultimately depends on that reproduction. The British Library catalogue explicitly records this transmission history ([British Library, Cotton MS Otho B X](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-001102864)).

Representative opening, normalized from Hickes:

> **Feoh byþ frofur fira gehwylcum;  
> sceal ðeah manna gehwylc miclun hyt dælan,  
> gif he wile for drihtne domes hleotan.**
>
> “Wealth is a comfort to every person; yet each must distribute it freely if he wishes to gain honor before the Lord.”

For *ūr*:

> **Ur byþ anmod ond oferhyrned,  
> felafrecne deor, feohteþ mid hornum,  
> mære morstapa; þæt is modig wuht.**
>
> “The aurochs is proud and great-horned, a very fierce beast; it fights with its horns, a famous moor-stepper, a courageous creature.”

These stanzas describe the words serving as rune names; they are not a surviving handbook assigning divinatory outcomes.

### Norwegian Rune Poem

**[Text, late copies]** Usually dated in composition to the twelfth or thirteenth century, the poem survives through early-modern copies of a lost medieval manuscript, most prominently Ole Worm’s publication. It treats the sixteen Younger Futhark runes in compact paired lines.

Opening:

> **Fé vældr frænda róge;  
> føðesk ulfr í skóge.**
>
> “Wealth causes strife among kinsmen; the wolf is reared in the forest.”

The poem’s *úr* means slag or dross, unlike the Old English “aurochs.” This warns against treating every cognate rune name as an unchanged prehistoric dictionary definition.

### Icelandic Rune Poem

**[Text]** Preserved in late-medieval and early-modern manuscripts; an important witness is **AM 687 d 4to**, fifteenth century. Each of its sixteen entries supplies kennings and usually a Latin gloss.

Opening:

> **Fé er frænda róg  
> ok flæðar viti  
> ok grafseiðs gata.**
>
> “Wealth is the strife of kinsmen, the fire of the flood-tide, and the path of the grave-fish [serpent].”

The full manuscript-based text and literal translation are available in the [Icelandic Rune Poem edition](https://norserunes.com/learn/rune-poems/icelandic-rune-poem/) and in the Viking Society’s [critical discussion](https://vsnrweb-publications.org.uk/The%20Icelandic%20Rune-Poem.pdf).

### What the poems can and cannot prove

They establish medieval rune names and learned associations. Agreement across Old English, Scandinavian, and Gothic evidence makes many Proto-Germanic reconstructions strong. They do not prove that:

- every name had identical meaning in c. 160;
- each rune carried a fixed divinatory message;
- the three *ættir* were assigned to particular gods;
- modern keywords such as “energy,” “manifestation,” or “chakra” are ancient;
- the poems were originally pagan ritual manuals.

## The letters as numbers and signs

### Numbers

**[Negative finding]** There is no established runic counterpart to Greek isopsephy, Hebrew gematria, or Arabic *abjad* numerals. Individual rune positions can be counted, but that does not make their ordinal numbers historical numeric values.

Runic calendars use rune-like or runic markers for weekdays and the nineteen-year lunar/Golden Number cycle. This is positional notation embedded in calendar technology, not an alphabetic number system in which ᚠ inherently equals 1.

Claims that rune rows encode fixed totals or that inscriptions should be decoded by adding 1–24 are normally modern unless independently demonstrated. A concise evidence review reaches the same conclusion: rune names and sounds are ancient; inherent numeric values are not ([runic numerals review](https://norserunes.com/learn/runic-numerals/)).

### Acrophony and “concept runes”

The name of ᛗ begins with /m/ and means “person”; the name of ᛏ begins with /t/ and names Týr. That is an acrophonic principle comparable to Phoenician letter names, though the runic names were Germanic and often reconstructed.

**[Artefact/Text]** A single rune could abbreviate its name. Manuscripts use the *maðr* rune for “man”; inscriptions may use name-runes rebus-like. This is genuine ideographic use, but does not license interpreting every isolated rune as an elaborate occult archetype.

### Bind-runes

**[Artefact]** Bind-runes combine strokes of two or more letters, often to save space, create symmetry, form a signature, or integrate a word into ornament. Some may have apotropaic or ritual force, depending on context. There was no single ancient “bind-rune grammar.”

Modern sigils freely combining arbitrary runes into desired concepts are modern design unless modeled on a documented inscription.

### Magic and charms

There is substantial evidence for some magical or ritual runic use:

- amulets with protection, healing, curse, or invocation formulae;
- repeated runes and formulaic sequences;
- the Ribe skull-fragment and other healing texts;
- the Canterbury charm, which addresses illness;
- the Bergen wooden amulet traditions;
- saga descriptions of carved, reddened, scraped, or corrected runes;
- *Sigrdrífumál*’s literary lists of victory, ale, birth, wave, branch, speech, and mind runes.

But the evidence does not show that every rune was principally a divination token. Writing names on tools, memorializing the dead, marking ownership, and composing Christian prayers are equally documented.

### Odin’s self-hanging

**[Legend documented in a medieval text]** *Hávamál* 138–141, preserved in the thirteenth-century Codex Regius **GKS 2365 4to**, has Odin recount a nine-night self-sacrifice:

> **Veit ek at ek hekk  
> vindga meiði á  
> nætr allar níu,  
> geiri undaðr  
> ok gefinn Óðni,  
> sjálfr sjálfum mér …**
>
> “I know that I hung on the wind-swept tree for nine whole nights, wounded by a spear and given to Odin, myself to myself…”

He then says:

> **nam ek upp rúnar,  
> œpandi nam,  
> fell ek aptr þaðan.**
>
> “I took up the runes, crying out I took them; then I fell back from there.”

In stanza 141, he begins to flourish and grow wise.

This is the fullest Norse myth of rune acquisition, not an eyewitness account of alphabet invention. *Rúnar* can encompass letters, mysteries, and occult lore. The sole complete manuscript is around 1270, centuries after the Elder Futhark was obsolete; the component poems may be older, but their oral dates are reconstructed. A manuscript-based edition discusses the irregular composition and stanzas 138–145 ([Viking Society edition](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Havamal-complete.pdf)).

### Tacitus and divination

Modern books often combine Tacitus’s marked wooden lots with *Hávamál* and infer an unbroken Elder Futhark tarot system. That synthesis is **[modern reconstruction]**, not a continuous ancient manual. Tacitus does not identify his marks as letters; *Hávamál* does not prescribe casting 24 rune stones.

### Modern rune divination

**[Modern invention/revival]** Twentieth-century occultists developed standardized spreads, reversed meanings, blank runes, and keyword tables.

Ralph Blum’s *The Book of Runes* (1982) popularized:

- rune-stone casting modeled partly on modern divination systems;
- fixed oracular meanings;
- a blank “Odin rune.”

The blank rune has no basis in an ancient rune row. Blank lots may exist as physical objects, but “blank rune = unknowable destiny” is Blum’s system.

Postwar Germanic neopaganism, occultism, New Age publishing, fantasy games, and online tattoo culture produced many further systems. Some responsibly identify themselves as contemporary spiritual practices; others incorrectly present their meanings as direct Viking-Age doctrine.

## People

### Unknown creator or creators

**[Absence of evidence]** No inventor is known. A bilingual person or small circle familiar with Roman or Alpine literacy is a plausible reconstruction.

### Early and medieval users

Most rune-carvers are anonymous. Viking-Age signatures name some specialists:

- **Öpir**, late eleventh-century Uppland runemaster;
- **Åsmund Kåresson**, active in eleventh-century Sweden;
- **Fot**, **Balle**, **Visäte**, and others known through signed or stylistically attributed stones.

“Runemaster” is a useful modern category, but not every attributed stylist was a professional holding a formal office. Some monuments were collaborative.

### Antiquarians and scholars

- **Johannes Bureus** (1568–1652): copied Swedish stones, devised recording systems, and also developed esoteric “Adulruna” speculation.
- **Ole Worm** (1588–1654): assembled Danish monuments and published early rune scholarship.
- **Olaus Magnus** (1490–1557): helped circulate early-modern northern antiquarian traditions.
- **George Hickes** (1642–1715): printed the Old English Rune Poem in 1705.
- **Wilhelm Grimm** (1786–1859): comparative runological study in 1821.
- **Sophus Bugge** (1833–1907): philological readings and historical interpretation.
- **Ludvig Wimmer** (1839–1920): influential development history.
- **Wolfgang Krause** (1895–1970): major corpus and linguistic studies.
- **Elmer Antonsen** (1929–2008): linguistic analysis of early inscriptions.
- **R. I. Page** (1924–2012): foundational critical work on English runes and repeated warnings against occult romanticization.
- **Klaus Düwel** (1935–2020): runic epigraphy and magic.
- **Michael Barnes**, **Henrik Williams**, **Judith Jesch**, **Lisbeth Imer**, **Tineke Looijenga**, **Marie Stoklund**, and others: modern corpus, linguistic, archaeological, and social scholarship.
- **Michael Everson**: central contributor to Unicode runic encoding.

### Legendary attribution

Odin is not described as manufacturing each letterform from a Mediterranean alphabet. In *Hávamál*, he sacrificially obtains *rúnar*. Later retellings transform acquisition into “Odin invented the Elder Futhark”; that stronger formulation is **[modern simplification of legend]**.

## Culture

### Names, ownership, and making

The oldest corpus contains many personal names and maker/ownership formulae. These establish ordinary social uses: “X made,” “X owns,” or simply a name. Shortness is often caused by object size, not secrecy.

### Law and inheritance

Viking-Age stones publicly recorded:

- kinship;
- heirs and sponsors;
- land claims;
- bridge and road construction;
- deaths on expeditions;
- conversion and prayer.

Because a monument fixed relationships in a visible landscape, it could possess legal or quasi-legal force without being a statute book.

### Religion

Runes occur in both non-Christian and Christian settings.

Non-Christian or mythic references include divine names, *Týr* abbreviations, Thor-invocations, and possible cultic formulae. Christian inscriptions include crosses, prayers, invocations of God, Christ, Mary, and saints, and runic Latin.

The scripts therefore cannot be cleanly divided into “pagan runes” and “Christian Latin.” Medieval users crossed that boundary routinely.

### Memorial monuments

The classic Viking runestone is usually a memorial commissioned by living kin, not a gravestone placed directly over a burial. Formulae often name commissioner, deceased, relationship, carver, and prayer.

### Coins, seals, and commerce

Futhorc appears on Anglo-Saxon coins; Scandinavian runes appear on trade and ownership objects. The Bryggen sticks include business correspondence and tallies as well as personal messages. Runes participated in urban commerce alongside Latin literacy.

### Calendars

Runic calendar staffs used recurring seven-day runes and expanded signs for the nineteen Golden Numbers. They survived into post-medieval rural practice. Their symbols are practical computistical notation, although folklore and ornament could surround them.

### Literature and learned play

Runes appear in:

- the three rune poems;
- riddles and acrostic-like name encodings;
- Old English manuscripts, where runes sometimes replace their name-words;
- skaldic and Eddic accounts of charms;
- saga scenes in which incorrect carving causes harm;
- cryptographic alphabets in learned manuscripts;
- memorial verse and alliterative inscriptions.

The Franks Casket turns script itself into learned visual play: direction, language, cipher, image, and riddle are interdependent.

## Appropriation, revival, and modern inventions

### Guido von List and the Armanen runes

**[Modern invention]** Austrian völkisch occultist **Guido von List** (1848–1919) published *Das Geheimnis der Runen* in 1908. He proposed an eighteen-sign **Armanen** row, allegedly revealed during temporary blindness after cataract surgery in 1902. It was patterned on the eighteen spells of *Hávamál* 146–163, not discovered in an ancient inscription.

List attached racial, nationalist, sexual, and esoteric doctrines to the signs. His system influenced Ariosophy and later German rune occultism. A detailed scholarly analysis calls it a “fictitious version of Germany’s past” deployed for mystical racial ideas ([Findell, “From Hávamál to racial hygiene”](https://figshare.le.ac.uk/articles/chapter/From_H_v_m_l_to_racial_hygiene_Guido_List_s_Das_Geheimnis_der_Runen_The_Secret_of_the_Runes_1908_/10160192)).

### Nazi appropriation

**[Documented modern political use]** National Socialist organizations appropriated selected runic and pseudo-runic forms:

- doubled *Sig* runes as the SS emblem;
- *Odal* forms in racial and settlement symbolism;
- *Tyr* and *Life/Death* rune conventions;
- rune-like insignia within party and youth organizations.

Some signs came through List and Karl Maria Wiligut; some were redesigned logos rather than historically faithful orthography. “All rune use is Nazi” is historically false; “Nazi symbols were simply authentic ancient rune meanings” is also false. Their political meanings were produced by twentieth-century selection, renaming, and institutional propaganda.

Several signs remain used by neo-Nazi and white-supremacist groups, but context matters: the same historical rune may appear in museum transcription, Scandinavian heritage, modern Heathen religion, military heraldry, or extremist insignia.

### Postwar rune magic

**[Modern religious/occult revival]** Writers including Karl Spiesberger, Edred Thorsson/Stephen Flowers, Ralph Blum, and many later neopagan authors produced competing rune-yoga, divination, meditation, magical, and initiatory systems. These may draw on rune names, poems, Eddic texts, Listian occultism, ceremonial magic, or Jungian psychology. Their exact correspondences are modern even when their raw materials are medieval.

### Bluetooth

**[Modern design, 1990s]** Jim Kardach proposed “Bluetooth” in 1996 as a code name for a technology intended to unite communications protocols, invoking the unifying reputation of King Harald “Bluetooth” Gormsson. The logo is a modern bind-rune combining Younger Futhark **ᚼ** *hagall* (H) and **ᛒ** *bjarkan* (B). This account is given by the [Bluetooth Special Interest Group](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/).

The logo is historically inspired but not an attested Viking-Age monogram of Harald.

### Tolkien

**[Modern literary invention]** J. R. R. Tolkien, a philologist thoroughly familiar with Old English, used adapted Anglo-Saxon runes for English text on Thrór’s map and related material in *The Hobbit* (1937). His later **Cirth** are an invented script whose angular appearance recalls historical runes but whose systematic values and fictional history are distinct. *The Lord of the Rings*, Appendix E, is the primary published chart.

Thus:

- the writing on *The Hobbit* map is adapted futhorc;
- the Cirth/Angerthas are Tolkien’s constructed writing systems;
- visual similarity does not make Cirth an historical futhark daughter.

The [Tolkien Estate’s writing-system discussion](https://www.tolkienestate.com/fr/savoir/arden-smith-systemes-decriture/) confirms the Anglo-Saxon adaptation underlying Tolkien’s experiments.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Which parent alphabet?

**Status:** unresolved scholarly reconstruction.

| Position | Strongest evidence | Principal problem |
|---|---|---|
| Latin | chronology, Roman frontier contact, many obvious shapes | difficult signs and futhark order |
| Raetic/North Italic | closer parallels for some signs; Negau contact | no single complete prototype; chronological/geographic gaps |
| Greek/Gothic | some graphic comparisons and later Gothic context | secure runes predate the relevant Gothic–Greek contact |
| Eclectic | explains more shapes by multiple sources | risks unfalsifiable sign-by-sign selection |
| Punic | attempts to explain structural problems | lacks a securely documented transmission route and broad acceptance |

### 2. The date of invention

Secure inscriptions cluster from the middle second century. The system must be older than the earliest preserved object, but by how much is unknown. A first-century or early-second-century creation is economical; much earlier dates depend on disputed objects or hypothetical transmission.

### 3. Meldorf

It may be:

- an early runic inscription;
- proto-runic writing;
- Roman letters;
- non-linguistic marks.

No consensus reading resolves the orientation and letter identity. It must remain labeled disputed.

### 4. The *īhwaz*, *perþō*, and *algiz* problems

The names, sounds, and etymologies of these Elder runes are not equally secure.

- ᛇ has uncertain phonetic function.
- ᛈ has a secure /p/-like function but an obscure reconstructed name.
- ᛉ clearly participates in the /z/ → /ʀ/ historical development, but its name and “elk/protection” semantics are contested.

Modern claims of precise psychological meanings exceed the evidence.

### 5. Why sixteen Younger runes?

No medieval reform decree survives. Competing functional, social, and structural explanations remain hypotheses. The result was genuinely ambiguous but evidently usable in context.

### 6. Were runes invented for magic?

Evidence for magical use is strong; evidence for **exclusive or primary invention for magic** is absent. Early names and ownership inscriptions weigh against an exclusively sacerdotal script. Ritual objects, charm formulae, and literary testimony weigh against an exclusively mundane script.

### 7. Did Tacitus describe rune casting?

Possible, not demonstrated. His *notae* could be nonalphabetic signs. Modern 24-rune divination sets cannot be projected backward from this passage.

### 8. Are the rune poems pagan survivals?

They preserve old names and inherited poetic material, but their surviving forms are Christian-era productions. The Old English poem explicitly includes Christian moral language. The Icelandic witnesses add Latin glosses. Separating inherited pagan phrase from medieval learned composition must proceed stanza by stanza.

### 9. “Marcomannic runes”

**[Text; misleading traditional name]** Carolingian manuscripts of *De inventione litterarum*, associated with Hrabanus Maurus, attribute a mixed rune alphabet to the *Marcomanni, quos nos Nordmannos vocamus*. It is an early-medieval learned attempt to supply runic equivalents for Latin letters, not evidence that the historical Marcomanni invented the Elder Futhark. Wilhelm Grimm discussed it in 1821.

### 10. The Kensington Runestone

**[Artefact; overwhelmingly regarded by specialists as modern]** Olof Öhman reported finding the inscribed greywacke slab in 1898 near Kensington, Minnesota. Its text claims that Norse/Götish explorers were present in 1362.

Arguments once offered for authenticity included:

- the reported tree-root context;
- weathering;
- alleged medieval linguistic forms;
- Scandinavian exploration as a historical possibility after the Norse settlement at L’Anse aux Meadows.

Evidence for modern manufacture includes:

- mixed Swedish, Norwegian, English, and nineteenth-century dialect features;
- modern-looking vocabulary and syntax;
- rune forms traceable to nineteenth-century published tables and Dalecarlian practice;
- anachronistic or otherwise problematic numerals and letter shapes;
- lack of a corroborating fourteenth-century archaeological context.

Henrik Williams and colleagues traced the Kensington row to three sources: Viking-style runes disseminated through nineteenth-century popular literature, Dalecarlian runes, and newly created forms ([SwePub research summary](https://swepub.kb.se/bib/swepub%3Aoai%3ADiVA.org%3Auu-438139?language=en&tab2=abs)). Sweden’s National Heritage Board describes the language as using obviously modern forms and explains the newly identified nineteenth-century parallels ([RAÄ, “How the runes went from Hassela to Minnesota”](https://www.raa.se/in-english/how-the-runes-went-from-hassela-to-minnesota/)).

Hjalmar Rued Holand promoted the stone during the early twentieth century. Authenticity continues to be defended in popular and local contexts. The most defensible classification is **modern inscription/forgery or immigrant-era antiquarian creation**, with the identity and precise intention of its maker not conclusively established.

### 11. Dalecarlian continuity

The script is unquestionably early modern and locally used. The dispute concerns the degree of uninterrupted vernacular transmission versus nineteenth-century antiquarian relearning. The 1900 D Rv317 inscription is the conservative endpoint securely accepted by the Swedish register.

### 12. Uthark theory

**[Modern esoteric theory]** Sigurd Agrell proposed moving ᚠ to the end and treating an “uthark” order as a concealed magical sequence. Ancient ordered rune-rows beginning with ᚠ, especially Kylver, directly document *futhark* order. Hidden-order interpretations may be studied as twentieth-century esotericism, not substituted for the epigraphic alphabet.

### 13. Political ownership of runes

Runes long predate nationalism and Nazism. Modern extremist use is historically real, while blanket claims that every display is extremist erase archaeological, linguistic, religious, regional, and artistic contexts. Interpretation requires the exact rune, arrangement, accompanying imagery, user, and setting.

## Open questions

1. Which specific alphabet—or combination of alphabets—provided the immediate model?
2. Where did borrowing occur: the Rhine, Danube, Alpine region, Baltic contact networks, or elsewhere?
3. Was the row devised by one bilingual creator, a workshop, military retainers, or through gradual adaptation?
4. How much earlier than c. 150 is the invention?
5. Is Meldorf writing, and if so is it Latin, runic, or transitional?
6. What were the earliest exact names of ᛇ, ᛈ, and ᛉ?
7. What principle produced the unusual futhark order?
8. Why were the three *ættir* created, and what did their grouping mean before medieval cipher practice?
9. What phonetic distinctions did ᛇ represent?
10. How should opaque repeated sequences be divided between language, magic, writing exercises, ciphers, and accidental marks?
11. What proportion of ordinary wooden rune writing has vanished?
12. Was Younger Futhark reduction socially directed or an emergent orthographic convergence?
13. How much continuity connects rune poems with prehistoric rune-name lore?
14. To what extent did women, merchants, children, and non-specialists write runes?
15. How should Unicode balance searchable character unification with the epigraphic need to preserve variants?
16. Where exactly does inherited Dalecarlian practice end and antiquarian revival begin?
17. Who made the Kensington stone, and was it intended as fraud, satire, commemoration, or community myth-making?
