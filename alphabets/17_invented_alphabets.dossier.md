# Invented and reformed alphabets: Enochian, Cherokee, Shavian and the shorthand scripts: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier concerns a family of otherwise unrelated scripts united by deliberate invention or reform. It does not assume that all are “alphabets” in the technical sense.

Claims are marked as follows:

- **[A: documented artefact/text]** Directly attested in a manuscript, printed work, inscription, archival record, or official standard.
- **[R: scholarly reconstruction]** An inference accepted or proposed in specialist scholarship.
- **[T: tradition]** A historical attribution preserved by users or later writers but not independently demonstrated.
- **[D: disputed]** A claim for which substantial alternatives exist.
- **[L: legend/revelation]** Supernatural or legendary history reported as belief, without treating it as empirically verified.
- **[M: modern invention/reception]** A later construction, adaptation, revival, or interpretation.

“Alphabet” below means a script whose ordinary signs primarily represent phonemes; a “syllabary” represents syllables; “stenography” represents language through heavily abbreviated phonographic or logographic outlines.

# The scripts in detail

## 1. Enochian

### Identification and earliest record

**[A]** The surviving manuscript witness for the alphabet is John Dee’s spiritual diary, British Library Sloane MS 3188, fol. 104r, dated 6 May 1583. It displays twenty-one “Adamical” or “Angelic” letters. A digital reproduction identifying that folio is available through the manuscript-derived image record. The principal companion manuscript, Sloane MS 3189, contains *Liber Mysteriorum Sextus et Sanctus*, normally called *Liber Loagaeth* or the “Book of Enoch”; the British Library catalogue dates its transcription to 1583. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/?f%5Bcollection_area_ssi%5D%5B%5D=Western+Manuscripts&f%5Bmaterial_type_si%5D%5B%5D=Archives+and+Manuscripts&f%5Burl_stub_si%5D%5B%5D=access.bl.uk+%28unavailable%29&page=162&per_page=20&search_field=all_fields), [Sloane MS 3188 fol. 104 image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Enochian_letters_(1583_manuscript).png).

**[A]** Dee generally called the material “Angelical,” “Adamical,” “Celestial Speech,” or related names. “Enochian,” now standard, is primarily a later reception term.

**[L]** According to Dee’s diary, the characters and texts were communicated by angels through the scryer Edward Kelley. Dee understood the language as the primordial speech used by Adam and associated the book with Enoch.

**[R]** Historians can document that Dee recorded Kelley’s utterances and visions; the documentary record cannot establish whether their alleged angelic source existed. Possible naturalistic explanations include conscious invention, glossolalic or automatic production, cryptographic play, and collaborative visionary practice.

**Absence of evidence:** no stable numerical series comparable to Hebrew gematria or Greek isopsephy is demonstrated in Dee’s alphabet table. Golden Dawn, Aurum Solis, Crowleyan, and later occult writers assigned different number, Tarot, geomantic, elemental, or astrological correspondences. Those are **[M]**, not recoverable as an original alphabetic numeral system. One published Golden Dawn compilation explicitly constructs Greek/Hebrew correspondences and leaves the theta position empty; that is evidence for later syncretism, not Dee’s practice. [Regardie compilation](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf).

### Orthographic structure

- **Direction:** **[A]** Left-to-right.
- **Vowels:** **[A]** Full vowel letters occur. It is not an abjad and has no system of *matres lectionis* or obligatory pointing.
- **Case:** **[A]** No native uppercase/minuscule opposition is established. Modern fonts sometimes create display forms or use Roman-style capitalization.
- **Ligatures and diacritics:** No stable general system comparable to Arabic joining or Latin accents. Manuscript letter variants and a dotted form associated with *Gon/Y* occur in later discussions, but inventories differ.
- **Punctuation:** Dee’s Roman-script transcriptions use contemporary manuscript punctuation. A distinct Enochian punctuation standard is not attested.
- **Names:** The names do not have demonstrable acrophonic meanings like Semitic *ʾālep* “ox” or *bēt* “house.” Claimed esoteric meanings are normally later inventions.

### Corpus and chronology

1. **1581–83:** **[A]** Dee records early “actions with spirits.”
2. **March–May 1583:** **[A]** *Liber Loagaeth* and the letter tables are recorded. Large 49-by-49 grids dominate the book.
3. **1584:** **[A]** Kelley dictates the texts conventionally called the Calls or Keys, with English versions.
4. **1659:** **[A]** Meric Casaubon publishes substantial diary material as *A True & Faithful Relation of What Passed for Many Yeers Between Dr. John Dee … and Some Spirits*. This makes parts of the system publicly accessible.
5. **1880s–1900s:** **[M]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn rearranges Dee’s material into elemental Watchtowers, ritual grades, color scales and magical correspondences.
6. **1900 and 1909:** **[M]** Aleister Crowley works the thirty Aethyrs, publishing *The Vision and the Voice* in *The Equinox* in 1911. Crowley’s experiences are new visionary texts, not lost works of Dee. [Crowley’s text](https://hermetic.com/crowley/equinox/i/v/eqi05016).
7. **1978:** **[R]** Linguist Donald C. Laycock’s *The Complete Enochian Dictionary* supplies the most influential modern linguistic collation and pronunciation discussion. [Publisher record](https://www.simonandschuster.co.uk/books/The-Complete-Enochian-Dictionary/Donald-C-Laycock/Weiser-Enochian-Library/9781578637966).

### The “48 Calls” problem

**[A]** There are nineteen distinct Call texts: eighteen fixed Calls and a nineteenth used with thirty successive Aethyr names. Counting the last operation thirty times produces 18 + 30 = 48 “Keys.” Thus “48 Calls” does not mean forty-eight independently worded texts.

### Language status and controversy

- **[L] Angelic language:** Dee and Kelley’s stated explanation.
- **[R] Constructed or glossolalic language:** Laycock observed that the Calls often track English syntax and translations unusually closely and lack the morphological depth expected of the primordial natural language claimed for them.
- **[D] Cipher:** Parts of Dee’s magical records employ cipher-like procedures, but no accepted method converts the whole Enochian corpus into a hidden English or Latin plaintext.
- **[D] Kelley as fraud:** Kelley demonstrably participated in at least one separate antiquarian treasure hoax, strengthening suspicions about his credibility; it does not by itself prove that every spiritual action was consciously fraudulent. [Francis Young, “Edward Kelley’s Danish treasure hoax”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17496977.2019.1643182).
- **[A] Undeciphered material:** Much of *Liber Loagaeth* lacks a continuous translation. Modern “complete translations” therefore require unverified procedures.

## 2. Cherokee

### Type and phonological design

Cherokee is a true syllabary: most signs represent an onset plus vowel, while the six vowel signs represent vowel-only syllables and Ꮝ represents /s/. It writes syllabic units rather than segmenting every consonant and vowel.

Standard romanization has six vowels: *a, e, i, o, u, v*, where *v* conventionally represents a central nasalized vowel, often analyzed approximately as /ə̃/ or /ʌ̃/ depending dialect and description. The syllabary normally leaves tone, vowel length, and some phonetic distinctions underrepresented.

### Standard 85-sign inventory

The table follows the Unicode Cherokee block and conventional Latin transliteration. The printed order was standardized in the nineteenth century and differs from Sequoyah’s earliest manuscript arrangements. Unicode’s principal Cherokee letters occupy U+13A0–U+13F5; Cherokee Supplement U+AB70–U+ABBF supplies lowercase forms. There are no inherent numerical values and no traditional Cherokee analogue of gematria. Arabic numerals and ordinary punctuation are now used.

### Orthographic features

- **Direction:** Left-to-right.
- **Vowels:** Every ordinary syllable sign includes a vowel; independent vowels have their own signs.
- **Ligatures:** None required in ordinary typography.
- **Diacritics:** The historical syllabary did not systematically mark tone or vowel length. Linguistic materials may add combining marks or use Roman transcription.
- **Case:** Sequoyah did not design case. The original Unicode encoding treated Cherokee as unicameral. Unicode 8.0 added lowercase characters to represent casing practices that had developed in modern typography. Cherokee’s legacy U+13A0 characters are treated as uppercase, an inversion of many scripts’ encoding histories.
- **Punctuation:** Borrowed chiefly from English print practice.
- **Acrophony:** Sign names are their syllabic readings, not object-names selected by an acrophonic principle.

### Origin and first attestations

**[A]** Sequoyah—also known in English-language records as George Guess, Gist or Guest—began experimenting around 1809–10, though the precise starting year rests on retrospective accounts. He was a Cherokee silversmith and participated in the Creek War in 1813–14.

**[T]** Narratives describe successive experiments: first a sign for every word, then signs for ideas, and finally syllabic analysis. They also recount his teaching his daughter Ayokeh and demonstrating writing by sending a message between separated witnesses. The broad sequence appears in nineteenth-century testimony, but individual dramatic details accumulated in biography.

**[A]** In 1821 Sequoyah publicly demonstrated the completed 86-sign system to Cherokee leaders. It was accepted informally and spread in manuscript before printing. [Scancarelli, “Learning to Write in the Cherokee Syllabary”](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/bitstreams/319d1038-2791-470b-a725-ed6e507bece3/download), [Bender, “The Cherokee Syllabary”](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ936103).

### From manuscript to type

**[A]** The earliest handwritten forms were often more cursive and less typographically Latin-looking than modern Cherokee. During the 1820s, missionary Samuel Austin Worcester, printer John Wheeler, editor Elias Boudinot, and Cherokee collaborators arranged signs and prepared type. Forms that resembled available Latin, Greek and typographic sorts were easier to manufacture, but their sound values bear no relation to their Latin appearance.

**[A]** One sign was eliminated, producing the standard 85. Worcester’s 1827 arrangement and print design substantially shaped later ordering and appearance.

**[A]** The bilingual *Cherokee Phoenix* began publication at New Echota on 21 February 1828, with Elias Boudinot as editor. The press also printed laws, religious translations, hymnals and educational matter.

**[A]** In 1826 the Cherokee National Council commissioned copies of Cherokee laws in the new system. The syllabary thereby entered government, journalism, education and religion almost immediately.

### Literacy and the “fastest adoption” claim

**[A]** Contemporary observers agree that uptake was extraordinarily rapid. Within a few years, manuscript correspondence was widespread; by the mid-1830s a large share of Cherokee households reportedly contained a reader. One historical study cites 3,914 Cherokee-language readers in contemporary enumeration, compared with 1,070 English readers; another concludes that more than half of Cherokee households had a Cherokee reader within fourteen years. [Walkiewicz on Cherokee print culture](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5C35AD80BD3BC99A91D67CC609C63751/S0009640722002803a.pdf/politics_of_media_format_printing_poor_sarah_during_the_removal_crisis_in_cherokee_nation.pdf), [Worcester and Cherokee print study](https://thescholarship.ecu.edu/items/3009b561-98bc-4571-b52c-8f99c43aa903).

**[D]** “The fastest literacy adoption in recorded history” is a superlative frequently repeated without a comparative dataset or consistent definition of literacy. It should not be presented as a measured world record. The defensible claim is that Cherokee literacy spread exceptionally quickly and is unusually well attested for a newly invented script.

### Removal, survival and modern use

The syllabary remained a vehicle of national identity through the forced removals of the 1830s. It was used in newspapers, legal records, correspondence, scripture, hymnody, gravestones and public signage. Today it appears in Cherokee Nation and Eastern Band educational programs, keyboards, signage and digital publications. Its vigorous symbolic visibility should not be confused with universal functional literacy: fluent Cherokee speakers and highly proficient syllabary readers remain a minority, and language revitalization is urgent.

### Parentage and Vai

**[R]** Sequoyah borrowed the *idea* that graphic marks could encode speech and reused or imitated some visible European letter shapes. The system’s syllabic analysis and values, however, are his own; it is not a cipher of English spelling.

**[D]** Konrad Tuchscherer and P. E. H. Hair investigated whether Cherokee stimulated the Vai syllabary through Austin Curtis, a Cherokee emigrant who became prominent in Vai country. Chronology and personal contact make diffusion conceivable, but there is no document saying that Curtis showed a Cherokee chart to Vai inventors. Graphic resemblance is weak, and independent stimulus remains possible. Their conclusion is a carefully argued possibility, not proof. [Tuchscherer and Hair](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/cherokee-and-west-africa-examining-the-origins-of-the-vai-script/5123DB37F287706DF5F0EAB6E0A3B283).

## 3. Shavian

### Origin

**[A]** George Bernard Shaw (1856–1950), a supporter of phonetic spelling reform, directed in his will that a competition be held for a new English alphabet of at least forty letters, without Latin letters and capable of economical writing and printing.

A legal dispute over the will reduced the resources available. The Public Trustee’s competition produced four prize-sharing designs in 1958–59. Ronald Kingsley Read (1887–1975), a signwriter and letterer, revised his submission; the judges selected the revised design for publication.

**[A]** The normative public specimen is the dual-script Penguin edition of Shaw’s *Androcles and the Lion* (1962), with conventional English and Shavian on facing pages. Unicode explicitly treats this book as the normative version. [Unicode core specification, Shavian](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-8/).

### Inventory

The playful names are modern mnemonic labels, generally exemplifying the sound rather than continuing ancient acrophonic object-names. Shavian letters have no numeral values.

### Orthography and typography

- **Direction:** left-to-right.
- **Vowels:** fully written.
- **Case:** none. A raised dot or “namer dot” marks proper names; Unicode counts it as a punctuation convention rather than a second case.
- **Featural organization:** related voiceless and voiced consonants are graphically paired by tall/deep orientation. Vowels form short forms; compounds and rhotic vowels occupy the final positions.
- **Ligatures:** U+1047C–U+1047F function as conventional composite sequences. Ordinary handwriting can join letters, but Shavian is not obligatorily cursive.
- **Punctuation:** ordinary Western punctuation is used.
- **Spelling:** essentially phonemic but standardized around a British pronunciation model. It does not simply authorize every writer to spell idiolectally.

### Quikscript

**[M]** After testing Shavian with users, Read produced Quikscript, also called the Read Alphabet or Second Shaw Alphabet, publicly introduced in the 1960s. Its Junior form is relatively explicit; Senior Quikscript uses joining variants, abbreviations and common-word contractions, approaching shorthand. It abandons some of Shavian’s elegant rotational symmetry to reduce pen lifts. [Read’s own introduction](https://www.quikscript.net/manual-introduction/).

Quikscript is not independently encoded in Unicode. A proposal for Shavian additions has circulated, but proposed characters are not standardized merely by appearing in a proposal. [Quikscript/Shavian additions proposal](https://www.evertype.com/standards/iso10646/pdf/n4xxx-shavian-additions.pdf).

### Adoption and controversy

**[A]** Shavian achieved a prestigious specimen book and, in 2001, Unicode encoding. **[A]** It did not displace traditional English spelling.

Recurring difficulties include:

- dialect variation, including cot–caught, father–bother and rhotic/non-rhotic differences;
- the cost of relearning and converting the existing printed corpus;
- unfamiliar typefaces and keyboards;
- loss of visible morphological and etymological relations;
- the difficulty of establishing an authority for canonical spellings;
- network effects: a private writer gains little unless readers also learn the script.

A small active digital community maintains dictionaries, fonts and transliterators. That is continuing use, though not mass adoption. [Read Lexicon](https://github.com/Shavian-info/readlex).

# Origins, spread and change: comparative chronology

## Ancient and medieval shorthand

### Greek tachygraphy

**[A/R]** Greek systems of rapid writing existed in antiquity, but surviving evidence is fragmented and does not yield one uniform alphabet. Ancient stenographers often produced notes intelligible chiefly to themselves or their school, after which they expanded them into longhand. Modern scholarship therefore warns against treating “Greek shorthand” as a single standardized script.

### Tironian notes

**[T]** Marcus Tullius Tiro, Cicero’s enslaved secretary and later freedman, is traditionally credited with inventing Roman shorthand in the first century BCE.

**[A]** The earliest detailed historical narrative is much later. Isidore of Seville, *Etymologiae* I.22–25, says:

- Ennius first devised 1,100 common signs;
- Tiro added signs for prepositions;
- Vipsanius, Philargius, Aquila and others enlarged the collection;
- Seneca organized approximately 5,000.

A tenth-century British Library manuscript preserves Isidore’s opening *Vulgares notas Ennius primus mille et centum invenit*. [British Library Add MS 21164](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002091538).

Thus “Tiro invented shorthand” is **[T/D]**, not an artefact-secured fact. No autograph Tironian manual survives. Medieval lexica—not republican Roman originals—provide most of the recoverable sign inventory.

Tironian notes combine base signs and modifiers, making thousands of words and morphemes. They are consequently neither a simple alphabet nor a pure logography. A ninth-century French lexicon in British Library Add MS 37518 is catalogued as containing about 4,000 signs in its medieval form. [British Library Add MS 37518](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002053888).

The best-known survival is ⁊, Tironian *et*, still used for “and” in Irish and Scottish contexts. Unicode encodes it as U+204A TIRONIAN SIGN ET, but does not encode the full ancient/medieval system.

### Early-modern English systems

Timothy Bright’s *Characterie* (1588) created a word-sign system. John Willis’s *Art of Stenographie* (1602) popularized geometrical analysis and the word “stenography.” Thomas Shelton’s *Short Writing* (1626), later *Tachygraphy*, was used by Samuel Pepys. Samuel Taylor’s 1786 system spread widely and was adapted into French by Théodore-Pierre Bertin in 1792.

These systems increasingly decomposed speech into reusable signs, but continued to employ abbreviation, positional devices and arbitrary logograms.

## Nineteenth-century phonographic shorthand

### Gabelsberger

**[A]** Franz Xaver Gabelsberger devised a cursive German system around 1817 and published *Anleitung zur deutschen Redezeichenkunst oder Stenographie* in 1834. It followed the motion of German longhand more closely than English geometric systems. It influenced numerous European national shorthands and ultimately the Deutsche Einheitskurzschrift. [University of Helsinki, Gabelsberger project](https://www.helsinki.fi/en/researchgroups/godel-enigma/research/gabelsberger-stenography).

### Pitman

**[A]** Isaac Pitman published *Stenographic Sound-Hand* in 1837 and renamed the developed system “Phonography” in 1840.

Its structural principles include:

- consonants represented by straight or curved strokes;
- paired consonants distinguished by thin versus shaded/heavy strokes;
- stroke direction and orientation as distinctive features;
- vowels represented by dots or dashes in positions beside the consonant skeleton;
- vowel quality and length differentiated by position and weight;
- word position relative to the writing line indicating a principal vowel;
- hooks, circles, halving and doubling to add consonants or grammatical material;
- extensive grammalogues and phraseography.

Pitman therefore writes vowels, but frequently omits explicit vowel marks in advanced reporting style when context suffices. It is phonographic stenography, not an abjad in the historical Semitic sense.

The system went through repeated revisions. This promoted efficiency but created edition incompatibilities. It became dominant in British commercial education, journalism and office work and was adapted for many languages. [Historical *Manual of Phonography*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4b/The_manual_of_phonography_(IA_manualphonopitm00pitmiala).pdf).

Pitman is not encoded as a complete Unicode script. Its dependence on stroke thickness, exact position and context poses difficult character-versus-glyph questions.

### Duployan

**[A]** Émile Duployé published his French shorthand in 1860. It uses joining lines and curves, with orientation, length and attached affixes carrying distinctions. Descendants wrote French, English, German, Spanish and Romanian. Missionary adaptations became a primary writing system for Chinook Jargon and several Indigenous languages in British Columbia.

Unicode 7.0 encoded Duployan at U+1BC00–U+1BC9F. Unicode treats many letters as default-joining characters; specialized signs and shorthand format controls support overlap and continuation. The representative glyphs show stroke direction and terminals, information that is not necessarily part of final appearance. [Unicode Technical Note 37](https://www.unicode.org/notes/tn37/), [Unicode 17.0 Duployan chart](https://www.unicode.org/charts/PDF/U1BC00.pdf).

Encoding Duployan rather than “all shorthand” reflects its relatively analyzable character repertoire and its use as community orthography. Pitman, Gregg and Tironian notes remain substantially unencoded.

### Gregg

**[A]** John Robert Gregg published *Light-Line Phonography* in Liverpool in 1888. The New York Public Library preserves manuscripts, legal documents and correspondence concerning Gregg’s earlier collaboration and dispute with Thomas Stratford Malone. The archival record says Gregg and Malone worked on a Duployan-derived “Script Phonography”; Malone claimed sole authorship, while Gregg claimed joint authorship. The lawsuit’s outcome is unclear. [NYPL John Robert Gregg collection](https://archives.nypl.org/mss/1242).

Gregg’s mature system:

- resembles cursive longhand;
- avoids Pitman-style obligatory shading;
- distinguishes sounds by curve shape, length and direction;
- represents vowels with circles and hooks incorporated into outlines;
- joins most word forms continuously;
- uses brief forms, prefixes, suffixes and phrase-writing.

Later editions traded brevity for easier learning: Pre-Anniversary, Anniversary, Simplified, Diamond Jubilee, Series 90 and Centennial. This created communities whose outlines can differ by edition.

Shorthand flourished in parliamentary, legal, journalistic and secretarial work. Its decline followed sound recording, stenotype machines, dictation equipment, word processors and the restructuring of clerical labor. The skill survives among court reporters in particular traditions, hobbyists, journalists and personal note-takers, but claims that it is “dead” are too absolute. [Research on shorthand’s decline and digital potential](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/5614).

# Other invented and reformed scripts

## Deseret

Brigham Young promoted English orthographic reform as part of Latter-day Saint institution-building in Utah. On 19 January 1854 the University of Deseret regents announced a new alphabet, developed chiefly by George D. Watt with a committee.

The printed norm has 38 phonemic letters, with uppercase and lowercase forms. Experimental versions had 40, adding signs conventionally called *oi* and *ew*. It appears in schoolbooks, the *Deseret News*, portions of the Book of Mormon and manuscript historical records.

Its expense, the small printed corpus, competition with conventional spelling and absence of a sufficiently large educational transition prevented general adoption. Unicode encodes Deseret at U+10400–U+1044F. [Deseret Alphabet Portal](https://deseretalphabet.org/), [Unicode Deseret names list](https://www.unicode.org/charts/nameslist/n_10400.html).

## Unifon

John R. Malone designed Unifon in the 1950s as a roughly one-symbol-per-phoneme English alphabet. A supporter’s institutional history says Bendix originally commissioned a universal phonemic code for aviation; after that commercial purpose disappeared, Malone applied it to literacy teaching. This origin narrative is documented chiefly by the Unifon community and should be treated as **[T/A mixed]**, pending corporate archival confirmation. [Unifon Foundation history](https://www.unifon.org/pages/john-malone.html).

Unifon was tested in American schools and later adapted for Hupa, Tolowa, Karuk and Yurok. Inventories consequently differ. It is not independently encoded as one Unicode block; many shapes can be represented through Latin and Greek letters, but this risks conflating characters that users regard as script-specific.

## Initial Teaching Alphabet

Sir James Pitman promoted i/t/a from 1960–61 as a transitional literacy system. Its approximately 44 characters retain recognizable Latin forms, add ligatures and special letters, and regularize English sound–symbol relations.

Unlike Shavian, i/t/a was not chiefly proposed as permanent adult orthography. Children were expected to transfer to traditional spelling. Large school trials produced mixed results: initial decoding could improve, but transition costs, teacher training, material supply and inconsistent implementation limited durable advantage. [Historical study of i/t/a](https://scholarworks.montana.edu/bitstreams/2b4d3a82-44c4-458f-8706-93a8e9d651e5/download).

## Vai and the West African script-creation movement

### Vai

**[A]** The Vai syllabary was first documented in Liberia in the 1830s and is closely associated with Momolu Duwalu Bukele of Jondu. S. W. Koelle interviewed Bukele in 1849 and published an account, grammar and vocabulary in 1854.

**[T]** Vai narratives describe Bukele receiving or conceiving the script in a dream. Other accounts assign collaborators and earlier dates such as 1814, 1816 or 1819. The earliest secure documentation favors invention or public emergence around 1832–33; early dates are retrospective and sometimes appear designed to exclude missionary influence.

Vai evolved markedly: signs became less pictorial and graphically simpler over generations of use. Quantitative work finds declining visual complexity across about 171 years, making Vai unusually valuable for observing script evolution. [Kelly et al., “Predictable Evolution of Letter Shapes”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/717779).

Unicode encodes Vai at U+A500–U+A63F.

### Wider West African pattern

- **Bamum:** Sultan Ibrahim Njoya of Cameroon developed the script from about 1896/97. It moved through several stages from pictorial/logographic signs toward a syllabary.
- **Mende Kikakui:** Connected with Mohammed Turay and later Kisimi Kamara in the early twentieth century. Accounts naming Kamara alone as inventor oversimplify a collaborative history.
- **N’Ko:** Solomana Kanté created this right-to-left alphabet in 1949 for Manding languages. It developed an extensive book culture, Qur’an translations and a durable grassroots literacy movement.
- **Bassa Vah, Kpelle, Loma, Masaba and others:** Created in nineteenth- and twentieth-century networks of trade, religion, ethnic organization and anti-colonial or vernacular education.
- **Adlam:** Created in Guinea in the late 1980s by the brothers Abdoulaye and Ibrahima Barry for Fulani; it has achieved substantial contemporary digital use.

The general pattern is not isolated “genius without context.” Even creators who lacked conventional literacy inhabited regions where writing, books, amulets, trade records and the social authority of Arabic or Latin literacy were visible. Scholars distinguish *stimulus diffusion*—borrowing the idea that speech can be graphically represented—from direct borrowing of sign shapes or values. [Kelly, “Invention, transmission and evolution of writing”](https://rune.une.edu.au/entities/publication/0960102b-0590-4843-a0d8-74659eaf5027), [West African writing systems survey](https://okwuid.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/West-african-writing-systemshistory-development-and-evolution.pdf).

## Tengwar

J. R. R. Tolkien began constructing scripts as part of his linguistic art by about 1919. The Rúmilian *Sarati* preceded the Valmaric and early “Qenya Alphabet” stages; mature Tengwar appeared during the 1930s and received its best-known public account in *The Lord of the Rings*, Appendix E (1955).

Tengwar is featural:

- 24 primary letters occupy a four-by-six grid;
- a stem (*telco*) and bow (*lúva*) are modified systematically;
- doubled bows commonly indicate voicing;
- raised or extended stems can indicate manner changes;
- additional letters serve liquids, glides and other sounds;
- vowel *tehtar* are placed above or below consonants in many modes;
- in “full-writing” modes, vowels have independent letters;
- values are not intrinsic: Quenya, Sindarin and English modes assign the same form differently.

Within Tolkien’s fiction, **[M/L literary]** Fëanor invents the Tengwar from Rúmil’s Sarati. This is internal legend, not external publication history.

Tengwar is not part of standard Unicode as of Unicode 17.0. Michael Everson submitted a formal proposal in 1997; later proposals reserve a supplementary-plane range. Community fonts often use the unofficial ConScript Unicode Registry Private Use Area U+E000–U+E07F, which is not interoperable Unicode standardization. [1997 proposal](https://www.unicode.org/wg2/docs/n1641.pdf), [ConScript registry](https://www.evertype.com/standards/csur/tengwar.html).

## Blissymbolics

Charles K. Bliss, born Karl Blitz, published *International Semantography* in Sydney in 1949. His system sought a language-independent graphic medium. Basic symbols combine in ways analogous to semantic radicals; position, size and indicator marks alter meaning.

**[M]** Beginning in 1971, educators at the Ontario Crippled Children’s Centre used Bliss symbols with nonspeaking children. This use transformed the system from Bliss’s universal-language project into augmentative and alternative communication.

**[D]** Bliss objected to new symbols and to using Blissymbolics as a bridge toward spoken and alphabetic language. Copyright and control disputes followed. The eventual licensing and standard-setting history illustrates a recurring conflict: inventor ownership versus community adaptation.

Blissymbols are not a phonetic alphabet and cannot be evaluated by alphabetic criteria. A symbol may represent a concept across languages, while syntax and interpretation remain language- and community-dependent.

## Voynich script

The Voynich Manuscript is Yale Beinecke MS 408. Radiocarbon testing dates its vellum to 1404–1438 at 95% confidence. Palaeography and illustrations support a fifteenth-century European context. Wilfrid Voynich acquired it from a Jesuit collection in 1912; H. P. Kraus donated it to Yale in 1969. [Yale Beinecke record](https://beinecke.library.yale.edu/beinecke/collections/beinecke-cipher-voynich-manuscript).

Its script runs left-to-right and contains recurring simple characters, loops, “gallows” glyphs and ligature-like forms. But a glyph inventory depends on whether ambiguous pen strokes are letters, allographs, compounds or abbreviations. EVA—the Extensible Voynich Alphabet—is a modern scholarly transcription convention, not the manuscript’s decipherment.

Claims include:

- encrypted Latin, Hebrew, Romance, Nahuatl, Turkic, Germanic and many other languages;
- an invented language;
- a verbose cipher;
- meaningful text generated under unusual constraints;
- sophisticated nonsense or a hoax.

No proposal has produced a generally accepted, reproducible reading of substantial continuous text. Statistical analyses find word-like structure, positional restrictions and topic-dependent distributions; these favor meaningful or highly structured production but do not identify the language. [Bowern and Lindemann, “The Linguistics of the Voynich Manuscript”](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011619-030613).

# The letters as numbers and signs

## Numerical systems

None of the core invented systems here originally uses alphabetic numerals in the classical Hebrew, Greek or Arabic manner.

- **Enochian:** Dee’s tables are numerically structured, especially around 7 and 49, but this is not the same as each letter having one stable numeral. Later occultists supplied competing gematrias.
- **Cherokee:** no traditional letter numerals.
- **Shavian:** ordinary Arabic numerals; no gematria.
- **Pitman/Gregg:** ordinary numerals, supplemented by contractions and reporting conventions.
- **Tironian:** numeral abbreviations occur, but the signs do not form a simple alphabetic numeral series.
- **Tengwar:** Tolkien devised numeral signs and decimal/duodecimal conventions in some materials; these belong to his constructed script tradition, not to alphabetic gematria.
- **Deseret, Unifon and i/t/a:** ordinary Arabic numerals.
- **Blissymbolics:** numerical and mathematical concepts are written semantically.

## Enochian magic

In Dee, letters are components of:

- *Liber Loagaeth*’s grids;
- the Holy Table and Great Table;
- angel names generated by reading specified table positions;
- the Calls;
- the thirty Aethyrs;
- ritual seals and directional/elemental arrangements.

The Golden Dawn superimposed Hermetic Qabalah, Tarot, elemental colors and geomancy. Crowley then incorporated the Aethyrs into Thelemic initiation. Later Enochian gematria systems—including Greek-order, Hebrew-order and bespoke prime-number systems—are mutually incompatible. Their existence documents creative religious reception; it does not prove an ancient angelic arithmetic.

## Shorthand as secrecy

Shorthand is not inherently cryptography. Its opacity to non-users nevertheless made it useful for diaries, private notes, political reporting and restricted professional communication. Samuel Pepys’s diary is a famous Shelton example. Tironian notes could likewise appear mysterious after their pedagogical tradition was lost.

The distinction matters:

- shorthand compresses language for speed;
- cipher transforms it for secrecy;
- an invented script changes visible signs;
- an invented language changes linguistic structure.

A text may combine all four.

# People

## John Dee and Edward Kelley

John Dee (1527–1608/09) was a mathematician, astrologer, bibliophile and adviser active at Elizabeth I’s court. Edward Kelley (1555–1597/98) served as scryer and alchemical practitioner. Dee kept the documentary record; Kelley voiced or reported most received communications. Assigning sole “authorship” is therefore interpretively difficult.

Meric Casaubon (1599–1671) edited the influential 1659 publication, intending in part to warn readers about spirit deception. S. L. MacGregor Mathers (1854–1918), William Wynn Westcott (1848–1925), Israel Regardie (1907–1985) and Aleister Crowley (1875–1947) were principal modern transmitters and reformulators. Donald C. Laycock (1936–1988) supplied the major linguistic analysis.

## Sequoyah and Cherokee collaborators

Sequoyah’s birth and death dates are uncertain, often given approximately as c. 1770–1843. His individual achievement is securely central, but the public script was also shaped by:

- Ayokeh, his daughter, in early teaching narratives;
- Cherokee leaders who tested and accepted the system;
- David Brown and George Lowrey in language and national work;
- Elias Boudinot (1802–1839), editor of the *Cherokee Phoenix*;
- Samuel Austin Worcester (1798–1859), missionary, translator and printing organizer;
- John Wheeler, printer and type technician.

Calling the print syllabary “Sequoyah’s untouched original” obscures these later typographic changes; denying his invention because some printed shapes look European is equally unsupported.

## Shaw, Read and Pitman

George Bernard Shaw supplied the institutional and financial stimulus but did not design the final Shavian letters. Kingsley Read designed them. Isaac Pitman’s grandson Sir James Pitman (1901–1985) served as Shaw’s biographer and trustee and later promoted i/t/a. These overlapping reform networks explain why Shavian and i/t/a emerged in the same period yet pursued opposite goals: permanent replacement versus transitional teaching.

## Shorthand inventors and practitioners

- Marcus Tullius Tiro, traditional Roman founder.
- Quintus Ennius, named by Isidore as earliest inventor of 1,100 notes.
- Timothy Bright (c. 1551–1615), *Characterie*, 1588.
- John Willis, *Art of Stenographie*, 1602.
- Thomas Shelton, *Short Writing*, 1626.
- Samuel Taylor, universal stenography, 1786.
- Franz Xaver Gabelsberger (1789–1849), German cursive shorthand.
- Isaac Pitman (1813–1897), phonography.
- Émile Duployé (1833–1912), Duployan shorthand.
- John Robert Gregg (1867–1948), Light-Line/Gregg shorthand.
- Thomas Stratford Malone, collaborator and rival in the Gregg origin dispute.

## Other creators

- George D. Watt (1812–1881) and the University of Deseret regents.
- Momolu Duwalu Bukele, Vai promoter/inventor.
- Sultan Ibrahim Njoya (c. 1860–1933), Bamum.
- Mohammed Turay and Kisimi Kamara, Mende Kikakui.
- Solomana Kanté (1922–1987), N’Ko.
- John R. Malone, Unifon.
- Charles K. Bliss (1897–1985), Blissymbolics.
- J. R. R. Tolkien (1892–1973), Tengwar and its fictional histories.

# Culture and uses

## Law and government

Cherokee entered national law almost immediately and appeared in the *Cherokee Phoenix*. Deseret was promoted by a territorial government and university. Shorthand became institutional infrastructure for legislatures, courts, journalism and administration. These uses supplied what most invented scripts lack: a recurring practical reason to learn them.

## Scripture and religion

Cherokee scripture translation reinforced literacy, although the script was not invented by missionaries. Deseret printed Latter-day Saint texts. N’Ko developed a substantial Islamic scholarly corpus. Enochian functioned as ritual language and sacred diagram. Tolkien’s Tengwar was fictional calligraphy but was presented with the philological density of a historical tradition.

## Newspapers and print

A script becomes socially durable when it acquires:

1. a standardized repertoire;
2. teachers;
3. cheap reproducible type;
4. a body of useful reading;
5. institutions that reward literacy;
6. intergenerational transmission.

Cherokee possessed many of these by 1828. Shavian received only one major commercial book. Deseret produced several books but not a self-sustaining print economy. Pitman and Gregg succeeded for decades because employment rewarded mastery.

## Literature and art

- Enochian grids combine text, diagram and sacred object.
- Cherokee printing became a medium of sovereignty and political argument.
- Shavian’s *Androcles* is simultaneously literature, demonstration and typographic manifesto.
- Tolkien used scripts on maps, title pages, inscriptions and artefacts; the One Ring inscription made Tengwar one of the most recognizable fictional scripts.
- Voynich writing is inseparable from botanical, astronomical, balneological and pharmaceutical-looking imagery.
- Blissymbolics treats composition itself as visible semantic analysis.
- Shorthand notebooks turn individual hand movement into a personal graphic style.

# Why invented alphabets usually fail—and why some succeed

“Failure” requires definition. Shavian failed to replace English orthography but succeeded as design, digital script and community practice. Deseret failed as compulsory regional reform but remains historically and devotionally significant. Enochian never sought ordinary mass literacy and has therefore succeeded on different terms.

Common barriers are:

- **Network effects:** readers and writers obtain value only when others share the system.
- **Installed textual base:** replacing a script appears to obsolete libraries, signage, keyboards and literacy.
- **Institutional discontinuity:** a founder, ruler or reform society dies before transmission stabilizes.
- **Dialect selection:** phonemic spelling forces politically charged decisions about whose pronunciation is normative.
- **Morphological opacity:** strictly phonemic spelling can hide relations preserved by historical spelling.
- **Transition cost:** adult readers resist returning to beginner status.
- **Typography and technology:** type, keyboards, fonts, search and OCR arrive late.
- **Competing standards:** revisions fragment a small user base.
- **Status:** a dominant script carries educational and economic capital.
- **Mismatch of purpose:** systems optimized for speed may be hard to read; systems optimized for logical phonemic transparency may be slow to write.
- **Creator control:** excessive restriction can inhibit adaptation; uncontrolled alteration can destroy interoperability.

Success is likelier where the script supports an underserved language, expresses collective autonomy, is teachable peer-to-peer, receives institutional reinforcement and is used for consequential communication. Cherokee, Vai, N’Ko and Adlam illustrate this combination more strongly than English replacement alphabets.

# Controversies and disputes

## Was Enochian invented, forged, or received?

- **Revelatory position [L]:** Kelley saw and heard angels; Dee faithfully recorded them.
- **Conscious-invention position [R/D]:** English-like syntax, dependence on translations and Kelley’s known treasure hoax suggest fabrication.
- **Visionary-production position [R]:** sincere altered-state production can generate structured language without supernatural transmission or deliberate fraud.
- **Cipher position [D]:** grids and extraction methods invite cryptanalysis, but no accepted plaintext has emerged.

Evidence establishes the sessions and manuscripts, not their supernatural cause.

## Were the Enochian letters borrowed?

Individual shapes resemble forms in Renaissance magical alphabets, ordinary scripts and scribal marks. No documentary chain identifies a parent alphabet. Resemblance alone is weak evidence because a 21-sign set built from strokes, loops and angles will inevitably produce parallels.

## Was Sequoyah illiterate?

Many accounts call him illiterate because he could not read English conventionally. Yet he had extensive exposure to writing, lettering and printed materials, and as a silversmith may have used marks. “Nonliterate inventor” is reasonable in the narrow sense that he did not derive his system by reading a grammatical account, but “wholly untouched by writing” is misleading.

## Did missionaries redesign Cherokee?

Missionaries and printers clearly affected order and type forms. Evidence from Sequoyah’s own writings, early Cherokee charts and contemporary testimony nevertheless supports Cherokee agency in both invention and adaptation. The extremes—no external typographic effect, or missionary authorship of the syllabary—are both untenable.

## Was Cherokee the fastest literacy revolution?

Rapid adoption is documented; the world-record formula is not. Comparisons with Hawaiian, Korean, missionary literacy campaigns and modern mass schooling require consistent measures that no popular version of the claim supplies.

## Did Cherokee cause Vai?

The Austin Curtis route is chronologically possible. There is no direct transmission document. The hypothesis remains significant because it explains stimulus rather than visual copying, but independent invention is still viable.

## Who invented Tironian notes?

Tiro’s attribution is traditional. Isidore’s account distributes invention among Ennius, Tiro and later systematizers. No first-century manual survives, so “Tironian” names the received tradition rather than proving sole authorship.

## Who invented Gregg?

Gregg’s 1888 publication is indisputable. NYPL papers document Malone’s competing authorship claim regarding the precursor Script Phonography and an unresolved lawsuit. Mature Gregg bears Gregg’s name and sustained revision, but its origin should not erase Malone or Duployan influence.

## Is Tengwar derived from a historical alphabet?

Tolkien knew Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Gothic and runic writing and studied historical linguistics. Comparisons with Francis Lodwick’s seventeenth-century universal alphabet or Indic-style vowel placement are suggestive analogies. No demonstrated direct parent supersedes Tolkien’s own documented series of script experiments.

## Has the Voynich Manuscript been deciphered?

No. Numerous claimants obtain short plausible phrases by assigning multiple values, rearranging letters, ignoring inconvenient passages or identifying illustrations first and fitting text afterward. A credible decipherment must specify a repeatable system, read unseen passages, explain the script’s distributions and generate coherent extended text. Current scholarship can identify structural properties but not a language or plaintext.

## Unicode controversies

Unicode encodes characters, not every visual glyph. Invented and shorthand scripts complicate this distinction:

- stroke direction and connection can be semantically important;
- shading may distinguish phonemes;
- one written outline may abbreviate multiple underlying words;
- community fonts may use incompatible Private Use Area assignments;
- an inventor’s ideal inventory may differ from attested publication;
- Unicode generally requires demonstrated textual use, not merely a complete design.

Shavian, Deseret, Cherokee and Duployan are encoded. Enochian, Pitman, Gregg, Quikscript, i/t/a as a separate script, Blissymbolics and Tengwar do not have complete standard Unicode blocks. Private-use fonts are not equivalent to encoding.

# Open questions

1. Can a fuller palaeographic sequence be established between Sequoyah’s earliest manuscript signs and the 1827–28 type forms?
2. Will new archival evidence clarify Austin Curtis’s knowledge of Cherokee writing and his contacts with Vai inventors?
3. Can the earliest Vai manuscripts be dated more securely and their sign evolution reconstructed writer by writer?
4. Does *Liber Loagaeth* encode recoverable linguistic material, a generative procedure, or structured but non-semantic text?
5. Which Enochian pronunciations belong to Dee’s annotations, which to editorial reconstruction, and which to Golden Dawn practice?
6. Can Tironian repertoires be encoded without mistaking composites, abbreviations and glyph variants for atomic characters?
7. Could a workable character model represent Pitman’s shading and positional vowels across fonts and handwriting recognition?
8. Should Gregg editions be encoded together or treated as historically distinct shorthand standards?
9. Will Tengwar’s mode-dependent values and combining vowel placement reach Unicode consensus?
10. What longitudinal evidence distinguishes temporary gains in transitional teaching alphabets from durable literacy improvement?
11. Which social institutions enable N’Ko and Adlam to expand where most replacement alphabets stagnate?
12. Is Voynichese natural language, ciphered language, an engineered text-generation system, or some combination? Current statistics underdetermine the answer.
