# Velnias (Velns): Research Dossier

> **Class:** In securely documented Lithuanian and Latvian sources, *velnias/velns* is primarily a masculine devil, demon, chthonic spirit, or class of devils. Its reconstruction as the proper name of a pre-Christian god of the dead, livestock, wealth, magic, and the underworld is a modern scholarly interpretation, plausible in parts but not directly established by a pre-Christian inscription, image, sanctuary, or indigenous mythological text.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The closest early theonymic evidence is Jan Łasicki’s *Vielona Deus animarum*—“Vielona, god of souls”—written about 1582 and first printed in 1615. The earliest secure attestation of the Lithuanian common noun in approximately its present sense is Mikalojus Daukša’s Catholic catechism of 1595, which uses forms of *welinas/velinas* for the Christian devil. Neither source proves that a deity called *Velnias* had an organized pre-Christian cult. Latvian *velns* is securely documented as “devil” in the early-modern and later lexical/folkloric tradition; the supposed ancient god behind it has to be reconstructed retrospectively.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY]** Securely attested before or close to Baltic Christianization.
- **[EARLY MODERN]** Sixteenth–eighteenth-century written testimony, normally by Christian observers.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Oral material recorded chiefly in the nineteenth or twentieth century.
- **[REGIONAL]** A geographically restricted variant.
- **[SYNcretism]** A documented fusion or translation between religious systems.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** A conclusion proposed by modern scholars from language, folklore, or comparison.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A demonstrably modern systematization presented, sometimes, as ancient.
- **[UNESTABLISHED]** Possible or repeatedly asserted, but not demonstrated by the surviving evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Velnias is an unusually difficult dossier subject because three historically different things share one name:

1. the Lithuanian *velnias* and Latvian *velns*, ordinary words for “devil”;
2. the numerous devils of Baltic folk narrative—often dangerous but also gullible, comic, economically active, and associated with wetlands, stones, livestock, music, treasure, smithcraft, sexuality, and the dead;
3. a reconstructed pre-Christian chthonic god, usually called *Velnias*, *Velinas*, or *Vėlinas*, whom twentieth-century scholars connected with Lithuanian *vėlė* and Latvian *velis* “soul of a dead person,” Slavic Veles/Volos, and the adversary of the Baltic thunder-god Perkūnas/Pērkons.

The first two are abundantly documented. The third is a historical reconstruction. It may preserve genuine pre-Christian structures, but treating every recorded folktale devil as a transparent account of an ancient god erases centuries of Christian preaching, folk Catholicism or Lutheranism, literary borrowing, and international tale circulation.

### Lithuanian forms

- **vélnias**, plural **velniai** — the standard Lithuanian noun “devil; demon.” **[EARLY MODERN onward]**
- **velinas, vėlinas, welinas** — older written or dialectal forms. Daukša’s 1595 *Katekizmas* has several spellings of *welinas*. **[EARLY MODERN]**
- **velnas, Velas, Vels** — dialectal or abbreviated forms recorded by lexicographers and mythographers. **[FOLKLORE / RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **vėlė**, plural **vėlės** — “shade, soul or spirit of a deceased person.”
- **velionis / velionė** — “the deceased,” masculine/feminine.
- **Vielona / Veliona** — Łasicki’s name for a “god of souls.” The grammatical gender, exact form, and whether this is a true divine proper name are disputed. **[EARLY MODERN, contested]**

Common euphemisms in folklore and speech include:

- **nelabasis** — “the evil/unclean one”;
- **negerasis** — “the not-good one”;
- **kipšas** — devil, imp;
- **pinčiukas** — little devil or imp;
- **biesas** — from Slavic *běsъ*, demon;
- circumlocutions equivalent to “he,” “the black one,” or “the one not to be named.”

These are names for the folkloric or Christian devil, not securely attested ancient cult titles.

### Latvian forms

- **velns**, plural **velni** — “devil,” and the principal figure of numerous Latvian legends and tales.
- **velis**, plural **veļi** — a dead person’s soul or the collective dead.
- **Veļu māte** — “Mother of the Dead/Souls,” a distinct Latvian female being in the dainas. She must not automatically be collapsed into a male Velns.
- **jods** — devil or evil spirit, partly distinct in usage and etymology.
- **nelabais** — “the evil one.”
- **melnais** — “the black one,” a circumlocution.

The Latvian form has no securely documented ancient nominative such as “Vels, god of the underworld” in a pre-Christian Latvian text. Calling Velns an ancient god is a reconstruction from language and folklore.

### Old Prussian evidence

No surviving Old Prussian source securely names **Velnias**, **Velns**, or an exact cognate as a god. Old Prussian religious catalogues instead contain names such as **Peckols/Pikuls**, **Pockols**, and, in Simon Grunau, **Patollo**. Later authors have equated these death- or underworld-associated figures with Velnias. That equation is **[RECONSTRUCTION]**, not an Old Prussian identification.

The *Sūduvių knygelė* or *Sudovian Book*, composed in the first half of the sixteenth century, lists *Peckols* and *Pockols*. Recent linguistic source criticism accepts only a minority of its names as authentic Baltic theonyms and judges many entries authorial formations, misunderstandings, or pseudo-theonyms. Simon Grunau’s triad of Perkūnas, Potrimpo, and Patollo is an early-sixteenth-century learned construction, heavily embellished and partly invented; it cannot supply a missing Old Prussian “Velnias.”

### Etymology

The most economical Baltic derivation relates *velnias/velns* to the words for the dead:

- Lithuanian *vėlė* “dead soul,” *velionis* “deceased”;
- Latvian *velis*, plural *veļi*, “spirits of the dead”;
- older Lithuanian *velinas*.

On this reading, *velinas* originally meant something like “being belonging to the dead” or “spirit of the dead.” Kazimieras Būga, Ernst Fraenkel, Marija Gimbutas, Norbertas Vėlius, and others developed variants of this argument. **[RECONSTRUCTION, widely accepted lexical relationship; the exact prehistoric morphology remains disputed]**

A wider Indo-European root *wel- / *welH-* has been connected with death, the slain, or the otherworld and compared with Old Norse *valr* “the slain.” Vladimir Toporov and Vyacheslav Ivanov built an expansive comparative complex from these forms. Such comparisons are suggestive but become increasingly uncertain as they extend to Odin, Ullr, Völundr, Varuṇa, or unrelated-looking divine names.

### Relation to Slavic Veles/Volos

Baltic *velnias/velns* and Slavic **Velesъ/Volosъ** have often been treated as cognate in both name and function. Supporting considerations are:

- similar consonantal structure;
- association with the earth, dead, livestock, wealth, magic, and the opponent of a thunder-god;
- the Baltic words for dead souls.

Objections are substantial:

- the precise derivation of Slavic *Velesъ* is disputed;
- early Rus’ texts attest Veles/Volos as a god of cattle and oaths but do not narrate a Perun–Veles dragon combat;
- the Baltic Perkūnas–velnias narratives were recorded after Christianization, when *velnias* already meant “devil”;
- similarity need not prove that every function belonged to a single Proto-Balto-Slavic god.

The responsible conclusion is: **a lexical and mythological relationship is credible but not demonstrated in every proposed detail**. “Velnias and Veles are the same god” is too strong.

### Christian identification

Daukša’s 1595 catechism uses *velinas* as a translation for the Christian devil. Konstantinas Sirvydas’s trilingual dictionary, about 1620, glosses Lithuanian *velinas* with Polish *czart* and Latin *cacodaemon*. This shows that the identification was established by the beginning of Lithuanian printing. **[SYNcretism / EARLY MODERN]**

It does not by itself show that missionaries deliberately selected the name of a major pagan god. They may have used a vernacular word for a dangerous spirit or revenant. Conversely, the semantic connection with *vėlės* makes some pre-Christian association with the dead plausible.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### What counts as the earliest evidence?

There is no known pre-Christian inscription, coin legend, temple inventory, idol label, or indigenous scripture naming Velnias. Archaeology documents Baltic funerary practice, sacrifice, sacred places, and animal deposition, but no artifact can securely be labeled “Velnias.”

The earliest textual witnesses therefore require careful separation.

### Łasicki’s *Vielona*

Jan Łasicki wrote *De diis Samagitarum caeterorumque Sarmatarum et falsorum Christianorum* around 1582; it was printed in Basel in 1615. It contains the compact notice:

> *Vielona Deus animarum: huic parentant, cum mortuos pascunt.*

Approximately: “Vielona, god of souls; to this one they make offerings when they feed the dead.”

A following phrase about cakes or food for the deceased is textually and linguistically difficult. Later writers sometimes convert *Vielona* into a clearly female “goddess Veliona,” but Łasicki writes *deus*, grammatically masculine. Other interpreters suspect that he or his informant misunderstood an expression involving *velionis*—“of the deceased”—as a proper name.

What this passage establishes:

- a sixteenth-century writer reported a being or name *Vielona* connected with souls and feeding the dead;
- mortuary feeding was recognizable enough to be described.

What it does not establish:

- that *Vielona* was simply Velnias under another spelling;
- gender, iconography, sanctuary, priesthood, myth, or social rank;
- an uninterrupted cult from antiquity;
- that Łasicki understood Lithuanian accurately.

### Daukša, 1595

Mikalojus Daukša’s Lithuanian translation of a Catholic catechism, printed in Vilnius in 1595, gives forms of *welinas/velinas* for the Christian devil. This is the earliest secure printed Lithuanian lexical witness regularly cited for the word. It is evidence of Christian vocabulary, not a direct description of pagan theology.

### Sirvydas, c. 1620

Konstantinas Sirvydas’s *Dictionarium trium linguarum* equates *velinas* with Polish *czart* and Latin *cacodaemon*. This fixes its early-seventeenth-century Christian and demonological sense.

### The major early source collections

The four-volume *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai*, edited initially by Norbertas Vėlius and published in 1996, 2001, 2003, and 2005, assembles sources from the fifth century BCE through the eighteenth century in original languages and Lithuanian translation. Its editorial introduction explicitly warns that sources written while pre-Christian systems were living differ in value from post-conversion folklore and learned compilations.

Relevant bodies of material include:

- **Peter of Dusburg, *Chronicon terrae Prussiae* (completed 1326):** describes Old Prussian religion, Romowe, the *Criwe*, sacrifice, divination, and funerary customs, but does not name Velnias. His “pagan pope” analogy is contested. **[EARLY, contextual only]**
- **The *Sudovian Book* (sixteenth century):** lists purported Prussian/Sudovian gods, including Peckols and Pockols, and describes rites for the dead. It does not securely attest Velnias. Its independence, date, and authenticity are debated. **[EARLY MODERN, heavily contested]**
- **Simon Grunau, *Preussische Chronik* (completed c. 1530):** supplies an elaborate Prussian pantheon and the Perkūnas–Potrimpo–Patollo triad. Modern scholarship treats much of this as invented or seriously embellished. **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Maciej Stryjkowski, *Kronika Polska, Litewska, Żmódzka i wszystkiej Rusi* (1582):** preserves information about Lithuanian and Samogitian ritual but is shaped by Renaissance historiography and classical comparison. It is not a secure witness to a god Velnias.
- **Łasicki, *De diis Samagitarum* (written c. 1582; printed 1615):** the key *Vielona* notice.
- **Matthäus Praetorius, *Deliciae Prussicae* (late seventeenth century):** an enormous compilation on Prussian-Lithuanian customs and religion, valuable but post-conversion and often systematizing.
- **Jesuit annual letters and mission reports, sixteenth–seventeenth centuries:** describe sacrifices, household ritual, sacred groves, serpents, divination, funerary meals, and the campaign against “superstition.” They are evidence of living mixed practice but generally do not document an organized cult of Velnias by name.

### Folklore corpora

#### Lithuanian

The richest Velnias corpus consists of nineteenth- and twentieth-century:

- *sakmės*—belief legends;
- *padavimai*—place legends;
- *pasakos*—wonder tales and comic tales;
- etiological narratives;
- proverbs, curses, riddles, and beliefs;
- local names involving *velnias*.

Norbertas Vėlius compiled and analyzed thousands of items. His *Chtoniškasis lietuvių mitologijos pasaulis* (1987) remains fundamental. The English collection *Lithuanian Mythological Tales* was compiled from his earlier Lithuanian work and published in translation in 1998. Its Velnias material includes “The Shot Velnias,” “The Cured Velnias’ Wife,” “The Baby Suckled by the Velnias’ Wife,” treasure narratives, and Perkūnas–Velnias episodes.

These texts are primary evidence for nineteenth- and twentieth-century belief and oral narrative. Their individual motifs may be older, but recording date and prehistoric origin are not the same thing.

#### Latvian

Krišjānis Barons’s *Latvju dainas*, six volumes published from 1894 to 1915, contains 217,996 song texts and variants collected mostly during the nineteenth century. The original slips are held by the Archives of Latvian Folklore.

The dainas are indispensable for Latvian religion, but Velns is much more prominent in prose tales and legends than as a coherent deity in mythological songs. Latvian material distinguishes *velns*, the devil, from *veļi*, the ancestral dead, and *Veļu māte*, Mother of the Dead. Any theory that makes all three one prehistoric god must therefore be argued rather than assumed.

### Manuscript and transmission problems

Baltic mythological material has an unusually large chronological gap:

- Christian authors wrote the earliest descriptions.
- Indigenous Lithuanian printing began only in 1547.
- Extensive folklore collection began in the nineteenth century.
- Oral texts were edited, normalized, translated, and reorganized by collectors.
- International Christian, German, Polish, Russian, and pan-European tale types circulated throughout the region.

Accordingly, “preserved in folklore” cannot automatically mean “unchanged survival from pagan antiquity.”

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Securely documented folkloric domains

Across Lithuanian and Latvian folklore, velnias/velns is associated with:

- marshes, bogs, lakes, rivers, caves, hollows, ravines, forests, and underground places;
- stones, especially erratic boulders bearing supposed footprints or claw marks;
- darkness, night, boundaries, crossroads, mills, bathhouses, barns, threshing barns, taverns, and manor houses;
- buried or enchanted treasure;
- contracts, loans, wagers, impossible labor, and payment;
- music, dancing, feasting, drinking, and sexual pursuit;
- livestock, especially horses, cattle, goats, and pigs;
- smithcraft, strength, construction, and the moving of stones;
- disguise and shape-shifting;
- the dead, suicides, sinners, persons who died violently or prematurely, and revenant-like apparitions;
- storms, chiefly as the being hunted by Perkūnas/Pērkons.

These associations belong to different narrative strata. Treasure contracts and souls owed to the devil are deeply Christian. Wetlands, cattle, the dead, and opposition to thunder may preserve older chthonic structures, but this is an inference.

### Forms

Velnias may appear as:

- a man, stranger, gentleman, musician, hunter, soldier, priest, German landlord, estate owner, or well-dressed suitor;
- a small man in a green cap;
- a black man or soot-covered figure;
- a being with tail, horns, hooves, chicken feet, one nostril, or other concealed anomaly;
- horse, bull, goat, pig, dog, cat, fox, hedgehog, snake, frog, crow, raven, duck, or whirlwind.

The horned, goat-legged figure is not securely ancient Baltic iconography. It belongs largely to the Christian and European visual language of the devil. **[MEDIEVAL/EARLY-MODERN ACCRETION and FOLKLORE]**

### Characteristic powers

- enormous physical strength;
- moving or carrying stones;
- raising bridges, dams, churches, hills, or lakes, usually incompletely;
- transformation into animals or people;
- concealment of treasure;
- music and dancing;
- prophecy or knowledge of death in some legends;
- granting wealth, skills, or power;
- contractual possession of a person’s soul;
- harming, frightening, strangling, flaying, misleading, or tempting people;
- creating troublesome animals, plants, or landscape features;
- occasionally helping poor, wronged, or clever humans.

### Limitations

Velnias is defeated or driven off by:

- Perkūnas’s thunderbolt;
- dawn, sunrise, or cockcrow;
- the sign of the cross, prayers, holy water, sacred names, or church bells;
- disclosure of his name;
- human cunning;
- deliberately literal interpretation of a bargain;
- being tricked at harvest division;
- smiths, soldiers, shepherds, women, children, or the supposedly foolish younger brother.

The comic “stupid devil” is often powerful but cognitively inflexible. He cannot anticipate an ambiguous bargain and loses to humans who alternate “tops” and “roots” of crops. Comparable stupid-ogre and devil tales occur widely across Europe; the motif is not evidence by itself for an ancient Baltic theology.

### Reconstructed divine functions

Scholars have proposed that a pre-Christian Velnias/Velinas governed:

- the dead and the underworld;
- cattle and pastoral wealth;
- fecundity and earthly abundance;
- magic, prophecy, and poetic or musical inspiration;
- water and subterranean wealth;
- warfare or the warrior dead;
- binding and contracts.

The death association is linguistically the strongest. Cattle, wealth, and magic gain comparative support from Slavic Veles. The assignment of warfare, sovereignty, or Odin-like one-eyed wisdom is much more speculative.

### Sacred animals, plants, days, colors, and planets

No early source securely assigns Velnias a fixed sacred animal, plant, color, day, number, or celestial body. Later reconstructions often supply:

- black as his color;
- cattle, goat, horse, snake, or frog as animals;
- alder, willow, or other wetland vegetation;
- Tuesday, Thursday, autumn, or a lunar association;
- particular stones and springs.

These derive from statistical folklore associations, comparative mythology, or modern ritual construction—not an ancient Baltic cult calendar.

### Iconography and material remains

There is **no securely identified pre-Christian image of Velnias**.

Later imagery includes:

- Christian church art showing Satan or devils;
- folk carvings and sculptures of horned devils;
- images accompanying folktale editions;
- the Devil Museum in Kaunas, based on the collection of artist Antanas Žmuidzinavičius, which displays modern and folk devil figures rather than ancient cult statues;
- “devil’s stones,” footprints, hills, pits, bridges, and lakes named in local legend.

Grunau’s image of idols in an evergreen oak at Romowe was repeatedly illustrated from the seventeenth century onward. It is not archaeological evidence. Even older scholarship warned that no evidence demonstrated Old Prussian anthropomorphic cult statues of the form he described.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No securely attested genealogy survives.

Velnias has:

- no securely named parents;
- no canonical birth narrative;
- no consistently named spouse;
- no securely named children;
- no stable sibling group;
- no documented divine household comparable to Greek or Norse genealogies.

Folktales mention a devil’s wife, mother, grandmother, daughters, sons, or community of devils, but these figures are usually unnamed and vary from tale to tale. They should not be turned into a pantheon.

The absence is important: reconstructions that insert Velnias into a fixed family tree are modern arrangements.

### Hierarchical position

Three competing models should be kept separate:

1. **Christian model:** Velnias is Satan or one of his subordinate devils, enemy of God and humanity. **[EARLY MODERN onward]**
2. **Folkloric dual-creator model:** God and Velnias work together or compete in creating the earth, animals, and landscape. Velnias is subordinate, imitative, and disruptive but sometimes indispensable. **[FOLKLORE]**
3. **Reconstructed pre-Christian model:** Velnias/Velinas is a major chthonic god opposed and complementary to the celestial Perkūnas and bright-sky Dievas. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Perkūnas hunts Velnias

**[FOLKLORE; ancient origin disputed]**

In the common Lithuanian legend complex, thunder is Perkūnas firing at Velnias. The devil hides:

- beneath stones;
- in hollow trees;
- under roots;
- in water;
- inside animals;
- in houses or barns;
- beneath a woman’s skirt or within a person’s clothing.

Lightning may split a tree or stone because Velnias is concealed there. People are warned not to shelter under particular trees, stand near stones, or remain close to animals during a storm. In some variants Velnias borrowed money, livestock, or grain from Perkūnas and refuses repayment. In others the cause of hostility is absent.

This is one of the strongest Baltic parallels to the hypothesized Slavic Perun–Veles conflict. Ivanov and Toporov interpreted both as descendants of a Proto-Indo-European “basic myth”: the storm-god attacks a serpent-like chthonic enemy who steals cattle, wealth, water, or the thunder-god’s wife.

The objections are decisive enough to prevent certainty:

- the Baltic narratives were recorded after *velnias* had become “devil”;
- the opponent is not consistently a serpent or dragon;
- the theft of cattle or a wife is not uniform;
- some tales make the cause a comic unpaid debt;
- no early Baltic source tells the whole reconstructed myth;
- no early East Slavic text narrates a Perun–Veles combat.

The securely documented fact is the folklore belief that thunder attacks the devil. The prehistoric cattle-theft dragon myth is a comparative model.

### 5.2 The earth-diver and dual creation

**[FOLKLORE / Christian–vernacular syncretism]**

In Lithuanian and neighboring East European earth-diver legends, God and the devil exist over primordial water. God sends the devil to dive for sand or earth. The devil conceals some in his mouth or otherwise tries to appropriate it. The earth brought up expands miraculously; the hidden portion also expands, forcing the devil to spit it out and thereby producing uneven, marshy, stony, or barren terrain.

Variants make Velnias:

- God’s servant;
- companion;
- brother-like opponent;
- malicious imitator;
- creator of mountains, bogs, stones, reptiles, wolves, or troublesome plants.

This cycle cannot simply be labeled a pagan Baltic creation scripture. Earth-diver stories occur widely in Christianized eastern and northern Eurasia. The Baltic narratives document a vernacular dualism in which the devil’s disruptive action helps explain the imperfect world.

### 5.3 Creation of animals and plants

**[FOLKLORE]**

God and Velnias produce opposed creatures:

- God creates a useful animal; Velnias imitates it and produces a dangerous, ugly, or comic counterpart.
- Velnias may create the goat, pig, wolf, snake, frog, or troublesome insects.
- In other tales he is a livestock guardian or wealthy cattle owner.

The contradiction is genuine. A being associated with animal abundance can be both master of herds and creator of animals condemned by Christian symbolism. Assigning all “bad” animals to a prehistoric god is unwarranted.

### 5.4 The stupid devil and the clever peasant

**[FOLKLORE; international tale complex]**

A peasant, shepherd, smith, soldier, or poor younger brother makes a bargain with Velnias. Typical contests include:

- dividing crops into “tops” and “roots”;
- harvesting grain one year and root crops the next;
- measuring strength;
- carrying or throwing stones;
- racing;
- eating;
- squeezing a “stone,” actually cheese;
- making a horse, child, or bird appear stronger than the devil;
- forcing the devil to complete work and withholding the promised soul.

The devil possesses land, tools, wealth, livestock, and superhuman power, yet loses because he understands agreements literally. His stupidity has been interpreted as the degradation of an older god under Christianity. That is possible, but similar stupid-devil and stupid-ogre tales are international. The stories can equally reflect peasant fantasies of defeating landlords, creditors, and supernatural enforcers.

Latvian Velns is frequently represented as a rich Germanic-style manor lord. This social costume belongs to the era of Baltic German estates, not prehistoric religion.

### 5.5 Builder and landscape maker

**[FOLKLORE / REGIONAL]**

Velnias carries enormous stones to:

- dam a river;
- destroy or block a church;
- build a bridge;
- construct a road;
- create a hill;
- close a valley.

Cockcrow, dawn, church bells, or human intervention interrupts him, and the stone falls. Boulders with hollows or marks become the devil’s footprints, fingers, seat, bowl, or hoofprints. Lakes, bogs, hills, and ravines receive names incorporating *velnias/velns*.

These legends often attach wandering international motifs to actual glacial boulders and landforms. They are powerful evidence for the localization of belief, not proof that the boulders were cult monuments.

### 5.6 Treasure, wealth, and contracts

**[FOLKLORE, strongly Christianized]**

Velnias owns or guards underground treasure. Money may burn as a blue flame, move from place to place, demand silence, or turn to rubbish when Christian rules are broken. A person can contract for wealth, musical ability, work, or knowledge in exchange for a soul.

The treasure association may continue a chthonic wealth complex comparable to Veles. Written contracts in blood, fixed terms of years, and soul-collection belong to European Christian devil lore.

### 5.7 Velnias, music, dancing, and sexuality

**[FOLKLORE]**

The devil:

- plays violin or pipes at night;
- joins dances in barns, inns, or manor houses;
- appears as an attractive stranger;
- courts or abducts women;
- is exposed by a tail, hoof, chicken foot, or other bodily defect;
- dances victims to exhaustion or death.

Conversely, human musicians may learn from devils or outperform them. Such stories blend clerical suspicion of dancing and taverns with older associations between supernatural strangers, music, wilderness, and the dead.

### 5.8 Velnias and the dead

**[FOLKLORE with linguistic support; divine reconstruction disputed]**

Velnias appears near graveyards, funeral routes, places of suicide, battlefields, and sites associated with violent death. He may resemble a revenant, accompany restless souls, or receive condemned souls in the Christian model. The lexical family *vėlė–velionis–velinas* supports a connection between the figure and the dead.

Norbertas Vėlius argued that the folkloric devil retains the attributes of an earlier lord or representative of the dead. Critics emphasize that “devil,” “dead person,” and “ghost” naturally converged within Christian folk demonology. The evidence supports historical association, but not a recoverable full theology.

### 5.9 Fire and smithcraft

**[FOLKLORE / RECONSTRUCTION]**

In some tales devils possess fire or smithing before humans acquire it. A bird—often a swallow—steals fire. Devils or Velnias may work as smiths, and human smiths sometimes defeat them.

Greimas connected Velnias with the divine smith Teliavelis mentioned in the thirteenth-century Malalas chronicle interpolation. The linguistic and functional identification is ingenious but not established: no medieval text equates Teliavelis with Velnias.

### 5.10 The one-eyed god theory

**[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION, weakly supported]**

Gimbutas and others compared Velnias with Odin and cited blindness, one-eyed figures, prophecy, magic, horses, and the dead. Online retellings sometimes claim that Velnias’s priests sacrificed an eye into a sacred well.

No securely documented Baltic ritual requiring priests of Velnias to sacrifice an eye has been established. The claim should not be repeated as ancient fact. Individual folklore about a one-eyed devil or a smith injuring the devil’s eye does not prove an Odin-like cult.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No securely identified temple

No temple, sanctuary, altar, priesthood, idol, or inscription can be securely assigned to Velnias.

This negative finding matters. Archaeological Baltic religion includes:

- cremation and inhumation cemeteries;
- horse burials;
- weapon and jewelry deposits;
- sacred groves and waters;
- fire installations;
- animal sacrifice;
- ritual drinking;
- household offerings.

None carries a label permitting confident attribution to Velnias.

### Łasicki’s mortuary offering

The *Vielona Deus animarum* passage is the nearest evidence for ritual directed to a Velnias-like being. It connects Vielona with offerings when people “feed the dead.” Even here:

- the name may be corrupt or misunderstood;
- the being’s gender is unclear;
- “they make offerings” may refer to mortuary rites rather than a specialized temple cult.

### Vėlinės and Ilgės

Lithuanian **Vėlinės**, now All Souls’ Day on 2 November, is linguistically connected with *vėlės*, the dead. The older autumnal commemoration is also called **Ilgės**.

Documented practices include:

- communal feasting;
- leaving food for the dead;
- placing food on graves;
- inviting or expecting ancestral souls;
- opening doors or windows;
- preparing a bath for souls;
- giving food to beggars as mediators for the dead;
- visiting and cleaning graves;
- later, lighting candles.

Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century writers describe extended autumnal feasts and offerings to gods and ancestors. Catholic All Souls observance gradually absorbed and transformed these rites. Food on graves survived in parts of Dzūkija and eastern Aukštaitija into the late nineteenth or early twentieth century; widespread grave candles became characteristic in the twentieth century.

The name **Vėlinės** connects the festival with *vėlės*, not necessarily with direct worship of Velnias. No secure source says “Vėlinės was the annual festival of the god Velnias.”

### Latvian veļu laiks

Latvian traditions recognize an autumn “time of the dead” or **veļu laiks**, with meals and hospitality for ancestral spirits. The dead may be led or cared for by **Veļu māte**. This provides strong Baltic evidence for an autumnal ancestral complex but not for a cult of the male Velns.

### Sacrifice

Animal sacrifice and offerings to the dead are documented in Baltic sources generally. Grunau alleges human blood and human sacrifice to Patollo and Potrimpo. Peter of Dusburg also reports human sacrifice among the Prussians in particular historical contexts.

No reliable source documents human sacrifice specifically to Velnias/Velns. Equating Grunau’s Patollo with Velnias and then transferring every allegation about Patollo to Velnias would compound two uncertain steps.

Likewise, there is no evidence for temple prostitution associated with Velnias.

### Priests

No office of “priest of Velnias” is securely attested. The *Criwe*, *waidelotte/vaidila*, and other ritual specialists reported for Baltic religion are not assigned to Velnias in reliable sources.

Modern claims about an organized Velnias priesthood, one-eyed initiations, or an underworld temple should be labeled **[MODERN INVENTION or UNESTABLISHED]** unless accompanied by a demonstrable primary citation.

### Magic and divination

Velnias is deeply involved in folk magic as:

- contractual provider of wealth or knowledge;
- helper of sorcerers;
- possessor of grimoires or forbidden learning;
- source of familiars;
- being repelled by church ritual;
- explanation for enchanted treasure.

This is predominantly Christian-era magic and demonology. No ancient Baltic magical text invoking Velnias survives.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Prehistory to the thirteenth century: possible background, no named witness

Baltic archaeology demonstrates sophisticated mortuary religions and long-lived concern with ancestors, fire, grave goods, horses, and the afterlife. The languages preserve *vėlė/velis* words for the dead.

A proto-Baltic chthonic figure ancestral to later Velnias is possible. It cannot be directly described, named with certainty, or assigned a temple from the archaeological record. **[RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries: conquest and conversion records

The Teutonic Order’s conquest generated extensive Christian descriptions of Old Prussian religion. Peter of Dusburg completed his chronicle in 1326. Lithuania’s official Catholic conversion occurred in 1387, with Samogitia’s following in the early fifteenth century.

These sources document sacrifice, divination, sacred groves, funerary rites, and resistance to Christianity but do not name Velnias. Their silence does not prove absence, yet it prevents calling Velnias a securely attested medieval high god.

### Sixteenth century: the name enters writing

- c. 1530: Grunau elaborates a Prussian pantheon containing Patollo, not Velnias.
- c. 1582: Łasicki records *Vielona Deus animarum*.
- 1582: Stryjkowski publishes his chronicle.
- 1595: Daukša prints *welinas/velinas* for the Christian devil.

This is the decisive documentary transition: a possible soul-deity name and the ordinary Christian devil word appear nearly together.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: demonological consolidation and mixed practice

Sirvydas’s dictionary fixes *velinas* as devil/demon. Clergy and Jesuit reports condemn sacrifices, divination, serpent keeping, sacred places, funerary meals, and other customs.

The old and new systems did not simply replace one another. Ancestors, household beings, saints, the Christian God, and the devil coexisted in local explanatory worlds. Velnias accumulated biblical, Catholic or Lutheran, international folktale, and local chthonic traits.

Praetorius attempted to describe and systematize Prussian-Lithuanian religion in *Deliciae Prussicae*. His work is valuable testimony but already antiquarian and interpretive.

### Nineteenth century: folklore collection and national romanticism

Collectors began recording tales, songs, legends, and beliefs on a large scale. Velnias emerged as one of the most numerous Lithuanian supernatural characters and as a central Latvian tale figure.

This is the period from which the “stupid devil,” builder, musician, treasure-owner, seducer, landlord, animal-shifter, and thunder-god’s quarry are most richly documented.

National romanticism encouraged readers to search beneath the Christian devil for a native god. The surviving folk corpus thereby became both primary evidence and raw material for reconstruction.

### 1894–1915: *Latvju dainas*

Barons’s monumental edition fixed a vast corpus of Latvian songs in print. The Dainu skapis preserves the underlying slips, though editorial selection, normalization, and classification remain subjects of study.

The dainas strongly document *veļi* and other divine figures, but they do not deliver a complete “Book of Velns.” Prose narrative remains essential.

### Twentieth-century scholarship

Marija Gimbutas characterized Velnias as a Lithuanian god of the underworld and dead, related to Veles and comparable in some respects to Odin and Varuṇa.

Algirdas Julien Greimas sought to reconstruct a coherent Lithuanian divine system from folklore and semiotics. He emphasized Velnias’s relation to magic, earthly wealth, war, and smithcraft.

Norbertas Vėlius undertook the most extensive structural and folkloric study, mapping associations with nature, animals, wealth, creation, Perkūnas, love, the dead, human society, and the Christian devil.

Haralds Biezais analyzed Latvian divine figures with greater attention to the dainas’ textual structure. His work is important precisely because Latvian evidence does not always support the same system reconstructed from Lithuanian prose.

Ivanov and Toporov placed Velnias in a reconstructed Indo-European storm-god/opponent myth. This was enormously influential but is not universally accepted.

### Soviet period

Official atheism promoted folklore as national culture while discouraging or repressing religious organization. Devil imagery could be exhibited as folk art or treated humorously while active pagan reconstruction remained restricted.

Vėlinės retained extraordinary social power. Cemetery visitation and grave lights continued; Soviet authorities attempted to channel commemoration into secular ceremonies, including remembrance of Soviet war dead.

### Revival to the present

Lithuanian Romuva traces its modern organizing history to Ramuva cultural activity in 1967, underground or restricted practice during the Soviet period, registration after independence, and consolidation of communities in 2001. Jonas Trinkūnas led the movement; Inija Trinkūnienė was elected *krivė* in 2014.

Latvian Dievturība was explicitly systematized in 1925 by Ernests Brastiņš and Kārlis Marovskis-Bregžis. It was suppressed under Soviet occupation and revived from the late 1980s. The Latvian National Encyclopedia calls it a newly created tradition whose founders, doctrine, and development are historically traceable.

Modern Baltic pagans may honor ancestors and chthonic powers, but Velnias is not uniformly worshipped as a principal god. Some practitioners reject him as the Christian devil; others recover Velnias or Velinas as a god of the dead, magic, earth, animals, or wealth. These are modern theological choices built from incomplete evidence.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship becoming mythology

Gimbutas, Greimas, Vėlius, and Ivanov–Toporov materially shaped the modern image of Velnias. Popular books and websites frequently merge their separate hypotheses into a synthetic biography:

- ancient lord of the dead;
- cattle god;
- serpent;
- magician;
- divine smith;
- one-eyed god;
- adversary of Perkūnas;
- Baltic Veles;
- equivalent of Odin and Varuṇa.

No primary source supplies that complete profile. It is a twentieth-century composite.

### Romuva

Romuva treats Baltic religion as a living, adaptive tradition drawing on Lithuanian, Latvian, and Prussian heritage. Its rites prominently use fire, songs, seasonal festivals, and ancestral commemoration.

The movement’s own history sometimes presents Romuva as institutionally continuous with a central Baltic sanctuary. The alleged ancient central hierarchy at Romowe/Romuva ultimately depends on Peter of Dusburg and Grunau; archaeology has not confirmed the elaborate institution. Modern Romuva is historically documented as a twentieth-century revival even when participants understand it as renewal or continuity.

Velnias appears variably in Romuva discourse. A standardized ancient “Velnias liturgy” is not recoverable.

### Dievturība

Brastiņš and his collaborators constructed Dievturība from Latvian folklore, nationalism, ethical teaching, and selective reconstruction. The movement often privileges Dievs, Māra, Laima, and the dainas. Velns commonly remains an antagonist or lower figure rather than a rehabilitated high god.

Claims that a fixed Latvian trinity or fully systematized pantheon appears unchanged in ancient dainas should be treated as modern doctrinal arrangement.

### Literature and visual culture

Velnias/Velns became:

- a comic and sinister character in children’s tale collections;
- an emblem of Lithuanian and Latvian folk humor;
- a subject of sculpture, graphic art, ceramics, and masks;
- a figure in nationalist literature;
- a fantasy-game and role-playing deity;
- an online “Baltic underworld god.”

The Kaunas Devil Museum has especially reinforced the image of Lithuania as a land of many devils. Its objects are ethnographic and modern artworks, not ancient idols.

### Occultism and New Age use

Unlike Hecate, Isis, Odin, or Lilith, Velnias has no substantial documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or classical ceremonial grimoires. Modern occult references are mainly late-twentieth- or twenty-first-century neopagan, internet, comparative, or personal constructions.

Assertions that Velnias possessed an ancient initiatory cult, standardized sigil, planetary correspondence, demonological rank, or grimoire invocation are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless individually sourced.

### Nationalism and politics

Baltic revival religion has participated in national cultural recovery after imperial Russian, German, Soviet, and Christian domination. This can range from cultural preservation to exclusivist ethnonationalism.

No evidence located establishes Velnias as a central symbol of an organized extremist movement. Broad claims that worship of Velnias is inherently nationalist, fascist, or Satanist would be inaccurate. Conversely, individual political appropriations should not be projected backward into ancient religion.

### Popular media distortions

Common modern errors include:

- “Velnias was definitely the chief Baltic god of death.”
- “The Prussians called him Patollo.”
- “His priests sacrificed one eye.”
- “Vėlinės was his festival.”
- “Perkūnas fought Velnias because Velnias stole his wife.”
- “Velnias and Veles are exactly the same deity.”
- “Horned goat iconography is ancient Baltic.”
- “Romowe was a proven Vatican-like pagan temple complex.”

All combine evidence with conjecture without preserving the distinction.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Velnias an ancient god or only the Christian devil?

**God hypothesis:** The lexical family of the dead, Łasicki’s *Vielona*, persistent chthonic traits, cattle and wealth associations, and combat with Perkūnas preserve a major pre-Christian deity.

**Demon/folk-spirit caution:** The first unambiguous textual uses mean the Christian devil; the rich folklore is late; no ancient cult is directly attested; international devil tales can account for much of the corpus.

A prudent formulation is that the folk devil probably absorbed indigenous chthonic concepts, but the exact rank, name, and theology of any antecedent deity cannot be recovered.

### Is *Vielona* Velnias?

Arguments for identification:

- phonetic proximity;
- common association with *vėlės*;
- Łasicki’s description as god of souls;
- mortuary offerings.

Arguments against:

- possible scribal or linguistic misunderstanding;
- uncertain gender;
- isolation—the name occurs only in this notice;
- possible derivation from an ordinary phrase concerning “the deceased.”

It is important evidence, but not a decisive bridge.

### Is Velnias cognate with Veles?

Many linguists and comparativists accept a probable relationship or at least serious comparison. Others object to particular Proto-Indo-European derivations or to inferring identical divine personalities.

The responsible distinction is:

- **possible or probable lexical relationship**;
- **overlapping reconstructed functions**;
- **no ancient source equating them**;
- **no surviving shared canonical myth**.

### The “basic myth” controversy

Ivanov and Toporov reconstructed a storm-god myth in which Perun/Perkūnas attacks Veles/Velnias, a serpent below the world tree who steals cattle, water, or a woman.

Strengths:

- repeated thunder-versus-chthonic-opponent pattern;
- Baltic belief that lightning targets Velnias;
- functional parallels with Veles;
- wide Indo-European storm-combat comparisons.

Weaknesses:

- late folklore;
- inconsistent serpent form;
- variable reason for the quarrel;
- selective arrangement of motifs;
- risk of turning structural resemblance into a lost verbatim epic.

The “basic myth” remains an influential hypothesis, not a recovered Baltic scripture.

### Grunau and the Prussian triad

Grunau’s Perkūnas–Potrimpo–Patollo triad shaped later pictures of Baltic religion. His chronicle combines earlier texts, oral material, classical models, Christian analogies, and invention. Modern scholarship considers his Rickoyoto temple, images, hierarchy, and myth-history deeply unreliable.

Thus:

- Patollo cannot automatically be treated as the Old Prussian Velnias;
- allegations of blood sacrifice cannot be transferred to Velnias;
- the triad is a sixteenth-century textual event in the history of Baltic mythology, not transparent evidence of antiquity.

### The *Sudovian Book*

Its catalogues and rites were repeatedly copied into church constitutions and later mythographies. Recent linguistic criticism argues that only a few listed theonyms are authentic and that much supposedly “Sudovian” ritual material derives from German, Polish, biblical, or authorial models.

Other scholars have defended more of the text as an independent ethnographic witness. Every name must therefore be evaluated separately.

### Peter of Dusburg and Romowe

Peter’s 1326 account of a *Criwe* at Romowe compared him to the pope. Interpretations include:

- genuine memory of a central religious authority;
- distorted description of a local priest;
- Christian literary construction of a pagan “counter-church”;
- a legend heard from Prussian informants.

Recent close reading argues against both wholesale dismissal and literal acceptance. Peter switches between past tense for the Criwe/Romowe story and present tense for customs he presents as contemporary. Nothing in the passage documents Velnias.

### Folklore dating

A nineteenth-century text may preserve archaic structures while also containing recent language, Christian theology, estate society, and print influence. Motif age cannot be decided from thematic “paganism” alone.

Barons’s editing of dainas and the practices of Lithuanian collectors must also be considered. Archives preserve variants, provenience, and manuscript layers that popular anthologies often omit.

### Gimbutas

Gimbutas’s “Lithuanian God Velnias” brought the figure to international comparative mythology. Her links among death, cattle, magic, one-eyedness, Odin, and Varuṇa are stimulating but not equally secure. Her broader Old European/Indo-European model has been influential in archaeology, goddess spirituality, and neopaganism but remains disputed in method and scope.

### Greimas

Greimas treated myth as a structured sign system and reconstructed relations among gods from fragmentary folklore. This can disclose coherent oppositions, but it risks presenting analytic structures as once-canonical narratives.

### Vėlius

Vėlius’s strength is the scale of his folklore corpus and attention to geographic and social distribution. His conclusion that the folkloric devil preserves a chthonic deity is better grounded than many popular summaries, yet it remains a reconstruction from late materials.

### Biezais

Biezais’s studies of Latvian gods stress the textual daina corpus. Latvian evidence complicates easy Lithuanian equations: Velns, *veļi*, and Veļu māte are related lexically and conceptually but do not form a single clearly attested personality.

### Beresnevičius

Gintaras Beresnevičius developed historical models of Baltic religious change, reform, and the afterlife. His syntheses are valuable but should be read as interpretations of fragmented evidence rather than translations of a lost native canon.

### State of the evidence

The essential repositories and editions include:

- Archives of Latvian Folklore, including the Dainu skapis;
- Lithuanian Institute of Literature and Folklore collections;
- National Library of Lithuania’s digitized *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai*;
- early printed catechisms and dictionaries in Lithuanian heritage collections;
- European libraries holding Łasicki, Grunau, and other early-modern works;
- regional museums and archaeological repositories.

The record is especially weak in indigenous pre-Christian writing and especially strong in late oral tradition.

## 11. Conclusions

The documentary record does not permit a seamless biography of “Velnias, ancient Baltic god of the dead.” It permits a more historically revealing reconstruction:

- Baltic languages contain an old and productive word-family concerning deceased souls.
- A sixteenth-century author recorded a possibly divine *Vielona*, “god of souls,” associated with feeding the dead.
- At almost the same time, Lithuanian Christian texts securely used *velinas* for the devil.
- Nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore preserves an immense, contradictory figure: dead-associated spirit, animal lord, treasure guardian, builder, musician, seducer, helper, murderer, fool, and quarry of the thunder-god.
- Comparison with Slavic Veles makes an older chthonic divine background plausible.
- No pre-Christian inscription, temple, cult image, priesthood, genealogy, or narrative cycle securely names Velnias.
- The Perkūnas–Velnias combat is genuine folklore; its reconstruction as a Proto-Balto-Slavic or Proto-Indo-European cattle-theft myth remains a scholarly hypothesis.
- Vėlinės and Latvian *veļu laiks* securely concern the dead, but neither is demonstrably an ancient festival of a god Velnias.
- Grunau’s Prussian triad and elaborate Romowe temple are learned early-modern constructions, not reliable direct descriptions.
- Modern Romuva and Dievturība are documented revival and reconstruction movements. Their theological rehabilitation—or rejection—of Velnias belongs to the modern history of the figure.
- Claims of a one-eyed Velnias priesthood, a standardized ancient icon, fixed planetary correspondences, or a continuous occult cult are unestablished or modern inventions.
