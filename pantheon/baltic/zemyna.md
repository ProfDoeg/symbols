# Žemyna (Zemes māte): Research Dossier

> **Class:** Lithuanian **goddess/divinized Earth**, specifically the fertile soil as giver, sustainer, and receiver of life. Latvian **Zemes māte** (“Mother of the Earth”) is a cognate earth-mother figure within the Latvian system of *mātes* (“mothers”), but the two should not be treated as a single historically unified personality. No Old Prussian deity securely bearing the name Žemyna is directly attested.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Jan Łasicki’s Latin *De diis Samagitarum caeterorumque Sarmatarum et falsorum Christianorum*, composed about **1582** and first printed in **1615**, names *Zemina* as *dea terrestris*, “earth goddess.” Although the printing is later than Mikalojus Daukša’s 1599 *Postilė*, Łasicki’s composition is earlier. The evidence begins in the late sixteenth century, not in a surviving pre-Christian inscription or medieval Lithuanian scripture. [Łasicki text and publication history](https://www.vostlit.info/Texts/Dokumenty/Litva/XVI/1580-1600/Lasicki_Jan/text1.htm)

## Evidentiary key

- **Secure early attestation:** contemporary or near-contemporary written testimony that explicitly names the being or rite.
- **Early-modern ethnographic testimony:** sixteenth- to eighteenth-century observation, often by Christian clergy or antiquarians, requiring source criticism.
- **Folklore:** orally transmitted material collected principally in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; potentially old in content, but dated securely only by collection.
- **Regional variant:** evidence confined to a particular Baltic-speaking region.
- **Learned systematization:** an author’s attempt to arrange scattered beings into a pantheon or theology.
- **Syncretistic identification:** a historical equation with a Christian or classical figure, not proof of original identity.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from language, ritual, folklore, or comparison.
- **Modern invention/reception:** a demonstrably modern doctrine, image, symbol, genealogy, or ritual arrangement.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Lithuanian forms

**[Secure early attestation / early-modern testimony]** Łasicki writes **Zemina**, describing her succinctly as an earth goddess and pairing her in the text with the bee goddess *Austheia*. He says both were believed to assist growth. His report concerns Samogitia, but it is not a verbatim indigenous liturgy: it is a Latin antiquarian account written by a Polish Protestant intellectual, partly dependent on the observations of others. [Łasicki, *De diis Samagitarum*](https://www.vostlit.info/Texts/Dokumenty/Litva/XVI/1580-1600/Lasicki_Jan/text1.htm)

Subsequent recorded forms include:

- **Žemyna / Zemyna / Zemina** — the standard modern theonym.
- **Žemynė / Žemyne** — an inflected or dialectal written form.
- **Žemynėlė / Zemynele** — affectionate or reverential diminutive, approximately “dear/little Earth.”
- **Žemelė / Žemela** — “dear/little earth,” appearing both as a common noun and as a sacral address.
- **Žiedkelė / žiedeklė / žiedkelėja** — an epithet recorded in prayers, interpreted as “raiser/bringer of blossoms” or “she who causes flowering.”
- **Žemė** — ordinary Lithuanian “earth, ground,” which in ritual address can denote the sacral Earth without a sharp lexical boundary between substance and person.

Nijolė Laurinkienė emphasizes that *žemyna* could function as the ordinary substance “earth” and as a divine addressee: the distinction rests partly in ritual context, capitalization being a modern editorial convenience. [Laurinkienė, “Žemyna and Her Mythical World”](https://www.llti.lt/failai/Nijole%20Laurinkiene,%20Zemyna%20and%20her%20mythical%20world.pdf)

**[Secure linguistic relationship; theological reconstruction uncertain]** The name belongs to the Baltic word-family represented by Lithuanian *žemė*, Latvian *zeme*, and Old Prussian *same/semme*, “earth.” These continue the Indo-European “earth” word conventionally reconstructed as \**dʰéǵʰōm* or a comparable metathesized form. This securely establishes linguistic ancestry; it does **not** by itself prove an unchanged Proto-Indo-European goddess-cult continuing into early-modern Lithuania. Comparative equations with Greek *khthōn*, Latin *humus*, Slavic *zemlja*, Phrygian *Semelē*, or Iranian *zam-* are linguistic/comparative reconstructions, not historical encounters between Žemyna and those figures. [Indo-European vocabulary study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/00437956.1993.11435902)

### Latvian Zemes māte

**[Folklore / regional Latvian tradition]** Latvian **Zemes māte** literally means “Earth’s Mother” or “Mother Earth” (*zeme*, earth; genitive *zemes*; *māte*, mother). She belongs to the extensive Latvian nomenclature of domain-governing “mothers”: *Meža māte* (Forest Mother), *Jūras māte* (Sea Mother), *Vēja māte* (Wind Mother), *Veļu māte* (Mother of the Dead), *Kapu māte* (Grave Mother), and others.

The category is not a securely documented centralized pantheon with a canonical membership. It is a poetic and religious naming system preserved especially in folk songs. Whether every *māte* was conceived as an independent goddess, whether some are functional titles, and whether several death-related mothers are variants of one figure remain debated.

### Classical and Christian identifications

**[Syncretistic identification, 1653]** Daniel Klein’s *Grammatica Litvanica* glosses **Žemyna/Žemynė** as *dea terrae, Ceres*—“goddess of the earth, Ceres.” This is a learned classical equivalence intended to explain a Lithuanian term, not evidence that worshippers regarded Žemyna as literally Roman Ceres. [Mažosios Lietuvos encyclopedia entry](https://www.mle.lt/straipsniai/zemyna)

**[Syncretistic religious language, seventeenth century]** Prätorius’s recorded prayers combine Žemynėlė with Christian **Dievas/God**, a “holy angel,” and, in childbirth material, sometimes the Virgin Mary. This demonstrates coexistence and ritual blending under Christianity; it does not establish that Žemyna was simply Mary in disguise.

Claims that Žemyna was originally identical with Latvian **Māra**, Slavic **Mati Syra Zemlia**, Greek **Demeter**, Phrygian **Semele**, or a universal Neolithic “Great Goddess” are comparative theories of unequal strength. Linguistic relationship and functional resemblance are not identity.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### What is not attested

**[Absence of evidence]** No securely identified prehistoric image bears Žemyna’s name. No Old Prussian inscription, medieval Lithuanian hymn, temple dedication, coin, or theophoric personal name securely attests her. Archaeology can establish agricultural offerings, sacred places, or animal deposition, but cannot normally assign them specifically to Žemyna without textual evidence.

### Sixteenth-century corpus

1. **Jan Łasicki, *De diis Samagitarum***  
   **Language:** Latin.  
   **Composition:** about 1582.  
   **First printing:** Basel, 1615.  
   **Tier:** Secure earliest named attestation, mediated ethnography.

   Łasicki states: *Est praeterea Zemina, terrestris dea, Austheia apum*—in substance, “There is also Zemina, an earth goddess, and Austheia of the bees”—and says they aid increase or growth. The passage supplies class and function but no genealogy, image, temple, priesthood, or narrative myth. [Online text and translation](https://www.vostlit.info/Texts/Dokumenty/Litva/XVI/1580-1600/Lasicki_Jan/text1.htm)

2. **Mikalojus Daukša, Lithuanian *Postilė***  
   **Language:** Lithuanian.  
   **Printed:** 1599.  
   **Tier:** Secure early printed linguistic evidence.

   Daukša condemns people who honor fire, “*žemyną*,” snakes/grass snakes, and thunder. The grammatical form can be read as the divine Earth or earth-cult; it corroborates a sacral use independent of Łasicki but does not describe a developed personality. [Historical summary and citations](https://www.mle.lt/straipsniai/zemyna)

3. **Anonymous Lithuanian catechetical material, around 1605**  
   **Tier:** Secure early Christian polemical evidence.

   This continues the clerical denunciation of earth-oriented observance. Such texts prove that clergy perceived an active practice; their categories and demonizing language are not neutral indigenous theology.

### Seventeenth-century corpus

4. **Daniel Klein, *Grammatica Litvanica* (1653)**  
   **Language:** Latin and Lithuanian.  
   **Tier:** Lexicographic attestation / learned classical interpretation.

   Klein records *Zemine/Žemynė* as *dea terrae, Ceres*. It is important onomastic evidence but not a cult narrative.

5. **Jesuit annual reports, especially the Vilnius College report of 1634**  
   **Language:** Latin.  
   **Tier:** Missionary testimony.

   The 1634 report says that at the commemoration of the dead, some people buried food deeply to appease the goddess they called Žemyna, so that she would not release the souls entrusted to her. This is major evidence for her chthonic and funerary dimension. It remains an adversarial clerical description and should not be expanded beyond what it says. [Summary with source references](https://www.mle.lt/straipsniai/zemyna)

6. **Anonymous Lithuanian proverb/prayer collection, seventeenth century**  
   **Tier:** Secure early-modern vernacular ritual formula.

   It preserves: *Žeminėle žiedkele, pakilėk mūsų rankų darbus*—approximately “Dear Earth, flower-raiser, raise/bless the work of our hands”—said when beer was drunk and some poured onto the earth, including at the Christmas Eve meal. This independently supports Prätorius’s description of libation. [Vaitkevičienė’s source study](https://www.llti.lt/failai/e-zurnalai/TD28/10%20Vaitkeviciene.pdf)

7. **Matthäus Prätorius, *Deliciae Prussicae oder Preussische Schaubühne***  
   **Language:** German with Lithuanian ritual expressions.  
   **Compiled:** principally late seventeenth century; portions printed only later.  
   **Tier:** Principal early-modern ethnographic corpus.

   Prätorius describes Lithuanian-speaking inhabitants of Lithuania Minor and descendants of Prussian regional populations. His exceptionally detailed accounts include sowing, harvest, threshing, livestock, childbirth, weddings, funerals, house consecration, beer libations, prayers, and sacrifices. They cannot automatically be projected backward into an undifferentiated prehistoric “Baltic religion,” but multiple independent records make wholesale invention unlikely. The critical material appears in volume III of *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai*. [BRMŠ volume III](https://melc.lnb.lt/img/cms/E_leidiniai/Velius_Baltu_3t.pdf)

### Eighteenth-century evidence

8. **Jacob Brodowski/Brodovskis, dictionary and ethnographic material, about 1740**  
   **Tier:** Late survival of a ritual formula.

   Brodowski records a mixed prayer asking Žemynėlė, “raiser of blossoms,” to bless things eaten, drunk, sown, and ploughed and to protect them from hungry beasts. [Study reproducing the prayer](https://www.llti.lt/failai/e-zurnalai/td35/TD35.pdf)

9. **August Wilhelm Hupel, 1789**  
   **Region:** Latvian territory, especially the Valmiera district.  
   **Tier:** Regional Latvian early-modern testimony.

   Hupel reports fenced sacred spots—an old tree, stone heap, open plot, or burned homestead—where offerings of milk, butter, wool, money, and other goods were made to an “earth goddess.” Before 23 April, a black cock might be killed at an offering stone. He does not provide a canonical narrative linking this goddess to Lithuanian Žemyna. [Laurinkienė’s discussion of Hupel](https://www.llti.lt/failai/Nijole%20Laurinkiene,%20Zemyna%20and%20her%20mythical%20world.pdf)

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore

10. **Lithuanian dainos, laments, agrarian sayings, and customary reports**  
    **Tier:** Folklore collected late.

    These preserve reverential addresses to Earth, harvest prayers, prohibitions against striking or insulting the ground, farewell-to-earth customs in weddings, and laments imagining the dead flowering as plants. Their collection date is secure; their precise prehistoric age is not.

11. **Krišjānis Barons, *Latvju dainas*, 1894–1915, and later Latvian song editions**  
    **Tier:** Folklore corpus.

    Latvian funeral songs call Zemes māte the keeper of the body and grave. Song 27520 greets her and asks whether the dead will rest well; 27521 asks her to guard the deceased’s *augumiņš*, bodily form. Other variants give the grave key to Zemes māte, Kapu māte, Saules meita, or Veļu māte. The variation is evidence against forcing these names into one fixed divine identity. [Latvian daina corpus, funeral section](https://latviandainas.lib.virginia.edu/tei.latv09.xml%3Flang=eng&div_id=latv09_s3ss8.html)

### Sources often cited but not direct Žemyna attestations

- **Peter of Dusburg, *Chronicon terrae Prussiae* (completed 1326):** describes Prussian religion, sacred places, sacrifice, and the disputed *Criwe/Romwe* institution, but does not securely attest Žemyna by name.
- **The *Sudovian Book* (sixteenth-century manuscript tradition):** lists supposedly Sudovian/Prussian gods and rites but does not securely establish Žemyna. Its unstable manuscripts, Germanizing spellings, and dependence problems demand caution.
- **Maciej Stryjkowski, chronicle published 1582:** names a masculine or grammatically male earth deity **Ziemiennik/Žemininkas**, associated with feeding household snakes milk and sacrificing black hens. This is relevant comparative earth-cult evidence, not a transparent alternate spelling of the female Žemyna. [Lithuanian religion overview](https://www.lnkc.lt/eknygos/eka/mythology/relmyth.html)
- **Simon Grunau, *Preussische Chronik*, early sixteenth century:** his Prussian triad Peckols–Potrimpo–Perkūnas and centralized Romowe hierarchy are learned constructions containing extensive invention and untraceable sources. They cannot supply Žemyna with an ancient Prussian genealogy or pantheon position. Modern scholarship has often rejected major portions as fictional, while still studying the chronicle as early-modern Prussian historical imagination. [Cambridge study of Grunau](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/b54273a7-071c-43ba-8b76-da5f56e3ed7f)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Earth, crops, and flowering

**[Secure early-modern testimony]** Žemyna is the earth as generative soil, not merely an agricultural department in an abstract pantheon. Prätorius’s prayer addresses her:

> *Žemynėle, žiedkelė, žydėk rugiais, kviečiais, miežiais ir visais javais…*

“Dear Žemyna, raiser of blossoms, flower in rye, wheat, barley, and all grains…”

The prayer associates her with germination, flowering, ripening, and protection of human labor. Its continuation invokes God and a holy angel, showing a seventeenth-century Christian-pagan ritual synthesis rather than a pristine pre-Christian text. [Laurinkienė’s edition and translation](https://www.llti.lt/failai/Nijole%20Laurinkiene,%20Zemyna%20and%20her%20mythical%20world.pdf)

Her power was activated at:

- ploughing and sowing;
- flowering rye and spring/summer field visits;
- first reaping and the last sheaf;
- harvest completion;
- threshing and grain consecration;
- brewing and bread-making from the crop.

### Sustainer of animals and humans

**[Early-modern testimony]** Prätorius says Žemynėlė gives and maintains life in human beings, livestock, and all living creatures. She therefore received attention when horses or calves were born, when cattle first went to pasture, at weaning, and at the end of the grazing season.

**[Folklore / scholarly interpretation]** Placing a newborn briefly on the ground has been interpreted as presenting the child to Mother Earth so that she would accept and strengthen it. Although the custom is documented ethnographically, its explanation as a formal Žemyna rite is partly reconstruction.

### Receiver of the dead

**[Secure early-modern testimony]** At funerals Prätorius records the address:

> *Žemynėle, būk linksma ir priimk šią dūšelę, ir gerai kavok.*

“Dear Žemyna, be gracious/happy, receive this little soul, and keep it well.”

Three pieces of bread, three pieces of meat, and three spoonfuls or libations of drink could be dropped to the ground. The 1634 Jesuit report similarly places souls in her custody.

**[Latvian folklore]** Zemes māte receives and guards the dead and may possess the key to the grave. In the dainas this role overlaps with Veļu māte, Kapu māte, Smilšu māte, and occasionally Saules meita. The corpus preserves alternatives, not a canonical administrative map of an underworld. [Latvian funeral songs](https://latviandainas.lib.virginia.edu/tei.latv09.xml%3Flang=eng&div_id=latv09_s3ss8.html)

### Physical form and images

**[Absence of evidence]** No historical source gives Žemyna a stable anthropomorphic appearance. There is no securely identified ancient statue, face, costume, weapon, animal companion, sacred color, celestial body, or canonical emblem.

The material Earth itself—soil, field, turf, house floor, and grave—was her effective presence. Offering stones, sacred plots, grain, bread, beer, eggs, salt, pigs, poultry, and vessels are cultic objects or offerings, not necessarily her “symbols” in the modern heraldic sense.

### Stones and household containers

**[Early-modern testimony / disputed interpretation]** Jesuit reports describe stones kept in granaries and called *deivės*, “goddesses,” connected with full bins and household prosperity. Laurinkienė has argued that their functions make an association with Žemyna or grain-mother conceptions plausible. No inscription identifies any surviving stone as an image of Žemyna, so “Žemyna statue” is too strong. [Laurinkienė, “Stones-Goddesses in Granaries”](https://e-journals.ku.lt/journal/AB/article/1695/info)

### Modern symbols

**[Modern invention/reception]** Internet diagrams assigning Žemyna a particular geometric “sigil,” rune, triangle, cross, color palette, zodiacal correspondence, crystal, or tarot card lack early documentation unless a modern creator and date are supplied. Traditional woven or carved ornaments may be genuinely Baltic, but identifying one exclusively as “the ancient sign of Žemyna” is normally a modern interpretive act.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

**[Absence of evidence]** Early sources give Žemyna no secure parents, siblings, spouse, or divine children. She is Earth personified and addressed as maternal, but “mother” is a cosmic or functional classification, not an attested genealogy.

**[Early-modern testimony]** Prätorius calls Žemyna the **sister of Žemėpatis/Žemepatys** in one learned arrangement. This is a seventeenth-century regional systematization and should not be elevated into an ancient canonical family tree.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Comparative Indo-European models commonly pair Father Sky and Mother Earth. Lithuanian scholarship and modern religious practice have consequently paired Žemyna with **Dievas** or **Perkūnas**, especially rain/thunder fertilizing Earth. The agricultural symbolism is persuasive, but no early Lithuanian narrative says plainly that Perkūnas married or sexually united with Žemyna.

**[Modern invention]** Genealogies making her the daughter of **Saulė and Mėnulis**, or assigning named divine children, circulate in popular summaries but are not secured by the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Žemyna record.

### Every documented or claimed god–god interaction

Because the corpus is ritual rather than epic, the securely documented interactions are few.

- **Austėja — joint functional mention.** Łasicki lists Zemina, earth goddess, beside Austheia, bee goddess, and says both assist growth. This is textual association, not a narrated meeting or family bond. **Source:** Łasicki, composed c. 1582, printed 1615. **Tier:** Secure early attestation.

- **Žemėpatis — sibling classification.** Prätorius presents Žemyna as Žemėpatis’s sister in his arrangement of earth powers. No episode between them is narrated. **Source:** *Deliciae Prussicae*, late seventeenth century. **Tier:** Learned/regional systematization.

- **Dievas / the Christian God — joint recipient of ritual.** In Prätorius’s *Sambariai* account, offerings and poultry sacrifice are directed to God and Žemyna; prayers to Žemyna can also invoke God. **Source:** *Deliciae Prussicae*, late seventeenth century. **Tier:** Secure syncretistic ritual conjunction.

- **Holy Angel — joint invocation.** A Žemyna crop prayer asks a holy angel to stand nearby and turn harmful people away. **Source:** Prätorius, late seventeenth century. **Tier:** Christian syncretistic accretion.

- **Laimė — co-invocation around childbirth.** Prätorius reports that birth or christening rites could call on Žemyna and Laimė. No narrative conversation is recorded. **Source:** late seventeenth century. **Tier:** Regional early-modern testimony.

- **Virgin Mary — alternate or additional childbirth patron.** Mary could be invoked in the same birth complex in which Žemyna received libation. This is coexistence or substitution under Christianity, not proven identity. **Source:** Prätorius, late seventeenth century. **Tier:** Syncretistic accretion.

- **Veļu māte — overlapping custodianship of the dead.** Latvian song clusters alternate Zemes māte and Veļu māte as receivers or guardians of the dead. No song in the consulted corpus narrates their meeting. **Source:** nineteenth-century collected dainas. **Tier:** Folklore/regional variant.

- **Kapu māte — overlapping grave function.** Songs assign the grave key to Zemes māte in one variant and Kapu māte in another. This is functional alternation, not demonstrated identity or interaction. **Source:** daina nos. 27519–27522. **Tier:** Folklore/regional variant.

- **Saules meita — variant holder of the grave key.** A variant asks the Daughter of the Sun rather than Zemes māte for the key. **Source:** variants adjacent to daina 27519. **Tier:** Folklore variant.

- **Perkūnas — fertilizing counterpart.** Rain/thunder awakening or impregnating Earth is a modern scholarly and revivalist reconstruction from seasonal structure and comparison. No early source consulted narrates a meeting, marriage, or sexual union. **Tier:** Modern scholarly reconstruction, frequently retold as ancient myth.

- **Saulė and Mėnulis — alleged parents.** No secure early Žemyna source documents this genealogy or an interaction. **Tier:** Modern popular systematization/invention.

- **Māra — proposed equation.** Latvian religious scholarship and Dievturi theology sometimes merge Māra’s material/earthly domain with Zemes māte. Other scholarship stresses Christian Mary’s contribution to Māra. No early source establishes a timeless Māra–Zemes māte identity. **Tier:** Contested learned and modern systematization.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### No surviving Žemyna epic

**[Absence of evidence]** Žemyna has no securely preserved birth myth, combat, marriage cycle, descent narrative, divine judgment, theft, or heroic adventure comparable to Greek or Norse literary mythology. This absence is historically important. Her record is overwhelmingly ritual, lexical, agrarian, and funerary.

### The annual life of grain

**[Early-modern ritual complex; modern narrative reconstruction]** The nearest equivalent to a mythic cycle is the cultivated grain’s sequence:

1. Seed and bread are entrusted to the soil.
2. Žemyna is asked to make rye, wheat, barley, and other crops flower.
3. Beer made from grain is returned to her by libation.
4. Food is placed where the first or last rye is cut.
5. The last ears are tied or braided around bread and salt.
6. Threshing concludes with grain, bread, beer, and poultry offered to divine powers.
7. Seed re-enters Earth for the next season.

Prätorius documents many individual rites; Laurinkienė interprets them as a coherent “history of grain.” The sequence is a defensible reconstruction of ritual logic, not a surviving indigenous prose myth. [Laurinkienė’s synthesis](https://www.llti.lt/failai/Nijole%20Laurinkiene,%20Zemyna%20and%20her%20mythical%20world.pdf)

### Life, burial, and vegetal return

**[Early-modern testimony plus folklore; reconstruction in its final step]** Earth gives and sustains life, receives the dead, and is asked to keep the soul. Lithuanian laments ask in what leaves or blossoms the dead person will reappear. Planting trees or flowers by graves can therefore be read as expressing regeneration through Earth. The ritual and poetic elements are attested; a formal doctrine of reincarnation administered by Žemyna is a scholarly reconstruction, not a surviving catechism.

### Zemes māte and the grave key

**[Latvian folklore]** In Latvian funeral dainas, the deceased greets Zemes māte or asks her for secure rest; a ritual speaker asks for the grave key. Other song variants assign the key to Kapu māte or Saules meita. The cycle concerns entry, closure, custody, and repose rather than a narrated journey through a systematically mapped underworld. [Dainas 27519–27525](https://latviandainas.lib.virginia.edu/tei.latv09.xml%3Flang=eng&div_id=latv09_s3ss8.html)

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult places

**[Secure negative finding]** There is no record of a temple built specifically for Žemyna. Laurinkienė concludes that her rites normally occurred at home, on the farm, in cultivated fields, and at burial places. [Laurinkienė](https://www.llti.lt/failai/Nijole%20Laurinkiene,%20Zemyna%20and%20her%20mythical%20world.pdf)

Possible ritual loci included:

- the household table and dirt floor;
- the threshold, hearth, and grindstones;
- barns and granaries;
- newly ploughed or sown fields;
- the first- and last-reaped portions of a field;
- graves and funeral-feast spaces;
- in Latvian testimony, fenced trees, stones, stone heaps, or abandoned plots.

These were working environments rather than monumental urban sanctuaries.

### Priests and household officiants

**[Early-modern testimony]** The ordinary officiant in Prätorius’s descriptions is commonly the household head, host, hostess, midwife, matchmaker, or another participant—not a permanent Žemyna priesthood. No securely documented temple estate, dedicated priestly college, initiation hierarchy, or economic bureaucracy belonged specifically to her.

The broader Prussian *Criwe* institution reported by Peter of Dusburg is disputed and cannot be assigned to Žemyna. Modern analysis warns that Dusburg may have reshaped Prussian information through Christian categories, including comparison with the pope. [Mažeika on Peter of Dusburg](https://e-journals.ku.lt/journal/AHUK/article/2897/file/pdf)

### Žemynėliauti

**[Secure early-modern regional testimony]** The verb **žemynėliauti / žemynelauti**, formed from Žemynėlė, denoted performing the libation or service addressed to her.

The recurrent sequence was approximately:

1. A clean, dry ritual cup or ladle was brought.
2. The host poured in a small amount of beer.
3. He poured that first portion onto the ground for Žemynėlė.
4. The cup was filled again.
5. A prayer for flowering crops, health, protection, or the dead was spoken.
6. Participants greeted or blessed one another and drank.
7. Some ceremonies included repeated libations, food deposition, or animal sacrifice.

The rite occurred at sowing, the opening and close of pasture, harvest, threshing, weddings, baptisms/christenings, funerals, house entry, and the blessing of livestock. It was adaptable rather than tied to one temple festival. [Ritual-vessel study quoting Prätorius](https://etalpykla.lituanistika.lt/object/LT-LDB-0001%3AJ.04~2018~1575037985916/J.04~2018~1575037985916.pdf)

### Beer, bread, and food returned to Earth

**[Secure early-modern testimony]** Beer was particularly appropriate because it was transformed grain: the crop Žemyna enabled was returned in liquid form. Bread likewise represented the harvest.

Documented or reported offerings include:

- beer poured directly onto the soil;
- bread buried before ploughing or sowing;
- bread with salt or an egg placed in the earth;
- bread and salt left by the first or last rye;
- three morsels each of bread and meat, plus drink, at funerals;
- grain products offered during *Sambariai*;
- milk, butter, wool, money, and poultry in Latvian earth-goddess rites.

The statement “bread and beer were poured on the ground” requires correction: **beer** was poured; **bread** was dropped, laid down, or buried.

### Seasonal rites

**[Early-modern testimony]**

- **Before ploughing/sowing:** bread, sometimes salt or egg, placed in the soil.
- **After sowing:** feast and ritual pork, including head, feet, or snout.
- **Pentecost/St John’s season:** field visits, beer, food, and prayers during rye flowering.
- **Beginning of harvest:** libation after the first day’s work.
- **Last sheaf:** bread and salt placed beside tied or braided ears.
- **Early December threshing/Sambariai:** mixed grain, bread, beer, three libations, and poultry offering.
- **Pastoral transitions:** prayer at first driving-out and final return of cattle.
- **Funerals:** food and beer committed to Earth with a request that Žemyna receive the soul.

### Animal and human sacrifice

**[Animal sacrifice documented or reported]** Prätorius describes hens and cocks sacrificed in the threshing complex, and rites involving a calf or pig. Hupel reports a black cock offered to a Latvian earth goddess. Details vary, and it is not always certain whether Žemyna was the sole recipient.

**[No specific evidence for human sacrifice to Žemyna]** Medieval chronicles accuse Baltic peoples of sacrificing captives or enemies, and archaeological evidence supports some sacrificial animal deposits. None of the sources examined securely attributes human sacrifice to Žemyna or Zemes māte. General claims that “Žemyna demanded human victims” are unsupported. Even Peter of Dusburg’s broader religious reportage must be separated from later elaboration and analyzed as crusading-era Christian writing. [Discussion of the medieval evidence](https://e-journals.ku.lt/journal/AHUK/article/2897/file/pdf)

### Temple prostitution, cult violence, and oracles

**[Absence of evidence]** No documentary or archaeological evidence associates Žemyna with temple prostitution, a dedicated oracle, organized cult violence, ritual sexual service, or a warrior sodality.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Prehistory to the fourteenth century

**[Modern reconstruction]** Baltic languages inherited an ancient Indo-European word for earth, and archaeological evidence shows long-standing reverence for land, water, stones, groves, grain, animals, and the dead. This makes a pre-Christian earth cult highly plausible.

It does not establish that prehistoric people used the exact name Žemyna, conceived a female anthropomorphic goddess identical to the early-modern figure, or possessed a unified Lithuanian-Latvian-Prussian theology.

Lithuania’s official Christianization began in 1387, with Samogitia formally Christianized in the early fifteenth century. Latvia and Prussia had undergone conquest and Christianization earlier under crusading and episcopal regimes. These political dates did not instantly eliminate household or agrarian practices.

### Sixteenth century: first named record

**[Secure attestation]** Around 1582 Łasicki names Zemina. Daukša’s 1599 condemnation corroborates sacral reverence toward Earth in Lithuanian-speaking society. These texts reflect a Christian environment in which older observance persisted, changed, and attracted clerical criticism.

### Seventeenth century: richest documentation

**[Secure early-modern testimony]** Jesuit reports, Klein, anonymous formulas, and Prätorius document a broad complex:

- crop fertility;
- beer libation;
- household ritual;
- livestock;
- birth and marriage;
- funerary custody;
- Christian-pagan mixed prayers.

This is the evidentiary peak—not necessarily the historical peak—because the record is densest then.

Suppression operated mainly through preaching, confession, catechism, visitation, ridicule, confiscation of ritual objects, and reinterpretation of observance as superstition. The sources are often products of that campaign. The evidence nevertheless shows accommodation: God, angels, Mary, Christmas Eve, baptisms, and Christian funerals could coexist with Žemyna-directed acts.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: ritual diffusion into custom

**[Late survival / folklore]** Named prayers remain in Brodowski, while Hupel records Latvian earth offerings. Over time, personified Žemyna becomes harder to distinguish from reverence for *žemė* itself. Ritual actions survive as peasant custom, taboo, proverbial speech, harvest ceremony, funeral offering, or song after explicit divine theology weakens.

The Latvian dainas were orally transmitted but largely collected and edited in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. They preserve Zemes māte most vividly in relation to burial and bodily repose.

### Twentieth century: scholarship and religious reconstruction

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Researchers including Wilhelm Mannhardt, Haralds Biezais, Marija Gimbutas, Algirdas Julius Greimas, Norbertas Vėlius, Gintaras Beresnevičius, and Nijolė Laurinkienė organized the fragmentary record in different ways.

- **Mannhardt** assembled early reports and emphasized agrarian religion.
- **Biezais** studied Latvian gods and the *mātes*, stressing the daina corpus.
- **Gimbutas** placed Baltic goddesses in a broad prehistoric “Old European” framework. Her documentation of Baltic folklore remains influential, but the uninterrupted Neolithic Great-Goddess model is disputed.
- **Greimas** used structural and semiotic reconstruction to infer relations within Lithuanian mythology. His elegant systems should not be confused with a surviving native canon.
- **Vėlius** assembled the indispensable four-volume *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai*, presenting sources in their original languages with Lithuanian translations and commentary. [National Library edition](https://lnb.lt/istekliai/mokslo-ir-enciklopediju-leidybos-centro-istekliai/baltu-religijos-ir-mitologijos-saltiniai)
- **Laurinkienė** produced the most concentrated modern reconstruction of Žemyna from early reports and folklore.

### Present

Žemyna is actively honored in Lithuanian Romuva and in individual Baltic reconstructionist practice. Zemes māte remains part of Latvian folklore, cultural performance, and Dievturi religious discourse. These are living modern traditions informed by historical evidence, but their institutional structures and synthesized theologies are modern.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Romuva

**[Modern revival]** Lithuanian Baltic religious revival developed through nationalist, folkloric, and religious initiatives. Romuva’s own institutional history traces a community to 1930, *Ramuva* activity from 1967, and official registration of Romuva in 1992. [Romuva institutional history](https://romuva.lt/apie-2/romuva/)

Modern Romuva places Žemyna among the major Baltic deities and uses songs, grain, bread, beer, fire altars, seasonal festivals, and reconstructed prayer. These practices may consciously continue early-modern forms, but their selection, standardized sequence, priestly roles, and national synthesis belong to the revival.

### Dievturība

**[Modern invented/reconstructed tradition]** Ernests Brastiņš and Kārlis Marovskis-Bregžis published *Latviešu dievturības atjaunojums* in 1925; a Latvian Dievturi organization was registered in 1926. The movement combined dainas, nationalism, folklore, art, ethical teaching, and reconstructed religion. It was suppressed under Soviet rule and revived in the late 1980s, with official registration in 1990. [Academic history of Dievturība](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/10/3/195)

Latvia’s National Encyclopedia explicitly classifies Dievturība as a **newly created tradition**, notwithstanding its claim to renew ancient Baltic religion. Its recoverable modern founders and texts make that historical classification possible. [Latvian National Encyclopedia](https://enciklopedija.lv/skirklis/22202)

Within Dievturi theology, the relation among Māra, Zemes māte, and other *mātes* is systematized more tightly than the song variants themselves require. Such theology is legitimate modern reception but not automatically ancient doctrine.

### Literature, art, and popular spirituality

**[Modern reception]** Žemyna appears in modern poetry, choral and folk music, fantasy, ecological spirituality, children’s mythology books, goddess encyclopedias, role-playing material, and online devotional practice. Common modern changes include:

- turning Earth herself into an anthropomorphic woman in green or brown clothing;
- assigning a fixed “Žemyna symbol”;
- making Perkūnas her husband;
- making Saulė and Mėnulis her parents;
- assigning crystals, tarot correspondences, chakras, planets, colors, or astrological signs;
- treating all Baltic female earth powers as one universal Great Goddess.

None of these is securely established by the earliest corpus unless separately attributed to a modern author or community.

### Nationalist and political appropriation

**[Modern reception]** Both Romuva and Dievturība developed partly within national-cultural movements responding to Christianization, Russification, Soviet atheism, and perceived cultural loss. Dievturība was especially intertwined with interwar Latvian nationalism; contemporary groups vary politically. Historical association with nationalism does not make every practitioner an extremist, and no evidence supports portraying Žemyna herself as an ancient ethnic-supremacist deity.

Claims that Baltic earth religion was uniformly ecological, egalitarian, matriarchal, pacifist, or racially exclusive are modern ideological readings. Early sources document agrarian dependence, household welfare, sacrifice, and treatment of the dead, not a modern political program.

### Occultism

**[Negative finding]** Žemyna has no substantial documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, Solomonic grimoires, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or classical chaos-magic literature. Modern occult correspondences are largely recent internet-era or individual creations. They should not be backdated.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Reliability of the witnesses

All extensive early accounts were written under Christianity, often by outsiders, converts, clergy, or antiquarians. Their recurrent problems include:

- treating diverse local customs as one religion;
- forcing practices into Greco-Roman categories;
- mistaking ordinary nouns or ritual phrases for names of separate gods;
- duplicating deities through spelling variants;
- demonizing customs as idolatry;
- copying earlier authors;
- inventing pantheon hierarchies to resemble classical systems.

Yet total rejection is also unwarranted. Independent recurrence of the name, earth-directed beer libation, vernacular prayers, funerary offerings, and later folklore supplies cumulative evidence.

### Was Žemyna a goddess or simply “the earth”?

**Side A: personified goddess.** Łasicki explicitly calls her *dea*; Klein calls her *dea terrae*; prayers address her in the second person; Prätorius attributes agency, life-giving power, protection, and custody of souls.

**Side B: sacral substance without a sharply bounded person.** *Žemyna* and *žemė* can mean earth itself; no fixed image or biography survives; many rites act directly on soil. The category “goddess” may partly be an observer’s translation.

**Assessment:** The sources support a divinized and addressable Earth, but not necessarily a sharply individualized Olympian-style personality.

### Žemyna and Žemėpatis/Žemininkas

Stryjkowski’s **Ziemiennik** is a masculine earth god connected with snakes and black hens. Łasicki’s plural *Zemopacios* may describe multiple “earth lords” or terrestrial beings. Prätorius distinguishes Žemyna and Žemėpatis while calling them siblings.

Possible explanations include:

- regional male and female earth powers;
- a functional pair;
- differing grammatical personifications;
- observers splitting titles into separate gods;
- later learned organization of an unstable vernacular system.

The evidence does not authorize silently merging all names into one deity.

### Lithuanian Žemyna and Latvian Zemes māte

Their names and functions are closely related, and both embody/command Earth and receive the dead. Nevertheless:

- Žemyna’s richest record is ritual prose from Lithuania Minor;
- Zemes māte’s richest record is Latvian funeral song;
- the Latvian figure exists in a larger *mātes* naming system;
- no common Baltic scripture identifies them.

They are best described as closely cognate Baltic earth-divinity traditions, not one canonical goddess with two translations.

### Indo-European Earth Mother

The linguistic ancestry of Baltic “earth” terminology is secure. A Proto-Indo-European Earth figure paired with Sky is a plausible comparative reconstruction. Less secure are claims that every daughter tradition preserved the same genealogy, myth, or cult.

Gimbutas’s proposed continuity from Neolithic southeastern European goddess religion to historical Baltic goddesses is especially contested. Archaeological female figurines do not identify themselves as Žemyna, and similarity in fertility symbolism cannot bridge millennia without intermediate evidence.

### Grunau and the invented Prussian pantheon

Grunau’s triad and centralized Romowe establishment long shaped popular accounts of “Baltic mythology.” His alleged ancient sources are lost or likely invented, and his narrative contains demonstrable fabrication. He remains evidence for sixteenth-century historiography, not a reliable direct witness to an ancient Prussian system. [Cambridge repository study](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/b54273a7-071c-43ba-8b76-da5f56e3ed7f)

### The source archive

The essential modern corpus is Norbertas Vėlius’s four-volume **BRMŠ**, covering sources from antiquity through the eighteenth century in original languages and Lithuanian translation. The volumes appeared in 1996, 2001, 2003, and 2005. [Digital volumes and catalog](https://melc.lnb.lt/lt/content/44-baltu-religijos-ir-mitologijos-saltiniai)

Material survives in printed chronicles, clerical annual reports, dictionaries, antiquarian manuscripts, folklore archives, and song collections. Much indigenous pre-Christian oral material was never written down; losses through Christianization, warfare, linguistic assimilation, and the disappearance of Old Prussian make reconstruction intrinsically incomplete.

## 10. Dedicated Contested-Material Findings

- **Etymology:** The name is transparently related to Baltic “earth” and ultimately to an Indo-European earth word. A fully reconstructed Proto-Indo-European goddess biography is not thereby proved. **Tier:** Secure linguistics versus modern reconstruction.

- **Origin:** A pre-Christian earth cult is highly probable; Žemyna’s exact prehistoric form is unrecoverable. **Tier:** Modern reconstruction.

- **Syncretism with Ceres:** First a learned explanatory gloss, not indigenous self-identification. **Tier:** Seventeenth-century interpretatio classica.

- **Syncretism with Mary/Māra:** Christian Mary participates in the same ritual world in Prätorius; Latvian Māra’s relationship to Zemes māte remains historically layered and disputed. **Tier:** Christian syncretism plus modern systematization.

- **Marriage to Perkūnas:** Symbolically plausible as rain fertilizing Earth but not narrated in the early corpus. **Tier:** Modern scholarly/revival reconstruction.

- **Human sacrifice:** Broad Baltic human-sacrifice allegations exist, but none securely ties the practice to Žemyna. **Tier:** Unsupported deity-specific claim.

- **Temple cult:** No Žemyna temple is documented. **Tier:** Absence of evidence.

- **Temple prostitution:** No evidence. **Tier:** Modern fabrication if asserted.

- **Ancient image or sigil:** None securely identified. **Tier:** Modern invention where marketed as ancient.

- **Old Prussian Žemyna:** No direct Old Prussian attestation under that name. Prätorius’s Prussian-border material reflects Lithuanian-speaking and mixed regional populations. **Tier:** Unestablished.

- **Grunau’s Prussian triad:** Not an authentic framework into which Žemyna can safely be inserted. **Tier:** Early-modern learned invention.

- **Occult lineage:** No demonstrated Renaissance-to-modern occult transmission. **Tier:** Modern invention.

- **Continuous organized worship:** Household customs and folklore show continuity of motifs, but an uninterrupted institutional Žemyna church or priesthood is not documented. Romuva and Dievturība are modern revivals. **Tier:** Modern reconstruction based on historical survivals.

## 11. Chronology

- **Prehistory–first millennium CE:** Baltic earth vocabulary and agrarian ritual backgrounds develop. No named Žemyna attestation survives. **Evidence:** linguistic and archaeological reconstruction.

- **c. 1230–1283:** Crusading conquest and Christianization of Old Prussian territories. No direct Žemyna record. **Evidence:** medieval chronicles and documents.

- **1326:** Peter of Dusburg completes the *Chronicon terrae Prussiae*. It describes Prussian religion but not Žemyna by name. **Tier:** Context only.

- **1387:** Formal Christianization of Lithuania begins under Jogaila. **Tier:** Historical context.

- **1413–1417:** Formal Christianization of Samogitia. Household and agrarian observance persists afterward. **Tier:** Historical context.

- **Early sixteenth century:** Simon Grunau constructs an elaborate Prussian sacred history and triad. **Tier:** Unreliable learned systematization.

- **c. 1520s–1530s:** The *Sudovian Book* textual tradition develops. It is important for Prussian/Sudovian religion but does not securely attest Žemyna. **Tier:** Context.

- **1582:** Stryjkowski’s chronicle is published, naming masculine *Ziemiennik* and associating him with snakes, milk, and black hens. **Tier:** Comparative regional testimony.

- **c. 1582:** Łasicki composes *De diis Samagitarum*, naming *Zemina* as an earth goddess and associating her with growth. **Tier:** Earliest secure named attestation.

- **1599:** Daukša’s Lithuanian *Postilė* condemns reverence for *žemyna*, fire, snakes, and thunder. **Tier:** Secure printed corroboration.

- **c. 1605:** Anonymous Lithuanian catechetical evidence continues condemnation of earth cult. **Tier:** Early-modern testimony.

- **1615:** Łasicki’s work is first printed at Basel. **Tier:** Publication milestone.

- **1634:** Vilnius Jesuit report describes food buried to appease Žemyna as custodian of souls. **Tier:** Secure missionary testimony.

- **1653:** Daniel Klein’s *Grammatica Litvanica* glosses Žemyna as earth goddess/Ceres. **Tier:** Lexicographic attestation and classical equation.

- **Late seventeenth century:** Prätorius compiles *Deliciae Prussicae*, the fullest account of *žemynėliauti*, crop, livestock, wedding, birth, and funeral rites. **Tier:** Principal ethnographic testimony.

- **Late seventeenth century:** Anonymous vernacular material records beer poured to Žemynėlė and the prayer asking her to raise human labor. **Tier:** Independent ritual attestation.

- **c. 1740:** Brodowski records a prayer to Žemynėlė as flower-raiser and protector of food, drink, seed, and ploughing. **Tier:** Late survival.

- **1789:** Hupel reports Latvian earth-goddess offering sites and a black-cock sacrifice before 23 April. **Tier:** Latvian regional testimony.

- **Nineteenth century:** Lithuanian agrarian customs, laments, prayers, and earth taboos are increasingly collected as folklore. **Tier:** Folklore.

- **1894–1915:** Barons publishes *Latvju dainas*, preserving Zemes māte as grave keeper and receiver of the dead. **Tier:** Published folklore corpus.

- **Early twentieth century:** Comparative and agrarian-mythology scholarship systematizes Baltic source material. **Tier:** Modern scholarship.

- **1925:** Brastiņš and Marovskis-Bregžis publish the Dievturi revival manifesto. **Tier:** Modern religious invention/reconstruction.

- **1926:** Latvian Dievturi organization is registered. **Tier:** Modern revival.

- **1930:** A Lithuanian Romuva-associated community is founded, according to Romuva’s institutional history. **Tier:** Modern revival.

- **1940s:** Baltic revival organizations are broken up under Soviet occupation; Ernests Brastiņš is executed in 1942. **Tier:** Political suppression of modern revival.

- **1967:** Lithuanian *Ramuva* cultural-religious activity is organized under Soviet rule. **Tier:** Modern revival.

- **Late 1980s:** Dievturība and Lithuanian Baltic religious movements revive publicly during national reawakening. **Tier:** Modern revival.

- **1990:** Dievturība is officially registered again in Latvia. **Tier:** Modern institutional history.

- **1992:** Romuva is officially registered in independent Lithuania. **Tier:** Modern institutional history.

- **1996–2005:** Vėlius and collaborators publish the four volumes of *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai*. **Tier:** Critical source publication.

- **2011:** Laurinkienė publishes “Stones-Goddesses in Granaries,” reassessing household cult objects. **Tier:** Modern scholarship.

- **2013:** Laurinkienė publishes *Žemyna ir jos mitinis pasaulis*, the major dedicated synthesis. **Tier:** Modern scholarship.

- **Present:** Žemyna is honored in Romuva and individual reconstructed practice; Zemes māte remains active in Latvian folklore and Dievturi discourse. Claims to exact ritual continuity must be distinguished from conscious reconstruction. **Tier:** Living modern reception.

## 12. Sources and Research Assessment

The best-supported reconstruction is modest but substantial. Žemyna was an addressable, female-coded sacred Earth among Lithuanian-speaking rural communities, explicitly named from the late sixteenth century onward. By the seventeenth century she received beer, bread, grain products, food, and animal offerings at decisive agricultural and domestic transitions. She caused crops to flower, sustained animals and people, protected household prosperity, and received the dead. Her sanctuary was principally the ground itself.

Zemes māte is the Latvian cognate in function and language, documented most richly in late-collected funeral songs and eighteenth-century reports. She receives bodies and controls access to the grave, but her boundaries with Veļu māte, Kapu māte, Smilšu māte, Māra, and other maternal figures remain fluid.

What cannot be recovered is equally clear: no ancient scripture, canonical genealogy, anthropomorphic iconography, dedicated temple, securely named priesthood, epic mythology, divine marriage, human sacrifice, or occult symbol belongs to the documented early corpus. Later scholarship and revival religions have constructed fuller systems from genuine fragments. Those systems form an important part of Žemyna’s history, but they are the modern afterlife of the evidence, not substitutes for it.
