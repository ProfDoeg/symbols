# Saulė (Saule): Research Dossier

**Class:** Female solar deity/goddess in Lithuanian and Latvian tradition; in Old Prussian evidence, the sun is an object of worship, but a named, anthropomorphic goddess equivalent to Lithuanian *Saulė* or Latvian *Saule* is not securely documented.

**Earliest secure attestation:** A Lithuanian myth about the manufacture and liberation of the Sun occurs in the Baltic interpolation in the Slavonic translation of John Malalas’s *Chronographia*, conventionally dated 1261. It does not explicitly call the Sun “the goddess Saulė.” The earliest unambiguous documentary statement that a Baltic people worshipped the sun is Peter of Dusburg’s *Chronicon terrae Prussiae* of 1326, concerning the Old Prussians. The richly anthropomorphic goddess known as Saulė/Saule is documented principally in Lithuanian and Latvian folklore recorded from the eighteenth through twentieth centuries, especially nineteenth-century song collections. Earlier origin is probable on linguistic and comparative grounds but is a reconstruction, not direct documentation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Saulė—Latvian *Saule*—is simultaneously the ordinary Baltic word for the physical sun and the name of its feminine personification. In Latvian dainas she is one of the most frequently represented celestial beings: a shining woman, mother, landholder, traveler, weaver, bride or wife, and mistress of horses, chariots, sledges, and boats. Lithuanian songs, tales, beliefs, and charms preserve related but less abundant personification. A modern survey by Vaira Vīķe-Freiberga identifies roughly four thousand separately numbered Latvian sun songs, with more than two thousand primarily describing the observable or “physical” sun rather than an unambiguously divine personality.

That distinction matters. Every occurrence of grammatical *saule/saulė* is not necessarily evidence for a cultic goddess. Poetic personification, divine agency, and observation of the celestial body coexist in the same corpus and cannot always be separated.

### Names and forms

- **Lithuanian:** *Saulė*, pronounced approximately “sow-LAY”; the common noun and divine name are identical. Diminutives include *Saulutė*, *Saulytė*, *Saulelė*, and *Saulužė*. These mean approximately “little/dear Sun” and are affectionate poetic forms, not demonstrably separate goddesses.
- **Latvian:** *Saule*; affectionate *Saulīte* (“dear/little Sun”). In grammatical constructions: *Saules meita* or plural *Saules meitas*, “daughter/daughters of the Sun.”
- **Old Prussian:** *saule*, “sun,” is lexically cognate. Medieval evidence reports worship of *sol*, the sun, but supplies no secure indigenous personal name or mythology for an Old Prussian sun goddess.
- **Proto-Baltic reconstruction:** commonly *\*Sauliā* or a comparable feminine formation.
- **Proto-Indo-European comparison:** usually derived from the inherited solar noun reconstructed as *\*seh₂u̯el-*, *\*séh₂ul*, or an inflectional variant. Cognates include Latin *sōl*, Greek *hēlios*, Old Norse *sól*, Gothic *sauil*, and related Indo-Iranian forms. The exact ablaut, accentuation, and morphology remain technical questions, but the Baltic word’s Indo-European inheritance is not seriously disputed. The Lithuanian etymological database documents the long comparison with Latin, Greek, Germanic, Iranian, and Old Irish forms.

**Evidence tier — modern scholarly reconstruction:** A Proto-Indo-European female Sun deity is reconstructed from linguistic gender, inherited formulas, and comparative mythology. This is not an independently attested historical individual named Saulė. The Baltic, Germanic, Indic, Greek, and other solar figures should not be collapsed into one canon.

### Titles and poetic descriptions

The song corpus supplies descriptions rather than a fixed liturgical titulary:

- *Saulīte māmiņa* / “Mother Sun” or “dear Sun-mother”
- “golden Sun,” referring to radiance, dress, equipment, or celestial substance
- “white Sun,” especially in brightness formulas
- “dear little Sun”
- mistress or mother of the *Saules meitas*
- an orphan’s mother or protector in Latvian lyric usage
- celestial bride, wife, mother, or household mistress
- traveler over the “hill” or “mountain” of heaven
- wearer of a golden crown, wreath, shawl, shoes, or brooches

These are **folkloric poetic formulas**, not evidence for a centralized priestly cult or canonical list of epithets.

### Comparable and syncretistic identifications

No ancient Baltic interpretatio comparable to Roman equations of foreign gods is securely documented for Saulė. Early-modern authors sometimes translated Baltic deities into classical categories, but no stable equation such as “Saulė = Sol” or “Saulė = Apollo” constituted an indigenous doctrine.

Christian-era songs and customs could place solar imagery beside the Virgin Mary, Christ, St John, St Peter, or other saints. This is **folkloric and Christian-period accommodation**, not proof that Mary or a saint was simply the pagan goddess under another name.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The 1261 Malalas interpolation

A Slavonic manuscript of John Malalas’s chronicle contains a Baltic interpolation dated to 1261. It reports that people once worshipped the sun, moon, and other powers and relates how a powerful smith named Teliavelis forged the Sun. A king had imprisoned it in a tower; the zodiacal signs broke the tower with a great hammer and released the Sun into the sky.

**What it establishes:** A thirteenth-century Lithuanian-connected source knew a myth in which the Sun could be made, imprisoned, and liberated, and in which celestial or calendrical signs acted collectively.

**What it does not establish:** It does not present the mature Latvian daina portrait of a goddess, name Saulė as Teliavelis’s daughter, describe a temple, or provide the later marriage-and-infidelity story.

**Tier:** securely attested medieval source, but preserved through a Christian Slavonic chronicle and therefore not a transcript of an intact pagan scripture.

### Peter of Dusburg, 1326

Peter’s Latin *Chronicon terrae Prussiae* says the Prussians, because they did not know the Christian God, worshipped creation: “the sun, moon, and stars,” thunder, birds, quadrupeds, and even the toad. His report is the earliest explicit documentary statement of Baltic solar worship.

Peter does not:

- use an indigenous form *Saulė/Saule*;
- call the sun female;
- supply a myth, image, priesthood, sacrifice, or sanctuary specifically belonging to it.

His list is shaped by Christian polemical categories. Recent historiography nevertheless cautions against treating everything he reports as fiction: the account combines observed or reported particulars with biblical and clerical models.

### Simon Grunau, c. 1517–1530

Grunau’s *Preussische Chronik* says that the earliest Prussians honored the sun before the Cimbri introduced the three images Patollo, Potrimpo, and Perkuno. This is **early-modern systematization**. His triad is not independently confirmed as the organizing trinity of Old Prussian religion; modern scholarship treats much of Grunau’s detailed pantheon, priestly hierarchy, banners, idols, and pseudo-ancient narrative as invention or creative antiquarian construction.

Consequently:

- his statement about solar honor may preserve or repeat older reporting;
- his chronological tale of primitive solar religion followed by a Cimbrian triad is not reliable religious history;
- the triad must not be placed in Saulė’s genealogy.

### The *Sudovian Book*

The anonymous German *Sudauer Büchlein*, compiled in the sixteenth century and surviving in variant manuscripts and editions, presents a catalog of Sudovian/Prussian gods and ritual descriptions. Its testimony is valuable as evidence of what Reformation-era clerics believed or claimed about local practice, but its divine list is partly dependent on other learned compilations and does not give a securely independent Saulė dossier.

**Finding:** No named, anthropomorphic Old Prussian sun goddess with the Latvian narrative complex can be recovered from the *Sudovian Book*. Claims that it supplies one are modern harmonization.

### Stryjkowski, Lasicki, and later ethnographic writers

- **Maciej Stryjkowski, 1582:** His Polish-language chronicle lists Lithuanian and neighboring religious customs and gods. It belongs to Renaissance antiquarian historiography, not direct pagan testimony. Solar rites or calendrical customs mentioned in this milieu require individual source criticism; Stryjkowski does not securely document a temple of Saulė.
- **Jan Łasicki/Lasicki:** *De diis Samagitarum caeterorumque Sarmatarum et falsorum Christianorum*, written from late-sixteenth-century materials and printed in 1615, lists Samogitian divinities and practices. Its very short notices, often dependent on the Protestant surveyor Jakub Łaszkowski, are crucial but difficult: spellings and functions are unstable, and later mythographers frequently constructed more coherent pantheons from them than the text warrants.
- **Jesuit annual letters and mission reports, late sixteenth–seventeenth centuries:** These document continuing offerings, sacred fires, household and agricultural rites, divination, veneration of natural places, and clerical campaigns against practices classified as pagan. They provide important evidence for religious persistence after formal Christianization. They do not reveal a surviving institutional priesthood or temple dedicated specifically to Saulė.
- **Matthäus Praetorius, late seventeenth century:** His *Deliciae Prussicae oder Preussische Schaubühne*, written in manuscript and published much later, records beliefs and rites in Prussian Lithuania. It combines observation, hearsay, antiquarian comparison, and systematization. It is evidence for seventeenth-century folk religion and its scholarly framing, not a transparent window into pre-conquest religion.

The principal modern documentary collection is Norbertas Vėlius’s four-volume *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai* (1996–2005), which prints sources from antiquity through the eighteenth century with translations and commentary. Its breadth is indispensable, but inclusion does not certify every source as equally accurate.

### Lithuanian songs

The best-known Lithuanian text is “*Mėnuo Saulužę vedė*” (“The Moon married the dear Sun”), printed by Liudvikas Rėza in *Dainos, oder Litthauische Volkslieder* in 1825:

> Mėnuo Saulužę vedė  
> Pirmą pavasarėlį.  
> Saulužė anksti kėlės,  
> Mėnužis atsiskyrė.  
> Mėnuo viens vaikštinėjo,  
> Aušrinę pamylėjo.  
> Perkūns, didžiai supykęs,  
> Jį kardu perdalijo.

“The Moon married the dear Sun / in the first spring. / The Sun rose early; / the Moon went apart. / The Moon wandered alone / and loved Aušrinė. / Perkūnas, greatly angered, / split him with a sword.”

**Contested authenticity:** This song is culturally foundational, but Lithuanian folklorists have questioned Rėza’s editing. Its compact myth resembles abundant Latvian material more closely than most independently collected Lithuanian songs. Rėza may have regularized, combined, or supplemented texts according to early nineteenth-century editorial practice. It must therefore be labeled **nineteenth-century recorded folklore, textually suspect in its received form**, not quoted as an uncontaminated prehistoric hymn.

### Latvian dainas

Krišjānis Barons and Henrijs Visendorfs published *Latvju dainas* in six volumes between 1894 and 1915. The collection drew upon manuscripts gathered principally in the nineteenth century. The Dainu skapis preserves the paper slips and variants that underlie the edition.

The songs are normally short quatrains, not continuous epics. Modern “myths” of Saule are scholarly reconstructions made by placing hundreds of motifs and variants beside one another. The corpus securely documents recurring traditional formulas, but it rarely supplies a single complete narrative with beginning, middle, and end.

Vīķe-Freiberga and Imants Freibergs published a major selected corpus, *Saules dainas / Latvian Sun Songs* (1988; revised 1991). Vīķe-Freiberga’s *Trejādās saules* series—*Kosmoloģiskā saule* (1997), *Hronoloģiskā saule* (1999), meteorological volumes, and later mythological analysis—maps the semantic layers of the corpus.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**Folklore, securely recorded in modern collections:**

- daylight, warmth, ripening, and the annual solar cycle;
- the daily passage across heaven;
- sympathy with laborers, shepherds, orphans, brides, and the unfortunate;
- celestial domestic work: weaving, spinning, washing, dressing, tending daughters and horses;
- calendrical turning points, especially midsummer and midwinter;
- the border between this world and *viņsaule*, literally the “other/beyond-sun,” associated in Latvian usage with the realm beyond death.

“Goddess of fertility,” “goddess of life,” or “supreme Baltic goddess” are useful modern summaries only when qualified. No surviving Baltic theological treatise assigns her an official departmental portfolio.

### Appearance and equipment

In Latvian songs, Saule is an anthropomorphic woman whose visible radiance is expressed through gold, silver, white, and red:

- golden crown or wreath;
- golden shawl, belt, shoes, rings, and brooches;
- garments woven with gold or silver thread;
- golden or shining hair;
- two or more horses, often yellow, bay, golden, or silver;
- a chariot or wagon by day;
- a sledge in winter imagery;
- a golden, silver, or reed boat at sunset or during the nocturnal journey.

The color red is particularly associated with sunset, berries, fish, and the evening sky. These are variable poetic images, not a canonical costume.

### The sun’s boat

The boat is one of the strongest Latvian solar motifs. Saule descends toward or into the western sea, sometimes washes or swims her horses, enters a golden boat, and crosses the dark water. In some songs the Dieva dēli row or rescue the sinking Sun or her daughters.

Nijolė Laurinkienė’s study of the solar wagon and boat argues that the two vehicles encode phases of the daily journey. Comparisons with Bronze Age Nordic and Central European sun-ship imagery are suggestive.

**Tier:** the daina boat is securely attested folklore. Its direct derivation from a particular Bronze Age artifact or a continuous Bronze Age “solar cult” is a **modern comparative reconstruction**, not documentary fact.

### Apples and round solar objects

Latvian songs and later interpretations compare or associate the Sun with a golden apple, golden acorn, red berry, rose, ball, or disk. Sometimes the object is in Saule’s hand or belongs to her daughters; sometimes it is a metaphor for the orb itself.

**Finding:** There is no single canonical myth in which an invariant set of “Saulė’s sacred apples” performs a fixed action. “The apples of Saulė” is a motif cluster assembled from poetic variants. Claims that she possesses magical apples equivalent to the Norse Iðunn’s are unsupported.

### Sacred animals and plants

- Horses are her principal animals and agents of motion.
- Birds, fish, and waterfowl can accompany solar-sea imagery, but no one species is universally “Saulė’s sacred animal.”
- Linden, birch, oak, apple tree, rose bush, and other plants occur in solar songs. Their participation varies by song and cannot be reduced to a single sacred tree.
- Solar wheels, rosettes, concentric circles, and radiating crosses are common in Baltic folk ornament.

**Iconographic caution:** A circular rosette or wheel is not automatically an image of the goddess. Prehistoric Baltic solar symbols cannot ordinarily be identified with the named Saulė without accompanying inscriptions. There is no securely identified pre-Christian statue, cult image, or temple relief of Saulė.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

There is no single canonical family tree.

### Competing genealogies

1. **Saule as wife of Mēness/Mėnulis:** Found in Latvian song traditions and the famous Rėza text. Their estrangement explains why sun and moon usually occupy different parts of the sky.
2. **Sun and Moon as siblings or kin:** Lithuanian belief texts sometimes call them brother and sister or simply relatives.
3. **Parents of stars, Earth, or celestial daughters:** Late folklore and scholarly syntheses variously call the stars, Earth, Aušrinė, Vakarinė, or named planets their children. No stable ancient list exists.
4. **Mother of the Saules meitas:** Abundantly represented in Latvian dainas. The daughters’ number is unstable; singular and plural alternate.
5. **Sun-daughter and Saule conflated:** In celestial-wedding songs it is sometimes impossible to determine whether the bride is Saule herself or a *Saules meita*. Scholarly retellings often harmonize distinct variants.
6. **Relationship to Dievs:** Saule may participate in Dievs’s celestial household, attend feasts, or interact with the Dieva dēli. She is not securely his wife in every version.

### Documented divine interactions

Each line below represents an attested relationship or episode, not merely membership in the same reconstructed pantheon.

- **Teliavelis — manufactures the Sun.** In the 1261 Malalas interpolation, the smith Teliavelis forges the Sun. The text does not explicitly make Saulė his daughter. **Tier: securely attested medieval Lithuanian mythic notice.**
- **The zodiacal signs — liberate the Sun.** In the same source, the signs break a tower with a great hammer and release the imprisoned Sun. They are a divine or astral collective rather than named individual gods. **Tier: securely attested medieval notice.**
- **Mėnulis/Mėnuo — spouse, estranged partner, or relative.** The Moon marries Saulė but separates from her; elsewhere the two are siblings or disputing cosmic custodians. Source: Lithuanian folklore, especially Rėza 1825 and later belief records. **Tier: folklore; Rėza text contested.**
- **Mēness — spouse or participant in a celestial wedding.** Latvian dainas distribute the wedding roles inconsistently between Saule, a Sun-daughter, Mēness, Auseklis, and the sons of Dievs. **Tier: regional Latvian folklore.**
- **Aušrinė — rival, daughter, or servant.** In the Rėza song Mėnuo loves Aušrinė after separating from Saulė. Other Lithuanian traditions make Aušrinė Saulė’s daughter or morning servant who prepares her way. **Tier: folklore; relationships vary, and the adultery text is contested.**
- **Vakarinė — evening attendant or daughter.** Lithuanian folklore makes the Evening Star prepare Saulė’s bed or receive her at day’s end; some modern genealogies call her a daughter. **Tier: folklore and modern systematization.**
- **Perkūnas — avenger against Mėnulis.** In Rėza’s 1825 song, Perkūnas angrily splits the Moon with a sword after his love of Aušrinė. Later retellings make this a punishment for adultery. **Tier: contested recorded folklore; later canonical popularization.**
- **Pērkons — thunderer in the celestial wedding complex.** Latvian dainas associate Pērkons with disrupted weddings, damaged celestial trees, or punishment, but individual roles vary. **Tier: Latvian regional folklore; no single canonical episode.**
- **Dievs — host, neighbor, or superior household figure.** Saule goes to Dievs’s feast or participates in the ordered celestial farmstead in Latvian songs. **Tier: folklore.**
- **Dieva dēli — suitors, rowers, and rescuers.** The sons of Dievs court the Saules meitas and in some dainas row a golden boat to assist or rescue Saule or a sinking Sun-daughter. **Tier: Latvian folklore.**
- **Saules meita/meitas — daughters under Saule’s care.** They are dressed, married, courted, lost, drowned, or rescued in variant songs. Their identities and number remain unstable. **Tier: Latvian folklore.**
- **Auseklis — suitor to a Sun-daughter.** The Morning Star courts or participates in the wedding of a *Saules meita* in Latvian song variants. Direct marriage to Saule herself is not uniformly attested. **Tier: regional folklore.**
- **Laima — joint presence at life-cycle or wedding scenes.** Saule and Laima can occur within the same lyric and ritual world, especially in songs of fate, birth, marriage, and women’s lives. A fixed genealogical tie or major shared narrative is not securely attested. **Tier: folkloric co-occurrence.**
- **Saint John/Jānis — calendrical conjunction, not ancient genealogy.** Saule’s annual course is celebrated in songs and customs of Jāņi/Joninės. Jānis is a Christian saint and folkloric midsummer figure, not securely an ancient husband or son of Saule. **Tier: Christian-period syncretic folklore.**
- **Saint Peter — late calendrical or narrative substitution.** Some Christianized songs transfer actions involving celestial actors to St Peter. **Tier: late folkloric accommodation.**

**Absence-of-evidence finding:** No reliable primary source documents Saulė meeting Grunau’s Patollo or Potrimpo, the *Sudovian Book* gods, or a supposedly pan-Baltic council of twelve Olympian-style gods.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The forged and liberated Sun

The 1261 interpolation says a mighty smith forged the Sun. A ruler imprisoned it in a tower, after which the zodiacal signs acquired a huge hammer, shattered the tower, and returned the light to the sky.

This story supplies a technological cosmology: sunlight is imagined as metalwork, while the hammer and tower dramatize concealment and release. Later Lithuanian tales of a smith forging or placing the sun may preserve related imagery, but dependence, continuity, or independent survival must be tested text by text.

**Development:** Scholars have associated the metallic Sun with the prestige of smithcraft and the spread of metal technologies. That historical interpretation is plausible but remains reconstruction. The tale does not prove a temple cult of Teliavelis and Saulė.

### Marriage of Sun and Moon

The Rėza song gives the most familiar compact narrative: Moon marries Sun in spring; Sun rises early; Moon separates, wanders alone, and loves Aušrinė; Perkūnas splits him.

Popular retellings explain:

- lunar phases as the wound inflicted by Perkūnas;
- day and night as the estranged spouses’ separate custody of the world;
- eclipses as meetings or conflicts;
- morning Venus as the third party in a celestial triangle.

Only the first compact sequence occurs in the famous song. The explanatory extensions come from other folklore or modern synthesis.

**Competing versions:**

- Sun and Moon are spouses.
- They are siblings.
- They quarrel because they cannot divide care of Earth.
- They alternate watching their child Earth.
- They quarrel because they have no children.
- Moon’s offense is love of Aušrinė.
- The celestial bride is not Saule but her daughter.
- Pērkons disrupts a wedding involving other celestial parties.

No version can responsibly be declared the original canonical myth.

### Saule and the Sun-daughters

In Latvian dainas Saule’s daughters inhabit a celestial household, wash or bathe, spin, weave, tend gardens, adorn themselves, and participate in weddings. Their suitors include the Dieva dēli, Mēness, or Auseklis. A daughter may lose a ring or wreath in the sea, drown, or require rescue.

The singular *Saules meita* can resemble the Indo-European “Sun Maiden” reconstructed by comparativists, but the Latvian corpus also presents several daughters. “The mother of a fixed pair of solar twins” is therefore too definite.

### The daily celestial journey

At morning, attendants or stars prepare the way. Saule mounts the heavenly hill in a wagon drawn by shining horses. At evening she descends toward the sea, washes her horses, hangs her crown in a tree, and enters a golden boat. She may cross the nocturnal waters to return east, rest on an island, or pass into *viņsaule*.

The texts do not agree on a single mechanism. Wagon, walking, horse riding, boat, sleep, and uninterrupted motion are parallel images. Their coexistence is evidence of a living poetic tradition, not a defective cosmology requiring harmonization.

### The golden apple

Round golden objects can stand for the Sun or its transferable brilliance. Saule or her daughters may cast, hold, roll, or lose such an object. The motif participates in wedding, game, and cosmological imagery.

**Finding:** Modern descriptions of a single “myth of Saulė’s apples” exaggerate the narrative unity of the sources. The authentic evidence is a constellation of brief daina formulas.

### Saule and the dead

Latvian expressions contrast *šī saule*, “this sun/world,” with *viņsaule*, the “other sun/world.” Funeral songs and cosmological texts allow the setting Sun’s westward passage to image death and the otherworld.

The conclusion that the dead literally travel in Saule’s boat is a scholarly reconstruction from distributed motifs. It is stronger to say that solar travel and death share a spatial-poetic vocabulary.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Material record

No excavated sanctuary can presently be identified securely as a temple of Saulė. No dedicatory inscription addressed to *Saulė/Saule* and no authenticated cult statue survive. Solar disks, wheel pendants, rosettes, horse imagery, boats, and astronomical orientations are insufficient by themselves to identify her.

Peter of Dusburg’s 1326 statement establishes Old Prussian veneration of the sun but gives no solar cult center or priesthood. Grunau’s elaborate central sanctuary at Romowe and its triad belong to a much less reliable account and do not center on Saulė.

### Prayer and ordinary observance

Folklore records respectful conduct toward the Sun:

- greeting sunrise;
- avoiding indecent gestures toward it;
- timing work, washing, burial, and household activity by sunrise and sunset;
- prayers or lyric addresses asking for warmth, blessing, health, or protection;
- regarding the Sun as seeing human conduct;
- lamenting when clouds, winter, or night conceal it.

These practices are **ethnographic folklore**, usually recorded after Christianization. They may preserve older attitudes, but their precise pre-Christian form cannot be dated.

### Sacrifice and offerings

No primary source securely documents human sacrifice to Saulė. Baltic sources do contain allegations or reports of killing prisoners, funerary sacrifice, animal sacrifice, and ritual offerings in other settings, but attributing them to a solar-goddess cult would exceed the evidence.

Similarly, there is no evidence for “temple prostitution” connected with Saulė.

Food, beer, wreaths, flowers, fire, and symbolic offerings occur in modern Romuva and Dievturība ceremonies and in seasonal custom. Their current dedication specifically to Saulė is evidence of revival practice, not automatic proof of an unchanged ancient rite.

### Rasos, Joninės, Jāņi, and Līgo

Lithuanian midsummer is variously named *Rasos*, *Kupolės*, *Joninės*, and related regional terms; Latvian midsummer is *Jāņi*, with *Līgo* refrains and associated terminology. Customs include:

- hilltop bonfires;
- rolling or raising fiery wheels;
- wreath making and divination;
- singing through the short night;
- gathering plants believed especially potent at midsummer;
- bathing or collecting dew;
- searching for the legendary fern blossom;
- greeting the rising Sun;
- dancing, feasting, and courtship.

These rites are solar in their seasonal setting, but their surviving form is layered. St John’s feast supplied Christian names and chronology; nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalism, folklore performance, tourism, and neopaganism further reshaped them.

**Saulė’s status:** She is prominent in songs about the height, warmth, lateness, or turning of the Sun. It is misleading to describe all Jāņi or Rasos ritual as a documented ancient liturgy “to the goddess Saulė.”

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Proto-Baltic and Indo-European background

The inherited word and its cognates make a Proto-Baltic solar concept certain and a personified female Sun plausible. Comparative formulas involving the Sun’s daughters, divine twins/sons, horses, wheels, and celestial weddings may preserve Indo-European inheritance.

**Tier:** linguistic and comparative reconstruction. No Proto-Baltic hymn or image bears Saulė’s name.

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries

- **1261:** The Malalas interpolation records the forged and liberated Sun.
- **1326:** Peter of Dusburg explicitly reports Old Prussian sun worship.
- **Fourteenth century onward:** Christianization and political incorporation transformed public religion. Lithuania formally adopted Christianity in 1387; Samogitia followed in the early fifteenth century. These events did not instantly eliminate household custom or oral cosmology.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

Renaissance chroniclers, Protestant polemicists, Catholic missionaries, and local antiquarians recorded gods and rites while suppressing what they regarded as idolatry or superstition. The sources are indispensable but often:

- impose classical pantheon structures;
- interpret agricultural custom as demon worship;
- conflate Prussian, Lithuanian, Samogitian, and neighboring traditions;
- provide names without narratives;
- report under conditions of conversion and confessional conflict.

Saulė does not emerge from these texts as the head of a centralized temple system.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: folklore collection

Under Russian imperial rule and Baltic German cultural dominance, antiquarians and national intellectuals collected songs, tales, beliefs, and customs. Rėza’s 1825 Lithuanian collection and the great Latvian collecting movement converted oral, local, performative materials into printed national archives.

This was both preservation and transformation. Editors selected, normalized, combined, and categorized texts. The modern image of Saulė owes much to this archival era.

### 1894–1915: *Latvju dainas*

Barons and Visendorfs’s edition made the daina corpus available at unprecedented scale. Saule’s daily work, clothing, family, travel, and emotional life could now be studied as a vast motif system. The publication did not create the songs, but classification encouraged scholars to reconstruct coherent mythology from fragments.

### Twentieth-century scholarship

- **Haralds Biezais** treated the Latvian celestial gods and solar tradition systematically, emphasizing the richness of the dainas.
- **Marija Gimbutas** placed Baltic goddesses within a broad theory of prehistoric “Old European” religion. Her documentation of Baltic folklore remains influential, while her claims for a widespread matrifocal goddess civilization and direct prehistoric continuity are disputed.
- **Algirdas Julien Greimas**, especially in *Of Gods and Men*, used structuralist comparison to reconstruct Lithuanian divine systems. His readings are intellectually powerful but sometimes create systematic relations exceeding explicit source statements.
- **Norbertas Vėlius** combined folklore, historical sources, and spatial or structural models of Baltic mythology.
- **Gintaras Beresnevičius** developed historical-comparative interpretations of Baltic religion and the afterlife.
- **Vaira Vīķe-Freiberga** analyzed the Latvian sun-song corpus with close attention to formulas, semantics, and poetic structure.

### Survival and displacement

The institutional pre-Christian cult disappeared. Solar imagery survived through language, work rhythms, songs, charms, funeral vocabulary, textiles, carvings, seasonal festivals, and Christianized calendars. This is cultural continuity with extensive transformation, not proof of an unbroken secret priesthood.

### Soviet period and revival

Public religious organization was restricted under Soviet rule, while officially acceptable folklore ensembles and ethnographic study could preserve seasonal materials. In Lithuania, the Ramuva cultural movement organized a midsummer celebration at Kernavė in 1967. Romuva registered as a religious community in 1992 and received Lithuanian state recognition in 2024.

In Latvia, Dievturība was systematized by Ernests Brastiņš and Kārlis Bregžis in 1925. Organizations were registered in 1926 and 1927. Soviet occupation destroyed open institutional practice; émigré communities continued it abroad, and revival in Latvia accelerated from the late 1980s.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Dievturība

The Latvian National Encyclopedia explicitly classifies Dievturība as a **newly created tradition whose founders, doctrines, and development are historically known**, notwithstanding practitioners’ claim to restore ancient Baltic religion.

Its main landmarks include:

- Brastiņš and Bregžis, *Latviešu dievturības atjaunojums* (1925);
- Brastiņš’s *Dievtuŗu cerokslis*, a catechism-like systematization (1932);
- interwar congregations and publications;
- suppression during occupation;
- diaspora continuation;
- Latvian revival from the late Soviet period onward.

Saule occupies a major place because the dainas offer unusually abundant primary material. Dievturi prayers, calendars, ritual art, and theological ordering are nevertheless twentieth- and twenty-first-century constructions.

### Romuva

Modern Lithuanian Romuva draws on folk song, sacred fire, seasonal festivals, historical sources, archaeology, and scholarly reconstruction. Its institutional antecedents include interwar initiatives, the 1967 Ramuva cultural movement, and post-1990 registration.

Modern Rasos ceremonies greeting the Sun, passing through ritual gates, floating wreaths, and tending sacred fire are authentic contemporary religious acts. Individual elements may be traditional; the integrated liturgy is modern reconstruction and revival.

Romuva’s own historical narratives sometimes accept the medieval Romowe sanctuary, chief priest *krivis*, or very early foundation dates more confidently than critical historiography permits.

### Nationalism and politics

Both Lithuanian and Latvian national movements used solar songs and ornaments as emblems of linguistic survival, land, cyclical renewal, and cultural antiquity. Saule consequently became:

- a national-cultural mother image;
- a visual emblem in folk art and public design;
- part of the symbolism of independence and post-Soviet recovery;
- a subject of choral, textile, theatrical, and monumental art.

National use is not inherently extremist. However, Baltic neopagan and “Indo-European” imagery can also be recruited into ethnic-essentialist or racial narratives. No evidence makes such ideologies intrinsic to the historical Saulė tradition; each modern appropriation must be documented separately.

### Art, music, and literature

Solar motifs pervade Baltic textiles, roof finials, jewelry, paintings, song festivals, and national design. A notable modern Latvian work is “Saule, Pērkons, Daugava,” with music by Mārtiņš Brauns and text taken from Rainis’s 1916 drama *Daugava*. It is sometimes mistaken online for an ancient folk song; it is a modern choral composition using national-mythic language.

Contemporary fantasy, games, goddess spirituality, and internet occultism commonly present Saulė as a fully systematized “Great Mother,” assign her standardized crystals, tarot correspondences, chakras, planetary hours, or Wiccan sabbats, and give her fixed orange/gold altar prescriptions.

**Tier: modern invention.** No early Baltic text supplies those occult correspondences. Nor is there evidence that Saulė belonged to the Golden Dawn, Renaissance Hermetism, a surviving witch cult, or a historical pan-European goddess order.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Goddess, personification, or celestial body?

The evidence supports all three descriptions in different contexts. Grammatical gender alone does not prove cult, but explicitly anthropomorphic dainas make mere grammatical personification too reductive. Conversely, reading every meteorological mention as theology inflates the evidence.

The sound conclusion is that *Saule/Saulė* ranges across:

1. the observable sun;
2. conventional poetic personification;
3. a mythic female agent;
4. an object of reverence;
5. in modern revival, a consciously worshipped goddess.

### Age of the dainas

Dainas can preserve archaic vocabulary, meter, formulas, and comparative motifs. Individual texts, however, were recorded largely in the modern period and cannot automatically be dated to the Bronze Age or even to pre-Christian centuries.

Comparative parallels may reveal inheritance, borrowing, convergent imagery, or later scholarly selection. They do not date a specific quatrain.

### Authenticity of “Mėnuo Saulužę vedė”

This is the central textual controversy. The song’s presence in Rėza’s 1825 collection is certain; its exact oral antecedent is not. Modern educational and popular sources acknowledge that Rėza freely edited texts and may have incorporated Latvian mythic material. It should remain in the dossier because it has profoundly shaped Lithuanian cultural memory, but it cannot carry the full weight often placed upon it.

### Biezais and reconstructed celestial mythology

Biezais treated recurring daina complexes as evidence for Latvian celestial religion. Critics warn that thousands of short songs recorded in different places and periods cannot always be assembled into one coherent narrative. The disagreement is methodological: whether patterned recurrence permits reconstruction of a religious system, and how much contradiction such a reconstruction may absorb.

### Gimbutas

Gimbutas made Baltic materials central to her theory of an Old European goddess civilization. Her broad claims about matrifocal social organization, prehistoric script, and continuous Great-Goddess religion remain controversial and are often rejected in their strongest form. Archaeological solar symbols cannot be assigned automatically to Saulė.

### Greimas and Vėlius

Structuralists identify oppositions—upper/lower, celestial/chthonic, morning/evening, life/death—and reconstruct transformations among divine figures. Such analyses can illuminate patterns invisible in isolated sources. Their reconstructed “system,” however, must not be quoted as if it were itself a medieval primary text.

### Old Prussian evidence

Old Prussian religion is especially vulnerable to retrospective harmonization. Peter of Dusburg mentions sun worship but no Saulė. The *Sudovian Book* and Grunau provide elaborate but problematic early-modern constructions. The prudent formulation is:

> Old Prussians worshipped or revered the sun; their language possessed the cognate *saule*. The surviving evidence does not let us reconstruct a named Old Prussian goddess with the full Lithuanian-Latvian family and narrative.

### Cult violence and sacrifice

The contested-material search produced no reliable evidence of:

- human sacrifice specifically to Saulė;
- sexual rites or temple prostitution in her cult;
- a solar priestess-queen ruling a Baltic theocracy;
- an ancient exclusive women’s mystery cult;
- a surviving underground cult continuous to modern Romuva or Dievturība.

These are absences in the present record, not demonstrations that no undocumented act ever occurred.

## 10. Chronology

- **Proto-Indo-European period, reconstructed:** Inherited solar noun *\*seh₂u̯el- / \*séh₂ul* and possible female solar personification. **Evidence: linguistic-comparative reconstruction.**
- **Proto-Baltic period, reconstructed:** Development of the common Baltic solar word behind Lithuanian *saulė*, Latvian *saule*, and Old Prussian *saule*. No written mythology survives.
- **1261:** Baltic interpolation in the Slavonic Malalas chronicle records Teliavelis forging the Sun and the zodiacal signs liberating it from a tower.
- **1326:** Peter of Dusburg reports that Old Prussians worship the sun, moon, stars, thunder, animals, and other parts of creation.
- **1387:** Formal Christianization of Lithuania; pre-Christian public institutions displaced, while local practice continues unevenly.
- **Early fifteenth century:** Formal Christianization campaigns in Samogitia.
- **c. 1517–1530:** Simon Grunau composes his Prussian chronicle, claiming primitive solar worship and inventively systematizing a Prussian triad.
- **Sixteenth century:** The *Sudovian Book* and related synodal texts catalog supposedly Prussian/Sudovian gods and rites; no secure Saule mythology emerges.
- **1582:** Stryjkowski publishes his chronicle with Baltic religious notices.
- **1615:** Lasicki’s *De diis Samagitarum* is printed from late-sixteenth-century material.
- **Late sixteenth–seventeenth centuries:** Jesuit reports document suppression and persistence of rites labeled pagan or superstitious.
- **Late seventeenth century:** Praetorius compiles *Deliciae Prussicae*.
- **1825:** Rėza publishes *Dainos*, including “Mėnuo Saulužę vedė”; later scholarship questions the text’s editorial purity.
- **Nineteenth century:** Large-scale Latvian and Lithuanian folklore collection records solar songs, beliefs, riddles, and customs.
- **1894–1915:** Barons and Visendorfs publish *Latvju dainas*.
- **1925:** Brastiņš and Bregžis issue the Dievturība restoration manifesto.
- **1926–1927:** Latvian Dievturi organizations receive registration.
- **1932:** Brastiņš publishes *Dievtuŗu cerokslis*, systematizing revival theology.
- **1940s:** Soviet and Nazi occupations disrupt Baltic religious and cultural organizations; Dievturība continues partly in exile.
- **1967:** Lithuanian Ramuva-associated cultural activists celebrate Rasos at Kernavė, a landmark in modern revival.
- **1970s–1990s:** Biezais, Gimbutas, Greimas, Vėlius, Beresnevičius, and others reshape scholarly and popular understanding.
- **1988:** Vaira Vīķe-Freiberga and Imants Freibergs publish *Saules dainas / Latvian Sun Songs*.
- **Late 1980s:** Open Dievturība revival resumes in Latvia.
- **1992:** Romuva is officially registered in Lithuania.
- **1996–2005:** Vėlius’s four-volume *Baltų religijos ir mitologijos šaltiniai* appears.
- **1997–2010s:** Vīķe-Freiberga’s *Trejādās saules* studies provide a detailed thematic analysis of Latvian sun songs.
- **2017:** Laurinkienė publishes a focused study of the solar wagon and boat.
- **2024:** Lithuania grants Romuva state recognition.
- **Present:** Saulė/Saule remains a figure of folklore scholarship, seasonal custom, national art, Romuva and Dievturība worship, popular spirituality, and internet reinvention. No evidence establishes an uninterrupted institutional cult from the medieval period to today.
