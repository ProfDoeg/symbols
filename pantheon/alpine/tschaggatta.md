# The Tschäggättä: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Collective carnival figures enacted by masked human performers; in local documentation they are *Fasnachtsgestalten* (“carnival figures”), not gods, goddesses, demons, or independently worshipped spirits. Singular: **Tschäggätta**; plural: **Tschäggättä**.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Prior Johann Baptist Gibsten’s manuscript parish chronicle at Kippel, written during the early part of his tenure beginning in **1864**. It describes people “called Tschäggättä” wearing wooden masks, horns, and skins during Carnival. A museum mask attributed to approximately **1790** may be older material evidence, but its retrospective dating is less secure than Gibsten’s contemporary written testimony.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### What the Tschäggättä are

**[Securely attested historical practice]** The Tschäggättä belong specifically to the Lötschental in German-speaking Upper Valais, Switzerland—especially the villages of Blatten, Wiler, Kippel, and Ferden. A performer covers the body with sheep- or goatskins, wears an oversized carved wooden face-mask, pads the shoulders, puts on coarse woollen gloves, and carries or wears a large cattle bell. Traditionally the masked figures ran individually or in small, fluid groups through the villages during the Catholic Carnival season.

“The Tschäggättä” does not denote a singular supernatural individual. Nor does the evidence support placing them in a Swiss–Tyrolean–Bavarian–Austrian “pantheon.” Comparisons with Austrian and Bavarian Perchten and Krampus runs are legitimate comparative folklore; identity with Perchta, Krampus, a Wild Hunt deity, or any pre-Christian god is not documented.

**[Folklore and ritual classification]** In performance, a Tschäggätta is temporarily treated as a frightening, transgressive, quasi-monstrous presence. The performer’s anonymity is central, but nothing in the early record demonstrates belief in an autonomous being that exists apart from its human bearer.

### Names and grammar

- **Tschäggätta**: feminine singular in Lötschental/Upper Valais usage.
- **Tschäggättä**: plural.
- **Tschäggete / Tschäggätä**: spelling variants reflecting dialect rather than separate beings.
- **Roitschäggätta / Roitschäggättä**, also written **Roichtschäggete(n)**: a qualified form used for the dark, soot-associated masked figure.
- **Tschäggättu**: dialectal verbal or nominalized usage for “doing/playing Tschäggättä,” encountered in accounts of the custom.
- **Larve**: the conventional German-speaking Alpine term for the face-mask. The mask alone is not properly “a Tschäggätta”; the complete costumed figure is.
- **Triichla**: the large cattle bell.
- **Triämhändsche**: coarse hand-knitted gloves, often made from yarn remnants.
- **Gigiszischtag**: the local name for Shrove Tuesday, the final day of the customary season.

### Etymology

**[Regional linguistic explanation]** The Upper Valais dialect dictionary derives *Tschäggäta* from **tschäggeren**, “to work wood untidily or roughly.” This suits the deliberately coarse carving traditionally associated with the masks. It is a plausible dialect etymology, not proof of the custom’s age.

**[Disputed/popular etymologies]** Modern French and Italian summaries sometimes offer “witch,” “simple-minded woman,” or “astonishing being” as loose translations. These are not secure historical etymologies. The form should ordinarily remain untranslated.

**[Regional explanation]** *Roi-* has been explained through local words for smoke or soot, referring to masks or costumes darkened in a chimney. The spelling and explanation vary by source.

### Classification through time

- **1860s:** Gibsten calls them persons “known as Tschäggättä,” describing their costume and misconduct. He does not classify them as gods or spirits.
- **1898–1916:** Friedrich Gottlieb Stebler and Leopold Rütimeyer treat them as survivals of an archaic mask-culture and compare them with Alpine Perchten.
- **Mid-twentieth century:** local history, Swiss national representation, museums, and tourism increasingly present them as primordial or emblematic figures of the valley.
- **Contemporary official classification:** a living tradition encompassing oral expression, performing arts, social practice, and traditional craftsmanship.

No historical epithet comparable to a divine cult-title is attested.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Documentary Corpus

### Material evidence attributed to approximately 1790

**[Material evidence, dating qualified]** The Lötschental Museum exhibits a wooden mask identified as its oldest known Tschäggättä mask and dated to approximately **1790**. This is potentially the earliest surviving object. The published public-facing descriptions do not supply a dendrochronological result, an original acquisition record from 1790, or a contemporary text identifying the object. It therefore establishes the likely existence of an old local mask, but not every feature of the later Tschäggättä custom.

The Museum der Kulturen Basel has masks acquired between 1905 and 1926, at least two of which it dates to approximately 1880. Its current interpretation stresses that collectors once selected these objects to illustrate supposedly “wild” and “archaic” European culture, obscuring continuing stylistic change.

### Gibsten’s parish chronicle, beginning in the 1860s

**[Earliest secure written attestation]** Johann Baptist Gibsten, prior of Kippel from 1864 to 1876, recorded in the parish chronicle:

> “Zur Fasnachtszeit war hier ein abscheulicher Gebrauch, den die sogenannten Tschäggättä ausübten …”

He describes the face hidden by an “abscheuliche Holzlarve,” horns on the head, animal skins over the body, frightening children, and smearing girls with soot and blood. The manuscript is held in the Kippel parish archive, generally cited as **Pfarrarchiv Kippel G7**. Later researchers who examined parish and municipal holdings reported no earlier documentary witness.

This passage establishes:

- the name;
- wooden face-masks;
- horns;
- animal skins;
- Carnival performance;
- pursuit or intimidation of children and young women;
- smearing with soot and allegedly blood;
- ecclesiastical opposition.

It does **not** establish:

- pre-Christian origin;
- a rite for expelling winter;
- the Schurtendiebe as historical founders;
- descent from Celtic, Germanic, or Alemannic religion;
- identity with Perchta, Krampus, or a deity.

### Friedrich Gottlieb Stebler, 1898

**[Early ethnographic attestation]** Stebler’s “Fastnacht im Lötschental,” published in the *Schweizerisches Archiv für Volkskunde* 2 (1898), is the first known printed ethnographic notice focused on the custom. Stebler also brought masks into museum circulation. His work marks the transition from a locally regulated practice to an object of national ethnographic attention.

### Leopold Rütimeyer, 1907

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Rütimeyer’s “Über Masken und Maskenbräuche im Lötschental (Kanton Wallis),” *Globus* 91 (1907), pp. 201–204 and 213–218, gives the first extended published account. He learned of the figures through masks collected by Stebler and visited the valley in 1905.

Rütimeyer regarded the custom as an archaic “survival,” drawing on Edward Tylor’s evolutionary anthropology and contemporary ideas about an Alpine *homo alpinus*. His description and collecting remain valuable primary documentation; his prehistoric interpretation is not established by the evidence.

### Rütimeyer, 1916

In “Über einige archaistische Gerätschaften und Gebräuche im Kanton Wallis und ihre prähistorischen und ethnographischen Parallelen,” *Schweizerisches Archiv für Volkskunde* 20 (1916), Rütimeyer revisited the custom. He reported that:

- it ran on the afternoons of Monday and Tuesday before Ash Wednesday;
- it had been prohibited in 1915 because of the war;
- clergy had previously prohibited it as a “pagan custom”;
- it had reportedly been allowed again around 1902;
- informants associated it with the Schurtendiebe.

**[Important distinction]** A priest’s description of a practice as “heathen” is evidence for clerical condemnation, not evidence that the practice descends from pre-Christian religion.

### Photographic, film, and object corpus

- Masks taken to the Swiss National Museum by or through Stebler at the end of the nineteenth century.
- Rütimeyer’s Basel acquisitions, beginning in 1905.
- Albert Nyfeler’s photographs and paintings from the early twentieth century.
- A 1916 ethnographic film sequence of masked performers.
- The so-called **Tschäggätunschbrizza**, a large syringe acquired in 1916 and preserved at the Museum der Kulturen Basel.
- Extensive twentieth- and twenty-first-century photographic, tourist, documentary-film, and museum records.

### The named Alpine legend corpora

The Grimm brothers’ *Deutsche Sagen* and *Deutsche Mythologie*, Vernaleken’s *Alpensagen* (1858), Zingerle’s Tyrolean collections, Heyl, Rochholz’s Aargau legends, Jecklin’s Graubünden material, Kuoni’s St. Gallen legends, and Müller’s Uri collections are indispensable comparative corpora for German-speaking Alpine supernatural traditions. They are **not**, on evidence located for this dossier, primary attestations of the Lötschental Tschäggättä under that name.

That negative result matters. Importing Frau Perchta, the Wild Hunt, witches, demons, or named spirits from neighboring regional collections would manufacture a theology absent from the local record.

The *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens* and later Perchten studies likewise provide comparative categories rather than an early Tschäggättä scripture or mythology. The Tschäggättä possess no hymn, saga-cycle, liturgy, scripture, or temple text.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Calendar and setting

**[Documented contemporary tradition]** The officially described season extends from **3 February**, the day after Candlemas, until midnight on Shrove Tuesday. Tourism literature sometimes says “from Candlemas”; the more exact living-traditions inventory says the day after Candlemas. Runs traditionally did not occur on Sundays.

**[Historical variation]** Rütimeyer’s 1915 inquiry described afternoon appearances specifically on Monday and Tuesday before Ash Wednesday. The long modern season should not automatically be projected backward.

### Costume

The conventional ensemble includes:

- a hand-carved mask, commonly of Swiss stone pine (*Arve*, *Pinus cembra*);
- sheep- or goatskins;
- a reversed jacket and rough or jute trousers;
- shoulder padding that makes the figure abnormally large or hunchbacked;
- the *Triämhändsche*;
- heavy footwear concealed with sacking;
- a cattle-bell belt or single large *Triichla*;
- a stick or fork-like implement;
- historically, horns, teeth, hair, and reused domestic materials.

Masks commonly measure approximately 30–50 cm. Teeth could be real animal teeth, and horns could be inserted. Early masks were often unpainted or simply treated; Albert Nyfeler is credited with encouraging painted masks during the 1920s.

### Characteristic actions

**[Securely documented, nineteenth–twentieth centuries]**

- pursuing women and children;
- frightening onlookers;
- sounding bells and shouting;
- smearing faces with soot;
- rubbing captured people with snow;
- concealing the performer’s identity;
- moving between neighboring villages;
- courtship under disguise.

Gibsten also reports smearing with blood. This cannot simply be dismissed, but the nature and source of the blood are unstated. It is not evidence of sacrifice.

The disputed syringe was said to project blood, liquid manure, or soot-water. Werner Bellwald’s object-history study notes that it is the only known specimen, that Carnival literature otherwise lacks this alleged local specialty, and that its authenticity or representativeness remains uncertain.

### Gender and social function

**[Historical practice]** Until the late 1950s the role was reportedly restricted principally to unmarried local men. Pursuit and soot-marking of young women formed part of a courtship field in which masked anonymity temporarily relaxed ordinary supervision.

**[Modern development]** Married men, outsiders, women, and children now participate in some contexts. Organized processions, contests, tourism, and photography have displaced some of the older interpersonal and courtship functions.

### Sacred correspondences

No primary evidence assigns the Tschäggättä sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers, planets, temples, priesthood, divine weapons, or theological powers. Sheep and goats provide costume materials; cattle bells are agricultural objects redeployed in Carnival. Calling these “sacred animals” or “divine emblems” would be unwarranted.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

The Tschäggättä have no attested parents, children, consorts, divine siblings, servants, mounts, or pantheon rank. The sources describe enacted figures, not a genealogical supernatural species.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

**None securely attested.**

No primary Lötschental source records a Tschäggätta meeting, fighting, seducing, deceiving, judging, rescuing, or appearing in a narrative scene with:

- Perchta or Berchta;
- Krampus;
- St Nicholas;
- the Christian Devil;
- Wotan/Odin;
- Holda/Holle;
- Wild Hunt leaders;
- any Celtic, Germanic, Roman, or Christian divine being.

### Comparative and proposed relations

- **Perchten:** Rütimeyer compared the Tschäggättä with “ugly” Perchten of Pongau and Pinzgau. **Tier: modern scholarly comparison**, not documented identity or interaction.
- **Krampus:** Both traditions employ frightening masks, skins, bells, and Alpine winter performance. **Tier: comparative morphology**, not genealogy.
- **The Devil:** A contemporary museum interpretation considers the devil of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Catholic Baroque theatre a possible visual predecessor. **Tier: modern scholarly hypothesis**; no text documents transformation from a named theatrical devil into Tschäggättä.
- **Winter spirits/evil spirits:** Contemporary publicity often says the figures expel winter or evil spirits. **Tier: modern explanatory folklore**, unsupported as an early local doctrine.

## 5. Major Legends and Origin Narratives

There is no surviving myth-cycle about a supernatural Tschäggättä. What exists is a cluster of etiological legends explaining the masked custom.

### The Schurtendiebe

**[Folklore; recorded 1905, published 1907/1916]** Rütimeyer recorded a local account at Blatten in 1905. The Schurtendiebe were said to have occupied farmsteads or ruins at Giätrich/Dietrich in the forest on the valley’s shady southern side, opposite Wiler. Small but powerful men, they put on masks, skins, and bells and crossed to the sunny, inhabited side at night to steal grain and other provisions.

A candidate had supposedly to leap across the Lonza carrying a hundred-pound load before admission to their band. Rütimeyer connected *Schurten-* with *Schürte*, “short coat/skirt,” interpreting it through their short sheepskin garments.

**Development of the legend:** Prior Johann Siegen, Kippel’s priest from 1914 to 1974 and a local historian, maintained contact with Rütimeyer and helped popularize his interpretation. The cautious claim that the maskers resembled legendary thieves hardened into the assertion that the Tschäggättä were their descendants.

**Assessment:** The legend is a genuine part of twentieth-century Lötschental tradition. It is not independent evidence for a fifteenth-century robber fraternity. Archaeological-looking wall remains do not by themselves identify inhabitants as masked robbers.

### Refugees from Alemannic invasion

**[Later elaboration]** Some modern versions make the Schurtendiebe an older population displaced into the shaded woods by invading Alemanni. No early text, archaeology, or securely transmitted medieval account located for this dossier establishes that ethnic narrative. It appears to elaborate the recorded robber legend through nineteenth- and twentieth-century racial-historical speculation.

### The Trinkelstierkrieg of 1550

**[Historical event plus unattested costume legend]** The Trinkelstierkrieg was a real Valais uprising surrounding renewal of the French alliance, salt prices, and foreign pensions. Later tradition calls it a “war in Lötschental masks” or “bull masks” and says rebels concealed their identity in masks resembling Tschäggättä.

The *Historical Dictionary of Switzerland* states explicitly that **no document attests the rebels’ use of masks**. Therefore:

- the uprising is historical;
- its association with Tschäggättä is folklore or later historical legend;
- 1550 is not a secure first attestation.

### Expulsion of winter or evil spirits

**[Modern folkloric systematization]** A widespread explanation identifies the noisy run as a survival of a pre-Christian rite expelling winter, winter spirits, or harmful powers and welcoming spring.

Season, bells, skins, and comparison with other European masked customs make that interpretation intuitively attractive. They do not date it. Neither Gibsten nor the earliest material evidence supplies a rite-text or stated belief that a supernatural winter was being driven away.

### Descent from Baroque religious theatre

**[Modern scholarly hypothesis]** Lötschental Museum ethnologist Thomas Antonietti has proposed that the form may have developed from devil figures in seventeenth- or eighteenth-century Catholic Baroque theatre. This would explain monstrous faces and horns within a thoroughly Christian visual culture. It remains a hypothesis because no known chain of documents connects a particular play, costume, workshop, or actor to the later Carnival form.

### Composite origin

The most defensible reconstruction is not a single origin event. The documented custom may combine ordinary European Carnival masking, local courtship, youth-group behavior, agricultural materials, Christian calendrical structure, changing artistic conventions, and retrospective legends. Claims of a single prehistoric founder, deity, invasion, or medieval robber band exceed the evidence.

## 6. Practice, Places, and Ritual Organization

### Geography

The custom belongs to the Lötschental communities of:

- Blatten;
- Wiler;
- Kippel;
- Ferden.

The figures traditionally moved between settlements rather than worshipping at a sanctuary. There are no Tschäggättä temples, altars, priests, sacrifices, prayers, or oracles.

### Calendar

The contemporary framework is:

1. preparation around Candlemas;
2. running from 3 February onward, excluding Sundays;
3. major appearances on *Feister Donnerstag* or *Schmutziger Donnerstag*;
4. a large Saturday procession at Wiler;
5. conclusion by midnight on Shrove Tuesday.

The Saturday parade is a relatively organized form, scheduled partly so young people working or apprenticed outside the valley can attend. It includes evaluation or competition for masks. This differs from older decentralized runs.

### Making a mask

**[Living craft]** Masks are traditionally carved locally, often from stone pine. The object may incorporate horns, teeth, hair, paint, metal, and recycled materials. The carver’s identity has become increasingly prominent.

Notable makers and artistic mediators include:

- **Albert Nyfeler (1883–1969):** painter and photographer; colored masks with his palette and shaped their public image.
- **Willy Rieder:** identified as an early commercial or specialist mask-carving pioneer.
- **Jakob Tannast:** likewise associated with the development of mask production and sale.
- **Agnes Rieder:** began carving in the 1970s, challenged the male monopoly, made witch-like faces, and signed and dated her work.
- **Heinrich Rieder:** contemporary collector/carver whose mask cellar and deliberately provocative designs exemplify continuing innovation.
- **Oskar Ebiner** and the **Imsenger** family: documented contemporary workshops.

A recent report counted approximately fifteen active carvers and twelve “mask cellars,” although such figures necessarily fluctuate.

### Regulation and suppression

**[Securely documented]** Gibsten condemned the practice and penalized or attempted to suppress it during his tenure. Rütimeyer was told it had once been forbidden by clergy as “heathen” and was again permitted around 1902.

**[Securely documented]** The run was prohibited in 1915 during the First World War.

**[Twentieth-century regulation]** With winter tourism expanding from the 1960s, political and ecclesiastical authorities attempted to control excesses that might lead to complaints from visitors. Regulation helped favor scheduled, observable processions over uncontrolled pursuit.

### Violence, blood, and sacrifice

- Chasing, grabbing, snow-rubbing, sooting, frightening, and rough behavior are documented.
- Gibsten mentions blood-smearing.
- A unique syringe was alleged to have dispensed blood, manure, or soot-water.
- No evidence establishes killing, human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, cult violence, or sacrificial worship.
- No evidence links the practice to “temple prostitution” or any comparable institution.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before approximately 1790

No secure Tschäggättä attestation is known. Earlier Alpine mask customs cannot automatically be counted as Tschäggättä evidence.

### Approximately 1790–1864: material possibility, documentary silence

A museum mask is assigned to about 1790. If its dating and attribution are correct, some recognizable mask tradition existed by the late eighteenth century. Its contemporary name, exact use, and associated beliefs remain unknown.

### 1864–1898: first written record and clerical discipline

Gibsten documents an already established Carnival practice and opposes it. The record concerns social conduct—fear, soot, blood, disguise—not the worship of gods.

### 1898–1916: ethnographic “discovery”

Stebler published the custom and moved masks into national museum collections. Rütimeyer collected objects and testimony, photographed or filmed performers, and interpreted the practice as an archaic survival comparable to Perchten.

This was not passive observation. Outside collecting encouraged inhabitants to produce masks for scholars and museums, while scholarly theories supplied local narrators with prestigious accounts of prehistory.

### 1920s–1930s: aesthetic consolidation and national symbolism

Nyfeler’s painting and photography helped establish a recognizable “Lötschental mask” type. Professional or semiprofessional carvers emerged. During the interwar era, masks became emblems of an authentic Alpine Switzerland and participated in the ideology of *geistige Landesverteidigung*—the cultural defense of a supposedly rooted national identity.

Tschäggättä imagery appeared in exhibitions, including Swiss representational contexts in 1939. Claims that a particular mask traveled to a specific world fair should be checked against its museum accession and exhibition history rather than generalized to the whole tradition.

### 1950s–1970s: social change

Economic modernization, roads, wage labor, apprenticeships outside the valley, and tourism transformed the ritual:

- courtship under masks declined;
- the unmarried-male monopoly weakened;
- organized processions grew;
- masks became saleable art and souvenirs;
- makers’ authorship became visible.

Agnes Rieder’s work in the 1970s is a landmark in women’s participation and in the shift from anonymous, expendable disguise to signed art object.

### 1980s–2000s: heritage and commercial emblem

Tourism boards, railways, museums, advertisers, writers, filmmakers, and local authorities made the Tschäggättä the visual shorthand of the Lötschental. This supported craft and tourism but created disputes over who could define an “authentic” mask.

A 2007 Swiss Federal Railways campaign showing masked figures under a slogan about exploring “rough peoples” provoked criticism for reproducing the old ethnographic image of Valais inhabitants as primitive.

### 2012–present: officially recognized living tradition

The practice entered Switzerland’s inventory of living traditions in 2012. Heritage recognition did not freeze it: women and children participate, carvers experiment, competitive parades coexist with informal runs, and documentary cinema continues to reinterpret the figures.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and visual culture

Painter-photographer Albert Nyfeler and poet Maurice Chappaz intensified the figures’ visual and literary aura. Chappaz-Wirthner and Mayor show that modern carvers draw not only on inherited local forms but on art reproductions, fantasy imagery, photography, journalism, and earlier ethnographic images.

The result is feedback:

1. carvers make masks;
2. ethnographers and artists select dramatic specimens;
3. museums and media establish those specimens as “authentic”;
4. later carvers consult the published images;
5. the externally codified type returns to the valley as tradition.

### Cinema and media

Documented examples include:

- a 1916 ethnographic film sequence;
- Bernhard Rieder’s 2002 *Tschäggättä – Sagen und Geschichten: Fürchte dich, denn sie kommen!*;
- a French horror/fantasy production filmed around the tradition in 2009;
- later short films and stage works, including *Tschäggättä, der Geist des Frühlings*;
- Raphael Stäger’s 12-minute documentary *Tschäggättä* (2025).

These works may personify “the Tschäggätta” as a singular creature or spring spirit. That is artistic reinvention, not recovered ancient theology.

### Tourism and commerce

Masks and imagery appear in:

- railway and destination advertising;
- postcards and tourist brochures;
- beer, medicine, and other commercial promotion;
- souvenir masks;
- museum exhibitions;
- organized public processions.

Some families obtained meaningful income from mask sales. Commercialization is therefore not merely external corruption; it has become part of the tradition’s transmission and has sustained carving expertise.

### Occultism, neopaganism, and political appropriation

**[Negative finding]** The dedicated searches found no documented Tschäggättä cult in Renaissance magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, chaos magic, modern grimoires, or an organized reconstructionist religion. No occult lineage or pre-Christian liturgy is attested.

**[Modern invention when asserted]** Treating Tschäggättä as ancient winter demons, a Celtic or Germanic divine race, servants of Perchta, or surviving pagan gods is modern mythmaking unless a claimant can provide evidence earlier than the nineteenth-century folkloric record.

**[Political use]** Their strongest documented political appropriation is Swiss national and regional identity-making, particularly the interwar valorization of Alpine authenticity. No specific extremist Tschäggättä cult or sustained racial-nationalist organization was established by the consulted evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The survivalist school

Rütimeyer read the custom through Tylor’s theory of survivals and early physical anthropology. Isolation supposedly preserved a primitive cultural layer in the Lötschental. Perchten and other masked customs were treated as fragments of a once-widespread archaic system.

This theory shaped collecting and local explanation but depended heavily on formal resemblance and assumed cultural isolation.

### Functional and social interpretations

Later work emphasized what the performance demonstrably did:

- created anonymous male peer groups;
- enabled courtship;
- reversed ordinary behavioral constraints;
- structured rivalry among villages and age groups;
- provided a temporary Carnival license for aggression and mockery.

These interpretations do not require a prehistoric origin.

### Reflexive ethnography and invented authenticity

Werner Bellwald, Suzanne Chappaz-Wirthner, Grégoire Mayor, and Thomas Antonietti examine how ethnographers, museums, artists, tourism, and residents jointly produced the modern idea of the Tschäggättä.

Bellwald’s analysis of the unique syringe shows the danger of treating a collected object as transparent proof of an old norm. Chappaz-Wirthner and Mayor demonstrate that “authenticity” is actively contested among carvers and families.

### Principal disputes

| Question | Evidence-based conclusion |
|---|---|
| Are Tschäggättä gods or demons? | No historical source classifies them as divine beings. They are masked Carnival figures. |
| Are they prehistoric? | Not demonstrable; the first secure text is from the 1860s. |
| Does the ca. 1790 mask prove the whole modern custom? | It supports an older masking practice, but not the later name, calendar, legends, or meanings by itself. |
| Did the Schurtendiebe found the custom? | A legend recorded in 1905; no medieval documentation confirms it. |
| Did masked rebels fight in 1550? | The uprising is historical; contemporary evidence for masks is absent. |
| Does the run expel winter spirits? | Common modern explanation, not an early documented Lötschental belief. |
| Is it derived from Baroque devils? | Plausible modern hypothesis, not proven transmission. |
| Is it identical with Perchten or Krampus? | No; comparative similarities do not establish identity. |
| Did clergy suppress it? | Yes, ecclesiastical hostility and prohibition are documented; this does not prove pagan origin. |
| Was blood used? | Gibsten says so, and one collected syringe was associated with blood; extent and authenticity remain disputed. |
| Was human sacrifice involved? | No evidence. |
| Is modern tourist performance “inauthentic”? | Historically new organization is documented, but change and commercialization are now part of the living tradition. |

### State of the evidence

Important holdings include:

- **Pfarrarchiv Kippel:** Gibsten’s chronicle.
- **Lötschental Museum, Kippel:** historical and modern masks, photographs, exhibition material, local documentation.
- **Museum der Kulturen Basel:** masks, the disputed syringe, photographs, and early collecting records.
- **Swiss National Museum:** masks associated with Stebler’s collecting.
- **Médiathèque Valais:** Nyfeler photographs and wider visual documentation.
- **Swiss folklore journals and e-periodica:** early ethnographic publications.
- **Swiss inventory of living traditions:** contemporary ethnographic synthesis.

## 11. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

The fullest defensible history is considerably more interesting than the assertion of an “ancient Alpine demon.” The documented subject is a changing institution of disguise.

Its earliest likely object is a mask assigned to about 1790; its earliest secure contemporary text is Gibsten’s chronicle from the 1860s. By 1898 it entered print and museums. Early ethnographers then supplied the prehistoric-survival theory that shaped both national reception and later local origin stories. During the twentieth century the custom moved from a predominantly unmarried-male courtship and intimidation practice toward organized processions, artistic authorship, tourism, heritage administration, and wider participation.

The Schurtendiebe, masked rebels of 1550, expulsion of winter spirits, and descent from Baroque devils all belong in its history—but as differently dated explanations, not interchangeable facts. The Schurtendiebe are attested folklore; the rebels’ masks are unsupported legend; winter expulsion is comparative systematization; Baroque theatrical descent is a modern scholarly hypothesis.

No primary evidence makes the Tschäggättä gods, worshipped demons, sacrificial beings, or members of a pan-Alpine pantheon. Their closest documented “supernatural” quality is performative: while the mask is on, a known or unknown villager becomes a socially licensed monstrous figure. That transformation—not an unattested mythology—is the historical core recoverable from the sources.
