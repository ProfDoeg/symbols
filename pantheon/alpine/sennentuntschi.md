# The Sennentuntschi: Research Dossier

**Class:** Alpine folkloric being: an artificial female figure or “herdsmen’s doll” that becomes animate and, in many variants, demonic or monstrous. It is not securely attested as a goddess, a recipient of worship, or a member of an organized Alpine pantheon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the fourteen-stanza poem **“Die drei Melker”** (“The Three Milkers”), printed in **1839** and set in the Austrian Zillertal. Gotthilf Isler identified it as the earliest printed Sennenpuppen text in his systematic survey. The poem’s precise oral antecedents remain undated. The earliest presently identifiable Swiss prose witness is Nikolaus Senn’s **“Geschichte von der Puppe”** (1854). [Alois Senti, “Die Sennenpuppe auf Ruhegg”](https://www.ortsgemeinde-flums.ch/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Sennenpuppe.pdf)

**Evidentiary conclusion:** the record supports a nineteenth- and twentieth-century complex of Alpine migratory legends, probably drawing on older Christian moral narratives and broader animated-image motifs. It does **not** support claims that the Sennentuntschi was an ancient pre-Christian deity, a Perchten figure, an object of organized cult, or the remnant of human sacrifice.

## Evidence labels used

- **Secure early attestation:** dated nineteenth-century print or demonstrably earlier archival evidence.
- **Regional variant:** a geographically localized version, whether printed or orally collected.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** a version that became influential through scholarship, literature, theatre, or film.
- **Learned systematization:** a collector’s, folklorist’s, psychological, or comparative reconstruction.
- **Syncretistic identification:** an explicit equation with another supernatural being.
- **Folklore:** orally transmitted belief or narrative recorded by a collector.
- **Modern invention/retelling:** an identifiable artistic, commercial, esoteric, or popular-culture reworking.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

The Sennentuntschi is usually not an independently existing mountain spirit that happens upon herdsmen. It begins as an object made by them: a roughly human—and normally female—figure constructed from wood, straw, rags, discarded clothing, cheese or curd, utensils, bottles, or other materials available in an Alpine hut. The men feed it, speak to it, carry it, put it to bed, baptize it mockingly, beat it, or use it sexually. It begins eating, moving, growing, or speaking; at the seasonal descent it demands that one man remain with it, then kills and frequently flays him.

Its position between object and being is fundamental. Before animation it is a doll, surrogate woman, joke, or ritual parody. After animation, collectors variously call it a *böser Geist* (“evil spirit”), demon, monster, or simply the living doll. These descriptions are narrative classifications rather than evidence of a coherent demonological species.

### Names

- **Sennentuntschi / Sännatuntschi:** literally “herdsman’s doll.” *Senn* denotes a dairyman or herdsman working on an Alp; *Tuntschi* is a Swiss German word for a doll. The Muotathal dialect dictionary glosses *Tuntschi* simply as “Puppe” and *Sännä tuntschi* as a legendary being in Uri. [Muotathaler Wörterbuch](https://dialekt.muotathal.ch/search/Tuntschi)
- **Tunsch:** the Swiss *Idiotikon* records *Tunsch* as a feminine rag doll; its diminutives include *Tunschel*. This lexical evidence supports an ordinary “doll” origin for the name rather than the name of a primordial goddess. [Swiss Academy discussion of the *Idiotikon* entry](https://www.sagw.ch/fileadmin/redaktion_sagw/dokumente/Publikationen/Sprachen_und_Kulturen/Idiotikon_low.pdf)
- **Sennenpuppe / Sennerpuppe:** Standard German, “herdsmen’s doll.” This is the most useful comparative scholarly term and the title of Gotthilf Isler’s monograph.
- **Sennpoppa:** dialectal “herdsmen’s doll,” reported in Alpine variants.
- **Toggel / Toggeli / Toggäli:** another Swiss dialect doll-name. It can elsewhere denote a nightmare or pressure spirit, but lexical overlap does not prove that the Sennenpuppe is genealogically identical with that being.
- **Joggäli:** the name of the figure in the Flums/Ruhegg cycle, apparently because the men themselves were remembered as Jakob/Joggäli.
- **Joggäli-Meischinder / Joggäli-Meïschinder:** “Joggäli who flays more [of them],” derived from its threat, “Joggäli have you flayed; Joggäli have I flayed; more Joggäli will I flay.” This is a localized Flums epithet. [Senti](https://www.ortsgemeinde-flums.ch/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Sennenpuppe.pdf)
- **Ghürsch/Gürsch:** a contemptuous or objectifying designation used for the roughly constructed Flums figure.
- **Hausäli:** an alternative reported in modern summaries and film publicity. Its exact early geographical record has not been established from the accessible primary corpus.
- **“Puppe auf der Alp,” “Puppe von Valésa,” “Guschger Sennenpuppe”:** descriptive local titles rather than universal names.

### Etymological findings

The transparent compound *Senn-en-tuntschi* is adequately explained as “herdsmen’s doll.” No credible evidence located derives *Tuntschi* from the name of a pre-Christian deity. Its occurrence among ordinary regional doll-words weighs against that theory.

The relationship among *Tuntschi*, *Toggel/Toggeli*, *Tunsch*, *Docke*, and other doll or spirit terms is dialectal and partly semantic. A name can move from “doll” to “animate doll” to “legendary creature” without implying that every being called *Toggel* is the Sennentuntschi.

### Claimed identifications

- **Pygmalion’s statue/Galatea:** **learned comparison**, not an attested Alpine equation. Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 10.243–297 supplies the ancient story of a man whose fashioned woman is animated by Venus. The structural resemblance is real, but no documented chain of oral transmission from Ovid to the Alpine legend has been demonstrated. The Swiss National Museum explicitly says the intermediate route is unknown. [National Museum exhibition text](https://www.landesmuseum.ch/landesmuseum/ausstellungen/wechselausstellungen/2022/sagen-aus-den-alpen/medien/sagen-ausstellungstext-komplett.pdf) [Peter Egloff](https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/2023/01/pygmalion-forever/)
- **Succubus:** principally a **modern cinematic identification**, especially through Georg Tressler’s *Sukkubus—Den Teufel im Leib* (1989). Traditional texts may demonize the doll and include sex, but they do not thereby make it a learned medieval succubus.
- **The Devil:** an explicit agent in some Christianized versions: a bad spirit or the Devil enters and animates the doll. This is a regional narrative explanation, not a universal identity.
- **Perchten, Krampus, Frau Percht, Tschäggättä:** no primary Sennenpuppen narrative located stages an identity or encounter with them. They belong to different winter-mask and procession traditions. Modern Perchten scholarship itself warns that claims of direct survival from a pre-Christian goddess are poorly evidenced; resemblance and shared Alpine geography are insufficient. [Seiser, “Traditionalizing the Present”](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3030665_2/component/file_3030666/shh1185.pdf)
- **Salige Frauen, Fänggen, Wild Women:** thematic comparisons only. No secure source establishes the Sennentuntschi as one of these pre-existing female nature beings.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest witnesses

1. **“Die drei Melker,” 1839 — Zillertal, Austria.**  
   **Secure early attestation.** Fourteen stanzas; the earliest printed example located by Isler. It already places the animated-doll catastrophe among Alpine dairy workers. The accessible secondary description does not establish the poem’s author, printer, or oral date; those particulars must therefore remain open.

2. **Nikolaus Senn, “Geschichte von der Puppe,” 1854 — Rhine Valley/eastern Switzerland.**  
   **Secure early Swiss attestation.** In the summary preserved by Senti, rejection of a beggar—“*Packe dich fort, Alter!*” (“Be off, old man!”)—calls down divine punishment, and an evil spirit enters the doll. This makes failed charity, not merely sexuality, the decisive transgression.

3. **Dietrich Jecklin, *Volksthümliches aus Graubünden*, part 1, 1874 — Valésa near Somvix/Sumvitg.**  
   **Regional variant.** Herdsmen make a doll from cheese matter, fondle and treat it like a child. At the descent it commands one man to remain; a lot selects the victim. A returning companion finds the creature grown into a white-capped monster spreading the man’s skin on the roof. A modernized transcription identifies the punishment as retribution for misuse of “God’s gifts.” [Modern transcription citing Jecklin](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/custom/data/ckeditorfiles/Brauchtum/MF95herbst2022Puppe.pdf)

4. **Anton Henne/Henne-Am Rhyn, *Die deutsche Volkssage*, second edition, 1879, p. 308 — Flums.**  
   **Secure attestation, but extremely brief.** A five-line note transmitted through a teacher-training student. Senti notes a related school collection from 1850–51 that is now unlocated; its contents cannot be treated as securely verified.

5. **Johannes Natsch, “Die drei Joggäli auf Ruhegg,” collected in the later nineteenth century; printed by Jakob Kuoni in *Sagen des Kantons St. Gallen* (1903), p. 170.**  
   **Regional folklore.** Natsch visited the Alp Ruhegg. His figure shares the men’s name, Joggäli; the men feed and mock it until it eats and speaks. At the descent it insists that the master dairyman stay. He is later found flayed, while the doll threatens to flay more Joggäli.

6. **Johann Adolf Heyl, *Volkssagen, Bräuche und Meinungen aus Tirol* (1897).**  
   This is a major Tyrolean source corpus, but the accessible searchable evidence does not securely identify a Sennenpuppen text by title or number. It should not be cited as a direct witness without checking the relevant page in the full edition. The volume itself is verifiable. [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books?id=VKNZAAAAMAAJ)

7. **Arnold Büchli collection, Alp Naustgel, Surselva; testimony recorded from Donat Maissen in 1963.**  
   **Twentieth-century oral testimony.** The men engage in “all possible foolishness,” including indecent acts. Maissen added that older workers mistreated boys and exposed them to sexualized talk; he therefore would not let his sons work on the Alp. [Egloff](https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/2023/01/pygmalion-forever/)

### Systematic corpus

Gotthilf Isler’s Zürich dissertation, published as *Die Sennenpuppe: Eine Untersuchung über die religiöse Funktion einiger Alpensagen* (Basel, 1971; dissertation completed 1970), is the central corpus study. It contains a distribution map, formal motif analysis, and a variant catalogue. Contemporary bibliography describes 285–287 pages and explicitly notes its inclusion of Austrian variants. [Austrian Folklore Museum bibliography](https://www.volkskundemuseum.at/jart/prj3/volkskundemuseum/data/publikation/1533519994773/1533519994773.pdf) [Contents record](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/buecherdatenbank/8758/die-sennenpuppe)

Counts vary with corpus revision:

- Peter Egloff’s 1993 article says Isler assembled **111 numbered variants**.
- Alois Senti’s later discussion speaks of **120 tales**, **78 from Switzerland**.

This is not necessarily a contradiction: one may represent Isler’s published catalogue and the other an expanded working corpus. Neither count means 111 or 120 independent ancient traditions; collectors frequently record closely related localizations.

### The named nineteenth-century corpora

The following works are important for locating parallels and regional context, but it would be methodologically unsafe to assert that every one contains the Sennentuntschi proper:

- Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, *Deutsche Sagen*; Jacob Grimm, *Deutsche Mythologie*: foundational comparative works, but no secure occurrence of the name *Sennentuntschi* has been located.
- Theodor Vernaleken, *Alpensagen* (1858): relevant Alpine corpus; no direct Sennentuntschi passage securely verified here.
- Ignaz Vinzenz Zingerle, Tyrolean saga collections: relevant to the regional environment; a direct occurrence remains unestablished.
- Johann Adolf Heyl, *Volkssagen, Bräuche und Meinungen aus Tirol* (1897): regionally important; direct entry requires page-level verification.
- Ernst Ludwig Rochholz, Aargau collections: relevant to Swiss folklore history; no secure named witness identified.
- Dietrich Jecklin: direct witness, Valésa.
- Jakob Kuoni: direct witness through Natsch’s Ruhegg narrative.
- Later Uri collections: direct versions include *Der geschundene Senn*, but a precise original print citation should accompany any quotation.

The *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens* discusses the broader symbolism of skin and flaying rather than establishing an ancient Sennentuntschi cult. The *Enzyklopädie des Märchens* treats Sennenpuppen stories under disturbed supernatural-human relations and taboo violation, citing Isler and Barbara Hauser-Schäublin. [Encyclopedia preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783110921717_A19981839/preview-9783110921717_A19981839.pdf)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The being belongs to the seasonal world of the **Alp**: an isolated high pasture occupied during summer by a temporary, often hierarchically organized work group. Its narrative “domain” is not fertility or mountains in the manner of a deity. It enforces, or embodies the collapse of, rules governing:

- proper use of milk, cream, cheese, whey, and other food;
- hospitality to beggars and the poor;
- sexual restraint and the treatment of vulnerable people;
- respect for baptism and other sacraments;
- disciplined Alpine work;
- the danger of treating a made object as a subordinated person;
- the return from the liminal summer Alp to village society.

### Form and materials

Regional texts name wood, rags, straw, discarded clothes, cheese matter, and domestic implements. Hansjörg Schneider’s play influentially uses a bottle, pitchforks, straw, and cheese. These materials are not a fixed sacred iconography.

Animation can occur gradually:

- the doll opens its mouth;
- accepts food;
- licks or grows;
- speaks;
- performs household work;
- changes into a woman;
- grows into a roof-haunting monster;
- becomes possessed by an evil spirit.

The white cap in the Jecklin/Valésa version is a local narrative detail, not a universal emblem.

### Powers

- animation and bodily transformation;
- speech and appetite;
- unnatural strength and speed;
- foreknowledge or manipulation of the lot;
- pursuit of fleeing herdsmen;
- killing and flaying;
- in some modern versions, stuffing the men’s skins with straw.

Its limitations are poorly systematized. Some survivors possess consecrated objects; others escape by obeying the command not to look back or by leaving before the killing. No universally attested charm, festival, sacred animal, color, number, weekday, or celestial association exists.

### The Masciadon figure

The sole widely publicized material object is a roughly **40-centimetre wooden figure**, partly wrapped in cloth, painted brown, with human hair and pronounced sexual features. Peter Egloff acquired it in 1978 from the last resident of Masciadon near Cauco in the Calanca Valley and transferred it to the Rätisches Museum in Chur in the mid-1980s.

The museum lists it as **undated**. It is evidence that such a doll existed, not proof that it became animate, served in a cult, or dates to antiquity. Its history before 1978 is inadequately documented. [Rätisches Museum catalogue](https://raetischesmuseum.app/objects/daemonen-auf-der-alp?tour=/tours/zeitreise-durch-die-buendner-geschichte) [SRF exhibition report](https://www.srf.ch/kultur/kunst/kunst-das-schandobjekt-tuntschi-entpuppt-sich-als-kulturgut)

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin rather than genealogy

The Sennentuntschi has no parents, consort, children, siblings, mount, or stable pantheon position. The makers are its “creators,” but human manufacture is not divine genealogy.

Competing origin mechanisms are:

1. **Men manufacture and anthropomorphize it; it spontaneously becomes alive.**
2. **Mock baptism or sacramental parody animates it.**
3. **An evil spirit or the Devil enters it as punishment.**
4. **Literary versions make masculine desire itself the animating force.**
5. **Modern feminist retellings treat the being as the collective return of exploited female or feminized bodies.**

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **The Devil — possession/animation:** In Nikolaus Senn’s 1854 version, after the dairyman drives away an old beggar and incurs divine punishment, “the evil spirit” enters the doll. **Tier: secure early regional variant.**
- **God — punitive moral framework, not an embodied meeting:** Some narrators call the catastrophe punishment for wasting “God’s gifts”; no scene of the Sennentuntschi meeting God is recorded. **Tier: Christian folkloric interpretation.**
- **Venus — no interaction:** Venus animates Pygmalion’s statue in Ovid, but the Sennentuntschi never meets her in an attested Alpine version. **Tier: learned comparison only.**
- **Pygmalion’s animated woman/Galatea — no interaction:** analogous created female figures, never co-present in a traditional episode. **Tier: learned comparison.**
- **Percht/Frau Perchta — no documented interaction.**
- **Krampus — no documented interaction.**
- **Tschäggättä — no documented interaction.**
- **Salige Frauen, Fänggen, Wild Women — no documented interaction.**
- **Christian saints, Mary, angels, pagan gods — no documented interaction located.**

Thus the requested interaction ledger is unusually short: this is an isolated legendary figure, not a participant in a narrative pantheon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The core Sennenpuppe cycle

**Folklore complex, nineteenth century onward.**

A small group spends summer on an isolated Alp. Loneliness, boredom, drunkenness, mockery, sexual deprivation, or contempt for work leads them to construct a female figure. They set it at table and offer milk, cream, porridge, or cheese, often smearing food into its mouth. They talk to it, carry it, strike it when it does not answer, sleep with it, or subject it to acts that collectors leave euphemistic.

The doll begins to eat, speak, work, or assume flesh. When the men prepare the autumn descent, it refuses abandonment: “One of you must remain.” A lot is drawn, normally selecting the master dairyman or principal offender. The others descend with the herd, sometimes forbidden to look back until reaching a named bridge or other boundary. When they return or turn around, they see their companion flayed and his skin stretched over the hut roof.

The flaying reverses objectification: the men made a body from dead materials; the animated body converts a man into raw material or a displayed hide.

### B. Valésa/Somvix

**Regional variant; Jecklin, 1874.**

The herdsmen fashion the doll from cheese matter and treat it like a child. At departure it demands a companion. A man returning for his knife sees it enlarged into a white-capped monster spreading the victim’s skin on the roof. He develops a lasting fever and fear. The version foregrounds the profanation of food described as divine gifts. [Jecklin transcription](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/custom/data/ckeditorfiles/Brauchtum/MF95herbst2022Puppe.pdf)

### C. Ruhegg/Flums and the three Joggäli

**Regional variant; nineteenth-century collection, printed 1903.**

The men and doll all bear the diminutive of Jakob. The men waste dairy foods while poor people need whey; mock the doll; stuff porridge into its mouth; beat it; and take it to their beds. The doll eventually speaks and demands company through winter. The selected dairyman is killed and flayed.

In one strand it pursues the descending herd and pulls the victim’s skin “over his ears.” In another, a search party finds grotesque men in the hut and the corpse displayed. Consecrated objects protect one visitor. The Alp becomes unusable for years until a conscientious worker begins every task “in God’s name.”

This complex attaches the legend to local geography, alleged personal names, an Alp cross, and a claim that a bloody board still bore names in 1778. Senti records these assertions but also notes that a local herdsman attributed the cross to an unrelated fatal prank. The material landmark therefore does not independently authenticate the legend. [Senti](https://www.ortsgemeinde-flums.ch/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Sennenpuppe.pdf)

### D. Beggar-punishment cycle

**Secure early regional variant; Nikolaus Senn, 1854.**

A beggar is rejected. The master’s hardheartedness brings divine punishment; an evil spirit enters the doll. This aligns the tale with Alpine punishment legends in which refusal of charity and waste precede destruction of a pasture.

### E. Sacrilegious baptism

**Regional Christian variant.**

The men baptize the doll mockingly, sometimes ringing cowbells as if they were church bells. Animation follows. This is not evidence of a pre-Christian animation rite. In the recorded context it is a Christian warning about sacramental parody.

### F. The sexual-abuse version

**Folklore, often euphemistically recorded; explicit in modern literature.**

Many collectors write that the men took the doll to bed, “sinned against” it, or did “indecent” things. Modern retellings translate those phrases into sexual use or rape. The underlying sexual implication is not wholly modern, but explicit dialogue, repeated rape scenes, and an articulate revenge consciousness are chiefly literary and cinematic elaborations.

### G. Austrian, Tyrolean, Bavarian, and Liechtenstein forms

Isler’s catalogue establishes the tale beyond Switzerland, with Austrian variants central to the corpus and the earliest print witness set in the Zillertal. Liechtenstein’s Guschg version includes carrying, feeding, and beating the doll. Reports extend the type into Vorarlberg, Tyrol, Carinthia, Styria, and Upper Bavaria.

The evidence warrants speaking of a **German-speaking Alpine migratory legend**, not treating every local story as a report of the same named individual.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No evidence supports a Sennentuntschi cult.

- **Temples and shrines:** none.
- **Priesthood:** none.
- **Sacrifices or votive offerings:** none securely documented.
- **Festival calendar:** none.
- **Theophoric names:** none.
- **Pilgrimage:** none.
- **Oracle or divination:** none.
- **Continuous worship:** none.
- **Institutional suppression or forced conversion:** none documented.

Feeding and mock baptism occur inside the legend. They should not be converted into historical ritual practice without independent evidence.

The Ruhegg cross is a Christian memorial object around which competing stories accumulated. Its repeated alleged return to the Alp after removal is folklore. A local witness instead associated it with an accidental killing during a prank. It is not demonstrably a shrine to the doll.

The Masciadon figure is the only prominent physical analogue, but its use as a sexual surrogate is based on oral attribution and museum cataloguing rather than a fully documented provenance.

### Perchten, Krampus, and Tschäggättä

Krampus and Perchten runs in Salzburg, Gastein, Bavaria, and Austria are winter calendrical mask customs. Lötschental Tschäggättä are masked carnival figures. They supply comparative evidence for Alpine masking, inversion, aggression, and modern heritage-making, but not for Sennentuntschi ritual.

No evidence located places a Sennentuntschi effigy in those runs or names the figure as a Percht. Treating all frightening Alpine beings as survivals from a single pagan cult is precisely the kind of learned harmonization the record does not justify.

The *Atlas der schweizerischen Volkskunde* is valuable for the geographic study of Swiss customs and effigy practices, but no dedicated Sennentuntschi cult-map has been established here. Isler’s own distribution map is the relevant tale map.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1839: unattested antecedents

Animated statues, dolls, golems, homunculi, magical images, artificial spouses, and punitive Alpine legends are much older than the Sennentuntschi record. Ovid’s Pygmalion story is the most famous parallel. Such motifs establish comparability, not direct descent.

No medieval text, ecclesiastical prohibition, trial record, place-name, or pre-Christian inscription naming the Sennentuntschi has been identified.

### 1839–1903: entry into print

The Zillertal poem supplies the first fixed witness. Swiss collectors then localize the figure in the Rhine Valley, Graubünden, Flums, Uri, and elsewhere. These publications occur during the European expansion of folklore collecting. Their texts preserve local speech and belief but are also shaped by editors, moral language, literary conventions, and the collectors’ choices.

### Early to mid-twentieth century: continued collection

The tale remains part of living oral memory. Collectors acquire versions featuring waste, mock baptism, sexual behavior, exploitation of boys, and the dangers of seasonal male groups.

Literary writers transform it:

- Traugott Vogel, 1939;
- Meinrad Inglin, 1958;
- Marie Luise Kaschnitz, **“Der Tunsch,” 1966**.

These works are literary witnesses to reception, not evidence for ancient form.

### 1970–1971: Isler’s systematization

Isler’s dissertation makes the Sennenpuppe a distinct scholarly corpus. His approach combines comparative folklore with C. G. Jung’s concepts of archetype, anima, shadow, and the autonomous unconscious. For Isler, the legend can be “true” inwardly: a disowned psychic content becomes stronger than the conscious self.

This is a datable interpretive school, not an indigenous Alpine theology.

### 1972–1981: Schneider and national scandal

Hansjörg Schneider’s Swiss German play *Sennentuntschi* premiered in the Nachtstudio of Schauspielhaus Zürich in 1972. His archive preserves manuscripts beginning in October 1971 and both Swiss German and Standard German versions. [Swiss Literary Archives](https://ead.nb.admin.ch/html/schneider_A.html)

A 1979 television recording was held back and finally broadcast late on 4 May 1981. Explicit language, sexuality, and the dismantling of the morally pristine Alpine idyll produced organized protest, particularly from conservative Catholic circles. A criminal complaint concerning obscene publication was discontinued in late 1981; the broadcast complaint was likewise rejected, with the work characterized as relevant Swiss cultural production. Archival holdings preserve the prosecutorial decision and extensive press material. [Archival catalogue](https://www.kalliope-verbund.info/de/ead?ead.id=CH-000015-0-677181)

This controversy did more than preserve folklore: it standardized the name *Sennentuntschi* for a national audience and fixed sexuality near the center of the story.

### 1978–1986: materialization in the museum

Egloff’s acquisition of the Masciadon doll in 1978, followed by its transfer to the Rätisches Museum, gave the legend a tangible object. Its presentation as the only surviving “real Sennentuntschi” is heritage interpretation, not proof that it is the original object behind any tale.

### 1983–2011: opera and film

- Jost Meier’s five-act opera *Sennentuntschi*, using Schneider’s text, premiered in Freiburg im Breisgau in April 1983.
- Felix Mitterer’s *Die wilde Frau* (1986) uses related material.
- Georg Tressler’s film *Sukkubus—Den Teufel im Leib* (1989) recasts the Alpine doll story as erotic horror.
- Ernst Ludwig Leitner’s three-act *Die Sennenpuppe*, libretto by Alois Schöpf, premiered at the Gmunden festival in 2008; further productions followed in Salzburg, Wels, and Innsbruck. The opera makes the youngest man carve the woman partly to redirect the other men’s sexual aggression away from himself. [Salzburg Wind Philharmonic](https://www.salzburg-windphilharmonic.at/de/die-sennenpuppe/)

### 2010: Michael Steiner’s film

Michael Steiner’s Swiss-Austrian feature *Sennentuntschi* premiered at the Zurich Film Festival in September 2010. It shifts the action to 1975 and interweaves an Alpine-hut story with a village investigation. A mysterious woman may be supernatural, but the film also permits a human history of abuse, captivity, murder, and communal concealment. The ambiguity moves responsibility away from a simple external demon and toward human violence. [Swiss Films](https://www.swissfilms.ch/en/movie/sennentuntschi/262a53a8ea0041e49c66958564a72fb0/) [ORF review](https://newsv2.orf.at/stories/2065909/)

### 2015–present: heritage and feminist reinterpretation

The Rätisches Museum and Bündner Kunstmuseum’s 2015 exhibition placed the Masciadon figure beside contemporary art, turning a once-shameful object into recognized cultural heritage. [Bündner Kunstmuseum](https://kunstmuseum.gr.ch/de/ausstellungen/archiv/2015/Seiten/Sennentuntschi.aspx)

In 2021 Lydia Haider, Barbi Marković, Maria Muhar, and Stefanie Sargnagel premiered *Tuntschi. Eine Häutung* at Bühnen Bern. Their collective Sennentuntschi invokes sex dolls, avatars, incels, and toxic masculinity; the figure becomes a chorus of exploited constructed bodies. This is an explicit modern political reinvention. [Production record](https://www.schaefersphilippen.de/werke/tuntschi-eine-haeutung/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and theatre

- **Traugott Vogel (1939):** literary adaptation.
- **Meinrad Inglin (1958):** adaptation within twentieth-century Swiss Alpine literature.
- **Marie Luise Kaschnitz, “Der Tunsch” (1966):** modern literary treatment of the autonomous artificial being.
- **Hansjörg Schneider, *Sennentuntschi* (1972):** decisive theatrical canonization.
- **Jost Meier, *Sennentuntschi* (1983):** operatic adaptation.
- **Felix Mitterer, *Die wilde Frau* (1986):** related Alpine female-being motifs.
- **Ernst Ludwig Leitner/Alois Schöpf, *Die Sennenpuppe* (2008):** opera with symphonic wind ensemble.
- **Haider, Marković, Muhar, and Sargnagel, *Tuntschi. Eine Häutung* (2021):** feminist and anti-incel revision.
- **Olga Tokarczuk, *Empuzjon* (2022):** a literary work sometimes discussed beside Tuntschi traditions; it should not automatically be called a direct traditional Sennentuntschi text.

### Film

- **Sukkubus—Den Teufel im Leib** (1989): exploitation/horror recoding.
- **Sennentuntschi** (Michael Steiner, 2010): the most internationally visible version. Its woman is not merely an animated bundle of straw; the plot implicates patriarchal violence, clerical power, village hostility, and the unstable manufacture of a “monster” by collective belief.
- **S’Tuntschi vo Juf** (2026, 30 minutes): a contemporary Swiss-German film that presents a woman narrating from heaven and includes the animated doll from the mountain legend. [KHM programme](https://en.khm.de/rundgang_2026_filmprogramm_samstag/)

### Folklore, tourism, and heritage

Museums, hiking literature, regional websites, and retellings now present the Sennentuntschi as a representative Swiss or Alpine legend. This broad identity is modern heritage formation: the earlier corpus is intensely local and bears many names.

### Psychological interpretation

Isler’s Jungian approach treats the doll as an autonomous projection of repressed or undeveloped psychic content. Later popular psychology calls it an anima, shadow, or return of denied desire. These readings can illuminate reception but cannot date the legend or establish what nineteenth-century narrators consciously meant.

### Violence in the Alpine workplace

The interpretation of the story as one about Alpine violence has unusually strong contextual support:

- collectors’ euphemisms indicate sexual use of the doll;
- Maissen’s 1963 testimony explicitly connects Alpine crews with mistreatment of boys and indecent behavior;
- variants record beatings, humiliation, food waste, rejection of the poor, and hierarchical selection of a victim;
- the plot turns an object made for service into a punitive subject;
- modern versions foreground rape, homosocial coercion, and village complicity.

It remains interpretation rather than a record of one historical crime. The legend may simultaneously police sexual behavior, dramatize labor discipline, warn against sacrilege and waste, and give narrative form to abuse within isolated male work groups.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no established Sennentuntschi current in ceremonial magic, theosophy, the Golden Dawn, chaos magic, modern grimoires, Wicca, or documented Alpine reconstructionist worship. Online lists that fold it into a general “Alpine pantheon” are modern taxonomy.

There is likewise no demonstrated nationalist or extremist cult of the figure. Its political use has more often been satirical, feminist, anti-patriarchal, or a critique of sanitized national folklore.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is it ancient?

**Position favoring deep roots:** animated artificial women have ancient precedents, especially Pygmalion; Christian Alpine legends may preserve much older materials.

**Evidentiary objection:** motif similarity does not establish continuous transmission. The first Sennenpuppen witness is from 1839. The Rätisches Museum emphasizes that Alpine tales in their recoverable form rest on literary transmission, with many recorded principally in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The responsible conclusion is “older analogues, nineteenth-century documentary record.”

### Pygmalion derivation

The Pygmalion comparison explains manufacture, desire, and animation, but the endings are reversed: Ovid’s divinely animated woman legitimates erotic fulfillment and family; the Alpine doll punishes its makers. Egloff proposes that Christian sexual discipline helped invert the happy ending, while acknowledging that the transmission route is unknown. This is a plausible learned reconstruction, not established textual descent.

### What constitutes the transgression?

Variants disagree:

- making a human likeness;
- attempting to animate it;
- mock baptism;
- sex or sexual mockery;
- beating and humiliating the doll;
- waste of milk and food;
- rejection of a beggar;
- neglect of labor;
- treating boys or subordinates abusively;
- abandoning the creature after demanding its service.

The instability is historically important. It shows the tale being adapted to different moral economies rather than preserving a single doctrine.

### Is flaying an ancient sacrificial survival?

Barbara Hauser-Schäublin specifically examined the motif of spreading the bloody skin on the roof. Comparative ritual parallels may explain why flaying is symbolically powerful, but no archaeological or textual evidence links Sennentuntschi tales to actual human sacrifice. The hypothesis must remain comparative speculation.

### Did the doll exist as a real sexual object?

The Masciadon figure proves that at least one sexually marked anthropomorphic doll existed. Oral attribution says such figures served as surrogate women. Its uncertain date and provenance prevent generalizing to a standard Alpine institution. The tale may reflect practices, fantasy, accusation, or combinations of all three.

### Jungian interpretation

Isler’s work was foundational in collecting the corpus, but his theory of archetypal religious reality is not the only possible method. Narrative-form, social-historical, gender, labor, and performance approaches need not accept an autonomous universal archetype.

### Collector effects

Nineteenth-century collectors often converted dialect speech into literary German, moralized sexually explicit material, or omitted details. Conversely, modern theatre and film may make implicit sexuality explicit. Neither version should be mistaken automatically for an unmediated oral original.

### Status of the named Perchten scholarship

Work by Richard Wolfram, Will-Erich Peuckert, and later Perchten researchers is valuable for the historiography of Alpine masks. Some older studies sought pre-Christian continuities aggressively. Recent scholarship is more cautious and sometimes interprets Perchten runs as historical carnival and parish customs rather than transparent pagan survivals. No source found supplies the missing bridge from those traditions to the Sennentuntschi.

### State of the evidence

- **Printed texts:** scattered across nineteenth- and twentieth-century saga collections.
- **Central synthesis:** Isler’s dissertation and variant list.
- **Material object:** Rätisches Museum, Chur.
- **Modern manuscripts:** Hansjörg Schneider archive, Swiss Literary Archives.
- **Performance and controversy records:** Schneider press and legal files.
- **Lost/unlocated material:** the 1850–51 Sarganserland school collection mentioned by Senti.
- **Oral evidence:** collectors’ field notes and published retellings, sometimes decades after the alleged event.
