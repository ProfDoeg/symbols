# The Wilde Mann (Wild Man): Research Dossier

**Class:** In Alpine vernacular tradition, chiefly a **forest or mountain spirit, monstrous wild human, giant, or member of a collective “wild people”**—not a god with a stable personality, theology, priesthood, or cult. In medieval literature and art he is also an allegorical wild human (*wodewose*), while in heraldry and carnival he becomes an emblem or performed role. “Alpine pantheon” is therefore a modern cataloguing convenience, not an indigenous pantheon documented in historical sources.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The general European wild-human complex has literary antecedents in early medieval Latin and vernacular texts, but an unambiguous hairy, club-bearing “Wild Man” type crystallizes in high-medieval literature and art, especially from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The earliest presently identifiable Alpine textual witness named in the ritual literature is the late-twelfth-century **Stamser Arzneibuch**, which uses *waltscrate* (“forest shaggy one/forest goblin”) in a discussion of masks; it does not document a god, cult, or modern carnival. The earliest secure evidence for the specifically Oberstdorf **Wilde-Mändle-Tanz** is an archival chronicle entry of **26 or 29 August 1793**, not the frequently claimed Celtic or Columbanian antiquity. The wider Alpine legends were recorded principally in nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century folklore collections from oral informants.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

The name denotes a family of related but non-identical figures:

1. A hairy, enormously strong inhabitant of woods, caves, rocks, and high pastures.
2. A member of a collective race—*wilde Leute*, wild men and wild women—living outside settled society.
3. A giant, cannibal, child-stealer, hunter, or persecutor of benevolent female mountain beings.
4. A keeper of animals or possessor of pastoral knowledge, especially dairying.
5. A medieval allegory of uncontrolled appetite, madness, sin, erotic force, or the human condition outside civilization.
6. A later positive emblem of strength, fertility, endurance, naturalness, and territorial independence.
7. A human performer in foliage, lichen, straw, or fur costume at courtly entertainments, carnival, village drama, tourist festivals, and revived “traditional” runs.

These categories overlap historically but must not be collapsed into one divine biography.

### Documented names

- **German:** *der wilde Mann*, plural *wilde Männer*; also *der Wilde*, *Wildmann*, *Waldmann*, *Waldmensch*, *Waldleute*, *wilde Leute*.
- **Alemannic and Swiss German:** *Wildmannli*, *Wildmannle*, *Woldmanndli* and local diminutives.
- **Allgäu:** *Wilde Mändle*, singular *Wildes Mändle*.
- **Tyrol:** *Wilder Mann*; sometimes terms belonging to neighboring wild-folk categories, including *Fängg/Fängge*, are treated as equivalents, although the Fänggen are often female or collective beings and should remain distinct.
- **Late-twelfth-century Tyrol:** *waltscrate*, approximately “forest shaggy one” or “forest goblin.” Its precise semantic relation to the later Wild Man is interpretive.
- **Middle High German:** *wilde man*, *wildez wîp*, *wilde liute*.
- **Middle English:** *wodewose*, *woodwose*, from a compound involving “wood” and a second element of disputed derivation.
- **Medieval Latin:** *homo silvaticus*, *homo sylvestris*; plurals such as *silvani* can mean forest beings without establishing identity with the Roman god Silvanus.
- **French:** *homme sauvage*.
- **Italian:** *uomo selvatico*, *uomo salvatico*, *selvaggio*.
- **Ladin and Trentino dialects:** forms such as *om salvadech*, *l’om salvadegh*, *Salvanel*, or *Salvan*. These descend through Romance vocabulary for “woodland/wild,” ultimately Latin *silva*, “wood.”
- **German-speaking Folgaria traditions:** *wilder mon*, *Bilmon*, *Bedelmon*, as reported in comparative folklore literature.

German *wild* historically covers “uncultivated,” “outside social control,” “feral,” and “uninhabited,” not simply aggressive. Consequently *wilder Mann* initially identifies social and spatial condition rather than a personal name. The capitalization that turns it into “the Wild Man” can conceal the diversity of local beings.

### Etymological disputes

**Secure linguistic finding:** German *wilder Mann* is transparently “wild/uncultivated man.” Romance *uomo selvatico* and its Alpine dialect descendants belong to the Latin *silva/silvaticus* family.

**Disputed:** The second component of English *wodewose* has attracted numerous proposals but no consensus adequate to make it the name of a Germanic god.

**Discredited or unsupported:** Richard Bernheimer connected certain female wild-being words, such as *holzmoia*, with the Roman Maia and interpreted an early penitential passage as a rite of the goddess Maia. Later philological criticism observes that the passage concerns the **Kalends of May**, not necessarily Maia, and that the word history does not establish a surviving Maia cult. This illustrates the danger of deriving a deity from superficial verbal resemblance.

### Classifications through time

- **Medieval clerical and courtly literature:** monstrous race, mad or degraded human, allegory, antagonist, lover, or theatrical disguise.
- **Alpine folklore:** spirit-like forest resident, giant, wild hunter, culture hero, or wild human. Some stories make him mortal and capturable; others give him supernatural knowledge, strength, prophecy, or disappearance.
- **Heraldry:** an armorial supporter or charge, generally signifying strength, antiquity, fertility, or mastery of uncultivated nature.
- **Carnival:** a masked human role, sometimes captured, tried, killed, resurrected, or made to police the procession.
- **Romantic and nationalist scholarship:** conjectural survival of a Germanic nature god or “Lord of Animals.”
- **Contemporary neopagan or popular writing:** sometimes called a vegetation god, horned god, Wotan survival, or Green Man. No pre-Christian Alpine inscription, sanctuary, priesthood, or liturgy securely attests such a deity.

### Proposed identifications

- **Silvanus:** A learned comparison based on forest habitat and Latin vocabulary. No secure Alpine source says that the folkloric Wild Man is the Roman Silvanus.
- **Pan, satyrs, fauns, Silenus:** Medieval and early-modern artists and scholars used classical types to understand hairy forest beings. Similarity and artistic borrowing are attested; timeless identity is not.
- **Hercules:** In heraldry and art, a club-bearing, lion-skinned Hercules could resemble or merge visually with the Wild Man. This is a learned iconographic convergence.
- **Wotan/Wodan:** Romantic-nationalist and local heritage accounts repeatedly connect Wild Men with Wotan. The alleged proof from Columbanus is erroneous; see below.
- **“Lord of Animals”:** A twentieth-century comparative reconstruction based on stories in which the being herds, protects, or controls game. It may describe a narrative role but is not an attested Alpine title.
- **Green Man:** A modern comparative conflation. The leafy architectural head conventionally called the Green Man and the full-bodied hairy Wild Man have intersecting vegetation imagery but distinct iconographic histories.
- **Krampus and Perchten:** Neighboring masked traditions influenced modern representations of one another, but the names, calendars, dramatic functions, and documentary histories differ. Neither is simply another ancient name of the Wild Man.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest evidence problem

No ancient inscription, altar, temple record, hymn, or theophoric personal name identifies an Alpine god called *Wilder Mann*. Claims of Celtic, pre-Germanic, or prehistoric continuity proceed backward from medieval imagery or modern masking customs.

The figure’s genealogy lies instead in a broad medieval field:

- biblical wildness and punishment, especially Nebuchadnezzar’s animal-like degradation and interpretations of Esau;
- classical descriptions of satyrs, fauns, Silenus, and forest peoples;
- late-antique and medieval marvel literature describing monstrous races;
- saints, hermits, penitents, madmen, and exiles living outside society;
- vernacular romance figures who become wild through madness or bereavement;
- local forest beings and giants;
- festive disguise and courtly play.

A study of French sculpture identifies possible wild-human imagery at eleventh-century Thuret and twelfth-century Foussais, while interpreting the unambiguous mid-thirteenth-century figure at Notre-Dame de Semur-en-Auxois as Esau. These are important antecedents, not evidence for an Alpine cult.

### Stamser Arzneibuch

**Securely attested early material:** A manuscript conventionally called the *Stamser Arzneibuch*, dated to the late twelfth century, includes *scheme*, *larve*, and *waltscrate*. Telfs historical documentation treats *waltscrate* as a designation for a Wild Man. The word establishes knowledge of a shaggy forest-mask or being in medieval Tyrol; it does not establish the scenario, costume, or institutional continuity of the modern Telfs Schleicherlaufen.

### Medieval textual field

- **Beowulf:** Sometimes cited as the first literary Wild Man because its hostile wilderness beings are placed in the *wōs*/*wudu* semantic field. This is a modern typological comparison; the poem does not present the later club-bearing Alpine *Wilder Mann*.
- **Wigamur** (thirteenth century): Contains *wilde man* and *wildez wîp*. This is a secure medieval vernacular usage.
- **Dietrich-cycle poetry and Sigenot material:** Includes wild men and giants. The surviving transmission is complicated; later manuscripts should not be projected intact into the fragmentary thirteenth-century stage.
- **Yvain and related Arthurian romances:** Knights become wild through madness and social separation. These are “wild-man episodes,” not encounters with the Alpine being as a named god.
- **Taymouth Hours** (England, c. 1325–40): A marginal image shows a wild man abducting a maiden. It documents the international iconographic type.
- **Jehan de Grise’s illustrated Marco Polo** (1344): Shows wild men as cannibals, besiegers, and hunters, illustrating the motif’s broad medieval range.
- **Court entertainments:** The disastrous Paris *Bal des Ardents* of 1393 used resinous hairy “wild men” costumes; four dancers died when they caught fire. It documents aristocratic performance, not Oberstdorf ritual continuity.

### Alpine legend collections

The decisive textual corpus is largely nineteenth-century, even when the stories may be older orally:

- Ignaz Vinzenz Zingerle, *Sagen aus Tirol* (first edition 1850; enlarged later editions) and *Sagen, Märchen und Gebräuche aus Tirol* (1859).
- Zingerle, “Der wilde Mann” and “Einiges über den wilden Mann,” in *Zeitschrift für deutsche Mythologie* (1850s).
- Theodor Vernaleken, *Alpensagen* (1858).
- Christian Schneller, *Märchen und Sagen aus Wälschtirol* (1867).
- Johann Adolf Heyl, *Volkssagen aus Tirol* (1897).
- Ernst Ludwig Rochholz, Swiss and Aargau legend collections.
- Dietrich Jecklin, *Volkstümliches aus Graubünden*.
- Jakob Kuoni, *Sagen des Kantons St. Gallen*.
- Josef Müller, *Sagen aus Uri*, based on systematically recorded oral testimony in the early twentieth century.
- Jacob Grimm, *Deutsche Mythologie* (1835; substantially expanded later) and Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm’s legend and tale collections.
- *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens* (1927–42), which systematized older collections under entries including *Wilder Mann*, *Wilde Leute*, and related beings.

These publications are evidence of what collectors printed and how they classified it. Their dates are not automatically dates of origin for their tales.

### Grimm corpus

Jacob Grimm assembled forest women, wild folk, giants, hunters, and seasonal customs into a reconstructed “German mythology.” His work was foundational but frequently sought pagan continuities where the surviving record begins much later. It must be read as nineteenth-century comparative scholarship, not as a transcription of an intact ancient faith.

The Grimms’ Low German tale **“De wilde Mann”** appeared in editions of *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* through the fifth edition and was omitted from the final edition. Its Wild Man ravages crops, carries away a child, and becomes connected with marvelous military aid. The editorial history shows that even the printed “canonical” Grimm corpus changed.

**“Iron Hans”** (*Der Eisenhans*, KHM 136), introduced into the collection in 1844 and revised thereafter, contains a rust-colored wild man captured from a forest pool, a king’s son, magical assistance, and eventual restoration. It has powerfully shaped modern Wild Man psychology, but it is a nineteenth-century literary-folkloric text and not evidence for an ancient Alpine deity.

### Manuscript and testimony gaps

The medieval material survives in manuscripts often centuries removed from conjectured oral antecedents. Alpine oral traditions were mostly fixed in print after Romantic nationalism had already taught collectors to look for pagan survivals. Dialect normalization, editorial rewriting, and the absence of recordings for early collectors limit recovery of exact performances.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic actions

Across the corpus, the Wild Man:

- inhabits forest edges, caves, ravines, rock shelters, remote farms, and high pastures;
- possesses extraordinary strength and may uproot trees;
- controls, protects, hunts, or travels with wild animals;
- steals cattle, milk, eggs, food, women, or children;
- teaches dairying, butter-making, or cheese-making, sometimes after capture or intoxication;
- prophesies weather or events and utters cryptic messages;
- pursues the *Salige* or wild maidens;
- punishes mockery and indiscreet demands;
- abducts women in medieval imagery;
- can be captured, chained, shot, or ritually “killed”;
- in heraldry, protects the shield;
- in festivals, represents disorder, territorial strength, fertility, theatrical wonder, or an outsider brought under communal control.

There is no single canonical domain comparable to that of a temple god.

### Physical form

The standard medieval type is:

- adult male;
- nude except for dense body hair, moss, leaves, lichen, or a belt;
- bearded, long-haired, and physically powerful;
- human-faced, unlike a satyr with hooves or a devil with explicit demonic anatomy;
- armed with a club, uprooted tree, branch, or staff.

Wild women and children form family groups in late-medieval art. The female may be hair-covered while the breasts, hands, feet, face, elbows, and knees remain exposed. Alpine performers reproduce “hair” with fir lichen (*Baumbart*), moss, ivy, bark, conifer branches, straw, or animal skins.

### Iconographic development

**High Middle Ages:** Monstrous or morally degraded outsider; sometimes identifiable with biblical or romance figures.

**Fourteenth and fifteenth centuries:** Rapid expansion in manuscript illumination, tapestry, ivory, metalwork, prints, marginal art, court spectacle, and heraldry. Wild people hunt, feast, rear children, fight knights, storm the Castle of Love, or inhabit an imagined woodland society.

**Late medieval positive turn:** The Wild Man could embody virility, fecundity, toughness, freedom from corrupt urban life, or a paradisal natural simplicity. These meanings coexist with cannibal and abductor imagery.

**Dürer and German Renaissance art:** The hairy club-bearer became a sophisticated ornamental and heraldic motif. The Metropolitan Museum’s late-fifteenth-century Nuremberg ewer finial shows an enameled Wild Man holding a shield; museum interpretation associates him with heraldic strength, virility, and procreation.

### Heraldry

Wild Men occur as both charges and supporters. Their clubs and naked strength make them visually suitable guardians of a shield.

- A Schaffhausen stained-glass armorial panel of about 1505, from the workshop of the Lucerne glass painter Oswald, shows two Wild Men supporting an armorial pyramid.
- The Wild Man increasingly displaced the cross as an emblem connected with the **League of the Ten Jurisdictions** in Graubünden; this unconventional form remained visible in the 1803 Act of Mediation and on a Rhaetian Museum architectural stone of 1899.
- Swiss civic and family heraldry used Wild Men widely, though it is inaccurate to speak of one universal “Swiss coat of arms” supported by them.
- The great royal arms of Prussia used two club-bearing Wild Men as supporters. Their adoption belongs to early-modern dynastic heraldry, not an ancestral Prussian cult. Assertions that they were borrowed specifically from Denmark or Pomerania remain heraldic hypotheses.
- The figures’ survival into modern armorial display reflects institutional heraldic continuity, not continuous worship.

### Colors, numbers, plants, and animals

No universally attested sacred color, number, weekday, planet, or animal belongs to the Wild Man.

Locally recurring materials include:

- green foliage and ivy;
- brown fur;
- gray-green beard lichen;
- uprooted fir or leafy branch;
- club or tree trunk;
- game animals, cattle, goats, bears, and deer.

The “twelve Wild Men” of the 1793 Oberstdorf comedy is a performance cast, not demonstrably a sacred number.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

No early source gives the Wild Man a stable divine parentage. Wild men and wild women mate and have children in medieval art and story, but they form a race or household rather than a divine dynasty.

Modern assertions that the Wild Man is:

- a son or form of Wotan,
- a descendant of Pan or Silvanus,
- Cernunnos,
- the consort of a mother goddess,
- the husband of Perchta,
- the ancestor of Krampus,

are unattested as Alpine traditional genealogies.

### Human and supernatural relations

Wild Women may be wives, companions, counterparts, captives, or independent beings. The *Salige*—usually beneficent female mountain beings—are sometimes his victims. Fänggen can overlap with the wild-people category but also appear as separate captors and monsters. Human woodcutters, herders, farmers, hunters, and children are the most frequent interaction partners.

### Documented named being-to-being interactions

The source record contains very few interactions with named gods. The following are the checkable supernatural relations:

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The death-message legend

**Folklore; nineteenth-century record.** Near Imst, a silent wild woman visits a family’s hearth for seven years. In the mountains a Wild Man orders the farmer to tell Stitzl and Wizl that Thorizl is dead. When the message is delivered, the wild woman laments, says the family could have learned much had they questioned her, and disappears forever.

The tale belongs to an international migratory-legend family in which a supernatural visitor departs upon hearing that another being—often named in strange, alliterative language—has died. It suggests a hidden wild-folk society but supplies no theology or genealogy.

### Capture of the culture-bringer

**Folklore; Alpine variants recorded in the nineteenth century.** Humans catch a Wild Man, sometimes by making him drunk. He possesses knowledge of dairy processing and teaches the manufacture of butter or cheese. In one common punch line, he claims that if held longer he would have taught humans how to make wax from whey or milk.

The story simultaneously renders him inferior—capturable by villagers—and culturally senior—the keeper of knowledge. Comparisons with the drunken Silenus are learned and plausible as motif analogies, not proof of direct descent.

### The unwanted share of the hunt

**Regional folklore.** In Folgaria and neighboring districts a man hears the Wild Man or Wild Hunter and calls for a portion of his quarry. The being leaves half a human corpse at the house. The petitioner must persuade him to take it back.

The motif warns against demanding participation in a supernatural hunt. Some versions shift among Wild Man, Wild Hunter, and wild host; collectors’ classifications therefore matter.

### Persecution of the Salige

**Regional Tyrolean folklore.** The Wild Man pursues benevolent wild maidens, sometimes tearing them apart and leaving pieces for humans who requested a share of the hunt. In another motif, a Salige can take refuge on a tree stump marked with three crosses. This incorporates Christian apotropaic protection into the legend rather than preserving a pristine pagan narrative.

### Hitte Hatte and Jordan

**Regional folklore with later elaboration.** The giant Wild Man Jordan and his gigantic wife live above the Gurgltal. Jordan steals children and livestock and persecutes the Salige. He captures a young Salige, intending to eat her; his wife keeps the captive as a servant and mockingly names her Hitte Hatte. Hitte Hatte escapes, enters a poor farmer’s household, works with supernatural efficiency—especially at flax—and brings prosperity.

This cycle opposes destructive male wildness to the domestic blessing of a female mountain spirit. Printed retellings vary greatly in narrative fullness, so details in modern anthologies should not all be credited to Zingerle.

### The Wild Man’s reversed clothing and eggs

**Regional folklore.** Some Tyrolean stories emphasize incomprehensible behavior. The being wears a coat in summer heat but leaves the choice open in bad weather. In Langtaufers he visits homes during church services, drinks eggs, and arranges the shells into figures; if disturbed, he restores the arrangement on his next visit. Scholars compare the egg-shell episode with tests used to expose changelings or elvish beings.

### Iron Hans

**Later canonical fairy-tale elaboration, not specifically Alpine.** Hunters vanish in a dangerous forest. A mysterious wild man is pulled from a pool and caged. A prince releases him and enters his protection; the Wild Man gives him tasks, magical aid, golden hair, armor, and eventual success. At the end Iron Hans is restored as an enchanted king.

The Grimms’ nineteenth-century text recasts the Wild Man as initiator and benefactor. Robert Bly’s *Iron John* (1990) subsequently turned it into a psychological myth of masculine initiation, a highly influential modern reinvention rather than historical Alpine interpretation.

### Courtly capture and Wild Man games

From the fourteenth century onward Wild Men appear in court entries, weddings, masquerades, and staged combats. They may be chained by maidens, conquer or defend a castle, dance, or submit to civilized rulers. The 1393 *Bal des Ardents* became notorious because flammable costumes ignited. Such performances supplied models that could circulate into urban and village festivals, complicating claims that every rural Wild Man mask independently preserves prehistoric religion.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No institutional cult

There is no verified:

- temple or shrine dedicated to the Wild Man;
- priesthood;
- sacrificial calendar;
- hymn or prayer;
- votive inscription;
- theophoric personal-name series;
- oracle;
- ecclesiastical record condemning worship of a god by this name.

“Cult dance” is therefore not an appropriate documentary description of the Oberstdorf performance unless used explicitly as the terminology of its modern advocates.

### Oberstdorf Wilde-Mändle-Tanz

The earliest secure local evidence is found in two chronicles:

- Ignaz Math’s chronicle: “26.8.1793” a “Comedy of the twelve wild men” was performed for Clemens Wenzeslaus of Saxony, elector of Trier and bishop of Augsburg.
- The Schöllang chronicle gives 29 August 1793 and says that a comedy of twelve Wild Men and a Dance of Death were presented.

In 1811 Oberstdorf performers again danced before Clemens Wenzeslaus at Oberdorf near Hindelang. A published 1893 description says thirteen strong men wore costumes made from fir beard-lichen, moved by great leaps, and formed tableaux without a continuous dramatic plot. Documented revivals occurred in 1871, 1873, and 1891; its present periodic form is a product of these revivals and later heritage organization.

**Unsupported origin claim:** Local literature has linked the dance to the *Vita Columbani*. Jonas of Bobbio, writing around 640, reports that Columbanus encountered Suebi preparing a large vessel of beer as an offering to Wodan. He does not describe the Oberstdorf choreography, lichen costume, wooden cups in the claimed modern form, or a Wild-Man dance. Columbanus did not author his own vita. The proposed direct continuity is therefore modern invention.

### Telfs Schleicherlaufen

Telfs possesses unusually rich documentation for its carnival, but the evidence must be layered:

- late twelfth century: vocabulary of mask and *waltscrate* in the Stamser manuscript;
- c. 1500 onward: governmental restrictions on “public rejoicings” and carnival;
- early-modern period: courtly mummings, guild practices, and popular theater supply relevant contexts;
- nineteenth century: the modern form consolidates;
- present practice: Wild Men covered in tree lichen act as order-keepers around the *Panzenaff*.

The University of Innsbruck’s cultural-history project explicitly cautions against older one-cause interpretations as fertility cult or mythic survival. It emphasizes play, inversion, court entertainment, urban guild custom, and late-medieval theater.

### Tramin Egetmann procession

The South Tyrolean Egetmann procession includes a Wild Man covered in ivy, with a hare-skin face, who is shot by a hunter in the town square. The figure belongs to a dramatic carnival sequence that also includes Schnappviecher, bear figures, laundresses, and tradespeople. The “death” is theatrical; it does not document human sacrifice or a former sacrifice of a vegetation god.

### Captured and judged Wild Men

In several Alpine carnival forms a foliage- or fur-clad Wild Man is chased, bound, brought into the village, accused of the year’s offenses, and symbolically punished or killed. Early folklore scholarship interpreted this as a dying vegetation deity, scapegoat, or fertility demon. Those explanations remain comparative hypotheses. The performance can also be understood within carnival justice, festive inversion, civic theater, and medieval capture imagery.

### Perchten, Krampus, and Gastein

Perchten and Krampus traditions are relevant comparanda, not identical cults.

- The earliest archival notice found by Hans Schuhladen records people being rewarded in **1582 at Dießen** for “hunting the Percht,” without describing modern costumes.
- Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Bavarian, Tyrolean, and Salzburg sources use *Percht*, not *Krampus*, for many masked activities.
- Church and governmental bans targeted nocturnal disorder, begging, noise, violence, disguise, and unauthorized festivity. A ban does not prove that authorities recognized a pagan god.
- Krampus is historically tied to St Nicholas visitation and Christian devil imagery.
- Modern runs increasingly mix Perchten, Krampuses, Wild Men, heavy-metal aesthetics, fire effects, fantasy masks, and tourism.

### Lötschental Tschäggättä

The fur-clad, wooden-masked Tschäggättä of the Lötschental resemble Wild Men visually but possess their own name and local history.

The earliest commonly cited official mention is a **Kippel church chronicle of 1860**, complaining that prohibitions had failed to suppress the “terrible abuse” of the Tschäggättä. Local origin legends derive them from the **Schurtendiebe**, powerful outlaws or earlier inhabitants who raided in disguise. These stories are folklore, not independent proof of migration-era origin. Present practice is curated through the Lötschental Museum, tourism, carving traditions, and public processions.

### Swiss folklore atlas

The *Atlas der schweizerischen Volkskunde* arose from questionnaires devised by Paul Geiger and Richard Weiss. Between 1937 and 1942, roughly 1,200 contributors in 400 communities returned about 80,000 responses and 4,000 photographs; results eventually appeared on 320 maps. It is exceptional evidence for twentieth-century distribution, terminology, and practice. It cannot by itself date mapped customs to antiquity. Its “Maskenbräuche” map records geographic patterning but does not establish that all masked figures share an origin.

### Sacrifice and violence

No source establishes human sacrifice to the Wild Man. Legends do contain cannibalism, abduction, dismemberment of Salige, child-taking, and the leaving of human body parts. Carnival includes symbolic execution and real risks from beating, fire, intoxication, and crowd disorder. These facts must not be converted into an undocumented sacrificial cult.

There is no evidence of temple prostitution associated with the Wild Man.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Early medieval antecedents, c. 600–1000

Christian, classical, and marvel traditions supplied categories of animalized humans, wilderness penitents, demons, satyrs, and monstrous peoples. No Alpine Wild Man cult is attested. The *Vita Columbani* records a Wodan beer offering but not Wild Men.

### High Middle Ages, c. 1100–1300

Words for wild men and women enter vernacular literature. The late-twelfth-century *waltscrate* is the earliest relevant Tyrolean term located in the present research. Sculpture and manuscripts develop a recognizable hairy human. The figure participates in Christian moral thought, romance, and theories of humanity outside social order.

### Fourteenth and fifteenth centuries

The image proliferates. Wild people appear in manuscripts, tapestries, household objects, tournaments, court festivities, and heraldry. Their meanings range from sin and bestiality to love, fertility, natural freedom, and chivalric strength. The 1393 Paris fire demonstrates embodied court performance.

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Printed imagery and heraldry stabilize the club-bearing type. Wild Men serve as shield supporters in German and Swiss contexts. Carnival and public spectacle draw on the established mask. Authorities issue restrictions on public festivity. None of this documents an underground temple cult.

### Eighteenth century

Court entertainment, village drama, and carnival continue, though documentation is uneven. Governments and churches regulate masking and nocturnal disorder. Wild-Man inns, signs, civic emblems, and armorial figures extend the motif into commercial life.

### 1793–nineteenth-century Oberstdorf

The 1793 “Comedy of the twelve Wild Men” is the first secure Oberstdorf performance. The 1811 court performance and later revivals show a tradition responsive to elite patronage, war charity, local heritage, and public entertainment.

### Romantic-nationalist collection, 1800–1900

Grimm, Zingerle, Vernaleken, Rochholz, and other collectors recorded and reorganized oral traditions. Their achievement preserved much that would otherwise be lost, but their mythological frameworks often treated late evidence as fragments of ancient Germanic religion. Folklore became both archive and nation-building material.

### Early twentieth century

The *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens* and regional studies classified motifs systematically. At the same time, continuity theories intensified. Richard Wolfram, Viktor von Geramb, and related folklorists interpreted Alpine customs as Germanic survivals overlaid by Christianity.

National Socialist cultural policy appropriated this scholarship. A 1940 photo essay on the Gastein Perchten procession described it as ancient, repeatedly banned pagan heritage and as an expression of peasant *Volkskraft*. Later archival work found no evidence sufficient for that narrative. The political use is documented; the proposed prehistoric continuity is not.

### Postwar reassessment

Richard Bernheimer’s *Wild Men in the Middle Ages* (1952) made the figure a major art-historical and cultural-historical subject. His broad synthesis remains indispensable, but some etymological and ritual-continuity proposals have been criticized.

Hans Moser, Hans Schuhladen, and later ethnologists shifted attention toward dated archival evidence, theatricality, social organization, and repeated reinvention. The Innsbruck carnival project likewise stresses Renaissance and Baroque court festivities, guild customs, and folk drama.

### Present

The Wild Man survives through:

- Oberstdorf’s periodic Wilde-Mändle-Tanz;
- Telfs and other Tyrolean carnival roles;
- South Tyrolean Egetmann and related dramatic figures;
- Swiss Wildmannli events and heraldry;
- museums, mask collections, municipal signs, and tourism;
- reenactment, photography, fantasy culture, and neopagan reinterpretation.

This is continuous cultural use of a mutable image, not demonstrable continuous worship of one god.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Psychology and the men’s movement

Robert Bly’s *Iron John: A Book About Men* (1990) interpreted the Grimm tale as a map of masculine psychological initiation. It made the Wild Man a symbol of wounded, instinctive, and recoverable masculinity and helped shape the mythopoetic men’s movement.

This is a modern Jung-influenced reading. Its “ancient initiation” is not documented as the function of the Alpine legends or Oberstdorf dance.

### Neopaganism and esotericism

Modern practitioners sometimes identify the Wild Man with:

- a horned god;
- Cernunnos;
- Pan;
- Wotan;
- the Green Man;
- an archetypal forest guardian;
- an ecological spirit;
- a masculine counterpart of Perchta.

Such practices may be sincere contemporary religion, but no documented line of initiation connects them to a medieval Wild Man priesthood. No significant role for the figure in Renaissance Hermetic orders, the Golden Dawn, or classical grimoire demonology has been established.

### Literature and visual culture

The figure materially influenced:

- the Grimms’ “Iron Hans” and “De wilde Mann”;
- medievalist fantasy’s hairy woodland races;
- J. R. R. Tolkien’s Drúedain, whom Tolkien playfully related to later Wild Men within his fictional history;
- children’s books and regional pageants;
- heraldic branding, inns, breweries, and place names;
- Charles Fréger’s photographic project *Wilder Mann* (2012), which assembled European masquerade traditions into a striking comparative visual corpus.

Photography and festival marketing can make regionally separate practices appear to be manifestations of a single ancient being. That unity is largely curatorial.

### Nationalist appropriation

Nineteenth-century nationalism sought ancestral Germanic religion beneath Christian custom. National Socialist folklore research intensified racial and *völkisch* readings, especially for Perchten and winter masquerades. The Wild Man’s bodily strength, “primitiveness,” and imagined autochthony made him politically useful.

No evidence found in this pass supports a present-day standardized extremist cult of the Wild Man. Individual deployments in ethnonationalist or “blood and soil” imagery should be documented case by case rather than presumed.

### Commercial and civic use

“Wilder Mann” is common as an inn, hotel, tavern, street, mine, and mountain name. A Wild Man sign can refer to an establishment rather than belief in a local apparition. Heraldic supporters likewise function as civic and dynastic symbols, not objects of worship.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Bernheimer’s synthesis

Richard Bernheimer’s *Wild Men in the Middle Ages: A Study in Art, Sentiment, and Demonology* (1952) remains foundational. Its chapters address natural history, mythology, theatrical embodiment, learned interpretation, eroticism, and heraldry. Its enduring achievement is to treat the Wild Man as a historically variable cultural construction.

Its limitations include diffusionist speculation, attempts to recover ancient ritual beneath late evidence, and particular etymological proposals such as the Maia connection.

### Survival theory versus historical performance

**Older position:** Alpine Wild Men were remnants of pre-Christian Germanic or Celtic vegetation, fertility, hunting, or initiation cults. Modern masking preserved ancient sacred action beneath Christian labels.

**Evidence offered:**

- vegetation costumes;
- capture and symbolic killing;
- seasonal appearance;
- great strength and sexuality;
- forest residence;
- church prohibitions;
- comparison with Pan, Silvanus, Wotan, and “Lord of Animals” figures.

**Critical position:** Those features are widespread and can be generated by medieval allegory, carnival inversion, court spectacle, guild drama, Christian devil imagery, and repeated revival. The earliest records are medieval or modern, while claimed prehistoric stages lack bridging evidence.

**Current defensible conclusion:** Pre-Christian European concepts of wilderness may have contributed remotely, but no uninterrupted Alpine Wild Man cult can be reconstructed. Each local practice must be dated from its own documents.

### Was the Wild Man a real feral human?

Medieval and early-modern observers sometimes treated wild humans as possible biological beings. Hermits, mentally ill wanderers, exiles, people with hypertrichosis, and displayed captives could be interpreted through the Wild Man image.

However, identifying the tradition as a folk memory of Neanderthals, isolated pre-Indo-European populations, or a surviving hominin lacks evidence. Such theories commonly confuse the historicity of reports with the biological reality of their described creature.

### “Lord of Animals”

Some Alpine legends make the Wild Man master or protector of game, and comparative folklorists have placed him in the widespread “Master of Animals” pattern. This is useful motif analysis. It becomes overstatement when treated as proof that all Wild Men descend from a single Paleolithic god.

### The Columbanus controversy

The *Vita Columbani* is frequently used in Oberstdorf heritage literature to claim that modern dancers reproduce an Alamannic Wodan sacrifice of c. 610.

What is secure:

- Jonas of Bobbio wrote the saint’s life after Columbanus’s death.
- It describes a beer offering associated with Wodan among Suebi near Bregenz.

What is not in the source:

- Wild Men;
- lichen costumes;
- the Oberstdorf dance;
- twelve dancers;
- wooden dance tablets;
- an unbroken chain to 1793.

The supposed proof is therefore a modern etiological construction.

### Perchten/Krampus continuity debate

Early-twentieth-century folklorists portrayed the runs as fossilized Germanic rites. Later archival research located a late-sixteenth-century starting point for detailed documentation and showed that early records call the activity *Percht*, not Krampus. Contemporary anthropologists further document tourism, internet networking, fire shows, commercial mask-carving, and identity politics.

The lesson applies directly to Wild Men: resemblance is not continuity, and prohibition does not prove pagan origin.

### State of evidence and archives

Important evidence survives in:

- medieval manuscripts and church sculpture;
- European museum collections of prints, tapestries, stained glass, ivory, metalwork, and masks;
- Tyrolean, Bavarian, Swiss, and municipal archives;
- local chronicles at Oberstdorf;
- the Empirische Kulturwissenschaft Schweiz archive and its *Atlas der schweizerischen Volkskunde* holdings;
- the Lötschental Museum;
- the Austrian Museum of Folk Life and Folk Art’s journal archive;
- digitized nineteenth-century books and journals.

The Swiss Atlas preserves an extraordinary twentieth-century distributional snapshot, but much early oral performance is irrecoverable.

### Evidentiary conclusions

1. The Wild Man is securely medieval, but his specific Alpine narratives are mainly documented in nineteenth-century collections.
2. There is no securely attested Alpine god, institutional cult, temple, priesthood, or sacrifice bearing this name.
3. Medieval literary, artistic, heraldic, and theatrical forms materially shaped later Alpine representations.
4. Oberstdorf’s documented history begins in 1793; its claimed Celtic/Wodanic continuity is unsupported.
5. Perchten, Krampus, Tschäggättä, and Wild Men belong to interacting but distinguishable traditions.
6. Pagan-survival explanations are part of the figure’s modern historiography, particularly its Romantic and *völkisch* phases; they are not established ancient history.
7. The Wild Man’s plurality—monster, spirit, mad human, teacher, abductor, heraldic guardian, carnival performer, and modern archetype—is the historical record, not corruption of a single original identity.
