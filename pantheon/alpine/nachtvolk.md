# The Nachtvolk (the Night Folk): Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective nocturnal spirit-host (*Geisterschar*, “host of spirits”); in some eastern-Swiss variants a procession of the dead or death-omen, but not a god, goddess, demon species with a fixed genealogy, or organized “pantheon.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** The exact vernacular name **Nachtvolk** is securely printed in Franz Josef Vonbun’s *Volkssagen aus Vorarlberg* (Vienna, **1847**), subsequently enlarged as *Die Sagen Vorarlbergs* (Innsbruck, **1858**). A possibly related but not identical belief is documented much earlier as **Nachtschar** (“night-host”) in the 1586–1587 witch-trial records of the Oberstdorf horse-herder Chonrad Stoeckhlin. Renward Cysat’s Lucerne writings from around 1600 attest the neighboring **Guotisheer/Wuotisheer**, not the name *Nachtvolk*. The Nachtvolk therefore has an early-modern comparative prehistory, but its exact name and characteristic corpus are securely documented only from the nineteenth century.

**Controlling finding:** “The Nachtvolk” is authentic regional folklore, but “the Nachtvolk of an Alpine pantheon” is a modern classificatory construction. No primary source establishes an Alpine pantheon, a single trans-Alpine Nachtvolk cult, temples, priests, sacrifices, canonical genealogy, or worship. The documented tradition is concentrated in **Vorarlberg, Liechtenstein, the Swiss cantons of Graubünden, St. Gallen, Glarus, and neighboring Allgäu**, with local extensions and analogues elsewhere. Tyrolean, Bavarian, Salzburg Perchten, Krampus, Tschäggättä, Wild Hunt, and Walliser Gratzug materials belong to overlapping but historically distinct traditions.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early documentary analogue]** Evidence predating the printed Nachtvolk corpus but not using the exact name or preserving all its features.
- **[Secure nineteenth-century attestation]** A printed regional collection whose collector identified a local oral source or locality.
- **[Regional variant]** A local form not safely generalized to the entire tradition.
- **[Learned systematization]** A collector’s or scholar’s attempt to organize heterogeneous traditions.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A secondary equation with another figure or mythic complex.
- **[Folklore]** A reported oral legend or customary belief, evidence that the story was told rather than that its supernatural event occurred.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical interpretation inferred by scholars from comparative material.
- **[Modern invention or reinvention]** A recent attribution, synthesis, artistic representation, revival, or commercial use.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[Secure nineteenth-century attestation; folklore]** In the core Vorarlberg tradition, *das Nachtvolk* is a collective, normally leaderless company that travels after dark along customary routes, through ravines and woods, over crossroads, and sometimes straight through buildings. It may be invisible except for wind, noise, or extraordinarily beautiful instrumental music. It occupies abandoned alpine dairies after the seasonal descent, holds feasts, interferes with cattle, teaches music, warns or punishes human witnesses, and sometimes kills, eats, and restores a cow.

**[Regional variant]** In Graubünden, St. Gallen, and Glarus the designation shades into *Nachtschar* and *Totenvolk*. The host may predict death, pestilence, or war; appear as black figures; march two by two behind a coffin; or include the “shadows” of living people who will soon die. Klaus Beitl’s foundational distribution study therefore describes a continuum, not a single universally consistent supernatural population: beneficent or ambivalent musical spirits in parts of Vorarlberg and Allgäu; more dangerous death-portents in eastern Switzerland; and aerial *Wuota/Muota* hosts toward the northern and eastern margins. See Beitl’s [“Die Sagen vom Nachtvolk”](https://volkskundemuseum.at/jart/prj3/volkskundemuseum/data/publikation/1538569885085/1538569885085.pdf).

### Names

- **Nachtvolk** — Standard German, literally “night people” or “night folk.” Grammatically neuter collective: *das Nachtvolk*. This is descriptive rather than a recoverable ancient divine name.
- **Nachtvolk / s Nachtvolk** — Alemannic regional usage in Vorarlberg, Liechtenstein, and German-speaking Switzerland.
- **Nachtschar** — “night-host” or “night-company.” Especially important in Graubünden and in the Stoeckhlin trial evidence.
- **Totenvolk / Todtenvolk** — “dead folk,” prominent in eastern-Swiss variants where the procession is explicitly composed of the dead.
- **Totenzug / Totenprozession** — “procession of the dead/dead procession,” a broader Swiss and Alpine complex.
- **Gratzug** — Walliser designation, conventionally understood as a procession moving along a mountain ridge (*Grat*), though its legendary routes also descend through valleys, ravines, villages, and customary funeral ways.
- **Symphonie** — An older Walliser designation reported for the Totenprozession, apparently referring to its audible music, chanting, or collective sound.
- **Volkgang** — “going of the folk,” another Swiss term for a nocturnal spirit-procession.
- **Fliegendes Heer** — “flying host,” used by Dietrich Jecklin for a Prättigau Nachtvolk variant.
- **Wütendes Heer / wildes Heer** — “furious host/wild host,” the broader German-language legend complex.
- **Wuota, Wuotas, Muota, Wuetisheer, Wuotisheer, Guotisheer, Wuotinshör** — local names for neighboring nocturnal hosts. Their resemblance to *Wodan/Wuotan* encouraged antiquarian derivations, but phonetic resemblance alone does not prove that every local name originally meant “Wodan’s army.”
- **Wilde Jagd** — “Wild Hunt,” a learned umbrella term as well as a vernacular expression in some places. It overlaps with Nachtvolk traditions but is not universally synonymous with them.

No traditional individual epithets, liturgical titles, sacred scripts, or names in non-European source languages are documented. The subject belongs to Alemannic and neighboring German/Romansh oral culture; Arabic, Sanskrit, Persian, Akkadian, Sumerian, and Old Norse sources contain no attestations of this regional name.

### Etymology and disputed equations

**[Secure linguistic reading]** *Nachtvolk* is an ordinary German compound, *Nacht* + *Volk*. There is no evidence that it encrypts the name of a forgotten deity.

**[Learned systematization]** Jacob Grimm placed *nachtvolk* among night-riding beings, witches, and travelling supernatural companies in later editions of *Deutsche Mythologie*, explicitly citing Vonbun. Grimm’s placement was influential but comparative: it did not produce an ancient textual attestation. His discussion can be read in the digitized [*Deutsche Mythologie* chapter](https://projekt-gutenberg.org/authors/brueder-grimm/books/jacob-grimm-deutsche-mythologie/chapter/34/).

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century mythographers often treated the Wild Hunt, *Wuotas*, Türst, and similar hosts as degraded survivals of Wodan/Odin. This remains possible for particular names and motifs but is not demonstrable for the entire Nachtvolk corpus. A particularly revealing example occurs in Jakob Kuoni’s 1903 Flums legend: the text describes numerous black figures surrounding a tall white man in a broad hat, while the printed parenthesis “**Wodan!**” is an editorial interpretation, not something the informant’s narrative establishes. The text is preserved online as [“Das Nachtvolk vom Berg”](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/maerchendatenbank/3103/das-nachtvolk-vom-berg-flums-sg).

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Wolfgang Behringer argues that the joyful, musical *Nachtschar* belongs to a “good society” complex, related to medieval and early-modern *bona societas* traditions rather than simply to a demonic Wild Hunt. Critics have cautioned that his reconstruction of sixteenth-century belief draws partly on nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore collected centuries later. The evidentiary link is therefore suggestive, not a demonstrated continuous creed.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earlier analogues

#### Chonrad Stoeckhlin, 1586–1587

**[Early documentary analogue]** Chonrad Stoeckhlin, a horse-herder of Oberstdorf in the Allgäu, testified during witch proceedings that he travelled with a **Nachtschar**. His account involved nocturnal journeying, the souls of the dead, moral or penitential business, and a supernatural companion. The interrogations eventually contributed to witch prosecutions and executions.

This is the earliest securely datable evidence for the word *Nachtschar* in the relevant region. It is not an attestation of every later Nachtvolk motif, and the judicial record is mediated by interrogation, scribes, and demonological expectations. Behringer’s reconstruction appears in *Chonrad Stoeckhlin und die Nachtschar* (1994; English translation, *Shaman of Oberstdorf*, 1998). A contemporary review summarizes the proposed connection with the *ludus bonae societatis* and its methodological limits: [Persée review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhmc_0048-8003_1997_num_44_2_1871_t1_0360_0000_2?pageId=T1_361).

#### Renward Cysat, late sixteenth/early seventeenth century

**[Early documentary analogue]** Lucerne town clerk Renward Cysat (1545–1614) recorded beliefs about the **Guotisheer/Wuotisheer** and related nocturnal phenomena in his *Collectanea Chronica*. These manuscripts are early evidence for the central-Swiss furious host later associated with Türst and Sträggele. They do not securely name the Nachtvolk.

### The exact Nachtvolk corpus

#### Franz Josef Vonbun, 1847 and 1858

**[Earliest secure exact-name attestation]** Vonbun’s *Volkssagen aus Vorarlberg* (1847) is the earliest securely located print source for the regional Nachtvolk by that name. The enlarged *Die Sagen Vorarlbergs* (1858) organizes a distinct “NACHTVOLK” section, including:

- “Wie das Nachtvolk die Kuh verschmauset”
- “Der Einaug”
- “Das Messer im Knie”
- “Die Musik und das Weib”
- stories of houses whose cross-aligned doors must be left open
- punishment and later healing at the same place
- musical instruction and enforced secrecy.

The digitized 1858 volume and contents are available through [Google Books](https://books.google.at/books?id=71SWASWyIIsC). Vonbun’s material is primary evidence for nineteenth-century collection and reported oral tradition, not proof of prehistoric antiquity.

#### Theodor Vernaleken, *Alpensagen*, 1858

**[Learned regional compilation]** Vernaleken brought together traditions from Switzerland, Vorarlberg, Carinthia, Styria, Salzburg, Upper Austria, and Lower Austria. Its value is comparative reach; its limitation is that heterogeneous local traditions are placed within a single “Alpine” frame. A digitized copy is linked from the bibliographical discussion of [*Sagen aus Uri*](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagen_aus_Uri).

#### Ignaz Vinzenz Zingerle, 1859; later *Sagen aus Tirol*, 1891

**[Regional comparative corpus]** Zingerle’s *Sagen, Märchen und Gebräuche aus Tirol* (1859) documents Tyrolean spirit processions, Wild Hunts, revenants, Perchten-like beings, and alpine-dairy legends. It is indispensable comparative material, but it does not make the Vorarlberg-Graubünden Nachtvolk a pan-Tyrolean deity. The 1859 volume survives in a [Wikimedia digital facsimile](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ASagen%2C_M%C3%A4rchen_und_Gebr%C3%A4uche_aus_Tirol-_ges._und_hrsg._von_Ignaz_Vincenz_Zingerle_%28IA_10130186bsb%29.pdf).

#### Ernst Ludwig Rochholz, *Schweizersagen aus dem Aargau*, 1856

**[Regional comparative corpus]** Rochholz records Aargau Wild Hunt, Wotan, death, revenant, and nocturnal-rider material. His mythological commentary often seeks Germanic divine survivals more confidently than later scholarship permits. The work is available in [Google Books](https://books.google.ch/books?hl=de&id=KzNCAAAAcAAJ).

#### Dietrich Jecklin, *Volksthümliches aus Graubünden*, 1874–1878

**[Secure nineteenth-century attestation]** Jecklin’s corpus is central. Its first part opens with “Das wütende Heer,” “Das Nachtvolk in der Jeninser-Alpe,” “Die Nachtschaar im Schmittener-Tobel,” “Das Nachtvolk auf Obersaxen,” “Das Todtenvolk im Prätigau,” and related narratives. Later material includes “Das Nachtvolk in Peist.”

Jecklin distinguishes *wütendes Heer*, *Nachtvolk*, *Nachtschar*, and *Totenvolk*, even though individual legends cross their boundaries. The original publication history and contents are indexed by the [Märchenstiftung](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/buecherdatenbank/3113/volkstuemliches-aus-graubuenden), while a digitized edition is catalogued by the [Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek](https://www.deutsche-digitale-bibliothek.de/item/5775AMP2ULADMHROIBEVVP6SXC3L7P4X).

#### Johann Adolf Heyl, *Volkssagen, Bräuche und Meinungen aus Tirol*, 1897

**[Regional comparative corpus]** Heyl’s 847-page compilation is a major Tyrolean source for Wild Hunt, spirits, Perchten, witches, the blessed dead, alpine beings, calendar customs, and Christian reinterpretation. Like Zingerle, it supplies comparative context rather than evidence for one unified Nachtvolk extending throughout Tirol. Bibliographical details and a full-view edition are available at [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?id=VKNZAAAAMAAJ).

#### Jakob Kuoni, *Sagen des Kantons St. Gallen*, 1903

**[Secure regional attestation]** Kuoni gathered more than 500 texts from his own research and contributors. Relevant entries include:

- No. 114, “Das Totenvolk oder Nachtvolk”: dead and living figures in a nocturnal funeral procession; living participants will soon die; Fronfastenkinder possess second sight.
- No. 341, “Das Nachtvolk vom Berg”: black figures and an editorially Wodanized white leader.
- Pfäfers variants in which the last figure mirrors a watcher who came to the window half-dressed.

The complete text of no. 114 is available from the [Märchenstiftung](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/maerchendatenbank/2653/das-totenvolk-oder-nachtvolk).

#### Josef Müller, *Sagen aus Uri*, 1926, 1929, 1945

**[Oral-history corpus]** Müller collected approximately 1,600 legends, principally between 1903 and 1925, often recording narrator, place, and circumstances. One story, “Das Nachtvolk in der Alphütte am Ort,” was told in 1924 by twelve-year-old Dom. Arnold of Altdorf: three blue lights enter an alpine hut, and unseen beings operate the dairy equipment. It is preserved online as [“Das Nachtvolk in der Alphütte am Ort”](https://www.schweizermaerchenpreis.ch/maerchendatenbank/4566/das-nachtvolk-in-der-alphuette-am-ort).

#### Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens, 1927–1942

**[Learned systematization]** The ten-volume *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens*, edited by Hanns Bächtold-Stäubli and Eduard Hoffmann-Krayer, organizes Wild Hunt, dead procession, Percht, Wodan, Quatember, second sight, revenants, and related beliefs. It remains a vast repository but reflects its period’s search for ancient survivals, broad ethnic continuities, and “primitive” strata. Entries must therefore be checked against the local texts they cite.

#### Swiss folklore atlas, 1937–1995

**[Modern ethnographic survey]** The *Atlas der schweizerischen Volkskunde* arose from surveys conducted in 1937–1942: about 1,200 respondents in 400 communities produced roughly 80,000 answer sheets and 4,000 photographs, later represented on 320 maps. It records twentieth-century distribution and memory, not direct medieval continuity. The project archive is described by [Empirische Kulturwissenschaft Schweiz](https://www.ekws.ch/de/archiv/sammlungen/SGV_05).

#### Arnold Büchli, 1947; Paul Geiger, 1951; Klaus Beitl, 1965/1974

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

- Arnold Büchli, “Wilde Jagd und Nachtvolk,” *Schweizerisches Archiv für Volkskunde* 44 (1947), pp. 65–69.
- Paul Geiger, “Der Totenzug,” *Schweizerisches Archiv für Volkskunde* 47 (1951), pp. 71–76.
- Klaus Beitl, “Die Sagen vom Nachtvolk. Untersuchung eines alpinen Sagentypus,” first published from the 1964 Athens congress in 1965, pp. 14–21; reprinted in 1974, pp. 69–75.

Beitl maps the Nachtvolk tradition across Vorarlberg, Graubünden, St. Gallen, Glarus, Liechtenstein, and the western edge of Bavaria. His distinction between regional motif-zones remains the most disciplined alternative to treating everything as one Wild Hunt.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic activity

**[Core folklore complex]**

- Night travel along fixed routes.
- Ravines, wooded stream-gorges, crossroads, ridge paths, funeral roads, and buildings with opposing doors.
- Winter and the period after the cattle have descended from alpine pastures.
- Quatember or **Fronfasten** nights—the quarterly Ember Days of the Christian calendar.
- Music: strings, pipes, drums, kettledrums, zither, Schwegel flute, and jaw harp.
- Feasting in deserted alpine huts.
- Dairying from residue: some traditions say the host collects milk spilled during the summer and makes butter or cheese from it.
- Punishment of mockery, disobedience, obstruction, staring, or answering a call.
- Death-warning and collective-disaster omen in eastern-Swiss forms.
- Abduction or compulsory participation.
- Recovery when a victim returns at the same hour and place one year later.

### Appearance

There is no fixed iconographic canon.

**[Vorarlberg regional variant]** The host is frequently unseen. A rushing wind, uproar, distant voices, or music constitutes the manifestation. Where visible, members may be black, hideous, indistinct, or shadowlike.

**[Eastern-Swiss regional variant]** The company may resemble a funeral procession: paired figures, prayer or murmuring, a coffin, white or old-fashioned clothing, and recognizable dead or living persons.

**[Flums regional variant]** Numerous black figures surround a very tall white man wearing a broad hat. Kuoni’s contributor or editor adds “Wodan!” in parentheses; that exclamation must not be converted into secure iconography.

**[Modern invention]** Contemporary tourism and fantasy art often supplies antlers, animal skulls, horned masks, black cloaks, or a Wodan-like mounted leader. None is a universal traditional Nachtvolk attribute. A 2026 Montafon site explicitly labels its spectral-house illustration as AI-generated, a useful example of transparent modern visual reinvention: [Muntafuner, “Das Nachtvolk”](https://muntafuner.at/ort/schruns/sagen/das-nachtvolk).

### Material attributes and protections

- **Music and instruments:** central in Vorarlberg and Allgäu; less prominent in Beitl’s eastern-Swiss death-procession zone.
- **Knife, axe, or nail:** the host wounds a witness; the embedded implement or pain is removed when the victim returns later.
- **Hazel hook:** in the Peist legend, a friend uses a naturally hooked hazel pole to pull a living participant beneath the protective roof-drip.
- **Wormwood and rue:** a Satteins household is spared because its occupants are helpful and keep these protective herbs.
- **Open doors:** houses crossing a traditional route must allow the host through.
- **Rightward movement or lying flat:** a traveler may be ordered to step three paces right or lie on the ground because the host travels approximately one shoe-height above it.
- **Hat removal and respectful withdrawal:** prescribed in some St. Gallen Totenvolk variants.
- **Silence:** often essential when listening to supernatural music or receiving instruction.

No securely traditional sacred color, number, celestial body, temple emblem, canonical weapon, or feast-day exclusively belonging to the Nachtvolk is documented.

### Naturalistic interpretations

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Wind through ravines, storm noise, animal cries, migratory birds, acoustics, and the sounds of distant human processions have been proposed as stimuli around which narratives formed. Such explanations can account for reported sound but do not by themselves explain a legend’s precise motifs or social meanings.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

There is none. The Nachtvolk has no securely attested parents, creation story, consort, offspring, siblings, mount, divine court, or pantheon rank. It is normally a collective and often expressly leaderless.

People within it are variously classified as:

- unidentified spirits;
- enchanted or accursed people;
- dead souls;
- shadows or doubles of the soon-to-die;
- living people compelled to travel;
- a “good society” of spirits;
- a wild or furious host.

These are variant explanations, not branches of a canonical family tree.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

No primary Nachtvolk legend located in the surveyed corpus records a discrete meeting, combat, marriage, seduction, judgment, alliance, theft, rescue, or joint adventure between the Nachtvolk and another named god or supernatural individual. This absence is a substantive finding.

For cross-reference purposes:

- **Wodan/Wuotan — alleged leadership or identity; Kuoni, 1903, no. 341.** **[Learned systematization, not an attested interaction.]** A white, broad-hatted figure within the Flums procession is followed by the editorial parenthesis “Wodan!” The narrative neither names him Wodan nor recounts an interaction.
- **Thor/Donar — comparative “bone miracle”; nineteenth- and twentieth-century commentary.** **[Learned comparative identification.]** Scholars compared the slaughter-and-restoration of the cow with Thor’s goats in the Old Norse *Gylfaginning*. Thor does not appear in a Nachtvolk story.
- **Türst/Dürst — neighboring Wild Hunter.** **[Regional analogue, no interaction.]** Lucerne legends concern Türst and his own hunt; no source found has Türst meet the Nachtvolk.
- **Sträggele/Sträggeli — neighboring witch or Wild Hunt woman.** **[Regional analogue, no interaction.]** She appears with Türst or in her own calendar-night stories, not in a documented encounter with the Nachtvolk.
- **Posterli — Entlebuch female pursuer.** **[Regional analogue, no interaction.]**
- **Perchta/Berchta — comparative winter figure.** **[Syncretistic identification in scholarship and popular culture; no interaction.]**
- **Krampus — Christian-seasonal masked figure.** **[Modern conflation; no interaction.]**
- **Saint Nicholas — leader of Nikolaus/​Krampus visits.** **[Separate customary complex; no interaction.]**
- **The Devil/Satan — occasional explanatory category elsewhere in Alpine legend.** **[No documented Nachtvolk interaction.]**
- **God, Christ, Mary, saints, poor souls — Christian interpretive environment.** **[No discrete Nachtvolk episode found in which the collective interacts with a named divine person.]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The cow feast and restoration

**[Secure nineteenth-century attestation; regional core]** In Vonbun’s Vorarlberg and Jecklin’s Graubünden materials, the Nachtvolk enters an abandoned alpine hut, kills a cow, cuts meat from it, cooks, and feasts. A human witness may be invited to participate. At dawn the company restores the animal, but the piece eaten or removed by the human remains missing; the cow thereafter limps or bears the wound.

In the Jenins form, the host spends the winter in deserted dairies and turns summer’s spilled milk into butter and cheese. During one feast it takes flesh from a man’s cow. He imitates the spirits and cuts his own portion. In the morning the cow is alive and intact except for precisely his cut. The complete modern transcription identifies Herzog’s 1871 *Schweizer Sagen* as its source: [“Das Nachtvolk”](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/maerchendatenbank/13190/das-nachtvolk).

**Variants:**

- The human merely watches; restoration is complete.
- The spirits offer him meat, and the cow later lacks that piece.
- Bones must remain intact or be gathered.
- A feast occurs without an explicit slaughter, possibly an abbreviated version of the same narrative.
- In Oberstdorf/Allgäu the story is attached to the *Gute Gesellschaft*, *Fahrt*, or *Nachtschar*.

**[Learned comparison]** Antiquarians compared the narrative with Thor slaughtering and restoring his goats, provided their bones remain unbroken. Wider Eurasian “bone miracle” narratives make direct borrowing impossible to prove. Similarity is evidence of a migratory motif, not proof that the Nachtvolk “is” Thor’s company.

### B. Supernatural music and instruction

**[Core Vorarlberg-Allgäu folklore]** The Nachtvolk passes with extraordinarily beautiful music: strings, fifes, drums, kettledrums, zithers, jaw harps, or Schwegel pipes. A person who waits silently may learn an instrument from the company. Disclosure, mockery, or breaking an instruction can cancel the gift or bring punishment.

At Satteins the host descends beside Schwarzhorn through the Hollagasse with string music, piping, drums, and kettledrums, while remaining invisible except as wind. A resident removes part of a manure pile that obstructs its established route: [Satteins Nachtvolk account](https://satteins.jimdofree.com/sagen/nachtvolk/).

At Tschagguns a man asks the host to teach him the Schwegel pipe. This belongs to the local pattern in which supernatural gifts are granted under precise, sometimes treacherous conditions: [“Der Schwegelpfeifer”](https://muntafuner.at/ort/tschagguns/sagen/der-schwegelpfeifer).

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Behringer treats beautiful music and conviviality as marks of a benevolent *bona societas*, distinguishing it from the terrifying dead host. Beitl is more regional and typological: the music is central in Vorarlberg but fades toward eastern-Swiss death-procession forms.

### C. The wound that is removed one year later

**[Secure nineteenth-century attestation]** A witness mocks, obstructs, looks at, or otherwise encounters the Nachtvolk. One member drives a knife, axe, or supernatural “nail” into his body. Ordinary remedies fail. He is told to return to exactly the same place, often one year later. A member of the passing host remarks that it left an implement in a stump or similar object and removes it; the human is healed.

In the Satteins axe version, a man receives an axe in the shoulder. On the host’s next passage, one spirit says it left its axe in a “stock”; when it takes the tool back, the man is free of it: [“Das Beil in der Achsel”](https://satteins.jimdofree.com/sagen/das-beil-in-der-achsel/).

**Interpretive caution:** The spirits’ language treats the human as wood or landscape. That may be comic, threatening, or expressive of altered supernatural perception; it is not evidence of an initiation ritual.

### D. Compulsory participation and rescue

**[Graubünden regional variant]** Jecklin’s Peist story names the living municipal clerk Chr. Brunold. His godmother had prophesied in infancy that he must choose among living badly, dying suddenly, or running with the Nachtvolk. He chooses the third. A knowledgeable friend waits beneath Brunold’s eaves with a naturally hooked hazel pole and pulls him under the roof-drip as the host passes, ending his obligation. The text is available as [“Das Nachtvolk in Peist”](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/maerchendatenbank/2193/das-nachtvolk-in-peist).

This is unusually concrete, giving the reputed participant a name and civic occupation. It remains a collected legend, not a verified biographical event.

### E. Passages through houses and across roads

**[Core folklore]** The host follows routes that human construction cannot safely block. Houses with four doors or aligned front and rear doors are especially vulnerable. Occupants leave them open; if barred, the company makes an unbearable noise or brings harm. Crossroads and narrow passages recur.

The architectural motif explains unusual house rules and renders old paths supernaturally inalienable. It also parallels the Wild Hunt command to clear the way.

### F. Death omen, pestilence, and funeral procession

**[Eastern-Swiss regional variant]** In Graubünden, Glarus, and St. Gallen, the Nachtvolk is more explicitly dangerous. Its passage can foretell an individual death, epidemic, war, or mass mortality. Jecklin’s Prättigau text calls it *Nachtvolk* or *fliegendes Heer*: a dairyman sees the dark company fly past, and the host is said to appear before war or pestilence. The account is transcribed as [“Das Nachtvolk im Prätigaue”](https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/maerchendatenbank/2191/das-nachtvolk-im-praetigaue).

Kuoni’s St. Gallen *Totenvolk oder Nachtvolk* marches through the village at night. Many figures are dead, but living people also appear and will soon die. A respectful observer steps aside and removes his hat. Only the “spirit-sighted,” especially **Fronfastenkinder**—children born during Ember periods—can necessarily see it.

In the Flums story, the company comes down from the mountain when a resident lies dying, pauses at the old town hall, murmurs as though negotiating, and returns uphill. The route echoes the actual custom by which a priest met a descending funeral procession at that location. The legend can therefore be read as a supernatural doubling of social funeral geography.

### G. The observer sees himself at the rear

**[Regional death-procession variant]** A person hears the host, rushes to the window incompletely dressed, and sees a final participant matching his own state of dress—one trouser-leg on, for example. The apparition predicts that the watcher will die or join the procession. Balzers preserves a version in which the final figure, like the observer, has only one leg in his trousers: [Liechtenstein tourism transcription](https://tourismus.li/reiseland/unser-land/feiertage-brauchtum/top-sagen-liechtensteins/das-nachtvolk.html).

This is related to European “second sight” and *alter ego* legends, not necessarily to the musical Vorarlberg host.

### H. The Walliser Gratzug and Totenprozession

**[Separate but overlapping regional complex]** The **Gratzug** is not simply another name for every Nachtvolk. Walliser sources describe an immense procession of dead souls moving at night along fixed routes, ridges, ravines, valleys, and former funeral or ecclesiastical paths. Participants may sing, pray, murmur, laugh, weep, play music, or beat drums. The procession is particularly associated with Quatember nights.

A nineteenth-century account calls the belief general in the mountains and valleys of Valais and says the Totenprozession was also named *Gratzug* and *Symphonie*. Later research notes that such processions often followed established paths. In one historical case, a real annual procession from Zermatt toward Sion was rerouted to Täsch in 1665 because the old route across the Col d’Hérens had become difficult; scholars have proposed that remembered ritual geography contributed to local dead-procession legend. The argument is summarized by the [Swiss Alpine Club](https://www.sac-cas.ch/de/die-alpen/lalpinisme-entre-sagesse-et-folie-11800/).

The dead are usually interpreted within Catholic ideas of **arme Seelen**, souls awaiting full heavenly joy. One recorded deceased man says that he has been released from suffering but must still wander before receiving heaven. Scholars debate whether this is the original logic or a later Christian explanation for an older procession motif. The surviving evidence establishes Christian-era narration; it cannot securely recover a pre-Christian original.

### I. Türst, Sträggele, Posterli, and the Lucerne Wild Hunt

**[Separate regional complex]** Grimm’s *Deutsche Sagen* no. 269 (1816), citing Franz Joseph Stalder, states:

- when night storms rage in Lucerne country, people say **Türst/Dürst is hunting**;
- in Entlebuch, **Posterli**, a female monster or witch, has a hunt represented in an annual noisy procession on the Thursday before Christmas;
- in Lucerne city, **Sträggele** is a witch active on the Wednesday Ember night before Christmas, harassing girls who have not completed their spinning.

The primary text is available at [Wikisource](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Der_T%C3%BCrst%2C_das_Posterli_und_die_Str%C3%A4ggele).

A later moralized story makes Sträggele an aristocratic woman who demands fresh wild boar on a fast Friday and rides hunting with her lover. Both are condemned to hunt eternally with dogs and horses. This is a Christian etiological legend of punishment, not an ancient divine genealogy.

**Relationship to Nachtvolk:** both involve winter nights, storm-noise, fixed routes, a dangerous supernatural company, and ritualized avoidance. But Türst has an individual hunter identity and dogs; the core Nachtvolk is often leaderless and musically convivial. Treating Türst and Nachtvolk as identical suppresses those local differences.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No cult institutions

There are no documented Nachtvolk temples, shrines, cult statues, priesthoods, initiation grades, liturgies, votive inscriptions, theophoric personal names, sacred treasuries, temple economies, or state cult.

No evidence was found for:

- human sacrifice;
- animal sacrifice offered to the Nachtvolk;
- temple prostitution;
- cult warfare;
- legal suppression of a “Nachtvolk religion”;
- continuous secret worship;
- a historical sect calling itself the Nachtvolk.

The cow-feast is a supernatural legend episode, not evidence that humans sacrificed cattle to this host.

### Apotropaic and accommodating conduct

**[Folklore/customary belief]** Conduct around the host includes:

- leaving doors open along its route;
- clearing obstacles;
- stepping to the right;
- lying flat;
- removing one’s hat;
- remaining silent;
- avoiding looking from the window;
- not answering calls;
- keeping rue and wormwood;
- respecting Fronfasten restrictions;
- returning to the encounter-site for healing;
- avoiding deserted alpine huts after seasonal departure.

These are household precautions and legend prescriptions, not proof of worship.

### Performative analogues

#### Sträggelejagen

**[Early-modern/modern custom related to a different figure]** Entlebuch residents represented Posterli’s or Sträggele’s hunt with noise and procession. Authorities sometimes opposed disorderly masked or noisy customs. The existence of performance does not establish that performers believed themselves to be members of the Nachtvolk.

#### Gastein Perchten

**[Distinct living tradition]** The Gasteiner Perchtenlauf occurs every four years between New Year and Epiphany and includes roughly 140 roles: Three Kings, Hanswursts, witches, Kramperl, Schnabelperchten, and tall cap-bearers. It was placed in Austria’s intangible-cultural-heritage inventory in 2011. The [Austrian UNESCO Commission](https://www.unesco.at/kultur/immaterielles-kulturerbe/verzeichnis/element/gasteiner-perchten/) states that its history reaches into Renaissance-era customs, that church and secular authorities repeatedly prohibited it, and that regular performance dates only from around the turn of the twentieth century.

This is evidence for a changing Salzburg procession custom, not a Nachtvolk cult.

#### Krampus

**[Distinct Christian-seasonal tradition]** Krampus is a devil-like companion of St Nicholas, documented in household visits and plays. Vienna has sources for Nicholas-and-Krampus visits from the seventeenth century; commercial Krampus markets and imagery flourished in the nineteenth century. Rural Krampus packs and modern public runs developed further thereafter. The [Wien Museum](https://magazin.wienmuseum.at/der-krampus) documents this Christian disciplinary, theatrical, commercial, and later mass-event history.

Claims that Krampus is straightforwardly a surviving pre-Christian Nachtvolk member are unsupported. Modern scholarship instead emphasizes Baroque religious theatre, Nicholas visits, local youth culture, and repeated reinvention.

#### Lötschental Tschäggättä

**[Distinct Fasnacht tradition]** Tschäggättä are masked carnival figures wearing carved pine masks, reversed clothing, sheep or goat skins, a bell, gloves, and a staff. They traditionally appeared between Candlemas and Shrove Tuesday; formerly the performers were principally unmarried young men. The oldest museum mask is dated about 1790, while the first presently known written notice is Prior Johann Baptist Gibsten’s **1860 prohibition** of carnival masking. The Museum Lötschental’s curators emphasize that competing origin legends lack a secure scientific basis: [Swissinfo museum report](https://www.swissinfo.ch/ger/ungewohnliche-schweiz/tsch%C3%A4gg%C3%A4tt%C3%A4-in-der-werkstatt-der-grotesken-masken/88938483).

The practice changed markedly after the 1950s; women and others now participate, organized parades complement spontaneous roaming, and masks are major artistic and tourist commodities. Switzerland’s official living-traditions inventory documents this development: [Tschäggättä inventory](https://www.lebendige-traditionen.ch/de/tschaeggaettae).

Theories connecting Tschäggättä with pre-Christian spirits, fertility cults, invading Alemanni, masked cattle thieves, or Wild Hunt figures remain theories. No source makes them embodiments of the Nachtvolk.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1500: no exact-name evidence

**[Absence of evidence]** No medieval manuscript, charter, inscription, church record, or image securely attests an Alpine collective called *Nachtvolk*. Medieval *bona societas*, nocturnal ladies, Hellequin’s host, dead processions, and penitential souls provide comparative context but cannot be retroactively renamed Nachtvolk.

### 1586–early 1600s: documentary analogues under judicial and clerical observation

Stoeckhlin’s *Nachtschar* and Cysat’s *Guotisheer/Wuotisheer* show that nocturnal supernatural companies were meaningful in the region. Their records were produced in environments of witch prosecution, Catholic reform, moral regulation, and learned demonology.

Stoeckhlin’s fate also documents suppression: visionary journeys could be reclassified as diabolical witchcraft. That is not the suppression of an organized Nachtvolk cult but the criminalization of an individual’s supernatural claims.

### 1816–1859: collectors create a printed comparative object

Grimm’s 1816 Türst/Posterli/Sträggele text and the collections of Vonbun, Rochholz, Vernaleken, and Zingerle transferred local oral reports into printed literature. Editorial headings such as “NACHTVOLK” made a dispersed body of narratives visible as a category.

The printed record does not date the oral stories’ invention. It supplies a terminus ante quem for their collection, while collector language, arrangement, and mythological assumptions remain part of the evidence.

### 1870s–1903: eastern-Swiss differentiation

Jecklin and Kuoni documented the movement from ambivalent musical host to death procession and omen. The Nachtvolk becomes difficult to distinguish from *Totenvolk* and *Nachtschar* in some cantons, while remaining different enough in others that collectors list the categories separately.

### 1903–1945: intensive field collection

Müller’s Uri collection and related regional projects record named narrators, approximate dates, local geography, and variants with far greater density. These collections show continuing storytelling but also reveal Christian reinterpretation, abbreviated motifs, and local reclassification.

### 1930s–1970s: mapping and motif scholarship

The Swiss folklore atlas and the work of Büchli, Geiger, and Beitl moved study from speculative divine genealogy toward geographical distribution and motif analysis. Beitl’s principal conclusion is not that all nocturnal hosts are one being, but that adjacent cultural zones emphasize different features.

Some older scholarship nevertheless retained “survival” language: Wodan, Thor, pre-Christian fertility, or ancient Eurasian ritual were invoked to explain individual motifs. Such comparisons are hypotheses, not attestations.

### Late twentieth century to present: heritage, tourism, art, and digital circulation

The Nachtvolk now appears in:

- regional legend books and school materials;
- Liechtenstein and Vorarlberg tourism;
- Montafon legend trails and theatrical productions;
- Märchenstiftung digital archives;
- fantasy art and AI illustration;
- fiction, music, soap, merchandise, and local branding;
- revived interest in Wild Hunt, Rauhnächte, Perchten, and Alpine horror.

These developments preserve the name while tending to give the host a more standardized monster-image than the older, often invisible tradition possessed.

There is no documented large-scale continuous Nachtvolk worship. Individual spiritual or neopagan reinterpretations may exist, but no sourced organization, membership total, ritual corpus, or continuous lineage was located.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

Regional museums, archives, and cultural organizations now treat Nachtvolk narratives as intangible narrative heritage. The Märchenstiftung database is particularly important because it reproduces stories while identifying collectors and editions. Tourism sites often retell them more smoothly and visually.

### Theatre

The Silbertal *Sagenfestspiele* dramatize regional legends, including the musically active Nachtvolk. Stage embodiment necessarily supplies visible costumes, individual characterization, and coherent plots absent from many older reports. The production history and cited source in Richard Beitl’s 1953 collection are described by the [Montafon Sagenfestspiele](https://www.sagenfestspiele.at/de/programm-spielplan/chronik.html).

### Music

The Liechtenstein darkwave/neoclassical band **WeltenBrand** released *Das Nachtvolk* in 1997, drawing its wider project from Liechtenstein legends. This is an important modern artistic reception, not evidence of ritual revival.

### Literature and children’s media

Armin Öhri’s *Das Nachtvolk* (2009) uses the name for modern Liechtenstein literary fiction. Contemporary children’s productions place the Nachtvolk alongside Wildmandli, dragons, the Devil, and other local figures, effectively constructing a shared “legend world.” That arrangement is artistic and pedagogical, not a recovered traditional pantheon.

### Tourism and commerce

Liechtenstein markets the Balzers story; Montafon promotes Nachtvolk trails and tales; a Liechtenstein producer sells “Nachtvolk” soap inspired by the legend. These uses turn an unstable, often invisible host into a recognizable regional brand.

### Occult and neopagan reception

**[Negative finding]** The dedicated searches for Nachtvolk combined with *occult*, *esoteric*, *neopagan*, *Wicca*, *ritual*, and related terms did not identify a historically influential occult order, grimoire, ceremonial invocation, or reconstructionist denomination devoted specifically to the Alpine Nachtvolk.

Modern practitioners may fold the term into generalized Wild Hunt, ancestor, Rauhnacht, “Germanic,” or spirit-procession practices. Without dated organizational sources these should be described as personal modern syncretism, not ancient continuation.

### Nationalist appropriation

No major political appropriation specific to the Nachtvolk was located. The surrounding field is less innocent:

- nineteenth-century Germanic mythography frequently sought Wodan behind regional hunts;
- National Socialist folklore promoted some Perchten and Krampus customs as survivals of supposedly pure Germanic religion;
- postwar popular writing has often repeated “pagan fertility rite” explanations without documentary proof.

A modern exhibition catalogue warns that assertions of pagan, Germanic, or Celtic Krampus origins often derive from older, sometimes politically motivated folklore scholarship: [Krampus catalogue](https://goegl.com/media/1452/krampus-katalog.pdf). That history cautions against transforming the Nachtvolk into evidence of an ethnically uniform prehistoric Alpine religion.

### Human sacrifice and violent-cult claims

No source supports human sacrifice by or to the Nachtvolk. Internet material using the same name for fictional voodoo sects, cannibals, game factions, or fantasy races is unrelated modern invention. It must not be imported into the Alpine dossier.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is the Nachtvolk the Wild Hunt?

There are three defensible positions:

1. **Broad comparativist position:** Nachtvolk is a regional name for the Wild Hunt or furious host.
2. **Regional-type position, favored by Beitl:** Nachtvolk is a south-Alemannic subtype within the wider family of nocturnal spirit-host legends, distinguished by leaderlessness, music, alpine-hut feasting, healing-return stories, and the cow-restoration motif.
3. **Pluralist position:** “Nachtvolk” names different local traditions that only partially overlap—beneficent spirit society, Wild Hunt, dead procession, disease omen, and supernatural dairy workers.

The evidence most strongly supports positions 2 and 3. Some local narrators and collectors explicitly equate the categories; others distinguish them.

### Are its members dead?

The sources conflict.

- In core Montafon accounts, they are not expressly dead and may be merely “enchanted people.”
- In St. Gallen and some Graubünden texts, they are explicitly dead, accompanied by living people soon to die.
- In Behringer’s reconstructed “good society,” the host can include otherworldly beings and selected living travelers.
- In Wild Hunt parallels, the company may contain the violently dead, unbaptized, criminals, witches, or generalized spirits, depending on locality.

“Procession of the dead” is therefore correct for some Nachtvolk traditions, not for all.

### Wodan/Odin

The derivation of *Wuota/Wuetisheer* from *Wuotan* is attractive, and the Wild Hunt was widely Wodanized by Grimmian scholarship. Yet the Nachtvolk is normally leaderless and its exact name contains no divine name. Kuoni’s “Wodan!” is historiographically valuable precisely because it shows the collector interpreting the legend.

The safe formulation is: **Wodan is a learned identification applied to some nocturnal-host material; he is not securely attested as the Nachtvolk’s universal leader.**

### Thor and the restored animal

The reconstructed cow resembles the restoration of Thor’s goats after their bones are collected. The parallel is real. Direct descent is unproved because animal-restoration or “bone miracle” motifs occur widely. “Thor myth preserved unchanged in an Alpine cow legend” overstates the evidence.

### Christianization versus pre-Christian survival

The traditions contain unmistakably Christian material:

- Ember Days;
- fast-day violations;
- poor souls and purgatory;
- funeral processions and priests;
- crosses, churches, prayer, and moral punishment;
- witches and diabolical reinterpretation.

They also contain motifs that need not originate in Christian doctrine: otherworldly feasts, supernatural music, living doubles, fixed spirit roads, and restored animals.

The historical record demonstrates Christian-era narration and adaptation. It cannot date every motif to pre-Christian religion.

### Reformation hypothesis

Beitl tentatively suggested that the harsher, death-omen forms of eastern Switzerland might reflect a Reformation-era rationalization or “animization” of an older mythic host. This is an interpretive model, not an established causal chain. Local confessional boundaries do not automatically explain every variant.

### “Bona societas” and Stoeckhlin

Behringer connects Stoeckhlin’s *Nachtschar* with medieval good-society traditions and later Walser Nachtvolk lore. The strengths of the argument are geography, music, benign company, and the horse-herder’s Walser connections. Its weakness is chronological: much of the comparative Nachtvolk material was collected three centuries after Stoeckhlin. Albrecht Burkardt and other reviewers have warned against reconstructing a complete sixteenth-century system from later folklore.

### Folklore atlases and their limits

The Swiss atlas offers unparalleled geographical density, but its questionnaires recorded responses in 1937–1942. Maps can make fluid oral categories appear sharply bounded and can encourage “relic-area” reasoning. Its data show where informants remembered or reported a custom at that time; they do not directly map prehistoric belief.

### Images and material evidence

Unlike Perchten or Tschäggättä, the Nachtvolk has no established traditional mask-type. Old photographs of masked runners, museum masks, and carved horns are evidence for those performance customs, not depictions of the Nachtvolk.

Modern illustrations are usually imaginative. They should be dated and credited as reception history rather than used to reconstruct nineteenth-century appearance.

### State of the archive

Important surviving evidence is held or published through:

- Vorarlberg regional collections and the Austrian Museum of Folk Life and Folk Art;
- the Zentral- und Hochschulbibliothek Luzern for Cysat manuscripts;
- regional Swiss libraries and the Swiss Society for Folklore;
- the Canton Library Graubünden;
- the Atlas der schweizerischen Volkskunde archive in Basel;
- Museum Lötschental and the Museum der Kulturen Basel for Tschäggättä material;
- digitized nineteenth-century books at Google Books, Internet Archive, Wikisource, Wikimedia Commons, and the Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek.

The decisive loss is contextual rather than necessarily physical: many collectors gave polished summaries without verbatim performance, recording date, full narrator biography, audience, or questions asked.
