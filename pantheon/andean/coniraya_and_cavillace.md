# Coniraya and Cavillace: Research Dossier

**Class:** Cuniraya Viracocha is classified in the surviving Quechua source as a male **huaca**—a superhuman sacred person or power who can also be manifested in places and objects—and as a culture hero, trickster, creator, and organizer of the inhabited landscape. Cavillace, conventionally **Cavillaca/Cahuillaca**, is a female **huaca**, maiden, mother, and petrified landscape-being. Neither is securely reducible to a standardized “Inca god” or “goddess” in the Greco-Roman sense.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Both are first securely attested in the anonymous Quechua **Huarochirí Manuscript**, principally chapter 2, written around **1608** and now Biblioteca Nacional de España, MS 3169, fols. 64r–114r. Chapter 2 occupies approximately fols. 64v–66r. The manuscript preserves traditions that may be older, perhaps substantially so, but there is presently no securely identified pre-Hispanic inscription, datable image, or archaeological object naming either figure. Its traditions were collected under the direction or within the documentary project of Francisco de Ávila during the opening campaigns to suppress Indigenous religion in Huarochirí. [The manuscript’s date, language, extent, and disputed authorship are summarized in the Museo Chileno study](https://boletinmuseoprecolombino.cl/html/art-02-ramon-30-1/) and the [ethnophilological study of BNE MS 3169](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-68942016000100009&script=sci_arttext).

**Evidence labels used below:** **[Early attestation]** means the circa-1608 Quechua manuscript or Ávila’s contemporary Spanish digest; it does not mean that the narrated event is historical. **[Colonial systematization]** denotes interpretation shaped by early-colonial collection, translation, or Christian conceptual categories. **[Regional variant]** denotes locally or textually divergent material. **[Modern reconstruction]** denotes a scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement. **[Modern retelling/invention]** denotes a postcolonial literary, popular, occult, or commercial development.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Cuniraya

The manuscript writes forms conventionally transcribed **Cuniraya**, **Coniraya**, or segmented **Cuni Raya**, commonly followed by **Viracocha/Huiracocha**. Modern normalized Quechua spellings include **Kuniraya Wiraqucha** and **Quniraya Wiraqucha**. These are editorial normalizations; the alphabetic form in the manuscript is colonial.

**[Early attestation]** Chapter 1 distinguishes or at least juxtaposes “another huaca called Cuniraya” with Viracocha, “creator of men,” while chapters 2, 14, and 15 employ the compound **Cuniraya Viracocha**. The compound therefore cannot safely be treated as a timeless personal name. It may record the association of a local huaca with an Inca-period creator title, an early-colonial theological recasting, or both.

**Viracocha/Wiraqucha** is not demonstrably an epithet invented specifically for Cuniraya. It was a widely used divine name or title in colonial descriptions of southern and imperial Andean religion and was also used honorifically for Spaniards. The Huarochirí text’s joining of the names is a historical act of identification, not proof that every tradition about Viracocha belongs to Cuniraya.

The etymology of **Cuniraya** remains unsettled:

- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Julio C. Tello connected the first element with the coastal god **Con/Kon**, although the remainder was not satisfactorily explained.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino has considered relationships among *Con*, *Contiti*, Quechua *q’uñi/qoni* (“warm”), and a remodeled form suggesting “one who maintains warmth” or “eternal fire.” The proposal is ingenious but is not an ancient gloss.
- **[Modern speculative etymology]** Segmentations such as *qun-niray-ya*, “essence of giving and saying,” circulate in recent writing but are not supported by a colonial source and should not be presented as established.
- González Holguín’s 1608 Quechua lexicon supplies lexical forms resembling *cuni* (“long” or “high”), but resemblance alone does not establish the god-name’s derivation.

A useful survey explicitly states that no definitive etymology exists and reviews Tello, Cerrón-Palomino, González Holguín, and possible comparison with the coastal Kon tradition. [Ecos de Huarochirí, p. 56](https://repositoriodigital.bnp.gob.pe/bnp/recursos/2/html/ecos-de-huarochiri/56/).

The manuscript gives functional descriptions rather than a fixed litany of cult titles:

- **Huaca:** sacred superhuman being.
- **Viracocha:** creator-associated name or title.
- **Maker/animator of people and world:** explicitly expressed in prayer, though its Christian-colonial framing is debated.
- **Father of Pariacaca:** reported as what “people say” in chapter 15, rather than presented as an uncontested genealogy.
- **Wakcha-like poor wanderer:** he appears in rags and outside ordinary social relations. *Wakcha* can imply poverty, orphanhood, or lack of kin, not merely “beggar.”
- **Trickster:** a modern comparative category, useful because he conceals his status, changes form, deceives other huacas, and overturns expectations; it is not his recorded Quechua title.

### Cavillace/Cavillaca

The user-supplied **Cavillace** is not the usual scholarly spelling. Colonial and editorial forms include **Cavillaca**, **Caui Llaca**, **Cauillaca**, and **Cahuillaca**; modernized spellings sometimes include **Kawillaka** or **Kaviyaka**. The safest dossier headword is **Cavillaca**, with variants supplied rather than silently standardized.

**[Early attestation]** Chapter 2 calls her a female huaca and represents her as an exceptionally beautiful virgin or sexually autonomous maiden desired by the assembled huacas. “Virgin goddess” is a reasonable translation of the narrative role but not a stable cult title demonstrated outside this episode.

No secure ancient etymology of **Cavillaca** is supplied by the manuscript. Recent semantic derivations are hypotheses, not established translations. Nor does the text assign her a domain such as “love goddess,” “fertility goddess,” or “sea goddess.” Those labels are modern extrapolations from her beauty, pregnancy, motherhood, and transformation at the sea.

### Pair or couple?

They are not an attested married divine couple. Cuniraya secretly impregnates Cavillaca without her knowledge; she rejects him when the child identifies him; he pursues her; she escapes permanently into the sea. Calling them “lovers,” “consorts,” or a romance falsifies the narrative relationship.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Huarochirí Manuscript

**Physical witness:** Biblioteca Nacional de España, MS 3169, approximately fols. 64r–114r, within a composite volume formerly belonging to Francisco de Ávila. It contains thirty-one chapters and two supplements. The language is colonial **lengua general**, a supraregional Quechua based substantially on southern Quechua but containing local Huarochirí forms and orthographic interference. [Manuscript description](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-68942016000100009&script=sci_arttext).

**Date:** Usually circa **1608**, sometimes broadly 1598–1608. The work is anonymous and undated. Ávila began his Spanish *Tratado y relación de los errores, falsos dioses…* in 1608, and the Quechua document belongs to the same documentary environment.

**Authorship:** There is no consensus.

- Pierre Duviols proposed a bilingual Indigenous Christian writer/editor working under partial supervision by Ávila.
- Gerald Taylor distinguished compiler/editor from possible copyist while treating the Quechua organizing intelligence as Indigenous.
- Alan Durston has argued from handwriting and documentary context for **Cristóbal Choquecasa** as redactor and possibly author.
- Scholars broadly agree that Ávila was not simply the Quechua manuscript’s author, though its production was inseparable from his investigation and anti-idolatry project.

The manuscript’s reportative particles frequently mark narrated material as hearsay or received tradition. This is important: its prose preserves not an omniscient creed but statements attributed to communities and tellers.

### Passages concerning the dossier subjects

- **Chapter 1:** names Cuniraya in the attempted chronology of successive huacas and records invocations made to “Cuniraya Viracocha” before difficult work, especially fine weaving. It admits uncertainty whether he preceded or followed Huallallo Carhuincho and Pariacaca.
- **Chapter 2:** complete Cavillaca narrative: Cuniraya’s powers and disguise; impregnation through the lúcuma; paternity assembly at Anchicocha; flight; blessings and curses of animals; Cavillaca and her child becoming sea rocks; Cuniraya’s encounter with Urpayhuachac’s household; origin of sea fish.
- **Chapter 14:** places Cuniraya in a retrospective narrative about Huayna Capac immediately before the Spaniards’ arrival. He travels toward Cusco, summons the Inca toward Titicaca, and participates in an oracle-like episode involving messengers or ritual specialists.
- **Chapter 15:** asserts Cuniraya’s primordial creator status and reports that people consequently call him Pariacaca’s father. Gerald Taylor identifies this chapter’s language as especially close to colonial catechism and sermon diction. [Taylor’s discussion](https://repositorio.minedu.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/20.500.12799/8251/Historia%20de%20las%20literaturas%20en%20el%20Per%C3%BA.%20Volumen%201%2C%20Literaturas%20orales%20y%20primeros%20textos%20coloniales.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1).
- **Later landscape notice:** Cuniraya is associated with petrification near the upper part of the Huincompa irrigation channel in the Chuquisuso material. This is a distinct local manifestation, not necessarily the “death” of a single anthropomorphic god.

Cavillaca appears substantially in chapter 2 and in references back to her petrified maritime presence. No comparably developed Cavillaca cycle survives elsewhere in the manuscript.

### Ávila’s Spanish *Tratado*

Ávila’s **1608** Spanish digest translates or paraphrases much of the first seven chapters. It is a contemporary secondary witness, valuable for comparison but not textually identical with the Quechua. Its Catholic labels—“idols,” “false gods,” and “errors”—are the investigator’s polemical framework, not Indigenous classifications.

### Editions and translations

- 1873: Clements Markham produced an English translation from Ávila’s Spanish material.
- 1879: Marcos Jiménez de la Espada drew attention to Ávila’s dependence upon an Indigenous text.
- 1918: chapters 2–7 appeared in a documentary collection.
- 1939: Hermann Trimborn published the first German translation of all thirty-one main chapters with Quechua transcription.
- 1942: Hipólito Galante published a Latin translation, photographs, and transcription; Ricardo Espinosa supplied Spanish.
- 1941–43: Toribio Mejía Xesspe prepared an unpublished Spanish translation.
- 1966: José María Arguedas published the influential bilingual *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí*.
- 1987 and later editions: Gerald Taylor published a philologically controlled Quechua text and Spanish translation, *Ritos y tradiciones de Huarochirí*.
- 1991: Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste published the principal English edition, *The Huarochirí Manuscript: A Testament of Ancient and Colonial Andean Religion*, with Quechua transcription and historical commentary. [University of Texas Press](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292730533/).

### Negative corpus finding

A dedicated search of the named chronicles and extirpation corpora found **no secure independent narrative of Cavillaca** in Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Molina, Garcilaso, Guaman Poma, Albornoz, or Arriaga. These writers are indispensable for comparative Inca religion, Viracocha, Pachacamac, sacrifice, and colonial suppression, but they should not be cited as if they independently confirm Cavillaca’s story.

Likewise, the published Cajatambo trial corpus documents huacas, ancestor cults, offerings, clandestine removal of Christian burials, and memories of capacocha, but no securely verified Coniraya–Cavillaca narrative was located. [PUCP/IFEA Cajatambo corpus](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/1f4ac161-c30e-4416-be48-dd1bf26b5765).

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Cuniraya

**[Early attestation]** His domains are situational rather than bureaucratically fixed:

- creation or animation of communities and living things;
- miraculous agricultural engineering;
- preparation of fields and stabilization of terraces by speech;
- opening an irrigation channel by throwing a *pupuna* reed flower;
- extraordinary knowledge, deceit, and humiliation of rival huacas;
- shape-changing, including transformation into a bird;
- control of appearance: ragged, kinless wanderer versus radiant, splendid divine form;
- fertility and impregnation;
- authoritative blessing and cursing of animals;
- establishment of customs concerning animal treatment;
- release or multiplication of sea fish;
- assistance with difficult work and divination or inspired recollection;
- retrospective prophecy concerning the Spanish arrival.

A 2025 linguistic study emphasizes that his speech is performative: *rimaspallas*, approximately “merely by speaking,” he reshapes land and water. [“La fuerza oral de Cuniraya”](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2413-26592025000100005&script=sci_arttext&tlng=es).

His powers have limits. He cannot make Cavillaca accept him, fails to overtake her, cannot reverse her petrification, fails to catch Urpayhuachac’s younger daughter, and must flee Urpayhuachac’s attempted vengeance.

### Cavillaca

**[Early attestation]** Her attributes are beauty, weaving, virginity or sexual autonomy, motherhood, refusal, rapid flight, and permanent lithic transformation. She convenes the huacas rather than passively awaiting recognition, devises the paternity test, and chooses escape after its result. The sea receives rather than destroys her: she and the child become two visible rocks or islands.

The narrative does not say that she governs love, childbirth, weaving, the moon, or the sea. Nor does it record spells, festivals, priests, colors, numbers, days, or celestial correspondences belonging to her.

### Iconography

No securely identified pre-Hispanic image of either Cuniraya or Cavillaca is known. The bird, ragged garments, lúcuma, brilliant ceremonial clothing, infant, weaving position, road animals, sea, and islands are narrative identifiers, not a demonstrated canonical iconographic program.

The famous painted wooden **Pachacamac Idol**, radiocarbon dated approximately **760–876 CE**, is an object of the Pachacamac cult complex, not an established representation of Cuniraya or Cavillaca. Claims identifying its wood or imagery through the Cavillaca legend have been weakened by material analysis showing that it was made from young algarrobo and was polychromed. [Peruvian Ministry newspaper report on the scientific study](https://elperuano.pe/noticia/88685-santuario-de-pachacamac-secretos-del-idolo).

The two principal rocks of the Pachacamac Islands are the only surviving landscape forms explicitly identified with Cavillaca and her child in the recorded myth. Their geological existence does not date the story, but their visibility anchors it in the Lurín coastal landscape.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical claims

- **Cuniraya → unnamed child by Cavillaca:** **[Early attestation]** chapter 2. The child is male in major translations, although popular retellings sometimes make the child female. The manuscript does not name him.
- **Cuniraya → Pariacaca:** **[Colonial systematization/regional report]** chapter 15 says people call Cuniraya Pariacaca’s father because Cuniraya created him. This is not the same as chapter 2’s child and does not establish that Cavillaca is Pariacaca’s mother.
- **Pachacamac → Cuniraya:** Some modern summaries infer a father-son relationship from the chapter 14 oracle scene or confused later genealogy. This is not a secure chapter-2 genealogy.
- **Urpayhuachac → two daughters:** **[Early attestation]** chapter 2. They are not Cuniraya’s daughters, despite ambiguous popular summaries.
- **Cavillaca’s parents, siblings, and consort:** not stated.
- **Cuniraya’s parents, spouse, and siblings:** not securely stated.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Cavillaca — Cuniraya Viracocha:** secret impregnation through semen placed in a lúcuma; paternity disclosure; rejection, pursuit, and permanent separation. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, ch. 2, circa 1608. **[Early attestation]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Cavillaca’s unnamed son:** Cuniraya fathers him; the crawling child identifies Cuniraya at Anchicocha; Cuniraya fails to catch him and Cavillaca before their transformation. Ch. 2, circa 1608. **[Early attestation]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — assembled unnamed huacas and huillcas:** Cuniraya hides among them in rags; they have sought Cavillaca, and the child’s recognition exposes Cuniraya as father. Ch. 2. **[Early attestation]**
- **Cavillaca — assembled unnamed huacas and huillcas:** she summons them to Anchicocha and commands the child to identify his father. Ch. 2. **[Early attestation]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Urpayhuachac:** while she visits Cavillaca’s sea-rock, he enters her household, assaults or “seduces” her elder daughter, attempts the younger, and throws Urpayhuachac’s fish into the sea; she later tries unsuccessfully to kill him with a rock. Ch. 2. **[Early attestation]**
- **Cavillaca — Urpayhuachac:** Urpayhuachac goes to visit Cavillaca after her transformation. No dialogue or earlier kinship is stated. Ch. 2. **[Early attestation]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Urpayhuachac’s elder daughter:** he has intercourse with her; in modern ethical terms the scene is coercive and is translated or summarized by some scholars as rape. Ch. 2. **[Early attestation; translation-sensitive]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Urpayhuachac’s younger daughter:** he attempts intercourse; she escapes by transforming into a dove. Ch. 2. **[Early attestation]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Pariacaca:** Cuniraya is said to have created Pariacaca and is consequently called his father. Ch. 15. **[Colonial systematization/regional genealogy]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Huallallo Carhuincho:** both are placed in a disputed sequence of ancient huacas; the text explicitly says their chronological priority cannot be established. Chs. 1 and 14–15. No direct meeting or battle is recorded. **[Early attestation; absence of interaction]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Pachacamac:** chapter 2 locates the coastal conclusion near Pachacamac’s sanctuary and household sphere, but it does not narrate a direct meeting between the two named huacas. **[Early attestation; geographical association, not interaction]**
- **Cavillaca — Pachacamac:** she enters the sea near his sanctuary and becomes a rock/island. No direct encounter is narrated. **[Early attestation; geographical association]**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — Viracocha:** the names are compounded and their functions partly fused; no narrative presents them meeting as separate persons. **[Inca/colonial syncretistic identification]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The lúcuma conception

**[Early attestation: chapter 2]** Cuniraya wanders dressed in a ragged mantle and tunic, although his hidden power lets him create fields, reinforce terraces, open channels, and shame other huacas. Cavillaca sits weaving beneath a lúcuma tree. Beautiful and desired by all the huacas, she has rejected every suitor.

Cuniraya becomes a bird, deposits his semen in a ripe lúcuma, and causes the fruit to fall near her. She eats it without knowing its contents and becomes pregnant. The account explicitly preserves the absence of ordinary intercourse and her ignorance of the father.

After the birth she nurses the child for approximately a year. When he can crawl, she summons the huacas to Anchicocha. They arrive washed, adorned, and richly dressed, each hoping to be chosen. Cuniraya alone sits to one side in his ragged guise. No one acknowledges paternity. Cavillaca releases the child, who crawls directly to Cuniraya.

The modern language of “courtship” or “love story” obscures the central fact: Cavillaca did not consent to impregnation and rejects the father once identified. Conversely, the text’s miraculous-conception form should not automatically be treated as a borrowing from Christianity. Supernatural conception is widespread; what is demonstrably colonial is the manuscript’s documentary setting and, especially in chapter 15, some of its creator language.

### The flight and transformation

Cavillaca is appalled that the apparent ragged wanderer fathered her child. She lifts the child and flees downhill toward the sea. Cuniraya changes into splendid golden or radiant attire and calls for her to turn and see him. She refuses or does not look back, remembering only the contemptible appearance, and he pursues her.

At the coast beside Pachacamac she enters the sea with the child. They become two rocks or islands. The story thereby maps a social and sexual rupture onto an enduring coastal landmark. The familiar formulation that Pachacamac himself turned them to stone is **not** what chapter 2 says.

### The animal judgments

Because Cavillaca is far ahead, Cuniraya asks animals whether he will catch her. Their answers generate present animal characteristics and ritual attitudes:

- **Condor:** says she is nearby. Cuniraya blesses it with long life, carrion, prestige, and lethal protection against anyone who kills or eats it.
- **Female skunk:** gives a discouraging answer. He condemns it to travel at night, stink, and be hated.
- **Puma:** says she is close. Cuniraya grants it power to take the llamas of guilty people and predicts that, if killed, its intact skin and head will be worn and made to “sing” in festivals; a llama will be sacrificed in its honor.
- **Fox:** discourages him. Cuniraya condemns it to hatred and dishonorable disposal after death.
- **Falcon:** encourages him. He blesses it with fortunate hunting, including hummingbirds, and ritual mourning and sacrifice when killed.
- **Parrot:** gives a discouraging answer. It is cursed with incessant noisy cries and vulnerability to human hostility.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** This is simultaneously an etiological fauna catalogue, a charter for ritual treatments of animals, and a demonstration that Cuniraya’s speech orders the world. The alternating favorable and unfavorable animals also structure the descent from highlands to coast. The text does not explicitly call the sequence a zoological cosmology.

### Urpayhuachac and the origin of fish

Finding Cavillaca beyond his reach, Cuniraya approaches the household associated with **Urpayhuachac**, “she who gives birth to doves” or “mother/maker of doves,” who has gone to visit Cavillaca. A serpent guards her two daughters.

Cuniraya has sex with the elder daughter and tries to seize the younger, who escapes as a dove. He discovers that Urpayhuachac keeps fish in a small pool or vessel. Angered, he throws them into the ocean; from them sea fish multiply. This is an origin myth for marine abundance.

When Urpayhuachac returns and learns what happened, she pursues him. Pretending friendliness, she offers to delouse him while secretly intending to crush him with a rock. Cuniraya recognizes the trap, excuses himself to defecate, and escapes.

### Cuniraya, Huayna Capac, and the coming of the Spaniards

**[Colonial retrospective myth: chapter 14]** Cuniraya goes toward Cusco shortly before the *viracochas*—Spaniards—appear. He speaks with Huayna Capac and proposes a journey to Titicaca, directing the Inca to command ritual experts and persons animated by different birds or powers. The ensuing signs are woven into an explanation of Huayna Capac’s death, the succession conflict, and the Spaniards’ appearance at Cajamarca.

This is not contemporary pre-Conquest prophecy. It is a post-Conquest narrative that places catastrophe within an Indigenous sacred history.

### Primordial creator and father of Pariacaca

**[Colonial systematization: chapter 15]** Before Cuniraya, the text says, nothing existed in this world; he created mountains, trees, rivers, animals, fields, and the means of human subsistence. Therefore, people call him Pariacaca’s father and say he created Pariacaca.

Taylor and César Itier observe that this chapter strongly resembles Christian instructional language, including sixteenth-century Quechua catechetical discourse. It may preserve older creator attributes, but its universal “before him nothing existed” theology cannot safely be projected unchanged into pre-Inca antiquity.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Secure evidence

**[Early attestation]** Chapter 1 records an actual formula of invocation. Before difficult work, people cast coca on the ground and appealed to Cuniraya Viracocha for knowledge or recollection; fine-cloth weavers did so when confronted with demanding designs. Another formula calls him maker of humans and world, owner of fields and people. This is the strongest direct evidence for his worship.

The coca offering and occupational invocation show more than literary interest, but the text does not specify a dedicated Cuniraya priesthood, festival calendar, initiation, temple treasury, or sacrificial institution.

Chapter 2’s statement that a llama would be killed in honor of a slain puma concerns the ritual status Cuniraya assigns the puma; it is not evidence for a llama sacrifice directly to Cuniraya. No human sacrifice to either Cuniraya or Cavillaca is documented.

### Possible sanctuary association

Recent archaeological synthesis proposes that the Incas transformed a local sanctuary of Cuniraya and Urpayhuachac in the Pachacamac region into an imperial sanctuary of the setting Sun and Pachacamac. This is a **modern archaeological-historical reconstruction**, not an inscription naming the building. [Bilingual synthesis on pre-Hispanic Peruvian deities](https://assets.ey.com/content/dam/ey-sites/ey-com/es_pe/topics/growth/ey-dioses-creencias-tomo-2-vs2.pdf).

Pachacamac itself has a long archaeological sequence—from early occupation through Lima, Wari, Ichma, and Inca phases—and became an immense pilgrimage and oracle complex. That history cannot automatically be assigned to Cavillaca or Cuniraya. [Official sanctuary history](https://mnaahp.cultura.pe/santuario-arqueologico/historia-del-santuario).

### Cavillaca’s cult

No priesthood, shrine, offering schedule, prayer, or festival for Cavillaca is described. One modern study therefore cautions that, unlike more prominent Huarochirí female huacas such as Chaupiñamca, **Cavillaca’s cult is not described**. [PUCP study](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/bitstreams/769d8348-675e-43a2-8d01-53d5f8c7f879/download).

The island identification may imply sacred geography, visitation, or maritime veneration, but a narrated visit by Urpayhuachac is not archaeological proof of human pilgrimage.

### Violence, sacrifice, and sexual practice

- The narrative contains covert impregnation and coercive sexual conduct by Cuniraya.
- No “temple prostitution” is alleged or documented for either figure.
- No human sacrifice is attributed to them.
- Inca capacocha and human sacrifice are documented elsewhere, and the Cajatambo trials preserve memories of capacochas, but attaching those practices to this pair would be guilt by pantheon rather than source-based history.
- The colonial collection itself arose from coercive religious investigation and campaigns that confiscated or destroyed huacas, ritual objects, and ancestral remains and punished practitioners.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the Incas: unrecoverable local formation

The geographic specificity of Huarochirí, Anchicocha, the descent to Lurín, and Pachacamac suggests a local or interregional tradition predating the manuscript. Cuniraya’s affinity with agricultural speech, channels, and a coastal route may preserve an older culture hero. Cavillaca’s island transformation may preserve an older sacred interpretation of the Pachacamac rocks.

These are **reconstructions**, not dated attestations. No pre-Inca object bears either name.

### Inca period, approximately fifteenth to early sixteenth century

**[Modern reconstruction]** The compound Cuniraya Viracocha likely reflects engagement between a local huaca and an imperial or southern creator tradition. The Inca state commonly incorporated important regional huacas rather than simply abolishing them. The manuscript itself, however, preserves uncertainty over Cuniraya’s temporal relation to Pariacaca and Huallallo.

Pachacamac’s incorporation into imperial sacred geography provides a plausible setting for exchange between highland Huarochirí and the coast. Cavillaca’s placement offshore from that sanctuary connects—but does not merge—her with Pachacamac.

### Early colonial period, 1530s–1608

Spanish conquest, demographic collapse, Christian evangelization, and the resettlement of Indigenous communities altered the transmission of local religious knowledge. In chapters 14–15, conquest-era events and Christian creator diction entered the Cuniraya cycle.

The text nonetheless retained explicit huaca agency, sacred landscapes, offerings, and local rivalries. Its form is therefore neither untouched pre-Hispanic scripture nor merely Spanish fabrication: it is an Indigenous colonial document shaped under extreme asymmetry.

### Extirpation campaigns, 1608 onward

Ávila’s investigation helped initiate the first major Lima archdiocesan campaigns against “idolatry.” Local testimony was collected to identify huacas, priests, rites, and sacred objects for correction or destruction. The manuscript’s preservation is paradoxical: a campaign intended to eradicate these traditions produced their fullest surviving record.

Direct evidence that a physical Cavillaca or Cuniraya idol was destroyed has not been located. Broader Huarochirí suppression is documented; deity-specific destruction is not.

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century rediscovery

The manuscript remained in Spain among Ávila’s papers. Jiménez de la Espada publicized its significance in 1879; Trimborn’s 1939 edition and Arguedas’s 1966 literary translation transformed the pair into nationally recognizable mythic figures. Taylor’s philological work and Salomon–Urioste’s 1991 English edition restored attention to grammar, reportative stance, local geography, and colonial mediation.

### Present

The names survive in Peruvian education, museum interpretation, literature, performance, visual art, tourism, and the geographical name **Islas Cavillaca/Pachacámac**. The islands are now also protected within Peru’s system of guano islands and headlands.

No evidence found establishes an unbroken, organized present-day priesthood or congregational cult of either figure in Cusco, the Bolivian altiplano, or northwest Argentina. Broader Andean offerings to mountains, earth beings, ancestors, and Pachamama remain living traditions, but they cannot be relabeled as worship of Cuniraya or Cavillaca without local testimony.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and visual narrative

Cavillaca appears as a divine identity or referent in Óscar Colchado Lucio’s 1997 novel **Rosa Cuchillo**; the protagonist is recognized as an earthly manifestation of Cavillaca in the celestial world. This is a modern literary reuse, not a surviving ancient sequel. [Literary study](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/lexis/article/download/4943/4938/).

José María Arguedas’s **1966** translation decisively shaped the story’s modern Spanish form. Its title, *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí*, helped move the figures from a regional colonial manuscript into the Peruvian literary canon.

Modern illustrated, educational, and comic adaptations commonly make the narrative a tragic love story. That framing often suppresses Cavillaca’s lack of consent and Cuniraya’s later assaults, and sometimes incorrectly states that Pachacamac transformed mother and child.

### Museums, heritage, and popular education

Peruvian museum and educational materials use the story to explain the visual landscape around Pachacamac and the supposed origin of sea fish. The official heritage booklet calls it one of the major myths associated with the sanctuary. [Pachacamac educational booklet](https://pachacamac.cultura.pe/sites/default/files/item/archivo/Descubriendo%20el%20santuario%20de%20Pachacamac.pdf).

### Astronomy and planetary nomenclature

The International Astronomical Union has approved **Cavillaca Fluctus** as a planetary surface-feature name. This is modern scientific nomenclature, not evidence of an ancient astronomical association. [USGS Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature](https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/Feature/1090).

### Occultism, neopaganism, and New Age claims

The dedicated searches for Cuniraya or Cavillaca in occult, hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, chaos-magical, and organized neopagan sources found no historically influential tradition comparable to those surrounding Egyptian, Greek, or Mesopotamian gods.

Contemporary deity directories and spiritual websites sometimes assign Cavillaca generalized powers over love, feminine sovereignty, oceans, motherhood, or healing, and present Cuniraya as a universal Inca creator or fertility god. Unless such claims cite the Huarochirí text or documented contemporary practitioners, they are **modern extrapolations or inventions**.

No evidence found connects either being historically with astrology, tarot, ceremonial magic, chakras, crystals, fixed sacred colors, ritual weekdays, or planetary rulerships.

### Politics and commercial use

The pair participate in Peruvian regional heritage and tourism but have no well-documented sustained appropriation by a modern extremist movement. No reliable evidence was found for nationalist militias, racialist esotericism, or commercial cult organizations centered on them.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Cuniraya a local huaca, Viracocha, Kon, or a Christianized creator?

This remains the central debate.

- **Local-huaca model:** Cuniraya was a Huarochirí or central-coastal culture hero later joined to Viracocha.
- **Inca syncretism model:** the compound reflects imperial incorporation and the prestige of the Inca/southern Viracocha tradition.
- **Colonial-Christian model:** chapter 15’s universal creator resembles the Christian God because the Quechua author and collectors worked within decades of evangelization.
- **Kon comparison:** similarities between Cuniraya and the coastal Kon—rapid travel, reshaping land by speech, creator functions—suggest borrowing or common tradition, but do not prove identity.
- **Solar interpretation:** some scholars, including lines associated with Rostworowski and Fuenzalida, have considered Cuniraya a solar or setting-sun figure. The manuscript never simply calls him the Sun.

The best-supported conclusion is layered: a local huaca/culture hero was associated with the name and functions of Viracocha, and the resulting figure was further articulated through colonial Christian creator language. The relative contribution of each layer cannot be quantified.

### Trickster or creator?

These are not mutually exclusive Indigenous roles. Modern analysis classifies Cuniraya with Huatiacuri and Pariacaca as tricksters who appear socially marginal while concealing great power. [Comparative study](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S0254-92392015000200003&script=sci_arttext). “Trickster” helps describe narrative structure but can become misleading if it excuses sexual coercion or turns a locally specific huaca into a generic folklore archetype.

### Virgin conception and Christian influence

Cavillaca’s conception has been compared with the Virgin Mary. Similarity is real, direct borrowing is unproved. The episode’s semen-filled fruit, bird transformation, paternity assembly, sexual rejection, and lithic ending are not a Christian annunciation narrative. Chapter 15 provides much clearer evidence of catechetical influence than chapter 2.

### Sex, translation, and euphemism

Older translations and popular adaptations often call Cuniraya’s conduct “seduction,” “love,” or “courtship.” The text describes Cavillaca’s unwitting ingestion of semen and his pursuit after her explicit rejection. His encounter with Urpayhuachac’s elder daughter is variously translated as sleeping with, violating, or raping her. A source-driven account must preserve the coercive structure without pretending that a modern legal category is itself a Quechua cult title.

### Cavillaca’s child

The manuscript’s chapter 2 child is unnamed. Assertions that he is Pariacaca are unsupported conflations with chapter 15’s separate statement that Cuniraya created or fathered Pariacaca. Cavillaca cannot therefore be entered securely as Pariacaca’s mother.

### Archaeology and text

Pachacamac’s archaeology confirms an ancient, major sacred center, pilgrimage economy, Wari-period material, Ichma construction, and Inca intervention. It does not independently confirm every person or event in chapter 2. Associating particular buildings or the wooden idol with Cuniraya, Urpayhuachac, or Cavillaca remains interpretive unless supported by contextual or epigraphic evidence.

### What survives and what does not

- Original witness: BNE MS 3169.
- No known pre-Hispanic alphabetic record names either figure.
- No authenticated icon is securely identified as either.
- No independent Cavillaca liturgy survives.
- Cuniraya’s prayer and coca offering survive only through an anti-idolatry documentary setting.
- The Quechua text’s survival permits comparison with Ávila’s Spanish paraphrase and exposes his omissions and reframing.
- Much oral evidence and material cult equipment was undoubtedly lost through conquest, epidemic, Christian suppression, neglect, and ordinary change, but individual losses cannot be reconstructed by conjecture.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The complete secure record is sharply asymmetrical. **Cuniraya** has several manuscript roles—local huaca, ragged trickster, landscape organizer, recipient of coca and occupational prayer, retrospective prophet, and colonially universalized creator. **Cavillaca** is securely documented almost entirely within one exceptionally rich narrative and its coastal landmark: an autonomous female huaca subjected to secret impregnation, who identifies the father, rejects him, escapes, and becomes a permanent sea-rock with her unnamed child. Everything beyond that—formal marriage, love-goddess status, Pariacaca maternity, broad imperial cult, standardized iconography, occult correspondences, or continuous modern worship—remains unestablished or demonstrably modern.
