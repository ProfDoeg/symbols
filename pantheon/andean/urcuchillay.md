# Urcuchillay: Research Dossier

**Class:** celestial *huaca* or divine being associated with pastoralists, herd fertility, and the protection of domesticated camelids; described in the earliest source as a multicolored male llama embodied in, or represented by, a star. “God of all animals” is a convenient modern gloss but is broader and more anthropomorphic than the primary evidence warrants.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Juan Polo de Ondegardo’s *Tratado y averiguación sobre los errores y supersticiones de los indios* (Cusco, 1559), known through later copies and extracts because Polo’s original manuscript is lost. It says that llama herders venerated and sacrificed to a star called **Urcuchillay**, imagined as a multicolored male llama responsible for conserving livestock. The text’s identification of the star with European **Lyra** is colonial interpretatio astronomica, not an independently demonstrated Indigenous identification. Polo’s report was written for Archbishop Jerónimo de Loaysa and circulated in colonial ecclesiastical settings; a surviving extract was printed only in 1906.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early colonial attestation]** Sixteenth-century testimony, normally recorded after the Spanish conquest but potentially preserving pre-conquest practice.
- **[Later colonial elaboration]** Seventeenth-century testimony, derivative compilation, or reinterpretation.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally documented tradition not automatically transferable to all of Tawantinsuyu.
- **[Colonial systematization]** A Spanish or Christian reorganization of Andean categories into “gods,” “idols,” constellations, or European astronomical equivalents.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A reasoned identification or historical model proposed by modern researchers.
- **[Living folklore/practice]** Modern or recent ethnographic evidence; continuity of a practice does not prove an unchanged divine name or theology.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim that cannot be traced to an early source or conflicts with the surviving evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Polo’s Urcuchillay is not introduced through a creation myth, genealogy, temple narrative, or royal theology. He appears within a system in which particular occupational groups venerated celestial beings responsible for the propagation and welfare of corresponding earthly species. Pastoralists appealed to Urcuchillay because the celestial llama “understood” or had charge of the conservation of livestock. Other stars similarly governed serpents, felids, and other animals. **[Early colonial attestation]**

Accordingly, the closest documentary classification is:

1. a named star or asterism;
2. a celestial llama;
3. a species-protecting *huaca* or *camac*-like power;
4. an occupational patron of llama herders.

Calling him a “llama god” is defensible shorthand. Calling him the universal “Inca god of animals” is a **modern enlargement**: Polo’s passage concerns livestock, especially the camelids called *carneros* or *ovejas* by Spanish writers, while neighboring celestial powers had charge of other animal classes.

### Names and spellings

- **Urcuchillay** — the most familiar Hispanicized spelling.
- **Urquchillay / Urqu Ch’illay** — modern Quechua-oriented spellings. These are editorial normalizations, not spellings in an Inca alphabet; there was no pre-conquest alphabetic Quechua inscription naming the being.
- **Urcuquillay, Uruchillay, Urcuchillai, Orcochillay** — colonial or later orthographic variants.
- **Urcuchillay/Urucchillay as the purported calf’s name** — probably a corrupt duplication of the adult male’s name in part of the manuscript tradition.
- **Uñachillay** — Robert Lehmann-Nitsche’s proposed correction for the calf: Quechua *uña*, a young llama or suckling animal, plus *chillay*. This is a **modern philological reconstruction**, not a securely preserved colonial reading.
- **Catachillay / Catuchillay** — the large neighboring celestial llama, usually female, accompanied by a smaller animal. This is not securely a mere epithet of Urcuchillay.
- **Yacana / Yakana** — the dark llama in chapter 29 of the Quechua Huarochirí Manuscript. Its equation with Urcuchillay is possible only at the level of a related celestial-camelid complex; the sources distinguish their names and, in Cobo, their positions.

### Etymology

The first component is comparatively intelligible:

- Quechua **urqu** can mean a mountain or highland and, in animal terminology, a male animal. In this context “male” or “male llama” is attractive because Polo explicitly calls Urcuchillay a *carnero*, the male term in colonial Spanish livestock vocabulary.
- Older works sometimes render the whole name “wild male llama,” “resplendent male,” or “llama of the mountain.” These expansions depend on disputed divisions of *-chillay* and are not secured by the earliest dictionaries.

The second component, **chillay**, remains uncertain. Attempts to derive it from brilliance, ferocity, color, or an otherwise unattested animal term are speculative. It may also preserve a proper-name element no longer transparently analyzable. Therefore:

> **Secure minimum:** *urqu* plausibly marks maleness or a male camelid.  
> **Unestablished:** a complete literal translation of *Urcuchillay*.

The termination should not casually be identified with modern ritual **ch’alla/challa** (“libation” or blessing). Phonetic resemblance is not evidence of common derivation.

### Titles and epithets

No formal litany of epithets survives. The following are descriptions rather than independently attested cult titles:

- “carnero de muchos colores” — “a ram/male llama of many colors,” Polo, 1559; repeated by Acosta, Calancha, and others.
- one who “entiende en la conservación del ganado” — “has charge of the preservation of livestock,” Polo, 1559.
- protector or multiplier of herds — a fair interpretive summary of the office described by Polo and of the wider celestial-species system.
- “llama god of the herders” — modern scholarly/popular shorthand.
- “dark-cloud constellation of the llama” — properly a description of **Yacana**, not securely an epithet of Urcuchillay.

### Sex and number

Polo’s *carnero* and the semantic possibility of *urqu* indicate a **male** celestial llama. The adjacent large llama with a nursing young is presented as another entity or pair. Popular accounts that make Urcuchillay a female mother-llama, identify him directly with Yacana, or give him a fixed spouse and child conflate these records.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Polo de Ondegardo, 1559

Polo’s lost *Tratado y averiguación* was prepared in Cusco at the request of Archbishop Loaysa. Modern study identifies it as Polo’s earliest known report and notes its later use in the Second and Third Lima councils. Only extracts and derivative witnesses survive.

The crucial passage says, in substance:

> Pastoralists made veneration and sacrifice to a star called Urcuchillay, said to be a male llama of many colors, which had charge of conserving livestock; it was understood to be what European astrologers called Lyra. They also adored two nearby celestial figures called Catuchillay and Urcuchillay, imagined as a female llama with her young.

**What this establishes:** the name; celestial location; pastoral worship; sacrifice; multicolored male-llama form; responsibility for herd welfare; and an adjacent mother-and-young configuration.

**What it does not establish:** a genealogy, a temple, a priesthood, an image on earth, a narrative cycle, an empire-wide festival, or a direct identity with Yacana.

The duplicated name in the final clause is textually troublesome. Lehmann-Nitsche proposed that the young animal was originally **Uñachillay**, corrupted by a recorder or copyist. That correction is plausible but cannot be treated as recovered fact.

### José de Acosta, *Historia natural y moral de las Indias*, 1590

Acosta repeats the pastoral-star passage in book V, chapter 4, including the multicolored male llama, conservation of herds, Lyra, and the nearby mother-and-young figures. He then generalizes that earthly animals and birds had celestial counterparts responsible for their “procreation and increase.” **[Colonial systematization based substantially on Polo]**

Acosta is an important witness to the circulation of Polo’s information but not an independent earlier attestation. His language also compares Andean ideas with European classical and philosophical categories, especially celestial “offices” and Platonic forms.

### Miguel Cabello Valboa, *Miscelánea antártica*, 1586

Cabello lists Urcuchillay among notable stars venerated after the Pleiades/Collca. His notice confirms the name’s colonial circulation but gives little independent information about ritual or narrative. **[Early colonial attestation, possibly dependent or cognate reporting]**

### Anonymous Jesuit/“Blas Valera,” late sixteenth century

The *Relación de las costumbres antiguas de los naturales del Pirú* describes stars as having offices to create, guard, and sustain animals and plants, and says earthly images could represent celestial models. The attribution to Blas Valera is disputed. It supports the broader celestial-prototype system but does not supply an independent Urcuchillay biography. **[Early colonial learned systematization]**

### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales*, 1609

Garcilaso describes dark patches in the Milky Way imagined as a complete female llama nursing a lamb. He reports that he could see the patches but not the figure because he did not know how to imagine it. He does not call this figure Urcuchillay in that passage. **[Regional/Cusco recollection]**

This is strong evidence for a Milky Way llama-and-young tradition, but not for the proposition that Urcuchillay himself was that dark constellation.

### Huarochirí Manuscript, approximately 1598–1608

Chapter 29 of the anonymous Quechua manuscript compiled in Francisco de Ávila’s anti-idolatry milieu describes **Yacana**:

- a black or shadow-like double of a llama;
- very large, with a long neck and two eyes;
- moving through the middle of the celestial river;
- descending at night to drink water;
- the vitalizing *camac* of earthly llamas;
- a source of extraordinary good fortune when it falls upon a person;
- responsible for the increase of the fortunate person’s herd.

It also describes Yacana’s young and other stars. The manuscript is regional testimony from Huarochirí, not an empire-wide Inca catechism. Salomon and Urioste’s 1991 edition provides Quechua transcription, English translation, annotations, and a major study; the manuscript is held in Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional de España, MS 3169.

**Relationship to Urcuchillay:** structurally similar but textually unproven identity. Yacana is a dark Milky Way figure; Polo’s Urcuchillay is a named star or asterism identified by him with Lyra, while Catachillay and its young stand nearby. **[Regional analogue, not secure synonym]**

### Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, completed 1653

Cobo separates Urcuchillay from Catachillay. His account says herders venerated Urcuchillay, while another celestial llama, **Catachillay**, large beside a smaller one, represented a llama with her young and was said to proceed from Urcuchillay. Cobo is late and extensively compilatory; the “proceeded from” phrase supplies a relation absent or unclear in the earlier extracts. **[Later colonial elaboration]**

### Antonio de la Calancha, *Corónica moralizada*, 1638–1639

Calancha repeats the multicolored Urcuquillay/Lyra report and the adjacent mother-and-young pair. His larger purpose is Christian moralized comparison, including an analogy to Aries. **[Later colonial derivative testimony]**

### Sources named in the brief but silent or non-specific

Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Molina, Guaman Poma, Cristóbal de Albornoz, Arriaga, and the Cajatambo trial corpus are indispensable for Inca and colonial Andean religion, sacrifice, pastoral ritual, huacas, and suppression. In the accessible editions and scholarship reviewed here, however, they do **not** furnish a clear independent narrative about a being named Urcuchillay.

Their silence matters. General evidence for llama sacrifice cannot automatically be assigned to Urcuchillay, and every camelid image or pastoral rite cannot be called his cult.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Urcuchillay’s securely documented domain is **the conservation of pastoral livestock**, principally llamas and probably alpacas within Spanish writers’ imprecise category of *ganado de la tierra*. Acosta’s broader system adds species generation and increase. **[Early colonial attestation]**

He is therefore associated with:

- herd health and survival;
- reproduction and increase;
- pastoral occupation;
- the correspondence between terrestrial animals and celestial prototypes.

No source explicitly assigns him veterinary medicine, wild animals universally, hunting, commerce, textile production, weather, or sea regulation. Those associations may follow indirectly from camelid life but are not documented divine offices.

### Form and color

Polo’s “male llama of many colors” is the defining description. “Many colors” may describe stellar scintillation, a mottled celestial figure, a symbolic ideal of diverse herd colors, or ritual color classification. The source does not explain it. Later illustrations showing a rainbow-striped llama are imaginative modern literalizations. **[Modern invention where presented as ancient canonical art]**

### Stellar location: Lyra or not?

Polo and writers dependent on his description say Urcuchillay was what “the astrologers call Lyra.” That is the earliest explicit European equation. Yet it is problematic:

- Lyra is far north of the southern Milky Way dark llama.
- The nearby mother-and-young configuration does not fit neatly with Lyra if identified with the Alpha/Beta Centauri llama.
- Early European observers often imposed classical constellation labels on Indigenous asterisms without recording enough positional information.
- A historical critique already observed that drawing a llama across Lyra and neighboring Hercules required selectively joining faint stars.
- Modern proposals include Vega/Lyra, the Southern Cross region, or another southern asterism. None can be proven from Polo’s short text.

Thus **“Urcuchillay = Lyra” is an attested colonial identification, not a securely reconstructed sky map**.

### Urcuchillay versus Yacana

The popular equation collapses two types of Andean constellation:

- **bright-star constellations or asterisms**, formed by joining stars;
- **dark-cloud constellations**, perceived in the dust lanes of the Milky Way.

Yacana is explicitly dark. Polo’s Urcuchillay is called a star, with another pair near it. Cobo likewise distinguishes Urcuchillay from the llama Catachillay. Zuidema and Urton treated the traditions comparatively, but similarity does not erase the names or source distinctions.

The safest reconstruction is a **family of related celestial-camelid conceptions**, varying by region and recorder, rather than one universally named constellation.

### Alleged images

No pre-conquest image bears an inscription identifying it as Urcuchillay.

- **Taira rock art, northern Chile:** camelids, including nursing animals, have been compared with Yacana/Catachillay and the celestial llama complex. The site’s imagery reflects camelid-centered ceremonialism, but identifying a particular figure as Urcuchillay is a **modern scholarly interpretation**, not an epigraphic identification.
- **Choquequirao llama terraces:** white-stone camelid mosaics have been interpreted as caravans, processional herds, or the celestial llama. Their identification with Urcuchillay remains conjectural.
- **Metal and shell camelid miniatures:** many Inca examples survive, including capacocha assemblages. They demonstrate the ritual importance of camelids, not the individual identity of Urcuchillay.
- **Conopas/illas:** small stone camelid figures used in household and herd fertility rites belong to the wider pastoral sacred complex. No securely proven specimen can be labeled a cult statue of Urcuchillay solely by form.

### Symbols and absent attributes

Secure: llama form, plurality of colors, starry/celestial position, livestock.

Not securely attested: weapons, regalia, sacred plant, fixed color, weekday, number, spouse, temple emblem, or anthropomorphic form.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No source gives Urcuchillay parents, birth, consort, siblings, or a full family tree.

Cobo’s statement that the mother llama and young “proceeded from Urcuchillay” is the nearest thing to genealogy. It might mean descent, emanation, or simply association. It should not be expanded into a canonical father–mother–child triad.

### Every documented named divine interaction or relationship

- **Catachillay/Catuchillay — adjacent celestial association.** The large celestial llama and its young are said to be near Urcuchillay; Cobo adds that they “proceeded from” him. Polo, 1559; Acosta, 1590; Cobo, 1653. **[Early attested adjacency; later genealogizing elaboration]**
- **Uñachillay — possible young llama.** A modern philological correction for the smaller animal whose copied name appears as Urcuchillay. No surviving early text securely names it Uñachillay. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Yacana — homologous regional celestial llama, not a narrated encounter.** Huarochirí Manuscript, chapter 29, ca. 1598–1608. The two never meet in a surviving story. **[Regional analogue; modern comparative equation]**
- **Viracocha — no documented interaction.** Urcuchillay occurs in a religious classification that also discusses Viracocha, but no episode joins them.
- **Inti/Sun — no documented interaction.**
- **Illapa/Chuquilla/Catuilla — no documented interaction.**
- **Pachamama — no documented interaction.**
- **Mama Cocha/Sea — no documented interaction with Urcuchillay.** Yacana’s drinking of water should not be transferred automatically to him.
- **Chuquichinchay, Ancochinchay, Machacuay — functional parallel only.** Polo/Acosta place these animal-governing stars in the same classificatory system. No meeting, alliance, conflict, or kinship is narrated.

**Finding:** there is no surviving myth in which Urcuchillay converses, fights, marries, deceives, judges, rescues, or is punished by another named divine being.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The herders’ celestial llama

This is not a developed plot but the core Urcuchillay tradition.

Herders recognized a celestial multicolored male llama responsible for preserving their animals. Because their livelihood required his favor, they venerated him and offered sacrifice. Nearby they saw a large llama and a smaller young animal. **Earliest source:** Polo, 1559. **[Early colonial attestation]**

Theologically, the account belongs to a larger claim that animals on earth had celestial counterparts responsible for their generation and increase. Whether this was an empire-wide formal doctrine or Polo and Acosta’s consolidation of several occupational practices is debated.

### The Yacana descent story

The Huarochirí story is often retold as “the myth of Urcuchillay,” but the manuscript names Yacana.

Yacana is the dark double and vitalizing prototype of llamas. She moves through the celestial river and descends at night to drink. If she falls over a fortunate person, she overwhelms him with her wool or presence; the person must obtain a male and female llama, and the herd subsequently multiplies prodigiously. Yacana has a young animal that suckles from her. **[Regional variant: Yauyos/Huarochirí, ca. 1598–1608]**

Later retellings commonly add that she drinks from the ocean to prevent a world-destroying flood or urinates rain back into the cosmos. The water-circulation interpretation has an intelligible Andean cosmological logic, but the full standardized formulation belongs largely to modern synthesis. It should not be represented as a verbatim Urcuchillay myth.

### No surviving cosmogony or heroic cycle

There is no securely attested story of Urcuchillay’s creation, descent to earth, battle, marriage, death, resurrection, gift of medicinal plants, or instruction of the first shepherd. Internet stories in which he personally teaches veterinary medicine, rescues individual herders, or speaks in an adventure narrative are **modern inventions unless accompanied by a traceable oral collection**.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Worshippers

Polo specifically identifies **ovejeros**, camelid herders, as the worshipping group. This occupational organization is important: Urcuchillay was not said to require universal worship. Acosta explains that people attended particularly to the celestial power whose favor their work required. **[Early colonial attestation]**

### Sacrifice

Polo says herders made “veneration and sacrifice” to Urcuchillay but does not specify the victim, calendar, place, officiant, or procedure. Therefore:

- a sacrificial cult is securely attested;
- “llamas were sacrificed to Urcuchillay” is plausible but not explicit in the key sentence;
- detailed Inca llama-sacrifice rites directed to Inti, Illapa, royal ancestors, huacas, capacocha destinations, or state festivals cannot be reassigned to Urcuchillay without evidence.

Colonial accounts document color-selected camelid offerings in the state calendar, and archaeology confirms the ritual killing and burial of camelids. At Tambo Viejo, sacrificed llamas were deposited during the establishment of an Inca provincial center. At El Pacífico, analysis reconstructed the removal of a yearling llama’s heart and rapid burial. These discoveries corroborate the material reality of Inca camelid sacrifice but do not identify Urcuchillay as recipient.

### Human sacrifice

There is **no evidence of human sacrifice specifically to Urcuchillay**. The Inca state did perform capacocha rites involving children, as both chroniclers and high-altitude archaeology establish, often together with camelid figurines and other offerings. Conflating this with Urcuchillay’s cult would be unsupported.

### Priests, temples, and festivals

No named priesthood, temple, shrine, oracle, pilgrimage, estate, or calendar festival of Urcuchillay is securely documented. Worship may have occurred through herders, household ritual specialists, or local custodians, but the sources do not say.

Claims of a dedicated “Temple of Urcuchillay” or a universal annual Urcuchillay festival are **modern inventions or unverified extrapolations**.

### Herd fertility objects and rites

Pastoral communities used camelid-shaped stones, *illas*, *conopas*, fat, wool, coca, chicha, colored cords, and animal offerings in regional fertility rites. Seventeenth-century Cajatambo records document offerings, llama blood, conopas, local huacas, ritual specialists, and determined resistance to colonial suppression. Because the trials were coerced proceedings and record locally named beings, they must be read critically.

The accessible Cajatambo corpus does not establish that all such rites were addressed to Urcuchillay. It is evidence for the religious environment in which a herd-conserving celestial being makes sense, not automatically for his named cult.

### Economics and politics

Camelids provided transport, fiber, meat, hides, dung, sacrificial victims, and wealth. State herds and local herds were important economic resources. A celestial patron of herd survival therefore occupied a domain with direct economic consequences.

Yet Polo’s evidence suggests occupational, not necessarily centralized, worship. No evidence places Urcuchillay alongside Inti as the beneficiary of a great state temple economy.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the Inca state

Camelid ritual long predates Tawantinsuyu. At Pacopampa in Peru’s northern highlands, young llamas were ritually consumed and four immature llamas sacrificed in contexts dating within 1200–400 BCE. Numerous later Andean societies represented and offered camelids.

This demonstrates an ancient ritual field but **not the antiquity of the name Urcuchillay**. No pre-Inca inscription or securely identified image names him.

### Inca period, approximately fifteenth century–1532

Urcuchillay likely belonged to an established Inca-period or southern Andean pastoral cosmology by the time Polo recorded it in 1559. The short interval after conquest, the occupational specificity, and multiple derivative colonial notices make wholesale Spanish invention improbable. Nevertheless, the exact pre-conquest geographical range cannot be reconstructed.

Possible material correlates include camelid miniatures, pastoral *illas*, rock art, Choquequirao’s llama mosaics, and sacrificed llamas. None bears the name.

### 1530s–1559: conquest and early evangelization

Spanish conquest disrupted state institutions, appropriated herds and land, and imposed Christian categories. Missionaries translated varied sacred entities as *dioses*, *ídolos*, or demonic errors. Polo recorded Urcuchillay as part of a campaign to understand and govern Indigenous religion.

### 1559–1590: ecclesiastical codification

Polo’s report was used in colonial church deliberation and apparently fed the *Confessionario para los curas de indios* milieu. Acosta’s 1590 history reproduced and generalized the celestial-species doctrine. The figure thus entered a written Christian archive specifically designed to identify beliefs requiring correction.

### ca. 1598–1653: regional documentation and synthesis

The Huarochirí Manuscript recorded Yacana in Quechua, preserving a richer regional celestial-llama narrative. Garcilaso remembered a mother llama and young in the Milky Way. Calancha and Cobo repeated or expanded Polo’s Urcuchillay passage. In this period, several distinguishable traditions were preserved, but also increasingly harmonized by colonial authors.

### Seventeenth-century suppression

Beginning especially around 1610 in the Archdiocese of Lima, extirpation campaigns interrogated Indigenous people, destroyed sacred objects, burned or confiscated ritual materials, punished specialists, and sought to replace local rites with Catholic observance. Cajatambo became a major target. The trials record persistence and concealment as well as forced confession.

No surviving proceeding reviewed here narrates the destruction of a named Urcuchillay temple. His cult’s institutional suppression must therefore be inferred from the campaign against celestial worship and pastoral huacas generally, not from a specific documented demolition.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

The divine name largely disappears from prominent written documentation, while Andean pastoral ritual continued under local names and in relation to mountain owners, Pachamama, saints, crosses, livestock *illas*, and Catholic feast calendars. This is better described as transformation and regional continuity than as proof of a secret, unchanged Urcuchillay church.

### Twentieth-century ethnography

Zuidema and Urton reopened the question by comparing the chronicles, Huarochirí, and living Quechua astronomy. Urton’s work in Misminay documented dark-cloud constellations, including a llama, within an active agricultural and pastoral cosmology.

Modern Quechua and Aymara communities continue to observe celestial llamas, perform herd-marking rites, decorate animals, make libations, offer food or animals, and relate livestock to mountains and the Milky Way. In southern Bolivia, pastoral rites use decorated camelids, cords, pompons, chicha, and offerings; in northwestern Argentina, *señalada* ceremonies and offerings to Pachamama or local owners continue.

What is continuous is the **pastoral sacred complex**. The proposition that all participants call its celestial patron Urcuchillay, or consciously worship the exact being described by Polo, is not established.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

Urcuchillay now appears in museum interpretation, archaeology, ethnoastronomy, educational material, and popular lists of “Inca gods.” Modern accounts frequently fuse:

1. Polo’s multicolored male llama-star;
2. Catachillay’s mother-and-young pair;
3. Huarochirí’s dark Yacana;
4. Urton’s modern ethnographic dark llama.

This produces a vivid composite but suppresses historical and regional differences.

### Literature, games, and digital media

Urcuchillay appears in mythology websites, fan pantheons, fantasy art, and games. *Shadow of the Tomb Raider* includes an Urcuchillay mural or lore reference presenting him as an animal-protecting Inca deity. Such works materially spread the image of a benevolent llama god but are modern adaptations, not sources for pre-conquest tradition.

The widespread rainbow or brightly striped llama image visualizes “many colors.” No surviving Inca artwork establishes that design.

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search found no evidence that Urcuchillay held a documented place in Renaissance Hermeticism, theosophy, the Golden Dawn, classical ceremonial magic, or early modern European grimoires. Claims of an ancient Urcuchillay sigil, planetary correspondence, invocation, tarot attribution, or magical color table are therefore **modern inventions** unless accompanied by recent creator attribution.

Contemporary Andean spirituality and pan-Indigenous or neopagan practice may invoke Urcuchillay today, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes the scale of a distinct Urcuchillay-centered movement. Modern worship should be reported as modern religious creativity, not dismissed, but it is not proof of unchanged continuity.

### Political and commercial appropriation

No substantial extremist appropriation or exclusive nationalist cult emerged in the searched evidence. Urcuchillay is occasionally used as a symbol of Inca heritage, pastoral identity, tourism, and commercial “Andean spirituality.” A Peruvian spider species, *Myrmecium urcuchillay*, was named in 2019; this is taxonomic commemoration, not cult.

### Modern false or unsupported claims

The following commonly circulating statements exceed the evidence:

- Urcuchillay was securely the dark constellation Yacana.
- He was unquestionably the constellation Lyra in modern IAU boundaries.
- Vega was definitively his eye or body.
- He was female and nursed a calf.
- He descended to drink the ocean and prevent a flood.
- He taught herders medicinal plants.
- He had a named wife and son in a canonical family.
- Choquequirao or Taira contains a proven portrait of him.
- He had a great temple, formal priesthood, fixed feast, or empire-wide liturgy.
- human beings were sacrificed specifically to him.
- his worship has continued unchanged under the same name to the present.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Dependence among chroniclers

A central historiographical problem is that repeated colonial testimony is not necessarily multiple independent confirmation. Polo’s language was copied or adapted by Acosta, Calancha, Cobo, and other writers. Modern scholarship on Polo emphasizes intertextual borrowing among chroniclers.

Therefore, the statement “many chroniclers independently witnessed Urcuchillay worship” is misleading. The evidence may descend substantially from one influential report.

### The lost original

Polo’s original 1559 manuscript is lost. What survives is an extractive textual tradition, later publication, and reuse by colonial authors. This raises problems of orthography and omission, particularly the confusing repetition of Urcuchillay as the young animal’s name.

### The Uñachillay correction

Lehmann-Nitsche argued that the young llama’s name should have been **Uñachillay**, based on Quechua *uña*. This elegantly explains the duplicated Urcuchillay, but it remains conjectural emendation. Modern writers should print the transmitted reading and explain the proposed correction rather than silently substituting it.

### Lyra versus a southern asterism

The inherited Lyra identification has been challenged on positional and methodological grounds. European constellation names may be approximations, and the mother-and-young complex fits the southern Milky Way/Centaurus region better than northern Lyra. Some modern guides propose the Southern Cross or Vega, but the source data are insufficient for certainty.

The most rigorous formula is:

> Polo equated Urcuchillay with European Lyra; the exact Indigenous star pattern is unresolved.

### Urcuchillay, Catachillay, and Yacana

Zuidema and Urton showed the value of comparing the three traditions but did not erase their textual differences. The nursing llama image has broad Andean distribution, while names and astronomical constructions vary.

- Polo: Urcuchillay plus nearby Catachillay and young.
- Cobo: Catachillay and young near, and proceeding from, Urcuchillay.
- Garcilaso: unnamed dark mother and young in the Milky Way.
- Huarochirí: Yacana, dark *camac* of llamas, with young.
- modern Misminay: living dark-cloud llama astronomy.

“Variant forms of a wider celestial-livestock complex” is better supported than “all are one deity.”

### Archaeological interpretation

Taira, Choquequirao, camelid miniatures, and sacrifice deposits provide valuable independent evidence for camelid sacredness. None identifies Urcuchillay by name. Iconographic equations must remain hypotheses.

### Colonial-source bias

Polo, Acosta, Ávila, Arriaga, and extirpation records were created within governance and conversion projects. Terms such as *idolatría*, *hechicero*, and *demonio* are colonial classifications. Testimony from trials could be coerced or shaped by leading questions.

At the same time, dismissing the records entirely would erase much of the only surviving Indigenous religious testimony. The appropriate method is source criticism: identify recorder, purpose, language, transmission, and regional setting.

### Major modern scholarship

- R. Tom Zuidema and Gary Urton, “La constelación de la Llama en los Andes peruanos” (1976), established the foundational comparison of colonial and ethnographic celestial-llama evidence.
- Gary Urton, *At the Crossroads of the Earth and the Sky* (1981), documented living Quechua astronomy in Misminay and distinguished dark-cloud constellations from star-to-star figures.
- Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste, *The Huarochirí Manuscript* (1991), supplied the critical English treatment of Yacana in its full regional textual context.
- Pierre Duviols’ work on extirpation and the Cajatambo corpus reconstructed colonial suppression and preserved trial evidence.
- Brian Bauer and David Dearborn reassessed Inca astronomy and the methodological difficulty of mapping chroniclers’ names onto modern constellations.
- Sabine MacCormack, María Rostworowski, Irene Silverblatt, and other historians situated such beings within colonial translation, gender, state power, and changing Andean religion.

## 10. Chronology

- **1200–400 BCE:** Ritual consumption and sacrifice of young llamas at Pacopampa. **[Archaeological background; not Urcuchillay by name]**
- **Pre-fifteenth century:** Camelid imagery, offerings, and pastoral ritual develop across multiple Andean societies. **[Background; named continuity unproven]**
- **ca. 1400–1532:** Inca imperial period; camelids become central to state transport, textile wealth, tribute, sacrifice, and capacocha offerings. Urcuchillay may already be worshipped by pastoralists, but no contemporary alphabetic text names him.
- **1532 onward:** Spanish conquest and Christianization disrupt Inca state religion and recategorize Andean sacred beings.
- **1559:** Polo de Ondegardo records Urcuchillay in Cusco as a multicolored celestial male llama honored with sacrifice by herders for livestock conservation. **Earliest secure attestation.**
- **1567–1580s:** Polo’s religious report circulates in ecclesiastical contexts and influences colonial pastoral literature.
- **1586:** Cabello Valboa lists Urcuchillay among venerated stars.
- **1590:** Acosta prints the influential account of Urcuchillay and celestial animal counterparts in *Historia natural y moral de las Indias*.
- **ca. 1598–1608:** The Quechua Huarochirí Manuscript records Yacana, the dark celestial llama and *camac* of terrestrial llamas. **[Regional related tradition]**
- **1609:** Garcilaso describes a dark mother llama nursing its young in the Milky Way, without naming it Urcuchillay.
- **ca. 1610–1671:** Organized extirpation campaigns target Indigenous religion in the Lima archdiocese, including Huarochirí and Cajatambo; sacred objects are confiscated or destroyed and practitioners punished.
- **1621:** Arriaga publishes *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú*, codifying methods for detecting and suppressing huaca worship. He supplies religious context but no distinct Urcuchillay narrative.
- **1638–1639:** Calancha repeats the Urcuquillay/Lyra and mother-with-young account.
- **1653:** Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, distinguishing Urcuchillay from Catachillay and her young while saying the latter proceeded from him.
- **Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries:** The name recedes from major written sources; pastoral celestial and herd-fertility practices continue under diverse regional and Christianized forms.
- **1906:** Polo’s surviving extract is printed in *Revista Histórica*, making the key passage widely available.
- **1920s:** Lehmann-Nitsche examines the stellar names, disputes careless sky identifications, and proposes Uñachillay for the calf.
- **1966:** José María Arguedas publishes an influential Spanish translation of the Huarochirí Manuscript.
- **1976:** Zuidema and Urton publish “La constelación de la Llama en los Andes peruanos.”
- **1981:** Urton publishes *At the Crossroads of the Earth and the Sky*, documenting living dark-cloud constellation knowledge in Misminay.
- **1987:** Gerald Taylor publishes a major Quechua–Spanish edition of Huarochirí.
- **1991:** Salomon and Urioste publish the annotated Quechua–English *Huarochirí Manuscript*.
- **2003:** Duviols and collaborators publish the extensive seventeenth-century Cajatambo idolatry-trial corpus.
- **Twenty-first century:** Archaeological studies document Inca llama sacrifice at sites including Tambo Viejo and El Pacífico; heritage and popular media increasingly present Urcuchillay as a benevolent “Inca llama god.”
- **2019:** The spider *Myrmecium urcuchillay* is formally named.
- **Present:** Celestial-llama knowledge and pastoral blessing rites remain alive in portions of Peru, Bolivia, and northwestern Argentina, but continuity of the specific divine name Urcuchillay is uneven and cannot be assumed.

### Critical conclusion

The historically recoverable Urcuchillay is a narrowly but clearly documented celestial protector of pastoral herds, first recorded in 1559 as a multicolored male llama-star receiving veneration and sacrifice from herders. Almost everything beyond that core—his identification with the dark Yacana, a fixed celestial map, a family, myths of ocean drinking, temples, formal festivals, universal animal sovereignty, and detailed iconography—is either a regional analogue, later colonial elaboration, modern scholarly reconstruction, or modern invention. The small corpus is not evidence that he was insignificant; it is evidence that the colonial archive preserved only a fragment of an occupational and largely non-literate pastoral religion.
