# Catequil: Research Dossier

**Class:** male *wak’a*—simultaneously a named god, animate mountain/cliff, stone image, oracle, lineage-founder, and thunder/lightning power—in the Huamachuco tradition of Peru’s northern Andes. “God” is convenient English shorthand, but colonial testimony treats Catequil less as an incorporeal member of a fixed pantheon than as a powerful nonhuman person materially present in Cerro Icchal, sacred rocks, an anthropomorphic stone, subsidiary stones, waterworks, and priests.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest presently identifiable written naming is Juan de Betanzos’s Spanish *Suma y narración de los incas*, composed at Cuzco about 1551, which reports Atahualpa’s destruction of the Huamachuco oracle. The fuller Augustinian *Relación de la religión y ritos del Perú en Huamachuco* derives from fieldwork in the 1550s and is conventionally dated 1557 or circa 1560, although its surviving sixteenth-century manuscript is anonymous and undated. Archaeology at Namanchugo documents ritual activity beginning about CE 400, but no pre-Hispanic inscription names Catequil; identifying the early phases specifically with him is a modern archaeological reconstruction, not a contemporary written attestation.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[early colonial attestation]**, **[later colonial elaboration]**, **[regional variant]**, **[Inca-period development]**, **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**, **[folklore/ethnographic survival]**, and **[modern invention or unverified claim]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Catequil was the principal oracular *wak’a* of late pre-Hispanic Huamachuco, a Culle-speaking region centered in the modern Peruvian department of La Libertad. The sources attribute several functions to the same named being: he makes thunder, lightning, and rain; speaks through an oracle; supplies food, livestock, and children; defeats the primordial Guachemines; restores his dead mother; and opens Cerro Guacat so that the ancestors of the Huamachucos can emerge. Under Inca rule his sanctuary became an oracle of imperial political consequence. He supported—or his priests spoke for—the Huáscar faction in the civil war, after which Atahualpa destroyed the sanctuary shortly before the Spaniards reached Cajamarca. The cult nevertheless survived clandestinely until Augustinian friars seized and destroyed recovered fragments of the image.

### Names and spellings

The evidence is entirely in Spanish colonial orthographies; there is no known indigenous inscription preserving the name.

- **Catequil / Cataquil / Gatequil** — the usual forms in the Augustinian tradition. Orthographic fluctuation between *e/a*, *c/g*, and final *-quil/-quill* is normal in early transcriptions.
- **Apo Catequil / Apocatequil / Apocatiquillay** — *apu* is Quechua for a powerful lord and, in later usage, a mountain power. Cristóbal de Albornoz’s *Instrucción* (after 1581, usually dated c.1582–1584) writes **Apocatiquillay** and calls it a principal *guaca* of the Huamachucos. The final *-y* may reflect a possessive or affective Quechua form, but no secure linguistic analysis has been established.
- **Cataquilla / Catequilla** — forms appearing in Sarmiento de Gamboa and later toponyms, especially in Ecuador. Similar spelling does not by itself prove that every Ecuadorian Catequilla site belonged to the Huamachuco cult.
- **Guaracayoc** — Albornoz gives this as the Huamachuco *pacarisca*. Because Quechua *waraka-yuq* means approximately “one possessing a sling,” John Topic suggests it may be a title of sling-bearing Catequil. This remains **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**; the Augustinian account separately lists Guaracayoc among nine important local *wak’as*.
- **“Son(s) of Catequil”** — a class rather than a personal epithet: lightning stones and dependent *wak’as* whose power derived from Catequil. The Augustinians say these multiplied after the Spaniards entered Peru.

No critical Culle etymology of **Catequil** has been demonstrated. The name may be Culle or contain a Culle element, since Huamachuco was historically Culle-speaking, but popular derivations that simply equate it with “thunder” are circular. Claims that *Catequil* means an aquatic animal-lord, fortune, valor, or a specifically Ecuadorian initiation rite are not supported by the early Huamachuco texts and should be treated as **[modern invention or unverified synthesis]** unless independently documented.

### Classification and later equations

- **Huamachuco classification:** *wak’a*, *apu*, oracle, thunder-maker, ancestor-founder, and stone/mountain person.
- **Inca-period classification:** a major provincial oracle incorporated into imperial networks rather than merely an aspect of a uniform “Inca pantheon.”
- **Catequil and Illapa:** both are lightning powers armed with slings, but no early text simply says “Catequil is Illapa.” Their equation is best treated as **[modern comparative reconstruction]** or, at most, an Inca-period functional assimilation.
- **Catequil and Santiago:** post-conquest traditions across the Andes transferred thunder, mounted-warrior, and conqueror imagery to Santiago. Topic describes a reciprocal local syncretism in which Santiago absorbed attributes of Catequil. Direct documentation of a formal cult titled “Catequil-Santiago,” however, has not been established.
- **Catequil and Libiac/Yaro:** seventeenth-century central-Andean extirpation records document Libiac and related names for lightning powers. They illuminate a widespread religious category but do not prove identity with Catequil.
- **Catequil and Pachacamac:** not equivalent. In the Huarochirí Manuscript, Catequil causes Llocllayhuancupa—presented as a child or emissary of Pachacamac—to speak. That is an interaction among distinct *wak’as*.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaeology before the texts

Namanchugo, on a plain beneath Cerro Icchal near San José de Porcón, was occupied from approximately CE 400 until the sixteenth century. Ritual and feasting activity preceded the formalized late sanctuary. Around CE 1200, according to the Topics’ reconstruction, access became more restricted and monumental; Cerro Icchal may then have served worshippers excluded from the elite sanctuary. These dates establish a long sacred history but not the name Catequil during the earliest phases. The equation Namanchugo–Catequil rests on unusually close agreement between excavated topography, architecture, ritual equipment, and sixteenth-century descriptions.

### Principal texts

1. **Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los incas*** — Spanish, c.1551; surviving through later manuscript copies, with the complete text first critically published by María del Carmen Martín Rubio in 1987. **[early colonial attestation]** Betanzos says Atahualpa treated the Huamachuco oracle as an enemy and spent an extended period destroying it by fire. Betanzos is the earliest securely dated named witness, but he wrote after the event from indigenous and Inca informants rather than as an eyewitness.

2. **Anonymous Augustinian *Relación de la religión y ritos del Perú en Huamachuco*** — Spanish, based on Augustinian activity during the 1550s; conventionally 1557/c.1560. The Archivo General de Indias manuscript is AGI, Patronato 192, ramo 6, no. 2; it comprises eighteen folios, is anonymous and undated, and was first printed in 1865. Lucila Castro de Trelles produced the modern critical edition in 1992 and a revised edition in 2022. **[early colonial attestation]** It is the indispensable source for Catequil’s cosmogony, oracle, shrine, cult personnel, ritual initiation, recovered fragments, and Augustinian suppression. Its limitations are equally important: testimony was elicited amid evangelization, confiscation, coercion, and the destruction—claimed by the friars—of more than 3,000 *wak’as*.

3. **Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia índica*** — Spanish, 1572. **[early colonial attestation]** Sarmiento gives the most dramatic account of Atahualpa confronting the oracle: two nobles sacrifice and inquire; the response condemns Atahualpa as cruel and predicts a bad end; Atahualpa beheads the aged shell-covered priest and then breaks the stone image and levels the sanctuary. His formulation reflects Toledo-era political historiography and Christian demonological vocabulary.

4. **Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú y sus camayos y haziendas*** — Spanish, after 1581, generally c.1582–1584. **[early colonial attestation]** Lists “Apocatiquillay” among the principal *wak’as* of “Guamachuco y Caxamarca,” while specifying that it belonged to the Huamachucos. Albornoz’s administrative grouping helped generate later confusion about a supposedly Cajamarcan origin.

5. **Huarochirí Manuscript**, Quechua, compiled at the beginning of the seventeenth century, probably under Francisco de Ávila’s anti-idolatry inquiry; modern critical editions include Gerald Taylor and Frank Salomon/George Urioste. **[regional variant]** Chapter 20 places Catequil in Huarochirí: he makes the previously mute *wak’a* Llocllayhuancupa speak and disclose its connection with Pachacamac. This is firm evidence that Catequil’s reputation as an oracular enabler circulated well beyond Huamachuco.

6. **Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú*** — Spanish, 1621. **[later colonial elaboration/regional variant]** Gives the Conchucos version: after a ruler descended from Topa Inca burned Porcón, priests carried Catequil to Cabana and Tauca. Arriaga calls the ruler Huáscar, an impossible genealogy if he is Topa Inca’s son.

7. **Antonio de la Calancha, *Corónica moralizada*** — Spanish, first volume 1638. **[later colonial elaboration]** Repeats and adjusts the Conchucos story, replacing Arriaga’s Huáscar with Huayna Cápac. This solves the genealogy but conflicts with the Huamachuco tradition that Atahualpa destroyed the oracle around 1532.

8. **Cieza de León** — mid-sixteenth century. Cieza says Cajamarca and Huamachuco shared religious customs, but surviving passages do not give the full Catequil cycle. Using Cieza to make Catequil originally Cajamarcan exceeds his evidence.

9. **Cobo, Molina, Garcilaso, Guaman Poma and the general anti-idolatry corpus** — valuable for Inca lightning theology, *wak’a* practice, sacrifice, divination, sacred landscapes, and colonial repression, but they do not preserve an independent full biography of Catequil. Garcilaso’s hymn to thunder has sometimes been assigned to Catequil; the text itself does not name him. Guaman Poma’s images of Sun and lightning provide comparisons, not secure portraits of Catequil.

10. **Huarochirí and Cajatambo trials** — seventeenth-century Quechua/Spanish testimony documents continuing devotion to speaking stones, mountains, Libiac-like lightning beings, ancestral *mallquis*, and “children of thunder.” They demonstrate the religious environment in which a Catequil-like power was intelligible, but most do not name Catequil. Treating every northern or central-Andean lightning cult as his cult would erase local beings.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Early colonial attestation]**

- Thunder, lightning, flashes, and—by implication—rain.
- Oracular speech and political prediction.
- Warfare and expulsion of enemies.
- Human emergence and lineage foundation.
- Fertility: food, camelids, children, water, and crop renewal.
- Authorization of religious specialists.
- Protection through subsidiary stones.

The Augustinians say Catequil makes lightning and thunder by throwing stones with a **sling**. Archaeological sling stones at Namanchugo occur in pavements, offering deposits, and stone libation vessels, strengthening—but not independently proving—the identification.

### Material forms

Catequil was not confined to one image:

- **Cerro Icchal and its cliffs:** the mountain itself functioned as the *apu*.
- **Three great rocks:** identified in the Augustinian account as Apocatequil, his mother, and Piguerao.
- **Anthropomorphic stone image:** Atahualpa allegedly knocked off its head; fragments were later recovered, hidden, worshipped, and destroyed by friars.
- **Lightning stones:** small, smooth or rounded stones understood as products, children, or localized powers of the thunder being.
- **Portable extensions:** Inca rulers could move a stone, image, or cloth bearing a *wak’a’s* power without implying that the entire divine person had ceased to inhabit its mountain.

### Excavated sanctuary

The sanctuary at Namanchugo incorporated:

- A platform and restricted summit compounds.
- Artificial forms imitating the cliffs of Cerro Icchal.
- A stone interpreted as Mama Catequil/Cautaguan, with a natural abdominal hollow suggestive of an extracted egg.
- Rounded, egg-like river stones used in a retaining wall.
- Stone receptacles for libations.
- Drainage channels.
- A more complex channel in which changing liquid flow may have produced divinatory responses.
- *Spondylus* shell offerings, widely associated in the Andes with the sea, water, rain, fertility, and elite exchange.
- Sling stones.

The interpretation of the fluid system as an oracular mechanism is **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**: archaeologists found the channels, but no surviving indigenous instruction explains exactly how answers were generated. Claims that coca leaves moved through the channels are plausible modern elaborations, not securely stated in the early account.

### Iconographic limits

No securely identified pre-Hispanic painting or standardized sculptural canon of Catequil survives. The excavated Mama Catequil stone is a contextual identification. Popular modern images showing Catequil as a muscular human with a lightning-bolt staff, jaguar torso, prescribed colors, or zodiacal/celestial emblems are **[modern invention or unverified synthesis]**. The securely attested attributes are the sling, stones, mountain, lightning, water, and the triadic cliff landscape.

There is no secure ancient evidence for a sacred color, number, weekday, planet, or constellation peculiar to Catequil.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

The Augustinian Huamachuco cycle gives the following sequence:

- **Ataguju/Ataguchu:** supreme sky-associated progenitor, Catequil’s grandfather.
- **Huamansuri/Guamansuri:** Ataguju’s emissary or son and Catequil’s father.
- **Cautaguan/Cautahuan/Mama Catequil:** a woman of the Guachemin people, Catequil’s mother.
- **Piguerao/Pujuerao:** Catequil’s twin brother.
- **“Sons of Catequil”:** lightning stones and subsidiary *wak’as*, not a finite genealogical list.

Huamansuri descends to a world occupied by the Guachemines, conceals his power in a humble appearance, and impregnates Cautaguan. Her kin kill him. She lays two eggs and dies; the eggs are thrown onto a refuse heap, where Catequil and Piguerao hatch or emerge. Catequil revives his mother and takes the slings his father left behind.

There is no securely attested consort. Lists naming Tanta Guaganay or numerous local *wak’as* as biological “children” require case-by-case verification; colonial *kinship* among *wak’as* could express origin, dependence, political affiliation, or replicated power rather than human-like reproduction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The descent of Huamansuri and birth of the twins

**[Early colonial attestation: Augustinian *Relación*, c.1557–1560]**

Ataguju sends Huamansuri from the upper world into Huamachuco, then occupied by the Guachemines. Huamansuri arrives poorly dressed and conceals his divine status. He impregnates Cautaguan, a Guachemin woman. Her people kill him. Cautaguan produces two eggs and dies; the eggs are discarded on a rubbish heap. Two crying boys emerge, Catequil and Piguerao, and a woman raises them.

The report’s Christian writers call indigenous beings demons and frame the myth polemically; this vocabulary is evidence about missionary interpretation, not an indigenous classification.

### B. Resurrection and vengeance

Catequil visits the place where Cautaguan died and restores her to life. She gives him two slings left by Huamansuri. Catequil uses them to kill or expel the Guachemines, freeing the land.

Castro de Trelles notes that the *Relación* assigns the decisive action to Catequil alone. Rostworowski’s retelling lets both twins defeat the enemies and approach Ataguju together. That is a **[modern scholarly harmonization]**, not the most literal reading of the early account.

The historical meaning is debated:

- A mythic charter for Huamachuco identity and territorial possession.
- A memory of displacement between ecological or ethnic groups.
- Possibly a transformed memory of Wari-period conflict.
- A cosmogonic replacement of an earlier humanity.

Only the first two are strongly supported by the story’s structure; tying it to a specific archaeological war is speculative.

### C. Bringing the Huamachucos from Cerro Guacat

Catequil reports his victory to Ataguju and asks that humans be made to inhabit and cultivate the land. Ataguju sends him to the hill called Ipuna/Guacat. Catequil digs with gold and silver *tacllas*—Andean foot-ploughs or hoes—and brings the people out of the mountain. Guacat is therefore their *pacarina*, place of emergence and ancestral origin.

The myth links upper-world authority, lightning, mineral brightness, agricultural implements, mountain interiors, and human fertility. It does not describe creation from nothing: people emerge from an animate landscape.

### D. Catequil as thunder-maker

Catequil makes thunder, lightning bolts, and flashes by hurling stones with his sling. Fallen thunderstones are his children or material extensions. They protect, fertilize, heal, and reproduce sacred power. The story converts the weapon of vengeance into an enduring meteorological action.

### E. The political oracle and destruction by Atahualpa

Three early witnesses broadly agree that Atahualpa destroyed the Huamachuco oracle, but their particulars vary.

- **Betanzos, c.1551:** Atahualpa treats Catequil as an enemy following an adverse response and spends considerable effort destroying the sanctuary by fire.
- **Augustinian *Relación*, c.1557–1560:** Catequil supports Huáscar in the succession struggle; Atahualpa’s forces break the image, throw its head into a river, seize the sanctuary’s gold and silver, and burn the place so extensively that the author says charcoal remained visible.
- **Sarmiento, 1572:** two noblemen sacrifice and consult the oracle, which predicts Atahualpa’s bad end because he is a cruel, blood-shedding tyrant. Atahualpa personally beheads an extraordinarily old priest dressed in a long, shaggy, shell-covered garment; he then smashes the stone image and levels the hill.

The versions agree on adverse prophecy and violent retaliation. They differ on whether Catequil expressly favored Huáscar, whether Atahualpa personally killed the priest, and the physical sequence of destruction. Sarmiento’s moralized speech may reflect anti-Atahualpa and Toledo-era historiography.

### F. Recovery of the head and second destruction

In the Augustinian tradition, the chief priest recovered Catequil’s head and three pieces of the body from the river. As Spaniards advanced, devotees hid them in a cave or cleft and continued worship. Augustinian friars eventually discovered the remains, ground or burned them, and cast the residue into water. The exact year is often given as 1561 and the friars named Antonio Lozano and Juan Ramírez, but the anonymous *Relación*’s complicated dating makes “during the early Augustinian mission, around 1560” safer.

This is not proof that a god “died”; it is documentation of competing understandings of material personhood. Atahualpa and the missionaries acted as though destroying the stone and its cult personnel could disable an active political being, while devotees acted as though fragments retained Catequil’s agency.

### G. The Conchucos relocation

Arriaga’s 1621 account says a son of Topa Inca burned the Porcón sanctuary, but Catequil’s priests rescued the image and transferred it to Cabana and Tauca in Conchucos. Arriaga calls the ruler Huáscar; Calancha in 1638 changes him to Huayna Cápac. In this tradition the image remains intact enough to move, contradicting the Huamachuco story of smashing and recovery in pieces.

Topic’s reconstruction distinguishes a widespread thunder power from the particular Huamachuco oracle: more than one material Catequil could exist, and the Conchucos image need not have been the broken Porcón stone. This explains the evidence without declaring one account simply false, but it remains a modern model.

### H. Making another *wak’a* speak

In the Huarochirí Manuscript, an Inca-introduced Catequil enables Llocllayhuancupa to speak. The episode suggests that Catequil’s special competence was not merely answering questions but conferring oracular voice. It is the clearest primary evidence for his reception outside the northern cult center.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Principal sanctuary

The best-supported identification is **Namanchugo beneath Cerro Icchal**, near San José de Porcón in the historic province of Huamachuco, roughly 25 km from the modern town. Topic located the candidate landscape in 1997 and began excavation in 1998, guided by the Augustinians, Betanzos, and Albornoz.

The mountain, three cliffs/rocks, and built sanctuary formed one sacred complex. The late architecture imitated the mountain’s profile, materializing myth in built form. The shrine was not simply a “temple containing a statue”; mountain, stone, image, water, and building were related bodies of the *wak’a*.

### Cult personnel

The Augustinian material indicates:

- A principal oracle priest.
- Additional priests or attendants.
- *Camayos* or hereditary custodians of sacred things.
- Mayordomos/administrators managing property and offerings.
- Persons undergoing fasts and initiation before becoming religious specialists.
- A supporting settlement whose labor served the shrine.

Later antiquarian claims of exactly five priests, two stewards, and numerous slaves descend through secondary retellings and should not be generalized without reference to the manuscript passage and terminology.

Sarmiento’s elderly priest wears a long garment described as hairy or shaggy and covered with marine shells. The shells were likely prestigious *Spondylus* or related marine material, but that zoological identification is inferential.

### Oracle procedure

The sources establish consultation through priests and sacrifice, but not a single universal technique. Excavated channels and basins suggest controlled liquids and libations; Topic proposes that manipulation of water flow produced visible answers. Coca divination is common in Andean religion, but claims that Catequil specifically answered by moving coca leaves through these channels remain unproven.

The oracle’s political force was practical as well as theological. A prophecy for or against an Inca claimant could express the position of Huamachuco’s priesthood, local elites, and dependent communities. This does not reduce the oracle to fraud: in the surviving Andean framework, priests, rulers, stones, mountains, and ancestors were all political agents.

### Initiation

The *Relación* describes a disturbed man who experienced calls or manifestations. A specialist told him that the gods wanted him as a priest: he had to undertake prescribed fasts and travel to “visit and *mochar*”—revere—Catequil. Afterward he became a master. Catequil therefore authorized at least some specialists beyond his immediate sanctuary.

### Offerings and property

Attested or archaeologically represented offerings include:

- Camelids or “sheep.”
- Food and chicha/libations.
- Fine clothing and textiles.
- Trumpets and ritual equipment.
- Gold and silver objects.
- Marine shell, especially *Spondylus*.
- Stones connected with slings, lightning, and fertility.
- Labor and service from dependent personnel.

Inca rulers provisioned Huamachuco *wak’as* with garments, animals, and other wealth. The shrine’s estates, herds, textiles, and pilgrimage traffic formed an economic institution as well as a devotional center.

### Sacrifice and contested material

Sarmiento says Atahualpa’s noblemen “made sacrifice” before consulting the oracle but does not specify a human victim. The Augustinian Catequil cycle records the mythic killing of Huamansuri, the Guachemines, and the historical killing of the oracle priest; these are not evidence that worshippers routinely sacrificed humans to Catequil.

General Andean sources attest *capacocha*, animal sacrifice, blood offerings, and the sacrificial treatment of persons or animals struck by lightning or born anomalously. Yet no securely cited early passage establishes a regular Catequil-specific program of human sacrifice. Modern statements that “Catequil’s cult required human victims” are therefore **[unverified generalization]** unless tied to a particular document.

There is likewise no evidence for “temple prostitution.”

### Ordinary worship and sacred stones

Rounded stones linked to lightning were kept as protective and fertilizing objects. Archaeology found comparable stones in ritual contexts at Namanchugo. The Augustinians complained that dependent “children” of Catequil multiplied after the conquest—evidence both for decentralized survival and for missionary anxiety that destroying one image could not destroy a distributed sacred person.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### CE 400–c.1200: early Namanchugo

Namanchugo hosted ritual and communal activity from about CE 400. Feasting and sacred use can be established archaeologically; the name Catequil cannot. Calling this the “Catequil cult” is retrospective.

### c.1200–1470: formalized regional oracle

Around 1200 the sanctuary was rebuilt or reorganized with restricted access, specialized ritual space, and architecture referencing Cerro Icchal. Topic argues that Catequil developed as a major oracle during the Late Intermediate Period from an older regional lightning/sky power. Cerro Icchal may have offered a parallel arena for less elite pilgrims.

### Inca incorporation, late fifteenth–early sixteenth century

Huamachuco entered the Inca state. Rather than abolishing Catequil, Inca rulers patronized and politically deployed the oracle. A material extension was reportedly taken to Cuzco; Huayna Cápac carried or sponsored Catequil in campaigns and probably helped spread the name northward. This was negotiated incorporation, not necessarily the doctrinal subordination of Catequil to Illapa.

Sarmiento places “Cataquilla” with Huayna Cápac at Quito. Topic found a group of Ecuadorian **Catequilla** place-names, several associated with springs; this makes an Inca-period northern diffusion plausible. It does not validate every later Ecuadorian story attached to those names.

### c.1532: Atahualpa’s suppression

During the war with Huáscar, Catequil’s adverse prophecy placed the oracle in open political opposition to Atahualpa. The ruler destroyed the shrine despite news of Huáscar’s capture and the Spaniards’ arrival. Topic interprets the prolonged operation as evidence that Atahualpa regarded the oracle’s allegiance as a serious political threat.

### 1530s–c.1560: clandestine restoration

Priests recovered fragments and continued veneration in concealment. This demonstrates the inadequacy of describing the cult as ending in 1532.

### 1550s–1560s: Augustinian destruction

Augustinian missionaries searched out sacred sites, questioned practitioners, destroyed images, and established Christian settlements. The *Relación* boasts of thousands of destroyed *wak’as*. Coercion, confiscation, punishment, and the replacement of indigenous ritual landscapes were integral to this campaign. The same operation that preserved Catequil’s fullest narrative also attempted to eradicate its practice.

### Seventeenth century: dispersed survivals

Arriaga and Calancha record a Catequil tradition in Conchucos, while the Huarochirí Manuscript preserves his reputation as a giver of oracular voice. Cajatambo and Huarochirí trials show continued devotion to regional lightning beings, stones, mountains, and ancestral remains despite repeated destruction. Catequil himself, however, is not named in every such trial.

### Colonial-to-modern transformation

Named institutional worship at Namanchugo did not survive continuously in documented form. Broader practices did:

- Sacred treatment of places struck by lightning.
- Keeping “lightning stones.”
- Selection or healing of persons struck or marked by lightning.
- Mountain offerings and fertility rites.
- Christianized pilgrimages to powerful springs and mountains.
- Santiago as a thunder-associated saint.

Topic reports that people near Huamachuco still find buried “lightning stones” and use them to cure *susto*. That is a **[folklore/ethnographic survival]** of the same religious complex, but it is not proof that participants secretly name the stones Catequil.

### Geographic cautions

- **Cusco and the altiplano:** living lightning ritual is well documented, but usually under names such as Illapa, Santiago, or local *apus*. Direct living Catequil worship is not established.
- **Argentine northwest:** ethnography documents *rayo* beliefs, lightning-marked places, and protectors of livestock. Comparisons with Catequil are scholarly; no secure evidence shows a continuous cult carried intact from Huamachuco.
- **Ecuador:** Catequilla toponyms and spring pilgrimages are significant, and some may reflect Inca-sponsored diffusion. Elaborate modern narratives about “catequillado” initiates, aquatic animal release, jaguar-lunar iconography, or a Colombia-to-Peru Catequil religion lack equivalent early documentation.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Catequil’s modern recovery has been primarily scholarly, archaeological, regional-literary, and heritage-oriented rather than occult.

- **Nineteenth-century publication:** the Augustinian report first appeared in the 1865 *Colección de documentos inéditos*. Daniel Brinton’s *The Myths of the New World* popularized Apocatequil internationally as a thunder god whose sling stones became charms, but it generalized freely from colonial sources.
- **Twentieth-century synthesis:** Tello, Means, Rostworowski, Duviols, Zuidema, Silverblatt, MacCormack, Urton, and others placed Catequil within studies of Andean creation, dualism, *wak’as*, gender, oracles, imperial religion, and extirpation.
- **Literature:** Miguel Garnett published the Spanish-language novel *Catequil* in Lima in 1996, a modern literary reimagining rather than transmission of an ancient narrative.
- **Archaeological heritage:** work from 1997 onward transformed Cerro Icchal and Namanchugo into identifiable heritage sites and gave Catequil a stronger public profile in La Libertad.
- **Internet mythology:** contemporary pages frequently portray Catequil with a lightning staff, jaguar body, aquatic domain, prescribed initiation cycle, or a pan-Ecuadorian/Colombian cult. These features should be marked **[modern invention or unverified synthesis]** unless a primary citation is supplied.
- **Neopaganism and occultism:** the dedicated search located scattered modern invocations and encyclopedia/game-style profiles but no historically traceable Catequil-centered occult order, Golden Dawn lineage, established grimoire tradition, or demonstrably sizable reconstructionist community. Absence of documentation is the finding.
- **Popular culture:** Catequil appears in mythology lists, fantasy pantheon compilations, and amateur visualizations. These materially increase name recognition but often collapse Catequil into generic “Inca thunder god” imagery.

No credible evidence was located for a modern extremist appropriation, commercial mass cult, or politically organized Catequil movement.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Catequil originally Huamachuco, Cajamarca, Conchucos, or Ecuadorian?

The strongest source-critical case favors **Huamachuco**:

- The full origin myth is localized there.
- Albornoz calls Catequil a *wak’a* of the Huamachucos.
- The Augustinians locate the shrine at Porcón.
- Namanchugo’s archaeology fits the account.
- Sarmiento’s broader “Cajamarca and Huamachuco” wording reflects Inca and colonial administrative grouping.

Topic argues that Catequil was an old thunder power across Huamachuco and Conchucos, becoming a specialized oracle at Huamachuco during the Late Intermediate Period. Claims of an Ecuadorian origin depend heavily on Catequilla place-names and recent synthesized traditions, not an Ecuadorian document as early or detailed as the Huamachuco *Relación*.

### One image or many embodiments?

The Huamachuco account says Atahualpa shattered the image; Conchucos tradition says priests rescued and moved it intact. Andean *wak’a* ontology permits replication through stones, cloth, offspring, and transported emanations. Thus the contradiction need not be solved by choosing one “real statue,” but this ontological solution is a modern interpretation.

### Who destroyed the sanctuary?

- Atahualpa: Betanzos, Augustinians, Sarmiento.
- Huáscar, called son of Topa Inca: Arriaga.
- Huayna Cápac: Calancha’s correction.

The three earlier sources make Atahualpa the best-supported historical agent. Arriaga and Calancha preserve an important Conchucos relocation tradition but are chronologically inconsistent.

### Was Catequil an “Inca god”?

Only in a qualified sense. The Inca state incorporated and patronized him, but his myth, sacred landscape, and local constituency were Huamachuco. “Andean and Inca pantheon” should not obscure provincial origin and imperial appropriation.

### Etymology

No consensus derivation exists. The Culle context is probable but insufficient to parse the word. Quechua readings of *apu* and possible *-y* are clearer than the root *Catequil*. Confident internet translations are unsupported.

### Archaeology and text

Namanchugo is a major success of text-guided archaeology, but circular reasoning remains a risk. Features can corroborate the reported ritual complex without proving every excavated phase belonged to the named god. The Mama Catequil stone and egg symbolism are strong contextual interpretations, not inscriptions.

### Colonial-source bias

The Augustinians are both destroyers and recorders. Their report:

- Preserves indigenous testimony otherwise lost.
- Translates *wak’as* into “idols” and “demons.”
- Was produced amid confiscation, punishment, and evangelization.
- May systematize fluid local relations into European genealogies.
- Describes survivals partly to demonstrate missionary success.

The correct response is neither to discard the account nor to read it transparently.

### Human sacrifice

General Andean sacrifice is well attested; Catequil-specific routine human sacrifice is not. Sarmiento’s sacrifice before the oracle is unspecified, while the priest’s beheading is political violence against cult personnel, not an offering to Catequil. Popular claims should not be upgraded beyond the source.

### Huarochirí and Cajatambo

The Huarochirí Manuscript names Catequil once in an explicitly oracular role. The idolatry trials offer comparison rather than a Catequil corpus. Their testimonies were frequently compelled, and the surviving records represent prosecution archives, not neutral ethnographies.

### State of the evidence

- **Primary manuscript:** AGI, Patronato 192, ramo 6, no. 2; another copy entered the Muñoz collection.
- **Critical Augustinian edition:** Lucila Castro de Trelles, 1992; revised 2022.
- **Archaeology:** Proyecto Catequil field seasons beginning in 1998, with later seasons in 2001, 2003, and 2005; reports deposited with Peru’s cultural authorities.
- **Major synthesis:** John R. Topic, Theresa Lange Topic, and Alfredo Melly Cava, “Catequil: The Archaeology, Ethnohistory, and Ethnography of a Major Provincial Huaca” (2002); John and Theresa Topic, *En la tierra del oráculo de Catequil* (2020).
- **Cajatambo corpus:** Pierre Duviols and collaborators, *Procesos y visitas de idolatrías: Cajatambo, siglo XVII* (2003).
