# Chasca: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A celestial *huaca* or divinized astral power, identified in colonial Quechua sources with the bright “star” now understood as Venus, especially the morning star. Modern summaries usually call Chasca a goddess, but the earliest sources do not consistently assign a female body or grammatical gender.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Domingo de Santo Tomás’s *Lexicón o vocabulario de la lengua general del Perú* (Valladolid, 1560), which records *chasca* among Quechua names for the morning star. This is secure lexical evidence that the name denoted a prominent celestial object by 1560; it does not by itself establish the later composite portrait of a female deity of beauty, flowers, and maidens. The earliest reasonably explicit evidence for cult is the anonymous *Relación de las costumbres antiguas de los naturales del Pirú*, conventionally associated with Blas Valera and usually dated to the late sixteenth century, followed by Garcilaso de la Vega (1609), Juan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui and Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala (both early seventeenth century).

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early attestation]** Sixteenth-century linguistic, narrative, or administrative evidence reasonably close to the Inca and early colonial periods.
- **[Colonial elaboration]** Seventeenth-century testimony describing or reconstructing pre-Hispanic practice.
- **[Regional variant]** Evidence for a local Andean name or practice that should not automatically be assigned to imperial Cusco.
- **[Learned reconstruction]** A modern scholarly interpretation combining incomplete colonial, linguistic, astronomical, or archaeological evidence.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historical equation between Chasca and another figure, planet, saint, or celestial category.
- **[Folklore]** Post-conquest oral tradition not securely traceable to the imperial cult.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim appearing in recent mythology compendia, spiritual literature, entertainment, or internet circulation without demonstrable support in the colonial corpus.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identification

The documentary core is narrow but firm: *Chasca/Ch’aska*, often expanded to *Chasca Coyllur/Ch’aska Quyllur*, names a conspicuous “star,” normally the morning star and, in Garcilaso’s explanation, Venus moving alternately before and behind the Sun. The name was attached to worship, fasting, elite ritual, and—in Guaman Poma—to a temple entered by Inca princes and princesses.

The popular composite “Chasca, Inca goddess of dawn, dusk, beauty, flowers, and young maidens” is not a single anciently attested profile. It fuses:

1. the documented Venus/morning-star designation;
2. Garcilaso’s “long and curly hair” etymology and “page of the Sun” statement;
3. Guaman Poma’s association with male and female royal youth and selected women;
4. uncertain rain imagery sometimes reassigned from a colonial Quechua poem about a celestial woman and her brother;
5. modern extrapolations about beauty, flowers, fertility, twilight, and feminine patronage.

### Names and spellings

- **Chasca, Chaska, Ch’aska.** Colonial Spanish spellings generally omit the modern apostrophe used to mark an ejective consonant in Southern Quechua orthographies. Santo Tomás’s 1560 lexicon gives *chasca* as one name for the morning star. **[Early attestation]**
- **Chasca Coyllur / Chasca Cuyllor / Chazca Coyllur.** Colonial forms of modern **Ch’aska Quyllur** in three-vowel spelling or **Ch’aska Qoyllur** in five-vowel spelling. *Quyllur/qoyllur* means “star” and could embrace planets, comets, and stellar bodies in colonial usage. **[Early attestation]**
- **Pacari Coyllur / Pacarina Coyllur / Paqarin Quyllur.** “Morning/dawn star,” recorded as an alternative designation in colonial and later lexicographic discussion. It is a descriptive astronomical name, not securely an independent goddess. **[Early attestation/regional terminology]**
- **Aranyak, Huarac/Warak, Guara/Wara.** Regional or lexical names for the dawn star. Domingo de Santo Tomás gives a sequence commonly read as *aranyac o guara o chasca*. Pablo José de Arriaga later reports that the morning star was called *Huarac* in a province where it received offerings during boys’ breeching or initiation. These terms demonstrate a diverse Andean Venus vocabulary; they do not prove that every local morning-star cult worshipped the Cusco figure Chasca under another name. **[Regional variants]**
- **Achachi Ururi.** The Aymara label beside the morning-star figure in Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s cosmographic drawing. *Achachi* evokes an old man or grandfather, while *ururi* denotes the morning star. This complicates the modern presentation of Chasca as uniformly female. **[Colonial regional/linguistic equation]**
- **Choquechinchay/Chuquichinchay and Apachi Ururi.** Labels associated in Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s diagram with the counterpart on the other side of the celestial scheme, often interpreted as the evening star. The precise astronomical and theological relation remains debated. **[Colonial evidence; identification disputed]**
- **Chuqui Ylla/Choque Illa.** A name frequently placed near Chasca in Guaman Poma and elsewhere associated with the thunder or lightning complex. Mariusz Ziółkowski proposed that Chasca represented morning Venus and Chuqui Ylla evening Venus, the latter imagined as the celestial slinger’s golden projectile. That is a stimulating reconstruction, not a direct statement by Guaman Poma. Other editors have tentatively identified Chuqui Ylla with Mars, Antares, or another luminous phenomenon. **[Learned reconstruction, disputed]**
- **Manchu Coyllur.** “Ancient star,” reported in nineteenth-century annotation and lexicographic discussion as another name for Venus; its status in securely pre-Hispanic cult is uncertain. **[Late lexicographic report]**

### Etymology

Garcilaso de la Vega states twice that *Chasca* means “curly” or “of long and curly hair,” explaining the name through Venus’s many rays: “A la estrella Venus llamaban Chasca, que quiere decir de cabellos largos y crespos.” He also uses the diminutive *crespita o crespa*. González Holguín’s 1608 dictionary records forms associating *chhasca* with tousled or disordered hair and gives *Chhasca ccoyllur* for the morning star. The semantic connection between radiance and dishevelled or curly hair is therefore colonial, not merely modern fantasy. [Garcilaso’s text is accessible in the digitized *Royal Commentaries*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bd/First_Part_of_the_Royal_Commentaries_of_the_Yncas%2C_Vol._I-_containing_books_I.%2C_II.%2C_III._and_IV._%28IA_dli.granth.86427%29.pdf); González Holguín’s 1608 work is bibliographically documented by [Glottolog](https://glottolog.org/resource/reference/id/104046).

Two cautions follow:

- *Ch’aska* can itself mean the morning star; *Ch’aska Quyllur* may therefore be an emphatic “Chasca-star,” not necessarily a personal name plus title.
- The “golden curls” formulation often found online elaborates the colonial etymology. Garcilaso speaks of hair and rays, but an ancient canonical description of a gold-haired goddess has not been found.

### Gender and class

Colonial testimony does not produce a consistent grammatical biography. Garcilaso calls Venus *paje del Sol*, “page of the Sun,” grammatically masculine in Spanish. Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s Aymara *Achachi Ururi* likewise suggests a male or ancestral designation. Guaman Poma depicts and lists the star among *dioses de ellos de los menores*, “their gods of the younger ones,” without supplying a narrative female persona. Modern writers commonly feminize Chasca because of hair imagery, association with female *acllas*, and comparison with the Roman Venus. The safest classification is therefore:

> **Celestial huaca/personified Venus; gender variable or underdetermined in the colonial record; predominantly represented as a goddess in modern reception.**

There is no pre-Hispanic text equating Chasca with Roman Venus the goddess. The equation is astronomical—Chasca’s “star” is the planet Europeans call Venus—and only subsequently mythographic. **[Syncretistic identification]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The evidentiary problem

No securely deciphered pre-Hispanic Inca inscription names Chasca. The Incas did not leave alphabetic religious texts, and no quipu has been decoded as a Chasca record. All named testimony passes through colonial writing, usually Spanish or missionary-normalized Quechua. Thus “Inca-period belief” is reconstructed from sixteenth- and seventeenth-century witnesses whose purposes included evangelization, imperial history, native petition, antiquarian inquiry, and suppression of “idolatry.”

### Principal sources

#### Domingo de Santo Tomás, *Lexicón* (1560)

Language: Spanish and an early colonial form of the Quechua “general language.” Physical form: printed book, Valladolid.

It records *chasca* as a name of the morning star, alongside other names. This is the earliest secure datable occurrence located in the relevant corpus. It establishes astronomical nomenclature by 1560 but says nothing explicit about sex, genealogy, flowers, iconography, or a dedicated temple. The work and its 1560 printing are documented in the [Open Library catalogue](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL16326533W/Lexicon_o_Vocabulario_de_la_lengua_general_del_Peru) and by Peru’s [Ministry of Culture](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/cultura/noticias/497787-estudio-sobre-el-mas-antiguo-diccionario-quechua-en-la-biblioteca-virtual-del-ministerio-de-cultura). **[Early attestation]**

#### Anonymous/“Blas Valera,” *Relación de las costumbres antiguas de los naturales del Pirú*

The text was first printed by Marcos Jiménez de la Espada in 1879 from a colonial manuscript. It has often been attributed to the mestizo Jesuit Blas Valera and dated about 1586, but authorship and textual formation are disputed. Modern critical work treats “Anonymous Jesuit” more cautiously.

The relevant passage says that “the star” meant not a night star but the morning star called Chasca. Worshippers fasted from the preceding day until the star disappeared in the Sun’s presence and then ate their ordinary food. The author disputes Polo de Ondegardo’s report about eating dogs and substitutes deer, birds, and lambs. This is important ritual testimony but also an apologetic correction of another colonial authority. The text is available in the digitized [*Tres relaciones de antigüedades peruanas*](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/research/tres-relaciones-de-antiguedades-peruanas--recopiladas-por-marcos-jimenez-de-la-espada/4aadf812-5347-4184-bbd9-481c891a1cfa.pdf). **[Early/colonial attestation; authorship disputed]**

#### Diego González Holguín, *Vocabulario* (1608)

Language: Spanish and Cusco Quechua; printed in Lima.

Entries distinguish or associate *Chhasca ccoyllur*, *chazca coyllur*, morning-star terms, and hair imagery. The dictionary is crucial for historical semantics but does not by itself describe an anthropomorphic goddess. **[Early lexical attestation]**

#### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales*, Part I (Lisbon, 1609)

Garcilaso gives the most influential concise characterization:

- the Peruvians called Venus *Chasca* because of its many rays;
- they understood its varying movement, seeing it sometimes before and sometimes after the Sun;
- they honored it because they called it the Sun’s *paje*, the attendant closest to him;
- *Chasca* meant long, curly hair.

This is the primary basis for “page of the Sun.” Garcilaso wrote in Spain decades after leaving Peru and constructed a dignified account of Inca religion partly from memory, family testimony, earlier chronicles, and Renaissance categories. His astronomical claim is valuable but should not automatically be converted into a fully narrated myth. **[Colonial canonical elaboration]**

#### Juan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui Salcamaygua, *Relación de antigüedades deste reyno del Pirú* (c. 1613)

Language: Spanish with Quechua and Aymara terminology; manuscript with a famous cosmological diagram purporting to reproduce or interpret the arrangement of the Coricancha.

A star beneath the solar side bears *Chasca Coyllur* and *Achachi Ururi*, while another celestial figure is labelled *Choquechinchay/Apachi Ururi*. The drawing is primary evidence for an early-colonial learned ordering of celestial powers. It is not a surviving pre-Hispanic temple mural, nor can every element be mechanically treated as a photograph of the Coricancha. Its pairing of a Quechua name with an Aymara “old-man/morning-star” term undercuts the idea of one universally female Venus goddess. **[Colonial indigenous systematization]**

#### Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (c. 1615–1616)

Physical support: Guaman Poma’s autograph illustrated manuscript, Copenhagen, Royal Danish Library, GKS 2232 4°. The [Guaman Poma Website](https://poma.kb.dk/) supplies the manuscript facsimile and annotated transcription.

Relevant passages include:

- an ordinance forbidding people to blaspheme the Sun, Moon, stars, and “the morning star Chasca Cuyllor”;
- a drawing and text describing “another temple of the morning star Chasca Cuyllor, Chuqui Ylla, [and] *uaca billcacona*,” entered by *auquicona* and *ñustacona*—royal sons and daughters or princes and princesses—to sacrifice;
- a list for *Capac Raymi* that places the Inca, his wife, the Moon festival, their children Chuqui Ylla and Chasca Cuyllor, Huanacauri, Tambotoco, and Titicaca together;
- a passage on *guayrur aclla*, selected women serving the Sun, Moon, and the stars Chasca Cuyllor and Chuqui Ylla.

Guaman Poma therefore supplies the strongest evidence for an institutionally differentiated Chasca cult involving royal youth and certain *acllas*. He does not depict the elaborate long-haired “flower goddess” now common online. Modern scholarship’s reading of the temple passage and elite youth is summarized in [Ziółkowski’s study](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/633/618) and in [Zuidema’s analysis of Cusco social and calendrical structure](https://centroderecursos.cultura.pe/sites/default/files/rb/pdf/LA%20IDENTIDAD%20DE%20LAS%20DIEZ%20PANACAS%20%20EN%20EL%20CUZCO%20INCAICO.pdf). **[Colonial indigenous attestation]**

#### Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621)

Arriaga reports that, when boys received *huaras* at approximately eight or ten years of age, people sacrificed to the morning star, locally called *Huarac*. He suggests that the term might relate to *huaras*. This confirms a seventeenth-century regional relationship between the morning star and male initiation, but Arriaga does not say that this local *Huarac* was a female flower goddess or explicitly call it Chasca. The comparison with Guaman Poma underlies modern reconstructions of Venus as patron of Inca youth. **[Regional colonial evidence]**

#### Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (completed 1653)

Cobo’s Book XIII synthesizes extensive earlier material on Inca religion, including sources now lost. It belongs to the later colonial synthesis rather than the eyewitness conquest generation. Cobo is valuable for the astral hierarchy and Cusco cult but must be checked against earlier witnesses because he systematizes heterogeneous traditions. A digitized historical edition is catalogued by [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL6910924M/Historia_del_Nuevo_mundo). **[Colonial synthesis]**

### Sources named in the brief that do not furnish a clear Chasca dossier

- **Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Molina, Albornoz:** These authors are indispensable for Inca religion generally, but no equally explicit, unambiguous Chasca narrative was located in their commonly cited passages. Molina’s festival descriptions are sometimes made to support detailed Chasca roles by modern summaries, but a direct passage naming Chasca must be produced before treating that role as secure.
- **Huarochirí Manuscript (c. 1598–1608):** This Quechua manuscript contains rich narratives of Pariacaca, Cuniraya, Cavillaca, Chaupiñamca, Huallallo Carhuincho, and other local powers, but no clear narrative of the Cusco Venus-being Chasca has been established. Astral figures or words for stars should not be silently identified with her.
- **Huarochirí and Cajatambo trials:** These provide exceptional evidence for local huacas, ancestors, sacrifices, ritual specialists, and colonial coercion. The published Cajatambo corpus describes forced testimony and preserves recollections of *capacochas*, but it does not yield a clearly documented Chasca cult under that name. See the [PUCP/IFEA edition description](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/1f4ac161-c30e-4416-be48-dd1bf26b5765/full). **Absence of a name in these corpora is significant:** the imperial Chasca label did not displace the locally specific celestial powers recorded by extirpators.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Securely documented domains

- **Morning star/Venus:** Secure from Santo Tomás, González Holguín, the Anonymous Jesuit, Garcilaso, Guaman Poma, and later synthesis.
- **Proximity and attendance to the Sun:** Garcilaso explicitly calls Chasca the Sun’s *paje*, explaining that Venus appears before or behind the Sun. **[Colonial attestation]**
- **Elite youth:** Guaman Poma’s *auquicona* and *ñustacona* sacrificed in the associated temple; Arriaga’s regional *Huarac* received offerings at boys’ initiation. The broader proposition that Chasca was “patron of young people” is a defensible reconstruction, not a surviving divine proclamation. **[Colonial evidence plus learned reconstruction]**
- **Selected women:** Guaman Poma says *guayrur aclla* served the Sun, Moon, Chasca Cuyllor, and Chuqui Ylla. This supports a relation with a particular institutional class of selected women. It does not establish patronage of all maidens, virginity as Chasca’s independent domain, or a generalized “goddess of women.” **[Colonial attestation]**
- **Dawn:** Implicit in “morning star.” Treating Chasca as an autonomous personification of Dawn is interpretive. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Evening/twilight:** Garcilaso knew Venus appeared on either side of the Sun; whether the evening manifestation bore the same cult name is disputed because Santa Cruz Pachacuti and Guaman Poma may distinguish or pair it with another figure. **[Disputed]**

### Claims requiring rejection or qualification

- **Goddess of flowers / creator of flowers:** No early source located here states that Chasca created flowers or governed them. The formula is widespread in modern encyclopedia and mythology pages but lacks an identified colonial passage. **[Modern invention or unsupported modern elaboration]**
- **Goddess of beauty:** The inference probably arose from Venus’s brilliance, the hair etymology, and comparison with Roman Venus. No colonial text examined assigns Chasca a domain called beauty. **[Modern invention]**
- **Protector of all virgins or young women:** Guaman Poma supports service by particular *acllas* and sacrifice by *ñustas*, not this universal title. **[Modern overgeneralization]**
- **Goddess of rain who shakes water from her hair:** No secure Chasca passage supports this. It appears to conflate Chasca’s hair with the Quechua poem preserved by Garcilaso in which a celestial woman carries a water vessel and her brother breaks it, producing thunder and rain. The poem is generally connected with rain/thunder theology and is not explicitly titled a Chasca hymn. **[Misattribution/modern synthesis]**
- **Rainbow, fixed sacred colors, sacred number, sacred flower, sacred animal:** Not established in the primary corpus.
- **A canonical husband, parents, children, or independent creation myth:** Not established.

### Iconography

There is no securely identified pre-Hispanic statue, textile figure, ceramic image, or metal effigy bearing Chasca’s name. A star or disk in Inca art cannot be assigned to Chasca without contextual evidence.

The most important named representation is Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s early-seventeenth-century cosmographic drawing, which labels *Chasca Coyllur/Achachi Ururi*. It is schematic rather than a portrait. Guaman Poma’s “temple of the morning star” drawing organizes worshippers and celestial symbols hierarchically; it does not securely show a distinct long-haired goddess. Claims that Guaman Poma portrayed Chasca as a young woman with flowing hair and attendant stars exceed what his labels and figures demonstrate.

Recognizable emblems are consequently limited to:

- the star sign in early-colonial diagrams;
- Venus as visible morning or evening luminary;
- metaphorical long or curly hair, representing rays, from Garcilaso’s etymology.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

No securely independent pre-Hispanic genealogy survives. Guaman Poma’s festival passage places “the Inca and his wife,” the Moon festival, and “their children, Chuqui Ylla, Chasca Cuyllor” in a compressed and grammatically difficult list. Some scholars read this as making Chasca and Chuqui Ylla children of Sun and Moon. Others understand it as a political-cosmic analogy: the Inca and Coya worship Sun and Moon, while their sons and daughters worship the lesser celestial powers.

Accordingly:

- **Sun as father of Chasca:** possible reading of Guaman Poma, not a secure narrative genealogy.
- **Moon as mother:** same qualification.
- **Chuqui Ylla as sibling:** possible within that reading; elsewhere Chuqui Ylla is a thunder name or separate astral figure.
- **Consort or offspring:** none securely attested.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 The “page of the Sun”

This is less a narrative than a compact etiological explanation. Garcilaso says Venus was called Chasca for its long, curly hair and honored as the Sun’s page because it stayed closest to him, now preceding and now following. The observable alternation of morning and evening Venus supplies the physical basis.

- **Earliest explicit source:** Garcilaso, 1609.
- **Early core:** name, radiance, Venus, proximity to the Sun.
- **Later development:** modern accounts convert the page into a beautiful celestial maiden, daughter, companion, or handmaiden of Inti.
- **Historical function:** it integrates an anomalously bright moving object into a solar court corresponding to Inca hierarchy.
- **Limitation:** Garcilaso records no dialogue, journey, love story, conflict, or birth.

### 5.2 Chasca and Chuqui Ylla, the two manifestations of Venus

Guaman Poma repeatedly places the two names together. Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s diagram presents distinct morning- and evening-side figures. Ziółkowski argued that Chasca was morning Venus and Chuqui Ylla evening Venus, the latter also conceptualized through the thunder god’s sling projectile. This explains why two apparently different powers protect royal youth and why the names sometimes seem interchangeable.

It remains a theory. Guaman Poma does not write, “Chasca and Chuqui Ylla are the two phases of Venus.” *Chuqui Ylla* is elsewhere a thunder title, and editors have proposed Mars or another star. Gary Urton has warned more generally that European identifications of named Andean lights with modern astronomical objects are often tentative.

- **Source base:** Guaman Poma c. 1615; Santa Cruz Pachacuti c. 1613; comparative colonial dictionaries.
- **Tier:** **[Modern scholarly reconstruction from colonial evidence]**
- **Status:** plausible, influential, unproved.

### 5.3 Venus and the initiation of youth

Guaman Poma says princes and princesses entered the temple associated with Chasca and Chuqui Ylla to sacrifice. Arriaga says boys receiving *huaras* sacrificed to the morning star called *Huarac*. Modern scholarship joins these observations into a model in which Venus guarded the preparatory phase of elite Inca initiation.

- **Source base:** Guaman Poma and Arriaga.
- **Variant:** Cusco royal youth versus a provincial boys’ rite.
- **Function:** protection and celestial legitimation during social transition.
- **Tier:** the separate practices are **[colonially attested]**; their unification as one imperial Chasca initiation system is **[learned reconstruction]**.

### 5.4 The celestial woman with a water jar

A famous Quechua poem transmitted by Garcilaso concerns a celestial maiden or woman whose vessel is broken by her brother, producing rain, hail, thunder, or lightning. Modern webpages sometimes identify her as Chasca because of flowing-water and hair imagery. The poem itself does not securely name Chasca; other readings associate its female figure with the Moon, a rain power, or a vessel called *paqcha*.

It must therefore not be presented as “the myth of Chasca and her brother Illapa.” **[Colonial poem; Chasca identification unestablished]**

### 5.5 Modern narratives

Recent mythology websites add that Chasca created flowers, pours dew, shakes rain from her curls, protects romantic love, or mediates between Sun and Moon. These may be attractive literary developments, but without a cited colonial passage they are **[modern inventions]**.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cusco temple

Guaman Poma’s clearest notice reads, in substance: “another temple of the morning star Chasca Cuyllor, Chuqui Ylla, [and] local sacred beings; the princes and princesses entered to sacrifice, for these were gods of the younger ones.” The syntax does not make clear whether this was one temple housing several powers or a category of temples. No excavated structure has been securely identified by inscription as “the Temple of Chasca.”

The temple belongs to Guaman Poma’s hierarchical map of worship:

- Inca → Sun and Coricancha;
- Coya and female ritual specialists → Moon;
- royal sons and daughters → Venus, Chuqui Ylla, and lesser huacas;
- common people → peripheral *apachetas*.

Zuidema interprets this as a social and calendrical ordering of Cusco worship rather than a simple list of gods. **[Colonial attestation; spatial reconstruction debated]**

### Acllas

Guaman Poma’s *guayrur aclla* were selected women around twenty years old who served the Sun, Moon, Chasca Cuyllor, and Chuqui Ylla. They were part of an institution controlled by the Inca state and performed textile, food, drink, ritual, and political functions. Chasca was therefore one recipient within a wider complex, not necessarily their exclusive patroness.

The statement “Chasca was goddess of virgins” loses these institutional specifics. A more accurate formulation is: **some state-selected women were assigned to service that included Chasca and Chuqui Ylla.**

### Offerings and fasting

- **Fasting:** The Anonymous Jesuit reports a fast beginning the preceding day and ending when Chasca disappeared in daylight.
- **Food:** That author disputes Polo’s alleged dog meat and names deer, birds, and lambs. Because the passage is polemical, both the denial and alternatives require caution.
- **Sacrifice by royal youth:** Guaman Poma explicitly uses sacrificial language but does not specify the offering in the Chasca-temple passage.
- **Regional boys’ offerings:** Arriaga documents sacrifice to *Huarac* during the giving of breechcloths.

### Human sacrifice

No primary text examined ties a human sacrifice specifically to Chasca by name. Children and adolescents were sacrificed in the Inca *capacocha* system to major state and local powers, mountains, and imperial occasions, but it would be methodologically wrong to assign a *capacocha* victim to Chasca merely because Venus participated in calendrical thought.

Claims of “virgin sacrifices to Chasca” are unsupported. Association with *acllas* is not evidence that those women were sacrificed to her.

### Priesthood and festival

No distinctive priestly title for Chasca is securely recorded. Guaman Poma names worshipping social groups rather than a Chasca priesthood.

No independent annual “Festival of Chasca” can be securely reconstructed. Her placement around *Capac Raymi* and initiation rites associates the star with the ceremonial calendar, but popular claims about a dedicated dawn or flower festival lack primary documentation.

### Archaeology and astronomical alignments

Archaeology securely establishes the importance of solar observation, horizon markers, huacas, ceques, and sacred landscape around Cusco. Brian Bauer, David Dearborn, Zuidema, and others have debated individual alignments and celestial identifications. No accepted archaeological find carries Chasca’s name. An orientation toward a Venus extreme is not by itself evidence of a Chasca temple, since alignments may be ambiguous and Venus’s horizon positions shift through complex cycles.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1532: probable Inca and pre-Inca background

Venus was conspicuous across the Andes, and regional societies possessed multiple morning-star names. A pre-Inca history is highly probable, but continuity from any particular archaeological image to Chasca cannot be demonstrated. Claims that a named Chasca cult existed at Tiwanaku, Wari, Chavín, or Nazca are **[unsupported modern projection]** unless an independent chain of evidence is supplied.

### 1530s–1560: conquest and first lexical preservation

Spanish invasion dismantled the imperial cult’s political support. Sanctuaries were looted, reassigned, or Christianized; state-controlled ritual personnel were dispersed or incorporated into colonial households and communities. Santo Tomás’s 1560 dictionary preserves *chasca* as a living or recently remembered dawn-star term. The absence of a narrative suggests that missionaries first captured vocabulary more readily than theological structure.

### Late sixteenth century: ritual description

The Anonymous Jesuit records fasting associated with Chasca, while polemicizing against reports of dog consumption. Garcilaso later drew on remembered and written testimony. This period supplies the closest explicit reports of ritual but already reflects Christian categories—“gods,” “idols,” fasting, sacrifice—and disputes among colonial authors.

### c. 1608–1621: indigenous diagrams and extirpation

González Holguín fixes Cusco Quechua terminology in print. Santa Cruz Pachacuti and Guaman Poma embed Chasca in indigenous-Christian presentations of Andean cosmic and political order. Their works were not neutral ethnographies: both addressed colonial audiences and organized ancestral religion within Christian universal history.

At the same time, ecclesiastical extirpation campaigns sought out huacas, ritual objects, priests, ancestor bundles, sacrifices, and ceremonies. Arriaga’s 1621 manual documents a regional morning-star rite but not a surviving institutional Chasca priesthood. The campaigns used coercive interrogation, public punishment, destruction and confiscation of sacred objects, forced catechesis, and surveillance. Chasca’s disappearance as a clearly named public cult must be situated within this larger destruction, though no document records a single decisive demolition of “Chasca’s temple.”

### Mid-seventeenth century: antiquarian synthesis

Cobo collected earlier reports into a systematic Inca religion. By this point, the imperial institutions described no longer functioned publicly. His categories helped define the later scholarly “Inca pantheon,” sometimes making a fluid field of huacas look more like a classical pantheon of fixed divine persons.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: lexical survival and antiquarian recovery

Words derived from *ch’aska* continued in Quechua and regional Spanish as names or descriptions of the morning star and dishevelled hair. Nineteenth-century editors printed previously inaccessible chronicles, especially Jiménez de la Espada’s 1879 *Tres relaciones*. European and Peruvian antiquarians increasingly mapped Andean beings onto Greco-Roman categories and translated celestial terms as named gods or goddesses.

### Twentieth century: structural and ethnohistorical reconstruction

Scholarship by Zuidema, Duviols, Rostworowski, Urton, Silverblatt, MacCormack, Bauer, Salomon, Ziółkowski, and others shifted attention toward:

- ritual calendars and Cusco social organization;
- colonial mediation and the politics of the chronicles;
- regional huacas rather than a uniform empire-wide pantheon;
- gender and the political economy of the *acllas*;
- astronomy embedded in landscape, kinship, and state ceremony;
- the evidentiary difference between imperial Cusco and local Andean religion.

Ziółkowski’s morning/evening-Venus interpretation of Chasca and Chuqui Ylla is among the most developed Chasca-specific reconstructions, but it has not eliminated alternative identifications.

### Twenty-first century: heritage, spirituality, and mass media

*Chaska/Chasca* remains a Quechua personal name and a common cultural label. Contemporary Andean ritual life in Cusco, the altiplano, Bolivia, and northwestern Argentina continues to address stars, dawn, mountains, Pachamama, saints, and the Sun within locally specific, often Christian-Andean forms. This is genuine living religion, but it is not evidence for an uninterrupted, separately organized Chasca cult.

No reliable census or ethnography located here documents a substantial contemporary community centered specifically on worship of Chasca under a continuous hereditary priesthood. Modern ceremonies advertised as “Chasca worship” therefore require individual investigation: they may be reconstructionist, tourist, artistic, familial, or New Age.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Esotericism and neopaganism

Chasca does not occupy a substantial documented place in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, classical Theosophy, or the major European grimoire traditions. Claims that she belongs to a timeless global “Venus goddess” mystery tradition are comparativist constructions, not a transmission history.

Contemporary goddess spirituality often supplies her with:

- pink or gold colors;
- roses or unnamed sacred flowers;
- romantic love;
- feminine beauty;
- fertility;
- emotional balance;
- dawn meditation;
- a “divine feminine” archetype.

One modern goddess site openly says it “speculates” that Chasca prefers vivid pink—useful evidence of how invention occurs, not of Inca belief. **[Modern invention]**

Reconstructionist practitioners may legitimately create new devotional forms, but historically such practices should be described as contemporary reconstruction unless they document a community-specific chain of transmission.

### Literature and popular mythology

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century mythology dictionaries transformed sparse colonial statements into a character biography. “Page of the Sun” became “beautiful maiden attendant,” service by *acllas* became “patroness of virgins,” and curly rays became golden hair. The title “goddess of flowers” appears repeatedly without a traceable colonial source.

### Digital entertainment

In 2024 HoYoverse introduced **Chasca**, a playable character in *Genshin Impact*. The official trailer, “Chasca: Feather-Fletched Peace,” was published on 18 November 2024. The character is a fantasy mediator and warrior associated with Natlan, flight, feathers, and firearms; she is not a portrayal of the documented Inca astral huaca. The borrowed name has nevertheless made “Chasca” globally searchable and now competes with the Andean figure in popular awareness. See the [official character trailer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dRbFN8hrCLs). **[Modern commercial appropriation/name borrowing]**

### Political appropriation

Modern Inca revivalism and Andean nationalist imagery frequently use solar and stellar vocabulary, but no well-documented extremist or state movement centered specifically on Chasca was located. It would be misleading to attach general neo-Inca nationalism to her without direct evidence.

### Confusion with Qoyllur Rit’i

The Cusco pilgrimage of the Señor de Qoyllur Rit’i is a major living Christian-Andean complex whose name includes *qoyllur*, “star.” Similar vocabulary and celestial themes do not make the Lord of Qoyllur Rit’i a survival of Chasca worship. Any proposed continuity requires historical demonstration rather than word association. **[Common modern conflation]**

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Chasca a goddess?

The popular answer is yes; the primary evidence is less definite. Garcilaso’s masculine *paje*, Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s *Achachi* label, and the lack of a female narrative leave room for a male, female, dual, or non-anthropomorphic astral power. Calling Chasca a “goddess” is acceptable shorthand only if its modern conventional status is acknowledged.

### One Venus or two beings?

Garcilaso presents one named Venus whose apparent position changes. Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s diagram suggests paired manifestations; Guaman Poma repeatedly joins Chasca and Chuqui Ylla. Scholarship divides among:

1. one Chasca encompassing morning and evening Venus;
2. Chasca as morning Venus and another being as evening Venus;
3. Chasca as Venus and Chuqui Ylla as Thunder, Mars, Antares, or another astral power;
4. a relational system in which identities shift by ritual and calendrical context.

No option has an unambiguous pre-Hispanic statement behind it.

### What does *ch’aska* mean?

The colonial sources support both an astronomical meaning, “morning star,” and a hair-related semantic field, “curly/tousled/dishevelled.” Whether the celestial name originated as a metaphor from rays or whether Garcilaso supplied an explanatory folk etymology remains debatable.

### Patron of maidens or of elite youth?

Guaman Poma’s evidence includes male and female royal youth and a particular class of *acllas*. “Patroness of young women” selects one part and omits the princes. Ziółkowski instead emphasizes initiation of Inca youth. Both should be reported, but only the underlying passages are primary evidence.

### Chasca and flowers

This is the clearest case where popular repetition has outrun documentation. No colonial source identified in this research calls Chasca the maker, mother, or goddess of flowers. The claim should remain labeled **[modern, source unestablished]** until a precise manuscript, folio, and wording are produced.

### Chasca and rain

The rain-bearing woman in the Quechua poem cannot be confidently named Chasca. Linking her to Chasca through hair and celestial location is a modern harmonization. The same applies to making Illapa her brother.

### Quality and bias of the evidence

- Missionary dictionaries preserve vocabulary but reshape pronunciation and semantic categories.
- Garcilaso’s work is learned, retrospective, apologetic, and partly memorial.
- Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s diagram is an early-colonial indigenous-Christian cosmography, not a photograph.
- Guaman Poma is exceptionally valuable because the manuscript is autograph and illustrated, but his theology, social criticism, and Christian providential history shape every page.
- Extirpation records contain coerced testimony and institutional incentives to discover “idolatry.”
- Cobo compiles earlier sources but writes after the destruction of the imperial cult.

### Discredited or unsupported propositions still circulating

- Chasca was securely the Inca equivalent of Aphrodite.
- She was universally female throughout the Andes.
- She created flowers.
- She was sister of Illapa and poured rain from a jar.
- Her sacred color was pink or gold.
- She presided over a documented independent annual flower festival.
- The Qoyllur Rit’i pilgrimage is simply her Christianized cult.
- Archaeologists have identified her statue or temple.
- *Acllas* were sacrificed to her.
- Modern devotional descriptions are verbatim survivals of ancient priestly teaching.

None is established by the cited primary record.

| 1980 | John Murra and Rolena Adorno, with Quechua work by Jorge Urioste, publish a critical Guaman Poma edition based on direct study of the manuscript. |

## 11. Conclusions

The recoverable historical Chasca is considerably more interesting—and less tidy—than the modern encyclopedia goddess. The secure nucleus consists of a Quechua name for the morning star, its identification with Venus, an etymological association with curly or dishevelled radiant hair, its attendance on the Sun, fasting, and an elite cult involving royal sons, daughters, and selected women. Guaman Poma supplies the strongest institutional evidence; Garcilaso supplies the enduring poetic image.

Nearly everything resembling a conventional divine biography remains absent: no birth narrative, no securely attested spouse, no children, no independent epic, no identified statue, no excavated temple inscription, and no documented myth in which Chasca speaks or acts with another deity. “Goddess of flowers,” “goddess of beauty,” universal patroness of maidens, and rain shaken from her hair belong to modern synthesis unless better primary citations emerge.

The variations are not defects to be harmonized. They reveal a historical transformation from diverse Andean morning-star terms and rites, through early-colonial indigenous and missionary classification, into an increasingly personified “Inca goddess” modeled partly on European expectations of a pantheon.
