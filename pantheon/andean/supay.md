# Supay: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest secure evidence, *supay* is not demonstrably the proper name of one god. It denotes a spirit, apparition, household goblin, or morally variable supernatural being; in colonial Christian usage it becomes “demon/devil,” singular or collective. In later Andean mining and festival traditions, Supay may be individualized as an underground master, identified with the Devil, the mine-owner called **El Tío**, or represented by male and female devils.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Fray Domingo de Santo Tomás, *Lexicón o vocabulario de la lengua general del Perú*, printed at Valladolid in **1560**, s.v. *çupay*: “ángel, bueno o malo,” “demonio,” and “trasgo de casa.” This is post-conquest missionary lexicography, not pre-Hispanic material evidence. No securely identified pre-conquest inscription, image, temple, priesthood, or myth names a singular ruler called Supay.  
> **Central finding:** The familiar formula “Supay, Inca god of death and lord of Ukhu Pacha” is not securely attested in the sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century corpus. It conflates an early, semantically broad spirit category; missionary translation of “devil”; Andean relations with the dead, caves, mountains, and subterranean wealth; later mine spirits; the Bolivian Diablada; and modern mythographic systematization.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century texts, manuscripts, or objects.
- **[CE] Colonial Christian elaboration:** missionary translation, demonology, catechesis, or extirpation discourse.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally documented tradition that should not be generalized to all Quechua, Aymara, Inca, or Andean communities.
- **[SYN] Syncretistic identification:** a historically produced equation between previously distinct beings or categories.
- **[FOL] Folklore/ethnography:** orally transmitted or observed practice documented after the colonial period.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation rather than a statement directly present in a primary source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular synthesis:** a claim repeatedly presented as ancient although surviving early evidence does not establish it.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### 1.1 What *supay* originally designated

**[EA]** Santo Tomás’s 1560 *Lexicón* supplies the earliest secure lexical evidence. Its unusually broad definitions—“good or bad angel,” “demon,” and “household goblin”—show that *çupay* could still be used for more than an absolutely evil being. The entry is nevertheless already mediated by Christian categories: “angel” and “demon” are the Dominican compiler’s Spanish equivalents, not transparent Indigenous classifications. The book survives in print; a modern facsimile is catalogued by [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Lexicon_o_Vocabulario_de_la_lengua_gener.html?id=kJxkAAAAMAAJ).

**[CE]** The Third Lima Council’s Quechua catechetical program, culminating in the 1584–1585 catechisms and the anonymous 1586 vocabulary, narrowed the term. A catechetical formulation glossed “those bad angels whom we call devils” with *mana alli angelcunactam çupay ninchic*. The modifier *mana alli*, “not good,” was initially required to specify evil, but missionary usage increasingly allowed unmodified *supay* to mean the Christian Devil or demons.

**[CE]** Diego González Holguín’s 1608 Cuzco Quechua *Vocabulario* records the completed narrowing: “*Çupay. El demonio*”; *çupay runa*, a person possessed or wicked “like a demon”; and *ucu pacha çupaypa huacin*, “hell: the inner/lower world, the demon’s house.” The original printing and bibliographic details are available through [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL15367279W/Vocabulario_dela_lengua_general_de_todo_el_Peru_llamada_lengua_Qquichua_o_del_Inca); a searchable facsimile is also available in a [public-domain PDF](https://indigenasdelperu.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/1608-vocabvlarioqqichuadeholguin.pdf).

The contrast between 1560 and 1608 is the strongest documentary evidence for semantic Christianization:

| Date | Source | Form and meaning | Evidentiary significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1560 | Santo Tomás, *Lexicón* | *çupay*: good or bad angel; demon; household goblin | **[EA/CE]** Broad and morally variable, although already translated through Christian terms |
| 1584–1585 | Lima catechisms | bad angels called *supay* | **[CE]** Deliberate theological standardization |
| 1586 | Anonymous Quechua vocabulary | devil/demon usage | **[CE]** Institutional consolidation |
| ca. 1608 | Huarochirí Manuscript | *supay* normally functions as “demon” in Christian framing | **[CE]** Indigenous-language text already affected by missionary semantics |
| 1608 | González Holguín | *çupay*: “the demon” | **[CE]** The positive possibility has disappeared from the headword |

Gérald Taylor consequently observes that by the Huarochirí Manuscript’s time *supay* had substantially completed its conversion into “demon.” His account of the manuscript’s unstable colonial religious vocabulary appears in his study of [Huarochirí as literary and ethnohistorical evidence](https://repositorio.minedu.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/20.500.12799/8251/Historia%20de%20las%20literaturas%20en%20el%20Per%C3%BA.%20Volumen%201%2C%20Literaturas%20orales%20y%20primeros%20textos%20coloniales.pdf).

### 1.2 Spellings and grammatical range

**[EA]** Colonial spellings include *çupay, cupay, zupay,* and *supay*. Modern normalized Quechua commonly writes **supay**. The alternation reflects colonial orthography rather than securely distinct beings.

**[EA/FOL]** The plural is Quechua **supaykuna**, “supays/demons/spirits.” This grammatical fact matters: much early and later usage concerns a class of beings, not a unique divine individual. Possessive and compound formations include:

- *supaypa* — “of the supay/devil.”
- *supay wasi* or colonial *çupaypa huacin* — “house of the supay/devil.”
- *supay runa* — demon-person, possessed person, or person wicked like a demon.
- *alli supay* — “good spirit,” reported in discussions of the older morally variable usage.
- *mana alli supay* or *saqra supay* — bad or dangerous spirit/demon.
- *china supay* — female devil; in modern festival usage, a female or feminized Diablada character.
- *supaypa wawan* — “child of the Supay/devil,” a context-dependent expression that may be abuse, self-description, or ritual idiom.

### 1.3 Etymology

**[MSR—disputed]** No etymology is universally accepted. Suggested connections include Quechua forms referring to shadow, soul, disappearance, death, or becoming impoverished; dialect forms such as *hupay* “soul/spirit” and *hupani* “shadow”; and *supa* “shadow.” The semantic field makes an ancestral or soul-like meaning plausible, but resemblance is not proof of a single Proto-Quechua derivation.

**[MSR]** Taylor’s influential reconstruction treats the pre-Christian referent as something like the soul-shadow or spirit of the dead, capable of benefit or harm depending on its relation to the living. Regina Harrison and Irene Silverblatt similarly emphasize moral ambiguity rather than an indigenous counterpart to Satan. A modern source-critical treatment, “Supay and its Narrators,” stresses this [ambiguous sacredness](https://spanish-portuguese.northwestern.edu/documents/quispe-agnoli.pdf).

**[MI]** “Supay literally means shadow and therefore was the single Inca god of death” exceeds the linguistic evidence. Even if “shadow/soul” belongs to the word’s history, it does not establish a unique deity, a royal office in an underworld, or an empire-wide cult.

### 1.4 Titles and identifications

No securely early cult title equivalent to “Lord of Ukhu Pacha” has been found. The following are historical identifications, not timeless synonyms:

- **The Christian Devil/Satan — [CE/SYN], later sixteenth century onward.** Missionaries selected *supay* to translate “devil/demon,” suppressing or marginalizing its earlier ambiguity.
- **A demon or demons — [CE], 1580s onward.** Both singular and plural.
- **A soul or spirit of the dead — [MSR with dialectal support].** Plausible reconstruction from lexicography, mortuary associations, and later Quechua forms; not an intact early creed.
- **Dweller or owner of the lower interior — [CE/SYN/FOL].** González Holguín’s “*ucu pacha çupaypa huacin*” makes the interior world the demon’s house; this is not identical to calling Supay the indigenous king of a tripartite afterlife.
- **El Tío/“Uncle,” master of Bolivian mines — [RV/FOL/SYN], firmly documented in modern ethnography.** Miners may call him *Tío, diablo, maestro,* or *supay*, but the terms are not everywhere perfectly interchangeable.
- **Tiw, an Uru being associated with Oruro — [RV/SYN].** UNESCO’s heritage narrative identifies the *llama llama*/Diablada with worship of Uru Tiw, but this does not demonstrate that pre-Hispanic Tiw was simply Supay.
- **Saqra — [RV/SYN].** Sometimes paired with or substituted for Supay, but González Holguín distinguished *saqra* (“rough, vile, dirty, badly made”) from *supay* (“demon”). They should not automatically be collapsed.
- **Anchanchu — [RV/SYN].** An Aymara dangerous place-being sometimes compared to the Devil or mine spirits; not securely an alternate ancient name for Supay.
- **Muki/Muqui — [RV/comparative].** A central-Peruvian mine being comparable in function to El Tío, not evidence for a single pan-Andean Supay cult.
- **Salamanca Devil — [RV/FOL], especially northwestern Argentina.** Supay/Zupay is the master, teacher, pact-maker, or dangerous presence of the cave-like Salamanca.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 What is not attested

**[Finding]** The Inca left no alphabetic scripture. No khipu has been securely deciphered as naming Supay. No pre-conquest image has an identifying inscription reading *Supay*. Archaeological evidence for caves, ancestor veneration, mountain shrines, capacocha sacrifice, and underworld symbolism cannot by itself be assigned to this name.

**[MI]** Images circulated online as “ancient statues of Supay” are generally unlabeled archaeological objects, modern Diablada masks, tourist art, or illustrations. Formal resemblance to horns, fangs, snakes, or subterranean animals is not identification.

### 2.2 Primary and near-primary corpus

#### Santo Tomás, *Lexicón* — 1560

**[EA/CE]** Printed in Valladolid; Quechua–Spanish missionary lexicography based on years in Peru. It is the earliest secure occurrence located for the name and preserves both positive and negative potential. It offers no biography, genealogy, iconography, myth, temple, sacrifice, or divine office.

#### Third Lima Council catechisms and the anonymous vocabulary — 1584–1586

**[CE]** These institutional texts belong to the evangelizing translation project that regularized *supay* as the bad angel/demon. They document a policy of terminology, not passive reporting of an unchanged pre-conquest theology.

#### Cristóbal de Molina, *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas* — ca. 1575

**[EA/CE]** Molina preserves Cuzco ritual prayers, festivals, creation traditions, royal ceremonies, and capacocha material. Supay is not a central named deity in his reconstruction of state cult. That absence weighs strongly against claims that a singular Supay held an official rank comparable to Inti, Viracocha, or the Thunder complex.

#### Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú* — ca. 1570s–1580s

**[EA/CE]** Albornoz catalogues huacas and instructs investigators how to expose their cults. His demonological framework treats native powers as devilish, but it does not document a universal temple or priesthood of a singular Supay. The text’s evidentiary value lies partly in showing how many locally named powers were administratively redescribed as manifestations or instruments of “the devil.”

#### Guaman Poma de Ayala, *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno* — ca. 1615–1616

**[EA/CE]** Guaman Poma’s autograph manuscript mixes Spanish, Quechua, Christian providential history, Andean memory, polemic, and approximately four hundred drawings. He reports that Indigenous people called devils *zupay/supay* and employs the term within an explicitly Christian moral history. He is indispensable evidence for an Indigenous Christian intellectual’s usage, but he does not preserve an untouched pre-conquest “Supay mythology.” The complete autograph is accessible at the [Royal Danish Library](https://poma.kb.dk/).

#### Huarochirí Manuscript — probably ca. 1608, surviving colonial manuscript

**[EA/CE]** This Quechua manuscript, associated with Francisco de Ávila’s anti-idolatry investigations, contains the richest early corpus of central-Andean narratives. Its named superhuman actors include Pariacaca, Cuniraya Viracocha, Chaupiñamca, Huallallo Carhuincho, Macahuisa, Llocllayhuancupa, and numerous local huacas. *Supay* usually operates as the Christianizing narrator’s classification—“demon” or bad spirit—rather than as one character with a coherent genealogy.

One episode describes Don Cristóbal violating an old huaca’s precinct by urinating there. The offended being appears as a radiance and pursues or attacks him; the narrator calls the huaca a *supay*. **[EA/CE]** This is evidence that a locally specific huaca could be demonized as *supay*, not that a god named Supay personally fought Don Cristóbal. A recent source study makes precisely this point in its analysis of [dreams and religious syncretism in Huarochirí](https://www.scielo.cl/article_plus.php?lng=es&pid=S0719-26812024000100005&tlng=es).

The standard English edition is Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste’s 1991 *The Huarochirí Manuscript*, described by [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/730526).

#### Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* — 1621

**[CE]** Arriaga provides procedures for interrogating communities, destroying huacas and ancestor bundles, confiscating ritual objects, punishing specialists, and substituting Christian practice. His “devil” is often the theological agent presumed behind diverse local cults. References to demonic deception cannot safely be converted into reports of one native god named Supay.

#### Cajatambo and Huarochirí idolatry trials — seventeenth century

**[EA/CE]** These judicial records preserve named huacas, mallquis, offerings, ritual speech, forced testimony, concealment, and church suppression. Their evidentiary context is coercive: statements were produced through prosecution, interpretation, leading theological questions, and sometimes violence. The Cajatambo corpus, largely from 1617–1664, is published in Pierre Duviols and collaborators’ *Procesos y visitas de idolatrías*, catalogued by the [Pontifical Catholic University of Peru](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/1f4ac161-c30e-4416-be48-dd1bf26b5765). It contains extensive cults of ancestors and local powers but does not establish a centralized Supay institution.

#### Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Cobo, Garcilaso

**[EA/CE]** These chroniclers differ substantially in access, purpose, date, and theological framework. They document Inca origins, ritual, royal ideology, huacas, the dead, oracles, and missionary interpretations of “the devil.” None supplies a securely independent narrative cycle in which a singular Supay is born, rules Ukhu Pacha, marries, produces offspring, or combats the major Inca gods. Garcilaso explicitly distinguishes Pachacámac from *Zupay*, saying Indigenous people named the latter with abhorrence; valuable as early colonial testimony, this remains Garcilaso’s Christian and retrospective interpretation.

### 2.3 Textual conclusion

The corpus supports a history of a **word and category under colonial transformation**, not a recoverable divine biography. Claims about Supay’s “myths” must therefore be divided among:

1. pre-Christian semantic residue in early lexicography;
2. colonial demonological usage;
3. local beings reclassified as supays;
4. mine and festival traditions;
5. modern synthesized mythology.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### 3.1 Domain

**[EA]** The earliest text does not assign Supay a bounded divine portfolio. Santo Tomás suggests morally variable spirit activity, apparition, and domestic haunting.

**[CE]** Missionary usage assigns the Christian domains of demon, possession, temptation, false worship, and hell.

**[CE/SYN]** González Holguín’s expression for hell—*uku pacha supaypa wasin*, approximately “inner world, house of the demon”—joins Christian infernal geography to an Andean spatial term. *Ukhu/uku* means inward, interior, or below; *pacha* can signify world, space-time, era, or ordered sphere. The phrase establishes residence, not necessarily monarchy.

**[RV/FOL]** In Bolivian mines, the Tío/Supay governs mineral wealth, veins, luck, productive strength, sexuality, accidents, collapse, illness, and death underground. He is an owner with whom miners establish reciprocity. Outside the mine, miners may emphasize Christian saints, the Virgin, or Pachamama; underground, the Tío’s jurisdiction becomes immediate.

**[RV/FOL]** In Santiago del Estero and neighboring northwestern Argentina, Zupay/Supay can govern the Salamanca, dangerous knowledge, music, dance, riding skill, wealth, erotic power, witchcraft, storms, pestilence, whirlwinds, and infernal pacts. A municipal cultural summary records the being’s many forms—animal, tree, wind, handsome gaucho, old country philosopher, or powerful Black man—and his association with Salamanca specialists in [Santiagueño folklore](https://www.santiagociudad.gov.ar/cultura/supay).

### 3.2 Appearance

**[EA]** There is no securely identified pre-Hispanic Supay iconography.

**[CE]** Colonial Christian art supplied Devil conventions: horns, tail, claws, fangs, wings, dark or fiery color, grotesque hybridity, and defeat beneath angels or saints.

**[RV/FOL]** Bolivian mine Tíos are modeled figures of clay, plaster, stone, or mineral matrix, often seated and life-sized or smaller. Common traits include horns, staring or red eyes, beard or moustache, boots, an erect oversized penis, and a mouth able to receive cigarettes, coca, and alcohol. These are not fixed canonical attributes; individual mines and work groups make and remake their own Tíos.

**[RV/FOL]** Diablada masks are spectacular composite objects with bulging eyes, fangs, horns, snakes, lizards, toads, dragons, or other creatures. The imagery combines Catholic Devil art, local mountain and underworld symbolism, nineteenth- and twentieth-century workshop innovation, imported decorative motifs, and competitive enlargement. It cannot be projected unchanged into the Inca period. Dimitri Karadimas argues that Oruro’s diabolical figure may retain features of Andean mountain beings and the ominous *nina-nina* wasp, but presents this as an iconographic interpretation, not a proven one-to-one survival, in [“The Nina-Nina, the Devil and Oruro”](https://journals.iai.spk-berlin.de/index.php/indiana/article/view/2187).

### 3.3 Symbols, substances, and offerings

**[RV/FOL]** Documented mining offerings include coca leaves, cigarettes, alcohol or singani, chicha, food, streamers, confetti, llama blood, llama hearts, and sometimes miniature or composite offering bundles. The figure is “fed,” given drink, and made to smoke.

**[RV/FOL]** Characteristic substances are mineral ore, earth, blood, alcohol, tobacco, coca, fat, and fire. Characteristic places are mine galleries, ore veins, caves, mountain interiors, rock fissures, and Salamanca caverns.

**[Finding]** No securely early evidence connects Supay specifically with a canonical color, planet, weekday, number, weapon, sacred plant, or animal. Modern lists assigning such correspondences usually reproduce occult-style systematization without an Andean primary source.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### 4.1 Genealogy

**[Finding]** There is no securely attested early genealogy for Supay. The chronicles, Huarochirí Manuscript, dictionaries, and extirpation corpus do not identify Supay’s parents, siblings, spouse, or children as parts of a stable Inca genealogy.

**[MI]** Online family trees making Supay the son of Viracocha, spouse of Mama Cocha or Pachamama, father of named demons, or hereditary king of Ukhu Pacha lack support in the standard early sources.

**[RV/FOL]** *China Supay* means a female devil or female Diablada character, not securely “the wife of the god Supay.” Likewise, plural *supaykuna* are not necessarily his offspring.

### 4.2 Every documented or claimed god–god interaction

Because early *supay* is primarily a category, very few interactions qualify as meetings between a named individual Supay and another named divine being.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 No recoverable “Inca Supay cycle”

**[Finding]** There is no early narrative cycle comparable to the Huarochirí stories of Pariacaca or Cuniraya in which Supay is a recurring named protagonist. The absence is especially conspicuous because the Huarochirí Manuscript supplies long, detailed narratives about many other powers.

### 5.2 The spirit of the dead

**[MSR]** The best-supported reconstruction is not a plot but a category: an ancestral soul, shadow, apparition, or dangerous dead presence could affect the living beneficially or harmfully. Death did not necessarily create absolute moral opposition. Correct reciprocal relations mattered more than an essence of metaphysical evil.

**[CE]** Missionary translation converted this field into the Christian demon. Ancestor cults, huacas, oracles, dreams, and apparitions could then be explained as demonic fraud. The change was juridical as well as semantic: an ancestral being receiving offerings became “the devil” soliciting idolatry.

### 5.3 The house below

**[CE/SYN]** González Holguín’s *ukhu pacha supaypa wasin* generated a durable association among Supay, the world inside/below, and Christian hell. Later retellings transformed “the lower/interior world is the demon’s house” into “Supay is king of the Inca underworld.” The latter adds kingship, singularity, and systematic pantheon structure absent from the cited dictionary entry.

### 5.4 Don Cristóbal and the offended huaca

**[EA/CE]** In the Huarochirí Manuscript, Don Cristóbal violates a former sacred precinct. The being manifests as radiance, pursues him, terrifies him, and nearly causes him to fall. The Christian narrator calls it a *supay*. The narrative preserves Indigenous assumptions about a place-power’s capacity to retaliate, while its vocabulary marks that power as demonic. It is therefore a prime example of a colonial double register, not a personal myth of a pan-Andean Supay.

### 5.5 The mine-owner

**[RV/FOL]** Modern mine narratives portray the subterranean master as owner of ore. A miner who neglects him may lose the vein, suffer accident, become ill, go mad, or die. A miner who feeds him may receive rich ore and unusual strength, but dangerous generosity creates obligations.

Three origin narratives recorded in Potosí ethnography are especially important:

1. **The banished Devil:** God expelled the Devil underground, where he took possession of mineral riches.
2. **The imprisoned pre-Hispanic deity:** Spaniards forced an older mountain power beneath the earth, but miners continued bargaining with him.
3. **The dead mitayos:** workers killed by colonial mine labor became or belonged to the Tío; prolonged subterranean existence could transform miners into Tíos.

These are **[FOL] competing narratives**, not stages of one canonical myth. A recent Bolivian historical discussion summarizes the three versions and notes a proposal that the Tío’s wide diffusion accompanied the late-nineteenth-century tin boom in [“El Tío, Dios de los mineros bolivianos”](https://www.scielo.org.bo/scielo.php?pid=S2519-53522024000100003&script=sci_arttext).

### 5.6 The Devil pact

**[RV/FOL]** A miner may be said to enter an individual pact yielding a spectacular vein, money, productivity, or sexual potency, followed by ruin or premature death. Reports differ on whether miners affirm that such pacts really occur, accuse unusually successful colleagues, or tell them as warnings. They should not be generalized from rumor into ordinary ritual.

**[MSR—debated]** Michael Taussig interpreted the mine Devil as a critique of capitalist commodity production: money and mineral wealth severed from reciprocal regeneration become sterile and deadly. His 1980 study is available through [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5149/9780807898413_taussig.4). Later scholars object that his opposition between a reciprocal pre-capitalist world and evil capitalist extraction is too rigid. Guillermo Salas Carreño instead emphasizes continuing negotiation with earth-beings as owners of material resources and reviews the criticism in [“Mining and the Living Materiality of Mountains”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1359183516679439).

### 5.7 Oruro: devils, Virgin, and angel

**[RV/SYN]** Oruro tradition links the Virgin of the Socavón with a mortally wounded outlaw or miner, often Nina-Nina or Chiru-Chiru, who is found with her image. Versions differ on identity, date, and miracle. The legend becomes an origin for Marian mine devotion.

**[RV/CE]** A dramatic text conventionally dated to 1818 and attributed to Ladislao Montealegre, the *Narración de los siete pecados capitales*, stages Michael’s struggle against Lucifer and the deadly sins. Its relationship to the precise origin of today’s Diablada is debated; it documents Christian dramatic organization, not an unchanged pre-Hispanic choreography.

**[RV/FOL]** In performance, magnificent devils journey toward and submit devotionally to the Virgin. This does not simply dramatize Christianity defeating an Indigenous god. It gives dangerous beings a legitimate place inside a Catholic festival while preserving their visual and affective power.

### 5.8 The Salamanca of northwestern Argentina

**[RV/FOL]** The Salamanca is a cave, hidden school, dry riverbed, or unseen gathering place from which music sounds. Candidates endure frightening animals, abandon Christian protections, and bargain with Zupay for knowledge or excellence in music, dance, fighting, livestock handling, love, or wealth.

**[EA/CE/FOL]** A 1761 Santiago del Estero witchcraft prosecution already contains Salamanca-like accusations. Torture and judicial stereotyping profoundly shaped the confessions; the trial cannot be read as an objective ritual transcript. Judith Farberman’s study compares the accused women’s changing testimony, the prosecutor’s demonological construction, and the defense in [“Las hechiceras de la Salamanca”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hispa_0007-4640_2000_num_102_1_5033?pageId=T1_67).

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### 6.1 Pre-Hispanic institutional cult

**[Finding]** No named Supay temple, priesthood, state festival, oracle, landholding institution, or securely Supay-specific offering assemblage is attested before the conquest. Bauer’s work on the Inca sacred landscape, Rostworowski’s studies of Andean cults, Zuidema’s ceque scholarship, and the chroniclers document numerous named huacas and state rites but do not supply the missing institution.

**[Finding]** Inca capacocha included human sacrifice, securely supported by archaeology and colonial descriptions, but no evidence assigns capacocha victims specifically to Supay. The Llullaillaco burials demonstrate high-altitude capacocha practice, coca and alcohol consumption, and prolonged ritual preparation; they do not identify Supay as recipient. The archaeological findings are summarized in the [PNAS study of the Llullaillaco children](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1305117110).

**[Contested-material verdict]** Statements that the Inca sacrificed humans “to Supay” are unsupported unless a specific source is supplied. Human sacrifice is real in the ancient Andes; the attribution to this being is not established.

### 6.2 Colonial suppression

**[CE]** Extirpation campaigns destroyed huacas and mallquis, burned ritual objects, relocated ancestral bodies, prosecuted ritual experts, compelled confession, and installed crosses and Christian images. “Supay” became part of the prosecution’s language: prohibited cult was service to the Devil.

**[EA/CE]** Cajatambo records document coca, chicha, guinea pigs, llamas, blood, fat, cloth, feathers, and other offerings to locally named huacas and ancestors. These offerings should not be reassigned wholesale to Supay. The corpus’s chief value is showing how diverse local beings disappeared under the extirpators’ aggregate category of idolatry and demonism.

### 6.3 Mining ritual

**[RV/FOL]** Inside Bolivian mines, miners greet, feed, smoke, and drink with the Tío. Offerings establish reciprocity and seek ore, protection, and consent to extract. A work group may possess its own image and ritual specialists or senior miners may conduct the offering.

**[RV/FOL]** At major ch’allas or sacrificial rites, a llama may be killed; its blood is applied to mine entrances, machinery, rock, or the Tío, and the heart or other parts may be buried or offered. Ordinary observance more often uses coca, cigarettes, alcohol, and food. There is no verified modern human sacrifice cult to Supay.

**[RV/FOL]** The Tío’s exposed phallus expresses generative mineral power, masculinity, fertility, danger, and the male sociality of underground work. Women’s access to working galleries has often been restricted through beliefs that female presence, jealousy, or salt can make ore disappear; actual rules vary by mine and have changed with women’s participation.

**[RV/FOL]** Crosses at mine entrances do not simply negate the Tío. Pascale Absi describes crosses as simultaneously opposed to and entangled with the subterranean master. Her monograph *Los ministros del diablo* is available through the [Institut de recherche pour le développement](https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/divers11-03/010038256.pdf).

### 6.4 Oruro ritual

**[RV/SYN]** The Carnival of Oruro centers on pilgrimage and procession to the Virgin of the Socavón, with the Diablada as a principal dance. Dancers make promises, rehearse, fund elaborate costumes, travel the processional route, and enter the sanctuary. The devotion is simultaneously Catholic, civic, occupational, familial, Indigenous, and commercial.

**[Institutional evidence]** UNESCO proclaimed the carnival a Masterpiece in 2001 and inscribed it on the Representative List in 2008. Its official account says an Uru Ito festival was Christianized around Candlemas and that the *llama llama*/Diablada associated with Tiw became the principal dance. That account is a heritage narrative and should not be mistaken for direct proof of an uninterrupted choreography from antiquity. See [UNESCO’s Carnival of Oruro dossier](https://ich.unesco.org/en/lists?RL=00003).

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1532: reconstructed substrate

**[MSR]** Quechua-speaking and neighboring Andean societies recognized ancestors, souls, place-beings, mountain owners, dangerous wild powers, caves, inner earth, and reciprocal supernatural relations. A precursor semantic field for *supay* probably belonged to this world.

**[Finding]** A singular named god Supay cannot presently be isolated archaeologically or textually.

### 1530s–1560: first colonial translation

**[EA/CE]** Missionaries searched for Indigenous equivalents for Christian theological terms. Santo Tomás’s 1560 dictionary preserves a moment when *supay* could still be good or bad, while mapping it onto angel, demon, and goblin.

### 1580s: official demonization

**[CE]** The Third Lima Council standardized doctrine in Quechua and other Indigenous languages. *Supay* became the preferred equivalent for bad angel/devil. Spanish *ángel* tended to remain attached to the good celestial category, while the Indigenous term was increasingly restricted to evil.

### ca. 1600–1660s: extirpation and textual fixation

**[CE]** González Holguín defines *supay* simply as demon and locates the demon’s house in *ukhu pacha*. Guaman Poma employs the Christianized meaning. Huarochirí’s writer uses *supay* to condemn huacas. Arriaga and regional prosecutors transform many beings and ancestral rites into evidence of Satanic deception.

### Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries: mining worlds

**[MSR/RV]** Colonial extraction brought Indigenous mountain ontologies, Christian demonology, forced labor, migrant communities, new technologies, and immense mortality into sustained contact. A subterranean master emerged in different local forms. Continuity with older mountain and ancestor powers is plausible; a direct unchanged descent from one Inca god is not documented.

### Eighteenth century: Salamanca and Socavón formations

**[RV]** The 1761 Santiago del Estero trial documents colonial Salamanca demonology. Oruro’s Socavón devotion is conventionally connected with a 1789 miracle tradition. Both demonstrate already-mestizo supernatural worlds, not untouched survivals.

### Nineteenth century: dramatic and mining consolidation

**[RV]** Oruro’s devil drama is associated with the 1818 *Narración*. Expanding mining and labor communities encouraged the spread and rearticulation of mine-owner figures. Some historians place the Tío’s broad Bolivian consolidation particularly in the late-nineteenth-century tin economy.

### 1904 and the twentieth century: fraternity, spectacle, ethnography

**[RV]** The Gran Tradicional Auténtica Diablada Oruro traces institutional organization to 1904. Costumes, brass music, choreography, masks, national folklore policy, tourism, and fraternal competition transformed the dance.

**[FOL/MSR]** Twentieth-century ethnographers including June Nash, Olivia Harris, Michael Taussig, and later Pascale Absi documented Tío ritual. Their interpretations disagree: cultural survival, reciprocal earth ownership, labor consciousness, commodity fetishism, masculinity, and occupational sovereignty are overlapping rather than mutually exclusive explanations.

### 2001–present: global heritage and digital mythology

**[RV]** UNESCO recognition globalized Oruro’s Diablada. Bolivian and Peruvian disputes over dance ownership intensified around heritage, national branding, migration, and tourism.

**[MI]** Online mythology sites increasingly present Supay as a neatly bounded “Inca god of death, ruler of Uku Pacha,” complete with fixed appearance and family. This format resembles Greco-Roman pantheon handbooks and role-playing encyclopedias more than the colonial evidence.

**[FOL]** Living observance continues in Bolivian mines, Oruro, Quechua-speaking Peru, the Altiplano, and northwestern Argentina, but it is neither uniform nor necessarily self-described as “worship of the ancient god Supay.”

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### 8.1 Anthropology and political interpretation

**[MSR]** June Nash’s *We Eat the Mines and the Mines Eat Us* connected mine ritual with labor, dependency, danger, and community. Taussig’s *The Devil and Commodity Fetishism in South America* made the Devil a critique of capitalist production. Absi’s *Los ministros del diablo* supplied fuller Potosí ethnography and emphasized miners’ social and ritual worlds.

**[Debate]** Taussig’s approach remains influential but contested. Critics argue that it overstates the novelty of markets, idealizes pre-capitalist reciprocity, and derives Bolivian conclusions too heavily from others’ ethnography. Salas Carreño instead treats mountains and mine beings as owners with whom extraction always requires negotiation.

### 8.2 Heritage nationalism

**[RV/modern politics]** The Diablada is claimed within competing Bolivian, Peruvian, Chilean, and transborder histories. Claims often confuse several questions: the antiquity of devil dances; the location where the modern brass-band Diablada was institutionalized; the circulation of performers and mask-makers; and the antiquity of the underlying Andean beings. None can be resolved merely by declaring Supay the national god of one modern state.

### 8.3 Literature, music, visual culture, and commerce

**[FOL/modern reception]** Supay and Salamanca appear in Argentine folklore literature, songs, dance, crafts, regional tourism, and accounts of musical mastery. Oruro masks have become globally recognizable symbols reproduced in museums, posters, souvenirs, tattoos, fashion, and digital art.

**[MI]** Modern fantasy illustrations frequently combine Inca imperial regalia, a European horned Devil, skeletal “death-god” imagery, and Diablada masks. Such images are creative composites, not reconstructions of an identified Inca original.

### 8.4 Neopagan and occult claims

**[Contested-material pass]** No evidence located establishes a large, continuous, institutionally organized modern “Church of Supay,” a pre-conquest grimoire tradition, a Golden Dawn lineage, or an old hermetic planetary correspondence for Supay.

**[MI]** Modern practitioners may privately invoke Supay within eclectic demonolatry, occultism, chaos magic, or pan-Andean neopagan systems. Unless supported by community history and documented transmission, claims that these rites reproduce an ancient Inca Supay priesthood are modern invention.

**[Important distinction]** Living mine ch’allas, Diablada vows, and regional Salamanca traditions are not properly reduced to “neopagan revival.” They possess local social transmission, although that transmission has continuously changed under Catholicism, labor migration, tourism, and national folklore institutions.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### 9.1 Was Supay originally good, evil, or neutral?

- **Neutral/ambivalent reconstruction:** Supported by Santo Tomás’s “good or bad,” dialectal soul/shadow evidence, and the relational morality of Andean beings.
- **Always dangerous interpretation:** Early positive language may partly reflect the missionary need to translate “angel,” while household goblin and demon senses already indicate danger.
- **Conclusion:** Moral ambivalence is better supported than an originally benevolent god, but no pristine pre-Christian definition survives.

### 9.2 Was Supay a singular god?

- **Against:** Plural usage; broad lexical senses; absence of genealogy, temple, priesthood, mythology, or secure image; category use in Huarochirí.
- **For or partially for:** Later individualized Devil, Tío, Salamanca master, and festival chief can receive proper-name treatment.
- **Conclusion:** Singularity is late, regional, or contextual—not securely imperial Inca.

### 9.3 Was Supay lord of Ukhu Pacha?

- **Evidence:** González Holguín’s Christian lexicon calls hell *ukhu pacha* and “the demon’s house.”
- **Problem:** A house is not a kingdom; *ukhu pacha* is not everywhere the Christian hell; early sources do not give Supay a throne or administration of the dead.
- **Conclusion:** “Associated with the inner/lower world” is supportable. “Ancient Inca king of Ukhu Pacha” is an overstatement.

### 9.4 Is El Tío simply Supay?

- **For:** Miners and ethnographers sometimes use *Tío, diablo, maestro,* and *supay* interchangeably; both occupy dangerous subterranean space and mediate wealth.
- **Against:** Names, images, gender, origin stories, and ritual jurisdictions vary; comparable mine beings include Muki and Anchanchu.
- **Conclusion:** The equation is a documented regional syncretism, not a timeless identity.

### 9.5 Is the Diablada an unchanged pre-Hispanic Supay rite?

- **For continuity:** Andean inner-earth and mountain symbolism, Uru festival traditions, local animals, occupational mine devotion.
- **Against simple continuity:** Christian mystery-play structure, Michael and the seven sins, Marian pilgrimage, modern musical ensembles, fraternity organization, and changing mask manufacture.
- **Conclusion:** The Diablada is a layered colonial and modern formation with Andean continuities, not an unchanged Inca ritual.

### 9.6 Human sacrifice

**[Finding]** Inca and earlier Andean human sacrifice is archaeologically and textually documented. Supay as its specific recipient is not. Modern mine rites include camelid sacrifice, not an established institutional practice of human killing. Stories that the Tío “takes” miners refer to accident, occupational death, supernatural predation, or rumor, not proof of deliberate sacrificial homicide.

### 9.7 Archaeological identification

**[Finding]** Archaeology can establish offerings, sacrificial contexts, mountain shrines, caves, mortuary cult, and iconographic themes. Without inscriptions or securely transmitted identifications, assigning an artifact to Supay is circular if based only on the modern idea that he rules death or the underground.

### 9.8 State of the evidence

Major surviving repositories and editions include:

- Santo Tomás and González Holguín print copies in European and American rare-book collections.
- Guaman Poma’s autograph, GKS 2232 4°, Royal Danish Library.
- The Huarochirí Quechua manuscript, Biblioteca Nacional de España, with editions by Hermann Trimborn, José María Arguedas, Gerald Taylor, and Salomon–Urioste.
- Huarochirí and Cajatambo extirpation records, especially the Archivo Arzobispal de Lima, with editions by Duviols and collaborators.
- Archaeological materials in Peruvian, Bolivian, Chilean, Argentine, European, and North American collections, rarely identifiable as Supay objects.
- Modern ethnographic recordings, fieldnotes, photographs, and films dispersed across university and national archives.

## 11. Sources and assessment

The most probative primary evidence is the sequence Santo Tomás (1560) → Lima catechetical standardization (1580s) → Huarochirí and González Holguín (ca. 1608) → Guaman Poma and extirpation records (seventeenth century). That sequence documents a semantic transformation unusually clearly.

The chronicles of Cieza, Betanzos, Molina, Sarmiento, Cobo, Garcilaso, Albornoz, Arriaga, and Guaman Poma remain essential for reconstructing the religious field around the term. Their references to “the devil,” however, cannot automatically be replaced with the proper name Supay. The chroniclers frequently use Christian causation to explain huacas, oracles, ancestors, and Indigenous rites.

Rostworowski, Zuidema, Duviols, Urton, Bauer, Salomon, MacCormack, Silverblatt, Harrison, Taylor, Nash, Taussig, and Absi illuminate different components—pantheon formation, ceque organization, language, sacred landscape, colonial translation, gender, extirpation, mining, and labor. None supplies the missing pre-Hispanic dossier for a singular underworld king. The responsible reconstruction is therefore asymmetrical: the colonial and modern transformations are richly documented; the supposed god’s pre-conquest personality is not.

The fullest defensible conclusion is that **Supay’s documented history is the history of an unstable supernatural category transformed by translation**. A morally variable spirit or soul-like being entered Christian discourse as the Devil; “inner world” became “hell”; local huacas became demons; subterranean wealth generated mine masters; Catholic drama produced devils defeated by Michael yet dancing for the Virgin; and modern mythography turned these plural histories into one ancient god. Supay is historically real as a word, classification, feared presence, ritual partner, festival persona, and modern divine image. What cannot be recovered is the neat pre-Hispanic biography now so often supplied for him.
