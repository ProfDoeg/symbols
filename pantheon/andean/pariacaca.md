# Pariacaca: Research Dossier

**Class:** *huaca* (Quechua *wak’a*): a powerful, animate sacred being simultaneously manifest as a mountain, storm and water power, oracle, culture hero, ancestor, and materially localized shrine. “Mountain god” or modern *apu* is serviceable shorthand, but neither captures the full colonial Quechua category. Pariacaca is primarily the tutelary being and ancestral conqueror of Yauyo-speaking populations in the central Peruvian Andes; Inca rule subsequently incorporated his regional sanctuary and oracle into an imperial religious network.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Pedro Cieza de León, writing during the 1540s and publishing the first part of his *Crónica del Perú* in 1553, securely records the snowy mountain and road/pass called Pariacaca. This proves that the name was attached to that conspicuous sacred landscape by the first decades after conquest, but Cieza’s surviving notice does not preserve the full mythology. The earliest extensive description of Pariacaca as a worshipped being is late sixteenth-century: Cristóbal de Albornoz’s anti-idolatry materials and, above all, the anonymous Quechua *Huarochirí Manuscript*, compiled around 1598–1608 under circumstances connected with the priest and extirpator Francisco de Ávila. The manuscript is colonial in date but records Indigenous testimony about cults and territorial traditions understood by its narrators as substantially pre-Hispanic.

**Evidentiary labels used below:**

- **[Early colonial attestation]** Sixteenth- or earliest seventeenth-century documentary testimony about practices or traditions presented as already existing.
- **[Huarochirí narrative]** Material specifically attested by the Quechua manuscript, approximately 1598–1608.
- **[Inca-period reconstruction]** A modern historical inference combining colonial testimony, topography, and archaeology.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally bounded or textually competing version.
- **[Colonial systematization]** A representation shaped by missionary inquiry, Christian genres, translation, or anti-idolatry procedure.
- **[Folklore/living practice]** Modern or recent community belief and ritual, not automatically projected backward.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An interpretation rather than an explicit proposition in a primary source.
- **[Modern invention/unverified]** A recent claim for which no ancient or early-colonial evidence has been located.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Pariacaca is not best understood as one interchangeable member of a homogeneous “Inca pantheon.” The primary record makes him the leading *huaca* of Huarochirí and the Yauyos, whose rise mythically encodes their movement into lands formerly belonging to Yunca populations and the displacement of the older ruler Huallallo Carhuincho. He brings rain and destructive torrents, controls irrigation water, conquers territory, founds ritual institutions, receives pilgrims and camelid sacrifices, acts as an oracle, animates male humanity, and serves as the ancestral focus of communities calling themselves his children.

His identity is distributive rather than consistently singular. He appears as five eggs, then five falcons, then five humanlike persons; the five can fight separately and later coalesce. The manuscript calls the five brothers, yet also makes “Pariacaca” the collective or senior name. Elsewhere beings designated his sons enact his power across the region. Any neat distinction among “the god,” “his four brothers,” “his manifestations,” and “his children” imposes greater consistency than the Quechua text possesses.

### Names and spellings

- **Pariacaca / Paria Caca** — conventional colonial and modern Spanish spellings.
- **Paryaqaqa / Parya Qaqa** — normalized modern Quechua orthography.
- **Pariacacca / Pariacaca** — variants found in older editions and maps.
- **The snow mountain/cordillera of Pariacaca** — the divine name and geographic name cannot be cleanly separated.
- **Apu Pariacaca** — common modern Spanish-Andean usage. *Apu* means a lord or powerful mountain being. It accurately describes much living usage but should not be retrojected as the manuscript’s exclusive ancient title.
- **Huaca principal / principal *wak’a*** — the manuscript’s functional classification for the region.
- **Father / “our father”** — relational ritual language. The annual feast name *Auquisna* is glossed in the manuscript’s scholarly editions as “for our father.”
- **Animator of men** — the *Huarochirí Manuscript* says Pariacaca animates or gives vital force to men, while Chaupiñamca performs the corresponding function for women.

### Etymology

The etymology is disputed.

1. **Quechua interpretation:** *parya* has been glossed as reddish, copper-colored, or sparrow, while *qaqa* means rock, crag, or rocky mountain. “Reddish rock/crag” is therefore possible. The landscape itself contains reddish stone, making this semantically plausible.

2. **Quechua-Aymara hybrid proposal:** Some modern writers analyze a form such as *pari-ya-q qaqa* as an “igneous/burning crag.” This is attractive in light of Huallallo’s fire and Pariacaca’s storm combat but is not securely established by an early lexical gloss.

3. **Toponym first or god first:** The evidence cannot determine whether a pre-existing mountain name became a divine name, whether the being’s name was transferred to the mountain, or whether that distinction was meaningless within local ontology.

Claims that the name definitively means “god of fire,” “five-headed thunder,” or a cosmic union of five directions are **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** or **[Modern invention]** unless tied to an explicit source. The Quechua manuscript never supplies a formal etymology.

### Comparative and syncretistic identifications

No primary source securely equates Pariacaca with Zeus, Jupiter, Thor, or another Old World deity. Such equivalences are modern comparative shorthand.

Pariacaca’s meteorological character encouraged comparison with **Illapa**, the Inca-associated thunder/lightning complex. One manuscript reading apparently supplied or then struck out *Sullca Illapa* as the missing fifth brother’s name. This is evidence of textual instability or an attempted identification, not proof that Pariacaca and Illapa were timelessly identical.

The regional system sometimes opposes or complements **Pariacaca “above”** with **Pachacamac “below”**: highland/Yauyo, water and mountain versus coastal/Yunca, earth and sea. This is a historically situated highland–coast articulation, not an identity between them.

Modern pastoral rites combine reverence for Pariacaca with Catholic devotion to **Santiago**, saints, crosses, masses, and vigils. Santiago is associated with livestock and thunder throughout parts of the Andes, but no source located demonstrates a simple one-to-one colonial substitution “Santiago = Pariacaca.” The documented reality is layered coexistence.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Material before the written record

The Pariacaca massif, associated springs, caves, lakes, pathways, terraces, cairns, and shrine structures necessarily predate the colonial manuscripts. Archaeological survey demonstrates pre-Hispanic traffic and construction along the transversal route later called Xauxa–Pachacamac. Nevertheless, an excavated building or stone cannot be assigned specifically to Pariacaca merely because it lies near the modern mountain. Identification requires colonial geographical correspondence, local names, or associated ritual evidence.

No securely read pre-Hispanic inscription names Pariacaca; the Andes did not leave an alphabetic epigraphic corpus comparable to Greek or Mesopotamian traditions. No independently deciphered *quipu* gives his name.

### Cieza de León

Pedro Cieza de León wrote his *Crónica del Perú* in the 1540s; Part I appeared in 1553, while much of the remaining project was published only centuries later. Cieza calls attention to the formidable snowy Pariacaca road, using it as an example of Inca road engineering. This is the earliest presently secure written occurrence of the name. It establishes:

- an already established mountain/pass name;
- a major trans-Andean route;
- early Spanish familiarity with the landscape.

It does **not**, in the relevant notice, document the five eggs, Huallallo, or the cult’s complete ritual organization.

### Cristóbal de Albornoz

Albornoz’s *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú y sus camayos y haciendas*, generally dated to the 1580s, belongs to a clerical program for identifying shrines, their attendants, properties, and offerings. It is indispensable for reconstructing how Spanish investigators categorized Andean sacred powers. Pariacaca appears within a world of major provincial *huacas* that Inca imperial religion incorporated rather than simply abolishing.

Because the *Instrucción* was an operational anti-idolatry document, its taxonomy is **[Colonial systematization]**. Its silences do not establish that unmentioned local rites were absent.

### The *Huarochirí Manuscript*

The manuscript—usually dated about 1598–1608—is an anonymous Quechua text containing 31 chapters and two supplements. It survives as a colonial manuscript associated with Francisco de Ávila’s papers in Madrid. Its composition probably involved multiple Indigenous narrators, scribes, editors, and local traditions. Ávila’s precise role remains debated: collector, sponsor, interrogator, owner, or some combination.

It is exceptional because it records Andean sacred narratives at length in Quechua rather than merely paraphrasing them in Spanish. Yet it is not an untouched pre-conquest scripture. Its chapter divisions, retrospective historical scheme, concern with true and false *huacas*, and possible biblical resonances belong to a colonial encounter. Frank Salomon and George Urioste’s bilingual critical translation remains the principal English edition.

Relevant chapters include:

- **Ch. 1:** Succession of earlier regional powers—Yanañamca and Tutañamca, then Huallallo Carhuincho, then Pariacaca.
- **Chs. 3–4:** Flood and disappearance of the sun traditions forming the manuscript’s cosmic prehistory.
- **Ch. 5:** Huatiacuri, Tantañamca, the foxes, and discovery of Pariacaca as five eggs.
- **Ch. 6:** Birth as five falcons and transformation into persons; punishment of Huayquihusa; relations with Chuquisuso; movement toward the mountain.
- **Chs. 7–8:** Chuquisuso’s cult; ascent; rescue of a child intended for Huallallo; combat with Huallallo.
- **Ch. 9:** Pariacaca establishes his cult and festivals.
- **Chs. 10–13:** Chaupiñamca, Chanco, Tutayquiri, and conquest by Pariacaca’s children.
- **Chs. 15–16:** Relative chronology involving Cuniraya; names and kinship of the five.
- **Ch. 23:** Túpac Inca Yupanqui summons the *huacas*; Pariacaca sends Macahuisa to defeat rebels.
- **Later passages:** service at the sanctuary, prophecy or recollection of the Spaniards’ coming, and colonial loss of ritual wealth.

The manuscript’s temporal paradoxes are evidence, not defects to be silently corrected. Huatiacuri is called Pariacaca’s son while traveling to witness Pariacaca’s emergence. Either “son” expresses ritual affiliation rather than biological sequence, the narrative joins separate traditions, or sacred genealogy does not follow ordinary chronology.

### Jesuit annual letters, 1609–1611

Jesuit *Cartas Anuas* describe the active sanctuary shortly before its destruction. A 1609 account refers to a great cave—variously transcribed as Chutinhuaque or associated by modern survey with Cuchimachay—where worshippers assembled with dressed representations or remains of their dead, followed by offerings of guinea pigs, llamas, coca, and other goods. Reports from 1611 concern the campaign against the sanctuary and destruction of its central material representation.

These letters provide rare near-contemporary descriptions of actual colonial-period ritual. They are hostile missionary reports but cannot be dismissed: their detailed locations and sequences correspond sufficiently with local geography to be historically valuable.

### Arriaga

Pablo José de Arriaga’s *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621) systematizes the knowledge produced by early campaigns. It explains *huacas*, priestly specialists, offerings, festivals, confession, ancestors, and methods for destroying shrines. Arriaga is not an independent neutral ethnography of Pariacaca. His book is a manual of suppression shaped by Christian demonology and the administrative needs of visitors.

### Guaman Poma, Molina, Betanzos, Sarmiento, Cobo, and Garcilaso

These authors provide necessary comparative evidence for Inca religion, imperial sacrifice, road systems, provincial cults, and the political handling of conquered *huacas*, but not all preserve independent Pariacaca narratives.

- **Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas*, c. 1551:** important for Inca traditions and Quechua-mediated court testimony; not a source for the five-egg cycle.
- **Cristóbal de Molina, *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas*, c. 1575:** crucial for Cusco ritual and the *capacocha*, but it should not be used to attribute every imperial rite automatically to Pariacaca.
- **Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los Incas*, 1572:** political history and royal genealogy; comparative rather than primary evidence for Pariacaca’s regional myths.
- **Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, c. 1615:** illustrates and describes Andean ritual categories, months, sacrifices, roads, and colonial abuses, but supplies no securely identified portrait of Pariacaca.
- **Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, completed 1653:** a late synthesis using earlier reports. Valuable for imperial religious administration, but chronologically secondary.
- **Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales*, 1609:** presents a learned, often harmonizing account of Inca religion. His theology should not override the locally specific Huarochirí testimony.

### Idolatry trials

The Huarochirí and Cajatambo *visitas de idolatrías* preserve testimony about hidden stones, replacement of destroyed images, community ritual officers, clandestine ancestor cults, sacrifices, and resistance. Their statements were often obtained under coercion and framed by leading questions. They document what witnesses said in judicial conditions; they do not transparently reproduce pre-conquest religion.

Pierre Duviols’s editions remain fundamental. The Cajatambo corpus shows that destroying a *huaca’s* image did not necessarily end worship: ash, fragments, or substitute stones could retain or renew sacred presence. Cajatambo material is comparative unless Pariacaca is named; it must not be treated as direct evidence for his cult merely because the provinces shared certain religious forms.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**Water and rain.** Pariacaca’s snow, springs, rainstorms, irrigation works, and torrents sustain agriculture. The manuscript also makes water his principal weapon. His waters may nourish, reorder territory, or annihilate a settlement.

**Storm, wind, thunder, and hail.** These are strongly implied by his violent atmospheric manifestations. Calling him exclusively “the Inca storm god,” however, collapses a regional *huaca* into the imperial category of Illapa.

**Mountain and watershed.** The divine body is inseparable from the Pariacaca massif and its high-altitude lakes, passes, caves, and descending waterways. The mountain joins Pacific-draining valleys with the Mantaro side of the Andes.

**Conquest and political order.** Pariacaca defeats the previous ruler, redistributes land and water, sponsors Yauyo expansion, and establishes ritual obligations. The myths simultaneously naturalize a sacred order and remember political conflict.

**Oracle.** Inca rulers and local peoples consult or serve the sanctuary. César Astuhuamán interprets Pariacaca as an “imperial Andean oracle,” comparable in political function—but not identical—to Pachacamac, Catequil, and Huarivilca.

**Ancestry and postmortem destination.** Communities call themselves Pariacaca’s children. Some Huarochirí traditions say the dead travel or return to Pariacaca, making him a place of origin and destination.

**Male animation.** The manuscript’s division assigns Pariacaca the animation of men and Chaupiñamca that of women. This is a local learned classification, not necessarily a universal Andean doctrine.

### Forms

Pariacaca’s documented forms include:

- five eggs on Condorcoto/Kuntur Qutu;
- five falcons;
- five humanlike persons;
- a poor or socially insignificant traveler;
- a storm, torrent, rain, hail, or lightning-like force;
- one or several stones;
- the snow mountain itself;
- an oracle speaking through attendants or material embodiments.

The progression egg → falcon → person → fused or mountain being is **[Huarochirí narrative]**. Modern images depicting him as a fixed, crowned anthropomorphic “Inca god” are artistic choices.

### Material images

The sanctuary contained sacred stones rather than a securely documented monumental anthropomorphic statue. The 1609–1611 reports refer to a cave, a place of sacrifice, and a small group of stones or pebbles associated with Pariacaca. The precise relation among these objects—whether five embodiments, portable oracle-stones, attendants, or parts of one shrine—is not completely recoverable.

No surviving pre-Hispanic sculpture can presently be identified beyond reasonable doubt as a portrait of Pariacaca. Guaman Poma did not leave an uncontested labeled image. Consequently:

- a five-headed warrior is **not** canonical iconography;
- a condor is not securely his exclusive sacred animal;
- the falcon is his directly attested emergence form;
- eggs and the number five are textual attributes;
- snow peaks, rain, lightning, watercourses, and stone are defensible modern visual shorthand but not a fixed ancient iconographic code.

### Animals, substances, and offerings

- **Falcon:** emergence form.
- **Llama/alpaca:** sacrificial and pilgrimage animal; prized animals were named under Pariacaca’s patronage.
- **Guinea pig:** documented colonial-period offering.
- **Mullu** or thorny-oyster shell: elite ritual substance; brought by the father who had intended to sacrifice his child to Huallallo and requested by Macahuisa in the imperial narrative.
- **Coca:** offering and ritual consumable.
- **Chicha:** offered to Pariacaca in traveler disguise; hospitality or refusal has decisive consequences.
- **Ticti:** a prepared offering/drink ingredient in the Huallallo episode.
- **Water:** gift, weapon, and territorial medium.
- **Stone:** enduring embodiment of divine and human transformations.

No ancient source securely assigns Pariacaca a particular weekday, planet, zodiac sign, modern chakra, crystal, tarot card, or occult color. Such tables are **[Modern invention]**.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

#### The fivefold being

Chapter 16 gives four secure names among the five:

1. Pariacaca  
2. Churapa  
3. Puncho  
4. Pariacarco  
5. unnamed in the main textual statement

*Sullca Illapa* appears in connection with the missing fifth name but is textually uncertain or canceled. The five are called brothers. Elsewhere “the Pariacacas” can function collectively, and after Huallallo’s defeat the five are said to merge. Thus the manuscript supports both plurality and unity.

#### Parentage

One genealogical formulation makes Pariacaca and Chaupiñamca children of the **Sun** and **Hanan Maclla**. Another passage has the five say they are children of **Cuniraya**. These versions should not be harmonized into a single tree. They may reflect different communities, ritual classifications, or editorial layers.

#### Children

Named beings called Pariacaca’s children include:

- **Huatiacuri** — healer, trickster, and culture hero who precedes his father’s emergence.
- **Chuquihuampo** — fights beside Pariacaca and is wounded by Manañamca.
- **Macahuisa** — sent to aid Túpac Inca Yupanqui.
- **Tutayquiri** — major conquering son, checked by Chuquisuso.
- **Chucpaico**
- **Chancharuna**
- **Huariruma**
- **Utcochuco**
- **Sasenmale**

Some children are said to have emerged from fruit; another came from the earth. “Child” may denote genealogical descent, emanation, affiliated shrine, conquering lineage, or community descended from the *huaca*. Modern scholarship stresses this semantic breadth.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The succession of regional worlds

**[Huarochirí narrative]** The manuscript begins not with one creation ex nihilo but with successive ages ruled by different *huacas*. Yanañamca and Tutañamca belong to the oldest remembered stratum. Huallallo Carhuincho displaces them and becomes the life-giving regional power.

Huallallo’s age is prodigiously fertile: crops mature rapidly, colorful parrots inhabit the region, and the dead return after five days. Population therefore becomes excessive. Huallallo permits only two children and demands one as food. Pariacaca eventually defeats him and becomes the region’s principal *huaca*.

This is not an external chronology verifiable year by year. Scholars interpret it as a mythologized succession of populations, ecological regimes, and cults. The association of Huallallo with Yunca or Huanca groups and Pariacaca with Yauyo expansion is persuasive but remains **[Modern historical reconstruction]**, not a literal ethnic chronicle.

### Huatiacuri and the birth not yet born

Huatiacuri, a poor man whose name is associated with gleaning baked potatoes, travels from the coast toward Condorcoto to witness the emergence of his father Pariacaca. He overhears two foxes—one from the highlands and one from the coast—discussing Tantañamca’s sickness.

Tantañamca has claimed divine knowledge and accumulated wealth, but his household is polluted. A maize grain touched his wife’s genitals, was unknowingly fed to another man, and generated a symbolic adultery. Snakes occupy the roof, and a two-headed toad beneath the grinding stone drains Tantañamca’s vitality.

Huatiacuri diagnoses and cures the household, exposes Tantañamca as a false *huaca*, and claims the younger daughter as partner. He then defeats his wealthy brother-in-law in contests through Pariacaca’s power. Reaching Condorcoto, he finds Pariacaca as five eggs.

The narrative reverses social rank: the despised poor stranger possesses authentic sacred knowledge, while the wealthy pretender does not. Christian editors could read this as a morality tale, yet stranger-disguise and reciprocal hospitality are deeply embedded in Andean sacred geography. Its exact pre-colonial form cannot be reconstructed.

### The five eggs and five falcons

The five eggs hatch as five falcons. The falcons become persons and begin their movement toward the mountain. “Birth” here is emergence from a named landscape, closer to a *paqarina* origin than ordinary biological delivery.

The number five recurs in:

- five eggs;
- five falcons/persons;
- the five-day warning before destruction;
- the five-day interval associated with older resurrection traditions;
- fivefold spatial or social readings advanced by scholars.

A center-plus-four-directions cosmology is possible, but the manuscript never explicitly diagrams Pariacaca as a compass. Treating the five as proof of a universal Andean quincunx is **[Modern structural interpretation]**.

### Destruction of Huayquihusa

Pariacaca travels in the appearance of a poor stranger to a community celebrating with heavy drinking. Everyone neglects him except one woman, who gives him a large vessel of chicha. He warns her that in five days he will destroy the settlement and orders her to leave with her children without disclosing the warning.

He then overwhelms Huayquihusa with rain and a *lloqlla*—a violent flood, mudflow, or debris flow. The hospitable woman survives. The story establishes his capacity to reward reciprocity and punish collective refusal, but it also plainly depicts mass divine violence.

The episode resembles flood and hospitality tales from many traditions. A direct borrowing from Noah, Sodom, or a European saint legend has not been demonstrated. Colonial Christian narrative expectations may have affected its written form, while destructive Andean rainfall provides an immediate local referent.

### Pariacaca and Chuquisuso

Pariacaca encounters Chuquisuso weeping because insufficient water prevents her from irrigating her people’s fields. He desires her and offers water in exchange for intimacy. She does not simply submit: she negotiates successive improvements—water supply, canal works, and durable benefit for her community—before accepting him.

The pair establish irrigation and become petrified or permanently localized in the landscape. The story explains water rights, canal construction, reciprocal obligations, and the sacred authority of a female water being. It is therefore simultaneously erotic narrative, irrigation charter, and topographic memory.

Modern summaries that call Chuquisuso merely Pariacaca’s “wife” erase her bargaining power and independent cult.

### The rescued child and battle with Huallallo

A father approaches carrying a child, coca, *mullu*, and ritual substances. He intends to give the child to Huallallo as food. One of the Pariacacas tells him to take the child home, surrender the offerings, and return in five days to witness the combat.

Pariacaca attacks Huallallo with water, rain, and storm; Huallallo answers with fire. Their conflict transforms the terrain, producing or explaining lakes, scorched rock, mountain formations, and displaced populations. Pariacaca wins and drives Huallallo toward the Antis. Pariacarco remains stationed as a guard against his return.

The manuscript contrasts Huallallo’s human child offering with Pariacaca’s acceptance of camelids and other goods. This does not prove that Pariacaca’s historical cult never participated in Inca human sacrifice. It proves only that this local narrative represents him as stopping one child from being sacrificed to his rival.

### Establishment of the cult

After victory Pariacaca orders worship and institutes festivals. Three ritual complexes are described:

- **Auquisna**, “for our father,” associated with Pariacaca and pilgrimage to his sanctuary;
- **Chaycosna**, “for our mother,” associated with Chaupiñamca;
- **Chanco**, a dance later made to coincide with the Catholic feast of Saint Andrew in November.

Ritual included dancing, singing, chicha, coca, animal offerings, races, and selection or naming of prized llamas. Failure to dance or participate was an offense. Different communities held defined positions in the pilgrimage and maintained specialized ritual personnel, including *huacsas*.

### Conquests of the children

Pariacaca’s sons advance through Yunca territory. Tutayquiri is especially formidable but is stopped by Chuquisuso, preserving limits within the new order. The conquests explain community distributions, land claims, water arrangements, and why particular local shrines acknowledge Pariacaca.

Scholars since María Rostworowski have read these myths alongside evidence for Yauyo migration and conflict with Yunca-speaking populations. The broad correspondence is credible; individual divine campaigns cannot be converted directly into dated military events.

### Pariacaca, Macahuisa, and Túpac Inca Yupanqui

In chapter 23, Túpac Inca Yupanqui faces rebellion and summons the empire’s powerful *huacas*. His authority to do so is linked to his status as the Sun’s son and therefore himself a sacred being. Pachacamac and many other *huacas* attend or are represented.

The assembly is reluctant. Pariacaca asks who will help; Macahuisa volunteers. When the Inca offers ordinary food, Macahuisa demands *mullu*. He then defeats the rebels through rain, lightning, or inundation so extreme that the enemies are annihilated or forced into submission.

The story presents an alliance rather than total Inca theological domination. The emperor needs provincial sacred power; Pariacaca retains autonomy and sends a son on negotiated terms. The episode may preserve how imperial rule incorporated regional oracles and turned their cult personnel and myths toward state legitimacy.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Sanctuary and sacred landscape

Pariacaca’s cult center was not a single enclosed masonry temple of the Greco-Roman type. It was a high-altitude sacred complex integrating:

- the twin-peaked or multi-summited snowy massif;
- the pass and Inca road;
- lakes and springs;
- caves, including the place recorded in missionary sources;
- offering places;
- sacred stones;
- high-puna pastures;
- routes and stations used by pilgrims.

The Xauxa–Pachacamac road crossed the Pariacaca region at elevations approaching 4,800 meters. It connected Hatun Xauxa in the Mantaro highlands with the coastal oracle of Pachacamac. Sections preserve paving, retaining walls, steps cut or laid into slopes, and way stations. Spanish observers marveled particularly at the Pariacaca stairway.

Archaeological survey supports intensive Inca investment in the route, but some paths and settlements were earlier. Calling every visible structure “the temple of Pariacaca” is unjustified.

### Oracle

Pariacaca could answer political and ritual questions through shrine attendants and material embodiments. Astuhuamán argues that the Inca state integrated this oracle into a broader imperial system without eliminating its local genealogies. The oracle’s prestige would help mediate between state, local communities, pastoralists, and travelers.

### Priests and ritual personnel

The manuscript and extirpation literature identify:

- *huacsas*, performers or ritual representatives attached to communities;
- custodians of sacred objects;
- diviners and speakers for *huacas*;
- community authorities responsible for mobilizing offerings;
- pilgrims assigned by ethnic or local group;
- retainers or servants sent under Inca authority.

The exact hierarchy of Pariacaca’s priesthood is not fully recoverable. Spanish visitors often translated multiple offices as *sacerdote*, *hechicero*, or *ministro de ídolos*, flattening distinctions.

### Pilgrimage

The annual Auquisna pilgrimage brought participants to Pariacaca. The journey included rapid movement or ritual running, dancing, offerings, competitive activity, and prescribed community roles. The shrine stood within a two-directional system: highland peoples could travel toward Pachacamac, while coastal or western-valley travelers moved toward Pariacaca.

This circulation moved more than belief. It organized exchange in camelids, shell, coca, cloth, food, labor, and information.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Securely documented offerings include:

- llamas and alpacas;
- guinea pigs;
- coca;
- chicha;
- *mullu* shell;
- cloth or fine garments;
- prepared ritual substances;
- animal blood;
- food and drink;
- service, dance, and pilgrimage.

#### Human sacrifice

The dossier requires a strict distinction:

1. **Mythic claim:** Huallallo demands and consumes children; Pariacaca saves one intended victim and substitutes camelid/other offerings. This is securely in the *Huarochirí Manuscript*.

2. **Imperial context:** Inca *capacocha* ceremonies involved human victims, including children, and major provincial *huacas* could be linked to imperial sacrifice. Molina, Cobo, archaeological discoveries, and Cajatambo testimony establish the broader institution.

3. **Pariacaca-specific historical cult:** No source examined securely documents an excavated human victim demonstrably sacrificed at Pariacaca’s own sanctuary. It is therefore unwarranted either to assert confidently that his priests practiced human sacrifice or to use the rescued-child story as proof that they never did.

There is no evidence of “temple prostitution” at Pariacaca. Sexual episodes in myth and colonial allegations about festival behavior are not evidence for an institution of sacred prostitution.

### Funerary practice

The 1609 letter’s account of worshippers bringing their dead or representations of the dead to a great cave links Pariacaca with ancestor cult and postmortem return. The manuscript also associates the mountain with the destination of the deceased. Colonial authorities treated such practices as idolatry and sought to move bodies into church-controlled cemeteries.

### Cult economy and politics

The sanctuary drew labor, animals, cloth, shell, coca, and other valuables. Inca incorporation probably entailed road maintenance, designated service groups, storehouses, and state participation in offerings. The chapter 23 narrative legitimizes reciprocal political theology: the Inca honors the *huaca* and supplies its preferred offering; the *huaca* supplies military weather power.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before Inca rule

**Evidence:** landscape archaeology, settlement patterns, later oral traditions.

The mountain and water system were almost certainly sacred before Inca conquest. Pariacaca’s precise age cannot be archaeologically established because no securely labeled pre-Inca image or inscription survives. The myths remember conflict among populations conventionally described as Yauyos and Yuncas. Rostworowski interpreted Pariacaca’s victory as a sacred rendering of Yauyo expansion into western valleys.

### Inca expansion, approximately fifteenth century

The Inca conquered Huarochirí during the expansion conventionally associated with Pachacuti and Túpac Inca Yupanqui. They developed or monumentalized the Xauxa–Pachacamac road and integrated major provincial shrines.

Pariacaca was neither simply suppressed nor reduced to a local household spirit. The state recognized his oracle, assigned service, and incorporated him into an imperial assembly of *huacas*. The Macahuisa narrative expresses this negotiated relationship.

The claim that Pariacaca became one of “the four supreme gods of the Inca Empire” is **[Modern systematization]**; colonial evidence does not provide such a fixed ranking.

### First Spanish decades, 1530s–1570s

Spanish travel preserved the mountain and pass name. Cieza’s notice shows continuity of the toponym and the road’s fame. Missionization, *reducción* settlements, seizure of cult property, and Catholic parish organization disrupted the public cult, but evidence from 1609 proves it survived.

### Late sixteenth-century documentation

Albornoz’s investigations catalogued major *huacas* and their personnel. The *Huarochirí Manuscript* was compiled amid sharpening conflict between Indigenous communities and ecclesiastical authority. It preserves the richest record precisely because the cult was being investigated for elimination.

### Ávila and the first major Huarochirí campaign, 1608–1611

Francisco de Ávila publicly denounced Huarochirí idolatry in 1608–1609. His career is complicated: local parishioners had earlier accused him of abuses, and scholars debate how his anti-idolatry initiative related to those proceedings. Indigenous informants and opponents acted within a coercive colonial field, not as passive repositories.

Jesuit reports state that the Pariacaca sanctuary remained active. In 1611 missionaries and officials destroyed its central image or sacred stones and attacked associated ritual places. Sacred objects were confiscated or burned; specialists were prosecuted; Christian crosses and instruction were imposed.

### Seventeenth-century suppression and persistence

The campaigns associated with Ávila, Gonzalo de Ocampo, Pedro de Villagómez, and regional visitors did not produce immediate extinction. Huarochirí and comparative Cajatambo trials show:

- hidden sacred objects;
- worship of fragments or ashes;
- replacement stones;
- ritual under Catholic festival cover;
- continued reverence for ancestors and mountains;
- use of rosaries or Christian observances as concealment;
- punishment, imprisonment, whipping, public penance, and forced instruction.

“Syncretism” should not imply a peaceful blending chosen under equal conditions. Catholic–Andean combinations developed amid coercion, adaptation, strategic concealment, and genuine religious creativity.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Direct references to an organized Pariacaca priesthood become scarce. The mountain remained a landmark, pasture guardian, weather power, and local identity marker. Road use and arriería sustained knowledge of the landscape even after the imperial pilgrimage institution disappeared.

Absence from clerical texts is not proof of absence from local practice, but neither is it evidence of uninterrupted institutional continuity.

### Twentieth-century recovery

Major milestones include:

- archaeological and ethnohistorical study of the Inca road;
- José María Arguedas’s 1966 Spanish translation, *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí*;
- Gerald Taylor’s Quechua philological editions;
- Frank Salomon and George Urioste’s 1991 Quechua–English edition;
- Rostworowski’s work on Yauyo–Yunca relations and regional religious politics;
- Duviols’s archival research on extirpation;
- modern cultural-landscape surveys by Peruvian institutions.

These publications transformed Pariacaca from an obscure entry in colonial chronicles into one of the best-documented Andean sacred beings.

### Twenty-first-century living practice

Government cultural-landscape work in Tanta and Huachipampa documents living pastoral rites in which the Apu Pariacaca receives libations and animal blood during *herranza*. Participants also celebrate Catholic mass, keep vigils for Santiago or other saints, use music, coca, alcohol, and family ritual offices, and bury clipped ear-marks, ribbons, grain, sweets, and coins in corrals. This is **[Living Andean practice]**, not a pristine survival of chapter 9.

The Ministry of Culture and SERNANP promote the mountain, Inca road, and reserve as cultural heritage and tourism resources. Organized treks and ceremonial visits are sometimes called “pilgrimages.” Evidence supports continuing reverence and ritual orientation toward the mountain; it does not demonstrate restoration of the entire pre-Hispanic priesthood or festival calendar.

The item-specific suggestion of present Pariacaca practice in Cusco, the Bolivian altiplano, or northwest Argentina could not be substantiated as a distinct continuous cult. General reverence for *apus*, Pachamama, Santiago, and mountain beings is widespread in those regions, but that is not evidence that the named Pariacaca historically possessed active cult centers there. Claims of continental worship should therefore be labeled **[Unverified]**.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, literature, and art

Arguedas’s *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí* made the narratives central to Peruvian literary and anthropological discussion. Subsequent dramatizations, illustrated adaptations, school texts, visual art, and comics often anthropomorphize Pariacaca as a heroic weather warrior. These works transmit the manuscript but also stabilize an appearance that the manuscript itself leaves fluid.

“Ecos de Huarochirí,” produced within Peru’s scholarly and cultural sphere, explicitly treats modern visual reconstruction as imaginative engagement with territory, number five, and manuscript narrative—not as the discovery of ancient canonical portraits.

### Heritage and tourism

Pariacaca now functions as:

- a regional emblem for Yauyos and Huarochirí;
- a symbol of the Nor Yauyos-Cochas landscape;
- a focus for Qhapaq Ñan conservation;
- a destination for trekking and “mystical tourism”;
- a marker of Indigenous ecological identity.

Tourist descriptions often state that a single ceremonial road ran directly from Pachacamac to Pariacaca exclusively for pilgrimage. The route certainly connected the sanctuaries and carried pilgrims, but it was also administrative, economic, and military infrastructure.

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search found no well-documented historical Pariacaca current in Renaissance Hermeticism, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, European ceremonial magic, or established twentieth-century grimoire traditions. Online invocations or deity lists that assign him elemental correspondences are **[Modern invention]**.

Contemporary Andean spirituality and Indigenous cultural renewal may invoke Apu Pariacaca. These practices should be described by their practitioners’ own identities rather than automatically categorized as “neopagan,” a term developed primarily in Euro-American religious history.

### Psychological and New Age readings

Pariacaca has occasionally been interpreted as:

- an archetype of the victorious storm father;
- a symbol of ecological reciprocity;
- the integration of five cosmic directions;
- a Jungian movement from fragmented multiplicity to wholeness.

None of those formulations occurs in the colonial sources. They are modern interpretive systems.

### Nationalist and political use

Pariacaca’s myths can be read as an Indigenous charter of resistance, Yauyo territorial identity, or pre-Hispanic national unity. Each emphasis selects part of the evidence:

- the stories do preserve anti-Yunca conquest, not universal harmony;
- Inca incorporation involved both recognition and political appropriation;
- colonial preservation occurred within an extirpation project;
- modern heritage discourse often turns a contested sacred landscape into a national asset.

No documented extremist organization centered on Pariacaca was located.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Nature of the manuscript

The central debate concerns how to read the *Huarochirí Manuscript*:

- as a repository of pre-Hispanic mythology;
- as a colonial Indigenous history;
- as evidence collected for extirpation;
- as a partly Christianized didactic work;
- as a polyphonic compilation preserving rival local voices.

The best practice is to treat it as all of these in different proportions. Its colonial production does not make its traditions fictitious, but neither may its prose be projected unchanged into the fifteenth century.

### Ávila’s role

Older editions sometimes called the text “Francisco de Ávila’s manuscript.” Modern scholarship emphasizes its anonymous Quechua authorship and multiple Indigenous sources. Ávila possessed or commissioned the collection and used local knowledge in anti-idolatry work, but there is no basis for treating him as sole author.

### Myth as migration history

Rostworowski and later scholars relate Pariacaca’s victory to Yauyo expansion into Yunca territories. Supporting evidence includes:

- explicit ethnic geography in the manuscript;
- conquest by Pariacaca’s children;
- redistribution of water and land;
- remembered opposition between highlands and western valleys;
- settlement archaeology.

The limitation is chronological precision. Myths can combine events separated by generations and legitimate later property arrangements.

### Imperial oracle thesis

Astuhuamán’s archaeological and ethnohistorical synthesis treats Pariacaca as an oracle integrated into Inca imperial strategy. The road, shrine complex, state service, and Macahuisa episode support this. “Imperial oracle” describes a historical phase, not Pariacaca’s original essence.

### Christian influence

Floods, five-day disappearances, poor divine strangers, moralized hospitality, and opposition between true and false sacred powers invited Christian comparison. Direct dependence is difficult to prove because Andean environments and religious traditions independently supplied floods, sacred poverty, reciprocity, petrification, and successive ages.

A claim such as “Pariacaca is an Andean version of Noah” is reductive. Conversely, claiming the manuscript is uncontaminated pre-Christian scripture ignores its production under colonial evangelization.

### Human sacrifice

Popular summaries frequently say either that Pariacaca abolished human sacrifice or that he demanded it as an Inca god. The evidence supports neither blanket proposition. The manuscript has him stop a sacrifice demanded by Huallallo and institute camelid offerings. General Inca evidence establishes human *capacocha*, but the Pariacaca sanctuary lacks a securely identified victim.

### Iconography

Modern illustrations have created a recognizable heroic image, sometimes with five heads, golden armor, a lightning weapon, or condor motifs. No primary artifact establishes this iconography. The securely attested forms remain eggs, falcons, persons, poor traveler, stones, storm, and mountain.

### Name and linguistic history

The Quechua etymology remains uncertain, and the region’s linguistic history includes Quechua, now poorly documented Yauyo varieties, possible Aymaran influence, and Spanish transcription. Exact phonological reconstruction from *Pariacaca* alone is hazardous.

### Archive and editions

Important repositories and editions include:

- the manuscript associated with Ávila’s papers in the Biblioteca Nacional de España;
- Arguedas, *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí* (1966);
- Gerald Taylor’s Quechua editions and translations;
- Salomon and Urioste, *The Huarochirí Manuscript* (1991);
- Duviols’s editions of extirpation documents;
- Peruvian ecclesiastical and civil archives containing Huarochirí trial materials;
- archaeological and cultural-landscape records held by Peru’s Ministry of Culture.

### Discredited or unsupported ideas still circulating

- Pariacaca was a universally worshipped “supreme Inca creator god.”
- His five forms prove five heads or five physical bodies in every manifestation.
- He was simply another name for Viracocha or Illapa.
- All modern *apu* worship is unchanged Inca religion.
- Santiago transparently replaced Pariacaca everywhere.
- A surviving statue or standardized portrait of Pariacaca has been identified.
- He had an ancient zodiac sign, occult seal, elemental correspondence table, or tarot attribution.
- A named continuous Pariacaca cult is documented throughout Bolivia and northwest Argentina.
