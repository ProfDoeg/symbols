# Illapa: Research Dossier

> **Class:** a male divine power/god in the imperial Inca state religion; more broadly, *illapa* denotes lightning, thunder, and the thunderbolt as a connected atmospheric complex. In regional traditions the corresponding power could be a named *huaca*, ancestral being, mountain lord, or a plurality of thunder persons rather than one universal anthropomorphic god.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Pedro Cieza de León’s *Crónica del Perú* (published 1553, based on inquiries of the late 1540s) securely records *illapa* as the Andean name applied to the thunder-like Spanish arquebus; this is early lexical evidence, not a complete description of a god. The earliest surviving explicit descriptions of Illapa as a cult power belong to Polo de Ondegardo’s reports of the 1560s, followed by Cristóbal de Molina’s *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los incas* (c. 1573–1575). No securely identified pre-Hispanic inscription names Illapa: the Inca did not leave alphabetic religious texts, and proposed pre-Inca images of a “thunder god” remain scholarly identifications rather than inscribed portraits.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY]** Secure early-colonial testimony based substantially on surviving Inca or immediately post-conquest practice.
- **[COLONIAL]** Later sixteenth- or seventeenth-century description, elaboration, or retrospective report.
- **[REGIONAL]** A local Andean tradition that must not automatically be equated with the Cusco state god.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** A chronicler’s, missionary’s, or scholar’s attempt to organize variable evidence into a coherent pantheon.
- **[SYNCRETISM]** A historically produced identification between Illapa and another sacred figure.
- **[FOLKLORE/LIVING]** Ethnographically documented colonial or modern practice.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern scholarly inference.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A recent claim for which no ancient or colonial evidence has been demonstrated.
- **[UNESTABLISHED]** A circulating assertion not securely supported by the inspected record.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[EARLY]** Illapa is the Quechua atmospheric power encompassing lightning seen, thunder heard, and the thunderbolt striking the earth. Inca Garcilaso de la Vega explains the semantics particularly clearly in *Comentarios reales* I.II.21 and I.III.21 (published 1609): “*viste la Illapa*” meant the flash, “*oíste la Illapa*” the thunder, and “*la Illapa cayó*” the destructive bolt. His formulation demonstrates that *Illapa* could denote the phenomenon collectively rather than only a human-shaped god. Garcilaso, however, denies that the Inca regarded it as a god, calling the phenomena servants of the Sun. This directly conflicts with Cobo, Molina, Acosta, Guaman Poma, and the ritual record, which assign the thunder power independent images, sacrifices, priests, and very high rank. Garcilaso’s solar subordination is therefore testimony to one learned colonial interpretation, not a neutral consensus. The relevant Garcilaso passage is preserved in a complete modern PDF of the first part of the *Comentarios*.

**[EARLY/COLONIAL]** Molina calls the cult image *Chuqui Ylla Yllapa* and identifies it as “the huaca of the lightning.” José de Acosta, writing in the 1580s and publishing in 1590, reports three names—*Chuquilla*, *Catuilla*, and *Intiillapa*—and ranks Thunder after Viracocha and the Sun. Bernabé Cobo repeats a similar hierarchy in *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (completed 1653), saying that Thunder received the third degree of veneration after Viracocha and the Sun. “Third” is a chroniclers’ ranking of the Cusco state cult, not proof that every Andean community shared a fixed pan-Andean hierarchy.

### Names and spellings

- **Illapa, Yllapa, Ilyapa, Ilyap’a.** **[EARLY]** Colonial orthographic variants of Quechua *illapa*. Standardized modern Southern Quechua normally writes *illapa*. The primary meaning is “lightning/thunderbolt,” with context distinguishing flash, sound, or strike.
- **Apu Illapa.** **[COLONIAL/REGIONAL]** *Apu* means lord or powerful person/being. “Lord Illapa” is an honorific, not demonstrably the single official name of the Cusco cult in all periods.
- **Chuqui Illa / Chuquiylla / Chuqui Ylla Yllapa.** **[EARLY]** Molina’s name for the Coricancha-associated lightning *huaca*. *Chuki/chuqui* can evoke a lance, precious metal, or shining object in colonial vocabularies; translations such as “golden lance,” “noble thunderbolt,” or “lance of lightning” are interpretive and should not be treated as a settled etymology.
- **Inti Illapa / Intiillapa.** **[COLONIAL]** Literally “Sun-Illapa” or “solar thunder”; given by Acosta among the thunder power’s names. It may reflect state-theological subordination to Inti rather than a universally distinct person.
- **Catu Illapa / Catuilla / Katoylla.** **[COLONIAL]** One of Acosta’s reported names. Its precise parsing and relation to a threefold scheme are disputed.
- **Yayan Illapa, Chaupi Churin Illapa, Sullca Churin Illapa.** **[COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]** Guaman Poma presents “father Illapa,” “middle-son Illapa,” and “younger-son Illapa” in a threefold arrangement. Christian Trinitarian vocabulary and Guaman Poma’s polemical purposes make it unsafe to project this exact system unchanged into remote antiquity.
- **Illapa camayoc.** **[COLONIAL]** A specialist connected with thunder or, after conquest, an artilleryman; the semantic transfer illustrates how firearms entered the word’s range.
- **Yllapa cancha / Illapa Kancha.** **[COLONIAL]** “Enclosure of Illapa,” reported as a Cusco place or cult precinct. Its exact location and architectural history remain uncertain.
- **Pucamarca.** **[COLONIAL]** Molina associates the lightning image with a cult place called Pucamarca. It is not securely recoverable as a surviving dedicated “Temple of Illapa.”
- **Illapa as firearm/artillery.** **[EARLY LEXICAL TRANSFER]** Domingo de Santo Tomás’s 1560 Quechua lexicon includes *yllapa* vocabulary for artillery, while Cieza records the comparison between an arquebus and heavenly thunder. This does not mean pre-conquest Illapa originally governed guns.
- **Illapa as royal mummy bundle.** **[EARLY/COLONIAL]** Albornoz and Guaman Poma use *illapa* for the preserved body or bundle of an Inca or eminent ancestor, beside the more general *mallqui*. The usage connects potent dead rulers, shining regalia, ancestral efficacy, and control of water/weather, but the precise semantic path remains debated.

### Regional names: related, not interchangeable by default

**[REGIONAL]** Colonial investigators encountered numerous thunder powers:

- **Libiac/Lliviac** among Llacuaz-identified communities of Cajatambo and the central highlands.
- **Yana Raman** and related names among Yaro/Llacuaz traditions.
- **Pariacaca** in Huarochirí and Yauyos: a mountain and storm *huaca* with his own myths, kin, pilgrimage, and territorial cult.
- **Catequil/Catequilla** in Huamachuco, Cajamarca, and farther north.
- **Apocatequil**, a learned or regional northern form associated with Catequil.
- **Tumayricapac** in central-highland traditions.
- **Pusikaqcha/Pusikajcha** in southern accounts.
- **Tunupa/Thunupa** in the Aymara-speaking south, where storm, volcano, creator, apostolic traveler, and culture-hero traditions overlap.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** Calling all these beings “regional forms of Illapa” is useful shorthand for comparison but historically dangerous. Inca administrators may have incorporated local weather cults into an imperial category; missionaries later grouped them as instances of “the thunder”; modern pantheon lists complete that homogenization. Pariacaca and Catequil, in particular, possess narrative identities that cannot be reduced to aliases of Illapa.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What can and cannot be early

No pre-Hispanic Quechua alphabetic manuscript survives, and no image bears a contemporary caption “Illapa.” Archaeology demonstrates Inca sacrifice, storm symbolism, mountain shrines, slings, precious-metal cult objects, and regional thunder worship, but it cannot by itself securely attach the proper name *Illapa* to an individual artifact.

1. **Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú* (1553).** **[EARLY]** Records the thunder-like arquebus and the application of *illapa* to Spanish weapons. His account of the lord of Tumbes pouring maize beer into an arquebus and addressing it through its thunder-like noise has been interpreted by Sabine MacCormack and later scholars as engagement with the animating power of Illapa. The interpretation is plausible, but Cieza does not there give a systematic biography of a god.

2. **Domingo de Santo Tomás, *Lexicón o vocabulario de la lengua general del Perú* (1560).** **[EARLY LEXICAL]** Records *yllapa* and derivatives in the firearm/artillery semantic field, confirming rapid post-conquest transfer.

3. **Polo de Ondegardo, reports of c. 1561–1571.** **[EARLY]** Describes offerings to the Creator, Sun, and Thunder and supplies early evidence for the state ritual hierarchy. His reports were administrative and evangelizing documents, not disinterested ethnography.

4. **Cristóbal de Molina, *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los incas* (c. 1573–1575).** **[EARLY]** The strongest connected account of Cusco ritual. Molina was a capable Quechua speaker and questioned elderly Cusco informants. He names *Chuqui Ylla Yllapa*, places its image with Punchao (the Sun) and Pachayachachi (the Maker), records animal and human offerings, prayers for water, and calendrical processions. The surviving text is colonial testimony gathered under church authority, not a pre-conquest liturgy copied verbatim. A digitized historical edition is available through the CSIC.

5. **Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia Índica* (1572).** **[EARLY/SYSTEMATIZATION]** Associates the thunder image with Pachacuti’s political and military transformation of Cusco. Sarmiento wrote for Viceroy Toledo’s legitimating project; his chronology and royal narratives require source criticism.

6. **Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú* and service reports (chiefly 1570s–1580s).** **[EARLY/EXTIRPATION]** Names thunder cults, *illapa*, regional *huacas*, ritual specialists, and the application of *illapa* to royal ancestral bodies. His evidence is invaluable but produced for detecting and destroying Indigenous religion.

7. **José de Acosta, *Historia natural y moral de las Indias* (1590).** **[COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]** Ranks Thunder after Viracocha and Sun and gives the names Chuquilla, Catuilla, and Intiillapa. Acosta synthesizes other reports and interprets them through Christian natural and theological categories.

8. **Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales* I (1609).** **[COLONIAL COUNTER-VERSION]** Describes *illapa* as flash, sound, and bolt, provides the heavenly-maiden-and-water-jar story, and places a chamber for lightning, thunder, and thunderbolt in the Sun temple. He insists they were servants of the Sun rather than gods. His elegant solar theology conflicts with ritual evidence and likely reflects apologetic harmonization.

9. **Huarochirí Manuscript (Quechua; compiled c. 1598–1608).** **[REGIONAL PRIMARY CORPUS]** Preserved in Madrid, BNE MS 3169, and translated by Gerald Taylor and by Frank Salomon and George Urioste. It records Huarochirí’s local *huacas*, notably Pariacaca and his sons, rather than a canonical Cusco Illapa cycle. *Sullca Illapa* appears as one of Pariacaca’s sons in chapter 16. This is the relevant “Apu Illapa of Huarochirí” thread: Huarochirí preserves storm beings and an Illapa-named member of Pariacaca’s kin, but it does not simply retell a standard biography of the imperial god. The manuscript itself arose amid Francisco de Ávila’s anti-idolatry work.

10. **Guaman Poma, *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (c. 1615–1616).** **[INDIGENOUS COLONIAL]** Supplies triadic Illapa terminology, royal-mummy usage, images, ritual calendar information, and a denunciatory catalogue of *illapa*, *huacas*, divination, and ritual practices. The autograph manuscript, GKS 2232 4°, is available in the Danish Royal Library’s complete facsimile and transcription.

11. **Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621).** **[COLONIAL/EXTIRPATION]** A manual for identifying and suppressing living Indigenous cults. It documents thunder-linked specialists, offerings, ancestor bundles, twins, lightning-struck persons, and clandestine rites, but filters them through the category of demonic “idolatry.”

12. **Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (1653).** **[COLONIAL SYNTHESIS]** Gives the most influential full description: third-ranked cult, celestial warrior or star figure, shining clothes, club and sling, and water derived from the celestial river. Cobo used earlier materials, including a now-lost description behind the Cusco *ceque* list. Roland Hamilton’s *Inca Religion and Customs* is the standard English selection.

13. **Huarochirí and Cajatambo idolatry proceedings, seventeenth century.** **[REGIONAL JUDICIAL PRIMARY SOURCES]** Testimony—often coerced—records Libiac, lightning specialists, ancestor cults, sacrifices, clandestine removal of Christian burials, and local *huacas*. Pierre Duviols’s *Procesos y visitas de idolatrías: Cajatambo, siglo XVII* publishes a major corpus. Statements must be read as evidence given under threat, not transparent transcripts of free religious testimony.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Powers

**[EARLY/COLONIAL]** Illapa controls rain-bearing storms, thunder, lightning, hail, and destructive bolts. The same force sustains highland agriculture and can ruin it. Prayers ask *Chuqui Illapa* to send water so crops may grow. This agricultural role is better attested than the modern generic label “god of war.”

**[EARLY]** Military associations arise from the sling, club, thunderous destructive power, state processions, and a thunder image connected to Pachacuti as protective *huauque* (“brother,” ritual double). It is reasonable to call Illapa a state military protector, but “war god” as a separate bureaucratic portfolio is a modern pantheon-style abstraction.

**[COLONIAL/REGIONAL]** Lightning could select ritual specialists. Persons struck and surviving, twins, breech births, and persons marked as extraordinary were associated in different regions with Thunder. Some testimony records children with unusual births being offered to a thunder power; the evidence is regional and cannot be generalized to all Inca society.

### Sling, club, and celestial warrior

**[COLONIAL]** Cobo describes a humanlike celestial figure wearing bright or shining clothing, with a club in the left hand and sling in the right. The movement or brilliance of the garments produces lightning; the sling’s crack produces thunder; the projectile is the falling bolt. Later scholars have compared the stellar figure with Orion or another prominent constellation. The identification with Orion is **[RECONSTRUCTION]**, not stated by Cobo in modern astronomical coordinates.

### The Milky Way and celestial water

**[COLONIAL]** Cobo says the Inca regarded the Milky Way as a great celestial river from which Thunder took the water poured onto the earth. This supplies the documented “Milky Way’s water” thread.

**[COLONIAL VARIANT]** Garcilaso gives a related but different etiological account: the Maker placed a royal maiden in heaven with a water jar; her brother breaks the jar at the proper time, producing thunder, lightning, bolt, and rain. The maiden and brother are not securely named Illapa and Mama Cocha in Garcilaso’s text. Modern summaries that turn the unnamed maiden into Illapa’s named sister or wife, or identify her automatically with Mama Quilla or Mama Cocha, are **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION/UNESTABLISHED]**.

### Material images

**[EARLY]** Molina names a *huaca* image *Chuqui Ylla Yllapa* carried with Punchao and Pachayachachi. Other accounts describe a gold or precious-metal thunder idol and a lightning-linked ritual double of Pachacuti. Most state-cult metal images were seized or melted after conquest; no surviving museum object is universally accepted as the original Coricancha Illapa.

**[INDIGENOUS COLONIAL IMAGE]** Juan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui’s early-seventeenth-century Coricancha diagram labels a lightning complex (*chuqiylla/yllapa*) alongside Sun, Moon, stars, rainbow, earth, and river. The drawing is colonial testimony about remembered temple cosmology, not a photographed copy of the pre-conquest wall.

**[RECONSTRUCTION]** The central staff-bearing figure on Tiwanaku’s Gateway of the Sun has sometimes been called Illapa or an Aymara equivalent. There is no inscription establishing that name, and direct identification with the late Inca Illapa is methodologically unsound.

### Animals, colors, objects, and dates

- **Sling and slingstone:** **[COLONIAL, SECURE].**
- **Club or mace:** **[COLONIAL, SECURE].**
- **Shining garments, often interpreted as silver:** **[COLONIAL; metal/color varies by retelling].**
- **Water vessel:** **[COLONIAL VARIANT]**, especially Garcilaso’s unnamed heavenly maiden.
- **Serpent or double-headed serpent:** **[DISPUTED]**. Lightning and serpents are broadly associated in Andean art, and a Pachacuti-linked talisman is described as serpentine, but not every Andean zigzag or serpent is an image of Illapa.
- **Feline manifestation:** **[REGIONAL/FOLKLORE]**, appearing in later central/southern traditions; not the standard Cusco state icon.
- **July 25/Santiago’s feast:** **[SYNCRETISM]**, a Christian calendrical focus rather than a known pre-Hispanic “birthday” of Illapa.
- **Fixed sacred color, number, planet, or weekday:** **[UNESTABLISHED]**. Modern lists assigning these without a colonial citation are inventions or esoteric correspondences.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

Illapa lacks a single stable genealogy. That absence is substantive.

### Genealogical versions

1. **Garcilaso’s solar model.** **[COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]** Illapa’s three atmospheric effects are servants of Inti, not independent gods. The water-bearing maiden is a daughter of an unnamed king, and an unnamed brother breaks her jar.

2. **Guaman Poma’s threefold Illapa.** **[INDIGENOUS COLONIAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]** Yayan Illapa, Chaupi Churin Illapa, and Sullca Churin Illapa are arranged as father, middle son, and younger son. Whether this records an Indigenous senior/middle/junior structure, Christian Trinitarian influence, or both is debated.

3. **Huarochirí.** **[REGIONAL]** Sullca Illapa is a son of Pariacaca. This is not automatically the same genealogy as Guaman Poma’s Cusco triad; *sullca* means junior/younger and could recur structurally.

4. **No secure consort or named children.** **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]** Colonial Cusco sources do not establish Mama Quilla, Mama Cocha, or another familiar goddess as Illapa’s canonical wife. Internet genealogies that do so conflate separate passages.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Inti (Sun)** — Illapa is placed below or beside the Sun in the Cusco state cult; Garcilaso calls lightning, thunder, and bolt Inti’s servants. *Sources:* Polo de Ondegardo, 1560s; Acosta, 1590; Garcilaso, 1609; Cobo, 1653. **[EARLY/COLONIAL; competing classifications]**
- **Viracocha/Pachayachachi (Maker)** — Illapa’s image appears with the Maker and Punchao in Molina’s ritual descriptions; Cobo and Acosta rank the three cults. No narrative conversation or combat is recorded. *Sources:* Molina, c. 1573–1575; Acosta, 1590; Cobo, 1653. **[EARLY/COLONIAL JOINT CULT]**
- **Punchao (solar image)** — *Chuqui Ylla Yllapa* is carried and offered to in the same calendrical complex as Punchao. *Source:* Molina, c. 1573–1575. **[EARLY JOINT PROCESSION]**
- **Pariacaca** — Sullca Illapa is listed as Pariacaca’s son in the Huarochirí corpus. This is the only explicit close kinship involving an Illapa-named being in that regional text. *Source:* *Huarochirí Manuscript*, chapter 16, c. 1598–1608. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Yayan Illapa / Chaupi Churin Illapa / Sullca Churin Illapa** — father-and-sons or senior/middle/junior aspects jointly constitute Guaman Poma’s thunder complex. *Source:* *Nueva corónica*, c. 1615. **[INDIGENOUS COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Unnamed heavenly maiden** — her brother breaks her jar, releasing rain and producing thunder, lightning, and bolt. The brother is often identified as Illapa, but Garcilaso does not supply a stable divine proper name for either sibling in the passage. *Source:* Garcilaso, 1609. **[COLONIAL VARIANT; identification partly reconstructed]**
- **Santiago Apostle** — Santiago becomes the Christian successor, mask, analogue, or transformed continuation of the thunder power. The relationship is historical syncretism, not a pre-Hispanic meeting myth. *Sources:* Garcilaso’s account of the 1536 siege; Murúa; Bertonio’s Aymara vocabulary (1612); extirpation records; later Andean ritual. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Catequil, Libiac, Yana Raman, Tunupa, and other regional thunder beings** — colonial and modern writers compare or equate them with Illapa, but no surviving myth records Illapa personally meeting them. **[REGIONAL EQUATION; ABSENCE OF INTERACTION]**
- **Mama Quilla, Pachamama, Mama Cocha, Kuychi** — they occur in the same cosmological and ritual environments, but no securely inspected early text records a personal Illapa episode with them. **[ABSENCE OF DOCUMENTED INTERACTION]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Illapa has no surviving epic comparable to the Huarochirí cycle of Pariacaca. His “myths” are mostly ritual explanations, royal-political episodes, and colonial transformations.

### The celestial slinger

**[COLONIAL]** Cobo’s fullest version makes Thunder a shining human figure in the sky. The sling’s crack is thunder, the flash of clothing is lightning, and the cast stone is the bolt. Water comes from the celestial river identified with the Milky Way. The narrative explains the linked sensory components of a storm and makes the highland sling a cosmic instrument.

**Development:** The secure form is seventeenth-century Cobo, probably drawing on earlier reports. The often repeated detail that Illapa “shoots his sister’s jar” combines Cobo’s slinger with Garcilaso’s separate sibling-and-jar account and is therefore a **[MODERN COMPOSITE]** unless a specific intermediate source is supplied.

### The heavenly water jar

**[COLONIAL VARIANT]** Garcilaso says the Maker placed a royal maiden in heaven with a jar of water, to pour it when earth needed rain. A brother breaks it at the proper time; the shock yields thunder, flash, bolt, and rain. Garcilaso embeds the story within a solar theology that denies independent godhood to the phenomena.

**Development:** Later handbooks name the brother Illapa and the maiden his sister; some call her Mama Quilla or Mama Cocha. The first identification is plausible contextually, but the named female identity is **[LATE/UNESTABLISHED]**.

### Pachacuti and the thunder image

**[EARLY ROYAL TRADITION]** Molina, Sarmiento, and Cobo preserve partially divergent traditions in which Inca Yupanqui/Pachacuti receives, adopts, or establishes a thunder-linked image as a protective *huauque* or ritual brother. Some accounts associate the object with a serpent or double-headed form and with the victory over the Chanca.

**Dispute:** Betanzos’s early Chanca-war narrative emphasizes a supernatural apparition and an image called *Caccha* rather than using *Illapa*. Treating Caccha, Chuqui Illa, the rainbow, and Illapa as one object is a later synthesis. The story’s political function is clearer than its original form: it ties the ruler who reorganized the empire to a terrifying celestial protector and legitimizes an expanded state cult.

### Illapa and the dead Inca

**[INDIGENOUS COLONIAL]** Guaman Poma calls the deceased Inca an *yllapa*. Albornoz likewise uses the word for potent ancestral bodies. Royal mummies remained socially and ritually active through their *panacas*, estates, servants, appearances, and offerings.

**Interpretation:** The dead ruler did not merely become “the weather god” in a simple apotheosis. *Illapa* here marks an efficacious, preserved, luminous, potentially weather-controlling ancestral presence. The overlap among bolt, precious object, firearm, and royal bundle suggests a semantic field of concentrated dangerous power, but any single original etymological explanation remains **[RECONSTRUCTION]**.

### Santiago at Sunturhuasi

**[COLONIAL CHRISTIAN LEGEND/SYNCRETISM]** Garcilaso’s posthumously published second part narrates Santiago appearing on a white horse during Manco Inca’s 1536 siege of Cusco, wielding a sword that flashed like lightning. Indigenous onlookers supposedly ask who the *Viracocha* is who holds the *illapa*. The episode is Christian miracle historiography written decades later, not contemporary battle reportage.

**Historical consequence:** Horse-hoof thunder, flashing sword, Spanish battle cry, firearms called *illapa*, and Santiago’s European title *Boanerges* (“son of thunder”) made the saint unusually available for Andean reinterpretation. Santiago did not simply erase Illapa: communities could make Santiago a rain giver, mountain traveler, dangerous outsider, patron, or *huaca*.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cusco state cult

**[EARLY/COLONIAL]** Molina places *Chuqui Ylla Yllapa* among the principal images carried in calendrical rites with Punchao and Pachayachachi. Llamas were led around these images and sacrificed; prayers asked Sun to warm and Thunder to send water. The state distributed sacrificial obligations across the empire.

**[COLONIAL]** Cobo says Thunder had the third rank after Viracocha and Sun. This is the source behind the common “third most important Inca god” statement. It should be formulated as “third in Cobo’s reconstruction of the Cusco state cult,” not a measurable empire-wide popularity ranking.

### Coricancha and the “third companion”

**[EARLY/COLONIAL]** The defensible core is that a thunder/lightning image participated in the Coricancha ritual complex and appeared with the Sun and Maker. Garcilaso gives lightning, thunder, and bolt a separate chamber in the Sun temple; Molina groups their *huaca* with Punchao and Pachayachachi.

**[DISPUTED WORDING]** Calling Illapa “Inti’s third companion in the Coricancha” compresses several sources. “Third” comes from the ranked cult; “companion” may reflect the grouping of images or the royal *huauque* institution. The chronicles do not unanimously present a formal office called “Inti’s third companion.”

### Pucamarca and Illapa Cancha

**[COLONIAL]** Molina associates the thunder image with Pucamarca, while Guaman Poma and later traditions refer to an Illapa enclosure. No excavated building is securely established as a freestanding, exclusively Illapa temple bearing an original dedicatory inscription. Spanish urban rebuilding and church construction obscured or destroyed much of the sacred topography.

### Ceques and landscape

**[COLONIAL TEXT + ARCHAEOLOGICAL RECONSTRUCTION]** Cusco’s *ceque* system linked the Coricancha to hundreds of springs, stones, passes, hills, and other *huacas*. Cobo’s list, reconstructed through Brian Bauer’s survey and archival work, identifies 328 shrines on 42 lines. Thunder worship was therefore not confined to one statue; it intersected water sources, mountains, royal kin groups, and calendrical duties.

### Offerings and sacrifice

- **Llamas, coca, maize beer, cloth, feathers, fat, and precious objects:** **[EARLY/COLONIAL, WELL ATTESTED].**
- **Prayer for rain and protection from hail or destructive storms:** **[EARLY/REGIONAL].**
- **Capacocha children:** **[EARLY TEXT + ARCHAEOLOGY].** Molina and other chroniclers say principal powers including the Sun, Maker, Thunder, and major provincial *huacas* received human offerings. Archaeology confirms Inca child sacrifice at high-altitude shrines, although assigning an individual burial specifically to Illapa requires site-specific evidence.
- **Children marked by unusual birth:** **[REGIONAL COLONIAL].** Some extirpation testimony says twins, breech-born children, or other extraordinary births could belong to or be sacrificed to Thunder. This is not evidence for a universal annual policy.
- **Lightning-struck bodies or places:** **[REGIONAL/LIVING].** A strike could mark a place as dangerous and sacred or select a future ritual specialist.

### Priesthood

**[COLONIAL]** Molina mentions priests of Thunder alongside Sun priests. Later documents describe local ministers of Libiac and diviners or healers whose authority came through lightning. No complete administrative roster of a centralized Illapa priesthood survives.

### Political economy

**[RECONSTRUCTION GROUNDED IN SOURCES]** Control of rain ritual, sacrificial herds, temple lands, and royal images connected Illapa’s cult to imperial redistribution. Incorporating provincial thunder *huacas* allowed the state to acknowledge local powers while relocating offerings and representatives toward Cusco. The same structure made cult destruction a political as well as theological act.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the Inca state

**[ARCHAEOLOGICAL/COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION]** Storm, mountain, water, serpent, feline, and staff-bearing imagery long predates the Inca. Local northern and central-highland thunder cults were also older than imperial rule. No surviving pre-Inca label establishes that Tiwanaku, Wari, Moche, or Chavín images were called Illapa. “Illapa was worshipped for thousands of years under the same name” is therefore **[UNESTABLISHED]**.

### Fifteenth to early sixteenth century: imperial formulation

**[EARLY RETROSPECTIVE]** Under Pachacuti and his successors, a Cusco thunder image was incorporated into the highest state ritual, military ideology, the royal-double institution, and empire-wide sacrifice. Local weather beings were not necessarily abolished; the empire could translate or affiliate them while preserving local names.

### 1530s–1560s: conquest and semantic expansion

**[EARLY]** Arquebuses and artillery became *illapa* because they flashed, thundered, and killed at a distance. The 1536 Santiago apparition legend retrospectively fused Christian military victory with lightning language. Cieza’s 1553 record is the earliest secure printed evidence for the term’s post-contact field.

### 1560s–1620s: documentation and suppression

**[EARLY/COLONIAL]** Toledo-era inquiries, Molina, Sarmiento, Albornoz, Acosta, and church councils documented the cult while attacking it. Images were seized, metal objects melted, ritual specialists punished, and offerings criminalized. Taki Onqoy investigations treated reviving *huacas* and Illapa-related practices as threats to both church and colonial rule.

**[DOCUMENTED VIOLENCE]** Extirpation campaigns destroyed *huacas* and ancestor bundles and imposed whipping, public humiliation, confiscation, imprisonment, exile, forced labor, and Christian penance. Judicial testimony from Huarochirí and Cajatambo was often obtained under coercive conditions.

### Seventeenth century: local persistence and Santiago transformation

**[REGIONAL/SYNCRETISM]** Cajatambo proceedings show communities still serving Libiac, hiding ancestor bodies, making offerings, and maintaining ritual specialists. Santiago acquired control over lightning, rain, fertility, herds, and mountains. Missionaries sometimes encouraged the saint as a replacement; Andean actors also appropriated him for purposes not controlled by clergy.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

**[FOLKLORE/LIVING]** Explicit state worship of a Coricancha Illapa did not survive as an intact institution. Thunder traditions persisted in Catholic festivals, healing, divination, pastoral rites, mountain worship, and the language of lightning-selected specialists. Documentary coverage is uneven, so institutional “continuity” should not be overstated.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

**[LIVING]** In Cusco and the southern Peruvian highlands, lightning remains a source of vocation for some *paqos*, *altomisayoq*, and other ritual experts. A person struck and restored may be understood as chosen or remade by the celestial power. A 2025 profile of the Cusco ritual specialist María—struck by lightning in 1943—records a living triadic language of Chuqui Illapa, Cutu Illapa, and Inti Illapa. This is valuable contemporary testimony, not proof that the precise triad is unchanged from pre-conquest Cusco.

**[LIVING/REGIONAL]** In Bolivia and the Argentine northwest, lightning stones, meteorites, animal *illas*, Tata Santiago, and Santiago’s feast can retain weather and fertility meanings. Archaeological work at Pucará de Tilcara notes that “lightning stones” remain associated with Illapa through Santo/Tata Santiago. These are regionally transformed practices, not a unitary “Church of Illapa.”

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship, heritage, and Indigenous revitalization

**[MODERN RECEPTION]** Illapa now appears in museums, school materials, tourist interpretation, Indigenous cultural recovery, visual art, and pan-Andean religious discourse. Such uses often recover a figure suppressed by colonial Christianity, but they also tend to produce a cleaner pantheon with standardized portfolios and family relations.

**[MODERN INVENTION]** Claims that Illapa has a universally fixed sigil, zodiac sign, sacred crystal, chakra, tarot correspondence, weekday, invocation formula, or surviving secret grimoire are not supported by the colonial corpus inspected here.

### Neopagan and esoteric use

**[MODERN, POORLY DOCUMENTED]** Individual reconstructionist, New Age, and eclectic polytheist practitioners invoke Illapa online and in private ritual. No evidence located establishes a large, continuous, separately organized pre-Hispanic Illapa priesthood surviving into the present, or a major international denomination devoted solely to him. Practices advertised as exact “ancient Illapa rituals” should be treated as modern reconstructions unless they document their community transmission.

Illapa has little genuine role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or classical European grimoire traditions. Lists inserting him into those systems are **[MODERN ECLECTICISM]**, not recovered Andean antiquity.

### Art and popular culture

**[MODERN RETELLING]** Contemporary Peruvian animation and fantasy projects increasingly include Illapa beside Inti and Pachamama; the viral project *Ninakami/El regreso de los dioses* is a recent example. Such depictions materially broaden recognition but freely invent character designs and plots.

**[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Commercial art usually depicts a muscular male warrior with sling, club, lightning bolts, and Inca regalia. Only the sling, club, shining clothing, and celestial-warrior outline have clear colonial support; detailed faces, armor, color schemes, and family scenes are modern design.

### Political use

**[MODERN RECEPTION]** Illapa may function as a symbol of Andean pride, decolonization, Indigenous sovereignty, environmental concern, or national heritage. No well-documented major extremist appropriation specific to Illapa was identified in the inspected evidence. Absence here should not be converted into a claim that none exists anywhere.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The source problem

The field is built on hostile or entangled archives. Spaniards recorded religion to govern, convert, rank, or eradicate it. Indigenous and mestizo authors wrote within Christian institutions and colonial genres. “God,” “idol,” “temple,” “priest,” and “sacrifice” are translations that can obscure *huaca*, *camay*, *mallqui*, and relational sacred power.

### Major interpretive lines

- **John H. Rowe** treated Cobo and the chroniclers comparatively and emphasized careful reconstruction of Inca culture at conquest.
- **R. Tom Zuidema** read the Cusco *ceque* system, social organization, calendar, and triadic structures together. His structural reconstructions were foundational but are not direct transcriptions of Inca doctrine.
- **María Rostworowski** emphasized plural regional powers, political uses of cult, and the differences hidden by a uniform “Inca pantheon.”
- **Pierre Duviols** made extirpation records central to reconstructing colonial and pre-Hispanic religion while documenting the machinery of suppression.
- **Sabine MacCormack**, in *Religion in the Andes* (1991), examined how European and Andean religious languages confronted and transformed one another.
- **Irene Silverblatt** situated religion, gender, kinship, and colonial power within Inca and Spanish political projects.
- **Gary Urton** connected celestial observation, the Milky Way, dark-cloud constellations, and living Andean cosmology, while distinguishing ethnography from direct pre-conquest proof.
- **Brian Bauer** tested the textual Cusco *ceque* system through archaeological survey and archival geography.
- **Frank Salomon and George Urioste**, and separately **Gerald Taylor**, established critical access to the Huarochirí Quechua corpus.
- **Ariadna Baulenas** has treated Illapa’s terrestrial, political, economic, and martial competencies as a focused research problem.
- **John Staller and Brian Stross**, editors of *Lightning in the Andes and Mesoamerica* (2013), placed Illapa within a wider archaeological, colonial, linguistic, and ethnographic history of lightning.

### Principal debates

1. **God or servant of the Sun?** Garcilaso says servant; Molina, Acosta, Cobo, ritual specialists, sacrifices, and images support independent cult status. The contradiction should remain visible.

2. **One god or a category?** *Illapa* is simultaneously a name, atmospheric phenomenon, sacred force, image, royal ancestor, and colonial word for firearms. A single character model is inadequate.

3. **Threefold deity or Christianized trinity?** Guaman Poma’s father-and-sons formulation may preserve Indigenous ranked plurality, reflect Christian Trinitarian language, or combine both.

4. **Milky Way myth versus water-jar myth.** Cobo and Garcilaso preserve related but nonidentical explanations. The popular synthesis is later.

5. **Illapa and regional thunder gods.** Imperial translation and colonial classification likely encouraged equation, but Pariacaca, Libiac, Catequil, and Tunupa must retain their local histories.

6. **Illapa and royal mummies.** The word’s application to dead rulers is secure; whether it proves full identification, metaphor, ritual title, or a shared category of concentrated power remains debated.

7. **Iconographic identification.** No surviving pre-Hispanic image is securely captioned. Identifications of Tiwanaku’s staff god, zigzag serpents, felines, or stellar figures as Illapa range from plausible comparison to overreach.

8. **Santiago as replacement or resistance.** Missionary substitution, Indigenous appropriation, coexistence, and transformation all occurred. A simple story of either total Christian victory or unchanged pagan survival is inadequate.

### Lost and surviving evidence

The Coricancha’s precious-metal cult images were removed or melted. Many *huacas*, mummy bundles, and ritual objects were destroyed. Molina’s relation survives; other works attributed to him are lost. The Huarochirí manuscript survives uniquely in Madrid. Guaman Poma’s autograph survives in Copenhagen. Cajatambo proceedings survive principally through the Archivo Arzobispal de Lima and modern editions.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before c. 1400:** **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Long histories of regional Andean storm, mountain, water, and lightning cults; none securely bears an inscribed name “Illapa.”
- **Fifteenth century:** **[EARLY RETROSPECTIVE]** Pachacuti-era traditions place a thunder image or protective double within imperial reorganization and warfare.
- **Late fifteenth–early sixteenth centuries:** **[EARLY RETROSPECTIVE]** Illapa/Chuqui Illa belongs to the upper tier of Cusco state ritual and receives offerings with Sun and Maker.
- **1532–1533:** Spanish conquest introduces firearms whose flash, report, and killing power invite the term *illapa*.
- **1536:** Siege of Cusco; later Christian narratives place Santiago’s lightning-like apparition at Sunturhuasi. **[LATE ACCOUNT OF EVENT]**
- **1553:** Cieza publishes early lexical evidence connecting Andean thunder language and firearms.
- **1560:** Santo Tomás publishes *yllapa*-derived firearm/artillery vocabulary.
- **1560s:** Polo de Ondegardo records Creator–Sun–Thunder offerings and hierarchy.
- **1560s–1570s:** Albornoz’s investigations attack Taki Onqoy and catalog *illapa*, *huacas*, ritual specialists, and regional cults.
- **1572:** Sarmiento completes his Toledo-sponsored history, including Pachacuti’s thunder-image traditions.
- **c. 1573–1575:** Molina writes his detailed Quechua-informed account of festivals, images, prayers, and sacrifices.
- **1590:** Acosta publishes the three thunder names Chuquilla, Catuilla, and Intiillapa.
- **c. 1598–1608:** Huarochirí traditions are written in Quechua; Sullca Illapa appears among Pariacaca’s sons.
- **1609:** Garcilaso publishes the first part of *Comentarios reales*, subordinating Illapa to Inti and recording the water-jar story.
- **1612:** Bertonio’s Aymara vocabulary documents the colonial linguistic field in which Santiago, thunder, and firearms converge.
- **c. 1615–1616:** Guaman Poma completes the *Nueva corónica*, recording triadic Illapa language and *yllapa* as royal dead.
- **1617–1664:** Cajatambo investigations document continuing Libiac and ancestor cults under coercive prosecution.
- **1621:** Arriaga publishes his anti-idolatry manual.
- **1653:** Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, preserving the classic celestial slinger, Milky Way water, and third-rank account.
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** Santiago becomes an Andean rain, lightning, fertility, pastoral, and mountain power in numerous localities.
- **1879:** Pachacuti Yamqui’s *Relación de antigüedades* is first published, making the Coricancha cosmogram influential in modern scholarship.
- **1936:** Guaman Poma’s manuscript receives its first full facsimile publication after twentieth-century scholarly rediscovery.
- **1966:** José María Arguedas’s Spanish rendering of the Huarochirí traditions greatly expands their readership.
- **1987:** Gerald Taylor publishes a critical Quechua–Spanish Huarochirí edition.
- **1990:** Roland Hamilton publishes the English selection *Inca Religion and Customs* from Cobo.
- **1991:** Salomon and Urioste publish *The Huarochirí Manuscript*; MacCormack publishes *Religion in the Andes*.
- **1998:** Bauer publishes *The Sacred Landscape of the Inca*, integrating the *ceque* archive with field survey.
- **2003:** Duviols and collaborators publish the major Cajatambo trial corpus.
- **2013:** *Lightning in the Andes and Mesoamerica* provides a major comparative reassessment.
- **2016:** Baulenas publishes a focused study of Illapa’s earthly and political powers.
- **2021:** Archaeological research at Pucará de Tilcara discusses lightning, *illas*, and continuing Santiago/Illapa associations in the Argentine northwest.
- **2025:** Contemporary reporting from Cusco documents lightning-selected ritual vocation and living Illapa terminology.
- **2026:** Illapa remains present through Santiago-centered local religion, Andean ritual practice, heritage work, scholarship, and explicitly modern artistic or reconstructionist revivals; no demonstrable unbroken independent imperial institution survives.
