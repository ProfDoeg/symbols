# Ekeko: Research Dossier

**Class:** A living Aymara and south-Andean tutelary being, variously classified as a **deity/god of abundance and fertility**, a household **spirit or power-bearing image**, and an **illa**—a sacred object concentrating generative force. “Inca god” is too broad: Ekeko belongs most securely to the Aymara-speaking Titicaca–Altiplano world, though that world experienced Inca rule and later circulated across Bolivia, Peru, northern Chile, northwestern Argentina, and migrant communities farther afield.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Ludovico Bertonio’s Aymara dictionary, printed at Juli in **1612**, records **“Ecaco, I. Thunnupa”** and describes him as a figure about whom the “ancient Indians” told many stories still believed in Bertonio’s day. This is the earliest presently identifiable written attestation of the named being. It proves an early-seventeenth-century Aymara tradition equating or closely associating *Ecaco* with *Thunnupa/Tunupa*; it does **not** securely identify any surviving Tiwanaku or Pukara image as Ekeko. The claim that a particular ca. 200 BCE–200 CE Pukara-style statue is Ekeko begins as a modern archaeological interpretation, principally Carlos Ponce Sanginés’s 1969 reconstruction, and remains disputed.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** primary textual, archaeological, or documentary evidence securely dated near the earliest record.
- **[LE] Later elaboration:** a later tradition expanding an earlier figure.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** practice or interpretation limited to a locality or historical community.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, archaeological, clerical, nationalist, or scholarly attempt to assemble disparate evidence into a coherent theology.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification:** historical equation or ritual conjunction of distinguishable beings or traditions.
- **[F] Folklore:** orally transmitted or subsequently collected legend, preserved as tradition but not contemporary documentation of its purported events.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a scholarly hypothesis rather than a statement directly contained in early evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or demonstrably modern development:** a recent form, claim, or attribution, whether or not regarded as sacred by present communities.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

In present practice Ekeko is the smiling, pot-bellied, often mustachioed little man covered with miniature food, money, household goods, tools, vehicles, diplomas, houses, and other desired possessions. He is especially associated with La Paz’s **Alasita/Alasitas**, beginning at noon on **24 January** and continuing for two or three weeks. UNESCO describes him as the city’s beneficent god of fertility and associates him with “good-luck” miniatures; Bolivian usage more commonly emphasizes abundance, prosperity, fertility, household well-being, and the fulfillment of desires [LE/RV].

A useful distinction is:

1. **Ecaco/Ekako of the colonial linguistic source**—a named being associated by Bertonio with Tunupa [EA].
2. **Small power-bearing images, illas, hunchbacks, dwarfs, and fertility figures** found in pre-Hispanic and ethnographic Andean contexts [EA as objects; MSR when individually called Ekeko].
3. **The modern loaded household figurine**, whose documented visual form becomes clear in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries [LE/MI].
4. **The “Illa del Ekeko” returned from Bern**, a genuinely ancient Pukara-style sacred figure whose specific identification as Ekeko is modern and disputed [MSR/modern sacred reidentification].

These categories overlap historically, but they are not automatically identical.

### Names and spellings

- **Ekeko / Equeco:** Standard modern Spanish spellings.
- **Ekeko, Ekhekho, Ekhako, Ekako, Ecaco:** Historical or modern orthographic variants attempting to represent Aymara sounds.
- **Iqiqu:** Standardized Aymara spelling now frequently given as the underlying form. Modern dictionaries apply it to the loaded abundance figurine and sometimes to a short or dwarfish person. Because the colonial source writes *Ecaco*, the exact phonological and semantic route to modern *Iqiqu* is not entirely settled.
- **Ecaco, I. Thunnupa:** Bertonio’s 1612 entry. “I.” is normally understood as an equivalence or alternate identification: “Ecaco, [that is] Thunnupa.” His accompanying notice calls the being one about whom the ancient Indians told many fables, many still accepted as true [EA/SY].
- **Tunupa / Thunupa / Thunnupa / Tonapa:** A widely distributed Altiplano creator, transformer, culture hero, preacher, volcanic or storm-associated being in colonial chronicles and later scholarship. Bertonio supplies the earliest explicit Ekeko–Tunupa linkage. Treating every Tunupa story or image as an Ekeko story, however, is Ponce Sanginés’s and related scholars’ systematization, not something the colonial corpus itself warrants [EA for the identification; LS for total fusion].
- **Illa del Ekeko:** Contemporary Bolivian designation of the repatriated Pukara-style figure. *Illa* is not merely “idol”: in Andean ritual language it denotes a concentrated source, model, double, or generative embodiment of beings and productive abundance. Bolivia’s National Archaeology Museum describes an illa as concentrating *ajayu*, vital or animating force [modern institutional interpretation].
- **Ekeko Tunu:** A modern ceremonial designation used during the restitution campaign, linking the image to a root, trunk, or originating form and to Tunupa [LS/modern ritual usage].
- **Dios de la abundancia / dios de la fortuna / dios de la fecundidad:** Modern Spanish cult and heritage titles [LE].
- **Protector de la ciudad:** La Paz heritage discourse’s civic title [LE/RV].

### Etymology

Two propositions must be separated:

- **Secure:** The colonial spelling *Ecaco* and modern *Ekeko/Iqiqu* are historically connected in Aymara reception [EA with linguistic development].
- **Uncertain:** Popular explanations that the name intrinsically means “dwarf,” “large-headed dwarf,” “hunchback,” “prosperity,” or “fortune” often combine later dictionary meanings, iconographic interpretation, and the deity’s function. Bertonio’s cited entry calls Ecaco a named traditional being; it does not transparently derive the name from “abundance.”

A separate Bertonio entry glosses *ecaco* as an ingenious or resourceful man—*“hombre ingenioso que tiene muchas trazas.”* Whether this is the deity’s etymology, a homonym, or a secondary semantic development cannot be securely established.

### Alasita

*Alasita/Alasitas* is conventionally connected with Aymara *alasiña/alasi-*—“to buy”—and popularly glossed “buy me.” Bertonio’s 1612 vocabulary documents the relevant buying vocabulary. The etymology supports the fair’s commercial language; it does not by itself prove that the modern January fair existed in 1612.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The first securely dated evidence

#### Ludovico Bertonio, *Vocabulario de la lengua aymara* (Juli, 1612)

**Language:** Aymara and Spanish.  
**Physical form:** Printed missionary dictionary.  
**Place:** Juli, Chucuito, on the Peruvian side of Lake Titicaca.  
**Relevant entry:** “Ecaco, I. Thunnupa.”

Bertonio says this was the name of one about whom ancient Indigenous people told many “fables,” still regarded by many as true; as a missionary, he calls that belief a demonic deception to be eradicated. Elsewhere he discusses Tunupa as a being to whom Indigenous people attributed many deeds, some compared—polemically—to Christian mysteries [EA].

What Bertonio establishes:

- a named *Ecaco* tradition existed no later than 1612;
- it was understood as old;
- stories about the being remained current;
- Ecaco was identified with or referred to Tunupa;
- missionaries regarded the belief as religious error requiring suppression.

What he does **not** establish:

- the pot-bellied, mustachioed loaded figurine;
- a January 24 festival;
- a cigarette ritual;
- identity with a specific Pukara or Tiwanaku statue;
- the 1781 siege narrative.

### Guaman Poma, ca. 1615

Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala’s *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, completed around 1615 and surviving as Copenhagen, Royal Library, GKS 2232 4º, uses forms transcribed as *equeco* in descriptions of social types, stature, offices, or punishers. Juan Carlos La Serna’s source table identifies occurrences connected with an Inca subgroup, a small person, an authority, and a May punisher. These passages are evidence for a word or type, but not a secure appearance of Ekeko as the modern abundance deity [EA linguistically; uncertain religious identification].

### Other colonial Andean corpora

No securely identified Ekeko episode has been established in:

- Cieza de León;
- Betanzos;
- Sarmiento de Gamboa;
- Cobo;
- Cristóbal de Molina;
- Garcilaso de la Vega;
- Cristóbal de Albornoz;
- Pablo José de Arriaga;
- the *Huarochirí Manuscript*;
- the published Huarochirí and Cajatambo idolatry proceedings.

These sources are indispensable for Inca and colonial Andean religion, huacas, illas, extirpation, and Tunupa-related comparative material. They cannot be silently converted into Ekeko sources. The absence is significant: the large colonial corpus does **not** give Ekeko a stable genealogy, temple network, priesthood, or mythic cycle comparable to Viracocha, Pariacaca, Pachacamac, or the Sun.

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century documentary corpus

- **Johann Jakob von Tschudi, *Reise durch Südamerika*, vol. 5 (1869):** describes how in 1858 he obtained a small sacred stone image at Tiwanaku. He observed offerings and candles and compared its local treatment to devotion toward a Catholic saint. His account and illustrations identify the object later found in Bern. He did not supply an archaeologically secure ancient name for it [EA for nineteenth-century veneration and removal; not EA for “Ekeko”].
- **Arthur Posnansky, “El Ekeko y la fiesta de Alasitas”** (published in twentieth-century Bolivian anthropological literature): an early attempt to reconstruct pre-Hispanic origins and relate solstitial miniature rites to the modern fair [LS].
- **Rigoberto Paredes, collection first published around 1920 and in later editions:** principal literary source for the 1781 Paulita Tintaya/Isidro Choquehuanca legend [F].
- **Carlos Ponce Sanginés, *Tunupa y Ekako* (1969):** extensive archaeological-iconographic comparison of bent-backed, hunchbacked, dwarfish, phallic, and small anthropomorphic images from Pukara, Tiwanaku, Inca, and ethnographic contexts [MSR/LS].
- **MUSEF, *Alasitas: universo de los deseos* (2011) and *Alasitas: donde crecen las illas*:** ethnographic, historical, material-culture, and ritual documentation [modern scholarship].
- **Juan Carlos La Serna Salcedo, *Dioses y mercados de la fortuna* (Peruvian Ministry of Culture, 2013):** the strongest accessible documentary synthesis for Puno. It explicitly differentiates old illa/miniature principles, colonial image appropriation, and the modern Ekeko’s relatively late prominence [modern critical scholarship].
- **UNESCO nomination and inscription materials (2017):** documentary evidence for present ritual journeys, miniature acquisition, Aymara ritual consecration, Catholic blessing, family transmission, artisanship, and civic safeguarding [EA for contemporary practice].

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

Ekeko’s best-attested modern domains are:

- abundance and household sufficiency;
- food, clothing, work, money, property, and trade;
- fertility and generative prosperity;
- luck and successful projects;
- fulfillment of materially represented desires;
- urban aspirations—houses, cars, diplomas, passports, businesses, computers, health certificates, and increasingly topical wishes.

The domain’s history is not simply a shift from “agriculture” to “consumerism.” It is better understood as adaptation of the Andean miniature/illa principle—representing and ritually activating desired productivity—to new agrarian, mercantile, and urban economies. Peru’s 2016 heritage resolution explicitly describes this movement from livestock and agricultural productivity toward money, appliances, education, and housing [LE].

### Standard modern iconography

The canonical twentieth- and twenty-first-century figure is:

- male;
- short and thick-bodied or pot-bellied;
- smiling, rosy-cheeked, often mustachioed;
- dressed in poncho, ch’ullu, trousers, sandals, or an urban/mestizo ensemble;
- arms extended or body covered with goods;
- burdened with sacks and miniatures;
- commonly supplied with an opening in the mouth for a cigarette.

Its dress is variable. Some examples emphasize an Aymara peasant, some a mestizo merchant, some a prosperous urban man. The current form is not a straightforward survival of one ancient sculpture. Peruvian Ministry of Culture research instead identifies appropriation of Iberian colonial sculptural conventions, translated through Indigenous artisanship and religious categories, with the recognizable loaded figure clearly prominent only in modern documentation [LE/MSR].

### Miniatures

Miniatures are often more central to Alasita than the statue itself. They may represent:

- staple food and market baskets;
- livestock, fields, seed, and tools;
- houses, building materials, shops, vehicles, and cash;
- diplomas, professional credentials, passports, visas, and marriage certificates;
- babies, medical care, relationships, travel, or political hopes;
- miniature newspapers, whose satire records contemporary public concerns.

They are bought—ideally at noon—then consecrated through **ch’alla**, smoke, alcohol, flower petals, incense, coca, prayer, or a yatiri’s blessing. Many participants also carry them to a Catholic priest for holy-water blessing. UNESCO’s wording carefully describes sequential ritual journeys rather than declaring the practices theologically identical [SY].

### The cigarette

Giving Ekeko a lit cigarette is a living household practice [LE/RV]. A favorable sign is often said to be that he “smokes” readily; smoke activates or pleases the image, paralleling wider Andean uses of tobacco smoke in offerings and diagnosis. Devotees differ about timing: Tuesday, Friday, the first Tuesday, last Friday, or January 24 are all reported. Therefore no single schedule should be declared canonical.

The mouth hole is a feature manufactured for the rite and is not documented on the Bern Pukara-style sculpture. Claims that cigarette smoking is an unbroken Tiwanaku custom are unestablished.

### Older images claimed as prototypes

Ponce Sanginés compared the modern Ekeko with:

- hunchbacked or bent-backed stone figures;
- naked, sometimes phallic anthropomorphs;
- small metal Inca figurines;
- figures on pins or *tupus*;
- Kallawaya *keko* images.

The formal parallels are real objects; their naming is the issue. Archaeological figures ordinarily lack inscriptions saying “Iqiqu.” Similarity of size, bodily difference, fertility symbolism, or posture cannot by itself prove identity [MSR].

### Colors, animals, planets, numbers

No early source securely assigns Ekeko a canonical animal, plant, color, number, weekday, or celestial body. Tobacco, coca, alcohol, incense, food, money, and miniatures are associated substances or objects in modern ritual. Tuesday and Friday schedules are living customary variants, not ancient calendrical dogma.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

No securely attested early source gives Ekeko parents, siblings, spouse, children, mount, servant, or enemy. Modern summaries that insert him into a fully ordered “Inca pantheon” create a genealogy the evidence does not supply.

Bertonio’s equivalence with Tunupa is the only early named divine relation. It may indicate identity, an explanatory cross-reference, or local equivalence; it does not prove that every Tunupa narrative originally concerned Ekeko.

### Every documented named divine interaction or identification

- **Tunupa/Thunnupa — identity or close equivalence.** Bertonio’s *Vocabulario* (1612) gives “Ecaco, I. Thunnupa.” **Tier:** [EA/SY]. This is an equation, not a narrated encounter.
- **Our Lady of Peace (*Nuestra Señora de La Paz*) — calendrical and civic conjunction.** In the legend collected by Rigoberto Paredes around 1920, Sebastián de Segurola associates the post-siege miniature fair with January 24, the Virgin’s feast, in thanksgiving for La Paz’s deliverance. Ekeko and the Virgin do not meet or converse in an early text. **Tier:** [F/SY].
- **Catholic God, Christ, or saints — no specific narrative interaction established.** Catholic priests bless Alasita miniatures, and Tschudi compared devotion to the stone image with saint veneration, but these are ritual juxtapositions, not documented god-god episodes. **Tier:** [SY].
- **Viracocha — no independent Ekeko interaction securely attested.** Some scholarship equates Tunupa with Viracocha and then extends that equation to Ekeko. This is a chain of learned identifications, not a primary Ekeko narrative. **Tier:** [LS/MSR].
- **Pachamama — no early narrative interaction securely attested.** Both may be invoked within contemporary Andean ritual systems, but shared ritual context does not constitute a documented episode.
- **Illapa, Inti, Pachacamac, Pariacaca, Supay, and other named Andean beings — no documented Ekeko interaction found.**

**Finding:** Ekeko has no recoverable polytheistic narrative network. His dossier is overwhelmingly cultic, linguistic, iconographic, and folkloric rather than genealogical.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Ecaco–Tunupa traditions

Bertonio’s testimony says numerous stories existed, but his dictionary does not preserve a full Ekeko cycle. Colonial chronicles preserve several divergent Tunupa traditions: a wandering preacher or transformer; a being rejected, punished, tied to a raft, or moving across the Altiplano; and a figure subsequently read through Christian apostolic motifs. Because Bertonio equates Ecaco and Tunupa, these stories are relevant to the historical field around Ekeko, but assigning all of them to Ekeko would overstate the evidence [EA for lost story tradition; LS for reconstructed cycle].

### B. The 1781 siege legend

The most influential modern origin story comes not from a 1781 siege diary but from Rigoberto Paredes’s folklore collection roughly 140 years later [F].

Its developed form says:

1. **Paulita Tintaya**, an Indigenous servant in the household of Josefa Úrsula de Rojas Foronda and the colonial commander Sebastián de Segurola, had a relationship with or affection for an Indigenous man commonly named **Isidro Choquehuanca**.
2. Before separation, Isidro gave Paulita a little figure—sometimes described as modeled on himself.
3. During the Indigenous siege of La Paz led by Túpac Katari’s forces in 1781, famine afflicted the city.
4. Food nevertheless continued to appear for Paulita or the household, attributed to the little image’s abundance-bestowing power or to Isidro’s secret deliveries.
5. After the siege, Segurola learned of the figure’s role and permitted or promoted its cult.
6. He moved or attached the miniature fair to January 24, feast of Our Lady of Peace.

Variants rename characters, turn the miraculous provisioning into a clandestine human act, make the figure pre-existing rather than newly fashioned, or say its appearance caricatured Segurola or another Spaniard. Díaz Villamil’s 1944 literary treatment proposed that artisans modeled the colonial Ekeko on a Spaniard named Rojas. Another popular version says the mustachioed figure mocks Segurola. These are later variants, not mutually corroborating 1781 records [F/LE].

The historically secure substrate is the 1781 siege, severe scarcity, Segurola’s command, and the later prominence of January 24. The romance, magic provisioning, and official founding decree are not secured by contemporary documentation. Paredes’s account should therefore be told fully as a constitutive La Paz legend, but not quoted as eyewitness history.

### C. The ancestral hunchback

Ponce Sanginés reconstructed a very long sequence in which bent-backed, dwarfish, naked, and sometimes phallic pre-Hispanic figures represent ancestral Ekeko/Tunupa: a fertility and weather power gradually transformed into the clothed, loaded modern household provider [MSR].

The theory’s strength is its wide iconographic comparison and attention to the Andean ritual potency of smallness, bodily difference, fertility, and illas. Its weakness is the lack of labels, inscriptions, or an uninterrupted securely documented image sequence. The Bern museum noted that Ponce’s was essentially the sole published archaeological identification of its Pukara figure as Ekeko when restitution was debated.

### D. The returned ancestor

A new sacred-historical narrative formed between 2012 and 2014: the exiled *Illa del Ekeko*, removed by a European collector, was called home, recovered its *ajayu*, and became an emblem of decolonization, reconciliation, and national cultural sovereignty [MI/modern sacred history].

This narrative is not invalidated by the uncertainty of the ancient statue’s name. It documents a new phase in the object’s social and religious existence. Bolivia’s National Archaeology Museum explicitly describes its return as reactivation of a living huaca and restoration of relationship with community and territory.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult centers

Ekeko has no securely identified pre-Hispanic temple. The major contemporary center is **La Paz**, especially the January Alasita fair. Important regional centers include El Alto and the Lake Titicaca corridor; Puno, Juli, Chucuito, Ilave, Juliaca, Yunguyo, Azángaro, and other southern Peruvian towns; and migrant celebrations in Buenos Aires and elsewhere.

In Puno, major miniature fairs are often associated with the **May Cross/3 May** rather than January 24 [RV]. Peru’s heritage resolution lists numerous twentieth-century Puno fairs and stresses that their cross-border cultural geography predates modern national boundaries.

### Household cult

Ekeko is commonly kept in a prominent home or business location, loaded with miniatures of what the household wants to obtain or preserve. Devotees may offer:

- cigarettes;
- coca leaves;
- incense and aromatic smoke;
- alcohol or beer through ch’alla;
- sweets, grain, food, or flower petals;
- fresh miniatures.

Treatment varies from explicit worship of a god to affectionate maintenance of a luck-bearing household image. The statue may be treated almost as a socially responsive person, but no universal priestly theology governs the practice.

### Alasita ritual sequence

The La Paz sequence documented by UNESCO is:

1. Participants enter the fair and buy miniatures expressing their wishes.
2. Purchase and exchange place the wishes into a social and economic circuit.
3. Yatiris or other Andean ritual specialists consecrate, smoke, or ch’alla the objects.
4. Many participants then obtain Catholic blessing.
5. The miniatures are carried home, placed with Ekeko, or otherwise preserved.

The fair also contains games, foods, journalism, political satire, artisan competitions, and commercial entertainment. It is at once market, civic festival, ritual journey, and display of material imagination.

### Personnel

- **Yatiris/amautas:** Ritual specialists who read coca, prepare offerings, ch’alla miniatures, and address Andean powers.
- **Artisans/miniaturists:** Central transmitters of the visual and technical traditions.
- **Vendors and associations:** Organize production and exchange.
- **Catholic clergy:** Bless miniatures in a parallel or sequential rite.
- **Municipal and national agencies:** Regulate, promote, curate, and patrimonialize the festival.

There is no securely documented hereditary Ekeko priesthood comparable to an ancient temple college.

### Economy and politics

Alasita gives ritual value to commerce without reducing belief to commerce. Its miniatures continually record changing economies: agrarian animals and stores, wage employment, migration papers, academic degrees, real estate, digital technology, and political hopes.

The fair has also become an arena of Bolivian–Peruvian heritage politics. Peru’s 2016 resolution states explicitly that neither Ekeko nor Alasitas can be confined to one modern national territory because the Altiplano is a transnational cultural and religious region. UNESCO’s 2017 inscription concerns **“Ritual journeys in La Paz during Alasita,”** not ownership of every Alasita practice.

### Sacrifice, sexual rites, and cult violence

No credible evidence found associates Ekeko’s own cult with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or an institutional cult militia. General evidence for Inca *capacocha*, Andean animal sacrifice, or colonial religious violence cannot be reassigned to Ekeko without a source.

Suppression is documented in the more limited form represented by Bertonio’s missionary demand that belief in Ecaco be extinguished as diabolical deception and by broader campaigns against Andean “idolatry.” No specific surviving Ekeko prosecution has yet been identified in the Huarochirí or Cajatambo trial material.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1612: archaeological possibilities

Small anthropomorphs, illas, fertility figures, hunchbacks, and Pukara/Tiwanaku images demonstrate an ancient material field of miniature and embodied sacred power [EA]. Calling particular examples *Iqiqu* is retrospective [MSR].

The Bern figure is Pukara-style and dated approximately **200 BCE–200 CE**. It was reportedly found or kept at Tiwanaku, but style, find location, ancient cultural affiliation, and modern divine name are separate questions. Some specialists interpreted it as female or water-associated rather than as male Ekeko.

### 1612–seventeenth century: first named record and suppression

Bertonio’s dictionary proves Ecaco/Tunupa traditions in the Aymara world and simultaneously records missionary hostility. The figure is not prominent in the major extirpation or chronicle corpus under this name. Either the documentation is thin, traditions circulated under other names, or later prominence should not be projected backward.

### Eighteenth century: siege and later memory

The 1781 siege was a decisive catastrophe in La Paz’s history. Later folklore made it the moment when Indigenous abundance power entered or was tolerated within colonial urban life and when Alasita was tied to January 24 [F]. Documentary proof of Segurola’s purported decree remains lacking.

### Nineteenth century: sacred image and urban fair

Tschudi’s 1858 account is strong evidence that a small pre-Hispanic statue at Tiwanaku still received offerings and candles. He secured it through a coercive, alcohol-assisted transaction and departed while local people pursued him. His own 1869 publication preserves the chain of custody and the sacred context.

The recognizable modern Ekeko and fair developed within an increasingly commercial and urban Altiplano. Precise nineteenth-century visual chronology remains thinner than later folkloric accounts imply.

### Early twentieth century: icon crystallization and folklorization

By the early twentieth century the short, smiling, loaded male figure is firmly visible. Paredes transformed oral history into a durable written siege legend; Posnansky sought pre-Hispanic and solstitial antecedents; civic authorities and folklorists made Alasita a marker of regional identity [LE/LS].

In Peru, the loaded Ekeko becomes especially prominent from the early twentieth century and very popular between the 1950s and 1990s. Peruvian evidence also shows that he may now be displaced in some fairs by the “fortune toad” and other images.

### 1969–2000: archaeological grand narrative and mass commodity

Ponce Sanginés’s *Tunupa y Ekako* gave the strongest scholarly form to a millennia-long continuity thesis. Mass-produced plaster and ceramic statues, tourism, national folklore, and expanding urban consumption further standardized Ekeko’s appearance. This was both continuity of propitiatory practice and substantial modern reinvention.

### Twenty-first century: migration, heritage, and decolonization

Alasita and Ekeko practices accompany Bolivian and Peruvian migration to Buenos Aires, Madrid, and other cities. Ethnography in Buenos Aires documents yatiris organizing annual renewals with Ekeko at Parque Avellaneda, Villa Soldati, Villa Celina, Liniers, and related migrant spaces [RV].

The Bern restitution campaign turned Ekeko into an international figure of cultural-property justice. UNESCO’s 2017 inscription institutionalized the La Paz ritual journeys while recognizing living family and artisan transmission.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Heritage and national identity

Bolivia promotes Ekeko as a major symbol of La Paz, Aymara heritage, and living plurinational culture. Peru recognizes the Puno miniature fairs as national cultural heritage and rejects exclusive national origin claims. The tension is modern and political, but it arises from a genuinely shared Altiplano history.

### The Bern figure

The small greenish stone figure, Bern Historical Museum inventory **Pe 145**, entered the museum in 1929 with Tschudi’s collection. Bolivia formally sought its transfer in 2013 after diplomat Elizabeth Salguero renewed attention to it. Bern questioned both the object’s identification as Ekeko and the proposed ownership terms. A Bern parliamentary vote strongly favored return because the object’s cultural importance to Bolivia exceeded its significance in Bern.

The figure was transferred in October 2014 and arrived in Bolivia in November. It was publicly welcomed and later incorporated into ritual and museum narratives. “Returned Ekeko” is therefore simultaneously:

- a real Pukara-style antiquity;
- an object documented as locally sacred in 1858;
- a disputed archaeological identification;
- a modern nationally and ritually recognized Ekeko;
- a restitution precedent.

### Commercial image

Ekeko is sold as household statuary, souvenir, corporate luck emblem, and decorative “prosperity god.” Commercial examples sometimes prescribe universal rules—where to place him, which cigarette brand he prefers, or an exact weekday—that are actually vendor lore or regional custom [MI].

### Literature and popular media

The siege romance has circulated in children’s books, school texts, tourist literature, newspapers, stage adaptations, and online retellings. These media often turn the Paredes legend into simple history and suppress its late collection date [modern retelling].

### Occult and New Age claims

Ekeko appears in international amulet catalogues, prosperity magic, “manifestation” practices, and eclectic New Age shops. No evidence places him in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, classical European grimoires, or an ancient global occult system. Assertions that he has fixed planetary correspondences, belongs to a universal “abundance archetype,” or requires standardized spells are modern esoteric adaptations [MI].

No significant organized Ekeko neopagan denomination or extremist appropriation was documented in the consulted sources. Living devotion is principally Andean popular religion, family practice, festival ritual, heritage performance, and diaspora religion—not a reconstructionist church.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Continuity versus transformation

The central debate is not whether ancient Andeans used miniatures and sacred images—they did—but whether specific ancient objects can be named Ekeko and whether the present figure continues one deity unchanged.

- **Strong continuity position:** Ponce Sanginés links Pukara, Tiwanaku, Inca, Kallawaya, colonial, and modern figures through hunchbacked form, smallness, sexuality, fertility, weather, and good fortune.
- **Critical transformation position:** La Serna and Peru’s heritage documentation distinguish an ancient miniature/illa complex from the colonial appropriation of Iberian sculptural forms and the late emergence of the recognizable loaded Ekeko.
- **Best-supported synthesis:** An old Aymara named being is documented in 1612; ancient miniature and illa practices provided a deep ritual grammar; colonial and republican artisans created or transformed the current image; modern scholarship and heritage politics then attached selected archaeological figures to that lineage.

### Ekeko and Tunupa

Bertonio’s equation is primary evidence. The controversy concerns its scope. It supports some relationship but not Ponce’s total absorption of every Tunupa attribute—preacher, lightning, volcanoes, water, fertility—into Ekeko. The direction of identification may also have been missionary lexicography: explaining one local name through another.

### The 1781 foundation story

Paredes’s account is culturally important but late. Historians must distinguish:

- contemporary evidence for the siege;
- later testimony about urban custom;
- twentieth-century literary folklore;
- still later civic claims that Segurola “founded” or officially rescheduled Alasita.

The purported founding decree should not be cited without archival identification.

### The Pukara figurine

The object is ancient; its name is disputed. In the restitution debate:

- Bolivian officials, Indigenous representatives, and Ponce-derived scholarship regarded it as Ekeko or an ancestral Ekeko;
- Bern specialists noted that only one major archaeological author had made that identification and proposed alternatives, including a female or water-related being;
- both sides ultimately recognized that modern cultural significance did not depend entirely on resolving ancient iconographic identity.

This is a model case in which archaeological classification, provenance, living sacred recognition, and legal ownership answer different questions.

### Bolivia versus Peru

Nationalist presentations sometimes claim exclusive invention. Peru’s official 2016 resolution offers the more historically careful conclusion: Alasita and Ekeko practices belong to a connected southern Altiplano world whose ritual and trading networks cross present borders. Local forms and dates nevertheless differ substantially.

### State of the evidence

Important repositories include:

- Bolivia’s National Archaeology Museum, now custodian of the returned figure;
- MUSEF and La Paz municipal collections of twentieth-century Ekekos and Alasita material;
- the Royal Library in Copenhagen for Guaman Poma;
- libraries holding Bertonio’s 1612 vocabulary;
- Peruvian Ministry of Culture archives and Puno ethnographic collections;
- Bern Historical Museum and Swiss archival records concerning Tschudi and restitution.

The evidence is uneven: one early dictionary entry; ambiguous colonial lexical occurrences; abundant but anonymous archaeological figures; nineteenth-century travel testimony; and increasingly rich twentieth- and twenty-first-century ethnography.

### Discredited or unproven claims still circulating

- “The Bern statue is definitively the original Ekeko”: **unproven**.
- “Ekeko is securely documented as a Tiwanaku god”: **unproven**.
- “The contemporary figurine has survived visually unchanged for two thousand years”: **contradicted by the documented iconographic transformation**.
- “Segurola’s 1781 decree founded Alasita”: **not established without a contemporary document**.
- “Ekeko has a complete Inca genealogy”: **modern systematization**.
- “The cigarette rite is pre-Columbian”: **anachronistic in that form**.
- “Bolivia or Peru alone originated every manifestation”: **historically reductive**.
- “Ekeko worship involved human sacrifice or temple prostitution”: **no supporting evidence found**.
