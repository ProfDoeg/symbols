# Viracocha: Research Dossier

**Class:** creator deity, ordering power, culture hero, and—in regional traditions—wandering huaca; grammatically and iconographically usually treated as male, although some colonial prayers describe the creative power as capable of being male or female.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Juan de Betanzos’s Spanish-language *Suma y narración de los Incas*, composed at Cusco in about 1551 from Quechua-speaking informants. Its opening chapters name **Contiti Viracocha Pachayachachic**, who emerges from a lake in Collasuyu, creates celestial light at Tiwanaku, makes and disperses peoples, travels northwest, and departs across the sea. There is no securely deciphered pre-Hispanic inscription naming Viracocha and no pre-Hispanic image that can be identified as Viracocha without relying on colonial testimony or modern inference.

## Evidence labels

- **[Early colonial attestation]** Sixteenth-century testimony, normally recorded after the Spanish invasion but close to surviving Inca institutions and informants.
- **[Later colonial elaboration]** Seventeenth-century account, compilation, or theological interpretation.
- **[Regional variant]** A tradition belonging to a locality or non-Cusco religious complex.
- **[Learned systematization]** A chronicler’s or priest’s attempt to reconcile diverse traditions into an ordered pantheon or theology.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically situated equation with another divine figure or with the Christian God.
- **[Material evidence]** Archaeological or art-historical evidence independent of, or considered beside, narrative texts.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An interpretation proposed by modern researchers rather than directly stated in a primary source.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** A recent attribution, reconstruction, occult claim, tourist image, or popular narrative.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Viracocha is best understood not as a single, invariant “supreme god of the Incas” but as a colonial documentary cluster containing at least four distinguishable formations:

1. the Cusco imperial creator or world-orderer called **Contiti/Tiqsi Viracocha Pachayachachic**;
2. Viracocha’s subordinate, filial, messenger, or hypostatic figures, including **Imaymana Viracochan**, **Tocapo Viracochan**, and **Taguapaca/Tarapaca**;
3. regional beings bearing *Viracocha* as part of a compound name, especially **Cuniraya Huiracocha** in the Huarochirí Manuscript;
4. *viracocha* as a colonial designation for Spaniards or socially powerful outsiders.

Treating all four as one person produces a synthetic deity that no single primary source presents.

### Names and spellings

**[Early colonial attestation]**

The colonial orthographies include:

- *Viracocha*
- *Uiracocha*
- *Huiracocha*
- *Viracochan*
- *Con Tici Viracocha*
- *Contiti Viracocha*
- *Ticci Viracocha*
- *Tiqsi Wiraqucha* in modern normalized Quechua
- *Viracocha Pachayachachic*
- *Illa Tecce* or comparable spellings in some colonial theological texts
- *Cuniraya Huiracocha*, a regional compound in the Huarochirí Manuscript.

The final *-n* in forms such as *Viracochan* can represent Quechua relational or possessive morphology, not necessarily a different divine name.

### Epithets and titles

- **Tiqsi/Ticci**: “foundation,” “origin,” or “beginning.” **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Pachayachachiq/Pachayachachic**: conventionally translated by Spaniards as “teacher of the world” or “maker of the world.” The verb is disputed. César Itier and other linguists argue that in this context it more probably denotes one who makes the *pacha* attain or inhabit its proper condition—an organizer, cultivator, or fulfiller—rather than a school-like “teacher.” **[Early colonial attestation; modern linguistic reconstruction]**
- **Hacedor/Criador**: Spanish “Maker/Creator,” used by chroniclers and missionaries. It conveys genuine native creation and ordering traditions but also places them in Christian theological vocabulary. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Apu**: lord or powerful authority, occasionally attached in later or reconstructed formulations. Its use should not be projected automatically onto every early source.
- **Caylla/Qaylla**: associated in prayers with nearness, boundary, or presence; interpretations differ. **[Early colonial attestation; disputed]**
- **Tocapo/Tuqapu** and **Acnupo/Aqnu-pu**: opaque or contested ritual epithets connected by Molina with a Viracocha figure “in whom all things are included.” **[Early colonial attestation; learned systematization]**

### Etymology: a genuinely unresolved problem

The popular translation “foam of the sea” or “fat of the sea” analyzes Quechua *wira* “fat, grease” and *qucha* “lake, body of water, sea.” It is old, memorable, and widespread, but already troubled early writers: Garcilaso de la Vega insisted that *Viracocha* was a proper name, not a transparent compound. Quechua constituent order and the poor semantic fit also count against treating “sea-foam” as established.

Major scholarly proposals include:

- **“fat/grease of the lake or sea”**: traditional folk etymology; possible as a colonial reanalysis, not demonstrated as the original meaning.
- **Itier’s Wari–qucha proposal**: an earlier *Wari Qucha*, associated with *wari* as ancient people or beings of the first dawn, later metathesized to *Wiraqucha*. Itier connects the deity with the subterranean or circumterrestrial ocean feeding springs, lakes, and irrigation water. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Cerrón-Palomino’s Aymara proposal**: *wila quta*, “blood lake,” subsequently Quechuanized as *wira qucha*. This is connected with Titicaca, sacrifice, and a Puquina/Aymara linguistic setting. The phonological and historical argument remains debated. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Unanalyzable inherited name**: the cautious position already approached by Garcilaso and still defensible because colonial spellings may represent a non-Quechua name adapted into Quechua and Spanish.

No etymology presently commands universal agreement.

### Spaniards as *viracochas*

**[Early colonial attestation]** Cieza de León reports that Andeans applied *Viracocha* to Spaniards and records an explanatory story involving the Inca Viracocha and Spanish arrival. Other sources use the word for Spaniards, lords, or foreigners. This usage is real colonial evidence.

The traditional conquest story—that Indigenous people spontaneously believed the Europeans were returning white gods—is not thereby established. Itier argues that Spaniards themselves helped circulate or solicit the title, interpreting it in Christian terms. Colonial Andeans could also use it strategically, ironically, deferentially, or as a category for powerful foreigners. Cieza states that Andeans soon judged Spanish sacrilege and abuse incompatible with divinity, calling some Spaniards worse than *supay*. **[Contested material]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidentiary limit

No known Inca alphabetic scripture survives. Quipus, architecture, images, offerings, oral specialists, and ritual performances carried knowledge, but none presently supplies a deciphered preconquest spelling of *Viracocha*. Consequently, the named deity enters the recoverable written record after invasion.

The pre-Hispanic antiquity of some component traditions—Titicaca as a place of emergence, Tiwanaku as ancestral ruins, pacarina origins, stones as embodied ancestors, and wandering culture heroes—is likely. Their exact preconquest configuration under the name Viracocha cannot be securely dated.

### Principal corpus

#### Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas* (c. 1551)

**Language:** Spanish incorporating Quechua names and translated discourse.  
**Transmission:** composed in Cusco; partly published in 1880, with a fuller manuscript identified in the twentieth century.

**[Early colonial attestation]** Betanzos supplies the earliest extensive surviving Viracocha narrative. Contiti Viracocha emerges during primordial darkness, goes to Tiwanaku, makes sun, moon, and stars, fashions peoples, orders them into the landscape, sends assistants along different routes, punishes hostile Canas at Cacha with heavenly fire, travels through Urcos and Cusco, and finally walks over the sea near Puerto Viejo.

Betanzos was an experienced Quechua interpreter and married into the Inca royal elite. That gives his work exceptional value, not immunity from translation, elite politics, Christian vocabulary, or narrative reorganization.

#### Pedro Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú* and *El señorío de los Incas* (1553; second part unpublished until later)

**[Early colonial attestation]** Cieza records Titicaca and Tiwanaku creation traditions, a bearded or clothed culture hero, the name’s application to Spaniards, and disagreement among informants. His willingness to report alternative explanations is important; his “white” or Spanish-like comparisons are colonial descriptions, not preconquest portraits.

#### “Molina the Almagrist” (c. 1552)

A distinct early chronicler, not Cristóbal de Molina “the Cuzqueño.” His surviving account connects Titicaca origin material and Inca lineage. The fragmentary transmission and authorial confusion require caution.

#### Cristóbal de Molina, *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas* (c. 1573–1575)

**Language:** Spanish, with Quechua prayers and ritual formulae.  
**Manuscript:** the authorial original is lost; the principal copy is in Biblioteca Nacional de España MS 3169.

**[Early colonial attestation]** Molina gives alternative creation accounts, a flood, Viracocha’s two sons or agents Imaymana Viracochan and Tocapo Viracochan, and eleven formal prayers associated with the Citua/Zitua ritual. These prayers invoke a remote yet sustaining creator and ask for health, peace, food, increase, and protection.

Because Molina wrote for an ecclesiastical superior and translated Quechua theological language into Christian Spanish, his “Creator” cannot be read without missionary mediation. The Quechua prayer texts nevertheless preserve evidence not reducible to his prose glosses.

#### Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los Incas* (1572)

Commissioned under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo, Sarmiento’s work helped argue that Inca sovereignty was tyrannical and therefore legitimately displaced.

**[Early colonial attestation; learned systematization]** Sarmiento presents Viracocha Pachayachachic creating a first race of giants in darkness, destroying them for disobedience—by flood or petrification—and making a second humanity at Tiwanaku. He orders model figures to emerge from regional *pacarinas*. The political purpose and biblical-looking sequence warn against treating this as unfiltered preconquest canon.

#### Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú* (c. 1580s)

An extirpator’s catalogue of huacas and ritual survivals. It is valuable for demonstrating that actual Andean religious practice remained dispersed among local beings, places, ancestors, and objects rather than being exhausted by a neat Viracocha-centered pantheon.

#### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales* (1609)

**[Later colonial elaboration]** Garcilaso distinguishes the deity from the ruler Viracocha Inca, rejects the “sea-fat” etymology, and constructs an elevated Inca natural theology compatible with providential Christianity. His testimony combines family memory, literary apologetic, Renaissance historiography, and Christian philosophy.

#### Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (c. 1615)

**Language:** Spanish and Quechua; autograph manuscript in the Royal Danish Library.

**[Later colonial elaboration]** Guaman Poma incorporates Viracocha language into a Christian universal history while documenting Inca rulers, ritual specialists, idols, extirpation, and colonial abuses. His drawings of Viracocha Inca are images of the ruler, not secure portraits of the creator deity.

#### Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (completed 1653)

**[Later colonial compilation]** Cobo synthesizes earlier reports, including material related to Polo de Ondegardo. He describes Viracocha’s Cusco temple, statue, sacrifices, and the ceque system. Because he frequently compiles older material without always exposing its chain of transmission, early information and seventeenth-century arrangement must be separated.

#### Huarochirí Manuscript (compiled c. 1598–early 1600s)

**Language:** Classical Quechua; preserved in BNE MS 3169.  
**Modern critical access:** José María Arguedas; Gerald Taylor; Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste.

**[Regional variant]** Its **Cuniraya Huiracocha** is a ragged wandering creator-trickster. He shapes terraces and irrigation channels by speech, secretly impregnates the virgin huaca Cahuillaca through a lúcuma fruit, pursues her to the coast, rewards or curses animals he questions, and becomes entangled with Pachacamac’s household. This is not simply the Cusco imperial creator wearing another name.

#### Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621), and Huarochirí/Cajatambo trials

These document campaigns against huacas, ancestral mummies, diviners, offerings, festivals, and local ritual experts. Viracocha is much less central than later textbook pantheons imply. That relative absence is historically significant: everyday and regional religion centered heavily on local powers.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Early colonial attestation]**

Viracocha’s securely recorded functions include:

- bringing celestial light to a dark world;
- making or ordering sun, moon, and stars;
- forming prototypes of peoples, often in stone;
- assigning peoples their dress, language, customs, and homelands;
- commanding them to emerge from caves, springs, mountains, trees, and lakes;
- ordering agricultural terrain, water, terraces, and irrigation;
- punishing disobedience by fire, flood, or petrification;
- validating rulers, especially Pachacuti in the Chanca-war tradition;
- receiving prayers for health, prosperity, peace, crops, and dynastic stability.

“Creation from nothing” is not securely attested as an indigenous philosophical doctrine. Much of the evidence concerns transformation, animation, differentiation, emergence, and proper emplacement.

### Form

Betanzos’s Cacha informants described a tall human figure in a long white garment reaching the ankles, belted, with short hair and a head covering resembling that of a priest, carrying an object Betanzos compared to a breviary. The comparison is Spanish; the object’s indigenous identity is unknown.

Cobo reports a small, solid-gold anthropomorphic statue at Cusco’s **Quishuarcancha/Kiswarkancha**, about the size of a ten-year-old boy. Other traditions describe a much larger stone image at Cacha/Raqchi.

These descriptions conflict because they refer to different cult images and perhaps different Viracocha manifestations. There was no single surviving canonical portrait.

### The Tiwanaku “Viracocha” problem

**[Material evidence]** The frontal staff-bearing figure on Tiwanaku’s Gateway of the Sun belongs to Tiwanaku iconography, centuries before the Inca Empire. Similar staff-deity imagery has deeper Central Andean precedents.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Scholars have variously called this figure a Staff God, solar deity, weather deity, Tunupa, or Viracocha. No accompanying text names it. Calling it “Viracocha” as a fact is unjustified. The familiar modern image of Viracocha with a rayed face and two staffs largely results from this speculative identification, museum illustration, tourism, textbooks, comics, and commercial art.

### Symbols and substances

No securely documented exclusive Viracocha animal, color, weekday, planet, or numerical emblem survives. White clothing belongs to one colonial description; it is not a universal sacred color. Water, stone, fire from heaven, staffs or rods, roads, springs, and the sea belong to particular narratives. Gold statues indicate state cult and elite value but do not make gold uniquely his substance.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical systems

Viracocha is usually unparented in the Cusco creation accounts. Calling him “father of the Sun” is appropriate where a source says he made or generated the Sun, but it should not automatically be turned into biological genealogy.

Molina’s account gives him two sons or emanated agents:

- **Imaymana Viracochan**, sent along the mountain or eastern route and associated with ordering useful and harmful plants;
- **Tocapo Viracochan**, sent by another route and described through an opaque title that Molina interprets as encompassing creation.

Betanzos instead speaks of Viracocha’s created or accompanying helpers, one of whom becomes the rebellious **Taguapaca**. Chroniclers and later scholars variously connect Taguapaca/Tarapaca with Tunupa; the equation is plausible in some colonial systems but not universal.

Cuniraya Huiracocha has a child by Cahuillaca in the Huarochirí account, but that regional genealogy cannot automatically be assigned to the imperial creator.

### Documented divine interactions

The following list is exhaustive for the principal narrative complexes surveyed, not for every occurrence of the word *viracocha* in all surviving colonial litigation.

- **Inti/Sun — creates and sets the Sun’s course.** Betanzos, *Suma*, c. 1551. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Moon — creates the Moon after establishing daylight.** Betanzos, c. 1551; parallels in Molina and Sarmiento. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Stars — creates and orders them at Tiwanaku.** Betanzos, c. 1551. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Imaymana Viracochan — sends his son/agent to order peoples, plants, and landscapes along one route.** Molina, c. 1575. **[Early colonial attestation; learned systematization]**
- **Tocapo Viracochan — sends his second son/agent along another route.** Molina, c. 1575. **[Early colonial attestation; learned systematization]**
- **Taguapaca — assistant rebels or disobeys; Viracocha has him bound and cast onto water, after which he continues as a disruptive figure.** Betanzos, c. 1551. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Tunupa — equated by some colonial and later writers with Taguapaca/Tarapaca or a Viracocha-like itinerant preacher.** Santa Cruz Pachacuti and later scholarship. **[Syncretistic identification; disputed]**
- **Cahuillaca/Cavillaca — Cuniraya secretly impregnates her through a lúcuma fruit; she rejects him and flees with their child.** Huarochirí Manuscript, c. 1598–1608. **[Regional variant]**
- **Cahuillaca’s child — Cuniraya is revealed as father when the child crawls to him; the child later becomes an offshore island with its mother.** Huarochirí Manuscript. **[Regional variant]**
- **Other assembled huacas — Cuniraya sits among them in beggar’s rags during the paternity test and is despised.** Huarochirí Manuscript. **[Regional variant]**
- **Pachacamac — Cuniraya reaches Pachacamac’s coastal domain and household while pursuing Cahuillaca. No secure source makes the two simply identical.** Huarochirí Manuscript. **[Regional variant]**
- **Urpayhuachac — Cuniraya, finding her absent, attempts sexual relations with her daughters and retaliates against her by casting her fish into the sea, explaining the sea’s abundance.** Huarochirí Manuscript. **[Regional variant]**
- **Urpayhuachac’s daughters — objects of Cuniraya’s attempted sexual aggression; one escapes by transforming into a dove in the fullest versions.** Huarochirí Manuscript. **[Regional variant]**
- **Pariacaca — both occur in the Huarochirí religious universe, but no major direct narrative encounter between Pariacaca and Cuniraya is securely recorded in the core episodes.** **[Absence of attested interaction]**
- **Christian God — Viracocha is translated, equated, or subordinated to *Dios/Creador* by missionaries and Christian Andean authors.** Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries. **[Syncretistic identification]**

No early source securely gives Viracocha Mama Cocha or Pachamama as a wife. Such family trees are mainly modern harmonizations.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Darkness, Titicaca, and Tiwanaku

**[Early colonial attestation: Betanzos, c. 1551]**

The land initially lacks daylight. Contiti Viracocha emerges from a lake in Collasuyu and proceeds to Tiwanaku. There he makes the Sun and day, commands the Sun’s course, and makes Moon and stars.

He fashions human figures or prototypes in stone, giving each nation distinctive clothing, hair, language, songs, crops, and homeland. These are not all animated at Tiwanaku. Viracocha and his agents travel through the Andes calling them from their *pacarinas*. Thus creation is also imperial geography: ethnic diversity is represented as one ordered deployment.

**Variants:** Sarmiento adds a first creation of giants, their disobedience, and their destruction by flood or petrification before the second humanity. Molina preserves multiple accounts rather than one fixed sequence. Cieza connects Titicaca, Tiwanaku, and a traveling civilizer but reports disagreements.

**Historical assessment:** The Titicaca–Tiwanaku connection plausibly reflects Inca appropriation of ancient monumental ruins and altiplano sacred geography. It does not demonstrate that Tiwanaku people called their deity Viracocha.

### B. The helpers and the peopling of the Andes

Viracocha sends assistants on different routes while he proceeds through the center. They call nations from hills, caves, springs, and other origin places. The accounts map creation onto roads and imperial divisions.

In Molina, Imaymana and Tocapo are sons or extensions of the creator. In Betanzos, one helper’s disobedience produces conflict with Taguapaca. These cannot be merged without losing the sources’ different structures.

### C. Cacha/Raqchi and fire from heaven

**[Early colonial attestation: Betanzos]**

At Cacha, the Canas come armed against the unknown traveler. Viracocha calls down fire that burns a mountainside. When they submit, he stops the fire with his staff and identifies himself as their maker. They erect a huaca and a great stone image.

Betanzos says he inspected the burned mountain and interviewed elders. That is evidence that a sixteenth-century local community explained a visible landscape feature through Viracocha; it is not physical proof of supernatural fire.

The narrative helped integrate Canas territory and a monumental cult center into Inca sacred history. Archaeology at Raqchi shows substantial Inca construction over a place with earlier occupation, including Wari-period remains.

### D. Urcos, Cusco, and the journey to the sea

Viracocha continues through Urcos, where a rich shrine commemorates his resting place, reaches Cusco, appoints or creates a lord called Alcaviza in Betanzos’s account, and moves northwest. He reunites with his assistants near the coast and walks over the ocean “as if on land.”

The sources do not consistently say that he arrived from across the Atlantic, promised to return, or was white. “Departure over the sea” became the foundation of the colonial and modern returning-white-god story, but that elaboration is not the early narrative itself.

### E. Pachacuti’s vision and the Chanca war

In traditions transmitted by Betanzos and Cobo, the future Pachacuti receives a vision, prophecy, or promise of victory from Viracocha before defeating the Chancas. Cobo says this victory intensified devotion and prompted Pachacuti’s temple and image at Cusco.

Other accounts make the apparition solar or give greater place to the Sun. The variation probably reflects political theology: Pachacuti’s authority could be grounded in Viracocha, Inti, an ancestral double, or several powers.

### F. Cuniraya and Cahuillaca

**[Regional variant: Huarochirí Manuscript]**

Cuniraya Huiracocha wanders disguised as a filthy, ragged poor man, though his speech alone forms fields, terraces, and irrigation works. Desiring the virgin huaca Cahuillaca, he changes into a bird, deposits semen in a lúcuma fruit, and causes it to fall near her. She eats it and conceives.

When the child can crawl, Cahuillaca assembles the male huacas and asks the child to identify its father. It crawls to the despised Cuniraya. Horrified that so poor-looking a being fathered her child, she flees toward the sea. Cuniraya puts on splendid golden clothing and pursues her, but she never turns back. Mother and child enter the sea and become islands.

On the road Cuniraya asks animals whether he can catch her. Helpful animals receive blessings; discouraging ones are cursed, creating their subsequent characteristics. At Pachacamac he enters Urpayhuachac’s household, assaults or attempts to assault her daughters, and throws her fish into the ocean, an etiology for marine abundance.

This is a trickster and sexual-aggression narrative, not a sanitized monotheistic creation myth. Its Viracocha compound may reflect Inca overlay, regional translation, or a shared title; identity with Betanzos’s Contiti cannot be assumed.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cusco: Kiswarkancha/Quishuarcancha

Cobo describes a temple called **Quishuarcancha**, dedicated to Viracocha and associated with Pachacuti. It housed a solid-gold anthropomorphic image. Colonial urban transformation erased or incorporated much of the complex; the area became entangled with episcopal and cathedral property.

Viracocha Inca also possessed a royal compound bearing the Kiswarkancha name in some accounts, so references to “palace” and “temple” must not be conflated automatically.

### Coricancha

Chroniclers describe creator imagery or a place for Viracocha within Cusco’s principal state sanctuary, but the Coricancha was above all a complex containing solar, lunar, stellar, weather, dynastic, and conquered-huaca relationships. Statements that it was simply “Viracocha’s temple” overstate the evidence.

Spanish seizure stripped its precious metal and the Dominican complex of Santo Domingo was built over and through it. Surviving masonry attests the sanctuary, not the identification of every chamber.

### Raqchi/Cacha

The monumental building at Raqchi is identified in colonial and later records as Viracocha’s temple. Its central wall supported an enormous roofed hall approximately 92 meters long. Archaeological work has corrected older interpretations: 152 circular buildings once routinely called Inca storehouses contain evidence of earlier Wari-period labor habitation or occupation, while the conspicuous temple is Inca.

The site materializes an imperial appropriation of Canas sacred geography and perhaps earlier Wari authority. It does not prove an unbroken Wari-to-Inca worship of a deity with the same name.

### Urcos and other shrines

Betanzos reports a wealthy Urcos shrine with a gold bench and image commemorating Viracocha’s stop there. Cobo’s ceque information mentions a rounded stone regarded as the *guauque*—sacred double or brother-image—of Ticci Viracocha. Such evidence suggests multiple material embodiments rather than a wholly imageless creator.

### Priests, prayers, and offerings

Molina’s Quechua prayers are the best direct textual evidence for formal worship. They ask Viracocha for vitality, peace, freedom from illness, fertility, food, and protection of the Inca and people. The creator may be addressed as remote—“Where are you?”—yet effective through the celestial and terrestrial order.

Offerings recorded for major state deities included textiles, coca, chicha, maize, camelids, precious metal, shell, and, on exceptional occasions, humans. Exact allocation among Viracocha, Inti, Illapa, royal ancestors, and local huacas varies by source.

### Human sacrifice

**[Documented practice; attribution requires care]**

Colonial sources including Molina, Betanzos, Cobo, Guaman Poma, Polo de Ondegardo, Murúa, Acosta, and Hernández Príncipe describe *qhapaq hucha/capacocha*, sometimes dedicating children to Viracocha, the Sun, Thunder, major huacas, or the welfare of the ruler. Archaeology independently confirms Inca child sacrifice at high-altitude and other shrines.

No excavated child can normally be assigned specifically to Viracocha without an associated text. Statements that “Viracocha demanded hundreds of children” depend on late reports, theological interpretation, or disputed readings and should not be generalized.

### Political economy

State cult redistributed labor, textiles, livestock, maize, chicha, precious materials, and captives or selected children through Cusco and provincial shrines. Viracocha’s myths converted roads, conquered peoples, and regional origin places into parts of a single imperial sacred history. Raqchi particularly shows how cult, architecture, ethnicity, labor, and state authority converged.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before c. 1400: reconstructable background

**[Material evidence; modern reconstruction]** Titicaca and Tiwanaku had been sacred and politically important long before the Inca Empire. Staff-bearing divine imagery existed for centuries, and Central Andean religions widely treated springs, lakes, stones, mountains, ancestors, and origin places as animate powers.

There is no secure evidence that these older images or cults used the name Viracocha. Claims extending his named worship to Chavín, Wari, Caral, or Tiwanaku are hypotheses or modern inventions unless carefully qualified.

### Fifteenth century: imperial formulation

Colonial accounts associate heightened Viracocha patronage with Pachacuti’s victory over the Chancas and his rebuilding of Cusco. Some scholars reconstruct competition or complementarity between a creator cult and the dynastic solar cult of Inti. Others see less a doctrinal struggle than overlapping royal, lineage, calendrical, and regional institutions.

The construction or monumentalization of Raqchi and the integration of Titicaca into Inca pilgrimage and imperial memory belong to this phase.

### 1530s–1570s: invasion, translation, and confiscation

Spanish forces looted temples, melted images, seized lands, and attacked religious personnel. Missionaries rendered Viracocha as *Dios*, *Hacedor*, or a distorted memory of the biblical creator. At the same time Andean speakers used *viracocha* for Spaniards.

This period produced both preservation and transformation: the first extensive texts exist because colonial writers questioned Indigenous informants, yet their questions, genres, theology, and coercive setting shaped the answers.

### 1570s–1650s: extirpation and learned theology

Toledo’s regime reorganized communities and used historiography to delegitimize Inca sovereignty. Extirpation campaigns seized huacas, burned or removed sacred objects and mummies, punished ritual specialists, and prosecuted continued worship. Huarochirí and Cajatambo trial records demonstrate resilient local cults but rarely a centralized underground Viracocha church.

Molina, Sarmiento, Garcilaso, Guaman Poma, Arriaga, and Cobo increasingly systematized diverse traditions. The polished “Inca supreme creator” familiar today is partly the product of this colonial archive.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: antiquarian deity

Viracocha persisted in chronicles, dictionaries, local place traditions, and antiquarian writing. Nineteenth-century travelers and archaeologists linked Tiwanaku’s Gateway figure to the creator and helped establish the modern visual convention.

### Twentieth century: national and scholarly reconstruction

Indigenismo, archaeology, comparative religion, tourism, and school curricula turned Viracocha into a recognizable emblem of pan-Andean civilization. Rowe, Zuidema, Duviols, Rostworowski, Urbano, MacCormack, Bauer, Salomon, Urton, Itier, Cerrón-Palomino, and others complicated that picture by emphasizing source politics, regional cults, linguistic ambiguity, ceque organization, missionary translation, and imperial appropriation.

### Present

Contemporary Quechua and Aymara religious life is diverse and often combines Catholic saints, Christ, the Virgin, Pachamama, apus, ancestors, household rites, pilgrimage, and offerings. Viracocha appears in educational, heritage, artistic, New Age, and some consciously reconstructionist settings.

The available ethnography does not establish a large, continuous, institutionally distinct cult of Viracocha surviving unchanged from Inca times in Cusco, the altiplano, or northwestern Argentina. Modern invocations may be sincere living practice but must be dated as revival, heritage religion, syncretism, or individualized spirituality rather than presumed continuity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### The “white god”

The expanded popular portrait—tall, white-skinned, blue-eyed, blond or white-bearded, robed like a European, arriving from overseas, teaching civilization, promising to return—is not a securely transmitted preconquest description.

Early chroniclers sometimes say “white,” “bearded,” robed, or Spanish-looking, but they wrote after Europeans had arrived and compared unfamiliar Andean imagery to Christian priests and apostles. Different sources refer to different wandering figures. The full racialized package was amplified by colonial providential history, nineteenth-century diffusionism, Mormon apologetic, occult writing, and twentieth-century pseudoarchaeology.

The conclusion is not that every garment or beard was invented—pre-Hispanic Andean art can show facial hair—but that neither facial hair nor a tunic demonstrates a European culture-bringer.

### Tiwanaku commercial icon

The Gateway of the Sun’s frontal figure is now sold as Viracocha on jewelry, textiles, statues, tattoos, and tourist art. This identity is modern and uncertain. The image is materially ancient; the proper name attached to it is not securely recoverable.

### Kon-Tiki and transoceanic diffusion

Thor Heyerdahl’s 1947 *Kon-Tiki* expedition popularized an equation between the name Kon-Tiki/Viracocha and hypothetical voyages from South America into Polynesia. The voyage demonstrated that a raft journey was possible, not that Viracocha was a historical navigator or that the myth records such colonization. Linguistic, archaeological, and genetic questions about Pacific contact must be evaluated independently of the divine narrative.

### Esoteric and New Age reception

Modern esoteric literature treats Viracocha as a cosmic mind, ascended master, extraterrestrial architect, Atlantean survivor, or keeper of “ancient frequencies.” No Inca or early colonial source supports these identities. “Seven shining ones,” global pyramid grids, alien technology, and lost white master-builders are modern inventions.

Viracocha has not had a major documented place in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or the classical European grimoire tradition. Claims of an ancient Western occult Viracocha current are therefore anachronistic.

### Literature, comics, games, and heritage

Viracocha appears in historical fiction, fantasy games, comics, museum interpretation, national imagery, music, tourism, and online mythology. These media often merge:

- the Gateway of the Sun figure;
- the Betanzos creator;
- Tunupa;
- Cuniraya;
- Inti;
- the ruler Viracocha Inca;
- and the returning-white-god legend.

That composite is influential but modern.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Creator or missionary construction?

One pole accepts a major pre-Hispanic creator called Viracocha whose traditions the chronicles imperfectly preserve. Another stresses that missionaries selected and elevated creator-like terms to build a native anticipation of Christianity. Fernando Armas Asín speaks strongly of colonial “invention”; Itier reconstructs a real Andean water and agricultural divinity subsequently transformed by evangelization.

The evidence favors neither total fabrication nor transparent preservation. Independent chronicles, Quechua prayers, temples, and regional compounds support pre-Hispanic religious material; the systematic supreme-being theology is visibly colonialized.

### Creator, organizer, or water power?

Translations of *Pachayachachiq* have decisive consequences. “World teacher” suggests a civilizing sage; “world maker” suggests creation; “one who makes the world attain its productive condition” suggests agricultural ordering. The colonial corpus supports creation, animation, emplacement, water control, and teaching, but not necessarily one abstract doctrine.

### One god or a divine category?

The compounds Contiti Viracocha, Cuniraya Viracocha, Imaymana Viracochan, Tocapo Viracochan, and the plural-like traveling *viracochas* have led scholars to ask whether *Viracocha* could sometimes be a title, class, quality, or divine mode rather than a unique personal name. The sources do not yield one answer for all periods and regions.

### Viracocha versus Inti

Older scholarship often ranked Viracocha as remote supreme creator and Inti as active state god. Other reconstructions connect Viracocha with particular royal factions, especially the ideology of Pachacuti, or see his elevation as part of colonial monotheizing. Cobo’s claim that victory over the Chancas strengthened Viracocha’s cult is important but late.

### Tiwanaku continuity

The Inca demonstrably revered and reused Tiwanaku as an ancestral place. Direct continuity from the Tiwanaku Gateway deity to Inca Viracocha is not demonstrated. Similar iconography can survive or be reinterpreted without preserving the same name or theology.

### Source criticism

- **Betanzos:** early and linguistically valuable, but shaped by Cusco royal informants and Spanish categories.
- **Cieza:** comparative and often cautious, but still colonial and Christian.
- **Sarmiento:** rich but written for Toledo’s anti-Inca political project.
- **Molina:** uniquely preserves prayers but wrote within evangelization.
- **Garcilaso:** indispensable, apologetic, and Renaissance-Christian.
- **Guaman Poma:** an Indigenous-Christian intellectual constructing universal history.
- **Cobo:** systematic and detailed but late and compilatory.
- **Huarochirí Manuscript:** unparalleled Quechua regional evidence collected amid extirpation; not a neutral transcript of timeless oral tradition.
- **Idolatry trials:** detailed but coercive; testimony could be strategic, translated, and produced under threat.

### Lost and surviving evidence

Most precious-metal cult images were looted and melted. Organic ritual objects and many local shrines disappeared or were Christianized. Molina’s original manuscript is lost; the surviving copy is in BNE MS 3169. The Huarochirí Quechua manuscript survives in the same composite collection. Guaman Poma’s autograph survives at the Royal Danish Library and was rediscovered for modern scholarship in 1908.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 500 CE:** Staff-bearing divine imagery and sacred-water traditions exist in the Central Andes. No image is securely named Viracocha. **[Material evidence]**
- **c. 500–1000:** Tiwanaku flourishes; the Gateway of the Sun’s frontal figure is carved. Its identity remains unknown. **[Material evidence]**
- **c. 600–1000:** Wari occupation or labor facilities exist at Raqchi. Continuous worship of a named Viracocha is unproven.
- **c. 1400–1532:** Inca rulers monumentalize Cusco, Titicaca, Raqchi, and imperial pilgrimage networks. Colonial sources later associate Pachacuti with a Viracocha vision and temple building.
- **1532–1530s:** Spanish invasion; temple property and images are seized or destroyed. *Viracocha* begins appearing as a designation for Spaniards.
- **c. 1551:** Betanzos composes the earliest surviving extensive Viracocha narrative.
- **1553:** Cieza publishes the first part of his *Crónica*; his Inca history and creation traditions circulate in manuscript.
- **1572:** Sarmiento completes the Toledan *Historia de los Incas*.
- **c. 1573–1575:** Cristóbal de Molina records creation accounts, rituals, and Quechua prayers.
- **c. 1580s:** Albornoz catalogues huacas and methods for detecting continued worship.
- **c. 1598–1608:** The Huarochirí Manuscript records Cuniraya Huiracocha in Quechua.
- **1609:** Garcilaso publishes Part I of the *Comentarios reales*.
- **c. 1615:** Guaman Poma completes the *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*.
- **1621:** Arriaga publishes *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú*; Ramos Gavilán publishes his Copacabana history.
- **1653:** Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, preserving temple and ceque information.
- **Nineteenth century:** Travelers and early archaeologists increasingly name the Tiwanaku Gateway figure Viracocha.
- **1880:** A partial Betanzos text is published by Marcos Jiménez de la Espada.
- **1892:** Stübel and Uhle publish major documentation of Tiwanaku and discuss Viracocha identifications.
- **1908:** Richard Pietschmann identifies Guaman Poma’s manuscript in Copenhagen.
- **1936:** Guaman Poma facsimile published.
- **1946:** John H. Rowe publishes a foundational reconstruction of Inca culture and religion.
- **1947:** Heyerdahl’s *Kon-Tiki* voyage gives global publicity to diffusionist Viracocha speculation.
- **1966:** Arguedas’s influential Spanish Huarochirí translation appears.
- **1970s–1990s:** Zuidema, Duviols, Rostworowski, Urbano, MacCormack, Salomon, and others foreground regional variation, colonial mediation, and political theology.
- **1991:** Salomon and Urioste publish a Quechua-English Huarochirí edition and translation.
- **1998 onward:** The Raqchi Project surveys and excavates the temple complex and surrounding occupation.
- **2012–2013:** Itier publishes his water/oceanic and linguistic reconstruction of Viracocha.
- **2010s–2020s:** Archaeological and linguistic studies increasingly reject automatic identification of every ancient Staff God as Viracocha while heritage and New Age uses continue expanding.
- **Present:** Viracocha remains active in scholarship, heritage discourse, tourism, art, individualized spirituality, and limited revivalist practice; unbroken centralized worship is not demonstrated.
