# Pachacamac: Research Dossier

**Class:** male coastal Andean *wak’a* and creator/sustainer deity; an embodied oracular power attached to the sanctuary at the mouth of the Lurín River, later incorporated into the Inca imperial pantheon without simply becoming an Inca sun god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** materially, the surviving bifront wooden cult image from Pachacamac was carved in approximately **760–876 CE**, but its original name cannot be recovered from the object itself. Textually, the place, oracle, priesthood, and wooden idol called Pachacama/Pachacamac enter the surviving record in accounts of **Hernando Pizarro’s expedition of January 1533**, especially Pizarro’s 1533 letter and Miguel de Estete’s near-contemporary narrative. The common assertion that the named cult existed from ca. 200 CE confuses the sanctuary’s early occupation with secure evidence for the later deity’s name.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Materially attested]** Archaeological, osteological, radiocarbon, pigment, or architectural evidence.
- **[Early colonial testimony]** Sixteenth-century observations or reports, usually mediated by Spanish categories and produced after invasion.
- **[Late colonial tradition]** Seventeenth-century Indigenous testimony, missionary collection, or chronicler synthesis.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally collected narrative not automatically applicable throughout the Andes.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historical equation between otherwise distinguishable powers.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An interpretation joining incomplete archaeological and textual evidence.
- **[Modern reception/invention]** A postcolonial literary, religious, commercial, or popular construction.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**[Early colonial testimony]** Pachacamac was the feared and prestigious oracle of the central Peruvian coast. His principal sanctuary stood about 31 km southeast of modern Lima, near the mouth of the Lurín River. Sixteenth-century Spaniards found an active priesthood, restricted sacred precincts, tributary wealth, female religious personnel, storehouses, pilgrims, and a wooden image housed in a dark inner chamber. Hernando Pizarro called the complex a *mezquita*, the chief priest an *obispo*, and the deity a *diablo*: European analogies that document Spanish perception, not Indigenous classifications.

**[Late colonial tradition]** Andean testimony calls him a maker or animator of the earth, an oracle, a giver of sustenance, and a power whose movement caused earthquakes. These functions do not form a single timeless theology. Creation, food, subterranean motion, prophecy, fertility, and political sovereignty are distributed differently among the sources.

### Names and spellings

- **Pacha Kamaq**, modern standardized Quechua spelling.
- **Pachacamac, Pachacámac, Pachacama**, common colonial and modern Spanish forms.
- **Pachakamaq**, another modern normalized form.
- **Ychsma, Ychma, Ichma, Ishma, Irma**, names associated with the lower Lurín–Rímac polity, valley, sanctuary, pigment, and perhaps the pre-Inca deity.
- **Pachacamac / Ychsma-Pachacamac**, a useful scholarly compound, but not itself a securely attested ancient formal title.

Quechua **pacha** encompasses earth, world, space-time, era, or ordered totality. **Kamaq** is the agentive form of *kamay*: to give being, animate, sustain, order, or produce. Accordingly, “Maker of the World,” “Animator of the Earth,” “Giver of Being,” and “Sustainer of the Pacha” are defensible contextual translations. “Earth-maker” is concise but narrower than the semantic range. “Soul of the Earth,” frequent in tourism literature, is interpretive rather than literal.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The frequent claim that *Ychsma* was Pachacamac’s original divine name rests on the pre-Inca regional name, colonial statements about the valley’s renaming, and the association of *ichma* with red pigment or cinnabar. It is plausible, especially in Rostworowski’s reconstruction, but there is no surviving pre-Inca inscription saying “this deity is Ychsma.” Krzysztof Makowski emphasizes that the colonial record often comes second- or third-hand and that some traditions were recorded far from Lurín or a century after the events they describe. He also notes proposals that *Ychsma/Irma* is Aymaran rather than Quechua. The equation must therefore remain a hypothesis, not a recovered native self-identification. [Makowski’s study](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/667af451-4f8f-4dae-9cdd-7e203f0082b4)

### Titles and functional descriptions

The following are descriptions rather than a securely preserved litany of formal epithets:

- **“He who gives being to the earth.”** Reported in colonial explanations of the Quechua name.
- **“Creator of the plains/coast.”** A colonial and modern contrast with highland Viracocha.
- **Oracle.** Securely attested in conquest accounts and later chronicles.
- **Lord/master of earthquakes.** Strong in later colonial descriptions and modern Peruvian memory.
- **Giver or creator of food.** Central to Calancha’s seventeenth-century Vichama narrative.
- **Father.** The Huarochirí chapter on *qhapaq hucha* offerings records victims presented to him with the formula “here they are; I offer them to you, father.”
- **Principal *wak’a* of Ychsma.** A historically appropriate classification, although Spanish writers normally render *wak’a* as “idol,” “temple,” or “devil.”

### Equations with other beings

- **Pachacamac and Viracocha:** Colonial authors sometimes treated both names as titles of a single supreme creator. Other evidence keeps them distinct. This is an Inca/colonial-era theological equation, not proof of primordial identity.
- **Pachacamac and the Christian God:** Cieza de León and Garcilaso report or construct an equivalence between the invisible creator and the Christians’ God. This interpretation is entangled with missionary monotheism and elite colonial apologetics.
- **Pachacamac and Inti/Punchao:** The Inca built a conspicuous Temple of the Sun above the older sacred precinct. Co-location and political hierarchy do not mean identity. Peter Eeckhout interprets the arrangement as an imperial opposition or complementarity between coastal/lower Ychsma-Pachacamac and Inca solar power. [Eeckhout’s argument](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-73562004000200019&script=sci_arttext)
- **Pachacamac and the Señor de los Milagros:** María Rostworowski proposed a historical transformation linking the earthquake-associated coastal deity, Pachacamilla, red/purple symbolism, and Lima’s Black Christ. This is an influential modern syncretism thesis, not demonstrated continuity of a single cult.

No secure evidence makes Pachacamac simply another name for Pachamama.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaeological sequence versus named deity

**[Materially attested]** The Pachacamac locality was occupied by at least the late Formative or early centuries CE. Monumental construction conventionally assigned to the Lima culture includes the Old Temple and early phases of the Painted Temple. Wari influence became pronounced during the Middle Horizon, followed by Ychsma occupation and then Inca remodeling after approximately 1470. These phases establish a long sacred and political landscape; they do not, by themselves, identify every period’s divine recipient as Pachacamac. The Peruvian Ministry of Culture accordingly distinguishes the site’s occupational history from the later documentary identification of the god. [Official sanctuary history](https://pachacamac.cultura.pe/bienvenidos-al-santuario-arqueologico-de-pachacamac)

### The wooden cult image

**[Materially attested]** Alberto Giesecke found the approximately 2.34-metre carved wooden post in rubble at the Painted Temple in 1938. The image has two anthropomorphic upper faces and densely carved human, feline, avian, serpentine, and plant figures below. Direct AMS dating produced **1289 ± 25 BP, calibrated 760–876 CE**. Wood anatomy indicates a legume, probably *Prosopis* or *Vachellia*. Microscopy and X-ray fluorescence detected white, yellow, and red pigments; the red included cinnabar that had to be transported from outside the immediate area. Its iconography has Middle Horizon/Wari affinities. [Sepúlveda et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6961831/)

This establishes a major polychrome cult object at Pachacamac for the Middle Horizon. It does **not** prove:

1. that it was originally named Pachacamac;
2. that its iconographic figures constitute a literal portrait of one anthropomorphic god;
3. that it is the intact object Hernando Pizarro claimed to have broken in 1533.

It may be the famous oracle, another image concealed or displaced during the attack, or a surviving part of a longer succession of images. That it was found near the historically described sacred chamber makes the identification strong but not absolute.

### Conquest-period testimony

#### Hernando Pizarro, letter of 1533

**[Early colonial testimony]** Pizarro says he traveled to a “mosque” at a town called Pachacama. He reports:

- coastal communities tributary to the sanctuary rather than directly to Cuzco;
- a chief priest then with Atahualpa at Cajamarca;
- restricted female compounds and storehouses;
- messengers consulting ritual specialists who entered a small chamber;
- a greatly feared oracle;
- a dark, dirty cave or chamber containing the idol;
- coercive entry into the sanctuary and the forced exposure of the chamber.

He also admits torturing an elderly attendant to obtain information. His description is eyewitness evidence for the institution but openly hostile testimony framed by demonology and conquest. [Pizarro’s letter](https://kuprienko.info/hernando-pizarro-carta-de-hernando-pizarro-a-los-oidores-de-la-audiencia-de-santo-domingo-1533/)

#### Miguel de Estete, ca. 1533–1535

Estete, a participant in the expedition, gives the fullest early account of the approach through successive guarded doors, the dark chamber, the wooden image, accumulated offerings, and the Spaniards’ destruction. He also records the oracle’s political reach. His narrative and Pizarro’s letter are the nearest surviving texts to the attack; later chronicles frequently depend on conquest testimony of this kind.

### Major colonial corpus

- **Francisco de Xerez, *Verdadera relación de la conquista del Perú* (1534):** conquest narrative describing the riches and religious prestige of the sanctuary.
- **Miguel de Estete, conquest relation (composed ca. 1533–1535):** eyewitness description of Pizarro’s expedition and the idol.
- **Pedro Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú*, Part I (1553), and *El señorío de los Incas* (written ca. 1550s):** antiquity and authority of the temple, Inca accommodation of the cult, and conflicting reports about imperial visits.
- **Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas* (1551):** Inca creator theology and terminology relevant to later equations with Viracocha.
- **Cristóbal de Molina, “the Cuzqueño,” *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas* (ca. 1575):** Inca ritual calendar and *qhapaq hucha* context; more informative about imperial religion generally than about the local pre-Inca cult.
- **Hernando de Santillán, *Relación del origen, descendencia, política y gobierno de los Incas* (1560s):** reports offerings of girls to Pachacamac and an imperial equation of Ticci Viracocha with Pachacamac.
- **Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú* (ca. 1580s):** cataloguing logic of extirpation and provincial *wak’as*; useful for the colonial classification of Andean sacred powers.
- **Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los Incas* (1572):** Toledo-era official history, including the political employment of oracles.
- **José de Acosta, *Historia natural y moral de las Indias* (1590):** comparative missionary interpretation of Indigenous religion, idols, divination, and pigments.
- **Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales* (1609) and *Historia general del Perú* (1617):** an elite mestizo-Christian synthesis identifying Pachacamac with an invisible supreme maker more readily than earlier local evidence warrants.
- **Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (ca. 1615):** places sacrifices to Pachacamac within the Inca calendar and preserves an Indigenous-Christian critique of both Inca and Spanish orders.
- **Huarochirí Manuscript (Quechua, ca. 1598–1608):** collected in the orbit of Francisco de Ávila’s anti-idolatry campaign; the richest Indigenous-language corpus touching Pachacamac’s family, islands, fish, pilgrimages, and annual *qhapaq hucha*. Frank Salomon and George Urioste’s 1991 edition and translation, alongside Gerald Taylor’s Quechua/Spanish editions, are fundamental. [Edition record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/730526)
- **Antonio de la Calancha, *Corónica moralizada del Orden de San Agustín en el Perú* (1638):** preserves the fullest surviving Pachacamac–Vichama creation story, reportedly from the north-central coast. It is a late regional narrative, not a pre-Conquest pan-Andean scripture.
- **Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (completed 1653):** systematic Jesuit synthesis of creator gods, temples, offerings, and live burial.
- **Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621):** procedural manual for locating and destroying *wak’as*, sacred objects, ritual specialists, and ancestral cults. It is crucial evidence for suppression, although not a neutral ethnography.

### Huarochirí and Cajatambo trials

The Huarochirí and Cajatambo “idolatry” dossiers record seventeenth-century testimony about local *wak’as*, ancestral bundles, ritual specialists, offerings, and concealed observances. Their value lies in named witnesses and local ritual detail; their limitations include coercive examination, translation through clerks and priests, and the trials’ purpose of prosecution. They reveal the persistence of Andean sacred networks after official Christianization but do not establish that every surviving rite was addressed to Pachacamac.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

- **Creation or animation:** Colonial explanations make Pachacamac the being who animates or gives existence to the world, especially the coast.
- **Earthquakes and subterranean force:** Later descriptions locate him beneath or within the earth; a movement of his head could shake the world.
- **Oracular speech:** The most securely documented functional attribute in 1533. Priests conveyed his answers concerning politics, warfare, tribute, health, and required offerings.
- **Food and fertility:** Calancha’s narrative explains cultivated plants as transformations of a murdered divine child’s body.
- **Fish and marine abundance:** The Huarochirí story of Urpayhuachac makes the sanctuary’s divine household the possessor or producer of fishes, though Urpayhuachac—not Pachacamac alone—performs the decisive act.
- **Healing:** Later accounts and archaeology characterize pilgrims as seeking cures; human remains and burial assemblages have been interpreted in relation to healing pilgrimage.
- **Death and burial:** Elite and nonlocal burials accumulated near the sanctuary. Proximity to the *wak’a* appears to have carried prestige, although burial at the site does not prove worship of an underworld god.

### Image and recognizability

The surviving wooden image is tall, narrow, bifront, and carved in registers. The upper portion bears two human faces looking in opposite directions, each with headdress or coiffure. Lower registers include human and nonhuman figures, felines, snakes, birds, and vegetation.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The two faces have prompted interpretations involving duality, complementary opposites, day/night, male/female, past/future, or the deity’s omnivision. None is securely decoded by an accompanying inscription. The Huarochirí Manuscript does not describe the statue in enough detail to validate one reading.

**[Materially attested]** The image was polychrome, including cinnabar red, yellow, and white. Descriptions of it as an unpainted brown “totem pole” reproduce its post-depositional condition, not its original appearance.

### Architecture as iconography of power

The sacred complex itself communicated hierarchy:

- the Painted Temple and its restricted inner chamber;
- enclosing walls and sequential gates;
- the North–South Street;
- Ychsma pyramids with ramps;
- the Inca Temple of the Sun dominating the older sanctuary;
- the Pilgrims’ Plaza;
- an *ushnu* platform controlling ceremonial entry;
- the *Acllawasi* or house of chosen women;
- storehouses and the residence associated with Taurichumpi.

Eeckhout calculates that the Inca Pilgrims’ Plaza, about five hectares, was approximately twenty times the area of the likely earlier gathering hall. This supports a major imperial enlargement of pilgrimage infrastructure, although the chroniclers’ claim that pilgrims waited a full year may be schematic. [Architectural analysis](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-73562004000200019&script=sci_arttext)

### Associated materials

- **Cinnabar/ichma:** elite pigment, funerary coloring, and image paint.
- **Spondylus shell:** high-value ritual offering brought through long-distance exchange.
- **Gold and silver:** tribute and offerings documented by Spaniards.
- **Camelids:** sacrificed and deposited.
- **Maize and chicha:** food and libation.
- **Textiles:** offerings, funerary wrapping, and elite display.
- **Fish and seabirds:** prominent in coastal subsistence and myth.
- **Human beings:** sacrificed in at least some Inca-period rituals, discussed below.

No securely documented canonical color, weekday, planet, weapon, numerical symbol, or zodiacal correspondence belongs to Pachacamac. Modern lists assigning such correspondences are inventions unless a specific historical source is supplied.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

There is no single Pachacamac genealogy.

- **Huarochirí regional tradition:** Pachacamac is husband of **Urpayhuachac** and father of at least two daughters. The manuscript does not center him as universal creator.
- **Calancha’s coastal tradition:** Pachacamac is a creator and opponent or brother of **Vichama**, with the **Sun** functioning as generative rival and higher paternal power.
- **Inca theological synthesis:** Pachacamac may be made a brother, coastal counterpart, subordinate manifestation, or alternative name of **the Sun** or **Viracocha**, depending on the chronicler.
- **Modern reconstruction:** A celestial “Pachacamac above” and chthonic “Pachacamac below” has been proposed to explain contradictory attributes. It is analytically suggestive but not an attested divine pair bearing those formal names.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Pachacamac, Cuniraya, Cahuillaca, and the origin of fishes

**Source:** *Huarochirí Manuscript*, principally chapter 2, Quechua, ca. 1598–1608.

Cuniraya Huiracocha, appearing as a poor man despite his divine power, impregnates the beautiful Cahuillaca by placing his semen in a fruit she eats. At an assembly of *wak’as*, her child recognizes Cuniraya as father. Ashamed that the apparently wretched figure fathered her child, Cahuillaca flees toward the coast. Cuniraya follows, questioning animals along the road and rewarding or cursing them according to their answers. Cahuillaca and her child reach the sea and become stone at the offshore islands.

Cuniraya reaches the vicinity of Pachacamac. Pachacamac himself is not the protagonist: his wife Urpayhuachac is away visiting Cahuillaca in the sea, leaving their daughters guarded. Cuniraya rapes the elder daughter and pursues the younger, who escapes as a dove. In retaliation against the absent Urpayhuachac, he throws her fish into the sea. They multiply and become the fishes of the ocean.

This story makes Pachacamac the head of a divine coastal household rather than the acting creator. Modern summaries that say “Pachacamac created all fish” collapse Urpayhuachac’s ownership and Cuniraya’s destructive-generative act into a single male creator.

### B. Annual *qhapaq hucha* to Pachacamac

**Source:** *Huarochirí Manuscript*, chapter 22.

The manuscript states that people from throughout Tawantinsuyu were offered annually in a *qhapaq hucha*. On reaching Pachacamac, victims were buried alive and offered as “father.” Gold, silver, llamas, food, and drink were also presented at the full moon.

**Evidentiary caution:** The statement was written during an extirpation campaign and reflects late-sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century testimony about Inca-period practice. Archaeology independently supports human sacrifice at the site, but it cannot verify every detail or the claimed annual frequency.

### C. The Con–Pachacamac succession

**Source:** Antonio de la Calancha, 1638.

Con, a primordial coastal creator associated with an earlier age, makes the first landscape and people. Human disobedience or failure leads to punishment and the withdrawal of water or fertility. Pachacamac then overcomes, displaces, or succeeds Con and creates a new humanity.

This is a **late colonial coastal cosmogony**, not securely attested in pre-Inca material. Rostworowski proposed that it preserves a historical memory of successive cult expansions—Con earlier, Pachacamac later—but the chronological mapping is a modern reconstruction based partly on names, geography, and iconography. Archaeology cannot identify a “Con horizon” followed neatly by a “Pachacamac horizon.”

### D. Pachacamac, the starving woman, and the food plants

**Source:** Calancha, 1638, from a north-central coastal tradition associated with Végueta.

Pachacamac creates a man and woman but fails to provide food. The man dies. The woman appeals not to Pachacamac but to the Sun, lamenting hunger and abandonment. The Sun impregnates her with a ray. Pachacamac, angered by the appeal to a rival, kills and dismembers the newborn.

He then produces food from the body:

- maize from the teeth;
- tubers and fruits from bones, flesh, or other parts;
- cultivated abundance from sacrificial dismemberment.

The story simultaneously depicts Pachacamac as failed provider, jealous killer, and giver of food. Attempts to sanitize him into a uniformly benevolent creator erase the narrative’s central contradiction.

### E. Vichama’s revenge and the repopulation of the world

The Sun creates or restores **Vichama** from the slain child’s remains, commonly the navel. While Vichama travels, Pachacamac kills his mother and gives portions of her body to birds or condors. Vichama returns, reassembles or revives her bones, and seeks revenge. Pachacamac escapes into the sea rather than kill another brother.

Vichama then punishes or transforms the existing inhabitants, turning them to stone. Humanity is renewed from eggs—gold, silver, and copper—from which social ranks emerge in Calancha’s hierarchical version. Elsewhere Calancha reports a different creation involving pairs of stars sent by Pachacamac.

The variants cannot be combined without distortion. In one, Pachacamac is an enemy displaced to the sea; in another, he sends celestial progenitors. Calancha’s Christian moral framework, Indigenous informants, and seventeenth-century coastal politics all shaped the surviving presentation.

### F. The Inca’s forty-day encounter with Pachacamac

In later colonial accounts, **Topa Inca Yupanqui** travels to the valley of Irma, fasts and prays for an extended period—often forty days—and receives an answer from the *wak’a*. Pachacamac identifies himself as the giver of life below, while the Sun gives life above or is his brother. The Inca builds or sponsors a major temple and the valley receives the name Pachacamac.

The forty-day fast may carry Christian narrative coloring. The tradition nevertheless encodes a credible historical process: the Incas did not abolish the old oracle but monumentalized, taxed, supervised, and subordinated its landscape to imperial solar institutions.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Sanctuary and cult center

**[Materially attested]** The sanctuary developed through multiple cultural phases:

1. early occupation and cemetery;
2. Lima-culture monumental construction;
3. Middle Horizon Wari-affiliated transformation;
4. Ychsma political and ritual florescence, ca. 1000–1470;
5. Inca imperial restructuring, ca. 1470–1533;
6. violent colonial abandonment, looting, reuse, excavation, and conservation.

The principal divine image stood in the Painted Temple according to the conjunction of conquest descriptions and archaeological context. The Temple of the Sun, *Acllawasi*, Pilgrims’ Plaza, *ushnu*, storehouses, roads, and Taurichumpi complex belong mainly to the Inca reorganization. [Peruvian management plan](https://pachacamac.cultura.pe/sites/default/files/item4/archivos/Plan%20de%20manejo%20del%20santuario%20arqueologico%20de%20Pachacamac%202012.pdf)

### Ychsma lords and priests

Pachacamac was the ideological center of the Ychsma polity occupying the lower Lurín and Rímac valleys. “Ychsma state,” “lordship,” and “loose federation” represent different archaeological models; the evidence does not disclose a single uncontested constitution.

The 1533 texts distinguish:

- a senior priest or oracle-chief;
- attendants with access to the inner chamber;
- messengers from regional lords;
- guarded women’s institutions;
- storehouse administrators;
- local and imperial authorities.

Hernando Pizarro’s “bishop” should not be converted into a precise Indigenous office title. It establishes a perceived hierarchical priesthood but not its native nomenclature.

### Oracle procedure

Petitioners apparently passed through graded zones of restriction. Ordinary pilgrims probably did not see the image directly. Priestly intermediaries entered the chamber and communicated answers. Later accounts say worshippers approached backward or avoided the deity’s gaze. The elaborate access system is consistent with the architecture, though individual ritual details are colonial reports rather than excavated facts.

The oracle’s authority was political. It received tribute, influenced local lords, and was reportedly consulted about Inca succession, military affairs, illness, and the invading Spaniards. An alleged prophecy that the strangers posed no danger may reflect post-defeat blame placed on oracle or priests.

### Pilgrimage

Pachacamac was a regional pilgrimage center before the Incas and an imperial one afterward. Roads connected it with highland Huarochirí and the Inca road network. Pilgrims brought valuables, food, animals, textiles, shell, and possibly human offerings.

Assertions that pilgrims always fasted for a year before admission come from colonial tradition and should not be treated as a universally observed regulation. Architecture does support prolonged, managed congregation and increasingly controlled approach under Inca rule.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Documented or archaeologically recovered offerings include:

- maize, chicha, and other food;
- camelids;
- guinea pigs and marine products;
- textiles;
- ceramics;
- *Spondylus*;
- gold and silver;
- pigments;
- miniature objects;
- human beings.

#### Human sacrifice

**[Textually attested]** Cobo says children were buried alive at Pachacamac; Santillán mentions girls; Guaman Poma places sacrifices in the Inca ritual calendar; Huarochirí chapter 22 describes male and female *qhapaq hucha* victims buried alive.

**[Materially attested, with limits]** Max Uhle excavated a cemetery of women, some apparently strangled with cotton ligatures. Later osteological work identified unusual burials, perimortem blunt-force trauma in adults, and contexts compatible with sacrifice. Many remains came from looted or mixed deposits, and numerous subadults lack skeletal evidence of cause of death. Eeckhout and Owens therefore argue cumulatively from trauma, ligatures, placement, demography, and colonial testimony rather than claiming that every atypical burial was sacrificial. [Full study](https://www.latinamericanstudies.org/moche/pachacamac.pdf)

The evidence supports human sacrifice at Pachacamac, particularly under the Inca. It does not support sensational claims of indiscriminate mass killing, nor does it permit every woman or child buried at the sanctuary to be labeled a victim.

### “Temple prostitution”

No reliable evidence establishes temple prostitution at Pachacamac. Pizarro describes secluded “women of the devil,” using hostile European language for women in a religious compound. The Inca *Acllawasi* housed selected women who produced textiles and chicha, performed ritual service, and could be assigned in marriage or sacrifice. Converting this institution into a brothel is a modern sensationalization unsupported by the cited evidence.

### Cult economy and politics

The oracle accumulated goods from a broad tributary and pilgrimage network. Pizarro observed that much of the coast rendered tribute to the sanctuary. The Inca enlarged the complex and installed imperial architecture, officials, solar worship, and an *Acllawasi* while leaving the local oracle operative. This was coexistence under domination: accommodation, appropriation, and surveillance at once.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before ca. 500 CE: sacred place without a securely named Pachacamac

Occupation and ritual construction are early, but the divine name is unattested. Later authors often project Pachacamac backward across the entire sequence. That is possible but not demonstrable.

### Ca. 600–900: Middle Horizon transformation

Wari-associated architecture, offerings, iconography, and the wooden bifront image mark a major transformation. The dated image shows that a powerful embodied cult existed by the eighth or ninth century. Whether Wari agents introduced the image, local people adopted Wari imagery, or the object was imported remains unresolved.

### Ca. 1000–1470: Ychsma florescence

The sanctuary became the center of Ychsma political and ritual life. Pyramids with ramps, elite residences, cemeteries, workshops, and managed routes proliferated. Scholars dispute whether ramp pyramids were temples, palaces, chiefly residences, dynastic monuments, or multipurpose compounds. The cult’s pre-Inca geographic reach was significant but should not automatically be called “pan-Andean.”

### Ca. 1470–1533: Inca incorporation

Tradition attributes conquest to Topa Inca Yupanqui. The Incas:

- preserved the oracle;
- constructed the dominant Temple of the Sun;
- expanded pilgrimage facilities;
- installed an *ushnu*, *Acllawasi*, roads, and administrative buildings;
- collected tribute and integrated the sanctuary into imperial state ritual.

Pachacamac was thus neither destroyed nor admitted as an equal partner free of imperial control. The Inca transformation made him more widely known while visually subordinating the older precinct to solar and royal power.

### 1533: looting and desacralizing performance

Hernando Pizarro left Cajamarca seeking treasure for Atahualpa’s ransom and arrived at Pachacamac around 30 January 1533. He found less gold than expected, forced access to the oracle, exposed the image, preached against it, compelled local lords to enter the chamber, and claimed to have broken or cast down the wooden idol.

The episode was economic looting, religious suppression, and political theatre. Making the deity fail publicly to punish the Spaniards attacked the oracle’s authority. Pizarro’s own account records torture and the transfer of fear: local people allegedly said they no longer feared the sanctuary but feared the Spaniards.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: suppression and textual preservation

The sanctuary ceased operating as its former institution. Christian clergy and colonial officials destroyed images, prosecuted ritual specialists, confiscated sacred objects, and relocated populations. Yet the campaigns also generated records: the Huarochirí Manuscript, Ávila materials, Arriaga’s manual, and trial dossiers preserve Indigenous statements precisely because authorities sought to eradicate the practices.

The surviving narratives are consequently double documents—evidence for Andean tradition and for its coercive colonial collection.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: antiquarian afterlife

The deity survived principally in chronicles, local geography, ruins, oral traditions, and antiquarian writing. There is no documented institutional continuity of the old Pachacamac priesthood or oracle.

### 1896–1903: scientific excavation

Max Uhle’s work produced the first major stratigraphic and archaeological treatment of the site, published as *Pachacamac* in 1903. Uhle documented cemeteries, architecture, pottery sequences, textiles, and the women’s burials, establishing the site as central to Andean archaeological chronology.

### 1938 onward: recovery of the image

Giesecke’s discovery transformed the material study of the god. Julio C. Tello and later Peruvian projects excavated, restored, and interpreted the sanctuary. Some early restoration—especially of the *Acllawasi*—was extensive enough to complicate reconstruction of original form.

### Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Rostworowski re-centered coastal political history, Ychsma identity, myth, and the possible Señor de los Milagros connection. Eeckhout’s Ychsma Project, Peruvian Ministry of Culture programs, and interdisciplinary analyses have refined chronology, function, sacrifice, pigment, and pilgrimage. The modern museum displays the wooden image and interprets the site as a long-duration sanctuary rather than a single-period “Inca temple.” [Museum overview](https://pachacamac.cultura.pe/museo-pachacamac)

### Living practice

Andean offerings to earth, mountains, ancestors, saints, and local sacred places remain active in Peru, Bolivia, northern Chile, and northwestern Argentina. Pachamama devotion, *apus*, Catholic-Andean pilgrimage, and household offerings are not evidence of a continuous trans-Andean Pachacamac cult.

At the Pachacamac site, heritage ceremonies, Indigenous cultural reclamation, occasional earth offerings, tourism, and educational performances occur. No well-documented priesthood continuously transmitting the pre-1533 Pachacamac oracle has been established. Claims of an unbroken modern cult in Cusco, the altiplano, or Argentina generally conflate Pachacamac with Pachamama or broad “Inca spirituality.”

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Señor de los Milagros

Rostworowski’s *Pachacamac y el Señor de los Milagros* (1992) argues for a trajectory between the old earthquake deity and Lima’s colonial Black Christ of Pachacamilla. Supporting parallels include:

- the names Pachacamac/Pachacamilla;
- earthquake protection;
- coastal Lima geography;
- color symbolism;
- African, Indigenous, and Catholic populations interacting in colonial Lima.

The Christian image, however, has its own documented seventeenth-century history among an Angolan confraternity. Similarity of earthquake function does not demonstrate that worshippers secretly preserved a single unchanged god. The safest formulation is **possible structural and local religious translation**, not simple survival. [Rostworowski publication summary](https://www.casadelaliteratura.gob.pe/publicacion-la-semana-pachacamac-senor-los-milagros-maria-rostworowski/)

### Literature, museums, and national identity

Pachacamac appears in Peruvian schoolbooks, poetry, historical fiction, archaeological exhibitions, tourism campaigns, public art, and narratives of pre-Hispanic national greatness. Modern retellings commonly merge:

- the Huarochirí household narrative;
- Calancha’s Con–Vichama cycle;
- Inca creator theology;
- the archaeological idol.

That composite is useful for storytelling but did not exist as a single surviving ancient canon.

### Neopagan and New Age material

Pachacamac appears sporadically in modern “Inca pantheon” lists, online rituals, role-playing systems, deity encyclopedias, and eclectic pagan practice. Common unsupported additions include:

- fixed elemental correspondences;
- crystals, chakras, tarot cards, zodiac signs, or weekdays;
- a standardized prayer or sigil;
- characterization as Pachamama’s husband;
- treatment as a universal “Inca earth god” worshipped identically from Ecuador to Argentina;
- a two-faced statue with an established Janus-like doctrine.

These are **modern inventions or private religious interpretations** unless individually sourced. The research pass found no evidence that Pachacamac became a significant deity in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Theosophical Society, the Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoire traditions.

### Games and popular mythology

Games and online pantheon-building frequently portray Pachacamac as a combatant controlling earthquakes, summoning fish, or battling Vichama and Con. Those powers selectively adapt colonial narratives. They should be described as modern creative works, not repositories of lost ancient doctrine.

### Political appropriation

Pachacamac is primarily used as a symbol of Peruvian antiquity, coastal Indigenous heritage, and heritage tourism. Unlike some European or Near Eastern gods, he has no well-documented major modern extremist cult. Broad nationalist presentations can nevertheless relabel the local Ychsma oracle simply “Inca,” minimizing the sanctuary’s pre-Inca coastal history.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Pachacamac originally Ychsma?

- **Rostworowski and followers:** Ychsma was probably the older deity and polity name; the Incas imposed or promoted the Quechua title Pachacamac.
- **Cautionary position:** Ychsma is securely a regional/place/polity term and may relate to cinnabar, but direct proof that it was the idol’s personal name is absent.
- **Linguistic issue:** Ychsma/Irma has been given Quechua and Aymaran explanations. Colonial spelling obscures local phonology.

### Wari introduction or local continuity?

The idol’s date and Wari-style imagery support a Middle Horizon cult strongly connected with Wari visual culture. Yet architectural continuity and local agency make “Wari imported Pachacamac” too simple. The image may reflect negotiated adoption rather than imperial religious replacement.

### Was Pachacamac identical with Viracocha?

- **Colonial theological synthesis:** yes, both are names for the supreme maker.
- **Local and archaeological evidence:** they remained distinguishable; Pachacamac retained a specific coastal sanctuary, image, family, and oracle.
- **Historical interpretation:** the equation was probably an Inca or colonial act of translation and systematization.

### Benevolent creator or dangerous chthonic god?

Both are attested. Calancha presents him as creator, jealous killer, food-giver, and earthquake power. Huarochirí presents a largely absent household patriarch. Conquest accounts foreground fear and prophecy. The contradiction is historical evidence, not a problem to solve by choosing one “true” personality.

### Did pilgrims come from the entire Andes before the Inca?

The pre-Inca sanctuary had interregional links, but the most expansive pilgrimage infrastructure is Inca. “Pan-Andean for 1,500 years” is a heritage shorthand, not a securely demonstrated constant. [Change-and-continuity study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00438243.2012.759516)

### Was the 1938 object Pizarro’s idol?

Its age, size, sacred findspot, and long use make the identification plausible. Pizarro and Estete, however, claimed the idol was destroyed. Possible explanations are incomplete destruction, concealment, misidentification by the Spaniards, replacement, or confusion among several images. The object should be called the “so-called Pachacamac Idol,” with the uncertainty retained.

### Human sacrifice

The old debate opposed literal acceptance of hostile chronicles to wholesale dismissal as Spanish propaganda. Current work uses converging evidence: ligatures, trauma, nonnormative placement, demographic selection, colonial descriptions, and Inca comparison. Sacrifice is supported, but individual diagnoses and victim counts remain contested.

### The ramp pyramids

Earlier scholarship often called them temples for successive divine cults. Eeckhout and others have argued for elite palaces or dynastic compounds used by Ychsma lords. Functions may have changed, and “palace” versus “temple” may impose a separation less meaningful in Andean political religion.

### Archaeological and archival losses

The sanctuary suffered:

- Spanish seizure and deliberate destruction beginning in 1533;
- centuries of grave robbing;
- removal of artifacts without modern provenance;
- erosion and urban pressure;
- early reconstructions that altered architecture.

Primary manuscripts and editions are dispersed among European and American libraries. The original Huarochirí Quechua manuscript is preserved in Madrid’s Biblioteca Nacional de España; Guaman Poma’s autograph manuscript is held by the Royal Danish Library. Excavated objects are distributed among Peru’s Pachacamac museum, the Museo Nacional de Arqueología, Antropología e Historia del Perú, the Ethnologisches Museum in Berlin, the Penn Museum, and other collections.

### Scholarship that changed the field

- **Max Uhle, 1903:** archaeological sequence and cemeteries.
- **Julio C. Tello:** Peruvian-led excavation, conservation, and national interpretation.
- **María Rostworowski:** coastal lordships, Ychsma, myths, political economy, and syncretism.
- **Pierre Duviols:** extirpation archives and historical critique of Andean religion.
- **Frank Salomon and George Urioste; Gerald Taylor:** critical work on the Huarochirí Manuscript.
- **Sabine MacCormack:** colonial intellectual transformation of Andean religion.
- **Irene Silverblatt:** gender, state, and colonial religious power.
- **Tom Zuidema:** Inca cosmology, ritual organization, and calendrics.
- **Brian Bauer:** Inca sacred landscapes and imperial pilgrimage.
- **Gary Urton:** Andean cosmology, khipu, and epistemology.
- **Peter Eeckhout:** Ychsma archaeology, architecture, sacrifice, and Inca transformation.
- **Marcela Sepúlveda and collaborators:** direct dating and pigment analysis of the idol.
