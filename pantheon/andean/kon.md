# Kon: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A male creator and landscape-transforming god (*huaca* in broader Andean terminology), remembered in colonial sources as an ancient deity of Peru’s Pacific coast. “Wind god” and “rain god” are modern functional classifications, not titles preserved in the earliest text.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Francisco López de Gómara’s *Historia general de las Indias*, first printed at Zaragoza in **1552**, chapter 22 of the Peruvian section. Gómara calls him **Con**, says that he came from the north at the beginning of the world, lacked bones or joints, created the first coastal people, and later withdrew their rain. Agustín de Zárate’s *Historia del descubrimiento y conquista del Perú* (**Antwerp, 1555**) independently prints a closely related account, but the relationship between the two chronicles’ information is uncertain.
>
> **Critical distinction:** The equation **Con/Kon = Cuniraya Viracocha** is not stated by either Gómara or the early-seventeenth-century *Huarochirí Manuscript*. It is a modern comparative reconstruction, especially associated with María Rostworowski and writers following her. The dossier therefore treats (1) the securely attested coastal **Con** and (2) Huarochirí’s **Cuniraya Viracocha** separately before assessing their possible relationship.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Con / Kon

**[Securely attested early material]** The first printed form is Spanish **Con**. Modern Peruvian writing usually substitutes **Kon** or **Kón**, partly to prevent the name from being mistaken for the Spanish preposition *con*. No indigenous manuscript spelling, pre-Hispanic inscription, or securely identified image supplies an original orthography.

Gómara’s being is:

- primordial, present near the beginning of the world;
- anthropomorphic enough to travel and act, but without bones or joints;
- exceptionally swift;
- capable of shortening roads, lowering mountains, and opening valleys by speech;
- creator of an initial human population;
- initially a giver of abundance;
- subsequently the cause of the rainless coastal desert;
- displaced by Pachacamac, another powerful creator.

The frequently used modern designation **“god of wind and rain”** is an inference from his lightness, rapid motion, rain-withholding power, and relation to coastal rivers. Neither Gómara nor Zárate preserves a formal title equivalent to “Wind God.” Likewise, **“flying god,” “winged god,” “feline flying god,” “Oculate Being,”** and **“Masked Being”** are modern iconographic identifications, not colonial epithets.

### Proposed etymologies

No etymology commands consensus.

- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An early proposal connected *Con/Kon* with the Quechua root **kam-**, “to order, create, animate,” treating *Kon* as a phonetic or dialectal relative of the element in *Pachacamac/Pachakamaq*. José Sebastián Barranca developed a version of this argument in 1905, also associating *kam/kon* with animation, force, noise, or roaring. This is historically important but linguistically insecure because the required sound correspondences and original language of the divine name are not established.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Some Peruvian writers connect the name with Quechua **q’uñi/quñi**, “warm,” making Cuniraya or Kon a warm, solar, or coastal power. This remains conjectural.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Rostworowski and followers compare **Con**, **Cuniraya** and sometimes **Wakon/Huacón**, regarding them as transformations of an older coastal name or religious complex.
- **[Unestablished]** Popular claims that *Kon* simply means “wind,” “rain,” “fire,” “water,” or “flying one” in an ancient coastal language are not supported by a surviving lexicon.
- **[Modern invention]** **Apu Kon** is a respectful modern expansion using Quechua *apu*, “lord” or powerful mountain being. It is not found as Con’s title in Gómara or Zárate.
- **[Modern conflation]** **Kon-Tiki**, **Con-Tici**, or **Kon-Tiki Viracocha** belongs primarily to colonial names and epithets of Viracocha and was popularized internationally by Thor Heyerdahl’s 1947 raft expedition. It cannot be used as independent evidence that coastal Con was anciently called Kon-Tiki.

### Cuniraya Viracocha

The *Huarochirí Manuscript* writes forms conventionally rendered **Cuniraya Viracocha**, **Coniraya Viracocha**, or in modern Quechua orthography **Kuniraya Wiraqucha**. The first element’s derivation is uncertain. *Viracocha* was already a divine name and honorific with a complicated Inca and colonial history.

**[Secure colonial indigenous-language attestation]** The manuscript classifies Cuniraya as a **huaca**: an animate, powerful nonhuman person associated with places, material manifestations, creative action, and worship. It calls him *runa camaq, pacha camaq*—approximately “animator/maker of people, animator/maker of the world”—in a prayer formula. Translating *kamaq* merely as “creator” loses its sense of imparting order or vital efficacy.

**[Colonial learned or evangelizing systematization]** Chapter 15 says that before Cuniraya nothing existed and that he made mountains, trees, rivers, animals, and fields. César Itier and other scholars warn that this chapter employs creation language unusually close to colonial Christian catechesis. It may preserve Andean ideas, Christian-Andean reformulation, or both. The manuscript itself contains uncertainty about whether Cuniraya preceded, followed, or coexisted with Pariacaca and Viracocha.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The earliest Con texts

#### Francisco López de Gómara, 1552

**Language/support:** Castilian Spanish, printed book.  
**Authorial position:** Gómara never visited Peru. He compiled oral and written reports in Spain. His immediate informant for Con is unknown.

**[Securely attested early material]** Gómara says that at the beginning of the world Con came from the north. He had no bones or joints, traveled extraordinarily fast, reduced distances, lowered mountains, and cut valleys by his word. He created the people then living and supplied abundant fruit. When the coastal inhabitants angered him, he deprived them of rain and turned their country into deserts, while leaving rivers so that they could survive through irrigation and labor. Pachacamac then came “from the south,” expelled Con, transformed Con’s people, and made another humanity.

Gómara is the **earliest surviving datable witness to the name and complete myth**. His work proves that this story circulated by the early colonial period; it does not by itself date the story to Paracas, Nasca, Chavín, or any specific archaeological culture. A searchable modern edition of Gómara is preserved by the [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/historia-general-de-las-indias--0/html/fef81d62-82b1-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_2.html).

#### Agustín de Zárate, 1555

**Language/support:** Castilian Spanish, printed at Antwerp.

**[Securely attested early material]** Zárate provides the same basic configuration: a primeval creator without bones or joints, capable of manipulating terrain and distances; punishment of the coast by eliminating rain; and replacement by Pachacamac. Zárate had been in Peru, but the extent to which his religious chapters depend on earlier accounts or editorial borrowing remains debated. The first-edition textual history is unusually important because the religious chapters were modified in the 1577 edition. A modern repository record and edition are available from the [Pontifical Catholic University of Peru](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/73b63aba-f281-4833-99d8-b6c12ce69289).

The agreement of Gómara and Zárate strengthens the conclusion that a coastal Con narrative existed in the 1550s. It does not necessarily constitute two wholly independent indigenous testimonies.

### Antonio de la Calancha, 1638

**[Correction to a frequent conflation]** Calancha’s *Corónica moralizada del Orden de San Agustín en el Perú* preserves the important **Pachacamac–Vichama** creation cycle, derived in part from an account by the Augustinian Luis de Teruel. In that cycle Pachacamac creates a human pair without food; the man dies, the woman appeals to the Sun, and Pachacamac kills and dismembers her solar child, whose body becomes food plants. Vichama is then born, contests Pachacamac’s actions, and participates in another repopulation.

Calancha is therefore essential comparative evidence for coastal creator succession, subsistence, death, and repopulation, but **he does not securely provide the fullest version of Gómara’s boneless Con story**. Modern retellings frequently splice Gómara’s Con cycle and Calancha’s Pachacamac–Vichama cycle into one continuous “Kon–Pachacamac–Vichama epic.” That composite is **[modern systematization]**. Calancha’s original book is digitized by [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?id=HJwzAQAAMAAJ); a modern archaeological discussion emphasizes that the chronicler’s sources are not always recoverable and cannot be treated as transparent pre-Hispanic transcripts ([Peruvian pre-Hispanic beliefs, vol. I](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/es-pe/insights/growth/documents/ey_dioses-creencias-peru-prehispanico-tomo-i.pdf)).

### The Huarochirí Manuscript, approximately 1598–1608

**Physical witness:** Biblioteca Nacional de España, Madrid, **MS 3169**.  
**Language:** colonial Quechua, with Spanish headings and annotations.  
**Context:** compiled by an indigenous writer or writers in the orbit of the priest Francisco de Ávila while Ávila investigated indigenous religion in Huarochirí.  
**Significance:** the only extended early colonial account of Andean religious traditions written principally in an Andean language.

The manuscript is not simply a stenographic record of untouched pre-Hispanic belief. It incorporates distinct community traditions, editorial ordering, colonial Quechua, Christian vocabulary, and information gathered in a coercive anti-idolatry setting. Its survival nevertheless makes it uniquely valuable. The manuscript and Gerald Taylor’s edition are catalogued by the [Biblioteca Nacional de España](https://datos.bne.es/resource/bimo0000058493); Frank Salomon and George Urioste’s edition and introduction are available through [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/730526).

Cuniraya appears in chapters 1, 2, 6, 14, 15, 16, and 31:

- **Chapter 1:** prayer to Cuniraya Viracocha as animator of humanity and the world; ritual invocation before difficult work, particularly intricate weaving.
- **Chapter 2:** Cuniraya’s disguise, impregnation of Cahuillaca, pursuit, blessings and curses of animals, encounter with the household of Urpayhuachac, release of fish, and attempted sexual assaults.
- **Chapter 6:** a place where Cuniraya was or became fixed in the landscape is mentioned.
- **Chapter 14:** Cuniraya is “very ancient,” meets the Inca Huayna Capac, directs a journey toward Titicaca, sends a mysterious container, and disappears from the narrative.
- **Chapter 15:** expanded creator language; the manuscript admits uncertainty about his date and says some regarded Pariacaca as his son.
- **Chapter 16:** the five Pariacaca brothers are said, with explicit qualification, to be sons of Cuniraya.
- **Chapter 31:** another abbreviated or retrospective reference; the surviving work appears textually unfinished.

The [Arguedas bilingual edition](https://www.letras.ufmg.br/padrao_cms/documentos/profs/romulo/ManuscritodeHuarochiriArguedas.pdf) and [Gerald Taylor’s critical Spanish edition](https://escriturasvirreinales.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/121103492-manuscrito-quechua-de-huarochiri.pdf) allow comparison of divergent translations.

### Chronicles that do not securely attest Con

Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Molina, Cobo, Garcilaso de la Vega, Guaman Poma, Cristóbal de Albornoz, and Pablo José de Arriaga are indispensable for Inca theology, Viracocha, Pachacamac, the *ceque* system, provincial huacas, ritual calendars, and extirpation. They do **not**, however, furnish an independent, unmistakable narrative of Gómara’s Con.

This absence matters. It argues against calling Kon straightforwardly a major “Inca god.” He is best described as a coastal creator known from a very narrow colonial record, subsequently inserted into generalized modern “Inca pantheons.”

The Huarochirí and Cajatambo trials document extensive clandestine worship of local huacas, ancestors and sacred objects, but a distinct cult of Con under that name has not been demonstrated in the published trial corpus. The Cajatambo documents are available in Pierre Duviols and collaborators’ [critical collection](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/1f4ac161-c30e-4416-be48-dd1bf26b5765).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Secure attributes of Con

**[Securely attested early material]**

- **Bodiless or boneless constitution:** “no bones or joints” does not necessarily mean incorporeal; it describes radical flexibility or a body unlike an ordinary human body.
- **Speed and altered distance:** roads contract before him.
- **Speech as productive action:** he lowers highlands and cuts valleys by command.
- **Anthropogenesis:** he makes an initial humanity.
- **Abundance and subsistence:** his first people receive plentiful produce.
- **Rain control:** he commands that rain cease.
- **River and irrigation order:** rivers remain as the coast’s means of survival.
- **Desert formation:** coastal aridity is explained as a divine punishment.
- **Limits:** Pachacamac can displace him; Con is not the final or universally supreme creator.

“Boneless wind” is an attractive physical interpretation: a flexible, swift, invisible power resembles wind or flowing water. But the earliest chroniclers do not explicitly say “Con is the wind.” The water interpretation emphasizes the rivers and irrigation; the solar/fire interpretation emphasizes heat and desertification. Both are **modern reconstructions**.

### Secure attributes of Cuniraya

**[Colonial indigenous-language attestation]**

- travels disguised as a ragged, louse-ridden poor man;
- can appear splendidly clothed in gold and illuminate the world;
- assumes bird form;
- makes terraces by speech;
- opens irrigation channels using a reed flower;
- animates or creates the world’s useful forms;
- outwits other huacas;
- controls the destinies and social valuations of animals through blessings and curses;
- provides fish to the sea;
- is invoked in difficult acts of design and skilled textile work;
- is sexually aggressive, deceitful, and repeatedly unsuccessful in obtaining Cahuillaca or Urpayhuachac’s daughters.

Calling Cuniraya only a “benevolent creator” suppresses much of chapter 2. The story explicitly represents conception without Cahuillaca’s knowledge and later attempted sexual violence against Urpayhuachac’s daughters.

### Iconography

**[Absence of secure evidence]** No pre-Hispanic image bears a written label naming Con. No excavated idol can presently be identified as Con through an associated inscription or unambiguous colonial description.

Rostworowski proposed that flying or horizontally extended beings in Paracas textiles and Nasca ceramics, and perhaps imagery associated with Nasca geoglyphs, represented Kon. She connected the figures’ apparent flight, trophy heads, masks, seasonal water, and south-coast setting with the chronicled god. This is **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**, not secure identification.

Later archaeology generally names such images descriptively—**Oculate Being**, **Masked Being**, or **Anthropomorphic Mythical Being**—precisely because their indigenous names are unknown. Donald Proulx, Helaine Silverman and others have challenged direct mappings between sixteenth-century names and images one or two millennia older. Lambers concludes that Rostworowski’s Nasca hypothesis is likely impossible to prove archaeologically and notes objections to the “flying” interpretation ([Lambers dissertation](https://www.archaeologie.uzh.ch/static/pdf/Lambers_Diss_2005.pdf)).

Thus the familiar modern picture of Kon as a winged, feline-masked man with huge eyes, a staff, agricultural produce, and trophy heads is a **modern composite** of:

1. Gómara’s boneless creator;
2. Paracas/Nasca iconographic categories;
3. generalized Andean staff-god imagery;
4. modern fantasy illustration.

It is not a securely attested ancient portrait.

No traditional color, sacred number, weekday, constellation, plant, sacrificial animal, weapon, or canonical emblem can be established for Con.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other divine beings

### Genealogical claims

- **Con as son of the Sun:** **[later or modern systematization]** Some summaries make Con a son of the Sun; the most cautious reading is that this is secondary and not necessary to Gómara’s core narrative.
- **Pachacamac as Con’s son:** **[variant/uncertain]** Modern retellings sometimes call Pachacamac Con’s son; others call both sons of the Sun. The early story’s essential relation is succession and displacement, not securely established biological paternity.
- **Con and the Moon:** **[modern invention or unsupported elaboration]** The widespread claim that Con is the child of Inti and Mama Quilla is not securely attested for the coastal Con.
- **Cuniraya as father of Pariacaca:** **[colonial regional variant]** Huarochirí chapters 14–16 report this with reservation: “they say” that Pariacaca and the five brothers were Cuniraya’s sons. The manuscript elsewhere makes Pariacaca emerge from five eggs and does not give a stable family tree.
- **Cahuillaca’s child:** **[colonial regional narrative]** Cuniraya fathers an unnamed child by Cahuillaca. Mother and child become stone/islands off Pachacamac.
- **Consort:** No securely attested spouse exists for either Con or Cuniraya. Cahuillaca rejects Cuniraya; describing her as his wife or consort falsifies the story.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The first creation and desertification

**[Securely attested early colonial account]**

At the beginning of the world, Con arrives from the north. Though lacking bones and joints, he moves rapidly over land and sea. His speech restructures geography: mountains diminish, routes shorten, and valleys open. He creates men and women and gives them abundant food.

The created people commit an offense which the source does not define. Con responds by stopping rain. Fertile country becomes desert. He nevertheless leaves rivers, so survival remains possible through irrigation, work, and social organization.

This is an extraordinarily close fit with coastal Peru’s ecology: an almost rainless desert broken by river valleys descending from the Andes. The story explains why water arrives laterally through rivers rather than vertically as rain, why settlement clusters in irrigable valleys, and why agriculture requires labor.

**What cannot be established:** the people’s offense; a named cult center; the ritual occasion on which the myth was recited; whether the story originated in northern, central, or southern coastal Peru; whether “came from the north” encodes migration, conquest, seasonal weather, or sacred geography.

### Pachacamac’s displacement of Con

Pachacamac arrives from the south and proves more powerful. He drives Con away and transforms Con’s people—reported in variants or translations as monkeys, black cats, birds, fish, or other animals—before creating another humanity.

The variable animal list should not be harmonized. The stable structure is:

1. first creator;
2. first humanity;
3. environmental punishment;
4. arrival of rival creator;
5. destruction or transformation of the former population;
6. new human order.

Rostworowski interpreted this as a possible memory of one religious cult replacing another. That is plausible as a structural reading but not a dated event. No archaeological stratum is labeled “cult of Con,” and no evidence proves a historical war between Con priests and Pachacamac priests.

### Cuniraya and Cahuillaca

**[Colonial regional tradition]**

Cuniraya travels as a contemptible-looking poor man while secretly possessing immense power. Cahuillaca, a beautiful virgin huaca desired by other huacas, rejects every suitor. Cuniraya changes into a bird, deposits semen in a lúcuma fruit, and drops it near her. She eats it and becomes pregnant without knowing the father.

When the child is about a year old, Cahuillaca assembles the huacas and asks each to acknowledge paternity. Cuniraya sits apart in rags. The child crawls past the splendid huacas and climbs joyfully onto Cuniraya. Horrified at having borne the child of one who appears destitute and diseased, Cahuillaca flees toward the sea. Cuniraya puts on gold garments, stands radiant, and calls after her, but she refuses to turn. She and the child enter the sea and become offshore rocks or islands.

The episode does not present informed consent. Cuniraya’s act is reproductive deception; later admiration for him as a lovable “trickster” should not conceal what the text says.

### Blessings and curses of the animals

While pursuing Cahuillaca, Cuniraya questions animals about how far ahead she is.

- The **condor** encourages him; Cuniraya grants it long life, carrion to eat, and ritual honor if killed.
- The **female skunk** discourages him; he condemns it to nocturnal wandering, foul smell, and human hatred.
- The **puma** encourages him; he grants it rights over llamas and ceremonial honor.
- The **fox** gives discouraging news; Cuniraya curses it to be despised and discarded when killed.
- The **falcon** encourages him; it receives honored prey and mourning or offerings.
- The **parrot** discourages him; it is condemned to noisy cries and human hostility.

This cycle explains animal behavior and human evaluations of species. Cuniraya’s spoken judgments permanently order ecological and ritual relationships. A recent linguistic analysis emphasizes the performative force of these utterances ([“La fuerza oral de Cuniraya”](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2413-26592025000100005&script=sci_arttext&tlng=es)).

### Cuniraya, Urpayhuachac and the origin of sea fish

Cuniraya reaches the region of Pachacamac after Cahuillaca has become stone offshore. Urpayhuachac, associated with Pachacamac and with fish or seabirds, is away visiting Cahuillaca. Cuniraya finds fish confined in her pond and casts them into the ocean; from these, the sea becomes populated.

He then attacks or attempts to possess Urpayhuachac’s daughters. One escapes as a dove. Urpayhuachac returns and tries to lure Cuniraya into a trap or precipice; he recognizes the danger, excuses himself, and escapes.

The story explains marine abundance and the name or nature of Urpayhuachac—commonly glossed “she who gives birth to doves.” It also presents an unresolved contest between two powerful huacas rather than a clear victory.

### Cuniraya, Huayna Capac, and the coming colonial rupture

In chapter 14 Cuniraya meets Huayna Capac as the “Viracochas”—Spaniards—are about to appear. He invites the Inca toward Titicaca and demands that ritual specialists and those claiming descent or inspiration from condor, falcon, and swallow accompany the enterprise. A small container is sent or entrusted with instructions that Huayna Capac himself must open it. Its opening reveals a richly adorned woman or dangerous manifestation; the party is struck by disorder, and Cuniraya disappears.

The passage is fragmentary and difficult. It places Cuniraya across primordial, Inca, and colonial time, which is why Salomon describes him as a being present in multiple eras rather than belonging neatly to one mythic age. It is not evidence that the historical Huayna Capac maintained a state cult of Kon.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### What is actually attested for Con

No securely identified temple, priesthood, festival, idol, sacrifice, oracle, pilgrimage route, or votive deposit can be assigned to Con by name.

The site popularly called **Huaca or Templo Con Con** in Carabayllo has sometimes been presented as Kon’s sanctuary. The name alone is inadequate proof: *Concon* may be a toponym, and published archaeological evidence does not establish an inscription, cult image, or colonial testimony linking the building to Gómara’s god. Claims that worshippers there continued “sacrifices and prayers to Kon” are **unverified local or modern interpretation**.

Likewise, Pachacamac’s vast sanctuary cannot be relabeled a temple of Con. Its documented archaeology, pilgrimage, oracle, and later Inca Temple of the Sun concern Pachacamac/Ychsma and Inca religious administration. The Inca construction is conventionally dated around 1470 ([Peruvian museum description](https://mnaahp.cultura.pe/santuario-arqueologico/descripcion-del-sitio/templo-del-sol)).

No evidence specifically connects Con with human sacrifice or “temple prostitution.” Human and animal offerings are documented in several ancient Peruvian religious settings, but attributing them to Kon without a named context would be false.

### What is attested for Cuniraya

The *Huarochirí Manuscript* gives several concrete ritual notices:

- people prayed, “Cuniraya Viracocha, animator of people, animator of the world”;
- before difficult tasks, worshippers cast coca on the ground and asked him to help them understand or accomplish the work;
- master weavers invoked him before complex textile design;
- his powers were localized in landscape places and petrified manifestations;
- knowledge about him continued among Huarochirí communities into the early colonial period.

The manuscript does not describe a freestanding Cuniraya temple, hereditary priesthood, annual festival, or human sacrifice. Its evidence is strongest for invocation, coca offering, occupational devotion, narrative memory, and sacred geography.

### Suppression

Huarochirí became a center of institutionalized **extirpation of idolatry** in the first decade of the seventeenth century. Ávila and indigenous Christian collaborators conducted investigations, interrogations, confiscations, and destruction of ritual objects. The manuscript was produced within that unequal environment—simultaneously a tool for suppression and a vehicle by which indigenous religious memory survived. The process is examined in [Herrera Villagra’s study](https://revistaschilenas.uchile.cl/handle/2250/79616).

It cannot be shown that a campaign singled out Con. Cuniraya’s traditions were caught within the broader suppression of Huarochirí huacas.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1552: unattested or archaeologically anonymous

Coastal Peru possessed complex religious systems for millennia, visible in temples, burials, textiles, ceramics, geoglyphs, offerings, and sacred landscapes. None of this material independently supplies the name Con.

Rostworowski provisionally assigned his cult to at least the Early Intermediate Period and possibly earlier, linking him to Paracas and Nasca. This remains a hypothesis projected backward from colonial narrative. It is methodologically improper to call Con “the chief Paracas god” as established fact.

### 1552–1555: first documentary horizon

Gómara and Zárate record a creator-displacement story. This is the only firm historical horizon for the boneless Con. The account already speaks of him as belonging to a past superseded by Pachacamac.

### Late sixteenth to early seventeenth century: Cuniraya tradition

The Huarochirí text records a living or recently living body of ritual speech and narrative concerning Cuniraya Viracocha. This is more locally detailed than the Con notices, but it uses a different name and mythic cycle. The compound with Viracocha may reflect Inca-era religious translation, colonial reformulation, or a longer regional synthesis.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: suppression and textual dormancy

Catholic evangelization, reducciones, destruction of huacas, and extirpation trials transformed Andean ritual life. Local sacred beings often survived through landscape practice, ancestor cult, saints, crosses, healing and agricultural rites. No clear documentary chain shows Con surviving under his own name.

Calancha’s Pachacamac–Vichama story kept a related coastal succession mythology in print. Con himself largely disappeared from the record.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries: rediscovery and reconstruction

The chronicles were republished; the Huarochirí manuscript was studied and translated; archaeologists excavated Pachacamac and south-coast cultures. Scholars attempted to unite chronicles, linguistic fragments, iconography, oral tradition, and archaeology.

José María Arguedas’s 1966 Spanish translation, Gerald Taylor’s 1987 philological edition, and Salomon and Urioste’s 1991 English edition transformed access to Cuniraya. Rostworowski’s essays gave Kon a much larger historical and iconographic profile by connecting him to Paracas, Nasca, water, flight, and Pachacamac.

### Present

Andean ritual traditions remain vibrant in Cusco, the altiplano, Huarochirí, Bolivia, and northwestern Argentina, particularly devotion to mountains, Pachamama, ancestors, saints, crosses, agricultural cycles, coca offerings, and reciprocal feeding of powerful places. This does **not** establish continuous contemporary worship of Kon.

No reliable demographic or ethnographic evidence identifies a substantial continuous Kon cult. Modern invocations appear primarily in educational folklore, heritage performance, tourism, internet neopaganism, individual reconstruction, illustration, and pan-Andean spiritual synthesis. These are genuine modern religious or cultural phenomena, but continuity from a named pre-Hispanic Kon priesthood cannot be demonstrated.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and children’s literature

Rostworowski retold the story in **Kon, el dios volador y el pequeño Naycashca** (1995). Such retellings made Kon accessible as a flying creator and ecological deity. They are modern literary adaptations, not additional ancient sources.

Peruvian educational material now presents a concise sequence: Kon creates the first people, punishes them by drought, and is displaced by Pachacamac. A Ministry of Culture educational resource explicitly derives this from Rostworowski ([Mitos de origen de la costa central](https://centroderecursos.cultura.pe/sites/default/files/rb/pdf/Lecciones-audiovisuales-para-ciudadanos-y-ciudadanas-interculturales.pdf)).

### Visual reinvention

Modern images commonly give Kon:

- wings or a hovering pose;
- a bird or feline mask;
- huge “oculate” eyes;
- staffs;
- trophy heads;
- crops or flowing water;
- Paracas textile colors.

This iconography is **modern archaeological synthesis**. It may be evocative, but none of its individual objects is securely labeled “Kon.”

### Kon-Tiki

Thor Heyerdahl named the 1947 raft **Kon-Tiki** after a name he associated with Viracocha. The expedition gave “Kon-Tiki” global recognition. Its diffusion encouraged the conflation of Con, Viracocha, Tiki, a white culture hero, and trans-Pacific migration theories. Those identifications are not supported by the Con passages and should be separated from the coastal myth.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search for Kon in Renaissance occultism, ceremonial magic, Theosophy, Golden Dawn materials, modern grimoires, chaos magic, and established neopagan organizations produces no documented historical Kon current comparable to modern cults of Egyptian, Greek, or Norse gods.

Contemporary web pages sometimes supply prayers, correspondences, sigils, elemental attributions, astral functions, or a spouse and family for Kon. Unless explicitly identified as modern devotional creation, these are **modern inventions**. No ancient source supplies a canonical sigil, planetary correspondence, initiation rite, sacred day, or magical seal.

### Political and commercial use

Kon sometimes functions as an emblem of pre-Hispanic Peruvian identity, environmental mastery, irrigation, desert ecology, or Indigenous resilience. This is cultural reception rather than continuous cult. No substantial modern extremist appropriation was found. Claims linking Kon to ancient aliens, nonhuman Paracas skulls, or a lost transoceanic master race are pseudohistorical modern inventions unsupported by archaeology or genetics.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Con the same as Cuniraya?

**Arguments for:**

- partial phonetic resemblance, *Con–Cuni-*;
- both are ancient mobile creators;
- both restructure terrain and irrigation;
- both are associated with the central coast and Pachacamac;
- both can be deceptive or socially disruptive;
- both occupy a religious stratum remembered as ancient.

**Arguments against:**

- the sources never equate them;
- *Cuniraya* is not demonstrably derived from *Con*;
- Con’s defining bonelessness, northern arrival, rain withdrawal, and defeat by Pachacamac are absent from Cuniraya’s story;
- Cuniraya’s Cahuillaca, animal judgments, textile invocation, fish-release story, and relation to Pariacaca are absent from Con’s;
- Cuniraya is paired with the name Viracocha in a specific Huarochirí religious and colonial context;
- similarities between creator-transformers may reflect widespread Andean narrative structures rather than identity.

**Assessment:** **Con = Cuniraya is possible but unproved.** “Coniraya” should not be segmented into “Con + Iraya” without linguistic demonstration.

### Was Kon a Paracas or Nasca god?

Rostworowski argued yes, interpreting winged or flying south-coast figures and Nasca’s water-oriented sacred landscape through the Con myth.

Objections are substantial:

- Paracas and Nasca left no readable texts naming their gods;
- the iconography predates Gómara by roughly one to two millennia;
- “flying,” “masked,” “oculate,” and “trophy-head-bearing” figures may represent different beings, ritual specialists, ancestors, or transformation states;
- the Masked Being has an archaeological development that can be studied without assigning a colonial name;
- no falsifiable bridge connects a particular artifact to Con.

The responsible label is **modern scholarly hypothesis**, not identification.

### Was Kon wind, water, rain, fire, or sun?

- **Wind:** bonelessness and rapid flight-like travel.
- **Water/rain:** power to remove rain; preservation of rivers; irrigation and fertility.
- **Solar/fire:** heat and desiccation; possible “warm” etymology; opposition to a flood-bringing rival in later reconstructions.
- **Landscape creator:** the least speculative classification because terrain transformation is explicit.

These need not have been mutually exclusive in Andean thought, but the evidence does not authorize a single definitive elemental classification.

### Christian influence on Cuniraya

Chapter 15’s absolute creation language closely resembles missionary descriptions of the Christian Creator. Yet chapters 1–2 contain deeply Andean features: huaca assemblies, coca offerings, irrigation, textile work, animal destinies, landscape petrification, and reciprocal relations.

The text should therefore be understood as colonial Andean religious discourse, neither a pure pre-Conquest transcript nor a mere Christian fabrication. A detailed discussion of Cuniraya as trickster and colonial creator is available in [“El trickster en el Manuscrito de Huarochirí”](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S0254-92392015000200003&script=sci_arttext).

### Evidence lost or unavailable

- No pre-Hispanic coastal script identifies divine images.
- Gómara does not name his informant.
- Zárate’s editions differ.
- The Huarochirí manuscript is incomplete and internally multivocal.
- Anti-idolatry investigations were coercive and selectively recorded what clergy wished to eradicate.
- Images and ritual objects were destroyed, hidden, displaced, or stripped of provenance.
- Archaeological cultures are modern classifications, not self-recorded ethnic or theological units.

|---|---|
| c. 800 BCE–200 BCE | Paracas visual traditions develop the beings now called Oculate or Masked figures. Their identification as Kon is a modern hypothesis. |

| c. 900–1470 CE | Pachacamac grows as a major central-coast sanctuary under successive political formations. This does not establish a preceding Kon temple. |

## 11. Sources and evidentiary conclusions

The reconstructable dossier is unusually narrow. The coastal Con is securely known from two mid-sixteenth-century Spanish chronicles, while Cuniraya is richly known from a single early-seventeenth-century Quechua manuscript. The strongest conclusions are therefore negative as well as positive:

- Con is securely a boneless, mobile coastal creator who causes desertification and is displaced by Pachacamac.
- The source does not securely call him a wind god, give him wings, identify parents, describe a temple, or preserve his ritual calendar.
- Cuniraya is securely a Huarochirí huaca invoked with coca, associated with creation, irrigation, difficult weaving, deception, sexuality, animal destinies, fish, and sacred landscape.
- Con and Cuniraya may be historically related, but their identity is not demonstrated.
- Kon’s identification with Paracas/Nasca imagery is an influential twentieth-century theory, not an archaeological fact.
- Claims for a living Kon cult in Cusco, the altiplano, Bolivia, or Argentina were not substantiated by the consulted ethnographic record.
- Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, occult correspondences, nationalist extremism, and a canonical visual form are not documented specifically for Kon.
- Much of the detailed “Kon mythology” now circulating online is a modern composite of Gómara, Huarochirí, Calancha, Rostworowski, archaeological imagery, and imaginative reconstruction.
