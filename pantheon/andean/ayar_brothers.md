# The Ayar Brothers: Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective of ancestral culture-founders; in the colonial sources they begin as human or superhuman siblings, but three become localized **wak’as**—sacred persons, stones, or places—and Ayar Manco becomes the dynastic founder Manco Cápac. Calling all four “gods” is therefore convenient but imprecise.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Pedro de Cieza de León’s *Segunda parte de la Crónica del Perú*, composed principally in **Cusco, c. 1549–1550**, chapters 6–8. Juan de Betanzos’s independently valuable *Suma y narración de los Incas*, completed in **1551**, chapters 3–4, supplies the earliest securely dated full four-brother version. Both are Spanish transcriptions of Quechua oral testimony made roughly two decades after the Spanish seizure of Cusco; no securely identified pre-Conquest inscription, manuscript, image, or archaeological object names the Ayar Brothers.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** recorded by approximately 1550–1580 from people who knew Inca institutions before or soon after the conquest.
- **[LC] Later colonial elaboration:** seventeenth-century chronicle or synthesis drawing on earlier testimony.
- **[RV] Regional or informant variant:** an explicitly divergent local, lineage, or authorial form.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, priestly, structuralist, calendrical, or linguistic organization not directly stated by the earliest witnesses.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically situated equation between different beings or religious categories.
- **[F] Folklore or living oral tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned interpretation rather than an ancient statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or creative adaptation.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

“The Ayar Brothers” is the modern collective name for the male half of a group of eight sibling-spouses who emerge from a cave or “window” at Tampu T’uqu near Paqariq Tampu and migrate toward Cusco. Their surviving names are:

| Figure | Principal colonial spellings | Conventional modern Quechua | Usual female partner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ayar Manco | Ayar Mango, Mango Cápac, Manco Cápac | Ayar Manqu / Manqo | Mama Ocllo/Uqllu |
| Ayar Cachi | Ayar Cache, Ayar Cachi | Ayar Kachi | Mama Huaco/Waqu |
| Ayar Uchu | Ayar Oche, Ayar Ucho, Ayar Uchu | Ayar Uchu | Mama Cura, Ipacura or Qura |
| Ayar Auca | Ayar Auca, Ayar Sauca | Ayar Awqa | Mama Raua/Rawa |

The pairings change between authors. They should not be regularized into a supposedly original canonical arrangement.

### “Ayar”

**[Uncertain]** No accepted etymology exists. “Ayar” does not function as a transparent personal name in the colonial Quechua dictionaries. Proposals include:

- **[LS/MSR]** connection with *ayar*, a wild quinoa or chenopod;
- **[MSR]** “ancestor,” inferred from the cycle’s social function;
- **[MSR]** survival of the Incas’ “particular” or court language—frequently hypothesized to have been Puquina or a related language—later reinterpreted through Quechua.

The last possibility is strengthened by Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino’s demonstration that several royal names traditionally given easy Quechua explanations may instead be Quechua adaptations of older, non-Quechua forms. It remains a linguistic reconstruction, not an attested Inca gloss.

### Individual names

- **Ayar Kachi.** Quechua *kachi*, “salt,” is lexically secure. **[LS]** This produced an agricultural interpretation in which the brothers embody cultivated foods or condiments. Cerrón-Palomino alternatively proposes a Puquina-related sense, approximately “the enclosed one,” matching his entombment. That is ingenious but disputed and partly dependent on the narrative it explains.
- **Ayar Uchu.** Quechua *uchu*, “chili pepper,” generated the food-series interpretation. **[MSR]** Cerrón-Palomino instead derives the element through Puquina as “small/younger,” opposing Ayar Auca as elder.
- **Ayar Awqa.** Quechua *awqa* means an enemy, opponent, rebel, or warrior. “Warrior Ayar” is therefore plausible. Some later sources spell the name *Sauca*, which may reflect scribal corruption or a distinct interpretation.
- **Ayar Manqu.** *Manqu/Manqo* has no uncontested Quechua derivation. Attempts to connect it to the extinct crop *Bromus mango*, to “lord/chief,” or to a Puquina title are **[MSR]**.
- **Manco Cápac / Manqu Qhapaq.** In the narrative Ayar Manco assumes the royal name Manco Cápac after the other brothers have become stones or disappeared. Quechua *qhapaq* denotes someone rich, powerful, royal, or magnanimous.

### Paqariq Tampu and Tampu T’uqu

*Paqariq Tampu* is conventionally analyzed from Quechua *paqar(i)y*, “to dawn, be born, originate,” plus *tampu*, a lodging or station: approximately “the lodging/place of origin” or “dawning tambo.” Spanish spellings include Pacaritambo, Paccari-tampu and Paccarectambo. *Tampu T’uqu* means approximately “tambo of the openings/niches”; early Spanish writers translated *t’uqu* as “window.”

Sarmiento distinguishes three openings:

- **Qhapaq T’uqu**, “royal/principal opening,” whence the Ayars emerge;
- **Maras T’uqu**, whence the Maras people emerge;
- **Sutiq T’uqu**, whence the Tampu people emerge.

This arrangement converts a landscape into a charter of ranked ethnic origins: the royal Inca center flanked by allied or subordinate peoples.

### Huanacauri / Wanakawri

Spellings include Guanacauri, Huanacaure, Huanacauri, Wanakawri and Wanakauri. Its etymology is unresolved. Sarmiento connects the name with the rainbow seen on the mountain; Juan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti uses a form interpreted by Markham as *Huayna-captiy*, but that antiquated analysis is not secure. In the chronicles “Huanacauri” can denote:

1. the mountain southeast of Cusco;
2. a pre-existing stone or being encountered there;
3. Ayar Uchu after petrification;
4. a portable or representational idol used in state ceremony;
5. the shrine complex itself.

These referents overlap but are not necessarily identical.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The absence of a pre-Conquest written record

The Inca used khipus, oral specialists, ritual performance and landscape monuments to preserve knowledge. No surviving khipu has been securely deciphered as a named Ayar narrative. Nor can any pre-Conquest image be identified by inscription as Ayar Cachi, Uchu or Auca. Accordingly, the story’s antiquity before 1532 is highly probable—particularly because its places and Huanacauri cult were embedded in Inca ritual—but its pre-Conquest wording and sequence cannot be recovered directly.

### Huarochirí and Cajatambo: relevant parallels, not witnesses

The Quechua *Huarochirí Manuscript*, compiled around the beginning of the seventeenth century in Francisco de Ávila’s extirpation milieu, names regional beings such as Pariacaca, Wallallu Qarwinchu, Cuniraya and Chaupiñamca—not the Ayar Brothers. The Cajatambo idolatry trials likewise preserve local ancestral and wak’a traditions but do not constitute an independent Ayar corpus.

Their relevance is comparative: emergence from a particular landscape, an ancestor’s transformation into stone, a wak’a’s territorial constituency, and the continued social presence of sacred beings. Using Huarochirí stories as if they were missing chapters of the Ayar migration would flatten distinct regional traditions.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Collective domain

**[EA]** The Ayars govern no single natural “portfolio.” Their collective function is foundational:

- origin of the Inca ruling community;
- migration and selection of Cusco;
- differentiation of political, military and ritual offices;
- taking possession of land;
- conversion of mobile ancestors into permanent sacred landmarks;
- establishment of royal descent and initiation.

The story is therefore simultaneously cosmogonic, genealogical, territorial and constitutional.

### Ayar Cachi

**Attributes:** prodigious strength, violence, sling and stones. In Sarmiento he breaks mountains or opens ravines with sling-stones. His excessive power alarms his siblings.

**Limitation:** he is deceived by collective strategy and physically enclosed at Qhapaq T’uqu.

**Transformation:** versions differ:

- **Betanzos/Sarmiento [EA]:** sealed in the cave; he curses the servant or messenger, who becomes stone.
- **Cieza [EA/RV]:** later appears with colored wings, announces the Inca destiny, and becomes a sacred stone.
- **Later summaries [LC]:** sometimes merge his petrification with Uchu’s.

The popular formula “Cachi was simply walled in forever” accurately follows Sarmiento but suppresses Cieza’s return.

### Ayar Uchu

**Attributes:** ritual mediation, prophecy and bestowal of noble valor. In Sarmiento he sits on or challenges the Huanacauri wak’a; his feet adhere to it, and he becomes stone.

**Domain after petrification:** father or patron of the *warachikuy/huarachico*, the male initiation and investiture of elite youths. He commands that he receive the first offering in their ceremonies and promises courage, nobility and knighthood.

**Iconography:** There is no securely identified pre-Conquest portrait of Ayar Uchu. His recognizable “form” in the texts is lithic: the Huanacauri stone, associated shrine and possibly a portable cult image. Modern pictures of an individualized warrior are reconstructions.

### Ayar Auca

**Attributes:** movement, warfare and territorial seizure. In Sarmiento he is assigned to “take possession” wherever the group decides to settle. He flies to or alights upon the selected site and becomes stone.

**Function:** his petrified body is a boundary or possession marker within Cusco. Sources and modern retellings variously locate the stone at or near the future Coricancha, Inticancha or another central site. “The stone of Cusco” is more defensible than an exact surviving identification.

### Ayar Manco / Manco Cápac

**Attributes:** leadership, fertility, dynastic continuity and settlement. He survives in human form, marries Mama Ocllo, fathers Sinchi Roca in Sarmiento, assumes the title Manco Cápac and negotiates or fights for settlement in Cusco.

**Emblems:** a gold staff or rod appears prominently in some origin accounts, especially those harmonized with the Manco Cápac–Mama Ocllo cycle. It tests the soil and reveals the chosen settlement. It is not uniformly present in every early Ayar account.

### Stones as persons

The transformations are not merely punishments or “deaths.” Within Andean ontology as recorded by the chroniclers, stone could be the durable embodied condition of an ancestor or wak’a. Cachi’s forced enclosure differs morally from Uchu’s ritual self-localization and Auca’s act of possession. Later European summaries often collapse all three into “turned to stone.”

### Colors, animals, planets and numbers

No primary source securely assigns each Ayar a fixed color, planet, weekday, sacred animal or modern elemental power. Four brothers, four sisters, four quarters and four projected sling-stones invite quadripartite interpretation, but direct equations—Cachi = north, Uchu = fire, and so forth—are **[LS/MI]** unless tied to a particular scholar or adaptation.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Competing origins

1. **Viracocha’s creations — Sarmiento, 1572 [EA/official systematization].** The Ayars know no father or mother; Ticci Viracocha creates them and orders them out through Qhapaq T’uqu to become lords.
2. **Children of the Sun — later dynastic harmonization [LC/SI].** Garcilaso’s preferred origin makes Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo children or emissaries of the Sun. Later retellings import this parentage into the four-Ayar story.
3. **Children of Apotampo and Pachamama Achi — Santa Cruz Pachacuti, c. 1613 [RV].** Apo Manco Cápac has named parents, producing a human-divine ancestral genealogy different from Sarmiento’s creation.
4. **Autochthonous emergence [EA].** Betanzos emphasizes “production” from the cave; genealogy is secondary to emergence from the land.

### Sibling-spouses

All eight are called siblings in major accounts, and the women also function as wives. Pairings differ. Sarmiento’s allocation—Manco/Mama Ocllo, Cachi/Mama Huaco, Uchu/Mama Ipacura and Auca/Mama Raua—is common but not universal.

The women are not passive appendages. Mama Huaco in several traditions is forceful and warlike; she intimidates local inhabitants and helps secure settlement. Mama Ocllo anchors Manco’s dynastic fertility. Treating the episode as “four brothers accompanied by wives” obscures the original eightfold founding group.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or sacred being

Because the Ayars interact mostly with one another, their sisters and localized wak’as, the list is short. Shared membership in “the Inca pantheon” is not counted as interaction.

- **Ticci/Contiti Viracocha — creates or commands the Ayars to emerge and rule.** Sarmiento, *Historia de los Incas*, ch. 11, 1572. **[EA/official systematization]**
- **Huanacauri, treated as a pre-existing wak’a — Ayar Uchu sits upon or challenges it; the being turns its head, Uchu becomes fastened to it, and the two identities thereafter converge as “Ayar Uchu Huanacauri.”** Sarmiento, ch. 12, 1572. **[EA/SI]**
- **Inti, the Sun — Manco Cápac is made the Sun’s child or emissary in Garcilaso’s preferred origin account; later retellings combine that relationship with the Ayar cycle.** Garcilaso, *Comentarios reales*, book I, 1609. **[LC/SI]**
- **Rainbow/Huanacauri — Sarmiento reports a rainbow on the summit as the favorable sign associated with the place-name; Manco warns the group concerning it.** Sarmiento, ch. 12, 1572. **[EA, textually ambiguous whether the rainbow is a separate being]**
- **No securely attested interaction with Pachamama as a goddess.** Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s *Pachamama Achi* is a named maternal figure, but equating her without qualification with the later generalized earth-being Pachamama is **[MSR/SI]**.
- **No interaction is recorded with Pariacaca, Pachacamac, Illapa, Mama Quilla or the Huarochirí deities.** Their appearance beside the Ayars in modern pantheon lists is classification, not narrative contact.

### Interactions within the Ayar collective

- **Ayar Manco, Uchu and Auca against Cachi:** conspire to neutralize Cachi by sending him back to the cave; Betanzos, Cieza and Sarmiento, c. 1550–1572. **[EA, with variant motives and agents]**
- **Cachi and the sealing servant:** Cachi curses the betrayer, who becomes stone in Sarmiento; 1572. **[EA]**
- **Cachi and the remaining siblings:** winged Cachi reappears, prophesies their success and requests worship in Cieza; c. 1550. **[EA/RV]**
- **Uchu and Manco:** Uchu orders Manco to continue, establish the polity and honor him in initiation; Sarmiento, 1572. **[EA]**
- **Auca and Manco:** Auca carries out Manco’s order to occupy the chosen ground and, after becoming stone, confirms Manco’s leadership; Sarmiento, 1572. **[EA]**
- **Manco and Mama Ocllo:** sibling marriage and procreation of Sinchi Roca; Sarmiento, 1572. **[EA]**
- **Manco and Mama Huaco:** some versions give her to Manco after Cachi’s removal, creating a second consort relationship; Betanzos-derived tradition. **[EA/RV]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 Emergence from the three openings

**[EA: Betanzos and Sarmiento]** After a destruction or reordering of the former world, the founding people emerge at Tampu T’uqu. From the principal central opening come the four Ayar men and four women; from the lateral openings come Maras and Tampu groups. Ten ayllus or accompanying communities appear in some versions.

Sarmiento explicitly makes Viracocha the creator and commissioner. Betanzos places the episode after his Viracocha creation narrative but emphasizes the ancestors’ emergence. Cobo later sets it after the universal flood, language partly shaped by Christian universal history.

**Function [MSR]:** a ranked charter linking royal Incas, nonroyal Inca groups and allied peoples to distinct openings in a single sacred mountain.

### 5.2 Cachi’s displays and imprisonment

Cachi’s sling-stones rupture hills in four directions. The others fear that his strength will dominate them. They persuade him to return to Qhapaq T’uqu for sacred objects, seeds, vessels or a golden llama, depending on the version. A companion seals the entrance with a great stone.

This is not a simple heroic monster-slaying: the dangerous figure is a sibling whose necessary military power threatens internal hierarchy. Sarmiento says the survivors later regret losing him because they will need strength in war.

Cieza’s distinctive sequel has the imprisoned brother return with multicolored wings. He announces that the others will found Cusco, then becomes stone. This early variant proves that the popular sequence—Cachi disappears, Uchu becomes Huanacauri, Auca becomes the Cusco stone—was never the only one.

### 5.3 Ayar Uchu and Huanacauri

At Quirirmanta the surviving group divides functions: Manco will reproduce the lineage and lead it; Uchu will remain as a religious wak’a; Auca will take possession of the chosen territory.

On Huanacauri’s summit they see the rainbow. In Sarmiento, Uchu sits on a sacred stone, asks what it is doing there, and becomes attached to it. Unable to leave, he interprets his transformation as service to his siblings. He demands precedence in offerings and patronage over the *huarachico*. He then becomes stone and is called **Ayar Uchu Huanacauri**.

This version simultaneously:

- provides an origin for a state shrine;
- makes political descent dependent upon ritual investiture;
- converts a possibly older local sacred place into an Inca sibling;
- makes permanent territorial presence from bodily petrification.

Whether an already-venerated local Huanacauri was absorbed into an Inca dynastic myth is a persuasive **[MSR]**, not something the texts explicitly confess.

### 5.4 Ayar Auca takes Cusco

From Matahua or another overlook, the migrants see the Cusco valley. A golden staff or projectile identifies suitable ground. Auca is dispatched to claim it. In Sarmiento he flies or miraculously traverses the space, lands at the chosen place and becomes stone.

His transformation is an act of juridical and sacred occupancy: the ancestor’s body fixes the group’s claim in the terrain. Later sources disagree over the stone’s exact location and over whether Cachi, Uchu or Auca performs particular feats.

### 5.5 Manco Cápac and the settlement

With the other brothers immobilized as wak’as or removed, Ayar Manco becomes Manco Cápac. Accounts alternate between peaceful entry, negotiation with earlier inhabitants and violent displacement.

Betanzos names local communities and describes the marshy early Cusco landscape. Mama Huaco’s aggression is decisive in some versions: she attacks an opponent with a *bolas* or *ayllu*, opens his body and inflates the lungs, terrifying his companions into flight. Later sanitized school versions frequently omit this episode.

Manco establishes residence, divides land, institutes cultivation and begins the dynasty. The surviving brother does not abolish the others: their stones sacralize the route and authorize his rule.

### 5.6 Relation to the Lake Titicaca origin

Garcilaso’s famous account sends Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo from the Sun—often popularly localized at Lake Titicaca—with a golden staff. The Pacaritambo migration and Titicaca mission are distinct colonial narrative forms, though later writers combine them.

Possible explanations include:

- **[MSR]** rival lineage traditions;
- **[MSR]** an older local Cusco migration story overlaid by an imperial solar theology associated with Titicaca;
- **[MSR]** different genres—corporate origin charter versus moralizing royal foundation story;
- **[LC]** Garcilaso’s literary and theological preference for a civilizing solar couple.

There is no basis for declaring one account “the true Inca version.”

## 6. Cult, temples and ritual practice

### Huanacauri as principal cult center

Huanacauri, approximately southeast of Cusco, was among the most important wak’as of the capital’s ritual landscape. Sarmiento calls it the most solemn and richly offered-to wak’a of the realm. Cobo’s ceque catalogue and Molina’s ritual account place it within the state ceremonial system.

Brian Bauer’s reconstruction describes the Cusco ceque system as hundreds of shrines arranged along radiating lines from the Coricancha and maintained by particular kin groups. His archaeological survey identified probable ritual locations around Huanacauri, but matching a present stone or ruin to the precise sixteenth-century idol remains uncertain.

The shrine could include natural rock, built space, attendants and a transportable representation. Colonial “idol” language often compresses this complex into a single object.

### Qhapaq Raymi and *warachikuy*

Molina’s c. 1575 account is the primary witness. During Qhapaq Raymi, elite youths underwent ordeals, received clothing and insignia, had their ears pierced, ran prescribed routes and made offerings. Huanacauri was a culminating or central station.

Sarmiento retrojects the first *huarachico* to Sinchi Roca at Matahua and makes Ayar Uchu its divine patron. The ceremony reproduced the migration: each generation of noble youths traversed ancestral space and acquired status from the petrified founder.

### Priests and custodians

Molina refers to persons “who had charge of the wak’a of Huanacauri,” indicating a permanent custodial body. The broader ceque system assigned maintenance and offerings to ayllus and royal descent groups. Surviving sources do not permit a complete personnel roster specifically for each Ayar shrine.

### Offerings

Documented or reported offerings at Huanacauri include:

- maize beer or chicha;
- coca;
- textiles and fine clothing;
- camelids;
- gold, silver and shell objects;
- food;
- in exceptional state ceremonies, humans.

Sarmiento’s claim of superlative offerings agrees with Huanacauri’s rank but comes from an author committed to condemning “idolatry.”

### Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice in the Inca *qhapaq hucha/capacocha* system is supported independently by chronicles and archaeology. Cieza reports that two boys and two girls were sacrificed before the Huanacauri idol; other chroniclers give much larger empire-wide numbers for royal accessions, crises or festivals. Archaeological finds from high-altitude shrines verify that selected children were killed or buried in state ceremonies, though they do not verify every colonial total.

Therefore:

- **Secure:** the Inca state at times sacrificed children in *qhapaq hucha* ceremonies.
- **Textually attested:** Huanacauri received human victims according to Cieza and later summaries.
- **Uncertain:** the frequency and exact totals.
- **Unsupported:** that each Ayar brother maintained a separate routine human-sacrifice cult.

Garcilaso’s denial of Inca human sacrifice reflects his apologetic presentation and conflicts with multiple witnesses and material evidence.

### Paqariq Tampu

Sarmiento says Pachacuti later visited and richly honored the ancestral opening, installed golden doors and established worship, oracle consultation and sacrifice. This is **[EA as a sixteenth-century report about imperial memory]**, not archaeologically proven building history.

Urton’s fieldwork around modern Pacariqtambo documented competing local identifications, oral memories and a 1560s legal case in which local elites invoked descent from Manco Cápac to claim chiefly status and exemption. The “origin place” was therefore not static: colonial law encouraged communities to historicize and localize the myth.

### Politics and economics

The cult validated:

- the royal Incas’ claim to Cusco;
- the rank of particular ayllus;
- elite initiation;
- labor obligations for shrine maintenance;
- distributions of offerings and prestige;
- colonial claims to noble descent and exemption.

The stones were thus religious persons, territorial title-deeds and nodes in political administration.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1532: reconstructable Inca phase

No named contemporary text survives. The Huanacauri cult, ceque embedding, pilgrimage route and elite initiation system strongly indicate a pre-Conquest institutional basis. The exact age of the four-brother narrative cannot be established.

Archaeological models often place early Inca formation in the Cusco region during the late first or early second millennium CE, but archaeology cannot assign the chronicle characters as historical individuals. Reading four brothers as four literal tribal chiefs is **[MSR]**.

### 1530s–1550s: conquest and first recording

Spanish occupation disrupted the state cult, but Inca nobles, priests and khipu specialists remained available to Cieza and Betanzos. Their accounts already disagree, demonstrating that variation preceded the late colonial literary tradition.

### 1560s–1570s: legal and viceregal historicization

A Pacariqtambo descent claim documented in the 1560s shows the narrative operating in colonial litigation. In 1572 Toledo’s government used Sarmiento’s history to portray the Incas as recent, violent usurpers. The same narrative could therefore authorize Indigenous nobility locally and undermine Inca sovereignty imperially.

Sarmiento says Christians had abolished Huanacauri initiation approximately twenty years before he wrote. This suggests suppression around the 1550s, though “abolished” does not prove that all private observance ended.

### Late sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: extirpation and textual preservation

Albornoz, Ávila, Arriaga and other ecclesiastical agents sought sacred objects, interrogated ritual specialists and destroyed wak’as. Their records are indispensable but coercive evidence: testimony could be shaped by leading questions, translation, fear and Christian demonological categories.

The Huarochirí and Cajatambo archives demonstrate that Andean sacred practice persisted, adapted and was prosecuted. They do not prove an Ayar cult outside the Cusco sphere.

Cobo’s 1653 synthesis preserves disagreement while subordinating the material to Christian polemic. By then the Ayar cycle had become both an antiquarian account and a condemned religious past.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

Direct institutional worship of the Ayar collective is poorly documented. Elements persisted through attachment to mountains, caves, ancestors, agricultural rites and Christianized festival landscapes, but tracing any particular practice continuously to an Ayar brother requires local evidence.

The publication of previously manuscript chronicles—especially Cieza in 1880 and Sarmiento in 1906—turned the cycle into a modern national and academic text.

### Twentieth century: national history and ethnohistory

The Ayars entered school curricula, public monuments, theater and narratives of Peruvian origins. Scholars including R. Tom Zuidema interpreted the migration through ceques, moieties, calendar and kinship; María Rostworowski emphasized political and agricultural meanings; Pierre Duviols analyzed wak’as and colonial extirpation; Sabine MacCormack studied how European theological categories transformed descriptions of Andean religion; Brian Bauer tested textual reconstructions against landscape archaeology.

Urton’s *The History of a Myth* moved the question from “Where did the myth really happen?” toward “How did communities, nobles and colonial institutions make this account into history?”

### Twenty-first century: revitalization

A documented **Ceremonia Ritual del Wanakawri: Tawantinsuyuq Paqarinan** began as a school-based performance in **2001**, reportedly devised by teacher Jaime Araoz Chacón and colleagues. It expanded from approximately 300 participants to a larger civic-cultural event involving students, families and cultural organizations.

This is a genuine modern tradition but not proof of uninterrupted performance since Inca times. It is best labeled **[modern cultural revitalization]**, combining historical research, regional identity, education, ceremony and theatrical representation.

Claims of direct, unchanged Ayar worship in the Bolivian altiplano or Argentine northwest could not be securely established. Those regions contain living Andean veneration of ancestors, mountains and Pachamama, and were incorporated into Tawantinsuyu, but similarity is not evidence of a continuous Ayar cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and theater

Juan Ríos’s drama *Ayar Manko* appeared in **1952**, transforming the origin cycle into modern Peruvian theater. School and community dramatizations further standardize costumes, speeches and moral roles absent from the chronicles.

### Comics and commercial media

Tawa Producciones, founded in Arequipa in 2009, developed *Ayar, la leyenda de los inkas*, a multi-volume comic. Reporting by PromPerú and *El Comercio* documents international ambitions, manga-style experiments and proposed film adaptation. Character-specific supernatural powers in such adaptations are **[MI]**, even where the plot draws on Betanzos or Sarmiento.

### Tourism and heritage

Peruvian tourism agencies market Pacaritambo and Paruro as “the land of the Ayar Brothers.” Official tourism descriptions often blend Sarmiento, Betanzos, Garcilaso, folklore and present-day staging into one seamless story. These initiatives help preserve local identification but are not critical editions of the tradition.

### Neopagan and occult claims

The dedicated search found no evidence that the Ayar Brothers played a documented role in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy or classical European grimoires. Assertions that they possess fixed planetary, elemental or tarot correspondences are therefore **[MI]** unless the modern author is named and dated.

Contemporary Andean spiritual or reconstructionist groups may honor Manco Cápac, Mama Ocllo, Wanakawri or ancestral wak’as. Their practice deserves description as present religion where documented, but “revival” should not be relabeled uninterrupted survival.

### Political and nationalist use

The Ayars serve as:

- founders of Cusco and Peru in school narrative;
- symbols of Indigenous antiquity and dignity;
- emblems of Pacaritambo and Paruro;
- models of fourfold unity;
- resources in decolonial cultural revitalization.

The cycle has not been shown to have a stable modern extremist appropriation comparable to the political afterlives of some European deities. General Inca imagery has, however, been used by governments, regional movements and commercial nationalism across the political spectrum.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Chronicle versus history

Older scholarship frequently attempted to extract literal migrations, dates and tribal chiefs from the narrative. Current source criticism more often treats it as **mythohistory**: claims about the past that organized land, kinship, hierarchy and ritual, subsequently reshaped by colonial recording.

This does not make it “mere fiction.” A stone’s identity as an ancestor could structure pilgrimage, sacrifice, property and political office whether or not a modern historian can identify an individual behind the name.

### One original myth or several traditions?

The sources favor plurality. Cieza, Betanzos and Sarmiento agree on the broad pattern but disagree about:

- the number or enumeration of brothers;
- spouse pairings;
- which brother returns or sprouts wings;
- which figure becomes which stone;
- the presence and role of Viracocha;
- the use of a golden staff;
- peaceful versus violent occupation;
- named parents;
- the precise sacred itinerary.

A hypothetical common pre-Conquest narrative is plausible, but no recoverable “original text” survives.

### Cieza’s “three brothers”

Some editions and summaries state that Cieza knew only three brothers. Cerrón-Palomino argues that the apparent discrepancy results from punctuation or manuscript reading: Cieza’s phrase can be parsed as naming four. The dispute should remain textual rather than resolved from later canonical expectation.

### Food-symbol interpretation

The traditional series reads *kachi* as salt, *uchu* as chili, Manco as an obsolete grain and Auca as either warrior or toasted maize. It is attractive because it makes the founders agricultural culture-bearers, but the series is linguistically uneven: *awqa* does not ordinarily mean toasted maize, and *Manqu* remains problematic.

Cerrón-Palomino’s Puquina-oriented readings offer a coherent hierarchy—elder, younger, enclosed, chief—but depend on reconstruction of poorly documented Puquina and cannot be treated as final.

### Structural and calendrical interpretations

Zuidema related the siblings, stations and transformations to Cusco’s moieties, ceques, kin groups and ritual calendar. Such analysis illuminates patterned institutions, but a tidy fourfold diagram can exceed what any single chronicler states. It is **[MSR/LS]**, not recovered Inca doctrine verbatim.

### Urton’s intervention

Urton combined:

- chronicler comparison;
- the 1560s Pacariqtambo legal dossier;
- local geography;
- twentieth-century fieldwork;
- community narratives.

His central insight is that “Pacariqtambo” became authoritative through political and social use. Colonial actors did not merely preserve a fossilized myth: they selected locations, genealogies and versions that served present claims. Reviews recognized the book as an important fusion of ethnography and historical memory.

### Archaeology and the “real” Pacaritambo

Archaeological survey can identify settlements, roads, shrines and occupation histories around candidate places. It cannot prove that a particular cave literally emitted the Ayars. Modern announcements that archaeology has “proved the legend” generally move beyond the evidence.

### Colonial mediation

MacCormack emphasizes that Spaniards interpreted wak’as through inherited categories of “idol,” demon, false god and natural object. Indigenous Christian writers also reconfigured the past through biblical chronology and providential history. Neither Spanish nor Indigenous colonial authors are transparent windows onto the fifteenth century; both are indispensable historical witnesses to the sixteenth and seventeenth.

### Images

No securely authenticated pre-Conquest image bears an Ayar name. Guaman Poma’s drawings are colonial visual arguments, not photographs of Inca ritual. Modern depictions—four muscular warriors in coordinated colors, each with an elemental power—are creative iconography.

### State of the sources

- Cieza’s second part survived in manuscript and appeared in print only in 1880.
- Betanzos was known chiefly through an eighteen-chapter fragment until the fuller manuscript was identified in Palma de Mallorca in the 1980s.
- Sarmiento’s 1572 manuscript circulated poorly and was published in 1906.
- The Huarochirí manuscript survives in Madrid and became widely accessible through twentieth-century editions by Hermann Trimborn, José María Arguedas, Gérald Taylor, and Frank Salomon with George Urioste.
- Extirpation records survive dispersed through ecclesiastical and civil archives; their evidentiary value is inseparable from coercive collection.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Event in the evidentiary history |
|---|---|
| Before 1532 | Huanacauri functions within the Inca sacred landscape and elite ceremonial system. Exact date of the Ayar narrative’s formation is unknown. |
| 1532–1533 | Spanish invasion begins the political destruction of the Inca state cult; no contemporary conquest document yet records a full Ayar cycle. |
| c. 1549–1550 | Cieza de León writes the earliest surviving account of the Pacaritambo ancestors and the winged return of the imprisoned brother. |
| 1551 | Betanzos completes *Suma y narración de los Incas*, preserving an early four-brother/four-sister account. |
| c. 1550s | Approximate suppression of Huanacauri initiation inferred from Sarmiento’s statement in 1572 that Christians had stopped it about twenty years earlier. |
| 1560s | Pacariqtambo elites employ descent from Manco Cápac in colonial legal claims, the central dossier analyzed by Urton. |
| 1572 | Sarmiento completes his Toledo-commissioned *Historia de los Incas*, the fullest ordered version of Cachi’s enclosure, Uchu’s Huanacauri transformation and Auca’s occupation of Cusco. |
| c. 1575–1576 | Molina records Huanacauri’s place in Qhapaq Raymi, elite initiation and the Inca ritual calendar. |
| c. 1580s | Albornoz compiles instructions for identifying and suppressing wak’as; the colonial campaign transforms sacred landscapes while generating records about them. |
| 1586 | Cabello de Balboa incorporates Inca origins into *Miscelánea antártica*. |
| c. 1590–1616 | Murúa records a variant that reallocates some brothers’ deeds. |
| 1609 | Garcilaso publishes part I of *Comentarios reales*, privileging the solar Manco Cápac–Mama Ocllo account while acknowledging Pacaritambo tradition. |
| c. 1613 | Santa Cruz Pachacuti records a distinctive parental genealogy for Apo Manco Cápac. |
| c. 1615 | Guaman Poma completes the *Nueva corónica*, placing Andean antiquity within an Indigenous-Christian universal history. |
| 1621 | Arriaga publishes *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú*, systematizing methods for dismantling living Indigenous cult. |
| 1653 | Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, preserving multiple incompatible endings for Manco’s brothers. |
| 1880 | Cieza’s second part receives its first major printed edition. |
| 1906 | Richard Pietschmann publishes Sarmiento’s long-unavailable history. |
| 1939–1991 | Successive editions of the Huarochirí Manuscript reshape comparative study of Andean wak’as, although it does not name the Ayars. |
| 1952 | Juan Ríos’s *Ayar Manko* gives the cycle a prominent modern theatrical form. |
| 1980s | Discovery of the fuller Betanzos manuscript greatly enlarges the available chronicle. |
| 1990 | Gary Urton publishes *The History of a Myth: Pacariqtambo and the Origin of the Inkas*. |
| 1990–1995 | Brian Bauer’s field project surveys and reconstructs the Cusco ceque landscape. |
| 1998 | Bauer publishes *The Sacred Landscape of the Inca*. |
| 2001 | Educators initiate the modern Wanakawri ceremonial dramatization, initially with approximately 300 participants. |
| 2009 onward | Tawa Producciones develops *Ayar, la leyenda de los inkas* as a modern comic franchise. |
| 2010s–2020s | Pacaritambo, Paruro and Wanakawri receive renewed cultural, educational and tourism promotion; staged ritual participation expands. |
| 2026 | The Ayars remain active chiefly in scholarship, education, regional identity, tourism, art and selective Indigenous cultural revitalization. Continuous institutional worship of the full four-brother collective from Inca times to the present is not demonstrable. |
