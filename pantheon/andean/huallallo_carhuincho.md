# Huallallo Carhuincho: Research Dossier

> **Class:** *huaca*—a localized sacred being embodied in or associated with landscape, shrine, and natural powers. Colonial sources variously render such beings as “god,” “idol,” or “demon”; “fire god” is a useful modern shorthand but not the source’s complete classification. In present Mantaro practice the name may identify an *apu* or *wamani*, especially the being resident in the Huaytapallana massif.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The name appears as **Uallallo/Wallallo** in late-sixteenth-century documentary traditions: Cristóbal de Albornoz’s *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú* (normally dated ca. 1581–1585) and Diego Dávila Briceño’s 1586 *Descripción y relación de la provincia de los Yauyos*. These notices are brief and do not preserve the complete narrative. The earliest extensive evidence is the anonymous Quechua **Huarochirí Manuscript**, compiled around 1598–1608 in Francisco de Ávila’s anti-idolatry milieu. The manuscript survives in Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional de España, MS 3169. Its Huallallo material appears principally in chapters 1, 5–6, 8–9, and 16–17. [S1][S2][S3]

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** a sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century primary source.
- **[LE] Later early-modern elaboration:** a colonial account later than, or dependent on traditions comparable to, the earliest evidence.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a locally distinct oral or written tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian, theological, linguistic, or scholarly reconstruction.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** an equation or merger between historically distinguishable beings.
- **[F] Folklore/ethnography:** modern or recent oral and ritual evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim:** a recent assertion not demonstrated by early sources.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Huallallo Carhuincho is the defeated sovereign of a former world order in the Huarochirí Manuscript. He defeats the earlier huacas Yanañamca and Tutañamca, rules a landscape imagined as ecologically *Yunca* even in what are now cold highlands, permits or causes extraordinary fertility and five-day revival after death, but requires one of every two children as food. Pariacaca, a fivefold mountain-and-storm being, displaces him by rain, hail, lightning, cold, and flood. Huallallo retreats eastward toward the *Anti* lowlands; Pariacaca sentences him to eat dogs rather than humans and assigns his worship to the Huanca. This is the manuscript’s Checa/Yauyo-centered account, not a neutral description of Huanca religion. [S2][S4]

### Documented forms

- **Huallallo Caruincho / Huallallo Carhuincho** — spelling in modern editions of the Huarochirí Manuscript. **[EA]**
- **Caruancho Uallullo** — Guaman Poma, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, ca. 1615–1616; notably the order is reversed. **[EA]**
- **Uallollo / Uallallo** — Juan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui Salcamayhua, *Relación de antigüedades deste reyno del Pirú*, ca. 1613, fols. 15v and 20r. **[EA]**
- **Wallallo / Huallallo / Huallullu** — normalized scholarly or regional spellings. **[LS/F]**
- **Wallallu Qarwinchu / Qarwanchu Wallullu** — modern Quechua-oriented normalizations. The historical manuscript itself uses colonial orthography rather than a standardized modern alphabet. **[LS]**
- **Qalalu Karwancho** and similar forms circulate online but have not been securely traced here to an early manuscript or reliable linguistic edition. **[MI unless independently documented]**
- **Allqu mikuq**, “dog eater,” is a descriptive modern Quechua label derived from the colonial dog-sacrifice narrative, not a securely demonstrated ancient cult title. **[LS/F]**

### Etymology

The name’s etymology is disputed and should not be treated as settled.

Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino analyzes **Huallallo/Huallullo** from Aymara *wallu*, “rocks adjoining hills or standing in open ground,” reduplicated as *wallu-wallu*, “cordillera/range of rocks.” He reads **Carhuancho/Qarwanchu** from Central Quechua *qarwa*, “yellow, golden,” plus an obsolete or uncertain adjectival element *-nchu*, yielding approximately “yellowish range of rocks” or “yellowish snowy cordillera.” He argues that the two components remained a loose rather than fixed compound: their order could reverse, and *Carhuincho* could be omitted. This is an explicit modern linguistic hypothesis, not a translation supplied by the Huarochirí narrator. **[LS]** [S5]

Popular segmentations into *hualla* “warrior,” *allu* “dog,” and *winchu* “hummingbird” are not supported by the manuscript’s grammar or by a historical linguistic demonstration comparable to Cerrón-Palomino’s. They appear to be retrospective explanations assembled from traits assigned to the deity. **[MI/contested]**

### Historical classifications

- **Huarochirí Manuscript:** a *huaca* and former ruler; also “Man Eater, Man Drinker.” The English editors’ phrase “fire-monster” is interpretive. **[EA]**
- **Colonial Christian discourse:** an *ídolo*, *demonio*, or *supay*. These labels express the extirpators’ theology and are not Indigenous taxonomic equivalents. **[EA]**
- **Modern scholarship:** deity, fire deity, culture hero, or personified former ecological order. **[LS]**
- **Modern Mantaro testimony:** *apu*, *wamani*, or the being in Huaytapallana; sometimes related to **Tayta Shanti** (Saint James). **[F/SI]** [S6]
- **“Inca fire god”:** too broad. The evidence concerns a central-Andean regional being incorporated into Inca-era and colonial accounts; no primary source establishes a uniform imperial “Inca pantheon” in which he held a standardized fire-god office.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Pre-Conquest evidence

No securely identified pre-Hispanic inscription, named statue, painted image, or archaeological object bearing Huallallo’s name is presently known. Andean sacred beings could be materially embodied without writing, but linking an anonymous figure or shrine to this name requires independent evidence. Proposed identifications of metal ornaments, ceramics, the Huariwillka complex, Chavín imagery, or the emblem of the Universidad Nacional del Centro del Perú are hypotheses or modern inventions, not secure iconographic identifications.

The absence of a pre-Hispanic written name is especially important: the oldest surviving documentation was produced after Spanish conquest, frequently for administration, evangelization, or extirpation.

### Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción* (ca. 1581–1585)

**Language/support:** Spanish manuscript.  
**Role:** Catalogues huacas and methods for discovering Indigenous worship. Albornoz reportedly uses the shortened form *Uallallo*. His notice demonstrates that the name circulated before the Huarochirí Manuscript, but it does not transmit the complete fire-and-flood combat cycle. **[EA]**

### Diego Dávila Briceño, *Descripción y relación de la provincia de los Yauyos* (1586)

**Language/support:** Spanish administrative *Relación geográfica*, accompanied by a map.  
**Role:** Describes Yauyos geography, settlements, and religion. It belongs to the earliest documentary horizon for Wallallo traditions and establishes the regional context later elaborated in the Quechua manuscript. **[EA]** The surviving documentation should not be made to say that every feature of the later narrative was already present in 1586.

### Huarochirí Manuscript (ca. 1598–1608)

**Language/support:** Mainly colonial General Quechua, with Spanish chapter headings, marginalia, and editorial queries; paper manuscript now BNE MS 3169. It is associated with Francisco de Ávila’s investigation of “idolatry,” but its anonymous Indigenous compiler/redactor and multiple informants must not be collapsed into Ávila himself. Salomon and Urioste stress its Checa-centered viewpoint and unfinished editorial character. **[EA]** [S1][S2]

Relevant passages:

- **Chapter 1:** Huallallo defeats Yanañamca and Tutañamca; restricts reproduction to two children and eats one; the dead revive on the fifth day; food ripens five days after planting; the highlands are *Yunca* and contain brilliantly colored tropical birds. Pariacaca later drives Huallallo and his creatures toward the Anti.  
- **Chapters 5–6:** Pariacaca matures and seeks Huallallo, explicitly called “Man Eater, Man Drinker”; the approaching conflict is announced.
- **Chapter 8:** Huallallo’s dwelling is placed at Upper Pariacaca, at the site subsequently called Mullucocha. A man carrying a child, *mullu*, coca, and *ticti* to Huallallo is intercepted by Pariacaca. Pariacaca sends the child home and orders the man to return as witness. The five Pariacacas rain from five directions; Huallallo becomes an immense fire reaching almost to heaven. Llacsa Churapa dams the waters, forming Mullucocha, and Huallallo flees toward the Anti. Manañamca, “with” Huallallo, is separately expelled to the sea.
- **Chapter 9:** Pariacaca sentences Huallallo: because he ate humans, he shall eat dogs, and the Huanca shall worship him. The narrator uses this as an origin for the slur “dog-eating Huanca.”
- **Chapter 16:** Expanded pursuit narrative. Huallallo hides in Caquiyoca, releases a two-headed Amaru, and later deploys a *caqui* bird. Pariacaca petrifies the Amaru with a golden staff and breaks/petrifies the bird. Paria Carco remains as a snow peak guarding the pass to prevent Huallallo’s return.
- **Chapter 17:** Huallallo releases the unidentified *hugi* animal to kill or harm Pariacaca. It is eventually captured by a Checa man, and the episode becomes a charter for ritual authority.

The manuscript itself distinguishes report from eyewitness-like assertion by Quechua evidential particles, and its margins repeatedly demand additional information—“find out” the five brothers’ names; determine Huallallo’s present state; clarify kinship. Those uncertainties are part of the evidence.

### Guaman Poma, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (ca. 1615–1616)

Guaman Poma lists Chinchaysuyu cults and reports that Huanca/Xauxa groups sacrificed dogs, coca, food, dog blood, and *mullu* to “señor guaca Caruancho Uallullo.” He gives a ritual utterance explaining a bark—*uac*—as the sound of “our livestock” and calls the Huanca *alco micoc*, dog eaters. He condemns continued dog eating as punishable. **[EA]**

This corroborates a seventeenth-century association among the name, Huanca identity, dogs, blood offerings, and polemical stigma. It does **not** independently corroborate the Huarochirí child-eating story.

### Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui, *Relación de antigüedades* (ca. 1613)

Santa Cruz Pachacuti gives two different learned-historical placements:

1. Tunupa/“Ttonapa Uari Uillca” expels huacas including Pariacaca and Uallollo to snowy mountains.
2. Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui encounters seven huacas “in the form of very great curacas,” including Aysavillca, Pariacaca, Chinchacocha, Uallallo, and Chuquihuacra; after their defeat they are assigned labor.

**[EA/learned colonial systematization]** These are not variants of the detailed Huarochirí combat so much as different universalizing narratives subordinating local huacas first to Tunupa and then to Inca sovereignty. [S7]

### Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales* (1609)

Garcilaso reports that the Huanca worshipped a dog image, relished dog flesh at their greatest feast, and fashioned trumpets from dogs’ heads for music and warfare. He does not securely name Huallallo in the relevant passage. Joining his dog cult automatically to every Huarochirí episode is therefore an inference, even though Guaman Poma and the manuscript make a named Huallallo–Huanca–dog connection. **[LE/corroborative but not identical]**

### Other named chroniclers

Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Molina, Cobo, Arriaga, and the first part of Garcilaso are indispensable comparative sources for Inca religion, sacrifice, huacas, and suppression. In the material verifiable for this dossier, however, they do not supply an independent, detailed Huallallo narrative. Their silence should not be filled with reconstructed episodes. Arriaga’s *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621) is important for the institutions and material procedures of anti-idolatry, not as a principal Huallallo text.

### Editions and rediscovery

- Hermann Trimborn issued an early modern edition/translation in German.
- José María Arguedas published the influential Spanish *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí* in 1966.
- Gérald Taylor produced a paleographic Quechua edition and Spanish translation in 1987, with later revised editions.
- Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste published the Quechua transcription, English translation, introduction, and annotations in 1991. Their edition is especially valuable for evidential particles, editorial layers, and geography. [S1][S2]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Securely documented attributes

**Fire.** In chapter 8 Huallallo does not merely possess fire: he becomes or blazes as a gigantic fire extending almost to the sky. Pariacaca’s waters nearly submerge him. “Fire deity” is consequently well grounded, though “god of volcanoes” is an interpretation. **[EA]**

**Former fecundity and rapid renewal.** Under his order crops mature five days after planting and humans revive five days after death. This abundance creates overcrowding and misery. The text thus associates him with superabundant vitality as well as predation. **[EA]**

**Human consumption.** He eats one child from each pair. The Quechua permits ambiguity over whether the surviving child is the one preferred by the parents or by Huallallo. The text calls him “Man Eater, Man Drinker.” **[EA]**

**Tropical/Yunca ecology.** Huge snakes, tropical birds, and other warm-zone animals inhabit his highland domain. His expulsion explains their removal toward the eastern or lower warm country. **[EA]**

**Metamorphosis and monster-making.** He is fire, flies as a bird, hides within rock, and produces an Amaru, a *caqui*, and a *hugi*. **[EA]**

**Dogs.** Dog consumption is not his primordial defining attribute: it is Pariacaca’s punitive sentence after defeat. Guaman Poma independently associates his early-colonial worship with dog sacrifice and dog blood. **[EA]**

### Limits and contested attributes

- **Volcano:** Dávila Brizeño and later interpreters read the battle as volcano versus snow peak. Salomon and Urioste note that Mullucocha does not appear geologically to be a caldera. “Volcano god” is therefore a plausible naturalistic reading, not the manuscript’s technical classification. **[LS]**
- **Drought/aridity:** inferable by opposition to Pariacaca’s rain and cold, but not explicitly formulated as a formal office in the principal text. **[LS]**
- **Underworld:** no secure early passage establishes Huallallo as ruler of a standardized *Uku Pacha*. **[MI/unsupported systematization]**
- **Moon, Venus, morning/evening star, or lightning:** proposed in twentieth-century scholarly or regional reconstructions. They are not defining attributes in the Huarochirí narrative. **[LS/RV]**
- **Colors:** yellow and red occur in the rain/hail of Pariacaca’s combat and in tropical birds associated with Huallallo’s era. No early source establishes a canonical personal color scheme.
- **Sacred number five:** five-day revival, five-day crop ripening, and a fivefold opponent are prominent. Yet the text does not explicitly assign “five” as Huallallo’s cult number.
- **Weapons/regalia:** no secure personal weapon is named. The golden staff belongs to Pariacaca.
- **Sex:** grammatical and narrative treatment is masculine, but *huaca* itself is not a gender class equivalent to “male god.”

### Iconography

No surviving pre-Hispanic representation is securely identified by inscription or archaeological context as Huallallo. The manuscript supplies vivid narrative forms—towering flame, bird, indwelling cliff—but no canonical portrait.

Acrylic paintings and internet illustrations are modern reception. Josué Sánchez’s 1984 painting *Huallallo Carhuincho*, used on the cover of *Ecos de Huarochirí*, is an acknowledged modern imaginative work, not ancient iconography. Likewise a 2021 Wikimedia photograph titled “Huallallo Carhuincho sobre el cielo de Choquequirao” is a modern naming of a landscape image, not documentary evidence of an ancient representation. [S8][S9]

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

No early source gives Huallallo named parents, birth, siblings, or securely identified children. Claims that Huaytapallana is his daughter, that an Amaru becomes his son-in-law, or that he fathers five descendants belong to recent regional tales and popular retellings unless a datable earlier witness is supplied.

Manañamca’s status is deliberately uncertain. The manuscript calls her a *supay* “with” Huallallo. This could mean companion, ally, sibling, spouse, or counterpart. A marginal note equates her with Chaupiñamca, but the editors treat this as a secondary interpretation within a complicated cluster of female *Namca* identities. She must not simply be declared his wife.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The reign of excessive vitality

**[EA]** Yanañamca and Tutañamca rule first; Huallallo defeats them. He then orders humans to have only two children, one of whom he consumes. Death lasts five days and crops require only five days to ripen. Population consequently becomes excessive, forcing people to survive miserably on narrow mountain plots.

The narrative does not portray Huallallo’s age as simple evil. It joins fecundity, revival, tropical richness, scarcity of habitable land, and compulsory sacrifice. Salomon and Urioste interpret the logic as an Andean meditation on why mortality is necessary: immortality plus reproduction produces catastrophic crowding. The interpretation is persuasive but remains modern analysis. [S2]

### The rescued child and elemental battle

**[EA]** As the fivefold Pariacaca prepares for combat, he meets a weeping man carrying a child, *mullu*, coca, and *ticti* as offerings to Huallallo. A marginal reading may identify the man as Quinti. Pariacaca orders him to return the child home, takes the other offerings, and commands him to return in five days as a witness.

Pariacaca attacks through rain from five directions. Huallallo rises as fire nearly reaching heaven. The struggle lasts from morning until sunset—not, in the critical Salomon–Urioste translation, three days and three nights. Llacsa Churapa dams the descending waters, producing Mullucocha; the lake nearly submerges Huallallo’s fire. Lightning prevents him from recovering and he retreats toward the Anti.

The widespread “three days and three nights” duration belongs to later summaries or other retellings and should not replace the manuscript’s “morning to sunset” formulation.

### The pursuit and petrified monsters

**[EA]** Chapter 16 expands what chapter 8 compresses. Huallallo flies in bird form to the Caquiyoca cliff and hides inside it. Pariacaca and his brothers blast the rock with lightning. Huallallo releases a two-headed Amaru; Pariacaca strikes its spine with a golden staff, freezing and petrifying it. Travelers chip stone from the visible formation as prophylactic medicine.

At Pumarauca Huallallo erects a *caqui* bird as a barrier. Pariacaca breaks its wing and turns it to stone. Huallallo, exhausted, flees to the Anti. Paria Carco becomes a snow-covered sentinel at the pass.

These transformations make the narrative a mapped itinerary: lake, cliffs, snake-like rock, bird-like formation, mountain, and pass are simultaneously terrain and surviving evidence of divine action.

### The *hugi* and ritual authority

**[EA]** Even after exile, Huallallo causes a mysterious *hugi* to appear to injure Pariacaca. Neither ordinary pursuit nor Pariacaca’s storm kills it. A man of the Checa Cacasica ayllu captures it, but a Quinti man falsely claims the achievement. Pariacaca eventually assigns ritual authority to the true captor’s line. Huallallo’s monster thus becomes part of a charter for Checa religious office and intergroup precedence.

### Sentence to dog eating

**[EA]** Pariacaca declares that Huallallo, formerly an eater of people, must eat dogs and be worshipped by the Huanca. The manuscript narrator says the Huanca sacrifice and eat dogs because their god does, explaining the insult “dog-eating Huanca.”

This is simultaneously myth, an ethnographic allegation, and political rhetoric. The Checa-centered manuscript projects a defeated enemy god onto an eastern rival people. Guaman Poma corroborates the Huanca–Huallallo–dog complex, but his own condemnation also belongs to colonial moral and ethnic polemic.

### Historical substrate: Yauyo, Yunca, and Huanca conflict

**[LS]** The manuscript casts Pariacaca’s people as highland invaders who replace or subordinate earlier *Yunca* inhabitants. The Huanca assignment suggests another frontier: Salomon proposes that narrators remembered or imagined an earlier Huanca penetration of the western valleys, reversed by Pariacaca’s people. The myth thereby legitimates land, water, pilgrimage routes, and social superiority.

Calling the conflict simply “Yauyos versus Huanca” is too neat. The text also opposes incoming highlanders to Yunca valley populations and then incorporates Yunca female and agricultural powers into the new order. Mythic geography may preserve several layers of migration, alliance, and competition rather than one datable war.

No archaeological horizon can presently be equated securely with Huallallo’s reign or Pariacaca’s conquest. Attempts to date the sequence to the end of the first millennium CE are scholarly reconstructions, not direct manuscript dates. [S10]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Mullucocha and Upper Pariacaca

The manuscript locates Huallallo’s dwelling and shrine at Upper Pariacaca, on the site later called Mullucocha, approximately 4,300 meters above sea level and about thirty kilometers east-northeast of Huarochirí. The name means “Lake of *mullu*,” referring to prized *Spondylus* shell. The lake is presented as a result of Pariacaca’s victory.

No excavated temple at Mullucocha has been demonstrated conclusively as “Huallallo’s temple.” The manuscript itself says the shrine’s earlier name was unknown. **[EA plus geographical identification]**

### Offerings

The child-bearer carries:

- a child intended as food;
- *mullu* or thorny-oyster shell;
- coca;
- *ticti*, a prepared offering placed in a drink.

**[EA]** This is the clearest ritual inventory tied to Huallallo in the manuscript.

Guaman Poma’s separate list gives dogs, coca, food, dog blood, and *mullu*. **[EA]** It presents dog offerings as surviving or remembered Huanca practice around 1615.

### Human sacrifice

The manuscript unequivocally says that Huallallo ate one of two children and shows a father transporting a child to him. It is therefore evidence that around 1600 narrators told and perhaps believed in a former child-sacrifice obligation.

It is **not**, by itself, archaeological proof of a recurring historical institution in which exactly one of every two Huanca or Yunca children was killed. Nor does it identify the rite as the imperial Inca *qhapaq hucha*. Equating the two is a modern reconstruction. No osteological assemblage securely tied by inscription or closed context to Huallallo has been established here.

### Dogs and feasting

Three distinct propositions must remain separate:

1. The Huarochirí story makes dog eating a sentence imposed after Huallallo’s defeat.
2. Guaman Poma describes named offerings of dogs and dog blood to Caruancho Uallullo.
3. Garcilaso describes a Huanca dog cult and feast without securely naming Huallallo in that passage.

Together these make some historical Huanca ritual use of dogs plausible. They do not prove every detail of later tales involving flaying warriors, drinking blood mixed with chicha, or converting enemies into dogs.

### Priesthood and festivals

The Huarochirí Manuscript extensively documents Pariacaca’s *huacsa* and *yanca* offices after Huallallo’s defeat. It does not preserve a comparably detailed named priesthood or annual festival calendar for Huallallo. The old cult appears chiefly as the superseded order against which Pariacaca’s institutions are defined.

Claims about a standardized Huallallo clergy, temple hierarchy, sacred day, or imperial festival cannot presently be established.

### Suppression

The manuscript was created within the documentary world of extirpation. Local shrines were destroyed, sacred objects confiscated or burned, and crosses installed. Tello and Miranda reported that two guardian huacas at Casta were destroyed during the first decade of the seventeenth century and crosses erected in their places. Whether either object was specifically Huallallo is less certain than their general use in Tello’s reconstruction of “Wallallo” religion. **[EA/F with identification disputed]** [S11]

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before ca. 1580: undocumented antiquity

Huallallo’s traditions plainly refer to a pre-Christian sacred geography and were already sufficiently established to enter late-sixteenth-century reports. No surviving named evidence dates them to a particular pre-Hispanic century. Proposed links to Chavín, Wari, or a first-millennium political horizon remain comparative hypotheses.

### Ca. 1581–1586: first documentary horizon

Albornoz and Dávila Briceño record Wallallo/Uallallo amid ecclesiastical cataloguing and regional administration. The name is already associated with the central Andes, but the surviving brief notices do not yield a full “biography.”

### Ca. 1598–1608: Huarochirí synthesis

The Quechua manuscript preserves Huallallo as the ruler of a displaced epoch and Pariacaca’s principal enemy. Its synthesis combines:

- landscape explanation;
- memory of ethnic conflict;
- charters for land and ritual office;
- polemic against the Huanca;
- Indigenous efforts to preserve traditions;
- information collected in a Christian anti-idolatry setting.

The manuscript itself says that uncertainty remained over who still worshipped Huallallo.

### Ca. 1613–1616: broader colonial repositioning

Santa Cruz Pachacuti incorporates Uallollo into universal histories of Tunupa and Inca conquest. Guaman Poma treats Caruancho Uallullo as an active or remembered Huanca huaca receiving dog offerings. These sources show the name beyond the manuscript’s single Checa narrative, but with substantially different stories.

### Seventeenth century: extirpation and displacement

Catholic campaigns dismantle shrines and suppress public offerings. Huacas are verbally recoded as idols and demons. Sacred places are not necessarily abandoned: crosses, saints, and Christian festivals may occupy or reorganize them. Direct continuity must be demonstrated locally rather than presumed universally.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: textual rediscovery and ethnographic reconstruction

The Madrid manuscript re-enters scholarship after centuries of obscurity. Julio C. Tello and Próspero Miranda’s 1923 “Wallallo” study combines early documents, archaeology, and ceremonies observed in the cis-Andean Lima highlands. It was foundational, but recent reassessment notes that Casta informants did not necessarily make Wallallo as central as Tello’s title and reconstruction implied. **[LS/F, contested]** [S11][S12]

### Later twentieth century: Huaytapallana identification

Scholars and regional intellectuals increasingly identify Huallallo with the Huaytapallana snow massif east of Huancayo. Cerrón-Palomino reports that local Quechua speakers know the massif as *Huallallu*. This accords with the eastward exile and rocky/snowy etymology but is not stated in the Huarochirí Manuscript, which says he fled toward the Anti rather than explicitly became Huaytapallana. **[F/LS]**

### Twenty-first century: living Mantaro practice

Fieldwork reported in 2024 documents Huallallo Carhuincho being named in *herranza* offerings for family prosperity and healing. One informant prayed to Huallallo, resident in Huaytapallana, for a disabled girl. The same mountain being could be called *apu* and **Tayta Shanti**; other ritual specialists described offerings to Pachamama or mountain *apus*. This is living, plural practice rather than a standardized reconstructionist church. **[F/SI]** [S6]

The identification with Saint James is situational and overlapping, not evidence that colonial Huallallo and Santiago were always “the same god.” Likewise the ethnography concerns the Mantaro region, not documented survival in Cusco, the Bolivian Altiplano, or northwestern Argentina. Broad claims of continuous Huallallo worship throughout those regions were not substantiated in the sources examined.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, art, and regional identity

Arguedas’s 1966 translation made the narrative widely accessible in Peru. Taylor and Salomon–Urioste later changed readings, restored linguistic detail, and emphasized the manuscript’s editorial layers.

Huallallo now appears in regional histories, school materials, murals, paintings, online mythology databases, videos, games, and fantasy discussions. These often consolidate him into a single “Inca god of fire, volcanoes, drought, fertility, the moon, and the underworld.” That portfolio is a modern composite assembled from early narrative, naturalistic interpretation, regional traditions, and invention.

### Modern legends

Stories about Huaytapallana as Huallallo’s daughter, her romance with an Amaru, five children, a cosmic association with the Moon, or a dog named Karwancho are current regional or internet narratives. They deserve preservation as **modern folklore**, but their antiquity has not been demonstrated by a dated colonial witness. **[F/MI]**

Likewise, modern accounts in which Huallallo creates dogs from defeated captains, teaches the flaying of enemy skins for drums, or institutes a detailed blood-and-chicha warrior ritual exceed the Huarochirí and Guaman Poma texts. Some elements resemble Garcilaso’s dog-head trumpets, but the fully integrated episode is a later synthesis.

### Occult and neopagan reception

A dedicated search found no securely documented role for Huallallo in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, historical grimoire traditions, or a large organized neopagan denomination. Contemporary personal invocation or internet “pantheon” lists may exist, but scale, lineage, and historical continuity cannot be established.

### Political use

The deity participates in regional Huanca identity, especially through Huaytapallana. Conversely, the Huarochirí account’s “dog-eating Huanca” theme can reproduce an old ethnic insult. A modern local publication argues that Huallallo was never a Huanca god and that the story was an anti-Huanca fabrication created by hostile Cuzco/colonial writers. This revision usefully identifies the partisan character of the sources but overreaches when it claims there were no pre-Hispanic dogs in the Mantaro valley; Andean dogs are archaeologically pre-Columbian. The responsible conclusion is narrower: the early textual record attests the accusation, while the extent and exact form of the historical cult remain contested. [S13]

No documented extremist appropriation was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Huallallo originally Yunca or Huanca?

The Huarochirí Manuscript makes his former highland domain “entirely Yunca,” yet after defeat assigns him to the Huanca. Salomon calls him “possibly Huanca.” Cerrón-Palomino calls him protector of the Yunca and associates the exiled form with Huaytapallana. These are not mutually exclusive if the story preserves successive political mappings, but the sources do not permit a single timeless ethnic identity.

The safest formulation is:

- Huallallo is an old ruler of Yunca-associated terrain in the Checa narrative.
- The same narrative polemically assigns his post-defeat worship to the Huanca.
- Guaman Poma independently names him in a Huanca ritual context.
- Modern Mantaro practice associates him with Huaytapallana.

### Does the battle encode Yauyo–Huanca war?

It plausibly encodes territorial and ethnic conflict. Salomon reads Pariacaca’s victory as a claim that his people reversed earlier Huanca intrusion and liberated Yunca communities. Other scholars emphasize highland immigrant conquest of valley Yunca populations and control of land and irrigation. No text supplies a calendar date or historical commander, so the myth cannot be reduced to one reconstructable battle.

### Fire god or volcano?

The fire form is textual fact. Volcano is a naturalistic interpretation. Mullucocha is not demonstrably a volcanic caldera, weakening literal geological readings. Fire can signify former warmth, destructive burning, ritual consumption, or a contrasting ecological order without identifying an actual eruption.

### Did human sacrifice occur?

The narrative attestation is secure; the historical practice in the exact narrated form is not independently verified. Calling it *qhapaq hucha* imports an imperial category the manuscript does not use. Conversely, dismissing it simply because the hostile narrator belonged to a rival tradition would discard evidence rather than evaluate it.

### Was Huallallo absorbed by Pachacamac?

Some structural interpretations suggest that functions of defeated terrestrial beings moved to Pachacamac or that a general succession of cults can be constructed. The Huarochirí Manuscript does not narrate Huallallo becoming Pachacamac or meeting him. This remains **[LS]**, not a secure syncretistic event.

### Huallallo and Huari/Wallallo

Tello and Miranda related *Wallallo* to widespread names such as Wari, Wali, and Walla and treated cis-Andean ceremonies as survivals of a broad cult. This was influential diffusionist scholarship. Similar-sounding divine names and comparable rituals do not by themselves prove one prehistoric pan-Andean deity. Recent work on Casta explicitly questions how central Wallallo was to the informants whose ceremonies Tello interpreted.

### Archaeological identification

No named icon has been verified. The Huariwillka sanctuary, Huaytapallana landscape, rock formations along the Pariacaca route, and Casta shrines are indispensable settings, but assigning every local sacred object to Huallallo risks circular reasoning.

### State of the evidence

The central manuscript survives in Madrid, while editions and translations differ in segmentation, names, and marginal readings. Particularly uncertain matters include:

- the original name of Huallallo’s Mullucocha shrine;
- the names and kinship of all five Pariacaca persons;
- whether the child bearer was Quinti;
- the exact species of *caqui*;
- the identity of *hugi*;
- Manañamca’s relationship to Huallallo;
- who still worshipped Huallallo around 1600;
- whether *Anti* means the eastern tropical lowlands specifically or warm lowlands more generally.

These are explicit lacunae, not problems to be solved through confident modern storytelling.

|---|---|
| **Pre-Hispanic, undated** | Traditions of a former huaca named Wallallo/Huallallo develop in the central Andes. No securely named material object fixes their beginning. |
| **Inca period, retrospectively narrated** | Santa Cruz Pachacuti later places Uallollo among huacas expelled by Tunupa and among seven great huacas confronted by Pachacuti Inca. **[EA source; retrospective myth-history]** |
| **ca. 1581–1585** | Albornoz’s anti-idolatry *Instrucción* records Uallallo among regional huacas. |

| **ca. 1598–1608** | Huarochirí Manuscript compiled: earliest full account of Huallallo’s former reign, child consumption, battle with Pariacaca, exile, and dog sentence. |
| **1608** | Ávila produces a Spanish *Tratado* from related investigatory material; his choice of wording informs interpretation of the two-child passage. |
| **1609–1611** | Intensified extirpation in Huarochirí; shrines and sacred objects destroyed or confiscated. |
