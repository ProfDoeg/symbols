# The Apus: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A collective and open-ended class of territorially rooted, nonhuman mountain persons—variously translated as “lords,” “mountain gods,” “mountain spirits,” “owners,” “ancestors,” or “guardian powers.” An *apu* is not simply a disembodied spirit inhabiting an inert mountain: in many Quechua-speaking traditions the visible mountain and the powerful person are aspects of one being. “The Apus” are not one god and never formed a closed, empire-wide pantheon.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The distinction between the institution and the word is essential. Archaeologically, offerings on Andean summits securely attest state-sponsored Inca relations with powerful mountains during the Late Horizon, especially c. 1400–1532 CE; the Ampato, Llullaillaco, El Plomo, Aconcagua, Quehuar, Misti, Pichu Pichu, and other high-altitude sanctuaries are the clearest evidence. The earliest surviving written descriptions date from the Spanish-conquest generation: Pedro de Cieza de León’s inquiries of the 1540s and *Crónica del Perú* (printed 1553) describe named mountains as *guacas*, temples, oracles, and recipients of sacrifice. The Quechua title *apu*, “lord, chief, superior,” is colonial-era lexically secure, including in Diego González Holguín’s 1608 dictionary, but early chroniclers did not consistently use *apu* as the generic technical name for every mountain divinity. Treating all pre-Hispanic sacred mountains as members of a uniform category called “the Apus” is therefore partly a modern scholarly and popular systematization.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The category

**[Secure colonial and ethnographic attestation]** In Southern Quechua, *apu* fundamentally means a lord, chief, powerful superior, or person of authority. As applied to mountains, it is an honorific and relational title: *Apu Ausangate* is “Lord Ausangate,” not a specimen of a zoological class called “an apu.” The plural is properly Quechua *apukuna*; Spanish and English commonly use *apus*. González Holguín’s *Vocabulario de la lengua general de todo el Perú* (Lima, 1608) documents the political and honorific semantic field of *apu* within colonial Cuzco Quechua. It does not establish that every sacred mountain in Tawantinsuyu was uniformly called an *apu*.

Important related terms are:

- **Apu / apukuna:** lord or lords; in southern Peruvian usage, powerful mountain persons.
- **Wak’a / huaca:** a much broader Quechua category embracing powerful beings, places, stones, springs, ancestors, images, temples, and other concentrations of sacred agency. Early colonial sources usually call named sacred mountains *guacas*.
- **Wamani:** in parts of central and south-central Peru, a mountain lord and, historically, also a province or territorial division. It is not everywhere a synonym for *apu*.
- **Achachila / achachilas:** Aymara “grandfather” or ancestral mountain powers. Equation with *apus* is a useful translation, not identity of vocabulary or theology.
- **Mallku:** Aymara title for a lord, chiefly authority, or powerful mountain; also associated with the condor. Its relationship to *achachila* varies locally.
- **Awki / awkillu:** elder, ancestor, or male mountain being in several regional usages.
- **Razu, rao, rit’i:** snow or snow peak. Pablo José de Arriaga noted these terms while listing snow ranges among the beings and places condemned as *huacas* in 1621.
- **Tayta:** “father,” attached to mountains and Christian sacred figures alike: *Tayta* may express kinship and respect without erasing the distinction between saint, Christ-image, and mountain.
- **Pachamama:** earth/time-place mother. She is regularly addressed with mountains in contemporary offerings but is not merely the female collective counterpart of the Apus.

**[Regional variation]** Apus may be male or female, although Spanish and tourist descriptions often default to masculine “mountain gods.” Particular mountains marry, own herds, father children, maintain servants, quarrel, punish, desire, and exchange wealth. Gender can differ between neighboring communities and may depend upon the mountain’s relationship to another peak.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** “Apu cult” is convenient shorthand for overlapping local systems. It must not obscure that Ausangate, Salkantay, Illimani, Pariacaca, Coropuna, and a community’s unnamed hill do not belong to one standardized liturgy or genealogy.

### Principal named mountains

- **Apu Awsanqati / Ausangate:** the great snow mountain southeast of Cusco, especially important in Quispicanchi and among pastoral communities around the Vilcanota range. Modern ethnography presents Ausangate as a high-ranking lord associated with water, weather, alpaca and llama fertility, wild animals, minerals, healing, and regional protection.
- **Apu Salkantay / Salcantay:** the great snow peak west of Cusco and south of Machu Picchu. It is a powerful regional mountain person, but claims that it occupied a single fixed place in an “official Inca pantheon” exceed the surviving evidence.
- **Apu Illimani:** the dominant mountain above La Paz. Aymara accounts more often employ *achachila* or *mallku* than the Quechua title *apu*. Modern pan-Andean usage nevertheless calls Illimani an apu.
- **Pariacaca / Paryaqaqa:** a fivefold storm-and-mountain divinity of Huarochirí. The Quechua *Huarochirí Manuscript*, written around 1608, preserves the richest surviving colonial narrative cycle concerning a named mountain deity.
- **Wallallu Qarwinchu / Huallallo Carhuincho:** volcanic or fiery mountain power and defeated rival of Pariacaca in the Huarochirí traditions.
- **Chaupiñamca:** female huaca central to Huarochirí ritual geography, often related to Pariacaca as sister.
- **Coropuna:** a major southern Peruvian snow mountain and oracle. Cieza reports it as a celebrated sanctuary visited by rulers.
- **Ampato, Llullaillaco, Quehuar, Misti, Pichu Pichu, Sara Sara, El Plomo, Aconcagua:** mountains with Late Horizon summit or high-altitude sanctuaries. Archaeology establishes ritual use; it does not by itself recover each mountain’s spoken name, gender, personality, or mythology.

### Etymological limits

**[Secure]** *Apu* means lord or superior authority.

**[Unestablished]** No evidence supports popular derivations of *apu* from an acronym, a lost global “sun language,” Sanskrit, Sumerian, or extraterrestrial terminology. Comparisons with unrelated words in other languages are modern inventions unless supported by historical linguistics.

**[Colonial Christian adaptation]** Quechua Christian vocabulary could apply lordship terms to the Christian God, Christ, saints, officials, and Indigenous powers. *Apunchik*—“our lord”—therefore does not prove either that the Christian God was merely renamed as a mountain or that all apus became forms of Christ. It records translation and contested semantic reuse.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaeological evidence before written testimony

**[Secure Late Horizon evidence]** The Inca built ritual installations on or near numerous summits and deposited textiles, ceramics, *Spondylus* shell, food, coca, featherwork, gold and silver miniatures, camelids, and—in exceptional *qhapaq hucha/capacocha* ceremonies—children. The survival of platforms, roads, structures, burials, and offerings proves that selected mountains were destinations and recipients within imperial ritual.

**[Interpretive limit]** A summit deposit does not prove that its recipients used the modern generic label *apu*, nor that the sacrifice was addressed only to the mountain. Chroniclers associate *capacocha* with the Sun, Viracocha, thunder, royal succession, political emergencies, and major huacas. A burial on a mountain materially joined imperial and local powers; its theology could be multiple.

Earlier Andean societies certainly sacralized mountains and water sources, and some pre-Inca sites were deliberately oriented toward peaks. Direct continuity from a particular Formative-period image to a named modern apu, however, is usually **[modern scholarly hypothesis]**, not a documented genealogy.

### Principal colonial witnesses

1. **Pedro de Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú*, Parts I–II; inquiries in the 1540s, publication beginning 1553.**  
   Cieza identifies Guanacaure, Vilcanota, Ancocagua, Coropuna, Pachacamac, and other sanctuaries as *guacas*, temples, and oracles. He says Vilcanota possessed priests, women, lands, herds, and almost annual *capacocha* offerings. Significantly, he rejects the Spanish claim that human victims were killed at every temple: in his account, human sacrifice was restricted to especially credited oracles, while lesser huacas received beads, feathers, and small offerings. This is early testimony but already filtered through Christian demonology and post-conquest informants.

2. **Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas*, c. 1551–1557.**  
   Betanzos describes Inca state ritual, sacrifices, sacred places, and royal relationships with regional powers. His knowledge of Quechua and access to Cuzco elites make him indispensable, but his account is not a transparent transcript of pre-conquest doctrine.

3. **Cristóbal de Molina “the Cuzqueño,” *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas*, c. 1573–1575.**  
   Molina supplies one of the fullest descriptions of the *capacocha*: children and offerings assembled at Cusco and dispatched to huacas throughout imperial space for the Inca’s health, peace, longevity, and political order.

4. **Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú y sus camayos y haciendas*, c. 1580s.**  
   This is an extirpator’s cataloguing instrument: named huacas, their attendants, properties, and ritual networks were recorded so that they could be dismantled. It demonstrates both the local specificity of sacred powers and colonial efforts to make them administratively legible.

5. **José de Acosta, *Historia natural y moral de las Indias*, 1590.**  
   Acosta systematized Indigenous religion for a European readership. Useful as colonial intellectual history, it is less direct than local testimony.

6. **The anonymous Quechua *Huarochirí Manuscript*, c. 1608, produced in the orbit of Francisco de Ávila.**  
   This is the outstanding Indigenous-language source. It calls powerful nonhumans *wak’as* rather than arranging them as a generic “Apu pantheon.” It records Pariacaca’s birth from five eggs, his combat with Wallallu Qarwinchu, Cuniraya’s pursuit of Cavillaca, Chaupiñamca’s rites, intercommunity rivalries, irrigation claims, transformations into stone, and the disruption caused by Christianity. The manuscript survives in Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional de España, MS 3169. Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste’s 1991 Quechua transcription and English translation remain fundamental.

7. **Diego González Holguín, *Vocabulario*, 1608.**  
   Essential for historical semantics, including *apu*, but a missionary dictionary shaped by colonial language planning.

8. **Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, c. 1615–1616.**  
   The autograph manuscript, now GKS 2232 4º in Copenhagen, depicts and denounces Indigenous “idols” while also documenting ritual calendars, sacrifices, huacas, and colonial abuses. His famous illustration of the Inca addressing assembled huacas represents sacred places as political interlocutors.

9. **Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú*, 1621.**  
   Arriaga explicitly says people worshipped high hills, mountains, great stones, and snow ranges, called them by individual names, and told stories that they had once been human. He says every ayllu knew its principal huaca and that huacas could be spouses or children of one another. His book is both evidence and a manual of repression: it recommends identifying, exposing, burning, or otherwise neutralizing cult objects and retraining their adherents.

10. **Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, completed 1653.**  
    Cobo preserves detailed accounts of Inca ceremonies, the Cusco ceque system, sacrifice, and state religion, while writing more than a century after the conquest and frequently synthesizing earlier authorities.

11. **Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales de los Incas*, 1609.**  
    Garcilaso’s elegant account shaped later images of Inca religion but tends to rationalize and hierarchize it around solar monotheistic themes. It cannot simply override earlier or more local testimony.

12. **Huarochirí and Cajatambo idolatry records, seventeenth century.**  
    These depositions preserve names, offerings, priestly offices, songs, ancestral narratives, and clandestine ceremonies. They were produced under coercive prosecution. Pierre Duviols’s editions of the Cajatambo trials and Frank Salomon’s work on central-Peruvian communities demonstrate both their exceptional richness and their forensic distortion.

### What the corpus does not supply

No surviving pre-conquest Andean “book of the Apus” exists. There is no canonical list, universal genealogy, standardized iconographic manual, or single creation narrative for the collective. Much evidence survives because clergy intended to destroy the practices it described.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Powers and obligations

**[Colonial and living regional attestation]** Mountain persons commonly govern or influence:

- rain, snow, hail, lightning, springs, irrigation, and drought;
- fertility of fields and camelid herds;
- ownership of wild vicuña, deer, minerals, and treasure;
- community boundaries, prosperity, and political legitimacy;
- health, illness, divination, and ritual knowledge;
- safe travel through high passes;
- punishment for disrespect, broken reciprocity, illicit entry, or neglected offerings.

Their agency is reciprocal rather than automatically benevolent. A mountain may feed and protect its people, but may also seize livestock, send hail, cause illness, lure a person into an interior realm, or demand costly service.

**[Regional ethnography]** Q’ero and Cusco-region accounts describe mountains as socially ranked persons. Greater snow peaks command lesser hills; each community may have its immediate guardian while recognizing distant regional lords. This resembles political hierarchy but is not a single fixed chain of command.

### Form

Apus have no universal anthropomorphic image. Their primary public body is the mountain itself.

Reported manifestations include:

- a wealthy hacendado, chief, soldier, priest, rider, white-clad gentleman, or Indigenous elder;
- a condor, puma, bull, stallion, or other powerful animal;
- thunder, cloud, wind, colored light, smoke, or a voice;
- a stone, summit, or miniature;
- a person encountered in dreams, coca divination, trance, or lightning initiation.

**[Colonial Christian and republican accretion]** European horses, military uniforms, hacienda wealth, saints, crosses, and church festivals entered mountain narratives after conquest. Their presence is evidence of historical transformation, not proof that the complete modern story is pre-Hispanic.

### Iconography and material recognition

There is no secure rule by which an isolated Inca anthropomorphic figurine can be identified as “an image of an apu.” Summit assemblages commonly include male and female metal or shell figurines dressed in miniature textiles, camelid figures, feather ornaments, and *Spondylus*. They may represent offering persons, attendants, ancestors, imperial ranks, or divine recipients; interpretation depends on context.

Guaman Poma’s drawing of huacas summoned before the Inca is a colonial visual systematization rather than a portrait gallery. Modern illustrations showing transparent giants rising from mountains, color-coded “Apu chakras,” or a standardized council of twelve mountain masters are **[modern inventions or esoteric schemata]** unless tied to a named community and informant.

### Sacred substances

Securely or repeatedly documented offerings include coca, maize beer (*aqha/chicha*), maize, llama fat, camelids, guinea pigs, textiles, feathers, shell, precious metals, aromatic plants, colored fibers, sweets, alcohol, and manufactured miniatures. Contents vary sharply by period, place, ritual specialist, and purpose.

No universal ancient correspondence system of one color, planet, weekday, crystal, tarot card, or zodiac sign for each apu is attested.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy as landscape history

Mountain kinship is local and plural. Arriaga reports that huacas were described as husbands, wives, and children of other huacas. The *Huarochirí Manuscript* supplies named divine families, but these belong to Huarochirí rather than to all Andes. Contemporary Cusco narratives similarly arrange peaks as parents, spouses, siblings, or rivals; neighboring narrators may disagree.

There is consequently no defensible universal statement such as “Inti fathered all Apus” or “Pachamama is the wife of every Apu.” Those formulations are **[modern systematization]**.

### Discrete documented interactions

Because “the Apus” form an unlimited regional class, no finite dossier can list every interaction ever recorded in every oral community. The following entries cover the named interactions in the principal early mountain corpus and the major relations required by the present brief. Mere coexistence in a ritual list is not counted as an episode.

- **Pariacaca — Wallallu Qarwinchu:** Pariacaca defeats the fiery, human-eating Huanca patron, drives him toward the eastern lowlands, and transforms the landscape through storm, flood, and cold. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, chapters 8–9, c. 1608. **[Early colonial attestation of a regional cycle]**
- **Pariacaca — Cuniraya Wiraqocha:** Cuniraya encounters or is positioned within the same Huarochirí sacred history; their cultic spheres and narrative roles overlap without a universal genealogy. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, c. 1608. **[Regional corpus; relationship incompletely harmonized]**
- **Pariacaca — Chaupiñamca:** Chaupiñamca is treated as Pariacaca’s sister in the Huarochirí tradition; their festival and territorial cults complement one another. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, c. 1608. **[Regional canonical relationship]**
- **Pariacaca — Macahuisa:** Macahuisa is a son or emanated martial agent of Pariacaca who assists the Inca by defeating enemies with rain, lightning, and flood. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, chapter 23, c. 1608. **[Regional account incorporating Inca political memory]**
- **Pariacaca — Huayllama:** A local huaca or community power implicated in the expansion of Pariacaca’s cult and ritual order. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, c. 1608. **[Regional; details vary by translation and chapter division]**
- **Cuniraya Wiraqocha — Cavillaca:** Cuniraya covertly impregnates Cavillaca by placing semen in a lucuma fruit; after the child identifies him as father, she flees and becomes stone at the sea with the child. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, chapter 2, c. 1608. **[Regional early-colonial attestation; sexual deception stated without harmonization]**
- **Cuniraya Wiraqocha — Urpayhuachac:** Cuniraya, seeking Cavillaca, encounters Urpayhuachac’s coastal domain and retaliates against or disrupts her fish-related order. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, c. 1608. **[Regional variant]**
- **Cuniraya Wiraqocha — animals encountered on the road:** He blesses helpful animals and curses unhelpful ones, accounting for their habitats and relations with humans. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, chapter 2, c. 1608. The animals are powerful beings but not consistently named gods. **[Etiological regional myth]**
- **Pariacaca — the Inca:** After an Inca ruler fails to honor him adequately, Pariacaca or his son Macahuisa demonstrates overwhelming weather power; a negotiated relationship follows. *Huarochirí Manuscript*, c. 1608. **[Regional memory of incorporation into Tawantinsuyu]**
- **Named huacas — Topa Inca Yupanqui:** Guaman Poma represents the ruler convening and interrogating the huacas. *Nueva corónica*, c. 1615. **[Early-colonial political-religious representation]**
- **Ausangate — Qullqipunku/Colquepunku:** In living Qoyllur Rit’i geography, the mountains frame a shared sacred field involving glacier, pilgrimage, livestock fertility, and the Lord of Qoyllur Rit’i. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century ethnography and UNESCO documentation. **[Living regional syncretism]**
- **Ausangate — the Lord of Qoyllur Rit’i:** Pilgrims relate the Christian image and sanctuary to the powerful mountain landscape rather than simply declaring the two identical. Modern festival documentation. **[Colonial-to-modern syncretistic relationship]**
- **Apus — Pachamama:** They are jointly addressed in many *despachos*, but their precise kinship varies. Twentieth-century and contemporary ethnography. **[Living regional practice]**
- **Apus — Inti:** Inca state ceremonies could offer to both solar and mountain powers. No secure primary source makes all apus children or servants of Inti. **[Joint imperial cult; genealogy unestablished]**
- **Apus — Illapa:** Mountains, thunder, lightning, hail, and rain overlap functionally, and summit sacrifices could address both. A universal identity between every apu and Illapa is unattested. **[Functional association; modern over-equation rejected]**
- **Illimani — Pachamama/achachilas:** Bolivian rites jointly honor earth and ancestral mountains. Contemporary yatiri testimony describes apus as protective ancestors. **[Living Aymara-Quechua syncretic vocabulary]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Pariacaca and Wallallu Qarwinchu

**[Regional early-colonial attestation]** The Huarochirí narrative remembers an earlier age dominated by Wallallu Qarwinchu, associated with heat and the Huanca region. Pariacaca comes into being from five eggs on the mountain Condorcoto and manifests multiply. He learns of Wallallu’s abuses—including the demanded consumption or sacrifice of humans in the manuscript’s hostile account—and attacks.

The contest is cosmological and territorial. Pariacaca sends rain, snow, and flood against a fiery opponent. The struggle changes ecological zones and validates a new ritual order. Wallallu is expelled toward Antisuyu; Pariacaca becomes the dominant power of the Huarochirí highlands.

This should not be reduced to “good weather god defeats evil volcano.” Scholars have interpreted it as compressed memory of ethnic conflict, irrigation politics, ecological contrast, and Inca-era reorganization. Those readings are **[modern scholarly reconstructions]**; the text itself presents a war among huacas.

### Cuniraya and Cavillaca

Cuniraya appears as a poor or repellent-looking wanderer while possessing creator-like power. At an assembly of huacas, he impregnates the beautiful Cavillaca without consent by placing his semen in a fruit she eats. When their child later crawls to Cuniraya and reveals his paternity, Cavillaca refuses him and flees toward the Pacific. Cuniraya casts off his disguise and pursues her, questioning animals along the route. Helpful animals receive blessings; obstructive ones receive curses. Cavillaca reaches the sea and becomes stone with her child.

**[Regional early-colonial attestation]** This is simultaneously a divine pursuit, a geography of roads and animal habitats, and an explanation for coastal sacred rocks. Later summaries often romanticize it as unrequited love; that sanitizes the manuscript’s reproductive deception.

### Macahuisa and the Inca

An Inca ruler summons sacred powers for military assistance. Macahuisa, associated with Pariacaca, appears unimpressively but destroys the enemy through catastrophic rain, thunder, or flood. The episode establishes that imperial power depends upon negotiated access to local huacas.

**[Regional political narrative]** It can be read as Huarochirí’s claim that its mountain power was indispensable to the empire. It is not independent proof that the episode occurred as narrated.

### Mountains as transformed ancestors

Arriaga reports “a thousand fables” in which named mountains or stones had formerly been people and underwent metamorphosis. The *Huarochirí Manuscript* repeatedly ends divine movements in lithic permanence.

**[Secure documentation of a widespread narrative form; individual stories regional]** Landscape was not merely scenery commemorating myth: a stone or mountain could be the enduring body of the transformed person.

### Ausangate cycles

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century oral research records Ausangate as a wealthy lord, herd owner, water source, ancestor, and distributor of livestock prosperity. Stories link it with neighboring peaks, pastoral routes, treasures, and Qoyllur Rit’i.

**[Folklore and living religion]** These accounts are neither fossilized Inca scripture nor modern fiction. Their presently documented forms include colonial animals, Catholic figures, markets, hacienda relationships, and modern political conditions.

### Salkantay and Illimani

Both mountains possess extensive living story complexes, but no single early colonial narrative cycle survives comparable to Pariacaca’s. Claims that an exact modern Salkantay or Illimani tale is “an Inca myth” require a traceable publication or oral-history record.

**[Absence finding]** Their sacred status is well documented; a complete pre-conquest biography is not.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Sacred landscape rather than temple-exclusive cult

The mountain itself is the principal sacred body. Cult places may include a summit, pass, spring, cave, rock, platform, roadside cairn, household altar, corral, field boundary, or sightline. Cieza’s “temples” at important huacas show that some powers also possessed buildings, lands, herds, priests, and attendant women.

Brian Bauer’s work on the Cusco sacred landscape and R. Tom Zuidema’s reconstruction of the ceque system show that Inca ritual joined shrines, kin groups, calendrical duties, water, and imperial space. The ceques should not be reimagined as invisible global “energy lines”; that language is **[modern esoteric invention]**.

### Priests and ritual specialists

Colonial sources mention *camayoc* attendants and priests attached to particular huacas. Extirpation records reveal specialists who guarded images, transmitted narratives, divined, made offerings, and organized dances.

Modern regional titles include:

- **paqo:** ritual practitioner in parts of Cusco;
- **pampa misayuq:** bearer or specialist of a “lower/plain mesa” in some ranked systems;
- **alto misayuq:** higher-ranking ritual specialist credited with directly convening powerful mountains;
- **yachaq:** knowledgeable person;
- **yatiri:** Aymara ritual specialist, especially in Bolivia.

Jorge Flores Ochoa reports that powerful *alto misayuq* could maintain personal relationships with numerous apus, summon them, converse with them, and conclude ceremonies with offerings. These titles and grades vary locally; tourist literature falsely standardizes them into a universal Andean priesthood.

### Despacho, pago, mesa, haywarisqa

A *despacho* is an arranged offering bundle. Other names include *pago*, *pagapu*, *entrega*, *mesa*, *misa*, and *haywarisqa*. The officiant constructs a materially ordered gift—often including coca, fat, grains, sweets, fibers, incense, alcohol, shell, or miniatures—addresses intended recipients, folds it, and burns, buries, or otherwise delivers it.

**[Living regional practice]** “Payment” is an imperfect translation. The rite enacts feeding, invitation, gratitude, petition, diagnosis, reciprocity, and repair. It may address Pachamama, one or more apus, ancestors, saints, or other powers.

**[Modern commodification]** Short “authentic Inca despacho ceremonies” sold to tourists may draw on genuine regional practice but are adapted to market schedules, client expectations, and New Age vocabularies. Their commercial form should not automatically be projected backward.

### Coca divination

Coca leaves may be selected, breathed upon, offered, and read for messages concerning weather, illness, travel, herds, relationships, or an apu’s disposition. Colonial extirpators documented coca offerings; contemporary practitioners continue them. The procedure is not uniform.

### Qoyllur Rit’i

The pilgrimage to the sanctuary of the Lord of Qoyllur Rit’i, in the Sinakara valley near Qullqipunku and within the greater Ausangate sacred landscape, combines Catholic devotion with relationships to pre-Hispanic or non-Christian nature powers. UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2011.

Pilgrim “nations,” the Brotherhood, dancers, musicians, and *pablitos/pabluchas* organize and police the event. Catholic narratives concern the apparition and image of Christ; mountain-centered interpretations concern glacier, fertility, livestock, water, and regional powers.

**[Syncretistic, colonial-to-modern tradition]** It is misleading either to call the pilgrimage secretly and purely pagan or to erase its mountain relations as decorative folklore. The currently documented institution is both Catholic and Andean, with participants assigning different emphases.

Earlier pilgrims cut glacier ice and carried it home as potent water. Restrictions introduced in response to glacier retreat changed this practice. Claims of an unchanged “thousand-year-old pilgrimage” are not securely documented; the modern sanctuary’s history is colonial and republican even where it incorporates older sacred geography.

### Qhapaq hucha and human sacrifice

**[Secure textual and archaeological evidence]** The Inca state sacrificed children and young people in exceptional ceremonies. Molina, Cobo, Betanzos, Cieza, and other chroniclers describe selection, assembly at Cusco, imperial procession, distribution toward major huacas, and death by methods including burial, exposure, strangulation, or blows. Archaeology confirms sacrifice and clarifies individual cases.

The practice had several reported purposes:

- royal accession or death;
- military or political crisis;
- drought, volcanic activity, or other disaster;
- health and longevity of the Inca;
- fertility, abundance, and imperial peace;
- establishing relations among Cusco, provincial elites, local communities, and huacas.

Cieza expressly denies that humans were sacrificed at every shrine.

#### Ampato

The teenage girl popularly called **Juanita** or the **Lady of Ampato** was recovered in September 1995 by Johan Reinhard and Miguel Zárate after volcanic activity exposed the summit area. Bioarchaeology indicates that she died from a severe blow to the head. Other child sacrifices were recovered at Ampato and nearby southern Peruvian peaks.

Calling her “the bride of the Apu” may reflect one interpretation of capacocha symbolism, but it is not a securely recovered personal biography. Her modern nickname is not her Indigenous name.

#### Llullaillaco

In 1999 an archaeological expedition recovered three children—the Maiden, the Boy, and the Lightning Girl—from a ceremonial complex just below Llullaillaco’s summit in present-day Salta Province, Argentina. Rich textiles, figurines, coca, food, ceramics, and other offerings accompanied them. Scientific analysis indicates prolonged preparation and consumption of coca and alcohol, especially by the older girl.

The Museo de Arqueología de Alta Montaña in Salta conserves the children and assemblage. Exhibition of human remains is contested by Indigenous representatives and bioethical critics; museum conservation does not resolve questions of consent, ancestry, and custodianship.

**[Interpretive caution]** “Messengers to the gods” is supported by ethnohistorical interpretation, but archaeology alone cannot recover the children’s own beliefs. Political readings emphasize that capacocha materially anchored Inca authority in newly incorporated territories; religious and political functions are not mutually exclusive.

### Argentine Salta and Jujuy

The southern Andes were not marginal to Inca mountain religion. Summit sites in Salta and Jujuy, including Llullaillaco and Quehuar and finds associated with Chuscha and other peaks, document imperial activity. Contemporary Puna communities maintain offerings to Pachamama, mountains, springs, and ancestors within Catholic and civic life.

Terminology varies. Speakers may use *cerro*, *Pachamama*, *apacheta*, *achachila*, or local names rather than *apu*. Treating every Argentine hill rite as a transplant of a standardized Cusco “Apu religion” erases local histories.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before c. 1400: deep background

**[Archaeologically supported, terminology unestablished]** Andean peoples had long organized settlements, water systems, burials, and ceremonies in relation to mountains. Evidence supports deep mountain sacrality, but not a single unchanged “cult of the Apus” stretching from Chavín to the present.

### c. 1400–1532: Inca imperial articulation

The Inca incorporated local huacas into an imperial network through offerings, pilgrimage, shrine personnel, ceque organization, state consultation, and capacocha. Incorporation did not necessarily abolish local identities. The Huarochirí account of Pariacaca and the Inca remembers negotiation, coercion, and mutual dependency.

High-altitude sanctuaries across Peru, Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina materially extended Cusco’s reach. Local sacred mountains thereby became nodes of imperial geography without becoming interchangeable.

### 1530s–1570s: conquest and first documentation

Spanish clergy and chroniclers translated huacas as idols, gods, demons, or oracles. Temples were looted or converted; ritual estates and priesthoods lost institutional support. Yet Cieza, Betanzos, Molina, and others recorded traditions from people who remembered Inca rule.

The evidence is double-edged: it is exceptionally early but produced within conquest, forced conversion, epidemic mortality, and Christian polemic.

### 1570s–1600s: systematic discovery and suppression

Toledo-era reforms, reductions, parish reorganization, and campaigns against huacas sought to detach communities from sacred landscapes and resettle them in administratively controlled towns. Albornoz’s *Instrucción* exemplifies the cataloguing of huacas, ministers, and property for suppression.

### c. 1608–1720s: extirpation and clandestine adaptation

The Huarochirí manuscript emerged within Ávila’s campaign. Arriaga’s 1621 manual declared high mountains and snow ranges objects of illicit worship and prescribed their eradication from Indigenous hearts when the mountains themselves could not be removed.

Cajatambo prosecutions record confiscation and destruction of portable huacas, interrogation, denunciation, punishment of ritual specialists, and clandestine ceremonies held during Catholic festivals. Salomon notes campaigns from approximately 1608 through the 1670s and again in the 1720s. Testimony from San Pedro de Hacas describes night rites, coca offerings, drums, songs, recitation of huaca and ancestor deeds, and competitions maintained beneath an outward Catholic calendar.

This was not simple “survival unchanged.” Communities concealed, reinterpreted, recombined, and sometimes abandoned practices under coercion.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Documentation becomes thinner and more regionally uneven. Mountains persisted in oral tradition, herding rites, agricultural offerings, healing, pilgrimage, and Catholic festival geographies. Republican land tenure, haciendas, mining, and national borders reshaped relations with them.

### Twentieth century: ethnographic recovery and transformation

Anthropologists documented living mountain-person traditions in Cusco, Ayacucho, central Peru, the Bolivian altiplano, and northwest Argentina. Ausangate, Salkantay, Illimani, and community hills appear as guardians, owners, ancestors, and partners in reciprocity.

At the same time, scholarship sometimes built a generalized “Andean cosmology” by combining non-equivalent local data. The best work preserves who said what, where, and when.

### Late twentieth century to present

Indigenous cultural movements, urban migration, heritage programs, tourism, environmental struggle, and transnational spirituality have increased the public visibility of the Apus. Contemporary offerings are made by rural herders, farmers, miners, city residents, political officials, tour operators, and international seekers, but these actors do not necessarily share one theology.

Climate change gives mountain relations a new historical setting. Glacier retreat at Qoyllur Rit’i and around Ausangate and Illimani affects water, pilgrimage, and perceptions of the mountains’ vitality. This is not merely symbolic: shrinking ice alters the material body through which many communities encounter a powerful being.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, folklore, and national heritage

The Apus have become emblems of Andean identity in schools, museums, literature, tourism, and regional politics. Ausangate and Illimani function simultaneously as living powers, geographic landmarks, ecological resources, and national or departmental icons.

Museums presenting capacocha increasingly describe reciprocity and sacred landscape rather than the older sensational label “mummy.” Nevertheless, heritage institutions may still detach remains and offerings from descendant authority.

### Mystical tourism and neo-Incaism

From the late twentieth century, Cusco and the Sacred Valley became centers of international “Andean mysticism.” Commercial offerings include despacho ceremonies, initiations, “Apu energy” journeys, and purported Q’ero lineages.

Macarena Gómez-Barris identifies New Age tourism, asymmetrical cultural exchange, and commodification in the Sacred Valley. Related scholarship describes “neo-Incaism,” energetic places, fast ritual formats, and global networks as modern conditions shaping what visitors encounter.

The correct evidentiary conclusion is not that all tourist-facing paqos are fraudulent. Rather:

- many practices use genuine Andean forms;
- particular performances may be shortened, standardized, translated, or newly assembled;
- “shaman” is often an outsider’s umbrella term, not the practitioner’s local title;
- claims of an unbroken secret order preserving a fixed Inca initiation require evidence;
- the popular **Munay-Ki “nine rites” system** is a modern transnational construction, not an attested Inca priestly curriculum.

### Occult and neopagan material

**[Absence finding]** The Apus do not have a substantial documented place in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, classical ceremonial magic, or early European grimoires. Websites placing apus alongside angels, demons, elementals, tarot correspondences, or generic “earth spirits” represent late twentieth- or twenty-first-century comparative occultism.

Modern pagans may respectfully honor a local mountain as an apu, but this is a new transregional religious reception unless embedded in a documented Andean community tradition. There is no ancient canonical “summoning sigil,” planetary seal, demonological rank, or universal invocation for the Apus.

### Literature, art, and entertainment

José María Arguedas’s fiction and translations were decisive in presenting animated Andean landscapes and huaca traditions to modern readers. His Spanish translation *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí* helped move the manuscript beyond specialist philology.

Popular games, fantasy art, travel media, and online mythology sites increasingly turn “Apu” into a species of elemental or a singular god. That simplification is **[modern invention]**. The historically responsible model is a network of individually named, place-bound persons.

### Political and environmental uses

Mountains can symbolize Indigenous territorial claims, anti-extractive politics, regional identity, and ecological guardianship. Invoking an apu in protests against mining may continue local obligation while also translating it into environmental and legal language. Neither dimension cancels the other.

No well-documented modern extremist movement appears to have appropriated the Apus on a scale comparable to European pagan symbols. Romantic nationalist claims of a racially pure, uniform “Inca religion of the Apus” are modern ideological constructions.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### God, spirit, place, or person?

Older scholarship often separated material mountain from immaterial “spirit.” More recent ontological approaches describe an apu as a nonhuman person whose mountain body is not merely a container. Q’ero-focused research characterizes apus as guardians, masters of wild animals and minerals, healers, and patrons of ritual specialists.

The debate partly concerns translation. “God” stresses power and worship; “spirit” risks dematerializing the mountain; “owner” captures mastery; “ancestor” fits some Aymara and local accounts; “person” emphasizes social relations. None is universally sufficient.

### Pre-Inca continuity versus Inca construction

One position emphasizes the antiquity of mountain worship. Another stresses Inca imperial organization and the difficulty of projecting modern southern Quechua terminology backward. The evidence supports both deep regional mountain sacrality and major Inca transformation, but not timeless uniformity.

### Imperial domination versus reciprocity

Capacocha has been interpreted as devotion, political integration, elite exchange, territorial incorporation, hostage-like extraction, and imperial violence. Archaeology demonstrates careful preparation and lavish offerings; it does not make child death nonviolent. Conversely, describing the ceremony only as terror discards Indigenous concepts of postmortem agency and sacred reciprocity reported in the sources. Both the religious and coercive-political dimensions belong in the record.

### Chroniclers as evidence

Rostworowski, Zuidema, Duviols, MacCormack, Silverblatt, Urton, Bauer, Salomon, and others have shown that colonial sources cannot be read as neutral ethnography. Chroniclers selected, translated, moralized, and systematized. Extirpation records are especially rich precisely because they were coercive.

Sabine MacCormack examined the Christian and classical concepts through which Spaniards interpreted Andean beings. Irene Silverblatt foregrounded gender, colonial rule, and transformations of power. Pierre Duviols reconstructed repression and edited idolatry trials. María Rostworowski emphasized regional cults and political history. Zuidema analyzed ceques, kinship, calendar, and spatial organization. Gary Urton explored cosmology, social organization, and khipu-related knowledge. Brian Bauer integrated archaeology and sacred landscape. Frank Salomon demonstrated how Indigenous categories, substances, transformations, and communal memory survive in Quechua texts and archives without forming a simple Western “pantheon.”

### Iconography debate

The absence of labels makes it hazardous to call a prehistoric image “an apu.” Orientation toward a mountain, summit deposition, and later oral evidence can strengthen an interpretation, but resemblance alone cannot establish identity.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

The following circulate popularly but lack adequate evidence:

- all apus belong to one ranked imperial council;
- exactly twelve principal apus governed Tawantinsuyu;
- every apu is male and married to Pachamama;
- every Inca child sacrifice was a “bride of the mountain”;
- Qoyllur Rit’i has continued in an unchanged form for thousands of years;
- the ceque system was a global crystal-energy grid;
- modern Munay-Ki initiations are a verbatim Inca transmission;
- apus correspond to planets, chakras, archangels, or tarot trumps;
- Salkantay, Ausangate, and Illimani possess one universally agreed genealogy.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1400 CE:** Mountains, water sources, caves, and high places hold ritual importance across multiple Andean societies. **[Archaeological background; “apu” terminology unproven]**
- **c. 1400–1532:** Inca state expands and incorporates major huacas through pilgrimage, consultation, offerings, shrine estates, and capacocha. Summit sanctuaries proliferate across the central and southern Andes. **[Secure archaeological and later textual evidence]**
- **c. 1440–1532:** Principal period to which known Ampato, Llullaillaco, El Plomo, Aconcagua, and related high-altitude capacocha complexes are assigned. **[Secure Late Horizon context; exact years usually uncertain]**
- **1532–1530s:** Spanish invasion breaks imperial institutions; shrines are looted, lands reassigned, and Christian evangelization begins. **[Secure historical context]**
- **1540s:** Cieza de León collects information concerning huacas, oracles, temples, and sacrifice.
- **1553:** First part of Cieza’s *Crónica del Perú* printed.
- **c. 1551–1557:** Betanzos composes *Suma y narración de los Incas*.
- **c. 1573–1575:** Molina writes *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas*, including capacocha.
- **1570s–1580s:** Toledo-era reductions and systematic investigation of Indigenous religion intensify.
- **c. 1580s:** Albornoz prepares his *Instrucción* for identifying huacas, their attendants, and properties.
- **1590:** Acosta publishes *Historia natural y moral de las Indias*.
- **c. 1608:** The Quechua *Huarochirí Manuscript* is compiled in the context of Francisco de Ávila’s campaign.
- **1608:** González Holguín publishes his Quechua dictionary, securely documenting the lordship field of *apu*.
- **1609:** Garcilaso publishes Part I of the *Comentarios reales*.
- **c. 1615–1616:** Guaman Poma completes the *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*.
- **1621:** Arriaga publishes *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú*, explicitly describing named mountains and snow ranges as worshipped huacas.
- **1608–1670s:** Major central-Peruvian extirpation campaigns generate Huarochirí and Cajatambo records, confiscations, trials, and punishments.
- **1653:** Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*.
- **1720s:** Renewed campaigns in parts of central Peru show that huaca-centered practices and memories persisted.
- **Nineteenth century:** Mountain rites continue within local Catholic, agrarian, pastoral, and mining worlds, though documentary coverage is uneven.
- **Early–mid twentieth century:** Ethnographers increasingly record *apus*, *wamanis*, *achachilas*, and living offering traditions.
- **1954:** The sacrificed child of El Plomo is recovered in Chile, transforming modern study of high-altitude Inca ritual.
- **1966:** Arguedas and Duviols publish an influential Spanish edition and translation of the Huarochirí traditions.
- **1970s–1990s:** Rostworowski, Zuidema, Duviols, MacCormack, Silverblatt, Urton, Bauer, Salomon, and others reshape study of Andean religion around regional history, colonial mediation, sacred landscape, and Indigenous-language evidence.
- **1991:** Salomon and Urioste publish *The Huarochirí Manuscript: A Testament of Ancient and Colonial Andean Religion*.
- **September 1995:** Reinhard and Zárate recover the Lady of Ampato/Juanita.
- **1999:** Three sacrificed children and their assemblage are excavated at Llullaillaco.
- **2004:** The Museo de Arqueología de Alta Montaña opens in Salta to conserve and interpret the Llullaillaco finds.
- **2011:** UNESCO inscribes the Qoyllur Rit’i pilgrimage on its Representative List.
- **Twenty-first century:** Glacier retreat changes Qoyllur Rit’i practice and the physical landscapes of Ausangate and Illimani; heritage, Indigenous rights, tourism, and New Age commercialization become central to public representations of the Apus.
- **Present:** Mountain-directed offerings and relationships remain living practices in Cusco, the southern Peruvian Andes, the Bolivian altiplano, and parts of northwestern Argentina. They coexist with Catholicism, urban life, heritage institutions, ecological activism, and commercial spirituality.

The record supports a robust conclusion but not a single creed: the Apus are historically changing, locally named mountain persons whose relations with communities survived imperial appropriation, Christian repression, republican transformation, and modern commercialization. Their unity lies in a family resemblance of lordship, territoriality, reciprocity, and embodied landscape—not in membership in a closed pantheon.
