# Mama Quilla: Research Dossier

**Class:** female celestial divinity/huaca of the Moon in Inca state religion; more cautiously, the personified Moon rather than a universally uniform pan-Andean “goddess.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** Pedro de Cieza de León’s *Crónica del Perú*, printed in Seville in **1553**, reports that Andean peoples worshipped the Sun and Moon. The earliest presently verified description of the Cuzco lunar huaca as a specifically female, institutionally tended image is Cristóbal de Molina’s *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los incas*, composed approximately **1573–1575**: he calls it **Passamama/Pacsamama**, not Mama Quilla. The explicit name **Mama Quilla**, “Mother Moon,” is securely documented in Inca Garcilaso de la Vega’s *Comentarios reales* of **1609**. Thus the cult is documented earlier than the now-standard divine name.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

**[Secure early-colonial attestation]** Mama Quilla is the female personification and state-cult image of the Moon documented for late Inca Cuzco by sixteenth- and seventeenth-century writers. The most substantial accounts are postconquest: Cieza de León (1553), Molina (c. 1575), Garcilaso (1609), Guaman Poma (c. 1615), and Bernabé Cobo (completed 1653). They describe a female lunar power associated with calendrical time, nighttime light, the Inca royal women, and a silver image in the Coricancha. Garcilaso identifies her as the Sun’s sister-wife and the dynastic mother of the Incas; Molina describes a female lunar huaca called Pacsamama carried by women. These reports overlap but are not identical and should not be collapsed into one canonical theology.[S1][S2][S3]

The historical entity is therefore best reconstructed as three overlapping phenomena:

1. the astronomical Moon, observed for phases, months, and eclipses;
2. a female Cuzco huaca represented by a silver or silver-colored image;
3. a dynastic being retrospectively systematized as Inti’s sister-wife and mother of the Incas.

### Names

**Mama Killa** is the normalized modern Quechua spelling:

- **mama**: mother; also a respectful title for female beings, places, substances, and generative powers;
- **killa**: Moon and lunar month.

The literal sense is **“Mother Moon.”** Spanish colonial spellings include **Mama Quilla, Mamaquilla, Quillamama**, and forms with *c/qu* and doubled *ll*. No indigenous alphabet existed before conquest; “Quilla” is therefore not an original Inca orthography but a colonial Roman-script representation.

**[Secure lexical attestation]** Domingo de Santo Tomás’s 1560 *Lexicón* defines *quilla* as “mes, docena parte de un año, luna, planeta”—month, the twelfth part of a year, Moon, planet. The entry demonstrates the Moon/month semantic field but does not by itself attest a goddess called Mama Quilla.[S4]

**Pacsamama/Passamama.** Molina calls the female lunar huaca **Passamama**. Colonial Quechua dictionaries clarify the name: González Holguín (1608) defines *pacsa* through moonlight, brightness, and a moonlit night, and *Pacsa mama* as a radiant mother or bright/full Mother Moon. The name may consequently be translated approximately “Radiant Mother” or “Mother Moon in her light.” It is not securely established that Pacsamama and Mama Quilla were two separately worshipped beings; the sources more naturally suggest variant names or registers for the Cuzco lunar huaca.[S4]

**Pacsa Mama.** Markham later reported *Pacsa Mama* as the Moon “as a deity” and *Quilla* as the Moon giving nocturnal light. That neat functional division is a nineteenth-century scholarly arrangement and should not be projected backward without qualification.[S5]

**Coya/Quya and Coya Raymi.** *Qoya* designated the Inca’s principal wife or queen and, more broadly, a high-ranking woman. The month-name **Coya Raymi** appears in colonial calendrical lists, but Molina principally describes it as the month of the **Situa**, a great purification ceremony. Calling Coya Raymi simply “Mama Quilla’s festival” is a common modern simplification; lunar, feminine, royal, agricultural, and purification meanings intersect, but the surviving early descriptions do not present an uncomplicated festival biography of one goddess.[S6][S7]

### Etymological dispute

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The ordinary Quechua meaning of *killa* is secure. Its deeper origin is not. Comparative work notes related lunar words in Quechua, Aymara, and Mapudungun and has considered borrowing through ancient contact, possibly involving Puquina. This does not establish that a complete cult of “Mama Quilla” was borrowed from a single ancestral source. Lexical resemblance, divine identity, and shared myth are separate propositions.[S8]

### Classifications and purported epithets

Securely documented classifications include:

- **Moon/lunar huaca** — Molina and Cobo;
- **female celestial divinity** — Molina describes a female figure tended and carried by women;
- **wife and sister of the Sun** — Garcilaso;
- **mother of the Incas and their lineage** — Garcilaso;
- **Mama Quilla, Mother Moon** — Garcilaso.

Modern descriptions such as “goddess of marriage,” “protector of childbirth,” “defender of all women,” “goddess of menstrual cycles,” and “third power after Inti and Illapa” circulate widely. They are plausible extrapolations from lunar time, royal female patronage, and later comparative interpretations, but the early passages located for this dossier do not securely attest that exact portfolio. They should be labeled **modern synthesis**, not quoted as a single ancient catechism.

There is no surviving Inca source equating her with Greek Selene, Artemis, or Roman Luna/Diana. Such equations are modern comparative shorthand, not documented acts of Inca syncretism.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Evidentiary limitations

No securely deciphered preconquest Inca inscription names Mama Quilla. Inca record keeping included khipus, architecture, images, performance, and oral specialists, but no surviving preconquest narrative text identifies her. Nearly all verbal evidence passed through colonial collection after the destruction and dispersal of imperial institutions.

Consequently, “earliest attestation” must be divided into:

- evidence for lunar worship;
- evidence for a female Cuzco lunar cult-image;
- evidence for the proper title Mama Quilla;
- later systematic genealogy.

### Primary corpus

#### Pedro de Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú* (1553)

**[Secure early-colonial attestation]** Cieza reports worship of the Sun and Moon alongside stones, trees, sea, earth, and other sacred entities. His work is the earliest printed evidence located here for lunar worship among Andean peoples under or recently subject to Inca rule. It does not, in the relevant passage, name Mama Quilla, specify her genealogy, or establish a homogeneous empire-wide cult.[S9]

#### Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas* (composed 1551)

Betanzos preserves Cuzco traditions obtained close to the imperial court, but the surviving manuscript history is complicated and the lunar material is much thinner than his accounts of Viracocha, the Sun, rulers, and conquest. References in later scholarship to an *aposento de Mamaquilla* require verification against a critical edition. Betanzos cannot safely be used to construct a detailed Mama Quilla mythology.

#### Domingo de Santo Tomás, *Lexicón* (1560)

**[Secure lexical attestation]** The dictionary fixes the semantic equivalence of *quilla* with Moon and month. It is linguistic evidence, not proof of a divine biography.[S4]

#### Cristóbal de Molina, *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los incas* (c. 1573–1575)

**[Secure early-colonial attestation]** Molina writes that a female figure, “the huaca of the Moon,” was called **Passamama**. Women had charge of it and carried it on their shoulders when it was brought from its separate room in the House of the Sun, located where the Santo Domingo lookout stood. Molina explains the female attendants by reporting that the Moon was considered a woman, as her figure showed.[S4]

Molina’s creation narrative says that the Creator made or ordered the Sun, Moon, and stars to go from Titicaca and rise into heaven. This makes the Moon subordinate to the Creator within that particular collected cosmogony. It does not call the Moon Inti’s wife or make her Manco Cápac’s mother.[S10]

His month-by-month ritual account is indispensable for Coya Raymi/Situa, offerings, the ceremonial movement of huacas, royal women, female cult specialists, and capacocha. It also shows why later summaries that isolate a tidy “Moon festival” can be misleading.[S6][S7]

#### Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú* (c. 1580s)

Albornoz catalogs huacas and gives procedures for finding their ritual personnel, property, and images during campaigns against indigenous religion. It is central evidence for systematic suppression, although it contributes less to Mama Quilla’s individual characterization than Molina or Cobo. Its categories must be read as those of an extirpator attempting to expose and destroy cults.

#### José de Acosta, *Historia natural y moral de las Indias* (1590)

Acosta places Sun, Moon, stars, lightning, earth, sea, and local powers within an apologetic hierarchy of “idolatry.” He is useful for the missionary classification of celestial worship but not for an independent, detailed Mama Quilla mythology.

#### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales de los Incas* (1609)

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Garcilaso gives the most influential named presentation:

- Mama Quilla means Mother Moon.
- She was the Sun’s wife and sister.
- She was mother of the Incas and their lineage.
- Her hall stood nearest the Sun’s principal chapel in the Coricancha.
- The hall, including its doorways, was plated with silver.
- Her image was a woman’s face on a silver plate.
- Dead royal women were placed beside her in rank order.
- People entered to visit and commend themselves to her.
- Garcilaso says she did not receive sacrifice in the same way as the Sun.[S2]

This account helped create the modern canonical figure. Garcilaso, born in 1539 to an Inca noblewoman and Spanish father, had exceptional access to Cuzco family memory, but he wrote in Spain roughly seventy years after the conquest and organized Inca religion into an elegant dynastic system. His testimony is invaluable and interested, not a transparent transcript of preconquest doctrine.

Garcilaso also records a lunar-eclipse tradition: the Moon became ill, might die and fall, and could bring universal destruction. People sounded instruments, made dogs howl, and ordered children to cry out to “Mama Quilla” so she would not die. A separate tale explained lunar markings as a fox that attempted to seize the beautiful Moon and became attached to her.[S11][S12]

#### Guaman Poma de Ayala, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (c. 1615)

**[Regional/indigenous colonial testimony]** Guaman Poma’s illustrated manuscript gives month names and ritual activities, including **Coya Raymi Quilla** and other month names ending in *quilla*. His calendar is crucial because *quilla* means both Moon and month. His drawings and captions document an indigenous Christian intellectual’s reconstruction and critique of Inca time, not an untouched preconquest almanac.

Guaman Poma distinguishes ritual roles of the Inca and Coya and supplies major evidence for gendered calendrical organization. Zuidema cautioned that the seven surviving colonial month lists do not agree: several sources call December *Raymi Quilla*, whereas Polo, Molina, and others call it *Capac Raymi*. A single standardized “Inca calendar” is therefore a scholarly reconstruction.[S13]

#### Francisco de Ávila’s Huarochirí materials (c. 1598–1608)

The anonymous Quechua *Huarochirí Manuscript*, preserved in Madrid as MS 3169, was assembled around 1600 in the setting of Ávila’s investigations. It is the only long colonial account of Andean sacred traditions written in an indigenous language near the beginning of Spanish rule. Its major beings are regional huacas such as Cuniraya Viracocha, Pariacaca, Chaupi Ñamca, Huallallo Carhuincho, and Pachacamac.[S14][S15]

**The “Quilla of Huarochirí” finding:** the manuscript does not present an imperial goddess Mama Quilla as a major narrative actor comparable to Pariacaca or Chaupi Ñamca. Words and place-names involving *quilla* need not name her: *killa* can mean Moon/month and occurs in toponyms. Claims that every Huarochirí lunar reference is an appearance of the Cuzco goddess exceed the evidence. The manuscript instead shows that local sacred histories retained their own huacas under Inca and Spanish rule. This absence is historically important.

#### Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621)

Arriaga supplies procedural evidence for colonial investigations: questioning communities, identifying ministers and offerings, confiscating or burning sacred objects, and replacing indigenous ceremonies with Christian instruction. His book documents the machinery that made open cults such as Mama Quilla’s institutionally impossible. It is hostile testimony and must not be treated as neutral ethnography.

#### Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (completed 1653)

**[Later colonial synthesis preserving earlier information]** Cobo says the Moon was recognized as divine for her beauty and benefits; imagined as a woman; represented by a female statue in the Sun temple; served by female priestly attendants; and carried by those women when brought out.[S1][S16]

Cobo’s eclipse account differs from Garcilaso’s illness narrative. He reports that a **lion/puma or serpent attacked the Moon to tear her apart**. People shouted, beat dogs to make them howl, sounded horns and drums, brandished weapons, and shot arrows or darts toward the Moon to frighten the aggressor. Cobo adds that the custom survived among baptized people in his time, although he interpreted it as habit rather than continuing belief.[S1]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Secure domains

**Moon and nocturnal light.** This is the irreducible core.

**Calendrical time.** *Killa* means both Moon and month. Lunar phases helped articulate months and ritual timing, but scholars disagree about how lunar counts were reconciled with the solar/agricultural year. Colonial month lists vary, and no complete uncontested Inca calendar survives.[S13][S17]

**Royal femininity and the Coya.** Garcilaso locates deceased queens around the lunar image and makes the Moon mother of the Inca dynasty. The Coya’s celestial analogy to the Moon complemented the Inca ruler’s solar descent. Modern shorthand calling Mama Quilla simply “patroness of the Coya” captures this relationship but turns a dynastic analogy and mortuary arrangement into a formal title not quoted in the earliest sources.

**Women’s ritual service.** Molina and Cobo explicitly say women tended and carried the lunar image. The evidence supports a gendered cultic office. It does not establish that every aclla was a “priestess of Mama Quilla”; acllas served diverse state, textile, ceremonial, diplomatic, and sacrificial functions.

### Iconography

**[Secure textual description]**

- Molina: a female figure;
- Garcilaso: a woman’s face on a silver plate;
- Cobo: a female statue/image kept in the Sun temple;
- Garcilaso: the lunar chamber coated with silver plates;
- royal women’s preserved bodies arranged beside the image.

No securely identified surviving preconquest statue can be proven to be the Coricancha’s Mama Quilla. Popular modern illustrations of a young woman with crescent crown are reconstructions, usually influenced by European lunar iconography.

The crescent Moon is not securely established as her exclusive Inca emblem. A full silver face or disc is better grounded in Garcilaso. Claims that she wore a particular crown, blue mantle, or fixed palette are modern artistic conventions.

### Silver

Silver’s whiteness provides the explicit architectural logic in Garcilaso: it denoted the Moon, in contrast to the Sun’s gold. The popular statement that silver was literally “the Moon’s tears” is widespread but was not located in the principal early passages examined here. It should be labeled **late popular attribution or modern synthesis** unless a precise primary citation is supplied.

### Eclipses and lunar markings

Two early-colonial versions must remain separate:

- **Garcilaso (1609):** the Moon became ill, might die, and could fall; noise and crying—especially dogs and children calling Mama Quilla—were intended to awaken or move her. A fox explained the dark markings.
- **Cobo (1653):** a puma/lion or serpent attacked the Moon; noise, dogs, weapons, and projectiles frightened the attacker.

The familiar statement “a puma attacked Mama Quilla during eclipses” is therefore only the Cobo version, with a serpent as an alternative. It is not the only attested Inca eclipse explanation.[S1][S11]

### Modernly assigned attributes requiring caution

No sufficiently early source located here securely assigns her:

- a special color beyond silver/white;
- a sacred number;
- an exclusive weekday;
- ocean tides;
- willow trees;
- universal governance of fertility;
- an individual mythology of menstrual creation;
- the title “mother of the firmament.”

These are either naturalistic extrapolations, regional/New Age additions, or unsourced encyclopedia repetition.

## 4. Genealogy and Divine Relations

### Competing genealogies

**Garcilaso’s dynastic system (1609; later canonical elaboration):**

- Inti/Sun: brother and husband;
- Incas collectively: children/descendants;
- Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo are often made her direct children in modern retellings, but Garcilaso’s different origin accounts and solar commission require careful distinction from a simple nuclear family.

**Molina’s creation account (c. 1575; earlier attestation):**

- Viracocha/Creator creates or orders the Sun, Moon, and stars and sends them from Titicaca into the sky.
- No marriage of Sun and Moon is required.
- No direct maternity of Manco Cápac by the Moon is stated in the cited passage.

**Modern standardized genealogy:**

- Viracocha and Mama Cocha are parents of Inti and Mama Quilla;
- Inti and Mama Quilla are parents of Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo;
- Pachacamac is sometimes added as another son.

This elegant family tree is **modern systematization** assembled from sources that do not all give the same relationships. The Getty iconographic record, for example, anomalously lists Mama Quilla as consort of Viracocha, illustrating the instability of modern cataloging.[S18]

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Inti — sister-wife and paired celestial power.** Garcilaso, *Comentarios reales*, 1609. **Tier: later canonical elaboration based on Cuzco dynastic tradition.**
- **Inti — architectural adjacency in the Coricancha:** her silver hall stood nearest his principal golden chapel. Garcilaso, 1609. **Tier: later colonial architectural testimony.**
- **Viracocha/Pachayachachi — Creator orders the Moon, Sun, and stars to rise from Titicaca and take their celestial places.** Molina, c. 1575. **Tier: securely attested early-colonial cosmogony.**
- **The stars — jointly created or installed in the sky with Sun and Moon.** Molina, c. 1575. These are a collective rather than individually named beings. **Tier: securely attested early-colonial cosmogony.**
- **Manco Cápac — dynastic descendant/child of the celestial pair in Garcilasian and later retellings.** Garcilaso, 1609, with subsequent elaboration. **Tier: later canonical elaboration; direct biological maternity varies by retelling.**
- **Mama Ocllo — dynastic descendant/child and female founder in later genealogical synthesis.** Garcilaso, 1609, with subsequent elaboration. **Tier: later canonical elaboration; direct biological maternity should not be treated as universally attested.**
- **Pachacamac — no securely documented personal encounter with Mama Quilla was found.** Claims that he is her son belong to modern composite genealogies unless tied to a precise early source. **Tier: unattested/modern systematization.**
- **Illapa — no narrative interaction found.** Their co-presence in state cult or modern ranked lists is not an interaction.
- **Chaska/Chasca Coyllur — no narrative interaction found.**
- **Pariacaca — no narrative interaction found.**
- **Cuniraya Viracocha — no narrative interaction found.**
- **Chaupi Ñamca — no narrative interaction found.**
- **Pachamama — no early text found making them sisters, rivals, or a fixed female pair.**
- **Mama Cocha — no securely attested early interaction or maternity relation found.**
- **Fox — attempts to reach/seize the Moon and becomes attached, explaining lunar markings.** Garcilaso, 1609. The fox is an animal actor, not securely a named god. **Tier: early-modern recorded folklore.**
- **Puma/lion or serpent — attacks the Moon during eclipse.** Cobo, 1653. The aggressor is unnamed and not certainly a deity. **Tier: early-modern recorded eclipse tradition.**

The limited list is itself a finding: Mama Quilla has a substantial cultic and cosmological record but a remarkably small surviving narrative network.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The celestial installation

**[Secure early-colonial version: Molina, c. 1575]** In the primordial darkness, the Creator fashions peoples and establishes order. He makes or commands the Sun, Moon, and stars to go to Titicaca and rise from there into the heavens. The Moon’s role is collective: she is one of the celestial lights placed by the Creator.[S10]

**[Variant: Sarmiento de Gamboa, 1572]** Sarmiento reports that the Moon was initially brighter than the Sun, provoking jealousy; traditions surrounding the celestial lights were embedded in a broader Viracocha creation cycle. This is a variant cosmogony, not evidence for a single orthodox creation text.[S19]

### Sister-wife of the Sun and mother of the Incas

**[Later canonical elaboration: Garcilaso, 1609]** The Moon is Inti’s sister and wife and the mother of the Incas. This maps celestial duality onto royal sibling marriage and succession. The female bodies placed beside her image made the Coricancha lunar room a dynastic mortuary counterpart to the Sun’s association with male rulers.[S2]

No early narrative describes a courtship, wedding, sexual union, quarrel, or reconciliation between Inti and Mama Quilla. “Marriage” is a status relation, not a surviving story cycle.

### Lunar eclipse

**Garcilaso version:** eclipse is illness or dying. The Moon may fall and crush the world. Instruments, cries, dogs, men, women, and children calling “Mama Quilla” rouse or persuade her.[S11]

**Cobo version:** eclipse is predation. A lion/puma or serpent attacks the Moon. The community stages a defensive mobilization with shouting, dogs, horns, drums, arrows, darts, and spears.[S1]

The two versions may preserve different informants, regions, ritual explanations, or levels of interpretation. They should not be harmonized into “the puma makes Mama Quilla sick.”

### The fox in the Moon

**[Early-modern recorded folklore: Garcilaso, 1609]** A fox falls in love with or desires the beautiful Moon, climbs into heaven to seize her, and becomes attached to her, creating the visible dark patches. Garcilaso compares and disparages the tale through classical European categories. The comparison to Diana is his editorial frame, not an Inca identification.[S11]

### The Huarochirí question

**[Negative finding]** No major Mama Quilla cycle occurs in the Huarochirí Manuscript. The local manuscript’s sacred history centers on regional huacas and their rivalries, migrations, marriages, victories, irrigation works, and cult communities. Treating those traditions as subordinate chapters in a Cuzco “Mama Quilla mythology” would erase precisely the regional variation the manuscript preserves.[S14]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The Coricancha lunar chamber

**[Textually attested; archaeology does not preserve the furnishings]** Garcilaso locates a Moon hall next to the Sun’s principal chapel. Silver sheets covered its surfaces and doorways; the image was a woman’s face on silver; royal women’s bodies stood beside it. Molina independently places the Moon’s separate room in the House of the Sun and says women carried its image.[S2][S4]

After the conquest, the Coricancha became part of the Dominican complex of Santo Domingo. Masonry survives, but the silver cladding and lunar image do not. The precise identification and original functions of individual surviving rooms remain partly dependent on colonial descriptions.

### Cult personnel

Molina and Cobo explicitly assign the image to women who served and carried it. These women may be described cautiously as female cult attendants or priestly functionaries. The sources do not provide:

- their indigenous office-name;
- exact recruitment rules;
- whether they were permanently attached only to the Moon;
- a complete hierarchy comparable to the reconstructed solar priesthood.

Garcilaso’s placement of coyas beside the image joins lunar worship to royal female ancestry. This is the strongest basis for calling Mama Quilla the Coya’s patroness.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Garcilaso says sacrifice was not offered to her as it was to the Sun. This need not mean she received no ritual attention: people entered to commend themselves to her, and the image participated in a staffed institution.

Molina’s calendar documents animal offerings, food, chicha, textiles, and capacocha within the broader state system. Children were killed in capacocha rites for major state huacas and political occasions. No examined early source, however, isolates a specific recurring program of human sacrifice exclusively to Mama Quilla. Claims that her women’s festival centered on child sacrifice are therefore **unsupported conflations** with general Inca state ritual.

No credible evidence was found for “temple prostitution” in her cult.

### Coya Raymi and Situa

Molina calls August/Coya Raymi the month of the Situa in his calendrical arrangement. Images of huacas came to Cuzco, purification was conducted, diseases and evils were driven out, and ritual circuits joined the capital to the four quarters. Other sources place named months differently because the calendar was not transcribed consistently.[S6][S13]

**Evidentiary conclusion:** Coya Raymi had strong royal-female and lunar semantics, but the modern formula “September festival dedicated solely to Mama Quilla and celebrated only by women” is an overstatement. The best early evidence describes a much larger state purification complex involving the Inca, Coya, huacas, lineages, ritual runners, sacrifices, and the ordering of Cuzco.

### Eclipses as public ritual

Cobo and Garcilaso both attest communal noise, dogs, and collective intervention. Their independent but variant descriptions establish that eclipse response was ritual action, not merely explanatory myth. Cobo’s claim that it continued after Christianization is direct evidence of early colonial survival, filtered through his insistence that it had become habit.[S1]

### Island of the Moon/Koati

On Koati in Lake Titicaca, the Inca constructed the complex now called **Iñak Uyu**, conventionally identified as an acllawasi or “House/Palace of the Chosen Women.” The islands formed a major state pilgrimage landscape focused especially on the Island of the Sun. Association of Koati with a Moon cult is plausible and traditional, but calling Iñak Uyu a securely demonstrated “Temple of Mama Quilla founded by Túpac Inca Yupanqui” goes beyond what architecture alone can prove. Archaeological study establishes Inca construction, residence, ceremony, and pilgrimage; divine dedication depends substantially on later textual and toponymic interpretation.[S20][S21]

### Pachacamac’s Mamacona

The so-called Mamacona/Acllawasi at Pachacamac is sometimes labeled a Temple of the Moon. Archaeology identifies an Inca-period compound and hydraulic installations associated with selected women. The equation “acllawasi = Mama Quilla temple” is not automatic; acllas served state cult and production more broadly.[S22]

### Killarumiyoq/Quillarumiyoc

The site near Ancahuasi contains Inca masonry, terraces, waterworks, and a prominent carved rock. Its Quechua name means approximately “the one/place with a Moon stone.” Peruvian tourism authorities identify it as a major Mama Quilla sanctuary.[S23]

That dedication remains **contested interpretation**. A local academic thesis argues that the celebrated semicircular carving could instead form the eye of a larger puma-shaped rock. The monument is genuine; “lunar calendar,” “largest temple of Mama Quilla,” and exclusive dedication are interpretations, not facts fixed by an inscription.[S24]

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the Inca state

Lunar observation and worship long predate the Incas across the Andes, but similarities do not prove that earlier Moche, Chimú, Tiwanaku, or other lunar beings were all Mama Quilla under different names. On the north coast the Chimú Moon, often called **Si**, could outrank the Sun. This is a distinct regional cult subsequently encountered by Inca expansion, not an early attestation of the Quechua-named goddess.[S25]

### Imperial formation, approximately fifteenth to early sixteenth century

**[Historical reconstruction]** The Inca state incorporated celestial beings, local huacas, royal ancestors, pilgrimage places, and conquered cults into a political ritual network. The Cuzco Moon’s female image, silver chamber, attendants, and relationship to royal women fit this system.

The state did not simply abolish every local god. It relocated images, demanded participation and offerings, sponsored shrines, and inserted Cuzco’s dynastic beings into regional landscapes. Mama Quilla’s imperial reach should therefore be imagined as uneven and negotiated, not as a universally accepted creed.

### Conquest and plunder, 1530s onward

Spanish seizure of Cuzco dismantled the Coricancha’s treasury and state personnel. Precious-metal cult images and wall coverings were destroyed, melted, hidden, or dispersed. The absence of the silver lunar image is thus historically explicable; no secure object can now be matched to Garcilaso’s description.

### Missionization and extirpation, sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

Missionaries categorized the Moon as a created body wrongly worshipped as divine. Albornoz, Ávila, Arriaga, and other investigators sought huacas, ministers, ritual objects, offerings, and community property. Sacred objects were confiscated or burned; ritual specialists were interrogated and punished; churches, crosses, confraternities, and Christian calendars reorganized sacred space.

The Huarochirí Manuscript exists partly because such suppression generated intensive documentation. It is both a repository of indigenous traditions and an artifact of their investigation.

Cobo’s eclipse passage shows partial survival: shouting at eclipses continued among baptized populations. His claim that belief had vanished while “custom” remained cannot be independently verified and reflects missionary expectations.[S1]

### Colonial accommodation and transformation

Lunar reckoning survived in agriculture, herding, healing, weather prediction, pregnancy discourse, and feast timing. “Moon” remained linguistically ordinary as *killa* in Quechua and *phaxsi/paxi* in Aymara. Such practices do not necessarily constitute continuous worship of an individual goddess called Mama Quilla.

Female, Marian, lunar, mountain, earth, and water symbolism sometimes overlapped under colonial Christianity, but no simple equation **Mama Quilla = Virgin Mary** is historically universal. Specific local cases must be documented rather than inferred from shared femininity or crescent imagery.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Nationalist historiography and tourism consolidated a portable Inca pantheon resembling a classical pantheon: Viracocha as creator, Inti as Sun god, Mama Quilla as Moon goddess, Illapa as thunder god, and Pachamama as Earth goddess. Garcilaso’s elegant relationships became the chief template.

The Huarochirí Manuscript’s publication history changed scholarship:

- Hermann Trimborn’s German edition, 1939;
- José María Arguedas’s Quechua-Spanish *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí*, 1966;
- Trimborn and Antje Kelm, 1967;
- Gérald Taylor’s critical Quechua-Spanish edition, 1987 and later revision;
- Frank Salomon and George Urioste’s Quechua-English edition, 1991.[S14][S15]

These editions made visible a regional sacred world far less centered on Mama Quilla than popular Inca pantheon charts imply.

### Present practice

In Cusco and elsewhere, *Killa/Mama Killa* appears in cultural festivals, educational performances, tourism, healing discourse, indigenous activism, art, and private devotion. Ecuadorian **Kulla/Killa Raymi** revivals often connect the September equinox with women, fertility, planting, Pachamama, and the Moon. Their contemporary form is a revival/reconstruction shaped by schools, municipalities, cultural organizations, and pan-Andean identity; it should not be represented as an unchanged Inca ceremony.

In the Bolivian altiplano, ordinary lunar practice is more often framed through Aymara *phaxsi* and local agricultural or ritual knowledge than through exclusive personal worship of a goddess named Mama Quilla. In northwest Argentina, archaeological and ethnographic work documents lunar observation within Inca and later landscapes, but continuous named devotion to Mama Quilla has not been demonstrated.[S26]

No reliable census establishes the scale of present-day worship. Online testimony demonstrates individual neopagan interest, not an institutionally continuous priesthood.[S27]

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Esotericism and occultism

No evidence was found that Renaissance Hermetists, the Theosophical Society, the Golden Dawn, or early modern European grimoires maintained a tradition devoted to Mama Quilla. Claims placing her in an ancient transcontinental occult lineage are **modern invention**.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century New Age literature often recasts her as:

- a universal Divine Feminine;
- guardian of menstruation and fertility;
- ruler of intuition, dreams, and emotional healing;
- an Andean equivalent of Selene, Isis, Diana, or Hecate;
- a patron for “moon water,” crystals, silver magic, or Wiccan lunar rites.

These practices may be meaningful modern religions, but they are not documented continuations of Coricancha ritual.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist practice

Online practitioners sometimes say they “work with” Mama Quilla using candles, moonlight, silver objects, menstrual rites, or generalized Wiccan ritual. The vocabulary and format are commonly Euro-American neopagan. Surviving Inca sources do not provide an initiation liturgy or private devotional manual that could support exact reconstruction.[S27]

### Heritage revival

Modern Killa/Kulla Raymi celebrations are better understood as indigenous and civic cultural revitalization than as straightforward neopaganism. They combine reconstructed Inca calendrics, contemporary gender symbolism, agricultural thanksgiving, dance, tourism, environmentalism, and local political identity.

### Art, commerce, and popular media

“Mama Quilla” names artworks, music, jewelry, retreats, tourism businesses, and wellness products. Danielle Hatch’s contemporary artwork *Mama Killa*, for example, explicitly uses the goddess to explore gendered power through modern industrial materials. Ben Holt’s music describes a blend of ancient and modern ceremonial energy. Both are transparent modern receptions rather than ancient survivals.[S28][S29]

Popular digital art usually shows a beautiful young woman crowned by a crescent Moon. That image has materially shaped modern recognition but is not derived from a securely identified Inca portrait.

### Political and nationalist appropriation

Mama Quilla can appear as an emblem of Andean indigeneity, decolonization, feminine sovereignty, or resistance to Catholic patriarchy. These are modern political uses of an ancient figure. No significant extremist appropriation specific to Mama Quilla was documented in the sources examined.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Colonial-source problems

The principal debate is not whether Inca people revered the Moon—they did—but how much of the familiar goddess profile comes from late systematization.

- **Cieza** securely establishes lunar worship but supplies little biography.
- **Molina** supplies a female image, women attendants, a separate Coricancha room, and the name Pacsamama.
- **Garcilaso** supplies the influential name Mama Quilla, sister-wife relation, dynastic maternity, silver room, royal female mummies, eclipse illness, and fox story.
- **Cobo** supplies beauty/benefit theology, female priestly attendance, and the puma-or-serpent eclipse attack.

The resulting modern dossier is composite. Garcilaso and Cobo cannot automatically be used to fill every silence in Molina.

### Calendar debate

Tom Zuidema treated Cuzco’s calendar, ceque system, kinship, social ranking, and ritual offices as an integrated structure. Gary Urton and others developed astronomical and calendrical interpretations; Brian Bauer’s archaeological work mapped the Cuzco sacred landscape. Yet colonial month lists conflict, and lunar-solar adjustment remains debated. A month name ending in *quilla* is not automatically the proper name of a goddess.[S13][S17][S30]

### Gender and power

Irene Silverblatt’s work emphasized the gendered political order of Inca and colonial society, including the solar Inca/lunar Coya analogy and the changing position of women under conquest. Later scholarship complicates any claim that gender complementarity amounted to social equality. Mama Quilla’s high celestial rank coexisted with an imperial system that appropriated women’s labor, arranged elite marriages, and selected acllas for state purposes.

### Regional religion versus “the Inca pantheon”

María Rostworowski, Pierre Duviols, Sabine MacCormack, Frank Salomon, Karen Spalding, and Gérald Taylor helped redirect inquiry from static god lists toward regional cults, colonial translation, political incorporation, and extirpation. The Huarochirí evidence demonstrates that Inca imperial religion was layered over powerful local sacred histories rather than replacing them with a single theology.

### Archaeological identification

Architecture rarely names its divine recipient. Coricancha identifications rest on close colonial testimony, whereas claims for Killarumiyoq or every acllawasi as a Mama Quilla sanctuary are less secure. Toponyms can preserve memory but also shift. Archaeological function, later local tradition, tourism designation, and primary textual attestation must be kept separate.

### Discredited or weakly supported claims still circulating

- Mama Quilla was universally the “third-ranked Inca deity”: **modern ranking, not securely attested doctrine**.
- She was the fixed goddess of marriage and all women: **modern portfolio expansion**.
- Every September Coya Raymi was exclusively her women-only festival: **oversimplification**.
- She personally caused ocean tides and crop growth: **naturalistic extrapolation without an identified early citation**.
- Her tears created silver: **popular attribution not established here in primary testimony**.
- Killarumiyoq’s carving is unquestionably a lunar calendar: **contested**.
- She was daughter of Viracocha and Mama Cocha, wife of Inti, and mother of Manco Cápac, Mama Ocllo, and Pachacamac in one universal genealogy: **composite modern family tree**.
- Huarochirí preserves a major narrative cycle about her: **not demonstrated**.
- Modern Wiccan rites reproduce ancient Inca worship: **modern invention**.
- Mama Quilla was anciently identical with Selene, Diana, or Isis: **comparative analogy, not historical syncretism**.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Before c. 1400 | Diverse Andean societies observed and revered the Moon; none can automatically be called Mama Quilla. |
| Fifteenth–early sixteenth century | Inca imperial consolidation creates the probable historical setting for the Cuzco lunar chamber, state attendants, and lunar-Coya dynastic analogy. |
| 1530s | Spanish conquest of Cuzco dismantles Coricancha institutions and disperses precious-metal cult furnishings. |
| 1551 | Betanzos composes *Suma y narración de los Incas* from Cuzco testimony; lunar evidence is limited and manuscript transmission complicated. |
| 1553 | Cieza de León publishes the earliest located printed statement that Andean peoples worshipped Sun and Moon. |
| 1560 | Santo Tomás publishes *quilla* as Moon/month in the first printed Quechua lexicon. |
| c. 1572 | Sarmiento records a Viracocha creation tradition involving Sun and Moon, including a brightness rivalry. |
| c. 1573–1575 | Molina records Pacsamama, the female lunar huaca, her women attendants, separate room, and participation in Cuzco’s sacred order. |
| 1560s–1580s | Albornoz and other investigators combat huaca cults and Taqui Onqoy; suppression increasingly destroys or drives rituals underground. |
| c. 1598–1608 | Huarochirí traditions are written in Quechua during Ávila’s investigations; Mama Quilla is not a principal narrative actor. |
| 1608 | González Holguín’s dictionary records *pacsa* terms connected with moonlight and Pacsa Mama. |
| 1609 | Garcilaso publishes the defining Mama Quilla account: name, silver room, sister-wife of Inti, mother of the Incas, royal women, eclipse illness, and fox myth. |
| c. 1615 | Guaman Poma completes his illustrated calendrical and political account, including Coya Raymi Quilla. |
| 1621 | Arriaga publishes the manual of extirpation, documenting systematic investigation and destruction of indigenous cult practice. |
| 1653 | Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, preserving the puma/lion-or-serpent eclipse account and women’s service of the lunar image. |
| Colonial period | Open state cult ends; lunar timing and eclipse customs survive in local and domestic forms amid Christianization. |
| 1939 | Trimborn publishes the first major modern edition/translation of the Huarochirí Manuscript. |
| 1966 | Arguedas publishes *Dioses y hombres de Huarochirí* in Quechua and Spanish. |
| 1987 | Taylor publishes a critical Quechua-Spanish Huarochirí edition. |
| 1991 | Salomon and Urioste publish the Quechua-English *Huarochirí Manuscript*. |
| Late twentieth century | Structuralist, gender-historical, archaeological, and regional approaches displace simple pantheon lists. |
| Early twenty-first century | Killa/Kulla Raymi heritage revivals, tourism, art, wellness culture, and individual neopagan devotion expand Mama Quilla’s modern image. |
| Present | The figure remains active as heritage symbol, reconstructed deity, artistic subject, and name for the Moon; continuity with the imperial Coricancha institution cannot be assumed. |
