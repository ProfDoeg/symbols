# Mama Sara: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest sources, **Mama Sara is a maize *wak’a***—a sacred, efficacious “mother” instantiated in selected maize, unusual ears, dressed cornstalk dolls, or maize-shaped *conopas*. “Goddess of maize” is a defensible modern shorthand, but it misleadingly suggests a single anthropomorphic member of a formally organized pantheon.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Juan Polo de Ondegardo’s 1559 account of the May harvest festival, which says households made a maize *huaca* called **Mamaçara**, installed it in a small granary, watched over it for three nights, venerated and consulted it, and retained or ritually renewed it according to its answer. No securely identified pre-Conquest inscription names Mama Sara. Earlier archaeological maize images and maize-shaped ritual objects establish the antiquity of maize sacrality, but identifying any particular pre-Conquest object as “Mama Sara” remains a retrospective interpretation.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century testimony, vocabulary, trial record, or securely excavated Inca context.
- **[LE] Later colonial elaboration:** seventeenth- or eighteenth-century repetition, amplification, or Christian polemic.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a local practice or classification not safely generalized to the whole Andes.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** a chronicler’s or modern author’s organization of diverse sacred beings into a pantheon.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** documented combination with, or translation into, another religious system.
- **[F] Folklore or living oral practice:** ethnographically or officially recorded postcolonial tradition.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference joining archaeological, colonial, linguistic, or ethnographic evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim:** not located in the early corpus and traceable only to recent retellings.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[EA]** Polo de Ondegardo’s testimony defines Mamaçara not as a remote celestial personality but as “the mother of the maize of [one’s] field”: selected maize wrapped in a household’s richest textiles and placed in a *pirua*, a small storage structure. It could preserve and reproduce the crop, receive sacrifice, answer a ritual specialist, lose its strength, and be burned and renewed. Cristóbal de Albornoz subsequently classified beautiful maize specimens and their stone, gold, or silver counterparts as *guacas*. Arriaga distinguished three material forms: a dressed doll made from maize stalks, sculpted maize-shaped stones used as *conopas*, and unusually fertile or conjoined maize ears. These sources support “maize mother,” “crop-being,” “household wak’a,” and “animate ritual object” more directly than the modern category “goddess.” The fullest recent synthesis is Alejandro Ortiz Luna’s study of Mama Sara as an agentive agricultural object. [Ortiz Luna 2022](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368274148_Mamasara_o_saramama_la_madre_del_maiz_Reflexiones_sobre_agencia_de_objetos_agricolas_magico-religiosos_en_el_antiguo_Peru)

**[MSR]** Calling her a “goddess” translates Andean categories into a familiar comparative vocabulary. It is acceptable if qualified: *wak’a* could denote sacred beings, ancestors, places, stones, images, crops, and other powerful persons or things, and *conopa* denoted a household or lineage-associated object promoting increase. Mama Sara therefore crossed Western divisions between divinity, fetish, relic, crop specimen, and image.

### Names and orthography

**[EA]**

- **Mamaçara / Mama cara:** colonial spellings in Polo and later ecclesiastical sources.
- **Mamazara / Mama Zara / Zaramama:** Spanish-colonial spellings, with *z*, *ç*, and *s* representing unstable colonial attempts to write Quechua sibilants.
- **Mamasara / Mama Sara / Saramama / Sara Mama:** normalized or modern forms.
- Quechua **mama**, “mother,” plus reconstructed Proto-Quechua **\*sara**, “maize.” The compound therefore means “Mother Maize” or “Maize Mother,” not “Mother Grain” in a generic botanical sense. The translation “grain goddess” is a modern broadening. Proto-Quechua *sara* is recognized in comparative linguistic scholarship. [Proto-Quechua and Proto-Aymara](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/z.215.02eml/pdf)

**[EA/RV] Recorded subtype names**

- **Huantayzara / Wantaysara** and **Ayrihuayzara / Ayrway sara:** fertile stalks or joined ears associated with the *ayrihua* harvest dance.
- **Micsazara / Misa sara / Misha sara:** unusually colored or bicolored maize; in later ethnography red and white can signify silver and gold or complementary opposites.
- **Matayzara** and **Caullazara:** exceptional ears listed by Arriaga; their precise distinctions are not securely recoverable from his brief notice.
- **Piruazara:** an ear whose grains rise in a spiral; Arriaga says it was set in a maize heap or *pirua* to guard the crop.
- **Taqe sara / taqi sara / taqi mama / taqi saramama:** modern Cusco-area names for conjoined or multiple ears kept as signs and agents of abundance.
- **Llallawa:** a broader category of abnormally large, doubled, or otherwise prolific produce, including but not limited to maize.
- **Sara-conopa / sara qunupa:** a maize-shaped household fertility object.

These are not necessarily epithets of one goddess in the Greco-Roman sense. They are classifications of differently formed maize beings and objects whose powers overlap.

### Equations and disputed identities

**[LS]** Modern lists commonly place Mama Sara beside Pachamama, Mama Killa, Mama Qucha, Axomama, and Cocamama as a set of specialized “mother goddesses.” Colonial evidence supports a productive Andean *mama* category, but not a canonical divine family or a fixed council of goddesses.

**[MSR]** Some modern authors describe crop mothers as aspects, extensions, or daughters of Pachamama. The colonial Mama Sara passages do not give that genealogy. The equation is therefore an interpretive model, not an early attested family relationship.

**[RV/SI]** Twentieth-century reports from Bolivia and northwestern Argentina describe upright field stones called Mama Sara, or analogous stones associated with Pachamama, which protect maize fields. Christian gestures—crossing oneself, approaching on one’s knees—and Indigenous offerings could coexist at these stones. This documents localized syncretic practice, not a universal equation of Mama Sara with the Virgin Mary. [Andean field-stone study](https://publications.iai.spk-berlin.de/servlets/MCRFileNodeServlet/Document_derivate_00000158/IND_Beiheft_08_Part_1.pdf)

**[MI]** No early evidence establishes Mama Sara as:

- daughter of Pachamama;
- wife, daughter, or lover of Inti;
- resident of *Hanan Pacha*;
- one of four elemental mothers;
- goddess of willows;
- patroness of all grain;
- a storm goddess;
- identical with Mama Huaco.

Those statements belong to recent pantheon charts, derivative websites, creative works, or unsourced harmonizations.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest named evidence

**1559 — Polo de Ondegardo [EA].** In his calendrical description of **Hatun Cuzqu Raymoray**, corresponding approximately to May, Polo says that during **Aymoray/Aymorana**, when maize was brought from the field to the house, people sang and prayed that it endure. Each household made a maize *huaca* named **Mamaçara** from specially selected maize, placed it in a small *pirua*, wrapped it in its richest mantles, watched over it for three nights, worshipped it, and said it was the mother through whom the field’s maize was produced and preserved. A diviner asked whether it retained strength for the next year. A negative response required burning and replacing it in the field; a positive response allowed another year’s use. Polo added that the practice continued in his own day. This is the earliest securely dated explicit name yet identified. [Ortiz Luna 2022, pp. 107–08](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368274148_Mamasara_o_saramama_la_madre_del_maiz_Reflexiones_sobre_agencia_de_objetos_agricolas_magico-religiosos_en_el_antiguo_Peru)

The passage demonstrates a named, domestic, materially embodied crop mother by 1559. It does **not** establish her origin date, genealogy, a humanoid body, a state temple, or an independent narrative mythology.

### Principal corpus

- **Cristóbal de Albornoz, *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú* (c. 1581–1585) [EA].** Beautiful first fruits and maize replicas in stone, gold, or silver were called *mamaçara*. Chicha was offered to the “mother” of the material about to be worked. His testimony derives from an extirpator’s survey, especially in Huamanga, and is both valuable and coercively produced.

- **Martín de Murúa, *Historia general del Perú* (late sixteenth-century version; later recensions) [EA/LE].** Closely parallels Polo: selected maize was installed in a *pirua*, venerated as the field’s mother and preserver, and retained secretly after public rites had been suppressed. Dependence on Polo or shared reporting must be considered; it is not wholly independent corroboration.

- **Diego González Holguín, *Vocabvlario de la lengva general de todo el Perv* (Lima, 1608) [EA].** Records joined ears and grains as portents: *aya apa chocllo*, *ayrihua cara*, and *pituchocllo/atichocllo*. The dictionary establishes period terminology but does not itself identify all such forms as one deity. Its bibliographic and linguistic context is catalogued by Glottolog. [González Holguín record](https://glottolog.org/resource/reference/id/104046)

- **Anonymous Huarochirí Manuscript (compiled c. 1598–1608; Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional de España, MS 3169) [EA/RV].** This Classical Quechua collection supplies essential comparative evidence for regional *wak’a* personhood, agricultural reciprocity, irrigation, maize, and ritual. It does **not**, in its standard modern editions, give a narrative of a named Mama Sara. Its maize-associated female beings—especially Chuquisuso—must not be silently relabeled Mama Sara. The major editions are José María Arguedas (1966), Gerald Taylor (1987; revised 2008), and Frank Salomon and George Urioste, *The Huarochirí Manuscript* (1991). [Manuscript and editions overview](https://dedenbachsalazar.de/?page_id=71)

- **Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (c. 1615) [EA].** For May, **Aymoray Quilla**, Guaman Poma reports festivities and drunken celebration when doubled maize ears or paired potatoes were found and food was taken into storehouses. Elsewhere he calls doubled or abnormally large produce a bad sign. This contradiction is important: abnormal produce could be auspicious in one ritual context and ominous in another; there was no single pan-Andean reading.

- **Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú* (1621) [EA].** The fullest colonial taxonomy: (1) a woman-shaped maize-stalk doll wearing *anaco*, *lliclla*, and silver *tupus*, thought as a mother able to beget and bear much maize; (2) carved stone ears used as *conopas*; and (3) especially fertile stalks or two joined ears, “the principal Zaramamas.” Fertile stalks were hung from willow branches for the **Ayrihua** dance, then burned and sacrificed to **Líbiac** for a good crop. Colored and spiral-grained ears were kept in maize heaps and granaries as guardians. Arriaga counted 45 *mamazaras* among objects seized during his campaigns, within a coercive inventory that also enumerated thousands of *conopas* and hundreds of sacred beings.

- **Antonio de la Calancha, *Corónica moralizada* (1638) [LE].** Repeats a May Hatuncuzcu/Aymoray observance in which a small granary held the best maize for the idol Mamaçora, adorned with *cumbi*. His account explicitly frames the observance through Christian polemic. A later Aymara Christian context joined early-May agricultural rites to the Feast of the Cross. [Aymoray and Christianization study](https://scielo.org.bo/scielo.php?pid=S2077-33232008000200006&script=sci_arttext)

- **Francisco de Ávila and Fernando de Avendaño, Quechua/Spanish anti-idolatry sermons (1640s) [LE].** They attacked maize *conopas* and *mamasara*, acknowledging Indigenous claims that inherited objects protected maize from frost, increased crops, and received chicha.

- **Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (completed 1653) [LE].** Reports that unusually formed potatoes, maize ears, and legumes were kissed, honored through drinking and dancing, and treated with special ceremonies. His text preserves earlier information but should not automatically be treated as an eyewitness to every custom.

- **Cajatambo trial, Otuco/Acas, 1656–1657 [EA/RV].** Andrés Chaupis Yauri named **Saramama** among communal sacred beings receiving offerings. Pedro Sarmiento testified that harvesters gathered *airiguas*, *misa sara*, and *mama sara*, arranged them on a maguey stalk with *molle*, danced with drums before the idol **Tauris**, drank overnight, made part of the maize into chicha, and burned another part with chicha as sacrifice. These judicial statements are unusually specific local evidence, but they were elicited under prosecution.

- **Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales* (1609) [EA/LS].** Garcilaso describes royal gardens containing naturalistic maize plants in precious metal: stalk, roots, leaves, and ears chiefly of silver, with yellowing portions in gold. This attests the ceremonial and political representation of maize, not a statue or sanctuary of Mama Sara. [Garcilaso, gold-and-silver gardens](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Comentarios_reales_%28Primera_parte%29/XV)

### Chroniclers who do not provide a secure named Mama Sara narrative

**[Finding from absence]** Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Molina, and Garcilaso are indispensable for Inca religion, maize, state ritual, Coricancha, chicha, and calendrical festivals, but no securely verified passage in the material reviewed here gives Mama Sara a genealogy or personal myth. Their silence should not be filled by importing later internet stories. The same applies to the Huarochirí Manuscript.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and powers

**[EA]** Mama Sara’s securely attested domain is the **generation, increase, preservation, storage, and future fertility of maize**. She protects maize in the *pirua*, foretells whether seed vitality will endure, helps prevent scarcity or frost, and participates in harvest rites. Her power is reproductive: Arriaga says the dressed figure, “as mother,” could “engender and bear much maize.”

**[MSR]** The maternal idiom describes generative capacity rather than proving a divine genealogy. A crop specimen was not merely a symbol of an absent goddess; colonial descriptions and modern relational interpretations suggest it could itself be the powerful maize person.

### Recognizable forms

1. **Selected harvest maize [EA]:** the best or most marked portion of a household crop, bundled, richly wrapped, and installed in a *pirua*.
2. **Woman-shaped maize doll [EA]:** maize stalks dressed in an *anaco* dress, *lliclla* mantle, and silver *tupu* pins.
3. **Joined or multiple ears [EA/F]:** two or more ears united on one stalk or at the base; these were Arriaga’s “principal” Zaramamas.
4. **Exceptional kernels [EA/F]:** bicolored, variegated, spiral, unusually large, or paired grains and ears.
5. **Stone or metal *conopa* [EA]:** portable carvings shaped like maize, sometimes with raised kernels.
6. **Field stone [RV/F]:** upright stones called Mama Sara in parts of Bolivia and northwestern Argentina.
7. **Modern humanoid goddess art [MI unless specifically ethnographic]:** young women crowned with ears, green-and-gold gowns, cornucopias, and standardized “Inca goddess” costumes are not based on a known canonical pre-Conquest image.

### Archaeological evidence

**[MSR]** Archaeology supplies analogues, not name-labels:

- Moche ceramics depict joined ears and anthropomorphized maize; these long predate the Inca and cannot securely be named Mama Sara.
- The Old Temple at Pachacamac contained maize-shaped stone *conopas*, including a triple ear, in a context dated approximately 600–1000 CE with small vessels, *Spondylus*, minerals, and sacrificed camelids.
- Chimú-period stone carvings show multiple attached ears.
- Inca-period Pueblo Viejo-Pucará yielded maize *conopas* in domestic storage and ritual contexts.
- Inca ritual deposits at Pachacamac contained a doubled-ear stone *conopa* with textiles, *Spondylus*, *ishpingo*, guinea pigs, and ceramics.
- Inca silver figurines in the Musée du quai Branly and other collections show people holding joined ears; one seems to kiss or breathe toward them.

These objects demonstrate deep histories of sacred maize, unusual-ear imagery, offering, and storage. Their identification as *mamasara* is a plausible analogy grounded in colonial descriptions, not an excavated written caption. [Ortiz Luna 2022](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368274148_Mamasara_o_saramama_la_madre_del_maiz_Reflexiones_sobre_agencia_de_objetos_agricolas_magico-religiosos_en_el_antiguo_Peru)

### Associations

- **Maize and seed:** defining and early.
- **Pirua/storehouse:** defining and early.
- **Rich textiles and women’s dress:** early.
- **Chicha:** early as offering, shared drink, and product of ritual ears.
- **Song, dance, vigils, divination:** early.
- **Willow branches:** attested by Arriaga as supports from which fertile stalks hung during Ayrihua; this does not make the willow her “sacred tree.”
- **Molle and maguey:** attested in the 1657 Acas arrangement; regional ritual materials, not universal emblems.
- **Gold and silver maize:** securely attested for royal gardens and maize replicas, but not necessarily images of Mama Sara.
- **Colors, number, planet, weekday:** no canonical assignment is attested. Modern correspondence tables are modern occult systematization.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

**[Finding from absence]** No early source reviewed gives Mama Sara parents, siblings, spouse, children, birth, or a stable place in a divine hierarchy. Her “children” are maize ears in a reproductive or relational sense. Modern statements that she is a daughter or aspect of Pachamama lack a cited colonial genealogy.

**[MSR]** Mama Sara belongs analytically to a family of Andean generative “mothers”—maize, potatoes, coca, metals, waters—but that is a categorical relationship, not a recorded family tree.

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Líbiac — [EA/RV], Arriaga, 1621:** fertile maize stalks or joined ears identified among the Zaramamas were hung on willow branches and danced with in the Ayrihua; afterward they were burned and sacrificed to Líbiac, a lightning/weather *wak’a*, so that he would grant a good harvest. This is a joint ritual relation, not evidence that Líbiac was her husband or father.

- **Tauris — [EA/RV], Cajatambo idolatry trial, testimony of Pedro Sarmiento, 28 January 1657:** *airigua*, *misa sara*, and *mama sara* ears were assembled with maguey and *molle* and brought to the site of Tauris; the community danced, drank, made chicha from part, and burned part with chicha for the local sacred beings and ancestors. This is a shared cultic scene.

- **Pachamama — [MSR/F], not an early narrative interaction:** recent scholarship and practice may place maize mothers within the generative earth’s domain or use Mama Sara objects in Pachamama offerings. No colonial episode records the two meeting, speaking, marrying, or acting together as named characters.

- **Inti — [MI as personal interaction]:** maize and chicha were central to state rites for the Sun, and precious-metal maize adorned elite or temple environments. Nevertheless, no early source located describes Inti transforming a maiden named Sara into maize. Their institutional association must not be converted into a personal myth.

- **Mama Huaco — [MI/LS]:** Mama Huaco’s association with founding Cusco, agriculture, violence, and in some accounts maize cultivation does not make her identical with Mama Sara. No early equation has been established.

- **Chuquisuso, Pariacaca, Chaupiñamca, Viracocha, Mama Killa, Axomama, Cocamama — [Finding from absence]:** no specific, textually attested interaction with Mama Sara has been established.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The harvest-vigil cycle

**[EA]** The nearest thing to a securely attested Mama Sara narrative is enacted ritual:

1. The household identifies especially fine maize.
2. It carries the maize home from the field with songs during Aymoray.
3. It makes this maize into the household’s Mama Sara.
4. The being is placed in a small *pirua*, wrapped in the family’s best mantles, and watched for three nights.
5. Participants pray that the maize endure and venerate the granary.
6. A ritual specialist asks whether Mama Sara retains strength for another agricultural year.
7. If strong, she remains; if not, she is carried to the field, burned, and renewed so the seed will not perish.

The cycle explains and ritually manages crop continuity. It is simultaneously an agricultural rite, divination, domestic cult, and account of how the maize mother ages and is reborn.

### The three material births of Zaramama

**[LE but based on observed/seized objects]** Arriaga’s 1621 classification functions like a compact origin taxonomy:

- humans fabricate a maize-stalk woman;
- artisans reproduce maize in stone;
- the fertile field itself produces extraordinary stalks and joined ears.

All three can become maize mothers because form, fertility, inheritance, dress, placement, and ritual recognition create an efficacious being. Arriaga does not tell a story in which one primordial goddess assumes three disguises.

### Ayrihua and the sacrifice to Líbiac

**[EA/RV]** Fertile stalks and conjoined ears are danced with while suspended from willow branches, then burned for Líbiac. The rite joins crop fertility to rain, lightning, and weather. It may have been regional rather than a uniform imperial ceremony.

### Cajatambo harvest cycle

**[EA/RV]** At Acas in 1657, special ears were collected, bound with *molle* to maguey, carried before Tauris, accompanied by elderly women with small drums, danced with through the night, partly brewed into chicha, and partly burned with chicha. This is direct evidence that Mama Sara belonged to a network of local ancestors and *wak’as*, not an isolated pan-Inca personality.

### The maiden Sara and Kuru/Kuro

**[MI]** A widely copied modern tale says a beautiful maiden Sara rejected a sorcerer named Kuru or Kuro, appealed to Inti, and was transformed into maize; some variants add that only women may harvest her. No citation to Polo, Albornoz, Murúa, Guaman Poma, Arriaga, Cobo, Molina, Garcilaso, the Huarochirí Manuscript, or an identified early ethnographic collection has been found. Online retellings reproduce one another without a recoverable provenance. It should be presented as a recent literary or internet legend unless a datable oral-history recording is produced—not as an Inca myth.

### The Sarasara-mountain woman

**[F/RV]** A tradition officially recorded in a 2014 Peruvian cultural-heritage declaration says Saramama lived on Nevado Sarasara, descended carrying an *apascha* to tend maize, and rewarded a woman with ears that became golden seed. This is valuable modern regional folklore from Ayacucho, but it is not evidence of an empire-wide Inca myth. [Official Peruvian record](https://dataonline.gacetajuridica.com.pe/gaceta/admin/elperuano/1072014/10-07-2014.PDF)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Scale and institutional form

**[EA]** The strongest evidence is for **household and community cult**, not a dedicated monumental temple. Each household could maintain its own Mama Sara. *Conopas* were inherited, stored, dressed, fed with chicha, and used to promote production. Cajatambo evidence also shows communal rites.

**[Finding from absence]** No securely documented temple exclusively dedicated to Mama Sara, separate priesthood bearing her name, canonical oracle, or state-wide sanctuary has been identified. Claims of a “Temple of Mama Sara” require evidence.

### Aymoray

**[EA]** Aymoray or Aymorana occurred in the harvest month corresponding broadly to May, though translating Inca lunations directly into Gregorian months is approximate. Activities included bringing maize home, singing, filling storage facilities, feasting, dancing, sacrifice, and installing or renewing Mama Sara. Guaman Poma’s doubled-ear celebration and Polo’s three-night vigil represent overlapping but not identical accounts.

**[LE/SI]** In later Aymara regions, early-May agricultural practice could coincide with the Catholic Feast of the Cross. A 1739 La Paz synod complained of decorated crosses brought to Mass on 3 May. This is a documented process of calendrical and ritual coexistence; it does not demonstrate a simple one-to-one substitution of the Virgin or Cross for Mama Sara. [Christianization of Aymoray](https://scielo.org.bo/scielo.php?pid=S2077-33232008000200006&script=sci_arttext)

### Chicha

**[EA]** Chicha connected Mama Sara with household reciprocity and imperial political economy:

- it was poured or sprinkled onto maize objects;
- drinking accompanied dances and vigils;
- selected ritual ears could themselves be brewed;
- part of the maize and chicha could be burned for sacred beings;
- at the state level, *aqllakuna* produced maize beer and textiles for the Inca and Sun.

The last institution belongs to the wider maize complex; sources do not say the *aqllakuna* constituted a priesthood of Mama Sara. Archaeological and historical work confirms that selected women brewed chicha for state hospitality and ritual. [Women, chicha, and Inca institutions](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/food-and-labour-under-imperial-rule-unravelling-the-food-landscape-of-transplanted-workers-mitmaqkuna-in-the-inka-empire/565AFA7590F960CE8FDE6068DE9E5360)

### Sacrifice and contested practices

**[EA]** Animal offerings, food, chicha, coca, shell, textiles, precious metals, and the burning of maize are documented in the broader maize and *conopa* complex. Arriaga explicitly records burning Zaramamas for Líbiac.

**[Finding from absence]** No source reviewed documents human sacrifice **to Mama Sara**. The Inca state practiced *capacocha*, and maize/chicha could form part of sacrificial provisioning, but that does not make the victim an offering to Mama Sara. Chemical and archaeological study of the Llullaillaco children confirms maize/chicha consumption within *capacocha* preparations. [PNAS study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1305117110)

**[Finding from absence]** No evidence links Mama Sara to “temple prostitution.” Applying that disputed Old World category here would be baseless.

### Suppression

**[EA]** Extirpators seized and destroyed *mamasaras*, *conopas*, ancestral remains, and other *wak’as*; required denunciations and confessions; and used Indigenous testimony to compose anti-idolatry sermons. Arriaga’s figures—45 *mamazaras*, 3,418 *conopas*, 603 principal *huacas*, and 679 ritual specialists found during one campaign—show both the density of household religion and the violence of its documentary context. These records preserve practices precisely because colonial authorities meant to abolish them.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the Inca state

**[MSR]** Maize had ritual importance across Andean societies long before the Incas. Moche joined-ear imagery and Middle Horizon maize *conopas* at Pachacamac provide material antecedents. They establish continuity in sacred maize forms, not continuity of the exact name Mama Sara.

### Inca period, approximately fifteenth century–1532

**[EA/MSR]** The Inca state intensified highland maize production, storage, redistribution, chicha manufacture, and ceremonial feasting. Precious-metal maize gardens materialized imperial control over fertility and tribute. Household crop mothers probably operated alongside, rather than beneath a perfectly centralized state theology. Inca-period maize *conopas* from domestic, storage, and sanctuary contexts are compatible with the colonial descriptions.

### 1530s–1559: conquest and first documentation

**[EA]** Spanish seizure and melting of precious-metal objects destroyed much elite cult material. The first explicit name appears only in 1559, after upheaval, but Polo says the rite was still widely used. The chronological gap prevents a firm date for its origin.

### 1580s–1620s: description and criminalization

**[EA]** Albornoz documented maize “mothers” while teaching officials to locate and destroy *wak’as*. Murúa reported that rites continued secretly. Guaman Poma recorded harvest celebrations and conflicting omens. Arriaga converted seized sacred objects into a taxonomy useful to extirpators.

### 1640s–1650s: sermons and trials

**[LE/EA]** Ávila and Avendaño attacked inherited maize *conopas* through Christian argument. The Cajatambo proceedings show that communities continued to make offerings, dance, brew chicha, and organize special ears around local *wak’as* despite decades of suppression.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

**[SI/F]** Agricultural observances persisted within Christian calendars and local cults. Documentation becomes less consistent, and the specific name Mama Sara is sometimes replaced by broader devotion to Pachamama, saints, crosses, household luck objects, or crop-specific practices.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

**[F]** Ethnography records:

- doubled or multiple ears kept in granaries in Cusco;
- *t’inka/ch’alla* with chicha;
- incense and affectionate address to *taqi mama* or *taqi saramama*;
- bicolored *misa* ears used in offerings;
- maize-mother categories in Bolivian markets, where multiple small ears are described as children;
- paired white and red specimens hung for luck;
- field stones in Bolivia and northwestern Argentina.

In Oruro market taxonomy, *mama sara* denotes a multiple ear; subcategories distinguish a mother with one to four “children,” and ritual and economic value increase with their number. [Bolivian ethnobotanical study](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/47918/1/37WILKIN_2014_THESIS_Transmission%20and%20Commoditisation%20of_Medicinal%20Plant%20Knowledge%20in%20the%20Marketplaces%20of%20Oruro%20Bolivia_known.pdf)

**[F/MSR]** Ortiz Luna’s 2021 fieldwork in Urko and use of community documentation from Queromarca record doubled *taqe sara/taqi saramama* as good omens, stored above seed maize and offered chicha so that the maize’s force will not depart. He also reports that evangelical and Adventist opposition is contributing to decline in some communities. [Ortiz Luna 2022](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368274148_Mamasara_o_saramama_la_madre_del_maiz_Reflexiones_sobre_agencia_de_objetos_agricolas_magico-religiosos_en_el_antiguo_Peru)

Continuity is therefore strongest at the level of **practices and relational concepts**—recognizing exceptional ears, feeding them, storing them, and asking them to perpetuate maize—not necessarily continuous worship of one universally imagined goddess.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

**[MSR]** Twentieth-century Andeanists, including John Murra, María Rostworowski, Pierre Duviols, Irene Silverblatt, Gary Urton, Tom Zuidema, Brian Bauer, Sabine MacCormack, Frank Salomon, and others, shifted analysis away from simple pantheon lists toward political economy, gender, landscape, reciprocity, household religion, *wak’a* personhood, and the evidentiary biases of colonial writing.

**[MSR]** Ortiz Luna’s 2022 synthesis is presently the most focused published dossier on the objects called *mamasara/saramama*. Its valuable argument is that they were socially active persons rather than passive representations. Some of its pre-Inca identifications remain analogical and should not be mistaken for inscribed proof.

### Neopaganism and occultism

**[Finding from absence]** The dedicated searches found no documented Renaissance Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, or early modern grimoire tradition centered on Mama Sara. She is absent from the historic Western ceremonial-magic canon.

**[MI/modern religious innovation]** Contemporary goddess lists, neopagan pages, automated mythology sites, and commercial spirituality often assign her:

- fixed colors, planets, weekdays, crystals, or tarot correspondences;
- a throne in Hanan Pacha;
- membership in an elemental tetrad;
- a standardized human appearance;
- a family relation to Pachamama or Inti.

Such systems may be meaningful modern religious creations, but they are not ancient attestations unless their creators explicitly cite and date an Indigenous source.

### Literature, performance, and commerce

**[MI/F]** Mama Sara now appears in children’s mythology books, tourism copy, ecological theater, craft branding, food heritage, games, and online devotional art. These media reinforce the image of a benevolent, beautiful corn goddess while usually omitting the materially specific *pirua*, divination, burning, and extirpation records.

A game that makes Mama Sara a “goddess of storm,” for example, is openly creative adaptation, not preservation of colonial testimony. Likewise, recent books presenting invented journeys, priestly villains, or elaborate cosmic biographies should be classified as modern retellings.

### Political appropriation

**[MSR/F]** Agricultural and Indigenous-rights discourse sometimes invokes Mama Sara as an emblem of native seed sovereignty, women’s agricultural knowledge, or ecological reciprocity. These uses grow from real Andean maize relations but are modern political formulations. No substantial evidence located ties her to an organized extremist movement.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Mama Sara a goddess or an object?

The principal debate is partly terminological.

- **Pantheon model [LS]:** Mama Sara is a goddess who is represented by dolls, ears, and stones.
- **Relational/agentive model [MSR]:** the ears, bundles, dolls, and stones are not merely images but material persons whose identity emerges through recognition, dress, feeding, storage, inheritance, speech, and reciprocity.

The second model better explains why Polo’s Mama Sara could answer questions, lose strength, be renewed, and preserve a particular household’s maize.

### One deity or a class?

**[MSR]** The singular “Mama Sara” may conceal a distributed class of household and local beings. Polo says every household made one; Arriaga counts plural *Zaramamas*; trial records distinguish ritual ears. The evidence favors many materially situated maize mothers rather than one universally identical divine individual.

### Pre-Inca origin

**[Contested MSR]** Joined-ear Moche pottery and Middle Horizon maize *conopas* show that relevant iconography and ritual technologies predate the Incas. But “Mama Sara existed under this exact name and identity by 600 CE” cannot be demonstrated. Form and function may persist while names and ontologies change.

### Aymoray and Ayrihua

**[Contested]** Colonial authors use related but variable calendrical and ritual vocabulary. Aymoray is the harvest month/festival in Polo and Guaman Poma; Ayrihua is a dance and a term associated with fertile or paired maize in Arriaga and lexical sources. Treating them as one standardized empire-wide liturgy erases local variation.

### Auspicious or ominous abnormality

**[EA conflict]** Guaman Poma records doubled produce both amid joyful harvest festivities and as a “very bad sign.” González Holguín likewise glosses some joined ears as death portents or omens, whereas Arriaga calls paired ears the principal Zaramamas. Possible explanations include context, color, form, locality, owner, ritual handling, or simply incompatible traditions. The sources do not permit a single rule.

### Mama Sara and the Coricancha

**[Contested/unsupported identification]** Garcilaso’s precious-metal maize garden proves maize’s elite ritual representation. It does not name the metal plants Mama Sara. “The Sara of the Coricancha” is therefore best understood as a modern interpretive connection between royal maize imagery and the maize-mother complex, not a securely attested cult statue.

### Colonial source bias

Polo, Albornoz, Arriaga, Ávila, Avendaño, and the trial scribes documented Indigenous religion to govern or eradicate it. Terms such as “idol,” “superstition,” “witch,” and “drunkenness” are colonial judgments. Yet these sources contain precise Indigenous words, materials, gestures, and testimony. Critical use requires neither discarding them nor adopting their theology.

### Discredited or unverified popular claims

- the Kuru transformation story as “ancient Inca scripture”;
- a canonical parentage under Pachamama and Inti;
- a dedicated Coricancha sanctuary;
- universal willow worship;
- human sacrifice specifically to Mama Sara;
- temple prostitution;
- ancient occult correspondences;
- a fixed four-element goddess system;
- direct equivalence with Mama Huaco.

All are unattested, over-systematized, or demonstrably recent on present evidence.
