# Mama Cocha: Research Dossier

**Class:** In sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Quechua and Spanish sources, *Mama Cocha* is primarily the animate, female-coded sea—“Mother Sea”—and a *huaca*, a sacred power or being manifested in a natural feature. “Goddess of all water” is a useful modern shorthand, but broader than the earliest evidence.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently identifiable written testimony is Juan Polo de Ondegardo’s *Tratado y averiguación sobre los errores y supersticiones de los indios*, drafted in **1559** and circulated in manuscript from the 1560s. Polo reports that coastal people worshipped the sea for fish and safety, highlanders invoked it even without having seen it, Quechua speakers called it **Mamacocha**, and Aymara speakers **Mama Cota**. The earliest printed lexical attestation is the anonymous Quechua dictionary of **1586**, which gives *Mama cocha* simply as “the sea.” These are post-conquest records of practices remembered or observed within a generation of the Spanish invasion; no securely deciphered pre-Hispanic inscription names Mama Cocha.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Secure early attestation]** Mama Cocha is not initially documented as an anthropomorphic woman with a biography, fixed genealogy, temple statue, and centralized priesthood. The early record identifies the sea itself as an animate maternal *huaca*: nourishing because it supplies fish, dangerous because it can overturn vessels or cause illness, and powerful enough to receive offerings carried to it by rivers.

José de Acosta’s *Historia natural y moral de las Indias* (Seville, 1590) says that Andean peoples worshipped “the earth, which they called Pachamama … and the sea, which they called Mamacocha,” comparing the latter—through European *interpretatio*—with Tethys or Neptune. Acosta’s comparison is not an Indigenous equation and supplies no evidence that Mama Cocha possessed the myths or iconography of either Greco-Roman deity.

Inca Garcilaso de la Vega’s *Comentarios reales* (Lisbon, 1609, I.10) gives the fullest early explanation:

> “Generally the Indians of that coast … worshipped the sea … for the benefit its fish gave them for food … and so they called it Mamacocha, which means Mother Sea, because it performed a mother’s office in feeding them.”

Garcilaso describes a coastal religious complex rather than a universal, homogeneous Inca church. The coast contained numerous pre-Inca and Inca-period societies with their own marine beings, islands, fish prototypes, and local shrines.

### Names and spellings

- **Mama Cocha / Mamacocha** — colonial Spanish orthographies of Quechua *mama qucha*: “Mother Sea,” “Mother Lake,” or, idiomatically, “the sea.”
- **Mama Qucha / Mama Qocha** — modern normalized Quechua spellings. Quechua varieties differ between three- and five-vowel orthographies; *qucha* and *qocha* are therefore not separate divine names.
- **Mama Cota / Mama Quta / Quta Mama** — Aymara forms, from *mama*, “mother/lady,” and *quta*, “lake, body of standing water.” Polo de Ondegardo and Ludovico Bertonio’s Aymara vocabulary (1612) record *Mama Cota* for the great sea.
- **Cochamama / Quchamama** — reversed compound documented in discussions of offerings of marine shell; context may mean “Mother Sea,” “mother water,” or the sea as source of shells rather than a distinct personality.
- **La mar / Madre Mar / Madre Laguna / Mother Lake** — Spanish and English descriptive translations, not separate ancient epithets.

Domingo de Santo Tomás’s Quechua lexicon (1560) defines *cocha* as sea, lake, or pond. The anonymous 1586 vocabulary gives *cocha/qucha* for “sea, lagoon, pool, cistern” and *Mama cocha* for “the sea.” González Holguín (1608) similarly relates *ccocha* to the sea, lagoons, and pools and glosses *Mama cocha* as the “lake [used] for sea.” Bertonio (1612) gives Aymara *Mama cota* as “great sea.” The semantic field is therefore an expanse or container of water, distinct in these dictionaries from Quechua *mayu*, “river.” A recent lexical review conveniently assembles these early entries.[^1]

### “Mother” and personhood

**[Secure early material]** *Mama* can mark a literal mother, an honored female, a generative source, or a powerful prototype. The compound need not imply an Olympian-style goddess with human anatomy. Garcilaso’s explanation is functional: the sea “acted as mother” by feeding coastal people.

**[Colonial learned systematization]** Acosta’s “Tethys or Neptune” and later European descriptions as “sea goddess” translate an Andean animate landscape into classical and Christian-European categories. “Goddess” is serviceable if this translation is kept visible.

### Unsupported or late titles

No early source located gives Mama Cocha such formal epithets as “Queen of the Oceans,” “Guardian of All Sailors,” or “Mistress of the Tides.” Protection of travelers and fishermen is supported by ritual behavior; these capitalized titles are modern editorial creations.

The following widely repeated systems could not be traced to a colonial primary source:

- **[Modern systematization/invention]** the “Four Elemental Mothers”—Mama Cocha, Pachamama, Mama Nina, and Mama Wayra;
- **[Modern invention]** a “lunar trinity” of Mama Cocha, Pachamama, and Mama Quilla;
- **[Modern invention]** assignment of Mama Cocha to one of three neatly bounded cosmic “worlds” as her permanent residence;
- **[Modern popular elaboration]** a pale, beautiful young woman dressed in blue;
- **[Modern popular elaboration]** fixed colors, gemstones, weekdays, zodiacal correspondences, or an elemental altar system.

These claims proliferate on modern mythology, occult, tourism, and user-edited websites but are absent from the relevant early chronicles and dictionaries.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Limits of the evidence

The Inca did not leave alphabetic scriptures. Quipus carried administrative and perhaps narrative information, but no surviving quipu can presently be read as a text naming Mama Cocha. The written corpus is therefore colonial: Spanish clerics and officials, Indigenous and mestizo chroniclers, Quechua testimony collected under missionary conditions, dictionaries, and extirpation proceedings. These sources preserve invaluable information while also reflecting translation, coercion, Christian polemic, local politics, and retrospective imperial systematization.

### Principal attestations

#### Juan Polo de Ondegardo, 1559 and related reports

**Language:** Spanish, with Quechua and Aymara vocabulary.  
**Status:** Earliest secure named documentary testimony presently identified.

Polo states that coastal people worshipped the sea so it would provide fish and not become angry; inland people did likewise even if they had never seen it. He distinguishes Quechua **Mamacocha** from Aymara **Mama Cota**. Polo’s treatise was drafted in 1559, circulated in manuscript, and was partly incorporated into later ecclesiastical compilations. Because multiple texts and editorial titles became associated with Polo, quotations should be tied to a specific edition rather than assigned loosely to “Inca mythology.”

#### Domingo de Santo Tomás, *Lexicón*, 1560

Santo Tomás does not supply a myth or cult of a named goddess, but his definitions establish the colonial Quechua semantic field of *cocha*: sea, lake, and pond. This is linguistic evidence, not evidence of genealogy.

#### Cristóbal de Molina, *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas*, c. 1573–1575

Molina reports a Cusco ritual in which offerings were thrown into a river so that the Creator might receive them wherever he was—even if he were in “the sea, which they called Mamacocha.” The passage documents the sea as the terminus of a sacred hydrological route. It does not make Mama Cocha the Creator’s wife. One modern study connects this *moyucati* rite with the Vilcanota/Urubamba drainage and the ritual movement of offerings toward the sea.[^2]

#### Anonymous, *Arte y vocabulario en la lengua general del Perú*, 1586

The dictionary’s entry *Mama cocha o qucha: la mar* is especially important. It demonstrates that the expression could function as the ordinary or honorific Quechua term for the ocean. It cautions against assuming that every occurrence names a sharply individualized goddess.

#### José de Acosta, *Historia natural y moral de las Indias*, 1590

Acosta places Mamacocha among worshipped natural and celestial powers and compares it with Tethys or Neptune. His hierarchy, beginning with Viracocha, Sun, and Thunder, is a Jesuit analytical arrangement and cannot automatically be treated as the Indigenous canonical order.[^3]

#### Huarochirí Manuscript, c. 1598–1608

**Language:** Colonial Quechua.  
**Surviving support:** A unique manuscript now in the Biblioteca Nacional de España, copied in the missionary orbit of Francisco de Ávila.  
**Critical edition:** Frank Salomon and George L. Urioste, *The Huarochirí Manuscript* (1991).

The manuscript does **not** present Mama Cocha as a named, anthropomorphic protagonist. Its great marine narrative instead concerns **Urpayhuachac**, wife of Pachacamac, who keeps fish in a pond. Cuniraya throws the fish into the sea, where they multiply. This is essential negative and comparative evidence: a documented coastal fish-origin goddess must not simply be renamed Mama Cocha. The marine environment connects them; the text does not explicitly equate them.

#### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales*, 1609

Garcilaso reports broad worship of the sea along the coast from Trujillo toward Tarapacá because it supplied fish and fish fertilizer. He adds that people worshipped the whale for its immense size and that different provinces revered the species of fish most useful to them. Each species was believed to have a heavenly prototype that generated and sent its terrestrial or marine offspring.

This is the source behind the dossier’s “fish and whales” thread. It does **not** say that the whale was Mama Cocha’s mount, child, emblem, or manifestation. Whale cult and Mother Sea occur in the same coastal religious complex, but the relationship is not genealogically specified.[^4]

#### Joan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui, *Relación de antigüedades deste reino del Pirú*, c. 1613

Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s famous cosmographic drawing of the Coricancha’s claimed altar arrangement labels **mama cocha** as an enclosed body of water, opposite or paired spatially with the terrestrial field. This is the strongest colonial image associated with the name, but it is a diagram produced after conquest, not a surviving Inca portrait or a securely copied pre-Hispanic icon. The manuscript drawing is accessible in high resolution.[^5]

#### Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, c. 1615

In the “idols” chapter, Guaman Poma says that the Inca ordained that the South Sea should be worshipped and sacrificed to throughout the realm: “they called it Mama Cocha; *mama* means mother, *cocha* the sea.” He associates its particular worship with coastal *yungas* and fishermen and says their idols stood beside the sea.

Guaman Poma’s testimony supports imperial sponsorship or incorporation of marine worship, but it does not identify one empire-wide Mama Cocha temple, statue, or priesthood.

He also preserves a flood tradition in which “Mother Sea” wills to overflow. This is a colonial Andean-Christian universal history, not an independently datable pre-Inca scripture; its form may combine Indigenous flood memory, Christian chronology, and Guaman Poma’s own historical construction.

#### Pablo José de Arriaga, *Extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú*, 1621

Arriaga reports direct early-seventeenth-century practice:

> “Mamacocha, which is the Sea,” was invoked by all who descended from the highlands to the plains; on first seeing it, they asked it not to let them fall ill and to let them return quickly with health and silver from the *mita*.

Elsewhere he says travelers pulled out eyelashes and offered them toward the sea. At Huacho, people going by raft to guano islands fasted for two days, poured chicha on the beach so their rafts would not overturn, and then worshipped the local island huaca Huamancántac. These rites show a layered seascape: the sea itself, named island beings, occupational rites, and particular offerings—not a single exclusive cult.[^6]

Arriaga also records the machinery of suppression: confiscation and burning of huacas, destruction of ritual objects, punishment of specialists, compulsory catechesis, and disposal of sacred remains in the sea or rivers so communities could not recover them.

#### Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, manuscript completed 1653

Cobo synthesizes earlier chronicles, Jesuit observation, and accounts of the Cusco ceque system. His work is indispensable for Inca religion but late relative to the conquest and frequently dependent on earlier writings. Mama Cocha appears within a wider landscape of springs, lakes, rivers, canals, and shrines rather than through a developed independent narrative cycle.

### Sources named in the brief but yielding no distinct Mama Cocha cycle

Cieza de León, Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Molina, Cobo, Albornoz, and the Huarochirí traditions contain abundant creation, Titicaca, coastal, and water material. In the sources reviewed, however, none supplies a securely independent story of Mama Cocha marrying Viracocha or bearing Inti and Mama Quilla. Their silence matters.

The Huarochirí and Cajatambo records document Urpayhuachac, local water huacas, springs, lakes, rain powers, ancestors, and sacred origins. They illuminate the religious ecology in which “Mother Sea” can be understood, but local beings should not be collapsed into Mama Cocha without an explicit equation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Secure early material]**

Mama Cocha’s best-attested domains are:

- the Pacific or “South Sea” as an animate sacred being;
- provision of fish and, indirectly, fertilizer made from fish remains;
- safety or danger in marine travel;
- protection from illness for highland travelers encountering the coast;
- receipt of sacrifices carried seaward by rivers;
- a maternal or originating relation to water and marine products.

**[Later ethnographic and scholarly reconstruction]** Modern Andean cosmological studies describe seas, lakes, springs, subterranean water, rainfall, and rivers as one circulating system. Jeanette Sherbondy argued that the sea surrounds and underlies the world, feeding inland waters through subterranean routes; lakes consequently become local centers and sources. This helps explain how *Mama Qucha* can mean the Pacific in one setting and “Mother Lake” in another, but it is a reconstruction from colonial and ethnographic evidence—not a recovered Inca catechism. A recent synthesis discusses this water-axis model and Titicaca’s place within it.[^7]

### Fish

Garcilaso’s coastal testimony makes the sea maternal because it feeds people with fish. Individual communities also revered their most useful fish species. The “first” or celestial fish of each kind produced and sent its offspring for human sustenance. These fish prototypes are discrete sacred beings; Garcilaso does not state that they are children of a personified Mama Cocha, though the maternal metaphor invites that later interpretation.

The Huarochirí fish-origin story belongs explicitly to Urpayhuachac and Cuniraya. It should not be retold as a Mama Cocha myth unless clearly labeled as modern conflation.

### Whales

**[Secure colonial report]** Garcilaso says coastal people worshipped the whale “for its monstrous greatness.” No named whale, whale myth, temple, or ritual calendar is supplied. Modern claims that whales were Mama Cocha’s messengers or sacred animals are plausible imaginative extensions but are not documented by Garcilaso.

### Rain, rivers, springs, and irrigation

Early sources distinguish the sea, rivers, springs, and local lakes even while ritual links connect them. Arriaga separately lists Mamacocha, rivers, and *puquios*. Molina’s river-borne offerings demonstrate connectivity, not identity. Thus “goddess of every droplet, river, rainstorm, and well” is too categorical for the earliest corpus.

### Lake Titicaca

**[Secure early tradition concerning Titicaca]** Chroniclers including Cieza, Betanzos, Molina, Sarmiento, and Cobo identify Titicaca or Tiwanaku as a place of creation, emergence, or the appearance of sun and moon. The island and lake had major imperial sanctuaries.

**[Not securely established]** Those accounts do not consistently call Titicaca “the goddess Mama Cocha,” nor do they make the named Mama Cocha the agent who gives birth to the Sun there. *Mama Qucha/Quta Mama* can describe a revered mother-lake in present Quechua and Aymara usage, but “Titicaca is Mama Cocha” is a contextual living identification, not proof that imperial Inca theology treated the lake and Pacific as one person with one cult.

Contemporary Aymara communities do address Titicaca as **Quta Mama**, a living guardian. A 2019 ritual at Pomata offered flowers, quinoa, and coca to the lake, while a 2021 gathering at Karana sought ritual permission from Qotamama before environmental and educational work.[^8]

### Form and imagery

No securely identified pre-Hispanic anthropomorphic statue, ceramic figure, mural, textile, or temple image bears an inscription naming Mama Cocha. Consequently:

- a woman rising from waves is not automatically Mama Cocha;
- marine imagery in Moche, Chimú, Chancay, Inca, or other art cannot be assigned to her solely from subject matter;
- shells, fish, seabirds, waves, and whales are contextual marine symbols, not exclusive identifiers.

Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s c. 1613 diagram represents *mama cocha* schematically as a water enclosure. Its significance lies precisely in not supplying a human body.

### Materials and offerings

- **Fish and marine food:** the reason for coastal veneration in Garcilaso.
- **Chicha:** poured on beaches before marine journeys, according to Arriaga.
- **Eyelashes:** offered by travelers on seeing the sea.
- **Mullu/Spondylus:** marine shell offered to springs and other huacas because shells were described as daughters of the sea, “mother and origin of all waters.” The shell’s long-distance trade and archaeological distribution attest its ritual importance, but an object containing *mullu* cannot by itself be assigned to Mama Cocha.
- **River-borne offerings:** Molina reports sacrifices sent downstream toward Mamacocha.

No early source assigns her a weapon, throne, crown, sacred color, sacred number, or exclusive festival day.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy: what is and is not documented

**[Secure early material]** The principal early testimonies define Mama Cocha by place, substance, function, and ritual address—not by genealogy.

**[Unestablished modern claim]** The now-common assertion that Mama Cocha was **Viracocha’s wife and mother of Inti and Mama Quilla** could not be verified in Polo, Molina, Acosta, Garcilaso, Guaman Poma, Arriaga, the Huarochirí Manuscript, or the early dictionaries consulted. Modern webpages repeat it circularly, often without a page reference to a primary source. It should be labeled modern popular systematization unless a traceable premodern text is produced.

This genealogy likely answers a modern expectation that every “pantheon” possess a Greco-Roman-style family tree. Inca and wider Andean divine relations more often vary by place, shrine, lineage, manifestation, and political incorporation.

### Every documented named god–god interaction

Because the named corpus is unusually sparse, the list is short.

- **Creator/Hacedor — offerings may reach the Creator through Mamacocha.** Molina, *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas*, c. 1573–1575. Sacrifices cast into a river were expected to reach the Creator, even if he was then in the sea called Mamacocha. **Tier: secure colonial ritual report.** This is spatial mediation, not marriage.

- **Pachamama — paired in a catalogue of worshipped natural powers.** Acosta, *Historia natural y moral*, 1590: earth/Pachamama and sea/Mamacocha are named successively and compared to Greco-Roman powers. **Tier: colonial learned classification.** No meeting or narrative action occurs.

- **Pachamama/Kamaq Pacha — spatial counterpart in the Coricancha diagram.** Santa Cruz Pachacuti, c. 1613, places terrestrial and marine fields on corresponding sides of his cosmogram. **Tier: early-colonial Indigenous-Christian systematization.** It is a visual relation, not a marriage.

- **Inti — no direct interaction securely attested.** The sources place Sun and Sea in the same religious universe but do not narrate a meeting, parent-child exchange, contest, or alliance between them. **Finding: absence of evidence.**

- **Mama Quilla — no direct interaction securely attested.** **Finding: absence of evidence.**

- **Viracocha/Wiraqucha — no securely attested marriage or procreation with Mama Cocha.** Molina’s Creator may receive offerings through or while present in the sea, but that does not establish a conjugal pair. **Finding: popular genealogy unverified.**

- **Urpayhuachac — no explicit identification or meeting.** The Huarochirí Manuscript makes Urpayhuachac the marine-associated keeper of fish and wife of Pachacamac; it never calls her Mama Cocha. **Tier: related regional tradition, not an attested interaction.**

- **Pachacamac — no explicit interaction with Mama Cocha.** Pachacamac’s coastal sanctuary, marine setting, and marriage to Urpayhuachac encourage later conflation, but the relevant text does not name his wife Mama Cocha. **Finding: no attested tie.**

- **Cuniraya — no interaction with Mama Cocha under that name.** Cuniraya’s throwing of Urpayhuachac’s fish into the sea is an interaction with the sea as setting and with Urpayhuachac as owner. **Tier: Huarochirí regional narrative; modern retellings sometimes misassign it to Mama Cocha.**

- **Huamancántac — associated through sequential marine ritual, not narrative interaction.** At Huacho, travelers first offered chicha on the shore and later worshipped Huamancántac as lord of guano on the islands. Arriaga, 1621. **Tier: secure regional cult report.**

There is therefore no primary-source basis for a long Mama Cocha family tree. The absence is not a deficiency to repair: it is a defining feature of her documentation.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Mother Sea feeds the coast

**Earliest form:** Polo de Ondegardo, 1559; elaborated by Garcilaso, 1609.

This is less a single plot than an explanatory sacred history. Coastal people worship the sea because it supplies fish. Garcilaso makes the maternal analogy explicit: Mamacocha performs a mother’s office by feeding them. Fish remains also fertilize some coastal fields, extending the sea’s provision into agriculture.

**Development:** Later summaries turn this relationship into a benevolent goddess who “created fish,” “controls tides,” or personally patrols the ocean. The early sources say that the sea provides fish; they do not give her a creation speech or adventure.

### 5.2 The sea, whale, and heavenly fish prototypes

**Source:** Garcilaso, 1609, I.10.

Coastal peoples worship the sea generally, the whale because of its size, and locally important fish species. The prototype of each fish lives in the world above, generates its kind, and sends offspring for human sustenance.

This should be presented as a cluster of related coastal beliefs, not as a narrative in which Mama Cocha gives birth to a whale and commands an army of fish. Garcilaso’s chapter juxtaposes distinct objects of reverence.

### 5.3 Offerings travel to Mamacocha

**Source:** Molina, c. 1573–1575.

Participants cast sacrifices into the river, saying the water would carry them to the sea. The rite materializes a sacred geography connecting Cusco’s rivers with the distant ocean and the Creator’s possible location.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Sherbondy and others interpret such rites through Andean models in which the sea surrounds and underlies the land and inland waters return to it. This is persuasive comparative reconstruction, not an explicit Molina commentary.

### 5.4 The flood of Mother Sea

**Source:** Guaman Poma, c. 1615.

Guaman Poma describes a primordial age destroyed when Mother Sea resolved to overflow and rain down. The episode belongs to his synthesis of Andean eras with biblical universal history.

**Evidentiary tier:** early-colonial accretion or synthesis. It preserves an Indigenous attribution of volition to the sea but cannot safely be reconstructed as an unchanged pre-Hispanic “Mama Cocha flood myth.”

### 5.5 Urpayhuachac and the origin of sea fish

**Source:** Huarochirí Manuscript, c. 1598–1608.

Urpayhuachac keeps the only fish in a pond at her house. While she is away visiting Cahuillaca in the sea, Cuniraya violates her elder daughter and attempts to seize the younger; the younger escapes by transforming into a dove. Cuniraya throws Urpayhuachac’s fish and belongings into the ocean. The fish multiply, explaining why the sea contains them. Urpayhuachac pursues Cuniraya and tries unsuccessfully to crush him with a growing rock.

This narrative is important to Mama Cocha’s dossier because it is the fullest early marine-fertility myth in the relevant region. It is **not**, however, a Mama Cocha story in the manuscript. Urpayhuachac has a husband, daughters, home, pond, and adversary; Mama Cocha does not. The distinction demonstrates the danger of flattening local coastal beings into one modern “water goddess.”

### 5.6 Titicaca as Mother Lake and source

The colonial Titicaca cycles place emergence, creation, Sun, Moon, ancestral founders, and Inca imperial pilgrimage at or near the lake. They vary substantially among Cieza, Betanzos, Molina, Sarmiento, and Cobo. In some, the Creator produces or orders celestial beings at Tiwanaku; in others, Manco Capac or ancestral founders emerge from or are sent from the Titicaca region.

**Finding:** No reviewed version makes a securely named Mama Cocha the mother who physically bears Inti and Mama Quilla. Modern “Mama Cocha gave birth to Sun and Moon from Titicaca” narratives combine several separate traditions.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Coastal worship

Polo, Garcilaso, and Guaman Poma agree that veneration of the sea was especially characteristic of coastal peoples and fishermen. Garcilaso gives a geographical claim stretching roughly from Trujillo to Tarapacá. That should be read as a broad mestizo chronicler’s generalization, not proof of one standardized liturgy along the entire coastline.

Guaman Poma says coastal idols stood beside the sea but provides no catalogued Mama Cocha sanctuary. It is therefore unsafe to label every coastal temple a “Temple of Mama Cocha.”

### Highland engagement

Polo reports that highlanders worshipped Mamacocha even if they had never seen the ocean. Arriaga describes migrants descending to coastal *mita* labor who invoked it on first sight for health, safe return, and silver. This shows that its cultic reach was not confined to hereditary fishing communities.

### Ritual acts

Documented practices include:

- prayer or direct verbal address;
- raising the hands;
- pulling eyelashes or eyebrows and blowing or offering the hairs;
- pouring chicha on a beach;
- fasting before marine voyages;
- throwing offerings into rivers to be carried to the sea;
- worship of associated but separately named islands and marine-resource huacas;
- offerings of shells or powdered shell to springs, lakes, and other powers.

### Priests and offices

No source reviewed identifies a unique empire-wide priesthood titled “priest of Mama Cocha.” Coastal ritual specialists, fishermen, shrine custodians, local priests, and imperial religious personnel undoubtedly operated within marine cults, but the titles and jurisdictions remain local or unspecified.

### Temples and shrines

No excavated building can presently be identified by inscription as Mama Cocha’s temple. Important relevant sites include:

- the Pacific shoreline and beaches used for offerings;
- guano islands and their named huacas;
- Pachacamac and the Urpiwachaq lagoon, associated with the separately named Urpayhuachac;
- Titicaca’s islands and sanctuaries;
- Cusco’s water shrines, fountains, canals, and ceque huacas.

Brian Bauer’s reconstruction of the Cusco ceque system documents **328 huacas on 42 ceques**, many natural or hydraulic features maintained by particular social groups. It establishes the institutional density of sacred landscape but does not isolate an independent Mama Cocha establishment.[^9]

The Urpiwachaq lagoon at Pachacamac is a natural formation beside a sanctuary occupied for more than a millennium. Archaeology establishes use of its reeds, fish, and birds; textual tradition associates it with Urpayhuachac. This is evidence for a local coastal water complex, not an excavated “Mama Cocha temple.”[^10]

### Sacrifice

**Human sacrifice:** Inca *qhapaq hucha* is well documented at the imperial level, including offerings associated with mountains, rulers, crises, and major shrines. No reviewed source specifically states that a human being was sacrificed **to Mama Cocha**. Guaman Poma’s general statement that the sea was “worshipped and sacrificed to” does not identify human victims. Claims of a dedicated Mama Cocha child-sacrifice cult are therefore unestablished.

**Animal and material offerings:** Chicha, shell, coca, food, textiles, camelids, and other materials occur widely in Andean ritual. Only those explicitly attached to a marine rite should be attributed to Mama Cocha.

### Temple prostitution and cult violence

No credible evidence was found for temple prostitution in a Mama Cocha cult. “Virgins of the Sun” and *aqllakuna* belong to different institutions and should not be assimilated to a supposed sea-goddess priestesshood.

Violence surrounding the cult is better documented in the colonial suppression of Indigenous religion: searches, confiscations, public burnings, destruction of ancestral remains and ritual objects, punishment, forced instruction, and coerced denunciation.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the Inca: archaeological depth without a name

Fishing, shell exchange, guano exploitation, marine iconography, coastal pilgrimage, and sacred water landscapes long predate the Inca. Moche, Nasca, Wari, Chimú, Chincha, and other societies had distinctive marine religions.

**Methodological limit:** Archaeology cannot project the Quechua name Mama Cocha backward merely because an object depicts waves, fish, a female figure, or a sea creature. A pre-Inca “Mother Sea” concept is plausible; the named identity remains unattested.

### Fifteenth to early sixteenth centuries: Inca incorporation

The Inca state incorporated coastal peoples, shrines, fisheries, islands, and long-distance shell networks. Guaman Poma’s statement that the Inca ordered worship and sacrifice to the South Sea suggests imperial recognition of coastal marine power. This may represent incorporation rather than invention: Tawantinsuyu commonly adopted, ranked, endowed, relocated, or politically subordinated local huacas.

### 1530s–1550s: conquest and first documentation

Spanish invasion disrupted state institutions, confiscated temple wealth, and introduced Christian categories. Polo’s 1559 account records marine worship soon enough to preserve first-generation testimony, but already under colonial scrutiny.

### 1560s–1590s: lexical and missionary classification

Dictionaries codified *cocha*, *Mama cocha*, and related vocabulary. Molina and Acosta incorporated the sea into generalized accounts of “Inca rites” and “idolatry.” European comparisons transformed an animate natural being into a “goddess” comparable to classical deities.

### c. 1598–1621: local testimony and extirpation

The Huarochirí Manuscript preserved local Quechua narratives while Francisco de Ávila investigated Indigenous religion. Guaman Poma documented and criticized both Inca worship and Spanish colonial abuses. Arriaga described living invocation of the sea while prescribing systematic eradication.

Arriaga’s book reports visits beginning in 1617, seizure of thousands of sacred objects and ancestral remains, burning of huacas where possible, installation of crosses, compulsory festivals, and repeated inspections. Fixed natural huacas such as sea, mountain, or spring could not be physically removed; missionaries aimed to remove them “from the heart.”[^6]

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: persistence and Christian coexistence

Named local water powers, offerings to springs and lakes, marine occupational rites, and sacred geography persisted, often beside Catholic saints, crosses, Marian images, and feast calendars. Documentation becomes increasingly regional. It is usually impossible to prove uninterrupted institutional continuity from a specific imperial Mama Cocha cult.

Claims that Mama Cocha simply “became” the Virgin of Carmen, Stella Maris, or the Virgin of Cocharcas are too sweeping. Marian water associations and phonetic or geographic convergences can produce syncretism, but each shrine requires its own historical evidence. “Cocharcas,” for example, is a toponym and Marian cult with its own colonial history; resemblance to *cocha* does not by itself prove substitution for Mama Cocha.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries: folklore, indigenismo, and reconstruction

Ethnographers recorded lakes, springs, the sea, and subterranean water as living beings receiving reciprocal offerings. Indigenista literature and educational mythology increasingly represented Inca religion as a standardized pantheon, often giving Mama Cocha a human body and nuclear family.

Andrés Alencastre Gutiérrez, writing as **Kilku Warak’a**, addressed “Titiqaqa mama qucha” in a Quechua poem published in 1955. This is a modern Indigenous literary sacralization of Titicaca, not a recovered Inca hymn. Scholarship analyzing the poem places Mama Qucha alongside Pachamama and Mama Killa in a modern cosmological arrangement.[^11]

### Twenty-first century: living Mother Lake

Living practices are strongest as localized relations with particular waters:

- Uru communities at Lake Poopó offer an annual **white mesa** to Mama Quta in gratitude for fish, birds, and other products. One account describes sweets, herbs, and coca prepared by ritual specialists and placed in the lake near sacred underwater springs.[^12]
- Aymara communities around Titicaca address Quta Mama as a guardian and seek permission before communal and environmental work.
- Public rituals increasingly combine Indigenous reciprocity, environmental activism, tourism, municipal participation, and global “Mother Earth” language.
- In 2021 authorities and organizations around the Bolivian side of Titicaca promoted declaring **Quta Mama** a subject of rights. This is a contemporary legal-political development, not an ancient Inca statute.[^13]

“Continuous worship of the same goddess” is therefore too simple. There is demonstrable continuity in treating waters as animate mothers and recipients of offerings; names, ritual forms, social institutions, Catholic relations, and political meanings have changed.

### Argentine northwest

Quechua and Aymara water vocabulary, Pachamama rites, lake and spring beings, and Inca sacred landscapes extend into the southern Andes. The search located no comparably strong documentation of a distinct contemporary Argentine cult explicitly centered on the proper name Mama Cocha. Its presence there should be stated as regional Andean vocabulary or modern pan-Andean revival unless local ethnography documents more.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, education, and visual art

Modern children’s books, school materials, public murals, oracle decks, and digital illustrations commonly depict Mama Cocha as a blue-robed young woman surrounded by fish. These images are modern creations; they do not reproduce a securely identified Inca icon.

The name is also used for conservation projects, charities, wellness businesses, boats, restaurants, and marine cultural initiatives. A Peruvian support center opened in 2005, for example, explicitly chose “Mama Cocha” for its modern symbolism of provision and protection.[^14]

### Neopaganism, New Age practice, and occultism

Contemporary ceremonial providers invoke Mama Cocha as “the water element,” sometimes alongside Mama Nina, Mama Wayra, and Pachamama. Workshops combine New Moon rites, chakra language, shamanic branding, crystals, “divine feminine” discourse, or generic elemental magic.

**[Modern invention/synthesis]** No evidence connects Mama Cocha with Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, medieval grimoires, planetary magic, or a premodern four-element initiatory system. Modern occult practice is a legitimate modern reception but should not be advertised as an unchanged Inca liturgy.

### Genealogical reinvention

The popular family tree—Viracocha + Mama Cocha producing Inti and Mama Quilla—dominates current mythology websites and commercial summaries. Its precise origin remains unclear. A 2014 popular reference work, *Hidden Religion*, helped circulate it in English, but no colonial citation establishes it. Web repetition is not independent attestation.

Similarly unverified are stories in which:

- Mama Cocha and a brother called “Inca” descend from heaven to teach architecture;
- she is simultaneously daughter of Inti and Mama Quilla;
- she lives permanently in Hanan Pacha or Uku Pacha;
- she constitutes one of four elemental mothers;
- she belongs to a lunar trinity.

These appear to combine Mama Ocllo foundation stories, three-world teaching diagrams, Greco-Roman genealogy, and modern elemental spirituality.

### Politics and environmentalism

Mama Qucha/Quta Mama increasingly appears in water-rights campaigns, anti-pollution activism, Indigenous sovereignty discourse, and legal-personhood proposals. These uses draw on living relational understandings of water while operating within modern environmental law and transnational “rights of nature” movements.

No significant extremist appropriation specific to Mama Cocha was identified. General nationalist idealization of the Inca can employ her as part of an imagined pure, uniform ancestral pantheon, but the documentary record instead shows multilingual, regional, and historically changing traditions.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Mama Cocha a proper name or sacred common noun?

This is the central problem. The dictionaries translate *Mama cocha* as “the sea,” while chroniclers describe worship of that sea. It can therefore function simultaneously as:

1. a respectful ordinary name;
2. a title, “Mother Sea”;
3. an animate sacred being;
4. a modern proper name for a goddess.

Treating only the fourth as real imposes a modern pantheon model. Treating it only as a mundane noun ignores explicit prayer, sacrifice, and personhood.

### Inca deity or broader coastal complex?

Garcilaso presents worship along hundreds of coastal leagues. Guaman Poma attributes imperial authorization to the Inca. The likely historical process is incorporation of diverse coastal marine cults into imperial sacred geography. Yet the evidence cannot show that every coastal community called the sea Mama Cocha before Quechua imperialization.

### Mama Cocha and Urpayhuachac

Rostworowski and other scholars emphasize the importance of Urpayhuachac, Pachacamac, marine islands, fish, and female coastal powers. Similarity of domain does not establish identity. Huarochirí’s Urpayhuachac is a named local personality with a genealogy and plot; Mama Cocha in the chronicles is the sea addressed as mother. They may have overlapped ritually or been interpreted through one another, but explicit fusion is not documented.

### Mama Cocha and Titicaca

The word *qucha/quta* applies naturally to a lake, and Titicaca is demonstrably sacred. Modern people call it Mother Lake. Nevertheless, the Pacific Mamacocha of Polo, Garcilaso, and Arriaga and the imperial Titicaca sanctuary must not be silently merged. The proposition that both belong to a connected circulation of sacred water is a scholarly and ethnographic reconstruction, not proof of one ancient divine personality.

### Anthropomorphic “goddess” versus animate landscape

Older comparative scholarship often translated Andean powers into a hierarchy of gods and goddesses. More recent anthropology emphasizes *huacas*, ancestors, places, substances, reciprocal relations, and distributed agency. Mama Cocha exemplifies why “natural object” and “personified goddess” are both inadequate if treated as mutually exclusive.

### Iconographic identification

No “Mama Cocha type” has achieved scholarly consensus. Proposed female marine figures should be described by culture, date, medium, archaeological context, and visual attributes before any divine name is assigned. Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s diagram is the only early image explicitly labeled *mama cocha*, and it is schematic and colonial.

### Source bias

- Polo and Arriaga wrote within colonial governance and extirpation.
- Acosta organized Andean religion for Christian readers.
- Garcilaso defended Inca civilization while distinguishing imperial religion from what he considered local cults.
- Guaman Poma combined Indigenous political memory, Christian universal history, and polemic.
- Huarochirí testimony was collected in a campaign against “idolatry.”
- Cobo systematized earlier material a century after conquest.

Their agreements are powerful, but none is a transparent transcript of pre-Hispanic belief.

### Major scholarly contributions

- **Rostworowski:** coastal religion, Pachacamac, Urpayhuachac, marine economies, islands, and political integration.
- **Zuidema:** structural and calendrical readings of Cusco’s ceque system.
- **Bauer:** archaeological and archival remapping of the ceque system, correcting overly abstract reconstructions.
- **Duviols:** publication and analysis of extirpation records, including Cajatambo.
- **Salomon and Urioste:** critical transcription, translation, and contextualization of the Huarochirí Manuscript.
- **Sherbondy:** water, irrigation, ancestors, social organization, and the sea–lake–spring circulation model.
- **MacCormack:** colonial transformations of Andean religion and the conceptual effects of Christian/classical comparison.
- **Silverblatt:** gender, imperial organization, and colonial transformation.
- **Urton:** Andean cosmology and astronomical/spatial organization.

These scholars illuminate Mama Cocha’s context far more extensively than her sparse named corpus. A responsible dossier must distinguish contextual reconstruction from direct attestation.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Before c. 1400 | Archaeology demonstrates ancient fishing, marine pilgrimage, shell exchange, guano use, water ritual, and marine iconography along the Andes. No inscription securely names Mama Cocha. |
| Fifteenth–early sixteenth centuries | Inca expansion incorporates coastal societies, marine resources, islands, and water shrines. Later Guaman Poma attributes empire-wide authorization of South Sea worship to the Inca. |
| 1530s | Spanish conquest begins the destruction, seizure, translation, and Christian reclassification of Andean religious institutions. |
| **1559** | Polo de Ondegardo drafts his *Tratado y averiguación*, the earliest secure named testimony: Mamacocha in Quechua, Mama Cota in Aymara; coastal and highland worship of the sea. |
| **1560** | Santo Tomás defines *cocha* as sea, lake, and pond, establishing the early colonial semantic range. |
| c. **1573–1575** | Molina reports offerings cast into rivers to reach the sea called Mamacocha. |
| **1586** | Anonymous Quechua dictionary prints *Mama cocha o qucha: la mar*. |
| **1590** | Acosta lists worship of Mamacocha and compares it to Tethys or Neptune. |
| c. **1598–1608** | Huarochirí Manuscript records Urpayhuachac’s fish-origin myth but does not identify her as Mama Cocha. |
| **1608** | González Holguín’s vocabulary records *ccocha* and *Mama cocha*. |
| **1609** | Garcilaso explains Mother Sea as provider of fish and reports whale and fish-prototype worship. |
| **1612** | Bertonio records Aymara *Mama cota* for “great sea.” |
| c. **1613** | Santa Cruz Pachacuti labels *mama cocha* in his Coricancha cosmogram. |
| c. **1615** | Guaman Poma describes Inca authorization of worship and sacrifice to the South Sea and records a Mother Sea flood tradition. |
| **1617–1621** | Arriaga participates in extirpation campaigns, records living invocation of Mamacocha, and publishes his manual in 1621. |
| **1653** | Cobo completes *Historia del Nuevo Mundo*, synthesizing Inca religion and Cusco’s sacred landscape. |
| Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries | Water, lake, spring, and marine practices persist under extirpation and alongside Catholic saints, crosses, and Marian cults; direct institutional continuity is unevenly documented. |
| Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries | Folklore collection, archaeology, and indigenismo recast Andean powers as members of a standardized “Inca pantheon.” |
| **1939** | Hermann Trimborn publishes the first complete translation of the Huarochirí material, making its coastal-water narratives widely available to scholarship. |
| **1955** | Kilku Warak’a publishes a modern Quechua poem addressing “Titiqaqa mama qucha.” |
| **1960s–1990s** | Zuidema, Rostworowski, Duviols, Sherbondy, MacCormack, Silverblatt, Urton, Salomon, Urioste, and Bauer reshape understanding of Andean landscape, water, gender, regional cults, and colonial transmission. |
| **1991** | Salomon and Urioste publish the major English critical edition and translation of the Huarochirí Manuscript. |
| **1998** | Bauer publishes *The Sacred Landscape of the Inca*, synthesizing archaeological and archival study of Cusco’s ceque system. |
| **2005** | A Peruvian social-service center adopts Mama Cocha as a modern symbol of nurture and protection. |
| **2010s** | Online mythology and occult literature widely circulate the unverified Viracocha marriage, elemental-mother system, and fixed anthropomorphic imagery. |
| **2019** | Public ritual at Pomata offers flowers, quinoa, and coca to Titicaca as Mama Quta in an environmental context. |
| **2021** | Karana community gathering seeks Qotamama’s permission for environmental work; Bolivian lakeside organizations promote Quta Mama as a legal subject. |
| Present | Mama Qucha/Quta Mama remains living language and ritual address for particular waters, especially Titicaca and Poopó, while “Mama Cocha” also functions globally as a reconstructed Inca goddess, environmental emblem, commercial name, and New Age water archetype. |

## 11. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

The securely reconstructable Mama Cocha is at once simpler and more historically significant than the elaborate modern goddess:

- The earliest record knows **the sea itself as Mother Sea**, prayed to and offered to.
- Coastal subsistence, fish, fertilizer, whales, voyages, guano islands, and local fish powers form her best-attested religious environment.
- Rivers could carry offerings to her, but early sources still distinguish sea, river, spring, and lake powers.
- Titicaca is an ancient sacred lake and a living Quta Mama, but its wholesale identification with the Pacific Mamacocha is not textually secure for the Inca period.
- No securely identified ancient statue, portrait, exclusive temple, specialist priesthood, calendar festival, or fixed genealogy survives.
- Urpayhuachac is a related but separately named coastal goddess and must not be silently absorbed.
- Viracocha as husband and Inti and Mama Quilla as children are unverified modern systematization.
- The “four elemental mothers,” lunar trinity, blue-robed form, and occult correspondences are modern inventions or syntheses.
- Living Mother Lake rituals are documented, but they represent historical continuity through transformation rather than an unchanged imperial institution.
- No evidence was found for temple prostitution, a Mama Cocha-specific human sacrifice, a premodern occult order, or extremist cult appropriation.
