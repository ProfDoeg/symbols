# Tartalo: Research Dossier

**Class:** folkloric monster—specifically a one-eyed anthropophagous giant, ogre, or cyclops; sometimes assimilated to a *jentil* or *Basajaun*, but not securely attested as a god.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** Basque-language oral narratives collected principally in Labourd by Wentworth Webster in the early 1870s and published in *Basque Legends* in 1877. Jean‑François Cerquand’s approximately contemporary collections began appearing in 1874–1875; Antoine d’Abbadie published a Souletin *Légende du Tartaroa ou Tartarua* in 1878. No ancient, Roman-period, medieval, epigraphic, archaeological, or iconographic attestation of Tartalo is presently known.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Classification

Tartalo belongs to the modern-recorded oral folklore of the Basque-speaking country on both sides of the Pyrenees. In the most characteristic narratives he is an enormous man with one eye in the middle of his forehead, living in a cave or isolated house, keeping sheep, capturing humans, and eating them. A prisoner blinds him with a heated spit and escapes among sheep or beneath a sheepskin; in several Basque forms, a supernatural ring betrays the fugitive by crying out his location.

This is a folktale figure rather than a documented cult-being. Webster’s informants identified Tartaro promptly as “the man with one eye in the middle of his forehead,” while his collected narratives also use the word for ordinary giants or ogres. The modern classification “Basque Cyclops” is therefore accurate typologically but does not make Tartalo a member of the Greek race of Cyclopes.

### Documented names

- **Tartaro / Tartaroa** — the usual form in nineteenth-century French-Basque collections. Basque final *-a* is normally the definite article, so *Tartaroa* can mean “the Tartaro.”
- **Tartarua** — dialectal spelling in the title of d’Abbadie’s 1878 Souletin version.
- **Tartare** — Cerquand’s French rendering.
- **Tartalo** — localized principally in the Zegama tradition recorded from Andrés Arrieta in 1920. It later became the most familiar standardized name in Spanish- and English-language summaries.
- **Torto** — a regional Basque name grouped with the same cyclopean figure by Barandiarán.
- **Alarabi** — reported especially around Markina; its equivalence with Tartalo is classificatory rather than proof that every Alarabi narrative originally concerned the same being.
- **Anxo / Antxo / Ancho** — inconsistently applied. Some catalogues treat it as a Tartalo name, while other traditions apply it to Basajaun. Webster speculated that *Ancho* might derive from the personal name Sancho; this remains conjectural.
- **Tartari** — an ogre or malignant being recorded in the Agenais north of Gascony; Jean‑François Bladé reported the phrase “wicked as Tartari” and a female *Tartarino*. This is a comparative regional analogue, not automatically the Basque Tartaro.
- **Moiriak** — a Souletin comparative form invoked by Webster in discussing Cerquand’s etymology, but not a normal name for the cyclops throughout the Basque country.

### Etymology

**[Nineteenth-century scholarly reconstruction]** Cerquand derived *Tartaro* from “Tartar,” comparing the supposed development of French *ogre* from *Hongrois/Ugri*. Webster regarded this as plausible but noted the chronological difficulty: western Europeans encountered the historical Tatars only in the thirteenth century. The derivation could explain a relatively late ogre-name but cannot demonstrate an ancient Basque myth.

**[Unestablished popular etymology]** Association with Greek **Tartaros**, the underworld, is based on sound resemblance. No historical chain of transmission or early Basque text establishes it.

**[Unestablished]** No accepted derivation from inherited Basque vocabulary has been demonstrated. The alternation *Tartaro/Tartalo/Torto* may reflect dialect, oral transmission, and collectors’ orthographies, but it does not by itself settle the word’s origin.

### What Tartalo is not securely shown to be

There is no evidence that Tartalo was:

- a pre-Roman Iberian or Celtic god;
- a recipient of sacrifice, prayer, or votive inscriptions;
- named in Strabo, Pliny, Martial, or another classical author;
- represented in an ancient Iberian image;
- identical with Endovelicus, Ataecina, or any other epigraphically attested deity;
- part of an ancient organized “Basque pantheon.”

The Latin inscriptions and sanctuaries of Endovelicus and Ataecina are essential evidence for ancient western Iberian religion, but they do not mention Tartalo. Their inclusion in an alleged Tartalo cult would be a modern synthetic construction.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest recoverable record

**[Secure early folklore documentation]** Webster gathered tales chiefly near Saint‑Jean‑de‑Luz in Labourd during the early 1870s. He stated that he sought narrators who knew Basque alone, or who had known only Basque when they learned the stories, but also warned that the resulting repertoire was visibly intermixed with French and Spanish material. Published in London in 1877, *Basque Legends: Collected, Chiefly in the Labourd* is the earliest readily verifiable substantial Tartaro corpus.

Webster’s introductory claim that Tartaro, Heren‑Suge, Basa‑Jaun, and the *lamiñak* were familiar to essentially every Basque peasant he questioned is valuable testimony for nineteenth-century circulation. It is not evidence that the stories had survived unchanged from antiquity.

**[Probable slightly earlier publication]** Cerquand, an educational inspector, had schoolteachers in Soule and Lower Navarre record Basque oral narratives. The installments of *Légendes et récits populaires du Pays Basque* appeared between 1874 and 1883. Cerquand himself cautioned that the prescribed conditions for exact transcription and translation might not always have been followed. His collection is therefore primary folklore evidence but mediated by teacher-collectors and editorial translation.

**[Secure early folklore documentation]** D’Abbadie’s *Légende du Tartaroa ou Tartarua*, published in the *Bulletin de la Société des Sciences, Lettres et Arts de Bayonne* in 1878, came through Canon Inchauspe from Abbé Heguiagary of Esquiule in Soule. D’Abbadie also remembered hearing stories of *tartaroak* in the plural during his childhood.

### Webster’s Tartaro corpus

The 1877 volume contains:

1. **“The Tartaro” / d’Abbadie’s version and variants.** A captive blinds the one-eyed giant with a red-hot spit, attempts escape among the sheep, receives or is thrown a speaking ring, amputates his finger, and escapes.
2. **“Errua, the Madman.”** A supposedly foolish hero defeats Tartaro in contests through tricks involving a bird substituted for a thrown stone, thread seemingly encircling every oak, and other feats.
3. **“The Three Brothers, the Cruel Master, and the Tartaro.”** Tartaro functions as one dangerous episode in an international wonder-tale complex.
4. **“The Tartaro and Petit Perroquet.”** The hero repeatedly steals from or deceives Tartaro and ultimately captures him through disguise.
5. **“The Grateful Tartaro and the Heren‑Suge.”** A released Tartaro becomes a supernatural helper, supplying a prince with the means to defeat a seven-headed serpent and establish his identity.
6. **Variant involving three Tartaros.** A prince kills three card-playing Tartaros and takes their riches, horses, and three intelligent messenger-beasts called *olano*.

The corpus proves that *Tartaro* was not invariably one unique proper name. It could designate a class of beings, since tales contain multiple Tartaros. Nor was every Tartaro necessarily one-eyed, a shepherd, or permanently hostile.

### Twentieth-century Basque documentation

- **José María de Auzmendi, Ataun, 1910:** “The Gentil of Muskia,” recorded by Barandiarán. A *jentil* confines a Christian with a speaking ring. The prisoner hides under sheepskins, cuts off the ring-bearing finger with a stone, and throws it into the Mikolalde pool; the pursuing giant jumps after the voice and drowns. The surviving summary does not require that this giant have one eye.
- **Andrés Arrieta, Zegama, 1920:** the fullest localized “Tartalo” account. Tartalo lives at Tartaloetxeta on Mount Saadar, has one eye in his forehead, captures two brothers, roasts and eats one, rings the survivor, is blinded with the heated spit, and drowns following the severed finger and ring into a well.
- **Resurrección María de Azkue, *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*, vol. II:** includes Tartalo material and helped transmit it into the major twentieth-century folklore corpus.
- **José Miguel de Barandiarán, *El mundo en la mente popular vasca* (1960), *Mitología vasca*, and the *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca* (1972):** synthesized collected variants, names, localizations, and comparative observations.
- **Juan Inazio Hartsuaga:** later comparative scholarship, including the Auñamendi entry and *Mitología vasca comparada* (2011), distinguishes the cyclopean shepherd from the partially overlapping giant, *jentil*, and Basajaun traditions.

There is no ancient manuscript tradition. The interval between possible formation of the tale and its nineteenth-century recording cannot presently be measured.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Functions and habitat

**[Folklore]** Tartalo is not said to govern a cosmic domain. His narrative “domain” is the remote mountain, cave, sheepfold, or forest margin—the dangerous space beyond the Christian village. He is hunter, shepherd, captor, cannibal, and owner of extraordinary animals or treasure.

**[Regional variant]** In the Zegama narrative he eats wild animals and humans rather than being solely a pastoral specialist. In Webster’s tales Tartaro may own sheep, pigs, horses, riches, or a house. In “The Grateful Tartaro,” he changes from captive monster to loyal helper.

### Form

The most stable diagnostic feature is:

- gigantic human form;
- one eye in the center of the forehead;
- prodigious physical strength.

Less stable attributes include:

- anthropophagy;
- solitary residence;
- sheep herding;
- inability to reason through elementary tricks;
- possession of a speaking or tracking ring;
- ownership of a wife, mother, or supernatural servants;
- extraordinary stone-throwing.

Tartalo is generally male in grammatical and narrative presentation, but the tales supply no theological account of sex, species, creation, or immortality.

### Objects

- **Red-hot spit:** the blinding weapon in the cyclops cycle.
- **Speaking ring:** cries *Heben nuk!* in Webster’s Labourd text—glossed there as “Thou hast me here”—or “aquí estoy” in Spanish summaries of the Ataun and Zegama traditions. Modern retellings often give standardized Basque *Hemen nago*.
- **Sheep, ram, bell, or sheepskin:** facilitate escape.
- **Cave-closing stone:** present in several retellings and characteristic of the international Polyphemus tale.
- **Iron bar, stones, uprooted trees:** signs of strength in ogre-outwitting narratives.
- **Horses, sword, perfumed water, and *olano*:** gifts or aids in Webster’s grateful-helper cycle.

### Iconographic record

No ancient or medieval Tartalo image has been identified. The familiar modern image—a huge hairy cyclops with shepherd’s crook or sheep—is an illustration of collected narrative traits, not inherited cult iconography. A modern sculpture at the Izenaduba Basoa attraction in Mungia exemplifies this recent visualization.

No traditional sacred colors, numbers, weekdays, planets, plants, ritual emblems, or consecrated animals are securely documented. Sheep are narrative livestock, not demonstrated sacred animals.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

Tartalo has no stable genealogy. No collected source names divine parents, siblings, consort, or offspring.

Some versions mention:

- **an unnamed mother**, who gives or helps deploy the speaking ring;
- **an unnamed wife**, in ogre-household variants;
- **multiple Tartaros**, showing that the term can designate a species or class;
- **three *olano***, intelligent dog-like servants in one Webster variant.

Assertions that Tartalo is the son of a Basque deity, belongs to Mari’s court, or descends from Greek Cyclopes are unattested modern systematizations.

### Every documented interaction with another named supernatural being

- **Heren‑Suge — alliance by proxy:** in Webster’s “The Grateful Tartaro and the Heren‑Suge” (collected early 1870s; published 1877), Tartaro equips and advises Petit Yorge for combat against the seven-headed serpent. Tartaro and Heren‑Suge do not conduct a direct divine meeting or battle; their relationship is mediated through the human hero. **Tier: securely documented nineteenth-century folklore.**
- **Basajaun/Basa‑Jaun — no discrete interaction attested:** collectors and later classifiers sometimes overlap or confuse the two figures, but no checked early narrative presents a named Tartalo meeting a named Basajaun. **Tier: later classificatory assimilation, not narrative interaction.**
- **Jentil — identity substitution rather than interaction:** the Ataun ring story assigns Tartalo-like actions to “the Gentil of Muskia.” Tartalo and the *jentil* do not meet; one figure occupies the other’s narrative role. **Tier: regional variant recorded 1910.**
- **Polyphemus — comparative identification only:** no Basque tale narrates Tartalo meeting Polyphemus. Their relationship is typological and historiographical. **Tier: modern scholarly comparison.**
- **Balor, Bécut, Ulhart, Ojáncanu, Alarabi — no documented meetings:** these are comparative one-eyed giants or regional analogues, not participants in shared traditional episodes.

This extraordinarily short list is itself significant: Tartalo is not embedded in a densely interacting pantheon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The blinding of the cannibal shepherd

**[Secure nineteenth-century folklore]** A young man falls into Tartaro’s power. Tartaro consumes a sheep and is expected to eat the captive next. While the giant sleeps, the prisoner heats a spit and drives it into Tartaro’s single eye. The blinded giant partially opens the enclosure and feels the sheep as they pass between his legs. The hero removes the ram’s bell, covers himself in the eaten sheep’s skin, and goes on all fours. Tartaro detects or nearly detects the disguise, but the youth slips free.

In Webster’s version, Tartaro’s mother then presents the apparently helpful ring. It immediately cries *Heben nuk! Heben nuk!* and guides the pursuit. Because it cannot be removed, the fugitive cuts off his finger and throws it away. Variant endings have him cast finger and ring down a precipice or into a bog, causing Tartaro to fall or drown.

The ring is absent from Homer’s *Odyssey*. Webster observed parallels in Celtic story and confessed that he could not interpret its significance. Modern summaries that call it “Tartalo’s defining ancient magical artifact” exceed the evidence: it is an important but not universal folktale motif.

### B. The two brothers of Zegama

**[Regional folklore; recorded 1920]** Two brothers approach Tartalo’s dwelling at Tartaloetxeta on Mount Saadar. Tartalo imprisons them, skewers one brother, roasts him over the fire, and eats him. He places a speaking ring on the survivor and sleeps.

The surviving brother heats Tartalo’s own spit and puts out the monster’s sole eye. He conceals himself under a heap of sheepskins. Tartalo stands at the doorway and throws the skins outside one at a time, unknowingly expelling the prisoner. The youth runs down Saadar, but the ring guides Tartalo. The fugitive tears off the finger and throws it into a well. Tartalo follows the voice and drowns.

This is the source that most firmly connects the name **Tartalo**, the single eye, anthropophagy, blinding, skins, ring, and Zegama landscape.

### C. The Gentil of Muskia

**[Regional folklore; recorded 1910]** A *jentil* living in a cave on Muskia mountain captures a Christian and rings him so that the ring continually calls “Here I am.” The Christian hides among sheepskins and escapes when the giant goes outside to investigate the apparently distant voice. Pursued toward Mikolalde, he cuts off his finger with a stone and throws it and the ring into the pool. The *jentil* jumps in and drowns.

There is no blinding in the surviving report and the monster is called *jentil*, not Tartalo. This shows a mobile tale-complex whose components could be assigned to different giant figures.

### D. Tartaro outwitted by the fool

**[Secure nineteenth-century folklore]** In Webster’s “Errua, the Madman,” the supposed fool defeats Tartaro in strength contests through verbal and practical tricks:

- he substitutes a bird for a stone so that his “stone” never lands;
- threatens to throw an iron bar to Salamanca, where Tartaro says his parents live;
- winds thread around the trees as if preparing to uproot the whole forest;
- survives an attempted night killing by placing a corpse in his bed.

This is the international “strong giant outwitted by weak trickster” complex rather than a uniquely cyclopean myth. Tartaro here behaves as a gullible ogre and need not perform the cannibal-shepherd episode.

### E. Petit Perroquet and the captured Tartaro

**[Secure nineteenth-century folklore]** Petit Perroquet is sent repeatedly to obtain dangerous objects from Tartaro. He hides in ferns, steals what is demanded, and finally disguises himself as an infernal being—“the oldest of all the devils in hell”—to terrify and capture the giant. The narrative belongs to international quest and ogre-outwitting traditions.

### F. The Grateful Tartaro

**[Secure nineteenth-century folklore]** A king captures Tartaro and imprisons him in a stable. The king’s son frees him while retrieving a ball. When the prince is later exiled or endangered, the grateful monster provides horses, clothing, weapons, and the means to confront Heren‑Suge. Here Tartaro is powerful, reciprocal, and morally capable of gratitude, not an invariably evil cannibal.

### G. The Polyphemus problem: borrowing or cognate tradition?

The correspondences with *Odyssey* 9 are substantial:

- isolated giant shepherd;
- cave and flock;
- eating of human captives;
- blinding with heated wood or iron;
- exit controlled by touching livestock;
- escape beneath an animal or skin.

Important differences include:

- the speaking ring;
- finger amputation;
- Tartalo’s fall or drowning;
- frequent absence of Odysseus’s “Nobody” name-ruse;
- Christian-versus-*jentil* framing;
- transfer of the cycle to fools, brothers, or anonymous youths.

**Direct borrowing hypothesis.** Basque storytellers could have received the episode through classical education, clerical preaching, translated literature, Mediterranean oral tradition, or later European tale circulation. Because the Basque texts were not recorded until the nineteenth century—and collectors acknowledged French and Spanish admixture—direct or indirect postclassical borrowing cannot be excluded.

**Independent oral-tradition hypothesis.** The Polyphemus story is internationally distributed in hundreds of versions. Comparative scholarship generally treats Homer’s narrative itself as a literary reshaping of an older folktale, conventionally classified as ATU 1137, “The Blinded Ogre.” The Basque ring motif has no Homeric counterpart and is shared with other nonclassical traditions. Thus the Basque story need not derive directly from Homer.

**D’Abbadie and Barandiarán’s position.** D’Abbadie invoked a similar tale he heard from an Amharic-speaking Muslim while travelling toward Oromo country in 1843 and argued for a story older than Homer and the separation of peoples. Barandiarán likewise rejected simple Homeric derivation.

**Current defensible conclusion.** The Basque narratives belong to the international “Blinded Ogre” family. Their direct descent from Homer is unproved; their independent preservation from a Paleolithic or prehistoric common ancestor is also unproved. Phylogenetic attempts to reconstruct a Paleolithic ATU 1137 prototale are modern models dependent on coding, sample selection, and assumptions about oral transmission. They cannot date the Basque name *Tartalo* or demonstrate continuity in the Basque country.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No cult of Tartalo is documented.

There are no known:

- temples, altars, shrines, priests, or sacred offices;
- prayers, hymns, sacrifices, votive gifts, pilgrimages, or festivals;
- theophoric personal names;
- magical invocations or grimoires;
- Roman inscriptions;
- medieval ecclesiastical condemnations naming Tartalo;
- archaeological deposits that can securely be attributed to his worship.

### Tartaloetxeta and the Zegama landscape

*Tartaloetxeta* means approximately “place of Tartalo’s house.” On the heights of Saadar near Zegama, the supposed *Tartaloetxe* is identified with a dolmen. This is evidence of folklore attaching a giant’s house to a prehistoric monument, not evidence that the dolmen was built for or used in a Tartalo cult.

Such attribution belongs to a widespread European pattern in which megaliths and unusual stones are explained as the work or dwellings of giants, Moors, *jentilak*, fairies, or saints. The place-name’s antiquity has not been securely established before the collection of the legend.

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

The stories graphically describe murder, roasting, and cannibalism. These are narrative acts by the monster, not evidence of ritual human sacrifice to Tartalo. No archaeological or textual source documents sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult warfare, political priesthood, or persecution conducted in his name.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the nineteenth century

No identifiable Tartalo record survives. The international tale-pattern is demonstrably ancient because *Odyssey* 9 was composed in archaic Greece and comparable folktales are geographically widespread, but this does not date a Basque Tartalo tradition.

### 1843: comparative observation

D’Abbadie heard a similar blinded-ogre story in eastern Africa. This later shaped his argument that the cycle was pre-Homeric and international.

### 1870s–1880s: documentation

Cerquand, Webster, d’Abbadie, and Vinson transformed oral stories into a printed corpus. Their work fixed variant spellings and introduced explicit comparisons with Polyphemus, Celtic tales, and Indo-European mythology.

The period also created unavoidable mediation:

- narrators were usually identified by location or personal name, but not recorded audiovisually;
- translations passed through French or English;
- collectors selected and arranged tale types;
- Webster admitted “softening” direct or coarse expression;
- Cerquand doubted whether every teacher had transcribed with complete fidelity.

### Early twentieth century: Spanish-Basque localization

Barandiarán’s fieldwork documented the Muskia story in Ataun in 1910 and the Zegama Tartalo story in 1920. These records rooted the cycle in specific landscapes, pools, caves, and megalithic sites. The Zegama account helped make *Tartalo* the dominant modern name.

### Mid-twentieth century: mythographic systematization

Azkue and Barandiarán incorporated the narratives into reference works on Basque belief. In this process Tartalo increasingly appeared as one named “being of Basque mythology,” although the primary tales often treat *tartaro* as a class noun and vary its traits considerably.

### Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries: heritage figure

Tartalo became a recognizable emblem of Basque fantastic folklore in children’s books, illustration, tourism, sculpture, animation, festivals, and mythological dictionaries. The modern composite—hairy cyclops, shepherd’s crook, cave, sheep, cannibalism, and magic ring—draws from several variants rather than any single canonical text.

### Christianization and suppression

The tales contrast “Christian” captives with *jentil* or man-eating outsiders, showing that they circulated in an already Christian social world. That language can reflect retrospective Christian classification of a dangerous non-Christian “other.” No record documents an organized church campaign against Tartalo worship, because no such worship is attested.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Folklore and children’s media

The 1985 Basque animated film *Kalabaza tripontzia* includes the cyclops Tartalo and his speaking ring. Later educational retellings give protagonists such as Patxi or Antimuño names that are absent from some early variants, simplify the narrative, and standardize modern Basque dialogue.

### Crime fiction and screen adaptations

Dolores Redondo’s Baztán trilogy uses **Tarttalo** as an ominous symbolic name; the film *Legado en los huesos* carries this usage into screen culture. This Tarttalo is a modern literary appropriation, not a recovered traditional cult narrative.

### Public culture

The Tartalo International Week of the Fantastic in the Arts in Vitoria‑Gasteiz uses the monster’s name as a regional emblem for fantasy art. By 2025 it was holding its third edition, and a 2026 program and interactive-fiction competition continued the brand. This is cultural commemoration, not revived worship.

The Izenaduba Basoa attraction at Mungia presents a sculptural Tartalo among figures of Basque mythology. Such works have materially stabilized his appearance despite the absence of traditional iconography.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated searches found no substantiated Tartalo tradition in Renaissance occultism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, historical grimoires, or twentieth-century initiatory orders. Nor was a continuous Tartalo cult or an organized reconstructionist priesthood established.

Individual modern practitioners may incorporate any folkloric figure into personal spirituality, but isolated personal practice would not establish ancient cult continuity. Websites that list Tartalo as a “deity” without primary evidence represent modern pantheon-building.

### National and political use

Tartalo is often presented as proof of an immemorial, uniquely pre-Indo-European Basque religion. The Basque language may indeed preserve a deep non-Indo-European linguistic inheritance, but that fact does not date every recorded Basque folktale. Applying “prehistoric Basque god” to Tartalo is a modern identity claim unsupported by the documentary chronology.

No sustained extremist appropriation, political cult, or violence committed under Tartalo’s name was identified.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Collectors and foundational works

- **Jean‑François Cerquand:** organized schoolteacher collection in Soule and Lower Navarre; proposed the Tatar-name etymology and compared tales with international material.
- **Wentworth Webster:** provided the first large, accessible Tartaro corpus and unusually candid methodological warnings.
- **Antoine d’Abbadie:** supplied a Souletin version and the eastern-African comparison.
- **Julien Vinson:** contributed linguistic study and helped publish and contextualize Basque folklore.
- **Azkue:** assembled a broad Basque ethnographic and linguistic collection.
- **Barandiarán:** recorded place-specific Spanish-Basque variants and created the dominant twentieth-century synthesis.
- **Hartsuaga:** emphasizes comparative structure and the need to distinguish overlapping giant traditions.

### Central disputes

1. **Is Tartalo one being or a class?**  
   Modern dictionaries often treat him as a singular named monster. Early tales use plural Tartaros and give substantially different roles. The evidence favors a flexible type or class that sometimes becomes an individualized character.

2. **Cyclops or ogre?**  
   Webster insisted that Basque informants recognized Tartaro first as a one-eyed cyclops, while admitting that tales also substitute him for the generic giant or ogre. Both uses are documented.

3. **Direct Homeric borrowing or independent folktale?**  
   Direct borrowing remains possible but unproved. The tale’s international distribution and non-Homeric ring motif argue against treating the Basque text as merely a corrupted classroom retelling of the *Odyssey*. Conversely, nineteenth-century collection is too late to prove independent prehistoric continuity.

4. **Basajaun, *jentil*, Alarabi, or Tartalo?**  
   Narrative roles migrate. A story can be assigned to a *jentil* in Ataun and to Tartalo in Zegama. This is evidence of folkloric interchange, not proof that all names originally denoted one god.

5. **Solar interpretation.**  
   Webster’s phrase “the Cyclops, the sun’s round eye” belongs to nineteenth-century solar-myth interpretation. No Basque ritual or primary testimony establishes Tartalo as a sun god.

6. **Paleolithic reconstruction.**  
   Statistical and phylogenetic studies have proposed very ancient ancestry for the Polyphemus tale. These studies address a reconstructed international narrative type, not the antiquity of the Basque name, Zegama localization, or ring episode. Presenting their results as proof of a Paleolithic Tartalo cult is unwarranted.

### Evidence conservation

The core evidence survives in printed collections, later reprints, digital scans, the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore*, and Barandiarán’s collected works. The absence of nineteenth-century sound recordings means pronunciation, performance, gesture, and some dialectal detail are irrecoverable. Editorial translation must always be considered part of the transmission.

## 11. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

The secure documentary history of Tartalo begins with modern folklore collection, not antiquity. The richest early corpus is Webster’s 1877 *Basque Legends*, supplemented by Cerquand, d’Abbadie, Azkue, and Barandiarán. These sources establish a vigorous oral figure with several forms:

- the one-eyed cannibal shepherd;
- the blinded ogre pursued by his own speaking ring;
- the giant defeated by a “fool” through tricks;
- the dangerous owner of treasure;
- and, exceptionally, the grateful supernatural helper.

They do not establish a god, cult, genealogy, priesthood, temple, sacrifice, or pre-Roman continuity.

The Zegama story is genuine documented folklore, but its documentation dates to Andrés Arrieta’s 1920 testimony. Tartaloetxeta’s dolmen is a prehistoric object subsequently interpreted through legend; it is not an excavated Tartalo sanctuary.

The Polyphemus parallel is real and exact enough to require analysis. The evidence supports membership in the international “Blinded Ogre” tale family. It does not decide conclusively between a very old shared oral inheritance and one or more episodes of historical diffusion. The responsible finding is therefore double: Tartalo is more than a modern copy of Homer, but claims that he is a Paleolithic or pre-Roman Basque deity go beyond what the evidence can bear.
