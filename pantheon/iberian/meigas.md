# The Meigas: Research Dossier

**Class:** **collective human/supernatural witch-figure and category of magical practitioner in Galician folklore**; sometimes a feared neighbor, healer, diviner, or alleged servant of the Devil, and sometimes a wholly legendary shape-shifting night-being. The sources do **not** establish the Meigas as goddesses, a divine collective, or members of a pre-Roman Iberian pantheon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** practices later associated with meigas—divination, sortilege, augury, and incantation—are documented in the Compostela synod of **17 August 1289**, but the text does not use *meiga*. The earliest presently verified lexical attestations found in the consulted historical dictionaries are nineteenth-century; *meiga chuchona* is securely printed in Rosalía de Castro’s *Cantares gallegos* in **1863**. No ancient inscription, classical author, pre-Roman image, or medieval source yet identified attests a deity or supernatural race called “the Meigas.”

**Evidentiary conclusion:** placing “The Meigas” in a “Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician, pre-Roman pantheon” is a **modern learned systematization**, not an ancient classification. The documented tradition is overwhelmingly Galician and early-modern-to-modern. Basque *sorginak* and Asturian/Cantabrian *bruxas*, *guaxas*, or magical practitioners are comparative neighbors, not regional names for a historically demonstrated pan-Iberian being.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

A *meiga* is, in the central Galician usage, a woman credited with extraordinary or magical efficacy. Depending on speaker and context she may be:

- a malicious witch who produces illness, infertility, crop or livestock losses, bad luck, or *mal de ollo*;
- an alleged human practitioner prosecuted as a *hechicera*, diviner, or curer;
- a healer or “wise woman” capable of diagnosing and undoing magical harm;
- a legendary night-being who flies, changes shape, sucks blood or vitality, or attends diabolical assemblies;
- figuratively, an alluring, uncanny, clever, or unpleasant woman;
- in modern heritage discourse, a benign symbol of Galician mystery, independence, femininity, or folk wisdom.

These meanings overlap but are not interchangeable. Carmelo Lisón Tolosana’s fieldwork-based analysis emphasizes the local semantic distinction among *meiga*, *bruxa*, *sabia*, and curer, as well as the instability of those categories. His informants’ statements associate the harmful meiga with envy, domination, illness, loss of health, damage to children, animals, crops, and fishing. That ethnography concerns twentieth-century belief and speech, not an unchanged survival from antiquity. [Lisón Tolosana, *Brujería, estructura social y simbolismo en Galicia*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Brujer%C3%ADa_estructura_social_y_simbolismo.html?id=56hmbdTrvN0C)

### Name and etymology

- **Galician:** *meiga*, plural *meigas*.
- **Masculine:** *meigo*, less culturally dominant but linguistically regular.
- **Related forms:** *meigar/enmeigar*, “to bewitch”; *meigallo*, an act or condition of bewitchment; *meigaría/meiguería*, witchcraft.
- **Portuguese and border speech:** cognate or borrowed *meiga* occurs, especially in northern traditions.
- **Castilian:** normally *bruja* or *hechicera*, though *meiga* is borrowed when the specifically Galician figure is meant.
- **Chiloé:** *meica* designates a healer in southern Chile, but its colonial and intercultural history must not be projected backward into Galicia.

The Real Academia Galega’s lexical explanation derives *meiga* through Latin *magicus, -a, -um*, “magical,” ultimately from Greek *magikós*, related to *magos*. The still deeper history leads to Iranian *maguš*, a member of a priestly group. This etymology does **not** make Galician meigas Persian priestesses, Zoroastrian survivals, or ancient Celtic magi; it records the history of a word. [Real Academia Galega/Fundación Barrié](https://portaldaspalabras.gal/lexico/palabra-do-dia/meiga/)

The theory that meigas descend institutionally from Celtic druids is a **nineteenth-century and later comparativist reconstruction**. Manuel Murguía and later Celticizing writers helped place Galician folklore within an idealized Celtic past, but neither the Latin etymology nor the folklore proves continuity of an office from Iron Age priesthoods to early-modern curers. No pre-Roman inscription contains the name.

### Documented descriptors and subtypes

The following are best treated as folklore classifications, not ranks in a formal supernatural order:

- **Meiga chuchona / chupadora** — “sucking witch.” She drains blood, fat, strength, or vitality, especially from infants. Some accounts give her animal or insect transformations. The compound is securely literary by 1863, when Rosalía describes a feverish, wasting speaker as if “*un-ha meiga chuchona / a miña sangre bebera*”—“a sucking witch had drunk my blood.” [Rosalía de Castro, “Airiños, airiños aires”](https://gl.wikisource.org/wiki/Cantares_gallegos/Airi%C3%B1os%2C_airi%C3%B1os_aires)

- **Meiga voadora** — flying witch. Flight by night, sometimes after applying an ointment kept by the hearth, belongs to the broader European witch complex and is recorded in later Galician lexicography and folklore.

- **Meiga de mal / harmful meiga** — worker of illness, the evil eye, infertility, livestock losses, or domestic misfortune. This is a descriptive category rather than a securely old fixed title.

- **Meiga curadora, sabia, menciñeira, curandeira** — healing or diagnostic practitioner. Some local speakers sharply distinguish such women from malicious *meigas*; other sources let the same practitioner both cause and remove affliction.

- **Bruxa and meiga:** sometimes synonyms; sometimes a *bruxa* is imagined as more overtly diabolical or as flying to assemblies, while *meiga* denotes the locally known bearer of harmful power. The distinction varies by district, informant, and author. It should not be converted into a universal taxonomy.

Long internet lists of sharply bounded “types of meiga” frequently recycle one another without identifying field records. Unless traceable to a dated ethnographic collection, they are **modern folkloric systematization**.

### “Eu non creo nas meigas, pero habelas, hainas”

The familiar expression means approximately, “I do not believe in meigas, but they do exist” or “I don’t believe in them, but there are some.” The idiomatic point is ironic assent to a possibility publicly denied. *Habelas, hainas* is the grammatically Galician form; *haberlas, haylas* is its widespread Castilian counterpart.

It is commonly attributed to anonymous Galician popular speech and sometimes casually to Álvaro Cunqueiro or another famous Galician. No securely identified first author has been established here. Therefore:

- the saying is securely part of **modern Galician and Spanish proverbial culture**;
- its exact first wording and authorship are **unestablished**;
- attribution to an ancient or medieval tradition is unsupported.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Medieval precursor evidence

The Compostela synod of 17 August 1289 ruled:

> *statuimus quod nullus clericus sit sortilegius vel incantator, vel augur vel adivinator…*

It forbids a cleric to be a sortilegist, enchanter, augur, or diviner, threatening deprivation of benefice and excommunication. The provision was repeated in 1309. This securely demonstrates that ecclesiastical authorities in medieval Galicia recognized and condemned such practices. It does **not** attest the word *meiga*, a meiga collective, flight, blood-sucking, or a pre-Christian cult. [Study quoting the synodal text](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/MADR/article/download/104572/4564456573856/4564456758578)

### Early-modern judicial corpus

The Santiago tribunal of the Spanish Inquisition became operative relatively late, in the sixteenth century. Its documents generally call defendants *hechiceras*, *brujas*, diviners, or practitioners of superstition rather than presenting “the Meigas” as supernatural beings.

Documented cases summarized from inquisitorial records include:

- **María Rodríguez, 1577:** transferred from ecclesiastical authorities in Braga through Tui to Santiago; tortured, made to renounce relations with the Devil, displayed in penitential insignia, whipped, and banished.

- **Ana de Castro, 1626 and 1651:** accused of divination, demonic dealings, and healing. Her recorded curative formula invoked Christ’s baptism, Saint Peter, Saint John, God, and the Virgin rather than a pagan divinity. In 1626 she was sentenced to two hundred lashes and six years’ banishment; after a second prosecution she was again tortured, whipped, and banished.

- **María Soliña, 1621:** the historical woman from Cangas became a major cultural symbol of persecution. The surviving record concerns an accused woman, property, interrogation, and social conflict—not proof that she practiced supernatural magic. Later literature and commemoration have expanded her into a paradigmatic Galician “witch.”

- **Lucía Fidalgo, 1640s–1650:** a destitute woman accused after livestock trouble; her tortured or frightened declarations became so incoherent that judges recorded that she appeared not to be in her right mind. She was nevertheless whipped and banished.

A Consello da Cultura Galega account, drawing especially on Jaime Contreras, reports ninety-two proceedings against women in the Santiago tribunal, often concerning healing, undoing spells, love remedies, or sexual matters. It also stresses that the Galician Inquisition did not conduct a witch hunt on the scale seen in some other European regions and at times intervened against escalating collective accusations. [Consello da Cultura Galega dossier](https://consellodacultura.gal/album-de-galicia/biobibliografia-doi.php?bio=23749)

These records establish accusation, coercion, punishment, healing practice, neighborhood conflict, and institutional belief. They do not establish that the accused possessed the powers alleged.

### Nineteenth-century literary and lexical corpus

The historical dictionary portal of the Instituto da Lingua Galega aggregates definitions beginning with nineteenth-century lexicography, including *meiga* as witch, “semibruja,” cunning or deceitful woman, companion of the *bruxa*, healer, and several unrelated zoological names. This is important evidence for the word’s semantic breadth but not for antiquity. [ILG historical dictionaries](https://ilg.usc.gal/ddd/ddd_pescuda.php?lang=gl&pescuda=meiga&tipo_busca=lema)

Rosalía de Castro’s *Cantares gallegos* (Vigo, 1863) supplies a secure, culturally consequential literary witness. In “Airiños, airiños aires,” the *meiga chuchona* is a simile for wasting illness: she has seemingly drunk the sufferer’s blood. The poem proves that the image was intelligible to Rosalía’s audience; it does not tell us its first date in oral tradition.

Late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century regionalist, ethnographic, and literary writing progressively made the witch an emblem of Galicia. Murguía’s Celtic interpretation, Valle-Inclán’s Galician magical atmosphere, and later ethnographic collections helped consolidate an image more coherent and nationally distinctive than the local evidence itself.

### Twentieth-century ethnographic corpus

Principal research corpora include:

- Vicente Risco’s studies of Galician “spiritual culture,” belief, the evil eye, death traditions, and folklore;
- José Ramón and Xaquín Lorenzo’s ethnographic work;
- Víctor Lis Quibén’s *Medicina popular en Galicia*;
- Carmelo Lisón Tolosana’s fieldwork on witchcraft, symbolism, accusation, healing, and village social structure;
- Antonio Fraguas and other Seminario de Estudos Galegos collectors;
- Xosé Miranda, Antonio Reigosa, and Xoán R. Cuba’s modern mythological dictionaries.

These sources preserve twentieth-century testimony and earlier collected lore. They must be dated to their collection and publication, not treated automatically as transcripts of pre-Roman religion.

### Non-Galician comparison

Barandiarán, Azkue, Satrústegi, and Hartsuaga document Basque *sorginak* and associated beliefs; Cabal, Aurelio de Llano, Constantino Cabal, Manuel Llano, and García-Lomas document Asturian and Cantabrian figures. They are valuable comparatively, but they do not establish that Basque *sorginak*, the Asturian *guaxa*, and Galician meigas were one pre-Roman being. Similarities may reflect widespread European witch motifs, neighboring transmission, Catholic demonology, early-modern judicial culture, or independent folk interpretation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic acts

**Folklore and testimony:**

- inflicting or removing *meigallo*;
- causing *mal de ollo*, wasting, convulsions, weakness, infertility, or mental disturbance;
- damaging livestock, milk, crops, fishing, household prosperity, or relationships;
- divining thefts and hidden objects;
- treating illness with herbs, water, prayer, ritual passage, amulets, or Christian formulas;
- flying at night or attending assemblies;
- changing into insects or animals;
- sucking blood, fat, or vitality from children;
- acting from envy, resentment, or revenge.

Not every meiga has every power. The composite list results from multiple periods and localities.

### Meigallo

The Real Academia Galega defines *meigallo* as a magical action producing an event or effect, generally negative. Symptoms assigned to it could include convulsions, rolling eyes, foaming, shouting, blasphemy, or aversion to Mass. Such symptoms were variously interpreted as bewitchment, demonic possession, *ramo cativo*, *tangaraño*, or other culturally recognized afflictions. [RAG, “Meigallos e san Xoán”](https://portaldaspalabras.gal/lexico/setestrelo/meigallos-e-san-xoan/)

This is a historical description of diagnosis, not a modern medical endorsement. Conditions grouped under these names may have included epilepsy, malnutrition, rickets, mental illness, trauma, or other diseases, but retrospective diagnosis of an individual case is often impossible.

### Cure at fountains and sanctuaries

Water is a major curative medium in Galician folk religion. Accounts prescribe washing, drinking, passing a sufferer through or around a prescribed place, leaving garments, or visiting a fountain at a ritually marked time. The exact formula varies locally; there is no single canonical “cure at the fonts.”

Especially important are:

- **O Corpiño**, a Marian sanctuary famous for treating afflictions described as possession or *meigallo*, with pilgrimage, prayer, exorcistic practice, and the Virgin’s intercession;
- springs and fountains visited at Saint John’s Eve or dawn;
- water in which Saint John’s herbs have rested overnight;
- ritual use of odd numbers, repeated circuits, silence, or prescribed prayers;
- La Lanzada and other sanctuaries where fertility, the evil eye, and release from enchantment intersect with Marian devotion.

These practices are best classified as **Christian folk ritual with locally inherited uses of healing water**, not automatically as intact pagan cult.

### The meiga chuchona

The *chuchona* is imagined as draining blood or vitality and causing pallor, wasting, or infant sickness. Later summaries sometimes describe her transformation into a blowfly, bumblebee, or other intruding creature. Rosalía’s 1863 line is the strongest early literary anchor presently identified. Claims that the chuchona is an ancient Galician vampire goddess are unsupported.

### Appearance and visual markers

No ancient or medieval iconographic canon exists. The modern stereotyped meiga generally appears as:

- an elderly woman;
- dark or rural dress, shawl, kerchief, or cloak;
- broom, cauldron, herbs, cards, distilling or cooking vessel;
- cat, owl, moon, fire, mist, or rural stone architecture;
- alternatively, a young, sexually alluring “enchantress.”

Most of these markers belong to the wider European witch image. Their use as specifically Galician emblems is largely nineteenth- and twentieth-century.

Camilo Díaz Baliño’s **1919 painting *A meiga*** represents a young woman through an intense, uncanny gaze rather than simply repeating the pointed-hat Halloween witch. Later artists including Urbano Lugrís, Laxeiro, and “Compostela” contributed to a Galician visual vocabulary in which the meiga became mysterious, lyrical, or national rather than merely diabolical. [Discussion of Díaz Baliño and later artists](https://historiadegalicia.gal/2022/08/diaz-balino-e-a-meiga-como-referente-popular/)

The pointed hat, striped stockings, and smiling broom-rider common in souvenir shops are chiefly **commercial modern iconography**.

### Sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers, and celestial correspondences

No securely attested ancient meiga cult assigned canonical animals, colors, numbers, days, planets, or offerings.

Documented folklore associates witchcraft more loosely with:

- nocturnal insects and animals;
- cats, owls, toads, goats, and corvids in modern imagery;
- herbs collected around Saint John;
- protective laurel, fennel, foxglove, rosemary, rue, and other locally varying plants;
- odd-numbered ritual repetitions, particularly three, seven, or nine;
- night, crossroads, hearths, cemeteries, fountains, and liminal boundaries.

Any chart assigning a fixed planet, crystal, color, or “Celtic correspondence” to the Meigas is normally **modern occult synthesis**.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The Meigas have no securely documented divine genealogy. They are not described in early sources as daughters of a god, a primordial race, or a divine sisterhood. In different materials they are:

- ordinary women wrongly or rightly reputed to practice magic;
- women whose skill is learned or inherited;
- women empowered by a pact with the Devil;
- supernatural witches in legend;
- folk healers recast by outsiders as witches.

Thus there is no attested parentage, consort, offspring, or pantheon hierarchy.

### Documented interactions with named divine or supernatural beings

Because the tradition is plural and usually concerns human practitioners, the following are the checkable named interactions—not a divine family tree:

| Other being | Recorded interaction | Source and date | Evidentiary tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Devil / Demo** | Alleged source of a meiga’s powers through pact, sexual relations, instruction, or sale of a child; also a figure invoked in coerced confessions. | Galician inquisitorial cases, sixteenth–seventeenth centuries; later folklore and lexicography | Early-modern demonological accusation; later folklore |
| **Jesus Christ** | Invoked in Ana de Castro’s curative formula through Christ’s baptism in the Jordan. | Inquisitorial record, 1626 | Securely recorded early-modern healing formula |
| **The Virgin Mary** | Invoked in cures; at O Corpiño the Virgin intercedes against possession or *meigallo*. | Early-modern formulas; later pilgrimage practice | Christian folk religion |
| **Saint Peter** | Holds Christ by the hand in Ana de Castro’s healing formula. | Inquisitorial record, 1626 | Securely recorded early-modern formula |
| **Saint John** | Appears with Saint Peter in the same formula; Saint John’s Eve becomes a major time for protective fire, herbs, water, and remedies against harmful forces. | 1626 formula; later folklore | Early-modern attestation plus later seasonal elaboration |
| **Satan** | Named as an evil presence to be expelled in the popular queimada text. | Mariano Marcos Abalo’s revised *Conxuro da Queimada*, 1974 | Modern invention |
| **Beelzebub** | Named with Satan in the revised queimada performance text. | Abalo, 1974 | Modern invention |
| **Virgin of O Corpiño** | Asked to cure conditions interpreted as possession or meigallo. | Modern ethnography of an older pilgrimage complex | Folklore/Christian cult |
| **Santa Compaña** | Appears beside meigas in modern Galician supernatural imagery and in the queimada text, but no stable narrative makes it a genealogical relation. | Folklore collections; 1967/1974 queimada text | Folklore and modern literary conjunction |

No securely attested interaction with Endovelicus, Ataecina, Bandua, Nabia, Reue, Lugus, Mari, Basque *sorginak*, or any other pre-Roman deity has been found. Their occasional placement together in modern “Iberian pantheons” is taxonomic proximity, not a recorded mythic meeting.

## 5. Major Legends and Narrative Complexes

### The harmful neighbor and the meigallo

**Folklore.** Misfortune strikes after quarrel, envy, praise, a threatening glance, or denied hospitality. A child wastes away, a cow ceases giving milk, pigs fail to suckle, crops decline, or a person becomes ill. Suspicion falls upon a socially marginal or locally feared woman. A healer identifies the affliction, prescribes prayer, water, herbs, amulets, smoke, or pilgrimage, and may name or symbolically reverse the aggressor.

The narrative is socially consequential even when no supernatural claim can be verified: accusation transfers unexplained misfortune into relations of envy, reciprocity, gender, poverty, and neighborhood tension. Lisón Tolosana treats this structure as a cultural-semantic system rather than simply a collection of fabulous tales.

### The meiga chuchona

**Secure literary attestation, 1863; probably earlier oral folklore, date unknown.** A woman-like being drains a child or adult at night, leaving pallor and weakness. Later versions introduce animal or insect disguise and magical ointments made from stolen bodily substances. The story overlaps European witch, nightmare, vampire, and child-wasting traditions. Its exact historical origin cannot be reconstructed.

Rosalía’s usage is metaphorical but culturally legible: fever consumes the exile as though a sucking witch drank her blood. Later folklorists transform that image into a more formal creature description.

### Night flight and transformation

**Early-modern European demonological complex; Galician folklore.** Witches leave at night, sometimes through chimney or hearth, after applying ointment; they fly or take animal form and meet other witches or the Devil. The motifs are not uniquely Galician and may derive from interaction among oral belief, sermons, judicial interrogation, printed demonology, and international tale patterns.

Specific claims that every meiga flies, rides a broom, or attends a Sabbath flatten locally inconsistent evidence.

### Undoing the meigallo at fountains

**Regional folk ritual.** The patient is taken to a sacred spring, Marian sanctuary, or ritually powerful site. Water may be drunk, used for washing, or carried away. The rite may require dawn, midnight, Saint John’s Eve, an odd number of repetitions, prescribed silence, garments, herbs, or prayers. At O Corpiño, the curing complex became strongly Marian and exorcistic.

The narrative logic is one of displacement and purification: the affliction is diagnosed, transferred, washed away, or defeated by a power stronger than the aggressor. It should not be reduced to one universal script.

### María Soliña

**Historical prosecution followed by literary-national elaboration.** María Soliña of Cangas was accused during the seventeenth-century crisis following Ottoman and Barbary attacks and local economic conflict. Modern memory has made her an emblem of persecuted Galician women and resistance. The surviving evidence supports prosecution and coercion, not the supernatural charges.

Her transformation from defendant into national symbol is part of the history of the meiga image, but modern poems, songs, and commemorations must not be read back into her trial record.

### “Habelas, hainas”

**Modern proverb.** A skeptical speaker denies belief while conceding existence. The saying dramatizes the socially prudent ambiguity surrounding witchcraft: disbelief may coexist with reluctance to challenge possible power. Its anonymous proverbial authority is genuine modern culture; claims of a specific ancient origin are unverified.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There was no documented temple, priesthood, sacrificial establishment, or civic cult of “the Meigas.” No Latin inscription from Galicia or Lusitania dedicates an altar to them. The sanctuaries of Endovelicus and Ataecina therefore do not belong in their cult record.

What exists instead is a distributed complex of domestic, therapeutic, judicial, seasonal, and devotional practice.

### Practitioners

- curers and herbalists;
- birth attendants;
- diviners;
- specialists in the evil eye;
- sellers or makers of love remedies;
- people reputed to undo spells;
- alleged harmful witches, often identified by neighbors rather than self-description.

Women were prominent, especially poor, widowed, single, elderly, socially independent, or otherwise vulnerable women, but male practitioners also existed.

### Remedies and protections

Recorded or collected remedies include:

- Christian prayers and invocations;
- crosses, scapulars, medals, relic-like objects, and blessed substances;
- herbs, smoke, fire, and water;
- passing a child through a split tree, hole in a stone, or other narrow opening;
- pilgrimage to Marian shrines;
- protective objects near a cradle;
- reversing, returning, or symbolically exposing the aggressor;
- Saint John’s herbs left in water under the night sky and used for washing at dawn;
- jumping bonfires an odd number of times.

The accuracy and age of each prescription depend on its individual collector and locality.

### Persecution and suppression

Church councils condemned divination and enchantment; episcopal and inquisitorial courts prosecuted people accused of superstition, sorcery, or demonic relations. Punishments documented in Galicia include interrogation, confiscation, torture, public penance, whipping, and banishment.

Galicia does not support the popular picture of an indiscriminate mass burning of thousands of “meigas.” The Inquisition’s treatment could be brutal, but its officials also sometimes resisted uncontrolled local witch panics. Secular and local court records remain important because inquisitorial statistics alone cannot reveal the whole extent of punishment.

No evidence found connects the Meigas to human sacrifice or temple prostitution. Those motifs belong neither to the secure judicial record nor to documented Galician folk practice.

### Queimada

Aguardiente burned with sugar and often citrus peel became an important Galician social drink. The familiar theatrical ritual—darkened room, blue flame, ladle, and spoken spell against meigas—is modern.

The famous *Conxuro da Queimada* was written in **Vigo in 1967** by **Mariano Marcos Abalo**, reportedly for a gathering aboard a confiscated vessel moored in the harbor. In **1974** he added the references to Satan and Beelzebub and performed it at the Fausto discotheque. Printed copies circulated without his name, helping the authored poem acquire the appearance of anonymous ancient tradition. Abalo registered it in 2001. [History of the text](https://www.atlantico.net/vigo/hombre-mas-quema-galicia_1_20180708-1616066.html)

The distinction is essential:

- burning aguardiente existed before 1967;
- improvised blessings or words may also have accompanied social drinking;
- the now-standard poem beginning *“Mouchos, coruxas, sapos e bruxas…”* is a dated twentieth-century composition;
- it cannot be Celtic because distilled aguardiente and refined sugar are post-antique technologies in Galicia;
- the complete staged complex is an exemplary **invented tradition that became genuine modern folklore**.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1289: no identifiable Meiga record

Pre-Roman Galicia had deities, ritual sites, and religious practices attested through archaeology and Roman-period inscriptions, but none names the Meigas. Strabo’s generalized descriptions of northwestern Iberian peoples do not supply them. Continuity theories from Celtic priestesses or goddesses are modern hypotheses without a documentary chain.

### 1289–1500: condemned magical practices

The Compostela synod attests clerical concern with diviners, enchanters, augurs, and sortilegists. This is evidence for categories of forbidden practice, not yet for the named Galician figure.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: judicial construction and local accusation

Episcopal, secular, and inquisitorial records document healers, diviners, alleged witches, and neighborhood accusations. Learned demonology introduced or reinforced the pact with the Devil, sexual relations with demons, the Sabbath, and witch-flight as interpretive templates.

The categories *bruxa*, *hechicera*, and later *meiga* could converge. A recent study explicitly describes the seventeenth-century meeting of “bruxa” and “meiga” as a **syncretism of two realities** rather than an eternal identity. [Universidade de Santiago research record](https://investigacion.usc.es/documentos/66b11507b634ba3a7e60e242?lang=es)

### Eighteenth century: medicine, superstition, and skepticism

Enlightenment writers reclassified folk healing as either useful empirical knowledge or superstition. Martín Sarmiento defended aspects of women’s herbal and medical knowledge. This did not produce a meiga cult; it contributed to later reinterpretation of the meiga as custodian of popular medicine.

### Nineteenth century: lexical visibility and national Romanticism

Galician lexicography records *meiga* with overlapping meanings. Rosalía’s 1863 *Cantares gallegos* gives canonical literary form to the *chuchona*. Romantic and regionalist historiography increasingly sought Celtic roots for Galician difference. The meiga’s transformation from feared local woman into representative figure of Galicia begins here.

### 1900–1936: ethnographic collection and artistic nationalization

The Rexurdimento’s cultural legacy, the Irmandades da Fala, the Seminario de Estudos Galegos, and artists such as Díaz Baliño transformed folklore into material for national culture. Risco and others collected and systematized beliefs. Their work is indispensable, but the act of collection also selected and organized fluid local traditions.

### Franco period: folklore under cultural restriction

Galician language and political expression were constrained, while regional folklore could survive in depoliticized, picturesque, literary, or ethnographic forms. Meiga imagery remained available as “mystical Galicia,” sometimes drained of the social history of accusation and punishment.

### 1960s–1980s: spectacularization

- **1967:** Abalo writes the queimada conjuration.
- **1969:** the association Meigas e Trasgos creates the Noite Meiga of Sarria; the organization itself dates the celebration to that year. [Meigas e Trasgos](https://meigasetrasgos.gal/)
- **1974:** Satan and Beelzebub enter Abalo’s revised text.
- Tourism, festivals, graphic art, and souvenir production normalize the broom-riding meiga as a friendly regional mascot.

### Late twentieth century to present: heritage, feminism, tourism, and revival

Guadalupe Jiménez-Esquinas describes the modern change as a conversion of stigma into heritage resource. Formerly marginal or feared women are selectively reimagined as healers, feminists, wise women, and symbols of cultural autonomy, while festivals and souvenirs turn the figure into an economic asset. [“Las meigas: la transformación de un estigma en recurso patrimonial”](https://investigacion.usc.gal/documentos/5d1df6dd29995204f767ae90)

Contemporary self-described Galician pagan and occult communities now claim *meiguería* as a living spiritual path. One current example links an *Irmandade das Meigas Pagás de Galiza*, “Galician paganism,” and occult arts. This establishes contemporary religious creativity, not proof that the organization preserves an institution continuously transmitted from pre-Christian Galicia. [Contemporary practitioner site](https://www.zeltiaalobameiga.com/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### National emblem

The meiga has become one of the most portable signs of “Galicianness,” alongside the bagpipe, pilgrimage, green landscape, stone village, Celtic spiral, and queimada. Modern uses include:

- tourism posters and shops;
- restaurant and beverage branding;
- dolls, magnets, jewelry, and figurines;
- festivals and “witch nights”;
- feminist art and music;
- football, commercial, and hospitality branding;
- descriptions of Galicia as *terra meiga*.

This status is not simply a survival of ancient belief. It is the product of Romantic Celticism, the literary canon, ethnographic recovery, twentieth-century visual art, tourism, and regional identity politics.

Jiménez-Esquinas’s 2013 study notes that patrimonialization foregrounds the meiga’s positive symbolism while often minimizing the historical stigma imposed on suspected women. The image can simultaneously empower, memorialize, commercialize, and sanitize.

### Literature and performance

Important milestones include:

- Rosalía de Castro, *Cantares gallegos* (1863);
- Galician magical and rural imagery in Valle-Inclán;
- Vicente Risco’s folklore-informed literary and ethnographic production;
- Guridi’s zarzuela *La meiga* (1928), whose Sabela is a rural adviser and maker of charms;
- poems, songs, plays, children’s literature, and commemorations of María Soliña;
- the recited queimada text after 1967.

### Occult and neopagan reinterpretation

Modern occult writing often treats the meiga as:

- an initiated nature-priestess;
- hereditary witch;
- Celtic shaman;
- keeper of an unbroken matriarchal religion;
- Galician equivalent of a Wiccan witch.

These constructions may be meaningful contemporary religion, but an unbroken institutional lineage has not been demonstrated. Fixed correspondences with tarot, crystals, lunar phases, or named Celtic gods are modern esoteric synthesis unless an older source is produced.

### Political and gender reinterpretation

Feminist recovery reads accused witches as women marginalized for independence, healing knowledge, sexuality, poverty, widowhood, or nonconformity. That approach usefully restores the social conditions behind accusation. It becomes historically unsafe only when every accused woman is assumed to have been a professional midwife, herbalist, feminist rebel, or member of a suppressed pagan religion. Individual records vary.

The meiga has not acquired a documented major extremist appropriation comparable to the use of some Germanic or classical symbols. Celticized Galician imagery can enter nationalist discourse, but no single political meaning is inherent in the figure.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is the meiga ancient or Celtic?

**Romantic/learned claim:** meigas descend from druids, Celtic priestesses, or pre-Roman magical women.

**Evidence against treating this as established:** the name is Latin-derived; no pre-Roman inscription names them; no ancient author describes them; medieval prohibitions use general Latin clerical categories; the securely visible meiga figure belongs to early-modern records, nineteenth-century literature, and modern folklore collection.

**Assessment:** possible remote continuities in healing places, water rites, or seasonal practice cannot be excluded, but a direct institutional or mythological genealogy is **unproven modern reconstruction**.

### Meiga versus bruxa

One position treats them as synonyms. Another distinguishes the *meiga* as local spell-worker from the *bruxa* as demonological, flying, Sabbath-attending witch. Field usage varies, and seventeenth-century judicial culture encouraged convergence. The historically responsible formulation is that the categories overlap and became partially syncretized.

### Persecuted healer or harmful witch?

Both images exist in the evidence:

- some defendants demonstrably offered cures, divination, or remedies;
- some were accused after threats or neighborhood conflict;
- some confessions were produced by torture;
- folklore informants nevertheless describe the meiga as genuinely harmful within their belief system;
- modern positive heritage emphasizes healing and wisdom.

No single moral biography encompasses all people called meigas.

### Scale of persecution

Popular accounts sometimes assert vast Galician burnings. The documented Santiago inquisitorial corpus indicates prosecution and severe punishment but not mass execution on the scale imagined in tourist legend. Local and secular archives may modify totals. Claims must distinguish execution, death in custody, whipping, banishment, public penance, and mere accusation.

### Meigallo as possession, culture-bound illness, or metaphor

Traditional discourse may classify symptoms as witchcraft, evil eye, demonic possession, *tangaraño*, *aire*, or *ramo cativo*. Medical and anthropological observers have variously interpreted them as neurological illness, malnutrition, psychological distress, social conflict, or culture-bound syndrome. Retrospective diagnosis remains uncertain; the ethnographic fact is that shared cultural interpretation shaped symptoms and cures.

### Queimada’s antiquity

**Popular claim:** a Celtic ritual transmitted from antiquity.

**Documentary finding:** the standard conjuration is Abalo’s 1967 composition, revised in 1974. Burned spirits are necessarily post-distillation; the familiar performance developed in the twentieth century.

**Assessment:** **modern invention**, subsequently traditionalized. Calling it invented does not make its present cultural significance unreal.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is dispersed among:

- synodal constitutions;
- the Archivo Histórico Nacional and other inquisitorial holdings;
- diocesan, municipal, and secular judicial archives;
- historical dictionaries and linguistic corpora;
- literary editions;
- the archives of the Seminario de Estudos Galegos and Museo do Pobo Galego;
- oral-history notebooks and published ethnographies;
- visual-art collections;
- modern festival, tourism, and practitioner archives.

The greatest losses are contextual: oral accounts were often recorded centuries after their supposed origins, collectors sometimes normalized dialect or compressed variants, and judicial records preserve the court’s categories more fully than defendants’ own understandings.

## 11. Sources and Research Assessment

The foundational distinction is between four different archives:

1. **Normative ecclesiastical evidence:** synods tell us what church authorities condemned.
2. **Judicial evidence:** trials tell us what was alleged, confessed—sometimes under coercion—and punished.
3. **Folkloric and ethnographic evidence:** collections tell us what particular informants and collectors reported at identifiable dates.
4. **Literary, artistic, commercial, and revival evidence:** these show how the meiga was transformed into a Galician cultural emblem.

None may simply substitute for another. An accusation is not proof of supernatural action; a twentieth-century field report is not automatically a pre-Roman survival; a modern pagan practice is not evidence of ancient institutional continuity; and an invented text can nevertheless become authentic contemporary folklore.

The fullest defensible reconstruction is therefore not the biography of an ancient divine being. It is the history of a mutable category: prohibited magical practice in medieval ecclesiastical discourse; accused curers and witches in early-modern courts; blood-draining and spell-casting women in folklore; a canonical figure of nineteenth-century Galician literature; an object of twentieth-century anthropology; and finally a national, touristic, feminist, occult, and commercial emblem. The variants are not corruptions of one original Meiga. They are the documented history of the name.
