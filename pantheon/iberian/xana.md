# The Xana: Research Dossier

**Class:** Female water-associated supernatural being—usually classified in recorded Asturian tradition as a *ninfa*, *hada*, *encantada*, or *genio mítico*, sometimes collectively as *xanes*. “Goddess” is a modern or speculative classification, not the usual classification in the earliest records.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Enrique Gil y Carrasco’s article “Los asturianos,” published in the *Semanario Pintoresco Español* on 12 May 1839, describes *janas* as tiny, powerful women emerging from springs to wash and dry white clothing by moonlight. His information came from an 1838 journey through Asturias, but the printed article is the first presently located secure documentary attestation. Francisco de Paula Mellado supplied a fuller description under the spelling *xanas* in *Recuerdos de un viaje por España* (1849), p. 87.

**Essential finding:** The Xana is securely documented as a nineteenth- and twentieth-century Asturian folkloric being. No known pre-Roman inscription, Roman altar, medieval manuscript, archaeological image, temple, priesthood, or classical author names a *Xana*. Proposed descent from pre-Roman water divinities or Roman Diana is a modern historical-linguistic reconstruction—not direct evidence of an ancient Xana cult.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Earliest attestation:** Nineteenth-century documentary evidence reporting an already existing oral belief.
- **[F] Folklore:** Oral narratives, sayings, customs, and beliefs recorded from informants.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** Material confined to, or differently developed in, a particular district.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** A folklorist’s or antiquarian’s organization and interpretation of oral material.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** A historical, linguistic, or comparative hypothesis.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** Equation with another being rather than a narrated relationship.
- **[ML] Modern literary or popular elaboration:** Literature, tourism, entertainment, or contemporary mythography.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported ancient claim:** A recent assertion presented without early evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

The securely documented Xana is not a single named goddess with a biography. *Xana* is a class-name for female supernatural beings associated especially with springs, streams, riverbanks, caves, and underground or underwater dwellings. The traditions overlap several narrative types:

1. the diminutive spring fairy who washes clothes or spins;
2. the beautiful, long-haired woman who combs herself beside water;
3. the guardian or possessor of gold and enchanted treasure;
4. the mother who substitutes her abnormal *xanín* for a human infant;
5. the enchanted maiden who requires a human to perform a dangerous or exact ritual of liberation;
6. in some eastern variants, a small, dark underground woman associated with “the Moors.”

These types should not be collapsed into a single canonical personality.

### Names and regional forms

- **xana**, plural Asturian **xanes**: the usual central and eastern Asturian form.
- **jana/janas**: the spelling used by Gil y Carrasco in 1839; also documented in Leonese areas.
- **inxana/injiana**: Llanes and adjoining eastern districts.
- **xiana**, plural **xianas**: Ponga and parts of the Asturian–Leonese borderland.
- **xania/xanias**: Cudillero and Muros del Nalón.
- **siana/sana**: additional localized phonetic variants catalogued by the *Diccionario General de la Lengua Asturiana*.
- **xanín**, occasionally **xan**: child or son of a Xana; especially the substituted child in changeling narratives.
- **xanu**: a masculine form in some tales. Caro Baroja recalled a story in which a *xan* was caught by putting pitch on a horse; he later corrected his earlier supposition that the masculine form was merely anomalous, because *xanín* occurs independently in several tales.
- **encantada**, **mora**, **moza**, **señora**: generic designations used in narratives structurally similar to Xana tales. They do not always name precisely the same being.
- **anjana/onjana/juáncana**: Cantabrian figures sometimes called the Xana’s “cousin.” Their close relationship is a modern comparative classification, not a genealogical statement made by the beings in an old narrative.
- **moura/moura encantada**: the closest Galician and Portuguese narrative analogue. The Real Academia Galega describes the Xana as Asturian and says she is equivalent to the Galician *moura*, rather than identifying *Xana* as the normal Galician name.

The Asturian dictionary records both supernatural and transferred human meanings: a *xana* may also mean an industrious woman, while one regional entry glosses the word as a pool or deep place in water. Its accumulated citations reach back to Gumersindo Laverde, Apolinar de Rato’s 1880 vocabulary, and Aurelio de Llano’s field collection. [The dictionary entry supplies the fullest accessible lexical map](https://mas.lne.es/diccionario/palabra/76006).

### Etymology

#### Latin *Diana*

**[SR; plausible but not proved as cultic continuity]** The most widely accepted proposal derives *xana* from Latin **Diāna**, through the palatal development of initial /dj/ in Asturian. Xosé Lluis García Arias, Corominas and Pascual, and later Asturian toponymists have supported this phonological route. A University of Oviedo study illustrates it with comparable Asturian developments and applies it to such place-names as *Cueva la Xana* and *Cueva les Xanes. [The dissertation summarizes the linguistic argument](https://digibuo.uniovi.es/dspace/bitstream/10651/12904/1/TD_CristianLongoViejo.pdf).

The etymology does not by itself demonstrate that Xana stories descend unchanged from worship of the Roman goddess. Words derived from a divine name can become generic terms for fairies, witches, or female spirits, as happened with cognate-looking forms elsewhere in Romance-speaking Europe.

#### Other proposals

- **[LS/SR]** Derivation from *Xuana/Juana*, encouraged by the being’s manifestation on Saint John’s night (*San Xuan*), has been proposed popularly. It may reflect later reinterpretation rather than original etymology.
- **[SR, weak]** A Celtic root glossed as “white” has circulated in popular mythography, but no securely demonstrated Celtic form-to-Asturian sound history establishes it.
- **[SR, weak]** Comparison with Provençal *jana*, Sardinian *janas/gianas*, Romanian *zână*, Aromanian forms, and Albanian *zanë* identifies a broad European family of names and beings. Some of those forms may independently continue Diana; resemblance alone does not prove direct transmission into Asturias.
- **[MI]** Claims that *xana* derives from Sanskrit *deva(s)* or directly from a reconstructed pre-Roman goddess *Deva* are not established etymologies. The existence of Iberian rivers named Deva and of Indo-European divine vocabulary does not bridge the evidentiary gap to the Asturian word.

### Epithets and titles

There is no epigraphically attested cult title. Recorded descriptions—“the fairy of the springs,” “the enchanted one,” “the beautiful Xana”—are descriptive phrases rather than formal epithets. “Queen of the Xanas” belongs chiefly to recent retellings; no early Xana theology or stable monarchy of the Xanas has been demonstrated.

### Comparanda and equations

- **Xana = Diana:** **[SI/SR]**, linguistic or survivals-based interpretation; not a recorded myth in which the two meet.
- **Xana = classical nymph/naiad/ondine:** **[LS]**, classificatory analogy used by nineteenth- and twentieth-century writers.
- **Xana ≈ Galician moura encantada:** **[RV/LS]**, a strong narrative comparison, not proof of identical names or origins.
- **Xana ≈ Basque lamia:** **[LS]**, comparison encouraged by shared combing, water, cave, and treasure motifs. Caro Baroja observed that stories assigned to Basque *lamias* correspond to Asturian Xana stories, but did not thereby document a Basque being called Xana.
- **Xana ≈ Cantabrian anjana:** **[RV/LS]**, likely related in name and fairy classification, but the elaborately benevolent Anjana described by Manuel Llano has its own literary and ethnographic history.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 1839: Gil y Carrasco

**[EA]** Gil’s “Los asturianos,” based on travel in 1838 and printed on 12 May 1839, reports belief in *janas*: beautiful silver-colored or silvery little women, about a cubit tall, who emerge through openings in springs, wash clothes whiter than snow, dry them by moonlight, and withdraw when interrupted. He calls them mysterious and powerful and speculates—without evidence—about a remote Scandinavian origin. The article establishes:

- the name *janas*;
- diminutive female form;
- residence or passage through springs;
- nocturnal washing;
- white clothing;
- belief attributed to local people.

It does **not** yet document the golden comb, changeling, cuélebre, St John’s ordeal, priesthood, or ancient cult. The article and its date are discussed and quoted in the proceedings on [Gil y Carrasco and Romanticism](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/libro/582645.pdf).

### 1849: Mellado

**[EA]** Mellado’s *Recuerdos de un viaje por España*, p. 87, calls the beings *xanas*: beautiful women about a cubit tall who inhabit crystal palaces beneath solitary springs, emerge after midnight to wash very white clothes, and give favored humans skeins that must always be wound to the right so the thread never runs out. This is the earliest located printed use furnishing a substantial Xana episode under the modern spelling. [A transcription and bibliographic identification are preserved here](https://www.descubreleyendas.es/Info/Consultas.aspx?idLeyenda=747), while the [1849 volume is catalogued and digitized by Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Recuerdos_de_un_viaje_por_Espa%C3%B1a.html?id=4acxAQAAMAAJ).

### Late nineteenth-century lexicography

**[EA/F]** Gumersindo Laverde, Apolinar de Rato’s *Vocabulario de las palabras y frases bables* (1880), Braulio Vigón, and other regional lexicographers establish that *xana* was not merely Mellado’s literary invention. Their entries classify her as an imaginary nymph or fairy of springs. Nevertheless, dictionaries condense belief; they do not supply an ancient date for it.

### Aurelio de Llano, 1922

**[F]** *Del folklore asturiano: mitos, supersticiones, costumbres* is the central early ethnographic corpus. De Llano gathered material directly in numerous municipalities and mapped local terminology and sites. His Xana chapter records:

- tiny, extraordinarily beautiful women;
- long hair, white tunic or regional dress;
- fountains, caves, rivers, and lakes as dwellings;
- spinning and washing;
- gold thread, combs, scissors, distaffs, balls, hens, and chicks;
- manifestations around dawn on St John’s Day;
- treasures;
- courtship and disenchantment;
- substituted *xaninos*;
- localized songs and formulae.

De Llano was also unusually critical of invented folklore. He explicitly rejected the Busgoso, Espumeros, Ventolines, and Lavanderas as recent literary imports for which he found no oral tradition. This strengthens the comparative value of what he did accept, although his reports remain early-twentieth-century records, not proof of antiquity. [The complete digitization is available here](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9e/Del_folklore_asturiano-_mitos%2C_supersticiones%2C_costumbres_%28IA_delfolkloreastur00llan%29.pdf).

### Caro Baroja’s prologue and comparative studies

**[LS/SR]** Julio Caro Baroja warned against mistaking regional survival for regional uniqueness. He located the Xana in the medieval Leonese and wider European world of fairies, female night spirits, and treasure beings. He also acknowledged correcting his own interpretation of *xan*. His comparative work is methodologically important because it resists both an isolated “pure Asturian pantheon” and easy claims of direct pagan continuity.

### Constantino Cabal, 1925

**[LS]** Cabal devoted a substantial portion of *La mitología asturiana* to the Xana’s origin, etymology, stories, and comparison with Diana, Janus, nymphs, lamias, Parcae, and European fairy traditions. His work preserves valuable traditions but is more speculative and literary than de Llano’s field-based collection. Its derivations should therefore be cited as Cabal’s historical system, not anonymous folk doctrine. [The book record and searchable text identify the sections on origin, etymology, and the *xanu*](https://books.google.com.gt/books/about/La_mitolog%C3%ADa_asturiana.html?id=Lr5ZAAAAMAAJ).

### Martín of Braga, c. 574

**Not an attestation of the Xana.** In *De correctione rusticorum* 8, written in the Suevic kingdom of Gallaecia, Martín condemns rural sacrifice to spirits identified as Neptune in the sea, Lamiae in rivers, Nymphs in springs, and “Dianas” in forests:

> “in fluminibus Lamias, in fontibus Nymphas, in silvis Dianas”

This is important evidence for sixth-century Christian discourse about rural supernatural beings and offerings in northwestern Iberia. It is not the word *Xana*, not an Asturian Xana narrative, and not proof that those beings evolved into Xanas. Martín may also be applying inherited classical and patristic categories to local practices. The [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/martinbraga/rusticus.shtml) and a [translation with bibliographic discussion](https://www.roger-pearse.com/weblog/2014/01/08/an-english-translation-of-martin-of-bragas-de-correctione-rusticorum/) are available online.

### Pre-Roman and Roman evidence

No Xana occurs in:

- Strabo, *Geography* III;
- the relevant Roman literary descriptions of northern Iberia;
- published indigenous theonyms from Asturias, Galicia, Lusitania, or Celtiberia;
- the Endovelicus dedications from São Miguel da Mota;
- Ataecina dedications from Turóbriga and elsewhere;
- known dedications to Navia/Nabia;
- dedications to indigenous *genii*, springs, or nymphs.

Those corpora provide comparative evidence that waters and local places could receive cult. They do not name or depict a Xana. Endovelicus, Ataecina, and Navia therefore belong to the history of ancient Iberian religion, not to the Xana’s documented corpus.

### Basque materials

Barandiarán, Azkue, Satrústegi, and the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* document *lamiak*, Mari, and other female numina. They do not establish “Xana” as an indigenous Basque divine name. Similar motifs—caves, water, combing, beauty, gifts, punishment, treasure—justify comparison but not incorporation into a single Basque–Asturian goddess.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[F]** The Xana’s primary domain is fresh water: springs, fountains, streams, pools, waterfalls, and water-bearing caves. Some traditions extend her to mountains, underground chambers, meadows, or rocky openings, normally with water or treasure nearby.

Secondary domains are:

- spinning, winding thread, washing, and drying linen;
- gold and hidden treasure;
- enchantment and disenchantment;
- fertility, nursing, and infant substitution;
- dangerous or rewarding contact between human and supernatural worlds;
- liminal times, especially midnight, dawn, and St John’s night/morning.

Calling her a “goddess of water, fertility, the moon, and sovereignty” goes beyond the recorded material unless expressly labeled modern reconstruction.

### Appearance

The sources conflict:

- **[EA/F]** Gil and Mellado: tiny, about a cubit tall.
- **[F]** Common twentieth-century description: extremely beautiful young woman with very long, often golden or blond hair.
- **[RV]** Llanes/eastern traditions: small and sometimes dark-complexioned, especially where *inxanas* are explained as women “left by the Moors.”
- **[LS]** Cabal: white tunic.
- **[F]** De Llano: regional Asturian dress in at least some narratives.
- **[ML]** Contemporary illustration standardizes her as a full-sized blond fairy in translucent white or blue robes.

### Recognizing a Xana

There is no ancient or medieval cult image. Modern images usually combine:

- long loose hair;
- a gold comb;
- water or a cave;
- white garments;
- golden thread, distaff, skein, hens, chicks, balls, or treasure.

The gold comb is now emblematic but is not present in the earliest 1839 description. It is securely documented in later collected folklore.

### Powers and limitations

**[F]** Xanas may possess or produce inexhaustible thread, reward cleanliness and generosity, enrich a successful liberator, prophesy conditions, transform into a serpent, steal or exchange infants, enchant humans, punish failure, and vanish when observed. Their powers are frequently constrained by an exact rule: wind thread in one direction, do not break it, choose the maiden rather than treasure, touch her with a consecrated cloth, kiss or embrace her serpent form, seize a flower from her mouth, or refrain from fear and speech.

### Sacred animals, colors, dates, and planets

No animal, color, number, weekday, or planet is securely attested as belonging to a Xana cult.

- Golden hens and chicks are treasure manifestations, not demonstrated sacred animals.
- Serpent form is an ordeal or transformation motif.
- White is associated with washed clothes and later clothing descriptions.
- Gold is the dominant treasure color/material.
- St John’s night, 23–24 June, is the principal calendrical setting in collected folklore.
- Lunar associations arise from nocturnal activity and the Diana hypothesis; no independent Xana–Moon theology is attested.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

No securely collected genealogy names the Xana’s parents, creator, siblings, or divine ancestors. Assertions that she is a daughter, avatar, priestess, or remnant of Diana, Deva, Navia, or a Celtic mother goddess are reconstructions or modern inventions.

Xanas have children, the *xaninos*, but collected narratives seldom name a father. Human–Xana unions occur as fairy-bride stories, yet they do not produce a stable pantheon genealogy.

### Relations

- **Xaninos:** offspring of Xanas; exchanged for human infants in changeling narratives.
- **Human mothers:** unwilling foster-mothers in the changeling cycle.
- **Human men:** suitors, would-be liberators, thieves of golden objects, or recipients of gifts and punishments.
- **Cuélebre:** a serpent or dragon frequently associated in later syntheses with caves, treasure, and captive enchanted maidens. Not every Xana has a cuélebre guardian, and the connection is less secure in the earliest records than modern summaries imply.
- **Moura encantada:** Galician–Portuguese analogue, not a genealogical cousin.
- **Anjana:** Cantabrian analogue popularly called a cousin, but no old tale documents their kinship.
- **Lamia:** Basque comparative counterpart; no narrative meeting is known.
- **Diana:** proposed etymological ancestor or syncretistic identity, never a character interacting with a Xana.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

**Finding:** No securely identified traditional source records an individual Xana interacting with another specifically named god, goddess, saint, angel, demon, or named member of a divine pantheon.

- **Diana — proposed identity/descent, not an interaction.** Source: Cabal and later Caro Baroja; twentieth-century learned reconstruction. **[SI/SR]**
- **Anjana — comparative “cousin,” not an interaction.** Source: modern regional mythographies. **[RV/LS]**
- **Basque lamia — functional comparison, not an interaction.** Source: Caro Baroja and subsequent comparisons. **[LS]**
- **Cuélebre — guardian/adversarial creature in the broader treasure-and-enchanted-maiden cycle.** Sources: collected Asturian folklore and later systematizations; the term names a class, not an individually named divinity. **[F/LS]**
- **St John — his feast supplies the ritual date, but no securely collected story requires a personal meeting between the saint and a Xana.** **[F; calendrical association only]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The washer and spinner at the spring

**[EA/F]** In the earliest form, little women emerge from springs at night, wash spotless clothing, and spread it in moonlight. Mellado adds the gift of a supernatural skein whose thread remains inexhaustible if wound continuously in the proper direction. The domestic labor is not incidental: washing, spinning, and exact performance are central to many European female-spirit traditions.

Later collectors add golden distaffs, scissors, skeins, and combs. The recipient often loses the gift through impatience, curiosity, impurity, or violation of instructions.

### The Xana and the golden treasure

**[F]** Xanas display gold in the form of combs, thread, hanks, hens and chicks, or bowling equipment. At the Peña del Cueto near Villanueva in Teverga, de Llano reports a Xana guarding a golden hen and chicks on St John’s morning. At La Llera in Villaviciosa, a rider steals a golden hank; the pursuing Xana curses or reproaches him, declaring that he has taken wealth enough for himself and his descendants.

These are treasure legends, not evidence that offerings were historically made to a treasure goddess.

### The *xanín* and changeling cycle

**[F]** A Xana substitutes her old-looking, dark, hairy, voracious, or preternaturally knowing child for a healthy human infant, usually so that the human woman will nurse it. The human mother discovers the substitution through abnormal appearance or behavior.

One test places eggshells or an implausible number of vessels around the hearth. The child betrays its great age by marveling that in a hundred years it has never seen so many pots on the fire. Another strategy is deliberate hunger or rough handling, intended to make the supernatural mother return. She then exchanges the children, with localized formulae such as:

> “Inxana, inxanuda, / lleva a la to cría… / y dame la mía…”

The wording varies and should not be standardized. The tale belongs to the widespread European changeling complex. The Asturian form is securely collected; claims that it reflects a specific ancient medical or sacrificial institution are speculative.

In a more benevolent Grado story, a woman finds a cold *xanín*, wraps it in an ordinary rag, and warms it. The returning Xana says that if she had used a clean white cloth she would have become rich. This version makes ritual cleanliness and hospitality, rather than infant theft, the point.

### The enchanted Xana on St John’s night

**[F/RV]** The enchanted woman appears at a spring, cave, or treasure place at midnight or dawn on 24 June. She asks a human to perform a task exactly. Success frees her and yields marriage or treasure; failure leaves her enchanted and may injure or doom the challenger.

Common forms include:

- winding an unbreakable golden thread without releasing or snapping it;
- preferring the woman over displayed jewels;
- touching her with linen or clothing that has been in a church;
- seizing one of the golden chicks;
- receiving bread of a prescribed shape;
- speaking a set formula such as “Xana amiga, take my poverty and give me your riches.”

The date connects older midsummer-water lore to the Christian feast of St John. The tales do not document a pre-Christian festival named for the Xana.

### The four-cornered loaf

**[F/RV]** In one disenchantment pattern, the hero must bring or preserve a special loaf, frequently with four corners. A missing, eaten, or damaged piece ruins the rite. The tale dramatizes exact reciprocity and taboo. Versions differ about who prepares the bread, whether a wife or family member interferes, and whether failure merely perpetuates the enchantment or carries a curse.

### The serpent woman

**[F/RV]** The Xana warns a man that she will assume a terrifying serpent form. He must allow her to coil around him, kiss her, or take a flower from her mouth. If he holds firm, she resumes human form and is liberated. If he recoils, she strikes him with her comb, curses him, leaves him lame, or predicts his death.

This is closely related to widespread Iberian and European enchanted-woman/serpent tales. Calling it a direct survival of a named Celtic goddess is unsupported.

### Courtship and fairy marriage

**[F]** Some tales allow a pastor or farmer to marry or live with a Xana under a prohibition. Wealth, livestock, food, or children may follow. Violation of the taboo ends the union, after which she returns to water or an enchanted site. Details differ too widely to construct a single marriage myth.

### Captive Xana and cuélebre

**[F/LS]** Modern accounts commonly describe a Xana imprisoned with treasure by a cuélebre and temporarily accessible when the monster sleeps on St John’s night. Treasure guardian, serpent transformation, and enchanted maiden are all traditional motifs, but their fusion into a universal Xana–cuélebre system is substantially the product of later mythographic organization.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrated institutional cult

There is no evidence for:

- temples dedicated to Xana;
- altars or votive inscriptions naming her;
- priesthood;
- a formal festival administered in her name;
- canonical hymns or prayers;
- ancient images;
- theophoric personal names securely derived from a Xana deity;
- organized animal or human sacrifice.

Consequently, “cult of the Xana” is too strong for the documented nineteenth- and twentieth-century record.

### Places

Xana place-names are abundant: *Fuente la Xana*, *Cueva la Xana*, *Cueva les Xanes*, *Peña les Xanes*, and the *Desfiladero de las Xanas*. De Llano lists sites from eastern Asturias to approximately Cudillero, while later lexicographic collections broaden the distribution.

Toponyms demonstrate that landscapes were narrated through the Xana. Their dates are often unknown, however. A place-name first written down in the modern period cannot automatically be assigned to antiquity.

García Arias cautions that even where Roman epigraphy records a *genius*—for example *Genio Conventus Asturicensis* or *Fontis Aginees(is) Genio*—its survival in later toponymy cannot be demonstrated. [His Asturian toponymy study states this evidentiary limitation](https://mas.lne.es/toponimia/index.php?leer=294&palabra=xana).

### Customary actions

**[F]** Human actions in Xana stories include:

- approaching a spring at dawn on St John’s Day;
- watching for supernatural laundry or treasure;
- winding thread;
- offering or using clean white cloth;
- carrying ritual bread;
- repeating an exchange formula;
- testing a changeling at the hearth;
- decorating or visiting water sources in the wider St John’s complex.

The records rarely establish whether narrators personally performed these acts, believed others had done them, or simply transmitted stories. Narrative prescription must not automatically be converted into an observed ritual.

### Sacrifice and allegations

The dedicated contested-material search found no documentary evidence of sacrifice—human or animal—to a Xana. Martín of Braga says generally that pagans had shed animal and human blood for demons and continued to sacrifice to spirits of waters and forests. He does not identify a Xana, a Xana victim, or an Asturian sacrificial site. Applying his general polemic specifically to Xanas would be unjustified.

### Christianity and demonization

Some Xanas are explicitly called Christian, and church objects can break their enchantment. That is not a contradiction: vernacular supernatural beings often inhabit a Christian narrative universe. The Xana is less consistently demonized than many fairies; she may be dangerous, benevolent, cursed, or morally exacting.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the nineteenth century: unattested as Xana

Ancient Iberia possessed numerous indigenous gods, localized *genii*, nymph cults, and sacred waters. Roman and late-antique writers attest worship at natural places. No source connects any one of these securely to the later Xana.

Martín of Braga’s c. 574 sermon demonstrates clerical efforts to redirect offerings and beliefs involving rivers, springs, and forests. It supplies a historical environment in which vernacular water-spirit traditions could have changed, but it does not close the thirteen-century documentary gap before Gil.

### 1839–1849: Romantic discovery

Gil y Carrasco and Mellado placed the *jana/xana* in print. Both wrote picturesque travel or costumbrista prose, genres capable of observation, selection, literary coloring, and classical comparison. Their reports establish the tradition’s existence by the early nineteenth century but not every ornamental detail as verbatim oral testimony.

### 1880–1910: Lexical and regional consolidation

Regional dictionaries and folklore studies classified the Xana as an Asturian nymph or fairy and accumulated local forms. Romantic writers sometimes assimilated her to ondines, nymphs, Valkyries, or Germanic fairies. Such comparisons shaped educated reception.

### 1922–1925: Folkloric canon formation

De Llano’s fieldwork created the principal documentary archive. Cabal arranged the traditions into an expansive “Asturian mythology” and argued for deep origins. This period fixed the modern canonical picture: water maiden, gold comb, spinning, treasure, St John’s appearance, changeling, and disenchantment.

### Mid-twentieth century: comparison and critique

Caro Baroja and later anthropologists placed the Xana within Iberian and European folklore. Scholars increasingly distinguished collected tradition from literary fabrication and warned against declaring every water-fairy motif Celtic or pre-Roman.

### Late twentieth century to present: heritage figure

The Xana became an emblem of Asturian identity, children’s literature, regional art, tourism, commercial naming, and popular mythological catalogues. The Las Xanas gorge and trail are now major heritage-tourism references; the trail itself was cut in the twentieth century, even though the landscape-name preserves the folkloric association. [Asturias’s official tourism site describes the route and its modern construction](https://www.turismoasturias.es/en/senderismo/rutas/senderistas/ruta-de-las-xanas).

There is no reliable evidence of an unbroken organized Xana religion. Individual neopagan or spiritual interpretations may exist, but the search found no documented Xana-centered organization, clergy, membership statistics, liturgy, or continuous cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Cantabrian Anjana

Manuel Llano’s *Las Anjanas* and *Brañaflor* (1931), followed by García-Lomas, helped define the Anjana as Cantabria’s benevolent fairy: long braids, floral crown, white tunic, blue cloak, and magical staff. García-Lomas and later critics questioned whether some Cantabrian beings were partly Llano’s literary creations. Therefore “the Anjana is the Xana’s ancient Cantabrian cousin” should be labeled modern regional systematization, not prehistoric genealogy. The [Government of Cantabria’s educational edition](https://www.educantabria.es/documents/39930/291491/Libro%2BLa%2BAnjana%2By%2Bel%2BOj%C3%A1ncano.%2BMitos%2By%2Bleyendas%2Bde%2BManuel%2BLlano.pdf/656d1b46-669f-8ecb-5414-9f68efb62d9d?t=1679473507690) explicitly presents the material through Manuel Llano’s writings.

### Galician reception

Galician folklore normally assigns the relevant role to the *moura encantada*: a beautiful woman at a spring, river, cave, rock, fort, or archaeological site; often combing her hair, guarding treasure, and awaiting liberation. Cuba, Miranda, and Reigosa’s *Diccionario dos seres míticos galegos* can use formulas reinforcing “Moura = Xana,” but this is a comparative lexicographic equation. A modern Galician collected example describes a beautiful blond woman at a dangerous river pool, transformed into a siren and combing herself with a silver comb; the report was collected by a school pupil from a great-aunt and published in 2005—valuable contemporary folklore, but not ancient evidence. [The account and provenance are supplied by Galicia Encantada](https://galiciaencantada.com/lenda.asp?cat=17&id=402).

### Basque reception

Popular “northern Iberian pantheons” sometimes merge Xana, Anjana, lamia, moura, and Mari. Barandiarán’s and Azkue’s evidence supports comparison among female cave and water beings but not the construction of a single prehistoric goddess under different names. The Xana should not be filed as a Basque goddess without an explicit modern comparative label.

### Occultism

Mario Roso de Luna’s *El tesoro de los lagos de Somiedo* (1916) drew Xanas or Chanas into a theosophical and occult reading of Asturian landscape, comparing them with Greco-Roman undines and placing them in an esoteric cosmology. This is an identifiable modern occult reinterpretation, not transmitted rural doctrine. [The digitized text preserves its occult framing](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1916__roso_de_luna___tesoro_de_los_lagos_de_somiedo.pdf).

No substantial evidence was found for a distinct Golden Dawn, Renaissance Hermetic, grimoire, or chaos-magic tradition centered on the Xana.

### Literature and film

- A short film titled *Xana*, made in 1974 by Jesús-Ángel Prieto’s group, describes its subject as one of the “witches” of Asturian mythology—a modern generic classification rather than traditional consensus. [The film’s record is available on Vimeo](https://vimeo.com/483046741).
- Daína Chaviano uses a Xana encounter in *La isla de los amores infinitos*, where the being gives advice that alters the narrative. This is literary reception, not a primary folklore source.
- Contemporary fantasy art and games commonly merge Xana traits with generic elves, mermaids, naiads, or Celtic goddesses.

### National and commercial identity

The Xana appears in business names, music, souvenirs, hiking promotion, children’s materials, and local-brand imagery. This modern ubiquity has encouraged the impression that a fully articulated “Xana goddess” is ancient. The securely documented older figure is more local, variable, and narratively fragmented.

No well-documented extremist appropriation was located. General “Celtic Asturias” nationalism and heritage discourse sometimes present the Xana as proof of an unbroken Celtic religion, but that conclusion exceeds the evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was the Xana a pre-Roman goddess?

**Evidence for continuity:**

- water and springs received cult in pre-Roman and Roman Iberia;
- indigenous and Roman inscriptions attest local divine powers and nymphs;
- Martín of Braga condemns water- and forest-associated spirits;
- Xana stories concentrate on liminal water sites;
- the name may derive from Diana.

**Evidence against claiming a demonstrated identity:**

- no ancient occurrence of *Xana*;
- no medieval Xana text;
- no altar, image, temple, or ritual inscription;
- roughly thirteen centuries separate Martín from Gil;
- many Xana motifs belong to widely diffused European tale complexes;
- early collectors wrote through Romantic and classicizing categories.

The defensible conclusion is possible historical layering, not proof of an unchanged pre-Roman goddess.

### Diana etymology versus Diana cult

The phonological derivation from *Diāna* is substantially stronger than the historical claim that every Xana attribute comes from Roman Diana. Martín distinguishes spring *Nymphae* from woodland *Dianae*, whereas Xanas characteristically inhabit springs. His categories therefore cannot be quoted as if he wrote “Xanas lived in Asturian fountains.”

### “Celtic” origin

Comparative Celtic interpretations emphasize water, treasure, fairy women, sovereignty, and midsummer liminality. These comparisons may be productive, but none uniquely identifies an Asturian Celtic prototype. Germanic, Romance, Basque, Galician-Portuguese, and pan-European parallels are equally relevant.

### The Anjana problem

The Cantabrian Anjana is regularly treated as ancient because her modern description is vivid. Yet the detailed portrait is mediated by Manuel Llano, and García-Lomas questioned literary fabrication in parts of that system. Comparison in name and function is justified; prehistoric identity is not established.

### Authenticity and collectors

The principal historians occupy different positions:

- Gil and Mellado: Romantic witnesses with literary shaping.
- De Llano: field collector and skeptical gatekeeper, though not operating under modern recording standards.
- Cabal: expansive comparativist and system-builder.
- Caro Baroja: comparative critic stressing diffusion, survival, and the danger of false regional uniqueness.
- Recent popular mythographers: often synthesize incompatible variants and retroject them into prehistory.

A scholarly history of the Xana must therefore study both rural stories and the intellectuals who produced the modern “Asturian pantheon.”

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- The Xana is securely attested in pre-Roman inscriptions. **False.**
- Strabo describes Xanas. **False.**
- Martín of Braga explicitly names Xanas. **False.**
- Endovelicus or Ataecina is the Xana under another name. **Unsupported.**
- Xana, Anjana, Basque lamia, Galician moura, Navia, Deva, and Diana are one historically demonstrated goddess. **Unsupported synthesis.**
- Human sacrifice to Xanas is documented. **No supporting evidence found.**
- A continuous organized Xana cult survived from antiquity to modern neopaganism. **No supporting evidence found.**
- Every Xana is imprisoned by a cuélebre. **Later harmonization of variable motifs.**
- The “Xana queen” is a traditional sovereign of a formal fairy hierarchy. **Predominantly modern elaboration.**

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Pre-Roman period | Sacred-water practices and indigenous gods are attested elsewhere in northwestern Iberia, but no Xana is named. |
| Roman period | Nymphs, *genii*, Diana, and indigenous deities appear in Iberian religion; no inscription identifies any of them as Xana. |
| c. 574 | Martín of Braga’s *De correctione rusticorum* condemns sacrifice to spirits he classifies as Lamiae, Nymphs, and Dianae. Contextual evidence only. |
| c. 906–12th century | The continental *Canon Episcopi* tradition condemns women’s supposed nocturnal following of Diana. It contributes to the wider medieval history of Diana as a fairy/witch name but is not an Asturian Xana record. |
| 1838 | Gil y Carrasco travels through Asturias and records notes about local *janas*. |
| 12 May 1839 | “Los asturianos” prints the first presently located secure description of *janas*. |
| 1849 | Mellado’s *Recuerdos de un viaje por España* prints a fuller *xana* description with crystal palaces and magical thread. |
| 1880 | Apolinar de Rato’s Asturian vocabulary lexically records *xana* as a popular mythological nymph. |
| 1896 | Braulio Vigón’s Colunga vocabulary contributes regional folklore and lexical documentation. |
| Late 19th–early 20th c. | Folklore and local studies multiply Xana sites, names, and stories; Romantic comparisons with ondines, nymphs, and northern European fairies proliferate. |
| 1916 | Roso de Luna incorporates Xanas into a theosophical reading of Somiedo. |
| 1922 | Aurelio de Llano publishes the principal field-based Xana corpus in *Del folklore asturiano*. |
| 1925 | Constantino Cabal systematizes Xana origins, etymology, and comparative mythology. |
| 1931 | Manuel Llano’s Cantabrian Anjana writings consolidate the neighboring figure’s modern form. |
| Mid-20th c. | Caro Baroja places Xanas within broader Leonese, Iberian, and European traditions and criticizes narrow regionalism. |
| 1964–67 | García-Lomas systematizes Cantabrian mythology while questioning the authenticity of some literary beings. |
| 1974 | The short film *Xana* adapts the being to cinema. |
| Late 20th c. | Regional dictionaries, folklore encyclopedias, children’s books, and heritage projects stabilize the familiar blond, gold-combed water-fairy image. |
| 1999 | Cuba, Miranda, and Reigosa’s Galician dictionary formalizes comparisons between the *moura* and Xana. |
| 2002–04 | García Arias’s *Diccionario General de la Lengua Asturiana* consolidates lexical forms, distributions, citations, sayings, and transferred meanings. |
| 21st c. | The Xana remains prominent in Asturian tourism, literature, popular art, commercial naming, and internet mythography; no continuous institutional cult has been demonstrated. |
| 2026 | Popular writing continues both critical documentation and unsupported claims of direct Celtic or pre-Roman continuity. |

### Source assessment

The strongest evidence is the nineteenth-century printed record followed by de Llano’s early-twentieth-century field collection and later lexical mapping. The ancient-continuity argument is circumstantial and comparative. The documentary record supports a rich, regionally variable Xana tradition; it does not support a recoverable pre-Roman Xana theology, institutional cult, or pantheon genealogy.
