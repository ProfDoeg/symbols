# Mari: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the locally collected Basque material, a female *numen*, cave-dwelling Lady, mountain spirit, witch, saint-like being, or supernatural woman, depending on locality; in José Miguel de Barandiarán’s twentieth-century synthesis, the principal female *numen* of Basque mythology; in later popular, philosophical, and neopagan treatments, a “goddess,” “Mother Earth,” or queen of a reconstructed pantheon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The name **Mari** is not attested for a goddess in ancient inscriptions or classical literature. The earliest potentially ancestral narrative is the supernatural Lady of Biscay in Pedro Afonso, Count of Barcelos’s *Livro de linhagens*, compiled c. 1340–1344; that being is not called Mari, and identifying her with Mari is a later folkloristic comparison. Lope García de Salazar’s *Crónica de siete casas de Vizcaya y Castilla* (1454) records the related Biscayan *Culebro* tradition. The earliest secure written notice of the **Lady of Anboto** is Esteban de Garibay’s late-sixteenth-century memoir, written near the end of his life and first printed posthumously in 1854. “Mari” as the unifying name for a network of localized Ladies was established chiefly by Barandiarán in the twentieth century. No Roman-period altar, inscription, temple, or securely identified ancient image names Mari.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Documentary identification

Mari is best understood historically as a **scholarly unification of related but not necessarily identical local beings**. Barandiarán grouped under this name the Lady of Anboto, Lady of Murumendi, Lady of Aralar, Witch of Aketegi, Red-Skirt of Lescun, and numerous cave or mountain women. The current Auñamendi synthesis explicitly states that the common designation *Mari* is owed to Barandiarán, who chose it to express what he considered the beings’ “substantial unity.” That unity is therefore a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not something demonstrated by an ancient text or a pan-Basque liturgy.

The locally attested figure is characteristically:

- female;
- resident in caves, chasms, mountains, or an underground realm;
- mobile between mountain dwellings;
- visible as a woman, animal, gust, cloud, burning object, fiery sickle, or fireball;
- connected with thunderstorms, hail, wind, rain, and the fortunes of crops;
- rich in gold and frequently seen spinning or combing her hair;
- responsive to offerings, petitions, proper etiquette, promises, and moral conduct;
- hostile to lying, boasting, theft, breach of promises, denial of hospitality, and refusal of mutual assistance;
- sometimes married to a serpent-like male being, Maju, Sugaar, Sugoi, or Suarra;
- sometimes married to a mortal;
- sometimes mother of Atarrabi and Mikelats, or of seven unnamed children;
- sometimes queen of witches, *lamiñak*, or subterranean beings;
- sometimes herself called a witch, saint, malignant being, or simply “the Lady.”

These categories should not be collapsed. “Goddess,” “witch,” “spirit,” and “Lady” reflect different collectors, periods, and interpretive systems.

### Etymology

No etymology is secure.

1. **Christian María hypothesis.** *Mari* and *Maddi* are Basque forms associated with María, while *Andre Maria/Andra Mari* is a traditional Basque designation of the Virgin Mary. This makes Christian influence a straightforward explanation for at least part of the name-complex. Julio Caro Baroja and others emphasized the depth of Christian reshaping in Basque folklore.

2. **Basque internal derivations.** Popular and speculative proposals derive Mari by apheresis from *Amari*—interpreted as *ama* (“mother”) plus an occupational or active suffix—or from *emari*, “gift.” These derivations are not demonstrated by early forms.

3. **Mairi/Maide/Maindi comparison.** Barandiarán noted possible formal comparison with *Mairi*, legendary builders of megaliths; *Maide*, male mountain beings; and *Maindi*, interpreted in some districts as ancestral souls. He also stressed that their narrative cycles differ.

4. **Maia/Maya.** Caro Baroja sometimes preferred Maia/Mayi when comparing the Lady with Mediterranean female divinities. This is comparative interpretation, not a securely reconstructed ancient theonym.

5. **“Mother-thread” derivations.** Recent claims connecting *Mari* to *ama* “mother” and *hari* “thread,” or to an alleged primordial maternal lineage, are modern symbolic etymologies, not established historical linguistics.

### Classification history

- **Medieval:** unnamed fairy-like or demonic Lady in aristocratic lineage narratives.
- **Late sixteenth century:** Lady of Anboto, reportedly credited with prophecy and treated within Christian categories of superstition or witchcraft.
- **Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century folklore:** Lady, witch, cave woman, supernatural woman, or local spirit.
- **Barandiarán:** female *numen*, central to a reconstructed Basque mythological complex.
- **Ortiz-Osés and related interpreters:** archetypal Mother Goddess or personification of a pre-Indo-European, “matrial” worldview.
- **Modern popular culture and neopaganism:** supreme goddess, Earth goddess, queen of a Basque pantheon.

The last two classifications exceed what the earliest texts themselves establish.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Ancient evidence: an explicit absence

Neither Strabo nor other Greco-Roman writers names Mari. Strabo, *Geography* 3.4.16, says that Celtiberians and peoples neighboring them to the north worshipped an unnamed divinity at full moon with nocturnal family dances. Assigning this rite specifically to the Vascones is already an inference; identifying the unnamed god with Mari is unsupported.

Roman-period Latin votive inscriptions from the western Pyrenees name such beings as Leherennus, Baicorrixus, Ilixo, Artahe, Erge, and others. They demonstrate vigorous local cults and Roman-indigenous syncretism, but **none names Mari**. The same caution applies to Endovelicus, Ataecina, Nabia, Bandua, Reue, Cosus, and other divinities of western Hispania: no inscription equates any of them with Mari.

Consequently:

- Mari has no known ancient inscription.
- She has no securely identified Roman altar.
- She has no ancient temple.
- She has no securely identified prehistoric image.
- She is not named by Strabo.
- The Hand of Irulegi does not mention her.
- Endovelicus and Ataecina are not forms of Mari.
- Asturian, Cantabrian, Galician, Lusitanian, and Celtiberian cult evidence does not document her.

### *Livro de linhagens*, c. 1340–1344

Pedro Afonso’s Portuguese *Livro de linhagens* contains the earliest securely dated narrative often compared with the Mari cycle. Diego López, Lord of Biscay, hears a beautiful, richly dressed woman singing on a rock while he hunts. She says that she is of very high lineage and agrees to marry him if he promises never to cross or bless himself. She has a cloven foot like a goat. They have a son, Íñiguez Guerra, and a daughter. When Diego makes the sign of the cross during a disturbing scene between household dogs, the woman seizes the daughter, leaps through a window, and disappears into the mountains. She later supplies a supernatural horse that enables her son to rescue his father.

This is a **medieval aristocratic foundation legend of the Melusine type**. The woman is not called Mari or Lady of Anboto. Caro Baroja and later writers linked the story to the cave-Lady cycle because of the supernatural wife, animal feature, anti-Christian taboo, mountain departure, and Biscayan setting. That is a reasonable comparative observation but not proof of identity. The critical date is c. 1340–1344, not the erroneous sixteenth-century dating sometimes repeated in encyclopedic summaries.

### Lope García de Salazar, 1454

In the *Crónica de siete casas de Vizcaya y Castilla*, García de Salazar reports that a Scottish princess at Mundaka was impregnated in a dream by a devil called **Culebro**, “lord of the house,” and bore Don Zuria, legendary first lord of Biscay. Later folklorists compared Culebro with Sugaar/Sugoi, Mari’s serpent partner. Again, the source does not name Mari as the princess or mother.

### Esteban de Garibay, late sixteenth century

Garibay records the Lady of Anboto as a being about whom people sought information or prophecy. This is the first secure textual witness to the Anboto figure. Garibay’s memoir was composed in the late sixteenth century; the relevant manuscript material became widely available through nineteenth-century publication. It establishes that a Lady associated with Anboto was known in early-modern Biscay. It does not establish a prehistoric cult, temples, priestesses, or a complete pantheon.

### Early-modern witchcraft records

The Labourd persecutions of 1609 and the Spanish Inquisition’s Zugarramurdi proceedings of 1609–1614 constitute a large documentary corpus concerning alleged witches, sabbaths, goats, caves, poisons, flights, apostasy, and the Devil. Pierre de Lancre’s *Tableau de l’inconstance des mauvais anges et démons* appeared in 1612; Juan de Mongastón’s pamphlet about the Logroño auto-da-fé appeared in 1611. Alonso de Salazar Frías subsequently investigated the claims and found pervasive evidentiary failure.

These records are evidence for persecution, interrogatory scripts, rumor, confession, recantation, and demonological imagination. They **do not securely document worship of Mari at Zugarramurdi**. The later placement of Zugarramurdi’s Akelarre cave among Mari’s residences reflects folkloristic synthesis. Equating the accused women with “priestesses of Mari” is a modern invention.

### Nineteenth-century Romantic literature

Gertrudis Gómez de Avellaneda’s *La dama de Amboto* and other nineteenth-century retellings transformed local tradition into Romantic literary legend. Alexandre Herculano’s *A Dama Pé-de-Cabra*, published in *Lendas e narrativas* in 1851, reworked the medieval Biscayan cloven-footed Lady. These works materially influenced later images of a noble, tragic, pagan mountain woman but are not transcripts of pre-Christian belief.

### Resurrección María de Azkue

Azkue gathered oral traditions from Basque speakers from the late nineteenth century until the Spanish Civil War interrupted his work in 1936. *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*, published in four volumes between 1935 and 1947, includes Mari Urraca, the Lady of Anboto, the Lady of Murumendi, *lamiñak*, beliefs, customs, and local narratives. Azkue’s witnesses are primary ethnographic evidence for what informants then said; their testimony cannot by itself date each story to antiquity.

### José Miguel de Barandiarán

Barandiarán’s decisive contribution began with early articles and *Mitología vasca* (1924), continued through the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore*, and culminated in syntheses including the *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca* and his collected works. He documented informants, places, vocabulary, ritual etiquette, caves, narratives, and variants more systematically than his predecessors.

His work is simultaneously:

- the indispensable primary record of twentieth-century oral testimony;
- a scholarly classification;
- the principal mechanism by which many local Ladies became the single modern figure “Mari.”

### Later studies

Julio Caro Baroja compared Mari and the Basque *lamiñak* with European supernatural-wife, fairy, and Melusine traditions. Juan Inazio Hartsuaga distinguished the popular material from intellectual constructions such as Mari-as-Mother-Earth, explicitly observing that “Mari as incarnation of Mother Earth” is not itself a popular concept but an elaboration by mythologists. Anuntxi Arana has challenged the assimilation of every local female being to Mari and favors “Lady of Anboto” or *Anderea* when the evidence warrants it. Andrés Ortiz-Osés developed an influential but highly interpretive hermeneutic of Mari as the central Great Mother of a pre-Indo-European Basque worldview.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Mountains, caves, and the underground

Mari’s strongest documented domain is not an abstract theological office but a **landscape network**: caves, chasms, peaks, weather, and the movement of storms between them. Reported residences include:

- Anboto and Mariurrika/Mariren cave;
- Balzola at Dima;
- Supelaur at Orozko;
- Atxorrotx at Eskoriatza;
- Zaldiaran;
- Aketegi in the Aizkorri massif;
- Agamunda and several Sugaar caves at Ataun;
- Murumendi near Beasain;
- Marizulo near Amezketa;
- Txindoki/Aralar;
- Obantzun at Berastegi;
- Odabe near Altsasu;
- Akelarre at Zugarramurdi;
- Lezia at Sara;
- Zelharburu at Bidarray;
- Azalegi at Alçay;
- Otsibarre;
- Kakueta in Zuberoa;
- Gorbea and other locally named cavities.

The lists differ by collector and informant. They do not prove that every community conceived the resident being as one person travelling among all the caves.

### Weather

The clearest recurring power is control or production of weather.

- Smoke issuing from a cave may be explained as the smoke from Mari’s oven.
- Clouds and storms emerge from the subterranean world through her caves.
- Her movement from one residence to another can announce a storm.
- Her anger produces hail or destructive weather.
- Her meeting with Maju/Sugaar causes a furious storm.
- In some accounts she delegates the direction of clouds to Odei/Hodei, Eate, or Eluauso.
- Weather may depend on which cave she presently occupies: favorable in one district and destructive in another.

People accordingly petitioned clergy to conjure or seal a cave, redirected storm clouds through gestures and formulas, offered animals or gifts, or celebrated Mass at a cave mouth. These are local weather rites and legends, not evidence for a standardized priesthood.

### Forms

Reported appearances include:

- a beautiful, richly clothed woman;
- a long-haired woman combing herself with a golden comb;
- a woman spinning or winding golden thread;
- a woman with an animal foot;
- a woman trailing flame;
- a fireball;
- a flaming sickle or crescent;
- a fiery horse or carriage;
- a gust of wind;
- cloud, lightning, or rainbow-like fire;
- vulture, ram, goat, sheep, cow, calf, bull, horse, dog, pig, or serpent.

Some animal manifestations may originally belong to independent cave beings later absorbed into the Mari complex. A ram beside Mari is recurrent in modern descriptions, but it is not a universally attested identifying emblem.

### Gold, domestic work, and wealth

Inside her dwelling Mari possesses golden furniture or treasure. She spins, winds thread, combs her hair, bakes bread, or receives visitors. A stolen golden comb must be returned after threatening voices or misfortune. These details present the cave as an inverted or magnified household.

### Moral authority

Barandiarán’s synthesis attributes to Mari punishment of:

- lies;
- theft;
- boasting and arrogance;
- breach of one’s word;
- disrespect;
- denial of assistance;
- denial that one possesses goods.

The proverb commonly paraphrased as “what is given to denial, denial takes away” explains her claim over animals, crops, or possessions whose owners falsely deny having them. These ethical motifs are well attested in collected folklore, though describing them as an ancient “religious code of Mari” would go beyond the evidence.

### Ritual etiquette

Visitors to a Mari cave are instructed to:

- address her familiarly rather than with deferential plural speech;
- avoid sitting;
- leave in the same orientation as they entered;
- never turn their back on her;
- keep promises;
- answer honestly.

Calling “Lady of Aketegi” three times is said in some accounts to obtain aid or counsel.

### Iconography

No ancient or medieval image is securely identified as Mari. The cloven-footed Lady exists in written medieval narrative but not in a securely associated cult statue. Modern artists generally show Mari as:

- a regal long-haired woman;
- a red-clad or flame-surrounded woman;
- a woman at a cave mouth;
- a woman with a golden comb;
- an airborne fiery female figure;
- an Earth Mother accompanied by ram, serpent, moon, or mountains.

These are modern visualizations assembled from folkloric motifs. Venus figurines, prehistoric “goddess” figures, cave images, and Roman female statuettes cannot be assigned to Mari without independent evidence.

Colors, numbers, and celestial assignments are similarly unstable. Red follows *Yona-gorri* and fire imagery; seven occurs in seven-year residence cycles, seven children, and seven Marian sisters. Neither is demonstrably an ancient canonical sacred correspondence.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### No single genealogy

The corpus gives no stable birth story or parents. Mari can instead be:

- an eternal or unexplained cave resident;
- a human girl cursed by her mother;
- a girl handed over to a cave being;
- a noble supernatural woman;
- a mortal’s wife;
- a mother refusing baptism for her children;
- partner of a serpent being;
- mother of paired good and evil sons;
- one of several sister-Ladies;
- the single being whose local forms visit one another.

The absence of parents or cosmogony is significant. Modern descriptions of her as creator of the universe or mother of all Basque gods are not securely present in the early corpus.

### Sugaar, Maju, Sugoi, Suarra

In Azkoitia, Maju is Mari’s husband. In Goierri, Sugaar—literally interpreted as “male serpent”—is treated as the corresponding figure; Dima has Sugoi and Betelu Suarra. He travels as a fiery sickle, serpent, lightning-like fire, or human. In Zarautz testimony, he visits Mari on Friday afternoons to comb her hair; elsewhere the pair meet every Friday. Their union produces violent rain and hail.

The equation Maju = Sugaar = Sugoi = medieval Culebro is plausible within Barandiarán’s comparative system but remains a systematization of regional names.

### Atarrabi and Mikelats

Some accounts call Atarrabi and Mikelats Mari’s children—sometimes sons, sometimes with grammatical or retelling variation. Atarrabi represents beneficial power and becomes a guardian or priest; Mikelats produces storms and attempts to destroy crops. In the Sara narrative, Atarrabi’s prayer or conjuration defeats Mikelats’s destructive weather.

Other stories make Atarrabi, Mikelats, and Axular students or companions in a cave-school under a supernatural master. Their genealogy is therefore not uniform.

### Mortal spouses and children

An Ataun version says Mari married a young man from the Burugoena farm at Beasain and bore seven children. Because she was not Christian, she refused to baptize them. When her husband tried to carry her and the children to church, she escaped in flame to Murumendi, declaring: *nee umeek zeruako, ta ni oaiñ Muruako*—approximately, “my children for heaven, and now I for Muru.” Other versions alter the place, number of children, constraint, and words.

The medieval Lady of Biscay similarly marries Diego López under a religious prohibition and escapes when he crosses himself. The structural resemblance is strong, but direct continuity cannot be proven.

### Sisters and doubled Ladies

In Markina, Marije Kobako is sometimes the sister of the Lady of Anboto, and the two visit one another. This contradicts the scholarly claim that every local Lady is one Mari. Barandiarán compared the duplication to traditions in which local images of the Virgin Mary are popularly called sisters although theologians regard them as manifestations of one Virgin.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The moving Lady and the fiery flight

**Folklore core:** Mari changes residences among mountains and caves. Witnesses see a ball of fire, burning sickle, fiery tail, or flaming woman cross the sky. Her entry into the new cave produces a thunderous noise. Storms follow or her movement foretells them.

A Murumendi version says that she flies to Txindoki as a fireball or fiery sickle. An Orozko account describes men gathering fern who see an enormous fireball cross toward Gorbea; the oldest identifies it as the Lady of Anboto. The people and oxen are terrified. In Olazagutía, María Roca flies from Aitzgorri to Auzkolarre with tail ablaze, scattering burning brands.

**Variant:** At Zeanuri she spends seven years at Anboto and seven at Supelegor/Oiz. Elsewhere residence cycles differ or are absent.

**Development:** Meteor, lightning, cloud, wind, and fire imagery may have been locally attached to several beings before Barandiarán consolidated them.

### Mari and the making of storms

Mari generates storms in subterranean regions and releases them from caves. In other versions she directs weather through Odei, Eate, Eluauso, or Mikelats. Her mood, location, or meeting with Sugaar determines rain and hail.

The cycle explains:

- clouds gathering at a peak;
- smoke or mist from caves;
- thunder at mountain entrances;
- hail descending from a particular direction;
- why adjacent valleys receive different weather;
- why ritual specialists and priests attempt to turn storms away.

This is the most geographically persistent and functionally coherent part of the Mari complex.

### Mari and Sugaar

Maju/Sugaar visits Mari, commonly on Friday. At Zarautz he combs her hair; in Azkoitia the meeting causes a violent storm. The sexual or marital reading is explicit in the collected material, though elaborate modern accounts of their cosmic union, fertility theology, or dualistic Earth-and-Sky marriage are later expansions.

### The denied goods

A person falsely says that crops, animals, food, or possessions do not exist or are unavailable. Because the owner has “given” them to denial, Mari takes them. A shepherd may lose a ram; a household loses goods it concealed; boasts and lies are punished.

The story enforces truthfulness and hospitality. It does not require a theological doctrine that Mari is omniscient judge of the world.

### The golden comb

A shepherd or youth sees Mari combing her hair with a golden comb at a cave mouth. Someone steals the comb. Voices, threats, illness, fear, or supernatural pressure force its return.

The motif overlaps widely with European fairy, *lamia*, moura, and water-woman tales. It may belong to a broader supernatural-woman repertoire rather than to an originally discrete “myth of Mari.”

### Marriage to a mortal and refusal of baptism

Mari marries a mortal and has children. Her refusal to enter a church or allow baptism exposes her other-than-Christian nature. When forcibly taken toward church, she escapes in fire and returns to her mountain.

Variants include:

- husband’s breach of a prohibition;
- mother’s curse;
- seven children;
- two children;
- church at Beasain;
- Murumendi as destination;
- transformation into fire;
- animal features concealed until marriage.

The medieval cloven-footed Lady of Biscay belongs to the same broad supernatural-wife tale type but should remain separately identified.

### The cursed girl

A mother, enraged at her daughter, utters a curse such as wishing that the Devil or mountain being take her. The girl disappears into a cave and becomes or is identified with the Lady. Other versions say she habitually avoided church or was delivered to a cave woman instead of attending Mass.

This is plainly framed through Christian sin, curse, church attendance, and demonization. It is not secure evidence of Mari’s pre-Christian birth mythology.

### Atarrabi and Mikelats

Mari’s opposed children embody beneficent and destructive forces. Mikelats sends hail to ruin a community’s crops; Atarrabi, now its guardian or priest, counters him through prayer. This cycle combines local weather beings with Christian clerical power and moral dualism.

### The Lady of Biscay

In the medieval narrative, Diego López marries the cloven-footed supernatural Lady on condition that he never bless himself. When he does so, she escapes with their daughter. Her son later seeks her help to rescue his father.

The story serves aristocratic genealogy and political legitimacy. Calling its protagonist “Mari” is a later interpretive act. Comparison with Melusine and other European fairy ancestresses cautions against treating every element as uniquely Basque.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrated temple cult

There is no excavated Mari temple, dedicatory inscription, cult statue, liturgical calendar, named ancient priesthood, or financial archive. “Cult of Mari” must therefore be used carefully. The recoverable evidence concerns folklore, household belief, landscape practice, weather ritual, and offerings, chiefly recorded in modern ethnography.

### Caves and offerings

Reported practices include:

- leaving or delivering a ram at a cave;
- giving annual gifts;
- asking advice at the cave;
- invoking the Lady three times;
- entering and departing according to prescribed etiquette;
- returning a stolen object;
- requesting favorable weather;
- taking Mass, prayer, or conjuration to a cave entrance.

Some accounts say that a ram was the largest annual gift. The historical date and continuity of each practice are seldom recoverable.

### Mugiro procession

A tradition from Mugiro says villagers formerly processed on 3 May to a nearby chasm of Mari, where the priest celebrated Mass at its entrance. The Christian Feast of the Invention of the Cross and the Mass frame the rite. It is evidence for Christian management of a feared or sacred landscape, not an unchanged pagan ceremony.

### The alleged offering by the Lords of Biscay

A modern encyclopedic synthesis reports that fourteenth-century Lords of Biscay offered cow entrails at a rock in Busturia. This claim requires caution: the surviving medieval lineage texts do not straightforwardly document an institutional cult of Mari under that name. It may combine noble foundation legend, later testimony, and folkloric interpretation. It should not be repeated as certain evidence of a fourteenth-century Mari priesthood.

### Priests and weather conjuration

Clergy appear not as Mari’s priests but as Christian specialists asked to:

- say Mass;
- bless or seal a cave;
- prevent Mari’s departure;
- redirect storms;
- counter subterranean or demonic forces.

This is evidence of accommodation, pastoral response, and Christian recoding.

### Sacrifice and violence

No reliable source documents human sacrifice to Mari. Classical reports of human sacrifice among Lusitanians or other northern Iberian peoples cannot be transferred to Mari. No evidence documents temple prostitution, organized cult violence, or ritual killing in her name.

Animal offerings, especially rams, occur in folklore. Their dating, frequency, and sacrificial form are uncertain.

### Zugarramurdi

The inquisitorial events began after accusations in 1608 and culminated in the Logroño auto-da-fé of November 1610. Six living defendants were executed and the effigies or remains of others were burned. Salazar’s later investigations undermined the mass allegations.

The trials document judicial violence and the construction of a diabolical sabbath. They do not establish that the accused worshipped Mari. Modern claims that Zugarramurdi preserved an organized Mari cult or that *sorginak* were her priestesses are unsupported.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Roman antiquity

The western Pyrenees and northern Hispania possessed many local gods, recorded chiefly in Latin votive epigraphy. Mari is absent. Strabo’s anonymous northern lunar rite supplies context for religious diversity, not an attestation of Mari.

### Middle Ages, c. 1340–1454

Supernatural-woman and serpent-ancestor narratives enter written noble genealogy. The *Livro de linhagens* gives the cloven-footed Lady of Biscay; García de Salazar gives Culebro and the conception of Don Zuria. Their political function is the construction of the antiquity and distinction of Biscayan lordship.

### Late sixteenth century

Garibay attests the Lady of Anboto. The being exists within an already Christian society and is approached by writers through categories of superstition, prophecy, witchcraft, and demonic deception.

### Seventeenth century

Basque-speaking communities undergo witch persecutions in Labourd and Navarre. Elite demonology, local feuds, children’s accusations, confession procedures, and rumor interact. The Mari complex is not clearly named in the central trial records, but later folklore inherits the association of caves, night gatherings, goats, witches, and illicit supernatural women.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Local oral tradition continues while Romantic and antiquarian authors literaryize the Lady. The medieval cloven-footed woman becomes a tragic fairy or pagan ancestress; Anboto becomes emblematic landscape. Garibay’s material appears in print.

### 1890s–1930s: ethnographic collection

Azkue and Barandiarán record testimony from rural Basque speakers. The material is still plural: local names, local caves, conflicting genealogies, Christian prayers, priests, saints, witches, and remembered offerings.

### 1920s–1970s: construction of the canonical Mari

Barandiarán organizes the data into a Mari cycle and gives Mari a central place among Basque *numina*. His documentation preserves material that might otherwise have disappeared, but his classification also produces the now-familiar unified being.

Caro Baroja situates the tales within wider European folklore and Christian history, challenging simple claims of unchanged prehistoric survival.

### Late twentieth century: Great Mother interpretation

Ortiz-Osés and allied authors interpret Mari as Mother Earth, a survival of pre-Indo-European or Neolithic matriarchal symbolism, and the organizing center of Basque psychic and cultural identity. Marija Gimbutas’s “Old Europe” model supplies comparative vocabulary.

These claims are influential but evidentially weak when stated as history. No continuous chain connects Neolithic female images to the modern Mari corpus. Hartsuaga explicitly distinguishes the popular narratives from the learned identification of Mari with Mother Earth.

### Present

Mari remains prominent in museums, tourism, children’s literature, fantasy art, comics, games, music, environmental rhetoric, feminist art, and Basque cultural education. Small numbers of eclectic pagans venerate Mari, but no reliable census establishes the scale or continuous institutional descent of such worship.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Basque identity

Mari’s transformation into a national emblem accompanies the larger modern use of language, folklore, archaeology, and landscape in defining Basque difference. Academic work on folklore and nationalism notes how myth and memory have been mobilized in constructing Basque identity. Mari’s advantages as a symbol are clear: she is female, attached to unmistakably Basque place-names, embedded in an oral corpus, and not reducible to Greco-Roman mythology.

This cultural role does not make the folklore false; it changes its form. A plurality of village Ladies becomes one emblematic national goddess.

### Matriarchy and feminism

Twentieth-century theories of “Basque matriarchy” used Mari as evidence of an ancient female-centered culture. Ortiz-Osés described a “matrial” symbolic system opposed to Indo-European patriarchal sky religion. Feminist artists and writers have recovered Mari as autonomous, sensual, shape-changing, disobedient, ecological, and resistant to Christian or male control.

Other scholars and Basque commentators reject the inference that stories about a powerful female being prove that historical Basque society was feminist or matriarchal. Mari can support modern feminist meaning without serving as proof of prehistoric social structure.

### Psychoanalytic and Jungian interpretation

Mari has been interpreted as Great Mother, anima, Earth archetype, mediator between subterranean and celestial domains, and representation of a Basque collective unconscious. These readings belong to twentieth-century depth psychology and philosophical hermeneutics. They are not indigenous explanations recorded in the earliest testimonies.

### Neopaganism and occult use

Modern practitioners variously worship Mari as:

- Earth Mother;
- supreme Basque goddess;
- chthonic goddess;
- weather goddess;
- consort of Sugaar;
- head of a Goddess-and-Horned-God polarity;
- patron of witches;
- ecological deity.

These systems typically combine Barandiarán, modern Goddess spirituality, Wicca, environmentalism, and online mythography. Claims that historical *sorginak* formed a secret Mari priesthood, or that an unbroken Mari religion survived the Inquisition, are not documented.

Mari is not a standard figure in the historical Renaissance grimoires, Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or classical ceremonial magic. Her presence in contemporary occultism is mainly regional, eclectic, and post-twentieth-century.

### Literature and visual culture

Important stages include:

- medieval Biscayan lineage narrative;
- Herculano’s *A Dama Pé-de-Cabra* (1851);
- Gómez de Avellaneda’s nineteenth-century Lady of Anboto;
- folkloric collections by Azkue and Barandiarán;
- late twentieth- and twenty-first-century illustrated mythologies;
- fantasy novels, comics, sculpture, educational animation, games, and folk or metal music.

Modern images of Mari as a conventionally beautiful woman flying in flame are artistic reconstructions, not copies of ancient iconography.

### Political appropriation

Mari has been used in nationalist, feminist, ecological, and anti-clerical discourse. The broad equation “Mari = primordial Basque nation = matriarchal freedom” is a modern ideological construction. No evidence connects her securely with a specific contemporary political party or establishes an ancient territorial state cult.

No substantial documented extremist cult centered on Mari has emerged. General racial or primordialist claims that she proves unchanged descent from Paleolithic Europe are pseudohistorical when presented as established fact.

### Commercial use and tourism

Anboto, its caves, and “Mari’s dwelling” are promoted in cultural tourism. Mari appears on souvenirs, books, posters, festivals, guided walks, and cultural websites. Zugarramurdi’s witch-trial history is likewise commercialized, sometimes blurring the distinction between inquisitorial accusation, folklore, and modern witch identity.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Barandiarán: preservation and synthesis

Barandiarán’s strength lies in geographical detail, informants, variants, vocabulary, and restraint about unverifiable prehistoric claims. Yet the singular “Mari” is partly his classificatory achievement. Later summaries often convert his cautious *numen* into a fully systematized supreme goddess.

### Caro Baroja: comparison and Christian context

Caro Baroja stressed European parallels: *lamiae*, fairy wives, Melusine traditions, noble genealogical myth, witchcraft discourse, and Christian reinterpretation. His work complicates ethnic-essentialist claims that every Mari motif is uniquely or immemorially Basque.

### Hartsuaga: structure without naïve antiquity

Hartsuaga employs comparative mythology but distinguishes folk narrative from scholarly metaphysics. His observation that Mari-as-Mother-Earth is an intellectual construction is crucial. A being can function through caves, storms, and terrestrial forces without informants identifying her as the planet or universal Earth Mother.

### Arana: dismantling the single Mari

Anuntxi Arana questions whether all local Ladies, *lamiñak*, witches, and supernatural wives should be assimilated to Mari. Her preference for *Anderea* or “Lady of Anboto” foregrounds the local beings obscured by canonical systematization.

### Ortiz-Osés and the Great Mother school

Ortiz-Osés’s hermeneutic reading has been culturally influential. It treats Mari as the Great Mother at the center of a pre-Indo-European, telluric, matrifocal Basque symbolic order. Its value is interpretive rather than demonstrative: archaeology and ancient texts do not supply the required historical chain.

### Major disputed questions

**Was Mari prehistoric?**  
Possibly some motifs are old, but no source securely carries her name or complete persona before the Middle Ages. Claims of Paleolithic or Neolithic continuity are reconstructions.

**Is Mari a goddess?**  
“Goddess” is defensible as a modern comparative label for a powerful female *numen*. It is not the sole indigenous classification. Informants also called her Lady, witch, saint, malign being, or Mary.

**Is she Mother Earth?**  
This is the dominant modern interpretation, not a securely recorded early folk identification.

**Is the name pre-Christian?**  
Uncertain. Derivation from María remains plausible; internal Basque proposals are possible but unproven.

**Is the medieval cloven-footed Lady Mari?**  
The tales share motifs, geography, and later reception, but the medieval text does not call her Mari. Identity is a comparative hypothesis.

**Is Sugaar certainly her ancient husband?**  
Some local testimony explicitly calls Maju/Sugaar her husband. The consolidation of Maju, Sugaar, Sugoi, Suarra, and Culebro into one primordial consort is secondary.

**Were the Zugarramurdi accused Mari worshippers?**  
No surviving trial evidence establishes that. This is a modern romantic or neopagan interpretation.

**Did the Basques preserve a matriarchal religion?**  
Powerful female folklore and certain inheritance or household customs do not prove prehistoric matriarchy. The thesis remains controversial and is often presented more confidently in popular than critical scholarship.

**Does Mari belong to an Iberian-wide pantheon?**  
No evidence establishes this. The documented Mari complex is Basque and western-Pyrenean. Similar supernatural women elsewhere in Iberia invite folktale comparison, not automatic identity.

### Relation to Asturian, Cantabrian, Galician, and pre-Roman traditions

Asturian *xanas*, Cantabrian *anjanas*, Galician *mouras*, and widespread Iberian enchanted Ladies share caves, springs, treasure, combing, taboos, supernatural marriage, and animal features. Cabal, Aurelio de Llano, Manuel Llano, García-Lomas, Risco, Lisón Tolosana, and later dictionaries document these regional corpora. Similarity may reflect shared European tale types, medieval transmission, neighboring folklore, or older substrate traditions.

No primary source calls these beings Mari, and no inscription connects Mari with Ataecina or Endovelicus. They should remain parallel corpora.

## 11. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

The fullest defensible conclusion is not that Mari was securely the supreme goddess of an unchanged prehistoric Basque religion. It is that northern Basque communities preserved a dense body of traditions about mountain and cave Ladies, supernatural wives, weather-makers, fiery aerial beings, treasure-keepers, witches, saints, and moral enforcers. Medieval Biscayan genealogies recorded related supernatural-woman and serpent-ancestor stories. Garibay documented the Lady of Anboto in the late sixteenth century. Azkue and especially Barandiarán recorded the surviving oral network, and Barandiarán organized much of it as the cycle of Mari. Subsequent scholars, artists, national interpreters, feminists, and pagans transformed that cycle into the now-familiar image of a supreme Basque Earth goddess.

The figure is therefore historically real as a documented complex of names, stories, places, practices, interpretations, and modern commitments. What cannot presently be established is an ancient name, inscription, temple, canonical genealogy, prehistoric icon, continuous priesthood, Iberian-wide identity, or unbroken institutional cult.
