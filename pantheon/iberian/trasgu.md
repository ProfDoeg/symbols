# The Trasgu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Domestic spirit, household goblin, familiar spirit, or minor demon, depending on speaker, region, and period. It is **not securely attested as a pre-Roman god** and has no demonstrated ancient cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Antonio de Nebrija’s Latin–Spanish lexicography, conventionally dated to the 1490s, records **“drasgo de casa: incubus.”** The next especially important evidence is Niccolò Landucci’s Basque–Spanish vocabulary of 1562, which glosses Basque **etxajauna** as “drasgo o duende.” Neither source describes the later Asturian red cap, perforated hand, tail, or millet test. The earliest presently located detailed ethnographic portrait is Rogelio Jove y Bravo’s *Mitos y supersticiones de Asturias* (1903), followed by Aurelio de Llano Roza de Ampudia’s *Del folklore asturiano* (1922).

**Evidentiary conclusion:** “The Trasgu” is best treated as a historically variable north-Iberian family of vernacular household-spirit traditions—not as a single ancient deity with a continuous pre-Roman biography. Claims of Celtic, Celtiberian, Roman, “Celtic-Roman,” Germanic, or pan-Indo-European origin are scholarly or popular reconstructions. No Latin inscription, sanctuary, ancient image, Roman literary passage, or archaeological deposit securely names or depicts the being.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

The Asturian **trasgu** is a small, nocturnal household spirit whose conduct oscillates between useful domestic service and disruptive mischief. In documented nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century descriptions it cleans kitchens, handles pots, fetches water, tends fire, spins flax, or assists with livestock when well disposed; when offended, it breaks or rearranges household objects, tangles fiber, spills water, frightens animals, opens or slams doors, imitates voices, and conceals things.

The Galician **trasno** and Cantabrian or Castilian **trasgo** occupy much the same semantic field, but their regional forms should not be collapsed into the complete modern Asturian composite. Galician descriptions more readily overlap with the Christianized **demo**, **diaño**, or “little devil”; some Cantabrian representations add conspicuously diabolical horns and a tail. Portuguese **trasgo**, **estrago/strago**, **tardo**, and **demónio da mão furada** belong to the same comparative complex.

The being is normally individual in a particular story but collective as a class: Jove explicitly stated that Asturias did not have a single Trasgu; each village might possess ten or twelve. It therefore has no unique divine biography, genealogy, or canonical personality.

### Documented names and regional labels

- **Trasgu** — Asturian singular; plural **trasgos**. The final *-u* is the regular Asturian masculine ending.
- **Trasgo** — Castilian, Cantabrian, Leonese, and Portuguese form; also recorded in Galicia.
- **Trasno** — standard Galician form and an Asturian western regional name.
- **Drasgo** — the earliest securely documented lexicographical form, in Nebrija; repeated by later lexicographers, partly through copying.
- **Etxajauna / etxe-jauna** — Basque, literally “lord/master of the house.” Landucci’s 1562 vocabulary glossed it as “drasgo o duende.” This is evidence for an early-modern translator’s equivalence, not proof that the Asturian Trasgu and every Basque etxajaun were originally one being. Corominas reproduces the Landucci evidence in his discussion of *trasgo*.
- **Tardo**, **trado**, **tatro** — northern Portuguese and Galician-associated forms, sometimes treated as related household or oppressive night spirits. Their precise relationship to *trasgo* varies locally.
- **Strago / estrago** — Portuguese regional reshaping, readily associated by folk etymology with *estrago*, “damage.”
- **Trasgo loiceiro**, **tardo moleiro**, **tatro azeiteiro** — Portuguese functional labels cited by Corominas from Leite de Vasconcelos: approximately “dish/crockery trasgo,” “miller tardo,” and “oil-worker tardo.”
- **Demonio/demónio da man/mão furada** — “demon of the perforated hand,” in Galician-Portuguese comparison.
- **Pisadiel de la Mano Furada** — “Pisadiel of the perforated hand,” reported for the Navia area in later Asturian compilations.
- **El del Gorru Coloráu / Gorretín Coloráu / El de la Gorra Encarnada** — “the one with the red cap,” descriptive Asturian labels reflecting the best-attested early color attribute.
- **Cornín** — western Asturian regional designation, literally suggestive of a “little horned one.”
- **Xuan dos Camíos** — “John of the Roads,” reported in western Asturias; this name may blur the household Trasgu with waylaying outdoor spirits.
- **Meque** — reported for Laviana.
- **Papudo** — reported for Tineo.
- **Reveyeco** — reported for Grandas de Salime.
- **Sumiciu** — a disappearing or concealment spirit whose name is connected with Asturian *sumir*, “to make disappear.” Constantino Cabal systematized it as essentially a Trasgu specialized in removing things, whereas Aurelio de Llano doubted that it was an independently personified being. It should therefore be treated as a disputed neighboring category, not an automatic epithet.
- **Diañu Burlón** — “mischievous/mockery devil,” an overlapping Asturian trickster category, especially outside the house. Modern summaries often merge it with the Trasgu more freely than early collectors did.

### Etymology

The etymology remains disputed, but the most carefully argued historical-linguistic account is Joan Corominas’s.

1. **Derivation from medieval *trasgreer/trasgueer*** — Corominas’s preferred reconstruction. Fifteenth-century Castilian uses forms such as *trasgueo* and *trasgueades* in the sense of moving restlessly, behaving pertly, or making mischief. He derives the verb from Latin **transgredī**, “cross, go beyond, transgress,” with a semantic development toward “commit an excess/mischief.” The noun *trasgo* would then be a back-formation: the one who *trasguea*. This account has both textual and semantic support.

2. **From *drago*, “dragon/serpent/devil”** — suggested by Nebrija’s *drasgo*. Corominas regards the resemblance as a secondary folk-etymological influence rather than the true origin.

3. **From *tarasca*** — proposed by Martín Sarmiento in 1760 and earlier entertained by Covarrubias: *tarasco/tarasca* supposedly lost a vowel or was rearranged to produce *trasco/trasgo*. This is historically interesting but phonologically unproven.

4. **From Greek τράγος, “he-goat”** — associated with the demonic goat shape. This is a learned speculation without a secure transmission chain.

5. **From Latin *Tartarus*** through Portuguese *tardo/tatro* — proposed in older Portuguese scholarship. Corominas considered the phonetic and semantic route forced.

6. **From *trasegar*** — advanced by Grimm and adopted by Diez, but rejected by Corominas because the expected noun would be closer to *trasiego*.

7. **Celtic, Germanic, or pan-Indo-European derivation** — frequently asserted in mythology guides and tourism writing, usually by comparison with kobolds and northern European household spirits. No specific Celtic lexeme securely accounts for the documented Romance forms. Similarity of narrative type is not linguistic derivation.

Accordingly, “from Latin *transgredī* through a medieval Romance verb” is the strongest linguistic hypothesis, but even Corominas headed the entry “origin uncertain.”

### Historical classifications

- **Early-modern lexicography:** *incubus*, household goblin, or *duende*.
- **Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century demonology:** “domestic demon,” familiar spirit, or an entity comparable to classical *lemures*.
- **Oral folk classification:** a *duende*, *genio*, *espíritu familiar*, *diablejo*, *demo*, or unexplained household presence.
- **Romantic and regionalist scholarship:** often a degraded Celtic or Celtic-Roman divinity.
- **Modern tourism and children’s culture:** a benign, playful emblem of Asturias or northern Iberian folklore.
- **Fantasy vocabulary:** frequently “goblin,” sometimes losing the domestic-spirit meaning altogether.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest documentation

#### Nebrija, late fifteenth century

**[Secure early attestation]** Nebrija’s vocabulary records:

> *drasgo de casa: incubus*

This establishes a named domestic supernatural being by the end of the fifteenth century. It does **not** establish:

- an Asturian origin;
- a pre-Roman cult;
- Celtic or Roman antiquity;
- a red cap;
- a tail, horns, lameness, or dark skin;
- a hole in the hand;
- the impossible task involving millet.

The word is translated through learned Latin demonological vocabulary, so *incubus* need not mean that every later Trasgu performed sexual night-assaults. It is a lexicographic approximation.

#### Landucci, 1562

**[Secure early attestation / cross-linguistic equation]** Niccolò Landucci’s Basque–Spanish vocabulary gives:

> *etxajauna: drasgo o duende*

The Basque word means “lord of the house.” The gloss shows that a sixteenth-century compiler regarded the Spanish *drasgo/duende* as a useful equivalent for a Basque household entity. It does not demonstrate that *trasgu* is a Basque word or that all traditions concerning *etxajaun* belong to the Asturian Trasgu corpus.

#### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Corominas identifies sixteenth-century attestations and a substantial Golden Age record:

- Cristóbal de las Casas: *“trasgo o duende: scazambrello.”*
- John Minsheu or Richard Percivale’s lexicographic tradition: *“trasgo de noche or duende: hobgoblin, Robin Goodfellow.”*
- César Oudin: *“luiton ou lutin, esprit domestique, esprit follet.”*
- Mateo Alemán’s *Guzmán de Alfarache* uses *trasgos* while discussing apparently inexplicable entry into a closed room.
- Lope de Vega uses the word in *Pedro Carbonero*.
- The verb *trasguear* appears in early-modern sources for acting mischievously.

These attest a recognized category of troublesome nocturnal or domestic spirit but still supply no continuous Asturian iconographic canon.

#### *Diccionario de Autoridades*, eighteenth century

**[Learned systematization]** The Royal Academy’s early dictionary defined *trasgo* as a household demon which disturbed houses at night, knocked over tables and furnishings, threw stones without injuring people, played bowls, and made noises that kept occupants awake. This is valuable evidence for early-modern belief-language and for a poltergeist-like profile.

#### Martín Sarmiento, 1760

**[Learned etymology]** In a letter dated 6 June 1760, Sarmiento proposed that *trasgo* came from *tarasco/tarasca*, the monstrous serpent figure. His wording also shows the common equation of the trasgo with “the devil when he is in a playful mood.” The Galician Language Institute preserves this entry in its historical dictionary collection.

### Asturian ethnographic corpus

#### Rogelio Jove y Bravo, *Mitos y supersticiones de Asturias* (1903)

**[Folklore record, with identifiable informant context]** This is the earliest detailed source located for the familiar modern Asturian portrait. Jove first describes the Trasgu as:

- a household *duende* with an individual personality;
- a small black or dark man;
- bright eyes and a mocking expression;
- lame;
- dressed in red, including a red cap;
- entering houses through the chimney;
- able either to perform domestic labor or cause disorder.

In the book’s later “Notas del folk-lore,” Jove gives a fuller account attributed in part to a young woman from Riosa who said she saw one in her kitchen on Christmas night. It was approximately eighty centimeters tall, dark-skinned, thin, crooked-legged, long-nailed, flat-nosed, lame, agile, and clothed in a red blouse and cap. Jove explicitly records disagreement: some villagers called it a low-ranking devil; others, a *genio*.

Jove’s account is unusually important because it separates reported testimony from his own naturalistic interpretation. He suggests that wind, rain through roof tiles, household noises, darkness, and expectation could generate particular episodes. That explanation documents the collector’s interpretation; it does not erase the villagers’ belief reports.

Notably, the accessible 1903 text does **not** make a perforated hand or tail part of this detailed portrait.

#### Aurelio de Llano Roza de Ampudia, *Del folklore asturiano* (1922)

**[Folklore record plus learned comparative theory]** De Llano describes a diminutive, friendly-looking being in a red baize blouse and red cap. At night it performs household work or, when ill disposed, breaks vessels, disarranges clothes, transfers water between pails, and drives livestock to water while shouting.

He calls its origin “Celtic-Roman” and compares trasgos with gnomes, sylphs, kobolds, and classical *lemures*. That statement is **not ancient evidence**; it is a comparative hypothesis characteristic of early-twentieth-century folklore scholarship.

De Llano also records dismissal devices:

- requiring it to bring water in a basket;
- making it gather millet or linseed;
- requiring it to whiten the hide of a black animal.

In the grain episode, later summaries explain failure through the hole in its left hand. De Llano also preserves a verse in which the frustrated being says, in effect, that even all the soap between Madrid and Valencia cannot whiten the black hide.

The work was compiled from oral materials but has a major methodological limitation: later Asturian-language criticism objected that de Llano rendered vernacular material in Castilian and thereby altered or erased its linguistic form.

#### Constantino Cabal, *La mitología asturiana* (volumes first published 1925–1928)

**[Learned systematization based on folklore]** Cabal integrated the Trasgu into a broad reconstructed Asturian mythology and compared it with European household spirits. His work remains influential but often converts localized reports into systematized “mythology.” It is a secondary synthesis, not evidence for a unified ancient pantheon.

#### Luciano Castañón and later collectors

Twentieth-century compilations expanded the dossier with more local names, tales, and comparisons. These are useful where they identify places or informants but must not automatically be treated as ancient survivals.

### Galician corpus

#### Lexicography and oral tradition

**[Folklore / regional variant]** Galician **trasno** is documented as a small household troublemaker, devil, or spirit responsible for misplaced objects, noises, frightened animals, and pranks. The derivatives **trasnada**, “mischief,” and **atrasnado**, “confused/dazed,” show the term’s integration into ordinary speech.

Historical Galician dictionaries variously define *trasgo* as “demon,” a variant of *trasno*, or a mischievous child. These meanings caution against assuming that every occurrence denotes a fixed supernatural species.

#### Vicente Risco, *Etnografía: cultura espiritual* (1962)

**[Folklore synthesis]** Risco treated the *trasno* among Galicia’s mythical beings alongside the *tardo*, *diaño bulreiro*, and other vernacular entities. His work is a major codification of collected Galician spiritual culture, but its 1962 publication date and comparative framework preclude using it as direct proof of pre-Roman belief.

#### Cuba, Reigosa, and Miranda, *Diccionario dos seres míticos galegos*

**[Modern scholarly/popular codification]** This dictionary consolidates Galician variants and descriptions. Its familiar horned, tailed, goat-legged forms document the modern received image, assembled from regional traditions and prior literature. Such composite illustrations should not be projected wholesale onto sixteenth-century or Asturian evidence.

### Cantabrian corpus

#### Manuel Llano and Adriano García-Lomas

**[Twentieth-century folklore collection and regional systematization]** Cantabrian materials describe a *trasgo* or equivalent mischievous spirit of houses, mills, and stables. Adriano García-Lomas’s *Mitología y supersticiones de Cantabria* (1964) placed it within a reconstructed Cantabrian supernatural world.

The Cantabrian dossier is later and less textually precise than the early-modern lexical record. Modern depictions frequently add horns and a tail, but those attributes should be labeled regional or late iconographic developments unless tied to a particular field report.

### Basque comparative corpus

Barandiarán’s *Mitología vasca*, Azkue’s *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*, and the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* preserve Basque household-spirit and supernatural-worker narratives. A Basque tale recorded by Azkue and comparative musical materials published around 1930 parallel an Asturian story in which a husband substitutes for a woman at spinning; the supernatural visitor notices that the spinner has a beard.

These are legitimate comparative tale correspondences. They do not establish that Basque beings in those stories were themselves called *trasgu* by narrators. Landucci’s 1562 *etxajauna = drasgo o duende* remains the clearest historical bridge.

The *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* began in 1921; its early volumes were systematically digitized, providing an unusually rich repository of twentieth-century field material. Its institutional history also warns against mistaking publication date for the unknown age of an oral tale.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The Trasgu’s most stable documented domain is the **household**, particularly:

- kitchen and hearth;
- chimney;
- storage chests and cupboards;
- spindle, flax, and women’s domestic work;
- pails and water vessels;
- stable, livestock, and sometimes mills;
- household objects liable to disappear or move.

It is not securely a god of the hearth in the institutional sense. No altar, priesthood, temple, or formal liturgy is documented. “Household spirit” is more accurate than “hearth god.”

### Characteristic powers and actions

**[Early-modern through modern folklore]**

- entering a locked or closed house;
- entering through the chimney;
- invisibility or elusiveness;
- extraordinary agility despite lameness;
- cleaning utensils and the hearth;
- fetching water;
- spinning flax;
- lighting the morning fire;
- handling or watering livestock;
- hiding objects;
- opening or slamming doors and windows;
- throwing stones or household things without necessarily injuring people;
- breaking pots or tiles;
- tangling flax, hair, or fibers;
- mimicking frightening voices;
- accompanying a family when it moves.

No secure source gives omniscience, cosmic authority, control of weather, immortality as a theological doctrine, or a fixed place in a divine hierarchy.

### Physical form: stratified evidence

#### Secure early detailed layer, 1903

Jove’s account supplies:

- masculine humanoid form;
- roughly eighty centimeters tall in one Riosa report;
- very dark skin;
- bright eyes;
- large mouth and flat nose;
- thin body and crooked legs;
- lameness;
- long nails;
- red blouse and red cap;
- mocking or angry expression.

#### 1922 layer

De Llano confirms diminutive form and red blouse and cap. His treatment helped establish red clothing as canonical in later retellings.

#### Regional and later composite layer

Later compilations add:

- a hole through the left palm;
- tail;
- small horns;
- goat ears or legs;
- yellow-green or reddish skin;
- invisibility in some eastern variants;
- a pointed rather than merely red cap.

The **tail and horns are not established by the earliest full descriptions located here**. They may reflect regional demonization, overlap with *diañu*, *cornín*, and goat-shaped devils, or modern visual standardization.

### The perforated hand

**[Folklore, securely attested in twentieth-century transmission; exact first attestation unresolved]**

The hole through the left palm is now the Trasgu’s most recognizable feature. It explains why it cannot collect millet or linseed: each handful falls through its palm. It also underlies names such as *Pisadiel de la Mano Furada* and Portuguese/Galician *demonio da mão/man furada*.

The trait’s relationship to the impossible-task story requires caution:

- Early detailed portrait: Jove, 1903, does not include it.
- De Llano, 1922, records impossible grain-gathering and other expulsion tasks.
- Later syntheses make the hole the causal mechanism and universal anatomical marker.
- Therefore, the secure conclusion is that perforated-hand lore circulated by the early twentieth-century collection era; its antiquity before that remains unestablished.

### The red cap

**[Folklore, securely attested by 1903]**

Red clothing and a red cap are well established in Jove and de Llano. Jove compared this with a Germanic household goblin he called *Klabher*. Comparison does not establish borrowing. Red caps are widespread among European fairy and goblin figures.

The vernacular phrase likening a troublesome child to “the one with the red cap” documents the attribute’s entry into everyday characterization.

### The tail

**[Regional variant / later canonical iconography]**

A tail, often with a thickened end, is common in late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century art, guidebooks, figurines, and websites. It is especially associated with demonized Cantabrian or composite north-Iberian imagery. Its absence from Jove’s precise full-body description is significant. It should not be called an original or universal feature.

### Sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers, and calendar

No sacred animal, plant, planet, weekday, or canonical number is securely associated with the Trasgu.

- **Red** is an iconographic clothing color, not demonstrably a sacred cult color.
- **Millet and linseed** are instruments in an impossible task, not sacred grains.
- **Black goat, lamb, calf, or ram hide** varies by retelling; the hide is an impossible object to whiten, not a sacrificial victim.
- **Christmas night** appears in Jove’s Riosa sighting report but is not a universal festival of the Trasgu.
- **Hearth embers, bread, corn cakes, butter, or warmth** are sometimes offered or left for it in later tradition; these are domestic appeasements, not evidence of an organized sacrificial cult.

### Surviving images

There is no authenticated ancient or medieval depiction identifiable by inscription as a Trasgu. The earliest visual corpus is modern: book illustration, regionalist art, children’s media, public sculpture, commercial figurines, and tourism graphics. The present pointed cap, horns, tail, and perforated palm form a modern composite visual canon rather than an archaeologically traceable iconographic sequence.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No reliable traditional source gives the Trasgu:

- parents;
- a birth narrative;
- siblings;
- consort;
- offspring;
- a king or queen;
- a pantheon rank;
- a divine mount;
- a death or eschatological destiny.

A “genealogy” linking it to Celtic gods, Roman *lares* or *lemures*, Germanic kobolds, or Basque household entities is comparative classification, not narrated descent.

### Comparative and syncretistic relations

- **Classical *incubus*** — Nebrija’s late-fifteenth-century translation. A lexicographic equation, not a mythic meeting.
- **Classical *lemures*** — early-modern and nineteenth-/twentieth-century learned comparison, mediated through demonological writers and Menéndez Pelayo.
- **Duende** — the ordinary Spanish umbrella category used from the sixteenth century onward.
- **Robin Goodfellow / hobgoblin** — English lexicographic equivalents in early-modern bilingual dictionaries.
- **Lutin / esprit follet** — French lexicographic equivalents.
- **Kobold** — comparative Germanic household spirit; Jove and de Llano use the resemblance to support origin theories.
- **Etxajaun** — Landucci’s 1562 Basque translation-equivalent.
- **Sumiciu** — disputed Asturian specialization or neighboring spirit.
- **Diañu Burlón** — overlapping mischievous devil, especially outside the house.
- **Trasno** — Galician and western Asturian regional form rather than a separately demonstrated divine individual.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

**No secure primary or ethnographic source located records the Trasgu meeting, fighting, marrying, judging, seducing, deceiving, rescuing, or appearing jointly in an episode with another individually named god or supernatural being.**

Relevant negative distinctions:

- Comparisons with kobolds, *lemures*, sylphs, gnomes, or *etxajaun* are taxonomic comparisons, not narrative interactions.
- Modern ensemble works may place a Trasgu beside the Anjana, Ojáncano, Musgoso, Guajona, Cuélebre, or Nuberu. These are modern literary or theatrical crossovers unless an older tale is independently supplied.
- “The Devil,” God, saints, or angels occur in collectors’ Christian classification systems, but no named divine encounter central to Trasgu tradition was found.

This absence is consistent with the being’s narrative scale: Trasgu tales concern households and human occupants rather than a pantheon’s divine politics.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

The Trasgu has no epic cycle. Its narrative record consists of recurring international migratory motifs adapted to north-Iberian domestic life.

### The helpful and destructive household worker

**Tier:** Folklore; securely documented in detailed form by 1903 and 1922.

A Trasgu occupies or visits a rural house, especially its kitchen. When it likes the family, it cleans cookware, removes ashes, spins flax, fetches water, lights the fire, or assists with animals. When offended or simply inclined to mischief, it reverses those actions: throwing down pots, tangling flax, spilling water, hiding tools, frightening livestock, and making night noises.

Jove presents both modes and reports that villagers differed over whether the being was a *genio* or minor devil. De Llano similarly emphasizes ambivalence. Modern children’s retellings often moralize the pattern: hardworking humans receive help, become lazy, and are then disciplined by the spirit. That explicit labor fable is a modern narrative elaboration unless tied to a specific collected variant.

### The impossible tasks

**Tier:** Folklore, documented by 1922; details vary regionally and in later retelling.

To expel an intolerable Trasgu, occupants impose tasks that its obsessive or proud nature compels it to attempt:

1. bring water in a basket;
2. gather scattered millet or linseed;
3. whiten the hide of a black goat, lamb, calf, or ram.

The spirit repeatedly fails, becomes ashamed or furious, and leaves.

#### The millet and perforated hand

In the best-known modern version, the family spills millet on the floor and orders the Trasgu to gather every grain. It scoops the seed with its left hand, but the grains fall through the hole in its palm. Unable to complete the task, it departs.

The tale explains the anatomical attribute and vice versa. However, the chronology currently established is weaker than popular accounts imply. Jove’s 1903 portrait lacks the hole; de Llano’s 1922 material supplies the impossible-task complex; later summaries fuse the two into a fixed anatomy-and-task narrative.

#### The black hide

The Trasgu tries to scrub a black animal hide white. De Llano preserves a comic verse expressing its realization that no quantity of soap will succeed. Species vary in later retellings, indicating a migratory impossible-task motif rather than one canonical text.

### The family that moves house

**Tier:** Folklore; collected in twentieth-century literature, with widespread European analogues.

A family, exhausted by the spirit’s disturbances, secretly packs and moves. At the new house, a voice announces that it has brought an old broom, pot, or other forgotten object. In another formula it rides on the cart and announces that since the whole household is moving, it and its red cap are coming too.

The point is that the Trasgu attaches to the family or household community, not merely the building. Later sentimental retellings sometimes interpret this as affection; older versions may present it as inescapable harassment.

### The hot cake or heated stone

**Tier:** Regional folklore, recorded in Asturian and comparative Basque materials.

In one Asturian complex the Trasgu steals corn cakes or descends to take food. The household substitutes a heated stone or otherwise burns it. The spirit retreats through the chimney crying that it has burned itself.

This belongs to a broad corpus of tales in which humans exploit a supernatural visitor’s greed. A Basque parallel and variants in the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* show diffusion or shared narrative stock; they do not prove the Trasgu was originally Basque or that the stories descend unchanged from antiquity.

### The bearded spinner

**Tier:** Regional comparative folklore.

A supernatural visitor expects a woman to be spinning, but a bearded husband has taken her place. The visitor recognizes the deception or marvels at the bearded spinner. Asturian and Basque variants were compared by Aita Donostia, who cited Azkue and 1930 *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* materials.

The identity of the supernatural visitor varies across versions; therefore the tale is evidence for narrative circulation, not for a fixed Trasgu biography.

### Explanatory “poltergeist” cycle

**Tier:** Folk attribution documented by collectors.

Unaccountable noises, missing objects, wind-slamming windows, dripping roofs, frightened animals, or broken crockery are attributed to the Trasgu. Jove explicitly juxtaposes the supernatural explanation with physical causes he observed. This makes the account valuable testimony to both belief and early folkloristic rationalization.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No institutional cult

There is no secure evidence for:

- a Trasgu temple or shrine;
- a priesthood;
- a public festival;
- a votive inscription;
- a theophoric personal name;
- sacrifice;
- an oracle;
- pilgrimage;
- formal initiation;
- temple wealth or cult economy;
- an ancient image dedicated to the being.

The Endovelicus sanctuary at São Miguel da Mota, the inscriptions of Ataecina associated with Turóbriga, Hispania Epigraphica, and the broader Latin epigraphy of western Iberia are indispensable for actual pre-Roman or Roman-period gods. They do **not** presently supply the name *Trasgu*, *Trasgo*, or *Trasno*. Including the Trasgu in the same “pantheon” is therefore a modern atlas convention, not an epigraphically demonstrated ancient relationship.

Strabo’s *Geography* III and other Greco-Roman descriptions of Iberian religion do not name the Trasgu. General reports about Celtiberian or northern Iberian ritual cannot serve as evidence for it.

### Domestic appeasement

**[Folklore]** Later accounts recommend leaving:

- hearth warmth or embers;
- bread or corn cakes;
- butter or food;
- an undisturbed place near the fire.

Such behavior resembles offerings to European household spirits, but the sources describe household accommodation rather than a formal cult.

### Expulsion rites

The practical ritual sequence is more strongly developed than appeasement:

- command an impossible task;
- scatter grain;
- provide a basket for carrying water;
- provide a black hide to whiten;
- deceive the food-stealing spirit with a heated object;
- leave the house—usually unsuccessfully.

These procedures are narrative and customary magic. Their performance in a story does not establish how commonly they were enacted outside storytelling.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and cult violence

The dedicated contested-material search found **no evidence** connecting the Trasgu with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual killing, organized cult violence, or political persecution. Such claims should be rejected unless new primary evidence is produced.

Nebrija’s Latin gloss *incubus* does not by itself document sexual assault traditions. No securely sourced Trasgu narrative located here makes sexuality a defining function.

### Christian suppression

No documented ecclesiastical campaign specifically targeted a named Trasgu cult. Christian vocabulary clearly affected classification: the household spirit could be called a demon, little devil, or the Devil at play. Yet this is vernacular demonization, not proof that church authorities destroyed Trasgu temples or forcibly converted its worshippers.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Hypothetical pre-documentary background

Household spirits are widespread in Europe, and northwestern Iberian domestic architecture, hearth labor, livestock keeping, and nocturnal storytelling supplied an obvious social environment for such beliefs. A common European narrative background is plausible.

What cannot be established is whether this particular named being existed:

- before Roman conquest;
- as a Celtic god;
- as a Roman *lar* or *lemur*;
- as a Germanic spirit introduced by Sueves or Visigoths;
- as an unchanged Indo-European survival.

### Fifteenth century: linguistic prehistory and first attestation

The verbs *trasgreer/trasgueer* occur in fifteenth-century Castilian with meanings connected to restless or mischievous conduct. Late in the century Nebrija records *drasgo de casa*. On Corominas’s reconstruction, the supernatural noun emerged through back-formation from the mischief verb.

This documentary sequence supports a medieval Romance lexical history more directly than an unattested Celtic divine name.

### Sixteenth century: diffusion and translation

The being enters multilingual dictionaries. Landucci pairs Basque *etxajauna* with *drasgo o duende* in 1562. Italian, English, and French bilingual lexicography translates *trasgo* by local goblin terms. These equations show that the category was recognizable throughout early-modern Europe.

### Seventeenth century: literary familiarity

Spanish Golden Age literature uses *trasgo* without elaborate explanation, implying that readers recognized it as a nocturnal or domestic goblin. It is not yet an exclusively Asturian figure.

### Eighteenth century: dictionary demonology and antiquarian etymology

The *Diccionario de Autoridades* defines a noisy household demon in poltergeist-like terms. Sarmiento discusses Galician usage and proposes derivation from *tarasca*. Portuguese dictionaries likewise record *trasgo*.

This is the period in which writers increasingly theorize the being rather than merely use its name.

### Nineteenth century: regional folklore and comparative mythology

Romantic nationalism and comparative folklore encouraged collectors to classify oral traditions as surviving “mythologies.” The Trasgu’s resemblance to kobolds, brownies, goblins, and classical spirits became an origin argument. Much later popular certainty about “Celtic roots” descends from this mode of scholarship.

### 1903–1922: detailed Asturian ethnographic canon

Jove’s 1903 description fixes the dark, small, lame, red-clad household figure in print. De Llano’s 1922 compilation reinforces the red clothing and domestic actions and records the impossible-task complex. These works are the decisive documentary turning point in the modern Trasgu’s history.

### 1920s–1960s: regional systematization

Cabal constructs an overarching Asturian mythology; the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* publishes comparable Basque tales; Azkue collects Basque oral literature; Risco codifies Galician spiritual ethnography; García-Lomas systematizes Cantabrian mythology.

This period did not simply preserve an unchanged ancient canon. It selected, translated, compared, and organized heterogeneous local materials into regional mythological systems.

### Late twentieth century: standardization

Guidebooks, school materials, dictionaries of mythical beings, fantasy publishing, and regional illustration create the now-familiar composite:

- red pointed cap;
- dark or greenish body;
- lameness;
- tail and sometimes horns;
- perforated left hand;
- compulsive mischief;
- defeat by millet.

Traits that earlier sources kept separate become universalized.

### Twenty-first century: heritage emblem

The Trasgu now functions as:

- an Asturian cultural mascot;
- a children’s-story character;
- a name for restaurants, lodging, associations, music groups, awards, and commercial products;
- a figure in public mythology routes and tourism promotion;
- a generic fantasy goblin.

This is vigorous cultural continuity of the name and story complex, but not proof of continuous worship.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

Rafael Dieste’s **1926** *Dos arquivos do trasno* is a foundational work of modern Galician prose. Its title uses *trasno* as a framing figure for mysterious, extraordinary, and folk-inflected stories; it is not a documentary scripture about the being. The first edition contained eight stories; the expanded 1962 edition added further material. Its influence helped make *trasno* a symbol of Galician imaginative modernity.

In fantasy translation, Spanish **trasgo** often renders English **goblin**. Consequently, modern readers may imagine an outdoor humanoid species, warrior, or monster rather than the traditional household presence. This is a modern semantic expansion driven by translated fantasy and games.

Gonzalo Torrente Ballester’s *Crónica del rey pasmado* includes a diabolical manifestation described in Trasgu-like form, demonstrating the figure’s continued literary usefulness as a familiar minor demon.

### Music

- The Asturian folk group **Trasgu**, associated with Avilés, became an early participant in the regional folk revival and released *La isla de Hélice*.
- Víctor Manuel’s album *Ay amor* (1981) included a song titled **“Trasgu.”**
- RTVE’s children’s project *Lunnis de Leyenda* released **“El Trasgu”** in 2020. Its plot gives the helpful-worker story an explicit moral arc: a couple becomes lazy while the Trasgu works, provoking its retaliatory mischief. This is a clear modern adaptation.

### Theater and public heritage

In 2026 Torrelavega presented **El Bosque Mágico del Trasgu**, a family music-and-theater production bringing the Trasgu together with the Anjana, Trentí, Musgoso, Ojáncano, and Guajona. This is a documented modern ensemble crossover, not evidence of an old tale in which those beings interacted.

### Commercial and civic use

The name is used by:

- rural houses and restaurants;
- an Asturian animal-protection association;
- folk ensembles and awards;
- children’s products and figurines;
- tourism routes and educational projects.

Such uses transform a troublesome house spirit into a friendly regional brand. Modern artisan figures commonly treat the perforated palm, pointed red cap, horns, and tail as obligatory even though the early record is less uniform.

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search located general Iberian and Celtic-themed neopaganism but no well-documented organized religion centered on the Trasgu, no identifiable initiatory order, and no established modern ritual lineage claiming continuous Trasgu worship.

Individual practitioners may regard household spirits as real, leave offerings, or incorporate the Trasgu into eclectic Celtic, animist, witchcraft, or reconstructionist practice. Without dated organizational or textual evidence, these remain personal modern adaptations.

The Trasgu is absent as a significant named entity from the canonical Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, and major grimoire traditions. Claims that it has an ancient occult seal, planetary correspondence, summoning rite, or priestly mystery tradition should be classified as modern invention unless supported by a dated text.

### Nationalist and political reception

Regional myth collections contributed to Asturian, Galician, Cantabrian, and Basque cultural self-definition. The Trasgu’s use is primarily heritage-oriented rather than a major extremist symbol. No substantial documented modern extremist appropriation was located.

The phrase “Celtic mythology” in tourism and popular culture often supplies antiquity and regional distinctiveness without demonstrating an actual Celtic-language name or ancient cult. That is a modern identity claim, not necessarily deliberate falsification, but it must be kept separate from the evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is it pre-Roman?

**Claim:** The Trasgu descends from a pre-Roman Celtic god or spirit.

**Evidence offered:** northern Iberian geography, supposed Celtic cultural survivals, resemblance to household spirits elsewhere, and the red cap or diminutive shape.

**Problems:** no ancient name, inscription, image, sanctuary, or classical description; the word’s strongest linguistic history is Romance; detailed traits appear very late in writing.

**Assessment:** possible deep folkloric antecedents, but a specifically pre-Roman Trasgu is **unproven modern reconstruction**.

### Is it Roman?

**Claim:** It descends from Roman *lares*, *penates*, *lemures*, or *incubi*.

**Evidence offered:** domestic function and early Latin glosses.

**Problems:** early lexicographers routinely translated vernacular supernatural beings by approximate classical terms. Functional similarity is not proof of historical identity.

**Assessment:** Roman concepts may have influenced learned classification or vernacular demonology; direct descent is unestablished.

### Is it Germanic?

**Claim:** Sueves, Visigoths, or other Germanic peoples brought a kobold-like being to Iberia.

**Evidence offered:** parallels with kobolds and red-clad household goblins.

**Problems:** no historical transmission evidence, no Germanic etymology for *trasgo*, and comparable household spirits occur across Europe.

**Assessment:** legitimate comparative folklore; unsupported as an exclusive origin theory.

### Is it “Celtic-Roman”?

This formula comes prominently from Aurelio de Llano’s 1922 synthesis. It joins a Celtic-origin assumption to Roman or classical comparanda without producing ancient evidence. It should be cited as de Llano’s theory, not reported as fact.

### Is *etxajaun* the Basque Trasgu?

Landucci’s 1562 gloss makes the comparison historically real. Yet *etxajaun*, “lord of the house,” can carry Basque ancestral and household meanings not identical to every Asturian tale. Barandiarán and Azkue document related Basque supernatural traditions, but they do not justify replacing their local names with *Trasgu*.

**Assessment:** early-modern translation-equivalence and comparative kinship, not timeless identity.

### The perforated hand problem

The hand-hole is now treated as immemorial and universal, but the accessible evidence suggests a more complicated development:

- absent from Jove’s exceptionally detailed 1903 portrait;
- connected with regional names and twentieth-century impossible-task narratives;
- universalized in later handbooks and art.

The responsible conclusion is not that the trait was invented in 1922, but that its documented antiquity remains much shallower than popular presentations imply.

### Collector mediation

Jove names at least one informant context and sometimes separates testimony from explanation. De Llano and Cabal provide far fewer modern ethnographic controls than would now be expected. Asturian-language critics have objected that rendering oral Asturian testimony in Castilian distorted its linguistic evidence.

Risco, García-Lomas, Barandiarán, and Azkue likewise worked within particular regional and intellectual projects. Their books are indispensable records, but they are not transparent windows onto antiquity.

### State of the evidence

The source base consists chiefly of:

- late-medieval and early-modern dictionaries;
- Golden Age literary references;
- eighteenth-century lexicography and antiquarian speculation;
- nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore collection;
- modern regional syntheses;
- contemporary art, tourism, and popular media.

What does **not** survive is equally important:

- no ancient inscription;
- no papyrus or manuscript mythology;
- no medieval Trasgu narrative securely identified here;
- no temple archive;
- no ancient cult image;
- no canonical hymn;
- no direct chain linking a Roman-period being to twentieth-century rural testimony.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “The Trasgu is a securely attested Celtic god.”
- “Roman writers described the Trasgu.”
- “Its cult continued unchanged from pre-Roman Asturias.”
- “The red cap, tail, horns, and perforated hand are all ancient and universal.”
- “Endovelicus or Ataecina is another name for the Trasgu.”
- “It has a traditional occult seal, planetary day, sacred number, or formal invocation.”
- “It received human sacrifice.”

None is established by the evidence reviewed.
