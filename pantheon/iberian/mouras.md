# The Mouras: Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective female supernatural beings—usually “enchanted women,” “ladies,” “maidens,” or members of the subterranean *mouros*—in Galician and Portuguese folklore. Regional analogues include western Asturian *moras/encantadas* and *xanas*, Cantabrian *moras* and *anjanas*, and, more distantly, Basque *mairuak*, *lamiak*, and other beings associated with water or ancient monuments. **They are not a securely attested pre-Roman goddess or a historically documented unified pantheon.**

**Earliest secure attestation:** A *Moura Encantada* appears as a character in Gil Vicente’s Portuguese court play *Cortes de Júpiter*, performed in Lisbon in **1521**. This is the earliest presently identifiable, securely dated literary attestation to the named figure found in this investigation. It does not establish prehistoric antiquity. A particularly valuable later record is a **1653 notarial act from Gouveia**, printed in *O Ramalhete* in 1838, describing a serpent that becomes a maiden and rewards a boy with gold. Systematic ethnographic documentation begins principally in the nineteenth century, culminating in Ataíde Oliveira’s *As mouras encantadas e os encantamentos no Algarve* (1898).

**Evidentiary warning:** Archaeological sites associated with mouras may be Neolithic, Bronze Age, Iron Age, Roman, or medieval; the traditions recorded about them are ordinarily much later. The antiquity of a dolmen does not date its moura legend. No ancient inscription names a *Moura*, *Moura Encantada*, or collective *Mouras* as a deity.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

[**Folklore; primarily nineteenth–twentieth-century documentation**] A *moura encantada* is normally a supernaturally beautiful woman bound by an *encanto*: an occult constraint ties her to a fountain, well, river, cave, rock, dolmen, hillfort, castle, bridge, or subterranean palace until a mortal completes a prescribed act. She may comb her hair with a golden comb, spin or carry stones, guard gold, appear at midnight or dawn, transform into a serpent, and offer marriage or treasure to the person capable of releasing her.

The Galician moura is often one member of a larger indigenous imaginary called the *mourindade*: the *mouros* and *mouras* who inhabit or built castros, *mámoas* (burial mounds), dolmens, caves, rock shelters, and underground passages. These beings are characteristically “before us,” unbaptized, nocturnal, wealthy, and subterranean. They are not consistently imagined as the historical Muslims of al-Andalus. In many Galician descriptions they are pale or fair-haired, eat pork, drink wine, and antedate Romans as well as medieval Christians. The University of Santiago summary of Buenaventura Aparicio Casado’s research—based on traditions attached to 525 archaeological sites—classifies the material as a broad “folklore of mouros” generated within traditional Galician rural society rather than a transparent survival of prehistoric ritual.

### Names and forms

- **Portuguese:** *moura encantada*, plural *mouras encantadas*; also *moura*, *moira*, *encantada*, *dona*, *senhora*, *princesa*, *rainha*, *moura-serpente*, *moura-fiandeira* (“spinning moura”), *moura-mãe* (“mother moura”), and *moura-velha* (“old moura”). *Mourama* can mean either the land of historical Moors or, in folklore, the otherworldly domain of the enchanted *mouros*.

- **Galician:** *moura*, plural *mouras*; *mouro/mouros* for the male or collective people; also *dona*, *dama*, *señora*, *señorita*, *princesa*, *raíña*, *encantada*, and *vella*. The compound *moura encantada* also occurs. Dictionary traditions record the *mouro* as a supernatural or legendary being living underground and credit the *mouros* with castros, *mámoas*, caves, fountains, carvings, and buried gold.

- **Asturian:** *mora*, *encantada*, and especially *xana*; western Asturian traditions frequently overlap so heavily that collectors have treated “moras,” “princesses,” “ladies,” and “enchanted women” as local names for the same narrative role. A distinct Asturian being should not automatically be renamed “Moura.”

- **Cantabrian:** *mora*, *moza del agua*, *anjana*, and local names such as *Moras de Carmona*. The literary-standardized Anjana—small, benevolent, long-haired, equipped with a golden comb—is particularly associated with Manuel Llano and later Cantabrian mythography. It is an analogue, not a demonstrated historical identity.

- **Basque:** *mairu*, plural *mairuak*, with recorded forms including *mairiak*, *maideak*, and *maindiak*. These are usually a collective ancient race or builders of megaliths, not simply female mouras. Basque place-names include *mairu-baratze* (“Mairu garden,” a cromlech), *mairu-etxe* (“Mairu house,” a dolmen or standing-stone site), and *mairu-mahai* (“Mairu table”). Basque *lamiak*—often female water beings who comb their hair—and Mari, the Lady of Anboto and other mountains, provide closer motif parallels in some respects, but no early source equates them with the Galaico-Portuguese mouras.

### Etymology

Three explanations must be separated.

1. **Latin *Maurus*, “Moor/Mauretanian.”**  
   [**Secure linguistic history; exact folkloric development debated**] Portuguese and Galician *mouro/moura* are formally the ordinary descendants of Latin *Maurus/Maura*, later meaning a Muslim or “Moor.” Medieval Christian narratives about Muslim departure, hidden treasure, abandoned castles, and forbidden love plainly contributed to many individual legends. This is the least speculative immediate explanation of the recorded word.

2. **The “dead/ancestors” proposal.**  
   [**Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed**] Isidoro Millán González-Pardo connected Galician *mouro* with a reconstructed Celtic death-word, *\*mṛwos*, compared with Old Irish *marb*, Brittonic *marw*, Latin *mortuus*, and reconstructed Gaulish *\*maruos*. Fernando Alonso Romero adopted this proposal in 1998 and interpreted the *mouros* as the pre-Roman dead or remote ancestors: beings no longer belonging to the community of living Christians but continuing in an underground existence. José Joaquim Dias Marques objects that the great majority of collected narratives do not describe *mouros* as deceased humans or ghosts. Phonetic resemblance and an anthropological fit do not by themselves establish this etymology.

3. **Pre-Roman *mor-* “stone heap/mound” and related proposals.**  
   [**Modern speculative reconstruction**] Popular and antiquarian literature sometimes derives *moura* from a pre-Roman root attached to mounds or stones, or relates it to Greek *Moira*, Basque Mari, Latvian Māra, Scandinavian *mara*, or Celtic female beings. None of these equations has been demonstrated by a securely transmitted ancient-to-modern name sequence. Spellings such as *moira* are sometimes adopted today to signal a supposedly pre-Islamic origin, but that spelling is itself an interpretive choice, not proof.

The safest historical conclusion is that the medieval Romance word for “Moor” became a highly productive label for imagined former inhabitants and supernatural antiquity. Older European motifs—water women, fairy brides, serpent maidens, treasure guardians, builders, spinners, and ancestral inhabitants—could then be expressed through that label. Whether any particular motif descends continuously from pre-Roman religion is usually unprovable.

### Classification through time

- [**Early literary attestation, 1521**] Gil Vicente presents a singular enchanted Moorish woman, exotic and magical, within a courtly Christian-classical entertainment.
- [**Early-modern/modern folklore**] A bound or enchanted maiden, sometimes represented as a historical Muslim princess.
- [**Galician folklore**] A member of a nonhuman or other-human subterranean people associated with the pre-Christian past.
- [**Learned nineteenth-century systematization**] A fairy, nymph, water genius, nature spirit, or vestige of a pagan goddess.
- [**Modern archaeological anthropology**] A symbolic inhabitant of ruins and a figure of alterity through which rural communities interpreted abandoned material remains.
- [**Modern revival and popular media**] A localized “goddess,” “Celtic fairy,” or emblem of Portuguese/Galician heritage. These classifications are modern reinterpretations when they exceed the recorded local legend.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 1521: Gil Vicente’s *Cortes de Júpiter*

[**Secure early literary attestation**] *Cortes de Júpiter* was staged for King Manuel I on the departure of his daughter Beatriz for Savoy. Its dramatis personae combine Providence, Jupiter, planets or gods, winds, the sea, and a *Moura Encantada*. The moura possesses an enchanted ring that reveals events in the world, a productive magical thimble, and a sword said to have been taken after Roland’s death. A song releases her enchantment; she speaks in a theatricalized foreign idiom.

This character establishes that the expression and recognizable idea were available to a Portuguese court audience in 1521. She is not the later standardized fountain maiden, however: the play places her in a literary spectacle among classical deities and Carolingian material. It cannot prove a prehistoric moura cult.

The surviving text belongs to Vicente’s printed transmission, especially the posthumous *Copilaçam de todalas obras* of 1562. Composition/performance and surviving print must therefore be distinguished.

### 1653: the Gouveia notarial narrative

[**Secure early-modern documentary attestation; event reported as testimony, not independently verified**] An anonymous item, “Mouras Encantadas,” published in *O Ramalhete* on 23 August 1838, reproduced a contemporary notarial act concerning an alleged 1653 occurrence at Gouveia. A serpent appeared to a boy, transformed into a maiden, and gave him gold. The notarial form is evidence that a claim was recorded in 1653; it is not evidence that the supernatural event occurred.

This record is important because it joins three durable motifs—serpent transformation, release or encounter with a maiden, and treasure—well before nineteenth-century folklore collecting.

### Romantic nationalism and print

- **1828, Almeida Garrett, *Adozinda*.** [**Romantic learned reception**] Garrett’s notes treat enchanted-moura legends as characteristic Portuguese oral literature. This is early evidence for their elevation into national heritage.

- **1836–1868.** [**Romantic literary elaboration**] Portuguese poets repeatedly converted local oral legends into narrative verse. Such poems document nineteenth-century reception but may introduce names, psychology, courtly settings, or historical detail absent from oral versions.

- **1838, “Mouras Encantadas,” *O Ramalhete*.** [**Documentary publication of the 1653 record**]

- **Teófilo Braga, 1867.** [**Learned regionalization**] Dias Marques identifies this as the earliest known printed assertion that moura legends were especially characteristic of the Algarve.

### Ethnographic and folkloric corpus

- **José Leite de Vasconcelos, from the 1880s; later collected in *Opúsculos*, vol. V (1938).** [**Ethnographic collection plus comparativist interpretation**] Vasconcelos defined *encantados* as beings compelled by a supernatural force to remain in a particular state and place until a specified circumstance breaks the enchantment. His early comparative explanations—including solar, storm, and Vedic comparisons—belong to nineteenth-century mythological theory and should not be confused with the informants’ own accounts.

- **Consiglieri Pedroso, “As Mouras Encantadas.”** [**Nineteenth-century scholarship**] Pedroso rejected Vasconcelos’s proposed derivation of the milk-loving serpent from the Vedic Ahi/Indra myth and instead compared mouras with female water spirits. This early dispute demonstrates how quickly collected tradition was absorbed into broad Indo-European reconstruction.

- **Ataíde Oliveira, *As mouras encantadas e os encantamentos no Algarve* (Tavira, 1898).** [**Major primary folklore collection**] The volume contains 44 principal texts, some with variants, gathered largely through correspondents. It is the first Portuguese collection dedicated specifically to the genre, but Oliveira’s preference for stories framed around Muslim occupation and Christian reconquest affected its organization.

- **José Leite de Vasconcelos, “Lendas de Mouras Encantadas,” *Revista Lusitana* XIV (1911), and later collections.** [**Ethnographic corpus**]

- **Vicente Risco and the Galician ethnographic tradition.** [**Twentieth-century folklore documentation and systematization**] Risco records *mouros* as pre-Roman inhabitants in popular reckoning and integrates them into a wider account of Galician belief.

- **Aurelio de Llano Roza de Ampudia, *Del folklore asturiano* (1922) and related works; Constantino Cabal, *La mitología asturiana*; Jesús Suárez López and later collectors.** [**Asturian regional corpus**] These works document xanas, enchanted women, subterranean palaces, treasure, golden chickens, and castro legends. They do not establish that all such beings bore one indigenous name.

- **Manuel Llano and Adriano García-Lomas, *Mitología y supersticiones de Cantabria* (1964).** [**Cantabrian corpus, partly literary-systematic**] Their Anjanas and Moras share the golden comb, fountains, caves, and enchantment complex. Some named figures may owe their fixed biographies to collectors and literary elaboration.

- **José Miguel de Barandiarán, *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* and *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca* (1972); Resurrección María de Azkue, *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*; J. M. Satrústegi.** [**Basque ethnographic corpus**] These record *mairuak*, *lamiak*, Mari, and builders or inhabitants of megaliths. They supply parallels, not direct attestations of “the Mouras.”

- **Xosé Ramón Cuba, Antonio Reigosa, and Xosé Miranda, *Diccionario dos seres míticos galegos* (1999 and later editions).** [**Modern reference synthesis**] This organizes dispersed oral and printed evidence but is secondary to the individual collections it cites.

- **Alexandre Parafita, *A mitologia dos mouros* (2006).** [**Modern Portuguese synthesis**] Parafita catalogued 263 legends and variants while distinguishing historical Moors from supernatural *mouros*.

- **Buenaventura Aparicio Casado, *El folklore en los yacimientos arqueológicos de Galicia*, dissertation defended 1997.** [**Modern archaeological-anthropological reconstruction**] Its 525-site corpus argues that the narratives reveal the symbolic structures of traditional peasant society more reliably than they preserve prehistoric ritual.

- **María del Mar Llinares García, *Mouros, ánimas y demonios* (1990).** [**Modern structural/social interpretation**] Llinares reads the moura partly as a projection of rural gender categories: attractive, rich, sexually autonomous, dangerous, and positioned outside ordinary communal reciprocity.

- **Fernando Alonso Romero, “Las mouras constructoras de megalitos” (1998).** [**Modern comparative reconstruction**] Alonso Romero compares Galician building-women with Irish and other European old women, goddesses, and fairy figures. His inference of a Celtic or pre-Celtic territorial goddess is a scholarly hypothesis, not an epigraphically demonstrated genealogy.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

[**Folklore**] The mouras govern no uniform divine portfolio. Their recurrent fields are:

- hidden treasure and underground wealth;
- liminal water: fountains, springs, wells, rivers, pools, and cisterns;
- ancient or abandoned structures: castros, dolmens, *mámoas*, caves, castles, bridges, rock formations, and mines;
- enchantment, transformation, and the testing of mortals;
- spinning, weaving, and extraordinary construction;
- marriage, sexuality, fertility, and childbirth in particular variants;
- boundaries between Christian society and an older, non-Christian population;
- calendrical openings, especially midnight, dawn, and St John’s Eve or morning.

No source establishes that one collective being “governed” all these domains. They are a motif cluster distributed among many local figures.

### Appearance

[**Folklore; variable by locality**] The most familiar moura is young, exceptionally beautiful, pale, and long-haired. Hair may be gold, blond, red, or black depending on region and collector. She may wear white, red, brilliant clothing, or rich “Moorish” dress. Older builder traditions instead feature a gigantic or ancient *vella* carrying stones.

Common alternative bodies include:

- a great serpent or snake with a woman’s head;
- a woman who becomes a serpent when the mortal fails;
- an old woman or giantess;
- a pregnant or nursing woman;
- a spinner carrying a distaff, a child, and an enormous stone simultaneously;
- a white apparition resembling a restless dead woman;
- a historical Muslim princess enchanted by a father, brother, magician, or curse.

### Emblems and objects

- **Golden comb:** [**Widespread late-recorded folklore**] Used beside a fountain, river, or cave. It signifies beauty and wealth but may also be bait or a test.
- **Long hair:** [**Folklore**] An emblem of sexuality, liminality, and non-domesticated femininity in modern scholarly readings; informants do not necessarily give this symbolism explicitly.
- **Gold:** combs, rings, scissors, chains, bowls, beams, looms, hens and chicks, bulls, horses, and ordinary household utensils.
- **Distaff and spindle:** [**Builder-moura motif**] She spins while bearing megalithic stones on her head, sometimes with a child in her arms.
- **Serpent:** guardian form, transformed body, or obstacle in the disenchantment test.
- **Milk, bread, cakes, or food:** offerings or required objects in some local encounters.
- **Book of Saint Cyprian:** [**Early-modern/modern magical accretion**] Some treasure-seeking traditions require its ritual reading; this is part of Christian grimoire folklore, not evidence of an ancient moura scripture.
- **Golden chain or thread:** an apparently inexhaustible treasure whose cutting causes it to vanish and ruins the release.
- **Enchanted ring, thimble, and Roland’s sword:** [**1521 literary version**] Specific to Vicente’s theatrical moura and not standard pan-regional attributes.

### Sacred time

[**Christian-calendar folklore**] The principal date is **St John’s Eve and dawn, 23–24 June**. Mouras emerge, spread or display treasures, wash, dance, or comb their hair; mortals can see them or attempt release. The calendrical setting coincides with the summer solstice, but describing it as direct survival of a prehistoric solar cult is a modern inference. The transmitted date is Christian and must be documented as such.

### Iconography and surviving images

There is no securely identified ancient image of a Moura. No Roman statue, Iron Age figure, rock carving, or megalithic painting bears an inscription identifying it as one.

The earliest secure “iconography” is theatrical and textual: Vicente’s 1521 character with magical objects. Printed illustrations in nineteenth-century folklore books, especially Ataíde Oliveira’s illustrated 1898 collection, provide later visualizations. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century municipal statues, festival costumes, tourist graphics, and fantasy illustrations usually standardize the moura as a long-haired woman in white or “Moorish” dress. These are modern reception images, not copies of cult icons.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogy

There is no common genealogy.

- [**Historical-romance legend**] A moura may be the daughter of a Muslim king, governor, or commander, left in a castle with treasure during Christian conquest.
- [**Local folklore**] She may have been enchanted by her father, mother, brother, lover, magician, or an unnamed occult force.
- [**Moura-mãe cycle**] A pregnant moura summons a human midwife, gives birth underground, and rewards the helper.
- [**Collective-mouros tradition**] Mouras are simply the women of the subterranean *mouros*.
- [**Ghost-like interpretation**] She is a dead or penitent woman awaiting release.
- [**Learned pagan-survival theory**] She is a degraded goddess, nymph, territorial sovereignty figure, ancestor, or water spirit. No ancient genealogy supports these identifications.

### Relations and proposed equations

- **Mouros:** The closest and most consistently documented relation. Mouras are their female counterparts, relatives, or members of their subterranean community.
- **Human men:** The principal narrative partners. The moura tests, courts, rewards, terrifies, kills, or attempts to marry a mortal.
- **Human women:** Midwives, rivals, recipients of gifts, or observers; some narratives punish envy or failure to keep a taboo.
- **Serpents:** Sometimes the moura’s own alternate form; elsewhere a separate guardian or form imposed by an enchanter.
- **Xanas:** Regional analogue in Asturias, sometimes locally interchangeable with “mora” or “encantada,” but not universally identical.
- **Anjanas:** Cantabrian analogue developed in regional folklore and literary mythography.
- **Lamiak:** Basque water-side female beings and builders provide close motifs, but no source records a historical meeting with a moura.
- **Mairuak:** Basque ancient builders of megaliths parallel the Galician and Portuguese *mouros*. The grammatical and narrative categories are not identical.
- **Mari:** Modern authors sometimes equate the Basque Lady with the moura as an earth or sovereignty goddess. Anuntxi Arana has criticized conflations among Mari, *mairu*, *mairi*, and similar forms. No traditional narrative records Mari and a moura as the same individual or depicts them meeting.
- **Virgin Mary and female saints:** Some building legends transfer the moura’s feat—carrying a vast stone while spinning—to the Virgin. Alonso Romero interprets this as Christian replacement of a pre-Christian female figure. The shared motif is real; the exact historical mechanism remains a reconstruction.
- **Classical nymphs, Diana, Proserpina, Ataecina, and other ancient goddesses:** Proposed comparisons or survivals, not documented identities.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The following is the complete securely identified list within the consulted corpus:

- **Jupiter —** summons or participates in the divine court within which the Moura Encantada is released and equipped for the Infanta Beatriz’s voyage; Gil Vicente, *Cortes de Júpiter*, 1521. **Tier: early literary attestation; learned classical-Christian court fiction.**
- **Providence —** appears within the same dramatic structure and initiates the divine arrangements for Beatriz’s voyage; *Cortes de Júpiter*, 1521. The text does not establish a separate moura cult relationship. **Tier: early literary attestation.**
- **Mars, Venus, Sun, and Moon —** joint appearance in the same court spectacle; *Cortes de Júpiter*, 1521. No independent myth of personal interaction with the Moura has been identified. **Tier: early literary attestation; joint scene only.**

**Negative finding:** Outside this literary pageant, no securely documented traditional episode found in the consulted folklore presents a named Moura meeting, fighting, marrying, or forming a genealogy with a named pre-Roman god such as Endovelicus or Ataecina, or with Basque Mari. Similarity, proximity, and scholarly equation are not interactions.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The fountain moura and the golden comb

[**Folklore; nineteenth–twentieth-century collections**] At a fountain, spring, pool, or river, a man sees a beautiful woman combing her long hair with a golden comb. The apparition commonly occurs before sunrise on St John’s morning, at noon, or at midnight. She may offer the comb, ask the man to choose between her and treasure, or invite him to release her.

Variants make the comb:

- a reward;
- bait that kills or enchants the taker;
- an object the man must refuse;
- proof of the moura’s wealth;
- an ordinary instrument in a scene the witness observes without intervention.

The motif is shared with Asturian xanas, Cantabrian anjanas, Basque lamiak, and numerous European water women. Shared morphology does not date its arrival in Galicia or Portugal.

### The disenchantment test

[**Central folklore cycle**] A moura tells a mortal how to break her spell. He must return at an exact hour, remain silent, avoid invoking God or making the sign of the cross, kiss a serpent, feed or carry a monstrous animal, accept a frightening transformation, bring prescribed loaves, or correctly choose between the woman and treasure.

At the decisive moment she becomes a serpent, dragon, bull, or other terror. The mortal cries out, crosses himself, speaks God’s name, recoils, drops the object, or violates secrecy. The moura laments that the enchantment has been renewed—sometimes for another hundred or thousand years.

This cycle dramatizes incompatible worlds: Christian mortal society and an older enchanted domain. That structure is textually observable. Interpretations involving initiation, sacred marriage, repression of pagan religion, or male fear of female sexuality are modern scholarly readings.

### The serpent maiden

[**Securely attested by the 1653 Gouveia record; widespread later folklore**] The moura appears as a serpent or transforms between serpent and woman. Successful contact may release her and yield gold or marriage. Failure can make her disappear, double the enchantment, or kill the mortal.

The serpent is variously:

- the moura’s imposed body;
- a guardian around her;
- the required object of a kiss;
- a huge form that must be carried;
- the visible sign of an underground treasure.

Ancient Iberian serpent imagery cannot independently identify this legend. Claims that the moura is directly descended from a named Celtic serpent goddess remain unproved.

### The treasure guardian

[**Folklore**] Before withdrawing from the human world, *mouros* hide gold in a castro, cave, well, bridge, or castle and leave a woman to guard it. She may possess hens with golden chicks, golden bulls, beams, cauldrons, looms, or chains.

Treasure is often paired in antitheses:

- a beam of gold and a beam of pitch;
- treasure and plague;
- gold and poison;
- a beautiful woman and a serpent;
- prosperity and death.

The seeker must choose correctly or use a written treasure-book. Greed, broken secrecy, and impatience commonly destroy the opportunity.

[**Historical layering**] Medieval and early-modern stories plausibly attached hidden wealth to fleeing Muslims; in northern areas with little or no durable Islamic settlement, *mouros* nevertheless became generic previous inhabitants. The term therefore carries both historical-Moorish and mythical-subterranean meanings.

### Builders of castros and megaliths

[**Galician-Portuguese folklore; recorded in modern collections**] The *mouros* are credited with building castros, towers, caves, underground roads, fountains, and megaliths. Mouras themselves carry immense slabs on their heads while spinning and sometimes nursing or carrying a child. Dolmens consequently bear names such as *Casa da Moura* or *Arca da Moura*.

Fernando Alonso Romero compares these builders with the Irish Cailleach and European giantesses or old women. He concludes that an ancient Celtic or pre-Celtic goddess may stand behind the type. Aparicio Casado offers a more cautious model: the beings are a traditional society’s symbolic explanation for archaeological remains whose actual makers had been forgotten. Both models recognize genuine folklore; they differ over whether it retains prehistoric religious identity.

### The ancient dead as “Moors”

[**Modern reconstruction rooted in informant descriptions**] Some traditions explicitly say the *mouros* lived before the Romans or are the most ancient inhabitants. Their subterranean existence, association with tombs, opposition to baptized Christians, and ownership of ancestral wealth encouraged Millán González-Pardo and Alonso Romero to connect *mouro* with “dead.”

Counterevidence is substantial: most stories do not say that mouros died, were once ordinary local humans, or are ghosts. They eat, marry, work metal, keep animals, and form a parallel living society. “The pre-Roman dead” is therefore an illuminating possible interpretation, not a universal emic definition.

### The moura as fairy bride

[**Folklore; regional variants**] A mortal marries a supernatural woman after agreeing to a taboo: he must not call her a moura, strike her, mention a particular object, reveal her origin, or use a Christian sign. When he breaks the condition, she returns to the spring, cave, or hill. In some versions she continues helping her children.

This belongs to the international supernatural-wife or Melusine complex. Basque narratives of Mari or lamiak marrying mortals are comparative parallels, not evidence that all versions descend from one named deity.

### The moura-midwife and mother

[**Regional Portuguese folklore**] A moura in labor summons a human midwife into an underground dwelling. Payment may initially look worthless—coal, onion skins, or ashes—but becomes gold if retained. The helper may be warned not to touch or eat something, not to reveal what she saw, or not to use a magical ointment on both eyes.

This is an international fairy-midwife tale adapted to moura language. It complicates the modern stereotype of the eternally unmarried treasure princess.

### St John’s morning

[**Christianized calendrical folklore**] On 24 June the barriers around enchanted places open. Mouras comb their hair, wash, dance, expose treasure to the sun, or lead golden animals from underground. Galician traditions place them at fountains, castros, *mámoas*, and *penedos*; Portuguese versions favor springs, cisterns, castles, and rocks according to local landscape.

Calling this observance a direct pagan solstice rite is possible only as inference. The securely transmitted ritual date and saint are Christian.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Absence of an institutional cult

No moura temple, priesthood, canonical hymn, public sacrifice, divine image, votive altar, theophoric personal name, or ancient festival is securely attested. “The cult of the Mouras” is therefore not an appropriate description of the documented evidence.

What exists is a landscape of legend and occasional practice:

- visiting a fountain or rock on St John’s Eve;
- leaving bread, cake, milk, or other food in some local versions;
- searching for treasure;
- performing verbal formulas, silence, kissing, or carrying tests;
- reading or invoking the *Book of Saint Cyprian*;
- avoiding a dangerous place or hour;
- retelling a legend attached to a named archaeological site.

These may be ritualized acts, but they do not amount to evidence for an organized moura religion.

### Archaeological places

Moura traditions adhere especially to:

- castros and abandoned fortified settlements;
- Neolithic dolmens and passage tombs;
- *mámoas* and other burial mounds;
- prehistoric carvings and worked rocks;
- caves, mines, springs, wells, and cisterns;
- ruined Roman or medieval structures;
- castles associated retrospectively with Islamic rule.

The tradition can preserve valuable information about the position and local significance of a site. It cannot by itself identify the site’s builders or original cult. Aitor Freán Campo argues that this “imaginary of alterity” can assist archaeological survey precisely because oral narratives locate unusual remains, even when their historical explanation is mythical.

### The pre-Roman gods problem

The brief’s Endovelicus, Ataecina, and classical testimonia are essential controls on the evidence.

- **Endovelicus:** Approximately eighty Latin votive inscriptions and associated sculpture, coins, ceramics, and architectural remains document a Romano-Lusitanian sanctuary at São Miguel da Mota near Terena. Archaeological and epigraphic evidence places its flourishing principally in the first to third or fourth centuries CE. Formulae such as *ex visu*, *ex responsu*, and *iussu numinis* suggest dreams, divine commands, and oracular responses. This is what an epigraphically documented indigenous cult looks like. No inscription equates Endovelicus with a moura.

- **Ataecina:** Latin dedications identify a goddess associated through Roman interpretation with Proserpina and with Turobriga. Her attested distribution lies mainly across Extremadura, the Alentejo, and neighboring regions, with outliers. No dedication calls her a moura or connects her with a golden comb, St John’s morning, or the disenchantment tale.

- **Strabo, *Geography* III.3–4, composed around the Augustan age/early first century CE:** Strabo reports Lusitanian sacrifice and divination, including the killing of prisoners, the dedication of severed right hands, and offerings of goats, horses, and captives to a deity rendered as Ares. He also reports nocturnal household dancing to a nameless god at the full moon among northern Celtiberians and their neighbors. These passages are classical ethnography, transmitted through Greek literary conventions. They do not mention mouras.

Accordingly, allegations of **human sacrifice to the Mouras** are unsupported. Ancient authors allege or report human sacrifice among some Iberian peoples, but transferring that evidence to later moura folklore would be a category error. No evidence of temple prostitution, formal cult violence, or a moura priesthood was found.

### Christianization and suppression

Individual legends often encode Christian superiority: the moura cannot endure a cross, bell, saint’s name, baptism, or invocation of God. The disenchanter’s involuntary Christian response may nevertheless doom her to remain outside salvation. Place legends also transfer construction feats from mouras or old women to the Virgin or a saint.

These are evidence of Christian reframing. They do not document a specific ecclesiastical campaign against an organized moura cult. No securely sourced edict, trial series, or destruction of a “Temple of the Mouras” exists.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1500: hypothetical substrates, no named evidence

[**Unestablished**] Water spirits, supernatural women, ancestral builders, serpent beings, and treasure guardians existed widely in European narrative. Pre-Roman Iberia had documented local gods and goddesses. Neither fact demonstrates that a being called *Moura* existed in the Iron Age.

Medieval Christian-Muslim conflict supplied a powerful historical category—“the Moors”—to whom abandoned castles, buried treasure, technological marvels, and lost sovereignty could be assigned. The precise date when that category fused with older supernatural motifs is unknown.

### 1521–seventeenth century: literary and documentary visibility

Vicente’s *Cortes de Júpiter* proves courtly familiarity with an enchanted moura in 1521. The 1653 Gouveia record proves that a serpent-maiden-and-gold narrative could enter formal testimony in provincial Portugal. These witnesses already show diversity: theatrical magical princess versus local supernatural encounter.

### Eighteenth–early nineteenth century: continued oral circulation

Documentation is thinner than belief must have been. Local traditions continued around ruins, wells, castles, and treasure. Absence from print cannot be converted into proof of either disappearance or unchanged survival.

### 1820s–1870s: Romantic nationalization

Garrett and other Romantic writers made oral poetry and enchanted-moura legends evidence of national character. Learned authors increasingly sought Celtic, Indo-European, Arab, or primitive-religion origins. Literary poems historicized mouras as Muslim princesses and reshaped oral fragments into polished narratives.

### 1880s–1930s: the collecting era

Vasconcelos, Pedroso, Ataíde Oliveira, Risco, Azkue, Barandiarán, Aurelio de Llano, Cabal, and regional correspondents recorded extensive material. This preserved narratives but also classified fluid local beings into mythological “types.” Comparative mythology often treated resemblance as descent and sought solar, storm, Vedic, Celtic, or nymphic origins.

Ataíde Oliveira’s 1898 corpus fixed the Algarve as the emblematic Portuguese moura region. In Galicia, folklore scholarship consolidated the idea of the *mouros* as supernatural pre-Roman inhabitants of archaeological sites.

### Mid-twentieth century: regional pantheons

Regional mythographers organized Galician, Asturian, Cantabrian, and Basque traditions into named pantheons. This made material accessible but sometimes sharpened boundaries or identities that oral testimony left vague. “Xana,” “Anjana,” “Lamia,” “Mari,” and “Moura” came to appear as parallel taxonomic entries even where informants used generic words such as lady, enchanted woman, or Moorish girl.

### 1970s–2000s: social and archaeological interpretation

Researchers increasingly asked what moura stories did within the communities that told them.

- They made ancient ruins intelligible.
- They separated “our” baptized agricultural society from wealthy, nocturnal, non-Christian others.
- They expressed ambivalence about buried wealth, luck, labor, and social mobility.
- They represented both idealized beauty and threatening female autonomy.
- They preserved site knowledge useful for archaeological survey without preserving literal prehistoric history.

Llinares emphasized social and gender symbolism; Aparicio Casado emphasized the archaeological landscape and popular *interpretatio*; Alonso Romero renewed Celtic and pan-European comparison.

### Twenty-first century: heritage and performance

The moura remains active as heritage rather than as a demonstrably continuous cult. Municipalities stage legends, name routes and festivals, mark moura stones, and use the figure in tourism. Tavira continues to perform its Moura Encantada legend at the castle on St John’s Eve; its 2025 municipal program explicitly presented the event as culture, identity, and collective memory. The *Noites da Moura Encantada* at Cacela Velha, running from 2001, transformed the name into a Mediterranean cultural festival with idealized Muslim, Christian, and Sephardic coexistence.

No reliable demographic evidence establishes a substantial organized modern worship community devoted specifically to the Mouras. Individual neopagan veneration may exist, but presenting it as an ancient continuous religion would be a modern invention.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Celtic goddess theory

[**Modern scholarly and popular reconstruction**] Alonso Romero proposes that the megalith-building moura may descend from an ancient Celtic or pre-Celtic goddess, comparing the Irish Cailleach: an old woman, landscape maker, grain figure, and territorial sovereign. The comparison identifies a real European motif complex. It does not establish the ancient moura’s name, worship, or continuous transmission.

Popular summaries frequently remove this qualification and call the moura “a Celtic goddess.” That is stronger than the evidence permits.

### Nymph and water-spirit theory

[**Nineteenth-century comparativism**] Vasconcelos entertained assimilation of local nymphs or nature spirits; Pedroso described mouras as female water genii. This model fits the fountains, combing, beauty, danger, and fertility motifs but has no dedicatory inscription joining *nympha* to *moura*.

### Ancestor/dead theory

[**Twentieth-century reconstruction**] Millán González-Pardo and Alonso Romero link the word and subterranean people to Celtic “dead.” This offers a coherent reading of tombs and pre-Roman predecessors. Dias Marques’s critique—that most narratives do not actually make them ghosts—is decisive against treating the hypothesis as settled.

### Gender and psychoanalytic readings

[**Modern interpretation**] Structural and gender-focused scholarship sees the moura as the rural male imagination’s desired and feared woman: beautiful and wealthy but sexually active, powerful, unmarried, and outside village reciprocity. Marriage or disenchantment domesticates her; failure restores the boundary. Such readings explain narrative structure but are not informant testimony about an ancient goddess.

Jungian and archetypal publications may call her an anima, Great Mother, or shadow figure. These are twentieth-century psychological frameworks, not traditional classifications.

### Occult and grimoire reception

Some treasure legends incorporate the *Livro de São Cipriano*, magical circles, prayers, invocations, and prescribed midnight operations. These belong to Iberian Christian magic and modern grimoire circulation. No distinct Renaissance hermetic order, Golden Dawn rite, or historical occult lodge centered on the Mouras was identified.

Rosicrucian and esoteric websites now present the moura as a survival of Lusitanian water cults, goddess religion, or elemental spirits. Such claims generally synthesize folklore and comparative speculation without new ancient evidence.

### Literature and performance

The moura moved through:

- Gil Vicente’s court theater in 1521;
- Romantic national literature from Garrett onward;
- nineteenth-century legend collections and historical romances;
- local drama and pageants;
- contemporary fantasy art, games, online bestiaries, and heritage festivals.

Modern depictions favor a singular glamorous princess in “Moorish” costume. That image suppresses older and less marketable variants: giant builder, old woman, mother, serpent, dangerous tempter, anonymous underground inhabitant, or collective people.

### Political and ethnic implications

“Moor” historically functioned as a Christian label for Muslims and could carry hostility, exoticization, or racial difference. Moura legends sometimes romanticize defeated Muslim princesses; others make the *mouros* demonic, non-Christian, or incapable of salvation. Modern festivals may instead use the moura to promote multicultural Iberian heritage.

No evidence found supports a significant modern extremist cult of the Mouras. The more common political use is regional or national heritage-making—Galician Celticism, Portuguese Romantic identity, or tourist celebration of al-Andalus. Claims that the figure proves an ethnically pure Celtic religion or direct biological memory of pre-Roman inhabitants are not supported by the record.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Survival versus popular interpretation

The central dispute is methodological.

- **Survivalist/comparative position:** Repeated links among women, stone-building, water, serpents, fertility, spinning, sovereignty, and solstitial time suggest degraded remnants of pre-Christian goddesses. Alonso Romero’s work is the most explicit version named in the brief.

- **Historical-anthropological position:** Motifs may be old, but recorded moura legends are products of medieval and modern Christian rural society. Aparicio Casado concludes that they reflect their peasant creators more directly than prehistoric rites. Freán Campo similarly treats them as an “imaginary of alterity” organized around real archaeological remains.

These positions need not be absolute opposites: a narrative can reuse old international motifs while acquiring its actual social form in recent centuries. What cannot be done is to identify a prehistoric cult simply from resemblance.

### Muslim princess or non-Muslim supernatural race?

Both are attested.

- Southern Portuguese castle legends commonly historicize the moura as a Muslim princess left at the Reconquista.
- Galician *mouros* may predate Romans, inhabit prehistoric sites, eat pork, and bear little resemblance to historical Muslims.
- Some stories mix the categories: ancient supernatural treasure guardians are said to have fled “when the Moors left Spain.”

There was no single original version recoverable from present evidence.

### Etymological controversy

Latin *Maurus* provides the normal Romance form and historical semantic environment. Celtic “dead,” pre-Roman “mound,” Greek *Moira*, and other proposals aim to explain why “Moors” inhabit places much older than Islam. None has displaced the straightforward Romance derivation in a way that proves a separate ancient theonym.

### The “Celtic continuity” problem

Galicia and northern Portugal participated in Atlantic and Celtic-language cultural networks in antiquity, and medieval Irish narratives offer genuine comparanda. Yet parallel motifs can result from inheritance, diffusion, recurrent storytelling, or modern comparison. The ancient inscriptions name many local gods but never the Mouras. “Celtic” should therefore label a comparative hypothesis, not an attested divine identity.

### Reliability of collectors

Collectors preserved indispensable evidence but were not neutral recording machines:

- correspondents selected what seemed ancient or picturesque;
- dialect was regularized;
- fragmentary beliefs became complete “legends”;
- historical commentary was sometimes merged with oral text;
- national and regional programs favored Celtic, Arab, or indigenous explanations;
- named pantheons were systematized from overlapping generic beings.

Best practice is to retain collection place, collector, date, informant where known, and exact wording.

### State of the evidence

The physical evidence consists primarily of the archaeological places to which stories became attached; those sites do not identify the storytellers’ beings. The documentary evidence consists of literary texts, notarial testimony, printed folklore, field notes, dictionaries, sound recordings, and modern performances.

Relevant records survive in the Portuguese National Library and National Archaeology Museum, Galician and Portuguese ethnographic archives, the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore*, regional museums, university repositories, and published folklore corpora. Hispania Epigraphica is essential for testing alleged ancient connections: at present it supplies no inscription for a Moura.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- The Mouras are a securely identified pre-Roman goddess collective.
- A known statue or rock carving depicts them.
- Ataecina, Endovelicus, Mari, the lamiak, xanas, anjanas, and mouras are one timeless deity.
- St John’s legends directly document prehistoric solstice worship.
- *Moura* certainly derives from Celtic “dead,” Greek *Moira*, or a universal Mother Goddess.
- Strabo recorded sacrifices to the Mouras.
- A continuous secret moura cult survived Christianization.
- All moura stories are literal memories of historical Muslim women.
- Conversely, no story contains any historical Muslim layer.

Each assertion exceeds the evidence. The record supports a historically layered narrative complex, not either a single ancient goddess or a purely medieval invention.

## 11. Sources

The most important primary or near-primary witnesses are Gil Vicente’s *Cortes de Júpiter*; the 1653 Gouveia notarial report as printed in 1838; nineteenth- and twentieth-century field collections by Vasconcelos, Pedroso, Ataíde Oliveira, Risco, Aurelio de Llano, Cabal, Azkue, Barandiarán, Manuel Llano, and García-Lomas; and the site-specific oral traditions assembled by later Galician and Portuguese researchers. Ancient inscriptions and Strabo serve primarily as controls demonstrating what is—and is not—attested in pre-Roman and Roman Iberian religion.

Modern research particularly important to the conclusions above includes María del Mar Llinares García on Galician symbolic structure and gender; Buenaventura Aparicio Casado on folklore at 525 archaeological sites; Fernando Alonso Romero on megalith-building mouras; Aitor Freán Campo on alterity and archaeology; Anuntxi Arana’s criticism of Basque name-conflations; and José Joaquim Dias Marques on Portuguese-Galician patrimonialization and competing etymologies.
