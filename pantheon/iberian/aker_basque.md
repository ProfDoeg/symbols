# Aker (the Basque he-goat): Research Dossier

> **Class:** The evidence supports three historically distinct classifications:  
> **(1)** *Aherbelste*, a Roman-period Aquitanian deity known from one Latin votive altar, whose name has sometimes—uncertainly—been interpreted as “black goat”;  
> **(2)** the goat-shaped **Devil of the witches’ sabbath** in seventeenth-century Basque witchcraft accusations; and  
> **(3)** **Aker/Akerbeltz**, a folkloric “genius,” protective black he-goat, or reconstructed pre-Christian divinity in twentieth-century Basque ethnography and modern reception.  
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** A Roman-period marble altar, conventionally dated broadly between the **second and fifth centuries CE**, inscribed *AHERBELSTE DEO* and found at **Landorthe, Haute-Garonne, France**. It securely attests a god named *Aherbelste*, but not securely the later folkloric Aker/Akerbeltz. The proposed equation depends on a disputed etymology. The earliest secure documentary appearance of the goat presiding over the specifically Basque witch-sabbath complex belongs instead to the **Labourd and Zugarramurdi prosecutions of 1609–1614**. “Aker” as the proper name of an independently documented ancient Basque god is not securely attested.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

“Aker” is first of all ordinary Basque **aker**, “adult male goat, billy-goat.” Modern mythological dictionaries use the same word as a name for a goat-shaped underground genius or Devil who presides over assemblies of witches. **Akerbeltz** is transparent modern Basque *aker* “he-goat” + *beltz* “black”: “black he-goat.”

These forms must not be treated as automatically identical with:

- the unnamed goat- or animal-shaped Devil described in seventeenth-century prosecutions;
- the Roman-period Aquitanian god *Aherbelste*;
- the Christian Satan;
- Greek Pan, Dionysus, or the modern neopagan Horned God;
- Gaulish Cernunnos;
- Mari or any supposed male consort of Mari.

Those identifications belong to different historical periods and possess very different levels of evidence.

### Documented names and forms

| Form | Language/context | Meaning or status | Evidentiary tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **aker** | Basque | Ordinary noun, “male goat”; later a mythological label | Secure lexical fact; mythological capitalization is late |
| **akher** | Northern/eastern Basque orthographic and dialectal form | “Male goat” | Secure lexical variant |
| **akerra** | Basque with definite article *-a* | “The male goat” | Secure lexical form |
| **Aker** | Modern mythographical capitalization | Goat-shaped “genius” or Devil | Learned folkloric systematization |
| **aker beltz / Akerbeltz / Aker Beltz** | Basque | “Black he-goat” | Secure compound as language; classification as an ancient god is disputed |
| **Aherbelste** | Aquitanian theonym in a Latin dedication | Name of a god; second element plausibly related to *belex/bels*, “black” | Securely attested deity; “black goat” translation uncertain |
| **Akelarre** | Basque/Spanish | Conventionally parsed as *aker* + *larre*, “goat’s meadow”; from 1610 onward, a witches’ assembly or its site | Early-modern attestation; etymology contested |
| **aquelarre** | Spanish adaptation | Witches’ sabbath; later any turbulent or sinister gathering | Secure from early-modern Spanish documentation |
| **Alkelarre / Alkelarreko larrea** | Zugarramurdi toponymic forms reported in the debate | Possibly “meadow of *alka* grass,” rather than “goat meadow” | Contested toponymic explanation |
| **le grand bouc / bouc puant** | French demonology | “Great goat,” “stinking goat” as a form of the Devil | Early-modern demonological vocabulary |
| **El gran cabrón** | Spanish art-historical title | “The Great He-Goat,” Goya’s sabbath Devil | Modern title/reception |

The Real Academia Española’s historical dictionary records Spanish *aquelarre* from **1610** and derives it conventionally from Basque *akelarre*, *aker* + *larre*. It also acknowledges the historical-etymological Basque dictionary’s caution that the original form may have been merely a place-name before it became a term for an assembly ([RAE historical dictionary](https://temporalraeweb.rae.es/dhle/aquelarre)).

### The *akelarre* controversy

Two principal interpretations must be preserved:

1. **Traditional etymology — “goat’s meadow.”**  
   *Aker* “he-goat” + *larre* “meadow/pasture.” This parsing explains why prosecutors and later writers associated the word with a goat-shaped Devil.

2. **Toponymic reinterpretation — “meadow of alka grass.”**  
   Mikel Azurmendi and others argued that the Zugarramurdi name was originally *Alkelarre*, associated with *alka*, a local grass, and that inquisitors or their informants resegmented it as *aker-larre*. Pablo Fermín Irigaray reported local forms *alkelarre*, *alkerdi*, and *alkezuri*, remarking that the name did not “smell of the goat.” Henningsen’s response emphasizes that the documentary history is more complicated than the strongest “complete invention” thesis allows ([Henningsen, “A vueltas con el término aquelarre”](https://www.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/PDFAnlt/rievcuadernos/09/09042053.pdf)).

The safest conclusion is therefore:

- **Secure:** *akelarre/aquelarre* entered witch-trial vocabulary in the early seventeenth century.
- **Probable:** its later general meaning developed from a Zugarramurdi place-name.
- **Disputed:** whether contemporaries originally understood that place-name as “goat meadow.”
- **Not established:** that the word preserves the ancient name of a pre-Christian cult or deity.

### Classification through time

- **Roman Aquitanian inscription:** *Aherbelste* is explicitly *deus*, “god.”
- **Seventeenth-century prosecutions:** the presiding animal is the Christian **Devil/Demon/Satan**, not consistently a local god named Aker.
- **Twentieth-century ethnography:** Aker/Akerbeltz becomes a **genius**, underground numen, protective animal spirit, or “divinity.”
- **Modern popular mythology and neopagan discourse:** often promoted to a **pre-Christian god of livestock, fertility, healing, storms, magic, or the underworld**. Some of these functions extrapolate beyond the primary evidence.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The altar to Aherbelste

The earliest possible evidence is a small marble votive altar found at **Landorthe**, in the French department of Haute-Garonne. Its inscription reads:

> **AHERBEL / STE DEO / SENIVS ET / HANNA / PROCVL[I—]**

A cautious translation is:

> “To the god Aherbelste, Senius and Hanna, [sons or freedpersons] of Proculus …”

The French national museum catalogue dates the object broadly to the **second–fifth century CE**. It records the object as marble, 26.2 cm high, with a sacrificial focus in the top. By 1822 it belonged to Alexandre du Mège’s antiquities cabinet and by 1844 had entered the Toulouse collections ([French Ministry of Culture catalogue](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/05630030072)).

What the altar establishes:

- a god called *Aherbelste* received a Latin-form votive dedication;
- the dedicants bore indigenous Aquitanian names;
- *Aherbelste* is a **hapax**, attested on this one monument;
- the second component resembles Aquitanian personal-name elements *belex/bels*, often compared with Basque *beltz*, “black.”

What it does **not** establish:

- the god’s image;
- a goat form;
- a sanctuary or organized priesthood;
- mythology, genealogy, rituals, sex, or domain;
- worship south of the Pyrenees;
- continuity into early-modern witch beliefs.

The first element has been compared both with Basque *akher/aker*, “goat,” and with *harri*, “stone.” The museum catalogue expressly calls these semantic identifications uncertain. Thus “Black Goat God” is a defensible scholarly proposal, not the inscription’s literal, securely deciphered meaning.

### Early-modern witchcraft corpus

#### Pierre de Lancre, *Tableau de l’inconstance des mauvais anges et démons* (1612)

Pierre de Rosteguy de Lancre participated in the royal commission sent into **Labourd in 1609**. His *Tableau*, printed in French in 1612, describes alleged sabbaths using confessions, judicial materials, demonological authorities, and his own polemical narration.

The sabbath Devil is variously reported as:

- seated on a black chair;
- crowned with black horns;
- furnished with a luminous horn;
- nearly human-faced, enormous, and terrifying;
- two-faced, front and rear;
- a large black greyhound;
- a great ox lying on the ground;
- or a he-goat.

The plurality matters. De Lancre does not preserve a stable portrait of an indigenous god named Aker; he organizes variable testimony within learned Christian demonology. His full early edition is available through [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Tableau_de_l_inconstance_des_mauvais_ang.html?id=s6l-hFTNPCwC).

#### The Logroño tribunal and Zugarramurdi, 1609–1610

Proceedings began after reports from Urdax and Zugarramurdi reached the Logroño tribunal in January 1609. The public auto-da-fé took place on **7–8 November 1610**. Thirty-one defendants were presented in the principal witchcraft cause; eleven received death sentences, conventionally counted as six people burned alive and five already dead in prison whose effigies and remains were burned.

The public relation attributes to the sect:

- renunciation of Jesus, the Virgin, and Christian signs;
- adoration of the Devil;
- dancing to flute and drum;
- infernal but non-burning fires;
- magical flight;
- animal familiars;
- poisoning and murder;
- cannibalism and abuse of corpses;
- sexual relations with the Devil.

These are accusations and confession narratives shaped by interrogation, public spectacle, European demonology, expectation, and recirculating testimony. They are documentary evidence for what authorities and frightened communities said, not independent proof that the described rites occurred.

A digitized nineteenth-century edition of the auto relation survives through [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AAuto_de_F%C3%A9_celebrado_en_la_ciudad_de_Logro%C3%B1o_en_los_d%C3%ADas_7_y_8_de_Noviembre_del_a%C3%B1o_de_1610_siendo_Inquisidor_General_el_Cardenal%2C_Arzobispo_de_Toledo%2C_Don_Bernardo_de_Sandobal_y_Roxas_%28IA_b30363755%29.pdf).

#### Alonso de Salazar Frías, visitation papers and memorials, 1611–1614

After the auto, inquisitor **Alonso de Salazar Frías** undertook an extensive visitation. He received 1,802 confessions or declarations—1,546 in Navarre during the first four months and 256 in Gipuzkoa, Biscay, and Álava afterward—and conducted experiments and cross-checks.

His first two principal memorials are dated **24 March 1612**; another major report followed on **3 October 1613**. Salazar found no independently corroborated evidence for the alleged sabbaths, flights, ointments, murders, or bodily presence at gatherings. He showed how accusations multiplied through preaching, questioning, fear, children’s statements, and pressure to confess.

His work informed the Suprema’s **32-article instructions of 29 August 1614**, which imposed stringent evidentiary controls. Henningsen’s study of these papers calls this a turning point in the Spanish treatment of witchcraft ([Henningsen, “Los documentos de Alonso de Salazar Frías”](https://revistas.navarra.es/index.php/PV/article/download/1714/1092)).

The surviving records reside chiefly in the **Archivo Histórico Nacional, Madrid**, among papers transmitted to the Suprema. Much of the Logroño tribunal’s local archive disappeared during the Napoleonic period. One of Salazar’s original visitation registers survived because it had reached the central archive; other experimental records are lost.

### Modern folkloric corpus

The consolidated Aker/Akerbeltz entry belongs principally to twentieth-century collection and systematization:

- José Miguel de Barandiarán, articles in the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* and *Mitología vasca*;
- Resurrección María de Azkue, *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*;
- Julio Caro Baroja, *Las brujas y su mundo*, *Brujería vasca*, and related studies;
- later dictionaries and summaries drawing on them.

Barandiarán’s formulation describes Akerbeltz as an underground genius in black-goat form, protector or healer of animals, while also connecting it to the sabbath Devil. This is important ethnographic evidence for twentieth-century memory and classification. Its statements about remote antiquity remain reconstructions unless independently supported.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Attested domains

#### Aherbelste

No domain is given. “Goats,” “blackness,” “stones,” livestock, or the underworld are etymological or comparative deductions, not statements on the altar.

#### The early-modern sabbath Devil

In de Lancre and the Logroño narratives, the figure:

- presides over assemblies;
- receives reverence and offerings;
- inducts novices;
- orders renunciation of Christianity;
- preaches or performs a parody of Mass;
- distributes mock communion;
- directs dancing and alleged malefic activity;
- participates in sexualized rites;
- appears in multiple animal and humanoid forms.

This is standard Christian demonological sovereignty: a parody of God, priest, king, and master. It is not a securely recoverable indigenous cult office.

#### Folkloric Akerbeltz

Later ethnographic summaries attribute:

- protection of livestock;
- healing or prevention of animal disease;
- underground habitation;
- command of lesser spirits;
- association with caves;
- storm-making;
- possession or guardianship of treasure.

The best-supported domestic custom is the reported belief that keeping a male goat—sometimes specifically a black one—in the stable benefited the other animals. Auñamendi’s ethnographic lexicon calls the he-goat healthy and health-giving and records the belief that its presence ensured livestock health ([Auñamendi, “Aker”](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/es/aker/ar-7737/)). Whether that husbandry belief originally concerned a personified deity is not established.

### Iconography

No ancient depiction is securely identifiable as Aherbelste or Aker.

The first major visual type is therefore the **European sabbath goat-Devil**, not a demonstrated cult image. Its defining traits are:

- large dark goat body or goat head;
- horns;
- central or elevated position;
- a circle of witches;
- nocturnal setting;
- offerings, children, or corpses;
- parody of worship.

#### Goya, *El Aquelarre*, 1797–1798

Goya’s oil painting for the Dukes of Osuna places a great horned goat at the center of a moonlit assembly. Women offer an infant and an emaciated child; dead infants hang from a staff. Vine leaves around the goat’s horns introduce a Bacchic visual allusion. The Fundación Goya catalogue treats the painting as a criticism of superstition and ignorance and cautiously proposes that the 1611 Logroño relation or Moratín’s work on it informed the imagery ([Fundación Goya en Aragón](https://fundaciongoyaenaragon.es/obra/el-aquelarre/526)).

This is not an ethnographic portrait of Aker. It is Enlightenment-era artistic reception of demonological literature.

#### Goya, *El aquelarre o El gran cabrón*, 1820–1823

The vast Black Painting from the Quinta del Sordo presents a dark goat-like speaker before a crowd. It was painted directly on a wall and later transferred to canvas. The Prado records dimensions of 140.5 × 435.7 cm and its transfer and museum provenance ([Museo del Prado](https://www.museodelprado.es/coleccion/obra-de-arte/el-aquelarre-o-el-gran-cabron/09559184-cfeb-48fe-8acc-89b070b64d92)).

Again, its proper art-historical identity is the Great He-Goat/Devil of the sabbath, not securely the Basque Akerbeltz.

#### Néstor Basterretxea, *Aker Beltz*, 1972

Basterretxea’s oak sculpture, 210 cm high, belongs to his *Basque Cosmogony Series*, based on Barandiarán’s 1972 mythological dictionary. The series was first exhibited in 1973. The work consciously monumentalizes Akerbeltz as part of modern Basque cultural identity ([Bilbao Fine Arts Museum](https://bilbaomuseoa.eus/explora/obra-de-arte/aker-beltz/24c7f283-b817-41e9-a89e-d0dc110fbdb3)).

The museum’s description calls the being a black goat, reincarnation of the Devil, partial substitute for Mari, and archaic Neolithic divinity. The first two statements summarize folklore; “Neolithic” is a modern primitivist reconstruction unsupported by Neolithic inscriptions or identifiable images.

### Unsupported or weakly supported attributes

No early source securely assigns Aker:

- a sacred day, number, planet, or color other than the lexical “black” in Akerbeltz;
- a weapon or formal regalia;
- a temple;
- a priesthood;
- a canonical consort or children;
- a specific celestial office;
- exclusive government of fertility, sexuality, storms, magic, or the dead.

These circulate in modern mythographic and occult summaries but should not be retrojected.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

No source provides Aker/Akerbeltz with a securely attested genealogy. There is no ancient text naming parents, siblings, consort, or offspring.

### Claimed relation to Mari

Modern summaries sometimes make Akerbeltz:

- an attribute of Mari;
- Mari’s male counterpart;
- one of her animal forms;
- her consort;
- a subordinate or “substitute” for her.

The defensible evidence is only that twentieth-century systematizers compared their underground character, cave associations, animal manifestations, and powers. No early narrative records a meeting, marriage, sexual union, dialogue, conflict, or joint action between Mari and Aker. “Consort of Mari” is therefore **modern reconstruction**, not attested genealogy.

### Syncretistic comparisons

- **Satan/the Devil:** secure identification within seventeenth-century Christian demonology.
- **Pan:** modern comparison based on goat form and pastoral associations; no historical cult equation.
- **Dionysus/Bacchus:** an iconographic allusion in Goya’s vine-crowned goat; not a Basque cult identity.
- **Cernunnos:** modern comparative mythology and neopagan syncretism; no ancient source equates them.
- **Horned God of modern witchcraft/Wicca:** modern reception, not continuity.
- **Aherbelste:** possible linguistic predecessor, but historical identity and transmission remain unproved.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

| Other being | Interaction | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Christian God** | Sabbath initiates allegedly renounce God; the Devil’s rites parody divine worship | Labourd and Logroño witchcraft records, 1609–1612 | Early-modern demonological accusation |
| **Jesus Christ** | Initiates are instructed not to utter Jesus’s name; sabbath ritual inverts Christian allegiance | Logroño auto relation, 1610/printed 1611 | Early-modern demonological accusation |
| **Virgin Mary** | Initiates allegedly renounce or avoid invoking the Virgin | Logroño auto relation, 1610/1611 | Early-modern demonological accusation |
| **Satan/the Devil** | The goat is not a separate interacting being but one of the Devil’s alleged manifestations | De Lancre and Logroño corpus, 1609–1612 | Secure historical identification inside Christian demonology |
| **Bacchus/Dionysus** | Goya gives the sabbath goat vine-leaf ornament recalling Bacchic iconography | *El Aquelarre*, 1797–1798 | Modern artistic allusion |
| **Mari** | No actual interaction is documented; only resemblance, substitution, or association is asserted by modern mythography | Barandiarán-derived twentieth-century systematization; Basterretxea museum interpretation, 1972 onward | Modern scholarly/artistic reconstruction |

There are **no securely attested interactions** with Endovelicus, Ataecina, Lugus, Nabia, Erge, Baeserte, Asto Ilunno, Sugaar, Maju, Basajaun, the Asturian *xanas*, Cantabrian beings, or Galician mythic figures. Membership in a modern “Iberian pantheon” does not create a narrative relationship.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Aker possesses no ancient epic or canonical narrative cycle. Its narrative corpus consists of accusation patterns, folklore motifs, and modern retellings.

### The sabbath assembly

**Early-modern demonological version.** At night, witches are transported or travel to a meeting place. The Devil presides in a terrifying animal or composite form. Novices renounce Christianity, kiss or worship the Devil, receive a mark or familiar, participate in a parody of Mass, feast, dance, engage in sex, and receive orders to commit maleficia.

The fullest versions occur in:

- de Lancre’s Labourd material, published 1612;
- the Logroño/Zugarramurdi relation, printed 1611;
- subsequent summaries and polemics.

The elements are not peculiarly Basque. They belong to a trans-European learned sabbath model shaped by medieval heresy accusations, demonology, judicial questions, and circulating print. Local contributions—place-names, languages, named dancers and musicians, caves, community rivalries, children’s stories—were fitted into that model.

### The black goat as guardian of livestock

**Folklore.** A black male goat kept in a stable protects other domestic animals from illness. In its restrained form, this is an animal-health belief documented by ethnographers. The stronger story—that an underground deity Akerbeltz inhabits every black goat and was once universally worshipped—is a systematizing extrapolation.

### Cave, underground spirits, and treasure

Later mythological dictionaries give Akerbeltz an underground dwelling, subordinate spirits, caves, and sometimes guarded treasure. These motifs resemble widespread Basque and Pyrenean folklore but lack a clearly dated early Aker narrative. They should be classified as **folkloric aggregation**, not a recoverable ancient cycle.

### “The Inquisition demonized an old goat god”

This influential modern story says:

1. pre-Christian Basques worshipped a benign black goat deity;
2. Christianity transformed it into Satan;
3. inquisitors misrepresented surviving pagan rites as the witches’ sabbath.

There are pieces compatible with such a possibility: the Aherbelste altar, livestock-protection folklore, the ordinary Basque goat word, and the sabbath goat. But no surviving source supplies the historical chain connecting them. Salazar’s findings instead demonstrate that much of the 1609–1614 “sect” was generated through accusation, suggestion, repeated interrogation, dreams, fear, and communal contagion.

Thus the continuous-cult narrative is **modern scholarly/popular reconstruction**, not an established historical sequence.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Aherbelste

The Landorthe altar demonstrates at least one votive act to a god. Its focus could receive a small offering or libation. Nothing establishes:

- a temple;
- a sanctuary at Landorthe;
- a recurring festival;
- priests;
- animal or human sacrifice;
- an organized cult extending into the Basque Country.

### Zugarramurdi cave

The cave now called **Akelarren Lezea** is deeply associated with witch memory and tourism. Later accounts say the Devil received witches through a window-like opening and that devotees adored Aker, offered food or money, reported their deeds, and received instructions.

These descriptions combine:

- early-modern trial mythology;
- local place-memory;
- antiquarian inference from prehistoric occupation;
- later folkloric and tourist narration.

Prehistoric remains in a cave do not show that prehistoric residents worshipped Aker. Nor does the seventeenth-century accusation establish that actual nocturnal goat worship occurred there.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Alleged sabbath offerings include bread, eggs, money, and reverential acts. Cannibalism, infant murder, corpse abuse, and human flesh appear in demonological testimony. These are **documented allegations**, not archaeologically or independently substantiated rites.

Modern descriptions of goats bought, killed, and roasted in the cave concern later local gatherings or reported custom. They do not establish ancient sacrifice to Aker.

There is no secure evidence of:

- human sacrifice to Aker;
- temple prostitution;
- a hereditary priesthood;
- a cult treasury;
- an oracle;
- theophoric names built from Aker;
- a pre-Christian Aker festival calendar.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Roman Aquitania, approximately second–fifth centuries CE

A god *Aherbelste* receives one Latin dedication. A Basque or Aquitanian interpretation of the name is plausible; “black goat” remains uncertain. No myth or image survives.

### Medieval period

No securely identified medieval source documents Aker as a named god. European theology and judicial culture gradually developed the witches’ sabbath and goat-shaped Devil, but this is broader Christian demonological history, not evidence of an Aker cult.

### 1609–1614: construction and dismantling of the Basque witch sect

- **1609:** de Lancre’s Labourd commission and the opening of proceedings around Zugarramurdi.
- **1610:** Logroño auto-da-fé; *aquelarre* enters documented witchcraft vocabulary.
- **1611:** printed relations spread the case.
- **1611–1612:** Salazar conducts his visitation and evidentiary tests.
- **1612:** de Lancre publishes his elaborate demonological account; Salazar submits major memorials.
- **1613–1614:** further debate leads to the Suprema’s restrictive instructions.

Henningsen’s strongest contribution is not that every accused person or every local belief was invented from nothing. It is that the alleged organized sect described by prosecution narratives cannot be treated as a straightforward survival of pagan religion. Interrogation and publicity actively manufactured the standardized witch.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

*Aquelarre* expands in Spanish from a local or Basque witch-meeting term to a general word for a witches’ sabbath. Literary and artistic reception increasingly fixes the goat as the central icon.

Goya’s paintings, especially those of 1797–1798 and 1820–1823, become more influential on the modern appearance of “Aker” than any securely identified pre-Christian image.

### Late nineteenth–twentieth-century folklore collection

Basque ethnographers record oral narratives, vocabulary, rural customs, and memories. Barandiarán and Azkue preserve invaluable evidence, but dictionary organization also turns fluid motifs into named mythological “beings.” Aker/Akerbeltz consequently acquires the appearance of a stable pantheon member.

Caro Baroja and Henningsen reinsert the figure into the history of European demonology, judicial institutions, and social panic. Their work makes a continuous pagan-cult explanation increasingly difficult to sustain without qualification.

### Late twentieth century to present

Akerbeltz functions as:

- a symbol of Basque cultural antiquity;
- a visual figure in sculpture and illustration;
- a name for bands, products, festivals, and cultural groups;
- a neopagan or occult deity;
- a figure in online mythological catalogues;
- shorthand for the witch persecutions.

Continuous worship from antiquity has not been demonstrated. Contemporary devotion exists on an individual and small-group basis, but reliable demographic scale and institutional continuity are unavailable.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Ethnographic canonization

Barandiarán’s dictionary and mythological writings supplied the framework for much later popular representation. The move from “a black goat is protective” to “Akerbeltz is the ancient Basque god of livestock” is a characteristic example of modern mythological systematization.

### Goya

Goya’s two major sabbath paintings established the dominant modern image: a monumental horned goat surrounded by women, children, corpses, and nocturnal terror. They illustrate Enlightenment and post-Enlightenment engagement with superstition, violence, irrationality, and social control, not documentary pre-Christian iconography.

### Basterretxea

Néstor Basterretxea’s *Aker Beltz* (1972) translates the ethnographic dictionary into an assertive modern Basque sculptural canon. The Bilbao museum records that his *Basque Cosmogony Series* comprised eighteen mythological and cultural forms made between 1972 and 1975 and first displayed in 1973 ([series catalogue](https://bilbaomuseoa.eus/en/visit/whats-on/exhibition/basque-cosmogony-series/b10cead2-046a-453b-8fae-c4e30e2a0642)).

### Film

Pablo Agüero’s **2020 film *Akelarre*** dramatizes young women accused by inquisitorial authorities. Its central “sabbath” emerges through coercive interrogation and the prisoners’ strategic performance, closely reflecting the modern historiographical insight that prosecutors demanded a story and defendants learned to supply it. The production describes the alleged akelarre as a ceremony in which the Devil initiates and mates with his servants ([official production page](https://www.garizafilms.com/projects/akelarre/)).

### Neopagan and occult reception

Modern occult encyclopedias and online devotional discourse commonly describe Akerbeltz as:

- a horned mountain god;
- a fertility deity;
- an animal guardian;
- a god of magic or witches;
- an analogue of Pan, Cernunnos, or the Wiccan Horned God;
- Mari’s consort.

These propositions are **modern syncretistic constructions**. They may be meaningful within living religious practice, but they are not ancient facts established by the sources.

No evidence found connects Akerbeltz specifically with the Renaissance Hermetic corpus, the Golden Dawn, classical ceremonial magic, or an early modern grimoire tradition. Its occult career is mainly late twentieth- and twenty-first-century mythographic and neopagan reception.

### Political and cultural appropriation

The witch persecutions have become symbols of:

- repression of women;
- suppression of Basque language and culture;
- state and clerical coercion;
- resistance to imposed authority;
- feminist and regional memory.

These readings emerged long after the trials and are not interchangeable with the accused persons’ own beliefs. Nevertheless, they are genuine parts of the figure’s modern historical record.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Aker an ancient Basque god?

**Evidence in favor:**

- a Roman deity *Aherbelste*;
- plausible comparison of *Aher-* with *aker/akher*;
- plausible comparison of *-belste* with *beltz*, “black”;
- later black-goat livestock folklore;
- the goat-shaped sabbath Devil.

**Evidence against certainty:**

- *Aherbelste* occurs once;
- its first element may admit another analysis;
- no iconography identifies it as a goat;
- approximately a millennium separates the altar from the trials;
- the trials describe the Christian Devil, not consistently a god named Akerbeltz;
- the protective livestock material was collected much later;
- no transmission chain survives.

The defensible verdict is **possible linguistic and folkloric relationship, unproved cultic continuity**.

### Was the Basque witch a creation of the Inquisition?

The dispute is best framed in layers.

- Authorities did not invent every belief in magic, harmful sorcery, night travel, animals, healing, or communal hostility.
- European demonologists supplied the organized, anti-Christian sect model.
- Interrogators elicited expected elements.
- accused adults and children repeated, embellished, and circulated them;
- sermons and public proceedings amplified the panic;
- Salazar’s tests found no objective confirmation.

Thus “invention” is substantially correct for the coherent international-style witch sect presented by prosecution literature, but too absolute if taken to mean that the population possessed no prior magical beliefs, conflicts, dreams, stories, or ritual practices.

### Was *akelarre* invented in 1609?

The word’s witchcraft meaning crystallized in the prosecution. Whether the underlying Zugarramurdi place-name already meant “goat meadow” is disputed. The strongest claim—that inquisitors fabricated both word and place from nothing—is not established. The more cautious account is semantic reanalysis and judicial popularization.

### Comparative and pan-Iberian claims

The dossier brief places Aker in a broad “Iberian (Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician, pre-Roman) pantheon.” The evidence does not support such a unified historical pantheon.

- Strabo’s Book III and other classical descriptions of Iberian peoples do not name Aker.
- Latin inscriptions to Endovelicus, Ataecina, Nabia, and other western Iberian gods do not connect them to Aker.
- Asturian and Cantabrian folklore collections do not supply a securely identical named Aker.
- Galician folklore contains goat-shaped Devils and witch assemblies within broader Iberian and European traditions, but no documented genealogy or cultic identity with Basque Aker.
- Similarity is insufficient to prove shared deity or common cult.

### State of the evidence

The record is unusually asymmetrical:

- one Roman altar, without narrative;
- thousands of pages of hostile early-modern judicial and demonological writing;
- much later oral folklore;
- influential modern art and scholarship.

That asymmetry explains why confident biographies of an ancient “goat god” exceed what can be reconstructed.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest defensible history is not the biography of a single, continuously worshipped goat god. It is the history of a **possible Aquitanian theonym**, an **early-modern goat-shaped Devil manufactured and circulated through witchcraft prosecution**, a **rural protective-animal belief**, and a **modern Basque mythological figure assembled from those materials**.

Aherbelste may indeed preserve the linguistic ancestor of *Akerbeltz*. That possibility is important and should not be discarded. But neither the inscription nor the witch records establish a continuous cult, and no source securely supplies Aker with the elaborate pantheon position, genealogy, fertility theology, or ancient ritual calendar commonly assigned to it today.
