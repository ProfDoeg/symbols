# Endovelicus: Research Dossier

**Class:** male god (*deus*, *numen*) of Roman Lusitania, bearing a non-Latin indigenous name but documented almost entirely through Roman-period Latin inscriptions and Greco-Roman votive art.

**Earliest secure attestation:** first century CE, probably a marble votive pedestal from São Miguel da Mota dedicated by Marcus Vibius Bassus. No securely datable pre-Roman inscription, image, or sanctuary layer names Endovelicus. The indigenous character of the name makes an earlier cult plausible, but does not prove an Iron Age cult site or recover its rites. Most datable inscriptions belong to the second and third centuries CE. The standard modern corpus is concentrated in *CIL* II 127–142, 5201–5209, 6265–6269, 6329–6331 and 6334–6336; José d’Encarnação subsequently catalogued IRCP 483–559, and recent excavation has raised the known assemblage from “about eighty” toward one hundred monuments.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Secure Roman]**, **[Probable Roman reconstruction]**, **[Disputed scholarly reconstruction]**, **[Medieval/early-modern tradition]**, **[Modern reception]**, and **[Modern invention or unsupported claim]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identification

Endovelicus was the principal divine recipient at the terrace sanctuary on the eastern slope of São Miguel da Mota, near Terena in the modern municipality of Alandroal, Évora district, Portugal. The site lay above the Lucefécit valley, within Roman Lusitania and near the probable boundary between the *conventus* of Pax Iulia and Emerita Augusta. The surviving record presents a beneficent, responsive male *numen*: worshippers discharged vows, sought health for themselves or relatives, obeyed his commands, and sometimes reported visions. Chthonic, oracular, healing, woodland, animal-protecting and salvific interpretations are modern attempts to synthesize these indications; no surviving indigenous myth explains his nature.

The item-specific classification “Iberian (Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician, pre-Roman) pantheon” is too broad. Endovelicus is securely Lusitanian, specifically south-central Portuguese, evidence. There is no Endovelicus cycle in Basque collections such as Barandiarán or Azkue, in Asturian or Cantabrian folklore corpora, or in Galician mythology. Nor does Strabo III name him. Those traditions must not be combined merely because all are Iberian.

### Attested names and spellings

All secure ancient forms occur in Latin script, normally in the dative because the monuments are dedications:

- **ENDOVELLICO / Endovellico**: the commonest monumental spelling.
- **ENDOVELICO / Endovelico**: single-*l* spelling.
- **ENDOVOLLICO / Endovollico**: vowel variant.
- **INDOVELLICO / Indovellico**: initial-vowel variant, as in Petronia Albilla’s altar: *Deo Indovellico votum…*.
- **ENDOVELECO / Endoveleco**: reflected in Turrecia’s marble altar, *Endoveleco deo sacrum … animo libens votum solvit faciendum curavit*.
- **ENOBOLICO / Enobolico** and other damaged or assimilated forms occur in older corpora, although some readings remain uncertain.

Catarina Gaspar argues that at least part of this variation reflects ordinary Latin phonology and spelling in Lusitania rather than distinct gods. Her linguistic study also finds both nonstandard/Vulgar Latin features and conspicuously literate Latin, consistent with a socially mixed pilgrim population. [Gaspar’s article and corpus summary](https://ojs.lib.unideb.hu/classica/article/view/7931).

The modern Portuguese form is **Endovélico**; Spanish normally uses **Endovélico**; English scholarship alternates **Endovelicus** and **Endovellicus**. The doubled-*l* form most closely represents the dominant epigraphic spelling.

### Titles and cult descriptions

- **deus**, “god”: extremely common—*Deo Endovellico sacrum*, “Sacred to the god Endovelicus.”
- **sanctus**, “holy, venerable, inviolable”: *Endovellico Sancto*.
- **numen**, “divine power/presence”: sometimes grammatically another noun for Endovelicus, not a second named being.
- **praestantissimum et praesentissimum numen**, “most excellent and most immediately present divine power”: used by the Roman equestrian Sextus Cocceius Craterus Honorinus, CIL II 131/IRCP 492. The complete text is catalogued by [DEpHis](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/dephis/item/63872).
- **Avernus** in *ex imperato Averno*, approximately “by an underworld command,” is textually difficult. It may characterize the command or divine sphere; it is not a securely attested personal epithet equivalent to “Endovelicus of Avernus.”

No inscription securely calls him Apollo, Asclepius, Pluto, Serapis, Silvanus, Faunus or Sucellus. Those equations are scholarly *interpretationes*, not ancient bilingual identifications.

### Etymologies

No etymology commands consensus.

1. **“Very good/best” Celtic derivation — [Disputed scholarly reconstruction].** José Leite de Vasconcelos reconstructed *Ande-vellicos*, combining an intensive or superlative first element with Celtic *uello-* “good/better.” This produced the popular gloss “the very good one,” sometimes compared with the Irish Dagda. The exact morphology and sound history are problematic, and the reconstruction is not an attested ancient form.

2. **“Very black” or “blackest” — [Disputed scholarly reconstruction].** The second member has been compared with Iberian personal-name elements such as *Beles/Bels* and Basque *beltz*, “black,” while the first is treated as an intensive. This suited an already-assumed infernal character, making the reasoning partly circular. It does not establish a Basque origin.

3. **Indo-European/Celtic “inside” or elevated-place formation — [Disputed scholarly reconstruction].** Blanca María Prósper analyzes *endo-well-ik-o-* and has compared *endo-* with Indo-European “within” and the second member with roots involving seeing, ruling or elevation. On this reading the name may originally have been topographic, belonging to the place before the personalized deity.

4. **Wolf derivation — [Disputed scholarly reconstruction].** José Cardim Ribeiro compares Endovelicus and Vaelicus with Celtic *uailo-* “wolf,” reconstructing an original wild or lupine place-numen subsequently anthropomorphized. This coheres with his proposed Faunus/Silvanus comparison but is not demonstrated by an inscription.

5. **Phoenician *Bel* or hybrid Celtic-Phoenician analysis — [Discredited early-modern/antiquarian theory].** António da Visitação Freire and successors divided the name into Celtic and Phoenician pieces. Modern linguistics does not accept this mechanical segmentation.

6. **Toponyms Andévalo, Postoloboso and Vellicus — [Disputed].** Similarity to *Andobellicus* at Cerro Andévalo and *Vellicus* at Postoloboso has prompted theories of one divine name or related local hypostases. Similar spelling alone cannot demonstrate identity, cult transfer or common function.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[Secure Roman]** The earliest presently datable named object is generally placed in the first century CE: the marble pedestal dedicated by **M. Vibius Bassus** from São Miguel da Mota. Specialist surveys date several other sculptural and epigraphic objects to the first century, including a small altar bearing pinecone and palm reliefs and an altar with a boar. The great majority of the epigraphic corpus, however, is second–third century CE.

This evidence establishes that:

- a deity named Endovelicus received formal vows in Roman Lusitania;
- the cult used Latin, Roman votive formulas and local marble;
- the sanctuary attracted Roman citizens and Romanized inhabitants;
- the cult was flourishing by the High Empire.

It does **not** establish:

- a pre-Roman date for the built sanctuary;
- the god’s Iron Age appearance or theology;
- a Lusitanian-language form of the name;
- any creation story, genealogy or narrative myth.

Excavators explicitly reported that known cult objects are Greco-Roman in typology and that no unequivocal pre-Roman sanctuary layer had been demonstrated. The detailed 2002 report is [Guerra, Schattner, Fabião and Almeida](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/751423.pdf).

### The corpus

There is no surviving hymn, scripture, epic, indigenous ritual manual or classical literary narrative about Endovelicus. His primary corpus consists of:

- roughly eighty traditionally catalogued Latin inscriptions, now approaching one hundred fragments or monuments after renewed excavation;
- votive altars, plaques, statue bases, small architectural dedications and inscribed bronze;
- human and animal sculpture from the sanctuary;
- archaeological remains and reused blocks;
- post-medieval antiquarian reports recording objects now lost or displaced.

The official Portuguese heritage record conservatively describes “about eighty Latin inscriptions” collected over four centuries. [Património Cultural inventory](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=74089).

Representative inscriptions include:

- **Turrecia:** *Endoveleco deo sacrum … animo libens votum solvit faciendum curavit*—“Sacred to the god Endovelicus … willingly and deservedly discharged the vow and saw that [the monument] was made.” It is a small marble altar found in the 2002 trenches, AE 2004, 705 = AE 2005, 729 = AE 2008, 119. [DEpHis 958](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/DEPHis/item/63856).
- **Petronia Albilla:** *Deo Indovellico votum…*, preserving the initial-*I* variant. [DEpHis 959](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/DEPHis/item/63858).
- **Titus Annius Aper:** *Deo Endovellico sacrum … animo libens votum posuit*, CIL II 5206/IRCP 485. [DEpHis 978](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/DEPHis/item/63894).
- **Arrius Badiolus:** *Deo Endovelico sacrum ad relicticium ex iussu numinis…*, “sacred to the god Endovelicus … by order of the numen,” CIL II 129/IRCP 488. The obscure *ad relicticium* resists confident translation. [DEpHis 975](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/DEPHis/item/63888).
- **Sextus Cocceius Craterus Honorinus:** dedication to the god as *praestantissimum et praesentissimum numen*, documenting an equestrian devotee.
- **Gaius Julius Novatus:** a vow *pro salute Vivenniae Venustae*, “for the health/safety of Vivennia Venusta,” CIL II 134. This is the clearest direct basis for the healing interpretation.
- Other formulas include *ex visu* (“following a vision”), *iussu ipsius* (“by his own command”), *ex iussu numinis* (“by command of the numen”), *ex responso* (“in accordance with a response”) and the difficult *ex imperato Averno*. These support divine communication; they do not by themselves prove a staffed oracle or therapeutic incubation ritual.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**Responsive oracle — [Secure Roman].** The command, response and vision formulas show that worshippers understood Endovelicus to communicate. Whether this occurred through sleep, waking vision, priestly oracle, lot or private interpretation is usually unstated.

**Healing and protection — [Secure but limited Roman evidence].** The *pro salute* dedications establish petitions for health or well-being. Anatomically unusual votive figures may represent diseased or healed bodies, but diagnoses such as hemiplegia are modern readings. Calling Endovelicus “the Lusitanian Asclepius” goes beyond the inscriptions.

**Chthonic or funerary power — [Probable/contested reconstruction].** Supporting evidence includes *Averno*, the sanctuary’s dramatic liminal landscape, apparent concern with souls in later scholarship, and possible reuse of funerary imagery. Against certainty: no inscription calls him *inferus*, Pluto or lord of the dead; *Averno* may be corrupt or differently construed.

**Sylvan/wild-nature deity — [Disputed but evidence-based reconstruction].** Pinecone, palm, fruits, birds and suids, combined with the rural sanctuary, led Cardim Ribeiro to propose an indigenous deity reinterpreted through Faunus/Silvanus. He expressly did not argue that Endovelicus was simply Silvanus under another name. [Cardim Ribeiro’s comparative study](https://ifc.dpz.es/recursos/publicaciones/26/22/31cardim.pdf).

### Images and symbols

The sanctuary sculpture is Roman in technique and visual language. Proposed images of Endovelicus include several mature bearded male heads and nude or mantled male torsos. Their identification depends upon provenance and stylistic reconstruction; none bears an attached label naming the figure. One bearded head, Museu Nacional de Arqueologia inventory 988.3.168, became the god’s standard modern “face,” but should be called an attributed image, not a certain portrait.

The 2002 excavators found six major sculptures deposited together beneath/near the ruined chapel floor:

1. a caryatid;
2. a clothed female;
3. a female offering-bearer;
4. a toga-clad man;
5. a male torso with a mantle over the shoulder;
6. a marble boar.

The boar is anatomically secure; a second suid may be a domestic pig or boar. Neither proves that Endovelicus himself was boar-shaped. The animals could represent sacrifice, offerings, divine attributes or a wider sanctuary program. The archaeological description and illustrations appear in the [2002 excavation report](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/751423.pdf).

Other attested motifs include:

- pinecone and palm on the sides of a first-century altar;
- birds and fruit carried by an offering figure;
- a dagger and an uncertain animal—variously bird, dog or caprid—on a damaged votive;
- human worshippers, including men, women and children;
- architectural miniatures or *aediculae*.

No ancient source securely assigns him a color, number, weekday, planet, weapon or fixed festival date. Modern lists doing so are inventions unless explicitly presented as new ritual choices.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

No parentage, birth, consort, offspring, sibling, mount, enemy or pantheon hierarchy is attested. There is no defensible genealogical tree.

### Every documented or claimed divine interaction/equation

- **Silvanus — comparative reinterpretation, not a narrative interaction.** Similarities in titles, votive formulas, family worship, social profile, fruits and suids led Cardim Ribeiro in 2005 to propose a Roman-period local *interpretatio* through Silvanus. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction].**
- **Faunus — comparative reinterpretation.** Prophecy and wilderness provide the comparison; there is no ancient text in which the two meet or are jointly named. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction].**
- **Asclepius/Aesculapius — functional equation.** Health petitions, visions and the bearded iconographic type prompted the “Lusitanian Asclepius” theory. The expected Asclepian complex of snake, dog and cock is not securely established, and no bilingual dedication equates them. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction].**
- **Apollo — occasional functional comparison through healing/oracle.** No ancient equation or joint dedication. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction].**
- **Pluto/Hades — chthonic comparison.** Based on infernal interpretations, not an ancient identification. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction].**
- **Serapis — healing/chthonic/iconographic comparison.** No secure shared inscription or episode. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction].**
- **Sucellus — Celtic comparative hypothesis.** No Sucellus dedication at São Miguel da Mota and no interaction. **[Speculative modern comparison].**
- **Vaelicus, Vellicus and Andobellicus — possible linguistic or regional relations.** Identity is disputed and no narrative relation is recorded. **[Disputed reconstruction].**
- **Saint Michael the Archangel — sacred-site succession, not a surviving pagan myth.** A chapel dedicated to Michael eventually occupied the hilltop and reused Roman material. The physical succession is real; transfer of Endovelicus’s healing, soul-guiding or underworld functions to Michael is interpretive and cannot be documented as a continuous popular belief. **[Medieval succession; modern continuity theory].**
- **Ataecina — no documented interaction.** Although Ataecina was worshipped elsewhere in Lusitania and is also frequently classified as chthonic, no text or image makes her Endovelicus’s consort, relative or companion.
- **Jupiter and Cupid — antiquarian identifications.** Early-modern writers sometimes called the local god “Jupiter Endovellicus” or “Cupido Endovellicus.” These are learned misidentifications, not ancient interactions.

**Finding:** no securely attested scene contains Endovelicus interacting with another named deity. No divine combat, marriage, seduction, judgment, alliance or joint epiphany survives.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

There are none in the surviving ancient record.

The nearest thing to a recoverable cult narrative is a repeated ritual sequence:

1. a worshipper made a vow, sometimes for another person’s health;
2. the god was believed to issue a command, response or vision;
3. the worshipper fulfilled the vow by erecting an altar, statue, plaque or other gift;
4. the monument recorded willing fulfillment—*votum solvit libens merito* or a related formula.

This is ritual testimony, not a mythic cycle. Claims that Endovelicus journeyed through an underworld, fought another deity, married Ataecina, died and returned seasonally, or literally assumed boar form are **[Modern invention or unsupported synthesis]**.

An early-modern legend reported that Christians destroyed a fine marble “idol of Cupid,” inserted part of it into the Church of São Miguel and that local boys later broke it. This preserves valuable testimony about the discovery and destruction of sculpture, but its identification of the image as Cupid and its implied eyewitness chain are antiquarian tradition, not ancient evidence.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### São Miguel da Mota

**[Secure Roman]** The principal and only securely attributable cult center stood on the eastern slope of the São Miguel da Mota ridge, about four to five kilometres from Terena. Surface remains, imported granite blocks, local marbles, votive inscriptions, sculpture and geophysical anomalies indicate a substantial terraced sanctuary. The 2002 team recognized a symmetrical succession of platforms and ramps comparable to Roman terrace sanctuaries in Italy and at Munigua. They did not locate a conventional temple cella with certainty.

The hill controlled a broad landscape above the Lucefécit stream. Cardim Ribeiro proposed that the stream continued the *Atrum flumen*, “Black River,” of the Antonine Itinerary and served as both sacred boundary and infernal landscape marker. The linguistic/topographic chain is ingenious but remains disputed; an Edinburgh study summarizes the argument and its limitations. [Landscape discussion](https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/1c49df80-f094-4d2c-a321-a4b94e72190c/content).

### Rocha da Mina

The nearby rock-cut complex called Rocha da Mina has steps, basins and terraces and is sometimes described as Endovelicus’s pre-Roman sanctuary, supposedly replaced by São Miguel da Mota in the first century CE.

**Assessment:** **[Disputed archaeological reconstruction].** Rocha da Mina is a genuine ritual site, but no inscription there names Endovelicus. A direct institutional transfer from Rocha da Mina to São Miguel da Mota is possible, not demonstrated. Reports that worshippers crossed the stream for ritual purification before approaching it are reconstructions, not recorded ancient liturgy.

### Worshippers

Names and titles document:

- Roman citizens and highly Romanized provincials;
- women—about one third of named dedicants in Cardim Ribeiro’s count;
- enslaved and freed persons;
- soldiers;
- families making intergenerational or third-person vows;
- at least one Roman equestrian;
- people connected with Emerita Augusta, including a member of the Papiria voting tribe.

Indigenous names are a minority. This is evidence for broad Roman-period participation, not proof that the cult was restricted to an ethnic “Lusitanian” population.

### Practices

**Securely or strongly attested:**

- vows and fulfillment of vows;
- dedication of marble altars, statue bases, human images and animal images;
- requests *pro salute*;
- divine command, response and vision;
- family petitions;
- probable offerings of fruit, birds and suids;
- costly patronage of sculpture and architecture.

**Probable but not proved:**

- sacrifice of pigs or boars: suid images and broader Roman practice make it likely, but no sacrificial register or securely identified altar deposit specifies the rite;
- priestly mediation: a sanctuary of this scale probably had attendants, but no securely identified priestly title or organization survives;
- dream incubation: *ex visu* and command formulas permit it, but do not prove that pilgrims slept in a designated incubation building;
- formal oracle: response formulas imply divination, but its personnel and method are unknown.

**Not documented:**

- human sacrifice;
- temple prostitution;
- sacred kingship;
- cult violence;
- compulsory conversion to the cult;
- a fixed annual festival;
- secret initiatory grades.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Possible indigenous formation: before the first century CE

**[Probable but unattested].** The non-Latin divine name most likely originated in a local language and therefore before or during Roman acculturation. No pre-Roman text names him, and excavation has not securely demonstrated an Iron Age Endovelicus sanctuary at São Miguel da Mota. “Lusitanian god” is defensible; “documented Iron Age god” is not.

### Monumental Roman establishment: first century CE

The earliest pedestal and sculptural pieces indicate an operating sanctuary in the first century. Roman architecture, Latin epigraphy and classical figure types were integral to the surviving form of the cult, not a superficial overlay on recoverable indigenous rites.

### Peak: second–third centuries CE

Most inscriptions belong to these centuries. The extraordinary density of dedications to one local god, the expensive marble program, the social range of dedicants and the sanctuary terraces justify describing this as the cult’s documented peak. Endovelicus was regionally important, but claims that his worship spread “through most of the Roman Empire” are unsupported; nearly all secure evidence clusters at São Miguel da Mota.

### Late antiquity: fourth century and after

Coins and late material show continued activity on the hill into late antiquity, but they do not necessarily prove continuing Endovelicus worship. No firmly dated inscription demonstrates the cult in the fifth century. Statements that it “survived until the fifth century” are conventional estimates, not a recorded suppression date.

There is no surviving decree, martyr narrative or episcopal account describing the forced closure of Endovelicus’s sanctuary. General imperial prohibitions on sacrifice provide context but not a local event history.

### Christian reuse and Saint Michael

A Christian chapel eventually stood on the crest and incorporated Roman stones. However:

- the Roman sanctuary was primarily on the eastern slope;
- excavation found no direct chapel-over-temple superposition;
- the chapel’s foundation date remains uncertain;
- proposed Visigothic architectural pieces are suggestive but not decisive;
- the visible chapel was medieval or later and underwent rebuilding.

Thus “Endovelicus survived as Saint Michael” is too strong. What is secure is **site reuse and architectural spoliation**; what is plausible is deliberate Christian resacralization; what cannot be demonstrated is continuous ritual, population memory or direct transfer of a specific feast. The archaeological caution is summarized in the [Edinburgh landscape study](https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/1c49df80-f094-4d2c-a321-a4b94e72190c/content).

### Loss, dispersal and rediscovery

By the sixteenth century ancient marble and inscriptions were being removed. The dukes of Braganza ordered several inscribed stones transported to the Augustinian church at Vila Viçosa. Antiquarians including André de Resende, Bernardo de Brito and later Thomas Reinesius discussed the god.

The ruined chapel and sanctuary suffered systematic stone reuse. Gabriel Pereira surveyed the remains in 1889. José Leite de Vasconcelos dismantled much of the already-ruined chapel in 1890 while recovering inscriptions and sculpture for the institution that became the Museu Nacional de Arqueologia. Modern excavation resumed in 2002 under the University of Lisbon and German Archaeological Institute.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, museums and heritage

The Museu Nacional de Arqueologia in Lisbon preserves the principal Endovelicus collection. A bearded marble head has become its emblematic image, although the attribution is inferential. The site was classified as an *Imóvel de Interesse Público* in 1997.

Municipal programs such as *Terras do Endovélico*, conferences, heritage routes, exhibitions and the *Cadernos do Endovélico* have recast the god as a focus of Alandroal’s cultural identity. This is heritage-making based on genuine archaeology, but the coherent modern public persona—bearded healing-oracle god with a boar—is a reconstruction from disparate remains.

### National and political reception

From the nineteenth century onward Endovelicus could be presented as an especially “Portuguese” or “Lusitanian” god and therefore as evidence of deep national ancestry. A modern hard-rock/nationalist band and local cultural publicity have used the name. The evidence supports a regional Roman-Lusitanian cult, not an ancient unified Portuguese national religion.

No reliable evidence encountered establishes a substantial organized extremist movement centered on Endovelicus. Broad claims of fascist or white-nationalist appropriation should therefore not be transferred from other European paganisms without documentation.

### Neopaganism

Small twenty-first-century Iberian neopagan groups worship Endovelicus and construct rituals from archaeology, comparative Celtic religion and personal revelation. Brunella Lago Velloso’s 2020 master’s thesis specifically studies how such groups appropriate archaeological discourse in forming Endovelicus cults and “Celtic” identities. [University of Lisbon thesis record](https://repositorio.ul.pt/entities/publication/4a531907-ca5d-47ba-ba64-56ae63f6f444).

Modern devotional practices include reconstructed offerings, solstice observances, prayers, boar symbolism and sometimes pairings with Ataecina. These are **new religious traditions**, not continuously transmitted ancient rites. Their modernity does not make them insincere; it determines their historical classification.

### Esoteric and occult claims

No substantial Renaissance hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn or early grimoire tradition centered on Endovelicus has been documented. Claims that ancient priests taught a preserved initiatory mystery, necromantic technique or exact dream ritual are unsupported. Contemporary Rosicrucian or esoteric ceremonies reported at the hill are modern appropriations.

### Popular media

Endovelicus remains far less reshaped by international film, games or comics than Greco-Roman gods. Portuguese historical fiction, blogs, music and tourism have had greater impact. Online images often combine the attributed Roman head, boars, Celtic knotwork and modern fantasy design; only the first two derive from the sanctuary, and even the head’s identification is uncertain.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Antiquarian phase

- **Sixteenth century:** inscriptions removed to Vila Viçosa and recorded by Portuguese antiquarians.
- **Thomas Reinesius, 1637:** *De deo Endovellico ex inscriptionibus in Lusitania repertis*, an early inscription-based study.
- **Miguel Pérez Pastor, 1760:** *Disertación sobre el Dios Endovélico*, part of Enlightenment-era cataloguing of supposedly indigenous Spanish gods.
- Early antiquarians proposed Carthaginian origins, Cupid, Jupiter and hybrid Phoenician etymologies. These theories are historically important but obsolete.

### Archaeological and comparative phase

- **Gabriel Pereira, 1889:** surveyed São Miguel da Mota.
- **José Leite de Vasconcelos, 1890–1905:** recovered and synthesized the corpus in *Religiões da Lusitânia* II. He established the site’s importance and influential “very good” Celtic etymology, but assumed archaeological continuities no longer demonstrable.
- **Scarlat Lambrino, 1951:** emphasized an infernal, prophetic and healing god and reconstructed iconography.
- **José María Blázquez:** incorporated Endovelicus into broad accounts of pre-Roman Iberian religion.
- **José d’Encarnação, IRCP 1984 and later work:** provided the essential epigraphic corpus and social analysis. His summary dates most inscriptions to the second and third centuries and stresses worshippers of many classes. [“Endovélico—400 anos depois”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277041700_Endovelico_-_400_anos_depois).
- **Vasco de Souza and José Luís de Matos:** catalogued the Roman sculpture.
- **Cardim Ribeiro:** reconstructed a sylvan, prophetic figure reinterpreted through Faunus/Silvanus and developed the wolf and Black River hypotheses.
- **Prósper and other palaeolinguists:** reopened the etymology using Indo-European and Palaeohispanic linguistics.
- **Guerra, Schattner, Fabião and Almeida, from 2002:** restored archaeological context and showed the dangers of treating old assumptions as stratigraphic facts.
- **Gaspar, 2019/2020:** analyzed Latin variation in the votive corpus.

### Principal disputes

1. **Pre-Roman cult versus Roman creation.** An indigenous name strongly suggests local roots; every securely contextualized cult object is Roman. The safest formulation is an indigenous-named god whose recoverable cult is Roman provincial.

2. **Temple or open/terraced sanctuary.** The complex is certainly cultic, but its exact built plan remains incomplete. A naturalistic sacred precinct and monumental terraces need not be mutually exclusive.

3. **Rocha da Mina as predecessor.** Geographically plausible, epigraphically unproved.

4. **Healing god.** Supported by *pro salute* and possible anatomical votives, but later “physician god” summaries often overstate the evidence.

5. **Oracle and incubation.** Commands, responses and visions are secure; a formal dream-oracle institution is reconstructed.

6. **Underworld god.** Plausible from *Avernus*, funerary/salvific interpretations and landscape arguments, but not explicitly defined in a theology.

7. **Faunus/Silvanus equation.** A sophisticated Roman *interpretatio* hypothesis, not an epigraphically stated identity.

8. **Boar.** Secure sanctuary iconography; uncertain whether victim, attribute, manifestation or independent offering.

9. **Saint Michael continuity.** Secure later Christian presence and reuse; no proof of unbroken worship or one-to-one substitution.

10. **Corpus size.** “About eighty,” “ninety-odd” and “near one hundred” reflect different counting rules: complete inscriptions only, fragments, lost antiquarian copies, and discoveries after IRCP.

### State of the evidence

Most portable monuments are in Lisbon’s Museu Nacional de Arqueologia; others were historically embedded at Terena, Vila Viçosa or nearby structures, and some are lost. The essential reference systems are CIL II, IRCP, AE, HEp/HEpOL, EDH, EDCS, Trismegistos and the modern DEpHis catalogue. DEpHis currently exposes a representative linked group of twenty-one Endovelicus records rather than the entire historical corpus. [DEpHis theonym index](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/DEPHis/item/63853).

## 10. Chronology

- **Before the first century CE:** probable formation of a local divine name; no secure named object or cult layer. **[Reconstruction]**
- **First century CE:** earliest datable Endovelicus pedestal; first-century altars and sanctuary sculpture; probable monumental development of the terraced complex. **[Secure Roman]**
- **Second–third centuries CE:** principal epigraphic and sculptural floruit; health petitions, visions, commands, elite and non-elite dedicants. **[Secure Roman]**
- **Third–fourth centuries CE:** some continued activity or occupation; precise terminal date of the cult unknown.
- **Later antiquity/early Middle Ages:** Christianity becomes dominant in Lusitania; no locally documented act of suppression. Possible early Christian architectural material on or near the hill remains disputed.
- **Medieval period:** a chapel dedicated to Saint Michael exists on the crest; its exact foundation date is undetermined.
- **Sixteenth century:** D. Teodósio of Braganza has inscriptions transported to Vila Viçosa; antiquarians record the god. In 1559 substantial ancient marble was reportedly removed for building at Évora, though individual quantities in later accounts require caution.
- **1597:** Bernardo de Brito discusses Endovelicus in *Monarchia Lusitana*.
- **1637:** Reinesius publishes *De deo Endovellico*.
- **Eighteenth century:** geographical and antiquarian accounts repeat stories of Cupid, Carthaginians and Christian destruction.
- **1760:** Pérez Pastor publishes his dissertation.
- **1882:** Padre Espanca writes *O Deus Endovellico dos Celtas do Alentejo*.
- **1889:** Gabriel Pereira publishes and plans the sanctuary.
- **1890:** Leite de Vasconcelos excavates/retrieves material and dismantles much of the ruined chapel.
- **1905:** *Religiões da Lusitânia* II gives the foundational modern synthesis.
- **1951:** Lambrino’s study consolidates the healing-oracular-chthonic interpretation.
- **1984:** d’Encarnação publishes IRCP, cataloguing inscriptions 483–559.
- **1997:** São Miguel da Mota is classified as an *Imóvel de Interesse Público*.
- **2002:** renewed University of Lisbon/German Archaeological Institute excavation discovers new inscriptions, statuary and the boar and identifies the terrace organization.
- **2003–2005:** excavation report and Cardim Ribeiro’s Faunus/Silvanus analysis reshape debate.
- **2010s:** municipal *Terras do Endovélico* heritage programs and scholarly conferences expand public reception.
- **2019/2020:** Gaspar publishes the linguistic study; Velloso documents modern Iberian neopagan appropriations.
- **Present:** the god is an archaeological, municipal-heritage and small-scale neopagan figure. There is no evidence for an institutionally continuous cult from antiquity.

## 11. Sources

The dossier’s conclusions rest principally on the Latin inscriptions and their modern corpora, the 2002 excavation report, specialist studies by d’Encarnação and Cardim Ribeiro, the Portuguese heritage inventory, Gaspar’s linguistic analysis and Velloso’s study of modern revival. General encyclopedic claims were excluded where they exceeded those sources—especially claims of a securely pre-Roman temple, empire-wide diffusion, fifth-century survival, an Alan-built chapel, fixed equivalence with Asclepius/Pluto/Serapis, or continuous survival under Saint Michael.
