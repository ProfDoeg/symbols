# The Jentilak: Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective race of primordial, non-Christian beings—usually giants or exceptionally strong “old people,” sometimes simply pagans or wild mountain-dwellers. They are **not securely attested as gods**, and no source documents worship directed to them.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Basque word *jentil* is attested in print from **1545**, deriving through Romance *gentil* from Latin *gentīlis*, but that occurrence does not securely attest the mythological giants. The earliest presently verifiable, source-specific records of the developed folklore are ethnographic: traditions collected around Aralar and Ataun in the early twentieth century, including José Miguel de Barandiarán’s field record at the Jentilarri dolmen in **August 1917**, followed by Telesforo de Aranzadi’s *Los gentiles del Aralar* (**1919**) and Barandiarán’s publications. Everything claimed for a prehistoric Jentil religion predates the documentation by millennia and is reconstruction, not attested continuity. [Folklore; securely documented modern collection.]

---

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early lexical]** An early written word or name, without necessarily attesting the developed being.
- **[Folklore]** Oral tradition documented by a collector; its date is the date of documentation unless earlier transmission can be independently demonstrated.
- **[Regional variant]** A localized version not automatically transferable to the entire Basque-speaking area.
- **[Christian legendary elaboration]** Folklore whose narrative presupposes Christianity.
- **[Learned systematization]** A collector’s or scholar’s arrangement, synthesis, or interpretation of separate traditions.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical or comparative hypothesis, not a primary tradition.
- **[Modern reinvention]** A recent narrative, image, ritual, or identification presented more definitively than the older evidence allows.
- **[Not attested]** No relevant primary evidence located.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The being

The Jentilak belong primarily to **Basque folklore**, not to an epigraphically attested pre-Roman pantheon. In collected narratives they are the mighty inhabitants of an age before the present one: cave-dwellers, shepherds, miners, builders, stone-throwers, early farmers and smiths, and sometimes neighbors—peaceful or hostile—of Christians. Their great strength explains dolmens, cromlechs, unusual boulders, caves, bridges, houses, churches, and landscape formations. One group of legends makes their disappearance coincide with a new cloud, star, rain, snow, “small people,” Christianity, or the birth of Christ. [Folklore.]

Antón Erkoreka’s systematic catalogue concluded that several themes of different provenance had accumulated under the name: wild man, giant, pagan, remote mountain-dweller, culture-bringer, megalith-builder and predecessor population. That is a valuable description of the corpus, not proof that all these roles belonged to one prehistoric theology. [Learned systematization.]

### Names and grammar

- **Basque singular:** *jentil*.
- **Basque definite plural:** *jentilak*, literally “the jentils/gentiles.” English frequently treats *Jentilak* as if it were a singular proper name; that is grammatically misleading.
- **Older or dialectal spellings:** *jentillak*, *gentilak*; Spanish scholarship generally uses *gentiles* or retains *jentilak*.
- **Toponymic stem:** *jentil-*.
- **Related ordinary meanings:** pagan, idolater, non-Christian or—in biblical translation—Gentile/non-Jew. The mythological dictionary sense is “member of a race of giants to whom megalithic monuments are attributed.” [Early lexical; later lexicography.]

The etymology is not securely prehistoric. Trask’s etymological dictionary dates *jentil* to 1545 and derives it from Romance *gentil*, ultimately Latin *gentīlis*. Basque biblical translation history likewise shows *jentil* competing with *pagano*, *arrotz* and later *fedegabe* for “Gentile/pagan.” The resemblance between the beings and Christian “gentiles” is therefore historically substantive, not accidental. Claims deriving the name from an otherwise unattested prehistoric Basque ethnonym are unsupported. [Early lexical; contested-material finding.]

### Toponymic and descriptive forms

- **Jentilarri / Jentilharria** — “stone of the jentil(s)”; particularly the Aralar dolmen associated with their burial.
- **Jentiletxe / Jentiletxea** — “house of the jentil(s),” commonly a dolmen, cave, or megalithic place.
- **Jentilzulo** — “jentil-hole/cave.”
- **Jentilkoba** — “jentil cave.”
- **Jentilbaratza** — “garden/enclosure of the jentils,” applied to cromlechs and other ancient enclosures.
- **Jentilen Sukaldea** — “the jentils’ kitchen,” the artificial cave at Urdiain.
- **Jentilen Laihoa** — “the jentils’ window,” at Urdiain.
- **Jentilzubi** — “bridge of the jentils.”
- **Jentillen Paso-lekua** — “place of the jentils’ passage,” at Aitzabal-Arantzazu.
- **Jentillatxa / Zentellatxe**, **Jentilko Lezia**, **Jentilen Txabola** and numerous local cave or rock names. [Folklore/toponymy documented chiefly in the twentieth century.]

A “Jentil place-name” shows that speakers interpreted a place through this category; it does not date the associated story to the construction of a Neolithic monument.

### Named individuals

The overwhelming majority are anonymous. Erkoreka’s catalogue records:

- **Kurri-Kurri**, a *mairu* or jentil of Elduain.
- **Zirpi-Zarba**, a jentil of Ai-Iturrieta, Ataun.
- **Zirpi-Zirbi**, a female jentil of the same place, companion of Zirpi-Zarba.
- An unnamed blind or nearly blind **ancient chief/sage**, central to the ending legends.
- An unnamed survivor later identified by Barandiarán with **Olentzero**. [Regional folklore; learned identification.]

### Jentilak, Mairuak and other giants

*Mairuak* (“Moors”) can occupy the same roles: former inhabitants, cave-dwellers and builders of megaliths. Some local figures are expressly called both *jentil* and *mairu*. Erkoreka’s decision that Jentilak, Mairuak, Basajaunak, Errolan and related giants could be treated as manifestations of one giant complex is comparative classification, not evidence that tradition everywhere considered them identical.

Similarly, the attribution of thrown boulders shifts locally among Jentilak, **Samson**, **Roland/Errolan**, **Sugaar**, *mairuak* and other strong figures. These are transferable folkloric motifs, not necessarily theological syncretisms.

### Iberian classification

The item-specific description “Iberian (Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician, pre-Roman) pantheon” is too broad for the evidence.

- **Basque:** Jentilak are well documented in modern folklore.
- **Asturian-Cantabrian:** giants, *moros/moras*, enchanted builders and stone-carriers occur, but they are not thereby Jentilak.
- **Galician:** *mouros/mouras* explain castros, dolmens, caves and treasure places; comparative similarities are strong, identity is not documented.
- **Pre-Roman Iberia:** no Latin inscription, indigenous inscription, sanctuary, classical text or securely ancient image names the Jentilak. Endovelicus, Ataecina and other epigraphic deities belong to a different evidentiary corpus. [Absence of evidence.]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **1545: lexical *jentil*.** The word is attested, but the surviving lexical evidence cited by Trask gives the meaning “kind/gentle,” while ecclesiastical usage supplied “Gentile/pagan.” It cannot alone prove the folklore complex. [Early lexical.]

2. **August 1917: Jentilarri field account.** While investigating the double-chambered Jentilarri dolmen in Aralar, Barandiarán heard an old shepherd say that the Jentilak had been overtaken by a mysterious cloud and crowded beneath the monument, where they were buried. This version contains no explicit Christ, Kixmi or Olentzero. [Securely documented folklore.]

3. **1919: Telesforo de Aranzadi, *Los gentiles del Aralar*.** Erkoreka lists this Bilbao publication as an early printed study devoted to the subject. [Published folklore/scholarship.]

4. **1920s onward: Barandiarán and *Eusko-Folklore*.** Barandiarán established the systematic field archive from which many later summaries derive. His 1927 article on the Jentiletxetu caves and his collected works preserve localized narratives and place associations. [Folklore corpus.]

### Principal corpus

- **José Miguel de Barandiarán, *Obras completas* and *Mitología vasca*.** These supply the largest canonical-looking synthesis: residences, feats, culture-bringing, human contacts and ending legends. They must be read as twentieth-century ethnography and arrangement, not ancient scripture.
- **Anuario de Eusko-Folklore / Eusko-Folklore: Materiales y Cuestionarios.** Founded under Barandiarán’s direction in 1921; crucial because it preserves locality, informant context and variant rather than only a composite myth.
- **Resurrección María de Azkue, *Euskalerriaren Yakintza* (1935–1947; later editions).** Four-volume collection of Basque customs, beliefs, stories, sayings, prayers and folk knowledge. It is a primary ethnographic collection for modern oral tradition, not evidence that each item is prehistoric.
- **José María Satrústegui, “La cueva artificial Jentilen-Sukaldea de Urdiain” (1973) and “El ojo Jentilen Laihoa de Urdiain” (1974).** Local archaeological-folkloric studies.
- **Antón Erkoreka, “Los gentiles (El mito de los gigantes en el País Vasco)” (1974–75).** A 58-page analytical catalogue with bibliography, geographic distribution, topographic indexes and provenance codes. It is especially useful for keeping local variants distinct.
- **Julio Caro Baroja.** His work on Basque religion, festival and history helped distinguish historical cultural processes from timeless “pagan survival.”
- **Juan Inazio Hartsuaga.** Structural and comparative interpretations of the “end of the Jentilak” treat the legends as transformations of older mythic patterns. These interpretations are hypotheses, not direct ancient witnesses.

There is no ancient hymn, liturgy, scripture, priestly commentary, magical papyrus or medieval manuscript corpus devoted to the Jentilak. Oral narratives collected in the modern period are the primary corpus.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic actions

The Jentilak have no securely attested divine “domain.” Their recurrent activities are:

- **Superhuman strength:** carrying immense loads, moving slabs, throwing boulders between mountains, and raising buildings. [Folklore.]
- **Megalith building:** dolmens called *Jentiletxe* or *Jentilarri* and cromlechs called *Jentilbaratza*. [Folklore/toponymy.]
- **Church building:** Ataun, Ibarrangelua, Markina-Jemein, Muxika, Elgeta, Oñati, Opakua, Oiartzun, Urdiain and various hermitages are attributed to them. One Zumarraga account has a jentil throw the stone that determines the church site. This Christian architecture is decisive evidence that the corpus is not simply a fossilized Neolithic religion. [Christian legendary elaboration.]
- **Culture possession or transfer:** wheat cultivation, milling, mining, metallurgy, stone-working and sometimes the saw or other technologies. [Folklore; motif varies among Jentilak and Basajaun.]
- **Pastoralism:** the Aralar ending narrative explicitly calls them transhumant shepherds. [Regional variant.]
- **Prediction:** their blind elder reads a cloud, star, first rain or snow as the end of their age. [Folklore.]
- **Relations with farms:** visiting for milk, assisting with agricultural work, borrowing tools or food, and trading services. [Regional folklore.]

### Form

They are usually imagined as immense, exceptionally strong people. Some accounts emphasize wildness, remoteness or skins as clothing; others make them physically admirable, tall and well formed. Both sexes and children occur. At Jentilarri the crowd includes “men, women and children.” A female Jentil at Santimamiñe gives birth and employs a neighboring human woman as midwife. [Folklore.]

Hairiness, cyclopean form and uniform mountain-monster imagery are common in modern illustrations but not stable across the collected corpus. The one-eyed monster belongs more properly to Tartalo/Torto traditions.

### Iconography

**No securely ancient image of a Jentil is known.** No diagnostic weapon, costume, sacred animal, color, celestial body, number or canonical emblem is attested.

Modern iconography uses:

- enormous human bodies;
- stones or dolmen slabs;
- pastoral or rough mountain clothing;
- caves and megaliths;
- the luminous cloud or star;
- sometimes Olentzero’s charcoal-burner costume.

These are illustrations of collected narratives, not ancient cult images.

### Powers and limits

Their strength is prodigious but not omnipotent. They can be deceived by ordinary humans, fail to understand ordinary concepts such as resting, and cannot survive the transition to the new age. The blind sage alone interprets the portent. Some die, some retreat underground, some disperse, and a few survive into human society. Contradictory endings are integral to the corpus.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

No stable creation, parentage or divine genealogy is attested. Jentil families include fathers, daughters, spouses, companions and children, but not a pantheon-style descent from named gods. Their unnamed elder functions as chief or sage, not creator.

No canonical consort, offspring, sibling deity, mount or divine hierarchy is documented. [Absence of evidence.]

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or mythic being

Because most catalogue references concern parallel attribution rather than a shared scene, the distinction is essential.

- **Jesus Christ / Kixmi —** The blind elder identifies a luminous cloud or star as announcing Kixmi’s birth and the destruction of the Jentil age; a survivor announces the birth in the valley. Source: Ataun/Aralar traditions collected by Barandiarán in the early twentieth century and synthesized in his works; catalogued by Erkoreka, 1974–75. **[Christian legendary elaboration; direct interaction is recognition at a distance, not a meeting.]**

- **Olentzero —** Barandiarán identifies the surviving announcer as the figure known in some regions as Olentzero. The recorded 1917 shepherd’s version merely buries the Jentilak and does not name Olentzero. The linkage is therefore source-dependent, not universal. **[Folklore plus learned systematization; later popular canon.]**

- **Sugaar —** A stone-throwing feat attributed to a jentil elsewhere is attributed at Arbotxota, Ataun, to Sugaar. No source in Erkoreka’s catalogue places Sugaar and a Jentil together in one episode. **[Parallel regional attribution; no documented interaction.]**

- **Mari / the Lady of Anboto —** Some broader Basque-mythology presentations place both within one mythic world, but no specific Jentil–Mari encounter has been established in the consulted catalogue. **[No documented interaction.]**

- **Basajaun —** Jentilak and Basajaun share giant, mountain-dwelling and culture-bringer motifs. Erkoreka grouped them comparatively, but a specific joint scene was not established. **[Learned comparison; no documented interaction.]**

- **Mairuak —** Kurri-Kurri and Zirri-Mirri can be called either *mairu* or jentil; buildings and megaliths likewise receive alternative attribution. This is overlapping classification, not necessarily an interaction between two races. **[Regional synonymy/variant.]**

- **Tartalo/Torto —** Giant and cave-dwelling motifs overlap; no shared episode was located. **[Comparative association only.]**

- **Samson —** Particular boulders attributed in one locality to Jentilak are attributed elsewhere to Samson. **[Christian/Biblical substitution; no shared scene.]**

- **Roland/Errolan —** Rock-throwing and landscape feats shift between Jentilak and Roland. Later claims that Jentilak personally defeated Roland at Roncevaux are poorly sourced in comparison with the main ethnographic catalogue and should not be treated as early canon without a precise local text. **[Parallel attribution; contested modern retelling.]**

- **Saint Martin / Martin Txiki —** Culture-theft narratives are often told about Martin Txiki obtaining technology from older beings, especially Basajaun. The consulted Jentil catalogue records Saint Martin among related material, but a universal Jentil version cannot be assumed. **[Regional folklore; motif migration.]**

- **Saint Michael, Saint John, Saint Marina, Saint Engracia, the Virgin and other saints —** Jentilak are said to have built churches or hermitages dedicated to them. This constitutes participation in the saint’s sacred architecture, not a narrated face-to-face encounter. **[Christian legendary elaboration.]**

- **Lamiak —** Childbirth, theft, inability to leave a cave after taking food, and silent-visitors stories have parallel Lamia versions. The sources explicitly call them analogous stories; they do not document the beings meeting. **[Parallel folklore; no interaction.]**

- **Devil —** Sites or construction feats may shift between Jentil and Devil attribution. No stable joint episode was established. **[Christian substitution; no interaction.]**

Thus, the only securely documented named supernatural relationship central to the Jentil cycle is with **Christ/Kixmi**, mediated through a portent. Olentzero is the disputed survivor-identification.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The builders of the megaliths

Jentilak transport stones of otherwise inexplicable size and build dolmens, cromlechs, caves, enclosures and bridges. A woman may carry an enormous slab on her head while carrying a child; a giant may throw a rock from a distant mountain. The names *Jentiletxe*, *Jentilarri* and *Jentilbaratza* embody this explanatory folklore. [Folklore.]

The actual monuments are Neolithic or Bronze/Iron Age constructions whose archaeological dates long precede the recordings. The legend explains ruins after their makers and purposes had been forgotten. It does not establish that the builders called themselves Jentilak.

Closely comparable explanations use *Mairuak* in Basque areas and *mouros/mouras* in Galicia. Alonso Romero’s comparative study documents female *mouras* carrying megalithic slabs while spinning. Similar tales occur elsewhere in western Europe. This breadth weakens attempts to treat the motif as proof of one ethnic memory extending intact from the Neolithic. [Modern comparative scholarship.]

### B. Stone-throwers between mountains

The Jentilak throw stones for sport, strength, warfare or construction. Boulders that fall short remain as landscape markers. The motif also attaches locally to Samson, Roland, Sugaar, *mairuak* and unnamed giants. [Regional variants.]

The motif supplies an etiology for erratics, conspicuous rocks and building stones. No corresponding archaeological evidence identifies the stones as projectiles, and no fixed ritual accompanied them.

### C. Masters of wheat, iron and crafts

Some traditions call the Jentilak the first cultivators of wheat, smiths, miners or millers. They possess skills newly arrived humans or Christians lack. In other Basque tales these roles belong to Basajaun, from whom Martin Txiki steals technological knowledge. [Folklore; variant attribution.]

This complex makes the “old people” culturally superior as well as physically stronger. It should not be converted into a historical claim that one identifiable prehistoric population introduced every listed technology.

### D. Relations with human farms

The relationship is not uniformly hostile. A female Jentil helps at a human farm; human neighbors care for the last old Jentil at Urdiain; Jentilak visit houses for milk; a human midwife assists a Jentil birth and is rewarded with bread. Other accounts feature theft, coercion or fighting between Jentilak and Christians. [Regional folklore.]

The Santimamiñe childbirth story has close Lamia analogues. A human takes a piece of the cave-dwellers’ white bread and becomes unable to rise until she returns it; the Jentil family then freely gives her a whole loaf. This is a widespread “fairy food/taboo/reward” narrative structure, localized as Jentil folklore.

### E. The end of the Jentilak

This is the principal cycle, but no single form is original or universal.

#### The 1917 Jentilarri form

At Argaintxabaleta the Jentilak see a mysterious cloud approaching from the north. They flee through Intzensao to Arraztaran and crowd beneath the Jentilarri dolmen, which buries all the men, women and children. Barandiarán recorded this from an old shepherd while excavating the dolmen in August 1917. The recorded form, as reproduced by Erkoreka, does **not** explicitly mention Christ, Kixmi or Olentzero. [Securely documented folklore.]

#### Kixmi form

In a fuller Christian version the Jentilak see a luminous cloud from the east. Their blind elder is brought out to interpret it. He announces: “Kixmi has been born, and the end of our race has come,” asks to be thrown over the precipice, and dies. The others flee west and bury themselves beneath Jentilarri. A survivor descends to announce Christ’s birth. [Christian legendary elaboration.]

*Kixmi* is glossed by the collected tradition as “monkey,” a contemptuous Jentil name for Christ. The gloss is part of the recorded legend; a securely demonstrated historical etymology for *Kixmi* was not established in the consulted linguistic material.

#### Star form

At Leizadi a beautiful star terrifies the Jentilak. They pry open the nearly blind elder’s eyelids with an oven shovel or levers. He sees the star, announces Kixmi, and asks to be thrown down the rocks. Christianity spreads and the Jentilak disperse. At Arano, the star-reader says, *Gue denbora juan duk*—“our time has gone.” [Regional variants.]

#### First cloud, rain or snow

At Ernaga the elder smells something unprecedented; the appearance of a cloud, rain or a new people signals extinction. At Segura a 400-year-old elder sees the first cloud. At Orozko a sea-mist announces Christianity. At Iturbei the first snow means the *pilistriak*, Christians, are coming. At Cerain the portent may be interpreted merely as a vessel bringing water from the sea. [Regional variants.]

These versions show that “Christ’s star” is not the sole form. The deeper narrative unit may be the arrival of a novel atmospheric phenomenon and replacement population; precisely how old that unit is cannot be dated from the late documentation.

#### “Small dog-like people”

At Urdiain the elder reads the stars and laments the replacement of the *jende umanua* by *jente txiki perrua*, conventionally rendered “human people” by “small dog-like people.” This need not be harmonized with the Nativity version. [Regional variant.]

### F. Olentzero as the one who stayed

Barandiarán’s synthesis says that a survivor of the Jentilarri catastrophe descended to announce Kixmi and was known in some areas as Olentzero. The evidence is layered:

- The word or season *Onenzaro* is attested in the seventeenth century in Lope de Isasti’s description of Christmas Eve.
- Local Olentzero customs include logs, effigies, charcoal-burners, songs, processions and burning.
- Barandiarán’s 1917 Jentilarri field text does not name Olentzero.
- Emilio Xabier Dueñas shows that the published trail linking the Jentil survivor, “first Olentzero,” effigy-burning and the Christmas figure changes across Barandiarán’s and Caro Baroja’s works. Some details trace to information received around 1928; later texts sometimes repeat them without the original attribution.

Consequently, “Olentzero was the last Jentil” is a documented twentieth-century synthesis and now a major popular tradition, but not the securely earliest form of either the Jentil ending or Olentzero. [Folklore synthesis; modern popular canon.]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult

No sanctuary, altar, dedicatory inscription, priesthood, festival calendar, sacrifice, prayer, oracle, pilgrimage or theophoric personal name securely belonging to a Jentil cult is known. [Not attested.]

The large catalogue instead depicts beings remembered in stories and place-names. Even churches attributed to them are feats of legendary construction, not Jentil temples.

### Megaliths

Jentilarri and other monuments are real archaeological sites, but archaeological occupation and folklore must be separated:

- the monument may be prehistoric;
- the name may be much later;
- the story was recorded later still;
- excavation of a dolmen does not confirm that giants were believed in when it was built.

Barandiarán’s own research is historically significant because a legend about buried Jentilak helped direct attention toward an archaeological monument. The relationship ran from folklore to modern archaeological investigation, not from an ancient Jentil inscription to a known cult.

### Sacrifice and violence

The dedicated contested-material search found **no credible evidence of human or animal sacrifice to the Jentilak**, temple prostitution, a Jentil priesthood or cult violence. The elder’s requested killing—being thrown from a precipice—is narrative self-destruction at the end of the old age, not an attested sacrificial rite.

Stories of war between Jentilak and Christians or of their violent disappearance belong to folklore. They are not documentary evidence of an organized historical persecution of a community called Jentilak.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Prehistory and antiquity

Megalithic monuments and Iron Age sites later attached to the Jentilak are ancient. The name and legends are not contemporaneously attested on them. The Iron Age Irulegi hand demonstrates a Vasconic writing tradition in the region, but it does not name Jentilak. Roman-era indigenous divine names in Hispania and Aquitania likewise do not establish them.

Strabo and other classical authors discuss northern Iberian peoples and customs but provide no securely recognizable Jentil narrative. [Absence of evidence.]

### Christian and medieval background

The name’s ordinary sense, “Gentile/pagan,” supplies a plausible Christian category for people imagined as having lived before or outside Christianity. Giants and older peoples elsewhere in Europe were similarly made builders of ancient monuments.

Exactly when Basque communities first applied *jentil* to giants is unknown. The developed Kixmi narrative necessarily presupposes Christian Nativity tradition. Its localized forms may be old, but no medieval manuscript presently dates them.

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

- **1545:** written *jentil* is linguistically attested.
- **1571 and later:** Basque religious texts use competing terms for biblical Gentiles/pagans.
- **Seventeenth century:** *Onenzaro* is documented as a Christmas-season expression; this does not yet document Olentzero as the last Jentil.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

European folklore collection, archaeology and Basque cultural scholarship created the conditions for systematic documentation. Oral stories were written down as prehistoric monuments were surveyed.

Barandiarán, Aranzadi and Eguren united archaeological fieldwork and ethnography. This preserved important local knowledge, but it also encouraged readers to view oral legend as a direct key to prehistory.

### 1917–1975: formation of the documentary canon

- 1917: Jentilarri field tradition recorded.
- 1919: Aranzadi publishes on the Aralar gentiles.
- 1921 onward: *Eusko-Folklore* builds a systematic archive.
- 1927: Barandiarán publishes on Jentiletxetu caves.
- 1935–47: Azkue publishes *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*.
- 1950s–70s: Barandiarán’s syntheses make the Kixmi ending widely accessible.
- 1973–74: Satrústegui documents Urdiain sites.
- 1974–75: Erkoreka catalogues the corpus, variants and geography.

### Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Jentilak became established characters of “Basque mythology” in encyclopedias, children’s books, heritage interpretation, festivals, art and tourism. This reception often compresses many local accounts into a standard story: ancient Basque giants built dolmens, saw Christ’s star, buried themselves, and left Olentzero as survivor.

Modern presentations sometimes call them a “pre-Roman race,” “gods,” or the literal builders of the megaliths. None of those formulations is securely documented ancient evidence.

Contemporary cultural associations and public programs continue storytelling. Ataun’s Mitoaldia, for example, advertised the 2026 guided route “Jentilak eta otsoak!” as an all-ages encounter with nature and older beliefs. This is active heritage culture, not demonstrated continuous worship.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Basque identity and prehistory

The Jentilak provide a compelling figure for “Basque prehistory as myth”: powerful indigenous predecessors tied to caves and stone monuments, displaced by a new religion and new humanity. This has made them available as symbols of antiquity, cultural persistence and rupture.

The responsible historical formulation is not “the Jentilak preserve an unchanged Neolithic religion,” but: modern Basque oral narratives used the category of pagan predecessors to make megaliths and cultural change intelligible.

### Olentzero

The popular Olentzero of modern Christmas—cheerful charcoal-burner, parade figure and gift-giver—has absorbed Santa Claus, gift-giving and civic-festival elements, especially during the later twentieth century. His description as the sole surviving Jentil is one strand of his modern mythology, not the sole historically documented origin.

### Scholarship and public mythology

Museum, school and tourism materials now give the Jentilak stable visual form despite the absence of ancient iconography. Modern illustrations tend to merge giant, caveman, Basque shepherd and fantasy-ogre conventions. [Modern reinvention.]

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search located general modern Basque-mythology and neopagan interest but no well-documented, historically continuous Jentil cult, established liturgy, major occult order, Renaissance grimoire tradition or large organized reconstructionist denomination centered on them. Individual practitioners may honor or reinterpret them, but scale, doctrine and continuity cannot presently be established.

Claims that the Jentilak were ancient gods suppressed by the Church are modern reconstructions unless supported by evidence beyond the folklore itself.

### Political appropriation

They can symbolize a pre-Christian or indigenous Basque past, but no single political reading is intrinsic to the folklore. Treating the replacement of the Jentilak as a literal historical account of ethnic conquest or as proof of one contemporary political ideology exceeds the sources.

No documented extremist organization centered upon Jentil worship was found in the contested-material pass.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Are they gods?

The evidence favors **mythical predecessor people/giants**, not gods. They have no known cult, individual divine names, temples, sacrifices, priests or iconographic canon. Hartsuaga explicitly distinguishes the Jentil from a god or symbolic nature-power. “Divine being” is therefore an imposed dossier category; “collective of legendary giants/pagans” is more accurate.

### Prehistoric survival or later folklore?

Two broad readings recur:

1. **Survivalist reading:** Jentil legends preserve memory of pre-Christian or even prehistoric inhabitants and religion.
2. **Historical-folkloric reading:** the stories are later cultural explanations of monuments, otherness and Christianization, assembled from widespread European motifs.

The evidence can support a cultural memory of “people before us” but cannot carry specific stories unchanged back to the Neolithic. The Christian etymology and explicit building of churches by Jentilak strongly demonstrate post-conversion development.

### Hartsuaga’s structural interpretation

Hartsuaga reads the Jentil ending through comparative myth and argues that the Christian Kixmi form may replace or transform an older story of epochal catastrophe. This helps explain variants in which the portent is cloud, rain, snow or small humanity rather than Christ. The historical age of the reconstructed structure remains unverified. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

### Jentilak and Mairuak

Their roles overlap extensively, and some figures receive both labels. Whether this means original identity, motif exchange, or later convergence probably varies by locality. A universal equation is unwarranted.

### Kixmi

“Kixmi means monkey in the language of the Jentilak” is explicitly present in Barandiarán’s transmitted legend. It is not automatically an independently verified Basque etymology. Popular sources frequently turn the folkloric gloss into a linguistic fact. [Contested.]

### Olentzero’s survival

The strongest popular version—Olentzero alone survives the mass burial—is not in the plain 1917 Jentilarri account. Dueñas’s source audit shows variable attribution and later combination of the Jentil survivor, Olentzero, effigy and fire customs. The link is traditional in the broad modern sense but textually layered.

### Wider Iberian parallels

Galician *mouros/mouras*, Asturian *moras*, Cantabrian giants and European fairy-builders form a valuable comparative field. They do not justify a reconstructed “Iberian Jentil pantheon.” Similar explanatory needs and traveling motifs can generate similar beings without identity of name, cult or origin.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Jentilak are directly attested Neolithic gods.
- Jentilarri was built as their temple.
- Archaeology has recovered giant Jentil skeletons.
- *Jentil* is demonstrably a prehistoric Basque ethnonym.
- A continuous Jentil priesthood survived Christianization.
- The Kixmi story is recorded in antiquity.
- Olentzero’s identity as the last Jentil is the earliest documented form of his tradition.
- All Basque giant figures are interchangeable members of one fixed pantheon.
- Similar Galician and Cantabrian beings prove one ancient Iberian religion.

None is established by the consulted primary or scholarly record.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Attestation or development |
|---|---|
| Neolithic–Bronze Age | Dolmens and other megaliths later called *Jentilarri*, *Jentiletxe* or related names are constructed. No contemporary Jentil inscription or image is known. |
| Iron Age | Vasconic and other regional cultures occupy northern Iberia; the Irulegi hand supplies writing evidence but does not mention Jentilak. |
| Roman period | Indigenous gods appear in Latin and Aquitanian/Hispanic epigraphy. No secure Jentil attestation. |
| Christian Middle Ages | “Gentile/pagan” becomes a category for pre-Christian people; the date when Basque *jentil* acquired its giant-folklore sense is unknown. |
| 1545 | Earliest lexical attestation cited for Basque *jentil*; not securely the mythological race. |
| 1571 onward | Basque religious translation documents *jentil/pagano* vocabulary and its competition with other terms. |
| Seventeenth century | Lope de Isasti records *Onenzaro* as a Christmas-season expression; no explicit last-Jentil narrative is thereby attested. |
| Nineteenth century | Folklore and antiquarian collection grows; exact first recoverable printed giant narratives require further archive-level bibliography. |
| August 1917 | Barandiarán records the cloud-and-burial account from a shepherd during investigation of the Jentilarri dolmen. |
| 1919 | Telesforo de Aranzadi publishes *Los gentiles del Aralar*. |
| 1921 | Barandiarán begins *Eusko-Folklore: Materiales y Cuestionarios* and organizes systematic collection. |
| 1927 | Barandiarán publishes on the Jentiletxetu caves at Mutriku. |
| ca. 1928 | A source later used by Caro Baroja describes an Olentzero straw figure processed and burned; subsequent publications alter or omit attribution. |
| 1935–1947 | Azkue publishes *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*. |
| 1946 | Caro Baroja discusses Olentzero material. |
| 1956–1970s | Barandiarán’s syntheses help canonize the Jentil–Kixmi–Olentzero connection. |
| 1972–1975 | Barandiarán’s collected works make a large source corpus readily available. |
| 1973 | Satrústegui publishes on Jentilen-Sukaldea at Urdiain. |
| 1974 | Satrústegui publishes on Jentilen Laihoa. |
| 1974–1975 | Erkoreka compiles “Los gentiles,” cataloguing variants, places, motifs and source provenance. |
| Late twentieth century | Jentilak become standardized in encyclopedias, children’s culture and “Basque mythology” presentations; Olentzero becomes a mass gift-giving figure. |
| 1990s–present | Cultural associations, mythological festivals, tourism, illustration and digital media continue and reshape the traditions. |
| 2026 | Ataun’s Mitoaldia advertises a public “Jentilak eta otsoak!” folklore-and-nature route, documenting continuing cultural reception rather than cultic continuity. |
