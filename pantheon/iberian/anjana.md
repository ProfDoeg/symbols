# The Anjana: Research Dossier

**Class:** female fairy, beneficent enchantress, or localized cave-and-water spirit in Cantabrian oral tradition; normally a collective species (*las anjanas*), not a singular goddess with an individual biography. Some variants are morally ambiguous or harmful.

**Earliest secure attestation:** a Palencia schoolteacher’s folklore notebook compiled in 1930, which describes the *Anjana* as a “good witch or fairy,” records her star-bearing golden staff, subterranean palace, aid to the poor, and a complete tale. For Cantabria proper, Manuel Llano’s *Las anjanas*, printed in the *Boletín de la Biblioteca de Menéndez Pelayo* in 1931–1932, is the first securely dated substantial publication. Llano’s related folklore had appeared in *Mitos y leyendas populares recogidas de la tradición oral* (1929) and was subsequently reworked in *Brañaflor* (1931) and *Rabel* (1934), but an earlier published Cantabrian occurrence of the name has not been established here. [The 1930 Palencia notebook and text](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/el-cuaderno-de-folklore-de-palencia-de-la-maestra-jovita-coloma-1930-784093/html/); [bibliographical record for Llano’s *Las anjanas*](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/sociedad_literatura_general_y_comparada/obra/las-anjanas-981234/).

**Critical finding:** no ancient inscription, classical text, medieval manuscript, temple, cult image, sacrifice, priesthood, or archaeological deposit names an Anjana. Consequently, “pre-Roman goddess,” “Celtic deity,” and membership in a unified Basque–Asturian–Cantabrian–Galician pantheon are **modern scholarly or popular reconstructions**, not securely attested ancient classifications. Asturias has the related *xana/inxana*; León the *jana*; Galicia the *moura/encantada* complex; and the Basque country the *lamia/lamiña*. Similarity warrants comparison, not their automatic identification as one being.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

In Manuel Llano’s literary-folkloric synthesis, Anjanas are the merciful female beings of the Cantabrian mountain landscape. They inhabit inaccessible caves, emerge at dawn and dusk, clean springs, bless the flocks of good shepherds, repair trees and terrain damaged by storms or Ojáncanos, help the poor and lost, reward compassion, and punish selfishness. Llano calls them *hechiceras dulcísimas*—“sweetest enchantresses”—and *mujeres de los milagros y de las limosnas*, “women of miracles and alms.” These are **twentieth-century folklore/literary attestations**, even when Llano presents them as inherited oral belief. His best-known description appears in *Rabel* (1934), now reproduced by the Cantabrian educational authorities. [Official modern reproduction of Llano’s text](https://www.educantabria.es/documents/39930/11206926/SA%2BPRIMARIA%2BManuel%2BLlano%2Balta%2Bresoluci%C3%B3n.pdf/000f182f-0ee3-457c-3eba-77772a74a924?t=1678449298231).

The familiar singular “the Anjana” is useful for describing the type, but Llano regularly writes of *las Anjanas*. No named sovereign, genealogy, or canonical individual stands behind the collective.

### Names and variants

- **Anjana / anjana; plural anjanas** — the standard Cantabrian and neighboring Palencian form in twentieth-century records.
- **Jana** — reported in León and parts of Cantabria; also remembered uncertainly by a modern Cantabrian informant who said her grandmother might have called the being *jana* or *anjana*.
- **Onjana** — a Cantabrian variant. Corominas’s proposed explanation, reported in later linguistic discussion, is contamination by *Ojáncano*.
- **Angana** — the form recorded orally at La Rabia/El Tejo in 1974 for small cave women who stole maize cakes.
- **Ijanas** — Valle de Aras beings frequently grouped with Anjanas in later systematizations, although their reputed ugliness, enlarged breasts, and shape-shifting make them a distinct local type rather than simply another spelling.
- **Inxana** — eastern Asturian form found in the Asturias–Cantabria border tradition.
- **Xana** — the standard Asturian relative. It must not be silently substituted for *Anjana*: the two corpora overlap typologically and geographically but have different regional collection histories.
- **Moras / Moras de Carmona** — in one Carmona variant, Anjanas are said to have been Muslim women who hid in a cave after Covadonga and stole *borona* while villagers attended Mass. This is a **regional historicizing legend**, not evidence that the general Anjana tradition originated in eighth-century refugees. [Haya Martínez’s study and 1974 variants](https://repositorio.uam.es/bitstream/handle/10486/7991/43876_9.pdf?sequence=1).

### Etymology

Three levels must be separated:

1. **Documented lexical comparison:** *anjana* belongs to an Iberian and Romance cluster including *jana, xana, inxana,* Sardinian *jana/giana*, Old Occitan *jana*, and related fairy/witch words.

2. **Diana hypothesis:** a widely repeated derivation runs Latin *Diana* → popular *Jana* → Asturian *Xana* or Cantabrian *ajana/anjana*, with reanalysis after the article and nasal propagation. This connects the medieval Romance word ultimately with Diana’s name, not the documented Anjana figure directly with a Roman cult of Diana. The detailed proposed sequence remains a **linguistic reconstruction**. [Presentation of the Diana derivation and Corominas’s *onjana* proposal](https://www.vacarizu.es/drupal/articulo/jano-en-cantabria-nuevas-etimologias-de-bijanera-pico-jano-suano-y-similares).

3. **Dispute:** Juan Ibero’s “El secreto de la Xana” (1945), cited by García-Lomas, rejected derivation of *xana* from Diana and compared Neapolitan *janara*. García-Lomas consequently called the etymology doubtful and also compared Cantabrian Anjanas with Basque *lamiñak* and Sardinian *giana*. Thus claims of a settled consensus are too strong. [García-Lomas’s discussion](https://centrodeestudiosmontaneses.com/wp-content/uploads/DOC_CEM/BIBLIOTECA/EDICION_CEM/los_pasiegos_1960.pdf).

“Anjana” has no ancient native script or securely attested pre-Roman spelling. It is recorded in the Latin alphabet in modern Spanish and regional speech transcription.

### Comparanda, not timeless identities

- **Asturian xana/inxana:** closest lexical and geographic parallel; especially dense in eastern Asturias adjoining Cantabria.
- **Leonese jana:** lexical relative.
- **Basque lamia/lamiña:** cave, spring, beauty, treasure, and combing motifs invite comparison, but Basque lamias may have birdlike or animal feet and are not called Anjanas in Basque collections.
- **Galician moura/encantada:** shares cave, treasure, water, beauty, enchantment, and historicizing “Moorish woman” motifs. No Galician source examined identifies a moura as the Cantabrian Anjana.
- **Diana/Artemis:** etymological and comparativist hypothesis, never a documented ancient cult equation.
- **Nymph, fairy, “good witch”:** classificatory translations used by collectors and later writers, not indigenous ancient pantheon ranks.

Barandiarán, Azkue, Caro Baroja, Satrústegi, Hartsuaga, Risco, Cuba and Reigosa, Lisón Tolosana, and the corpora of pre-Roman Iberian religion are relevant for comparison and method. They do not supply early attestations of a Cantabrian divine individual named Anjana.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 1930: Jovita Coloma’s Palencia notebook

The earliest presently secure document is *El cuaderno de folklore de Palencia de la maestra Jovita Coloma, 1930*. Its entry says:

- the Anjana is Palencia’s “good witch or fairy”;
- she wears a white cape, blond braids, and weasel-skin sandals;
- she carries a golden staff with a star in its crook, illuminating shepherds and travelers lost in fog;
- she lives beneath the earth among treasure;
- she relieves the needs of the poor.

The accompanying tale has an enchanted maiden trapped in a tree. A woodcutter must consult an Anjana living beneath a river pool and summon her by striking the water. This record is exceptionally important because it predates or is contemporary with Llano’s first substantial Anjana publications and attests both the star-staff and a water dwelling outside modern Cantabria. What it does **not** establish is antiquity beyond the informant tradition behind the 1930 notebook. [Primary text](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/el-cuaderno-de-folklore-de-palencia-de-la-maestra-jovita-coloma-1930-784093/html/).

### Manuel Llano corpus

- ***Mitos y leyendas populares recogidas de la tradición oral* (1929):** prize-winning early collection. Its title explicitly claims oral derivation, but Llano generally did not preserve the modern ethnographer’s full apparatus of named narrators, verbatim recordings, recording dates, or complete provenance.
- ***Las anjanas* (1931–1932):** published in the *Boletín de la Biblioteca de Menéndez Pelayo*, extra no. 1, pp. 177–215. This is the foundational sustained treatment.
- ***Brañaflor* (1931):** includes Anjana-centered moral tales, notably “La anjana y la sarna” and “El arquetu y el mayorazgo.”
- ***Rabel* (1934):** contains the influential lyrical description “Las Anjanas,” “Metamorfosis: El pájaro de los diez colores,” “El Ojáncano,” and “La novia del Ojáncano.” The 2023 Cantabrian government edition identifies these pieces as deriving from *Rabel*. [Modern edition and contents](https://www.educantabria.es/documents/39930/291491/Libro%2BLa%2BAnjana%2By%2Bel%2BOj%C3%A1ncano.%2BMitos%2By%2Bleyendas%2Bde%2BManuel%2BLlano.pdf/656d1b46-669f-8ecb-5414-9f68efb62d9d?t=1679473507690).

Llano says that his materials came from shepherds and rural people in Santillana, Valdáliga, Rionansa, Lamasón, Polaciones, Cabuérniga, Aras, Meruelo, and adjoining districts. His prose, however, is artfully composed. The source is therefore simultaneously **folklore testimony and literary redaction**.

### Later documentation

- **García-Lomas, *Mitología y supersticiones de Cantabria* (1964; expanded edition 1967):** systematized Llano and other local materials. Its chapter “La Anjana, el hada buena de la Montaña” helped establish the modern canonical portrait. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Mitolog%C3%ADa_y_supersticiones_de_Cantabria.html?hl=es&id=Hc3WAAAAMAAJ).
- **La Rabia/El Tejo, 1974:** Luis González Estrada described small cave women called *anganas*, with long breasts and children on their backs, who stole *borona*. His grandmother Rosario Rubín Fernández, who died aged ninety-six, had told him the story. This is a recorded local variant sharply unlike the ethereal benevolent fairy.
- **Carmona:** Llano’s cave Anjanas/Moras likewise stole cakes during Sunday Mass. Later field investigation found that Llano and García-Lomas had mislocated the cave: it stood opposite Peña de la Mena in the *castru de las anjanas*, not in Peña de la Mena itself.
- **Navedo, 1990:** Fausto Diego Prieto supplied a stone-substituted-for-cake story about an Anjana.
- **Niembru, Asturias:** *inxanas* were said to steal cakes and leave a heated stone. The collectors’ judgment “I think they were very bad” contradicts the universal “good fairy” classification.
- **Cícero testimony:** a grandmother’s Anjana was a good, beautifully dressed apparition who foretold marriage and children in dreams, but also had one eye and large breasts—features elsewhere assigned to Ojáncanas. This demonstrates oral category mixing. [Recent critical collation of Llano and oral variants](https://ebuah.uah.es/dspace/bitstream/handle/10017/65734/jardin_voz_26_ecos.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1).

There is no scripture, ancient hymn, magical papyrus, saga, or medieval manuscript devoted to the Anjana.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Functions

**Llano’s canonical literary-folkloric Anjana**:

- relieves hunger, thirst, pain, anxiety, and injustice;
- gives alms or treasure and rewards compassion;
- cleans springs and converses with running water;
- blesses deserving shepherds’ flocks;
- heals or consoles wounded animals;
- repairs trees, rocks, heath, and pathways damaged by weather or Ojáncanos;
- guides people lost in woods, snow, or fog;
- protects lovers and poor young women;
- heals illness, sometimes through a bottle of restorative liquid;
- transforms into women, old beggars, pigeons, or many-colored birds;
- tests human charity while disguised.

The ecological language now attached to her—“guardian of the environment,” “teacher of the forest”—is a **late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century pedagogical interpretation**, though it develops genuine Llano motifs of repairing natural damage.

### Appearance

The corpus is not uniform:

- young, very beautiful, pale woman;
- long blond hair or braids decorated with silk ribbons;
- crown of wildflowers, sometimes mixed with silver flowers;
- white tunic and blue cloak;
- serene, affectionate eyes and a gentle musical voice;
- staff or wand of ash, hawthorn, hazel, or willow; alternatively a golden pike;
- luminous star either at the staff’s tip or on the forehead;
- combs of coral;
- in modern children’s imagery, small stature and transparent wings.

The **star-bearing staff is securely documented by 1930** in the Palencia notebook. Claims that it changes color on every weekday or always terminates in a curative bottle occur in later popular systematization and illustration and should not be projected backward without a specific citation.

Transparent wings are especially characteristic of modern fairy illustration. Llano’s transformations into birds do not require permanent wings.

### Habitat and cycle

Llano places Anjanas in nearly inaccessible caves with floors of gold and walls of silver. They leave at dawn, return at mid-morning, emerge again at evening, pray on returning, and sleep in luminous beds “like moon and morning star.” At the beginning of spring they gather at midnight on a high *braña*, dance hand in hand around multicolored roses until dawn, and scatter the roses on paths. Whoever finds one will be happy until death. This is **Llano’s 1934 literary-folkloric formulation**, not a documented public festival or temple rite.

Cave, underground palace, spring, river pool, woodland path, upland pasture, mist, and threshold spaces are the defining landscape. No fixed sacred color, weekday, planet, constellation, number, or liturgical calendar is securely attested.

### Images and material culture

No ancient or medieval image is identifiable as an Anjana. Modern representations derive largely from Llano and García-Lomas: blond fairy, white dress, blue cloak, flowers, star wand, wings, or medicine bottle. Fernando Calderón created public visual embodiments from Llano’s creatures by 1984. Later illustrators, school projects, statues, theme environments, cards, and tourist imagery have further regularized the design. These are **modern reception objects**, not cult icons.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

No source supplies parents, birth, siblings, consort, or offspring for the Anjanas as a class. Some later tradition says an Anjana who marries a mortal loses her powers and becomes an old woman; this is a **late collected fairy-marriage motif**, not a stable genealogy.

A modern popular claim calls Anjanas celestial beings sent by God for four hundred years. Its early source has not been established. Llano does say that they pray, but prayer indicates a Christianized narrative setting, not proof of a formal angelic rank.

### Every documented named supernatural interaction

- **Ojáncano — antagonism and repair.** Anjanas undo damage attributed to Ojáncanos and stand as their moral opposite. Source: Llano, especially *Las anjanas* (1931–1932) and *Rabel* (1934). **Tier: folklore in literary redaction; later canonical Cantabrian systematization.** General sources frequently call the Ojáncano her “enemy,” but a surviving Llano passage documenting a direct face-to-face combat between them has not been located.
- **Ojáncana — contrast, not necessarily an episode.** Later systematizations oppose the beneficent Anjana to the destructive female ogress. **Tier: learned twentieth-century systematization.** No securely identified primary episode in which the two meet has been established.
- **Ramidreju — medicinal use of its gall.** A Viaña story attributed to Llano says Anjanas in the valley of Cieza drink the Ramidreju’s gall, which cures every disease and reveals buried treasure. **Tier: folklore reported through Llano; local variant.** [Summary and source attribution](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramidreju).
- **God — service, prayer, or mission.** Llano’s Anjanas pray; later retellings call them beings sent by God to do good. **Tier: Christianized folklore / later systematization.** No revelation text or ecclesiastical doctrine recognizes them.
- **Enchanted maiden in the tree — assistance toward release.** The 1930 Palencia tale directs a woodcutter to an Anjana beneath a river pool to learn how to disenchant a maiden. The maiden is supernatural but unnamed. **Tier: securely documented folklore, 1930.**
- **Xana, lamia, moura, Diana, Artemis, Mari:** no documented narrative meeting with the Anjana. Their relationships are comparative, etymological, or syncretistic hypotheses—not interactions.

No interaction with Endovelicus, Ataecina, or another named pre-Roman Iberian god is attested.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The daily works of the Anjanas

In Llano’s *Rabel*, the Anjanas leave their precious subterranean rooms at dawn. Their movement makes streams livelier. They clean water sources, bless the livestock of good shepherds, and tend damaged trees, rocks, and burnt vegetation. They return before noon, walk again at dusk, pray, and sleep in radiant beds. This is not a plot-driven myth so much as an ordered fairy ecology. **Earliest substantial source:** Llano, 1931–1934; **tier:** literary redaction of claimed oral tradition.

### The spring rose dance

At spring’s beginning, Anjanas dance through the night around multicolored roses on a mountaintop pasture, then scatter the flowers along paths. Finding one guarantees lifelong happiness. **Source:** Llano, *Rabel* (1934); **tier:** later canonical literary-folkloric formulation. No independent ritual observation confirms that Cantabrians enacted this ceremony.

### “Metamorphosis: The Bird of Ten Colors”

A good young woman feeds a ten-colored bird at her window throughout spring, summer, and autumn. In winter she finds it nearly frozen in the snow, warms it against her body, and tends it in the kitchen. Once restored, smoke smelling of roses issues from it and an Anjana appears, rewarding the woman’s compassion with wealth. The Anjana finally departs in another avian form, a blue pigeon with a red beak. The tale explicitly teaches charity and states that an Anjana can change merely by willing it. **Source:** Llano, *Rabel* (1934); **tier:** literary-folkloric moral tale. [Textual excerpts and official study questions](https://www.educantabria.es/documents/d/educantabria/la-anjana-y-el-ojancano-mitos-y-leyendas-de-manuel-llano-educacion-primaria-pdf).

### The Anjana beneath the river pool

A woodcutter hears a tree cry when struck. A maiden imprisoned inside tells him to seek an Anjana beneath a nearby river pool, summoning her by beating the water with a stick; the Anjana gives instructions connected with a red flower and the maiden’s release. **Source:** Jovita Coloma’s Palencia notebook (1930); **tier:** securely documented regional folklore. It is the earliest securely datable complete Anjana narrative located in this investigation.

### The cave women and stolen *borona*

In Carmona, women identified as Anjanas or Moras hide in a cave after Covadonga, enter houses while Christians attend Sunday Mass, and steal maize cakes cooking in the hearth. Villagers substitute round hot stones. The thieves burn their hands and flee crying. In the 1974 La Rabia/El Tejo testimony, the *anganas* are tiny, long-breasted women carrying children; in Navedo and Niembru, closely related cake-and-stone stories survive under *anjana/inxana*. **Sources:** Llano; 1974 oral testimony; Sordo Sotres’s 1990 collections; **tier:** regional variants and historicizing folklore.

This cycle contradicts the exclusively benevolent Llano portrait. It also resembles a widespread migratory folktale about supernatural food thieves, so it cannot by itself prove ancient Cantabrian ethnicity or cult.

### “La anjana y la sarna” and “El arquetu y el mayorazgo”

These *Brañaflor* tales employ supernatural reward and punishment in moral narratives, particularly against greed and misconduct. They belong to Llano’s 1931 synthesis. Complete critical texts were not recoverable from the consulted web sources; therefore plot details circulating in summaries should not be treated as verbatim oral records.

### Prophetic dream Anjanas

A Cícero testimony describes Anjanas appearing in dreams to girls and foretelling whether they would marry or have children. The teller remembered good fairies in fine clothing but also a single eye and enlarged breasts, demonstrating contamination between Anjana and Ojáncana imagery. **Tier:** modern oral testimony preserving unstable classification.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There is no evidence for an organized Anjana cult.

- **Temples and sanctuaries:** none.
- **Altars and inscriptions:** none.
- **Priesthood:** none.
- **Sacrifice or formal offering:** none securely documented.
- **Human sacrifice:** no evidence whatsoever.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence.
- **Theophoric ancient names:** none.
- **Cult statues or votive images:** none.
- **Calendar festival:** none historically documented as worship.

Narrative practices include summoning an Anjana by striking river water, appealing to her aid, leaving or giving food through acts of hospitality, and seeking lucky flowers or treasure. Later descriptions say people petitioned Anjanas to sing cheerful or sorrowful songs to alter the weather; the precise field source requires verification.

Caves and place-names—Cueva de las Anjanas, Castru de las Anjanas, and related sites—are material evidence that the name entered local landscape memory. They do not establish temples. The correction of the Carmona cave’s location shows why place-lore must be field-checked.

Nor do the sanctuaries of Endovelicus at São Miguel da Mota or Ataecina at Turóbriga supply missing evidence. Their Latin inscriptions document different named Iberian deities. Strabo III and other Roman authors discuss Iberian peoples and customs but do not mention Anjana.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1900: unrecorded or unidentified antecedents

Romance fairy vocabulary, medieval traditions about Diana and nocturnal women, and northern Iberian stories of xanas, lamias, mouras, enchanted women, caves, and springs provide plausible background. Yet no source securely carries the complete Cantabrian name-and-character complex backward into antiquity or the Middle Ages.

### 1920s–1934: documentation and literary formation

Aurelio de Llano’s Asturian collections (1922) provided neighboring xana material. Manuel Llano began publishing Cantabrian oral and costumbrista material in the 1920s, issued the 1929 collection, *Las anjanas* in 1931–1932, and the influential *Rabel* treatment in 1934. The 1930 Palencia notebook independently shows that the name and star-staff portrait were not confined to Llano’s books.

Llano’s importance is double: he preserved genuine regional motifs, and his poetic prose selected and stabilized a merciful “good fairy” ideal from a much rougher body of cave-woman traditions.

### 1930s–1960s: canonization

Llano’s early death in 1938 turned his work into a central regional literary archive. García-Lomas’s 1964 and 1967 syntheses classified “La Anjana” as the good fairy of the Montaña and compared her with Asturian, Basque, Mediterranean, and classical beings. This was **learned systematization**, not recovery of a lost native theology.

### 1970s–1990s: field survival and regional identity

The 1974 El Tejo testimony documented *anganas* that looked more like long-breasted cave mothers and food thieves than Llano’s elegant benefactors. Sordo Sotres recorded related border material in 1990. Simultaneously, Llano’s Anjana became a symbol of Cantabrian identity through collected works, folklore books, illustration, public exhibitions, children’s literature, and tourism.

### 2000s–present: heritage and pedagogy

The Anjana now appears in the MitoCasuca de Anievas, the “Un pueblo de leyendas” festivities, school curricula, environmental education, tourist promotion, public sculpture and illustration, storytelling, and merchandise. Cantabria’s education authority issued *La Anjana y el Ojáncano* teaching materials in 2023, explicitly connecting Llano’s language, tradition, and regional culture.

This is a heritage revival, not demonstrable uninterrupted worship. No reliable evidence was found for a stable Anjana-centered neopagan congregation, occult order, initiatory lineage, or continuous household cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

The dominant modern Anjana is smaller, prettier, more winged, more explicitly ecological, and more visually standardized than the surviving oral variants.

- **Literature:** Llano’s work is itself the decisive reinvention. Later authors such as Gustavo Cotera and regional mythographers further codified the character.
- **Visual art:** Fernando Calderón’s 1984 representations translated Llano’s beings into public images. Later illustrations commonly add transparent wings and a potion bottle.
- **Education:** the 2023 government materials teach “Las Anjanas,” “The Bird of Ten Colors,” the Ojáncano, dialect vocabulary, and environmental themes.
- **Museums and festivals:** MitoCasuca and Anievas’s “Un pueblo de leyendas” turn the mythological catalogue into performed regional heritage.
- **Games:** Adriana Diloy included the Anjana in a sixty-five-card Cantabrian mythology game launched in 2023. [EFE report](https://efe.com/cultura/2023-05-15/seres-mitologicos-juego-de-cartas/).
- **Commercial naming:** wellness businesses, accommodation, crafts, and tourism use “Anjana” as a local emblem. This is branding, not cult.
- **Children’s fantasy:** the Anjana is often recast as a conventional miniature winged fairy and benevolent forest guardian. Claims that she controls the climate, embodies a universal Mother Goddess, or belongs to an intact Celtic pantheon are **modern elaborations unless tied to a named collected narrative**.
- **Occultism and neopaganism:** the contested-material search found scattered modern magical and “Celtic fantasy” descriptions but no foundational Renaissance grimoire, Golden Dawn text, Theosophical canon, established reconstructionist liturgy, or measurable organized cult centered on the Anjana.
- **Political appropriation:** she participates in Cantabrian regional identity, but no well-documented extremist appropriation was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Llano collector or creator?

This is the central debate. García-Lomas accused Llano of borrowing from a highly fantasized Asturian mythology. Juan Haya Martínez defended him as a faithful transmitter who traveled among farmers and shepherds. Neither extreme is fully adequate: independent and later oral attestations validate a traditional substrate, while Llano’s stylized prose, incomplete informant apparatus, recombination, and moral aesthetics unmistakably shaped the canonical figure.

A major recent contribution is Juan M. Haya Martínez’s *Ecos de Manuel Llano* (2024/2025), which compares Llano’s 1924–1925 *Castilla Gráfica* writings and later revisions with a large oral corpus. It concludes that Llano was both a literary author and a knowledgeable folklorist, with Anjana narratives centrally concerned with contested relations between supernatural inhabitants and humans. [Repository record and abstract](https://ebuah.uah.es/dspace/handle/10017/65734).

### Asturian borrowing or shared border tradition?

García-Lomas leaned toward Asturian “paternity.” Against this, the distribution is continuous across eastern Asturias, western Cantabria, northern Palencia, and León; independent *anjana/angana/inxana* variants persist in border communities. The responsible conclusion is a shared and mobile north-Iberian folklore zone, not proven one-way descent.

### Diana, Celtic survivals, and pre-Roman religion

The Diana etymology is plausible but disputed. Even if linguistically correct, it establishes the historical ancestry of a word or fairy category, not continuity of theology, ritual, or identity from Roman Diana to Llano’s Anjana.

“Celtic nature goddess,” “pre-Roman Cantabrian deity,” and direct derivation from water worship remain **modern reconstructions**. No inscription in HEp or related Hispano-Roman corpora names her; no archaeology ties her to Endovelicus or Ataecina; Strabo III does not supply the missing bridge.

### Good fairy versus dangerous cave woman

Llano and García-Lomas privileged an aesthetically coherent beneficent fairy. Oral variants include cake thieves, ugly long-breasted beings, one-eyed apparitions, fortune tellers, and “very bad” *inxanas*. The variants are not corruptions of a single canonical original; they are the historical record of an unstable category.

### Reliability and preservation

The evidence survives chiefly in:

- printed folklore and literary works;
- regional newspaper and periodical archives;
- later field notes and transcriptions;
- place-names and living memory;
- modern collected editions and educational reproductions.

The greatest losses are oral performances never recorded, informants whom early collectors left anonymous, exact dates and locations, and the distinction between a narrator’s language and a collector’s embellishment.

## 10. Chronology

- **Antiquity:** no Anjana attestation in Iberian inscriptions, archaeology, Strabo III, or Roman literature. Later pre-Roman classifications are retrospective.
- **Middle Ages–early modern period:** Romance traditions of *jana/xana* and learned associations with Diana provide possible lexical background; no securely identified Cantabrian Anjana text.
- **1922:** Aurelio de Llano publishes *Del folklore asturiano*, documenting neighboring Asturian supernatural traditions.
- **1924–1925:** Manuel Llano publishes costumbrista pieces in *Castilla Gráfica*, later reworked.
- **1929:** Llano publishes *Mitos y leyendas populares recogidas de la tradición oral*.
- **1930:** Jovita Coloma’s Palencia notebook records the Anjana, her star-staff, subterranean treasure palace, charitable function, and river-pool tale.
- **1931:** Llano publishes *Brañaflor*, including Anjana moral tales.
- **1931–1932:** *Las anjanas* appears in the *Boletín de la Biblioteca de Menéndez Pelayo*, pp. 177–215.
- **1934:** *Rabel* fixes the influential description, daily cycle, spring rose dance, metamorphosis, Ojáncano opposition, and “Bird of Ten Colors.”
- **1938:** death of Manuel Llano.
- **1945:** Juan Ibero’s “El secreto de la Xana” disputes derivation from Diana.
- **1964:** García-Lomas publishes *Mitología y supersticiones de Cantabria*, including “La Anjana, el hada buena de la Montaña.”
- **1967:** expanded García-Lomas edition and collected Llano publications further canonize the figure.
- **1974:** Luis González Estrada’s El Tejo testimony records *anganas* as small, long-breasted cave women who steal *borona*.
- **1976–1977:** Haya Martínez publishes discussion defending Llano’s folkloric field basis while correcting the location of the Carmona cave.
- **1984:** Fernando Calderón’s visual interpretations bring Llano’s mythic catalogue into contemporary public art.
- **1990:** Navedo and Niembru border variants are collected by Ramón Sordo Sotres.
- **1998:** Gustavo Cotera’s *Mitología de Cantabria* contributes to popular codification.
- **2000s:** museums, interpretive centers, festivals, tourism, and schools increasingly make the Anjana a regional heritage symbol.
- **2018:** Paula Matabuena Martín’s study surveys Cantabrian mythic beings and modern iconography, including the star, wand, and variant Anjanas. [Study](https://uvadoc.uva.es/bitstream/handle/10324/33360/TFG_F_2018_171.pdf?sequence=1).
- **2023:** Cantabria’s education authority republishes and teaches Llano’s Anjana and Ojáncano texts; Adriana Diloy releases a Cantabrian-mythology card game.
- **2024–2025:** Haya Martínez’s *Ecos de Manuel Llano* offers the most substantial recent comparison between Llano’s revisions and documented oral tradition.
- **Present:** the Anjana remains a vigorous folkloric, educational, artistic, and commercial emblem; organized continuous worship remains unestablished.
