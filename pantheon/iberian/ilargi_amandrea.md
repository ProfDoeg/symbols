# Ilargi Amandrea: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the securely documented Basque tradition, the Moon is a **feminine personified celestial being**, addressed honorifically as *Ilargi amandrea* (“Grandmother/Lady-Mother Moon”). Calling her a formally organized “goddess” is a reasonable modern shorthand, but the surviving evidence does not demonstrate an ancient priesthood, temple cult, canonical mythology, or fixed pantheon around a deity bearing that proper name.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The common noun *ilargi* “moon” is attested in sixteenth-century Basque literature; R. L. Trask dates it to **1545**. The earliest securely located publication presently found for the personalized formula *Ilargi amandrea* is José Miguel de Barandiarán’s **1923** article “La religion des anciens Basques,” recording children’s oral formulas at **Ataun and Ormaiztegi in Gipuzkoa**. No pre-Roman inscription, medieval manuscript, cult image, altar, temple, or theophoric personal name securely names Ilargi Amandrea.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** primary or near-primary textual/material evidence, securely dated.
- **[F] Folklore:** oral custom or belief documented by an identifiable collector, generally in the nineteenth or twentieth century.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally restricted terminology or belief.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an ethnographer’s or mythographer’s organization of disparate evidence.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference extending beyond what the source itself states.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification:** an equation with another divine figure.
- **[MA] Modern accretion or retelling:** modern literary, educational, artistic, or popular elaboration.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported ancient claim:** presented as traditional or ancient without adequate primary documentation.
- **[Ø] Absence of evidence:** no secure documentation found in the searched corpus.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[F]** Ilargi Amandrea is not initially encountered as the heroine of an extended myth but as the visibly present Moon addressed as a female elder or revered mother. Barandiarán recorded that both Sun and Moon were treated as feminine in several Basque localities. Children greeted the rising Moon:

> *Ilargi amandrea, / Zeruan ze berri?*  
> “Grandmother Moon, what news is there in heaven?”

The answer was:

> *Zeruan berri onak, / Orañ eta beti.*  
> “Good news in heaven, now and always.”

Barandiarán explicitly located this usage at Ataun and Ormaiztegi, while saying comparable formulas were widespread. He also cautioned that related verses occurred outside the Basque Country and had been transferred to the ladybird and, in a Christianized Ondarroa song, to Saint Clare. The formula is therefore genuine Basque folklore but not proof by itself of an unchanged prehistoric liturgy ([Barandiarán 1923](https://www.ehu.eus/documents/d/jose-migel-barandiaran-katedra/038_1923_barandiaran_religion-anciens-basques-pdf)).

### Names and dialect forms

**[EA/F]** Recorded Basque words for the Moon include:

- *hilargi, ilhargi, ilargi, illargi* — the largest dialect group.
- *illergi, irargi, idargi* — phonological or dialectal variants.
- *iretargi, iratargi, itargi, igetargi/i(g)ertagi* — principally Bizkaian forms recorded by lexicographers and ethnographers.
- *ilaski* — Souletin in Trask; Barandiarán recorded *Ilazki* in Salazar. Trask cautions that the specifically normalized form *ilazki* in Azkue’s 1905 dictionary may be erroneous.
- *argizagi, argizari, argizai* — eastern dialect words. Azkue glossed *argizagi* as “wineskin of light,” but the segmentation is disputed.
- *goiko(a)* — “the one above,” recorded at Bidankoze/Bidángoz in Roncal; an indirect designation rather than a divine proper name.
- *il* or reconstructed *iLe* — survives principally in compounds, with the related meanings “moon/month.”
- Lunar-phase terms: *ilberri/ilbarri* “new moon,” *ilbete* “full moon,” *ilgora* “waxing moon/first quarter,” *ilbehera/ilbera* “waning moon/last quarter,” and *ilzar* “old moon.”

Azkue’s list is reproduced with dialect assignments in Juan Antonio Urbeltz’s “La luna y los tejedores” ([Urbeltz](https://www.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/PDFAnlt/zainak/08/08087116.pdf)); standardized astronomical terminology is given by [Euskaltzaindia](https://www.euskaltzaindia.eus/dok/iker_jagon_tegiak/Astronomia_Oinarrizko_Lexikoa.pdf).

### Meaning of *amandre(a)*

**[F]** *Amandre* can mean “grandmother,” “lady-mother,” or an honorific maternal elder. With the definite article, *Ilargi amandrea* means approximately “the Grandmother/Lady-Mother Moon.” It is an address or title; the evidence does not show that *Amandrea* was an independent divine name.

### The disputed etymology of *ilargi*

1. **“Moon/month-light” — strongest linguistic account.**  
   **[SR]** Michelena and Trask analyze the first element as the old lunar/month stem *iLe-* and *argi* “light”: originally “moonlight” or “light of the moon/month.” Trask notes that the reconstructed lunar stem cannot plausibly be identified with *hil* “dead,” although the forms became homophonous. Related *hilabete* “month” is literally associated with fullness, plausibly “full moon” in origin.

2. **“Light of the dead.”**  
   **[SR, disputed]** Van Eys and C. C. Uhlenbeck preferred an analysis from *hil* “dead” + *argi* “light.” Yuri Zytsar defended this interpretation through association with *hilerri* “cemetery,” taboo names, and comparative lunar symbolism. Yet his case is interpretive rather than a demonstration of regular historical morphology; he concedes that Azkue, Schuchardt, and Vinson favored “moonlight.” His Lithuanian and wider Eurasian comparisons do not constitute Basque primary evidence ([Zytsar](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/26321.pdf)).

3. **“Night-light.”**  
   **[SR, weak]** This was proposed by relating the first element to a hypothetical word for night or darkness. Michelena’s analysis of *ilun* and the lack of a demonstrated independent root weaken it.

4. **Fern-, weaving-, and sickle-based readings.**  
   **[SR, speculative]** Urbeltz associates *iretargi* with *ire* “fern,” then links Moon, plant fibers, spinning, pastoralism, fertility, and the sickle. His own language—“presumably” and “according to our hypothesis”—properly marks this as reconstruction, not recorded belief. It should not be presented as the ancient meaning of *Ilargi*.

Thus “light of the dead,” although culturally evocative and now widespread online, is **not a settled etymology**. “Moon/month-light” has the better historical-linguistic support.

### Classification through time

- **1545 onward [EA]:** a Basque noun for the astronomical Moon.
- **Recorded rural tradition [F]:** a feminine, addressable celestial presence and maternal elder.
- **Twentieth-century mythology handbooks [LS]:** a “divinity” or “goddess,” commonly placed in a system with Earth and Sun.
- **Late twentieth century to present [MA]:** a proper-named lunar goddess with genealogy, personality, emblems, and sometimes a reconstructed cult.
- **[Ø]:** No ancient source identifies Ilargi with Roman Luna, Greek Selene, Phoebe, Diana, Hecate, or a named Hispano-Celtic deity.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidence in chronological order

**c. 20 BCE–20 CE — Strabo, *Geography* 3.4.16 [EA, contextual only].**  
Strabo, probably drawing on Posidonius, says that Celtiberians and unnamed neighboring peoples to their north sacrificed to an unnamed god at full moon, outside or before the gates, while households danced and kept vigil through the night. The Greek reads *anōnymōi tini theōi*—“to a certain nameless god.” It neither names Ilargi nor unequivocally says that the god was the Moon. The subject populations cannot simply be equated with all Basques, Asturians, Cantabrians, or Galicians. The English text is available in the [LacusCurtius edition](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/3D%2A.html), with the Greek accessible through [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Strab.+3.4&lang=original).

**1545 — Bernat Etxepare, *Linguae Vasconum Primitiae* [EA, lexical].**  
This first printed Basque book establishes a sixteenth-century literary horizon for Basque vocabulary. Modern historical lexicography dates *hilargi/ilargi* “moon” to 1545. This is evidence for the word, not for a goddess, prayer, genealogy, or cult. Bibliographic confirmation of the Bordeaux edition appears in the [Basque government’s Etxepare entry](https://www.euskadi.eus/web01-a2lurhiz/es/contenidos/termino/_c02043/eu_e_4348/e4348.html).

**Nineteenth century — comparative philology and calendar reconstruction [LS/SR].**  
Astarloa, Van Eys, Vinson, Schuchardt, Uhlenbeck, and others attempted to reconstruct archaic Basque religion from day names, month names, and astronyms. These studies preserve useful lexical data but frequently move from etymology to religious history without independent documentation.

**1923 — Barandiarán, “La religion des anciens Basques” [F].**  
This is the earliest securely located publication in this investigation to print *Ilargi amandrea*. It records:

- Moon names by locality;
- the Moon’s usual feminine gender;
- a masculine Moon in a tale published by Julien Vinson;
- the Ataun and Ormaiztegi greeting;
- transfer of the formula to the ladybird;
- a Christianized Saint Clare adaptation.

Barandiarán interpreted these as possible survivals of astral cult, but his evidence consisted of then-current folklore rather than pre-Christian documents.

**1920s–1940s — Azkue and Caro Baroja [F/LS].**  
Resurrección María de Azkue’s *Euskalerriaren Yakintza* and dictionary assembled vocabulary, sayings, stories, and customs. Julio Caro Baroja’s studies of ancient Basque religion and the calendar critically organized linguistic, Roman, festival, and ethnographic evidence. Their works remain indispensable, but neither chronological antiquity nor an organized Ilargi priesthood follows automatically from the age of a custom’s collectors.

**1960–1972 — Barandiarán’s syntheses [LS].**  
*Mitología vasca* and the *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca* gathered decades of fieldwork into a mythographic reference system. The latter’s 1972 bibliographic record is preserved by [Euskariana](https://www.euskariana.euskadi.eus/euskadibib/eu/bib/1328509.do). These works helped turn locally recorded lunar sayings into the familiar encyclopedia figure “Ilargi.”

**Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries [MA].**  
Educational, tourism, children’s-literature, neopagan, and internet accounts increasingly give Ilargi a fixed divine genealogy and a narrative role extending beyond the short folkloric record.

### Material and iconographic corpus

**[Ø]** No securely attributed ancient representation of Ilargi Amandrea is known. Lunar crescents, rosettes, discs, and astral motifs occur on northern Iberian stelae and later funerary monuments, but without inscriptions naming Ilargi they cannot be assigned to her individually. A crescent proves lunar symbolism, not the identity of a named Basque goddess.

**[Ø]** The Latin epigraphic corpora of Hispania contain many indigenous divine names, but none securely supplies *Ilargi*, *Ilazki*, or *Ilargi Amandrea*. The sanctuaries of Endovelicus at São Miguel da Mota and Ataecina at Turóbriga concern different, epigraphically named Lusitanian deities. They are valuable comparanda for what a materially attested cult looks like, but they are not evidence for Ilargi.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Securely documented functions

- **Visible Moon and nocturnal light [EA/F].** Her indispensable “attribute” is the lunar body itself.
- **News-bearing celestial elder [F].** The greeting asks what news she brings from heaven.
- **Marker of time [EA/F].** The lexical bond between Moon and month appears in *hilabete* and phase terms.
- **Regulator of rural labor [F].** Agricultural operations were scheduled by waxing and waning phases.
- **Female or maternal personification [F].** Barandiarán’s local testimony makes this secure for several communities, though Vinson’s masculine-Moon tale shows it was not universal.
- **Connection with death [SR/F].** Later accounts call the Moon the light of dead souls. The semantic etymology remains disputed, but death-and-night associations are present in Basque scholarly and popular interpretation.
- **Nightly return to Earth [LS/MA].** Modern syntheses state that Sun and Moon emerge from Earth and return into her. The better documented traditional formula concerns the Sun returning to “her mother”; its extension into a complete paired genealogy for Moon and Sun requires caution.

### Agriculture and sowing

**[F]** The *Atlas Etnográfico de Vasconia* documents numerous local rules:

- At Abadiño, leeks, tomatoes, onions, peppers, and turnips were sown in the waning phase, *ilbeheran*.
- At Bedarona and Beasain, vegetables generally were planted in waning moon.
- At Elgoibar, early sowing especially required attention to the waning phase.
- At Donazaharre, crops that “make a head”—potatoes, turnips, legumes, wheat, and maize—were associated with the descending or waning Moon.
- At Agurain, beans, vetch, and potatoes were sown in waning Moon to resist weevils or undesirable sprouting.
- In Carranza, most seedbeds, transplanting, harvesting, and storage were traditionally regulated by lunar condition.
- Friday could substitute when the proper waning phase was unavailable because the Moon was said to stand still or be “dead” that day.

These are documented farming customs, not proof of worship or of the Moon’s biological efficacy. The Atlas also records their decline under mechanized agriculture ([Atlas Etnográfico de Vasconia](https://atlasetnografico.labayru.eus/index.php/Influencia_de_la_luna)).

### Iconography

**[Ø]** There is no canonical ancient anthropomorphic form, costume, weapon, mount, sacred animal, plant, color, or number attributable specifically to Ilargi.

**[MA]** Contemporary illustrations often show a pale woman with a crescent, stars, silver robes, or long hair. Modern websites sometimes assign her a “sigil.” These draw upon international lunar-goddess conventions rather than documented Basque iconography.

**[F/MA]** The ladybird’s address as *iratargi amandre ona* is an instance of transferred naming, not evidence that the ladybird was Ilargi’s sacred animal.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

**Earth as mother.**  
**[LS, possibly based on folklore]** Twentieth-century synthesis presents Earth—*Lur*, *Ama Lur/Ama Lurra*—as mother of Sun and Moon, each returning to her interior after crossing the sky. The explicitly recorded sunset verse calls the Sun’s destination “her mother,” but the fully articulated family—Ama Lur as goddess, Eguzki and Ilargi as daughters and sisters—is clearer in later mythographic presentation than in an ancient text.

**Sun as sister.**  
**[LS/MA]** Modern handbooks regularly call Eguzki and Ilargi sisters. The older evidence securely makes both feminine, addresses both as *amandre*, and associates celestial setting with return to the mother; it does not preserve an extended sibling episode.

**Mari.**  
**[Ø]** No early source securely identifies Ilargi’s mother as Mari, makes Ilargi an aspect of Mari, or narrates a personal encounter between them. Modern triads combining Mari, Sun, and Moon are systematizations.

### Every documented or claimed god–god interaction

- **Ama Lur / Lur — mother–daughter relationship and nightly reception.** **Source:** chiefly Barandiarán’s twentieth-century synthesis and later retellings; no ancient text. **Tier:** **[LS/MA]**.
- **Eguzki / Eki — paired feminine celestial bodies, commonly made sisters.** **Source:** Barandiarán’s collected material establishes parallel titles and gender; explicit sibling genealogy is prominent in later summaries. **Tier:** **[F → LS/MA]**.
- **Christian God (*Jainko/Jaungoikoa*) — Moon called God’s face in some later summaries.** **Source:** modern Basque mythographic websites, apparently combining folkloric expressions with Christian cosmology. **Tier:** **[MA];** no securely identified early testimony located.
- **Saint Clare — the lunar greeting formula transferred to a Christian song at Ondarroa.** **Source:** Barandiarán 1923. This is a textual/ritual substitution rather than a narrative meeting. **Tier:** **[F/SY]**.
- **Mari — no documented encounter.** **Tier:** **[Ø]**.
- **Selene, Luna, Diana, Hecate, Phoebe — no documented Basque narrative interaction or ancient equation.** **Tier:** **[Ø]**.
- **Any Asturian, Cantabrian, Galician, Celtiberian, Lusitanian, or Roman indigenous god — no named interaction.** **Tier:** **[Ø]**.

No combat, marriage, seduction, judgment, theft, rescue, punishment, divine council, or joint narrative scene involving Ilargi and another named god has been securely located.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

The surviving record contains motifs and ritual speech rather than a developed Ilargi epic.

### The celestial greeting

**[F]** Children see or greet the Moon and ask for news from heaven; she answers that the news is good “now and always.” The exchange makes the Moon addressable and auspicious. Barandiarán’s 1923 text is the controlling witness.

**Variants:**

- **[RV]** Ataun and Ormaiztegi: direct address to Moon.
- **[RV]** The same or similar formula addressed to a ladybird.
- **[SY]** Ondarroa: Christianized as a Saint Clare song.
- **[MA]** Patxi Zubizarreta’s 1996 children’s book *Ilargian ditarean* embeds the greeting in a literary story; its publication record and summary are preserved by [Galtzagorri Elkartea](https://www.ibbygaltzagorri.eus/eu/haur-eta-gazte-literatura/argitalpenak/ilargian-ditarean).

### Daughter of Earth and sister of Sun

**[LS/MA]** The now-standard story says Ama Lur created or bore the two daughters Ilargi and Eguzki; both emerge from Earth, cross the sky, and return to her. In expanded modern versions, Earth creates Moon to protect humans from nocturnal beings; when the beings become accustomed to moonlight, she creates Sun and finally the protective *eguzkilore*.

The simple earth-return motif has roots in collected solar folklore. The full sequence—Earth deliberately creating successive protective daughters and a thistle—is best treated as a modern integrated cosmogony unless a collector’s dated local testimony is supplied. Popular recitations frequently cite “Barandiarán” generically without giving an informant, place, collection date, or exact passage.

### Light of the dead

**[SR → MA]** The Moon is said to illuminate dead people or their souls and sometimes to mediate between living and dead. This accords with the “light of the dead” etymology favored by Uhlenbeck and Zytsar, but that etymology is contested. Modern educational material often converts a philological hypothesis into an ancient mythological fact. The evidentiary sequence is therefore:

1. homophonous Basque elements *hil* “dead” and *argi* “light”;
2. comparative philological interpretation;
3. twentieth-century religious reconstruction;
4. popular retelling as established ancestral belief.

### The man in the Moon

**[F/RV]** Basque storytelling includes local “man in the Moon” material, including versions where the Moon is grammatically or narratively male. Barandiarán specifically notes a masculine Moon in a tale published by Vinson. This contradicts any claim that Ilargi was invariably imagined as one fixed goddess.

### Lunar calendar myth

**[SR]** Scholars have reconstructed an archaic lunar calendar from *il-* compounds, month names, and the Basque weekday series. Some schemes divide the lunation into phases or interpret *aste* “week” as a lunar unit. The language unquestionably preserves lunar terminology; the exact architecture of a pre-Roman Basque calendar remains disputed. Modern claims that a specific weekday was Ilargi’s ancient festival or that the week began at a particular phase exceed the secure evidence.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Direct cult evidence

- **Temples:** **[Ø]** None identifiable.
- **Altars or dedications:** **[Ø]** None naming Ilargi.
- **Priesthood:** **[Ø]** None documented.
- **Cult statue:** **[Ø]** None documented.
- **Theophoric names:** **[Ø]** No ancient personal name securely derived from Ilargi.
- **Cult center:** **[Ø]** None.
- **Institutional festival:** **[Ø]** None demonstrably dedicated to her.

### Household and popular practices

**Greeting and augury [F].** Addressing the rising Moon and asking for good news is the clearest devotional-looking behavior. It may be prayer, greeting, rhyme, or augural dialogue; the source does not establish an officiant or sacrifice.

**Lunar scheduling [F].** Sowing, transplanting, pruning, felling timber, slaughtering animals, harvesting, storage, and medicinal activities could be timed by lunar phases. These practices display confidence in lunar influence but need not imply worship of a personal goddess.

**Friday [F/SR].** Some Basque agricultural records treat Friday as a day when the Moon is stationary or dead and therefore a substitute for waning Moon. Folklore also associates Friday with witches and magical operations. Claims that Friday was a formally consecrated “day of Ilargi” are reconstructions, not securely attested cult calendars.

### Strabo’s full-moon rite

**[EA, not attributable to Ilargi].** Strabo describes sacrifice, household choral dancing, and an all-night vigil at full moon among Celtiberians and northern neighbors. Modern writers have attached the passage to Basques, Cantabrians, or an Iberian lunar cult. Problems remain:

- the deity is grammatically called a “nameless god,” not the Moon;
- the full moon may mark time rather than identify the recipient;
- ethnic coverage is imprecise;
- the rite predates the recorded phrase *Ilargi amandrea* by nearly two millennia.

A recent treatment of writing and indigenous religion emphasizes the ambiguity of Strabo’s “godlessness” and nameless gods rather than accepting a straightforward named Moon cult ([Ca’ Foscari volume](https://edizionicafoscari.unive.it/media/pdf/books/978-88-6969-932-0/978-88-6969-932-0_Yj3TXnG.pdf)).

### Human sacrifice, sexuality, and violence

**[Ø for Ilargi].** No source documents human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual violence, warfare, or coercive cult activity specifically for Ilargi Amandrea.

Strabo elsewhere reports human and horse sacrifice to a war god among northern Iberians. That passage must not be reassigned to Ilargi merely because another passage mentions full-moon timing.

### Suppression and Christianization

**[F/SY]** The Saint Clare adaptation is affirmative evidence of Christian reuse of a lunar formula. It shows transformation, not necessarily an organized campaign against an Ilargi cult.

**[Ø]** No ecclesiastical decree, inquisitorial prosecution, conversion narrative, destroyed shrine, or demonization can be tied specifically to Ilargi. General histories of Christianization or Basque witch trials do not supply that missing connection.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Pre-Roman and Roman periods

**[EA, contextual; Ø for the name].** Northern Iberia possessed lunar observance and astral symbolism, most famously Strabo’s full-moon rite. Roman-period indigenous religion is richly epigraphic in some regions, but no inscription names Ilargi. It is therefore impossible to place her alongside Endovelicus or Ataecina as an epigraphically demonstrated pre-Roman deity.

The dossier brief’s umbrella “Iberian (Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician, pre-Roman) pantheon” is itself misleading. These regions did not form one demonstrated pantheon, and *Ilargi* is specifically Basque vocabulary. Asturian, Cantabrian, Galician, Celtiberian, and Lusitanian lunar evidence supplies comparison, not additional attestations of Ilargi.

### Medieval period

**[EA, linguistic context; Ø for cult].** Basque remained largely oral. No medieval manuscript securely preserves *Ilargi amandrea*. Christian Basque terminology and calendrical systems developed, but the record does not permit a continuous institutional history of an Ilargi cult.

### Sixteenth to nineteenth centuries

**[EA/F].** The word *ilargi* enters printed documentation. Rural lunar lore continued in sayings, labor practices, songs, and stories later collected by folklorists.

### c. 1880–1936: collection and reconstruction

**[F/LS].** Vinson, Azkue, Barandiarán, and Caro Baroja recorded living traditions and sought pre-Christian survivals. Their work saved crucial evidence but also encouraged readers to turn scattered customs into a reconstructed “ancient Basque religion.”

### Mid- to late twentieth century: canonization as mythology

**[LS].** Dictionaries and surveys established the now-familiar system: Mother Earth, daughters Sun and Moon, Mari, nature spirits, and other beings. This is the period in which Ilargi becomes a stable encyclopedia entry rather than merely the Moon addressed in several local traditions.

### Late twentieth century to present

**[MA].** Ilargi becomes:

- a Basque feminine given name;
- a figure in children’s literature and songs;
- a subject of contemporary illustration;
- an emblem of language and regional identity;
- a goddess in neopagan and feminist-spirituality discourse;
- an entry in international lists of lunar deities.

There is no reliable census of worshippers and no evidence of an unbroken priestly or congregational cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, education, and music

**[MA]** Modern Basque children’s books and songs reuse the traditional greeting. Ene Kantak’s song “Ilargi Amandrea,” for example, addresses Moon as a changing light in the sky and links her explicitly to month names. This is creative continuity, not an ancient hymn ([Ene Kantak](https://enekantak.org/letrak/astonauta/)).

The 1996 *Ilargian ditarean* uses the greeting as a literary motif. Modern poems likewise portray Ilargi as queen, princess, companion, or guide—attributes not documented in the early folklore.

### Visual culture

**[MA/MI]** Contemporary artists supply female human form, crescent crowns, silver colors, star fields, occult sigils, and individualized personalities. None is inherited canonical iconography. Websites presenting such images without dating should be read as modern mythopoesis.

### Neopaganism and esotericism

**[MA]** Ilargi appears in modern pagan lists and reconstructed Basque cosmologies as a lunar goddess associated with intuition, cycles, women, fertility, death, or magic. These categories frequently derive from international Goddess spirituality, Wicca, Jungian symbolism, or generalized lunar correspondences rather than documented Basque sources.

**[Ø]** No evidence was found for Ilargi in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, the classical ceremonial grimoires, or historical chaos-magic texts. Claims of an ancient Ilargi sigil, planetary seal, tarot correspondence, crystal, incense, or initiatory mystery are modern inventions unless separately sourced.

### National and political uses

**[MA]** The feminine Sun–Moon–Earth scheme has been used to describe Basque culture as originally matriarchal, pre-Indo-European, egalitarian, or uniquely nature-centered. These may function as modern identity narratives, but linguistic survival and female celestial grammar do not independently prove prehistoric matriarchy. Assertions that a continuous Stone Age worldview survives intact into modern Basque folklore go beyond the evidence.

### Commercial use

**[MA]** *Ilargi* is used as a personal name and in brands, artworks, publications, music, and software. Such use attests the modern cultural attractiveness of the word, not worship.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Survivalism

Barandiarán’s generation often treated contemporary rural customs as “survivals” of ancient religion. His 1923 article itself says the tradition “undoubtedly” recalled ancient astral cult. Yet he could be cautious—regarding Mari, for example, he warned that identifying a surviving folklore figure as an ancient deity required reserve. The same caution should govern Ilargi.

The central methodological problem is the long evidentiary gap:

> Roman-era lunar observance → sixteenth-century lunar vocabulary → nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore.

Continuity is possible, but each arrow is an inference rather than a chain of dated documents.

### Etymology

The “moon/month-light” and “light of the dead” explanations remain the most important dispute. The first rests on comparative internal morphology and related calendar vocabulary; the second draws strength from cultural associations but risks folk etymology. Modern pages routinely report “light of the dead” as certain when the specialist history shows it is contested.

### Lunar calendar

Caro Baroja gave the most influential study of ancient Basque religion and calendar. The existence of rich lunar vocabulary is secure; reconstructions of an exact indigenous week or cult schedule are not. A modern ethnolinguistic review warns that explanations of Basque month names often enter conjectural territory ([Euskaltzaindia journal](https://euskera-ikerketa.euskaltzaindia.eus/index.php/euskera/eu/article/view/303)).

### Matriarchy and primordial antiquity

The progression “feminine Moon → mother goddess → matriarchal society → Paleolithic continuity” is not demonstrated. Basque has no grammatical gender in the Indo-European sense, while folklore can nevertheless personify bodies as female. Archaeological astral symbols lack labels. Broad comparativism cannot fill those gaps.

### “Iberian pantheon”

The term collapses distinct peoples, languages, periods, and evidentiary systems:

- Basque folklore supplies *Ilargi*.
- Celtiberian evidence supplies Strabo’s nameless full-moon rite.
- Asturian-Cantabrian and Galician folklore contains lunar beliefs but not the Basque name.
- Lusitanian epigraphy supplies other named gods.
- Roman *interpretatio* created still other equations.

No source makes Ilargi a deity jointly worshipped across all those regions.

### State of the archive

The core evidence survives primarily in:

- Barandiarán’s articles, field collections, and dictionaries;
- the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* tradition;
- Azkue’s dictionaries and *Euskalerriaren Yakintza*;
- Caro Baroja’s religious and calendrical studies;
- modern ethnographic atlases;
- Basque historical and etymological dictionaries.

The evidence is abundant for lunar vocabulary and custom but sparse for a discrete divine personality.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Ilargi Amandrea is securely attested in pre-Roman inscriptions.” **False: [Ø].**
- “Strabo names Ilargi.” **False.**
- “All northern Iberians shared one Moon goddess.” **Unsupported.**
- “*Ilargi* certainly means light of the dead.” **Disputed.**
- “Friday was certainly her ancient holy day.” **Reconstruction.**
- “She had temples, priestesses, sacrifices, a sigil, and fixed sacred correspondences.” **No evidence found.**
- “Ama Lur, Mari, Eguzki, and Ilargi formed a canonical ancient pantheon.” **Modern systematization.**
- “Female lunar personification proves prehistoric Basque matriarchy.” **Unsupported inference.**

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the evidentiary record |
|---|---|
| c. 1st century BCE–1st century CE | **[EA, contextual]** Strabo records sacrifice, household dancing, and an all-night vigil at full moon among Celtiberians and northern neighbors; neither the deity nor Ilargi is named. |
| Roman period | **[Ø]** Indigenous altars document numerous Hispanic gods, but none securely names Ilargi. |
| Middle Ages | **[Ø]** No known medieval Ilargi cult text, image, temple, or dedication. |
| 1545 | **[EA, lexical]** Sixteenth-century literary horizon for *ilargi*; Trask dates the word to this year. |
| 19th century | **[LS/SR]** Astarloa, Van Eys, Vinson, and others reconstruct calendars, astral religion, and etymologies. |
| Late 19th–early 20th century | **[F]** Folklorists collect Moon tales, vocabulary, sayings, and agricultural customs. |
| 1923 | **[F]** Barandiarán publishes *Ilargi amandrea* and the Ataun–Ormaiztegi greeting, noting feminine and masculine variants and Christian transfer. |
| 1928 | **[SR]** Uhlenbeck publishes “Quelques observations sur le mot *ilargi*,” favoring “light of the dead.” |
| 1920s–1940s | **[F/LS]** Azkue and Caro Baroja assemble dialect vocabulary, calendar data, and folk practice. |
| 1960 | **[LS]** Barandiarán’s *Mitología vasca* provides an influential synthesis. |
| 1972 | **[LS]** *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca* consolidates Ilargi within a reference pantheon. |
| 1980s–1990s | **[SR/MA]** Structural, symbolic, and comparative interpretations emphasize female-centered Basque cosmology; children’s and popular literature expands Ilargi’s narrative presence. |
| 1996 | **[MA]** *Ilargian ditarean* reuses the traditional greeting in children’s fiction. |
| 1999 | **[MA]** Elhuyar publishes “Ilargi Amandrea,” combining Basque mythological framing with discussion of modern claims about lunar influence ([Elhuyar](https://zientzia.eus/artikuluak/ilargi-amandrea/)). |
| 21st century | **[MA/MI]** Websites, art, music, tourism, neopaganism, and international deity lists present Ilargi as a fully individualized Moon goddess; unsupported ancient attributes proliferate. |
| 2026 | The evidence still supports a feminine and addressable Moon in Basque folklore, but not a reconstructable ancient institutional cult or a pan-Iberian goddess. |

## 11. Sources

The decisive primary or near-primary witnesses are Strabo’s *Geography* 3.4.16 and Barandiarán’s 1923 publication. Azkue, Caro Baroja, Barandiarán’s later syntheses, Trask, Michelena, and regional ethnographic collections supply the linguistic and folklore framework. Popular and educational sources are used only to document modern reception, not ancient fact.
