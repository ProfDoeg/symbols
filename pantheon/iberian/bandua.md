# Bandua: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Indigenous western-Hispanic deity—grammatically and iconographically disputed as male, female, a male–female divine pair, or a divine title rather than one person. Most Roman-period epithets are masculine, but the sole inscribed image is female. “God” is therefore conventional, not certain.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Latin-script votive inscriptions of the **first century CE**. An altar from A Capela at Catoira, Galicia, is assigned to that century from the dedicator’s Roman nomenclature; an altar from the Orgaz–Sonseca area of Toledo is palaeographically dated to the middle or second half of the first century. No securely pre-Roman text names Bandua.  
> **Documentary limits:** Nearly everything securely known comes from approximately thirty to fifty short inscriptions, depending on admissibility and grouping. There is no surviving Bandua hymn, myth, scripture, priestly text, temple archive, or ancient narrative. Assertions that Bandua was specifically a war god, a water god, a road god, or the sovereign head of a Celtic pantheon are modern reconstructions, not information stated by the inscriptions.

## Evidence labels

- **[Secure Roman-period attestation]** An ancient inscription, inscribed object, or archaeological context whose reading and attribution are reasonably accepted.
- **[Disputed attestation]** An ancient object or text whose reading, provenance, authenticity, or divine identification is contested.
- **[Ancient comparative evidence]** An ancient source relevant to the religious environment but not naming Bandua.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference from distribution, grammar, comparative linguistics, or iconography.
- **[Folklore hypothesis]** A proposed survival in later custom without demonstrable continuity.
- **[Modern religious reception]** A contemporary devotional or reconstructionist use.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim supplied by recent writers without corresponding ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]** The entity conventionally called *Bandua* was invoked in western and north-western Hispania, particularly inland Gallaecia and Lusitania: modern Galicia, northern and central Portugal, and Extremadura, with outlying testimony in the Spanish Meseta. Worshippers normally placed the name, often followed by a local epithet, on small stone altars and recorded fulfillment of a vow in Latin formulas such as *votum solvit libens merito*—“gladly and deservedly fulfilled the vow.”

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Most researchers regard the epithets as names of settlements, *castella*, *vici*, peoples, or districts. Bandua consequently appears to have functioned as the supernatural protector or personification of numerous small rural communities. This is the strongest functional reconstruction because:

1. numerous epithets contain Celtic-looking settlement-forming elements, especially *-briga/-brigo*;
2. each epithet is ordinarily localized;
3. the dedications are overwhelmingly rural rather than municipal;
4. Romanized cities more commonly assigned comparable tutelary functions to *Genii*, *Lares*, Jupiter, or Fortuna.

This does **not** prove that all inscriptions refer to one pan-regional god. “Bandua” may have named one deity in multiple local forms, a divine class or title, or related local beings subsumed by modern cataloguers.

### The problem of the nominative

The ancient records overwhelmingly preserve dative forms—forms meaning “to/for [the deity]”—not an unambiguous nominative. Attested or reported spellings include:

- *Bandue*
- *Bandui*
- *Bandu*
- *Bandi*
- *Bande*
- *Bandei*
- *Bandiae*
- *Banduae*
- occasionally reconstructed or doubtful forms such as *Banda*, *Bandus*, *Bandua*, *Bane*, and *Bandonga*

Consequently:

- **Bandua** is a convenient conventional headword.
- **Bandue/Bandi** more closely reproduce the dominant epigraphic forms.
- **Bandus** is a linguistically reconstructed masculine nominative, not the universally preserved ancient name.
- **Bandua** may be a feminine derivative, a Latinized spelling, or an erroneous back-formation from a dative.

The Spanish Society for the Sciences of Religions deliberately named its journal *Bandue*, emphasizing the deity’s unresolved, connective, and liminal identity.

### Gender

Three models remain in circulation.

1. **Predominantly male deity.**  
   **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Most qualifying epithets—*Aetobrigus/-o, Roudaecus/-o, Vordeaecus/-o*, and similar forms—are morphologically masculine. Olivares Pedreño therefore speaks of a male protector comparable functionally to Mars or a civic Genius.

2. **Female deity.**  
   **[Disputed attestation]** The silver-gilt patera inscribed *Band(i) Araugel(ensi)* depicts a female figure with mural crown, cornucopia, and libation vessel. If inscription and figure identify one another, this is direct evidence for a female Bandua or for Bandua represented through Roman Tutela/Fortuna iconography.

3. **A masculine–feminine pair or variable gender.**  
   **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Patrizia de Bernardo Stempel proposes *Bandus* and its feminine counterpart *Bandua*, comparing Celtic divine pairs such as Bormanos/Bormana. Another possibility is that indigenous morphology did not map neatly onto Latin grammatical gender, allowing different Roman visual translations of the same divine power.

The evidence cannot decide securely among these. Calling Bandua categorically “a goddess” or “a male war god” suppresses genuine ambiguity.

### Etymologies

#### “Binding, bond, command”

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** From the nineteenth century onward, Holder, Leite de Vasconcelos, Michelena, García, de Hoz, and others connected *Band-* with Proto-Indo-European *bʰendʰ-* or a related “bind” root. Cognates cited include Sanskrit *bandha-* “bond,” Germanic words represented by English *bind/bond*, and forms conveying attachment or association.

Religious interpretations built upon that comparison include:

- “the binder”;
- deity of bonds, oaths, social cohesion, or political union;
- ruler or commander;
- protector of the group bound together.

The linguistic comparison is possible, but the inscriptions never call Bandua an oath-god or narrate an act of binding. Those functions are secondary deductions.

#### “Passage, going, ford, route”

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Blanca María Prósper objected that the binding theory inadequately explains the *u*-stem morphology. She derives reconstructed *bandu-* through an action noun related to Indo-European *gʷem-tu-*—“going, passage.” On this interpretation the deity was connected with access, passageways, paths, or protected movement of persons and property.

This has encouraged comparisons with fords and roads. It remains disputed: no inscription explicitly identifies Bandua as a god “of roads,” and most securely recognizable epithets look communal rather than topographical in that narrow sense.

#### Other proposals

- **Water god:** inferred from finds near rivers or baths, proposed hydronymic parallels, and comparison with the Illyrian Bindus/Neptune. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The geographic associations are inconsistent and cannot independently establish the domain.
- **Generic word for “deity”:** some scholars have asked whether *bandua/bandi* was a common religious noun followed by the actual local divine name. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The recurrence of the root over a coherent region makes the proposal worth considering, but it is not proven.
- **War-band deity:** derives the name from bands or warrior fraternities and combines this with the Mars hypothesis. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction, frequently popularized]** Neither an ancient narrative nor an unambiguous martial image confirms it.

### Ethnic classification

“Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician, pre-Roman Iberian pantheon” is too broad if treated as one pantheon.

- **[Secure attestation]** Bandua belongs primarily to the epigraphic religious world of inland Gallaecia and Lusitania under Roman rule.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The language of the names is normally classified as Celtic or western Indo-European, with the Lusitanian language’s exact position still debated.
- Direct attribution to the historical **Basques** is unsupported.
- Clear Bandua inscriptions are not a defining feature of **Cantabria**.
- Asturian connections belong to the wider north-western distribution debate, not to a surviving Asturian narrative tradition.
- “Iberian” is accurate geographically but misleading linguistically if it implies the non-Indo-European Iberian language of eastern Spain.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

No Iron Age inscription securely naming Bandua has been identified. Archaeological occupation of some associated settlements predates Rome, but an old settlement does not date a reused or later altar.

#### A Capela, Catoira

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]** An altar from the A Capela site records a dedication to Bandua and closes with the conventional fulfillment formula. The dedicator is reconstructed as *Ti(berius) Cla(udius) Ci(...)*. His nomenclature has been used to assign the monument to the first century CE. Its archaeological setting appears to combine funerary and religious activity.

What it establishes: worship under an indigenous divine name in the first century.  
What it does not establish: pre-Roman antiquity, mythology, gender, or function.

#### Orgaz–Sonseca district

**[Secure or probable Roman-period attestation]** A badly worn altar attributed to Bandua is dated palaeographically to the middle or second half of the first century CE. Because the reading is damaged, A Capela is the cleaner basis for stating the earliest secure horizon.

### Nature of the corpus

There is no literary “Bandua corpus.” The corpus is epigraphic:

- granite or other stone votive altars;
- a Lusitanian-language sacrificial inscription from Arronches;
- the inscribed silver-gilt patera;
- several lost, reused, or heavily damaged monuments;
- no undisputed theophoric personal-name series;
- no ancient author who narrates Bandua’s deeds.

Most monuments belong broadly to the first through third centuries CE. Precise dates are unusual because indigenous votive altars rarely name consuls or emperors.

### Representative inscriptions

#### Bandui Isibraiegui, Bemposta

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]**

> *Paull[inus?] … Maelonis f(ilius) Bandui Isi[…]*  
> “Paullinus … son of Maelo, [dedicated this] to Bandui Isi[…].”

The granite altar was reused in Bemposta’s medieval tower. The surviving text demonstrates a *Bandui* form and an indigenous patronymic beside a Roman personal name.

#### Bandu Picio, São Vicente da Beira

**[Secure Roman-period attestation; c. 150–200 CE]**

> *Bandu Picio / Pellico / Tangini f(ilius) / v(otum) l(ibens) s(olvit)*  
> “To Bandu Picius, Pellico son of Tanginus gladly fulfilled his vow.”

This is among the more securely dated examples. Its indigenous personal names show that Latin epigraphic practice did not require cultural or onomastic Romanization.

#### Bandue Cadogo, Mixós

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]**

> *Bandue / Cadogo / Ter[enti]a / Rufina / v(otum) l(ibens) m(erito) s(olvit)*  
> “To Bandue Cadogus, Terentia Rufina gladly and deservedly fulfilled her vow.”

A female dedicator is explicitly recorded. This says nothing by itself about the deity’s gender.

#### Bandue Roudaeco, Trujillo territory

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]**

> *… [Do]qiri f(ilius) Bandue Roudaeco animo libens votum solvit*  
> “… son of Doquirus willingly fulfilled his vow to Bandue Roudaecus.”

The altar was found near the Villavieja fortified settlement at the confluence of the Tozo and Almonte. Other *Roudaeco* dedications around Madroñera and Coria suggest either multiple worshipping groups carrying a communal cult name or a wider territorial cult. The latter is not automatic.

#### Roudeaeco/Vordeaeco, Coria

**[Disputed restoration; monument lost]**

> *[Ban(due) Rou]/deaeco …*  
> or *[Ban(due) Vor]/deaeco …*

The altar was reportedly discovered in 1835 during construction of the seminary at Coria and is now lost. Its first lines permit competing restorations. It must not be counted as a certain occurrence of either epithet without qualification.

#### Bandua Lansbricae, Eiras

**[Secure altar; disputed reading]**

The altar associated with the territory of San Cibrao de Las is variously read *Bandua Lansbricae, Lanobrigae, Lambricae,* or *Alaniobricae*. The strongest common inference is a connection with the local fortified community. The altar’s later reuse as a Christian object or building stone illustrates survival of the monument, not necessarily survival of worship.

#### Arronches sacrificial inscription

**[Secure ancient text; linguistic interpretation disputed]**

The long Lusitanian inscription includes:

> *IFATE X BANDI HARACVI AV…*

It is one of the rare indigenous-language religious texts from western Hispania and appears to enumerate offerings or sacrificial quantities to several divine recipients. *Bandi Haracui* is commonly identified as a Bandua form with epithet, but segmentation, syntax, and the exact offering remain debated. It proves that the divine name was not confined to ordinary Latin votive prose. Its Roman-period date does not make it pre-conquest.

#### The inscribed patera

**[Disputed attestation]**

The inscription is read approximately:

> *Band(i) Arugel(ensi)*  
> “To Bandi, [deity] of the Araugelenses.”

Its relationship to the central female figure makes it the most important—and most dangerous—piece for reconstruction because its provenance is unknown and readings differ.

### Ancient literary evidence

Strabo, *Geography* 3.3.6–7, describes north-western Iberian peoples sacrificing goats, captives, and horses to a deity translated as Ares and performing martial dances. **[Ancient comparative evidence]** Strabo does **not** name Bandua. Identifying his Ares with Bandua is a modern inference and competes with identification as Cosus or another unnamed martial deity.

No passage in Pliny, Ptolemy, Silius Italicus, or another surviving Roman author securely names Bandua. The silence is material: Bandua is reconstructed from local practice rather than literary mythology.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Community protection

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction, strongly supported]** Bandua’s most defensible domain is protection or personification of small communities. Olivares Pedreño observed that Bandua’s epithets frequently correspond to rural settlements, while Roman *Genii* more often protect municipalities or *civitates*. The distribution suggests an indigenous institution translated into Roman votive language.

Protection need not mean warfare. A tutelary being could guard territory, fertility, collective welfare, boundaries, political cohesion, or military security simultaneously.

### Warfare

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** Arguments for a war god are:

- the disputed association with Mars;
- comparison between Bandua’s communal epithets and local forms of Mars in Gaul;
- dedications by military personnel;
- the possibility that protection of fortified communities included war;
- etymological association with warrior “bands.”

Counter-evidence and cautions:

- the supposed direct Mars inscription is textually and possibly materially insecure;
- soldiers dedicated to many non-martial gods;
- the patera’s image is Fortuna/Tutela-like, not martial;
- no weapon, armor, battle scene, or martial epithet is securely attached to Bandua;
- Strabo’s Ares is anonymous.

“War god” should therefore be presented as an influential hypothesis, not an established ancient classification.

### Passage and roads

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Prósper’s *gʷem-tu-* derivation supports a deity of passage, access, fords, or protected circulation. Some finds near crossings and watercourses can be fitted to this model. No dedicatory text states the function, so linguistic acceptance would not by itself determine the complete cult.

### Water and healing

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Water associations have been proposed from:

- river-confluence findspots;
- finds near baths;
- the landscape represented on the patera;
- proposed hydronymic comparisons;
- comparison with Illyrian Bindus, equated with Neptune.

The thesis lacks a consistent epigraphic formula such as *fontanus*, *aquaticus*, or *salutaris*. Bandua cannot securely be classified as a water or healing deity.

### The Bandua patera

The object often called the “Bandua patera” was in the former Calzadilla Collection of Badajoz and is associated today with Badajoz’s archaeological collections. References to a “Madrid patera” conflate its publication, exhibition, or cataloguing history with its reported location. Its original archaeological provenance is unknown; São Cosmado near Mangualde and an unspecified place in Cáceres have both been proposed.

The silver or silver-gilt dish depicts:

- a centrally enthroned or standing female figure;
- a mural or turreted crown;
- a cornucopia in the left hand;
- a patera or offering dish in the right;
- altars, several with flames;
- rocks, vegetation, and perhaps a spring or nymph scene.

**[Secure iconographic description; disputed divine identification]** These are Roman attributes of communal abundance and protection familiar from Fortuna, Tutela, Tyche, and city personifications. If *Band(i) Arugel(ensi)* captions the principal figure, Bandua was here represented as the Fortuna/Tutela of the Araugelenses. Alternatively, the image may be a Roman personification receiving or accompanying the offering, while the inscription names a different recipient. Unknown provenance prevents contextual confirmation.

This is the only substantial image plausibly identified by Bandua’s name. Generic warrior figurines, horned figures, and castro statues should not be labeled Bandua without inscriptions.

### Alleged symbols and correspondences

No ancient source assigns Bandua a standard:

- weapon;
- sacred animal;
- sacred plant;
- color;
- number;
- weekday;
- planet or star;
- seasonal festival;
- magical seal.

Modern claims assigning wolves, bulls, ravens, swords, knots, red colors, Mars the planet, or particular calendar dates are **[Modern invention]** unless explicitly described as new devotional symbolism.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

There is no attested parent, child, sibling, consort, birth narrative, rival, servant, mount, or divine genealogy for Bandua.

The apparent masculine–feminine pair *Bandus/Bandua* is a **linguistic reconstruction**, not a myth in which two divine persons marry or interact. Modern pairings of Bandua with Cosus, Nabia, Reue, Trebaruna, Ataecina, Endovelicus, or other western-Hispanic deities are pantheon-building exercises unless based on one monument or ritual text.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

No ancient Bandua myth or narrative cycle survives.

This absence should not be filled by assembling Strabo, Irish warrior-band traditions, Roman Mars mythology, and Galician folklore into a synthetic tale. Those materials may illuminate scholarly comparison, but they are not a Bandua story.

### The “warrior protector” narrative

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Popular summaries sometimes describe Bandua as a warrior who defended castros, led war-bands, or fought beside Mars. The reconstruction combines:

- local protective epithets;
- the uncertain Mars inscription;
- Strabo’s anonymous Ares;
- comparative Celtic warrior associations;
- modern etymological interpretation.

No ancient source supplies an episode, character, outcome, or ritual drama.

### The “binder of the tribe” narrative

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The binding etymology has generated an implicit mythic function: Bandua binds the community together, enforces oaths, and protects collective order. This is a plausible theological interpretation but is absent from the surviving inscriptions.

### The “guardian of passages” narrative

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Prósper’s etymology permits a deity guarding roads, crossings, or access into protected territory. Again, no narrative survives.

### The bull of Saint Mark

Some scholars have compared Bandua with western Iberian customs in which a bull was led, controlled, blessed, or ritually incorporated into celebrations of Saint Mark.

**[Folklore hypothesis]** Geographic overlap and the protective/martial reconstruction motivated the comparison. There is no continuous chain of documentation from Roman Bandua worship to the Christian bull rite. The custom is evidence for later regional ritual life, not automatically a fossilized Bandua festival.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult geography

Bandua dedications concentrate in:

- Ourense and southern inland Galicia;
- Trás-os-Montes and the Beiras;
- the Guarda–Castelo Branco–Idanha region;
- northern Extremadura, particularly Cáceres province;
- scattered western and central outliers.

The distribution is inland and often associated with castro territories or small rural communities. It is not evenly “pan-Celtic,” Iberian, or Hispanic.

Olivares Pedreño argued that Bandua, Reue, Arentius, Quangeius, Nabia, and related theonyms help define a Lusitanian religious zone. Other researchers stress the cult’s Gallaecian and northern forms or propose north-to-south diffusion. Since Roman provincial borders do not necessarily reproduce indigenous ethnic boundaries, “Lusitanian” and “Gallaecian” are overlapping geographical-historical labels here.

### Sanctuaries and temples

No monumental temple can securely be identified as a dedicated temple of Bandua. Altars have been found:

- near castros;
- in rural settlements;
- at river confluences;
- reused in churches, chapels, towers, walls, or baptismal furnishings;
- without archaeological context.

An altar’s reuse in a church does not show that the church replaced a Bandua temple on the same spot. It may have been transported as convenient stone.

San Cibrao de Las and its surroundings provide an important settlement context for the *Lansbricae* dedication, but there is no surviving architectural complex conclusively labeled “the temple of Bandua.”

### Vows and offerings

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]** The dominant ritual was the vow. Formulas include:

- *votum solvit*—“fulfilled the vow”;
- *votum libens solvit*—“gladly fulfilled the vow”;
- *votum libens merito solvit*—“gladly and deservedly fulfilled the vow”;
- *sacrum*—“sacred [to].”

These formulas show reciprocal petition and fulfillment but do not disclose the requested favor. Dedicants included men and women, persons with indigenous names, persons with Roman names, and at least some people connected with the military.

### Sacrifice

**[Secure but incompletely understood]** The Arronches inscription appears to prescribe or record offerings, potentially including animal victims and numbers, to several deities including *Bandi Haracui*. Because Lusitanian vocabulary is only partially understood, the exact victim or commodity assigned to Bandi must be left open.

**Human sacrifice:** Strabo says some north-western Iberians sacrificed captives to an Ares-like deity. **[Ancient comparative evidence]** He does not name Bandua. No Bandua altar, sanctuary, or osteological assemblage securely documents human sacrifice to this deity. “Human sacrifice to Bandua” is therefore unsupported.

### Priesthood

No priest of Bandua is securely titled in the corpus. No evidence establishes:

- hereditary clergy;
- priestesses;
- temple estates;
- initiation;
- cult colleges;
- oracular offices;
- tax privileges.

Ritual specialists may have existed, but nothing specifically Bandua-related survives.

### Festivals and calendar

No festival name or date survives. The bull of Saint Mark hypothesis does not establish a Roman Bandua calendar.

### Politics and economics

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A deity tied to a *vicus*, *castellum*, or local group could express community identity under Roman government. Latin votive forms let worshippers maintain an indigenous divine name while participating in empire-wide epigraphic practice. This represents cultural translation, not simple replacement.

No Bandua temple treasury, landholding, market privilege, or state subsidy is documented.

### Theophoric names

No large, securely agreed corpus of personal names derived from Bandua demonstrates naming popularity. Superficial similarities to modern words or place-names are insufficient without historical phonology.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the Roman conquest

**[Unestablished]** The indigenous morphology and local epithets make a pre-conquest origin plausible. Nevertheless, no securely dated pre-Roman inscription names Bandua. Claims about Bronze Age or early Iron Age worship are retrospective reconstruction.

### First century CE: earliest epigraphic visibility

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]** Bandua enters the recoverable record through Latin-script altars. The first-century A Capela and Sonseca-area evidence indicates that the cult was already geographically dispersed when it became epigraphically visible.

Roman literacy did not create the deity, but it created the surviving documentary record.

### Second–third centuries CE: main votive horizon

**[Secure Roman-period attestation]** Most dedications are conventionally placed in the high Roman imperial period. The São Vicente da Beira altar dated about 150–200 CE is a representative securely bounded example.

During this phase:

- the divine root appeared in multiple case-forms;
- local epithets proliferated;
- Roman vow formulas became standard;
- indigenous patronymics remained common;
- iconographic translation through Tutela/Fortuna was possible;
- military or Mars associations may have developed, although their direct documentation is weak.

Calling this the cult’s “peak” means only that most surviving inscriptions probably belong here; it does not measure attendance or belief.

### Late antiquity

No inscription securely records a formal suppression of Bandua’s cult. As civic and rural pagan cults lost public and material support under Christian rule, production of such votive altars ceased. That broad transformation is historically documented for Hispania, but no edict or martyr narrative names Bandua.

Reused altars in churches show material appropriation. They do not by themselves distinguish deliberate triumphal reuse from practical masonry.

### Medieval period

There is no securely documented medieval worship under Bandua’s name.

A proposed connection between Bandua/Bande and Saint Torquatus or Santa Comba de Bande is **[Folklore/Christianization hypothesis]** based on geography, place-name resemblance, and perceived functional continuity. The toponym *Bande* has not been securely shown to derive from the deity, and ecclesiastical traditions do not name Bandua as their predecessor.

### Early modern antiquarian rediscovery

Ancient altars were copied, transported, or reported by clerics and antiquarians. Loss of provenance began early: stones served as construction material, and some records survive only in drawings or transcriptions. The Coria monument, discovered in 1835 and now lost, exemplifies the problem.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries: creation of the modern “Bandua”

Comparative philology and national archaeology assembled scattered spellings into a single dossier. Key stages include:

- Emil Hübner’s incorporation of western Hispanic inscriptions into the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*;
- José Leite de Vasconcelos’s *Religiões da Lusitânia*;
- José María Blázquez’s catalogues of pre-Roman Hispanic religion;
- linguistic work by Tovar, Michelena, Untermann, de Hoz, Búa, Prósper, and de Bernardo Stempel;
- Juan Carlos Olivares Pedreño’s distributional and functional studies.

This scholarship made the deity historically visible but also encouraged a unified personality beyond what the fragmented local evidence necessarily warrants.

### Present

Bandua is now:

- an object of epigraphic and linguistic research;
- the namesake of the journal *Bandue*;
- a figure in Galician and Portuguese cultural history;
- worshipped or invoked by some Lusitanian, Iberian-polytheist, Celtic-neopagan, and eclectic practitioners.

No reliable census establishes the scale of contemporary worship, and no demonstrable continuous community links it institutionally to antiquity. Modern worship is revival or reconstruction.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and public heritage

Bandua appears in museum displays, archaeological guides, regional-history works, and digital epigraphic databases. The inscribed patera has disproportionately shaped public imagery because it supplies a visually complete deity where the altar corpus supplies only names.

The journal *Bandue* consciously uses the deity’s uncertain identity as an emblem for interdisciplinary religious studies.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

**[Modern religious reception]** Contemporary practitioners variously approach Bandua as:

- a male protector or war god;
- a female tutelary goddess;
- a gender-variable or nonbinary deity;
- guardian of home, village, borders, paths, and travelers;
- deity of bonds, oaths, and community;
- local counterpart or companion of Cosus or Mars.

These positions selectively reproduce modern scholarly hypotheses. Modern prayers, feast days, offerings, gender pronouns, divine marriages, and “personal revelations” document present religion, not ancient practice.

### Occult and esoteric literature

No significant Renaissance grimoire, Hermetic corpus, Golden Dawn paper, Theosophical scripture, or early modern demonology is known to feature Bandua. Claims that Bandua possesses an ancient sigil, planetary seal, angelic correspondence, tarot card, or established ceremonial invocation are **[Modern invention]** unless their recent author is named.

### Literature, games, and commercial use

Bandua has a modest presence in regional fantasy, online deity lists, role-playing contexts, music, and heritage branding. These uses often standardize the deity as a Celtic warrior or armored goddess. No single film, novel, comic, or game has yet established a universally dominant modern image comparable to the mass-media reception of major classical gods.

### National and political appropriation

Bandua has been used in narratives of:

- Celtic Galicia;
- Lusitanian Portuguese ancestry;
- pan-Iberian pagan heritage;
- regional cultural distinctiveness.

Such uses are modern identity projects. The ancient dedications do not attest a unified Galician, Portuguese, Spanish, Basque, or pan-Celtic nation.

No well-documented large extremist movement appears to have made Bandua a central symbol. General warnings about ethnic neopagan appropriation should not be converted into an allegation specific to this deity without evidence.

### Modern misinformation

Common unsupported or overstated claims include:

- “Bandua was certainly the supreme Celtic god of Iberia.”
- “Bandua was the Iberian Mars.”
- “Bandua’s wife/husband was Cosus.”
- “Bandua commanded warrior brotherhoods.”
- “Human victims were sacrificed specifically to Bandua.”
- “Bandua’s festival occurred on Saint Mark’s Day.”
- “Bandua was continuously worshipped in Basque or Galician folklore.”
- “The Madrid patera proves a universal female form.”
- “Bandua had a canonical sword, wolf, bull, planet, color, rune, or sigil.”

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### From catalogue to interpretation

Early scholarship concentrated on reading and collecting inscriptions. Because many monuments were weathered, lost, reused, or known only through antiquarian copies, editorial mistakes could create gods and epithets that later scholarship removed.

The notorious *Bandueaetobrigus* problem illustrates this. Letters were combined and expanded in ways that created a supposedly complete divine name; later reassessment showed that the reading and line division had been mishandled. Modern catalogues must therefore record alternative readings rather than silently inheriting an attractive form.

### Major interpretive schools

#### Bandua as communal protector

Associated especially with Olivares Pedreño. The argument rests on local epithets, rural findspots, and comparison with *Genii*, *Lares*, and Gallic local Mars cults. It is currently the most evidence-sensitive functional model.

#### Bandua as war god/Mars equivalent

Supported by the communal-defender model, military dedicants, regional comparison, and the disputed Mars text. Its weakness is the lack of securely martial iconography or direct interpretative formula such as *Mars Bandua*.

#### Bandua as sovereign deity

Dumézilian or comparativist readings have assigned Bandua a sovereign, ordering, or protective “first” or “second” function. Such systems can organize the evidence but risk forcing fragmentary local cults into a reconstructed Indo-European theology.

#### Bandua as deity of passages

Prósper’s linguistic reconstruction treats morphology as decisive and interprets the deity through “going/passage.” It challenges older “binding” etymologies but remains functionally underdetermined by the inscriptions.

#### Bandua as water deity

Based upon landscape, finds, proposed name parallels, and the patera. The evidence is geographically suggestive but not systematic.

#### Bandus and Bandua as a divine pair

De Bernardo Stempel’s morphological and comparative model explains masculine epithets alongside a female image. Critics may answer that Roman iconography can feminize an abstract community-protector without requiring a separate ancient goddess.

#### “Bandua” as divine title

This explains why many local complements look more individuating than the repeated root. Yet the corpus does not contain an indigenous glossary establishing “bandua” as the common noun “god.”

### The Mars inscription

The reading *deo vexillor(um) Martis socio Banduae* has often been treated as decisive proof that Bandua was Mars’s associate. More recent scholarship warns that the monument’s authenticity, syntax, or reading is doubtful. Even accepted literally, *socio Banduae* could identify the otherwise named recipient as “companion of Bandua,” distinguishing Bandua from the Mars-associated god rather than equating them.

Thus:

- **Direct equation “Bandua = Mars”: not securely attested.**
- **Association with Mars: possible but textually disputed.**
- **Comparable protective/martial function: scholarly inference.**

### The patera debate

The central questions are:

1. Is *Band(i) Arugel(ensi)* the correct reading?
2. Does the inscription label the female figure?
3. Is the figure the indigenous deity, Roman Tutela/Fortuna, or the community itself?
4. Where was the vessel found?
5. Does one local representation determine the deity’s gender throughout the entire corpus?

Blázquez emphasized Fortuna/Tutela iconography. Later discussions used the object either to defend a female Bandua or to propose a divine pair. Scholars favoring a male god stress masculine epithets and the capacity of Roman allegory to represent a protected community as female.

### Corpus size

Counts vary because scholars disagree about:

- whether fragmentary *Band-* forms belong;
- whether epithet-only dedications should be included;
- whether similarly spelled forms are one deity;
- whether dubious or lost monuments count;
- whether the Arronches form belongs in the same group.

Published totals range from roughly thirty secure name-bearing inscriptions to about forty-eight or more when doubtful, reconstructed, and epithet-only cases are included. Any exact total requires a stated inclusion policy.

### Geographic-origin debate

One model proposes north-to-south spread because *Bandue* appears more characteristic north of the Douro and *Bandi/Bandei* farther south, while southern epithet-only dedications may represent later localization. Another treats Bandua as specifically Lusitanian. Still others describe a shared Gallaecian-Lusitanian religious formation.

The current map records surviving monuments under Rome, not necessarily the place where the cult began.

### Relationship to regional folklore scholarship

The works of Barandiarán, Azkue, Caro Baroja, Satrústegi, Hartsuaga, Cabal, Aurelio de Llano, Manuel Llano, García-Lomas, Risco, Cuba and Reigosa, and Lisón Tolosana are indispensable for documented Basque, Asturian, Cantabrian, and Galician folklore. They do not provide an independent ancient Bandua narrative or establish continuous worship. Importing lamias, mouras, xanas, household spirits, or Wild Hunt traditions into Bandua’s dossier solely because they occur in the same modern regions would flatten distinct bodies of evidence.

### State of the physical evidence

The monuments are dispersed among:

- local and regional museums;
- church and municipal property;
- archaeological collections in Spain and Portugal;
- in-situ secondary reuse;
- lost antiquarian records.

Major research tools include:

- *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* II;
- *Hispania Epigraphica* and HEpOnline;
- the Epigraphic Database Heidelberg;
- Epigraphik-Datenbank Clauss-Slaby;
- regional Portuguese corpora;
- UC3M’s DEpHis database.

Digital records improve comparison but reproduce editorial judgments; photographs and autopsy remain preferable where readings are contested.

## 11. Sources

### Primary evidence and corpora

- The Bandua corpus consists principally of CIL II, *Hispania Epigraphica*, HEpOnline, AE, EDH, EDCS, regional Portuguese corpora, and the DEpHis database.
