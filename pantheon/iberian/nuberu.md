# The Nuberu: Research Dossier

**Class:** An anthropomorphic storm-being in Asturian folklore—variously classified by informants and collectors as a *genio*, supernatural man, cloud-demon, or member of a race of weather-workers. The cognate Galician *nubeiro* is often a living human sorcerer rather than a spirit; Basque *Odei/Hodei* is a personified storm cloud or atmospheric *genio*. “God” is principally a modern popular and revivalist classification, not one securely documented in the early record.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The creature is documented lexicographically by the later nineteenth century: Gumersindo Laverde and an Asturian dictionary dated 1880 define *nuberus* as imaginary little men who conduct storm clouds and cause thunderstorms. The first extensive, securely dated narrative dossier is Aurelio de Llano Roza de Ampudia’s *Del folklore asturiano: mitos, supersticiones, costumbres* (Madrid, 1922), pp. 6–15, based partly on named oral informants interviewed in 1921. No ancient or medieval inscription, classical text, temple, image, or theophoric personal name securely attests a being called Nuberu.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Documentary identification

The Asturian Nuberu controls or conducts clouds, thunderstorms, rain, and especially crop-destroying hail. He can direct a storm toward one holding and away from another. Oral descriptions make him a frightening, usually bearded man, commonly dark or blackened by weather, wearing an exceptionally broad hat; some accounts make him enormous, others small. He travels on or within clouds but can be stranded on the ground. Hospitality shown to him may earn protection, warning, treasure, or supernatural transportation; disrespect can bring targeted hail.

This is not a single canonical divinity. The record consists of related vernacular conceptions:

- **Asturias and adjoining León:** an embodied supernatural storm-conductor, sometimes singular, sometimes a plurality or “race.”
- **Cantabria:** *nubero*, primarily a weather-being in twentieth-century regional folklore compilations.
- **Galicia:** *nubeiro*, *tronante*, *tronador*, *tempestario*, or *escolar*—often a living male magician believed able to ascend into clouds and direct hail.
- **Basque Country:** *Odei/Hodei*, literally “cloud,” which can mean thunder, the storm cloud, or its personalized agent. It is a functional analogue, not an attested alternate name of the Asturian being.
- **Wider European comparison:** medieval *tempestarii*, alleged human weather-magicians, furnish a useful comparative category but are not proof that an identical Nuberu tradition existed in early medieval Asturias.

### Names and linguistic forms

The Asturian lexical corpus records:

- **Nuberu**
- **Ñuberu / ñubiru**
- **Nubeiru**
- **Nubiru**
- **Nubero**
- **Nubleiru**
- Galician-Asturian plural **nubeiros**
- **Reñubeiru/Reñuberu**, associated with *reñube* or storm cloud
- Narrative personal names **Xuan Cabritu**, **Xuan Cabrita**, and **Xuan Orito**
- **Escolar**, shared with Galician weather-magician traditions

The *Diccionario General de la Lengua Asturiana* distinguishes several senses: “storm cloud”; a dark cloud containing a malign being; a storm-governing mythic figure; a cloud-demon; an untidy, ugly, angry, or frightening person; and, regionally, juniper. Its citations preserve an important early definition from Laverde and the 1880 dictionary: “*casta de hombrecillos imaginarios que se supone que son conductores de los nublados y autores de las tronadas*”—a race of imaginary little men believed to conduct storm clouds and cause thunderstorms. This plural, diminutive conception conflicts with the gigantic or corpulent singular man in later descriptions and should not be harmonized away. [DGLA](https://mas.lne.es/diccionario/palabra/54078)

**Etymology—secure:** *nuberu/nubeiro/nubero* is transparently connected with Romance *nube*, “cloud,” ultimately Latin *nūbēs*. The suffix makes an agentive or associated-being formation: approximately “cloud-worker/cloud-man.”

**Etymology—uncertain:** The precise history of every dialect form, especially *reñubeiru*, requires dialectological treatment; an ancient divine name is unnecessary to explain the word.

**Modern speculative comparisons:** Thor/Donar, Odin/Wotan, Taranis, Jupiter, the Lusitanian-Gallaecian god Reue, and Egyptian or “Libyan-Asiatic” gods have all been proposed as predecessors or equivalents. These comparisons rest on shared storm functions, appearance, supposed one-eyedness, or the story’s “Egypt” motif—not on a historical name equation, inscription, or demonstrable textual transmission. Aurelio de Llano himself rejected identification with human *tempestarii* and speculated that the Nuberu’s origin might lie among African or Asian gods because tales located him in “Egito.” That is an early twentieth-century diffusionist hypothesis, not primary evidence of origin; the relevant passage is in his 1922 book. [De Llano, *Del folklore asturiano*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9e/Del_folklore_asturiano-_mitos%2C_supersticiones%2C_costumbres_%28IA_delfolkloreastur00llan%29.pdf)

There is no securely attested ancient epithet, liturgical title, original script, or pre-Roman name for the Nuberu.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Early lexical evidence

Gumersindo Laverde’s nineteenth-century material and the *Diccionario asturianu* of 1880 supply the earliest presently verifiable lexical attestations found in this investigation. Their definition already joins cloud-control with imaginary anthropomorphic beings. Junquera Huergo’s nineteenth-century dictionary records *nuberu/ñuberu* in the meteorological sense “storm cloud,” but the available citation does not independently establish a fully personified being.

This matters: the word for a storm cloud may precede and coexist with personification. A dictionary entry cannot establish prehistoric antiquity.

### Aurelio de Llano, 1922

The first substantial documentary corpus is Aurelio de Llano Roza de Ampudia, *Del folklore asturiano: mitos, supersticiones, costumbres* (Madrid, 1922), with a prologue by Ramón Menéndez Pidal. The Nuberu occupies the opening section, pp. 6–15. De Llano gives descriptions, stories, counter-storm practices, local variants, and named testimony. One unusually valuable datum is attributed to Hermenegildo Rodríguez, aged seventy-four, of San Martín de Luiña, recorded 12 March 1921: in a forceful priestly exorcism of the storm, two men supposedly had to hold the priest so the *Nubeiru* would not carry him away.

The work’s strengths are proximity to living oral tradition and attribution of some testimony. Its limitations are equally important: de Llano recasts oral material in literary prose and interprets it through the diffusionist and comparative mythology characteristic of his era.

### *Cuentos asturianos*, 1925

De Llano’s *Cuentos asturianos recogidos de la tradición oral* (Madrid, 1925) belongs to the same collection project. Its table of contents includes “Dios te ayude, Xuan,” and its stories preserve the structure of reciprocal hospitality that became central to later retellings. [Google Books bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Cuentos_asturianos.html?hl=en&id=EBSgAAAAMAAJ&output=html_text)

### Constantino Cabal

Cabal’s volumes on Asturian mythology—beginning in the 1920s—systematized regional folklore and explicitly warned against fashionable attempts to “orientalize” western European myths. His treatment is secondary systematization rather than an ancient source. Later popular works frequently repeat Cabal and de Llano without distinguishing their field reports from their theories.

### Cantabrian corpus

Manuel Llano and Adriano García-Lomas are the principal twentieth-century compilers invoked for Cantabrian myth and custom. García-Lomas’s *Mitología y supersticiones de Cantabria* appeared in the 1960s. The Cantabrian *Nubero* belongs to this modern ethnographic-literary corpus; no securely dated ancient Cantabrian source for him was found. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books?hl=es&id=a39WAAAAMAAJ)

### Galician corpus

Vicente Martínez-Risco’s “Los ‘nubeiros’ o tempestarios de Galicia,” *Boletín del Museo Arqueológico Provincial de Orense* 1 (1943), pp. 71–91, is the essential study. Risco treats *nubeiros* as male weather-sorcerers who raise storms, ascend to clouds, and cast hail on another’s crops. He catalogues bells, written and spoken prayers, blessed candles, magical defenses, and priestly exorcism. A contemporary bibliographic notice summarizes them as “hechiceros, siempre varones.” [Bibliographic notice](https://www.icatm.net/bibliotecabalmes/sites/default/files/public/analecta/AST_17/AST_17_217.pdf)

Risco’s decisive qualification is that, in the fuller accounts, the Galician *nubeiro/tronador/escolar* is “*un ser humano viviente, y no un espíritu*”—a living human, not a spirit. [Textual extract](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/2507483.pdf)

Later Galician dictionaries and story collections, including the work of Xosé Miranda, Antonio Reigosa, and Xoán R. Cuba, synthesize this material for modern audiences. These are valuable reception sources but should not be retrojected into antiquity.

### Basque comparative corpus

Barandiarán’s *Mitología vasca* records *Odei/Hodei* as thunder, storm cloud, or the personal agent of thunder. Rural expressions include:

- *Odeiak jo* — “Odei/the thunderer has struck”
- *Odei asarrea* — “angry thunder”
- *Odeia dator* — “the storm cloud is coming”
- *Odei dago* — “there is a storm in the air”

At Gesalibar near Arrasate/Mondragón, *Odeiaixe* means the east or southeast “wind of Odei,” believed to bring storms. [Auñamendi summary](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/odei/ar-99036/)

Barandiarán’s writing sometimes presents Odei as a storm divinity that supplanted an older celestial god, Ortzi. That is a scholarly reconstruction, not a surviving narrative of an actual divine succession. Later claims that Odei killed or defeated Ortzi lack secure grounding in the cited ethnographic corpus and are best classified as modern elaboration.

### Earlier analogues, not Nuberu attestations

- **Agobard of Lyon, *De grandine et tonitruis*, c. 815–816:** denounces belief in *tempestarii* who made hail and in cloud-ships from Magonia whose crews collected damaged crops. This proves an early medieval western European weather-magic complex, not an Asturian Nuberu. [Critical-discussion summary](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=7778332)
- **Carrio slate, Asturias, probably early medieval:** a Christian-magical Latin text invokes Michael, Gabriel, Oriel, Raphael, Ananiel, and Marmoniel, commanding the beings who “hold the clouds” to drive them away from the estate of Auriolus. It documents local protection against storms, but it neither names nor depicts Nuberu. [Carrio discussion and translation](https://astures.es/la-pizarra-de-carrio-entre-la-magia-y-el-cristianismo-proteccion-contra-las-tormentas-en-la-asturias-altomedieval/)
- **Strabo, *Geography* III, and Latin epigraphy:** neither provides an identifiable Nuberu. Indigenous gods such as Endovelicus, Ataecina, Reue, and regional Mars forms are real epigraphic subjects, but no inscription equates any with Nuberu.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**Folklore, securely collected in the twentieth century:** clouds, rain, thunder, lightning, fog, snow, hail, crop fertility, and crop destruction. His most characteristic act is selective discharge: he can spare one person’s field while ruining a hostile neighbor’s.

Rain makes him ambivalent. Agricultural communities need the water he brings but fear hail. “Master of clouds and storms” is therefore an accurate modern summary, provided it is not mistaken for an ancient cult title.

### Physical form

Collected and synthesized variants include:

- a tall, corpulent, aged man;
- alternatively, a little man or race of little men;
- thick or grey beard;
- exceptionally wide mouth and large teeth;
- fiery or bright eyes;
- large or pointed ears;
- dark or scorched face and clothing;
- animal skins, sometimes specifically goatskins;
- black cloak;
- broad-brimmed, sometimes pointed black hat.

The broad hat is the most stable modern identifying attribute. It visually assimilates the Nuberu to a rural traveler, beggar, or feared weather-worker. The elaborate combination of every trait into one fixed costume is a modern composite.

### Vehicle and characteristic actions

His “vehicle” is the cloud itself. He walks, rides, sits on, or works within it. Some accounts say he transports great spherical thunders and crashes them together so that water and hail fall. Others make him shear goats before a storm; every hailstone is then said to contain a goat hair. He may fall with lightning, descend through mist, or lose his cloud and become stranded.

No securely identified ancient, medieval, or early-modern image represents him. Modern drawings and sculptures derive from literary descriptions.

### Sacred correspondences

There are no securely attested sacred animals, colors, numbers, weekdays, celestial bodies, temples, or priestly insignia. Goats, goat hair, the cloud, hailstones, black clothing, and the broad hat are narrative attributes, not evidence of formal cult symbolism.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

No collected Asturian source supplies parents, spouse, children, siblings, or a stable divine hierarchy. The Nuberu may belong to a plurality of *nuberus*, but this is class-membership, not genealogy.

The Galician *nubeiro* is commonly human and therefore outside divine genealogy altogether. The Basque Odei has sometimes been made a son of Mari or assigned good and evil offspring in modern systematizations, but those family trees cannot be transferred to the Asturian Nuberu.

### Documented interactions with named divine beings

Because the corpus is sparse, the negative finding is substantial:

- **No named god or goddess — no interaction attested.** The securely collected Asturian Nuberu narratives pair him with unnamed farmers, boys, priests, spouses, kings, and travelers, not named deities.
- **Christian God — antagonistic ritual relation, not a narrative meeting.** Storm charms assert that God is stronger than the threatening cloud or weather-agent; nineteenth- and twentieth-century oral ritual tradition. No scene depicts God meeting Nuberu.
- **Saint Barbara — invoked against thunder and hail.** Christian protective prayer in Galician, Asturian, and wider Iberian storm practice; no direct narrative encounter with Nuberu is documented.
- **Saint Bartholomew — invoked in some Asturian anti-storm prayers.** Again, a ritual opposition, not a narrated meeting.
- **Archangel Michael and the angels named on the Carrio slate — not an interaction with Nuberu.** They restrain clouds in an early medieval charm; connecting their adversary to Nuberu is modern inference.
- **Odei/Hodei — no interaction attested.** A Basque analogue, not a sibling, opponent, or alternate identity in primary tradition.
- **Ortzi/Urtzi — no interaction attested with Nuberu.** Modern comparativists sometimes group them as sky powers.
- **Thor/Donar, Odin/Wotan, Taranis, Jupiter, Reue — no interaction or historical equation attested.** These are scholarly or popular comparisons based on function.
- **Mari — no interaction with Nuberu attested.** Mari’s control of storms belongs to Basque tradition; the beings should not be merged.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The stranded cloud-rider and the hospitable farmer

**Tier:** Folklore securely collected by the early twentieth century; later canonicalized in regional retellings.

After watering the land, the Nuberu pauses on a rock or descends to inspect his work. His clouds escape, leaving him stranded overnight. A farmer or farm family gives the frightening stranger food and lodging when others would reject him. At departure he reveals, directly or indirectly, that he is no ordinary traveler. In versions involving his distant homeland he says that, if the host ever reaches “Egito,” “Brita,” or another remote place, he should ask for Xuan Cabritu.

Years later the farmer is conscripted, travels on crusade, is shipwrecked, enslaved, or otherwise taken abroad. He finds Xuan Cabritu living in a remote or aerial country. The Nuberu recognizes his former host and learns that the man’s wife, believing him dead, is about to remarry. He provides a flying goat or transports the farmer through the air on a cloud, returning him in time to interrupt the wedding. Hospitality is rewarded across an enormous distance.

Variants alter:

- how the Nuberu reaches the ground—lost cloud, rest on a rock, or fall with a lightning bolt;
- who helps him—one farmer, two boys, or a household;
- the distant place—Egypt, “Egito,” Brita, or a fantastic cloud-country;
- the vehicle of return—cloud or male goat;
- the reward—safe return, treasure, advance warning, or protection from hail.

The tale combines the international “supernatural stranger rewards hospitality” pattern with a localized weather-being. “Egypt” need not preserve Egyptian religion; it can simply mean an unimaginably remote country, and Cabal already warned against treating it as proof of oriental origin. [De Llano text and modern source discussion](https://astures.es/mitologia-asturiana-el-nuberu/)

### The insulted or unthanked Nuberu

**Tier:** Regional folklore.

The Nuberu appears as a beggar, traveler, or weathered old man. A landowner fails to greet him, mocks him, refuses food, or neglects to express gratitude for good rain. The Nuberu ascends to a high point, mounts a cloud, and hails specifically on that owner’s fields while sparing neighboring land.

The story explains the apparently arbitrary borders of hail damage and enforces hospitality and humility.

### The Nuberu brought down by priestly conjuration

**Tier:** Christianized folklore, documented in the modern ethnographic record.

A priest reads from ritual books, blesses the fields, or commands the cloud. Bells are rung. The Nuberu may flee, fall to earth, or threaten to carry the priest away. In the 1921 San Martín de Luiña testimony, two men must supposedly restrain the priest during the confrontation.

This is not simply “the Church suppressing a pagan god.” It is an internally Christian rural system in which ordained prayer, blessed substances, saints, bells, and quasi-magical gestures coexist.

### Galician *nubeiros* and *tronantes*

**Tier:** Galician folklore, chiefly documented by Risco in 1943.

A living man reputed to possess occult knowledge enters or mounts a cloud and directs hail against a target. *Escolar* probably reflects lore about learned magicians or “schoolmen”; Risco connects the figure with the popular category of *nigromante*. Villagers may believe a fallen stranger is the *tronante* and assault him. One tale describes a handsome Castilian stranger, caught during a hailstorm, whom villagers beat as the storm-maker.

Galician belief therefore moves between sorcerer, social outsider, stranger, and supernatural weather-agent. It is related to, but not identical with, the Asturian cloud-rider.

### Rains of animals

**Tier:** Later regional folklore synthesis.

Some accounts say the Nuberu’s “flock” falls from the clouds as frogs, snakes, slugs, or salamanders. This belongs to widespread European explanations of anomalous animal rains. Its exact occurrence in the earliest Nuberu collections requires verification before it is treated as central.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No established cult

There is no evidence for a Nuberu temple, sanctuary, altar, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, votive inscription, theophoric name, sacred image, or institutional cult. Calling him a “pre-Roman god” with an organized cult goes beyond the evidence.

The material instead documents **defensive weather ritual**.

### Bells and hail

Church bells were rung to warn of and repel storms. Across northern Iberia this was called *tentenublo*, *tente-nube*, *toque de nublo*, or a storm/conjuration peal. A Basque-Alavese formula runs:

> *Tente nube, tente tú;  
> si eres agua, ven acá;  
> si eres piedra, vete allá…*

“Hold, cloud, hold; if you are rain, come here; if you are hail, go elsewhere.” In some communities such rites were performed daily between the feasts of the Invention of the Cross on 3 May and the Exaltation of the Cross on 14 September. Priests faced the fields, recited conjurations, sprinkled holy water toward the cardinal directions, and bells supplied the community’s audible protective boundary. [Atlas Etnográfico de Vasconia](https://atlasetnografico.labayru.eus/index.php/Toques_de_campanas_y_conjuros_contras_las_tormentas._Traslado_de_imagenes_de_santos)

The Spanish Ministry of Culture notes storm-warning and protective functions within the wider national tradition of manual bell-ringing. [Spanish Ministry of Culture](https://www.portalinmaterial.cultura.gob.es/en/pci-nacional/toquecampanas.html)

It is not possible to isolate one function everywhere: bells alerted villagers, summoned collective action, accompanied Christian prayer, and were also believed to disperse hail or frighten its supernatural maker.

### Other protections

Documented or recurrently reported measures include:

- burning blessed laurel;
- lighting candles blessed during Holy Week, including the Asturian *tinieblina*;
- placing oven implements in a cross;
- setting an axe, knife, scissors, or sickle with its edge toward the cloud, symbolically cutting it;
- putting an axe blade upward in a doorway;
- invoking Saint Barbara and other saints;
- priestly reading and exorcism;
- sprinkling holy water;
- directing the dangerous cloud toward uncultivated ground, mountains, or sea.

At Saint-Esteben in Labourd, Basque households reportedly lit blessed candles, burned laurel, or placed an axe edge-up at the threshold against Odei. [Basque ritual summary](https://mitologiadevasconia.amaroa.com/personajes-mitologicos-de-vasconia/hodei)

These practices are documented in Christian-era communities. Claims that each is a direct survival from a pre-Roman Nuberu cult are reconstructions, not demonstrated historical continuity.

### Sacrifice, sexuality, and violence

No human or animal sacrifice, temple prostitution, sexual rite, or formal cult violence is attested for Nuberu.

Violence does occur in Galician stories about suspected *tronantes*: an outsider can be beaten because villagers believe he caused the hail. Such tales are evidence for feared weather-witchcraft and xenophobic identification of strangers, though each reported historical incident requires independent verification.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the nineteenth century: analogues without the name

Early medieval western Europe knew accusations against weather-magicians. Agobard’s c. 815–816 polemic records *tempestarii* and cloud sailors from Magonia. The Carrio slate shows early medieval Asturians using a Christian-magical text to protect property from clouds. These establish a long history of personified or manipulable storms but do not attest Nuberu.

The Iberian pre-Roman religious record contains real storm- or sky-god candidates, but no inscription names Nuberu or equates him with Reue, Taranis, Jupiter, or any other deity. Endovelicus and Ataecina are irrelevant to the identification except as demonstrations of what genuine ancient attestation looks like: named dedications on durable material.

### Nineteenth century: lexical emergence

Asturian dictionaries record the term first as a cloud and then as imaginary storm-driving men. This is the first secure stage in the Nuberu’s recoverable history.

### 1920s: ethnographic canonization

De Llano’s 1922 and 1925 works transform local oral variants into a regional literary corpus. Cabal and other writers organize these materials under “Asturian mythology.” At this point the Nuberu becomes a named mythological character available to readers outside the communities from which the stories came.

### 1943 onward: Galician comparison

Risco’s study places Galician *nubeiros* within comparative weather-magic. His insistence that fuller Galician accounts describe living men rather than spirits resists the modern tendency to make every regional analogue a god.

### Mid-twentieth century: regional pantheons

García-Lomas and other compilers give Cantabrian beings a more systematic mythological presentation. Barandiarán and successors organize Basque atmospheric terms and beliefs around Odei, Ortzi, Mari, and related figures. These projects preserved much oral knowledge but could also make fluid vocabulary appear like a fixed pantheon.

### Late twentieth century: cultural emblem

During the Asturian *Surdimientu* and democratic transition, “Nuberu” became a positive marker of Asturian language and identity. Chus Pedro and Manolo Peñayos formed the musical group Nuberu in 1976; its first album, *Asturies, ayeri y güei*, appeared in 1978. The group joined folk instrumentation, especially gaita, with social and political song in Asturian. [Contemporary retrospective](https://elpais.com/diario/1980/10/17/cultura/340585213_850215.html)

This political-cultural appropriation is well documented, but it is not extremist appropriation. No reliable evidence encountered in this investigation establishes significant fascist, racialist, or violent extremist use of the Nuberu.

### Twenty-first century

The Nuberu persists in heritage websites, children’s stories, tourist literature, illustration, theater, festivals, and online “Celtic mythology” lists. Some modern pagans and artists address him as an ancient storm god or pray for favorable weather. Such practice is a modern revival or personal spirituality; continuity of Nuberu worship from antiquity has not been demonstrated.

A digital cooperative in Asturias uses **Nuberu** as a cloud-computing pun, showing commercial and civic rather than religious reception. [Nuberu Digital](https://nuberu.org/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### The “ancient Celtic storm god”

**Tier:** Modern scholarly-popular reconstruction or invention.

Modern summaries commonly label Nuberu an Asturian/Cantabrian/Galician “Celtic god,” sometimes equating him with Taranis, Thor, Odin, or Reue. Similarity of function can justify comparison; it cannot establish descent. There is no name continuity, cult inscription, iconographic sequence, or medieval Asturian text connecting them.

### Fixed iconography

The standardized modern figure—huge, one-eyed, black-cloaked, goatskin-clad, fiery-eyed, pointed-eared, broad-hatted—combines traits that vary among sources. The image is useful artistic shorthand but is not an ancient canon.

### Neopaganism

Web-based pagan and regionalist writing sometimes treats the Nuberu as a still-worshipped nature spirit or ancient god who can receive prayer. One modern arts feature explicitly speaks of people who “still believe in ancient gods and spirits of the Earth” praying to him for gentle rain. That is evidence of contemporary reception, not proof of an unbroken cult. [Modern artistic reception](https://fusionasturias.com/cultura/mitologia/el-nuberu-y-pena-mea.htm)

No reliable census, organization, temple, or continuous initiatory lineage was located.

### Music and regional politics

The band Nuberu is the most consequential modern appropriation. Founded in the mining region during Spain’s democratic transition, it used Asturian language, labor themes, folk music, and regional consciousness. The name turns the feared storm-being into an emblem of cultural force and resurgence.

### Occultism and esotericism

No significant role for Nuberu was found in Renaissance hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or traditional grimoire literature. Claims that place him inside a universal occult pantheon are modern additions.

### Literature, games, and commerce

The being appears in children’s adaptations, fantasy lists, tourism, illustration, educational stories, and locally themed media. These works have amplified the “storm lord” image, often omitting the Galician human-sorcerer form and the absence of ancient evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is the Nuberu a god?

The evidence supports several emic classifications: storm cloud, spirit, demon, supernatural man, race of little weather-men, or human magician. “God” is poorly supported by the earliest Nuberu documentation. It belongs mainly to twentieth- and twenty-first-century systematization.

### Pre-Roman survival or modern folklore figure?

Two positions must be distinguished:

1. **Continuity hypothesis:** northern Iberian storm lore, medieval weather magic, anti-hail ritual, and ancient storm gods may preserve a very old religious substrate.
2. **Documentary minimum:** the named Nuberu is securely attested only in modern-era folklore and lexicography. Shared function cannot bridge the evidentiary gap.

The second position is methodologically firmer. It does not imply that nineteenth-century speakers invented the being; it means the earlier history cannot presently be demonstrated.

### Nuberu and *tempestarii*

De Llano argued that the supernatural Nuberu was not simply the human *tempestarius*. Risco’s Galician evidence, however, makes *nubeiro* precisely a living weather-sorcerer. The disagreement is regional and classificatory rather than something to resolve into one answer.

Agobard offers a striking parallel—weather-magicians cooperating with cloud sailors to steal storm-damaged crops—but no direct transmission to Asturias has been established.

### “Egypt” and oriental origin

De Llano treated the Nuberu’s residence in Egypt as important and speculated about African or Asian origins. Cabal cautioned that scholars of the period habitually orientalized western European myths. Modern analysis should treat “Egypt” as an element in the collected tale, while the origin theory remains unproven.

### Basque equation

Odei is linguistically the “cloud” and can be personified as the thunderer. This is a strong structural comparison with *Nuberu*, also named from “cloud.” Nevertheless:

- the names are independent formations in unrelated language families;
- no source says Nuberu and Odei are one being;
- Odei often remains the storm phenomenon itself;
- Basque cosmology connects storms with Mari and subterranean origins in ways absent from the Asturian corpus.

Hartsuaga’s comparison emphasizes that in Basque mythology storm clouds, whirlwinds, sun, and moon emerge from the Earth; this complicates the modern image of a simple Indo-European male sky god. [Hartsuaga discussion](https://euskerarenjatorria.eus/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/AMA-edo-AMA.pdf)

### Ortzi problem

The twelfth-century pilgrim Aymeric Picaud wrote *et Deum vocant Urcia*—“and they call God Urcia.” Barandiarán noted that Picaud may have misunderstood a term related to *ortzi*, “sky/thunder,” though the root survives in meteorological vocabulary. Thus “Ortzi the ancient Basque sky god” is possible reconstruction, not an uncontested fact. [Discussion quoting Barandiarán](https://www.bizkaia.eus/fitxategiak/04/ondarea/Kobie/PDF/5/Kobie_17_web.pdf)

Claims that Odei literally supplanted or killed Ortzi are late systematization unless an independent traditional text can be produced.

### State of the evidence

The principal evidence survives in:

- nineteenth-century Asturian lexicographic manuscripts and dictionaries;
- early twentieth-century printed folklore collections;
- oral-history and sound archives;
- regional ethnographic periodicals;
- later dictionaries and cultural encyclopedias;
- the Carrio slate as contextual, not Nuberu-specific, evidence.

The lack of an ancient inscription or image is not an accidental minor gap: it defines what can responsibly be claimed.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Pre-Roman and Roman antiquity | Northern and western Iberia possess numerous locally named gods in Latin inscriptions, but none securely identifies Nuberu. |
| c. 815–816 | Agobard of Lyon’s *De grandine et tonitruis* denounces *tempestarii* and cloud-ships from Magonia—comparative evidence for weather-magic, not a Nuberu attestation. |
| Early medieval period | The Carrio slate in Asturias invokes named angels to restrain clouds and divert them from an estate; no Nuberu is named. |
| Twelfth century | Aymeric Picaud writes that Basques call God *Urcia*; its relationship to *Ortzi* and an older sky divinity remains debated. |
| Nineteenth century | Junquera Huergo records *nuberu/ñuberu* as a storm-cloud term. |
| By 1880 | Asturian lexicography defines *nuberus* as imaginary little men who guide storm clouds and cause thunderstorms: the earliest secure personified attestation located. |
| 1921 | Aurelio de Llano records named oral testimony concerning the Nubeiru and priestly storm conjuration at San Martín de Luiña. |
| 1922 | De Llano publishes *Del folklore asturiano*, including the first extensive Nuberu dossier. |
| 1925 | De Llano publishes *Cuentos asturianos recogidos de la tradición oral*. |
| 1920s–1940s | Constantino Cabal systematizes Asturian mythology and contests facile “oriental” origin theories. |
| 1943 | Vicente Martínez-Risco publishes “Los ‘nubeiros’ o tempestarios de Galicia,” defining the fuller Galician type as a living male sorcerer rather than a spirit. |
| Mid-twentieth century | Barandiarán’s Basque synthesis makes Odei/Hodei central to comparative discussion of northern Iberian storm beings. |
| 1960s | García-Lomas’s synthesis helps canonize the Nubero within modern Cantabrian mythology. |
| 1976 | The Asturian musical group Nuberu is founded at El Entrego. |
| 1978 | Nuberu releases *Asturies, ayeri y güei*, making the name a prominent emblem of Asturian cultural and political revival. |
| Late twentieth century | Regional mythological dictionaries consolidate a composite Nuberu image and increasingly describe him as an ancient god. |
| 2002 onward | The online *Diccionario General de la Lengua Asturiana* makes dialect forms and documentary citations widely accessible. |
| 2019 | Spain recognizes manual bell-ringing as intangible cultural heritage; storm and protective ringing form part of the documented functional repertoire. |
| 2020s | Nuberu remains active in heritage presentation, art, children’s literature, regional music, digital branding, and small-scale pagan reinterpretation. No continuous ancient cult has been demonstrated. |

## 11. Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not that the Nuberu was a securely known pre-Roman god who survived unchanged. It is that northern Iberian communities possessed a durable complex of ideas about controllable storms, hostile hail, personalized clouds, weather-magicians, bells, priestly conjuration, blessed plants and objects, and supernatural reciprocity.

Within that complex, the **Asturian Nuberu** emerges clearly in nineteenth-century lexicography and early twentieth-century oral collection as a cloud-riding supernatural man or class of beings. The **Galician nubeiro** is frequently a human male magician. The **Cantabrian Nubero** is a related regional folklore figure documented through modern collectors. The **Basque Odei/Hodei** is a storm cloud or thunder-agent with close functional resemblance but no documented identity with Nuberu.

The broad-hatted old man stranded after losing his cloud, helped by a hospitable farmer and later repaying him with supernatural transportation or protection, is the Nuberu’s major narrative. Hail-averting bells and Christian-magical countermeasures are his strongest ritual context. His supposed identity with Taranis, Thor, Odin, Ortzi, Reue, or an Egyptian deity remains unproved comparative speculation. No temple, inscription, ancient image, genealogy, sacrifice, or documented interaction with another named god belongs securely to his record.
