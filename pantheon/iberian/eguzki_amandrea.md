# Eguzki Amandrea: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the securely documented record, Eguzki Amandrea is a **female-personified Sun of Basque folklore and domestic belief**—addressed as a revered female elder, holy person, or protective celestial presence. Calling her a formally organized “goddess” is a defensible modern shorthand but exceeds the evidence if it implies temples, priesthood, canonical mythology, or an ancient pan-Iberian cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** José Miguel de Barandiarán’s ethnographic article “Folklore vasco: necesidad de su estudio,” published in 1922, records Basque-language sunset salutations collected in Elosua, Soraluze/Placencia, Errigoiti/Rigoitia, and neighboring localities. The underlying material was contemporary oral folklore, not an ancient text. No Roman inscription, medieval manuscript, sanctuary, or securely identified ancient image names Eguzki Amandrea. The 1922 publication is therefore the earliest presently verifiable documentary attestation of the figure under this title.

**Central evidentiary conclusion:** The documented core consists of short oral salutations to the setting sun, its feminine or elder-person address, its journey “to its mother,” and a broader body of beliefs in sunlight as protection from nocturnal beings. The now-common narrative in which Amalur deliberately creates daughters Eguzki and Ilargi and then produces the eguzkilore is a **modern synthetic myth** assembled from related but originally separate folkloric motifs. The attempted classification of Eguzki Amandrea as a goddess of a single “Iberian (Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician, pre-Roman) pantheon” is not supported by the evidence: the name and specific persona are Basque; neighboring traditions have solar customs of their own, but no source establishes their identity with Eguzki Amandrea.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Eguzki

**Eguzki** is the usual Standard Basque word for “sun.” Dialect forms documented in dictionaries and ethnographic writing include **iguzki, iduzki, iruzki, iuzki, eguzku**, and related forms; eastern dialects also use **eki/ekhi**. These are principally common nouns and astronyms. Their occurrence alone does not prove belief in a goddess.

R. L. Trask’s etymological dictionary groups *eguzki* with the *egu-/egun* “day” complex and records the dialectal forms. The transparent modern explanation is approximately “daylight/day-sun,” with a formative element *-zki*, but historical analyses have differed over the exact segmentation. Barandiarán offered “day-light” (*egu* + a light-forming element), while other writers have connected the second component with *uzki* “opening/eye,” encouraged by the folk term **eguzkibegi**, “sun-eye.” The latter is not an established consensus etymology. Trask’s treatment is more cautious than many mythological summaries.

**Eki/Ekhi** is a lexical and dialectal name of the sun, especially in eastern varieties. Treating “Eki” as the personal name of a separate ancient deity, or as a masculine solar god opposed to a female Eguzki, is **modern systematization**, not something established by an ancient source.

### Amandrea

**Amandrea** can mean “grandmother,” but its historical and dialectal structure also permits the honorific sense “mother-lady” or “Lady Mother” (*ama* + *andrea*). This produces a real translation problem:

- **“Grandmother Sun”** reflects ordinary modern meanings of *amandre* and Barandiarán’s Spanish translation *la abuela Sol*.
- **“Lady/Mother Sun”** may better preserve its reverential use in some prayers and addresses. A 1930s philological discussion warned that *amandre(a)* in invocations need not always mean “grandmother,” comparing addresses to female saints.

The safest translation is therefore **“Grandmother/Lady Sun,”** with the ambiguity retained rather than resolved.

### Recorded names and titles

- **Eguzki Amandrea / Euzki amandria** — “Grandmother/Lady Sun”; Elosua and Soraluze sunset verse, published by Barandiarán in 1922. **[secure folklore attestation]**
- **Eguzki santu bedeinkatua** — “holy, blessed Sun”; Errigoiti/Gernika-area sunset verse, published in 1922. The Christian adjectives are integral to the collected text. **[secure syncretic folklore]**
- **Eguzkibegi / Euzkibegie** — “eye of the sunlight/sun-eye”; reported at Ataun and Berastegi for the visible solar body, as distinguished locally from *eguzki* as daylight. **[regional lexical tradition]**
- **Jainkoaren begi** — “eye of God”; another Christianized name reported at Ataun and Berastegi. **[regional Christian folklore]**
- **Santa Klara** — “Saint Clare,” used in some areas for the sun or bright weather. Its classification as a transformation of a pre-Christian Sun-lady is interpretive; its Christian form is explicit. **[regional syncretism]**
- **Iuzki/Luzki** — eastern dialect forms appearing in the Ezpeleta lamina story. **[regional lexical variant]**
- **Eki/Ekhi** — dialectal word for “sun,” not securely a distinct divine personality. **[language fact; deity status reconstructed]**

### Classification through time

1. **In the collected sayings:** a personified and sacred female/elder sun addressed directly.
2. **In Barandiarán’s syntheses:** a solar numen, commonly treated as Earth’s daughter because she returns “to her mother.”
3. **In late twentieth-century encyclopedias and art:** a member of a reconstructed Basque mythological cosmos.
4. **In contemporary popular culture:** usually a “Basque sun goddess,” daughter of Amalur and sister of Ilargi.
5. **In reconstructionist or spiritual presentations:** an object of renewed devotion. Evidence for the scale, organization, and continuity of such worship is poor.

No ancient Greek or Roman author supplies an *interpretatio graeca* or *interpretatio romana* for Eguzki. Equations with Helios, Sol, Sulis, the Norse Sól, or other female solar beings are comparative analogies, not historical identifications.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest documentary layer: Barandiarán, 1922

Barandiarán’s “Folklore vasco: necesidad de su estudio” gives the following material:

> **Euzki amandria / Juan da bere amagana. / Biar etoriko da, / Denpora ona bada.**

“Grandmother/Lady Sun has gone to her mother. She will return tomorrow, if the weather is good.”

Locations: **Elosua and Soraluze/Placencia**, Gipuzkoa. The wording is dialectal. Barandiarán presents it as a living oral formula, not a translation of an ancient hymn.

A second form, from **Errigoiti/Rigoitia** in Bizkaia, reads:

> **Eguzki santu bedeinkatue, / Zoaz zeure amagana. / Etorri zaitez biar, / Denpora ona bada.**

“Holy, blessed Sun, go to your mother. Come tomorrow, if the weather is good.”

These verses securely establish:

- direct address to the Sun;
- grammatical and social feminization through *amandrea*;
- a “mother” to whom the Sun goes at sunset;
- an expectation of return;
- Christian sacral vocabulary in at least one version.

They do **not** establish:

- the mother’s name as Amalur or Mari;
- a birth myth;
- Ilargi as a named sister;
- a priesthood or organized cult;
- prehistoric continuity;
- a story in which the Sun was created to defeat monsters;
- an ancient Iberian solar pantheon.

Barandiarán’s article also records **Ilargi Amandrea**, “Grandmother/Lady Moon,” in a children’s address at Ataun and Ormaiztegi. Parallel honorific address supports a shared mode of personifying celestial bodies, but it is not by itself a genealogy.

### Anuario de Eusko-Folklore and related field records

The *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore*, founded in 1921, is the principal documentary corpus behind later accounts. Its importance lies in locality, informant memory, and date—not in providing an ancient scripture. Early volumes were produced between 1921 and 1935; publication was interrupted by the Spanish Civil War and Barandiarán’s exile, resuming in 1955.

Material later assembled in the *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca* includes:

- sunset greetings;
- the idea that failing to greet the Sun makes her go weeping to her mother;
- sunlight disabling witches and nocturnal beings;
- the Ezpeleta lamina story;
- solar names, calendar customs, and house protections;
- the eguzkilore.

### Eguzkilore field evidence

A 1973 *Eusko-Folklore* note preserves earlier collection dates:

- **Ataun, 1917:** a wild cardoon flower fixed to the house door wards off the lightning-being and witches.
- **Osintxu, collected 1920:** the plant called *sorginbelarra*, “witch-herb,” prevents witches from entering.
- **Mendiola, c. 1920–25:** comparable flowers were nailed to house doors.
- Some villages called it **eguzkilore**, “sun-flower,” and attributed to it the Sun’s power against nocturnal beings.
- At **Idiazabal**, however, the same plant could be called **ilargilore**, “moon-flower.” This is important contrary evidence against a single exclusive solar interpretation.

The documented protective custom is therefore old enough to have been observed in the early twentieth century, but its prehistoric antiquity is unproven.

### Major later syntheses

- **José Miguel de Barandiarán, *Mitología vasca*:** first published in the twentieth century and repeatedly revised. It organizes dispersed folklore by mythological subject.
- **Barandiarán, *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca y algunas de sus fuentes* (1972):** the decisive modern reference work; later editions appeared under shortened titles.
- **Resurrección María de Azkue, *Euskalerriaren Yakintza* (1935–47):** a broad compilation of Basque traditional knowledge, songs, customs, tales, and beliefs. It is a folkloric corpus, not evidence that all entries belonged to one coherent religion.
- **Julio Caro Baroja:** treated Basque ritual and witchcraft historically and comparatively, generally with greater caution toward timeless reconstructions than popular mythology manuals.
- **José María Satrústegui:** documented Navarrese calendar ritual, solstitial songs, and local tradition.
- **Juan Inazio Hartsuaga:** reanalyzed the Eguzki material cosmologically, distinguishing two possible models—Eguzki Amandrea as a celestial traveler returning into Earth and *eguzkibegi* as the visible “eye” or aperture of a greater light. This is **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not an informant’s complete ancient cosmology.

### Absence from ancient and medieval corpora

There is no known occurrence of *Eguzki Amandrea* in:

- pre-Roman Iberian or Celtiberian writing;
- the Hand of Irulegi;
- Aquitanian or Hispano-Roman votive inscriptions;
- Strabo, Pliny, Ptolemy, or other classical ethnographers;
- medieval Basque texts;
- a surviving pagan hymn, liturgy, calendar, or law code.

The chronological gap between Roman-era religion and the collected oral material is nearly two millennia. Continuity is possible in individual motifs but cannot be demonstrated simply from Basque linguistic continuity.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Secure folklore]** Eguzki Amandrea is the setting and returning Sun: giver of daylight, marker of the boundary between day and night, and power before which nocturnal beings lose freedom or strength.

**[Later synthesis]** She is protector of human beings against the dangerous population of night.

**[Modern systematization]** She is sometimes assigned life, fertility, seasonal renewal, justice, surveillance of children, or sovereignty over a solar cult. These attributes require source-by-source verification and are not part of the 1922 core.

### Femininity

Basque nouns do not have grammatical gender in the Romance-language sense. Eguzki’s femininity therefore does not result from noun gender. It is conveyed culturally through **amandrea**, the greeting formulas, and later characterization as a daughter.

This makes the feminine personification significant, but it does not prove a prehistoric matriarchy. Claims that a female Sun demonstrates survival from “Old Europe,” an original matriarchal civilization, or Paleolithic goddess religion are **modern comparativist hypotheses**, not direct deductions from the folklore.

### The daily journey

The greeting says the Sun goes **bere amagana**, “to her mother.” Barandiarán inferred that the mother is Earth and that the Sun returns into Earth at night. This fits the visible descent below the horizon and other telluric features of Basque folklore.

The sources, however, preserve several levels:

- the verse itself names no mother;
- Barandiarán interprets the mother as Earth;
- some later informants or summaries identify her with **Andra Mari/Virgin Mary**;
- modern mythology books identify her as **Amalur**, a reified Mother Earth;
- some modern systems make **Mari** and **Amalur** interchangeable, while others distinguish them.

These should not be collapsed into a single ancient genealogy.

### Protective sunlight

In the Ezpeleta story collected and retold by Barandiarán, a shepherd takes a golden comb left beside a cave at Mondarrain. A **lamina** pursues him and nearly catches him, but the first solar ray touches his clothing. The lamina tells him, approximately, “Give thanks to the Sun,” and withdraws.

The story establishes the sunlight’s protective effect within a specific regional tale. It does not say that Eguzki fought the lamina in bodily form.

Witches and beings engaged in nocturnal activity are likewise said to become powerless, immobilized, or forced to retreat when surprised by sunlight. Hartsuaga emphasizes that this strong day/night moral opposition may have been sharpened by Christian demonization of pre-Christian beings; it is not always consistent with stories in which lamiak, Basajaun, or jentilak interact peacefully with human households at night.

### Eguzkilore

The **eguzkilore** is a dried basal flowerhead of a wild cardoon, usually identified as *Carlina acaulis* and sometimes confused in practice or literature with related *Carlina* species.

Documented functions include protection against:

- witches;
- nocturnal spirits;
- lightning and storms;
- a lightning-being;
- disease-bearing beings.

Interpretations recorded or later supplied include:

1. the flower resembles the solar disk;
2. witches mistake it for the Sun and flee;
3. a being must count its numerous bracts and is overtaken by dawn;
4. it embodies the Sun’s apotropaic power.

Only the door-hanging practice and protective attribution are securely documented in early twentieth-century fieldwork. The detailed creation myth—humans beg Amalur for help; she creates Moon, then Sun, then the eguzkilore when night beings adapt—is widespread today but was not found in the earliest dossier material. It should be labeled **modern synthetic legend unless an earlier primary collection is produced**.

The plant also had non-solar names, including *sorginbelarra*, *kardu santua*, and *ilargilore*. These variants show that its meaning was locally variable.

### Visual symbols

Circles, rosettes, disk-stelae, lauburu forms, pentagrams, monstrances, eastward orientations, and church rose windows have all been interpreted as solar symbols.

Their evidentiary status differs:

- **Eguzkilore on doors:** ethnographically attested.
- **Rosettes and disks on houses or funerary stelae:** materially attested; an intended solar meaning is plausible in some cases but cannot be assumed universally.
- **Lauburu as Eguzki’s personal emblem:** modern symbolic attribution, not an ancient labeled image.
- **Church rose windows as deliberate pagan protection:** speculative interpretation.
- **A human-shaped ancient image of Eguzki Amandrea:** none securely identified.

No color, weapon, sacred animal, canonical number, weekday, or unique cult image is securely attested.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

#### Version A: unnamed mother

The 1922 sunset verse says Eguzki goes to **her mother**, without identifying that mother. This is the earliest and most secure form. **[secure folklore]**

#### Version B: Earth as mother

Barandiarán interprets the destination as the Earth’s interior; Eguzki is consequently described as **Lurraren alaba**, “daughter of Earth.” This is a reasonable inference from the formula and cosmological setting but still an interpretation. **[modern ethnological reconstruction grounded in folklore]**

#### Version C: the Virgin Mary/Andra Mari

In some local Christian explanations, the Sun’s mother is the Virgin Mary. The Basque title **Andra Mari**, “Lady Mary,” overlaps verbally with **Mari**, the principal female numen of many Basque traditions. This produced fertile ambiguity but does not prove that the Virgin simply replaced an ancient goddess in a single datable event. **[regional syncretic folklore]**

#### Version D: Amalur, Eguzki, and Ilargi

Modern retellings typically state that **Amalur** created or bore two daughters, Eguzki and Ilargi. The early greetings support female Sun and Moon figures and a maternal relation for the Sun, but not this full family tree. **[later systematization/modern retelling]**

#### Version E: Mari as mother

Some modern accounts substitute Mari for Amalur or identify the two, making Eguzki and Ilargi daughters of Mari. Barandiarán’s collected Mari traditions do not establish this as a universal or canonical genealogy. **[modern synthesis]**

No consort, offspring, father, mount, servant, or hereditary office is securely documented for Eguzki Amandrea.

### Every documented divine or supernatural interaction

- **Earth/Lurra — Mother and nightly destination.** The Sun goes “to her mother” at sunset; Earth is Barandiarán’s identification of that mother. Source: Barandiarán, “Folklore vasco,” 1922, followed by his later dictionary. **[secure formula; mother-as-Earth is scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Virgin Mary/Andra Mari — Identified locally as the mother to whom the Sun returns.** Reported in Barandiarán’s later synthesis for Mañaria and related traditions. **[regional Christian syncretism]**
- **Ilargi/Ilargi Amandrea — Parallel female celestial elder; later represented as Eguzki’s sister.** Both receive *amandrea* salutations in Barandiarán’s 1922 article, but that source records no encounter or kinship between them. The sister relation belongs to later systematic accounts. **[parallel secure attestation; kinship later systematization]**
- **A lamina of Mondarrain — Sunlight ends her pursuit of a shepherd and forces her back to her cave.** Ezpeleta oral story, published in Barandiarán’s twentieth-century collections. **[regional folklore]**
- **Sorginak/witches — Solar light or its floral representation blocks, immobilizes, or repels them.** Door-flower reports collected at Ataun in 1917, Osintxu in 1920, and Mendiola c. 1920–25; later summaries generalize the principle. **[secure folklore]**
- **The lightning-being — Eguzkilore repels it.** Ataun report dated 1917. It is not individually named in the cited record. **[regional folklore]**
- **Gaueko — Modern summaries sometimes cast the Sun as Gaueko’s opposite.** No early narrative of a meeting, combat, kinship, or dialogue between named Eguzki Amandrea and Gaueko was located. **[modern structural interpretation; no documented interaction]**
- **Jentilak — Dawn or cockcrow ends their nocturnal activity in several legends.** These stories demonstrate a day/night boundary, but none of the located texts makes Eguzki Amandrea a speaking participant. **[folklore; direct interaction absent]**
- **Basajaun — Frequently grouped among beings affected by daylight in modern summaries, but no specific Eguzki–Basajaun episode was located in the foundational sources consulted.** **[absence of evidence]**
- **Mari — Frequently made Eguzki’s mother or identified with Earth in modern retellings, but no early collected scene of Mari and Eguzki meeting was located.** **[modern systematization; direct interaction absent]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Eguzki Amandrea possesses no surviving epic cycle comparable to those of classical literary gods. Her narrative corpus is a cluster of short formulas, explanations, and encounters.

### The sunset return

**Story:** At sunset, people greet the Sun as she leaves for her mother and ask her to return the next day in good weather. In Mañaria, failure to say goodbye could make her go away crying.

**Earliest source:** Barandiarán, 1922.

**Variants:**

- “Grandmother Sun has gone to her mother” — Elosua and Soraluze.
- “Holy, blessed Sun, go to your mother” — Errigoiti.
- simple *agur*, “farewell” — Mañaria.
- the mother interpreted as Earth, the Virgin, Andra Mari, or Mari in later accounts.

**Function:** Household acknowledgment of sunset, petition for return and favorable weather, and ritual management of the day/night boundary.

### The Sun rescues the shepherd from a lamina

**Story:** A shepherd takes a lamina’s golden comb from near the Mondarrain cave. The lamina chases him and is on the point of catching him when the first sunlight touches his clothing. She acknowledges the Sun’s intervention and returns to the cave.

**Source:** Ezpeleta oral tradition in Barandiarán’s twentieth-century synthesis.

**Tier:** **Regional folklore.**

**Function:** A supernatural chase legend explaining dawn as a limit on lamina power. The Sun operates through light, not anthropomorphic combat.

### The eguzkilore protection cycle

**Early documented form:** A dried cardoon fixed above the door protects against witches, lightning, or malign beings. Some communities call it *eguzkilore* and associate its power with the Sun.

**Expanded explanatory forms:**

- witches see it, think dawn has arrived, and withdraw;
- witches must count every bract and cannot finish before sunrise;
- Amalur creates the flower as the final defense after nocturnal beings learn to tolerate Moon and Sun.

The first form is early twentieth-century field evidence. The counting motif may be traditional but is poorly dated in the accessible sources. The full Amalur creation sequence appears principally in recent educational, tourism, commercial, and neopagan retellings and should not be backdated without a traceable field citation.

### The two daughters of Earth

Modern accounts tell of Earth as mother of Sun and Moon, both female and often sisters. The nucleus is supported indirectly:

- the Sun goes to her mother;
- Sun and Moon are both called *amandrea*;
- telluric imagery is prominent in Basque cosmological folklore.

The explicit family drama is **learned systematization**, not a verbatim ancient or early collected myth.

### Solstitial songs and fires

Solstitial fires, circular movement, invocations of the Sun, and formulas asking it to return “at a good limit/season” occur in Basque calendar folklore. A frequently repeated claim states that women at **Urdiain** circled solstitial bonfires singing to Eguzki Amandrea.

These customs may preserve a solar interpretation, but “priestesses of a pagan Sun cult” is an inference. Calendar fires can carry agricultural, purificatory, communal, and Christian St John meanings simultaneously. The exact song text and collection history should be cited before treating the Urdiain custom as direct worship of a named goddess.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### What is attested

- Spoken greetings at sunset.
- Petitions for the Sun’s return and good weather.
- Farewell gestures or *agur*.
- Solstitial fires and songs with solar interpretation.
- Protective cardoon flowers attached to doors, lintels, or stable beams.
- Solar terminology in Christianized formulas: “holy,” “blessed,” “eye of God.”
- Continuing domestic display of eguzkilore, now often decorative or identitarian.

### What is not attested

There is no secure evidence for:

- a temple dedicated to Eguzki Amandrea;
- an altar bearing her name;
- a priestly college;
- temple estates or cult taxes;
- sacrificial regulations;
- human or animal sacrifice to her;
- sacred prostitution;
- pilgrimage to her shrine;
- an oracle;
- a canonical cult statue;
- a theophoric personal name securely naming her as a deity;
- a public festival historically titled for Eguzki Amandrea.

The domestic and oral setting is not a deficient temple cult; it is the actual form in which the figure enters the documentary record.

### Sacrifice and violence

The dedicated searches found no evidence linking Eguzki Amandrea to human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual warfare, or cult violence. Strabo’s descriptions of warfare, suicide, exposure, sacrifice, and ritual among northern Iberian peoples cannot be reassigned to this figure. No relevant inscription or archaeological deposit names her.

### Material sites

No archaeological sanctuary can be identified as hers. The cited Endovelicus sanctuary at São Miguel da Mota, Ataecina’s cult in western and southern Iberia, and other Hispano-Celtic theonyms belong to distinct epigraphically attested traditions. Their existence demonstrates the variety of ancient Iberian religions, not a larger pantheon containing Eguzki Amandrea.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Pre-Roman and Roman antiquity: no direct attestation

Classical sources name or describe numerous Iberian peoples and religious practices, while Latin inscriptions preserve local theonyms. None records Eguzki Amandrea.

Strabo’s *Geography* III, composed in the late first century BCE and early first century CE, discusses Callaeci, Astures, Cantabri, and Vascones, their feasting, warfare, divination, and sacrifice. It does not name a Basque female Sun. Passages about the Sun’s appearance at the Atlantic horizon are geographical and optical, not testimony to Eguzki’s cult.

The widespread statement that unnamed northern Iberians worshipped a moon or celestial god cannot be turned into evidence for this figure. Nor can the Roman-period altars of Endovelicus, Ataecina, Lacubegi, Baigorrixo, or other local gods be treated as forms of Eguzki without inscriptions or historical links.

**Period label:** **absence of direct evidence**.

### Medieval to early modern period

Christianity became institutionally dominant in Basque-speaking territories over a long and regionally variable period. Solar language was absorbed into Christian forms:

- “holy, blessed Sun”;
- “eye of God”;
- the Virgin as the Sun’s mother;
- protective crosses used alongside or attached to eguzkilore;
- St John’s Day as the setting for midsummer fires.

No document located records an ecclesiastical campaign specifically against Eguzki Amandrea. Witch prosecutions and the demonization of *sorginak* form part of the region’s history, but it would be an error to call accused women “priestesses of Eguzki” without evidence.

**Period label:** **syncretic folklore; poorly documented transition**.

### Nineteenth-century Romanticism and nationalism

European Romanticism encouraged the reconstruction of coherent national mythologies from songs, folktales, language, and monuments. In the Basque case, solar etymologies also entered nationalist speculation. Sabino Arana’s coined **Euzkadi** was associated by later linguistic discussion with his historically incorrect or speculative solar analysis of *euzko-*.

Claims that the Basques literally meant “children of the Sun,” or that *Euskaldun* derives from solar worship, are not accepted historical linguistics.

**Period label:** **modern ideological etymology**.

### 1917–1935: ethnographic documentation

This is the decisive evidentiary period:

- 1917 report of the protective door-flower at Ataun;
- 1920 report from Osintxu;
- 1921 foundation of the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore*;
- 1922 publication of the sunset greetings;
- continuing collection of local beliefs before industrialization and language shift transformed rural life.

Barandiarán’s collaborators preserved genuine oral traditions, but their questionnaire method and comparative categories helped shape the material into a “mythology.”

### Civil War, exile, and postwar scholarship

The Spanish Civil War interrupted the *Anuario*. Barandiarán continued working in exile and later resumed publication. Francoist repression affected Basque language and cultural institutions, but no evidence was found for a state measure aimed specifically at Eguzki Amandrea.

Caro Baroja’s historical anthropology of witchcraft and ritual brought greater attention to documentary context and warned, implicitly and explicitly, against reconstructing a pristine pagan system from late survivals.

### 1960s–1980s: canonization as “Basque mythology”

Barandiarán’s dictionary and popular editions standardized a pantheon-like presentation. His scholarship remained source-conscious, but extraction of dictionary entries from their local contexts allowed later readers to imagine a unified canon.

In 1972–73, **Néstor Basterretxea** created the *Euskal Serie Kosmogonikoa/Serie Cosmogónica Vasca*, giving sculptural bodies to figures that often possessed no traditional fixed iconography. His later **Eguzki Loreak** series transformed the protective flower into modern Basque art. These works are modern interpretations, not copies of ancient cult images.

### Late twentieth century to the present

Eguzki Amandrea has become:

- a standard entry in mythology dictionaries and teaching materials;
- a symbol of female sacrality in matriarchalist interpretations;
- a figure in reconstructed family trees headed by Amalur or Mari;
- a subject of children’s literature, visual art, folklore tourism, jewelry, tattoos, and domestic decoration;
- occasionally a focus of neopagan or nature-spiritual devotion.

The eguzkilore’s visible revival is especially strong. The *Atlas Etnográfico de Vasconia* notes that younger householders again place it on doors, often as decoration. This is genuine contemporary tradition, although not necessarily continuity of belief.

There is no reliable census of present-day worshippers and no evidence of a centralized Eguzki priesthood or church.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Matriarchalist interpretation

Writers influenced by symbolic anthropology have used Eguzki Amandrea, Mari, female household authority, and female funeral roles to argue for a Basque “matriarchal” or “matristic” symbolic system. Such work can illuminate gendered symbolism but becomes historically unsafe when it equates symbolic prominence with prehistoric female political rule.

The female Sun is evidence for a feminine celestial personification. It is not by itself evidence for Paleolithic continuity, matrilineal descent, or rule by women.

### “Old Europe” and prehistoric continuity

The idea that Eguzki descends unchanged from a Neolithic or Paleolithic Great Goddess is a **modern reconstruction**. No inscription or prehistoric image names the figure, and solar disks or female figurines cannot be identified as Eguzki by resemblance alone.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist use

Contemporary spiritual presentations address Eguzki as a goddess, celebrate sunrise or solstice, and integrate her with Amalur, Mari, and Ilargi. These practices are valid evidence for modern religion but not for antiquity. Searches located scattered online and cultural presentations, not a demonstrably ancient continuous institution or reliable membership figures.

### The Amalur creation myth

The most influential modern narrative says:

1. humans originally lived in darkness;
2. monsters and spirits tormented them;
3. they appealed to Amalur;
4. she created Ilargi;
5. the night beings adapted;
6. she created Eguzki;
7. beings still ventured out at night;
8. Amalur created the eguzkilore for human doors.

Its components draw on authentic motifs—female Sun and Moon, Earth-mother imagery, nocturnal beings, protective flower—but the continuous cosmogonic plot is not present in the earliest located sources. It should be identified as **modern folkloric synthesis**, not quoted as an ancient Basque creation scripture.

### Modern visual art

Basterretxea’s *Serie Cosmogónica Vasca* was first exhibited in 1973. Museum documentation describes the series as a modern attempt to give tangible form to the sacred universe documented by Barandiarán. The related *Eguzki Loreak* works date principally to the mid-1970s.

The series materially shaped how an imageless oral tradition could be imagined visually. It belongs to the history of Eguzki’s modern image, not ancient iconography.

### Commercial and civic use

The eguzkilore appears in:

- jewelry and door ornaments;
- logos;
- tourism marketing;
- cultural education;
- tattoo designs;
- the title of the Basque Institute of Criminology’s journal, chosen for the flower’s protective symbolism.

Commercial texts frequently repeat the Amalur creation narrative without identifying its date. Such circulation has helped make the synthetic story appear ancient.

### Political appropriation

Solar etymologies have appeared in Basque nationalist mythmaking, while modern Basque artists used indigenous mythic vocabulary during a period of linguistic repression and cultural renewal. Nevertheless, no significant extremist cult or organized violent appropriation centered on Eguzki Amandrea was found.

The use of the lauburu as both Basque emblem and alleged ancient solar sign requires caution. Its historical architectural and funerary forms are real; its assignment as Eguzki’s personal symbol is modern.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

The search found no substantial pre-twentieth-century hermetic, grimoire, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or ceremonial-magic tradition centered on Eguzki Amandrea. Recent eclectic occult references appear derivative of mythology dictionaries and internet summaries. Claims of an ancient initiatory solar cult should therefore be treated as **modern invention unless supported by specific documents**.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Eguzki a goddess?

The evidence shows personification, direct address, sanctity, kinship language, and supernatural efficacy. These justify “divine being” or “solar numen.” Yet the word “goddess” can mislead if it imports a Greek-style divine biography, temple system, or anthropomorphic iconography.

A careful formulation is: **Eguzki Amandrea is a female-personified and sacralized Sun in documented Basque folklore, reconstructed as a goddess in modern mythology.**

### Is the figure ancient?

Arguments for antiquity cite:

- the non-Indo-European Basque language;
- the female solar conception;
- apparent telluric cosmology;
- widespread solar symbolism;
- persistence in conservative rural settings.

Objections are decisive for dating:

- the name is unattested in antiquity;
- the first secure texts are twentieth-century ethnography;
- the greetings contain overtly Christian vocabulary;
- common solar and protective motifs can persist, spread, or be reinterpreted without preserving a named prehistoric deity;
- material symbols cannot be assigned to Eguzki without labels or contextual evidence.

The tradition may contain old motifs; its absolute age cannot be established.

### Is the mother Earth, Mari, Amalur, or the Virgin?

- **Unnamed mother:** what the earliest verse says.
- **Earth:** Barandiarán’s cosmological inference.
- **Virgin Mary:** regional Christian explanation.
- **Mari:** possible interpretive convergence through *Andra Mari* and telluric associations.
- **Amalur:** modern systematized Mother Earth.

No single solution should be declared original.

### Does *amandrea* mean “grandmother”?

Both “grandmother” and the honorific “mother-lady” are linguistically and ethnographically defensible. Translation should preserve the ambiguity. Popular English “Grandmother Sun” is not wrong, but it is not the only possible nuance.

### Are all circular motifs solar?

No. Rosettes, disks, wheels, spirals, swastikas, and lauburu designs may acquire solar readings, but geometry alone cannot establish meaning. The eguzkilore has the strongest documentary link because informants and collectors explicitly associate its form and power with the Sun.

### Are Asturias, Cantabria, Galicia, and western Iberian gods part of Eguzki’s pantheon?

No primary source establishes this.

Strabo treats the Callaeci, Astures, Cantabri, and Vascones as neighboring but distinguishable populations. Modern regional folklore collections record many solar customs, female beings, door protections, bonfires, and Christianized celestial invocations. Similarity may support comparative study but not identity.

Likewise:

- **Endovelicus** is epigraphically attested principally in Lusitania, especially São Miguel da Mota.
- **Ataecina** is attested in Latin votive inscriptions in western and southwestern Iberia.
- Cantabrian, Asturian, Galician, Aquitanian, Celtiberian, Lusitanian, and Vasconic divine names belong to different corpora.
- Neither Endovelicus nor Ataecina is an attested relative, form, or counterpart of Eguzki Amandrea.

The proposed “Iberian pantheon” is therefore a modern umbrella category, not an ancient institution.

### Evidence preservation

The central evidence survives in:

- the *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore* and its digitized volumes;
- Barandiarán’s papers and publications;
- regional ethnographic atlases;
- later dictionaries and museum collections;
- living speech, custom, and domestic display.

Because the earliest witnesses are ethnographers recording oral testimony, editions should preserve locality, wording, informant context, and collection date. Extracting only a deity-name erases the strongest historical information.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Eguzki Amandrea is named in pre-Roman inscriptions.
- She had temples across northern Iberia.
- All Iberian solar cults were forms of her.
- The Amalur–Ilargi–Eguzki–eguzkilore story is a preserved Paleolithic creation myth.
- The lauburu is securely her ancient emblem.
- *Euskaldun* or *Euzkadi* historically means “people/land of the Sun.”
- Female Sun imagery proves a prehistoric political matriarchy.
- Modern images reproduce an ancient cult statue.
- Witches prosecuted in early-modern Navarre were her priestesses.
- Endovelicus or Ataecina was her consort or regional equivalent.

None is supported by the presently identified primary evidence.

## 11. Sources

### Primary and near-primary folklore sources

- José Miguel de Barandiarán, “Folklore vasco: necesidad de su estudio,” 1922. This contains the earliest securely verifiable published salutations.
- *Anuario de Eusko-Folklore*, especially the early volumes and the 1973 materials recording field information from 1917–25.
- José Miguel de Barandiarán, *Mitología vasca*.
- José Miguel de Barandiarán, *Diccionario ilustrado de mitología vasca y algunas de sus fuentes* (1972), with later editions.
- Resurrección María de Azkue, *Euskalerriaren Yakintza* (1935–47).
- José María Satrústegui’s collections of Navarrese calendar folklore.
- Strabo, *Geography* III, for the ancient ethnographic context and, importantly, the absence of Eguzki Amandrea.

### Critical and interpretive scholarship

- R. L. Trask, *Etymological Dictionary of Basque*, for lexical history and dialect forms.
- Julio Caro Baroja’s historical studies of Basque religion, calendar custom, and witchcraft.
- Juan Inazio Hartsuaga, “Eguzki Amandrea/Sol,” Auñamendi Encyclopedia, for modern cosmological analysis and an explicit discussion of uncertainty and Christian reinterpretation.
- The *Atlas Etnográfico de Vasconia*, for the geographic distribution and modern status of the eguzkilore custom.
- Museum documentation on Néstor Basterretxea for the modern creation of fixed visual forms.

### Assessment of the requested regional corpora

Asturian, Cantabrian, and Galician folklore collections remain appropriate comparative sources for northern Iberian solar customs. The HEp and related epigraphic corpora, Olivares Pedreño, Blázquez, Prósper, and the Endovelicus and Ataecina sanctuary literature are essential for reconstructing ancient western Iberian religion. They do not, however, supply attestations of Eguzki Amandrea. Their evidentiary contribution here is principally negative: they demonstrate how much firmer an ancient divine identification becomes when a name appears on altars, something entirely lacking for Eguzki.
