# Ataecina: Research Dossier

**Class:** indigenous female deity—consistently *dea* and/or *domina* in the surviving Latin inscriptions—whose cult belonged chiefly to Roman-period western and southwestern Hispania. “Chthonic goddess,” “underworld goddess,” “goddess of night,” and “goddess of rebirth” are scholarly interpretations, not classifications preserved in an indigenous narrative text.

**Earliest secure attestation:** no demonstrably pre-Roman text or image names Ataecina. Her earliest secure evidence consists of Latin votive inscriptions of the Roman imperial period, broadly datable to the first–third centuries CE. Individual monuments are often undatable more closely because they lack consular or imperial dating formulas. The most important concentration—fifteen inscribed altars among thirty-one Roman inscriptions recovered at Santa Lucía del Trampal near Alcuéscar, Cáceres—probably belongs to this imperial-period horizon. Thus Ataecina may be pre-Roman in origin, but the documented Ataecina is already a deity operating within Roman epigraphic and religious practice. [Securely attested early material.](https://aespa.revistas.csic.es/index.php/aespa/article/view/396)

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Ataecina is one of the best-attested indigenous goddesses of Roman Hispania. Her dedications cluster principally between the Tagus and Guadiana, especially in southern Cáceres, Badajoz, and adjacent Portugal. Outliers occur farther east and north and, apparently through personal mobility, at Cagliari in Sardinia. She was attached to a place called *Turibriga*, *Turobriga*, or something similar and was sometimes explicitly identified with Roman Proserpina. Her documented functions are much narrower than modern summaries usually imply: inscriptions establish her as a powerful *dea/domina*, receiver of vows, guardian of a regional sanctuary, and—in the Mérida petition—an avenger or restorer of stolen property. Her identification with Proserpina supports, but does not by itself prove in every locality, a chthonic or underworld character.

### Ancient name forms

The Latin inscriptions preserve considerable orthographic variation:

- *Ataecina*
- *Adaecina*
- *Adaegina*
- *Addaecina*
- *Adecina*
- *Adegina*
- *Atacina*
- *Attaecina*
- *Attaegina*
- *Attegina*
- abbreviated *A.*, *Ad.*, *Ate.*, and reconstructed fragmentary forms

The variability probably reflects the transfer of a non-Latin divine name into Latin spelling, regional pronunciation, engravers’ practices, and occasional uncertainty by dedicants. It does not authorize treating every form as a separate goddess. Abascal and colleagues’ corpus lists the variants and maps thirty-six then-recognized dedications, fifteen from Santa Lucía del Trampal. [Securely attested Roman-period material.](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/descargaPdf/ataecina-0/)

Modern normalized forms include Spanish and Portuguese **Ataecina**, **Ataegina**, **Atégina**, and French **Atégine**. None can be demonstrated as the unique ancient “correct” spelling.

### Titles and cult epithets

Documented titles include:

- **dea** — “goddess.”
- **domina** — “lady” or “mistress.”
- **sancta** — “holy,” “sacred,” or “venerable.”
- **dea domina sancta** — a combined honorific especially characteristic of the Trampal material.
- **Turibrigensis / Turobrigensis / Turibrigensis-like variants** — “of Turibriga/Turobriga.”
- **Proserpina** — the Roman goddess’s name, joined directly to Ataecina in a small group of inscriptions.

Neither *domina* nor *sancta* was unique to Ataecina. Comparable honorific language was used for other Hispano-Roman divinities, including Endovelicus, Silvanus, and local gods. It is therefore unsafe to infer a special mystery theology merely from the titles. [Securely attested titles; comparative interpretation.](https://es.scribd.com/document/143900016/Antigua-Ataecina-Divinidad-Celta)

The well-preserved Mérida formula is:

> **DEA ATAECINA TURIBRIG(ENSIS) PROSERPINA**

“Goddess Ataecina, Turibrigian Proserpina.”

Other explicit Ataecina–Proserpina equations are reported from Cárdenas, Salvatierra de los Barros, and La Garrovilla. Scholars warn that these equations do not license counting every Hispano-Roman dedication to Proserpina as an otherwise concealed dedication to Ataecina. [Securely attested syncretistic identification.](https://e-revistas.uc3m.es/index.php/REVHISTO/es/article/download/4209/2794/5725)

### Etymological dispute

No ancient writer explains the name. Every etymology is modern reconstruction.

1. **“Reborn” hypothesis.** D’Arbois de Jubainville and José Leite de Vasconcelos connected the name with Celtic *ate-* “again, re-” and a form of *gen-* “be born,” yielding “Reborn One.” This reading dovetailed attractively with Proserpina’s seasonal return but is linguistically disputed and may reason backward from the Roman equation. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

2. **“Night” hypothesis.** Eugenio Luján, beginning from the spelling *Adaecina*, compared Old Irish *adaig* “night,” while Patrizia de Bernardo Stempel proposed Celtic *atakī* and later *Atakī-nā*, approximately “the divine nocturnal time/the one of night.” Luján expressly treated his proposal as tentative. Blázquez considered the comparison difficult rather than established. [Modern scholarly reconstruction, contested.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274786494_La_diosa_Ataecina_y_el_nombre_de_la_noche_en_antiguo_irlandes)

3. **Indigenous deity with a Celticized name.** Leite de Vasconcelos and later historians allowed that the goddess might be locally indigenous even if the recoverable name were Celtic. “Celtic name,” “Celtic cult,” and “ethnically Celtic goddess” are not equivalent propositions. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

4. **Modern speculative compounds.** Derivations from an archetypal *Atta* plus “Jana,” Janus, or Diana lack support in the ancient inscriptions or accepted historical linguistics. [Modern invention/speculation.]

### Ethnic and regional classification

The evidence does not support the brief’s composite label “Basque, Asturian-Cantabrian, Galician” as an ancient pantheon for Ataecina. Her secure cultic geography is Lusitanian, Vettonian, Celtici, and Emeritan/southwestern Hispano-Roman. No named Ataecina appears in the traditional Basque, Asturian, Cantabrian, or Galician folklore collections of Barandiarán, Azkue, Cabal, Aurelio de Llano, García-Lomas, or Risco as an inherited local folklore being. Those corpora are essential for their own regional traditions, but they are not primary sources for Ataecina. Assigning her to a unified pan-Iberian pantheon is a modern systematization.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Nature of the corpus

Ataecina has no surviving hymn, scripture, epic, indigenous myth, priestly manual, or ancient prose narrative. Her ancient corpus is overwhelmingly epigraphic:

- votive altars;
- dedicatory slabs;
- abbreviated divine titles;
- one unusually expansive petition concerning stolen clothing;
- inscribed monuments recovered in secondary architectural use;
- small bronze goat figures archaeologically associated with some cult contexts, although most are not themselves securely inscribed.

Consequently, claims about her genealogy, seasonal death and return, lunar government, healing, magic, or detailed underworld mythology do not come from an Ataecina narrative. They are deductions—sometimes plausible, sometimes circular—from her Proserpina equation, etymological proposals, sanctuary locations, and modern comparison.

### Santa Lucía del Trampal

Excavation and restoration of the early medieval church at Santa Lucía del Trampal produced thirty-one Roman inscriptions, fifteen of them altars dedicated to Ataecina. Numerous uninscribed monuments accompanied them. This is the largest known concentration of her dedications and is comparable, among western Iberian indigenous cults, to the sanctuary evidence for Endovelicus. The stones had been reused in the Christian building; their presence proves access to a substantial Roman sacred assemblage but does not prove that every altar stood exactly where it was found. [Securely attested archaeology and epigraphy.](https://aespa.revistas.csic.es/index.php/aespa/article/view/396)

Typical Trampal formulas invoke:

- *Dea Domina Sancta Turibrigensis Ataecina*;
- *Domina Turibrigensis* plus a spelling of the name;
- abbreviated sequences such as *D.D.S.T.A.*

The inscriptions show formal vow-making but preserve no explanatory mythology. The sanctuary’s dedicants include persons with Roman naming habits; the corpus is evidence for a locally rooted yet Romanized religious community, not an untouched pre-Roman cult.

### The Mérida theft petition

The best-known text is a marble inscription found at the Proserpina reservoir near Mérida and now associated with the Museo Nacional de Arte Romano. Its letter forms and context place it in the Roman imperial period, conventionally within the first few centuries CE. It invokes:

> *Dea Ataecina Turibrig(ensis) Proserpina, per tuam maiestatem te rogo, oro, obsecro, uti vindices…*

“Goddess Ataecina, Turibrigian Proserpina, by your majesty I ask, pray, and beseech you that you avenge … the theft committed against me.”

The petitioner lists stolen garments—tunics, linen cloaks, and underclothes—and asks the goddess to act against whoever removed, stole, exchanged, or diminished them. The damaged lower portion prevents recovery of the full sanction. [Securely attested Roman-period text.](https://ceres.mcu.es/pages/Main?idt=6519&inventary=CE10302&museum=MNAR&table=FMUS)

It is often called a “curse tablet,” but physically it is a marble slab rather than the more usual rolled or folded lead *defixio*. Functionally it belongs near judicial prayers and prayers for justice: the victim hands an unknown thief and the stolen property to divine jurisdiction. It proves an avenging/restorative capacity. It does not prove a general doctrine of necromancy, human sacrifice, or the petitioner’s self-dedication.

The text was published in detail after its twentieth-century discovery by José Álvarez Sáenz de Buruaga. [Modern discovery and edition.](https://www.dip-badajoz.es/cultura/ceex/reex_digital/reex_XI/1955/T.%20XI%20n.%201-4%201955%20en.-dic/RV10140.pdf)

### Other principal findspots

The historical corpus includes dedications reported from:

- Alcuéscar/Santa Lucía del Trampal and its environs;
- Mérida/Augusta Emerita;
- Cárdenas;
- La Garrovilla;
- Medellín/Metellinum;
- Herguijuela;
- Malpartida de Cáceres;
- Salvatierra de Santiago;
- Salvatierra de los Barros;
- El Gordo;
- Santa Ana;
- Talavera de la Reina and Caleruela;
- La Bienvenida;
- Quintos in the Beja region;
- Saelices;
- an outlying dedication at Cagliari, Sardinia;
- a reported small altar from Oña, Burgos, whose isolated context calls for caution.

The DEpHis record from Metellinum, for example, reads *Dominae Turibrig(ensi) Adaeginae Marituma…*, a vow by a woman named Marituma. [Securely attested epigraphy.](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/dephis/item-set/14648?page=40&sort_by=created&sort_order=desc)

### Literary sources and their limits

Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 3.14, lists a *Turobriga* among communities of Baeturia Celtica. He does **not** mention Ataecina. Whether Pliny’s settlement is the same place embedded in Ataecina’s epithet is disputed because the apparent geographical position does not match the densest cult concentration near Alcuéscar.

Strabo, *Geography* 3, describes peoples and cult practices of Iberia but never names Ataecina. Statements that his discussion of unnamed lunar worship proves Ataecina was a moon goddess are conjectural. Roman authors therefore provide regional context, not a literary mythology for her.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### What is directly attested

The inscriptions securely establish:

- receipt of vows and dedications;
- the honorific status of goddess, lady, and holy deity;
- attachment to Turibriga;
- capacity to avenge theft or recover justice;
- identification with Proserpina in several local inscriptions;
- worship across a regional network centered in the Tagus–Guadiana zone.

### Chthonic and underworld character

The strongest basis for calling Ataecina chthonic is the explicit name **Proserpina**. Since Roman Proserpina was queen of the underworld, worshippers who wrote the double name were making an intelligible religious identification. The Mérida judicial prayer also resembles appeals to gods empowered over offenders and the dead.

Nevertheless, the evidence does not show that every community or worshipper conceived Ataecina exclusively as an underworld goddess. “Chthonic” is a scholarly synthesis of the syncretism and petition, not an indigenous definition preserved in prose. [Syncretistic identification and modern scholarly reconstruction.]

### Spring, rebirth, fertility, nature, and vegetation

These familiar modern attributes depend principally upon:

- the disputed “Reborn One” etymology;
- Proserpina’s Greco-Roman seasonal mythology;
- comparison with the agricultural cycle;
- later interpretation of the goat as a fertility symbol.

No surviving Ataecina inscription says “goddess of spring,” narrates her annual return, or assigns her control of crops. These domains remain plausible but unproven reconstructions. It is also possible that some Proserpina-related attributes entered the local cult through Roman interpretive fusion rather than representing its pre-Roman core.

### Night and moon

The night domain follows the Luján/de Bernardo Stempel etymology. A lunar character has sometimes been inferred from crescent-like motifs and Strabo’s anonymous account of Iberian worship, but no inscription calls Ataecina “Moon,” and no securely identified image supplies an unambiguous lunar emblem. “Goddess of night” is a defensible linguistic hypothesis, not a securely attested ancient title; “moon goddess” is still less secure.

### Healing and waters

Some dedications and bronze goats have been associated with springs, streams, or washing and pastoral landscapes. The Mérida slab was found near the Proserpina reservoir, and proposed sanctuaries at Malpartida have watery settings. Yet the evidence does not contain a healing formula comparable to the explicit *pro salute* dedications of established healing cults. A general healing domain should therefore be marked as scholarly inference or modern expansion.

### The goat

Small bronze goats form the most important candidate iconography. A 2022 study assembles more than thirty goat bronzes alongside more than thirty Ataecina inscriptions and argues that the figures attest a popular votive rite continuing for over half a millennium. Some specimens have archaeological or epigraphic associations strong enough to connect the species with her cult; others are uninscribed and cannot be assigned merely because they are goats. [Archaeological interpretation, with securely associated and uncertain specimens.](https://www.cultura.gob.es/mnromano/en/dam/jcr%3Abce81c34-9bae-4589-9bc6-c1d4c7ee7ab5/01-anas-35-m-almagro-et-alli.pdf)

Possible meanings include animal offering, substitution for a live victim, pastoral prosperity, fecundity, or a divine animal. No ancient commentary chooses among them. There is no secure evidence that Ataecina herself had goat form or a goat’s head.

### Anthropomorphic appearance and alleged emblems

No surviving image is universally accepted as an anthropomorphic portrait of Ataecina. Claims that her standard emblem was a cypress branch, that she wore fixed colors, or that she possessed a canonical weapon, number, weekday, or celestial body are unsupported by the secure corpus. These details generally derive from Proserpina or modern ritual systems.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No indigenous source names Ataecina’s parents, siblings, consort, offspring, mount, servants, rivals, or enemies. She cannot securely be inserted into a family tree.

Calling her daughter of Jupiter and Ceres or wife of Pluto imports Proserpina’s Greco-Roman genealogy. Some Roman-period worshippers did explicitly equate her with Proserpina, but no Ataecina inscription states those family relationships. They belong to the receiving Roman mythology, not to a documented indigenous Ataecina cycle.

### Every documented divine interaction or association

- **Proserpina — identification/equation:** Ataecina and Proserpina are joined in single invocations at Mérida, Cárdenas, Salvatierra de los Barros, and La Garrovilla, Roman imperial period. This is the only secure named deity-to-deity relation. Because it is an equation rather than a narrative meeting, the sources do not portray the two as separate characters conversing or acting together. **Tier: securely attested syncretistic identification.**

- **Jupiter — cultic coexistence only:** inscriptions to Jupiter reportedly belonged to the wider Roman sacred assemblage around Trampal. No text records Jupiter interacting with Ataecina. **Tier: securely attested co-presence at a cult locality; no interaction.**

- **Lares — cultic coexistence only:** material from the Trampal complex has been interpreted as showing worship of the Lares alongside Ataecina. No shared episode survives. **Tier: regional cultic coexistence; no interaction.**

- **Feronia — proposed scholarly comparison:** García-Bellido’s identification of the Trampal region with a *lucus Feroniae* led to proposals of a local Ataecina–Feronia relationship. The archaeological and topographical chain is disputed, and no inscription equates them. **Tier: modern scholarly reconstruction.**

- **Mater Magna/Cybele — proposed functional assimilation:** earlier scholarship compared or assimilated Ataecina to a Great Mother. No surviving Ataecina inscription names Mater Magna. **Tier: learned modern systematization.**

- **Tanit / Dea Caelestis — proposed cultural succession or syncretism:** Phoenician-Punic influence in Iberia has encouraged comparison, but no ancient dedication equates Ataecina with Tanit. **Tier: modern comparativist hypothesis, not attested interaction.**

- **Ceres, Jupiter, Pluto/Dis Pater — relations imported through Proserpina:** if Ataecina is treated as Proserpina, Roman mythology supplies her mother, father, and husband. No surviving Ataecina text does so. **Tier: secondary consequence of syncretistic identification, not an indigenous attestation.**

- **Endovelicus — modern comparative pairing:** the two cults are compared because both are richly attested in western Iberia. No ancient source makes them siblings, consorts, allies, or opponents. **Tier: modern scholarly comparison; absence of interaction.**

That is the complete recoverable list. There is no documented divine combat, seduction, judgment, theft, rescue, or dialogue involving Ataecina.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### No surviving indigenous Ataecina myth

No ancient narrative involving Ataecina survives. This negative finding is central. She does not appear in an epic, hymn, prose mythography, medieval legend, or orally collected folklore narrative preserving a recognizable ancient story.

### The supposed death-and-return myth

Modern accounts often say Ataecina descends underground in winter and returns in spring, governing death, rebirth, and vegetation. No Ataecina source relates this story. It is produced by combining:

1. the “Reborn One” etymology;
2. the Roman equation with Proserpina;
3. the Greek Demeter–Persephone cycle;
4. modern seasonal-goddess frameworks.

The Proserpina equation makes the borrowing historically intelligible, but it does not prove that an equivalent indigenous narrative preceded Roman rule. **Tier: modern reconstruction built on ancient syncretism; often presented popularly as ancient myth.**

### The avenging goddess and the stolen clothes

The Mérida petition is the closest surviving material to an episode:

- an unnamed person has lost garments through theft or fraudulent removal;
- the petitioner appeals to Ataecina-Proserpina’s *maiestas*;
- the goddess is asked to avenge the wrong;
- the offender is not narratively identified, and the outcome is unknown.

This is an actual ancient request, not a mythic cycle. It documents how one worshipper expected the goddess to operate in ordinary social conflict. **Tier: securely attested Roman-period cultic testimony.**

### Modern retellings

Contemporary Pagan and popular-mythology accounts increasingly narrate Ataecina as an Iberian Persephone or Hecate: a queen of the dead, nocturnal magician, healer, and returning spring maiden. These portraits can be meaningful modern religious constructions, but their narrative detail is not recoverable from antiquity. **Tier: modern retelling and invention.**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Turibriga/Turobriga

Ataecina’s recurring geographical epithet makes Turibriga the defining place of her cult. The site remains disputed.

- Pliny’s *Turobriga* in Baeturia Celtica is often identified with the excavated Hispano-Roman town near Aroche, Huelva.
- Ataecina’s inscriptions, however, concentrate much farther north around Alcuéscar and the Emeritan territory.
- Abascal and others therefore propose a different Turibriga near Santa Lucía/Las Torrecillas, perhaps a sanctuary settlement or small community rather than a large city.
- The two places may be distinct; similar *-briga* names were common.

Accordingly, “goddess of Turobriga” is secure, but locating that Turobriga at Aroche is not.

### Santa Lucía del Trampal sanctuary

The fifteen Ataecina altars make the Trampal area her strongest known cult center. The precise Roman sanctuary plan is not securely recovered: the stones were incorporated into a later church, and some may have been moved from nearby Las Torrecillas. The safest reconstruction is of a regional sacred place—possibly a grove or open sanctuary—with a substantial votive economy.

The Christian church reused pagan altars and other Roman masonry. This demonstrates material appropriation of the older cult’s monuments. It does not, by itself, prove that the church was intentionally dedicated to Saint Lucy as a direct substitute for a nocturnal goddess. The date of the church—Visigothic or later Mozarabic/emiral-period in parts—has itself been debated. [Secure reuse; disputed chronology and continuity.](https://dehesa.unex.es/bitstreams/9d58a142-590a-42ce-9a9b-29b2b3d98d8c/download)

### Ritual forms

Securely or reasonably attested practices include:

- **Vow-making:** formulas such as *votum solvit libens merito*, “fulfilled the vow willingly and deservedly.”
- **Dedication of stone altars:** the dominant surviving medium.
- **Petitionary prayer:** unusually explicit in the Mérida theft text.
- **Requests for divine justice:** the goddess was asked to punish or compel an unknown offender.
- **Animal-shaped votives:** bronze goats, at least some cultically associated with Ataecina.
- **Possible animal sacrifice:** goat votives make a connection to goat offerings plausible, but a figurine is not proof that a live animal was killed in every instance.
- **Personal and family devotion:** names of individual dedicants, including women, show worship beyond an exclusively civic priesthood.

### Priesthood and institution

No securely identified Ataecina priestly title or complete priestly organization survives. A “Turobrigensian priestess” has been inferred from inscriptions discussed in older scholarship, but damaged texts and uncertain site identifications require caution. There is no secure evidence for hereditary clergy, initiation grades, temple prostitution, or a mystery cult.

### Festivals and calendar

No festival name, calendar date, procession, or annual ritual is preserved. Modern observances at equinoxes, Samhain, or spring festivals are contemporary constructions.

### Sacrifice, violence, and human sacrifice

Animal sacrifice would be normal in Roman provincial religion, and goats may have been relevant. However:

- no Ataecina inscription records a human victim;
- no sanctuary deposit has been securely identified as human sacrifice to her;
- the Mérida prayer does not say that the petitioner sacrificed a person or self-dedicated;
- online descriptions of the inscription as a *devotio* involving human sacrifice go beyond the text.

**Finding:** human sacrifice in Ataecina’s cult is neither documented nor positively alleged by a relevant ancient source. Modern claims to the contrary are speculative.

### Cult economics and social reach

Stone altars, marble slabs, and bronzes required material expenditure and artisans. The distribution suggests local landholders, urban and rural devotees, and mobile individuals rather than an empire-wide state cult. The Cagliari dedication and other outliers may reflect migration or travel from the cult’s homeland, not foreign sanctuaries of comparable scale.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before Roman rule: possible formation, no named evidence

The non-Latin theonym and regional concentration strongly suggest an indigenous origin. Yet no pre-Roman inscription securely names Ataecina, and no Bronze or Iron Age image can be identified with her. Her pre-Roman character is therefore inferred from language and geography. **Tier: modern scholarly reconstruction.**

### First–third centuries CE: documented florescence

The cult becomes visible under Roman rule through Latin epigraphy. This is not simply “survival” of a static native religion: worshippers adopted Roman altars, Latin formulas, legal-petitionary language, and the name Proserpina while retaining an indigenous divine name and regional epithet.

The dense Trampal assemblage and wide regional scatter justify calling the Principate the cult’s documented peak. The evidence probably spans the first three centuries CE, although many monuments cannot be individually dated. [Securely attested imperial-period cult; dating broad.](https://aespa.revistas.csic.es/index.php/aespa/article/view/396)

### Mechanism of Romanization

Ataecina was not simply “renamed Proserpina.” The evidence displays several practices:

- dedications to Ataecina without Proserpina;
- dedications to Ataecina with *dea*, *domina*, or *sancta*;
- explicit double-name equations with Proserpina;
- dedications to Proserpina that cannot automatically be reassigned to Ataecina.

This points to selective, local interpretatio rather than complete absorption.

### Fourth–sixth centuries: disappearance of the epigraphic cult

No securely dated late-antique literary defense or continuous sequence of Ataecina dedications survives. The public polytheistic environment of Hispania contracted during the fourth century as Christian imperial policy restricted sacrifice and temples; regional practice may have persisted privately, but Ataecina becomes epigraphically invisible.

The cessation cannot be assigned to a documented single destruction event. Statements that Christians specifically “eradicated” her sanctuary are stronger than the evidence. [Regional historical context; no Ataecina-specific suppression decree.](https://e-archivo.uc3m.es/bitstreams/2b50d3c9-0c5f-42b8-8812-3821c59791e8/download)

### Early medieval reuse at Trampal

A Christian church was built with reused Roman sacred stones. This is material succession, not evidence of continuous worship. Proposals that Saint Lucy replaced Ataecina because “Lucía/light” opposed a night goddess are appealing but undocumented. No medieval hagiography identifies the saint with Ataecina.

### Medieval to early modern periods

No continuous folklore line has been established. Ataecina does not appear as a named being in inquisitorial material, grimoires, Renaissance mythography of comparable influence, or the major regional folklore collections cited in the brief. She survived mainly as an unread or antiquarianly reported name on stone.

### Nineteenth–twentieth-century recovery

The growth of Latin epigraphy, especially the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*, made the scattered dedications comparable. Leite de Vasconcelos, Toutain, Blázquez, and others incorporated her into studies of Lusitanian and Hispano-Roman religion.

The 1950s publication of the Mérida slab revealed an otherwise unknown personal prayer. Excavation and restoration at Santa Lucía del Trampal, followed by Abascal and collaborators’ 1995 corpus, transformed her from a scattered epigraphic name into the tutelary deity of a major regional sanctuary.

### Twenty-first century

New finds, digital epigraphic catalogues, renewed study of goat bronzes, heritage interpretation, and online reconstructionist religion have expanded her modern presence. There is no evidence for an institutionally continuous cult from antiquity; all present worship is revival or reconstruction.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

Ataecina has become emblematic of indigenous–Roman religious hybridity in Extremadura and Lusitania. Museums, archaeological tourism, and accounts of Santa Lucía regularly present her as a major pre-Roman goddess. Public summaries often turn contested deductions—spring, moon, fertility, healing—into settled attributes.

### Astronomy: the proposed name “Ataecina”

José Luis Ortiz’s Spanish team proposed **Ataecina** on 11 September 2006 as the name for trans-Neptunian object 2003 EL61, citing the goddess’s Iberian origin and connection with Proserpina and Pluto. The International Astronomical Union instead announced **Haumea** in September 2008. Ataecina therefore never became the object’s official astronomical name. [Documented modern reception.](https://www.infoastro.com/200809/26ataecina-haumea.html)

### Contemporary Paganism

Small online Iberian-polytheist, Celtic-reconstructionist, witchcraft, and eclectic Pagan communities invoke Ataecina today. Common modern roles include:

- queen or guide of the dead;
- goddess of witchcraft and night;
- healer;
- spring-return or resurrection deity;
- Iberian counterpart of Persephone or Hecate;
- patron of goats, caves, springs, and liminal transitions.

The first two have partial ancient foundations in Proserpina and the “night” etymology; the remainder usually combine ancient fragments with modern ritual creativity. No reliable census establishes the scale of the worship, and no continuous priestly lineage is documented.

A 2025 Pagan treatment explicitly compares Ataecina with Hecate while acknowledging the interpretive step. Such material documents present belief but cannot serve as evidence for ancient mythology. [Modern religious reinvention.](https://thepagancollective.com/2025/07/31/ataegina-iberian-goddess-of-the-underworld-chapter-6-by-ness-bosch/)

### Occultism and esoteric orders

No evidence located places Ataecina in Renaissance Hermetism, the canonical Golden Dawn papers, Theosophy, or the standard early modern grimoires. Her present occult profile is largely internet-era and draws upon generalized goddess spirituality, witchcraft, Hecatean practice, and underworld symbolism. Claims of an ancient Ataecina mystery tradition or transmitted coven lineage are unsupported.

### Nationalist and political appropriation

Ataecina is occasionally styled an “Iberian national goddess” or made evidence of a unified pre-Christian Spanish/Portuguese religion. The ancient distribution was regional, linguistically and politically heterogeneous, and known only under Roman rule. Pan-Iberian nationalization is modern. No substantial documented extremist organization appears to have made her a central symbol.

### Commercial and popular culture

Her modern presence is strongest in websites, devotional art, jewelry, social media, and tourism rather than in major films, novels, or internationally influential games. Many modern images portray a dark-haired woman with goats, crescent moon, cypress, torch, or underworld scenery. These are modern visual syntheses; no canonical ancient portrait controls them.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal stages

1. **Epigraphic collection:** nineteenth-century antiquaries and the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* assembled scattered stones under variant spellings.
2. **Comparative religion:** Leite de Vasconcelos, Toutain, and later Blázquez sought Celtic, Lusitanian, and Greco-Roman meanings.
3. **Sanctuary archaeology:** the discovery and study of reused inscriptions at Santa Lucía del Trampal shifted attention toward landscape and cult center.
4. **Corpus and distribution:** Abascal, Caballero, Sánchez, and collaborators’ 1995 study catalogued the Trampal inscriptions and mapped the wider cult.
5. **Linguistic reassessment:** Luján, Prósper, and de Bernardo Stempel revisited the name using Celtic historical linguistics.
6. **Religious-dynamics approach:** recent scholarship emphasizes hybrid local practice rather than a simple native-versus-Roman opposition.
7. **Material/iconographic reassessment:** recent work has systematized the bronze goat evidence.

### Major disputes

#### Where was Turibriga?

The epithet is secure; the location is not. Candidates include Pliny’s Baeturian Turobriga at or near Aroche and a separate settlement or sanctuary near Alcuéscar/Las Torrecillas. The epigraphic concentration favors the latter as Ataecina’s principal cult landscape, but it does not yield an excavated city securely bearing the name.

#### What does the name mean?

“Reborn” and “night” are competing Celtic explanations. Neither has direct ancient glossing. The former fits the later Proserpina analogy suspiciously well; the latter handles some spellings and Celtic comparanda but remains a reconstruction.

#### Was she inherently chthonic?

Explicit Proserpina equations make a chthonic interpretation historically grounded. The debate concerns whether that quality belonged to the pre-Roman deity, arose through Roman interpretatio, or was accentuated only at certain sanctuaries.

#### Was she a spring or fertility goddess?

Possible, but not explicit. Much of the claim is generated by the Proserpina equation and “reborn” etymology. Scholars must avoid using the presumed spring character to prove the etymology and then using the etymology to prove the spring character.

#### Are the goats hers?

Some associations are persuasive, particularly where goat bronzes occur with dedications or in the same cult landscape. An uninscribed goat from western Iberia is not automatically Ataecina’s. The corpus requires find-by-find evaluation.

#### Was Trampal a pagan temple converted into a church?

A large Ataecina cult center existed in the immediate region, and its stones were reused in the church. The plan and exact location of the Roman sanctuary, the movement of the stones, the date of Christian construction, and deliberate saint-for-goddess continuity all remain debated.

#### Was Ataecina Tanit, Cybele, Feronia, Diana, or Hecate?

No ancient text makes these equations. They are comparativist proposals or modern devotional analogies. Proserpina alone is epigraphically secure.

### State of the evidence

The inscriptions are dispersed among museums, archaeological stores, churches, and publications. Principal reference systems include:

- *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* II;
- *L’Année épigraphique*;
- *Hispania Epigraphica*;
- the Santa Lucía del Trampal corpus;
- DEpHis;
- the Museo Nacional de Arte Romano and Spain’s CER.es catalogue.

The corpus is not closed: new altars and revised readings can change the distribution. Provenance remains a serious issue because numerous monuments were reused or reported before modern excavation.

## 11. Evidentiary Conclusions

Ataecina’s securely reconstructable history is substantial but sharply bounded. She was a powerful regional goddess of Roman-period western Hispania, particularly the middle Tagus–Guadiana zone. Worshippers called her goddess, lady, holy, and Turibrigian; made vows to her; dedicated stone altars and probably bronze goat votives; and, in several places, identified her with Proserpina. One petitioner asked her to avenge stolen clothing.

Beyond those facts, degrees of inference rapidly increase. A chthonic character is well supported by the Proserpina equations. A connection with night is a serious but contested linguistic proposal. Spring, resurrection, vegetation, fertility, lunar rule, healing, magic, and fixed iconographic emblems are not directly stated in the corpus. No genealogy, indigenous myth, festival calendar, priestly organization, or anthropomorphic portrait survives. No evidence connects her cult with human sacrifice or temple prostitution.

Her “life” is therefore the history of a locally rooted deity becoming visible through Roman epigraphy, acquiring selective Proserpina identifications, disappearing from the public record in late antiquity, surviving materially as reused stone, and being reconstructed in modern scholarship and revived religion. The variants and silences are not defects in that history; they are its defining evidence.
