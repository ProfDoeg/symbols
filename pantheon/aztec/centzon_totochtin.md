# The Centzon Totochtin (the Four Hundred Rabbits): Research Dossier

**Class:** A collective of male and sometimes gender-indeterminate *teteoh*—localized pulque, fertility, feast, and intoxication divinities—rather than literally four hundred zoological rabbits. Individual members could also function as civic patrons, calendrical powers, mountain gods, or divine impersonators.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Individual rabbit-, maguey-, and pulque-associated divinities occur in Late Postclassic central-Mexican pictorial manuscripts, especially the pre-Conquest or very early colonial Borgia-group tradition. The collective name **Centzon Totochtin** is securely documented in alphabetic Nahuatl in Bernardino de Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*, compiled at Tepepulco about 1558–1561 from Indigenous testimony. Because nearly all surviving named descriptions were recorded after 1521, claims that a particular genealogy or systematic roster is “the original Aztec doctrine” require caution.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The collective

**[Secure sixteenth-century attestation]** Classical Nahuatl **Centzon Tōtōchtin** or compounded **Centzontōtōchtin** means “Four Hundred Rabbits”:

- *centzontli*: four hundred, conventionally twenty twenties;
- *tōchtli*: rabbit;
- *tōtōchtin*: plural, “rabbits.”

In ritual language “four hundred” can signify a full, multitudinous, or practically innumerable company. It should not automatically be read as a census of exactly 400 individually named gods. No surviving source supplies 400 names.

The common gloss “four hundred kinds or degrees of drunkenness” is an intelligible modern summary of Nahua ideas about drinkers manifesting different divine “rabbits,” but no surviving primary text presents a numbered psychological catalogue. Sahagún’s Nahua informants say that when someone became drunk, people said **“his rabbit”** was of such-and-such a kind: the person’s conduct revealed the particular divine intoxication possessing or expressed through him. Alfredo López Austin emphasizes the rabbit’s wider lunar, terrestrial, agricultural, and excess-related symbolism; reducing the group to comic “party gods” loses much of this religious field. The ambivalence of pulque—ritual nourishment and dangerous agent of antisocial conduct—is explicitly preserved in the sources. [Arqueología Mexicana, “Los conejos y la embriaguez”](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/3049)

Relevant words include:

- **octli**: the fermented maguey drink now normally called pulque;
- **metl**: maguey/agave;
- **tōchtli**: rabbit and the eighth day-sign of the twenty-day sequence;
- **Ome Tōchtli**, “Two Rabbit”: a calendrical name and general title closely associated with pulque gods.

“Pulque” is not the principal pre-Conquest Nahuatl term; *octli* is preferable when describing Nahua testimony.

### Ometochtli

**[Secure sixteenth-century attestation; classification disputed]** **Ome Tōchtli**, “Two Rabbit,” appears variously as:

1. a particular chief or representative of the pulque gods;
2. the calendrical name of an individual local pulque deity;
3. a generic or collective manifestation of the Centzon Totochtin;
4. a title shared by several regional pulque gods.

These usages must not be collapsed. They reflect a ritual complex in which distinct local gods could wear substantially the same insignia and be treated as manifestations of a divine class. The *Telleriano-Remensis* commentary describes the priest of Ome Tochtzin gathering the *centzototochtin*, a word that there may designate both pulque divinities and their corresponding ritual personnel. [UNAM, *Telleriano-Remensis*, fol. 17v](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/telleriano/385_17v)

**Ometochtli is not Ometeotl.** The former is *ome* + *tochtli*, “Two Rabbit”; the latter is the heavily debated modern scholarly name “Dual God.” Popular conflation is philologically erroneous.

### Principal named members and local manifestations

The surviving lists differ. Sahagún, the *Primeros Memoriales*, and the Magliabechiano-group manuscripts preserve overlapping but non-identical selections. Names securely or plausibly belonging to the complex include:

- **Tezcatzōncatl** — commonly interpreted “Mirror-Hair” or “He of the Mirror Hair”; Sahagún expressly places him among the Centzon Totochtin.
- **Tepōztēcatl** — “Person/Lord of Tepoztlan,” patron of Tepoztlan; also called **Ometochtli-Tepoztēcatl** in local and later descriptions.
- **Yiauhtēcatl / Yauhtēcatl** — probably connected with *yauhtli*, Mexican marigold (*Tagetes lucida*), an incense and ritual plant.
- **Papāztac** — a pulque god associated in one creation account with striking the Moon with a rabbit-shaped object or rabbit.
- **Tōltēcatl** — “Toltec/person of Tollan”; distinguish from manuscript variants such as **Totoltēcatl**, whose exact relationship is uncertain.
- **Tlāltēcatl / Tlāltecayōhua** — name and spelling vary across manuscripts.
- **Colhuacatzincatl / Colhuatzincatl** — a local or ethnic title associated with Colhuacan.
- **Acolhua / Acolhuatzincatl** — an Acolhua-associated manifestation.
- **Izquitēcatl** — connected by some glosses with roasted maize or the preparation of drink.
- **Tlilhua** — “possessor of blackness/ink,” depending on segmentation.
- **Tlamatzincatl** — local-title formation.
- **Cuextēcatl** — “Huastec person”; important because Nahua sources stereotypically associated Huastecs with pulque, nakedness, sexual display, and ritual transgression.
- **Mācuīltōchtli**, “Five Rabbit” — one of the *Macuiltonaleque*, beings of dangerous excess; his relation to the Centzon Totochtin is associative rather than consistently genealogical.
- **Teatlahuiani**, “One Who Drowns People,” and **Tequechmecaniani**, “One Who Hangs/Strangles People by the Neck” — names expressing destructive forms of intoxication.

The *Codex Magliabechiano* devotes folios 48v–59r to pulque gods: the Indigenous-style figures generally occupy the rectos, while Spanish explanatory prose occupies the facing versos. The manuscript is a mid-sixteenth-century descendant of a lost prototype rather than a pristine pre-Conquest original. [UNAM, “Códice Magliabechiano”](https://www.iifilologicas.unam.mx/wikfil/index.php/Magliabechiano%2C_C%C3%B3dice)

### “Rabbit” as a condition of the drinker

**[Secure colonial testimony about pre-Conquest belief]** The “rabbit” was not simply a mascot. A drunk person’s violent, melancholy, noisy, amorous, sleepy, or self-destructive conduct could be attributed to the kind of *tochtli* manifested in him. This is the historical basis for the often-repeated claim that every mode of intoxication had its own rabbit god. The evidence supports plurality and behavioral differentiation, but not a surviving list of 400 neatly defined intoxication types.

### Comparable identifications

There is no securely attested Indigenous equation of the Centzon Totochtin with Dionysus/Bacchus. Spanish friars naturally described them through European categories such as “gods of wine” and “drunkenness,” but this is descriptive interpretatio, not evidence for a pre-Hispanic syncretism. Later writers’ use of “Aztec Bacchus” for Ometochtli or Tepoztecatl is therefore **[early-modern or modern learned comparison]**, not a Nahua cult title.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaeological and pictorial background

Maguey use and fermented beverages long predate the Mexica empire. Earlier Mesoamerican vessel forms, murals, and botanical associations establish a deep history for ritual drinking, but they do not securely name the Centzon Totochtin. Claims that a Classic-period figure is already “Ometochtli” normally rest on retrospective iconographic comparison.

**[Late Postclassic pictorial evidence]** The Borgia-group divinatory books contain gods identifiable through rabbit day signs, crescent-shaped nose ornaments, black-and-white facial patterns, foaming drink vessels, maguey imagery, and other pulque-god insignia. The *Codex Borgia* itself is generally dated to the Late Postclassic and probably comes from the Puebla–Tlaxcala–Mixteca cultural zone. Its scenes are not accompanied by alphabetic names. Identification of every depicted figure with a named god is therefore scholarly reconstruction, not a caption supplied by the manuscript.

The date of the *Codex Borbonicus* remains disputed: its pictorial program is thoroughly Indigenous, but scholars disagree whether manufacture began immediately before or shortly after the conquest; later Spanish annotations are unquestioned. Scientific examination has not converted that debate into certainty. [French Ministry of Culture, examination of the *Codex Borbonicus*](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/thematiques/sciences-du-patrimoine/thematiques-de-recherche/etude-des-materiaux/L-examen-du-codex-Borbonicus)

### Principal sixteenth-century sources

#### *Primeros Memoriales*, ca. 1558–1561

Compiled by Sahagún at Tepepulco from Nahua elders and specialists. It preserves Nahuatl descriptions of divine apparel and rites and explicitly names the Centzon Totochtin. This is the strongest secure alphabetic attestation of the collective.

Its evidentiary limits are important: it records mid-sixteenth-century recollection under Franciscan supervision, decades after conquest, epidemic mortality, temple destruction, and prohibition of public Indigenous religion.

#### Florentine Codex, principally 1570s; completed ca. 1577

The bilingual Nahuatl-Spanish *Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España* contains several distinct evidentiary layers:

- Book 1 describes individual gods, including Tezcatzoncatl, “who belonged among the Centzon Totochtin.”
- Book 2 records the festival calendar, temples, priestly offices, divine impersonators, offerings, and sacrifice.
- Book 4 explains calendrical destinies, including rabbit signs and the danger of excess.
- Book 7 gives the creation of Sun and Moon and the rabbit impressed on the Moon’s face.
- Book 10 describes social categories and stereotypes, including drunken conduct.
- Book 11 discusses maguey and useful plants.

The surviving manuscript is in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence. Anderson and Dibble’s twelve-book English edition remains the standard full translation, although individual readings have been refined. The manuscript’s paintings and parallel columns can now be consulted through the Digital Florentine Codex. [Getty Research Institute, Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/)

#### *Codex Magliabechiano*, mid-sixteenth century

Its pulque-god sequence is the fullest pictorial roster. It is written and painted on European paper and belongs to a family including the *Codex Tudela*, *Codex Ixtlilxochitl*, and other derivative manuscripts. Elizabeth Hill Boone and Juan José Batalla Rosado reconstructed a lost prototype and copying history; consequently, repeated pictures across the group are not independent testimonies. The codex was apparently in Europe by about 1550, but its exact production date remains uncertain. [Persée review of Boone’s reconstruction](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hom_0439-4216_1986_num_26_99_368735)

#### *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, ca. 1550s–1560s

A central-Mexican pictorial-alphabetic manuscript with French custody history, now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. It links Ome Tochtli, the pulque deities, priests, calendrical signs, and sacrificial imagery. Its Christian-era glosses must be distinguished from its painted signs.

#### *Codex Mendoza*, ca. 1541–1542

Commissioned under Spanish colonial authority. It does not provide a full theology of the Centzon Totochtin but is important for:

- the place-name and conquest record of Tepoztlan;
- tribute involving maguey products;
- depictions of regulated drinking and tolerated drinking among the elderly;
- social penalties for illicit drunkenness.

Its evidence concerns lived regulation of *octli*, not simply divine myth.

#### Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme*, ca. 1570s–1581

Durán describes gods, festivals, ritual intoxication, priesthoods, and sacrifice. His *Book of the Gods and Rites* is indispensable but polemical: he wrote to identify and eradicate “idolatry,” while also preserving Indigenous explanations otherwise lost. Similarities with other chroniclers partly reflect common lost sources.

#### *Leyenda de los Soles*, manuscript dated 1558

An alphabetic Nahuatl cosmological narrative preserved within the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. It supplies variants of solar and lunar creation and is central to interpreting the rabbit in the Moon. The surviving manuscript history is complicated by loss of the original and dependence on copies.

#### *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, sixteenth century

Also transmitted through the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. Its account of Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl’s intoxication is crucial background for the sacred and politically destructive power of pulque, though the Centzon Totochtin are not necessarily actors in that episode.

#### *Histoyre du Mechique*, French manuscript preserving sixteenth-century Spanish material

Contains alternative creation and deity traditions, including the transformation of Mayahuel into maguey. Its language and transmission make it a mediated source: it cannot automatically be combined with Sahagún into a single canonical myth.

#### *Cantares Mexicanos*, principally later sixteenth century

The Nahuatl songs contain feast, flower, death, war, and intoxication imagery. They demonstrate that drink could signify elite conviviality, altered consciousness, transience, and ritual performance, but poetic “wine” imagery is not always a literal invocation of the Centzon Totochtin.

### Sources that do not securely attest the collective

The Coyolxauhqui Stone, Tlaltecuhtli monolith, and Sun Stone are not direct portraits of the Four Hundred Rabbits. They matter for the reconstructed ritual-cosmological setting and modern history of Mexica studies, not as independent attestations of this collective.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Secure sixteenth-century attestation]** Their shared domains are:

- *octli*/pulque and its fermentation;
- intoxication and its divergent behaviors;
- feasting and abundance;
- maguey fertility;
- agricultural and mountain fertility;
- calendrical fortune, especially rabbit days;
- lunar symbolism;
- dangerous excess, disorder, and sometimes death.

The gods are ambivalent. Ritual drinking could nourish the gods, seal communal rites, mark agricultural abundance, or belong legitimately to elders and selected participants. Unlicensed drunkenness could be punished severely. The same divine power that produced fecundity could produce violence, exposure, loss of rank, strangulation, drowning, or social ruin.

### Standard iconography

Pulque gods are generally anthropomorphic—not naturalistic rabbits—and are recognized by combinations rather than one universal attribute:

- **yacametztli**, a crescent or lunar nose ornament;
- black, red, yellow, and white facial painting in deity-specific arrangements;
- a rectangular or shield-like paper headdress, sometimes bearing a rabbit sign;
- feather tufts or down marking consecration;
- necklaces or wreaths of *yauhtli*;
- maguey-fiber or ritual-paper ornaments;
- shields and banners;
- foaming pulque vessels;
- paired or adjacent rabbit calendrical signs;
- occasional animal or costumed attendants.

The lunar nose ornament is important but not exclusive to one god. An isolated crescent ornament cannot by itself identify Ometochtli.

The *Codex Magliabechiano* images illustrate substantial visual variation among pulque gods. This variation supports the interpretation of the collective as a family of local manifestations rather than 400 identical rabbit beings. [INAH, *Iconografía mexicana VII*](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/repositorio/islandora/object/libro%3A423/datastream/OBJ/view)

### The rabbit, Moon, and maguey

The rabbit belongs to a dense symbolic system:

- the Moon’s markings were seen as a rabbit;
- the Moon’s waxing and waning paralleled vegetal and liquid cycles;
- maguey sap and fermentation were connected with lunar fertility;
- the rabbit day-sign could signal fertility and abundance but also dangerous excess;
- rabbits’ prolific reproduction reinforced fertility associations.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** López Austin interpreted a greenstone sculpture from the Templo Mayor as Mayahuel on the basis of calendrical signs One Rabbit and Two Rabbit and the myth of her death, dismemberment, and rebirth as maguey. The identification is influential, not an inscription spelling out her name. Guilhem Olivier notes both the strength and reconstructive character of López Austin’s lunar-maguey reading. [Arqueología Mexicana, Olivier on López Austin](https://www.arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/los-centzon-totochtin-o-400-conejos-de-alfredo-lopez-austin)

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

#### Mayahuel and Patecatl as parents

**[Later colonial systematization; popularly dominant]** Modern reference works frequently state that the Four Hundred Rabbits are the children of **Mayahuel**, goddess or personified power of maguey, and **Patecatl**, associated with fermentation, medicine, and the root or additive that strengthened pulque.

This formulation coherently joins plant, fermentation process, and finished intoxicant, but the surviving sixteenth-century corpus does not uniformly state a biological parentage of all 400. It is safest to describe this as a colonial-era reconstruction or one transmitted variant, not universal Mexica dogma.

#### Mayahuel as many-breasted nourisher

**[Colonial tradition]** Mayahuel is sometimes described as possessing many breasts and nursing the pulque gods. Numerical versions vary in later retellings—often 400, sometimes fewer. The image expresses inexhaustible nourishment and the many flows of maguey sap; exact numbers should not be standardized without citing the manuscript used.

#### Tezcatzoncatl as member, chief, or parent

**[Conflicting colonial and later traditions]** Sahagún securely calls Tezcatzoncatl one of the Centzon Totochtin. Later compilations sometimes elevate him to father or commander. These cannot all be treated as literal genealogy. They likely reflect the interchangeability of a class-name, a principal manifestation, and local cult titles.

#### Ometochtli as leader or collective form

Ometochtli is more securely understood as the calendrical and representative identity of the complex than as one universally acknowledged biological father.

### Every securely documented or materially relevant divine interaction

Because many sources list divine figures without placing them together in a narrative, “membership in the same pantheon” is not counted as an interaction.

- **Mayahuel — generative/nourishing relationship:** She provides the maguey and, in later genealogical systematization, is mother or nurse of the Four Hundred Rabbits. *Magliabechiano*-group imagery and colonial mythographic texts, mid-to-late sixteenth century. **Tier: later canonical elaboration / learned systematization.**

- **Patecatl — generative and technical relationship:** Credited with the medicinal or fermenting agent that turns maguey sap into powerful *octli*; later accounts make him father of the group with Mayahuel. Sixteenth-century deity lists and colonial compilations. **Tier: colonial attestation; genealogy is later systematization.**

- **Ometochtli — chief, calendrical representative, or collective embodiment:** The priest of Ome Tochtli gathers the *centzototochtin* in the *Telleriano-Remensis*. Mid-sixteenth century. **Tier: securely attested colonial record of earlier rite.**

- **Tezcatzoncatl — membership/joint classification:** Sahagún says he belongs among the Centzon Totochtin. *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, 1570s. **Tier: securely attested colonial testimony.**

- **Tepoztecatl — local membership and patronal specialization:** Listed or depicted as a pulque god; his local cult at Tepoztlan is a regional embodiment of the larger complex. *Primeros Memoriales*, Magliabechiano-group sources, sixteenth century. **Tier: regional variant securely attested in colonial sources.**

- **Papaztac and Tecuciztecatl/the Moon — violent transformation:** Papaztac strikes or casts a rabbit against the Moon’s face, diminishing its brilliance. The exact assailant differs among versions; Sahagún’s account leaves the acting god unnamed, while another tradition names Papaztac. Sixteenth-century solar-creation accounts. **Tier: regional/textual variant.**

- **Tlaloc/Tlaloque — functional association:** Pulque gods as mountain and fertility powers overlap with the *Tlaloque*, but surviving sources do not make every rabbit god a literal child or servant of Tlaloc. **Tier: modern scholarly reconstruction from ritual and iconographic association.**

- **Quetzalcoatl-Ehecatl and Mayahuel — indirect mythic relation:** In the *Histoyre du Mechique* complex, Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl brings Mayahuel away; she is killed and becomes maguey. This creates the substance from which the rabbits’ drink derives, but the Centzon Totochtin do not necessarily appear as co-actors. Sixteenth-century mediated text. **Tier: attested mythic background, not direct interaction.**

- **Tezcatlipoca and Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl — pulque episode, no direct rabbit appearance:** Tezcatlipoca or agents cause Topiltzin to drink and transgress in traditions preserved in Sahagún and the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **Tier: colonial attestation of a separate pulque myth; no documented Centzon Totochtin interaction.**

- **Centzon Huitznahua — proposed equation, not an explicit narrative meeting:** Karl Taube argues that the Four Hundred Rabbits and Four Hundred Southerners overlap as drunken, stellar, sacrificial collectives, citing the greenstone Mayahuel/Ometochtli figure found by the Coyolxauhqui monument and comparative myths of 400 drunken youths. **Tier: modern scholarly reconstruction, disputed.** [Taube, “The Bilimek Pulque Vessel”](https://www.mesoweb.org/publications/Works1/Taube_Works_v1.s.pdf)

No secure primary source was located in which the entire Four Hundred Rabbits collectively fight, marry, deceive, or converse with another named deity as a group. Most relationships are taxonomic, genealogical, or iconographic.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The multiplication of intoxication

**[Secure conceptual tradition; no single continuous myth]** The group explains why intoxication is not uniform. A person may sing, fight, weep, sleep, expose himself, commit sexual misconduct, drown, or hang himself: each course manifests a different “rabbit.” The mythic proposition is distributed among deity names, ritual descriptions, and statements about drunk people rather than preserved as one continuous story.

The narrative logic is:

1. Maguey yields sweet sap.
2. Fermentation or medicinal additives transform it.
3. The transformed drink contains many divine agencies.
4. Ingestion permits one of those agencies to manifest through the drinker.
5. Social identity and self-command can thereby be altered or destroyed.

This is why “he has such-and-such a rabbit” is historically more precise than saying the gods merely hosted celestial parties.

### Creation of pulque

Several traditions distinguish stages:

- Mayahuel embodies or becomes maguey.
- Humans learn to extract *aguamiel*.
- Patecatl discovers or supplies the substance enabling strong fermentation.
- Named pulque gods perfect or regionalize the drink.
- Tepoztecatl is sometimes credited in local tradition with perfecting pulque near Tepoztlan or at a mountain later identified with Popocatepetl.

**[Regional and late colonial development]** These accounts often reconcile formerly independent local patrons into an invention sequence. No single early text authoritatively gives all stages or makes all the named figures one nuclear family.

### The rabbit in the Moon

**[Secure sixteenth-century myth with variants]** In Sahagún’s account of the creation of the present Sun and Moon at Teotihuacan, wealthy Tecuciztecatl and afflicted Nanahuatzin perform penance. Nanahuatzin courageously enters the fire first and becomes the Sun; Tecuciztecatl follows and also shines. Because two equally brilliant lights are unacceptable, one god throws a rabbit into Tecuciztecatl’s face, dimming him into the Moon. The rabbit’s imprint remains visible.

Variants differ on:

- whether the object is a living rabbit, its image, or a rabbit-shaped vessel;
- whether the assailant is unnamed or Papaztac;
- whether the Moon was struck as punishment for cowardice, to establish cosmic hierarchy, or simply to reduce excessive brightness;
- whether “rabbit in the Moon” is integrated directly with the pulque gods.

The *Leyenda de los Soles* wording emphasizes reduction of the Moon’s original brilliance; later summaries often add explanatory psychology absent from the text. The identity of the thrower as Papaztac is therefore a **textual variant**, not a detail to insert silently into every version.

### Tepoztecatl’s adventures

**[Regional folklore layered over pre-Hispanic cult]** Tepoztlan traditions make Tepoztecatl a miraculous local hero: born to a maiden or washerwoman through supernatural agency, endowed with extraordinary powers, swallowed by or entering a monster and cutting his way out, defeating rivals, mastering pulque, and becoming lord or tutelary god of Tepoztlan.

Versions vary greatly and have continued changing under colonial Catholic and modern civic conditions. The monster episode resembles widespread heroic tale patterns and cannot be securely projected into a specific pre-Conquest manuscript. The archaeological temple establishes Tepoztecatl’s cultic importance, not the antiquity of every episode now told about him.

### Topiltzin’s intoxication: related but separate

In the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* and Sahagún’s account, Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl is tricked into drinking pulque, becomes intoxicated, and commits a transgression that contributes to his departure from Tollan. It shows pulque as an instrument of political-cosmic disorder. The Four Hundred Rabbits themselves are not securely named as participants; later retellings that insert them are expansions.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Tenochtitlan

Sahagún’s catalogue of the sacred precinct records a **Centzontotochtin** temple or ceremonial location. During **Tepeilhuitl**, the “Feast of the Mountains,” the gods’ priests or impersonators assembled and participated in drinking and ritual performance. Mountain, rain, crop, and pulque powers intersected here.

The testimony distinguishes:

- named gods;
- their temples;
- priests bearing the gods’ names;
- **ixiptlah**, human embodiments or representatives;
- sacrificial victims dressed as gods.

Translation that calls every such person “the god” can obscure which category the Nahuatl describes.

### Offerings

Documented or strongly associated offerings include:

- pulque presented in dedicated vessels;
- maguey products;
- ritual paper;
- incense, including *yauhtli*;
- flowers, food, and agricultural first fruits;
- bloodletting;
- quail and other animal offerings;
- human victims in the broader festival system.

### Human sacrifice

**[Documented, but deity-specific attribution requires care]** Nahua ritual religion included human sacrifice, and pulque deities participated in festivals where divine impersonation, victim consecration, decapitation, heart extraction, or other killing could occur. Some pictorial representations of pulque gods or their attendants bear sacrificial markers such as white down or paper insignia.

The evidence does **not** justify saying that all victims represented the Centzon Totochtin or that the 400 gods required 400 victims. The surviving record is particular and festival-specific.

The Huei Tzompantli discoveries of 2015–2017 materially confirm large-scale display and processing of human heads in the Tenochtitlan sacred precinct. INAH reports two excavation seasons at 24 República de Guatemala and identifies the remains as part of the Huei Tzompantli; continuing work includes a sample of 214 skulls. This validates descriptions of institutional sacrifice and skull display, but it does not assign those individuals specifically to the Four Hundred Rabbits. [INAH, Huei Tzompantli](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907) [INAH, bioarchaeological study](https://inah.gob.mx/boletines/a-10-anos-del-hallazgo-del-huei-tzompantli-de-tenochtitlan-los-craneos-humanos-son-objeto-de-analisis-de-vanguardia?highlight=WyJtdXNlbyIsInRlbXBsbyJd)

### Regulation of drinking

Pulque consumption was socially differentiated. Sources permit ritual drinking by specified participants and greater latitude for elderly people, while recording severe penalties for illicit drunkenness, especially among elites or officeholders. Claims that “only people over fifty-two could drink pulque” are too absolute: ritual, medical, postpartum, sacrificial, and festival contexts produced exceptions.

### Tepoztlan and the Tepozteco temple

The mountaintop sanctuary above Tepoztlan is the best-known surviving cult site connected with a named member of the group. The present structure belongs principally to the Late Postclassic period. It consists of a platform and temple commanding the valley; archaeological and historical evidence associates it with Ometochtli-Tepoztecatl and pilgrimage.

INAH’s current account dates major settlement construction by Xochimilca/Tepozteca groups to around 1200 CE and identifies Tepoztecatl among the pulque gods, while recognizing Ometochtli as the general pulque power. [INAH, Zona Arqueológica Tepozteco](https://www.inah.gob.mx/zonas/zona-arqueologica-tepozteco)

The building’s surviving form and its patron’s regional political importance are secure. Modern claims that every architectural element symbolizes intoxication are not.

### Priesthood

Named pulque gods had corresponding priests, whose office names could duplicate divine names. Their duties included:

- maintaining divine images and costume;
- organizing festival participants;
- handling pulque vessels and offerings;
- overseeing impersonators;
- singing and processional performance;
- enforcing ritual rules.

The *Telleriano-Remensis* statement that Ome Tochtli’s priest gathered the Centzon Totochtin may refer to an assembly of divine representatives or priests, not supernatural rabbits physically convening.

### Temple prostitution

No reliable evidence establishes an institution properly described as “temple prostitution” for the Centzon Totochtin. Sexual license, nudity, fertility symbolism, and colonial accusations of “sodomy” or promiscuity occur in accounts of intoxication and Huastec identity, but these do not demonstrate a formally organized system of cultic prostitution. **Finding: unestablished.**

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before ca. 1200: antecedents

Maguey exploitation, fermented-drink ritual, rabbit symbolism, and lunar imagery have earlier Mesoamerican histories. These are antecedents, not secure attestations of the named collective. Scholarly attempts to recognize the entire Centzon Totochtin complex at Teotihuacan remain comparative reconstructions.

### Ca. 1200–1521: Late Postclassic florescence

Regional pulque gods were integrated into expanding central-Mexican ritual and political networks. Their names often preserve places or peoples—Tepoztlan, Tollan, Colhuacan, Acolhuacan, Cuextlan—suggesting aggregation of local cults rather than invention of a uniform Mexica group.

The Borgia-group manuscripts show mature pulque-god iconography. Tepoztlan’s mountain sanctuary demonstrates a strong civic cult. At imperial Tenochtitlan, pulque gods entered the sacred-precinct and festival systems.

### 1521–ca. 1580: conquest, suppression, and documentation

Spanish victory dismantled the public temple institutions. Images were destroyed or hidden, temples demolished or converted, priests displaced, and sacrifices prohibited. At the same time, friars and Indigenous assistants produced the documents that now preserve most names and rites.

This is not a neutral transition. The corpus was created amid forced conversion, epidemics, confiscation of sacred objects, and campaigns against “idolatry.” Nevertheless, Indigenous-language testimony often preserves distinctions flattened by Spanish prose.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: survival and transformation

Pulque remained economically and socially important, but the public cult of the Four Hundred Rabbits ceased to operate as a state institution. Local mountain rites, agricultural offerings, stories, and drinking customs persisted in Christianized or covert settings. Colonial authorities taxed and regulated pulque while clergy continued campaigns against intoxication and non-Christian ritual.

Evidence supports continuity of substances, places, and practices more strongly than continuous formal worship of a collective called Centzon Totochtin.

### Nineteenth century: antiquarian recovery and nationalization

Printed editions and collections turned colonial manuscripts into sources for a national pre-Hispanic past. “Aztec Bacchus” language and tidy Greco-Roman-style family trees became common. Tepoztlan’s shrine became simultaneously archaeological monument, local sacred place, and tourist destination.

### 1900–1970s: comparative iconography

Eduard Seler’s readings of the Borgia group established many iconographic identifications still used today. Later scholars refined or challenged them by treating manuscripts as internally structured divinatory systems rather than picture dictionaries.

Henry B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology grouped central-Mexican gods into deity complexes. This clarified repeated traits but could also make fluid local gods appear more systematic than the primary sources warrant.

### 1978–present: Templo Mayor archaeology

On 21 February 1978 electrical workers uncovered the monumental Coyolxauhqui relief, initiating the modern Proyecto Templo Mayor. Excavation supplied stratified ritual deposits, including objects associated with Mayahuel and pulque symbolism. [Proyecto Templo Mayor history](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/proyecto-templo-mayor)

The Tlaltecuhtli monolith, found on 2 October 2006, transformed knowledge of late imperial sculpture and funerary-political ritual but does not portray the Centzon Totochtin. [INAH, Tlaltecuhtli discovery](https://inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/tlaltecuhtli-la-deidad-de-tierra-que-emergio-del-templo-mayor)

These discoveries are essential contextual controls: they show how written testimony can correspond to excavated architecture and offerings, while also warning against assigning every lunar or fertility object to the rabbit gods.

### Present-day Tepoztlan

The **Reto al Tepozteco**, performed annually around 8 September, dramatizes the conversion and baptism of the last Tepoztecan ruler and confrontation with neighboring lords. It is performed partly in Nahuatl and combines civic memory, Catholic observance, Indigenous identity, pilgrimage, theatrical reinvention, and tourism.

It is not an unchanged survival of a pre-Conquest Tepoztecatl festival. Its present narrative specifically stages conversion to Christianity. Yet its attachment to the mountain, patronal hero, local authorities, and communal offerings gives it genuine historical continuity as a transformed tradition. Mexico’s tourism authority records the annual 8 September performance. [SECTUR, Tepoztlán](https://www.sectur.gob.mx/gobmx/pueblos-magicos/tepoztlan-morelos/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and popular mythology

Modern summaries commonly turn the collective into 400 literal cartoon rabbits holding perpetual parties. This is a **modern simplification**. Pre-Hispanic and colonial images usually show anthropomorphic gods, priests, or impersonators; rabbit signs identify their calendrical and symbolic field.

The following frequently repeated assertions are not uniformly ancient:

- exactly 400 individually distinct personalities existed;
- each had a fixed, named type of drunkenness;
- all were literal children of Mayahuel and Patecatl;
- they met regularly for divine parties;
- Tepoztecatl was universally their king;
- they were harmless comic gods;
- they were equivalent to a single “Aztec Dionysus.”

Each derives from partial evidence, synthesis, or modern narrative convenience.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist reception

**[Modern revival]** Individual practitioners of Mexicayotl, Nahua reconstructionism, eclectic paganism, and internet-based polytheism sometimes honor Mayahuel, Ometochtli, Tepoztecatl, or the Centzon Totochtin. Public evidence does not support reliable estimates of scale or a single standardized liturgy. Modern offerings of pulque may consciously reconstruct codical imagery, continue local custom, or combine both.

No documented continuous secret priesthood transmitting a complete pre-Conquest Centzon Totochtin liturgy to the present was found.

### Occult and esoteric claims

Searches for Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Hermetic, grimoire, and chaos-magic traditions produced no evidence that the Centzon Totochtin held a significant place in the foundational texts of those movements. Contemporary occult webpages may reinterpret “400” as psychic multiplicity, ego fragmentation, mystical intoxication, or elemental abundance. Those are **modern inventions**, not translations of a surviving Nahua doctrine.

Claims that pulque encoded sexual alchemy, kundalini, “Christic energy,” or universal Logos metaphysics lack support in the sixteenth-century corpus.

### Literature, games, and commerce

The collective appears increasingly in fantasy art, role-playing settings, online fiction, and games as multiplying drunken rabbit units. Such portrayals materially strengthen the literal-rabbit image but belong to modern entertainment.

Commercial uses include bars, beer and spirits branding, restaurants, artworks, and perfume—Black Phoenix Alchemy Lab, for example, markets a “Centzon Totochtin” perfume. These attest contemporary name recognition, not revived ritual tradition. [Black Phoenix Alchemy Lab](https://blackphoenixalchemylab.com/products/centzon-totochtin-perfume-oil)

A modern error particularly common in commercial material is replacing pulque with tequila. Tequila is a distilled agave spirit whose production history and ritual identity differ from pre-Conquest *octli*.

### National and local politics

Tepoztecatl functions as an emblem of Tepoztlan’s distinctiveness, Indigenous inheritance, landscape, and tourism economy. The mountain and pyramid can become sites of disputes over access, conservation, development, and commercialization. This is civic appropriation rather than evidence that the ancient cult survived institutionally unchanged.

No substantial modern extremist appropriation specific to the Centzon Totochtin was found. General nationalist uses of “Aztec spirituality” should not be attributed to this collective without direct evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Seler and iconographic identification

Eduard Seler established many correlations among codex figures, calendrical signs, apparel lists, and Spanish glosses. His work remains foundational, but some identifications depend on assuming that similar regalia always denotes the same god.

### Nicholson’s typology

H. B. Nicholson’s “Religion in Pre-Hispanic Central Mexico” (1971) organized deities into functional complexes. The Centzon Totochtin belong principally to the pulque/fertility complex. The model is analytical, not an Indigenous theological chart.

### Boone and the pictorial manuscripts

Elizabeth Hill Boone’s work demonstrated that divinatory codices organize time, fate, ritual action, and meaning through visual programs. Her reconstruction of the Magliabechiano group also showed that multiple surviving manuscripts may descend from a lost common source. Therefore apparent multiple attestation sometimes represents copying rather than independent confirmation.

### Batalla Rosado

Juan José Batalla Rosado separates the Indigenous painted “book” from the European written glosses and reconstructs the sequence of lost and surviving Magliabechiano-group manuscripts. This is crucial for the pulque section: image and commentary may have different dates, authors, and intentions.

### López Austin

López Austin emphasized:

- the rabbit-Moon-maguey nexus;
- bodily and psychological models of divine possession;
- the mountain as repository of water, seed, ancestors, and fertility;
- the historical layering of Mesoamerican myth.

Some identifications, including the greenstone Templo Mayor figure as Mayahuel/Ometochtli, remain scholarly arguments rather than explicit inscriptions.

### Taube’s Centzon Totochtin–Centzon Huitznahua proposal

Karl Taube argues that the rabbit gods may overlap with the Four Hundred Southerners as nocturnal, drunken, sacrificial, and stellar beings. Evidence includes:

- the Templo Mayor association of a Mayahuel/Ometochtli figure with the Coyolxauhqui context;
- myths of 400 drunken youths;
- directional and calendrical symbolism;
- imagery on the Bilimek pulque vessel.

This is a stimulating **modern comparative reconstruction**, not a plainly stated sixteenth-century equation. Similarity in the number 400 does not by itself prove identity.

### Major evidentiary problems

1. Almost all explanatory prose is post-Conquest.
2. Christian authors translated *teteoh* as “gods,” “idols,” or “devils,” each carrying different assumptions.
3. Indigenous informants were recalling suppressed rites.
4. Manuscripts copied common prototypes.
5. Divine names could also name priests and impersonators.
6. Local gods shared costumes and calendrical titles.
7. Iconography lacks alphabetic captions.
8. Modern summaries routinely harmonize conflicting sources.
9. A ritual category is often mistaken for a biological family.
10. General pulque evidence is frequently attributed to the Centzon Totochtin without proof.

### Primary evidence and present locations

- *Florentine Codex*: Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence; digitally reunited and searchable by the Getty Research Institute.
- *Codex Magliabechiano*: Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence.
- *Codex Borgia*: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana.
- *Codex Borbonicus*: Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris.
- *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*: Bibliothèque nationale de France.
- *Codex Mendoza*: Bodleian Library, Oxford.
- *Primeros Memoriales*: Real Biblioteca/Palacio Real, Madrid.
- Templo Mayor excavated objects: principally Museo del Templo Mayor and associated INAH repositories.
- Tepozteco architecture: preserved in situ under INAH administration.

## 11. Conclusions and Evidentiary Findings

The Centzon Totochtin are best reconstructed not as a fixed family of 400 individually characterized rabbit gods but as a historically layered collective of pulque powers. Their unity lies in shared drink, calendrical, lunar, agricultural, mountain, and behavioral symbolism; their diversity lies in local patronage, distinct names and regalia, and the many ways intoxication could alter a person.

The secure core is comparatively narrow:

- the collective name is sixteenth-century Nahuatl;
- multiple named pulque gods existed;
- Ometochtli/Two Rabbit represented or led the complex;
- the drinker’s behavior could be described as “his rabbit”;
- pulque gods had distinctive regalia, priests, rites, and temple settings;
- Tepoztecatl possessed an important regional cult at Tepoztlan;
- rabbit, Moon, maguey, fertility, and intoxication were closely associated.

The familiar genealogy from Mayahuel and Patecatl, a roster of exactly 400 psychological types, and a single organized “pantheon of party rabbits” belong to later systematization or modern simplification. Conversely, reducing them to metaphor also goes too far: texts and images treat the pulque gods as real ritual agents who could be embodied by priests and *ixiptlah*, approached through offerings, and manifested in dangerous human behavior.

The documentary record therefore preserves neither one biography nor one canonical myth. It preserves a network: regional gods absorbed into a central-Mexican ritual category; a substance understood as divine and socially perilous; calendrical signs turned into names; drinkers made temporary sites of divine manifestation; and, after conquest, a suppressed cult reconstructed from manuscripts, surviving places, material deposits, and transformed communal memory.
