# Tezcatlipoca: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A male *teōtl*—a divine power or god—in Late Postclassic Nahua, especially Mexica and central Mexican, religion. Colonial missionaries sometimes reclassified him polemically as a “devil,” but that is a Christian colonial interpretation, not the Indigenous category.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The deity identifiable by Tezcatlipoca’s diagnostic black facial paint, obsidian smoking mirror, and amputated or mirror-replaced foot appears securely in Late Postclassic central-Mexican ritual manuscripts, above all the preconquest **Codex Borgia**, conventionally dated approximately 1250–1521. Proposed Epiclassic or Early Postclassic precursors, including figures at Tula and earlier mirror-bearing or one-legged beings, remain iconographic hypotheses because no surviving preconquest alphabetic inscription names them. The earliest securely *named* attestations are therefore sixteenth-century Nahuatl and Spanish colonial texts that record traditions demonstrably current at conquest.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

Tezcatlipoca was one of the most comprehensive and politically charged divine powers of Late Postclassic central Mexico. Nahua sources describe him as invisible like “night and wind,” omnipresent, knowing human thoughts, conferring and withdrawing rulership, creating prosperity and reversing it without warning, causing war and discord, punishing concealed wrongdoing, rejuvenating or destroying people, appearing in deceptive forms, and presiding over divination, fate, night, jaguars, warriors, rulers, slaves, young men, obsidian, and mirrors.

No single modern label—“night god,” “trickster,” “sorcerer,” “creator,” “god of providence,” or “god of war”—contains him. The *Florentine Codex* presents precisely this instability: he could give wealth, office, courage, and victory, then remove them; he “mocked” human certainty. Guilhem Olivier consequently interprets metamorphosis, inversion, deception, conflict, and unstable identity as structural rather than incidental features of the deity.

### Name

**Tezcatlipoca**, Classical Nahuatl *Tēzcatlipōca*.

- *Tēzcatl* securely means “mirror.”
- *Pōca* is associated with smoking or emitting smoke.
- “Smoking Mirror” is the conventional translation.
- A stricter grammatical rendering sometimes proposed is “the mirror smokes” or “smoke of the mirror.” Nahuatl specialists caution that the fossilized divine name need not behave like a transparent ordinary compound.
- The mirror is normally understood as polished obsidian. Its smoke may signify burning resin, the opacity and revelation of divination, the night sky, or the dangerous visual power of reflected images. These are scholarly interpretations, not an ancient prose definition.

Spellings in colonial manuscripts include **Tezcatlipoca, Tezcatlipuca, Tezcatepoca, Tezcatlepoca**, and other forms reflecting unstable early-modern orthography.

### Major documented names and titles

The following are principally attested in Books I, III, VI, and XII of the *Florentine Codex*, the *Primeros Memoriales*, Durán, and related Nahua records. A title appearing in a prayer to Tezcatlipoca is not necessarily his exclusive personal name: Nahua divine titles circulated among gods.

- **Titlacahuan / Titlacāhuan** — conventionally “He whose slaves/men we are,” “We are his people,” or “Our master.” The morphology has been debated; the title emphasizes human dependence. **Tier: securely attested conquest-era tradition.**
- **Yāōtl** — “Enemy,” “Warrior,” or “one associated with enmity.” **Securely attested.**
- **Necoc Yāōtl** — “Enemy on both sides,” the maker of conflict between opposing parties. **Securely attested.**
- **Moyocoyani / Moyocoya** — “He who creates or devises himself,” also “the arbitrary inventor.” Missionary translations such as “almighty” import Christian vocabulary and should not be treated as literal equivalence. **Securely attested; interpretation disputed.**
- **Tloque Nahuaque / in Tloque in Nahuaque** — approximately “Owner of the near and the surrounding,” conventionally “Lord of the Near and the Nigh.” Molina’s 1571 dictionary glossed the expression as the one beside whom or through whom all things exist and are sustained. It is employed for Tezcatlipoca but is not demonstrably exclusive to him. **Securely attested title; later monotheistic readings are scholarly or confessional constructions.**
- **Yohualli Ehecatl** — “Night, Wind,” a paired expression for what cannot be seen or grasped. The *Florentine Codex* describes Tezcatlipoca as invisible like night and wind. Quetzalcoatl may also bear this expression. **Securely attested shared title.**
- **Telpochtli** — “Young man” or “youth,” connected with warriors and the *telpochcalli*, the young men’s house. **Securely attested.**
- **Telpochtli Yaotl** — “Young Warrior/Enemy.” **Securely attested.**
- **Nezahualpilli** — “Fasting prince” or “fasting noble.” **Securely attested.**
- **Ipalnemoani** — “He through whom one lives” or “Giver of life.” This is a mobile theological title, not proof that all beings called *Ipalnemoani* were understood as Tezcatlipoca. **Secure title; identification varies by context.**
- **Teyocoyani** — “Creator/fashioner of people.” **Securely attested title.**
- **Teimatini** — “Knower of people.” **Securely attested.**
- **Techichihuani** — “Adorner or arranger of people.” **Securely attested.**
- **Moquequeloa / Moquequeloatzin** — “The mocker,” one who makes sport of people. **Securely attested.**
- **Monenequi** — approximately “the capricious/arbitrary one”; translations such as “tyrant” are interpretive. **Securely attested.**
- **Yayauhqui Tezcatlipoca** — “Black” or “dark Tezcatlipoca.” **Securely attested colonial cosmological designation.**
- **Tepeyollotl** — “Heart of the Mountain,” a jaguar-form night and cave power. His relation to Tezcatlipoca is iconographically and conceptually strong, but sources do not reduce every Tepeyollotl image to a mere “avatar.” **Syncretistic or aspectual identification, source-dependent.**
- **Itztli** — “Obsidian” or “obsidian blade,” a calendrical deity closely related to Tezcatlipoca in divinatory imagery. Complete identity is a modern systematization if applied universally.

Sahagún compared Tezcatlipoca to **Jupiter** because of perceived supremacy and governance. This is sixteenth-century *interpretatio* by a Franciscan author, not a pre-Hispanic equation.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The evidentiary limit

Central Mexican manuscripts employ conventional imagery rather than phonetic captions naming every god. Accordingly, identifying a figure depends upon an attribute cluster: black paint, yellow facial bands, the *anahuatl* pectoral, mirror, smoke or flame, missing foot, jaguar associations, and characteristic regalia. A lone ancient obsidian mirror is evidence for mirror technology and ritual use, not automatically evidence for Tezcatlipoca.

Proposed antecedents at Classic-period Teotihuacan or among Maya gods are **modern comparative reconstructions**. Shared mirrors, jaguars, nocturnal power, serpentine replacement limbs, or rulership do not establish identity.

### Principal pictorial and documentary corpus

- **Codex Borgia**: preconquest or very early conquest-period ritual-divinatory screenfold, probably from Puebla–Tlaxcala or a neighboring central-Mexican region. Tezcatlipoca appears in calendrical and cosmological sequences, notably the large body mapped with day signs on page 17 and in the enigmatic pages 29–46. Seler read much of the latter as astronomical or ritual; Boone interpreted it as a structured cosmogonic narrative; other scholars have proposed priestly initiation or visionary experience. **Early secure iconographic corpus; interpretation disputed.**
- **Codex Fejérváry-Mayer, Codex Laud, and Codex Vaticanus B**: members of the Borgia-group divinatory tradition, Late Postclassic in content and probably preconquest in manufacture. They situate mirror- and jaguar-associated gods within almanacs, directions, night-lords, and ritual prognostics. **Secure early iconographic material.**
- **Codex Borbonicus**: central-Mexican calendrical manuscript made either immediately before or shortly after conquest; its exact date remains disputed. It depicts festival and divinatory cycles, including Toxcatl-related material. **Early but dating disputed.**
- **Codex Mendoza**, commissioned about 1541: primarily imperial history, tribute, and social organization; important for the political and material setting of state religion rather than as a sustained Tezcatlipoca mythology. **Early colonial documentary source.**
- **Primeros Memoriales**, compiled at Tepepolco around 1558–1561 by Sahagún and Nahua collaborators: preserves early questionnaires, deity descriptions, vestments, priestly offices, and festival accounts that preceded the final *Florentine Codex*. **Secure early colonial testimony, mediated by a missionary project.**
- **Florentine Codex**, compiled through several stages and completed about 1577 at Tlatelolco: Nahuatl text, Spanish text, and Indigenous paintings. Book I describes Tezcatlipoca; Book II gives Toxcatl; Book III contains Toltec/Quetzalcoatl traditions; Book VI preserves prayers to the deity, especially enthronement and crisis speeches; Book XII narrates the conquest and the Toxcatl massacre. Anderson and Dibble’s English translation follows the Nahuatl column, which often differs materially from Sahagún’s Spanish. **Foundational conquest-generation corpus, not an untouched preconquest scripture.**
- **Diego Durán, *Libro de los ritos* / *Book of the Gods and Rites***, composed principally in the 1570s: chapters IV–V describe Tezcatlipoca, his temple, clergy, image, and Toxcatl. Durán’s account differs from Sahagún’s chronology and details. **Early colonial compilation from Indigenous and missionary testimony.**
- **Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas**, probably based on traditions collected in the 1530s: Spanish prose cosmogony known through later transmission. It gives the primordial couple’s four sons, but explicitly names only the black son as Yayauhqui Tezcatlipoca while another son bears the red designation. The familiar diagram making all four sons “four Tezcatlipocas”—black Tezcatlipoca, red Xipe Totec, white Quetzalcoatl, and blue Huitzilopochtli—is a later scholarly synthesis, not a diagram preserved in the manuscript. **Early colonial learned systematization; modern expansion disputed.**
- **Histoyre du Mechique**, preserved in French in a manuscript associated with André Thévet and based on sixteenth-century Mexican information: describes Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl lowering and tearing apart the many-mouthed earth being. **Early colonial regional cosmogony.**
- **Leyenda de los Soles**, Nahuatl text copied in 1558 within the manuscript conventionally called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*: records the successive suns and their catastrophes. **Early colonial record of Indigenous cosmogony.**
- **Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, Nahuatl annalistic text compiled in the sixteenth century: contains the fullest surviving account of Tezcatlipoca’s deceptions of Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl and the destruction of Tollan. **Early colonial regional-historical tradition.**
- **Cantares Mexicanos**, principally recorded in the later sixteenth century: ritual and courtly songs contain mobile divine titles such as *Tloque Nahuaque*, *Ipalnemoani*, and *Titlacahuan*. Christian and preconquest registers coexist, so theological attribution requires poem-by-poem analysis. **Mixed colonial corpus.**
- **Codex Tudela and Codex Magliabechiano group**, mid-sixteenth century: paintings and Spanish glosses of gods, festivals, calendrics, and ritual practices. The Magliabechiano manuscript’s folio 89r juxtaposes Tezcatlipoca with Quetzalcoatl, Tlaloc, and Xiuhtecuhtli; related manuscripts illustrate Toxcatl. **Early colonial pictorial testimony copied from a lost prototype.**
- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis and Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos**: sixteenth-century calendrical-historical manuscripts with related imagery and European annotations. Their glosses must be separated from the Indigenous paintings they interpret. **Early colonial mixed pictorial-commentarial corpus.**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Secure conquest-era tradition:** Tezcatlipoca governed or penetrated rulership, judgment, fortune, night, conflict, war, divination, youth, slavery, penitence, feasting, and reversal. Book VI prayers address him during the installation or death of rulers, famine, war, and plague. The ruler was metaphorically the god’s flute: an instrument elevated and discarded at will.

He was not simply “evil.” The same source tradition makes him giver of office, wealth, children, courage, victory, and life. His menace lay in sovereignty without human control.

### Diagnostic iconography

- Black facial or body paint, frequently combined with transverse yellow bands.
- A polished obsidian mirror, often emitting smoke or flame.
- A mirror at the temple or back of the head, at the chest, or replacing a foot.
- The circular **anahuatl** pectoral.
- Flint knives, arrows, spear-thrower, warrior equipment, and elaborate feather regalia.
- Jaguar skin or complete jaguar form.
- A missing foot or lower leg.

No single feature always suffices. Mirrors, black paint, jaguar skins, and circular pectorals also occur with related gods or ritual actors.

### The missing foot

Several distinct traditions have been compressed into one explanation:

1. In the *Histoyre du Mechique*, Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl lower the monstrous earth deity and divide her body. Later retellings say Tezcatlipoca used a foot as bait and lost it when the monster bit it; that precise bait episode is not uniformly present in the earliest surviving versions.
2. In iconography, a smoking mirror, serpent, or exposed bone can replace the absent foot. This is securely visible but the images do not supply a prose explanation.
3. Olivier compares the missing foot with initiation, sacrificial loss, hunting, and Mesoamerican one-legged supernatural beings. These are **modern comparative interpretations**, not a single attested Indigenous exegesis.

### Jaguar, night, and mirror

The jaguar’s nocturnal vision, predatory power, spotted coat, cave associations, and elite-warrior symbolism made it an apt Tezcatlipoca form. In the Five Suns tradition, he becomes a jaguar after Quetzalcoatl casts him down, and jaguars devour the first age’s inhabitants. Tepeyollotl, the jaguar “Heart of the Mountain,” shares Tezcatlipoca’s mirror and regalia in several manuscripts.

The smoking obsidian mirror is at once emblem, body part, and instrument. Colonial sources associate mirrors with seeing concealed things and with Tezcatlipoca’s revelation of Quetzalcoatl’s embodied, aging face. Calling it straightforwardly a “scrying mirror” is a plausible modern description but not a literal sixteenth-century technical label.

### Calendar

Tezcatlipoca is associated with:

- the trecena beginning **1 Ocelotl (1 Jaguar)**;
- the day sign **Acatl (Reed)** in particular almanac arrangements;
- the lords of the night and directional-cosmological schemes;
- **Toxcatl**, the fifth twenty-day period in Sahagún’s ordering.

Calendar correlations differ across codices. Modern charts often falsely imply a single uniform pan-Nahua system.

### The Sun Stone

The Mexica Sun Stone, carved under Axayacatl or a successor in the late fifteenth century, visualizes calendrical and solar ideology, including the prior world-ending signs. It supports the existence of the sequence of ages but does not itself narrate Tezcatlipoca’s rivalry with Quetzalcoatl. Reading the central face simply as Tezcatlipoca is one proposal among identifications including Tonatiuh and earth/solar composites.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Competing cosmologies

**Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas:** Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl have four sons. The black son is Yayauhqui Tezcatlipoca; Quetzalcoatl and Huitzilopochtli are separate brothers. This source supports a group of four creator brothers but not, without interpretation, the later textbook claim that all four were uniformly called colored Tezcatlipocas.

**Later systematization:** Black Tezcatlipoca—north; red Xipe Totec—east; white Quetzalcoatl—west; blue Huitzilopochtli—south. Parts of this color-direction system have codical support, but its perfectly symmetrical presentation is largely twentieth-century synthesis associated with scholarship following Alfonso Caso. **Tier: modern scholarly reconstruction, now often presented incorrectly as a verbatim ancient doctrine.**

Other sources call primordial creators Tonacatecuhtli/Tonacacihuatl, Ometecuhtli/Omecihuatl, or use titles such as *Tloque Nahuaque*. Miguel León-Portilla’s interpretation of **Ometeotl** as an underlying Nahua metaphysical supreme deity was influential; later specialists have questioned whether the evidence sustains so unified a theology. Tezcatlipoca must not automatically be collapsed into Ometeotl.

### Documented god–god interactions

The list below separates discrete scenes. Shared membership in an almanac is not counted as narrative interaction unless the image or text joins the beings in an action.

- **Quetzalcoatl — cosmogonic collaborator:** Together they lower, attack, and divide the many-mouthed earth being. *Histoyre du Mechique*, sixteenth-century manuscript preserving early colonial testimony. **Regional early-colonial variant.**
- **Tlaltecuhtli/Atlateutli — violent creation:** Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl transform her body into the earth; she continues demanding human blood or offerings. *Histoyre du Mechique*. **Regional early-colonial variant.**
- **Quetzalcoatl — first-sun rivalry:** Quetzalcoatl strikes down Tezcatlipoca; Tezcatlipoca becomes a jaguar and the first age ends. *Leyenda de los Soles* and related colonial cosmogonies. **Canonical conquest-era attestation with variant ordering.**
- **Quetzalcoatl — second-sun retaliation:** Tezcatlipoca casts down Quetzalcoatl’s sun; wind destroys the age. *Leyenda de los Soles* and *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*. **Canonical, variants differ.**
- **Tlaloc — installation or displacement as sun:** Creator gods place Tlaloc in a later solar age, which ends in fiery rain. *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* and related solar accounts. Tezcatlipoca’s precise individual role varies. **Learned early-colonial systematization.**
- **Chalchiuhtlicue — solar-age destruction:** She rules the water sun; Tezcatlipoca’s accusation that her generosity is self-interested makes her weep until the world floods in the *Leyenda de los Soles*. **Canonical early-colonial attestation.**
- **Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl — mirror deception:** Tezcatlipoca shows the Tollan priest-ruler his face in a mirror, producing shame and destabilization. *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **Regional conquest-era tradition.**
- **Quetzalcoatl — intoxication and fall:** Tezcatlipoca and fellow sorcerers induce him to drink pulque; the transgression precipitates departure from Tollan. *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*; Durán and other chronicles give related but not identical narratives. **Regional variants.**
- **Ihuimecatl and Toltecatl — alliance of sorcerers:** They cooperate with Tezcatlipoca in deceptive actions against Quetzalcoatl and Tollan. *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. Their exact status—gods, divine sorcerers, or named manifestations—remains uncertain. **Regional attestation.**
- **Huemac — deception and humiliation:** Tezcatlipoca appears in forms that provoke disastrous desire, commerce, dancing, and war among the Toltecs; in one episode sorcerers take female form in relation to Huemac. *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **Regional attestation.**
- **Xochiquetzal — sexual or marital appropriation:** Later chronicles report that Tezcatlipoca took Xochiquetzal from Tlaloc. The versions and divine identities are unstable. **Early-modern accretion/regional variant; not a uniform genealogy.**
- **Tlaloc — rivalry over Xochiquetzal:** Present in colonial chronicle traditions, but not securely reconstructable as a single preconquest canonical episode. **Regional early-modern variant.**
- **Tlazolteotl/Ixcuina — parallel sexuality, confession, and transgression:** They share thematic territory in ritual theology, but no secure major narrative encounter between two independent characters has been identified. **Absence of evidence for a discrete interaction.**
- **Huitzilopochtli — shared Toxcatl:** In the conquest-period festival complex, Tezcatlipoca’s Toxcatl observance and Huitzilopochtli’s image and rites overlap; Book XII’s massacre narrative centers the latter. **Secure ritual association, not necessarily a narrative encounter.**
- **Xipe Totec/Camaxtli/Mixcoatl — “Red Tezcatlipoca” relation:** Some colonial cosmologies and modern reconstructions associate the red creator brother with one or more of these gods. The equations vary by source and region. **Syncretistic identification, disputed.**
- **Tepeyollotl — jaguar manifestation:** Manuscript attributes closely join Tepeyollotl with Tezcatlipoca; whether “same god,” manifestation, or cognate power is an analytical choice. **Iconographically secure relation; ontology uncertain.**
- **Itztli — obsidian manifestation:** Almanacs connect Itztli’s obsidian knife and nocturnal judgment with Tezcatlipoca. **Aspectual or syncretistic relation; no surviving prose scene.**
- **Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl — parentage:** They produce the creator brothers in *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*. **Early-colonial learned cosmology.**
- **Four Toxcatl “wives”—Xochiquetzal, Xilonen, Atlatonan, Huixtocihuatl:** These goddesses are embodied by women paired ceremonially with Tezcatlipoca’s impersonator shortly before his death. The relationship belongs first to ritual representation, not necessarily to permanent divine marriage. *Florentine Codex*, Book II. **Secure ritual attestation.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The successive suns

**Earliest surviving full accounts:** *Leyenda de los Soles* (copy dated 1558) and *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* (traditions collected early in the colonial period).

In one principal sequence, Tezcatlipoca becomes the first sun. Because he is incomplete or only half a sun, Quetzalcoatl strikes him down. Tezcatlipoca becomes a jaguar; jaguars eat the inhabitants, remembered as giants. Quetzalcoatl governs the following age until Tezcatlipoca throws him down, raising a destructive wind and transforming survivors into monkeys. Tlaloc’s age ends in fiery rain; Chalchiuhtlicue’s ends in flood. The current sun arises through a distinct divine sacrifice at Teotihuacan.

The sources disagree about order, duration, the agent responsible for each catastrophe, and whether a deity “is” the sun or rules it. The now-standard “Five Suns” chart is a reconciliation of variant records.

### Creation from the earth monster

In the *Histoyre du Mechique*, Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl bring down a female earth being covered with mouths and eyes. Transforming into great serpents, they seize and tear her; her body becomes the ordered earth. From hair, skin, eyes, mouth, shoulders, and other parts come vegetation, caves, springs, and mountains. Because of the violence, she weeps or refuses nourishment unless compensated with human blood and hearts.

Later summaries often call her **Cipactli**, while this version uses a form of **Tlaltecuhtli/Atlateutli**. Treating these as one universally named monster is harmonization. The lost-foot bait episode is attached to this complex in modern retellings but is not equally explicit in all early witnesses.

### The fall of Tollan

The *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* presents Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl as priest-ruler of Tollan. Tezcatlipoca arrives with a mirror and forces him to confront an aged or embodied appearance. Other disguised sorcerers offer pulque as medicine. Quetzalcoatl becomes drunk, sings, and transgresses with his sister or a female relation in the annals’ difficult wording. Ashamed, he leaves Tollan.

Tezcatlipoca’s campaign continues through grotesque transformations and social disasters: he appears as a naked Huastec whose body arouses a ruler’s daughter; he becomes a vendor or deceptive dancer; crowds die in panics or are led into water or ravines; the Toltecs attack him, only for his corpse to become so foul and heavy that moving it kills them. Tollan collapses through desire, credulity, faction, scarcity, and ritual pollution.

This narrative is not neutral history of archaeological Tula. It is sixteenth-century Nahua sacred history explaining the ruin of an exemplary city. Whether Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca encode historical factions, calendrical opposites, priestly institutions, or cosmic alternation remains disputed.

### Mockeries and reversals

The *Florentine Codex* describes Tezcatlipoca as giving a poor person wealth and then removing it, appointing a ruler and then destroying him, and exposing secret faults. Encounters with nocturnal apparitions—an ash-covered bundle, a headless or chest-open being, or the sound of chopping—could become ordeals: a courageous person might seize the apparition and compel a gift; a coward might be harmed. The exact identity of every apparition is not always Tezcatlipoca, although later summaries often collect them under his name.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Toxcatl

Toxcatl was Tezcatlipoca’s great annual feast in Sahagún’s Book II. Correlating the Indigenous calendar with Julian dates is disputed: Sahagún’s and Durán’s reconstructed dates differ, and neither should be converted mechanically into a permanent modern date.

A physically unblemished captive young man was selected as Tezcatlipoca’s **ixiptla**, a living embodiment or representative. For approximately a year he was trained to speak elegantly, play flutes, carry flowers and tobacco, and move through Tenochtitlan in divine regalia. People saluted him as the god’s presence. This was not merely an actor playing a role: *ixiptla* denoted an efficacious embodiment constituted by costume, ritual, and social recognition.

During the final twenty days, four women embodying Xochiquetzal, Xilonen, Atlatonan, and Huixtocihuatl accompanied him as wives. He traveled ceremonially by canoe and, at a small temple commonly located by the sources outside the city, ascended the stairs alone, breaking a flute on each step. Priests stretched him on the sacrificial stone and removed his heart. A replacement was then selected.

**Evidence class:** exceptionally well attested textually in Sahagún and Durán, with corroborating imagery in related manuscripts. The sources disagree over route, timing, ornaments, secondary offerings, and whether the victim was a war captive or purchased slave.

The rite enacted sovereignty’s fragility: the honored, beautiful embodiment reached maximal public status and was then deliberately destroyed. Political readings see in him the ruler, warrior captive, and ideal youth simultaneously.

### Other rites

- Incense, especially copal, was burned before his image.
- Priests and penitents offered blood from ears, tongue, limbs, or other body parts.
- Rulers addressed him in enthronement prayers, acknowledging that office was borrowed and revocable.
- Merchants, warriors, slaves, sick people, and those fearing punishment invoked him in differing settings.
- Night vigils, fasting, black body paint, tobacco, flowers, paper, food, textiles, and quail or other animals occur in the wider cultic record.
- Mirrors and obsidian linked material wealth, long-distance exchange, ritual sight, and sacrificial blades.

### Priesthood and temple

Durán describes a major Tezcatlipoca temple at Tenochtitlan, its image normally screened from ordinary view, priestly ranks, ceremonial ornamentation, and the public display at Toxcatl. One office bore the god’s name, **Titlacahuan**.

Archaeological work south of the Templo Mayor, beneath or adjoining the former Archbishop’s Palace and present SHCP museum area, identified the boundaries of a structure interpreted as Tezcatlipoca’s temple in 1994. Identification combines colonial descriptions, placement, and archaeological remains; the surviving structure has not yielded anything like a dedicatory inscription naming the god.

Tezcatlipoca was not one of the two principal gods atop the dual Templo Mayor—that summit belonged to Tlaloc and Huitzilopochtli. His separate temple and political theology nevertheless made him central to the Sacred Precinct.

### Human sacrifice: documentary and material evidence

The Toxcatl killing is explicit in independent sixteenth-century descriptions. Human sacrifice should neither be denied because colonial writers were hostile nor accepted in every reported number without criticism.

The **Huei Tzompantli** excavations begun in 2015 and continued through 2017 identified skull-rack foundations and towers near the Templo Mayor. INAH has studied a sample of 214 skulls; many display preparation or perforation consistent with display. These discoveries independently confirm large-scale sacrificial display in Tenochtitlan. They do **not**, by themselves, assign each skull to Tezcatlipoca or prove chroniclers’ highest numerical estimates. The assemblage belongs to the broader state sacrificial system.

The 1978 discovery of the **Coyolxauhqui Stone** initiated the modern Templo Mayor project. Its location below Huitzilopochtli’s stairway materially confirmed ritual staging associated with Coyolxauhqui’s dismemberment; it is important comparative evidence for Mexica sacrifice but not a Tezcatlipoca image.

The enormous **Tlaltecuhtli monolith**, found in 2006, likewise transformed knowledge of the precinct’s earth, death, and rulership symbolism. It illuminates the theological environment in which the earth-monster narrative operated, but it should not be cited as direct evidence of Tezcatlipoca’s cult.

### Politics and economics

Tezcatlipoca’s cult legitimated kingship while denying rulers independent ownership of power. Enthronement speeches made the *tlatoani* the deity’s temporary instrument. Captive-taking supplied sacrificial victims; tribute and long-distance trade supplied obsidian, feathers, textiles, cacao, flowers, and ritual luxuries. Toxcatl publicly joined elite expenditure, military domination, theatrical embodiment, and divine sovereignty.

Claims of “temple prostitution” do not belong in this dossier: no dependable source establishes an institution of cult prostitution serving Tezcatlipoca. The four ritual wives of the Toxcatl impersonator must not be relabeled prostitutes.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Epiclassic and Early Postclassic, approximately 700–1200

Mirrors, jaguar-warrior imagery, one-legged supernaturals, and later Tezcatlipoca-like regalia appear in several Mesoamerican traditions. Some figures at Tula have been identified as precursors. **Finding:** continuity is possible, but naming any such figure “Tezcatlipoca” is a modern reconstruction, not a secure epigraphic identification.

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1519

The Borgia-group codices securely establish a mature mirror-bearing, black-painted deity within central-Mexican divination. Mexica imperial religion made Tezcatlipoca a patron of rulership, warriors, elite youth, and unpredictable fortune. His reach was not confined to Tenochtitlan: related forms and titles occur across Nahua regions, while local identifications with Camaxtli, Mixcoatl, Tepeyollotl, and other powers varied.

### Conquest, 1519–1521

During Toxcatl in 1520, Spanish forces under Pedro de Alvarado massacred celebrants in Tenochtitlan’s sacred precinct. Book XII of the *Florentine Codex* and Spanish narratives disagree over motives and details. The Nahua account describes unarmed celebrants attacked during ritual preparation and dancing. Because Huitzilopochtli’s festival image is prominent in Book XII while Toxcatl elsewhere belongs especially to Tezcatlipoca, the event demonstrates overlapping festival complexes rather than a clean dedication to only one god.

### Missionization and documentation, 1520s–1600

Temples and images were destroyed, converted, buried, or reused. Public sacrifice and state priesthood ended under colonial rule. Missionaries equated Tezcatlipoca with Satan or a demon while collecting extensive information intended to expose and suppress “idolatry.” That double process destroyed cult institutions yet preserved texts.

Sahagún’s Nahua informants still reported concealed observances under Christian festivals, including concern that the feast of St John might hide rites for Telpochtli/Tezcatlipoca. Such reports demonstrate missionary suspicion and possible adaptive practice, not unbroken survival of the complete Toxcatl cult.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

Nahua ritual systems persisted in transformed Christian and local forms, but a continuous, separately organized Tezcatlipoca priesthood is not documented. Antiquarians published and translated colonial manuscripts. European authors frequently cast Tezcatlipoca as a Mexican Satan, an interpretation shaped by Christian demonology.

### Twentieth-century reconstruction

Eduard Seler’s codex studies established iconographic comparisons but often imposed astronomical identifications. Alfonso Caso’s color-direction schemes and pantheon charts popularized the “four Tezcatlipocas.” H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology grouped deities into complexes, including a Tezcatlipoca complex; useful as classification, it is not an Indigenous taxonomy.

Miguel León-Portilla read titles such as *Tloque Nahuaque* and *Ipalnemoani* within an elite Nahua philosophical theology. Critics contend that this sometimes harmonized diverse texts and understated Christian-era reframing.

Excavations following the Coyolxauhqui discovery in 1978, the Tlaltecuhtli discovery in 2006, and the Huei Tzompantli discoveries from 2015 onward materially transformed the study of Mexica ritual.

### Twenty-first century

Guilhem Olivier’s comparative study remains the most extensive monograph devoted to Tezcatlipoca. It treats the deity through transformations, bodily anomalies, hunting, kingship, sacrifice, sexuality, and deception rather than reducing him to one domain.

Small modern Mexicayotl, reconstructionist, occult, and eclectic pagan communities invoke Tezcatlipoca, but no reliable census establishes their scale. Their rites are revivals constructed from colonial texts, modern scholarship, personal revelation, and contemporary Indigenous identity—not a demonstrable uninterrupted institutional succession from Tenochtitlan.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### National and Indigenous-cultural reception

Tezcatlipoca appears in Mexican muralism, literature, dance, educational media, public sculpture, and Indigenous-revival discourse. Modern treatments commonly cast Quetzalcoatl as benevolent order and Tezcatlipoca as evil chaos. This ethical dualism exaggerates Christian and modern literary categories: early sources make both gods creators and destroyers.

A contrary nationalist or decolonial reading presents Tezcatlipoca as Indigenous self-knowledge, discipline, or resistance. These are modern political-theological interpretations even when based on genuine Nahua titles.

### Occult and neopagan reception

Modern occult writing associates the obsidian mirror with scrying, shadow work, Jungian confrontation, chaos magic, and personal transformation. These practices may draw analogically on real attributes, but formulas involving “shadow integration,” chakras, Hermetic planetary correspondences, demons, or a universal “dark masculine” are **modern inventions or syncretisms** unless a specific historical source is supplied.

There is no evidence that the Golden Dawn or Renaissance Hermeticism transmitted an ancient Tezcatlipoca cult. Claims connecting him directly with Egyptian Set, the Christian devil, Norse Loki, Hindu Shiva, or Maya K’awiil are comparative modern constructions, not ancient equations.

### Popular media

Tezcatlipoca has been adapted as a villain, antihero, monster, summoned spirit, or combatant in novels, comics, animation, tabletop games, and video games. These media favor the jaguar, obsidian mirror, smoking prosthetic leg, and rivalry with Quetzalcoatl. They materially amplify the now-standard “chaos god” image. Such depictions are **modern retellings**, even where their visual design borrows accurately from codices.

### Extremist and political appropriation

Mesoamerican religious imagery has occasionally been recruited into ethnonationalist, racialist, or anti-colonial rhetoric. No evidence supports describing Tezcatlipoca himself as the center of a large organized extremist movement. Claims should therefore be assessed organization by organization; the general existence of militant rhetoric is not proof of an ancient political doctrine.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Seler

Eduard Seler’s late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century studies provided foundational iconographic identifications across the Borgia group. His command of the codices remains important, although later scholars reject many overly rigid astral or naturalistic equations.

### Nicholson

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 “religious complex” typology grouped the central-Mexican pantheon into functional clusters such as Ometeotl, Tezcatlipoca, Xiuhtecuhtli, Tlaloc, and Quetzalcoatl complexes. It clarified recurring attributes but can mislead when treated as a native fixed classification.

### León-Portilla

*Aztec Thought and Culture* reconstructed a philosophical tradition centered on *teotl*, flux, and titles such as *Tloque Nahuaque*. Its achievement was taking Nahua intellectual texts seriously. Its weakness, critics argue, was synthesizing poetry, prayers, and colonial records into a more unified metaphysics than the evidence necessarily warrants.

### Graulich

Michel Graulich read the Suns and Tollan traditions as structured oppositions, especially the fraternal conflict of Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca. He also proposed calendrical reconstructions that place recorded festivals out of phase with an ideal original cycle. Both his structural insights and calendar theories remain debated.

### Olivier

Olivier’s *Moqueries et métamorphoses d’un dieu aztèque* (1997; English translation, *Mockeries and Metamorphoses of an Aztec God*, 2003, paperback 2008) assembled Nahuatl, Spanish, French, iconographic, archaeological, and comparative material. It is the indispensable specialist synthesis. Its comparisons sometimes move beyond what any one source explicitly states, so each interpretation should still be checked against the cited primary witness.

### Boone

Elizabeth Hill Boone treated divinatory codices as systems for generating meaning rather than picture-writing versions of prose myths. Her *Cycles of Time and Meaning in the Mexican Books of Fate* emphasizes almanac structure, reading order, and performance. This corrects attempts to force every Borgia image into a continuous narrative.

### Current disputed questions

- Whether Tezcatlipoca has a demonstrable Classic-period ancestor: **unresolved**.
- Whether his name means exactly “Smoking Mirror”: conventional but grammatically debated.
- Whether Tepeyollotl and Itztli are manifestations, cognates, or distinct gods: context-dependent.
- Whether *Tloque Nahuaque* designates Tezcatlipoca exclusively: it does not appear to.
- Whether Ometeotl was a supreme preconquest deity: heavily debated.
- Whether the “four Tezcatlipocas” was a coherent preconquest doctrine: evidence supports four creator brothers and color associations, but the standard symmetrical chart is substantially modern synthesis.
- Whether Tollan’s fall preserves historical conflict at archaeological Tula: unproved.
- Whether Tezcatlipoca was literally a “god of evil”: contradicted by his beneficent and sovereign roles.
- Whether colonial sacrifice totals are exact: they are not independently demonstrable, although archaeology confirms real, repeated, large-scale sacrifice.
- Whether modern worship is continuous: no continuous Tezcatlipoca institution has been established; present worship is revival or reconstruction.

### State of the evidence

The principal surviving manuscripts are dispersed among the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Vatican Library, Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Bodleian Library, National Museum of Anthropology, and other European and Mexican collections. Much preconquest material was destroyed through conquest, missionary campaigns, reuse, climate, and ordinary manuscript loss. The surviving record is therefore rich but drastically selected.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 700–1200:** Proposed one-legged, mirror-bearing, jaguar, or Tezcatlipoca-like precursors appear in Mesoamerican art. **Modern reconstruction; none securely named.**
- **c. 900–1150:** Tula flourishes. Later Nahua sources locate Quetzalcoatl–Tezcatlipoca conflict there. **Archaeological city secure; mythic identification retrospective.**
- **c. 1250–1521:** Borgia-group screenfolds preserve the earliest secure Tezcatlipoca iconography.
- **1325–1521:** Mexica Tenochtitlan develops; later evidence places a Tezcatlipoca temple and Toxcatl cult in its Sacred Precinct.
- **late fifteenth century:** Sun Stone carved, monumentalizing the calendrical-cosmic framework of successive ages.
- **1519:** Spanish-led invasion begins.
- **May 1520:** Massacre during Toxcatl at Tenochtitlan; Nahua and Spanish accounts dispute motives.
- **1521:** Fall of Tenochtitlan; public state cult and sacrifice violently terminated.
- **c. 1530s:** Traditions underlying *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* collected.
- **1541:** Codex Mendoza produced.
- **1558:** Date attached to the surviving *Leyenda de los Soles* copy.
- **c. 1558–1561:** *Primeros Memoriales* compiled at Tepepolco.
- **mid-sixteenth century:** Tudela, Magliabechiano, Telleriano-Remensis, and related colonial codices record deities and festivals.
- **1570s:** Durán composes his ritual and historical works.
- **1571:** Molina publishes his Nahuatl–Spanish dictionary, including an influential gloss of *Tloque Nahuaque*.
- **1577:** Florentine Codex reaches completed manuscript form.
- **seventeenth century:** Colonial campaigns continue against Indigenous “idolatry”; no continuous public Tezcatlipoca cult institution is documented.
- **late nineteenth–early twentieth century:** Seler publishes comparative codex studies.
- **1971:** Nicholson publishes his influential central-Mexican religious typology.
- **1978:** Coyolxauhqui Stone discovered; Proyecto Templo Mayor begins major excavations.
- **1994:** Archaeologists delimit portions of the structure identified as Tezcatlipoca’s temple.
- **1997:** Olivier publishes the original French edition of his monograph.
- **2003:** English translation of Olivier’s study published.
- **2006:** Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered in the Sacred Precinct.
- **2015–2017:** Huei Tzompantli excavations provide major osteological evidence for sacrificial display.
- **2023:** Getty Research Institute launches the Digital Florentine Codex, making its Nahuatl, Spanish, translations, and images jointly searchable.
- **2026:** Tezcatlipoca remains an object of scholarship, heritage interpretation, artistic adaptation, and small-scale revived or reconstructed religious practice; no reliable evidence demonstrates uninterrupted institutional continuity.
