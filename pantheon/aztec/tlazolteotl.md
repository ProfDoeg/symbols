# Tlazolteotl: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Female *teotl*—a goddess or divine power in Mexica and neighboring Central Mexican traditions; in some sources a collective of four divine sisters, the *Ixcuinameh*. She is not securely classifiable as a demon, despite colonial authors’ polemical use of *demonio*.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Her recognizable iconographic complex—unspun cotton, spindle whorls, black mouth paint, crescent-shaped nose ornament, and childbirth imagery—occurs in Late Postclassic Borgia-group manuscripts, probably fifteenth to early sixteenth century. The first unambiguous surviving phonetic name and prose description are sixteenth-century Nahuatl and Spanish colonial records, especially Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* (compiled at Tepepulco, approximately 1558–1561) and Book I, chapter 12 of the *Florentine Codex* (completed approximately 1575–1577). Earlier Huastec sculptures are often identified as Tlazolteotl or the related Tének goddess Teem, but the objects do not name themselves; those identifications remain modern iconographic attributions, not secure written attestations.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Late Postclassic attestation]** Preconquest or conquest-period pictorial evidence, normally without alphabetic captions.
- **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]** Sixteenth-century Nahuatl accounts produced through Indigenous informants and scribes, though within Christian colonial institutions.
- **[Colonial elaboration]** Spanish or translated accounts whose terminology and organization may impose Christian concepts.
- **[Regional variant]** Evidence belonging specifically to Huastec, Puebla-Tlaxcala, Mixtec, or another non-Tenochca setting.
- **[Learned systematization]** A colonial or modern attempt to impose a single pantheon or genealogy on fluid divine complexes.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An interpretation assembled by comparison of manuscripts, objects, linguistics, or ethnography.
- **[Modern invention or retelling]** A claim not demonstrable in the preconquest or early colonial record.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Tlazolteotl is the divine owner, producer, and remover of *tlazolli*: literal refuse, dirt, worn-out matter, and figuratively disorderly or illicit conduct, especially sexual misconduct. Sahagún’s Nahua informants describe her as ruling *āhuihnemiliztli*, “lustful/debauched living.” The same chapter says she inspires such conduct and afterward removes or “eats” it when a penitent discloses the heart before an authorized ritual specialist. This is not simply a contradiction between “evil” and “good.” In the documented Nahua system, dangerous generative force, waste, sexuality, fertility, illness, and purification belong to a connected process.

Her other securely documented dimensions are cotton and weaving, conception and childbirth, midwifery, divination, healing, the sweat-bath, the earth-mother complex, and calendrical patronage. Whether all these belonged originally to one being is doubtful. The sources instead show a historical convergence among the Nahua Tlazolteotl, the quadripartite Ixcuina complex, Huastec cotton/fertility powers, and the Central Mexican mother goddesses Toci–Teteo Innan.

### Names

**Tlahzōlteōtl / Tlazolteotl.** Classical Nahuatl *tlahzōlli* or colonial spelling *tlaçolli*, “rubbish, refuse, dirt, something worn out,” plus *teōtl*, “deity/divine power”: approximately “Deity of Filth/Refuse.” “Filth Eater” is technically the translation of a different name, Tlaelcuani. “Goddess of Sin” is a colonially inflected paraphrase: *tlazolli* has wider material and social senses than Christian “sin.” [Early colonial Indigenous testimony]

**Tlaēlcuāni / Tlaelquani.** “Eater of filth/excrement,” from *tlaēlli* and *cuā*, “to eat.” Sahagún explains that she consumed the disclosed moral dirt and thereby ended its power over the penitent. [Early colonial Indigenous testimony]

**Īxcuina / Ixcuinan / Ixcuinān.** Sahagún presents this as her second principal name and links it to four sisters. It is not transparently Nahuatl. Thelma Sullivan argued from Tének/Huastec vocabulary that it meant “Lady of Cotton” or “Cotton Woman,” matching the goddess’s cotton headband and spindles. That interpretation is influential but not universally settled; alternative antiquarian explanations such as “four faces” depend more on the fourfold structure than on secure morphology. A recent iconographic study argues that the Codex Borbonicus embeds visual name-signs for Tlazolteotl, Teteo Innan, and Ixcuina. [Modern scholarly reconstruction]

**Ixcuinameh / Tlazolteteoh.** Plural collectives: the Ixcuinas or Tlazolteotl goddesses. The four are:

- **Tiacapan** (*Tiyacāpan*), the firstborn or leader.
- **Teicu**, the younger or second sister.
- **Tlaco** or **Tlacoehua**, the middle one.
- **Xocotzin/Xucotzin**, the youngest.

Sahagún’s Spanish column calls them four sisters and goddesses of carnal pleasure; the Nahuatl treats them as multiple *teteoh*. They may represent ages, ranks, or phases of femininity, but a complete life-cycle scheme is a modern inference, not stated in the source. [Early colonial Indigenous testimony; interpretation disputed]

**Teteo Innan**, “Mother of the Gods,” and **Toci**, “Our Grandmother.” These are securely named deities in their own right, especially in the Ochpaniztli festival. Some manuscripts and modern scholars treat Tlazolteotl-Ixcuina, Toci, and Teteo Innan as manifestations of one earth-mother complex; other evidence preserves different costumes, festivals, and functions. “They are identical” is therefore too strong. Nicholson placed them within a Teteo Innan subcomplex rather than treating every name as a simple synonym. [Learned systematization / modern scholarly reconstruction]

**Temazcaltoci**, “Grandmother of the Sweat-bath.” This title belongs especially to Toci and the divine image installed at a *temazcal*. Tlazolteotl receives the sweat-bath association through her overlap with the Toci–Teteo Innan complex, childbirth, and purification. Sources should not be made to say that every Temazcaltoci image was explicitly named Tlazolteotl. [Early colonial evidence; syncretistic identification]

**Tlazolmiquiztli**, approximately “death from filth/lust,” is better understood as a dangerous condition or death caused by sexual transgression than as a stable personal name. Related terms identify persons harmed or marked by Tlazolteotl. [Early colonial Indigenous testimony]

### Geographic classification

The *Florentine Codex* says that all who called themselves Mexica worshiped her, especially Mixtecs and Olmecs, who regarded her as particularly their own. It separately reports that the Huastecs worshiped the Tlazolteotl goddesses but did not confess sexual conduct because they did not regard it as wrongdoing. The passage both supports a Gulf-coast connection and shows that the Mexica rite of confession was not simply copied intact from a Huastec cult.

No securely deciphered pre-Nahua Huastec inscription names “Tlazolteotl.” Modern Huastec archaeology uses **Teem** for a cotton/fertility goddess and sometimes calls her the deity whom Nahuas later identified as Tlazolteotl. This is plausible cultural translation, not proof that the Huastecs themselves used the Nahuatl name.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest material evidence

**Huastec stone sculptures, approximately 900–1521.** A monumental female figure reportedly from the Pánuco River region, now British Museum Am1842,0611.2, has bare breasts, hands on the abdomen, and a tall conical headdress. The museum identifies her as Tlazolteotl. The date and Huastec provenance are broad; no inscription confirms the name. [Regional material evidence; modern identification]

Comparable sculptures from Castillo de Teayo and the Veracruz–Huasteca region are identified as Ixcuinan/Tlazolteotl or Teem. They establish a Late Postclassic Gulf-coast female fertility/cotton cult but cannot alone prove all the attributes described by Sahagún.

Claims that much earlier figurines from Xochitécatl, sometimes dated to the first millennium BCE, depict Tlazolteotl are not secure attestations of this named being. They document old female fertility and maternity imagery, not necessarily historical continuity of the later goddess.

### Preconquest and conquest-period manuscripts

**Codex Borgia**, generally Late Postclassic and probably from the Puebla-Tlaxcala region. It is a painted deerskin ritual-divinatory manuscript. Page 12 places a Tlazolteotl-type figure with the day Jaguar; page 55 shows her in a journey or directional almanac. Her diagnostic features include an unspun-cotton headband, inserted spindles and whorls, black paint around the mouth, a triangular shell pectoral, and a crescent nose ornament. The manuscript contains no surviving alphabetic name; identification is comparative. [Late Postclassic attestation; regional]

**Codex Vaticanus B, Codex Cospi, Codex Fejérváry-Mayer, Codex Laud.** These Borgia-group or cognate ritual books contain figures identified through the same cotton, nose-ornament, mouth-paint, and calendrical signs. They demonstrate geographic circulation beyond Tenochtitlan. Their scenes are almanacs, not continuous myths; narrative paraphrases must therefore remain tentative. [Late Postclassic attestation]

**Codex Borbonicus**, painted in Central Mexico very near the conquest, with its precise pre- or early-postconquest date disputed. Page 13 governs the trecena beginning 1 Movement. The frontal goddess crouches in a birth posture as a small figure descends or emerges. Modern interpreters usually call the child Cinteotl, but the pictorial manuscript supplies no alphabetic caption. Pages 29–30 show Ochpaniztli imagery associated with the Toci–Teteo Innan complex. [Late Postclassic/conquest-period attestation]

### Early colonial manuscripts and texts

**Primeros Memoriales**, approximately 1558–1561. Compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún with Nahua elders and scribes at Tepepulco, it preserves early deity descriptions and costumes. It predates the final Florentine synthesis and is important when its wording or images differ from it. [Early colonial Indigenous testimony]

**Florentine Codex**, approximately 1575–1577, Nahuatl and Spanish, twelve books, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana. Book I, chapter 12 supplies the fullest identification: Tlazolteotl, Ixcuina, Tlaelquani, the four sisters, sexual appetite, confession, and geographic worship. Book VI describes confession and penitential speech; Book IV and calendrical passages situate the diviners; Book II documents Toci–Teteo Innan and Ochpaniztli; Book X supplies ethnographic claims about Huastecs. Anderson and Dibble’s English edition remains fundamental, but the bilingual digital manuscript makes it possible to compare their translation with the Nahuatl and Spanish columns.

A key Nahuatl formula is: *īxpan neyōlcuītilo, īxpan neyōlmelāhualo*—before her one “takes out” or declares the heart and “straightens” it. Christian “confession” is a useful comparison but not an exact indigenous gloss.

**Codex Mendoza**, approximately 1541–1542. It is primarily imperial, tributary, and social rather than a Tlazolteotl myth-book. Cotton tribute and the political economy of textiles illuminate the material world behind Ixcuina, but no major independent Tlazolteotl narrative occurs. [Colonial documentary context; absence finding]

**Codex Telleriano-Remensis**, mid-sixteenth century. Its calendrical tables include Tlazolteotl among the nine Lords of the Night. Later European annotations interpret the images, sometimes through Christian categories. [Colonial pictorial continuation]

**Codex Tudela**, mid-sixteenth century, and **Codex Magliabechiano**, later sixteenth century. These cognate works preserve festival and deity images, including the sweat-bath and Toci/Ochpaniztli complexes. The manuscripts’ genealogical relationship means parallel pictures are not independent witnesses. [Colonial pictorial corpus]

**Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*, approximately 1570s–1581.** Durán documents Central Mexican festivals, temples, penitence, sacrifice, and the persistent colonial survival of Indigenous rites. His tendency to compare Mesoamerican and biblical practices, and his theory of ancient Old World contact, are not reliable evidence of origin. His Ochpaniztli material is valuable but focuses more on Toci/“Mother of the Gods” than on a neatly isolated Tlazolteotl. [Colonial elaboration]

**Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, Nahuatl annals compiled in the later sixteenth century; **Leyenda de los Soles**, 1558 manuscript; **Histoyre du Mechique**, French text derived from a lost sixteenth-century Spanish account; **Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas**, sixteenth century; **Cantares Mexicanos**, mostly sixteenth-century Nahuatl songs. These are crucial for Central Mexican cosmogony and divine relationships but do not provide a substantial independent Tlazolteotl narrative cycle. Genealogies involving Piltzintecuhtli, Xochiquetzal, and Cinteotl vary among them. Assigning every Cinteotl birth story to Tlazolteotl silently conflates variants. [Absence and conflict finding]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**Sexual desire and transgression.** Sahagún explicitly makes her mistress of *āhuihnemiliztli*. She both incites and withdraws dangerous desire. Adultery could be punished severely under Mexica law, but Tlazolteotl should not be reduced to a punitive “goddess of cheating”; her sphere includes the generative potency of sexuality. [Early colonial Indigenous testimony]

**Purification and one-time confession.** A person consulted an authorized diviner or priest, chose an auspicious day, swept the ritual place, invoked the deity, disclosed wrongs, and received penance. Recorded penances include fasting, offerings, song or dance, restitution, and bloodletting of the tongue. Sahagún says the absolving rite could not simply be repeated: the deity heard or removed the matter once, which helps explain its association with old age and approaching death. The assertion “every Aztec confessed exactly once” exceeds the evidence; it was an available rite, and practice probably varied. [Early colonial testimony]

**Cotton, spinning, weaving.** The cotton headband and spindles are among her most stable graphic identifiers. Sullivan argued that the iconography spells Ixcuina, “Lady Cotton.” Cotton was economically and politically important tribute in the highlands, so this is also a material and gendered domain, not merely decoration. Claims that she personally “invented embroidery” occur in later summaries more confidently than in the primary evidence and should be treated cautiously. [Late Postclassic iconography / modern reconstruction]

**Pregnancy, childbirth, and midwifery.** Codex Borbonicus page 13 is the decisive image. Colonial descriptions also connect the larger Toci–Teteo Innan complex with midwives, pregnant women, medicines, and abortion providers. The goddess’s crouching pose is a documented Central Mexican birthing position. [Late Postclassic/conquest-period attestation]

**Sweat-bath.** The *temazcal* served postpartum, therapeutic, and marriage-related purification. An image of the divine “grandmother of the sweat-bath” stood at its entrance. This function is securely documented for Temazcaltoci/Toci; its attribution to Tlazolteotl depends on the historically attested overlap of the goddess complexes. [Syncretistic identification, not an exclusive office]

**Healing and divination.** Diviners who read birth fortunes, cast maize kernels, used cords, or looked into water appear among the specialists associated with the goddess complex. Medical practitioners and midwives honored Toci–Teteo Innan. Statements that Tlazolteotl governed all medicine often collapse Toci into her. A modern medical article identifies her as a goddess who caused and cured epilepsy, reflecting the broader Mesoamerican pattern in which divine powers both send and remove illness; “goddess of epilepsy” is not her primary ancient title. [Early colonial complex / modern specialization]

**Calendrical power.** She presides over the trecena 1 Movement in the Codex Borbonicus tradition, appears with Jaguar in Codex Borgia page 12, and is one of the nine Lords of the Night in several manuscripts. Different manuscripts attach her to different day configurations; these are layered almanac roles, not contradictions requiring correction. [Late Postclassic attestation]

### Diagnostic iconography

- Unspun cotton band or bundles at the head.
- One or two spindles with spindle whorls inserted in the headdress.
- Black rubber or bitumen paint around the mouth.
- Crescent-shaped **yacameztli**, “moon nose ornament.”
- Conical Huastec-style cap in some regional sculptures.
- Triangular shell pectoral.
- Naked or partly exposed torso in some almanacs.
- Crouching birth posture.
- Brooms or yellow-grass bundles where purification and sweeping are emphasized.
- Occasionally a flayed skin, connecting her to agricultural renewal and the Toci/Xipe complex.
- Quail feathers, cotton ear ornaments, and patterned garments depending on the manuscript.

### Moon, maguey, pulque, and earth

The lunar interpretation rests particularly on the crescent nose ornament, nocturnal calendrical role, fertility, and comparisons with lunar vessels and rabbits. It is widespread in scholarship but no surviving Nahuatl text simply defines her as “the Moon.” [Modern scholarly reconstruction]

Katarzyna Mikulska has argued from shared iconographic elements that maguey is more central to Tlazolteotl than conventional summaries recognize. Pulque, fertility, altered states, and moral disorder form a plausible symbolic field. This remains a scholarly reinterpretation, not a newly discovered ancient title “Goddess of Maguey.”

Her classification as an “earth goddess” is defensible within the Teteo Innan complex and the cycle by which decayed matter becomes fertility. It does not make her identical to Tlaltecuhtli, Coatlicue, or every female earth power.

### Modernly misidentified “birthing statue”

A famous small stone figure of a crouching woman giving birth, frequently sold in replicas as “Tlazolteotl,” has an insecure archaeological history and an unusual style. Its first documented modern appearance and collection history have produced persistent authenticity doubts. It should not outrank Codex Borbonicus page 13 as evidence. The Borbonicus image is the secure historical representation; the free-standing “birthing goddess” is a contested object. [Contested material]

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

There is no single securely ancient family tree.

One widespread reconstruction makes Tlazolteotl the partner of **Piltzintecuhtli** and mother of **Cinteotl**. The Borbonicus birth image supports a mother–maize-child relationship iconographically, but identifying the emerging infant and naming Piltzintecuhtli as father require comparison with alphabetic traditions.

Other sixteenth-century sources give **Xochiquetzal**, not Tlazolteotl, as Piltzintecuhtli’s partner and mother of Cinteotl or Xochipilli. This is a genuine variant, not necessarily a scribal error. Xochiquetzal represents youthful erotic and floral fertility; Tlazolteotl the dangerous, mature, purifying side of sexuality; their functions overlap without proving identity.

Modern web genealogies naming Omecihuatl as Tlazolteotl’s literal mother, placing her among exactly 1,600 siblings, or making Xolotl her consort and co-founder of Texcoco are not supported by a clear early source in the form usually circulated. They should be labeled learned reconstruction or modern invention unless a precise manuscript passage is supplied.

### Every securely or plausibly documented god–god interaction

- **Cinteotl — childbirth/motherhood:** Tlazolteotl is conventionally identified as giving birth to the maize deity in Codex Borbonicus page 13, early sixteenth century. **[Late Postclassic/conquest-period image; identification strong but not captioned]**
- **Piltzintecuhtli — sexual or marital union producing Cinteotl:** reported in later syntheses by aligning divergent sixteenth-century genealogies. **[Colonial/modern systematization; variant and not uniformly attested]**
- **The four Ixcuinameh — plurality or sisterhood:** Tiacapan, Teicu, Tlaco, and Xocotzin are four sisters collectively identified with Ixcuina/Tlazolteotl in *Florentine Codex* I.12. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Toci — overlapping identity and functions:** shared earth, purification, childbirth, cotton, medicine, flaying, and sweat-bath imagery; most explicit in Ochpaniztli and comparative iconography. No surviving narrative records a meeting between them. **[Syncretistic identification; not a narrative interaction]**
- **Teteo Innan — overlapping identity/title:** Tlazolteotl imagery can encode “Mother of the Gods,” while Sahagún’s Ochpaniztli account treats Teteo Innan/Toci as festival patron. No discrete conversation or combat is recorded. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Xochiquetzal — complementary/competing sexual-fertility role:** colonial sources contrast or overlap the young erotic goddess with the mature purifier. No secure myth has the two meet. **[Modern comparative reconstruction based on separate cult evidence]**
- **Xipe Totec — shared flayed-skin and renewal complex:** iconographic and festival correspondences connect them, but no secure surviving episode narrates a personal interaction. **[Cultic/iconographic relation]**
- **Chicomecoatl — coordinated agricultural-festival complex:** Toci’s Ochpaniztli ceremonies interact with maize rites and with Chicomecoatl’s cult; direct narrative action by Tlazolteotl is not securely stated. **[Festival relation through Toci identification]**
- **Tezcatlipoca — joint calendrical appearance:** Tlazolteotl and Tezcatlipoca occupy the principal panel of the thirteenth trecena in Codex Borbonicus page 13. This is a joint almanac scene, not proof of marriage, combat, or conversation. **[Late Postclassic/conquest-period attestation]**
- **Chalchiuhtlicue — purification analogy or association:** water purification and shared female-fertility attributes cause colonial commentators and modern scholars to relate them; no independent mythic encounter is preserved. **[Learned association]**
- **Cihuateteo — childbirth entourage/protected dead women:** later descriptions place women who died in childbirth within Tlazolteotl’s maternal-lunar sphere, but the primary sources more often give the Cihuateteo their own calendrical and dangerous roles. **[Later synthesis]**
- **Mixcoatl — negative geographic comparison:** Sahagún says Chichimecs did not worship Tlazolteotl because their sole guarded deity was Mixcoatl. This is not an interaction but an explicit source-defined exclusion. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Xolotl — alleged consort and co-founder of Texcoco:** no firm sixteenth-century passage establishing this was located. **[Unestablished; likely modern or late retelling]**

No securely attested narrative describes Tlazolteotl fighting, deceiving, rescuing, or judging another named god. Her surviving divine relations are chiefly genealogical, calendrical, iconographic, or produced by fusion among cult complexes. That absence is a substantive finding.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Tlazolteotl has no surviving epic cycle comparable to the birth of Huitzilopochtli or Quetzalcoatl’s journey to Mictlan. Her principal “stories” are ritual explanations and iconographic events.

### The eater of filth and the once-in-life confession

**Earliest full source:** *Florentine Codex* I.12 and VI, sixteenth century.

A person burdened by grave misconduct sought one of the diviners who possessed the painted books. A suitable day was selected. The ritual space was swept; fire and incense could mark divine presence. The penitent addressed the divine power through the ritual specialist and disclosed deeds without concealment: sexual transgression, theft, injury, and other wrongs. The specialist imposed acts of repair or penance. Tlazolteotl, as Tlaelcuani, consumed the disclosed pollution. The matter could not simply be confessed again; hence people might defer the rite until advanced age.

The Spanish column analogizes her to Venus and the rite to confession. The Nahuatl vocabulary—opening, declaring, or straightening the heart—shows a native conceptual structure. Colonial Christian influence cannot be excluded from the final wording, but the rite’s divinatory calendar, bloodletting, invocation, and one-time character differ sharply from Catholic sacramental confession. The alternatives “purely pre-Hispanic” and “invented by friars” are both too simple. [Early colonial Indigenous testimony, transmitted under colonial conditions]

### The four Ixcuina sisters

*Florentine Codex* I.12 says Ixcuina comprises four sisters of ranked ages. Each is a goddess of carnal life. The text gives no extended adventure, parentage, or separate temples for them. Modern accounts often make them four stages of a woman’s life or four lunar phases; those are interpretive expansions. [Early colonial testimony; modern elaboration distinguished]

### The birth of maize

Codex Borbonicus page 13 shows the goddess in labor, conventionally delivering Cinteotl. Footprints or directional signs have been read as the infant descending from a celestial place into the womb or emerging into the world. The child’s maize identity connects sexuality, gestation, cultivated fertility, and food.

Later genealogical texts disagree over whether Tlazolteotl or Xochiquetzal is the mother and whether Cinteotl or Xochipilli is the child of Piltzintecuhtli. The safest reconstruction is that several related traditions paired a solar/young-lord figure with a female fertility power and generated maize or flowers; later pantheon charts stabilized one combination. [Late Postclassic image plus conflicting colonial genealogies]

### Ochpaniztli: sweeping, death, flaying, and renewal

The eleventh twenty-day festival honored Teteo Innan/Toci. A woman chosen as the goddess’s *ixiptla*—a living embodiment, not merely an actor—was kept cheerful, danced, and participated in public rites. She was eventually killed, conventionally by decapitation, and flayed. A priest wore her skin. The divine embodiment then participated in staged combat and agricultural-military ceremonies. The *Florentine Codex* records that the skin-wearer went before Huitzilopochtli and tore the hearts from four captives.

This material must not be sanitized: colonial texts describe the killing of the female *ixiptla*, flaying, heart extraction, captive sacrifice, and state spectacle. Yet assigning the entire festival simply to “Tlazolteotl” depends on identifying her with Toci/Teteo Innan. Codex Magliabechiano explicitly names Toci in its Ochpaniztli description. [Early colonial ritual record; Tlazolteotl identification syncretistic]

The festival joined sweeping and repair, harvest renewal, childbirth, warfare, and imperial power. The worn skin materialized divine transformation. It was not “temple prostitution”; no good evidence documents an institution properly described by that term in Tlazolteotl’s cult.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers

No excavated temple can presently be identified as Tlazolteotl’s central sanctuary with the confidence possible for Tlaloc and Huitzilopochtli at the Templo Mayor. Colonial sources imply worship across Mexica territory and especially among Mixtec, “Olmec” or Gulf-region communities, and Huastecs. Castillo de Teayo and the Pánuco region preserve strong female fertility/cotton imagery, but deity names remain inferred.

This lack of a securely identified freestanding temple is important. Tlazolteotl’s cult may have operated through diviners, household or neighborhood images, sweat-baths, calendrical rites, and incorporation into larger mother-goddess festivals rather than a single monumental state sanctuary.

### Religious specialists

The confession rite belonged to diviners or ritual experts who possessed calendrical books and read birth destinies. Colonial Spanish calls them *sátrapas* or priests, but the Nahua occupational categories were more differentiated. Midwives, healers, physicians, bath keepers, and female diviners appear in the broader Toci–Tlazolteotl sphere.

### Offerings and penance

Attested or reported acts include:

- sweeping and preparing the ritual place;
- incense and invocation;
- disclosure of transgressions;
- restitution of stolen goods;
- fasting;
- offerings;
- ritual song and dance;
- piercing the tongue with a maguey spine and drawing reeds or cords through the opening;
- bathing or steam bathing;
- sacrifice in the Ochpaniztli complex.

There is no secure evidence that a confessor personally impersonated Tlazolteotl in every rite, nor that all sexual acts required confession.

### Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice is textually and archaeologically real in Mexica state religion. In the Toci–Teteo Innan festival complex, the killing and flaying of a female embodiment and subsequent sacrifice of captives are specifically described. The degree to which these should be filed under Tlazolteotl rather than Toci is a question of divine identification, not whether sacrifice occurred.

Excavations of the **Huei Tzompantli** between 2015 and 2017 identified the great skull-rack complex of Tenochtitlan and associated skull towers, materially confirming large-scale public display of sacrificial remains. This evidence does **not** identify the victims as sacrifices to Tlazolteotl. It corroborates the wider institutional setting in which the written festival accounts must be evaluated.

Similarly, the Coyolxauhqui monolith found in 1978, the Tlaltecuhtli monolith found in 2006, and the Sun Stone rediscovered in 1790 transformed knowledge of Mexica ritual art but are not images of Tlazolteotl. Their inclusion in her history is contextual and historiographic, not evidence of her personal cult.

### Political economy

Cotton, spun thread, cloth, captive bodies, and agricultural produce connected divine imagery to imperial extraction. Codex Mendoza records cotton textiles among tribute obligations. Tlazolteotl-Ixcuina’s cotton insignia therefore belonged to an economy in which elite clothing, women’s labor, tribute, and conquered tropical lands were politically charged. The claim that the Mexica imported her cult only after conquering the Huasteca is plausible as a mechanism of intensification but is not directly proven; Gulf-highland religious exchange long predated the final Mexica conquests.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before approximately 1200: antecedents, not secure Tlazolteotl

Mesoamerica possessed ancient female fertility, earth, birth, weaving, and sweat-bath powers. Earlier figurines and sculptures can establish antecedent themes but not the continuous worship of a deity named Tlazolteotl. Retroactive identification is common and should be resisted.

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1521

A recognizable cotton-spindle, black-mouth, crescent-nose goddess circulated through Central Mexican ritual manuscripts. Huastec female sculptures and highland pictorial books document related but not necessarily identical beings. By the final Mexica period, Tlazolteotl-Ixcuina belonged to calendrical divination and the larger mother-earth complex.

### Mexica imperial period, especially fifteenth to early sixteenth century

The goddess’s functions were integrated into a cosmopolitan imperial pantheon that absorbed and reordered regional powers. Nahua testimony remembered particularly strong Huastec, Mixtec, and Gulf affiliations. Cotton tribute and military expansion supplied mechanisms for the movement of cult images and specialists.

### 1521–approximately 1580: conquest, suppression, and documentation

Spanish conquest destroyed or converted temples, broke priestly institutions, and prohibited public sacrifice. Missionaries simultaneously gathered detailed Indigenous testimony to identify and eliminate “idolatry.” Sahagún’s project preserved Nahuatl accounts while translating them into a Christian intellectual environment. Words such as “sin,” “Venus,” “confession,” and “devil” are therefore documentary evidence of colonial interpretation as well as imperfect glosses of Indigenous concepts.

Public cult was dismantled. Household practice, healing, divination, bathing, weaving symbolism, and reverence for maternal powers were harder to police and may have persisted in transformed forms. Direct continuity under the specific name Tlazolteotl is rarely demonstrable.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

Colonial antiquarians reproduced earlier descriptions while living worship became obscure to the written archive. Indigenous ritual specialists continued to be prosecuted under categories such as idolatry and superstition, but a specific continuous Tlazolteotl priesthood is not documented.

### 1790–early twentieth century: archaeological rediscovery and classification

The discoveries of Coatlicue and the Sun Stone in 1790 initiated a new phase of collecting and interpretation. Huastec sculptures entered European and Mexican museums in the nineteenth century; the British Museum figure was acquired in 1842. Eduard Seler and other late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars compared codices systematically, establishing much of the modern iconographic vocabulary. Their identifications remain influential, although some were overly confident or evolutionist.

### 1950–1982: textual consolidation

Anderson and Dibble published the monumental English edition of the *Florentine Codex*. Nicholson’s 1971 typology placed Tlazolteotl in a Teteo Innan earth-mother subcomplex. Miguel León-Portilla emphasized Nahua philosophical categories and calendrical thought. Thelma Sullivan’s 1982 study, “Tlazolteotl-Ixcuina: The Great Spinner and Weaver,” made cotton, weaving, and Huastec etymology central to later scholarship.

### 1978–present: contextual archaeology and digital recovery

The discovery of Coyolxauhqui in 1978 initiated the modern Templo Mayor Project under Eduardo Matos Moctezuma; later work by Leonardo López Luján and others established increasingly precise ritual contexts. The Tlaltecuhtli monolith in 2006 and Huei Tzompantli excavations in 2015–2017 strengthened the material basis for studying Mexica sacrifice and earth deities, although neither discovery is a Tlazolteotl attestation.

Digital access to the Borgia group, the Getty’s Digital Florentine Codex, and UNAM’s Tlachia and linguistic databases now permits direct comparison that older syntheses could not easily make.

### Present-day worship

Individual neopagans, Mexicayotl participants, and polytheistic reconstructionists report devotion to Tlazolteotl. Online testimony demonstrates contemporary practice but not scale, organizational continuity, or direct transmission from a preconquest priesthood. Present worship is best described as revival, reconstruction, or new religious practice drawing on colonial texts, archaeology, nationalism, and personal revelation.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, feminism, and psychology

Modern writers frequently interpret Tlazolteotl as an archetype of integrating sexuality, shame, waste, death, and renewal. Such Jungian or psychotherapeutic readings can be meaningful modern reception but are not recoverable preconquest doctrine. Descriptions of her as a universal “Dark Goddess,” patron of shadow work, or divine therapist are modern.

Feminist and Chicana artists and writers have used Tlazolteotl to reclaim Indigenous sexuality and childbirth from colonial moral categories. These works belong to modern intellectual and political history rather than serving as transparent evidence of Mexica belief.

### Neopagan and occult use

There is no significant documented Tlazolteotl role in Renaissance Hermetism, the nineteenth-century Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoires. Claims of an ancient continuous occult initiation lineage are modern inventions.

Late twentieth- and twenty-first-century eclectic paganism incorporates her in cleansing, confession, sexuality, fertility, and “shadow” rituals. Practitioners often substitute symbolic offerings for historical blood sacrifice. Personal claims that the goddess communicates through dreams or selects devotees are contemporary religious testimony; they cannot be used to reconstruct ancient cult.

### Games and commercial culture

The 1980 *Deities & Demigods* role-playing book recast Tlazolteotl as a game statistic block, assigned a moral “alignment,” and later Greyhawk material transformed her into an “Olman” earth-mother. These are documented modern inventions. They materially spread an image of her as either “chaotic evil” or a generic agricultural goddess—classifications foreign to the Nahua sources.

Commercial statues, jewelry, perfumes, tattoos, and prints commonly reproduce the contested crouching birth sculpture or Codex Borbonicus page 13. Product descriptions often call her simply “the Aztec goddess of sex,” “goddess of purity,” or “poop goddess.” Each removes the historically essential unity of pollution, generativity, confession, cotton, and ritual danger.

### Political and nationalist use

Tlazolteotl appears within broader indigenist and Mexicayotl recovery of Mexica religion. This may function as cultural decolonization, Mexican nationalism, or contemporary polytheism depending on the participant. No substantial, specifically Tlazolteotl-centered extremist movement was identified. Generic claims that modern devotion is inherently nationalist or extremist are unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The Huastec-origin thesis

**Evidence for:** the nontransparent Nahuatl name Ixcuina; its plausible derivation from Tének words relating to woman/lady and cotton; Huastec-style conical caps and crescent nose ornaments; Sahagún’s explicit statement that Huastecs worshiped the Tlazolteotl goddesses; Gulf-coast female sculptures; the Huasteca’s cotton economy.

**Qualifications:** Huastec archaeological figures are not inscribed with her Nahuatl name. A recent study of Tamtoc emphasizes that Tlazolteotl and Ehecatl have not been securely identified in Huastec remains predating Central Mexican influence. “Huastec origin” is therefore a strong historical hypothesis, not a demonstrated place-and-date of invention.

### What does *tlazolli* mean?

Earlier translations narrowed it to excrement, sexual sin, or filth. More recent work treats it as a semantic field: rubbish, worn matter, disorder, physical dirt, moral danger, and the decomposed material from which fertility can re-emerge. Christian translators intensified its equivalence with “sin,” but the sexual association is genuinely present in Nahuatl testimony and is not solely missionary invention.

### Tlazolteotl versus Toci–Teteo Innan

One school treats the names as aspects of a single great goddess. Nicholson’s typology instead groups several distinct beings in an earth-mother subcomplex. Costume differences in Sahagún and differing festival names favor maintaining distinction; shared cotton, flaying, sweeping, childbirth, medicine, and earth functions demonstrate historical fusion. The most faithful formulation is “partly overlapping divine complexes,” not either complete identity or complete separation.

### Moon goddess?

The *yacameztli*, Lords-of-the-Night role, childbirth, fertility, pulque imagery, and comparisons with Gulf iconography support lunar associations. No source establishes her as a personification of the astronomical moon in all contexts. “Lunar goddess” is a defensible secondary classification, not her sole definition.

### Childbirth image

The Codex Borbonicus image is secure; interpretation of the small emerging figure as Cinteotl is strong but comparative. The famous three-dimensional birthing statue has contested provenance and authenticity. Popular histories frequently reverse this evidentiary order.

### Confession and Christian influence

The resemblance to Catholic confession impressed friars. Yet the Indigenous rite’s once-only force, calendrical scheduling, diviner-confessor, sweeping, bloodletting, and divine consumption of pollution are not Catholic. The surviving descriptions were nevertheless written after evangelization, and their Spanish moral vocabulary cannot be treated as untouched preconquest speech. Scholars therefore debate the degree of accommodation rather than choosing between wholesale authenticity and wholesale fabrication.

### Human sacrifice

Older polemics either sensationalized Mexica killing or denied it as colonial propaganda. Textual descriptions, cut-marked human remains, sacrificial deposits, skull racks, and skull towers now make denial untenable. Exact numbers, motives, and the attribution of particular remains to particular gods remain open questions. The Huei Tzompantli does not prove sacrifice to Tlazolteotl specifically.

### Major modern frameworks

- **Eduard Seler:** foundational comparative reading of Borgia-group iconography; indispensable but sometimes overly systematizing.
- **H. B. Nicholson, 1971:** typological organization of Central Mexican religion; useful map, not an Indigenous canonical theology.
- **Miguel León-Portilla:** Nahua thought, calendrics, and philosophical interpretation; influential, with later criticism of overly unified “Aztec philosophy.”
- **Thelma Sullivan, 1982:** cotton, weaving, Ixcuina, and Huastec connections.
- **Elizabeth Hill Boone:** painted books as systems of time, ritual, and meaning rather than failed alphabetic narratives.
- **Michel Graulich:** festival sequence and myth-ritual interpretation; influential but not universally accepted in chronology.
- **David Carrasco:** religion, urbanism, sacrifice, and imperial power.
- **Guilhem Olivier:** divine identities as unstable transformations, especially Tezcatlipoca; methodologically important for resisting fixed “pantheon chart” identities.
- **Mary Miller and Karl Taube:** accessible iconographic synthesis.
- **Katarzyna Mikulska:** semiotics, graphic communication, maguey associations, and the interaction between image and sacred speech.
- **Matos Moctezuma and López Luján:** archaeological contextualization of the Templo Mayor and Mexica ritual state.
