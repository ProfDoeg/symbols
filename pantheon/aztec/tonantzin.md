# Tonantzin: Research Dossier

**Class:** Female deity/title in central Mexican Nahua tradition. In the clearest sixteenth-century description, *Tonantzin* is a reverential designation of the goddess Cihuacóatl; elsewhere the related form *Tonan* names Ilamatecuhtli. It can also function linguistically as “our revered mother” rather than as the unique proper name of one goddess.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely datable surviving written attestations belong to the first generation after the Spanish conquest. Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*, compiled with Nahua collaborators at Tepepulco beginning about 1558, preserves the maternal-earth-goddess complex subsequently elaborated in the *Florentine Codex*. The first wholly explicit surviving equations are in the latter manuscript, completed in 1577: “Cihuacoatl … they also called her Tonantzin,” and “Ilamateuctli, and by another name Tonan.” Its Book XI appendix additionally records an earlier cult and pilgrimage at Tepeyac dedicated to “the mother of the gods whom they called Tonantzin.” No presently known pre-Conquest inscription or securely provenanced image bears a phonetic label identifying its subject simply as *Tonantzin*.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** sixteenth-century text preserving information attributed to pre-Conquest Nahuas, or securely provenanced pre-Conquest material evidence.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a tradition that became normative within a later religious or literary corpus.
- **[RV] Regional or local variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an analytical equation made by Indigenous, colonial, or modern intellectuals rather than directly stated by the earliest evidence.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically datable equation or overlap between religious figures.
- **[EM] Early-modern accretion:** colonial development not securely attested before the conquest.
- **[F] Folklore or popular practice.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a defensible inference, not an explicit primary-source statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unverified popular claim.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The central identification problem

**[EA]** *Tonantzin* is Classical Nahuatl *to-nān-tzin*: *to-* (“our”) + *nān(tli)* (“mother”) + reverential/diminutive *-tzin*. “Our revered mother,” “our honored mother,” or contextually “our dear mother” is more exact than the often romanticized “little mother.” The suffix conveys respect and affection, not merely physical smallness. Colonial spellings include *Tonantzin*, *Tonatzin*, *Tonāntzin*, *Tonancin*, and, without the reverential suffix, *Tonan* or *Tonā*.

**[EA]** Because the possessive prefix is built into the expression, *Tonantzin* behaves differently from an ordinary personal name. Sixteenth-century Nahuatl could apply it to:

- Cihuācōātl, explicitly in *Florentine Codex* Book I.
- A pre-Christian “mother of the gods” associated with Tepeyac, explicitly in the Book XI appendix.
- The Virgin Mary and other Christian maternal institutions or figures in colonial Nahuatl.
- In the shorter form *Tonan*, Ilamatecuhtli/Cozcamiauh in the feast of Tititl.
- More broadly, an honored collective or institutional mother, including the Church in Christian Nahuatl.

Consequently, “Tonantzin was the Aztec goddess of X” is underdetermined unless the source, date, and contextual deity are supplied.

### Secure divine equations

**Cihuācōātl — “Snake Woman.” [EA]** *Florentine Codex* Book I, chapter 6, states: “The first of these goddesses was named Cihuacoatl … And they also called her Tonantzin, which means ‘our mother.’” She brings poverty, dejection, and troubles; appears at night in female dress; and screams through the air. Book VI names Cihuacoatl-Quilaztli as “our mother” in childbirth prayers.

**Quilaztli. [EA]** A name or manifestation paired with Cihuacoatl in childbirth orations. The sources treat Cihuacoatl and Quilaztli as sufficiently close to be invoked together; whether they began as separate local beings later fused cannot be established from the surviving corpus.

**Ilamatēuctli/Ilamatēcuhtli — “Old Lady/Old Female Lord.” [EA]** In *Florentine Codex* Book II’s account of Tititl she is also called *Tonan* (“Our Mother”) and *Cozcamiauh* (“Maize-tassel necklace”). A female ixiptla, or living deity-impersonator, was killed, decapitated, and danced with.

**Tēteoh īnnān — “Mother of the Gods”; Toci/Tocih — “Our Grandmother”; Tlalli īyōlloh — “Heart of the Earth.” [EA]** Book I treats these as names of one goddess complex, patronized by physicians, midwives, diviners, and healers. Book II places her central festival in Ochpaniztli. Sahagún’s Book XI description calls the Tepeyac figure “mother of the gods” and “Tonantzin,” creating a strong textual connection, but he does not there explicitly say “this is Toci” or repeat the Book I equation with Cihuacoatl.

### Disputed and overextended equations

**Coatlicue — “She of the Serpent Skirt.” [LS/MSR]** Modern summaries commonly make “Tonantzin” a proper name of Coatlicue. Coatlicue belongs to the same broad maternal, terrestrial, sacrificial complex and is mother of Huitzilopochtli in the Coatepec narrative. That makes comparison reasonable. Nevertheless, the most explicit Sahaguntine equation is *Tonantzin = Cihuacoatl*, while *Tonan = Ilamatecuhtli*. A timeless equation “Tonantzin is Coatlicue” exceeds the wording of that evidence.

**Tlaltecuhtli — “Earth Lord.” [LS/MSR]** Nahua speech can call the earth *tonantzin tlalli*, “our revered mother earth,” while Tlaltecuhtli personifies the consuming and generative earth. The linguistic and conceptual relationship does not prove that every image of Tlaltecuhtli depicts a proper-named goddess Tonantzin.

**Chicomecoatl, Xilonen, Centeotl, and Tlazolteotl. [LS/MSR]** These maize, fertility, sexuality, purification, and earth-related beings overlap with Toci/Teteo Innan in calendars, regalia, and ritual functions. Older comparativist writing sometimes collapses them into one “Great Mother.” Current evidentiary practice preserves the named distinctions unless a source itself makes an equation.

**Ichpochtli Tonantzin. [RV/uncertain]** Recent work on Tepeyac-area archaeology uses this designation in reconstructing local female cults. Its precise antiquity and relation to the figure Sahagún called “mother of the gods” remain debated.

**“Tonantzin Coatlaxopeuh.” [MI]** The popular assertion that *Guadalupe* derives from a Nahuatl name *Coatlaxopeuh*, often glossed “she who crushes the serpent,” lacks a sixteenth-century Nahuatl textual pedigree. Early sources use Spanish *Guadalupe*. Proposed Nahuatl reconstructions vary radically and are modern apologetic or popular etymologies, not securely attested pre-Conquest titles.

### Classification

**[EA]** Sahagún classifies Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin as a *diosa*, a goddess, while the underlying Nahuatl category *teōtl* does not map perfectly onto the European category “god.” The figure is simultaneously:

- a named female divine agent;
- a title-bearing maternal deity;
- a manifestation within an overlapping earth-mother complex;
- and, after conquest, a reverential word applied to Mary.

**[MSR]** “Collective” is therefore useful as a modern analytical warning but is not itself the traditional classification. Tonantzin is neither securely a single pan-Nahua individual nor merely an empty generic word.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Material and textual limits

No securely pre-Conquest inscription reads *Tonantzin*. Central Mexican pictorial manuscripts contain female deities recognizable through costume, face paint, calendrical position, and ritual action, but normally not phonetic captions that settle the title’s application. Identifying a codical figure as “Tonantzin” therefore depends on comparison with colonial alphabetic descriptions.

### Principal evidence

**The *Primeros Memoriales*, ca. 1558–1561. [EA]** Compiled in Nahuatl by Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators at Tepepulco, preserved principally in the Madrid codices. It includes divine costumes, festivals, and *teocuicatl*, or divine songs, and is an earlier documentary layer behind the *Florentine Codex*. It establishes the source environment of the Toci–Teteo Innan–Cihuacoatl complex but does not support every later popular assertion about an autonomous Tepeyac goddess named Tonantzin.

**The *Florentine Codex*, 1575–1577 manuscript. [EA]** A twelve-book bilingual Nahuatl-Spanish encyclopedia produced by Sahagún with Nahua elders, grammarians, scribes, and artists at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco.

- **Book I, fol. 2v:** Cihuacoatl is called Tonantzin. She inflicts adversity and appears crying at night.
- **Book I, fol. 3r:** Teteo Innan is also Tlalli Iyollo and Toci, patroness of healers and midwives.
- **Book II, fol. 8r onward:** Ochpaniztli honors Teteo Innan/Toci with the killing and flaying of a female ixiptla and subsequent ritual performances.
- **Book II, fol. 11r:** Tititl honors Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan/Cozcamiauh; her female ixiptla is killed, beheaded, and her head carried in dance.
- **Book VI:** childbirth prayers invoke Cihuacoatl-Quilaztli as “our mother”; birth is described as women’s battle and death in childbirth as martial death.
- **Book XI, fols. 234r–v:** Tepeyac once possessed a temple for the mother of the gods called Tonantzin. Pilgrims of all sexes came from more than twenty leagues with offerings and sacrifices. Sahagún says the contemporary Guadalupe church is also called Tonantzin and condemns the ambiguity as a possible concealment of idolatry.

The manuscript is colonial and missionary-mediated, not a transparent pre-Conquest transcript. Its Nahua column, Spanish paraphrase, illustrations, and editorial interventions are distinct witnesses and sometimes diverge. The standard English edition is Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble’s *Florentine Codex: General History of the Things of New Spain* (1950–1982); the Getty’s Digital Florentine Codex now permits comparison of the manuscript, transcriptions, and translations.

**Sahagún’s *Kalendario mexicano, latino y castellano*, ca. 1585. [EA/colonial polemic]** Repeats the concern that festivals of Cihuacoatl/Tonantzin and analogous cults might persist under Christian names. It confirms that the complaint was not an isolated scribal accident.

**Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*, composed principally in the 1570s–1580s. [EA/colonial ethnography]** Durán gives extensive accounts of the female earth and maize divinities, ritual impersonation, flaying, sacrifice, and the political functioning of Mexica religion. He is important comparative evidence but does not furnish an independent, equally explicit Tepeyac biography for a unique Tonantzin.

**Codex Magliabechiano, mid-sixteenth century. [EA/colonial pictorial]** Depicts and glosses central Mexican festivals and gods, including female earth and death imagery. It helps identify Cihuacoatl/Toci-related regalia but cannot by itself prove that an uncaptained image is “Tonantzin.”

**Codex Borbonicus, late pre-Conquest or very early colonial, dating disputed. [EA]** Its festival sequence, particularly Ochpaniztli, is central to reconstructing the Toci/Teteo Innan ritual complex. The manuscript does not supply an uncomplicated written label *Tonantzin*.

**Borgia Group, chiefly Codex Borgia, Cospi, Fejérváry-Mayer, Laud, and Vaticanus B. [EA]** Probably late Postclassic ritual-divinatory manuscripts, many of uncertain exact provenience. Eduard Seler’s commentaries and Elizabeth Hill Boone’s contextual and structural readings remain foundational. Their earth, death, fertility, and calendrical goddesses supply comparative iconography, not a secure Tepeyac identification.

**Codex Mendoza, ca. 1541. [EA/colonial pictorial-administrative]** Records Mexica imperial history, tribute, and social life. It contextualizes state cult and political economy but contains no securely isolated Tonantzin dossier.

**Codex Telleriano-Remensis, mid-sixteenth century; Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos; Codex Borbonicus. [EA/colonial or transitional]** Important for calendar festivals and deity iconography. They demonstrate that female divine identities were organized calendrically and ritually rather than as one stable “mother goddess” biography.

**Codex Aubin, *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, *Leyenda de los Soles*, and *Histoyre du Mechique*. [EA/early-colonial mythography]** These preserve migration histories, cosmogonies, and divine narratives. They contextualize Coatlicue, Cihuacoatl, Tlaltecuhtli, Quetzalcoatl, and other beings but do not establish a freestanding narrative cycle whose protagonist is unambiguously Tonantzin.

**Cantares Mexicanos, chiefly recorded ca. 1550s–1580s. [EA/colonial manuscript preserving Nahua song]** Maternal invocations and divine songs belong to a poetic register in which *tonan*, “our mother,” need not be a unique name. Each occurrence must be read grammatically and ritually rather than indexed automatically to one goddess.

**Guadalupe texts. [EM/LC]**

- The 1556 inquiry into Franciscan Francisco de Bustamante’s sermon documents an existing shrine, image, offerings, miracles attributed to it, and ecclesiastical dispute, but not the later complete apparition narrative.
- Miguel Sánchez’s Spanish *Imagen de la Virgen María, Madre de Dios de Guadalupe* appeared in 1648.
- Luis Laso de la Vega’s Nahuatl *Huei tlamahuiçoltica*, containing the *Nican mopohua*, appeared in 1649. Its dating and authorship remain disputed; proposed attribution to Antonio Valeriano is not securely demonstrated by a surviving sixteenth-century manuscript.
- Subsequent accounts by Luis Becerra Tanco, Mateo de la Cruz, and Francisco de Florencia expanded and normalized the apparition tradition.

These texts are evidence for the development of Guadalupe devotion, not retrospective proof of a pre-Conquest Tonantzin apparition narrative.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[EA: Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin]**

- Childbirth and its dangers.
- Midwifery and maternal mortality.
- War-like female power: childbirth is rhetorically a battle.
- Adversity, poverty, grief, and terrifying nocturnal appearances.
- Political sovereignty through the parallel office of *cihuacoatl*, the high-ranking Mexica official.
- Earth, death, and maternal generation by association with the broader goddess complex.

**[EA: Teteo Innan/Toci]**

- Healing, medicine, midwifery, divination, and sweat baths.
- Earth’s fertility and the seasonal renewal enacted at Ochpaniztli.
- Military and agricultural renewal following the goddess-impersonator’s death and flaying.
- Patronage of occupational groups, especially healers and midwives.

**[EA: Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan]**

- Aged maternity.
- The dry or mature agricultural season.
- Maize tassels through the name Cozcamiauh.
- Death and calendrical transition in Tititl.

**[MSR]** Fertility, earth, motherhood, death, war, and medicine are therefore documented domains of the figures bearing the title. The common description “Tonantzin, benevolent fertility goddess” removes the terrifying, sacrificial, martial, and death-bearing dimensions present in the sources.

### Iconography

There is no uncontested, standardized “portrait of Tonantzin.” Identifications must specify the manifestation.

**Cihuacoatl. [EA]** Sahagún describes white facial coloration, a mouth area painted black, an eagle-feather headdress or related crown, white clothing, shield, and weaving batten or other martial/ritual implement, depending on the recension. Her nocturnal female appearance and cry are textual attributes rather than a uniquely identifying pictorial sign.

**Teteo Innan/Toci. [EA]** In Sahaguntine descriptions she bears red-ochre face paint, a paper headdress, water-flower garments, foam sandals, and a sun-emblem shield. Ochpaniztli imagery also involves the flayed skin of the sacrificed ixiptla.

**Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan. [EA]** Associated with aged female appearance, white or contrasting facial paint, paper ornaments, and maize-tassel imagery. The Tititl ritual’s severed head is part of her enacted iconography.

### Alleged Tepeyac images

**[EA/uncertain identification]** The so-called *Códice de Teotenantzin*, an eighteenth-century drawing associated with Guillermo Dupaix’s antiquarian materials, records sculptures said to have come from Tepeyac. Modern comparison finds attributes of Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin on one figure. It is valuable evidence for the later documentation of Tepeyac antiquities, but it is neither a pre-Conquest codex nor an excavated sculpture with an intact archaeological context. Its conventional title “Teotenantzin” is itself misleading.

**[MSR]** The absence of a securely excavated Tepeyac idol does not disprove Sahagún’s report; conquest-era destruction, reuse, incomplete excavation, and urban development are plausible causes of loss. Conversely, Sahagún’s report does not license identifying every female sculpture found around Tepeyac as Tonantzin.

### Monumental comparanda that are not portraits of Tonantzin

**Coyolxauhqui stone. [EA]** Discovered on 21 February 1978 at the foot of the Huitzilopochtli stair of the Templo Mayor; dated to Stage IVb, about 1469. It depicts the dismembered Coyolxauhqui and materially anchors the Coatepec-Huitzilopochtli cycle. It does not depict Tonantzin.

**Tlaltecuhtli monolith. [EA]** Discovered 2 October 2006 near the Templo Mayor; more than twelve tons, approximately four meters per side, with surviving pigment. It depicts the earth deity in a birth-giving and devouring posture. It illuminates Mexica earth imagery but is not epigraphically labeled Tonantzin.

**Sun Stone. [EA]** A late Mexica state monument concerned with calendrical-cosmic order. Claims that its central face is Tonantzin are unsupported; the face’s identification is itself debated, commonly involving Tonatiuh or an earth/sun composite.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### No single secure genealogy

**[EA]** No early source provides a genealogy headed by a unique individual “Tonantzin.” Genealogies belong to the named manifestations:

- Cihuacoatl/Quilaztli participates in creation, childbirth, and Culhua-Mexica foundation traditions.
- Coatlicue is mother of Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, and the Centzon Huitznahua in the Coatepec account.
- Teteo Innan is “mother of the gods,” a title of rank and generative status, not a surviving list of individually named offspring.
- Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan is an old-mother agricultural figure without a securely attested nuclear family.

Combining those statements into “Tonantzin married X and bore Y” would manufacture a genealogy.

### Every securely documented divine interaction or joint identification

Because *Tonantzin* is a title, this list distinguishes direct source statements from interactions imported through proposed equations.

- **Cihuacoatl — identity relation. [EA]** “They also called her Tonantzin.” *Florentine Codex* I.6, manuscript completed 1577.
- **Quilaztli — paired identity/invocation. [EA]** Cihuacoatl-Quilaztli, called “our mother,” exacts the tribute of death in childbirth and is invoked by midwives. *Florentine Codex* VI, 1577.
- **Teteo Innan — Tepeyac title relation. [EA but not a fully explicit identity sentence]** The Tepeyac deity is called “mother of the gods” and Tonantzin. *Florentine Codex* XI appendix, 1577.
- **Toci — same goddess complex as Teteo Innan and Tlalli Iyollo. [EA]** Book I explicitly lists the three names; the relation of this exact figure to Tepeyac Tonantzin is inferred through “mother of the gods,” not stated as a four-name equation in one sentence.
- **Tlalli Iyollo — identity with Teteo Innan/Toci. [EA]** *Florentine Codex* I.8, 1577.
- **Ilamatecuhtli — identity with Tonan. [EA]** Tititl festival account, *Florentine Codex* II.17, 1577.
- **Cozcamiauh — identity with Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan. [EA]** Same source and episode.
- **Mary, mother of Jesus — colonial name transfer and contested identification. [SI/EM]** Nahuatl preachers and worshippers called Mary at Guadalupe *Tonantzin*; Sahagún objected that Mary should be called *Dios inantzin*, “God’s mother.” Book XI appendix, ca. 1576–1577.
- **Saint Anne — parallel, not identity with Tonantzin. [SI/EM]** Sahagún reports that Indigenous people called Saint Anne *Toci*, “our grandmother,” and continued traveling to her shrine as pilgrims had done to the earlier Toci. Book XI appendix, 1577. This is evidence for a broader colonial process of Christian maternal-name transfer.
- **Huitzilopochtli — no direct Tonantzin interaction securely attested.** Coatlicue gives birth to Huitzilopochtli in the Coatepec cycle, but applying that episode to Tonantzin requires the disputed Coatlicue equation. **[LS if included]**
- **Coyolxauhqui — no direct Tonantzin interaction securely attested.** She attacks Coatlicue and is killed and dismembered by Huitzilopochtli. Calling Coatlicue “Tonantzin” retrospectively creates an interaction absent under that name. **[LS]**
- **Centzon Huitznahua — same limitation. [LS]** Their conflict is with Coatlicue and Huitzilopochtli in the surviving account.
- **Quetzalcoatl — no securely attested personal encounter with Tonantzin.** Later national and comparative writing juxtaposes Quetzalcoatl and Guadalupe/Tonantzin, but it does not preserve an early mythic meeting.
- **Tezcatlipoca, Tlaloc, Xipe Totec, Mictlantecuhtli, Xochiquetzal, Tlazolteotl, Chicomecoatl, Xilonen, Centeotl, Mayahuel, and the pulque gods — no discrete interaction with a figure explicitly named Tonantzin has been securely identified.** Their calendar adjacency or structural resemblance is not an interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Cihuacoatl’s nocturnal appearances

**[EA]** In *Florentine Codex* Book I, Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin appears dressed as a palace woman and cries or howls through the night air. The text regards her as a source of misfortune. Related passages and later chronicles connect Cihuacoatl’s cries to omens before the Spanish invasion.

**[LC/F]** This became one strand in later explanations of *La Llorona*, the wandering weeping woman. The genealogy is plausible as reception history but not exclusive: colonial Spanish, Christian, and other Indigenous motifs also contributed to Llorona traditions. “Tonantzin was La Llorona” is therefore an overstatement.

### Cihuacoatl-Quilaztli and childbirth

**[EA]** Book VI’s speeches treat childbirth as battle. Midwives invoke Cihuacoatl and Quilaztli; a successful mother has fought bravely, while a woman who dies in childbirth becomes a *cihuateteotl/mocihuaquetzqui*, a dangerous and honored female spirit analogous to a fallen warrior. Cihuacoatl “exacts the tribute of death.”

This is ritual theology rather than a continuous adventure narrative: the goddess establishes the mortal danger of birth and the social status of mothers.

### Teteo Innan/Toci at Ochpaniztli

**[EA]** The fullest cycle is performed rather than narrated. In the month Ochpaniztli:

1. A woman served as the ixiptla of Teteo Innan/Toci.
2. Female healers and midwives accompanied her and engaged in mock combat.
3. She was deceived about her approaching death so that she would not grieve.
4. She was killed in silence and flayed.
5. A male ritual specialist wore her skin, making the slain woman’s body the continuing presence of the goddess.
6. Subsequent martial and agricultural actions connected purification, fertility, sovereignty, and war.

Human sacrifice is not an outsider’s unsupported allegation here: it is described in multiple early colonial sources and is consistent with archaeological evidence for sacrificial death and postmortem processing in Mexica ritual. The exact sequence and theological interpretation vary by source.

### Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan at Tititl

**[EA]** A female impersonator of Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan/Cozcamiauh danced while lamenting her imminent death. Priests brought her to the temple, removed her heart, decapitated her, and danced carrying the head. The episode connects aged motherhood, seasonal decline, maize, and renewal.

This is the clearest ritual attached directly to the name *Tonan*. It should not automatically be assigned to the Tepeyac cult, for which Sahagún reports sacrifices but not this exact ceremony.

### The Coatepec story

**[EA as a Coatlicue myth; LS as a Tonantzin myth]** Coatlicue becomes pregnant when a ball of feathers descends while she sweeps. Coyolxauhqui and the Centzon Huitznahua move to kill her, but Huitzilopochtli emerges fully armed, kills Coyolxauhqui, and defeats her brothers.

Modern retellings frequently rename Coatlicue “Tonantzin.” The textual record supports a broad maternal-earth comparison, not the claim that the earliest story names its protagonist Tonantzin.

### Tepeyac and Guadalupe

**[EA for the sixteenth-century cult; LC for the apparition story]** The historical sequence is more secure than a single seamless legend:

1. Sahagún reports that Tepeyac had possessed a temple for the mother of the gods called Tonantzin, attracting long-distance pilgrims and offerings.
2. A Christian chapel and image of Our Lady of Guadalupe were established there by the mid-sixteenth century.
3. The 1556 controversy records Archbishop Alonso de Montúfar’s promotion of devotion and Franciscan Bustamante’s objection to the image, miracles, and offerings.
4. By about 1576, Sahagún complained that Indigenous pilgrims still called the figure Tonantzin and came “as formerly.”
5. The fully developed apparition narrative—Mary appearing to the Nahua Juan Diego in December 1531, requesting a church, producing Castilian roses, and imprinting her image on his tilma—first survives in major publications of 1648 and 1649.
6. That narrative became Catholic canonical devotion and later received ecclesiastical affirmation, including Juan Diego’s canonization in 2002.

The apparition belongs fully in the history of belief. Its late surviving documentation must nevertheless be distinguished from the sixteenth-century evidence for the shrine.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Tepeyac

**[EA: textual]** Sahagún identifies Tepeyacac/Tepeaquilla as one of several mountain-associated pilgrimage centers. He states that a temple there honored “the mother of the gods” called Tonantzin; pilgrims traveled more than twenty leagues and brought many offerings; men, women, youths, and maidens attended; and “many sacrifices” occurred.

**[Unestablished]** He does not specify:

- the temple’s architectural plan;
- its foundation date;
- its exact location on the hill;
- the form, number, or sex of sacrificial victims;
- the deity’s image;
- or an uninterrupted priestly succession into the Christian era.

**[Archaeological caution]** The pre-Conquest Tepeyac sanctuary has not been recovered as an uncontested excavated architectural complex. Recent studies of the surrounding Tepeyacac, Zacahuitzco, and Yohualtecatl hills report evidence of ritual use and local female and male divine cults, strengthening the sacred-landscape context without solving every identification.

### Tenochtitlan and other cult places

Cihuacoatl, Toci, and related manifestations possessed cultic locations in Tenochtitlan, including Tocititlan and the Atempan/Temazcaltitlan complex. The political office *cihuacoatl*, famously held by Tlacaelel, embodied the close relation between divine authority and the government of Tenochtitlan.

Sahagún also describes a Toci/Tzapotlatenan pilgrimage near Tlaxcala. After a church of Saint Anne was erected, Indigenous people called the saint *Toci* because she was Christ’s grandmother. Sahagún suspected continuity beneath the Christian name. This parallel demonstrates that Tepeyac was not an isolated case.

### Personnel and offerings

Depending on manifestation, cult personnel included:

- midwives and female healers;
- physicians, herbalists, and diviners;
- priests conducting sacrifice and wearing flayed skins;
- ixiptla impersonators who embodied rather than merely represented the deity;
- civic and military officers;
- pilgrims bearing offerings.

Offerings likely included incense, food, textiles, flowers, paper, and sacrificial blood, but only offerings explicitly assigned by a source should be claimed for a particular shrine.

### Human sacrifice

**[EA and archaeological]** The festival descriptions explicitly record women killed as the embodiments of Teteo Innan/Toci and Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan. Methods include heart extraction, flaying, decapitation, and ritual reuse of skin or head. These rites connected divine presence to an embodied victim; “impersonator” must not be misunderstood as merely theatrical.

The Huei Tzompantli discovered in Mexico City during 2015–2017 excavations confirms the large-scale display and architectural incorporation of human skulls in the Mexica sacred precinct. Earlier Templo Mayor finds document men, women, and children among sacrificial remains. These discoveries validate the material reality of sacrifice and postmortem processing.

They do **not**, however, demonstrate that the Huei Tzompantli belonged to Tonantzin or that its victims came from Tepeyac. The Templo Mayor was chiefly dedicated to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc. Linking every Mexica sacrifice to Tonantzin would be as misleading as denying sacrifice altogether.

### Temple prostitution

No reliable primary or archaeological evidence establishes “temple prostitution” as an institution of Tonantzin’s cult. Sexualized readings of fertility goddesses and loose comparison with Mediterranean religions are not evidence. **Finding: unestablished.**

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1521: reconstructable late Postclassic background

**[EA/MSR]** Late Postclassic central Mexico possessed multiple maternal-earth-war-healing divinities rather than a single universally standardized Mother Goddess. Local cults, calendar rites, and imperial incorporation produced overlapping names and costumes. Cihuacoatl, Quilaztli, Toci, Teteo Innan, Tlalli Iyollo, Ilamatecuhtli, Coatlicue, and Tlaltecuhtli shared features without becoming interchangeable in every context.

Tepeyac lay on the northern causeway from Tenochtitlan and formed part of a larger sacred hill and lakeshore landscape. Sahagún’s post-Conquest testimony is the principal explicit evidence for its Tonantzin pilgrimage.

### 1521–1555: conquest and conversion

Spanish victory brought destruction or abandonment of temples, seizure and reuse of sacred spaces, suppression of sacrifice, and intensive Christian evangelization. The earliest Tepeyac chapel’s exact foundation history is obscure. The proposition that missionaries deliberately selected the site specifically to “replace Tonantzin” is plausible within broader colonial practice but is not documented by a surviving foundation order stating that policy.

### 1556–1577: the shrine and the Franciscan objection

By 1556 devotion to the Guadalupe image, offerings, pilgrimage, and reported miracles were established. Archbishop Montúfar supported it; Francisco de Bustamante attacked what he saw as improper promotion of a painted image and commercial or credulous miracle claims.

Sahagún later framed the problem in Indigenous-language terms: because preachers called Mary *Tonantzin*, pilgrims could understand the Christian cult through the old title. He preferred *Dios inantzin*, “mother of God,” as unambiguous. His testimony proves his perception of dangerous continuity; it does not disclose each pilgrim’s private intention.

### 1648–1700: apparition narrative and Creole appropriation

Miguel Sánchez’s 1648 *Imagen* gave the cult an elaborate providential and biblical interpretation. Laso de la Vega’s 1649 *Huei tlamahuiçoltica* supplied the classic Nahuatl apparition account. Later seventeenth-century authors expanded the story.

Guadalupe became a sign of New Spain’s divine election and increasingly of Creole distinctiveness. Indigenous devotion remained important, but the cult cannot be reduced either to disguised Indigenous resistance or to elite Creole invention; institutions and communities appropriated it differently.

### Eighteenth century: national patroness and learned system

Creole intellectuals integrated Guadalupe with theories of ancient Mexican revelation, sometimes paralleling Mary/Tonantzin with Saint Thomas/Quetzalcoatl. Papal approval of Guadalupe as patroness of New Spain in 1754 strengthened official standing. Sermons, histories, and images multiplied.

### 1810–nineteenth century: political emblem

Miguel Hidalgo used a Guadalupe image in the insurgency of 1810. Royalists also invoked Marian symbols, but Guadalupe’s insurgent use helped consolidate her as a Mexican national figure. Liberal anticlerical politics did not erase popular devotion.

Antiquarians and early historians increasingly treated Tonantzin as Guadalupe’s pre-Hispanic predecessor. This helped turn a sixteenth-century title and local cult report into a national historical symbol.

### Twentieth century: scholarship, Indigenismo, and Chicana revision

Archaeology, critical editions, art history, anthropology, and nationalism all reshaped the figure. Tonantzin became:

- a shorthand for Indigenous maternity;
- the suppressed “Aztec original” beneath Guadalupe;
- an earth-goddess emblem in muralism and nationalist art;
- and, in Chicana thought, one part of a reclaimed female genealogy including Coatlicue and Guadalupe.

Gloria Anzaldúa’s *Borderlands/La Frontera* (1987) influentially opposed patriarchal splitting of Coatlicue/Tonantzin and Guadalupe and made their recombination a language of mestiza and queer spiritual identity. This is a modern critical-spiritual construction, not a recovered pre-Conquest creed.

### Twenty-first century

Catholic Guadalupe devotion remains massive and transnational. Some Indigenous, Mexicayotl, Chicano/a, feminist, neopagan, and eclectic practitioners address Guadalupe as Tonantzin, worship Tonantzin separately, or understand both as manifestations of an Indigenous mother power.

There is no reliable census of a continuous institutional priesthood of Tonantzin from the fifteenth century to the present. Contemporary worship is better documented as revival, reconstruction, creative continuity, or Catholic-Indigenous religious hybridity, depending on the community’s own account.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Guadalupe as Tonantzin

**[F/SI]** Calling Guadalupe *Tonantzin* is itself sixteenth-century and therefore not a modern invention. What is disputed is what the usage meant. It could express:

- ordinary translation of “our mother”;
- Indigenous appropriation of Mary;
- continued attachment to a sacred place and maternal title;
- deliberate concealment of old devotion;
- or a combination varying among worshippers.

### “The Spaniards simply renamed Tonantzin as Guadalupe”

**[MI as stated]** This compresses a century of contested development into a single planned act. There is evidence for site and name continuity, missionary participation, ecclesiastical conflict, and eventual fusion. There is no surviving document demonstrating a single coordinated substitution program or showing that all Indigenous pilgrims understood the two figures as identical.

### Lafaye’s thesis

Jacques Lafaye’s *Quetzalcóatl et Guadalupe* (French thesis, 1972; major edition 1974; English translation 1976/1987) traced how Saint Thomas–Quetzalcoatl and Mary–Tonantzin constructions contributed to Creole and Mexican national consciousness. Its strength is the long history of intellectual appropriation: it treats syncretism as a historical process bound to nation formation.

Its more sweeping formulations can imply a stable pre-Hispanic “Tonantzin mother goddess” transferred into Guadalupe. The title’s multivalence and the sparse archaeology of Tepeyac require more restraint.

### Poole’s critique

Stafford Poole’s *Our Lady of Guadalupe: The Origins and Sources of a Mexican National Symbol, 1531–1797* (1995) and *The Guadalupan Controversies in Mexico* (2006) subject the apparition tradition to documentary chronology. Poole emphasizes:

- the silence of expected early witnesses about the 1531 apparitions;
- the difference between evidence for an early shrine and evidence for the complete apparition story;
- the decisive 1648–1649 publication moment;
- and the danger of assuming that use of *Tonantzin* proves survival of one pre-Christian goddess.

Poole does not erase Sahagún’s testimony. His critique narrows what it can prove: continuity of name, place, pilgrimage, and Sahagún’s suspicion are documented; a simple identity of cultic content is not.

### Feminist, Chicana, and artistic reception

**[Modern reception]** Anzaldúa and subsequent writers and artists treat Coatlicue-Tonantzin-Guadalupe as a fractured and recombinable genealogy of Indigenous and mestiza femininity. Alma López’s *Our Lady* (1999) and related Guadalupan art rework Marian imagery through queer, feminist, and Indigenous symbolism. Such works have materially affected contemporary understandings even when ecclesiastical critics reject them.

### Neopaganism and Mexicayotl

**[Modern revival]** Contemporary practitioners reconstruct offerings, prayers, dances, and calendar observances from codices and colonial descriptions. Practices vary widely:

- some distinguish Cihuacoatl, Toci, Coatlicue, and Tonantzin;
- others treat them as aspects of one Great Mother;
- some identify Tonantzin with Guadalupe;
- others reject the Marian identification as colonial.

Claims of an unchanged secret lineage from pre-Conquest Tepeyac require evidence and generally cannot be verified.

### Occult claims

No significant Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoire tradition concerning Tonantzin has been documented. Her presence in eclectic goddess spirituality, New Age writing, chaos magic, and internet occult lists is chiefly late twentieth- or twenty-first-century borrowing.

Equations with Isis, Cybele, Demeter, Kali, Gaia, or the Jungian Great Mother are **[modern comparativism]**, not ancient cross-cultural identifications.

### Political uses

Tonantzin-Guadalupe imagery has supported Mexican nationalism, Chicano/a activism, Indigenous reclamation, feminism, migrant solidarity, and Catholic political mobilization. It has also been commercialized in fashion, tourism, tattoo culture, music, and branding.

No major documented modern extremist organization has made the historically reconstructed cult of Tonantzin its central ideology. Generalized use of Mexica imagery by racial nationalists should not be projected specifically onto Tonantzin without group-specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Tonantzin a proper name?

The best-supported answer is “sometimes treated as one, but linguistically and historically also a title.”

Sahagún’s Spanish prose treats it as the appellation of an ancient goddess, while its morphology transparently means “our revered mother.” The same expression’s application to Cihuacoatl, Mary, the Church, and maternal figures prevents a purely proper-name reading.

### Which goddess was worshipped at Tepeyac?

Principal positions include:

1. **Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin:** supported by Sahagún’s explicit Cihuacoatl/Tonantzin equation elsewhere and by iconographic comparison.
2. **Toci/Teteo Innan:** supported by “mother of the gods,” the shared maternal-earth complex, and parallels in pilgrimage and healing.
3. **A distinct local mother goddess known principally by the title Tonantzin:** possible because local cult identities need not match metropolitan classifications exactly.
4. **A modern scholarly composite:** emphasizes that surviving evidence is insufficient to choose one unique identity.

The evidence does not justify absolute certainty. “Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin” is the strongest explicit textual connection; “Coatlicue-Tonantzin” is culturally influential but less directly stated.

### Was Guadalupe a survival of Tonantzin?

**Continuity evidence:**

- same general place;
- long-distance pilgrimage before and after conversion, according to Sahagún;
- continued Indigenous participation;
- continued title *Tonantzin*;
- Sahagún’s contemporary suspicion;
- analogous Toci/Saint Anne naming;
- broader colonial patterns of sacred-site reuse.

**Discontinuity or cautionary evidence:**

- no securely excavated Tepeyac temple plan or idol;
- *tonantzin* was legitimate Nahuatl for an honored mother and was used in Christian discourse;
- Christian Marian devotion possessed its own Iberian Guadalupe history;
- the 1556 and later church institutions actively constructed a Catholic cult;
- the complete Juan Diego narrative appears securely in print only in 1648–1649;
- worshippers’ intentions cannot be recovered from the shared word alone.

The defensible conclusion is layered continuity and transformation, not either perfect survival or total replacement.

### Nicholson’s typology

Henry B. Nicholson’s 1971 classification of Late Pre-Hispanic central Mexican religion grouped deities by conceptual complexes. It remains useful for recognizing related functions but can encourage readers to convert analytical clusters into single gods. Tonantzin demonstrates the danger particularly clearly.

### Boone and the Borgia Group

Elizabeth Hill Boone reads ritual-divinatory manuscripts as structured systems of time, fate, and performance. This approach discourages treating every visually similar female figure as a portrait of one Great Goddess. Context, day signs, costume, and sequence govern identity.

### Archaeological transformation of the field

The Coyolxauhqui discovery in 1978 initiated the modern Templo Mayor Project and materially confirmed the close relation between architecture and the Coatepec narrative. The 2006 Tlaltecuhtli monolith revealed monumental earth imagery and surviving polychromy. The 2015–2017 Huei Tzompantli excavations strengthened archaeological evidence for large-scale sacrificial display.

None carries the phonetic name Tonantzin. Their relevance is comparative: they make the maternal-earth-sacrificial world materially visible while delimiting what can be assigned to the Tepeyac title.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- **“Tonantzin Coatlaxopeuh” was the ancient name heard as Guadalupe:** unsupported modern etymology.
- **The Guadalupe image is a surviving Aztec codex whose every star, fold, flower, and color forms a fixed Indigenous message:** some imagery could communicate across cultures, but exhaustive symbolic decoding systems are chiefly modern apologetic constructions.
- **Tonantzin was simply Coatlicue’s benevolent side:** modern harmonization.
- **All Nahua goddesses were forms of one primordial Mother Goddess:** comparativist system, not a statement of the manuscripts.
- **There was no Indigenous cult at Tepeyac because archaeology has not found its temple:** argument from absence that discounts Sahagún.
- **Sahagún proves the Juan Diego apparition was invented to suppress Tonantzin:** his passage neither mentions Juan Diego nor explains the apparition story’s authorship.
- **Human sacrifice was wholly a Spanish fabrication:** contradicted by Indigenous-authored or collaborative texts, imagery, osteological evidence, and the tzompantli.
- **Every sacrifice described for Toci or Ilamatecuhtli occurred at Tepeyac:** unsupported conflation.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Late Postclassic, ca. 1200–1521 | Female maternal, earth, healing, maize, and war deities are represented in central Mexican ritual art and later-described calendars. No surviving object securely bears the phonetic label *Tonantzin*. |
| Fourteenth–early sixteenth centuries | Mexica Tenochtitlan incorporates Cihuacoatl, Toci/Teteo Innan, Coatlicue, Ilamatecuhtli, and other female cults into civic and calendar religion. |
| Before 1521, exact date unknown | According to Sahagún’s later report, a Tepeyac temple honors the mother of the gods called Tonantzin and receives pilgrims, offerings, sacrifices, men, women, and youths from distant communities. |
| 1519–1521 | Spanish-Indigenous war destroys the Mexica imperial order; temple cult and public human sacrifice are suppressed under colonial rule. |
| 1520s–1550s | Christian evangelization establishes chapels and reuses or contests Indigenous sacred landscapes. The precise founding history of the earliest Tepeyac hermitage is uncertain. |
| 1556 | Bustamante-Montúfar controversy documents an active Guadalupe shrine, image, offerings, miracle claims, and Franciscan opposition. |
| ca. 1558–1561 | Sahagún and Nahua collaborators compile the *Primeros Memoriales*. |
| 1575–1577 | Final *Florentine Codex* produced. It explicitly identifies Cihuacoatl as Tonantzin, Ilamatecuhtli as Tonan, and the former Tepeyac mother-of-gods cult as Tonantzin. |
| ca. 1576–1577 | Sahagún denounces calling Guadalupe *Tonantzin*, fearing concealed idolatry; he records continuing long-distance pilgrimage. |
| ca. 1585 | Sahagún’s calendrical writing reiterates concern about Cihuacoatl/Tonantzin and Christianized cult continuity. |
| 1648 | Miguel Sánchez publishes the first extensive Spanish apparition theology, *Imagen de la Virgen María*. |
| 1649 | Laso de la Vega publishes the Nahuatl *Huei tlamahuiçoltica*, including the *Nican mopohua*. |

| 1754 | Papal action confirms Guadalupe’s patronal status in New Spain and authorizes liturgical observance. |
| 1794 | Servando Teresa de Mier’s sermon connects Guadalupe to theories of pre-Hispanic evangelization and Saint Thomas/Quetzalcoatl, provoking controversy. |
| 1810 | Hidalgo’s insurgency uses Guadalupe as a political-religious emblem. |
| Nineteenth century | Antiquarian, nationalist, Catholic, liberal, and Indigenous interpretations increasingly debate Tonantzin–Guadalupe continuity. |

| 1950–1982 | Anderson and Dibble publish the standard English/Nahuatl edition of the *Florentine Codex*. |
| 1971 | Nicholson publishes his influential typology of central Mexican deity complexes. |
| 1972–1974 | Lafaye’s *Quetzalcóatl et Guadalupe* develops the syncretism and national-consciousness thesis. |

| 1987 | Gloria Anzaldúa’s *Borderlands/La Frontera* gives Coatlicue-Tonantzin-Guadalupe a foundational role in mestiza feminist spirituality. |
| 1995 | Stafford Poole publishes his source-critical history of Guadalupe, challenging simple continuity and early-apparition assumptions. |

| 2006 | Poole’s *Guadalupan Controversies* extends the documentary critique. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavation identifies remains of the Huei Tzompantli of Tenochtitlan, materially confirming large-scale skull display. |
| 2023 | Getty Research Institute launches the searchable Digital Florentine Codex, enabling direct comparison of manuscript images and multilingual texts. |
| Present | Guadalupe remains a major Catholic and national devotion; Tonantzin is separately or jointly invoked in Indigenous revival, Mexicayotl, Chicana feminism, art, folk practice, and neopagan religion. No unbroken institutional Tonantzin cult from before 1521 has been demonstrated. |
