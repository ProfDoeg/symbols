# The Tzitzimime: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A collective of powerful celestial and chthonic beings—predominantly female in the strongest pre-Hispanic iconographic reconstruction—variously classified as goddesses, ancestral spirits, stellar beings, dangerous night powers, and, in colonial Christian vocabulary, “demons” or “devils.” *Tzitzimitl* is singular; *tzitzimimeh* is the Classical Nahuatl plural, conventionally written *Tzitzimime*.
>
> **Earliest secure explicit attestation:** The named figure is securely documented in the first post-Conquest generation, most visibly in the mid-sixteenth-century *Codex Tudela* (folio 46r) and *Codex Magliabechiano* (folio 76r), followed by Sahagún’s Nahuatl materials collected principally in the 1550s–1570s. Pre-Conquest Borgia-group figures and Mexica stone sculpture may represent Tzitzimime, but those identifications are iconographic reconstructions rather than surviving pre-Conquest captions.
>
> **Historical core:** At moments when the ordered passage of celestial time seemed liable to fail—solar eclipses and especially the completion of a fifty-two-year calendar round—the Tzitzimime were expected to descend from the darkened sky and eat human beings. That dangerous aspect coexisted with generative, healing, obstetrical, calendrical, and ancestral functions. The common modern description “evil star demons” preserves only one side of an ambivalent category and partly reproduces colonial Christian demonization.

## Evidence labels used

- **[EA] Securely attested early:** pre-Conquest or first-generation colonial evidence preserving Indigenous-language testimony or iconography.
- **[CE] Colonial elaboration:** sixteenth- or seventeenth-century record, often mediated by Christian authors or terminology.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a tradition restricted to a locality or cultural region.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** colonial or modern arrangement of disparate beings into a uniform pantheon.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historical equation made across religious systems.
- **[F] Folklore:** later oral or ethnographic continuation.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference from comparative texts, images, archaeology, or linguistics.
- **[MI] Modern invention or popular retelling:** unsupported or materially transformed modern claim.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**[EA/CE]** Classical Nahuatl *tzitzimitl* denotes one member of the class; *tzitzimimeh* is its plural. Sixteenth-century lexicographers and friars commonly translated it with Christianizing terms such as Spanish *demonio* and *diablo*. Molina’s 1571 vocabulary gives “nombre de demonio” and “demonio”; Olmos’s grammar and Sahagún’s materials likewise use demonic glosses. The University of Mexico’s historical dictionary assembles these forms and occurrences but also demonstrates that “demon” is a colonial translation, not an etymology. The literal derivation remains uncertain. [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/tzitzimitl/167630)

Orthographic forms include:

- *tzitzimitl* — normalized singular.
- *tzitzimimeh* — morphologically explicit plural; often printed *tzitzimime*.
- *tzitzimi*, *tzitzimis*, *cicimime*, *zizimitl* — colonial Spanish or manuscript spellings.
- *tzitzimicihuatl* — “Tzitzimitl-woman,” a compound preserved lexically.
- *Ilhuicatzitzquiqueh* — “those who hold up the sky,” associated or partially overlapping aerial/celestial beings in Tezozomoc’s colonial account, but not demonstrably an exact synonym in every context.

**[MSR—disputed etymology]** No widely accepted compositional etymology exists. Popular explanations such as “arrow that pierces,” often constructed from *mītl* (“arrow”), or “old grandmother,” lack secure historical-linguistic demonstration. Cecelia Klein therefore leaves the term untranslated. The absence of a transparent etymology is important: translations such as “star demon” describe an interpreted function, not the word’s established literal meaning. Klein’s study remains the fullest focused assessment. [Klein, “The Devil and the Skirt”](https://nahuatl.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/view/9222)

### Collective or individual?

**[EA/CE]** The sources use both a collective plural and an individuated singular. The Magliabechiano and Tudela images show one *Tzitzimitl*. The *Histoyre du Mechique* calls Mayahuel’s grandmother a member of the class and her companions *Cicimime*. Consequently, “the Tzitzimime” is not a single goddess with a fixed biography.

**[MSR]** Modern authors sometimes produce a closed roster, usually naming Itzpapalotl, Cihuacoatl, Coatlicue, Citlalinicue, Tlaltecuhtli, and one or more color-qualified Tzitzimime. The evidence is uneven. Some figures possess explicit textual associations; others are grouped through shared skeletal, stellar, skirt, shell-border, skull-and-crossbones, or splayed-body iconography. A closed canonical membership list does not survive.

### Named and qualified forms

- **Citlalinicue, “Star-Her-Skirt”** — **[EA/MSR]** A creator or celestial goddess represented in the *Codex Borbonicus*. Seler, Caso, and Klein identify her with an important Tzitzimitl on the basis of her skeletal head, banner crest, starry or dotted shell-bordered skirt, and position in calendrical imagery. The name itself does not mean “Tzitzimitl.”
- **Itzpapalotl, “Obsidian Butterfly”** — **[EA/LS]** A skeletal, warriorlike female supernatural of the Borgia group and narrative sources. Her classification among the Tzitzimime is well established in scholarship, but modern assertions that she was their formally installed “queen” or “commander” exceed the wording of the surviving sources.
- **Cihuacoatl, “Woman-Serpent”** — **[EA/MSR]** A childbirth, warfare, earth, and death goddess sharing Tzitzimitl attributes and repeatedly associated with women who died in childbirth.
- **Ilamatecuhtli, “Old-Woman Lord/Lady”** — **[EA/MSR]** Aged female deity whose Tititl rites and regalia belong to the same nocturnal, ancestral, and warrior-female complex.
- **Coatlicue, “Serpents-Her-Skirt”** — **[EA/MSR]** Connected by Klein to Tzitzimitl garment symbolism and primordial generative power. She should not automatically be called “a star demon.”
- **Tlaltecuhtli, “Earth Lord/Lady”** — **[EA/MSR]** An earth being, simultaneously devourer and generator. Tzitzimitl identifications of particular splayed earth-deity images remain interpretive.
- **Xoxouhcaltzitzimitl** and color-associated Tzitzimime — **[CE/LS]** Names circulated in colonial lists and later syntheses, but the spellings, meanings, and roster vary. They should not be presented as a universally canonical “four Tzitzimime” without specifying a source.
- **Omitecuhtli, “Bone Lord”** — **[CE/MSR]** A male being whom Klein treats as a Tzitzimitl associated with healing, especially of children, and generative power. His existence warns against making “female” an exceptionless rule.

### Classification through time

1. **Late Postclassic central Mexico:** **[EA/MSR]** A porous category of stellar, ancestral, calendrical, earth, death, and female generative powers.
2. **Early colonial period:** **[CE/SI]** “Monsters,” *demonios*, fallen or airborne devils, and beings of darkness. Native imagery nevertheless retained female dress and pre-Conquest regalia.
3. **Modern popular mythology:** **[MI]** A standardized “race of female skeletal star demons” led by Itzpapalotl. This is partly source-based but falsely tidy.
4. **Some modern Nahua traditions:** **[F]** Cognate terms may denote harmful airs, dangerous envy, old supernatural women, forest beings, or rulers of a realm of the dead. These are later regional developments, not transparent fossils of imperial Mexica doctrine.

There was no surviving Greco-Roman *interpretatio* of the class. Comparisons with fallen angels, Lilith, Valkyries, “zombies,” or generic demons are modern analogies unless explicitly presented as colonial Christian translation.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest evidence

No securely dated pre-Conquest inscription spelling the name *Tzitzimitl* has been identified. The evidentiary sequence is instead:

- **Borgia-group manuscripts, probably Late Postclassic:** **[EA/MSR]** Skeletal and star-marked beings in the *Codex Borgia*, *Codex Cospi*, and related divinatory books are plausible Tzitzimime. Since these books contain pictorial-semantic signs rather than identifying alphabetic captions, the attribution is interpretive. Boone emphasizes that these books are divinatory almanacs, not Western-style narrative pantheon manuals. [University of Texas Press, *Cycles of Time and Meaning*](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292712638/)
- **Pre-Conquest Mexica sculpture, fifteenth–early sixteenth century:** **[EA/MSR]** Skull-and-crossbones skirts, shell-bordered garments, stellar eyes, exposed ribs, claws, and splayed bodies create a strong iconographic field. Individual identifications remain contested.
- **Codex Tudela, approximately 1550s, folio 46r:** **[CE]** A skeletal, female-garbed Tzitzimitl with blood issuing from its mouth.
- **Codex Magliabechiano, mid-sixteenth century, folio 76r:** **[CE]** The best-known explicitly labeled image: skeletal head and limbs, open mouth, disheveled hair, talons, monstrous faces at joints, sacrificial-paper banners, necklace of human organs or hearts, and a female *huipil*. Its Spanish commentary calls it a dead man or demonlike being, creating a gender conflict between commentary and dress. [Facsimile description](https://www.facsimiles.com/facsimiles/codex-magliabechiano)
- **Sahagún corpus, 1550s–1570s:** **[CE with EA testimony]** Nahuatl testimony collected from Indigenous informants, ultimately incorporated into the *Florentine Codex*.

The earliest explicit record is therefore colonial in physical manufacture, although it preserves Indigenous categories and imagery only decades after the conquest. Claims that a specific unlabeled Borgia image proves a much earlier textual name must be qualified.

### Principal textual and pictorial witnesses

#### Sahagún: *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex*

**Language and date:** Nahuatl materials initially gathered at Tepeapulco around 1558–1561; expanded and reorganized at Tlatelolco; final bilingual *Florentine Codex* prepared principally in the 1570s.

**Tzitzimime material:**

- **Book 7, New Fire account:** If new fire could not be drilled at the close of the fifty-two-year round, permanent night would follow and the Tzitzimime would descend and eat human beings. This is the strongest source for the supposed periodic “end of the world.”
- **Book 8, folio 2r:** An eclipse during or associated in the annalistic narrative with Ahuítzotl’s era made stars visible at midday. People feared that the Tzitzimime would descend and devour them. The Nahuatl reads in part, *ca oaltemozque in tzitzimime, tequazque*—“the Tzitzimime would come down; they would eat people.” [Digital Florentine Codex, Book 8, folio 2r](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/book/8/folio/2r)
- **Book 2 and associated festival texts:** Female deity impersonators and earth/ancestral goddesses possess regalia later used to identify the Tzitzimitl complex.
- **Book 6:** Childbirth speeches and the classification of a woman who dies in labor as a warrior support the broader Cihuateteo–Tzitzimime relationship, although they do not make every Cihuateteotl a Tzitzimitl by simple definition.

**Transmission warning:** Sahagún’s work is a collaborative Indigenous-colonial archive created for an evangelizing project. The Nahuatl and Spanish columns sometimes classify beings differently; neither should automatically be treated as an unmediated “Aztec Bible.”

#### *Codex Magliabechiano* and *Codex Tudela*

**[CE]** Both descend from the so-called Magliabechiano-group prototype. Their cognate Tzitzimitl images are crucial because the Tudela version substitutes a stream of blood for the Magliabechiano’s projecting sacrificial-knife tongue and serpentlike lower element. The images preserve female garments despite masculine Spanish glosses.

#### *Codex Borbonicus*

**[EA/early CE; dating disputed]** Its divinatory section repeatedly presents a skeletal-headed figure with disordered hair, banners, and a red shell-bordered or star-dotted skirt. Seler and Caso called her Citlalinicue; Klein connects her to the Tzitzimime. Because the manuscript’s exact pre- versus early post-Conquest date remains debated, it cannot alone settle the chronology.

#### *Codex Borgia* and Borgia group

**[EA/MSR]** Pages 47–48 contain male and female calendrical spirit pairs commonly termed Maquiltonaleque and Cihuateteo; pages 49–52 contain skeletal, splayed, star-associated beings identified by several scholars as Tzitzimime. Page 29’s skeletal entity and descending spider/wind imagery have also been compared with the class. These scenes concern divination and cosmic forces, not necessarily a literal narrative assembly of a “demon army.” A Princeton exhibition summarizes the association of the Borgia beings with diviners, midwives, and curers. [Princeton Art Museum](https://static.artmuseum.princeton.edu/Sorcerers/codex.html)

#### *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*

**[CE/SI]** Folio 18v’s account of divine beings expelled from Tamoanchan for cutting flowers and branches has been connected with Tzitzimime. The story exhibits possible biblical Edenic influence and therefore cannot safely be projected unchanged into the pre-Conquest period.

#### *Histoyre du Mechique*

**Language and transmission:** A mid-sixteenth-century French rendering of a lost Spanish account drawing on Indigenous Mexican information.

**[CE preserving an Indigenous myth]** In the maguey origin story, Ehecatl takes the young Mayahuel from the sky. They hide on earth as the two branches of a tree. Her grandmother, a Tzitzimitl, summons the other Tzitzimime, descends, recognizes Mayahuel’s branch, breaks it, and distributes her body for the group to eat. Ehecatl later buries Mayahuel’s remains, from which maguey grows. The source calls the Tzitzimime goddesses, contradicting exclusively male colonial classifications.

#### *Leyenda de los Soles* / Manuscript of 1558

**[CE preserving earlier traditions]** This Nahuatl cosmogonic compilation records the succession of Suns, divine sacrifices, celestial transformations, and beings accompanying the moon. Tzitzimime-related stellar and destructive motifs belong to its cosmological environment, but the popular formula “the Tzitzimime certainly destroy the Fifth Sun” combines this cosmology with Sahagún’s New Fire eschatology more neatly than either surviving text does alone.

#### *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*

**[CE]** Important for calendrical, Toltec, and Quetzalcoatl traditions and transmitted with the *Leyenda de los Soles* in the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. It supplies comparative cosmological context but is not the main direct witness for the named collective.

#### Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*

**[CE]** Written in the later sixteenth century. Durán documents Mexica festivals, temple practice, deity impersonation, sacrifice, and New Fire ceremonialism. His material helps reconstruct the ritual environment in which fear of cosmic interruption operated, but his Spanish theological vocabulary and tendency to compare Mexican traditions with biblical or Old World history require source criticism.

#### *Codex Mendoza*

**[CE]** Created around 1541–1542. Its tribute lists, conquest history, and social-life section illuminate the imperial economy and priestly discipline but do not provide an unambiguous named Tzitzimitl dossier. Its omission is evidence against assigning the collective a major separately administered state temple solely on later assumptions.

#### *Cantares Mexicanos*

**[CE]** A late-sixteenth-century Nahuatl song collection preserving pre-Conquest-style and colonial compositions. It is essential for Nahua sacred poetics and the imagery of flowers, death, warfare, ancestors, and celestial transformation, but no single uncontested Tzitzimime narrative comparable to the *Histoyre* story anchors the collection.

#### Tezozomoc and Chimalpahin

**[CE]** Tezozomoc describes Tzitzimime and *Ilhuicatzitzquiqueh* as airborne angels or gods who support the sky and bring rain, water, thunder, lightning, and thunderbolts. Chimalpahin’s account of an eclipse in the year 10 Rabbit—usually correlated with 1478—mentions stars, phantoms, harmful airborne beings, and terror. These broaden the category beyond man-eating apocalypse.

### Significant absences

No surviving Tzitzimime hymn corpus, dedicated royal annals, independent priestly manual, known alphabetic pre-Conquest inscription, or securely identified exclusive temple archive survives. The record is dispersed across cosmology, calendrical books, eclipse and New Fire accounts, female-deity iconography, ritual dress, childbirth practice, and colonial demonological translation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Celestial darkness and interrupted time

**[EA/CE]** Their best-attested threatening domain is the darkened sky. They become perceptible or able to descend when sunlight fails: at eclipse, at the perilous end of the fifty-two-year Calendar Round, and ultimately in imagined cosmic collapse. They are related to stars, but “stars” here are not benign decorative bodies. They are powers revealed when the Sun is absent.

**[MSR]** The statement that the stars themselves “attack” or “eat” the Sun is a reasonable interpretation of eclipse terror but should not replace the more precise source statement: people saw stars in daytime and feared that the Tzitzimime would descend and eat humans.

### Devouring and dismemberment

**[CE]** Their characteristic catastrophic action is eating people. In the *Histoyre du Mechique*, they actually dismember and eat Mayahuel. In Sahagún’s eclipse and New Fire passages, their anthropophagy is prospective and conditional.

### Generation, childbirth, and healing

**[EA/MSR]** The Tzitzimime’s threatening side coexists with reproductive power:

- Their female dress, aged-female identities, and relations with Cihuacoatl, Citlalinicue, Ilamatecuhtli, Coatlicue, and the Cihuateteo associate them with conception, delivery, maternal death, and regeneration.
- Midwives and curers likely petitioned related powers.
- Omitecuhtli was asked to heal sick persons, especially children.
- Klein argues that garments and garment-shaped platforms materialized the beings’ generative essence, allowing dangerous power to be approached protectively.
- The *Histoyre* episode converts Mayahuel’s death into the birth of maguey: destruction produces nourishment and intoxicating drink.

Calling this merely a “good side” would be reductive. In Nahua ritual logic, death, dangerous female sexuality, childbirth, warfare, sacrifice, and renewed life could form one transformational field.

### Recognizing a Tzitzimitl

The Magliabechiano/Tudela type has:

- a skull or fleshless face;
- visible ribs and skeletal limbs;
- large round eyes;
- disordered black hair;
- bannerlike paper ornaments;
- clawed hands and feet, sometimes containing “eye” signs;
- fanged faces at elbows and knees;
- a necklace of human hearts, hands, or viscera;
- an extended sacrificial-knife tongue or blood stream;
- a short female *huipil* or skirt;
- shells along a garment border;
- skull-and-crossbones or star patterns;
- frontal, spread-limbed posture.

**[MSR]** Not every skeletal deity is a Tzitzimitl. Mictlantecuhtli, death impersonators, sacrificed ancestors, earth deities, and skeletal calendrical powers can share individual signs. Identification requires a cluster and a relevant manuscript context.

### “Star-skirts”

**[EA/MSR]** Starry or dotted skirts, sometimes shell-bordered, connect the beings’ femininity, clothing, and celestial nature. Citlalinicue’s name makes the relation explicit: “Star-Her-Skirt.” Klein argues that divine identity could be materially concentrated in a skirt or cape rather than simply represented by it.

### Animals and substances

- **Spiders:** **[EA/MSR]** Descending spiders and threadlike cords in Borgia-group imagery have been linked with stellar descent, weaving, female powers, and the Tzitzimime. This is an iconographic association, not proof that all Tzitzimime “were spiders.”
- **Shells:** **[EA/MSR]** Border ornaments on skirts and back panels; perhaps celestial, aquatic, generative, or ancestral in force, but without a surviving explanatory gloss.
- **Flint or obsidian knives:** **[EA]** Sacrifice, cutting, and dismemberment.
- **Human hearts, hands, liver, and blood:** **[CE with pre-Conquest conventions]** Signs of sacrificial nourishment and bodily power.
- **Maguey:** **[CE narrative]** Not inherently their plant; related through their killing of Mayahuel in the maguey origin story.
- **Color, number, day, or planet:** No universally attested exclusive color, numeral, calendrical day, or single planet belongs to the whole class. Modern charts assigning one should be treated as **[MI]** unless tied to a particular named member.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical evidence

The collective has no single attested parentage. Popular genealogies making all Tzitzimime daughters of Citlalinicue and Citlaltonac are **[LS/MI]** unless attached to a specific colonial passage; they usually systematize stellar names into a Greco-Roman-style family tree.

The clearest family relation is in the *Histoyre du Mechique*:

- A singular Tzitzimitl is Mayahuel’s grandmother.
- The other Tzitzimime are presented as divine female companions or relatives, often understood as Mayahuel’s aunts.
- Mayahuel lives among them in the sky before Ehecatl removes her.

The relationship between Tzitzimime and Cihuateteo is classificatory rather than a genealogy. Cihuateteo are the dangerous spirits or divinized forms of women who died in childbirth; iconographic and functional overlap led some sources and scholars to treat them as a Tzitzimitl subgroup or cognate category. The equivalence is not uniform.

Coyolxauhqui and the Four Hundred Southerners are separately named. Stellar symbolism permits comparison, not automatic identity.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The descent at the end of a fifty-two-year cycle

**Earliest full witness:** Sahagún’s sixteenth-century Nahuatl account, principally *Florentine Codex* Book 7, chapters 9–13.

At the conjunction ending a Calendar Round, household fires were extinguished, images and domestic objects could be discarded, pregnant women were guarded or masked, and the population watched the Pleiades and the rite on Huixachtlan. Priests drilled fire in the chest cavity of a sacrificed captive. Successful ignition showed that ordered time, fire, and the Sun’s course would continue; the flame was distributed to temples and households.

If the fire failed, the night would become permanent and the Tzitzimime would descend to eat humanity. The rite did not celebrate an event known to have happened in mythical antiquity: it ritually confronted a repeatedly possible future.

- **[EA testimony/CE manuscript]** The best documented Mexica performance occurred in 1507, within living memory of some later informants.
- **[MSR]** The phrase “end of the Fifth Sun” is an interpretive joining of Calendar Round anxiety with the broader sequence of cosmic Suns.
- **[MI]** The statement that the Tzitzimime were scheduled to arrive on one precisely predicted Gregorian apocalypse date is unsupported.
- **Ritual function:** renewal of political, domestic, and cosmic order; reaffirmation of Mexica control over central fire distribution; and management of calendrical uncertainty.

### B. Eclipse descent

*Florentine Codex* Book 8 records a daytime eclipse so dark that stars appeared. People became terrified and said the Tzitzimime would descend and eat men and women. An INAH survey of eclipse traditions notes shouting, drumming, sacrifice, and fear that a nocturnal devourer was consuming the Sun. [INAH on eclipses](https://www.inah.gob.mx/especiales-inah/articulos/el-significado-de-los-eclipses-en-la-epoca-prehispanica-y-en-el-mundo-premoderno?highlight=WyJkdXJhY2lcdTAwZjNuIiwiZGUiLCJsb3MiLCJtYXlhcyJd)

Important distinctions:

- **[CE with Indigenous testimony]** The descent was feared; the cited episode does not say it occurred.
- **[RV]** Different Mesoamerican peoples responded to eclipses with different victims and rites. The sacrifice of albinos or persons with particular bodies is not automatically a Mexica Tzitzimime rule merely because a colonial author places it in a regional eclipse survey.
- **[MSR]** Stars visible in an eclipse plausibly identified the beings as stellar powers.
- **[MI]** “They possess all men during every eclipse” is an expansion found in modern summaries, not the precise Sahaguntine claim.

### C. Mayahuel, Ehecatl, and the first maguey

In the *Histoyre du Mechique*, the gods seek something that will gladden human beings. Ehecatl goes to the celestial dwelling of the virgin Mayahuel, who lives with her grandmother, a Tzitzimitl. He persuades her to descend. On earth the pair become two branches of one tree: Ehecatl’s branch and Mayahuel’s branch.

The grandmother awakens, discovers Mayahuel missing, summons the other Tzitzimime, and descends. The transformed tree splits. The grandmother recognizes Mayahuel’s branch, tears it apart, and apportions its pieces among the Tzitzimime, who devour them. After they return to the sky, Ehecatl regains divine form, collects Mayahuel’s bones, and buries them. The first maguey grows from the grave.

This myth gives the Tzitzimime a fully enacted role:

- They form a celestial female kin group.
- They enforce a boundary between their realm and earth.
- They dismember and consume a divine relative.
- Their violence is the precondition for vegetal generation.
- Ehecatl, not the Tzitzimime, performs the burial and regenerative act, but regeneration depends upon the dismemberment.

The surviving version is colonial and filtered through French translation; its precise pre-Conquest form cannot be recovered.

### D. Tamoanchan and the broken tree

**[CE/SI]** The *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* relates the expulsion of divine beings from Tamoanchan after flowers or branches were cut. Tzitzimime have been identified among the expelled beings. Because the commentary resembles the biblical loss of Eden, two interpretations must remain open:

1. It preserves a Nahua myth of forbidden cutting, divine rupture, and descent.
2. Its colonial recorder reshaped that tradition through Genesis.

It should not be retold as an uncontaminated “Aztec Adam and Eve.”

### E. Cosmic stellar women, maternal warriors, and earth monsters

**[MSR]** No single surviving narrative unites the following into one myth, but their repeated visual and ritual convergence is historically important:

- Cihuateteo who die in childbirth receive warrior status.
- Cihuacoatl and Ilamatecuhtli combine maternity, warfare, death, and nocturnal danger.
- Citlalinicue embodies a starry skirt.
- Tlaltecuhtli devours the dead while regenerating life.
- Coatlicue’s serpent skirt possesses animate force.
- Tzitzimime descend from the sky to consume but can also heal and protect.

This is a reconstructed conceptual complex, not a lost canonical epic.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No securely exclusive temple

There is no securely identified “Temple of the Tzitzimime” comparable to the Templo Mayor shrines of Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc. Nor is there an independently documented hereditary Tzitzimime priesthood. Their cultic presence was likely distributed through:

- calendar divination;
- childbirth and midwifery;
- curing;
- female deity impersonation;
- eclipse rites;
- New Fire ceremonialism;
- offerings to related goddesses;
- platforms and garments bearing Tzitzimitl signs.

### Ritual specialists

**[EA/MSR]** Diviners, midwives, curers, elderly women, priests managing calendrical renewal, and participants in female-deity festivals are the best-supported human mediators.

Borgia pages 47–48 associate male Maquiltonaleque and female Cihuateteo with divinatory specialists. Their grouping in the broader Tzitzimitl field indicates that the beings’ powers could be invoked, not merely warded off.

### Childbirth

The Tzitzimime were not straightforward “patron saints of childbirth.” The evidence indicates an ambivalent relationship:

- beings sharing their nature protected conception, labor, mothers, and children;
- women who died in childbirth entered a dangerous honored class related to the Cihuateteo;
- childbirth was metaphoric combat;
- failure in labor could produce a formidable supernatural woman;
- related powers were invoked to cure sick children.

This is precisely why colonial reduction to “devils who attack pregnant women” is inadequate.

### New Fire and sacrifice

The 1507 New Fire ceremony included the killing of a captive and drilling fire in his opened chest. The sacrifice was not described as a victim offered directly to an individual Tzitzimitl. Its successful fire averted the feared descent and re-established cosmic time.

Human sacrifice must be stated plainly, but the evidence must not be misallocated. The Tzitzimime appear as the catastrophe averted; the rite belonged to the wider state-cosmic order.

### Eclipse rites

Sahagún and regional colonial accounts describe shouting, noise, sacrifice, and protective measures during eclipses. Pregnant women and children were considered especially vulnerable in several accounts. Precisely which acts were addressed to Tzitzimime, rather than to the endangered Sun or other celestial forces, varies.

### Sacrificial imagery

The Magliabechiano and Tudela figures carry hearts, hands, viscera, blood, and a sacrificial blade. This is direct evidence that their represented power was articulated through sacrificial bodies. It does not establish a numerical scale or prove a separate Tzitzimime sacrificial calendar.

### Huei Tzompantli

Excavations between 2015 and 2017 identified part of Tenochtitlan’s Great Skull Rack at 24 República de Guatemala. The platform had construction phases corresponding to approximately 1486–1502 and 1502–1521; a sample of 179 skulls consisted mostly of young adult males with smaller numbers of females and children. [INAH, Huei Tzompantli](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907)

This materially confirms large-scale sacrificial display described in colonial sources. It is context for the organ necklace and devouring imagery of Tzitzimime, but no inscription assigns the rack exclusively to them.

### Politics and economics

The New Fire’s centralization at Huixachtlan and redistribution of sacred flame made cosmic renewal a state event. Mexica authority could present itself as the power that ensured the Sun, calendar, and domestic life continued. Klein further proposes that Tzitzimitl generative powers were appropriated into Huitzilopochtli’s state cult and rulers’ accession rites. This is a plausible political reading, but it remains a reconstruction from ritual and iconographic correspondences.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before approximately 1200: antecedents

**[MSR]** Skeletal celestial beings, earth monsters, splayed female figures, star signs, death-fertility symbolism, and dangerous maternal ancestors have deep Mesoamerican precedents. None can be called *Tzitzimitl* securely without a name or a demonstrable continuous iconographic chain.

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1521

**[EA/MSR]** The Borgia-group books and central Mexican sculpture attest the visual-religious complex from which the named beings emerge: skeletal goddesses, starry garments, Cihuateteo, divinatory patrons, sacrifice, earth generation, and dangerous celestial descent.

Under Mexica rule, the complex was incorporated into:

- the fifty-two-year New Fire rite;
- eclipse interpretation;
- childbirth ideology;
- state god and rulership symbolism;
- the Templo Mayor’s death-and-renewal program.

This was probably the period of greatest state visibility, but an independent mass cult cannot be quantified.

### 1507

The last pre-Conquest Mexica New Fire ceremony was performed. Informants later described fear that failure would allow the Tzitzimime to descend. The rite is the clearest datable historical occasion in which belief in them shaped large-scale public behavior.

### 1519–1521: invasion and fall of Tenochtitlan

Spanish warfare, epidemic disease, the siege, and the destruction or reuse of the sacred precinct ended the institutional setting of the state cult. No source records a discrete “suppression decree against the Tzitzimime”; their displacement occurred through the larger dismantling of Indigenous temples, ritual offices, and public sacrifice.

### 1520s–1570s: translation into Christian demonology

**[CE/SI]** Missionaries and colonial commentators rendered the beings as devils, demons, monsters, or fallen aerial spirits. This process:

- emphasized their devouring and nocturnal aspects;
- associated them with Satanic beings;
- increasingly masculinized them in prose;
- recoded midwives and female curers through European witchcraft stereotypes;
- preserved, often unintentionally, female dress and generative signs in Indigenous-painted images.

Klein argues that this transformation explains the contradiction between male colonial captions and female visual attire.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

The collective largely ceased to appear as an openly administered cult. Lexicography, chronicles, local oral traditions, curing language, and demonological categories preserved its names and fragments. Tezozomoc’s “air angels” show that colonial transmission was not limited to the Christian Devil model.

### Nineteenth century: philology and collection

European and Mexican scholars collected, reproduced, and interpreted the codices. The Magliabechiano image became detachable from its manuscript context and increasingly circulated as a singular monster illustration. Seler’s comparative work organized Borgia-group beings into a systematic iconographic pantheon—a foundational achievement, but also a learned reconstruction.

### Twentieth century: reconstruction of Nahua religion

Nicholson’s 1971 typology placed central Mexican supernaturals into functional religious categories. Such classifications made comparative study possible but risked turning overlapping manifestations into fixed “departments.” [Nicholson, “Religion in Pre-Hispanic Central Mexico”](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/6980/)

León-Portilla foregrounded Indigenous-language philosophical and poetic materials, correcting purely demonological readings, although his reconstructions of unified “Nahua thought” have themselves been debated. [University of Oklahoma Press](https://www.oupress.com/9780806188553/aztec-thought-and-culture/)

Klein’s 2000 study decisively reframed the Tzitzimime as originally or principally female, generative as well as destructive, and transformed by colonial gender ideology.

### Twenty-first century

Digitization of the *Florentine Codex*, renewed study of codex materiality, and major Templo Mayor discoveries have sharpened the distinction between manuscript testimony and modern composite mythology. Archaeology has confirmed the physical scale of sacrifice and the centrality of earth/death goddesses, while not producing an inscribed Tzitzimime temple.

Continuing Nahua uses of cognate terms for harmful airs, envy, dangerous old women, forest spirits, or powers of the dead demonstrate survival through transformation. They should be documented community by community rather than treated as unchanged Mexica theology.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and museums

The Magliabechiano Tzitzimitl has become the dominant modern image because it is explicit, visually dramatic, reproducible, and in the public domain. This has made one colonial manuscript figure stand for a historically variable collective.

Museum and popular descriptions often call the group “star demons.” That phrase is serviceable if understood as a shorthand for stellar beings dangerous during darkness. It becomes misleading when it implies an ontologically evil race equivalent to Christian demons.

### Occult and esoteric reception

**[Negative finding]** No substantial Renaissance Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, or early ceremonial-magic Tzitzimime tradition has been established. A 1920 issue of *The Occult Review* mentioned them as fearsome stellar demons, showing that they had entered English-language occult miscellany by the early twentieth century, but not that a stable ritual cult existed. [*The Occult Review*, July 1920](https://iapsop.com/archive/materials/occult_review/occult_review_v32_n1_jul_1920_uk_nc_nw.pdf)

Contemporary online spells, invocations, planetary tables, “sigils of Tzitzimitl,” or claims of an ancient initiatory order are **[MI]** unless they cite a verifiable Nahuatl or pictorial source. They may be valid as modern religious creations but are not ancient survivals.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Individual practitioners of Mexica reconstructionism, eclectic polytheism, witchcraft, and Indigenous cultural revival sometimes honor Tzitzimime or named related goddesses. No reliable demographic evidence establishes the scale, organization, or continuity of such worship. Claims of an unbroken secret priesthood from Tenochtitlan are undocumented.

There is an important distinction between:

- living Nahua community traditions;
- Indigenous cultural and ceremonial revitalization;
- Mexican nationalist or *Mexicayotl* reconstruction;
- non-Indigenous eclectic occult practice.

They are historically different even when they share imagery.

### Literature, comics, and games

Modern games convert the collective into standardized combat monsters:

- Atlus’s *Megami Tensei* and *Persona* games use “Tzitzimitl” as a summonable demon, filtering the being through the franchise’s universal demon taxonomy.
- *Pathfinder* presents Tzitzimime as enormous undead or outer-space monsters.
- Fantasy bestiaries frequently combine the Magliabechiano skeleton, astronomical apocalypse, and modern undead conventions.

These adaptations materially reinforce the “evil cosmic skeleton” image while usually losing childbirth, curing, female ritual clothing, and calendrical ambivalence.

### National and political uses

Mexica deities broadly serve Mexican national heritage, Indigenous reclamation, Chicano/a identity, tourism, and commercial design. Tzitzimime are less politically central than Quetzalcoatl, Huitzilopochtli, Coatlicue, or Coyolxauhqui. No well-documented large extremist movement centered on them was found. Unsupported claims of such appropriation should not be manufactured merely because the dossier template asks the question.

### Commercial image

Jewelry, tattoos, apparel, game art, and internet “Aztec zodiac” material reproduce the Magliabechiano image. Commercial pages often add unsupported fixed correspondences—Saturn, a zodiac sign, a gemstone, a single sacred color, or a modern demon hierarchy. These are **[MI]** unless independently sourced.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Are they fundamentally evil demons?

- **Colonial/pro-demon reading:** Sahagún, Molina, and the Magliabechiano commentary translate them as monsters, demons, or dead men. Their threatened anthropophagy and Mayahuel’s dismemberment are genuine source material.
- **Ambivalence/female-power reading:** Female garments, star skirts, childbirth connections, curers, child healing, and generative transformations contradict an exclusively evil interpretation.
- **Current best conclusion:** They were dangerous and potentially destructive, but not the moral opposite of benevolent gods. “Demon” is a colonial comparative term, not a neutral Indigenous category.

### Were they female?

- **Evidence for femininity:** *Huipilli* and skirts, shell-bordered and starry garments, the female group in the *Histoyre*, aged women, Cihuacoatl/Citlalinicue/Coatlicue affiliations, childbirth, midwifery, and maternal ancestors.
- **Evidence complicating exclusivity:** Colonial texts masculinize them; Omitecuhtli and possibly Huitzilopochtli can participate in the category; Borgia imagery includes paired male and female dangerous calendrical beings.
- **Conclusion:** The principal pre-Conquest forms were probably female, but the larger category was not necessarily biologically or grammatically exclusive.

### Are they literally stars?

Their descent when stars appear during an eclipse, star-marked clothing, Citlalinicue, and Borgia celestial imagery justify calling them stellar beings. Yet Tezozomoc’s rain-bearing sky supporters, their earth-goddess correspondences, and childbirth functions show that they were not merely personified astronomical points.

### Tzitzimime and Cihuateteo

Three positions appear in scholarship:

1. They are essentially the same category.
2. Cihuateteo are a subgroup of Tzitzimime.
3. They are overlapping but distinguishable classes.

The third is safest. “Woman-gods” who died in childbirth have a specific mortuary origin; not every named Tzitzimitl is said to have died in childbirth.

### Tzitzimime and earth deities

Caso, Seler, Taube, Klein, and others differ over whether particular skeletal or splayed figures represent Tlaltecuhtli, Cihuacoatl-Ilamatecuhtli, Citlalinicue, Mayahuel’s grandmother, or a generic Tzitzimitl. The disagreement arises because the imagery deliberately shares devouring mouths, clawed joints, earth posture, stars, skulls, and generative female bodies. A modern demand for one exclusive “character name” may be mismatched to the material.

The 2006 Tlaltecuhtli monolith confirms the immense political and ritual prominence of the earth deity, depicted as generator and devourer, but it does not resolve all earlier Tzitzimitl identifications. [INAH on the Tlaltecuhtli monolith](https://www.inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/tlaltecuhtli-senor-o-senora-de-la-tierra)

### The Coatlicue hypothesis

Klein argues that the famous Coatlicue statue is best understood through living-garment and Tzitzimitl symbolism rather than only as an illustration of Huitzilopochtli’s birth story. Critics may prefer the established Coatepec narrative or broader earth-deity readings. The sculpture bears no alphabetic label; interpretation depends on comparative iconography.

### Were Coyolxauhqui and the Centzon Huitznahua Tzitzimime?

Their stellar and nocturnal character makes comparison attractive, but Sahagún names them separately. The Coyolxauhqui monolith, rediscovered on 21 February 1978 at the foot of Huitzilopochtli’s Templo Mayor stair, materializes her dismemberment and the state ritual reenactment of Coatepec. It does not label her a Tzitzimitl. [INAH on the 1978 discovery](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/con-teatro-y-talleres-festejaran-el-aniversario-del-hallazgo-del-monolito-de-la-diosa-coyolxauhqui)

### Did they cause eclipses?

The sources most securely say that an eclipse created the conditions in which their descent was feared. “The Tzitzimime ate the Sun” may be a modern or generalized interpretation combining eclipse-devouring imagery with their anthropophagy. It should not be substituted silently for the actual passage.

### Did they cause or receive human sacrifice?

Their images explicitly display sacrificial body parts. Human sacrifice accompanied New Fire and eclipse responses. Nevertheless, no evidence makes all Mexica sacrifice an offering to the Tzitzimime, nor does the Huei Tzompantli prove a dedicated Tzitzimime cult. The archaeological evidence confirms the practice, not every theological attribution.

### Sources, losses, and archives

- *Florentine Codex*: Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence; now digitally accessible through the Getty-led project.
- *Codex Magliabechiano*: Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence.
- *Codex Tudela*: Museo de América, Madrid.
- *Codex Borgia*: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana.
- *Codex Borbonicus*: Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris.
- *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*: Bibliothèque nationale de France.
- *Primeros Memoriales*: principally divided between Madrid collections.
- *Codex Chimalpopoca* originals: the manuscript containing the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* and *Leyenda de los Soles* disappeared in the twentieth century; study depends on earlier copies, photographs, and editions.
- Mexica stone monuments and Templo Mayor finds: principally Museo Nacional de Antropología and Museo del Templo Mayor, Mexico City.

Spanish destruction and confiscation of Indigenous books sharply limit reconstruction. Boone notes that only a small number of divinatory manuscripts survived the colonial campaign against them. Consequently, absence from the surviving corpus is significant but not proof that a tradition never existed.
