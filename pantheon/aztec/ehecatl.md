# Ehecatl: Research Dossier

**Class:** male *teotl*—a divine power or god—personifying wind and moving air in Nahua traditions; most often the wind manifestation of Quetzalcoatl, hence **Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl**. Colonial Nahua language also permits *ehecatl* to mean ordinary “wind,” dangerous wind, breath, or an invisible pathogenic force, so not every occurrence denotes an anthropomorphic god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the identification must be divided by type of evidence. The earliest securely identifiable **pre-Hispanic images** of the conventional duck-billed or avian-masked wind god occur in Late Postclassic central Mexican manuscripts of the Borgia group, approximately thirteenth–early sixteenth century, and in Late Postclassic sculptures and round temples. The oldest securely readable written name, **Ehēcatl**, belongs to alphabetic Nahuatl records made after the Spanish invasion, principally Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex* in the mid-to-late sixteenth century. Earlier figures at Teotihuacan, Gulf Coast sites, or Chalcatzingo have sometimes been called prototypes of Ehecatl, but none bears a readable name; equating them directly with the historically documented deity is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not a secure continuous attestation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The being

**[Securely attested, Late Postclassic/early colonial]** Ehecatl is simultaneously wind itself and a named divine actor. Sahagún’s Nahuatl account says, *Ehecatl … motenehuaya ic quinotzaya Quetzalcoatl*—approximately, “The wind: thus they named/called Quetzalcoatl.” Its Spanish parallel says the people attributed wind to Quetzalcoatl “as god of the winds.” The text explains that winds come from the four directions at his command. This is stronger evidence than the modern shorthand that Ehecatl was merely a subordinate “aspect”: the sources allow wind, deity, and Quetzalcoatl to overlap without defining them through a rigid theology.

The most characteristic formulation is **Ehēcatl-Quetzalcōātl**, “Wind–Quetzalcoatl.” Depending upon context, it can name:

- wind as Quetzalcoatl’s operative form;
- Quetzalcoatl wearing the wind-god costume;
- a cult image or impersonator carrying that costume;
- the calendrical day sign Wind;
- a wider creator and culture-hero complex.

### Name and etymology

- **Classical Nahuatl:** *Ehēcatl*, conventionally written **Ehécatl**, “wind; air.” The lengthened second vowel is supplied in normalized modern orthography; sixteenth-century spelling normally omits vowel length.
- **Vocative:** *Ehēcatlé*, “O Wind.”
- **Plural/collective forms:** *ehecameh*, “winds” or wind beings, survives in several modern Nahua traditions. These beings should not automatically be identified with the singular Mexica god.
- **Quetzalcōātl:** literally “quetzal-feather serpent” or “precious/plumed serpent.” **Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl** is therefore “Wind–Feathered Serpent,” not a separate transparent lexical compound meaning “wind serpent.”
- No persuasive deeper derivation of *ehēcatl* beyond the Nahuatl noun “wind” is established. Claims that it derives from the name of a particular bird, from Maya languages, or from Old World deities are **modern speculation**.

### Documented titles and forms

- **Ehēcatl-Quetzalcōātl** — Quetzalcoatl as wind; ubiquitous in modern scholarship and firmly grounded in Sahagún’s identification.
- **Quetzalcōātl Ehēcatl** — reverse-order formulation encountered in descriptions of the deity.
- **Tlachpānqui / “road sweeper”** — descriptive function rather than a consistently isolated proper name. In Sahagún, Quetzalcoatl is the leader and sweeper of the road for the rain gods.
- **Tlālocāyōtl**, approximately “that which pertains to Tlaloc” — the mild east wind from Tlalocan in Sahagún’s discussion of winds; it is not simply another personal name for Ehecatl.
- **Mictlampaehecatl**, north wind; directional wind terminology in the *Florentine Codex*.
- **Nauhehecatl**, “Four Wind” or “Wind of the Four Directions,” appears in colonial glosses to the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* and *Vaticanus A* for a figure combining Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl and Tlaloc attributes. **[Colonial learned/glossarial classification]**
- **Chicnahui Ehecatl / 9 Wind** — especially important in Mixtec and broader central Mexican creator traditions. The Mixtec culture hero 9 Wind is related iconographically and conceptually to Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl but should not be collapsed into the Mexica god without qualification. **[Regional correspondence]**
- **Yohualli Ehecatl**, “Night and Wind,” is a Nahua metaphor for an invisible, impalpable divine presence. It is also a modern alternate name for the Codex Borgia. It is not securely an exclusive epithet of Ehecatl.
- **Ehecatotontin**, “little winds,” collective wind beings. **[Regional/lexical category]**
- **Huitztlampaehecatl**, southern wind, and other directional winds occur in early colonial classifications. Whether every one was an independently worshipped “god” is not established.

### Identifications and comparisons

- **Quetzalcoatl:** secure indigenous identification, not Spanish *interpretatio*. Its precise history before the Late Postclassic remains disputed.
- **Tlaloc:** complementary, not equivalent. Wind precedes and clears the way for rain. Some manuscript figures combine their attributes, but this is not evidence that they were universally one god.
- **Tezcatlipoca:** *ehecatl*, in the general sense “invisible wind,” can describe Tezcatlipoca’s unseen agency; that does not make the named wind god identical with him.
- **Mixtec 9 Wind/Q Chi:** regional analogue and, in some manuscripts, a Quetzalcoatl-like creator. **[Regional syncretism/correspondence]**
- **Otomí Edähi/Edahi:** often presented as an Otomí wind-god counterpart. Direct identity is a modern comparativist shorthand unless demonstrated for a particular bilingual community or colonial source.
- No ancient equation with Aeolus, Hermes, Satan, an angel, or an Old World god is documented. Such equations are colonial polemic or modern invention.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The problem of “first evidence”

Ehecatl is difficult to date because wind leaves no archaeological signature and because attributes later associated with him—avian mouths, shells, feathered serpents, circular buildings—are not names. Three evidentiary levels must remain separate:

1. **Possible antecedents:** Preclassic and Classic-period avian-serpent or masked beings and circular temples. Their interpretation as Ehecatl is retrospective.
2. **Secure iconographic identity:** Late Postclassic figures with the buccal mask, conical cap, curved ear ornaments, and cut-shell wind jewel, especially in the Borgia group.
3. **Secure named descriptions:** sixteenth-century alphabetic Nahuatl and Spanish documents.

The great Feathered Serpent Pyramid at Teotihuacan, constructed about the third century CE, is therefore evidence for a feathered-serpent cult, not secure evidence for the named god Ehecatl. Its approximately 200 dedicatory sacrificial burials cannot responsibly be assigned to Ehecatl.

### Principal manuscripts and texts

#### Codex Borgia

**[Securely attested pre-Hispanic or very early contact-period pictorial corpus]** A screenfold ritual-divinatory manuscript, probably from central or southern Puebla–Tlaxcala or a neighboring region, conventionally dated to the Late Postclassic. It is now Vatican Library Borg. Mess. 1.

Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl appears repeatedly with the wind mask and shell pectoral. Page 56 divides calendrical influence between life-giving Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl and death-associated Mictlantecuhtli. Pages 29–46 contain a complex ritual-cosmological sequence in which Quetzalcoatl is prominent.

Interpretation is contested:

- Eduard Seler read the central sequence as a divine narrative.
- Karl Anton Nowotny emphasized ritual and calendrical liturgy.
- Elizabeth Hill Boone interprets it as structured cycles of time, place, and ritual meaning.
- Anders, Jansen, and Reyes García proposed an oracular/liturgical reading.
- Astronomical and initiatory interpretations have also been advanced.

The manuscript contains no alphabetic captions resolving these disputes. Calling every masked protagonist “Ehecatl” or treating pages 29–46 as an illustrated version of a particular colonial prose myth exceeds the evidence.

#### Codices Laud, Fejérváry-Mayer, Cospi, and Vaticanus B

**[Secure Late Postclassic regional corpus]** These Borgia-group divinatory books share the 260-day count and closely related deity costumes. They demonstrate that the wind-god complex was not an invention of Spanish-period chroniclers and was wider than Tenochtitlan.

#### Codex Borbonicus

**[Late pre-Hispanic/early colonial; dating disputed]** A Nahua ritual-calendar manuscript on amate paper, probably from the Basin of Mexico. Plate 22 presents Quetzalcoatl in wind-god regalia during the 52-year cycle material; other pages associate Quetzalcoatl with trecenas and ritual patronage. Whether the book was painted immediately before or shortly after conquest remains disputed, but its pictorial system is indigenous.

#### Codex Mendoza

**[Early colonial, c. 1541–1553]** Created in Mexico City for a colonial audience. It is not chiefly a mythological book, but it preserves wind signs and place glyphs such as *Ecatl Icuapechco*. It supplies evidence for the name’s calendrical and toponymic use rather than a connected Ehecatl myth.

#### Codex Magliabechiano group

**[Early colonial]** This group illustrates deity costumes, feast customs, day signs, and ritual objects. Quetzalcoatl’s wind costume and the *ehecacozcatl* belong to the diagnostic ensemble. Because surviving manuscripts are colonial copies, individual details must not automatically be projected unchanged into deep antiquity.

#### Codex Telleriano-Remensis and Vaticanus A

**[Mid-sixteenth-century pictorial books with European-language glosses]** These preserve the succession of cosmic Suns, calendrical signs, and composite deity imagery. A glossed figure named *Nauhehecatl* combines wind and rain attributes, showing colonial-era systematization of directional and calendrical powers.

#### Primeros Memoriales

**[Early colonial ethnographic testimony, c. 1558–1561]** Compiled under Bernardino de Sahagún at Tepepulco from Nahua informants, with images and Nahuatl text. It precedes the completed *Florentine Codex* and is an indispensable witness to deity costumes and ritual offices. Differences from the later codex show editorial development rather than a single frozen “Aztec Bible.”

#### Florentine Codex

**[Early colonial Nahua testimony under Franciscan supervision, completed 1577]** Twelve books in Nahuatl and Spanish, produced by Sahagún and Indigenous scholars and painters. Anderson and Dibble’s edition remains the standard full English translation; the Getty digital edition now permits comparison of image, Nahuatl, Spanish, and newer translations.

Relevant material includes:

- Book 1: Quetzalcoatl’s divine identity and insignia.
- Book 2: festivals and sacrifice; *Atl Cahualo/Quauitl Ehua* is variously said to honor the Tlalocs, Chalchiuhtlicue, or Quetzalcoatl, and children were killed for rain.
- Book 7: creation of Sun and Moon at Teotihuacan; Ehecatl moves the stationary Sun and kills the gods when their deaths are required.
- Book 11: winds from the four directions, their properties, and the identification of wind with Quetzalcoatl.

The “sweeper of the road” passage says Quetzalcoatl-Ehecatl leads and clears the way for rain-bearing gods. This is the primary source behind the familiar statement that he “swept the road for Tlaloc.”

#### Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme, Diego Durán

**[Early colonial, c. 1570s–1581]** Durán describes Quetzalcoatl’s cult, circular temples, priesthood, Cholula, and sacrificial customs. His material mixes Indigenous testimony, pictorial sources, Christian interpretation, and authorial reconstruction. His insistence on analogies to Christian history is evidence for colonial interpretation, not pre-Hispanic doctrine.

#### Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas

**[Colonial compilation, c. 1530s]** Preserves creation narratives apparently derived from Indigenous pictorial books and testimony. It is important for Quetzalcoatl’s cosmogonic relations but does not consistently distinguish his wind form.

#### Histoyre du Mechique

**[Colonial, French manuscript preserving mid-sixteenth-century material]** Contains an explicit myth in which Ehecatl seeks a source of joy for humanity, encounters Mayahuel, and brings about the origin of maguey. Its peculiar transmission—Indigenous material mediated through Spanish and surviving in French—requires caution. It is nevertheless the principal source for the Ehecatl–Mayahuel narrative.

#### Leyenda de los Soles

**[Nahuatl text copied in 1558 from earlier pictorial/oral materials]** Formerly bound in the lost manuscript conventionally called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. It recounts the prior Suns, Quetzalcoatl’s descent to Mictlan for the bones, their accidental breakage, their grinding by Quilaztli/Cihuacoatl, and the gods’ blood sacrifice to form present humanity.

A crucial correction: in this text it is **Quetzalcoatl**, not explicitly “Ehecatl,” who fetches the bones. Identifying the actor specifically as wind-god Ehecatl is reasonable within the wider Quetzalcoatl complex but remains an interpretive specification. Likewise, popular versions saying that he “blew breath into the bones” conflate separate motifs: the *Leyenda* says the bones were ground and quickened by divine blood; it does not plainly say Ehecatl inflated intact skeletons with breath.

#### Anales de Cuauhtitlan

**[Nahuatl annalistic compilation, sixteenth century]** Also transmitted through the former *Codex Chimalpopoca*. It presents Quetzalcoatl of Tollan, his conflict with deceivers including Tezcatlipoca’s agents, his departure, death/immolation, and transformation associated with the Morning Star. This priest-ruler Quetzalcoatl is related to but not simply identical with every episode of the wind god.

#### Cantares Mexicanos

**[Nahuatl song manuscript, principally compiled c. 1550s–1580s]** Divine titles, wind, breath, flowers, precious birds, creator language, and Quetzalcoatl imagery appear in ritual-poetic settings. Because the songs are multivocal and some are colonial Christian compositions, isolated metaphors cannot safely be converted into a systematic theology of Ehecatl.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and powers

**[Securely attested]**

- Wind and moving air.
- The four directional winds.
- Clearing the way for rain.
- Motion: most dramatically, forcing the newly created Sun into movement.
- Breath and animation by broader symbolic association.
- Creation and ordering of the present world through the Quetzalcoatl complex.
- Calendrical time: *Ehecatl* is the second of the twenty day signs.
- Destructive wind: the second cosmic age, **Nahui Ehecatl**, “4 Wind,” ends in catastrophic wind; survivors become monkeys in several colonial sources.

Wind was not uniformly benevolent. Sahagún distinguishes mild and dangerous winds; *ehecatl* could also denote an invisible agency causing illness. The modern reduction of Ehecatl to a gentle “breeze god” suppresses this range.

### Diagnostic costume

**[Secure Late Postclassic iconography]**

- **Buccal mask:** a projecting red or dark mouth covering, often described as duck-billed or avian. “Duck bill” is convenient but zoologically overconfident; “avian buccal mask” is safer.
- **Conical cap:** frequently painted dark or patterned.
- **Ehecacozcatl:** “wind jewel,” a pectoral made in the form of a transversely cut conch shell. Its spiral or star-like interior evokes breath, rotation, shell sound, and wind.
- **Curved shell ear ornaments.**
- **Red-and-black facial areas**, varying by manuscript.
- **Round or hooked mouth/nose projection**, the principal recognition feature.
- **Conch trumpet:** associated with wind and Quetzalcoatl, though not every scene shows Ehecatl personally blowing one.

Monkeys appear in the 4 Wind destruction myth and can wear wind-god insignia, but “monkey” is not his single fixed animal form. Ducks and aquatic birds are often inferred from the mask, yet the sources do not supply a canonical statement that the mask is literally a duck’s beak.

### Round temples

**[Securely attested architecture; interpretation partly colonial]** Durán and other early writers state that temples of Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl were round. Archaeology confirms that circular or apsidal temples were widespread in Postclassic central Mexico. The conventional explanation is that a round building offers no corner obstructing the wind. Eduardo Matos Moctezuma has also suggested that the rounded body and serpent-mouth doorway materialize the coiled Feathered Serpent. These explanations are compatible but neither should be mistaken for an engineering test conducted by ancient builders.

Important examples include:

- **Calixtlahuaca, Monument 3:** a circular, stepped temple conventionally assigned to Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl. Its visible fabric is principally Late Postclassic Matlatzinca/Aztec-period. A distinctive wind-god sculpture supports the identification.
- **Pino Suárez Metro temple, Mexico City:** found during Metro construction in 1969; a round shrine on a rectangular platform. An associated monkey-form wind-god sculpture and offerings support its attribution. Its surviving phases are Late Postclassic.
- **Mexico-Tenochtitlan, Guatemala Street:** rear portion discovered beginning in 2010 and publicly presented in 2017. The visible stage belongs to the reign of Ahuizotl, 1486–1502. It stood west of and faced the Templo Mayor/Tlaloc side, consistent with the wind-before-rain relationship.
- **Mexico-Tlatelolco, Temple R:** circular temple with offerings, incense burners, and human remains assigned to Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl.
- **Texcoco:** a circular temple and offering complex interpreted through the human-creation myth.
- Circular buildings elsewhere in Mesoamerica are not automatically Ehecatl temples. Function must be established by context, sculpture, offering, or historical testimony.

### Calendrical and cosmic symbols

- **Ehecatl/Wind:** second day sign of the *tonalpohualli*.
- **4 Ehecatl:** name of a destroyed cosmic Sun.
- **9 Ehecatl:** prominent creator name/date in regional traditions.
- **Four directions:** the wind comes from four quarters; later charts systematize distinct directional winds.
- No single sacred color, planet, or number belongs exclusively and universally to Ehecatl. Venus associations derive chiefly from Quetzalcoatl/Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli and must not be transferred indiscriminately to every wind-god image.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to divine beings

### Genealogical instability

Ehecatl generally has no independent, consistently attested birth genealogy. Genealogies belong to the wider Quetzalcoatl complex and differ by text:

- Some colonial cosmogonies make Quetzalcoatl one of the creator sons of the primordial pair, alongside Tezcatlipoca and other gods.
- The Tollan ruler Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl is made the son of Mixcoatl and Chimalma in some historical-mythic traditions.
- *Histoyre du Mechique* supplies distinct divine family arrangements and should not be treated as universal.
- Mayahuel is Ehecatl’s beloved or companion in one myth, not securely his permanent spouse.
- No securely attested children of Ehecatl as an independently defined god are known.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The wind clears the road for rain

**[Securely attested early-colonial explanation]** Sahagún explains that before the rains there are strong winds and dust. The Nahuas therefore said Quetzalcoatl-Ehecatl was the guide and road sweeper of the rain gods. The myth is an observation-based etiological sequence:

1. wind rises;
2. dust and obstructions are swept away;
3. rain-bearing deities arrive;
4. agricultural fertility follows.

The temple discovered west of the Templo Mayor faced toward Tlaloc’s northern shrine. This archaeological arrangement makes the wind–rain complement materially visible, although archaeology alone cannot prove the precise verbal myth.

### Creation of the Fifth Sun

**[Secure early-colonial narrative, probably derived from pre-Hispanic tradition]** In *Florentine Codex* Book 7, the gods assemble at Teotihuacan. Wealthy Tecuciztecatl and humble, diseased Nanahuatzin perform penance. Nanahuatzin leaps into the sacred fire; Tecuciztecatl follows. They emerge as Sun and Moon, but both stand motionless.

The Sun demands the death of the gods. The gods consent or are killed; Ehecatl performs the sacrificial execution. Xolotl refuses, fleeing through transformations before he too is caught. Even after the deaths the Sun does not move. Ehecatl then blows with extraordinary force, making the Sun begin its path; the Moon follows.

Important variants and cautions:

- Other creation sources arrange the sacrifices and divine actors differently.
- Some versions emphasize that divine blood/death creates cosmic debt later repaid by human offerings.
- “Ehecatl created the Sun” is too broad: Nanahuatzin becomes it, the divine assembly establishes the rite, and Ehecatl makes it move.
- The episode makes wind an agent of motion, not merely weather.

### Recovery of the bones and creation of humanity

**[Secure for Quetzalcoatl; wind/breath expansion is later harmonization]** In the *Leyenda de los Soles*, the gods ask who will obtain the precious bones from Mictlan. Quetzalcoatl descends and asks Mictlantecuhtli for them. The lord of death orders him to sound a conch without holes. Quetzalcoatl has worms perforate it and bees make it sound. He declares that he will leave the bones but secretly plans to take them.

Mictlantecuhtli causes a pit to be dug. Quetzalcoatl falls; quail startle him and peck the bones. He revives, gathers the now-broken bones, and escapes. Because the bones were broken, human beings differ in size. Quilaztli/Cihuacoatl grinds them in a precious vessel. Quetzalcoatl bleeds his penis over the bone meal; other gods also offer blood, and humanity is formed.

The requested “breath of life blown into dead bones” must therefore be labeled carefully:

- The source securely associates Quetzalcoatl with recovering dead bones and making living humans.
- The named creative medium in the *Leyenda* is **blood upon ground bone**, not an explicit breath blown into reassembled skeletons.
- Ehecatl’s identity as wind/breath makes the breath interpretation symbolically attractive.
- Modern children’s books, spiritual retellings, and encyclopedic summaries often turn that symbolism into a literal scene. Unless another primary source is cited, this is **modern harmonization**, not the wording of the *Leyenda de los Soles*.

### Ehecatl and Mayahuel: origin of maguey

**[Colonial attestation; no surviving pre-Hispanic written version]** In *Histoyre du Mechique*, the gods or humanity require something that will bring happiness. Ehecatl remembers or is directed toward Mayahuel, a maiden kept in heaven by her formidable grandmother, a Tzitzimitl.

Ehecatl goes to Mayahuel and brings her to earth. They unite or hide as two branches of one tree. The grandmother and accompanying Tzitzimime descend, recognize Mayahuel’s branch, break it, and consume her. Ehecatl’s branch remains unharmed. When the attackers leave, he resumes his form, collects Mayahuel’s bones or fragments, and buries them. The first maguey grows from them. Its sap eventually supplies the basis of *octli*, pulque.

The story makes wind an intermediary between heaven and earth and connects vegetal fertility with death and regeneration. Popular versions regularly add marriage, romantic dialogue, tequila, or a complete genealogy of the Four Hundred Rabbits. Tequila is a post-conquest distilled product and cannot be part of the ancient etiological myth.

### The Sun of 4 Wind

**[Early-colonial canonical cosmogony with variants]** In the sequence of previous worlds, the age named Nahui Ehecatl ends in devastating wind. Houses, trees, and people are swept away; surviving humans become monkeys in prominent versions. The order of the Suns and the presiding gods differs among the Sun Stone, *Leyenda de los Soles*, *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, and colonial annotated codices.

The **Sun Stone**, carved under Mexica rule, probably late fifteenth century, includes the 4 Wind sign among the four previous eras around the central face. Identification of that central face as Tonatiuh, the earth monster, or a more composite solar-earth power remains debated. The monument does not itself narrate the full prose story.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult centers

#### Cholula

Quetzalcoatl was the preeminent divine patron of Cholula and a source of priestly and mercantile authority. Colonial accounts describe a major cult and regional pilgrimage. How sharply Cholulans distinguished Feathered Serpent, wind god, and a deified priestly founder cannot always be recovered. Durán’s account is important but colored by Christian comparison.

#### Mexico-Tenochtitlan

The circular Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl temple stood west of the Templo Mayor and faced the rain-god side. Excavations at República de Guatemala 16 recovered part of its rounded rear, multiple construction stages, and an Ahuizotl-period phase. The adjoining ballcourt and nearby skull-display installations belonged to the broader sacred precinct.

#### Tlatelolco

Temple R and its offerings give unusually direct evidence:

- incense burners and ritual ceramics;
- animal offerings;
- adults and numerous children or infants;
- a decapitated young man with skull-shaped whistles;
- activity extending into the crisis of conquest in some deposits.

Isotope study indicates that most analyzed victims from Temple R grew up in the Basin of Mexico rather than being uniformly distant war captives.

#### Calixtlahuaca

Monument 3 is the best-known surviving circular wind-god temple outside Mexico City. Its assignment rests on architectural form and Ehecatl imagery, but the popular presentation of the building as a purely “Aztec” foundation understates the Matlatzinca history of the site and later imperial incorporation.

#### Pino Suárez

The shrine discovered in 1969 remains visible inside the Metro. Offerings included a monkey-form sculpture with a wind mask. It is a small urban sanctuary, not evidence that every circular platform was monumental or state-controlled.

#### Texcoco

A Late Postclassic offering in the circular temple included materials interpreted as an allegory of the human-creation episode. The association shows how myth could be deposited materially, but the precise intended narrative remains an archaeological interpretation.

### Priesthood

“Quetzalcoatl” also functioned as a high-priestly title, notably in offices conventionally rendered **Quetzalcoatl Tlaloc Tlamacazqui** and **Quetzalcoatl Totec Tlamacazqui**. These titles do not mean that the officeholder was simply “the priest of Ehecatl.” They show Quetzalcoatl’s authority as a priestly paradigm within state ritual.

Priests and deity impersonators wore divine regalia, fasted, offered incense and blood, and maintained temple fires. Sources often blur cult statue, costumed impersonator, office, and god because ritual embodiment was central to Nahua practice.

### Offerings and sacrifice

**[Textually and archaeologically attested]** Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl’s cult was not bloodless.

- *Florentine Codex* Book 2 reports child sacrifice during the rain-making festival *Atl Cahualo*, whose dedication varied among Tlalocs, Chalchiuhtlicue, and Quetzalcoatl according to different informants.
- Tlatelolco Temple R contained sacrificed adults and children associated archaeologically with Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl.
- A drought-related offering dated around the famine of 1454–1458 has been interpreted as an appeal to the wind god’s power to bring rain.
- Human bone, sacrificial knives, incense, figurines, shell insignia, animals, food, and ceramics occurred in cult deposits.

The **Huey Tzompantli** discoveries of 2015–2017 confirm large-scale skull display in Tenochtitlan. The skull towers stood near the Ehecatl temple, but proximity does not establish that every displayed victim was sacrificed specifically to Ehecatl. The installation belonged to the entire state ceremonial precinct, with particularly strong connections to warfare and Huitzilopochtli.

Likewise:

- the Coyolxauhqui Stone, found in 1978, materially anchored Huitzilopochtli’s dismemberment myth at the Templo Mayor;
- the great Tlaltecuhtli monolith, found in 2006, belonged to an elite mortuary and earth-goddess complex;
- neither is a direct image or primary cult object of Ehecatl.

Their relevance is contextual: they demonstrate the mythically organized ritual landscape in which the nearby wind temple functioned.

### Sound and “death whistles”

Two skull-shaped whistles were found with a decapitated young man at Tlatelolco. Their archaeological context links sound, death, sacrifice, and an Ehecatl temple. Beyond that:

- the term **“Aztec death whistle”** is modern;
- no sixteenth-century text says armies blew such whistles to terrify enemies;
- reconstructed whistles vary substantially in sound;
- an association with the howl of wind is plausible but not proved;
- viral claims that thousands were sounded during mass sacrifice are **modern invention**.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Possible antecedents: before c. 900 CE

Feathered-serpent imagery extends back far earlier than the Mexica. Teotihuacan’s Feathered Serpent Pyramid and earlier avian-serpent images establish deep Mesoamerican histories of serpent, bird, rain, rulership, and sacrifice. They do not establish an unchanged god named Ehecatl. Claims of a continuous single deity over two millennia are **modern reconstruction**.

### Epiclassic and Early Postclassic, c. 650–1200

Feathered Serpent imagery became prominent at sites such as Xochicalco, Cacaxtla, Tula, and Chichen Itza. Traditions of Tollan and the priest-ruler Quetzalcoatl later drew this era into origin history. The exact point at which the buccal-masked wind figure fused with Feathered Serpent remains unknown.

### Late Postclassic, c. 1200–1521

This is the securely recoverable florescence of Ehecatl:

- Borgia-group manuscripts display standardized wind-god insignia.
- Circular temples spread across central Mexico.
- Mexica, Tlatelolca, Texcocan, Cholulan, Matlatzinca, Mixtec, and neighboring systems used related but non-identical wind powers.
- Tenochtitlan monumentalized the wind–rain sequence by facing Ehecatl’s temple toward Tlaloc.
- The day sign Wind, 4 Wind cosmic era, and 9 Wind creator complex linked the deity to divination and world order.
- State and local cults included incense, bloodletting, animal offerings, and human sacrifice.

### Conquest and suppression, 1519–sixteenth century

The siege and fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 ended the state institutions that maintained the great temples. Spanish and Indigenous Christian forces demolished or built over shrines, prohibited sacrifice, confiscated and burned sacred objects, and punished continued “idolatry.” Churches and colonial buildings occupied sacred precincts.

The cult did not vanish instantaneously. Nahua intellectuals preserved extensive knowledge in pictorial manuscripts, alphabetic histories, songs, and Sahagún’s project—although always under colonial conditions. Household rites, divination, healing, and wind beliefs could survive without a monumental priesthood. No evidence establishes an unbroken, publicly organized priesthood of Ehecatl after the sixteenth century.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

*Ehecatl* continued as a Nahuatl word for wind and as part of place names such as Ecatepec/Ehecatepec, “hill of Ehecatl.” Colonial dictionaries documented associations between wind, invisibility, and sickness. Antiquarians increasingly treated the god as an object of historical study.

The recovery and publication of codices transformed interpretation. Eduard Seler’s late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century comparative iconography established many identifications still used, although later scholars have revised his strongly astral and narrative readings.

### Twentieth-century archaeology and scholarship

Major developments included:

- excavation and reconstruction of Calixtlahuaca;
- discovery of the Pino Suárez shrine in 1969;
- discovery of the Coyolxauhqui Stone in 1978 and creation of the Templo Mayor Project;
- systematic excavation at Tlatelolco;
- studies by Henry B. Nicholson, Miguel León-Portilla, Michel Graulich, Elizabeth Hill Boone, Richard Townsend, Davíd Carrasco, Guilhem Olivier, Eduardo Matos Moctezuma, and Leonardo López Luján;
- new analyses of iconographic pigments, shell insignia, sacrifice, and urban orientation.

Nicholson’s 1971 typology grouped central Mexican deities into “cult themes” and treated Quetzalcoatl within a wider complex. It remains influential, but its tidy categories can obscure local overlap and historically fluid identities.

### Twenty-first century

Discoveries at the Guatemala Street temple, bioarchaeological study of Tlatelolco victims, digital publication of the *Florentine Codex*, and renewed analysis of Borgia-group manuscripts have made the evidence more accessible and more complex.

Modern Nahua and Mexican cultural uses range from language revitalization and heritage ceremonies to explicitly reconstructionist or neopagan devotion. There is no reliable census of Ehecatl worshippers, and continuity claims differ. A modern practitioner’s belief may be sincere without proving uninterrupted transmission of a specific pre-Hispanic rite.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### National and cultural symbolism

Ehecatl appears in Mexican museum education, public sculpture, Metro interpretation, municipal emblems, dance, and Indigenous cultural revival. Ecatepec’s civic symbolism often depicts the wind god because of the place name.

These uses can recover suppressed heritage, construct national identity, or commercialize it; they are not a single movement.

### Literature and visual culture

Modern retellings typically emphasize:

- romance with Mayahuel;
- a benevolent wind bringing rain;
- breath reviving the ancestral bones;
- the duck-billed mask;
- circular temples;
- a binary opposition between creative Ehecatl and destructive death gods.

The first, second, fourth, and fifth have primary-source bases. The literal breath-resurrection scene is usually a modern synthesis. Modern art frequently gives Ehecatl feathers, wings, or a fully avian head not required by surviving codices.

### Games and popular media

Ehecatl or Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl appears in mythology games, role-playing supplements, fantasy fiction, comics, and online encyclopedias. Games commonly turn a fluid divine identity into a discrete “wind unit” with combat statistics. This is a modern genre convention, not Nahua classification.

### Esoteric and neopagan currents

Unlike Egyptian and Greco-Roman gods, Ehecatl did not acquire a substantial documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoires. Assertions that Golden Dawn magicians transmitted an ancient Ehecatl rite are unsupported.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century occult and New Age material sometimes interprets Ehecatl as:

- elemental Air;
- the eastern quarter;
- universal prana or life breath;
- a kundalini force;
- an Atlantean teacher;
- an incarnation of Christ, Hermes, or an extraterrestrial intelligence.

These are **modern syncretisms or inventions** unless explicitly presented merely as contemporary theology.

Reconstructionist practitioners use codices, Nahuatl prayers, copal, conch sound, fasting, and directional invocation. Their source-based character varies. No surviving pre-Hispanic manual supplies a complete modern Ehecatl liturgy.

### Political appropriation

Ehecatl can appear in Chicano, Mexicayotl, pan-Indigenous, nationalist, or decolonial symbolism. Such use is historically modern. There is no evidence that Ehecatl himself has become a stable emblem of a major extremist organization, though broader “Aztec” symbolism has occasionally been appropriated by racial-nationalist ideologues. Specific claims require case-by-case documentation.

### Commercial reinvention: the death whistle

The strongest commercial transformation is the “Aztec death whistle.” Replicas, horror soundtracks, videos, and games often claim it was a standardized psychological weapon. Archaeology establishes skull whistles in ritual/sacrificial contexts; warfare claims remain unsubstantiated. The modern name and mass-market object postdate the excavations and experimental reproductions of the late twentieth century.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Ehecatl a separate god or merely an aspect?

The sources do not impose modern ontological categories. Sahagún can identify wind as Quetzalcoatl while manuscripts show a distinctive costume and temples have a specialized form. It is therefore defensible to call Ehecatl:

- a god;
- the divine wind;
- Quetzalcoatl’s wind manifestation;
- a ritual costume/persona.

It is misleading to insist that only one formulation is “the Aztec belief.”

### How old is the identity?

The Feathered Serpent is much older than the securely identified Ehecatl costume. Scholars have proposed Preclassic or Classic antecedents, but a chronological gap and absence of written names prevent certainty. The safe conclusion is:

- deep antecedents for avian-serpent, shell, breath, rain, and wind symbolism;
- secure Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl complex in the Late Postclassic;
- uncertain date and mechanism of fusion.

### What is the mask?

Older literature routinely calls it a duck-bill mask. Current iconographic analysis often prefers “avian buccal mask,” because its forms vary and may be a supernatural blowing apparatus rather than a naturalistic bird beak. The mask’s wind function is secure; exact species is not.

### Why are the temples round?

The colonial “so wind could pass without obstruction” explanation is widespread. The coiled-serpent/serpent-mouth interpretation emphasizes Quetzalcoatl’s form. Roundness also had broader Mesoamerican architectural histories. None of these explanations licenses identifying all circular structures as wind shrines.

### Did Ehecatl breathe life into the bones?

This is a major source-critical dispute generated largely by retelling:

- *Leyenda de los Soles*: Quetzalcoatl retrieves bones; Quilaztli grinds them; divine blood makes humanity.
- Ehecatl symbolism: wind and breath are life-associated.
- Modern synthesis: Ehecatl blows breath into the bones.

The modern image is thematically coherent but not the explicit action in the best-known primary text.

### Was Quetzalcoatl a peaceful opponent of sacrifice?

The theory that Quetzalcoatl represented a wholly pacifist, anti-sacrificial religion opposed to “militaristic Aztec” gods is not supported by the full evidence. Ehecatl sacrifices gods in the Fifth Sun narrative; human sacrifice occurred at a Tlatelolco wind temple; Quetzalcoatl’s creation of humans depends on autosacrificial blood. Some sources associate the Tollan ruler with penance and restrictions on certain offerings, but that does not establish an ahistorical bloodless cult.

### How should the Borgia sequence be read?

Seler’s narrative-astral reading, Nowotny’s ritual approach, Boone’s temporal-spatial interpretation, and subsequent astronomical or initiatory readings disagree. Since the manuscript has no explanatory alphabetic text, confident captions that transform the entire sequence into a single Ehecatl biography are unsound.

### Human sacrifice: text versus archaeology

Colonial reports were once dismissed wholesale as Spanish propaganda or accepted without criticism. Archaeology now independently verifies killing, decapitation, heart extraction in some contexts, dismemberment, skull racks, and sacrificial burial. It does not verify every colonial number or rhetorical claim.

For Ehecatl specifically, Tlatelolco provides direct cult-context evidence. The Huey Tzompantli confirms state sacrifice nearby but cannot be assigned wholesale to him.

### State of the evidence

Major repositories include:

- Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence: *Florentine Codex*.
- Vatican Apostolic Library: Codex Borgia and Vaticanus manuscripts.
- Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris: Codex Borbonicus.
- Bodleian Libraries, Oxford: Codex Mendoza.
- Bibliothèque nationale de France: Telleriano-Remensis and other Mexican manuscripts.
- Museo Nacional de Antropología and Museo del Templo Mayor, Mexico City: sculptures, offerings, and excavated materials.
- Site museums and archaeological zones at Calixtlahuaca and Tlatelolco.
- Pino Suárez Metro: in-situ circular shrine.

The original manuscript once called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*, containing the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* and *Leyenda de los Soles*, disappeared from Mexico’s National Museum in the mid-twentieth century. Photographs, copies, and editions preserve its text, but the loss prevents renewed physical examination.
