# Chalchiuhtlicue: Research Dossier

**Class:** female *teōtl*—conventionally “goddess”—in Mexica and related central-Mexican traditions; a personification, owner, and potentially destructive controller of terrestrial water.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Chalchiuhtlicue is securely identifiable in late Postclassic central-Mexican ritual-divinatory manuscripts and Mexica sculpture of approximately the fifteenth to early sixteenth centuries. The preconquest or conquest-period **Codex Borgia** depicts her repeatedly (folios 11, 14, 20, 65), while Mexica stone figures identifiable through her costume survive from roughly 1325–1521. No inscription securely names her before the Postclassic. Identifying the much earlier, anonymous Teotihuacan “Great Goddess” or the colossal figure from the Pyramid of the Moon as Chalchiuhtlicue is a modern scholarly hypothesis, not an ancient attestation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Securely attested late-Postclassic/early-colonial tradition]** Chalchiuhtlicue governs rivers, springs, lakes, canals, and the sea. Bernardino de Sahagún’s Nahua informants describe her as having power to drown travelers, overturn boats, and create storms and whirlpools; they also call her sister of the rain gods, the Tlaloque. In ritual she is the divine presence in water used to wash newborn children. In cosmogony she rules the age **4-Water**, destroyed by a universal flood. Her identity consequently embraces water as sustenance, cultivated fertility, preciousness, purification, danger, and cosmic dissolution.

### Name and orthography

The normalized Classical Nahuatl name is:

- **Chālchiuhtlicuē** or **Chalchiuhtlicue**: *chālchihui(tl)*, “precious greenstone, jade,” plus *ī-cuē* or the incorporated form *-tlicuē*, “her skirt”; conventionally “She of the Jade Skirt,” “Jade Is Her Skirt,” or “She Whose Skirt Is of Precious Greenstone.”
- Colonial spellings include **Chalchiutlicue**, **Chalchihuitlicue**, **Chalcihuitlicue**, and **Chalchihcueye**.
- **Chalchiuhtlatonac / Chalchiuh-tlatonac**, invoked in the newborn-bathing discourse, is usually understood as a radiant or shining manifestation of precious water: approximately “Precious-One-Who-Shines.” It should not automatically be made a separate goddess.
- **Chalchiuhtli**, literally “precious greenstone,” occurs as an abbreviated divine address in the Florentine Codex’s Nahuatl text.

**[Modern linguistic caution]** “Jade” is convenient but narrower than *chalchihuitl*, which could denote prized greenstones generally. The name is a predicative divine title, not evidence that worshippers imagined a literal mineral garment. In manuscripts, however, a jade-bead sign attached to a skirt can make the name visually legible: a pictorial name-glyph.

### Titles and descriptions

- **“Goddess of water”**—Sahagún’s Spanish heading; a colonial descriptive classification.
- **“Our mother”**—the midwife’s address to the water in Florentine Codex Book VI.
- **“Elder sister of the gods” / “mother of the gods”**—language recorded in the ceremonial speeches of Book VI; it expresses rank and generative precedence rather than a complete genealogy.
- **Owner or embodiment of river and sea water**—Sahagún, Book I.
- **Consort/wife of Tlaloc**—reported in several colonial compilations.
- **Sister of the Tlaloque**—the explicit version in Sahagún, Book I.

These last two relationships must not be silently harmonized. “Wife of Tlaloc” and “sister of the rain gods” belong to overlapping but not identical systems of divine classification.

### Interpretive identifications

Sahagún calls her “another Juno.” **[Early-modern interpretatio]** This is a sixteenth-century Franciscan comparison for European readers, not evidence of a preconquest equation with the Roman Juno.

Identifications with a “Mexican Neptune,” “water nymph,” “baptismal goddess,” or universal “Great Mother” are colonial or modern explanatory analogies. “Baptismal goddess” is especially misleading if it implies the Christian sacrament: the recorded rite is an Indigenous birth, washing, naming, and fate ceremony.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest material evidence

**[Secure]** Numerous late-Postclassic Mexica kneeling female stone figures wear the banded headdress, side tassels, paper ornament, skirt, and *quechquemitl* associated with Chalchiuhtlicue. One basalt figure in the Metropolitan Museum is dated broadly to 1325–1521; another head is dated to the fifteenth–early sixteenth century. Their original archaeological contexts are poorly documented, so precise dating and cult location remain uncertain. The Met’s full figure retains pigment and is identified by costume rather than by an inscription.

**[Secure, but culturally broader than Mexica]** The Codex Borgia, a central-Mexican screenfold probably made shortly before or around the Spanish invasion, depicts Chalchiuhtlicue on folios 11, 14, 20, and 65. Its precise place of manufacture remains disputed. It is therefore evidence for a wider late-Postclassic central-Mexican ritual system, not automatically for Tenochtitlan alone.

### Principal manuscripts and chronicles

#### Codex Borgia

**Language/support/date:** largely nonalphabetic pictorial divinatory manuscript on prepared animal skin; central Mexico; approximately late fifteenth or early sixteenth century.

**Role:** Chalchiuhtlicue appears in calendrical and ritual complexes. Her diagnostic yellow face, paired dark cheek strokes, water imagery, jade signs, elaborate headdress, and flowing skirt participate in the manuscript’s visual grammar. Folio 14 places her among the nine Lords of the Night. Folio 20 concerns passage through or action involving water; folio 65 belongs to the manuscript’s complex ritual-cosmological sequence.

**Caution:** Eduard Seler’s monumental commentary remains foundational but sometimes turns pictorial associations into overly systematic mythology. Elizabeth Hill Boone treats the manuscript as a performative cycle of time and meaning rather than a simple illustrated “book of myths.”

#### Codex Borbonicus

**Language/support/date:** pictorial manuscript with later alphabetic glosses, probably made immediately before or soon after the conquest; provenance and exact date disputed.

**Role:** folio 5 presents Chalchiuhtlicue as patron of the trecena beginning **1-Reed**. Her image has a banded headcloth, paper ornament, facial mark, jade-associated costume, and water. Its festival section also documents the rain-deity ceremonial cycle.

#### Codex Cospi and Borgia-group manuscripts

**Date:** late Postclassic or early colonial, depending on manuscript.

**Role:** abbreviated deity heads and calendrical scenes preserve Chalchiuhtlicue’s paired cheek marks and water/jade sign complex. They show that her representation belonged to a regional visual vocabulary rather than to one Mexica workshop.

#### Codex Mendoza

**Date/language:** commissioned in early colonial New Spain, usually about 1541–1542; Nahua pictorial information with Spanish glosses.

**Role:** not a narrative biography of Chalchiuhtlicue. Its tributary and place-name glyphs nevertheless document the same graphic vocabulary—water bands, spirals, shells, droplets, jade signs, and “houses of mist”—used to construct her image.

#### Codex Magliabechiano and related Tudela-group manuscripts

**Date:** mid-sixteenth century; pictorial images plus Spanish text.

**Role:** preserve festival and costume information. Folio 75r gives an abbreviated facial mark associated with Chalchiuhtlicue; the manuscript family records colonial descriptions of deity impersonators and ritual observance.

#### Codex Telleriano-Remensis

**Date:** mid-sixteenth century, with pictorial annals/calendrics and European-language commentary.

**Role:** supplies colonial calendrical and cosmogonic parallels. Its value lies partly in comparison with the Vaticanus A/Ríos tradition, whose Christian commentators often moralized Indigenous gods.

#### Primeros Memoriales

**Language/support/date:** Nahuatl alphabetic and pictorial material collected by Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators at Tepepulco, approximately 1558–1561; preserved in the Matritense manuscripts.

**Role:** records deity costumes, prayers, and the veintena calendar before the Florentine Codex’s final redaction. It is an earlier documentary stratum than the completed *Historia general* and must not be treated merely as a summary of it.

#### Florentine Codex

**Language/support/date:** parallel Nahuatl and Spanish encyclopedia produced by Sahagún with Nahua grammarians, writers, and painters; final manuscript approximately 1575–1577.

- **Book I, chapter 11:** calls Chalchiuhtlicue goddess of water, sister of the Tlaloque, controller of sea and river, drownings, storms, whirlpools, and vessels; associates her festival with Etzalcualiztli.
- **Book II:** records the annual festivals Atlcahualo and Etzalcualiztli, rainmaking, fasting, deity impersonation, offerings, and human killing.
- **Book VI, chapters 32 and 37:** preserves speeches used in a newborn’s first washing and later naming bath. The midwife invokes Chalchiuhtli/Chalchiuhtlatonac to receive, wash, and cleanse the infant.
- **Book VII:** gives a sequence of world ages or suns, though details must be compared with the *Leyenda de los Soles*.
- **Book XII:** records conquest and destruction through a heavily mediated Indigenous-colonial narrative.

The Anderson–Dibble edition is the standard complete English translation, but its English must always be checked against the Nahuatl column.

#### Diego Durán

**Works/date:** *Book of the Gods and Rites* and the *Ancient Calendar*, composed in the 1570s; the *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España* completed about 1581.

**Role:** supplies festival descriptions, temple ritual, priestly activity, and mythographic systematization. Durán sometimes preserves material independent of Sahagún, but he also interprets through Christian biblical history. Agreement between Durán and Sahagún strengthens documentation of a practice; it does not eliminate colonial mediation.

#### Leyenda de los Soles

**Language/date:** Nahuatl text copied in 1558 as part of the Codex Chimalpopoca; the surviving manuscript is a later copy after the original was lost.

**Role:** principal textual witness for the sequence of suns. Chalchiuhtlicue is associated with **Nahui Atl, 4-Water**, the sun lasting 676 years and ending when the heavens collapsed and people became fish.

#### Anales de Cuauhtitlan

**Language/date:** Nahuatl annals compiled in the sixteenth century, also preserved through the Codex Chimalpopoca tradition.

**Role:** gives historical-cosmogonic material relevant to the suns and central-Mexican sacred history, but not a continuous Chalchiuhtlicue narrative.

#### Histoyre du Mechique

**Language/date:** French manuscript deriving from sixteenth-century Spanish missionary material, approximately the 1540s.

**Role:** preserves variant creation and divine-genealogy traditions, including Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue as a couple. Its textual transmission is indirect; unique details require caution.

#### Cantares Mexicanos

**Language/date:** Nahuatl song manuscript, principally copied in the later sixteenth century.

**Role:** aquatic, jade, floral, and rain imagery illuminates the ritual-poetic semantic field in which Chalchiuhtlicue operated. Not every “jade water” passage names or personifies her.

### Limits of the corpus

There is no surviving preconquest alphabetic hymn giving Chalchiuhtlicue a continuous biography. The record is distributed among pictorial almanacs, sculpture, festival descriptions, ritual speeches, and colonial cosmogonies. The apparent completeness of modern encyclopedia entries is produced by combining those genres.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Secure]**

- rivers, streams, lakes, springs, canals, and sea water;
- irrigation and the fertility sustained by terrestrial water;
- navigation, boats, and those whose livelihoods depended on waterways;
- whirlpools, storms, drowning, and inundation;
- newborn washing, purification, naming, and admission to social life;
- the cosmic age 4-Water and its flood.

Tlaloc primarily governs rain descending from the sky and the mountain-stored waters from which rain comes; Chalchiuhtlicue characteristically governs water spread or collected upon the earth. The division is useful but not absolute: festival and manuscript imagery overlap.

### Diagnostic appearance

Danièle Dehouve’s analysis shows that Chalchiuhtlicue is “constructed” by combinations of signs rather than one immutable portrait:

- a skirt marked by a jade bead or greenstone disk—the pictorial spelling of her name;
- a tall, layered or banded cotton headdress;
- large side tassels;
- a pleated bark-paper ornament, *amacuexpalli*, behind the head;
- yellow facial paint;
- one or two rectangular black or red cheek marks, interpreted since Seler as droplets but not explicitly explained that way by Sahagún’s informants;
- streams with alternating blue bands, spirals, shell or droplet edging;
- precious-stone disks, gold signs, rubber-dripped sacrificial paper, serpents, turtles, and *yauhtli* flowers in particular regional manuscripts.

**[Regional variation]** Borgia-group painters and Mexica manuscript painters do not use exactly the same sign inventory. The double cheek stroke is systematic in the Borgia; the Borbonicus may reduce it to one dark stroke, and the Magliabechiano renders it red.

### Sculptural identification

The Met’s kneeling basalt figure wears the dress of a noble woman: skirt, triangular shoulder cape, tasselled headdress, and knotted rear bands. Such images are often labeled Chalchiuhtlicue, but Chicomecoatl can share the headdress. An identification is strongest when water or jade signs survive; costume alone may indicate a broader class of young fertility/water goddesses.

### Sacred associations

- **Material:** *chalchihuitl*, precious greenstone; associated with water, vitality, rank, and enduring value.
- **Color:** blue-green from water and greenstone; yellow facial ground in Borgia-group images.
- **Calendar:** patron of the trecena **1-Reed** in the Borbonicus; one of the nine Lords of the Night.
- **Festival:** Etzalcualiztli, the sixth twenty-day month in Sahagún’s ordering; also present in the opening rainmaking complex Atlcahualo.
- **Food:** *etzalli*, a maize-and-bean preparation that gives Etzalcualiztli its name.
- **Occupational clients:** farmers near irrigated land, boatmen, water carriers or sellers, and makers/sellers of water jars, as later summaries of colonial testimony report.

No secure source establishes a universally sacred “number,” a single sacred animal, or an exclusive celestial body for her.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

1. **Sister of the Tlaloque:** Florentine Codex Book I, chapter 11. This is the clearest Sahaguntine formulation.
2. **Wife or consort of Tlaloc:** sixteenth-century colonial mythographic tradition, including material behind the *Histoyre du Mechique*; repeated by later authors and museum catalogues.
3. **Female counterpart of Tlaloc:** modern structural description based on complementary rain/earth-water roles. It is analytically useful but not itself an ancient kinship statement.
4. **Mother/elder sister of the gods:** ceremonial rhetoric in Florentine Codex Book VI. This signals primordial maternal rank, not necessarily literal maternity of every named god.
5. **Mother of a son proposed as a sun:** a colonial creation variant states that Tlalocatecuhtli sought to make a son born of himself and Chalchiuhtlicue into the sun. This is not the dominant Five Suns account and should not be fused with it.

### Documented god–god interactions

Only interactions actually present in the evidence are listed.

- **Tlaloc** — husband/consort in one colonial genealogical tradition and co-recipient of water festivals; *Histoyre du Mechique* tradition and sixteenth-century ritual sources. **[Early-colonial variant/canonical ritual association]**
- **The Tlaloque** — Chalchiuhtlicue is their sister and shares Etzalcualiztli rites with them; Florentine Codex Books I–II, approximately 1575–1577, drawing on earlier Nahua testimony. **[Secure early-colonial attestation]**
- **Tlalocatecuhtli/Tlaloc** — in a creation variant, the couple have a son whom Tlaloc proposes for solar office; colonial mythographic text cited through the *Histoyre du Mechique* complex. **[Regional or learned early-colonial variant]**
- **Quetzalcoatl** — participates in the same divine council/contest over whose son should become the sun in that variant; no independent intimate relationship with Chalchiuhtlicue is established. **[Early-colonial variant]**
- **Tonatiuh** — during the newborn naming rite, the infant washed through Chalchiuhtlicue’s water is subsequently lifted and presented to the sun; Florentine Codex Book VI, chapter 37. This is ritual sequence, not genealogy. **[Secure ritual interaction by joint invocation]**
- **Citlalatonac and Citlalicue** — invoked after the Chalchiuhtlicue washing when the infant is lifted toward celestial powers; Florentine Codex Book VI, chapter 37. **[Secure joint ritual appearance]**
- **Tlaltecuhtli** — receives the fourth presentation of the washed infant; same source. **[Secure joint ritual appearance]**
- **Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl** — named in the birth discourse as the remote generators/senders of the child, while Chalchiuhtlicue receives and cleanses it; Florentine Codex Book VI. **[Secure joint ritual theology]**
- **Chicomecoatl** — shares elements of headdress and fertility imagery in surviving art, but no specific narrative meeting between the two is securely attested. **[Iconographic relationship; interaction absent]**
- **Huixtocihuatl** — belongs to the wider water/salt complex and is called elder sister of the Tlaloque in Sahaguntine material, but a direct episode with Chalchiuhtlicue is not securely recorded. **[Interaction absent]**
- **Tezcatlipoca** — both occur in calendrical manuscripts and the wider creation tradition, but no securely attested one-to-one episode with Chalchiuhtlicue has been found. **[Interaction absent]**
- **Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, Coatlicue, and Tlaltecuhtli as monumental figures** — their archaeological discoveries illuminate the Mexica sacred precinct but do not document personal interactions with Chalchiuhtlicue. **[Absence of narrative evidence]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The fourth sun: Nahui Atl, 4-Water

**[Early-colonial attestation of a preconquest cosmology]** In the *Leyenda de los Soles* the world passes through successive suns, each defined by a calendar name, presiding power, inhabitants, duration, and catastrophe. The fourth is **4-Water**. It lasts 676 years—thirteen 52-year bundles—and ends in a flood. The heavens collapse; the water rises for fifty-two years; human beings become fish.

Later summaries commonly say “Chalchiuhtlicue was the fourth sun.” That formulation reflects a widespread scheme assigning each age a presiding deity, but the surviving texts are not uniform about whether a god literally is the sun, governs the sun, or causes its destruction. The firm core is her association with Nahui Atl and the flood.

On the Sun Stone, the 4-Water day sign is one of the four previous-world signs around the central solar face. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Reading the sign as a compressed reference to Chalchiuhtlicue’s age depends on comparison with colonial Five Suns texts; the stone does not spell her name.

### The flood and transformation into fish

**[Canonical colonial record]** The fourth age ends by overwhelming water rather than drought, jaguars, wind, or fire. Humanity’s transformation into fish is the best-attested outcome in the *Leyenda de los Soles* tradition. Some later retellings add details from other Mesoamerican flood narratives—boats, chosen survivors, divine punishment, or a moral cause. These should not be attributed to Chalchiuhtlicue’s narrative without a specific source.

### The newborn’s encounter with divine water

This is ritual speech rather than a free-standing myth, but it narrates a divine event. After severing the umbilical cord, the midwife washes the child and addresses the water:

> “Approach thy mother Chalchiuhtli, Chalchiuhtlatonac! May she receive thee; may she wash thee, may she cleanse thee.”

The later naming bath touches mouth, chest, head, and body; water removes pollution and calamity. The infant is lifted four times and presented to celestial and terrestrial powers.

**Evidentiary classification:** **[Secure early-colonial transcription of Indigenous ritual]**. Calling this “Aztec baptism” is an early-modern/modern Christian analogy. Similarities—water, cleansing, language of renewed life—are real; equivalence to Christian baptism is not. The rite also assigns a name and gendered social implements and situates the child within the calendrical cosmos.

### Water’s benevolent and destructive duality

Sahagún’s Book I description supplies a compressed mythic theology: Chalchiuhtlicue provides indispensable water but can drown people, break boats, and raise whirlpools. This is not a story of moral reward and punishment. No secure source says victims drowned because the goddess judged them sinful.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult geography and institutions

No independent monumental temple can presently be assigned to Chalchiuhtlicue with the security of the Templo Mayor shrines of Tlaloc and Huitzilopochtli. Her cult operated through:

- water sources, canals, and lakes;
- household or neighborhood images;
- festival impersonators (*ixiptla*);
- the rain-god complex centered institutionally on Tlaloc;
- birth and naming ceremonies conducted by midwives;
- agricultural and occupational communities dependent on water.

The absence of a securely identified exclusive temple is important: it does not imply a marginal deity. Distributed ritual can leave a different archaeological footprint from a state temple.

### Atlcahualo

**[Secure colonial description]** The opening veintena belonged to Tlaloc, the Tlaloque, Quetzalcoatl in some accounts, and Chalchiuhtlicue. Ceremonies sought rain and agricultural renewal. Sahagún records the sacrifice of children at mountains and water places during the early rainmaking cycle. Their tears were read as favorable rain signs.

The evidence does not always identify every child as sacrificed specifically “to Chalchiuhtlicue.” The victims belonged to the collective rain-and-water cult; assigning all of them exclusively to her overstates the sources.

### Etzalcualiztli

The sixth veintena, **Etzalcualiztli**, “Eating of *etzalli*,” honored Tlaloc, Chalchiuhtlicue, and the Tlaloque. Sahagún describes:

- prolonged priestly fasting and penance;
- gathering reeds and making ritual seats or mats;
- bathing and aquatic activity;
- processions and dances carrying maize and pots of *etzalli*;
- offerings of food, paper, rubber, incense, and water-associated materials;
- impersonators dressed as the water gods;
- sacrificial killing of divine images/impersonators.

**[Secure and unsanitized]** Human sacrifice formed part of this state ceremonial complex. Victims embodying Tlaloc, Chalchiuhtlicue, and Tlaloque figures were killed. The sources are colonial and sometimes disagree on sequence and numbers, but the practice itself is supported by multiple accounts and by the broader sacrificial archaeology of Tenochtitlan.

### Archaeological evidence

At the Templo Mayor museum, a polychrome vessel from near Chamber II is identified as Chalchiuhtlicue. It bears a modeled face and feet, a frontal *quechquemitl*, a pleated paper ornament behind the head, and surrounding marine shells. This is contextual evidence connecting her with the imperial sacred precinct and exotic aquatic offerings.

Templo Mayor deposits contain greenstone, shells, aquatic animals, human remains, and vessels. Not every watery offering belongs uniquely to Chalchiuhtlicue: Tlaloc and the broader rain complex dominate the northern half of the temple.

The **Coyolxauhqui Stone**, found in 1978, initiated the modern Templo Mayor Project; the **Tlaltecuhtli monolith**, discovered in 2006, opened another major excavation zone. Neither depicts Chalchiuhtlicue. Their relevance is methodological: controlled excavation transformed the evidence for Mexica divine images, sacrifice, offerings, and sacred architecture.

Between 2015 and 2017, INAH excavations at 24 República de Guatemala identified remains of the **Huei Tzompantli** and a skull tower. The exposed structure belongs to phases approximately 1486–1502 and contained hundreds of skulls, including those of women and children. This corroborates large-scale sacrificial display at imperial Tenochtitlan. It does **not** prove that the recovered individuals were killed for Chalchiuhtlicue; no such exclusive attribution has been established.

### Priesthood and economics

Her ceremonies were administered within the larger Mexica priestly establishment. Sources name specialized Tlaloc priests more clearly than an autonomous permanent Chalchiuhtlicue hierarchy. Water-dependent occupations and tribute goods—greenstone, shells, cotton, paper, rubber, maize, beans, and vessels—linked cult to imperial extraction and long-distance exchange.

Claims of “temple prostitution” have no credible documentation in Chalchiuhtlicue’s cult.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before approximately 1200: antecedents, not attestations

Water, storm, mountain, cave, fertility, and greenstone symbolism is ancient in Mesoamerica. Olmec, Gulf Coast, Oaxaca, Maya, and central-Mexican traditions all possessed aquatic supernatural beings. These are comparative antecedents, not appearances of Chalchiuhtlicue by name.

### Classic Teotihuacan, approximately 100–550 CE

A colossal female-presenting stone figure found near the Pyramid of the Moon was called “Chalchiuhtlicue” by nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars. Teotihuacan murals at Tepantitla and Tetitla were later organized under the label “Great Goddess,” associated with water, vegetation, caves, spiders, birds, and fertility.

**[Contested modern reconstruction]** There is no Teotihuacan text naming either figure Chalchiuhtlicue. Esther Pasztory and Janet Berlo developed the Great Goddess synthesis; Zoltán Paulinyi argued that it speculatively fused at least six distinct supernatural complexes. Elisa Mandell challenged both the assumed femaleness and the application of a simple Western gender binary. Current INAH presentation calls the figure enigmatic and acknowledges the dispute. Chalchiuhtlicue may preserve or reconfigure older central-Mexican water-goddess imagery, but direct identity or cult continuity is unproved.

### Epiclassic and Toltec periods, approximately 600–1150

Aquatic and storm-god iconography persists, but the record does not securely name Chalchiuhtlicue. Assertions of an unbroken Teotihuacan–Toltec–Mexica goddess cult are reconstructions.

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1521

This is her securely attested florescence. Borgia-group manuscripts show a regionally shared divinatory deity; Mexica codices, sculpture, festival organization, and imperial offerings situate her within the Tlaloc complex. Her presence in domestic-sized sculpture, state ritual, calendrics, and birth practice indicates several social scales of worship.

### Spanish conquest and sixteenth century

The fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 dismantled the temple institutions that supported public sacrifice. Temples and images were destroyed, buried, reused, or collected; Christian churches occupied sacred urban spaces. Missionaries criminalized “idolatry,” trained Indigenous assistants to identify prohibited rites, and redirected water ceremonies into Christian sacramental frameworks.

At the same time, Indigenous collaborators preserved Chalchiuhtlicue in the Florentine Codex, *Primeros Memoriales*, annals, songs, and pictorial manuscripts. The colonial archive is therefore simultaneously a record of suppression and a product of negotiated preservation.

There is no demonstrated, institutionally continuous Chalchiuhtlicue priesthood from the sixteenth century to the present. Nor should all later Indigenous reverence for springs or rain be relabeled her cult without named testimony.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

Her active public cult largely disappears from the documentary record. Antiquarian manuscripts preserve her as an example of Indigenous “idolatry.” Proposed fusions with the Virgin Mary or local female saints are possible in particular communities, but a general equation **Chalchiuhtlicue = Virgin of Guadalupe** is not securely documented and should be labeled modern comparativism unless tied to a local historical source.

### Nineteenth century

Antiquarian collecting and national archaeology returned her images to public visibility. Alexander von Humboldt encountered Guillermo Dupaix’s antiquarian network in 1803. The Teotihuacan colossus was transported by Leopoldo Batres and installed in the National Museum’s Gallery of Monoliths, where its designation as Chalchiuhtlicue gave a speculative identification institutional authority.

### Twentieth century

Seler’s codex commentaries, Sahagún editions, Alfonso Caso’s calendrical work, Henry B. Nicholson’s 1971 deity typology, Miguel León-Portilla’s reconstruction of Nahua philosophy, and archaeological work at Teotihuacan and Templo Mayor produced the modern scholarly Chalchiuhtlicue.

The 1978 discovery of Coyolxauhqui led to Eduardo Matos Moctezuma’s Templo Mayor Project. Excavated offerings enabled far stronger contextual interpretation than older museum pieces with unknown provenience.

### Twenty-first century

Research has shifted from fixed “attributes” toward the visual and linguistic rules through which divine identities were assembled. Dehouve’s work on Chalchiuhtlicue’s signs exemplifies this development. The Teotihuacan Great Goddess debate has become a caution against projecting later Nahua names, universal goddess models, or binary gender assumptions backward by a millennium.

Modern Mexica revivalists, Indigenous cultural organizations, dancers, artists, environmental activists, and eclectic pagans sometimes invoke Chalchiuhtlicue. These practices are contemporary revivals or reconfigurations; their scale and lines of transmission are not comprehensively documented.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, museums, and nationalist image

Museum labels and popular art have standardized the image of a youthful woman with a blue-green skirt and streams pouring from it. This is partly ancient and partly a modern synthesis of different manuscripts.

The Teotihuacan colossus illustrates the power of museum naming: “Chalchiuhtlicue” became familiar long before the identification was critically tested. Its alternative description as a generic water deity, female ancestor, or unnamed supernatural remains possible.

### Literature, games, and commercial imagery

Chalchiuhtlicue appears in myth handbooks, tarot and oracle decks, games, jewelry, tattoos, online deity guides, and environmental art. These media commonly add:

- a fixed “turquoise aura”;
- zodiac correspondences;
- crystal prescriptions;
- modern altar instructions;
- personality traits such as compassion, emotional healing, or maternal serenity;
- universal “water goddess” equivalence with Isis, Yemọja, Oshun, Amphitrite, or Celtic deities.

**[Modern invention/syncretism]** None of these correspondences is documented in the Borgia group, Sahagún, Durán, or the Five Suns texts unless a modern author explicitly reconstructs it from water symbolism.

### Occult and neopagan use

Unlike Egyptian, Greco-Roman, or Norse gods, Chalchiuhtlicue did not acquire a major documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or classical ceremonial grimoires. Claims that she had an ancient Western occult sigil, planetary seal, chakra, or Kabbalistic correspondence are modern inventions.

Contemporary practitioners sometimes construct offerings from archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence—water, greenstone-colored objects, flowers, maize, incense—while others work within eclectic Wiccan or New Age systems. These are modern devotional practices, not evidence of uninterrupted preconquest ritual.

### Political and environmental appropriation

Water protectors and artists have used Chalchiuhtlicue as a symbol of threatened rivers and Indigenous ecological knowledge. This is a modern political activation of an authentically aquatic deity, but environmentalist formulations such as “guardian of all planetary waters” are contemporary universalizations.

No substantial evidence was found for sustained extremist appropriation centered specifically on Chalchiuhtlicue. Broader nationalist uses of Mexica religion should not be imputed to her without documentation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The nature of “Aztec gods”

Nicholson’s 1971 typology organized central-Mexican deities into conceptual complexes, placing Chalchiuhtlicue in the rain-moisture-agricultural fertility sphere. It remains useful for comparison but can make fluid, locally assembled identities look like a fixed pantheon.

León-Portilla emphasized philosophical unity and *teotl* as sacred generative power. Critics caution that this can systematize diverse ritual traditions into a coherent metaphysics resembling Western philosophy.

Graulich treated festivals and myths as elements of a grand cosmogonic pattern. His reconstructions are influential but sometimes depend on harmonizing sources from different regions and dates.

Boone’s semiotic and performative reading of codices, and Dehouve’s analysis of Chalchiuhtlicue’s graphic construction, better preserve manuscript-specific variation.

### Etymology

The basic derivation from *chalchihuitl* plus “her skirt” is secure. What remains debated is its conceptual force:

- a literal jade garment;
- a metaphor for shining or green-blue water;
- a visual name-glyph assembled from jade and skirt signs;
- a condensed statement that terrestrial water is precious.

These are compatible interpretive levels, but only the linguistic construction and pictorial pun are directly demonstrable.

### Tlaloc’s wife or sister of the Tlaloque?

Both are attested. Treating “the Tlaloque” as a class including Tlaloc can make the statements appear contradictory; treating Tlaloc as their chief and Chalchiuhtlicue as their sister permits kinship-system reconciliation. The sources themselves do not explain the logic. A dossier must therefore preserve both.

### The Great Goddess question

- **Continuity position:** shared water, cave, vegetation, jade, tassel, and headdress imagery suggests that Chalchiuhtlicue inherited features of a major Teotihuacan female deity.
- **Critical position:** “Great Goddess” amalgamates distinct images; sex and gender were inferred from costume; no name bridges the thousand-year gap.
- **Current defensible conclusion:** iconographic ancestry is plausible; identity is unproved; calling the Teotihuacan figure Chalchiuhtlicue is a modern attribution.

### “Aztec baptism”

Early Spanish writers noticed Christian-sounding language of washing, purification, and rebirth. The rite is securely documented, but the label “baptism” risks assuming Christian influence or sacramental equivalence. Since Sahagún recorded it after conquest, exact wording may also reflect translation across religious vocabularies. Its Indigenous structures—calendar fate, fourfold lifting, naming, gendered implements, sun and earth presentation—show that it is not reducible to baptism.

### Human sacrifice

Two errors recur:

1. denial that sacrifice occurred because colonial writers were hostile;
2. acceptance of every colonial number and attribution without criticism.

Texts, osteology, sacrificial architecture, cut marks, skull racks, and offerings establish that human sacrifice occurred. Exact totals and the deity assigned to a given victim require contextual proof. The Huei Tzompantli confirms the institution at Tenochtitlan but is not a Chalchiuhtlicue-specific deposit.

### State of the evidence

The principal pictorial manuscripts are dispersed among European collections; the Codex Borgia is in the Vatican Library, the Borbonicus in the Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale in Paris, and the Florentine Codex in Florence’s Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana. The original Codex Chimalpopoca manuscript disappeared in the twentieth century; scholarship depends on photographs and copies. Many stone figures lack excavated provenance. These losses sharply limit chronological reconstruction.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the evidentiary record |
|---|---|
| c. 100–550 CE | Teotihuacan water/fertility figures and the Pyramid-of-the-Moon colossus are created. Their identification as Chalchiuhtlicue is modern and disputed. |
| c. 1200–1521 | Late-Postclassic central-Mexican water-goddess iconography develops; securely attributable Mexica sculptures belong principally to this horizon. |
| Late 15th–early 16th century | Codex Borgia depicts Chalchiuhtlicue in calendrical and ritual sequences. |
| c. 1500–1521/early colonial | Codex Borbonicus represents her as patron of the 1-Reed trecena; exact pre/postconquest date disputed. |
| 1521 | Fall of Tenochtitlan ends the imperial temple system and initiates suppression and transformation of public Indigenous cult. |
| c. 1540s | Material behind the *Histoyre du Mechique* records creation and genealogy variants. |
| c. 1541–1542 | Codex Mendoza documents related water and jade visual vocabulary. |
| 1558 | Date carried by the *Leyenda de los Soles* manuscript tradition; 4-Water flood account recorded. |
| c. 1558–1561 | *Primeros Memoriales* records deity costumes and festival data at Tepepulco. |
| 1570s | Durán composes descriptions of gods, rites, calendar, and sacrifice. |
| c. 1575–1577 | Florentine Codex final manuscript records Chalchiuhtlicue’s domains, Etzalcualiztli, and newborn washing speeches. |
| c. 1581 | Durán completes his historical compilation. |
| 17th–18th centuries | Public cult is absent from the institutional record; antiquarian and missionary texts preserve the deity as suppressed “idolatry.” |
| 1790–1791 | Coatlicue and the Sun Stone are unearthed in Mexico City, beginning a new monumental-archaeological context for Mexica religion. |
| 1803 | Humboldt encounters Dupaix’s antiquarian circle; Mexican sculpture enters international comparative scholarship. |
| 1887 | National Museum’s Gallery of Monoliths opens; Batres’s Teotihuacan colossus, labeled Chalchiuhtlicue, enters the national display canon. |
| 1900s–1940s | Seler and subsequent scholars publish codex commentaries that standardize her iconography. |
| 1950–1982 | Anderson and Dibble publish the Nahuatl/English Florentine Codex edition. |
| 1971 | Nicholson publishes his influential deity typology. |
| 1978 | Coyolxauhqui discovery initiates the modern Templo Mayor Project; not a Chalchiuhtlicue find but pivotal for cult archaeology. |
| 1990s | Pasztory and Berlo develop influential Teotihuacan Great Goddess interpretations and possible later survivals. |
| 2006 | Paulinyi argues that the “Great Goddess” is a scholarly amalgam; the Tlaltecuhtli monolith is discovered at Tenochtitlan. |
| 2015 | Mandell reopens the Teotihuacan figure’s gender attribution; INAH begins excavation of the Huei Tzompantli remains discovered at Guatemala 24. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavations identify the skull-rack platform and skull tower, materially documenting imperial sacrificial display. |
| 2018 | Dehouve publishes a systematic study of how Chalchiuhtlicue’s identity is assembled from water, jade, paper, flower, and animal signs. |
| 2020s | Scholarship continues to reassess divine gender, sign systems, and fusion; Chalchiuhtlicue also appears in revivalist, ecological, artistic, and eclectic devotional settings. |
