# The Ahuiateteo (the Macuiltonaleque): Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective of male calendrical gods or dangerous spirits in Late Postclassic central Mexican religion; more precisely, a recurrent iconographic and ritual class of “Five” deities associated with pleasure, excess, punishment, divination, the south, and solar or warrior qualities. The familiar five-member list is a scholarly reconstruction from several sources, not a surviving narrative canon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The five figures depicted on pages 47–48 of the preconquest or Contact-period *Codex Borgia*, conventionally dated to the Late Postclassic, approximately the fifteenth or very early sixteenth century. Their identification as Macuiltonaleque depends on comparison with sixteenth-century Nahuatl and Spanish descriptions, especially the *Primeros Memoriales*. Individually named “Five” gods may have older Central Mexican antecedents, but no earlier inscription securely names the collective.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** preconquest/Contact-period pictorial evidence or early Indigenous testimony preserved in sixteenth-century manuscripts.
- **[CE] Colonial ethnographic elaboration:** sixteenth- or early seventeenth-century descriptions collected or written after the conquest.
- **[RV] Regional or manuscript variant:** a local, pictorial, or textual form that should not be generalized automatically to all Nahuas.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** colonial or modern attempts to arrange fluid divine complexes into fixed lists and hierarchies.
- **[SR] Syncretistic identification or fusion.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular elaboration.**
- **[NE] Negative evidence:** something frequently asserted for which the consulted primary record supplies no secure evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

The Ahuiateteo/Macuiltonaleque are best understood as a **class of calendrical divine powers**, not as five characters belonging to a single surviving myth. Their names combine the coefficient **mācuīlli**, “five,” with day signs of the 260-day *tōnalpōhualli*. They preside over pleasurable but dangerous activities—games, gambling, music, dance, feasting, sexuality, and intoxication—and embody the illnesses, impoverishment, social disorder, or loss of control that may attend excess. They also appear as armed, descending, sometimes deathlike beings allied iconographically with Cihuateteo and Tzitzimime. These functions overlap but are not perfectly identical in every source.

### Collective names

- **Āhuiatēteoh / Ahuiateteo.** Conventionally analyzed as the plural of a compound involving *āhui(a)*, “to enjoy oneself, take pleasure,” and *tēteoh*, “gods.” “Gods of pleasure” or “pleasure-gods” is therefore a reasonable gloss, but modern references rarely document the precise colonial occurrence and morphology. The spelling **Ahuiateteo** should not be treated as proof that all five were already a formally bounded corporation under that exact name. **[LS/MSR]**

- **Mācuīltōnalequeh / Macuiltonaleque.** Commonly glossed “owners/possessors of five-*tonalli*” or “lords of the five day-signs/souls”: *mācuīlli*, “five,” plus *tōnalli*, a multivalent word embracing day-sign, solar heat, animating force, destiny, and a component of personhood, with the possessive plural suffix *-ehqueh*. The museum rendering “Lords of the Five Souls” is evocative but compresses the broader semantic range of *tonalli*. **[CE/MSR]** A sixteenth-century healing incantation preserved through Hernando Ruiz de Alarcón invokes *tlamacazque macuiltonalleque*, translated in the colonial Spanish tradition as “five solar [powers] of different destinies.” [The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl preserves the passage and lexical record.](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/macuiltonaleque%2B%2B%2B/277682)

- **“Gods-Five” / *dioses cinco*.** A useful modern scholarly shorthand emphasizing that the coefficient five, rather than a common genealogy, constitutes the group. **[MSR]**

### The canonical modern five

1. **Mācuīlxōchitl — “Five Flower.”** Patron of games and gambling, dance, song, flowers, palace festivities, and pleasurable display; the best-documented member and often treated as the group’s preeminent figure. The *Primeros Memoriales* explicitly calls him a deity and supplies his array and hymn. [The Nahuatl Dictionary identifies him as the principal Macuiltonaleque and cites both the *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex*.](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/macuilxochitl) **[EA/CE]**

2. **Mācuīltōchtli — “Five Rabbit.”** A rabbit-day god associated particularly with *octli* (pulque), intoxication, and its excesses. His precise relationship to the numerous pulque gods called the **Centzon Totochtin**, “Four Hundred Rabbits,” is classificatory and overlapping rather than a securely narrated family relationship. **[EA/CE/MSR]**

3. **Mācuīlcuetzpalin — “Five Lizard.”** One of the calendrical five; securely identifiable by name and day sign but comparatively poorly individualized in surviving alphabetic texts. **[EA]**

4. **Mācuīlcōzcacuāuhtli — “Five Vulture.”** One of the five; later summaries assign gluttony or the hazards of wealth to him, but these narrow portfolios are less securely documented than the collective association with excess. **[EA/MSR]**

5. **Mācuīlmalīnalli — “Five Grass.”** One of the five; individually sparse outside calendrical and iconographic contexts. **[EA]**

### A larger class, not necessarily only five

The *Codex Borgia* and comparable divinatory systems can support a broader category of day names formed with “Five.” A Mexica stone figure at the Metropolitan Museum is identified by a five-dot serpent glyph as **Macuilcoatl, “Five Serpent,”** although Macuilcoatl is absent from the familiar canonical five. This materially demonstrates that *macuil-* deities were not restricted in every context to the standard list. The figure is dated approximately 1450–1521 and bears the diagnostic hand at the lower face. [Metropolitan Museum object record.](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/307606) **[EA/RV]**

Modern lists naming nineteen or twenty “Macuil-” beings usually extrapolate mechanically from possible coefficient-five day names. Such a complete pantheon is a **learned systematization**, not a securely attested preconquest council of twenty gods. **[LS]**

### Macuilxochitl and Xochipilli

The *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, chapter 14, says that the god called Macuilxochitl was also called **Xochipilli**, commonly glossed “Flower Prince” or “Noble/Precious Flower.” This is strong sixteenth-century evidence for an identification in the Mexica source tradition, but it need not mean every image or local cult of Macuilxochitl was indistinguishable from every Xochipilli manifestation. [The colonial Spanish chapter explicitly gives both names and describes the flower feast.](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/clacso/se/20191121033043/El_Mexico_antiguo.pdf) **[CE/SR]**

Claims that Macuilxochitl was literally Xochipilli’s brother, son, or permanently separate colleague are generally modern harmonizations of contradictory deity lists. **[MI unless independently sourced]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Codex Borgia, pages 47–48

The earliest secure group representation is the procession of five male figures paired with five Cihuateteo on pages 47–48 of the *Codex Borgia*. The manuscript is a painted deerskin screenfold of the Borgia Group, probably made in central or south-central Mexico shortly before or around the conquest. Its precise place and date remain disputed; “Aztec manuscript from Tenochtitlan” is too specific.

The figures bear calendrical names and the group’s diagnostic costume, including the painted hand at the mouth. Because the manuscript contains no alphabetic caption identifying them collectively, “Macuiltonaleque” is a comparative identification established through later descriptions, especially Sahagún’s material and Seler’s iconographic analysis. The pages pair dangerous male and female powers and place them in a divinatory rather than narrative sequence. [A Princeton exhibition reproduces the procession and explains its relationship to diviners and the Cihuateteo.](https://static.artmuseum.princeton.edu/Sorcerers/codex.html) **[EA/MSR]**

### Primeros Memoriales, approximately 1558–1561

Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*, compiled from Nahua testimony at Tepepulco and subsequently worked on at Tlatelolco, provides the most precise early alphabetic descriptions. Folio 265v describes and illustrates **Macuiltochtli** and **Macuilxochitl**:

- Macuiltochtli has “a hand painted on his mouth,” a feathered crest, claw- or toe-shaped necklace, bells, white sandals, shield, and obsidian axe.
- Macuilxochitl likewise has the painted hand, a bright-red face, feathered headdress and back ornament, solar banner and shield, red-bordered loincloth, bells, sandals, and a heart staff.

The Tlachia edition explains that the open hand refers both to the group identity and to five through its fingers. [Tlachia, *Primeros Memoriales*, fol. 265v.](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/matritenses/RP_265v) **[CE preserving Indigenous testimony]**

The manuscript also preserves the **Song of Macuilxochitl**, beginning in translation, “From where flowers stand erect, thence come I, the priest, lord of red dusk.” It invokes or mentions Centeotl, Tezcatlipoca, and a female “grandmother” interpreted by the modern editors in relation to Toci/Teteoinnan. The surviving text is difficult, abbreviated, and accompanied by Indigenous exegesis; its fragmentary divine references must not be converted into a continuous modern myth. [UNAM publishes the Nahuatl text of “Macuil Xochitl icuic.”](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/veinte_himnos/067_04_22_Macuil.pdf) **[CE]**

### Florentine Codex, completed 1577

The final *Florentine Codex*, produced by Sahagún and Indigenous writers and painters at Tlatelolco, was completed in 1577 and is now at the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana. [The Getty’s Digital Florentine Codex documents its completion, transmission, and present location.](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/resources/1_Richter_Houtrouw) **[CE]**

Relevant passages include:

- **Book 1, chapter 14:** Macuilxochitl/Xochipilli, the four-day flower fast, sexual abstinence, sickness as punishment for violating the fast, vows undertaken for healing, floral food, song, dance, and the feast *Xochilhuitl*.
- **Book 1:** descriptions of related pleasure, music, medicine, dance, and pulque gods, including Ixtlilton and pulque deities; these establish a divine complex but not automatic identity.
- **Book 4:** prognostications attached to day signs and coefficients.
- **Book 8, chapter 10:** elite diversions, the ballgame and *patolli*; the accompanying image shows the board game.
- **Book 10 and magical/medical material:** social categories and illnesses relevant to excess, although not every sexual disease in these books is expressly inflicted by the Ahuiateteo.

The Anderson–Dibble edition remains the standard full English translation, but its vocabulary—particularly “soul” for *tonalli*—must be checked against the Nahuatl.

### Codex Magliabechiano, mid-sixteenth century

Page 48 depicts Macuilxochitl presiding over *patolli*. The manuscript belongs to the Magliabechiano/Tudela colonial pictorial complex. Its image is not preconquest eyewitness illustration in a simple sense; it is an early colonial copy within a transmitted pictorial tradition. **[CE/RV]**

### Diego Durán, approximately 1570s–1581

Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites* supply two especially important notices:

- Players invoked **Macuilxochitl** aloud when throwing the five marked beans used as dice, rubbed them in their hands, struck a loud clap, and examined their points. Durán translates the name as “Five Roses/Flowers.” [The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl reproduces Durán’s passage and citation.](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/macuilxochitl/188896) **[CE]**
- In Durán’s description of the Tlacaxipehualiztli ceremonies, impersonators or “likenesses” of numerous gods—including Macuilxochitl, Tonatiuh, Huitzilopochtli, Quetzalcoatl, Ixtlilton, and Mayahuel—were killed by heart extraction. This is explicit documentary evidence for a sacrificed Macuilxochitl impersonator in Durán’s account, though it does not prove that every Macuiltonal had a separate sacrificial cult. **[CE]**

### Ruiz de Alarcón, early seventeenth century

The *Tratado de las supersticiones y costumbres gentílicas* records a healing incantation summoning the *macuiltonalleque*. Their presence in medicine and verbal magic shows colonial survival of the category beyond pictorial divination. It does not demonstrate an unchanged public temple cult. **[CE/folkloric survival]**

### Corpora named in the brief but not directly evidentiary here

The *Codex Mendoza*, *Codex Borbonicus*, *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, *Leyenda de los Soles*, *Histoyre du Mechique*, *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, and *Cantares Mexicanos* are indispensable for Mexica religion broadly, but no secure major narrative of the five Ahuiateteo as a group was located in them. Their silence is important. **[NE]**

The Sun Stone, Coyolxauhqui Stone, and Tlaltecuhtli monolith likewise illuminate Mexica calendrics, sacrificial state religion, and the Templo Mayor, but none is a secure representation or inscription of the five-member group. **[NE]**

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

The best-attested collective domains are:

- Pleasure and its dangerous surplus.
- Music, singing, dance, flowers, courtly performance, and festivity.
- Gambling and games, especially *patolli* under Macuilxochitl.
- Pulque and drunkenness, especially through Macuiltochtli.
- Sexual pleasure and illness attributed to ritual or sexual transgression.
- Divination, prognostication, diagnosis, matchmaking, and healing.
- Violence, punishment, and the armed descent of celestial or death-associated beings.
- The southern direction and solar warmth in scholarly reconstructions of the calendrical system.

“Gluttony god,” “god of greed,” or separate portfolios assigning one vice to each of the five are mostly modern simplifications. **[MSR/MI]**

### The hand over the mouth

The defining emblem is an extended white hand painted or carved across the mouth or lower face, described in Nahuatl as *motenmacpalhuiticac* in the Sahaguntine material. Its five fingers make it an intelligible visual sign for *mācuīlli*, five. It is present in the *Primeros Memoriales*, the *Codex Borgia* figures, and the stone Macuilcoatl. [Tlachia explicitly interprets the hand as a reference to five.](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/matritenses/RP_265v) **[EA/CE]**

It is not securely a gesture of shame, an instruction to remain silent, or a “wagging finger of reprimand.” Those are modern visual rationalizations. **[MI]**

### Other diagnostic features

- A yellow or otherwise colored band across the eyes.
- Bright-red or patterned facial paint.
- Feathered headdress or crest.
- Bells and white sandals.
- Red-bordered loincloth.
- Claw- or toe-shaped pectoral.
- Shield and obsidian axe.
- Heart staff, solar shield, solar banner, and flaring back ornament for Macuilxochitl.
- Calendrical name glyph combining five dots with flower, rabbit, lizard, grass, vulture, serpent, or another sign.

Not every figure possesses every feature. Identification should combine the day glyph, hand motif, costume, and manuscript context.

### Corporeal form

The Borgia figures are male anthropomorphic beings; some are skeletal, disfigured, or ominous in ways linking them with dangerous celestial and death powers. The Met’s Macuilcoatl is a youthful seated standard-bearer. Thus “cadaverous demons” and “youthful solar gods” are not mutually exclusive descriptions but emphasize different manifestations. **[EA/RV]**

### Disease and punishment

The *Florentine Codex* says those who broke the abstinence required by Macuilxochitl’s flower fast—particularly by having sexual intercourse—could be afflicted with disease in the genitals or private parts. The offender might undertake a vow and repeat the fast for healing. [The Nahuatl Dictionary summarizes the passage and cites *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, pp. 12–13.](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/macuilxochitl) **[CE]**

Calling these afflictions “venereal diseases” is a defensible translation category but not a laboratory diagnosis. No source identifies syphilis, gonorrhea, or another modern pathogen by microbiological criteria. The point of the text is a divinely inflicted consequence of broken ritual-sexual restraint. **[MSR]**

The broader thesis that each Ahuiateteotl distributed a particular disease corresponding to a particular vice cannot be reconstructed securely from the surviving texts. **[NE]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No securely attested source supplies a common father, mother, birth story, spouse, or offspring for the five-member collective. Their unity is calendrical and iconographic rather than genealogical. **[NE]**

Macuiltochtli’s inclusion among the pulque/rabbit gods permits association with Mayahuel, Patecatl, Ometochtli, and the Centzon Totochtin. Modern websites sometimes turn this classificatory association into a literal statement that Mayahuel and Patecatl were his biological parents. That genealogy was not established in the primary material consulted. **[MI unless a specific primary passage is produced]**

### Every securely documented or materially supported divine interaction

Because the evidence is commonly pictorial, “interaction” here includes deliberate pairing or joint ritual appearance, not merely narrative speech.

- **Cihuateteo — paired male and female dangerous powers.** Five Macuiltonaleque appear with five Cihuateteo on *Codex Borgia* 47–48, in a divinatory sequence. No surviving prose narrative explains a marriage or battle between them. Late Postclassic pictorial evidence. **[EA]**

- **Centeotl — invoked or answered in the Song of Macuilxochitl.** The hymn’s difficult final lines call for an answer to Centeotl; modern editors see overlap between the young maize deity and the Macuilxochitl/Xochipilli complex. *Primeros Memoriales*, approximately 1558–1561. **[CE; interpretation disputed]**

- **Tezcatlipoca — named in the Song of Macuilxochitl.** The hymn refers to “God of ill omen, my lord Tezcatlipoca” in the surviving translation. The narrative relationship is obscure; the source does not furnish a full episode. *Primeros Memoriales*, approximately 1558–1561. **[CE]**

- **Toci/Teteoinnan/Tlazolteotl — possible “grandmother” of the hymn.** The Song of Macuilxochitl addresses a female elder with thigh-skin face paint. Identification with Toci or Teteoinnan is an editorial reconstruction based on costume and the Ochpaniztli complex, not an explicit unambiguous name in the verse. **[MSR]**

- **Xochipilli — explicit alternate identity of Macuilxochitl.** *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, chapter 14, completed 1577, says Macuilxochitl “was also called Xochipilli.” This is identity or fusion, not a meeting between two separate gods. **[CE/SR]**

- **Huitzilopochtli — joint appearance in Durán’s Tlacaxipehualiztli procession.** Impersonators of both gods are brought out and killed in sequence. No personal dialogue or combat is recorded. Durán, approximately 1570s. **[CE]**

- **Quetzalcoatl — joint appearance in the same sacrificial procession.** As above: ritual co-presence, not a narrative relationship. **[CE]**

- **Tonatiuh — joint appearance in the same procession.** The solar impersonator precedes Huitzilopochtli, Quetzalcoatl, and Macuilxochitl in Durán’s account. **[CE]**

- **Ixtlilton — joint appearance in the same procession and iconographic affinity.** Durán places both impersonators in the ritual sequence; Sahaguntine descriptions give Ixtlilton facial features resembling the Macuiltonaleque complex. **[CE/MSR]**

- **Mayahuel — joint appearance in Durán’s procession.** This does not by itself establish kinship with Macuilxochitl or Macuiltochtli. **[CE]**

- **Chililico and Tlacahuepan — joint appearance in Durán’s procession.** The sources provide ritual co-presence but no distinct narrative interaction. **[CE]**

- **Tzitzimime — iconographic/classificatory association.** The Borgia pairings and armed descent led Seler, Pohl, Boone, Graulich, and others to relate Macuiltonaleque and Cihuateteo to the broader category of dangerous stellar beings. This is strong comparative interpretation, not a surviving myth saying that all Ahuiateteo “became” Tzitzimime. **[MSR]**

- **Centzon Totochtin/Ometochtli — Macuiltochtli belongs to the rabbit-pulque complex.** The shared rabbit and intoxication symbolism is secure; his exact rank or kinship within “Four Hundred Rabbits” is not. **[CE/MSR]**

- **Tlaloque — Nicholson notes a close structural relationship among pulque gods, Tlaloque, and Macuiltonaleque.** Recent analysis of stone pulque vessels from Templo Mayor offerings applies this relationship to Macuiltochtli, but it is an iconographic-religious complex, not a recorded meeting. [A 2025 archaeological study summarizes Nicholson’s position and the offering evidence.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C4C3B4B1304E94B79131DECD0FD40FAE/S104566352400052Xa.pdf/vasijas-rituales-para-pulque-analisis-de-un-simbolo-estandarizado-en-el-templo-mayor-de-tenochtitlan.pdf) **[MSR]**

No secure personal interaction was located between the collective and Coyolxauhqui, Tlaltecuhtli, Coatlicue, or Mictlantecuhtli. **[NE]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### No surviving collective origin myth

There is no securely attested Nahua cosmogony in which the five Ahuiateteo are born together, divide the vices among themselves, or receive their offices from a superior god. The modern expectation that every deity must have an origin story exceeds the evidence. **[NE]**

### The drama of pleasure, excess, and punishment

What survives is a ritual logic rather than a continuous tale:

1. Flowers, song, dance, games, food, sex, and pulque are legitimate or valued pleasures.
2. Calendrical “Five” marks a dangerous intensification or tipping point.
3. The same gods who patronize pleasure may impose its consequences: sickness, intoxication, ruin in gambling, social loss, or violent disorder.
4. Fasting, abstinence, offering, invocation, or healing ritual negotiates the danger.

This reconstruction rests on the *Florentine Codex*, *Primeros Memoriales*, Borgia iconography, and Durán. It should not be quoted as an ancient prose myth. **[MSR]**

### The Song of Macuilxochitl

The hymn is the closest surviving narrative-poetic piece. Macuilxochitl speaks as a priest from a place “where flowers stand erect,” associated by editors with Xochitlicacan/Tamoanchan. A female elder, Centeotl, and Tezcatlipoca appear in compressed ritual language. The poem likely accompanied performance and carried meanings intelligible through costume, dance, and ceremonial context now partly lost.

Modern retellings that turn it into a detailed journey from Tamoanchan, a marriage story, or a battle with Tezcatlipoca add narrative connective tissue absent from the manuscript. **[MI]**

### The fifth cup and Cuextecatl

A well-known pulque narrative recounts Cuextecatl drinking beyond the permitted measure and becoming shamefully drunk. It illuminates why five and drunken excess were conceptually linked, but it is not securely a myth starring Macuiltochtli. Modern retellings that have Tezcatlipoca deliberately create or test Macuiltochtli, or call Macuiltochtli “the fifth rabbit born,” conflate separate traditions. **[CE context/MI as Macuiltochtli biography]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Xochilhuitl and the flower fast

According to *Florentine Codex* Book 1:

- The feast was called **Xochilhuitl**, “Flower Feast.”
- Participants fasted for four days.
- Some abstained from chile, ate only at midday, and drank a maize gruel at midnight decorated with a flower.
- Sexual abstinence was compulsory.
- On the feast, images or insignia of the god were worn; offerings, flowers, singing, drumming, and dancing formed the ceremony.
- Violation could bring genital illness; a renewed vow and fast could seek relief.

This is the best-attested formal cultic complex. It belongs especially to Macuilxochitl/Xochipilli and cannot automatically be assigned identically to all five gods. **[CE]**

### Patolli

Durán gives the clearest ritual action: five beans served as dice in honor of Five Flower; players rubbed them, cried “Macuil Xochitl,” clapped, and threw. The *Codex Magliabechiano* depicts the god watching a game. Players could gamble valuables and, according to wider colonial testimony, sometimes property or personal freedom.

The rules are incompletely transmitted. Sahagún and pictorial sources indicate four throwing beans in some accounts, while Durán gives five. Modern boards with fixed movement tables are reconstructions, not complete survivals. [A comparative French summary identifies the four-versus-five discrepancy and the colonial sources.](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patolli) **[CE/RV/MSR]**

### Divination and healing

The Macuiltonaleque could be invoked through the diviner’s five fingers. The Met interprets the class as spiritual guides who possessed or inhabited a ritual specialist’s fingers, enabling prognostication, diagnosis, and matchmaking. [Metropolitan Museum.](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/307606) Ruiz de Alarcón’s healing charm independently confirms their survival in curative invocation. **[CE]**

### Sacrifice

Durán plainly reports the killing and heart extraction of a Macuilxochitl impersonator during Tlacaxipehualiztli. This should neither be suppressed nor inflated:

- It is direct colonial textual testimony to a human victim embodying Macuilxochitl.
- It is not proof of a temple maintained exclusively for the whole five-member group.
- It does not show that venereal disease sufferers were sacrificed.
- It does not establish that every game or flower feast ended in human sacrifice.

The large skull racks excavated near the Templo Mayor in 2015–2017 corroborate the scale and material reality of Mexica human sacrifice generally, but no published identification connects those particular skulls to the Ahuiateteo. **[EA for the archaeological institution; NE for attribution to this group]**

### Archaeology and cult centers

The dossier’s subject lacks a securely excavated standalone “Temple of the Ahuiateteo.” Templo Mayor offerings containing pulque vessels, musical associations, and adjacent deity complexes may bear on Macuiltochtli or Macuilxochitl, but the attribution is interpretive. The Coyolxauhqui Stone discovered in 1978 and the Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered in 2006 transformed understanding of the Templo Mayor precinct, yet neither is evidence for this collective. **[NE]**

Claims that the group lived in a universally accepted “fifth heaven” may reflect colonial cosmographic systematization and modern exhibition language rather than a well-attested cult doctrine. **[LS]**

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before approximately 1400: antecedents

Calendrical gods, flower/maize divinities, pulque-rabbit symbolism, gambling, and dangerous stellar beings have older Mesoamerican histories. Comparisons have been proposed with Maya Akan and *wahy* beings and with Zapotec flower gods. These are comparative antecedents, not proof that a collective named Ahuiateteo existed unchanged in Classic-period Maya or Zapotec religion. **[MSR]**

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1400–1521

The Borgia Group establishes a mature iconography of coefficient-five beings paired with Cihuateteo. Mexica sculpture supplies Macuilcoatl and probably other members of the class. Palace ritual, games, divination, song, and pulque linked such beings to both aristocratic culture and specialist practice. **[EA]**

### 1521–1580s: conquest and ethnographic documentation

Spanish conquest destroyed or repurposed temples, ended state sponsorship, and criminalized Indigenous ceremonies. At the same time, Nahua writers and artists working with Sahagún preserved names, costumes, hymns, and ritual accounts. Durán recorded gaming and sacrificial practices while interpreting them through Christian demonological language.

The resulting sources are indispensable but mediated: neither transparent preconquest transcripts nor mere Spanish fantasies. **[CE]**

### Seventeenth century: survival in curing and “idolatry” prosecutions

Ruiz de Alarcón’s incantation shows that *macuiltonalleque* remained operative in healing speech. This is evidence for adaptation under colonial rule, not necessarily continuity of public priesthoods, temples, or state festivals. **[CE/folkloric survival]**

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: manuscript collecting and antiquarian interpretation

The *Codex Borgia* passed through European collections and became associated with Cardinal Stefano Borgia. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars, especially Eduard Seler, compared its images with colonial descriptions and identified the Macuiltonaleque procession. The group as presented in modern encyclopedias owes much to this reconstructive phase. **[MSR]**

### Twentieth century: pantheon taxonomies

Nicholson’s 1971 typology situated the gods within Central Mexican religious complexes rather than treating every name as an isolated personality. Seler, Nicholson, Spranz, Boone, Taube, Pohl, Graulich, and others variously emphasized calendrics, directionality, solar/warrior character, intoxication, performance, and Tzitzimime affinities.

The fixed formula “five gods of pleasure and excess” is a useful modern condensation, but it obscures the larger family of *macuil-* day powers and the different evidentiary status of each member. **[LS/MSR]**

### Twenty-first century

Digitization of the *Florentine Codex*, *Primeros Memoriales*, and pictorial manuscripts has made comparison of image, Nahuatl text, and translation easier. Museum interpretation now often foregrounds divination and embodiment through the fingers rather than presenting the beings merely as “vice gods.”

No documented continuous institutional cult, priestly lineage, or demographically measurable revival devoted specifically to all five was located. Individual modern Indigenous, Mexicayotl, neopagan, artistic, or personal devotion may exist, but its antiquity and scale cannot be inferred from internet mentions. **[NE]**

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Occult and popular-reference literature

Modern spirit encyclopedias present the Ahuiateteo as “Lords of Excess,” assign them the number five and southern direction, and give readers quasi-devotional warnings about drinking, sex, cigarettes, coffee, and gambling. These works preserve genuine features—five, excess, illness, divination—but recast them as a unified pantheon suitable for contemporary spirit work. An influential example is Judika Illes’s *Encyclopedia of Spirits*, which calls Macuilxochitl their chief and states that the group administers punishment for overindulgence. [A searchable copy reproduces the entry.](https://legacy-eutopia.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Encyclopedia-of-Spirits.pdf) **[MI based partly on ancient evidence]**

The idea that “the fifth cigarette” or “fifth cup of coffee” activates an Aztec rule is plainly modern analogy. **[MI]**

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

No evidence was located for a continuous preconquest-to-present Ahuiateteo cult. Contemporary invocations should therefore be described as revival, reconstruction, or eclectic religious creation rather than uninterrupted survival. **[NE/MSR]**

### Games and commercial culture

Modern *patolli* sets and digital implementations regularly name Macuilxochitl and provide complete rules. The divine patronage is historically based; the exact movement, scoring, and penalty systems often combine Durán, Sahagún, pictorial boards, comparison with other race games, and modern design choices. **[MSR/MI]**

### Common modern inventions

The following claims are unestablished or demonstrably overconfident:

- Each of the five governed one of exactly five deadly vices.
- Macuilcozcacuauhtli was exclusively “the god of gluttony.”
- Macuiltochtli was the fifth child of Mayahuel and Patecatl.
- Tezcatlipoca created Macuiltochtli in a drinking contest.
- The hand over the mouth means “silence,” shame, or a scolding gesture.
- All Ahuiateteo were deformed dwarfs.
- The group had a single chief temple with a securely known priestly hierarchy.
- A surviving ancient rule permitted exactly four cups of pulque and made the fifth intrinsically sinful.
- All modern reconstructions of *patolli* reproduce exact Aztec rules.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Are Ahuiateteo and Macuiltonaleque exact synonyms?

Modern reference works usually say yes. The safer conclusion is that the names overlap substantially but may emphasize different features:

- *Ahuiateteo*: pleasurable or pleasure-related gods.
- *Macuiltonaleque*: calendrical “Five” powers.

The standard five satisfy both descriptions, but the existence of Macuilcoatl and other coefficient-five names suggests that *Macuiltonaleque* could be structurally broader. **[MSR]**

### Exactly five or a larger series?

The iconic Borgia passage contains five paired male figures, supporting a ritual quintet. Yet the full calendrical logic permits more “Five + day sign” beings. Therefore:

- A five-member set is securely represented in a particular almanac.
- A larger class of coefficient-five powers also existed.
- A canonical mythological “family of exactly five” is less secure.

### What does five mean?

Five’s association with excess is well supported by colonial interpretation, intoxication traditions, gambling symbolism, and modern scholarship. But “five always meant evil” is false. Five also structures the center plus four directions, the fingers, throws in games, and calendrical destinations. Its valence depends on context.

### Solar gods, death spirits, or demons?

They can appear youthful and solar, yet also armed, skeletal, punitive, and Tzitzimime-like. Christian colonial words such as *demonio* are the friars’ classification, not a neutral translation of Nahua ontology. “Demon” is therefore inappropriate as their sole class. **[CE colonial polemic/MSR]**

### Xochipilli and Macuilxochitl: one or two?

Sahagún explicitly supplies the alternate name. Iconographic and local variation nevertheless warn against assuming perfect identity in every manuscript. The useful formulation is: **identified or fused in major sixteenth-century Mexica testimony, distinguishable in some analytical and regional contexts**.

### Venereal disease

The source supports genital affliction resulting from broken sexual abstinence. It does not permit retrospective biomedical diagnosis. Some secondary literature expands this into punishment for all sexually excessive behavior; that wider reading is plausible in the Nahua moral-medical complex but exceeds the specific passage.

### Borgia provenance and date

The *Codex Borgia* is certainly a ritual-divinatory Central Mexican manuscript, but whether it was painted in Puebla–Tlaxcala, the Mixteca, or another neighboring region remains debated. Its pages cannot automatically be labeled “Mexica imperial theology.” The manuscript’s old-world provenance before Cardinal Borgia is also incomplete. [The Vatican Library identifies the Borgia manuscript collection with Cardinal Stefano Borgia, while modern catalog histories remain cautious about earlier ownership.](https://www.vaticanlibrary.va/en/the-collections/manuscript-section.html)

### Human sacrifice

Durán’s Macuilxochitl impersonator is specific documentary evidence. The Huei Tzompantli archaeology corroborates state sacrificial practice generally. Neither warrants treating the Ahuiateteo as primarily “bloodthirsty gods” or assigning the Templo Mayor skulls specifically to them. The evidentiary levels must remain separate.

### Primary evidence and repositories

- *Codex Borgia*: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Vatican City.
- *Florentine Codex*: Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence; completed 1577 and digitally published by the Getty.
- *Codices Matritenses/Primeros Memoriales*: manuscript components in Madrid collections; digitally studied through projects such as UNAM’s Tlachia.
- *Codex Magliabechiano*: Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze.
- Macuilcoatl stone standard-bearer: Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, accession 1979.206.1111.
- Much preconquest evidence was destroyed, dispersed, or stripped of original archaeological context, making the surviving corpus radically incomplete.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The strongest reconstruction is modest but substantial. The Ahuiateteo were not merely abstract “sins,” nor are they known through a complete mythology. They were calendrical divine powers materially pictured, ritually embodied, sung to, invoked in gambling and healing, and feared as agents of the bodily and social consequences of excess. Macuilxochitl and Macuiltochtli emerge clearly; the other three remain largely pictorial-calendrical identities. Their fivefold form coexisted with a larger family of coefficient-five beings. The sources document pleasure and punishment as a single sacred field, but the familiar modern chart assigning each god a discrete vice, genealogy, and biography goes well beyond the evidence.
