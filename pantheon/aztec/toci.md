# Toci (Teteo Innan): Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*cihuāteōtl*, female divinity) in Mexica and neighboring Nahua traditions; specifically a maternal, earth, agricultural, medical, midwifery, sweat-bath, weaving, and war-associated deity whose human ritual representative was also called Toci.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely identifiable representations and names belong to the Late Postclassic/early colonial transition. The Ochpaniztli page of the *Codex Borbonicus*—painted either shortly before or within the first decades after the Spanish invasion, its exact dating disputed—depicts the festival’s mother-goddess complex. Unambiguous written identifications occur in sixteenth-century Nahuatl sources: the *Primeros Memoriales* or Matritense materials, collected principally at Tepepulco and Tlatelolco approximately 1558–1565, and the *Florentine Codex*, completed about 1577. No securely proven Classic-period or Teotihuacan image bears Toci’s name. Identifications of older anonymous female sculptures as “Toci” are retrospective scholarly hypotheses, not contemporary attestations.

**Evidence labels used below**

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** pre-Conquest or sixteenth-century Indigenous/pictorial evidence whose identification is reasonably secure.
- **[CE] Colonial canonical elaboration:** sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century narratives compiled after the invasion, frequently from Indigenous testimony but mediated by alphabetic recording and missionary agendas.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** evidence specific to a city, ethnic community, or local cult.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** a colonial or modern attempt to arrange overlapping divine manifestations into a fixed pantheon.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically situated equation between Toci and another deity or saint.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an explicit statement by a primary source.
- **[F] Folklore or later oral tradition.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern claim.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Toci is the Nahua “Our Grandmother,” a formidable maternal divinity rather than merely a benign grandmother. In the best primary description, *Florentine Codex* Book 1, chapter 8, she is **Tēteoh īnnān**, “Mother of the Gods,” also named **Tlālli Īyōllo**, “Heart of the Earth,” and **Toci**, “Our Grandmother.” Physicians, bloodletters, healers, midwives, abortion providers, diviners, and specialists who extracted harmful objects or “worms” from patients venerated her. Book 2 makes her the principal recipient of Ochpaniztli, a harvest-season festival combining sweeping, fertility, weaving, sacrifice, flaying, military display, and renewal. Books 6 and 10 connect her medical aspect with pregnancy, childbirth, and the *temazcalli*, the steam bath.[1][2]

These names designate a closely connected ritual personality, but the sources do not authorize a simple equation of every central Mexican mother or earth goddess with Toci. Nahua divinities could share costume, offices, epithets, and ritual actions without losing all distinctness.

### Classification and boundaries

Nicholson’s influential 1971 typology placed Toci-Teteo Innan within an “earth-mother” complex. This remains useful descriptively but can obscure the fact that Nahua sources distinguish Toci, Coatlicue, Cihuacoatl, Ilamatecuhtli, Tlazolteotl, Xochiquetzal, and the maize goddesses even while allowing them to overlap.[3]

The following popular equations require qualification:

- **Toci = Tlazolteotl:** Calendrical manuscripts and costume analysis show close iconographic and functional overlap, especially through Huastec cotton, rubber facial paint, fertility, medicine, childbirth, and the steam bath. Eduard Seler sometimes described them as identical. Most cautious modern treatments instead speak of convergence or participation in a shared mother-goddess complex. **[LS/MSR]**
- **Toci = Coatlicue:** Both may be “mothers of gods,” but no primary narrative simply makes them the same individual. Coatlicue is specifically Huitzilopochtli’s mother in the Coatepec birth narrative; Toci’s relation to him belongs to a different Culhuacan and Ochpaniztli complex. **[MI if asserted without qualification]**
- **Toci = Tonantzin/Guadalupe:** This is not established by Sahagún. The much clearer colonial transformation is Toci/Tzapotlatenan into, or beneath, the cult of **Saint Anne** near Chiautempan. **[SI]**
- **Toci = a universal prehistoric “Great Goddess”:** This is a modern comparativist construction unsupported by a continuous named record. **[MI/MSR]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

The precise dating of the *Codex Borbonicus* is disputed. Its Indigenous pictorial section may be immediately pre-Conquest; other scholars place the manuscript after 1521 because of its paper, format, annotations, or colonial production circumstances. Its Ochpaniztli page remains among the earliest extant visual records of the ritual complex, but using it as an unquestionably pre-1521 witness would overstate the evidence.

The first indisputable alphabetic naming comes from Sahagún’s project:

1. **Primeros Memoriales / Codices Matritenses**, principally Nahuatl, approximately 1558–1565, drawing on Indigenous specialists at Tepepulco and later Tlatelolco. These preserve deity costumes, hymns, festival outlines, and calendrical material. **[EA]**
2. **Florentine Codex**, Nahuatl and Spanish, completed around 1577. The manuscript is Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Mediceo Palatino 218–220. Relevant portions include:
   - Book 1, chapter 8: names, domains, worshippers, and costume.
   - Book 2, chapter 30: the extended Ochpaniztli ritual.
   - Book 6: midwives’ speeches and invocations during childbirth.
   - Book 10: medical practitioners and steam-bath practice.
   - Book 11’s appendix: Toci/Tzapotlatenan and the conversion of her sanctuary into a cult of Saint Anne.[1][2]
3. **Codex Borbonicus**, pictorial with colonial annotations, early sixteenth century: Ochpaniztli imagery and festival sequence. **[EA; date disputed]**
4. **Codex Magliabechiano**, approximately mid-sixteenth century, derived from an earlier pictorial prototype: steam-bath deity imagery, commonly labeled Yoalticitl or Temazcalteci, and calendrical-religious commentary. **[CE]**
5. **Codex Telleriano-Remensis**, mid-sixteenth century: calendrical mother-goddess imagery and commentary. Identifications among Teteoinnan, Tlazolteotl, and related goddesses require iconographic argument rather than reliance on modern captions.[4]
6. **Codex Mendoza**, approximately 1541–1542: invaluable for tribute, costume, conquest history, and social institutions, but not a principal narrative source for Toci. It supplies the political-economic setting rather than a full Toci theology.
7. **Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites***, composed approximately 1570s–1581: Ochpaniztli, the Culhua princess narrative, Toci’s sanctuary, and warfare involving her cult. Durán drew on Indigenous informants and a lost pictorial/chronicle tradition often called the “Crónica X,” but also imposed Christian polemic and his own explanatory structure. **[CE]**
8. **Codex Aubin, *Crónica Mexicayotl*, Codex Azcatitlan, and related migration histories:** varying versions of the Culhuacan episode. Their dates and dependencies differ; not every version names the woman Toci.
9. **Anales de Tlatelolco**, earliest stratum often dated about 1528: an exceptionally early Nahuatl migration and dynastic source. It is important for comparing the Culhuacan sequence, but its treatment is not identical to Durán’s fully developed Toci story.[5]
10. **Cantares Mexicanos**, songs recorded principally in the later sixteenth century: the so-called “Hymn to the Mother of the Gods” invokes Teteo Innan in dense ritual language. Editorial reconstruction and translation are difficult, and the date of the performed tradition cannot simply be equated with the date of the surviving manuscript.
11. **Histoyre du Mechique**, French manuscript based on sixteenth-century Spanish material, and the **Leyenda de los Soles**, copied in Nahuatl in 1558: crucial for central Mexican cosmogony but not primary full-length witnesses to Toci’s festival.
12. **Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, surviving in the *Codex Chimalpopoca*, copied in the seventeenth century from earlier materials: useful for comparative Nahua history but not a principal Toci source.
13. **Borgia Group manuscripts**—*Codex Borgia*, *Cospi*, *Vaticanus B*, *Fejérváry-Mayer*, and *Laud*—probably produced in central or south-central Mexico around the Late Postclassic/early colonial boundary: they contain cognate divinatory female figures. Assigning an image specifically to Toci rather than Tlazolteotl or another earth-mother manifestation is often disputed. **[MSR]**

### What is not attested early

No surviving pre-Conquest alphabetic inscription says “Toci.” No named Toci temple has been archaeologically identified at Tenochtitlan with an uncontested dedicatory inscription. A sculpture stylistically assigned to Teotihuacan has been published as Toci, but because “Toci” is a much later Nahuatl name and the sculpture lacks a contemporary identifying text, the designation is an iconographic hypothesis.[6]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

Toci’s securely attested domains form an interconnected complex:

- **Earth:** As Tlalli Iyollo she is the earth’s heart or interior vitality. **[EA]**
- **Agriculture and harvest renewal:** Ochpaniztli occurred as maize matured and linked the sacrificed mother with Cinteotl, young maize. **[EA/MSR on precise agricultural symbolism]**
- **Medicine:** Physicians, bloodletters, eye healers, purgers, and specialists treating bodily intrusions worshipped her. **[EA]**
- **Midwifery and childbirth:** Midwives invoked her; childbirth was conceived as battle, with women dying in labor receiving warrior-like status. **[EA]**
- **Steam bath:** The *temazcalli* served obstetric, therapeutic, and purification purposes; Toci/Temazcalteci/Yoalticitl presided over it. **[CE]**
- **Textile production:** Her unspun cotton, spindles, and weaving implements connect her with paradigmatic female labor. **[EA]**
- **War:** Her festival culminated in captive sacrifice and a staged military chase or battle; an unhappy ixiptla portended soldiers’ deaths and deaths in childbirth. **[EA]**
- **Divination:** Book 1 includes maize-counters, cord-diviners, water-gazers, and other prognosticators among her devotees. **[EA]**

### Recognizing Toci

The *Florentine Codex* and related pictorial sources give her:

- a black rubber-painted mouth or lip region;
- a dark circular cheek mark;
- an **ichcaxochitl**, “cotton flower,” a headband of raw, unspun cotton;
- inserted spindles and spindle whorls;
- a rear ornament or bundle of palm leaves, **zoyatemalli**;
- a skirt decorated with shells or stars, **citlalcueitl**;
- sandals;
- a golden or yellow shield;
- a broom, **izquiztli**, the emblematic implement of Ochpaniztli, “Sweeping”;
- in festival embodiment, the flayed skin of the sacrificed woman.

These elements vary between manuscripts. A cotton headdress, rubber paint, and spindle equipment also characterize Tlazolteotl and other female figures, so no single trait independently proves identity.[4]

The Huastec link is explicit in the ritual costume: the ixiptla’s escorts dressed as Huastecs; cotton, palm, rubber, nudity/fertility imagery, spindle equipment, and coastal costume evoked the Gulf Coast. That does not prove that the whole Toci cult was simply imported from the Huasteca. It demonstrates that Mexica ritual deliberately coded part of her manifestation as Huastec. The stronger origin claim is a modern reconstruction.[4]

### Colors, numbers, animals, and celestial bodies

The record does **not** securely assign Toci a unique sacred color, planet, weekday, or fixed magical number. Black rubber paint, white raw cotton, gold/yellow equipment, and the star skirt are costume facts, not necessarily an abstract color system. Quail were decapitated before her during Ochpaniztli, but this does not make the quail her exclusive sacred animal. Claims that Toci “rules” a particular modern weekday, planet, crystal, tarot card, or zodiac sign are **[MI]** unless explicitly presented as modern ritual innovation.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical structures

Toci’s title “Mother of the Gods” is a status statement; the sources do not provide a stable list of divine children.

The clearest child relation is **Cinteotl/Centeotl**, the maize deity. During Ochpaniztli, the flayed Toci ixiptla goes to fetch “her son, Cinteotl or Itztlacoliuhqui.” A piece of the woman’s thigh skin became the mask worn by Cinteotl’s impersonator. The double naming likely marks a ritual transformation rather than a universally fixed genealogical identity.[2]

Her relationship with **Huitzilopochtli** is deliberately unstable:

- In Durán’s migration account, Huitzilopochtli orders the Mexica to request the Culhua ruler’s daughter, sacrifice and flay her, and invite her father to worship her. Huitzilopochtli “takes” her as mother and wife in different formulations. **[CE]**
- During Ochpaniztli, Toci proceeds toward Huitzilopochtli’s temple and participates in rites staged within his political-sacred precinct. **[EA]**
- This is not the same genealogy as the Coatepec myth, in which **Coatlicue** gives birth to Huitzilopochtli. The traditions should not be harmonized into “Toci-Coatlicue was always his mother.”

No securely attested husband, parents, or complete sibling set belongs uniquely to Toci.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Culhua princess and the making of Toci

**Fullest developed version: Durán, late sixteenth century. [CE]**

During the Mexica migration, the Mexica live under Culhua authority. Huitzilopochtli orders them to ask Achitometl, ruler of Culhuacan, for his daughter, ostensibly so that she may be joined to the god. After receiving her, they kill and flay her. A male priest or noble wears her skin. The Mexica invite her father to honor his daughter as a goddess. In the dark temple he burns incense; when the brazier illuminates the skin-clad figure, he recognizes the act and reacts in horror. The Culhua attack or expel the Mexica. The woman thereafter becomes the goddess Toci, “Our Grandmother,” mother of the gods.

The episode operates simultaneously as:

- a myth of Toci’s cultic manifestation;
- an explanation of Mexica expulsion from Culhuacan;
- a charter for human flaying;
- a violent claim to Culhua sacred and dynastic legitimacy;
- a model for the annual Ochpaniztli transformation.

The story is not uniform across migration sources. Early annals may give the Culhuacan conflict without Durán’s entire theological drama. Names of rulers, chronology, and the woman’s precise status vary. Some later retellings state categorically that the event occurred in 1343; such precision comes from reconciling annalistic chronologies and should not be treated as an independently verified year.

David Carrasco and other historians have interpreted the episode as “mythic history”: it may preserve memories of political conflict and affinal diplomacy, but its surviving form is a charter for Mexica ritual sovereignty. The claim that a recoverable historical princess literally became the goddess cannot be tested from present evidence.

### Ochpaniztli: death, skin, maize, and war

**Earliest extensive witness:** Sahagún’s Nahuatl festival account, based on mid-sixteenth-century testimony about pre-Conquest practice. **[EA]**

Ochpaniztli means approximately “Sweeping” or “the Sweeping of the Roads.” It was counted as the eleventh *veintena* in Sahagún’s scheme, though colonial sources differ in correlating the eighteen festivals with European dates.

The central ixiptla—a living embodiment, not merely an actress—was an enslaved or captive woman adorned as Teteo Innan/Toci. For days women healers and midwives attended her. Female performers, including *ahuianime*, kept her cheerful; her sadness or tears were a bad omen predicting deaths of warriors or women in childbirth.

The festival unfolded through:

1. **Sweeping and abstention:** Roads and sacred spaces were swept. The broom expressed purification, preparation, and the opening of the earth/agricultural cycle.
2. **Female ritual and mock combat:** Women healers and midwives divided into groups and fought with leaves, flowers, grasses, or other non-lethal projectiles. This was not mere entertainment: childbirth, medicine, agriculture, and warfare were conceptually joined.
3. **The Huastec escort:** Men in Huastec costume accompanied the ixiptla, wearing raw cotton and spindle-related insignia. Their role encoded coastal fertility and textile associations while also participating in Mexica stereotyping of the Huastecs.
4. **Deception and silence:** The woman was not told openly that she would die. The killing occurred at night and in silence. Sahagún’s Spanish account says her mouth was held or kept from crying out. Modern retellings that confidently describe a long, conscious “sawing” of the neck often dramatize ambiguous colonial wording and should be treated cautiously.
5. **Decapitation and flaying:** The victim was killed, decapitated, and immediately flayed. A strong male priest put on her skin and thereby became the renewed Toci ixiptla.
6. **Cinteotl’s birth/appearance:** Skin from a thigh became a mask for the impersonator of Cinteotl, Toci’s son. This material transformation joined the death of the mature mother-earth with the emergence of maize.
7. **Captive sacrifice:** The skin-clad Toci killed four captives by opening their breasts; other priests killed additional captives. Quail and incense were also offered.[2]
8. **Arming Toci:** The ixiptla received headdress, shield, broom, and martial equipment. Her Huastec attendants followed.
9. **Procession and pursuit:** Toci’s party advanced, retreated, and engaged in a staged battle or rout. Warriors pursued the figure toward Tocititlan, outside or at the southern edge of the city.
10. **Doffing the skin:** At Tocititlan the priest removed the human skin. Costumes were hung or deposited, and the cycle ended.

Interpretations vary. Agricultural readings stress the husking or “flaying” of maize and renewal of the earth. Political readings emphasize the movement from a peripheral/local maternal power into Tenochtitlan’s Huitzilopochtli-dominated center and back to a boundary sanctuary. Gender studies emphasize the deliberate male embodiment of an aged female deity and the parallelism between childbirth and warfare. None of these interpretations cancels the others, but the sources themselves do not supply a single explanatory key.

### The hymn to the Mother of the Gods

The Nahuatl sacred song traditionally titled “Hymn to the All-Mother” or “Hymn to Teteo Innan” survives through Sahaguntine materials and later editions. It evokes the mother, the earth, divine birth, plants, deer, and charged landscape in archaic or specialized ritual language. Several lines remain philologically obscure. Daniel Brinton’s nineteenth-century English version is historically important but relies on now-dated linguistic and comparativist assumptions.[7]

### Toci, earthquakes, and the earth’s heart

Durán says the name “Heart of the Earth” referred to the cause of earthquakes. This is a documented colonial explanation, not an independently confirmed pre-Conquest myth. No surviving extended narrative depicts Toci producing a particular earthquake. **[CE]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Tenochtitlan and Tocititlan

Sahagún and Durán describe **Tocititlan**, “Place of Toci,” as the location where the flayed skin and Huastec costumes were removed at the end of Ochpaniztli. Historical-geographical work places it toward the southern approaches of Mexico-Tenochtitlan, associated with the Iztapalapa causeway, Xoloco, Acachinanco, or a nearby liminal waterside zone. The precise location remains disputed.

A proposed identification with structures near the Pino Suárez metro area is not secure. The archaeological complex there includes a round temple generally assigned to Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl, and no dedicatory evidence proves that it was Toci’s Tocititlan. Alessandra Pecci and related scholarship stress that colonial descriptions place Toci’s modest boundary sanctuary farther south than some proposed monumental identifications.[8]

### Tlatelolco

Toci’s record is inseparable from Tlatelolco in two senses:

- Sahagún’s mature research was produced with Nahua scholars at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco.
- Reconstructions of the island’s sacred geography include temples of Toci and the Cihuateocalli within or between the Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco urban systems.[9]

However, a uniquely “Tlatelolca Toci” with a securely excavated, inscribed temple and a separate mythology cannot presently be reconstructed. Maps assigning a “Temple of Toci” depend heavily on colonial descriptions and twentieth-century urban reconstruction. This is a case where textual likelihood exceeds archaeological certainty.

### Chiautempan and the Tlaxcala-area sanctuary

In the *Florentine Codex* Book 11 appendix, Sahagún reports a celebrated sanctuary near the Sierra de Tlaxcala called Toci. People traveled long distances to it. He states that she was also called Tzapotlatenan and was goddess of steam baths and medicine. Franciscans established a church of **Saint Anne**, grandmother of Christ, at or near the indigenous sacred place. Preachers used the Nahuatl name Toci for Saint Anne because both were “grandmother,” and Sahagún suspected that pilgrims continued honoring the older deity beneath the Christian designation.[10]

Later accounts locate this transformation at **Santa Ana Chiautempan** and attribute the early foundation to Martín de Valencia. Present traditions around Saint Anne and ritual foods such as *mole prieto* are locally said to continue Toci’s memory. The continuity claim has genuine colonial roots but should not be interpreted as proof that every present custom descends unchanged from the pre-Conquest cult.[11]

### Practitioners and offices

Toci was patron of:

- midwives;
- women managing pregnancy and childbirth;
- physicians and herbal specialists;
- bloodletters and specialists using obsidian;
- eye healers;
- purgers;
- diviners using maize kernels, cords, water, and books;
- practitioners said to remove intrusive objects or worms;
- proprietors or attendants of steam baths;
- weavers and women working cotton.

The sources describe a professional constituency rather than a single centralized priesthood. Ochpaniztli nevertheless required state-controlled temple priests, victim keepers, impersonators, musicians, warriors, and high-ranking officials.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Documented offerings included incense, quail, food, textiles, paper regalia, rubber pigment, and human lives. The principal Toci ixiptla was female; the priest wearing her skin was male. Captives killed afterward were normally male warriors.

The human sacrifice cannot responsibly be dismissed as Spanish invention. The ritual’s detailed Nahuatl testimony, Indigenous pictorial evidence, cut-marked remains from Mexica contexts, and the Huei Tzompantli excavations collectively establish that state sacrifice and display of human heads occurred. Between 2015 and 2017, INAH excavations at Guatemala Street identified part of Tenochtitlan’s Huei Tzompantli and a skull tower; continuing osteological work confirms women as well as men and children among the dead.[12][13]

This evidence is contextual rather than Toci-specific: no published osteological method can presently assign an individual skull from the Huei Tzompantli to Ochpaniztli or to Toci. The archaeology validates the larger sacrificial institution, not every numerical claim or every detail in a colonial narrative.

### “Temple prostitution”

No credible primary evidence establishes an institution properly described as “temple prostitution” in Toci’s cult. The *ahuianime* who entertained the ixiptla and the sexualized colonial descriptions of Huastecs do not by themselves constitute such an institution. Applying that label is **[MI]** unless tightly defined and independently evidenced.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the fourteenth century: unattested by name

Older Mesoamerican cultures possessed aged female, earth, fertility, weaving, healing, and steam-bath divinities. Archaeologists have proposed antecedents at Teotihuacan and on the Gulf Coast. Without contemporary names, these are possible genealogies of imagery, not attestations of Toci. **[MSR]**

### Fourteenth-century mythic history

Mexica chronicles place Toci’s emergence during the stay at Culhuacan, before Tenochtitlan’s foundation. The princess’s sacrifice explains both the rupture with Culhuacan and the appropriation of ancient Culhua legitimacy. This is traditional chronology, recorded only after the invasion. **[CE]**

### Fifteenth to early sixteenth centuries: imperial cult

Under the Mexica state, Ochpaniztli became an elaborate capital festival integrating agricultural renewal, female medical corporations, tribute wealth, captive sacrifice, military hierarchy, and the sacred geography of Tenochtitlan. The Huastec costume points to imperial incorporation and ritual representation of the Gulf Coast.

Durán also reports a regional Toci festival among Tlaxcalans and conflict in which Huexotzinca attackers burned her sanctuary. This should be read as evidence that Toci was not confined to Tenochtitlan, although Durán’s chronology and rhetoric require caution.

### 1519–1521: war, epidemic, and destruction of the state cult

The Spanish-indigenous war, siege of Tenochtitlan, epidemic disease, and collapse of the imperial temple administration ended public Ochpaniztli in its state form. Temples were demolished, filled, reused, or covered by Christian construction. No evidence indicates that the full public flaying-and-captive-sacrifice rite survived legally after conquest.

### 1520s–1570s: suppression, translation, and covert continuity

Missionaries destroyed divine images and prosecuted “idolatry,” while simultaneously recording Indigenous knowledge. Nahua scholars translated Toci’s cult into alphabetic Nahuatl and painted it in the manuscripts now constituting its principal archive.

At Chiautempan, substitution by Saint Anne created a durable semantic bridge: Christian grandmother and indigenous “Our Grandmother.” Sahagún regarded continued pilgrimage as suspicious precisely because Indigenous devotees preserved the place and name.[10]

### Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries

Toci ceased to be a publicly institutional deity, but names and associations persisted in manuscripts, local memory, healing customs, steam-bath practice, and Saint Anne devotion. European and Mexican antiquarians increasingly treated her as a member of an “Aztec pantheon,” often flattening distinct goddesses into a universal earth mother.

Seler’s late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century iconographic studies systematically compared Teteo Innan with Tlazolteotl and Borgia-group figures. His philology and visual comparisons remain foundational, but his equations can be more categorical than current evidence supports.[3]

### Twentieth century

Critical editions transformed the evidence:

- Anderson and Dibble’s twelve-volume English translation of the *Florentine Codex* made the Nahuatl text widely accessible.
- Nicholson’s 1971 typology situated Toci in an earth/fertility deity complex.
- López Austin emphasized the organization of the body, earth, medicine, and divine complexes.
- Michel Graulich developed a strongly structural and seasonal reading of the festivals.
- Elizabeth Hill Boone clarified the operation of divinatory manuscripts and warned against reading pictorial cycles as Western-style narrative.
- Davíd Carrasco and others examined sacrifice, urban space, and imperial power.
- Inga Clendinnen analyzed ritual performance and gender, memorably describing the doubled sexual embodiment of the flayed female skin and male wearer.
- Cecilia Klein and other scholars brought gender, costume, and political hierarchy to the center of interpretation.

### Twenty-first century

Digital publication of the *Florentine Codex* has allowed direct comparison of Nahuatl, Spanish, and paintings.[1] Archaeological work on the sacred precinct, including the Huei Tzompantli, has reinforced the material reality of sacrifice while permitting more careful demographic study.[12][13]

Toci persists in scholarship, museum interpretation, Chiautempan civic identity, Indigenous cultural revival, online devotional experiments, and popular “Aztec mythology.” These are not all the same phenomenon and do not constitute an unbroken organized priesthood.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Local religious memory

The strongest case for post-Conquest continuity is Chiautempan’s association of Toci with Saint Anne. Local histories, foods, festivities, and civic representations remember the pre-Hispanic deity. The present practice is Catholic and regional, shaped by five centuries of transformation; calling it simply the unchanged worship of Toci would erase that history.[11]

### Mexicayotl and reconstructionist devotion

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century Mexicayotl and Indigenous-revival groups sometimes invoke Toci or Teteo Innan in ceremonies, educational work, dance, or online devotion. There is no reliable census isolating Toci devotees, nor evidence for institutional continuity from an Ochpaniztli priesthood. Reconstructions based on Sahagún, Durán, modern Nahuatl, and political Indigenous identity are **modern revivals**, not frauds but not untouched survivals.

Any revival of Ochpaniztli without killing or flaying necessarily creates symbolic substitutes—cloth, maize husks, flowers, dance, paper, or dramatic performance. Such substitutions are modern religious choices.

### Occult and New Age material

The dedicated search found no evidence that Toci had a significant documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Theosophical Society, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or the principal European grimoire traditions. Claims assigning her to tarot paths, chakras, crystals, planetary hours, “dark goddess” initiations, or a universal “triple goddess” system are modern inventions unless their modern author and date are explicitly supplied.

Likewise:

- “Toci was the Aztec Gaia” is a modern analogy.
- “Toci is Coatlicue’s crone aspect” generally imports a modern maiden-mother-crone scheme.
- “Toci was worshipped continuously in secret covens” is unsupported.
- Internet lists giving her fixed colors, incense recipes, gemstones, or moon phases usually synthesize modern pagan correspondences rather than translate a Nahua source.

### Popular culture

Toci is less prominent in global fiction and games than Quetzalcoatl, Tezcatlipoca, Huitzilopochtli, or Coatlicue. Her modern image is consequently shaped more by museum displays, mythology dictionaries, websites, and festival reconstructions than by a single dominant film or novel. Modern artwork frequently merges her with Tlazolteotl or Coatlicue; such works document reception, not ancient identity.

### Political uses

The Culhua-princess story has been read as a narrative of Mexica brutality, religious devotion, gendered violence, political marriage, or the appropriation of Toltec-Culhua legitimacy. Colonial authors used sacrifice as evidence for the necessity of conversion; modern nationalist presentations have alternately glorified the rite as ancestral resolve or minimized it as Spanish propaganda. Neither pole substitutes for the converging textual and archaeological evidence.

No well-documented, significant modern extremist movement centered specifically on Toci emerged from the dedicated search. General nationalist appropriation of Mexica imagery should not automatically be attributed to her.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Toci a distinct goddess or an aspect?

The primary sources explicitly join Toci, Teteo Innan, and Tlalli Iyollo. Beyond that nucleus, identities become contextual. Tlazolteotl and Temazcalteci share major features, but “aspect” is partly a modern classificatory term. Nahua religion permitted divine beings to merge through costume, naming, ixiptla embodiment, calendrical position, and ritual action without requiring a single permanent identity.

### Huastec origin

**Evidence for a Huastec connection:**

- Huastec-costumed escorts at Ochpaniztli;
- raw cotton, spindle, palm, and rubber insignia;
- overlap with Tlazolteotl, whose Gulf Coast associations are strong;
- Mexica descriptions of Huastecs as fertile, sexually marked, and distinctive in dress.

**Limits:**

- The name Toci is Nahuatl.
- The Culhuacan origin myth is Basin-of-Mexico political theology.
- The sources do not state that the complete cult was imported on a known date from a named Huastec city.

The defensible conclusion is that Toci’s imperial form incorporated conspicuous Huastec/coastal codes; a simple foreign-origin claim remains **[MSR]**.

### The Culhua princess: event or charter myth?

One position treats the episode as a transformed memory of a political marriage and violent rupture. Another treats it mainly as ritual charter myth. The sources are too late and ideologically charged to identify the woman historically. The event may combine history and legend, but the dossier can securely establish only that sixteenth-century Mexica traditions narrated it.

### Borbonicus dating

If the *Codex Borbonicus* is pre-Conquest, its Ochpaniztli image is Toci’s earliest surviving secure pictorial attestation. If early colonial, it remains approximately contemporary with the first generation after conquest. Its evidentiary value is high under either dating, but its use as an untouched pre-Hispanic manuscript remains disputed.

### Toci and Tonantzin/Guadalupe

A popular theory identifies the Tepeyac deity Tonantzin directly as Toci and presents Guadalupe as her transparent replacement. The evidence is weaker than often claimed:

- *Tonantzin* is a title, “Our Revered Mother.”
- Sahagún discusses Tonantzin at Tepeyac and Toci/Saint Anne near Tlaxcala as separate geographical cases.
- The *Codex Teotenantzin*’s female figures have been variously identified as Tonantzin-Toci-Teteo Innan, Chicomecoatl, Ilamatecuhtli, Chalchiuhtlicue, or generic fertility/water beings; its imagery may itself be antiquarian reconstruction.[14]

A broad historical pattern of Marian substitution and Indigenous reinterpretation is secure. A one-to-one Toci-to-Guadalupe identity is not.

### Sacrifice and colonial bias

Missionary sources are hostile and must not be read naively. Nevertheless, the Nahuatl column of the *Florentine Codex*, Indigenous pictorial manuscripts, multiple chronicle traditions, and archaeology agree on the existence of human sacrifice, decapitation, flaying, and skull display. Debate properly concerns frequency, ritual variation, political meaning, and the accuracy of particular descriptions—not whether all sacrifice was invented.

### Archaeological identifications

The discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone in 1978 and the Tlaltecuhtli monolith in 2006 transformed understanding of the Templo Mayor, earth imagery, dismemberment, offerings, and imperial sacred space. Neither monument depicts Toci by secure inscription. They illuminate her cultural setting but should not be relabeled as Toci.

The Sun Stone likewise contributes to Mexica calendrical and cosmological study but is not an image of Toci. Including famous monuments as if every one directly attested her would manufacture evidence.

### State of the archive

The principal manuscript survives in Florence; related Sahaguntine materials are in Madrid. Other codices are dispersed among Paris, Oxford, Rome, Florence, Liverpool, Mexico City, and other collections. The survival pattern is colonial: many pre-Conquest ritual books and temple objects were destroyed, while surviving manuscripts were collected, annotated, reorganized, and carried abroad.

Digitization has reduced dependence on nineteenth-century copies and isolated illustrations. It also exposes how often popular iconography has detached an image from its folio, caption, language, and ritual sequence.
