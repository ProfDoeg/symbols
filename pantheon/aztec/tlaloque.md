# The Tlaloque: Research Dossier

**Class:** A collective or host of animate rain, storm, mountain, and agricultural powers—usually subordinate or complementary manifestations, assistants, or plural counterparts of Tlaloc, but sometimes treated as locally individualized gods. Classical Nahuatl singular/plural boundaries are not equivalent to a modern distinction between “one god” and “his servants.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest evidence specifically reconstructable as a directional plurality of Tlaloc-like rain beings is pictorial: the Late Postclassic, probably pre-Conquest *Codex Borgia*, especially pages 27–28, conventionally dated approximately 1450–1521. It depicts four directional and one central rain-god manifestations distributing different rains and harvest outcomes. Because the manuscript has no alphabetic gloss naming them *Tlaloque*, identification of these figures with the named collective depends on comparison with sixteenth-century Nahuatl and Spanish texts. The earliest secure alphabetic attestations of *tlālohqueh/tlaloque* belong to the first colonial decades, most importantly the mid-sixteenth-century Sahaguntine materials and the *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*. Earlier Mesoamerican “Tlaloc-like” images, extending back at least to Teotihuacan, establish the antiquity of a rain-deity iconographic complex, but not the Nahuatl name *Tlaloque* or the exact Mexica collective documented after 1500.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Definition

**[Securely attested Late Postclassic/early colonial]** The Tlaloque are the plural rain powers to whom clouds, ordinary rain, hail, mist, lightning, thunder, and destructive storms “come to an end”—that is, in whose agency these phenomena culminate. The Nahuatl text excerpted in the *Florentine Codex* tradition says:

> *In iehoantin, in moteneoa tlaloque: intech tlamiloia in mixtli, in quiahuitl, in tecihuitl, in cepayauitl, in ayauitl, in tlapetlaniliztli, in tlatlatziniliztli, in teuitequiliztli.*

“They are those called the Tlaloque: with them end/are completed cloud, rain, hail, snow or cold rain, mist, lightning, thunder, and the striking of thunderbolts.” The lexical record is preserved in the [Nahuatl Dictionary](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/node/201456).

They were not merely “weather sprites.” Collectively they joined meteorology, mountains, caves, springs, stored maize, fertility, crop failure, disease, death by water or lightning, and the paradisal afterlife called Tlalocan. Depending on context, a mountain itself, an image of a mountain, a rain-god figure, a dead child assimilated to a rain power, and a named occupational or local patron could all participate in the category.

### Names and grammar

- **Tlālohqueh / Tlālōque / Tlaloque**: normalized Classical Nahuatl plural, conventionally “the Tlalocs” or “rain gods.” Sixteenth-century spelling usually omits vowel length and final glottal features. Frances Karttunen records the plural form *tlālohqueh*; modern databases translate it as a host of rain deities resembling Tlaloc. [The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/tlaloque/69199) documents uses from the *Florentine Codex*, Hernando Ruiz de Alarcón, and later lexicography.
- **Tlāloc / Tlalloc**: singular or chief rain power. Older scholarship often treated *Tlaloque* simply as the regular plural of a personal name, “the Tlalocs.” Other analyses understand *Tlaloc* as a singular back-formation or privileged member of a broader class.
- **Āhuahqueh / ahuaque**: “owners/possessors of water,” a partly overlapping term prominent in colonial and modern central Mexican traditions. It must not automatically be substituted for every pre-Conquest *Tlaloque*.
- **Tepictoton**: “Small Molded Ones” or “little formed/modelled things,” from forms of *tepi-/tepictli* associated with molding or shaping plus the diminutive plural. Sahaguntine evidence describes miniature mountain figures made of amaranth dough. The term denotes ritual mountain embodiments and overlaps with, but is not necessarily a universal synonym for, all Tlaloque. The [Nahuatl Dictionary entry](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/tepictoton) traces the word to the *Primeros Memoriales*.
- **Tepēhua / Tepēhuahqueh**, “owners of the mountains,” and expressions translated as “mountain gods” belong to the same semantic field, but the colonial corpora do not consistently collapse them into one taxonomic class.
- **Tlamacazqueh**, “givers/providers” or “those who will give something”: applied both to priests and rain powers. The shared designation expressed reciprocal provision—humans gave offerings; mountain-rain beings gave water, seed, and food. This interpretation is discussed in Guilhem Olivier’s work on Nahua ritual substitution and divine embodiment.
- **Quiauhteoh**, “rain gods,” is a descriptive category, not necessarily a proper collective name.

### Etymology

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** No etymology of *Tlaloc* commands universal agreement.

1. A traditional analysis derives it from *tlālli*, “earth, land, soil,” plus an obscure suffix; hence “earth-covered one,” “one made of earth,” or “he who is of the earth.” INAH’s Templo Mayor guide cautiously gives “probably ‘He who is made of earth’” ([INAH museum guide](https://templomayor.inah.gob.mx/images/home/tripticos/museo.pdf)).
2. Thelma Sullivan proposed an older form resembling *tlalloqui*, “covered/full of earth,” with *tlalloque* as its plural and *Tlalloc* as a later shortened singular.
3. Popular renderings such as “nectar of the earth,” often obtained by combining *tlalli* with *octli* (“pulque”), are folk etymologies and are not established by historical morphology.
4. The morphology of *tlālohqueh* has led some linguists to ask whether “Tlaloc” was historically extracted from a plural or relational expression. This remains a linguistic proposal, not a documented Mexica theological doctrine.

### Named members and local manifestations

Colonial sources associate numerous named powers with the Tlaloque, but no dependable early source supplies the tidy modern chart in which four named gods each control exactly one compass point and exactly one weather type.

- **Nappatecuhtli**, “Four-times Lord” or “Lord of the Four [Directions]”: patron of mat-makers and reeds, and a rain/water power in Sahagún, *Florentine Codex* Book 1, chapter 20. His position among the Tlaloque is well attested; the modern assignment “the Tlaloque of hail” is less secure.
- **Opochtli**, “the Left-Handed One”: patron of fishers and lake hunters, inventor of fishing devices, and a deity of the aquatic complex in Sahagún.
- **Yauhquemeh/Yauhqueme**, usually interpreted in relation to the *yauhtli* plant and its ritual insignia: a named rain or mountain power in colonial lists.
- **Tomiyauhtecuhtli**: a deity combining rain, mountain, and pulque associations; classification varies by source.
- **Amimitl** and **Atlahua**: hunting and aquatic patrons sometimes placed within the broader Tlaloc complex.
- **Huixtocihuatl**, goddess of salt water and salt workers: described as Tlaloc’s elder sister in Sahagún, not securely as one of a fixed four.
- Individual sacred mountains—Mount Tlaloc, Iztaccihuatl, Popocatepetl, Matlalcueye, Yauhqueme, Tepepolco, and others—could be treated as divine persons or as seats and embodiments of rain beings.

**[Modern systematization]** The frequently repeated roster “Opochtli, Nappatecuhtli, Yauhqueme, Tomiyauhtecuhtli,” each assigned to a cardinal direction and to rain, hail, thunder, or drought, is not presented as such in the principal sixteenth-century sources. It appears to combine independent Sahaguntine deity notices with the four-vessel cosmology. It should be treated as a modern harmonization unless an edition identifies a specific primary passage.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

**Codex Borgia, pages 27–28 — approximately 1450–1521, central Mexico.**  
**[Secure pictorial evidence; interpretation partly reconstructed]** Page 27 places rain-god figures in four quarters and a center. The black figure sends crop-nourishing rain; the yellow is associated with destructive conditions and insects; the blue waters productive ground; the red accompanies crop loss to rodents; the central striped figure releases water marked by skeletal, martial, or sacrificial signs. Page 28 develops a related rain-and-maize sequence. The schema corresponds closely to the colonial account of multiple jars or rooms, but the manuscript itself does not alphabetically say “Tlaloque.” Modern readings ultimately descend from Eduard Seler and Karl Anton Nowotny; Elizabeth Hill Boone stresses that the manuscript is a divinatory and ritual book rather than a Western-style narrative. A recent archaeological study summarizes both the Borgia configuration and the textual parallel ([Ancient Mesoamerica study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/from-simple-row-of-dots-to-the-rain-god-calendar-interpretation-of-the-pecked-cross-petroglyph-from-the-late-postclassic-tetzcotzinco/2E5526CF3AE4AA581A063FCF3E8DFD01)).

**Archaeological rain-god tradition — earlier than the named collective.**  
“Goggle-eyed” storm-deity images occur at Teotihuacan and elsewhere many centuries before the Mexica Empire. Nicholson’s 1971 typology placed Tlaloc within a long central Mexican iconographic tradition. Continuity is probable at the level of rain-and-fertility symbolism; continuity of name, myths, institutions, or a four-member collective is not demonstrable. Calling every earlier goggle-eyed image “Tlaloc” is an archaeological convention, not an ancient label.

### Principal primary corpus

- **Codex Borgia**: pre-Conquest or Contact-era ritual-divinatory screenfold, deerskin, no alphabetic glosses; pages 27–28 provide the strongest early directional-rain evidence.
- **Codex Borbonicus**: early sixteenth-century Mexica-style calendrical manuscript, possibly immediately before or after conquest. Its veintena images document mountain and rain ceremonies, especially *Atlcahualo*, *Huey Tozoztli*, *Tepeilhuitl*, and *Atemoztli*. Dating remains debated because its indigenous painting and later Spanish glossing need not be contemporary.
- **Codex Mendoza**, approximately 1541–1542: tribute and historical manuscript made for a Spanish audience. It provides evidence for imperial economic geography and ritual costume but less direct exposition of Tlaloque doctrine.
- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis**, approximately 1550s–1560s, Mexico-Tenochtitlan region: calendrical and historical pictography with Spanish annotations. Its Christian and Dominican interpretive layers must be separated from indigenous images. The manuscript is BnF MS Mexicain 385; the [UNAM Tlachia project](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/telleriano/estudio) gives its codicological history.
- **Codex Magliabechiano**, mid-sixteenth century: pictorial-ethnographic festival notices; colonial copy, not an untouched pre-Conquest witness.
- **Primeros Memoriales**, Sahagún’s Tepepulco investigations, approximately 1558–1561: Nahuatl testimony and images collected from Indigenous elders and artists. It contains the most important early description and illustration of *tepictoton*.
- **Florentine Codex**, completed 1577 at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco: parallel Nahuatl and Spanish encyclopedic work assembled by Bernardino de Sahagún with Nahua scholars, scribes, and painters. Book 1 classifies gods; Book 2 records festivals and sacrifices; Books 3 and 7 treat divine history and cosmology; Book 11 covers the environment. The surviving manuscript is Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Mediceo Palatino 218–220. The [Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/) supplies page images, Nahuatl/Spanish transcription, and English translation.
- **Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas**, approximately 1530s–1540s: anonymous Spanish account, probably dependent on Indigenous pictorial and oral testimony, traditionally associated with Andrés de Olmos’s circle. It supplies the clearest jar-and-thunder account.
- **Diego Durán, Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme / Book of the Gods and Rites**, composed approximately 1574–1581: extensive descriptions of Tlaloc’s shrine, Mount Tlaloc pilgrimage, royal participation, and child sacrifice. Durán combined Nahua information with Christian polemic and sometimes imposed European analogies.
- **Leyenda de los Soles**, written 1558 in Nahuatl, preserved in the *Codex Chimalpopoca*: includes the recovery of maize from Tonacatepetl, with the Tlaloque breaking open the mountain.
- **Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, mid-sixteenth century Nahuatl annals in the *Codex Chimalpopoca*: includes Tlaloc/Tlalloque appearances in Toltec and Mexica sacred history.
- **Histoyre du Mechique**, French manuscript preserving a lost Spanish account assembled in the sixteenth century: contains central Mexican creation traditions, though its transmission is especially indirect.
- **Cantares Mexicanos**, chiefly copied in the later sixteenth century: songs invoke rain, flowers, war, precious water, and Tlalocan imagery, but individual poems require cautious dating; manuscript date is not composition date.
- **Hernando Ruiz de Alarcón, Tratado de las supersticiones y costumbres gentílicas**, 1629: records anti-idolatry investigations and Nahuatl incantations addressing *Tlaloque*, *ahuaque*, earth lords, and weather powers. It proves continued colonial ritual language while filtering it through clerical prosecution.
- **Juan de Pomar, Relación de Texcoco**, 1582: important for Mount Tlaloc, its stone image, offerings of multicolored maize, and the Texcocan royal cult.
- **Motolinía/Toribio de Benavente, Memoriales and Historia de los indios de la Nueva España**, 1530s–1540s: early Franciscan descriptions of calendars and sacrifice, polemical but chronologically valuable.
- **Juan de Torquemada, Monarquía indiana**, 1615: late compilation preserving earlier material, including claims concerning sacrificed children’s relationship to maize and seasonal return. It is not independent wherever it copies prior friars.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

**[Securely attested]** The Tlaloque controlled beneficial rain and destructive excess alike: cloud, mist, dew, thunder, lightning, hail, cold precipitation, floods, crop-rotting water, drought, and meteorological injuries. Their ambivalence is fundamental. They released food and germination, but could withhold rain, destroy maize, or strike persons with lightning.

Mountains were conceived as storehouses filled with water, seed, maize, and wealth. Caves and springs were openings into this interior system. Thus the Tlaloque were at once sky powers and beings within mountains; a modern nature/spirit distinction does not fit the evidence.

People killed by lightning, drowning, certain dropsical or pustular diseases, and related “watery” deaths were associated with Tlalocan rather than the ordinary underworld. The clearest texts speak of Tlaloc’s destination and subjects collectively; assigning every such dead person the formal rank of a Tlaloque would go beyond the evidence.

### The jars, rain, thunder, and lightning

**[Early colonial attestation, probably preserving pre-Conquest doctrine]** The *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* says Tlaloc’s dwelling contained four rooms or vessels holding different waters: one made crops flourish; others caused blight, freezing, rotting, or drying. Tlaloc made numerous small ministers and placed jars and striking-sticks in their hands. They poured the water as rain. When they broke a jar, the sound was thunder; a fragment descending to earth was a thunderbolt or lightning strike.

The archaeological correlate is unusually strong. Templo Mayor Offering 48 contained at least forty-two sacrificed children together with eleven reclining, polychrome tezontle vessels bearing Tlaloc faces. Leonardo López Luján interprets the tipped vessels as material enactments of the Tlaloque pouring precious water ([“Ollas azules: lluvia a cántaros”](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/ollas-azules-lluvia-cantaros)).

**[Modern distortion]** A common internet version assigns one jar respectively to “rain, hail, thunder, and drought.” The primary account instead distinguishes kinds of water and treats thunder as the breaking of a vessel. The neat four-function list is therefore a simplification.

### Direction and color

**[Secure pictorial evidence; debated interpretation]** Borgia page 27 gives black, yellow, blue, and red directional rain figures and a central fifth. The four quarters organize weather, time, maize, and prognostication. Precise assignment of individual compass directions varies among Seler, Nowotny, Boone, and later investigators; direction should not be inferred from modern page orientation without reconstructing the manuscript’s reading order.

This is consequently better described as **four directional manifestations plus a center** than as four permanently named “sons” of Tlaloc. The fivefold arrangement is comparable to Mesoamerican quincunx organization.

### Appearance and recognition

Images identified as Tlaloc or a Tlaloque commonly display:

- ringed or “goggle” eyes;
- an upper-lip or mouth mask formed by intertwined serpent elements;
- projecting fangs;
- blue, black, red, or multicolored body paint depending on direction and ritual;
- pleated paper crown or *amateteuitl* banners;
- water-lily, maize, shell, jade, rubber, and paper ornaments;
- a lightning implement, serpent, staff, or water jar;
- jaguar, serpent, amphibian, and aquatic associations.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Esther Pasztory and others demonstrated that the supposed “goggles” and moustache may be a composite serpent mask rather than anatomical eyes and facial hair. Individual rain-god images cannot always be labeled “Tlaloc,” “a Tlaloque,” or a named local mountain without accompanying text or context.

### Sacred materials

- **Blue-green stone and turquoise:** water, preciousness, fertility.
- **Chalchihuitl/jade beads:** water drops, seed, precious liquid; found with sacrificed children.
- **Blue pigment:** placed on rain offerings and some human remains.
- **Rubber-spattered paper:** characteristic rain-god offering.
- **Copal:** burned in mountain, temple, and domestic rites.
- **Amaranth (*huauhtli*) and maize dough (*tzoalli*):** material for embodied mountain figures.
- **Yauhtli** (*Tagetes lucida*, Mexican tarragon): aromatic ritual plant associated with rain deities.
- **Marine sand, shells, fish, crocodilians, and aquatic birds:** deposited in the Tlaloc side of the Templo Mayor to materialize a watery world.

### Tepictoton and edible divine bodies

**[Secure early colonial attestation]** During *Tepeilhuitl* and *Atemoztli*, households and temples formed small mountains from amaranth or mixed seed dough, gave them “bones” of wood, dressed them in paper, placed small human heads or faces upon them, sang to individual mountains, then decapitated, divided, and ate the figures. These were *tepictoton*, temporary bodies rather than merely illustrative models.

Recent scholarship describes them as ritual “metapersons” whose presence was activated by making, dressing, feeding, sacrificing, and consuming them—not as decorative statues of distant gods ([Botta, “Divinità di amaranto”](https://books.fupress.com/catalogue/divinit-di-amaranto-corpi-effimeri-in-mesoamerica/16226)). That ontology is a modern analytical proposal; the material and ritual sequence themselves come from Sahagún and related colonial witnesses.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No early source supplies a stable genealogy for the collective.

- **[Securely attested classification]** Tlaloc is their leader, principal, or singular concentration; the *Historia de los mexicanos* says he made the smaller rain ministers.
- **[Later harmonization]** Modern charts often call the Tlaloque children of Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue. Some sources make Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue spouses, siblings, or members of the same water complex, but no principal early passage clearly gives the named four Tlaloque as their biological offspring.
- **[Regional ontology]** Mountains themselves could be Tlalocs/Tlaloque rather than residences owned by a separate family.
- **[Ritual transformation]** Sacrificed children were dressed and treated as images or embodiments of rain powers. Some interpretations say they became Tlaloque; the evidence more securely establishes identification in ritual than a universal doctrine of postmortem promotion.

### Every documented divine interaction

The following list distinguishes episodes from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Tlaloc — creates, commands, or heads the smaller rain ministers; they distribute the contents of his jars.** *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, approximately 1530s–1540s. **[Early colonial attestation of pre-Conquest tradition]**
- **Tlaloc — the directional rain forms surround or pluralize the central rain power.** *Codex Borgia* 27–28, approximately 1450–1521. **[Secure pictorial evidence; identity reconstructed]**
- **Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl — precedes, sweeps the roads for, or leads the Tlaloque because wind precedes rain.** *Florentine Codex* and colonial Nahuatl lexical citations: “Quetzalcoatl-Ehecatl, their leader, their road-sweeper, the Tlaloque’s.” Mid-to-late sixteenth century. **[Secure colonial attestation]**
- **Quetzalcoatl — seeks food for newly created humanity; after an ant reveals maize inside Tonacatepetl, Quetzalcoatl carries maize out, and the Tlaloque break or blast open the mountain to release its stores.** *Leyenda de los Soles*, manuscript dated 1558. **[Canonical colonial Nahuatl myth]**
- **Nanahuatl — is summoned to split Tonacatepetl with a thunderbolt before the Tlaloque seize or distribute its maize.** *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **[Canonical colonial Nahuatl myth]** This Nanahuatl is textually involved as the splitter; harmonization with every other being named Nanahuatzin is unsafe.
- **Cinteotl/maize powers — the Tlaloque receive, release, or distribute maize from the food mountain.** *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558, though the narrative’s exact personification of maize varies by translation. **[Canonical myth; interpretive detail disputed]**
- **Chalchiuhtlicue — jointly belongs to the water-and-rain complex; festivals could be described as honoring either the Tlaloque or “their sister,” Chalchiuhtlicue.** Sahagún, *Florentine Codex* Book 2, approximately 1577. **[Secure attestation; relationship wording varies]**
- **Huixtocihuatl — identified as elder sister of the rain gods/Tlaloc complex and associated with salt water.** Sahagún, *Florentine Codex* Book 1, sixteenth century. **[Secure colonial attestation]**
- **Huemac — Tlaloque confront or negotiate with the Toltec ruler in the drought-and-fall-of-Tollan cycle.** *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, mid-sixteenth century. In one episode Huemac plays ball with rain powers, rejects maize leaves in favor of precious stones, and receives destructive frost/drought; in another, a Tlaloque retrieves an ear of green maize and announces Toltec decline and Mexica succession. **[Canonical colonial Nahuatl account with political theology]**
- **Mexica patronal powers collectively — the Tlaloque announce that Toltec time has ended and Mexica time will be established.** *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*. **[Early colonial historical-mythic construction]**
- **Tepictoton/mountain beings — not an interaction between two cleanly separate classes: the small mountain bodies are themselves treated as Tlaloque or embodiments of their mountains.** *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex* Book 2. **[Secure ritual identity]**
- **Huitzilopochtli — shares the Templo Mayor’s twin pyramid with Tlaloc’s rain complex, creating an architectural-political conjunction of conquest/solar warfare and agricultural provision.** Archaeology and Durán. **[Secure cultic co-presence, not a narrative encounter]**
- **Tezcatlipoca — no securely attested episode has been located in which the Tlaloque collective meets, fights, seduces, or bargains with Tezcatlipoca.** Shared cosmological schemes do not constitute interaction. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Xipe Totec, Xochiquetzal, Coyolxauhqui, Tlaltecuhtli, Coatlicue, Mictlantecuhtli — no specific collective-Tlaloque narrative interaction is securely attested merely from their presence in the same pantheon or precinct.** **[Absence of evidence]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The rain jars

**Earliest explicit version:** *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, approximately 1530s–1540s.  
**[Early colonial attestation]**

Tlaloc occupied a many-roomed dwelling containing several kinds of water. Good water caused seed and maize to grow; other water frosted, blighted, drowned, rotted, or dried vegetation. He created or stationed small rain ministers with jars and sticks. When commanded, they poured water over the earth. Their blows shattered the vessels: the breaking was thunder, and falling pieces were lightning or thunderbolts.

**Borgia variant:** Pages 27–28 convert the same meteorological differentiation into a directional divinatory diagram. Rain is not intrinsically beneficent: its value depends on direction, timing, color, crop condition, and accompanying plague.

**Development:** The pictorial witness precedes the surviving prose, making it likely that differentiated directional rains are genuinely pre-Conquest. The exact prose physics of shards and thunder is first securely recorded after conquest.

### The opening of Tonacatepetl

**Source:** *Leyenda de los Soles*, written 1558.  
**[Canonical colonial Nahuatl myth]**

After humanity requires sustenance, Quetzalcoatl observes an ant carrying maize. He follows or questions the ant, transforms himself so as to enter Tonacatepetl, “Mountain of Our Sustenance,” and retrieves a kernel. The gods chew the maize and place it in human mouths. They then consider how to obtain the mountain’s entire contents. Nanahuatl is called to strike it open, and the Tlaloque take or release its stores: white, yellow, red, and black maize, beans, chia, amaranth, and other foods.

The myth casts the Tlaloque not simply as rainmakers but as controllers of the subterranean agricultural treasury. Its logic matches the conception of mountains as hollow, water-filled storehouses.

**Variant and dispute:** Popular retellings often make the Tlaloque Quetzalcoatl’s helpers throughout, or say they alone shatter the mountain. The surviving narrative separates Quetzalcoatl’s discovery, Nanahuatl’s thunderbolt, and the Tlaloque’s appropriation or distribution. Translation of the crucial verbs determines whether their act appears cooperative, possessive, or competitive.

### Huemac and the rain gods

**Source:** *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, mid-sixteenth century, reporting legendary Toltec history.  
**[Canonical historical-mythic tradition]**

Huemac plays ball against rain beings. When they lose, they offer maize leaves or young maize; he demands jade and quetzal feathers instead, misrecognizing agricultural wealth. The Tlaloque satisfy the literal demand but impose crop failure, frost, or drought. The episode explains Tollan’s famine and moral-political decline. Elsewhere a Tlaloque emerges through water with a green ear of maize and announces that the Toltec order is ending while the Mexica order will be seated.

The narrative is simultaneously agricultural theology and imperial historiography: proper rulership requires recognition of the rain powers’ food as true wealth.

### Directional rain and the agricultural year

**Source:** *Codex Borgia* 27–28.  
**[Secure pictorial cycle; readings contested]**

The four colored manifestations and central figure forecast alternating growth, rain, infestation, rodent damage, and death. Seler interpreted the sequence as quarters or year-bearer rains; Nowotny stressed divinatory structure; Boone situates it within cycles of ritual time and meaning. Attempts to turn it into a literal meteorological calendar remain debated.

### Children, maize, and return

**Sources:** Sahagún Book 2; Durán; Torquemada; archaeological contexts.  
**[Secure ritual, theological interpretation partly reconstructed]**

Children offered to the rain powers were conducted to mountains and waters, adorned with paper and precious beads, made to weep, and killed. Their tears were taken as a favorable sign of coming rain. Later sources and scholarly reconstruction connect their small, moist, seed-like bodies with maize and the Tlaloque. Torquemada reports an idea that the children returned with the ripened maize at the end of the rainy season. This should not be projected without qualification into every earlier ceremony.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Templo Mayor

The northern half of Tenochtitlan’s Templo Mayor was dedicated to Tlaloc; the southern half to Huitzilopochtli. This paired agricultural sustenance and tribute-supported warfare at the center of imperial sovereignty. Numerous deposits on the Tlaloc side contain aquatic animals, marine shells, blue pigment, greenstone, rubber, copal, Tlaloc vessels, and human remains. The archaeological zone confirms the dual temple independently of colonial narratives ([INAH Templo Mayor](https://www.inah.gob.mx/zonas/zona-arqueologica-templo-mayor)).

### Offering 48

**[Secure archaeological evidence]** Excavated in 1980–1981 and assigned to construction stage IVa under Motecuhzoma Ilhuicamina, Offering 48 contained at least forty-two children, largely aged two to seven, placed over marine sand. Some wore greenstone necklaces or had a green bead in the mouth; blue pigment, copal, gourds, birds, marine materials, an obsidian blade, and eleven Tlaloc-faced stone jars accompanied them.

INAH’s reassessment correlates the deposit with the severe drought and famine around 1452–1454 and the calendrical year One Rabbit, 1454. Twenty-two individuals could be identified as male and six as female; approximately half displayed porotic hyperostosis associated with nutritional stress. Stable-isotope work suggests diverse origins, including southern regions, though not every individual’s origin is resolved ([INAH report](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/ofrenda-masiva-de-infantes-dedicada-a-tlaloc-fue-coincidente-con-la-gran-sequia-de-1454-en-la-cuenca-de-mexico?highlight=WyJtZXhpY2FzIl0%3D)).

The deposit proves mass child killing in a rain-god context. Whether every child was verbally called a Tlaloque cannot be recovered from bones alone; that interpretation joins archaeology to colonial descriptions of children as divine embodiments.

### Mount Tlaloc

Mount Tlaloc, east of Lake Texcoco, possessed a high-altitude walled precinct or *tetzacualco*, processional causeway, central sacred space, and stone images. Durán describes rulers of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan, and allied polities ascending in *Huey Tozoztli*, carrying offerings and participating in rites centered on a child and the rain image. Pomar describes a stone figure with a cavity containing maize of four colors; a sacrificed child was placed in or given to the mountain.

The sanctuary materially bound rain-making to interstate kingship. The pilgrimage traversed the Basin horizontally and ascended vertically into the rain source; this political and spatial reading is developed in [“The Ritual Ascent at Mount Tlaloc”](https://mavcor.yale.edu/mavcor-journal/ritual-ascent-mount-tlaloc-mexico).

### Veintena festivals

- **Atlcahualo:** opening rain petition, approximately February in common colonial correlations. Children were taken to named mountains and water places. Sahagún records seven destinations and victims in one version. Tears were omens of rain.
- **Tozoztontli:** continued agricultural and rain rites; offerings at caves, springs, fields, and mountains.
- **Huey Tozoztli:** royal Mount Tlaloc pilgrimage and offerings connected with maize and the approaching rains.
- **Etzalcualiztli:** major rainy-season festival honoring Tlaloc and the rain priesthood; fasting, reed gathering, water rites, and sacrifice.
- **Tepeilhuitl, “Feast of Mountains”:** mountain gods were personified; amaranth-dough *tepictoton* were made and consumed.
- **Atemoztli, “Descent of Water”:** domestic and public rain/mountain images were fashioned, decorated, ritually killed, divided, and eaten. [Arqueología Mexicana](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/5213) identifies the associated beings as *Tlaloque tepictoton*.

Calendar conversion is approximate and disputed; veintenas should not be assigned fixed Gregorian dates without naming the correlation used.

### Priesthood

The **Tlalocan tlenamacac**, “fire-priest/offering-priest of Tlalocan,” was a high-ranking rain priest. Descriptions give blackened face, long hair, rain-god mask or paint, mist-rattle, and austere ritual duties. The Templo Mayor Tlaloc priesthood participated in fasting, incense offerings, bloodletting, child processions, and state ceremonies. Named local priests served particular mountains and occupational gods.

### Human sacrifice

The evidence is both textual and archaeological.

Sahagún records heart extraction and, in some contexts, decapitation. Durán describes a child killed at Mount Tlaloc. Colonial sources say children were purchased, donated, or selected; some were prized for double hair whorls, interpreted as watery signs. Their crying was cultivated or observed because abundant tears predicted abundant rain. Claims that priests universally tore out fingernails specifically to force tears circulate widely but require passage-by-passage verification and should not be treated as a universal rite.

Bioarchaeology confirms multiple rain-associated deposits. A review notes that rain-god sacrifices are the largest archaeologically supported category of Mexica child sacrifice and distinguishes the Templo Mayor group from thirty or more children found at the Ehecatl temple at Tlatelolco ([McGill archaeological analysis](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/5h73pz202)).

The Huei Tzompantli excavated from 2015 onward confirms large-scale display of sacrificed skulls at Tenochtitlan, including men, women, and children. It is evidence for the wider sacrificial system, not direct evidence that those skulls belonged to Tlaloque ceremonies. Conflating it with Offering 48 would be erroneous.

### Ordinary offerings and domestic practice

Households made small mountain images, burned copal, spattered paper with rubber, offered food, pulque, quail, maize, flowers, and blood, and addressed local heights and springs. Mat-makers revered Nappatecuhtli; fishers and lake hunters honored Opochtli. These occupational cults show that “the Tlaloque” were not confined to the imperial temple.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before approximately 900 CE: antecedents

Central Mexico possessed ancient storm-god imagery, cave-water cults, mountain shrines, and directional cosmologies. Teotihuacan art is especially important. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** These are antecedents, not alphabetically named Tlaloque. A direct, unchanged cult line from Teotihuacan to Tenochtitlan cannot be proved.

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1428

Nahua city-states organized rain worship around local mountains, springs, agricultural calendars, and rulers. Mount Tlaloc’s precinct may incorporate earlier use. The Mexica entered an already populated religious landscape rather than inventing central Mexican rain worship.

### Imperial Mexica period, 1428–1521

This is the best-documented florescence. The dual Templo Mayor institutionalized Tlaloc beside Huitzilopochtli. The Triple Alliance’s rulers participated at Mount Tlaloc. Tribute supplied food, paper, rubber, precious stone, captives, and other ritual resources. The Borgia rain almanac and the Templo Mayor deposits show differentiated, materially elaborate rain theology.

Offering 48 links the cult to state crisis management during the 1450s drought. Ritual action presented the rulers as capable of negotiating seasonal renewal.

### Conquest and first colonial generation, 1521–approximately 1600

Temples were destroyed, stripped, buried, or replaced; public sacrifice was suppressed, and missionaries classified rain powers as idols or demons. Yet this period generated most surviving alphabetic documentation. Sahagún’s Nahua collaborators recorded terminology and ritual memory only decades after conquest.

Cult did not simply disappear. Mountain processions, cave offerings, domestic rites, and rain petitions were reconfigured around crosses, saints, and Christian feast days. Identification of Tlaloc or the Tlaloque with a single saint is not uniform. St Michael, St John, the Holy Cross, the Virgin, and local Christs could absorb different weather and mountain functions.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

Ruiz de Alarcón’s 1629 prosecutions document continuing invocations to Tlaloque, *ahuaque*, and earth lords. The evidence is adversarial: clerics recorded formulas in order to suppress them. Nevertheless, the Nahuatl texts demonstrate persistence beyond antiquarian memory.

Colonial ritual specialists negotiated with rain, hail, springs, and mountains under increasingly Christian vocabularies. This is syncretic transformation, not evidence that an unchanged state cult survived clandestinely.

### Nineteenth century

Publication and collecting of codices converted the Tlaloque into objects of comparative mythology and national antiquarianism. Lord Kingsborough’s *Antiquities of Mexico* reproduced major manuscripts between 1831 and 1848. European scholars frequently compared Tlaloc to Greco-Roman, Egyptian, or Asian gods on superficial iconographic grounds.

### Twentieth century

Seler’s iconographic commentaries, Alfonso Caso’s calendrical studies, Nicholson’s 1971 deity typology, and Sahagún editions by Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble established the modern documentary framework.

The 1978 discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone triggered the large-scale Proyecto Templo Mayor. It was not a Tlaloque image, but the resulting excavations exposed the Tlaloc half of the imperial precinct and Offering 48. The stone was found on 21 February 1978 and belongs to Templo Mayor stage IVb ([INAH](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/con-teatro-y-talleres-festejaran-el-aniversario-del-hallazgo-del-monolito-de-la-diosa-coyolxauhqui)).

### Twenty-first century

The 2006 discovery of the monumental Tlaltecuhtli slab again expanded knowledge of the sacred precinct, pigments, royal burial programs, and earth-water relations. It does not depict a Tlaloque; its relevance is contextual. The twelve-ton monolith was found on 2 October 2006 ([INAH](https://inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/tlaltecuhtli-la-deidad-de-tierra-que-emergio-del-templo-mayor)).

Digital codex projects now permit comparison of Nahuatl, Spanish, image, and translation at folio level. Bioarchaeology, isotopes, pigment analysis, and paleoclimate studies have made the sacrificial and ecological record more precise.

### Contemporary practice

In central Mexico, *graniceros*, *tiemperos*, *ahuaques*, and related weather specialists continue rituals at mountains, springs, and caves. They may interpret lightning strikes, dreams, or illness as recruitment by water beings and negotiate for rain or against hail. Ethnographers recognize significant structural continuities but warn against calling every contemporary water being a direct survival of an Aztec Tlaloque. The history includes colonial Christianity, local innovation, and community-specific terminology. A substantial review is [“Graniceros, los ritualistas del rayo en México”](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-16592009000300009&script=sci_arttext).

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and national culture

Tlaloc became a major emblem of Mexican antiquity; the plural Tlaloque remain less individualized. Museum displays, schoolbooks, public murals, and weather journalism commonly depict them as four childlike assistants carrying jars. That image is an intelligible condensation of colonial evidence but is not a single ancient canonical portrait.

The discovery of Coyolxauhqui in 1978 and Tlaltecuhtli in 2006 reshaped public understanding of the Templo Mayor, although neither object represents the Tlaloque. The Sun Stone likewise supplies cosmological context but no secure portrait or narrative of the collective.

### Literature, games, and popular media

Modern fantasy frequently turns the Tlaloque into elemental minions, dwarfs, or a fixed team of four. Games and illustrated mythology books assign each a color and weather attack. These are **modern retellings**, even when loosely inspired by Borgia page 27 and the jar myth.

The singular Tlaloc appears much more frequently than the collective in comics, animation, games, and commercial branding. Where a product calls any small rain creature a “Tlaloque,” that use should not be mistaken for traditional nomenclature.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist use

Mexicayotl and other Indigenous-revival or reconstructionist groups may invoke Tlaloc, the Tlaloque, sacred mountains, or the four directions in rain ceremonies. Public evidence does not support a reliable estimate of scale or a single orthodox liturgy. Some ceremonies deliberately reconstruct Sahaguntine festival forms; others combine Mexica symbolism, pan-Indigenous environmentalism, Catholic elements, and contemporary ritual creativity.

Claims of an unbroken, secret priestly transmission identical to the fifteenth-century Templo Mayor institution are not demonstrated by presently published evidence. Conversely, lack of institutional continuity does not make contemporary Indigenous mountain ritual inauthentic; it has its own colonial and modern history.

### Occultism and New Age material

Unlike Quetzalcoatl, the Tlaloque have no substantial documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the classic European grimoire tradition. Lists of “Aztec elementals” equated with sylphs, angels, archangels, or the four classical elements are modern comparativist constructions. No early Nahua source assigns them the Western elements earth-air-fire-water.

Jungian treatments that call them archetypes of the collective unconscious or the “shadow side of fertility” are twentieth- or twenty-first-century interpretations, not traditional doctrines.

### Politics and appropriation

The Tlaloc complex has been used in Mexican national narratives of environmental mastery, Indigenous heritage, and resistance to colonialism. No well-documented extremist movement appears to have made the Tlaloque collective a central symbol. Assertions of extremist appropriation should therefore not be manufactured from the broader use of Aztec imagery by nationalist groups.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major approaches

- **Eduard Seler:** monumental iconographic comparison of the Borgia Group; foundational but inclined toward broad equivalences and astronomical reconstruction.
- **Karl Anton Nowotny, *Tlacuilolli*:** emphasized indigenous divinatory organization and moved interpretation away from treating manuscripts as imperfect Western narratives.
- **Henry B. Nicholson, 1971:** established a typology of Late Pre-Hispanic central Mexican deities. Valuable for classification, but later scholars have questioned whether rigid pantheon taxonomies misrepresent fluid embodiment.
- **Miguel León-Portilla:** reconstructed Nahua philosophical concepts from songs and Sahaguntine sources. His “Aztec thought” synthesis is influential but sometimes treats elite Nahua materials as a coherent philosophical system.
- **Elizabeth Hill Boone:** interprets pictorial manuscripts as systems of performance, divination, and temporal meaning; her *Cycles of Time and Meaning in the Mexican Books of Fate* is central for Borgia interpretation.
- **Michel Graulich:** developed structural and calendrical readings of sacrifice and festival cycles. His calendar correlations and “historical shift” theories remain debated.
- **Johanna Broda:** linked rain worship, sacred landscape, astronomy, agriculture, and political ritual.
- **Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Leonardo López Luján:** joined archaeological deposits to the Templo Mayor’s cosmological and political organization.
- **Guilhem Olivier:** emphasizes divine impersonation, substitution, sacrifice, and unstable divine identity.
- **Richard Townsend and Davíd Carrasco:** interpret landscape, pilgrimage, empire, and sacred centers.
- **Mary Miller and Karl Taube:** synthesized iconography across Mesoamerica; useful orientation, not a substitute for source-specific analysis.

### Core disputes

1. **Are the Tlaloque separate gods or plural Tlalocs?**  
   The evidence supports both usages in different contexts. A rigid hierarchy—one “major god” plus four minor servants—fits the jar text but not every mountain embodiment or directional image.

2. **Are there four or five?**  
   Prose emphasizes four rooms or quarters; Borgia imagery has four directions plus a center. Other texts speak of innumerable small ministers. “Exactly four Tlaloque” is therefore context-specific.

3. **Are the Tepictoton identical to the Tlaloque?**  
   They are securely associated and could embody mountain-rain beings. Yet *tepictoton* names a kind of made ritual body, while *Tlaloque* names a divine collective; complete lexical identity is unwarranted.

4. **Do named occupational gods form the canonical four?**  
   No early document securely fixes Nappatecuhtli, Opochtli, Yauhqueme, and Tomiyauhtecuhtli into the popular modern chart. They are separately linked with the rain complex.

5. **Can Teotihuacan’s storm god be called Tlaloc?**  
   Iconographic convention says “Tlaloc” or “Tlaloc-like”; historically, the ancient name and exact theology are unknown.

6. **Did sacrificed children “become Tlaloque”?**  
   Ritual dress, blue pigment, bodily smallness, mountain deposition, and textual language support identification with rain beings. A precise universal afterlife doctrine is reconstructed, not directly stated in every source.

7. **Did Spanish authors invent or exaggerate sacrifice?**  
   Individual numbers and rhetorical descriptions require criticism, but Offering 48 and other deposits independently establish child sacrifice in rain-god contexts. The practice cannot responsibly be dismissed as missionary propaganda.

8. **Were the four rains four seasons?**  
   Central Mexico’s agricultural pattern was commonly organized around wet/dry cycles, not the European four seasons. Interpreting the four directions as four modern seasons is a late analogy.

9. **Was amaranth cultivation outlawed by the Spaniards?**  
   Missionaries condemned and suppressed idolatrous uses of *tzoalli*, but widely repeated claims of a universal death-penalty ban on possessing or cultivating amaranth lack a securely cited blanket law. Suppression of ritual use should not be inflated into an empire-wide botanical prohibition.

### State of the evidence

The *Codex Borgia* is in the Vatican Library; the *Codex Borbonicus* is in the library of the French National Assembly; the *Codex Mendoza* is Bodleian Library MS Arch. Selden. A.1; the *Telleriano-Remensis* is BnF MS Mexicain 385; the *Florentine Codex* is in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana. The *Codex Chimalpopoca* original disappeared in the twentieth century, so the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* and *Leyenda de los Soles* depend on earlier copies, photographs, and editions.

Colonial manuscripts preserve Indigenous testimony but were produced under conquest, missionary questioning, censorship, and translation. Images, Nahuatl text, and Spanish glosses must be treated as related but non-identical witnesses.
