# Mayahuel: Research Dossier

**Class:** Female *teōtl*—a central-Mexican divine power conventionally translated “goddess”—who personifies the maguey/agave and participates in the religious complex of pulque, fertility, nourishment, lunar imagery, and the earth.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The pre-Conquest **Codex Borgia**, probably painted in the Puebla–Tlaxcala–western Oaxaca cultural zone during the Late Postclassic, approximately the fifteenth or very early sixteenth century. It depicts a female maguey deity in ritual-calendrical contexts. The identification as Mayahuel rests on comparison with named early-colonial images, particularly the **Codex Borbonicus**, **Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos**, **Codex Magliabechiano**, and Sahagún’s Nahuatl testimony. No securely readable pre-Conquest alphabetic inscription names her.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Secure early]** pre-Conquest or first-generation Indigenous testimony; **[Early colonial]** sixteenth-century manuscript or testimony preserving Indigenous material under colonial conditions; **[Regional variant]** locally bounded tradition; **[Learned systematization]** an Indigenous or European attempt to organize multiple gods and accounts; **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly inference from dispersed evidence; **[Modern invention/reception]** a demonstrably recent elaboration, adaptation, or commercial claim.

---

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[Secure early / early colonial]** Mayahuel is primarily the divine personification of the maguey, not simply a generic “goddess of alcohol.” Images place her in or emerging from the plant, while early-colonial prose attributes to her the first tapping of maguey for its sweet sap. Because fermented maguey sap becomes *octli*—now usually called pulque—she belongs to the pulque complex alongside Pahtécatl and the many rabbit gods. Her maternal and nutritive character is expressed most emphatically in the Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos statement that she possessed four hundred breasts and was transformed into maguey because of her extraordinary fruitfulness. The numeral “four hundred” (*centzontli*) is both a number and a conventional expression of innumerable abundance.

Calling her “the Aztec goddess of tequila” is **[Modern invention/commercial reception]**. Tequila is a distilled agave spirit whose historical production belongs to the colonial and modern periods. The documented pre-Hispanic association is with maguey products and fermented *octli*, not modern tequila. “Mezcal goddess” is similarly imprecise unless *mexcalli* is meant in its older sense of cooked agave rather than the later distilled drink.

### Names and spellings

- **Mayāhuel / Mayahuel** — the normalized Classical Nahuatl form most commonly used in scholarship.
- **Mayauel** — a frequent sixteenth-century spelling, including Sahagún’s manuscript tradition.
- **Mayaguil / Mayahuil** — spellings in the Italian commentary associated with Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos.
- **Mayavel** — Spanish orthography in the Codex Magliabechiano gloss.
- **Meyehual** and similar forms — colonial-transmission variants, especially in modern transcriptions of the *Histoyre du Mechique*.

**[Uncertain]** No universally accepted linguistic decomposition of *Mayāhuel* exists. The Codex Magliabechiano interpreter says the name “means maguey,” but this is a semantic identification, not a defensible morpheme-by-morpheme Nahuatl etymology: the ordinary Nahuatl word for maguey is *metl*. Nineteenth-century claims connecting *maya-* to the Maya region are speculative and were already disputed by contemporary Mexican scholars. Popular derivations from *metl* plus *yohualli* (“night”) or translations such as “the one surrounding the maguey” are not securely established by historical linguistics.

### Titles and calendrical designations

- **“Woman with four hundred breasts”** — **[Early colonial]**, explicitly stated in the commentary to Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos, fol. 20v, for the eighth trecena. The same passage says the gods transformed her into maguey because of her fruitfulness. This is the secure textual source behind the famous motif; it should not be attributed vaguely to “Aztec legend.” [FAMSI transcription](https://www.famsi.org/research/pohl/jpcodices/rios/index.html)
- **Patroness of the eighth trecena, 1 Grass through 13 Lizard** — **[Secure early / early colonial]**, shown in the Codex Borgia calendrical corpus and explicitly named in Borbonicus and Vaticanus A traditions.
- **Ce Tochtli, “One Rabbit”** — **[Learned systematization / iconographic reconstruction]**, a calendrical identity associated with the terrestrial-lunar and pulque complex. It must not be confused with **Ome Tochtli, “Two Rabbit,”** the generic or leading pulque god.
- **“Goddess of maguey”** — a sound modern shorthand supported by Indigenous imagery and the Magliabechiano gloss.
- **“Goddess of pulque”** — partly valid but secondary: the plant is her most stable identity, while particular processes and kinds of fermentation belong to Pahtécatl and named rabbit gods.

No securely attested *interpretatio graeca* or *interpretatio romana* equates Mayahuel with a classical deity. Comparisons to Artemis of Ephesus because both could be represented as many-breasted are **[Modern comparativism]**, not an ancient identification.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[Secure early] Codex Borgia.** This pre-Conquest painted deerskin screenfold contains no alphabetic captions. Its provenance remains disputed, most often situated in the Puebla–Tlaxcala–western Oaxaca region, and estimates generally place it in the fifteenth or early sixteenth century. The Mayahuel identification is therefore iconographic: a woman integrated with a maguey, pulque imagery, the rabbit sign, and attributes recognizable from named colonial cognates. The manuscript is now Vatican Library Codex Borgianus Mexicanus 1. Its ritual-divinatory character means it attests her participation in calendrical religion, not a narrative biography. A digital facsimile and the history of the wider Borgia group are available through [FAMSI](https://www.famsi.org/research/pohl/jpcodices/) and the [Vatican Library](https://digi.vatlib.it/view/MSS_Borg.mess.1).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[Secure early]** Mayahuel is the maguey as a living, productive divine power. The plant yielded sap, fiber, cordage, cloth, nets, bags, roof material, paper-like cuticle, edible flesh, needles, and thorns used in autosacrifice. Her domain therefore joined subsistence, household manufacture, exchange, bodily penance, nourishment, and intoxication.

**[Early colonial]** Sahagún distinguishes successive technical acts rather than crediting one deity with the entire manufacture of pulque:

1. Mayahuel first taps or hollows the maguey and extracts its “honey,” *necuhtli* or aguamiel.
2. Pahtécatl discovers the root or additive placed in it.
3. Named culture heroes/pulque gods perfect the beverage at Chichinauhya.
4. Drinking is ritually and socially regulated.

Thus “Mayahuel invented pulque by herself” is a later simplification.

### Iconographic diagnostics

Mayahuel can be recognized by a constellation of signs rather than one immutable portrait:

- a female figure seated or emerging within a maguey;
- blue-green coloring shared with the plant in Codex Borbonicus;
- the crescent-shaped **yacametztli**, “moon nose ornament,” associated with pulque vessels and pulque gods;
- pulque cups, foaming vessels, or drinking reeds;
- the rabbit day sign;
- cord or fiber derived from maguey;
- facial painting and headdress elements overlapping those of other fertility, earth, and pulque deities.

The *yacametztli* is documented both on deities and on representations of pulque containers, supporting its role as a substance-marker rather than proof that every wearer is Mayahuel. [Visual Lexicon](https://aztecglyphs.wired-humanities.org/content/yacametztli-mdz71r)

### Four hundred breasts

**[Early colonial]** Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos explicitly says she had four hundred breasts. The text does not explicitly say in the same sentence that each breast nurses one of the Centzon Totochtin. That familiar mother-and-four-hundred-sons formulation is a **[Learned systematization]** produced by combining the Ríos passage with the separately documented collective of rabbit-shaped pulque gods. It is plausible within Nahua symbolism but should not be presented as a verbatim statement from Sahagún.

The breasts express inexhaustible yield and the analogy between pale maguey sap and milk. The agave leaves themselves may invite a visual analogy with multiple breasts, but that explanation is interpretive rather than stated in the primary passage.

### Calendrical position

**[Secure early / contact-era]** In the Borbonicus tradition she governs the eighth trecena beginning **1 Grass, Ce Malinalli**. The Vaticanus A commentator calls its opening sign dangerous and says children were kept indoors because airborne beings might harm them. Other days within the trecena could receive different prognoses. Mayahuel’s calendrical patronage therefore included danger as well as fertility.

### Limits and overlaps

Mayahuel shares signs with Tlazolteotl, Toci, Xochiquetzal, earth/lunar goddesses, and pulque gods. Scholars including Henry B. Nicholson classified these within broader complexes; Michel Graulich stressed transformations among earth, moon, fertility, and sacrificial figures. These taxonomies are **[Modern scholarly reconstructions]**, not evidence that all named goddesses were simply one being. Similar costume elements can denote participation in a shared domain without erasing named identities.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

No single pre-Hispanic genealogy survives. Three different relational systems must remain separate.

### A. The sky-maiden narrative

**[Early colonial narrative]** In the *Histoyre du Mechique*, Mayahuel is a maiden living in the sky with a grandmother who is a Tzitzimitl. Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl induces or carries her down to earth. The Tzitzimime pursue them, identify Mayahuel’s half of a two-branched tree, tear it apart, and devour or scatter her. Quetzalcoatl buries what remains, producing the maguey.

The story does not establish Pahtécatl as her husband or the Centzon Totochtin as children. Those belong to another classificatory complex.

### B. The pulque family

**[Learned systematization from early colonial evidence]** Later summaries make Mayahuel and Pahtécatl spouses and parents of the Centzon Totochtin. Primary sources securely associate all three with maguey and pulque, but their precise family structure varies. Some treatments call Pahtécatl husband, brother, collaborator, or simply the discoverer of the fermenting additive. “Four hundred rabbits” is a collective designation for abundant/localized intoxication powers; it does not require a recoverable list of exactly four hundred personal names.

### C. Earth-and-crop genealogy

**[Early colonial variant]** Vaticanus A/Ríos material has been read as making Mayahuel mother of Cinteotl. This belongs to an earth-fertility system, not necessarily to the *Histoyre* story. Graulich consequently interpreted Mayahuel as a specialized aspect of a wider Toci–Xochiquetzal complex. That is a comparative historical model, not a self-declared identity in every manuscript.

### Every securely documented divine interaction

- **Quetzalcoatl / Ehecatl** — brings or persuades Mayahuel to descend from heaven; joins her in a bifurcated tree; survives the attack and buries her remains, from which maguey grows. *Histoyre du Mechique*, c. 1543. **[Early colonial transmitted narrative]**
- **Tzitzimitl, Mayahuel’s grandmother** — awakens, pursues her, identifies Mayahuel’s branch, and initiates or performs her dismemberment. *Histoyre du Mechique*, c. 1543. **[Early colonial transmitted narrative]**
- **The Tzitzimime** — accompany the grandmother and consume or disperse Mayahuel’s pieces. *Histoyre du Mechique*, c. 1543. **[Early colonial transmitted narrative]**
- **Pahtécatl** — complements Mayahuel’s extraction of aguamiel by discovering the root/additive used in fermentation. Florentine Codex, Book 10, completed 1577 from earlier testimony. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]** A sexual or marital encounter is not narrated there.
- **Tepoztecatl** — participates in perfecting pulque after Mayahuel and Pahtécatl’s discoveries. Florentine Codex, Book 10. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Cuatlapanqui** — participates in perfecting pulque at Chichinauhya. Same source and date. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Tlilhuah** — participates in perfecting pulque. Same source and date. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Papaztac** — participates in perfecting pulque. Same source and date. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Tzocaca** — participates in perfecting pulque. Same source and date. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Xochipilli** — jointly occupies the divinatory field of the eighth trecena in Codex Borbonicus p. 8. **[Secure early/contact-era joint appearance]** No narrative meeting follows from the juxtaposition.
- **Centzon Totochtin** — Mayahuel is linked to the collective through the nourishment/pulque complex and later explicitly treated as their mother. **[Early colonial complex; genealogy systematized later]**
- **Ome Tochtli** — linked through rabbit calendrics and the pulque complex; sometimes treated as leader or generic embodiment of the Centzon Totochtin. **[Early colonial/learned systematization]** No independent narrative meeting with Mayahuel is securely attested.
- **Cinteotl** — called her child in the Vaticanus A/Ríos tradition as interpreted by Graulich. **[Early colonial variant]**
- **Tlazolteotl, Toci, Xochiquetzal, Chalchiuhtlicue, Tlaltecuhtli** — share selected earth, water, maternity, fertility, lunar, or maguey signs. **[Modern comparative reconstruction]** No source warrants turning every resemblance into a personal interaction.

There is no securely documented Mayahuel interaction in the surviving text of the **Leyenda de los Soles**, no Mayahuel scene on the **Sun Stone**, and none on the monumental **Coyolxauhqui** or **Tlaltecuhtli** stones.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The descent, dismemberment, and birth of maguey

**Earliest full source:** *Histoyre du Mechique*, c. 1543, surviving in French through a lost Spanish intermediary.

**[Early colonial transmitted narrative]** After humanity has been created, the gods consider what will give human beings joy or move them to song and dance. Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl thinks of the maiden Mayahuel, who lives above with her Tzitzimitl grandmother. He goes to her while the guardian sleeps and takes or persuades her to descend.

On earth the pair become a single tree divided into two branches or trunks: Mayahuel the *xochicuahuitl*, “flower tree,” and Quetzalcoatl the *quetzalhuexotl*, “quetzal willow.” The grandmother discovers the absence, descends with the other Tzitzimime, and breaks the tree. They recognize Mayahuel’s portion and tear it apart; in versions or translations they eat the fragments. Quetzalcoatl’s branch survives. After the attackers depart, he resumes his form, gathers Mayahuel’s bones or remains, and buries them. From the burial grows the first maguey.

The myth makes plant propagation a transformation through divine bodily destruction. Wind, sky, earth, burial, plant growth, nourishment, and intoxication form one causal sequence.

**Variants and cautions:**

- “Quetzalcoatl was romantically in love with Mayahuel” is a reasonable reading of their union but more explicit in modern romantic retellings than in the surviving terse text.
- “They had sex as intertwined branches” is interpretive; the arboreal joining strongly suggests union and fertility, but modern retellings often amplify it.
- The guardian is singular Tzitzimitl in some translations and one among plural Tzitzimime in others.
- Some retellings call the event a kidnapping, others a rescue or willing elopement. The transmission does not permit certainty about the lost Nahuatl wording.
- This story explains the maguey’s origin. It does not by itself explain every technical stage of pulque fermentation.
- The notion that Quetzalcoatl’s tears watered her grave is a common **[Modern embellishment]** unless tied to a particular late retelling.

### The discovery and perfection of pulque

**Source:** Florentine Codex, Book 10, ch. 29.

**[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]** Mayahuel, here presented in humanized culture-hero form, first discovers how to pierce the maguey and extract its honey. Pahtécatl discovers the fermenting root placed into it. Tepoztecatl, Cuatlapanqui, Tlilhuah, Papaztac, and Tzocaca perfect pulque at Mount Chichinauhya, renamed Popozonaltepetl, “Foaming Mountain,” because of the drink’s foam.

The inventors hold a banquet for elderly male and female nobles. Each receives four cups, not five, to prevent drunkenness. A Huastec lord drinks five, loses self-control, removes his loincloth, and exposes himself. Ashamed, he departs with his people. The narrative explains ethnic stereotypes, ritual drinking limits, and the association of pulque with loss of social personhood.

This account conflicts with a simple “Mayahuel invented pulque” formula: she discovers tapping; other figures discover fermentation and perfect the drink.

### Quetzalcoatl’s intoxication at Tollan

**Source:** *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, sixteenth-century Nahuatl material preserved in the Codex Chimalpopoca.

**[Related cycle, not a direct Mayahuel episode]** Tezcatlipoca and associated adversaries deceive Tollan’s ruler-priest Quetzalcoatl into drinking intoxicating liquor; he becomes drunk and violates his discipline, after which he abandons Tollan. The episode is central to the political theology of pulque and rulership but Mayahuel does not personally act in it. Later popular compilations often splice this tale to her origin myth. That composite is **[Modern harmonization]**.

### The four-hundred-breasted mother

**Source:** Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos, fol. 20v.

**[Early colonial]** Mayahuel is a woman of four hundred breasts whom the gods transform into the extraordinarily productive maguey. This is a compact aetiology, independent in wording from the *Histoyre*. It lacks Quetzalcoatl, the Tzitzimime, and burial.

The widespread fuller story in which each breast feeds one of her four hundred rabbit children is **[Learned synthesis]**. It coherently combines two attested complexes but survives most clearly in modern reference literature rather than as one continuous sixteenth-century narrative.

### Mortal inventor later deified

**[Early colonial historical rationalization]** Sahagún’s Book 10 account can be read as presenting Mayahuel as a woman inventor rather than an explicitly primordial goddess. Nineteenth-century writers consequently proposed that a historical woman was divinized. The text itself does not prove euhemerism; Nahua accounts routinely describe gods as discoverers, ancestors, rulers, or embodied persons. “Mayahuel was originally a real woman” remains **[Unestablished scholarly conjecture]**.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Institutional position

No securely identified major temple exclusively dedicated to Mayahuel is documented at Tenochtitlan. Her cult was embedded in maguey agriculture, household production, calendrical divination, pulque ritual, and the collective cult of pulque gods. This distributed presence may explain why the evidence is strong in manuscripts but weak for a monumental standalone sanctuary.

Named pulque gods often have place-derived names—Tepoztecatl from Tepoztlan, Colhuatzincatl from Colhuacan, and others—suggesting that local communities and mountains were organized into a wider ritual system rather than governed by one centralized “church of Mayahuel.”

### Offerings and archaeological evidence at Zultépec–Tecoaque

**[Archaeologically attested, interpretation probable]** Excavations in 1992–1993 at Zultépec–Tecoaque, Tlaxcala, recovered maguey-shaped ceramics and thirty-six *octecómatl*, pulque vessels, in and around a circular temple associated with Ehecatl–Quetzalcoatl. In the adjoining plaza archaeologists found the remains of an adolescent Otomi girl with cut marks, dismemberment, and postmortem manipulation, accompanied by four maguey-form vessels.

Investigators proposed that the burial materially reenacted Mayahuel’s dismemberment. The sacrifice and its association with maguey vessels are archaeological findings; identifying the individual as an *ixiptla* of Mayahuel is a plausible but not demonstrable interpretation. [Archaeological summary](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/1071)

### Tepeilhuitl sacrifice

**[Early colonial ritual report / scholarly reconstruction]** Michel Graulich’s analysis of the twenty-day festivals identifies an enslaved woman impersonating Mayahuel among those sacrificed during **Tepeilhuitl**, the mountain festival. A Tlalhuica regional account substitutes Xochiquetzal. This is important evidence that divine embodiments varied by region and that the same festival could map differently onto named goddesses. [Graulich, *Ritos aztecas*](https://www.inpi.gob.mx/plan-winaodme/docs/ritos-aztecas-las-fiestas-de-los-veintenas-ini.pdf)

The evidence should be stated plainly: human beings representing deities could be killed in ritual. It does not follow that every maguey harvest or Mayahuel observance involved human sacrifice.

### Pulque rules

**[Early colonial]** Pulque consumption was differentiated by age, rank, feast, medicinal need, and ritual office. Codex Mendoza depicts elderly persons allowed amounts that younger adults could not lawfully consume. Sahagún’s four-cup/five-cup myth dramatizes the boundary between controlled sacred consumption and antisocial drunkenness.

Pulque was an offering and a medium of communion with numerous rabbit gods, but it was also an ordinary economic product. Sacred and mundane uses were not mutually exclusive.

### Bloodletting and maguey implements

Maguey thorns served as bloodletting tools in penitential rites. This connects Mayahuel’s plant materially to autosacrifice, although texts do not make her the exclusive recipient of every act performed with a maguey spine.

### Human sacrifice in the wider Mexica system

The Templo Mayor excavations beginning after the 1978 discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone established extensive material contexts for Mexica sacrifice and offerings. The Tlaltecuhtli monolith was discovered in 2006. Between 2015 and 2017, excavations at Guatemala 24 identified part of Tenochtitlan’s **Huei Tzompantli**, including a skull tower; INAH reports more than eleven thousand skull fragments and at least 650 skulls. [INAH Huei Tzompantli](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907) [2025 INAH retrospective](https://www.gob.mx/cultura/prensa/tzompantli-simbolo-de-muerte-arte-y-eternidad)

These finds corroborate large-scale human sacrifice and skull display in the Mexica capital. They are not evidence that those particular victims were sacrificed to Mayahuel. The principal monumental complex concerned Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, and the state’s military-cosmic order. Connecting the Huei Tzompantli directly to Mayahuel would exceed the evidence.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### Before the Late Postclassic

Agave use in Mesoamerica is much older than the Mexica state, but archaeological evidence for ancient maguey processing is not automatically evidence for a goddess named Mayahuel. No securely identified Classic-period inscription presently names her. Proposals that earlier many-breasted, lunar, earth, or agave figures “are Mayahuel” are **[Modern reconstruction]**.

### Late Postclassic, c. 1200–1521

**[Secure early]** The Borgia-group manuscripts demonstrate an established calendrical and iconographic maguey deity across a cultural region wider than Tenochtitlan. By the final century before the Conquest, Mayahuel participated in a shared pictorial language linking plants, days, directions, fertility, pulque, rabbits, and lunar signs.

Mexica imperial religion incorporated regional pulque gods and their places. The cult’s apparent “peak” is therefore not measurable by a count of Mayahuel temples; it is visible through the dense manuscript system, regulated pulque economy, local mountain gods, and state-calendar integration.

### Conquest and first colonial generations, 1521–c. 1600

Spanish conquest destroyed the institutional framework of state cult and brought coerced conversion, church building, suppression of Indigenous images, and Christian reinterpretation. Missionary manuscripts nevertheless preserved extensive Nahua testimony. Christian commentators called Mayahuel and related gods “demons”; their texts are simultaneously evidence and instruments of suppression.

The principal Mayahuel record was produced in this period:

- *Histoyre du Mechique*, c. 1543;
- Codex Mendoza, c. 1541–1542;
- Sahagún’s Tepepulco and Tlatelolco investigations, c. 1550s–1570s;
- Codices Tudela, Magliabechiano, Telleriano-Remensis, Vaticanus A, and Borbonicus in their surviving colonial forms.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

Direct public cult disappears from the documentary record. Maguey cultivation and pulque production continued on a major economic scale, while Indigenous religious meanings could survive within local practices, stories, landscape devotion, and Christianized ritual. There is no documentary basis for claiming an unbroken, publicly organized priesthood of Mayahuel.

No secure colonial source equates Mayahuel specifically with the Virgin of Guadalupe or the Virgin of Los Remedios. General mother-goddess/Marian syncretism is well documented, especially around Tonantzin, but extending every Marian cult to Mayahuel is **[Modern speculative syncretism]**.

### Nineteenth century

Publication and facsimile study of Mexican codices transformed Mayahuel from a suppressed divine figure into an object of antiquarian, national, and comparative scholarship. Eduard Seler’s studies of the Borgia group and the Loubat facsimiles made images widely available. Some writers rationalized her as a mortal inventor subsequently deified; others compared her four hundred breasts to Old World “many-breasted” goddesses. Both moves reflect nineteenth-century evolutionary and comparativist frameworks.

### Twentieth century

Sahagún scholarship, particularly Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble’s English edition of the Florentine Codex, restored the Nahuatl column to central importance. Nicholson’s 1971 typology situated Mayahuel within a wider complex of fertility and pulque deities; Miguel León-Portilla foregrounded Nahua philosophical categories; Michel Graulich reconstructed festival and sacrificial correspondences; Elizabeth Hill Boone interpreted divinatory codices as structured systems of time rather than illustrated myths.

At the same time, Mexican nationalism and the expanding tequila industry transformed Mayahuel into a generalized emblem of agave, Mexico, and distilled spirits.

### Twenty-first century

Contemporary artists, Indigenous cultural educators, restaurants, bars, distilleries, mezcal and tequila brands, and some polytheist practitioners invoke her. These receptions are real modern traditions, but their scale is not reliably quantified. No evidence establishes continuous institutional worship from the sixteenth century to modern devotional groups.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### National and commercial image

Mayahuel now functions as an immediately recognizable agave mother in murals, bottle labels, bar names, tourism, and spirits advertising. A mural in Tequila, Jalisco, presents her as civic and regional heritage. [Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/mural-de-la-diosa-mayahuel-en-tequila-secretar%C3%ADa-de-turismo-de-jalisco/MgHwR_CgjhfZ_g?hl=es-419)

A historical study of tequila-industry tourism argues that companies have constructed selectively “ancient” histories around Mayahuel to authenticate products and sell Mexican identity abroad. [Nichols, *The Creation of Fictional History in the Tequila Industry*](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark%3A/67531/metadc955073/m2/1/high_res_d/NICHOLS-THESIS-2016.pdf)

Claims that Mayahuel anciently presided over tequila, taught distillation, or blessed the blue Weber agave are **[Modern invention]**. Her authentic pre-Hispanic association with maguey makes the branding intelligible, but not historically literal.

### Art and literature

Modern images often combine:

- a conventionally beautiful young woman;
- enormous or wing-like agave leaves;
- a moon;
- rabbits;
- four hundred breasts reduced to a few exposed breasts;
- Quetzalcoatl as romantic lover;
- tears becoming pulque.

This is a modern visual synthesis of separate sources. It should not be mistaken for one surviving Aztec canonical portrait.

Contemporary graphic retellings have reconstructed the *Histoyre du Mechique* narrative using codical iconography. Such works can carefully distinguish source from invention, but their dialogue, emotions, and sequential imagery remain modern adaptations. [UC eScholarship project](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/5f69s31c)

### Neopagan and reconstructionist devotion

Individual modern practitioners invoke Mayahuel in offerings, agave-centered ritual, environmental spirituality, feminist theology, and polytheist reconstruction. Public internet testimony establishes that such devotion exists, but not a census, centralized church, or historical succession from pre-Conquest priesthoods.

Claims of an uninterrupted secret Mayahuel cult are **[Unestablished]**. Reconstruction based on codices and colonial texts is a modern religious practice even where participants understand it as renewing ancestral religion.

### Occultism and New Age material

Unlike Isis, Hecate, or Quetzalcoatl, Mayahuel did not acquire a major documented role in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Theosophical Society, or the Golden Dawn. Modern correspondence tables assigning her planets, chakras, tarot cards, crystals, or Wiccan sabbats are **[Modern inventions]** unless the modern author is explicitly being studied as their source.

Psychological readings of her dismemberment as trauma, rebirth, feminine resilience, ecological regeneration, or addiction recovery belong to modern archetypal interpretation. They may be meaningful, but they are not translations of a sixteenth-century Nahua exegesis.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Divinity, personification, or inventor?

Sahagún’s prose can present Mayahuel as “a woman” who first tapped maguey; pictorial sources present an unmistakably divine plant-being. The apparent contradiction results partly from Nahua modes of embodiment and partly from colonial prose categories. Treating her exclusively as either a historical inventor or an abstract plant symbol flattens the evidence.

### Borgia provenance and dating

The Codex Borgia is pre-Conquest or contact-era, but its precise place of production remains debated. “Aztec codex” is convenient but can wrongly imply that it was painted in Tenochtitlan by Mexica artists. Its Mayahuel image is secure evidence for Late Postclassic central-Mexican religion, not necessarily for a uniquely Mexica formulation.

### Mayahuel and Tlazolteotl

Shared frontal, birthing, earth, lunar, and fertility signs have led scholars to argue for overlap between Mayahuel and Tlazolteotl. Nicholson noted affinities among Mayahuel, Tlazolteotl, and Chalchiuhtlicue; Graulich placed Mayahuel within a broader Toci–Xochiquetzal pattern. These models illuminate structural relations but do not authorize replacing one name with another in every codex image.

### Lunar interpretation

Rabbit signs, the crescent *yacametztli*, pulque production cycles, and earth-fertility imagery support a lunar dimension. Nevertheless “Mayahuel is the moon goddess” is too exclusive. Nahua lunar meaning was distributed among several beings, calendrical signs, substances, and ritual complexes.

### The Bilimek vessel

Some scholars identify a skeletal or decapitated female on the Bilimek vessel as Mayahuel and connect her flowing liquid, breasts, and the cutting of the maguey’s head to extraction of aguamiel. Earlier identification as Cihuacoatl illustrates the uncertainty. This remains **[Contested iconographic attribution]**, not a named image.

### Four hundred children

The source securely gives Mayahuel four hundred breasts; other sources securely give a collective of four hundred rabbit intoxication gods. Her motherhood of all four hundred is traditional and widely accepted in modern scholarship, but the exact genealogy is more systematized than a single primary quotation suggests.

### Human sacrifice

Colonial descriptions of sacrifice must be assessed for polemical framing, but archaeology has decisively refuted wholesale denial that Mexica human sacrifice and skull display occurred. At the same time, the Huei Tzompantli cannot be used as direct evidence for Mayahuel’s cult. The strongest Mayahuel-specific case is the Zultépec–Tecoaque adolescent burial with maguey vessels, and even there the mythic identification remains interpretive.

### Supposed Marian equations

Broad continuities between Indigenous maternal religion and Marian devotion are historically important, but specific equations—“Mayahuel became the Virgin of Los Remedios,” for example—require local documentary evidence. In its absence they are **[Modern speculative harmonization]**.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is unusually uneven:

- no securely named pre-Conquest inscription;
- strong pre-Conquest pictorial attestation;
- multiple early-colonial paintings;
- two different prose origin traditions;
- fragmentary festival evidence;
- localized archaeology;
- abundant contextual evidence for pulque;
- extensive modern commercial reinvention.

The great loss is the destruction or disappearance of Indigenous ritual books and priestly commentaries after the Conquest. The *Histoyre du Mechique* itself survives only through translation layers. Consequently, silence in surviving texts cannot prove that a myth or cult never existed, but it does limit what may be asserted.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before c. 1200:** Agave exploitation is archaeologically ancient, but no named Mayahuel attestation is secure. **[Context only]**
- **c. 1200–1521:** Maguey and pulque gods become visible in Late Postclassic central-Mexican pictorial traditions. **[Secure early]**
- **c. 1400–1520:** Codex Borgia depicts the female maguey deity in calendrical-divinatory settings. **[Earliest secure attestation]**
- **Immediately before or after 1521:** Codex Borbonicus p. 8 places Mayahuel over the eighth trecena, beginning 1 Grass. **[Secure/contact-era]**
- **1521 onward:** Conquest and evangelization dismantle state cults; divine images are destroyed, hidden, reclassified, or copied for missionary study. **[Documented historical process]**
- **c. 1541–1542:** Codex Mendoza depicts regulated pulque consumption and the *yacametztli* sign on drinking vessels. **[Contextual]**
- **c. 1543:** *Histoyre du Mechique* records the descent, tree transformation, dismemberment, burial, and origin of maguey. **[Earliest full narrative]**
- **c. 1558:** *Leyenda de los Soles* and related Codex Chimalpopoca materials record cosmological and calendrical traditions relevant to her earth-lunar setting. **[Contextual]**
- **c. 1558–1561:** *Primeros Memoriales* records Indigenous lists and images of gods and ritual attributes. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **c. 1560s–1580s:** Codices Tudela, Magliabechiano, Telleriano-Remensis, and Vaticanus A/Ríos preserve complementary iconography and commentary. Vaticanus A explicitly gives Mayahuel four hundred breasts. **[Early colonial]**
- **c. 1575–1577:** Florentine Codex completed; Book 10 records Mayahuel’s tapping of maguey and the collective invention of pulque. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **c. 1570s–1581:** Durán completes major accounts of gods, festivals, pulque regulation, and sacrifice. **[Early colonial]**
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** No public institutional cult of Mayahuel is securely documented; maguey and pulque economies persist. **[Historical absence/continuity of substance, not proven continuity of cult]**
- **1790–1791:** Coatlicue and the Sun Stone are recovered in Mexico City, inaugurating a new antiquarian visibility for Mexica religion; neither is a Mayahuel monument. **[Contextual]**
- **Nineteenth century:** Codex publication and comparative mythology make Mayahuel a scholarly and national-cultural figure; speculative euhemerism and Old World comparisons proliferate. **[Modern historiography]**
- **1899:** Loubat’s facsimile of Codex Borbonicus aids international study of its Mayahuel page. **[Publication history]**
- **1905:** The *Histoyre du Mechique* is published in a modern scholarly edition, making its Mayahuel narrative widely accessible. **[Publication history]**
- **1950–1982:** Anderson and Dibble publish the critical English translation of the Florentine Codex. **[Modern scholarship]**
- **1971:** Nicholson’s typology provides a major classificatory framework for central-Mexican deities. **[Modern scholarly systematization]**
- **21 February 1978:** Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone launches the modern Proyecto Templo Mayor. It transforms knowledge of Mexica ritual archaeology but is not a Mayahuel find. [INAH project history](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/proyecto-templo-mayor)
- **1992–1993:** Excavations at Zultépec–Tecoaque recover maguey-form ceramics, pulque vessels, and the sacrificed adolescent interpreted as a possible ritual enactment of Mayahuel’s dismemberment. **[Archaeology plus interpretation]**
- **2 October 2006:** Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered near the Templo Mayor. Important for comparative earth-goddess iconography, but not an image of Mayahuel. **[Contextual]**
- **2015–2017:** Huei Tzompantli excavations materially document Tenochtitlan’s large-scale sacrificial system; no direct Mayahuel attribution is established. **[Archaeology]**
- **2016:** Felicia Rhapsody Lopez completes a dissertation centering Indigenous readings of Mayahuel and Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli across pictorial and alphabetic texts. [Dissertation record](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7zt399nt)
- **Twenty-first century:** Mayahuel flourishes in scholarship, public art, agave tourism, commercial branding, cultural education, and small-scale revived devotion. The modern “tequila goddess” is a commercial transformation of the older maguey deity. **[Modern reception]**
