# Itztlacoliuhqui: Research Dossier

**Class:** A male *teōtl*—a divine power or deity—in Late Postclassic and sixteenth-century central Mexican Nahua tradition. Depending on context, Itztlacoliuhqui is the personified frost, the divine identity of a curved or hooked obsidian implement, a calendrical patron, a punitive or ominous power, an aspect or ritual identity of Cinteotl, and—by scholarly reconstruction—an altered or sacrificed form of the Morning Star, Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli. “Demon” and “monster” are not indigenous classifications.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Diagnostic images conventionally identified as Itztlacoliuhqui occur in pre-Hispanic or conquest-period divinatory manuscripts of the Borgia group and in the *Codex Borbonicus*. Because these books are pictorial and do not spell out the name, the identification depends on comparison with named early-colonial images. The earliest securely datable alphabetic attestations belong to the mid-sixteenth century: the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, painted about 1554–1555 and annotated through 1563, and Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*, completed about 1558. The *Primeros Memoriales* explicitly says of frost or ice, *Çetl: tiquitoa Itztlacoliuhqui*—“Frost: we call it Itztlacoliuhqui.” [**Secure early attestation**]

## Evidentiary labels

- **Secure early attestation:** Pre-Hispanic or first-generation colonial evidence preserving Nahua pictorial, oral, or ritual knowledge.
- **Early-colonial elaboration:** Sixteenth- or early-seventeenth-century description recorded after the conquest, often under missionary supervision.
- **Regional variant:** Evidence belonging to a particular central Mexican or Borgia-group manuscript tradition rather than demonstrably to all Nahuas.
- **Learned systematization:** A synthesis made by colonial commentators or modern scholars from separate sources.
- **Syncretistic identification:** A historically situated equation of two divine names or manifestations.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A defensible inference, but not a proposition stated verbatim in a primary source.
- **Modern invention:** A claim for which no relevant early source has been demonstrated.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Itztlacoliuhqui

The normalized Classical Nahuatl form is **Itztlacoliuhqui**, with colonial spellings including *Yztlacoliuhqui*, *Iztlacoliuhqui*, *Itzlacoliuhqui*, and abbreviated or corrupted forms such as *Itzcoliuhqui*. Nahuatl was written alphabetically only after the conquest; consequently there is no indigenous alphabetic “original script” preceding the Roman-letter form.

The conventional translation is **“Curved Obsidian [Blade/Knife]”** or “That Which Is Curved Like Obsidian.” The transparent elements are:

- *itztli*: obsidian; by metonymy, an obsidian blade or cutting implement;
- *colihui / coliuhqui*: to become bent, curved, or twisted; “bent/curved”;
- an intervening *tla-* whose precise morphological analysis is less transparent than popular word-by-word etymologies imply.

Eduard Seler’s German rendering, *das gekrümmte Obsidianmesser*, “the curved obsidian knife,” became standard. Modern dictionary resources retain “curved obsidian blade” but also document that *itztlacoliuhqui* could designate the distinctive recurved headdress worn in the Ochpaniztli ritual. Thus the word may operate simultaneously as divine name, material-image description, and name of an article of regalia. The neat popular parsing “*itztli* + *tla* ‘thing’ + *colihui*” should be treated as an explanatory approximation, not an uncontested historical derivation. [**Secure lexical evidence / later linguistic analysis**] [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/itztlacoliuhqui/200387); [Online Nahuatl Dictionary](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/itztlacoliuhqui)

### Itztlacoliuhqui-Ixquimilli

**Ixquimilli** is commonly glossed “Eye Bundle” or “Bunched/covered eyes,” hence “the blindfolded one.” Colonial lexicography also gives the ordinary word an unfavorable human sense such as negligent or lazy. The compound **Itztlacoliuhqui-Ixquimilli** emphasizes the covered face or eyes characteristic of the god’s images. [**Early-colonial name and iconographic title**]

“Ixquimilli” should not automatically be translated as a European judicial blindfold. Its literal and iconographic sense is that the eyes or entire face are bundled, veiled, closed, or unavailable. The further interpretation “justice is impartial because it is blind” is not stated in a Nahua source.

### Cetl

**Cētl**, “frost” or “ice,” is not merely a modern domain assigned to the god. In *Primeros Memoriales* fol. 282v and the parallel *Florentine Codex*, frost is directly called Itztlacoliuhqui. The passage says that it arrives annually, begins during Ochpaniztli, lasts 120 days, and finishes during Tititl, when warmth and sowing return. [**Secure early attestation**] [Study reproducing the Nahuatl passage](https://tesiunamdocumentos.dgb.unam.mx/ptd2010/febrero/0654541/0654541_A6.pdf)

### Cinteotl anozo Itztlacoliuhqui

During Ochpaniztli, the maize god is called **Cinteotl, or Itztlacoliuhqui**—*in Cinteotl anozo Itztlacoliuhqui*. The sources describe Cinteotl’s personator wearing a pointed, recurved, notched cap called *itztlacoliuhqui*, explicitly linked to frost. This is a ritual identity, not proof that Cinteotl and Itztlacoliuhqui were everywhere and always one god. [**Secure early ritual identification / contextual syncretism**]

### Ce Cuetzpalin, “One Lizard”

In the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* tradition, **1 Lizard** is said to be Itztlacoliuhqui’s first day or calendrical birth-name. The deity governs the trecena beginning on 1 Lizard in the *Borbonicus* and Telleriano-Ríos systems. A seventeenth-century ritual text from Atenango also preserves **Ce Cuetzpalin** as a summonable *tonalli* associated with Itztlacoliuhqui. [**Regional calendrical identification; early-colonial continuation**]

### Tezcatlipoca complex

Twentieth-century classifications, especially H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology, place Itztlacoliuhqui-Ixquimilli in the **Tezcatlipoca complex** because of shared black-and-white or red-and-white striping, obscured vision, obsidian, sacrifice, punishment, fate, and calendrical power. Some modern summaries simply call him an “aspect of Tezcatlipoca.”

This is a useful iconographic and functional grouping, but no surviving Nahua narrative says, in so many words, “Tezcatlipoca transformed into Itztlacoliuhqui.” It is therefore a **modern scholarly classification**, supported by clusters of attributes rather than by a single canonical theology. [Arqueología Mexicana overview](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/senores-de-la-helada)

### “God of justice”

The early evidence securely associates Itztlacoliuhqui with **punishment**, especially in the Telleriano-Ríos commentary accompanying punished adulterers. Calling him “the god or personification of justice” is a broader interpretive gloss. Calling his face covering “the blindfold of impartial justice” imports a European iconographic explanation unless independent Nahua evidence is supplied.

The strongest defensible formulation is:

> Itztlacoliuhqui was a punitive calendrical deity before whom certain transgressors, particularly adulterers, were represented as punished; later interpreters generalized this into “justice,” and modern popular accounts converted his covered face into the blindfold of Lady Justice.

[**Punitive association: early-colonial attestation. Abstract impartial justice: modern reconstruction. European-style blind-justice symbolism: modern invention or unproven comparison.**]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Pictorial evidence

#### Borgia-group manuscripts

The *Codex Borgia*, *Codex Vaticanus B*, *Codex Fejérváry-Mayer*, *Codex Laud*, and related manuscripts were ritual-divinatory books from central Mexico, particularly the Puebla-Tlaxcala-Mixteca cultural sphere. Most are assigned to the Late Postclassic, with the *Borgia* commonly placed immediately before the conquest or near its threshold.

Figures identified as Itztlacoliuhqui display combinations of:

- the large recurved or hooked cap;
- black-and-white or red-and-white bands;
- an obscured eye or face;
- a projectile crossing the headdress;
- paper ties and wrapping suggestive of an offering or mortuary bundle;
- sacrificial or divinatory contexts.

Because the names are not alphabetically captioned, each identification is iconographic. Seler established much of the comparative nomenclature; Boone and later scholars have revised his narrative readings. [**Secure early image; individual identifications sometimes disputed**]

The *Codex Borgia*’s long central sequence, especially pages 29–46, remains contested. Seler treated it in astral-mythic terms; Karl Anton Nowotny emphasized ritual and divination; Boone interprets it as a sequence of rituals. Interpretations of pages 41–42 include sacrifice offered to Itztlacoliuhqui or a sacrificial episode involving his image. The image is evidence; a continuous prose “myth” extracted from it is scholarly reconstruction. [Study summarizing the competing ritual readings](https://www.scielo.org.mx/pdf/trace/n81/2007-2392-trace-81-247.pdf)

#### Codex Borbonicus, plate 12

The *Codex Borbonicus*, an amatl screenfold made shortly before or soon after 1521, contains the best-known image. Plate 12, corresponding to the trecena beginning **1 Lizard**, presents the patron identified as Itztlacoliuhqui. The being is nearly enclosed in regalia or wrapping, with a striped, covered face and enormous recurved headdress. A projectile passes through or behind the cap. Paper ornaments, dark stone imagery, and the compact bundled body reinforce coldness, sacrifice, deathlike immobility, or ritual offering.

The manuscript’s date remains debated. Its imagery and conventions are overwhelmingly indigenous and pre-Hispanic; some scholars consider it pre-conquest, others an extremely early colonial copy or commission. Spanish glosses are secondary. It is therefore safer to call plate 12 **conquest-period evidence preserving a pre-Hispanic divinatory configuration**, not an uncontested pre-1492 object. [BnF catalogue notice](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb16686675p); [French Ministry of Culture material study](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/thematiques/sciences-du-patrimoine/thematiques-de-recherche/etude-des-materiaux/L-examen-du-codex-Borbonicus)

### Codex Telleriano-Remensis

The manuscript was painted about 1554–1555 on European paper and annotated through 1563. It is now BnF Mexicain 385. Folio 18v shows Itztlacoliuhqui governing the 1 Lizard trecena; folio 19r continues the sequence with a dead man and woman identified by the commentary as adulterers punished before the deity. The accompanying tradition connects the god with nakedness, misery, transgression, frost, and punishment. The projectile through the curved headdress provides the strongest pictorial bridge to the Morning Star episode. [**Secure early-colonial image and commentary**] [BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb145132362); [UNAM Tlachia fol. 18v](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/telleriano/385_18v); [UNAM Tlachia fol. 19r](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/telleriano/385_19r)

The later *Codex Vaticanus A* or *Codex Ríos*, an expanded Italian-language cognate of the Telleriano tradition, says that Itztlacoliuhqui sinned, became naked, and had 1 Lizard as his day because the lizard is earthly, naked, and miserable. This is a colonial explanatory tradition, not a demonstrably pan-Mexica doctrine.

### Primeros Memoriales

Compiled by Sahagún with Nahua elders and scribes, principally at Tepepulco, and completed about 1558, the *Primeros Memoriales* contains two indispensable attestations:

1. fol. 282v: frost is called Itztlacoliuhqui and lasts from Ochpaniztli to Tititl;
2. the Ochpaniztli material: Toci accompanies her son “Cinteotl or Itztlacoliuhqui,” whose distinctive cap and thigh-skin mask belong to the ritual sequence.

The manuscript date is catalogued as 1558. [BnF authority record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb13493621g)

### Florentine Codex

Sahagún’s mature *Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España*, produced with Nahua writers and painters and completed in the 1570s, preserves parallel material:

- Book 2, Ochpaniztli: Toci’s female personator is sacrificed and flayed; a male personator of her son Cinteotl wears a mask made from her thigh-skin. His recurved, notched cap is called *itztlacoliuhqui*, “that is, frost.”
- Book 7: frost is called Itztlacoliuhqui and is assigned its 120-day season.
- The associated ritual is violent and agricultural: a woman embodying Toci is killed, skinned, and ritually transformed; her skin mediates the appearance of the maize son.

The text is primary evidence for what sixteenth-century Nahua informants told Sahagún, but its final bilingual arrangement is post-conquest and Franciscan-supervised. [**Secure early-colonial testimony to pre-conquest practice**]

### Leyenda de los Soles

The Nahuatl *Leyenda de los Soles*, written in 1558 in the manuscript conventionally called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*, supplies the celestial combat now routinely called Itztlacoliuhqui’s “origin myth.”

After Nanahuatzin and the Moon rise, the new Sun remains stationary and demands the gods’ blood and service. Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, “Lord of the Dawn,” becomes angry and shoots at the Sun. He misses. The Sun shoots back and hits him in the forehead. The ninefold heavens cover his face. The text then identifies Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli with **cētl**, frost or ice.

Crucially, the surviving passage does **not** explicitly say “he was transformed into Itztlacoliuhqui.” The name is supplied by joining this passage to the independent statement that frost is called Itztlacoliuhqui and to images showing the frost deity with covered face and pierced cap. That joining is strong but remains a **modern scholarly reconstruction of an implied identity**. [Nahuatl text indexed in the Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/yehuanti/197733)

### Ruiz de Alarcón, 1629

Hernando Ruiz de Alarcón’s *Tratado de las supersticiones y costumbres gentílicas que oy viven entre los indios naturales desta Nueva España*, composed in 1629, records ritual speech from Nahua communities. At Atenango, **Ce Cuetzpalin**, 1 Lizard, appears as a summonable *tonalli* associated in modern annotation with Itztlacoliuhqui. This establishes some survival of the calendrical-divinatory name more than a century after the conquest, although the colonial cleric classified the practice polemically as superstition. [**Early-modern regional survival**]

### Sources named in the brief that do not furnish secure direct evidence

- **Durán’s *Historia de las Indias* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*** describe Ochpaniztli and central Mexican ritual, but they do not provide an independent, full Itztlacoliuhqui mythology. Their ritual parallels help contextualize the Sahaguntine material.
- **Codex Mendoza:** no secure named or diagnostic Itztlacoliuhqui image is generally recognized.
- **Codex Magliabechiano:** useful for gods, festivals, and sacrifice, but not a principal secure witness to this deity.
- **Anales de Cuauhtitlan:** important for Quetzalcoatl and Venus traditions, but not a principal direct Itztlacoliuhqui text.
- **Histoyre du Mechique:** important for central Mexican cosmogony, but no secure, independent Itztlacoliuhqui narrative has been established.
- **Cantares Mexicanos:** no securely identified hymn addressed to Itztlacoliuhqui.
- **Sun Stone:** no figure on it is securely identified as Itztlacoliuhqui.
- **Coyolxauhqui Stone (excavated in 1978):** evidence for the Coyolxauhqui-Huitzilopochtli cycle and Templo Mayor sacrificial topography, not an Itztlacoliuhqui monument.
- **Tlaltecuhtli monolith (discovered in 2006):** not an image of Itztlacoliuhqui.
- **Templo Mayor offerings and Huei Tzompantli excavations:** vital evidence for Mexica sacrifice generally, but no excavated deposit has securely demonstrated an autonomous Itztlacoliuhqui temple or priesthood.

Absence here is material: Itztlacoliuhqui has a small and context-dependent corpus, not the institutional footprint of Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, or Tezcatlipoca.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Frost, ice, and seasonal cold

This is the best-attested domain. The Sahaguntine record directly equates frost with Itztlacoliuhqui and gives it a calendrical span from Ochpaniztli through Tititl. The power is therefore not simply “winter” in a European four-season sense; it is the dangerous cold and agricultural frost of the central Mexican highlands. It can destroy maize, but its passing also prepares the return to sowing. [**Secure early attestation**]

The connection with dawn is equally concrete: frost is most visible and dangerous around dawn, while Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli is the Morning Star. Thompson and later scholars understand the Itztlacoliuhqui–Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli connection through this conjunction of cold dawn, Venus, destructive projectiles, and seasonal danger. [**Modern scholarly interpretation**]

### Obsidian and the curved blade

The headdress graphically renders the name: a dark, hard, hooked or recurved form resembling an obsidian blade. Obsidian connects the figure with:

- cold, stone, and hardness;
- cutting;
- sacrificial implements;
- darkness and Tezcatlipoca’s obsidian-mirror complex;
- the “obsidian wind” of cold and dangerous otherworldly regions.

Not every curved headdress should be treated as a literal knife. Sahagún describes a conical cap whose tip curves back and whose edge is notched. The image works as pictorial name, regalia, and material metaphor. [**Secure iconography; precise object-type disputed**]

### Covered face and blindness

The face may be wholly wrapped, crossed by bands, or deprived of a visible functioning eye. The sources support several overlapping readings:

1. **Ixquimilli**, the bundled or covered eye;
2. the *Leyenda*’s statement that the nine heavens covered Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli’s face after the Sun struck him;
3. deathlike wrapping or sacrificial bundling;
4. blindness as punishment or incapacity;
5. invisibility and ominous divine knowledge within the Tezcatlipoca complex.

The proposition that the god wears a judicial blindfold “so that justice is impartial” is not an attested Nahua explanation. [**Blindness/covering: early evidence. Lady-Justice interpretation: modern invention.**]

### Projectile through the headdress

In the Telleriano image, and in comparable pictorial forms, a dart or arrow traverses the curved cap. This is conventionally related to the Sun’s retaliatory shot into Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli’s forehead. It is an unusually persuasive image-text correspondence, but the codex does not narrate the entire episode in pictures. [**Early image plus modern comparative reconstruction**]

Descriptions saying “the arrow of dawn struck Tonatiuh and turned him to stone” need correction:

- Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, the Dawn Lord, fires first and misses the Sun.
- Tonatiuh fires back and hits Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli.
- The *Leyenda* says the struck being is frost/ice and has his face covered.
- “Turned to stone,” “became Itztlacoliuhqui,” and “became evil and bitter” are later paraphrases or syntheses, not the wording of the extant Nahuatl passage.

### Colors and costume

Diagnostic representations commonly use:

- alternating black and white, or red and white, bands;
- white facial ground;
- dark eye covering;
- unspun cotton or cotton-like enveloping material;
- folded paper ties or banners;
- an egret-feather ornament (*aztaxelli*) in some Borgia-group images;
- the recurved, sometimes toothed cap;
- bundled or compact posture.

The bands connect the figure with stellar and sacrificial iconography; paper and wrapping can mark an offering, divine image, dead body, or consecrated bundle. A recent interpretation proposes that the entire costume depicts a sacrificed or deathlike Itztlacoliuhqui. This is plausible but remains an interpretation of pictorial grammar. [**Secure visual features / modern interpretation of their combination**] [Recent synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0956536126100911)

### Broom

Some modern presentations assign him a broom, *tlachpānōni*, said to clear winter’s death so that life may return. The broom belongs securely to the semantic and ritual field of Ochpaniztli, “Sweeping,” purification, and Toci. Its status as a permanent personal emblem of Itztlacoliuhqui is much less certain than popular icon lists imply. It is better treated as an **Ochpaniztli-associated ritual object**, not a universally diagnostic weapon.

### Punishment, sin, and misery

The Telleriano-Ríos complex connects the god’s trecena with nakedness, misery, transgression, and the punishment of adulterers. Stone and strangulation appear in the accompanying punishment image. This supports “punitive deity” more directly than “ethical judge of the universe.” The colonial word *pecado*, “sin,” may also Christianize indigenous categories of sexual and ritual transgression. [**Early-colonial interpretation with possible Christian lexical overlay**]

### Celestial and directional associations

He is patron of the trecena 1 Lizard, sometimes assigned to the south in later tabular systems. The Codex Ríos commentator describes him as a heavenly star that moves “backward,” possibly a reference to a planet’s retrograde motion. Scholars have connected this with Venus. These are regional or learned calendrical-astronomical constructions, not proof that all worshippers understood him principally as a planet.

Claims that a Dresden Codex Venus-table figure is literally Itztlacoliuhqui are comparative identifications by modern scholars; the Maya manuscript does not carry the Nahuatl name.

### Powers and limitations

The corpus attributes no broad catalogue of personal magical “powers.” He personifies frost; presides over a trecena; marks cold, adverse fate, sacrifice, or punishment; and participates through an inferred earlier identity in the attack on the Sun. In that attack he fails and is disabled. Popular claims that he controls earthquakes, volcanoes, all disasters, misery, darkness, or every bladed object are poorly sourced expansions.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

No surviving source gives Itztlacoliuhqui a stable, independent family tree. His relationships arise through ritual identification and transformation rather than ordinary genealogy.

### Toci / Teteoinnan

In the Ochpaniztli account, Toci—also called Teteoinnan, “Mother of the Gods”—is mother of Cinteotl. She accompanies “her son Cinteotl or Itztlacoliuhqui.” Her female ixiptla is sacrificed and flayed; skin from her thigh becomes the mask worn by the male personator of the maize son. [**Secure early ritual genealogy**]

This is the strongest parent-child relationship involving the name Itztlacoliuhqui, but it exists specifically through the Cinteotl identification.

### Cinteotl

Cinteotl is not merely an ally. In the Ochpaniztli ritual, **Cinteotl and Itztlacoliuhqui are alternative names or successive ritual states of the same represented being**. Scholars have interpreted the frost identity as the newly born maize seed’s dead, cold, subterranean, or dawnlike phase. The ritual source gives the equation; its cosmic explanation is scholarly reconstruction.

### Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli

The two are not placed together as separate characters. Rather, comparison of the *Leyenda*, Sahaguntine frost passages, and images supports the conclusion that Itztlacoliuhqui is the cold, covered, struck, or sacrificed manifestation of Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli. The sources preserve pieces of this equivalence, not one explicit transformation sentence.

### Tezcatlipoca

Itztlacoliuhqui belongs to what Nicholson termed the Tezcatlipoca complex. Shared obsidian, obscurity, fate, punishment, night, and striped facial motifs support the grouping. There is no securely attested genealogy, dialogue, battle, or meeting between two separate figures named Tezcatlipoca and Itztlacoliuhqui.

### Itztli

Itztli, “Obsidian/Knife,” is a personified cutting implement and calendrical being. Pictorial arrangements and modern analysis connect Itztli with Itztlacoliuhqui as related sacrificial and obsidian powers. Some Borgia-group scenes show them in a common ritual sequence, but no alphabetic myth describes their interaction.

### Itzpapalotl

Modern diagrams occasionally make Itzpapalotl a female counterpart to Itztlacoliuhqui or place both in a birth-life-death triad with Tezcatlipoca. No early text establishes such a triad or counterpart relationship. The similarity of *itz-* and shared sacrificial/stellar imagery is insufficient. [**Modern systematization**]

### Every documented divine interaction

Because the corpus is small, the following list is exhaustive only for securely identifiable named interactions, not for every anonymous figure sharing a codex page.

- **Tonatiuh — hostile projectile exchange.** Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli shoots at the stationary new Sun and misses; Tonatiuh shoots back, strikes his forehead, and causes the nine heavens to cover his face. The struck being is then identified as frost. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **Tier:** secure early-colonial myth; identification of the victim’s resulting frost-form specifically as Itztlacoliuhqui is modern reconstruction from converging sources.

- **Toci/Teteoinnan — mother accompanies and materially generates the son’s ritual identity.** Toci accompanies “her son Cinteotl or Itztlacoliuhqui”; skin taken from her sacrificed ixiptla’s thigh becomes the son-personator’s mask. *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex* Book 2, mid-to-late sixteenth century. **Tier:** secure early-colonial ritual testimony.

- **Cinteotl — identity or transformation, not a meeting.** Cinteotl wears the recurved regalia called Itztlacoliuhqui and is explicitly named “Cinteotl or Itztlacoliuhqui.” Sahaguntine Ochpaniztli accounts, ca. 1558–1570s. **Tier:** secure contextual syncretistic identification.

- **Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli — successive identity, not an interaction between two beings.** The Morning Star is struck, face-covered, and called frost in the *Leyenda*; frost is called Itztlacoliuhqui elsewhere. **Tier:** strongly supported modern scholarly reconstruction from secure early sources.

- **Tezcatlipoca — no narrated interaction.** Their relation is taxonomic and iconographic. Nicholson 1971 and subsequent iconographic studies. **Tier:** modern scholarly systematization.

- **Itztli — joint sacrificial/obsidian configuration in pictorial ritual sequences.** Borgia-group iconography; precise narrative content disputed. **Tier:** secure image, disputed interpretation.

No secure interaction with Quetzalcoatl, Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, Coyolxauhqui, Itzpapalotl, Mictlantecuhtli, or Xolotl has been established merely by their membership in the same pantheon.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The attack on the Fifth Sun

#### Earliest extant narrative

In the *Leyenda de los Soles*:

1. Nanahuatzin and the Moon undergo the fiery creation of the luminaries.
2. The new Sun appears but remains motionless.
3. He demands the gods’ blood, precious substance, and service.
4. The gods deliberate.
5. Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli becomes angry.
6. He shoots at the Sun, but his missiles fail.
7. The Sun shoots a dart back and strikes Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli in the forehead.
8. The ninefold heavens cover the defeated god’s face.
9. The text identifies Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli as frost/ice.
10. The other gods then die at Teotihuacan so that the Sun can move.

The story explains the subordination of the Morning Star to the Sun, the hostility of Venus’s rays, cold at dawn, and the sacrificial debt underpinning cosmic motion. [**Secure early-colonial version of an indigenous narrative**]

#### Formation of the familiar Itztlacoliuhqui version

The familiar retelling—

> Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli shot Tonatiuh, was struck by Tonatiuh’s arrow, turned to stone, and became the blind god Itztlacoliuhqui—

combines four distinct pieces of evidence:

- the *Leyenda*’s combat;
- its statement that the defeated Morning Star is frost;
- Sahagún’s statement that frost is Itztlacoliuhqui;
- images of Itztlacoliuhqui with covered face and pierced cap.

This is a coherent reconstruction, and probably recovers an intended relationship, but it should be identified as a synthesis. “Turned to stone” is an interpretive development from his obsidian identity; “became coldhearted” or “lost his cheerful personality” is modern storytelling.

### Ochpaniztli: Toci and the birth of Cinteotl-Itztlacoliuhqui

Ochpaniztli was a major veintena festival focused on Toci/Teteoinnan, fertility, war, renewal, sweeping, and maize.

In the Sahaguntine account, a woman representing Toci was killed and flayed. A priest wore most of her skin and became her living divine image. A section of thigh skin was fashioned into the **mexayacatl**, the “thigh-face” mask, worn by the male representative of her son Cinteotl. The son was presented with the recurved, notched cap called *itztlacoliuhqui*, identified with frost. Ritual pursuit, mock combat, offerings, quail sacrifice, and movement through the ceremonial precinct followed.

The complex united death and agricultural renewal without reducing either to metaphor. A human being was killed; her body was processed ritually; divine identities were materially transferred through skin and costume; the maize son appeared from the dead mother at the threshold of the frost season. [**Secure early-colonial report of pre-conquest ritual**]

Scholars differ in emphasis:

- **Seler:** celestial and seasonal drama, including the union or rebirth of divine powers.
- **Graulich:** ritual replay of cosmogony, transgression, death, and renewal.
- **Carrasco and others:** political and sacrificial dimensions of fertility ritual.
- **Recent ritual studies:** Toci’s martial character, staged battles, gendered transformation, and the spatial itinerary through Tenochtitlan.

The text explicitly supplies the Cinteotl-Itztlacoliuhqui equation. Interpretations of the god as “dead maize,” “frozen seed,” “Morning Star emerging from the underworld,” or “sacrificed new Sun” are scholarly models.

### The 1 Lizard trecena and punishment

The *Telleriano-Remensis* and cognate *Vaticanus A* place Itztlacoliuhqui over the trecena 1 Lizard. The commentary associates the lizard with naked terrestrial misery and makes the deity a patron of ominous or punitive consequences. A man and woman shown dead on the following folio are interpreted as adulterers punished before him—the man by stones, the woman by hanging or strangulation, with injuries also depicted.

This is not a continuous myth but an **augural dossier**: a calendrical period, its patron, characteristic fates, moralized explanation, and exemplary punishment. The colonial commentary may combine indigenous divination with Christian language about sin.

### The Borgia sacrificial sequence

Pages 29–46 of the *Codex Borgia* present an elaborate sequence involving temples, divine impersonation, offerings, bundles, blood, death, and rebirth. Some readings identify pages 41–42 with a human sacrifice offered to Itztlacoliuhqui; others see the god himself as sacrificed or embodied in a dough image. Because the manuscript has no explanatory prose, reconstructions differ substantially.

The responsible conclusion is:

- the imagery securely connects an Itztlacoliuhqui-like being with sacrifice, bundled death, and ritual transition;
- neither Seler’s astral mythology nor any single modern ritual scenario can be treated as the manuscript’s verbatim narrative;
- the sequence demonstrates that the being’s significance extended beyond “weather god.”

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No securely identified independent temple

No structure excavated at Tenochtitlan or elsewhere has been securely identified as a temple belonging solely to Itztlacoliuhqui. No independent priestly college, estate, treasury, or hereditary office is known under his name. This sharply distinguishes him from Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, Tezcatlipoca, Xipe Totec, or major marketplace and ward gods.

His documented cultic presence is instead embedded in:

- the *tonalpohualli* and divinatory books;
- Ochpaniztli;
- Cinteotl’s ritual impersonation;
- frost observation;
- sacrificial imagery;
- regional ritual speech.

### Calendrical worship

As patron of the trecena beginning 1 Lizard, he would have mattered to diviners, births, named days, auguries, and activities scheduled under that period. The *Borbonicus* image is therefore not necessarily a temple cult portrait; it is the operating image of a calendrical power.

### Ochpaniztli rites

The clearest ritual encounter involved Cinteotl’s personator becoming Itztlacoliuhqui through costume and the thigh-skin mask. Offerings included incense and sacrificed quail; the broader ceremony included the killing and flaying of Toci’s ixiptla, martial performances, and state participation.

It would be misleading to say simply “people were sacrificed to Itztlacoliuhqui.” The named human victim embodied Toci; Itztlacoliuhqui emerged through the son’s regalia and bodily relic. Some Borgia interpretations posit a more direct sacrifice to or of Itztlacoliuhqui, but those remain debated.

### Human sacrifice: textual and archaeological evidence

Human killing, decapitation, heart extraction, flaying, display, and bodily processing are extensively documented in Mexica texts and archaeology. The 2015–2017 investigations of the Huei Tzompantli near the Templo Mayor uncovered skull racks and towers containing human remains, corroborating the large-scale institutional practice of sacrificial display. The Templo Mayor offerings likewise include sacrificed adults and children.

This evidence confirms the broader sacrificial system in which Itztlacoliuhqui’s imagery and Ochpaniztli identity operated. It does **not** identify those skulls as victims of Itztlacoliuhqui. [Science report on the excavations](https://doi.org/doi%3A10.1126/science.aau5404)

### Images and offerings

Paper ties, bundled posture, sacrificial knives, cotton, and the pierced cap make the god resemble a consecrated offering. Recent scholarship accordingly proposes that his iconography represents a deity who has himself been defeated and sacrificed by the Sun. This may explain why no grand independent temple is known: the figure functions as a ritually altered state within larger sacrificial and agricultural complexes. That remains a reconstruction, not a demonstrated institutional fact.

### Theophoric names

The surviving evidence does not reveal a significant population of people bearing Itztlacoliuhqui as a theophoric personal name. Occurrences of the word in annals can also refer to persons or groups and must not automatically be counted as evidence of worship. No popularity estimate comparable to that available for major gods can be made.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late Postclassic, before ca. 1521

The diagnostic being was established in central Mexican divinatory iconography. Its attributes already included the curved cap, covered face, striping, calendrical patronage, and sacrificial or deathlike wrapping. The Borgia-group distribution shows that this was not exclusively a Tenochtitlan invention.

What cannot be established is the figure’s original center, date of origin, or whether “Itztlacoliuhqui” was the name used in every community represented by those manuscripts.

### Conquest threshold, ca. 1500–1530

The *Codex Borbonicus* records the figure as patron of 1 Lizard in a manuscript preserving Mexica ritual-calendrical conventions. Whether painted just before or shortly after the invasion, it is the closest surviving visual witness to the living pre-conquest system.

### First colonial documentation, ca. 1540s–1570s

Indigenous artists and informants working under colonial conditions recorded the name and its meanings:

- Telleriano painters and annotators connected the figure to 1 Lizard, punishment, misery, and the covered face.
- Sahagún’s collaborators explicitly called frost Itztlacoliuhqui.
- The Ochpaniztli account preserved the Cinteotl identity.
- The *Leyenda de los Soles* preserved the Morning Star’s defeat and transformation into frost.

Spanish conquest, epidemic mortality, destruction of temples and ritual books, and Christian suppression ended the public state system that had sustained these rites. There is no documentation of a special anti-Itztlacoliuhqui campaign; suppression followed the general dismantling of indigenous temples, sacrifices, priesthoods, and calendrical divination.

### Late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries

The Telleriano material was copied and expanded in the *Codex Vaticanus A*. Ruiz de Alarcón’s 1629 record shows continued ritual use of calendrical names such as 1 Lizard in provincial Nahua practice. Missionaries reclassified these powers as superstitions or diabolical agencies. This is evidence of partial survival, not of an unchanged public cult.

### Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries

No continuous institutional cult of Itztlacoliuhqui is documented. The figure survived chiefly in manuscripts removed into European collections. Colonial copying preserved the god while simultaneously detaching the images from their original ritual use.

### Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century rediscovery

Facsimile publication and comparative study made the figure visible to scholarship. Eduard Seler’s commentaries established the curved-obsidian translation and aligned images across the Borgia group, *Borbonicus*, and Telleriano-Ríos traditions. His astral interpretations were enormously influential but sometimes converted polyvalent ritual images into overly definite celestial narratives.

### Mid-to-late twentieth century

Key developments included:

- Sullivan’s study of the mask of Itztlacoliuhqui and its role in Ochpaniztli;
- Nicholson’s 1971 typology placing him in the Tezcatlipoca complex;
- Thompson’s explanation of the Morning Star/frost relationship through the cold of dawn;
- Graulich’s cosmological and sacrificial readings;
- Boone’s emphasis on divinatory structure and ritual cycles;
- Šprajc’s study of Venus, rain, and maize associations.

During the same period, encyclopedia treatments increasingly compressed these debates into a single character biography: cheerful Dawn God, rebellion against the Sun, transformation into the bitter blind god of cold justice.

### Twenty-first century

Digital facsimiles from the BnF and UNAM now permit direct examination of Telleriano folios. Material study of the *Borbonicus* has refined but not eliminated the dating debate. Recent studies emphasize bundled death, sacrifice, eastern cold sky, and the relationship between divine images, ritual offerings, and otherworldly geography.

Modern Indigenous cultural revitalization and Mexicayotl communities invoke many Mexica divine names. Public evidence does not establish the size, continuity, or standardized rites of a distinct contemporary Itztlacoliuhqui cult. Individual devotional practice may exist, but it should not be presented as an unbroken survival from Tenochtitlan.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### “Blind justice”

The most influential modern reinvention converts the god’s wrapped eyes into the blindfold of impartial justice. Early evidence supports covered eyes, punishment, and punished adulterers. It does not supply the European maxim that justice must not see rank or identity.

The construction probably arose through the convergence of:

- *Ixquimilli*, interpreted as “blindfolded one”;
- Telleriano punishment imagery;
- Seler’s “punishment” reading;
- modern familiarity with blindfolded Justitia.

“God of cold, punishment, and adverse fate” is supportable. “Aztec personification of incorruptible blind justice” is a modern philosophical recasting.

### Personality biography

Claims that he was originally joyful, compassionate, or benevolent, then became bitter, emotionless, and evil after Tonatiuh struck him have no identified early textual basis. They turn a change of cosmic state into modern character psychology. [**Modern invention**]

### Stone and “frozen matter”

Obsidian is stone, and the defeated Morning Star becomes frost, so modern retellings often say he was “turned to stone.” This is intelligible symbolic compression but not the surviving *Leyenda*’s exact claim. Elaborations in which he governs all lifeless matter, entropy, or metaphysical stasis are modern esoteric interpretations.

### Pulque, revelry, and moral punishment

Some modern encyclopedias connect him with pulque because Cinteotl, Huastec imagery, transgression, nakedness, and Tezcatlipoca-related revelry overlap in colonial interpretations. The association is indirect and historically layered. He is not securely attested as a principal pulque god comparable to Mayahuel or the Centzon Totochtin.

### Occult and neopagan reception

Unlike Quetzalcoatl or Tezcatlipoca, Itztlacoliuhqui did not acquire a substantial Renaissance Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, or early modern grimoire tradition. Searches for occult and neopagan material chiefly recover recent personal devotional pages, game concepts, online mythographies, and modern magical correspondences derived from codex imagery.

Claims that a named ancient mystery school transmitted his worship, that he belongs to a universal “left-hand path,” or that fixed correspondences link him with Saturn, tarot cards, chakras, or Western demons are modern inventions unless individually documented.

### Popular media

The figure remains relatively obscure. He appears more often in:

- independent artwork and tattoos based on *Codex Borbonicus*;
- proposed character kits for games such as *Smite*;
- fantasy worldbuilding;
- discussions comparing him to cold-, blindness-, or punishment-themed fictional characters;
- online mythology encyclopedias.

These retellings have materially strengthened the image of an “Aztec god of winter and blind justice,” even though central Mexico’s frost cycle and Nahua punitive divination are more precise historical categories.

### Political appropriation

No substantial fascist, white-supremacist, or state-nationalist cult centered on Itztlacoliuhqui has been documented. The broader Aztec pantheon has been used in Mexican nationalism, Indigenous revival, tourism, commercial branding, and occasionally racialized political discourse. Itztlacoliuhqui’s role within those movements appears minor and should not be inflated.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Seler and the comparative method

Eduard Seler created the foundational comparative apparatus for recognizing the same being across differently styled manuscripts. His work remains indispensable, especially for iconographic vocabulary, but his tendency to privilege astral mythology sometimes produced overly specific narratives from uncaptioned images.

### Sullivan and ritual embodiment

Thelma Sullivan drew attention to the **mask of Itztlacoliuhqui**, the thigh-skin mask, and the fusion of Cinteotl, frost, birth, and Ochpaniztli. Her philological use of the Sahaguntine materials helped correct approaches that isolated the calendrical image from performed ritual.

### Nicholson’s typology

H. B. Nicholson’s “Religion in Pre-Hispanic Central Mexico” (1971) organized deities into thematic complexes. Itztlacoliuhqui-Ixquimilli falls within the Tezcatlipoca complex. The typology is heuristic: it maps recurrent attributes and functions, not a native dogmatic taxonomy.

### Thompson and the dawn-cold explanation

J. Eric S. Thompson proposed that the relationship between Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli and Itztlacoliuhqui derives from two features of early morning: the Morning Star and dawn cold. This economical explanation remains influential but does not exhaust the sacrificial, maize, and punitive evidence.

### Graulich

Michel Graulich read Nahua festival cycles as ritualized myths of creation, fall, sacrifice, and renewal. His reconstruction of Cinteotl-Itztlacoliuhqui’s birth and the larger Ochpaniztli drama is influential, although critics caution that cosmological coherence may be imposed on heterogeneous city and manuscript traditions.

### Boone

Elizabeth Hill Boone emphasizes that divinatory manuscripts are operational books of time, fate, and ritual rather than illustrated prose myths. Her work makes it necessary to distinguish what the pictures visibly contain from the narrative a scholar constructs to connect them.

### Olivier and Tezcatlipoca

Guilhem Olivier’s study of Tezcatlipoca illuminates obscurity, mockery, transformation, punishment, sacrifice, and multiple identity in the wider complex. It supports comparison with Itztlacoliuhqui but does not erase the latter into Tezcatlipoca.

### Current debates

#### Is he Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli?

**For:** The defeated Morning Star becomes frost; frost is elsewhere named Itztlacoliuhqui; the covered face and forehead projectile match the narrative; both operate in dangerous dawn/Venus imagery.

**Against an unqualified equation:** No surviving version explicitly says “Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli took the name Itztlacoliuhqui”; the materials come from different manuscripts and contexts; Itztlacoliuhqui also has independent Cinteotl, trecena, and punitive identities.

**Assessment:** Strong contextual identification, but not timeless equivalence.

#### Is he Cinteotl?

**For:** Sahaguntine wording explicitly says “Cinteotl or Itztlacoliuhqui,” and the cap bears the name.

**Qualification:** The equation is specifically activated in Ochpaniztli. It does not make every representation or myth of Cinteotl an Itztlacoliuhqui episode.

#### Is the curved object a knife?

**For:** The name’s *itztli*, dark coloration, cutting associations, sacrifice, and Seler’s translation.

**Qualification:** Sahagún describes it as a cap with a recurved point and notched edge. The image may be a rebus or embodiment of the name, not a practical handheld knife worn literally on the head.

#### Is he a god of justice?

**For:** Punishment of adulterers, covered eyes, unfavorable fate, and later descriptions of corrective punishment.

**Against:** Early sources emphasize punishment, sin, misery, blindness, and frost; they never articulate impartial judicial abstraction. The “blind justice” formulation maps European Justitia onto Nahua imagery.

**Assessment:** “Punishment” is securely grounded; “justice” is interpretive; the Lady-Justice analogy is modern.

#### Was he worshipped with human sacrifice?

**For:** His name and costume occur inside the sacrificial Ochpaniztli complex; Borgia pages may show a sacrifice to or of him; paper ties and bundled form are sacrificial signs.

**Against an autonomous-cult claim:** The clearly described human victim represents Toci, not Itztlacoliuhqui. No independent temple, priesthood, or archaeologically identified victim assemblage belongs securely to him.

**Assessment:** He participated in a sacrificial religious system, but evidence for a separate recurring “sacrifice to Itztlacoliuhqui” is limited and disputed.

### State of the evidence

Principal objects and repositories include:

- *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, BnF, Paris, Mexicain 385;
- *Primeros Memoriales*, Real Biblioteca, Madrid, manuscript of Sahagún’s early research;
- *Florentine Codex*, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence;
- *Codex Borbonicus*, Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris;
- *Codex Borgia*, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana;
- *Codex Vaticanus B*, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana;
- *Codex Fejérváry-Mayer*, World Museum Liverpool;
- *Codex Laud*, Bodleian Library, Oxford;
- *Codex Chimalpopoca*, formerly Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City; the original disappeared in the mid-twentieth century, leaving copies and editions.

The loss of most pre-conquest ritual books and the disappearance of the *Codex Chimalpopoca* original impose permanent limits. The archive is not a complete pantheon handbook but a fragmentary convergence of divinatory images, missionary-era testimony, and later commentary.
