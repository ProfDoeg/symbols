# The Cihuateteo: Research Dossier

**Class:** A collective of deified dead women—especially women who died in a first childbirth—classified in sixteenth-century Nahua testimony as *tēteoh* (“deities/divine beings”), *cihuāpipiltin* (“noble women/princesses”), and *mocihuāquetzqueh* (“women who arose/stood up,” conventionally “woman-warriors”). Spanish clerical writers frequently recast them as demons, witches, or devils.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Late Postclassic central Mexican iconography, most securely the surviving Mexica stone *Cihuātēotl* sculptures dated stylistically to approximately 1400–1521. The Codex Borgia’s images on pages 47–48 may be equally early or earlier, but its precise place and date of manufacture remain disputed. The earliest explanatory alphabetic testimony was collected after the conquest: Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* at Tepepolco in 1559–1561, followed by the mature *Florentine Codex*, compiled principally in the 1560s–1570s.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Preconquest attestation]** Surviving object or pictorial manuscript securely or probably produced before 1521.
- **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]** Nahuatl-language evidence recorded with Indigenous informants and writers during the sixteenth century.
- **[Early colonial clerical account]** Spanish missionary description, valuable but shaped by evangelization and demonological language.
- **[Regional variant]** Evidence outside the narrowly Mexica/Tenochtitlan corpus or a local version not safely generalized.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A modern interpretation joining evidence that does not itself state the conclusion explicitly.
- **[Colonial syncretistic development]** A post-1521 fusion, analogy, translation, or transformation.
- **[Folklore]** A later vernacular tradition whose descent from preconquest religion may be possible but is not demonstrably continuous.
- **[Modern invention or retelling]** A recent claim, system, or narrative not securely present in the early evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

The Cihuateteo were not originally a single goddess with an individual biography. They were a recurring class of dangerous and honored female dead. Nahua ideology construed labor as combat: the mother attempted to “capture” the child, and a woman who died in that combat received a warrior’s death. Sahagún’s Nahua informants say that such a woman became a *mocihuāquetzqui*, was treated as sacred or divine, and joined the female dead associated with the western half of the sun’s course. Yet these dead were not simply benevolent heroines. On specified calendrical days they descended to crossroads, received offerings, afflicted children, and could cause seizures, paralysis, facial contortions, or sexual disorder. Honor and danger belong to the same documented complex; calling them either “goddesses” or “demons” alone suppresses half the evidence.

### Names

- **Cihuātēotl**, plural **Cihuātēteoh** or conventional Spanish/English **Cihuateteo**: from Classical Nahuatl *cihuātl*, “woman,” plus *teōtl*, a polyvalent term meaning deity, sacred or numinous being, or divine power. The literal rendering is “woman-deity”; the conventional plural “divine women” is preferable to “female demons.” The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl records colonial spellings including *cihoateteu*, *cioateteo*, and *çioateteu*. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Cihuāpipiltin**: *cihuātl* plus *pīpiltin*, “nobles,” “princes/princesses,” hence “noble women” or “princesses.” The term expresses elevated status, not necessarily biological descent from gods. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Mocihuāquetzqueh**, singular *mocihuāquetzqui*: commonly translated “woman-warriors” in context, though morphologically connected with *mo-cihuā-quetza*, “to rise/stand as a woman” or “the woman who has arisen.” Patrick Johansson stresses that this is a substantive ritual name, not simply a synonym mechanically interchangeable with every occurrence of *Cihuateteo*. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony; scholarly linguistic interpretation]**
- **Ixcuiname / Ixcuināmeh**: an obscure designation rendered by Johansson provisionally as “the bleary-eyed” or “rheumy-eyed ones.” Its exact derivation and whether every occurrence denotes precisely the childbirth dead remain disputed. **[Disputed early terminology]**
- **Tlazoltēteoh**: “filth deities” or deities connected with *tlazolli*, a word spanning refuse, moral/sexual transgression, and substances ritually transformed. Johansson relates this name to the dead women’s purificatory as well as threatening character. It should not automatically be translated “goddesses of sin.” **[Early terminology; disputed scope]**
- **Cihuātlāmpa**: “toward/place of women,” the west. It is the destination of the descending afternoon sun and the cosmographic quarter assigned to the female dead. It is a region, not another name for the beings.
- **Cihuātēotl + calendrical name**: surviving statues bear day names such as **Cē Calli**, “1 House,” and **Cē Ozomahtli**, “1 Monkey.” These are identifiers within the fivefold calendrical series, not secure personal biographies.

The five dangerous day signs are:

1. **Cē Mazātl** — 1 Deer  
2. **Cē Quiyahuitl** — 1 Rain  
3. **Cē Ozomahtli** — 1 Monkey  
4. **Cē Calli** — 1 House  
5. **Cē Cuāuhtli** — 1 Eagle  

They occur at regular fifty-two-day intervals through the 260-day *tonalpohualli*. **[Preconquest/early colonial calendrical system]**

### Classifications and their historical change

- In Nahua testimony they are “goddesses,” sacred dead, and female equivalents of warriors. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- In divinatory books they form a fivefold group paired with five male **Macuiltonaleque/Ahuiateteo**, and some scholarship places both within the broader category of **Tzitzimime**. The Codex Borgia itself presents the paired figures but supplies no alphabetic taxonomic sentence; the larger classification is interpretive and comparative. **[Preconquest image; scholarly reconstruction]**
- Spanish authors called them *diosas*, *demonios*, witches, or devils. These words document colonial translation and demonization rather than a preconquest Nahua consensus that the beings were morally equivalent to Christian demons. **[Early colonial clerical reinterpretation]**
- Modern museum labels often use “demon.” That captures their capacity to harm but not their honored solar and warrior status.

There is no established evidence that “Cihuateteo” was the name of one supreme goddess or that the five calendrical figures had elaborate individual personalities. Those are modern singularizations of a collective category.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Material and pictorial evidence

#### Monumental and portable sculpture, approximately 1400–1521

A group of monumental andesite or volcanic-stone female figures provides the earliest securely datable physical evidence. They kneel or sit aggressively, with skull-like faces, exposed teeth, circular eyes, disordered or elaborately looped hair, bare breasts, taut abdomens, and clawed hands and feet.

The Metropolitan Museum’s example, object 00.5.30, is dated to the fifteenth or early sixteenth century and carries the glyph **Cē Calli**, 1 House, on its head. Four closely related figures in Mexico City have been proposed as its companions. The Met suggests that a five-statue set may once have occupied a shrine, perhaps within the Tenochtitlan ceremonial precinct; that original grouping and location are plausible but not archaeologically proven because the objects lack controlled excavation contexts. **[Preconquest attestation; provenance-based reconstruction]**

The British Museum’s andesite figure, Am1990,10.1, carries **1 Monkey** and is dated approximately 1400–1521. It surfaced on a Scottish estate rather than in a controlled Mexican excavation, so its style and material are informative but its ancient placement is unknown. The Museum reports roughly a dozen comparable figures. **[Preconquest attestation; findspot lost]**

#### Codex Borgia, pages 47–48

The Codex Borgia is a screenfold of animal hide coated with a white ground, now Vatican Library Borg. mess. 1. It is generally treated as a preconquest or conquest-era ritual-divinatory manuscript from central or south-central Mexico, but neither its exact community of production nor a precise year is known. Pages 47–48 show five female deathly figures opposite five male figures conventionally identified as Cihuateteo and Macuiltonaleque. Each participates in calendrical and directional patterning. Because the manuscript has no indigenous alphabetic captions, identification depends upon comparison with early colonial texts and cognate pictorial books. **[Probable preconquest attestation]**

The female figures carry calendrical designations including 1 Deer, 1 Rain, 1 Monkey, 1 House, and 1 Eagle. Their skeletal, clawed, armed, or serpent-associated appearance demonstrates that the threatening fivefold complex predates Spanish prose descriptions. It does not by itself narrate the death of any individual mother.

### Sahagún corpus

#### *Primeros Memoriales*, 1559–1561

Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators gathered the Tepepolco materials later named *Primeros Memoriales*. The manuscript’s Nahuatl descriptions and pictures are the earliest systematic alphabetic record of the religious complex. Because the evidence was collected nearly forty years after Tenochtitlan’s fall under Franciscan supervision, it is not an untouched transcript of preconquest doctrine; nevertheless, its Indigenous language, named local setting, and proximity in time make it foundational. The standard complete scholarly publication is *Primeros Memoriales*, paleography and English translation by Thelma D. Sullivan, completed and revised by H. B. Nicholson, Arthur J. O. Anderson, Charles E. Dibble, Eloise Quiñones Keber, and Wayne Ruwet (1997).

#### *Florentine Codex*, Books 4, 5, 6, and related passages, approximately 1570s; manuscript completed by 1577

The surviving bilingual Nahuatl-Spanish manuscript is Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Mediceo Palatino 218–220.

- **Book 4**, on the divinatory calendar, records the Cihuateteo’s descents during day signs such as 1 Rain and 1 Eagle. Parents kept children indoors; shrines at road divisions were adorned with reeds, flowers, paper, rubber-spattered paper, food, drink, and incense. The younger dead women were sometimes said to be especially harmful. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Book 5**, on omens, records fears of night beings and crossroads, providing the omen-world in which Cihuateteo operated.
- **Book 6**, chapter 29 and adjacent childbirth speeches, gives the fullest account. Labor is “the hour of death”; successful delivery is the capture of a child. If the mother dies, the midwife addresses her as one who has earned a precious warrior’s death. Her body is guarded because warriors and ritual specialists seek hair, fingers, or an arm for their supposed efficacy in war or sorcery. The dead woman becomes divine. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- Sahagún’s Spanish column paraphrases and sometimes moralizes the Nahuatl. Anderson and Dibble’s twelve-volume English edition, published 1950–1982, remains indispensable, but the Digital Florentine Codex now permits comparison among manuscript image, Nahuatl text, Spanish column, and translations.

The formula “women who died in their **first** childbirth” appears prominently in colonial descriptions and later summaries. It should not be silently broadened to every maternal death. Some passages speak more generally of dying in labor, while the terminological emphasis on a first delivery may mark the paradigmatic or highest-status case. **[Textual variation]**

### Other sixteenth-century sources

- **Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*, composed mainly in the 1570s–1580s:** important for Cihuacoatl, childbirth-war symbolism, temples, festivals, and the broader female earth-deity complex. Durán does not supply an independent, full narrative biography of “the Cihuateteo.” **[Early colonial clerical account]**
- **Codex Magliabechiano**, mid-sixteenth century, fol. 76r, and its cognate **Codex Tudela**, fol. 46r: portray a skeletal, wild-haired, clawed being commonly classified as a *tzitzimitl*. Comparison with Cihuateteo iconography is significant, but simply relabeling every such figure a Cihuateotl erases distinctions. Cecelia Klein argues that colonial authors increasingly demonized and masculinized the formerly prominent female Tzitzimime. **[Early colonial pictorial evidence; contested identification]**
- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis** and **Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos**, sixteenth century: valuable for calendrical omens, Tzitzimime, and afterlife classifications, but they do not give a single self-contained Cihuateteo cycle.
- **Codex Borbonicus**, probably immediately before or after conquest: documents the *tonalpohualli* and annual ritual system. Its precise pre- or postconquest date remains debated. It supplies calendrical context, not a biography of the collective.
- **Codex Mendoza**, commissioned around 1541: records tribute, conquest history, and social upbringing. It illuminates Mexica gender roles but is not a principal Cihuateteo narrative source.
- **Codex Borgia-group manuscripts**—Borgia, Vaticanus B, Fejérváry-Mayer, Laud, and Cospi—preserve cognate divinatory structures. Elizabeth Hill Boone treats these as books of time and fate rather than Western-style narrative myth books.
- **Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, **Leyenda de los Soles**, and **Histoyre du Mechique**, preserved in colonial manuscripts: essential for Nahua cosmogony, Cihuacoatl, the suns, and divine histories, but none supplies an independent origin myth for the Cihuateteo collective.
- **Cantares Mexicanos**, largely recorded in the later sixteenth century: indispensable for Nahua poetics, warfare, flowers, death, and remembered ritual discourse. A discrete Cihuateteo narrative has not been securely isolated in it.

**Finding from absence:** The surviving corpus contains no canonical story in which the Cihuateteo are born together, choose a leader, conduct an extended war, or experience a final collective fate. Their documented “history” consists chiefly of ritual classification, solar service, calendrical descent, funerary treatment, and iconographic repetition.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

1. **Death in childbirth:** Their defining origin. Childbirth is represented through martial language, and maternal death is valorized as death in combat. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
2. **The western solar course:** Warriors who died in battle or sacrifice escorted the sun through the eastern/morning portion of its journey; the childbirth dead received it at noon and conducted it toward the west, *Cihuātlāmpa*. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
3. **Crossroads and liminal space:** Their terrestrial shrines stood at road junctions or street divisions. Crossroads joined cosmographic directions and marked socially dangerous boundaries. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
4. **Calendrical danger:** Their descents recurred on the five “1” days listed above. **[Preconquest pictorial and early colonial textual attestation]**
5. **Children’s illness:** They were feared as attackers or possessors of children, causing convulsions, paralysis, distortion, or wasting. Parents therefore restricted children’s movements on their days. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
6. **Sexual disorder:** Some colonial accounts associate their influence with adultery or illicit desire. Whether this was a preconquest category or intensified by Spanish moral vocabulary must be judged passage by passage.
7. **Ritual power in bodily remains:** Hair, a finger, or the left forearm of a dead parturient woman was coveted as martial or magical equipment. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**

### Standard iconography

Recognizable attributes include:

- skull-like or fleshless face;
- exposed teeth and open mouth;
- large circular eyes;
- disordered, swirling, or tightly looped hair;
- bare breasts;
- either a taut, youthful abdomen or, in related images, signs of the postpartum body;
- clawed hands and feet;
- long skirt, sometimes with death, bone, or geometric motifs;
- serpent belt or earth-goddess regalia in some examples;
- an aggressive forward-leaning posture;
- a day-sign glyph carved on the crown.

The tension between exposed breasts and a taut, non-postpartum abdomen in monumental figures has been interpreted as emphasizing frustrated maternity: the woman acquired maternal status but died before nursing or completing postpartum life. This is a modern iconographic reading, not an explanatory sentence preserved from a Mexica sculptor. **[Scholarly reconstruction]**

The five Cihuateteo on Borgia 47–48 carry different colors, accoutrements, and day signs. Their individuality is calendrical and directional. Assigning each a stable modern-style personality exceeds the evidence.

### Earlier and regional comparanda

At El Zapotal, Veracruz, archaeologists recovered large ceramic female figures called “Señoras de la Tierra” and interpreted as *Cihuateteotl*, associated with an ossuary and a monumental Mictlantecuhtli. The site belongs principally to the Classic-period Gulf Coast tradition, centuries before the Mexica state. The formal and thematic resemblance may demonstrate a long Mesoamerican history of dangerous female death/earth beings, but calling them literally the same Cihuateteo known from sixteenth-century Nahua texts is an archaeological interpretation, not an inscribed ancient identification. **[Regional archaeological interpretation]**

### Animals, materials, directions, and numbers

- **Direction:** west.
- **Time:** afternoon from solar zenith to sunset; nocturnal descent in some descriptions.
- **Number:** five, in the recurring calendrical set.
- **Day signs:** Deer, Rain, Monkey, House, Eagle, each with coefficient 1.
- **Places:** crossroads, road divisions, western quarter, childbirth chamber, grave, and shrines.
- **Offerings:** paper vestments or banners, rubber-spattered paper, reeds or rushes, flowers, food, drink, and white/copious incense.
- **Bodily emblems:** hair, fingers, arm; these belong to the dead woman’s postmortem ritual potency.
- **Solar association:** securely attested.
- **Lunar, Venusian, or “star demon” identity:** possible through comparison with Tzitzimime, but not a safe universal equation.

There is no secure evidence for a single exclusive Cihuateteo color, animal, weapon, temple, priestly college, or annual festival.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

The Cihuateteo have no documented divine parentage, consort, or divine offspring. They originate as human women transformed through a particular death. Assertions that they are literally the daughters of Cihuacoatl, wives of dead warriors, or inhabitants sent out by Mictlantecuhtli are unsupported unless attached to a specific late local or modern source.

Cihuacoatl is their closest divine analogue: a goddess of maternity, midwifery, war, earth, death, and terrifying nocturnal manifestation. Shared iconography and functions make association strong, but the sources do not reduce the collective to mere avatars or biological daughters of Cihuacoatl.

### Every securely documented or explicitly represented divine interaction

- **Tonatiuh / the Sun — solar escort:** The Cihuateteo receive the sun at zenith and accompany or carry it toward the western horizon. *Florentine Codex* and related Sahaguntine testimony, sixteenth century. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Dead male warriors — complementary service:** Male warrior dead conduct the sun through the eastern or morning course; the childbirth dead perform the western portion. This is a coordinated cosmological division rather than a narrated meeting. Sahaguntine corpus, sixteenth century. **[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**
- **Cihuacoatl — functional and iconographic association:** Both belong to the childbirth-war-earth-death complex; the dead woman can be addressed through Cihuacoatl/Quilaztli imagery, and Cihuateteo sculptures share skeletal faces and serpent/earth regalia. Sahagún and comparative sculpture, fifteenth–sixteenth centuries. **[Attested association; exact hierarchy reconstructed]**
- **Macuiltonaleque/Ahuiateteo — paired divinatory appearance:** Five Cihuateteo face or correspond to five male beings on Codex Borgia pages 47–48. **[Probable preconquest pictorial attestation]**
- **Tzitzimime — categorical association:** Cihuateteo and their male counterparts are treated by several modern interpreters as members or manifestations of the larger Tzitzimime class. The Codex Borgia supplies imagery; colonial texts supply overlapping names and attributes. **[Scholarly reconstruction; not an explicit Borgia caption]**
- **Tlazolteotl/Ixcuina — nominal and functional overlap:** Names involving *Tlazolteteoh* and *Ixcuiname*, plus shared relations to sexuality, childbirth, purification, and dangerous female divinity, generate an association. No surviving narrative records Tlazolteotl meeting the Cihuateteo. **[Early terminological overlap; disputed identity]**
- **Tlaltecuhtli/earth deities — iconographic affinity:** Skeletal jaws, claws, birthing posture, death, and earth consumption are comparable. No textually narrated interaction is known. **[Scholarly iconographic comparison]**
- **Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl — no securely documented direct interaction:** The Cihuateteo do not follow the ordinary dead’s route to Mictlan in the principal account. Modern claims making them servants of Mictlan should therefore be labeled unsupported or late.
- **Huitzilopochtli — no securely documented direct interaction:** Their warrior status and the solar-war ideology of Tenochtitlan create thematic proximity, but no early source securely narrates a meeting, command, battle, or genealogy.
- **Coyolxauhqui — no securely documented direct interaction:** Both concern violent female bodies and Mexica cosmic warfare, but no surviving early episode joins them.
- **Quetzalcoatl — no securely documented direct interaction:** Shared presence in the broader pantheon is not interaction.
- **Xochiquetzal — no securely documented direct interaction:** Both touch women’s life-cycle and sexuality, but the corpus does not furnish a discrete encounter.

**Finding:** The Cihuateteo are unusually rich in structural associations but poor in named interpersonal myths. Converting symbolic parallels into divine “relationships” would manufacture a mythology the sources do not contain.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The battle of childbirth and apotheosis

**[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**

Pregnancy and labor were framed as campaign and battle. At delivery the woman attempted to capture the infant. A successful mother had taken a captive; the midwife gave cries comparable to a warrior’s victory cry. Labor was also *imiquizpan*, “her hour/time of death.”

If she died with the child still within her or during the paradigmatic first labor, the midwife addressed her not as disgraced but as one rewarded with a precious death. She was washed and prepared; her husband and kin conducted the funeral. She had become *mocihuāquetzqui*, a woman who had stood up or fought as a warrior, and “they said she became a goddess.”

This is not evidence that every historical family felt only honor rather than grief. It is a ritual discourse placing catastrophic maternal death inside the state’s warrior cosmology.

### Solar service

**[Early colonial Indigenous testimony; no surviving extended narrative]**

Warriors slain in battle or sacrificed to the sun served the rising solar power through the eastern sky. At noon the Cihuateteo assumed the task and accompanied the sun downward to the west, *Cihuātlāmpa*. Some modern summaries add that they carried it through the underworld all night; the best-attested formulation restricts their distinctive service to noon-to-sunset. The nocturnal extension should be marked as interpretive unless a specific source is cited.

This myth simultaneously elevated maternal death and incorporated women into solar militarism. It did not erase their terrestrial menace.

### Calendrical descent and attacks on children

**[Preconquest pictorial framework; early colonial Indigenous explanation]**

On 1 Deer, 1 Rain, 1 Monkey, 1 House, and 1 Eagle the Cihuateteo descended. Parents prevented children from going outside or bathing. The spirits waited at crossroads, entered or struck children, and produced bodily distortion, seizure, paralysis, madness, or death.

Shrines at intersections were decorated and supplied with paper, rubber, flowers, food, drink, and incense. These offerings were propitiatory acts toward beings whose sacred prestige did not make them safe.

Differences among the day-sign accounts matter. Sahagún reports on 1 Eagle that not all descended: the younger women did, and they were the most feared. This implies differentiation within the collective rather than a perfectly uniform five-member system.

### Contest over the corpse

**[Early colonial Indigenous testimony]**

The dead woman’s corpse was guarded for four nights or days in accounts preserved by Sahagún. Young warriors attempted to take the middle finger and hair, placing them with or inside their shields in the belief that they conferred courage, blinded enemies, or protected their bearer. Sorcerer-thieves sought the left arm or hand for nocturnal housebreaking: an arm taken from a woman who died in first childbirth could stupefy a household while the thieves entered.

This is one of the clearest cases in which an honored person’s body became a contested sacred resource. The source describes theft, vigilance, and magical use; it does not describe human sacrifice to create Cihuateteo. The women became such beings through maternal death, not ritual killing.

### Transformation after solar service

Some later summaries say the solar companions served for four years and then became hummingbirds, butterflies, moths, or other bright flying creatures. The four-year transformation is securely associated in Nahua sources with the warrior dead; its automatic extension to all Cihuateteo is less consistently stated. Where applied to them, it should be treated as a later systematization or scholarly harmonization, not an uncontested core doctrine.

## 6. Cult, shrines, and ritual practice

### Crossroads shrines

The most securely documented cult sites were small **oratories at street and road divisions**. On descent days worshippers:

- cleaned or decorated the shrine;
- placed reeds/rushes and flowers;
- covered images with paper;
- offered paper marked with liquid rubber;
- offered food and drink;
- burned white incense.

This was a recurrent calendrical cult, not merely frightened avoidance. “Cult” is therefore justified, although there is no evidence that it possessed the centralized institution, landed endowment, or permanent priesthood of Huitzilopochtli or Tlaloc.

The monumental day-sign statues may have stood together in such a shrine or in Tenochtitlan’s sacred precinct. Their original archaeological contexts are missing, so neither location is proven.

### Childbirth and funerary specialists

Midwives were the principal ritual voices in the surviving childbirth account. They cared for the woman, interpreted labor as battle, announced victory or death, washed and addressed the deceased, and participated in funerary action. Calling them an organized “Cihuateteo priesthood” goes beyond the evidence.

The woman’s husband, kin, and other women conducted and protected the burial. Warriors and magical specialists who attempted to steal body parts were not officiants of a regular public sacrifice but rival claimants to the corpse’s power.

### Offerings and images

Sahagún describes votaries dressing or covering images in paper. This confirms physical cult images in addition to surviving large sculptures. Flowers, prepared foods, drink, incense, paper, and rubber are securely attested. No passage establishes an exclusive animal victim or regular human victim sacrificed specifically “to the Cihuateteo.”

### Human sacrifice: direct and contextual evidence

Human sacrifice was an extensively documented and archaeologically attested part of Mexica public religion. The Huei Tzompantli excavations begun at 24 República de Guatemala in 2015 and continued in 2017 identified architectural remains of Tenochtitlan’s great skull display; associated phases date principally to 1486–1502 and 1502–1521. This materially corroborates skull-display practices described in conquest-era and colonial accounts.

That evidence must not be misapplied. It verifies the broader sacrificial system of the same capital and period; it does **not** show that the excavated persons were offerings to Cihuateteo. Likewise, the Coyolxauhqui stone discovered in 1978, the Tlaltecuhtli monolith found in 2006, and the Templo Mayor’s numerous offerings illuminate Mexica death, earth, solar war, and bodily dismemberment but are not direct Cihuateteo cult deposits.

No evidence supports “temple prostitution” in their cult. Sexual-danger associations and Spanish accusations against Indigenous religion are not documentation of such an institution.

### Temples and administration

No securely identified major temple dedicated solely to the collective is known. Durán’s and Sahagún’s accounts of Cihuacoatl temples cannot simply be reassigned to the Cihuateteo. There is no documented hereditary Cihuateteo priesthood, treasury, estate, or imperial office. The evidence supports distributed crossroads worship and childbirth/funerary ritual more strongly than a centralized institution.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before approximately 1200: possible antecedents

Classic Veracruz “earth lady” figures, especially El Zapotal, and other Mesoamerican skeletal female beings demonstrate that childbirth, earth, female death, and terrifying divinity had a long history. Direct genealogical descent into the Mexica Cihuateteo is possible but unproved. **[Regional antecedent; scholarly reconstruction]**

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1521

The Borgia-group pictorial tradition formalized a fivefold female complex inside the 260-day divinatory calendar. Mexica stone sculptures gave these beings monumental bodies and named day signs. By the last century before conquest, the complex joined maternal death, warrior ideology, western solar service, crossroads offerings, and childhood danger. **[Preconquest attestation]**

### 1521–1580s: conquest, suppression, and documentation

Spanish conquest destroyed the Mexica state cult and much of the Tenochtitlan ceremonial precinct. Missionaries suppressed offerings classified as idolatry, destroyed images, and replaced public religious institutions. At the same time, Indigenous elders, writers, and painters working with Sahagún preserved the most detailed testimony.

Colonial vocabulary changed the beings. *Tēteoh* became Spanish *dioses* when described ethnographically but *demonios* when judged theologically. Klein’s iconographic study argues that the formerly female Tzitzimime complex was progressively assimilated to Christian devils and made more masculine in colonial descriptions. Cihuateteo remained recognizable but were increasingly translated as witches or demons. **[Colonial transformation]**

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: fragmentation into folklore

The organized calendrical cult disappears from the documentary record. Motifs persisted in stories about dangerous nocturnal women, crossroads apparitions, child-stealing spirits, witches, and crying mothers. Continuity is plausible at the level of motifs and Nahua categories; a continuous, self-identified Cihuateteo religious institution is not documented.

### Twentieth century: archaeological and scholarly reconstruction

Museum catalogues and studies by Eduard Seler, Alfonso Caso, H. B. Nicholson, Esther Pasztory, Michel Graulich, Cecelia Klein, Elizabeth Hill Boone, Patrick Johansson, Mary Miller, Karl Taube, and others reconstructed the class through codex comparison, linguistics, sculpture, and colonial texts.

Nicholson’s 1971 typology placed Mexica deities into conceptual complexes. It remains influential but can make fluid and overlapping beings look like fixed members of a systematic pantheon. Boone’s work instead emphasizes the divinatory books as operational systems of time and meaning.

The 1978 discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone initiated the modern Templo Mayor Project. The 2006 Tlaltecuhtli discovery and the 2015–2017 Huei Tzompantli excavations transformed knowledge of the material setting in which death, earth, sacrifice, and solar warfare were enacted, although none is a direct discovery of the Cihuateteo cult.

### Twenty-first century

The Cihuateteo are prominent in museums, feminist and decolonial readings, fiction, online mythology, modern Mexica-identitarian practice, and eclectic Pagan or occult material. There is no reliable census of contemporary devotees and no evidence of an unbroken institutional cult from the sixteenth century.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### La Llorona

The claimed line “Cihuateteo → Cihuacoatl → La Llorona” must be divided into evidence and inference.

Sahagún records a terrifying Cihuacoatl who cried or wailed at night and, in the conquest-omen cycle, lamented for her children. The Cihuateteo likewise were female night beings associated with childbirth, child danger, crossroads, and cries. Later La Llorona tales concern a wandering, weeping woman and often dead or endangered children. The resemblance is real.

What is not securely documented is a continuous chain in which colonial Nahua communities explicitly renamed the Cihuateteo as La Llorona. La Llorona also belongs to international “vanishing hitchhiker,” white lady, infanticidal mother, and colonial romance tale-types. The safest finding is **motif convergence and possible Indigenous contribution**, not identity. Álvaro Flores’s historical survey explicitly calls the degree of Cihuateteo influence on La Llorona an open question. Modern films and popular accounts that state “La Llorona was originally a Cihuateotl” turn a debated genealogy into fact. **[Folklore; modern simplification]**

### Guadalupe and Tonantzin

Sahagún complained that Indigenous pilgrims continued to call the Virgin worshipped at Tepeyac **Tonantzin**, “our revered mother,” the name/title associated with an earlier female sacred being there. This supplies direct evidence of colonial translation, suspicion, and continuity of pilgrimage vocabulary.

It does not directly identify the Tepeyac deity as the Cihuateteo collective. Tonantzin is a reverential title used within the wider mother/earth-goddess complex and is variously associated in scholarship with Cihuacoatl, Toci, Coatlicue, or other local divine forms. Because Cihuacoatl and the Cihuateteo share childbirth, death, and female sacred power, thematic echoes can be traced. A direct equation “Guadalupe = Cihuateteo,” however, is unsupported.

The Guadalupe apparition tradition was elaborated in print by Miguel Sánchez’s Spanish *Imagen de la Virgen María* (1648) and Luis Lasso de la Vega’s Nahuatl *Huei tlamahuiçoltica*, including the *Nican Mopohua* (1649). Modern scholarship disagrees over how best to describe its Nahua-Christian formation: simple replacement, negotiated translation, Indigenous Christian authorship, or syncretism. Louise Burkhart’s work cautions against treating every Nahua expression of Marian devotion as a concealed pagan survival. **[Colonial syncretistic development; disputed interpretation]**

### Occultism and neopaganism

No Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or early modern European grimoire tradition centered on the Cihuateteo has been documented. Claims that they possess ancient sigils, planetary seals, standardized invocations, initiatory ranks, or European occult correspondences are modern inventions unless accompanied by a traceable recent author.

Contemporary reconstructionist and devotional practitioners may honor them as ancestors, maternal dead, protectors, or embodiments of women’s endurance. Such practice can be religiously meaningful without being historically continuous. Historically responsible reconstruction distinguishes:

- attested offerings—paper, rubber, flowers, food, drink, incense;
- attested calendrical days;
- modern ethical and devotional interpretation;
- newly composed prayers and rituals.

Online claims that they were “Aztec vampires,” uniformly evil child-stealers, servants of Mictlantecuhtli, or a coven led by La Llorona have no secure early basis. **[Modern invention]**

### Literature, visual culture, games, and commerce

Modern fantasy and horror tend to individualize the collective, exaggerate corpse-like features, and use “Cihuateotl” as a monster-species name. Museum displays and popular art alternatively reclaim them as heroic mothers. Both selections amplify one authentic aspect while muting the other.

Commercial figurines, tattoos, game enemies, and Halloween imagery often copy the Met or British Museum statues. A work that reproduces the 1 House or 1 Monkey head glyph is therefore reproducing a particular surviving sculpture, not a generic “Aztec death goddess” symbol.

No well-documented modern extremist organization appears to have made the Cihuateteo a central emblem. Broader nationalist appropriations of “Aztec warrior women” exist, but a specific claim requires organization-level evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Divine women or sinister women?

Johansson frames the central problem: the sources simultaneously divinize and fear them. Earlier scholarship and museum prose sometimes resolved the tension by classifying them as demons; romantic recovery can resolve it oppositely by making them purely honored heroines. The evidence supports ambivalence: a powerful sacred dead can nourish cosmic order and threaten ordinary bodies.

### Did only women dying in first childbirth qualify?

Several colonial formulations specify first childbirth. Others speak simply of death in labor. Possible explanations include:

- strict qualification by first parturition;
- a paradigmatic first-birth case generalized in some descriptions;
- local difference among Sahagún’s informants;
- imprecision introduced by Spanish paraphrase.

The surviving evidence does not permit a universal rule covering every Nahua community.

### Are Cihuateteo a subset of Tzitzimime?

The Borgia imagery, skeletal features, calendrical organization, and colonial terminology support close association. Klein argues that Tzitzimime were prominently female before colonial demonization altered their gendered image. Yet the terms are not interchangeable in every source. “All Cihuateteo are Tzitzimime” is a defensible reconstruction in some taxonomies, not an uncontested indigenous definition.

### Were they five individuals?

The five day signs and five Borgia figures constitute a calendrical set. The sources also speak of a potentially numerous population of women who died in childbirth. The best explanation is that five calendrical manifestations organize a larger collective; reducing all maternal dead to exactly five persons mistakes ritual schematization for population.

### Was their cult centered in Tenochtitlan?

Mexica sculptures and Sahagún’s testimony demonstrate importance in central Mexico. Borgia-group evidence and Gulf Coast analogues show a larger regional horizon. Their monumental statues’ missing provenience prevents proof of a single Tenochtitlan temple.

### Problems of source mediation

The strongest prose was produced after conquest under missionary supervision. Spanish demonology affected translations, while Indigenous informants may have regularized, concealed, localized, or retrospectively interpreted practices. This does not make the record unusable; it requires comparison of Nahuatl, Spanish, image, object, and archaeology.

### State of the evidence

- Florentine Codex: Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence; digitally accessible.
- *Primeros Memoriales*: portions of the Códices Matritenses in Madrid collections; available in facsimile and critical edition.
- Codex Borgia: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana.
- Codex Borbonicus: Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris.
- Monumental Cihuateteo: Museo Nacional de Antropología, Museo del Templo Mayor, Metropolitan Museum of Art, British Museum, and other collections.
- El Zapotal material: INAH collections in Veracruz.
- Many sculptures lack controlled excavation records, sharply limiting reconstruction of their original shrines.

### Discredited or unproved claims that remain popular

- that the Cihuateteo were ordinary ghosts with no divine status;
- that they were exactly five historical women;
- that Cihuacoatl was their securely named queen or biological mother;
- that they served Mictlantecuhtli in Mictlan;
- that they carried the sun through the entire night as a securely universal doctrine;
- that La Llorona is simply a renamed Cihuateotl;
- that Guadalupe directly replaced the Cihuateteo;
- that archaeologists found sacrificial victims offered specifically to them in the Huei Tzompantli;
- that modern occult seals or invocations attributed to them are ancient.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Classic period, approximately 300–900 CE | Female earth/death figures at El Zapotal, Veracruz, later interpreted archaeologically as Cihuateteotl-like “Ladies of the Earth.” Direct identity with the later Nahua collective remains unproved. |
| Approximately 1200–1521 | Borgia-group divinatory tradition develops or preserves the fivefold calendrical female complex. |
| Approximately 1400–1521 | Surviving Mexica stone Cihuateotl statues, including 1 House and 1 Monkey examples, are produced. |
| Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century | Crossroads offerings and solar/childbirth-war beliefs operate in central Mexico, reconstructed from postconquest testimony and preconquest images. |
| 1521 | Fall of Tenochtitlan; state cult institutions and many images are destroyed, appropriated, buried, or abandoned under Spanish-Christian rule. |
| 1559–1561 | Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators compile the Tepepolco materials later called *Primeros Memoriales*. |
| Approximately 1560s–1577 | The *Florentine Codex* records calendrical descents, crossroads offerings, childbirth speeches, deification, funerary guarding, and corpse-part magic. |
| 1570s–1580s | Durán composes accounts of central Mexican gods and rites, including Cihuacoatl and the broader maternal-war complex. |
| 1648–1649 | Sánchez and Lasso de la Vega publish foundational Guadalupe apparition texts. Any Cihuateteo relationship is indirect, through debated Tonantzin/Cihuacoatl and Nahua-Christian continuities. |
| Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries | Codices enter or become documented in European collections; living institutional Cihuateteo worship is no longer securely recorded. |
| Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries | Scholars including Seler compare Borgia-group figures with Sahagún’s descriptions and stabilize the modern identification. |
| 1950–1982 | Anderson and Dibble publish the English critical translation of the *Florentine Codex*. |
| 1971 | H. B. Nicholson publishes his influential typological approach to central Mexican deities. |
| 21 February 1978 | Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone leads to the Templo Mayor Project; contextual, not direct Cihuateteo evidence. |
| 1993–1997 | Major facsimile and critical editions of *Primeros Memoriales* appear through the University of Oklahoma Press. |
| 2000 | Cecelia Klein publishes “The Devil and the Skirt,” arguing for the preconquest female nature and colonial demonization/masculinization of Tzitzimime. |
| 2006 | Patrick Johansson publishes his detailed linguistic and ritual study of the *mocihuaquetzqueh*. |
| 2 October 2006 | Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered at the Ajaracas property near the Templo Mayor; important comparative earth/death evidence. |
| 2007 | Boone’s *Cycles of Time and Meaning* advances contextual interpretation of the Borgia-group divinatory books. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavations identify substantial remains of the Huei Tzompantli; direct proof of the larger Mexica sacrificial system, not of sacrifices to Cihuateteo. |
| 2023 onward | The Digital Florentine Codex makes manuscript pages, transcriptions, translations, and images jointly searchable, permitting closer control over colonial translations. |
| Present | Cihuateteo persist in scholarship, museums, folklore comparison, art, fiction, Indigenous-revival discourse, and eclectic devotional practice; continuity of an organized preconquest cult is not established. |
