# Tecciztecatl: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male *teōtl*—a Nahua divine power or god—who becomes the moon in central Mexican cosmogony.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely datable **written naming** is colonial rather than pre-Conquest: *Tecuciztecatl* appears in Nahuatl materials gathered through Bernardino de Sahagún’s Indigenous scholarly project during the mid-sixteenth century, surviving most fully in Book VII of the *Florentine Codex*, completed about 1575–1577. The corresponding creation story also appears in the *Leyenda de los Soles*, a Nahuatl manuscript conventionally dated to 1558 but surviving in a later copy within the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. Certain pre-Conquest or early-colonial Borgia-group images have been identified as Tecciztecatl, but those identifications depend on later iconographic interpretation rather than accompanying phonetic captions. No securely identified pre-Conquest inscription naming him, individual temple, priesthood, or cult statue is presently known.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** Pre-Conquest evidence, or early-colonial Nahuatl testimony demonstrably preserving Indigenous discourse.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** A colonial account that became the best-known literary form of the tradition.
- **Regional variant:** A form belonging to a particular Nahua or neighboring central Mexican community.
- **Learned systematization:** Colonial or modern efforts to arrange disparate divine powers into a consistent pantheon or genealogy.
- **Syncretistic identification:** A historically situated equation with another divine figure.
- **Early-modern accretion:** A post-Conquest development not demonstrably pre-Hispanic.
- **Folklore:** Later oral or popular continuation.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An inference or identification proposed by researchers.
- **Modern invention:** A claim circulated as ancient for which no corresponding early evidence has been located.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[Securely attested early material / later canonical elaboration]** Tecciztecatl is the wealthy male god who volunteers to illuminate the world, undertakes four days of penitence beside the afflicted Nanahuatzin, recoils four times from the divine fire, and enters it only after Nanahuatzin. Both initially rise with solar brilliance. An unnamed god strikes Tecciztecatl’s face with a rabbit, diminishing his light and leaving the animal’s figure visible on the moon. The complete surviving account is in Sahagún’s *Florentine Codex*, Book VII, chapter 2, folios 2v–7v. The manuscript explicitly uses masculine Nahuatl references even though the Spanish column calls the moon *la luna*: the Getty’s digital edition notes that the figure who becomes the moon is grammatically and narratively male.

Tecciztecatl is therefore not simply an abstract “moon god.” His best-attested identity is an actor in the creation of the present sun and moon. Wealth, hesitation, belated self-sacrifice, reduced radiance, the lunar rabbit, and the conch-shell name form one narrative complex.

### Names and spellings

- **Tēcciztēcatl / Tecciztecatl.** A normalized Classical Nahuatl form commonly analyzed as *tēcciz(tli)*, “large shell, conch shell,” plus *-tēcatl*, “person or inhabitant of [a place].” A cautious rendering is “Person from the Place of the Conch” or “Conch-place person,” not automatically “Lord of the Conch.” The element *tēcciztli* is independently documented by Alonso de Molina in 1571 as “*otro caracol grande*” and by modern lexicography as a conch or large shell used as a trumpet.
- **Tecuciztecatl / Tēcuciztēcatl.** Sahagún’s common spelling. Colonial orthography was not standardized, and vowel length is usually unmarked.
- **Teucciztecatl / Teuciztecatl / Tecuciztecal.** Scribal, editorial, or popular variants. Final *-tl* is frequently lost or altered in European-language transmission.
- **Tecciztecatl Metztli.** A juxtaposition or compound found in modern indexing of colonial Nahuatl evidence: *mētztli* means moon and also forms the ordinary designation of the lunar body. It should not be assumed to have been a universal cult title.
- **Metztli.** “Moon.” Whether every image or invocation of *Metztli* denotes Tecciztecatl as a personal god is uncertain. Nahua celestial terminology could name a visible body, a calendrical period, and a divine agency without requiring modern taxonomic identity.

“Lord of the Conch Shell” is an intelligible loose translation but not a literal rendering of *-tēcatl*. Popular “Lord of Snails” is still less exact because *tēcciztli* in the relevant colonial vocabulary denotes a large marine shell or conch, not specifically the terrestrial animals ordinarily meant by English “snail.”

### Etymological cautions

**[Securely attested linguistic evidence]** Molina’s 1571 dictionary and later linguistic work support *tēcciztli* “large conch/shell.” The Nahuatl Dictionary gives reconstructed pronunciation approximately /teːksistɬi/ and documents the shell’s use as a trumpet.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Treating the entire name as *Tēcciztlān* + *-tēcatl*, “inhabitant of Tēcciztlān,” is morphologically attractive: Nahuatl ethnonyms regularly use *-tēcatl*. It does not establish that a historical cult center called Tēcciztlān existed.

**[Modern invention or unsupported popular elaboration]** Translations such as “God of the Old Moon,” “Lord of Pride,” or “Lord of Cowardice” are not lexical translations. Pride, wealth, and hesitation are interpretations of his conduct in the creation narrative.

### Classification and lunar gender

**[Securely attested early material]** In the *Florentine Codex*, Tecciztecatl is male. The Spanish grammatical femininity of *la luna* is not evidence for a female Nahua deity.

**[Regional variation / scholarly reconstruction]** Central Mexican lunar imagery is not uniformly male. Lunar associations also attach to female water, fertility, earth, childbirth, and pulque powers. Susan Milbrath stresses that postclassic central Mexico presents multiple lunar genders and roles. The *Leyenda de los Soles* calls the second luminary **Nahui Tecpatl**, “Four Flint,” and describes feminine-coded singing or dancing in some translations, rather than consistently naming Sahagún’s male Tecciztecatl. The proper conclusion is pluralism: Tecciztecatl is a male lunar person in one major Mexica account, not proof that “the Aztecs thought the moon was exclusively male.”

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

No securely readable pre-Conquest alphabetic or hieroglyphic caption naming Tecciztecatl has been demonstrated. The chronology must distinguish:

1. **Possible pre-Conquest imagery:** Deities carrying large white shells occur in manuscripts of the Borgia group. Identification as Tecciztecatl is plausible where the shell and lunar context converge, but is interpretive.
2. **Mid-sixteenth-century Nahuatl testimony:** Sahagún’s research began in the 1540s–1550s, working with Nahua elders and literate Indigenous collaborators at Tepepulco, Tlatelolco, and Mexico-Tenochtitlan.
3. **The surviving *Florentine Codex*:** Written and illustrated about 1575–1577, now Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence, MS Mediceo Palatino 218–220. Book VII, chapter 2 is the fullest primary account.
4. **The *Leyenda de los Soles*:** Conventionally dated internally to 1558; preserved in a later paper manuscript. Its relation to the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* is codicological rather than proof of common authorship.

### Principal primary corpus

#### *Florentine Codex*, Book VII, chapter 2

**Language and support:** Parallel Nahuatl and Spanish columns on European paper, with Indigenous illustrations.

**Date:** Approximately 1575–1577; underlying inquiries began earlier.

**Role:** The fullest Tecciztecatl narrative. It records:

- the assembly at Teotihuacan;
- Tecciztecatl’s volunteering;
- the gods’ selection of Nanahuatzin;
- four days of penitence;
- Tecciztecatl’s precious offerings;
- his four failed approaches to the fire;
- Nanahuatzin’s leap;
- Tecciztecatl’s following leap;
- the eastern appearance of two equally brilliant luminaries;
- the rabbit thrown into Tecciztecatl’s face;
- the gods’ deaths and the subsequent movement of the celestial bodies.

The useful critical English edition is Bernardino de Sahagún, *Florentine Codex: General History of the Things of New Spain*, Book 7, translated by Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble. The Library of Congress digitization identifies folio 228v as illustrating the rabbit in the moon.

#### Sahagún’s earlier memorial material

The *Primeros Memoriales*, based principally on Tepepolco testimony from about 1558–1561, preserves earlier stages of Sahagún’s project. It is essential for controlling the development of his compilation, although the complete polished Tecciztecatl episode belongs to the later Book VII recension. The standard edition is edited and translated by Thelma D. Sullivan and collaborators.

#### *Leyenda de los Soles*

**Language and support:** Classical Nahuatl in Latin script, on colonial paper.

**Date:** Text conventionally associated with 1558; surviving manuscript later.

**Role:** Gives a related Fifth Sun account but should not be treated as verbally identical to Sahagún. It names the poor, diseased solar candidate Nanahuatzin and, in published readings, identifies the second luminary as **Nahui Tecpatl** rather than consistently as Tecciztecatl. Xiuhtecuhtli has a directing or transforming role. The text dates the present sun as **4 Movement** and its birth to **13 Reed**.

The manuscript has been published with the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* under the editorial title *Codex Chimalpopoca*. Major editions/translations include Primo Feliciano Velázquez and John Bierhorst. The label “Codex Chimalpopoca” is itself nineteenth-century and can misleadingly suggest a unitary pre-Conquest codex.

#### *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*

A Nahuatl annalistic work surviving in the same manuscript assemblage. It provides wider cosmological and historical scaffolding but is not an independent full Tecciztecatl biography. References to the Fifth Sun should be checked against the *Leyenda* rather than attributed indiscriminately to the *Anales*.

#### *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*

A mid-sixteenth-century Spanish account based on Indigenous pictorial and oral materials. It preserves a sequence of suns and divine creation, but its arrangement differs from both Sahagún and the *Leyenda*. It does not justify importing every detail of the famous Tecciztecatl story into every Five Suns tradition.

#### *Histoyre du Mechique*

A French-language manuscript derived from lost Spanish missionary material, usually dated to the mid-sixteenth century. It is valuable for central Mexican cosmogony, but a recent source-critical study explicitly observes that it **does not name Tecciztecatl as Nanahuatzin’s rival**. Claims that it supplies his detailed genealogy are therefore suspect.

#### Durán

Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites* were composed in the later sixteenth century. Durán is indispensable for Mexica festivals, temples, sacrifice, and political religion, but no independent, comparably detailed Tecciztecatl cult is established there. Durán’s descriptions of solar sacrifice cannot automatically be reassigned to a Tecciztecatl priesthood.

#### Pictorial manuscripts

- **Codex Borgia:** Pre-Conquest or very early colonial ritual-divinatory manuscript, probably from Puebla-Tlaxcala or a neighboring region. Certain shell-bearing figures have been identified with Tecciztecatl, notably in Eduard Seler’s interpretive system. Elizabeth Hill Boone and other scholars caution that Borgia-group pictures encode ritual, calendrical, and cosmological complexes that cannot always be converted into European-style narrative scenes.
- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis and Codex Vaticanus A:** Early-colonial pictorial-historical manuscripts with Spanish or Italian glosses. A shell-bearing, richly adorned deity in the *Telleriano-Remensis* has been identified with Tecciztecatl or a related lunar figure; the identification is iconographic, not secured by a phonetic name beside the figure.
- **Codex Borbonicus:** Ritual-calendar manuscript, probably made around the Conquest. Relevant for trecena patrons and lunar-calendrical context, but not a narrative account of Tecciztecatl’s leap.
- **Codex Magliabechiano:** Mid-sixteenth-century festival and deity manuscript. It supplies comparative ritual iconography but no secure independent Tecciztecatl cult history.
- **Codex Mendoza:** Produced around 1541–1542. Its tribute, conquest, and educational sections do not provide a Tecciztecatl narrative.
- **Cantares Mexicanos:** Sixteenth-century Nahuatl song collection. It is crucial to Nahua religious-poetic language but does not supply a securely isolated Tecciztecatl hymn.
- **Sun Stone:** Late Mexica monument, probably carved between 1502 and 1521. It displays the sign 4 Movement and earlier cosmic eras. It does not name or portray Tecciztecatl unambiguously.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Securely attested early material]** Tecciztecatl’s certain domain is the moon as the second, diminished luminary created at Teotihuacan. His light is derivative only in sequence, not initially in quality: Sahagún’s account says that both celestial beings first shone alike. Lunar inequality is created afterward by the rabbit-blow.

**[Later interpretation]** He is associated with wealth, preciousness, self-regard, hesitation, and imperfect merit because his offerings and conduct contrast with Nanahuatzin’s poverty and resolution. The source narrates those contrasts; abstract portfolios such as “god of pride” are interpretive summaries.

### Offerings and regalia

In the *Florentine Codex*, Tecciztecatl’s four-day penance is ostentatiously rich:

- quetzal feathers instead of ordinary branches;
- balls of gold instead of grass or fir-needle bundles;
- precious-stone or jade spines instead of maguey thorns;
- red coral instead of bloodied thorns;
- fine incense.

Nanahuatzin gives green reeds, grass balls, genuine maguey spines stained with his own blood, and the scabs from his sores. The opposition is not “offering versus no offering” but simulated precious substitutions versus bodily austerity.

At the final investiture Tecciztecatl receives fine plumage and cloth; Nanahuatzin receives paper regalia. These contrasts belong securely to Sahagún’s narrative but should not be turned without corroboration into the costume of an annually impersonated Tecciztecatl.

### Conch shell

A large white conch carried on the back is the most commonly assigned visual diagnostic. The shell can signify the name and the moon’s pale, rounded body. However, conches also belonged to rain, water, wind, music, warfare, and ritual signaling. A shell alone does not prove that every figure is Tecciztecatl.

### Rabbit mark

**[Securely attested early material]** After the two transformed gods appear equally bright, the assembled gods ask how this can be. One—unnamed in the Nahuatl account—casts a rabbit into Tecciztecatl’s face. Its image remains visible. This is an etiological explanation for the dark lunar maria and for why the moon is dimmer than the sun.

**[Modern invention]** Naming the thrower as Quetzalcoatl, Tezcatlipoca, or another particular god is common in retellings but is not warranted by Sahagún’s wording unless a separate version is cited.

### Butterfly wings and other features

Images identified as Tecciztecatl sometimes show butterfly-like wings, white garments, jade jewelry, a flower band, a shell pectoral, and a large back-shell. Some of these attributes belong broadly to preciousness, fire, death, or transformation. Their assignment to Tecciztecatl is **modern iconographic reconstruction**, not a secure textual inventory.

### Calendrical association

Later tables commonly make Tecciztecatl a patron of the sixth trecena beginning **1 Miquiztli**, “1 Death,” sometimes paired with Tonatiuh. This association derives from scholarly readings of divinatory manuscripts. Because codices differ and figures can be polyvalent, it should be stated as a manuscript-specific identification, not a universal feast calendar.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No parentage, consort, offspring, or stable sibling group is given for Tecciztecatl in the principal Sahaguntine creation account.

The widespread claim that he is a son of **Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue** is repeated in modern encyclopedic literature, but a secure passage in the principal primary corpus has not been identified. The *Histoyre du Mechique* does not explicitly name Tecciztecatl as Nanahuatzin’s rival. Until a precise primary citation is produced, this genealogy must be classified as **learned systematization or modern unsupported repetition**, not established Mexica canon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The making of the Fifth Sun and moon

**Earliest full attestation:** Mid-sixteenth-century Nahua testimony preserved in Sahagún; final manuscript c. 1575–1577.

After a previous age ended, the gods gathered at Teotihuacan in darkness and asked who would illuminate the world. Tecciztecatl answered that he would accept the office. Asked for a second candidate, the gods selected Nanahuatzin, a poor, diseased god who had remained silent.

The candidates performed penance for four days on separate ritual structures. Tecciztecatl substituted valuable, immaculate objects for ordinary penitential materials and bodily bloodletting. Nanahuatzin used humble materials and shed his own blood. At midnight the gods assembled around the **teotexcalli**, the divine hearth or rock-oven.

Tecciztecatl was commanded to enter first. He ran toward the blaze but retreated from its heat. He attempted the act four times; ritual convention allowed no fifth attempt. Nanahuatzin was then ordered forward. He closed his eyes, rushed into the fire, and burned. Tecciztecatl, seeing this, followed.

The gods waited to see where the new light would emerge. Quetzalcoatl and certain others correctly looked east. Nanahuatzin rose first, transformed into a brilliant sun. Tecciztecatl followed, equally bright. The gods objected that two equal suns would destroy the necessary distinction in the cosmos. An unnamed god struck Tecciztecatl with a rabbit, dimming him; the rabbit’s figure remained on his face.

The new sun and moon at first stood still. The sun demanded the deaths of the remaining gods. Ehecatl performed the killings or impelled the process; Xolotl resisted and transformed repeatedly before dying. The wind then drove the sun into motion, followed by the moon.

### Interpretive function

**[Securely present in the narrative]** The story explains:

- why there are two luminaries;
- why the sun is brighter;
- why a rabbit appears on the moon;
- why divine self-sacrifice precedes the ordered cosmos;
- why motion requires further sacrifice after illumination exists;
- why Teotihuacan was understood as the place where gods were made.

**[Modern scholarly interpretation]** Scholars have read the contrast as one between inherited rank and achieved merit, precious substitution and genuine bodily offering, elite display and humble efficacy, or failed versus successful *tlamacehualiztli* (“merit-making,” penitential service). These readings are plausible but are not explicit sermon-like morals in the Nahuatl text.

### Important variants

1. **Sahagún Book I versus Book VII:** A shorter account can imply that the fire had diminished and the later entrant fell into ashes, naturally explaining weaker lunar light. Book VII instead foregrounds the rabbit-blow.
2. ***Leyenda de los Soles*:** Uses different names and directing gods, dates the sun as 4 Movement, and embeds the event in a different historical-cosmological sequence.
3. **Rabbit agent:** Sahagún does not name the thrower. Modern retellings frequently do.
4. **Tecciztecatl’s motivation:** “Proud” or “arrogant” is an interpretation of his wealth and initial volunteering. “Coward” is a moralizing condensation of four retreats, not a title in the source.
5. **Two suns:** The second body is not born already weak. Its subordination is an event occurring after emergence.
6. **Moon’s gender:** The Sahaguntine participant is male; other central Mexican lunar complexes are female or gender-ambiguous.

### Relation to Teotihuacan

The narrative situates the primordial event at already-ancient Teotihuacan, whose great urban florescence ended centuries before Mexica imperial power. “Here the gods were made” is an Indigenous post-Teotihuacan interpretation of the ruins, not archaeological evidence that Classic-period Teotihuacanos worshipped a god named Tecciztecatl. No Classic Teotihuacan inscription names him.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Direct cult evidence

No securely identified temple, permanent priestly office, festival, ixiptla impersonator, temple estate, tribute category, or sacrificial victim dedicated exclusively to Tecciztecatl has been established.

That negative finding matters. The richly developed cosmogonic role documented in Book VII does not entail an equally developed public cult.

### Penance and sacrifice within the myth

Tecciztecatl:

- fasts or performs penitence for four days;
- offers precious substitutions;
- approaches the fire four times;
- finally immolates himself.

This is **divine self-sacrifice in myth**, not evidence that human victims were burned for Tecciztecatl. Modern presentations sometimes blur those categories.

The myth nevertheless belongs to a wider Mexica sacrificial theology in which divine self-expenditure establishes a debt answered by human offerings. Archaeology independently confirms that Mexica human sacrifice was material practice, not solely a hostile Spanish allegation. Excavated sacrificial stones, cut human remains, dedicatory deposits, and skull-display structures corroborate major parts of the textual record.

### Templo Mayor evidence

The Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan was principally crowned by shrines to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc, not Tecciztecatl. Excavations begun after the 1978 discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monolith have recovered abundant evidence of state ritual and sacrifice, but no excavated complex has been securely assigned to Tecciztecatl.

The **Huei Tzompantli** section found during 2015–2017 excavations is direct evidence for the large-scale public display of human skulls. It contextualizes Mexica sacrificial religion but cannot be cited as a Tecciztecatl offering.

Similarly:

- The **Coyolxauhqui stone**, found on 21 February 1978, materializes the Huitzilopochtli–Coyolxauhqui narrative at the foot of the southern stair.
- The monumental **Tlaltecuhtli**, found on 2 October 2006, belongs to earth, death, and rulership complexes.
- Neither monument depicts Tecciztecatl merely because modern descriptions call Coyolxauhqui a “moon goddess.”

### Conch-shell ritual

Conch trumpets are well attested in Nahua ceremonies. Sahagún records conches sounded in ritual processions, and archaeological offerings contain marine shells transported far inland. These facts explain the cultural weight of Tecciztecatl’s name but do not prove that every ritual sounding of a conch invoked him.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1200 CE: antecedents without a name

Moon-rabbit imagery and varied lunar beings occur widely in Mesoamerica, including Maya and earlier central Mexican traditions. These establish deep regional antecedents for lunar animal imagery but not the named person Tecciztecatl.

### Late Postclassic, c. 1200–1521

A central Mexican divinatory and mythic complex connected conch shells, lunar imagery, penitence, fire, and cosmic succession. Borgia-group manuscripts may depict the relevant figure. Because their pictures lack alphabetic labels, certainty varies.

The Mexica appropriated Teotihuacan as the setting of cosmic origins. This is historically important but retrojects Late Postclassic theology onto Classic ruins.

### Early colonial documentation, 1520s–1580s

Spanish conquest dismantled Tenochtitlan’s state temples and Christian authorities suppressed public worship. Indigenous intellectuals nevertheless recorded extensive Nahua religious knowledge in alphabetic Nahuatl and pictorial form.

Tecciztecatl’s surviving record is largely a product of this colonial archive:

- Sahagún’s collaborative investigations;
- the *Leyenda de los Soles*;
- cognate pictorial manuscripts and annotations;
- missionary efforts to catalogue what Christianity classified as idolatry.

This archive is neither an untouched pre-Conquest transcript nor simply a Spanish fabrication. It is mediated testimony produced under colonial coercion by Nahua authors, painters, elders, and Christian friars.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

No evidence shows continuous institutional worship of Tecciztecatl by name. Rabbit-in-the-moon lore, lunar observance, agricultural timing, and Indigenous ritual persisted in many communities, but continuity of a motif must not be equated automatically with a secret Tecciztecatl cult.

### Nineteenth century

Mexican antiquarianism and European Americanist scholarship began publishing codices and arranging the gods into pantheon-like systems. Eduard Seler’s studies of the Borgia group were foundational but often assigned stable divine names to polyvalent images. Tecciztecatl became a catalogued “moon god.”

### Twentieth century

Critical editions changed the field:

- Anderson and Dibble made Sahagún’s Nahuatl text accessible in English.
- Miguel León-Portilla emphasized Nahua philosophical and cosmological thought.
- Henry B. Nicholson classified major Late Postclassic central Mexican deity complexes.
- Michel Graulich developed structural and historical readings of creation and sacrifice.
- Elizabeth Hill Boone reinterpreted divinatory manuscripts as systems of time, fate, ritual, and meaning rather than illustrated prose myths.
- Alfredo López Austin and Leonardo López Luján emphasized historically layered Mesoamerican cosmology.
- Eduardo Matos Moctezuma’s Templo Mayor work gave the documentary claims a greatly expanded archaeological context.

### Twenty-first century

The Getty’s Digital Florentine Codex now allows folio-level comparison of Nahuatl, Spanish, and imagery. Online popular culture has simultaneously amplified unsupported genealogies, moral titles, and confidently labeled Borgia images. Contemporary Indigenous and reconstructionist practitioners may include Tecciztecatl in personal observance, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes a large, continuous, or standardized modern cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, education, and nationalism

Tecciztecatl appears frequently in Mexican educational retellings of the Five Suns. These commonly sharpen the contrast into “the humble god versus the proud coward.” That is pedagogically effective but narrows the source’s more complex concern with offerings, merit, rank, and cosmic obligation.

The myth has also served modern discussions of social equality: a diseased and socially unimpressive being succeeds before a wealthy volunteer. Such readings are modern ethical receptions, not recoverable ancient dogma.

### Literature, visual art, comics, and games

Modern illustrators typically combine:

- an anthropomorphic male;
- a large white conch;
- lunar crescents;
- a rabbit;
- white or silver coloring;
- luxurious jewelry.

No single early image contains this complete standardized package. It is a modern composite assembled from the name, Sahagún’s narrative, and disputed codex identifications.

Tecciztecatl appears in myth collections, fantasy reference works, role-playing material, webcomics, and games, usually as either “Aztec moon god” or Nanahuatzin’s proud rival. These representations have materially popularized the name but rarely preserve the distinction between Sahagún and the *Leyenda de los Soles*.

### Esotericism and neopaganism

No significant Renaissance, Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or early modern European occult tradition centered on Tecciztecatl has been documented. Claims of ancient “Tecciztecatl invocations,” planetary seals, fixed correspondences, or hereditary initiatory rites should be treated as **modern inventions** unless accompanied by a verifiable manuscript citation.

Modern Mexicayotl, Indigenous-revitalization, and eclectic pagan practitioners may honor the moon or use Tecciztecatl’s name. Practices differ, and public web testimony cannot establish pre-Conquest continuity.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No substantial Tecciztecatl-specific extremist movement has been documented. Broader Mexica imagery has been appropriated by nationalist, racialist, and commercial projects, but attributing those projects to this relatively minor lunar figure without direct evidence would be misleading.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The principal debates

#### Is the Borgia-group figure really Tecciztecatl?

Selerian identifications rely on conch-shell and lunar attributes. Later scholars emphasize manuscript context, calendrical function, and iconographic polyvalence. The safest description is “a shell-bearing deity identified by some scholars as Tecciztecatl,” followed by manuscript and page—not “a portrait of Tecciztecatl” without qualification.

#### Does the name mean “Lord of the Conch”?

The noun “conch shell” is secure. “Lord” is loose; *-tēcatl* normally denotes a person or inhabitant. “Conch-place person” is more literal, though aesthetically awkward.

#### Was he always the moon?

The best-known story makes him the moon through transformation. That differs from assuming he existed from the beginning as a fixed departmental “god of the moon.” Nahua divine identities were relational, episodic, and capable of transformation.

#### Was he Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue’s son?

The claim is widespread online but lacks a verified passage in the principal early sources examined here. It should remain unestablished.

#### Who threw the rabbit?

Sahagún says one of the gods; he does not securely identify the thrower. Named answers are later harmonizations.

#### Was the moon male or female?

Tecciztecatl is male in Sahagún. Other lunar configurations are female or ambiguous. The evidence supports coexistence, not a single “Aztec belief.”

#### Did the myth authorize human sacrifice?

The narrative makes divine death and bodily offering conditions of cosmic order. Mexica state religion demonstrably practiced human sacrifice. But no direct evidence identifies a Tecciztecatl-specific human sacrifice, and the creation myth alone cannot supply one.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Attestation or historical event |
|---|---|
| Preclassic–Classic Mesoamerica | Rabbit-and-moon and varied lunar imagery provide broad antecedents; no named Tecciztecatl is securely identified. |
| c. 1–650 CE | Teotihuacan flourishes and declines. Centuries later Nahua accounts make its ruins the site where the sun and moon were created. |
| c. 1200–1521 | Borgia-group ritual manuscripts and related central Mexican iconography preserve shell-bearing and lunar divine figures; identification with Tecciztecatl remains partly interpretive. |
| c. 1450–1521 | Late Mexica imperial period in which the Five Suns cosmology and Teotihuacan origin traditions circulated. |
| c. 1502–1521 | Probable carving period of the Sun Stone, presenting 4 Movement and preceding cosmic eras but not naming Tecciztecatl. |
| 1519–1521 | Spanish invasion and fall of Tenochtitlan; subsequent suppression and dismantling of public Mexica temple institutions. |
| c. 1541–1542 | *Codex Mendoza* made; valuable context but no Tecciztecatl narrative. |
| 1540s–1570s | Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators collect, revise, translate, and illustrate Nahua knowledge. |
| c. 1558 | Conventional date attached to the *Leyenda de los Soles*. |
| c. 1558–1561 | Tepepolco materials underlying the *Primeros Memoriales*. |
| c. 1575–1577 | Completion of the surviving *Florentine Codex*; Book VII records the full Tecciztecatl–Nanahuatzin account and rabbit etiological myth. |
| 1571 | Molina publishes his Nahuatl dictionary, independently documenting *tecciztli* as a large conch. |
| Late sixteenth century | Durán and other chroniclers record central Mexican religion; no distinct institutional Tecciztecatl cult emerges from their descriptions. |
| Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries | Institutional cult by name is no longer documented; lunar and rabbit lore continue more broadly. |
| 1790 | Sun Stone excavated in Mexico City, stimulating antiquarian study of Mexica cosmology. |
| Late nineteenth–early twentieth centuries | Seler publishes major Borgia-group studies and helps establish modern iconographic identifications. |
| 1950s–1982 | Anderson and Dibble publish the English *Florentine Codex*, including Book VII. |
| 1971 | Nicholson’s influential typology systematizes central Mexican religious complexes. |
| 21 February 1978 | Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monolith initiates the modern Templo Mayor Project; important context, but not Tecciztecatl evidence. |
| 1992 | Bierhorst publishes a major English edition of the *Codex Chimalpopoca* texts. |
| 2 October 2006 | Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered near the Templo Mayor; again contextual rather than Tecciztecatl-specific. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavation of a section of Tenochtitlan’s Huei Tzompantli materially strengthens the evidence for Mexica human sacrifice but yields no demonstrated Tecciztecatl cult. |
| 2023 | Getty Research Institute launches the Digital Florentine Codex, allowing direct folio-level access to the Tecciztecatl narrative. |
| Present | Tecciztecatl survives in scholarship, education, art, popular mythology, games, and some contemporary spiritual practice; no standardized continuous cult is established. |
