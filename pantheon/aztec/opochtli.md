# Opochtli: Research Dossier

**Class:** male *teotl* (“god/divine power”) of the Nahua tradition; specifically a patron of the *atlaca*, “water people,” and a member, manifestation, or closely associated figure of the *Tlaloque*, the plural rain powers attached to Tlaloc.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the Nahuatl text and painted deity portrait in Bernardino de Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*, compiled from Nahua informants at Tepepolco/Tepeapulco approximately 1558–1561, especially fol. 263r–v. This is an early-colonial record of a pre-conquest cult, not a surviving pre-Hispanic inscription. No securely identified pre-conquest inscription, sculpture, temple, or painted manuscript image naming Opochtli is presently known. Sahagún’s later *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, chapter 17, completed in 1577, supplies the fullest textual account.

## Evidentiary key

- **Secure early-colonial attestation:** recorded in a sixteenth-century Nahuatl or Spanish source from Indigenous testimony.
- **Pre-conquest material context:** archaeological or pictorial evidence produced before 1521 but not necessarily naming Opochtli.
- **Later colonial elaboration:** a colonial author’s retelling, classification, or harmonization.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference by modern historians, philologists, or archaeologists.
- **Modern invention/unverified claim:** no adequate primary or scholarly source has been found.

Opochtli’s dossier is necessarily much shorter than those of Tlaloc, Huitzilopochtli, or Tezcatlipoca. His securely recoverable record is concentrated in a few pages of the Sahaguntine corpus. Expanding the general Tlaloc cult, the entire ritual calendar, or all lacustrine archaeology into “the cult of Opochtli” would create evidence that the sources do not provide.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Opochtli

The normalized Classical Nahuatl form is **Opochtli**; early manuscripts also spell it **Opuchtli**. The ordinary noun *opochtli* means “the left,” “the left side,” or “left-handed one.” Hence the safest translation of the divine name is **“The Left”** or **“The Left-Handed One.”** The name is written in the Latin alphabet in the Sahaguntine manuscripts; no unique logographic spelling belonging exclusively to the god is securely identified.

The word also appears independently in *Huītzilōpōchtli*, conventionally “Hummingbird on the Left/Left-Handed Hummingbird.” That shared component does not establish that Opochtli and Huitzilopochtli were the same being, relatives, or cult partners. No primary source documents such a relationship.

A sixteenth-century glyphic use in the *Matrícula de Huexotzinco*, fol. 545v, labels a man **Diego Opochtli** and depicts a hand—apparently a right hand although the gloss means “left.” This is evidence that *Opochtli* also functioned as a personal name after the conquest, not evidence that this individual was the god or his priest. The manuscript is dated approximately 1560.

### “Southern”

Because the left side can correspond to the south when one faces the rising sun, some writers translate Opochtli as “the southern one.” That directional interpretation is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not the explicit etymology given in the surviving Opochtli notice. It is possible but should not replace the lexical meaning “left.”

### “Wizard” or “magician”

Lewis Spence proposed “wizard” as an alternative sense and imagined that the fishing net’s ingenuity caused it to be attributed to a magician. This is an early twentieth-century comparativist conjecture, not a Nahua explanation. It has no independent primary attestation and should be classified as **obsolete or unsupported scholarly speculation**.

### Cult classifications and titles

The sources supply functional descriptions rather than a long set of formal epithets:

- **In atlaca inteuh**, “god of the water people”: *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, chapter 17.
- **One of the Tlaloque / representation of Tlaloc:** *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex*.
- **Patron of fishers, aquatic hunters, and people making their living on the water:** an inference directly grounded in the occupational group that worshipped him and the implements attributed to him.
- **Inventor or revealer of lacustrine technologies:** Sahaguntine testimony.

“God of the lake economy” is a useful modern description, not an ancient title. “God of fishermen” is accurate but incomplete: the evidence also encompasses waterfowl capture, canoe propulsion, and the broader occupational community called *atlaca*.

### Not Opochtli

Opochtli must not be silently merged with:

- **Atlahua**, an aquatic and hunting deity possessing a surviving sacred song.
- **Amimitl**, a god associated with Cuitlahuac and aquatic hunting.
- **Huitzilopochtli**, whose name contains *-opochtli*.
- **Tlaloc**, although the Nahuatl statement *Inin Opochtli ipan mixehuaya Tlaloc* connects them particularly closely.

Later authors have sometimes treated Amimitl and Opochtli as equivalents. That is a colonial or modern harmonization, not a securely attested pre-conquest equation.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The *Primeros Memoriales*, approximately 1558–1561

**Secure early-colonial attestation.** Sahagún collected this material at Tepepolco from Nahua elders working with Indigenous alphabetic scribes and painters. The surviving manuscripts belong to the *Códices Matritenses*, now divided between Madrid’s Real Biblioteca and Real Academia de la Historia.

The relevant folio contains a painted, labeled figure of Opochtli and a concise Nahuatl inventory of his costume. This is the earliest securely datable named image and text presently identifiable. It establishes:

- the divine name;
- a male anthropomorphic representation;
- affiliation with the Tlaloc/rain complex;
- black body or face paint;
- paper regalia;
- white-heron and quetzal feathers;
- a shield and rattle staff.

It does **not** establish when the cult began, an origin myth, a genealogy, a named temple, or a specific pre-conquest date for his festival.

### The *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, chapter 17, 1577

**Secure early-colonial attestation and fullest surviving notice.** The manuscript was completed at Tlatelolco in 1577 by Sahagún and a Nahua scholarly and artistic team. It contains parallel Nahuatl and Spanish columns and painted illustrations.

The chapter heading calls Opochtli a god formerly worshipped by “the people here.” The Nahuatl states:

> *Inin Opochtli ipan mixehuaya Tlaloc; atlaca inteuh catca, quimoteotiaya.*

A close rendering is: “In/through this Opochtli, Tlaloc was represented; he was the god of the water people, and they worshipped him.”

This wording is important. *Ipan mixehuaya* indicates representation, impersonation, manifestation, or enactment; it is stronger than mere companionship but does not require a modern doctrine in which Opochtli is simply an “avatar” of Tlaloc. Sahagún’s Spanish synopsis more generally says he was “one of the Tlaloque.” The two formulations may represent different attempts to classify a fluid divine complex.

The chapter credits Opochtli with revealing:

- *matlatl*, the net;
- *atlatl*, the spear- or dart-thrower;
- *minacachalli*, a three-pronged fishing weapon or harpoon;
- *ahuictli*, interpreted in this context as a paddle or implement for moving a canoe;
- *tzonuaztli*, a snare or noose, especially for birds.

The exact division of meanings among the last two terms varies in editions and dictionaries. The Spanish text unmistakably attributes to him the fishing net, a three-pointed fish spear also usable against birds, bird snares, and paddles. Therefore modern lists that turn every item into a separate “fishing pole,” “rudder,” or “atlatl-spear” should be checked against the Nahuatl rather than repeated as fixed terminology.

The worshippers offered water, food, *octli* or pulque, green maize stalks or cob-like wands, flowers, tobacco, copal, and aromatic *yiauhtli*. They sounded a *chicahuaztli* or rattle board/staff and earthen rattles, scattered toasted maize likened to hail, and sang to him. The singers are identified as the calpulli’s elderly men, elderly women, and leading members.

The source does not name the feast’s calendar date, identify a human victim, describe heart extraction, or specify a temple.

### The Sahaguntine costume text

The parallel costume description gives Opochtli:

- black soot paint;
- a facial patch or marking;
- a cut-paper crown;
- white-heron feather hair or crest, tipped with quetzal plumes;
- a paper shoulder band or crossed paper stole;
- a paper loincloth;
- white sandals;
- a shield carrying solar signs;
- a *chicahuaztli*, the rain-associated rattle staff.

Eduard Seler published and analyzed this material in 1909. His color reconstruction describes black skin, green paper crown, green shoulder band and staff, white heron feathers, green quetzal feathers, and a red shield with yellow border. Where these colors derive from the surviving painting they are manuscript evidence; where Seler regularized damaged paint they remain editorial reconstruction.

### Other named corpora

A dedicated review produces the following negative or qualified findings:

- **Codex Borgia and Borgia Group:** no figure securely named Opochtli. Rain gods, aquatic imagery, and *chicahuaztli* staffs occur, but assigning an anonymous figure to Opochtli requires a separate iconographic argument.
- **Codex Borbonicus:** no securely labeled Opochtli scene.
- **Codex Mendoza:** no securely identified divine portrait of Opochtli. Its economic and tributary evidence helps reconstruct the lacustrine world but is not an Opochtli cult document.
- **Codex Magliabechiano and Telleriano-Remensis:** no securely identified Opochtli narrative or festival.
- **Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*:** Durán preserves extensive Tlaloc, hunting, and calendrical material but no comparably full, independent Opochtli dossier has been verified.
- ***Leyenda de los Soles*, *Histoyre du Mechique*, *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*:** no securely identified myth centered on Opochtli.
- ***Cantares Mexicanos*:** no securely established Opochtli hymn.
- **Sahagún’s twenty sacred songs:** the aquatic hunter’s hymn belongs to Atlahua, not demonstrably to Opochtli. Readings that treat the word *yopuchi/opochtli* in the Atlahua song as proof that the two gods are identical remain disputed.

The silence of these corpora is historically meaningful: unlike the principal state gods, Opochtli does not possess a recoverable narrative cycle, calendrical chapter, or large colonial historiography.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**Secure early-colonial attestation:** Opochtli belongs to the economic and ecological world of the Basin of Mexico’s lakes. His worshippers were *atlaca*, people who worked on the water. Their livelihoods included fishing, taking waterfowl, and canoe work.

His divine competence is conveyed through technology rather than cosmic narrative. He “began,” “discovered,” “made manifest,” or “showed” the net, spear-thrower, fish harpoon, paddle, and snare. The language presents him as a culture-bringer for a specialized occupational community.

His attribution as an inventor need not mean that historical Nahua fishers believed the tools first appeared at a datable human moment. In the source, invention is a claim about sacred origin and occupational patronage.

### Lake economy

The term “lake economy” summarizes a real material setting: Tenochtitlan stood in a productive lacustrine system in which canoes, fish, waterfowl, amphibians, insects, reeds, salt, chinampa agriculture, and market exchange were fundamental. Opochtli’s implements map directly onto this economy.

This contextual fit does not prove that every fisher in every Basin community worshipped him. Sahagún records a community cult; he does not provide population totals, geographical limits, or universal adherence.

### Iconography

The only securely identified divine representation is Sahaguntine. Its diagnostic ensemble is:

- blackened body or face;
- pale facial mark;
- paper crown;
- heron and quetzal feathers;
- paper shoulder ornament and loincloth;
- white sandals;
- round, solar-marked shield;
- *chicahuaztli* rattle staff.

Several features—paper regalia, water-bird feathers, and the rattle staff—place him within the visual vocabulary of rain and fertility powers. The *chicahuaztli* was shaken to evoke or call rain. The solar markings introduce a second symbolic register, but no surviving text explains why Opochtli bears them.

The implements he invented do not all appear as handheld attributes in the surviving portrait. Modern images that make the trident or fishing net his permanent identifying weapon simplify the textual record.

### Animals, plants, colors, days, and numbers

- **Aquatic birds:** securely connected through his invention of bird-catching tools.
- **Fish:** securely connected through the net and *minacachalli*.
- **Heron:** present in his feather headdress.
- **Quetzal:** present in the headdress’s precious green plumes.
- **Maize:** offered as green stalks or toasted kernels.
- **Tobacco, copal, *yiauhtli*:** securely attested offerings.
- **Black, green, white, red, and yellow:** colors documented in the manuscript tradition or Seler’s reading of it.
- **Specific calendar day, numeral, star, or planet:** not securely attested.
- **South:** possible directional interpretation of the name, not an explicitly assigned cult quarter in the principal notice.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

No source supplies Opochtli with parents, birth narrative, siblings, consort, offspring, mount, or named enemy. Lists circulating online that give him romances, children, or a large family tree are **modern inventions unless separately documented**.

### Tlaloc

The strongest divine relation is to Tlaloc. The *Florentine Codex* states that Tlaloc was represented or manifested in Opochtli; Sahagún’s Spanish classification calls Opochtli one of the Tlaloque. Modern authors variously translate this as “companion,” “minister,” “aspect,” “emanation,” or “minor Tlaloc.” Of these, “member of the Tlaloc complex” is the least reductive.

### The Tlaloque

The Tlaloque are plural rain, mountain, storm, and fertility powers. Opochtli’s placement among them integrates fishing and lake subsistence into the rain-water complex. The text gives no complete membership list and does not narrate Opochtli meeting the other Tlaloque.

### Every documented god–god interaction

- **Tlaloc — manifestation/representation relation.** Opochtli is the form or person in whom Tlaloc was represented; *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, chapter 17, completed 1577. **Tier: secure early-colonial attestation of pre-conquest belief.**
- **The Tlaloque — collective membership.** Sahagún classifies Opochtli as one of the Tlaloque; Spanish *Historia general*, Book 1, chapter 17, late sixteenth century. **Tier: secure early-colonial classification.**
- **Atlahua — no documented interaction.** Both concern water and hunting, but no surviving narrative records a meeting, alliance, kinship, combat, or fusion. Later equation is **learned systematization**.
- **Amimitl — no documented interaction.** Later writers compare or identify the two aquatic-hunting gods, but no early narrative interaction is known. **Tier: later colonial/modern syncretistic equation.**
- **Huitzilopochtli — no documented interaction.** The shared word *opochtli* is linguistic, not a recorded divine encounter.
- **Chalchiuhtlicue — no documented interaction.** Common water-complex membership does not itself constitute an episode.
- **Nappatecuhtli — no documented interaction.** Both are classed among rain powers and occupational patrons; no shared scene is attested.
- **Huixtocihuatl — no documented interaction.** Aquatic affiliation alone supplies no checkable encounter.
- **Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, Mixcoatl, Xipe Totec, Xiuhtecuhtli, Coyolxauhqui, Tlaltecuhtli — no documented interaction.**

Thus Opochtli has a theological relation to Tlaloc and the collective Tlaloque, but no surviving interpersonal myth.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The revelation of lacustrine implements

This is Opochtli’s only securely recorded mythic action.

**Story:** Opochtli was the source, discoverer, or revealer of the net, spear-thrower, three-pronged fish harpoon, paddle/canoe implement, and bird snare. Fishers and other water workers consequently treated him as their god.

**Earliest source:** Sahaguntine material collected in the mid-sixteenth century and finalized in *Florentine Codex* Book 1, chapter 17.

**Function:** the account gives sacred origins to the principal technologies of aquatic subsistence and explains the reciprocal cult between specialist workers and their patron.

**Variants:** the Nahuatl emphasizes revealing or making known; Sahagún’s Spanish more simply says the people attributed the “invention” of the implements to him. Later summaries sometimes add pots, fishing rods, rudders, or an explicit story in which the gods create Opochtli after creating fish. Those additions are not present in the principal Nahuatl notice and should be labeled **modern retelling or expansion**.

No surviving source tells how Opochtli was born, how he joined the Tlaloque, how he invented the tools, or what opposition he faced. Turning the inventory into a dramatic creation narrative is modern literary invention.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Worshipping community

The cult belonged to the *atlaca*, people whose work and profit came from the water: fishers, waterfowlers, canoe workers, and perhaps associated households. The participation of aged men, aged women, and calpulli leaders shows a corporate community observance rather than a rite restricted to an individual craftsman.

The source does not identify a specialized priesthood by title. It also does not state whether *atlaca* was one named Tenochtitlan calpulli, a broad occupational category, or both in this passage.

### Offerings and performance

The securely attested rites comprise:

- water and prepared food;
- pulque;
- green maize stalks or cob-wands;
- flowers;
- tobacco;
- copal;
- aromatic *yiauhtli*;
- toasted maize scattered to resemble hail;
- sounding of the rain rattle and earthen rattles;
- songs by elders and community leaders.

The hail symbolism and *chicahuaztli* reinforce Opochtli’s connection with rain powers. Pulque, incense, food, music, and procession belong to broader Nahua ritual practice but are explicitly present here.

### Festival date

Sahagún says “when his feast was celebrated” but gives no veintena date. A modern publication assigns him sacrifice during **Tepeilhuitl**, the mountain festival, but the direct Sahaguntine Opochtli notice does not do so. Until a specific calendrical passage is produced, this must remain a **modern reconstruction from his Tlaloque status**, not a secure fact.

### Temple and cult image

No independently identified Opochtli temple is known. His portrait proves that a conventional cult image or deity impersonation could be represented, but it does not locate the image in a particular shrine.

The Templo Mayor’s northern shrine was dedicated to Tlaloc, and excavations have uncovered Tlaloc vessels, aquatic animals, shells, coral, fish, frogs, and rain-related offerings. This is essential context for the water cult of Tenochtitlan. None of these finds, however, bears a secure Opochtli label. Calling them “Opochtli offerings” would exceed the evidence.

### Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice in Mexica religion is established by Indigenous and Spanish texts, osteology, sacrificial deposits, skull racks, and cut-marked remains. The Huei Tzompantli remains discovered in 2015–2017 materially confirm the display of skulls in Tenochtitlan’s Sacred Precinct. Tlaloc’s cult included child sacrifice, and Offering 48 on the Tlaloc side of the Templo Mayor contained at least forty-two sacrificed children.

No surviving source specifically states that a human being was sacrificed to Opochtli, names an Opochtli victim, or assigns him a sacrificial ceremony. His recorded offerings are food, drink, plants, incense, music, and symbolic hail. General evidence for sacrifice to the Tlaloque must not be converted automatically into evidence for sacrifice to this individual god.

No source documents temple prostitution in Opochtli’s cult.

### Political and economic setting

Opochtli’s cult sacralized productive knowledge central to an island capital. Fishing, fowling, and canoe transport supplied food and movement through the lake system. Nevertheless, there is no evidence that Opochtli possessed an imperial state cult comparable to Huitzilopochtli’s or received tribute as a named political deity.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1521: recoverable only retrospectively

Opochtli was worshipped before the Spanish conquest, according to Nahua witnesses interviewed a generation afterward. His material culture fits the late Postclassic Basin of Mexico. There is currently no securely named pre-conquest Opochtli artifact from which to establish an earlier date.

The deity may preserve a much older occupational cult, but this is plausible inference rather than demonstrated chronology.

### 1521–1558: conquest and suppression

After the fall of Tenochtitlan, temples and public Indigenous ritual institutions were dismantled, converted, or placed under missionary attack. Sahagún explicitly frames the gods as beings worshipped “formerly.” The surviving Opochtli record was produced within an evangelizing investigation of Indigenous religion.

No document recovered here describes the destruction of Opochtli’s particular idol or shrine. General narratives of Templo Mayor destruction cannot fill that gap.

### 1558–1561: documentation at Tepepolco

Nahua elders and artists recorded Opochtli’s image and costume for Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*. This is the first securely datable attestation.

### 1560: personal-name evidence

The *Matrícula de Huexotzinco* records Diego Opochtli. The survival of the name into Christian colonial society does not demonstrate continuing worship but shows that the word/theonym remained socially usable.

### 1575–1577: final Florentine redaction

The expanded Book 1 account fixed Opochtli’s modern documentary identity: Tlaloc manifestation, god of water workers, inventor-revealer of aquatic tools, recipient of community offerings.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

Opochtli largely disappeared into antiquarian listings and broader treatments of “Mexican gods.” Unlike some local rain, cave, mountain, and agricultural beings, he has no securely documented continuous colonial folk cult under his own name.

Possible analogy with Saint Peter, patron of fishers and nets and keeper of the keys of heaven, has recently been proposed for communities in Morelos. This is a **modern ethnographic-historical interpretation**, not proof that Opochtli was deliberately converted into Saint Peter throughout colonial Mexico.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

Publication of Sahagún’s manuscripts and Eduard Seler’s iconographic studies made Opochtli accessible to comparative religion and museum scholarship. Seler’s 1909 presentation remains important because it reproduces the Nahuatl costume description and colored figure.

Popular mythographers then embellished the sparse evidence. Lewis Spence’s suggestion that the ingenious net implied a magician-god is representative of now-dated speculation.

### 1978 to the present: archaeological recontextualization

The 1978 discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone initiated the modern Templo Mayor Project. The Tlaltecuhtli monolith was found in 2006. Excavations have transformed knowledge of Mexica state religion, offerings, aquatic symbolism, sacrifice, and the Tlaloc complex.

Neither monument depicts Opochtli. Their value to this dossier is contextual and historiographical: they reveal the sacred precinct within which water and rain religion operated, but they do not enlarge Opochtli’s personal corpus.

Digital publication of the *Florentine Codex*, the *Códices Matritenses*, Nahuatl dictionaries, and glyph databases has recently made the original evidence directly inspectable.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

Opochtli has had a modest modern reception compared with major Mexica gods.

### Scholarship and museums

Modern works generally present him as a minor rain or water deity, patron of fishers, and inventor of fishing equipment. This outline is sound when derived from Sahagún. Calling him “minor” describes his documentary visibility, not necessarily his importance to the historical *atlaca*.

### Popular mythology

Common modern expansions include:

- a detailed creation story in which the gods create fish and then create Opochtli;
- an asserted fixed trident comparable to Poseidon’s;
- identification with Atlahua or Amimitl without qualification;
- claims that he threw every spear left-handed;
- consorts or male lovers, especially unsupported online claims involving Huehuecoyotl or Xochipilli;
- a fixed southern quarter and ownership of white maize;
- a distinct feast involving human sacrifice.

These should be labeled **modern invention, conflation, or unverified reconstruction** unless a primary citation is supplied.

### Occult and neopagan use

The dedicated search found no historically influential Renaissance, Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, or established grimoire tradition centered on Opochtli. He is absent from the principal European occult canons.

Contemporary Mexicayotl, reconstructionist, devotional, and internet communities may mention or honor him, but no reliable source presently establishes the date, continuity, organization, or scale of a distinct Opochtli cult. Any current worship is revival or reconstruction, not demonstrated institutional continuity from the sixteenth century.

### Literature, games, and commerce

Opochtli appears sporadically in online deity lists, fantasy discussions, art, and naming projects. No single novel, film, comic, or game was found that has substantially remade his public image. The scarcity itself is notable: modern representations normally recycle Sahagún’s few details or invent a generic Aztec fisherman with a trident.

### Political or extremist appropriation

No documented nationalist or extremist appropriation specifically centered on Opochtli was found. Broader appropriations of Mexica imagery should not be attributed to him without explicit evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Sahagún’s evidentiary position

The central problem is that virtually all specific knowledge derives from one documentary enterprise. The *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex* are not wholly independent witnesses: the latter emerged through revision, expansion, and translation of material gathered within Sahagún’s long research program.

Their great value lies in Nahuatl testimony and Indigenous-painted images. Their limitations include:

- production after conquest;
- missionary framing;
- editorial selection by Sahagún;
- possible differences among Tepepolco, Tlatelolco, and Tenochtitlan informants;
- translation shifts between the Nahuatl and Spanish columns;
- loss of context when costume lists are treated as complete theology.

### “One of the Tlaloque” versus “Tlaloc represented in him”

The Spanish and Nahuatl formulations have encouraged two models:

1. Opochtli as a subordinate or companion rain god.
2. Opochtli as a particular manifestation or impersonated aspect of Tlaloc.

Nahua divine identities were often overlapping and situational, so these models need not be mutually exclusive. Yet translating the relation as a permanent, systematic “avatar” doctrine is more precise than the evidence permits.

### Etymology

“The Left/Left-Handed One” is secure. “Southern one” is a directional interpretation. “Wizard” is unsupported conjecture. Claims that the name proves he physically cast weapons with his left hand are not made in the surviving Opochtli chapter.

### Tool terminology

The list of implements has accumulated mistranslations. The central set—net, spear-thrower, three-pronged harpoon, paddle/canoe implement, and bird snare—is secure. Whether individual Nahuatl terms should be rendered “oar,” “pole,” “rudder,” “snare,” or “fishing rod” requires philological attention. The etymology of *minacachalli* is itself marked doubtful in modern critical commentary.

### Identity with Atlahua or Amimitl

All three gods concern water, hunting, or fishing. Similar function is not identity. Atlahua has an individual hymn; Amimitl has local associations with Cuitlahuac. Opochtli has the distinctive Tlaloc manifestation statement and technology inventory. Treating them as three names for one god is a learned harmonization unless a specific source makes the equation.

### Archaeological identification

No excavated image bears an Opochtli name glyph in a secure pre-conquest context. Archaeologists may identify Tlaloc-complex figures through diagnostic goggles, fangs, blue paint, paper regalia, and rain implements, but attributing one specifically to Opochtli remains difficult without the Sahaguntine costume combination or a name.

### Nicholson’s typology

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 classification of central Mexican deities into conceptual complexes helps place Opochtli within the Tlaloc fertility-rain assemblage. Such typology is an analytic tool, not an Indigenous canonical pantheon chart. It can illuminate shared attributes but can also conceal local occupational identities if treated too rigidly.

### León-Portilla and philosophical interpretation

Miguel León-Portilla’s work established the intellectual seriousness and internal complexity of Nahua thought and helped recover Nahuatl sources from older dismissive readings. It does not supply an otherwise lost Opochtli theology. General philosophical claims about *teotl* should not be substituted for the specific evidence.

### Graulich, Carrasco, Townsend, Boone, Olivier, Miller and Taube

These scholars clarify sacrifice, ritual calendars, imperial symbolism, pictorial manuscripts, and divine transformation. Their broader frameworks illuminate Opochtli’s world, but the god remains peripheral in those corpora. Olivier’s analysis of Tezcatlipoca, for example, warns against treating divine identity as fixed, yet it does not document an Opochtli–Tezcatlipoca encounter.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Opochtli was a form of Huitzilopochtli because both names contain *opochtli*.
- Opochtli was the Aztec Poseidon.
- His harpoon proves transatlantic influence from Greek trident iconography.
- He had named romantic relationships with Huehuecoyotl or Xochipilli.
- The Coyolxauhqui stone, Tlaltecuhtli monolith, or Sun Stone depicts him.
- The 2015–2017 skull-tower discoveries prove human sacrifice specifically to him.
- A modern Saint Peter festival demonstrates unbroken Opochtli worship.

None is supported by the available primary evidence.

| 1909 | Eduard Seler publishes the Sahaguntine costume passage and colored iconographic discussion in the *Journal de la Société des Américanistes*. |

| 1971 | Nicholson’s pantheon typology provides a modern framework for classifying central Mexican divine complexes. |
| 1978 | Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone begins the large-scale Templo Mayor Project. The find is not an Opochtli image but transforms archaeological understanding of his religious environment. |
| 1997 | Thelma Sullivan and collaborators publish a major paleography and English translation of the *Primeros Memoriales*. |
| 2006 | Discovery of the Tlaltecuhtli monolith expands knowledge of the Sacred Precinct; it has no direct Opochtli identification. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavation identifies part of Tenochtitlan’s Huei Tzompantli. This confirms large-scale sacrificial display but is not evidence for sacrifice to Opochtli specifically. |
| 2022–present | New mirror editions and digital projects place the Nahuatl, Spanish, images, and manuscript folios online, permitting renewed scrutiny of the small Opochtli corpus. |
| Present | Opochtli persists in scholarship, educational media, internet mythology, and limited reconstructionist interest; no demonstrable unbroken institutional cult is known. |

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary and critical-source core

- Bernardino de Sahagún and Nahua collaborators, *Primeros Memoriales*, c. 1558–1561, especially fol. 263r–v. The standard modern edition is Thelma D. Sullivan et al., *Primeros Memoriales* (University of Oklahoma Press, 1997).
- Bernardino de Sahagún and Nahua collaborators, *Florentine Codex*, Book 1, chapter 17, fols. 15v–16r, completed 1577.
- Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble, eds. and trans., *Florentine Codex: General History of the Things of New Spain, Book 1—The Gods* (1950).
- Josefina García Quintana et al., modern mirror edition of *Códice florentino, Libro primero*, supplying manuscript paleography and Spanish translation.
- Eduard Seler, “Costumes et attributs des divinités du Mexique, selon le P. Sahagun,” 1909.
- *Matrícula de Huexotzinco*, fol. 545v, c. 1560, for Opochtli as a personal name.

### Comparative and contextual scholarship

Nicholson, León-Portilla, Boone, Carrasco, Graulich, Townsend, Olivier, Miller and Taube, Matos Moctezuma, and López Luján remain indispensable for Mexica divine classification, ritual, pictorial manuscripts, sacrifice, and Sacred Precinct archaeology. For Opochtli himself, however, they do not supersede the direct Sahaguntine witnesses.

The central conclusion is correspondingly restrained: Opochtli is securely recoverable as a male Tlaloc-linked patron of the Basin’s water workers and sacred revealer of fishing, fowling, and canoe technologies. His image, offerings, and constituency are documented; his birth, family, adventures, temple, calendar date, individual priesthood, human victims, and continuous modern cult are not.
