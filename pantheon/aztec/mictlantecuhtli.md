# Mictlantecuhtli: Research Dossier

**Class:** *teōtl*—a male divine power or god in Mexica and related Central Mexican Nahua traditions; ruler and personification of Mictlan, the region of the ordinary dead. “Demon” is a colonial Christian classification, not the indigenous one.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest evidence securely identifiable specifically as Mictlantecuhtli belongs to Late Postclassic Central Mexico, approximately the fifteenth to early sixteenth century: skeletal death gods in the preconquest Borgia-group manuscripts and the two monumental ceramic figures deposited in the House of Eagles at Tenochtitlan, principally during its late-fifteenth-century construction. The name itself is first securely preserved in alphabetic Nahuatl and Spanish manuscripts compiled after the conquest, beginning with Sahagún’s research of the 1540s–1580s. Earlier skeletal death deities—from Classic-period Veracruz, Teotihuacan, and elsewhere—demonstrate a much older Mesoamerican death-god tradition, but identifying any one of them by the later Nahuatl name *Mictlāntēuctli* is a modern scholarly comparison, not an ancient written identification.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Preconquest attestation]** Material physically produced before the Spanish conquest or securely recovered from an undisturbed preconquest context.
- **[Early colonial testimony]** Sixteenth-century indigenous or missionary record preserving Nahua testimony, but produced after conquest and mediated by colonial conditions.
- **[Regional variant]** Evidence belonging to a non-Mexica or locally distinctive tradition.
- **[Learned systematization]** A colonial or modern ordering of material more schematic than any single indigenous source.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically datable equation made across religious systems.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion inferred by comparison rather than expressly stated in a primary source.
- **[Modern invention or retelling]** A recent elaboration presented in literature, tourism, entertainment, occultism, or popular mythology.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[Preconquest attestation / early colonial testimony]** Mictlantecuhtli is the ruler of *Mictlan*, the destination of people whose manner of death did not assign them to such other postmortem places as Tlalocan, the solar entourage, or the place of dead infants. He is neither a judge who assigns souls according to moral deserts nor an Aztec equivalent of the Christian Devil. Sahagún’s Nahua informants place him in Mictlan with his wife, Mictecacihuatl. The *Leyenda de los Soles* makes him custodian of the bones of earlier human generations and the antagonist whom Quetzalcoatl must outwit to obtain them.

Death in the surviving Nahua evidence is primarily classified by manner rather than moral character. Mictlan is thus not simply “hell,” despite missionaries frequently translating it as *infierno*. Mictlantecuhtli’s functions encompass possession of the dead, dissolution, bones, darkness, nocturnal calendrics, and—through the reuse of ancestral bones—the material from which renewed life can be generated.

### Name

**Mictlāntēuctli / Mictlāntēcuhtli**  
Normalized Classical Nahuatl: *Mictlāntēuctli*, conventionally *Mictlantecuhtli*. Colonial spellings include *Mictlantecutli*, *Mictlanteuctli*, *Mictlantecuhtli*, and related orthographic variants.

The transparent compound is:

- *mic-* / *miqui*: “to die,” “dead”;
- *-tlān*: a locative suffix, here in *Mictlān*, “place among/of the dead”;
- *tēuctli* or *tēcuhtli*: “lord,” a title of high status.

The ordinary translation is **“Lord of Mictlan”** or **“Lord of the Place of the Dead.”** “Lord of Death” is serviceable shorthand but less exact: the name makes him ruler of a place or condition, not necessarily the abstract causal agent of every death.

**[Early colonial testimony]** Sahagún’s Book 3 appendix calls him *Mictlāntēuctli* and explicitly says that he resides in Mictlan with *Mictēcacihuātl*, his wife. The Nahuatl Dictionary based on colonial texts also records the paired invocation *in tonān, in totah, in Mictlān Tēuctli*—“our mother, our father, the Lord of Mictlan.” This does not turn the male god into a female deity; it is an instance of Nahua paired or difrasistic language embracing complementary generative authority.

### Related titles and designations

- **Tzontemoc**, “He Who Descends Headfirst” or “Head-Descending.” **[Early colonial testimony]** Some sources use it for a lord of the ninth underworld and identify or closely associate it with Mictlantecuhtli. Whether it is always a strict alternate name is uncertain.
- **Chiconauhmictlan / Chicnāuhmictlān**, “Nine-Mictlan” or “Ninth Place of the Dead.” This is primarily a place-name, though its lord can consequently be called lord of the ninth region.
- **Yohualtecuhtli**, “Lord of Night.” **[Contested]** It may function as a broad title for nocturnal powers and is not consistently an exclusive name for Mictlantecuhtli.
- Spanish colonial glosses include *señor del infierno*, *señor de los muertos*, and “demonio.” **[Colonial Christian reinterpretation]** These identify Mictlan with Christian hell and its ruler with a demon; they document evangelization, not preconquest Nahua classification.
- “God of death,” “Aztec death god,” and “Lord of the Underworld” are modern analytic labels. They are useful provided they do not import Greco-Roman or Christian theology.

### Mictecacihuatl and the paired sovereignty

*Mictecacihuatl*—more exactly *Mictēcacihuātl*, “Woman/Lady of the People of Mictlan”—is consistently presented in Sahagún’s mortuary account as Mictlantecuhtli’s wife. The frequent modern title “Lady of the Dead” is a translation rather than a separately attested fixed epithet. Assertions that she alone “kept the bones” or presided over a direct precursor of the modern Day of the Dead are poorly anchored in the principal sixteenth-century sources.

### Comparanda and identifications

**[Regional comparison]** Skeletal death deities appear widely in Mesoamerica. The monumental ceramic death figure from El Zapotal, Veracruz, conventionally called Mictlantecuhtli, dates much earlier than the Mexica state. Its Nahuatl identification is retrospective; it belongs to a Gulf Coast tradition whose indigenous name is unknown.

**[Modern scholarly comparison]** Mixtec codices contain skeletal death beings, sometimes identified in scholarship as Lord 1 Death or as analogues of Mictlantecuhtli. Shared Mixteca-Puebla iconography does not prove identical names or cults.

**[Colonial Christian syncretism]** Missionaries translated Mictlan as hell and represented its ruler as a devil. Nahua-Christian texts consequently sometimes use *Mictlan* for the Christian infernal realm. This semantic reuse must not be projected backward.

**[Modern contested identification]** Santa Muerte is frequently described in journalism and contemporary occult literature as Mictlantecuhtli or Mictecacihuatl continued under Catholic dress. The historical evidence does not establish an unbroken cultic genealogy. Santa Muerte’s documented modern formation is deeply entwined with Catholic personifications of holy death, European skeletal imagery, folk saints, healing and love magic, and twentieth-century popular devotion. Indigenous death imagery may contribute to later reinterpretations, but direct descent is an argument, not an established fact.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest material evidence

#### Borgia-group manuscripts

**[Preconquest attestation]** The *Codex Borgia*, a ritual-divinatory screenfold probably made in the Puebla–Tlaxcala–Mixteca cultural sphere during the Late Postclassic, contains repeated skeletal death deities conventionally identified as Mictlantecuhtli. Its exact date and place of manufacture remain disputed; “late fifteenth or early sixteenth century” is safer than an exact year.

Important appearances include:

- Plate 14: a sequence associated with the Lords of the Night, including the skeletal death lord.
- Plate 50 and neighboring ritual pages: death imagery within temple and sacrificial complexes.
- Plate 52: the southern or underworld quarter represented through a skeletal temple and nocturnal birds.
- Plate 56: Mictlantecuhtli paired with Quetzalcoatl in a calendrical series frequently interpreted as death and life structuring the 260-day count.
- Plate 73: another paired appearance of Mictlantecuhtli and Quetzalcoatl as calendrical patrons.

These pages are not narrative captions naming the figures. Identification rests on the deity’s diagnostic skull face, exposed jaw, skeletal or partly defleshed body, dark or blood-spotted coloring, bone ornaments, and parallels in annotated colonial codices.

Related death gods occur in the *Codex Fejérváry-Mayer*, *Codex Laud*, and other Borgia-group manuscripts. Because those books are regional ritual manuals rather than “Aztec Bibles,” their figures should not automatically be flattened into the Tenochtitlan form.

#### House of Eagles sculptures

**[Preconquest archaeological attestation]** Two nearly life-size hollow ceramic sculptures were excavated by the Templo Mayor Project in the House of Eagles, immediately north of the Templo Mayor. The building was constructed and enlarged between approximately 1430 and 1502; the relevant architectural phase is generally associated with late-fifteenth-century imperial Tenochtitlan, often around Phase V, approximately 1480.

The figures have:

- skull-like, partly defleshed faces;
- exposed ribs;
- a fleshy pendant organ generally identified as the liver;
- clawed hands raised aggressively;
- perforations that probably held human hair;
- traces of paint and stucco.

They stood near an entrance leading symbolically northward, the direction associated with Mictlan. Their identification as Mictlantecuhtli is strong but still archaeological and iconographic: no excavated name-label accompanies them.

#### Other Late Postclassic objects

A carved and painted wooden skull mask in the Walters Art Museum is dated approximately 1450–1521 and identified as Mictlantecuhtli. It lacks eye holes and was probably mounted on a support rather than worn. Its provenance is less archaeologically controlled than that of the House of Eagles figures.

Small ceramic “death whistles” and effigy instruments from Late Postclassic contexts sometimes bear skeletal faces, owl features, or fire-serpent imagery. Modern oversized “Aztec death whistles” that emit theatrical screams are often replicas or inventions; authentic instruments vary, and their precise ritual use is not recorded.

### Alphabetic and annotated corpus

#### *Primeros Memoriales*

**[Early colonial testimony]** Sahagún’s earlier research at Tepepulco, conventionally dated to the late 1550s, survives as the *Primeros Memoriales*. It contains deity lists, ritual descriptions, vestments, festivals, and indigenous illustrations that preceded the expanded *Florentine Codex*. The compact divine catalog demonstrates the layered process by which indigenous testimony was reorganized within a missionary encyclopedia.

#### *Florentine Codex*

Compiled through several campaigns of inquiry from the 1540s to 1570s, with the surviving manuscript completed around 1577 and now in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence. Its Nahuatl text was generated through collaboration between Sahagún and learned Nahua assistants and informants; the parallel Spanish is not always a literal translation.

Relevant portions include:

- **Book 1, “The Gods”:** divine classifications and descriptions.
- **Book 3, appendix, chapters 1–3:** destinations of the dead, funerary treatment, the journey to Mictlan, the yellow dog, repeated commemorative rites, and Mictlantecuhtli with Mictecacihuatl.
- **Book 4:** calendrical divination and day-sign patrons.
- **Book 6:** ritual speech and paired invocations involving Mictlan and its lord.
- **Books 2 and 12:** festival and conquest material supplying the ritual and colonial context.

Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble’s twelve-volume English translation remains foundational, though modern work stresses the independence of the Nahuatl text and the distortions introduced by treating the Spanish column as its definitive meaning.

#### *Leyenda de los Soles*

**[Early colonial testimony]** A Nahuatl prose manuscript dated internally to 1558 and preserved within the manuscript commonly called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. It records cosmogonic eras and the recovery of ancestral bones. Here Quetzalcoatl enters Mictlan, bargains with Mictlantecuhtli, uses worms and bees to sound an unperforated conch, takes the bones, falls into a pit prepared on Mictlantecuhtli’s orders, and carries the broken bones to Tamoanchan. Quilaztli/Cihuacoatl grinds them, and the gods bleed their penises over the bone meal to generate humanity.

The text does not say that Quetzalcoatl traverses the now-popular standardized list of nine obstacles before reaching Mictlantecuhtli. Combining that list with this myth is a modern harmonization.

#### *Histoyre du Mechique*

**[Early colonial testimony]** A French-language colonial compilation, generally dated to the mid-sixteenth century, derived from lost Spanish material and indigenous accounts. It offers cosmogonic variants, including the creation or establishment of underworld rulers. Because it is multiply mediated—indigenous testimony, Spanish redaction, French survival—its differences from the *Leyenda* should be preserved rather than silently corrected.

#### *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*

**[Early colonial testimony]** Nahuatl annalistic and mythohistorical material compiled in the sixteenth century and transmitted in the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. It is particularly important for Quetzalcoatl traditions and the history of Tollan. It supplies comparative context but is not the fullest source for Quetzalcoatl’s confrontation with Mictlantecuhtli.

#### *Codex Vaticanus A* / *Codex Ríos*

**[Early colonial pictorial testimony]** Produced after conquest, probably in the later sixteenth century, with pictorial cosmography and Italian annotations. Its underworld diagrams are the chief visual basis for the familiar vertically stacked levels of Mictlan. The manuscript displays European influence in arrangement and commentary; its diagram cannot automatically be treated as an untouched preconquest cosmological chart.

#### *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*

**[Early colonial pictorial testimony]** A sixteenth-century annotated manuscript now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Folio 15r depicts and names a death lord with dark facial treatment. It helps connect preconquest skeletal iconography to the alphabetically named Mictlantecuhtli.

#### *Codex Magliabechiano* and its group

**[Early colonial pictorial testimony]** The *Magliabechiano*, made in the mid-sixteenth century, belongs to a family that includes the *Codex Tudela* and related manuscripts. Its images include:

- a colossal skeletal idol receiving vessels or streams of blood;
- ritual human sacrifice and anthropophagy accompanied by a Spanish Christianizing gloss;
- funerary customs and festival material.

One notorious gloss calls Mictlantecuhtli a demon and states that cooked human flesh from sacrificial victims was distributed before his image. This is valuable but mediated evidence. The image-copying history of the group is important: some scholars argue that an especially frightening Mictlantecuhtli image was substituted for Huitzilopochtli in a copying sequence, magnifying European demonization.

#### *Codex Borbonicus*

**[Preconquest/early colonial disputed dating]** This long pictorial tonalamatl contains calendrical patrons and ritual cycles. Whether it was painted just before or shortly after conquest remains debated. It is valuable for comparison of skeletal divine imagery, the Lords of the Night, festivals, and day-count structure, but it supplies less continuous prose about Mictlantecuhtli.

#### *Codex Mendoza*

**[Early colonial testimony]** Made about 1541–1542 for Spanish authorities. It is chiefly tributary, historical, and ethnographic rather than a theological account of Mictlantecuhtli. Its funerary and life-cycle images help reconstruct the broader mortuary setting but should not be cited as though it contained the complete Mictlan narrative.

#### Durán

Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme*, including the *Book of the Gods and Rites* and the ancient calendar, was composed principally in the 1570s–1580s. Durán supplies extensive descriptions of cult, festivals, temples, sacrifice, and political theology. He is close to indigenous traditions but writes as a Dominican intent on exposing and eradicating “idolatry.” His statements about a death god must be assessed against the Nahuatl and pictorial evidence rather than used alone.

#### *Cantares Mexicanos*

This late-sixteenth-century Nahuatl song collection invokes death, the place of the fleshless, transience, flowers, warfare, and divine destinations. Its poetry illuminates Nahua understandings of mortality but is not a systematic Mictlantecuhtli mythology. Not every reference to Mictlan names or personifies its lord.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[Early colonial testimony]** Mictlantecuhtli rules Mictlan with Mictecacihuatl. Sahagún distinguishes three principal destinations:

1. Mictlan for many who died through ordinary causes;
2. Tlalocan for deaths associated with water, lightning, and certain illnesses;
3. the House of the Sun for warriors killed in battle and sacrificial victims, with women who died in childbirth forming a related female solar company.

Infants who died before meaningful earthly life could be assigned to another nurturing place. Therefore the statement that Mictlantecuhtli ruled “all souls” is not supported.

His authority includes:

- receiving the ordinary dead;
- possession of ancestral bones;
- darkness, cold, decomposition, and disappearance;
- calendrical and nocturnal patronage;
- the dangerous boundary where living divine beings enter the region of the dead;
- participation, paradoxically, in regeneration because old bones become the substrate of new humanity.

### Appearance

Diagnostic traits vary by manuscript and object:

- skull or partly defleshed face;
- bared teeth and nasal cavity;
- exposed ribs, vertebrae, and joints;
- black, red, or yellow spotting interpreted as blood, decay, or painted body pattern;
- claw-like hands and feet;
- bone ear-spools or other skeletal ornaments;
- paper regalia associated with sacrifice and death;
- disordered or inserted human hair;
- liver or other viscera hanging from the thorax in the House of Eagles statues;
- arms raised as if seizing or receiving.

The common modern image of a completely clean, white skeleton is reductive. Preconquest and early colonial images often mingle living flesh, exposed bone, blood, hair, liver, claws, and regalia.

### Owl and other animals

**[Preconquest attestation]** Owls belong to the nocturnal, ominous, and underworld iconographic complex. In the *Codex Borgia* an owl can bear skull-like features, and owl beings occur in underworld or directional scenes. Mictlantecuhtli is associated with the owl as a day or night patron in later calendrical explanations.

The owl is not simply his personal “pet.” It is a broader sign of night, death, sorcery, and ill omen. Sahagún records owl calls as portents of death, producing the well-known Nahuatl expression *cuācuācuācuā, tēcān mictlān*—loosely, the owl’s call announces death. Modern claims that one unique owl species was exclusively sacred to Mictlantecuhtli exceed the evidence.

Other connected animals include:

- **dog:** psychopomp and river-crossing helper of the dead, but not ordinarily Mictlantecuhtli’s mount;
- **bat:** nocturnal and underworld associations, although modern media exaggerate a direct personal equation;
- **spider, scorpion, centipede, and other earth-creatures:** part of a wider telluric-death complex, not exclusive attributes;
- **bee and worm:** helpers of Quetzalcoatl in sounding Mictlantecuhtli’s conch in the *Leyenda de los Soles*.

### Calendrical position

**[Preconquest attestation / learned reconstruction]** In tonalamatl imagery Mictlantecuhtli participates in the nine Lords of the Night and is associated with the day sign *Itzcuintli*, “Dog.” Sources and modern tables differ over whether he is counted as a particular numbered night lord or as patron of portions of adjacent days. The safer finding is that he belongs firmly to the nocturnal and divinatory sequence; exact standardized tables depend on manuscript and reconstruction.

Plate 56 of the *Codex Borgia* divides a calendrical sequence between Mictlantecuhtli and Quetzalcoatl. It is often described as a life/death opposition, but “life god versus death god” is an interpretive gloss on a more complex calendrical image.

### The north

Mictlan is frequently associated with the north, cold, dryness or darkness, and the House of Eagles figures guarded a northern passage. Yet Mictlan can also function less as a compass point than as a distant ontological region. It should not be forced into a single modern map.

### The nine levels: source and correction

**[Early colonial testimony]** Sahagún describes a four-year mortuary process and dangers that include clashing mountains, a place of obsidian wind, arrows, beasts, and a broad water crossed with the aid of a yellow dog. At the end, the dead reach *Chicnāuhmictlān*, “Nine-Mictlan,” and disappear or are completed there.

**[Early colonial pictorial testimony]** *Codex Vaticanus A* represents multiple underworld strata or regions.

**[Learned systematization]** The familiar numbered itinerary—

1. Itzcuintlan;
2. Tepectli Monamictlan;
3. Iztepetl;
4. Itzehecayan;
5. Paniecatacoyan;
6. Timiminaloyan;
7. Teocoyohuehualoyan;
8. Izmictlan Apochcalolca;
9. Chicunamictlan—

is a modern synthesis of nonidentical texts and diagrams. Sahagún does not give this exact nine-item list in this exact numbered form. Recent scholarship also disputes the assumption that every “level” was vertically stacked: some evidence describes a horizontal journey through regions, while the numbers eight and nine may classify transit versus destruction/transformation rather than architectural floors.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to divine beings

### Origin and family

No surviving preconquest inscription or early Nahuatl text provides a universally accepted parentage for Mictlantecuhtli.

**[Early colonial variant]** The *Histoyre du Mechique* embeds the establishment of Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl in a broader act of divine creation. Later retellings sometimes specify that primordial creator gods made the pair to rule the underworld. Details differ by redaction.

**[Secure relationship]** Mictecacihuatl is his wife in the *Florentine Codex*, Book 3 appendix. No securely attested children are consistently assigned to the couple.

Assertions that Mictlantecuhtli is the son of Omecihuatl and Ometecuhtli, that he is the father of a fixed roster of underworld demons, or that he has a canonical sibling rivalry with a solar god are mostly modern pantheon-building unless a particular colonial source is supplied.

### Documented god–god interactions

Each line below records an actual joint episode or image rather than mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Mictecacihuatl — co-ruler and spouse:** She resides with Mictlantecuhtli in Mictlan as his wife. *Florentine Codex*, Book 3 appendix, compiled in the later sixteenth century from Nahua testimony. **[Early colonial testimony; secure relationship]**

- **Quetzalcoatl — petitioner, trickster, and thief of ancestral bones:** Quetzalcoatl asks for the bones of former humans; Mictlantecuhtli sets the conch test, appears to consent, then orders a pit dug and has quail startle the fleeing god. *Leyenda de los Soles*, Nahuatl manuscript dated 1558. **[Early colonial testimony preserving a preconquest myth]**

- **Quetzalcoatl — paired calendrical power:** The two appear opposite one another in calendrical arrangements, conventionally interpreted as complementary death and life. *Codex Borgia*, plates 56 and 73, Late Postclassic. **[Preconquest attestation; interpretation disputed]**

- **Mictecacihuatl and Quetzalcoatl — joint presence in the bone episode:** Quetzalcoatl approaches the rulers of the dead in later summaries, but the surviving *Leyenda* gives most direct speech and action to Mictlantecuhtli. Treat claims that Mictecacihuatl independently bargains over the bones as **[later elaboration]** unless tied to another version.

- **Quilaztli/Cihuacoatl — sequential, not face-to-face interaction:** After Quetzalcoatl escapes Mictlan, Quilaztli grinds the recovered bones at Tamoanchan. She and Mictlantecuhtli do not converse in the surviving passage; their relationship is mediated through the bones. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558 manuscript. **[Early colonial testimony; indirect interaction]**

- **The assembled gods — opposing claims over human substance:** Mictlantecuhtli holds the ancestral bones; the gods later perform autosacrifice over those bones to generate humans. They do not confront him directly in the extant passage. *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[Early colonial testimony; indirect opposition]**

- **Worms and bees — Quetzalcoatl’s divine or animate helpers against Mictlantecuhtli’s test:** Worms perforate the conch; bees enter it and make it sound. *Leyenda de los Soles*. Their exact ontological status is not equivalent to major named gods. **[Early colonial testimony]**

- **Quail — agents of Mictlantecuhtli:** Quail startle Quetzalcoatl so that he falls into the pit and the bones are scattered and gnawed. *Leyenda de los Soles*. They are animals, not independently named deities. **[Early colonial testimony]**

- **Tzontemoc — possible alternate identity or fellow underworld lord:** Colonial passages locate Tzontemoc in the ninth underworld and sometimes conjoin the name with Mictlantecuhtli. The evidence does not securely show a separate meeting between two distinct gods. **[Early colonial testimony; identity disputed]**

- **Nine Lords of the Night — serial association:** Mictlantecuhtli appears in the same calendrical sequence as other night patrons in tonalamatl manuscripts. Co-membership is not a narrative interaction. **[Preconquest calendrical attestation]**

- **Tezcatlipoca:** No secure myth in the principal named corpus records a personal meeting, battle, or alliance between Tezcatlipoca and Mictlantecuhtli. Shared nocturnal or transformative qualities are comparative scholarship, not an episode. **[Absence of evidence]**

- **Xolotl:** Although Xolotl is a dog-associated psychopomp and accompanies Quetzalcoatl in other myths, the *Leyenda de los Soles* bone-recovery passage names Quetzalcoatl alone. Modern illustrations commonly add Xolotl to the journey. **[Modern harmonization unless another source is cited]**

- **Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, Coatlicue, Coyolxauhqui, Tonatiuh, Xipe Totec, and Tlazolteotl:** No specific face-to-face interaction with Mictlantecuhtli is securely recorded in the principal sources reviewed here. Calendrical juxtaposition or common reception at the Templo Mayor does not constitute an interaction. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The recovery of the bones

**Earliest surviving narrative:** *Leyenda de los Soles*, manuscript dated 1558, probably transmitting older Nahua mythology.

After the destruction of earlier worlds, the gods consider who will inhabit the renewed earth. Quetzalcoatl goes to Mictlan and asks Mictlantecuhtli what he guards. Mictlantecuhtli demands that Quetzalcoatl circle his realm while sounding a conch. The conch has no openings. Quetzalcoatl calls worms, which bore holes in it, and bees, which enter and make it resound.

Mictlantecuhtli then tells Quetzalcoatl he may take the bones but immediately instructs his messengers to announce that they must not be surrendered. Quetzalcoatl’s *nahual* advises him to pretend to leave the bones. Instead he gathers separate male and female bones and flees. Mictlantecuhtli commands his servants to dig a pit. Quail startle Quetzalcoatl; he falls, dies or loses consciousness, and scatters the bones. The quail peck them. Quetzalcoatl revives, laments the damage, regathers them, and brings them to Tamoanchan.

Quilaztli, identified with Cihuacoatl in many readings, grinds the bones and puts the meal into a precious vessel. Quetzalcoatl bleeds his penis over it; other named gods perform the same penitential autosacrifice. Humanity emerges.

The myth explains several conditions:

- living humans are made from dead predecessors;
- humans differ in size because the bones were broken and gnawed;
- divine blood creates an obligation between humans and gods;
- death is not the opposite of creation but its material prerequisite;
- Mictlantecuhtli is not defeated permanently; his realm’s substance is borrowed or stolen.

**Variants and accretions**

- The *Histoyre du Mechique* presents a related cosmogonic complex with different framing. **[Early colonial variant]**
- Versions adding Xolotl as Quetzalcoatl’s companion merge the bone episode with Xolotl’s broader psychopomp and twin functions. **[Later synthesis]**
- Versions in which Quetzalcoatl fights Mictlantecuhtli physically, kills him, or completes nine explicitly numbered video-game-like trials are **[modern retellings]**.
- Claims that Mictlantecuhtli voluntarily gives one giant’s bone and then simply changes his mind simplify a more elaborate deception sequence.
- The tale is not securely depicted as one continuous narrative in a surviving preconquest manuscript, though its imagery and concepts are preconquest in character.

### The journey of the ordinary dead

**Primary account:** *Florentine Codex*, Book 3 appendix.

At death, relatives address the deceased and prepare the body. Goods, paper, clothing, water, and a dog may accompany the dead. The route contains lethal or disintegrative obstacles: clashing mountains, an obsidian-bladed region, violent winds, projectiles, beasts, and a broad water. A yellow dog recognizes its former master and carries the dead person across. White and black dogs refuse in the explanatory tale; the prescribed color conveys ritual specificity.

Commemorative rites recur after fixed intervals through four years. At the completion of the journey the dead arrive at Nine-Mictlan, where Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl dwell, and are said to disappear or be completed.

This is not a moral trial administered by Mictlantecuhtli. The obstacles transform and strip the dead. Nor is the endpoint described in Sahagún as eternal bliss in the Christian sense.

### Calendrical opposition of Quetzalcoatl and Mictlantecuhtli

**Primary image:** *Codex Borgia*, plates 56 and 73.

The paired figures organize portions of the sacred calendar. Modern scholarship commonly reads them as life/death, wind/stasis, creation/dissolution, or complementary halves. Because the manuscript lacks alphabetic commentary, none of those glosses should be mistaken for a preserved title of a myth. It is a ritual-calendrical structure rather than demonstrably a scene from the bone-recovery story.

### Death, maize, and regeneration

Nahua thought repeatedly places bones, seeds, ancestors, and subterranean storage into analogous relations. Mictlan holds the bones from which humans return; the earth holds seeds from which maize returns. Scholars including Alfredo López Austin and Miguel León-Portilla developed influential reconstructions of these complementary transformations. The comparison is well grounded, but a single ancient text saying “Mictlantecuhtli is a maize god” does not exist.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Tenochtitlan and the House of Eagles

The House of Eagles stood within Tenochtitlan’s sacred precinct, north of the Templo Mayor. Excavations from 1980 to 1997 uncovered multiple building phases, murals, benches, offerings, eagle-warrior sculptures, and the two ceramic Mictlantecuhtli figures.

The building was probably connected with elite military and royal rites, including ceremonies of accession and penitence. It was not simply “Mictlantecuhtli’s temple.” His figures participated in a program combining death, sacrifice, royal transformation, Toltec revivalism, and passage through sacred space.

Residue studies have been interpreted as evidence of blood offerings around the figures. Such findings support the practice of feeding divine images with blood, although popular accounts sometimes overstate the certainty that every residue was human blood or that the statues themselves received a particular rite described in a codex.

### Temples and priesthood

Colonial descriptions enumerate structures and priestly offices devoted to many divine powers, but the evidence does not permit a complete institutional history of an autonomous, empire-wide Mictlantecuhtli church. He had images, offerings, calendrical functions, and ritual specialists; the surviving record is stronger for his place within multi-deity ceremonial complexes than for a single exclusive high priesthood.

Priests impersonating or serving death gods could use skeletal paint, cut paper, hair, bones, and flayed or death-associated regalia. Specific identifications must be made image by image because skeletal costume could signify an ixiptla—an embodied representative—or a related death being rather than Mictlantecuhtli himself.

### Offerings and sacrifice

**Blood and autosacrifice:** Nahua ritual depended heavily on blood offered by piercing ears, tongue, limbs, or genitals. In the bone myth, divine autosacrifice generates humans. The *Codex Magliabechiano* depicts blood poured over a large skeletal image identified in its commentary with Mictlantecuhtli.

**Human sacrifice and anthropophagy:** The *Magliabechiano* commentary describes sacrificed human flesh cooked and distributed to nobles and temple personnel before Mictlantecuhtli’s image. The text is explicitly hostile and calls the god a demon, but ritual killing and postmortem processing cannot be dismissed as Spanish fabrication: osteological evidence, sacrificial knives, cut marks, skull racks, and skull towers independently demonstrate human sacrifice and display at Tenochtitlan.

The Huei Tzompantli excavations of 2015–2017 identified portions of a major skull-rack complex and a tower of mortared human crania near the Templo Mayor. Current analysis includes at least 214 stabilized skulls from the excavated sample. This confirms large-scale sacrificial display, including women and children as well as adult men.

That evidence does **not** show that every Huei Tzompantli victim was offered specifically to Mictlantecuhtli. The complex belonged to the ceremonial precinct dominated by Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc and embodied imperial warfare, sacrifice, and cosmic maintenance. Linking the entire skull installation exclusively to the death lord would exceed the evidence.

### Funerary practice

Sahagún records cremation, bundling of remains, paper equipment, water, clothing, and sacrifice of a dog. Observances recur through four years. Archaeology shows both cremation and inhumation in Central Mexico; status, age, community, and period affected treatment. No single “Aztec funeral” applied uniformly.

Offerings to the dead were not necessarily offerings to Mictlantecuhtli. A rite assisting a deceased relative on the route through his domain differs from direct worship of its ruler.

### Festivals

Mictlantecuhtli is connected with calendrical rites and mortuary observances, but the frequent modern claim that the ninth and tenth Mexica month festivals were simply ancient “Day of the Dead” celebrations for Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl is too tidy. *Miccailhuitontli* and *Hueymiccailhuitl* were complex seasonal festivals integrated with war, vegetation, children, ancestors, and state ritual. Their correlation with European calendar months also shifted.

## 7. Historical evolution of the figure and cult

### Before approximately 1200: deep Mesoamerican background

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Skeletal gods, skull masks, jaws, underworld beings, caves, bones, owls, and psychopomp animals are attested throughout Mesoamerica long before the Mexica. The El Zapotal death figure, Classic-period Veracruz death imagery, Maya death gods, and Teotihuacan skeletal forms show that the Mexica inherited an ancient symbolic vocabulary.

Continuity of motif does not prove continuity of divine name. Calling every early skeletal figure “Mictlantecuhtli” erases local languages and histories.

### Approximately 1200–1428: formation in Late Postclassic Central Mexico

Nahua-speaking polities entered and reshaped a Central Mexican world already sharing Mixteca-Puebla ritual iconography. Mictlan, death gods, calendrical books, and ninefold nocturnal structures probably developed through regional interchange rather than originating at one moment in Tenochtitlan.

Direct evidence securely naming Mictlantecuhtli remains absent in this period. Reconstruction rests on later sources and regional imagery.

### 1428–1521: imperial Mexica florescence

With the Triple Alliance and Tenochtitlan’s expansion, state ritual concentrated tribute, exotic animals, ancient heirlooms, sacrificial victims, and divine images in the sacred precinct. The House of Eagles sculptures and preconquest tonalamatl imagery show Mictlantecuhtli as a fully formed divine personality.

His importance should not be measured solely by possession of a summit shrine. The Templo Mayor’s twin sanctuaries belonged to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc; death and underworld symbolism nevertheless permeated offerings, royal initiation, warfare, calendrics, and architecture.

### 1519–1600: conquest, recording, and suppression

Spanish conquest destroyed or dismantled the public cult system. Temples were razed or repurposed, divine images broken or buried, ritual specialists persecuted, and sacrifices prohibited. Missionaries learned Nahuatl partly to identify and eradicate indigenous religion.

At the same time, the colonial campaign generated the richest textual record: Sahagún’s multilingual project, Durán, the Magliabechiano group, the *Leyenda de los Soles*, and annotated pictorial codices. These records preserve indigenous voices but also reorder them through Christian categories such as idol, demon, hell, and false god.

Mictlantecuhtli’s public cult ceased to be institutionally visible. The word *Mictlan* survived in Christian Nahuatl as a translation of hell, producing genuine semantic syncretism but also obscuring the old place’s nonmoral character.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: antiquarian survival

European antiquarians copied Mexican codices and incorporated them into comparative histories of paganism. Detached from ritual communities, skeletal images were especially easy to classify as demonic. Indigenous mortuary customs persisted regionally under Catholic saints’ days, confraternities, household memorial rites, and local funeral practice. Direct continuous invocation of Mictlantecuhtli is not securely documented at a scale comparable to the preconquest evidence.

### Nineteenth century: nationalism and print culture

Mexican antiquarianism and national archaeology reclaimed preconquest gods as historical patrimony. Death imagery converged with political caricature and print satire, particularly the skeletal engravings associated with José Guadalupe Posada. Posada’s calaveras were not straightforward revivals of Mictlantecuhtli; they belonged to modern urban printing, Catholic memento-mori traditions, journalism, class satire, and festival culture.

### 1900–1978: scholarly reconstruction

Manuel Gamio, Eduard Seler, Alfonso Caso, Ángel María Garibay, Miguel León-Portilla, and others reconstructed Mexica religion from codices, archaeology, and Nahuatl texts. Seler’s monumental Borgia commentary established many identifications still used today, though later scholars revise his astronomical and pan-Mexican syntheses.

León-Portilla’s *Aztec Thought and Culture* foregrounded Nahuatl philosophical texts. His reconstructions restored indigenous intellectual coherence but have been criticized when elite poetic theology is treated as a unitary “Aztec philosophy.”

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology grouped Central Mexican deities into functional complexes. Mictlantecuhtli belongs to the death-underworld cluster. The scheme remains useful as a comparative index but can make fluid divine identities appear more fixed than the sources warrant.

### 1978–present: Templo Mayor archaeology

The discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monolith in 1978 initiated large-scale excavation of the Templo Mayor precinct. Subsequent work revealed the House of Eagles and its Mictlantecuhtli figures, materially confirming monumental death-god imagery in the imperial capital.

The Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered in 2006 expanded understanding of earth, death, royal burial, and regenerative sacrifice. Neither the Coyolxauhqui stone nor the Tlaltecuhtli monolith depicts Mictlantecuhtli, but both transformed the archaeological framework within which his role is interpreted.

The Huei Tzompantli discoveries of 2015–2017 provided further independent evidence for the bodily reality of state sacrifice and skull display.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Day of the Dead and the continuity dispute

The relationship between preconquest mortuary rites and modern *Día de Muertos* is contested.

**Continuity/synthesis position:** Indigenous communities retained offerings of food, flowers, candles, household visitation, and reciprocal relations with the dead, relocating them into the Catholic feasts of All Saints and All Souls. Regional ethnography shows specifically Indigenous understandings surviving within colonial Catholic forms. On this account, modern practice is a changing synthesis, not a purely European import.

**Colonial-formation position:** Stanley Brandes and other scholars emphasize that the modern festival’s calendar, sugar skulls, breads, cemetery practices, and public iconography arose within colonial and modern Catholic society. European All Saints/All Souls observance, confraternities, baroque death imagery, sugar production, nineteenth-century calavera prints, and twentieth-century nationalism are indispensable. The familiar claim that today’s two-day festival directly continues a single month-long Aztec feast is historically untenable.

**Best-supported conclusion:** Preconquest Central Mexico unquestionably had elaborate funerals, periodic offerings, festivals for dead persons, and divine rulers of Mictlan. The modern Day of the Dead is not the unchanged survival of a festival “founded by Mictecacihuatl and Mictlantecuhtli.” It is a colonial and modern formation in which Indigenous mortuary traditions could persist, adapt, or be reinterpreted within a Catholic calendar. The degree of continuity varies by region and practice.

### Santa Muerte

Claims that Santa Muerte is Mictlantecuhtli or Mictecacihuatl renamed are **[contested modern syncretism]**. Modern devotees themselves hold diverse views. Some identify or associate her with Indigenous death divinities; others understand her wholly within folk Catholicism.

Documented Santa Muerte devotion becomes visible in twentieth-century ethnography, especially love magic and private household practice, before expanding publicly around the turn of the twenty-first century. Her robe, scythe, globe, scales, rosary, and European female Grim Reaper form differ sharply from Mictlantecuhtli’s preconquest regalia. The equation is therefore a modern theological choice or scholarly hypothesis, not demonstrable unbroken identity.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Individual practitioners and small online groups now invoke Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl in Mexicayotl, polytheist, occult, and eclectic pagan settings. Scale, lineage, and ritual consistency are not securely measured. Some attempt reconstruction from codices; others combine Santa Muerte, ceremonial magic, modern witchcraft, and generalized “underworld deity” practice.

No evidence supports claims that contemporary groups possess an uninterrupted secret priesthood descending from the Templo Mayor. Such claims require independent documentation.

### Occultism

Mictlantecuhtli did not play a significant documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoire traditions. Lists that place him beside Hades, Anubis, Thanatos, and Satan are predominantly twentieth- and twenty-first-century comparative occultism.

Modern spell books and websites assign him crystals, tarot cards, planets, sigils, incense, or “shadow-work” correspondences. Unless derived explicitly from a codex, these are **[modern inventions]**. Obsidian, darkness, bones, owls, and the north have historical grounding; fixed correspondences with Pluto, Scorpio, the Death tarot card, Saturday, or black candles generally do not.

### Literature, film, comics, and games

Modern works characteristically turn Mictlantecuhtli into an active villain, final boss, giant skeleton, or demon:

- *El Muerto* comics and the 2007 film use a shrine and supernatural Aztec-death motif.
- *La leyenda de las momias de Guanajuato* incorporates him into animated fantasy.
- *V/H/S/85* (2023), in the “God of Death” segment, turns him into horror imagery.
- The *Bayonetta* game series uses the name for a gigantic bat-like infernal demon, a design without direct preconquest precedent.
- Tabletop games, role-playing settings, fantasy novels, and online games repeatedly make him a ruler of a tiered dungeon.

These works materially shape the international image of the god but are not evidence for ancient tradition.

### National and commercial reception

Museums, tourism, tattoos, clothing, heavy-metal imagery, Halloween merchandise, and “Aztec death whistle” marketing emphasize his skeletal horror. Mexican cultural nationalism more often frames him within the grandeur and philosophical depth of Mexica civilization. Both can detach him from the mortuary, calendrical, and sacrificial systems documented in the sources.

The oversized screaming “death whistle used to terrify conquistadors” is a notable modern legend. Authentic Late Postclassic whistles exist, including skeletal and owl-shaped examples, but the specific battlefield-terror narrative lacks early documentation, and many commercial instruments are modern acoustic redesigns.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Was Mictlantecuhtli a universal god of death?

No. He rules Mictlan and participates in death symbolism, but the manner of death could send a person elsewhere. Tlalocan, the solar afterlife, the dead infants’ region, earth monsters, Cihuacoatl, tzitzimime, and other death beings occupy overlapping fields.

### Is Mictlan hell?

Preconquest evidence does not make Mictlan a place of punishment for sin. Missionaries translated it as hell because both were subterranean destinations of the dead. Modern scholarship distinguishes indigenous mortuary classification from Christian moral judgment.

### Were there exactly nine vertical levels?

“Chicnāuhmictlān,” Vaticanus A’s diagrams, and ninefold symbolism are genuine. The standardized staircase of nine named levels is partly a learned synthesis. Recent work argues that some passages imply horizontal movement and that numerical categories may designate types of sacred space rather than literal floors.

### Is every skeletal deity Mictlantecuhtli?

No. Central Mexican and neighboring traditions contain several skeletal or skull-faced entities. Identification requires diagnostic attributes, calendrical setting, annotations, and provenance. Naming the El Zapotal figure “Mictlantecuhtli” is conventional comparison, not an excavated Totonac name.

### Was he chiefly malevolent?

He obstructs Quetzalcoatl and owns the dead, but “evil” is a Christianizing simplification. His bones enable creation; his realm receives the dead according to cosmic order. Conversely, portraying him as a gentle modern “guardian of transformation” can sanitize the sources’ violence, bodily dissolution, sacrifice, and terror.

### Did the Mexica perform human sacrifice?

Yes. Indigenous and Spanish texts, images, sacrificial architecture, cut-marked remains, cranial perforations, knives, deposits, and the excavated Huei Tzompantli converge. Exact totals reported by conquerors are debated and sometimes exaggerated. Evidence for sacrifice generally must not be converted into evidence that every victim was sacrificed to Mictlantecuhtli.

### Did he have an exclusive temple at the Templo Mayor?

The House of Eagles contained monumental Mictlantecuhtli figures and blood-related ritual evidence, but it was a multifunctional elite ceremonial building. The summit of the Templo Mayor was dedicated to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc. Calling the House of Eagles simply “the Temple of Mictlantecuhtli” is misleading.

### Was Day of the Dead his ancient festival?

Not in its present form. Preconquest festivals and mortuary offerings are real antecedent materials, while the modern dates, Catholic framework, sugar arts, cemetery customs, calavera graphics, and national symbolism have colonial and modern histories.

### State of the evidence

The archive is structurally damaged:

- Spanish conquest and evangelization destroyed buildings, images, books, and priestly institutions.
- Surviving codices are geographically uneven.
- Most alphabetic descriptions were written decades after conquest.
- Missionaries selected material useful for conversion.
- Nahua assistants nevertheless preserved language, categories, ritual speech, and divergent traditions that cannot be reduced to the friars’ intentions.
- Major manuscripts are dispersed among Florence, Paris, Rome, Oxford, Liverpool, Mexico City, and other collections.
- Archaeology has corrected the old assumption that sacrifice was merely hostile Spanish propaganda while also demonstrating that colonial numerical claims require caution.

### Major scholarly contributions

- **Eduard Seler:** foundational iconographic identifications and Borgia-group commentary; sometimes excessively pan-Mesoamerican or astral.
- **Alfonso Caso:** calendrical and pantheon reconstruction.
- **Ángel María Garibay and Miguel León-Portilla:** editions and philosophical interpretation of Nahuatl texts.
- **Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble:** complete English translation of the Nahuatl *Florentine Codex*.
- **H. B. Nicholson:** 1971 functional typology of Central Mexican religion.
- **Alfredo López Austin:** body, cosmos, myth, and the historical “hard nucleus” of Mesoamerican tradition.
- **Michel Graulich:** calendrical, sacrificial, and mythic interpretation, influential but debated in its historical synchronizations.
- **Elizabeth Hill Boone:** pictorial writing, Borgia-group divination, and cycles of time.
- **Leonardo López Luján and Eduardo Matos Moctezuma:** Templo Mayor and House of Eagles archaeology.
- **Michel Olivier:** divine transformation, impersonation, and Tezcatlipoca; methodologically relevant to avoiding rigid divine identities.
- **Mary Miller and Karl Taube:** comparative iconographic reference work.
- **Richard Townsend and Davíd Carrasco:** imperial art, sacred landscape, sacrifice, and political religion.
- **Stanley Brandes and Claudio Lomnitz:** modern Mexican death culture and the critique of simple pre-Hispanic continuity.

## 10. Chronology

- **Classic period, approximately 250–900:** Skeletal death gods appear in several Mesoamerican traditions, including the Gulf Coast and Teotihuacan. **[Regional antecedents; not securely named Mictlantecuhtli]**
- **Approximately 600–900:** Monumental ceramic death figure installed at El Zapotal, Veracruz. **[Regional analogue; Nahuatl name retrospective]**
- **Approximately 1200–1500:** Mixteca-Puebla divinatory iconography develops shared conventions for skeletal gods, night, sacrifice, and calendrical opposition.
- **Approximately 1430–1502:** The House of Eagles at Tenochtitlan is built and enlarged in three principal phases.
- **Approximately 1480:** A major House of Eagles phase receives two monumental ceramic death-god sculptures identified as Mictlantecuhtli. **[Secure archaeological context; identity iconographic]**
- **Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century:** The *Codex Borgia* and related screenfolds depict skeletal calendrical powers, including figures conventionally identified as Mictlantecuhtli. **[Preconquest; precise date and origin disputed]**
- **1450–1521:** Approximate date assigned to the Walters wooden skull mask.
- **1519–1521:** Spanish invasion, siege, and fall of Tenochtitlan; public state cult and ceremonial institutions are violently dismantled.
- **1530s–1540s:** Colonial pictorial and alphabetic documentation of Mexica history and religion expands.
- **Approximately 1541–1542:** *Codex Mendoza* produced.
- **1540s–1570s:** Sahagún’s successive research campaigns collect Nahua accounts.
- **Late 1550s:** *Primeros Memoriales* compiled from Tepepulco inquiry.
- **1558:** Date within the *Leyenda de los Soles* manuscript containing the bone-recovery myth.
- **Mid-sixteenth century:** *Histoyre du Mechique*, *Codex Magliabechiano*, and related manuscript traditions record or reshape death-god material.
- **Later sixteenth century:** *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* and *Codex Vaticanus A* supply annotated images and cosmological diagrams.
- **Approximately 1577:** Surviving *Florentine Codex* completed.
- **1570s–1580s:** Durán composes his history, gods-and-rites book, and calendar.
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** European antiquarians circulate copied codex images; Christian “hell” and “demon” identifications dominate.
- **Nineteenth century:** Mexican antiquarianism and national archaeology reclaim Mexica divine imagery; calavera print culture develops independently of a simple cultic revival.
- **1904–1909:** Seler’s Borgia commentary systematizes death-god iconography.
- **1950–1982:** Anderson and Dibble publish their English *Florentine Codex* volumes.
- **1956/1963 and later editions:** León-Portilla’s *La filosofía náhuatl* and *Aztec Thought and Culture* popularize Nahua philosophical interpretation.
- **1971:** Nicholson publishes his influential typology of Central Mexican religion.
- **1978:** Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monolith launches the modern Templo Mayor Project.
- **1980–1997:** Excavation of the House of Eagles reveals the monumental Mictlantecuhtli figures and associated ritual architecture.
- **1997:** Brandes publishes “Sugar, Colonialism, and Death,” challenging direct-continuity accounts of Day of the Dead.
- **2006:** The Tlaltecuhtli monolith is discovered near the Templo Mayor, substantially expanding archaeological understanding of earth-death symbolism and royal ritual.
- **2006:** López Luján’s comprehensive study of the House of Eagles appears.
- **2009:** The Walters Art Museum receives the Late Postclassic wooden mask identified as Mictlantecuhtli.
- **2015–2017:** Excavations identify major remains of Tenochtitlan’s Huei Tzompantli.
- **2023:** The Getty Research Institute launches the Digital Florentine Codex, providing searchable access to manuscript images, Nahuatl, Spanish, and translations.
- **2020s:** Mictlantecuhtli remains active in museum exhibitions, Mexicayotl and eclectic pagan practice, Santa Muerte debates, horror, animation, comics, and games. No continuous preconquest priestly institution has been demonstrated.
