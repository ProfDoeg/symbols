# Tepeyollotl: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male *teōtl*—a divine power/deity—usually represented as a jaguar or jaguar-clad anthropomorphic being. In the surviving divinatory manuscripts he is a calendrical patron and one of the nine rotating night powers. Colonial annotations identify him with the mountain’s interior, echo, jaguar, and earth; modern scholarship usually treats him as an aspect or *nāhualli* (“disguise,” “animal counterpart,” or transformative form) of Tezcatlipoca.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The fifteenth-century *Codex Borgia*, a preconquest ritual-divinatory screenfold probably painted in the Puebla–Tlaxcala region. Tepeyollotl appears there iconographically, notably among the nine night patrons on plate 14 and as patron of the trecena beginning 1 Deer on plate 63. Because the manuscript is pictorial and its surviving leaves contain no alphabetic Nahuatl captions, the identification rests on concordance with named figures in early colonial cognate manuscripts. The original is Vatican Library, Borg. mess. 1. The precise year, workshop, and city of production remain unknown; recent scholarship favors the fifteenth century and Puebla–Tlaxcala provenance. [Vatican digital manuscript](https://digi.vatlib.it/view/MSS_Borg.mess.1); [Oxford Academic summary of Susan Milbrath’s dating and provenance](https://academic.oup.com/florida-scholarship-online/book/58444).

## Evidentiary labels used

- **Securely attested, preconquest:** present in a surviving manuscript or object made before the Spanish conquest, although modern identification may depend on comparison.
- **Early colonial Indigenous testimony:** recorded by Indigenous painters, scribes, or informants in the sixteenth century under colonial conditions.
- **Colonial gloss/systematization:** interpretation supplied by an Indigenous, mestizo, or European annotator after the conquest; valuable testimony, but not identical with the preconquest image.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference connecting images, texts, linguistic evidence, or wider Mesoamerican patterns.
- **Modern invention or unsupported popular claim:** not demonstrated in the presently identifiable primary corpus.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Tepeyollotl

The normalized Classical Nahuatl form is **Tepēyōllōtl**, conventionally written **Tepeyollotl** or **Tepeyóllotl**. It is a transparent compound:

- *tepētl*: mountain, hill, inhabited or numinous elevation;
- *yōllōtl*: heart, vital interior, center, or inside.

The most literal translation is therefore **“Mountain-Heart,” “Heart of the Mountain,”** or idiomatically **“Interior of the Mountain.”** The Nahuatl Dictionary describes the name as “Hill-Heart” and places the figure in the Tezcatlipoca complex. [Nahuatl Dictionary](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/tepeyollotl).

This etymology is secure at the lexical level. What the compound denoted cosmologically is less certain. “Heart” in Nahuatl can mean a vital or animating center as well as a physical organ. Consequently, the name need not mean merely a god who lives inside a mountain; it may denote the mountain’s animate, responsive interior. Connecting that interior specifically to caves, stored water, fertility, the underworld, or volcanic and seismic forces requires separate evidence.

### Manuscript spellings and colonial reinterpretations

The *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* labels the image **tepeyolotl**. Its surviving manuscript was produced in Mexico City, approximately 1550–1563, on European paper. [Visual Lexicon entry and manuscript image](https://aztecglyphs.wired-humanities.org/content/tepeyollotl-tr8r).

A colonial commentator rendered the name as **Tepeolotlec/Tepeolotle** and attempted to explain it as “lord of beasts” and in relation to “earth sacrifices” or the earth after a flood. Those are not sound morphological analyses of *Tepēyōllōtl*. They are historically important as colonial exegesis, but must not replace the transparent Nahuatl compound. The same commentary also identifies the being with the reverberating voice heard from hill to hill. A transcription and English rendering of this passage was reproduced in Lewis Spence’s 1913 *Myths of Mexico and Peru*, although Spence’s surrounding synthesis is now antiquated. [Spence transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AMyths_of_Mexico_and_Peru.djvu/156).

### Functional descriptions

The following titles are useful descriptions, but most are not independently attested Nahuatl epithets:

- **“Heart of the Mountain”** — direct translation of the name; securely attested linguistically.
- **“The echo”** — sixteenth-century colonial interpretation in the *Telleriano-Remensis* and *Vaticanus A/Ríos* tradition.
- **“Lord of beasts/animals”** — colonial gloss attached to the corrupted form *Tepeolotlec*; its status as an ancient cult title is uncertain.
- **“Jaguar god”** — modern descriptive designation securely grounded in the iconography.
- **“God of caves”** — largely a modern scholarly formulation derived from the name, temple/cave imagery, and the echo gloss.
- **“God of earthquakes”** — common in modern summaries, but less directly established than “echo.” It grows from the mountain/earth complex, the word *ollin* and seismic interpretations, and later scholarly synthesis. The surviving *Telleriano-Remensis* passage concerns reverberation and an altered post-flood earth; it is not a straightforward preconquest hymn naming him “god of earthquakes.”
- **“Eighth Lord of the Night”** — conventional scholarly description of his eighth position in the nine-member sequence. “Eighth hour of the night” is misleading: the sequence appears to assign a patron to an entire night in a repeating nine-night cycle, not to nine hourly divisions. The hourly interpretation originated in older systematizations, especially Seler’s, and is not securely established.

### Relationship to Tezcatlipoca

**Modern scholarly reconstruction from preconquest iconography:** Tepeyollotl is commonly classified as a jaguar form, manifestation, or *nāhualli* of Tezcatlipoca. The case rests primarily on shared insignia:

- characteristic black-and-yellow or dark facial patterning;
- the **anāhuatl**, a circular pectoral;
- a smoking mirror or mirror-foot in the *Codex Borbonicus*;
- Tezcatlipoca’s broader, independently attested jaguar associations.

This is stronger than a superficial resemblance, but no surviving preconquest alphabetic sentence says “Tepeyollotl is Tezcatlipoca.” “Aspect,” “manifestation,” and *nāhualli* are scholarly translations of a fluid iconographic relationship, not interchangeable dogmatic definitions. Guilhem Olivier treats Tepeyollotl within the jaguar, earth, obsidian, and transformative complexes of Tezcatlipoca. [Smithsonian bibliographic record for Olivier](https://www.si.edu/object/mockeries-and-metamorphoses-aztec-god-tezcatlipoca-lord-smoking-mirror-guilhem-olivier-translated%3Asiris_sil_716271).

There is no secure Greek, Roman, Christian, or other cross-cultural *interpretatio* of Tepeyollotl in the early sources. Comparisons to Maya, Zapotec, and other Mesoamerican jaguar or mountain beings are comparative hypotheses, not documented equations made by the communities themselves.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Codex Borgia

**Securely attested, preconquest; fifteenth century, precise date disputed.**

The *Codex Borgia* is a folded deerskin ritual-divinatory manuscript. Its imagery, calendar, and style point toward central Mexico, probably Puebla–Tlaxcala. It is now Vatican Library Borg. mess. 1.

Relevant appearances include:

- **Plate 14:** the sequence conventionally called the Nine Lords of the Night. Tepeyollotl occupies the eighth position.
- **Plate 63:** the trecena beginning **1 Deer (*cē māzatl*)**, with Tepeyollotl as its principal patron and Tlazolteotl as a secondary or paired divine figure.
- Other Borgia-group scenes have been connected to Tepeyollotl through jaguar, temple, mountain, shell, and Tezcatlipoca insignia, but individual identifications vary among Seler, Nowotny, Spranz, Anders–Jansen–Reyes García, Boone, and later interpreters.

The manuscript itself does not narrate a biography or sustained myth of Tepeyollotl. It records his operation in calendrical and divinatory structures.

### Codex Vaticanus B

**Securely attested, preconquest or conquest-era Borgia-group manuscript.**

*Codex Vaticanus B* (Vatican Library Vat. Borg. 3773) contains cognate divinatory material. On plate 22, a jaguar-associated Tepeyollotl appears before a temple or cave-like structure containing a shell. This is a major basis for interpreting the deity as associated with caves, mountain interiors, sound, and water held within the earth. The shell may signify water, sound, breath, or a ritual trumpet; a single image cannot decide among these possibilities. A study of sacred hills and churches notes this precise image and Seler’s edition. [Ruiz Medrano study](https://sitios.colmich.edu.mx/relaciones25/files/revistas/086/pdf/Ethelia_Ruiz_Medrano.pdf).

### Codex Fejérváry-Mayer

**Securely attested, preconquest Borgia-group manuscript.**

The opening cosmogram distributes calendrical powers through the four directions and center. In the conventional reading, Tepeyollotl and Tlaloc occupy the northern quarter. This is spatial-cosmological association, not a narrative in which the two gods meet or converse. The same codex also carries the repeating night-lord sequence. [Discussion of the directional arrangement](https://www.revistas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/download/9194/8572/8978).

### Codex Borbonicus

**Late preconquest or very early colonial; dating remains disputed.**

On plate 3, governing the trecena 1 Deer through 13 Rain, Tepeyollotl appears in jaguar form opposite or alongside Quetzalcoatl. His Tezcatlipoca-like pectoral and replaced foot are important evidence for the Tepeyollotl–Tezcatlipoca relationship. The manuscript’s painting may be indigenous and very early, while alphabetic Spanish glosses are demonstrably postconquest. The physical manuscript is held by the Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale in Paris under shelfmark Y 120; it was acquired in 1826. [Assemblée nationale collection](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/bibliotheque/la-bibliotheque-et-ses-tresors); [French Ministry of Culture material study](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/thematiques/sciences-du-patrimoine/thematiques-de-recherche/etude-des-materiaux/L-examen-du-codex-Borbonicus).

### Codex Telleriano-Remensis

**Early colonial Indigenous painting with colonial alphabetic commentary, ca. 1550–1563.**

This is the indispensable named witness. It includes:

- an image labelled *tepeyolotl*;
- the third trecena;
- the explanation of Tepeyollotl as the roar or reverberation of a voice across a valley;
- an association with the jaguar and earth;
- confused or syncretizing remarks about a flood, impure/earth sacrifices, bad bodily conditions, and feast days.

The manuscript is BnF Mexicain 385. Its contents include a fixed-festival calendar, *tonalamatl*, and historical annals through 1562. [BnF manuscript record](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/8c1d12589b096f30a2c04fbd5646cd27eeea72d0).

The figure’s green pleated neck and back fans, quetzal feathers, divided facial coloring, red mouth area, and exposed teeth can be inspected directly. Eloise Quiñones Keber interprets the green fans as an earthly-fertility rather than aquatic marker and the facial colors as linking him to Tezcatlipoca. That is modern iconographic interpretation, not a manuscript caption. [Visual Lexicon](https://aztecglyphs.wired-humanities.org/content/tepeyollotl-tr8r).

### Codex Vaticanus A / Codex Ríos

**Colonial derivative compilation, later sixteenth century.**

This manuscript transmits material closely related to the *Telleriano-Remensis*, with expanded Italian commentary. Its definition of Tepeyollotl as the mountain’s heart—echo or reverberation within the mountain—corroborates the existence of that colonial explanation, although the two witnesses are textually related and therefore not wholly independent.

### Sahagún corpus: Primeros Memoriales and Florentine Codex

**Important negative evidence.**

Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* were compiled at Tepepulco around 1558–1561; the later *Florentine Codex* was completed through a decades-long collaboration with Nahua scholars and artists and survives in twelve books, Nahuatl and Spanish, in Florence. [Spanish Ministry of Culture manuscript history](https://www.cultura.gob.es/cultura/archivos/difusion/registro-memoria-unesco/2015/obra-fray-bernardino-sahagun.html); [Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/es).

Eduard Seler observed, and later scholarship has reiterated, that Tepeyollotl is not given an independent deity chapter or cult description by Sahagún, Durán, Motolinía, or Mendieta. [INAH study discussing this absence](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/historias/article/download/12917/14026). Claims that “Sahagún’s informants said echoes were literally Tepeyollotl’s answering voice” should therefore be treated cautiously unless an exact Nahuatl passage and folio are supplied. The directly identifiable echo explanation belongs to the *Telleriano-Remensis/Ríos* commentary tradition.

### Other named corpora in the brief

No securely identified independent appearance of Tepeyollotl by name has been established here in:

- Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España* or *Book of the Gods and Rites*;
- the *Codex Mendoza*;
- the *Codex Magliabechiano*;
- *Leyenda de los Soles*;
- *Histoyre du Mechique*;
- *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*;
- *Cantares Mexicanos*.

These works are essential for Mexica religion, creation cycles, Tezcatlipoca, ritual, and imperial institutions, but they must not be converted into Tepeyollotl witnesses by association alone. Their silence helps define the figure’s documented profile: he is prominent in divinatory pictorial systems but remarkably inconspicuous in the surviving alphabetic mythographic and festival narratives.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Mountain interior and caves

**Early colonial testimony plus modern reconstruction.** The name itself securely identifies the mountain’s “heart/interior.” Temple or cave-mouth imagery in the Borgia-group manuscripts strengthens the association with enclosed mountain spaces. Nahua cosmology more generally treated mountains as animate repositories of water and agricultural fertility and caves as passages into the earth, but that wider complex should not automatically be assigned in every detail to Tepeyollotl.

### Echo

**Early colonial testimony.** The *Telleriano-Remensis* commentator says Tepeyollotl is the “retumbo de la voz” when it resounds from one hill to another across a valley. The *Vaticanus A/Ríos* commentary similarly defines the heart of the mountain as the echo or reverberation inside it. This is the clearest verbal statement of his domain.

The jaguar’s roar provides an attractive bridge between animal and acoustic meanings. Nicholson described the mountain-to-mountain echo as like a jaguar’s mighty roar. That formulation is scholarly synthesis, not a surviving Nahuatl hymn.

### Jaguar

**Securely attested iconographically.** Tepeyollotl can appear:

- as a full jaguar;
- as an anthropomorphic being wearing jaguar skin;
- as a human-divine body with feline mouth, ears, claws, or pelt;
- with Tezcatlipoca insignia.

The jaguar signifies nocturnal predation, caves, the earth’s dark interior, rulership, danger, and power across Mesoamerica. Which of these meanings operates in a particular almanac depends on context.

### Earthquakes and earth movement

**Modern scholarly reconstruction with a colonial foundation, but often overstated popularly.** Mountains, subterranean sound, the jaguar’s roar, and the shaking earth readily converge. The *Telleriano-Remensis* commentary discusses the earth after a flood and harmful prognostics, but it does not supply a clean, preconquest-style title “lord of earthquakes.” Calling Tepeyollotl the “Aztec earthquake god” is defensible as shorthand only when marked as a reconstruction; it should not be presented as a quotation from Sahagún.

### Calendar

Tepeyollotl is securely associated with several distinct calendrical roles:

- **Patron of the day sign Calli (“House”)** in day-sign patron series.
- **Principal patron of the trecena 1 Deer–13 Rain**, the third thirteen-day division of the 260-day *tonalpohualli*.
- **Eighth in the repeating sequence of nine night patrons.**

These are separate systems. He does not rule “the third day” in the ordinary numerical sense, nor is “eighth hour of every night” a secure reading.

### Recognizing Tepeyollotl

Common diagnostic features include:

- jaguar body, skin, jaws, teeth, spots, ears, or claws;
- black, yellow, ochre, or divided facial paint comparable to Tezcatlipoca;
- the circular **anāhuatl** pectoral;
- a smoking mirror or mirror replacing a foot;
- quetzal-feather headdress;
- green pleated paper or feather fans;
- white staff or twisted-paper implement in colonial codices;
- proximity to a temple, cave, or mountain sign;
- shell or conch imagery;
- calendrical placement beside 1 Deer or in the eighth night-lord position.

No single attribute except an explicit gloss is infallible. Jaguar figures may instead represent Tezcatlipoca, earth monsters, warriors, day signs, or unnamed predatory forces.

### Colors, directions, and prognostics

- **North:** Tepeyollotl shares the northern quarter with Tlaloc in the standard reading of *Codex Fejérváry-Mayer* page 1.
- **West:** the 1 Deer trecena belongs to the third position in the four-directional ordering of the twenty trecenas and is conventionally assigned west.
- **Good omen:** the *Telleriano-Remensis* gloss evaluates Tepeyollotl’s night-lord influence as “good.” This is a calendrical prognosis, not a general moral classification of the deity.
- **Night:** secure through the nine-night cycle and jaguar imagery.
- **Earth/fertility:** likely, especially in the green terrestrial costume of the *Telleriano-Remensis*, but scholarly rather than explicitly captioned.

No securely attested sacred plant, unique weapon, priestly color, temple number, celestial body, or annual festival exclusively belonging to Tepeyollotl is known.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No surviving source supplies Tepeyollotl with parents, birth narrative, consort, children, or siblings. Modern god lists that insert him into a fixed family of “four Tezcatlipocas” are systematizations built from creation accounts concerning Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, Huitzilopochtli, and Xipe Totec; Tepeyollotl himself is not securely given a place in such a genealogy.

His relationship to Tezcatlipoca is transformational or identificatory, not genealogical.

### Every documented divine interaction or joint scene

Because pictorial almanacs often juxtapose patrons without supplying verbs, “joint scene” must be distinguished from narrative interaction.

- **Tezcatlipoca — identity/manifestation.** Tepeyollotl wears Tezcatlipoca’s anāhuatl and mirror-foot in *Codex Borbonicus* plate 3 and shares his facial marking elsewhere. **Tier:** securely attested iconographic overlap; identification as a *nāhualli* or aspect is modern scholarly reconstruction.

- **Quetzalcoatl — co-patronage and presentation scene.** *Codex Borbonicus* plate 3 places Quetzalcoatl opposite Tepeyollotl in the 1 Deer trecena. Seler noted cognate trecena scenes in which Quetzalcoatl—or, in another version, the Earth Mother—presents a captive to Tepeyollotl. **Tier:** securely attested joint scene; the exact action and its ritual meaning remain disputed. [Early Seler discussion](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7f/Zeitschrift_f%C3%BCr_Ethnologie._Jg._033%2C_1901_%28IA_zeitschriftfuret3319unse%29.pdf).

- **Tlazolteotl — paired trecena patrons.** *Codex Borgia* plate 63 places Tlazolteotl with Tepeyollotl in the almanac for 1 Deer. **Tier:** securely attested preconquest joint appearance; no narrative relationship is stated.

- **Tlaloc — shared northern quarter.** *Codex Fejérváry-Mayer* page 1 places Tlaloc and Tepeyollotl in the north. **Tier:** securely attested spatial pairing; its interpretation as alliance, residence, or complementary earth-and-water rule is scholarly.

- **Chalchiuhtlicue — marriage-almanac pair.** A Borgia/Fejérváry-Mayer marriage-almanac scene interpreted by Elizabeth Hill Boone places the earth deity Tepeyollotl and water goddess Chalchiuhtlicue together beneath a fringed blanket in a jeweled house topped by maize, a favorable marriage prognosis. **Tier:** securely attested pictorial pairing; the identification and mantic reading are modern scholarly interpretation. [Boone article](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/369/36908906.pdf).

- **Xochiquetzal — colonial commentary, not a clearly pictured interaction.** The *Telleriano-Remensis* annotator connects four feast days in the 1 Deer period with “Suchiquezal,” evidently Xochiquetzal, while recounting confused post-flood material. **Tier:** colonial gloss; the nature of any Tepeyollotl–Xochiquetzal relationship cannot be securely recovered.

- **Xiuhtecuhtli, Piltzintecuhtli/Centeotl, Mictlantecuhtli, Chalchiuhtlicue, Tlazolteotl, and Tlaloc — co-members of the nine-night sequence.** They appear with Tepeyollotl in the same structured almanac on *Codex Borgia* plate 14 and cognate manuscripts. **Tier:** securely attested co-membership, not a narrated meeting.

There is no securely attested combat, seduction, marriage, deception, punishment, rescue, or genealogical episode involving Tepeyollotl as an independently named actor.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The mountain’s answering voice

**Early colonial explanatory tradition.** The nearest thing to a Tepeyollotl-specific myth is the *Telleriano-Remensis/Ríos* explanation: the voice resounding through a valley or within a mountain is Tepeyollotl, the mountain’s heart. The jaguar is invoked because of its terrifying power and earth association. In the commentary, this reverberation is also called a remnant or sign of the flood.

This is an etiological explanation of echo and perhaps subterranean rumbling, not a plot-driven story. Its association with a flood may reflect Indigenous tradition, colonial rearrangement, biblical framing, or all three; the sources do not allow these layers to be cleanly separated.

### The 1 Deer prognostic complex

**Preconquest almanac, interpreted through colonial glosses.** Tepeyollotl presides over the third trecena, beginning 1 Deer. Its images include divine partners, offerings, captives, and signs that varied between manuscript traditions. These are mantic templates used to evaluate births, undertakings, or ritual circumstances. They do not constitute a continuous myth in the literary sense.

### Tezcatlipoca’s jaguar transformation and the First Sun

Creation histories such as the *Leyenda de los Soles* describe Tezcatlipoca as the sun of an earlier world and jaguars devouring its giants after Quetzalcoatl strikes him down. **Tepeyollotl is not named in the surviving narrative.** Modern retellings frequently rename the transformed jaguar or devouring-jaguar episode “Tepeyollotl.” That is an inference based on the Tepeyollotl–Tezcatlipoca relationship, not an attested version of the myth.

Accordingly:

- Tezcatlipoca’s jaguar transformation belongs to the documented creation cycle.
- Its relevance to Tepeyollotl is plausible and illuminating.
- “Tepeyollotl fought Quetzalcoatl,” “Tepeyollotl ruled the First Sun,” or “Tepeyollotl devoured the giants” are not secure primary-source statements.

### Jaguar swallowing the sun

Seler interpreted jaguar imagery in *Codex Vaticanus B* as the beast that devours the sun during an eclipse and connected this with Tepeyollotl as darkness and the mountain interior. This remains a recognizable scholarly interpretation, but it should not be promoted into a securely attested narrative that Tepeyollotl personally pursues or eats the sun. The image may encode night, eclipse, solar danger, or a broader earth-jaguar complex.

### What is absent

There is no surviving independent Tepeyollotl epic, hymn, birth cycle, temple legend, or death-and-return narrative. This absence is central to his dossier. His documented existence is primarily calendrical, iconographic, and exegetical.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Independent cult

No securely identified temple dedicated solely to Tepeyollotl is known. No priestly office bearing his name, named priesthood, annual veintena festival, temple treasury, or landholding institution can presently be established.

This is not evidence that no one invoked him. Divinatory books demonstrate ritual significance, but they do not prove an autonomous institution comparable to the Templo Mayor cults of Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc.

Seler explicitly remarked that Sahagún, Durán, Motolinía, and Mendieta do not describe such a cult. The documentary contrast is striking: Tepeyollotl is visually recurrent yet institutionally elusive.

### Divination

This is the strongest documented ritual setting. Specialists consulted painted *tonalamatl* books to read the qualities of days, births, marriages, journeys, offerings, and other undertakings. Tepeyollotl’s roles as Calli patron, 1 Deer trecena patron, and eighth night patron placed his influence within overlapping cycles.

The *Telleriano-Remensis* calls his night influence “good,” while its trecena commentary also preserves ominous material involving palsy, harmful humors, impure offerings, and fasting. These statements should not be harmonized into a single moral character: different almanac components could yield different prognoses.

### Offerings and sacrifice

The Borgia-group images show Tepeyollotl in ritual settings involving incense, wood, conch or shell, bloodletting instruments, captives, or offerings. Whether every depicted object represents an offering directly to him is scene-dependent.

The *Telleriano-Remensis* commentary refers to sacrifices during his thirteen-day period and to four preceding fast days. Its language is corrupt and filtered through colonial interpretation. It does not securely describe a standardized annual festival.

### Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice in Mexica state religion is documented textually and archaeologically. The Huei Tzompantli excavated in Mexico City between 2015 and 2017 is a physical structure incorporating human skulls; ongoing INAH research includes a sample of 214 crania. [INAH Huei Tzompantli site](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/es/node/5907); [INAH bioarchaeological update](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/a-10-anos-del-hallazgo-del-huei-tzompantli-de-tenochtitlan-los-craneos-humanos-son-objeto-de-analisis-de-vanguardia?highlight=WyJ0ZW5vY2h0aXRsYW4iXQ%3D%3D).

That evidence confirms sacrificial killing and skull display in Tenochtitlan; it does **not** identify victims as sacrifices to Tepeyollotl. No excavated skull, offering deposit, or sacrificial installation has been securely tied to him by inscription or exclusive iconography. A captive presented in his trecena imagery indicates sacrificial symbolism but cannot establish the scale or institutional form of an independent Tepeyollotl cult.

There is no evidence of temple prostitution associated with Tepeyollotl.

### Templo Mayor archaeology

The Templo Mayor was principally dedicated to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc. Its rediscovery followed the finding of the Coyolxauhqui stone on 21 February 1978, and the Tlaltecuhtli monolith was found nearby on 2 October 2006. [Proyecto Templo Mayor](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/proyecto-templo-mayor); [INAH on Tlaltecuhtli](https://www.inah.gob.mx/index.php/foto-del-dia/tlaltecuhtli-la-deidad-de-tierra-que-emergio-del-templo-mayor).

These discoveries transformed knowledge of Mexica earth, death, sacrifice, and imperial ritual. They are not Tepeyollotl objects. Some seated stone figures from Templo Mayor offerings, traditionally identified as Xiuhtecuhtli–Huehueteotl, have been reinterpreted by H. B. Nicholson and others as Tepeyollotl on iconographic grounds. That identification remains disputed; archaeological context alone does not decide it. [Nicholson’s discussion in *Art of Aztec Mexico*](https://www.nga.gov/sites/default/files/migrate_images/content/dam/ngaweb/research/publications/pdfs/art-of-aztec-mexico.pdf).

### Caves and local practice

Cave offerings, mountain petitions, rainmaking, and jaguar symbolism are widespread in central and southern Mexico. Some modern scholars see Tepeyollotl as one historical articulation of this complex. Without an inscription, named invocation, or continuous local testimony, however, a cave assemblage should not automatically be labelled a Tepeyollotl sanctuary.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the fifteenth century: antecedents, not attestations

Jaguar, cave, mountain, earth, rulership, and night imagery are extremely old in Mesoamerica, appearing among Olmec, Teotihuacan, Maya, Zapotec, and other traditions. These provide a cultural background but are not early attestations of the name Tepeyollotl. Claims that every ancient were-jaguar, cave jaguar, or Teotihuacan feline “is Tepeyollotl” collapse distinct regions and millennia into a single god and are methodologically unsound.

### Fifteenth century: Borgia-group presence

The first secure evidence is the mature divinatory iconography of the *Codex Borgia* and related manuscripts. By this period the figure was integrated into:

- the nine-night rotation;
- the twenty day-sign patrons;
- trecena patronage;
- directional and mantic schemes;
- a recognizable Tezcatlipoca-jaguar visual complex.

The distribution of Borgia-group manuscripts suggests a broader central Mexican religious-intellectual environment, not simply the state cult of Tenochtitlan.

### Ca. 1500–1521: Mexica manuscript tradition

The *Codex Borbonicus* demonstrates that the 1 Deer patron was known within or close to the Mexica calendrical tradition. Whether the surviving painting was completed shortly before or shortly after the conquest remains disputed.

### 1521–1560s: conquest, suppression, and colonial documentation

Spanish conquest dismantled the public temple system, destroyed or dispersed ritual books, and criminalized public worship of the old gods. Indigenous painters and interpreters nevertheless reproduced calendrical knowledge in manuscripts such as the *Telleriano-Remensis* and Sahagún’s projects.

Tepeyollotl’s record changes at this point:

- formerly uncaptioned images receive alphabetic names;
- the echo explanation is written down;
- flood language and European humoral terminology enter the commentary;
- his old institutional setting, if any, becomes unrecoverable.

### Late sixteenth century: derivative transmission

*Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos* recopied and expanded *Telleriano-Remensis*-related material in an Italian ecclesiastical context. This preserved the echo interpretation but also increased the distance from its Indigenous sources.

### Seventeenth to eighteenth centuries: antiquarian survival

The deity survives principally within manuscripts rather than in documented continuous public cult. The Borgia manuscript was in Italy by the early modern period and later entered Cardinal Stefano Borgia’s collection; it ultimately became part of the Vatican’s Borgiani collection. Its precise journey from Mexico is not documented.

No evidence establishes a continuously named Tepeyollotl cult through these centuries.

### Nineteenth century: facsimiles and comparative reconstruction

European and Mexican antiquarians published and compared the codices. Eduard Seler systematized Tepeyollotl as:

- heart/interior of the mountain;
- echo;
- jaguar;
- cave and earth deity;
- form of Tezcatlipoca;
- eighth “Lord of the Night.”

His comparative work remains foundational, but some conclusions—especially nine rulers equated with nine hours or underworld levels—went beyond the evidence.

### Twentieth century: iconographic typologies

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology placed Tepeyollotl within a major Tezcatlipoca-related deity complex and proposed identifying certain sculptures as him. Bodo Spranz catalogued recurring deity traits throughout the Borgia group. Karl Anton Nowotny shifted interpretation away from free mythological storytelling toward the manuscripts’ ritual and divinatory structures. Elizabeth Hill Boone further analyzed them as “books of fate” in which arrangement, number, gesture, and juxtaposition communicate mantic meaning.

Guilhem Olivier’s work on Tezcatlipoca integrated Tepeyollotl into a broader study of jaguar transformation, earth, night, rulership, and the god’s multiple embodiments.

### Twenty-first century: digitization and qualified revival

High-resolution digitization by the Vatican Library, BnF, Getty, and other institutions makes the primary manuscripts globally accessible. Modern Mexicayotl, Teotecayotl, and reconstructionist polytheist communities sometimes honor Tepeyollotl, but reliable demographic evidence and a standardized liturgy do not exist. A contemporary Teotecayotl site explicitly presents a twenty-first-century religion reconstructed from preconquest Mexica belief. [Teochan](https://teochan.org/).

Such worship is real as modern religious practice. It is not proof of uninterrupted institutional transmission from the fifteenth century.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and museum culture

Tepeyollotl’s modern image largely derives from reproduced codex plates. Color restorations, museum graphics, encyclopedias, tattoo designs, and digital art often isolate the jaguar from its surrounding day signs and prognostic imagery. This turns a relational calendrical figure into a freestanding “god of earthquakes, caves, echoes, and jaguars.”

That summary is serviceable as an introduction but falsely suggests four equally and explicitly documented departmental functions. The evidence is uneven:

- jaguar: directly pictured;
- mountain-heart: directly named;
- echo: explicitly stated in colonial commentary;
- caves: strong contextual inference;
- earthquakes: later synthesis.

### Occultism and esotericism

No substantial role for Tepeyollotl has been located in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the foundational chaos-magic corpus. General occult encyclopedias sometimes absorb Mesoamerican gods into comparative lists, but no historically influential Tepeyollotl-specific occult doctrine has been demonstrated.

Modern websites may assign him crystals, chakras, zodiac signs, invocations, “shadow work,” elemental correspondences, or ritual sigils. Unless explicitly sourced to a preconquest manuscript or a documented modern author, these are modern inventions.

### Neopaganism, Mexicayotl, and reconstructionism

Modern adherents may treat Tepeyollotl as:

- a living *teōtl* within Teotecayotl;
- the indwelling power of mountains;
- a jaguar protector;
- a patron of caves, echo, earth movement, introspection, or ecological defense;
- a form of Tezcatlipoca.

These interpretations can be religiously meaningful while remaining historically modern. There is no evidence for a single recognized contemporary priesthood, central temple, or census of Tepeyollotl worshippers.

Mexicayotl and “Neomexicanidad” also differ. Scholarship describes Neomexicanidad as a hybrid spiritual movement reshaped through New Age frameworks and transnational circulation. [CIESAS discussion](https://tecolotl.ciesas.edu.mx/puntos-de-encuentro-anteriores/vectores-de-transnacionalizacion-del-movimiento-espiritual-neomexicanidad/). Neither should be treated automatically as the direct continuation of a preconquest Tepeyollotl institution.

### Literature, games, and popular culture

Tepeyollotl appears in modern mythological dictionaries, online fiction, role-playing material, fantasy art, and games, usually as an earthquake-producing jaguar. These representations frequently add:

- deliberate control of seismic magnitude;
- a personal rivalry with Quetzalcoatl;
- guardianship of an underworld gate;
- eclipse-devouring as an individual adventure;
- a fixed personality, family, or combat biography.

These are modern narrative elaborations unless a specific codex scene is cited. The deity’s recent visibility in entertainment owes more to striking jaguar imagery and concise online summaries than to a large surviving ancient myth cycle.

### Political and nationalist use

Jaguar and Mexica imagery have broad nationalist and Indigenous-revival significance, but no major, well-documented extremist movement centered specifically on Tepeyollotl has been established. It would be irresponsible to infer such appropriation from general Aztec symbolism.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Tepeyollotl an independent god or Tezcatlipoca?

**Independent-figure evidence:**

- distinct name;
- separate place in night-lord and trecena lists;
- recognizable mountain and echo semantics;
- individual calendrical functions.

**Tezcatlipoca-form evidence:**

- mirror-foot;
- anāhuatl pectoral;
- shared facial painting;
- jaguar transformation;
- wider Nahua pattern in which Tezcatlipoca appears under multiple names and embodiments.

The most cautious formulation is: **Tepeyollotl is a distinguishable calendrical and iconographic divine power closely embedded in the Tezcatlipoca complex and often represented as Tezcatlipoca’s jaguar form.** “Separate god” and “mere alias” are both too rigid.

### Echo versus earthquake

Echo is textually explicit in colonial commentary. Earthquake is interpretively plausible but less explicit. Popular reference works often reverse this evidentiary balance, presenting “earthquake god” as the primary ancient title and echo as secondary.

### Lord of one night versus one hour

The sequence of nine powers repeats through the calendar. Seler’s “nine hours of the night” and correspondence with nine underworld levels became widely repeated. Later calendrical analysis favors rulership of successive complete nights. Thus **“eighth Night Lord”** is preferable to **“god of the eighth hour.”**

### Mexica or broader central Mexican?

Calling Tepeyollotl simply “Aztec” obscures the Borgia group’s likely Puebla–Tlaxcala setting and broader central Mexican circulation. The named colonial witness is from Mexico City, while comparative evidence reaches into Oaxaca and other regions. The being belongs to a shared Late Postclassic religious vocabulary adopted within Mexica practice, not necessarily created at Tenochtitlan.

### Oaxaca and Zapotec comparison

Seler and later writers compared Tepeyollotl with Zapotec beings, including names rendered *Pèchetào*, and with “Heart of the Town/Kingdom” figures elsewhere in Mesoamerica. Such comparisons may reveal cognate concepts of jaguar rulership, cave interiors, and territorial centers. They do not establish simple one-to-one identity or prove that the Nahuatl deity originated in Oaxaca.

### Sculpture identifications

Nicholson proposed recognizing certain seated stone figures—some formerly identified as Xiuhtecuhtli–Huehueteotl—as Tepeyollotl. The diagnosis depends on headdress knobs, facial elements, posture, and codex comparisons. Without naming glyphs, these identifications remain contestable.

### Manuscript transmission

The most important methodological divide is between:

1. preconquest pictorial evidence;
2. early colonial Indigenous repainting and naming;
3. colonial European-language glosses;
4. modern reconstruction.

The echo passage, for example, is early and valuable but not preconquest alphabetic testimony. Conversely, the preconquest *Borgia* image is early but does not name itself. The dossier must use the two together without pretending either supplies what only the other contains.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- That Sahagún gave a detailed Tepeyollotl myth or cult: unsupported.
- That Tepeyollotl was universally “the eighth hour of night”: overconfident older interpretation.
- That he personally ruled the First Sun and fought Quetzalcoatl: conflation with Tezcatlipoca.
- That he had a securely excavated temple at Tenochtitlan: unsupported.
- That the Huei Tzompantli proves human sacrifices specifically to him: unsupported.
- That Olmec were-jaguars are depictions of Tepeyollotl: anachronistic.
- That fixed crystal, chakra, zodiac, or psychological correspondences are ancient: modern invention.
- That every modern jaguar-mask rain festival is continuous Tepeyollotl worship: unproven.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Event in the documented record |
|---|---|
| Before ca. 1200 CE | Widespread Mesoamerican jaguar, cave, mountain, night, and earth symbolism supplies antecedents, but no secure Tepeyollotl name or individually identifiable figure. |
| Fifteenth century | Probable production of the *Codex Borgia* in the Puebla–Tlaxcala region. Tepeyollotl appears among the nine night patrons and as patron of 1 Deer. This is the earliest secure attestation. |
| Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century | Related Borgia-group manuscripts, including *Vaticanus B* and *Fejérváry-Mayer*, place the figure in cave/temple, directional, night-lord, and divinatory contexts. |
| Ca. 1500–1521 or shortly thereafter | *Codex Borbonicus* plate 3 depicts Tepeyollotl with Quetzalcoatl during the 1 Deer trecena and gives him Tezcatlipoca-like insignia. |
| 1519–1521 | Spanish invasion and fall of Tenochtitlan initiate destruction and suppression of public Mexica cult institutions and loss or dispersal of ritual manuscripts. No Tepeyollotl-specific suppression event is recorded. |
| Ca. 1550–1563 | *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* labels Tepeyollotl and preserves the explanation of him as mountain-to-mountain echo, with jaguar, flood, fasting, sacrifice, and prognostic commentary. |
| Ca. 1558–1561 | Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* are compiled at Tepepulco. They provide essential deity and ritual evidence but no securely identified independent Tepeyollotl notice. |
| 1570s | Completion of the *Florentine Codex*. Again, no independent Tepeyollotl chapter is identifiable. |
| Later sixteenth century | *Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos* recopies and expands the echo/mountain-heart commentary. |
| 1600–1611 | A Mexican Indigenous book appears in Giustiniani-family inventories in Italy; its identification with the *Codex Borgia* is possible but uncertain. |
| Eighteenth century | The manuscript later called *Codex Borgia* belongs to Cardinal Stefano Borgia. |
| 1826 | The French Chamber’s library acquires the manuscript later called *Codex Borbonicus*. |
| 1899 | E.-T. Hamy publishes an important facsimile and commentary on the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*. |
| 1901–1909 | Eduard Seler’s comparative studies systematize Tepeyollotl as jaguar, cave/earth, echo, Tezcatlipoca form, and night lord. |
| 1950 | J. Eric S. Thompson compares Tepeyollotl with Maya night and jaguar beings; the proposed equivalences remain comparative rather than historically documented identifications. |
| 1963 | Spanish edition of Seler’s *Codex Borgia* commentary appears through Fondo de Cultura Económica. |
| 1971 | H. B. Nicholson’s deity typology places Tepeyollotl in the Tezcatlipoca complex and influences subsequent iconographic identifications. |
| 21 February 1978 | Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone begins the modern Templo Mayor excavation era. The stone is not an image of Tepeyollotl. |
| 1995 | Eloise Quiñones Keber publishes a major study and facsimile of the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, enabling closer separation of image, gloss, and commentary. |
| 2003 | English publication of Guilhem Olivier’s *Mockeries and Metamorphoses of an Aztec God*, a major reconstruction of Tezcatlipoca’s transformations including the Tepeyollotl complex. |
| 2006 | Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered near the Templo Mayor; it illuminates Mexica earth and underworld religion but is not Tepeyollotl. |
| 2007 | Elizabeth Hill Boone’s *Cycles of Time and Meaning in the Mexican Books of Fate* emphasizes the operational, divinatory logic of Borgia-group manuscripts. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavation of part of the Huei Tzompantli confirms the archaeological reality of large-scale skull display and sacrifice at Tenochtitlan, but supplies no Tepeyollotl-specific attribution. |
| 2020s | Vatican, BnF, Getty, and scholarly digital projects expand direct access to the manuscripts; modern reconstructionist communities and popular media continue to reinterpret Tepeyollotl. |
| Present | Tepeyollotl survives simultaneously as a manuscript-defined calendrical power, an object of academic reconstruction, a deity honored by some modern practitioners, and a heavily simplified “earthquake jaguar god” in popular culture. No continuous institutional cult or reliable worshipper count is established. |
