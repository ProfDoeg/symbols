# Itzpapalotl: Research Dossier

**Class:** A female *teōtl* (divine power/deity) in central Mexican Nahua sources; more specifically a skeletal or death-marked warrior and hunt goddess, a calendrical patron, and a being closely assimilated to the *tzitzimimeh*, the dangerous stellar powers imagined as descending from the sky. Colonial interpreters sometimes call her a “demon,” “witch,” or even male, but these are unstable translations and classifications rather than a single Indigenous category.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The safest pre-Conquest attestation is her painted image and calendrical role in the central Mexican **Codex Borgia**, probably produced in the Puebla–Tlaxcala region during the Late Postclassic, conventionally c. 1400–1521. She appears on pages 11 and 66. A purported Itzpapalotl stela and associated offering from El Cerrito, Querétaro, have sometimes been assigned to an earlier Toltec-associated horizon, but both the dating and the identification require caution; the site museum presents the identification as archaeological fact, while the evidence available online does not establish that the stela bears an independently readable name. The earliest *named and securely contextualized* evidence is therefore the Borgia-group image, followed by the mid-sixteenth-century Codex Telleriano-Remensis and the Nahuatl narrative preserved in the 1558 *Leyenda de los Soles*.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY ATTESTED]** Pre-Conquest or securely derived from pre-Conquest pictorial and ritual tradition.
- **[EARLY COLONIAL]** Sixteenth-century Indigenous or Indigenous-informed testimony, often recorded under Spanish and Christian conditions.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** Local or non-Mexica central Mexican evidence not safely generalizable to the whole Nahua world.
- **[COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]** A Spanish, missionary, or Indigenous-Christian interpreter’s organization or gloss.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern inference from iconography, linguistic comparison, or comparison among incomplete sources.
- **[MODERN RETELLING]** A modern literary, artistic, occult, or popular-cultural transformation.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A claim presented as ancient for which no ancient or early-colonial source has been found.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orienting identification

Itzpapalotl is not adequately described by the modern formula “Aztec goddess of death.” In the surviving record she combines several related but non-identical complexes:

1. A calendrical patroness of the trecena beginning **1 House (*Ce Calli*)**.
2. A skeletal, clawed, sometimes winged female power marked by obsidian or flint knives.
3. A participant in Chichimec hunting and migration mythology, especially the cycle of Mixcoatl and the Mimixcoa.
4. A being associated with Tamoanchan, the mythic place of origin, generation, broken trees, flowers, sex, and expulsion.
5. A *tzitzimitl*-like celestial-terrestrial predator.
6. A power iconographically related to the dead women of childbirth, the *cihuateteo* or *cihuapipiltin*.
7. A sacrificial victim whose burned body produces colored flints, one of which becomes Mixcoatl’s sacred bundle.

These roles coexist, but no surviving source says simply that she was “queen of all the Tzitzimime” or “leader of all Cihuateteo.” Those formulations are modern syntheses of iconographic resemblance and scholarly classification.

### Name and language

**Classical Nahuatl:** *Ītzpāpālōtl*, conventionally written **Itzpapalotl** or **Itzpapálotl**.

The regular analysis is:

- *itztli*: obsidian; by extension, an obsidian blade or cutting stone.
- *pāpālōtl*: butterfly or moth.

The straightforward translation is therefore **“Obsidian Butterfly.”** Because Nahuatl compounds can evoke form, substance, color, function, or costume rather than literal anatomy, “butterfly of obsidian,” “obsidian-bladed butterfly,” and “knife butterfly” are interpretive expansions. The Codex Telleriano-Remensis gloss renders the name as “*navaja de mariposas*,” approximately “knife/blade of butterflies,” and explains her surrounding knives through that etymology. The Telleriano figure and gloss can be examined in the [Visual Lexicon of Aztec Hieroglyphs](https://aztecglyphs.wired-humanities.org/node/2816).

“Clawed Butterfly,” sometimes repeated online as an alternative etymology, is not a literal translation of *itz-* and is best classified **[MODERN OR SCHOLARLY PARAPHRASE]**. It probably arose from her conspicuous animal claws or from proposals connecting her imagery with bats.

### Historical spellings and labels

The alphabetic and pictorial sources use or generate forms including:

- **Itzpapalotl / Ytzpapalotl / Yxpapalotl / Izpapalotl**: orthographic variation typical of sixteenth-century Nahuatl and Spanish.
- **Itzpapálotl**: Spanish scholarly orthography marking stress.
- **Ītzpāpālōtl**: normalized Classical Nahuatl with vowel length.
- **“Cuchillo de mariposas” / “navaja de mariposas”**: colonial Spanish explanatory renderings.
- **Xomoco**: the Codex Telleriano-Remensis gloss says, “*Dezíase Xomoco y después que pecó se dize Itzpapalotl*”—“She was called Xomoco, and after she sinned she is called Itzpapalotl.” This is **[COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]**, inseparable from the interpreter’s Christianizing language of “sin.” The passage is discussed in the study [“Tamoanchan: una imagen verbal del origen”](https://classic.scielo.org.mx/pdf/ecn/v49/v49a3.pdf).
- **Tzitzimicihuatl**, “Tzitzimitl Woman”: one of the self-designations associated with her in the *Leyenda de los Soles* narrative complex. **[EARLY COLONIAL, PROBABLY PRE-CONQUEST NARRATIVE MATERIAL]**
- **Itzcueye**, “She of the Obsidian Skirt,” has sometimes been identified with her in reconstructions of the two-headed deer story. The equivalence is not uniform or securely stated in every manuscript and should not be treated as an automatic second name.

### Gender and classification

The iconographic and narrative balance favors a female being:

- she is shown in female dress or a skirt;
- her bodily and costume features overlap the *cihuateteo*;
- colonial glosses commonly call her a woman or compare her to female witches;
- modern scholarship normally calls her a goddess.

Yet the Telleriano interpreter sometimes treats her as male. Earlier scholars suggested that her warrior loincloth or other masculine costume caused this identification. More fundamentally, European grammatical gender and a binary “god/goddess” taxonomy do not always map neatly onto Nahua deity costumes and transformations. The safest conclusion is: **female in the dominant tradition, but with gender presentation and colonial classification that were not entirely stable**.

### Claimed epithets that require correction

- **“Queen/leader of the Tzitzimime”**: common in modern summaries but not located as a formal title in the major sixteenth-century sources. **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **“Leader of the Cihuateteo”**: Nicholson, Heyden, and subsequent iconographic scholarship connect her with the dead childbirth women; a formal ancient office as their commander is not securely attested. **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **“Goddess of fate”**: used in modern astronomical naming explanations but not a faithful summary of her documented Nahua portfolio. **[MODERN RECLASSIFICATION]**
- **“Mother of war”** and **“patroness of human sacrifice”**: widespread popular descriptions, but not recovered as ancient cult titles.
- **“Ruler of Tamoanchan”**: a reasonable shorthand for her strong association with Tamoanchan and the 1 House scene, but the primary texts do not provide a stable constitutional title equivalent to “queen of Tamoanchan.”

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Codex Borgia

**Date and support:** Pre-Conquest or very early sixteenth century; painted on deerskin in a screenfold format, probably in the Puebla–Tlaxcala cultural region. It is a ritual-divinatory manuscript, not a narrative history.

**Appearances:** Pages 11 and 66 are normally identified as Itzpapalotl. Page 11 belongs to the tonalamatl sequence and associates her with the day sign Vulture and the 1 House trecena. She has a skeletal or death-marked face, claws, butterfly elements, and edged knives. Page 66 contains another death-marked deity figure identified as her in Borgia scholarship.

**What this establishes:** By the Late Postclassic, a deity recognizable through the Itzpapalotl complex belonged to a calendrical and divinatory system extending beyond the imperial Mexica capital.

**What it does not establish:** The codex has no surviving pre-Conquest alphabetic captions. Her name and interpretation result from comparison with colonial annotated manuscripts. It therefore cannot by itself prove every later narrative about Tamoanchan or Mixcoatl.

Elizabeth Hill Boone’s *Cycles of Time and Meaning in the Mexican Books of Fate* remains a major study of how these manuscripts function as divinatory systems. A useful overview of the Borgia’s organization is the [Codex Borgia catalogue summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Borgia), though its claims must be checked against Boone and the manuscript.

### El Cerrito, Querétaro

The El Cerrito site museum identifies a stone stela as the only Mesoamerican stone stela depicting Itzpapalotl. An offering in a small altar contained burned white flint points, skull-shaped shell beads, shell, and obsidian tubes. The museum treats the offering as dedicated to her. The site had substantial occupation during the Epiclassic and Postclassic and strong connections with Toltec-Chichimec cultural traditions.

This is potentially earlier than the Borgia. Nevertheless:

- the stela apparently lacks an alphabetic name;
- iconographic identification depends on modern comparison;
- the museum’s accessible description does not give a tight archaeological date for the stela or offering;
- similar skeletal, celestial, butterfly, and earth-monster imagery can overlap.

It should therefore be reported as **[REGIONAL ARCHAEOLOGICAL IDENTIFICATION, PROBABLE BUT NOT EPIGRAPHICALLY SECURE]**, not unconditionally as her first attestation. See the [INAH El Cerrito museum description](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/es/node/4544) and [museum guide](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/images/museos/guias/17_MS_ElCerrito_2022_ES.pdf).

### Codex Telleriano-Remensis

**Date:** c. 1550–1563; painted in Mexico City by Indigenous artists and annotated by several colonial hands.

**Relevant folio:** 18v in conventional foliation. It places Itzpapalotl over the 1 House trecena. Her image has butterfly-wing elements, knives, claws or raptorial feet, skeletal teeth, a cheek disk, and warrior plumage.

The Spanish gloss:

- explains her name as “knife/blade of butterflies”;
- says she sometimes appeared showing only eagle-like feet;
- relates the day 1 House to female aerial beings whom the annotator calls “demons” and “witches”;
- reports women going naked at night to crossroads, leaving clothing as a sign that they had abandoned sexual misconduct;
- connects the scene with Tamoanchan, the broken or violated tree, and expulsion by Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl.

The manuscript is first-rank evidence, but its demonology, “sin,” earthly-paradise comparison, and witch vocabulary are **[COLONIAL CHRISTIAN INTERPRETATION]**, not neutral translations of Nahua categories. The manuscript image is available through the [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b8458267s/f62.item.zoom), with a transcription through [Tlachia](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/telleriano/385_21v).

### Codex Vaticanus A / Ríos

This colonial manuscript is closely related to the Telleriano-Remensis tradition and repeats or expands portions of its explanatory apparatus. Its Itzpapalotl material belongs to the same 1 House–Tamoanchan complex. Because the two manuscripts are textually related, they are not two wholly independent witnesses.

### *Leyenda de los Soles* / Manuscript of 1558

**Language and date:** Nahuatl; copied or composed in 1558, preserving older central Mexican narratives. It survives in the manuscript conventionally grouped within the *Codex Chimalpopoca*.

This is the principal narrative source for Itzpapalotl. It contains the Mimixcoa/Chichimec cycle:

- celestial or ancestral hunters fail in prescribed duties;
- they become the four hundred Mimixcoa;
- Itzpapalotl attacks or devours them;
- Mixcoatl survives, hides in or around a barrel cactus, shoots her, and burns her;
- colored flints emerge from her burned body;
- Mixcoatl takes the white flint, wraps it, and carries it as a sacred bundle.

A related episode has two Mimixcoa, Xiuhnel and Mimich, encounter two two-headed deer that become women. One man has intercourse with or is overcome by one and is devoured; the other escapes by entering a fire. The exact mapping of the deer-women to Itzpapalotl and Chimalman varies by translation and interpretation. The narrative has been analyzed as a structured account of dangerous women, hunting, predation, fire, and the creation of sacred bundles in [“Sisters, Liaisons, and Dangerous Things”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/sisters-liaisons-and-dangerous-things-a-cognitive-approach-to-a-nahua-metaphor-of-early-colonial-stories/92ED4710B6AF1244ABE67E090B71968C).

### Florentine Codex and *Primeros Memoriales*

Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* were compiled chiefly at Tepepulco c. 1558–1561. The twelve-book Florentine Codex was developed through the 1560s–1570s and survives in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana manuscript, with parallel Nahuatl and Spanish and approximately 2,500 images.

These works are crucial for the categories surrounding Itzpapalotl:

- *tzitzimimeh* and the terror that they might descend during cosmic instability;
- *cihuapipiltin/cihuateteo*, women who died in childbirth and received warrior-like postmortem status;
- Tamoanchan;
- butterfly terminology;
- calendrical divination;
- Mexica ritual and human sacrifice.

However, a full-text digital search does not support the common popular impression that the Florentine Codex supplies a long, self-contained biography of Itzpapalotl. The corpus is contextually indispensable, while her direct named dossier there is sparse. The complete manuscript can be searched through the [Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/en).

A further complication is **Itzpapalotzin**, a Mexica captain whose name includes the goddess’s name with the reverential suffix and who fought during the Spanish invasion. This is evidence for the theonym’s use in a personal name, not an appearance of the goddess. See the [Visual Lexicon entry](https://aztecglyphs.wired-humanities.org/node/2816).

### Codices Borbonicus, Magliabechiano, Mendoza, and related books

- **Codex Borbonicus:** Important for the tonalamatl, festivals, butterfly and winged imagery, and comparable trecena patrons. Some interpreters see eagle rather than insect wings in its Itzpapalotl-related imagery. **[ICONOGRAPHIC COMPARISON]**
- **Codex Magliabechiano:** Essential for festival, mortuary, sacrifice, and deity-costume comparison, but not a rich independent narrative source for Itzpapalotl.
- **Codex Mendoza:** Provides the political, tributary, military, and educational setting of Mexica society. It does not supply a substantial Itzpapalotl myth.
- **Codices Borgia, Vaticanus B, Fejérváry-Mayer, Laud, and Cospi:** Their divinatory pictures provide parallels for death deities, Tamoanchan, knife imagery, directionality, trecenas, and dangerous female powers. Identifying every comparable skeletal female as Itzpapalotl would be circular.

### Durán

Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites* were composed in the later sixteenth century, drawing on Indigenous informants and pictorial sources. Durán is important for Mexica festivals, temples, priesthood, war, and sacrifice. He does not provide an independent, extensive Itzpapalotl cult narrative comparable to his accounts of Huitzilopochtli, Tezcatlipoca, or Tlaloc. This imbalance is evidence: she was not among the most institutionally prominent named recipients of state festival chapters in the surviving Mexica record.

### *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*

The Nahuatl annals, surviving in the *Codex Chimalpopoca*, combine annalistic and mythic history. Their value is strongest for broader central Mexican chronology and traditions connected with Chichimec migration and Tollan. They should not be conflated with the *Leyenda de los Soles*, although both survive in the same manuscript complex.

### *Histoyre du Mechique*

This sixteenth-century French-language compilation preserves Indigenous Mexican creation traditions mediated through André Thevet’s collection and later editors. An obscure passage gives the father of Nanahuatl/Nanahuatzin a name transcribed **Izpatl/Itzpatl**. Rafael Tena tentatively suggested that this might represent Itzpapalotl; other philologists have proposed *itzpatli*, a medicinal plant term. Turning this uncertain masculine name into “Itzpapalotl and Cozcamiauh were Nanahuatzin’s parents” is therefore **[DISPUTED PHILOLOGICAL RECONSTRUCTION]**, not secure genealogy. A recent French study summarizes the problem and objections in [this Toulouse dissertation](https://dante.univ-tlse2.fr/access/files/original/c92df0d96ff9ba362a600fa441d319490d085e7b.pdf).

### *Cantares Mexicanos*

The sixteenth-century Nahuatl songs preserve ritual-poetic language about flowers, war, ancestors, divine embodiment, and the Chichimec past. They are relevant to the cultural vocabulary surrounding Itzpapalotl, but no major, uncontested hymn addressed specifically to her has been established in the standard corpus.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Calendrical function

**[EARLY ATTESTED]** Itzpapalotl presides over the fifteenth trecena, beginning **1 House**. This is her clearest formally documented office. A trecena patron did not “rule thirteen days” in a modern administrative sense; the image supplied the divinatory quality and ritual associations of a numbered-sign sequence.

She is also associated in the Borgia sequence with **Vulture (*Cozcacuauhtli*)**, though day-sign and trecena relationships differ depending on the manuscript section being interpreted.

### Obsidian butterfly

The butterfly is both name and visual matrix. Butterflies in central Mexican art could evoke:

- fire and flame;
- the souls or transformations of warriors and the dead;
- flowers and nectar;
- celestial or stellar motion;
- delicacy joined with edged danger.

The “obsidian” element makes the image materially lethal: obsidian was used for cutting tools and ritual blades. Yet the wings in surviving images may be edged with forms interpretable as either obsidian (*itztli*) or flint (*tecpatl*) knives. The two materials must not be casually interchanged: the narrative of her burned body is specifically about colored flints, while her name invokes obsidian.

Hoffmann proposed a natural model in the saturniid moth **Rothschildia orizaba**, whose wing markings resemble blades or polished stone. This remains **[SCHOLARLY NATURAL-HISTORY IDENTIFICATION]**, not an ancient species label. The Late Postclassic sculpture known as the Altar of Itzpapalotl is discussed by Hernández Celis and Noguez in [Arqueología Mexicana](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/el-altar-de-itzpapalotl-del-mna).

### Knife-tipped wings

**[SECURE ICONOGRAPHIC ATTRIBUTE]** Her costume or transformed body carries wing-like forms edged with pointed blades. Some pictures look butterfly-like; others suggest eagle or raptorial wings. The phrase “she could cut enemies merely by flying past them,” although common in modern fantasy descriptions, is not securely quoted from a sixteenth-century source. The images establish cutting wings; a specific superpower with operating rules is modern extrapolation.

### Skeletal face and death signs

Her face may be partly or completely defleshed, with exposed teeth, a knife-like nose, and death-deity coloration. This places her visually near:

- Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl;
- the *tzitzimimeh*;
- the *cihuateteo*;
- earth monsters with skeletal mouths;
- sacrificed and dismembered divine figures.

This does not reduce all these beings to one identity. Nahua iconography worked by shared bundles of attributes; a skull face could mark death, danger, celestial descent, sacrifice, or transformation.

### Claws, eagle feet, and warrior costume

Borgia and Telleriano images give her animal or jaguar-like claws. The Telleriano gloss says that she sometimes appeared with only eagle-like feet visible. She also wears warrior plumage and, in some readings, a male warrior loincloth. These support a warrior-hunter classification and explain colonial uncertainty over her gender.

### Tamoanchan

Tamoanchan is a mythic place of beginnings, generation, flowers, bodily creation, and transgression. It is variously located in cosmic, terrestrial, western, and ancestral geography. It should not be reduced to “the Aztec heaven.” In the Telleriano tradition, divine inhabitants cut flowers or branches from its tree, provoking Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl to expel them. The imagery includes a broken tree bleeding sap or blood.

Itzpapalotl is strongly joined to this scene, but saying she exclusively owned or governed all Tamoanchan goes beyond the surviving evidence. The place was also connected with mother goddesses, infant generation, maize, sex, and divine origins. Christian scribes compared it to Eden, an unmistakable colonial analogy rather than proof that the Nahua story was a direct borrowing from Genesis.

### Tzitzimimeh

The *tzitzimimeh* were dangerous skeletal celestial powers, often female in early representations, feared as possible devourers of humanity during eclipses, darkness, or the crisis at the completion of a 52-year calendar round. Their classification as “star demons” is a convenient but imperfect modern translation. Some also possessed generative, ancestral, or protective aspects.

Itzpapalotl shares their skeletal body, celestial danger, and female form. She calls herself **Tzitzimicihuatl** in a narrative context. Thus her membership or close identification is well supported. Her formal rank as their queen is not.

### Cihuateteo

The *cihuateteo* or *cihuapipiltin* were the deified spirits of women who died in childbirth, understood as having taken a captive in battle and therefore receiving warrior-like honors. They were feared at crossroads on particular calendrical days and could harm children or cause affliction.

Itzpapalotl’s claws, skeletal face, warrior insignia, female dress, and Telleriano crossroads context connect her closely with them. Nicholson and later scholars accordingly treated her as a representative or patroness of the class. No surviving early text straightforwardly calls her “commander of the Cihuateteo.” That title is **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION POPULARIZED AS FACT]**.

### Colors, substances, and objects

Secure or well-supported associations include:

- black or dark butterfly wings;
- obsidian;
- white and colored flint;
- fire, because her cremation releases flints;
- arrows and the hunting bow through Mixcoatl;
- a sacred bundle containing her flint;
- cactus/biznaga vegetation in the death episode;
- eagle, jaguar, or raptorial claws;
- the 1 House trecena;
- Tamoanchan’s broken flowering tree.

Claims that she had an ancient fixed sacred number, gemstone, incense recipe, lunar phase, or modern-style altar correspondence cannot presently be established.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

No single secure genealogy survives.

- **No securely named parents:** The principal Borgia, Telleriano, and *Leyenda de los Soles* materials do not provide parents.
- **No securely named consort:** Her relation with Mixcoatl is adversarial and transformational, not securely marital.
- **No secure offspring:** The proposed maternity or paternity of Nanahuatzin rests on the disputed reading of *Izpatl* in the *Histoyre du Mechique*.
- **Collective kinship:** She is structurally related to the *tzitzimimeh*, Mimixcoa, mother goddesses, and dead warrior women, but structural resemblance is not literal genealogy.

### Documented divine interactions

Each line below separates an actual narrative encounter from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Mixcoatl — combat, killing, cremation, and relic formation.** Itzpapalotl devours or destroys the four hundred Mimixcoa; Mixcoatl survives, shoots her while she is associated with a cactus, burns her body, selects the white flint that emerges, wraps it, and carries it as a sacred bundle. *Leyenda de los Soles*, Nahuatl manuscript dated 1558. **[EARLY COLONIAL RECORD OF OLDER CHICHIMEC MYTH]**

- **The Four Hundred Mimixcoa — predation.** She attacks and consumes the collective hunters after their failure or transformation within the ancestral hunting cycle. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **[EARLY COLONIAL/CHICHIMEC CYCLE]**

- **Xiuhnel — seduction or sexual encounter followed by devouring.** A two-headed deer becomes a woman; Xiuhnel joins her and is eaten. In contextual readings this deer-woman is Itzpapalotl or one of a pair including her. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **[ATTESTED EPISODE; IDENTIFICATION WITH ITZPAPALOTL PARTLY RECONSTRUCTED]**

- **Mimich — pursuit and escape.** The second deer-woman pursues Mimich, who escapes by leaping into a fire. The episode parallels the Mixcoatl–Itzpapalotl complex, but which woman is Itzpapalotl and which is Chimalman varies among reconstructions. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **[ATTESTED EPISODE; IDENTITY DISPUTED]**

- **Chimalman/Chimalma — paired two-headed-deer transformation.** Itzpapalotl and Chimalman are commonly identified as the two deer-women in the paired narrative. The association is supported by narrative structure but should not be converted into a securely stated sisterhood unless a particular edition’s Nahuatl warrants it. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION FROM EARLY COLONIAL TEXT]**

- **Tonacatecuhtli — expulsion from Tamoanchan.** The male creator/progenitor becomes angry when the divine residents damage the flowering trees and expels them; Itzpapalotl is placed in this interpretive scene. Codex Telleriano-Remensis, fols. 13r and 18v, c. 1550–1563. **[EARLY COLONIAL PICTORIAL-GLOSS TRADITION]**

- **Tonacacihuatl — expulsion from Tamoanchan.** She joins Tonacatecuhtli in expelling the transgressing gods. Same source and date. **[EARLY COLONIAL PICTORIAL-GLOSS TRADITION]**

- **Xomoco — transformation or renaming.** The Telleriano gloss says the figure was called Xomoco before “sinning” and Itzpapalotl afterward. It is unclear whether Xomoco is another deity, an earlier identity, or a corrupted name. Codex Telleriano-Remensis, fol. 18v. **[COLONIAL GLOSS; MEANING DISPUTED]**

- **Nanahuatzin — alleged parent-child relation.** A corrupted or difficult *Histoyre du Mechique* name, *Izpatl/Itzpatl*, has been emended by some editors to Itzpapalotl and treated as Nanahuatzin’s father. The reading is linguistically and theologically problematic and is not corroborated by the better-known Nanahuatzin traditions. *Histoyre du Mechique*, sixteenth-century material surviving through French transmission. **[CONTESTED PHILOLOGICAL HYPOTHESIS]**

- **Cozcamiauh/Tonantzin — alleged co-parent relation.** This follows only if the preceding *Izpatl = Itzpapalotl* emendation is accepted. **[CONTESTED; NOT SECURE]**

- **Piltzintecuhtli and Xochiquetzal — alleged adoptive-family relation through Nanahuatzin.** Also dependent on the disputed *Histoyre* reconstruction. **[CONTESTED]**

- **Quetzalcoatl — no secure direct narrative interaction identified.** Modern retellings sometimes connect her with Quetzalcoatl through Tamoanchan or sacred-bundle creation, but the principal Itzpapalotl episode makes Mixcoatl the operative figure. **[ABSENCE OF SECURE EVIDENCE]**

- **Tezcatlipoca — no secure direct encounter.** Shared nocturnal, jaguar, transformative, and Chichimec symbolism has led to comparisons, but comparison is not interaction. **[SCHOLARLY COMPARISON ONLY]**

- **Mayahuel — no secure encounter.** Modern internet summaries sometimes place Itzpapalotl into the *Histoyre du Mechique* story in which Mayahuel’s *tzitzimitl* grandmother pursues her. The source does not securely name that grandmother Itzpapalotl. **[MODERN CONFLATION]**

- **Cihuacoatl, Coatlicue, Tlazolteotl, Teteoinnan, and Xochiquetzal — iconographic and functional comparison, not automatically identity.** Scholars have grouped or partially equated these mother-earth, death, childbirth, sex, and sacrifice figures. No universal ancient equation is attested. **[SCHOLARLY TYPOLOGY/SYNCRETISTIC RECONSTRUCTION]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The death of Itzpapalotl and the sacred flints

**Earliest full source:** *Leyenda de los Soles*, manuscript dated 1558.

In the mythic past, the Mimixcoa—ancestral hunting or cloud-serpent beings associated with northern Chichimec peoples—fail to satisfy a divine demand or carry out the proper hunt and offering. Their condition becomes one of vulnerability and predation. Itzpapalotl falls upon or captures the four hundred Mimixcoa and devours them.

One hunter survives: Mixcoatl, or a figure who becomes the paradigmatic Mixcoatl. He takes refuge around a large barrel cactus. When Itzpapalotl charges or descends upon the cactus, he shoots her repeatedly. She dies and is burned. From her blazing body leap several colored flints—usually enumerated as blue, white, red, yellow, and black. Mixcoatl chooses the white one, wraps it in cloth, and carries it on his back as a sacred bundle during war or migration.

The version preserved in a Telleriano-related gloss says that Mixcoatl lifted the white flint and wrapped it “as a relic of the goddess Itzpapalotl,” thereafter carrying it in his wars. This is one of the clearest sources for interpreting Indigenous sacred bundles as containing transformed divine substance rather than merely commemorative objects.

**Functions proposed by scholars:**

- origin of a hunting-warrior sacred bundle;
- transition from victim to weapon or relic;
- incorporation of a powerful female/earth/celestial being into a male hunter’s ritual equipment;
- mythic charter for Chichimec hunting, conquest, and sacrificial practice;
- transformation of flesh through fire into durable cutting stone.

Calling the event Mixcoatl’s “marriage” to Itzpapalotl is unsupported. His relationship to her is violent appropriation after combat.

### The two-headed deer-women

**Source:** *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558.

Xiuhnel and Mimich encounter two two-headed deer. The animals transform into women and call to the hunters. Xiuhnel responds sexually or lies with one; the woman devours him. Mimich resists or flees the other. He leaps into a fire, escaping the pursuing being through fiery transformation or protection.

The women are commonly identified as Itzpapalotl and Chimalman/Chimalma. Some versions or translations attach Itzpapalotl more directly to Xiuhnel’s killer; others describe the identification less clearly. The episode is not simply a cautionary tale about women. It is organized around hunter and prey exchanging roles, the peril of sex in liminal wilderness, fire as defense or transformation, and the “sister” terminology of Nahua narrative.

The deer’s two heads may signify doubling, transformation, a composite supernatural animal, or the narrative pairing of the women. Interpretations that present a zoologically literal two-headed deer species miss the pictorial-mythic register.

### Tamoanchan’s broken tree and expulsion

**Sources:** Codex Telleriano-Remensis and related Vaticanus A tradition, mid-to-late sixteenth century.

The gods dwell in Tamoanchan/Xochitlicacan, a place “where the flowers stand.” They cut flowers or branches from sacred trees. Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl become angry and expel them; some descend to earth, others to the underworld. A broken flowering tree exudes blood-like sap.

The Itzpapalotl figure appears beside this complex and is said to have been called Xomoco before “sin.” The Christian vocabulary of disobedience, sin, and “earthly paradise” is the annotator’s interpretive overlay. The Indigenous image may encode separation, sexuality, childbirth, the rupture necessary for created beings to enter the world, or the division of cosmic levels. See [“Tamoanchan: una imagen verbal del origen”](https://classic.scielo.org.mx/pdf/ecn/v49/v49a3.pdf).

The cautious reconstruction is that Itzpapalotl is a participant in, embodiment of, or calendrical patron attached to the Tamoanchan rupture. It is not certain that she alone broke the tree or that the narrative originally resembled Eve’s transgression.

### Tzitzimimeh descent

Nahua sources feared that during an eclipse or an unsuccessful New Fire ceremony the sun might fail and the *tzitzimimeh* descend to consume humanity. Itzpapalotl’s skeletal celestial features place her within this threat complex. Yet no surviving text makes her the sole leader commanding that eschatological descent.

The New Fire ceremony occurred at the completion of a 52-year Calendar Round. Fire was extinguished and rekindled after astronomical observation. The association between Itzpapalotl’s fiery dismemberment, celestial danger, and New Fire is compelling but remains partly structural rather than a narrated episode devoted to her.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Institutional standing

No securely documented major state temple in Tenochtitlan can presently be assigned solely to Itzpapalotl. She lacks the extensive temple personnel, annual festival chapter, and state cult record preserved for Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, Tezcatlipoca, Xipe Totec, or the major agricultural goddesses.

This absence matters. It suggests that her importance lay principally in:

- divinatory manuscripts;
- Chichimec ancestral mythology;
- deity costumes and sacred bundles;
- regional cult;
- specialist rites now poorly preserved.

It does not mean she was a purely literary character.

### El Cerrito evidence

The most concrete cultic claim comes from El Cerrito:

- a stela identified as Itzpapalotl;
- an altar offering containing hand censers;
- burned white flint points;
- skull-shaped shell beads;
- shell beads and obsidian tubes.

White flint is particularly suggestive because the *Leyenda de los Soles* says Mixcoatl selected the white flint from her burned body. If the archaeological context and identification withstand specialist review, this is striking convergence between myth and deposited material. It remains **[REGIONAL ARCHAEOLOGICAL EVIDENCE]**, not proof that the same ritual operated everywhere.

### Altar of Itzpapalotl, National Museum of Anthropology

The basalt monument called the **Altar of Itzpapalotl** dates stylistically to the Late Postclassic, c. 1250–1521. Its imagery includes the falling or defeated goddess, cactus forms with death faces, and the calendrical sign 12 Flint. It is now in the Sala Mexica of Mexico’s National Museum of Anthropology.

Its original provenience is unknown. It was reportedly reused as part of a colonial fountain and later entered antiquarian collections. Consequently, its original architectural setting and ritual function cannot be securely reconstructed. The most complete modern treatment is Beidi Merari Hernández Celis’s [2019 thesis](https://ri.uaemex.mx/handle/20.500.11799/110221).

### Crossroads and rites of moral or bodily separation

The Telleriano gloss says women considered sexually immoral or adulterous went naked at night to crossroads where the dangerous female beings walked, leaving clothing behind as a sign of abandoning misconduct. It is unclear whether this was:

- a widespread Indigenous rite;
- a local practice;
- a penitential ceremony reframed by Christian confession;
- or an annotator’s polemical description.

Itzpapalotl’s trecena governs the passage, but the ritual addresses a class of female aerial beings rather than necessarily a personal priesthood of Itzpapalotl.

### Hunting offerings

Modern scholarship has proposed that hunters offered deer hearts to Itzpapalotl as mistress of animals. This is plausible within the Mixcoatl complex and has been attributed to Ojeda Díaz’s reconstruction. It should be labeled **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION FROM ICONOGRAPHIC AND ETHNOHISTORIC DATA]** unless a specific primary passage is supplied.

### Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice is abundantly attested in Mexica religion through Indigenous-language testimony, Spanish descriptions, osteology, cut marks, sacrificial deposits, skull racks, and temple archaeology. The excavated Huei Tzompantli beside the Templo Mayor is particularly strong evidence. INAH reports that work from 2015–2017 uncovered the platform and a skull tower containing the remains of sacrificed individuals; deposits correspond to construction phases c. 1486–1502 and 1502–1521. See the [INAH site record](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907), [INAH’s tenth-anniversary research report](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/a-10-anos-del-hallazgo-del-huei-tzompantli-de-tenochtitlan-los-craneos-humanos-son-objeto-de-analisis-de-vanguardia?highlight=WyJ0ZW5vY2h0aXRsYW4iXQ%3D%3D), and [Templo Mayor sacrifice study](https://investigacion.inah.gob.mx/_flysystem/fedora/2024-07/pd-the-offering-of-life.-human-and-animal-sacrifice-at-the-west-plaza-of-the-sacred-precinct-of-tenochtitlan.pdf).

What this evidence does **not** show is that the Huei Tzompantli belonged specifically to Itzpapalotl. Its location and state ritual context connect it primarily with the Templo Mayor ceremonial system. Statements that large numbers of people were sacrificed “to Itzpapalotl” require deity-specific evidence that has not been recovered.

Her mythology unmistakably contains killing, eating, burning, and relic production. Her imagery is sacrificial. The existence of Mexica human sacrifice is factually established, while the scale reported by particular conquistadors and chroniclers remains debated.

### Priests, festival, and temple prostitution

- No securely named priestly college peculiar to Itzpapalotl has been identified.
- No annual veintena festival exclusively hers is securely documented.
- No evidence establishes “temple prostitution” in her cult. Applying that phrase to the Telleriano crossroads account would be misleading.
- No oracle center or pilgrimage network devoted uniquely to her is securely known.
- No large corpus of private votive inscriptions survives; central Mexican religion did not leave a theophoric epigraphic record comparable to Greek or Mesopotamian temple inscriptions.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before c. 1200: possible regional formation

**[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Itzpapalotl’s association with Mixcoatl, hunting, northern ancestors, and Toltec-Chichimec peoples suggests that important parts of her complex predate Mexica imperial dominance. El Cerrito may preserve material evidence of such a regional cult. Precise origins cannot be securely assigned to a single ethnic group or century.

### Late Postclassic, c. 1200–1521

This is the period of secure visual attestation:

- she enters the surviving Borgia-group calendrical system;
- her image combines butterfly, flint/obsidian, death, warrior, and clawed attributes;
- the Late Postclassic Altar of Itzpapalotl monumentalizes the cactus-and-burning myth;
- Mexica and neighboring Nahua peoples preserve Chichimec origin narratives in which her defeat creates a sacred bundle.

Her importance appears stronger in divination and ancestral myth than in the principal annual state festivals.

### 1519–1570s: conquest and textual preservation

Spanish conquest violently disrupted temples, priesthoods, manuscripts, and public ritual. Indigenous artists and informants nevertheless preserved her in:

- Telleriano-Remensis imagery and glosses;
- the 1558 *Leyenda de los Soles*;
- Sahaguntine categories and comparative material;
- related central Mexican manuscripts.

Christian vocabulary recast her as a demon, witch, or fallen inhabitant of an earthly paradise. This is not simple survival unchanged; it is a new colonial archive produced under coercive conversion.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

There is no securely documented continuous public cult of Itzpapalotl. The destruction, burial, reuse, and antiquarian recovery of sculpture—including the reported reuse of her altar as a fountain component—illustrate material displacement.

Claims that she survived secretly under a named Catholic saint have not been established. General continuities in butterfly, earth, dead-woman, cave, and ancestral symbolism do not demonstrate uninterrupted worship of this named deity.

### Nineteenth century: codex publication and comparative mythology

European and Mexican antiquarians reproduced the Borgia-group manuscripts and attempted to identify their figures. Comparative work by Eduard Seler and others created much of the modern iconographic dossier. These studies were foundational but sometimes over-systematized fluid deity complexes into a Greco-Roman-style pantheon.

### Twentieth century: typology and structural reconstruction

Major approaches included:

- **Eduard Seler:** codex comparison and calendrical identification.
- **Henry B. Nicholson:** classification of central Mexican deity complexes; Itzpapalotl placed among mother-earth, fertility, and related divinities while retaining war and death associations.
- **Doris Heyden:** earth, cave, moon, mother-goddess, and Tamoanchan interpretations.
- **Miguel León-Portilla:** Nahua concepts of divinity, cosmic balance, and philosophical language.
- **Michel Graulich:** myth-ritual structures, sacrifice, calendrical history, and Tamoanchan.
- **Elizabeth Hill Boone:** pictorial manuscripts as operational divinatory books.
- **Guilhem Olivier:** Tezcatlipoca, transformation, hunting, sacrifice, and deity interchange.
- **David Carrasco:** religion, political power, sacrifice, and sacred centers.
- **Miller and Taube:** comparative iconographic reference work.

The cumulative result was a richer Itzpapalotl, but also the danger of turning scholarly equations—Itzpapalotl = earth = moon = Cihuacoatl = Tlazolteotl = all Tzitzimime—into an artificial single goddess.

### Twenty-first century

Archaeological reinterpretation, digitization of the Florentine Codex, online access to codices, renewed study of Nahuatl narrative, and El Cerrito research have improved access. At the same time, social media and commercial spirituality frequently detach her from sources, presenting her as a universal “dark feminine,” transformation goddess, witch queen, or trauma-healing archetype.

No reliable census establishes the number of contemporary worshippers. Individual devotional practice is documented online, including prayer, meditation, sweat-lodge practices, and self-blood offerings, but these are **modern eclectic or revival practices**, not evidence of an unbroken priestly lineage.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Occult and neopagan Itzpapalotl

Itzpapalotl appears in modern deity lists, occult blogs, role-playing systems, and eclectic pagan communities. Common modern themes include:

- shadow work;
- “dark feminine” empowerment;
- rebirth through sacrifice;
- trauma transformation;
- astral protection;
- moth or butterfly magic;
- knife, obsidian, and blood correspondences;
- devotional self-bloodletting.

The historical sources support transformation through fire, obsidian/flint imagery, predation, death, and warrior power. They do **not** supply modern psychological language, standardized altar instructions, tarot correspondences, planetary rulerships, or a continuous initiation system. Those are **[MODERN INVENTIONS OR ADAPTATIONS]**.

Online testimony shows individuals “working with” or worshipping her—for example, a practitioner discussing offerings and self-blood sacrifice in a [contemporary pagan forum](https://www.reddit.com/r/paganism/comments/146dp3p). Such testimony documents present reception, not ancient ritual.

No evidence places Itzpapalotl in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the classical European grimoire tradition. Claims of an ancient transatlantic occult lineage are unsupported.

### Literature

Laurell K. Hamilton used the name in *Obsidian Butterfly* (2000), the ninth Anita Blake novel. The title helped circulate the English rendering, though the work is not a reconstruction of Nahua mythology.

Adam Nevill’s 2014 novel *No One Gets Out Alive* was adapted into the 2021 Netflix film. The film explicitly transforms Itzpapalotl into a large moth-like being housed in a ritual box and fed murdered women. This representation draws on her butterfly, sacrifice, monstrous, and female-death imagery but is a modern horror invention.

### Film and television

In **No One Gets Out Alive** (2021), Itzpapalotl becomes a sacrificial monster that offers benefits to killers. The plot and cult are fictional. The film materially shaped her contemporary visual reputation more than most scholarly publications; the [Netflix listing](https://www.netflix.com/title/80991313) documents the release.

The character **Orizaba**, the “moth fairy” in Disney’s *Elena of Avalor*, was reportedly inspired by Itzpapalotl. The character is a family-animation adaptation rather than a traditional account.

### Games and fantasy

Tabletop bestiaries often turn *tzitzimimeh* and Itzpapalotl into combat monsters with quantified abilities. These frequently make her a “queen of star demons.” That hierarchy is useful for game design but historically unproven.

Modern strategy and mythology-game discussions increasingly nominate her as an “Aztec minor god” or myth unit. These are evidence of reception, not ancient classification.

### Planetary nomenclature

**Itzpapalotl Tessera** on Venus, approximately 380 km across, was named in 1985. Venusian tesserae are conventionally named for goddesses of fate or fortune, which caused Itzpapalotl to be summarized administratively as a “goddess of fate.” That category reflects the International Astronomical Union’s nomenclatural theme, not a newly discovered Nahua epithet. NASA’s explanation of Venus naming categories identifies tesserae as polygonal terrain named for fate or fortune goddesses in [this nomenclature guide](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930017328/downloads/19930017328.pdf).

### Nationalism and political appropriation

Itzpapalotl participates in the broader modern reclamation of Mexica imagery in Mexican and Chicano art, Indigenous revival, tattooing, feminist imagery, and decolonial spirituality. Unlike Huitzilopochtli, Quetzalcoatl, or Cuauhtemoc, she has not become a dominant state-national emblem.

No well-documented major extremist organization centered on Itzpapalotl was identified. Claims of such appropriation require organizational publications or observed practice, not inference from the use of skeletal or Aztec imagery.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is the name “Obsidian Butterfly” literal?

The linguistic compound is secure. What remains disputed is what the creature represents:

- an actual butterfly;
- a nocturnal moth;
- fire in butterfly form;
- a bat interpreted through butterfly imagery;
- a deity costume combining insect, eagle, jaguar, and stone.

The imagery is composite. Declaring her “really a bat goddess” is too strong.

### Obsidian or flint?

Her name contains obsidian, while the burning myth produces flints and her pictorial wing-tips often resemble *tecpatl* knives. This is not necessarily a contradiction. Different cutting stones could occupy different semantic roles: identity, weapon, costume, calendrical sign, or transformed relic.

### Goddess, demon, or monster?

“Goddess” is the least misleading modern class, but *teōtl* did not mean an omnibenevolent immortal person. “Demon” largely reflects Christian translation of *tzitzimimeh*; “monster” describes her appearance and predation but obscures her calendrical and ancestral authority.

### Tamoanchan ruler or inhabitant?

Her strong juxtaposition with the Tamoanchan tree made “ruler of Tamoanchan” standard in modern reference works. The source record more securely establishes association and participation than exclusive sovereignty.

### Leader of the Cihuateteo?

Nicholson, Heyden, and later writers emphasize her formal resemblance to dead childbirth women. The ancient evidence supports affinity and perhaps exemplary representation. “Leader” is a modern hierarchical restatement.

### Leader of the Tzitzimimeh?

Her name Tzitzimicihuatl and skeletal celestial imagery support membership or identity. Formal supremacy is not explicitly established. Internet repetition has hardened a cautious scholarly relation into a royal title.

### Nanahuatzin’s parent?

This is among the clearest contested cases. The *Histoyre du Mechique* gives an obscure paternal name, *Izpatl*. An editor’s suggestion that it might be Itzpapalotl has been repeated as certain genealogy, sometimes producing the claim that a normally female goddess is Nanahuatzin’s father. Alternative derivation from *itzpatli*, “medicinal plant/remedy,” and the absence of corroboration keep the identification uncertain.

### Earth and moon identifications

Some twentieth-century interpretations describe Itzpapalotl as an earth-and-moon goddess or equate her with Tlazolteotl and related mother goddesses. Earth symbolism is well supported by cactus, predation, sacrifice, skeletal jaws, and Tamoanchan. Exclusive lunar rulership is much less secure and should be treated as a scholarly hypothesis.

### Archaeological identification at El Cerrito

If the El Cerrito stela and offering are securely hers, they substantially enlarge her cultic geography and antiquity. The key unresolved issues are exact context, date, iconographic criteria, and whether the name can be independently read. Museum identification is important but should not erase those questions.

### Sacrifice and colonial distortion

Two opposite errors persist:

1. Treating every Spanish number and polemical description as exact.
2. Denying large-scale human sacrifice because Spanish writers exploited it ideologically.

The archaeological record—including cut-marked bones, sacrificial deposits, skull racks, and the Huei Tzompantli—establishes the practice. Precise numbers and deity-specific allocations require separate proof. Nothing presently assigns the Huei Tzompantli to Itzpapalotl.

### State of the evidence

The record is unusually uneven:

- no known alphabetic pre-Conquest inscription naming her;
- two important Borgia images;
- a strong but colonial-era narrative in the 1558 manuscript;
- one richly annotated Telleriano figure;
- a Late Postclassic sculpture without secure provenience;
- potentially important El Cerrito material;
- no surviving dedicated hymn cycle;
- no known temple archive or priestly manual;
- no securely continuous post-Conquest cult.

The resulting dossier is less a biography than a network reconstructed from divination, iconography, migration myth, colonial glosses, and archaeology.
