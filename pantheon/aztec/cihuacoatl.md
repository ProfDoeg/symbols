# Cihuacoatl: Research Dossier

**Class:** Female *teōtl*—a Nahua divine power conventionally translated “goddess”—associated with earth, maternity, childbirth, warfare, sacrifice, darkness, political sovereignty, and dangerous nocturnal manifestations. She is not securely classifiable as a “snake goddess” in the zoological sense alone: her name, iconography, and offices express both serpent imagery and paired or complementary authority.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Material images now identified as Cihuacoatl belong to the Late Postclassic period, approximately 1400–1521, but most are uninscribed and their attribution remains iconographic rather than self-identifying. The earliest securely named and extensively described witness is therefore the Nahuatl *Primeros Memoriales*, compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators at Tepepolco about 1558–1561, especially folio 264r. It represents a tradition demonstrably alive at conquest, but is a post-conquest manuscript. The preconquest or very early-colonial dating of relevant pages in the *Codex Borbonicus* remains disputed.

**Evidentiary warning:** No uncontested preconquest narrative manuscript devoted to Cihuacoatl survives. The fullest accounts were written after 1521 by Indigenous informants, native annalists, and Spanish friars. They preserve substantial preconquest material but also reflect colonial selection, translation, Christian demonological vocabulary, and retrospective political historiography.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Cihuacoatl

Classical Nahuatl **Cihuācōātl**, colonial spellings *Ciuacoatl, Cioacoatl, Çihuacoatl,* and *Cihuacóatl*. The conventional segmentation is *cihuātl*, “woman,” plus *cōātl*, “snake/serpent”: **“Woman Serpent”** or **“Snake Woman.”** Durán explicitly glosses it *mujer serpiente*.

A second, contextually important interpretation is **“Female Twin,” “Woman-Companion,” or “Female Counterpart.”** Nahuatl *cōātl* can denote a snake but also a twin or close counterpart; Molina’s sixteenth-century lexicography supports “twin.” This sense helps explain the use of *cihuacoatl* for the male official who complemented the *tlahtoani*. The serpent and twin meanings need not have been mutually exclusive. Reading the name exclusively as “female co-regent,” however, understates the unmistakable serpent glyph used to write the office and the deity’s serpentine imagery. The two interpretations are a genuine lexical and historiographical dispute, not a choice between one “correct” ancient meaning and one error. The lexical record is collected in UNAM’s [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/cihuacoatl/193661); the twin interpretation is discussed comparatively in Baquedano’s [“Enemy Brothers or Divine Twins?”](https://www.whereareyouquetzalcoatl.com/mesoamerica/coatlicue/readings/Baquedano2014.pdf).

### Principal documented names and titles

- **Quilaztli / Quilāztli.** The most firmly attested alternate name. In the *Primeros Memoriales* the image is captioned in association with Quilaztli; in the *Leyenda de los Soles*, Quilaztli is expressly identified as Cihuacoatl and grinds the bones from which the present human race is made. “She who causes vegetables/herbs to grow” is a traditional gloss, but the exact derivation is not beyond dispute.

- **Tonantzin**, “Our Revered Mother” or “Our Dear Mother” (*to-nān-tzin*). Sahagún’s Spanish account says that Cihuacoatl was also called Tonantzin. This is an honorific relational title, not necessarily the proper name of one unique goddess. It could be applied to more than one maternal female power, including Toci/Teteo Innan. Treating “Tonantzin” as a single universally consistent deity is a later systematization.

- **Tonan**, “Our Mother.” This shorter maternal designation occurs in festival and pictorial traditions, including the Magliabechiano-group treatment of Tititl.

- **Yaocihuatl / Yāōcihuātl**, “War Woman” or “Woman of War.” Durán connects this name with the divinized daughter of the ruler of Colhuacan, sacrificed and flayed by the migrating Mexica. It marks Cihuacoatl’s martial and state-political character.

- **Cuauhcihuatl / Cuāuhcihuātl**, “Eagle Woman.” The eagle was a military and solar emblem; the epithet reinforces the equivalence between childbirth and battle.

- **Tzitzimicihuatl**, approximately “Tzitzimitl Woman.” This associates her with the fearsome skeletal or stellar beings called *tzitzimime*. “Infernal woman,” sometimes given as a translation, imports Christian cosmology and is too strong.

- **Teoyaomiqui / Teoyaomiqui Cihuacoatl**, a name connected with those who die in sacred war. Its application to Cihuacoatl belongs to learned classificatory sources and should not be made into a separate universally attested persona.

- **Cihuapipiltin / Cihuateteo.** These are principally collective names for deceased parturient women, not simple synonyms for Cihuacoatl. Later summaries often call her their ruler or leader; the primary evidence more securely establishes resemblance, patronage, and membership in the same childbirth-war-death complex than a formally described command hierarchy.

### Classification and divine complex

Sahagún places Cihuacoatl among the highest-ranking goddesses. Durán calls her the sister of Huitzilopochtli. Nicholson’s influential 1971 typology places her in the **Teteo Innan earth-mother complex**, alongside Toci, Tlazolteotl, Coatlicue, Itzpapalotl, and related powers. That classification is a modern scholarly aid, not an Indigenous canonical family tree. Nahua divine identities could overlap without becoming permanently interchangeable.

Cihuacoatl was simultaneously:

- an earth and fertility power;
- a patron and exemplar of childbirth;
- a dangerous nocturnal apparition;
- a martial deity;
- a recipient of human sacrifice;
- a patron of Colhuacan, Xochimilco, Cuitlahuac/Tláhuac, and the southern chinampa zone;
- a state deity incorporated into Tenochtitlan;
- and the name and divine model of a major political and priestly office.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Late Postclassic material evidence

Several stone sculptures dated stylistically to approximately 1400–1521 are identified as Cihuacoatl. The best-known type represents a serpent whose open jaws disclose a human female face. One example in Mexico’s Museo Nacional de Antropología is catalogued by INAH as a sculpture of Cihuacoatl, although its archaeological context is not securely documented. INAH describes it as a serpent with a human face emerging from its mouth and assigns it to the Mexica female-deity complex ([INAH catalogue](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/islandora_74/node/5682?language=en)).

A sculpture from Calixtlahuaca in the Toluca Valley has recently been reexamined as an image of Cihuacoatl. Its serpentine body, bifid tongue, fangs, human face, and regional political context support the identification, but it is still an iconographic attribution rather than a named inscription. The study also compares it with serpent sculpture no. 37 from Templo Mayor Stage III ([Estudios de Cultura Náhuatl](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0071-16752025000100055&script=sci_arttext)).

The conquest monuments known as the Stone of Tizoc and the Stone of Motecuhzoma show defeated towns accompanied by patron gods. Some identifications of the Xochimilco or Colhuacan figures as Cihuacoatl are disputed; Chantico is another proposed identification. They cannot safely be counted as unambiguous portraits.

### *Codex Borgia* and the Borgia Group

The *Codex Borgia* is a ritual-divinatory manuscript usually assigned to the Late Postclassic, perhaps thirteenth through fifteenth century, from somewhere in central or southern Puebla–Tlaxcala or a neighboring region. Female earth, death, and childbirth beings in the manuscript have been identified with Cihuacoatl, Itzpapalotl, Tlazolteotl, or the Cihuateteo. Because the manuscript supplies no alphabetic captions, these names depend on comparison with colonial sources. Boone’s *Cycles of Time and Meaning in the Mexican Books of Fate* is fundamental for treating these figures as participants in a ritual system rather than illustrations of a standardized “Aztec mythology.”

The *Codex Vaticanus B*, *Fejérváry-Mayer*, and *Cospi* provide related divinatory iconography. They are important comparative evidence for the female death-and-earth complex but do not yield a secure named biography of Cihuacoatl.

### *Codex Borbonicus*

The *Codex Borbonicus* contains a *tonalpohualli*, festival cycle, and New Fire sequence. Its pictorial style is Indigenous, but scholars differ on whether it was painted just before or shortly after the conquest. Page 34’s dark sanctuary has been interpreted as the **Tlillan**, Cihuacoatl’s “Place of Blackness,” possibly at Xochimilco rather than Tenochtitlan. The identification is plausible, not certain; the manuscript’s date and localization remain disputed ([UNAM codex notice](https://www.iifilologicas.unam.mx/wikfil/index.php/Ciuacoatl%2C_C%C3%B3dice)).

### *Primeros Memoriales*, ca. 1558–1561

This is the earliest securely named pictorial-textual record. Sahagún and Nahua intellectuals compiled it at Tepepolco. Folio 264r presents **Cihuacoatl-Quilaztli** with:

- facial paint divided red and black, with a white circle;
- enlarged rubber lips;
- crossed “hornlike” hair arrangements;
- a red-and-blue triangular *quechquemitl*;
- eagle-feather elements;
- a shield;
- and a turquoise-mosaic **tzotzopaztli**, the rigid weaving batten that could also function symbolically as a weapon.

The attributes combine women’s productive labor, fertility, warfare, and sacrifice. A modern description and image reference appear in Xavier Noguez’s [“Cihuacóatl en los Primeros Memoriales”](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/cihuacoatl-en-los-primeros-memoriales).

### Florentine Codex, ca. 1575–1577

The surviving manuscript, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana MS Med. Palat. 218–220, is bilingual Nahuatl and Spanish, illustrated by Indigenous painters.

- **Book I, chapter 6:** Cihuacoatl is among the “principal” goddesses. Sahagún’s Spanish column says her name means “woman of the serpent,” calls her Tonantzin, describes white dress, hornlike hair, nocturnal crying, a cradle, and disappearance. The Nahuatl and Spanish columns are not always exact equivalents.

- **Book II:** records temples, priestly offices, festivals, and sacrifices associated with the female-deity complex. A passage locates sacrifices of women for Cihuacoatl at the temple called Coatlan. Its festival framework must not be conflated indiscriminately with rites of Toci, Xilonen, or Ilamatecuhtli.

- **Book VI:** women in childbirth are compared to warriors; a woman who has delivered imitates Cihuacoatl/Quilaztli. This is among the strongest primary links between Cihuacoatl and parturition.

- **Book VIII:** during famine in Moteuczoma’s time, the apparition called Cihuacoatl cries through Mexico.

- **Book XII, chapter 1:** the sixth omen before the Spaniards is a woman heard by night crying, “My children, we must go far away,” or “My children, where shall I take you?” depending on translation and textual version. Book XII does not identify her by name as consistently as Book VIII; their combination underlies the standard Cihuacoatl omen.

The Anderson–Dibble edition remains the basic critical English translation. The University of Utah Press identifies its bilingual method and Book I contents ([publisher record](https://uofupress.com/books/florentine-codex-book-1/)); the original manuscript is accessible through the [Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/).

### Durán, *Historia de las Indias* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*, ca. 1570s–1581

Diego Durán offers the fullest Spanish narrative of the cult. He describes Cihuacoatl as Huitzilopochtli’s sister, patroness especially of Xochimilco, and a deity housed in the dark Tlillan. His account includes an image inaccessible to ordinary worshippers, divine hunger announced by priests, captives sacrificed before her, and rites involving burning, heart extraction, and the sacrifice of a female impersonator.

Durán also preserves:

- the Colhuacan princess/Yaocihuatl narrative;
- the political prominence of Tlacaelel as *cihuacoatl*;
- the equation Cihuacoatl–Quilaztli;
- and the assertion that her cult was particularly important in Xochimilco.

Durán wrote after the destruction of the temples and could interpret Nahua religion through Christian categories. Nevertheless, his local knowledge, Indigenous sources, and frequent divergence from Sahagún make him indispensable independent testimony.

### *Codex Magliabechiano*, *Codex Tudela*, and Magliabechiano Group

These mid-sixteenth-century pictorial manuscripts and derivatives document the monthly festivals, including **Tititl**, with an aged female figure labelled Tonan or Ilamatecuhtli in different witnesses. Some scholars identify this festival figure with Cihuacoatl; others keep Ilamatecuhtli, Tonan, and Cihuacoatl analytically separate. The manuscripts demonstrate the overlap of aged mother, earth, death, and renewal imagery but not a universally fixed identity.

### *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* and *Codex Vaticanus A*

Compiled in the sixteenth century, these historical-calendrical manuscripts preserve veintena gods and annals. Their female earth-deity imagery is comparative evidence, but unlabelled figures should not automatically be assigned to Cihuacoatl.

### *Codex Mendoza*, ca. 1541–1542

The codex primarily records imperial history, tribute, and social organization. Its strongest relevance is the political office of *cihuacoatl*, court hierarchy, conquest ideology, and glyphic conventions, rather than a sustained divine narrative.

### *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558 manuscript

Part of the manuscript commonly called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*, the Nahuatl *Leyenda* preserves the decisive creation episode: Quetzalcoatl descends to Mictlan and obtains the bones of previous humanity. After mishap and recovery, he brings them to Tamoanchan. **Quilaztli—“this is Cihuacoatl”—grinds the bones in a precious or jade bowl.** Quetzalcoatl and other gods bleed themselves over the ground remains, creating the present human beings.

This is the most explicit divine interaction involving Cihuacoatl. It presents her not as sole creatrix but as the female processor of ancestral matter in a cooperative creation involving Quetzalcoatl and the assembled gods. A scholarly discussion of the passage and its sexual-fecundatory symbolism appears in [“Miccacuicatl: cantos mortuorios nahuas prehispánicos”](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0071-16752014000200002&script=sci_arttext).

### *Histoyre du Mechique*, mid-sixteenth century

This French-language compilation of Spanish material preserves central Mexican cosmogonic traditions, including overlapping mother-earth figures and creation accounts. It is valuable as an independent colonial transmission but cannot automatically be read as a preconquest canonical text.

### *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, sixteenth-century Nahuatl manuscript

The annals preserve Toltec and Basin history, divine rulers, and political titles. Cihuacoatl’s relevance lies chiefly in the Colhua-Toltec ideological complex and in titles associated with rulers such as Huemac. Proposed Cihuacoatl identifications in this material are often modern reconstructions.

### *Cantares Mexicanos* and the *Cihuacoatl Hymn*

The manuscript now Biblioteca Nacional de México MS 1628 bis was copied mainly in the later sixteenth century. Its songs may preserve older ritual compositions but are not verbatim preconquest recordings. The hymn conventionally called the **Cihuacoatl song** portrays “our mother” as a warlike Colhuacan power, associated with eagles, drums, fields, and Mixcoatl. Brinton’s older English edition is accessible through the [*Rig Veda Americanus* transcription](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/14993/14993-h/14993-h.htm), but its translations and comparative framework are dated and require checking against modern editions by Garibay, Bierhorst, and León-Portilla.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Earth, fertility, and vegetation

Cihuacoatl’s motherhood is not merely domestic. She is an earth power who receives bodies, produces vegetation, and transforms death into sustenance. Quilaztli’s grinding of ancestral bones resembles the grinding of maize on a metate: humanity is processed as food is processed. Her importance in Xochimilco, Colhuacan, Cuitlahuac, and other chinampa-producing regions reinforces an agricultural dimension.

Nicholson therefore placed her within the rain-moisture-agricultural-fertility complex, specifically its Teteo Innan subdivision. This is **modern scholarly reconstruction**, useful but not itself an ancient taxonomy.

### Childbirth and midwifery

Book VI of the *Florentine Codex* provides the secure foundation: childbirth was addressed as battle, the parturient as warrior, and successful delivery could be described as imitation of Cihuacoatl/Quilaztli. Her open mouth, aggressive weaponry, and eagle associations express the peril and valor of delivery rather than a gentle modern maternity ideal.

### Warfare and political power

Her titles Yaocihuatl and Cuauhcihuatl, eagle-feather shield, and weapon-like weaving batten mark her as martial. The goddess’s feminine sovereignty complemented Huitzilopochtli’s male-warrior state power. The political *cihuacoatl*—especially Tlacaelel—could act as commander, judge, administrator, ritual deputy, and counterpart of the ruler.

### Death, sacrifice, and divine hunger

Cihuacoatl’s maternal power encompasses killing. Durán reports that when she “hungered,” priests demanded victims. Sahagún places female sacrifices for her at Coatlan. In Xochimilco, Durán describes victims exposed to a divine brazier, removed while alive, and killed by heart extraction. Accounts also describe the decapitation of a female impersonator and the presentation of her blood and heart.

These are **early-colonial documentary reports of preconquest practice**. They are not all independently verified in Cihuacoatl’s particular sanctuary, but archaeology now makes denial of Mexica human sacrifice generally untenable. The Huei Tzompantli excavated between 2015 and 2017 contained architectural skull displays and offerings dated to 1486–1521 ([INAH Huei Tzompantli](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907)). This corroborates the material institution of large-scale state sacrifice and skull display; it does not identify individual remains as offerings to Cihuacoatl.

### Nocturnal apparition and cradle

In Sahagún, she wanders at night crying or roaring. She carries a cradle on her back, leaves it in the marketplace, and disappears. A person opening the cradle finds not a child but a sacrificial flint knife. The episode joins motherhood, infant absence, sacrifice, omen, and fear. The Library of Congress’s folklore discussion reproduces and contextualizes this account ([“La Llorona: Roots, Branches, and the Missing Link from Spain”](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2021/10/la-llorona-roots-branches-and-the-missing-link-from-spain/)).

### Dress and diagnostic attributes

Across the *Primeros Memoriales*, *Florentine Codex*, and later pictorial sources, attributes include:

- white clothing;
- a triangular *quechquemitl*;
- half-red, half-black facial painting;
- a white facial disk or marking;
- rubber lips;
- crossed or hornlike hair;
- obsidian or gold ear ornaments;
- an eagle-feather headdress and shield;
- the **tzotzopaztli**, a weaving batten, sometimes ornamented with turquoise mosaic;
- skull imagery;
- serpentine jaws or body;
- exposed breast in some depictions;
- jade bands and water-flower designs.

Not every figure bearing one of these traits is Cihuacoatl. The tzotzopaztli, flayed-skin imagery, skulls, and bicolored paint also occur among Toci, Tlazolteotl, Itzpapalotl, Cihuateteo, and other beings.

### Sacred locations and temporal associations

- **Tlillan / Tlillancalco**, “Place/House of Blackness,” was her principal kind of sanctuary.
- The **marketplace** is the setting of the abandoned-cradle apparition.
- **Crossroads** and dangerous calendrical days belong most explicitly to the Cihuateteo; their wholesale transfer to Cihuacoatl is a later synthesis.
- **Colhuacan** is repeatedly invoked as ancestral home and cult center.
- **Xochimilco and the southern lake/chinampa zone** were major regional bases.
- No secure ancient evidence establishes a unique planet, color, number, or modern-style “correspondence table” for her. Lists assigning crystals, tarot cards, zodiacal signs, or modern occult elements are modern inventions.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

Cihuacoatl has no single stable genealogy. Sources more often define her by title, place, function, and ritual relation than by parentage.

### Competing genealogical models

1. **Sister of Huitzilopochtli:** Durán explicitly calls her Huitzilopochtli’s sister. This is the clearest genealogical statement but comes from one major colonial tradition.

2. **Mother of humankind:** The *Leyenda de los Soles* does not say she gives birth biologically to humanity. As Quilaztli she grinds the bones that Quetzalcoatl fertilizes with sacrificial blood. Calling her “mother of the human race” is a legitimate later summary, but it compresses a cooperative ritual creation.

3. **Mother of Mixcoatl:** Some later compilations make Cihuacoatl the mother of Mixcoatl or fold her into the mother of the Mimixcoa. The primary narratives vary, also naming Iztac Chalchiuhtlicue and Itzpapalotl-like figures. This is a regional or learned fusion, not secure pan-Nahua doctrine.

4. **Identity with Toci, Coatlicue, Itzpapalotl, or Ilamatecuhtli:** Shared attributes led Seler and later scholars to speak of aspects or manifestations of one earth-mother goddess. More recent work generally treats this as an overlapping complex rather than literal universal identity.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Quetzalcoatl — cooperative creation of humanity.** Quetzalcoatl retrieves ancestral bones from Mictlan; Quilaztli/Cihuacoatl grinds them in Tamoanchan before Quetzalcoatl and other gods bleed upon them. *Leyenda de los Soles*, manuscript dated 1558. **Tier: early-colonial attestation of a preconquest cosmogony; later canonical.**

- **Mictlantecuhtli — indirect participation in the bones episode.** Mictlantecuhtli opposes Quetzalcoatl’s removal of the bones that Cihuacoatl later grinds; Cihuacoatl and Mictlantecuhtli do not meet in the surviving text. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **Tier: securely attested same narrative, no direct interaction.**

- **Quilaztli — explicit identity statement.** “Quilaztli, who is Cihuacoatl,” grinds the bones. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558; also *Primeros Memoriales*, ca. 1558–1561. **Tier: securely attested alternate identity.**

- **Huitzilopochtli — sibling relationship and complementary state cult.** Durán calls Cihuacoatl his sister; women attached to her cult serve in relation to his sanctuary. Durán, 1570s–1581. **Tier: early-colonial canonical elaboration, not corroborated as genealogy by every source.**

- **Huitzilopochtli — institutional pairing.** The *tlahtoani*, ideologically associated with Huitzilopochtli, is complemented by the male *cihuacoatl*, embodying female-earth authority. Sixteenth-century historical sources on Tlacaelel. **Tier: learned/state systematization grounded in attested offices.**

- **Mixcoatl — joint appearance in the Cihuacoatl hymn.** The hymn compares or associates “our mother” with “our lord Mixcoatl” amid war and field imagery. *Cantares Mexicanos* manuscript, later sixteenth century, likely preserving older ritual poetry. **Tier: early-colonial attestation; precise original date unknown.**

- **Mixcoatl — alleged mother-son relation.** Later syntheses sometimes call her his mother, drawing together Mimixcoa and earth-mother traditions. The genealogy is unstable. **Tier: regional variant or modern scholarly harmonization.**

- **Itzpapalotl — overlapping identity, not a securely narrated meeting.** Both belong to the warrior-earth-death complex; some myths and scholars identify Itzpapalotl as a form of Cihuacoatl. The *Leyenda de los Soles* gives Itzpapalotl her own interactions with the Mimixcoa, not an encounter with Cihuacoatl. **Tier: learned systematization/syncretistic identification.**

- **Toci/Teteo Innan — cultic and functional overlap.** Both are maternal earth powers associated with warfare, fertility, and female sacrifice. Sahagún describes them in separate chapters; Nicholson groups them within one complex. **Tier: securely attested parallel figures; equivalence is modern reconstruction.**

- **Coatlicue — iconographic and functional overlap.** Colossal sculptures have been variously labelled Coatlicue, Cihuacoatl, or Tlaltecuhtli. No surviving primary narrative records the two meeting. **Tier: modern iconographic dispute.** Boone’s reassessment is summarized in [“The ‘Coatlicues’ at the Templo Mayor”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/coatlicues-at-the-templo-mayor/EAC85630B0289443B4D5C6EE80C1CC85).

- **Tlaltecuhtli — earth-deity overlap, no narrative meeting.** Their open jaws, death imagery, and earth functions have caused disputed sculptural identifications. **Tier: modern scholarly comparison; absence of attested interaction.**

- **Xilonen — co-occurrence in festival sacrifice.** Sources and Nicholson’s table place an impersonator of Cihuacoatl and the young-maize goddess Xilonen within Huey Tecuilhuitl rites; Sahagún describes Xilonen’s death more fully than Cihuacoatl’s. **Tier: colonial ritual attestation, details disputed.**

- **Ilamatecuhtli/Tonan — Tititl overlap.** Magliabechiano-group manuscripts depict the aged festival patron under names that have been equated with Cihuacoatl. No distinct meeting is narrated. **Tier: regional manuscript variant/learned identification.**

- **Cihuateteo/Cihuapipiltin — patronage and structural resemblance.** They resemble Cihuacoatl as dead mothers, warriors, and nocturnal dangers. No primary text presently establishes that they form a military “host” commanded by her. **Tier: strong functional association; formal rulership is later systematization.**

- **Tonantzin — title applied to Cihuacoatl.** Sahagún explicitly reports this usage. Because *Tonantzin* was a title applicable to several maternal beings, it is not proof that every Tonantzin reference denotes Cihuacoatl. **Tier: securely attested title; later conflation disputed.**

There is no securely attested direct interaction with Tezcatlipoca, Tlaloc, Coyolxauhqui, Chalchiuhtlicue, or the Christian Virgin Mary in a preconquest narrative. Later comparison or cultic replacement must not be rewritten as mythic meetings.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Creation of present humanity

**Source:** *Leyenda de los Soles*, Nahuatl manuscript dated 1558.

Quetzalcoatl journeys to Mictlan to retrieve the bones of people from earlier creations. Mictlantecuhtli imposes a test and engineers a pit; Quetzalcoatl falls, and quail gnaw or scatter the bones. He recovers what he can and carries them to Tamoanchan. The damage explains why present humans differ in size.

At Tamoanchan, Quilaztli—identified within the text as Cihuacoatl—grinds the bones in a precious bowl. Quetzalcoatl bleeds his penis over the powder; other gods likewise perform blood sacrifice. From this mixture humanity is formed.

**Interpretation:** The episode joins grinding, maize preparation, ancestral remains, sexual fertility, and autosacrifice. Cihuacoatl supplies indispensable transformative labor; Quetzalcoatl supplies retrieval and blood. Modern retellings that make Quetzalcoatl the sole creator omit her role; versions that make Cihuacoatl independently create humanity omit the collective structure.

### The Colhuacan princess and Yaocihuatl

**Sources:** Durán and later Mexica historical traditions, written in the sixteenth century.

During the Mexica migration, Huitzilopochtli directs or permits the Mexica to request a daughter of Achitometl, ruler of Colhuacan, ostensibly to make her a queen or goddess. They kill and flay her. A priest dons her skin, and her father is invited to worship; when torchlight reveals the skin, he recoils and the Mexica are driven from the area. The woman is associated with **Yaocihuatl**, “War Woman,” and consequently with Cihuacoatl.

**Historical level:** This is not contemporaneous evidence for an event in 1323 or any other exact year. It is a conquest-era origin tradition explaining Mexica conflict with prestigious Colhuacan, ritual flaying, dynastic legitimacy, and appropriation of a regional female power. Its political message may be more recoverable than its event history.

### The wailing mother and abandoned cradle

**Source:** Sahagún, Book I; a related manifestation in Book VIII.

Cihuacoatl roams at night, crying and moaning. She bears a cradle as though carrying an infant. She enters the marketplace among women, disappears, and leaves the cradle. Those who open it find a flint sacrificial knife rather than a child.

The image is not simply “a mother mourning drowned children.” No drowning and no act of child murder occurs. The knife makes the absent child and sacrificial victim overlap ominously.

### Sixth omen before the conquest

**Sources:** *Florentine Codex*, Books VIII and XII, finalized ca. 1575–1577; cognate omen traditions in Durán and Muñoz Camargo.

A female voice is repeatedly heard at night lamenting for “my children” and wondering where she can take them. Book VIII identifies the apparition as Cihuacoatl; Book XII presents the weeping woman as the sixth omen without the same explicit identification. The omen sequence was recorded decades after the conquest and is narratively shaped by knowledge of the outcome.

Thus:

- that a Cihuacoatl tradition of nocturnal wailing existed is securely documented;
- that informants associated such wailing with preconquest disaster is documented;
- that a supernatural cry was independently recorded before 1519 cannot be established;
- the detailed image of a white-gowned woman hovering over Lake Texcoco with long loose black hair is largely modern elaboration, not what the Florentine text actually says.

### Cihuacoatl and La Llorona

The resemblance is substantial: both are nocturnal female lamenters associated with threatened or lost children. But the colonial La Llorona cycle also has Iberian, Catholic, local ghost-story, infanticide, betrayed-woman, and Malinche strands. The recognizable story of a woman who kills or drowns her children and must search for them is not found in the Cihuacoatl passages.

The sound conclusion is **genealogical possibility and later cultural fusion, not demonstrated direct descent**. Gloria Anzaldúa’s *Borderlands/La Frontera* (1987) powerfully rejoined Cihuacoatl, La Llorona, Malintzin, and Guadalupe in Chicana feminist thought. That is an important modern reinterpretation, not evidence of an unchanged preconquest tale.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Colhuacan and the southern lakes

Cihuacoatl was especially associated with **Colhuacan, Xochimilco, Cuitlahuac/Tláhuac, Mixquic**, and the chinampa zone south of Tenochtitlan. The Cihuacoatl hymn invokes Colhuacan as an ancestral home. Durán calls her Xochimilco’s principal or patron goddess while also emphasizing her worship in Mexico and Tetzcoco.

Mexica incorporation of her cult probably carried several political meanings:

- annexation of southern agricultural wealth;
- appropriation of prestigious Colhua-Toltec ancestry;
- ritual control of the earth and food supply;
- and the construction of a complementary female authority within the imperial state.

Claims that her cult was originally “Otomí” occur in museum and modern scholarly summaries, but the route and date of such an origin cannot be reconstructed securely.

### The Tlillan

The **Tlillan**, “Place of Blackness,” was Cihuacoatl’s sanctuary. Durán describes a dark, restricted chamber, with an entrance so low that priests entered bent down or crawling. Her image remained secluded. The darkness was an active ritual environment connected with earth interiority, ancestry, oracular manifestation, and divine danger.

At Tenochtitlan, sources distinguish or conflate **Tlillan**, **Tlillan Calmecac**, **Coatlan**, and related priestly buildings. Modern reconstructions should not assume every reference names one architectural room. Sahagún lists a Tlillan Calmecac and the priestly title *Tlillantlenamacac*; Durán’s account supplies much of the vivid architectural description.

A probable Tlillan at Xochimilco is traditionally placed beneath or near the later church of San Bernardino de Siena. The church’s construction began in the colonial period on an Indigenous ceremonial center, but the exact archaeological identification of a surviving Cihuacoatl temple is not fully demonstrated.

### Priests and the political *cihuacoatl*

Priestly titles included forms derived from Cihuacoatl and Tlillan. The office should be distinguished from the civil-military **cihuacoatl**, although the two institutions drew on the same divine symbolism.

The civil *cihuacoatl* was second only to, or complementary with, the *tlahtoani*. Functions attributed in historical sources include:

- administration and adjudication;
- command in war;
- consultation on succession;
- management of tribute and public works;
- ritual substitution for the ruler;
- and custody of the capital when the ruler campaigned.

**Tlacaelel** is the most famous holder. He served under several rulers during the formation and expansion of the Triple Alliance. Later chronicles magnify him into architect of virtually all Mexica institutions; his importance is secure, but the portrait of a single all-designing statesman may reflect family historiography. Tezozomoc’s *Crónica Mexicana* repeatedly calls him *Cihuacoatl Tlacaeleltzin* and “captain general,” examples collected in the [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/cihuacoatl/176019).

It is misleading to call Tlacaelel “the second ruler” if this suggests another *tlahtoani*. He occupied a complementary supreme office, conventionally rendered vice-ruler, chief minister, or co-regent.

### Festivals and offerings

Cihuacoatl is tied most strongly to **Huey Tecuilhuitl** in Durán’s Xochimilco account and to the broader female agricultural cycle. Tonan/Cihuacoatl identifications also connect her to **Tititl**, the last veintena in many calendar reckonings. The documentary traditions disagree because local calendars and divine identifications varied.

Offerings included:

- copal incense;
- paper and textile regalia;
- food and agricultural produce;
- blood from autosacrifice;
- captive sacrifice;
- female divine impersonation;
- fire sacrifice followed by heart extraction;
- decapitation;
- and possibly blood applied to the sanctuary.

A useful documentary summary of the Xochimilco rite reports that victims were held over or cast into the “divine brazier,” removed in agony, and killed by heart extraction ([UNAM seminar](https://blogs.acatlan.unam.mx/scronicas/2015/06/18/rituales-de-sacrificio-iii/)). Numbers and exact sequences vary among modern retellings and should not be treated as archaeologically counted totals.

### Cihuateteo and women dead in childbirth

Women dying during pregnancy or delivery—especially first delivery in many accounts—could become **mocihuaquetzqueh**, “women who rise,” or **cihuateteo**, “divine women.” They accompanied the sun after midday, paralleling male warriors who accompanied it in the morning. They could also descend on specific day signs, haunt crossroads, injure children, cause seizures or paralysis, and become objects of dangerous magical acquisition.

Warriors reportedly attempted to obtain a dead parturient’s finger, arm, hair, or other bodily relics for courage or invulnerability; relatives guarded the corpse. Sahagún’s Nahuatl says the dead women were “made goddesses,” as preserved in the [Online Nahuatl Dictionary](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/cihuateteo).

Cihuacoatl is their divine analogue and patron because childbirth, warfare, death, and dangerous female power coincide in her. Calling the Cihuateteo her “host” is a useful modern shorthand, but no surviving primary passage sets out a command structure resembling an army.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before ca. 1400: possible antecedents

Female earth, snake, skeletal, birth, and death imagery has deep roots in Mesoamerica. Teotihuacan, Epiclassic, Toltec, Mixtec, and earlier central Mexican images supplied part of the symbolic vocabulary later found around Cihuacoatl. No securely named early inscription proves that all such figures are “Cihuacoatl.” Projecting her proper name backward into the Classic or Formative periods is modern reconstruction.

### Late Postclassic, ca. 1400–1521

By the fifteenth century, a recognizable Cihuacoatl-Quilaztli cult existed in the Basin of Mexico, especially the southern lake towns. Mexica expansion absorbed and monumentalized it. Her imagery entered imperial conquest sculpture, the sacred precinct, political office, and narratives of Colhua legitimacy.

This is the period represented by surviving sculptures and probably portions of the pictorial codex tradition. Yet named documentation chiefly survives through sixteenth-century copies.

### Spanish invasion and temple suppression, 1519–1520s

The conquest destroyed or converted the institutional basis of the cult. Tenochtitlan’s sacred precinct was razed, images were smashed, buried, reused, or removed, and Christian buildings were erected over or beside Indigenous ceremonial sites. Xochimilco submitted in 1521; Franciscan evangelization and church building followed.

There is no record of an authorized continuous public Cihuacoatl priesthood surviving colonial rule. Practices and concepts persisted through language, healing, childbirth ritual, oral narrative, place, and reclassification under Christian categories.

### Documentation under suppression, ca. 1530s–1580s

This paradoxical period preserved most of the record. Missionaries sought knowledge to eradicate “idolatry,” while Nahua intellectuals used alphabetic writing and painting to record their past. Sahagún, Durán, the Magliabechiano group, the *Leyenda de los Soles*, and annalists all belong here.

Christian terms such as *demonio*, “devil,” and “false goddess” document missionary judgment, not an Indigenous classification of Cihuacoatl as a demon.

### Tonantzin and Guadalupe, sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

By approximately 1531 a Christian shrine to Mary existed at Tepeyac, and Indigenous worshippers called the figure **Tonantzin**. In 1576 Sahagún complained that worshippers journeyed there from afar and continued using the old maternal title. He associated the preconquest Tonantzin with Cihuacoatl.

What this establishes:

- a preconquest or remembered Indigenous cult at Tepeyac was reported by Sahagún;
- *Tonantzin* was an old Indigenous maternal title;
- Indigenous Christians applied it to Mary at Tepeyac;
- Sahagún feared continuity or confusion.

What it does **not** establish:

- that Tonantzin was only Cihuacoatl;
- that a Cihuacoatl temple has been archaeologically located beneath the basilica;
- that the Spanish deliberately invented Guadalupe as a conscious replacement for Cihuacoatl;
- or that worshippers secretly regarded Mary and Cihuacoatl as literally identical.

The developed apparition literature appears later: Miguel Sánchez’s Spanish *Imagen de la Virgen María* in 1648 and the Nahuatl *Huei tlamahuiçoltica/Nican mopohua* in 1649. The historical development is summarized in the review of Gisela von Wobeser’s research on [the origins of the Guadalupe cult](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S2448-65312024000201869&script=sci_arttext). Guadalupe–Tonantzin is therefore a documented colonial contact zone and possible syncretism; “Guadalupe is Cihuacoatl unchanged” is a modern simplification.

### Colonial and nineteenth-century folklore

The nocturnal crying woman persisted in Mexican narrative, but the name, motives, and plot shifted. By the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries writers such as Vicente Riva Palacio and Luis González Obregón helped canonize La Llorona as a colonial urban ghost. The drowned-children plot acquired many local forms.

The Cihuacoatl connection is historically plausible through shared imagery and later explicit identification, but continuous transmission cannot be demonstrated link by link.

### Archaeological rediscovery, 1790–present

The great Coatlicue sculpture and Sun Stone were unearthed in 1790, prompting antiquarian reassessment of Mexica religion. Cihuacoatl’s identity was repeatedly drawn into disputes over monumental female earth figures.

On 21 February 1978 electrical workers found the Coyolxauhqui monolith, initiating systematic Proyecto Templo Mayor excavation ([INAH project history](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/proyecto-templo-mayor)). The find did not depict Cihuacoatl, but it transformed the archaeological context for state goddess cults and sacrificial reenactment.

On 2 October 2006 excavators found the enormous Tlaltecuhtli monolith near the Templo Mayor ([INAH discovery notice](https://inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/tlaltecuhtli-la-deidad-de-tierra-que-emergio-del-templo-mayor)). Its death-earth iconography sharpened questions about how Cihuacoatl, Tlaltecuhtli, Coatlicue, and related figures differ.

Excavation of the Huei Tzompantli in 2015–2017 materially confirmed the skull-display institution described in colonial sources. Again, this supports the general sacrificial setting rather than assigning the structure to Cihuacoatl.

### Present

There is no reliable census of exclusive Cihuacoatl worshippers. She appears in Mexicayotl and Nahua cultural-revival discourse, Chicano spirituality, feminist ritual, eclectic neopaganism, and online devotional communities. These movements differ sharply: some seek philological reconstruction, some emphasize Indigenous identity, and others adapt her to generalized “goddess spirituality.”

Claims of an unbroken clandestine Cihuacoatl priesthood from 1521 to the present are not documented. Contemporary worship itself is real as modern religious practice; its specific rituals should not be backdated without evidence.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Anthropology, nationalism, and museum display

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Mexican nationalism transformed Mexica deities into emblems of national antiquity. Museum labels sought fixed identities for objects whose original names were absent. The monumental sculpture long called “Coatlicue” has alternatively been read as Cihuacoatl or Tlaltecuhtli; Boone demonstrated how uncertain and historically layered the naming process is.

The National Museum’s placement of Cihuacoatl-related sculpture alongside the Sun Stone, Coatlicue, and Coyolxauhqui encourages viewers to perceive a unified cosmic family. That arrangement is modern museography, not a reconstructed temple grouping.

### La Llorona

Cihuacoatl is routinely called “the Aztec La Llorona.” The equation became especially prominent in folklore studies, school texts, heritage media, theatre, and Chicana writing. Its evidentiary status is:

- **Ancient/early-colonial core:** a maternal female deity cries at night and warns or terrifies people.
- **Colonial and modern accretion:** she murdered or drowned her children.
- **Modern synthesis:** she mourns the conquest itself or is Malintzin grieving for Indigenous Mexico.

Anzaldúa’s 1987 reinterpretation deliberately transforms the maligned mother/serpent into a source of female and mestiza power. It has materially shaped later academic and activist reception.

### Guadalupe

Modern nationalist, feminist, and New Age writing often produces a chain:

**Cihuacoatl = Tonantzin = Coatlicue = Guadalupe = La Llorona.**

Each link has some historical basis, but the full equation is not an ancient doctrine. Tonantzin was a transferable maternal honorific; Sahagún applied it to Cihuacoatl and recorded its use for Mary. Guadalupe developed within sixteenth- and seventeenth-century New Spanish Catholicism. Coatlicue is separate in Sahagún. La Llorona’s infanticide plot is later.

### Neopagan and esoteric practice

Unlike Quetzalcoatl, Cihuacoatl had little role in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, nineteenth-century ceremonial magic, or classic European grimoires. Claims that she possesses an ancient planetary seal, angelic correspondence, tarot attribution, or standardized invocation are modern inventions.

From the late twentieth century onward, practitioners have offered copal, candles, flowers, maize, water, or prayer and invoked her for:

- childbirth and reproductive protection;
- grief and maternal loss;
- Indigenous identity;
- feminine sovereignty;
- land defense;
- ancestor work;
- and militant or protective magic.

Online instructions to “summon” Cihuacoatl through generalized incense, candle, or visualization rituals are eclectic neopagan constructions, not reconstructed Mexica liturgies. Some are sincere modern practices; their antiquity is the disputed point.

### Literature, performance, and political symbolism

Cihuacoatl’s strongest modern literary afterlife is through La Llorona, Tonantzin, and Anzaldúa’s serpent imagery rather than works titled solely after her. She appears in poetry, murals, dance, Indigenous ceremonial performance, fantasy games, comics, and online mythology catalogues.

Feminist interpretations often recover:

- women’s labor as sacred;
- childbirth as battle;
- the silencing or demonization of female divinity after conquest;
- and the wailing mother as a voice of colonial catastrophe.

These are datable interpretive projects. They can illuminate reception without being passed off as sixteenth-century theology.

No major, well-documented extremist organization appears to have made Cihuacoatl its exclusive emblem. Broader Mexica imagery has been used by nationalist movements of several kinds, but a claim of specific Cihuacoatl appropriation requires evidence case by case.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Sahagún and Durán

Sahagún’s great strength is the Nahuatl testimony of named Indigenous collaborators and systematic comparison. His limitations include missionary purpose, Christian categorization, editorial layering, and the several decades between conquest and final manuscript.

Durán preserves material absent from Sahagún, particularly Xochimilco ritual and political narrative. His vivid sacrifices and genealogies cannot automatically be preferred merely because they are detailed.

### Seler and iconographic unification

Eduard Seler’s analyses of the Borgia-group manuscripts established much modern identification of central Mexican gods. His tendency to combine multiple female earth, lunar, death, and fertility figures into comprehensive natural-symbolic systems was enormously influential. Later scholarship has retained his comparisons while questioning overly unitary identities.

### Nicholson’s 1971 typology

Henry B. Nicholson’s “Religion in Pre-Hispanic Central Mexico” organized deities into complexes. Cihuacoatl falls within the Teteo Innan subdivision of the rain/fertility complex. The system remains useful for mapping shared traits but risks turning flexible ritual relationships into a rigid pantheon.

### León-Portilla

Miguel León-Portilla highlighted Nahuatl-language philosophical and poetic material, including the *Leyenda de los Soles* and sacred songs. His work restored Indigenous intellectual agency but has been criticized when “Aztec thought” is presented as more unitary or philosophical in a Western sense than the varied sources permit.

### Graulich

Michel Graulich emphasized calendrical structure, ritual myth, and sacrifice. His reconstructions illuminate correspondences between festivals but sometimes rely on a high degree of structural synthesis and on controversial calendar displacement theories.

### Boone

Elizabeth Hill Boone’s work on painted books treats images as structured religious knowledge rather than imperfect illustrations of European-style prose mythology. Her reinterpretation of the “Coatlicue” monuments is particularly important: the identity of a sculpture must be argued from context and attributes, not inherited from an old museum label.

### Gender-focused scholarship

Cecelia Klein, Susan Gillespie, Kay Read, Susan Kellogg, Louise Burkhart, and Pete Sigal have examined gender, embodiment, sacrifice, and political complementarity. Sigal’s “Imagining Cihuacoatl” stresses that colonial texts and Indigenous painters construct multiple sexual and gendered identities rather than documenting a single transparent goddess ([article record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-0424.2010.01610.x)).

### Major disputes

1. **Snake or twin?** Both meanings of *coatl* are historically relevant. “Snake Woman” fits glyph and image; “Female Twin/Counterpart” clarifies the political office.

2. **One mother goddess or many?** The sources distinguish Cihuacoatl, Coatlicue, Toci, Tlazolteotl, Ilamatecuhtli, and Itzpapalotl while allowing overlaps. A single “Great Goddess” is principally scholarly synthesis.

3. **Was she the Cihuateteo’s queen?** Patronage and analogy are secure; formal command is not explicitly described.

4. **Was she La Llorona?** The wailing-mother motif is a credible ancestor or ingredient. The complete infanticide legend is not attested for her.

5. **Was she Guadalupe?** Sahagún supplies a Cihuacoatl–Tonantzin link and a Tonantzin–Tepeyac link. Literal identity with Guadalupe is a colonial and modern interpretation, not a demonstrated preconquest belief.

6. **Was her cult originally Otomí, Toltec, Xochimilca, or Colhua?** Each proposal reflects a genuine regional association, but no source securely documents one moment of origin.

7. **Which sculptures depict her?** Serpent-body/human-face images are strong candidates. Colossal earth monsters and conquest figures remain contested.

8. **Can post-conquest texts be treated as direct preconquest evidence?** They are indispensable but mediated. Their date must always be stated.

### State of the evidence

The most important surviving materials are held in:

- Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence: *Florentine Codex*;
- Biblioteca Nacional de Antropología e Historia, Mexico City: *Primeros Memoriales* and associated reproductions;
- Biblioteca Nacional de México: *Cantares Mexicanos*;
- Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris: *Codex Borbonicus*;
- Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana: Vatican codices;
- Museo Nacional de Antropología: major sculptures and codices;
- Museo del Templo Mayor: excavated ritual objects and monuments;
- Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence: *Codex Magliabechiano*.

Unknown numbers of images and manuscripts were destroyed during conquest and evangelization. Consequently, apparent absence from surviving preconquest books cannot prove that a myth or cult did not exist.
