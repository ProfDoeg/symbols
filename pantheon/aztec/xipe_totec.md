# Xipe Totec: Research Dossier

**Class:** male *teotl*—a divine power or god—in Late Postclassic Nahua tradition; specifically a deity of warfare and captive sacrifice, agricultural and maize regeneration, goldworking, calendrical divination, and afflictions of the skin and eyes.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest securely contextualized image possessing the diagnostic ensemble later identified as Xipe Tótec is the ceramic figure from Xolalpan, Teotihuacan, found by Sigvald Linné in 1932 and archaeologically dated to about **1000 CE**. A hollow ceramic Xipe from Toltec-period Tula is dated approximately **900–1150 CE**. Earlier “flayed-god” figures—notably an Early Classic Gulf Coast figure dated **100–400 CE**, and proposed Zapotec and West Mexican examples—may belong to the same religious complex, but their identification as the named god Xipe Tótec is retrospective and disputed. The first completely secure occurrences of the **name**, accompanied by indigenous descriptions, are sixteenth-century Nahuatl and Spanish manuscripts produced after the conquest, especially Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex*. [Museo Nacional de Antropología](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/detalle_pieza_mes.php?id=78), [Secretaría de Cultura](https://www.cultura.gob.mx/noticias/patrimonio-cultural-arquitectura-y-turismo/2617-hallan-primer-xipe-totec-en-territorio-tolteca.html), [Museo de América](https://www.cultura.gob.es/museodeamerica/coleccion/america-prehispanica/xipe-totec.html)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** preconquest or early-colonial evidence whose identification is reasonably secure.
- **[LE] Later canonical elaboration:** colonial-era systematization derived from indigenous testimony, pictorial books, or learned synthesis.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** evidence restricted to a particular city, language community, or local cult.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation of Xipe with another divine figure; it records an act of classification, not necessarily original identity.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation assembled from separate sources rather than explicitly stated in one primary source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unverified modern claim:** widespread today but not recoverable from the known primary record.
- **[AE] Archaeological evidence:** material evidence, with interpretation distinguished from the physical find.
- **[Absence]** a proposition for which the consulted corpus supplies no explicit evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

Xipe Tótec is the god manifested in the yellowed or ocher human skin worn by ritual impersonators during *Tlacaxipehualiztli*, “the Flaying of People,” the second twenty-day festival of the Mexica ceremonial year. His cult joined several fields that modern summaries too often separate: the capture and public killing of enemy warriors; the renewal of maize and other vegetation; the presentation of seed for blessing; the authority and terror of imperial war; gold and goldworkers; calendrical destinies; and diseases expressed through the body’s surface. His characteristic impersonator wears a victim’s flayed skin, a pointed or tasselled *yopitzontli* headdress, red-and-white costume elements, and gold ornaments, and carries a shield and the rattle-staff called *chicahuaztli*. [EA/LE]

He was important to the Mexica but was not exclusively “Aztec.” Images and related rites occur in Toltec-period central Mexico, the Gulf Coast, Oaxaca, western Mexico, Puebla–Tlaxcala, Guerrero, and farther south. Whether all earlier or non-Nahua images represent the same named being is precisely the historical problem: a flayed-skin ritual complex may predate the Nahuatl name and may have been repeatedly translated into local divine taxonomies. [AE/MSR]

### Names

- **Xīpe Tōtec / Xipe Totec / Xipetotec.** Classical Nahuatl spelling varies because colonial manuscripts did not consistently mark vowel length or word division. *Tōtec* means “our lord,” from *to-* (“our”) plus *tēcuhtli/tec-* (“lord”). *Xīpe* is associated with *xīpehua*, whose colonial lexical range includes “to flay,” “peel,” “shell,” remove bark, or strip a covering. “Our Lord the Flayed One” is therefore serviceable, but “Our Lord, the One Associated with Peeling/Flaying” better preserves the grammatical and semantic uncertainty. The diminutive-sounding Spanish “el desolladito” appears in some scholarship but should not be mistaken for a universally accepted literal translation. [LE; etymology disputed] [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/xipe/76173)

- **Tōtec**, “Our Lord.” A shortened cult name. Captives killed and flayed for the festival were called *xipeme*; Sahagún also gives *tototecti*, persons killed in honor of Tōtec. [LE]

- **Yohuallāhuān / Yoalli Tlauana / Yoallahuana**, conventionally “Night Drinker” or “He Who Drinks by Night.” This appears in the Nahuatl hymn headed *Xippe ycuic, Totec (Yoallavana)*, preserved in the Sahaguntine corpus. The hymn’s language was already difficult for sixteenth-century commentators and its exact imagery remains disputed. It addresses rain, precious water, maize, war, and Xipe’s “golden garment.” [EA as recorded hymn; interpretation disputed] [UNAM hymn corpus](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/cantos_religiosos/antiguos_mexicanos.html), [Barlow’s textual study](https://revistas-filologicas.unam.mx/tlalocan/index.php/tl/article/download/324/322/0)

- **Tlatlauhqui Tezcatlipoca**, “Red Tezcatlipoca,” and **Tlatlauhqui Tezcatl**, “Red Mirror.” Durán and related colonial classifications identify Xipe in this way. The equation belongs to a learned scheme of color-directional Tezcatlipocas and cannot simply be projected backward onto every representation of Xipe. [LE/SI]

- **Anaoatl iteouh**, approximately “god of Anáhuatl/the waterside region,” appears in the Sahaguntine description. Interpretation of *Anáhuatl* is geographical and uncertain. [LE]

- **Yopi iteouh**, “god of the Yopi,” and the associated architectural name **Yopico/Yopicalco**, “at/in the house of the Yopi.” Sahagún identifies him as patron of the Yopi or Tlapanec-associated population of Yopitzinco. The repeated *Yopi-* vocabulary supports a remembered connection with Guerrero’s Pacific slope, although it does not by itself prove that the god originated there. [LE/RV/MSR]

- **Xīhuitl Cozauhqui**, “Yellow(-green) Turquoise/Year” or “Yellow *xihuitl*,” reported for the Tamazula–Zapotlán region in colonial geographical testimony. Because *xihuitl* can mean turquoise, vegetation, comet, or year depending on context, translations vary. [RV/LE]

- **Camaxtli/Camaxtle.** Tlaxcalan and Huexotzinco sources connect Camaxtli with Red Tezcatlipoca, Mixcoatl, and sometimes Xipe. These figures are not interchangeable in all sources. “Xipe was simply Camaxtli under another name” is therefore a syncretistic or scholarly equation, not a universal indigenous identity. [RV/SI]

- **Ehuacueye**, a western Mexican divine name invoked in modern origin theories, may identify a local skin-wearing or flaying figure. The proposed equivalence with Xipe is reconstructed, not explicitly stated in preconquest writing. [RV/MSR]

### Classifications and domains

Colonial Nahua testimony classifies Xipe as a *teotl*, not as a demon or monster. Spanish friars used *dios*, “god,” while condemning indigenous worship as idolatry. Modern labels such as “life–death–rebirth god,” “god of spring,” and “earth god” are interpretive summaries. “God of spring” is especially convenient but potentially misleading: central Mexican ceremonial time was structured by twenty-day periods, agricultural conditions, and movable correlations with the Julian calendar, not by a European four-season theology. [LE/MSR]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The early material record

1. **Gulf Coast ceramic figure, 100–400 CE.** The Museo de América catalogs a standing male figure in a full-body covering as Xipe Tótec. It has closed eyes, an open mouth, prominent genitals, crossed cords, ear and nose ornaments, and objects in its hands. The physical object is Early Classic; the divine identification is modern and iconographic. It establishes an early skin-suit image, not securely the Nahuatl name or every later Mexica function. [AE; identity disputed]

2. **Proposed Zapotec, West Mexican, and Maya-region antecedents.** Figurines and urns from Oaxaca, shaft-tomb western Mexico, and Classic-period southern sites have been read as flayed gods or skin-wearing ritual figures. Some show mouth-and-eye apertures or a second integument; others lack Xipe’s later diagnostic assemblage. They prove widespread Mesoamerican interest in altered or worn skins more readily than they prove worship of one transregional god. [AE/MSR]

3. **Xolalpan figure, about 1000 CE.** Found in Room XXVI of a residential compound at Xolalpan, Teotihuacan, but associated with post-Teotihuacan Mazapan-period occupation. It wears a human skin and *yopitzontli*, has the characteristic costume, and is the strongest early identification. It must not be cited as proof that Classic Teotihuacan worshipped Xipe: the deposit is centuries later than the city’s principal Classic florescence. [AE/EA]

4. **Tula figure, 900–1150 CE.** A hollow ceramic figure, 85 cm high, came from a rescue excavation near Tula with burials and offerings. Its costume supports identification as Xipe and establishes the cultic image in a Toltec-period context. [AE/EA]

5. **Ndachjian–Tehuacán temple, approximately 1000–1260 CE.** Excavation revealed a temple complex attributed to the Popoloca/Popoloca-speaking polity, with two stone heads displaying flayed-skin facial traits, a stone torso, and sacrificial installations. Announced publicly in January 2019 as the first archaeologically identified temple devoted specifically to Xipe Tótec, it materially confirms a pre-Mexica Puebla cult. “Found in 2019” is imprecise: the excavations and discoveries preceded the announcement. [AE/EA]

### Primary manuscripts

#### Sahaguntine corpus

- **Primeros Memoriales**, compiled principally at Tepepulco around **1558–1561**, Nahuatl with pictorial material. This is the earlier stage of Sahagún’s ethnographic project. It contains deity costumes, ritual calendars, and sacred hymns gathered from Nahua elders and specialists. It is postconquest testimony, but among the closest surviving textual witnesses to preconquest ritual knowledge. [EA as testimony]

- **Florentine Codex**, compiled through several stages and completed around **1575–1577**, Nahuatl and Spanish in parallel columns with indigenous paintings. Book 1 describes Xipe and his regalia; Book 2 gives the festival calendar and a sustained account of *Tlacaxipehualiztli*; Book 9 describes goldworkers and their relation to Tōtec. The two columns are not simple translations of one another: the Nahuatl testimony and Sahagún’s Spanish presentation sometimes differ. Anderson and Dibble’s English edition remains fundamental; the Getty digital edition supplies manuscript images, transcriptions, and newer translations. [LE based substantially on indigenous testimony] [Digital Florentine Codex, Book 2](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/book/2/folio/18r), [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_10613/?sp=81)

Book 2 says that on the principal day captives—men, women, and children in the Spanish column—were taken to the temple, killed, and flayed; hence *tlacaxipehualiztli*, glossed “flaying of people.” It calls the dead *xipeme* and *tototecti*. Subsequent chapters describe owners of captives, ritual eating, skin-wearing impersonators, begging circuits, the gladiatorial combat, and the eventual deposition of skins. [LE]

Book 9 identifies Tōtec as a patron of goldworkers. Gold was worked into divine ornaments, including Xipe’s crescent nose-piece and ear ornaments. The link is not merely a modern metaphor about gold as a “skin”: it is institutionally attested in the artisan account, although later symbolism built upon it. [LE] [Digital Florentine Codex, Book 9](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/en/book/9/folio/1r)

- **The twenty sacred hymns**, preserved as an appendix in the Sahaguntine tradition. The Xipe hymn uses archaic and obscure Nahuatl. Its lines invoke the god’s precious garment, rain, water, tender maize, and a first or leading warrior. Robert H. Barlow emphasized that even the sixteenth-century glossator struggled with the text. Claims that every line has a settled agricultural meaning are therefore unwarranted. [EA/LE; philologically contested]

#### Durán

- **Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Libro de los dioses y ritos***, composed approximately **1574–1581**, Spanish. Durán describes Xipe under the compound naming “Tōtec, Xipe, Tlatlauhqui Tezcatl,” the Yopico temple, the *temalacatl* sacrifice, skin-wearing, public circuits, offerings, and royal-political uses of the festival. His account overlaps Sahagún but also contains independent details and theological systematization. [LE]

Durán relates state performances in which conquered or invited rulers witnessed mass sacrifice, making the rite an instrument of intimidation and imperial display. Such numbers and speeches require criticism; the political function is nevertheless supported by ritual architecture, conquest imagery, and several colonial narratives. [LE/MSR]

#### Pictorial manuscripts

- **Codex Borgia**, preconquest or conquest-era ritual-divinatory screenfold, probably Puebla–Tlaxcala or adjoining central Mexico. Xipe-like beings appear in trecena sequences, day-sign patronage, the Nine Lords of the Night sequence, sky-bearer scenes, and scenes of sacrifice and transformation. Plate 49 is often connected with the gladiatorial-sacrifice complex. The manuscript carries no explanatory prose, so identification depends on recurring visual attributes and comparison with named colonial codices. [EA; individual scene readings debated]

- **Codex Vaticanus B, Codex Cospi, Codex Fejérváry-Mayer, and Codex Laud**, Borgia-group divinatory books. Parallel passages reveal that Xipe is not a single invariant portrait: attributes can be distributed among composite divine figures. Katarzyna Mikulska’s “deity as mosaic” approach cautions against treating every attribute as a permanent personal possession. [EA/MSR] [Mikulska study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/deity-as-a-mosaic-images-of-the-god-xipe-totec-in-divinatory-codices-from-central-mesoamerica/62D55E88E87CC87E3C1B711EF8F84388)

- **Codex Borbonicus**, probably produced around the conquest, with preconquest-style imagery and later glosses. Its *Tlacaxipehualiztli* page depicts skin-wearing participants and the festival’s public/domestic dimension. It also places the deity in the divinatory count. Whether the manuscript is strictly preconquest remains disputed. [EA/early colonial]

- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis**, mid-sixteenth century, pictorial Nahua manuscript with Spanish and Italian annotations. Its calendar and deity imagery are valuable but already filtered through European-format annotation. [LE]

- **Codex Magliabechiano** and related **Tudela-group** manuscripts, mid-to-late sixteenth century. They depict or describe Xipe’s feast, flayed skins, and ritual costume. Their textual and pictorial dependencies must be considered; multiple copies are not necessarily independent witnesses. [LE]

- **Codex Mendoza**, commissioned about **1541–1542**. It is crucial for tribute, conquest, costume, and imperial institutions, but is not a full narrative source for Xipe. It supplies context for warrior capture, tributary wealth, and the political economy within which the festival operated. [LE/contextual]

#### Annals and prose mythography

- **Historia de los Mexicanos por sus pinturas**, about the 1530s, Spanish summary of indigenous pictorial-historical material. Its four sons of the primordial couple were later mapped onto four colored Tezcatlipocas. Assigning Xipe unequivocally as the red brother is a colonial learned harmonization and is unstable across editions and interpretations. [LE/SI]

- **Histoyre du Mechique**, French manuscript translating or epitomizing lost Spanish material, mid-sixteenth century. It preserves central Mexican cosmogonic traditions but offers no extensive independent Xipe biography. [LE/Absence]

- **Leyenda de los Soles**, Nahuatl, recorded **1558**, and the **Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, Nahuatl annals copied in the sixteenth century, together forming the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. They are primary witnesses to the suns, Quetzalcoatl, Tollan, and migration history. Xipe is not a principal named actor in their major surviving narratives. Later reconstructions that insert him into the creation of the Fifth Sun rely on ritual and iconographic comparison, not an explicit full episode in these texts. [EA as colonial Nahuatl texts; Absence]

- **Cantares Mexicanos**, manuscript chiefly compiled in the later sixteenth century. It contains Nahuatl songs with preconquest forms, colonial performances, Christian interventions, and historically layered diction. It should not be confused with the separate Sahaguntine collection of twenty divine hymns, though both preserve sacred-poetic language. No secure, self-contained Xipe narrative cycle comparable to the Quetzalcoatl traditions emerges from it. [LE/Absence]

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Warfare and captives

Xipe’s best-attested public domain is war transformed into sacrificial performance. Owners presented captured enemies; the captive’s death generated prestige for the captor and nourishment for gods, kin, and political community. The *tlahuahuanaliztli* or “striping/scratching” sacrifice staged an unequal combat on a circular stone, the *temalacatl*. A captive was tied by a cord and given inadequate or mock weapons; successive well-armed eagle and jaguar warriors fought him. Exceptional captives could wound opponents, but the institution was designed to end in the captive’s defeat and sacrifice. [LE, corroborated by imagery and sacrificial stones]

The ceremony commemorated victories, displayed elite martial ranks, distributed captive bodies, and made subordinated peoples witness Mexica power. It cannot be reduced to either “religion” or “propaganda”; the sources do not separate those fields. [LE/MSR]

### Agriculture, rain, and regeneration

The Xipe hymn’s tender maize, water, and rain imagery; the timing of the veintena near the dry-to-rainy agricultural transition; offerings of selected seed; and the god’s rattling staff all support an agricultural domain. The sloughing of skin has often been compared with vegetation emerging from a dry covering or a seed shedding its coat. That metaphor is a plausible interpretation, not a surviving indigenous doctrinal statement in the form frequently quoted today. [EA/LE for agricultural association; MSR for the precise skin-equals-seed equation]

Calling Xipe simply “the god of spring” translates the festival into a European seasonal category. “God of agricultural renewal at the dry-season/rainy-season threshold” is historically safer. [MSR]

### Goldworkers

Book 9 of the *Florentine Codex* makes Tōtec patron of goldworkers. His ornaments include the *yacametztli*, a crescent-shaped gold nose ornament, gold ear plugs, and the *teocuitlaquemitl*, “golden/divine-metal garment.” Gold’s yellow surface and capacity to cover objects encouraged associations with skin and renewal, but the occupational patronage is primary evidence; the extended allegory is interpretive. [LE/MSR]

### Disease and healing

Sahaguntine testimony associates Xipe with pustules, eruptions, inflammations, and eye complaints. Persons afflicted made vows or offerings and participated in cult observances seeking relief. This is a reciprocal divine domain: a god could send or control an affliction and therefore cure it. Claims that Xipe was specifically a “smallpox god” are anachronistic unless restricted to postcontact reinterpretation; smallpox entered Mexico with Europeans, while the indigenous category embraced a wider range of skin and eye disorders. [LE; “smallpox god” rejected as generalized ancient title]

### Diagnostic iconography

The most dependable features, not all present in every image, are:

- a second, loose human skin over the body;
- crescent eye openings and a doubled or open mouth where the wearer’s living face appears through a dead face;
- hands of the flayed skin hanging slack at the wrists;
- incised seams, knots, or lacing;
- yellow, ocher, or golden surface;
- red body visible beneath the skin;
- the pointed or tasselled **yopitzontli** headdress;
- forked or bifurcated red-and-white streamers;
- a ruff or collar;
- gold nose and ear ornaments;
- **chicahuaztli**, a hollow rattle-staff often understood as sounding or calling fertility;
- shield and warrior costume;
- **zapote skirt** or leaf-like waist element in some sculptures.

Images are divine embodiments, not merely “pictures of a costume.” An *ixiptla*—a material or living embodiment—became the god in ritual terms. Accordingly, the flayed captive’s skin, its wearer, and a ceramic or stone sculpture can all present Xipe without implying the modern distinction between actor and represented character. [EA/LE]

### Calendrical positions

Borgia-group codices place Xipe-like figures in almanacs, among trecena patrons, in day-sign sequences, and in the series now called the Nine Lords of the Night. Different manuscripts assign or combine attributes differently. Confident Internet tables giving Xipe one immutable cardinal direction, day, planet, or number often collapse several manuscripts and modern reconstructions into a false canonical chart. [EA/MSR]

### Sacred animals and substances

Quail, eagle-warrior and jaguar-warrior imagery, maize, precious water, gold, rubber, paper, copal, blood, and human skin occur around the cult. Not all are Xipe’s exclusive “sacred animal” or substance. The quail was widely sacrificed in central Mexican ritual; eagle and jaguar refer especially to military orders and cosmic force. [LE; exclusivity unestablished]

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

Xipe has no securely attested, independent birth narrative and no stable consort or offspring in the principal primary corpus. [Absence]

The often-reproduced genealogy making him a son of the dual creator pair **Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl**, and brother of **Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, and Huitzilopochtli**, arises from combining a four-sons cosmogony with the colored-Tezcatlipoca system. The underlying colonial sources do not uniformly name Xipe as one of those sons. It is therefore a **later systematization**, not a securely pan-Nahua family tree. [LE/SI]

Similarly, the statement that **Xilonen–Chicomecoatl was Xipe’s “female equivalent”** is a modern taxonomic shorthand. Both are involved in maize renewal, but no consulted primary source establishes marriage, siblinghood, or a male/female pair. [MSR/Absence]

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 The supposed self-flaying of Xipe

A now-common story says that Xipe flayed himself to feed humanity, just as maize loses its husk or a snake sheds its skin. It is repeated by museums, encyclopedias, educational websites, and modern devotional pages. Yet the principal sixteenth-century sources describe **humans being killed and flayed in his honor** and impersonators wearing their skins; they do not, in the material examined, tell a narrative in which Xipe removes his own skin and gives food to humanity.

Accordingly:

- ritual flaying and Xipe’s skin garment: **[EA/LE]**;
- agricultural interpretation of the skin: **[MSR, plausible]**;
- a discrete primordial story in which Xipe voluntarily flays himself for humankind: **[MI/unverified modern explanatory myth]**.

Unless a specific Nahuatl passage or early pictorial sequence can be identified, the self-flaying narrative should not be presented as an “Aztec creation myth.”

### 5.2 The Fifth Sun

Some modern scholarship interprets *Tlacaxipehualiztli* as commemorating or ritually renewing the creation of the Fifth Sun. The gladiatorial stone could act as a solar disk; the captive’s combat and death fed the Sun; and conquest imagery linked ruler, warrior, sacrifice, and cosmic motion. [MSR grounded in LE/AE]

However, the principal surviving Fifth Sun narrative centers on Nanahuatzin and Tecuciztecatl at Teotihuacan and the subsequent sacrifice of the gods. It does not give Xipe a named speaking role. Therefore “Xipe helped Quetzalcoatl create the Fifth Sun” is not an early narrative fact unless carefully attributed to a modern reconstruction. [Absence]

### 5.3 The war-and-maize complex

Carlos Javier González González reconstructs a large Xipe complex connecting war, the death of captives, seed maize, seasonal regeneration, gold, rulership, and the Fifth Sun. This is better described as a **ritual-mythic structure** than a lost biography. The evidence consists of:

1. warfare and captive-taking;
2. gladiatorial and heart sacrifice;
3. flaying and embodiment of the deity;
4. blessing or presentation of seed;
5. rain and maize imagery in the hymn;
6. solar and conquest imagery on *temalacatl* stones;
7. elite political display.

The reconstruction is strong where these components are independently attested; it becomes more conjectural when arranged into a single lost myth. [MSR] [Matos Moctezuma’s review](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0071-16752012000200012&script=sci_arttext)

### 5.4 Yopi origin tradition

Sahagún states that the god was honored by peoples near the sea and locates an origin at Tzapotlán in Xalisco in one strand of testimony; other passages emphasize the Yopi and Yopitzinco. Modern scholars have consequently proposed Pacific-coast origins in Guerrero, Jalisco, or a broader western corridor.

These are competing traditions:

- **Yopi/Guerrero hypothesis:** supported by *Yopi-* cult vocabulary and Nahua memories of Yopitzinco. [LE/MSR]
- **Jalisco–Zapotlán hypothesis:** supported by Sahaguntine origin testimony, local names such as Xīhuitl Cozauhqui, and western material. [RV/LE/MSR]
- **Gulf Coast hypothesis:** supported by early skin-suit figures and strong later Gulf Coast presence. [AE/MSR]
- **Teotihuacan/Olmec antiquity hypothesis:** older scholarship pushed Xipe’s identity back into Classic Teotihuacan or even Olmec art. Diagnostic and contextual problems make these identifications insecure. [Disputed/discredited in strong form]

The defensible conclusion is that the **flaying/skin-wearing complex is old and geographically broad**, while the origin of the fully constituted, named Xipe Tótec remains unresolved. [MSR] [Jalisco-origin argument](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S2448-83722022000200109&script=sci_arttext)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Tlacaxipehualiztli

*Tlācaxīpēhualiztli* means approximately “the flaying of people.” It was the second veintena in Sahagún’s sequence. Fixed conversions such as “March 21–April 9” should be treated cautiously because colonial authors correlated indigenous calendars differently and because preconquest intercalation remains debated. Its ecological setting was the late dry season and approach to planting/rain. [LE]

Preparations included acquiring and dedicating captives, fasting, dancing, vigil, bloodletting, ritual hair-cutting, and public procession. On the principal day victims were taken up the temple stairs, stretched over a sacrificial stone, killed by heart extraction, and flayed. The Spanish column of the *Florentine Codex* includes male, female, and child captives; other passages center on adult war captives. The sources need not describe one homogeneous event or identical victim pool every year. [LE]

The owners of captives participated in ceremonies of prestige and mourning. Portions of victims could be distributed for ritual consumption, while the captor reportedly did not eat his own captive, whom he addressed in a kin-like idiom. This was restricted ceremonial anthropophagy embedded in sacrifice, not evidence that ordinary Mexica diet depended on human flesh. [LE]

### Wearing the skins

Priests or other designated men put on the removed skins and became *xipeme*, embodiments of Xipe. They wore them during a circuit of households and neighborhoods, blessing or threatening householders and receiving offerings. Codex Borbonicus suggests that this was not confined to monumental state space but entered streets and domestic economies. [EA/LE]

The skins were reportedly worn for **twenty days**, through the following ritual period, although individual accounts differ over timing and participants. Decomposition would have been materially present; colonial descriptions stress odor and bodily discomfort. At the end, the skins were deposited in containers or chambers at Yopico. The twenty-day duration is textually attested, not merely sensational folklore. [LE]

### Gladiatorial sacrifice

The *tlahuahuanaliztli* was conducted at the **Yopico/Ehuacalco** complex on a circular *temalacatl*. The captive, tied by waist or ankle, carried mock arms—accounts mention feather-edged or otherwise ineffective weapons—against properly equipped warriors. After defeat, priests extracted the heart. In some accounts an especially renowned captive who survived several opponents was ultimately dispatched by an additional fighter. [LE]

The so-called Stone of Tizoc and related monuments combine a solar upper surface with scenes of conquest around the side. Their exact original use remains debated: identification as *temalacatl* is persuasive but not proven merely by their circular form. [AE/MSR] [Museo Nacional de Antropología](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/detalle_pieza_mes.php?id=241)

### Arrow sacrifice

Some sources and images describe a victim fastened to a frame and shot with arrows or darts, with blood falling toward the earth. Scholars connect this *tlacacaliztli* with rain and agricultural fertility. Not every arrow-sacrifice image is necessarily Xipe, and not every *Tlacaxipehualiztli* account includes it. [LE/RV/MSR]

### Priests and offices

The *Florentine Codex* names a senior office **Totec Tlamacazqui**, “priest/ministry-holder of Tōtec.” The Yopico institution possessed personnel, buildings, skins, regalia, and responsibility for festival performance. Goldworkers maintained their own patronal observances. Evidence is insufficient for a complete organizational chart or hereditary recruitment rule particular to Xipe alone. [LE]

### Offerings and ordinary participation

Attested offerings include copal, rubber, paper, food, *octli*, textiles, precious ornaments, quail, blood from autosacrifice, seeds, and captives. Householders gave alms to skin-wearing impersonators. Persons with skin or eye ailments vowed offerings. Agricultural implements and seed could receive ceremonial attention during the wider seasonal cycle. [LE]

### Political economy

Captives were products of warfare; costume and gold depended on tribute and specialist craft; feasts redistributed food and human remains; rulers displayed conquests; and invited dignitaries witnessed the state’s capacity to capture and sacrifice enemies. The cult consequently joined household devotion, artisan identity, military promotion, tribute, and imperial intimidation. [LE/MSR]

### Temples and cult centers

- **Yopico, Tenochtitlan:** a temple or cult complex in the sacred precinct or an associated calpulli zone, named for the Yopi. Colonial descriptions place the *temalacatl* and skin rites there. Its exact archaeological footprint remains debated beneath colonial and modern Mexico City. [LE/AE uncertain]

- **Ndachjian–Tehuacán, Puebla:** pre-Mexica temple, approximately 1000–1260 CE, with two circular altars, stone flayed-skin heads, and a torso. This is the strongest material identification of a purpose-built Xipe sanctuary. [AE]

- **Tula:** Toltec-period sculpture and associated contexts attest the image, but do not yet establish a fully excavated Xipe temple equivalent to Ndachjian. [AE]

- **Xolalpan/Teotihuacan:** domestic-context image dated around 1000 CE; not a Classic-period temple. [AE]

- **Castillo de Teayo and Gulf Coast centers:** numerous stone sculptures demonstrate strong Postclassic reception. Individual building dedications are less secure than the sculptural evidence. [AE]

- **Oaxaca, western Mexico, Maya-region sites, El Salvador:** skin-wearing figures and Xipe-like heads document broad circulation. At Mayapan, for example, censer heads resemble Xipe, but wearing flayed skins was not unique to him; identification remains comparative. [AE/RV]

### Human sacrifice and archaeology

The historical reality of human sacrifice is supported independently by colonial indigenous testimony, Spanish accounts, pictorial manuscripts, sacrificial architecture, cut marks, heart-extraction-related treatment, decapitation, perforated skulls, and the Huei Tzompantli. The scale attributed to particular ceremonies remains debatable, and colonial rhetoric sometimes magnified numbers. Denying the practice wholesale is incompatible with the combined evidence; accepting every conquest-era number literally is equally unsound. [EA/AE]

The **2015–2017 Huei Tzompantli discoveries** at 24 República de Guatemala revealed part of Tenochtitlan’s great skull-rack platform and skull tower; continuing research includes a sample of 214 crania. This is direct evidence for large-scale sacrificial display, but it is not specifically an excavation of Xipe’s Yopico or proof that each individual died during *Tlacaxipehualiztli*. [AE] [INAH Huei Tzompantli](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907), [INAH research update](https://inah.gob.mx/boletines/a-10-anos-del-hallazgo-del-huei-tzompantli-de-tenochtitlan-los-craneos-humanos-son-objeto-de-analisis-de-vanguardia?highlight=WyJtdXNlbyIsInRlbXBsbyJd)

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 900 CE: antecedent complexes

Skin-wearing, flaying, altered-body imagery, and arrow sacrifice occur in several Classic-period regions. The Early Classic Gulf Coast figure is the clearest early candidate, but no readable inscription names Xipe. Scholars should speak of a “Xipe-like” or flayed-god complex rather than assume uninterrupted identity. [AE/MSR]

### About 900–1200: secure iconographic consolidation

Tula and post-Classic occupation at Xolalpan supply figures with the later diagnostic costume. Ndachjian provides a temple of approximately 1000–1260. This is the earliest period in which the iconographic identity is secure enough to call the figure Xipe, even though the surviving name is later. [EA/AE]

### 1200–1428: regional dissemination

The cult circulated through Puebla–Tlaxcala, the Basin of Mexico, Gulf Coast, Oaxaca, Guerrero, western Mexico, and trade or political networks reaching farther south. Divinatory manuscripts encode the deity in calendrical systems. Local variants cannot be reconstructed as branches of one centralized church. [EA/RV/MSR]

### 1428–1521: Mexica imperial incorporation

Under the Triple Alliance, Xipe’s rites were absorbed into an increasingly monumental state calendar. Conquest supplied captives; the festival displayed warrior ranks and tributary submission; the goldworking guild maintained patronal relations; and Yopico formed part of Tenochtitlan’s sacred geography. Colonial histories associate major performances with rulers such as Motecuhzoma Ilhuicamina, Axayacatl, Tizoc, Ahuitzotl, and Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin, though precise victim totals are uncertain. [LE]

The Mexica did not necessarily “invent” or first import Xipe after one specific conquest. Archaeology shows that the cult long predated the empire. Mexica power reorganized and publicized an older, transregional complex. [AE/MSR]

### 1519–1570s: conquest, suppression, and documentation

Spanish-led forces and Indigenous allies destroyed Tenochtitlan in 1521. Temples were dismantled, images broken or buried, priestly institutions prohibited, and Christian churches and the colonial city occupied the ceremonial landscape. Missionaries prosecuted sacrifice and “idolatry.” This was coercive religious suppression, intertwined with epidemic mortality, dispossession, and colonial rule. [historically attested]

At the same time, missionaries’ need to understand indigenous religion generated the manuscripts that preserve most named information about Xipe. Nahua elders, scribes, grammarians, and painters were active knowledge producers, not merely passive informants. Their testimony was nevertheless elicited under colonial conditions and shaped by Christian categories. [LE]

No evidence supports continued public sacrifice to Xipe after Spanish rule was consolidated. Elements of agricultural calendrics, dance, curing, costume, and local sacred geography could survive or be transformed, but demonstrating a continuous practice as specifically “Xipe worship” requires local evidence. [Absence/caution]

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: antiquarian afterlife

Xipe persisted mainly in manuscripts, collections, isolated sculptures, colonial polemic, and antiquarian histories. European and Mexican scholars progressively compared codices and archaeological objects. Earlier interpretations commonly portrayed him as a macabre “sacrifice god,” isolating violence from agriculture, craft, and political economy. [modern historiography]

### Twentieth century: archaeological and iconographic reconstruction

- Sigvald Linné’s 1932 Xolalpan excavation gave a securely contextualized early figure.
- Eduard Seler’s comparative work on the Borgia group shaped identifications, although later scholars revised his astronomical and mythological readings.
- Alfonso Caso, Ángel María Garibay, Miguel León-Portilla, Henry B. Nicholson, Michel Graulich, Elizabeth Hill Boone, Alfredo López Austin, Leonardo López Luján, Eduardo Matos Moctezuma, and others situated Xipe within broader calendrical, philosophical, iconographic, and political systems.
- Nicholson’s 1971 typology placed Xipe within a “war–sacrifice–sanguinary nourishment” complex. Typologies remain analytical tools, not indigenous creeds.
- The 1978 discovery of the **Coyolxauhqui Stone** initiated systematic Proyecto Templo Mayor excavation. The stone concerns Coyolxauhqui, not Xipe, but transformed the archaeological basis for studying the ceremonial precinct.
- The 2006 discovery of the **Tlaltecuhtli monolith** likewise concerns a different deity but expanded knowledge of late imperial sculpture, pigments, offerings, and royal ritual.
- The **Sun Stone** is not a portrait of Xipe. Its solar-era and sacrificial imagery supplies comparative context; using it as direct evidence for Xipe requires argument.

[Proyecto Templo Mayor](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/proyecto-templo-mayor)

### Twenty-first century

The Tula discovery, renewed analysis of codices, González González’s major synthesis, the Huei Tzompantli excavations, and the Ndachjian temple have shifted discussion from a narrowly “Aztec” god to a much older and geographically broader ritual complex. Digital publication of the *Florentine Codex* now allows line-by-line comparison of Nahuatl, Spanish, and images. [modern scholarship]

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship, museums, and national heritage

Xipe’s doubled face and loose skin have made him one of the most immediately recognizable Mesoamerican gods. Museum display often presents him as an agricultural “death and rebirth” symbol. That interpretation captures part of the evidence but may soften the equally central institutions of captive-taking, killing, bodily processing, and imperial spectacle. Conversely, horror-oriented displays can erase maize, rain, craft patronage, curing, and calendrical divination. [modern reception]

Mexican archaeological institutions present Xipe as both national patrimony and evidence of regional diversity. The Ndachjian temple is particularly important because it belongs to a Popoloca polity preceding Mexica conquest, complicating nationalist narratives that equate all central Mexican religion with the “Aztecs.” [modern reception]

### Occult and esoteric reception

Early twentieth-century popular occultists and comparative mythographers, including Lewis Spence, incorporated Mexican gods into generalized systems of “magic and mystery.” Their treatments often relied on outdated evolutionism and treated Xipe chiefly as a grim sacrificial or vegetation god. No evidence places Xipe within Renaissance Hermeticism, the historical Golden Dawn curriculum, or pre-twentieth-century European grimoire traditions. Claims of an ancient “Xipe grimoire” or continuous European occult lineage are modern inventions. [MI]

Contemporary chaos-magical and eclectic occult practitioners occasionally invoke Xipe as an archetype of shedding identity, painful transformation, bodily renewal, or sacrifice. These are modern appropriations, not transmitted Mexica liturgies. [modern invention/reinterpretation]

### Modern Indigenous and reconstructionist practice

There are contemporary Nahua cultural revivals and broader *Mexicayotl* or reconstructionist movements that honor preconquest deities. Publicly available evidence does not permit a reliable estimate of how many practitioners specifically worship Xipe, nor does it demonstrate uninterrupted institutional descent from Yopico’s priesthood. Modern rituals may be sincere living religion while also being reconstructed from colonial texts and contemporary cultural politics. [modern revival; scale unestablished]

### Psychology and “archetype”

Jungian and New Age readings turn the flayed skin into an archetype of ego-shedding, trauma, rebirth, or the “shadow.” These interpretations are twentieth- or twenty-first-century psychological allegories. They are not translations of an attested Nahua doctrine. [MI as ancient claim; modern interpretive tradition]

### Popular culture

Xipe appears or is invoked in horror fiction, fantasy, role-playing and video games, comics, metal music, online art, and fictional secret cults. These portrayals favor body horror, self-flaying, resurrection, or serial-killer imagery. They materially reinforce the invented story that the god personally flayed himself and obscure the political and corporate organization of the historical rites. Exact fictional portrayals should be evaluated work by work; a name or visual borrowing does not transmit ancient belief. [modern reception]

### Political appropriation

Modern Mexican and Chicano imagery sometimes uses preconquest gods as symbols of Indigenous resistance, national identity, decolonization, or ethnic pride. Xipe is less politically dominant than Quetzalcoatl, Huitzilopochtli, or Coatlicue but can symbolize renewal through the shedding of colonial identity. No single political interpretation is traditional or universally accepted. [modern reception]

No well-documented, durable extremist movement centered specifically on Xipe was identified in the consulted evidence. General claims that modern devotees constitute a violent sacrificial cult are sensational fiction unless supported by case-specific documentation. [Absence]

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The identity problem

The central methodological problem is whether a figure wearing another skin is:

1. Xipe Tótec himself;
2. a human impersonator of Xipe;
3. a different deity or victim employing similar ritual technology;
4. a generic flayed ancestor or captive;
5. a local being later translated as Xipe.

Older iconography frequently answered “Xipe” from a single facial feature. Current work favors bundles of attributes, parallel manuscript scenes, archaeological context, and acknowledgment that divine identities could be composite. [historiographic development]

### Etymology

The relation of *Xīpe* to *xīpehua* is strong, but the exact grammatical force and vowel length have been debated. The verb’s broad “peel/strip/shell/flay” range matters: it links violence with vegetal processing without proving that the name originally meant a complete agricultural doctrine. Garibay’s proposed phallic etymology, sometimes paraphrased as “the one who has a virile member,” has not displaced the flaying/peeling derivation and is not consensus. [contested]

### Origin

Four principal origin models remain in play:

- ancient pan-Mesoamerican flayed god;
- Pacific-coast/Yopi origin;
- western Mexican/Jalisco–Zapotlán origin;
- Gulf Coast origin or early center of development.

The evidence may reflect a network rather than one birthplace. Identifying an early skin-suit image does not determine where the later name, hymn, goldworking patronage, or gladiatorial rite developed. [debate unresolved]

### “Vegetation god” versus war god

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers often made Xipe a straightforward vegetation god whose skin represented the earth’s coat. Later scholarship emphasized that the primary rite involved captured warriors, military ranks, conquest monuments, political intimidation, and solar nourishment. González González’s formulation—war and maize regeneration as one structure—has been influential precisely because it avoids choosing only one. [scholarly synthesis]

### Calendar correlation

The placement of *Tlacaxipehualiztli* is secure within the sequence of veintenas, but its conversion to a fixed Gregorian date is not. Sahagún, Durán, and other sources begin or correlate the year differently; scholars also debate intercalation. Statements that the festival “always began at the spring equinox” overstate the evidence. [contested]

### Sacrificial scale

The forms of sacrifice are multiply attested; exact annual numbers are not. Colonial narratives could magnify totals for imperial grandeur, Christian polemic, Indigenous political memory, or literary effect. Archaeology confirms substantial institutional killing and skull display without validating every reported figure. [contested but not reasonably deniable in principle]

### Archaeological state of the evidence

Important objects survive in Mexico’s Museo Nacional de Antropología, Museo del Templo Mayor, regional INAH collections, the Museo de América in Madrid, the Smithsonian/National Museum of the American Indian, and other collections. Manuscripts are dispersed among the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Bodleian Library, and Mexican repositories.

Provenance is uneven. Some sculptures were excavated scientifically; others entered museums through antiquarian markets or old collections. The absence of archaeological context can make even a visually convincing Xipe identification uncertain.

### Corrections to recurrent popular claims

- “Xipe was securely worshipped by the Olmec”: **unproven**.
- “The Xolalpan statue proves Classic Teotihuacan worship”: **incorrect; its context is post-Classic-period occupation around 1000 CE**.
- “Xipe flayed himself to feed humanity”: **no early primary source located; modern explanatory myth**.
- “The priests flayed living victims as the normal rite”: **the principal descriptions have heart sacrifice followed by flaying**, though other forms of killing occurred.
- “The skins were worn for twenty days”: **textually attested**, though accounts differ in detail.
- “Xipe was simply Camaxtli”: **regional/syncretistic equation, not universal identity**.
- “Xipe was unambiguously one of Ometeotl’s four sons”: **colonial learned harmonization**.
- “The 2015–2017 Huei Tzompantli is Xipe’s skull rack”: **unsupported; it corroborates state sacrifice generally**.
- “Ndachjian was discovered in 2019”: **the temple was announced then; excavation preceded the announcement**.
- “Spring equinox was the immutable date”: **overprecise calendar conversion**.

## 11. Sources

### Primary-source foundation

The indispensable documentary base consists of:

- Bernardino de Sahagún and Nahua collaborators, *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex*, especially Books 1, 2, and 9 and the sacred-hymn appendix;
- Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*;
- Codex Borgia and the Borgia-group manuscripts;
- Codices Borbonicus, Telleriano-Remensis, Tudela/Magliabechiano, and Mendoza;
- *Historia de los Mexicanos por sus pinturas*;
- *Histoyre du Mechique*;
- *Leyenda de los Soles* and *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*;
- the Sahaguntine Xipe hymn and related Nahuatl song collections;
- sculptures, sacrificial stones, temple deposits, human remains, and architectural contexts from Xolalpan, Tula, Ndachjian, Tenochtitlan, and regional sites.

The documentary asymmetry must remain visible: nearly all explanatory prose was written after 1521 under colonial rule, while most preconquest evidence is pictorial or archaeological. The fullest possible dossier is therefore not a recovered canonical theology but a critical comparison of objects, images, Nahuatl testimony, missionary framing, regional evidence, and modern reconstruction.
