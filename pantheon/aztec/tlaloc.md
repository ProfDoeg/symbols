# Tlaloc: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Male *teotl*—a divine power or god—in Mexica and other Late Postclassic Nahua classifications; chief of a plurality of rain, mountain, and weather beings called the *Tlaloque*.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The name **Tlālōc** is securely documented only in Late Postclassic central-Mexican imagery and early-sixteenth-century Nahuatl and Spanish records. Archaeologically, an identifiable Mexica cult of Tlaloc is secure by approximately **CE 1375–1427**, in Stage II of the Templo Mayor at Mexico-Tenochtitlan. Goggle-eyed storm beings appear much earlier—most conspicuously at Classic-period Teotihuacan, approximately CE 150–650—but calling the Teotihuacan being “Tlaloc” is a modern historical hypothesis, not an ancient name-reading. Current scholarship generally uses the neutral term **Teotihuacan Storm God**.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST]** Material or imagery securely dated before 1521 and identifiable without depending exclusively on colonial prose.
- **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Sixteenth-century Indigenous, mendicant, or mixed pictorial-alphabetic records preserving pre-conquest knowledge, but produced after Spanish invasion.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A locally specific form that must not automatically be generalized to all Nahuas.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** A priestly, calendrical, antiquarian, or scholarly arrangement of diverse traditions.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]** A historically datable equation or merging with another being.
- **[COLONIAL ACCRETION]** A story or interpretation first attested under colonial conditions.
- **[FOLKLORE/ETHNOGRAPHY]** A post-conquest or contemporary community tradition, not automatically evidence for unchanged pre-conquest belief.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** A scholarly inference from comparative, linguistic, archaeological, or iconographic evidence.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A claim, genealogy, correspondence, or narrative created in modern popular, occult, commercial, or fictional contexts.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY; corroborated archaeologically]** Tlaloc was the principal Mexica power governing rain, storm, lightning, hail, mountain water, and agricultural fertility. He was neither simply benevolent nor simply destructive. The *Florentine Codex* says that he and the rain beings supplied germination and sustenance but also sent hail, lightning, inundation, frost, and dangerous illnesses. His manifestations encompassed the water stored inside mountains, the clouds gathered around their summits, rain released from celestial or subterranean vessels, lightning that broke those vessels, and the beings who died through water or weather and entered Tlalocan.

His importance was institutional and political as well as meteorological. At the Templo Mayor, Tlaloc’s northern shrine stood beside Huitzilopochtli’s southern shrine. This pairing monumentalized two foundations of Mexica state power: agricultural sustenance and tributary warfare. It does not, by itself, document a mythic conversation or kinship between the two gods.

### Names

- **Tlālōc / Tláloc.** Classical Nahuatl spelling varies because colonial orthography did not consistently mark vowel length or the internal consonant represented by modern normalized spellings. Some specialists prefer **Tlalloc**, arguing that the doubled consonant reflects the word’s derivation. The familiar Spanish form is *Tláloc*.

- **Tlālōctēuctli / Tlalocatecuhtli**, “Lord Tlaloc” or “Lord of the Tlaloc-domain.” This expanded honorific occurs in colonial descriptions and glosses.

- **Tlamacazqui**, conventionally “the Giver,” “Provider,” or “He Who Gives Things.” In Nahua usage *tlamacazqui* could also designate a priest; consequently references to “Tlaloc Tlamacazqui” can exploit an overlap between god, provider, and priestly office.

- **Xoxouhqui**, “the Green/Blue One.” This color term belongs to the rain-water-vegetation complex; its exact shade cannot be reduced to the modern English division between blue and green.

- **Tōtā / Tota**, “Our Father,” is applied to Tlaloc in ritual discourse, including festival descriptions.

- **Tlālōcān Tlamacazqueh**, approximately “providers/priests of Tlalocan,” can denote the plurality of rain beings rather than Tlaloc alone.

- **Tlaloque** (*Tlālōqueh*) is the plural of Tlaloc and designates subordinate or distributed rain powers—beings of mountains, directions, clouds, and rain vessels. Treating every *tlaloc* in a text as the single individual “Tlaloc” can therefore misread Nahua number and ontology.

### Etymology

The etymology remains disputed.

1. **“He who makes things sprout.”** This is the common public interpretation and accords with agricultural function, but it is not a transparent, universally accepted morphological derivation.

2. **“Made of earth,” “full of earth,” or “having the quality of earth.”** Thelma Sullivan derived a form *Tlalloc* from *tlalli*, “earth,” and the adjectival *tlallo*, emphasizing Tlaloc’s telluric and mountain nature. José Contel further developed this interpretation. The proposal has the advantage of explaining the deity’s intimate connection with mountains, caves, soil, and the earth’s interior, not merely falling rain. [Contel’s discussion](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_2004_num_117_113_12300) reviews colonial and modern alternatives.

3. **“He who lies/stretches upon the earth.”** This is another earth-based analysis found in modern scholarship.

4. **“Earth wine/pulque” or “nectar of the earth.”** This widely repeated popular etymology is not the scholarly consensus and should not be presented as settled.

The surviving spelling does not make a unique solution inevitable. Karen Dakin’s discussion of Uto-Aztecan comparisons underscores that the received explanation should be tested rather than treated as obvious.

### Classification and equivalents

**[SECURE/REGIONAL]** The Mexica classified Tlaloc as a male *teotl*, but Tlaloc also functioned as the head, personification, or concentrated form of a plural rain-power complex. Nahua sources can move between one Tlaloc and multiple *Tlaloque* without treating the two formulations as contradictory.

**[MODERN COMPARATIVE IDENTIFICATIONS]** Central Mexican and neighboring peoples had rain gods with comparable functions:

- Mixtec **Dzahui**
- Zapotec **Cocijo**
- Maya **Chaahk/Chahk**
- Totonac and Gulf Coast rain powers
- Teotihuacan **Storm God**

These are useful comparisons, not timeless aliases. Each belonged to a distinct language, iconographic system, and political-religious history. The British Museum’s older description of a Mixtec image as “Dzahui (known to the Aztecs as Tlaloc)” illustrates historical museum translation, not proof that Mixtecs called Dzahui “Tlaloc.”

There was no pre-Hispanic *interpretatio graeca* or Roman equation. Later comparisons with Zeus, Jupiter, Thor, Indra, or other thunder gods are modern comparativism, not attested Indigenous identifications.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Preclassic antecedents

**[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Goggle-eyed and fanged rain imagery has possible antecedents as early as the first millennium BCE. Similarity alone does not establish continuity of name, language, mythology, or cult organization. The Getty’s iconographic authority record cautiously states that related imagery occurs from at least the first century BCE; it does not prove that those figures bore the Nahuatl name Tlaloc.

#### Teotihuacan, approximately CE 150–650

**[SECURE ICONOGRAPHY; IDENTITY DISPUTED]** Teotihuacan painting, ceramic vessels, architectural ornament, and portable art repeatedly show a being with ringed eyes, a raised or curling upper lip, fangs, water-lily or aquatic signs, and lightning-like weapons. Older publications regularly call it “Teotihuacan Tlaloc.” Esther Pasztory and many later scholars use **Storm God** precisely to avoid projecting the much later Nahuatl name backward.

The Metropolitan Museum’s Teotihuacan vessel demonstrates the recognizable ringed eyes, lip, and fangs. Yet recent work emphasizes that:

- several visually related beings may be involved;
- some figures may be warriors or rulers wearing storm insignia;
- the so-called Great Goddess shares storm attributes;
- gender and divine individuality are not always recoverable;
- Teotihuacan’s religious categories may not have resembled a fixed Greek-style pantheon.

The safest formulation is: **a Teotihuacan storm-and-rain complex is a probable historical ancestor or close predecessor of the Late Postclassic Tlaloc complex; identity across the intervening centuries is not demonstrated by a surviving name inscription.**

#### Epiclassic and Early Postclassic

**[SECURE ICONOGRAPHY; REGIONAL]** Goggle-eyed rain beings persisted after Teotihuacan in central Mexico and were transmitted or reinterpreted elsewhere. Tula’s rain-war iconography, including goggle-eyed figures and *chacmool* offering stones, provides an important bridge, though “Tlaloc” remains a retrospective designation when no readable name accompanies the image.

#### Mexica Tenochtitlan, fourteenth–early sixteenth centuries

**[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST]** Stage II of the Templo Mayor, generally dated to approximately 1375–1427 and often more narrowly around 1390, preserves the double summit arrangement and Tlaloc-side architecture, including painted surfaces, a *chacmool*, aquatic symbolism, and later Tlaloc braziers and offerings. It is the earliest excavated portion of the Mexica state sanctuary that securely demonstrates an institutional Tlaloc cult.

### Primary textual and pictorial corpus

1. **Primeros Memoriales**, Nahuatl with images, gathered at Tepepolco approximately 1558–1561; surviving in the Madrid manuscripts.  
   **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Contains divine costumes, priestly and festival material, and the ritual song *Tlalloc icuic*, “Song of Tlaloc.” It is earlier in Sahagún’s documentary process than the completed *Florentine Codex* and often preserves less-systematized local testimony.

2. **Florentine Codex / Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España**, Nahuatl and Spanish, completed in 1577 at the Colegio de Santa Cruz, Tlatelolco; Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence.  
   **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** The central documentary source. Book 1 describes Tlaloc’s identity, powers, and image; Book 2 describes the *veintena* festivals and sacrifices; Book 3 and related passages concern divine places and destinies after death; Book 6 preserves ritual rhetoric; Book 11 treats natural phenomena within an Indigenous taxonomy; Book 12 occasionally uses divine analogies in conquest narrative. The manuscript has 2,446 pages and nearly 2,500 images and is now searchable in the [Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/). Anderson and Dibble’s English translation, published volume by volume from 1950 to 1982, remains fundamental, but the Nahuatl, Spanish, and paintings are not identical columns of one message.

3. **Codex Borbonicus**, pictorial manuscript, made near the conquest, either shortly before 1521 or in the first colonial decades.  
   **[DATE DISPUTED]** Its festival cycle depicts rain rites and Tlaloc-related ceremonies. Scientific and art-historical study has not settled whether it is wholly pre-conquest, a traditional manuscript completed after conquest, or a colonial copy of an earlier model. It must not be cited as securely pre-conquest without qualification.

4. **Codex Borgia**, ritual-divinatory screenfold, probably Puebla–Tlaxcala or neighboring central-southern Mexico, Late Postclassic, generally thirteenth–early sixteenth century.  
   **[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST; REGIONAL]** Depicts goggle-eyed rain gods in divinatory almanacs, directional schemes, temple scenes, and ritual sequences. It is not demonstrably a Mexica manuscript and contains no alphabetic glosses. Eduard Seler’s monumental commentary pioneered interpretation; Elizabeth Hill Boone and the Anders–Jansen–Reyes edition substantially revised its reading.

5. **Other Borgia Group manuscripts**—*Codex Vaticanus B*, *Codex Cospi*, *Codex Fejérváry-Mayer*, and *Codex Laud*.  
   **[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST OR EARLY COLONIAL, depending on manuscript]** These record rain beings within directional, calendrical, and mantic structures. Similar imagery does not guarantee that every painted rain god was called Tlaloc by its original makers.

6. **Codex Mendoza**, approximately 1541–1542, pictorial Nahua work with Spanish glosses, commissioned under viceregal authority.  
   **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Primarily tributary, historical, and social rather than mythographic. It documents the political-economic world sustaining state cult, including tribute goods and priestly institutions.

7. **Codex Telleriano-Remensis**, mid-sixteenth century, European paper, pictorial material with multiple Spanish annotations; Bibliothèque nationale de France.  
   **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Combines the fixed festival calendar, a *tonalamatl*, and annals through 1562. Its related but not identical *Codex Vaticanus A/Ríos* tradition contains important colonial explanations of the Suns, gods, and afterlife.

8. **Codex Magliabechiano and the Magliabechiano Group**, mid-sixteenth-century pictorial and Spanish manuscripts derived from a lost prototype.  
   **[EARLY COLONIAL; LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** Record divine costumes, festivals, mortuary practices, and calendrical gods. Differences among copies reveal transmission rather than a single transparent “Aztec Bible.”

9. **Diego Durán, Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme and Libro de los dioses y ritos**, composed approximately 1574–1581.  
   **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Chapter 8 of the *Book of the Gods and Rites* treats Tlaloc, including the mountain sanctuary, interstate royal pilgrimage, idol, priests, offerings, and child sacrifice. Durán relied on Indigenous informants and pictorial or alphabetic sources, but wrote as a Dominican seeking to identify and eradicate “idolatry.”

10. **Leyenda de los Soles**, Nahuatl, copied in 1558 in the manuscript conventionally called *Codex Chimalpopoca*.  
    **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Makes Tlaloc lord or sun of the era 4-Rain and gives the story of Quetzalcoatl, the red ant, the Mountain of Sustenance, and the Tlaloque’s seizure or distribution of maize and other foods.

11. **Anales de Cuauhtitlan**, Nahuatl annals compiled in the sixteenth century and preserved with the *Leyenda de los Soles*.  
    **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY; REGIONAL]** Relevant to central-Mexican cosmology, political histories, and divine eras, though it is not a Tlaloc biography.

12. **Histoyre du Méchique**, French text translated or adapted from a lost Spanish account, approximately 1540s.  
    **[EARLY COLONIAL; TRANSMISSION COMPLEX]** Preserves creation traditions absent or different elsewhere, including an account in which Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca act upon the earth being and Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue emerge within the resulting cosmogony. Its French transmission and unstable divine names require caution. The [UNAM virtual edition](https://bigbangmex.unam.mx/en/histoyre-du-mechique/el-documento/) makes the surviving document accessible.

13. **Cantares Mexicanos**, Nahuatl song manuscript principally copied in the later sixteenth century.  
    **[EARLY COLONIAL; PERFORMANCE CONTEXT DISPUTED]** Contains pre-conquest-style poetic language alongside colonial Christian and political layers. Rain, flowers, mountains, and Tlalocan imagery cannot automatically be read as verbatim pre-conquest liturgy.

14. **Tlaloc hymn / “Song of Tlaloc.”** Preserved through Sahagún’s collection and first translated through nineteenth- and twentieth-century editions.  
    **[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Its archaic Nahuatl is difficult, with contested segmentation and glosses. Daniel Brinton’s 1890 *Rig Veda Americanus* is historically important but philologically obsolete in several respects.

No surviving primary document is a continuous “scripture of Tlaloc.” The corpus consists of ritual calendars, painted divinatory books, songs, descriptions assembled by colonial investigators, fragments of oral narrative, archaeology, and later ethnography.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

**[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Tlaloc governed or embodied:

- rain and the timing of rain;
- clouds, mist, lightning, and thunder;
- hail, destructive storms, floods, and damaging cold;
- mountain springs and water stored within the earth;
- agricultural germination and the “maintenance” or sustenance on which human life depended;
- illnesses attributed to rain, water, coldness, or divine seizure;
- Tlalocan and the dead claimed by water-related causes;
- green or blue fertility, aquatic creatures, and the cyclical emergence of vegetation.

He did not possess unlimited control over every form of water. **Chalchiuhtlicue** is regularly distinguished as the power of rivers, lakes, springs, and flowing or standing terrestrial water, though the two share rites and form a complementary complex.

### Rain production

**[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY; MYTHIC MODEL]** Colonial accounts describe the Tlaloque as inhabiting compartments or mountains containing great water jars. They strike the jars with sticks; the breaking or striking produces thunder and lightning, and the water becomes rain. Different vessels or directions can yield beneficial rain, blight, frost, hail, or crop-destroying water. This is not a primitive attempt at physical meteorology separated from religion: it is a ritual model relating directional space, mountains, divine agency, seasonal agriculture, and the differing consequences of precipitation.

### Standard iconography

**[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST]** Tlaloc is normally recognized by a facial assemblage rather than by a naturalistic portrait:

- circular or ringed “goggle” eyes;
- a curling upper-lip or moustache band;
- long fangs;
- a frontal mask;
- a blue, blue-green, black, or otherwise rain-associated painted body or face;
- paper crown, heron-feather ornaments, or quetzal plumes;
- ear ornaments;
- water, cloud, shell, jade, or vegetation signs;
- lightning or serpent-shaped implements;
- vessels from which water flows.

A widely used interpretation understands the mask as composed from **two intertwined serpents**, whose bodies circle the eyes and meet around the nose and mouth. This reading is influential and visually persuasive but should not be mistaken for an explanatory sentence supplied by every ancient image-maker.

### Materials, animals, plants, and colors

- **Serpents:** lightning, flowing water, and the structural elements of the mask.
- **Jaguars:** caves, night, earth interior, rulership, and the roar of storms; association does not make Tlaloc simply a “jaguar god.”
- **Frogs and toads:** seasonal emergence and rain. Stone frogs occur near Tlaloc architecture at the Templo Mayor.
- **Aquatic and marine organisms:** shells, coral, fish, crocodilians, turtles, sawfish rostra, and other objects deposited on the Tlaloc side of the Templo Mayor made distant seas materially present within the mountain-temple.
- **Jade/greenstone (*chalchihuitl*):** precious water, vegetation, life, and fertility. Greenstone beads were placed with some sacrificed children.
- **Rubber, copal, paper, feathers, and blue pigment:** prominent offering and costume materials.
- **Maize and amaranth:** products of rain rather than exclusive possessions of Tlaloc.
- **Yauhtli**, often identified as *Tagetes lucida*: burned or offered in rain and mountain rites.
- **Blue-green:** especially characteristic in later reconstructions, codex images, architectural paint, and modern representation. No single immutable “official color” existed across every region and century.

### Calendrical associations

Tlaloc appears as a day- and period-patron in divinatory manuscripts and in the *veintena* cycle. The sign **4-Rain (*Nahui Quiahuitl*)** named the third cosmic era in the Five Suns system. Rain ceremonies occupied numerous parts of the agricultural year, not one isolated “Tlaloc Day.”

### Tlalocan

**[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Tlalocan, “Place of Tlaloc,” is described as verdant, moist, fertile, and abundant in edible plants. It received people whose deaths signaled Tlaloc’s claim: drowning, lightning, certain swellings, pustular or skin diseases, gout, dropsy, and related “divine sicknesses.” The dead destined there were reportedly buried rather than cremated.

Calling Tlalocan a “paradise” is useful only with qualification. It was not principally a moral reward analogous to Christian heaven. Destination followed the manner of death and divine possession. The colonial Spanish word *paraíso* may also have encouraged Christian comparison.

**[DISPUTED ICONOGRAPHY]** The Tepantitla murals at Teotihuacan have often been called a “Tlalocan mural.” They depict water, vegetation, play, and abundance, but that title is a twentieth-century interpretation; the scenes are roughly a millennium earlier than Sahagún’s Tlalocan description and carry no surviving Nahuatl label.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogical instability

Tlaloc lacks one universal genealogy.

- **[EARLY COLONIAL/CREATION VARIANT]** In the *Histoyre du Méchique* complex, Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca’s violent ordering of the earth results in or precedes the production of Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue. This is an origin account, not proof that all Nahuas regarded those gods as Tlaloc’s biological parents.

- **[LATER COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]** Some colonial summaries call Tlaloc a creation of the four Tezcatlipocas or primordial creator gods. The formulation belongs to a systematized cosmogony rather than a securely pre-conquest genealogical inscription.

- **[EARLY COLONIAL]** Chalchiuhtlicue is described variously as Tlaloc’s wife, sister, or female counterpart. These relationships coexist because divine kinship expressed complementary domains as much as domestic biography.

- **[COLONIAL VARIANT]** Xochiquetzal is named in some sources as Tlaloc’s wife before Tezcatlipoca took her. This story is much less securely and widely attested than modern myth summaries imply.

- **[EARLY COLONIAL]** Huixtocihuatl, salt-water or salt goddess, is called the elder sister of the Tlaloque in Sahagún’s festival account.

- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** Modern charts frequently list the Tlaloque as Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue’s biological “children.” The sources more securely establish them as subordinate rain beings, distributed manifestations, assistants, or members of the same divine class. Parentage should not be asserted without identifying the particular colonial source.

### Every documented interaction or joint episode

The following list separates narrative interaction from mere coexistence.

- **Chalchiuhtlicue — spouse, sister, or complementary water power.** Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue share rain-water festivals in Sahagún, while colonial descriptions distinguish his celestial/mountain rain from her rivers and lakes. *Florentine Codex*, Books 1–2, compiled 1550s–1577. **[EARLY COLONIAL; RELATIONSHIP VARIES]**

- **Chalchiuhtlicue — succession in the Five Suns.** In the *Leyenda de los Soles* tradition, the era 4-Rain ruled by Tlaloc precedes the watery era associated with Chalchiuhtlicue. The text establishes cosmic succession more securely than a narrative handover between spouses. *Leyenda de los Soles*, copied 1558. **[EARLY COLONIAL MYTHIC RECORD]**

- **The Tlaloque — collective subordinates or distributed forms.** They keep, strike, break, or distribute water vessels and supply different rains; in the maize myth they seize and distribute the foods hidden inside Tonacatepetl. Sahagún; *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[EARLY COLONIAL]**

- **Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl — wind precedes rain.** The *Florentine Codex* says Ehecatl sweeps or clears the roads before Tlaloc makes rain. Book 1, chapter 5, completed 1577. **[EARLY COLONIAL]**

- **Quetzalcoatl — Mountain of Sustenance.** Quetzalcoatl follows the red ant to maize; after human or divine efforts fail to carry the mountain away, Nanahuatl splits it, whereupon the Tlaloque take the maize and other foods. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558 manuscript. **[EARLY COLONIAL]** Tlaloc personally may not act in every wording; the named actors are often the Tlaloque.

- **Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca — cosmogonic origin context.** Their assault on or ordering of the earth being is connected in the *Histoyre du Méchique* with the coming into being of Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue. Approximately 1540s. **[EARLY COLONIAL; TRANSMISSION-DAMAGED VARIANT]**

- **Quetzalcoatl — overthrow of the Third Sun.** In some colonial Five Suns arrangements, Quetzalcoatl ends Tlaloc’s solar era, after which fire rains and the inhabitants become birds. The exact agent and ordering vary among the *Leyenda de los Soles*, *Historia de los Mexicanos por sus pinturas*, and Ríos traditions. **[EARLY COLONIAL; VARIANT]**

- **Tezcatlipoca — installation or succession in the Suns.** Systematized colonial versions have Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl alternating or replacing cosmic rulers, with Tlaloc installed as the third sun. This is not uniform across all surviving sources. **[COLONIAL SYSTEMATIZATION]**

- **Xochiquetzal — marriage and abduction.** In the Codex Ríos/Vaticanus A commentary tradition, Xochiquetzal is associated as Tlaloc’s wife and subsequently taken by Tezcatlipoca. Later popular retellings greatly expand the sparse colonial notice. **[COLONIAL VARIANT/LATE POPULAR ELABORATION]**

- **Huixtocihuatl — kinship with rain gods.** Sahagún identifies her as the elder sister of the Tlaloque and records a separate salt festival. *Florentine Codex*, Book 2, completed 1577. **[EARLY COLONIAL]**

- **Chicomecoatl — joint agricultural ritual field.** Tlaloc/rain ceremonies and rites for Chicomecoatl overlap in the agricultural festival sequence, especially around young and mature maize. Sahagún and Durán, sixteenth century. **[EARLY COLONIAL; RITUAL JOINT APPEARANCE, not a narrative meeting]**

- **Cinteotl — joint sustenance cycle.** Tlaloc’s rain and Cinteotl’s maize belong to connected festival sequences; texts document ritual coordination more clearly than personal interaction. Sahagún and pictorial calendars. **[EARLY COLONIAL]**

- **Huitzilopochtli — paired state shrines.** Their adjacent sanctuaries at the Templo Mayor are archaeologically and textually secure. No surviving primary myth records a conversation, battle, marriage, or other personal episode between them. **[SECURE CULTIC ASSOCIATION; ABSENCE OF NARRATIVE INTERACTION]**

- **Nanahuatl — opening the Mountain of Sustenance.** Nanahuatl splits Tonacatepetl so the Tlaloque can take its foods. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. This connects Nanahuatl directly with the Tlaloque, only indirectly with Tlaloc as their chief. **[EARLY COLONIAL]**

Claims that Tlaloc is securely the father of Tecciztecatl, the father of every named Tlaloque, or a brother of Huitzilopochtli belong to particular late charts or modern compilations unless a primary source is specified.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 The Third Sun: 4-Rain

**[EARLY COLONIAL MYTHIC RECORD]** The Five Suns traditions describe successive worlds distinguished by calendrical names and catastrophic endings. Tlaloc rules the Sun called **Nahui Quiahuitl**, “4-Rain.” Its destruction comes through a rain of fire; its inhabitants perish or become birds or fowl, depending on the version.

Important distinctions:

- The *Leyenda de los Soles* preserves a Nahuatl account copied in 1558.
- The *Historia de los Mexicanos por sus pinturas* and Codex Ríos/Vaticanus A commentaries rearrange divine agents, reign lengths, and successions.
- The Sun Stone, carved in the late fifteenth century, incorporates the sign 4-Rain among the previous eras, providing pre-conquest monumental evidence for the cosmic category. It does not narrate the full prose story or independently name every agent.

**Development:** The association of rain with destructive fire is not necessarily contradictory. Lightning, volcanic fire, burning rain, and the danger inherent in storm power coexist in the cycle. Modern retellings often convert the variant records into a smooth story in which Tezcatlipoca “appoints” Tlaloc and Quetzalcoatl “knocks him down”; that exact dramatized sequence is not universal.

### 5.2 The Mountain of Sustenance

**[EARLY COLONIAL MYTHIC RECORD]** After the creation of present humanity, Quetzalcoatl sees a red ant carrying maize. He questions or follows it into **Tonacatepetl**, the Mountain of Sustenance. The gods attempt to move the mountain; Nanahuatl splits it. The Tlaloque then take or disperse white, black, yellow, and red maize, beans, amaranth, chia, and other foods.

The myth’s logic places sustenance inside a mountain and rain powers in control of its release. Mountains are therefore reservoirs of both water and seed. The story links Quetzalcoatl’s civilizing search, Nanahuatl’s transformative force, and the Tlaloque’s authority over cultivated abundance.

A modern illustrated transcription is available through [Arqueología Mexicana’s discussion of the maize theft](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/3786).

### 5.3 The rain jars of the Tlaloque

**[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Tlaloc’s assistants keep water in jars situated in a divine house, mountain, or directional quarters. Their blows produce thunder, cracks of lightning, and rain. Different jars contain different meteorological outcomes. The story explains why rain is simultaneously necessary, uneven, locally controlled, and potentially ruinous.

This should not be harmonized into one canonical blueprint. Some sources emphasize four directional beings and colored waters; others emphasize mountain dwarfs or small rain beings; local traditions identify particular peaks as personal reservoirs.

### 5.4 Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue

**[EARLY COLONIAL; MULTIPLE FORMS]** The pair divide and overlap the watery cosmos. Tlaloc most often governs rain, storm, and mountain-held water; Chalchiuhtlicue governs rivers, lakes, springs, and flowing water. She rules or embodies the fourth world in several Five Suns accounts, destroyed by flood.

“Marriage” is one source’s relational language, not necessarily the pair’s only theological form. Other texts call her sister or place both within the Tlaloque collective.

### 5.5 Xochiquetzal taken by Tezcatlipoca

**[COLONIAL VARIANT]** The Ríos/Vaticanus A commentary tradition associates Xochiquetzal with Tlaloc and says Tezcatlipoca took her. Modern summaries turn this into an elaborate romance, kidnapping, or marital tragedy. The surviving early testimony is comparatively brief; no extensive pre-conquest narrative manuscript survives.

### 5.6 Tlalocan and divine seizure

**[EARLY COLONIAL BELIEF]** Tlaloc takes particular dead people into his domain. Drowning, lightning, dropsical swelling, pustules, and water-associated illnesses indicate not moral condemnation but the god’s claim. The flourishing landscape of Tlalocan expresses agricultural abundance released from inside the earth.

The familiar phrase “paradise of the drowned” is only partly adequate: lightning-struck and certain diseased persons also went there, while drowning was not represented as a virtuous achievement.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Templo Mayor

**[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST]** The Templo Mayor was a double pyramid. Tlaloc’s shrine occupied the **north** summit; Huitzilopochtli’s occupied the south. The northern precinct contained aquatic, mountain, fertility, and offering imagery. The Museo Nacional de Antropología confirms the north/south arrangement, while excavation has identified seven principal construction stages.

Stage II, the earliest substantially exposed stage, includes:

- two summit shrines;
- surviving mural paint;
- a *chacmool* before the Tlaloc shrine;
- frog sculptures and serpent elements;
- architectural orientation later modified in Stage III.

Successive enlargements deposited immense offerings. Marine organisms, greenstone, copal, fauna, vessels, and imported objects transformed the built pyramid into a material sacred mountain containing the waters and riches of the world.

### Cerro Tlaloc / Tlalocatepetl

**[SECURE ARCHAEOLOGICAL SITE; EARLY COLONIAL RITUAL DESCRIPTION]** Cerro Tlaloc rises on the eastern Basin of Mexico at approximately 4,100 meters. Its summit enclosure and long causeway formed one of the highest major ceremonial installations in Mesoamerica. Durán reports that rulers and representatives of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan, and other polities ascended for the **Huey Tozoztli** ceremonies, offering valuables, food, and a child to the image of Tlaloc.

The mountain’s ritual significance depended on its clouds, altitude, vegetation zones, eastern horizon, and commanding relationship to the Basin. Catherine Popovici’s study of [the ritual ascent](https://mavcor.yale.edu/mavcor-journal/ritual-ascent-mount-tlaloc-mexico) emphasizes the processional landscape rather than treating only the summit building as sacred.

Archaeoastronomical proposals connect the summit causeway and horizon with solar calendrical observation. Some alignments are plausible; claims that the sanctuary functioned as a perfectly precise observatory or encoded modern mathematical constants go beyond the evidence.

### Atlcahualo and the sacrifice of children

**[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY; ARCHAEOLOGICALLY CORROBORATED IN GENERAL]** The first *veintena* was called **Atlcahualo** among the Mexica and **Cuahuitlehua** elsewhere. The *Florentine Codex* explicitly says that many children were killed on mountain summits and at other water-related locations for the rain gods. Their hearts were extracted. If the children wept, the tears were welcomed as a sign of abundant rain.

The relevant [Florentine Codex folio](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/book/2/folio/3r) states both the competing divine dedications—Tlaloque, Chalchiuhtlicue, or Quetzalcoatl—and the interpretation of tears. This disagreement within Sahagún’s own record is important: Atlcahualo was a water-and-weather complex, not necessarily the exclusive feast of one isolated god.

Other festival descriptions extend child offerings through the months before the rains. Victims were taken to named mountains and lake sites, dressed as rain beings, carried in litters, and killed. Colonial sources say children were purchased or supplied by households. The texts’ missionary framing does not erase their detailed Indigenous place names, costumes, and ritual sequence, but individual figures and totals require corroboration.

### Ofrenda 48

**[SECURE ARCHAEOLOGICAL EVIDENCE]** Excavated in 1980–1981 on the Tlaloc side of the Templo Mayor, Ofrenda 48 contained at least **42 children**. The deposit belonged to Stage IVa, during Motecuhzoma Ilhuicamina’s reign, and is now associated with the severe drought and famine of approximately 1452–1454.

The children were placed over marine sand and accompanied by greenstone, blue pigment, copal, gourds, marine materials, birds, an obsidian blade, and eleven polychrome stone images bearing Tlaloc faces. Osteological examination found signs of nutritional stress in many individuals. Recent isotope work indicates that several came from regions outside the Basin, including possible Oaxaca and southern highland origins. The precise mechanisms—tribute, purchase, captivity, household dedication, or several routes—cannot be established for every child.

This deposit independently confirms that child sacrifice associated with the Tlaloc cult was not a Spanish fabrication. It does not validate every colonial numerical claim.

### Other festivals

Rain and sustenance rites crossed much of the year:

- **Atlcahualo/Cuahuitlehua:** initiation of the rain-cycle sacrifices.
- **Tozoztontli and Huey Tozoztli:** mountain, maize, and rain petitions; royal ascent to Cerro Tlaloc.
- **Etzalcualiztli:** an especially important Tlaloc festival involving priests, water, reeds, dance, fasting, offerings, and punishment of ritual transgression.
- **Atemoztli**, “Descent of the Waters”: household and temple images of mountains and Tlaloque fashioned from amaranth dough and ritually opened or consumed.
- Additional ceremonies for Chalchiuhtlicue, Huixtocihuatl, Chicomecoatl, and Cinteotl belonged to the same agricultural-water sequence.

Arqueología Mexicana provides a useful chart of [festivals practiced on the Tlaloc side](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/3767).

### Priesthood

The high priest of the Tlaloc shrine bore the title **Quetzalcoatl Tlaloc Tlamacazqui**. A corresponding senior priest served Huitzilopochtli. Lesser Tlaloc priests maintained vigil, fasting, incense, music, processions, image dressing, offerings, and sacrifices. The title *Quetzalcoatl* here was priestly and institutional; it does not mean the officeholder was the god Quetzalcoatl interacting bodily with Tlaloc.

### Sacrifice, offerings, and state power

Offerings included:

- children and, in some ceremonies, adults;
- blood from autosacrifice;
- copal incense;
- rubber-spattered paper;
- food, amaranth dough images, maize, and *etzalli*;
- jade and greenstone;
- feathers and textiles;
- ceramic Tlaloc vessels;
- frogs, serpents, birds, and marine animals;
- imported objects from conquered or tributary provinces.

The state’s capacity to gather exotic materials and victims made the Tlaloc cult a demonstration of imperial reach. At the same time, household figurines, village mountain rites, local shrines, and agricultural petitions show that rain worship was not reducible to imperial propaganda.

### Huei Tzompantli

**[SECURE ARCHAEOLOGICAL EVIDENCE; NOT EXCLUSIVELY TLALOC]** Excavations between 2015 and 2017 identified part of Tenochtitlan’s great skull-rack and associated skull tower. The [INAH site record](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907) dates exposed phases principally to 1486–1502 and 1502–1521.

The find confirms large-scale human sacrifice and public display of skulls. It should not be represented as a Tlaloc-specific installation: the Huei Tzompantli belonged to the Sacred Precinct’s broader sacrificial, warfare, and ballgame complex. Ofrenda 48 is the more direct archaeological evidence for sacrifice specifically to the rain powers.

### Absence of “temple prostitution”

No reliable Nahua, archaeological, or early colonial source establishes an institution of “temple prostitution” in Tlaloc’s cult. Claims to that effect generally import contested Old World categories or conflate ritual sexuality, deity impersonation, marriage metaphors, and prostitution. Its absence from the evidence should be stated plainly.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### Before CE 600: storm-god foundations

**[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Central Mexican rain-storm imagery developed long before the Mexica. Teotihuacan’s Storm God supplies the strongest visual predecessor: ringed eyes, fangs, aquatic signs, and lightning weapons. The being also acquired martial and state associations. Whether this was one god, several manifestations, or a ritual costume complex remains debated.

### CE 600–1200: transmission and regionalization

**[SECURE ICONOGRAPHIC CONTINUITY; NAMES UNKNOWN]** After Teotihuacan’s collapse, storm-god imagery survived and changed across central Mexico, the Gulf Coast, Oaxaca, western Mexico, and Maya regions influenced by central-Mexican visual culture. Tula linked rain imagery with warfare and elite offering. These movements show transmission of motifs, not a single unchanged pan-Mesoamerican church.

### CE 1200–1350: Late Postclassic codex religion

**[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST]** Borgia Group manuscripts systematized rain beings within day counts, directional almanacs, agricultural cycles, and temple rituals. Regional origin remains debated, probably within the Puebla–Tlaxcala–Mixteca zone. The imagery provides the fullest pre-conquest pictorial corpus but not a direct transcript of Mexica doctrine.

### Approximately 1375–1427: early Mexica state sanctuary

**[SECURE PRE-CONQUEST]** Templo Mayor Stage II confirms that the paired Tlaloc–Huitzilopochtli sanctuary was established early in the monumental development of Tenochtitlan. Tlaloc’s position beside the Mexica patron deity indicates that the rain god was not a marginal agricultural figure.

### 1427–1469: imperial consolidation and drought crisis

**[SECURE ARCHAEOLOGY; EARLY COLONIAL HISTORY]** Under Itzcoatl and Motecuhzoma Ilhuicamina, the Triple Alliance expanded while the Templo Mayor was enlarged. The mid-fifteenth-century drought brought extreme ritual intervention. Ofrenda 48’s mass child deposit belongs to this context.

### 1469–1502: expansion under Axayacatl, Tizoc, and Ahuitzotl

**[SECURE ARCHAEOLOGY]** Successive enlargements accumulated tribute materials from both coasts and distant provinces. The 1487 dedication reported in colonial histories became notorious for claims of enormous sacrificial totals. Archaeology confirms extensive sacrifice, but exact chronicler numbers—especially tens of thousands within a few days—remain debated.

The Coyolxauhqui monolith belongs to Huitzilopochtli’s south side and narratively to the Coatepec myth, not Tlaloc’s mythology. Its discovery in 1978 nevertheless initiated systematic excavation that transformed knowledge of Tlaloc’s northern sector.

### 1502–1521: final pre-conquest phase

Tlaloc remained central under Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin. Spaniards and Indigenous allies saw the twin summit shrines in 1519–1521. Descriptions by Cortés, Bernal Díaz, Andrés de Tapia, Sahagún’s informants, and Durán’s later sources disagree in vocabulary and theological interpretation but concur that the main temple housed major cult images and sacrifice.

### 1521–late sixteenth century: destruction and documentation

**[DOCUMENTED SUPPRESSION]** The fall of Tenochtitlan destroyed the institutional basis of the public cult. Temples were demolished, images burned or broken, priestly offices abolished, and Christian buildings erected within the former sacred landscape.

Muñoz Camargo’s late-sixteenth-century “Burning of the Idols” shows Franciscans and Indigenous assistants destroying images, including a recognizable Tlaloc figure. [Smarthistory’s manuscript study](https://smarthistory.org/burning-idols-diego-munoz-camargo-description-city-province-tlaxcala/) correctly treats it as both evidence for iconoclasm and a colonial representation of conversion.

A 1539 inquisitorial proceeding recorded allegations that major idols removed from the Templo Mayor had been concealed. Such cases show that destruction did not instantly erase Indigenous custody of sacred objects. Claims that Zumárraga personally destroyed a specific ancient Cerro Tlaloc idol because exactly 20,000 children were sacrificed annually derive from polemical colonial reporting and should not be accepted numerically.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: transformed continuities

**[FOLKLORE/ETHNOGRAPHY; CONTINUITY PARTLY INFERRED]** Rain petition, mountain offerings, cave veneration, weather specialists, and the personification of storms continued in many Indigenous communities under Christian names and calendars. Saint Michael, Saint Mark, the Holy Cross, local Virgins, and Christ could occupy ritual positions formerly or analogically associated with rain beings.

This is not simple camouflage with “Tlaloc underneath.” Colonial Indigenous religion generated new systems in which saints, ancestors, mountain owners, winds, and rain beings interacted. Continuity lies in practices and landscapes as well as names.

### 1790–1978: antiquarian recovery and nationalism

The 1790 discoveries of the Sun Stone and Coatlicue stimulated antiquarian study. Nineteenth-century scholars published codices and hymns, sometimes filtering them through biblical, solar-myth, racial, or diffusionist theories. The name Tlaloc became a convenient umbrella for ancient goggle-eyed beings throughout Mesoamerica.

The huge monolith moved from Coatlinchan to Mexico City in 1964 is popularly called “Tlaloc,” but some scholarship identifies it as Chalchiuhtlicue or another water being. The identification remains disputed.

### 1978–present: archaeological reconstruction

The accidental discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone in February 1978 led to the Proyecto Templo Mayor under Eduardo Matos Moctezuma. Excavation of the double temple, offerings, northern shrine, and surrounding precinct made Tlaloc’s cult materially recoverable on a scale impossible from chronicles alone.

The 2006 discovery of the Tlaltecuhtli monolith opened a new excavation phase around offerings associated with the reign and burial program of Ahuitzotl. Tlaltecuhtli is a distinct earth being, not another name for Tlaloc, although earth, mountain, fertility, and water symbolism intersect.

### Contemporary Indigenous practice

**[FOLKLORE/ETHNOGRAPHY]** Nahua and neighboring communities continue rain petitions, offerings on mountains and in caves, and work with weather specialists often called *graniceros*, *tiemperos*, *tesifteros*, or local terms. In the Sierra de Texcoco, ethnography records a Tlaloc–Nezahualcoyotl figure called King of the Sea and subordinate *ahuaques*, water owners or transformed humans. On Monte Tlaloc, contemporary ritual activity combines archaeological memory, local weather knowledge, Catholic forms, and heritage practice.

These traditions should neither be dismissed as inauthentic nor described as unchanged survival from 1500. The documented history includes adaptation, Christianization, local invention, and revival.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and national imagery

Nineteenth-century editions made Tlaloc legible to international readers. Daniel Brinton’s *Rig Veda Americanus* imposed an Indic comparison through its title; it did not demonstrate historical contact with Vedic religion.

After the Mexican Revolution, artists including Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, Rufino Tamayo, Juan O’Gorman, and others reworked Tlaloc as a symbol of Indigenous antiquity, water, agrarian Mexico, and national identity. Rivera’s monumental fountain and mosaic program in Chapultepec made the rain god a public icon of modern hydraulic nation-building. [Arqueología Mexicana’s survey](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/1811) documents this twentieth-century muralist reception.

### Mexicayotl and revival religion

**[MODERN REVIVAL]** Twentieth-century *Mexicayotl* and related Indigenous-national or reconstructionist movements revived Nahua names, calendrical rites, dances, offerings, and divine invocations. Tlaloc appears prominently in rain ceremonies and ecological discourse.

There is no centralized “Aztec church,” universally recognized priesthood, or reliable global membership figure. Contemporary practitioners range from Indigenous community ritualists to urban cultural organizations, nationalist groups, eclectic neopagans, and solitary online worshippers. Their practices are modern religious facts but cannot be projected backward as exact reproductions of Mexica state ritual.

### Occultism and New Age interpretation

**[MODERN INVENTION/ECLECTIC SYNCRETISM]** Unlike Egyptian, Greek, or Kabbalistic beings, Tlaloc was not a major figure in Renaissance Hermetism, the nineteenth-century Golden Dawn, or canonical Theosophy. Claims assigning him fixed planets, chakras, Tarot cards, demons, angels, or “elemental frequencies” are modern constructions unless an explicit modern author and date are supplied.

Jungian readings that make Tlaloc an archetype of unconscious fertility, destructive emotion, or the “terrible father” belong to twentieth- and twenty-first-century psychology and comparative mythology, not Nahua tradition.

### Politics

Tlaloc has served:

- revolutionary and post-revolutionary Indigenous nationalism;
- state museum and tourism narratives;
- local water-rights and ecological activism;
- pan-Indigenous or decolonial spirituality;
- occasional ethnonationalist rhetoric.

The use of Mexica symbols in extremist or racial-supremacist contexts is real but not intrinsic to Tlaloc’s historical cult. No evidence supports a single large Tlaloc-centered extremist movement. Political appropriations must be identified group by group.

### Literature, comics, film, and games

Modern fiction commonly turns Tlaloc into:

- an individual thunder-wielding humanoid analogous to Zeus;
- a boss monster;
- a source of rain magic;
- a horror god demanding indiscriminate blood;
- a female or personified incarnation, as in some Japanese game franchises;
- a generalized “Aztec god” detached from the Tlaloque, agricultural calendar, and mountain landscape.

These are **modern retellings**, not evidence for pre-conquest sex, personality, weaponry, or biography. Commercial designs often reduce the iconography to goggles and fangs—the two features most easily recognized.

### Common modern inventions

- “Tlaloc was an alien wearing goggles”: unsupported modern pseudoarchaeology.
- “The goggles prove advanced optical technology”: unsupported.
- “Tlaloc was identical to Chaahk, Cocijo, Dzahui, Indra, or Zeus”: comparative simplification, not an ancient equation.
- “The Coatlinchan monolith is unquestionably Tlaloc”: disputed.
- “The Tepantitla mural literally depicts the Aztec Tlalocan”: possible analogy, not demonstrated identity.
- “Tlaloc had one canonical wife and family tree”: contradicted by source variation.
- “Every skull in the Huei Tzompantli was sacrificed to Tlaloc”: unsupported.
- “Modern rain ceremonies are either pure survivals or wholly invented”: both extremes ignore documented transformation.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Sahagún and Durán

Sahagún’s project preserves the richest Nahuatl testimony but was organized within a Franciscan encyclopedia designed partly to expose and combat Indigenous religion. Durán similarly combined close knowledge, Indigenous sources, Christian typology, and an eradication program. Neither is a transparent window; neither can be discarded merely because he was a missionary.

The Digital Florentine Codex has materially changed research by allowing comparison of Nahuatl, Spanish, and paintings page by page. The project explains that the columns and images possess partly independent information and makes orthographic variants searchable.

### Seler and the Borgia Group

Eduard Seler’s late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century work created the foundational iconographic grammar for the Borgia Group. His readings remain indispensable but sometimes over-systematized distinct manuscripts into one unified “Mexican religion.”

Elizabeth Hill Boone reframed the codices as systems of time, divination, ritual performance, and visual communication rather than illustrated prose. Her work cautions against reading a painted figure as though it merely illustrates a lost paragraph.

### Nicholson’s typology

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 classification placed Tlaloc within a **Rain–Moisture–Agricultural Fertility** complex, alongside maize, flower, earth, pulque, and renewal subcomplexes. It remains useful for mapping functional relations. Its limitation is that typology can make fluid beings appear like members of a fixed departmental bureaucracy.

### León-Portilla and “Aztec thought”

Miguel León-Portilla’s *Aztec Thought and Culture* powerfully foregrounded Nahuatl concepts and Indigenous intellectual history. Critics caution that “Aztec philosophy” reconstructed from elite poetry and Sahaguntine texts may privilege a learned, late, and sometimes colonial corpus over regional and popular diversity.

### Tlaloc’s etymology

The main debate opposes functional translations centered on sprouting/rain-giving to earth-based derivations from *tlalli/tlallo*. The earth-based reading fits mountains, caves, and terrestrial water but is not proven merely by thematic appropriateness. Colonial spellings and historical phonology remain central.

### One god or a class of beings?

The singular/plural movement between Tlaloc and Tlaloque has produced competing models:

- a supreme rain god with small assistants;
- a collective class headed by one member;
- localized mountain powers aggregated under a state god;
- manifestations of one distributed sacred force.

The sources support aspects of all four; no single model explains every text and image.

### Teotihuacan ancestor or retrojection?

This is the central origin controversy. Older scholarship confidently named the Teotihuacan being Tlaloc. Current practice often says “Storm God” because:

- the Teotihuacan language and divine name are unknown;
- several storm-related iconographic variants exist;
- some “gods” may be costumed people;
- the Great Goddess shares attributes;
- nearly a millennium separates many images from the Nahuatl texts.

The continuity hypothesis remains strong at the level of visual and religious complexes, but personal identity and name continuity are unproved. The recent [Ancient Mesoamerica study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/from-the-great-goddess-to-the-storm-god-cosmic-transformations-at-the-boundary-between-the-dry-and-rainy-seasons-in-classic-teotihuacan/55283911DDF1E4B947B47517CBD0DC0A) presents the deity as a Storm God precisely to maintain this distinction.

### Human sacrifice: denial, exaggeration, and archaeology

Colonial writers had ideological incentives to portray Indigenous religion as diabolical, while modern apologetic accounts sometimes minimize sacrifice in response to colonial propaganda. The evidence requires a middle position grounded in particulars:

- Sahagún and Durán document named ceremonies, places, costumes, and methods.
- Ofrenda 48 contains at least 42 sacrificed children in a Tlaloc context.
- The Huei Tzompantli confirms large-scale skull display.
- Cut marks, perforation, mortuary arrangement, and sacrificial equipment corroborate lethal ritual.
- Colonial totals for individual events remain contestable.
- The skull rack was not exclusively Tlaloc’s.
- Not every Tlaloc offering involved killing.

### “Paradise,” “heaven,” and Christian translation

“Tlalocan paradise” is a colonial and modern convenience. Tlalocan was desirable and abundant, but admission depended on divine mode of death, not a Christian moral judgment. Scholars increasingly distinguish Indigenous destination systems from European heaven/hell models.

### The Coatlinchan monolith

The approximately 168-ton monolith moved to Mexico City in 1964 remains popularly identified as Tlaloc. Its skirt-like form and water symbolism have led others to identify Chalchiuhtlicue. The monument’s incomplete or damaged state prevents certainty. The debate illustrates how modern national naming can stabilize an ancient ambiguity.

### State of the evidence

Major collections and archives include:

- Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence: *Florentine Codex*.
- Biblioteca Nacional de Antropología e Historia and Museo del Templo Mayor, Mexico City: excavation archives and collections.
- Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City: monumental sculpture and codex-related collections.
- Biblioteca Nacional de España and Real Academia de la Historia, Madrid: Sahaguntine and colonial manuscripts.
- Bibliothèque nationale de France: *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*.
- Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris: *Codex Borbonicus*.
- Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana: *Codex Borgia* and *Vaticanus A/B*.
- Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence: *Codex Magliabechiano*.
- Bodleian Library, Oxford: *Codex Mendoza*.

The destruction of temples and manuscripts after conquest created irrecoverable losses. Surviving colonial texts were produced after epidemic mortality and institutional rupture. Accordingly, a “complete theology of Tlaloc” cannot be reconstructed.
