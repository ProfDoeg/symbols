# Coyolxauhqui: Research Dossier

**Class:** Female *teōtl* (divine/sacred being) in Mexica tradition; a warrior leader and defeated adversary of Huitzilopochtli. “Goddess” is the conventional English classification, though *teōtl* is not an exact synonym for the Western category “god.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** Archaeological images from the Templo Mayor of Mexico-Tenochtitlan, probably beginning with Construction Stage III under Itzcoatl, approximately 1427–1440 CE. Reclining stone figures interpreted as the Centzon Huitznahua and the remains or inferred former position of an associated Coyolxauhqui monument belong to this phase. The earliest securely contextualized surviving Coyolxauhqui image may therefore date to about 1430; the celebrated complete disk belongs to Stage IVb, 1469–1481. No securely identified pre-Mexica or pre-fifteenth-century image, inscription, or text names her. [INAH’s Stage III account](https://arqueologia.inah.gob.mx/publico/especifico.php?id=NTk%3D), [Stage III chronology](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/etapa-constructiva-iii-del-templo-mayor-de-tenochtitlan), [monument study](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/el-monolito-de-coyolxauhqui-investigaciones-recientes)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** fifteenth- or early-sixteenth-century archaeological evidence, or indigenous testimony recorded soon after the conquest.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** colonial Nahua or Spanish accounts that became standard but postdate the functioning cult.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant:** an alternative genealogy, event, or interpretation belonging to a particular source tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** a colonial or modern attempt to organize heterogeneous Nahua divinities and symbols into a unified system.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** an equation with another being or cosmological entity not explicit in the earliest evidence.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from texts, archaeology, iconography, or comparative structure.
- **[MI] Modern invention or retelling:** a demonstrably modern addition, creative reinterpretation, or unsupported claim presented as ancient.
- **[NE] Negative evidence:** a claim or category for which the surviving corpus supplies no explicit evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[EA/LC]** Coyolxauhqui is the female leader who organizes the Centzon Huitznahua against Coatlicue at Coatepec and is killed, decapitated, and dismembered by the newly born Huitzilopochtli. That identity is independently materialized in the fifteenth-century sculptural program of the Templo Mayor and narrated in Book III of the Florentine Codex, compiled in Nahuatl and Spanish under Bernardino de Sahagún during the later sixteenth century. The great disk lay at the foot of Huitzilopochtli’s stair, turning the southern half of the pyramid into Coatepec and the bodies cast down it into repetitions of Coyolxauhqui’s fall. [Digital Florentine Codex, Book III, fol. 1v](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/book/3/folio/1v), [Nahuatl text and Anderson–Dibble translation](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/nahuatl/ReadingCoatlicue.html), [ritual interpretation](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/5174)

### Name and spelling

**[EA/LC]** Classical Nahuatl: **Coyolxāuhqui**, commonly printed without vowel length as **Coyolxauhqui**. Colonial spellings include *Coiolxauhquj*. It is an agentive or possessed-form construction involving *coyōlli*, “bell” or “rattle,” and a form related to face-painting or adornment, conventionally rendered “She Who Is Painted/Adorned with Bells,” “Painted with Bells,” or “Bells-Her-Cheeks.” The cheek disks on her sculptures provide unusually strong iconographic confirmation of the ancient interpretation. Durán glossed the name in 1579 as “la que tiene pintura facial de cascabeles.” The UNAM Gran Diccionario Náhuatl records both Durán’s and the Florentine forms. [Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/coyolxauhqui/194178)

**[MSR]** English translations such as “Golden Bells,” “She of the Bell-Adorned Face,” and “Bells on Her Cheeks” are interpretive variants, not separately attested epithets. *Coyolli* can designate a bell or rattle; *xāuhqui* is grammatically and lexically more difficult than popular word-by-word explanations imply. “Painted with bells” best preserves the compound without pretending that its morphology is undisputed.

### Titles and epithets

**[EA/LC]**

- **īhuēltiuh Huitzilopochtli**, “Huitzilopochtli’s elder sister,” in the Florentine tradition.
- Leader or instigator of the **Centzon Huitznahua**, “Four Hundred Southerners”; “four hundred” functions as an innumerable multitude, not necessarily an exact census.
- Durán and Sahagún describe her through kinship and action rather than through a large litany of cult titles.

**[MSR/SI]**

- “Moon goddess,” “lunar goddess,” and “the Moon” are modern scholarly or museum classifications grounded in the sun–moon reading of the Coatepec battle. They are not secure indigenous epithets for her.
- “Goddess of the Milky Way,” “dark moon,” and “moon’s phases” are speculative modern interpretations, not ancient titles.

### Classification and purported equivalents

**[EA/LC]** The sources treat her as a female sacred being and divine combatant, but also in some migration histories as a defeated leader within a quasi-historical episode. Nahua narrative does not enforce a modern boundary between “historical woman,” ancestor, impersonator, and deity.

**[NE]** No ancient or early-colonial source securely equates Coyolxauhqui with a Greek, Roman, Maya, or other foreign deity. Proposed equivalences with Artemis, Selene, Hecate, or the K’iche’ deity Awilix are modern comparativism, not indigenous syncretism.

**[NE]** She has no securely documented calendrical day-name, patron trecena, sacred animal, separate temple title, named priesthood, consort, or offspring.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaeological priority

**[EA]** Coyolxauhqui’s earliest evidence is material rather than literary. At Stage III of the Templo Mayor, approximately 1430, reclining standard-bearer figures occupied the southern stair zone. Their weapons and gestures have been interpreted as the defeated Centzon Huitznahua; the arrangement presupposes or strongly suggests an associated Coyolxauhqui image. The conclusion is contextual, however: the Stage III Coyolxauhqui is less completely preserved and less securely reconstructed than the Stage IVb disk. [INAH Stage III discussion](https://arqueologia.inah.gob.mx/publico/especifico.php?id=NTk%3D)

**[EA]** The great disk belongs to Stage IVb, reign of Axayacatl, 1469–1481. It therefore predates every surviving alphabetic account. It demonstrates that the dismemberment narrative, distinctive insignia, and placement beneath Huitzilopochtli’s stair were already monumental state theology before the conquest. It does **not**, by itself, identify her as the literal moon.

### Primary textual witnesses

1. **Florentine Codex, Book III, chapters 1–2.**  
   **[LC preserving indigenous testimony]** Nahuatl and Spanish; compiled through Sahagún’s collaboration with Nahua elders and scribes, chiefly in the mid-to-late sixteenth century; surviving manuscript completed around 1577. It provides the fullest birth-and-battle narrative: Coatlicue’s feather conception, Coyolxauhqui’s anger, the Centzon Huitznahua’s ascent, Cuahuitlicac’s warnings, Huitzilopochtli’s armed birth, Coyolxauhqui’s decapitation and fall, and the rout of the Southerners. Anderson and Dibble’s *Florentine Codex, Book 3: The Origin of the Gods* remains the standard complete English translation. [Edition information](https://www.bibliovault.org/BV.book.epl?ISBN=9780874800029)

2. **Primeros Memoriales.**  
   **[LC]** Nahuatl pictorial-alphabetic materials compiled at Tepepulco, approximately 1558–1561, forming an early stage of Sahagún’s project. They supply costumes, divine categories, songs, festivals, and ritual offices relevant to Huitzilopochtli and Panquetzaliztli, but do not preserve as full an independent Coyolxauhqui biography as Book III. They should not be cited as though they were an uncontaminated preconquest manuscript.

3. **Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Libro de los dioses y ritos*.**  
   **[LC/RV]** Spanish, written principally in the 1570s–1581 from indigenous informants and pictorial histories. Durán preserves the Coatepec narrative, identifies the name through facial bells, and describes the Panquetzaliztli ceremonies and disposal of sacrificial bodies. His account belongs to the related “Crónica X” historiographic complex, not to Sahagún’s questionnaire tradition.

4. **Hernando Alvarado Tezozomoc, *Crónica mexicana* and *Crónica mexicáyotl*.**  
   **[LC/RV]** Late sixteenth or early seventeenth century, Spanish and Nahuatl traditions. These historicize Coatepec within Mexica migration and political conflict. In a striking variant, Coyolxauhqui can appear as Huitzilopochtli’s **mother**, not sister; Huitzilopochtli consumes the hearts of Coyolxauhqui and the defeated Huitznahua and thereby becomes a great *tzitzimitl* or formidable supernatural being. The modern critical edition of the *Crónica mexicáyotl* stresses its layered authorship and transmission through Tezozomoc, Chimalpahin, and possibly Alonso Franco. [UNAM critical edition](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/730/cronica_mexicayotl.html), [study of the versional problem](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0071-16752013000100007&script=sci_arttext), [heart-consumption variant](https://korpus21.cmq.edu.mx/index.php/ohtli/article/view/139/263)

5. **Codex Azcatitlan.**  
   **[LC/RV]** A postconquest pictorial history, probably later sixteenth century, representing the Mexica migration and battle at Coatepec. It contributes the historical-pictorial version of the conflict and a calendrical placement, but must not be treated as a preconquest manuscript.

6. **Other migration annals.**  
   **[LC/RV]** The *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, *Leyenda de los Soles*, and related annalistic compilations contextualize Mexica origins, Coatepec, solar eras, and Huitzilopochtli’s cult. They do not all give Coyolxauhqui the same role, and silence in one compilation must not be filled from another.

### Corpus audit: works often cited indiscriminately

**[NE]** The Codex Borgia, Codex Cospi, Codex Vaticanus B, and Codex Fejérváry-Mayer are essential for central Mexican ritual iconography, costume comparison, calendrics, and color conventions. They do not securely name or narrate Coyolxauhqui. Using Borgia-group imagery to reconstruct her colors is comparative analysis, not a direct Coyolxauhqui attestation.

**[NE]** The Codices Borbonicus, Mendoza, Magliabechiano, and Telleriano-Remensis are important for festivals, tribute, divinities, and calendrical systems, but they are not independent full witnesses to the Coatepec myth. A modern database gloss attached to a codex page is not automatically text written on that page.

**[NE]** The *Histoyre du Mechique*, *Cantares Mexicanos*, and surviving ritual songs illuminate Nahua cosmogony and sacred poetry but furnish no comparably explicit, securely attributable Coyolxauhqui cycle. The Sun Stone likewise does not depict a securely identified Coyolxauhqui.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[EA]** Coyolxauhqui’s securely demonstrable function is narrower than modern handbooks suggest: she is the defeated female enemy at Coatepec whose dismembered image structures Huitzilopochtli’s temple and the ritual descent of sacrificed bodies. She embodies rebellion defeated, the enemy rendered disordered, and the paradigmatic corpse beneath Mexica martial sovereignty.

**[MSR]** Political readings treat the myth as imperial ideology: every captive thrown down Huitzilopochtli’s stair becomes Coyolxauhqui, while Tenochtitlan’s enemies become the routed Huitznahua. This interpretation is strongly supported by the monument’s exact location and the ritual described by Sahagún and Durán, although the extent to which worshippers articulated it as a formal “state ideology” is reconstructed.

### The lunar question

**[MSR, widely accepted but contested]** The familiar astronomical reading identifies:

- Huitzilopochtli as the newly risen sun;
- Coyolxauhqui as the moon;
- the Centzon Huitznahua as the innumerable southern stars;
- their defeat as the sun’s daily victory over moon and stars.

The reading has genuine strengths: the number and name of the Huitznahua are celestial; Coyolxauhqui is opposed to a deity with strong solar associations; her round stone, decapitation, pale/yellow body, bells, and repeated defeat invite a lunar interpretation. Patrick Johansson calls her character fundamentally *selenic*. [Johansson’s lunar/placental interpretation](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/3843)

**[EA/NE against certainty]** Neither the Florentine narrative nor the great disk explicitly says “Coyolxauhqui is the moon.” Her disk lacks the most unmistakable central Mexican lunar signs: the moon vessel, water-filled crescent, and rabbit. The round support is architectural and may resemble a shield as readily as the moon. Huitzilopochtli’s solar identity itself is contextual rather than reducible to a simple one-to-one equation.

**Conclusion:** “Lunar being” is a strong and historically influential interpretation, not an uncontested ancient identification. It is safer to say that Coyolxauhqui acquired or expressed lunar meaning within a broader complex of war, dismemberment, celestial opposition, sacrifice, and political conquest.

**[MI]** The popular story that Huitzilopochtli threw her head into the sky and it literally became the moon is not in the fullest Florentine account. It is a modern harmonizing explanation of the lunar theory unless tied to a specific later source.

### The 1978 disk

**[EA]** The disk is an irregular circular block of pale pink andesite of lamprobolite, approximately 3.25 by 3.04 metres, about 30 centimetres thick, and close to eight tonnes. It depicts Coyolxauhqui naked or nearly naked, decapitated and divided into head, trunk, arms, and legs. The pieces are composed around the disk rather than shown in natural anatomical alignment. [Technical monument description](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/el-monolito-de-coyolxauhqui-investigaciones-recientes)

Recognizing features include:

- bell-shaped cheek ornaments and bells in the hair;
- elaborate feathered headdress;
- large ear ornaments;
- knotted or serpent belt carrying a skull;
- feather and down elements associated with the slain or sacrificial body;
- arm and leg joints ending in visible bone forms;
- clawed or monstrous motifs at the severed extremities;
- a dynamic twisting arrangement that makes the body appear to tumble.

**[EA]** Pigment analysis supports a polychrome surface rather than modern bare-stone appearance. Red, blue, yellow/ochre, white, and black were used selectively. Reconstructions made directly from surviving pigment supersede earlier colorizations based primarily on codices.

### The six sculptural manifestations

**[EA/MSR]** Eduardo Matos Moctezuma catalogued six images or fragments under the rubric “the six Coyolxauhquis.” They include complete dismembered bodies, fragmentary reliefs, and the monumental severed head. Their forms vary sharply, while cheek bells and the theme of bodily defeat recur. This variation warns against treating the 1978 disk as the only valid portrait. [Matos Moctezuma, “Las seis Coyolxauhqui”](https://nahuatl.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/view/78267)

**[EA]** The colossal diorite/greenstone head, found in March 1830 near the former Convent of La Concepción and transferred to the National Museum, represents a severed female head with closed or lowered eyes and bell motifs on both cheeks. It is now in the Museo Nacional de Antropología. [Museum catalogue entry](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/detalle_pieza_mes.php?id=191)

### Attributes not established

**[NE]** No primary source securely gives Coyolxauhqui a personal weapon. She leads an armed assault, but modern images assigning her a *macuahuitl*, spear, bow, or lunar magic are reconstructions.

**[NE]** No ancient source establishes silver as her metal, white as her exclusive color, the rabbit as her animal, thirteen as her number, or a lunar phase as her festival time.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

**Florentine genealogy — [LC, dominant modern version].** Coatlicue is mother of Coyolxauhqui, the Centzon Huitznahua, and the miraculously conceived Huitzilopochtli. Coyolxauhqui is Huitzilopochtli’s elder sister and a sister or leading sister among the Huitznahua.

**Tezozomoc/Chimalpahin migration genealogy — [RV].** Coyolxauhqui may be Huitzilopochtli’s mother. The episode is embedded more explicitly in migration conflict, and Huitzilopochtli’s extraction or eating of enemy hearts magnifies his supernatural power. This is not a scribal curiosity to be silently corrected to the Florentine version; it preserves a substantially different organization of kinship and authority. [Critical discussion of female authority and variant genealogy](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A4364C9130670CF8B81B6244D4508A3E/S0018246X17000474a.pdf/women_of_discord_female_power_in_aztec_thought.pdf)

**[MI]** Mixcoatl is frequently supplied online as Coyolxauhqui’s father. The Florentine Coatepec story does not name a father or consort of Coatlicue. This genealogy is a modern systematization formed by joining separate divine traditions.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Coatlicue — [LC].** Coyolxauhqui, angered by Coatlicue’s unexplained pregnancy, persuades her brothers to kill their mother; Coatlicue becomes frightened, but Huitzilopochtli reassures her from the womb. *Florentine Codex*, Book III, compiled c. 1550s–1577.
- **Huitzilopochtli — [EA/LC].** Coyolxauhqui leads the assault that precipitates his birth; he strikes her with Xiuhcoatl, decapitates her, and casts her body down Coatepec, where it breaks apart. Materially staged at the Templo Mayor by c. 1430 and especially in Stage IVb; textually preserved in the *Florentine Codex*.
- **Huitzilopochtli — [RV].** In Tezozomoc/Chimalpahin traditions, she can be his mother and a rebel leader; he kills her and consumes the hearts of the defeated. Late-sixteenth-/early-seventeenth-century migration histories.
- **Centzon Huitznahua — [LC].** Coyolxauhqui addresses and incites her four hundred brothers, leads their ascent of Coatepec, and is defeated with them. *Florentine Codex*, Book III.
- **Cuahuitlicac/Quauhtlicac — [LC].** A member of the Huitznahua defects from the conspiracy and reports the attackers’ successive positions to Huitzilopochtli. No surviving dialogue between him and Coyolxauhqui is recorded; their only secure relation is membership on opposing sides of her attack.
- **Xiuhcoatl — [LC, classification disputed].** Huitzilopochtli wields the “Turquoise/Fire Serpent” against Coyolxauhqui. It may be classified as an animated divine weapon, serpent being, or insignia rather than an autonomous god. Coyolxauhqui is its target, not its possessor.
- **Tlaloc — [NE].** Coyolxauhqui’s monument occupied the Huitzilopochtli half of the dual Templo Mayor, whose northern shrine belonged to Tlaloc. Architectural co-presence is not a narrated interaction.
- **Tlaltecuhtli — [NE/MSR].** Both appear as monumental, splayed female bodies in the sacred precinct and both participate in sacrificial/earth imagery, but no primary source records a meeting, kinship, fusion, or combat between them.
- **Metztli — [NE/SI].** Metztli is explicitly a moon deity or lunar personification in Nahua traditions. No early source securely identifies Metztli and Coyolxauhqui as the same being.
- **Coatlicue–Malinalxoch relation — [NE].** Malinalxoch is another dangerous female relation of Huitzilopochtli in Mexica migration narratives. Structural comparison is productive, but no text securely makes her another name or manifestation of Coyolxauhqui.

No consort, child, servant, mount, or stable divine ally of Coyolxauhqui is documented.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The birth of Huitzilopochtli and battle of Coatepec

**[LC, fullest canonical form]** Coatlicue performs penance by sweeping on Coatepec near Tollan. A ball of fine feathers descends; she picks it up and places it against her belly or under her clothing. When it disappears, she discovers that she is pregnant.

Coyolxauhqui is enraged at what she regards as dishonor. She tells the Centzon Huitznahua that their mother has disgraced them and should be killed. They arm themselves and prepare to ascend the mountain. Coatlicue is terrified, but the unborn Huitzilopochtli speaks from within her, telling her not to fear.

Cuahuitlicac defects and becomes Huitzilopochtli’s informer. At repeated intervals Huitzilopochtli asks where the attackers are. Cuahuitlicac reports their progress through named stations—Tzompantitlan, Coaxalpan, Apetlac, Tlatlacapan—until Coyolxauhqui emerges at their head. These names also designate or evoke architectural levels of the Templo Mayor, which makes the narrated mountain and the ritual pyramid mutually interpretive. [Nahuatl passage and translation](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/3843)

Huitzilopochtli is born fully armed or immediately assumes his insignia. Tochancalqui ignites or activates Xiuhcoatl at his order. Huitzilopochtli strikes Coyolxauhqui, cuts off her head, and sends her body falling down Coatepec. It breaks apart below. He then pursues the Huitznahua around the mountain, killing most and driving a remnant southward.

**[EA]** The disk confirms that decapitation, dismemberment, and location below Huitzilopochtli were not inventions of Sahagún’s colonial manuscript. The architecture had already made the myth public and repeatable.

**[MSR] Functions:**

- celestial: sunrise defeats moon and stars;
- sacrificial: the captive’s body repeats Coyolxauhqui’s fall;
- political: Tenochtitlan’s patron annihilates rebels and foreign enemies;
- migratory: internal Mexica division at Coatepec is translated into divine history;
- architectural: the Templo Mayor becomes Coatepec.

No one interpretation exhausts the evidence.

### B. The historicized migration conflict

**[RV]** In the annalistic and Crónica X-related traditions, the Coatepec episode is less a timeless celestial drama and more a conflict occurring during Mexica migration. Coyolxauhqui and the Huitznahua can represent a dissident faction resisting Huitzilopochtli’s authority. Their defeat explains political separation and the supremacy of the faction that continued toward Tenochtitlan.

**[MSR]** Some historians understand the divine combat as mythologized memory of an actual factional struggle, perhaps involving competing ritual specialists or leaders. No independent record identifies a historical Coyolxauhqui, however. “Historical woman later deified” remains a reconstruction, not a demonstrated biography.

### C. Heart consumption

**[RV]** Tezozomoc’s traditions say Huitzilopochtli consumes the hearts of Coyolxauhqui and the Huitznahua and becomes a powerful sorcerous or terrifying being. This feature is absent from the standard Florentine telling. It brings the myth closer to ritual theories in which consuming or offering a heart transfers *tonalli* or vital force, but that explanation is scholarly.

### D. The moon and stars narrative

**[MSR/MI boundary]** The compact modern version—“the sun god killed the moon goddess and scattered the stars every dawn”—is an astronomical condensation of the evidence. It is a defensible interpretation when labeled as such. It becomes modern invention when presented as a verbatim ancient text, especially when it adds that Coatlicue asked Huitzilopochtli to place Coyolxauhqui’s head in the heavens so she could see her daughter.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult site

**[EA]** The only securely demonstrated monumental focus is the southern, Huitzilopochtli half of the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan. Coyolxauhqui’s disk lay on the platform at the base of its stair, aligned with the shrine’s central axis. She did not occupy the summit as a coequal temple owner; her ritual place was below, defeated.

**[EA/MSR]** Successive temple enlargements appear to have renewed the Coatepec program with new Coyolxauhqui images. The archaeological sequence suggests repeated official investment rather than a single isolated sculpture.

### Panquetzaliztli and human sacrifice

**[LC corroborated materially in general]** Panquetzaliztli, “Raising of Banners,” was the principal annual veintena festival of Huitzilopochtli. Captives and slaves were taken up the stair, sacrificed at the summit by heart extraction, and their bodies thrown down. At the foot they reached the Coyolxauhqui zone and could be decapitated or dismembered by captors. This was a ritual reenactment of Huitzilopochtli’s treatment of Coyolxauhqui, not a sacrifice offered primarily to her. [Matos Moctezuma’s account](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/5174)

**[EA]** The rolling of bodies is thus not merely a sensational Spanish allegation. Its exact architectural logic is supported by the disk’s placement, impact damage, sacrificial facilities, human remains, and broader osteological record. Archaeology cannot establish that every body struck the disk, nor can it verify colonial numerical totals.

**[EA]** Excavations begun in 2015 at Guatemala 24 identified remains of the Huei Tzompantli and a skull tower described in colonial accounts. Work in 2015–2017 established a large purpose-built structure holding skulls of sacrificed persons; women and children were present alongside adult men. These finds corroborate the scale and institutional reality of human sacrifice, but they do not by themselves prove that every individual passed over Coyolxauhqui’s stone or died during Panquetzaliztli. [INAH excavation report](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/el-huei-tzompantli-de-tenochtitlan), [INAH discussion of women and children](https://www.inah.gob.mx/index.php?Itemid=329&catid=38&id=995%3Ael-pensamiento-mexica&option=com_content&view=article), [Science report](https://doi.org/doi%3A10.1126/science.aau5404)

### Priesthood, offerings, and ordinary devotion

**[NE]** No priestly office securely named “priest of Coyolxauhqui” is known. The officiants implicated in her ritual environment served Huitzilopochtli and the state festival.

**[NE]** No surviving prayer, hymn addressed directly to Coyolxauhqui, household altar, pilgrimage itinerary, oracle, initiation, marriage rite, healing rite, or divination practice is securely attested.

**[EA/MSR]** Offerings around her monument belong to the Templo Mayor’s dedicatory and sacrificial economy. Association in context does not automatically mean that every deposited object was “offered to Coyolxauhqui.”

**[NE]** No securely identified theophoric personal names demonstrate widespread domestic devotion to her.

### Politics and economics

**[MSR]** The monument translated military victory into cosmological inevitability: Mexica conquerors occupied Huitzilopochtli’s position, while captives occupied Coyolxauhqui’s. Tribute supported the temple, festivals, priests, armies, and captive-taking warfare. The theological and economic systems were connected, although it would be reductive to treat the myth as propaganda and nothing else.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before c. 1427

**[NE]** No secure Coyolxauhqui attestation. Earlier central Mexican female lunar, earth, death, or warrior images cannot be renamed Coyolxauhqui without diagnostic bells or contextual evidence.

### c. 1427–1440: emergence in imperial monumental form

**[EA]** Stage III, associated with Itzcoatl’s reign, presents the earliest reconstructable Coatepec sculptural program. This coincides with Tenochtitlan’s victory over Azcapotzalco and emergence as an imperial capital. Some scholars therefore locate the myth’s political monumentalization—though not necessarily its oral invention—in this period.

### 1440–1481: repeated monumentalization and the great disk

**[EA]** Further enlargements under Motecuhzoma I and Axayacatl renewed the southern stair program. The Stage IVb disk, 1469–1481, is the culmination of this surviving sequence. A proposed connection with the Tenochca–Tlatelolca civil war of 1473 is plausible: Coyolxauhqui’s failed rebellion could encode the defeat of Tlatelolco. But the monument’s dating is broad enough that the exact political occasion remains debated.

### 1481–1521: mature state ritual

**[EA/LC]** Panquetzaliztli, captive sacrifice, and the Coatepec staging formed part of the capital’s central ceremonial system. Later Coyolxauhqui sculptures show continued renewal through the final temple phases.

### 1521–1570s: destruction and documentary preservation

**[EA]** Spanish and Indigenous allied forces conquered Tenochtitlan in 1521. The Templo Mayor was demolished, its cult terminated, and colonial buildings erected over the precinct. Missionization criminalized and displaced public Mexica rites. Coyolxauhqui’s images were buried, broken, reused, or left below the colonial city. [INAH account of destruction and spiritual conquest](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5284), [archaeological site history](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/4463)

**[LC]** Indigenous elders, painters, grammarians, and Christian chroniclers nevertheless recorded the myth. This preservation is indispensable but mediated by colonial institutions, Christian categories, translation, censorship, and changing Indigenous memory.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

**[NE]** There is no demonstrated continuous public Coyolxauhqui cult. Her story survived in manuscripts rather than a traceable devotional community. Claims of an unbroken clandestine priesthood are unsupported.

### 1830–1977: antiquarian recovery

**[EA]** The colossal severed head discovered in 1830 was identified through its cheek ornaments and entered the National Museum. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scholars—especially Eduard Seler and later Mexican archaeologists—developed the lunar interpretation and compared her with other decapitated female beings.

### 1978 onward: archaeological reconstruction

**[EA]** On 21 February 1978 electrical workers from the Compañía de Luz y Fuerza uncovered the great disk beneath Guatemala Street. Archaeologists exposed and identified it; its location fixed the southern axis of the Templo Mayor. The find triggered the large-scale Proyecto Templo Mayor, directed initially by Eduardo Matos Moctezuma. Excavations proceeded from 1978 to 1982 and in subsequent seasons. [Proyecto Templo Mayor history](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/proyecto-templo-mayor), [INAH 45th-anniversary account](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/coyolxauhqui-a-45-anos-de-su-descubrimiento-una-memoria-del-monumento-y-del-templo-mayor)

The discovery transformed Coyolxauhqui from a figure known chiefly to specialists into an internationally recognizable image of Mexica religion and Mexican archaeology.

### 2006 and afterward

**[EA/MSR]** Discovery of the enormous Tlaltecuhtli monolith on 2 October 2006 opened a productive comparison between two monumental female bodies in the sacred precinct. Tlaltecuhtli is larger and belongs to a royal funerary/offering context; Coyolxauhqui belongs beneath Huitzilopochtli’s stair. They should not be fused into one “earth-moon goddess.”

### Present

**[MI/modern revival]** Coyolxauhqui is invoked in some Mexicayotl, Chicano/Xicano spiritual, feminist, Pagan, and personal devotional practices. These are real contemporary religious phenomena, but documentary evidence does not establish institutional continuity from the preconquest cult. Scale, organization, and doctrine vary, and no central authority defines modern worship.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Archaeological and national symbol

**[MSR/modern reception]** The 1978 discovery became a symbol of Mexico’s recovery of an Indigenous urban past beneath the colonial capital. The government authorized demolition or relocation of later buildings to expose the sacred precinct. The disk now anchors the Museo del Templo Mayor, while a replica marks its original position.

### Feminist and Chicana/Xicana re-readings

**[MI as creative reinterpretation, not as falsehood]** Cherríe Moraga, Ana Castillo, Gloria Anzaldúa, Alicia Gaspar de Alba, and numerous artists repositioned Coyolxauhqui from defeated rebel to emblem of women fragmented by patriarchal, colonial, racial, sexual, and national violence.

Gloria Anzaldúa’s **Coyolxauhqui imperative** describes the continuing work of dismemberment and re-membering: trauma breaks the self or community apart, and healing assembles the pieces differently rather than restoring an imagined untouched original. The formulation is best known from writings collected posthumously in *Light in the Dark/Luz en lo Oscuro* (2015), though its imagery develops across Anzaldúa’s earlier work. It is a modern philosophical use of the monolith, not a recovered Mexica doctrine. [Modern scholarly discussion](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11013-025-09932-5)

**[MI]** Feminist narratives sometimes make Coyolxauhqui an innocent daughter murdered solely because Huitzilopochtli feared female power. This is a deliberate counter-reading of a source tradition that portrays her as organizing matricide. It should be credited as modern political mythology rather than substituted for the Florentine episode.

### Mexican feminist spirituality

**[MI]** Feminist spiritual collectives in Mexico City during the 1980s adopted Coyolxauhqui and other Indigenous female beings within ritual, artistic, and consciousness-raising work. These movements reflect late-twentieth-century feminist spirituality, not continuous preconquest ceremony. [Study of *Cuarto Creciente*, 1984–1988](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-26202026000100181&script=sci_arttext)

### Literature, visual art, and performance

**[modern reception]** Coyolxauhqui appears in poetry, murals, installations, performance art, decolonial theory, and works addressing femicide, especially the murdered women of Ciudad Juárez. Artists exploit the disk’s simultaneously violent and formally integrated body: dismemberment becomes a language for damaged communities, while the circular composition becomes reassembly.

María Cristina Mena’s “The Birth of the God of War” (1914) is an early English-language literary retelling. Later Chicana work gives Coyolxauhqui political agency absent from museum shorthand. [Literary-historical study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/latinx-literature-in-transition-18481992/brown-modernism-from-maria-cristina-mena-to-gloria-anzaldua/C5D40073B93CA7130A08F47AADD1B0FC)

### Occult and New Age claims

**[NE/MI]** No substantial Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or classical ceremonial-magic tradition concerning Coyolxauhqui is documented. Claims assigning her planetary seals, “dark goddess” correspondences, tarot cards, crystals, chakra functions, or spells are modern constructions. Personal practices may be sincere, but they are not preconquest survivals.

### Popular culture

**[MI]** Games, comics, online myth lists, fantasy art, and commercial merchandise commonly render her as an intact silver-armored moon warrior carrying a bladed weapon. That form combines the lunar hypothesis with modern fantasy conventions. The ancient monumental image instead emphasizes her already defeated, dismembered body.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### From Seler to iconographic systematization

Eduard Seler’s studies of Mexican codices helped establish the astronomical reading in which Huitzilopochtli is sun, Coyolxauhqui moon, and Huitznahua stars. **[LS]** This comparative framework was enormously influential but often encouraged scholars to turn polyvalent beings into fixed celestial equivalents.

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology of Late Postclassic central Mexican religion classified deities through “theme complexes,” a more careful approach than assuming a single Greco-Roman-style portfolio. Coyolxauhqui belongs principally to the Huitzilopochtli–Coatepec complex, even when lunar meaning is accepted.

### Matos Moctezuma and the Templo Mayor model

Eduardo Matos Moctezuma’s decisive contribution was archaeological: the temple itself re-created Coatepec. The disk’s position showed that text, architecture, sacrifice, and imperial power formed one program. His “six Coyolxauhquis” established image variation across temple phases rather than a single canonical statue.

Leonardo López Luján and collaborators refined the construction sequence, offerings, material analyses, and sacred-mountain interpretation. Excavation around the monolith found a platform aligned through its center with the Huitzilopochtli shrine and operating during Stages IV–IVb. [Excavation report](https://www.mesoweb.com/es/articulos/sub/Al_pie_del_Templo_Mayor.pdf)

### Moon versus nonexclusive identity

**Pro-lunar evidence [MSR]:**

- opposition to solar Huitzilopochtli;
- celestial Huitznahua;
- circular monument and decapitated-head imagery;
- later central Mexican associations between female decapitation and lunar phases;
- repeated scholarly and Indigenous-museum reception.

**Cautions [EA/NE]:**

- no explicit “she is the moon” statement in the fullest narrative;
- no rabbit, lunar vessel, or unmistakable moon glyph on the disk;
- Coyolxauhqui’s primary monumental function is political-sacrificial;
- central Mexican religion permits overlapping identities rather than exclusive equations.

The best-supported conclusion is not “she was definitely not lunar,” but “lunar meaning is interpretive and nonexclusive.”

### Placenta and birth

Patrick Johansson proposes that the round, dismembered Coyolxauhqui below the newborn Huitzilopochtli also signifies an expelled placenta: the violent birth at the summit produces both victorious child and discarded bodily remainder. **[MSR]** This reading is linguistically and visually suggestive but is not stated by the Florentine text.

### Historical faction theory

Some scholars interpret Coyolxauhqui as the mythologized leader of a dissident Mexica group defeated at migration-era Coatepec. **[MSR]** The annalistic framing supports factional memory; lack of independent contemporary evidence prevents identification of a historical individual.

### 1473 political allegory

The great disk may encode Axayacatl’s defeat of Tlatelolco in 1473, transforming a civil war into a renewed Coatepec. **[MSR, disputed]** Stage IVb chronology and the theme of rebellious kin support the argument. The sculpture bears no surviving inscription that explicitly names Tlatelolco or the 1473 war.

### Gender and female power

Recent historiography asks why Mexica political thought repeatedly represents dangerous women—Coyolxauhqui, Malinalxoch, and others—as threats to migration and male sovereignty. One school reads these narratives as ideological containment of female authority; another warns that “patriarchy defeats goddess” can flatten distinct Nahua concepts of kinship, office, gender complementarity, and sacred danger. Both approaches agree that modern neutral-sounding summaries often reproduce the victor’s point of view.

### Human sacrifice

Spanish reports were once dismissed wholesale by some popular revisionists as colonial propaganda. The Templo Mayor sacrificial contexts and Huei Tzompantli excavations make denial of institutional human sacrifice untenable. Conversely, colonial numerical claims and descriptions of particular ceremonies still require source criticism. The archaeology confirms practice and scale, not every chronicler’s number.

### Lost and surviving evidence

The Templo Mayor’s upper stages and cult images were destroyed after 1521. Surviving evidence is therefore structurally biased toward buried foundations, reused stone, late temple phases, and colonial recollection. The Florentine Codex survives in Florence’s Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana; the *Primeros Memoriales* in Madrid; major sculpture is held by the Museo del Templo Mayor and Museo Nacional de Antropología. Numerous pictorial manuscripts are dispersed among European and Mexican collections.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before c. 1427 — [NE]:** No securely identified Coyolxauhqui attestation.
- **c. 1427–1440 — [EA]:** Templo Mayor Stage III under Itzcoatl; Coatepec-related sculptures provide the earliest reconstructable monumental program.
- **c. 1440–1469 — [EA/MSR]:** Successive temple renewal maintains the Huitzilopochtli–Coyolxauhqui arrangement.
- **1469–1481 — [EA]:** Stage IVb under Axayacatl; creation and installation of the great andesite disk.
- **1473 — [MSR]:** Possible political association with Axayacatl’s conquest of Tlatelolco; not explicit on the monument.
- **Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century — [EA]:** Further Coyolxauhqui sculptures accompany later Templo Mayor enlargements.
- **1519 — [EA]:** Spanish entry into Tenochtitlan; the Templo Mayor cult remains active.
- **1521 — [EA]:** Fall of Tenochtitlan; destruction and burial of the sacred precinct; termination of state cult.
- **c. 1558–1561 — [LC]:** Sahagún’s Tepepulco investigations produce the *Primeros Memoriales*.
- **c. 1550s–1577 — [LC]:** Compilation and completion of the Florentine Codex; fullest surviving Coatepec narrative.
- **1570s–1581 — [LC/RV]:** Durán writes his histories and ritual accounts.
- **Late sixteenth–early seventeenth century — [LC/RV]:** Tezozomoc/Chimalpahin traditions preserve alternative historical and genealogical versions.
- **March 1830 — [EA]:** Colossal severed head found near the Convent of La Concepción and transferred to the National Museum.
- **Late nineteenth–early twentieth century — [LS]:** Seler and successors consolidate the lunar/celestial interpretation.
- **1971 — [MSR]:** Nicholson publishes his influential typological study of central Mexican religion.
- **21 February 1978 — [EA]:** Electrical workers uncover the great Coyolxauhqui disk.
- **1978–1982 — [EA]:** First major Proyecto Templo Mayor excavation campaign under Matos Moctezuma.
- **1987 — [modern reception]:** Museo del Templo Mayor opens.
- **1991 — [MSR]:** Matos Moctezuma publishes “Las seis Coyolxauhqui.”
- **2006 — [EA]:** Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered, expanding comparative study of monumental female bodies in the precinct.
- **2007 onward — [EA/MSR]:** Direct pigment study produces a more evidence-based reconstruction of the disk’s colors.
- **2015–2017 — [EA]:** Huei Tzompantli and skull-tower excavations materially confirm the capital’s large-scale sacrificial infrastructure.
- **2015 — [MI/modern theory]:** Anzaldúa’s posthumous *Light in the Dark* gives the Coyolxauhqui imperative its most influential published formulation.
- **2023 — [modern reception]:** Forty-fifth-anniversary exhibitions and publications reassess the monolith, excavation, and public memory.
- **Present — [modern reception]:** Coyolxauhqui functions simultaneously as archaeological object, contested lunar being, Chicana/Xicana feminist figure, decolonial metaphor, and occasionally revived devotional power. No continuous cult from 1521 is demonstrable.
