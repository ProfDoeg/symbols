# Chantico: Research Dossier

**Class:** female *teotl*—a Nahua divine power conventionally translated “goddess.” The feminine classification is secure in Sahagún’s Spanish description and in female clothing depicted in the *Primeros Memoriales*, although one colonial glossator of the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* uses masculine language.

**Earliest secure named attestation:** approximately **1554–1561 CE**. The name and image occur independently in the colonial *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, whose paintings are generally dated about 1554–1555, and in the Nahuatl *Primeros Memoriales*, compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators at Tepepulco in 1559–1561. The precise priority between these witnesses cannot be established. Earlier-looking images in the *Codex Borbonicus* and Borgia-group manuscripts may preserve pre-Conquest iconography, but their identification as Chantico depends on comparison with named colonial sources; they therefore are not earlier secure written attestations.

**Core documentary profile:** Chantico is the divine power of fire situated in or constituting the household, patron of a Tenochtitlan priestly establishment and of Xochimilcan lapidaries, and ruler of the trecena beginning 1 Wind. Her calendrical name is 9 Dog. The story that she violated a fast, ate roasted fish, and was changed into a dog by Tonacatecuhtli is colonial-era canonical material from the *Telleriano-Remensis* tradition. Her supposed marriage to Xiuhtecuhtli, patronage of goldsmiths’ furnaces, rule over volcanoes, and motherhood of four color-coded fire gods are not established by the principal sixteenth-century sources examined here.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Early attested:** securely documented in a sixteenth-century Indigenous or Indigenous-informed source, or in a plausibly pre-Conquest pictorial tradition.
- **[CE] Colonial elaboration:** a sixteenth- or seventeenth-century account whose surviving form postdates the conquest.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** evidence localized to Xochimilco, Tlatelolco, Tepepulco, Tlaxcala, Puebla, or another particular community.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an identification or synthesis introduced by colonial commentators or modern scholars.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification:** an explicit or proposed fusion with another divine figure.
- **[MI] Modern inference or reconstruction:** plausible but not stated directly by the primary evidence.
- **[MN] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**
- **[AE] Absence of evidence:** a claim sought but not found in the relevant corpus.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Chantico

**[EA]** The normal spelling is **Chantico**, occasionally modernized or Hispanized as **Chántico**, **Xantico**, or **Cantico**. The name is usually glossed “in the house,” “at home,” or, less literally, “she who dwells in the house.” It contains *chan-* or *chantli*, “home/dwelling,” and the locative *-co*, “in/at.” The intervening *-ti-* is a real linguistic difficulty: the *Primeros Memoriales* editors note that ordinary Classical Nahuatl “in the house” would be *chanco*. They suggest that *ti* may be a nonsemantic ligature, an archaic locative feature, or possibly evidence of borrowing. “She who dwells in the house” is thus a useful semantic gloss, not an uncontested word-for-word derivation. The surviving Nahuatl name is written in the Latin alphabet; no securely phonetic pre-Conquest glyph spelling of the goddess’s name is known. The lexical evidence is summarized by the [Nahuatl Dictionary](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/chantico).

The domestic-fire interpretation is consistent with both the name and the colonial source tradition, but it should not be expanded automatically to every kind of fire. “Hearth fire” is secure; “volcano goddess” is common in modern reference works but not securely stated in the main sixteenth-century passages.

### Cuaxolotl, Quaxolotl, or Cuauhxolotl

**[CE/LS]** The *Telleriano-Remensis* calls the trecena ruler **Chantico o Cuaxolotl**, explaining that the latter name arose because she bore Xolotl’s adornments on her head. The manuscript’s Spanish is approximately: “*Chantico, o Cuaxolotl, que es lo mismo porque llevaba los adornos de Xólotl en su cabeza*.” The form has been parsed as *cuā-xōlōtl*, “Xolotl-head,” but variant spellings permit competing analyses, including associations with “split” or “double” head. The explanation is a colonial gloss, not an independently preserved pre-Conquest etymological treatise.

Modern writers often combine the names as **Chantico-Cuaxolotl**. That compound is useful as a source label but can conceal a historical problem: the sacred-precinct list distinguishes a temple of Quaxolotl-Chantico from Chantico’s *calmecac*, while Torquemada’s story speaks of sacrificial impersonators of both Chantico and Cohuaxolotl. The two names may consequently function sometimes as an equation and sometimes as distinguishable cult personae.

### Chiconahui Itzcuintli

**[EA]** **Chicnāhui Itzcuintli**, “9 Dog,” is Chantico’s calendrical name. It appears in the *Telleriano-Remensis/Vaticanus A* tradition and is connected by Sahagún’s Book 9 with the Xochimilcan festival of precious-stone workers. This name explains her repeated canine associations more securely than generalized claims that “the dog was her sacred animal.”

### Papaloxaual and Tlappapalo

**[EA/RV]** In the *Florentine Codex*, Book 9, chapter 17, the Xochimilcan lapidaries invoke patron deities including Chantico under the names **Papaloxaual/Papaloxāhual**, approximately “butterfly face-paint,” and **Tlappapalo/Tlāppapālō**, “red butterfly.” These names belong to the occupational and Xochimilcan context. They should not automatically be treated as universal titles across central Mexico. The relevant synthesis and source references are presented in UNAM’s [Tlachia record for *Primeros Memoriales* folio 266v](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/matritenses/RP_266v).

### “Yellow Woman”

**[CE]** The *Telleriano-Remensis* gloss calls the trecena lord *señor del chite*, interpreted by later commentators as “yellow woman.” The wording is corrupt or difficult, and the manuscript also employs masculine pronouns. It is better treated as evidence of colonial glossarial instability than as a secure independent epithet.

### Alleged titles not securely attested

- **“Lady of jewels” or “Lady of precious things” [MI/MN]:** an explanatory modern title derived from her connection with lapidaries, not a securely quoted Nahuatl epithet.
- **“Goddess of wealth” [MN]:** a commercial reinterpretation.
- **“Goddess of the heart” [MN]:** probably generated by confusion between “hearth” and “heart.”
- **“Goddess of volcanoes” [MI]:** inferable from fire symbolism but not clearly stated in the principal Chantico passages.
- **“Consort of Xiuhtecuhtli” [MN]:** not established in the primary corpus reviewed here.
- **Mother of blue, yellow, white, and red fire gods [MN]:** no supporting passage was located in Sahagún, Durán, the *Telleriano-Remensis*, or the named narrative sources.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Codex Telleriano-Remensis, folio 21v

**[EA/CE]** The earliest securely named pictorial witness is folio 21v of the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, a ritual-calendrical and historical manuscript painted probably around 1554–1555 and annotated through 1563. It was likely produced through several Indigenous painter-scribes and colonial annotators in Mexico-Tenochtitlan and Puebla. The manuscript is now BnF, Département des Manuscrits, Mexicain 385. UNAM’s codicological summary dates the paintings to approximately 1554–1555 and the annotations to the mid-1550s–1563; its last recorded date is 1563 ([UNAM Tlachia study](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/telleriano/estudio); [UNAM Wiki Filología](https://www.iifilologicas.unam.mx/wikfil/index.php/Telleriano-Remensis%2C_C%C3%B3dice)).

Folio 21v identifies Chantico/Cuaxolotl as ruler of the eighteenth trecena, beginning **1 Wind**, and gives her calendrical association with **9 Dog**. Its Spanish gloss contains the fast-breaking myth. This witness establishes by the mid-sixteenth century:

- the name Chantico;
- the alternate name Cuaxolotl;
- trecena rulership;
- canine symbolism;
- a story of roasted fish, smoke, divine anger, and transformation;
- a richly armed and adorned image associated with fire and war.

It does **not** establish a genealogy, marriage to Xiuhtecuhtli, universal patronage of metalworking, or a continuous modern cult.

### Primeros Memoriales, folio 266v

**[EA]** Sahagún and Nahua elders, scribes, and artists compiled the *Primeros Memoriales* at Tepepulco in 1559–1561, following Sahagún’s 1558 commission. The manuscript is now divided between Madrid’s Palacio Real and Real Academia de la Historia collections. The BnF catalog dates the work’s inception to 1558, while the University of Oklahoma Press describes the trilingual collaborative work of 1559–1561 ([BnF catalog](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb13493621g); [University of Oklahoma Press](https://www.oupress.com/9780806116884/primeros-memoriales-part-1/)).

Folio 266v gives **“Chantico yn inechichiuh”**, “the array/adornment of Chantico,” followed by a compact Nahuatl costume description. Sullivan’s translation records:

- rubber paint or a rubber bulge on the lips;
- half the face painted red;
- a *tlazolxochitl* headcloth or headdress;
- gold earplugs;
- a *meyotli* or radiant device on the back;
- a flower-patterned shift;
- an eagle-feather mosaic shield;
- a feathered staff covered with an obsidian-point design;
- white skirt, bells, and white sandals.

The text establishes a female-presenting deity with martial, fiery, solar, and precious-material regalia. It does not narrate her myths or identify parents, spouse, or children.

### Florentine Codex

**[EA/CE]** The *Florentine Codex* was completed in 1577 at the Colegio de Santa Cruz in Tlatelolco by Sahagún and Nahua authors, artists, and informants; the surviving manuscript is Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, MSS Med. Palat. 218–220. It contains parallel Nahuatl and Spanish columns and nearly 2,500 illustrations ([Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/)).

Chantico appears in two especially important contexts:

1. **Book 2, appendix: the sacred precinct.** The **Tetlanman Calmecac**, twenty-seventh building in Sahagún’s list, housed the fire priest and penitent attached to Chantico; service continued night and day. The twenty-ninth building, **Tetlanman**, was dedicated to **Quaxolotl Chantico**. Captives were killed there when the sign **1 Flower** ruled. These are distinct entries and should not be collapsed.

2. **Book 9, chapter 17: lapidaries.** Xochimilcan workers of precious stones honored deities including Chantico/9 Dog, Papaloxaual, and Tlappapalo. This is the best evidence for her artisanal patronage. It identifies **lapidaries**, not goldsmiths as such.

The [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_10096_001/?st=gallery) and Getty’s digital edition preserve access to the manuscript.

### Codex Borbonicus

**[EA?]** Page 18 depicts the patron of the trecena 1 Wind in imagery identified comparatively as Chantico. The manuscript may have been painted immediately before or shortly after the conquest; its dating remains debated. Material analysis has been conducted under the French Ministry of Culture’s [Codex Borbonicus project](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/thematiques/sciences-du-patrimoine/thematiques-de-recherche/etude-des-materiaux/L-examen-du-codex-Borbonicus).

Because the image is not accompanied by a contemporary alphabetic label “Chantico,” the identification depends on the matching trecena position and attributes in named manuscripts. It is strong but not equivalent to an inscribed name.

### Borgia-group manuscripts

**[EA?/LS]** The *Codex Borgia*, *Codex Vaticanus B*, and *Codex Fejérváry-Mayer* contain figures that Seler and later scholars identify as Chantico through trecena placement, fire coloration, facial paint, nose ornaments, dress, and the *atl-tlachinolli* sign. The *Borgia* is a divinatory manuscript from central or south-central Mexico, probably produced in the Late Postclassic or around the conquest. Its exact place, date, and reading remain debated.

The trecena series places Chantico with **1 Wind**. Other Borgia-group scenes sometimes identified as manifestations or emblems of her—such as a raptor foot on a blood vessel—are interpretive rather than explicitly named. Boone’s method in *Cycles of Time and Meaning* emphasizes that these books organize divinatory time rather than narrate a single mythic biography.

### Tonalamatl Aubin

**[CE?]** Its eighteenth surviving plate presents Chantico as trecena patron. Its date and authenticity are unusually contested: physical format and style could support a pre-Conquest or early colonial production, but its documented appearance in Boturini’s collection has also led to proposals that it is an eighteenth-century copy or fabrication based on a Borbonicus-like exemplar. UNAM consequently lists its date as undetermined ([UNAM Wiki Filología](https://www.iifilologicas.unam.mx/wikfil/index.php/Aubin%2C_Tonalamatl)).

### Codex Vaticanus A / Codex Ríos

**[CE]** This later sixteenth-century manuscript closely parallels the *Telleriano-Remensis* but adds Italian commentary. Its Chantico material belongs to the same general colonial interpretive family and is not an independent pre-Conquest narration.

### Torquemada, *Monarquía indiana*

**[CE]** Juan de Torquemada’s work, first printed in 1615, recounts events preceding the Tenochtitlan–Tlatelolco war of 1473. Moquihuix conducted a solemn sacrifice at Zacahuitzco and, ten days into Tecuilhuitl, captives impersonating **Chantico and Cohuaxolotl** were killed after fasting and ritual songs. Xavier Noguez and Amanda Uribe Cortés reproduce and analyze the passage in [*Arqueología Mexicana*](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/chantico-cuaxolotl). It is late testimony, probably dependent on earlier traditions, not a contemporary fifteenth-century record.

### Durán

**[AE/LS]** Diego Durán does not clearly name Chantico in his *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme* or *Book of the Gods and Rites*. He describes **Cihuacoatl** as a patron of Xochimilco. Eduard Seler proposed that Durán had confused Cihuacoatl with Chantico; H. B. Nicholson considered that inference doubtful. Durán therefore cannot be cited straightforwardly as a primary Chantico witness.

### Named corpora in which no secure Chantico episode was established

**[AE]** No secure named Chantico passage was identified in the *Codex Mendoza*, *Codex Magliabechiano*, *Leyenda de los Soles*, *Histoyre du Mechique*, *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, or *Cantares Mexicanos*. These sources remain fundamental for wider Nahua religion, fire gods, Xochimilco, and creation narratives, but assigning their stories to Chantico without an explicit name or diagnostic image would manufacture evidence.

The Sun Stone, the 1978 Coyolxauhqui disk, and the 2006 Tlaltecuhtli monolith likewise do not name or securely depict Chantico. They belong to the wider evidentiary history of Mexica religion, not to Chantico’s direct corpus.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domestic fire

**[EA]** Chantico’s most secure domain is fire “in the house”: the contained, maintained fire of habitation. The evidence is onomastic, cultic, and iconographic rather than a surviving hymn explicitly declaring “I am the hearth.” Her dedicated fire-priests and fiery coloration reinforce the interpretation.

**[MI]** Calling her “the hearth itself,” rather than its divine owner or resident, is theologically possible within Nahua understandings of *teotl*, but no examined text states this exact ontology.

### Lapidaries and precious-stone work

**[EA/RV]** Book 9 of the *Florentine Codex* connects Chantico with Xochimilcan **tlatecque**, cutters or workers of precious stones. Her gold ear ornaments, feather mosaic, turquoise nose ornament, and jeweled associations suit that occupational setting.

### Goldsmiths’ furnace

**[AE/MN]** The requested claim that Chantico was specifically “goddess of the goldsmiths’ furnace” is not verified by the principal source corpus. The sources securely name **lapidaries**. Mexica goldsmithing had distinct occupational settings; the *Codex Mendoza* depicts lapidaries and goldsmiths together, while other traditions connect luxury craftsmen with Xipe Totec or other patrons. Proximity between gem-cutting, precious metals, and fire probably encouraged the modern expansion. “Patroness of precious-stone workers” is secure; “patroness of goldsmiths and their furnace” should be marked unproven.

### War and conquest

**[EA/MI]** Chantico’s shield, feather staff, obsidian-point designs, warrior-feather ornaments, and *atl-tlachinolli* (“water and burning,” the conventional sign of war) establish martial iconography. The Stone of Tizoc and the Ex-Arzobispado Stone personify conquered polities through patron deities. Noguez identifies the defeated Xochimilco figure as Chantico, carrying an *atlatl* and darts. The identification is comparative but strong ([Noguez and Uribe Cortés](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/chantico-cuaxolotl)).

This does not mean that Chantico was simply “the war goddess.” Her martial form reflects the entanglement of patron gods, conquered cities, and state ideology.

### Standard visual markers

Depending on manuscript and region, Chantico may be recognized by:

- red, yellow, black, or divided red-and-black facial paint;
- rubber-blackened lips;
- female *huipil*, skirt, and white sandals;
- *tlazolxochitl* headcloth;
- gold earplugs;
- turquoise nose ornament;
- a radiant *meyotli* or solar device;
- eagle-feather shield;
- feather staff with obsidian-point pattern;
- bells;
- the *atl-tlachinolli* sign;
- dog or Xolotl-associated features;
- warrior feather crests.

No single feature is exclusive to her. Identification should rely on combinations of attributes and calendrical position.

### Animals, colors, numbers, and days

- **Dog [EA]:** her calendrical name is 9 Dog, and her central myth ends in canine transformation.
- **Nine [EA]:** securely part of her calendrical name; not evidence that nine was a universal mystical number of her cult.
- **Red, yellow, and black [EA]:** manuscript-dependent colors connected with fire, paint, and identity.
- **1 Wind [EA]:** first day of the trecena she rules.
- **1 Flower [EA/CE]:** sign during which sacrifices occurred at Tetlanman.
- **Butterfly [EA/RV]:** present in occupational names Papaloxaual and Tlappapalo; its precise relation to fire, souls, warriors, or costume must be argued contextually.
- **Serpent, cactus, volcano, chili as universal emblems [MI/MN]:** some arise from particular costume elements or the fast myth, but no fixed comprehensive emblem-list survives.
- **Celestial body [AE]:** no secure planet or star is uniquely assigned to Chantico.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

No early source supplies Chantico with parents, children, siblings, or a complete family tree. Her network is cultic, calendrical, and iconographic rather than genealogical.

### Claimed consort: Xiuhtecuhtli

**[MN/MI]** Modern summaries regularly make Chantico the wife of Xiuhtecuhtli, the central Mexica fire lord. The pairing is conceptually attractive—domestic female fire beside sovereign or cosmic male fire—but no primary passage located here calls them spouses. Nicholson’s placement of Chantico in a **Xiuhtecuhtli–Huehueteotl–Ixcozauhqui complex** is typological, not a recovered marriage statement. Their shared fire attributes establish affinity, not matrimony.

### Every documented or proposed god-god interaction

- **Tonacatecuhtli — punishment and transformation.** Chantico breaks a ritual fast by eating roasted fish; its smoke rises to heaven; Tonacatecuhtli becomes angry, curses her, and she becomes a dog. *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, fol. 21v, painted ca. 1554–1555 and glossed before 1563. **[CE; securely attested colonial myth]**

- **Xolotl — iconographic and nominal association, not a narrated encounter.** The *Telleriano-Remensis* explains Cuaxolotl by saying Chantico wore Xolotl’s adornments on her head. No scene of the two meeting, fighting, marrying, or exchanging objects is recorded. *Telleriano-Remensis*, fol. 21v. **[CE/LS]**

- **Cohuaxolotl/Cuaxolotl — joint sacrificial impersonation.** Torquemada says captives representing Chantico and Cohuaxolotl were killed during the events preceding the Tlatelolco war. If the names designate distinct beings here, this is a joint ritual appearance; if they are variant names of one divine complex, the passage may preserve duplication or paired aspects. *Monarquía indiana*, Book 2, chapter 58, published 1615, reporting 1473. **[CE; interpretively uncertain]**

- **Cihuacoatl — proposed equation, no direct interaction.** Durán associates Cihuacoatl with Xochimilco; Seler inferred confusion or identity with Chantico. Nicholson questioned the proposal. No primary source narrates the two goddesses interacting. **[SY/LS; disputed]**

- **Coyolxauhqui — proposed manifestation, no direct interaction.** Seler and Nicholson observed Chantico-like iconographic features on the monumental head historically called the Coyolxauhqui-Chantico monument. Nicholson argued for a manifestation or merger. No colonial text explicitly equates Chantico and Coyolxauhqui, and the famous 1978 Coyolxauhqui disk bears a distinct, secure Coyolxauhqui context. **[LS; disputed modern reconstruction]**

- **Xiuhtecuhtli — shared fire complex, no documented meeting or marriage.** **[AE]**

- **Huitzilopochtli — no direct mythic encounter.** Chantico’s possible identification with Coyolxauhqui would indirectly insert her into Huitzilopochtli’s birth and combat myth, but that depends entirely on the disputed equation. **[AE/LS]**

- **Quetzalcoatl — no documented interaction.** His image or “golden house” may occupy adjacent calendrical manuscript space, but adjacency is not narrative contact. **[AE]**

- **Xipe Totec — occupational comparison only.** Modern scholarship distinguishes or overlaps their relations to luxury crafts; no encounter is recorded. **[AE]**

The absence of a broad divine interaction-cycle is itself a result: Chantico’s surviving dossier is dominated by calendrics, costume, craft patronage, temple administration, and a single transformation myth.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The broken fast and transformation into a dog

**Earliest source:** *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*, folio 21v, mid-sixteenth century.  
**Tier:** **[CE]**, probably preserving an Indigenous tradition but surviving only in colonial annotation.

The gloss says Chantico was the first to sacrifice—or the first to commit the relevant ritual presumption, depending on how the difficult Spanish is punctuated. During a fast she ate roasted fish. Some modern retellings add chili or paprika, but the accessible gloss securely says *pescado asado*, roasted fish; the pepper detail requires a specific textual witness and should not be repeated unqualified. Smoke from the fish ascended into heaven. Tonacatecuhtli became angry and cursed Chantico, transforming her into a dog.

The dog outcome links the narrative to her calendrical identity, **9 Dog**. The act also dramatizes the force of fasting taboos: a domestic act of cooking produces smoke that crosses the boundary between earthly house and heavenly authority. Yet the codex does not explain whether she remained permanently canine, regained anthropomorphic form, or existed thereafter in both manifestations.

The gloss adds that from this audacity came destructions of the world. This may connect the episode with larger cosmogonic cycles, but Chantico does not appear by name as an actor in the surviving *Leyenda de los Soles*. It is unsafe to build a full “Chantico destroyed the world” cycle from this abbreviated colonial sentence.

### Xochimilco’s conquered patron

**[EA/MI]** On the Stone of Tizoc and related conquest monuments, a patron deity embodies the defeated altepetl Xochimilco. Noguez identifies the figure as Chantico on iconographic grounds. This is not a prose myth but an official political theology: the ruler’s victory over another polity is represented as domination of its sacred embodiment.

The visual record establishes that divine images could stand for towns in Mexica imperial discourse. It does not show that the goddess was defeated in a separately narrated divine battle.

### Chantico, Cihuacoatl, and Coyolxauhqui

**[LS]** Seler, Nicholson, and subsequent scholars have tried to relate Chantico to the wider family of armed, fiery, earth-associated or dangerous goddesses. Shared paint, headcloths, weaponry, butterfly imagery, and Xochimilcan associations make comparison productive. They do not create a lost canonical myth.

The **Coyolxauhqui-Chantico** proposal is especially important historiographically. A large sculpted head unearthed near the Templo Mayor in the nineteenth century displays bells and several Chantico-like elements. Seler first emphasized the Chantico correspondences; Nicholson later defended a combined identification. But the 1978 disk at the base of the Huitzilopochtli side of the Templo Mayor securely represents the dismembered Coyolxauhqui and triggered the modern Templo Mayor Project. It should not be relabeled Chantico merely because another monument exhibits overlapping attributes. Nicholson’s original argument is available in [“The New Tenochtitlan Templo Mayor Coyolxauhqui-Chantico Monument”](https://www.iai.spk-berlin.de/fileadmin/dokumentenbibliothek/Indiana/Indiana_10/IND_10_Nicholson.pdf).

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Xochimilco

**[EA/RV]** Xochimilco is Chantico’s clearest regional center. Sahagún’s Book 9 places her among the divine patrons honored by Xochimilcan precious-stone cutters on the day 9 Dog. The city’s craft population explains the association more securely than generalized statements that she was “the supreme goddess of Xochimilco.”

**[MI]** It is possible that Chantico originated as a Xochimilcan patron later incorporated into imperial Tenochtitlan. The geographic concentration and conquest imagery support this, but no surviving chronicle records a dated “adoption” ceremony. The proposition remains reconstruction.

The Stone of Tizoc’s defeated Xochimilco figure supplies political evidence for her patronage. The conquest of Xochimilco by Mexica power transformed a local deity into an emblem of imperial subordination without necessarily extinguishing local worship.

### Tetlanman Calmecac

**[EA]** Sahagún places the **Tetlanman Calmecac** as the twenty-seventh structure in the sacred precinct of Tenochtitlan. A fire priest (*tlenamacac*) and a penitent or devotee (*tlamaceuhqui*) lived there and served Chantico night and day. The institution was therefore more than a freestanding idol shrine: it possessed resident ritual specialists and continuous service.

“Monastery,” used in colonial Spanish translations, is an analogical Christian term for *calmecac*. It should not imply Christian vows, rule, or enclosure.

### Tetlanman temple

**[EA]** The twenty-ninth structure, **Tetlanman**, was dedicated to Quaxolotl-Chantico. Captives were killed there when **1 Flower** ruled. The Spanish source tradition says the occasion was not tied to one annual festival but could be ordered according to the devotion or decision of lords.

This passage constitutes direct textual evidence for human sacrifice in her cult. It does not tell us the victims’ sex, number on a typical occasion, method in every case, or archaeological identity.

### Tlatelolco and the war of 1473

**[CE]** Torquemada reports sacrifices of captives impersonating Chantico and Cohuaxolotl during preparations for Moquihuix’s attack on Tenochtitlan. The rite involved fasting and “funereal songs.” The episode gives the cult a political and military function: sacrificial theater ratified elite solidarity and fixed the time of war.

Because Torquemada wrote more than a century after the event, the report should be treated as colonial historiography grounded in earlier materials, not eyewitness testimony.

### Offerings and regalia

**[EA]** The costume inventories imply specialized manufacture of paint, garments, featherwork, gold ear ornaments, shield mosaics, bells, sandals, paper or cloth headdress elements, and staffs. These objects were probably assembled for an image or living **ixiptla**, a person who embodied the deity ritually.

**[EA]** The sacred-precinct account explicitly records captive sacrifice. Torquemada explicitly records divine impersonators killed in her name. It is therefore incorrect to portray Chantico as merely a benign kitchen-hearth goddess.

### Archaeology and the Huei Tzompantli

The Huei Tzompantli discoveries made at 24 República de Guatemala between 2015 and 2017 materially confirmed a major skull-display structure in Tenochtitlan’s sacred precinct. INAH identifies the excavated platform and skull tower as part of the Huei Tzompantli ([INAH site record](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907); [INAH research update](https://inah.gob.mx/boletines/a-10-anos-del-hallazgo-del-huei-tzompantli-de-tenochtitlan-los-craneos-humanos-son-objeto-de-analisis-de-vanguardia?highlight=WyJtdXNlbyIsInRlbXBsbyJd)).

**[AE]** No published archaeological result reviewed here attributes those particular skulls to Chantico, Tetlanman, or her festival. The find corroborates the wider material reality and scale of Mexica sacrificial practice; it is not direct archaeological proof of the Chantico passages.

### Temple archaeology

**[AE]** No excavated structure has been identified with universal scholarly confidence as the physical Tetlanman Calmecac or Tetlanman shrine. Sahagún’s list fixes them conceptually inside the sacred precinct, but exact mapping remains debated.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1519: recoverable Late Postclassic horizon

**[EA?/MI]** Chantico’s calendrical iconography was probably already established before the conquest. The Borgia-group books, *Borbonicus*, and conquest sculptures preserve an iconographic system too structured to be a purely missionary-period invention. Nevertheless, no securely dated pre-Conquest object bears her alphabetic name, and several individual identifications rest on colonial comparisons.

Her cult probably combined:

- domestic or contained fire;
- a regional Xochimilcan identity;
- lapidary craft patronage;
- calendrical divination;
- martial patronage and altepetl representation;
- institutional worship in the Tenochtitlan precinct.

### 1519–1521: conquest

The fall of Tenochtitlan destroyed or transformed the institutional setting in which Chantico’s priests, images, sacrifices, and calendrical rites operated. Temples were demolished, buried, or converted; Christian churches and colonial buildings occupied sacred spaces. No source records a separate formal edict suppressing Chantico by name.

### 1520s–1570s: documentation under evangelization

**[CE]** Missionary campaigns condemned Indigenous gods as idols or demons while simultaneously eliciting detailed testimony about them. Chantico’s best surviving descriptions were created in this setting by Indigenous painters, Nahuatl writers, elders, and European clerics.

The sources are therefore neither transparent windows into untouched pre-Conquest religion nor mere Spanish inventions. They are collaborative colonial documents shaped by memory, interrogation, translation, evangelization, and Indigenous intellectual agency.

### Seventeenth century

Torquemada incorporated Chantico into a universal colonial history. His account preserved otherwise lost material about Tlatelolco and sacrifice while filtering it through Christian terminology and historiography.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Manuscripts and sculptures entered European and Mexican antiquarian collections. Antonio de León y Gama discussed Chantico and Tetlanman in his 1792 study of the monuments discovered in Mexico City’s main plaza. His work illustrates the transition from colonial demonological description to antiquarian comparison ([Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/descripcion-historica-y-cronologica-de-las-dos-piedras-que-con-ocasion-del-nuevo-empedrado-que-se-esta-formando-en-la-plaza-principal-de-mexico-el-ano-de-1790--0/html/008ff7d0-82b2-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_1.html)).

### Seler and early comparative iconography

Around 1900, Eduard Seler systematically compared divinatory manuscripts and sculpture. He identified recurrent Chantico attributes across the *Borbonicus*, *Telleriano-Remensis*, *Vaticanus A*, *Borgia*, *Vaticanus B*, and *Tonalamatl Aubin*. This established the modern comparative dossier but also encouraged large-scale divine equations that later researchers have treated more cautiously.

### 1978 onward: Templo Mayor archaeology

The discovery of the monumental Coyolxauhqui disk in 1978 initiated the Proyecto Templo Mayor. Its excavations transformed understanding of the precinct’s stratigraphy, offerings, political symbolism, and sacrificial practice. The discovery did not identify a Chantico sanctuary but supplied the archaeological framework in which the textual Tetlanman entries must now be studied.

The 2006 discovery of the Tlaltecuhtli monolith likewise transformed knowledge of late imperial sculpture and royal mortuary ritual. It is not a Chantico object.

### Present

**[AE]** No documented institutional lineage of priests or uninterrupted Chantico worship from the sixteenth century to the present has been established.

Contemporary Nahua and Xochimilcan ritual life preserves Indigenous language, place memory, agricultural rites, Catholic-Indigenous syncretism, and local sacred landscapes. Those continuities should not be converted automatically into a claim that the pre-Conquest Chantico cult survived unchanged under a saint’s name. No securely documented saint-equation specific to Chantico was found.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Academic and museum reconstruction

Chantico’s modern identity has been produced largely by the juxtaposition of manuscripts that never circulated together in pre-Conquest Mexico. Seler, Nicholson, Boone, and manuscript specialists created a comparative figure spanning Xochimilco, Tenochtitlan, Puebla-Tlaxcala, and Borgia-group imagery. This is legitimate scholarship but remains reconstruction.

### Neopagan and occult practice

**[MN as claims of antiquity; modern religion as present fact]** Individual modern Pagans and witches report “working with” or worshipping Chantico. Online discussions show practitioners searching for rites and devotional material, but no historically continuous liturgy, priesthood, initiation, or ancient Chantico grimoire is documented.

No evidence was found that Chantico had a defined role in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, nineteenth-century ceremonial magic, or the classic European grimoire tradition. Claims of an old “Chantico occult tradition” are therefore modern inventions unless supported by a specific dated text.

Modern offerings, candle correspondences, crystals, tarot associations, planetary rulers, invocations, and reconstructed prayers may be meaningful contemporary creations. They should be labeled modern rather than presented as translated Aztec rites.

### Popular mythology

Modern profiles regularly aggregate the following into one smooth biography:

- wife of Xiuhtecuhtli;
- mother of four colored fires;
- goddess of volcanoes;
- guardian of treasures and wealth;
- patroness of goldsmiths;
- owner of fixed sacred colors, plants, crystals, or planets.

This composite exceeds the evidence. It blends secure hearth and lapidary material with extrapolation from fire-god typologies and contemporary goddess spirituality.

A particularly clear commercial transformation appears in Chantico Global’s description of her as a protector of wealth and precious things, used to explain an investment consultancy’s name. The page says that ancient Aztecs prayed to her for “wealth and stability,” but no corresponding primary prayer is supplied ([Chantico Global](https://www.chanticoglobal.com/about/)). This is **[MN] commercial mythology**, not evidence of ancient doctrine.

Her name is also used by jewelry, agave, restaurant, music, technology, and industrial-control brands. The commercial attraction comes from associations with hearth, fire, precious materials, protection, and Mexican heritage; it does not demonstrate modern cult continuity.

### Political and extremist appropriation

**[AE]** No well-documented extremist movement centered specifically on Chantico was identified. Broader nationalist and identity movements have appropriated Mexica imagery, but transferring that general history to Chantico without evidence would be misleading.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Nicholson’s typology

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 classification grouped central Mexican gods into functional and iconographic complexes. Chantico belongs near the Xiuhtecuhtli–Huehueteotl fire complex but also crosses fertility, craft, martial, and local-patron categories. The strength of the typology is comparative control; its danger is turning fluid, place-specific divine personae into fixed taxonomic species.

### The Chantico–Cihuacoatl question

Durán’s Cihuacoatl is tied to Xochimilco. Seler interpreted this as a confusion with Chantico; Nicholson found that doubtful. Shared Xochimilcan and female-warrior associations make contact possible, but the sources do not authorize simple identity. The proper conclusion is **disputed overlap**, not “Cihuacoatl was another name for Chantico.”

### The Chantico–Coyolxauhqui question

Nicholson’s 1985 study argued that the monumental head traditionally labeled Coyolxauhqui-Chantico legitimately combined both identities. The argument rests on:

- bell motifs associated with Coyolxauhqui’s name;
- Chantico’s headcloth and facial attributes;
- common martial and sacrificial symbolism;
- evidence that Nahua gods could overlap or manifest through one another.

Against a full equation:

- no colonial text calls Chantico Coyolxauhqui;
- their most secure narrative and cultic contexts differ;
- attributes circulate among multiple goddesses;
- the 1978 disk supplies a more contextually secure Coyolxauhqui representation.

The monument can therefore be described as a contested Coyolxauhqui–Chantico identification, not proof of canonical identity.

### Gender

Most visual and Sahaguntine evidence supports a goddess: female garments and Spanish *diosa*. The *Telleriano-Remensis* gloss uses masculine *señor* and *este*. This may reflect grammatical default, a male glossator’s uncertainty, corruption, or genuinely flexible divine gender. It prevents an absolute claim that every community always conceptualized Chantico identically.

### Etymology

“In the house” is semantically compelling, but *Chantico* is not the expected transparent form *chanco*. Sullivan’s editors properly retain several possibilities for *-ti-*. Popular translations such as “she who dwells in the house” add both gender and verbal agency not literally expressed by a simple locative.

### Xochimilcan origin

Seler inferred a Xochimilcan origin from the lapidary cult and town patronage. This is plausible but not documented as an origin story. Chantico might instead have belonged to a wider central Mexican fire complex with an especially visible Xochimilcan specialization.

### Goldsmiths versus lapidaries

This dossier’s dedicated contested-material search found the primary evidence consistently emphasizing **precious-stone workers**. Goldsmiths and lapidaries could appear together in social and artistic contexts, and both handled precious substances, but occupational proximity is not identity. “Goldsmiths’ furnace goddess” should be removed from unqualified summaries unless a specific primary passage is produced.

### Human sacrifice

The textual evidence is explicit: captives were killed at Tetlanman under 1 Flower, and Torquemada reports the killing of divine impersonators. Modern attempts to make all sacrifice metaphorical are contradicted by both texts and archaeology. Conversely, the Huei Tzompantli cannot be assigned specifically to Chantico without contextual evidence.

### State of the evidence

The principal witnesses survive in:

- Biblioteca Nacional de Francia: *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*;
- Real Academia de la Historia and Palacio Real, Madrid: Sahaguntine manuscripts including the materials called *Primeros Memoriales*;
- Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence: *Florentine Codex*;
- Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris: *Codex Borbonicus*;
- Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana: *Codex Borgia*, *Vaticanus A*, and *Vaticanus B*;
- Museo Nacional de Antropología and related Mexican repositories: sculptures and the *Tonalamatl Aubin*.

Most pre-Conquest books were destroyed or lost. Consequently, the corpus almost certainly represents a fraction of historical Chantico traditions.
