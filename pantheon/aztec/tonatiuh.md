# Tonatiuh: Research Dossier

**Class:** male solar deity/personified Sun in Mexica and broader Central Mexican Nahua traditions; also the Sun of the present cosmic age. The sources do not consistently separate the astronomical sun, its divine personification, the transformed Nanahuatzin, and solar manifestations attached to other gods.

**Earliest secure attestation:** a recognizable solar deity conventionally identified as Tonatiuh appears in pre-Conquest or conquest-era ritual-divinatory manuscripts of the Borgia Group, especially the *Codex Borgia*, probably produced in the Puebla–Tlaxcala–western Oaxaca region during the Late Postclassic, before or near 1521. Because these pictures generally lack alphabetic name labels, “Tonatiuh” is a scholarly iconographic identification rather than a surviving written name. The earliest securely named accounts are sixteenth-century Nahuatl manuscripts: Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales* and related Matritense material, compiled from Indigenous testimony beginning in the 1550s; the *Leyenda de los Soles*, explicitly dated 22 May 1558; and the Florentine Codex, substantially completed by 1569 and copied in its surviving form about 1575–1577.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[PRE-CONQUEST]** Material demonstrably or probably made before the Spanish conquest.
- **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]** Sixteenth-century evidence in Nahuatl or Indigenous pictorial convention, though produced under colonial conditions.
- **[EARLY COLONIAL–CHRONICLER]** Spanish, French, or bilingual missionary and chronicler testimony.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** Evidence belonging to a particular Central Mexican community or manuscript tradition.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** A colonial or modern attempt to organize heterogeneous gods and myths into a unified pantheon.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC]** A historically documented equation or combination with another religious figure.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** A conclusion reached by comparing incomplete texts, images, or archaeological contexts.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/RETELLING]** A claim that cannot be traced to an ancient or early-colonial witness.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[SECURELY ATTESTED, SIXTEENTH CENTURY]** Classical Nahuatl *Tōnatiuh* means “sun.” It is built from *tōna*, “to be warm, shine, give solar heat,” plus a directional or progressive formation commonly understood as “it goes shining/warming” or “that which proceeds emitting heat.” “Movement of the Sun” is an overcompressed popular translation, probably influenced by the calendrical name *Nahui Ollin*. The vowel length matters: *Tōnatiuh*, approximately /toːˈnatiw/. Colonial spelling varies: *Tonatiuh, Tonatio, Tonatíuh,* and *Tonatih*. [S1]

The word names both the visible sun and a divine agent. Sahagún’s Nahuatl informants call the sun “the soaring eagle, the turquoise prince, the god” and describe offerings made as it advances through the day. The grammar does not compel the modern distinction “natural object versus god”; context determines whether *tonatiuh* means the solar body, the divine Sun, or both.

### Documented names and titles

- **Tōnatiuh — “Sun; the one going along shining/warming.”** Ordinary noun and divine name in sixteenth-century Nahuatl sources. **[SECURELY ATTESTED]**
- **Teōtl Tōnatiuh — “God/numinous Sun” or “Divine Sun.”** Found in Sahaguntine material where explicit divinity must be distinguished from the ordinary celestial referent. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]**
- **Nahui Ollin / Nāhui Ōllin — “Four Movement.”** Calendar name of the present Sun and the prophesied mode of the current world’s destruction, by movement or earthquake. It identifies the Fifth Sun in the *Leyenda de los Soles* and in the glosses of the *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* and *Codex Vaticanus A*. It is not simply a synonym meaning “Tonatiuh” in every context. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS; LEARNED CALENDRICAL SYSTEMATIZATION]** [S2]
- **Ollin Tonatiuh — “Movement Sun.”** A compound or appositional designation used in later discussion of the Fifth Sun. Its antiquity as a fixed personal name is less secure than that of *Nahui Ollin*. **[COLONIAL/RECONSTRUCTED]**
- **Cuauhtlehuanitzin / ascending-eagle imagery.** The sun rises like an eagle and descends like one; “soaring eagle” is a documented metaphor, but it should not automatically be treated as a formal cult name. **[SECURELY ATTESTED METAPHOR]**
- **Xiuhpilli — “Turquoise Prince/Noble.”** A poetic solar designation in Sahaguntine rhetoric. *Xiuh-* can carry turquoise, preciousness, fire, year, and vegetation associations. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]**
- **Tlalchitonatiuh / Tlachitonatiuh — “Sun close to the ground,” hence a low, setting, or terrestrial sun.** Pictorially written in the *Codex Borbonicus* and *Tonalamatl Aubin*. The spelling and exact interpretation are debated. It is a differentiated solar aspect rather than automatically the same person as Tonatiuh in every scene. **[REGIONAL/CALENDAR-MANUSCRIPT VARIANT]** [S3]
- **Ipalnemohuani and Tloque Nahuaque.** These broad theological titles—“He through whom one lives” and “Lord of the Near and the Close”—are sometimes assigned to Tonatiuh in modern popular works. In the primary sources they are polyvalent designations of supreme or life-giving divinity, not secure exclusive epithets of Tonatiuh. **[MODERN OVER-IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Fifth Sun.** Accurate as a description of the current cosmic sun, but “fifth” is a modern ordinal shorthand: Nahua accounts usually enumerate the destroyed worlds through their calendar names and call the present one *Nahui Ollin*. **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Names that must not be silently equated

- **Nanahuatzin/Nanauatzin:** the humble, diseased god who enters the fire and emerges as the present Sun. Some accounts permit “Nanahuatzin became Tonatiuh”; others continue to call the resulting luminary simply *tonatiuh*. Treating them as two permanently independent gods is as misleading as treating every occurrence as the same personal name. **[TEXTUALLY VARIABLE]**
- **Huitzilopochtli:** patron of the Mexica and recipient of the southern Templo Mayor shrine. He acquired powerful solar and warrior meanings, but Sahaguntine and pictorial sources distinguish him iconographically and narratively from Tonatiuh. The statement “Huitzilopochtli is Tonatiuh” is a scholarly or popular fusion, not a universally attested Nahua equation. [S4]
- **Piltzintecuhtli/Xochipilli:** older or parallel Central Mexican solar beings. Similarity of domain does not prove identity.
- **Yohualtecuhtli (“Lord of Night”):** sometimes interpreted as the nocturnal or underworld sun. His proposed identification with the Sun Stone’s central face is modern scholarship.
- **Tlaltecuhtli:** earth deity with whom Tonatiuh shares devouring mouths, claws, and sacrificial imagery. This overlap lies at the center of the Sun Stone dispute; it is not evidence that the two were always a single god.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest visual evidence

**[PRE-CONQUEST OR CONQUEST-ERA]** The *Codex Borgia* is a screenfold of deerskin covered with gesso, now Vatican Library Borg. Mess. 1. Its exact place and date are disputed; Puebla–Tlaxcala or a neighboring region is more likely than Tenochtitlan, and it is commonly dated to the Late Postclassic, perhaps shortly before the conquest. Pages conventionally numbered 25, 39–40, 49, and 71 contain solar beings or solar rites identified by comparison with labeled colonial manuscripts. Page 71 famously shows a red solar figure receiving blood from a decapitated bird. Because the manuscript has no alphabetic gloss “Tonatiuh,” it securely proves a pre-Conquest Central Mexican solar cult and iconographic complex, not necessarily the pronunciation of the god’s name. [S5]

**[PRE-CONQUEST OR EARLY COLONIAL]** The *Codex Borbonicus* contains a divinatory almanac, the eighteen solar-year festivals, and New Fire material. Its precise manufacture—immediately before or soon after conquest—remains disputed. Its solar images and glottographic elements help identify *Tlalchitonatiuh*. The festival section also warns against treating every surviving veintena cycle as a faithful copy of Tenochtitlan practice: recent research finds regional ritual variants. [S6]

### What the corpus does not establish

No surviving pre-Conquest inscription spells out a Mexica theological biography of “Tonatiuh.” No recovered temple can be assigned to him with the confidence attaching the Templo Mayor shrines to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc. No source gives him a stable spouse and offspring comparable to a classical Mediterranean divine genealogy. Modern family trees largely force a mobile Mesoamerican divine system into a Greco-Roman model.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and powers

**[SECURELY ATTESTED]** Tonatiuh is the daylight Sun, source of heat and illumination, measure of daytime, ruler or embodiment of the present cosmic age, and receiver of sacrificial nourishment. He crosses the sky from east to west. His continued motion is contingent rather than automatic: Book 7 makes the newly created Sun remain stationary even after the other gods die, until Ehecatl’s wind drives it forward. The myth makes reciprocity—divine self-sacrifice answered by human offering—a condition of cosmic continuity.

**[SECURELY ATTESTED]** The present Sun’s calendrical sign is *Nahui Ollin*, Four Movement. The same sign predicts the present world’s termination by movement, conventionally earthquakes. This is not a securely dated prophecy for a particular modern year.

**[SECURELY ATTESTED]** Warriors killed in battle and sacrificial captives accompanied the Sun during part of its course; women who died in first childbirth, the Cihuateteo, accompanied it in the western segment. The exact division and duration vary by source and interpretation. “Tonatiuh’s heaven” is therefore a status- and manner-of-death destination, not a general moral heaven.

### Recognizing the solar deity

Across Borgia-group and colonial pictorial manuscripts, the identified Sun may have:

- a red-painted face or body;
- yellow or fiery hair;
- a solar disk with concentric colored bands and projecting rays;
- a quincunx or four-directional construction;
- jewel or jade nose ornamentation;
- eagle feathers, claws, or raptor associations;
- turquoise ornaments;
- a throne and the day sign Four Movement;
- sacrificial knives, blood streams, hearts, or vessels receiving blood.

No single trait is exclusive. Red paint, butterfly or solar pectorals, eagle attributes, and turquoise regalia also occur on other beings. Identification must depend on the whole scene and comparative manuscripts.

### Eagle and hearts

**[SECURELY ATTESTED]** Nahua verbal and pictorial sources associate the Sun with the eagle. The bird’s ascent and descent model sunrise and sunset; warriors are “eagle” captors; and the solar figure may bear eagle claws. On the Sun Stone, the central being’s two clawed hands grasp human hearts. Hearts could be called precious eagle-cactus fruit in sacrificial rhetoric. The relationship is more than a modern nature metaphor, but modern summaries often merge several distinct usages into “the eagle is Tonatiuh’s sacred animal.”

**[SECURELY ATTESTED]** Hearts and blood feed or “revive” solar and other divine powers. Codex Borgia imagery shows a solar being receiving blood, including bird blood. Colonial descriptions report human hearts raised toward or offered to the Sun. These claims are supported independently by images, texts, cut-marked human remains, sacrificial stones, skull racks, and deposits. The evidence does not validate every Spanish numerical estimate.

### Calendrical offices

**[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION BASED ON CODEX EVIDENCE]** Tonatiuh is commonly listed as the fourth Lord of the Day, with a quail as associated bird, and as patron of the day *Quiahuitl* (“Rain”) in the twenty-day-sign cycle. These assignments derive from comparative readings of divinatory codices. They should not be expanded into a modern “birthday,” personality profile, or zodiac without acknowledging that such readings are reconstructions of specialized divination.

### The Sun Stone

The basalt monument now called the Sun Stone or “Aztec Calendar Stone” was uncovered in the Plaza Mayor in December 1790. It weighs approximately twenty-four metric tons. A carved date 13 Reed has often been correlated with 1479, in Axayacatl’s reign, although the monument’s manufacture, original setting, orientation, and function remain debated. [S12–S13]

Its center shows a face inside *Nahui Ollin*, flanked by clawed hands holding hearts. Around it are the signs of four previous Suns, calendrical rings, rays, and two great fire serpents.

Competing identifications are:

1. **Tonatiuh.** Eduard Seler, Hermann Beyer, Alfonso Caso, many museum presentations, and much twentieth-century scholarship identified the face as the solar god, pointing to the Four Movement enclosure, solar rays, hearts, claws, and the monument’s cosmic program. **[DOMINANT HISTORICAL INTERPRETATION; NOT CERTAIN]**

2. **Tlaltecuhtli.** Carlos Navarrete and Doris Heyden argued in 1974 that the frontal mask, clawed extremities, and flint-knife tongue belong to the devouring Earth Lord. Karl Anton Nowotny and others had already drawn attention to earth-monster features. The interpretation gains force from better-documented Tlaltecuhtli sculptures, especially after the colossal monolith found in 2006. **[MAJOR MODERN SCHOLARLY ALTERNATIVE]** [S14]

3. **Yohualtecuhtli or nocturnal Sun.** Cecelia Klein emphasized death, darkness, and the underworld aspect of the central image. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY ALTERNATIVE]**

4. **Composite day/night or Sun/Earth being.** Michel Graulich and later writers read the upper and lower portions differently or understand the image as a deliberate fusion of celestial and terrestrial powers. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**

5. **Royal-cosmic image involving Motecuhzoma II/Huitzilopochtli.** David Stuart interprets name signs and the monument’s political program as making the central being more than a generic portrait of Tonatiuh. **[RECENT MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** [S15]

The responsible conclusion is that the monument is solar in overall organization, but its central face cannot be cited unqualifiedly as a secure portrait of Tonatiuh. The face may intentionally combine Sun, Earth, sacrifice, rulership, and time.

### Colors, plants, and modern additions

Red, yellow, turquoise, eagle feathers, blood, jade, fire, and solar disks are documented. Claims that Tonatiuh’s exclusive flower is the marigold, that he created the *cempasúchil*, or that particular crystals, incense blends, tarot cards, planets, chakras, or weekdays “belong” to him are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless supported by a specified colonial passage. The modern romantic story in which Tonatiuh creates the marigold for separated lovers has no established pre-Conquest source.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Origin rather than family tree

Tonatiuh’s principal “genealogy” is transformational: Nanahuatzin enters the divine fire at Teotihuacan and rises as the Sun. Tecuciztecatl follows and becomes the Moon after his excessive brilliance is reduced with a rabbit. This is not biological parentage.

Some late compilations assign Nanahuatzin parents or foster parents, but these genealogies are not consistently transmitted and must not be retrojected into Sahagún’s version. No securely attested wife or children of Tonatiuh form part of the central Mexica corpus. Solar figures such as Xochipilli, Piltzintecuhtli, and Huitzilopochtli are related by domain and historical fusion, not by a single canonical genealogy.

### Every securely identifiable god–god interaction

- **Nanahuatzin — transformation/identity:** Nanahuatzin enters the divine hearth and emerges as the present Sun; Florentine Codex Book 7, c. 1575–1577 from earlier Nahuatl testimony; parallel in the 1558 *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS; CENTRAL VERSION]**
- **Tecuciztecatl — paired creation and rivalry of brightness:** he hesitates before the fire, follows Nanahuatzin, rises as a second luminary, and is darkened into the Moon; Florentine Codex Book 7. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]**
- **Ehecatl — compelled motion:** after the gods’ deaths, the wind blows and the Sun begins its course; Florentine Codex Book 7. Ehecatl also serves as executioner of the gods. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]**
- **Xolotl — attempted refusal of the solar economy:** Xolotl flees Ehecatl’s sacrificial killing by changing into maize, maguey, and axolotl forms but is eventually caught; the resulting divine sacrifice enables the solar order. Florentine Codex Book 7. Tonatiuh does not personally chase him. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]**
- **Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli — combat:** the Morning Star shoots at the newly enthroned Sun and misses; the Sun’s answering dart strikes and transforms or disables him, often interpreted as his becoming Itztlacoliuhqui. *Leyenda de los Soles*, 1558. **[REGIONAL/TEXTUAL VARIANT]** [S16]
- **Itztlacoliuhqui — resulting identity:** the damaged or punished Morning Star becomes the curved-obsidian/cold being. This follows from the preceding episode; sources and translations differ on whether it is a full transformation. **[REGIONAL VARIANT; LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl — heavenly reception:** after reaching heaven, the Sun is seated on a quetzal-feather throne and given red head regalia; *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]**
- **Itztlotli — divine messenger:** an obsidian hawk is sent to question or negotiate with the stationary Sun; *Leyenda de los Soles*. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Titlacahuan/Tezcatlipoca — council preceding divine death:** Titlacahuan participates in the gathering after the solar confrontation; *Leyenda de los Soles*. The text does not establish a permanent alliance with Tonatiuh. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Huitzilopochtli — council and later functional convergence:** Huitzilopochtli participates in the divine assembly or slaughter sequence in the *Leyenda*. Mexica imperial ideology later gives him strong warrior-solar functions, but no surviving early text simply says that he and Tonatiuh are always identical. **[ATTESTED JOINT EPISODE; LATER SYNCRETISTIC CONVERGENCE]**
- **Xochiquetzal — present at divine mortality:** she belongs to the group assembled when the gods die in the *Leyenda de los Soles*. This is a joint episode, not evidence that she is Tonatiuh’s wife. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Yapalicue — present at divine mortality:** named among the goddesses in the *Leyenda* assembly. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Nochpalliicue — present at divine mortality:** likewise named in the *Leyenda* assembly. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **The unnamed gods collectively — reciprocal sacrifice:** they choose to die so the stationary Sun may be revived and humanity may live; Florentine Codex Book 7. **[CENTRAL EARLY-COLONIAL VERSION]**
- **Tlaltecuhtli — visual/cosmological juxtaposition or fusion:** no secure narrative describes a personal encounter. Proposed Tonatiuh–Tlaltecuhtli fusion comes from iconographic analysis, especially of the Sun Stone. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION; NO ATTESTED MEETING]**
- **Cihuateteo — procession with the Sun:** women dead in first childbirth accompany or receive the western Sun. These are collective divine dead rather than a single named goddess; Sahaguntine sources. **[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS]**
- **Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, and Centzon Huitznahua:** their Coatepec battle is frequently retold as “the Sun defeating Moon and stars,” but Tonatiuh is not a named actor in that narrative. Making it a Tonatiuh interaction is **[MODERN HARMONIZATION]**.
- **Quetzalcoatl:** Quetzalcoatl participates in other creation episodes, notably recovering human bones, but the Book 7 figure who kills the gods and moves the Sun is specifically Ehecatl. Calling him “Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl” is reasonable in broader theology but should not erase the wording of the passage. **[RELATED MANIFESTATION; TEXTUAL CAUTION]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The creation of the Fifth Sun: Florentine Codex version

**[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS; FULLEST SURVIVING VERSION]**

Before the present daylight, the gods gather at Teotihuacan and ask who will illuminate the world. The wealthy Tecuciztecatl volunteers. No second candidate comes forward until the gods summon Nanahuatzin, a poor, diseased, or pustular being who accepts immediately.

Both perform four days of penance. Tecuciztecatl offers precious materials: quetzal feathers, gold, greenstone, coral, and fine incense. Nanahuatzin offers humble reeds, ordinary maguey spines stained with his blood, and the matter from his sores. The contrast is moral and ritual, not merely economic: splendid substitutes are opposed to bodily self-giving.

At midnight the gods arrange themselves around the divine hearth, *teotexcalli*. Tecuciztecatl is commanded to enter. Four times he runs toward the fire and four times fear stops him. Because four attempts exhaust the permitted number, Nanahuatzin is called. He closes his eyes, commits himself at once, and enters. Shamed, Tecuciztecatl follows.

The gods wait to see where Nanahuatzin will reappear. Some guess incorrectly; Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl and several others look east. The horizon reddens, and Nanahuatzin rises as the Sun. Tecuciztecatl then rises too, equally bright. The gods object that two equal lights cannot cross the sky. One god throws a rabbit into Tecuciztecatl’s face, dimming him into the Moon and explaining the lunar rabbit.

Sun and Moon nevertheless remain motionless. The gods decide that they must die to revive the Sun. Ehecatl kills them. Xolotl refuses, weeps, and flees through transformations into twin maize, double maguey, and the aquatic *axolotl*, but is caught. Even after the deaths, the Sun does not move until Ehecatl blows with sufficient force. The Sun then commences its course; the Moon follows.

The source does not say that Tonatiuh personally demands “two hearts every day,” a common unsourced embellishment. It constructs a general obligation: the gods gave blood and life to establish the cosmos; human ritual reciprocates.

### The *Leyenda de los Soles* version

**[EARLY COLONIAL–INDIGENOUS; DISTINCT VARIANT, 1558]**

The *Leyenda* places the present Sun in a longer sequence of ages named Four Jaguar, Four Wind, Four Rain, Four Water, and Four Movement. Its narrative overlaps Book 7 but gives different emphases. After Nanahuatzin becomes the Sun, celestial powers enthrone and adorn him. The Sun remains in a position requiring negotiation. Itztlotli, the obsidian hawk, is sent to him.

Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, the Morning Star, becomes angry and shoots at the Sun. His missile misses. Tonatiuh answers with red-shafted darts, strikes the Morning Star, and covers or overwhelms his face with the nine heavens. Later systematization explains the wounded Morning Star as Itztlacoliuhqui, cold and punishment.

A gathering involving Titlacahuan, Huitzilopochtli, Xochiquetzal, Yapalicue, and Nochpalliicue precedes a “mortality of the gods.” The text is terse and presupposes oral or pictorial knowledge now lost. It should not be expanded into the much smoother modern story in which every named god willingly lines up to have a heart removed.

The present age is *Nahui Ollin*. Its people and world will perish through movement. The account embeds cosmic myth beside Toltec and Mexica historical traditions, not in a wholly separate category of “mythology.”

### The Five Suns

**[SIXTEENTH-CENTURY SYSTEMATIZATION OF OLDER TRADITIONS]**

Several sources agree that multiple worlds preceded the present one but disagree about order, duration, ruling gods, inhabitants, and catastrophes. Common correspondences are:

- Four Jaguar: inhabitants devoured by jaguars;
- Four Wind: destroyed by wind, survivors transformed into monkeys;
- Four Rain: destroyed by fiery rain, survivors transformed into birds;
- Four Water: destroyed by flood, survivors transformed into fish;
- Four Movement: the present Sun, destined to end through movement.

The popular fixed sequence assigning Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, Tlaloc, Chalchiuhtlicue, and Tonatiuh as five successive, neatly equivalent “sun gods” is a comparative reconstruction from sources that do not all give the same scheme. It is useful only when labeled.

### The solar war

**[ATTESTED CONCEPT; OFTEN OVER-SYSTEMATIZED]**

The Sun’s journey is martial. Warriors and sacrificial captives accompany it; eagles, darts, shields, and hearts surround solar imagery. Venus as Morning Star attacks the Sun in the *Leyenda*. Eclipses—“the Sun is eaten”—produce danger. These strands support a conception of solar motion as combat, but the popular daily drama “Tonatiuh fights the stars every night and needs exactly a fixed number of hearts” is a modern synthesis, not a verbatim ancient narrative.

### The Coatepec cycle is not a Tonatiuh myth

Huitzilopochtli’s birth from Coatlicue, killing of Coyolxauhqui, and rout of the Centzon Huitznahua was materially reenacted at the Templo Mayor. Modern works often identify Huitzilopochtli with the rising Sun, Coyolxauhqui with the Moon, and her brothers with stars. That solar reading is historically important, but Tonatiuh never replaces Huitzilopochtli as the named protagonist. The Coyolxauhqui Stone found in 1978 verifies the ritual centrality of Huitzilopochtli’s myth, not a separate cult image of Tonatiuh. [S17]

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult center and temples

There is no archaeologically secure “Great Temple of Tonatiuh” at Tenochtitlan. The Templo Mayor’s southern shrine belonged to Huitzilopochtli and its northern shrine to Tlaloc. Calling the southern shrine Tonatiuh’s temple erases a distinction made by the evidence. Nevertheless, solar ideology pervaded the precinct through orientation, calendrical rites, warrior sacrifice, eagle imagery, fire-serpent symbolism, and offerings.

Teotihuacan is the mythic place where the gods create the present Sun. The Mexica encountered that city as ancient ruins and named or reinterpreted it as “the place where gods were made.” This does not demonstrate that Classic-period Teotihuacanos worshiped the Mexica being under the name Tonatiuh. **[LATER NAHUA SACRALIZATION OF AN OLDER CITY]**

### Daily offerings

Sahaguntine sources preserve several related schedules of solar offering, commonly four offerings during daylight and additional observances at night. People offered incense as the Sun appeared, reached noon, declined, and entered its house. Variation among six surviving descriptions suggests local or redactional differences rather than one immutable liturgy. [S18]

Offerings included copal smoke, blood from autosacrifice, birds, food, precious materials, captive lives, and extracted hearts. Codex Borgia imagery proves that animal blood could nourish a solar effigy; therefore “the Sun accepted only human hearts” is false.

### Human sacrifice

**[TEXTUALLY, ICONOGRAPHICALLY, AND ARCHAEOLOGICALLY ATTESTED]**

Heart extraction, decapitation, gladiatorial sacrifice, arrow sacrifice, and display of skulls were practiced in Mexica state ritual. Captives taken in war were major victims; children were particularly associated with rain rites; some women and local residents also appear among recovered remains. Priests raised extracted hearts, deposited bodies or body parts, and displayed selected skulls on the *tzompantli*.

The ideological connection to Tonatiuh was direct where victims were offered to the Sun or conceptualized as sustaining solar motion. It was also indirect: Huitzilopochtli, Xipe Totec, Tezcatlipoca, Tlaloc, and other gods had their own ritual programs. Not every person killed at the Templo Mayor was “sacrificed to Tonatiuh.”

Between 2015 and 2017, INAH excavations at Guatemala Street identified a section of Tenochtitlan’s Huei Tzompantli and a circular tower of skulls. An official research program is analyzing a sample of 214 individuals. The find confirms large-scale skull display and sacrifice; it does not confirm the colonial claim that 80,400 victims died during one temple dedication. [S19–S21]

The distinction is essential:

- The existence of institutional human sacrifice is not merely a Spanish accusation.
- Spanish chroniclers had polemical reasons to emphasize it and could inflate numbers.
- Archaeology validates practices and substantial scale, but presently cannot recover a reliable annual total for the whole empire.
- Solar theology was one justification among several, not a complete explanation of Mexica warfare or government.

### Priesthood and impersonation

The surviving sources describe many priestly offices associated with temples and festivals, but no securely isolated hereditary “priesthood of Tonatiuh” can be reconstructed. Priests serving solar rites may have worked within larger institutions devoted to Huitzilopochtli, calendrical observance, captive sacrifice, or fire.

Likewise, no Tonatiuh *ixiptla*—a living deity-embodier—has the richly documented annual biography of Tezcatlipoca’s Toxcatl impersonator. Modern descriptions that transfer that institution to Tonatiuh are unsubstantiated.

### Warfare and imperial politics

Captive-taking joined political expansion to cosmic maintenance. Elite warriors gained status by taking prisoners; captives became gifts to divine powers; imperial ceremonies displayed tributary wealth and subordinated bodies. Solar rhetoric thereby naturalized Mexica military power as assistance to the universe. This does not mean the Mexica fought wars only to obtain victims. Tribute, territorial security, factional competition, dynastic legitimacy, and commercial routes were also material causes.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before c. 1200: antecedents

Solar deities, solar disks, eagles, Venus–Sun relations, sacrificed hearts, and calendrical ages long predate the Mexica. Comparable motifs occur at Teotihuacan, among Classic and Postclassic Maya polities, at Tula, and across Mixtec and Puebla–Tlaxcala traditions. Assigning every earlier solar image the name “Tonatiuh” is unjustified. **[MESOAMERICAN ANTECEDENTS, NOT DIRECT NAME ATTESTATION]**

### Late Postclassic, c. 1200–1521

A Central Mexican solar being recognizable through red coloration, disks, eagle traits, and blood offerings appears in the Borgia-group manuscripts. The Mexica state integrated solar reciprocity with the cult of Huitzilopochtli, military orders, tribute, and the sacred geography of Tenochtitlan. *Nahui Ollin* situated the empire inside the precarious Fifth Sun.

Tonatiuh does not seem to have displaced Huitzilopochtli as state patron or possessed a comparably monumental independent sanctuary. His importance was cosmological and distributive: the Sun structured time, sacrifice, warfare, afterlife, rulership, and east–west space.

### 1519–1521: conquest

Nahua speakers called Pedro de Alvarado *Tonatiuh* or a related form, apparently because of his conspicuous light or reddish hair and solar appearance. Bernal Díaz later says Indigenous people gave him the name after seeing his portrait and explains it as “Sun” or “child of the Sun.” The nickname is evidence for metaphorical naming, not proof that Alvarado was worshiped as the god. Claims of Indigenous “apotheosis” require separate demonstration. [S22]

Alvarado ordered or supervised the 1520 massacre during the Toxcatl celebration in Tenochtitlan. His solar nickname therefore acquired an exceptionally violent historical afterlife, especially in Nahua and Guatemalan memory.

### 1520s–1600: suppression and documentation

Spanish forces destroyed temples, burned images, prohibited sacrifices, seized temple wealth, and built Christian institutions over sacred precincts. Indigenous ritual specialists were prosecuted. Yet the same conversion campaign produced the largest textual archive: friars such as Sahagún, working with Nahua grammarians, painters, elders, and students, recorded names, speeches, images, rites, and myths.

The resulting record is neither a transparent pre-Conquest archive nor simply Spanish invention. It is collaborative, coerced, edited, regionally uneven, and often designed to expose practices for eradication.

### Christian solar translation

Missionaries and Indigenous Christian writers reused solar vocabulary. Christ could be *toteotonatiuh*, “our divine Sun,” or explicitly *tonatiuh in Iesus*, “the Sun Jesus.” A 1583 Nahuatl text describes three suns appearing and becoming one in Trinitarian instruction. Motolinía juxtaposed Christ as Sun of Justice with Mary as Moon. [S23]

This is a datable Christian translation strategy, not evidence that pre-Conquest Nahuas secretly worshiped Jesus under Tonatiuh’s name. At the same time, Indigenous audiences could interpret imported doctrine through existing solar concepts.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

Open state cult disappeared, but Nahua communities retained solar orientation, offerings, agricultural observances, dance, and Christianized celestial language in regionally diverse forms. In the Sierra Norte de Puebla, ethnography documents Sun–Christ identifications and solar meanings in the Dance of the Voladores. These practices are historically layered; they cannot automatically be called unchanged “Tonatiuh worship.” [S24]

The Sun Stone’s excavation in December 1790 transformed Tonatiuh from a suppressed being into a central object of antiquarian, national, and scholarly interpretation. Antonio de León y Gama’s early analysis, followed by nineteenth- and twentieth-century studies, helped make its disputed central face the standard modern image of Tonatiuh.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

The 1978 discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monolith initiated the modern Templo Mayor Project. The 2006 Tlaltecuhtli monolith furnished a monumental, polychrome comparison for the earth deity and consequently sharpened debate over the Sun Stone’s face. [S17, S25]

Modern Mexicayotl and broader Indigenous cultural-revival movements employ solar symbolism, Nahuatl calendar names, dances, dawn observances, and the Sun Stone. Some practitioners address Tonatiuh devotionally; others emphasize cultural philosophy rather than deity worship. No reliable census isolates Tonatiuh worshipers.

A separate, internationally networked polytheist movement now reconstructs worship of the *teteo* through codices and colonial texts. Public sites such as Teochan explicitly describe their practice as a twenty-first-century religion. This is evidence for real contemporary devotion but also an explicit reconstruction, not proof of an unbroken priestly succession. [S26]

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### National and political imagery

The Sun Stone became a national emblem reproduced on coins, museum architecture, schoolbooks, posters, tattoos, and commercial goods. Its central face is routinely labeled Tonatiuh even where specialists acknowledge alternatives. Modern national identity therefore helped canonize one disputed iconographic identification.

The name Tonatiuh remains a common given name in Mexico. Its modern use usually signifies the Sun, Indigenous heritage, masculinity, or national pride rather than membership in an organized cult.

Mexican Indigenous and Chicano movements have used *Nahui Ollin* as an emblem of cultural recovery, motion, historical renewal, and decolonization. Such usage is politically important but does not by itself reproduce a documented Mexica liturgy.

### Literature, visual art, film, comics, and games

Modern works usually combine Tonatiuh, Nanahuatzin, Huitzilopochtli, the Sun Stone, and the Five Suns into one character. This composite is visually effective but historically flattened. Common inventions include:

- a fixed personal rivalry between Tonatiuh and Tezcatlipoca;
- a nuclear family of Aztec gods;
- Tonatiuh as universal king of the pantheon;
- an exact daily ration of two human hearts;
- the Sun Stone as an uncontested portrait;
- Tonatiuh personally fighting Coyolxauhqui every night;
- direct equivalence to Greek Helios or Roman Sol;
- the marigold-lover creation story.

These are **[MODERN RETELLING OR INVENTION]** unless a work explicitly presents them as creative adaptations.

The animated film *The Five Suns: A Sacred History of Mexico* (1996) helped circulate the Nanahuatzin cycle in educational settings. Tabletop games, fantasy fiction, anime, and video games increasingly depict Tonatiuh as a fiery warrior or weapon. Such representations derive primarily from twentieth-century iconographic synthesis rather than one ancient narrative portrait.

### Occult and New Age material

Unlike Egyptian, Greek, or Mesopotamian gods, Tonatiuh did not acquire a substantial, traceable role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or classic European ceremonial magic. Modern web pages assigning him planetary hours, chakras, crystals, tarot correspondences, “lightworker” messages, or a standardized invocation belong to late twentieth- or twenty-first-century eclectic occultism. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

Claims that Tonatiuh was an extraterrestrial, Atlantean ruler, transatlantic counterpart of Christ, or encoded astronomical machine lack support in Nahua texts or archaeology. Comparisons with Christ become historically valid only when tied to colonial doctrinal writing or documented Indigenous-Christian practice.

### Extremist appropriation

Solar and “Aztec warrior” imagery appears in nationalist and masculinist subcultures, but no evidence located for this dossier establishes a major organized extremist cult specifically centered on Tonatiuh. It would be irresponsible to infer one merely from tattoos or online iconography. **Absence of evidence is the present finding.**

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major interpretive traditions

Eduard Seler’s late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century commentaries established comparative reading of the Borgia Group. His identifications remain foundational, though later scholarship has challenged his tendency to derive large astronomical systems from ambiguous images.

Henry B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology grouped Central Mexican deities by cult-theme complexes. It remains useful for recurrent attributes but can make fluid gods appear more discrete and systematic than the sources warrant.

Miguel León-Portilla’s *Aztec Thought and Culture* reconstructed a sophisticated Nahua philosophical tradition from poetry and Sahaguntine texts. Its importance is enormous, but critics caution that “Aztec philosophy” can over-unify texts of different provenance and that the concept of a supreme dual god, Ometeotl, may be less central than León-Portilla proposed.

Michel Graulich emphasized sacrificial myth, the failed dawn, solar cycles, and ritual as reenactment. His chronology and structural interpretations are influential but disputed, especially where he treats veintena festivals as displaced from an original seasonal calendar.

Elizabeth Hill Boone’s work on painted books insists that codices communicate through visual performance, sequence, and divinatory structures rather than acting as illustrated versions of alphabetic myths. This is crucial for Tonatiuh: a solar figure in the *Codex Borgia* cannot simply be supplied with the whole Florentine narrative.

Davíd Carrasco interpreted the Templo Mayor, cosmic terror, sacrifice, and imperial “world center” ideology as mutually constitutive. Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Leonardo López Luján anchored such interpretations in stratigraphic excavation, offerings, architectural stages, and recovered sculpture.

Guilhem Olivier’s work on Tezcatlipoca demonstrated the fluidity, mockery, transformation, and overlapping identities of Central Mexican gods. That method cautions against treating Tonatiuh as a person with one body and fixed family.

### Central controversies

1. **Is Nanahuatzin identical with Tonatiuh?**  
   The transformation narrative supports continuity, but the sources often shift vocabulary from the named sacrificer to “the Sun.” “Nanahuatzin becomes Tonatiuh” is a sound shorthand, not a preserved creedal formula.

2. **Is Huitzilopochtli the Sun?**  
   Huitzilopochtli bears solar-warrior meanings and his Coatepec myth can map onto sunrise, but the manuscripts distinguish him from Tonatiuh. “Solar deity” is safer than “the same god.”

3. **Who occupies the Sun Stone’s center?**  
   Tonatiuh remains the traditional identification; Tlaltecuhtli, Yohualtecuhtli, composite, eclipse-Sun, and royal embodiments remain live alternatives. The monument’s solar subject does not settle the mask’s personal name. [S14–S15]

4. **How early is the named god?**  
   Solar iconography is unquestionably pre-Conquest. The exact name *Tōnatiuh* is securely written only in colonial manuscripts, though the ordinary Nahuatl word must predate their manufacture.

5. **How many victims were sacrificed?**  
   Texts, images, and archaeology establish real and substantial sacrifice. Spectacular colonial totals cannot be accepted without demographic and archaeological control. Huei Tzompantli findings reject denial but do not justify maximalist arithmetic.

6. **Were the codices “myth books”?**  
   Borgia-group books are primarily ritual-divinatory technologies. Reading every scene as an episode in a linear myth is methodologically hazardous.

7. **Did worship survive continuously?**  
   Solar reverence and Sun–Christ practices survived regionally; institutional Tonatiuh cult and its priesthood did not demonstrably continue intact. Modern reconstruction is genuine religion but historically discontinuous.

### State of the evidence

The principal physical witnesses are dispersed:

- *Codex Borgia*: Vatican Apostolic Library.
- Florentine Codex: Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence; now fully digitized.
- Matritense manuscripts: Madrid collections.
- *Codex Telleriano-Remensis*: Bibliothèque nationale de France.
- *Codex Borbonicus*: Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris.
- *Codex Magliabechiano*: Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence.
- *Codex Mendoza*: Bodleian Library, Oxford.
- Sun Stone: Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City.
- Coyolxauhqui and Tlaltecuhtli monoliths, Huei Tzompantli materials, and Templo Mayor offerings: INAH and Museo del Templo Mayor.

The original *Codex Chimalpopoca*, containing the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* and *Leyenda de los Soles*, disappeared from Mexico’s National Museum in the mid-twentieth century. Editions, photographs, and copies preserve its text, but codicological reexamination of the original is impossible.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1200 CE:** Solar, eagle, heart, calendrical, and Venus–Sun complexes occur across Mesoamerica. Calling these figures Tonatiuh remains unproven. **[ANTECEDENTS]**
- **c. 1200–1521:** Central Mexican solar cult and iconography flourish during the Late Postclassic. **[MATERIAL AND PICTORIAL EVIDENCE]**
- **Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century:** Probable production horizon of the *Codex Borgia* and related ritual books; exact dating disputed.
- **1479 / 13 Reed:** Traditional date associated with completion of the Sun Stone under Axayacatl; interpretation is not unanimous.
- **c. 1486–1521:** Late construction and use phases of Tenochtitlan’s ceremonial precinct and Huei Tzompantli.
- **1519–1521:** Pedro de Alvarado receives the Nahua sobriquet *Tonatiuh*; conquest destroys the Mexica state cult.
- **1520:** Toxcatl massacre under Alvarado; the human bearer of the solar nickname becomes an agent of colonial violence.
- **c. 1530s:** *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* records the sequence of Suns.
- **c. 1540s:** *Histoyre du Mechique* and related Olmos-derived accounts preserve variant cosmogonies.
- **c. 1541–1542:** *Codex Mendoza* documents Mexica tribute, conquest, warrior formation, and captive-taking.
- **c. 1540s–1560s:** *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* identifies the present Sun with *Nahui Ollin*.
- **1550s:** Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators begin the Tepeapulco/Matritense documentation represented by the *Primeros Memoriales*.
- **22 May 1558:** The *Leyenda de los Soles* dates its own recording.
- **1569:** Sahagún’s twelve-book Nahuatl organization substantially complete.
- **c. 1575–1577:** Surviving Florentine Codex produced, including the fullest Fifth Sun narrative.
- **1570s–1581:** Durán composes his history and account of gods and rites.
- **1583:** Nahuatl Christian doctrine explicitly employs “Sun Jesus” and Trinitarian solar imagery.
- **Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries:** Temples and images destroyed or repurposed; sacrifice prohibited; solar language survives in Christian translation and local practice.
- **December 1790:** Sun Stone excavated in Mexico City’s Plaza Mayor.
- **1887 onward:** Seler develops the Borgia Group as a comparative scholarly corpus.
- **1903:** Francisco del Paso y Troncoso publishes the text he titles *Leyenda de los Soles*.
- **1950–1982:** Arthur J. O. Anderson and Charles E. Dibble publish the standard English translation of the Florentine Codex.
- **1971:** Nicholson publishes his influential deity-complex typology.
- **1974:** Navarrete and Heyden formally argue that the Sun Stone’s central face is Tlaltecuhtli rather than Tonatiuh.
- **21 February 1978:** Coyolxauhqui monolith discovered, initiating the modern Templo Mayor Project.
- **1996:** *The Five Suns: A Sacred History of Mexico* circulates an animated modern retelling.
- **2 October 2006:** Colossal Tlaltecuhtli monolith found near the Templo Mayor, expanding the comparative corpus for earth-deity imagery.
- **2015–2017:** INAH excavates portions of the Huei Tzompantli and skull tower.
- **2023:** Getty’s Digital Florentine Codex makes the manuscript, Nahuatl transcription, translations, and images broadly searchable.
- **2020s:** Public reconstructionist communities explicitly revive devotion to the *teteo*, including Tonatiuh; scale and continuity remain unquantified.
- **2026:** Tonatiuh persists simultaneously as a reconstructed deity, Nahuatl name for the Sun, national and Indigenous symbol, disputed museum identification, and commercial/fantasy character.

## 11. Sources

### Primary-source editions and documentary anchors

The indispensable starting point is the Digital Florentine Codex, checked against Anderson and Dibble’s edition, particularly Book 7 fols. 1r–6r. The *Leyenda de los Soles* should be read in Nahuatl and in modern critical translation, with explicit recognition that its original manuscript is lost. Borgia-group images require comparative iconographic study rather than narrative paraphrase. Durán, the Magliabechiano group, Telleriano-Remensis, Borbonicus, Mendoza, *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, *Histoyre du Mechique*, *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, and *Cantares Mexicanos* supply complementary but non-identical evidence.

### Selected modern scholarship

Especially important are Eduard Seler’s Borgia commentaries; Nicholson’s 1971 typology; Anderson and Dibble’s Florentine Codex; Elizabeth Hill Boone’s *Cycles of Time and Meaning in the Mexican Books of Fate*; Miguel León-Portilla’s *Aztec Thought and Culture*; Michel Graulich on myth and sacrifice; Davíd Carrasco on Templo Mayor and cosmic order; Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Leonardo López Luján on the sacred precinct; Guilhem Olivier on divine fluidity; Miller and Taube’s *Gods and Symbols of Ancient Mexico and the Maya*; and the scholarship of Cecelia Klein, Doris Heyden, Carlos Navarrete, Richard Townsend, and David Stuart on the Sun Stone.
