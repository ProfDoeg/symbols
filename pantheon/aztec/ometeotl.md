# Ometeotl (Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl): Research Dossier

**Class:** Primordial divine pair—male **Ometecuhtli** and female **Omecihuatl**—in several sixteenth-century central-Mexican sources. Their interpretation as two aspects of one supreme, omnipresent, metaphysical god called **Ometeotl** is an influential modern scholarly reconstruction, not an uncontested description found consistently in the primary corpus.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently identifiable written witness is the anonymous Spanish **_Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas_**, composed from Indigenous pictorial and oral materials in approximately the 1530s–1540s. It names the primordial pair **Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl**, says that they had always existed in the thirteenth heaven and that their beginning was unknown, and makes them parents of four creator gods. The text does **not** call this pair “Ometeotl.” The explicit names **Ometecuhtli** and **Omecihuatl** occur securely in later sixteenth-century sources, especially Nahuatl speeches incorporated into Sahagún’s corpus and the French **_Histoyre du Mechique_**. No securely identified preconquest inscription, temple, dedicatory monument, or independently attributable image names a unitary Ometeotl.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** sixteenth-century witness preserving Indigenous-language, pictorial, or informant-derived material, though recorded after the conquest.
- **[CE] Colonial elaboration:** sixteenth- or seventeenth-century exposition shaped by translation, Christian categories, or compiler synthesis.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant:** evidence belonging to a particular city, manuscript, or narrative strand.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** colonial or modern arrangement of disparate names and texts into a theological system.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned modern interpretation not stated in this form by the surviving primary evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim:** recent material presented, incorrectly or without evidence, as ancient.
- **[AE] Absence of evidence:** a claim for which the relevant archaeological, pictorial, or textual corpus supplies no demonstrable support.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identification

The securely documented core is a **male-female primordial pair associated with the upper heaven, human generation, and the origin or dispatch of other gods and human children**. Sources variously call that pair Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl, Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl, or use paired parental expressions. Whether all such pairs were understood everywhere as one pair under changing functional names—and whether they jointly constituted one being called Ometeotl—is precisely the disputed question.

Miguel León-Portilla’s reconstruction made **Ometeotl**, “Two-God” or “God of Duality,” the supreme Nahua metaphysical principle: simultaneously male and female, self-generated, invisible, omnipresent, and manifested through numerous divine titles. Richard Haly objected that this being was largely manufactured by combining unrelated or ambiguous passages, mistranslating texts, and elevating a very sparsely attested expression into the center of Mexica religion. More recent work generally accepts that paired generative divinities and the notion of **Omeyocan** are genuinely attested while treating the unitary, pantheistic “Ometeotl” theology much more cautiously. Haly’s article, “Bare Bones: Rethinking Mesoamerican Divinity,” remains the decisive published challenge ([History of Religions, 1992](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/463285)).

### The “bone” alternative

Haly emphasizes that colonial orthography permits confusion among forms read as **ome-**, **omi-/omitl**, and related sequences. An especially important passage in the _Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas_ names the fourth son something like **Omiteuctli**, followed by another name, Maquizcoatl, and identifies him with Huitzilopochtli. Haly argues that this is “Bone Lord,” not “Ometeotl.” Thus one of the passages once used to multiply attestations of Ometeotl does not contain that name at all. A UNAM study likewise notes the rival analyses “place/god of two” and “place/god of bone” in the relevant vocabulary ([Estudios de Cultura Náhuatl](https://nahuatl.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/download/9201/8579/8985)).

### Classification

- **[EA]** Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl are a divine lord and lady, addressed as parents in ritual rhetoric.
- **[EA/RV]** Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl are primordial spouses and parents of creator gods.
- **[SR]** Ometeotl is one androgynous or gender-complementary supreme being whose two “aspects” are Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl.
- **[MI]** Ometeotl as an impersonal “cosmic energy,” an exact equivalent of the Tao, Brahman, the Kabbalistic Ein Sof, Jung’s Self, or a pre-Columbian proof of monotheism. Such comparisons may be modern theology or comparative philosophy, but they are not ancient attestations.

No Greek, Roman, or other ancient interpretatio is recorded. Colonial equations with the Christian Creator or Trinity belong to evangelization and postconquest intellectual history, not preconquest theology.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### _Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas_

**Date and medium:** Anonymous Spanish prose, probably 1530s–1540s, derived from now-lost pictorial histories and Indigenous explanations.

**What it says [EA/CE]:** A god called Tonacatecuhtli and his wife Tonacacihuatl “had always been” in the thirteenth heaven; their beginning was unknown. They produced four sons. In the transmitted Spanish these are:

1. red Tezcatlipoca, also called Camaxtli;
2. black Tezcatlipoca;
3. Quetzalcoatl;
4. Omiteuctli/Maquizcoatl, identified with Huitzilopochtli.

After 600 years the sons gathered and began ordering the cosmos. The source therefore securely attests a primordial parental pair and four divine sons. It does **not** securely attest a single god Ometeotl. The oft-repeated formulation that Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl directly created the universe is an interpretive compression: in this account their sons perform most subsequent cosmogenesis.

### Sahagún’s Nahuatl corpus

**Primeros Memoriales:** compiled at Tepeapulco approximately 1558–1561, with Indigenous elders and painters. It is the earliest stage of Sahagún’s project, but it does not provide an extensive Ometeotl theology. Its importance is comparative: its detailed lists of gods, feasts, vestments, priests, and temples do not reveal a major public Ometeotl cult.

**Florentine Codex:** completed in its surviving bilingual, illustrated form in 1577 at Tlatelolco. The manuscript has parallel Nahuatl and Spanish columns and nearly 2,500 images; it is now held by the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana and available in the [Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/).

The decisive references occur principally in **Book VI**, the collection of formal speeches, prayers, and moral rhetoric:

- A midwife or ritual speaker asks how the child was bestowed by “your mother, your father, Ometecuhtli, Omecihuatl”: **_Quen omitztlamamacac in monan, in motah, in Ometecuhtli, in Omecihuatl_**. **[EA]**
- Other speeches locate human destiny or children with the divine parents in Omeyocan. **[EA]**
- Paired expressions such as “our mother, our father” are frequent but cannot all be converted into proper names for Ometeotl. **[Interpretive caution]**
- Book X includes a cosmological-geographical notice placing Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl above the layered heavens. **[EA/CE]**

These passages establish that the names were meaningful in colonial Nahua ritual discourse. They do not state that all other gods are manifestations of a unitary Ometeotl, nor do Books I–II give this being a feast comparable to Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, Tezcatlipoca, or major agricultural gods.

### _Histoyre du Mechique_

**Date and transmission:** A French-language compilation associated with André Thevet, based on a lost Spanish or Nahuatl-informed account, conventionally dated to the mid-sixteenth century.

**Evidence [CE/RV]:** Its cosmology says that in the thirteenth celestial level was a god named **Ometecuhtli**, glossed as “two gods,” and that one was a goddess. This is unusually explicit evidence for a divine two-in-one formulation. Yet the text reaches us through several linguistic and editorial filters, and its statement should not be retrojected automatically into all Mexica practice.

### _Cantares Mexicanos_

**Date and medium:** Alphabetic Nahuatl song manuscript, principally copied circa 1580s; Biblioteca Nacional de México, MS 1628 bis. The songs may preserve earlier performance materials but were collected in a colonial Christian environment.

A passage reads **_ōme ihcac yehhuān Dios_**, approximately “they/God stand(s) twofold” or “God stands in two.” León-Portilla treated this as a reference to Ometeotl. **[Disputed]** The manuscript actually uses the Spanish loanword **Dios**, not the proper noun *Ometeotl*. Christian and Nahua registers are interwoven throughout the collection; the line cannot independently establish a preconquest supreme god. The manuscript can be consulted through UNAM’s [Temoa Cantares Mexicanos portal](https://temoa.iib.unam.mx/cantares-cantares-mexicanos/50v).

### _Anales de Cuauhtitlan_

**Date:** Nahuatl annalistic text assembled in the later sixteenth century, surviving in the _Codex Chimalpopoca_.

It places **Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl** in primordial or upper-world settings and participates in traditions of Quetzalcoatl, creation, and celestial ordering. **[EA/RV]** It does not plainly supply León-Portilla’s full Ometeotl metaphysics.

### _Leyenda de los Soles_

**Date:** Nahuatl text copied in 1558, also transmitted in the _Codex Chimalpopoca_.

In the creation of the present sun, Nanahuatzin becomes the sun and ascends; **Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl receive him** or are associated with his upper-celestial station. Other passages name the celestial pair Citlallatonac and Citlalicue. **[EA/RV]** Their identity with the Ometeotl pair is a scholarly harmonization unless explicitly signaled in a given passage.

### Codex Vaticanus A / Codex Ríos

**Date and medium:** Colonial pictorial-explanatory manuscript, generally dated to the later sixteenth century; European paper with Italian commentary, now Vatican Library, Vat. Lat. 3738.

Folio 1v represents the layered cosmos and labels the highest region with a form commonly read **Hometeule/Ometeotl**. Elsewhere the same or closely related creator figure appears as Tonacatecuhtli, patron of 1-Cipactli. **[CE/RV]** This supplies genuine colonial evidence for equivalence or overlap, but the commentary is already a European-mediated systematization. It is not a preconquest inscription.

### Divinatory codices and the Tonacatecuhtli pair

The preconquest or early-colonial **Borgia Group**—especially the _Codex Borgia_, _Codex Vaticanus B_, _Codex Cospi_, and _Codex Fejérváry-Mayer_—preserves calendrical-divinatory imagery. **Tonacatecuhtli** is securely associated with the first trecena, 1-Cipactli, sometimes accompanied by a female partner. Elizabeth Hill Boone’s _Cycles of Time and Meaning in the Mexican Books of Fate_ and Eduard Seler’s commentaries remain fundamental.

These images are important evidence for an old creator/sustenance deity or pair. They cannot be identified automatically as portraits of “Ometeotl” because the manuscripts lack alphabetic name captions and different scholars identify individual figures differently.

The **Codex Borbonicus** also depicts the patronage of 1-Cipactli. Modern captions often call the pair “Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl,” but **Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl** is the safer identification. Claims that Borbonicus supplies an uncontested portrait of the unitary Ometeotl are **[MI/unsupported attribution]**.

### Other named codices

- **Codex Mendoza:** Valuable for tribute, imperial institutions, conquest history, education, and some ritual imagery. **[AE]** It supplies no clear named Ometeotl theology or cult.
- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis:** Important for calendar, deities, and annals and related to the source tradition behind Vaticanus A. Its cosmological glosses contribute indirectly, but it does not independently establish León-Portilla’s supreme principle.
- **Codex Magliabechiano:** Rich in feasts, gods, mortuary customs, and sacrifice. **[AE]** No demonstrable public feast or temple of Ometeotl.
- **Codex Borgia:** Essential for Tonacatecuhtli and the 1-Cipactli complex, but not alphabetic proof of Ometeotl.
- **Codex Borbonicus:** Same caution: a creator pair may be pictured; the names assigned in modern captions are interpretations.

### Durán

Diego Durán’s _Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme_, including the commonly separated _Book of the Gods and Rites_, was written principally in the 1570s–1580s. Durán gives extensive descriptions of state temples, festivals, divine images, sacrifice, and political ritual. **[AE]** He does not describe a public temple, priesthood, image, or annual festival of Ometeotl comparable to those of Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, Tezcatlipoca, or Quetzalcoatl. That silence is significant against claims that Ometeotl was the universally recognized head of the state pantheon, though it cannot disprove restricted, local, or elite discourse.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Securely supported functions

- **Divine parenthood [EA]:** Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl are invoked as the remote father and mother from whom a newborn has been bestowed.
- **Upper-heaven residence [EA/CE]:** They reside in or above the layered heavens, usually Omeyocan or the thirteenth heaven.
- **Generation of creator gods [EA/RV]:** Under the names Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl, they parent the four gods who organize the cosmos.
- **Human generation and sustenance [EA/RV]:** The Tonaca- names associate the pair with bodily substance, food, abundance, and the origin or dispatch of children.
- **Calendrical beginning [EA/iconographic]:** Tonacatecuhtli presides over 1-Cipactli, the beginning of the 260-day divinatory count.

### Reconstructed attributes

León-Portilla’s Ometeotl is self-generated, omnipresent, invisible, beyond ordinary cult, and immanent in every polarity: male/female, light/dark, life/death, motion/rest. **[SR]** The reconstruction draws on authentic Nahua parallelism and paired speech, but no surviving primary passage gathers these propositions into one systematic creed.

James Maffie subsequently interpreted **teotl** as a single, self-transforming sacred process and Nahua metaphysics as monistic or pantheistic. This is a sophisticated philosophical reconstruction rather than a translation of one Indigenous text. Treating *teotl* simply as “energy” is especially misleading: in the colonial corpus the term can denote a god, sacred potency, something extraordinary, or—in Christian usage—the biblical God.

### Iconography

No image can presently be identified as a unitary Ometeotl with the security available for Tlaloc’s goggle eyes, Xipe Totec’s flayed skin, or Tezcatlipoca’s smoking mirror.

Possible representations include:

1. **Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl in divinatory manuscripts [EA, identification variable]:** enthroned or seated creator figures attached to 1-Cipactli, sometimes with food, bloodletting, water, maize, or generative signs appropriate to the trecena.
2. **Upper-heaven figure in Codex Vaticanus A [CE]:** a seated divine figure or pair in the highest cosmic tier, explained by a colonial gloss.
3. **Borbonicus creator pair [disputed caption]:** often reproduced as Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl, but the manuscript itself does not supply those alphabetic names.

There is no established Ometeotl emblem, weapon, temple costume, sacrificial knife, sacred animal, color, or festival day. Assignments of red/black, masculine light/feminine darkness, a flint phallus, or a half-male/half-female body commonly encountered online are **[MI]** unless tied to a specific manuscript image and defended as an interpretation.

The **Sun Stone** does not securely depict Ometeotl at its center. Competing identifications include Tonatiuh, Tlaltecuhtli, or a composite solar-earth power. Calling the central face “Ometecuhtli-Omecihuatl” is a modern speculative attribution.

The Coyolxauhqui stone discovered in 1978 and the Tlaltecuhtli monolith found in 2006 illuminate the Templo Mayor’s ritual landscape but do not portray Ometeotl. Their relevance is methodological: unmistakable monumental cult images survive for other beings, whereas none has been securely assigned to Ometeotl. The INAH dates the discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monument to 21 February 1978 and records the Tlaltecuhtli discovery on 2 October 2006 ([Proyecto Templo Mayor](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/historia/proyecto-templo-mayor); [INAH, Tlaltecuhtli](https://inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/tlaltecuhtli-la-deidad-de-tierra-que-emergio-del-templo-mayor)).

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

#### Version A: Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl as primordial parents

In the _Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas_, the pair exists without a known beginning in the thirteenth heaven and produces four sons. **[EA/CE]**

- red Tezcatlipoca/Camaxtli;
- black Tezcatlipoca;
- Quetzalcoatl;
- Omiteuctli/Maquizcoatl/Huitzilopochtli.

This is the primary source for the familiar “parents of the four Tezcatlipocas” scheme. “Four Tezcatlipocas,” however, is a scholarly shorthand. The source does not consistently give all four that title, and its fourth name is textually difficult.

#### Version B: Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl as parents of human children

Sahagún’s formal speeches invoke them as the mother and father who bestowed a newborn. **[EA]** This is ritual kinship and divine generation, not a complete narrative genealogy.

#### Version C: Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl as calendrical creator pair

The divinatory books place the pair at calendrical beginning. **[EA/iconographic]** This establishes cosmological seniority but not necessarily the four-son genealogy.

#### Version D: one Ometeotl differentiating into male and female

This familiar modern account—first one god, then bifurcation into Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl—is **[SR]**. The _Histoyre du Mechique_ comes closest by describing Ometecuhtli as “two gods” of whom one was a goddess, but it does not narrate an original singular being splitting in two.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The primordial pair and the four sons

**Earliest source:** _Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas_, c. 1530s–1540s.

Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl have no reported beginning. They dwell in the thirteenth heaven and produce four sons. After a long interval—given as 600 years—the sons agree to make what is necessary for cosmic order. They create or organize water, earth, heavens, time, and divine offices; the narrative then proceeds into the contests of Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl and the successive Suns.

**Development:** León-Portilla recast the parents as Ometeotl and interpreted the four sons as directional/color manifestations: red, black, white, and blue Tezcatlipocas. Some color-direction relationships are well founded in broader Mesoamerican symbolism, but the perfectly ordered fourfold emanation scheme is **[LS/SR]** rather than the literal form of every source.

### The divine parents and human birth

**Source:** Florentine Codex Book VI, completed 1577 from earlier collected speeches.

In childbirth and naming rhetoric, the infant has been formed or bestowed in the upper divine realm and sent to earth by Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl, “your mother, your father.” Midwives speak to the child as a newcomer entrusted to earthly parents. The mythic action is not a single extended story but a recurring ritual proposition: human birth begins with the remote divine pair.

This is among the strongest evidence that the names were religiously functional rather than a purely modern fabrication. At the same time, it supports a pair of divine parents more directly than a unitary metaphysical Absolute.

### The uppermost heaven

Several sources arrange the cosmos vertically. Omeyocan appears at or beyond the upper heavens and is the home of Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl or the Tonacatecuhtli pair. The exact number of heavens and the placement of named couples vary. A recent source study stresses that not every account gives the same thirteen-level scheme and that different paired beings can occupy different strata ([UNAM, _Los cielos, los rumbos y los números_](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/cielos/CEI004.pdf)).

The common modern diagram—nine underworlds, earth, thirteen heavens, with one Ometeotl enthroned at the absolute summit—is therefore a useful composite, not a transcript of a single preconquest canonical chart.

### The birth of the Sun

In the _Leyenda de los Soles_, Nanahuatzin sacrifices himself in the divine fire and becomes the sun. The upper primordial couple receives or locates him in the celestial order. **[EA/RV]** This does not make them the sacrificial agents or the Sun itself.

### What is not attested

- no surviving “creation from nothing” by Ometeotl;
- no securely ancient story in which a solitary Ometeotl divides itself into two halves;
- no narrative of Ometeotl’s birth, death, dismemberment, descent, or resurrection;
- no direct creation of the present human race uniquely by Ometeotl;
- no ancient exposition equating Ometeotl with every god;
- no securely preconquest teaching that Ometeotl is “pure consciousness” or “cosmic energy.”

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Public cult

**[AE] No securely identified temple, shrine, priesthood, annual feast, state sacrifice, cult statue, or pilgrimage center dedicated under the name Ometeotl, Ometecuhtli, or Omecihuatl is known.**

This absence is especially notable because Sahagún, Durán, the Codex Magliabechiano, Codex Borbonicus, and archaeological work at the Templo Mayor document the rites of many other gods in great detail.

Possible explanations must remain separate:

1. **León-Portilla’s explanation [SR]:** Ometeotl was a remote, transcendent god of learned elites and therefore had no ordinary image or cult.
2. **Critical explanation [SR]:** there was no central supreme Ometeotl cult because the supposed unitary deity is a modern synthesis.
3. **Intermediate explanation [SR]:** primordial pairs were real components of ritual speech and divination but did not form an independent public cult institution.

The evidence presently cannot decide conclusively among all three, but it strongly excludes confident descriptions of a large Ometeotl temple establishment.

### Ritual speech and childbirth

The clearest practice is verbal invocation in birth rhetoric. Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl are named as the supernatural parents or donors of the infant. **[EA]** This suggests domestic and life-cycle relevance without proving a specialized priesthood.

### Divination

Tonacatecuhtli’s position as patron of the 1-Cipactli trecena placed the creator/sustenance complex within professional calendrical divination. Diviners reading the 260-day count would have handled the corresponding imagery and prognostics. **[EA/iconographic]**

### Offerings and sacrifice

No sacrificial rite can securely be assigned specifically to Ometeotl. Claims that all Mexica sacrifices were ultimately directed to Ometeotl are **[SR/MI]**.

Human sacrifice in Mexica state religion is nevertheless securely documented by Indigenous and Spanish texts, images, cut-marked human remains, skull masks, sacrificial deposits, and the Huei Tzompantli. Between 2015 and 2017, excavations at 24 República de Guatemala identified part of Tenochtitlan’s principal skull-rack complex and skull tower ([INAH, Huei Tzompantli](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907)). Bioarchaeological research also documents decapitated skulls and reused skull masks in Templo Mayor offerings ([Current Anthropology](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/686530)).

This evidence confirms sacrificial killing and ritual display at Tenochtitlan. It does **not** connect those remains to Ometeotl. The principal monumental complex was devoted especially to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc, with numerous subsidiary cults. Colonial numerical claims involving tens of thousands of deaths at individual ceremonies remain debated and should not be treated as archaeological counts.

### Temple prostitution

No evidence connects Ometeotl with temple prostitution. More broadly, “temple prostitution” is not an appropriate established category for Mexica cult on the present evidence. Sexual abstinence, ritualized sexuality, concubinage, and gendered service occur in colonial descriptions, but that does not substantiate an institution of sacred prostitution.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1521: reconstructable preconquest background

The Borgia-group manuscripts and early calendrical imagery support an old **Tonacatecuhtli creator/sustenance complex associated with 1-Cipactli**. Paired male-female organization is widespread in Mesoamerican religion. **[EA iconographic inference]**

What cannot be securely projected backward is a uniform imperial doctrine of one supreme Ometeotl. No preconquest stone inscription spells the name; no temple foundation deposit identifies such a cult; and no uncontested preconquest image depicts an androgynous supreme god.

### 1521–1600: preservation under conquest and evangelization

After Tenochtitlan’s fall in 1521, churches and colonial buildings replaced or incorporated Indigenous sacred architecture, cult images were destroyed or hidden, and missionaries investigated Indigenous religion in order to suppress “idolatry.” Sahagún explicitly framed his ethnography as knowledge needed for Christian diagnosis and conversion. The surviving evidence for the pair was therefore recorded within an asymmetrical colonial encounter.

Important witnesses appeared in rapid succession:

- _Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas_, c. 1530s–1540s;
- _Histoyre du Mechique_, mid-sixteenth century;
- _Leyenda de los Soles_, copied 1558;
- Sahagún’s Tepeapulco materials, c. 1558–1561;
- Florentine Codex, completed 1577;
- _Cantares Mexicanos_, chiefly 1580s;
- Vaticanus A/Codex Ríos, later sixteenth century.

Christian vocabulary affected interpretation. “God,” Creator, heaven, and Trinity offered translators ready categories, while Nahua speakers also appropriated Christian **Dios** in songs and discourse. Consequently neither complete Christian invention nor completely untouched preconquest transcription is an adequate model.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

Juan de Torquemada’s _Monarquía indiana_ (1615) further systematized earlier missionary information. Colonial extirpation continued against Indigenous ritual specialists, calendars, offerings, and images, but Ometeotl does not emerge as a well-documented clandestine popular cult.

Antiquarians including Lorenzo Boturini, Alexander von Humboldt, Eduard Seler, Alfredo Chavero, and Francisco del Paso y Troncoso recovered, copied, or published the codices. The divine pair became an object of comparative mythology rather than a documented living cult.

### Mid-twentieth century: construction of the supreme Ometeotl

Miguel León-Portilla’s _La filosofía náhuatl estudiada en sus fuentes_ (first published 1956) and its English adaptation, _Aztec Thought and Culture_, placed Ometeotl at the center of a philosophical Nahua theology. Drawing heavily on Ángel María Garibay’s philology and on elite poetic texts, León-Portilla argued that the **tlamatinime**, “knowers” or sages, discovered a single dual principle beneath popular polytheism.

This interpretation had enormous cultural force. It offered a sophisticated Indigenous philosophy equal to Old World philosophical systems and countered older depictions of Mexica religion as merely bloody or irrational. These intellectual and political stakes do not decide whether the philology is correct, but they explain the reconstruction’s influence.

### 1971 onward: typology and contextual religion

Henry B. Nicholson’s 1971 typology classified central Mexican deities into conceptual complexes rather than treating every name as a wholly independent personality. The approach helped scholars understand overlapping titles and manifestations but also warned against assuming that a modern synthetic name was itself the ancient center of the system.

Research by Michel Graulich, David Carrasco, Richard Townsend, Elizabeth Hill Boone, Alfredo López Austin, Leonardo López Luján, Guilhem Olivier, and others increasingly emphasized ritual context, calendrical books, embodiment, sacrifice, political theology, and regional variation.

### 1992 onward: the Haly critique

Haly argued:

- “Ometeotl” is not securely or frequently a divine proper name;
- León-Portilla silently supplies Ometeotl as the referent of passages that name other gods or use generic paired titles;
- _ōme ihcac yehhuān Dios_ in the _Cantares_ is not the name Ometeotl;
- the supposed fourth divine son is **Omiteuctli**, likely “Bone Lord,” not Ometeotl;
- many “supreme god” epithets properly belong to Tezcatlipoca;
- *Omeyocan* and male-female creator pairs do not prove philosophical monotheism.

The critique does not erase the attested Ometecuhtli/Omecihuatl pair. Its target is their transformation into the omnipresent unitary Ometeotl of modern exposition.

### Twenty-first century

Current scholarship is plural:

- some continue León-Portilla’s account of Ometeotl as supreme duality;
- some reject Ometeotl altogether as a modern scholarly construct;
- an increasingly useful middle position accepts real paired creator traditions and genuine Ometecuhtli/Omecihuatl references while declining to posit a single systematic doctrine behind every parental or dual title.

Modern Mexicayotl, reconstructionist, pan-Indigenous, and New Age communities employ Ometeotl in different ways. Some use the term as a greeting or blessing; some worship a dual creator; some understand it as universal sacred energy. These are living modern religious developments, not simply unchanged survivals.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Mexican philosophy and cultural nationalism

León-Portilla’s Ometeotl became emblematic of a rational, poetic, nondual Indigenous philosophy. The reconstruction entered textbooks, museums, murals, popular mythographies, and cultural-nationalist discourse. It also influenced interpretations of Rufino Tamayo and other modern artists; scholarship on Tamayo explicitly notes the twentieth-century transformation of the primordial duality into a modern artistic and intellectual symbol ([SciELO](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-19182011000300005&script=sci_arttext)).

### Mexicayotl and neomexicanidad

Mexicayotl revival developed through twentieth-century Mexicanidad networks rather than through a demonstrable unbroken priesthood of Ometeotl. Later neomexicanidad combined Indigenous revival, conchero dance, ecological discourse, alternative therapy, and international New Age ideas. Researchers distinguish more nationalist/reconstructionist Mexicanidad from eclectic neomexicanidad and document major transnational networking around the 1992 quincentenary ([SciELO netnography](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-16592012000300010&script=sci_arttext); [de la Torre and Gutiérrez Zúñiga](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00377686231216447)).

Practitioners are not uniform. Some reject the supreme-Ometeotl theory and emphasize polytheistic praxis; others treat Ometeotl as the divine totality. One contemporary reconstructionist resource explicitly warns against New Age claims, denial of human sacrifice, and purported secret unbroken lineages ([Teochan](https://teochan.org/about.html)).

### Occult and New Age equations

The following equations are modern and should be labeled **[MI or modern syncretism]** unless a practitioner is being described on their own terms:

- Ometeotl = Kabbalistic Ein Sof;
- Omeyocan = the highest Kabbalistic aeon;
- Ometecuhtli = “Cosmic Christ”;
- Ometeotl = Tao, Brahman, quantum field, universal vibration, or “male and female energy”;
- Ometeotl as the secret doctrine suppressed by both ordinary Mexica worshippers and Franciscans;
- an initiatory lineage transmitting a complete Ometeotl theology unchanged to the present.

These may be meaningful contemporary beliefs. They are not verified preconquest traditions.

### Literature, art, games, and commerce

Ometeotl appears in modern mythology handbooks, fantasy fiction, role-playing games, online deity databases, tattoos, jewelry, and spiritual merchandise. The dominant image—an abstract androgynous creator divided into complementary male and female halves—owes far more to León-Portilla, comparative religion, and contemporary graphic conventions than to a securely identified Mexica cult image.

The attribution of the Sun Stone’s central face to Ometeotl and images showing one body split vertically into man and woman are particularly widespread modern visual inventions.

### Political appropriation

Ometeotl can serve inclusive Indigenous revival, decolonial philosophy, gender-complementarity theology, or nationalist claims that ancient Mexico possessed a monotheism equivalent or superior to Christianity. Conversely, polemical writers sometimes use the Haly critique to dismiss all Nahua duality as fabrication. Both moves exceed the evidence: genuine paired divine traditions survive, but the grand unified system remains disputed.

No major, well-documented extremist organization centered specifically on Ometeotl was established in the sources reviewed. Broader ethnic-nationalist appropriation exists, but attributing extremism to Ometeotl worship as such would be unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### León-Portilla’s case

León-Portilla assembled the following:

- explicit Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl references in Sahagún;
- Omeyocan as their upper-world abode;
- the Tonacatecuhtli pair as primordial parents;
- poetic references to a god who “stands twofold”;
- paired titles such as Mother-Father of the Gods;
- epithets including Tloque Nahuaque, Ipalnemohuani, and Moyocoyani;
- broader Nahua preference for parallel, complementary expression.

He concluded that learned Nahua thought recognized **Ometeotl**, a single generative duality behind the divine plurality. His argument can be read directly in the UNAM digital edition of [_La filosofía náhuatl_](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/filosofia/046_04_22_otros_aspectos.pdf).

### Haly’s case

Haly’s criticism is both philological and historiographic. A divine pair is not equivalent to one metaphysical being; grammatical or rhetorical duality is not proof of monotheism; and names applied to Tezcatlipoca or other beings cannot be reassigned to Ometeotl without explicit evidence. The very scarcity of the alleged supreme god in descriptions of temples and festivals counts against centrality.

Haly sometimes states the negative conclusion provocatively—as though Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl themselves did not exist—but the primary speeches do name them. The strongest sustainable form of his argument is therefore: **the supreme unitary Ometeotl of modern accounts is not securely demonstrated**, not “every component of the tradition was invented in the twentieth century.”

### The problem of Christianization

The source base was written after 1521 by Indigenous authors, painters, assistants, and informants working with Christian clergy. Potential influences run in several directions:

- missionaries translated Indigenous plurality into “God” and “devil”;
- Indigenous speakers used Christian terms to preserve or reformulate older concepts;
- elite authors could present Indigenous religion through philosophical categories intelligible to Europeans;
- modern scholars, reacting against colonial denigration, could favor monotheistic or metaphysical readings.

Each passage must consequently be read in its manuscript setting. The word **Dios** in a colonial song is evidence of colonial religious language, not automatically a transparent transcription of a preconquest proper name.

### Tezcatlipoca as rival referent

Guilhem Olivier’s _Mockeries and Metamorphoses of an Aztec God_ demonstrates the extraordinary range of Tezcatlipoca’s names, transformations, and claims to omnipresence. Many epithets recruited into Ometeotl theology—especially Moyocoyani and Tloque Nahuaque—are securely applied to Tezcatlipoca. This strengthens the critique of a universal Ometeotl while showing why Mexica divine identity cannot be reduced to fixed Greco-Roman-style individuals.

### Material evidence

The archaeological record has greatly expanded since León-Portilla’s initial formulation:

- Coyolxauhqui stone, discovered 1978;
- sustained Templo Mayor excavation under Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Leonardo López Luján;
- Tlaltecuhtli monolith, discovered 2006;
- Huei Tzompantli remains excavated 2015–2017;
- extensive offerings containing animals, plants, prestige materials, weapons, and human remains.

These finds have transformed understanding of Mexica ritual, empire, and sacrifice. None has yet produced a securely inscribed Ometeotl shrine or image.

### State of the primary evidence

- Florentine Codex: Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence; complete digital edition available.
- Sahagún’s Madrid manuscripts/Primeros Memoriales: Real Biblioteca and Real Academia de la Historia collections, Madrid.
- _Cantares Mexicanos_: Biblioteca Nacional de México.
- _Codex Chimalpopoca_ original: lost after the mid-twentieth century; survives through photographs, copies, and editions.
- Codex Vaticanus A: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana.
- Codex Borgia: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana.
- Codex Borbonicus: Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris.
- Codex Mendoza: Bodleian Library, Oxford.
- Codex Telleriano-Remensis: Bibliothèque nationale de France.
- Codex Magliabechiano: Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence.

The loss of original pictorial sources behind colonial prose accounts prevents certainty about how much narrative structure was present in the paintings and how much was supplied orally or editorially.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| **Late Postclassic, before 1521** | Borgia-group and related divinatory manuscripts attest Tonacatecuhtli or a creator pair associated with 1-Cipactli. No alphabetic Ometeotl inscription is known. |
| **1325–1521** | Tenochtitlan develops its state cult and ceremonial precinct. Archaeology richly attests Huitzilopochtli, Tlaloc, earth gods, sacrifice, and calendrical ritual; no independent Ometeotl sanctuary is identified. |
| **1521** | Fall of Tenochtitlan to the Spanish-Tlaxcalan coalition; dismantling, destruction, concealment, and Christian reuse of Indigenous sacred sites follow. |
| **c. 1530s–1540s** | _Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas_ records Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl as beginningless inhabitants of the thirteenth heaven and parents of four sons. Earliest secure textual witness to the dossier’s core complex. |
| **Mid-sixteenth century** | _Histoyre du Mechique_ describes Ometecuhtli in the highest heaven as “two gods,” one a goddess. |
| **1558** | _Leyenda de los Soles_ copied; associates the Tonacatecuhtli pair with the ascent of Nanahuatzin/Sun and preserves other celestial-pair traditions. |
| **c. 1558–1561** | Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators compile the Tepeapulco materials later called _Primeros Memoriales_. |
| **1577** | Florentine Codex completed; Book VI preserves invocations of Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl as divine parents. |
| **1570s–1580s** | Durán records major state gods and rites but no independent Ometeotl cult. |
| **c. 1580s** | _Cantares Mexicanos_ copied; the ambiguous phrase _ōme ihcac yehhuān Dios_ later becomes important to León-Portilla’s argument. |
| **Late sixteenth century** | Codex Vaticanus A/Codex Ríos depicts the layered cosmos and provides a colonial gloss commonly read as Ometeotl/Hometeule. |
| **1615** | Torquemada’s _Monarquía indiana_ further systematizes early colonial reports of Indigenous theology. |
| **Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries** | Codices and colonial histories are reproduced and critically edited by antiquarians and Mesoamericanists including Seler and del Paso y Troncoso. |
| **1956** | First edition of León-Portilla’s _La filosofía náhuatl estudiada en sus fuentes_ makes Ometeotl the central principle of Nahua philosophy. |
| **1963** | English _Aztec Thought and Culture_ disseminates the interpretation internationally. |
| **1971** | Nicholson publishes his influential typology of central Mexican religious concepts. |
| **21 February 1978** | Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone initiates the modern Proyecto Templo Mayor. No Ometeotl connection is established. |
| **1992** | Richard Haly publishes “Bare Bones,” arguing that the supreme Ometeotl is a modern construction from thin and misread evidence. |
| **1990s–present** | Mexicayotl, neomexicanidad, philosophical, and New Age movements adopt varied concepts of Ometeotl. |
| **2 October 2006** | Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered near the Templo Mayor, greatly expanding material knowledge of Mexica earth theology but not attesting Ometeotl. |
| **2015–2017** | Excavations identify portions of the Huei Tzompantli and skull tower, materially confirming large-scale ritual skull display; no specific Ometeotl dedication is found. |
| **2023** | Getty Research Institute launches the expanded Digital Florentine Codex, enabling manuscript-level comparison of Nahuatl, Spanish, and images. |
| **Present** | Scholarly opinion remains divided between León-Portilla’s supreme-duality interpretation, strong constructivist rejection, and intermediate contextual readings of several related divine pairs. |

### Evidentiary conclusion

The historical record securely supports **primordial male and female divine figures named Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl**, an upper realm called **Omeyocan**, and a closely related or overlapping **Tonacatecuhtli–Tonacacihuatl** creator pair. It also securely supports a tradition in which the latter pair generated four major creator gods.

What the record does **not** securely establish is that preconquest Mexica religion possessed a universally recognized, single, supreme, omnipresent god named Ometeotl who subsumed every other deity. That proposition is principally León-Portilla’s twentieth-century synthesis. Haly demonstrated serious weaknesses in several of its textual warrants, but the strongest counterposition is not that every element was invented: it is that authentic paired gods, places, titles, and ritual expressions were joined into a much more systematic metaphysical unity than the surviving sources themselves articulate.
