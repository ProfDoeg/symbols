# Chicomecoatl: Research Dossier

**Class:** female *teōtl*—a Mexica/Nahua divine power conventionally translated “goddess”—personifying maize, agricultural sustenance, and especially mature or seed maize.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Late Postclassic central Mexico, before the Spanish invasion of 1519–1521. Preconquest or contact-period stone images identifiable by maize ears and the *amacalli* paper headdress provide the earliest physical evidence, though most surviving examples lack excavated provenance and can only be dated broadly, approximately 1325–1521. The earliest richly contextualized written and pictorial records are early-colonial: the *Primeros Memoriales* (about 1558–1561) and Florentine Codex (compiled approximately 1547–1577), based substantially on testimony from Nahua elders and specialists.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** preconquest or contact-period object, or early-colonial record demonstrably preserving Indigenous testimony.
- **[CE] Colonial elaboration:** sixteenth- or seventeenth-century description, image, or interpretation.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a locally distinct Nahua or central Mexican tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** scholarly or priestly effort to organize fluid divine identities into a fixed pantheon.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification:** historically situated equation between distinct beings or traditions.
- **[F] Folklore or living customary religion.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** plausible synthesis not stated by a single primary source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Chicomecōātl

**[EA]** Classical Nahuatl **Chicomecōātl**, commonly normalized *Chicomecoatl* or *Chicomecóatl*, is transparently composed of *chicōme*, “seven,” and *cōātl*, “serpent”: **“Seven Serpent.”** It is a calendrical name, formed like other divine names from a numeral and one of the twenty day signs. It means “7 Serpent,” not “seven serpents” and not “seven-headed serpent.” Those plural or monstrous translations are modern mistranslations.

**[EA/MSR]** In the divinatory calendar, numeral-sign names could designate a day, a fate-bearing calendrical identity, and a divine being. The name therefore does not establish that the goddess physically was a snake. Surviving images generally present her anthropomorphically. INAH interprets seven as especially connected with seed and with the “heart” of maize, but this is an iconological interpretation rather than a literal ancient etymology.

### Titles and descriptive names

- **“Goddess of sustenance” / *diosa de los mantenimientos*. [CE]** Sahagún’s Nahua testimony says that Chicomecoatl makes or provides “all our food” (*in ixquich tonacayotl*). Colonial Spanish writers consequently call her the goddess of provisions or sustenance.
- **Maize goddess. [EA/CE]** More precisely, she governs maize as food, harvested grain, stored seed, and the crop’s power to nourish or fail.
- **Xilonen, “She of the young ear/tender maize.” [EA, disputed relation]** Derived from *xīlotl*, the tender ear or green maize. Some sources and much later systematization treat Xilonen as another name or youthful phase of Chicomecoatl; others distinguish her as the goddess of tender maize, celebrated separately at Huey Tecuilhuitl.
- **Centeocihuatl, “Maize-god Woman” or “Female Maize Deity.” [CE/LS]** A descriptive feminine maize-divinity title sometimes brought into the Chicomecoatl complex.
- **Ilamatecuhtli, “Old Lady/Old Noblewoman.” [EA/LS]** Represents aged, dry maize and the old earth. She is associated or cyclically equated with Chicomecoatl in modern reconstructions of the maize life cycle, but the names should not automatically be collapsed into one timeless personality.
- **Chalchiuhcihuatl, “Jade Woman.” [CE/RV]** Durán uses or transmits this title in connection with the maize goddess. Its relation to Chicomecoatl varies by passage and manuscript interpretation.
- **Chicomexochitl, “Seven Flower.” [RV/F]** In the northern Veracruz Huasteca, this is a maize power honored in contemporary Nahua *costumbre*. Ethnographers compare it with Chicomecoatl, but Chicomexochitl is not simply an unchanged survival under a misspelled name. The calendrical sign has changed from Serpent to Flower, the being may be multiple or childlike, and the modern ritual complex reflects centuries of local development and evangelization.

### Classification and identity problems

**[EA]** Nahua religion did not necessarily treat a *teōtl* as an independently bounded “person” in the manner presupposed by modern pantheon charts. Chicomecoatl could be a named goddess, a calendrical potency, an image, maize itself, seed kept in storage, and a human *ixiptla* who temporarily embodied or manifested her.

**[MSR]** Scholars consequently differ over whether Chicomecoatl, Xilonen, Centeotl, and Ilamatecuhtli are:

1. separate deities assigned to successive stages of maize;
2. names or aspects of one maize power;
3. locally and ritually overlapping beings never reduced by Nahua practitioners to a single systematic identity.

The third formulation best respects the inconsistent evidence.

There is no ancient *interpretatio graeca* or *interpretatio romana* for Chicomecoatl. Comparisons with Demeter, Ceres, Persephone, or “Mother Earth” are modern analogies, not attested identifications.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Material evidence

**[EA]** Numerous Late Postclassic basalt and volcanic-stone female figures carry paired maize ears and an enormous rectangular *amacalli*, “paper house,” headdress with rosettes, twisted cords, and pendant strips. Examples in the Metropolitan Museum of Art and National Museum of the American Indian are dated broadly to 1325–1521. A Brooklyn Museum relief, dated about 1440–1521, shows a maize goddess with maize headdress and a serpent-like rattle staff.

These works securely attest a widespread maize-goddess image type. Identification of every example specifically as Chicomecoatl remains probabilistic because:

- most were collected without controlled archaeological context;
- several maize goddesses shared costume elements;
- some figures lack an inscribed name;
- modern catalogues sometimes alternate between Chicomecoatl and Xilonen.

A smaller set bears the calendrical glyph 7 Serpent and therefore identifies the figure much more securely.

### *Primeros Memoriales*

**[EA]** Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*, compiled from Nahua informants at Tepepulco around 1558–1561, preserves an illustrated divine catalogue. Folio 262r names and depicts Chicomecoatl with *amacalli*, jade necklace, flowered blouse and skirt, ankle bells, sandals, shield, and a hand-held object. This is among the earliest detailed textual-iconographic descriptions derived from Indigenous testimony.

The same corpus describes her *ixiptla* and calendrical ceremonies. Its testimony is early and indispensable, but it was produced after conquest within a Franciscan investigative project.

### Florentine Codex

**[EA/CE]** Bernardino de Sahagún and Nahua collaborators compiled the twelve-book *Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España*, preserved in the Florentine Codex, principally during the 1550s–1570s.

Relevant books include:

- **Book 1, “The Gods”:** classifies Chicomecoatl among the divinities and describes her costume and domain.
- **Book 2, “The Ceremonies”:** records Huey Tozoztli, Ochpaniztli, temple rites, impersonators, offerings, sacrifice, and the hymn *Chicomecoatl icuic*.
- **Book 6:** says she produces or governs all sustenance and calls her an elder sister of the Tlaloque.
- **Book 11:** material on maize and cultivated plants helps establish the agricultural setting.

The surviving manuscript was completed around 1577. Anderson and Dibble’s English edition remains the standard full translation, although individual Nahuatl terms and ritual sequences continue to be reinterpreted.

### “Song of Chicomecoatl”

**[EA/CE]** Book 2 preserves *Chicomecoatl icuic*, “Song of Chicomecoatl,” probably a ritual hymn remembered or still known in the sixteenth century. Its language is archaic and difficult. It associates the goddess with Tlalocan and uses compressed, allusive ritual imagery. Older translations—notably Brinton’s “Hymn to the Goddess of Food”—must be used cautiously: Brinton mistranslated or overinterpreted several expressions, including Chicomecoatl’s name.

The hymn is important because it is not a European narrative about the goddess but Indigenous liturgical speech. It nevertheless survives only through a colonial manuscript.

### Durán

**[CE]** Dominican Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme*, especially the component usually translated as *Book of the Gods and Rites and the Ancient Calendar*, was written in the 1570s–1580s. Durán describes Chicomecoatl’s feast, temple, maize offerings, an adolescent female impersonator, decapitation, blood offered to the divine image, flaying, and the wearing of the victim’s skin.

Durán sometimes combines information from different localities or imposes analogies with biblical and classical religion. His account cannot be treated as a direct transcript of one uniform “Aztec religion,” but it preserves details absent from Sahagún.

### Pictorial manuscripts

- **Codex Borbonicus, folio 30. [EA/CE, dating disputed]** Its festival section depicts Ochpaniztli and maize/earth divinities with diagnostic paper regalia. Whether the manuscript was painted immediately before or shortly after conquest remains disputed. Its images are primary evidence, but named identifications often depend on comparison with alphabetic sources.
- **Codex Magliabechiano and Tudela-group manuscripts. [CE]** Mid-sixteenth-century festival and deity pictures preserve central Mexican iconography with Spanish annotations. Their related exemplars should not be counted as independent testimony where one copied another.
- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis. [CE]** Made about the 1540s–1550s, with Indigenous paintings and European-language annotations. It documents calendrical religion but does not supply an independent full Chicomecoatl biography.
- **Codex Borgia and the Borgia Group. [EA/LS]** These preconquest ritual-divinatory manuscripts are crucial comparative evidence for central Mexican maize, earth, rain, and calendrical beings. Identification of particular anonymous figures as Chicomecoatl is often a scholarly attribution based on costume or day-sign association, not an alphabetically written label. Seler’s astral readings and Boone’s ritual/narrative readings differ substantially.
- **Codex Mendoza. [CE]** Created around 1541–1542, principally documents tribute, conquest history, and social life. Its maize tribute and agricultural economy illuminate the material system surrounding the cult; it is not a principal narrative source for Chicomecoatl.

### Other Nahua historical and mythological texts

**[Absence finding]** The *Leyenda de los Soles*, *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, and *Histoyre du Mechique* are central sources for Nahua cosmogony, successive Suns, and the creation or recovery of maize. Chicomecoatl does not play a securely identifiable leading role in their surviving creation narratives. Quetzalcoatl’s recovery of maize in such traditions should not be silently converted into an interaction with Chicomecoatl.

**[Absence finding]** The *Cantares Mexicanos* contains agricultural and divine imagery but is not the source of the specifically titled *Chicomecoatl icuic*; that hymn is transmitted in Sahagún’s Book 2. Confusing the two song collections is common.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[EA]** Chicomecoatl governs *tonacayotl*: that which sustains the human body, especially cultivated food and above all maize. Her power encompasses:

- ripening maize;
- harvested ears;
- seed reserved for planting;
- household and communal food stores;
- agricultural abundance;
- the corresponding possibility of crop failure and famine.

**[MSR]** Calling her merely a “fertility goddess” is too broad. It imports a modern comparative category and obscures her exceptionally specific relation to staple food and seed continuity.

### Recognizing her

Typical features are:

- a towering rectangular **paper-house headdress**, *amacalli*;
- large rosettes at its upper corners;
- pendant paper strips and twisted cords;
- one or two maize ears in each hand, or maize attached to the headdress;
- red-painted face, body, clothing, or surviving pigment;
- flowered blouse and skirt;
- jade necklace;
- ankle bells;
- sandals;
- shield, staff, dart, or rattle implement in painted representations.

**[EA/MSR]** The “double ears of corn” are the most dependable feature of many stone images, but paired ears alone do not prove the identity. Sculptures carrying the 7 Serpent glyph supply the strongest control for attributing otherwise unlabeled examples.

**[EA]** The *amacalli* represents actual perishable ceremonial costume translated into stone. During festivals, divine impersonators wore paper regalia; sculpture therefore depicts both deity and the embodied ritual image.

### Colors, substances, and objects

- **Red:** repeatedly associated with Chicomecoatl images and costume. Some sculptures retain red pigment.
- **Maize in many colors:** distributed or thrown during rites, materially expressing abundance and varietal diversity.
- **Seed maize:** treated as the “heart” of the granary and carried ceremonially for blessing.
- **Bark paper:** essential to the headdress and divine costume.
- **Blood:** offered to images in sacrificial descriptions.
- **Chicahuaztli or rattle staff:** a ritual instrument symbolically connected with sounding, penetrating, or awakening the earth; individual museum identifications remain interpretive.

No secure ancient source assigns her a planet, fixed astronomical body, or occult “correspondence” in the later European sense.

Chicomecoatl has no securely attested birth narrative, humanlike childhood, consort, or offspring in the principal early sources. Modern family trees frequently combine incompatible statements about different maize divinities.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### No surviving narrative cycle

**[Absence finding]** Chicomecoatl has no securely transmitted epic cycle comparable to the birth of Huitzilopochtli, Quetzalcoatl’s journey to Mictlan, or Tezcatlipoca’s conflicts. Her primary record is ritual, liturgical, calendrical, and iconographic.

This absence is historically significant. Modern encyclopedia entries often manufacture a biography by combining:

- the maize-acquisition story of Quetzalcoatl;
- genealogies of Centeotl;
- rites of Xilonen, Toci, and Ilamatecuhtli;
- generic agricultural symbolism.

The result is a smooth story that no early source actually tells.

### The goddess departs to Tlalocan

**[EA/CE]** The *Chicomecoatl icuic* addresses the goddess through obscure ritual language and says, in a defensible reading, “you went to your home in Tlalocan.” This is not a complete journey myth. It may encode the seasonal withdrawal and return of maize or the crop’s dependence on rain, but that interpretation is scholarly reconstruction.

### The maize life cycle as divine sequence

**[LS/MSR]** Modern scholarship frequently narrates the growing maize plant through divine female ages:

1. Xilonen: tender ear;
2. Chicomecoatl: growing, ripe, or harvest maize;
3. Ilamatecuhtli: aged, dry ear and exhausted field.

This model illuminates the ritual calendar but should not be mistaken for a surviving ancient myth told continuously in those terms.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Temple and cult images

**[CE with archaeological correlation]** Sahagún’s catalogue of Tenochtitlan’s sacred precinct includes a temple of Chicomecoatl. Modern reconstructions place it among the smaller sanctuaries around the Templo Mayor. Its exact archaeological identification is less secure than the literary name because colonial destruction and later construction radically disturbed the precinct.

A structure conventionally identified as the Temple of Chicomecoatl appears in INAH’s ceremonial-precinct plan. Archaeologists treat the correlation cautiously; it depends partly on colonial descriptions rather than a surviving dedicatory inscription.

### Huey Tozoztli

**[EA/CE]** Huey Tozoztli, “Great Vigil” or “Great Watching,” was the fourth twenty-day period of the solar cycle in Sahagún’s ordering. It joined maize, seed, mountains, and rain.

Recorded elements include:

- fasting and vigil;
- offerings to Cinteotl and Chicomecoatl;
- maize stalks or ears brought from fields;
- young women carrying maize to the temple;
- decoration of domestic altars;
- seed maize taken for ritual blessing;
- food offerings before the goddess;
- mountain rites directed to rain powers;
- sacrifice of a female *ixiptla* in some descriptions.

Consecrated seed was returned to household storage and preserved as the granary’s generative “heart,” later to be mixed with seed for planting. This made the goddess’s cult inseparable from practical subsistence.

### Ochpaniztli

**[EA/CE; sources differ]** Ochpaniztli, “Sweeping,” was a harvest-season veintena and a period of renewal, purification, earth fertility, and the opening of warfare. Sahagún’s fullest account centers on **Toci/Teteoinnan**, whose female impersonator was killed and flayed; a priest wore her skin. Maize deity embodiments then appeared in the ensuing rites.

Durán gives Chicomecoatl a more explicit parallel place. In his account, a girl embodying the maize goddess was killed upon maize, decapitated, her blood offered, and her skin worn by a priest. Other colonial versions describe death by an arrow through the throat. These are variants, not interchangeable details.

Therefore:

- it is secure that Ochpaniztli involved female divine impersonation, killing, flaying, and maize symbolism;
- it is secure that Chicomecoatl belonged to the festival complex in at least some accounts;
- it is not secure that Sahagún and Durán describe a single identical rite;
- statements that “the Chicomecoatl sacrifice occurred at the autumn equinox” impose modern calendrical precision on a movable Indigenous calendar and should be qualified.

### Sacrifice

**[EA/CE]** The written evidence plainly describes human sacrifice in Chicomecoatl’s cult:

1. a girl or young woman was selected as the goddess’s *ixiptla*;
2. she was ornamented and treated as the manifested deity;
3. maize, song, dance, procession, and offerings surrounded her;
4. she was killed—decapitation in Durán, an arrow wound in another colonial account;
5. blood was presented to or spread upon the divine image;
6. the body could be flayed;
7. a priest wore the skin, continuing the deity’s embodied presence.

The *ixiptla* was not merely an actor “pretending” to be a goddess. In Nahua ritual ontology, costume, image, person, and divine force could be substantially identified.

### Archaeological limits and corroboration

No osteological assemblage has yet been securely demonstrated to consist specifically of Chicomecoatl’s named victims. The 2015 onward excavation of the **Huei Tzompantli** corroborates the large institutional scale and demographic breadth of sacrifice at Tenochtitlan, not each detail of every festival account. INAH reported 655 identified crania in the tower sample: approximately 60% male, 38% female, and 2% juvenile. That evidence contradicts any claim that all displayed victims were adult male warriors, but it cannot assign particular women to Chicomecoatl.

Thus the archaeology strongly establishes sacrifice and skull display at the capital while the deity-specific reconstruction still depends largely on colonial texts and pictures.

### Household and commoner worship

**[EA/MSR]** The extraordinary number of small maize-goddess sculptures suggests household or neighborhood worship rather than a cult confined to imperial elites. Many figures are portable, standardized, and modestly carved. Their original context is often lost, so “mass-produced household idols” is a reasonable but not universally demonstrable reconstruction.

Offerings included maize, beans, chia, tamales, flowers, seed, paper, incense, quail, bloodletting, and—at state festivals—humans. The cult bound household food security to temple institutions and the imperial calendar.

### Priesthood and economics

Named offices specifically restricted to Chicomecoatl are poorly documented. Priests carried, dressed, guarded, and received offerings for images and impersonators. The broader temple system redistributed tribute and sacrificial goods.

The Codex Mendoza’s extensive maize tribute record does not prove that all such tribute belonged to Chicomecoatl. It does demonstrate the political economy in which a goddess of stored sustenance operated: imperial power depended on collecting, warehousing, and redistributing maize.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before approximately 1200: antecedents

**[MSR]** Maize deities are ancient in Mesoamerica, appearing in Formative and Classic traditions long before the Mexica. That history does not demonstrate that the named being Chicomecoatl already existed. Projecting her name backward onto every female maize image is methodologically unsound.

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1521

**[EA]** A calendrical maize goddess identifiable as Seven Serpent belonged to central Mexican ritual systems. Under Mexica imperial rule she received temple, household, agricultural, and calendrical worship. The large number of images indicates broad importance, while elaborate state festivals incorporated her into imperial ceremonial life.

This was likely the cult’s most visible institutional phase, but “peak” can only be inferred from the concentration of surviving Mexica-period sculpture and colonial testimony.

### 1519–1570s: conquest and documentation

**[CE]** Warfare, epidemic disease, destruction of Tenochtitlan in 1521, missionary campaigns, temple demolition, and bans on “idolatry” dismantled the public institutions of the cult. Images were destroyed, buried, reused, or dispersed. Churches and colonial buildings covered the ceremonial precinct.

At the same time, Nahua painters, grammarians, elders, and Christianized intellectuals recorded the deity for Franciscan and Dominican authors. The resulting corpus preserves Indigenous information but is inseparable from colonial surveillance and evangelization.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

**[CE/F]** Public sacrifice and state temples disappeared. Agricultural rites, invocations, sacred maize bundles, offerings at fields and crossroads, and divine names persisted or were reformulated inside local Christianity. Clerical manuals against “superstition,” including Ruiz de Alarcón’s 1629 treatise, document persistence but describe it through hostile demonological categories.

No continuous institutional priesthood of Chicomecoatl is securely documented through this period.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

**[MSR]** Antiquarian collecting removed many sculptures from their contexts. Scholars including Eduard Seler organized codex figures into taxonomic pantheons and interpreted Borgia-group imagery through astral and calendrical theories. Some identifications remain useful; others were revised when contextual and ritual readings displaced universal astral explanations.

The rediscovery of the Sun Stone in 1790 and later monumental finds stimulated Mexican antiquarianism, but neither the Sun Stone nor the Coyolxauhqui stone is a Chicomecoatl image.

### 1978–present: archaeological recontextualization

The discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monolith in 1978 initiated the modern Templo Mayor Project under Eduardo Matos Moctezuma. The 2006 discovery of the Tlaltecuhtli monolith and the 2015 onward Huei Tzompantli excavations transformed knowledge of Mexica ritual space. These discoveries contextualize Chicomecoatl’s world but do not depict or name her directly.

In April 1994, a distinctive Chicomecoatl sculpture was found at Culhuacán. It shows a female face within an *amacalli*, emerging serpent jaws, maize imagery, and rattlesnake elements. Unlike many museum pieces acquired through the art market, it has a documented modern findspot.

### Contemporary regional continuity

**[RV/F]** Nahua communities around Chicontepec, Veracruz, continue rites called **Chicomexochitl** or *el costumbre*. Cut-paper figures, music, food, flowers, mountains, rain petition, and the maize cycle are central. Ceremonies may be held around May for rain and around September for first maize.

Ethnographers identify structural and linguistic continuities with earlier maize religion, but present-day Chicomexochitl belongs to a living regional system shaped by colonial Christianity. It is neither a museum-frozen Mexica rite nor simply modern neopagan reconstruction.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Museums and national heritage

**[MSR]** Chicomecoatl has become a major museum emblem of maize agriculture. Institutions often display visually similar sculptures under her name even where provenance and identifying glyphs are absent. This makes the modern category clearer and more uniform than the ancient evidence.

Her image participates in Mexican celebrations of maize, Indigenous heritage, food sovereignty, and agricultural continuity. These are modern cultural uses, though grounded in authentic preconquest symbolism.

### Reconstructionist and neopagan practice

**[MI or modern revival unless continuity is demonstrated]** Individuals and small groups now address Chicomecoatl in Mexicayotl, reconstructionist, eclectic pagan, and online devotional settings. Common practices include offerings of maize, flowers, incense, water, gardening produce, and reconstructed Nahuatl prayers.

No reliable census establishes the number of dedicated worshippers. Online testimony proves contemporary interest, not an unbroken priestly succession. Newly composed prayers, altar instructions, planetary correspondences, “goddess archetype” readings, and ethical rules should be dated to their modern authors rather than presented as ancient.

### Occultism and New Age literature

The dedicated contested-material search found no evidence that Chicomecoatl had a significant role in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Theosophical Society, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or the principal European grimoire tradition. Claims giving her Tarot cards, chakras, crystals, zodiacal rulerships, or Wiccan sabbat correspondences are modern inventions.

Comparisons with Demeter, Ceres, or a Jungian “Corn Mother” are comparative interpretations. They are not Indigenous syncretisms unless a particular modern Nahua or Mexican community makes that identification.

### Popular culture and commerce

Chicomecoatl appears in educational illustration, tourist art, tattoos, jewelry, games, goddess encyclopedias, and commercial “Aztec zodiac” material. The most consequential distortion is the portrayal of her as a literal seven-headed snake woman. That image comes from mistranslating a calendrical name, not from securely attested Mexica iconography.

### Political appropriation

Mexicayotl and cultural-nationalist movements sometimes employ preconquest divine names to assert Indigenous continuity or resistance to colonialism. Chicomecoatl’s association with maize makes her particularly adaptable to environmental and food-sovereignty politics. These uses may be meaningful modern traditions, but they do not by themselves establish the form of preconquest worship.

No substantial, well-documented extremist appropriation specific to Chicomecoatl was found. General claims that her cult is central to a violent nationalist organization should therefore not be repeated without evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Nicholson’s typology

Henry B. Nicholson’s 1971 classification grouped central Mexican gods into conceptual complexes. Chicomecoatl belongs to an agricultural/fertility or rain-moisture complex rather than existing as an isolated classical-style personality. The typology remains influential but can encourage excessive systematization of overlapping local beings.

### Seler and Boone

Eduard Seler’s monumental studies of the Borgia Group made detailed identifications based on costume, day signs, astronomy, and calendrics. Elizabeth Hill Boone retained the manuscripts’ ritual-calendrical character but emphasized cycles of time, divination, transformation, and visual narrative. Their disagreement warns against treating every Borgia-group figure resembling a maize goddess as a securely labeled Chicomecoatl.

### Graulich and festival chronology

Michel Graulich argued that the historical position of the eighteen veintenas had drifted relative to the seasons, complicating apparently straightforward equations such as Ochpaniztli equals the autumn equinox. Other scholars retain closer agricultural-season readings. Festival meaning and historical calendrical placement must therefore be distinguished.

### The identity of maize divinities

The central debate is not whether maize mattered, but how its divine forms were differentiated. Scholars alternately emphasize:

- gender complementarity between Chicomecoatl and Cinteotl;
- age stages Xilonen–Chicomecoatl–Ilamatecuhtli;
- local cult differences;
- the *ixiptla* as a temporary material manifestation;
- colonial authors’ tendency to turn fluid powers into fixed “gods.”

### Human sacrifice

The occurrence of Mexica human sacrifice is established by converging Indigenous pictorial, Nahuatl-language, Spanish eyewitness or near-contemporary, architectural, and osteological evidence. Serious debate concerns scale, selection of victims, ritual meaning, political function, and the degree to which colonial narrators exaggerated particular numbers.

The Huei Tzompantli finds make blanket denial untenable. They do not validate every numerical claim in conquistador chronicles or prove that every skull came from a Chicomecoatl ceremony.

### Provenance and authentication

Dozens or hundreds of maize-goddess sculptures survive, but colonial destruction, early collecting, undocumented export, and the modern art market severed most from find context. Identification frequently rests on style. Works bearing the 7 Serpent sign, retaining pigment, or recovered archaeologically deserve greater evidentiary weight than unprovenanced look-alikes.

### Discredited or unsupported ideas still circulating

- “Chicomecoatl means seven-headed serpent.” **False.**
- “Every female maize image is Chicomecoatl.” **Unproved.**
- “She is simply the Aztec Demeter.” **Modern comparison.**
- “Xilonen, Centeotl, Chicomecoatl, and Ilamatecuhtli are unquestionably one goddess.” **Over-systematized.**
- “The Sun Stone depicts or is dedicated to Chicomecoatl.” **Unsupported.**
- “The 2015–2017 tzompantli excavations found her particular sacrificial victims.” **Unsupported.**
- “Modern crystal, Tarot, chakra, or zodiac correspondences are ancient Mexica knowledge.” **Modern invention.**
- “Chicomexochitl practice is either wholly preconquest and unchanged or wholly invented after conquest.” **Both extremes disregard the historical evidence for continuity through transformation.**

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Historical event and evidence |
|---|---|
| Before 1200 | Widespread Mesoamerican maize-divinity traditions provide background, but the name Chicomecoatl is not securely demonstrated. |
| c. 1200–1521 | Late Postclassic central Mexican ritual systems employ calendrical divine names including Seven Serpent. |
| c. 1325–1521 | Broad date assigned to numerous Mexica-style Chicomecoatl or maize-goddess stone sculptures. |
| c. 1440–1521 | Date assigned to the Brooklyn maize-goddess relief and several comparable works. |
| Preconquest or immediately postconquest | Codex Borbonicus is painted; folio 30 records Ochpaniztli imagery. Its exact side of the conquest remains disputed. |
| 1519–1521 | Spanish invasion and fall of Tenochtitlan destroy the political and temple order supporting the state cult. |
| c. 1541–1542 | Codex Mendoza records the imperial tribute economy, including maize on a large scale. |
| c. 1540s–1550s | Codex Telleriano-Remensis produced. |
| c. 1558–1561 | *Primeros Memoriales* records and pictures Chicomecoatl from Nahua testimony at Tepepulco. |
| c. 1547–1577 | Sahagún’s larger project culminates in the Florentine Codex, preserving descriptions, festival narratives, and *Chicomecoatl icuic*. |
| 1570s–1580s | Durán writes his history and detailed variant accounts of maize-goddess ritual and sacrifice. |
| 1629 | Ruiz de Alarcón publishes a campaign-oriented account of surviving Nahua ritual language and agricultural “superstitions.” |
| 1790–1791 | Coatlicue and the Sun Stone are recovered in Mexico City, inaugurating a new phase of antiquarian attention to Mexica sacred sculpture; neither is Chicomecoatl. |
| Late nineteenth–early twentieth century | Seler publishes comparative interpretations of central Mexican codices; museum collections accumulate unprovenanced maize-goddess figures. |
| 1971 | Nicholson publishes his influential typological analysis of central Mexican religion. |
| 1978 | Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone launches the modern Templo Mayor excavation program. |
| 1994 | Chicomecoatl sculpture discovered at Culhuacán in a documented local find. |
| 2006 | Tlaltecuhtli monolith discovered; it illuminates the ceremonial precinct but is not an image of Chicomecoatl. |
| 2010–2011 | Ethnomusicological fieldwork documents Chicomexochitl ceremonies in Nahua communities of Chicontepec. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavations reveal major portions of the Huei Tzompantli, materially confirming large-scale skull display and the participation of men, women, and juveniles. |
| 2020s | Museums, Indigenous cultural initiatives, Mexicayotl practitioners, and online pagans continue to reinterpret Chicomecoatl; these revivals coexist with distinct living Chicomexochitl traditions. |
| 2026 | No evidence establishes a centralized, numerically documented modern Chicomecoatl cult or unbroken institutional priesthood. |
