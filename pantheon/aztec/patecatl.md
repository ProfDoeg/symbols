# Patecatl: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male *teōtl* (“god,” more literally a sacred or divine power) of the Nahua traditions of central Mexico; specifically a pulque, fermentation, medicinal-root, and calendrical deity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Patecatl is identifiable pictorially as patron of the trecena beginning **1 Monkey** in Late Postclassic/early colonial divinatory manuscripts, especially Codex Borgia plate 66 and Codex Borbonicus plate 11. Because the precise date and pre-Conquest status of both manuscripts remain disputed, the earliest securely dated **named** attestations are colonial: the Codex Telleriano-Remensis, compiled about 1553–1563, and Sahagún’s *Primeros Memoriales*, collected at Tepepolco about 1558–1561. No securely identified Classic-period inscription, pre-Mexica sculpture, temple, or theophoric personal name presently demonstrates an earlier Patecatl cult.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early pictorial]** Late Postclassic or conquest-era indigenous pictorial evidence; **[Early colonial attestation]** sixteenth-century Nahua testimony or pictorial-textual manuscripts; **[Colonial elaboration]** interpretation supplied by sixteenth-century European or Christian-trained annotators; **[Regional variant]** a materially different local or manuscript tradition; **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** an inference or synthesis not stated directly in a primary source; **[Modern invention/unverified]** a recent claim lacking an identified early source.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Patecatl belongs to the divine complex surrounding *octli*, fermented maguey sap—now ordinarily called pulque—and its medicinal, ritual, and dangerous intoxicating powers. The best primary evidence makes him not the discoverer of the maguey plant or of its sap, but the discoverer or possessor of the **root or herbal additive placed in aguamiel to make potent pulque**. Sahagún’s Book 10 assigns the discovery of tapping the maguey to Mayahuel and the discovery of the relevant root to Patecatl; other named specialists then perfect pulque-making. The Telleriano-Remensis presents Patecatl as lord of the 1 Monkey trecena, husband of Mayahuel, and patron of the root without which drinkers supposedly could not become intoxicated. These are related but not identical accounts.

### Name and etymology

The principal forms are:

- **Pāhtēcatl / Pahtecatl**, frequently modernized as **Patecatl**.
- Colonial spellings include **Patecatl**, **Patecatle**, and occasionally erroneous or confused forms such as **Pantecatl**. *Pantecatl* is also an independently attested personal name and should not automatically be equated with the god.
- A defensible morphological analysis is *pahtli* or *pāhtli*—“medicine, remedy, medicinal preparation,” but also, contextually, a drug, poison, or fermenting preparation—plus *-tēcatl*, “inhabitant/person belonging to [a place or sphere].” The result is approximately “person of medicine,” “one from the place of medicine,” or “medicine-person.” **“He who makes medicine” is an explanatory gloss, not a literal, uncontested translation.**
- Arqueología Mexicana renders the name as “el de Patlan,” construing a notional or mythic place of medicinal roots called Patlan. That reading fits the productive Nahuatl inhabitant suffix but does not prove that a historical settlement named Patlan originated the god. [UNAM’s Gran Diccionario Náhuatl](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/patecatl/59220) records the divine name and its use for a ritual specialist, while [Arqueología Mexicana](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/node/4285) gives the “one from Patlan” interpretation.
- The frequent popular claim that the name means **“lord of the root of pulque”** is an interpretive epithet distilled from his function. It is not a literal translation of *Pāhtēcatl*.

### Titles and functional designations

- **“Lord of wine/pulque”** (*señor del vino*): colonial Spanish gloss in the Telleriano-Remensis. “Wine” here translates *octli*, not grape wine.
- **Lord or discoverer of the pulque root:** supported by Sahagún Book 10 and the Telleriano-Remensis glosses, though the precise plant is not securely identified.
- **Ōme Tōchtli Pāhtēcatl, “Two Rabbit Patecatl”:** Sahagún Book 2 associates this calendrical-pulque form with preparing sacred *teōoctli* during Panquetzaliztli. “Two Rabbit” is a widely used divine and calendrical designation in the pulque complex, not necessarily proof that every god called Ome Tochtli is simply another name for Patecatl. [The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl entry](https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/patecatl/59220) connects the passage with *Primeros Memoriales* folio 259v.
- **Patron of the trecena 1 Monkey–13 House:** securely represented across the central Mexican divinatory tradition.
- **“God of medicine” or “god of physicians”:** a reasonable modern classification based on the name and medicinal-root function, but the surviving sources are much fuller about fermentation than about physicians praying to him or about a separate medical institution under his patronage.
- **God of fertility:** common in modern reference works, but weakly specified in primary sources. It is usually inferred from the reproductive abundance of Mayahuel, maguey, drink, and the “four hundred” pulque gods. It should be labeled **[Modern scholarly or popular synthesis]**, not an independent ancient title.
- **Discoverer of peyote:** **[Modern invention/unverified]** unless used loosely to translate *ocpatli*. The Telleriano-Remensis says “roots” added to pulque. It does not securely identify them as peyote. *Ocpatli* means a pulque medicine/additive; equating it with peyote is botanically and philologically unproved.

### Calendrical clarification

Patecatl is securely the patron of the **eleventh trecena beginning 1 Monkey**, not straightforwardly “patron of the day-sign Monkey.” In some modern tables he also appears as patron associated with a particular day-sign, but trecena patrons, day-sign patrons, Lords of the Day, and Lords of the Night are distinct series. The Codex Borgia sequence distinguishes those systems explicitly. Calling him simply “the day-sign patron” therefore collapses separate divinatory structures.

No secure interpretatio graeca, interpretatio romana, or Christian saint-equation with Patecatl is documented.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Late Postclassic and conquest-era pictorial evidence

#### Codex Borgia

**[Early pictorial]** The Codex Borgia, Vatican Library Borg.mess.1, is an indigenous screenfold divinatory manuscript from central or south-central Mexico. Its date is conventionally Late Postclassic, often placed around the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, but its exact region and whether production preceded or shortly followed the Spanish invasion remain debated.

Plate 66 represents Patecatl as patron of the 1 Monkey trecena. This is an iconographic identification reached through comparison with named colonial tonalamatl manuscripts; the Borgia itself contains no alphabetic caption naming him. The manuscript proves that the divine image and calendrical office belonged to a broader central Mexican divinatory system. It does not by itself prove a state temple at Tenochtitlan or the detailed genealogy supplied by later glosses.

#### Codex Borbonicus

**[Early pictorial, date disputed]** The Borbonicus is another indigenous-style divinatory and festival manuscript, made immediately before or soon after the conquest. Its plate for 1 Monkey shows Patecatl with the thirteen days and their divinatory apparatus. A colonial gloss predicts that people born in this period would be valiant but die in war, apparently interpreting the eagle and jaguar figures accompanying the patron. The manuscript’s recent analysis emphasizes that its alphabetic annotations are later than, and interpret, the pictorial program. [A study of the Borbonicus glosses](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0071-16752026000100104&script=sci_arttext) identifies the 1 Monkey patron as Patecatl and preserves this martial prognostication.

#### Tonalamatl de Aubin

**[Regional variant/early colonial pictorial]** This amate screenfold, probably made in Tlaxcala either before or not long after the conquest, also presents trecena patrons. Its exact manufacture date is unresolved. INAH describes it as a book of days and destinies in which the patron occupies the principal panel, surrounded by day signs, Lords of the Night, Lords of the Day, and birds. Its Patecatl page is therefore further evidence for a geographically broader divinatory tradition, not necessarily a copy of a Mexica state original. [INAH’s catalogue](https://codices.inah.gob.mx/pc/contenido.php?id=2) notes both the uncertain dating and probable Tlaxcalan provenance.

### Named sixteenth-century sources

#### Codex Telleriano-Remensis

**[Early colonial attestation with colonial elaboration]** Compiled in central Mexico about 1553–1563, now BnF Ms. Mexicain 385. Folio 15v depicts and names Patecatl as patron of the 1 Monkey trecena. The Spanish gloss states:

- he rules the thirteen days;
- he is connected with roots added to pulque;
- without the roots drinkers supposedly could not become intoxicated;
- he gave the art or ordering of pulque-making;
- the trecena was favorable and produced courageous people;
- he was husband of Mayahuel;
- the eagle and “lion” (jaguar) signify the valor of their sons.

The same gloss confusingly calls him or associates him with **Cipactonal/Cipaquetona**, a primordial calendrical figure who survived a flood. That identification is isolated, grammatically muddled, and possibly the product of displaced or corrupted annotation. It should not be turned into a canonical myth in which Patecatl survives the deluge. The folio’s transcription can be inspected through [UNAM’s Tlachia project](https://tlachia.iib.unam.mx/telleriano/385_17v).

The Telleriano also contains demonstrable copying and labeling errors. On folio 14v, “Patecatl” was written beside a figure and then corrected to Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli. Scholars have used this and shifted trecena headings to argue that its surviving pictorial-textual layer copies a lost exemplar imperfectly. [Cecelia Klein’s discussion](https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/la-iconografia-y-el-arte-mesoamericano) illustrates the correction.

#### Codex Vaticanus A / Codex Ríos

**[Colonial elaboration]** Vaticanus A, probably made in Italy after about 1566 from material related to the Telleriano-Remensis, expands the annotations. Its commentator calls Patecatl Mayahuel’s husband and interprets him as the fermenting root itself or as the human discoverer of fermentation who was subsequently worshipped as a god.

That last formulation—an unfortunate human inventor later deified—may be European euhemerism rather than Indigenous theology. It is evidence for what the Vaticanus A interpreter thought, not secure proof that Nahua worshippers regarded Patecatl as a deceased historical man. Lewis Spence reproduces the passage in [*The Gods of Mexico*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71503/71503-h/71503-h.htm), though his 1923 synthesis must be checked against the manuscript rather than treated as primary evidence.

#### Sahagún, *Primeros Memoriales*

**[Early colonial Nahua testimony]** Compiled with Nahua elders and assistants at Tepepolco about 1558–1561. The manuscript names Patecatl among pulque-associated divine and priestly figures. In the Panquetzaliztli material, “Ome Tochtli Patecatl” prepares sacred pulque for the *macuiloctli* rite. Because the same divine names could be borne by officiants or deity impersonators, the word may identify both the god and a priestly office.

The surviving manuscripts are divided between Madrid’s Real Academia de la Historia and Palacio Real. They precede the final Florentine Codex and sometimes preserve religious details modified or omitted later.

#### Florentine Codex, Book 2

**[Early colonial Nahua testimony, finalized 1570s]** Book 2 describes the annual festivals. In Panquetzaliztli, an officiant called Ome Tochtli Patecatl prepares *teōoctli*, divine or sacred pulque. This is the clearest evidence for Patecatl’s place in performed ritual, although it does not establish a freestanding Patecatl temple or a permanent priesthood devoted exclusively to him.

#### Florentine Codex, Book 10, folio 144r

**[Early colonial Nahua testimony]** The fullest origin account says:

1. Mayahuel, a woman, first discovered how to pierce or tap the maguey and obtain its liquid.
2. Patecatl first found the roots placed in the “honey” or sap.
3. Tepuztecatl, Cuatlapanqui, Tlilhua, Papaiztac, and Tzocaca developed the art of making pulque at Mount Chichinauhia/Chichinauhya, also called Pozonaltepetl, “Foaming Mountain.”
4. They hosted rulers and elders, allotting four cups to each.
5. Cuextecatl, ruler of the Huaxtecs, demanded a fifth, became helplessly drunk, stripped, and brought shame upon himself.

The digital manuscript provides Nahuatl and Spanish transcriptions and modern translations at [Getty’s Digital Florentine Codex](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/en/book/10/folio/144r). Significantly, the passage does **not** call Mayahuel Patecatl’s wife and does **not** call the Four Hundred Rabbits their children.

### Sources in which a substantive Patecatl episode has not been established

A careful distinction is necessary between sources important for Mexica religion generally and sources actually mentioning Patecatl:

- **Codex Mendoza:** vital for tribute, conquest, education, and social regulation, but no securely identified Patecatl narrative or cult image has been established.
- **Codex Magliabechiano:** contains important pulque-god and Mayahuel material; older iconographic syntheses compare one pulque deity’s gear with Patecatl, but a secure, labeled Patecatl episode requires caution.
- **Durán’s *Historia* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*:** fundamental for Mexica festivals and sacrifice, but no major independent Patecatl narrative is securely identified.
- **Leyenda de los Soles, Anales de Cuauhtitlan, Cantares Mexicanos:** no securely identified Patecatl episode.
- **Histoyre du Mechique:** preserves Mayahuel’s transformation into maguey but does not make Patecatl central to that story.
- **Sun Stone:** no accepted Patecatl portrait or name.
- **Coyolxauhqui monolith, Tlaltecuhtli monolith, and Templo Mayor offerings:** indispensable evidence for the Mexica sacred precinct but none is securely identified as Patecatl.

Absence in those corpora sharply limits any attempted “complete biography.”

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

1. **Pulque fermentation and potency — securely attested.**  
   He discovers, embodies, owns, or presides over the medicinal root added to maguey sap.

2. **Sacred pulque preparation — securely attested.**  
   The Ome Tochtli Patecatl officiant prepares *teōoctli* in the Panquetzaliztli ritual.

3. **Divination and destiny — securely attested.**  
   He governs the trecena 1 Monkey–13 House. Telleriano and Borbonicus glosses connect it with courage, warfare, and favorable destiny.

4. **Medicine — strongly implied but incompletely institutionalized.**  
   His name and the semantic field of *pahtli* support a medicinal sphere. No surviving source demonstrates a separate guild of physicians, medical temple, or comprehensive therapeutic mythology belonging to him.

5. **Fertility — secondary reconstruction.**  
   This is inferred from maguey and from his relationship with Mayahuel. It is not his best-attested independent domain.

6. **Peyote or hallucinogenic mushrooms — unverified.**  
   No identified primary passage securely makes Patecatl discover peyote. The confusion arises from treating every intoxicating or medicinal *pahtli* as a particular entheogen.

### Iconography

Across the tonalamatl manuscripts Patecatl is a male anthropomorphic deity recognizable by a cluster rather than one invariant emblem:

- a **crescent-shaped nose ornament**, *yacametztli*, shared with pulque deities;
- a divided red-and-black facial painting, often with yellow markings, called *ixchictlapana* in modern iconographic description;
- paper, feather, or plant-fiber headdress elements;
- white or pale body painting in some manuscripts;
- pulque- or maguey-related objects;
- in some versions, a stone axe or curved staff;
- ornaments comparable with those of Quetzalcoatl, including a cut-shell breast ornament or curved wind-associated implement;
- accompanying eagle and jaguar figures in sacrificial or martial dress.

The attributes vary substantially between the Borgia, Borbonicus, Telleriano, and Aubin pages. They should not be combined into a single “canonical costume.” The Visual Lexicon notes the Telleriano figure’s crescent nose ornament and Quetzalcoatl-like hooked shell ornament while treating the identification as manuscript-specific: [Pahtecatl TR15v](https://aztecglyphs.wired-humanities.org/content/pahtecatl-tr15v).

The eagle and jaguar are not proven to be Patecatl’s sacred animals. The colonial gloss reads them as signs of the trecena’s martial courage. Likewise, a half-day/half-night sign in later descriptions of the Aubin page may belong to the almanac’s augury, not to a universal theology of Patecatl.

No securely excavated freestanding cult statue of Patecatl is known.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical traditions

#### Patecatl and Mayahuel as spouses

**[Colonial elaboration, securely documented in that layer]** Telleriano-Remensis and Vaticanus A explicitly call Patecatl Mayahuel’s husband. This is the strongest evidence for the marriage.

**[Early colonial parallel without marriage]** Florentine Codex Book 10 assigns complementary discoveries to them—Mayahuel taps the maguey; Patecatl finds the root—but never calls them spouses there.

Consequently, “husband of Mayahuel” is historically attested, but it should not be projected into every source.

#### Father of the Centzon Totochtin

The Four Hundred Rabbits are securely attested as the numerous gods or personified forms of pulque and drunkenness. The number “four hundred” functions as “innumerably many.” Mayahuel’s many breasts are explained as nourishment for this abundance in colonial commentary.

The exact claim that **Patecatl and Mayahuel jointly begot all Four Hundred Rabbits** is common in twentieth- and twenty-first-century reference works. The surviving passages examined here do not state that genealogy unambiguously. Telleriano says the eagle and jaguar predict that “their sons” will be valiant, but does not identify those sons as the Centzon Totochtin. Sahagún lists pulque gods without calling them Patecatl’s sons. Therefore:

- Patecatl as symbolic father or senior member of the pulque gods: **[Modern scholarly synthesis, plausible]**.
- Patecatl as literal father of precisely four hundred rabbit children by Mayahuel: **[Modern popular systematization unless a specific primary passage is supplied]**.

No securely attested parents, siblings, or individual child named Ixotecuhtli have been established. Online genealogies naming Ixotecuhtli as his son appear to derive from modern compendia rather than identified sixteenth-century evidence.

### Every documented interaction or joint episode

- **Mayahuel — complementary discovery of pulque technology.** She first taps maguey; Patecatl finds the root added to the sap. *Florentine Codex* Book 10, fol. 144r, finalized 1570s. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Mayahuel — husband and wife.** Codex Telleriano-Remensis fol. 15v, c. 1553–1563; repeated and elaborated in Vaticanus A after c. 1566. **[Colonial elaboration]**
- **Mayahuel — paired natural substances.** Vaticanus A interprets Mayahuel as maguey and Patecatl as its fermenting root or its discoverer. **[Colonial learned/euhemeristic systematization]**
- **Tepuztecatl — joint place in the pulque invention cycle.** After Patecatl finds the root, Tepuztecatl belongs to the group that perfects pulque at Chichinauhia. *Florentine Codex* Book 10. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Cuatlapanqui — same cooperative pulque-making episode.** *Florentine Codex* Book 10. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Tlilhua/Tlilhuah — same cooperative pulque-making episode.** *Florentine Codex* Book 10. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Papaiztac/Papaztac — same cooperative pulque-making episode.** *Florentine Codex* Book 10. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Tzocaca — same cooperative pulque-making episode.** *Florentine Codex* Book 10. **[Early colonial attestation]**
- **Ome Tochtli — compound identity or ritual conjunction.** Sahagún names an officiant/divine role “Ome Tochtli Patecatl,” who prepares sacred pulque. *Primeros Memoriales* and *Florentine Codex* Book 2, mid-to-late sixteenth century. **[Early colonial attestation; exact ontological relation uncertain]**
- **Cipactonal/Cipaquetona — confused identification.** Telleriano commentary appears to associate or identify Patecatl with a primordial flood survivor. The grammar and placement are corrupt, and no independent source confirms the equation. **[Colonial scribal or learned confusion]**
- **Quetzalcoatl — iconographic resemblance only.** Some Patecatl images carry shell or curved implements comparable to Quetzalcoatl’s regalia. No narrative meeting or explicit identity is attested. **[Modern iconographic comparison; not a divine interaction]**
- **Xochipilli — calendrical association but no narrative encounter.** Some modern tables name Xochipilli in relation to the 1 Monkey complex or to the day-sign series; the tonalamatl manuscripts do not thereby narrate an interaction. **[Systematic association, not an episode]**
- **Centzon Totochtin — putative paternity.** The familial relationship is widespread in modern summaries but remains incompletely grounded in the identified primary passages. **[Modern systematization]**

No combat, seduction, theft, judgment, punishment, rescue, or rivalry involving Patecatl and another named god is securely recorded.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The discovery and perfection of pulque

**Earliest full narrative:** *Florentine Codex* Book 10, folio 144r, based on Nahua testimony gathered in the sixteenth century.

The story distributes invention among several agents rather than crediting a solitary culture hero. Mayahuel recognizes how to tap the maguey. Patecatl finds the root that transforms or strengthens the sap. Tepuztecatl, Cuatlapanqui, Tlilhua, Papaiztac, and Tzocaca establish the perfected process at a named mountain. The finished drink foams, producing the name Pozonaltepetl. Rulers and elders are summoned to a controlled feast and receive four cups each.

Cuextecatl violates the four-cup limit by demanding a fifth. He becomes incapable of self-control, discards his loincloth, and exposes himself. His companions are ashamed, and the story continues as an explanation of Huaxtec behavior and ethnic stereotyping.

The myth therefore connects:

- technical discovery;
- geography;
- controlled ritual drinking;
- the boundary between sanctioned and excessive consumption;
- political hospitality;
- Nahua representations of Huaxtec alterity.

Patecatl’s role is important but brief. Modern retellings that depict him personally brewing the entire batch, creating every Rabbit god, or deliberately teaching humanity intoxication enlarge his role beyond Sahagún’s wording.

### The trecena of 1 Monkey

In the divinatory codices Patecatl presides over 1 Monkey–13 House. Telleriano and Borbonicus glosses make the period favorable and martial: those born in it may be courageous, symbolized by eagle and jaguar warriors, though one gloss also predicts death in battle.

This is not a narrative myth in the European sense but an operative cycle of divine time. The patron’s image makes the trecena a manifestation of pulque’s double character: medicinal and sacred, yet able to produce violent boldness and loss of control.

### Patecatl as deified human inventor

Vaticanus A says, in effect, that the unhappy man who discovered how to ferment the drink was afterward honored as a god. This is a distinct **euhemeristic version**. It conflicts with evidence presenting Patecatl straightforwardly as a divine patron within the tonalamatl. It may reflect a European annotator’s attempt to rationalize a god as a dead inventor.

### The Mayahuel transformation myth

*Histoyre du Mechique* recounts how Ehecatl/Quetzalcoatl brings Mayahuel to earth; the Tzitzimime discover, tear apart, and devour her; Ehecatl buries her remains, from which the maguey grows. Patecatl does **not** participate in the extant episode. Later composite retellings combine that story with the husband-wife statement and Patecatl’s fermentation role, making him Mayahuel’s partner throughout. That seamless version is **[Modern composite retelling]**, not one surviving early narrative.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Institutional evidence

No temple, chapel, altar, statue, or priestly college can presently be assigned securely and exclusively to Patecatl. Nicholson’s influential 1971 typology places him in the **Ometochtli or pulque-god complex**, a group containing numerous local and calendrical gods rather than necessarily separate major state cults.

The clearest cultic act is in Panquetzaliztli:

- an officiant called **Ome Tochtli Patecatl** prepared sacred *teōoctli*;
- the drink was required for a rite called *macuiloctli*;
- the god’s name could therefore function as an office or impersonated ritual identity.

This is positive evidence of cultic performance but insufficient to reconstruct recruitment, tenure, lands, privileges, or an autonomous Patecatl priesthood.

### Offerings and regulated drinking

Pulque was both food-drink and ritual substance. Sahagún’s origin feast emphasizes a norm of four cups and makes the fifth the threshold of disgrace. Regulations varied with age, ritual status, and occasion; blanket statements that “only elders could drink pulque” overstate a complex record that also includes festivals, offerings, medicine, and licensed ceremonial consumption.

Possible offerings to the pulque deities included pulque itself, food, paper adornments, bloodletting, and participation by impersonators. Yet no surviving offering deposit is securely labeled as belonging specifically to Patecatl.

### Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice was a documented part of Mexica state religion. Osteological and archaeological evidence, images, Nahuatl testimony, and Spanish accounts mutually establish that people were killed in ritual settings, although colonial authors sometimes exaggerated totals or interpreted rites polemically.

The 2015–2017 excavations at 24 República de Guatemala identified portions of Tenochtitlan’s Huei Tzompantli; [INAH’s site report](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5907) dates the discovery and field seasons. Templo Mayor excavations likewise document sacrificed and curated human remains. These discoveries confirm the wider sacrificial institution.

They do **not**, however, demonstrate a sacrifice made to Patecatl. No identified source assigns him a specific festival victim, mode of execution, skull-rack sector, or excavated human offering. Claims that Patecatl personally demanded human sacrifice are therefore **unestablished**. His Panquetzaliztli activity occurred during a festival that included sacrifice for Huitzilopochtli, but ritual co-presence is not recipient identity.

### Archaeological context and the named monumental finds

- The Coyolxauhqui stone was discovered on 21 February 1978, prompting the modern Proyecto Templo Mayor. [INAH documents the discovery](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/con-teatro-y-talleres-festejaran-el-aniversario-del-hallazgo-del-monolito-de-la-diosa-coyolxauhqui).
- The monumental Tlaltecuhtli relief was discovered on 2 October 2006. [INAH describes its findspot and significance](https://inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/tlaltecuhtli-la-deidad-de-tierra-que-emergio-del-templo-mayor).
- The Templo Mayor was principally dedicated to Tlaloc and Huitzilopochtli; [INAH’s site description](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/es/node/4463) identifies those dedications.

None bears an identifiable Patecatl image. Their relevance is contextual: they reveal the ritual-political system in which the better documented state gods operated and show how thin the material record for Patecatl remains by comparison.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before c. 1500: unknown origins

The name’s medical semantics, maguey technology, rabbit-pulque symbolism, and distributed local pulque gods may preserve traditions older than the Mexica Empire. But no artifact or inscription securely identifies Patecatl before the Late Postclassic. Toltec, Otomi, or Huaxtec origin theories remain speculative unless tied to a specific artifact or text.

### Late Postclassic, c. 1400–1521

**[Early pictorial]** Patecatl functioned within a transregional calendrical-divinatory system, especially as patron of 1 Monkey. His presence in related but nonidentical tonalamatl manuscripts suggests broader central Mexican circulation rather than merely a literary invention at Tenochtitlan.

### Early colonial documentation, 1520s–1580s

Indigenous painters, Nahua informants, and missionary compilers recorded Patecatl in several different media:

- pictorial calendrical image;
- Spanish trecena gloss;
- Nahuatl festival description;
- Nahuatl technological origin narrative;
- Italian/European euhemeristic commentary.

This is the most heavily documented phase, but it records a cult already being suppressed and transformed.

### Suppression and Christianization

Following the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521, temples were demolished or converted, divine images destroyed or concealed, Indigenous ritual offices attacked, and public cult redirected toward Christianity. The suppression was political and religious, while epidemics and demographic collapse disrupted transmission further.

Sahagún’s project itself illustrates the contradiction: Nahua intellectuals preserved extensive religious knowledge inside a missionary project intended to expose and eradicate “idolatry.” In 1575–1577 colonial authorities curtailed Indigenous-language religious work and impounded or removed Sahagún’s materials.

No documented episode describes destruction of a named Patecatl temple, prosecution of a Patecatl priest, or conversion of his shrine into a particular church. His public cult appears to disappear inside the general dismantling of the Nahua ritual system.

### Seventeenth through nineteenth centuries

Patecatl survives primarily in manuscripts and antiquarian scholarship rather than in a documented continuous cult. The Tonalamatl de Aubin entered colonial and European collections. Codices Telleriano-Remensis and Vaticanus A became objects of European manuscript study. Nineteenth-century scholars, especially Eduard Seler, established comparative iconographic identifications across the manuscripts.

Continuity of maguey cultivation, pulque production, herbal practice, and rabbit symbolism does not by itself prove continuity of worship directed to Patecatl by name.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Nicholson, Seler, Boone, Quiñones Keber, Graulich, León-Portilla, López Austin, Miller, Taube, and others repositioned Patecatl within broader analyses of deity complexes, calendrical books, intoxication, and Nahua classifications. Digitization now permits direct comparison of the Telleriano, Florentine Codex, Borgia, and Aubin traditions.

There are modern Indigenous-religious, Mexican nationalist, neo-Aztec, and neopagan efforts to honor the *teteo*. Some practitioners may address Patecatl, especially in connection with healing or pulque. No reliable demographic study establishes the number of his worshippers, organizational continuity, or a standard reconstructed liturgy. Modern worship should therefore be described as **revival or reconstruction**, not demonstrated uninterrupted survival.

---

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Patecatl has remained a comparatively minor figure in modern mass culture. His principal modern appearances are in mythology dictionaries, calendar websites, tourist explanations of pulque, commercial art, deity databases, and reconstructionist discussions.

Several recurrent reinventions require correction:

- **“Aztec god who discovered peyote.”** No secure primary source identified here says this. It apparently arises from mistranslating or botanically overidentifying *ocpatli*.
- **“God of all hallucinogenic plants and mushrooms.”** Unsupported expansion from *pahtli*.
- **“Father of exactly 400 rabbit-shaped children, each representing a precisely classified drunken mood.”** The Four Hundred Rabbits are authentic; the tidy nuclear family and exhaustive mood taxonomy are modern systematization.
- **“Aztec zodiac sign.”** Patecatl’s trecena patronage is authentic, but commercial “Aztec zodiac” systems often impose Gregorian birthday tables and Western sun-sign assumptions on the independently cycling 260-day count.
- **“Ixotecuhtli, god of freedom, was his son.”** No secure early source has been located; this appears to be modern internet genealogy.
- **Continuous secret cult since the conquest.** Unestablished.
- **Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Hermetic, or Renaissance occult cult.** No material evidence was located for Patecatl in the major European occult systems. Claims to an old Western esoteric current devoted to him would be modern inventions.
- **Jungian archetype of healing intoxication.** Possible as a modern interpretation, but no major, traceable Jungian tradition centered on Patecatl has been established.
- **Political or extremist appropriation.** Unlike Huitzilopochtli or generalized “Aztec warrior” imagery, Patecatl has no well-documented major extremist appropriation. Absence of evidence should not be replaced with conjecture.

Modern representations commonly copy the Telleriano figure or a redrawn Borgia image without noting that these are different manuscript traditions.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Deity complexes versus fixed personalities

H. B. Nicholson’s 1971 classification remains influential because it groups central Mexican gods by recurring ritual themes and iconographic complexes. Patecatl fits the Ometochtli-pulque complex. This approach helps explain shared crescent nose ornaments, calendrical Rabbit names, and overlapping functions.

Its danger is reification: a scholar’s group can look like a native canonical family tree. Nahua divine identities were situational, local, calendrical, and performative; shared costume does not always mean identity.

### Seler and comparative iconography

Eduard Seler pioneered systematic comparison among Borgia-group and central Mexican manuscripts. Many accepted deity identifications depend on that comparative method. Yet later scholars stress that colors, objects, scale, and manuscript structure must be read locally rather than treated as interchangeable hieroglyphic labels.

### Patecatl as god, priest, or ancestor

Three layers coexist:

1. divinatory manuscripts represent a deity patron;
2. Sahagún sometimes uses the name for a ritual officiant;
3. Vaticanus A describes a human inventor afterward worshipped as a god.

These need not be harmonized. A divine name could title a priest or impersonator; a colonial European commentator could simultaneously rationalize that god as a deified man.

### Identity of the fermentation root

The source term denotes medicinal or fermenting roots placed in sap. Modern writers have proposed various plants and frequently peyote. The evidence does not securely establish a botanical species. The key historical fact is that colonial informants conceptualized pulque-making through a special *pahtli*, not that modern taxonomy has identified it.

### Husband and father claims

Mayahuel as wife is explicit in Telleriano and Vaticanus A but absent from Sahagún’s technological account. Paternity of the Four Hundred Rabbits is a logical later synthesis rather than an equally well-attested statement. This illustrates how mythology handbooks produce a stable nuclear pantheon from fragmentary ritual relationships.

### Manuscript transmission problems

Telleriano-Remensis contains corrections and misplaced names. Vaticanus A depends on related material but expands it through an Italian and Christian intellectual filter. The Florentine Codex combines Nahuatl testimony, European book structure, translations, revisions, and missionary purpose. None is an unmediated transcript of “the Aztec religion.”

The Borgia manuscript is pictorial and lacks alphabetic names, so identifying Patecatl depends on comparison with colonial witnesses. Conversely, the colonial witnesses may preserve older pictorial structures while attaching new interpretations.

### Archaeology and disproportionality

Templo Mayor archaeology has transformed understanding of sacrifice, offerings, state ritual, and monumental imagery. The Coyolxauhqui, Tlaltecuhtli, and Huei Tzompantli finds are genuine landmarks, but none adds direct Patecatl evidence. Invoking them as if every Mexica god had an archaeologically excavated cult would be misleading.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Before c. 1400 | No securely identified Patecatl attestation. Older origins of the pulque complex remain possible but unproved. |
| Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century | Patecatl is represented as patron of 1 Monkey in the Codex Borgia tradition. Exact production date and provenance remain disputed. |
| Immediately before or after 1521 | Codex Borbonicus records the 1 Monkey trecena under Patecatl’s image; later Spanish glosses interpret its augury. |
| 1521 onward | Conquest, temple destruction, conversion campaigns, epidemics, and colonial restructuring end the publicly documented institutional setting of Mexica worship. No Patecatl-specific suppression episode survives. |
| c. 1553–1563 | Codex Telleriano-Remensis names and depicts Patecatl, explains his root and pulque function, calls him Mayahuel’s husband, and connects his trecena with courage. |
| c. 1558–1561 | Sahagún and Nahua collaborators compile the *Primeros Memoriales* at Tepepolco; “Ome Tochtli Patecatl” prepares sacred pulque. |
| After c. 1566 | Vaticanus A/Codex Ríos expands the Telleriano tradition, describing Patecatl as Mayahuel’s husband, the fermenting root, or a deified human inventor. |
| 1570s; manuscript completed 1577 | Florentine Codex Book 2 preserves his ritual role; Book 10 records the distributed invention of pulque. |
| Seventeenth century or earlier colonial period | Tonalamatl de Aubin, probably Tlaxcalan and possibly earlier, continues the 1 Monkey patronal image. |
| 1897–1901 | Aubin and Telleriano materials become broadly available through facsimiles and Seler’s comparative studies. |
| 1923 | Lewis Spence publishes *The Gods of Mexico*, popularizing Vaticanus A and Telleriano interpretations in English. |
| 1950–1982 | Anderson and Dibble publish the critical English translation of the Florentine Codex. |
| 1971 | Nicholson’s central-Mexican religious typology situates Patecatl in the pulque/Ometochtli complex. |
| 21 February 1978 | Discovery of the Coyolxauhqui monolith inaugurates the modern Templo Mayor project; no direct Patecatl material results. |
| 1995 | Quiñones Keber’s major study of the Telleriano-Remensis refines understanding of its layers and glosses. |
| 2 October 2006 | Discovery of the Tlaltecuhtli monolith expands knowledge of the sacred precinct but supplies no Patecatl attestation. |
| 2015–2017 | Archaeologists identify parts of the Huei Tzompantli, materially confirming Mexica skull-display practices; no association with Patecatl is demonstrated. |
| 2023 | Getty launches the expanded Digital Florentine Codex, enabling direct comparison of the Nahuatl, Spanish, images, and translations of the Patecatl passage. |
| Present | Patecatl circulates in scholarship, pulque heritage discourse, online mythology, commercial art, and small-scale reconstructionist practice. Claims about peyote, a detailed Rabbit genealogy, or continuous worship remain unproved. |
