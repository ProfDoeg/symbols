# Nanahuatzin: Research Dossier

**Class:** male *teōtl*—a divine or numinous being—in Nahua, especially Mexica and central-Mexican tradition; more narrowly, a diseased and socially humble god whose voluntary burning produces the present Sun. He is not securely attested as a demon, monster, collective, or merely human hero.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Histoyre du Mechique*, a French translation or adaptation of a lost Spanish account compiled from Indigenous central-Mexican testimony, conventionally dated to the mid-sixteenth century, probably the 1540s. It calls the figure **Nanauaton/Nanahuatl** and supplies a birth and fosterage narrative as well as his selection to become the Sun. Because the Spanish exemplar and Indigenous originals are lost, the earliest securely dated surviving Nahuatl witness is the *Leyenda de los Soles*, whose opening dates its writing to **22 May 1558**. Sahagún’s Tepepolco investigations began in 1558–1559, but the fullest surviving account is in Book VII of the bilingual *Florentine Codex*, completed in 1577. No presently accepted pre-Conquest inscription or image names Nanahuatzin beyond reasonable dispute.

**Evidence labels used below**

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** sixteenth-century text preserving Indigenous testimony or securely attributable contemporaneous material.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a later sixteenth- or seventeenth-century formulation that became especially influential.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned/priestly systematization:** an interpretation imposed by Indigenous or European intellectual ordering.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[EM] Early-modern accretion or colonial survival.**
- **[F] Folklore or later oral tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction or interpretation.**
- **[MI] Modern invention, adaptation, or unsupported popular claim.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

Nanahuatzin is the afflicted, materially poor, or ritually modest god who accepts what the other gods ask of him: to enter the divine furnace at Teotihuacan. In Sahagún’s version he does so only after the ostentatious Tecuciztecatl fails four times; Nanahuatzin jumps immediately, Tecuciztecatl follows, and both rise in the east as equally brilliant luminaries. A rabbit is thrown into Tecuciztecatl’s face to diminish him into the Moon. The transformed Nanahuatzin remains motionless until the other gods die and Ehecatl sets the luminaries moving. This is the best-known narrative, but it is only one version. **[EA/LC]**

The 1558 *Leyenda de los Soles* instead calls the lunar candidate **4-Flint**, identifies him with **Metztli**, and has him fall into the ashes after Nanahuatl enters the fire. It does not reproduce Sahagún’s four failed leaps, rich offerings, or rabbit episode in the same form. It also makes Nanahuatl the god who strikes open Tonacatepetl, the Food-Mountain. **[EA/RV]**

The *Histoyre du Mechique* embeds Nanahuatl in a substantially different life history: he has parents, foster-parents, siblings or foster-siblings, an ogress-like guardian, and adventures before becoming the Sun. **[EA/RV]** These incompatible accounts must not be converted into a single canonical biography.

### Names and forms

- **Nanāhuātzin**, frequently printed **Nanahuatzin** or **Nanauatzin**. Classical-Nahuatl spelling varies because sixteenth-century alphabetization was not standardized. The long vowels are reconstructed by modern linguistic convention.
- **Nanāhuatl / Nanahuatl / Nanáhuatl.** A form without the reverential suffix.
- **Nanauaton**, in the French *Histoyre du Mechique*, reflecting a diminutive or reverential form transmitted through French orthography.
- **Nanāhuātzin** is built from *nanāhuatl*, a person affected with pustules, sores, buboes, or a pustular disease, plus **-tzin**, a reverential or affective suffix that can convey respect, endearment, smallness, or social deference depending on context. Thus “Revered Sore-Covered One,” “Dear Afflicted One,” or the traditional Spanish **el bubosito** is safer than the overly precise “syphilitic god.” The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl records senses including “buboso,” “dios buboso,” and the diseased god transformed into the Sun. **[EA linguistic evidence]**

Translations such as “the leper” or “the syphilitic one” are historically common but diagnostically insecure. Sixteenth-century *bubas* could denote several visible swellings and ulcerative diseases. The narrative describes bodily eruptions; it does not provide enough clinical information to diagnose modern syphilis, leprosy, or tuberculosis. **[MSR, diagnosis unestablished]**

### Titles and transformed identities

- **“The poor one,” “the afflicted one,” “the humble one.”** These are descriptive roles rather than demonstrable formal cult epithets. Sahagún’s narrative makes his offerings materially humble and his conduct unhesitating; “god of humility” is a useful modern summary but is not a securely attested pre-Conquest divine office. **[EA narrative; MSR abstraction]**
- **Tonatiuh**, “He Who Goes Shining/He Who Makes Warmth,” the Sun. Nanahuatzin becomes the solar body in the creation narrative, but this does not prove that every independent image, prayer, or cult of Tonatiuh was addressed to Nanahuatzin under another name. “Nanahuatzin = Tonatiuh” is narratively correct after the transformation; universal identity across all Nahua contexts is not established. **[EA narrative; caution against LS]**
- **Nahui Ollin**, “4-Movement,” the calendrical name of the present Sun or world-age. This is the era and the functioning solar order generated by the sacrifice, not ordinarily a personal birth-name of Nanahuatzin. **[EA/LS]**
- **Quetzalcoatl-Nanahuatl.** Some modern syntheses infer or repeat a composite identity from the *Histoyre du Mechique*. The text’s transmission and the overlapping deeds of Nanahuatl and Quetzalcoatl warrant discussion, but a stable, universally recognized pre-Conquest fusion is not demonstrated. **[RV/MSR]**
- **Nanahuatzin-Xolotl.** Seler and later iconographic writers have identified a malformed figure in the *Codex Borgia* with Nanahuatzin, Xolotl, or a fused Nanahuatzin-Xolotl. This remains an interpretation, not a written caption. **[MSR, disputed]**

No Greek, Roman, Christian, or other Old World *interpretatio* of Nanahuatzin is documented in early sources. Comparisons to Christ, the biblical poor exalted over the rich, Prometheus, Buddhist self-sacrifice, or the *Popol Vuh* Hero Twins are modern comparative readings, not historical equations. **[MSR/MI if presented as ancient identity]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### A. The *Histoyre du Mechique*

**Date and form:** mid-sixteenth century, commonly placed in the 1540s; surviving as a French manuscript derived from a lost Spanish compilation, itself based on central-Mexican traditions. It was published by Édouard de Jonghe in 1905. **[EA]**

**Contribution:** It calls Nanahuatl/Nanauaton a child of Itzpapalotl and a mother variously transmitted or interpreted as Cozcamiauh/Tonantzin; Piltzintecuhtli and Xochiquetzal become foster-parents. It relates his nurture by Tlantepozilamatl, his relationship to Xochipilli, and his eventual selection by Ehecatl and other gods for solar transformation. The lunar rival is not narrated with the clarity found in Sahagún. **[EA/RV]**

Because this survives through at least two linguistic filters, exact Nahuatl nomenclature and kinship terminology cannot always be recovered. It is early evidence, but not a transparent transcript.

### B. *Leyenda de los Soles* (“Manuscript of 1558”)

**Language and date:** Classical Nahuatl; its opening states that writing began on 22 May 1558. The surviving witness formed the final portion of the manuscript later called the *Codex Chimalpopoca*. The extant manuscript was probably a seventeenth-century copy of the sixteenth-century composition. The original *Codex Chimalpopoca* disappeared from Mexico’s Museo Nacional in the twentieth century; copies, photographs, and editions preserve its contents. **[EA]**

**Physical and textual setting:** a short alphabetic Nahuatl manuscript on European paper, not a surviving pre-Conquest screenfold. “Codex Chimalpopoca” is a later collective title covering the *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*, a short treatise, and the *Leyenda de los Soles*. UNAM supplies a digital edition and facsimile resources.

**Nanahuatl’s roles:**

1. Oxomoco and Cipactonal divine that Nanahuatl will strike Tonacatepetl and release maize and other foods; the Tlaloque then seize the provisions. **[EA/RV]**
2. Before the present Sun exists, Nanahuatl is at Tamoanchan. **[EA/RV]**
3. Tonacateuctli and Xiuhteuctli tell him that he will “guard” or sustain heaven and earth. **[EA/RV]**
4. He performs penitential bloodletting with maguey spines and bathes first; Metztli/4-Flint follows with rich materials. **[EA/RV]**
5. Nanahuatl enters the fire first. Metztli falls only into the ashes. Eagle and jaguar enter afterward and are scorched. **[EA/RV]**
6. Tonacateuctli and Tonacacihuatl enthrone and adorn the solar Nanahuatl in heaven. **[EA/RV]**
7. As the motionless Sun, he demands the gods’ blood. Tlahuizcalpanteuctli attacks him and misses; the Sun shoots back and transforms or disables the attacker. **[EA/RV]**
8. The gods die at Teotihuacan; the Sun then enters the movement-sign 4-Ollin. **[EA/RV]**

This version is the strongest warning against treating Sahagún’s account as the single “Aztec creation myth.”

### C. Sahagún’s corpus

#### *Primeros Memoriales*

Compiled at Tepepolco/Tepeapulco with Indigenous noble informants and trilingual assistants, approximately 1558–1561. These materials are the first stage of Sahagún’s project. The surviving manuscript is divided between Madrid collections. It preserves divine descriptions, ritual calendars, sacred songs, and preliminary material, but the fully developed fire-jump narrative belongs most clearly to the later Book VII redaction. **[EA]**

#### Matritense manuscripts and scholia

The project was revised at Tlatelolco around 1564–1565. The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl preserves indexed forms of *nanāhuātzin* from these scholia. This helps demonstrate textual development between Tepepolco memoranda and the final codex. **[EA]**

#### *Florentine Codex*, Book VII, chapter 2

**Language and date:** parallel Nahuatl and Spanish, compiled by Sahagún with Indigenous writers and painters; final manuscript completed at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco in 1577. The original is in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence. Anderson and Dibble’s critical English translation of Book VII appeared in 1953.

**Account:** before daylight, the gods assemble at Teotihuacan. The rich Tecuciztecatl volunteers; no second candidate does, so the gods choose Nanahuatzin, who accepts respectfully. Both perform four days of penance.

Their ritual contrast is systematic:

| Tecuciztecatl | Nanahuatzin |
|---|---|
| Quetzal feathers | Green reeds |
| Gold balls | Hay or grass balls |
| Precious coral spines | Maguey spines stained with his own blood |
| Copal | Scabs from his sores |
| Fine regalia | Paper headdress, paper stole, paper loincloth |

At midnight, Tecuciztecatl approaches the fire four times and retreats. Nanahuatzin closes his eyes, gathers himself, and jumps immediately. Tecuciztecatl follows. Eagle and jaguar enter or approach the fire, explaining their scorched markings. The gods wait for dawn and disagree over the direction of sunrise; Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl and several others correctly face east. Nanahuatzin rises first, Tecuciztecatl second, both equally bright. A god—often left unnamed in the Nahuatl narrative—throws a rabbit into Tecuciztecatl’s face, dimming him into the Moon. The luminaries remain still. The gods must die; Ehecatl kills or sacrifices them and then blows the Sun and Moon into motion. Xolotl attempts to evade death by transformations before being killed. **[EA/LC]**

The Spanish column sometimes interprets rather than literally translates the Nahuatl. Anderson and Dibble’s English should therefore be checked against the Nahuatl column, now accessible through the Getty’s Digital Florentine Codex.

### D. *Anales de Cuauhtitlan*

A Nahuatl annalistic history compiled in the sixteenth century and copied into the manuscript later called *Codex Chimalpopoca*. It records the present Sun’s birth in a year 13-Reed and calls it 4-Movement. Its treatment is briefer and not identical to the attached *Leyenda*. **[EA/RV]** The two works should not be cited as though they were one authorial text.

### E. *Histoyre du Mechique* and related Olmos tradition

The anonymous *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, usually dated approximately 1530s–1540s and associated indirectly with Andrés de Olmos’s lost investigations, gives important sequences of the world-ages but does not preserve the same full Nanahuatzin–Tecuciztecatl drama as Sahagún. References to “the creation of the fifth Sun” in modern summaries sometimes import Sahagún’s details into this separate source. **[EA, with textual caution]**

### F. Ruiz de Alarcón

Hernando Ruiz de Alarcón’s *Tratado de las supersticiones y costumbres gentílicas que hoy viven entre los indios naturales desta Nueva España*, completed in 1629, records Nahua ritual language and beliefs from communities around Guerrero and Morelos while explicitly seeking to suppress them. Its references to the solar birth tradition show that related conceptions remained intelligible about a century after conquest. It is evidence of colonial survival under persecution, not a neutral pre-Conquest transcript. **[EM]**

### G. Pictorial manuscripts named in the brief

- **Codex Borgia:** probably Late Postclassic and pre-Conquest or very early colonial, from the Puebla–Tlaxcala–Mixteca sphere rather than demonstrably Mexica-Tenochtitlan. Page 10 contains a malformed deity governing 17-Movement, often identified by Seler and successors as Xolotl or “Nanahuatzin-Xolotl.” Pages 29–46 contain ritual-cosmological sequences including fire rites. None carries an alphabetic label “Nanahuatzin.” Boone reads the central section as a structured cosmological narrative; Nowotny emphasized ritual; other readings differ sharply. The codex is therefore possible comparative evidence, not the earliest secure attestation of Nanahuatzin. **[MSR, disputed]**
- **Codex Telleriano-Remensis and Vaticanus A/Ríos:** contain calendrical and solar-lunar material; later glosses associate Tecciztecatl with the Moon. Modern Tlachia commentary explains the Nanahuatzin story using *Florentine Codex* Book VII, but that commentary is not proof that the original painted page names Nanahuatzin. **[LS/MSR]**
- **Codex Borbonicus:** depicts calendrical patrons, solar imagery, and the New Fire ceremony; no uncontested labeled portrait of Nanahuatzin is present. **[absence finding]**
- **Codex Magliabechiano:** important for festivals, deity costumes, sacrifice, and colonial glosses; no secure independent Nanahuatzin cult-image has been established. **[absence finding]**
- **Codex Mendoza:** records tribute, conquest, education, and social institutions; it does not furnish an independent narrative or securely labeled image of Nanahuatzin. **[absence finding]**
- **Cantares Mexicanos:** contains solar, sacrificial, martial, and divine language but no securely identified major Nanahuatzin hymn comparable to the Book VII narrative. **[absence finding]**
- **Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme* and *Book of the Gods and Rites*:** essential for Mexica ritual and sacrifice, but not an independent full witness to Nanahuatzin’s fire-jump comparable to Sahagún or the *Leyenda*. **[absence finding]**

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

Nanahuatzin’s securely attested functions are narrative rather than institutional:

- voluntary self-offering;
- transformation into the present Sun;
- origination of daylight and, after further divine sacrifice, calendrical-cosmic movement;
- exemplary penitential bloodletting;
- in the *Leyenda*, breaking open Tonacatepetl and helping release human food;
- embodying the reversal by which the poor, diseased, or socially underestimated candidate surpasses the rich and prestigious one.

“God of humility,” “god of courage,” “patron of the diseased,” and “god of self-sacrifice” are modern taxonomic descriptions. No surviving ritual calendar assigns him an uncontested annual feast under those offices. **[MSR]**

### Body and dress

In Sahagún he is covered with sores or pustules. His ritual dress is paper; his branches are humble reeds, his incense or offering consists of scabs, and his bloodied maguey spines contrast with Tecuciztecatl’s coral. These are temporary narrative attributes, not necessarily a permanent divine costume. **[EA/LC]**

The common modern depiction of Nanahuatzin as short, hunchbacked, elderly, or visibly leprous exceeds the principal account unless tied to a particular image or retelling. **[MI or disputed MSR]**

### Fire and solar signs

His primary symbols are:

- the **teotexcalli**, divine furnace or hearth at Teotihuacan;
- penitential **maguey spines** and blood;
- humble **paper regalia**;
- the eastern dawn;
- the calendrical sign **4-Movement**;
- the solar disk only after transformation.

The **rabbit** belongs primarily to the diminution of the lunar rival, not to Nanahuatzin’s own permanent iconography.

### Claimed visual representations

1. **Florentine Codex, Book VII illustrations, 1577:** the only securely contextualized early images of the episode, accompanying the Nahuatl and Spanish text. These colonial paintings show the candidates, their offerings, the fire, and resulting luminaries. **[EA]**
2. **Codex Borgia, page 10:** the deformed or canine-associated figure governing 17-Movement has been called Nanahuatzin-Xolotl. Since it lacks a name-gloss and shares attributes with Xolotl, the identification is contested. **[MSR]**
3. **Figures emerging from fire in the Borgia group:** sometimes captioned online as Nanahuatzin; the Borgia sequence’s interpretation remains disputed, and no such caption should be treated as original. **[MSR/MI when asserted categorically]**
4. **British Museum turquoise mosaic mask:** formerly or popularly identified as Nanahuatzin because raised cabochons were interpreted as sores. Davide Domenici argues that both the Nanahuatzin and Xiuhtecuhtli identifications are doubtful: the cabochons are a broader formal device and the supposed fire-butterfly is insecure. **[disputed MSR]**
5. **Sun Stone:** carved in the late Mexica imperial period, conventionally around 1502–1521. Its center represents 4-Movement and a solar or earth-associated face, variously interpreted. It documents the present world-age and sacrificial solar cosmology, not a labeled portrait of Nanahuatzin. **[MSR, indirect evidence]**

There is no secure basis for assigning Nanahuatzin a unique weapon, sacred animal, color, temple costume, consort-symbol, or priestly insignia.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

#### Sahagún and the *Leyenda*

Neither supplies biological parents, spouse, children, or siblings for Nanahuatzin. He is already among the assembled gods. His social lowliness and disease matter more than descent. **[EA]**

#### *Histoyre du Mechique*

This source alone gives an extended genealogy:

- **Mother:** Itzpapalotl in one strand of the transmission; other passages or translations create ambiguity about Cozcamiauh/Tonantzin.
- **Father or co-parent:** uncertain in the damaged or filtered transmission.
- **Foster-parents:** Piltzintecuhtli and Xochiquetzal.
- **Foster-brother/household relation:** Xochipilli.
- **Guardian:** Tlantepozilamatl, “Old Woman of the Metal Teeth.”

Because the source survives in French from a lost Spanish intermediary, and because no parallel genealogy appears in Sahagún or the *Leyenda*, this is a **regional/textual variant**, not a dominant Mexica family tree. **[EA/RV]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### I. The Sahaguntine contest at Teotihuacan

**[EA/LC]** Darkness precedes the present era. The gods assemble at Teotihuacan and ask who will illuminate the world. Wealthy Tecuciztecatl volunteers; Nanahuatzin, silent and afflicted, is nominated by the gods and accepts.

Their four-day penances reveal the moral architecture of the story. Tecuciztecatl gives objects that appear costly but substitutes beauty for bodily cost: coral for bloodied thorns, feathers for common branches. Nanahuatzin gives poor objects but penetrates his body and supplies his own blood. This opposition is not simply wealth versus poverty; it is ostentatious representation versus efficacious self-expenditure.

At the furnace, Tecuciztecatl is summoned first. Four times he advances and retreats; a fifth trial is not allowed. Nanahuatzin is called. He does not deliberate, closes his eyes, and jumps. Shamed or emboldened, Tecuciztecatl follows.

After both disappear, the gods wait. Those who look east correctly see Nanahuatzin rise, then Tecuciztecatl. Both shine equally. The divine assembly refuses this equality because the late imitator cannot receive the same honor as the first sacrificer. A rabbit is thrown into Tecuciztecatl’s face; its silhouette remains visible on the Moon.

The new luminaries do not move. The Sun demands the other gods’ lives. Ehecatl sacrifices them, but Xolotl flees and transforms successively before being found. Even after the deaths, the Sun requires Ehecatl’s wind to begin its course. Creation is therefore not completed by Nanahuatzin alone: his leap produces radiance, the gods’ deaths supply reciprocal payment, and wind produces motion.

**Function:** The narrative explains the Sun, Moon, lunar rabbit, eagle and jaguar markings, eastern dawn, sacrifice, and the dependence of cosmic movement upon divine death. Whether it was deliberately composed as a state “charter” for imperial human sacrifice is debated. The text supports a logic of reciprocal sacrifice; direct proof that Mexica rulers invented the story solely to legitimate conquest is absent.

### II. The *Leyenda de los Soles* variant

**[EA/RV]** Nanahuatl first appears in the acquisition of food. Oxomoco and Cipactonal divine that he must break Tonacatepetl. He strikes it; the Tlaloque acquire the foods within. This makes him a culture-bringer as well as solar martyr.

For the solar creation, Tonacateuctli and Xiuhteuctli commission Nanahuatl. His counterpart is not called Tecuciztecatl but 4-Flint and Metztli. Nanahuatl uses green reeds and maguey thorns; the Moon-candidate uses quetzal feathers, jade, and rich incense. Nanahuatl enters the fire while the other falls into ash.

The eagle and jaguar follow. Nanahuatl rises and is enthroned by Tonacateuctli and Tonacacihuatl. For four days he remains stationary. He demands the blood and precious substance of the other gods. Tlahuizcalpanteuctli, angered, shoots at him; the dart misses. The Sun’s answering shot hits the dawn lord, providing an etiological conflict between Sun and Morning Star. A divine massacre or collective sacrifice occurs at Teotihuacan, and the Sun enters 4-Movement.

This text lacks the fully narrated four retreats and rabbit-throw found in Sahagún. The Moon is delayed and wrapped in rags by female celestial beings. The differences may reflect distinct communities, performances, or redactional purposes.

### III. The *Histoyre du Mechique* cycle

**[EA/RV]** Here Nanahuatl is not merely introduced at the furnace. He belongs to a mythic family and foster-household, is associated with Itzpapalotl, Xochiquetzal, Piltzintecuhtli, Xochipilli, and Tlantepozilamatl, and passes through events that overlap broader creation and vegetation traditions. Ehecatl and other gods select him for the solar office.

Modern summaries frequently splice this genealogy onto Sahagún’s contest, producing a seamless biography that no single source contains. The genealogy and the rich-poor contest should instead be printed side by side.

### IV. The five world-ages

Nanahuatzin belongs to the transition into the present age, but the number and order of previous Suns vary. Sources arrange eras of Jaguar, Wind, Fire-Rain, and Water differently. Michel Graulich argued that the specifically Mexica five-Sun arrangement may represent a political-historical restructuring of a more widespread four-age system, inserting Mexica ascendancy into cosmic chronology. Other scholars treat fivefold organization as more deeply embedded in Mesoamerican spatial and calendrical thought. **[MSR dispute]**

The Sun Stone’s 4-Movement center and four surrounding era signs make a fivefold structure materially visible in late Mexica state art, but do not settle when every narrative component arose.

### V. Humility and “the Mexica ethos”

Sahagún’s text unquestionably contrasts Nanahuatzin’s poverty, illness, real blood, obedience, and courage with Tecuciztecatl’s wealth, precious substitutes, hesitation, and diminished reward. **[EA]**

Calling this “the Mexica ethos of humility” is more difficult. Mexica elite ideology also prized magnificence, martial distinction, costly offerings, lineage, and public honor. Nanahuatzin’s lesson is not a generalized rejection of rank or wealth. It is better read as a ritual paradox: the socially least impressive being becomes supreme because he fulfills the gods’ demand completely. León-Portilla emphasized ethical and existential self-sacrifice; Graulich emphasized purification, death, and victorious rebirth; political readings see the younger, poorer newcomer defeating the established rich power. These are compatible possibilities, not explicit statements by the narrator. **[MSR]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No securely demonstrated independent cult

No temple securely identified as a **Temple of Nanahuatzin**, no hereditary Nanahuatzin priesthood, no annual veintena devoted exclusively to him, and no corpus of Nanahuatzin votive inscriptions is presently known. His documentary prominence is mythic and cosmological rather than that of a separately administered civic patron. **[absence finding]**

This matters because modern pantheon charts often give every named *teōtl* a discrete “portfolio,” feast, and sanctuary. That bureaucratic model is unsupported here.

### Teotihuacan

Colonial Nahua narrators located the divine gathering and furnace at Teotihuacan, a city whose major monumental phase had ended centuries before Mexica power. Mexica and other Late Postclassic visitors treated its ruins as a primordial sacred landscape. The later names “Pyramid of the Sun” and “Pyramid of the Moon” express post-Teotihuacan interpretation; they do not prove that the original Teotihuacanos built those monuments for Nanahuatzin and Tecuciztecatl.

No Classic-period Teotihuacan inscription identifies Nanahuatzin. Attempts to read murals or fire scenes as his pre-Conquest portrait remain retrospective **[MSR]**.

### New Fire and calendrical renewal

The New Fire ceremony every fifty-two years extinguished and rekindled fire when the calendrical rounds coincided. Codex Borbonicus and colonial descriptions document the rite. Scholars have compared the fire-drilling and rebirth of time to Nanahuatzin’s leap. This is a persuasive structural relationship, but surviving ritual texts do not simply say that every New Fire victim “was Nanahuatzin.” **[MSR]**

### Bloodletting and human sacrifice

Nanahuatzin’s own offering is self-bloodletting followed by voluntary immolation. The Sun then demands the other gods’ death. These narrative acts fit a wider Nahua economy of *nextlahualli*, debt-payment or reciprocation: life and motion arise through divine expenditure and require return.

Human sacrifice in Mexica state religion is not merely a Spanish allegation. It is supported by Indigenous pictorial and alphabetic sources, Spanish eyewitness and clerical accounts that require critical handling, osteological remains, sacrificial deposits, skull racks, cut marks, and temple contexts. Victims included war captives, enslaved persons, children in rain rites, deity impersonators, and others according to festival and political setting.

The Urban Archaeology Program’s 2015–2017 work at 24 República de Guatemala identified remains belonging to the **Huei Tzompantli** of Tenochtitlan, including the skull-rack platform and associated skull structures. These discoveries corroborate large-scale public display of sacrificial heads. They do **not** demonstrate a distinct cult of Nanahuatzin; their relevance is to the material reality of the sacrificial system within which his myth was interpreted.

The relation between myth and killing should be stated narrowly:

- The myth supplies a model in which gods sacrifice first and the Sun demands payment. **[EA]**
- Mexica rites demonstrably killed human beings and offered hearts, blood, bodies, or heads to various gods. **[archaeologically and textually attested]**
- The proposition that the Nanahuatzin myth alone caused or uniquely authorized all Mexica human sacrifice is reductive. **[rejected MSR simplification]**
- Claims that human sacrifice was wholly fabricated by Spaniards are contradicted by archaeology. **[MI/discredited]**
- Claims of fantastically precise annual totals are not archaeologically demonstrable and often derive from polemical colonial figures. **[contested]**

### Templo Mayor and major discoveries

Tenochtitlan’s Templo Mayor was principally dedicated to **Huitzilopochtli** and **Tlaloc**, not Nanahuatzin. The Coyolxauhqui monolith, discovered on 21 February 1978, initiated the modern Proyecto Templo Mayor; the Tlaltecuhtli monolith was discovered on 2 October 2006. Both transformed knowledge of Mexica ritual topography and sacrificial offerings, but neither is an image of Nanahuatzin.

Their inclusion in this dossier establishes the archaeological frame and prevents a textual solar myth from being mistaken for a fully excavated Nanahuatzin cult.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 1200 CE: possible deep background, no named attestation

Mesoamerican fire-renewal, solar succession, divine self-sacrifice, lunar-rabbit imagery, and paired luminary myths predate the Mexica. Comparisons with the *Popol Vuh* Hero Twins and other Indigenous traditions suggest shared or interacting narrative structures. No evidence securely carries the personal name Nanahuatzin back to Olmec, Maya, or Classic Teotihuacan inscriptions. **[MSR]**

### Late Postclassic, approximately 1200–1521

The myths recorded after conquest probably preserve substantial pre-Conquest material: Teotihuacan as creation-place, calendrical world ages, penitential bloodletting, divine sacrifice, solar hunger, and the Moon-rabbit were deeply integrated into central-Mexican cosmology. The Sun Stone provides late pre-Conquest material evidence for 4-Movement and the preceding eras.

Nevertheless, no surviving pre-Conquest object securely labels Nanahuatzin. His exact prominence in Tenochtitlan before 1521 cannot be measured from the surviving evidence. **[absence finding]**

### 1521–1558: conquest and first documentation

Spanish conquest dismantled state temples, killed or displaced ritual specialists, confiscated and burned sacred images and manuscripts, redirected tribute, and imposed Christianity. Indigenous intellectuals nevertheless recorded religious knowledge in Latin-script Nahuatl, pictorial manuscripts, and testimony gathered by friars.

The *Histoyre du Mechique* preserves the earliest surviving Nanahuatl material, while the 1558 *Leyenda* gives the earliest explicitly dated Nahuatl account.

### 1558–1577: Sahaguntine codification

Nahua elders, grammarians, scribes, and painters working with Sahagún converted performance and memory into an encyclopedic bilingual manuscript. The process preserved invaluable evidence but also selected, reorganized, translated, and framed Indigenous religion for Christian missionary study.

Book VII’s polished opposition between the humble and wealthy candidates became the dominant printed version because Sahagún’s corpus eventually circulated widely in modern translation—not because it was necessarily the single canonical version in pre-Conquest Mexico.

### Late sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: suppression and survival

Evangelization condemned the old gods as demons or false gods. Temples and images were destroyed or repurposed; ritual was prosecuted as idolatry. Ruiz de Alarcón’s 1629 *Tratado* demonstrates that Nahua ritual speech and solar-sacrificial traditions survived locally, often below the level of public institutions. His work is simultaneously documentation and an instrument of repression. **[EM]**

No secure evidence demonstrates an unbroken, publicly organized cult specifically to Nanahuatzin through the colonial period.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: antiquarian recovery

Collectors and scholars copied, catalogued, and sometimes renamed the manuscripts. Lorenzo Boturini described material resembling the later *Codex Chimalpopoca*. Antonio de León y Gama and nineteenth-century antiquarians interpreted the Sun Stone and solar epochs. Francisco del Paso y Troncoso supplied the title *Leyenda de los Soles* and published the Nahuatl text in 1903. De Jonghe published the *Histoyre du Mechique* in 1905.

These publications created the modern documentary Nanahuatzin by bringing previously dispersed witnesses into comparison. **[MSR]**

### Twentieth century: national and academic canonization

Seler, Caso, Garibay, León-Portilla, Nicholson, Graulich, López Austin, Carrasco, Townsend, Boone, and others integrated Nanahuatzin into scholarly models of cosmology, sacrifice, kingship, and cyclic time.

Post-Revolutionary Mexican education and art elevated Indigenous antiquity into national heritage. The humble god who becomes the Sun was particularly adaptable to narratives of courage, social reversal, sacrifice, and collective renewal. This was a modern national reception, not proof of an unchanged ancient ethic.

### Twenty-first century

Nanahuatzin circulates in museums, textbooks, animation, online mythology, Indigenous-revival discourse, games, and popular spirituality. Mexico’s official educational publishing now uses **Colección Nanahuatzin** as a series title and explicitly interprets him as the humble body whose courageous sacrifice provides light.

There are individuals and small revivalist groups who honor Mexica deities, but no reliable census isolates Nanahuatzin worship, and no evidence establishes institutional continuity from a pre-Conquest Nanahuatzin priesthood. Modern devotion should be described as reconstruction, revitalization, or new religious creation according to the group’s own history. **[modern revival]**

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and public education

Miguel León-Portilla’s *Aztec Thought and Culture* and related works presented Nahua cosmology as philosophically serious and emphasized sacrifice, *Nahui Ollin*, and humanity’s cosmic predicament. His reconstructions were enormously influential but have been criticized where they treat texts from different places and dates as expressions of a unified “Nahua mind.” **[MSR]**

The official Mexican **Colección Nanahuatzin** for secondary education calls him “Señor con el cuerpo llagado” and makes him a symbol of knowledge offered for communal life. That ethical educational program is a twenty-first-century reinterpretation, not an ancient cult title. **[MI, transparently modern]**

### Film and visual narrative

- **Juan Mora Catlett, *In Necuepaliztli in Aztlan / Return to Aztlán* (1991):** uses Nahua myth and Nahuatl dialogue in an Indigenous-centered cinematic reconstruction; the Fifth Sun complex provides part of its conceptual field. **[modern artistic reconstruction]**
- **Patricia Amlin, *The Five Suns: A Sacred History of Mexico* (1996; later high-definition distribution):** animated synthesis combining several colonial sources into a continuous sacred history. It vividly presents Nanahuatzin but necessarily harmonizes incompatible texts. **[MI/adaptation]**
- Museum graphics and online illustrations frequently copy the disputed Borgia figure and label it “Nanahuatzin.” Without an accompanying qualification, this converts Seler’s hypothesis into supposed fact. **[MI based on disputed MSR]**

### Literature, psychology, and politics

Nanahuatzin is regularly made an archetype of the wounded outsider, the despised poor man, disability transformed into power, or self-sacrificing revolutionary. Such readings may be illuminating, but there is no ancient evidence for Jungian archetypal terminology, modern disability identity, socialism, or nationalist martyrdom as his original “meaning.” **[MI/MSR]**

The story has also been used to express post-Revolutionary *indigenismo*: the humiliated Indigenous past becomes the light of modern Mexico. This political symbolism belongs to modern Mexican nation-building.

### Occultism, esotericism, and neopaganism

The dedicated contested-material search produced no evidence that Nanahuatzin held a significant place in Renaissance Hermetism, the Theosophical Society, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, traditional European grimoires, or early chaos magic. Online assertions assigning him planetary seals, angelic correspondences, chakras, tarot cards, or standardized magical colors are modern inventions unless a specific modern author is cited. **[absence finding/MI]**

Contemporary Mexicayotl, Nahua revitalization, and eclectic neopagan practice may invoke him as the Fifth Sun or exemplar of *teōtl*-centered sacrifice. Practices vary, and published evidence does not support claims of an unbroken secret Nanahuatzin order surviving conquest. **[modern revival; continuity unestablished]**

### Commercial and gaming use

Names and imagery circulate in role-playing settings, independent games, merchandise, tattoos, and online mythology databases. These commonly:

- turn him into a permanent armored “sun god” with a stable weapon set;
- describe him as a son of Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue;
- merge him completely with Xolotl, Tonatiuh, or Huitzilopochtli;
- give Tecuciztecatl’s rabbit to Nanahuatzin;
- treat every Borgia fire scene as an illustration of his myth.

None of those details should be called ancient without a specific primary source. **[MI]**

No significant modern extremist appropriation centered specifically on Nanahuatzin was located. Broader racial-nationalist or exclusionary appropriations of “Aztec” identity exist, but attaching them to this deity without evidence would be misleading.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Etymology and disease

The linguistic connection to pustules or buboes is strong. The medical identification is not. “Syphilitic Nanahuatzin” reflects older translation habits and post-Columbian disease debates; “pustule-covered” or “afflicted with sores” stays closer to the evidence. The possibility that bodily affliction marks ritual impurity transformed into sacred heat is interpretive. **[MSR]**

### Was he inherently the Sun?

One position treats Nanahuatzin as a low-ranking deity who becomes Tonatiuh through sacrifice. Another treats the pre-fire Nanahuatzin as already a solar form temporarily abased, or as one manifestation in a fluid complex of solar beings. The sources narrate transformation but do not provide a systematic theology resolving all solar identities. **[debate unresolved]**

### Nanahuatzin and Xolotl

Seler’s iconographic identification links the malformed Borgia deity, movement-sign, fire, and Xolotl’s sacrificial role. Yet in Sahagún, Nanahuatzin becomes the Sun while Xolotl resists death; they occupy contrasting narrative positions. A fusion may exist in certain iconographic or calendrical settings, but “Nanahuatzin was simply Xolotl” is not established. **[MSR dispute]**

### The Borgia interpretation

Eduard Seler read the Borgia’s central pages mythologically and astrally; Karl Anton Nowotny shifted attention to ritual; Elizabeth Hill Boone interprets the pages as cycles of time and cosmological meaning; other scholars propose initiation or visionary sequences. Since none of the painted figures is alphabetically named Nanahuatzin, his presence depends upon the chosen model. **[MSR dispute]**

### Political interpretation

Graulich read the contest sociologically: poor, valiant newcomer against wealthy, established, declining power; more widely, younger brother against elder. This fits other Mesoamerican reversals but is not stated by Sahagún’s narrator as an allegory of a particular conquest.

Carrasco and others emphasize sacrifice, sacred center, and state formation. The Mexica state could use solar debt to naturalize warfare and tribute. Yet the myth’s distribution beyond a single imperial text and its variant forms argue against reducing it to late Tenochtitlan propaganda.

### Ethical interpretation

León-Portilla’s treatment helped make Nanahuatzin the supreme image of humility and self-sacrifice. The source supports humility as a contrastive virtue, but “the Mexica ethos” risks flattening a society whose political ritual also celebrated rank, costly display, and captive-taking. **[MSR qualification]**

### Chronology of the five Suns

Graulich argued that a five-Sun scheme was a comparatively restricted Mexica reorganization of four-age traditions. Other scholarship emphasizes the five-part Mesoamerican cosmos—four directions plus center—and regards five ages as structurally coherent rather than an ad hoc imperial innovation. The surviving sources genuinely disagree in order, duration, and nomenclature.

### Human-sacrifice debate

The serious debate now concerns scale, local variation, political economy, victim categories, ritual meanings, and the reliability of particular colonial numbers—not whether ritual killing occurred. Templo Mayor excavations and the Huei Tzompantli discoveries independently establish it.

The Nanahuatzin narrative should be understood as one mythic model of divine precedent and reciprocal obligation. It cannot alone explain every sacrificial rite, many of which belonged to Tlaloc, Huitzilopochtli, Xipe Totec, Tezcatlipoca, earth deities, agricultural cycles, royal politics, or war.

### Source loss and preservation

The evidence is unusually dependent on colonial mediation:

- pre-Conquest screenfolds were destroyed in conquest and evangelization;
- oral performances were written down after catastrophic social disruption;
- friars shaped questionnaires and classifications;
- Indigenous writers made active editorial choices;
- the original *Codex Chimalpopoca* disappeared, leaving copies and photographs;
- the *Histoyre du Mechique* survives only through French translation of lost material;
- the *Florentine Codex* survives in Florence and has now been digitally reunited and indexed.

Consequently, “earliest attested” must never be confused with “date when the myth originated.”
