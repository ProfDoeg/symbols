# Coatlicue: Research Dossier

**Class:** female *teōtl*—a divine/sacred being—described in the principal named source as a woman and mother at Coatepec; conventionally classified as a Mexica goddess, especially an earth/mother deity. “Monster” is an aesthetic or analytical description of certain sculptures, not the tradition’s primary classification.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest evidence unquestionably naming Coatlicue is colonial: Nahuatl testimony collected for Bernardino de Sahagún’s project in the mid-sixteenth century, preserved in Book III of the *Florentine Codex*, completed about 1577. It recounts her miraculous conception of Huitzilopochtli at Coatepec. Monumental female sculptures conventionally identified as Coatlicue are older—probably fifteenth or early sixteenth century—but they bear no identifying inscription, and the identification of the largest example remains disputed. No surviving pre-Conquest inscription securely names her.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[secure named attestation]**, **[late-Postclassic material evidence]**, **[colonial canonical account]**, **[regional/authorial variant]**, **[learned systematization]**, **[syncretistic identification]**, **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**, and **[modern reinvention]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Secure named attestation / colonial canonical account]** In *Florentine Codex* III, chapter 1, Coatlicue is a woman living at **Coatepec**, “Serpent Mountain,” near Tollan. She is mother of Coyolxauhqui and the Centzon Huitznahua and miraculously conceives Huitzilopochtli when a ball of feathers descends while she performs penance by sweeping. The text does not initially introduce her through a universal theological office such as “Earth Mother”; it introduces her narratively, as a mother and penitent at a particular sacred mountain.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The familiar encyclopedic identity—primordial Earth Mother governing birth, fertility, death, and regeneration—combines this story with the imagery of the colossal “Coatlicue,” comparative study of other earth deities, and colonial descriptions of the larger mother-goddess complex. Those associations are plausible and often illuminating, but they should not be mistaken for a single surviving pre-Conquest creed.

### Name

The Classical Nahuatl form is conventionally normalized as **Cōātlīcue**, alternatively segmented **Cōātl īcue**:

- **cōātl**: serpent or snake;
- **ī-**: third-person possessive, “her”;
- **cuēitl**, in possessed form **īcue**: skirt.

The sense is therefore **“Serpents Are Her Skirt,” “She of the Serpent Skirt,”** or more idiomatically **“Serpent-Skirt.”** Colonial spelling lacks standardized vowel-length notation and permits forms such as *Coatlicue*, *Coatlique*, and *Cuatlicue*. The Gran Diccionario Náhuatl documents the form in Tezozómoc’s tradition and gives the familial identification “mother of Coyolxauhqui, the Centzonhuitznahuah, and Huitzilopochtli.”

The name is transparent enough that no major rival etymology is required. What is disputed is not chiefly its lexical meaning but whether every serpent-skirted sculpture represents this individually named being.

### Titles and proposed equations

- **Tēteoh īnnān**, “Mother of the Gods”: **[learned systematization / disputed equation]** Frequently attached to Coatlicue in modern literature. Colonial sources apply comparable mother-titles to a network of female beings, notably Toci and Teteoinnan. It is unsafe to treat all those names as timeless synonyms.
- **Toci**, “Our Grandmother”: **[syncretistic or taxonomic identification]** Older scholarship frequently identified Toci with Coatlicue. This may express overlapping maternal, terrestrial, sacrificial, and regenerative functions, but the ritual sources can treat Toci as a separately costumed and separately worshipped deity.
- **Cihuacoatl**, “Snake Woman”: **[disputed identification]** The colossal statue has sometimes been identified as Cihuacoatl rather than Coatlicue. Cihuacoatl also possessed distinct civic, martial, maternal, and political roles, including an office whose human holder was second only to the *tlatoani*.
- **Tlaltecuhtli**, “Earth Lord/Lady”: **[iconographic equation, disputed]** The earth-monster face carved beneath the colossal statue and its claws, mouths, blood, and devouring imagery connect it with Tlaltecuhtli. That does not prove that Coatlicue and Tlaltecuhtli were simply interchangeable names.
- **Yollotlicue**, “Hearts Are Her Skirt”: **[related sculptural type]** A second monumental female figure is distinguished by a skirt or garment of hearts. Early eyewitness evidence may place two enormous female statues near Huitzilopochtli’s sanctuary, but the precise names and identities remain debated.
- **Teoyaomiqui**, approximately “divine death in war” or a divinity of sacred war-death: **[colonial-antiquarian identification]** Antonio de León y Gama identified the 1790 statue under this name, not as Coatlicue. Alfredo Chavero’s later identification as Coatlicue became standard.
- **Tonantzin**, “Our Revered Mother”: **[title and disputed syncretistic identification]** Tonantzin is a reverential title, not demonstrably Coatlicue’s exclusive personal name. Assertions that the Virgin of Guadalupe simply “is Coatlicue” turn a complicated colonial history into an unsupported one-to-one equation.

No pre-Conquest equation under *interpretatio graeca* or *romana* exists. Comparisons with Cybele, Kali, Demeter, or the Virgin Mary belong to European, comparative-religion, artistic, feminist, or esoteric reception.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Material evidence

**[Late-Postclassic material evidence; identity disputed]** The colossal basalt or andesite sculpture now called the Coatlicue probably dates to late imperial Tenochtitlan, often assigned to the fifteenth or opening sixteenth century. Since it has no surviving name glyph, inscription, or archaeological discovery context that identifies it unequivocally, it is earlier physical evidence for a particular monumental female supernatural type—not necessarily the earliest secure named evidence for Coatlicue.

The sculpture’s discovery away from its primary ritual placement further weakens contextual identification. Its likely connection with the Sacred Precinct is strong; its exact original position is reconstructed rather than archaeologically observed.

### Sahagún’s corpus

#### *Primeros Memoriales*

Compiled at Tepepolco by Sahagún and Nahua collaborators approximately **1559–1561**, the *Primeros Memoriales* preserve early-stage Nahuatl accounts and paintings of deities, ritual costumes, sacred buildings, and festivals. The surviving leaves are divided between Madrid collections. They are indispensable for the structure of Nahua ritual religion, but no securely identified Coatlicue portrait comparable to the later colossal statue can simply be extracted from them without argument.

#### *Florentine Codex*, Book III

**[Secure named attestation / colonial canonical account]** Completed in its surviving bilingual illustrated form about **1577**, Book III, chapter 1, contains the fullest named Coatlicue narrative. On folios beginning around 3r–6r, Coatlicue:

1. lives at Coatepec near Tollan;
2. is mother of the Centzon Huitznahua and their elder sister Coyolxauhqui;
3. performs penance by sweeping;
4. places a descended ball of feathers against or beneath her clothing;
5. finds the feathers gone and herself pregnant;
6. becomes frightened when her children decide to kill her;
7. is reassured by the unborn Huitzilopochtli;
8. survives when he is born fully armed and destroys the attackers.

The Digital Florentine Codex permits comparison of the Nahuatl, Spanish, images, and modern translations. The manuscript is colonial but incorporates Indigenous-language testimony and Indigenous pictorial work. It is not a verbatim pre-Conquest codex.

#### *Florentine Codex*, Book II

Book II records the ritual calendar, temples, divine images, offerings, penitential bloodletting, and human sacrifice. In **Quecholli**, the fourteenth *veintena*, the central named recipient is Mixcoatl. Participants make arrows, fast, bleed their ears, hunt, visit graves, and kill numerous slaves. Later passages place sacrifices of impersonators of the Centzon Huitznahua at Coatlan. Coatlicue’s relation to this complex is reconstructed through the Coatepec myth, the temple name, and the wider Mixcoatl–Chimalman/mother-goddess pattern; the opening description does not say that all Quecholli victims were sacrificed “to Coatlicue.”

### Durán and the Crónica X tradition

Diego Durán’s *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e Islas de Tierra Firme*, including the *Book of the Gods and Rites* and historical narrative, was written mainly in the **1570s–1581**. Durán describes festivals, the Coatepec episode, the Mexica migration, Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, sacrificial performance, and the Templo Mayor as a materialized sacred mountain.

**[Regional/authorial variant]** In the Durán–Tezozómoc or “Crónica X” family, the conflict at Coatepec is often historicized as a rebellion during migration. Coyolxauhqui leads dissidents who want to remain at the mountain; Huitzilopochtli’s victory validates continued migration and Mexica political destiny. This is not identical to Sahagún’s miraculous-birth narrative, even when later retellings fuse them.

### Tezozómoc and Chimalpahin-associated texts

Fernando Alvarado Tezozómoc’s *Crónica Mexicana* and *Crónica Mexicayotl* preserve related migration histories in Spanish and Nahuatl. Their surviving textual histories are complicated, and the latter’s manuscript has connections with Domingo Chimalpahin.

**[Regional/authorial variant]** Some passages call **Coyolxauhqui**, or *Coyolxauhcihuatl*, Huitzilopochtli’s mother. Coatlicue may disappear from the maternal role. This is a decisive warning against harmonizing every narrative into the familiar “Coatlicue mother, Coyolxauhqui daughter” genealogy.

Durán also reports an episode in which Motecuhzoma sends emissaries toward the ancestral homeland. They meet an aged mother of Huitzilopochtli, still living in ascetic poverty and penance. Whether this ancestral mother is explicitly Coatlicue in every recension requires textual caution; later summaries routinely identify her as such.

### Other Nahua annals and cosmogonic texts

The *Leyenda de los Soles* (Nahuatl, copied in **1558**), *Anales de Cuauhtitlan* (sixteenth-century Nahuatl compilation), *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* (Spanish, mid-sixteenth century), and *Histoyre du Mechique* (French manuscript preserving sixteenth-century information) are essential for Mexica cosmogony, the Five Suns, Mixcoatl, Chimalman, Quetzalcoatl, and earth-deity traditions.

**Absence finding:** none supplies the same named Coatlicue birth narrative in the full Sahaguntine form. Their stories are valuable comparanda, not independent witnesses to every detail of Coatlicue’s myth.

The *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas* contains traditions about female divine beings sacrificing themselves to set the Sun in motion. Cecelia Klein uses this material when arguing that the colossal statue may represent one of those sacrificial women rather than the living mother awaiting Huitzilopochtli’s birth.

### Pictorial codices

The *Codex Borgia* and Borgia Group, *Codex Borbonicus*, *Codex Mendoza*, *Codex Magliabechiano*, and *Codex Telleriano-Remensis* document divinatory calendars, ritual dress, festivals, tribute, conquest, and deity complexes. Their dating and provenances differ: the Borgia Group is principally pre- or early colonial and non-Mexica or broadly central Mexican; Mendoza is about 1541; Borbonicus straddles the Conquest in scholarly dating; Magliabechiano and Telleriano-Remensis are colonial.

**Absence finding:** serpent skirts, skulls, hearts, earth mouths, clawed beings, and maternal deities occur across this corpus, but a pictorial resemblance does not automatically name a figure Coatlicue. The widely repeated claim that the Codex Borgia contains “the” canonical portrait of Coatlicue is not secure.

### *Cantares Mexicanos*

The *Cantares Mexicanos* manuscript, largely written in Nahuatl in the later sixteenth century, contains songs shaped by pre-Conquest performance traditions and colonial circumstances.

**Absence finding:** it is not a primary source for the complete feather-ball/Coatepec story, and no securely isolated Coatlicue hymn comparable to the hymns of major deities in Sahagún’s Book II survives. Claims that a modern devotional “Hymn to Coatlicue” is an unbroken ancient liturgy require a manuscript citation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Secure named attestation]** Motherhood, gestation, penance, and the sacred space of Coatepec are explicit.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction from iconography and deity complexes]** Earth, fertility, generation, consumption of the dead, sacrificial death, warfare, and cosmic regeneration are inferred from the great statue and its affinities with Tlaltecuhtli, Toci, Cihuacoatl, and Teteoinnan.

The formula “goddess of life and death” is useful as a description of the statue’s imagery, but no surviving Mexica catechism assigns her that exact portfolio.

### The colossal “Coatlicue”

The approximately 2.5-meter-tall monument is bilaterally organized and carved on all sides and beneath. Its identifying features include:

- a skirt formed from intertwined rattlesnakes;
- a belt or girdle with a skull;
- a necklace composed of severed human hands and hearts;
- pendent breasts, often read as those of an aged or repeatedly nursing mother;
- clawed hands and feet;
- a pair of opposed serpent heads rising from the severed neck, their profiles forming a frontal face;
- serpentine or blood-stream elements at the wrists;
- an earth-monster relief on the underside, invisible when the statue stood upright.

The paired neck serpents are commonly interpreted as streams of blood transformed into serpents after decapitation. The statue therefore represents a decapitated female supernatural, not simply a woman with a snake’s head.

### Identification dispute

- **Traditional identification:** The serpent skirt directly visualizes *Cōātl īcue*. Her maternity, breasts, and decapitation are linked with the mother of Huitzilopochtli and with sacrificial regeneration.
- **Cihuacoatl/Tlaltecuhtli reading:** The earth-mouth, claws, skulls, blood, and monumental placement resemble broader earth and death imagery.
- **Klein’s interpretation:** At least three closely related monumental figures once existed. Their decapitated state and paired monumental arrangement may represent sacrificed divine women who gave their lives to move the Sun, or a class of mythic female victims, rather than one narrative portrait of Coatlicue.
- **Political reading:** Monumental female bodies, severed parts, and Coyolxauhqui’s defeat could naturalize Mexica conquest by turning the defeated body into cosmic order. This is an interpretation of function, not an inscription stating the monument’s message.

### Color, animals, plants, numbers, and celestial bodies

No securely preserved source assigns Coatlicue an exclusive color, calendrical day, number, plant, or celestial body. Snakes are explicit in her name and sculptural type. Earth, mountains, sweeping, feathers, hearts, hands, skulls, and blood are associated through narrative or iconography.

The widespread identifications—

- Coatlicue = Earth,
- Coyolxauhqui = Moon,
- Centzon Huitznahua = southern stars,
- Huitzilopochtli = Sun—

form a powerful astronomical reading of the Coatepec episode. Huitzilopochtli’s solar character and the group’s stellar character are well grounded in broader scholarship; Coyolxauhqui’s exclusive identity as “the Moon goddess” is more interpretive than her name or Sahagún’s story alone establishes.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

| Version | Parent or consort | Children/relations | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sahagún, *Florentine Codex* III, c. 1577 | No sexual partner; Huitzilopochtli conceived through feathers | Mother of Coyolxauhqui, Centzon Huitznahua, and Huitzilopochtli | **[colonial canonical account]** |
| Modern synthetic genealogy | Mixcoatl named as husband/father | Same children | **[learned systematization]**; not stated in the principal birth passage |
| Tezozómoc/Chimalpahin-associated variant | Coatlicue absent or displaced | Coyolxauhqui may be Huitzilopochtli’s mother | **[regional/authorial variant]** |
| Mother-goddess taxonomy | Coatlicue equated with Toci, Teteoinnan, Cihuacoatl, Chimalman, or Tlaltecuhtli | Collective “mother of gods” | **[scholarly or priestly systematization]**, not one stable genealogy |

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Huitzilopochtli — miraculous conception, gestation, reassurance, birth, and rescue.** Coatlicue conceives him through the vanished feather-ball; he speaks from her womb and saves her from the attack. *Florentine Codex* III, chapter 1, compiled mid-sixteenth century and completed c. 1577. **[Colonial canonical account]**
- **Coyolxauhqui — mother and daughter; attempted matricide.** Coyolxauhqui discovers Coatlicue’s pregnancy, calls it shameful, and incites the Centzon Huitznahua to kill her. *Florentine Codex* III, c. 1577. **[Colonial canonical account]**
- **Centzon Huitznahua — mother and sons; collective rebellion.** They agree to kill their mother and are defeated by their newborn brother. *Florentine Codex* III, c. 1577. **[Colonial canonical account]**
- **Cuahuitlicac — indirect protective interaction.** One of the Centzon Huitznahua refuses the plot and repeatedly informs the unborn Huitzilopochtli of the attackers’ progress. His protection benefits Coatlicue, though the text’s spoken exchange is with Huitzilopochtli. *Florentine Codex* III, c. 1577. **[Colonial canonical account]**
- **Mixcoatl — inferred consort or ritual partner.** Later taxonomies call him Coatlicue’s husband; Quecholli scholarship reconstructs a ritual pairing between Mixcoatl and a Chimalman–Coatlicue figure. The feather-conception account does not name him as father. **[Learned systematization / modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Toci/Teteoinnan — equation, not narrative encounter.** Sources and scholars group their maternal and terrestrial aspects, but no securely attested story records Coatlicue meeting another being named Toci. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Cihuacoatl — equation, not narrative encounter.** Competing identification of statues and overlapping functions; no surviving mythic meeting. **[Syncretistic identification / scholarly dispute]**
- **Tlaltecuhtli — iconographic conjunction, not narrative encounter.** A Tlaltecuhtli-like earth image appears beneath the colossal sculpture. That juxtaposition is material, but it does not describe two characters acting together. **[Late-Postclassic material evidence / interpretation]**
- **Chimalman — proposed homolog, not meeting.** Both are mothers in miraculous-conception traditions; ritual scholarship can speak of “Chimalman-Coatlicue.” No primary narrative records the two as separate beings interacting. **[Comparative reconstruction]**
- **Xiuhcoatl — no direct interaction.** The fire/turquoise serpent is Huitzilopochtli’s weapon against Coyolxauhqui; Coatlicue is the threatened beneficiary. *Florentine Codex* III. **[Colonial canonical account]**

No securely attested encounter with Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, Tlaloc, Xipe Totec, Mictlantecuhtli, or the Virgin of Guadalupe is documented in a premodern Coatlicue narrative.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The birth of Huitzilopochtli

**[Colonial canonical account]** Coatlicue lives at Coatepec and performs penance by sweeping. A small ball of fine feathers descends. She picks it up and places it against her bosom or beneath her skirt; when she later searches for it, it has disappeared. She discovers that she is pregnant.

Her children are outraged. Coyolxauhqui characterizes the pregnancy as dishonor and persuades the Centzon Huitznahua to kill their mother. Coatlicue grieves and fears death, but Huitzilopochtli speaks from inside her, telling her not to fear because he knows what must be done.

The conspirators arm themselves and climb Coatepec. Cuahuitlicac reports their progress through successive locations. When they arrive, Huitzilopochtli is born fully grown and armed, painted and equipped with shield, darts, and Xiuhcoatl. He attacks Coyolxauhqui, cuts off her head, and casts her body down the mountainside, where it breaks apart. He then pursues the Centzon Huitznahua around the mountain and kills or scatters them; only a few escape.

Coatlicue herself does not fight, die, or become the colossal decapitated statue in Sahagún’s narration. Modern retellings sometimes say Coyolxauhqui decapitates her; the principal text says that the plan is frustrated. The statue’s decapitation must therefore be explained through another mythic or ritual logic if it represents Coatlicue.

### Political and ritual transformation of Coatepec

**[Regional/authorial variant]** Durán and Tezozómoc’s migration histories shift the episode from divine birth toward political schism. At Coatepec, Coyolxauhqui and her faction wish to stop the migration. Huitzilopochtli orders or performs their destruction. The story explains why the Mexica must continue to the promised destination and casts dissent as a repetition of primordial rebellion.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The Templo Mayor’s Huitzilopochtli side reproduced Coatepec architecturally. The Coyolxauhqui stone lay below its stair, so sacrificial bodies thrown down the steps reenacted her fall. This reading gained extraordinary material support when the stone was discovered in 1978 at the foot of the relevant stairway. It does not prove that every sacrifice reenacted the myth in exactly the same way.

### The aged mother in the ancestral homeland

**[Regional/authorial variant]** In Durán’s account of Motecuhzoma’s expedition to the Mexica homeland, emissaries encounter the ancient mother of Huitzilopochtli. She remains poor, fasting, and waiting, while her son and his people have grown wealthy through conquest. The episode contrasts ancestral austerity with imperial luxury and foretells instability. Later accounts call this mother Coatlicue, but the exact naming and textual recension should be checked before treating it as an independent Coatlicue myth.

### Astronomical reading

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Huitzilopochtli as the rising Sun defeats Coyolxauhqui as Moon and the Centzon Huitznahua as stars. This elegantly explains the repeated cosmic victory and the bodies’ celestial associations. It is interpretation, not a sentence spoken by the *Florentine Codex* narrator. Political and ritual readings—imperial victory, sacrificial reenactment, migration legitimacy—can coexist without requiring a single exclusive meaning.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Coatepec and the Templo Mayor

Coatepec is a mythic mountain located “near Tollan” in Sahagún. Several historical hills have been proposed as its terrestrial referent; no identification commands universal agreement.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction strongly supported by archaeology]** Tenochtitlan’s Templo Mayor recreated sacred geography. Its southern shrine belonged to Huitzilopochtli; the stair and Coyolxauhqui monument materialized her fall from Coatepec. Durán explicitly connects the temple and mountain. The 1978 discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone at the stair’s base transformed this from a mainly textual hypothesis into an archaeologically grounded interpretation.

Early conquest witness Andrés de Tapia described two large stone figures near the summit shrines. Scholars have proposed that these were the great Coatlicue and Yollotlicue sculptures. Because the statues were later displaced and buried, the match remains probable rather than proven.

### Dedicated buildings and offices

Sahagún’s list of the Sacred Precinct includes **Coatlan**, “Place of Serpents,” connected with the Centzon Huitznahua rites. A priestly title *coatecatl* may identify an official of Coatlan or Coatlicue, but the administrative inference is uncertain. No complete institutional history of a separately organized “priesthood of Coatlicue” survives.

### Quecholli

During Quecholli:

- people fashioned arrows and darts;
- participants fasted and performed ear bloodletting;
- miniature arrow-and-torch bundles and tamales were placed on graves;
- a collective hunt occurred;
- many enslaved victims or divine impersonators were killed;
- impersonators of the Centzon Huitznahua were sacrificed at Coatlan.

Mixcoatl is the festival’s principal named deity in the opening account. Guilhem Olivier’s reconstruction interprets female impersonators and the Mixcoatl complex as dramatizing the fertilization of a Chimalman–Coatlicue figure. This is an analytically argued reconstruction, not an explicit ritual rubric saying “this woman is Coatlicue impregnated by Mixcoatl.”

### Panquetzaliztli and sacrificial reenactment

The following *veintena*, Panquetzaliztli, celebrated Huitzilopochtli. Sacrifice, processions, banners, and the casting of victims down the temple stair have been linked to the Coatepec victory. The Coyolxauhqui stone’s position made a victim falling from Huitzilopochtli’s temple land upon or beside the image of his dismembered enemy.

### Human sacrifice: documentation and limits

Human sacrifice in Mexica state ritual is supported by:

- Nahuatl and Spanish colonial descriptions;
- conquest-period eyewitness reports;
- pictorial evidence;
- cut marks and ritual treatment of human remains;
- skull racks and skull towers;
- offerings at the Sacred Precinct.

The Huei Tzompantli excavations at 24 República de Guatemala in **2015–2017** identified part of the great skull-rack complex. One INAH study reported 445 individuals at that stage, mainly young adult males but also females and children. This material evidence confirms large-scale display and ritual processing of human heads. It does not establish that these individuals were sacrificed specifically to Coatlicue; the Huei Tzompantli served the wider state cult.

Likewise, no responsible assessment should repeat Durán’s vast numerical totals as exact audited counts. Archaeology confirms the institution, not every chronicler’s figure.

### Offerings and ordinary worship

The evidence is weakest here. No securely identified cache of household offerings inscribed “to Coatlicue” exists. Copal, food, blood, textiles, feathers, paper, captive bodies, and divine impersonators belonged to the broader Mexica offering system. Assigning an isolated figurine or serpent image to private Coatlicue devotion without inscription or context is speculative.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the Mexica empire

**[Background, not secure named Coatlicue attestation]** Serpent, earth-mouth, birth, death, cave, mountain, and maternal imagery has deep Mesoamerican ancestry. This establishes a visual and religious substrate, not Coatlicue’s personal name or genealogy in earlier civilizations.

### Late Postclassic and imperial Tenochtitlan, c. 1325–1521

The Mexica state elevated Huitzilopochtli’s Coatepec victory into monumental sacred geography. Monumental female sculptures, Coyolxauhqui reliefs, Xiuhcoatl images, sacrificial architecture, and the dual Templo Mayor expressed the interdependence of divine history, warfare, tribute, and royal power.

The great “Coatlicue” probably belongs to the empire’s mature monumental program. Proposed dates such as **1439** or **1491** depend on stylistic and historical reconstruction; the stone itself supplies no year.

### Conquest and suppression, 1519–sixteenth century

Spanish and allied Indigenous forces defeated Tenochtitlan in 1521. Temples were dismantled, divine images destroyed, displaced, reused, or buried, and Christian institutions were established over and around the Sacred Precinct. Missionary projects recorded Nahua religion partly to eradicate it. Durán states that knowledge of old rites was necessary to root them out.

No evidence demonstrates uninterrupted public institutional worship of Coatlicue after the suppression of the temples. Indigenous concepts, language, ritual forms, and sacred landscapes nevertheless persisted and changed within colonial society.

### Sixteenth-century textual preservation

Sahagún’s Indigenous collaborators preserved the fullest Coatlicue narrative in Nahuatl. This is simultaneously an act of documentation and a product of colonial evangelization. Durán and Tezozómoc preserved competing migration-political versions.

### 1790 discovery and colonial antiquarianism

On **13 August 1790**, workers leveling or installing drainage in Mexico City’s Plaza Mayor uncovered the colossal statue, face down. Viceroy Revillagigedo ordered it transferred to the Royal and Pontifical University. The National Museum of Anthropology regards this discovery as foundational to its collections history.

Antonio de León y Gama studied the monument and the Sun Stone in *Descripción histórica y cronológica de las dos piedras* (1792). He called the figure **Teoyaomiqui**, interpreting it through war and sacrificial death. His engravings and analysis made it a documentary object of New Spanish antiquarianism.

A later report by Benito María de Moxó said Indigenous visitors brought incense or otherwise venerated the statue. University authorities then concealed it. Modern accounts commonly say it was “reburied,” but historical reconstruction allows uncertainty over whether it was literally buried again or merely enclosed and hidden. The motive may have included fear of renewed “idolatry,” rejection of its religious presence, aesthetic revulsion, or some combination.

It was uncovered temporarily for Alexander von Humboldt’s examination in **1803–1804**, concealed again, and permanently exposed during the nineteenth century. Chavero’s identification as Coatlicue eventually displaced León y Gama’s Teoyaomiqui.

### National museum era

The statue entered successive national museum displays and finally the National Museum of Anthropology, opened at Chapultepec in **1964**. There it became a masterwork of Mexican antiquity and a canonical image of Mexica religion.

### Archaeological revolution, 1978 onward

The **21 February 1978** discovery of the more-than-three-meter Coyolxauhqui disk triggered the modern Templo Mayor Project. Its context materially linked the Coatepec narrative to Huitzilopochtli’s stair.

The **2 October 2006** discovery of the approximately four-meter, twelve-ton Tlaltecuhtli monolith expanded knowledge of earth-deity imagery. Its surviving pigment, birth posture, claws, blood, and devouring/regenerative symbolism provided a better-documented comparator for interpretations of the “Coatlicue.”

The **2015–2017** Huei Tzompantli excavations strengthened the archaeological basis for accepting human sacrifice and bodily display while making deity-specific attribution more exacting.

### Present

Coatlicue now exists simultaneously as:

- a figure reconstructed from colonial Nahuatl narrative;
- the conventional name of a disputed monumental sculpture;
- a museum and national icon;
- a Chicana feminist and decolonial symbol;
- an object of modern Indigenous and neopagan devotion;
- a commercial image.

Present devotion is real as present practice, but documentary evidence does not establish an unbroken institutional lineage from the Templo Mayor cult to modern neopagan worship.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Mexican art and national culture

Saturnino Herrán’s unfinished **Nuestros dioses: Coatlicue transformada** (designed 1914–1915) fused the great Coatlicue with a Christian crucifix and marigold garlands. Indigenous and Spanish worshippers flank the composite. The work made Coatlicue a vehicle for early twentieth-century theories of mestizaje.

Diego Rivera repeatedly used the statue’s bilateral, mechanical anatomy:

- in the **Detroit Industry** cycle (1932–1933), machinery and Mesoamerican form merge;
- in **Pan American Unity** (1940), the central southern figure combines Coatlicue with a Ford stamping machine.

These are neither restorations of Mexica belief nor neutral illustrations; they are modern industrial, political, and hemispheric transformations.

José Clemente Orozco and other muralists used Mexica divine and sacrificial imagery in critiques of nationalism, institutions, conquest, and modernity. Individual Coatlicue identifications should be tied to titled works rather than inferred from every skeletal or pre-Hispanic figure.

Gabriel Orozco’s *Anima* superimposes or conceptually relates Leonardo’s *Vitruvian Man* to Coatlicue, continuing the confrontation between European ideals of proportion and the monument once excluded from European aesthetic canons.

### Octavio Paz

Paz treated the statue’s changing status—from goddess to demonized idol, to monster, to masterpiece—as evidence of changing historical sensibility. His criticism helped establish Coatlicue as a philosophical work embodying unity through contradiction. This is modern aesthetic interpretation, not recovered priestly exegesis.

### Gloria Anzaldúa and “the Coatlicue state”

In *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (**1987**), Gloria Anzaldúa developed **“the Coatlicue state”**: a painful psychic and epistemic condition of rupture, paralysis, descent, contradiction, and transformation. She linked Coatlicue, Tonantzin, Guadalupe, serpent imagery, queer experience, and mestiza consciousness.

This has been enormously influential in Chicana feminism, queer theory, literary studies, and decolonial thought. It is a deliberate modern interpretive tradition, not a translation of a Mexica technical term.

### Guadalupe and Tonantzin

Sahagún complained that Indigenous people called the Virgin at Tepeyac **Tonantzin**, the old title “our mother.” The site and vocabulary make continuity, substitution, and entanglement serious historical questions.

However:

- Tonantzin is not securely Coatlicue’s exclusive name;
- the Guadalupe cult also has demonstrable Spanish, clerical, Marian, urban, and creole histories;
- William B. Taylor found early Guadalupe devotion more clerically and non-Indigenously mediated than a simple survival model implies;
- therefore “Guadalupe is Coatlicue in disguise” is **[modern simplification]**, not an established one-to-one historical succession.

The continuing artistic juxtaposition of Coatlicue and Guadalupe is itself well documented, beginning prominently with Herrán and continuing in Chicana art and literature.

### Occultism, goddess spirituality, and neopaganism

Unlike Egyptian, Greek, or Near Eastern gods, Coatlicue did not acquire a substantial documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, nineteenth-century ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, or classical Thelema. Modern occult pages that give her fixed planetary correspondences, crystals, tarot cards, “ancient invocations,” or chakra assignments generally create them from modern correspondence systems.

Contemporary practitioners do worship or invoke Coatlicue in Mexican, Chicano, Indigenous-revival, neopagan, witchcraft, and Goddess-spirituality settings. Public online testimony confirms these practices, but their organization, scale, and claimed lineages vary. Unless a community documents transmission from a specific Indigenous ceremonial lineage, these should be described as **modern revival or reinvention**, not continuous Aztec priesthood.

### Commercial and popular culture

The colossal statue appears in posters, jewelry, tattoos, fashion, tourism, museum merchandise, album art, fantasy games, and online “Aztec goddess” catalogues. These uses usually privilege the statue’s dramatic visual surface. Modern images frequently add a human face, living snake hair, pregnancy, lunar symbols, or European “Mother Goddess” traits absent from the monument.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The central methodological problem

The dossier’s greatest problem is that **the most famous text and the most famous sculpture do not identify each other**. Sahagún names Coatlicue but does not describe the colossal statue. The statue displays a serpent skirt but does not name itself. Scholarship bridges that gap.

### León y Gama and Chavero

León y Gama’s 1792 study was foundational but called the statue Teoyaomiqui. In the later nineteenth century Alfredo Chavero argued for Coatlicue. Museum practice transformed Chavero’s proposal into an apparently self-evident label.

### Earth Mother synthesis

Twentieth-century syntheses, including work by Miguel León-Portilla, Henry B. Nicholson, Esther Pasztory, Eduardo Matos Moctezuma, Elizabeth Hill Boone, Mary Miller, Karl Taube, Michel Graulich, Richard Townsend, Davíd Carrasco, and Alfredo López Austin, located Coatlicue within Nahua conceptions of earth, sacrifice, sacred mountains, calendrical ritual, and divine transformation.

Nicholson’s 1971 deity typology was valuable because Nahua gods rarely form a clean family of bounded Greco-Roman-style individuals. Complexes of costume, name, calendar, locality, and function overlap. Its danger lies in turning analytical complexes into supposed native equations.

### Cecelia Klein’s challenge

Klein’s “A New Interpretation of the Aztec Statue Called Coatlicue, ‘Snakes-Her-Skirt’” emphasized:

- the existence of multiple similar colossal female sculptures;
- the statue’s decapitated condition;
- inconsistencies between it and Sahagún’s birth narrative, where Coatlicue is not killed;
- traditions of divine women sacrificed to activate the Sun;
- the political meaning of monumental female defeat.

Her argument does not make “Coatlicue” unusable as a museum title, but it prevents the identification from being called certain.

### Gender and political readings

Some scholarship reads the imagery as patriarchal containment of dangerous female power: Coyolxauhqui’s rebellion and dismemberment validate masculine solar/military order. Other scholars stress that female earth beings were not merely victims; they generated, consumed, judged, and regenerated existence. Both positions depend on contextual analysis rather than literal theological definitions.

### Sacrifice debate

Two extremes are untenable:

- colonial accounts cannot all be accepted uncritically, especially their immense totals and Christian demonizing rhetoric;
- the sacrificial institution cannot be dismissed as a wholesale Spanish fabrication.

Architecture, cut-marked remains, skull racks, towers, offerings, and patterned placement confirm killing and postmortem ritual treatment. The historical question is scale, selection, meaning, chronology, and attribution—not whether sacrifice existed at all.

### Evidence state and archives

Important materials survive in:

- the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence: *Florentine Codex*;
- Madrid repositories: components of the *Primeros Memoriales*;
- Bibliothèque de l’Assemblée nationale, Paris: *Codex Borbonicus*;
- Vatican Library: *Codex Borgia* and *Vaticanus A/Ríos*;
- Bodleian Library, Oxford: *Codex Mendoza*;
- Bibliothèque nationale de France: *Telleriano-Remensis*;
- National Museum of Anthropology, Mexico City: the colossal “Coatlicue,” Sun Stone, and related sculptures;
- Museo del Templo Mayor: Coyolxauhqui, Tlaltecuhtli, offerings, and excavation archives.

The destruction of the Templo Mayor, removal of cult images, loss of codices, missionary filtering, and displacement of sculpture have left the record structurally incomplete.

### Claims not established

- that the colossal statue’s original name was certainly Coatlicue;
- that Coatlicue was universally considered the single supreme mother of every god;
- that Mixcoatl is securely named as Huitzilopochtli’s father in the feather-ball narrative;
- that Coatlicue was herself murdered in Sahagún’s account;
- that Guadalupe is a straightforward Christian repainting of Coatlicue;
- that a continuous Coatlicue priesthood survived from 1521 to modern neopaganism;
- that modern planetary, crystal, tarot, chakra, or fixed-color correspondences are ancient;
- that every serpent-skirted or clawed female image is Coatlicue;
- that the Sun Stone depicts Coatlicue. Its central identity is separately debated and should not be solved by association.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the documentary record |
|---|---|
| Before c. 1200 | Deep Mesoamerican traditions of serpents, earth mouths, caves, mountains, birth, sacrifice, and devouring exist; no secure named Coatlicue attestation. |
| c. 1325–1521 | Mexica Tenochtitlan develops; Coatepec ideology becomes integrated with Huitzilopochtli’s state cult. |
| Fifteenth–early sixteenth century | Colossal serpent-skirted female sculptures are produced. Their identification as Coatlicue is conventional but disputed. |
| c. 1480s | Monumental phases of the Templo Mayor strongly materialize the Coatepec–Coyolxauhqui program. |
| 1519–1521 | Conquest witnesses see the Sacred Precinct; temples and images are subsequently destroyed, displaced, or buried. |
| 1558 | The *Leyenda de los Soles* manuscript is copied; it preserves related cosmogonic traditions but not the full named Coatlicue story. |
| 1559–1561 | Sahagún and Indigenous collaborators compile the *Primeros Memoriales*. |
| 1570s–1581 | Durán composes his ritual and historical works, preserving migration and Coatepec variants. |
| c. 1577 | The surviving *Florentine Codex* is completed; Book III gives the fullest named account of Coatlicue and Huitzilopochtli’s birth. |
| Late sixteenth century | Tezozómoc’s histories preserve variant genealogies in which Coyolxauhqui may occupy the maternal role. |
| 13 August 1790 | The colossal statue is discovered face down in Mexico City’s Plaza Mayor and transferred to the Royal and Pontifical University. |
| 1792 | León y Gama publishes *Descripción histórica y cronológica de las dos piedras*, identifying the figure as Teoyaomiqui. |
| c. 1790s–early 1800s | The statue is concealed; Moxó later reports Indigenous incense offerings. Literal reburial versus enclosure remains uncertain. |
| 1803–1804 | The statue is exposed for Alexander von Humboldt’s examination and subsequently concealed again. |
| Nineteenth century | It enters permanent antiquarian and museum display; Chavero’s Coatlicue identification becomes influential. |
| 1914–1915 | Saturnino Herrán designs *Nuestros dioses: Coatlicue transformada*, merging Coatlicue and the Crucifix. |
| 1932–1933 | Rivera adapts Coatlicue’s form in *Detroit Industry*. |
| 1940 | Rivera combines Coatlicue and industrial machinery in *Pan American Unity*. |
| 1964 | The National Museum of Anthropology opens at Chapultepec; Coatlicue becomes a central masterpiece of the Mexica gallery. |
| September 1967 | A smaller “Metro Coatlicue” is found during subway construction; Doris Heyden publishes it in 1971. |
| 1971 | Nicholson publishes his influential typology of central Mexican religious complexes; Heyden discusses the Metro sculpture. |
| 21 February 1978 | Workers discover the monumental Coyolxauhqui disk, prompting the Templo Mayor Project. |
| 1987 | Gloria Anzaldúa publishes *Borderlands/La Frontera* and formulates the “Coatlicue state.” |
| 1998/2008 | Klein’s revisionist interpretation of the colossal statues challenges their automatic identification as Coatlicue. |
| 2 October 2006 | The monumental Tlaltecuhtli is discovered at the Ajaracas property beside the Templo Mayor. |
| 2015–2017 | Excavations identify remains of the Huei Tzompantli and skull tower, materially strengthening the record of state sacrifice. |
| 2023 | The multilingual Digital Florentine Codex makes the manuscript, transcriptions, and new translations broadly accessible. |
| Present | Coatlicue remains a museum icon, research problem, artistic symbol, feminist/decolonial figure, and object of revived devotion. |
