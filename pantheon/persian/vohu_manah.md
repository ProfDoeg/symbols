# Vohu Manah: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the Gāthās, an ethically charged faculty or condition—“Good Mind,” “Good Thought,” or “Good Purpose”—whose degree of personhood is disputed; in the Younger Avesta and later Zoroastrian tradition, a worship-worthy divine being (*yazata*) and the foremost of the six created Aməša Spəntas (“Beneficent Immortals”), conventionally masculine despite the grammatical neuter gender of the Avestan name.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Old Avestan Gāthās embedded in *Yasna* 28–34, 43–51, and 53. Their exact absolute date is disputed—proposals range broadly within the late second and early first millennia BCE—but their language and poetics securely predate the Achaemenid inscriptions. The earliest physical manuscripts containing these traditions are medieval, not ancient; the oldest securely dated Avestan/Pahlavi Yasna manuscripts belong to 1323 CE. Thus the earliest *composition layer* and earliest *surviving material witness* must not be conflated.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early—secure]** Old Avestan material in the Gāthās or *Yasna Haptaŋhāiti*.
- **[Younger Avestan—canonical]** Post-Gāthic Avestan liturgy, law, and myth, composed and transmitted orally over centuries before its late-antique written redaction.
- **[Pahlavi systematization]** Middle Persian doctrine and legend, substantially Sasanian in inheritance but preserved chiefly in ninth- to eleventh-century CE compilations and later manuscripts.
- **[Regional/syncretistic]** Central Asian, Kushan, Manichaean, or other adaptations not automatically identical with normative Zoroastrian doctrine.
- **[Medieval accretion/folklore]** Post-conquest Persian and Parsi tradition.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A conclusion proposed by modern scholars from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** A demonstrably recent attribution, image, equivalence, or fictional treatment.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[Early—secure]** Old Avestan *vohu manah-* combines *vohu-* “good” with *manah-* “mind, thought, intention, purpose.” The expression describes the morally sound disposition through which a person recognizes and performs what accords with *aša*—truth, right order, or correctness. It is simultaneously something Ahura Mazdā possesses or gives, something humans cultivate, and, in passages with verbs or relations suggestive of agency, an entity capable of standing beside Ahura Mazdā and other divine powers.

The ambiguity is structural, not an imperfection in the evidence. Johanna Narten summarizes the major positions: Mary Boyce and Helmut Humbach understood a synergy of abstraction and personification; H. S. Nyberg and Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin treated Vohu Manah as a fully personal being; Stanley Insler emphasized an abstract idea. The Gāthās do not supply a systematic ontology resolving the question.

**[Younger Avestan—canonical]** In later Avestan texts Vohu Manah is unmistakably a named divine being, invoked among the Aməša Spəntas and ranked immediately after Ahura Mazdā whenever the group is individually enumerated. This ranking differs subtly from the Gāthās, where Aša often appears conceptually closest to Ahura Mazdā.

**[Pahlavi systematization]** Middle Persian tradition makes Wahman the first of the six Aməšāspands, protector and spiritual counterpart of beneficent livestock, helper in creation, recorder of human conduct, guide of righteous souls, adversary of Akōman, and privileged intermediary between Ohrmazd and Zarathustra.

### Documented forms

- **Avestan:** 𐬬𐬊𐬵𐬎 𐬨𐬀𐬥𐬀𐬵, *vohu manah-*; inflected forms include *vaŋhə̄uš manaŋhō*. “Good Mind,” “Good Thought,” “Good Purpose,” or “good intention.”
- **Avestan superlative:** *vahišta manah-*—“Best Mind/Thought.” Whether every occurrence names the being is context-dependent.
- **Middle Persian:** 𐭥𐭤𐭥𐭬𐭭, conventionally transliterated *Wahman*, *Vahman*, or *Vohuman*.
- **New Persian:** بهمن, *Bahman*.
- **Bactrian in Greek script:** ΜΑΝΑΟΒΑΓΟ, *Manaobago*. Its identification with Vohu Manah is plausible but disputed.
- **Manichaean Middle Iranian:** *Wahman* or “Great Wahman” could render the Manichaean “Great Nous/Mind”; this is a religious translation or appropriation, not evidence that the Manichaean being was simply the Zoroastrian Aməša Spənta.

“Vohu Mainyu,” “Good Spirit,” and “Vahišta Mainyu,” “Best Spirit,” occur in some interpretive traditions but should not automatically be treated as original proper-name variants. Nor should *Aṣ̌əm Vohū*, the opening words of a central Zoroastrian prayer, be translated as a prayer addressed to Vohu Manah: there *vohū* modifies *aṣ̌əm*, approximately “Truth/Rightness is the best good.”

### Etymological dispute

**[Secure linguistic comparison]** Avestan *manah-* is cognate with Vedic Sanskrit *mánas-* “mind,” and *vohu-* with Vedic *vásu-* “good, excellent.” This establishes inherited Indo-Iranian vocabulary.

**[Modern reconstruction—disputed]** It does not establish an inherited Indo-Iranian deity named “Good Mind.” Narten observes that the Vedic compound required for a direct counterpart is absent and rejects comparison with Vedic *sumati-* as etymologically probative. Lommel therefore considered the specific ethical-divine formation probably Zoroastrian rather than inherited intact from Proto-Indo-Iranian religion.

### Class and gender

The Avestan compound is grammatically neuter, as are the names Aša Vahišta and Xšaθra Vairya. Later texts and English-language convention commonly use masculine pronouns. “Archangel” is frequent in older English scholarship and Zoroastrian devotional writing, but it is a comparative translation, not an indigenous Avestan classification. “Emanation” is similarly useful only if qualified: the tradition also treats each Aməša Spənta as a distinct *yazata*, worthy of worship.

### Historical equations

- **Manaobago:** **[Regional/syncretistic; disputed]** A deity labeled ΜΑΝΑΟΒΑΓΟ appears on Kushan gold coins, especially under Huvishka, second century CE. The second element is Bactrian *bago* “god”; the first has been connected with *manah*. The seated, haloed figure has lunar crescents and royal/prosperity attributes. Numismatists warn that this is among the hardest Kushan divinities to identify. “Manaobago = Vohu Manah” is therefore a strong proposal, not certainty.
- **Manichaean Great Nous:** **[Syncretistic]** Middle Iranian Manichaean vocabulary uses *Wahman* for the “Great Mind/Nous.” Mani’s system reorganized Iranian, Christian, Mesopotamian, and Buddhist terminology; it did not simply preserve the Zoroastrian pantheon.
- **Gabriel:** **[Late comparative identification/modern popular claim]** Analogies between Vohu Manah as revealer-guide and Gabriel as an angel of revelation occur in comparative and devotional writing. No Avestan or Pahlavi source identifies the two, and there is no continuous ancient equation.
- **Mercury, Hermes, Logos, Buddha, or Maitreya:** **[Modern speculation unless tied to a particular text]** No secure ancient interpretatio identifies Vohu Manah with these figures.
- **Mithraic identification:** No securely attested Roman Mithraic inscription or image names Vohu Manah. Broad claims that a figure in a Mithraeum “is Vohu Manah” remain unsupported.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Gāthās

**[Early—secure]** The oldest evidence is literary and liturgical rather than archaeological. Relevant passages include:

- *Yasna* 28.1–4: the poet seeks aid so that he may satisfy Vohu Manah and the Cow’s Soul, approaches Mazdā through Good Mind, and declares that he will safeguard the soul in association with Good Mind.
- 28.5–7: knowledge or vision of Ahura Mazdā is sought through Aša and Good Mind.
- 29: the Cow’s Soul laments oppression; the divine consultation searches for a protector. Vohu Manah belongs to the conceptual network connecting righteous pastoral care, revelation, and social order.
- 30.4: the paired moral alternatives are associated with the best and worst mind.
- 31.6, 31.8, 31.10: humans increase desirable dominion through Good Mind; Ahura Mazdā is understood as its father or creator; the good herdsman is Vohu Manah’s supporter.
- 32.2, 32.13, 32.15: the divine community answers through Good Mind; hell is associated with Worst Mind, while paradise is the “House/Abode of Good Mind.”
- 33.3: care for the truthful and for cattle leads to the “pasture/meadow of Aša and Good Mind.”
- 34.12–14: the “paths of Good Mind” and the community of the milch cow.
- 43: visionary encounter with Mazdā and his powers; later tradition expands this into the meeting with a visibly anthropomorphic Wahman.
- 44.4, 44.8, 44.13: Mazdā is questioned as creator, and Good Mind functions as divine counsel.
- 45.4: Ahura Mazdā is called father of Good Mind.
- 46.7: Mazdā’s Fire and Thought are possible protectors of the prophet.
- 47.1–3: the divine powers are brought together; Good Mind is an avenue of beneficent action.
- 49.3, 49.5, 49.10: partnership with Good Mind and the souls of the truthful in Mazdā’s dwelling.
- 50.1: Aša, Mazdā, and Best Mind are asked to protect the poet and his cattle.
- 51.16: the path or insight associated with Good Mind.

These passages prove the antiquity of the concept and its intimate relationship to Ahura Mazdā, moral cognition, pastoral responsibility, and salvation. They do **not** securely prove the complete later doctrine that Vohu Manah is a male, winged archangel, first-created member of a fixed celestial hierarchy, or exclusive guardian of all animals.

### *Yasna Haptaŋhāiti* and later Yasna

**[Early/transition]** *Yasna* 39.3 contains the oldest occurrence of the collective expression later translated “Beneficent Immortals,” with its elements reversed. The *Yasna Haptaŋhāiti* is linguistically Old Avestan but distinct from the Gāthic hymns.

**[Younger Avestan—canonical]** The later *Yasna* repeatedly invokes Vohu Manah in the fixed lists of the Aməša Spəntas. These liturgical enumerations securely attest worship but usually add little narrative characterization.

### Yašts

- *Yasht* 13.77: when Angra Mainyu attacks Aša’s creation, Vohu Manah and Fire intervene.
- *Yasht* 13.81: the Aməša Spəntas are forms in which the Creator “mingles” himself.
- *Yasht* 19.16–18: the six, together with Spənta Mainyu, are of one mind, voice, and action and have one father and ruler, Ahura Mazdā.
- *Yasht* 19.96: Aka Manah is defeated and Vohu Manah victorious in the final triumph.

**Absence:** No extant Avestan Yašt is dedicated to Vohu Manah. The supposed “Bahman Yašt” is not part of the surviving Avestan corpus.

### *Vendidad*

**[Younger Avestan—canonical]** *Vendidad* 19.31 presents the most anthropomorphic Avestan scene: after the righteous soul’s passage, Good Mind rises from a golden throne and welcomes it. Elsewhere ritual pollution can be described as directly or indirectly defiling Vohu Manah, sometimes through an exegetical association between mind, person, clothing, and purity.

### Pahlavi corpus

**[Pahlavi systematization]**

- **Bundahišn:** Ohrmazd produces the Aməšāspands from his own selfhood; Wahman guards cattle and the primordial bovine creation. The work is a composite Middle Persian cosmology drawing on lost Avestan materials, substantially compiled after the Islamic conquest.
- **Dēnkard**, especially books 5 and 7: Wahman participates in Zarathustra’s prehistory, carries the sacred hōm associated with formation of his body, dwells within the laughing newborn, meets the adult prophet, escorts him to Ohrmazd, helps rescue him in infancy, and later visits Wištāsp. Book 7 depends partly on traditions associated with the lost *Spand Nask*.
- **Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram:** develops Zarathustra’s first vision, Wahman’s guidance, the nourishment of the endangered infant, the five kinds of cattle, and Tištar’s appeal to Wahman as a guide in producing rain.
- **Dādestān ī dēnīg:** Wahman embodies goodness and thought, differs from Srōš as speech and Ard/Aša as action, and counts every person’s thoughts, words, and deeds three times daily.
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad 8.2:** Ohrmazd creates the Aməšāspands from his own light.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag 11:** Wahman receives or accompanies the righteous soul at the final stage of ascent into Ohrmazd’s presence.
- **Šāyest nē Šāyest**, supplement 15.9–11: pleasing and properly caring for beneficent cattle pleases Wahman, because they are his earthly counterpart.
- **Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg:** the production of the Aməšāspands is compared to lighting one torch from another.
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn:** despite its name, Wahman is not an actor in the surviving apocalypse. Its title and relationship to a hypothetical lost Avestan text are disputed.

The Pahlavi books survive in manuscripts much later than the traditions they claim to transmit. Their contents may preserve Sasanian exegesis and older oral or lost written material, but each particular claim must first be dated to its extant Middle Persian witness.

### Royal inscriptions and Classical authors

**[Secure negative evidence]** Vohu Manah is not named in the extant Old Persian Achaemenid royal inscriptions at Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam, Persepolis, Susa, or elsewhere. Darius and Xerxes name Auramazdā; later inscriptions also name Mithra and Anāhitā. Attempts to interpret six nobles around Darius as the six Aməša Spəntas are modern iconographic hypotheses, not inscriptions identifying them.

Herodotus describes Persian sacrifice and the Magi but does not name Vohu Manah. Plutarch’s *Isis and Osiris* 46–47 preserves a Greek account of Iranian dualism and names Oromazes and hostile or beneficent powers, but supplies no secure Greek equivalent for Vohu Manah. Their silence cannot disprove worship, but it prevents using them as direct attestations.

### Ferdowsi

**[Medieval literary distinction]** In Ferdowsi’s *Shāhnāma* (completed ca. 1010 CE), Bahman is principally the personal name of a Kayanian hero and king, son of Esfandiyār. That character is not Vohu Manah. The shared name reflects the survival of *Bahman* in Iranian onomastics, not a myth in which the Aməša Spənta becomes a human monarch.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Good Mind and moral agency

**[Early—secure]** Vohu Manah is the rightly oriented cognitive and volitional state enabling recognition of Aša and good action. “Mind” alone is too intellectual; “purpose,” “intention,” and “disposition” capture its ethical and active force. It advises, mediates divine understanding, enables partnership with Mazdā, and marks the blessed state after death.

### Cattle and animals

**[Early—disputed extent]** The Gāthās repeatedly connect Good Mind, the Cow’s Soul, pastoral protection, and the righteous herdsman. Boyce, Lommel, and Cameron saw in these allusions the already established pairing of Vohu Manah and cattle. Narten objected that no Gāthic passage unambiguously appoints the personified being as the exclusive guardian of animals. The safest reconstruction is that a semantic and theological association is early, while the fully systematized guardianship is later.

**[Pahlavi systematization—secure]** Wahman is explicitly guardian of cattle, counterpart of the five species of beneficent animals, and protector of the primordial bovine creation. “Cattle” can encompass domesticated livestock more broadly than modern bovines. Later ritual ethics made feeding, sheltering, and avoiding cruelty to livestock acts of devotion to Wahman.

### Afterlife

**[Early—secure]** The “House of Good Mind” is an expression for the blessed destination in the Gāthās.

**[Younger Avestan/Pahlavi elaboration]** Vohu Manah rises from a golden throne to welcome the righteous soul (*Vendidad* 19.31); *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag* restricts his guiding role to the final approach to Ohrmazd’s dwelling.

### Creation and eschatology

**[Younger Avestan/Pahlavi]** Vohu Manah assists Spənta Mainyu and the other beneficent beings against evil, guards a sector of creation, and will defeat Aka Manah. At the Renovation, the *Dēnkard* says Wahman will be consulted concerning everything.

### Appearance and emblems

No undisputed Zoroastrian cult image of Vohu Manah survives. Avestan and Pahlavi texts do not give him a stable body, face, weapon, animal mount, crown, or color. “White-robed angel,” “winged archangel,” and standardized modern portraits are devotional or artistic inventions.

The golden throne in *Vendidad* 19 is a narrative attribute, not proof of a standard iconographic type.

### Manaobago coins

**[Regional/syncretistic; disputed]** Kushan coins furnish the only substantial ancient image plausibly connected with the name. Manaobago is seated frontally or near-frontally, haloed, richly dressed, sometimes with lunar crescents at the shoulders and a royal or scepter-like object. Because Bactrian *manaobago* may mean a god of mind or a compound involving the Moon (*Mao*), the identification and attributes remain disputed. These coins should not be reproduced as “the definitive image of Vohu Manah.”

### Calendar, plants, colors, and numbers

- Second day of every thirty-day Zoroastrian month: Vohu Manah/Bahman.
- Eleventh month: Bahman.
- Days 2, 12 (Māh), 14 (Gōš), and 21 (Rām) became associated through Vohu Manah and his fellow-workers connected with animal creation.
- The number “two” is calendrically associated, not a securely attested mystical number.
- Modern lists assign white, jasmine, or particular gems to Bahman. Such assignments require a named late source; they are not established by the Gāthās or the principal Pahlavi passages.
- No celestial body belongs securely to Vohu Manah in mainstream Zoroastrian doctrine. Manaobago’s crescents are regional numismatic evidence, not a universal lunar identity.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Origin and hierarchy

**[Early—secure but metaphorical]** Ahura Mazdā is Vohu Manah’s “father” in *Yasna* 31.8 and 45.4 and creator in 44.4. The Gāthic language need not imply biological generation.

**[Younger Avestan—canonical]** The six Aməša Spəntas have one father and ruler, Ahura Mazdā, and act with one thought, word, and deed.

**[Pahlavi systematization]** Ohrmazd creates them from his selfhood (*Bundahišn*) or light (*Mēnōg ī Xrad*). These are alternate explanatory metaphors, not separate biological genealogies.

Vohu Manah has no securely attested spouse or offspring. The other Aməša Spəntas are colleagues sometimes loosely called siblings in modern summaries, but the primary sources more often describe common creation and coordinated function.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Ahura Mazdā/Ohrmazd — father, creator, possessor, and divine interlocutor; Vohu Manah advises, mediates access, and resides with him.** *Yasna* 28.2–3; 31.8; 32.2; 44.4, 8, 13; 45.4; 49.10; *Vendidad* 19; *Bundahišn* 1; *Dēnkard* 7. **[Early core; later narrative elaboration]**
- **Aša/Aša Vahišta/Ardwahišt — repeatedly invoked with Vohu Manah; together they define truthful thought and the blessed pasture and defend Aša’s creation.** *Yasna* 28.3; 33.3; 50.1; *Yasht* 13.77. **[Early and Younger Avestan]**
- **Xšaθra Vairya/Šahrewar — joint member of the divine group and participant in desirable dominion.** *Yasna* 28.3; 31.6; 47.1; later lists. No independent narrative encounter is recorded. **[Early joint attestation]**
- **Spənta Ārmaiti/Spandarmad — joint divine action; Ārmaiti increases dominion for those praised with Vohu Manah.** *Yasna* 28.3, 7; 47.1. **[Early]**
- **Haurvatāt/Hordād — joint member of the sevenfold beneficent order.** *Yasna* 47.1 and later enumerations. No discrete two-party myth survives. **[Early collective attestation]**
- **Amərətāt/Amurdād — joint member of the same order.** *Yasna* 47.1 and later enumerations. No discrete two-party myth survives. **[Early collective attestation]**
- **Spənta Mainyu — Vohu Manah, Aša, and Fire support the beneficent side in the contest for glory/light.** *Yasht* 19; collective creation texts. **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Ātar/Fire — together defend the creation when Angra Mainyu attacks; both may serve as protectors of Zarathustra.** *Yasna* 46.7; *Yasht* 13.77. **[Early allusion; Younger Avestan episode]**
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — attacks the beneficent creation that Vohu Manah and Fire defend.** *Yasht* 13.77; later cosmologies. **[Younger Avestan; Pahlavi elaboration]**
- **Aka Manah/Akōman — ethical and divine opposite; ultimately defeated by Vohu Manah.** *Yasna* 30, 32, 47; *Yasht* 19.96; *Dēnkard* 7; *Zādspram*. **[Early conceptual opposition; later personalized combat]**
- **Geuš Urvan, the Cow’s Soul — Vohu Manah belongs to the divine consultation about the oppressed cow and to the complex of her protection.** *Yasna* 28–29. **[Early; precise role disputed]**
- **Geuš Tašan, the Fashioner of the Cow — participates in the consultation concerning the Cow’s protector alongside Mazdā’s powers.** *Yasna* 29. **[Early collective scene]**
- **Sraoša/Srōš — accompanies Wahman in bringing a ewe to nourish the infant Zarathustra; later shares the accounting and judgment of souls.** *Zādspram* 10.11; *Dēnkard* 7.3.17; *Dādestān ī dēnīg* 13. **[Pahlavi legend/systematization]**
- **Miθra/Mihr — shares the accounting of deeds and judgment functions but has no independent surviving mythic encounter with Vohu Manah.** *Dādestān ī dēnīg* 13. **[Pahlavi systematization]**
- **Rašnu/Rašn — shares judgment/accounting duties; no discrete narrative encounter survives.** *Dādestān ī dēnīg* 13. **[Pahlavi systematization]**
- **Haoma/Hōm — Wahman carries the sacred hōm involved in forming Zarathustra’s body; later ritual texts associate Hōm with Wahman and other protectors of animals.** *Dēnkard* 7.2.17–25; Persian Rivāyats. **[Pahlavi legend; later ritual association]**
- **Tištrya/Tištar — summons Wahman as a guide or intermediary so rain may fall.** *Zādspram* 3.8. **[Pahlavi elaboration]**
- **Māh, Gōš, and Rām — later calendrical and ritual “fellow-workers” of Wahman in care of animals; their days become meat-abstinence days.** Pahlavi and later Zoroastrian practice. **[Later canonical/ritual systematization]**
- **Daēnā — Vohu Manah is adjacent to the soul’s postmortem journey in which Daēnā appears, but no securely independent two-party encounter is described.** *Vendidad* 19 and Pahlavi afterlife texts. **[Canonical association; no discrete interaction]**

No source records Vohu Manah in a marriage, sexual episode, theft, or conflict with another beneficent deity.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Zarathustra’s access to revelation

**[Early core]** In the Gāthās, Zarathustra seeks to see or know Ahura Mazdā through Aša and Good Mind. *Yasna* 43 presents a visionary recognition of the Wise Lord and divine powers but does not narrate a riverbank meeting with a tall anthropomorphic angel.

**[Pahlavi elaboration]** *Dēnkard* 7 and *Zādspram* 21 provide the familiar full story. At age thirty Zarathustra goes for water associated with a ritual observance. Returning from the water, he sees a radiant being identified as Wahman, whose stature and brilliance exceed those of ordinary humanity. Wahman questions him and leads him into the assembly of Ohrmazd and the other Aməšāspands. There Zarathustra receives revelation and subsequently undergoes further conferences.

The river, age, bodily dimensions, and formal escort are therefore later narrative specification, probably drawing on lost Avestan and Sasanian traditions but securely documented only in medieval Middle Persian recensions.

### Wahman and Zarathustra’s miraculous infancy

**[Pahlavi legend]** Evil sorcerers or demonic agents attempt to kill the infant through fire, trampling animals, wolves, and other dangers. In one episode Wahman and Srōš bring a ewe to suckle him. Wahman’s presence within the newborn makes him laugh at birth. Akōman’s attempts to enter or harm him fail before the beneficent power of Wahman.

These stories belong to the Pahlavi Zarathustra legend, especially *Dēnkard* 5 and 7 and *Zādspram*. They are not narrated in the Gāthās.

### Formation of Zarathustra’s body

**[Pahlavi systematization]** In *Dēnkard* 7, the components required for Zarathustra’s miraculous conception have different divine histories. Wahman carries or conveys sacred hōm that contributes to the prophet’s corporeal formation. This integrates Vohu Manah’s roles in animal life, revelation, and divine planning.

### Conversion of Wištāsp

**[Pahlavi elaboration]** After resistance to Zarathustra’s teaching, Ohrmazd sends divine beings to King Wištāsp. Wahman functions as messenger and guide who dispels doubt. The extant accounts vary in which Aməšāspands appear and how coercive or miraculous the demonstration is. The Gāthās know Vištāspa as a patron but do not tell this developed conversion narrative.

### Reception of the righteous soul

**[Younger Avestan—canonical]** In *Vendidad* 19.31, Vohu Manah rises from a golden throne and greets the righteous dead. The passage translates the Gāthic “House of Good Mind” into a celestial court scene.

**[Pahlavi elaboration]** In *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*, Wahman accompanies the soul only at the highest stage, into Ohrmazd’s presence. Other beings govern earlier judgment stages.

### Cosmic opposition to Aka Manah

**[Early core]** Good and bad mind identify opposed moral orientations.

**[Younger Avestan/Pahlavi personification]** Vohu Manah and Aka Manah become paired supernatural opponents. *Yasht* 19.96 declares the victory of Good Mind; Pahlavi legend dramatizes Akōman’s interventions against Zarathustra.

### The so-called Bahman Yašt

**[Contested textual tradition]** The *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* is a Middle Persian apocalypse describing successive ages of decline, invasions, and final restoration through symbolic visions. Wahman does not participate in its surviving narrative. West interpreted it as an epitome or commentary on a lost Avestan Wahman text; Boyce and Widengren proposed older Avestan, Hellenistic, or Parthian layers. Gignoux and Hultgård emphasized that the title “Bahman Yašt” is modern, the work is not a Yašt, and no solid basis establishes an original hymn to Vohu Manah. Its title belongs in Vohu Manah’s reception history, but its apocalypse is not one of his myths.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Worship and liturgy

**[Younger Avestan—secure]** Vohu Manah is invoked in the Yasna with the other Aməša Spəntas and receives *yasna*, praise, and offerings as a *yazata*. His cult was embedded in the general Zoroastrian liturgy rather than organized around a surviving independent hymn.

There is no extant Vohu Manah Yašt, no securely identified priesthood exclusive to him, and no named ancient temple conclusively dedicated solely to him.

### Animal care

**[Pahlavi and living tradition]** Caring for livestock is his principal practical cult. *Šāyest nē Šāyest* requires warmth, sufficient winter fodder, protection of mothers and young, and avoidance of deprivation. These prescriptions make husbandry an act of worship rather than merely an economic concern.

### Sacrifice and abstinence

Animal sacrifice existed in Iranian and Zoroastrian ritual history; cattle, sheep, and goats could be designated by formulas treating them as beneficent or holy livestock. Vohu Manah’s guardianship did not create a universal vegetarian prohibition.

Later custom avoided blood sacrifice and meat on Bahman day and on the days of Māh, Gōš, and Rām. The especially devout abstained throughout Bahman month. Reformist communities subsequently abandoned much older animal-sacrificial and bull-urine practices.

**Human sacrifice:** No primary source associates human sacrifice with Vohu Manah. Greek allegations or reports about Persian human sacrifice cannot be transferred to his cult without explicit evidence.

**Temple prostitution:** No evidence associates it with Vohu Manah.

### Calendar and Bahmanagān

**[Late antique/medieval]** The second day and eleventh month bear Bahman’s name. When day and month coincided, Bahmanagān/Bahmanjana was celebrated. Al-Bīrūnī and later lexicographical traditions describe it as a festival connected with livestock, food preparation, and abstinence. In Khorasan a mixed dish of grains, vegetables, fruits, and sometimes meat was called the *dīg-e Bahmanjana*. The celebration became confused with the nearby Sada festival in medieval sources.

### Material and onomastic evidence

*Bahman* became a Persian personal name, but Gignoux noted that divine-name compounds involving Wahman are less common in older Iranian onomastics than names formed with Mithra or Ādur. Modern use of Bahman as a given name and as the eleventh month of Iran’s Solar Hijri calendar preserves the name beyond specifically Zoroastrian worship.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Old Avestan phase

Vohu Manah appears as an ethically charged divine faculty inseparable from Mazdā’s wisdom, human choice, Aša, pastoral responsibility, and salvation. Personhood remains grammatically and poetically fluid.

### Younger Avestan phase

The figure becomes a clearly invoked *yazata*, gains first rank after Ahura Mazdā among the six Aməša Spəntas, participates in cosmic defense, opposes Aka Manah, and receives the righteous dead. Calendrical placement is established.

### Achaemenid period, ca. 550–330 BCE

No royal inscription names Vohu Manah. Achaemenid familiarity with related theology is possible but cannot be demonstrated by Behistun or Naqš-e Rostam. Iconographic readings of six royal attendants as Aməša Spəntas are hypotheses. Herodotus supplies no direct attestation.

### Hellenistic and Parthian periods

The evidence remains indirect. Iranian religious traditions spread and diversified, but no securely identified temple or narrative specifically concerning Vohu Manah can be dated to these centuries.

### Kushan Central Asia, first–third centuries CE

Manaobago appears on royal coinage. If the identification is correct, this is the only ancient named image connected with Vohu Manah and reflects an imperial, highly syncretistic pantheon. If incorrect, it is evidence only for a similarly named Bactrian god.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

Priestly exegesis systematized the Aməšāspands, their creations, adversaries, ritual functions, and roles in sacred history. Much relevant material survives only through post-Sasanian Pahlavi compilations. No securely identified Sasanian image or temple of Wahman is known; Zoroastrian worship centered on fire and liturgy rather than representational cult statues.

### Manichaean adaptation, third century onward

Iranian Manichaeans used *Wahman* to translate or name the Great Nous. This demonstrates the cultural prestige and semantic usefulness of the name, not a merger of the two religions.

### Islamic conquest and medieval Iran

After 651, Zoroastrian institutions lost royal patronage. Jizya, diminished legal status, local coercion, economic pressure, and gradual conversion reduced the community. The process was regional and prolonged, not a single instantaneous forced conversion. Pahlavi scholars compiled the *Dēnkard*, *Bundahišn*, *Zādspram*, and afterlife literature in this environment, thereby preserving and elaborating Wahman’s mythology.

Bahmanagān continued among Zoroastrians and some Muslims and was recorded by al-Bīrūnī. The name survived in the Persian calendar and personal nomenclature even as explicit cult contracted.

### Parsi transmission

Zoroastrian migrants and merchants established communities in western India. The *Qeṣṣa-ye Sanjān*, written in 1599–1600 from older oral tradition, presents flight from Islamic persecution as a founding story but is not contemporary evidence for the initial migration. In India, ritual invocations, calendrical observance, meat abstinence, and animal ethics maintained Bahman’s place.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

European philology recovered the textual history through editions by Anquetil-Duperron, Westergaard, Geldner, Darmesteter, Mills, Bartholomae, and later Humbach, Insler, Kellens, Malandra, Narten, Boyce, and Skjærvø. Reformist Zoroastrians often emphasized Vohu Manah as an ethical principle accessible through reason, while traditionalist usage continued to worship Wahman as a real *yazata*. Both readings draw selectively on the ancient ambiguity.

### Present

Vohu Manah remains part of living Zoroastrian prayer, theology, calendars, and festivals in Iran, India, and diasporic communities. There is no evidence of an uninterrupted independent temple cult devoted solely to him. Modern reconstructionist and convert communities frequently prefer “Good Mind” as an ethical faculty; traditional ritual communities more readily retain the personal *yazata*. Neither modern emphasis alone exhausts the historical record.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Comparative religion and esotericism

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers frequently called the Aməša Spəntas “archangels,” compared them with Christian angelic orders, or aligned Vohu Manah with divine Mind, Logos, or Platonic Nous. Widengren’s *The Great Vohu Manah and the Apostle of God* explored Iranian and Manichaean continuities. Its comparisons remain historically important but do not prove every proposed genetic connection.

Theosophical, New Age, and modern occult tables sometimes assign Vohu Manah a planet, color, gem, chakra, angel, or weekday. Unless a specific historical source is supplied, these are **[modern inventions]**, not recovered Avestan doctrine. Vohu Manah has no established role in the Golden Dawn’s classical ritual system or in the principal early-modern European grimoires.

### Modern Zoroastrian interpretation

Reformist explanations often identify Vohu Manah solely with rational or benevolent thought and present the Aməša Spəntas as non-personal “attributes of God.” Traditional interpretations treat them as both divine qualities and individual worship-worthy beings. The ancient texts support overlap between quality and being, but neither a rigidly secular psychology nor a simple Christian-style angelology reproduces all periods.

The slogan “Good Thoughts, Good Words, Good Deeds” authentically summarizes Zoroastrian ethics, but identifying its first element directly and exclusively as the deity Vohu Manah is a modern pedagogical simplification.

### Art, games, and popular culture

Japanese games and related fantasy media—including the *Shin Megami Tensei* franchise and *Granblue Fantasy*—use Vohu Manah as a named supernatural combatant. Weapons, elemental powers, monstrous bodies, and elaborate costumes in these works are **[modern fictional inventions]**. They should not be cited as evidence for ancient iconography.

Internet illustrations commonly show a winged male angel in white, sometimes holding cattle, a book, or a lotus. No ancient Zoroastrian source establishes this image.

### Political and nationalist use

Bahman is prominent in modern Iranian civic language because it names the eleventh month. Events dated to Bahman—including the 1979 Revolution—use the calendar name without invoking the deity. Modern Iranian cultural revival can celebrate Bahmanagān as pre-Islamic heritage; this may be secular, nationalist, religious, or some combination. No evidence establishes a significant extremist cult centered specifically on Vohu Manah.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Abstraction versus personhood

This is the central interpretive controversy. The grammar and poetic syntax often permit “good thought” where later theology expects “Vohu Manah.” Insler stressed abstraction; Nyberg and Duchesne-Guillemin stressed personhood; Boyce, Humbach, and Narten recognized interaction between the two. Capitalization in translation can predetermine an answer the Avestan does not.

### Cattle association

Boyce and Lommel reconstructed the Vohu Manah–cattle pairing as an integral part of Gāthic theology. Cameron interpreted Zarathustra through a herdsman context. Narten argued that the Gāthās never state the later guardianship unambiguously. The explicit protector of cattle belongs securely to Pahlavi systematization, while a less formal association is undeniably early.

### Pre-Zoroastrian origin

Linguistic cognates are Indo-Iranian, but an Indo-Iranian god *Vasu Manas* is unattested. The figure may therefore be a specifically Zoroastrian hypostasis created from inherited religious vocabulary.

### Date of the Gāthās

Absolute dating remains disputed because there are no contemporary manuscripts or inscriptions naming Zarathustra. Linguistic comparison places Old Avestan close to Vedic Sanskrit and well before the recorded Middle Iranian languages; historical chronologies vary. Giving “1500 BCE,” “1200 BCE,” or “600 BCE” as an exact secure date overstates the evidence.

### Lost Bahman Yašt

Older scholarship inferred a lost Avestan hymn behind the *Zand ī Wahman Yasn*. Gignoux rejected the inference; Hultgård and the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* discussion stress the text’s composite, repeatedly revised apocalyptic character and the absence of Wahman from it. Calling it an extant “Bahman Yasht” is misleading.

### Manaobago

The name’s resemblance to *manah* plus *baga* supports identification, while its lunar symbols and unusual iconography complicate it. Catalogues that caption the coin simply “Vohu Manah” suppress the uncertainty.

### Iconography

No uncontested image of Vohu Manah survives. Identification of generic Achaemenid winged symbols, Sasanian attendants, or Kushan divinities with him requires independent inscriptions or attributes that are generally absent.

### Transmission and manuscripts

The Avesta was orally transmitted and later written in the specialized Avestan script developed in late antiquity. The destruction or loss of much Avestan literature is reported by Zoroastrian tradition, but specific claims that Alexander or Arab armies alone destroyed a complete fixed “original Avesta” are partly polemical and cannot be independently reconstructed. The Avestan Digital Archive now provides images and descriptions of surviving manuscripts.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Vohu Manah was universally depicted as a winged angel: unsupported.
- He is identical with Gabriel: late comparison, not ancient identification.
- The *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* is his surviving ancient Yašt: rejected by substantial modern scholarship.
- Manaobago is certainly Vohu Manah: plausible, not certain.
- He ruled Mercury or the Moon throughout ancient Iran: unsupported; lunar imagery belongs specifically to the disputed Kushan figure.
- The Gāthās clearly describe him leading Zarathustra by the hand from a river: that full scene is Pahlavi.
- Bahman in the *Shāhnāma* is the god incarnate: unsupported conflation.
- Vohu Manah had a documented cult of human sacrifice or temple prostitution: no evidence.

## 10. Chronology

- **Late second/early first millennium BCE, exact date disputed:** Old Avestan Gāthās attest *vohu manah-* as divine/moral Good Mind; pastoral, revelatory, and eschatological associations appear.
- **Early Avestan liturgical phase:** *Yasna Haptaŋhāiti* attests the collective category later rendered Aməša Spəntas.
- **Younger Avestan period:** Vohu Manah becomes clearly enumerated as a divine being, first after Ahura Mazdā; *Yasht* 13 and 19 and *Vendidad* 19 provide cosmic-defense, victory, and afterlife scenes.
- **Sixth–fourth centuries BCE:** Achaemenid inscriptions invoke Auramazdā but never Vohu Manah by name; proposed visual allusions remain hypothetical.
- **Fifth century BCE:** Herodotus describes Persian religion without naming Vohu Manah.
- **First–second centuries CE:** Plutarch reports Iranian dualist theology without an unambiguous Vohu Manah equivalent.
- **Second century CE:** Kushan coins of Huvishka depict ΜΑΝΑΟΒΑΓΟ; identification with Vohu Manah remains disputed.
- **Third century onward:** Manichaean Iranian texts use Wahman terminology for Great Mind/Nous.
- **224–651 CE:** Sasanian priestly culture systematizes the Aməšāspands, their creations, adversaries, and calendar, although most relevant books survive only in later recensions.
- **Seventh century CE onward:** Islamic conquest removes Zoroastrian royal patronage; demographic contraction and regional conversion proceed over centuries.
- **Ninth–tenth centuries:** Major Pahlavi compilations preserve Wahman’s role in creation, Zarathustra’s infancy and revelation, animal guardianship, judgment, and eschatology.
- **Ca. 973–1048:** Al-Bīrūnī records Bahmanagān/Bahmanjana and the Iranian festival calendar.
- **Ca. 1010:** Ferdowsi completes the *Shāhnāma*; its human prince Bahman is distinct from Vohu Manah.
- **1323:** The oldest dated Pahlavi Yasna manuscripts J2 and K5 are copied in India, illustrating the long gap between Avestan composition and extant witnesses.
- **1496:** A New Persian retelling related to the *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* is composed at Yazd.
- **1599–1600:** *Qeṣṣa-ye Sanjān* records the Parsi migration legend.
- **1771:** Anquetil-Duperron’s publication introduces substantial Avestan material to European scholarship.
- **Nineteenth century:** Westergaard, Spiegel, Darmesteter, Geldner, and Mills produce editions and translations; “Bahman Yasht” becomes an influential but misleading title.
- **1916:** Bernhard Geiger publishes a major study of the Aməša Spəntas.
- **1930:** Herman Lommel’s *Die Religion Zarathustras* advances influential interpretations of the divine abstractions and their creations.
- **1945:** Geo Widengren publishes *The Great Vohu Manah and the Apostle of God*.
- **1959 onward:** Humbach’s work reshapes Gāthic philology.
- **1968:** George Cameron publishes “Zoroaster the Herdsman,” emphasizing the cattle complex.
- **1975:** Stanley Insler’s translation stresses the conceptual dimension; Boyce begins her multivolume *History of Zoroastrianism*.
- **1982:** Johanna Narten publishes *Die Aməša Spəṇtas im Avesta*, sharpening disputes over personification and cattle guardianship.
- **1986:** Philippe Gignoux argues against the existence of a recoverable Avestan Bahman Yašt behind the Pahlavi apocalypse.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** Kellens, Malandra, Skjærvø, Cantera, and others reassess ritual performance, dating, textual transmission, and manuscript evidence.
- **Present:** Bahman remains a calendrical name, personal name, festival figure, theological principle, and worship-worthy being in different Zoroastrian settings; modern fantasy and esotericism generate iconography absent from the ancient record.

## 11. Sources and critical orientation

The primary documentary base consists of the Avestan *Yasna*, Gāthās, Yašts, and *Vendidad*, read through critical editions rather than any single nineteenth-century translation; the Middle Persian *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, *Dādestān ī dēnīg*, *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*, *Šāyest nē Šāyest*, *Zādspram*, and *Zand ī Wahman Yasn*; Kushan coins bearing ΜΑΝΑΟΒΑΓΟ; and medieval calendrical testimony such as al-Bīrūnī.

Darmesteter and Mills remain valuable for the history of scholarship and convenient public-domain access, but their translations frequently embody nineteenth-century assumptions. Geldner’s edition remains foundational; Humbach, Insler, Kellens, Malandra, Narten, Boyce, Skjærvø, Gignoux, Hultgård, and Cantera are essential for philology, historical reconstruction, and transmission. The Avestan Digital Archive should be preferred for inspection of physical manuscript witnesses.
