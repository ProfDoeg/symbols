# Anahita: Research Dossier

**Class:** Yazata—“being worthy of worship”—and female divinity of the waters in Zoroastrian tradition; goddess in Greek, Armenian, and modern descriptive usage. In different historical strata she is a cosmic river, an anthropomorphic fertility and victory goddess, a royal patron, the planet Venus, and the tutelary “Lady” of particular sanctuaries.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest independently datable written attestations are the trilingual Old Persian–Elamite–Babylonian royal inscriptions of Artaxerxes II, especially A²Sa at Susa, approximately 404–359 BCE, which invoke **Anāhatā/Anāhitā** beside Ahuramazda and Mithra. The Avestan *Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā* may preserve substantially older religious language and Indo-Iranian conceptions, but neither the composition of *Yašt* 5 nor its surviving manuscripts supply a comparably secure early date. Earlier Achaemenid inscriptions—including Behistun and those of Darius I and Xerxes—do not name her.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Secure ancient textual, epigraphic, or material evidence.
- **[Later canonical]** Zoroastrian material preserved in the Younger Avesta or Middle Persian religious literature, often containing older traditions but reaching its surviving form later.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally transformed figure, especially Armenian, Anatolian, Mesopotamian, or Central Asian.
- **[Learned systematization]** Priestly, exegetical, or antiquarian organization of inherited material.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically situated equation with another divinity.
- **[Medieval accretion]** Post-Sasanian literary or religious development.
- **[Folklore]** Belief or ritual preserved without secure continuity of identity.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent claim presented without ancient documentation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Aredvi, Sura, Anahita

The fullest Avestan form is **Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā** (𐬀𐬭𐬆𐬛𐬎𐬎𐬍 𐬯𐬏𐬭𐬁 𐬀𐬥𐬁𐬵𐬌𐬙𐬁 in modern Avestan type). The three elements did not necessarily begin as an indivisible personal name.

- **Arədvī** is usually connected with moisture or flowing water, though its exact formation remains disputed.
- **Sūrā** means “mighty, powerful.”
- **Anāhitā** is conventionally analyzed as the negative prefix *an-* plus a form related to *āhita*, “defiled, polluted,” hence “undefiled,” “unstained,” or “immaculate.” The familiar translation “the Immaculate” is defensible as an epithet, but its later treatment as the goddess’s proper name should not be projected automatically into the earliest stratum.
- Suggested renderings of the whole include “the Moist, Mighty, Undefiled One” and “the mighty, immaculate Arədvī.”

A grammatical problem underlies the historical discussion. Greek **Anaïtis** and reconstructed Old Iranian *Anāhiti-* point toward an *i*-stem, whereas Avestan *Anāhitā* behaves differently. Scholars have treated this either as ordinary linguistic adaptation or as evidence that an epithet or separate western-Iranian goddess became attached to the older river *Arədvī Sūrā*. Malandra and de Jong favor a composite or syncretistic history; Boyce emphasizes the fusion of the Iranian river divinity with a western, image-bearing goddess. A more radical recent proposal identifies Anāhitā with Mesopotamian Annunitum, but this has not displaced the broader syncretistic model. [Modern reconstruction; contested] ([Achaemenica synthesis](https://achaemenica.org/articles/anahita); [Saadi-Nejad dissertation](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/handle/fub188/24610/Saadi-nejad_dissertation.pdf); [Iranian Studies origin debate](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00210862.2017.1348223))

### Historical forms

- **Avestan:** *Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā*; often simply *Arədvī*, *Arədvī Sūrā*, or *Anāhitā*.
- **Old Persian:** conventionally *Anāhatā* or *Anāhitā*, represented in Artaxerxes II’s inscriptions; the reading is complicated by damaged passages.
- **Greek:** Ἀναΐτις, **Anaïtis/Anaitis**; also “Persian Artemis,” “Artemis Anaïtis,” and occasionally Aphrodite or Aphrodite Urania.
- **Middle Persian:** **Ardwīsūr Anāhīd**, **Anāhīd**; inscriptional Pahlavi ʾnhyt.
- **New Persian:** **Anāhīd/Ānāhīd** (آناهید) and **Nāhīd** (ناهید). *Nāhīd* is also the ordinary literary name of Venus.
- **Armenian:** **Anahit** (Անահիտ).
- **Anatolian cult forms:** *Anaïtis*, with compounds and titles including Artemis Persikē and Artemis Tauropolos/Taurica.
- **Personal names:** Middle Iranian and later forms include Āb-Nāhīd, “Anāhīd of water,” and Ādur-Anāhīd, “Anāhīd of the fire.”

### Titles and descriptions

In *Yašt* 5 she is:

- “wide-flowing” or “wide-expanding”;
- healing;
- opposed to the daevas;
- obedient to Ahura’s law;
- life-, herd-, pasture/fold-, wealth-, and country-increasing;
- purifier of male seed;
- purifier of women’s wombs;
- provider of easy delivery and milk;
- “worthy of sacrifice” and “worthy of prayer” in the material world. [Later canonical: *Yašt* 5.1–5]

In Armenian tradition she becomes:

- **Anahit tikin**, “Lady Anahit”;
- **oskemayr**, “golden mother”;
- “benefactress of the whole human race”;
- “mother of all knowledge”;
- “daughter of the great Aramazd.” [Regional variant: *Agathangelos*, fifth-century composition describing the early-fourth-century conversion]

The Middle Persian description **Ardwīsūr ī Anāhīd, pid ud mād ī Ābān**, “Ardwīsūr Anāhīd, father and mother of the Waters,” exemplifies priestly cosmological systematization rather than biological genealogy. [Learned systematization] ([Iranica, “Ardwīsūr Anāhīd”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/anahid/anahid-i-ardwisur-anahid/))

### Divine class

In the Avesta she is a **yazata**, not an Amesha Spenta. Ahura Mazda authorizes and participates in her worship; she remains within the Mazda-worshipping order. Greek writers call her a goddess and identify her through Artemis or Aphrodite. Armenian royal religion makes Anahit a major national and dynastic goddess. Pahlavi texts sometimes distinguish the river **Ardwīsūr** from **Anāhīd**, the latter associated with Venus, showing that the fusion never became conceptually seamless.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What is—and is not—early

Anahita is absent from the **Gathas**, the Old Avestan hymns conventionally associated with Zarathustra. She is also absent by name from the securely dated inscriptions of Darius I and Xerxes, including Behistun and Naqsh-e Rostam. Those absences do not prove that no Iranian community revered her, but they prevent claims of documented Gathic or early-Achaemenid worship.

The first securely datable name occurs under Artaxerxes II. A²Sa at Susa invokes Ahuramazda, Anahita, and Mithra to protect the king and what he constructed. Related texts A²Sd and A²Ha belong to the same reign and ideological environment. A²Sa survives on palace column bases and in three imperial languages; its damaged state makes some restorations uncertain, but the triadic invocation is secure. [Early attested] ([Old Persian line and script](https://parsiandej.ir/en/old-persian-corpus/a2sa/lines/line-5); [A²Ha](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/a2ha/))

Artaxerxes III’s surviving Persepolis inscription invokes Ahuramazda and Mithra but omits Anahita. This warns against treating Artaxerxes II’s triad as an immediately fixed imperial trinity. [Early attested]

### The Avesta

#### *Yašt* 5, the Ābān Yašt

*Yašt* 5 is the principal textual dossier. It is a Younger Avestan hymn of 132 stanzas. Its language and inherited poetry antedate the manuscripts by many centuries, but its exact composition is disputed. Individual formulae and heroic traditions may be pre-Achaemenid; the received hymn was edited repeatedly and probably obtained something like its fixed form much later, possibly in the Sasanian redaction.

Its contents fall into overlapping strata:

1. A cosmological account of the river descending from Mount Hukairya into the sea Vourukaša and distributing water throughout the seven regions.
2. Fertility and purification formulae.
3. A catalogue of worshippers who sacrifice and petition her.
4. Her anthropomorphic appearance as a richly dressed maiden.
5. Ritual directions governing acceptable offerings.
6. Late passages concerning Zarathustra and his circle.

The full Avestan text rests on Geldner’s nineteenth-century critical edition; Darmesteter’s translation remains accessible but often reflects outdated philology. Modern work by Kellens, Malandra, Skjærvø, and others is preferable for language and dating. ([Avestan and English text](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt5.htm); [Iranica, “Ābān Yašt”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/aban-yast/); [Iranica, “Yašts”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/))

#### *Yasna* 65 and liturgical passages

*Yasna* 65 invokes Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā and the waters, petitions them for prosperity and offspring, and excludes morally or ritually disqualified persons from their benefits. The connection is primarily liturgical, not a separate biography or myth. Other recurring dedications place her among “the good, Mazda-created waters.” [Later canonical] ([Yasna text](https://avesta.org/yasna/index.html))

#### *Vendidad* and other Avestan material

The *Vendidad* elaborates purity law and the danger of contaminating water, but it does not supply an independent narrative career for Anahita. References to waters should not automatically be converted into appearances by her. Likewise, the Gathic Amesha Spenta **Haurvatāt**, associated with wholeness and water in later organization, is not simply another name for Anahita.

### Greek and Roman witnesses

- **Berossus**, as preserved in later quotation, says Artaxerxes II introduced statues of Aphrodite Anaïtis into Babylon, Susa, Ecbatana, Persepolis, Bactria, Damascus, and Sardis. Berossus wrote in the early third century BCE; the fragment survives only through later transmission. It is major evidence for royal sponsorship and image cult, but “introduced worship” may exaggerate the novelty of the deity rather than merely the statues. [Early attested report; contested interpretation]
- **Herodotus 1.131** states that Persians learned to sacrifice to “the heavenly Aphrodite” from Assyrians and Arabs and calls her “Mitra.” Many specialists understand this as confusion between Mithra and an Iranian goddess comparable to Anahita; it is not a secure occurrence of her name. [Early classical testimony; uncertain identification]
- **Plutarch, *Artaxerxes* 27**, written around the late first or early second century CE, says Artaxerxes II made Aspasia priestess of “Artemis of Ecbatana, whom they call Anaïtis,” imposing chastity upon her. [Roman-period testimony about an Achaemenid episode] ([Plutarch text](https://classics.mit.edu/Plutarch/artaxerx.html))
- **Strabo 11.14.16**, early first century CE, describes Armenian devotion to Anaïtis and reports premarital service involving sexual intercourse at her sanctuaries. [Regional testimony; contested generalization] ([Strabo text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/11N%2A.html))
- **Isidore of Charax, *Parthian Stations* 6**, around the turn of the era, mentions continuing sacrifice at an Artemis temple at Ecbatana and a temple of Artemis at Concobar, often associated with Kangavar.
- **Pliny, *Natural History* 33.82–83**, records that Antony’s soldiers smashed and divided the great gold image from Eriza in 34 BCE.
- **Cassius Dio** and **Cicero** supply geographical and political evidence for Armenia’s Anaïtis country and wealthy temple institutions.

### Armenian texts

The *History* attributed to **Agathangelos** was composed in Armenian in the fifth century, though it represents itself as an eyewitness account of Tiridates III and Gregory the Illuminator in the early fourth. It calls Anahit daughter of Aramazd, records royal pilgrimage and sacrifice, describes Gregory’s refusal to worship her, and narrates destruction of the old sanctuaries. It is indispensable but also Christian hagiography legitimating the Armenian church; its speeches and compressed national conversion cannot be read as verbatim fourth-century records. [Regional variant; late testimony to earlier cult] ([Oxford cult-replacement record](https://portal.sds.ox.ac.uk/articles/online_resource/E00102_Agathangelos_History_of_Armenia_written_in_Armenian_in_the_second_half_of_the_5th_c_tells_the_story_set_in_the_early_4th_c_of_Gregory_the_Illuminator_converter_of_Armenia_S00251_establishing_with_the_help_of_relics_the_commemoration_/13741471))

**Moses of Khoren**, in a work whose date and textual formation remain disputed, attributes the Eriza foundation to Tigranes the Great. That is not independently verified. [Medieval or late-antique learned tradition]

### Middle Persian books

The **Bundahišn**, **Dēnkard**, **Mēnōg ī Xrad**, and related works were compiled chiefly in the ninth and tenth centuries CE, while preserving much Sasanian and sometimes older exegetical material. Their late manuscripts cannot be treated as direct Achaemenid witnesses.

The *Bundahišn* systematizes Ardwīsūr within cosmic hydrology: water flows from the high mountain into the cosmic sea and thence through earthly rivers. Pahlavi tradition can distinguish Ardwīsūr, the river, from Anāhīd, the female planetary or fertility being. The *Dēnkard* summarizes now-lost Avestan and Sasanian learning but offers no securely datable new Anahita myth. The *Mēnōg ī Xrad* and *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag* are relevant to the religious cosmos but do not make Anahita a central narrative actor. [Learned systematization] ([Pahlavi literature overview](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/middle-persian/pahlavi-literature/); [Dēnkard history](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/denkard/); [Bundahišn overview](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bundahisn-primal-creation/))

### The *Šāhnāma*

Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed around 1010 CE, preserves Iranian heroic figures who appear among the worshippers in *Yašt* 5—Hōšang, Jamšīd, Żaḥḥāk, Ferēdun, Kay Kāvus, Kay Ḵosrow, Afrāsīāb, and others—but ordinarily removes their sacrifices to Anahita from the narrative. It witnesses the Islamic-period transformation of Iranian national legend, not continuous explicit goddess cult. [Medieval accretion and secularized epic transmission]

### Manichaean and Roman Mithraic material

No secure Manichaean scripture makes Anahita a principal divinity. Iranian **Nāhīd/Venus** can occur in astral terminology, but this is not automatically the Avestan river goddess. Roman Mithraic monuments do not securely identify Anahita as Mithras’s virgin mother. That idea is a modern comparativist construction, not an ancient Mithraic doctrine. [Absence of evidence; modern invention where asserted]

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Waters and cosmic geography

In *Yašt* 5, Arədvī flows from Mount Hukairya into the cosmic sea Vourukaša. Her single mighty current equals all earthly waters; it divides into a thousand channels and supplies the seven regions. She therefore embodies both a particular celestial river and the total regenerative circulation of water. This is more precise than calling her merely “goddess of water.”

### Fertility and health

She purifies semen, prepares wombs, makes women give birth safely, and supplies milk. She gives life, livestock, wealth, territorial prosperity, and healing. Fertility here includes humans, animals, fields, and political communities. The later popular formula “goddess of love” is incomplete: explicit erotic love is not her defining Avestan domain.

### Kingship, victory, and knowledge

The heroic catalogue makes her a grantor of sovereignty and military victory. Worthy Iranian petitioners receive boons; hostile or demonic petitioners are refused. Priests and pupils seek knowledge from her in *Yašt* 5.86. Armenian Anahit’s “mother of all knowledge” title may develop this wisdom aspect, though it belongs to a distinct regional formulation.

The strongly martial and royal aspects may reflect contact with Ištar, Nanaya, or another western-Iranian/Mesopotamian goddess. They need not derive from the river alone. [Syncretistic identification; reconstructed mechanism]

### Avestan anthropomorphic form

In *Yašt* 5.64 and 5.126–129 she manifests as a beautiful, noble maiden:

- upright and splendid;
- richly girdled;
- wearing golden shoes and ornaments;
- clothed in beaver skins;
- wearing a crown with stars and a hundred ornaments;
- with a four-horse chariot.

Her four white horses are identified symbolically with **wind, rain, cloud, and sleet**. This is meteorological personification, not a genealogy of divine children.

### Images and doubtful identifications

No securely identified Achaemenid cult statue survives. Berossus’s report establishes that statues were installed, not what they looked like.

Sasanian candidates include:

- the female figure investing **Narseh** at Naqsh-e Rostam, approximately 293–303 CE;
- the female figure beside **Khosrow II** at Taq-e Bostan, early seventh century;
- female figures on coins, seals, capitals, and silver vessels.

The Narseh figure is often Anahita but has also been identified as Queen Šābuhrduxtag. The absence of an inscription naming the figure prevents certainty. Taq-e Bostan is likewise widely but not unanimously read as Anahita. Numerous nude or dancing women on Sasanian silver cannot all securely be labeled Anahita; some may be entertainers, royal women, personifications, or Dionysiac figures. [Modern reconstruction; contested] ([Iranica art survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/anahid/anahid-iv-anahita-in-the-arts/); [Narseh identification debate](https://www.biblioiranica.info/not-the-queen-sabuhrduxtag-but-the-goddess-anahita/))

The bronze female head from Satala, now in the British Museum, was long publicized as Anahit. Its provenance and Greco-Roman style do not prove that identity; specialists have argued it more probably belonged to a Roman-period image of another goddess. [Disputed attribution]

### Animals, plants, colors, days, and planets

- **Horses:** four white chariot horses in *Yašt* 5.
- **Beaver:** her Avestan garment uses beaver skin; it marks aquatic richness, not a later universal sacred animal.
- **Bull:** bulls were sacrificed to Anaïtis in parts of Anatolia and Armenia; this encouraged identification with Artemis Tauropolos/Taurica. [Regional variant]
- **Star and crescent:** associated with Armenian and western forms, probably through Ištar/Venus syncretism. [Regional variant]
- **Venus:** Middle and New Persian Nāhīd is the planet Venus. The equation is later than the cosmic-river stratum and reflects western astral syncretism.
- **Calendar:** the tenth day of each Zoroastrian month belongs to the Waters. The coincidence of the day Ābān with the month Ābān produces the feast **Ābānagān**. It is a feast of the waters, with Arədvī Anāhitā named in the dedication—not evidence that every modern Ābān observance is an independent goddess cult. ([Iranica, “Ābān Māh”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/aban-mah/))

No ancient source securely assigns her a universal sacred color, magical number, weekday, or crystal. Lists circulating online that give such correspondences are normally modern occult inventions.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Genealogy

The Avesta gives Anahita no parents, birth narrative, consort, or offspring. She is a Mazda-created or Mazda-ordered beneficent being, but this is theological origin rather than genealogy.

Armenian *Agathangelos* calls Anahit the **daughter of Aramazd**. This is a regional divine family structure, not an Avestan doctrine. No secure ancient source makes her Mithra’s mother, wife, or lover.

### Every documented interaction or joint appearance with another named divine being

- **Ahura Mazda — worshipper and authorizer:** Ahura Mazda tells Zarathustra to sacrifice to Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā; *Yašt* 5.1. [Later canonical]
- **Ahura Mazda — petitioner:** Mazda himself sacrifices to her and asks that Zarathustra accept, speak, and practice the religion; *Yašt* 5.17–19. She grants the boon. [Later canonical; theologically striking but explicit]
- **Ahura Mazda — imperial co-invocation:** Ahuramazda, Anahita, and Mithra jointly protect Artaxerxes II and his works in A²Sa/A²Sd/A²Ha, fourth century BCE. [Early attested]
- **Mithra — imperial co-invocation:** Named beside Ahuramazda and Anahita under Artaxerxes II; no conversation, kinship, or shared narrative is recorded. [Early attested]
- **Angra Mainyu — cosmic opposition:** Her worship formula repudiates Angra Mainyu, and she refuses the request of his destructive creation Aži Dahāka; *Yašt* 5.29–31, 33–35. There is no direct combat scene between Anahita and Angra Mainyu. [Later canonical]
- **Apąm Napāt — shared aquatic sphere/joint cultic environment:** *Yašt* 5.72 invokes Apąm Napāt at the location where petitioners sacrifice to Anahita; Middle Iranian systematization places both within the waters. No direct dialogue or kinship is attested. [Later canonical]
- **Aramazd — father and royal triad partner:** Armenian Anahit is his daughter and appears with Aramazd and Vahagn in Tiridates III’s official devotion; *Agathangelos*, fifth century. [Regional variant]
- **Vahagn — royal triad and sanctuary association:** Anahit, Aramazd, and Vahagn form the royal Armenian triad; at Ashtishat her cult stood in a complex also associated with Vahagn. No narrative interaction is preserved. [Regional variant]
- **Astłik — sanctuary association:** Anahit, Vahagn, and Astłik had related cults at Ashtishat; no surviving myth records a personal encounter. [Regional variant]
- **Tiur/Tir — neighboring cult:** At Artashat, Anahit’s temple stood near that of the oracular Tiur. Proximity is documented; interaction is not. [Regional variant]
- **Nana/Nanē — possible fusion or functional overlap:** Both occur in Armenian and eastern Iranian environments, but no ancient narrative has them meet. Their equation is partial and scholarly, not a universally attested identity. [Syncretistic identification; contested]
- **Ištar/Inanna — reconstructed contributor:** Similarities in sexuality, warfare, Venus symbolism, lions, and image cult have led scholars to posit fusion. No Iranian primary text narrates Anahita meeting or becoming Ištar. [Modern scholarly reconstruction]
- **Annunitum — proposed identity:** Saadi-Nejad argues that Artaxerxes II’s Anahita was Annunitum/Antu in Iranian adaptation. The proposal is debated and is not stated by an ancient source. [Modern scholarly reconstruction; minority thesis]
- **Artemis — Greek equation:** Greek and Roman authors identify Anaïtis as Artemis, especially at Ecbatana, Concobar/Kangavar, Lydia, Cappadocia, and Armenia. This is interpretatio graeca, not an indigenous myth of two goddesses meeting. [Syncretistic identification]
- **Aphrodite/Aphrodite Urania — Greek equation:** Berossus and other classical traditions use Aphrodite terminology for her image cult and celestial/fertility aspect. [Syncretistic identification]
- **Aši — textual borrowing, not interaction:** Parts of the anthropomorphic description and royal-benefaction language resemble or derive from material belonging to Aši. The two never meet in a surviving episode. [Literary relationship; no attested interaction]
- **Sarasvatī — comparative relation only:** Both are mighty rivers connected with fertility and knowledge. Lommel proposed a particularly close historical identity, but the linguistic and geographic reconstruction is disputed. No Vedic or Avestan text makes them interact. [Modern comparative reconstruction]
- **Dionysos — proposed artistic syncretism:** Some Sasanian silver imagery may combine Anahita-like and Dionysiac forms. There is no textual myth of interaction. [Modern art-historical reconstruction]

**Negative finding:** No secure text supplies Anahita with a consort or divine child. Assertions that she gave virgin birth to Mithra are unattested in the Avesta, Iranian Mithraic evidence, and Roman Mithraic monuments.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The descent of the cosmic river

[Later canonical; probably containing older material]

Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā descends from Mount Hukairya to the sea Vourukaša. Her flow makes the cosmic sea surge to its shores and boil at its center. A thousand lakes and a thousand channels arise from her, each large enough to require a rider forty days to traverse. From this reservoir the waters reach all seven regions, continually purifying male seed, female wombs, and mothers’ milk.

The narrative’s early core is probably the personification of a celestial river feeding earthly waters. The elaborate human form, royal ideology, and western astral traits may be later accretions. Attempts to locate Arədvī as one ordinary river—the Oxus, Araxes, Helmand, or another stream—remain speculative; *Yašt* 5 describes cosmic geography.

### The catalogue of sacrificers

[Later canonical heroic cycle]

The hymn repeatedly stages sacrifice, request, and verdict. Petitioners usually offer one hundred stallions, one thousand cattle, and ten thousand sheep—a heroic formula, not evidence that each named worshipper performed a historically recorded hecatomb.

- **Ahura Mazda** asks that Zarathustra accept his religion; granted.
- **Haošyaŋha/Hōšang** asks for sovereignty over daevas and hostile peoples; granted.
- **Yima/Jamšīd** asks for supreme rule and prosperity; granted.
- **Aži Dahāka/Żaḥḥāk** asks to empty the seven regions of humanity; refused.
- **Θraētaona/Ferēdun** asks to defeat Aži Dahāka and obtain the women Saŋhavāc and Arənnavāc; granted.
- **Kərəsāspa/Garšāsp** asks to defeat the monster Gandarəβa; granted.
- **Fraŋrasyan/Afrāsīāb** asks to seize the Iranian *xᵛarənah* in Vourukaša; refused.
- **Kavi Usa/Kay Kāvus** asks for rule over hostile beings; granted.
- **Kavi Haosravah/Kay Ḵosrow** asks for sovereignty and victory over hostile riders; granted.
- **Tusa/Tus** and the sons of Vaēsaka make opposing requests; Tus is favored, the Turanians refused.
- **Vīfrō Navāza** is rescued after being carried aloft by Θraētaona in bird form: Anahita appears as a maiden, seizes him, and restores him safely to earth.
- **Jāmāspa**, **Vistauru**, and figures of Zarathustra’s circle receive aid or knowledge.
- Hostile petitioners such as **Vaēska’s sons** and **Vandarəmaini** are refused.

The catalogue’s theological function is adjudicatory: sacrifice is not mechanically effective. Anahita grants boons compatible with *aša*, the good order, and refuses those serving the Lie or hostile peoples. Its political function is to make Iranian sovereignty and victory depend on correct cult.

### Anahita’s manifestation to Zarathustra

[Later canonical]

Zarathustra asks how she should be worshipped. She appears as a beautiful, high-born maiden and instructs him about the ritual. Proper offerings contain haoma and milk, are ritually prepared, and are presented by qualified worshippers. Persons characterized as diseased, malformed, hostile, or ritually unacceptable are excluded in the text’s ancient purity system. These exclusions are part of the source and should not be erased, though they should not be presented as timeless practice among all Zoroastrians.

### Armenian royal pilgrimage and Christian confrontation

[Regional variant; preserved in fifth-century Christian hagiography]

Tiridates makes pilgrimage to Anahit’s sanctuary, offering wreaths, branches, and sacrifice. In the conversion narrative, he orders Gregory to honor the goddess. Gregory refuses, condemning the old cult; he is tortured and imprisoned. After Tiridates’ conversion, royal and ecclesiastical forces destroy sanctuaries, redistribute temple wealth, and replace festivals and sacred sites with Christian institutions.

The story preserves authentic knowledge that Anahit’s cult was royal and wealthy, but its speeches, miracles, and rapid nationwide conversion follow hagiographical design. Archaeology and later historical reconstruction indicate a more gradual and contested Christianization than *Agathangelos* claims.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Artaxerxes II’s statues

Berossus attributes statues to Artaxerxes II at Babylon, Susa, Ecbatana, Persepolis, Bactria, Damascus, and Sardis. This is the strongest literary evidence for deliberate imperial diffusion. What the king “introduced” was probably the officially sponsored **image cult**, not necessarily first knowledge of the divinity. The inscriptions themselves demonstrate promotion, not the foundation of every temple Berossus names.

Image worship may have been controversial among Iranian priests accustomed to worship through fire, water, and open-air ritual. Boyce’s reconstruction of an “orthodox” reaction and eventual iconoclasm is plausible but rests on indirect evidence.

### Istakhr and the Sasanians

At the beginning of the third century CE the house of Sasan appears to have been hereditary custodian of a sanctuary at Istakhr. Later Islamic historians call it the **fire of Anāhīd**. Ardashir I’s dynasty turned local priestly and royal authority into imperial power.

Kartir’s inscription at the Kaʿba-ye Zartosht, late third century, mentions “the fire of Anāhīd the Lady” and “the fire of Anāhīd-Ardashir.” This is secure epigraphic evidence for institutionalized Sasanian veneration, although it does not prove that a surviving Achaemenid statue remained there. By this period the cult may have centered on a consecrated fire bearing her name rather than a freestanding image. ([Iranica, “Eṣṭaḵr”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/estakr-/))

### Ecbatana

Classical sources consistently associate Anaitis with Ecbatana. Plutarch’s Aspasia episode implies a priesthood and chastity rule. Isidore reports continuing sacrifice. Ancient accounts also describe the sanctuary’s wealth and plundering, followed by restoration. Evidence for the building itself remains incomplete.

### Kangavar

Isidore’s **Concobar** had a temple of Artemis, inviting identification with Anaïtis. The monumental terrace at modern Kangavar was therefore long reconstructed as a Hellenistic or Parthian “Temple of Anahita.”

Excavation does not confirm that conclusion. The visible platform’s masonry, column details, and masons’ marks favor a late-Sasanian date, perhaps Khosrow II, and no diagnostic inscription or cult image names Anahita. It may be a palace, sanctuary terrace, or an unidentified monumental complex. The temple Isidore saw could have stood elsewhere nearby or beneath an earlier phase. Calling the visible ruin “the Temple of Anahita” is conventional, not an archaeological fact. [Contested material] ([Kangavar archaeological review](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kangavar-1/))

### Bishapur

The sunken cruciform structure beside Shapur I’s palace has channels and a stone-paved chamber capable of holding or circulating water. Ghirshman first termed it a fire temple; Sarfaraz proposed a water temple of Anahita. Its hydraulic design makes the latter attractive, but no dedication names her. “Temple of Anahita at Bishapur” therefore remains a hypothesis. [Modern archaeological reconstruction]

### Armenia

The chief sanctuary was at **Eriza/Erez** in Acilisene. It possessed a great gold image, lands, wealth, male and female personnel, and royal patronage. Antony’s soldiers destroyed its image in 34 BCE. The surrounding country could be called *Anaetica*, “land of Anaïtis.”

Other centers included:

- **Artashat**, near the temple of Tiur;
- **Ashtishat**, where Anahit appeared as the “golden mother” in a cult complex associated with Vahagn and Astłik;
- **Tomisa** in Sophene;
- other unlocated or poorly documented sanctuaries.

The famous Satala head cannot securely reconstruct the Eriza image.

### Lydia, Cappadocia, and Anatolia

Anaïtis appears under Artemis titles in temple associations and inscriptions. Festivals included:

- **Anaeiteia** at Philadelphia;
- **Artemisia** at Hypaipa;
- **Sakaia** at Zela.

Bull sacrifice and athletic or musical contests belonged to some local cults. Temple communities could own land and dependent personnel. These are regional Hellenistic and Roman transformations, not straightforward replicas of Avestan ritual.

### Sacrifice and offerings

The Avesta prescribes haoma-and-milk libations, baresman, prayer, and ritual purity. The heroic animal totals are literary-sacrificial formulas. Anatolian and Armenian sources attest bulls. Nineteenth-century Zoroastrian practice at the Bānū-Pārs shrine included cattle sacrifice, which Boyce interpreted as survival of Anahita-related worship; continuity of the precise divine identity cannot be demonstrated at every stage.

### “Temple prostitution”

Strabo explicitly says prominent Armenian families dedicated daughters to Anaïtis and that the women had sexual relations at the sanctuary before marriage. That is evidence for what Strabo reported, not direct administrative documentation from Eriza. Armenian Christian polemic, despite its incentive to denounce pagan practices, does not independently corroborate the institution. Modern scholarship has also challenged the broad nineteenth-century model of ubiquitous Near Eastern “sacred prostitution.”

The responsible conclusion is:

1. Strabo’s testimony cannot simply be erased.
2. It may describe a real local institution, misunderstand temporary temple service, or assimilate Armenian practice to Greek ethnographic stereotypes about “eastern” sexuality.
3. It is not evidence that sexual service belonged to all Anahita temples.
4. Plutarch’s story of Aspasia instead represents an Anaïtis priestess as permanently chaste.

The two testimonies should remain in tension rather than be harmonized.

### Human sacrifice

No Avestan text prescribes human sacrifice to Anahita. No securely identified Anahita sanctuary has yielded an unequivocal human-sacrifice deposit. Claims connecting her with sacrificed male partners normally arise from speculative comparisons with Ištar/Nanaya or from generalized theories of sacred kingship. **Human sacrifice to Anahita is not established.**

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the fourth century BCE

A cosmic female river and water cult may descend from Indo-Iranian religion, with Sarasvatī as a functional comparator. The evidence does not permit naming a securely documented pre-Achaemenid cult “Anahita.” The Gathas are silent.

### Artaxerxes II, 404–359 BCE

This is the documentary turning point. The king names Anahita and Mithra beside Ahuramazda and, according to Berossus, sponsors her statues across imperial capitals and provinces. The development can be interpreted as:

- elevation of an existing Iranian divinity;
- fusion of the Avestan river with a western-Iranian goddess;
- adoption of Ištar/Nanaya/Aphrodite-like image, war, and astral traits;
- or, in the minority Annunitum theory, direct transformation of a Mesopotamian royal goddess.

The inscriptions prove royal promotion but do not choose decisively among these origin models.

### Hellenistic and Parthian periods

The cult survived Alexander’s conquest and Seleucid rule, though temples were plundered. Greek identifications with Artemis and Aphrodite multiplied. Image worship flourished in Armenia and Anatolia. Parthian rulers continued to invoke a royal complex of Aramazd/Mihr/Anāhīd, while personal names attest popular devotion.

Nana became dominant in some eastern Iranian and Central Asian regions. Shared iconography does not justify calling every Nana image Anahita.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

The Sasanian dynasty’s connection with Istakhr made Anahita a dynastic patron beneath Ohrmazd. Kartir’s inscription establishes named sacred fires. Royal art may depict her conferring investiture. At the same time, freestanding cult images appear to have diminished in official Iranian Zoroastrianism, perhaps through priestly iconoclasm or the preference for fire and natural elements.

The Avestan canon and zand traditions were organized under Sasanian institutions. *Yašt* 5 may have reached a relatively fixed written form in this environment even though much of its poetry is older.

### Armenian suppression

The Christianization associated with Tiridates III and Gregory in the early fourth century targeted Anahit because her temples embodied royal legitimacy, landholding, wealth, and inherited ritual. *Agathangelos* describes their destruction and the transfer of wealth. Resistance at temple centers could be violent. Christianity did not erase every custom immediately, but the institutional cult ceased.

### Islamic conquest and post-Sasanian Iran

After the seventh-century conquest, state-supported Zoroastrian institutions contracted. No securely documented public temple cult of Anahita continued as such. The feast of the waters and liturgical recitation survived among Zoroastrians.

Some female Muslim shrine figures associated with mountains, springs, fertility, and royal lineage may have absorbed functions formerly attached to Anahita. The shrine of **Bībī Šahrbānū** near Ray has been interpreted as an Islamicized “Lady of the Land.” That is a historical-continuity hypothesis, not proof that worshippers secretly addressed Anahita. [Folklore; modern reconstruction]

### Present

Zoroastrians continue to honor the good waters, recite Ābān-related prayers, and celebrate Ābānagān. Whether this should be described as “continuous worship of the goddess Anahita” depends on terminology: the liturgical name and water-veneration are continuous, while the Achaemenid-style statue cult and Armenian temple institutions are not.

Small neopagan and reconstructionist communities also worship Anahita independently. Their rites are modern reconstructions drawing on *Yašt* 5, comparative religion, feminism, and contemporary devotional practice.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and national culture

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century archaeology, translation, and Iranian nationalism made Anahita a visible emblem of pre-Islamic Iran. **Anahita/Nahid** became a widespread female personal name. Archaeological sites at Kangavar and Bishapur were popularly branded her temples even where the identifications remain unproved.

Modern statues and illustrations often combine:

- Avestan crown and chariot imagery;
- Greco-Roman drapery;
- exposed water vessels;
- lions or Venus symbols derived from Ištar;
- modern nationalist costume.

No single surviving ancient image authorizes this composite.

### Occult and neopagan literature

Anahita appears in modern goddess encyclopedias, ceremonial magic, eclectic witchcraft, and devotional reconstruction. Frequent modern correspondences include Friday, Venus, silver, blue or green, pearls, fertility spells, sexual liberation, and “divine feminine” psychology. Venus has a genuine Middle Persian basis; most colors, crystals, weekdays, tarot cards, and ritual recipes do not.

Assertions that she was:

- the virgin mother of Mithra;
- a universal “Great Mother” behind Mary, Aphrodite, Sarasvatī, and all water goddesses;
- leader of a prehistoric matriarchy;
- served by universal sacred prostitutes;
- identical without qualification to Ištar, Nana, Artemis, and Sarasvatī;

are modern harmonizations or inventions unless explicitly presented as limited comparisons.

No evidence connects Anahita specifically with the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn’s original grade system. Later occultists may incorporate her, but that is successor eclecticism.

### Literature and popular media

Her name and image occur in Iranian and diaspora poetry, historical fiction, games, comics, fantasy art, and commercial branding. These uses typically favor the warrior-water-goddess synthesis. They matter for reception but are not sources for antiquity.

Manya Saadi-Nejad’s *Anāhitā: A History and Reception of the Iranian Water Goddess* grew from a 2019 dissertation and was published in 2021. It is the most sustained recent study of her transformation and reception, though its strong Mesopotamian-origin thesis remains contestable. ([Dissertation record and summary](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/24610))

### Political appropriation

Anahita can function as a secular-nationalist emblem of Iranian antiquity, a feminist symbol, or an alternative to Islamic public imagery. Such uses are modern political acts. Claims that a particular modern protest, flag, or women’s movement officially “belongs to Anahita” require direct evidence and should not be inferred from the goddess’s sex or Iranian origin.

There is no well-documented, large extremist cult centered on Anahita. Some ethno-nationalist media deploy simplified “Aryan goddess” imagery; its racialized primordialism is modern and is not supported by the plural Avestan, Mesopotamian, Armenian, and Hellenistic history.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Iranian river or Mesopotamian goddess?

Three broad models recur:

1. **Indo-Iranian river model:** Arədvī is an inherited celestial river comparable to Sarasvatī; Anāhitā is an epithet.
2. **Syncretistic model:** an Iranian river divinity fused with a western-Iranian or Mesopotamian goddess resembling Ištar/Nanaya, acquiring anthropomorphic, martial, royal, sexual, and Venus traits.
3. **Direct Mesopotamian-origin model:** the goddess is fundamentally Annunitum/Antu adopted under Artaxerxes II.

The second presently accommodates the evidence most economically, but the proportions and date of fusion remain disputed. The first explains cosmic hydrology; the third highlights Achaemenid Babylonian politics but risks underestimating Iranian linguistic and ritual material.

### Anahita and Sarasvatī

Lommel proposed a close identity and sometimes reconstructed an Iranian *Harahvatī*. Similarities in water, fertility, learning, and celestial-river mythology are real. The exact etymological equation and proposed replacement of an original name are not secure. Functional comparison is stronger than proof of a single recoverable goddess.

### Herodotus’s “Mitra”

Herodotus’s statement that Persians call Aphrodite Urania “Mitra” has been read literally, emended, or explained as confusion. Because Mithra is grammatically and traditionally male in Iranian evidence while the described foreign goddess is female, many specialists think Herodotus conflated two prominent Iranian cults. It cannot securely date an Anahita cult by name to Herodotus’s lifetime.

### Did Artaxerxes II introduce her?

Berossus says he established statues. Earlier kings speak collectively of “other gods,” and administrative evidence shows a diverse Achaemenid religious world. Therefore:

- first **named royal attestation** under Artaxerxes II: secure;
- first **imperially sponsored image cult** under him: probable;
- first-ever worship of the being under him: unproved.

### Kangavar and Bishapur

Both “temple” labels entered handbooks before archaeology could verify them. Kangavar’s visible structure is probably late Sasanian and of uncertain function; Bishapur’s hydraulic building is compatible with but not demonstrably dedicated to Anahita. These are exemplary cases of a literary identification hardening into tourist certainty.

### Sasanian images

The Narseh relief and Taq-e Bostan remain plausible identifications because of investiture, dress, and dynastic context. Other female images require case-by-case analysis. “Female Sasanian figure” is not synonymous with “Anahita.”

### Sacred prostitution

Older scholarship accepted Strabo at face value and connected the practice with “Semitic fertility religion.” Recent work treats “sacred prostitution” as a problematic scholarly category and reexamines Greek ethnographic stereotypes. For Anahit, the evidence is one explicit classical report, not a corpus of temple records. Complete denial and uncritical generalization both exceed the evidence.

### Manuscripts and transmission

The Avesta was transmitted orally and then through Sasanian and post-Sasanian scribal traditions. Surviving manuscripts are medieval or later; Geldner’s edition collated that late manuscript evidence. The interval between poetic composition and physical manuscript means that linguistic dating and comparative formulae, not manuscript age, establish relative antiquity.

The Pahlavi books survive still later and preserve selective priestly memory after conquest and institutional loss. Their value lies in Sasanian-derived systematization, not literal contemporaneity with the Achaemenids.

### Major modern scholars

- **James Darmesteter:** influential nineteenth-century translations and late-dating hypotheses; indispensable historically, often superseded philologically.
- **Karl F. Geldner:** foundational critical Avestan edition.
- **Mary Boyce:** major synthesis of Zoroastrian history; especially influential on Anahita’s fusion, Artaxerxes II, image cult, Istakhr, and survivals.
- **Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin, Herman Lommel, Ilya Gershevitch:** comparative and philological models of Iranian religion.
- **Jean Kellens:** textual stratification and Avestan philology.
- **William Malandra:** accessible source-based treatment emphasizing composite development.
- **Prods Oktor Skjærvø:** Avestan language, ritual, and textual transmission.
- **Albert de Jong:** Greek and Latin testimony and critique of classical misunderstandings.
- **Manya Saadi-Nejad:** comprehensive transformation-and-reception study and strong Annunitum thesis.
- **Carol Bier, Prudence Harper, Roman Ghirshman, Massoud Azarnoush, Wolfram Kleiss:** competing art-historical and archaeological identifications.

## 11. Conclusions and source-critical findings

1. The earliest securely dated Anahita is not a Bronze Age figurine or presumed river cult but the named divine protector in Artaxerxes II’s fourth-century-BCE inscriptions.
2. *Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā* preserves an Iranian cosmic-water conception, but the received figure is composite.
3. Image worship, Venus symbolism, warfare, royal investiture, and Artemis/Aphrodite equations belong to historically traceable transformations rather than a timeless identity.
4. Armenian Anahit was a major royal and territorial goddess with her own genealogy and cult titles; she should not be reduced to an unchanged export of *Yašt* 5.
5. Istakhr’s Anahid institution is historically consequential for the Sasanian dynasty. Kangavar and Bishapur are possible, not proven, Anahita temples.
6. Strabo’s temple-sex report must be recorded but cannot establish a universal institution. Human sacrifice to Anahita is not demonstrated.
7. No ancient source makes her Mithra’s virgin mother or supplies her with a consort.
8. Zoroastrian reverence for the waters continues, while modern individualized “goddess worship” reconstructs rather than simply preserves the Achaemenid, Armenian, or Sasanian cults.
