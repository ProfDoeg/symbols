# Vayu-Vata: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the Avestan tradition, **Vayu** is a male *yazata*—a divine being “worthy of worship”—associated with wind, atmospheric space, motion, martial power, and death. **Vāta** is both wind as a natural phenomenon and, in some passages and calendrical contexts, a distinct personified wind-divinity. Middle Persian theology distinguishes **Good Wāy** (*Wāy ī weh*) from **Bad Wāy** (*Wāy ī wattar*), but this fully articulated dual form is later than the Avestan evidence.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The word *vāta-* occurs in Old Avestan *Yasna* 44.4, conventionally assigned to the Old Avestan/Gāthic corpus of approximately the turn of the second to first millennium BCE, but that verse does not securely establish a discrete god named Vayu. The earliest unmistakable Iranian evidence for **Vayu as a worshipped divine personality** is the Young Avestan corpus, above all *Yašt* 15, the Rām Yašt. Its older materials may belong to the early first millennium BCE, but its surviving form is a late oral and editorial compilation; no Avestan manuscript is ancient. The oldest manuscript containing some Yašts is Jm4, dated 1352 CE, while the oldest complete “pure Yašt” codex, F1, dates to 1591. There is no named Vayu or Vāta in the surviving Achaemenid royal inscriptions.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Vayu and Vāta are not originally one hyphenated name

The modern compound **“Vayu-Vata”**, common in popular mythological dictionaries, is potentially misleading. Indo-Iranian possessed two inherited wind words and divine names:

- Avestan **vayu-**, corresponding exactly to Vedic Sanskrit **vāyú-**.
- Avestan **vāta-**, corresponding to Vedic **vā́ta-**.

In the Avesta, *Vayu* is consistently a proper name. *Vāta* can mean the physical wind and can also be invoked as a divine power. The Zoroastrian calendar assigns the twenty-first day to Rāman and the twenty-second to Vāta/Wād, yet the hymn conventionally called the Rām Yašt chiefly praises Vayu. Thus “Vayu-Vata” is best retained only as a dossier heading for a historically related pair, not treated as an attested Avestan double name. [Malandra, “Vāyu”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/vayu/)

### Forms in relevant languages and scripts

- **Avestan:** *vayu-*; accusative forms include transmitted *vaēm*, understood as a corruption of expected \**vayum*. The Avestan-script form varies by inflection and manuscript.
- **Avestan:** *vāta-* / Gāthic *vāta-*; wind, sometimes personified.
- **Vedic Sanskrit:** वायु, **Vāyu**; वात, **Vāta**.
- **Middle Persian:** **Wāy** or **Wāy/Wāy-bē**; Pahlavi spellings are heterographic and vary by edition.
- **Middle Persian:** **Wād**, “wind,” continuing *vāta-*.
- **Parthian:** *wyw*, Vayu; *wād*, wind.
- **Sogdian:** *wyšprkr*, conventionally vocalized **Wēšparkar/Wišparkar**; explained by Humbach as an Eastern Iranian continuation of *Vayuš uparō-kairyō*.
- **Khotanese:** *bāta-*, wind.
- **Bactrian coin legends:** **OAΔO**, *Oado*, generally identified with Iranian Vāta/Wād; **ΟΗϷΟ**, *Oēšo/Wēšo*, connected by a major line of scholarship with Vayu/Wēšparkar, although its iconography is strongly Śaiva.
- **Modern Persian:** وایو, *Vāyu*; وای, *Vāy*; باد, *bād*, ordinary “wind.”

### Etymology

**Secure comparative linguistics:** *Vayu* derives from Indo-Iranian \**vāyu-*, built on the Indo-European verbal root conventionally reconstructed as \**h₂weh₁-*, “blow.” *Vāta* continues a participial formation from the same root, approximately “that which blows” or “blowing.”

**Avestan learned etymology:** In *Yašt* 15.43–44, Vayu explains three names—*Vayu*, *apayate*, and *vanō.vīspå*—by saying that he “pursues,” “reaches,” or “conquers” both creations, that made by Spənta Mainyu and that made by Angra Mainyu. The passage is theological wordplay, not historical etymology. Its irregular forms suggest that a late compiler converted words extracted from other contexts into a name-list without fully understanding their grammar. [Rām Yašt 15.43–44](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt15.htm)

### Principal epithets

The most characteristic epithet is:

- **uparō.kairya-**, “of superior skill,” “whose action is above,” or “working on high.” The exact nuance remains disputed. It later contributed to the Sogdian name Wēšparkar.

*Yašt* 15 also calls or represents Vayu as:

- **uγra-**, strong or formidable;
- **aurva-**, swift;
- **taxma-**, vigorous, valiant, or fleet;
- **pərəθu.wara-**, broad-chested;
- **pərəθu.sraoni-**, broad-hipped;
- **bərəzi.pāδa-**, high-stepping;
- **uskāt̰.yāsta-**, high-girdled;
- **zaranyō.xaoδa-**, golden-helmeted;
- armed with a broad, sharp, impetuous spear;
- possessing golden weapons and a golden-wheeled chariot.

The additional list in *Yašt* 15.45–48 is textually difficult. Malandra argues that several supposed “names” are inflected words or verbs misread as citation forms by the redactor. They should not all be converted uncritically into independent ancient cult titles.

### Classification through time

- **Indo-Iranian reconstruction:** two cognate wind-divinities, Vayu and Vāta. This is a modern linguistic reconstruction supported by the Iranian and Vedic cognates.
- **Young Avesta:** Vayu is a mighty, worship-worthy male divine being; Vāta is wind and a calendrical divinity.
- **Avestan mortuary passages:** Vayu can be the unavoidable power carrying away the dying.
- **Middle Persian books:** Vayu is systematically divided into Good Wāy and Bad Wāy.
- **Eastern Iranian syncretism:** forms related to Vayu are joined to Śaiva imagery and, among Manichaeans, to the cosmic Living Spirit.
- **Modern popular mythology:** frequently compressed into a single “dual-natured god Vayu-Vata.” That description is a modern synthesis, not the wording of the primary sources.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Old Avestan evidence

*Yasna* 44.4 asks who established the course of sun and stars and “through whom the wind blows.” Its *vāta-* belongs to the inherited wind vocabulary, but the verse does not provide a myth, genealogy, image, or secure cult-personality. It is therefore the earliest occurrence of the relevant Iranian word, not the earliest fully documented Vayu cult.

Old Avestan is several centuries older than most Young Avestan material, although exact absolute dates remain disputed. A cautious range is approximately 1200–900 BCE for the Gāthic compositions, with continued oral transmission thereafter. [Kellens, “Avesta”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/)

### Young Avestan corpus

#### *Yašt* 15: the Rām Yašt

This is the central dossier text: 57 stanzas arranged in *kardah*s. Its present form is composite.

Stanzas 2–41 present a succession of worshippers who sacrifice to Vayu and request boons:

- Ahura Mazda;
- Haošyaŋha;
- Taxma Urupi;
- Yima;
- Aži Dahāka;
- Θraētaona;
- Kərəsāspa;
- Aurvasāra;
- Hutaosā;
- unmarried maidens.

The hymn follows a structure also found in the Ābān Yašt but is not simply copied from it; both likely drew on circulating oral formulae. The divine response is morally selective: legitimate figures receive their boons, while Aži Dahāka does not.

Stanzas 42–57 supply self-declared names, epithets, physical attributes, and circumstances in which Vayu should be invoked. The redactor appears not always to have understood the inherited language. The text is consequently evidence both for ancient Vayu traditions and for later priestly compilation.

#### Other Avestan passages

- **Sīh Rōzag 1–2:** associates Vayu with Rāman, Space, and forms of Time. It preserves the formula worshipping “that [aspect] of Vayu which belongs to Spənta Mainyu.”
- **Yasna 6.2:** invokes Miθra followed by Rāman, showing a liturgical connection between Miθra and Rāman rather than directly explaining Vayu.
- **Vendīdād 5.8–9:** “Astō.vīδōtu binds him; Vayu leads him bound.” The Pahlavi commentary identifies this carrier with Bad Wāy, though an alternative gloss says Good Wāy bears all.
- **Aogəmadaēcā 78–81:** escape is possible from many dangers but not from “merciless Vayu”; the meaning is inescapable death. [Aogəmadaēcā](https://www.avesta.org/fragment/aogsbe.htm)
- **Nyāyišn 1:** includes Vayu among invoked cosmic powers; the transmitted accusative *vayąn* is grammatically problematic.
- **Yašt 15.5 and related liturgies:** worship explicitly restricts itself to Vayu’s Spənta-Mainyu-associated side.

### Middle Persian corpus

#### *Bundahišn*

The *Bundahišn*, compiled from Sasanian-period learning but surviving in post-conquest recensions, supplies the fullest cosmological systematization.

- **1.4:** between Ohrmazd and Ahreman lay the Void, “which some call Wāy.”
- **1.26:** Ohrmazd fashions the form of Good Wāy because Wāy is required as an instrument of creation.
- **26.28–30:** Rām is identified with Good Wāy of long dominion; he is chief of the spiritual warriors, takes the righteous soul by the hand after the Chinvat crossing, and receives and comforts the life taken from the body by Evil Wāy.
- **27.43–44:** Astwihād/Bad Wāy takes the life-breath. His touch brings lethargy, his shadow fever, and his gaze death.

This is a later canonical elaboration: the Avestan ambivalence has become a formal dualism coordinated with Ohrmazd’s cosmic sovereignty. [Greater Bundahišn 26](https://www.avesta.org/mp/grb12.htm)

#### *Mēnōg ī Xrad*

This ninth-century or earlier Middle Persian wisdom text describes the soul’s arrival at the Chinvat Bridge on the fourth dawn with the cooperation of righteous Srōš, Good Wāy, and victorious Wahrām. Its demon Vīzarš binds the wicked soul and brings it, against Srōš’s opposition, to judgment by Rašn. Good Wāy is thus part of the psychopompic escort, but he is not the judge. [Mēnōg ī Xrad](https://avesta.org/mp/mx.html)

#### *Dēnkard*

The ninth-century *Dēnkard* preserves summaries of the lost Sasanian Avesta and priestly theology. It is valuable for reconstructing the canon but supplies no independent early Vayu biography. References to cosmic wind and the functions of *yazads* belong to learned systematization.

#### *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*

The surviving Middle Persian vision of heaven and hell elaborates the Chinvat judgment, with Srōš, Ādur, Rašn, Dēn, and Vīzarš prominent. Vayu is not a central speaking or acting figure in its surviving narrative. Claims that it makes Vayu “judge of the bridge” are therefore unsupported.

### Achaemenid and Classical evidence

The Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam, and other royal inscriptions repeatedly name Ahuramazda and, in later royal texts, other gods collectively or individually. **They do not name Vayu or Vāta.** They therefore cannot establish an Achaemenid state cult of Vayu.

Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131–32, says that Persians sacrificed to natural cosmic entities and reports worship of winds. This is valuable fifth-century BCE testimony for Persian wind-cult in general, but Herodotus gives no Iranian name and cannot securely identify the recipient as Avestan Vayu rather than winds collectively or Vāta. [Herodotus 1.131–32](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/149/herodotus-on-the-customs-of-the-persians/)

Plutarch’s *De Iside et Osiride* 46–47 preserves a Greek account of Iranian dualism, Ohrmazd and Areimanios, and their creations. It does not provide a secure independent account of Vayu.

### Vedic cognates

The *Ṛgveda* preserves Vāyu as an important Soma-drinking god:

- **RV 1.134:** Vāyu comes in a chariot with his *niyut* teams and receives the first drink of Soma.
- **RV 4.48:** another whole hymn to Vāyu.
- Joint invocations of **Indra-Vāyu** occur repeatedly.
- Vāta receives distinct hymns, especially **RV 10.168** and 10.186.

The family book containing RV 4.48 belongs to an older stratum than the final compilation, conventionally placed in the later second millennium BCE; absolute dating remains debated because the text was composed and transmitted orally. The Vedic evidence proves common Indo-Iranian inheritance, not that the Iranian myths were copied from India or vice versa. [Ṛgveda 1.134 text](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/rigveda/RV01)

### Manuscript transmission

The Avesta was orally composed and transmitted. A Sasanian-period written recension was later substantially lost. Surviving manuscripts are medieval or early modern:

- K7a, traditionally dated 1288, is the oldest extant Avestan manuscript cited by Kellens.
- Jm4, 1352, is the oldest Khordeh Avesta containing some Yašts.
- F1, copied at Navsari in 1591, is the principal oldest complete pure-Yašt codex.

Consequently, dating *Yašt* 15 depends on language, metre, formulaic relations, and religious history—not on an ancient physical manuscript. [“Yašts,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

#### Wind and motion

The inherited core is moving air. Vayu is the purposeful divine wind; Vāta more readily denotes the blowing phenomenon. The distinction was never absolute, and later texts sometimes merge their functions.

#### Atmospheric space

Middle Persian evidence expands Wāy from wind into the **intermediate atmospheric void or space** between the realms of Ohrmazd and Ahreman. This is not securely stated in the Gāthās. It is a later cosmological development, perhaps preserving older, otherwise lost conceptions.

#### War and sovereignty

*Yašt* 15 presents a heavily armed, chariot-driving warrior. The *Bundahišn* consequently makes Good Wāy chief of the spiritual warriors and patron of the *artēštārān*, the warrior estate.

#### Life-breath and death

Wind and breath are naturally associated. In the mortuary texts Bad Wāy removes the *gyān*, the life-breath; Good Wāy receives the righteous soul or life after death. Aogəmadaēcā’s “merciless Vayu” is death’s inescapable course.

#### Cosmic mediation

Because Vayu ranges over “both creations,” he occupies a space not straightforwardly confined to good or evil. Zoroastrian liturgy solves the danger by worshipping only his aspect belonging to Spənta Mainyu. Middle Persian authors solve it by dividing him into Good and Bad Wāy.

### Avestan appearance

The Rām Yašt’s recognizable figure is:

- male;
- broad-chested and broad-hipped;
- high-stepping and tightly/high girdled;
- golden-helmeted;
- clothed in golden cape, necklace, vestment, shoes, and girdle;
- mounted in a golden chariot with golden wheels;
- carrying golden weapons and a broad-bladed sharp spear.

No surviving Achaemenid sculpture can securely be labeled this Vayu.

### Kushan representations

Coins of Kanishka I, Huvishka, and successors depict:

- **Oado**, usually a bearded wind-god with windblown hair or garment;
- **Wesho/Oēšo**, commonly shown with the bull and attributes associated with Śiva.

Wesho was long treated simply as Śiva. Humbach and subsequent Iranian-oriented interpretations connect the name with Vayu/Wēšparkar, while acknowledging Indian Śaiva iconography. Recent numismatic work describes Kushan imagery as iconographically eclectic and cautions against imagining either a purely Indian or purely Zoroastrian pantheon. [Kushan coinage](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kushan-dynasty-04/)

### Sogdian Wēšparkar

At sites such as Panjikent and Bunjikat, an armed, sometimes bow-bearing god identified as Wēšparkar appears in early medieval visual culture. The identification rests on Eastern Iranian names, comparative iconography, and Manichaean evidence, not on an inscription attached to every image. The frequently reproduced Bunjikat painting, now in Tajikistan’s National Museum of Antiquities, dates approximately to the eighth or ninth century.

### Unattested modern attribute lists

No early Iranian source assigns Vayu a fixed sacred color, number, gemstone, zodiacal sign, plant, or modern weekday. Gold is repeatedly part of his Yaštic equipment, but turning “gold” into a universal occult correspondence is modern system-building.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The Iranian sources give Vayu **no securely attested parents, spouse, siblings, or biological offspring**. Vedic literature develops a separate Indian Vāyu genealogy and later makes Vāyu father of Bhīma and Hanumān; those are histories of the Indian cognate, not missing chapters of Iranian Vayu’s family.

Vayu’s important relations are cosmological and functional:

- he moves through both good and evil creations;
- Good Wāy is fashioned or subordinated by Ohrmazd in the *Bundahišn*;
- Bad Wāy is coordinated or identified with Astwihād;
- Good Wāy is identified with Rām;
- Vayu shares temporal-spatial epithets with Zurvan;
- Good Wāy assists the soul at Chinvat alongside Srōš and Wahrām.

### Every documented divine interaction or equation

- **Ahura Mazda/Ohrmazd — supplication:** Ahura Mazda worships Vayu and asks the power to strike Angra Mainyu’s creatures without harming Spənta Mainyu’s creation; Vayu grants the boon. *Yašt* 15.2–5, Young Avestan, composite early first-millennium BCE tradition. **Tier: securely attested Young Avestan myth.**
- **Spənta Mainyu — jurisdiction over creation:** Vayu says he pursues or reaches the creation made by Spənta Mainyu; worshippers invoke only his Spənta-associated side. *Yašt* 15.4–5, 43–44. **Tier: Young Avestan canonical formulation.**
- **Angra Mainyu — jurisdiction and opposition:** Vayu ranges through Angra Mainyu’s creation; Ahura Mazda asks Vayu’s help to strike it. *Yašt* 15.3–5, 43–44. **Tier: Young Avestan canonical formulation.**
- **Amesha Spentas — service:** Vayu declares that he does good for Ahura Mazda and the Amesha Spentas. *Yašt* 15.44. **Tier: Young Avestan learned/name-list section.**
- **Aži Dahāka — rejected petition:** the destructive dragon worships Vayu and asks for universal rule, but Vayu refuses him. *Yašt* 15.19–21. **Tier: securely attested Young Avestan mythic episode.**
- **Rāman/Rām — identification:** Middle Persian theology equates Rām, giver of peace or delight, with Good Wāy of long dominion. *Bundahišn* 26.28–30; anticipated by their liturgical/calendrical association. **Tier: later canonical systematization.**
- **Astō.vīδōtu/Astwihād — joint death-action or identification:** Astō.vīδōtu binds the dying and Vayu leads him bound; Middle Persian interpretation treats Astwihād and Bad Wāy as closely coordinated and at points effectively identical. *Vendīdād* 5.8–9; *Bundahišn* 27.43–44. **Tier: Avestan mortuary tradition plus later systematization.**
- **Zurvan — cosmic association:** Vayu and Zurvan share “long-dominion” language and are invoked with Space and Infinite Time in the Sīh Rōzag. No narrative meeting is recorded. **Tier: Avestan liturgical association; later Zurvanite interpretation disputed.**
- **Srōš/Sraoša — joint psychopompic work:** Good Wāy and righteous Srōš cooperate in bringing the soul to Chinvat; Srōš remains the principal protector. *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115–24. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**
- **Wahrām/Vərəθraγna — joint psychopompic appearance:** Good Wāy, Srōš, and strong Wahrām assist at the soul’s fourth-dawn journey. *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**
- **Rašn/Rashnu — same judgment sequence:** Good Wāy assists the soul’s journey; Rašn judges it. No direct dialogue between them survives. *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115–63. **Tier: later canonical joint sequence.**
- **Vīzarš — opposed functions around the wicked soul:** Vīzarš binds and drags the wicked soul while Srōš opposes him; Vayu’s good form belongs to the contrary escort tradition. No direct Vayu–Vīzarš combat is stated. **Tier: later canonical mortuary system.**
- **Śiva — syncretistic identification:** Kushan Wesho is depicted in Śaiva form, frequently with bull and trident-like attributes. Whether Wesho is “really” Śiva, Iranian Vayu in Śaiva form, or a deliberately polyvalent imperial god remains debated. Second–third centuries CE. **Tier: historical syncretism, not an Avestan equation.**
- **Manichaean Living Spirit — translated identification:** Sogdian Manichaean usage connects Wēšparkar with the Living Spirit, a cosmic being who structures the world from defeated demonic matter. This is an interpretive translation in Central Asian Manichaeism, not an original Zoroastrian myth. **Tier: late antique/early medieval syncretism.**

There is no documented Iranian divine encounter between Vayu and the Vedic Indra, Hanumān, or Bhīma. Those belong to Indian Vāyu’s independent history.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Ahura Mazda’s petition

In *Yašt* 15, Ahura Mazda himself prepares ritual offerings for Vayu in Airyanəm Vaējah by the good Dāityā. He asks permission or power to strike the creatures made by Angra Mainyu while sparing those of Spənta Mainyu. Vayu grants the request.

The episode is the strongest textual basis for claims that Vayu can appear “above” the dual creations: even Ahura Mazda petitions him. But the text does not plainly call Vayu supreme creator. Nyberg’s theory of a pre-Zoroastrian Vayu-centered religious community is a modern reconstruction, not an attested sect. Malandra considers such a background possible; it remains unproved.

### Taxma Urupi’s conquest of Angra Mainyu

Taxma Urupi petitions Vayu to become victorious over demons, sorcerers, and witches and to ride Angra Mainyu, transformed into a horse, around the earth. Vayu grants the boon. The myth’s central act is performed by Taxma, not by Vayu, but Vayu supplies victory.

### Yima’s golden reign

Yima asks Vayu to become the most glorious of mortals and to establish a realm without cold, heat, old age, death, envy, or scarcity. The boon is granted. This is a compact version of the ideal Yima kingship cycle found more extensively elsewhere in the Avesta.

### Aži Dahāka’s refused universal dominion

Aži Dahāka requests power to empty the seven regions of humankind. Unlike the righteous petitioners, he receives nothing. This refusal shows that the hymn’s Vayu is not morally indiscriminate even though he traverses both creations.

### Θraētaona’s victory

Θraētaona asks to defeat the three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed Aži Dahāka and rescue the women Saŋhawāc and Arənawāc. Vayu grants the boon. Later Persian tradition transforms Θraētaona into Ferēdūn and Dahāka into Żaḥḥāk, but Ferdowsi does not retain Vayu as an independently named patron in the episode.

### Kərəsāspa’s vengeance

Kərəsāspa asks Vayu for the strength to avenge his brother Urvāxšaya by killing Hitāspa and other hostile beings, including the aquatic Gandarəwa. Vayu grants the request.

### Aurvasāra and Kavi Haosravah

Aurvasāra asks that no warrior kill him until Kavi Haosravah does so in battle. The unusual request is granted, and Haosravah eventually kills him. The episode reflects heroic-cycle fatalism rather than a broad cult doctrine.

### Hutaosā’s household status

Hutaosā asks to be loved, welcomed, and acknowledged in Kavi Vištāspa’s house. Vayu grants her request. This links the hymn to the Vištāspa/Zarathustra legendary environment, though Zarathustra is not himself a petitioner in this Yašt.

### The maidens’ marriage prayer

Unmarried maidens worship Vayu on golden seats with ritual offerings and ask for young, handsome, well-providing husbands and children. He grants the boon. This is direct evidence that Vayu’s ritual usefulness extended beyond war and kingship to marriage and household continuity.

### The two Wāys and the soul

The fully narrated two-Wāy doctrine belongs to Middle Persian books:

1. Bad Wāy or Astwihād strikes the life from the body.
2. The soul remains near the body for three nights in the standard Zoroastrian mortuary scheme.
3. On the fourth dawn, Good Wāy assists its journey with Srōš and Wahrām.
4. After the righteous soul crosses Chinvat, Good Wāy takes its hand and brings it to its proper place.
5. Good Wāy receives even the life removed by Bad Wāy and gives it contentment.

This is later canonical elaboration of the Avestan contrast between beneficent Vayu and feared death-wind.

### The primordial Void

The *Bundahišn* says the interval between Ohrmazd and Ahreman was the Void, “which some call Wāy.” It then has Ohrmazd create or shape Good Wāy as an instrument of creation. The phrasing “some call” preserves awareness of competing cosmologies. A plausible scholarly inference is that the author was subordinating an older or Zurvanite cosmic Wāy to Ohrmazd; this cannot be demonstrated as the creed of a historical Vayu sect.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ritual form

*Yašt* 15 repeatedly describes worship on a golden seat, golden cushion, and golden carpet, beneath a golden canopy, with spread *baresman*, libations, milk, Haoma, and correctly spoken ritual words. These are poetic-liturgical descriptions and cannot automatically be read as inventories of ordinary temples.

The standard refrain invokes only the Vayu associated with Spənta Mainyu. This restriction is crucial: worship does not address an undifferentiated good-and-evil god.

### Calendar

- Day 21: **Rām/Rāman**.
- Day 22: **Wād/Vāta**.

Vayu appears under Rām’s day in the Sīh Rōzag rather than under Vāta’s. The Rām Yašt likely came to serve both adjacent calendrical powers. The historical reason for the Rāman–Vayu fusion remains obscure.

### Mortuary rites

A *drōn* service may be performed for Wāy, Rašn, Aštād, Srōš, and the *fravašis* before dawn following the third night after death and during *Frawardīgān*. This corresponds to the soul’s fourth-dawn approach to judgment. [“Drōn,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/dron/)

### Sacrifice

The primary texts mention Haoma, milk, libation, *baresman*, and sacrificial worship. Ancient Iranian religion also included animal sacrifice, and such sacrifice survived regionally among Zoroastrians, but there is **no evidence specific to Vayu for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or a specialized sacrificial priesthood**. Assertions of a Vayu human-sacrifice cult are unsupported.

### Temples and priesthood

No securely excavated Achaemenid, Parthian, or Sasanian temple can be identified as a temple dedicated solely to Vayu. No distinctive hereditary “priesthood of Vayu” is documented. Ritual would have been conducted by the ordinary Mazdayasnian priesthood.

Eastern Iranian images of Oado, Wesho, or Wēšparkar attest public or elite religious representation, but not necessarily Zoroastrian temples in the later orthodox sense. Sogdian sanctuary and palace art belongs to religious environments where local Iranian, Buddhist, Hindu, and Manichaean forms interacted.

### Theophoric names and popularity

Compared with Mithra, Vərəθraγna, or Anāhitā, securely identified Vayu-based personal names are uncommon. This may indicate lower use in personal naming, but the difficult transmission of Eastern Iranian names makes quantitative conclusions unsafe.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Proto-Indo-Iranian period

Comparative linguistics reconstructs \**Vāyu-* and \**Vāta-* before the Iranian and Indo-Aryan linguistic separation, probably in the second millennium BCE. The shared distinction between Vayu and Vāta is secure; detailed reconstructed myths are not.

### Old Avestan period

Gāthic *vāta-* shows the wind term was present. There is no developed Vayu mythology in the surviving Gāthās. Absence may reflect Zarathustra’s theological focus or the tiny surviving corpus; it cannot prove rejection of Vayu.

### Young Avestan period

Vayu became the subject of a major hymn. The older core presents an Iranian heroic patron and giver of victory; later editorial sections collect magical-theological names and epithets. The hymn’s repeated limitation to the Spənta-associated Vayu acknowledges a dangerous side without yet creating two independent personalities.

### Achaemenid period

Herodotus reports Persian worship of winds, but royal inscriptions do not name Vayu. Direct identification of Herodotus’s winds with *Yašt* 15’s Vayu is possible but unproved.

### Hellenistic, Parthian, and Kushan periods

In eastern Iran and the Kushan realm, Iranian wind deities entered a multilingual imperial visual system. Oado represents wind more transparently; Wesho combines an Iranian divine name with Śaiva iconography. The coins are material evidence of royal representation, not proof that every Kushan subject held one syncretistic theology.

### Sasanian period

Priestly compilation and commentary increasingly systematized Vayu:

- the cosmic void was subordinated to Ohrmazd;
- Good and Bad Wāy were distinguished;
- Rām was equated with Good Wāy;
- Vayu acquired an explicit place in the afterlife itinerary and warrior hierarchy.

Zurvanite ideas may have encouraged association of Vayu/Space with Time, but the boundaries of “Zurvanism” remain disputed.

### Sogdian and Manichaean Central Asia

Wēšparkar flourished in early medieval Sogdian art. Manichaeans used the name in translating or interpreting their Living Spirit. This was a creative syncretistic continuation, not evidence that the Avestan Vayu had always been equivalent to the Manichaean being.

### Islamic conquest and medieval Iran

Zoroastrian institutions contracted after the Arab conquests. There is no documented episode of a Vayu temple being individually destroyed or converted. The figure survived in Zoroastrian liturgy and Pahlavi literature, while Persian **bād** remained ordinary wind.

Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed about 1010, preserves many heroes who petition Vayu in the Yašt—Jamšid/Yima, Żaḥḥāk/Aži Dahāka, Ferēdūn/Θraētaona, Garšāsp/Kərəsāspa—but ordinarily omits Vayu as their named divine patron. Claims that Vayu is an active war-god throughout the *Šāhnāma* generally confuse metaphorical wind with a surviving divine character.

### Parsi and Iranian Zoroastrian continuity

The Rām Yašt and calendrical names remained in manuscript and liturgical traditions. This constitutes textual and ritual continuity, although there is little evidence of a distinct modern Vayu-centered cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Orientalist recovery

The modern scholarly Vayu was reconstructed through:

- nineteenth-century Avestan editions and translations by Darmesteter, Mills, and Geldner;
- comparative Vedic-Iranian philology;
- discovery and classification of Kushan coins;
- twentieth-century studies by Wikander, Dumézil, Nyberg, Humbach, Boyce, Malandra, Kellens, and Skjærvø.

James Darmesteter’s translations remain historically important but frequently rely heavily on Pahlavi interpretation. Geldner’s 1889–96 edition remains foundational, although newer manuscript work and the Avestan Digital Archive have exposed limitations in its apparatus.

### Comparative mythology

Stig Wikander’s *Vayu* and Georges Dumézil’s tripartite theories made Vayu important in Indo-European comparative religion. Dumézil proposed connections between the two Vayus and Zarathustra’s two *mainyus*. This is ingenious modern reconstruction, not an Avestan statement. Martin Schwartz instead treated the two *mainyus* through the semantics of *manyu-* and regarded Vayu’s division as a parallel rather than its direct source.

Nyberg proposed a pre-Zoroastrian Vayu-centered religious community. The hypothesis explains Vayu’s apparent supra-dual power in *Yašt* 15 but lacks direct inscriptions, temples, or self-identifying adherents.

### Occult and neopagan reception

Unlike Mithra, Anāhitā, or Ahriman, Iranian Vayu did not acquire a substantial Renaissance Hermetic or Golden Dawn tradition. Modern occult correspondence lists assigning him planets, tarot cards, colors, stones, or compass directions are recent inventions unless explicitly borrowed from Indian Vāyu.

Small internet-based Iranian-polytheist and Indo-European reconstructionist communities sometimes worship Vayu. Their existence is documented informally, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes their scale or continuity from ancient cult. They are modern reconstructionist movements.

### Popular culture

Modern websites and mythological lists usually describe “Vayu-Vata” as one dual god who stands neutrally between good and evil. This compresses three historical layers:

1. inherited Vayu and Vāta;
2. the Yašt’s worship of Vayu’s beneficent side;
3. the Pahlavi division into Good and Bad Wāy.

The resulting profile is intelligible but historically synthetic.

Modern fantasy games occasionally use “Vayu” as a wind elemental or summon, almost always drawing more heavily on Indian Vāyu or generic air magic than on the Iranian Rām Yašt. No single modern literary, film, or game representation has demonstrably replaced the traditional Zoroastrian image at a mass-cultural scale.

### Political appropriation

Vayu is occasionally included in Iranian nationalist lists of “ancient Persian gods.” Such lists often detach him from Avestan language, Zoroastrian liturgy, and eastern Iranian geography. There is no well-documented major extremist movement organized specifically around Vayu.

## 9. Historiography and Contested Material

### Is Vayu morally neutral?

**Evidence for an above-dual or ambivalent figure:**

- he reaches both creations;
- Ahura Mazda petitions him;
- he can be “merciless” death;
- he occupies the interval between Ohrmazd and Ahreman.

**Evidence for Zoroastrian moral division:**

- worship is explicitly directed only toward his Spənta-associated aspect;
- he refuses Aži Dahāka;
- Pahlavi theologians divide Good Wāy from Bad Wāy.

Calling Vayu “neutral” is therefore a scholarly shorthand, not a canonical Avestan definition.

### One god or two?

Three models must be distinguished:

- Vayu and Vāta were inherited distinct but overlapping wind powers.
- Vayu himself had beneficent and dangerous aspects.
- Middle Persian theology divided Wāy into Good and Bad forms.

“Vayu-Vata, the two-faced god” combines all three and is too smooth historically.

### Did Vayu precede or outrank Ahura Mazda?

*Yašt* 15 dramatizes Ahura Mazda petitioning Vayu. Nyberg inferred an older Vayu-centered sect; Malandra notes that Vayu may once have possessed a universality beyond the two creations. No independent document proves that worshippers historically proclaimed Vayu supreme over Ahura Mazda. The claim remains a modern reconstruction.

### Was Wesho Vayu or Śiva?

The Bactrian name and Sogdian continuations support an Iranian Vayu/Wēšparkar connection. The bull, trident, multiple faces or arms, and other attributes are unmistakably Śaiva. The strongest historical model is an intentionally syncretistic or polyvalent deity, not an exclusive either/or identification.

### Is Wēšparkar’s etymology secure?

Humbach derived Sogdian *wyšprkr* from *Vayuš uparō-kairyō*. The proposal is influential and explains the otherwise difficult name, but it is a historical-linguistic reconstruction rather than a bilingual ancient gloss.

### Vayu and Zurvanism

The shared epithet “long-dominion,” joint invocation of Space and Time, and the *Bundahišn*’s primordial Void allow a Zurvanite interpretation. Yet “Zurvanism” itself may cover several doctrines rather than one church. It is unsafe to present Vayu as the fixed spatial half of a universally accepted Zurvanite Space-Time pair.

### Human sacrifice, sexual cult, and temple violence

No primary or archaeological evidence establishes:

- human sacrifice to Vayu;
- temple prostitution in his service;
- a Vayu-specific cult militia;
- forced conversion conducted in his name;
- destruction of an individually identified Vayu temple.

Zoroastrian and broader Iranian traditions did practice animal sacrifice in some periods and communities, but this must not be inflated into unattested Vayu-specific rites. [“Sacrifice in Zoroastrianism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sacrifice-i/)

### State of the evidence

The evidence is radically uneven:

- no ancient Vayu temple inscription;
- no Achaemenid naming;
- no ancient Avestan manuscript;
- one composite but substantial Yašt;
- scattered mortuary references;
- extensive later Pahlavi systematization;
- important but contested eastern Iranian coins and paintings.

The loss of most of the Sasanian Avesta means absence from the surviving corpus is not proof that no additional Vayu traditions once existed.

## 10. Chronology

- **Second millennium BCE:** Proto-Indo-Iranian \**Vāyu-* and \**Vāta-* are reconstructed from matching Iranian and Vedic forms. **Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **Approximately 1500–1000 BCE:** Vedic hymns to Vāyu and Vāta are composed orally; exact dates remain disputed. **Early cognate evidence.**
- **Approximately 1200–900 BCE:** Old Avestan *Yasna* 44.4 refers to *vāta-*, wind. It does not securely describe the full deity. **Secure early linguistic attestation.**
- **Early first millennium BCE:** older poetic components behind the great Yašts probably develop in eastern Iran. Absolute dating remains uncertain.
- **Approximately seventh–fifth centuries BCE:** major materials now in *Yašt* 15 plausibly circulate orally; later redaction cannot be precisely dated. **Young Avestan evidence.**
- **522–486 BCE:** Darius’s inscriptions name Ahuramazda but not Vayu. **Documented absence.**
- **Fifth century BCE:** Herodotus reports Persian sacrifice to winds, without naming Vayu. **External classical testimony.**
- **First centuries BCE–CE:** Parthian and eastern Iranian forms of Wāy/Vayu continue; direct cult documentation remains sparse.
- **127–230 CE:** Kushan coins depict Oado and Wesho under Kanishka I, Huvishka, and Vasudeva I. **Material/numismatic evidence.**
- **Third–seventh centuries CE:** Sasanian priestly recension and Middle Persian commentary systematize Good and Bad Wāy, Rām, cosmic space, and the afterlife role.
- **Third–eighth centuries CE:** Wēšparkar is adapted in Sogdian and Manichaean environments. **Syncretistic identification.**
- **Sixth–eighth centuries CE:** major Sogdian monumental painting traditions flourish at Panjikent, Bunjikat, and related sites.
- **Seventh–ninth centuries CE:** Islamization reduces Zoroastrian institutional reach in Iran and Central Asia; no Vayu-specific suppression event is documented.
- **Ninth century CE:** *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, and *Mēnōg ī Xrad* reach surviving or near-surviving forms, preserving Sasanian learning.
- **Approximately 1010 CE:** Ferdowsi completes the *Šāhnāma*; Vayu’s former petitioners survive as epic heroes, but Vayu does not retain a comparable named narrative role.
- **1288 CE:** K7a, the oldest usually cited extant Avestan manuscript.
- **1352 CE:** Jm4, oldest Khordeh Avesta manuscript containing some Yašts.
- **1591 CE:** F1, principal oldest complete pure-Yašt codex and chief witness to *Yašt* 15.
- **1880–1898:** Darmesteter, Mills, Geldner, and other Iranists publish influential editions and translations.
- **1938:** Nyberg advances broad reconstructions of ancient Iranian religious communities.
- **1941:** Stig Wikander publishes *Vayu*, making the deity central to comparative Indo-Iranian study.
- **1952:** Dumézil connects the dual Vayu problem with wider Indo-European structural theories.
- **1975:** Humbach argues for the Vayu–Wēšparkar–Wesho connection in eastern Iranian syncretism.
- **Late twentieth–early twenty-first century:** manuscript digitization, renewed numismatic study, and the Avestan Digital Archive improve access and challenge reliance on a single printed edition.
- **2014–15:** William W. Malandra’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* synthesis restates Vayu’s linguistic, cosmic, martial, and mortuary history.
- **Present:** Vayu remains in Zoroastrian texts and calendars and is occasionally revived in small reconstructionist or online devotional settings; no reliable evidence shows a large independent Vayu cult.
