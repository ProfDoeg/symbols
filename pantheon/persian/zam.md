# Zam: Research Dossier

**Class:** *Yazata*—a divine being “worthy of worship”—and the divinized or ritually personified Earth. Avestan *zam-* is grammatically feminine, but Zam has little developed anthropomorphic personality; “earth,” “Earth as a sacred being,” and the later calendrical divinity Zamyād cannot always be separated cleanly.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **Yasna 38.1**, in the Old Avestan *Yasna Haptaŋhāiti*, worships *imąm … ząm*, “this earth here,” as the earth “that bears us.” Its language belongs to the oldest stratum of the Avesta, conventionally placed broadly around the late second or early first millennium BCE, although no absolute date can be demonstrated. The surviving manuscripts are medieval and early-modern copies of a much older oral-liturgical tradition. The fixed divine name **Zamyād/Zāmyād** is later: it is generated by the Younger Avestan calendrical expression “the Munificent Earth-yazata” and is securely transmitted in Middle Persian and Pāzand sources. [S1–S4]

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

Zam is the earth in two overlapping senses:

1. the physical ground, land, or terrestrial world; and  
2. that Earth addressed and worshipped as a sacred being.

The older evidence does not present a richly anthropomorphic “earth goddess” with a biography, image, spouse, and independent mythology. Yasna 38 worships the bearing Earth directly. Later calendrical and priestly texts classify the Earth as a *yazata*, and the phrase denoting the “Munificent Earth-yazata” contracts into the name **Zamyād**. Calling Zam a “goddess” is defensible because *zam-* is feminine and Earth is worshipped, but “female earth-yazata” is more exact than assuming the personality of a Greek-style anthropomorphic goddess.

Zam must also be distinguished from **Spəṇtā Ārmaiti** (Middle Persian **Spandarmad/Spendarmad**), the Bountiful Immortal associated with devotion, fertility, women, and the earth. They overlap in terrestrial function but are textually distinct: Zam is Earth itself or its yazata; Ārmaiti is an abstract-divine power who becomes the guardian or spiritual patron of the material Earth. Later summaries often collapse them into one “earth goddess,” thereby erasing an actual feature of Zoroastrian theology. [S5]

### Names and forms

- **Avestan:** *zam-*; inflected forms include accusative **ząm**, genitive **zəmō**, locative **zəmē/zəmī**. Meaning: “earth, soil, ground, land.”
- **Old Avestan cult phrase:** *imąm … ząm*, “this earth here” (Yasna 38.1).
- **Younger Avestan calendrical forms:**  
  - *zəmō huδåŋhō yazatahe*, approximately “of the beneficent/munificent Earth-yazata”;  
  - *ząm huδåŋhəm yazatəm*, “the beneficent Earth-yazata.”
- **Middle Persian:** *zamīg*, “earth”; the divine/day name appears as **Zāmyād/Zamyād**, written **zʾmyʾt** in the Pahlavi commentary on the *Sīh-rōzag* and **zʾmyʾd** in Manichaean Middle Persian.
- **Pāzand:** *zamyāṱ yazaṱ* in the introductory apparatus transmitted with Yasht 19.
- **New Persian:** **زمین** (*zamīn*), “earth, ground”; **زامیاد** (*Zāmyād*) remains the traditional name of the twenty-eighth day.
- **Modern English spellings:** Zam, Zamyad, Zamyād, Zam Yazad, Zāmyād Yazad.

The root is Indo-European, cognate with Sanskrit *kṣam-/kṣm-*, Greek *khthōn*, Latin *humus*, and Slavic and Baltic words represented by Russian *zemlja* and Lithuanian *žemė*. The lexical etymology “earth” is secure. [S1, S6]

### The disputed formation of *Zamyād*

A widely repeated popular explanation treats **Zamyād** as a transparent contraction of **Zam Yazad**, “the yazata Zam.” That describes how the name was understood by later speakers, but it may not give its exact historical phonology.

Humbach and Ichaporia, followed in the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* account, derive Middle Persian Zā/ămyād from an earlier phrase approximately **\*zā/ăm-huyād yazad < \*zā/ăm huδād yazad**, reflecting Avestan *zam huδåŋh-* “Munificent Earth.” On this analysis, *-yād* is not simply an unaltered *yazad* mechanically attached to *zam*; the contraction developed out of the full epithet. Middle Persian exegetes subsequently resegmented the phrase as *zamīg hudāg*, “beneficent earth,” preserving its meaning through learned interpretation. [S1]

**Evidentiary classification:** lexical *zam-* is securely early; Zamyād as a fixed personal name is later calendrical and priestly systematization.

### Titles and associations

- **“Munificent/beneficent Earth”** (*zam huδåŋh-*): the defining calendrical epithet.
- **“Earth that bears us”**: Yasna 38.1.
- **Yazata of the twenty-eighth day:** *Sīh-rōzag* 1.28 and 2.28.
- **Associated with Mount Uši.darəna, all mountains, the Kavyan Glory, and the Unseized Glory:** the twenty-eighth-day invocation.
- **Later ritual associations:** a flower assigned to Zamyād in the *Bundahišn*; priestly recitation of Yasht 19 in calendrical and fire-consecration sequences.

No ancient source securely gives Zam a throne, weapon, vehicle, consort, offspring, planetary body, or standardized human appearance.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Yasna 38.1: the earliest evidence

The Old Avestan text begins:

> *imąm āat̰ ząm gənābīš haθrā yazō … yā̊ nå baraitī …*

Almut Hintze translates:

> “Now we worship this earth here together with the noblewomen. (We worship the earth) that bears us…”

Humbach similarly translates the Earth “which carries us.” The verse addresses Ahura Mazdā and also worships feminine beings whose precise identity is debated; older translations called them Mazda’s “wives,” while more recent work prefers “women” or “noblewomen.” What is not disputed is that the Earth is the direct object of *yaz-*—ritual worship. [S2–S4]

This is the earliest secure evidence for worshipped Earth in the Zoroastrian textual tradition. It does **not** call her “Zamyād,” supply a genealogy, or narrate a myth.

### Yasna and the wider Avesta

Earth appears frequently as an ordinary cosmological noun—“earth” in opposition to heaven, the terrestrial creation, or the ground on which ritual and human life occur. Such occurrences are not automatically appearances of a distinct person named Zam.

Relevant textual concentrations include:

- **Yasna 37–38:** worship of Mazda’s creations, followed by direct worship of Earth and waters. Old Avestan.
- **Yasna 65.12–13:** plural/vocative forms of *zam-* occur in a ritual context involving the waters.
- **Yasna 68.15:** invokes all that is good and truth-possessing “between earth and heaven.”
- **Yasna 16.3–6:** a Younger Avestan enumeration connected with the thirty divine day names.
- **Sīrōza/Sīh-rōzag 1.28 and 2.28:** the decisive calendrical formula invoking the Munificent Earth-yazata, Mount Uši.darəna, all mountains, the Kavyan Glory, and the Unseized Glory. This is the principal bridge between Earth, day 28, and Yasht 19. [S1, S7]

### Yasht 19: the “Zamyād Yasht”

The extant text has **96 stanzas in fifteen kardes**, but its unity is secondary.

- **Yasht 19.1–8:** a geographical catalogue of mountains rising from Earth.
- **Yasht 19.9–96:** the **Kayān Yasn**, a hymn to *xᵛarənah*, especially the Kavyan or royal Glory, from cosmic creation through the Iranian kings and heroes to Zarathustra, Vištāspa, and the future Saošyant.

The oldest pure-Yasht manuscript is **F1**, copied at Navsari in 1591 CE; another important manuscript is E1 of 1601. The oldest surviving Khordeh Avesta, Jm4, dates to 1352, but Yasht transmission varies among such codices. The centuries separating composition from the manuscripts are therefore substantial. [S7]

The early codices F1 and E1 effectively identify the substantial composition as **Kayān Yasn**, “Worship of the Kayanians,” not as a narrative about an Earth goddess. The modern title “Zamyād Yasht” reflects the calendrical attachment of the text to day 28.

#### Composite formation

Ichaporia and Humbach reconstruct the following development:

1. an older Avestan geographical work once listed lands, mountains, seas, rivers, and lakes;
2. only its mountain section survived in Yasht 19.1–8;
3. related material survives in *Bundahišn* chapters 9–12;
4. the mountain fragment was later attached to the independent *Kayān Yasn*;
5. because the *Sīrōza* invocation joined Earth, Uši.darəna, mountains, and Glory, the combined text came to serve as the hymn for Zamyād’s day.

The absence of kardo divisions in 19.1–8, contrasted with the fifteen kardes beginning at 19.9, supports this composite analysis. [S1]

### Dating

No year or century can be assigned securely to Yasht 19. The language is Younger Avestan, and the work preserves layers of oral poetry. Scholars have variously placed important Yasht material before, during, or around the Achaemenid period. Specific passages may be older than the final compilation.

Old Avestan spellings embedded in the Yima episode at 19.34 suggest an older poetic prehistory. Conversely, the union of the mountain fragment with the *Kayān Yasn* is judged secondary. Claims that “Yasht 19 was written in 500 BCE,” or any similarly exact date, go beyond the evidence.

### Middle Persian corpus

- **Pahlavi *Sīh-rōzag* commentary:** preserves Zāmyād as day 28 and interprets the Avestan formula.
- **Bundahišn:** provides the great later cosmological exposition of Earth and mountains. Earth is a good material creation assaulted by Ahriman; the mountains arise from Earth during this assault and stabilize it. Chapter 9 lists 2,244 mountains, paralleling Yasht 19.
- **Bundahišn’s divine hierarchy:** Zamyād appears among helpers associated with Amurdad, Rashn, Aštād, and the Glories.
- **Šāyest nē Šāyest:** late-Sasanian or early Islamic-period priestly compilation. Later summaries associate Zam with decisiveness or conclusiveness, but this is learned classification, not an early myth.
- **Counsels attributed to Ādurbād ī Mahraspandān:** later calendrical advice warns against taking medicine on Zamyād day.
- **Āfrīn ī Haft Aməšaspand:** Zam, Amurdad, Rashn, and Aštād oppose hunger and thirst; this is later liturgical systematization.
- **Dēnkard 8.15:** summarizes the lost *Bagān Yašt Nask*, including worship of the visible and invisible yazads after whom the days were named. It does not preserve a separate Zam myth. [S7–S9]

The *Mēnōg ī Xrad* and *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag* contain extensive cosmological and eschatological material but no independent Zam narrative comparable to those of major heroic or judicial beings.

### Achaemenid and Classical evidence

No known Achaemenid inscription names **Zam/Zamyād as a personal deity**. Darius’s Naqš-e Rostam inscription DNa says Ahuramazda created “this earth,” while Behistun and related inscriptions repeatedly make Earth the territory over which Mazda grants royal authority. This is evidence for an Achaemenid theology of created Earth, not a named Zam cult. [S10]

Classical testimony is suggestive:

- **Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131–32**, fifth century BCE, says Persians sacrificed to heaven, sun, moon, **Earth**, fire, water, and winds. He describes animal sacrifice at an unpolluted place with a Magus chanting a “theogony.” This is direct external evidence for Persian reverence toward Earth, but “Earth” is Herodotus’s Greek category; he does not record the name Zam.
- **Xenophon, *Cyropaedia* 8.3.24**, fourth century BCE, says Cyrus’s procession sacrificed bulls to Zeus/heaven, horses to the Sun, and then sacrificed “to Earth, as the Magi directed.” The *Cyropaedia* is an idealizing literary work, not an archival report about Cyrus.
- **Plutarch, *Artaxerxes* 23.5**, early second century CE, reports Artaxerxes II praying to “Hera” for Atossa while clutching the earth. Mary Boyce suggested that the goddess understood through *interpretatio graeca* may be Spəṇtā Ārmaiti, not Zam. The passage therefore cannot establish a Zam–Hera equation. [S5, S11–S13]

### Shahnameh and later epic

Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāmeh*, completed around 1010 CE, preserves transformed descendants of many episodes in the *Kayān Yasn*: Jamshid’s loss of glory, Zahhāk, Fereydun, Garshāsp, Afrāsiāb, Kay Khosrow, and the Kayanian kings. It does **not** make Zam a narrative character.

“Zam” must not be confused with **Zahhāk** or Avestan **Aži Dahāka**. Claims that “Zam is the dragon king who killed Jamshid” result from name confusion in modern derivative material.

### Manichaean and Mithraic evidence

Manichaean Middle Persian attests the spelling **zʾmyʾd**, confirming the circulation of the day/divine name in Middle Iranian calendrical vocabulary. No securely identified Manichaean myth makes Zamyād an independent protagonist.

Roman Mithraism supplies no reliable evidence for a Zam cult or iconographic equation. General parallels between Iranian earth, caves, and Mithraic ritual space do not demonstrate identity or transmission.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Secure early material**

- the bearing and sustaining Earth;
- land and soil;
- the terrestrial creation beneath heaven;
- the ground upon which humans, animals, waters, and plants live.

**Later canonical elaboration**

- the twenty-eighth day of each thirty-day month;
- mountains and sacred geography;
- through Yasht 19’s calendrical assignment, association with Kavyan *xᵛarənah* and Iranian salvation history.

Zam does not “govern royal glory” in the straightforward manner that Mithra governs covenants or Tištrya rain. Yasht 19’s Glory is itself a worshipful supernatural power. Zam’s connection with it is a product of the hymn’s composite formation and calendrical liturgy.

### Gender and form

Because *zam-* is grammatically feminine and Yasna 38 places Earth among feminine beings, Zam is commonly described as female. No surviving Avestan text gives Zam a body, clothing, age, face, or transformation. The Earth is her material manifestation, not merely an emblem carried by an otherwise invisible goddess.

### Iconography

No ancient Iranian image is securely identified by inscription or unique attributes as Zam/Zamyād. There is consequently:

- no established anthropomorphic type;
- no securely identified cult statue;
- no recognizable crown, weapon, animal, or throne;
- no temple relief that scholarship can confidently label “Zam.”

Modern illustrations of a beautiful green-robed “Persian Earth goddess” are imaginative reconstructions unless explicitly presented as modern devotional or fantasy art.

### Symbols and natural associations

- **Earth/soil:** intrinsic, securely attested.
- **Mountains:** Yasht 19.1–8 and *Bundahišn* 6C and 9.
- **Uši.darəna/Ušidar:** especially prominent because of its eschatological connection with Saošyant and the Sistan basin.
- **Day:** twenty-eighth.
- **Flower:** the *Bundahišn* plant catalogue in one English rendering assigns Zamyād the **crocus**. Repeated internet claims that Zamyād’s flower is basil conflict with this translation and may reflect variant plant identifications or copying from secondary summaries; “basil” should not be presented as uncontested. [S9]
- **Celestial body, number, color, sacred animal:** none securely attested specifically for Zam. The number 28 is calendrical, not an independently documented mystical number.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

No surviving source gives Zam parents, a birth narrative, spouse, children, or siblings.

The *Bundahišn* places Earth among Ohrmazd’s good material creations and assigns **Spandarmad** responsibility for it. In that theological sense Ohrmazd is Earth’s creator, while Spandarmad is its divine guardian. Neither relationship is a biological genealogy.

### Pantheon position

Zam is a *yazata*, below or outside the sevenfold group of Ahura Mazdā and the Aməša Spəṇtas. The later divine calendar nevertheless guarantees Zam recurrent liturgical status. The *Bundahišn* assigns Zamyād a cooperative place alongside Rashn, Aštād, and the Glories under Amurdad’s grouping—an example of Sasanian or post-Sasanian priestly systematization rather than an early family tree.

### Every documented interaction or specific divine relation

Because Zam has almost no narrative personality, the list is short. Episodes in the *Kayān Yasn* involving Glory, Mithra, Ātar, Aži Dahāka, Apąm Napāt, and others are **not interactions with Zam** merely because they now occur inside “Zamyād Yasht.”

- **Ahura Mazdā — creator of Earth.** Yasna 37 and Achaemenid creation formulas attribute earth’s establishment or creation to Mazda; the *Bundahišn* develops this into a full cosmogony. **Tier:** securely attested cosmological relation, later elaborated.
- **Spəṇtā Ārmaiti/Spandarmad — guardian or spiritual patron of Earth.** The Gathic and later traditions associate Ārmaiti increasingly closely with the earth. Zam and Ārmaiti remain distinct. **Tier:** early theological association with later canonical systematization.
- **Aŋra Mainyu/Ahriman — attacker of Earth.** In the *Bundahišn*, Ahriman’s invasion makes Earth tremble; mountains grow from it and stabilize it. Earth wages one of creation’s “conflicts” against the Evil Spirit. **Tier:** Middle Persian canonical elaboration.
- **Amərətāt/Amurdad — cooperative grouping.** The *Bundahišn* lists Zamyād, Rashn, Aštād, and the three Glories among Amurdad’s helpers. **Tier:** learned Middle Persian systematization.
- **Rashn — joint later grouping.** Appears beside Zamyād in the *Bundahišn* and later blessing traditions. No narrative meeting survives. **Tier:** learned systematization.
- **Aštād/Arštāt — joint later grouping.** Same evidence and limitation as Rashn. **Tier:** learned systematization.
- **The three Glories/*xᵛarənah*s — joint later grouping and calendrical association.** The *Sīrōza* formula joins Earth and the Kavyan and Unseized Glories; Yasht 19 then becomes the principal surviving Glory hymn. No source narrates Zam possessing or conversing with Glory. **Tier:** calendrical-liturgical association.
- **The waters — co-worshipped cosmic element.** Yasna 38 moves from Earth to the life-bearing waters. This is liturgical juxtaposition, not a narrated interaction. **Tier:** securely attested early liturgical association.

**Absence of evidence:** no documented meeting, combat, marriage, seduction, deception, judgment, or spoken dialogue involving Zam and another named deity survives.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Earth that bears humanity

The closest thing to Zam’s own early myth is not a story but a liturgical proposition: Earth bears the worshipping community and is therefore worshipped. Yasna 38 joins land, women or female beings, and waters within a sustaining terrestrial order. **Tier:** securely attested early material.

### Earth’s creation and Ahriman’s assault

In the *Bundahišn*, Ohrmazd creates the good world in spiritual and then material form. Earth is among the ordered material creations. When Ahriman invades, Earth trembles; Alburz and all other mountains rise from it like plants, with interconnected roots. Their growth prevents Earth from continuing to shake. Mountains also channel waters and benefit priests, warriors, and husbandmen. **Tier:** Middle Persian canonical elaboration, drawing partly on lost Avestan material. [S9]

### The mountain catalogue

Yasht 19.1–8 says the first mountain to rise was **Harā Bərəzaitī**, stretching around the eastern, water-washed land. Darmesteter’s transcription and spellings differ from modern critical forms. The catalogue includes:

Harā Bərəzaitī; Zərəδō outside Manuša; Uši.dāuua; Uši.darəna; Ərəzifiia; Fraorəpa; Ərəzūra; Būmiia; Raoδita; Mažišuuant; Antarə.daŋ́hu; Ərəziša; Vāiti.gaēsa; Adarana; Bayana; Iškata Upairi.saēna; the Hamankuna, Vasna, Fravanku, and Vīδuuana groups; Aēzaχa; Maēnaχa; Vaχəδraka; Asaia; Tuδā̊ska; Isaua; Draošisuuant; Sāiriuuant; Naŋ́hušmant; Kakaŋ́ha; Antarə.kaŋ́ha; Sičiδauua; Ahuna; Raēmana; Aša.stəmbana; Uruiiō.vaēδa; Asnauuant; Ušaoma; Ušta.xᵛarənah; Siiāmaka; Vafraiia; Vouruša; Jatara; Aδutauua; Spitauuarəna; Spəṇtō.dāta; Kadrūuāspa; Kaoirisa; Taēra; Barō.sraiiana; Barana; Frāpaiia; Udriia; and Raēuuant.

The text concludes that named and unnamed mountains total **2,244**. Exact readings vary because the text is corrupt and many names are otherwise unknown. Proposed modern identifications—from Hindu Kush and Pamir ranges to Iranian Alborz peaks—range from plausible to conjectural. Uši.darəna’s placement in Sistan is strengthened by its connection with Lake Kąsaoya and the Saošyant tradition; it is often connected with Kūh-e Ḵᵛāja, but the identification remains scholarly reconstruction. [S1, S14]

### The *Kayān Yasn*: not a Zam myth

Yasht 19.9–96 traces Glory through sacred history:

1. *xᵛarənah* belongs to Ahura Mazdā and enables creation and final renovation.
2. It belongs to the Aməša Spəṇtas and other divine beings.
3. It adheres to Haošyaŋha, Taxma Urupi, and Yima.
4. Yima loses it after embracing falsehood.
5. Glory departs three times as a *varəγna* bird and is seized successively by Mithra, Θraētaona, and Kərəsāspa.
6. Good and evil powers contend for the “Unseized Glory.”
7. Ātar and Aži Dahāka threaten one another and neither secures it.
8. Glory escapes into Vourukaša, where Apąm Napāt protects or seeks it.
9. Fraŋrasyan dives three times for Aryan Glory and fails; inlets or lakes form as it escapes.
10. Glory attaches to the Kayanian rulers, especially Kavi Haosravah.
11. It supports Zarathustra and Vištāspa.
12. It will belong to Saošyant at the final renovation.

The threefold departure from Yima and the long Kərəsāspa catalogue may be minstrel expansions. The final-renovation passage at 19.89–90 is probably the original form of material duplicated earlier at 19.11–12, not vice versa. These are important findings about the history of the hymn, but they do not turn Glory’s protagonists into Zam’s personal relations. [S1, S7, S15]

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Direct worship

Yasna 38 proves that Earth received *yaz-* worship in the oldest liturgical stratum. Herodotus and Xenophon independently report Persian sacrifices to Earth during the Achaemenid era, apparently including animal victims. These reports are compatible with Iranian reverence for Earth but cannot identify the precise Avestan liturgy used.

Zoroastrian animal sacrifice is historically documented in several periods. Nothing, however, specifically connects **human sacrifice** with Zam. The dedicated contested-material search found no primary or credible scholarly evidence for human victims, temple prostitution, cult violence, or an exclusive Zam priesthood.

### Offerings

Yasht 19.8 lists offerings to Glory: libations, haoma, meat, *baresman*, sacred speech, and ritual acts. Since the passage worships *xᵛarənah*, it must not automatically be quoted as a menu of offerings made directly to Zam.

For Earth herself, Yasna 38 demonstrates praise or sacrificial worship but does not describe a separate altar, image, or priestly office.

### Calendar

Zamyād presides over the **twenty-eighth day** of every thirty-day Zoroastrian month. The association is explicit in *Sīrōza* 1.28 and 2.28. The earliest external evidence for the system of named Zoroastrian days is an Aramaic document from Bactria, possibly dated 329 BCE, which names Dēn day; it does not name Zamyād but shows that the calendrical structure was operating by the late fourth century BCE. [S7]

Unlike month-and-day names that coincide and generate major annual name-day feasts, no month is named Zamyād. Consequently there is no universally prominent annual “Zamyād-gān” comparable with Mihragān or Tīragān. Day 28 remains available for corresponding prayer and devotion in traditional calendars.

### Fire-temple consecration

In Parsi descriptions of the long consecration of sacred fire, repeated recitations are assigned across the thirty day-yazatas. One sequence requires 88 recitations distributed through the calendar and terminates with Zamyād, making Yasht 19 the final hymn in that numerical cycle. This prominence results from liturgical arithmetic and the ordering of day names, not from a belief that Zam is the supreme fire deity. [S16]

### Temples, priesthood, and images

No temple has been securely excavated or inscribed as a sanctuary dedicated exclusively to Zam. No specialist “priest of Zam” is documented. Earth worship functioned inside the broader Mazdaean sacrificial and calendrical system administered by priests/Magi and, later, by Zoroastrian hereditary priesthoods.

### Purity

Herodotus’s insistence that Persian sacrifice occurred on clean ground is compatible with Zoroastrian concern for pollution, though his report is external and generalized. Later Zoroastrian purity law treats soil as capable of pollution by corpses and other contaminants. These practices protect the created Earth but are not all addressed to Zam by name.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Old Avestan period

Earth is directly worshipped as the power that bears human life. The evidence is liturgical and non-anthropomorphic. No name Zamyād, narrative mythology, iconography, or separate institution is attested.

### Younger Avestan period

A sacred geography of mountains and a large heroic-es­chatological hymn to Glory develop in oral poetry. Separately, the thirty-day divine calendar makes the Munificent Earth the power of day 28. A priestly combination eventually joins the mountain fragment to the *Kayān Yasn*, producing the text later called Zamyād Yasht.

### Achaemenid period, 550–330 BCE

Royal inscriptions proclaim Ahuramazda creator of Earth and grantor of rule over it. Greek observers report sacrifice to Earth. Neither body of evidence uses Zam’s name. The evidence supports reverence for Earth but not a documented state temple or cult bureaucracy of Zamyād.

### Hellenistic, Parthian, and early Sasanian periods

Direct documentation is thin. Avestan liturgy continued through oral and manuscript transmission. There is no demonstrable Greek Gaia, Anatolian mother-goddess, or Mesopotamian earth-goddess fusion with Zam.

### Sasanian and post-Sasanian scholastic period

The Avesta was organized and accompanied by Middle Persian interpretation. Zamyād became firmly embedded in:

- the calendar;
- the *Sīh-rōzag*;
- classifications of yazatas;
- cosmological accounts of Earth and mountains;
- ritual recitation schedules.

The *Bundahišn* expands Earth’s story into the cosmic battle against Ahriman and correlates the mountain catalogue with known Iranian geography. This is the fullest later theology relevant to Zam.

### Islamic conquest and medieval survival

The seventh-century Arab conquest ended Zoroastrian imperial patronage. Zoroastrian communities continued in Iran and India while preserving ritual texts and composing or copying Middle Persian works. Later persecution, forced conversion, destruction of fire temples, fiscal pressure, and communal contraction affected the whole religion; no episode is specifically recorded as a targeted suppression of a Zam sanctuary. [S17]

The surviving manuscripts of Yasht 19 belong to this long post-conquest transmission: Jm4 in 1352; F1 in 1591; E1 in 1601.

### Early-modern and modern periods

Parsi printing and European Iranology moved the Avesta from manuscript communities into printed critical editions:

- Westergaard introduced modern stanza numbering.
- Geldner’s 1886–96 edition collated the manuscript tradition.
- Darmesteter translated and commented on Yasht 19 in French and English.
- Hintze issued a dedicated German edition and commentary in 1994.
- Humbach and Ichaporia published a new text, translation, and commentary in 1998.
- The Avestan Digital Archive now makes manuscript images and descriptions accessible. [S1, S7, S18]

Zamyād remains a day name in living Zoroastrian calendars, and Yasht 19 remains a recitable sacred text. Evidence for a large, independently organized modern Zam cult is lacking.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

Modern reception concentrates on three themes:

- Zam as an “Iranian Earth goddess”;
- Yasht 19 as a source for Iranian heroic tradition and *xᵛarənah*;
- the mountain list as evidence for Avestan sacred geography, especially Sistan.

The second and third have strong textual foundations. The first is accurate only if “goddess” is understood as a convenient rendering of a grammatically feminine, worshipped Earth-yazata—not as proof of a humanoid mother-goddess cult.

### Zoroastrian practice

Traditional and diaspora calendars continue to print Zamyād as day 28. Devotees may recite a yasht corresponding to the day’s yazata. Public evidence does not establish the scale of this practice, and calendar inclusion should not be mistaken for a dedicated popular festival.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist reception

Modern Iranian-revivalist and eclectic pagan sources sometimes present Zam as:

- “Mother Earth” with a modern ecological theology;
- a green-clad fertility goddess;
- the consort of a sky god;
- an Iranian equivalent of Gaia, Demeter, Semele, or a Slavic Earth Mother.

The first may be a legitimate modern devotional interpretation; the others are unsupported unless their modern authors explicitly identify them as comparison or creative reconstruction. No ancient text supplies Zam a consort, maternal genealogy, seasonal death-and-return myth, or Demeter-like iconography.

### Occultism

The dedicated search found no substantial, historically continuous Zam tradition in Renaissance magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the principal European grimoire lineages. Modern occult lists may include “Zam” as an elemental earth intelligence, but such usages are modern taxonomy, not recovered Avestan ritual.

### Popular errors

- **“Zam is Zahhāk.”** False name-conflation.
- **“Yasht 19 is entirely a hymn describing the Earth goddess.”** False; only the mountain preface establishes the Earth connection, while 19.9–96 praises Glory.
- **“Every hero in Yasht 19 worships Zam.”** Not stated.
- **“Zam has an ancient green-robed human image.”** No such securely identified image exists.
- **“Zam was the Persian Gaia.”** At best a loose comparative gloss, not a historical equation.
- **“The mountains can all be plotted exactly.”** Most identifications remain uncertain.

No documented modern extremist or ethnonationalist movement appears to have made Zam a major emblem. Broader nationalist uses of *xᵛarənah*, the Kayanian kings, and pre-Islamic Iranian religion should not be assigned automatically to Zam.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is there really a separate deity Zam?

One position treats Zam straightforwardly as the earth yazata: Yasna 38 worships Earth, and the calendar explicitly calls the Munificent Earth a *yazata*. Another stresses that the Avestan evidence never develops an individual personality called Zam and that “Zamyād” is a later calendrical name.

The evidence supports a qualified synthesis: Earth is unquestionably worshipped; later tradition unquestionably classifies the Munificent Earth as a yazata named Zamyād; but an independent anthropomorphic goddess with a narrative mythology cannot be reconstructed.

### What is Yasht 19 actually dedicated to?

Older titles and popular summaries call it the “Hymn to Earth.” Its fifteen kardes repeatedly invoke Glory, not Zam. The leading historical explanation is that an Earth/mountain fragment was appended to the *Kayān Yasn*, with the union motivated by the day-28 *Sīrōza* formula.

Thus:

- “Zamyād Yasht” is the correct traditional-liturgical title;
- “Kayān Yasn” describes the main literary composition;
- “hymn to Earth” is misleading if taken as a description of all 96 stanzas.

### Date and geography

Older scholarship often assigned Yasht passages rather confidently to precise dynastic periods. More recent work emphasizes oral stratification, interpolation, corrupt transmission, and the difficulty of turning linguistic archaism into an absolute date.

Sistan is strongly implicated by Lake Kąsaoya, the Haētumant/Helmand system, Ušaδā, and the Saošyant geography. Whether the entire final recension was created there remains an inference.

### Number and identity of mountains

The transmitted total is 2,244. Individual readings differ between manuscripts and editors. The *Bundahišn* list partly matches Yasht 19 but also rationalizes names through later Iranian geography. It preserves cognate tradition, not necessarily a verbatim copy of the same Avestan original.

### Zam and Ārmaiti

Boyce and other scholars emphasize Ārmaiti as the more active guardian genius of Earth. Popular accounts consequently sometimes absorb Zam into Spandarmad. Yet the calendar gives them different days—Spandarmad day 5 and Zamyād day 28—and the texts preserve both. “Two aspects of the same Earth” is an interpretive model, not their literal textual identity.

### Primary-evidence condition

The textual tradition is preserved through late copies:

- Old and Younger Avestan compositions were orally transmitted;
- the Avestan script itself was developed in late antiquity;
- the Sasanian nask organization was largely lost;
- the extant Yasht manuscripts are medieval or early modern;
- editors reconstruct readings from divergent witnesses and living recitation.

The Avestan Digital Archive and Corpus Avesticum projects materially improve access to these witnesses but do not eliminate the chronological gap.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Attestation or development |
|---|---|
| Late second/early first millennium BCE, approximate and disputed | Old Avestan Yasna 38.1 directly worships “this earth … that bears us.” |
| First millennium BCE, date unresolved | Younger Avestan mountain catalogue and *Kayān Yasn* develop in oral-poetic form. |
| By late fourth century BCE | The Zoroastrian system of named days is externally evidenced in a Bactrian Aramaic document; the named example is Dēn day, not Zamyād. |
| c. 490 BCE | Darius’s DNa inscription at Naqš-e Rostam calls Ahuramazda creator of “this earth”; Zam is not named. |
| c. 440s–420s BCE | Herodotus 1.131–32 reports Persian sacrifice to Earth and other cosmic powers. |
| Fourth century BCE | Xenophon, *Cyropaedia* 8.3.24, depicts sacrifice to Earth as directed by Magi. |
| Early second century CE | Plutarch reports Artaxerxes II clutching Earth while praying to “Hera”; possible Ārmaiti association, not secure evidence for Zam. |
| Sasanian period, 224–651 CE | Avestan compilation and Middle Persian scholastic interpretation consolidate the divine calendar and nask traditions. |
| Late Sasanian or early Islamic period | *Šāyest nē Šāyest* compiles ritual and purity law; later divine classifications include Zam. |
| Ninth–tenth centuries CE, with later redaction | Major Middle Persian books, especially the *Bundahišn* and *Dēnkard*, preserve cosmology, mountain traditions, and the organization of yazata worship. |
| c. 1010 CE | Ferdowsi completes the *Šāhnāmeh*, preserving transformed heroic material parallel to the *Kayān Yasn* but no acting deity Zam. |
| 1352 CE | Jm4, the oldest surviving Khordeh Avesta, is copied. |
| 1591 CE | F1, the principal and oldest pure-Yasht codex, is copied at Navsari; it is fundamental for Yasht 19. |
| 1601 CE | E1, another important Yasht witness, is copied. |
| 1886–1896 | K. F. Geldner publishes the foundational critical edition of the Avesta. |
| 1892/1898 | Darmesteter’s influential French/English treatment presents Yasht 19 as the “Zamyād Yasht” while recognizing its Glory-centered content. |
| 1994 | Almut Hintze publishes *Der Zamyād-Yašt: Edition, Übersetzung, Kommentar*. |
| 1998 | Helmut Humbach and Pallan Ichaporia publish *Zamyād Yasht: Yasht 19 of the Younger Avesta*. |
| Twenty-first century | The Avestan Digital Archive provides online access to manuscripts; Zamyād remains day 28 in living Zoroastrian calendars and Yasht 19 remains a ritual text. |
| Present | No exclusive Zam temple, securely inherited iconography, separate priesthood, or large independent revival movement is documented. |
