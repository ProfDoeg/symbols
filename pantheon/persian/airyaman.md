# Airyaman: Research Dossier

**Class:** Beneficent male *yazata* (“being worthy of worship”) in developed Zoroastrian tradition; a divinized or hypostatized power of fellowship/community and, securely in the Younger Avesta, healing. Middle Persian texts occasionally elevate him rhetorically to *Amahraspand* status, but he is not normally one of the canonical six Aməša Spəntas.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The name-form *airyəmā* occurs in the Old Avestan *Airyaman Išya*, Yasna 54.1, probably composed around the turn of the second to first millennium BCE, though proposed dates range broadly through the late second millennium. The language is Gathic/Old Avestan, but the identification of *airyəmā* there as a proper divine name is disputed: it may mean “the desired Airyaman,” “the desired friend/ally,” or collectively “the community.” The first unambiguous presentation of Airyaman as an individually acting divine healer is in the Younger Avestan *Vidēvdād* 22, composed later in the first millennium BCE or subsequently within the Younger Avestan period; its absolute date cannot be fixed.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early:** Old or Younger Avestan evidence, with the distinction between those strata stated.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** Middle Persian Zoroastrian exegesis or later established liturgy.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** A geographically localized form or practice.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Priestly or scholarly ordering not demonstrably present in the earliest evidence.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** A historically situated equation with another religious figure.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or popular devotional development.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A historical or comparative inference rather than an explicit traditional statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern attribution.**

Dates assigned to Avestan compositions are linguistic estimates, not manuscript dates. The surviving Avestan manuscripts are medieval: K7a has been dated either 1268 or 1288 CE, while J2, K5, and L4 belong to approximately 1323–24. The distance between composition and physical witness must therefore remain visible throughout.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

Airyaman is the Zoroastrian divine “friend,” ally, or embodiment of communal fellowship who became particularly associated with healing, protective recitation, marriage, and the final renovation of creation. His profile is unusually dependent on a prayer whose first word resembles or supplies his name. Unlike Mithra, Anāhitā, or Verethragna, he has no surviving monumental cult, identifiable temple, narrative cycle, or securely recognized image. He lives chiefly in liturgy and priestly literature.

The historical sequence matters:

1. In the oldest linguistic stratum, *airyaman-* is also an ordinary social noun, not necessarily a god.
2. Yasna 54.1 invokes an *airyəmā išyō*, but scholars disagree whether this is a divine individual or the desired community/ally.
3. Younger Avestan texts unequivocally treat Airyaman as a healer and recipient of divine summons.
4. Middle Persian books systematize him as helper of Ardwahišt/Aša Vahišta, cosmic healer, guide or collaborator of the final savior, and agent of the molten-metal purification.
5. The prayer remains alive in the Yasna and Zoroastrian wedding liturgy.

### Names and forms

- **Avestan:** *Airyaman-*; nominative or vocative forms vary by grammatical context. The prayer begins **ā airyəmā išyō**. Geldner-style transliteration often gives *ā airyəmā išyō*; older English works write *Airyema Ishyo*, *Airyaman Ishya*, or *A Airyema Ishyo*. **[EA]**
- **Avestan script:** Modern manuscript typography varies; the securely recoverable evidence is the transliterated sequence rather than a single standardized Unicode spelling.
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** **Ērmān**, also transcribed *Ērmān*, *Airyaman*, or *Aryāmān*. In Pahlavi contexts *yazd/yazad* may be appended: *Ērmān Yazd*. **[LC]**
- **Manichaean Middle Persian:** **Aryāmān**, particularly in **Yišōʿ Aryāmān**, “Jesus Aryāmān/Christ.” **[SI]**
- **New Persian scholarly usage:** آیریامن, conventionally *Āiryaman/Airyaman*; this is largely antiquarian transcription, not evidence of a distinct Islamic-period cult. **[MA/MSR]**
- **Vedic Sanskrit cognate:** **Aryamán-** (अर्यमन्). The Vedic god requires separate treatment: cognation does not establish that every Vedic function belonged to Iranian Airyaman. **[MSR]**

### Etymology and dispute

The inherited Indo-Iranian comparison between Avestan *airyaman-* and Vedic *aryaman-* is phonologically strong. Meanings proposed for the common noun include “friend,” “companion,” “member of the community,” “clan-fellow,” “guest,” or an institution/personification of hospitality and alliance.

Paul Thieme’s influential reconstruction connected Vedic *ari/arya/aryaman* with relations to the stranger and hospitality. Mary Boyce treated the Indo-Iranian Aryaman/Airyaman as a hypostasis of friendship and sworn friendly alliance. Other derivations have connected the first element with “Aryan,” “true,” or “wealth”; none commands universal agreement. The derivation from “Aryan” in the later racial sense is anachronistic. **[MSR]**

The principal historical dispute is sharper than the etymological one:

- **Divine-continuity position:** Vedic Aryaman and Iranian Airyaman continue a Proto-Indo-Iranian divinity of fellowship, alliance, and marriage. Yasna 54.1 invokes that inherited being. The cognate divine name and later Iranian functions support continuity. Boyce is the clearest modern advocate. **[MSR]**
- **Secondary-personification position:** Jacques Kellens argued that Old Avestan *airyəmā* in Yasna 54.1 is the common noun, perhaps collective “community,” and that later Zoroastrians converted the opening word of a revered formula into the name of a god. On this reading there was no demonstrable Iranian divine counterpart at the prayer’s original composition. **[MSR]**
- **Intermediate reading:** The verse may summon a personified or hypostatized power of fellowship without yet possessing the fully individualized healer of *Vidēvdād* 22. Christopher Brunner’s “hypostasis of the power of the tribe” belongs here. **[MSR]**

### Titles and epithets

- **išya-/išyō**, “desired,” “longed-for,” or “welcome”: Yasna 54.1. Whether it modifies a god or “community/fellowship” is disputed. **[EA]**
- **“Vow-fulfiller”** in Darmesteter’s translation of *Vidēvdād* 22.9: Airyaman is summoned to cure the diseases made by Angra Mainyu. The precise rendering reflects nineteenth-century philology and should not be treated as an independently attested cult title. **[EA]**
- **mazdaδāta-**, “created by Mazdā”: attached to Airyaman in the Sīrōza invocation with Aša. **[EA/LS]**
- The *Airyaman Išya* formula, rather than necessarily the god, is praised as “mighty,” “victorious,” hostile-repelling, healing, and the greatest utterance of Aša in Yasna 54.2 and Yasht 3. **[EA]**
- **Healer/friend:** accurate summaries of functions, but not stable ancient titulary comparable to a royal epithet. **[LS]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Old Avestan evidence

#### Yasna 54.1: the *Airyaman Išya*

**[EA, disputed interpretation]** Yasna 54.1 immediately follows Yasna 53, the final Gatha, and forms the liturgical closure of the Old Avestan core. A representative translation is:

> May the desired Airyaman come for support  
> to the men and women of Zarathustra,  
> for the support of Good Mind.  
> I ask for the desired recompense of Truth  
> which Ahura Mazdā will apportion.

Translations vary substantially because *airyəmā*, *rafəδra-*, *daēnā*, and the syntax of the latter half are contested. Mills rendered *airyaman* “desired friend and peersman”; Brunner capitalized it as Airyaman while acknowledging a communal sense; Kellens reads a social collective rather than a theonym.

The verse says nothing explicitly about medicine, herbs, fire, molten metal, or a final savior. Those associations arise from later Avestan and Middle Persian interpretation. Its language is “pure Gathic” in the philological sense, but it is not one of the five Gathas proper. Some scholars regard it as nearly contemporary with them; others allow pseudo-Old-Avestan composition or later placement. **Physical support:** no ancient original survives. It was transmitted orally and then in medieval liturgical manuscripts.

#### Other Gathic uses of *airyaman-*

Forms occur in Yasna 32.1, 33.3–4, and 49.7. These passages are generally read as ordinary social vocabulary—an ally, associate, or social group—not an acting god. Consequently, statements that “Zarathustra repeatedly worships Airyaman in the Gathas” are unsupported. **[EA]**

### Younger Avestan corpus

#### Yasna 54.2

**[EA, later liturgical framing]** This Younger Avestan addition explicitly worships the *Airyaman Išya* as powerful, victorious, hostile-repelling, and the greatest of Aša’s utterances. This is clear evidence that the formula had become an object of cultic praise, though it does not by itself settle the original grammar of 54.1.

#### Yasht 2, Haptān Yašt

**[EA/LS]** Airyaman appears after or in association with Aša Vahišta. The sequence helped establish the later theology in which he is Aša’s helper. It supplies no independent genealogy.

#### Yasht 3, Ardwahišt Yašt

**[EA, probably late Younger Avestan]** Although nominally dedicated to Aša Vahišta, much of this short, repetitive hymn praises the power of the *Airyaman Išya*. Sections 5–6 identify sacred utterance as the highest healing and exalt the formula above treatment by knife, plants, or drugs. Brunner notes that the particular identification in 3.5 may be a secondary insertion into material originally praising formulas in general.

Sections 8, 11, and 15 deploy the prayer against hostile supernatural powers, including Tarōmaiti, the daēvic counterpart of right-minded devotion. The formula is therefore both medicine and weapon.

#### Yasht 11

**[EA]** The prayer appears among potent protective utterances. The operative agent can be the formula itself; it should not automatically be rewritten as a narrative appearance by the personified god.

#### Sīrōza 1.3 and 2.3

**[EA/LS]** Airyaman is invoked alongside Aša on the third day’s liturgy, with *mazdaδāta-* (“created by Mazdā”). Airyaman nevertheless has no day or month of his own in the thirty-day Zoroastrian calendar. His placement is subordinate and associative.

#### Visperad 24

**[EA/LC liturgical reception]** The *Airyaman Išya*, like the Ahuna Vairya, is credited with producing good thoughts, good words, and good deeds and defeating evil. The text concerns the formula’s efficacy more directly than the god’s biography.

#### Vidēvdād 20

**[EA]** Airyaman “follows” Aša in a healing context. The *Airyaman Išya* is prescribed against sickness, death, sorcerers, witches, and persons characterized polemically as belonging to the Lie. The notorious sexualized language in older translations—“whores belonging to the Lie”—reflects both the Avestan polemic and translation history; it is not evidence for temple prostitution or an Airyaman cult involving it.

#### Vidēvdād 22

**[EA, clearest mythic narrative]** Angra Mainyu makes 99,999 diseases. Ahura Mazdā first appeals to Manthra Spenta, then orders his herald Nairyō.saŋha to travel to Airyaman’s dwelling and request healing. Airyaman obeys; the text moves into purification and disease-expelling formulas. Darmesteter understood the nine furrows as preparation for the *baršnūm* purification rite. The chapter’s conclusion is abrupt, perhaps because the narrative yields to liturgical performance.

This is the earliest unambiguous text in which Airyaman is an individual divine healer with a dwelling, a summons, and an action.

### Avestan fragments and lost nasks

**[LC/LS]** An Avestan fragment probably representing fragard 23 of the lost *Varštmānsr Nask*, known through Dēnkard 9.45, attributes the *Airyaman* formula to future saviors: through it Ahura Mazdā obtains final mastery and Angra Mainyu loses sovereignty. Three fragards reportedly bore the Middle Persian title *Ērmān*. The *Sūdgar Nask*’s twenty-second section was eschatological, while *Bag Nask* 22 interpreted the prayer in relation to Yasna 53. These works survive only through epitomes; reconstructing their full content is impossible.

### Middle Persian books

#### Dēnkard

Compiled principally in the ninth century CE from Sasanian and earlier learning.

- Dēnkard 3.157 states that a physician’s drugs succeed through Airyaman’s superior assistance and friendship. **[LC]**
- Dēnkard 8.37.13 assigns the spiritual debt for healing to Ardwahišt, effectively absorbing Airyaman’s function into Aša Vahišta. This is evidence for overlap, not two versions that should be harmonized. **[LC/LS]**
- Dēnkard 9 preserves summaries of lost Avestan nasks connecting the prayer to purification and renovation. **[LC/LS]**

#### Bundahišn

The extant Middle Persian cosmogony incorporates materials of varying ages and reached its surviving form during the ninth through twelfth centuries.

- Chapters 26.97–98 call Ērmān an *Amahraspand* and say that no medicinal drug would cure without Ohrmazd’s creation of Ērmān for healing. The title is exceptional and does not revise the standard heptad. **[LC/LS]**
- At the Renovation, Fire and Ērmān Yazd melt the metal in the hills and mountains until it flows like a river over the earth. The righteous experience it as warm milk, while evil is purged. **[LC]**
- Airyaman assists or guides the final savior Astvat-ərəta/Sōšāns in accomplishing *Frašō.kərəti*. **[LC]**

#### Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram

In chapters 34.38–39, Aša/Ardwahišt and Airyaman descend together at the end of time and begin the conquest of Āz, the demon of destructive greed. **[LC]**

#### Epistles of Manuščihr

The ninth-century letter 1.7.10 cites Zarathustra asking Ohrmazd how purification can be achieved until the *airyeme* formula has been recited. This attests the prayer’s importance in *baršnūm* purification. **[LC]**

#### Mēnōg ī Xrad and Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag

No securely identified episode centered upon Airyaman occurs in the standard texts. Their general eschatology supplies context but should not be mined to invent an appearance. **Absence finding.**

### Manichaean texts

Third-century Manichaean missionaries used **Aryāmān** to translate or interpret “Christ,” especially in **Yišōʿ Aryāmān**. The opening of Mani’s *Living Gospel* could become “I, Mani, apostle of Jesus Aryāmān” in Middle Persian. The choice exploits both *aryāmān* as “friend/beloved” and the sound resemblance between *Yišōʿ Aryāmān* and *Airyəmā Išyō*. Shared healer/savior associations probably reinforced it. This is translation and missionary accommodation, not evidence that orthodox Zoroastrians identified Airyaman with Jesus. **[SI]**

### Texts named in the brief but yielding no Airyaman dossier evidence

- **Achaemenid inscriptions:** Behistun and Darius’s Naqš-e Rostam inscriptions name Ahura Mazdā; later Artaxerxes inscriptions also name Mithra and Anāhitā. Airyaman is not named. **Absence finding.**
- **Herodotus and Plutarch:** neither supplies a secure, explicit Airyaman notice. General descriptions of Persian sacrifice or religion cannot be assigned to him. **Absence finding.**
- **Ferdowsi’s Šāhnāmeh:** no independent Airyaman figure occurs. The later popularity of Farīdūn as healer belongs to the history of Airyaman’s declining popular prominence, not to an Airyaman episode in Ferdowsi. **Absence finding.**
- **Roman Mithraism:** no secure inscription or image identifies Airyaman in a Mithraeum. *Arimanius* is normally connected with Ahriman/Angra Mainyu, not Airyaman; confusing the two is a modern error based on superficial spelling. **[MI]**

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

- **Fellowship, alliance, communal support:** likely the oldest domain if the Indo-Iranian comparison is accepted. **[EA/MSR]**
- **Marriage:** inferred comparatively from Vedic Aryaman and directly reflected in the later Zoroastrian use of the prayer in weddings. No early Iranian narrative makes Airyaman a divine spouse or wedding guest. **[MSR/LC]**
- **Healing:** explicit in the Younger Avesta and dominant in Middle Persian theology. Airyaman treats both embodied disease and the demonic/spiritual cause of illness. **[EA/LC]**
- **Purification and exorcism:** chiefly through the *Airyaman Išya* as performative speech. **[EA/LC]**
- **Eschatological renovation:** developed fully in the Pahlavi books, where he cooperates with Fire and Aša and assists Sōšāns. **[LC]**

### Powers and limitations

Airyaman cures the diseases produced by Angra Mainyu, validates the efficacy of drugs, helps defeat Āz, and participates in the final purgation. His power is normally commissioned by Ahura Mazdā and coordinated with Aša, Fire, sacred speech, and the savior. He is not represented as sovereign creator, judge of souls, warrior king, or independent ruler.

Healing by sacred utterance is ranked above knife surgery and herbal medicine in Yasht 3, but the tradition does not simply reject material medicine: Dēnkard 3.157 makes Airyaman the divine condition that renders medicinal herbs effective. **[EA/LC]**

### Iconography and material attributes

No securely identified ancient image of Airyaman is known. He has:

- no recognized anthropomorphic type;
- no securely assigned weapon, costume, animal, plant, color, number, planet, star, or constellation;
- no known coins or seals bearing a securely identified portrait;
- no dedicated day or month;
- no securely excavated temple.

Fire and molten metal are collaborators or media in his eschatological work, not personal emblems attested in images. Medicinal plants are part of the healing complex, not a fixed iconographic attribute. Modern illustrations of a winged Persian man, solar god, physician, or four-armed figure are imaginative or borrowed, often from generic *faravahar* imagery or Vedic/Puranic Aryaman. **[MI]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy and hierarchy

No Avestan or Middle Persian source supplies parents, spouse, siblings, or offspring for Airyaman. He is not one of Ahura Mazdā’s six canonical Aməša Spəntas, although *Bundahišn* 26 exceptionally calls him an *Amahraspand*. His normal classification is *yazata*.

His closest structural relationship is with Aša Vahišta/Ardwahišt. The developed theological logic is:

- Aša represents truth/order and has fire as his material creation.
- Airyaman heals and finally purifies.
- Fire and Airyaman jointly produce the molten-metal ordeal.
- Airyaman consequently becomes Aša’s helper.

This is a functional hierarchy, not genealogy.

### Every documented interaction with a named divine being

- **Ahura Mazdā — commission to heal:** Ahura Mazda sends Nairyō.saŋha to summon Airyaman against Angra Mainyu’s diseases; *Vidēvdād* 22.7–15, Younger Avestan. **[EA]**
- **Angra Mainyu — antagonistic healing:** Airyaman counters the 99,999 diseases Angra Mainyu creates; *Vidēvdād* 22.1–24. They do not meet in dialogue. **[EA]**
- **Nairyō.saŋha — messenger encounter:** Ahura Mazda’s herald travels to Airyaman’s dwelling and delivers the healing request; *Vidēvdād* 22.7–15. **[EA]**
- **Manthra Spenta — parallel healer:** Ahura Mazda first appeals to sacred speech and then to Airyaman; *Vidēvdād* 22.1–15. This is coordinated action, not genealogy. **[EA]**
- **Aša Vahišta/Ardwahišt — cultic association:** Airyaman follows Aša in Yasht 2 and *Vidēvdād* 20; the Sīrōza invokes them together. **[EA/LS]**
- **Aša Vahišta/Ardwahišt — joint eschatological descent:** they come to earth together to initiate Āz’s defeat; *Zādspram* 34.38–39, ninth-century Middle Persian preservation of lost Zand material. **[LC]**
- **Ātar/Fire — joint purification:** Fire and Airyaman melt the world’s metal into a river at the Renovation; *Bundahišn* 34.18. **[LC]**
- **Āz — combat/conquest:** Airyaman and Ardwahišt initiate the defeat of the demon Āz; *Zādspram* 34.38–39. **[LC]**
- **Sōšāns/Astvat-ərəta — guidance or assistance:** Airyaman guides or aids the final savior in bringing about *Frašō.kərəti*; Pahlavi eschatological tradition summarized in the *Bundahišn* and related literature. **[LC]**
- **Future Saošyants collectively — power of the prayer:** the saviors recite the *Airyaman* formula so Ahura Mazda becomes master and Angra Mainyu loses rule; lost *Varštmānsr Nask*, known through Dēnkard 9.45. **[LC/LS]**
- **Tarōmaiti — formulaic expulsion:** the *Airyaman Išya* puts the daēvic power Tarōmaiti to flight; Yasht 3.8, 11, 15. The actor is primarily the prayer, not necessarily an embodied Airyaman. **[EA]**
- **Jesus Christ — Manichaean equation:** Middle Persian Manichaeans use *Aryāmān* for Christ and write *Yišōʿ Aryāmān*; third century onward. This is linguistic-theological accommodation, not a Zoroastrian encounter myth. **[SI]**
- **Thraētaona/Frēdōn — functional displacement, not interaction:** the hero-physician’s popular cult or intercession later overshadowed devotion to Airyaman. No text records the two meeting. **[F/LC]**
- **Mithra — no specific Iranian episode:** comparative scholarship places Airyaman near the Indo-Iranian covenant gods, but no surviving Iranian text narrates an encounter. **Absence finding.**
- **Baga — inferred association:** the Middle Persian personal name *Bay-Aryāmān* may preserve an association between Baga and Airyaman comparable to Vedic Bhaga and Aryaman. It is not a narrative interaction. **[MSR]**
- **Sraoša — shared liturgical/healing context:** both are associated with sacred speech and Aša, but no surviving episode has them converse or act jointly in a scene. Claims of a concrete partnership exceed the text. **Absence finding.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The making and healing of 99,999 diseases

**Earliest source:** *Vidēvdād* 22, Younger Avestan. **[EA]**

Angra Mainyu casts his hostile gaze upon Ahura Mazda’s good creation and makes diseases in ascending multiples: nine, ninety, nine hundred, nine thousand, and nine times ten thousand—conventionally totaled as 99,999. Ahura Mazda offers rich recompense and a blessing to Manthra Spenta for a cure. Sacred Speech asks how the diseases are to be expelled. Ahura Mazda then calls Nairyō.saŋha and orders him to go to Airyaman’s dwelling. The herald repeats the divine request, promises horses, camels, cattle, and small livestock, and offers the blessing that restores the sick.

Airyaman responds obediently. The text proceeds to his digging of nine furrows and then into disease-expelling recitations. Darmesteter interpreted the furrows through the *baršnūm*, a nine-night purification performed in a demarcated ritual space. Because the extant chapter ends in formulas rather than narrative closure, reconstructions in which Airyaman dramatically “slays” all diseases go beyond the surviving text.

The chapter makes illness part of cosmic opposition, yet integrates ritual speech, purification, and healing. The number is rhetorical totality, not an ancient medical taxonomy of exactly 99,999 diagnosed conditions.

### The healing power of the *Airyaman Išya*

**Sources:** Yasna 54.1–2; Yasht 3; *Vidēvdād* 20; Visperad 24. **[EA]**

This is not a plot-driven myth but a ritual complex. A short Old Avestan petition for communal protection or Airyaman’s arrival became the supreme healing formula. Later texts praise it as more effective than plants, drugs, or the knife; use it against disease, death, witchcraft, and pollution; and make it a weapon against demonic powers.

The historical development can be described without choosing between the main interpretations:

- If *airyəmā* was already divine, later texts expanded a prayer to the god of fellowship into a healing and exorcistic instrument.
- If it originally meant “community,” the formula’s opening was reanalyzed as a divine name, producing or consolidating the healer-yazata.
- In either case, by the Younger Avestan period the formula and the healer were inseparable in ritual reception.

### The final molten-metal purification

**Sources:** *Bundahišn* 34.18 and related Pahlavi eschatology. **[LC]**

At the final Renovation, the metal in the mountains and hills melts and pours across the earth like a river. Fire and Airyaman cause this transformation. Every resurrected person passes through it; the righteous experience it as warm milk, while wickedness is burned away. The ordeal is restorative rather than an everlasting inferno: creation becomes perfected and deathless.

Airyaman’s role connects healing on the scale of an individual body to healing on the scale of the cosmos. Boyce suggested that the god of friendship was apt for a world in which enmity ends, but this is a modern explanation, not a statement in the ancient text. **[MSR]**

### Defeat of Āz and assistance to Sōšāns

**Sources:** *Zādspram* 34.38–39; Dēnkard summaries of lost nasks; Pahlavi eschatological tradition. **[LC]**

Airyaman and Ardwahišt descend together and begin the conquest of Āz, greed or insatiability personified. Airyaman also assists the final savior Sōšāns/Astvat-ərəta. The *Airyaman* formula contributes to the final removal of Angra Mainyu’s sovereignty. These notices form a coherent priestly eschatology, but no surviving ancient epic narrates a long “Airyaman cycle.”

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers and temples

No temple can presently be identified as an Airyaman sanctuary. No dedicatory inscription, priestly title, treasury, estate, pilgrimage center, or cult statue securely belongs to him. Claims for an Airyaman temple in Achaemenid or Sasanian Iran are unsupported unless new epigraphic evidence is produced.

This absence distinguishes him from Anāhitā and Mithra. Airyaman’s cultic existence is primarily **liturgical**, not architectural.

### Yasna worship

The *Airyaman Išya* closes the Gathic recitation at Yasna 54.1. Yasna 54.2 then venerates the formula explicitly. In the living Yasna ceremony it is recited multiple times according to the ritual arrangement. Its position after Yasna 53 makes it a protective and performative seal upon the Old Avestan corpus. **[EA/continuous liturgy]**

### Healing and purification

- Yasht 3 ranks healing by sacred formula over healing by knife and materia medica. **[EA]**
- *Vidēvdād* 20 prescribes the formula against disease and demonic agents. **[EA]**
- *Vidēvdād* 22 integrates Airyaman with the nine-furrow purification setting. **[EA]**
- Manuščihr’s ninth-century *Epistles* treat the prayer as indispensable to *baršnūm* purification. **[LC]**
- The Dēnkard says herbal remedies work through Airyaman’s assistance, preventing a simplistic opposition between prayer and medicine. **[LC]**

### Marriage

The prayer is recited four times in the *Āšīrvād/Ashirwad*, the Zoroastrian marriage blessing, among both Parsi and Iranian Zoroastrian communities in documented modern practice. Its words address the men and women of Zarathustra and ask for protection and good recompense; its suitability to marriage may also preserve Airyaman’s Indo-Iranian association with alliance between families.

This is secure evidence for the prayer in marriage, but not proof that every prehistoric Iranian wedding invoked an individualized Airyaman. **[LC/RV]**

### Offerings and sacrifice

*Vidēvdād* 22 represents Ahura Mazda promising horses, camels, oxen, and pregnant small livestock while invoking a Mazda-made blessing. The passage is mythic-liturgical language. It does not document a historical Airyaman sanctuary receiving thousands of animals.

There is no Airyaman-specific evidence for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual sexual service, or cult violence. General accusations about “Persian religion,” or hostile descriptions of Magi, cannot responsibly be transferred to this yazata. **Absence finding.**

### Calendar and priesthood

Airyaman lacks an independent calendrical day and therefore a standard monthly *jashan* tied to his own name. He is invoked with Aša on the third day. There is no documented priesthood exclusively serving Airyaman; ordinary Zoroastrian ritual specialists transmit and recite his prayer.

### Theophoric names

The Middle Persian personal name **Bay-Aryāmān** is evidence that the name retained prestige and perhaps linked Airyaman with Baga. The currently accessible evidence does not support a large onomastic corpus from which the geographic popularity of a cult could be quantified.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Proto-Indo-Iranian background, before approximately 1500 BCE

The close linguistic correspondence of Vedic Aryaman and Avestan Airyaman supports a shared Indo-Iranian term and possibly a shared divine institution connected with fellowship, hospitality, marriage alliance, or social order. This stage is reconstructed; no inscription names a Proto-Indo-Iranian god. **[MSR]**

### Old Avestan period, approximately 1200–1000 BCE, with wide scholarly uncertainty

The *Airyaman Išya* invokes *airyəmā išyō*. The linguistic form is early; its divine interpretation is not secure. Other Gathic occurrences are social nouns. The evidence therefore permits either an inherited but faint divine figure or the seed from which a later personification developed. **[EA/disputed]**

### Younger Avestan period, broadly first millennium BCE

Airyaman becomes unmistakably divine. He heals the diseases of Angra Mainyu, is approached through Nairyō.saŋha, accompanies Aša, and is inseparable from an enormously powerful formula. The *Ardwahišt Yašt* formalizes the link between healing, sacred speech, and Aša. **[EA]**

### Achaemenid period, approximately 550–330 BCE

No royal inscription names Airyaman. Behistun invokes Ahura Mazda; later inscriptions add Mithra and Anāhitā. The silence does not show that Airyaman was unknown, because royal inscriptions are selective, but it prevents claims of a state-sponsored cult. **[EA absence]**

The Zoroastrian calendar, probably organized in approximately the fourth century BCE, gives Airyaman no independent day. His subordinate association with Aša may reflect an already developed theological ordering. **[LS]**

### Parthian and early Sasanian periods, 247 BCE–third century CE

Direct Airyaman documentation is sparse. Avestan transmission remained predominantly oral before the Sasanian codification. No secure image or temple appears.

### Manichaean and Sasanian period, third–seventh centuries CE

Manichaeans translated Christ as **Aryāmān**, likely because “friend/beloved,” healer, and the phonetic echo of *Airyəmā Išyō* made it intelligible to Iranian hearers. This implies that Airyaman was recognizable in Sasanian religious culture but does not quantify his worship. **[SI]**

Zoroastrian priestly codification placed the inherited liturgy within a larger Avestan canon and developed Zand commentary. Later accounts of Alexander destroying a written Avesta and Sasanian rulers restoring it are indigenous sacred history; modern scholarship accepts substantial Sasanian compilation but not every detail of the loss-and-restoration narrative literally.

### Early Islamic period and Pahlavi synthesis, ninth–twelfth centuries

The Dēnkard, *Bundahišn*, *Zādspram*, and Manuščihr preserve the fullest theology of Ērmān: divine enabler of medicine, ally of Ardwahišt, enemy of Āz, assistant of Sōšāns, and co-agent with Fire in universal purification. This is the peak of surviving doctrinal detail, though not necessarily of popular cult.

### Medieval and early-modern communities

Zoroastrian institutions contracted under Islamic rule, migration, demographic loss, and conversion. No episode of suppression specifically targeting Airyaman is documented. The prayer survived because it remained embedded in core liturgy and marriage rather than depending on a separate temple.

Popular healing devotion increasingly favored Shah Farīdūn/Frēdōn, an heroic healer. Airyaman retained canonical legitimacy because, unlike the mortal hero, he was a yazata to whom religious service could be directed. **[LC/F]**

### Modern period

Parsi and Iranian Zoroastrians continue to recite the *Airyaman Išya* in Yasna-related worship and the marriage *Āšīrvād*. This constitutes continuity of liturgical invocation, not evidence for a large independent modern cult with temples or membership statistics.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Western scholarship

Nineteenth-century translations by Martin Haug, Lawrence Mills, and James Darmesteter made the prayer and healer myth widely available. Their “angel” terminology reflects Christianizing translation conventions; *yazata* is more exact.

Twentieth-century scholars moved from smooth theological biography toward stratified textual history. Boyce emphasized inherited Indo-Iranian continuity. Kellens emphasized the common noun and possible secondary formation of the deity. Humbach, Skjærvø, Malandra, and others refined the linguistic and ritual setting.

### Zoroastrian reform and devotional reception

Modern explanatory literature often renders the prayer “the brotherhood of mankind.” This communicates communal solidarity but is interpretive, not a literal consensus translation of every word. Wedding booklets sometimes present Airyaman straightforwardly as patron of marriage and healing, compressing the contested prehistory into devotional theology. **[modern traditional interpretation]**

### Occultism, neopaganism, and popular mythology

A dedicated search found no major Airyaman role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy’s central pantheons, or standard modern grimoires. General modern lists may call him:

- an “angel of health”;
- an “archangel”;
- a solar deity;
- ruler of a weekday, planet, color, crystal, herb, zodiac sign, or magical seal.

Unless such claims cite an actual Zoroastrian text, they are modern correspondential inventions. No ancient Airyaman seal, sigil, planetary rulership, or invocation in Solomonic magic is known. **[MI]**

Small reconstructionist or eclectic practitioners may invoke Airyaman as a healing or friendship deity, but no reliable statistics establish the scale of independent worship. Such practice is modern religious reception, not proof of an ancient ritual reconstructed intact.

### Literary and commercial reception

Airyaman has not acquired a major independent presence in internationally influential novels, films, comics, or games. Online deity catalogues and AI-generated mythography increasingly supply portraits and attributes without sources. Their common conflation of Iranian Airyaman with the full mythology and iconography of Vedic Aryaman is historically misleading. **[MI]**

### Political appropriation

The name’s resemblance to *airya/ārya* makes it vulnerable to “Aryan race” discourse. Ancient *airyaman-* is a social and religious term within Indo-Iranian languages; it does not encode nineteenth- or twentieth-century biological racial theories. No evidence makes Airyaman a god of racial purity, Iranian nationalism, or ethnic warfare. Such uses are modern ideological projections. **[MI]**

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Airyaman already a god in Yasna 54.1?

This is the central debate.

- **For an inherited god:** the exact Vedic cognate is divine; later Iranian evidence is coherent with fellowship, marriage, and healing; the vocative-like invocation naturally permits a personal being.
- **Against certainty:** Old Avestan uses *airyaman-* elsewhere as a social noun; Yasna 54.1 lacks unmistakable divine action; the individualized healer occurs only in younger material.
- **Current responsible conclusion:** the early form is secure, but divine personhood in the original composition is not.

### How did friendship become healing?

No source explains the transition. Proposed models include:

- fellowship as protection and social well-being;
- healing as restoration to the community;
- the prayer acquiring healing force first, after which its opening word became the healer’s name;
- an already existing healer-god lending his name to the formula.

All are reconstructions. Boyce’s suggestion that friendship suits the final abolition of strife is attractive but explicit in no ancient source.

### God or prayer?

Many passages praise the *Airyaman Išya* rather than narrate Airyaman. Later theology tends to merge them. A rigorous dossier must ask in every passage whether the grammatical agent is:

1. the being Airyaman;
2. the word *airyaman* as “friend/community”;
3. the sacred formula named from its first words.

Failure to distinguish these produces an artificially extensive divine biography.

### Status as yazata or Aməša Spənta

Standard tradition makes Airyaman a yazata and helper of Aša Vahišta. *Bundahišn* 26.97 calls him an *Amahraspand*. This probably expresses exceptional sanctity or follows a broader use of the term; it does not insert him into the canonical six. **[LC/LS]**

### Comparative Aryaman

The Vedic cognate strongly establishes linguistic inheritance but not identity of the developed gods. Vedic Aryaman is an Āditya associated with hospitality, social pathways, marriage, and the Mitra–Varuṇa complex. Iranian Airyaman’s explicit medical and molten-metal functions are not simply recoverable from the Veda. The comparison illuminates prehistory but cannot replace Iranian evidence.

### Sources lost and preserved

The Avesta emerged through long oral transmission and Sasanian compilation. Only a portion survives. Pahlavi books summarize lost nasks, but epitomes cannot reconstruct their wording. The oldest surviving manuscripts are more than two millennia later than the estimated composition of Old Avestan material.

Important stages in modern recovery include:

- a *Vidēvdād Sāda* specimen reaching Oxford in 1723;
- Anquetil-Duperron’s collection of manuscripts in India and deposit in Paris in 1762;
- Geldner’s late nineteenth-century critical Avesta;
- the Sacred Books of the East translations by Mills, Darmesteter, and West;
- modern manuscript digitization by the Avestan Digital Archive.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

- Airyaman is not securely depicted as a winged man. **[MI]**
- No evidence makes him a sun god or planetary ruler. **[MI]**
- Roman Mithraic Arimanius is not Airyaman. **[MI]**
- No Airyaman-specific human sacrifice or temple prostitution is documented.
- Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam do not attest him.
- The *Šāhnāmeh* does not preserve a disguised Airyaman narrative.
- “God of Aryan racial brotherhood” is a modern ideological distortion.
- Calling him an “archangel” is an approximate modern translation, not his indigenous class.
- The claim that he personally created 10,000 medicinal plants is not securely supported by the extant wording of *Vidēvdād* 22; it conflates broader Zoroastrian healing traditions.

| 1755–62 CE | Anquetil-Duperron studies with Parsi priests and deposits his manuscript collection in Paris. |

| 1977 | Jacques Kellens argues that the Old Avestan term denotes community and that the Iranian deity may be a later formation. |
| 1984 | Mary Boyce and Christopher Brunner publish the principal *Encyclopaedia Iranica* articles on Airyaman and the prayer, setting out competing historical interpretations. |
| Late twentieth–twenty-first century | Airyaman’s prayer remains part of Zoroastrian Yasna and marriage practice; manuscript digitization enables direct comparison of medieval witnesses. |
