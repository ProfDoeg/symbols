# Druj: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the oldest Zoroastrian texts, *druj-* is a grammatically feminine abstract power—“deception,” “falsehood,” or “illusory order”—that can be spoken of as an active, quasi-personal adversary of *aša-* (“real order,” “truth,” “rightness”). In Younger Avestan usage it becomes both the cosmic Lie and a productive class-term for female demons or polluting evils; *Druj Nasu* is the particular demon/power of corpse pollution. Middle Persian *druz/druj* can mean “demon,” “fiend,” or the evil power generally and is sometimes virtually interchangeable with Ahriman.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The eighteen occurrences of *druj-* in the Old Avestan Gāthās, especially Yasna 30, 31–33, 44, and 46–49. Their absolute date is disputed: linguistic arguments place them before the Younger Avesta, conventionally somewhere in the late second or early first millennium BCE, but no contemporary manuscript survives. The earliest independently datable physical evidence for the same Iranian word-complex is Old Persian *drauga-* in Darius I’s Behistun inscription, carved beginning around 520 BCE. The surviving Avestan manuscripts are medieval copies of a much older oral and written transmission.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The historical object

Druj is not securely attested as a goddess with a biography, temple, statue, parentage, or independent cult. Three overlapping phenomena must be kept distinct:

1. **Old Avestan *druj-*:** the false or deceptive alternative to *aša*, capable of taking adherents and being defeated.
2. **Younger Avestan *druj-*:** the Lie as a cosmic hostile power and, increasingly, a generic designation for particular female demons or pollutions.
3. **Old Persian *drauga-*:** “the Lie,” especially false claims to kingship and rebellion against Darius. This is cognate with, but not necessarily a direct cultic invocation of, the Gāthic Druj.
4. **Middle Persian *druz*:** demon, fiend, falsehood, or the hostile creation collectively.

Representations that turn Druj into a single named goddess with a stable body and mythology are therefore interpretive constructions, usually modern ones.

Jean Kellens emphasizes that translation depends on the disputed meaning of *aša*. If *aša* is “truth,” *druj* is “lie”; if *aša* is “order/justice,” it is closer to “error” or “deceit.” Bartholomae accordingly offered both “falsehood” and “deceit.” Kellens proposed an opposition between “real order” and an illusory or deceptive order, correlated symbolically with daylight and night. The Indic cognate root *druh-* may originally have meant “to harm” or “blacken”; the exact semantic prehistory remains disputed. [Kellens’s lexical synthesis](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/druj/)

There is no ancient evidence for a fixed epithet equivalent to “Druj, Goddess of Lies.” “The Lie” is an interpretive capitalization used in modern translations. “Demoness” is reasonable for certain Younger Avestan passages, particularly Nasu, but risks over-personifying the Gāthic abstraction.

### Classifications by period

- **Securely attested early material:** feminine abstract and hostile power; the organizing opposite of *aša*.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** cosmic enemy, infernal domain, and class of female demons.
- **Priestly systematization:** individual evils become named *druzes*, while the total demonic host is organized beneath Ahriman.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** “personification of the Lie” or “demoness of falsehood.”
- **Modern invention when stated literally:** a discrete goddess with a stable humanlike appearance, priesthood, sacred animal, zodiacal correspondence, or independent shrine.

No Greek or Roman interpretatio identifies Druj directly with Apate, Dolos, Eris, or another classical deity. Such equations are modern comparisons, not ancient identifications.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Old Avesta: the Gāthās

The Gāthās are metrical Old Avestan compositions embedded in Yasna 28–34, 43–51, and 53. Their grammar is close to Vedic Sanskrit and substantially older than Younger Avestan. Their dating remains controversial because they were orally transmitted and contain few externally datable events. Estimates commonly range from approximately 1500 to 1000 BCE, with some scholars preferring later dates. The proper evidentiary statement is therefore relative: they are the earliest linguistic layer of the Avesta, not securely datable physical inscriptions.

*Druj-* occurs eighteen times in Old Avestan. Important passages include:

- **Yasna 30.3–5, 8, 11:** the two primordial *mainiiu-* choose between opposing values; the Evil Spirit chooses the worst action, and the daevas are deceived. At the culmination, Druj is to be delivered into the hands of *aša*. This is early attested material, although whether it already constitutes an ontological cosmic dualism is debated.
- **Yasna 31.1–4, 20:** followers of Druj injure the world; the speaker seeks power with which to defeat it; the deceived face a dark afterlife.
- **Yasna 32.3–5, 12:** the daevas and their devotees are associated with Bad Thought, Druj, arrogance, and violence; ritual specialists are accused of choosing Druj over *aša*. [Comparative text and older English translations](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/)
- **Yasna 44.13–14:** Zarathustra asks how Druj may be driven from those who support it and delivered to *aša*.
- **Yasna 45.1:** warning against a teacher of falsehood.
- **Yasna 46.6, 11:** one who supports a deceitful person is himself on Druj’s side; hostile priests and rulers go to the House of Druj.
- **Yasna 48.1–2:** the question of whether *aša* will overcome Druj.
- **Yasna 49.3, 11:** rejection of Druj and the fate of its adherents.

The frequent English language of “wickedness” can obscure a philological problem: *druj* is not simply every moral wrong. Its verbal and compound uses often concern deception, broken agreements, and the falsification of a relation or claim.

Modern critical points of reference include Humbach and Ichaporia’s 1994 translation, available in digitized form, and the work of Kellens, Pirart, Malandra, Insler, and Skjærvø. [Humbach and Ichaporia](https://zoroastrians.net/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/the-gathas-humbach-ichaporia-1994.pdf)

### Younger Avesta

The Younger Avestan corpus was composed and redacted over a long period. Much may preserve material from the first millennium BCE, but its present organization reflects later priestly transmission.

- **Yasna 60.5 and 61.5:** ritual prayer asks that Druj be expelled and defeated.
- **Yasht 1.18–19, 28:** compounds and formulas directed against deception.
- **Yasht 3, the hymn to Aša Vahišta:** announces that Druj will perish, flee, and vanish. [Yasht 3](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt3sbe.htm)
- **Yasht 10.86:** cattle driven to the “den of Druj” appeal to Mithra, connecting deceit with violated pastoral and contractual order.
- **Yasht 11:** Sraosha turns Druj away.
- **Yasht 13.12–13:** Ahura Mazda states that without the aid of the righteous Fravashis, Druj would have obtained power, rule, and embodied existence and would have occupied the space between earth and heaven. This is a major Younger Avestan cosmic elaboration.
- **Yasht 13.129:** the future savior defeats Druj among humanity.
- **Yasht 19.93–96:** Vištāspa drives Druj away; the Lie and its brood are ultimately destroyed.
- **Vīdēvdād/Vendidad:** Druj becomes both the demonic enemy generally and a label for specific pollutions. The work is the only one of the Sasanian Avesta’s twenty-one *nasks* substantially preserved as a complete nask; its material is heterogeneous, and assigning it one date is misleading. [Survey of the Vendidad](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/vendidad/)

### Druj Nasu

The Vendidad provides the fullest narrative and ritual dossier for *Druj Nasu*:

- **Vd. 5 and 7:** after death, Nasu rushes upon the corpse. In Darmesteter’s translation she comes from the north in the form of a foul, buzzing fly with projecting knees and tail. Natural and violent death produce different timings for her arrival.
- **Vd. 7.3:** the gaze of a dog or the action of carrion birds makes Nasu retreat.
- **Vd. 8–9:** corpse-bearers and contaminated persons undergo prescribed purification.
- **Vd. 9.12–26:** repeated applications of purifying agents drive Nasu from one body part to another until she leaves through the toes.
- **Vd. 10:** sacred formulas repel Nasu.
- **Vd. 6:** corpses contaminate water and ground within prescribed areas, but removal and ritual action restore usability.
- **Vd. 19:** “Druj” can introduce the demon Būiti, whom Angra Mainyu sends against Zarathustra; prayer repels it. Here *druj* functions as a class or descriptor rather than necessarily naming the universal Lie. [Vendidad 7](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd7sbe.htm) [Vendidad 19](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd19sbe.htm)

The common statement that “Druj Nasu enters every body immediately at death” must be qualified: Vendidad 7 distinguishes modes of death and offers ritual casuistry. Its fly is iconography within a text, not evidence of a surviving ancient cult statue or painted standard.

### Achaemenid inscriptions

The great Behistun monument is a trilingual Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian inscription and relief on a cliff near modern Bisotun in western Iran. Darius began it after securing the throne in 522 BCE.

Its Old Persian version repeatedly presents defeated claimants as men who “lied,” usually by claiming a royal identity, and states that *drauga-* made the provinces rebel. Darius warns future rulers to protect themselves from the Lie and punish the *draujana*, liar. [Old Persian text and grammatical commentary](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/aveol/70)

This is:

- **Securely attested Achaemenid material:** *drauga* is a potent political and moral category.
- **Probable religious continuity:** its root and truth/lie opposition are cognate with Avestan *druj/aša*.
- **Not established:** that Darius is invoking a female demoness as the personal supernatural cause of rebellion. The inscription functions as royal legitimation: rival genealogical claims become “the Lie,” while Darius’s account is *hašiya*, true.

Darius’s inscription DPd at Persepolis places *drauga* in a triad of disasters with enemy invasion and famine. This broadens the term beyond individual mendacity while retaining its political force.

### Greek testimony

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, reports that Persian boys learned riding, archery, and “speaking truth” (Histories 1.136). This supports the cultural prestige of truthfulness but neither names Druj nor proves knowledge of Zoroastrian doctrine. [Herodotus 1.136](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D1&force=y)

Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 46–47, around the late first or early second century CE, describes Oromazes and Areimanius as rival makers of good and evil, Mithras as mediator, and apotropaic offerings to Areimanius. He does not identify Druj as an individual being. His report is important evidence for Greek reception of Iranian dualism, not an independent Druj myth. [Plutarch’s text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/plutarch/moralia/isis_and_osiris%2A/c.html)

### Middle Persian books

- **Bundahišn:** compiled from late antique traditions but surviving in post-Sasanian recensions, principally from the ninth century onward. It organizes Ahriman’s demons systematically. *Druz* may describe Ahriman or demonic beings generally; Nas is the demon causing corpse contamination. [Bundahišn text](https://avesta.org/mp/bundahis.html)
- **Dēnkard:** a ninth- to tenth-century encyclopedic compilation preserving summaries and interpretations of lost Avestan nasks. It speaks of invisible druzes, the Druj of magic, bodily corruption, and the final destruction of the evil power. It frequently glosses Druj as Ahriman or his operative force. [Dēnkard III](https://www.avesta.org/denkard/dk3s161.html)
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad:** probably compiled in late antiquity or the early Islamic period from older material. It calls individual vices and punishments *druzes* and depicts hell as crowded by them. [Mēnōg ī Xrad](https://avesta.org/mp/mx.html)
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag:** an early Islamic-period redaction of visionary material in which sins receive infernal punishments. It belongs to the same developed demonological world, but it does not add a securely separable biography for Druj.
- **Persian Rivāyats:** early-modern exchanges between Iranian and Indian Zoroastrians preserve detailed applications of corpse-purity law and helped standardize Parsi funerary practice.

### Ferdowsi, Manichaean, and Mithraic material

Ferdowsi’s *Shāhnāma*, completed around 1010 CE, inherits Iranian oppositions between rightful kingship, deceit, demons, and chaos, but it does not preserve an independent character equivalent to the Avestan Druj. New Persian *dorūğ* is ordinarily “lie”; *dīv* is the usual demon-word. Turning every occurrence of “lie” in the epic into the proper being Druj would be etymological overreach.

Iranian Manichaean texts appropriated much Zoroastrian vocabulary, including names for good and evil beings, but no secure evidence establishes a distinct Manichaean Druj cult or personality. Likewise, no Roman Mithraic image or inscription securely identifies a figure as Druj. Broad claims that a Mithraic monster “is Druj” are modern comparative conjectures. Scholarship increasingly treats Iranian Manichaeism and Sasanian Zoroastrianism as interacting formations rather than assuming that every shared term was a simple one-way borrowing. [Survey of Iranian Manichaeism](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-parent/manicheism-1-general-survey/) [Mithraism and disputed Iranian continuities](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithraism/)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and actions

**Old Avestan core:**

- False or deceptive order opposed to *aša*.
- Wrong religious and social choice.
- Deceptive teaching.
- Support for predatory or illegitimate authority.
- Broken trust, especially in compounds associated with covenants.
- An infernal destination: the House of Druj.

**Younger Avestan elaboration:**

- Cosmic power struggling for rule of the material world.
- Collective demonic force located symbolically in the north.
- A general category under which particular demons can be placed.
- Ritual impurity and material corruption, most explicitly as Nasu.
- False oath, broken contract, and injury to cattle.

**Achaemenid political usage:**

- Fraudulent kingship.
- Rebellion founded on a false identity.
- A threat to imperial and cosmic order.

**Middle Persian systematization:**

- The demonic creation collectively.
- Individual vices or contaminations treated as particular druzes.
- The force by which Ahriman operates within the mixed material world.

### Form

No securely identified ancient image of Druj survives. There is no canonical human or animal form.

The nearest thing to textual iconography is the Vendidad’s Nasu as a foul fly. This is **later canonical demonology**, not evidence that the Old Avestan Druj looked like a fly. The “burrow of Druj” in Vendidad 3 is an infernal place from which demons emerge. Darkness, north, decay, buzzing flies, and corpses are therefore literary associations, not a standardized emblem set.

No primary source securely assigns Druj:

- a weapon, throne, crown, color, number, day, month, planet, star, or zodiacal sign;
- a sacred animal or plant;
- an anthropomorphic sexed body beyond grammatical femininity and later descriptions of particular druzes;
- a temple image.

The corpse-eating dog and birds are opponents or removers of Nasu, not Druj’s sacred animals. Fire, earth, and water are endangered by corpse pollution rather than belonging to Druj.

### Powers and limitations

Druj persuades, deceives, corrupts relations, gains adherents, and—within later ritual texts—contaminates bodies, matter, and spaces. It cannot prevail finally: truthful choice, *aša*, effective prayer, purification, dogs, scavenger birds, the Fravashis, and the future renovation restrict or destroy it. Its power is strongest during the period of mixture between good and evil and ends at the *frašō.kərəti*, renovation.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No Old Avestan genealogy gives Druj parents, siblings, consort, or offspring. Expressions such as “seed of Bad Thought and Druj” in Yasna 32 describe moral-spiritual affiliation rather than a biological family tree.

Later texts subordinate druzes to Angra Mainyu/Ahriman and sometimes call them his creatures, children, confederates, or host. That is **later priestly systematization**. It should not be retrojected as the Gāthic genealogy of Druj.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The choice between Aša and Druj

**Earliest attested layer:** Yasna 30 presents paired spirits or mentalities who choose between better and worse, followed by corresponding choices among gods and humans. The daevas fail to choose rightly and are deceived into violence. Druj is not born; it is the false alignment to which agents commit themselves.

**Development:** Later interpretation turns this difficult poem into a more systematic cosmic war between Ahura Mazda’s and Ahriman’s creations. Scholars disagree over how fully developed ethical or ontological dualism already is in the Gāthās. Boyce and Gnoli read a strong ethical dualism; Kellens and Pirart warn against importing the later system wholesale. [Survey of the debate](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/evil-i-in-ancient-iranian-religions/)

### Druj and false religious authority

Yasna 32 connects daevas, hostile ritualists, Bad Thought, Druj, arrogance, and violence against cattle and communities. The “Karapan” chooses Druj rather than Aša. The passage is polemical and historically opaque: the opponents’ exact institutions cannot be reconstructed confidently.

Later summaries often convert this conflict into a simple contest between monotheism and paganism. That is not what the text securely documents. It records an intra-Iranian struggle over ritual, patronage, cattle, authority, and right order.

### The Lie of the rebel kings

Behistun constructs a political narrative:

1. Darius claims legitimate Achaemenid descent and Ahuramazda’s favor.
2. Rival kings claim identities such as Bardiya or members of older dynasties.
3. These statements are declared lies.
4. “The Lie” causes provinces to rebel.
5. Ahuramazda gives Darius victory.
6. Future rulers are instructed to punish liars.

This is not simply secular propaganda detached from religion, because legitimacy, divine favor, truth, and order are inseparable in the inscription. Nor is it demonstrably a myth in which a demoness possesses rebels. The safest classification is **Achaemenid sacral-political use of the inherited truth/deception complex**.

### Druj Nasu’s occupation and expulsion

In the Vendidad cycle, death creates an acute breach in the good creation. Nasu comes from the demonic north, enters or settles upon the corpse, and spreads pollution by contact. Dogs, carrion birds, prescribed distances, corpse-bearers, cleansing agents, water, and prayers contain the danger. During the *baršnūm* cleansing Nasu is driven step by step down the body and expelled.

The myth supplies an explanation for decomposition and a ritual map for controlling it. Calling it “primitive hygiene” alone is reductive: practical avoidance of decay and a dualist account of contamination coexist in the source.

### Assault on Zarathustra

Vendidad 19 says Angra Mainyu sends “the Druj,” identified in the next phrase as Būiti, to kill Zarathustra. Zarathustra recites the Ahuna Vairya, worships the good waters, and professes Mazda-worship. Būiti retreats and admits Zarathustra’s glory makes him impossible to kill.

Darmesteter’s proposal that Būiti represents “Buddha” or Buddhism is a **nineteenth-century scholarly interpretation**, influenced by the later Greater Bundahišn. It is not established by the Avestan episode and should not be reported as the original meaning.

### Final defeat

The Gāthās anticipate Druj’s submission to Aša. Younger Avestan texts expand this into the Saoshyant’s victory and the destruction of Druj’s brood. Middle Persian cosmology makes the defeat part of the final separation and impotence of Ahriman. Druj has no independent death scene; its end is the elimination of falsehood, pollution, and demonic agency from the renovated creation.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No independent cult

There is no securely documented temple, priesthood, festival, votive inscription, theophoric personal name, statue, sacrifice, pilgrimage, or oracle dedicated to Druj. This absence is consequential: Druj belongs principally to doctrine, polemic, ritual danger, and demonology.

Plutarch reports propitiatory or averting offerings to Areimanius, including a preparation associated with darkness and wolf’s blood. Even if based on an Iranian practice, it concerns Ahriman, not Druj specifically, and comes through a Greek intellectual account centuries after the Achaemenids.

No human sacrifice to Druj is documented. No evidence supports temple prostitution, sacred marriage, or an erotic cult attached to Druj.

### Ritual combat with Druj

The practical “cultic” record consists of actions against Druj:

- truthful speech and keeping agreements;
- recitation of the Ahuna Vairya and other formulas;
- maintenance of ritual purity;
- corpse isolation and disposal;
- *sagdīd*, the gaze of a dog upon the corpse;
- work by designated corpse-bearers;
- avoidance of contaminating fire, earth, and water;
- *baršnūm* purification;
- prayers to Sraosha and other protective beings.

Vendidad rules distinguish degrees and radii of pollution, the treatment of contaminated clothing and water, and the restoration of persons and places. [Vendidad corpus](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/)

### Funerary institutions

Exposure of corpses prevents burial in earth or cremation in fire, both of which risk contaminating a good creation. Yet archaeology and historical practice are diverse: Iranian communities used exposure, ossuaries, tombs, covered burial, and later cemeteries. A tower of silence cannot automatically be inferred from every ancient excarnation or ossuary.

Medieval and modern Iranian and Parsi communities developed mortuaries, professional corpse-bearers, towers or *dakhmas*, paired ritual attendance, and prayers protecting the living. The documented practice changed under local law, urbanization, migration, and the decline of vulture populations. [History of corpse disposal](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/corpse-disposal-of-in-zoroastrianism/) [Burial survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/burial-iii/)

## 7. Historical Evolution

### I. Old Avestan formation

Druj appears as the deceptive order chosen by hostile people, priests, rulers, and daevas. Its opposite, *aša*, structures ritual efficacy, social truth, and the right ordering of existence. Personification remains fluid.

### II. Younger Avestan expansion

The contrast becomes cosmological. Druj can acquire power over embodied existence, occupy an infernal north, gather a brood, and serve as a category for demons. Ritual specialists apply the term to contract violation and material pollution. Nasu becomes the most concretely narrated Druj.

### III. Achaemenid royal appropriation

Old Persian *drauga* becomes a language of rebellion and illegitimate kingship. Darius’s historical claims cannot be verified solely from his own monument: labeling every rival a liar is part of the victory narrative. Nevertheless, Behistun is indisputable evidence that the word-complex held extraordinary public and political force by approximately 520 BCE.

### IV. Hellenistic, Parthian, and early Sasanian reception

Greek authors describe Persians as valuing truth and represent Iranian religion through a two-power schema. Their testimony concerns the broader system, not an individualized Druj. Iranian religious traditions remain regionally diverse; direct evidence for Druj in Parthian cult is sparse.

### V. Sasanian and early Islamic systematization

Priestly redaction organizes the surviving Avesta and develops Middle Persian demonology. Druj becomes a generic term for the evil spiritual host and is integrated into the 12,000-year cosmic chronology, Ahriman’s assault, the mixed state of creation, and final renovation.

The surviving Dēnkard, Bundahišn, and related books were written or redacted after the Muslim conquest, though they transmit older Sasanian learning. They must not be treated as transparent transcripts of Achaemenid belief.

### VI. Islamic-period survival

Zoroastrian communities declined demographically and institutionally under changing political, fiscal, and social conditions. Demon terminology survived in Middle and New Persian, but *dorūğ* normally became the ordinary word for “lie.” General Persian folklore retained *dīv*, *parī*, and other beings more vividly than a proper demoness Druj.

It would be inaccurate to describe Islamization as a documented campaign specifically against Druj. The relevant history is the contraction of Zoroastrian institutions, manuscript transmission within minority communities, and the persistence of purity practices.

### VII. Parsi preservation and reform

Migration to western India created the Parsi tradition. Priests maintained Avestan recitation and developed extensive funerary institutions. From the eighteenth century onward, contacts between Iranian and Indian priests, calendrical disputes, colonial scholarship, sanitary criticism, and reform movements produced competing interpretations of Nasu:

- literal female demon;
- real but non-anthropomorphic evil influence;
- ritual description of decomposition;
- ancient hygienic symbolism;
- doctrine to be retained ritually without literal demonology.

All are historically attested interpretations; none should be substituted silently for the older text.

### VIII. Present

Druj remains part of Zoroastrian moral vocabulary, especially in explanations of *aša* versus falsehood. Druj Nasu still informs traditional funerary rules, although actual disposal now varies widely among communities. There is no reliable evidence for a separate contemporary Druj-worshipping movement of significant size.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, apologetics, and psychology

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century translators often rendered Druj as “the Lie,” “Demon of Falsehood,” or “embodiment of wickedness.” These formulations helped crystallize Druj as a discrete female personality in English-language reference works. M. N. Dhalla’s 1938 history explicitly called Druj an “evil genius of Wickedness,” while also documenting the shift from one Gāthic Druj to many later drujes. [Dhalla’s treatment](https://www.avesta.org/dhalla/history4.htm)

Modern reformist presentations often psychologize Druj as wrong thinking, delusion, or destructive choice. That is a datable theological interpretation compatible with parts of the Gāthās but does not exhaust the Vendidad’s literal ritual demonology.

Jungian and New Age writings sometimes place Druj among universal “shadow” archetypes. No evidence makes this an ancient Iranian doctrine.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The major Renaissance magical, Golden Dawn, and standard grimoire traditions do not give Druj a stable office, seal, planetary rank, or invocation. Online demon lists that supply such details should be classified as **modern invention** unless they cite a traceable modern fictional or occult source.

Small reconstructionist or eclectic practitioners may invoke Iranian divine names, but no documentary basis was found for an organized historical cult of Druj. Worship of Druj as a “dark goddess,” “chaos goddess,” or liberating patron of lies reverses the ancient source tradition and is modern religious creativity, not reconstruction.

### Popular culture

Nasu, rather than the abstract Druj, appears as a monster or demon in modern games, including several entries in Atlus’s *Megami Tensei* franchise. Such appearances typically turn a ritual pollution into a visually bounded combat creature. They materially reinforce the modern idea that every named Zoroastrian evil was an individual monster.

Generic fantasy uses of “Druj,” “Druji,” or “Drujan” often mean demon, deceiver, or undead being. Unless explicitly tied to an Avestan passage, their forms and powers are fictional.

### Political appropriation

Darius’s truth-versus-Lie language is sometimes recruited into modern Iranian nationalist narratives presenting ancient Persians as uniquely truthful or cosmic defenders against deceit. Behistun genuinely makes truth a royal ideology, but its accusations are also the victor’s delegitimation of rival kings. Using them as neutral historical verdicts repeats Darius’s political program.

No well-documented modern extremist organization appears to have adopted Druj as a central emblem. General claims that political opponents are “the Lie” may echo Iranian vocabulary but do not establish an institutional Druj cult.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### What does *druj* mean?

- **Truth/lie interpretation:** traditional and contextually strong, especially in Old Persian royal inscriptions.
- **Order/deceptive order interpretation:** emphasizes *aša* as the effective structure of reality and ritual, not mere factual accuracy.
- **Harm/hostility etymology:** comparison with Indo-Iranian *druh-* suggests an older semantic field of harming, hostility, or blackening.

No one English equivalent fits every occurrence.

### Personification

Older histories often speak confidently of “the demoness Druj.” More recent philology distinguishes grammatical femininity, poetic agency, personification, and ontological individuality. The Gāthās permit personified language but provide no body, genealogy, cult, or biography.

### Zoroaster’s innovation

One position holds that Zoroaster transformed an inherited Indo-Iranian truth/falsehood contrast into the foundation of Iranian ethical dualism. Another emphasizes continuity with Indo-Iranian ritual cosmology. Kellens questions readings that make the Gāthās a fully explicit moral-philosophical dualism, while Boyce and Gnoli give greater weight to prophetic reform.

### Behistun and Zoroastrianism

The shared root and structural opposition are clear. What remains debated is whether Darius’s theology was specifically Zoroastrian, broadly Mazdaean, or an Achaemenid royal religion drawing upon related Iranian concepts. “The Lie made them rebel” cannot by itself prove adherence to every later Zoroastrian doctrine.

### Druj Nasu: demon or decay?

The Vendidad describes Nasu as an active female contaminant, while the timing of her arrival tracks decomposition. Reduction to germ theory is anachronistic, but denying the text’s observation of material decay is equally misleading. The historical doctrine joins physical corruption, ritual danger, and cosmic evil.

### Transmission and surviving evidence

The Avesta was transmitted orally and repeatedly reorganized. A Sasanian recension is described in later tradition, but only part of the canon survives. The oldest extant Avestan manuscripts are many centuries later than the compositions. Geldner’s late nineteenth-century edition remains foundational, while the Avestan Digital Archive makes manuscript witnesses accessible for comparison. [Avestan Digital Archive](https://ada.geschkult.fu-berlin.de/ada/) [History and contents of the Avesta](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/)

Discredited or inadequately supported popular claims include:

- Druj was a fully anthropomorphic prehistoric goddess with a dedicated cult.
- Druj was the Zoroastrian “Satan” in a simple one-to-one equivalence.
- Every Achaemenid use of *drauga* denotes a literal demoness.
- Nasu proves that ancient Zoroastrians possessed modern germ theory.
- Būiti certainly means Buddha and records a historical Zoroastrian-Buddhist conflict.
- Roman Mithraic monsters can be securely identified as Druj.
- Modern occult seals, colors, planets, and summoning instructions are ancient Iranian survivals.

## 10. Chronology

|---|---|
| Late second–early first millennium BCE, highly disputed | Probable linguistic horizon of the Old Avestan Gāthās; *druj-* appears eighteen times as the opponent of *aša*. |
| First millennium BCE | Younger Avestan compositions expand Druj into a cosmic hostile power and a class of demons; exact dates for individual passages remain disputed. |
| c. 520 BCE onward | Darius has the Behistun inscription carved; Old Persian *drauga-* explains rebellion and fraudulent kingship. |
| Late sixth–early fifth century BCE | Darius’s DPd inscription lists the Lie with enemy army and famine among dangers to the country. |
| c. 440 BCE | Herodotus reports Persian education in truth-speaking; he does not name Druj. |
| Hellenistic–Parthian centuries | Iranian dualist concepts circulate regionally; direct evidence for a Druj cult remains absent. |
| c. late first–early second century CE | Plutarch describes Oromazes, Areimanius, Mithras, and ritual responses to good and evil; Druj is not separately named. |
| Third–seventh centuries CE | Sasanian priestly activity compiles and interprets the Avesta; demonology and purity law become increasingly systematized. |
| Third century CE onward | Iranian Manichaean texts adapt Iranian religious vocabulary; no distinct Druj cult is established. |
| Sixth century or later | Probable compilation horizon of *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, though surviving witnesses are later. |
| Seventh century onward | Muslim conquest and gradual Islamization reduce Zoroastrian institutional dominance; communities continue Avestan ritual transmission. |
| Ninth–tenth centuries | Major surviving Middle Persian compilations, including the Dēnkard and Bundahišn recensions, organize druzes beneath Ahriman and preserve summaries of lost texts. |
| c. 1010 | Ferdowsi completes the *Shāhnāma*; deceit and demons remain central themes, but Druj is not an independent epic character. |
| Medieval period | Manuscript copying preserves Vendidad and other Avestan ritual collections; corpse-purity practice continues in Iran and India. |
| Fifteenth–eighteenth centuries | Persian Rivāyats transmit Iranian priestly rulings to Parsis, including funerary and pollution law. |
| 1771 | Anquetil-Duperron publishes a European translation of the Avesta, opening a new phase of Western Druj interpretation. |

| 1885–97 | E. W. West publishes translations of major Pahlavi books. |
| 1886–96 | Karl Friedrich Geldner publishes his critical edition of the Avesta. |
| 1904 | Bartholomae’s *Altiranisches Wörterbuch* codifies the meanings “falsehood, deceit” and related vocabulary. |
| 1938 | Dhalla’s *History of Zoroastrianism* presents Druj as the embodiment of wickedness and documents its later multiplication into drujes. |
| 1975 onward | Mary Boyce’s *History of Zoroastrianism* reconstructs the development of doctrine and ritual, sometimes using later evidence to illuminate early periods—a method subsequently debated. |
| 1994 | Humbach and Ichaporia publish a major modern English translation of the Gāthās. |
| 1996 | Kellens’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article synthesizes the lexical and conceptual disputes surrounding Druj. |
| Twenty-first century | Digital manuscript projects, especially the Avestan Digital Archive, make textual witnesses more widely inspectable. |
