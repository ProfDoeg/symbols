# Aeshma: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Zoroastrian tradition Aeshma is a *daēva*: an evil, incorporeal spirit and the hypostasis of wrath, violent fury, bloodshed, and the destruction of rightly ordered worship. He is not an Iranian god subsequently “demonized” by Judaism or Christianity; the earliest Iranian evidence already places *aēšəma* on the destructive side of the Zoroastrian moral cosmos.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Old Avestan *aēšəma-* in the Gāthās, especially *Yasna* 29.1–2 and 30.6. These orally composed texts are commonly placed around 1000 BCE, although estimates range substantially and no Avestan manuscript survives from antiquity. At this earliest stage the word means “wrath,” “violent fury,” or “violence” and may be personified, but the fully individualized demon of the Younger Avesta should not simply be projected backward into every Gāthic occurrence. The surviving written witnesses are medieval, the oldest Avestan manuscript fragments dating only from the thirteenth–fourteenth centuries; the early date rests chiefly on comparative linguistics and the relationship of Old Avestan to Vedic Sanskrit. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Avesta”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/); [“Aēšma”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/aesma-wrath/).

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** Old or Younger Avestan evidence whose linguistic antiquity is generally accepted, although preserved in medieval manuscripts.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** Middle Persian Zoroastrian exposition developing Avestan material.
- **Learned systematization:** Scholastic organization of demons, virtues, cosmic periods, and correspondences.
- **Regional variant:** A geographically or linguistically localized development.
- **Syncretistic identification:** A historical equation or adaptation across religious systems.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** Material first documented well after the Avestan and Pahlavi corpora.
- **Folklore:** Vernacular survival or transformation not demonstrably part of the learned canon.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **Modern invention:** A new ritual, image, genealogy, sigil, correspondence, or narrative advertised as ancient without ancient documentation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

Aeshma occupies the unstable boundary between an emotion and a supernatural agent. In the Gāthās, *aēšəma-* is destructive rage manifested in human and demonic conduct. In the Younger Avesta, Aēšma is clearly individualized as a hostile *daēva*, the adversary of Sraoša—religious listening, discipline, and obedience—and a fomenter of violence, especially violence that corrupts sacrifice, cattle husbandry, social order, and human judgment. Middle Persian works turn Xēšm into one of Ahriman’s principal agents, give him offices and powers, and make him an eschatological combatant.

The English “demon of wrath” is therefore useful but incomplete: Aeshma is simultaneously the demon, the emotion, and the destructive agency through which that emotion acts.

### Documented forms

- **Old Avestan:** 𐬀𐬉𐬱𐬆𐬨𐬀, conventionally transliterated *aēšəma-*.
- **Younger Avestan:** 𐬀𐬉𐬴𐬨𐬀, *aēšma-*.
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** *xēšm*, *ēšm*; the spelling can appear as 𐭧𐭩𐭱𐭬 or 𐭠𐭩𐭱𐭬 in normalized presentations.
- **Pāzand and New Persian:** *xašm*, *ḵašm*, *ḵešm*, Persian خشم, ordinarily “anger, wrath.”
- **Manichaean Parthian:** *išmag*, written ʿšmg, and plural formations meaning “wraths.”
- **Greek Jewish:** Ἀσμοδαῖος, *Asmodaios*; Codex Vaticanus has a variant *Asmód(a)us*.
- **Jewish Aramaic/Hebrew transmission:** אשמדאי, *Ašmedai/Ashmedai*.
- **Latin and later European:** *Asmodeus*, *Asmodaeus*, subsequently Asmoday, Asmodée, Asmodäus, Asmodeo.
- **Georgian:** ეშმაკი, *ešmaki*, “devil,” is commonly regarded as an Iranian loan or derivative, but it is a generic vernacular word rather than proof that the Avestan individual retained an independent Georgian cult.

### Etymology

The immediate meaning “wrath, fury, violent impulse” is secure. Indo-Iranian and wider Indo-European comparisons connect the word with an impulse to rush, drive, or become violently excited; proposed cognates include Greek *oîma* (“rush, onset”), Latin *īra* (“wrath”), and Lithuanian *aistrà* (“violent passion”). The exact reconstruction and some individual cognates remain matters of historical-linguistic analysis, not explanations supplied by an Avestan text.

The compound **\*aēšmō-daēva**, “Wrath-demon,” is reconstructed principally to explain Greek *Asmodaios* and Jewish *Ashmedai*. It is **not preserved as such in the extant Avesta**. Middle Persian *Xēšm Dēw* is attested, however, and the surviving Avesta is only a fraction of the corpus described in later tradition. This makes Iranian derivation plausible and widely accepted, but the asterisk must not be silently removed.

### Epithets and titles

- **Avestan *xrvi.dru-*:** conventionally “of the bloody/gory club” or “bloody weapon.” This is Aeshma’s characteristic epithet and contrasts with Sraoša’s *darši.dru-*, “of the strong/Ahurian club.” Translators also render it “wounding spear,” “murderous spear,” or “cruel weapon”; the variation is philological, not evidence for several separate weapons.
- **Avestan *dušxᵛarənah-*:** “of evil fortune,” “ill-fated,” *Mihr Yašt* 10.95.
- **Avestan *duždā(y)-*:** “malignant,” *Yasna* 57.25 and *Mihr Yašt* 10.97.
- **Avestan *drvant-*:** possessing or belonging to the Lie, *Mihr Yašt* 10.93.
- **Avestan *pesō.tanū-*:** “whose body is forfeit/condemned,” *Mihr Yašt* 10.97.
- **Middle Persian *dēw ī Xēšm* / Xēšm-dēw:** “the demon Wrath”; later canonical elaboration, not an independently attested Old Avestan compound.
- “Archdemon,” “chief of the daevas,” and “lieutenant of Ahriman” are interpretive summaries of his later prominence. They are not stable Avestan titles.
- “Demon of lust” belongs primarily to the reception of Asmodeus. Aeshma’s close Middle Persian association with Āz and Waran facilitates that transition, but the Avestan Aeshma is defined chiefly by violent fury, not erotic appetite.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Gāthās

The Gāthās—*Yasna* 28–34, 43–51, and 53—are the oldest stratum of the Avesta. Their date cannot be fixed from inscriptions or historical names. A frequently used working date is around 1000 BCE, while other scholars place Zarathustra and the Gāthās earlier in the second millennium. The surviving text was transmitted orally and then through a Sasanian and post-Sasanian manuscript tradition. [The Avestan text of the *Yasna*](https://wikisource.org/wiki/Avesta/Yasna) permits direct inspection of the relevant forms.

- **Yasna 29.1–2 — securely attested early material.** The Soul of the Cow complains that *aēšəma*, along with violence, rapine, savagery, and oppressive force, afflicts it. Aša is asked for a ruler or protector able to drive wrath away. Whether *aēšəma* here is grammatically a named demon or a personified common noun remains debatable. What is secure is the connection among wrath, coercion, injury to cattle, and the failure of proper pastoral protection.
- **Yasna 30.6 — securely attested early material.** The *daēvas*, deceived in their choosing, hurry together toward or into *aēšəma*, through which mortals make existence sick. The passage joins wrath to the daevas’ wrong choice but does not supply a genealogy or physical portrait.
- **Yasna 44.20 — securely attested early context.** Zarathustra asks whether the daevas have ever been good rulers and associates their adherents with violence against cattle. Aeshma is not developed as a speaking character.
- **Yasna 48.7 — securely attested early material.** The religious program calls for the suppression of wrath and violence.
- **Yasna 49.4 — securely attested early material.** Wrong-minded persons promote wrath and violence; the term remains morally and psychologically active rather than narratively characterized.

Thus the earliest Aeshma is not born, enthroned, married, or represented visually. He is known through the effects of violent fury.

### Younger Avestan corpus

Young Avestan compositions are linguistically later than the Gāthās. Some major texts may have assumed recognizable form in the first millennium BCE, but exact dates for individual passages are rarely defensible; the liturgical compilation and written canon underwent Sasanian organization.

- **Yasna 10.8 — securely attested Young Avestan material.** Ordinary intoxicating drink, as opposed to ritually prepared haoma, encourages men to embrace or come under Aeshma’s power. The passage ties wrath to drunken violence and corrupted ritual behavior.
- **Yasna 57, the Sraoša hymn — securely attested Young Avestan material.** At 57.10 Sraoša smites Aeshma after sunset with a destructive weapon and gives him a bloody wound; at 57.25 worshippers seek protection from malignant Aeshma. The hymn supplies the central combat pairing. [The critical overview of Sraoša](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sraosa/?generate_pdf=1) translates and contextualizes these passages.
- **Sraoša Yašt, *Yašt* 11.15 — securely attested Young Avestan material.** Ahura Mazda establishes Sraoša as the antagonist of Aeshma “of the gory club.” The text does not say that the two are brothers or ontological equals.
- **Mihr Yašt, *Yašt* 10.93–97 — securely attested Young Avestan material.** Aeshma appears among hostile powers opposed by Mithra; he is deprived of glory, malignant, and condemned. At 10.97 the frightened demon flees before Mithra.
- **Ard Yašt, *Yašt* 17.5 — securely attested Young Avestan material.** Drunkenness and disordered conduct are linked to Aeshma.
- **Āštād Yašt, *Yašt* 18.2 — securely attested Young Avestan material.** The Iranian Glory overcomes Angra Mainyu, Aeshma of the cruel or wounding weapon, Bushyasta, contagion, Apaosha, and hostile lands. This is a litany of vanquished adversaries rather than a narrative council of demons. [Darmesteter’s translation](https://avesta.org/ka/yt18sbe.htm).
- **Zamyād Yašt, *Yašt* 19.46 — securely attested Young Avestan material.** Aeshma functions as Angra Mainyu’s messenger. This is the clearest foundation for the later description “Ahriman’s messenger.”
- **Zamyād Yašt 19.95–96 — securely attested Young Avestan eschatology.** Before Astvat.ərəta’s truthful, demon-smiting companions, Aeshma bows and flees; the Lie and other evils are overcome in the final renovation. [Primary translation](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt19sbe.htm).
- **Vīdēvdād/Vendidad 10.13 and 11.9–12 — securely attested Young Avestan ritual material.** Recited formulas drive Aeshma “of the murderous spear” from the house, settlement, land, and persons polluted by contact with a corpse. The demon is an object of expulsion, not worship. [*Vendidad* 10](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd10sbe.htm).

### Avestan manuscripts and editions

The ancient date of the language must not be confused with the physical age of the manuscripts. The corpus reconstructed by Westergaard and Karl Friedrich Geldner depends on medieval and early-modern copies. The manuscript tradition appears to descend through relatively narrow archetypal lines, with the oldest surviving witnesses in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries and many Yašt codices considerably later. The Avestan Digital Archive’s project of reproducing manuscripts is consequently central to current textual work, while translations by Mills and Darmesteter remain historically important but must be checked against Geldner, Humbach, Kellens, Malandra, Skjærvø, and newer philology.

### Middle Persian Zoroastrian corpus

Most extant Pahlavi religious works reached their surviving form under Islam, especially between the ninth and tenth centuries CE, even when they preserve Sasanian exegesis or older traditions.

- **Bundahišn — later canonical elaboration/learned systematization.** Xēšm possesses seven powers used to destroy creatures. At the final combat Srōš seizes Xēšm, paralleling Ohrmazd’s defeat of the Evil Spirit and the Amahraspands’ defeat of their adversaries. The work’s compilation history is layered and cannot be assigned a single secure date; its extant Iranian recension includes post-conquest material. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Bundahišn”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bundahisn-primal-creation/); [primary translation, later chapters](https://www.avesta.org/mp/grb27.htm).
- **Dēnkard 3.116 and related passages — learned systematization, ninth–tenth centuries.** Xēšm can be made the counterpart of Wahman/Vohu Manah; elsewhere he predominates in the violent, selfish warrior and is associated with the baleful planet Mars. The final redaction of the *Dēnkard* belongs to the tenth century, although Ādurfarnbag’s work began in the ninth. [Transmission and dating](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/denkard/).
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad 2.19, 2.114–15, 7.14, 8.13ff., 27.36–37 — later canonical elaboration.** Xēšm is terrifying like Ahriman; he and other demons assail the dead approaching the Činwad Bridge; Srōš protects and ultimately destroys him. A counterfactual declares that, had bodiless Xēšm obtained temporal sovereignty, he would have ruled immortally until the Resurrection—more terrible than Iran’s mortal tyrants.
- **Šāyest nē Šāyest 13.43; 18.1–3; 22.17 — later ritual elaboration.** Xēšm enters the material world nightly but Srōš keeps him away. Correct *gāhānbār* and *myazd* observances, and in this priestly text *xwēdōdah* or next-of-kin marriage, frighten him.
- **Dādestān ī Dēnīg — later canonical elaboration.** Ahriman commissions Xēšm to increase slaughter; if human discord fails, he incites demons to attack one another.
- **Zādspram 34.32 — learned systematization.** Xēšm is associated with Āz, lust/desire, and heresy and becomes a commander in the demonic order.
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn 4.3 and 7.34 — medieval apocalyptic elaboration.** Xēšm’s dishevelled-haired progeny figure among the calamities of the final age; Mihr defeats Xēšm.
- **Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg 15.28; 16.38 — medieval apocalyptic elaboration.** The dishevelled-haired offspring of Xēšm are explicitly identified with Arabs. This is a post-conquest political reading of traditional demonology, not an Avestan ethnic doctrine.
- **Pahlavi Rivāyat and Persian Rivāyats — medieval/early-modern ritual elaboration.** A badly performed *yasna* is said to go to Xēšm as if offered to him. The point is polemical: ritual negligence benefits the demon.
- **Ṣaddar Bundahišn — late Zoroastrian systematization.** Xēšm casts anger and malice into human hearts and emboldens sinners.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag — absence finding.** The work’s general hell-tour demonology is relevant context, but no securely located, discrete Aeshma episode comparable to the Sraoša combat can be established from the principal published translations. Claims that Aeshma personally tortures a named class of sinners require a chapter-level citation before acceptance.

### Manichaean material

Manichaeism adopted the term rather than simply copying an individualized Zoroastrian character.

- **Parthian fragment M 741, third century CE tradition preserved in Turfan manuscripts — syncretistic adaptation.** Dark *Išmag/Xēšm* participates in the seduction of the archons, becomes naked and ashamed, and descends into earthly matter.
- **Middle Persian M 6120 and related texts — syncretistic/learned adaptation.** Wrath, greed, and lust compose the “spirituality of the body”; Xēšm is the active, destructive spirit of Āz/Hyle.
- **Plural *xēšmān/išmagān*, “Wraths” — regional Manichaean development.** The name becomes a class of aggressive powers sent by Satan, not only one demon. [Werner Sundermann’s documentary survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-pandaemonium/?generate_pdf=1).

### Achaemenid, Greek, and epic evidence: necessary absences

- **Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam:** Aeshma is not named in the surviving Achaemenid royal inscriptions. Darius names Ahuramazda and sometimes collectively invokes “the other gods,” but this is not epigraphic evidence for Aeshma. Behistun therefore does not independently date his cult or image. [Ahuramazda in the royal inscriptions](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ahura-mazda/).
- **Xerxes’ “Daiva inscription”:** its prohibition of *daiva* worship does not name Aeshma and cannot establish that Aeshma personally had an Achaemenid cult.
- **Herodotus:** no identifiable Aeshma appears in the *Histories*.
- **Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 46–47:** preserves a Greek account of Iranian dualism and lists destructive creations, but does not securely name Aeshma.
- **Mithraic material:** no Roman Mithraic inscription, relief, or initiation scene securely identifies Aeshma. The Avestan interaction with Mithra must not be used to insert Aeshma into Roman Mithraism.
- **Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed c. 1010:** Persian *xašm*, “wrath,” remains a major moral and narrative force, and later demonological lists associate Kashm/Xēšm with other vices. Nevertheless, one must distinguish the ordinary noun from a consistently individualized character. The epic supplies no major Aeshma cycle and no stable portrait comparable to its White Demon or Żaḥḥāk. Scholarly discussion of Persian epic emphasizes precisely this mobility between embodied demons and interiorized passions. [“Šāh-nāma v. Excursus”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/shahnameh/v-excursus/).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and actions

Aeshma’s securely documented domain comprises:

- uncontrolled wrath and violent impulse;
- assault, war, quarrelling, and slaughter;
- cruelty toward cattle and disruption of the pastoral order;
- intoxication-induced violence, excepting ritually sanctioned haoma;
- corruption or misdirection of sacrifice;
- discord among humans and, failing that, among demons;
- night attack and danger to the household;
- assault upon souls approaching judgment;
- political catastrophe in later apocalyptic interpretation.

Aeshma does not govern righteous anger, warfare generally, Mars, fire, sexuality, or a calendrical day in any securely attested Avestan cult. Mars is connected with the violent warrior under Aeshma’s influence in a Pahlavi scholastic passage, not assigned to him as an ancient “planetary correspondence.”

### Form and iconography

No ancient Iranian image is securely identified as Aeshma. There is no labeled relief, coin, seal, temple statue, manuscript illustration, or archaeological figure that can serve as his canonical portrait. Consequently:

- the “bloody mace” is textual, not iconographically confirmed;
- later multiheaded or animal-bodied images belong to Asmodeus or modern fantasy;
- a red-skinned, horned, winged, bull-headed, lion-headed, or serpent-bearing Aeshma is modern or derivative unless tied to a separately documented Asmodeus tradition;
- the feminine character designs used in some Japanese games are explicit modern reinventions.

His body is especially elusive because Pahlavi sources call Xēšm *amēnōg* or bodiless/spiritual. The weapon epithet makes him imaginable as an armed aggressor but does not supply facial features, clothing, sex anatomy, color, animal, or throne.

### Symbols and correspondences

Securely attested:

- **Weapon:** bloody/gory club, or in some translations a murderous spear.
- **Behavioral signs:** drunken rage, slaughter, discord, malice.
- **Temporal association:** night, because Srōš guards creation after sunset and repels him.
- **Ritual countermeasures:** Sraoša, sacred recitation, properly executed sacrifice, and purification formulas.

Not securely attested in ancient Iranian sources:

- a sigil or seal;
- a sacred animal, plant, color, number, metal, incense, zodiacal sign, or feast day;
- independent temples or priesthood;
- erotic sovereignty;
- queenship or kingship in hell;
- a fixed direction, though one Pahlavi scholastic system makes him a commander of the east.

Modern occult lists supplying such correspondences are modern inventions or transfers from Asmodeus.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy and hierarchy

No Avestan text gives Aeshma parents, birth, spouse, offspring, or siblings.

Calling him a “son of Ahriman” is therefore an interpretive modernization. The texts make him Angra Mainyu/Ahriman’s messenger, commissioned agent, or subordinate. Pahlavi apocalypse speaks of Xēšm’s “progeny,” but these are collective invaders in political eschatology, not a stable mythic family with individually named children.

Aeshma’s principal structural oppositions vary by textual system:

- **Sraoša/Srōš:** his most consistent personal antagonist.
- **Aša or Aša Vahišta:** moral and cosmological order opposed to violent disorder; partly a scholarly or Pahlavi systematization when expressed as a one-to-one demon–Amahraspand pairing.
- **Vohu Manah/Wahman:** Pahlavi scholastic counterpart because good thought excludes wrath.
- **Mithra/Mihr:** pursuer or destroyer in Avestan and apocalyptic texts.
- **Āz and Waran:** allied demonic appetite, greed, and lust in Pahlavi cosmology.
- **Astvat.ərəta/Sōšyāns:** eschatological victor before whose followers Aeshma flees.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Aeshma does not possess a biography-like myth cycle. His record consists primarily of recurring antagonistic scenes.

### The afflicted Cow

**Earliest form:** *Yasna* 29, Old Avestan.

The Soul of the Cow laments that wrath, rapine, and violent power have oppressed it. The divine consultation seeks an effective protector and righteous husbandman. Aeshma is not defeated in a narrated duel; the hymn defines the religious crisis that Zarathustra’s teaching addresses. Later interpreters often retell this as though the demon personally attacked a divine cow. The text itself is more compressed: *aēšəma* participates in a cluster of social and pastoral violence.

**Development:** Younger and Middle Persian texts convert this moral force into an increasingly individualized enemy of right sacrifice and cattle protection.

### The wrong choice of the daevas

**Source:** *Yasna* 30.6, Old Avestan.

The daevas fail to discern correctly and rush toward wrath, through which human life is sickened. The passage joins demonology to ethical choice: destructive spirits and humans alike enact evil through erroneous judgment and violence.

**Later accretion:** Lists that make Aeshma a crowned general presiding over a fixed infernal legion are not in the Gāthā.

### Sraoša’s nightly combat

**Earliest full form:** *Yasna* 57 and *Yašt* 11, Young Avestan.

Sraoša, perpetually wakeful guardian of creation and embodiment of attentive obedience, patrols the world after sunset. With his raised weapon he strikes Aeshma, inflicting a bloody wound or crushing his head. Ahura Mazda has made Sraoša Aeshma’s antagonist. Worshippers invoke Sraoša so that the demon cannot enter the protected house.

**Middle Persian development:** Srōš keeps Xēšm away during the night, protects the soul during the three nights after death, accompanies it to the Činwad Bridge, and destroys Xēšm in the final renovation. The nightly liturgical combat thus expands into psychopompic and eschatological protection.

### Mithra’s pursuit

**Source:** *Mihr Yašt* 10.93–97, Young Avestan.

Aeshma is deprived of glory and flees before Mithra’s power. The passage is not a long combat narrative and gives no reason to identify Aeshma with Roman Mithras’s bull-slaying opponent.

**Later development:** *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* 7.34 explicitly assigns Mihr the defeat of Xēšm, likely reflecting the close ritual and judicial association of Mihr and Srōš.

### Ritual expulsion

**Source:** *Vendidad* 10 and 11, Young Avestan.

After corpse pollution, the officiant recites formulas driving Aeshma from the contaminated man or woman, household, settlement, town, and land. This is a ritual drama rather than a myth in narrative prose. Correct speech reverses the demon’s invasion; the repeated geographical expansion expresses complete purification.

### Aeshma at the judgment of the dead

**Source:** *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.114–15 and related Pahlavi texts.

Xēšm joins the demons who rush upon the soul as it approaches the Činwad Bridge. Srōš protects and conducts the soul. The Iranian texts do not make Aeshma judge the dead or own the bridge; he is an assailant opposed by the legitimate guardians.

### Final defeat

**Avestan core:** *Yašt* 19.95–96.

At the renovation, Aeshma flees before Astvat.ərəta’s truthful companions. Other paired evils are overcome in sequence.

**Pahlavi elaboration:** Srōš personally seizes Xēšm. Āz ultimately swallows Xēšm and other demonic commanders before she too is destroyed. These are compatible but distinct versions: the first emphasizes the renovator’s community, the second cosmic pairings, and the third evil consuming its own forces.

### Asmodaios and Sarah: the contested Jewish transformation

**Source:** *Book of Tobit*, principally 3.8, 6.14–18, and 8.1–3; composed in Aramaic around 200 BCE, with Aramaic and Hebrew witnesses among the Qumran Cave 4 fragments and fuller Greek recensions. [Oxford summary](https://dbpia.nl.go.kr/edited-volume/33426/chapter-abstract/290251498?redirectedFrom=fulltext); [Tobit 3.8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Tobit+3%3A8&version=NRSVUE).

Sarah, daughter of Raguel, has been married seven times. The demon Asmodaios kills each bridegroom before consummation. The angel Raphael instructs Tobias to burn the heart and liver of a fish. The smoke repels the demon; Raphael pursues him to Upper Egypt and binds him. Sarah and Tobias pray before consummating their marriage.

**Iranian derivation: case for it.**

1. Greek *Asmodaios* and Aramaic/Hebrew *Ashmedai* are phonologically explicable from reconstructed Iranian *\*Aēšm(a)-daēva*.
2. Middle Persian *Xēšm Dēw* demonstrates that “Wrath-demon” was possible Iranian usage.
3. Tobit’s Mesopotamian and Median setting and other Iranian-looking elements make transmission plausible.
4. Killing bridegrooms is destructive violence compatible with Aeshma even if sexual jealousy is new.
5. The opposition between a destructive demon and a protecting angel has invited comparison with Aeshma and Sraoša.

**Objections and limits.**

1. The exact compound is absent from the surviving Avesta.
2. Avestan Aeshma attacks ritual order, cattle, and social peace; Tobit’s demon is attached to one woman and her marriages.
3. Raphael’s fish-fumigation and pursuit to Egypt have no Avestan parallel.
4. The name has also been compared with Hebrew/Aramaic *šmd*, “destroy,” though critics observe that the proposed morphology is difficult.
5. An Egyptian divine name, Śmdj, has been proposed because of Egyptian features in Tobit; this remains a minority alternative.
6. Later Ashmedai traditions differ again, often presenting a cunning, comic, or ambivalent king of demons rather than either Avestan wrath or Tobit’s bridal murderer.

**Conclusion:** Iranian name-borrowing is the strongest and most widely accepted explanation, but *Tobit*’s Asmodaios is an adapted Jewish literary demon, not simply an untranslated episode of Avestan mythology. [The principal Iranological argument](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/aesma-wrath/); [German survey of alternatives](https://www.die-bibel.de/stichwort/14045).

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No positive cult

There is no securely documented temple, shrine, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, votive inscription, theophoric personal name, pilgrimage, or image devoted positively to Aeshma in ancient or medieval Zoroastrianism.

To call him a “Persian god of wrath” is misleading. The tradition classifies him as a *daēva* to be rejected and expelled. No evidence establishes that pre-Zoroastrian Iranians worshipped a god Aeshma later demoted by Zarathustra. That hypothesis is possible only at the broad comparative level—that violent states might have been sacralized in older warrior culture—not as the documented history of this named being.

### Negative ritual presence

Aeshma nevertheless has substantial ritual importance because correct worship actively defeats him:

- Sraoša’s liturgy protects the house after sunset.
- *Vendidad* purification formulas expel him from corpse-contaminated persons and territories.
- Prayer and sacred recitation oppose the rage induced by ordinary intoxicants.
- Properly ordered sacrifice prevents brutality from distorting religious intention.
- The *gāhānbār* seasonal observances and *myazd* offerings frighten Xēšm in Pahlavi teaching.
- Later authorities warn that an improperly performed *yasna* effectively reaches Xēšm.
- Ethical control of anger is itself participation in the cosmic struggle.

These practices are not propitiatory worship of Aeshma. The language of “feeding” or “propitiating” him in moral texts is generally condemnatory: supporting an impious teacher or acting violently amounts metaphorically to serving the demon.

### Sacrifice, violence, and disputed material

The sources associate Aeshma with brutality against cattle and sacrifice distorted by rage. They do **not** document human sacrifice to him. No temple prostitution, sexual rite, blood cult, or gladiatorial practice dedicated to Aeshma is attested. Modern statements that “blood shed in anger is an offering to Aeshma” are interpretive expansions of the textual association between wrath and slaughter, not quotations from an excavated ritual manual.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Old Avestan period, conventionally c. 1200–900 BCE

*Aēšəma* is wrath and violent coercion, perhaps already personified but not supplied with an individual biography. It oppresses the Cow, attracts the wrongly choosing daevas, and works through human violence.

### Young Avestan development, broadly first millennium BCE

Aēšma becomes an unmistakable named *daēva* with epithets, especially “of the bloody club.” He is opposed by Sraoša, routed by Mithra and the Iranian Glory, used by Angra Mainyu as a messenger, and expelled by purification recitations. Eschatology guarantees his defeat.

### Achaemenid period, 550–330 BCE

The royal inscriptions provide no direct attestation. Their naming of Ahuramazda and, in one inscription, rejection of *daiva* worship supplies religious context but not an Aeshma inscription, image, or cult. Any claim that Darius or Xerxes personally fought Aeshma is allegorical modern writing.

### Hellenistic and Parthian contact, c. 300 BCE–224 CE

Around 200 BCE, *Tobit* supplies *Asmodaios*, probably an Iranian loan transformed within Jewish narrative. This is the most consequential movement of Aeshma’s name beyond Iranian religion. Direct Iranian documentation from the relevant Parthian centuries is poor, so the mechanism—oral folklore, multilingual scribal exchange, or direct religious borrowing—cannot be reconstructed.

### Sasanian systematization, 224–651

The Avesta was organized and provided with Middle Persian commentary. Although most surviving Pahlavi books are later, their demonological arrangements preserve Sasanian learning. Xēšm becomes a principal agent of Ahriman, an opponent of Srōš or Wahman, and a danger at death and the final judgment.

### Manichaean adaptation, third century onward

Manichaeans translated Xēšm into their own myth of Light and Darkness. The term could denote an aggressive quality, a “Wrathful Self,” the active power of Hyle, or a plurality of Wraths. This is genuine Iranian syncretism, but not continuity of Zoroastrian doctrine.

### Post-conquest Pahlavi period, seventh–tenth centuries

Zoroastrian authors compiled and redacted the *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, apocalypses, and ritual jurisprudence. Xēšm acquired seven powers, command functions, nightly activity, attacks upon souls, and a detailed place in the final battle.

The apocalypse’s identification of Xēšm’s dishevelled-haired progeny with Arabs or Hāšemites is a historical reaction to Islamic conquest. It documents political demonization in a beleaguered religious community; it is not an ancient prophecy naming seventh-century Arabs.

### New Persian and epic transformation, tenth century onward

The proper demon-name and the ordinary noun *xašm* converge. Persian literature often internalizes demonic wrath as a force within heroes and tyrants. Ferdowsi does not preserve a large independent Aeshma narrative. The name’s clearest continuous life in Persian is lexical and ethical.

### Jewish, Christian, and Islamic-era Asmodeus

Ashmedai develops in rabbinic literature, most famously in stories involving Solomon. Christian demonology inherits Asmodeus from Tobit and later makes him a demon of lust, marriage, luxury, or one of the seven deadly sins. These traditions belong to Asmodeus’s dossier. They are relevant to Aeshma only as reception based on a probable Iranian name-borrowing.

Islamic demonological and Solomonic traditions also contain Asmodeus-like figures, but they do not preserve an independent, demonstrably continuous cult of Xēšm.

### Modern Zoroastrianism

Aeshma remains present through the recited Avestan liturgy and ethical teaching against anger. This is continuity of rejection, not revived worship. No reliable demographic data isolate “belief in Aeshma” from Zoroastrian observance generally.

### Modern demonolatry and revival

Contemporary occultists sometimes venerate “Aeshma Daeva” as a wrathful god, infernal king, or primordial current. Online communities have composed invocations, “enns,” offerings, sigils, and theological reconciliations with Asmodeus. These practices are genuine contemporary religious phenomena but are **modern inventions**, not recoveries of an Avestan Aeshma cult. A modern manual even misclassifies Aeshma as one of “seven archangels of the Persians,” illustrating the instability of popular material. [*Modern Demonolatry*](https://demonolatry.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/ModernDemonolatry.pdf).

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and encyclopedic reception

Nineteenth-century translations made Aeshma accessible to European comparative religion. Windischmann, Darmesteter, Mills, and later scholars drew the name into discussions of Asmodeus. Older reference works often wrote “Aeshma-daeva” without marking the compound as reconstructed and sometimes called him a god of lust. Modern Iranology more carefully separates:

1. Gāthic wrath;
2. the Younger Avestan demon;
3. Pahlavi Xēšm;
4. Jewish Asmodaios/Ashmedai;
5. Christian and occult Asmodeus.

### Western occultism

The canonical Renaissance and early-modern grimoires deal with **Asmodeus/Asmoday**, not an independently recovered Avestan Aeshma. Agrippa, the *Book of Abramelin*, the *Lemegeton/Ars Goetia*, and later demonological classifications assign Asmodeus ranks, seals, legions, planetary connections, animal heads, and erotic or treasure-giving functions. None of those attributes can be transferred automatically to Aeshma.

Modern authors sometimes reverse the process, declaring the Goetic Asmodeus to be “really” Aeshma and then giving Aeshma Asmodeus’s seal, title of king, lust correspondence, and animal composite. That is a modern syncretistic practice, not textual reconstruction.

Arundell Overman’s *The Lore of Asmoday* (2019) explicitly organizes a line from Aeshma through Tobit, Jewish lore, Kabbalah, and grimoires into personal occult practice. Its value is as evidence of twenty-first-century reception, not an ancient source. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Lore_of_Asmoday.html?id=wY3AzQEACAAJ).

### Popular media

- **Atlus/Megami Tensei:** Aeshma appears as a game demon beginning with *Ronde* and subsequently in the *Persona* and *Shin Megami Tensei* franchises. Designs and gender presentation vary; game taxonomies such as “Tyrant” or “Tower Arcana” are modern fictional systems. [Game appearance record](https://www.giantbomb.com/aeshma/3005-22371/).
- **Final Fantasy X-2:** “Aeshma” is the name of a fiend type; its combat abilities are franchise invention.
- **Raid: Shadow Legends:** Aeshma is a female fantasy champion. The visual form, faction, poison abilities, and biography do not derive from Iranian evidence.
- Modern fantasy bestiaries, role-playing games, and internet mythographies frequently combine Aeshma’s mace and wrath with Asmodeus’s kingship, lust, seal, and multiheaded body.

### Political appropriation

The clearest historical political appropriation is internal to medieval Zoroastrian apocalypse: Xēšm’s progeny are read as Arab conquerors and the name is associated by pun with the Hāšemites. No comparably well-documented modern nationalist or extremist cult centered on Aeshma was found. Assertions that Aeshma is an ancient patron of ethnic warfare are unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### When did Aeshma become a person?

One approach treats the Gāthic word as already a demon’s name; another emphasizes that Old Avestan religious language readily personifies abstractions and that individuality becomes secure only in the Younger Avesta. The safest formulation is graduated: the Gāthās attest a destructive force called Wrath in a demonological field; the Younger Avesta unequivocally presents a named hostile being.

### Pre-Zoroastrian origin

Wikander and other comparativists connected Aeshma with Indo-Iranian warrior fury and *Männerbund* ideology. The association is suggestive because the word’s root concerns violent impetus and the Gāthās condemn raiding and cattle violence. Yet no pre-Zoroastrian hymn to a god Aeshma survives. “Former warrior god demonized by Zarathustra” remains a reconstruction, not an attested historical event.

### Aeshma and Asmodeus

The Iranian derivation remains strong but must be stated precisely:

- *Asmodaios* probably preserves an Iranian compound;
- that exact compound is reconstructed, not extant in Avestan manuscripts;
- Middle Persian *Xēšm Dēw* supports its plausibility;
- the borrower substantially transformed the figure;
- later Asmodeus traditions cannot be read back into Iran.

The Hebrew *šmd* and Egyptian Śmdj proposals keep the debate formally open, but neither has displaced the Iranian explanation in most modern reference scholarship.

### Sraoša or Aša Vahišta as opposite?

Aeshma’s explicit narrative adversary is Sraoša. Statements that he is the exact opposite of Aša Vahišta arise from moral logic or later schemes pairing good and evil beings. Statements that he opposes Vohu Manah are directly supported in some Pahlavi scholastic texts. These are three different relationships, not contradictions to be harmonized:

- Sraoša: ritual and martial enemy;
- Aša: cosmic-moral contrary;
- Vohu Manah: scholastic counterpart.

### “Bloody mace” or “wounding spear”?

Translations differ over *xrvi.dru-*. The core sense is a blood-producing or gory striking weapon. Treating “mace” and “spear” as two separate canonical weapons manufactures a distinction from translation history.

### Was Aeshma worshipped?

No direct evidence says so. The *Vendidad* and other texts oppose daevas and expel Aeshma. Xerxes’ suppression of *daiva* worship cannot identify the prohibited beings individually. A positive Aeshma cult, temple, sacrifice, or priesthood is therefore unestablished.

### State of the evidence

The central methodological problem is the gulf between composition and manuscripts. Old Avestan material may be early first millennium or late second millennium BCE, but its extant physical witnesses are medieval. Pahlavi books are comparatively late yet sometimes preserve lost Avestan commentary. Scholars must therefore triangulate language, meter, intertextuality, manuscript genealogy, and internal theology rather than date a belief simply by the paper on which it survives.

Important milestones include:

- nineteenth-century publication of Avestan manuscripts by Westergaard and Geldner;
- the *Sacred Books of the East* translations by Mills, Darmesteter, and West;
- recognition that Darmesteter’s extremely late dating of the Avesta was unsustainable;
- comparative linguistic study by Humbach, Kellens, Hoffmann, and Skjærvø;
- Kreyenbroek’s work on Sraoša, which clarified Aeshma’s chief opposition;
- publication of the Qumran Tobit fragments in 1995, confirming ancient Aramaic and Hebrew textual forms while not resolving every question of provenance;
- digitization of Avestan manuscripts, allowing the medieval witnesses to be compared directly.

### Commonly repeated errors

- **“Aeshma was an ancient Persian god.”** Not established; all direct Zoroastrian evidence classifies or treats him negatively.
- **“Aeshma-daeva occurs repeatedly in the Avesta.”** False; *Aēšma* occurs, but the compound is reconstructed.
- **“Aeshma is one of seven Persian archangels.”** False and apparently produced by modern occult confusion.
- **“His ancient seal is the Goetic seal of Asmodeus.”** Modern conflation.
- **“He was the Zoroastrian demon of lust.”** Overstatement; wrath is primary, while lust enters through Pahlavi association and Asmodeus reception.
- **“Aeshma killed Sarah’s husbands in Persian myth.”** False formulation; Asmodaios does so in *Tobit*.
- **“Aeshma appears on Achaemenid reliefs.”** No labeled or securely identifiable image exists.
- **“Aeshma was part of Roman Mithraism.”** No direct evidence.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Event in the documented record |
|---|---|
| **Conventionally c. 1200–900 BCE** | Old Avestan Gāthās use *aēšəma-* for destructive wrath/violence: *Yasna* 29.1–2, 30.6, 48.7, 49.4. Exact absolute dating remains disputed. |
| **First millennium BCE** | Younger Avestan texts individualize Aēšma as a *daēva* with a bloody weapon, opposed by Sraoša and Mithra and expelled by ritual speech. |
| **c. 520–490 BCE** | Darius’s Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam inscriptions name Ahuramazda but not Aeshma. |
| **Fifth century BCE** | Xerxes’ *Daiva* inscription condemns unnamed *daivas*; it does not identify Aeshma. |
| **c. 200 BCE** | *Tobit* is composed substantially in Aramaic; Asmodaios kills Sarah’s seven bridegrooms and is expelled by Raphael. Probable but debated Iranian name-borrowing. |
| **First centuries BCE–CE** | Greek forms of *Tobit* transmit Ἀσμοδαῖος; Jewish Ashmedai traditions begin a separate reception history. |
| **Third century CE onward** | Manichaean Parthian and Middle Persian literature adapts Xēšm/Išmag as destructive Hyle, the wrathful self, and plural “Wraths.” |
| **Sasanian period, 224–651** | The Avestan canon and its Middle Persian exegesis are organized; later books preserve parts of this scholastic demonology. |
| **Seventh–tenth centuries** | Post-conquest Pahlavi works elaborate Xēšm’s powers, nightly activity, attack at the Činwad Bridge, and eschatological defeat. |
| **Ninth century** | Ādurfarnbag and successors compile major portions of the *Dēnkard*. |
| **Tenth century** | Final redaction of the *Dēnkard*; Xēšm is integrated into systematic virtue–vice correspondences. |
| **c. 1010** | Ferdowsi completes the *Šāhnāma*; *xašm* remains a major interior and ethical force but Aeshma has no independent epic cycle. |
| **Post-conquest apocalyptic redactions** | Xēšm’s “progeny” are interpreted as Arab or Hāšemite invaders. |
| **1323 and after** | Oldest surviving dated Avestan manuscript witnesses; the gap from oral composition remains fundamental. |
| **1771** | Anquetil-Duperron publishes a European translation of the Avesta, greatly expanding Western access. |
| **1850s–1890s** | Westergaard and Geldner publish major Avestan editions; Mills, Darmesteter, and West translate Avestan and Pahlavi texts in *Sacred Books of the East*. |
| **1863** | Windischmann discusses Aeshma’s etymology and relationship to Asmodeus. |
| **1920s–1950s** | Comparative and syncretistic studies by Reitzenstein, Schaeder, Wikander, Boyce, and others refine Iranian and Manichaean contexts. |
| **1952** | Aramaic and Hebrew Tobit fragments are found in Qumran Cave 4. |
| **1975 onward** | Boyce’s *History of Zoroastrianism* and increasingly rigorous Avestan studies historicize the textual layers. |
| **1983** | Jes P. Asmussen’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article synthesizes the Avestan, Pahlavi, Manichaean, and Jewish evidence. |
| **1985** | Kreyenbroek’s study of Sraoša provides a major analysis of Aeshma’s principal adversarial relationship. |
| **1995** | Qumran Tobit manuscripts receive full scholarly publication, transforming Tobit textual criticism. |
| **Twenty-first century** | Digital manuscript projects expand access; games and occult publications reinvent Aeshma as a visible, summonable, or playable demon. |
| **Present** | In living Zoroastrian practice Aeshma remains a figure rejected through liturgy and ethical control of wrath; positive “Aeshma worship” belongs to small, decentralized modern occult milieus whose scale cannot be established reliably. |
