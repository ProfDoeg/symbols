# Ahura Mazda: Research Dossier

**Class:** Supreme creator god in Zoroastrian tradition; in the Gāthās, the “Wise Lord” and highest of the *ahuras*; in later Zoroastrian theology, the uncreated, wholly good creator opposed by—but ultimately victorious over—Angra Mainyu/Ahriman.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The first unequivocal, independently datable physical attestations are the Old Persian royal inscriptions of Darius I, beginning with the monumental trilingual Behistun inscription, ca. 520–518 BCE. An Assyrian god-list containing *as-sa-ra ma-za-aš*, perhaps eighth–seventh century BCE, may preserve the name earlier, but both its dating and identification with Ahura Mazda remain disputed. The Old Avestan Gāthās are linguistically older than the Achaemenid inscriptions, but their oral composition cannot be assigned an uncontested absolute date.

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early]** Secure or probable early Iranian evidence: Old Avestan material or securely dated Achaemenid documentation.
- **[Canonical]** Younger Avestan doctrine incorporated into the liturgical canon.
- **[Late canonical]** Sasanian or early Islamic-period Zoroastrian systematization, principally Middle Persian texts.
- **[Regional]** A geographically restricted development.
- **[Syncretistic]** A documented equation, translation, or adaptation between traditions.
- **[External testimony]** Greek, Armenian, Syriac, Arabic, or other non-Zoroastrian reporting.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A conclusion proposed by modern scholarship rather than stated by an ancient source.
- **[Modern reception/invention]** A demonstrably recent reinterpretation, image, or practice.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

In the Gāthās—seventeen Old Avestan hymns embedded in *Yasna* 28–34, 43–51, and 53—Mazdā Ahura or Ahura Mazdā is Zarathustra’s highest divine interlocutor: knowing, truthful, creator or father of the ordered cosmos, allied with *aša* (“truth,” “right order”) and approached through *Vohu Manah* (“Good Mind”). The hymns ordinarily address him directly, but sometimes address him together with entities later classified as the Aməša Spəntas. Their ontology is therefore not reducible without remainder either to “separate gods” or to “mere attributes.” The Gāthās can speak of Mazdā as father of Aša and Vohu Manah and of Spəntā Ārmaiti as his daughter; later theology turns these relationships into a structured divine heptad. [Iranica’s survey of the Gāthās](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/) notes that the overwhelming majority of the first four Gāthās’ stanzas address Ahura Mazda.

In Achaemenid inscriptions he is *Auramazdā/Ahuramazdā*, “the great god,” creator of earth, sky, humankind, and human happiness, whose favor legitimates the king. Yet the inscriptions also acknowledge “the other gods who are,” and Artaxerxes II and III name Mithra and, in some texts, Anāhitā. Thus Achaemenid evidence establishes supremacy, royal patronage, and creator-language, but does not by itself establish the precise theology found in either the Gāthās or later Pahlavi books.

In Middle Persian texts he is Ohrmazd, the good, omniscient creator. His creation is attacked by Ahreman, but material existence becomes the arena in which evil will finally be rendered powerless. In living Zoroastrian worship he remains the supreme object and ultimate destination of worship, even where prayers honor individual *yazatas*.

### Names and scripts

- **Old Avestan:** *Mazdā Ahura* and *Ahura Mazdā*; Avestan script 𐬀𐬵𐬎𐬭𐬀 𐬨𐬀𐬰𐬛𐬁. The order varies in the Gāthās.
- **Young Avestan:** *Ahura Mazdā*, frequently with epithets such as *dātar-* (“creator”) and *mainiiu spəništa* (“most beneficent spirit”).
- **Old Persian:** *Auramazdā* or conventionally *Ahuramazdā*, written as one name in royal cuneiform.
- **Elamite:** forms such as *U-ra-maš-da*.
- **Imperial Aramaic:** ʾhwrmzd, including the Aramaic transmission of Darius’s Behistun text.
- **Greek:** Ὠρομάζης/Ὠρομάσδης, *Ōromazēs/Oromasdēs*; Latinized *Oromazes*.
- **Parthian:** *Aramazd*.
- **Armenian:** Արամազդ, *Aramazd*.
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** *Ohrmazd*, also *Hormizd/Hurmizd* in personal-name and later linguistic developments.
- **New Persian:** اهورامزدا *Ahurā Mazdā*; اورمزد *Ormazd*; هرمزد *Hormozd/Hormuzd*.
- **Arabic and Islamic Persian literature:** هرمز/Hurmuz and related forms.
- **Western early-modern usage:** *Ormuzd*, *Ormazd*, *Oromasdes*.

The forms and their imperial-language equivalents are conveniently assembled in the recent documentary overview at [Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/ahura-mazda).

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic core]** *Ahura-* means “lord” or a member of the class of “lords,” cognate with Vedic Sanskrit *ásura-*. The latter is not originally a simple word for “demon”; several early Vedic gods, notably Varuṇa, bear the title. Later Indian demonization of the *asuras* and Iranian demonization of the *daēvas* are divergent historical developments, not a primordial dictionary equivalence.

*Mazdā-* is connected with the Indo-Iranian vocabulary of thought and wisdom. Two closely related translations dominate:

1. “Wise Lord,” treating *mazdā-* adjectivally.
2. “Lord Wisdom” or “Lord of Wisdom,” treating it as a substantive/name.

The grammar and original semantic division remain debated. Almut Hintze summarizes the likely historical structure as Ahura “Lord,” the title, and Mazdā “Wise One,” the name; Mary Boyce records the continuing substantive-versus-adjective controversy in [“Ahura Mazdā”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ahura-mazda/).

**[Disputed reconstruction]** The claim that Ahura Mazda was simply identical with Vedic Varuṇa, or with an unnamed supreme Asura reconstructed behind the *Rigveda*, is not established. Shared Indo-Iranian terminology and functional parallels are real; one-to-one identity is a comparative hypothesis.

### Principal epithets and titles

- **Mazdā**, “Wise [One]/Wisdom.” [Early]
- **Ahura**, “Lord.” [Early]
- **Dadvah/Dātar**, “Creator.” Prominent in Younger Avestan and Middle Persian usage; reflected in the calendrical epithet *Day/Dae*, “Creator.” [Canonical]
- **Mainiiu Spəništa**, “Most Beneficent/Bounteous Spirit.” Younger Avestan language that tends to identify Ahura Mazda more closely with Spenta Mainyu than the Gāthās unambiguously do. [Canonical]
- **Ašavan**, “truthful,” “possessing aša.” [Canonical]
- **All-seeing/all-knowing:** Gāthic and later theological characterization; developed into Ohrmazd’s omniscience in the Pahlavi books. [Early → late canonical]
- **Greatest/best of the gods:** Younger Avestan and external formulations; compare Yašt 17’s genealogy of Aši. [Canonical]
- **Ohrmazd ī xwadāy**, “Ohrmazd the Lord.” Middle Persian devotional usage. [Late canonical]
- **Dādār Ohrmazd**, “Creator Ohrmazd,” common in later Zoroastrian usage. [Late canonical → living tradition]

The calendrical system assigns the first day of every month to Ahura Mazda and the eighth, fifteenth, and twenty-third to his creator-title *Dadvah/Day*; the tenth month is likewise *Day*. These four dedications produced four name-day observances in the month Day. See [Iranica, “Day”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/day/).

### Equations and translations

- **Oromazes as “light”:** Plutarch compares Oromazes with light and Areimanius with darkness and ignorance. This is Greek philosophical exposition of Iranian teaching, not an Iranian identification with a named Greek god. [External testimony, ca. 100 CE]
- **Zeus/Jupiter:** Classical and Armenian interpretive contexts sometimes place Oromazes/Aramazd in the sovereign position occupied by Zeus. This is interpretatio, not proof that worshippers regarded the beings as identical. [Syncretistic]
- **Armenian Aramazd:** An Iranian-derived supreme god incorporated into the Armenian pantheon, with cult images and local genealogy. This is a major regional development under Iranian political and religious influence, not simply unchanged Iranian Mazdaism. [Regional/syncretistic]
- **Manichaean terminology:** Middle Iranian Manichaean texts could translate members of Mani’s cosmology with Iranian divine names; such lexical equations do not prove that Manichaean beings were understood as the Zoroastrian originals. [Syncretistic]
- **The biblical/Christian/Islamic God:** Modern Zoroastrian apologetic and interreligious writing often calls Ahura Mazda “God” in an explicitly monotheistic sense. “God” is a reasonable English translation, but claims of exact theological identity are modern confessional comparisons, not ancient historical equations.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The possible Assyrian attestation

An Assyrian god-list names *as-sa-ra ma-za-aš*. Boyce assigned it probably to the eighth century BCE and treated it as the earliest western-Iranian trace of Ahura Mazda. The tablet’s underlying list may be older, however, and both the reading and identification have been challenged. It is therefore the **earliest possible**, not earliest indisputable, attestation. It says no more than a divine name in a list: it supplies no Zoroastrian doctrine, myth, image, or cult description.

### The Gāthās and Yasna Haptaŋhāiti

**Language:** Old Avestan.  
**Composition:** orally composed before the Younger Avesta and linguistically close to Vedic Sanskrit. Proposals range widely, often around the second or early first millennium BCE; no absolute date is secure.  
**Manuscripts:** much later—principally medieval and early-modern copies descended from Sasanian liturgical transmission.

The Gāthās portray Ahura Mazda as the source and guardian of the truthful order. Notable passages include:

- *Yasna* 28: invocation of Mazda, Aša, and Vohu Manah.
- 30.3–5: the two spirits who choose between good and evil. Whether the beneficent spirit is Ahura Mazda himself, his active aspect, or a distinguishable being is contested.
- 31.7–11: Mazda as primordial knower and creator through thought.
- 31.8 and 44.3: fatherhood of Aša and Vohu Manah.
- 43: recognition of Mazda through Good Mind.
- 44: a sequence of questions concerning creation and cosmic order.
- 45.4: Spəntā Ārmaiti called Mazda’s daughter.
- 47 and 51: creation through the beneficent spirit.

The *Yasna Haptaŋhāiti* (Y. 35–41), also Old Avestan, worships Ahura Mazda with Aša and the good creations. It may preserve a communal liturgical idiom distinct from the poetically individualized Gāthās.

Reliable modern study requires editions by scholars such as Karl Hoffmann, Helmut Humbach, Jean Kellens, Prods Oktor Skjærvø, and Almut Hintze; older translations by Mills and Darmesteter remain historically important but should not control disputed grammar. A public English corpus is available at [Avesta.org’s Yasna collection](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/).

### Younger Avesta

The Younger Avestan corpus was composed and redacted across a long period; individual passages may preserve pre-Zoroastrian Iranian material but survive in Zoroastrianized form.

- **Yasna:** the central liturgy. Ahura Mazda is repeatedly invoked as creator and highest recipient of worship.
- **Visperad:** liturgical extensions honoring “all the lords.”
- **Yašt 1, Ohrmazd Yašt:** a relatively late hymn structured as a dialogue in which Zarathustra asks Ahura Mazda for protective names; the famous enumerations of divine names belong here, not demonstrably to the Gāthās.
- **Yašt 5, Ābān/Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā:** Ahura Mazda instructs Zarathustra to worship Anāhitā; Mazda himself appears among her sacrificers in its mythic catalogue.
- **Yašt 10, Mihr Yašt:** Ahura Mazda says that when he created Mithra, he made him as worthy of sacrifice and praise as himself. The text is directly accessible in an instructional edition from the [University of Texas](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/aveol/50).
- **Yašt 14, Wahrām/Bahrām Yašt:** Ahura Mazda identifies the approaching figure as Vərəθraγna, “made by Ahura.”
- **Yašt 17, Aši Yašt:** Aši’s father is Ahura Mazda; her mother is Spenta Armaiti; Sraosha, Rashnu, and Mithra are her brothers; Daēnā her sister.
- **Yašt 19, Zamyād Yašt:** cosmic and royal glory and the final victory of the good creation.
- **Vendīdād/Vīdēvdād:** presented as a dialogue between Ahura Mazda and Zarathustra, including the story of Yima’s enclosure, lands and their counter-creations, purity law, demons, and ritual remedies. Its dialogue frame is canonical literary theology, not a datable transcript.
- **Khorda Avesta/Niyāyišns:** later prayer collections directing worship ultimately toward Ahura Mazda while honoring fire, sun, Mithra, waters, and other yazatas.

### Achaemenid inscriptions

**Behistun, ca. 520–518 BCE:** carved high on the cliff at Bisotun in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian, with accompanying royal relief. Darius repeatedly says his victories occurred *vašnā Auramazdāha*, “by the favor of Ahuramazda.” He attributes rebellion to the Lie and presents political restoration as divinely sanctioned. Column V says the Saka did not worship Ahuramazda and that Darius subdued them by the god’s favor; the preserved translation and transliteration are available at [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/behistun-persian-text/behistun-t-46/). [Early, political theology]

**Naqš-e Rostam, DNa/DNb, ca. 490 BCE:** Ahuramazda created earth, sky, humanity, and happiness and made Darius king. The text links divine creation to imperial hierarchy and royal ethical self-presentation. [Early]

**Persepolis and Susa inscriptions:** Darius and Xerxes invoke Ahuramazda as creator and protector; Xerxes’s “Daiva inscription” associates royal action against a place where *daiva* were worshipped with obedience to Ahuramazda, but its precise historical setting and whether it records religious persecution are disputed.

**Artaxerxes II, 404–359 BCE:** inscriptions at Susa and Hamadan invoke “Ahuramazda, Anāhitā, and Mithra.” [Early/syncretistic royal triad]

**Artaxerxes III:** A3Pa asks Ahuramazda and Mithra to preserve king and country; see the [text and monument record](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/a3pa/).

These inscriptions do not name Zarathustra, Angra Mainyu, the Aməša Spəntas as such, or a canonical Avesta. Whether “Achaemenid religion” should simply be called Zoroastrianism consequently remains disputed.

### Greek and Roman witnesses

- **Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131–140, ca. 430 BCE:** describes Persian sacrifice without temples, altars, or images and says Persians call the whole circuit of heaven “Zeus.” This probably interprets Iranian supreme-divinity language, but Herodotus never writes “Ahura Mazda”; identifying his “Zeus” directly with Mazda is an inference.
- **Eudemus of Rhodes, late fourth century BCE, preserved in Damascius:** reports that Magi derived Oromazes and Areimanios from Time or Space. This is crucial but indirect evidence for early forms of Zurvanite speculation.
- **Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 46–47, ca. 100 CE:** says Oromazes sprang from pure light and Areimanius from darkness; they war; Oromazes creates six gods and Areimanius six counter-gods; Mithra stands between them. The [Greek/Loeb presentation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/H/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Isis_and_Osiris%2A/C.html) preserves a major external account, but Plutarch filters Iranian material through Greek philosophy and his own comparative agenda.

### Sasanian inscriptions and art

Royal inscriptions use forms of Ohrmazd in titles such as “Mazda-worshipping god [king].” Kirdēr’s third-century inscriptions describe the institutional strengthening of the Mazdayasnian religion and attacks upon rival communities; a title *Ohrmazd-mowbed* appears in his Kaʿba-ye Zardošt inscription. The king’s religious legitimacy was thus made explicitly Mazdean, while priestly power and imperial policy became closely joined.

### Pahlavi books

Most surviving copies are late, but their works preserve Sasanian and early Islamic-period learning.

- **Bundahišn, principally ninth century with later recensions:** cosmogony, cosmic combat, creation, geography, anthropology, and eschatology. The Iranian and Indian recensions differ. Its declared subject is “the primal creation of Ohrmazd and the counter-onslaught of the Evil Spirit”; see [Iranica’s textual history](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bundahisn-primal-creation/).
- **Dēnkard, ninth–tenth centuries:** encyclopedic defense and organization of Zoroastrian knowledge; Books 8–9 summarize lost Avestan *nasks*. It records disagreement over whether only Gāthic material was genuinely Ohrmazd’s revelation.
- **Dādestān ī Mēnōg ī Xrad, probably ninth century:** wisdom-dialogue on creation, ethics, ritual, and eschatology.
- **Selections of Zādspram, ninth century:** cosmology and the developed biography of Zarathustra, including his assembly with Ohrmazd and the Amahraspands.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag, redaction probably ninth–tenth centuries:** visionary journey through heaven and hell, framed as restoration of religious confidence.
- **Škand Gumānīg Wizār, ninth century:** apologetic defense of Zoroastrian dualism against rival religions.
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn:** apocalyptic periodization, including catastrophe following the fall of Zoroastrian sovereignty.
- **Persian Rivāyats, fifteenth–eighteenth centuries:** correspondence between Indian Parsis and Iranian priests, preserving ritual rulings and later learned tradition.

E. W. West’s dated but important translations are collected in [*Pahlavi Texts*, Sacred Books of the East 5](https://sacred-texts.com/zor/sbe05/index.htm).

### Ferdowsi

The *Šāhnāmeh*, completed ca. 1010 CE, draws extensively on Iranian royal legend and Sasanian historical traditions but speaks in the Islamic-era language of *Khudā* and divine justice. “Hormozd” also occurs as the name of Sasanian kings. It is therefore not a straightforward scripture of Ahura Mazda; identifying every reference to “God” as a direct preservation of Ohrmazd theology would erase Ferdowsi’s Islamic Persian setting. [Medieval literary reception]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

Across the record Ahura Mazda is associated with:

- wisdom and right discernment;
- *aša*, truth and cosmic-social order;
- creation of the good world;
- light, though not simply identical with the physical sun;
- righteous sovereignty and royal legitimacy;
- protection of the good creation;
- revelation to Zarathustra;
- judgment and eventual renovation of the world.

The Gāthic Mazda is not primarily a specialized “sky god,” although creation of sky and association with light occur. “Sky god” is an overly narrow modern taxonomy.

### Form and anthropomorphism

The Gāthās use bodily metaphors: eyes, hands, tongue, fatherhood, speech. These do not by themselves establish cult statues. The Younger Avesta gives Ahura Mazda a star-decked garment. Such language permits anthropomorphic imagination while coexisting with the absence of a stable temple image.

### The Achaemenid winged figure

This remains the central iconographic dispute.

A winged disk, sometimes containing a bearded human figure holding a ring, appears above Achaemenid royal scenes. It has been interpreted as:

1. **Ahura Mazda:** the figure’s position above the king, gesture, and relation to inscriptions invoking Ahuramazda support a divine identification.
2. **Royal *xᵛarənah/farr*:** the symbol represents royal glory or fortune, not a portrait of the god.
3. **A polyvalent royal-divine emblem:** inhabited forms could evoke a deity and uninhabited forms royal glory; meanings may have shifted by context.

The motif has Egyptian and Mesopotamian antecedents and resembles Assyrian representations of Aššur. No surviving Achaemenid caption says “this figure is Ahuramazda.” Accordingly, modern uses of the symbol as an unquestionable portrait of Ahura Mazda—or as an equally unquestionable image of a *fravaši*—overstate the evidence. The disagreement can be followed in [Iranica’s survey of Achaemenid royal representations](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-visual-reps/) and its [Naqš-e Rostam article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/naqs-e-rostam/).

### Sasanian images

The identification is firmer in several Sasanian investiture reliefs:

- **Ardašir I at Naqš-e Rostam, ca. 224–240 CE:** Ohrmazd and Ardašir face one another on horseback; Ohrmazd hands the king a beribboned ring/diadem. Under their horses lie defeated figures, commonly Ahriman and the last Arsacid king. The composition equates earthly victory with cosmic order without making king and god identical.
- **Ardašir’s standing investiture:** god and king meet across a fire altar.
- **Šāpur I and successors:** the investiture type continues in varying forms.
- **Ṭāq-e Bostān relief conventionally assigned to Ardašir II, fourth century:** the king receives a ring from a figure usually identified as Ohrmazd, while another divine figure—commonly Mithra—stands on a lotus holding barsom.

Ohrmazd appears as a dignified male, standing or mounted, usually bearded, wearing a distinctive mural or turreted crown and sometimes holding priestly barsom. The precise identification of individual relief figures can remain debated, especially where no caption survives. The Ardašir relief sequence is described in [Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ardasir-ii/), while the investiture convention is summarized under [“Investiture”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/investiture/).

### Symbols

- **Fire:** foremost ritual symbol of *aša*, purity, and divine presence; fire is not Ahura Mazda’s literal body and Zoroastrians do not “worship fire” as a separate supreme god.
- **Light:** theological and comparative symbol, especially in external reports.
- **Barsom:** ritual bundle associated with priestly worship and sometimes held by Ohrmazd in art.
- **Investiture ring/diadem:** divine authorization of kingship.
- **Winged disk:** disputed Achaemenid emblem.
- **White:** commonly connected with purity in later practice, but no single ancient canon establishes it as Ahura Mazda’s exclusive color.
- **Calendar:** day 1 and the three *Day/Dae* days; month Day.
- **No exclusive sacred animal, planet, or weapon** is securely and consistently assigned to Ahura Mazda. Modern lists that give him an eagle, a particular crystal, zodiac sign, or magical “correspondence” generally derive from recent occult or neopagan synthesis.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical models

**Gāthic relational language:** Ahura Mazda is father of Aša and Vohu Manah; Spenta Armaiti is his daughter. These are theological metaphors as well as genealogical statements. No mother of Mazda is supplied. [Early]

**Later orthodox system:** Ohrmazd is uncreated. The Amahraspands are his creations, emanations, powers, or inseparable associates, depending on text and interpretation. The tradition does not maintain one philosophically uniform relation between substance, attribute, and independent divine person. [Canonical/late canonical]

**Earth as consort:** Spenta Armaiti, simultaneously Devotion and Earth, can occupy a consort-like relation to Ahura Mazda. The *Ahurānīs*, “wives of the Ahura,” are associated with waters. These are old ritual and mythic relationships, not evidence of a single stable domestic family. [Early/canonical]

**Zurvanite genealogy:** Zurvan produces Ohrmazd and Ahriman as twins. Ohrmazd results from Zurvan’s thousand-year sacrifice; Ahriman from his doubt. Ahriman breaks out first and claims temporary rule under Zurvan’s promise to the first-born. This account is known principally from Armenian, Syriac, Greek, Arabic, and Manichaean witnesses and is absent as an orthodox creation story from the extant Avesta. [Sectarian/external testimony]

### Documented divine interactions

The following are discrete interactions, not merely pantheon membership:

- **Aša/Aša Vahišta:** Mazda is called Aša’s father and creates or establishes through Aša; *Yasna* 31.8, 44.3 and related Gāthic passages. **[Early]**
- **Vohu Manah:** Mazda is his father; Vohu Manah mediates the worshipper’s recognition and approach to Mazda; *Yasna* 28, 31, 43, 45. **[Early]**
- **Spenta Armaiti:** called Mazda’s daughter; she grants strength and embodies devotion/earth; *Yasna* 45.4 and related passages. **[Early]**
- **Spenta Mainyu:** Mazda creates through the Beneficent Spirit; *Yasna* 31.3, 44.7, 47, 51.7. Later texts increasingly identify the two. **[Early; later canonical identification]**
- **Haurvatāt and Amərətāt:** Mazda associates Wholeness/Health and Immortality with the reward of the righteous; Gāthic passages including Y. 34 and 51. **[Early]**
- **The six Aməša Spəntas collectively:** Plutarch’s Oromazes creates six gods; *On Isis and Osiris* 47, ca. 100 CE. The Bundahišn makes Ohrmazd their creator and assigns each a material creation. **[External testimony; late canonical]**
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman:** cosmic antagonist. In the Bundahišn Ohrmazd offers peace, fixes the limited period of conflict, reveals final victory through the Ahunwar, and causes Ahriman’s stupor; Ahriman later invades the good creation. **[Gāthic opposition; late canonical narrative]**
- **Zurvan:** fathers Ohrmazd and Ahriman in the Zurvanite myth; Eudemus/Damascius, Eznik, Theodore Abū Qorra, and other witnesses. **[Sectarian/external]**
- **Mithra:** Ahura Mazda creates Mithra as worthy of sacrifice and praise as himself; *Mihr Yašt* 10.1. Mithra also addresses Mazda as lord in the same hymn. **[Canonical]**
- **Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā:** Mazda instructs Zarathustra to worship her; within Yašt 5’s catalogue Mazda also sacrifices to her for success in propagating the religion. **[Canonical; reciprocal ritual hierarchy]**
- **Vərəθraγna:** Mazda identifies him as “made by Ahura” when explaining his manifestations to Zarathustra; Yašt 14. **[Canonical]**
- **Aši Vanguhi:** Ahura Mazda is named as Aši’s father; Spenta Armaiti her mother; Yašt 17.15–16. **[Canonical genealogy]**
- **Sraosha:** later tradition makes Sraosha an obedient executor of divine order and guardian of souls; he belongs to Aši’s sibling group in Yašt 17. No independent combat or quarrel with Mazda is attested. **[Canonical]**
- **Rashnu:** likewise a just divine judge under Mazda’s order and Aši’s brother in Yašt 17; no separate conflict with Mazda is attested. **[Canonical]**
- **Daēnā:** named as Aši’s sister and the religion/vision of the Mazda-worshipper; no separate narrative meeting with Mazda is securely preserved. **[Canonical]**
- **Ātar/Fire:** fire belongs to or is called the son of Ahura Mazda in Avestan tradition and mediates judgment; the exact literalness of “son” varies. **[Canonical]**
- **Apąm Napāt:** one of the lesser *ahuras* in older Iranian religion; modern reconstruction proposes that Mazda absorbed some of his creator or water-cosmology functions. No surviving early story narrates a direct confrontation. **[Modern reconstruction from early cultic structure]**
- **Tištrya:** a Mazda-created or Mazda-aligned rain-star who fights Apaōša; Mazda strengthens him when worship is insufficient. *Tištar Yašt*. **[Canonical]**
- **Yima:** not a god in Zoroastrian classification, but a divinely favored primordial king. Ahura Mazda warns him of the deadly winter and commands construction of the *vara*; *Vendīdād* 2. **[Canonical]**
- **Gayōmard:** Ohrmazd creates and protects the primordial man; Ahriman’s assault causes his death, from which humanity ultimately develops; Bundahišn. **[Late canonical]**
- **Primordial Bull/Gāw ī ēwdād:** Ohrmazd creates it; Ahriman kills it, and its purified seed generates beneficent animals; Bundahišn. **[Late canonical]**
- **Ahriman’s demons collectively:** Ohrmazd does not create their evil in standard dualist theology; his creations and Amahraspands oppose them throughout the Bundahišn. **[Late canonical]**
- **Manichaean Father of Greatness/Zurvan:** Persian Manichaean vocabulary used *Zurwān* for the Father of Greatness and Iranian names for other beings. No sound evidence makes Ahura Mazda and the Manichaean Father simply identical. **[Syncretistic terminology]**
- **Armenian Anahit and Mihr:** Aramazd headed a localized Armenian pantheon in which Anahit could be described as his daughter and Mihr as related within the royal-divine family. These are Armenian developments, not recoverable as universal Iranian genealogy. **[Regional/syncretistic]**

No securely attested divine marriage ceremony, sexual adventure, theft, or episodic combat comparable to Greek myth is preserved for Ahura Mazda. His narratives are chiefly cosmogonic, revelatory, and juridical.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Gāthic choice of the two spirits

*Yasna* 30 describes two primordial “twins” or paired spirits, alike in sleep and speech but choosing oppositely: the better chooses truth, the worse the Lie. The passage does not plainly say that Ahura Mazda fathered both. Interpretations include:

- Spenta Mainyu and Angra Mainyu as two spirits subordinate to Mazda;
- Mazda’s beneficent spirit contrasted with Angra Mainyu;
- an ethical-poetic opposition later converted into a systematic cosmogony.

Claims that the Gāthās teach either fully developed later dualism or uncomplicated philosophical monotheism go beyond the passage’s ambiguities. [Early]

### Creation and assault in the Bundahišn

Ohrmazd exists in endless light above; Ahriman in darkness below, separated by a void. Ohrmazd, omniscient, knows the conflict’s outcome; Ahriman initially does not. Ohrmazd proposes peace, which Ahriman rejects, then sets a finite term for combat. In the common Pahlavi scheme, world history comprises 12,000 years:

1. 3,000 years of spiritual existence;
2. 3,000 years of material creation while Ahriman lies stunned;
3. 3,000 years of mixture after Ahriman’s assault;
4. 3,000 years leading toward renovation and evil’s defeat.

Ohrmazd recites the Ahunwar prayer and reveals the final outcome; Ahriman collapses for 3,000 years. Ohrmazd performs a spiritual *yasna* and creates sky, water, earth, plant, primordial bull, primordial human, and fire, assigning them to Amahraspand protectors. Ahriman attacks and contaminates each. Death paradoxically releases reproductive potential: plants multiply, animals arise from the bull’s seed, and humans from Gayōmard’s seed. Material creation is thus not a fall away from spirit but Ohrmazd’s trap and battlefield against evil. [Late canonical systematization]

The [Iranica cosmology survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cosmogony-i/) emphasizes both the ordered creations and Ahriman’s pollution of them.

### The Zurvanite “prequel”

Zurvan sacrifices for a son for a thousand years, then doubts. From sacrifice comes Ohrmazd; from doubt, Ahriman. Learning of the twins, Zurvan promises sovereignty to the first-born. Ahriman tears out prematurely; Zurvan recognizes his foulness but must grant him limited rule. Ohrmazd, bright and fragrant, remains ultimately superior.

This myth subordinates Ohrmazd genealogically without necessarily denying his ultimate victory. Its apparently coherent form comes mainly from hostile Christian accounts such as Eznik of Kolb and from scattered Manichaean and Islamic reports. Whether “Zurvanism” was a unified church, a widespread Sasanian orthodoxy, a court theology, or a family of speculative myths is debated. The safest conclusion is that the myth was genuinely Iranian and circulated widely enough to be reported independently, but its institutional extent cannot be measured. See the modern reassessment in [Iranica, “Zurvanism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zurvanism/).

### Revelation to Zarathustra

Younger Avestan texts repeatedly frame revelation as dialogue: “Ahura Mazda said to Spitama Zarathustra.” Later Pahlavi narratives literalize and expand the prophet’s encounter. Vohu Manah leads Zarathustra to an assembly of Ohrmazd and the Amahraspands; Zarathustra asks questions and receives doctrinal instruction.

The Gāthic passages from which this biography developed describe inspiration, recognition, and questioning but do not narrate the later meeting in continuous biographical form. The audience scene is therefore **later canonical elaboration**, not a securely recoverable episode from Zarathustra’s lifetime.

### Yima’s enclosure

In *Vendīdād* 2, Ahura Mazda offers Yima the role of propagating the religion; Yima declines but agrees to enlarge and protect the world. Later Mazda warns him of a catastrophic winter and commands an underground or enclosed *vara* stocked with the finest humans, animals, plants, food, and lights. The story resembles flood-survival narratives structurally, but water is not its destructive agent. Its relation to older Indo-Iranian Yama traditions and Near Eastern catastrophe myths remains debated. [Canonical, with probable older mythic material]

### Final renovation

Later Avestan and Pahlavi eschatology anticipates *frašō.kərəti/frashgird*, the making-wonderful or renovation of existence. The dead rise; creation undergoes purification, often represented as a river of molten metal experienced as warm milk by the righteous; Ahriman and the demons lose power; death, hunger, and corruption cease. Ohrmazd’s apparent limitation during mixed time is therefore temporary and strategic. Traditions differ on whether evil is annihilated, rendered permanently powerless, or driven back outside creation. [Canonical → late canonical elaboration]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Absence of a single “Temple of Ahura Mazda” model

Early evidence does not reveal a network of temples dedicated exclusively to Ahura Mazda. Herodotus claims Persians did not erect temples, altars, or statues, but his generalization cannot be applied uniformly across all Iranian regions and centuries. Achaemenid administrative tablets attest offerings to multiple deities, while royal inscriptions foreground Ahuramazda.

Armenian Aramazd had image cults in the Parthian period. Sasanian Iran developed monumental fire-temple institutions, but a consecrated fire was not normally an idol of Ohrmazd nor necessarily the exclusive property of one god.

### Fire and prayer

Fire is the visible focus before which worship is offered. The central liturgy, *yasna*—“worship” or “sacrifice”—uses fire, water, haoma, milk, bread, ritual implements, and memorized Avestan recitation. Its stated cosmic function is maintenance and renewal of Ahura Mazda’s good creation. See [Iranica, “Yasna”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasna/).

Daily practice includes:

- prayers during the five *gāhs* or watches of the day;
- untying and retying the *kusti* cord with prayer;
- the *Yathā Ahū Vairyō*, *Ašəm Vohū*, and other formulae;
- temple offerings of clean dry wood and fragrance;
- recitation of the Ātaš Niyāyišn;
- purity observances;
- commemorative rites for the dead.

The declaration “Ohrmazd is Lord” precedes obligatory prayers in traditional Middle Persian formulations. Modern reformist communities may abbreviate or reinterpret ritual without abandoning devotion to Ahura Mazda.

### Priesthood

Hereditary male priesthood predominated historically. Major offices and grades include *mōbed*, *hērbad/ervad*, and higher institutional titles such as *mōbedān mōbed*. Training centers on accurate ritual recitation, purity, and performance. Initiations such as *nāvar* and *martab* qualify Parsi priests for progressively complex ceremonies. The Sasanian alliance of crown and priesthood increased institutional authority; Kirdēr’s inscriptions show religious organization acting with political power.

### Sacrifice and offerings

Avestan *yaz-* covers worship, sacrifice, and veneration. Offerings included haoma, milk, water, bread, fat, meat, incense, and ritual praise. Animal sacrifice existed in Iranian religion and appears behind parts of the Avestan corpus, but later Zoroastrian practice varied, and modern communities often minimize it.

There is **no reliable evidence of a regular cult of human sacrifice to Ahura Mazda**. Classical accusations concerning Persian sacrifice require source-by-source analysis and cannot be transferred automatically to Mazdean worship. Likewise, **temple prostitution is not documented as an Ahura Mazda institution**. Its inclusion in generic lists of “ancient pagan practices” is unsupported.

### Calendar and festivals

No single great festival exclusively belonging to Ahura Mazda dominates the calendar. Every festival is ultimately celebrated within the Mazda-created order. Especially relevant are:

- the four *Day/Dae* name-days in the month Day;
- Nowruz, the New Year and renewal festival;
- the six seasonal *gāhānbārs*, associated with the stages of creation;
- *Hamaspathmaēdaya*, linked with the souls of the dead and cosmic completion.

### Magic and divination

Zoroastrian prayers were used apotropaically against demons, disease, and pollution. This should not be confused with later Western ceremonial magic. The Avestan *manthra* is sacred efficacious speech; texts such as the *Vendīdād* contain ritual and verbal defenses against harmful beings. Popular Iranian and Parsi practice also incorporated amulets and local healing customs, but not every Persian magical text is Zoroastrian or dedicated to Ahura Mazda.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Proto-Iranian background and Old Avestan religion

Shared Indo-Iranian vocabulary establishes a prehistory of *asura/ahura*, ritual fire, sacrifice, truth/order, and poetic revelation. Zarathustra’s hymns reorganized this inherited world around Mazda, Aša, moral choice, and hostility to the *daēvas*. Whether Ahura Mazda was newly conceived by Zarathustra or an existing Iranian high god radically elevated by him remains disputed. The probable Assyrian occurrence favors pre-Zarathustrian existence but proves little about theology.

### Achaemenid period, ca. 550–330 BCE

From Darius onward, Ahuramazda becomes the empire’s principal named divine guarantor. Darius’s suppression of rebellion is represented as victory over the Lie through divine favor. This was simultaneously religious language and imperial legitimation.

The kings did not normally impose uniform worship on conquered peoples; they patronized local cults for political and religious reasons. Xerxes’s Daiva inscription may record closure or reordering of a rebellious sanctuary, but the older picture of a general empire-wide “religious persecution” is unproved.

Under Artaxerxes II, royal inscriptions name Anāhitā and Mithra beside Ahuramazda. This need not mean Ahuramazda was demoted; it shows that royal Mazdaism could articulate a hierarchy containing other named gods.

### Hellenistic and Arsacid periods, 330 BCE–224 CE

Alexander’s conquest ended Achaemenid royal patronage, but Iranian religious traditions continued. The often-repeated later claim that Alexander burned the entire written Avesta is a Zoroastrian memory of catastrophe, not independently established in that maximal form.

Regional cults flourished. Armenian Aramazd became an image-bearing supreme god. Greek witnesses transmit increasingly systematized accounts of Oromazes, Areimanius, Mithra, and cosmic time. Iranian religion remained plural and locally variable.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

The Sasanians explicitly called themselves Mazda-worshipping kings and made Ohrmazd central to royal ideology. Investiture reliefs visually pair god and king. Fire temples and priestly hierarchies received political support, and the Avesta and its Middle Persian exegesis underwent collection and systematization.

This was not simply the establishment of one unchanging orthodoxy. Competing priestly interpretations, Zurvanite myths, local cults, Manichaeism, Christianity, Judaism, Buddhism, and later Mazdakism existed within or near the empire. Kirdēr claims the suppression or weakening of Jews, Buddhists, Brahmans, Nazarenes/Christians, Mandaeans, and Manichaeans. These are politically interested victory claims but are direct evidence that Mazdean institutions could participate in coercion.

Ohrmazd’s state role reached its maximum visibility here: royal names such as Hormizd, divine investiture art, the title *Ohrmazd-mowbed*, and cosmological alignment of Iran’s kingship with the defeat of Ahriman.

### Islamic conquest and gradual Islamization

The Arab conquest overthrew the Sasanian monarchy by 651. It destroyed the state structure that had funded and politically privileged Zoroastrian institutions. Conversion was gradual and regionally uneven, not a single forced-conversion event. Zoroastrians were often treated as protected *dhimmīs* and taxed through *jizya*, but legal inferiority, economic incentives, local violence, confiscation, loss or conversion of fire temples, and episodic forced conversion all occurred.

By the ninth century Islam was dominant in Iran, although substantial Zoroastrian communities remained. Pahlavi authors responded by codifying creation, ethics, eschatology, and sacred history. The fall of empire was interpreted through apocalyptic texts in which Ohrmazd foretells ages of oppression.

A concise scholarly account of institutional decline, migration, and survival is [Iranica’s historical review](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroastrianism-02-arab-conquest-to-modern/). It should replace both the apologetic claim of an entirely peaceful transition and the nationalist claim of an instantaneous, uniform genocide.

### Parsi migration and Indian development

Parsi tradition, recorded in the *Qeṣṣa-ye Sanjān* in 1599, tells of migration from Iran to Gujarat to preserve the religion. The migration itself is historical, but the work’s exact chronology and its story of negotiated customs are late literary memory. In India, Parsis created durable temple institutions, priestly lineages, charitable trusts, and distinctive community law.

Ahura Mazda remained supreme, but colonial encounters encouraged intensified debates over whether Zoroastrianism was monotheistic, dualistic, or ethical-philosophical. Protestant categories, Orientalist translations, Theosophy, and Parsi reform all reshaped its public description.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Parsi philanthropy aided Iranian Zoroastrians and campaigned against discriminatory taxes. Western philology recovered Avestan grammar and separated Gāthic, Younger Avestan, and Pahlavi strata more sharply than traditional exegesis had done.

Reformers such as M. N. Dhalla emphasized ethical monotheism and sometimes reduced ritual and demonological elements. Traditionalists defended inherited liturgy and priestly authority. Both continued to worship Ahura Mazda, but they construed the Aməša Spəntas, Ahriman, purity law, and conversion differently.

### Worship today

Ahura Mazda is continuously worshipped by Parsis and Iranian Zoroastrians and by diaspora congregations in North America, Europe, Australia, Pakistan, and elsewhere. Daily prayer, kusti practice, initiation, fire-temple worship, seasonal festivals, weddings, and funerary rites continue with regional variation. Priests still perform Yasna and Vendīdād ceremonies; [Iranica’s ritual survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroastrian-rituals/) documents both continuity and modern reform.

India’s 2011 census recorded Parsis/Zoroastrians in the “other religions and persuasions” tables; the official dataset is available from the [Census of India](https://www.censusindia.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/11398). Census counts do not capture all diaspora members or Iranian converts, and estimates of a worldwide population should not be presented as exact.

Modern Iranian interest in Zoroastrianism ranges from recognized minority practice to cultural nationalism, secular admiration for pre-Islamic Iran, and unregistered conversion. These categories must not be conflated: displaying the winged emblem or praising Ahura Mazda does not establish ritual membership.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Orientalism and comparative religion

Early-modern Europeans generally knew “Oromasdes/Ormuzd” through Greek authors. Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron’s eighteenth-century work on the Avesta transformed European access to Zoroastrian primary texts. Nineteenth-century translations in the *Sacred Books of the East* made “Ormazd versus Ahriman” a familiar comparative-religion model.

This frequently flattened historically distinct sources into an absolute God-versus-Devil dualism. It also encouraged speculative claims that Zoroastrianism directly “invented” every later Jewish, Christian, or Islamic teaching about angels, Satan, resurrection, and judgment. Influence is plausible in some domains, but direct borrowing must be demonstrated separately for each motif.

### Philosophy and literature

- **Nietzsche, *Also sprach Zarathustra* (1883–85):** uses Zarathustra as a philosophical persona; Nietzsche’s figure does not transmit traditional worship of Ahura Mazda.
- **Lord Byron and Romantic-era “Ormuzd/Ahriman” language:** employed as literary shorthand for moral dualism.
- **George R. R. Martin, fantasy, games, and comics:** names and dualistic structures sometimes borrow “Ahura,” “Ormuzd,” or “Ahriman,” usually without preserving Zoroastrian doctrine.
- **Mazda Motor Corporation:** the company name was explicitly associated with Ahura Mazda as a symbol of wisdom and with founder Jujiro Matsuda’s surname. This is commercial reception, not religious worship.

### The winged “Faravahar”

The Achaemenid winged symbol became a modern emblem of Zoroastrianism and Iranian identity. Popular explanations assign each feather, ring, hand, tail, and streamer a fixed moral meaning. Those complete symbolic charts are **modern didactic inventions**; no Achaemenid explanatory text supplies them. Calling the emblem definitively “Ahura Mazda” is likewise too certain, while calling it definitively an individual *fravaši* is not securely ancient.

### Theosophy, occultism, and esotericism

Theosophical writers incorporated “Ormuzd” into universalist hierarchies of cosmic light and treated Zoroastrian dualism as an esoteric allegory. Later ceremonial occultism sometimes placed Ahura Mazda in tables of solar beings or opposed him to Ahriman as psychological forces. No stable Golden Dawn invocation of Ahura Mazda comparable to its Egyptian and kabbalistic material became part of ancient Zoroastrian tradition.

Modern internet lists assigning Ahura Mazda crystals, tarot cards, chakras, runes, planetary hours, or “angel numbers” are **modern inventions unless a modern author is specifically cited**. They have no basis in the Avesta or Pahlavi books.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Small contemporary groups and individuals worship Ahura Mazda outside hereditary Parsi structures, often calling themselves Mazdayasni, Zoroastrian, Iranian pagan, or eclectic polytheist. Their approaches range from Gāthā-centered ethical monotheism to polytheistic veneration of Iranian yazatas. This is a real modern religious phenomenon, but its scale is unknown and its claim to institutional continuity varies.

Traditional communities disagree sharply over conversion. Some Iranian and diaspora organizations accept converts; many orthodox Parsi institutions restrict communal membership and access to consecrated spaces. There is no single worldwide Zoroastrian authority capable of settling the matter.

### National and political appropriation

Iranian nationalist movements have used Ahura Mazda and the winged emblem as symbols of pre-Islamic heritage, sometimes in secular opposition to clerical Islam. Monarchist iconography connected the Pahlavi state to Achaemenid kingship; post-1979 opposition movements also employ the emblem.

Some racialist and far-right authors have misrepresented Zoroastrianism as an “Aryan racial religion.” Ancient *airya-* was a cultural-linguistic self-designation, not a nineteenth-century biological race theory. Ahura Mazda’s Achaemenid use in an imperial, multiethnic monarchy does not support modern white-supremacist appropriation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Dating Zarathustra and the Gāthās

Positions range from a second-millennium BCE prophet to a figure not long before the Achaemenids. Linguistic proximity to Vedic Sanskrit supports substantial antiquity, but language is not an exact clock, and oral preservation complicates absolute dating. The safe statement is relative: the Gāthās and Yasna Haptaŋhāiti are the oldest Avestan strata and predate the Younger Avestan corpus.

### Did Zarathustra create Ahura Mazda?

- **Elevation model:** an inherited high god was made the sole or supreme focus of Zarathustra’s revelation.
- **Innovative formulation model:** Zarathustra created the distinctive conception, even if the terms *ahura* and *mazdā* were inherited.
- **Pre-Zarathustrian supreme-Asura model:** Ahura Mazda continues an Indo-Iranian sovereign god comparable to Varuṇa.

The possible Assyrian name favors prior existence but not any full theological model.

### Monotheism, dualism, or polytheism?

Each label isolates something real and can mislead:

- **Monotheistic:** Ahura Mazda is supreme and, in later orthodoxy, uncreated creator of all good.
- **Dualistic:** Ahriman is not merely a disobedient creature in standard Pahlavi cosmology; he is an independent hostile principle during cosmic history.
- **Polytheistic/henotheistic:** the Avesta worships numerous *yazatas*, and Mithra is declared worthy of worship like Mazda.

The evidence is best described historically rather than forced into one timeless category.

### Spenta Mainyu and Ahura Mazda

Older readings often treated Spenta Mainyu as Ahura Mazda’s separate good son and exact counterpart to Angra Mainyu. Other scholars see Spenta Mainyu as Mazda’s own beneficent mode of action. The Gāthās permit a distinction but do not define it scholastically; Younger Avestan language increasingly identifies Mazda as the “most beneficent spirit.”

### Was the Achaemenid religion Zoroastrian?

Arguments for include Ahuramazda’s supremacy, the political-moral contrast between Truth and Lie, fire ritual, and later Iranian continuity. Arguments against simple identification include the absence of Zarathustra, Ahriman, and explicit Avestan formulae in royal texts; royal invocation of multiple gods; and local imperial pragmatism. “Mazda-worshipping Iranian religion with significant affinities to Zoroastrianism” is less reductive than either categorical denial or complete identity.

### The winged figure

No consensus erases the contextual ambiguity. Ahura Mazda, royal *xᵛarənah*, protective royal genius, and deliberately polyvalent emblem have all been defended. Any museum label or modern graphic that gives only one answer suppresses an active scholarly dispute.

### Zurvanism

R. C. Zaehner reconstructed an extensive Zurvanite system and sometimes treated the Sasanian court as Zurvanite. Later scholars have been more cautious because the fullest accounts are hostile external witnesses and the internal evidence is scattered. The twin-birth myth is well attested; a centralized “Zurvanite church” is not.

### Text loss and manuscript transmission

Zoroastrian tradition says the Avesta once comprised twenty-one *nasks* and was damaged by Alexander and later invasions. The Dēnkard summarizes lost material, confirming that the surviving Avesta is incomplete, but it cannot establish the exact contents of a supposedly fixed Achaemenid archetype.

The surviving Avestan manuscripts are medieval or later ritual books. Their accuracy rests on disciplined priestly oral and written transmission, not on autograph antiquity. Modern digital work, especially manuscript collation and the Avestan Digital Archive, has shifted study from eclectic printed texts toward individual witnesses.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- Ahura Mazda is not securely depicted in every winged disk.
- The faravahar’s full “three rows of feathers = good thoughts, words, deeds” chart is not Achaemenid documentation.
- Zoroastrianism did not straightforwardly produce all Abrahamic theology.
- Mazda is not a solar god identical to the sun.
- There is no ancient Mazda–Jesus, Mazda–Odin, or Mazda–Zeus identity.
- “Ahura” and Norse *Æsir* cannot sustain a historical cultic equation merely because distant linguistic comparisons have been proposed.
- Ahura Mazda was not worshipped through documented temple prostitution.
- Regular human sacrifice to him is not established.
- The *Šāhnāmeh* is not an unchanged transcript of the Avesta.
- All modern Iranians displaying the winged emblem are not thereby practicing Zoroastrians.
