# The Fravashis: Research Dossier

**Class:** a collective of beneficent, powerful supernatural beings or spirits; in later Zoroastrian theology, pre-existent guardian prototypes associated—but never consistently identical—with individual souls.

**Earliest secure attestation:** *Yasna* 37.3 in the Old Avestan *Yasna Haptanghaiti*, which worships “the fravashis of the righteous, of men and women.” This linguistic stratum is older than the Younger Avestan *Yashts*, but its absolute date remains disputed; estimates generally place Old Avestan composition somewhere between the late second and early first millennium BCE. No securely identified Achaemenid inscription or image names or depicts a fravashi.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[Early]** securely attested Old or Younger Avestan material; **[Canonical]** later but normative Avestan or Zoroastrian tradition; **[Systematization]** Middle Persian priestly synthesis; **[Variant]** regional or textual alternative; **[Medieval]** post-Sasanian testimony; **[Reconstruction]** modern scholarly inference; **[Syncretism]** proposed historical equation; **[Modern]** recent reception or invention.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

The Avestan *fravašis* are an immense host of feminine-grammatical supernatural beings. In *Yasht* 13 they assist Ahura Mazda, sustain the ordered cosmos, release waters, promote vegetation and conception, guard heroes and the future savior’s seed, protect communities, defeat demonic aggression, and visit households at the year’s end. The same hymn also venerates the fravashis of named dead, living, and future righteous persons. Other texts sometimes identify a fravashi with the dead person’s *urvan* (“soul”), while *Yasht* 13.149 and 155 list *fravaši* and *urvan* separately. Any definition simply equating a fravashi with “the soul” or “guardian angel” therefore suppresses a real ancient contradiction. [1–3]

A useful documentary distinction is:

- **[Early]** A powerful collective associated with righteous men and women, already invoked in *Yasna* 37.3.
- **[Canonical]** Cosmic warriors, protectors, fertility powers, ancestral recipients of worship, and individual supernatural counterparts in *Yasht* 13.
- **[Systematization]** Pre-existent spiritual prototypes who choose embodied struggle, remain with Ohrmazd during mortal life, or reunite with the blessed soul after death—accounts that do not agree completely.
- **[Modern]** “Guardian angel,” “higher self,” and “divine spark” are interpretive approximations, not exact ancient definitions.

### Forms of the name

- Avestan: *frauuaši-* or normalized *fravaši-*; plural *fravašayō*; genitive plural *fravašīnąm*.
- Reconstructed Old Persian: *\*fravarti-*; it is not securely attested as the religious common noun in royal inscriptions.
- Middle Persian: *frawahr*, *fraward*, *frōhar*, *frawaš*, *frawaxš*; Book Pahlavi spellings are heterographic and vary by manuscript.
- New Persian: *farvahar/farohar* (فروهر), especially for the individual spiritual entity and, in modern usage, the winged emblem; *Farvardīn* (فروردین), first month and nineteenth day; *farvardagān* for the dead-related festival.
- Gujarati-Parsi usage: *fravashi/farohar*; *Muktād/Muktad* for the year-end observances.
- The liturgical expression *ašāunąm vaŋuhīš sūrå spəntå frauuašaiiō* means approximately “the good, strong, beneficent/bounteous fravashis of the righteous.” [1–4]

The noun is grammatically feminine in Avestan. That fact does not necessarily make every fravashi a “goddess”: the group includes the fravashis of men, women, gods, natural entities, and Ahura Mazda. It does, however, make the modern identification of the visibly male figure in the Achaemenid winged emblem with “a fravashi” iconographically problematic.

### Etymologies: no consensus

All major explanations are scholarly reconstructions:

- **[Reconstruction: ancestor/death]** Nathan Söderblom derived the word from *fra-* plus √*vart*, “turn away, depart,” hence departure or death. This explains the ancestral dimension but poorly explains the beings’ cosmic and martial functions.
- **[Reconstruction: fertility]** James Moulton connected √*var*, “make/be pregnant,” interpreting a fravashi as a “birth-promoter.” *Yasht* 13’s concern with conception, waters, plants, and offspring supports the semantic proposal but does not prove the derivation.
- **[Reconstruction: protection]** H. W. Bailey used √*var*, “cover, protect,” with *fra-* in an apotropaic sense: “protective valor.” This fits their warlike behavior and possible heroic cult.
- **[Reconstruction: choice]** Karl Hoffmann, Herman Lommel, and Bernfried Schlerath connected the word with √*var*, “choose.” Proposed senses include “decisive choice,” a spirit of religious profession, or the public “choosing forth” of a hero.
- **[Reconstruction: dual origin]** Gherardo Gnoli proposed convergence between ancestor and hero cults.
- Georges Dumézil’s comparison with the Vedic Maruts was challenged by William Malandra; no Indo-Iranian equation is established. [1]

The frequently repeated claim that *faravahar* straightforwardly means “good thoughts, good words, good deeds” is **[Modern invention]**. That triad is genuinely Zoroastrian, but it is neither an etymology of *fravaši* nor an ancient caption for the winged emblem.

### Titles and calendar names

The ordinary Avestan qualification is “of the righteous” (*ašāunąm*). “Good, strong, beneficent,” “awful/overpowering,” “victorious,” “swift,” and “mighty” recur in *Yasht* 13. Middle Persian *Ardā Fraward* or *frawahr ī ahlawān*, “Fravashi of the Righteous,” became a collective calendrical divinity. The nineteenth day of every month and the first month, Farvardīn, are dedicated to it. [3, 11]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[Early] *Yasna Haptanghaiti* 37.3.** The text ends an invocation of Ahura Mazda with worship of “the fravashis of the righteous, of men and women.” This establishes the name, plurality, righteous affiliation, and ritual worship. It does not establish pre-existence, an individual guardian for every person, the annual return of the dead, the creation-choice myth, or any visual form. [1]

The passage is transmitted orally and through medieval manuscripts rather than by an ancient physical copy. The oldest substantially dated Avestan manuscripts are medieval: Copenhagen K7a is usually dated 1278, the oldest surviving Pahlavi Yasna manuscripts J2 and K5 to 1323, and the principal complete pure-Yasht codex F1 to 1591. The gap between composition and manuscripts is therefore well over a millennium. [5–7]

### Avestan corpus

**The Gathas, *Yasna* 28–34, 43–51, 53.** No unambiguous fravashi doctrine appears in the Gathas proper. The reference in *Yasna* 37 belongs to the linguistically Old Avestan *Yasna Haptanghaiti*, not to Zarathustra’s verse Gathas. The absence is important to theories that fravashis were either pre-Zoroastrian beings subsequently incorporated or a post-Gathic religious construction.

**Yasna 16.7.** **[Canonical]** It worships the radiant abodes of Asha where the souls (*urvan*) of the dead dwell, “who are the fravashis of the righteous.” This expressly identifies the two categories. [1]

**Yasna 23 and 26.** **[Canonical]** These invoke the fravashis of righteous beings, including primordial Gayōmard, Zarathustra, Vištāspa, and eschatological figures. They also worship Ahura Mazda and the fravashis of the righteous within the larger liturgical sequence.

**Yasna 37.3.** **[Early]** Earliest linguistic attestation.

**Yasht 13, the *Frawardīn Yasht*.** **[Canonical]** The principal dossier, in Younger Avestan, 158 stanzas and among the longest and best-preserved Yashts. Its parts include:

1. Ahura Mazda’s declaration that the fravashis assist him in maintaining creation (1–19).
2. Protective recitations for dangerous travel and battle (20–48).
3. The fravashis’ ten-night return and request for household offerings (49–52).
4. Their release of waters, growth of plants, movement of celestial lights, protection of the Vourukaša sea, Kərəsāspa’s body, and Zarathustra’s seed (53–64).
5. Their rain-bearing competition on behalf of family, settlement, tribe, and country (65–72).
6. Their resistance to demonic attack (73–79).
7. The fravashi of Ahura Mazda; fravashis of the Amesha Spentas, named yazatas, cosmic elements, animals, and humanity (80–86).
8. A vast litany of the fravashis of Gayōmard, Zarathustra, his patrons and disciples, Iranian heroes, men and women, and future renovators (87–144).
9. Universal invocations of past, present, and future righteous people and a closing household blessing (145–158). [2–4]

The hymn preserves different conceptual layers rather than a single systematic doctrine. Its onomastic catalogue is a major repository of otherwise poorly known Iranian religious names. Its oral composition cannot be assigned one secure year. Scholars place its inherited and compositional strata broadly in the Old Iranian period; the surviving redaction is Younger Avestan. Attempts to isolate neat “pre-Zoroastrian” and “Zoroastrian” blocks remain controversial. [3, 7]

**Other Avestan ritual texts.** The *Sīrōza*, *Āfrīnagān*, *Vidēvdād* ritual tradition, and liturgical extensions of the Yasna invoke the collective fravashis, especially in calendar, mortuary, and commemorative settings. The *Vidēvdād*’s corpse laws regulate disposal and purity but do not identify the exposed corpse or the dakhma itself as a fravashi.

### Middle Persian corpus

**Bundahišn**, compiled in surviving form in the ninth century CE from older Zand materials:

- **[Systematization]** 3.13 separates body, soul, and fravashi and states that the soul inhabits the body while the fravashi remains with Ohrmazd, after which reunion occurs.
- **[Systematization]** 3.23–24 gives the great “choice”: Ohrmazd shows the fravashis the future and offers either protected, unembodied existence or birth into the material world, suffering, and participation in Ahriman’s defeat. They choose embodiment.
- The same corpus treats creation in spiritual and material phases, making the embodied creature the material realization of an antecedent spiritual form. [1, 8]

**Dēnkard**, chiefly ninth–tenth century:

- Book 7.2.15–47 recounts Zarathustra’s conception. His *frawahr* descends from heaven through a miraculous hōm plant and eventually joins his bodily substance and *xwarrah*.
- Elsewhere the blessed soul and fravashi unite, whereas the fravashi cannot enter hell. This conflicts with popular and medieval descriptions in which the “fravashis” of all the dead, including the punished, return at the festival.
- Book 8 preserves a summary of the lost Sasanian Bagān Yašt Nask. [1, 7]

**Selections of Zādspram** 3.2–3, ninth century: after Ahriman penetrates creation, the fravashis form close ranks along the rim of the sky, mounted and holding spears, imprisoning him. This is their most explicit martial tableau. [1]

**Mēnōg ī Xrad.** Its anthropology and eschatology distinguish components of the person and should not be used to erase the variation among *frawahr*, *ruwān*, *dēn*, and intellect.

**Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag.** This account of visionary travel through the afterlife chiefly concerns souls and postmortem recompense; it is evidence for later Zoroastrian afterlife systematization, not for a separate narrative cycle starring the fravashis.

### Classical and Islamic-period witnesses

Herodotus, *Histories* 1.132, fifth century BCE, says that a Magus chants a “theogony” at Persian sacrifice. Scholars have compared this with Yasht performance, but Herodotus neither names fravashis nor proves that *Yasht* 13 existed in its present form. [7]

Plutarch’s *De Iside et Osiride* preserves a late Greek account of Iranian dualism but no clear independent testimony to fravashis. It should not be recruited as proof of their doctrine.

Al-Bīrūnī, *al-Āṯār al-bāqiya*, ca. 1000 CE, records the Persians’ days for the dead and the belief that souls return to their former homes. His terminology reflects living post-Sasanian custom and illustrates the continuing fusion of fravashi and soul. [1, 12]

Ferdowsi’s *Shahnameh*, completed ca. 1010, transmits much Iranian heroic legend but does not present the fravashis as a developed collective divine protagonist. Similar hero names in *Yasht* 13 and the epic tradition establish transmission of Iranian legendary material, not a direct *Shahnameh* cult of fravashis.

No specific Manichaean or Roman Mithraic being has been securely demonstrated to be a direct equivalent of the fravashi. Iranian terminology and concepts traveled widely, but individual proposals require separate proof.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

In *Yasht* 13 the fravashis:

- Help Ahura Mazda hold up the sky, earth, waters, plants, animals, and humans.
- Release stationary waters and promote plant growth.
- Set sun, moon, stars, and lights in motion after demonic obstruction.
- Guard the sea Vourukaša, Kərəsāspa’s body, and Zarathustra’s seed.
- Protect embryos so that sons are born.
- Fight for water and rain for “family, settlement, tribe, and country.”
- Ride to battle in ordered ranks and grant victory when properly invoked.
- Protect travelers on dangerous roads.
- Visit homes for ten nights, asking who will praise and feed them.
- Bless a hospitable house with animals, people, horses, competent household leadership, and prosperity.
- Preserve the righteous creation until the final renovation. [2]

The recurrent numbers “many hundreds, many thousands, many tens of thousands” and 99,999 express an innumerable host. No canonical source assigns them one color, planet, metal, animal, or weapon as a universal emblem. Spears and martial riding occur in later creation narrative; water, plants, light, and household offerings are functional associations, not exclusive heraldic attributes.

### Are fravashis pre-existent souls?

The answer depends on period and text.

- **[Early]** *Yasna* 37 does not say.
- **[Canonical]** *Yasht* 13’s protection of future persons and participation in creation implies existence before particular human births.
- **[Systematization]** The *Bundahišn* explicitly places them before material embodiment and gives them a choice to enter history.
- **Qualification:** Calling them “pre-existent souls” collapses *fravaši* into *urvan*. The same textual tradition sometimes identifies and sometimes distinguishes those entities. “Pre-existent guardian or spiritual prototype” is more precise.

### Iconography and the winged disk

No Avestan passage gives a physical description of a fravashi. No ancient image bears an inscription identifying it as one.

The Achaemenid emblem consists in some examples of a winged ring or disk with a bearded male figure, seen at Behistun, Persepolis, and the royal tombs at Naqš-e Rostam. Its ancestry lies in ancient Near Eastern winged-disk and Assyrian Aššur imagery. Proposed interpretations include:

- **Ahura Mazda:** supported by its position above royal scenes, correspondence between the king’s and figure’s gestures, and adjacent inscriptions crediting Ahura Mazda. This remains a major scholarly interpretation.
- **Royal *xᵛarənah* or dynastic glory:** proposed because of the emblem’s royal context and variable resemblance to the king.
- **Royal genius or fravashi:** historically influential among Parsis and some earlier scholars, including Moulton and Unvala, but not supported by an ancient label.
- **Composite royal-theological emblem:** some analyses distinguish the anthropomorphic winged-ring figure from a separate winged disk and relate the components differently to Ahura Mazda and *xᵛarənah*. [9–10]

The equation with a fravashi is especially difficult because Avestan *fravaši* is grammatically feminine while the emblematic figure is visibly male, and because the Avesta does not describe fravashis as winged. Therefore:

> **The “Faravahar” as a picture of a fravashi is a modern or late traditional identification, not a securely identified Achaemenid depiction.**

Modern diagrams assigning the emblem’s three wing-feather rows to “good thoughts, good words, good deeds,” the three tail rows to their evil opposites, the two streamers to good and evil spirits, and the ring to eternity are **[Modern didactic interpretations]**. No Achaemenid caption supplies that key.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with divine beings

Fravashis have no parents, spouse, or collective offspring in the surviving Avesta. Later cosmogony places them with Ohrmazd before material creation but does not consistently say that he “created” them. They are better understood as a class or host than as a genealogical family.

Every good being may possess a fravashi in developed doctrine. *Yasht* 13.80 even worships the fravashi of Ahura Mazda, followed by those of the Amesha Spentas and various yazatas. Mary Boyce called the offering to Ahura Mazda’s fravashi “preposterous” from the standpoint of systematic theology, but textually it is unambiguous. It probably reflects expansion of the category until virtually everything belonging to the good creation could be assigned a fravashi. [3]

### Documented divine interactions

Joint listing alone is separated from actual action:

- **Ahura Mazda — assistance in creation and maintenance.** Ahura Mazda tells Zarathustra that the fravashis “come to help” him and that through their glory he sustains the cosmos; *Yasht* 13.1–19. **[Canonical]**
- **Ahura Mazda — object possesses its own fravashi.** The fravashi of Ahura Mazda is worshipped as greatest, best, wisest, and most righteous; *Yasht* 13.80–81. **[Canonical; theological anomaly]**
- **Ahura Mazda — offers embodiment or safety.** Ohrmazd shows the future to the fravashis, who choose birth, suffering, and combat against evil; *Bundahišn* 3.23–24. **[Systematization]**
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — collective combat.** Fravashis resist his incursion and prevent waters and plants from failing; *Yasht* 13.76–79. **[Canonical]**
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — armed containment.** Mounted fravashis mass with spears at the sky’s rim to imprison him; *Zādspram* 3.2–3. **[Systematization]**
- **Amesha Spentas — joint cosmic work.** Waters and plants proceed at the will of Ahura Mazda and the Amesha Spentas through paths opened by the fravashis; *Yasht* 13.53–56. **[Canonical]**
- **Amesha Spentas — their fravashis worshipped.** *Yasht* 13.82–84. This is a shared liturgical scene, not a narrated meeting. **[Canonical]**
- **Vohu Manah and Ātar — joint resistance to evil.** The obscure *Yasht* 13.77–78 sequence places them in the response to Angra Mainyu’s attack. **[Canonical; translation disputed]**
- **Sraosha — cooperative protection.** The fravashis protect worshippers with the assistance of Ahura Mazda, Sraosha, and the sacred Word; *Yasht* 13.145–146. **[Canonical]**
- **Ashi Vanguhi — household blessing.** Satisfied fravashis bless the house with Ashi’s presence; *Yasht* 13.156–157. **[Canonical]**
- **Daēvas — liberation of cosmic lights.** Fravashis set stars, moon, sun, and lights moving after daēvic oppression; *Yasht* 13.57–58. **[Canonical]**
- **Sraosha, Nairyō.saŋha, Rashnu, Mithra, and Māθra Spenta — each has a fravashi.** *Yasht* 13.85–86. **[Canonical joint litany, not narrative interaction]**
- **Ātar, sky, waters, earth, plants, primordial Bull, living humanity — each has a fravashi.** *Yasht* 13.85–86. **[Canonical classification, not interpersonal interaction]**
- **Tishtrya — no secure direct interaction located.** The fravashis’ rain functions overlap Tishtrya’s domain, but thematic proximity is not an attested meeting.
- **Ardvi Sura Anahita — no secure direct interaction located.** Both concern waters, but *Yasht* 13 does not narrate their encounter.
- **Mithraic or Manichaean gods — no securely documented interaction.** Comparisons and terminological influence do not establish a scene involving the fravashis.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The fravashis help order and preserve creation

**[Canonical]** In *Yasht* 13.1–19 Ahura Mazda tells Zarathustra that the host’s splendor enables him to maintain the sky, waters, earth, plants, animals, embryos, and people. This is not a complete creation epic: it is a hymnic catalogue of functions. Later writers sometimes recast it as though fravashis were Platonic archetypes, but the Yasht itself describes active protective powers.

### The cosmic counterattack

**[Canonical]** Demonic forces hold waters, plants, and celestial lights motionless. The fravashis open their proper paths and restore circulation and growth. At Angra Mainyu’s attack they stand firm and destroy his malice, keeping waters flowing and plants growing (*Yasht* 13.53–79).

**[Systematization]** Zādspram converts the martial theme into a sharper scene: mounted, spear-bearing fravashis close ranks along the boundary of the sky after Ahriman’s penetration. The narrative is later than the Avestan hymn and should not be retrojected as its missing illustration.

### The primordial choice

**[Systematization]** In *Bundahišn* 3.23–24, Ohrmazd asks the pre-existent fravashis whether they prefer to remain protected with him or enter material bodies, suffer assault, and help annihilate Ahriman. Having been shown the eventual victory, they choose embodiment. This supplies a theological explanation for material life and human participation in cosmic combat.

This story is not in the Gathas and is not stated in this form in *Yasht* 13. It is a Middle Persian construction drawing together pre-existence, free choice, embodiment, and eschatology.

### The annual return

**[Canonical]** *Yasht* 13.49–52 says the fravashis come for ten nights at Hamaspaθmaēdaya. They move about asking who will praise, worship, and welcome them with food and clothing. A satisfied fravashi blesses that house with prosperity, livestock, horses, and capable descendants.

**[Systematization/Medieval]** Later usage treats the visitors as the souls of departed relatives, including—according to al-Bīrūnī’s report—souls returning from both heaven and hell. This conflicts with learned doctrine that an uncorrupted fravashi never goes to hell. Ritual language preserved the fusion even when theologians distinguished the entities. [1, 12]

### The fravashis of heroes and future saviors

**[Canonical]** The Yasht names Gayōmard, Zarathustra, Vištāspa, disciples, legendary heroes, righteous women and men, and the future Saoshyant. Ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine fravashis guard Zarathustra’s seed, from which later tradition develops the miraculous conceptions of the future renovators. The hymn’s catalogue is not a connected epic but a ritual map of sacred history from first human to final savior.

### Zarathustra’s descending fravashi

**[Systematization]** *Dēnkard* 7 narrates the prophet’s *frawahr* descending through a unique hōm plant, joining his bodily substance and glory through the agencies of his future parents. Here *frawahr* behaves much like a pre-existent personal essence. This specialized prophet-birth myth cannot safely define every fravashi.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Worship and offerings

There is no securely identified ancient temple devoted solely to the fravashis. Their cult is fundamentally domestic, calendrical, mortuary, and liturgical. Evidence consists principally of Avestan invocations, calendar structure, Middle Persian ritual prescriptions, medieval reports, and continuous Zoroastrian observance rather than a dedicated monumental sanctuary.

*Yasht* 13 requests praise, meat, clothing, and hospitable welcome. Later practice uses flowers, water, fruit, food, lamps or fire, incense, and ritual recitation. These are offerings of hospitality and merit; they are not corpse offerings placed upon a dakhma.

The *Farōḵši* ceremony recites the *Frawardīn Yasht* for departed souls. The *Saddar Bundahišn* directs its performance on memorial days with foods including meat, eggs, fish, sweets, fruit, and herbs. A Persian Rivayat of 1526 attests continued performance. In living usage it occurs on the fourth day and later memorial days after death and during Farvardīgān. [13]

The Āfrīnagān liturgical family includes *Ardā Frawaxš*, a service for the righteous fravashis. Priests conduct ceremonies, but the household’s preparation and welcoming of its dead remain central. [14]

### Frawardīgān, Panjī, and Muktad

**[Canonical and continuous]** Frawardīgān is the ten-day festival at year’s end. Five ordinary final days are followed by five intercalary or “Gathic” days. Iranian Zoroastrians also call it *Panjī*; Parsis commonly call it *Muktad*, and many Parsi communities observe an extended eighteen-day form. *Yasht* 13.49 is the earliest explicit evidence for ten nights. [12]

Calendar drift produced conflicting dates. The 365-day calendar lacked regular intercalation; Sasanian reforms and later Parsi calendar divisions generated Shenshai, Kadmi, and Fasli observances on different civil dates. The ritual relationship to the year’s end and Nowruz is older than any one current Gregorian date. [12, 15]

### Farvardīn Jashan and the dakhmas

The nineteenth day of Farvardīn month produces a name-day feast, Farvardīn Jashan. Zoroastrians, especially families bereaved during the preceding year, have visited dakhmas and commissioned ceremonies for the departed. [1]

A dakhma or “Tower of Silence” is a structure for exposing corpses so that dead matter does not pollute earth, fire, or water. The corpse-purity system belongs especially to the *Vidēvdād* tradition. The fravashis are neither the vultures nor the corpse, and the tower is not their canonical dwelling. The connection is ritual and commemorative: worshippers visit a funerary complex to honor the dead and their fravashis. Claims that the winged emblem represents a soul physically flying from a Tower of Silence are **[Modern invention]**.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

Animal and food offerings occur in the vocabulary of the Yasht and later memorial practice. There is no securely documented human sacrifice to the fravashis, no fravashi temple prostitution, oracle, or specialized fravashi priesthood. Searches for these topics produce no primary evidence. Martial language and the possible roots of the cult in heroic commemoration are not evidence of human sacrifice.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Old Avestan period

*Yasna* 37.3 proves worship of plural fravashis of righteous men and women. Their absence from the Gathic poems prevents certainty about their position in Zarathustra’s own teaching. One theory sees an inherited hero or ancestor cult reluctantly incorporated into Zoroastrianism; another sees fravashis as a Zoroastrian abstraction that later absorbed ancestral and martial traits. Neither origin can be demonstrated directly.

### Younger Avestan period

*Yasht* 13 assembles several functions: cosmic maintenance, war, fertility, rainfall, ancestral return, and commemoration of named adherents. “Of the righteous” integrates them into the moral opposition of Asha and Druj. The hymn’s inconsistencies likely preserve convergence rather than logical failure by a single author.

### Achaemenid era, ca. 550–330 BCE

Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam repeatedly name Auramazdā but do not name fravashis. The rebel Fravartiš in the Behistun inscription bears a similar-looking personal name, but this is not a secure attestation of the theological common noun. Herodotus’ Magian “theogony” may indicate Yasht-like performance but not specifically the *Frawardīn Yasht*.

Achaemenid winged imagery cannot securely be placed in the fravashi record except as the object of later interpretation.

### Parthian and Sasanian periods

The Zoroastrian calendar institutionalized Farvardīn as month and day. Sasanian redaction and Zand exegesis linked Avestan worship with a more systematic anthropology and cosmogony. The collective *Ardā Fraward* became a calendrical yazad in its own right. Kirdēr’s third-century inscriptions reflect a state-supported ritual system containing the named calendar divinities, though they provide no independent fravashi myth. [11]

No direct equation between fravashis and Roman Mithraic torchbearers, souls, or planetary spirits is established. Manichaean Iranian texts sometimes reuse Middle Iranian religious vocabulary, but their radically different cosmology prevents automatic identification.

### Early Islamic and medieval periods

The Arab conquest ended Zoroastrian political supremacy and many institutions contracted, but there is no documented single decree specifically suppressing fravashi worship. The Pahlavi books, compiled under Islamic rule, preserved and elaborated the doctrine. Al-Bīrūnī recorded the annual return custom around 1000. Persian Rivayats connected Iranian and Indian Zoroastrian practice through the early-modern period.

### Modern communities

Iranian Zoroastrians and Parsis continue Farvardīgān/Panjī/Muktad, Farōḵši, memorial prayers, and Farvardīn-day observances. Practice varies over calendar, duration, permitted foods, location, and whether consecrated vessels represent individual departed persons.

“Revived worship” is therefore an incomplete description: ritual continuity exists, although explanations have changed. Modern English “guardian angel” theology often sharpens individual moral guidance beyond what the earliest sources explicitly say.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### The Faravahar emblem

During the nineteenth and especially twentieth centuries the Achaemenid winged figure became known popularly as the *Faravahar* and was increasingly interpreted as a visual fravashi. The Pahlavi dynasty’s cultivation of pre-Islamic imperial imagery from 1925 onward helped turn it into a national emblem. It now functions variously as:

- a Zoroastrian religious symbol;
- a secular Iranian or Persian ethnic emblem;
- a monarchist or Pahlavist sign;
- an oppositional pre-Islamic symbol used against the Islamic Republic;
- jewelry, tattoos, logos, and tourist merchandise.

These uses do not prove its Achaemenid meaning. The emblem’s modern ethical diagram and name are part of its reception history.

Nationalist use ranges from inclusive Iranian cultural pride to Persian-exclusivist and anti-Arab or anti-Muslim rhetoric. The symbol itself does not entail those ideologies, but particular deployments must be read politically. Its use by extremists is modern appropriation, not evidence that the ancient fravashis carried a racial doctrine.

### Esotericism and neopaganism

Nineteenth-century comparative religion translated fravashis through categories such as “manes,” “pitris,” “angels,” “genii,” “higher doubles,” and Platonic ideas. Theosophical and later occult literature adopted some of this vocabulary. No major Golden Dawn ritual cycle centered on the fravashis, and claims of an ancient Western magical order invoking “Farohars” require evidence not presently located.

Modern Zoroastrian revivalists and eclectic neopagans sometimes address a personal fravashi as higher self, immortal self, ancestral guardian, or tutelary angel. These are **[Modern reinterpretations]** built from real textual elements but generally harmonize contradictions that ancient texts leave unresolved.

Popular psychology occasionally treats the fravashi as a Jungian archetype or ideal self. This is a modern analogy, not an ancient doctrine. No reliable census isolates the scale of explicitly fravashi-centered neopagan worship.

### Literature, games, and popular culture

“Fravashi,” “Frohar,” and related spellings appear in fantasy, games, occult websites, and nationalist design, commonly as winged angels or personal combat guardians. Such representations derive more from the modern Faravahar emblem and the English term “guardian spirit” than from the unpictured, grammatically feminine collective of the Avesta.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### What were they originally?

The central debate remains unresolved:

1. **Ancestor spirits:** supported by the annual return, offerings, and Indo-Iranian parallels between ancestral rites and Vedic *pitṛs*. Against it stands the lack of an Indo-Iranian cognate class named *fravaši* and their extensive cosmic-martial activity.
2. **Heroic warrior spirits:** supported by martial language and protection in battle. Bailey, Dumézil, Schlerath, and Gnoli developed variants. Against it stands the fertility, female-grammatical, cosmic, and universalizing evidence.
3. **Zoroastrian personified choice or profession:** supported by proposed derivation from √*var*, “choose,” and the prominence of religious choice. Against it stands uncertain etymology and the beings’ mythic concreteness.
4. **Conflation model:** Boyce argued that a distinct fravashi concept blended early with the older *urvan* and ancestor cult. This best explains textual contradiction but remains historical reconstruction rather than directly narrated history. [1]

### Fravashi versus urvan

This is not solvable by choosing one passage:

- Identity: *Yasna* 16.7.
- Functional fusion: *Yasht* 13.49–52 and its list of dead persons.
- Explicit distinction: *Yasht* 13.149, 155.
- Later separation: *Bundahišn* 3.13.
- Later reunion only for the blessed: *Dēnkard*.
- Popular return from both heaven and hell: al-Bīrūnī.

The contradiction is evidence of historical development.

### Date and composition of *Yasht* 13

Its language is Younger Avestan, but oral composition, conservative liturgy, and late manuscripts prevent exact dating. Older scholarship often extracted pre-Zoroastrian blocks; more recent work warns that clean stratification is impossible. Skjærvø emphasizes continuing Old Iranian oral composition, while Kellens and others analyze the hymn’s ritual-poetic structure. The text likely contains inherited formulae and successive expansions, especially its large name lists, but individual verses rarely carry absolute dates. [3, 7]

### Winged emblem debate

Earlier Western scholarship often called the anthropomorphic emblem Ahura Mazda; many Parsis called it a *frawahr*; other scholars proposed *xᵛarənah*, a royal genius, or a dynastic symbol. Current responsible presentation must retain the uncertainty. The ancient evidence does not label it, and the modern word *Faravahar* should not be allowed to decide the Achaemenid question retroactively. [9–10]

### State of the evidence

Avestan texts were transmitted orally and then in late manuscripts. Important witnesses are held in Copenhagen, India, Oxford, Paris, and other European and South Asian collections. Geldner’s 1886–96 edition remains foundational; digital projects now provide manuscript images and transcriptions. The principal pure Yasht manuscript F1 dates to 1591, while independent Khorda Avesta transmission helps control some readings. [5–7]

Anquetil-Duperron brought a major collection to Paris in 1762 and published his translation in 1771. Rask and Burnouf established the linguistic basis of Avestan studies; Westergaard and Geldner produced critical editions. Darmesteter and Mills supplied the influential *Sacred Books of the East* translations, now philologically dated but still useful when checked against modern work. Kellens edited *Frawardīn Yasht* 1–70 in 1975; Malandra, Boyce, Humbach, Hintze, and Skjærvø have refined linguistic, ritual, and historical analysis.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- The winged figure is demonstrably a portrait of a fravashi: **unsupported**.
- Its feathers anciently encoded the ethical triad: **modern invention**.
- Every fravashi is a dead person’s soul: **overstatement contradicted by the corpus**.
- Fravashis are fallen angels, jinn, or demons: **no primary support**.
- They are identical with Vedic Maruts, Roman Manes, Christian angels, or Platonic Forms: **comparisons, not identities**.
- Towers of Silence were temples to fravashis or launch-points for winged souls: **unsupported**.
- Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or a fravashi oracle: **no documented evidence located**.
- The Achaemenid royal inscriptions explicitly call the winged emblem *Faravahar*: **false**.

## 10. Chronology

| Period/date | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Late second–early first millennium BCE, absolute date disputed | Old Avestan *Yasna Haptanghaiti* 37.3 invokes the fravashis of righteous men and women: earliest secure linguistic attestation. |
| Old Iranian period | Younger Avestan compositions underlying *Yasht* 13 develop cosmic, martial, ancestral, fertility, and protective functions. Exact strata are undatable. |
| Fifth century BCE | Herodotus 1.132 reports Magian recitation of a “theogony” at sacrifice; possible Yasht-type performance, but no fravashi named. |
| 522–486 BCE | Darius’ monuments use anthropomorphic winged-ring imagery and name Auramazdā. No inscription identifies the emblem as a fravashi. |
| Achaemenid period | Old Persian religious common noun *\*fravarti-* remains unattested or disputed; personal names are not sufficient proof of cult terminology. |
| Third–seventh centuries CE | Sasanian religious institutions consolidate the calendar, Avestan canon, Zand exegesis, Farvardīn observances, and year-end rites. |
| Third century CE | Kirdēr’s inscriptions attest the state-supported hierarchy of calendrical yazads; *Ardā Fraward* belongs to the later system. |
| ca. ninth century | *Bundahišn* records the fravashis’ primordial choice and systematized relation to body and soul. |
| ca. ninth century | Zādspram depicts mounted, spear-bearing fravashis containing Ahriman. |
| Ninth–tenth centuries | *Dēnkard* preserves Zarathustra’s descending fravashi and summaries of lost Avestan/Zand material. |
| ca. 1000 | Al-Bīrūnī records annual rites and the return of departed souls. |
| 1278 | Copenhagen K7a, among the oldest dated Avestan manuscripts. |

| 1352 | Jm4, oldest Khorda Avesta cited in the manuscript tradition. |

| 1591 | F1, principal and oldest complete pure-Yasht codex. |
| 1723 | An Avestan manuscript reaches the Bodleian, helping reopen European study. |

| 1771 | Anquetil publishes the first major European Avesta translation. |
| 1880s | Darmesteter and Mills publish influential English/French translations; Geldner begins the critical Avesta edition. |
| 1899 | Söderblom’s ancestor/death interpretation enters the modern debate. |
| 1913 | Moulton advances fertility etymology and identifies the winged emblem with the royal fravashi. |
| 1920s–1979 | Pahlavi state culture promotes Achaemenid imagery; the winged figure becomes a widely recognized Iranian national symbol. |
| 1943 | Bailey develops the “protective valor” and heroic-spirit theory. |
| 1975 | Kellens publishes a critical study and edition of *Frawardīn Yasht* 1–70. |
