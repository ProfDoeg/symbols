# Verethragna: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the Younger Avesta, Vərəθraγna is a male *yazata*—a worship-worthy divine being “made by Ahura”—who personifies aggressive victory, the power to break resistance, bodily vigor, health, and successful combat. In later Middle Persian Zoroastrianism he is Warahrān/Wahrām/Bahrām, still a *yazd* of victory; he is not an Aməša Spəṇta in the oldest sources.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The name is first securely attested in the transmitted Younger Avestan corpus, above all *Yašt* 14, the Bahrām Yašt. The hymn is linguistically Old Iranian and contains archaic material, but neither its absolute date nor every layer within it can be fixed. A broad first-millennium BCE date is safer than a precise year. No surviving Achaemenid royal inscription names Vərəθraγna. The earliest surviving manuscripts are vastly later: the oldest Khordeh Avesta codex is Jm4, copied in 1352 CE, and the principal complete “pure Yašt” manuscript F1 was copied in 1591. Thus the earliest evidence is an ancient, orally transmitted text preserved in medieval and early-modern manuscripts—not a contemporary Achaemenid inscription.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**Securely attested Younger Avestan material.** Vərəθraγna is the divine force that overcomes *vərəθra-*—resistance, obstruction, or defensive opposition. In *Yašt* 14.1 Ahura Mazdā calls him the “best-armed” of the heavenly gods. In 14.2–27 he repeatedly approaches Zaraθuštra in ten forms, each carrying Mazda-created *xᵛarənah*, glory or charismatic fortune. In 14.3 he calls himself strongest in strength, most victorious in victory, most glorious in glory, most beneficent, and “best-healing.” His sphere therefore exceeds battlefield victory: it includes bodily wholeness, eyesight, virility, verbal victory, defense against spells, political sovereignty, and the protection of the Iranian lands from enemy armies and disease.

The frequently used English “god of war” is serviceable but incomplete. “Divine Victory” or “the resistance-smasher” better captures the name and the range of *Yašt* 14.

### Names and scripts

- **Avestan:** 𐬬𐬆𐬭𐬆𐬚𐬭𐬀𐬖𐬥𐬀, conventionally transliterated **Vərəθraγna-**; older publications give Verethraghna, Verethragna, or Vṛθragna.
- **Underlying expression:** the corpus also has the neuter common noun *vərəθraγna-* “smiting/breaking of resistance” and adjective *vərəθraγan-* “victorious, resistance-breaking.”
- **Middle Persian:** **Warahrān**, **Wahrām**, later **Bahrām**; inscriptional spellings commonly use heterographic forms transliterated *wlhlʾn*. The development is phonological, not evidence for a separate god.
- **Parthian:** reconstructed or attested forms around **Warθagn/Warahrām**; Manichaean Parthian has forms conventionally read Wahrām.
- **New Persian:** **بهرام Bahrām/Behrām**, used as a personal name and as the name of Mars.
- **Bactrian, in Greek script:** **ΟΡΛΑΓΝΟ / Orlagno**, appearing on Kushan coins.
- **Sogdian:** **Wšγn / Wašaγn** and related spellings.
- **Saka:** **Varlaagn**, reported in comparative Iranian material.
- **Armenian:** **Վահագն Vahagn**, and personal-name forms **Vahram/Vṙam** derived through Middle Iranian.
- **Greek Commagene:** **Ἀρτάγνης / Artagnēs**, in the compound cult-name Artagnes–Herakles–Ares.

The modern spelling “Verethragna” is a typographically simplified scholarly convention, not a form used by ancient Persian speakers.

### Etymology and the Indra problem

**Secure linguistic comparison; disputed religious reconstruction.** Avestan *vərəθraγan-* corresponds formally to Vedic Sanskrit **vṛtrahán-**, “breaker/slayer of obstruction,” preeminently an epithet of Indra. The second element is from the Indo-Iranian root “strike/slay”; the first originally denotes an enclosure, obstacle, or resistance. In Vedic narrative, *Vṛtra* has become the name of the serpent-like blocker of the waters, so *vṛtrahán* can be read specifically as “Vṛtra-slayer.” In Avestan, however, *vərəθra-* need not name a personal monster.

Three positions must be kept distinct:

1. **Older comparativist reconstruction:** Proto-Indo-Iranian religion possessed an Indra *Vṛtrahan*, whose functions were divided in Iran between the demonized daēva Indra and the orthodox yazata Vərəθraγna.
2. **Alternative reconstruction:** *Vṛtraghan* was originally a divine “resistance-breaker” distinct from Indra; Vedic tradition attached his title and exploits to Indra, whereas Iranian tradition retained the abstraction as a separate deity.
3. **More cautious position:** the linguistic equation is secure, but a recoverable one-to-one Proto-Indo-Iranian personality is not. Vərəθraγna never kills a dragon in the surviving Avesta. In Iranian scripture it is Θraētaona who defeats Aži Dahāka. Armenian Vahagn’s dragon-slaying may preserve old Iranian material, or it may be an Armenian/Hurrian regional development.

The fourteenth-century BCE Mitanni treaty already invokes Indra as a god. This rules out the strongest version of the theory that Indra began merely as a human hero later promoted to divinity. It does not determine how the title *vṛtrahán-* developed before the Iranian and Indo-Aryan traditions separated.

### Principal Avestan titles

All are **securely attested in *Yašt* 14**, unless noted:

- **“Made by Ahura”** (*ahuraδāta-*): the hymn’s recurrent theological qualification, subordinating the older victory power to Mazdean creation.
- **“Best-armed of the yazatas”** (14.1).
- **“Strongest in strength”** and **“most victorious in victory”** (14.3).
- **Most endowed with attacking force, glory, favor, welfare, and healing** (14.3).
- **Destroyer of the malice** of daēvas, humans, sorcerers (*yātu-*), *pairikās*, oppressors, and ritual enemies (14.4; compare 14.62).
- **Maker or giver of virility**, strength of arms, bodily soundness, and extraordinary eyesight (14.28–33).
- **Protector of armies and Iranian lands** (14.43–53).
- **Companion of Mithra and Rašnu** in detecting false oath-takers (14.47).
- Associated with **Vanaintī Uparatāt**, “Conquering Superiority,” in the framing invocations of *Yašt* 14.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Dating and transmission

The Younger Avestan *Yašts* were composed and recomposed within a priestly oral culture. Individual hymns contain strata of different ages; archaic diction does not make every verse equally ancient. *Yašt* 14 is commonly counted among the older or archaizing hymns, yet it is visibly composite: passages overlap with *Yašts* 8, 10, and 16, and scholars disagree over the direction of borrowing in several cases.

The Avestan alphabet and written archetypes belong to late antique Zoroastrian redaction, probably under the Sasanians, but the surviving manuscripts are later still. The *Yašts* survive in Khordeh Avesta collections and in dedicated *Yašt* codices. Jm4 (1352) is the oldest surviving Khordeh Avesta; F1 (1591) is the principal complete pure-*Yašt* manuscript. Karl F. Geldner’s nineteenth-century critical edition collated the manuscript tradition. The Avestan Digital Archive now supplies manuscript images and metadata.

### Primary corpus

#### *Bahrām Yašt*, *Yašt* 14

**Language:** Younger Avestan.  
**Composition:** Old Iranian oral composition, layered; no exact date.  
**Manuscripts:** medieval/early modern, especially the *Yašt* codex tradition represented by F1.  
**Status:** principal and indispensable source.

Its 64 stanzas divide approximately as follows:

- 14.1–27: ten appearances to Zaraθuštra.
- 14.28–33: gifts of virility, bodily integrity, eyesight, and victory in thought, speech, action, address, and reply.
- 14.34–46: feather and bone magic associated with the *vārəγna* bird; apotropaic and battlefield use.
- 14.47–64: joint action with Mithra and Rašnu, national defense, sacrificial regulations, condemnation of rival ritualists, Haoma-assisted battle prayer, and final praise.

Darmesteter’s 1898 English translation remains readily accessible but follows many interpretations now considered uncertain; its identifications “raven,” “buck,” and several zoological details should not be treated as final philology.

#### *Mihr Yašt*, *Yašt* 10.70 and related passages

**Securely attested Younger Avestan material.** Mithra travels with a ferocious sharp-toothed boar that smashes adversaries. Because this description closely resembles Vərəθraγna’s fifth form in *Yašt* 14.15, Ilya Gershevitch and others identified Mithra’s boar, Dāmōiš Upamana, as Vərəθraγna or his manifestation. That identification is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**: *Yašt* 10 does not explicitly name the boar Vərəθraγna.

#### *Sīrōza* 1.20 and 2.20

The short and long “Thirty Days” invoke the divine patron of each day. Vərəθraγna governs day 20. This is direct evidence for his place in the Zoroastrian ritual calendar, although the date at which the entire day-name system assumed its surviving form is debated. The earliest external evidence for a Zoroastrian day-name system is a Bactrian Aramaic document, possibly from 329 BCE, naming Dēn’s day; it does not itself name Vərəθraγna.

#### Other Avestan occurrences

The adjective *vərəθraγan-* is applied more broadly to divine powers, Θraētaona, and future saviors. These occurrences show that “victorious/resistance-breaking” remained a productive title rather than an exclusive personal name. The Gāthās do not contain a developed narrative about Vərəθraγna. Neither the *Vendīdād* nor the Achaemenid inscriptions provide a biography or independent cult history for him.

#### Pahlavi literature

- **The *Bundahišn***, compiled from late Sasanian learning in the early Islamic period, names Mars **Wahrām** within a Greco-Babylonian-derived planetary system. This is **learned late antique systematization**, not evidence that the Younger Avestan god was originally a planet.
- **The *Dēnkard***, chiefly ninth-century, summarizes the lost Sasanian *Bagān Yašt Nask*, the collection containing hymns to named deities. It also preserves parallels involving Vāy’s camel form.
- **The *Dādestān ī Dēnīg* 31.7**, a ninth-century religious judgment, says Bahrām is seen in fire. This belongs to the later interpretive association between Bahrām, victory, and sacred fire.
- **The *Mēnōg ī Xrad*** and **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag** belong to the wider Middle Persian theological corpus but do not furnish a major independent Vərəθraγna myth-cycle.
- **Kerdīr’s third-century inscriptions** name the highest consecrated fire as *ādur ī wahrām*. Mary Boyce and Gherardo Gnoli argue that this originally meant “victorious fire,” with *wahrām* an adjective, and was only later understood as “Fire of Bahrām,” the god. The distinction matters: every Ātaš Bahrām is not straightforward evidence for a temple dedicated to Vərəθraγna.

#### Kushan coin legends

Coins of Kaniṣka I and successors, second century CE, name **ΟΡΛΑΓΝΟ** in Bactrian written with Greek letters. A standing martial figure wears a bird-shaped or winged headdress, holds a spear, and rests a hand on a sword. This is the clearest securely labeled ancient image of an eastern Iranian form of the god. The iconography is a Kushan creation; it is not merely copied from Herakles.

#### Commagene inscriptions and reliefs

At Nemrut Dağı and Arsameia, Antiochus I of Commagene, first century BCE, instituted a royal cult whose Greek inscriptions join Iranian and Greek divine names. **Artagnes–Herakles–Ares** is the explicit western equation of Vərəθraγna with Herakles and Ares. Reliefs show Antiochus clasping hands with the club-bearing, lion-skinned god. This is not timeless equivalence but a dated dynastic synthesis serving Antiochus’s Greco-Iranian kingship.

#### Armenian Christian historians

Agathangelos and Movsēs Xorenacʿi, writing in Christian Armenia centuries after conversion, preserve information about **Vahagn**:

- Vahagn is *kʿaǰ*, “brave.”
- His temple stood at Aštišat.
- He bore the title **višapakʿał**, “dragon-reaper.”
- Movsēs preserves the short archaizing song of his fiery birth: heaven and earth labor, the purple sea labors, a red reed gives birth to flame and then to a youth whose hair and beard are fire and whose eyes are suns.
- Movsēs also recasts him genealogically as a heroic Armenian royal figure.

These are **regional Armenian transformations**, transmitted through hostile or antiquarian Christian sources, not missing chapters of the Avesta.

#### Manichaean evidence

Sogdian Manichaean texts use **Wšγn** as the name of the cosmic hero Adamas. This is a **syncretistic translation-equation**: an inherited Iranian victory-god name was reassigned to a Manichaean salvific combatant. It does not establish that orthodox Zoroastrians identified Bahrām with Adamas.

#### *Šāhnāma*

Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed around 1010 CE, contains Sasanian kings and heroes named Bahrām, especially Bahrām Gōr and Bahrām Čōbīn. These are historical-legendary humans carrying a theophoric or victory-laden name. Ferdowsi does not preserve a continuous independent myth-cycle about the yazata. Confusing Bahrām Gōr’s adventures with deeds of the god is a **modern category error**.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Early Avestan:**

- breaking resistance and winning combat;
- military protection;
- victory in thought, speech, debate, action, and reply;
- royal and martial glory;
- virility and sexual potency;
- bodily integrity and healing;
- exceptional sight and alertness;
- counter-magic and curse reversal;
- protection of the Iranian lands from invasion, plague, leprosy, hostile weapons, and poisonous vegetation.

**Later developments:**

- moral and cosmic victory over evil in Middle Persian theology;
- planetary identity with Mars;
- royal patronage and victory symbolism;
- protection of travelers in later Zoroastrian practice;
- association—partly through linguistic reinterpretation—with the highest grade of sacred fire.

No ancient source assigns him a universally fixed color, number, flower, weekday, or zodiac sign. Modern lists making Tuesday, hyacinth, red, or the number ten permanent “correspondences” generally extrapolate from Mars, the ten forms, or modern occult systems. They are not securely Avestan.

### The ten forms of *Yašt* 14

These are appearances or embodied forms, not “avatars” in the developed Vaiṣṇava theological sense.

1. **Strong, beautiful wind** (14.2–5): carries glory, health, and strength. This connects Vərəθraγna functionally with Vāta/Vāyu, but does not identify them.
2. **Bull with yellow ears and golden horns** (14.7): strength and Victory rest upon its horns.
3. **White horse with yellow ears and golden trappings** (14.9): swift, noble, and glory-bearing.
4. **Rutting, burden-bearing camel** (14.11–13): the hymn stresses male sexual force, foam, far sight, strong limbs, and dominance.
5. **Ferocious boar** (14.15): sharp-tusked, killing at one stroke, furious, blood- or moisture-faced, and irresistible.
6. **Fifteen-year-old youth** (14.17): the Iranian ideal age of physical perfection.
7. **Vārəγna bird** (14.19–21): swiftest of birds, able to overtake an arrow, flying at dawn over mountains and valleys. Older translations call it a raven; others prefer falcon, hawk, or bird of prey. “Raven” is therefore traditional but zoologically uncertain.
8. **Wild ram with curved horns** (14.23).
9. **Fighting horned wild goat/ibex** (14.25); “buck” or “stag” in some older translations.
10. **Handsome armed man** with an ornamented golden-bladed sword (14.27).

The number ten is explicit in the sequence. Comparison with Viṣṇu’s ten avatāras is comparative and typological, not evidence of direct borrowing: the Avestan list predates the classical fixed Purāṇic *daśāvatāra* scheme.

### Boar

The boar is the most aggressive Avestan form. It also accompanies Mithra in *Yašt* 10.70, whether as Vərəθraγna himself or as a parallel martial creature. In Sasanian art, boars appear on seals, stucco, metalwork, textiles, vessels, and princely caps. Some instances probably invoke Bahrām, royal victory, or *xᵛarənah*; not every Sasanian boar is securely identifiable as the god. Uninscribed animal images require contextual argument.

### Bird and feather

The *vārəγna* bird is both Vərəθraγna’s seventh form and, in *Yašt* 19.35, a form associated with escaping royal *xᵛarənah*. Its feather or bone protects its bearer, restores glory, makes enemies tremble, turns back spells, and assists battlefield victory (*Yašt* 14.34–46). The rite is presented as restricted knowledge passed from father to son, brother to brother, or priest to pupil.

This material later encouraged identification of winged crowns, eagle or falcon emblems, and bird-topped altars with Bahrām. Bahrām II’s crown prominently uses wings; Pērōz and Xusrō II Parwēz reused victory-associated wing motifs. Such identifications are often plausible, but an unlabeled winged emblem may also signify *xᵛarənah* more generally.

### Secure and disputed images

- **Secure:** Kushan coins explicitly labeled Orlagno.
- **Secure syncretistic identification:** Artagnes–Herakles–Ares reliefs and inscriptions of Commagene.
- **Plausible but interpretive:** Sasanian winged crowns, boar-headed caps, bird-on-altar seals.
- **Disputed:** reclining Herakles figures along Iranian routes and small figures in Sasanian investiture reliefs are sometimes named Bahrām, but without labels their identity cannot be treated as certain.
- **Modern misidentification:** generic Achaemenid winged disks, Faravahar images, or any Sasanian boar are often labeled Verethragna online without sufficient evidence.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

The Avesta gives Vərəθraγna no father, mother, spouse, child, or sibling. “Made by Ahura” is a theological statement of creation or authorization, not a biological genealogy. Armenian tradition associates Vahagn with Anahit and Astłik and later historicizes him as a royal hero, but this cannot be imported into Avestan genealogy.

There is likewise no surviving canonical story of his birth in Iran. Vahagn’s fiery birth-song is specifically Armenian.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Ahura Mazdā — creator, declarer, and instructor.** Ahura creates Vərəθraγna, names him the best-armed yazata, and explains his cult and feather magic to Zaraθuštra. *Yašt* 14.1–5, 34–46; Younger Avestan. **Tier: securely attested early/canonical Avestan.**
- **Vanaintī Uparatāt (“Conquering Superiority”) — paired cultic power.** The framing dedication joins Vərəθraγna with this personified victorious superiority. *Yašt* 14.0, 64; Younger Avestan. **Tier: securely attested Avestan liturgical association.**
- **Xᵛarənah (“Glory”) — carried in every manifestation.** His forms repeatedly bear Mazda-created Glory; the *vārəγna* bird is also a form in which Glory moves in *Yašt* 19.35. *Yašt* 14.2–27; *Yašt* 19.35. **Tier: securely attested conceptual/divine association, not genealogy.**
- **Mithra — joint patrol and possible battlefield companionship.** Vərəθraγna travels among armies with Mithra and Rašnu to detect false oaths; the boar beside Mithra in *Yašt* 10.70 is probably but not explicitly Vərəθraγna. *Yašt* 14.47; *Yašt* 10.70. **Tier: secure joint appearance; boar identification is modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **Rašnu — joint patrol.** With Mithra and Vərəθraγna, Rašnu examines oath-breaking and threatened punishment. *Yašt* 14.47. **Tier: securely attested Avestan interaction.**
- **Haoma — coordinated martial invocation.** Immediately within Vərəθraγna’s hymn the worshipper offers victorious Haoma for bodily protection and victory over an army. *Yašt* 14.57–58. **Tier: securely attested ritual association; the text does not narrate a meeting between anthropomorphic gods.**
- **Gə̄uš Urvan, the Soul of the Bull — ritual complaint in his hymn.** Vərəθraγna proclaims that the Bull’s Soul does not receive proper worship because rival ritualists spill blood and burn forbidden material. *Yašt* 14.54–56. **Tier: securely attested but textually difficult Avestan passage.**
- **Vāta/Vāyu — shared wind and camel characteristics.** Vərəθraγna’s first form is wind, and Middle Persian tradition gives Vāy a camel form. No text stages a meeting. **Tier: modern comparative relationship; no documented interaction.**
- **Tištrya — shared bull, horse, and fifteen-year-old forms.** *Yašt* 8 gives Tištrya forms also found in *Yašt* 14. No meeting occurs. **Tier: securely attested parallel imagery, not interaction.**
- **Anahit — associated with Armenian Vahagn’s cult.** Armenian sources place Vahagn within a cultic triad or complex involving Anahit and Astłik. **Tier: regional Armenian development, recorded by late-antique Christian authors.**
- **Astłik/Aphrodite — consort or concubine in Armenian tradition.** Astłik is associated sexually with Vahagn and identified with Aphrodite. **Tier: regional Armenian syncretism; absent from Iranian scripture.**
- **Mihr in Armenia — possible rival or displaced counterpart.** Scholarly reconstruction sees conflict or functional competition between Mihr and Vahagn, drawing on Armenian and pseudo-Plutarchan material. No simple canonical combat narrative survives. **Tier: modern reconstruction from regional sources.**
- **Tešub — proposed Armenian identification.** Vahagn’s dragon-reaping and fiery/weather traits have been connected with Hurrian-Urartian Tešub. **Tier: regional syncretistic hypothesis, not an explicit Avestan equation.**
- **Herakles — explicit equation.** Artagnes–Herakles–Ares is named in Antiochus I’s Commagene cult; the god is shown with Herakles’ club and lion skin. **Tier: securely attested first-century BCE syncretism.**
- **Ares — explicit equation.** Same Commagene compound; later planetary logic reinforces the equation through Mars. **Tier: securely attested Hellenistic syncretism.**
- **Nergal — astrological correspondence.** Babylonian Nergal, Greek Ares/Mars, and Iranian Wahrām were aligned as the god/name of the red planet. **Tier: Greco-Babylonian-Iranian learned systematization, late antique rather than early Avestan.**
- **Adamas — translated as Wšγn in Sogdian Manichaean literature.** **Tier: Manichaean syncretistic translation.**
- **Angra Mainyu — cosmic opponent by later implication.** Later Zoroastrian theology presents Bahrām’s victory as directed against evil; *Yašt* 14 names the daēvas and the creatures or allies of evil but does not narrate a personal duel between Vərəθraγna and Angra Mainyu. **Tier: later canonical systematization; no direct early combat episode.**
- **Indra — no meeting or identity in the Avesta.** The name is linguistically cognate with Indra’s epithet *vṛtrahán*, while Avestan Indra is a daēva. No surviving text says Vərəθraγna “is” Indra or stages an encounter. **Tier: comparative reconstruction only.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The ten manifestations

This is the god’s principal Avestan “myth,” though it is structured as repeated epiphany rather than continuous adventure. Zaraθuštra asks Ahura who is best armed; Vərəθraγna then comes to the prophet in ten increasingly varied forms. Each is a package of victorious capacity: the wind cannot be restrained; the bull carries strength on its horns; the horse embodies speed and prestige; the camel combines endurance, sexuality, and nocturnal sight; the boar annihilates opposition; the youth embodies ideal vigor; the bird exceeds the arrow; ram and ibex bring horned force; the final warrior makes the abstraction culturally human and armed.

**Development:** The repetition, mixed zoology, shared motifs with Tištrya and Vāyu, and textual parallels elsewhere indicate compilation rather than a single authored story. Calling the forms “incarnations” is convenient, but the text says that the god “came in the shape/form” (*kəhrpa*) of each.

### Feather magic and invulnerability

When Zaraθuštra asks how to answer curses from enemies, Ahura instructs him to take a feather of the *vārəγna* bird, rub his body, and turn the curse back. Possessing its bone or feather prevents defeat, commands human respect, preserves glory, and terrifies enemies. Four feathers—or perhaps a four-feathered arrow—are used when armies meet. The correct invocation determines which army conquers.

**Function:** apotropaic magic, military ritual, and restricted priestly knowledge.  
**Historical layer:** archaic magical practice incorporated into a Mazdean dialogue and ethical frame.  
**Later echo:** the healing feather of the Sīmurgh in the *Šāhnāma* is comparable, but direct literary descent cannot be proved merely from resemblance.

### Victory for the Iranian lands

Correct worship protects the Aryan/Iranian countries from invading armies, plague, leprosy, hostile chariots, spears, and poisonous growth. Improper participation—especially by killers, ritually disqualified persons, and opponents of Mazdean law—causes Vərəθraγna to withdraw healing power, exposing the people to mass defeat.

This is religiously regulated war: victory depends not simply on bravery but on correct sacrifice and communal purity. It also demonstrates the political adaptability of the god to states and military aristocracies.

### Condemnation of the Vyāmburas

*Yašt* 14.54–56 attacks obscure ritual adversaries called Vyāmburas. They spill blood “like water,” mistreat the Soul of the Bull, and burn prohibited plant or wood. The text predicts that they will attempt to strike but fail.

This passage documents polemic against rival sacrifice; it does **not** securely document human sacrifice. Blood sacrifice is plainly present in the religious environment, and 14.50 prescribes cooking a monochrome head of cattle for Vərəθraγna, but the hymn’s objection concerns wrong agents, wrong ritual, excessive bloodshed, or improper treatment—not an abolition of all animal sacrifice. No secure evidence ties human sacrifice specifically to Vərəθraγna.

### Vahagn’s birth and dragon-reaping

Movsēs Xorenacʿi preserves an Armenian song in which heaven, earth, and the purple sea labor; a red reed gives birth to smoke, flame, and a fiery-haired youth with sun-like eyes. Vahagn later bears the epithet “dragon-reaper.”

**Regional variant:** Armenian, not Avestan.  
**Date:** recorded in the fifth-century Armenian historiographical tradition, though the song may be substantially older.  
**Interpretation:** Iranian Vərəθraγna, Armenian poetic tradition, Heraclean comparison, and Hurrian-Urartian storm/dragon combat may all have contributed. The surviving evidence cannot isolate a single origin.

### No Avestan dragon-slaying myth

Despite the name’s relationship to Vedic *vṛtrahán*, no Avestan narrative has Vərəθraγna kill Vṛtra, Aži Dahāka, or another dragon. *Yašt* 14.40 mentions Θraētaona riding or using the magical bird-power and then defeating Aži Dahāka. The victory god is adjacent to the deed, but Θraētaona is the slayer. Modern retellings that make Verethragna personally kill Aži Dahāka silently conflate separate figures.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Avestan worship

*Yašt* 14 prescribes:

- praise and sacrifice with Haoma;
- meat and libation;
- *baresman* bundles;
- sacred speech and correctly spoken formulas;
- a uniformly colored animal for cooking;
- exclusion of ritually or morally disqualified participants;
- battlefield invocation;
- restricted transmission of feather magic.

The hymn is evidence for an intended ritual system, not proof that every prescription was uniformly practiced throughout Iran.

### Calendar

The twentieth day of each thirty-day Zoroastrian month is Bahrām’s day, documented in the *Sīrōza*. Sogdian and Chorasmian twentieth-day names have likewise been derived from Vərəθraγna, suggesting broad Iranian calendrical diffusion. In living Zoroastrian calendars the day remains Rōz Bahrām, and the Bahrām Yašt may be recited for victory, protection, health, and success.

### Cult centers and temples

The archaeological record does not presently support a securely identified Achaemenid “Temple of Verethragna” in the Iranian heartland.

- **Nemrut Dağı and Arsameia, Commagene:** securely documented first-century BCE royal sanctuaries of Artagnes–Herakles–Ares, with statues, dexiosis reliefs, inscriptions, feasts, priests, and dynastic ritual.
- **Aštišat, Armenia:** literary sources locate Vahagn’s temple there. During Armenia’s Christianization, Gregory the Illuminator’s church establishment appropriated the site; it became associated with John the Baptist and Athenogenes. This is a documented process of cultic replacement, though the archaeology cannot reconstruct every claimed event.
- **Iranian fire temples:** buildings housing an Ātaš Bahrām are not automatically temples “to Bahrām.” The designation probably began as “Victorious Fire.” Later worshippers did understand Bahrām as present in, or especially related to, the fire.
- **Roadside Herakles figures:** some have been interpreted as Bahrām and linked to his later protection of travelers. Without inscriptions this remains attribution, not certainty.

### Ātaš Bahrām

The highest grade of Zoroastrian temple fire is the Ātaš Bahrām. Its elaborate consecration combines and purifies fires drawn from multiple occupational or social sources. Modern Parsi and Iranian worship at these temples is living Zoroastrian practice.

Historically, however:

1. Kerdīr’s third-century inscriptions have “victorious fire.”
2. Middle Persian religious interpretation increasingly associated *Wahrām* with the named yazata.
3. In Islamic-period and modern understanding, “Fire of Bahrām” became natural.

The cult of sacred fire and the cult of the victory yazata overlap, but they should not be collapsed into one institution from the Avestan period onward.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

Animal sacrifice or cooked meat-offering is explicitly prescribed in *Yašt* 14.50. The text condemns improper bloodshed by rival worshippers in 14.54. No source examined provides secure evidence for:

- human sacrifice to Vərəθraγna;
- temple prostitution in his Iranian or Armenian cult;
- a hereditary priesthood exclusively devoted to him;
- ritualized sexual intercourse based on his virility;
- a historical cult of killing enemies as literal sacrifices to the god.

Claims of these practices require evidence not presently available.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Indo-Iranian background: before direct attestation

**Modern scholarly reconstruction.** The name and Vedic *vṛtrahán-* descend from common Indo-Iranian language. A divine or heroic resistance-breaking complex probably existed before separation, but its mythology cannot be reconstructed in full. The Iranian god’s lack of an Avestan dragon combat is a central limit.

### Younger Avestan formation: first millennium BCE

Vərəθraγna becomes an orthodox *yazata*, repeatedly described as “made by Ahura.” A pre-Zoroastrian warrior power was incorporated into Mazdean theology, its victory redirected against daēvas, sorcery, false worship, disease, and enemies of the Iranian lands. *Yašt* 14 nevertheless preserves martial virility and magical feather rites.

### Achaemenid period: absence from royal inscriptions

Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam repeatedly invoke Ahura Mazdā and, in some later Achaemenid inscriptions, other named gods appear. Vərəθraγna does not. This does not prove that Persians did not worship him; it proves that he was not named in the surviving Achaemenid royal epigraphic program.

Herodotus’s Scythian “Ares” and Strabo’s report that the Carmanians worshipped Ares may concern Iranian war gods. Identifying either specifically as Vərəθraγna is inference through Greek interpretation. Strabo’s Carmanian report probably derives from the Alexander-era observer Nearchus, but “Ares” is not an Iranian name.

### Hellenistic and Parthian worlds

Vərəθraγna was translated through Greek martial and heroic categories:

- as **Ares**, god of battle;
- as **Herakles**, the club-bearing victorious strongman;
- at Commagene as the explicit compound **Artagnes–Herakles–Ares**.

This was political as well as theological. Antiochus I used mixed Iranian and Greek divine names to authenticate both sides of his ancestry and sacralize his dynasty.

In Armenia, Parthian-Iranian *Vahagn* acquired a major cult, Heraclean comparison, fiery birth, dragon-reaping, and relations with Astłik and Anahit. These are local developments, not corrupt copies of a single Persian original.

### Kushan and eastern Iranian period

Second-century Kushan coins place Orlagno in a multilingual imperial pantheon beside Iranian, Greek, Indian, and regional gods. His martial human form, spear, sword, and winged headdress translate the Avestan victory power into imperial numismatic language.

Sogdian Wšγn shows further eastern survival. The same name’s application to Manichaean Adamas demonstrates that “victory” could become a translation label across religions.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

Bahrām acquired strong royal resonance:

- **Bahrām I** (r. 271–274)
- **Bahrām II** (r. 274–293)
- **Bahrām III** (r. 293)
- **Bahrām IV** (r. 388–399)
- **Bahrām V Gōr** (r. 420–438)
- **Bahrām VI Čōbīn** (r. 590–591), a non-dynastic general and usurping king

“Six Sasanian kings” therefore includes Bahrām Čōbīn, who was not born into the reigning Sasanian house. The name derived from the god but did not make every bearer a cult figure.

Bahrām II’s winged crown advertises his name and divine victory. Boars, predatory birds, and wings became royal signs, though individual images remain open to interpretation. The god’s name also became the Middle Persian name of Mars through Greco-Babylonian astrology. The *Bundahišn* preserves the resulting system.

The Mars equation is **not early Avestan**. It resulted from correspondence:

- Babylonian Nergal: war/death and the red planet;
- Greek Ares/Roman Mars: war and planet;
- Iranian Wahrām: victory/war, consequently the planet.

### Christianization of Armenia

Traditional Armenian chronology places royal conversion in the early fourth century, while the detailed temple-destruction narrative survives in fifth-century Christian texts. Vahagn’s sanctuary at Aštišat was displaced by a Christian center. Some functions could be transferred: John the Baptist became a protector of travelers at the new shrine, recalling a later protective function of Bahrām. The evidence documents institutional replacement, not secret continuous Vahagn worship.

### Islamic conquest and medieval Iran

After the seventh-century conquest, Zoroastrian institutions lost royal sponsorship and contracted geographically. Bahrām remained:

- in Zoroastrian liturgy and calendar;
- in the term Ātaš Bahrām;
- as the Persian name of Mars;
- as a common personal name;
- in royal-historical memory through Bahrām Gōr and Bahrām Čōbīn;
- indirectly in epic animal and feather motifs.

The god was not simply converted into the Muslim literary king Bahrām Gōr. The shared name facilitated cultural continuity while the categories—yazata, planet, fire, king—remained distinct.

### Parsi and modern Zoroastrian practice

Zoroastrians in India maintained and founded Ātaš Bahrāms, including the Irānšāh fire at Udvada and later Bombay temples. Visits to major fires can function as pilgrimage. Bahrām’s day and hymn remain part of living devotion, especially in prayers for protection, overcoming difficulty, health, and travel.

This is continuous Zoroastrian veneration mediated by changing languages and institutions, not a twentieth-century neopagan reconstruction.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

Modern editions, translations, comparative philology, and digitization have made Vərəθraγna far more visible outside Zoroastrian communities. Geldner’s edition, Darmesteter’s translation, the studies of Benveniste and Renou, Boyce, Dumézil, Kellens, Malandra, Skjærvø, Gnoli, Hinnells, and the Avestan Digital Archive shaped the contemporary picture.

### National and political reception

Bahrām remains a prestigious Iranian personal name and evokes victory, Sasanian kingship, Mars, and epic Iran. Modern nationalist uses sometimes present Verethragna as an uncomplicated “Aryan war god” or as proof of an originally militarist Iranian religion. Such presentations usually omit:

- the Mazdean moralization of victory;
- the ritual and healing functions;
- the uncertain date of *Yašt* 14;
- the absence of Vərəθraγna from Achaemenid royal inscriptions;
- the difference between Iranian Bahrām and Armenian Vahagn.

No well-documented large extremist movement centered specifically on Vərəθraγna was found. Generic appropriation of Iranian or “Aryan” imagery should not be attributed to his cult without evidence.

### Occultism and neopaganism

Unlike Mithras, Anāhitā, or Ahriman, Verethragna did not acquire a major documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or early twentieth-century ceremonial magic. Modern websites sometimes create planetary correspondences, invocations, altar colors, weekdays, or “ten-avatar workings” from Mars and *Yašt* 14. These are **modern inventions or reconstructions** unless tied to living Zoroastrian practice.

Small contemporary polytheist and reconstructionist circles invoke Verethragna independently of Zoroastrian orthodoxy. Their scale is not securely measurable. Such practice is modern devotional reconstruction, distinct from continuous Bahrām Yašt recitation among Zoroastrians.

### Popular culture

The Japanese light-novel series *Campione!* and its manga/anime adaptation turn Verethragna into a youthful “Heretic God” whose ten forms become combat “Authorities.” He fights Melqart and transfers powers to the protagonist. This materially popularized the ten forms but is openly modern fantasy, not transmitted Iranian mythology.

Online games and fantasy compendia often make him a dragon-slayer, thunder god, or Persian Indra. These characterizations commonly combine Vedic Indra, Armenian Vahagn, and Avestan Vərəθraγna. The composite is modern.

### Claims requiring rejection or qualification

- **“Verethragna is simply Persian Indra.”** Linguistically related; historically unproved as identity.
- **“He slew Aži Dahāka.”** No; Θraētaona does in surviving Avestan tradition.
- **“The raven is certain.”** The *vārəγna* is a swift bird of prey; species identification remains disputed.
- **“Every Ātaš Bahrām is originally his temple.”** Probably false; “victorious fire” preceded the personalized reading.
- **“He was always Mars.”** False; planetary identity is late antique syncretism.
- **“The ten forms came from Viṣṇu’s ten avatāras.”** No evidence of such direct dependence.
- **“He had a wife named Astłik in Persian religion.”** Armenian regional tradition only.
- **“Achaemenid kings publicly worshipped him at Behistun.”** Not documented.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Benveniste and Renou

Émile Benveniste and Louis Renou’s *Vṛtra et Vṛθragna* (1934) remains foundational for comparing the Vedic and Iranian materials. They emphasized the history of the word “obstacle/resistance” and challenged simplistic reconstruction of a single Indo-Iranian dragon-slayer. Their treatment of Armenian Vahagn as substantially secondary or extra-Iranian has in turn been criticized.

### Mary Boyce

Boyce placed Vərəθraγna within the history of pre-Zoroastrian Iranian religion and its accommodation to Zoroaster’s reform. She also stressed the distinction between a “victorious fire” and a fire originally dedicated to the god. Her broader chronological reconstructions remain influential but are not universally accepted in every detail.

### Jean Kellens and ritual-philological approaches

Kellens and later Avestan scholarship resist reading the *Yašts* as transparent myth handbooks. Formula, liturgical voice, textual reuse, meter, and ritual setting matter. On this approach, *Yašt* 14 is not a clean biography but a composite performance-text producing victory.

### Dumézil and warrior ideology

Georges Dumézil interpreted the figure through Indo-European warrior ideology and the moral dangers of martial force. His comparative structures remain suggestive but should not substitute for direct textual evidence.

### Vṛtrahan debate

The secure datum is phonological and semantic correspondence. The disputed questions are:

- Was there one Proto-Indo-Iranian god behind both traditions?
- Was the original enemy a dragon, an obstacle, a dam, or a personification created from a title?
- Did Zoroastrian reform split Indra’s functions between a demonized Indra and orthodox Vərəθraγna?
- Does Armenian Vahagn preserve lost Iranian dragon combat or reflect Hurrian-Armenian synthesis?

No surviving source decisively settles the reconstruction.

### Textual stratification

*Yašt* 14 shares material with:

- *Yašt* 8.56–61 on national protection;
- *Yašt* 10.70 and 10.35–36 on Mithraic martial imagery;
- *Yašt* 16.6–13 on gifts and faculties.

In some cases *Yašt* 14 likely borrowed; in others it may preserve the earlier version. The hymn is therefore a dossier of inherited and adapted material, not a composition securely datable as a whole.

### Iconographic caution

Older scholarship readily labeled Iranian Herakles figures, boars, birds, wings, and animal seals as Vərəθraγna. Current method requires:

1. inscriptional naming;
2. archaeological context;
3. distinctive attributes;
4. comparison with securely labeled images.

Orlagno coins and Commagene inscriptions meet this standard. Many Sasanian attributions remain probable rather than certain.

### State of the evidence

- No original Avestan autograph survives.
- The *Yašt* manuscripts are medieval or early modern.
- The Avestan text preserves oral material many centuries older than its copies.
- Kushan coins and Commagene monuments provide contemporary labeled material evidence.
- Armenian evidence comes through Christian authors after the destruction or conversion of pagan institutions.
- Sasanian art supplies abundant possible symbolism but relatively few labels.
- Achaemenid silence remains significant: Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam cannot be cited as attestations of Vərəθraγna.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The reconstructable Vərəθraγna is not a single timeless personality. The record begins with a layered Avestan victory-power made orthodox as a servant of Ahura Mazdā; expands into ritual medicine, counter-magic, animal epiphany, and national defense; becomes Artagnes–Herakles–Ares in Commagene, Orlagno on Kushan coins, fiery dragon-reaping Vahagn in Armenia, Wšγn in Sogdian and Manichaean translation, Sasanian royal Bahrām and planetary Mars, and finally a living Zoroastrian yazata alongside multiple modern literary reinventions. The linguistic tie to Indra *Vṛtrahan* is secure. Identity with Indra, personal dragon-slaying, original ownership of the highest temple fire, and most modern occult correspondences are not.
