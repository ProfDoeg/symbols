# Atar: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the Younger Avesta, Atar is a **yazata**—a divine being “worthy of worship”—and the divine presence or hypostasis of fire. In the older Gathas, *ātar* is less clearly a separately personified god: it is Ahura Mazda’s fire, an active medium of illumination, ordeal, judgment, and future transformation. Middle Persian theology treats Ādur/Ātaxš both as a spiritual being and as the sacred fires present in creation and cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Old Avestan *ātar-* in the Gathas, especially Yasna 31.3, 34.4, 43.4 and 47.6. These orally composed texts are generally placed in the late second or early first millennium BCE, but no exact date is demonstrable. Their earliest surviving manuscript witnesses are medieval, more than two millennia later. Atar does **not** appear by name in the Achaemenid royal inscriptions.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Old Avestan or otherwise securely early Iranian evidence.
- **[YOUNGER AVESTAN]** Material in the Younger Avesta, composed and transmitted over a long period in the first millennium BCE, though preserved only in medieval manuscripts.
- **[CLASSICAL WITNESS]** Greek or Roman testimony, valuable but external and sometimes interpretive.
- **[SASANIAN / PAHLAVI SYSTEMATIZATION]** Middle Persian doctrine or institutional evidence, often written down or redacted in the ninth–twelfth centuries CE but preserving Sasanian and sometimes older learning.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A local Iranian, Parsi, or other geographically limited form.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]** A historically secondary equation between distinct beings or cults.
- **[MEDIEVAL ACCRETION]** Islamic-period epic, rivāyat, or folklore not securely recoverable from early evidence.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Scholarly inference from comparative, linguistic, archaeological, or ritual evidence.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A recent claim lacking an identifiable ancient or medieval source.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

Atar is simultaneously:

1. ordinary and ritual fire;
2. Ahura Mazda’s manifest fire and instrument;
3. a divine recipient of sacrifice and prayer;
4. the agent of moral testing and eschatological purification;
5. in later theology, a differentiated family of cosmic fires;
6. in institutional religion, the sacred fire maintained in hearths and consecrated temples.

These senses should not be collapsed. The Gathic *ātar* is not yet the fully catalogued, genealogized figure of the Younger Avesta and Pahlavi books. Conversely, a Sasanian temple fire is not merely a symbol: ritual and priestly texts address fire as a living, responsive presence, even though modern Zoroastrians commonly explain that devotion offered before fire ultimately honors Ahura Mazda.

### Names and scripts

- **Old and Younger Avestan:** 𐬁𐬙𐬀𐬭, *ātar-*; nominative singular *ātarš*, “fire.”
- **Middle Persian:** *ādur*, *ādar*; Book Pahlavi also *ātaxš/ātaš*, conventionally represented in Inscriptional or Book Pahlavi as forms such as 𐭠𐭲𐭥𐭥 and 𐭠𐭲𐭧𐭱.
- **Parthian:** ordinarily *ādar*; a “place of burning fire” was apparently *ātarōšan*, preserved in Armenian *atrušan*.
- **New Persian:** آتش *ātaš*, “fire”; آذر *āḏar/āzar*, also the name of the ninth day and ninth month of the Zoroastrian calendar.
- **Gujarati/Parsi usage:** *Atash*, particularly in *Atash Behram*, *Atash Adaran*, and *Atash Dadgah*.
- **Greek and Latin writers:** usually translate or describe Persian fire rather than preserving Atar’s Iranian divine name.

Mary Boyce’s lexical account considered the ultimate derivation of *ātar-* unknown. More recent etymological work has proposed Proto-Iranian *ātar-/*ātr-* from an Indo-European formation associated with heat or fire, but the reconstruction is not universally treated as settled. The old derivation of Avestan *āθravan-* “priest” from *ātar-* is unsound: *āθravan-* corresponds to Vedic *atharvan-* and is not simply “fire-priest.” [**MODERN SCHOLARLY DISPUTE**; [Ādur](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/adur-and-adar-middle-persian-word-for-fire-the-avestan-form-is-atar-of-unknown-derivation-and-the-late-form/); [Āθravan](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/atravan-priest/); [IE-CoR cognate set](https://iecor.clld.org/cognatesets/1957)]

### Titles and recurrent descriptions

- **Ātar, “son of Ahura Mazda”** (*ātarš … puθra ahurahe mazdā̊*): frequent in Younger Avestan liturgy, including Yasna 62 and the Atash Niyayesh. “Son” is a divine genealogy, not a birth story. **[YOUNGER AVESTAN]**
- **“Fire of Ahura Mazda”**: both possession and theological affiliation; the Niyayesh addresses it as a “great yazata.” **[YOUNGER AVESTAN]**
- **Spə̄ništa / Speništ, “holiest” fire**: one of the five canonical fires. Interpretations differ between Avestan and Middle Persian enumerations as to whether this is the cult fire used on earth or the fire before Ohrmazd. **[YOUNGER AVESTAN; PAHLAVI SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Bərəzi-savah, “of high benefit”**: later associated with the highest temple fire. **[YOUNGER AVESTAN; PAHLAVI EXEGESIS]**
- **Vohu-fryāna, probably “loving the good”**: fire or vital warmth in humans and animals.
- **Urvāzišta, “most joyful”**: fire in plants.
- **Vāzišta, conventionally “swiftest”**: lightning or atmospheric fire.
- **Ātar vərəθraγan, “victorious fire”**: probable predecessor of Middle Persian *ādur ī Wahrām* and New Persian/Parsi *Atash Behram*. The later interpretation “Fire of the god Bahrām/Vərəθraγna” arose because the adjective “victorious” and the divine name converged in Middle Persian. It is therefore not evidence that Atar and Vərəθraγna were originally one god. **[HISTORICAL LINGUISTIC DEVELOPMENT]**
- **Ādurān / Atash Adaran**, “fire of fires”: middle grade of consecrated temple fire.
- **Dādgāh / Atash Dadgah**, approximately “appointed-place fire”: lowest formal grade; also usable for ordinary domestic and ritual observance.

### Classification through time

- **Gathic:** Ahura Mazda’s active fire; whether every occurrence is a fully personified god is disputed.
- **Younger Avestan:** a male-coded yazata, explicitly Ahura Mazda’s son.
- **Pahlavi:** a spiritual and material creation under Ohrmazd, closely assigned to Ašawahišt/Asha Vahishta, with numerous individualized cosmic and temple fires.
- **Modern Zoroastrian:** commonly explained as sacred fire, divine energy, or the visible focus of worship offered to Ahura Mazda. Communities differ over how literally Atar should be described as a distinct personal yazata.

No secure tradition makes Atar a demon, monster, angel in the Abrahamic sense, or an Amesha Spenta. His close relation to Asha Vahishta does not make the two originally identical.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Gathas

The oldest evidence occurs in the Gathas, Old Avestan poems incorporated into Yasna 28–34, 43–51 and 53. Their language is close to that of the Rigveda. Most specialists place their composition in an eastern Iranian oral environment well before the Achaemenids, but proposals range widely; a precise year or century cannot be proved. Skjærvø places their cultural origin broadly in second-millennium BCE Central Asia, whereas more conservative formulations say only that Old Avestan is several centuries older than Younger Avestan. [**EARLY; DATING DISPUTED**; [Skjærvø on the Gathas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/9781118635193.ctwl0020); [Avesta survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/)]

Important occurrences include:

- **Yasna 31.3:** Mazda’s fire is connected with the fate or recompense assigned to the two parties of truth and deceit.
- **Yasna 31.19:** one should hear the knower who envisages or recognizes truth and can speak with the power associated with fire.
- **Yasna 34.4:** the worshipper desires Ahura Mazda’s “mighty fire,” strong through truth, which gives visible aid to the righteous but harms the hostile.
- **Yasna 43.4:** the speaker will recognize Mazda as strong and holy when recompense comes through fire.
- **Yasna 47.6:** through Spenta Mainyu and fire, Mazda distributes good to the two parties; the passage contributes to later doctrines of fiery judgment.
- **Yasna 51.9:** the two parties undergo judgment through blazing fire.

Translations differ because Old Avestan syntax and religious vocabulary are exceptionally difficult. Mills, Humbach, Insler, Kellens, and Skjærvø should not be silently harmonized. The secure core is that fire belongs to Mazda and reveals or executes a morally discriminating judgment. The later concrete picture of a river of molten metal is not stated in full in the Gathas.

### Yasna Haptanghaiti and the Yasna

The Old Avestan Yasna Haptanghaiti, Yasna 35–41, praises fire together with the waters and may preserve an inherited Indo-Iranian sacrificial liturgy. It is a principal foundation for fire’s place at the center of worship. [**EARLY, perhaps incorporating pre-Zoroastrian ritual forms**]

Younger Yasna 62 is the central liturgical address to Atar. Fire is worshipped with fuel, *baresman*, milk, and mortar in hand; it is asked to grant well-being, prosperity, offspring, intelligence, eloquence, and a long, vigorous life. A striking passage personifies fire as looking at the hands of passers-by and asking, in effect, what gift a friend brings to a friend. The text therefore witnesses reciprocity, not inert symbolism. Yasna 17.11 gives the canonical five-fire classification later expanded by Pahlavi commentary. [**YOUNGER AVESTAN**; [Atash Niyayesh text](https://www.avesta.org/ka/niyayesh.htm); [Darmesteter’s Atash Niyayesh](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-zend-avesta-part-ii/shell/v-atas-nyayis)]

### Zamyad Yasht, Yasht 19

Yasht 19.46–51 contains Atar’s only extended surviving mythic action. Spenta Mainyu, Vohu Manah, Asha Vahishta, and Atar contest the unseizable *xᵛarənah* (“Glory,” royal or divine fortune) against Angra Mainyu, Akem Manah, Aeshma, Aži Dahāka, and Spityura.

Atar advances to seize the Glory. Aži Dahāka threatens to extinguish him forever; Atar withdraws. When Aži advances, Atar threatens to blaze in his jaws and rear so that he can no longer ravage Mazda’s world. The dragon retreats. Neither combatant obtains the Glory: it passes into Lake Vourukaša, where Apąm Napāt, the “Son of the Waters,” takes it. This is a standoff and displacement, not Atar’s outright victory. [**YOUNGER AVESTAN**; [Yasht 19.46–51](https://theoldways.co/library/yashts/yasht-19-zamyad-yasht-hymn-to-the-earth)]

### Vendidad

The Vendidad/Vidēvdād is a Younger Avestan ritual-legal collection, probably later in formation than much of the Yasna and major Yashts, though exact dating is uncertain. It treats fire above all through purity law:

- corpses, decaying matter, and other pollutants must not contaminate it;
- punishments attach to mishandling or extinguishing fire;
- fires found in impure occupations are recoverable through purification;
- Vendidad 4.54–55 preserves a fiery or “blazing water” ordeal for detecting violated promises.

The Vendidad does not give a coherent biography of Atar. It documents the legal transformation of sacred fire into a rigorously protected ritual entity. Claims that “Vendidad 1 narrates Atar fighting Aži Dahāka” confuse that chapter with Yasht 19. [**YOUNGER AVESTAN; CORRECTION OF MODERN MISCITATION**]

### Atash Niyayesh

The Atash Niyayesh is the fifth of the five *Niyayishn* prayers in the Khordeh Avesta. It has seventeen sections:

- sections 1–3 reproduce Gathic Yasna 33.12–14;
- sections 7–16 substantially reproduce Yasna 62.1–10;
- section 17 reproduces Yasna 34.4;
- other sections invoke Fire, the Glory, Mount Asnavant, and Mount Raēvant.

A late-Sasanian Pahlavi commentary interprets allusions in sections 5–6 as the three great fires—Farnbāg, Gušnasp, and Burzēn-Mihr. This is exegesis, not something explicit in the Avestan wording. Neryosang Dhaval translated it into Sanskrit around 1200; Persian and Gujarati translations followed. It remains a living lay and priestly prayer. [**COMPOSITE AVESTAN LITURGY; PAHLAVI SYSTEMATIZATION**; [Iranica, Atash Niyayishn](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/atas-niyayisn-the-fifth-in-a-group-of-five-zoroastrian-prayers-niyayisn-q/)]

### Pahlavi books

- **Bundahišn**, substantially a ninth-century compilation with a later recension: classifies five fires, relates their place in creation, says Ahriman afflicted fire with smoke, narrates the legendary careers of the three great fires, and makes Fire and Airyaman melt the world’s metals at the Renovation.
- **Dēnkard**, ninth–tenth centuries: preserves summaries of lost Avestan *nasks*, including a dialogue in which Fire complains to Ohrmazd about pollution and the impossibility of remaining undisturbed in material creation.
- **Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram**, ninth century: develops cosmogony and eschatology, including the final defeat of evil.
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad**, probably sixth–ninth centuries in present form: discusses the created world, moral order, and eschatology; fire is part of the good creation.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag**, compiled in the Islamic period from Sasanian-period traditions: places the visionary journey within a temple-centered priestly world; it is evidence for the authority and institutional setting of sacred fire, not an Atar myth.
- **Kārnāmag ī Ardaxšīr ī Pābagān:** Pāpak sees Farnbāg, Gušnasp, and Burzēn-Mihr burning at Sāsān’s house, an omen of world sovereignty. **[PAHLAVI POLITICAL LEGEND]**
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn:** the three fires assist the future hero Pēšōtan against evil. **[LATE PAHLAVI APOCALYPTIC ELABORATION]**

### Achaemenid evidence

Darius’s Behistun inscription and the royal tomb inscriptions at Naqsh-e Rostam repeatedly invoke Ahura Mazda but never name Atar. Royal reliefs show kings before structures with flames, conventionally called fire altars. Yet the identification of every such structure as a Zoroastrian altar—and the flame as Atar—is methodologically unsafe. The Persepolis Fortification evidence displays a plural religious landscape, and “Mazdaism” may be the safest designation for royal devotion where specifically Zoroastrian doctrines cannot be demonstrated. [**ABSENCE OF DIRECT ATTESTATION**; [Achaemenid “fire altars” study](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc72.pdf); [Fire Altars](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/fire-altars/)]

### Classical witnesses

- **Herodotus, Histories 1.131–32**, c. 440 BCE, says Persians sacrifice to sky, sun, moon, earth, fire, water, and winds, initially claiming that they erect no images, temples, or altars. He also says their animal sacrifice does not itself kindle a fire. This is external ethnography and cannot simply be treated as a description of later Zoroastrian temple practice. **[CLASSICAL WITNESS]**
- **Strabo, Geography 15.3.14–15**, late first century BCE/early first century CE, describes Cappadocian Persian sanctuaries with enclosed places and altars where Magi maintained an undying flame and recited ritual texts for about an hour. This is strong evidence for a western-diaspora temple fire cult by the Hellenistic/Roman period. **[CLASSICAL WITNESS; REGIONAL]**
- **Curtius Rufus, History of Alexander 3.3 and 3.8**, first century CE, reports a sacred fire borne before the Persian army. It supports the use of royal or victorious fire as a military palladium but does not name Atar.
- **Plutarch, On Isis and Osiris 46–47**, early second century CE, describes Oromazes, Areimanius, Mithras, light, darkness, and Persian ritual. His philosophical synthesis contains no independent Atar narrative. **[CLASSICAL INTERPRETATION]**
- **Pausanias 5.27.5–6**, second century CE, describes Persian Magi in Lydia chanting from a book before fire which appears mysteriously to ignite. This is valuable for diaspora ritual but is shaped by Greek observation.

### Manuscript transmission

Avestan texts were composed and transmitted orally for centuries. Their surviving codices are medieval and early modern:

- K7a, perhaps 1268/1278, is among the oldest extant Avestan manuscripts.
- Jm4, dated 1352, is the oldest Khordeh Avesta manuscript and preserves some Yashts.
- F1, copied at Navsari in 1591, is the principal oldest complete pure Yasht codex.
- E1 dates to 1601.
- Manuscript stemmata suggest that surviving branches often descend from exemplars no earlier than about 1000 CE.

Consequently, the linguistic age of a passage and the date of its surviving physical witness must always be distinguished. [**TEXTUAL-HISTORICAL FACT**; [Avesta manuscript history](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/); [Yasht transmission](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/); [Codices Hafnienses](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/codices-hafnienses/)]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

Atar’s securely documented domains are:

- physical fire, heat, and light;
- sacrificial communication and reception of offerings;
- truth-revealing ordeal;
- moral judgment;
- purification, when fire itself remains unpolluted;
- household welfare, fertility, prosperity, and continuity;
- royal and communal protection through consecrated fires;
- the final purification of creation.

Fire is not primarily a destructive war-god in the texts. Its dangerous power is conditional: it harms deceit and pollution but benefits those aligned with *aša*, right order.

### Powers and limitations

Atar can distinguish truth from falsehood, transmit offerings, bless a house, frighten Aži Dahāka, and participate in final purification. It can also be polluted, extinguished, deprived of proper fuel, or ritually mishandled. In Yasht 19 it retreats when threatened, showing that it is neither omnipotent nor narratively invulnerable. Pahlavi myth explains smoke as an injury imposed upon originally pure fire by Ahriman. **[YOUNGER AVESTAN; PAHLAVI ELABORATION]**

### The five fires

The canonical classification is not simply five fuel types:

1. **Bərəzi-savah** — later associated with the victorious/high temple fire.
2. **Vohu-fryāna** — animal and human vital heat.
3. **Urvāzišta** — energy or fire in plants.
4. **Vāzišta** — lightning or atmospheric fire.
5. **Spə̄ništa** — “holiest” fire, interpreted as cultic fire or fire before Ohrmazd.

The ordering and exact identifications differ between Yasna 17.11 and later commentary. The scheme presents fire as a cosmic energy distributed across divine, atmospheric, vegetable, animal, and ritual existence. [**YOUNGER AVESTAN; PAHLAVI SYSTEMATIZATION**; [Bundahišn, “Nature of Fire”](https://www.wisdomlib.org/zoroastrianism/book/the-bundahishn/d/doc4465.html)]

### Attributes and substances

- **Fuel:** clean, dry wood, traditionally fragrant where available.
- **Offerings:** wood, incense, and in Avestan liturgy milk and related sacrificial materials.
- **Implements:** metal fire-vases, tongs, ladles, ash implements, mouth-veils used by priests to prevent breath or saliva reaching the fire.
- **Associated element:** clean water, frequently paired with fire in the liturgy without being mixed into it.
- **Calendar:** the ninth day (*Ādur rōz*) and ninth month (*Ādur māh*).
- **Numbers:** five cosmic fires; three great fires; three institutional temple grades; sixteen source-fires in later Atash Behram consecration. These belong to different classifications and should not be combined.

No securely ancient source gives Atar a fixed sacred color, numerical emblem beyond these ritual systems, or a unique animal. The proposed equine implication in Ādur Gušnasp belongs to that particular named fire, not necessarily to Atar universally.

### Iconography

No securely identified ancient anthropomorphic image of Atar survives. Iranian art normally represents sacred fire as flame rising from an altar or fire-holder:

- an eighth–seventh-century BCE installation at Tepe Nush-e Jan has been called an altar, but its precise religion and function remain debated;
- Achaemenid tomb façades show the king before a stepped fire structure;
- coins of Persis, Parthian-period material, and especially Sasanian coins display fire altars, sometimes flanked by attendants;
- Sasanian monumental and seal evidence associates fire with kingship, dynastic legitimacy, and institutions;
- the great stone altar base and temple layout at Takht-e Soleyman belong to the cult of Ādur Gušnasp.

A flame in Iranian art is therefore evidence for ritual or royal fire, not automatically a portrait of Atar. The Kaʿba-ye Zardošt and similar tower structures cannot securely be identified as fire temples. [**ARCHAEOLOGICAL CAUTION**; [Fire Altars](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/fire-altars/); [Āyadana](https://doi.org/10.1163/2330-4804_EIRO_COM_6133)]

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Atar’s sole stable divine parent is **Ahura Mazda**, who is repeatedly called his father in the Younger Avesta. No mother is named. There is no securely attested consort, offspring, sibling group, mount, or independent court.

The Gathas do not tell a birth myth. “Son of Ahura Mazda” is therefore a Younger Avestan theological relationship, not evidence for physical generation. In later creation doctrine, Ohrmazd creates fire and entrusts it to Ašawahišt, but this administrative or cosmological association does not replace the father-son formula.

### Position in the divine hierarchy

Atar is a yazata rather than an Amesha Spenta. His guardian or closest higher-order associate is **Asha Vahishta/Ašawahišt**, the Amesha Spenta of Best Truth, whose material creation is fire in later theology. Atar also belongs to the group of calendar divinities and receives direct liturgical worship.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The contest with Aži Dahāka for the Glory

**Earliest source:** Yasht 19.46–51.

The unseizable *xᵛarənah* becomes the object of a cosmic contest. The good and evil spirits marshal opposed companies and cast swift darts. Atar approaches first, intending to possess the Glory. Aži Dahāka comes behind and threatens that, should Atar take it, he will extinguish fire so completely that it will never again protect Mazda’s world. Atar recoils.

The dragon then advances to take the Glory himself. Atar follows and reverses the threat: if Aži takes it, Atar will enter and blaze within his body, preventing him from destroying the good creation. Aži also recoils. The Glory escapes to Lake Vourukaša and is taken by Apąm Napāt.

The narrative establishes Atar as protector of the good world and existential enemy of the dragon, but not as its killer. Later summaries that say Atar “defeated” or “slew” Aži Dahāka overstate the text. Thraētaona/Fereydun is the dragon’s captor in other traditions; the final destruction of Dahāg belongs to later eschatology. **[YOUNGER AVESTAN]**

### Fire as revealer and judge

The Gathas repeatedly envisage a future or present testing through Mazda’s fire. This probably draws upon real Iranian ordeals by fire or molten substances, but the passages operate poetically and eschatologically as well as juridically. Later tradition remembered numerous kinds of ordeal: passing through fire, having molten metal poured upon the body, or ingesting a heated substance. The righteous are vindicated; deceit is exposed.

The historical existence of Iranian ordeals is plausible and supported by legal and narrative tradition, but any reconstruction of a single “original Atar ordeal” is modern synthesis. [**EARLY CORE; LATER LEGAL ELABORATION**; [Ātaš](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/atas-fire/)]

### Fire’s complaint to Ohrmazd

The Dēnkard’s summary of a lost Avestan section says Fire complains to Ohrmazd that material existence exposes it to contamination and prevents it from remaining undisturbed. Ohrmazd explains or justifies its worldly role, and care for fire is enjoined.

Because the Avestan original is lost, the episode is known only through a ninth–tenth-century Middle Persian epitome. It may preserve Sasanian exegesis, older nask material, or both; its original wording and date cannot be recovered. **[PAHLAVI WITNESS TO LOST AVESTAN TRADITION]**

### The five cosmic fires

Pahlavi cosmology develops the five-fire list into a creation myth. Fire exists before or throughout material differentiation; its forms animate bodies and plants, inhabit lightning and ritual flame, and stand before Ohrmazd. Ahriman’s invasion introduces smoke into fire.

This scheme sacralizes natural energy while explaining the empirical impurity of smoke as an effect of cosmic assault. It is a learned cosmology, not a narrative found complete in the Gathas. **[SASANIAN / PAHLAVI SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### The three great fires crossing the sea

The Bundahišn says the three great fires—Farnbāg, Gušnasp, and Burzēn-Mihr—were borne on the back of the primordial bull Srīsōg during an early migration or sea-crossing. When the original fire was blown away or fell into the sea, the three blazed “like three Glories,” providing light until land was reached.

The story retrojects historically founded temple fires into primordial time. Its value is theological and institutional, not chronological evidence that the temples existed at creation. **[PAHLAVI FOUNDATION LEGEND]**

### Individual legends of the three fires

- **Ādur Farnbāg:** associated with Jam/Yima and his royal Glory; later texts say it rescued or preserved Yima’s *xwarrah* from Dahāg. It was moved through legendary eastern locations before later association with Pars.
- **Ādur Gušnasp:** linked with kings and warriors; royal pilgrims sought victory or gave spoils there.
- **Ādur Burzēn-Mihr:** associated with Vištāsp’s conversion and installed on Mount Raēvant; later assigned to cultivators.

The mapping Farnbāg–priests, Gušnasp–warriors, Burzēn-Mihr–farmers mirrors the Iranian social estates. It is clearest in Sasanian/Pahlavi tradition and should not be projected unchanged into the early Avesta.

### Final river of molten metal

At the Renovation, Fire and Airyaman melt the metals in mountains and hills. All humanity passes through the river. To the righteous it feels like warm milk; wickedness is burned away, and the creation becomes pure.

The Gathas contain fiery judgment, but the universal river, its topography, and its experience as warm milk belong to developed Pahlavi eschatology. It is therefore a canonical elaboration of an early motif, not a verbatim doctrine recoverable from Zarathustra’s poems. [**EARLY SEED; PAHLAVI ELABORATION**; [Airyaman](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/airyaman-an-ancient-iranian-divinity/)]

### Hushang discovers fire

Ferdowsi’s *Shahnameh*, completed c. 1010 CE, tells how King Hushang throws a stone at a black serpent. It misses and strikes another stone, producing a spark. Hushang praises the Creator, calls the flame divine light worthy of reverence, and institutes the festival of Sadeh.

This is the best-known Persian discovery-of-fire myth, but it is not an Avestan story of Atar’s birth. It belongs to medieval epic tradition and may preserve Iranian folklore; its precise antiquity cannot be established. The poem does not say that Hushang created the divine being Atar. [**MEDIEVAL EPIC / FOLKLORE**; [Shahnameh bilingual text](https://literatureofiranian.com/authors/ferdousi/shahname/en/hushang/ferdousi-shahname-hushang-sh2)]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### From hearth fire to temple fire

The oldest recoverable practice centers on domestic and sacrificial fire. The Yasna Haptanghaiti and Gathas assume worship conducted in fire’s presence but do not describe monumental temples. No Avestan word securely denotes a fire-temple institution. Herodotus’s statement that Persians lacked temples accords imperfectly with later evidence but warns against projecting Sasanian buildings into Zarathustra’s age.

The permanent temple cult probably developed during the later Achaemenid or early post-Achaemenid period. Its date remains debated. By Strabo’s time, perpetual fires in western Iranian diaspora sanctuaries are unmistakable. **[MODERN HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Grades of sacred fire

Later Zoroastrian practice recognizes:

1. **Atash Behram** — highest grade, “victorious fire.”
2. **Atash Adaran** — middle grade, “fire of fires.”
3. **Atash Dadgah** — ordinary temple or domestic liturgical fire.

The system is securely documented in post-Sasanian sources and living Parsi practice. Whether precisely the same three grades existed throughout Sasanian Iran is less certain.

An Atash Behram is assembled from sixteen categories of fire—traditionally including fires from varied trades and social settings, lightning, and fire produced from flint. Each component undergoes repeated purification and recitation before the purified fires are united and enthroned. Detailed counts differ in manuals and regional practice. The process can take more than a year. It ritually gathers society and nature into a single purified fire; it is not a survival of human sacrifice. [**LATE RITUAL SYSTEM; LIVING PRACTICE**; [J. J. Modi, consecration rites](https://www.avesta.org/ritual/rcc3.htm)]

### Care of fire

Priests tend the fire at the five watches of the day. Clean fuel and incense are offered; the fire is protected from breath, saliva, corpses, refuse, and unclean materials. The priest covers the mouth, uses metal implements, and approaches after purification. Ash may be applied devotionally, but fire itself is not handled casually.

The *boi* ceremony renews or serves the fire and may include the Atash Niyayesh and bell-ringing. Exact practice differs between Iranian and Indian communities.

### Atash Niyayesh in practice

The prayer is recited before household or temple fire. Traditional Parsi directions prescribe one recitation for a Dadgah, three for an Adaran, and varying larger numbers at an Atash Behram according to the daily watch. A person unable to recite it may substitute repeated Ahunwar prayers according to later prescription. These counts are late ritual tradition, not stated in Yasna 62. [**LIVING PARSI PRACTICE**]

### The three great fires

#### Ādur Farnbāg

“Glory-given fire,” eventually the fire of the priestly estate and first in Pahlavi lists. Traditions locate it first in Khwarazm or Kabulistan and later in Pars. Its late Sasanian center is commonly sought near Kārīān in Fars, but no excavation has established an identification comparable to Gušnasp. A fire preserved at Sharifabad was later claimed to continue or incorporate Farnbāg; continuity cannot be archaeologically proved. **[PAHLAVI TRADITION; LOCATION DISPUTED]**  
[Ādur Farnbāg](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/adur-farnbag-an-atas-bahram-see-atas-that-is-a-zoroastrian-sacred-fire-of-the-highest-grade-held-to-be-one-of-the/)

#### Ādur Gušnasp

“Fire of the Stallion,” assigned to the warrior estate and Sasanian kingship. Its securely identified later sanctuary is Takht-e Soleyman/Šiz in West Azerbaijan. Archaeology revealed a fortified royal-religious complex with lake, palace, domed sanctuary, subsidiary chambers, and fire-altar base. Bullae naming the high priest or “house of the fire of Gušnasp” reinforce the identification.

The complex reached its monumental height under the Sasanians, especially in the sixth century. Byzantine emperor Heraclius’s invasion in 624 CE is associated with the sanctuary’s sack. The site later received Ilkhanid construction. It is the only one of the three great fires whose temple can be identified through converging archaeological, sigillographic, and literary evidence. [**SECURE SASANIAN CULT CENTER**; [UNESCO Takht-e Soleyman](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1077); [Ātaškada](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ataskada-new-persian-house-of-fire-mid/)]

#### Ādur Burzēn-Mihr

“Exalted is Mihr,” probably originally a founder’s personal name. It belonged to Parthia, later to the agricultural estate, and was located by Pahlavi tradition on Mount Raēvant in northeastern Iran. Several sites near Nishapur and Sabzevar have been proposed; none is conclusively identified. Its foundation cannot securely precede the emergence of temple-fire institutions, despite primordial legends. [**PAHLAVI TRADITION; ARCHAEOLOGICALLY UNLOCATED**; [Ādur Burzēn-Mihr](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/adur-burzen-mihr-an-atas-bahram-see-atas-i/)]

### Priesthood and economics

Priestly terms include Avestan *zaotar*, *āθravan*, and *aēθrapaiti*; Middle Persian *hērbed/ērbed* and *mōbed*. “Āθravan means fire-priest” is at best a traditional folk-etymological rendering.

Fire foundations possessed land, endowments, offerings, and hereditary patronage rights. The Sasanian law book *Mādayān ī Hazār Dādestān* records disputes over temple incomes and the authority to appoint priests. Thus a sacred fire could be a legal corporation and economic center as well as a ritual presence. **[SASANIAN INSTITUTIONAL EVIDENCE]**

Kartir’s third-century inscriptions claim the establishment or promotion of numerous fires and priests under Sasanian rulers. His program also elevated Mazdayasnian institutions and attacked rival religions. Fire cult, imperial policy, property, and confessional coercion were therefore intertwined. [**CONTEMPORARY SASANIAN EPIGRAPHY**; [Kartir](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kartir/)]

### Offerings, animal sacrifice, and alleged abuses

Avestan worship included animal-derived offerings and inherited sacrificial forms. Greek observers also describe Persian animal sacrifice conducted with Magian participation. Later reform and modern practice tend to emphasize offerings of wood, incense, fruits, flowers, milk, and ritual foods.

There is **no reliable evidence that human sacrifice formed part of Atar’s cult**, that humans were burned as offerings to temple fire, or that “temple prostitution” belonged to fire temples. Such claims should be classified as unsupported unless tied to a specific source and context. Fiery ordeal, execution by rulers, cremation by neighboring cultures, and human sacrifice are distinct practices and must not be conflated.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Indo-Iranian background and the Old Avesta

Fire occupied a central place in Indo-Iranian sacrifice. Avestan *ātar* and Vedic Agni share functions as sacrificial fire, divine presence, and conveyor of offerings, but they do not share a name. Treating Atar as merely “the Persian Agni” is comparative shorthand, not an ancient identification. [**MODERN COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION**]

Zarathustra’s Gathas redirected fire toward the ethical opposition of truth and deceit. Whether this was innovation or development of existing ordeal theology cannot be established.

### Younger Avestan formation

In the Younger Avesta, Atar becomes explicitly personified, called Ahura Mazda’s son, addressed for domestic blessings, and inserted into mythic combat. Fire is protected by increasingly detailed purity law. The five-fire classification integrates ritual fire with cosmic biology and atmosphere.

### Achaemenid era, c. 550–330 BCE

Achaemenid kings invoked Ahura Mazda and represented royal ritual before flames, but did not name Atar in their inscriptions. The exact relationship of imperial Mazdaism to the surviving Avesta remains disputed.

A permanent temple-fire cult may have arisen in the fifth or fourth century BCE. The suggestion is influential, especially in Boyce’s reconstruction, but it is not demonstrated by a dated foundation inscription. Herodotus’s contrary-looking account may reflect period, region, his Greek definition of “temple,” or incomplete information.

### Hellenistic and Parthian eras, 330 BCE–224 CE

Alexander’s conquest disrupted royal and religious institutions, although later Zoroastrian tradition exaggerated this into a complete burning of a written Avesta; the early transmission was substantially oral. Persian priestly communities persisted in Anatolia, Armenia, Mesopotamia, and Iran. Strabo’s fire sanctuaries provide direct external testimony.

The three great fires were probably established no later than the Parthian period, since Sasanian tradition already regarded them as immemorial. Burzēn-Mihr’s Parthian linguistic form and location point particularly to northeastern origins.

### Sasanian era, 224–651 CE

This is the institutional peak:

- sacred fires were tied to kings, dynasties, provinces, estates, and social classes;
- royal coinage made the altar-and-flame image ubiquitous;
- Kartir expanded fires and priestly organization;
- an Avestan canon and Pahlavi commentary were organized under royal-priestly sponsorship;
- the great fire Gušnasp became a royal pilgrimage and victory sanctuary;
- temples could replace image-shrines during Zoroastrian iconoclastic programs.

The substitution of fire for cult statues is documented as a policy in some settings, including the Anāhitā sanctuary at Istakhr. It illustrates intra-Iranian religious transformation, not simply conflict between Zoroastrians and outsiders.

### Arab conquest and Islamization

The Sasanian state fell in the seventh century. The change was not a single empire-wide closure of every fire temple. Some communities obtained agreements protecting sanctuaries; other fires were extinguished, temples appropriated, destroyed, abandoned, or converted. Over the following six centuries, conversion, taxation, loss of patronage, migration, local coercion, and demographic contraction ended most Iranian temple fires.

Pahlavi religious writing flourished partly as preservation in this post-imperial setting. Claims that Arab armies burned a complete written Avesta everywhere are later traditional history and cannot be confirmed as a single documented event. [**HISTORICAL PROCESS; LATER LOSS TRADITIONS PARTLY UNVERIFIABLE**]

### Parsis in India

Zoroastrian migrants established communities in Gujarat. The *Qeṣṣa-ye Sanjān*, composed in 1599, tells the migration and establishment of the “Iran Shah” fire, but its narrative concerns events many centuries earlier and cannot be read as contemporary documentation.

The Udvada Iranshah became the most prestigious Parsi Atash Behram. Prosperity from the seventeenth century onward supported new Atash Behrams, Adarans, and Dadgahs in Gujarat and Bombay. Ritual manuals provide the fullest surviving accounts of fire consecration.

### Safavid and Qajar Iran

Safavid institutionalization of Twelver Shiʿism brought forced conversions, destruction of places of worship, executions, and displacement, especially under Soltan Husayn (r. 1694–1722). Zoroastrian survival concentrated around Yazd and Kerman. Sacred fires were kept in inconspicuous mud-brick buildings.

Parsi philanthropy and political advocacy in the nineteenth century helped Iranian Zoroastrians rebuild institutions and reduce disabilities. New temples increasingly adopted architectural forms recognizable today. [**DOCUMENTED EARLY-MODERN AND MODERN HISTORY**; [Iranica historical review](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroastrianism-02-arab-conquest-to-modern/)]

### Present

Consecrated fires continue in Iran and India, with additional temples and *dar-e mehr* in the diaspora. Not every diaspora center contains a fully consecrated Atash Behram; many house Dadgah fires or operate as community prayer halls. Iranian and Parsi ritual norms differ, including temple-access rules and the continued practical importance of the three-grade system.

Atar therefore has continuous ritual presence, even though no unbroken historical chain can be proved for most individual flames claimed to descend from antiquity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### “Fire-worshipper”

Greek observers already described Persians as reverencing fire, but the polemical label developed most sharply in Islamic and European discourse. New Persian *ātaš-parast*, “fire-worshipper,” and *gabr* became conventional designations, often pejorative. Medieval Persian poetry could use fire-worship as an emblem of error, passion, exotic religion, or steadfastness.

Zoroastrian responses reject the implication that fire is a material idol independent of Ahura Mazda. Yet an equally simplistic modern claim that Zoroastrians “do not worship fire at all” can obscure the primary texts, which explicitly call Fire worthy of *yasna* and direct prayer to it. The historically precise formulation is:

- Zoroastrian liturgy **does venerate and address sacred fire**;
- fire is understood within Mazda’s creation and as his son, energy, sign, or presence;
- it is not normally treated as an autonomous supreme creator;
- English “worship” carries theological implications that do not map neatly onto Avestan *yasna*.

Thus “fire-worshipper” is misleading when it implies idolatry or an exclusive fire god, but the importance of actual devotional address should not be erased. The modern apologetic distinction between “worship” and “reverence” is partly a response to centuries of polemic. [**HISTORICAL TERMINOLOGY DISPUTE**; [Gabr](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gabr/); [British Academy overview](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/publishing/review/28/who-are-zoroastrians/)]

### Scholarship and Western esotericism

Anquetil-Duperron’s eighteenth-century publication of the Avesta introduced European readers to Atar through textual translation. Nineteenth-century comparative religion aligned Atar with Agni and Indo-European fire cult; some comparisons remain useful, but racialized “Aryan religion” constructions are obsolete.

Theosophical and occult writings adopted “sacred fire,” “Zoroastrian flame,” and “Atar” as metaphors for spiritual energy. These systems usually extract fire from Avestan liturgy and combine it with Hindu, Hermetic, or universalist schemes. There is no ancient Zoroastrian basis for modern claims that Atar is a chakra energy, elemental salamander, tarot intelligence, or spirit summoned by a grimoire. Such claims are **[MODERN INVENTION or MODERN SYNCRETISM]**.

No major Renaissance grimoire, Golden Dawn grade paper, or classical Hermetic text gives Atar an established independent cult comparable to later occult receptions of Mithras or “Ahriman.” Generic occult “Persian fire” should not be back-projected onto the yazata.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist use

Small modern groups identify as Mazdayasni, Zoroastrian-inspired, Iranian pagan, or reconstructionist and may address Atar directly. Publicly verifiable numbers are unavailable, and these communities vary between adherence to living Zoroastrian tradition and eclectic polytheism. When Atar is given a new sigil, fixed gendered portrait, elemental correspondence table, planetary ruler, or ritual derived from Wicca or ceremonial magic, those features are modern unless an ancient source is cited.

Living Zoroastrian devotion to fire is not itself a “revival”; it is continuous, although particular organizations and newly founded temples are modern.

### National and cultural reception

Fire, the faravahar, Persepolis, and Zoroastrian festivals became symbols of Iranian national antiquity under both secular and religious political conditions. Sadeh survives as a Zoroastrian religious observance and a wider Iranian cultural festival. In 2023 UNESCO inscribed Sadeh as intangible heritage for Iran and Tajikistan.

Nationalist rhetoric sometimes turns Atar into an emblem of an ethnically pure pre-Islamic Iran. That racial or exclusionary construction is modern and is contradicted by the historically diverse, regional, and changing religious record.

### Popular culture

“Atar,” “Atash,” and the three great fires appear in historical novels, fantasy games, online mythology catalogues, and role-playing settings. Frequent modern distortions include:

- portraying Atar as a humanoid warrior with a fixed costume;
- calling him “god of all fire” in a Greek-style pantheon;
- claiming he killed Aži Dahāka;
- treating Atash Behram as a temple dedicated to the god Bahrām;
- identifying every Iranian flame altar as Atar’s image;
- asserting a continuous occult cult outside Zoroastrianism.

These are **[MODERN RETELLING]** or **[MODERN INVENTION]**, not primary tradition.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Nature of the deity

One school, associated especially with historical syntheses by Mary Boyce, speaks readily of Atar as the ancient Iranian fire divinity and reconstructs continuity from Indo-Iranian hearth cult to Zoroastrian temple fire. More text-critical approaches emphasize that Gathic *ātar* may be a divine instrument, substance, or faculty rather than a sharply individualized god. The Younger Avesta unquestionably personifies him.

### Dating and geography

The Gathas’ precise date remains contested. Linguistic comparison places them well before the classical Old Persian inscriptions, but estimates from c. 1500 to c. 600 BCE have circulated. The eastern Iranian geography of the Avesta is widely accepted; direct identification with a named archaeological culture is not.

Younger Avestan texts likewise accumulated over centuries. Yasht 19 may preserve old mythic diction, but its surviving form is composite. Medieval manuscript dates do not date composition, while linguistic antiquity does not prove that every stanza belongs to a single early period.

### Origin of temple fire

Boyce argued for the rise of permanent temple fires in the later fifth or fourth century BCE, possibly as a response to image-temples. Other scholars caution that archaeological “fire altars” are difficult to classify and that the Achaemenid evidence does not document later temple institutions directly. The oldest undisputed Zoroastrian fire-temple remains are usually placed in the Parthian or Sasanian horizons, with Kuh-e Khwaja important in the debate.

### Identification of monuments

“Fire temple” and *čahārṭāq* were once applied too freely to Iranian ruins. A four-arched domed structure may have served religious, palatial, commemorative, or other functions. Secure identification requires context: altar remains, ash or fuel evidence, ritual installations, inscriptions, seals, or converging literary testimony.

Takht-e Soleyman meets that standard. Proposed sites for Farnbāg and Burzēn-Mihr do not yet do so.

### “Monotheism,” “dualism,” and worship

Atar complicates rigid descriptions of Zoroastrianism as simply monotheistic or polytheistic. Ahura Mazda is supreme creator and lord; numerous yazatas, including Atar, receive *yasna*. Angra Mainyu is not worshipped in ordinary orthodox cult. Scholarly labels depend on whether “god” means independent creator, recipient of sacrifice, personified power, or member of a pantheon.

The primary terminology—Ahura, Amesha Spenta, yazata, daēva—is more precise than forcing the tradition into later Western categories.

### Atar and Agni

Both emerge from related Indo-Iranian ritual worlds, but:

- their names are not cognate;
- Agni possesses a far larger Vedic narrative corpus;
- Atar’s distinctive Zoroastrian role is tied to *aša*, Mazda’s judgment, anti-pollution law, and final renovation;
- no historical text says Atar “is” Agni.

Their equation is comparative, not an ancient syncretism.

### Manichaean and Mithraic material

Iranian Manichaean texts use inherited Middle Iranian religious vocabulary, and Manichaean cosmology employs light and fire. This does not demonstrate worship of Atar as a Manichaean god. Any proposed survival must be established passage by passage.

Roman Mithraism uses fire, torches, and solar imagery, while Iranian Mihr is entangled with one great fire’s name, Burzēn-Mihr. No Mithraic inscription securely identifies Atar with Cautes, Cautopates, Vulcan, or another Roman figure. Such equations are speculative. **Absence of evidence is the present finding.**

### Human sacrifice and sexual cult claims

No primary source securely documents human sacrifice to Atar or ritual prostitution in a fire temple. Older comparativist literature sometimes assembled sensational customs from unrelated Iranian, Mesopotamian, Armenian, and classical reports. Without a specific Iranian text, excavation, or inscription, these claims are rejected as unsubstantiated—not because they are morally inconvenient, but because the evidence is absent.

### Primary evidence lost and preserved

The surviving Avesta is a fraction of the Sasanian canon described in the Dēnkard. Much was lost through the collapse of institutions, shrinking priestly transmission, manuscript decay, and migration. A single dramatic destruction cannot account for all loss.

Important modern resources include Geldner’s edition, Darmesteter and Mills in *Sacred Books of the East*, Humbach and Insler for the Gathas, Kellens for textual analysis, Skjærvø’s grammatical and historical studies, the Avestan Digital Archive, and modern editions of Pahlavi texts. Older translations remain useful for access but must not be treated as the last word.
