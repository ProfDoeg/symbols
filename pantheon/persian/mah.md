# Mah: Research Dossier

**Class:** Yazata (Avestan *yazata*, “worthy of worship”), the masculine divine personification of the Moon in the Zoroastrian and older Iranian religious tradition. In Middle Persian theology he is a subordinate beneficent divinity (*yazd*), not an Aməša Spənta and not an independent creator-god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest externally datable evidence is Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131, written about 440 BCE, which says that Persians had “from the first” sacrificed to the Moon, although it neither records the name Māh nor proves that Herodotus’s Persians understood the Moon exactly as later Zoroastrian texts do. Achaemenid seals of approximately the fifth–fourth centuries BCE may depict an anthropomorphic lunar god, but individual identifications remain interpretive. The earliest indigenous textual characterization is the Younger Avestan *Māh Yašt* (Yašt 7), composed orally during the Old Iranian period—probably sometime in the first millennium BCE, without a securely recoverable year—and transmitted in manuscripts no earlier than the medieval period. The Old Persian word *māha-* “month” is securely inscribed in Achaemenid royal texts, but this is lexical evidence, not by itself an attestation of the god.

## Evidentiary labels used

- **Securely attested early material:** Old Iranian/Achaemenid evidence, or Younger Avestan material whose linguistic antiquity is secure even when its exact composition date is not.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** Material in the received Younger Avesta, Khordeh Avesta, and established Zoroastrian liturgy whose surviving form reflects transmission and redaction.
- **Learned systematization:** Middle Persian priestly cosmology, calendrics, and hierarchy, especially the *Bundahišn* and *Dēnkard*.
- **Regional variant:** Evidence peculiar to western Iran, Bactria, Anatolia, or another particular region.
- **Syncretistic identification:** A historical equation or iconographic borrowing involving another religion.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** Post-Sasanian Zoroastrian or Persian development.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A conclusion inferred by modern philology, archaeology, or comparative religion.
- **Modern invention:** A claim presented as ancient for which the ancient and medieval record supplies no adequate evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**Securely attested early material.** Māh is at once the visible Moon and a male divine recipient of sacrifice. Yašt 7 does not maintain a modern distinction between “physical object” and “person”: the Moon is seen, waxes, wanes, emits beneficial light, receives offerings, and is addressed as an efficacious god. He belongs to the class of natural-phenomenon yazatas alongside the Sun (*Huuar/Xwaršēd*), wind (*Vāyu*), and the star Tištrya. The hymn calls him *baγa*, “god” or “distributor,” a designation otherwise applied in the Avesta especially to Ahura Mazdā and Mithra. See Malandra’s edition and analysis of the [Māh Yašt](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mah-yast/).

Māh is grammatically masculine in Avestan. English translations that alternate between “he,” “she,” and “it” ordinarily reflect English conventions for the Moon or older translators’ preferences, not three Iranian gender traditions.

### Names and scripts

- **Avestan:** *māh-* and oblique stem *måŋha-/maŋha-* (𐬨𐬂𐬢𐬵𐬀 in normalized Avestan-script presentation), “Moon; month.”
- **Old Persian:** *māha-* (𐎶𐎠𐏃), “month,” and by ordinary lexical extension “moon.” In the Achaemenid inscriptions it is used in dating formulas. This does not amount to a named invocation of Māh.
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** *māh*, written with the conventions of Pahlavi script, “Moon; month”; as a divinity, *Māh yazd*.
- **New Persian:** ماه, *māh*, “moon; month.” In Islamic Persian it normally denotes the astronomical Moon or month, not necessarily the Zoroastrian yazata.
- **Bactrian:** μαο or related spellings, *mao/māh*, “Moon; month”; on Kushan coins the name *Mao* is used for a lunar divinity.
- **Parsi liturgical usage:** *Mah*, *Mohor*, and *Mah Bokhtar* occur in English and Gujarati transliterations. *Bōxtār/Bokhtar* means “saving, delivering, redemptive” and appears in the Pāzand opening of the Māh Niyāyišn: *māh ī bōxtār*.

The word belongs to the Indo-Iranian lunar vocabulary represented by Sanskrit *mās-* “month” and ultimately to the Indo-European family that includes English *moon* and *month*. This etymology establishes a common word-history, not a common, unchanged deity. The Indian lunar gods Soma and Candra must therefore not be labeled “the same god” merely because Iranian and Indo-Aryan lunar vocabulary is cognate.

### Principal epithets

**Securely attested early material:**

- *måŋha-/maŋha- gaočiθra-* — traditionally “the Moon containing/bearing the seed of the Bull/cattle.”
- *aṣ̌avan-* — “righteous,” aligned with *aša*, truth and right order.
- *aṣ̌ahe ratū-* — “master/judge according to Aša.”
- *baγa-* — “god,” possibly with the older sense “distributor, allotter.”
- *raēvant-* — rich, opulent.
- *xᵛarənaŋᵛant-* — possessing glory or *xᵛarənah*.
- Epithets in Yašt 7.5 and Māh Niyāyišn 7 describe him as possessing water, warmth, knowledge or discernment, wealth, welfare, verdure, goodness, generosity, and healing power.

The [received Yašt 7 text](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt7sbe.htm) describes him as “water-giving,” “warmth-giving,” “wisdom-giving,” “wealth-giving,” “verdure-giving,” and “healing.” Some English compounds are paraphrases of Avestan possessive adjectives and should not be mistaken for separately documented cult titles.

### The dispute over *gaočiθra*

This is the dossier’s most important philological controversy.

- **Traditional interpretation / later canonical elaboration:** *gao-* “cow, cattle” + *čiθra-* “seed, origin, lineage,” hence “having/holding the seed of the Bull.” The Middle Persian creation myth understands it literally: after the primordial bovine dies, its reproductive seed is carried to the lunar station, purified by moonlight, and becomes the source of animal species.
- **Alternative modern scholarly reconstruction:** *čiθra-* can also concern appearance, visible form, or brilliance. Earlier scholars sometimes rendered the compound in terms such as “having the form of a cow” or “bull-shaped,” connecting the crescent to bovine horns.
- **Panaino’s historical proposal:** the fully developed “semen preserved on the Moon” story may be a Middle Iranian development subsequently used to interpret an older Avestan epithet.
- **Hintze’s counteranalysis:** Almut Hintze argues from the compound’s grammar, its twelve Avestan occurrences, and Indo-Iranian comparison that it originally means the Moon “from whom cattle originate,” not simply “cow-shaped.” On this reading, the Avestan phrase already encodes a relationship between the Moon and the origin of cattle, although the detailed narrative in the *Bundahišn* remains later. See Hintze, [“The Cow that Came from the Moon”](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/349087/the-cow-that-came-from-the-moon-the-avestan-expression-mah-gaocithra) and the [full paper](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/349103).

A further complication is Middle Persian *Gōzihr*, from the same compound. It became the name of the hostile lunar node or celestial dragon responsible for eclipses. That being is not Māh. The development is a lexical and astrological transformation, not proof that the beneficent Moon yazata became demonic. See [Gōzihr](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gozihr/).

### Later classifications

**Learned systematization.** In the later hierarchy of “co-workers” (*hamkārān*), Māh is attached to Vohu Manah/Bahman, the Aməša Spənta associated with good purpose and the protection of beneficial animals. Māh, Gōš or Geuš Urvan, and Rām form Bahman’s subordinate group. This arrangement is a priestly classification, not stated in the Gāthās.

**Modern theological language.** “Angel” is sometimes used for *yazata* in English and older Parsi writing. It is an interpretive analogy, not a literal translation. “Moon spirit” is serviceable but less exact than “Moon yazata.” “Goddess,” “monster,” “demon,” and “collective” are incorrect for Māh in the Zoroastrian sources.

### Identifications with foreign beings

- **Soma/Candra:** comparative, not textually equated. The common Indo-Iranian lunar and calendrical background makes comparison legitimate, but no surviving Iranian text declares Māh to be Soma or Candra.
- **Mesopotamian Sîn:** no secure Iranian equation is documented. Crescent and lunar-disc imagery entered Achaemenid visual culture through Assyrian and broader Near Eastern conventions, but borrowing an emblem does not prove theological identity.
- **Anatolian Mēn:** possible influence in both directions has been proposed where Iranian and Anatolian cults met. No surviving declaration equates Māh and Mēn.
- **Greek Selene/Artemis and Roman Luna/Diana:** modern equivalence tables commonly make these pairings, but no securely attested ancient *interpretatio graeca* text identifies Māh by name with Selene, Artemis, Luna, or Diana.
- **Kushan Mao:** this is the strongest regional continuation or adaptation. Kushan coins name a lunar god ΜΑΟ and depict him, often with a crescent. The Iranian name and setting support connection with Māh, but Kushan royal religion was syncretic and Mao’s image cannot be treated as a transparent illustration of Yašt 7.
- **Manichaean Moon:** Eastern Manichaeans used familiar Iranian divine names for Manichaean cosmic beings. In Manichaean doctrine the Moon is a light-ship and may be associated with Jesus the Splendour; this is a new Manichaean role, not continuation of Māh’s Zoroastrian cattle-seed myth. The renaming process is surveyed in [“The Manichaean Pantheon”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-parent/manicheism-ii-the-manichean-pantheon/).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Herodotus, about 440 BCE

**Securely dated external testimony.** *Histories* 1.131 states that Persians sacrificed to the whole heavens, Sun, Moon, Earth, fire, water, and winds. It is the earliest plainly datable written report of Persian Moon worship. Herodotus neither gives Māh’s Iranian name nor recounts Yašt 7’s doctrines. His evidence supports a cult of the Moon among fifth-century BCE Persians, not every later Zoroastrian attribute. The passage is available in [Greek-historical context and translation](https://lexundria.com/hdt/1.131/mcly) and critically discussed by [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/herodotus-iii/).

Herodotus’s adjoining assertion that Persians called Aphrodite Urania “Mitra” is notoriously confused and probably mixes Mithra, Anāhitā, and foreign cults. It should not be turned into an equation of Māh with Aphrodite.

#### Achaemenid inscriptions, 520s–fourth century BCE

**Secure linguistic evidence, not secure divine attestation.** Darius’s Behistun inscription and other royal texts use *māha-* in calendar formulas. They invoke Ahura Mazdā, and later royal inscriptions name Mithra and Anāhitā, but none securely invokes Māh as a named god. Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam therefore do not supply a “temple of Māh,” priesthood, or theological narrative.

#### Achaemenid imagery

**Modern scholarly reconstruction.** Some seals depict a crescent-bearing anthropomorphic figure interpreted as the Iranian Moon god. At the rock-cut tomb of Qizqapan, a figure above a fire-altar scene has likewise been identified as lunar. The disc or crescent above Achaemenid royal tomb façades is disputed: lunar, solar, royal, Mithraic, or generally astral readings have all been proposed. The absence of captions and corroborating texts prevents certainty. [Iranica’s Naqš-e Rostam study](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/naqs-e-rostam/) explicitly notes both the possible Māh imagery and the uncertainty surrounding the tomb symbol.

### The Avestan corpus

#### The Gāthās and Yasna 44

**Securely attested early material, but not a named Māh cult.** The Old Avestan Gāthās do not name Māh as an individual yazata. Yasna 44.3 asks Ahura Mazdā who established the Moon’s waxing and waning. Yašt 7.2 later reuses or echoes that question. This makes lunar order part of Mazda’s creation in the oldest stratum without supplying the later epithet *gaočiθra*.

#### Yasna invocations

**Later canonical material.** The Younger Avestan ritual enumerations invoke *maŋhahe gaočiθrahe*, the Moon associated with cattle’s origin or seed. Yasna 3.13 groups the stars, Tištrya, Māh, the radiant Sun, Ahura Mazdā, Mithra, and the righteous fravašis as recipients of worship. Yasna 16 likewise includes the Moon in the sacrificial catalogue. The Avestan text may be consulted in the [Yasna transcription](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/yasna_transcription.pdf) and the documented [TITUS Avestan corpus](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/iran/airan/avesta/yasna/yasnavar/yasna001.htm).

#### Māh Yašt, Yašt 7

**Securely ancient language; exact date unestablished; later received form.** Yašt 7 is one of the “minor” or predominantly hymnic Yašts. It consists of introductory ritual matter and seven numbered stanzas:

1. Homage to Ahura Mazdā, the Aməša Spəntas, and the Moon *gaočiθra*; reciprocal looking between worshipper and Moon.
2. Fifteen days of waxing and fifteen of waning; the question of who causes this order.
3. Worship of Māh and observation of his light; the Aməša Spəntas hold and distribute his glory over Mazda’s earth.
4. Moonlight and warmth cause golden or green plants to grow in spring; worship of the new moon, full moon, and *višaptaθa* phase.
5. Catalogue of Māh’s beneficial powers: water, warmth, knowledge, wealth, verdure, welfare, and healing.
6. The general *ahe raya* sacrifice formula, including Haoma, milk or meat, *barəsman*, sacred speech, deeds, libations, and correctly spoken words.
7. Blessing of Māh, the uniquely created Bull, and the Bull or cattle of many species; request for glory, bodily health, prosperity, and the blessed abode.

The hymn does not narrate the primordial bull’s death. It supplies the epithet and jointly venerates Māh, the uniquely created Bull, and the many-specied cattle; the full sequence involving Ahriman and transported semen appears in Middle Persian literature.

The language contains metrically irregular and formulaic material. Malandra finds parts modeled on the Sun Yašt, including the vegetation passage. The hymn cannot responsibly be assigned wholesale to Zarathustra or to one named Achaemenid king.

#### Māh Niyāyišn

**Later canonical elaboration with living liturgical use.** The third of the five Niyāyišns in the Khordeh Avesta is a litany to the Moon. It has twelve sections and incorporates virtually all of Yašt 7. Its opening and closing contain later Pāzand material. Sections 10–11, prayers for strength, victory, cattle, human prosperity, glory, healing, and water, are absent from some manuscripts and also occur in Wištāsp Yašt 6–7; they do not refer specifically to the Moon.

It is prescribed at three lunar stations: new moon (*antarə.māh*), full moon (*pərənō.māh*), and *višaptaθa*, conventionally a seventh-day or half-moon station. Whether the Avestan phase terms originally designated individual days or five-day periods remains disputed. Dēnkard 3.259 systematizes them as three “good” pentads alternating with three contrary pentads, a thirty-day ideal scheme that drifts against the astronomical lunation.

The litany’s key lines and English translation are preserved in the [Māh Niyāyišn text](https://www.avesta.org/ka/niyayesh.htm). Its manuscript structure and ritual prescription are analyzed in [“Khordeh Avesta”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khordeh-avesta-2/).

#### Sīrōza

**Later canonical calendrical material.** The two Sīrōza texts enumerate the divinities of the thirty named days. Māh holds the twelfth day. This places him within a recurring household and liturgical calendar, rather than indicating an annual state festival or a separate temple economy. The Geldner-based text is available in the [Sīrōza edition](https://www.avesta.org/ka/s_mithra.htm).

#### Frawardīn Yašt, Yašt 13

**Later canonical interaction.** Yašt 13.57–58 credits the fravašis with establishing or sustaining the courses of the stars, Moon, and Sun. This does not subordinate Māh as a servant in a narrative scene; it places lunar motion within the cooperative maintenance of creation.

#### Vendidad and other Avestan works

No independent Māh myth comparable to Yašt 7 occurs in the *Vendidad*. References to lunar time and calendrical periods should not automatically be counted as appearances of the personified yazata. The same rule applies to the many occurrences of ordinary “month.”

### Transmission

The Avesta was composed and transmitted orally over centuries and written in its specialized alphabet under late antique/Sasanian scholarly conditions. The oldest surviving Khordeh Avesta manuscript, Jm4, is dated 1352 CE; the principal complete pure-Yašt manuscript F1 is dated 1591. Thus the language of Yašt 7 is ancient while every surviving physical witness is medieval or early modern. The distinction is fundamental. See the [Yašts transmission survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/) and the manuscript repository of the [Avestan Digital Archive](https://ada.geschkult.fu-berlin.de/ada/).

### Middle Persian corpus

#### Bundahišn

**Learned systematization containing older inherited material.** The *Bundahišn* exists in Iranian and Indian recensions and grew through several redactions after the Arab conquest, with additions extending to the twelfth century. Its lunar material includes:

- Ohrmazd creates stars, then Moon, then Sun.
- The Moon occupies a celestial station below the Sun.
- When Ahriman’s assault kills the uniquely created bovine, the bovine’s generative seed is taken to the lunar station.
- Moonlight purifies it; from it arise a male and female bovine and subsequently 282 animal species.
- The Moon influences tides.
- Happiness descending toward the material world passes through a sequence including the Moon and Ardwīsūr.
- The narcissus is allotted to Māh in a learned correspondence between plants and divinities.

The [Indian Bundahišn, chapter 10](https://avesta.org/mp/bundahis.html) says that the seed was “delivered to the moon station,” purified by lunar light, and generated embodied life. The [Greater Bundahišn](https://www.avesta.org/mp/grb.pdf) explicitly glosses “Moon having the seed of the beneficent animal” as meaning that the archetype of beneficial animals is at the lunar station.

The bovine is killed by Ahriman, not sacrificed to Māh by worshippers. Claims that this is a Zoroastrian ritual of cosmogonic bull sacrifice conflate hostile killing with priestly sacrifice. Recent criticism correctly notes that neither Avestan nor Pahlavi texts present the death as a sacrifice offered by a god; see the study [“Cosmogonic sacrifice: a ghost Zoroastrian doctrine”](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/cosmogonic-sacrifice-a-ghost-zoroastrian-doctrine/).

#### Dēnkard

**Learned systematization.** Dēnkard 3.259 explains the six lunar pentads. Book 8’s description of the lost *Bagān Yašt Nask* shows that the day-named yazatas and their hymns formed part of Sasanian and post-Sasanian priestly organization. The *Dēnkard*’s final form is chiefly ninth–tenth century, although it preserves and summarizes earlier material. See [“Dēnkard”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/denkard/).

#### Mēnōg ī Xrad

**Late Sasanian or early post-Sasanian doctrinal material.** Chapter 53 commends prayer and repentance before the Sun, Moon, and fire. The work also maps heaven in levels extending from the star station to the Moon station and thence to the Sun. It offers cosmological placement, not a narrative biography of Māh. The probable late-Sasanian dating and textual history are summarized in [“Dādestān ī Mēnōg ī Xrad”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/dadestan-i-menog/); a complete older translation is available at [Avesta.org](https://avesta.org/mp/mx.html).

#### Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag

**Medieval canonical vision literature.** Wīrāz crosses the star, Moon, and Sun stations while ascending toward paradise. These are graded celestial realms assigned to souls according to merit. Māh does not speak or perform a discrete action in the surviving narrative. The “Moon station” is therefore part of Māh’s cosmological reception, not an independent divine interaction.

### Classical and Persian literary sources

- **Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 46–47, about 100 CE:** reports Greek versions of Iranian dualist cosmology, including Oromazes, Areimanios, stars, Sun, and celestial order. It does not securely name Māh or transmit Yašt 7. It is contextual evidence only. The relevant text appears in the [Loeb-based extract](https://www.philipharland.com/Courses/Readings/4819/P%20Plutarch%2C%20Isis%20and%20Osiris%20%28on%20Zoroaster%27s%20dualism%29.pdf).
- **Ferdowsi, *Šāhnāma*, completed about 1010 CE:** includes an eight-line account of the Moon’s creation and abundant lunar similes. In the proem the Moon is part of God’s created cosmos, not an independently acting Māh yazata. The poem preserves Iranian legendary material but is not a reliable witness for a distinct medieval cult of Māh. See the indexed [Šāhnāma text](https://shahnameh.net/) and the Persian [“On the Creation of the Moon”](https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/aghaz/sh6?allaudio=1).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Securely attested early material:**

- The visible Moon and its regular phases.
- Lunar light and warmth.
- Spring vegetation and verdure.
- Water and moisture.
- Wealth, welfare, healing, discernment, and prosperity.
- A foundational relationship with cattle or the origin of animal life.
- The distribution of *xᵛarənah*, glory or beneficent fortune.

**Learned systematization:**

- Purification and preservation of the primordial bovine seed.
- Location of the archetype or generative principle of beneficial animals.
- A station in the layered heavens.
- Tidal movement.
- Membership among the cattle-associated collaborators of Vohu Manah.
- The narcissus as Māh’s allotted flower.

“Fertility god” is a reasonable summary only if qualified: fertility is mediated through vegetation, water, cattle, and generation. No source gives Māh a spouse, erotic myth, or human reproductive cult.

### Powers and limitations

Māh illuminates, warms, nourishes vegetation, distributes glory, provides healing and prosperity, and purifies the bovine seed. He does not create the world independently. The oldest theological question attributes lunar order ultimately to Ahura Mazdā; the Moon’s movement is also sustained by the fravašis. No source makes Māh sovereign over fate, death, magic, madness, or dreams.

The waxing and waning are described as fifteen days each, an ideal thirty-day scheme. This is a ritual-cosmological reckoning rather than exact astronomical observation.

### Iconography

#### Achaemenid-period possibilities

Anthropomorphic lunar figures on seals and the Qizqapan tomb relief have been identified as Māh. The likely markers are the crescent, lunar disc, elevated position, and royal or funerary setting. Since the images lack labels, the identification remains a scholarly attribution.

The disc above Achaemenid royal tomb scenes at Naqš-e Rostam is especially disputed. It derives iconographically from Assyrian winged or crescent celestial signs, but scholars have called it lunar, solar, Mithraic, or a general sign of celestial kingship. It cannot be cited as an uncontested “portrait of Māh.”

#### Kushan Mao

**Regional and syncretistic evidence, first–third centuries CE.** Kushan coins bearing the Bactrian legend ΜΑΟ provide the clearest named anthropomorphic lunar iconography in the Iranian-language sphere. Mao may appear nimbate or crescented, dressed in Iranian fashion, and participating in the Kushan royal divine assembly. He reflects a Bactrian-Iranian lunar god interpreted through Hellenistic and Central Asian visual forms. These images are highly relevant to Māh’s history but are not illustrations made for the Avestan hymn.

#### Sasanian art and coinage

**Secure material evidence, theological meaning partly reconstructed.** Crescents, stars, and star-and-crescent combinations occur on Sasanian crowns and coins, especially from the later fifth century onward. They express celestial glory, legitimacy, and the king’s association with astral powers. Some coin designs combine the crescent with bovine imagery evocative of *gaočiθra*. The association between bull imagery and Māh also appears on Sasanian seals. See [Iranica’s study of the iconography of *xᵛarənah*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/farr-ii-iconography/) and the Metropolitan Museum study [*Sasanian Seals in the Moore Collection*](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Sasanian_Seals_in_the_Moore_Collection_The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_14_1979.pdf).

Not every Sasanian crescent is a cult image of Māh. It can be an astral emblem, crown element, mint device, or sign of royal fortune.

### Emblems and correspondences

- **Secure:** Moon, moonlight, phases, crescent; cattle and the primordial bovine; water, warmth, plants, glory.
- **Later systematization:** narcissus; twelfth calendrical day; Vohu Manah’s cattle-oriented divine group.
- **Not securely established:** a unique weapon, a canonical chariot, a fixed garment, a sacred number other than calendrical associations, a weekly Monday observance, a consort, a personal temple statue type, silver as an exclusively sacred metal, or a single canonical color.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy and hierarchy

No Avestan or Middle Persian text gives Māh biological parents, a birth from another god, siblings, consort, or children. Ahura Mazdā is creator of the beneficent cosmos, including the luminaries, but this is creation, not genealogical fatherhood in a mythic family tree.

Māh’s “offspring” are sometimes loosely said to be cattle or animals. The texts are more precise: the primordial bovine’s seed is purified at the lunar station and becomes the source of animal species. The seed belongs to the bovine; Māh preserves or purifies it.

In later priestly hierarchy Māh is a yazad and *hamkār* of Vohu Manah. He is below the Aməša Spəntas in systematic ranking but remains a proper recipient of worship.

### Every documented divine interaction

The list distinguishes actual relations from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Ahura Mazdā — creation and governance.** Ahura Mazdā establishes the Moon and its waxing and waning; Yašt 7 opens by honoring Mazda before Māh. Source: Yasna 44.3; Yašt 7.1–2, Old and Younger Avestan strata, first millennium BCE. **Tier:** securely attested early material.
- **The Aməša Spəntas — bearing and distributing Māh’s glory.** They stand holding lunar *xᵛarənah* and distribute it over Mazda-created earth. Source: Yašt 7.3; Māh Niyāyišn 5. **Tier:** later canonical elaboration based on Old Iranian material.
- **The uniquely created Bull, Gāw ī Ēwdād/Gāw Aēvōdāta — preservation of seed.** Māh receives or houses the primordial bovine’s generative principle after its death; lunar light purifies it. Source: Yašt 7’s epithet and joint invocation; explicit story in *Bundahišn* 10. **Tier:** Avestan relationship with learned Middle Persian narrative elaboration.
- **The Bull/cattle of many species — source or protection.** Māh is worshipped jointly with the uniquely created Bull and the many-specied cattle. Source: Yašt 7.7; Māh Niyāyišn 9. **Tier:** securely attested Avestan formula/later canonical use.
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — indirect cosmic antagonism.** Ahriman kills the primordial Bull; the transfer of its seed to the Moon defeats the intended destruction by enabling animal life to proliferate. Māh and Ahriman do not meet or fight in a narrated scene. Source: *Bundahišn* 3 and 10, post-Sasanian compilation of older cosmology. **Tier:** learned systematization.
- **Vohu Manah/Bahman — hierarchical collaboration.** Māh is classed as one of Vohu Manah’s *hamkārān*, reflecting their common concern with beneficial animals. Source: later calendrical and Middle Persian theological organization. **Tier:** learned systematization.
- **Fravašis — regulation of Māh’s course.** The fravašis show or sustain the paths of stars, Moon, and Sun until renovation. Source: Yašt 13.57–58. **Tier:** later canonical Avestan material.
- **Ardwī Sūr Anāhitā/Ardwīsūr — transmission of beneficence.** In the Greater *Bundahišn*, cosmic happiness passes from Suk to the Moon, from the Moon to Ardwīsūr, and onward toward the material world. Source: Greater *Bundahišn*. **Tier:** learned systematization.
- **Suk — transmission of beneficence.** Suk hands cosmic happiness to the Moon in the same chain. Suk’s exact nature is obscure. Source: Greater *Bundahišn*. **Tier:** learned systematization; interpretation uncertain.
- **Sun/Xwaršēd — ordered celestial proximity and paired liturgical context.** Moon and Sun occupy successive celestial stations and are repeatedly invoked together in lists. There is no surviving conversation, conflict, marriage, or journey shared by them. Sources: Yasna 3.13; *Bundahišn* cosmology; *Mēnōg ī Xrad*. **Tier:** canonical juxtaposition and learned systematization, not a narrative interaction.
- **Tištrya — joint appearance only.** Tištrya, stars, Moon, and Sun occur in ritual catalogues; no specific encounter with Māh is narrated. Source: Yasna 3.13. **Tier:** later canonical joint invocation.
- **Mithra — joint appearance only.** Mithra and Māh occur within the same sacrificial catalogues and astral visual environments; no Avestan myth records their meeting. Source: Yasna 3.13 and later imagery. **Tier:** canonical joint invocation; iconographic association uncertain.
- **Gōš/Geuš Urvan and Rām — co-workers in Vohu Manah’s group.** This is a systematic relation, not an episode. Source: later Zoroastrian hierarchy and calendrical tradition. **Tier:** learned systematization.
- **Gōzihr — no identity or encounter.** Gōzihr’s name derives from *gaočiθra*, but he is a hostile lunar node/dragon in astrological eschatology. No source says Māh becomes Gōzihr or battles him personally. **Tier:** important negative finding.
- **Manichaean Jesus the Splendour — syncretistic reassignment, not an encounter.** Eastern Manichaean terminology and cosmology identify a Moon god with Jesus in the light-ship system. This is not a Zoroastrian narrative in which Māh meets Jesus. **Tier:** syncretistic identification.

No documented interaction has been found between Māh and Anāhitā in an erotic role, between Māh and Mithra in combat, or between Māh and a Greek or Roman lunar deity. Popular charts asserting such relations go beyond the sources.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Moon holding the seed of the Bull

#### Avestan core

**Securely attested early material.** The Moon is repeatedly *gaočiθra* and is invoked together with the uniquely created Bull and cattle of many species. This establishes a lunar-bovine generative relationship. It does not describe the Bull’s death, name its killer, or narrate the seed’s journey.

#### Middle Persian full narrative

**Learned systematization.** Ohrmazd creates the unique primordial bovine, white and bright like the Moon. During Ahriman’s assault on creation, the bovine dies. From its bodily substances grow grains and medicinal plants. Its reproductive seed is taken to the Moon station, where lunar light purifies and prepares it. From it come the first male and female cattle and, afterward, the many species of beneficial animals.

The myth converts an apparent catastrophe into proliferation: Ahriman kills one primordial creature, but divine preservation multiplies animal life. Māh’s function is custodial and transformative, not martial.

#### Developmental dispute

- The traditional scholarly view reads Yašt 7 as evidence that the seed myth already existed in some form in the Younger Avestan period.
- Panaino argues that the detailed myth lacks an Avestan and Indo-Iranian antecedent and emerged in Middle Iranian thought.
- Hintze answers that the Avestan compound itself means “from whom cattle originate,” so a generative lunar-cattle conception predates the Middle Persian prose, even if its narrative details do not.
- Comparisons with Vedic Soma, lunar fertility, and cattle are useful but cannot reconstruct a lost common myth with confidence.

#### Roman Mithraic comparison

**Contested modern reconstruction.** In Roman Mithraic art, Mithras kills a bull while Sun and Moon observe; plants and life emerge from the victim’s fluids. Cumont and successors connected this scene to Iranian bovine cosmogony. The comparison is real at the level of bull, life, Sun, and Moon, but there is no Iranian text in which Mithra kills the primordial Bull. Later scholarship warns against deriving the Roman tauroctony directly from the *Bundahišn*. The controversy is summarized in [Iranica’s Mithraic cosmology article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cosmogony-ii/).

### Waxing, waning, and the three lunar stations

**Securely attested early material.** Yašt 7 treats the waxing and waning as a balanced fifteen-day process established through divine order. It venerates the new moon, full moon, and *višaptaθa*. The text’s syntax and plural forms are difficult.

**Learned systematization.** Dēnkard 3.259 turns the three named stations into three favorable five-day periods, each followed by a corresponding counter-period. Malandra observes that this schematic thirty-day month will continually drift against the actual lunar cycle. It is therefore a ritual division, not a precise astronomical model.

### Lunar light and spring vegetation

**Securely attested Avestan theme.** When the Moon’s light warms, gold- or green-colored plants grow in spring. The verse was partly modeled on the preceding Sun Yašt, which suggests literary adaptation. The Moon’s verdure and water-giving epithets expand the theme.

No surviving myth names a plant goddess whom Māh impregnates or rescues. Such a narrative would be modern fabrication.

### Distribution of glory

**Avestan hymnic motif.** The Aməša Spəntas hold and distribute the Moon’s glory over the earth. The act connects visible lunar radiance with *xᵛarənah*, beneficent splendor. Sasanian rulers’ use of crescents and stars can reasonably be read against this tradition, although individual coin symbols need not depict the scene literally.

### The celestial station

**Learned systematization.** Middle Persian cosmology places stars, Moon, and Sun in ascending stations. The *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag* turns those stations into stages in the visionary ascent of souls. The Moon station is higher than the stars but below the Sun. This is cosmography and afterlife geography rather than a story centered on Māh.

### Tides

**Learned systematization.** The *Bundahišn* attributes ebb and flow to two winds associated with the Moon. The explanation reflects observation formulated through late antique cosmology; it is not present in Yašt 7.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Institutional evidence

No securely identified freestanding “Temple of Māh” is known from the Iranian heartland. No surviving inscription names a hereditary priesthood exclusively belonging to Māh, and no administrative archive documents a distinct lunar estate. This absence matters: Māh’s cult is best documented inside the common Zoroastrian liturgy, calendar, and royal iconography, not as an autonomous temple institution.

Herodotus’s claim that Persians used no anthropomorphic cult statues or constructed altars in the Greek manner describes fifth-century Persian practice through Greek categories. Later Iranian religion possessed temples and elaborate sacred installations, but none can be assigned to Māh merely because it contains a crescent.

### Sacrifice and offering

Yašt 7’s ritual frame offers:

- libations;
- Haoma;
- milk and, in older translations, meat;
- the *barəsman* bundle;
- sacred formulas and properly spoken words;
- praise, prayer, propitiation, and glorification.

These are components of broader Zoroastrian worship. They do not prove that animals were killed specifically for Māh. Zoroastrian animal sacrifice existed in some communities and periods, but the textual offering formula can reflect prepared ritual portions rather than a distinct lunar slaughter rite. The broader evidence is assessed in [“Sacrifice in Zoroastrianism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sacrifice-i/).

### Human sacrifice

**Negative finding.** No Avestan, Pahlavi, archaeological, or reliable classical evidence documents human sacrifice to Māh. Herodotus reports human sacrifices by particular Achaemenid figures in wartime or exceptional circumstances, but he does not connect them to the Moon. General claims that “the Persian Moon god demanded human sacrifice” are unsupported.

### Prayer practice

The Māh Niyāyišn is recited while looking toward the Moon at prescribed phases. Its opening says, in effect, “Homage to the Moon when looked at; homage with the look.” Later ritual notes prescribe bodily gestures, including bowing the head in some Parsi traditions.

The litany requests strength, victory, cattle, human prosperity, healing, glory, water, and salvation. The material is devotional and apotropaic but not a lunar oracle. There is no documented ancient practice of asking Māh to predict romantic compatibility or reveal one’s “moon sign.”

### Calendar

Māh is the twelfth day of each thirty-day Zoroastrian month. The matching of a day-name and month-name normally produces a *jashn*, but there is no month named Māh in the standard Zoroastrian calendar: *māh* is also the general word for “month,” which causes modern confusion. “Māh-rōz” most often means the day of Māh, not a large annual “Mah festival.”

The standard prescription to recite the Māh Niyāyišn three times in the lunation coexists with the solar Zoroastrian calendar. The lunar and solar schemes are not identical.

### Ordinary worship

The strongest evidence is manuscript and continuing recitation: Khordeh Avesta books contain the Māh Niyāyišn with Pahlavi, Sanskrit, Persian, and Gujarati translations or glosses. Its multilingual transmission shows use among Iranian and Indian Zoroastrians.

The lunar divinity could be addressed without a dedicated temple. The Moon itself supplied the visible focus, just as light and fire could orient prayer without being “idols.”

### Priesthood and economics

The general Zoroastrian priesthood performed the relevant liturgies. No office of “high priest of Māh,” lunar tithe, temple prostitution, or lunar sacred marriage is attested. Reports or websites assigning these institutions to Māh are modern extrapolation from Mesopotamian or Mediterranean moon cults.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### Indo-Iranian background, before approximately 1000 BCE

**Modern scholarly reconstruction.** The cognate vocabulary for Moon/month and parallel associations of lunar cycles with cattle, moisture, and growth indicate a shared Indo-Iranian background. A named Proto-Indo-Iranian god identical in every detail to Māh cannot be reconstructed. Iranian Māh, Vedic Soma, and later Candra followed different theological histories.

### Old Avestan religious reform

The Gāthās direct worship toward Ahura Mazdā and moral-divine powers such as Aša and Vohu Manah. They recognize the Moon as part of Mazda’s ordered cosmos but do not give Māh an independent hymn. Whether this represents demotion of an older moon god is possible but unprovable.

### Younger Avestan formation, first millennium BCE

Māh enters the surviving liturgical corpus as *gaočiθra*, recipient of offerings and benefactor of plants, water, cattle, healing, and prosperity. Yašt 7 is short and heavily formulaic, suggesting either that Māh never possessed a large surviving narrative cycle or that most of it was lost.

The Yašts were oral priestly compositions produced through the Old Iranian period; attempts to divide them cleanly into “pre-Zoroastrian pagan” and “Zoroastrian” passages are no longer regarded as methodologically secure. [Iranica’s Yašts survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/) reviews the Christensen, Gershevitch, Panaino, Kellens, and Skjærvø positions.

### Achaemenid period, 550–330 BCE

Herodotus verifies Persian sacrifice to the Moon. Royal inscriptions do not name Māh. Seals and possibly Qizqapan show a personified lunar god, while tomb crescents attest royal interest in celestial symbolism. The evidence suggests public familiarity but does not demonstrate a centralized state cult comparable to later royal promotion of Anāhitā and Mithra.

An Achaemenid calendar reform probably helped establish the divinity-named day calendar, but its precise date and mechanism remain debated. Old Persian month names in royal inscriptions are not yet the later Zoroastrian divine month-names.

### Hellenistic and Parthian periods, 330 BCE–224 CE

Direct Iranian evidence is sparse. Lunar iconography continued in royal and funerary settings. In Anatolia, Iranian, Greek, and local lunar forms interacted, particularly around Mēn and Persianate royal cults, but a formal Māh–Mēn equation is not documented.

Roman Mithraism personified the Moon and placed Luna opposite Sol in the tauroctony. It is historically connected with an Iranian-named mystery god but is not a preserved western branch of Māh worship.

### Kushan and eastern Iranian developments, first–third centuries CE

Bactrian ΜΑΟ appears on Kushan coins as a named lunar god. This is the clearest regional public image linked to Māh. The Kushan pantheon combined Iranian, Indian, Greek, and local elements, so Mao’s iconography represents adaptation rather than unchanged Avestan cult.

Some Kushan images show Mao and Miiro/Mithra within the same royal pantheon, but there is no narrative episode between them.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

The Zoroastrian priesthood and monarchy organized inherited scripture, ritual, calendar, and theology. Māh retained liturgical worship and acquired a clearer place in cosmic hierarchy. Crescents became prominent in royal iconography and coinage, especially under later Sasanians, linking celestial splendor to kingship.

The detailed bovine-seed narrative was probably formulated or systematized in this milieu, although extant *Bundahišn* manuscripts and redactions are later. The specialized Avestan script and written archetype of the Avesta also belong to late antique scholarly culture.

There is no evidence that Māh became a supreme god or displaced Ahura Mazdā.

### Manichaean reinterpretation, third century onward

Manichaeism treated Sun and Moon as vessels that recover and transport particles of Light. In eastern Iranian transmission, familiar divine names were applied to Manichaean beings; the Moon could be identified with Jesus the Splendour. The old lunar vocabulary thus survived inside a radically different salvific cosmology.

### Arab conquest and Islamization, seventh–tenth centuries

The fall of the Sasanian state removed institutional Zoroastrian dominance. Conversion was gradual and regionally uneven; it involved accommodation, taxation, social incentives, episodic coercion, temple loss, and at times violence. It is misleading either to describe the entire process as immediate forced conversion or to deny persecution.

No source records a campaign specifically against Māh’s temples or worshippers. Māh’s cult declined as part of Zoroastrian institutional contraction. The prayers survived within remaining communities because they were embedded in portable household and priestly collections. The historical process is surveyed in [“Zoroastrianism from the Arab Conquest to Modern Times”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroastrianism-02-arab-conquest-to-modern/) and [“Conversion of Iranians to Islam”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/conversion-ii/).

### Ninth–twelfth centuries

Priests compiled the *Dēnkard*, *Bundahišn*, and other Middle Persian works while Zoroastrians were becoming a minority. This is the period from which Māh’s fullest mythological “biography” survives. It is both preservation and systematization: older Avestan concepts were explained through late antique astronomy and Sasanian theology.

Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma* preserved Iranian cosmological and heroic language within an Islamic Persian epic. Its Moon is a created luminary and poetic image, not a restored public cult deity.

### Parsi and Iranian transmission, thirteenth–nineteenth centuries

The Khordeh Avesta and its Niyāyišns remained part of lay prayer. Indian Parsi manuscript culture added Sanskrit and Gujarati translations; Iranian traditions supplied Persian versions. European and Parsi scholars—including Anquetil-Duperron, Spiegel, Westergaard, Geldner, Darmesteter, Mills, Kanga, and Dhalla—printed the texts and made Māh visible to comparative scholarship.

### Twentieth century to present

Māh remains part of living Zoroastrian scripture and prayer. Traditional practitioners may recite the Māh Niyāyišn at the designated lunar phases and honor Māh on the twelfth day. Practice varies among Iranian, Shahenshahi, Kadmi, and Fasli communities.

This is continuity of a prayer tradition, not a demonstrably large separate “Moon cult.” No reliable demographic statistics isolate worshippers devoted primarily to Māh.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and translation

Darmesteter’s nineteenth-century *Sacred Books of the East* translation popularized “the Moon that keeps in it the seed of the Bull.” Geldner’s 1886–96 critical Avesta became the standard textual basis. Dhalla and Kanga supplied Parsi editions and liturgical translations. Boyce integrated Māh into her large historical reconstruction of Zoroastrianism. Malandra analyzed the hymn’s composition and metrics; Panaino and Hintze reopened the meaning and antiquity of *gaočiθra*.

### Theosophy and Khshnoom

**Modern reinvention.** Late nineteenth- and twentieth-century theosophical and Ilm-e-Khshnoom writers interpreted Māh through occult planes, vibrations, colors, astrology, and esoteric “currents.” These systems are genuine modern religious traditions but not direct translations of Yašt 7. The Avestan text does not teach astral bodies, Theosophical root races, or a seven-plane lunar initiation.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist practice

Small modern Mazdayasnian, Iranian reconstructionist, and eclectic pagan communities invoke Māh. Online devotional material often combines Yašt 7 with generalized moon magic, lunar phases, crystals, Monday, astrology, or Greek goddess correspondences. Recitation of Yašt 7 or the Māh Niyāyišn is grounded in Zoroastrian tradition; ritual correspondences with silver candles, tarot, Artemis, Diana, or “divine feminine energy” are modern eclectic additions unless a practitioner expressly presents them as personal adaptations.

No reliable census establishes the size of a distinct modern Māh-centered movement.

### Popular visual culture

Modern illustrations frequently show Māh as:

- a pale young man with a crescent crown;
- a winged or haloed figure holding a bull;
- a woman modeled on Selene;
- the Moon rescuing semen from a dying bull at Persepolis.

None is an authenticated ancient canonical portrait. A widely circulated caption describing a “Persepolis mural” of Gavaevōdāta’s death and Māh’s rescue is especially problematic: no securely identified Persepolis relief depicts that episode. This is a modern miscaption or imaginative identification.

### Games, occult lists, and commercial use

Māh appears sporadically in mythology databases, role-playing settings, deity lists, and astrology products. These often repeat unsourced claims that:

- Monday is his canonical holy day;
- twelve is his sacred number;
- Artemis or Diana is his exact equivalent;
- he is married to Anāhitā;
- he rules dreams and madness;
- he drives a bull-drawn chariot;
- a specific Persepolis relief portrays his myth.

These are **modern inventions or unverified extrapolations**, not claims established by the primary corpus.

### National and political use

Sasanian crescent-and-star imagery is sometimes promoted online as proof that the modern crescent symbol is exclusively Iranian or originally Zoroastrian. The symbols are much older and circulated widely across Mesopotamia, Anatolia, the Hellenistic world, Iran, and Central Asia. Sasanian use is historically important but neither exclusive nor a direct ancestor of every later Islamic crescent.

There is no substantial documented extremist movement centered specifically on Māh. Broader nationalist appropriations of “Aryan” or pre-Islamic Iranian religion may include him, but assigning those politics to the ancient yazata would be anachronistic.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Dating Yašt 7

The Yašt’s Younger Avestan language places its composition in the Old Iranian oral tradition. The text is not dated by an author, ruler, manuscript colophon, or datable event. Proposed dates range broadly within the first millennium BCE.

Earlier scholarship often assigned entire Yašts to “pre-Zoroastrian paganism” or isolated supposedly pure ancient layers. Current caution emphasizes oral recomposition, formulaic reuse, and the impossibility of assigning every passage to a sharp pre-/post-Zarathustrian boundary.

### *Gaočiθra*

This debate determines whether the bovine-origin myth has an Avestan core or is mainly Middle Iranian:

- Bartholomae and much later scholarship: “containing/bearing the seed of cattle.”
- Formal or iconographic alternatives: “cow-shaped,” “of bovine appearance,” or “brilliant.”
- Panaino: detailed seed myth is Middle Iranian and affected interpretation of the Avestan phrase.
- Hintze: grammar and comparison favor “from whom cattle originate,” preserving an older idea even if the prose narrative is later.

The safest conclusion is two-tiered: an Avestan lunar-bovine origin relationship is secure; the complete story of Ahriman, transported semen, purification, and 282 species is first explicit in Middle Persian literature.

### Māh and the Achaemenids

Herodotus securely attests Moon sacrifice. Named royal inscriptions do not mention Māh. Seal identifications are plausible, while tomb-disc identifications remain uncertain. The combination supports the presence of Iranian lunar worship but not a reconstructed centralized Achaemenid “Church of Māh.”

### Māh and Roman Mithraism

Cumont’s model treated Roman Mithraism as Romanized Mazdaism and connected the tauroctony to the Iranian primordial bovine myth. Later critics emphasize that Iranian Mithra never kills the Bull in extant sources and that Roman Mithraism possesses its own astronomical and Mediterranean visual grammar. Similarity does not prove direct narrative descent.

### Moon god versus physical Moon

Some interpreters say the hymns address only the physical Moon; others emphasize the masculine grammar, sacrificial language, title *baγa*, and person-like benefits. The opposition is artificial in an ancient religious context. The visible luminary is the divinity’s manifestation and the direct recipient of the worshipper’s gaze.

### “Monotheism” and the title “god”

Modern confessional and scholarly writers differ over whether yazatas should be called gods, angels, aspects, emanations, or created spiritual beings. Yašt 7 calls Māh *baγa* and worships him while placing him within Mazda’s order. “God” accurately reflects the ancient category in one sense; “subordinate divinity” prevents the mistaken implication that he rivals Ahura Mazdā.

### State of the evidence

- No Avestan autograph survives.
- Yašt manuscripts are medieval and early modern.
- F1, copied in 1591, is the principal old pure-Yašt codex.
- Jm4, 1352, is the oldest Khordeh Avesta witness.
- The *Bundahišn* survives in separate Iranian and Indian recensions; a 1587 colophon belongs to an important Iranian witness.
- Achaemenid and Sasanian images are dispersed across museums and archaeological publications, often without inscriptions identifying the figure.
- The Avestan Digital Archive is making manuscript witnesses available for comparison.
- Geldner remains foundational, but modern projects increasingly collate individual manuscripts rather than treating his eclectic text as a single ancient artifact.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- A Persepolis relief securely depicts Māh rescuing the Bull’s seed: unsupported.
- Māh was identical with Anāhitā because both relate to fertility: false conflation.
- Māh is a goddess: contradicted by Avestan grammatical gender and Iranian usage.
- The crescent on every Iranian object represents Māh: methodologically unsound.
- Roman Mithras’s bull killing is simply the Zoroastrian story in pictures: unproved and contradicted by the absence of an Iranian Mithraic bull-killing narrative.
- Zoroastrians worshipped the Moon instead of Ahura Mazdā: false.
- Māh demanded human sacrifice or temple prostitution: no evidence.
- Every occurrence of Persian *māh* in Ferdowsi is a survival of active Māh worship: false.
- Gōzihr is Māh’s evil form: lexical confusion.
- Māh is ancient proof of modern astrological “Moon-sign” doctrine: anachronistic.
