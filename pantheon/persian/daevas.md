# The Daevas: Research Dossier

**Class:** A collective category of divine beings rejected by Zarathustra; subsequently classified in Zoroastrian tradition as false gods, hostile spirits, demons, and, in Middle Persian cosmology, the organized counter-host of Ohrmazd’s good creation. In medieval New Persian epic and folklore, *dīv* additionally means an ogre-like, magical monster, a development related to but not identical with the Avestan category.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Old Avestan *daēuua-* in the Gāthās, especially *Yasna* 30.6 and 32, conventionally placed around 1000 BCE on linguistic grounds, though dates from approximately 1200 to 600 BCE remain in scholarly circulation. These compositions survived orally; the oldest extant Avestan manuscript is medieval, K7a, probably 1288 CE. The earliest physically datable occurrence is Old Persian *daiva-* in Xerxes I’s trilingual XPh inscription from Persepolis, carved during his reign, 486–465 BCE, and discovered in 1935.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Old Avestan, Old Persian, or other directly relevant evidence from the first millennium BCE.
- **[Later canonical]** Younger Avestan doctrine and ritual whose surviving form is later than the Gāthās but belongs to the Zoroastrian canon.
- **[Learned systematization]** Middle Persian/Pahlavi organization of older traditions, chiefly in texts compiled or redacted in the ninth–tenth centuries CE.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A datable appropriation or equation across traditions.
- **[Medieval accretion]** New Persian epic or Islamic-period literary development.
- **[Regional variant/folklore]** Local oral or vernacular tradition.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference from comparative linguistics, ritual, or fragmentary historical evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent assertion lacking an ancient or medieval source.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The category

**[Early attested]** Old Avestan *daēuua-* is grammatically the counterpart of Vedic Sanskrit *devá-* “god.” Both derive from Proto-Indo-Iranian \**daiwa-* and ultimately Indo-European \**deiu̯ó-* “celestial/shining one,” cognate with Latin *deus*. That etymology securely establishes inherited vocabulary; it does **not** establish that every named Vedic deva was once worshipped in Iran or that the Iranian and Vedic pantheons can be mechanically reversed.

In the Gāthās, the daēvas are rejected beings who chose—or were associated with—the “worst mind,” error, destructive action, and the Lie. Yet the text addresses them and their worshippers within an active religious dispute. They have not become the fully catalogued, biologically grotesque demons of later books. “Rejected gods” is therefore often the least prejudicial translation for the oldest layer.

**[Later canonical]** In Younger Avestan, *daēva-* is unambiguously hostile. The religion describes itself as *vi-daēva-*—“rejecting the daēvas” or “against the daēvas”—alongside *mazdayasna-* “Mazdā-worshipping” and *zaraθuštri-* “Zoroastrian.” The title *Vīdēvdād* means approximately “Law/ordinance against the daēvas.”

**[Learned systematization]** Middle Persian has *dēw*, conventionally written with the Aramaic heterogram ŠDYA or plural ŠDYAʾn. Pahlavi theology makes the dēws the destructive spiritual host of Ahreman. They embody such defects as evil thought, tyranny, discontent, hunger, wrath, greed, pollution, sleep, and death.

**[Medieval accretion]** New Persian دیو, *dīv*, plural دیوان *dīvān*, denotes a demon, ogre, giant, sorcerous monster, or nonhuman adversary. Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed around 1010 CE, preserves moral and cosmological associations inherited from Zoroastrianism, but its dīvs are also epic bodies: they fight, rule countries, cast spells, are wounded, bleed, surrender, and become servants.

### Forms in relevant languages

- Proto-Indo-European: reconstructed \**deiu̯ó-* — “celestial/shining being.”
- Proto-Indo-Iranian: reconstructed \**daiwa-* — divine being.
- Vedic Sanskrit: देव, *devá-* — god, heavenly one.
- Old Avestan and Younger Avestan: 𐬛𐬀𐬉𐬎𐬎𐬀, *daēuua-* in conventional transcription — rejected god/demon.
- Old Persian cuneiform: *daiva-* — a proscribed divine category in XPh.
- Middle Persian/Pahlavi: *dēw*, heterographically ŠDYA — demon.
- Manichaean Middle Persian and Parthian: *dēw/dyw* — demon or power of Darkness, within a specifically Manichaean cosmology.
- Sogdian: forms from Iranian *dēw-* occur in demonological vocabulary.
- Classical and New Persian: دیو, *dīv* — demon, ogre, giant.
- Pashto, Kurdish, and Balochi have cognate forms such as *dēw/dêw*, normally with hostile or monstrous senses.

English *devil* is **not** derived from Persian *dīv*. It comes through Old English and Germanic from Greek *diabolos*, “slanderer.” Claims that English *devil* is a borrowing from Iranian are **[modern false etymology]**.

### Titles and classifications

- *daēuuanąm daēuuō*, “daēva of the daēvas”: applied to Angra Mainyu in *Vīdēvdād* 19. **[Later canonical]**
- “Chief demons” or “arch-demons”: modern shorthand for the leading dēws arranged against the Amahraspands in Pahlavi books. The Avesta does not present a single stable “seven arch-demons” table. **[Learned systematization]**
- *dīv-e sapīd*, دیو سپید, “White Dīv”: ruler or champion of the dīvs of Māzandarān in the *Šāhnāma*. **[Medieval accretion]**
- *dīvband*, “binder of dīvs”: epithet of Tahmūraṯ/Tahmuras after his victory over them; also applied to Rostam. **[Medieval accretion]**

### The “Vedic reversal”

**[Secure linguistic relationship]** Iranian *daēva* and Vedic *deva* are cognates.

**[Secure historical difference]** Vedic *deva* remained a favorable divine designation, while Iranian forms are pejorative in every directly attested Iranian language. Conversely, Avestan *ahura* “lord” is favorable, whereas Sanskrit *asura* underwent a complicated, gradual demonization in later Vedic and post-Vedic literature.

**[Rejected simplification]** This is not evidence for a single prehistoric war in which Indians worshipped one faction and Iranians worshipped the other. Early Rigvedic *ásura* can be an honorific of gods such as Varuṇa and Mitra, and its unfavorable classification is late. Nor does the Avesta demonize the entire Vedic pantheon by name. Indra, Nāŋhaiθya, and perhaps Saurva have recognizable Indic comparisons; most Vedic devas do not occur as Iranian demons.

Three major explanatory models remain:

1. **Constituent hypothesis:** *daiva* was already unfavorable before specifically Zoroastrian reform. Older versions imagined an Indo-Iranian religious schism; those reconstructions are now considered overly schematic.
2. **Reform hypothesis:** Zarathustra himself demoted traditional gods. Yasna 32 strongly supports his active rejection, but does not prove that he single-handedly reversed a universally Iranian pantheon.
3. **Progressive hypothesis:** distrust or exclusion developed over time, with Zarathustra intensifying it and later communities completing the demonization. This best accommodates the mixed Gathic, linguistic, and later evidence, but its prehistoric stages remain reconstructed rather than documented.

The principal modern survey warns that none resolves every difficulty: the Gāthās seem to address recognizable rival divinities, while the uniformly negative Iranian descendants of the word suggest a very early and geographically broad change ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Daiva”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/daiva-old-iranian-noun/)).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Old Avestan Gāthās

**Date and support.** The Gāthās are Old Avestan ritual poetry transmitted orally, probably composed around the transition from the second to first millennium BCE. Precise dating and location cannot be established. The language is not southwestern Iranian. No ancient manuscript survives; the extant tradition descends through the Sasanian liturgical redaction and medieval copies ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Avesta”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/)).

The most important passages are:

- **Yasna 30.6:** the daēvas fail to choose rightly because delusion comes upon them; they choose the “worst mind” and rush to Wrath, by which mortals afflict existence. **[Early attested]**
- **Yasna 32.3–5:** the daēvas and their adherents are called offspring or associates of Evil Mind, the Lie, and arrogance; through their actions they deprive humanity of good life and immortality. Translation of the compact syntax differs significantly among Bartholomae, Insler, Humbach, Kellens-Pirart, and Skjærvø. **[Early attested]**
- **Yasna 32.8:** Yima is reproached in a notoriously difficult stanza, traditionally because he made cattle flesh available to mortals. Whether this condemns animal sacrifice, excessive slaughter, or something else remains disputed.
- **Yasna 32.12–15:** *kavis*, *karapans*, and other hostile ritual or political figures mislead people and harm cattle. Some interpreters connect their violence directly to daēva worship; others caution that the poem attacks human rivals and their ritual economy rather than describing a coherent “daēva cult.”
- **Yasna 44.20:** asks whether the daēvas have ever exercised good lordship and associates their worship with priests who deliver cattle to Fury/Wrath and oppose Aša.
- **Yasna 46.1:** records the speaker’s social isolation but is not itself a catalogue of demons.

These texts establish rejection and conflict. They do not name Indra, Saurva, or Nāŋhaiθya, do not call the daēvas creations of Angra Mainyu, and do not supply their later monstrous bodies.

### Younger Avesta

The Younger Avestan corpus is chronologically layered. Its oldest material may be broadly Achaemenid-era or earlier; the *Vīdēvdād* in its transmitted form is generally considered later than the major Yašts, possibly post-Achaemenid. Exact absolute dates are unattainable.

#### The Vīdēvdād/Vendidad

The title *Vī-daēva-dāta-* means “law established against the daēvas.” Its twenty-two chapters combine mythical geography, purity rules, corpse pollution, expiation, medicine, legal material, spells, and the temptation of Zarathustra. Its language contains late or defective forms, suggesting composition or redaction after Avestan had ceased to be normally spoken. It is the only Sasanian *nask* said in the Dēnkard to survive substantially complete ([Vīdēvdād dating discussion](https://poj.peeters-leuven.be/content.php?id=511&url=article); [Darmesteter translation index](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/)).

- **Vīdēvdād 10:** Gathic passages and expulsion formulae drive Angra Mainyu, Nasu, Indra, Sauru, Nāŋhaiθya, Tauru, Zairi, Aēšma, and Akataša from house, settlement, town, land, and polluted body. The familiar six-member arrangement in Darmesteter’s note partly reflects later commentary, not merely the Avestan verse ([Vīdēvdād 10](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd10sbe.htm)).
- **Vīdēvdād 19.1–10:** Angra Mainyu sends temptation against Zarathustra; Zarathustra rejects him and invokes sacred speech.
- **Vīdēvdād 19.43–47:** the daēvas panic at Zarathustra’s birth and flee toward the northern hell. The catalogue includes Angra Mainyu, Indra, Sauru, Nāŋhaiθya, Taurvi, Zairi, Aēšma, Akataša, Zaurva, Būiti, Driwi, Daiwi, Kasvi, and Paitiša. **[Later canonical]**
- **Vīdēvdād 7 and 8:** Nasu, the corpse-polluting female druj, attacks a corpse and threatens the living. Purification is therefore an act of material hygiene and cosmic combat.
- **Vīdēvdād 1:** Angra Mainyu answers each good land made by Ahura Mazdā with a destructive counter-creation. These afflictions belong to the broader demonic system, but not all are individually called daēvas.

#### Yasna and Visprad

The later Yasna repeatedly defines the worshipper as Mazdā-worshipping, Zarathustrian, and hostile to the daēvas. Yasna 12, the *Fravarānē* or confession of faith, contains the programmatic profession: “I profess myself a Mazdā-worshipper, a Zarathustrian, rejecting the daēvas.” **[Later canonical]**

Yasna 9, the Hōm Yašt, makes Haoma a force that repels hostile beings and destructive ritual influences. Yasna 57/Srōš Yašt presents Sraoša as a nocturnal guardian who strikes Aēšma. The opposition is enacted in liturgy, not merely narrated.

#### Yašts

- **Mihr Yašt, Yt. 10.26–27:** Mithra smashes the heads of daēvas and destroys defiant forces ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Mihr Yašt”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mihr-yasht/)).
- **Ābān Yašt, Yt. 5.94–95:** Zarathustra asks what happens to libations offered to Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā after sunset by *daēva-yasna-* worshippers. She replies, in effect, that the proper recipient is absent and the daēvas take the offering. The passage concerns illicit timing and ritual classification; it does not make Anāhitā a former daēva.
- **Tištar Yašt, Yt. 8:** Tištrya battles Apaōša, the drought demon. Apaōša belongs to the hostile supernatural class, though “daēva” is not interchangeable with every word for demon.
- **Zamyād Yašt, Yt. 19.46:** Akəm Manah, Aēšma, Aži Dahāka, and Spityura join Angra Mainyu’s struggle for the royal Glory against Vohu Manah, Aša Vahišta, and Ātar. **[Later canonical]**
- **Drvasp/Gōš and other Yašts:** hostile daēva-worshippers and demonic opponents appear in inherited heroic narratives, but these references do not yield a single consistent genealogy.

### Old Persian XPh

**[Early attested, physically datable]** Four stone tablets—two Old Persian, one Babylonian, one Elamite—were excavated at Persepolis on 26 June 1935. XPh first gives Xerxes’ royal and territorial formulae, then states that one listed country rebelled. In another or possibly the same context, Xerxes says that there was a place where *daiva* were worshipped; by Ahuramazdā’s favor he destroyed the *daivadāna* and proclaimed that the daivas should not be worshipped. Where they had been worshipped, he worshipped Ahuramazdā “in accordance with *arta* and with the proper rite/formula,” the difficult phrase *artācā brazmaniya*.

What XPh proves:

- Old Persian possessed the hostile or proscribed word *daiva-* in the fifth century BCE.
- The Achaemenid king represented suppression of their ritual as legitimate royal action.
- A *daivadāna*, literally a place or institution of daiva worship, was destroyed or dismantled.

What it does not prove:

- The country is unnamed; Babylon, Egypt, Greece, and several Iranian regions have been proposed without consensus.
- It does not identify the daivas.
- It does not mention Zarathustra.
- It does not prove that every Achaemenid was an orthodox Zoroastrian in the later sense.
- The verb *viyakanam* and the relation between rebellion and cult remain debated.

Boyce and Gnoli read the event as an Iranian Zoroastrian suppression of daēva worship. Heleen Sancisi-Weerdenburg and subsequent revisionists treated it more as royal ideology: gods supporting disorder could be branded *daiva*, and their unauthorized cult replaced with the king’s Ahuramazdā-centered rite. Both readings agree that theology and imperial obedience are inseparable in the inscription ([XPh text and context](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/xph/); [critical history of interpretation](https://achaemenica.org/articles/the-daiva-inscription); [University of Texas Old Persian text](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/aveol/100)).

### Greek evidence

Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131–132, describes Persian worship in the open, sacrifice, and the Magian officiant, but neither names daivas nor offers an exact Greek equivalent. It is evidence for fifth-century Persian practice, not direct testimony to a daēva cult.

Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 46–47, writing around the late first or early second century CE, reports a dualist teaching attributed to “Zoroaster”: Oromazes and Areimanius produce opposed hosts, and Areimanius receives an apotropaic sacrifice involving a plant called *omomi* and a wolf’s blood in a sunless place. This is valuable Greco-Roman testimony but is neither an Avestan text nor transparent reportage. Its alleged sacrifice to Areimanius conflicts with normative Zoroastrian rejection of demons and may reflect misunderstanding, a marginal rite, or Greek systematization.

### Pahlavi corpus

The surviving manuscripts are late, but their works preserve Sasanian exegesis and post-Sasanian scholastic compilations.

- **Bundahišn**, especially chapters 1–5 and 27–28: cosmic creation, Ahreman’s assault, production and functions of dēws, their opposition to good beings, and final defeat. Its extant recensions were compiled after the Arab conquest, principally in the ninth century, with later additions. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Dēnkard**, books 3, 7, 8, and 9: theological argument, Zarathustra’s legendary history, summaries of lost Avestan *nasks*, and anti-daēva doctrine. Compiled in the ninth–tenth centuries from older materials.
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad**, probably surviving in a post-Sasanian redaction: wisdom dialogue describing moral actions as blows against demons and naming greed and falsehood among human vulnerabilities.
- **Selections of Zādspram**: cosmogony, Zarathustra’s life, and organized demonology; ninth century.
- **Škand Gumānīg Wizār**: ninth-century apologetic argument, including analysis of evil’s dependent and destructive nature.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag**: a visionary tour of heaven and hell, redacted over time and surviving in late manuscripts. Demonic punishers embody sins, but its dēws should not be projected unchanged into the Gāthās.
- **Dādestān ī Dēnīg** and the Pahlavi *Rivāyats*: practical and eschatological demonology, including the expulsion of demons by prayer, ritual cleanliness, fire, and righteous acts.
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn:** apocalyptic depiction of hostile rulers as demons or descendants of Xēšm/Aēšma. This is political demonization in medieval Zoroastrian literature, not evidence that the historical invaders were understood literally as Avestan gods.

### Manichaean material

**[Syncretistic adaptation]** Iranian Manichaean texts from the Sasanian and Central Asian worlds use Iranian *dēw* terminology for powers of Darkness. Middle Persian, Parthian, Sogdian, and Old Turkic fragments were recovered especially at Turfan. Mani’s system is not the Pahlavi Zoroastrian counter-pantheon: its demons belong to a Gnostic cosmology of pre-existent Light and Darkness and incorporate Enochic giants, Iranian monsters, Indian yakṣas and rākṣasas, and other regional beings. A Parthian magical text, M 1202, invokes protection against “all demons, yakṣas, witches, drujs, rākṣasas, idols of darkness and spirits of evil” ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Manichean Pandaemonium”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-parent/manicheism-pandaemonium/)).

### Manuscripts and editions

The oldest Avestan witness, K7a, is probably dated 1288; J2 and K5, important Pahlavi Yasna witnesses, are dated 1323; K1/L4 preserve early Pahlavi Vīdēvdād traditions. This millennium-plus gap between composition and surviving manuscript is essential. It does not make the texts medieval inventions: archaic language, meter, ritual arrangement, and comparative Indo-Iranian evidence demonstrate ancient composition, while corruptions and redaction remain real ([Copenhagen manuscript survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/codices-hafnienses/); [Avestan Digital Archive](https://ada.geschkult.fu-berlin.de/exist/apps/ada/pages/about/sigla.html)).

Principal modern editions and translations include Karl Friedrich Geldner’s critical *Avesta*; James Darmesteter and L. H. Mills in *Sacred Books of the East*; Christian Bartholomae; Helmut Humbach; Stanley Insler; Jean Kellens and Éric Pirart; Johanna Narten; and Prods Oktor Skjærvø. Darmesteter and Mills remain accessible but should not silently control philology where later editions disagree.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

The daēvas have no single natural domain. They are a category defined relationally: beings whose choice, cult, or action opposes Aša, right worship, and the good creation.

- **Gathic layer:** bad judgment, deception, violent ritual, Fury/Wrath, political and priestly hostility.
- **Younger Avestan layer:** pollution, death, disease, drought, bad dreams, magical attack, disorder, false worship, and interference with sacrifice.
- **Pahlavi layer:** a comprehensive negative taxonomy. Every good faculty or creation may acquire an opposing dēw.
- **Epic layer:** foreign or fabulous armies, ogres, magicians, builders, teachers of writing, captors, tricksters, and servants.
- **Folklore:** possessors, nightmare-causers, kidnappers, many-headed monsters, subterranean treasure-keepers, fools, and dangerous helpers.

### Powers and limitations

**[Later canonical]** Daēvas pollute, deceive, afflict bodies, spread disease, disturb ritual, operate at night, and flee before correctly recited sacred speech. The Gāthās themselves become anti-demonic weapons in *Vīdēvdād* 10.

**[Learned systematization]** Dēws cannot create in the positive sense; they damage, corrupt, mix, freeze, dry, starve, or misdirect what Ohrmazd has made. Humans can strike them through good thoughts, words, and deeds; prayer, fire, ritual purity, and sunrise repel them. Their victories are temporary because Pahlavi cosmology guarantees their annihilation at the renovation.

**[Medieval accretion]** Epic dīvs possess great strength, magic, metamorphosis, invisibility, weather-making, and blindness-inducing power. Unlike the incorporeal moral forces of scholastic texts, they can be wrestled, dismembered, enslaved, or killed.

### Iconography

No securely identified Achaemenid image represents “the Daevas.” Horned figures in Luristan bronzes, Persepolis combat motifs, or Near Eastern “master of animals” compositions cannot be identified as daivas merely because modern viewers find them demonic. **Absence of evidence:** the early textual category has no securely established standard visual form.

A Sasanian relief at Naqš-e Rostam portrays a snake-haired, animal-eared figure beneath Ohrmazd’s horse, usually identified as Ahriman. It is evidence for Ahriman’s iconography, not an image of the collective daēvas.

The recognizable dīv type is medieval and early-modern Persian:

- large anthropomorphic body;
- horns, pointed or animal ears, tusks or fangs;
- claws and sometimes a tail;
- strongly colored skin—black, blue, red, orange, or white;
- short skirt, bracelets, anklets, and occasionally weapons;
- cave, mountain, wilderness, or battlefield setting.

The earliest securely attributable surviving images are manuscript illustrations, not ancient cult statues. A celebrated Isfahan folio of about 1330–40 shows Rostam cutting down the White Dīv in a dark cave. A later restorer mistakenly reattached the severed leg ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/452632)). A Qazvin painting of about 1576–77 shows Rostam stabbing the already maimed dīv and removing its liver ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1937-0710-0-327)). Later Persian and Mughal workshops standardized the horned hybrid monster; that standard form should not be retrojected into Avestan religion.

There are no securely attested sacred colors, numbers, weekdays, celestial bodies, plants, or animals belonging to the daēvas collectively. Darkness and night are recurrent hostile settings; “seven arch-demons” is a Pahlavi classificatory number, not a universal emblem.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Origin and hierarchy

**[Early attested]** The Gāthās say that the daēvas chose the worst mind; they do not supply parents, a creator, consorts, or offspring. Their evil is expressed as choice and allegiance.

**[Later canonical]** Angra Mainyu becomes their chief, “daēva of daēvas.” Younger Avestan narrative increasingly treats the hostile beings as a coordinated force.

**[Learned systematization]** Ahreman produces or deploys the dēws as counter-forces against Ohrmazd’s creation. Lists vary. A common Greater *Bundahišn* arrangement is:

- Akōman against Wahman/Vohu Manah;
- Indar against Ardawahisht/Aša Vahišta;
- Sāwul against Shahrewar/Xšaθra Vairya;
- Nāŋhais or Tarōmat against Spandarmad/Spənta Ārmaiti;
- Tārič against Hordād/Haurvatāt;
- Zārič against Amurdād/Amərətāt.

Aēšm, Āz, Astwihād, Jeh, Nasuš, Bušāsp, and others possess major functions but do not occupy identical places in every list. The symmetry is priestly systematization, not the oldest Iranian family tree.

### The Indra–Saurva–Nāŋhaiθya triad

*Vīdēvdād* 10 and 19 place Indra, Sauru/Saurva, and Nāŋhaiθya together.

- **Indra:** securely cognate in name with Vedic Indra, but the Avestan texts do not retell his Vedic battles. Pahlavi Indar obstructs righteousness or freezes the mind against good practice.
- **Saurva:** often compared with Sanskrit Śarva, an epithet of Rudra, but the equation is linguistically and historically less secure than Indra’s. Later Sāwul represents oppressive or bad sovereignty.
- **Nāŋhaiθya:** commonly compared with Sanskrit Nāsatya, the singular or dual title of the Aśvins. The Iranian being is singular in the demon list, and the semantic history is obscure.

Calling these three “the Vedic gods Indra, Rudra, and the Aśvins imported intact into hell” is **[modern overstatement]**. The names are comparative evidence for shared or interacting Indo-Iranian traditions; their Iranian functions were reconfigured.

### Documented divine interactions

Because “the Daevas” are collective, the following records interactions in which the collective or a named member is explicitly involved. Human figures such as Zarathustra, Yima, Tahmuras, and Rostam are included later under myths but are not mislabeled as gods.

- **Ahura Mazdā — rejection and ritual expulsion:** Ahura Mazdā teaches Zarathustra the prayers by which daēvas and pollution are expelled; *Vīdēvdād* 10, Younger Avestan, **[later canonical]**.
- **Angra Mainyu — leadership:** called “daēva of the daēvas” and leads their assembly; *Vīdēvdād* 19, **[later canonical]**.
- **Aša/Truth — opposition:** the daēvas’ error and the violence of their adherents oppose Aša; *Yasna* 30, 32, 44, around 1000 BCE, **[early attested]**.
- **Vohu Manah/Good Mind — opposition:** the daēvas choose the worst mind; *Yasna* 30.6 and 32, **[early attested]**.
- **Aēšma/Wrath — alliance:** the daēvas rush to Aēšma/Fury to afflict existence; *Yasna* 30.6, **[early attested]**.
- **Mithra — combat:** Mithra smashes the heads of daēvas; *Mihr Yašt* 10.26–27, first-millennium BCE Younger Avestan, **[later canonical]**.
- **Sraoša — combat:** Sraoša strikes Aēšma at night and protects ritual and settlements; *Yasna* 57 and *Srōš Yašt*, **[later canonical]**.
- **Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā — intercepted worship:** nocturnal offerings made by daēva-worshippers fail to reach the goddess and are taken by daēvas; *Ābān Yašt* 5.94–95, **[later canonical]**.
- **Tištrya — combat with Apaōša:** rain-star Tištrya battles the drought demon Apaōša; *Tištar Yašt* 8, **[later canonical]**.
- **Vohu Manah, Aša Vahišta, and Ātar — battle for Glory:** opposed by Akəm Manah, Aēšma, Aži Dahāka, and Spityura under Angra Mainyu; *Zamyād Yašt* 19.46, **[later canonical]**.
- **Spenta Mainyu — collective opposition:** later exegesis arrays Angra Mainyu and the daēvas against Spenta Mainyu and the good creation; Younger Avesta and Pahlavi books, **[later canonical/learned systematization]**.
- **Wahman — Akōman’s counterpart:** Greater *Bundahišn*, medieval recension, **[learned systematization]**.
- **Ardwahišt — Indar’s counterpart:** Greater *Bundahišn*, **[learned systematization]**.
- **Shahrewar — Sāwul’s counterpart:** Greater *Bundahišn*, **[learned systematization]**.
- **Spandarmad — Nāŋhais/Tarōmat’s counterpart:** Greater *Bundahišn*, **[learned systematization]**.
- **Hordād — Tārič’s counterpart:** Greater *Bundahišn*, **[learned systematization]**.
- **Amurdād — Zārič’s counterpart:** Greater *Bundahišn*, **[learned systematization]**.
- **Ohrmazd and the yazads — final combat:** the good spiritual host defeats and annihilates the dēws at the renovation; *Bundahišn*, *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*, and Pahlavi Rivāyats, **[learned systematization]**.
- **Mithras/Arimanius — alleged Roman connection:** dedications *Deo Arimanio* occur in Roman Mithraic contexts, but no inscription identifies Arimanius with the familiar lion-headed figure. This concerns Ahriman, not the daēvas collectively; Roman imperial period, **[syncretistic identification, disputed]** ([Mithraism survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithraism/)).

No text records every daēva meeting every yazata. Membership in opposing hosts is not, by itself, a documented individual encounter.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The wrong choice

**Source:** *Yasna* 30.6; approximately 1000 BCE.  
**Tier:** **[Early attested].**

The oldest “myth” is compressed theology rather than narrative. Faced with moral discernment, the daēvas fail to choose correctly. Deception or delusion overtakes them; they choose the worst mind and associate themselves with Wrath, thereby enabling violence against mortal existence. Later tradition interpreted this as the primal fall or exposure of the old gods. The stanza does not say that they were angels created good and then expelled from heaven.

### Zarathustra’s denunciation of the daēva cult

**Sources:** *Yasna* 32 and 44.20.  
**Tier:** **[Early attested].**

Yasna 32 addresses divine and human opponents together. The daēvas, their priests, and rulers are accused of deceit, destructive action, and harm to cattle and communities. The poem’s social setting appears to involve competition over sacrifice, patronage, cattle, and legitimate rule.

Two readings must remain distinct:

- Boyce and others saw Zarathustra protesting cruel mass slaughter carried out by warrior-priests under intoxication.
- Other philologists warn that obscure syntax and polemical diction do not permit reconstruction of a specific bloody festival. The text attests allegations of violence; it does not provide an ethnographic description of the rival rite.

### The temptation and flight before Zarathustra

**Source:** *Vīdēvdād* 19.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical].**

Angra Mainyu approaches or sends agents against Zarathustra, promising worldly sovereignty if he abandons the Mazdā-worshipping religion. Zarathustra refuses and invokes the sacred formula, mortar, cup, Haoma, and words taught by Ahura Mazdā. The daēvas then lament that Zarathustra’s birth means their destruction and flee northward toward the demonic realm.

This is a fully developed saint-versus-demons legend, later than the Gāthic polemic. It retrojects the canonical ritual apparatus into Zarathustra’s confrontation.

### The cosmic invasion

**Source:** *Bundahišn* 1–5 and related Pahlavi texts.  
**Tier:** **[Learned systematization].**

Ohrmazd exists in endless light and Ahreman in darkness and ignorance. On perceiving the light, Ahreman attacks. Ohrmazd fixes a finite period for the conflict, knowing that bounded time permits evil’s defeat. Ahreman produces the dēws and assaults sky, water, earth, plants, cattle, humanity, and fire. The material world becomes mixed: good creation remains ontologically prior, but death, disease, pollution, predation, and moral corruption enter it.

Humans are active combatants. Correct worship, work, cleanliness, care for useful creation, truth, and prayer injure the demons. At the final renovation, the dēws are defeated, Ahreman becomes powerless, the dead rise, and creation is purified. This elaborate drama cannot safely be read back into *Yasna* 30 as though every detail were already present.

### Tahmuras binds the dīvs and acquires writing

**Sources:** Pahlavi royal tradition; Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed c. 1010.  
**Tier:** **[Medieval accretion with older Iranian antecedents].**

Tahmuras defeats and binds the dīvs, earning the title *dīvband*. When threatened with destruction, they offer knowledge in exchange for life and teach him numerous scripts. The episode makes demons repositories of foreign, technical, or dangerous knowledge. It also reverses their status: defeated enemies become culture-bearers and servants.

### Jamšēd commands the dīvs

**Source:** *Šāhnāma*.  
**Tier:** **[Medieval accretion].**

Jamšēd employs dīvs in royal works and civilizational projects. This resembles widespread stories in which supernatural beings build for a sacral king, especially later Solomonic legends. The Gathic Yima is not described commanding demon labor; this is a later epic transformation.

### Kay Kāvus, Māzandarān, and the White Dīv

**Source:** Ferdowsi, *Šāhnāma*, reign of Kay Kāvus.  
**Tier:** **[Medieval accretion].**

A dīv disguised as a musician praises Māzandarān, enticing the vainglorious Kay Kāvus to invade. The epic Māzandarān is a fabulous demon country and should not automatically be identified with the modern Iranian province.

Kāvus’s army devastates the land. The king of Māzandarān summons the White Dīv, chief of the dīvs. The White Dīv raises a black magical storm of cloud, smoke, stones, and darkness; the Iranians are blinded, captured, and placed under guard. Kāvus sends for Rostam.

Rostam undertakes his Seven Labors, defeating beasts, a dragon, a witch, and demon forces. He kills Aržang Dīv, reaches the White Dīv’s cave at midday when dīvs sleep, and wrestles the enormous monster. Rostam severs a leg, kills him, removes his liver or takes its blood, and restores Kāvus and the Iranian nobles’ sight by applying it to their eyes. The cure belongs to an international folktale motif in which giant’s gall, liver, or blood remedies blindness.

The episode is not a transparent account of Zoroastrian suppression. Nöldeke’s proposal that the White Dīv preserves an ancient local deity is **[modern scholarly conjecture]**; no inscription or local cult proves it. Political readings identifying the dīvs with northern peoples are possible literary interpretations, not established historical referents.

### Akvān Dīv

**Source:** *Šāhnāma*.  
**Tier:** **[Medieval accretion].**

Akvān appears in the form of a dangerous wild ass, uproots Rostam’s sleeping place, and asks whether he wishes to be thrown onto mountain or sea. Knowing demons act perversely, Rostam requests the mountain; Akvān throws him into the sea, where he survives. Rostam later kills him. The episode presents dīvs as tricksters bound by inverted speech.

### Folkloric dīvs

**[Regional variant/folklore]** Persian oral tales multiply white-, black-, yellow-, one-eyed, many-headed, water-diving, or long-limbed dīvs. They kidnap or marry human women, guard treasure, alternate long periods of sleep and waking, and may become helpers if defeated. Some can be summoned by burning a hair they have given a hero. Oral stories add families absent from literary epic—for example brothers or sons of named dīvs—and insert demons into episodes where Ferdowsi has none ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Dīv”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/div/)).

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Was there a daēva cult?

**[Early attested]** Yes, in the limited sense that the Gāthās condemn people who revered or sacrificed to daēvas, and XPh says a *daivadāna* existed. No surviving adherent’s hymn, image, temple plan, or priestly archive identifies itself positively as “daēva worship.” Almost all evidence comes from opponents.

This asymmetry prohibits reconstructing a complete proscribed pantheon. The Vedic evidence supplies comparative possibilities, not the lost liturgy itself.

### The daivadāna

XPh is the sole direct Old Persian reference. *Daivadāna* can mean “place/establishment of the daivas.” Xerxes says he destroyed or dismantled it and established correct worship of Ahuramazdā there. Its location, architecture, priesthood, and gods are unknown. Proposed identifications with Babylon’s Esagila, the Athenian Acropolis, or an Iranian sanctuary remain unsupported.

The inscription is nevertheless evidence for politically enforced cultic suppression. Whether Xerxes suppressed a specific rebellion, unauthorized Iranian ritual, or any divine patrons construed as rebels is disputed ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Daivadāna”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/daivadana-lit/)).

### Offerings and animal sacrifice

The Gāthās connect hostile priests and rulers with injury to cattle. They may condemn cruel or excessive killing rather than all animal sacrifice. Later normative Zoroastrianism retained animal sacrifice in modified forms, and Iranian communities continued sacrificial slaughter after the Sasanian period. A simple equation “daēva religion = sacrifice; Zoroastrianism = no sacrifice” is false ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Sacrifice in Zoroastrianism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sacrifice-i/)).

No evidence establishes human sacrifice to the daēvas. Neither XPh nor the Avestan catalogues document it. Claims of a daēva human-sacrifice cult are therefore **[unestablished]**.

No evidence connects daēva worship with “temple prostitution.” Such claims are **[unestablished/modern importation]** from obsolete generalizations about ancient Near Eastern fertility cults.

### Anti-daēva ritual

The best-attested ritual practice concerning the daēvas is their rejection:

- confession as Mazdā-worshipper and daēva-rejecter;
- recitation of Gāthic formulas;
- purification after contact with corpses;
- maintenance of clean fire, water, earth, clothing, and ritual implements;
- nocturnal protection through Sraoša;
- cursing or repudiating demons;
- avoidance of improperly timed offerings;
- ethical combat through good thought, speech, and action.

The full *Vīdēvdād* ceremony intercalates the *Vīdēvdād* with Yasna and Visprad materials and historically required highly trained priests. It is not “worship of the daēvas”; it is a prolonged rite against pollution and hostile powers.

### Priests and institutions

No securely documented “priesthood of the daēvas” survives. Gathic *karapans*, *usigs*, and hostile *kavis* are human ritual or political antagonists, but their exact offices remain disputed. It is circular to translate every hostile priestly title as “daēva priest.”

Zoroastrian priests—the Avestan *āθravan*, later *hērbed* and *mōbed*—maintained the anti-daēva liturgy. The economics of this practice lay in patronage, sacrificial materials, funerary purity, and specialist recitation, but quantification is impossible.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Proto-Indo-Iranian background, before c. 1500 BCE

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** \**daiwa-* designated a divine or celestial being in the shared linguistic past. No inscription or image documents a proto-Iranian “Daēva pantheon.”

### Old Avestan reform, approximately 1200–900 BCE

**[Early attested]** The Gāthās place daēvas and their worshippers on the wrong side of moral and ritual choice. Their rejection is central, but they remain intelligible as objects of contemporary allegiance.

### Younger Avestan development, approximately first millennium BCE

**[Later canonical]** The daēvas become an explicitly demonic category. Named Indo-Iranian beings, moral abstractions, disease and pollution spirits, and Angra Mainyu’s agents accumulate under or beside it. Ritual speech physically expels them.

### Achaemenid imperial usage, 486–465 BCE

**[Early attested]** Xerxes’ XPh turns *daiva* rejection into royal policy and connects proper worship with imperial order. This is the earliest datable physical document.

### Hellenistic and Parthian periods

The Avestan corpus remained primarily oral and continued to be arranged in priestly schools. Greek writers reported Iranian dualism through Greek categories. Exact stages in daēva doctrine are poorly documented.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

Priestly and royal institutions collected, classified, translated, and interpreted the Avesta. The Avestan alphabet was developed for exact liturgical notation. Dēw theology became systematic.

Kerdīr’s third-century inscriptions boast that people were turned from the “religion of the dēws” to that of the yazads. The phrase applied Zoroastrian demonological language to rival religions and shows the political use of the category. Sasanian authorities at times used coercion against religious rivals, although policies varied by reign and circumstance.

### After the Arab conquest

The weakening of Zoroastrian institutions and gradual conversion of most Iranians to Islam reduced the ritual setting in which Avestan daēvas were understood. This was not a single instantaneous forced conversion. Treaties often protected communities; taxation, social mobility, institutional decline, local violence, and occasional coercion all contributed. Destruction of fire temples and execution of priests are reported for some regions, notably Khwarazm, while the overall process took centuries ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Conversion to Islam”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/conversion-ii/)).

Paradoxically, the dīv flourished in Persianate Islamic culture. It was absorbed into epic, romance, folklore, magical literature, and sometimes the broader category of jinn or shayāṭīn without becoming exactly synonymous with either.

### Pahlavi compilation, ninth–tenth centuries

Zoroastrian scholars in Islamic Iran preserved and systematized demonology in the *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, and related books. These are the principal sources for the symmetrical counter-pantheon.

### Ferdowsi and Persian epic, c. 977–1010

The *Šāhnāma* monumentalized dīvs as magical, embodied enemies and servants of Iranian kings and heroes. Its success shaped visual and oral culture far beyond the surviving Zoroastrian community.

### Manuscript-art era, fourteenth–seventeenth centuries

Illustrated *Šāhnāmas* standardized the horned, clawed, hybrid dīv. The White Dīv became the most recognizable individual representative of the class.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries

European philology discovered the deva/daēva cognation and often cast it as a dramatic Aryan religious schism. Comparative mythology, nationalist histories, occult writing, and popular encyclopedias simplified a layered development into “Zoroaster turned the Hindu gods into demons.”

### Present

Zoroastrian liturgy continues to reject daēvas, though modern communities differ over literal, psychological, and ethical interpretation of demons. The word *dīv* remains active in Persian cultural memory and children’s literature. There is no documented large, continuous community worshipping “the Daevas” as restored pre-Zoroastrian gods.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and religious reform

Nineteenth-century translators such as Haug and Darmesteter made the Iranian–Indian opposition central to comparative religion. Some Zoroastrian reformers, especially those emphasizing ethical monotheism, interpreted demons increasingly as harmful tendencies rather than autonomous beings. Traditional liturgy nevertheless retains personal and ritual language.

### Occultism

Western occult systems borrowed “Ahriman,” “Zoroaster,” and Persian dualism far more often than the individual Avestan daēvas. Theosophical and anthroposophical works turned Ahriman into a cosmic or psychological principle, but did not reconstruct an evidence-based daēva cult.

Roman Mithraic *Arimanius* was repeatedly identified with the lion-headed, snake-entwined figure. Modern study has shown that the inscriptions do not accompany the lion-headed statues; the identification is possible in some contexts but unproved. Treating that figure as “the chief Daeva” is **[modern invention]** ([York monument dossier](https://www.mithras.tertullian.org/display.php?page=cimrm833)).

Modern demonolatry and eclectic pagan websites sometimes propose “reclaiming the daevas” as pre-Zoroastrian gods. This is a modern religious construction. It may use the genuine Indo-Iranian etymology, but no surviving daēva liturgy allows a continuous ancient cult to be restored. Claims that every Hindu deva has an Iranian daēva counterpart are likewise inventions.

### Literature, art, and popular culture

The White Dīv remains a major figure in illustrated and adapted *Šāhnāma* narratives. Modern Iranian books, animation, theatre, and public imagery generally derive the creature from Ferdowsi and manuscript painting, not the Avesta.

Fantasy games and fiction use “daeva/deva” inconsistently for demons, angels, magical races, or beautiful dangerous spirits. Such portrayals are modern borrowings from the name and its ambiguous Indo-Iranian history. They are not evidence for ancient attributes.

### National and political appropriation

Iranian nationalist discourse sometimes presents Zarathustra’s victory over the daēvas as an ethnic triumph of “Aryan reason” over primitive superstition. Hindu nationalist or anti-Zoroastrian polemic may reverse the charge and describe Zoroastrianism as demonizing Hindu gods. Both conflate reconstructed linguistic ancestry with later communal identities.

No specific, well-documented extremist movement has made collective daēva worship a major political symbol. Claims of such an ancient political lineage should be marked **[unestablished]**. The broader use of “Aryan” discourse in Iranian nationalism is real, but it does not establish a daēva-centered ideology.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Demonization

The central debate concerns whether:

- daivas were traditional Iranian gods rejected by Zarathustra;
- the word was already pejorative in much of Iranian;
- two Indo-Iranian divine factions had begun diverging before Zarathustra;
- or several locally different processes produced the surviving evidence.

Jean Kellens and Clarisse Herrenschmidt’s survey emphasizes that linguistic distribution and Gathic rhetoric resist a simple solution. Mary Boyce gave Zarathustra a decisive role within a broader Iranian distrust of warlike gods. Earlier “Aryan schism” models associated with Haug and others are now regarded as too rigid.

### Dating

Older scholarship sometimes assigned the Gāthās to the sixth century BCE in order to place Zarathustra near the Achaemenids. Linguistic comparison with Vedic Sanskrit encouraged dates around 1000 BCE or earlier. Absolute dating remains inferential.

The *Vīdēvdād* is especially disputed. Its subject matter can preserve ancient rules, but its transmitted grammar suggests late composition or redaction. Dates extending from the late first millennium BCE into the early centuries CE have been proposed.

### XPh

The “Daiva inscription” has been read as:

- evidence Xerxes was a committed Zoroastrian;
- suppression of a specifically Iranian anti-Zoroastrian cult;
- response to rebellion in an unidentified province;
- suppression of Babylonian or Egyptian temples;
- a general statement that gods aligned with rebellion become *daiva*.

The text does not settle the location. Interpretations identifying the *daivadāna* with Esagila or the Athenian Acropolis lack direct evidence.

### Indra and the named triad

Indra’s name is secure; his Iranian function is not simply copied from the Rigveda. Saurva–Śarva and Nāŋhaiθya–Nāsatya comparisons are plausible but debated in detail. Their grouping may preserve memory of rejected cults, or may be a learned classification created after their names had become demonic.

### The White Dīv

Theories have interpreted him as:

- an ancient deity of the Caspian region;
- an ethnic memory of northern enemies;
- a personification of winter, whiteness, or disease;
- an epic giant formed from international folktale motifs.

None is proved. The secure evidence begins with medieval epic and its manuscript tradition.

### Evidence lost and preserved

The Avesta survives as a fraction of the Sasanian canon described in the *Dēnkard*. Indigenous tradition claimed that Alexander destroyed written copies, but modern scholarship finds no evidence for an Achaemenid written Avesta of the described form. The most plausible model is long oral transmission followed by Sasanian written codification.

Important repositories include:

- Royal Library, Copenhagen: K-series Avestan and Pahlavi codices;
- libraries in Mumbai, Navsari, and other Parsi centers;
- Bibliothèque nationale de France;
- British Library;
- Berlin Turfan Collection for Manichaean Iranian texts;
- Oriental Institute/Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures, Chicago, for Persepolis excavation records;
- National Museum of Iran and Persepolis site collections;
- Avestan Digital Archive and Corpus Avesticum Berolinense for manuscript images and transcription.

### Discredited or unsupported claims that remain popular

- “Daeva means devil and gave English *devil* its name.” False.
- “Zoroaster demonized all Hindu gods.” False.
- “The Avestan daēvas were fallen angels.” Unattested and dependent on Abrahamic categories.
- “Xerxes destroyed the Babylonian temple of Marduk because it was a daiva temple.” Possible only in older speculation; unproved.
- “The White Dīv is a securely identified pre-Zoroastrian god.” Conjecture.
- “Horned ancient Iranian images are depictions of daevas.” Identification requires context and is generally unavailable.
- “Daēva worship involved human sacrifice or temple prostitution.” No supporting evidence.
- “Modern daēva worship is an unbroken pre-Zoroastrian survival.” No evidence.
- “The lion-headed Mithraic figure is certainly Ahriman.” No inscribed identification establishes this.
