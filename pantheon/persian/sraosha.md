# Sraosha: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the Avesta, Sraosha is a masculine **yazata**—a divine being “worthy of worship”—and simultaneously the personification of attentive hearing, obedience, religious discipline, and reception of the sacred word. Middle Persian tradition calls him **Srōš**, while Islamic-period Persian usually has **Sorūš/Surūš**, increasingly classified as an angel or divine messenger.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Sraosha occurs in the Old Avestan **Gāthās**, especially *Yasna* 33.5, 43.12, and 44.16. Their absolute date remains disputed; linguistic estimates commonly place their composition somewhere around the late second or early first millennium BCE. No manuscript from that period survives: the hymns were transmitted orally, written down much later, and are preserved in manuscripts no earlier than the medieval period. “Earliest attestation” therefore means the oldest reconstructable textual stratum, not a contemporary inscription or artifact.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Old Avestan/Gāthic evidence.
- **[LATER AVESTAN]** Younger Avestan canonical material.
- **[PAHLAVI]** Middle Persian priestly elaboration, mostly redacted in the ninth–tenth centuries CE but often incorporating Sasanian or older learning.
- **[MEDIEVAL PERSIAN]** Islamic-period epic, poetry, lexicography, or folklore.
- **[REGIONAL/SYNCRETISTIC]** Local or cross-religious development.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/UNVERIFIED]** A recent claim unsupported by presently identifiable primary evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

Sraosha begins as both a quality and a divine presence. In the Gāthās the noun can mean “hearing,” “hearkening,” “obedience,” or “discipline,” yet *Yasna* 33.5 invokes Sraosha with the verb conventionally used for a deity. The texts consequently resist a clean division between abstraction and person: attentive reception of Ahura Mazda’s word is itself personalized as the being who makes that reception possible. In the Younger Avesta this figure is fully individualized as priest, warrior, guardian of houses and creation, enemy of demons, and teacher of the religion. Pahlavi literature adds the highly developed functions of protector of the newly dead, judge’s associate, psychopomp, ruler of the material world, and eschatological combatant. [Encyclopaedia Iranica’s synthesis and primary-text references](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sraosa/).

### Names and scripts

- **Avestan:** *Sraoša-* or *Səraoša-*; conventionally transliterated **Sraosha**, approximately “Hearkening/Obedience.”
- **Avestan script:** 𐬯𐬭𐬀𐬊𐬴𐬀 (*sraōša*), allowing for orthographic variation by case and manuscript.
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** **Srōš**; Book Pahlavi spelling is conventionally transliterated *srōš*.
- **Manichaean Middle Persian:** *srwšʾhrʾy*, Srōš Ahrāy/Ahlāy.
- **Manichaean Sogdian:** *srwšrt*, a form important because it preserves an interpretation connected with Aši/Reward rather than the later Middle Persian reinterpretation.
- **New Persian:** **سروش**, *Sorūš, Surūš, Soroush, Sorush, Surosh*.
- Older European scholarship also writes **Srosh**, **Serosh**, **Çraosha**, or **Craosha**.

The derivation is from Indo-Iranian √*sraw-/sru-* “hear.” Cognates include Vedic *śruṣṭí-* “obedience” and Gāthic *asrušti-* “disobedience.” The semantic movement is not simply auditory: like Latin *oboedire*, it is “to hear and comply.” Benveniste emphasized “discipline”; Malandra gives the broader range “harkening, obedience, discipline.” “Conscience” is a defensible functional paraphrase in some modern writing, especially because of Sraosha’s relation to religion and moral judgment, but it is not the primary lexical meaning.

### Principal epithets

The following are principally [LATER AVESTAN]:

- **aṣ̌ya** — “connected with Aši/Reward,” perhaps “reward-bearing” or “accompanied by Reward.” Later tradition reanalyzed it through *aṣ̌a*, producing Middle Persian **Srōš Ahlāy**, “Righteous Srōš.” Its precise original force remains disputed.
- **taxma** — brave, strong.
- **huraoδa** — beautiful or well-formed.
- **bərəza** — tall/high.
- **darši.dru** — possessing a strong or formidable club.
- **vərəθrājan** — resistance-smashing or victorious.
- **kamərəδō.jan** — demon-head-smashing.
- **θrātōtəma** — most protective.
- **harətar / aiβyāxštar** — guardian/overseer.
- **vasō.xšaθra** — ruling at will.
- **tanumąθra** — obscure; commonly understood as “whose body is the sacred formula” or “embodying the *mąθra*.” The epithet is not completely secure semantically.
- **daēnō.disō daēnayå** — teacher or instructor of the Religion, *Yasht* 11.14.
- **frādat.gaēθa** — promoter or increaser of the living/material world.
- **sraošō.carana** — Sraosha’s disciplinary instrument, variously interpreted as club, lash, or punitive implement.
- **sraošāwarəza** — the enforcer or operative of Sraosha; applied in *Vendidad* 18 to the cock.

In Islamic Persian, **sorūš** also became a common noun for an angel, heavenly voice, inspiration, oracle, or messenger. Lexicographers could gloss **sorūš-e raḥmat** as Gabriel and **sorūš-e ajal** as the angel of death. These are [MEDIEVAL/EARLY-MODERN SYNCRETISTIC] usages, not evidence that Avestan Sraosha was originally Gabriel or the Islamic angel of death. An early nineteenth-century dictionary already records these extended meanings ([Qatar Digital Library](https://qdl.qa/en/archive/81055/vdc_100085185907.0x000089)).

### Classification through time

1. **Gāthic:** divine abstraction/entity beside Aša, Vohu Manah, and Aši, but not securely one of a fixed sevenfold group.
2. **Younger Avestan:** individualized masculine yazata.
3. **Pahlavi:** powerful yazad, cosmic guardian, psychopomp, and ruler under Ohrmazd’s command.
4. **Islamic Persian:** angel or heavenly messenger, frequently assimilated to Gabriel in vocabulary and poetry.
5. **Modern comparative vocabulary:** sometimes called an “archangel,” but that imports a Judeo-Christian-Islamic taxonomy and should not replace the Zoroastrian category *yazata*.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Old Avestan core

The Gāthās are the oldest identifiable layer:

- **Yasna 28.5:** *sraoša* probably remains the common noun “obedience/hearkening” to Mazda’s sacred utterance.
- **Yasna 33.5:** Zarathustra says he will invoke “Sraosha, greatest/mightiest of all.” This is the clearest early personal invocation.
- **Yasna 43.12:** Sraosha approaches accompanied by **Aši**, Reward.
- **Yasna 44.16:** the poet asks that Sraosha come through **Vohu Manah**, Good Mind.
- **Yasna 45.5:** “giving obedience and reverence” probably uses the common noun.

These passages establish no club, cock, bridge, temple, iconography, or detailed biography. They establish a close relation to hearing and obeying Ahura Mazda’s revelatory word, while showing the term in transition—or deliberate poetic oscillation—between quality and divine agent.

### Younger Avestan corpus

- **Yasna 56:** a liturgical bridge whose stanzas repeatedly ask that Sraosha be present for worship.
- **Yasna 57, the great Srōš Yašt:** thirty-four stanzas in thirteen *kardags*. It presents Sraosha as handsome, sleepless, armed, victorious, ritually expert, guardian after sunset, antagonist of Aēšma, and protector of hospitable households. Because it is incorporated into the Yasna, it acquired a Pahlavi translation/commentary and later a Sanskrit rendering. The complete online Avestan text is available in the [Yasna edition](https://avesta.org/yasna/yasna_mithra.htm).
- **Yasht 11, Srōš Yašt Haδōxt:** twenty-two stanzas in five divisions, probably extracted from the lost *Haδōxt Nask*. It overlaps substantially with *Yasna* 57 but is transmitted independently in the Khordeh Avesta. A historical English translation is available [here](https://avesta.org/ka/yt11sbe.htm).
- **Yasht 10.41, Mihr Yasht:** Mithra drives terrified armies one way, Rashnu another, and Sraosha pursues them everywhere. This is the earliest explicit triadic action of the three later judges, but the passage itself is martial, not funerary.
- **Vendidad 18.14–26:** identifies the cock Parōdarš/Kahrkatās as Sraosha’s operative and ally. At dawn the cock hears Fire’s appeal, wakes human beings, and helps defeat Būšyąstā, the demon of sloth. The passage is preserved in [Darmesteter’s translation](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd18sbe.htm).
- **Siroza:** associates Sraosha with the seventeenth day, placed between Mithra on the sixteenth and Rashnu on the eighteenth.
- Scattered invocations occur throughout the Yasna, Visperad, Vendidad, and Khordeh Avesta.

Young Avestan works cannot normally be dated to a single year. Their language and oral-poetic techniques place their composition in the Old Iranian period, but individual passages may differ considerably in age. Their surviving written forms reflect later liturgical collection and editing. The hypothesis that *Yasna* 57 was once the eleventh free-standing Yasht, displaced into the Yasna and replaced by the Haδōxt hymn, is plausible scholarly reconstruction, not a manuscript colophon.

### Pahlavi corpus

Most extant Pahlavi religious books reached their present form under Islam, principally in the ninth–tenth centuries CE, although their authors inherited Sasanian exegesis and now-lost Avestan material.

- **Dādestān ī Dēnīg 13 and 27:** Srōš, Mihr, and Rašn sit at the Činwad Bridge; Srōš’s appointed function is protection of living beings.
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad 2.114–63:** the soul remains near the body for three nights. On the fourth day the demon Wizārišn attempts to bind it; Srōš opposes him, the soul reaches the bridge, and Rašn judges it. The relevant translation appears in the [Mēnōg ī Xrad](https://www.avesta.org/mp/mx.html).
- **Greater Bundahišn 5, 26, 34:** pairs Srōš against Xēšm/Aēšma; calls Srōš protector or ruler of the material world under Ohrmazd; and gives him an eschatological role. At the renovation he serves as ritual assistant when Ohrmazd performs the final rite. The [Greater Bundahišn translation](https://www.avesta.org/mp/grb.pdf) preserves these passages.
- **Supplementary Texts to Šāyest nē Šāyest 17:** Sraosha is invoked to protect the soul through the dangerous first three nights.
- **Pahlavi Rivāyat accompanying the Dādestān ī Dēnīg:** explains why Srōš may be worshipped alone as lord/ruler of the world and gives him actions at the final renovation.
- **Selections of Zādspram 35:** Srōš accompanies Ohrmazd in the final judgment.
- **Dēnkard:** preserves scattered cosmological, ritual, and funerary teachings and applies the expression *srōšahlāy ī gētīg* to an earthly ruler.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag:** Srōš Ahlāy and Ādur guide the visionary Wīrāz through the afterlife. Its narrative probably developed in the early Islamic period from Sasanian materials and acquired its extant form through successive redaction; it should not be quoted as a verbatim Sasanian document.

### Achaemenid and classical evidence

No securely identified occurrence of Sraosha appears in the royal Achaemenid inscriptions at Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam, Persepolis, or elsewhere. Darius and successors invoke Auramazdā, while later kings also name Mithra and Anāhitā; their silence about Sraosha is evidence only that he is absent from surviving royal epigraphy, not that ordinary worshippers did not know him.

Herodotus and Plutarch do not securely identify a god corresponding to Sraosha. Proposed equations with Greek divine figures are modern comparisons rather than ancient *interpretatio graeca*. Roman Mithraism supplies no secure cult of Sraosha.

### Manuscripts and editions

The physical Avestan manuscripts are medieval and early modern, many copied in India. Modern study relies upon comparative collation, the Pahlavi *zand*, Sanskrit and Gujarati translations, ritual manuscripts, and oral priestly performance. Historical English translations by Mills and Darmesteter remain useful documentary witnesses but predate major advances in Avestan philology. Humbach, Kellens, Malandra, Hintze, and Skjærvø should be preferred for philological questions. The [Avestan corpus hosted by Avesta.org](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/) and manuscript digitization projects such as the Avestan Digital Archive make witnesses accessible but do not eliminate editorial disagreement.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

- **Hearkening and obedience:** the early core.
- **Liturgical knowledge:** the first sacrificer, first stretcher of *barsman*, first reciter of all five Gāthās, and teacher of the religion, according to *Yasna* 57 and *Yasht* 11.
- **Nightly guardianship:** sleepless since the two Spirits made their creations; patrols the world after sunset.
- **Protection of house, settlement, clan, and country:** especially where he is ritually welcomed and fed.
- **Warfare against demons:** above all Aēšma/Xēšm, Wrath.
- **Death ritual:** guards the dead and their souls during the first three nights.
- **Psychopomp and judgment:** a Pahlavi elaboration in which he escorts the soul on the fourth morning and participates with Mihr and Rašn at the bridge.
- **Material sovereignty:** Pahlavi systematization calls Ohrmazd guardian of the spiritual world and Srōš guardian or ruler of the material world.
- **Final renovation:** assists Ohrmazd and defeats Xēšm or, in one late account, Āz.

### Appearance and equipment

Younger Avestan texts describe him as male, youthful, handsome, tall, strong-armed, fast, vigilant, and terrifying to enemies. His weapon is normally the **club**, especially the *darši.dru*, “strong-clubbed.” His more distinctively priestly weapons are spoken formulas and ritual acts: **Ahuna Vairya**, **Yasna Haptaŋhāiti**, **Fšūsō Mąθra**, and sacrifice itself. The juxtaposition is deliberate: correctly performed worship is a weapon against the demonic.

The phrase *sraošō.carana* may designate a club, lash, or disciplinary implement. Its semantic field encouraged later practical associations with a rod used by the *sraošāwarəz*, an assistant priest. Modern claims that Sraosha invariably bears a sword, trumpet, or standardized angelic regalia are unsupported.

### Cock and dog

The cock **Parōdarš**, also called *kahrkatās* in *Vendidad* 18, is Sraosha’s *sraošāwarəza*. Its dawn crow wakes worshippers, answers Fire, and drives away demons, particularly Būšyąstā. The Bundahišn also makes the dog an ally of Srōš. The cock is therefore not merely a generic “sacred animal”: it performs Sraosha’s command by making sleepers hear and obey.

### Day, time, and ritual associations

- **Seventeenth day of the Zoroastrian month:** Srōš day.
- **Night:** his principal protective period.
- **Dawn:** the cock’s decisive moment.
- **First three nights following death:** special Srōš prayers and rites.
- **No securely ancient color, planet, zodiacal sign, or number** belongs exclusively to him. Modern occult tables assigning these should be classified as modern invention unless a specific historical source is supplied.

### Images

Zoroastrian gods are often more securely identified by inscriptions or attributes than by later-looking winged forms. There is no uncontested Achaemenid or Sasanian portrait labeled Sraosha.

Two important modern identifications require caution:

1. **Bāmiyān, sixth century CE:** two half-bird, half-human priestly figures in the painting over the niche of the 38-meter Buddha have been interpreted as the manifestation of Srōš through his cock and the Yasna liturgy. The painting was destroyed in 1999 but is photographically documented. This is a scholarly interpretation, not an inscriptional identification ([Iranica, Mithra iconography](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithra-2-iconography-in-iran-and-central-asia/)).
2. **Akchakhan-kala, Chorasmia, early first century CE:** Michele Minardi and collaborators identify a colossal mural-crowned god and associated “bird-priests” as Srōš, connecting the imagery with Avestan formulas and *sraošō.carana*. This is a substantial recent argument, not yet equivalent to a labeled image. The study expressly presents the identification as newly established from contextual evidence ([Minardi, “The Image of the Zoroastrian God Srōš”](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Michele-Minardi/publication/353491096_The_Image_of_the_Zoroastrian_God_Srosh_New_Elements/links/61f78c22007fb5044727d42b/The-Image-of-the-Zoroastrian-God-Srosh-New-Elements.pdf)).

The first securely identifiable narrative images called “Sorush” are Islamic-period manuscript paintings illustrating the *Shahnameh*. A celebrated folio from the **Shahnameh of Shah Tahmasp**, Tabriz, approximately 1530–35, shows “The Angel Surush Rescues Khusrau Parviz.” Here he is represented according to Islamic Persian angelic convention, not reconstructed Avestan iconography ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/452182)).

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

Sraosha has **no securely attested parents, consort, offspring, or siblings**. Statements that he is literally Ahura Mazda’s son, one of seven original archangels, or the brother of another yazata are theological or modern harmonizations unless tied to a specific late source. He is created or commissioned by Ahura Mazda and functions within Mazda’s order, but functional dependence is not genealogy.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Ahura Mazda — [LATER AVESTAN]:** Mazda reveals the Religion to Sraosha; Sraosha becomes its teacher. *Yasht* 11.14.
- **Ahura Mazda — [LATER AVESTAN]:** Sraosha is the first to worship Mazda and the Amesha Spentas with spread *barsman* and recited Gāthās. *Yasna* 57.2–8.
- **Ahura Mazda/Ohrmazd — [PAHLAVI]:** Ohrmazd governs spiritual and material existence; Srōš exercises delegated protection or lordship in the material world. *Greater Bundahišn* 26.46–48; *Dādestān ī Dēnīg* 27.6.
- **Ohrmazd — [PAHLAVI, ESCHATOLOGICAL]:** Ohrmazd acts as *zōt* and Srōš as *rāspīg*, ritual chief and assistant, in the rite defeating the Evil Spirit at the renovation. *Bundahišn* 34/45.
- **Aši — [EARLY]:** Sraosha comes accompanied by wealth-giving Reward. *Yasna* 43.12.
- **Vohu Manah — [EARLY]:** Sraosha is asked to come to the chosen person “through Good Mind.” *Yasna* 44.16.
- **Aša — [EARLY/INTERPRETIVE]:** Sraosha stands in the same divine conceptual field and is later reinterpreted as “righteous” Srōš; an explicit family relationship is absent.
- **Mithra — [LATER AVESTAN]:** in battle Mithra drives enemy formations one way while Sraosha pursues them. *Yasht* 10.41.
- **Rashnu — [LATER AVESTAN]:** in the same battle scene Rashnu drives enemies another way while Sraosha chases them. *Yasht* 10.41.
- **Mithra and Rashnu — [PAHLAVI]:** the three form the tribunal at the Činwad Bridge; Srōš additionally escorts the soul. *Dādestān ī Dēnīg* 13; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.
- **Ātar/Ādur — [MEDIEVAL PAHLAVI NARRATIVE]:** Srōš and the personified Fire jointly guide Ardā Wīrāz through heaven and hell. *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*.
- **Aēšma/Xēšm — [LATER AVESTAN → PAHLAVI]:** Sraosha’s designated arch-enemy; he strikes Wrath after sunset and finally defeats him at the renovation. *Yasna* 57.10; *Yasht* 11.15; *Greater Bundahišn* 5 and 34.
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — [LATER AVESTAN]:** Sraosha patrols creation against Angra Mainyu’s demonic forces. He is not consistently said to defeat Ahriman single-handedly.
- **Wizārišn — [PAHLAVI]:** the demon binds the wicked soul and brings it to the bridge while Srōš opposes him. *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.161–63.
- **Astwihād — [LATER/PAHLAVI FUNERARY]:** Sraosha protects the dead against the demon of death; later funerary texts place them on opposing sides.
- **Būšyąstā — [LATER AVESTAN]:** Sraosha’s cock frustrates the female demon of sloth by waking sleepers. *Vendidad* 18.
- **Āz — [LATE PAHLAVI]:** Srōš strikes or defeats Greed at the renovation. *Pahlavi Rivāyat accompanying Dādestān ī Dēnīg* 48.94.
- **Haoma — [LATER AVESTAN]:** Haoma offers sacrifice to Sraosha on Mount Harā. *Yasna* 57.19.
- **Vāyu/Wāy and Wahrām — [PAHLAVI FUNERARY]:** the good Wāy and Wahrām assist the righteous soul’s passage with Srōš; evil counterparts oppose it in later accounts.
- **Parōdarš, the cock — [LATER AVESTAN]:** strictly an animal agent rather than a god; it serves as Sraosha’s operative in *Vendidad* 18.
- **Daēnā — [PAHLAVI ESCHATOLOGY]:** Srōš escorts the soul that encounters its own personified religion/conscience. The sources do not make Daēnā his consort.

No secure direct interaction is documented with Anāhitā, Tištrya, Zurwān, or the Roman Mithras. Mere presence in the same pantheon is not an episode.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The first liturgist

**[LATER AVESTAN]** Sraosha is the first being to lay out the sacred *barsman*, worship at it, and recite the five Gāthās. He thereby models ideal priestly action before human priests perform it. Ahura Mazda teaches him the Religion, and he becomes its instructor. This is not present in the surviving Gāthic references; it is the Younger Avestan narrative elaboration of his relation to hearing and sacred speech.

The story does not say that Sraosha composed the Gāthās. “First recited” means paradigmatic primordial performer, not author.

### The sleepless night guardian

**[LATER AVESTAN]** Since the two Spirits made their creations, Sraosha has not slept. After sunset he raises his weapon and protects Mazda’s world; three times by day or night he descends to Xvaniratha, striking demons. A household that honors and feeds him receives protection against calamity and famine. The narrative joins domestic cult, cosmic vigilance, and moral discipline: wakefulness and properly spoken prayer hold back disorder.

### Combat with Aēšma

**[LATER AVESTAN; PAHLAVI SYSTEMATIZATION]** Ahura Mazda creates Sraosha as the opponent of Aēšma, murderous Wrath “of the bloody club.” Sraosha’s own “strong club” answers and reverses that violence. *Yasna* 57.10 has him strike Aēšma after sunset. Pahlavi cosmology regularizes the opposition into a full table of paired yazads and demons: as Vohu Manah opposes Akōman, Srōš opposes Xēšm. At the renovation Srōš finally overcomes his adversary.

This pairing, rather than a single long epic combat, is the persistent mythic cycle.

### The cock’s dawn battle

**[LATER AVESTAN]** During the last watch of the night, Fire calls for help lest the demon Āz extinguish it. Sraosha wakes Parōdarš. The cock flaps its wings and commands humans to arise, cleanse themselves, tend the fire, and undertake religious duties. Būšyąstā tries to lull them back to sleep. Dawn routine thus becomes cosmic combat: getting up, hearing the cock, and tending fire enact Sraosha’s obedience.

### Guardian of the dead and guide to the bridge

The development must be separated into stages:

1. **Avestan basis:** Sraosha protects against nocturnal and demonic attack. He is associated martially with Mithra and Rashnu, but the surviving Avesta does not fully narrate the later tribunal.
2. **[PAHLAVI]:** for three nights after death, the soul remains near the body and is vulnerable. Sraosha is invoked continuously for protection.
3. On the fourth dawn, Srōš escorts the soul to the Činwad Bridge. Mihr presides with Srōš and Rašn; Rašn’s balance determines the weight of deeds.
4. The righteous soul, joined by its beautiful Daēnā, crosses safely; the wicked soul faces Wizārišn and the narrowed bridge.
5. Srōš is therefore both protective psychopomp and member of the judging company, though weighing is particularly Rašn’s function.

Popular summaries often project this entire scheme back into the Gāthās. The complete bridge-escort narrative is later.

### Ardā Wīrāz’s journey

**[MEDIEVAL PAHLAVI NARRATIVE]** The priest Wīrāz is selected to resolve religious uncertainty, consumes consecrated wine and a narcotic, and lies insensible for seven days. Srōš Ahlāy and Ādur meet his soul, conduct him across the bridge and through graded heavens, hells, and intermediate stations, explaining rewards and punishments. Srōš here functions as authoritative interpreter as well as guide. The text’s extant redaction belongs to Islamic-period Middle Persian literature, though it presents itself as recovering truth after Alexander’s destruction.

### Final renovation

**[PAHLAVI LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** At Frašgird, Srōš assists Ohrmazd in the final ritual and judgment, defeats Xēšm and sometimes Āz, and participates in the expulsion or neutralization of evil. This is the culmination of his priest-warrior identity: correct ritual and victorious discipline become the same action.

### Sorush in the Shahnameh

Ferdowsi completed the *Shahnameh* around 1010 CE. Its Sorush is an Islamic-Persian **fereshteh**, an angelic messenger, while retaining functions of the older Iranian divine envoy.

Major appearances include:

- Sorush warns Siyāmak of the demonic plot against Kayumars.
- He prevents Farīdun from killing Zahhāk and orders the tyrant chained on Mount Damāvand. A circa 1430 illustrated manuscript depicts the episode ([Fitzwilliam Museum project](https://shahnameh.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/explore/objects/no-33-zahhak-pinned-to-mount-demavend)).
- In a dream Sorush tells Gōdarz that Kay Khosrow survives in Turan and must be recovered.
- Sorush announces that Kay Khosrow’s earthly kingship is complete and summons him from the world.
- He rescues Khosrow Parviz from a dead end or dangerous confinement, the subject of the Tahmasp *Shahnameh* painting.

These are [MEDIEVAL PERSIAN] transformations, not lost verses of the Avesta. Sorush’s conversion from warrior-priest yazata to dream-messenger and angel allowed the figure to survive within Islamic literary culture.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Temples and cult centers

No temple can presently be identified securely as a temple dedicated exclusively to Sraosha. No labeled cult statue, priestly archive, or Achaemenid foundation inscription establishes such a sanctuary. His worship is predominantly embedded in the general Zoroastrian liturgical system, domestic observance, funerary rites, and the calendar.

Akchakhan-kala may supply early monumental imagery associated with Srōš, but it does not yet prove an exclusive “Temple of Sraosha.” Bāmiyān supplies possible regional iconographic testimony, again without an independent temple institution.

### Ritual offices

The **sraošāwarəz** is an assistant or disciplinary functionary associated with maintaining order in ritual; *Vendidad* 18 mythically identifies the cock as Sraosha’s own operative. Later ethnographic reports connect the office with a rod or strap used to discipline assistants, but this is evidence for later priestly practice, not necessarily the original meaning of every Avestan occurrence.

### Recitations

- *Yasna* 57 is integrated into the high liturgy.
- *Yasht* 11/“Srōš Yašt Haδōxt” is recited independently.
- **Srōš Bāj** is an important daily *drōn* prayer assembled from Avestan passages.
- Night recitation is especially appropriate because Sraosha guards creation after sunset.
- The Khordeh Avesta preserves forms for lay use.
- The seventeenth day of each month belongs to Srōš.

### Funerary practice

For three days and nights after death, prayers to Sraosha protect the soul. Traditional practice may include the Srōš Yašts, Yasna, and sometimes Vendidad recitations, with fire maintained near the deceased and priests observing the ritual watches. On the dawn of the fourth day, ceremonies correspond to the soul’s journey toward judgment. J. J. Modi’s early twentieth-century documentation records these practices among Parsis ([Religious Ceremonies and Customs of the Parsees](https://avesta.org/ritual/rcc5.htm)).

This is genuine living ritual continuity, though its exact forms vary by Iranian and Parsi community, priestly school, and historical period.

### Offerings and hospitality

*Yasna* 57 portrays a house in which Sraosha is “satiated” and hospitably received. The language supports food offering within ritual, not human sacrifice. Barsman, sacred formulas, libations, ritual bread, and the properly performed sacrifice are his characteristic offerings.

### Violence and contested practices

No source examined documents:

- human sacrifice to Sraosha;
- “temple prostitution” in his cult;
- a Sraosha-specific priesthood conducting coercive conversion;
- cult violence in his name;
- an ancient occult initiation centered on him.

Sraosha’s club, punitive vocabulary, and beating of demons are textual and ritual-symbolic facts. They should not be converted without evidence into claims about human sacrificial violence.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Old Avestan period

The earliest stratum knows *sraoša* as attentive obedience to divine revelation and at least incipiently as an invocable divine being. Absolute chronology remains disputed. Nothing securely connects him with royal institutions, temples, images, or a fixed funerary tribunal.

### Younger Avestan formation

During continuing Old Iranian oral composition, Sraosha acquires a rich personality: primordial priest, embodied sacred word, armed night watchman, protector of settlements, and enemy of Aēšma. The absence of a clear Vedic counterpart leads Malandra to regard him as specifically Iranian. Boyce suggested functional similarities to Vedic Bṛhaspati, but these are structural parallels, not proof of identity.

### Achaemenid period

Royal inscriptions remain silent. If Gāthic and Younger Avestan materials already circulated, Sraosha could have been known outside royal epigraphy, but the evidence cannot show the geographical or institutional reach of his cult. Claims that he appears at Behistun or Naqš-e Rostam are unsupported.

### Arsacid and early regional art

The evidence remains sparse. Possible Chorasmian imagery at Akchakhan-kala would show that Sraosha’s ritual and disciplinary symbolism had monumental expression by the early first century CE, if the recent identification is accepted.

### Sasanian systematization

The Avesta was collected and accompanied by Middle Persian interpretation; Sraosha’s liturgical importance and opposition to Xēšm became part of a systematic cosmic scheme. His funerary protection and material-world sovereignty likely developed substantially in learned priestly exegesis. Because most detailed texts survive only in post-Sasanian copies or compositions, individual doctrines cannot always be dated securely to the Sasanian period.

### Early Islamic centuries

Zoroastrian institutions contracted under Muslim rule, while priests composed or redacted the Bundahišn, Dēnkard, *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*, and *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*. These books preserve and enlarge Srōš’s role. It is misleading either to treat them as fresh Islamic inventions or as verbatim transcripts of pre-Islamic doctrine: they are post-conquest textual witnesses carrying heterogeneous older material.

### Persianate Islam

Sorush survived conspicuously as an angel, messenger, voice from the unseen, and poetic figure. Identification with Gabriel arose because both reveal or announce divine messages. The equation is a cultural translation, not a claim made by the Avesta.

Ferdowsi made Sorush an actor linking Iran’s mythic past to acceptable Islamic angelology. Later poets could use *sorūš-e ḡayb*, “the Sorush/angel of the unseen,” for inspiration or revelation. Hafez, for example, has a heavenly Sorush announce news at dawn and elsewhere an “angel from the unseen world” counsel the poet; Iranica discusses this speaking figure in [Hafez’s poetic art](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hafez-iii/).

### Safavid and Mughal-era esotericism

Illuminationist and Persian esoteric writing integrated Iranian angelic names into Islamic Neoplatonic hierarchies. Suhrawardī’s “Crimson Archangel” is identified with the Active Intellect, Holy Spirit, and Gabriel, but it should not automatically be labeled Sraosha; the resemblance belongs to the broader Persian angelology of revelation. Āẕar Kayvān’s sixteenth–seventeenth-century circle explicitly narrated visions and teachings from the angel Sorūš, an early-modern syncretistic reuse rather than an unbroken Avestan school ([Iranica, Āẕar Kayvān](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/azar-kayvan-priest/)).

### Nineteenth century to present

Zoroastrians in India and Iran continued reciting the Srosh prayers and observing funerary rites. Philological publication transformed Sraosha into an object of comparative religion: Darmesteter, Mills, West, Boyce, Kreyenbroek, Malandra, and others reconstructed the textual history.

Modern Zoroastrian communities continue to regard Sraosha/Srosh as a living yazata. The continuity is strongest in prayer, calendar, and death ritual, not in evidence for a separate temple cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Persian literature and language

**Sorūš** remains a male personal name and a word associated with heavenly messages, inspiration, and Gabriel. Its modern semantic range is the result of medieval Persian development. It does not prove that Islamic theologians formally absorbed the entire Zoroastrian yazata.

### The Báb

The requested thread requires correction. The Báb, Sayyed ʿAlī-Moḥammad of Shiraz (1819–1850), used or received titles including **Bāb** (“Gate”), **Dhikr/Zikr** (“Remembrance”), **Nuqṭa-ye Ūlā** (“Primal Point”), and related Bābī designations. A dedicated search did **not** locate a reliable primary Bābī text or critical academic source establishing **Soroush/Sraosha as a formal title of the Báb**.

Possible sources of confusion include:

- *sorūš* as a general Persian word for a revelatory angel or voice;
- later poetic comparisons of prophets or revealers to Sorush;
- confusion with the title **Dhikr**;
- confusion with modern persons surnamed Soroush.

Until a specific Persian or Arabic text is produced, “the Báb bore the title Soroush” must be labeled **[UNVERIFIED]**, not repeated as fact.

### Occult and neopagan material

Internet occult profiles commonly assign Sraosha planets, colors, crystals, sigils, angelic ranks, Tarot cards, or precise magical “correspondences.” These are not present in the Avesta or Pahlavi corpus unless independently demonstrated. Western ceremonial orders did not give Sraosha a major stable role comparable to Michael, Raphael, or the Amesha Spentas. Most contemporary invocations are eclectic modern compositions.

Zoroastrian reconstructionist or devotional use of authentic Srosh prayers is different from neopagan reinvention. Reliable numerical estimates for a distinct modern “Sraosha cult” do not exist.

### Popular culture

Sraosha has a modest presence in fantasy games, role-playing material, online mythology databases, and Iran-inspired fiction. These frequently emphasize the warrior angel, psychopomp, or club, sometimes conflating him with Gabriel. Such works have not displaced the traditional Persian image as completely as the *Shahnameh* and classical poetry did.

### Political appropriation

Soroush’s name belongs to Iranian cultural memory and appears in personal names, institutions, publishing, broadcasting, and political or intellectual life. This is not necessarily religious invocation. No major modern extremist movement was found that makes Sraosha its defining patron. Claims of such appropriation require organization-specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Abstraction or god?

The central debate begins inside the Gāthās. Some occurrences plainly mean obedience; others appear personalized. A developmental model—common noun becoming god—is possible, but Gāthic poetic language regularly makes abstractions simultaneously divine agents. The evidence does not require a crude “metaphor later mistaken for a deity” theory.

### Zoroaster’s creation or older Iranian god?

Sraosha’s prominence in Younger Avestan ritual and warfare has led scholars to doubt that he was created solely by Zarathustra’s speculation. Yet there is no obvious Vedic Sraosha. Malandra therefore calls him specifically Iranian, while Boyce proposed comparison with Bṛhaspati because both unite priestly speech and martial association. This remains **functional comparison**, not established common identity.

### Meaning of *aṣ̌ya*

The epithet may mean “accompanied by or belonging to Aši/Reward,” as suggested by *Yasna* 43.12. Middle Persian tradition understood it through Aša and rendered Srōš “righteous.” Manichaean Sogdian evidence supports the older connection with Reward. The disagreement materially changes how one reads his earliest theology.

### Dating the bridge function

Modern summaries often call Sraosha “guardian of the Chinvat Bridge” without qualification. The developed tribunal and psychopomp functions are explicit in Pahlavi texts, whereas the Younger Avesta offers only components: protection, hostility to demons, and association with Mithra and Rashnu. The full office is therefore a later canonical elaboration, though it may preserve lost Avestan teaching.

### Dating and transmission of the hymns

*Yasna* 57 resembles a major Yasht and may once have stood in the Yasht collection. *Yasht* 11 may be its replacement, extracted from the Haδōxt Nask. The theory explains duplication and textual form but cannot be proven from an early table of contents. The Yašts themselves accumulated during centuries of oral composition; “date of composition” is consequently a range, not a single event. [Iranica’s survey of the Yašts](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/) emphasizes this oral and heterogeneous character.

### Iconographic debate

Traditional scholarship had no securely identified image of Sraosha. Interpretations of Bāmiyān’s bird-priests and Akchakhan-kala’s crowned figure have changed that discussion, but neither image bears a simple label “Sraosha.” Iconographic identification depends on ritual objects, cock/bird symbolism, setting, and comparison with texts.

### Primary evidence lost or dispersed

The Sasanian Avesta and much of its exegesis are lost. Surviving Avestan and Pahlavi manuscripts are dispersed among libraries in India, Iran, Europe, and North America. Destruction, material decay, liturgical selection, and copying history created a highly uneven record. The survival of *Yasna* 57 inside the living liturgy makes its textual base stronger than many lost-nask traditions.

### Kreyenbroek and later study

G. Kreyenbroek’s *Sraoša in the Zoroastrian Tradition* (1985) remains the principal monographic collection and interpretation of the Avestan and Pahlavi evidence. A scholarly review describes it as methodologically pioneering and nearly exhaustive for those corpora ([Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhr_0035-1423_1988_num_205_1_1939)). Malandra’s 2014 Iranica article builds substantially upon it.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Sraosha was one of the six or seven Amesha Spentas: **not the normal Avestan classification**.
- He is simply the Zoroastrian Gabriel: **late functional equation**, not original identity.
- He appears explicitly in Achaemenid inscriptions: **unsupported**.
- Every winged male figure in Iranian art is Sraosha: **methodologically invalid**.
- Roman Mithraists worshipped Sraosha as a regular mystery god: **no secure evidence**.
- His cock symbolism proves a universal Indo-European solar god: **overextended comparison**.
- He bears a fixed ancient sigil, planet, crystal, color, or Tarot correspondence: **modern occult invention**.
- He was formally titled or incarnated as the Báb: **not established by the sources located**.
- The psychopomp tribunal is fully stated in the Gāthās: **anachronistic projection**.
