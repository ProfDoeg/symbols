# Khshathra Vairya: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Younger Avestan and later Zoroastrian tradition, a male *Aməša Spənta* (“Beneficent Immortal”), one of the six great divine powers surrounding Ahura Mazdā and, with Ahura Mazdā or Spənta Mainyu, forming a heptad. “Archangel” is a conventional late-antique/modern translation, not an Avestan classification. In the oldest texts, *xšaθra* is primarily an abstract but personifiable divine power—dominion, command, effective rule—rather than an independently characterized god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The common noun and divine concept *xšaθra-* occurs repeatedly in the Old Avestan Gāthās, conventionally placed around 1000 BCE but not securely datable to a particular year or century. The distinct compound name **Xšaθra Vairya**, “Dominion Worth Choosing/Desirable Dominion,” is not securely present there as the name of an individual being. It becomes explicit in the Younger Avesta, whose older portions may be broadly Achaemenid-period in linguistic composition but survive only through a much later, originally oral and then Sasanian manuscript tradition. This distinction—Gāthic *xšaθra* versus Younger Avestan Xšaθra Vairya—is fundamental.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early:** Old Avestan or comparably early evidence.
- **[YA] Younger Avestan canonical:** explicit in the Younger Avesta.
- **[LS] Later Zoroastrian systematization:** chiefly Middle Persian/Pahlavi books.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically localized form or representation.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification:** a historical equation or adaptation across traditions.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or popular custom.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction or inference.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern claim.**

Dates assigned to oral Avestan compositions are philological estimates, not manuscript dates. The extant Avesta descends from a Sasanian written archetype, conventionally dated to about the fourth century CE, and a later common “base manuscript,” probably of the ninth or tenth century; the oldest surviving Avestan codices are medieval. [The Avesta’s transmission history is surveyed by Jean Kellens in *Encyclopaedia Iranica*.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[EA]** In the Gāthās, *xšaθra* denotes Ahura Mazdā’s effective dominion and the good rule that humans should choose and help establish. It can mean power, command, authority, kingdom, or the realized social and cosmic order of the Wise Lord. The language sometimes approaches personification, but the hymns do not supply Xšaθra with a biography, body, genealogy, temple, or discrete mythic career.

**[YA]** In the Younger Avesta, **Xšaθra Vairya** has become one of the named Aməša Spəntas, regularly enumerated after Vohu Manah and Aša Vahišta. He remains the divine embodiment of legitimate or desirable rule but also acquires a concrete material association with metal.

**[LS]** Middle Persian theology calls him **Šahrewar** and develops his offices as guardian of metals, patron of rightful sovereignty, intercessor for the poor, and antagonist of oppressive rule. At the renovation of the world he is connected, directly in one source and indirectly in another, with the river of molten metal that purifies creation.

### Forms of the name

- **Avestan:** *Xšaθra Vairiia* or normalized *Xšaθra Vairya*; in Avestan script, approximately **𐬑𐬱𐬀𐬚𐬭𐬀 𐬬𐬀𐬌𐬭𐬌𐬌𐬀**.
- **Middle Persian:** *Šahrewar*, *Šahrēwar*, also scholarly *Šahrevar*; inscriptional Pahlavi spellings are heterographic and cannot be represented by one universally normalized character string.
- **Parthian:** *šhrywr*, documented as the calendar name.
- **New Persian:** **شهریور**, *Šahrivar/Shahrīvar*, now principally the name of the sixth month of the Iranian Solar Hijri calendar.
- **Bactrian on Kushan coins:** **ϷΑΟΡΗΟΡΟ**, normally read *Šaorēoro/Shaoreoro* and widely identified with Šahrewar.
- Older English spellings include *Khshathra Vairya*, *Kshathra Vairya*, *Khshathravairya*, *Shahrevar*, and *Shahrivar*.

### Etymology

**[EA/MSR]** Avestan *xšaθra-* is cognate with Vedic Sanskrit *kṣatrá-* (“dominion, ruling power”) and belongs to the same Iranian word-family as Old Persian *xšāyaθiya-* (“king”) and ultimately Persian *šāh*. It does not itself mean a hereditary Indian *kṣatriya* caste.

*Vairya-* is a verbal adjective from the root “choose,” hence “to be chosen,” “choice-worthy,” “desirable,” or “excellent.” Defensible translations include:

- “Dominion Worth Choosing”
- “Desirable Dominion”
- “Choice Rule”
- “Best/Ideal Sovereignty”
- older “Wished-for Kingdom”

The Middle Persian exegetical phrase is **xwadāyīh ī pad kāmag**, sovereignty “according to will/desire.” William Malandra stresses that the literal sense is “dominion to be chosen,” with “desirable/best dominion” as freer renderings. [His *ŠAHREWAR* article collects the Avestan and Pahlavi evidence.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sahrewar/)

### Related Gāthic expressions

**[EA]** These expressions are related but should not automatically be treated as personal names:

- *xšaθra-* — dominion, rule, power;
- *vohu xšaθra-* — good dominion;
- *xšaθra ištoiš* — dominion of desire/what is desired;
- *xšaθra vairya-* — a juxtaposition or compound rendered “dominion worth choosing.”

Older writers, notably Maneckji Dhalla, treated every such expression as an early name of the later archangel. Malandra’s stricter philological conclusion is that the entity **called** Xšaθra Vairya is absent from the Gāthās even though the words and theological raw material are present. Compare [Dhalla’s older reconstruction](https://avesta.org/dhalla/dhalla1.htm) with [Malandra’s reassessment](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sahrewar/).

### Classification and gender

**[YA/LS]** Xšaθra Vairya belongs to the highest created or immanent divine powers of Ahura Mazdā. Whether the Aməša Spəntas should be called attributes, aspects, emanations, hypostases, or gods depends partly on period and theological standpoint. The Avesta worships or venerates them as divine recipients of ritual, while later theological exposition can emphasize their inseparability from Ahura Mazdā.

The Avestan noun *xšaθra-* is grammatically neuter. Later tradition normally treats Šahrewar as male. Consequently, modern artistic descriptions of him as a conventionally male “archangel” belong to later personification, not to a Gāthic physical portrait.

### Classical identification

**[SY]** Plutarch, writing around 100 CE, reports that Oromazes made six gods: Goodwill, Truth, Good Order/Lawfulness, Wisdom, Wealth, and Pleasure in good things. These are generally understood as a Greek interpretive rendering of the six Aməša Spəntas. “Good Order” is the most natural position for Xšaθra Vairya, although some mappings vary because Plutarch supplies Greek abstractions rather than Iranian names. This is evidence that a sixfold Iranian scheme was known to a Greek author; it is not evidence that Iranians called Šahrewar a Greek god. [Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 47.](https://sacred-texts.com/cla/plu/pte/pte04.htm)

Herodotus, in the fifth century BCE, does **not** name Xšaθra Vairya. His “Zeus” identified with the entire firmament is usually taken as an interpretatio of Ahura Mazdā or an Iranian sky-god, not a secure equation with Xšaθra. [Herodotus’s evidence and its limitations are reviewed here.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/herodotus-iii/)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Old Avestan foundation

**[EA]** The Gāthās—Yasna 28–34, 43–51, and 53—are in Old Avestan. Their composition is often placed around 1000 BCE, though proposals range more widely and no exact date is demonstrable. Their preservation is liturgical and oral; there is no manuscript remotely contemporary with composition.

Important *xšaθra* passages include:

- **Yasna 28.3:** Zarathustra addresses Ahura Mazdā, Aša, and Vohu Manah and seeks access to divine rule.
- **Yasna 30.7:** Ārmaiti comes with *xšaθra*, Vohu Manah, and Aša in a densely personifying formulation.
- **Yasna 31.6, 31.22:** good dominion belongs to the wise or righteous order.
- **Yasna 32.7:** a difficult passage associates judgment with glowing or molten metal; its precise syntax remains disputed.
- **Yasna 34.4:** Ahura Mazdā’s fire, strong through Aša, visibly apportions good and harm.
- **Yasna 34.11:** Haurvatāt and Amərətāt, Ārmaiti, Aša, good thought, and enduring dominion appear together.
- **Yasna 43.1, 43.8:** the worshipper seeks power or dominion through righteousness.
- **Yasna 44.9:** asks how Ārmaiti may come with dominion.
- **Yasna 48.8:** invokes good dominion.
- **Yasna 51.1–2:** *vohu xšaθra* is the opening expression from which the Gāthā takes its traditional name.
- **Yasna 51.9:** another notoriously difficult metal/judgment passage helped later exegetes connect dominion, metal, and ordeal.

**Finding:** The Gāthās securely attest the theological concept and perhaps incipient hypostasis. They do not securely attest a separate guardian of terrestrial metals named Xšaθra Vairya.

### Yasna Haptaŋhāiti

**[EA]** Yasna 35–41 is Old Avestan prose and linguistically comparable in antiquity to the Gāthās. Yasna 41.2 asks for Ahura Mazdā’s everlasting or enduring dominion. Again, this is early evidence for the divine kingdom, not a narrative appearance by an individualized metal-god.

### Younger Avesta

**[YA]** Here the compound becomes an unequivocal divine name.

- **Yasna 1 and parallel invitational lists:** Xšaθra Vairya is invoked among the Aməša Spəntas.
- **Yasna 16.3:** named in the liturgical enumeration.
- **Yašt 1.25:** invoked as one of Ahura Mazdā’s great beneficent powers.
- **Yašt 13.2:** the sky is described in metallic terms; this became crucial to later explanations of why the guardian of metal is also guardian of the sky.
- **Mihr Yašt, Yašt 10.125:** *xšaθra vairya* appears in a difficult description of a metallic hook or fitting on Mithra’s chariot; some scholars take the phrase as a gloss meaning simply “metal.”
- **Vidēvdād 9.10; 17.6:** the expression denotes a sharp metal implement used to trace ritual furrows.
- **Vidēvdād 16.6:** it appears as a generic or quasi-generic designation for metal, including an interpretive gloss calling iron or lead the basest metal.
- **Sīrōza 1.4 and 2.4:** the fourth day is dedicated to Xšaθra Vairya.
- **Āfrīnagān 3.9:** associated with the sixth month.
- **Visperad 20.1:** worship is offered to *Vohu Xšaθra*, Xšaθra Vairya, metal-working or metal-founding, and the correctly spoken victorious words. [Mills’s translation preserves this liturgical cluster.](https://www.avesta.org/visperad/vr_sbe.pdf)

**Textual caution:** These passages demonstrate both divine invocation and lexical slippage: the divine name could become a learned term for “metal.” Context must determine whether a passage names the being, his material creation, or a metallic object.

### The Avestan manuscript record

**[MSR]** The Avestan material was transmitted orally for centuries. A special phonetic alphabet and written canon were established under the Sasanians, conventionally in the fourth century CE. All surviving manuscripts descend through a later common ancestor. Important codices include Copenhagen K7a, perhaps 1268; K5, dated 1323; and K1, dated 1324. [The Copenhagen manuscript evidence is catalogued by *Encyclopaedia Iranica*.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/codices-hafnienses/)

The interval between composition and manuscript is therefore enormous. The antiquity of Old Avestan is established principally through comparative linguistics, meter, and its relationship with Vedic Sanskrit—not by early physical manuscripts.

### Middle Persian/Pahlavi corpus

#### *Bundahišn*

**[LS]** The *Bundahišn* is a post-Sasanian Middle Persian compilation built from Zand and older cosmological learning. It survives in Iranian/Greater and Indian recensions and accumulated material between the early Islamic period and roughly the twelfth century.

Its principal Šahrewar materials are:

- the fourth spirit takes metal, derived from the sky, as his creation;
- his name is interpreted as kingship “according to will”;
- weapons made from metal make earthly rule effective;
- he intercedes before Ohrmazd for deserving poor people;
- treating metal well pleases him, mistreating it distresses him;
- royal basil is his flower;
- his antagonist is Sāwul, the demon of bad rule;
- at the renovation Fire and Ērmān/Airyaman melt the metals of the mountains into a river;
- Šahrewar then seizes or defeats Sāwul.

The [Greater *Bundahišn* translation](https://www.avesta.org/mp/grb12.htm) explicitly states: “Metal is Shahrewar’s own material.”

#### Pahlavi Rivāyat accompanying the *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*

**[LS]** This source assigns Šahrewar a more direct eschatological role than does the *Bundahišn*: he melts the metal of all the mountains to mouth-height and ultimately pours molten metal into the breach through which the demons entered creation. This is a genuine source disagreement, not merely two descriptions of an identical act.

#### *Šāyest nē Šāyest* and supplementary texts

**[LS]** The supplementary material says one pleases Šahrewar through proper care for metals: keeping the heart so pure that molten metal would not burn it, avoiding sinful uses of metal, and not giving gold and silver to the wicked. It connects this teaching to the ordeal reportedly undergone by the high priest Ādurbād ī Mahraspandān. [The translated passage is available here.](https://www.avesta.org/mp/shayest.html)

#### *Dēnkard*

**[LS]** *Dēnkard* Book 3 and Book 4 present Šahrewar within a hierarchical theological order: Ohrmazd, Vohuman, Ardwahišt, and then Šahrewar. He serves Ardwahišt, presides over metals, and enables wealth, generosity, religious propagation, and good governance. A righteous ruler should imitate Vohuman, Ardwahišt, and Šahrewar through good thought, purity, and material capacity. [*Dēnkard* Book 4 translation.](https://www.avesta.org/denkard/dk4.html)

#### *Mēnōg ī Xrad*

**[LS]** The sixth-century core or compilation conventionally assigned to the *Mēnōg ī Xrad* provides the ethical and political world in which good rule is valued, but Šahrewar has little independent narrative presence. Its praise of the innocent wise poor over an ignorant wealthy king is consonant with Šahrewar’s later intercession for the poor, but it should not be misreported as a myth in which Šahrewar personally appears. [Text and introduction.](https://avesta.org/mp/mx.html)

#### *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*

**[LS]** The surviving vision-text describes heaven, hell, judgment, and the rewards or punishments of deeds. It does not supply a distinct major Šahrewar episode. General invocations of Ohrmazd and the Amahraspands are not evidence of a separate interaction unless Šahrewar is expressly identified.

### Achaemenid inscriptions

**[EA-context, not an attestation of the being]** Behistun and the royal tomb inscriptions at Naqš-e Rostam repeatedly assert that Darius rules “by the favor of Ahuramazdā.” Old Persian *xšaça-* means empire, dominion, or sovereignty and is etymologically related to Avestan *xšaθra*. These inscriptions therefore document the political vocabulary that illuminates the Gāthic concept.

They do **not**, however, name Xšaθra Vairya as a deity. Turning Darius’s kingship into proof of an official cult of Šahrewar is an inference. Achaemenid royal theology may be compared with desirable dominion, but direct institutional continuity cannot be demonstrated merely from the cognate noun. [The Achaemenid kingship evidence is surveyed here.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kingship/kingship-ii-in-the-achaemenid-empire/)

### Greek and Roman witnesses

- **Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131–140, fifth century BCE:** no Xšaθra Vairya by name.
- **Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 46–47, ca. 100 CE:** Greek abstractional paraphrase of Oromazes’ six gods, probably including Xšaθra Vairya as order/lawful rule.
- No securely attributable Greco-Roman temple dedication to Xšaθra Vairya is known.

### Kushan material

**[RV/SY]** Coins of the Kushan ruler Huvishka, conventionally dated about 150–190 CE, depict a labeled deity **ϷΑΟΡΗΟΡΟ** (*Shaoreoro*). The standard identification is Šahrewar/Xšaθra Vairya. The figure is armed—standing with spear and shield in some types—and presents divine or ideal imperial power in a Bactrian, Kushan, and Hellenizing visual idiom.

This is the earliest securely labeled surviving image normally assigned to the being. It is regional and politically charged: not evidence that western Iranian Zoroastrians everywhere pictured Šahrewar this way. The Kushan pantheon is largely Iranian in conception but iconographically eclectic; “syncretic empire” is more accurate than treating every image as evidence of a single standardized Zoroastrian church. [Robert Bracey’s survey includes Shaoreoro among the important Kushan coin deities.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kushan-dynasty-04/) A published specimen describes Shaoreoro with spear and shield. [CNG coin record.](https://www.cngcoins.com/Coin.aspx?CoinID=121623)

### Manichaean evidence

**[SY]** Third- and later-century Manichaean translators used Middle Persian plural *(a)mahraspandān* for Mani’s five light elements—ether, wind, light, water, and fire. This is a borrowing of Zoroastrian divine-class vocabulary, not evidence that Mani adopted Šahrewar as an individual being. No securely documented independent Manichaean cult or myth of Khshathra Vairya was found.

### Mithraic evidence

No secure inscription, relief, or liturgy from Roman Mithraism names Khshathra Vairya. Mithra’s Avestan chariot has a possibly metallic *xšaθra vairya* fitting, and Šahrewar’s metal has Mithra among its cooperating divinities in the *Bundahišn*. These do not establish a separate Šahrewar cult in Roman Mithraea.

### *Šāhnāma*

**[MA/absence finding]** Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed around 1010 CE, is central to the reception of Iranian kingship, royal glory, justice, and the struggle against oppressive rulers. It does not preserve Khshathra Vairya/Šahrewar as an independently acting epic character. Connecting every just king in the poem directly with the Amahraspand is theological interpretation, not an explicit statement by Ferdowsi.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Desirable dominion

**[EA]** The earliest domain is not “metal” but good, divinely authorized dominion: Ahura Mazdā’s effective rule and the human choice to embody it. It includes the power to realize Aša in society, not mere coercive strength.

**[LS]** Later texts distinguish rightful sovereignty from tyranny. Šahrewar consequently represents:

- legitimate kingship;
- capacity to govern;
- protection through properly used weapons;
- wealth used for generosity;
- care for the poor and politically weak;
- final victory over oppressive rule.

Modern renderings such as “democracy,” “socialism,” or “divine right of kings” over-specify the evidence. The concept can support ethical limitations on power, but none of those modern political systems is its literal Avestan meaning.

### Metal

**[YA/LS]** Metal becomes Šahrewar’s characteristic material creation. It signifies several linked capacities:

- weapons defending the good creation;
- royal and social power;
- money that can relieve poverty;
- useful utensils and ornaments;
- judicial ordeal;
- the eschatological medium of universal purification.

In several Vidēvdād passages the divine name has become nearly a technical synonym for metal. This lexical development shows how fully the being and his material sphere were identified.

### Sky

**[YA/LS/MSR]** The Younger Avestan sky can be metallic, while Yasna 30.5 calls it “of hardest stone.” Pahlavi cosmology sometimes calls it crystal. R. C. Zaehner and Mary Boyce reconstructed a historical sequence: an early stone sky and stone weapons; later bronze or iron weapons; then priestly classification of crystal as both stone and metal, allowing the old sky and new metal correspondences to coexist.

That reconstruction is ingenious but not itself stated as history in an ancient source. What is securely attested is:

1. a Gāthic hard-stone sky;
2. a Younger Avestan/Pahlavi metallic sky;
3. Pahlavi crystal terminology;
4. Šahrewar’s guardianship of metal.

Boyce’s synthesis is set out in the [*Aməša Spənta* article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amesa-spenta-beneficent-divinity/).

### Weapons and regalia

**[LS]** Metal weapons are legitimate when they defend Aša and the good creation. The *Bundahišn* speaks of a well-sheathed mace directed at the demons and of the metallic weapons of Mithra and the Sun.

**[RV]** Kushan Shaoreoro carries spear and shield. Some coin descriptions also give him a mace-scepter. These are the only reasonably secure ancient visual attributes specifically fixed by a label.

### Flower, body-part, color, day, and month

- **Royal basil, *šāhesprahm*:** Šahrewar’s flower in the *Bundahišn*. **[LS]**
- **Spleen:** his sacrificial portion in the Pahlavi Rivāyat. **[LS]**
- **Fourth day:** Xšaθra Vairya’s day in the Zoroastrian thirty-day calendar. **[YA/continuing cult]**
- **Sixth month:** Šahrewar/Shahrivar. **[YA/LS/continuing calendar]**
- **Color:** Some modern Zoroastrian or esoteric tables assign royal purple, blue, or red. No single color was found securely fixed by the early Avesta. A red association in twentieth-century Khshnoom literature is modern esoteric systematization, not ancient evidence. **[MI]**
- **Number:** Four follows from the calendar day and usual position in a sequence that includes Ohrmazd; “three” follows lists counting only the six created Aməša Spəntas. Neither establishes an occult sacred number independent of those enumerations.
- **Planet:** No securely ancient planetary identity was established.
- **Animal:** No securely attested animal belongs uniquely to Šahrewar.

### Physical form and gendered imagery

Apart from Kushan coins, the tradition supplies no stable anthropomorphic canon. Modern pictures of a winged, bearded, armored angel are largely extrapolations from generic angelic, Sasanian, or fantasy imagery. The famous Achaemenid winged disk or winged royal figure is not securely an image of Khshathra Vairya.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Genealogy

Xšaθra Vairya has no ordinary birth myth, parents, consort, or offspring.

**[YA/LS]** He is one of the Aməša Spəntas of Ahura Mazdā. Sources can speak of these beings as created by Ahura Mazdā, belonging to him, or manifesting his own powers. Calling them Ahura Mazdā’s literal biological “children” is misleading unless clearly marked as metaphor.

The usual order is:

1. Ahura Mazdā/Spənta Mainyu;
2. Vohu Manah;
3. Aša Vahišta;
4. Xšaθra Vairya;
5. Spənta Ārmaiti;
6. Haurvatāt;
7. Amərətāt.

Lists that omit Ahura Mazdā call Xšaθra the “third” Aməša Spənta; Middle Persian hierarchical descriptions that include Ohrmazd call Šahrewar fourth.

### Every documented named divine interaction

Because the sources are liturgical and systematic rather than narrative, many “interactions” are joint invocations or cosmological relations.

- **Ahura Mazdā — embodiment/recipient of his dominion.** *Gāthās*, especially Y. 28.3, 31, 34, 43, 48, 51; ca. 1000 BCE conventional dating. **[EA]**
- **Ahura Mazdā — created or established within his divine order; Šahrewar later intercedes before him for the deserving poor.** *Bundahišn* 26.57; post-Sasanian compilation. **[LS]**
- **Vohu Manah — joint appearance with xšaθra as good thought enables good dominion.** Y. 28.3, 30.7, 34.11 and related Gāthic passages. **[EA]**
- **Vohu Manah — hierarchical superior/predecessor in creation.** *Dēnkard* Book 4; ninth-century recension preserving older material. **[LS]**
- **Aša Vahišta — dominion is realized through truth/order; joint Gāthic invocation.** Y. 28.3, 34.4, 34.11. **[EA]**
- **Aša Vahišta/Ardwahišt — Šahrewar is described as Ardwahišt’s obedient servant.** *Dēnkard* Book 4. **[LS]**
- **Spənta Ārmaiti — appears with dominion, and humans seek her aid in acquiring it.** Y. 30.7, 44.9. **[EA]**
- **Haurvatāt and Amərətāt — jointly named with enduring dominion and the other powers.** Y. 34.11; also later liturgical lists. **[EA/YA]**
- **All Aməša Spəntas — collective invocation and membership in the heptad.** Younger Avestan Yasna, Yašt 1, Sīrōza, and Visperad lists. **[YA]**
- **Mithra/Mihr — a metal fitting on Mithra’s chariot is called *xšaθra vairya*; in the *Bundahišn*, Mihr cooperates with Šahrewar’s metal.** Yt. 10.125; *Bundahišn* 26.59–62. **[YA/LS]**
- **Hvare Xšaēta/Khwar, the Sun — cooperates with Šahrewar’s metal and keeps metallic weapons at Mount Alburz.** *Bundahišn* 26.59–62. **[LS]**
- **Āsmān, Sky — cooperates with Šahrewar’s material sphere; metal itself is derived from the sky.** *Bundahišn* creation account and 26.62. **[LS]**
- **Anagrān/Anagran, Endless Lights — cooperates with Šahrewar’s metal.** *Bundahišn* 26.62. **[LS]**
- **Ātaxš/Fire — melts the mountain metals for the final river.** *Bundahišn* 34.18. **[LS]**
- **Airyaman/Ērmān — together with Fire melts the metal at the renovation.** *Bundahišn* 34.18. **[LS]**
- **Sāwul/Sāwar — demonic antagonist embodying evil rule, oppression, violence, and tyranny; seized by Šahrewar at the renovation.** *Bundahišn* 27.7, 34.27–29. **[LS]**
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — Šahrewar participates in the ordered defeat of Ahriman’s host; direct single combat is not assigned to him.** *Bundahišn* 34.27–31. **[LS]**
- **The demons collectively — Šahrewar pours molten metal into their breach in the Pahlavi Rivāyat.** PRDD 48.86. **[LS variant]**
- **Gōzihr/Gōčihr — the serpent is burned in the same molten metal that purifies hell, but the *Bundahišn* does not describe a direct meeting with Šahrewar.** *Bundahišn* 34.31. **[LS; indirect association only]**

No textual meeting, marriage, seduction, theft, or rivalry with Anāhitā, Tīr/Tištrya, Haoma, or Verethragna was established. Membership in one pantheon is not itself an interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Khshathra Vairya has no independent heroic cycle comparable to Mithra’s battles or Yima’s enclosure. His “myths” are cosmological and eschatological episodes.

### The creation and guardianship of metal

**[LS]** In the *Bundahišn*, Ohrmazd creates the spiritual beings and material creation in ordered stages. Šahrewar, the fourth spirit in the stated hierarchy, accepts metal derived from the sky as his particular creation. Metal is not inert property: human treatment of it affects its guardian. Clean, useful, generous, and righteous deployment pleases Šahrewar; abuse of metal and wealth distresses him.

The myth makes metal the physical form of desirable power. Weapons can defend creation, coin and treasure can support the poor, and worked metal can serve civilized life. The same material can also be abused by tyrants, explaining Šahrewar’s opposition to Sāwul.

**Development:** The metal guardianship is absent from the Gāthās, lexically visible in Younger Avestan ritual language, and fully explained in Pahlavi cosmology. It is therefore a later canonical elaboration rooted in, but not identical with, Gāthic dominion.

### The metallic sky

**[YA/LS]** Yašt 13.2 and Pahlavi cosmology describe the sky as metal or metal-like. Šahrewar can consequently be guardian of both earthly metals and the hard celestial shell.

**Variant:** Yasna 30.5 calls the sky hardest stone; Middle Persian texts can call it crystal. The doctrine was not transmitted in one materially consistent formulation. Later scholars’ evolutionary model—stone, crystal, metal—is a reconstruction, not a narrated ancient myth.

### The molten-metal ordeal

#### Gāthic antecedent

**[EA/disputed]** Yasna 32.7, 34.4, 47.6, and 51.9 contain difficult references to fiery or molten metal and divine judgment. Some translators find an ordeal already envisioned; others see punishment, testing, or a metal instrument without the later universal river. The complete Pahlavi scenario should not be silently inserted into these verses.

#### *Bundahišn* version

**[LS]** At the *Frašegird*, Fire and Airyaman melt the metal in the hills and mountains. It flows over the earth like a river. Every resurrected person must pass through it:

- to the righteous, it feels like warm milk;
- to the wicked, it feels like actual molten metal;
- nevertheless the process ultimately purifies creation;
- human beings are reconciled and praise Ohrmazd and the Amahraspands;
- the molten metal burns pollution from hell;
- Ahriman and Āz are driven back through the breach by which evil entered.

Here Šahrewar owns the metal, but Fire and Airyaman are the stated melting agents. [The translated *Bundahišn* passage is available here.](https://avesta.org/mp/bundahis.html)

#### Pahlavi Rivāyat version

**[LS variant]** Šahrewar himself melts the mountains’ metal, raising it to mouth-height, and later releases it into the demons’ entry-hole. This gives him a direct executive role lacking from the corresponding *Bundahišn* sentences.

The discrepancy shows that “Šahrewar pours the final river” is true of one Pahlavi tradition but not the only canonical formulation.

### Defeat of Sāwul

**[LS]** In the final paired defeat of evil powers, Ohrmazd overcomes the Evil Spirit, Vohuman overcomes Akoman, Ardwahišt overcomes Indar, Šahrewar overcomes Sāwul/Sāwar, Spandarmad overcomes Tarōmat/Nāŋhaiθya, Hordād and Amurdād overcome Tairēw and Zairič, and Srōš overcomes Ēšm.

This is not a detailed combat narrative. It is a systematic eschatological pairing: desirable rule defeats oppressive rule.

### Ādurbād’s ordeal

**[LS/hagiographic]** Later tradition says the Sasanian high priest Ādurbād ī Mahraspandān proved doctrinal truth by having molten metal poured upon his chest; it felt like warm milk because he was righteous. The story exemplifies Šahrewar’s metal and anticipates the final ordeal. It is hagiography preserved in late sources, not contemporary documentation of the event.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Temples and cult centers

No temple securely identifiable as dedicated solely to Xšaθra Vairya or Šahrewar is known. Zoroastrian worship centers on fire temples, domestic observance, calendrical rites, and composite invocation of Ahura Mazdā and the yazatas. A separate priesthood of Šahrewar is unattested.

Herodotus’s claim that Persians had no images, temples, or altars reflects a Greek outsider’s description of fifth-century Persian practice and cannot be generalized to all later Zoroastrian history. It nevertheless accords with the lack of an early temple-image cult for this highly abstract being.

### Liturgical worship

**[YA/continuing]** Xšaθra Vairya receives *yazamaide*, “we worship/sacrifice to,” in Avestan enumerations. The fourth day of each thirty-day month is his; when the day-name and month-name coincide, the occasion is Šahrewaragān/Shahrivargān.

Visperad 20 joins worship of desirable dominion with metal-working and victorious sacred speech. Later ritual manuals prescribe a *bāj* or dedicatory formula in the name of Šahrewar and touch metal ritual implements, including the barsom apparatus.

### Care of metals

**[LS]** Religious care includes:

- keeping metal clean and bright;
- using it for beneficial implements;
- using weapons only in defense of the good order;
- using money in charity;
- avoiding sinful destruction or pollution involving metal;
- not transferring wealth to wicked persons;
- contemplating personal purity through the image of molten-metal ordeal.

These are textual norms. Evidence for how consistently ordinary communities observed each prescription is limited.

### Offerings

**[LS]** The Pahlavi Rivāyat gives the spleen as Šahrewar’s sacrificial share. Royal basil is his flower. Later ritual descriptions report basil used when an Āfrīnagān is celebrated in his honor. These practices belong to developed priestly ritual, not the Gāthās.

### Festival: Šahrewaragān

**[MA/F/continuing]** Al-Bīrūnī, around 1000 CE, records Šahrīvaragān or Āḏar-jašn on the fourth day of Šahrīvar, including a fire-festival association and a regional calendrical connection with the beginning of winter in Tokharistan. The festival’s form varied with calendar systems and region. [The historical evidence is discussed in the *Sada Festival* article.](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sada-festival/)

Modern Iranian cultural presentations sometimes characterize Shahrivargan as a universal ancient “day of miners,” “day of fathers,” or festival of love, justice, and metallurgy. These may be reasonable contemporary reinterpretations, but their precise ceremonies are not all anciently documented.

### Sacrifice and contested allegations

No evidence was found for human sacrifice to Khshathra Vairya. The molten-metal ordeal is an eschatological and judicial motif, not evidence of a recurring human-sacrifice cult.

The Ādurbād story describes an ordeal performed on a living priest, but it is late hagiography and cannot establish a routine historical practice. No evidence associates Šahrewar with temple prostitution.

## 7. Historical evolution

### 1. Indo-Iranian background

**[MSR]** Proto-Indo-Iranian *kšatram* denoted ruling capacity or dominion. Vedic *kṣatra* belongs to the sphere of rulers and warrior power. This linguistic inheritance explains the Iranian word but does not prove a prehistoric god named Xšaθra Vairya.

### 2. Old Avestan period, conventionally ca. 1200–900 BCE

**[EA]** *Xšaθra* is Ahura Mazdā’s desired and effective rule. The ethical question is who truly exercises power: the righteous who choose Aša or rulers aligned with deceit. No guardian of metals or individualized mythic figure is securely present.

### 3. Younger Avestan formation, roughly first millennium BCE

**[YA]** The compound becomes a fixed divine name within the sixfold/heptadic system. Xšaθra Vairya gains calendar positions and liturgical invocation. Metal terminology begins to merge with the name.

The exact date is debated. “Achaemenid-period” is a linguistic-cultural approximation for portions of the Younger Avesta, not proof that a surviving passage was composed under a named king.

### 4. Achaemenid Empire, 550–330 BCE

**[EA-context]** Royal inscriptions display Ahuramazdian kingship and use cognate vocabulary for dominion. They do not name Xšaθra Vairya. The claim that Cyrus, Darius, or Xerxes personally followed a specific “Khshathra Vairya doctrine” goes beyond the documentation.

Herodotus supplies no named attestation. The absence is significant because it prevents direct reconstruction of a state cult.

### 5. Hellenistic and Parthian eras

Direct western-Iranian evidence remains sparse. Greek interpretatio gradually makes Iranian abstract divinities intelligible as personified ethical gods.

### 6. Kushan period, second century CE

**[RV/SY]** Huvishka’s labeled Shaoreoro coins create the clearest ancient anthropomorphic image. The armed deity expresses imperial might in a multicultural royal coinage. This is the strongest material evidence for a regional public representation of Šahrewar.

### 7. Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

**[LS]** The Avesta is codified in a special script; priestly theology closely connects orthodoxy and royal authority. Middle Persian exegesis elaborates correspondences among divine powers, creations, virtues, demons, and eschatological roles.

Šahrewar’s metal, weapons, royal capacity, charity, and opposition to bad rule all suit Sasanian political theology, but no securely identified Sasanian freestanding image of him survives. The Ādurbād ordeal belongs to the tradition of Sasanian orthodoxy, though its historical factuality cannot be verified.

### 8. Early Islamic Iran, seventh–tenth centuries

**[LS/MA]** Zoroastrian political power contracts. Priests compile and preserve Middle Persian learning in the *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, Rivāyats, and related books. Šahrewar becomes more fully the guardian of metal and eschatological opponent of tyranny while the older abstract “divine kingdom” recedes.

The conquest and gradual Islamization weakened institutions and manuscript transmission, but there is no documented suppression campaign aimed uniquely at Šahrewar.

### 9. Medieval Persianate culture

**[MA]** The name survives strongly in the calendar and month-name *Šahrīvar*. Ferdowsi preserves Iranian ideals of righteous and tyrannical kingship without restoring Šahrewar as an epic personality. Al-Bīrūnī documents the festival.

### 10. Parsi transmission and European philology

From the medieval period onward, communities in Iran and India preserved the Avestan and Pahlavi traditions. European scholarship moved through several stages:

- Anquetil-Duperron’s eighteenth-century access to Parsi manuscripts;
- nineteenth-century editions and translations by Westergaard, Geldner, Darmesteter, and Mills;
- comparative “Vedic” philology;
- twentieth-century work by Lommel, Bailey, Zaehner, Boyce, Narten, Humbach, Kellens, Malandra, and Skjærvø;
- twenty-first-century manuscript imaging and digital collation.

### 11. Present

Šahrewar remains a living calendar name, liturgical recipient, and theological concept among Zoroastrians. Modern believers variously emphasize:

- a personal Aməša Spənta;
- an aspect or attribute of Ahura Mazdā;
- the ethical ideal of benevolent government;
- environmental and moral care for metals and mineral resources.

These interpretations coexist; there is no single worldwide Zoroastrian magisterium fixing one ontology.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Translation as “archangel”

**[MA/MSR]** “Archangel” became common through comparison with Jewish, Christian, and Islamic angelologies. It usefully signals exalted subordinate divinity but imports an Abrahamic category. “Amesha Spenta,” “Beneficent Immortal,” or “divine hypostasis” is historically safer.

### Influence on the “Kingdom of God”

**[MSR/contested]** Some popular Iranian-nationalist and comparative-religion sites claim that Jewish *malkut*, Aramaic *malkuta*, and Greek *basileia* are direct translations or borrowings from Xšaθra Vairya and that Judaism was effectively created under the Achaemenids. Contact and possible Iranian influence on Second Temple thought are legitimate research questions, but no direct textual chain proves that the phrase “Kingdom of God” was simply borrowed from this specific Aməša Spənta. Presented as certainty, the claim is overreach.

### National and political use

**[MI where universalized]** Modern writers frequently invoke Khshathra Vairya as ancient proof of constitutional monarchy, liberal democracy, socialism, human rights, environmentalism, or specifically Iranian benevolent empire. The ancient sources do condemn bad rule and connect good authority with truth and care for the poor. They do not define those modern ideologies.

Likewise, claims that Cyrus, Khosrow I, or Queen Boran consciously governed as devotees of Khshathra Vairya require direct sources and usually lack them.

### Esoteric and occult systems

**[MI]** Modern occult correspondence lists assign Šahrewar a color, planet, gemstone, tarot meaning, angelic seal, magical hour, or chakra. No stable system of that kind is found in the Avesta or Pahlavi books. The twentieth-century Parsi esoteric movement Ilm-e-Khshnoom developed color and occult-cosmological correspondences, but these are modern esoteric theology, not recoverable ancient ritual.

No important role for Khshathra Vairya was found in the canonical systems of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Aleister Crowley, or major Renaissance grimoires. Modern web lists that insert him into generic “seven archangel” magic are syncretic inventions.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Small-scale modern devotional and reconstructionist use exists online, normally drawing from Zoroastrian scripture, calendars, and modern myth encyclopedias. Its scale is not reliably measured. Ethical meditations on good governance or offerings of cleaned metal, coins, or basil are modern adaptations unless tied to documented Zoroastrian liturgy.

Modern devotional use should not be confused with membership in living Zoroastrian communities, which have their own initiation, ritual, and communal debates.

### Popular media

Khshathra Vairya is much less prominent than Ahura Mazdā, Ahriman, Mithra, Anāhitā, or the *Šāhnāma* heroes. Fantasy games and fan taxonomies sometimes make him a sky, air, storm, metal, or warrior god with combat powers. Only metal, sovereignty, and the regional armed coin image have ancient support. Cyclones, air magic, angel wings, elaborate armor, and personal adventure narratives are modern inventions unless a work explicitly labels them creative adaptation.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Xšaθra Vairya already a distinct Gāthic being?

Two approaches must be distinguished.

- **Traditional/older synthesis:** Dhalla and many theological translations speak straightforwardly of Khshathra Vairya in the Gāthās.
- **Stricter philological position:** Malandra notes that the exact entity called Xšaθra Vairya is absent; *xšaθra* remains a multivalent noun, divine mode, and incipient personification.

The disagreement is partly semantic: how much personification is necessary before a concept counts as a being? The safest historical statement is that the Gāthās provide the concept and personifying grammar, while the Younger Avesta provides the fixed divine individual.

### Dominion, power, or kingdom?

“Kingdom” can falsely suggest a territory; “power” can imply raw force; “dominion” can sound authoritarian. Avestan *xšaθra* encompasses capacity, command, sphere of rule, and realized order. The adjective *vairya* ethically qualifies it: this is authority worth choosing, not any ruler’s power.

### Metals and sky: ancient doctrine or later adjustment?

- **Post-Gāthic-development view:** Because metal is absent from Xšaθra’s Gāthic contexts, the material guardianship is later systematization.
- **Continuity reconstruction:** Boyce, following Bailey and Zaehner, argues that the sky/stone/metal complex preserves an early correspondence adjusted as material culture changed.

The direct evidence supports later explicit systematization. The proposed Stone Age-to-Bronze Age continuity is plausible reconstruction, not demonstrable chronology.

### The molten-metal passages

Old Avestan syntax is difficult. Mills and other older translators sometimes read the later ordeal directly into Yasna 51.9; more cautious philologists separate:

1. an early judgment through fire or metal;
2. later universal molten-metal river imagery;
3. medieval hagiographic ordeals.

### Shaoreoro’s identity

The Bactrian name, Iranian pantheon, and association with imperial might make identification with Šahrewar strong. Yet Kushan coin imagery is eclectic. The spear-and-shield figure should be called the regional Kushan visualization of Shaoreoro, not necessarily a universally canonical portrait.

### Plutarch’s mapping

Plutarch’s “Good Order/Lawfulness” is usually mapped to Xšaθra Vairya; “Wealth” could seem equally plausible once the metal/wealth connection is considered. His list is an interpretatio, and exact one-to-one equations remain reconstructed.

### Opponent Sāwul and Vedic Śarva

Avestan *Saurva* and Middle Persian Sāwul/Sāwar are commonly connected with Vedic *Śarva*, an epithet or form of Rudra. In Zoroastrian demonology the figure becomes the adversary of desirable dominion. It does not follow that Xšaθra Vairya is simply an Iranian replacement for Rudra or Indra; that is a schematic comparativist inference.

### Primary-evidence condition

What survives is uneven:

- archaic liturgy preserved through medieval manuscripts;
- very little early narrative;
- no dedicated temple archive;
- Kushan coins as exceptional labeled images;
- extensive but late Pahlavi systematization;
- living ritual and calendrical continuity.

Much of the original Sasanian Avesta is lost. The *Dēnkard* describes twenty-one nasks, but estimates that only about a quarter survives are approximate and depend on comparing Pahlavi quotations with extant Avestan texts.

### Important editions and studies

- Karl F. Geldner, *Avesta: The Sacred Books of the Parsis*—foundational critical edition.
- James Darmesteter and L. H. Mills, *Sacred Books of the East* translations—historically important but philologically dated.
- Helmut Humbach, *The Gāthās of Zarathushtra*—major modern philological translation.
- Jean Kellens and Éric Pirart, *Les textes vieil-avestiques*—influential linguistic reconstruction.
- Johanna Narten, *Die Aməša Spəṇtas im Avesta*—central study of the divine group.
- Mary Boyce, *A History of Zoroastrianism*—large historical synthesis, sometimes more confident about continuity than later minimalists.
- William Malandra, studies of ancient Iranian religion and the Iranica article *ŠAHREWAR*.
- Prods Oktor Skjærvø, text editions and teaching materials emphasizing language, transmission, and ritual context.
- A. V. Williams, edition and translation of the Pahlavi Rivāyat accompanying the *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*.
- Behramgore T. Anklesaria; more recently Domenico Agostini and Samuel Thrope, translations of the *Bundahišn*.

## 11. Conclusions by evidentiary strength

### Secure early core

- *Xšaθra* is divine, desirable, effective dominion belonging to Ahura Mazdā.
- Humans are expected to choose or help realize that dominion through Aša, good thought, and right action.
- The oldest texts do not provide a separate biography, image, genealogy, metal guardianship, or named demon-opponent.

### Younger Avestan canon

- Xšaθra Vairya becomes a named Aməša Spənta.
- He occupies a fixed place in liturgy and calendar.
- His name becomes intimately associated, even lexically interchangeable, with metal.

### Later canonical elaboration

- Šahrewar guards metal derived from the sky.
- He supports legitimate kingship, proper weapons, charity, and the poor.
- He opposes Sāwul, demon of oppressive rule.
- His metal participates in the final purification.
- The *Bundahišn* and Pahlavi Rivāyat disagree about whether Fire and Airyaman or Šahrewar himself melt and release it.

### Regional material form

- Kushan Shaoreoro coins provide the clearest ancient labeled image: an armed deity of imperial power in a Bactrian-Hellenizing idiom.

### Things not established

- A dedicated ancient temple, priesthood, or sacrificial cult.
- Human sacrifice or temple prostitution.
- A personal consort, children, mount, or independent adventure cycle.
- A secure role in Roman Mithraism.
- A named character in Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*.
- A canonical ancient color, planet, gemstone, sigil, tarot card, or magical seal.
- Direct authorship of Jewish or Christian “Kingdom of God” theology.
- Proof that specific Achaemenid kings consciously worshipped Xšaθra Vairya as an individual deity.
