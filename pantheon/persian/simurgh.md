# The Simurgh: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** In the Avesta, a gigantic supernatural **bird** (*mərəγō Saēnō*, “the bird Saēna”), not a god and not the object of a separately documented cult. Middle Persian theological zoology classifies the **Sēnmurw** primarily as a bird, though one contradictory passage makes it a dog-bird-muskrat or bat-like mixed genus. In Ferdowsi it is a sapient, providential female wonder-bird; in Persian Sufism, especially ʿAṭṭār, it becomes the transcendent king of birds and an image of divine unity. “Spirit,” “demon,” and “goddess” are not secure ancient classifications.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest recoverable testimony is in the orally transmitted Younger Avestan corpus: most importantly *Rašn Yašt* 12.17, which names Saēna’s tree in the cosmic sea Vourukaša, and *Warahrān/Verethragna Yašt* 14.41, which compares the enveloping protection of victory and *xᵛarənah* to the great bird Saēna. Their exact dates are disputed; they belong to the Old Iranian oral period, plausibly in or before the Achaemenid era, but cannot responsibly be assigned a precise year. The relevant surviving manuscripts are medieval or early modern: the oldest Khorde Avesta containing Yašt material is Jm4, dated 1352 CE, while the principal complete pure-Yašt codex F1 is dated 1591. The early date is therefore linguistic and tradition-historical, not the date of a surviving physical witness. [Securely attested early material.]

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

The Simurgh is the end product of a long, discontinuous transformation: an Old Iranian great raptor associated with healing vegetation, rain, protection, and fortune; a Middle Persian cosmic bird that mechanically distributes every plant’s seed; an ambiguously zoomorphic creature in late Sasanian learned taxonomy and, perhaps, royal art; the wise foster-mother and healer of Iran’s heroic house in epic; and the inaccessible divine mirror of Persian mystical poetry. These forms are historically related, but they are not interchangeable.

The historically sound etymology is Avestan *Saēna* plus “bird,” with Indo-Iranian cognates denoting a raptor. The celebrated Persian wordplay *sī murḡ*, “thirty birds,” is ʿAṭṭār’s poetic exploitation of the later Persian sound-shape, not the word’s historical etymology. [Secure etymology versus medieval literary wordplay.] The popular derivation from Persian *sag* “dog” plus *murḡ* “bird” is a retrospective explanation of the dog-headed artistic creature and is linguistically untenable as the origin of the name.

### Epithets and titles

There is no extensive liturgical epithet-list comparable to those of an Avestan yazata. Attested descriptions include:

- “Great bird Saēna,” in *Yašt* 14.41. [Securely attested early.]
- “Three-fingered Sēn,” *Bundahišn* 13.10. [Learned Middle Persian zoology.]
- Largest of the birds and first among birds to reach the sea Frāxkard/Vourukaša, *Bundahišn* 13.10, 13.34–35. It is not, however, chief of birds there; that office belongs to Karšipt. [Learned systematization.]
- “King of the birds,” in ʿAṭṭār’s *Manṭeq al-ṭayr*. [Medieval Sufi elaboration.]
- Possessor of *farr*—radiant fortune or grace—in Ferdowsi; Zāl speaks of the shadow of the Simurgh’s *farr*. [Later canonical epic.]
- Knowing the “secret of heaven/fate” (*rāz-e sepehr*) in the Rostam–Esfandiyār episode. [Later canonical epic.]

### What the Simurgh is not

The Gathas do not mention it. No Achaemenid royal inscription at Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam, or elsewhere securely names it; Herodotus and Plutarch supply no clear Simurgh testimony. No ancient source calls it a yazata, Amesha Spenta, goddess, or demon. Its absence from those corpora is material: the oldest record belongs to Younger Avestan mythic zoology, not to the royal inscriptional theology of Ahuramazda.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Avestan evidence

#### *Rašn Yašt* 12.17

The passage invokes Rašnu and refers to the tree “of the eagle/Saēna” standing in the middle of the sea Vourukaša. It is the tree of good, powerful, and all-healing remedies, upon which rest the seeds of all plants. Darmesteter translated the bird-word as “eagle”; later scholarship identifies this as Saēna, ancestral to Sēnmurw/Simurgh.

What the passage establishes:

- Saēna is associated possessively or locatively with the cosmic tree.
- The tree stands in Vourukaša.
- It contains all plant seeds and is medicinal.

What it does **not** say:

- It does not narrate the Simurgh shaking the seeds over the world.
- It does not describe a dog’s head, peacock’s tail, nursing, wisdom, immortality, or divine kingship.
- It does not identify the bird with the tree itself or with the separate White Hōm/Gaokerena.

Those elaborations belong to later texts or modern harmonization. The online Darmesteter translation preserves the key wording, while modern Yašt scholarship emphasizes that these texts were composed and recomposed orally during the Old Iranian period. The oldest complete pure-Yašt manuscript, F1, was copied in 1591, and E1 in 1601; the gulf between composition and manuscript must remain visible.

#### *Warahrān Yašt* 14.41

Vərəθraγna/Warahrān, the victory-being, surrounds the worshipper’s house with *xᵛarənah* “fortune, glory” as the great bird Saēna does, and as watery clouds cover great mountains. The simile connects Saēna with encompassing protection, prosperity in cattle, cloud-like breadth, and probably rain. [Securely attested early.] It does not constitute worship of Saēna; the hymn is to Vərəθraγna.

#### Other Avestan material

*Vidēvdād* 2.42 names Karšipt, the bird that carries or recites the Mazda-worshipping religion inside Yima’s enclosure. Later Middle Persian classifications place Karšipt and Sēnmurw together, but the two must not be merged. Karšipt, not Sēnmurw, is chief of birds in the *Bundahišn*. [Secure distinction.]

No Simurgh occurs in the Gathas or in the securely Old Avestan layer.

### Middle Persian and Zoroastrian books

#### *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 62.37–41

Probably compiled in its surviving form during the early Islamic period from older Zoroastrian learning, this is the fullest cosmological account. Sēnmurw nests on the “tree without evil and of many seeds.” When it rises, a thousand shoots grow; when it alights, it breaks a thousand shoots and releases their seeds. Cīnāmrōš/Camrōš gathers them and carries them to where Tištar takes up water; Tištar then rains water and seeds upon the earth. [Learned Middle Persian systematization.]

This is the source—not *Yašt* 12.17—for the familiar seed-distribution mechanism.

#### *Bundahišn*

The surviving recensions are medieval compilations preserving Sasanian cosmological learning.

- 13.10: the three-fingered Sēn is the largest bird.
- 13.22: Sēnmurw belongs to the bird species.
- 13.23: contradictorily, Sēnmurw belongs among bat-like creatures combining dog, bird, and muskrat: it flies like a bird, has canine teeth, and lives in a cave like a muskrat.
- 13.34–35: it reaches the sea Frāxkard before other birds.
- 15.13: it is oviparous, contradicting the mammalian/bat classification.
- 16.4: its movement causes the all-seed tree to wither, a variant of the branch-breaking account.
- 17.11: it was created first among birds, while Karšipt is their chief.
- 24.24: Camrōš destroys Iran’s enemies as a bird pecks grain.

The contradictions likely preserve multiple editorial layers. Hanns-Peter Schmidt inferred an older bird-only account and a later rationalization generated by the composite creature known in art. That is a scholarly reconstruction, not an explicit ancient explanation.

#### *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram*

Zādspram 3.39 abbreviates the seed-tree narrative; 3.65 groups Sēnmurw with anomalous birds that possess teeth and suckle their young. In 23.2 Karšipt and Sēnmurw attend Ohrmazd’s consultation concerning animals protected by Wahman. [Ninth-century learned systematization.]

#### Pahlavi *Rivāyat* accompanying the *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*

At the final renovation, when the earth is flattened and the waters stand still, Sēnmurw nests in a forest. This has been interpreted to mean that its seed-distributing work has ended. [Eschatological elaboration.]

#### *Ayādgār ī Zarērān* 12.3

Zarēr’s horse is called *sēn ī murwag*, apparently “Saēna-bird,” probably praising its speed or strength. This is a metaphorical animal-name, not an appearance by the cosmic bird. [Middle Persian heroic usage.]

#### New Persian Zoroastrian Rivāyats

In the *Rivāyat* of Dārāb Hormazyār, Amrōš replaces Sēnmurw in the seed cycle while Camrōš retains the collecting role. This is a genuine variant, not evidence that all three names designate one timeless being. [Early-modern Zoroastrian variant.]

The *Dēnkard* and *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag* are important witnesses to Zoroastrian textual history but contain no comparably central, unambiguous Simurgh episode. Claims that the Simurgh is a major actor in either require a chapter citation that has not been produced.

### Ferdowsi and epic transmission

Ferdowsi completed the *Šāhnāma* around 1010 CE, drawing on the lost Middle Persian royal tradition and especially the lost prose *Šāhnāma-ye Abū Manṣūrī* of 957. The earliest surviving illustrated manuscripts are centuries later. The closely parallel account in Ṯaʿālebī’s *Ḡorar aḵbār molūk al-Fors* indicates a shared written source, not invention by Ferdowsi.

The Simurgh appears in three major functions:

1. foster-mother and tutor of Zāl;
2. surgical and medicinal savior at Rostam’s birth;
3. healer and fatal counselor in Rostam’s combat with Esfandiyār.

Ferdowsi also knows a hostile, elephant-carrying Simurgh killed by Esfandiyār. Whether it is a separate species-member, evil counterpart, or an ambivalent form of the same mythic animal is not settled by the poem.

### ʿAṭṭār

Farīd al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār’s Persian *Manṭeq al-ṭayr* (“Speech/Conference of the Birds”), composed around the late twelfth or early thirteenth century, turns the Simurgh into the inaccessible king sought by all birds. Its frame developed earlier Arabic and Persian bird-journey works associated with Avicenna and Aḥmad or Moḥammad al-Ġazālī. The oldest famous illustrated copy is the Timurid manuscript begun at Herat in 1487 and now dispersed, with major leaves in the Metropolitan Museum.

### Other relevant textual equations

- Sogdian *synmrγ* translates Sanskrit Garuḍa in *Pañcatantra* transmission. [Sasanian-period translation-equivalence.]
- The early Syriac version of the Middle Persian *Kalīla wa-Dimna* renders Garuḍa with Simurgh terminology. [Cross-cultural translation.]
- Fourteenth-century *Tāriḵ-e moʿjam* says the peacock carries Siyāmak’s spirit and Simurgh his soul to the eight paradises. [Late syncretistic elaboration.]
- Judeo-Persian Bible translations use *sīmurγ* for Hebrew *nešer*, “eagle.” This shows that the name could denote an ordinary large raptor as well as a monster. [Translation practice.]
- A manuscript illustration of the *Thousand and One Nights* identifies Simurgh with the roc. [Late manuscript conflation.]

There is no securely relevant Manichaean myth in which Simurgh acts under that name. Manichaean Middle Persian *pšqwc* occurs in a description of the fever-spirit Idra’s wings, relevant to the Caucasian *paskuč* complex but not proof that Manichaeans worshipped Simurgh. Roman Mithraic iconography supplies no securely labeled Simurgh; attempts to read every winged quadruped in Mithraic art as one are speculative.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

| Period | Function | Evidence and tier |
|---|---|---|
| Younger Avestan | Association with healing plants and the cosmic all-seed tree | *Yašt* 12.17; securely attested early |
| Younger Avestan | Cloud-like protection, rain, prosperity, *xᵛarənah* | *Yašt* 14.41; securely attested early |
| Middle Persian | Reproduction and worldwide distribution of plant life | *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, *Bundahišn*, *Zādspram*; learned systematization |
| Middle Persian | Primordial zoological status | *Bundahišn* 13; learned systematization |
| Persian epic | Fosterage, medicine, surgery, healing, prophecy, aerial transport, protection of the house of Zāl | *Šāhnāma*; later canonical epic |
| Persian epic | Monstrous predation and heroic obstacle | Esfandiyār’s Seven Labors; later canonical counter-tradition |
| Persian mysticism | Divine kingship, inaccessible truth, annihilation of separate self in unity | ʿAṭṭār; medieval Sufi reinterpretation |
| Folklore | Rescue from underworld or remote land, protection of nestlings, healing | Kurdish, Armenian, and Georgian tales; regional folklore |

### Physical form

The Avesta calls Saēna a great bird but supplies no dog’s head or peacock’s tail. The *Bundahišn* calls it three-fingered; Schmidt regards this as a description of three visible forward talons rather than proof of an ostrich. Proposals that the natural model was a falcon, black vulture, or golden eagle remain scholarly reconstructions. Cognate Sanskrit *śyena-* favors a raptor; size and arboreal nesting led Schmidt to prefer the golden eagle.

The *Šāhnāma*’s benevolent Simurgh is female, has nestlings, can carry Zāl and later Zāl/Rostam, and is vast enough to resemble a dark cloud. She nurses Zāl indirectly with blood from tender meat. Some folklore makes the bird mammalian enough to suckle its own young.

### The “Sasanian Simurgh”

A late Sasanian composite animal has:

- a snarling canine head;
- feathered wings;
- forepaws of a predator, commonly with three visible claws;
- a large, upcurving vegetal or peacock-like tail;
- frequently a pearl-bordered roundel.

Its most famous monumental occurrence is on the garment of the royal horseman in the large grotto at Ṭāq-e Bostān, generally associated with Ḵosrow II, r. 591–628, though the grotto’s royal identification and precise completion history have been debated. [Secure image; disputed name and interpretation.]

Camilla Trever’s influential identification of this creature as Sēnmurw joined the image to the *Bundahišn*’s dog-bird-bat description. Schmidt defended the identification but regarded the bat taxonomy as a secondary rationalization. More recent scholarship, particularly Matteo Compareti’s, often calls it the **“so-called senmurv”** and stresses that no inscription labels the creature. Near Eastern griffins, Scythian composites, a Mesopotamian lion-griffin, and the Hellenistic hippocamp have all been proposed as antecedents; none has been demonstrated as a unique source.

Accordingly:

- “A Sasanian composite conventionally identified as Sēnmurw” is defensible.
- “The definitive ancient portrait of the Avestan Saēna” is not.
- “The Sasanian national emblem” or “exclusive royal insignia” overstates the evidence. Its connection with auspiciousness and *xwarrah* is plausible, but it does not appear in the central investiture scene at Ṭāq-e Bostān.

### Textiles and their attribution

The motif is commonly called a Sasanian textile design because reliefs depict it on royal garments and later silks repeat related roundels. Yet most celebrated “Sasanian silks” were found outside Iran, often at Antinoë, and many have been reassigned to Byzantine, Coptic, Sogdian, or early Islamic manufacture under Iranian influence. Scientifically excavated Sasanian-period textiles from Šahr-e Qūmes and Tall-e Malyan are comparatively plain. Therefore a silk with a pearl-roundel “Simurgh” motif is not automatically a Sasanian product.

Post-Sasanian appearances include Čāl Tarḵān-ʿEšqābād in Iran; Qaṣr al-Ḥayr al-Ġarbī in Syria; Mshatta in Jordan; Khirbat al-Mafjar in Palestine; the Caucasus; Byzantium; and Central Asia. The Moshchevaya Balka caftan, dated around the late eighth or early ninth century, carries roundels with related creatures. These prove the longevity and mobility of the visual type, not continuous worship.

### Later manuscript form

Persian book painters usually abandoned the quadrupedal dog-bird and depicted a magnificent feathered bird, often with streaming multicolored plumage and a crown or crest. The transformation is visible in illustrated *Šāhnāma* manuscripts from the Mongol and Timurid periods onward. No evidence establishes exactly how Ferdowsi imagined it.

### Unattested modern attributes

No ancient source securely assigns the Simurgh a sacred weekday, number, planet, fixed color, temple incense, priestly weapon, or ritual festival. Lists giving it “thirty colors,” a solar day, a chakra, an element, or a modern magical correspondence are recent esoteric constructions unless tied to a particular modern author.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

No Avestan or Pahlavi source gives Saēna/Sēnmurw parents, spouse, or named divine offspring. The *Šāhnāma* gives the benevolent bird unnamed young in its nest, and the hostile Simurgh two enormous young. No source says these two broods belong to one individual.

In the *Šāhnāma*, the Simurgh becomes Zāl’s foster-parent and thereby the ritual protector of Zāl’s son Rostam. This is social and mythic kinship, not biological genealogy. Later visual and literary treatments sometimes call it the ancestor or guardian of the house of Sām, but the poem makes it foster-mother and patron.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The corpus is unusually sparse in god-to-god encounters. Most of the Simurgh’s named partners are heroes, animals, or cosmological agents rather than gods.

- **Tištar/Tištrya** — Sēnmurw releases the all-seed tree’s seeds; Camrōš carries them to where Tištar takes up water and rains them over the world. *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 62.37–41, early Islamic-period compilation of older Zoroastrian cosmology. **[Learned systematization; indirect cooperative interaction.]**
- **Ohrmazd/Ahura Mazdā** — Sēnmurw and Karšipt attend Ohrmazd’s consultation concerning the animals. *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram* 23.2, ninth century. **[Learned systematization; joint council scene.]**
- **Wahman/Vohu Manah** — In the same passage the animals under discussion are creatures protected by Wahman; Sēnmurw appears within that administrative order, but no dialogue or direct action between the two is recorded. *Zādspram* 23.2. **[Learned systematization; association, not a fully narrated encounter.]**
- **Vərəθraγna/Warahrān** — The deity’s encompassing gift of *xᵛarənah* is compared to the great bird Saēna in *Yašt* 14.41. **[Secure early co-occurrence, but only a simile; no narrated interaction.]**
- **God (*Ḵodā*) in Ferdowsi’s monotheistic narration** — God places love for the abandoned Zāl in the Simurgh’s heart. *Šāhnāma*, Zāl cycle, completed ca. 1010. **[Later canonical Islamic-era epic narration.]**

Camrōš is a supernatural bird, but surviving texts do not classify it clearly as a god. For completeness:

- **Camrōš/Cīnāmrōš** — Collects the seeds Sēnmurw dislodges and transports them into Tištar’s rain-cycle. *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 62.37–41. **[Learned systematization; cooperative cosmological action.]**
- **Karšipt** — Appears beside Sēnmurw at Ohrmazd’s animal council and outranks it as chief of birds. *Zādspram* 23.2; *Bundahišn* 17.11. **[Learned systematization; joint appearance and hierarchy.]**

No securely attested meeting, combat, marriage, or parentage connects the Simurgh with Anāhitā, Mithra, Spenta Ārmaiti, Ātar, Sraoša, Rashnu, Apaoša, Ahriman, or the Amesha Spentas collectively. Its tree appears in a hymn to Rashnu, but Rashnu and Saēna do not interact in a narrative scene.

### Comparative beings are not genealogical relations

- **Garuḍa:** functional and translational equation in Sogdian and Syriac *Pañcatantra/Kalīla wa-Dimna* transmission; not a shared genealogy.
- **ʿAnqāʾ:** Arabic-Persian literary fusion, increasingly common in Islamic cosmography and Sufism.
- **Phoenix/qoqnus:** later comparison or conflation; the self-immolating rebirth cycle is absent from early Simurgh material.
- **Roc:** occasional manuscript and European bestiary equation.
- **Anzû:** motif comparison—great bird, rescued offspring, blessing or hostility—but no Iranian text identifies Saēna with Anzû.
- **Slavic Simargl:** Iranian derivation has been proposed from phonetic resemblance and winged-dog imagery, but Simargl’s name, number, and nature remain disputed. It cannot be listed as a securely exported Simurgh.
- **Melek Ṭāʾūs:** Wilhelm Fauth proposed a remote common primordial-bird background. This is comparative reconstruction, not a historical identification made by Yazidi sources.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Saēna and the tree of all seeds

**Early core:** *Yašt* 12.17 locates Saēna’s medicinal tree in Vourukaša, bearing the seeds of all plants. *Yašt* 14.41 associates the great bird with encompassing fortune, cloud, and probably rain.

**Middle Persian expansion:** The bird’s takeoff makes a thousand shoots grow; landing breaks or shakes a thousand branches; Camrōš collects the seed; Tištar incorporates it into rain. The cycle explains the continual distribution of plant species and medicines.

**Variants:**

- *Bundahišn* says the bird’s action makes the tree wither.
- *Zādspram* abbreviates the account.
- A Pahlavi gloss confuses the all-seed tree with White Hōm.
- The later Rivāyat substitutes Amrōš for Sēnmurw.

The often-repeated synthesis “Simurgh lives on Gaokerena, the Tree of Life, and scatters its seeds by flapping its wings” conflates at least two trees and several textual periods. The all-seed tree and White Hōm/Gaokerena stand near one another in Vourukaša but are distinguishable in the better evidence.

An astral interpretation identifies Sēnmurw with Aquila/Altair, Camrōš with Cygnus, and Tištar with Sirius. Tištar’s Sirius identity is secure; the other equations are Schmidt’s reconstruction. A proposed Babylonian/Greek astronomical contribution is possible but not demonstrated by a direct textual loan.

### B. Zāl’s exposure and adoption

Sām’s son is born with white hair. Ashamed and fearing a demonic or Ahrimanic birth, Sām abandons the infant on Mount Alborz. The Simurgh discovers him while seeking food for her young. God implants love in her; she carries the child to her nest and raises him with her brood, feeding him tender flesh whose blood substitutes for milk.

Years later travelers report a magnificent young man living on the mountain. Sām receives warning dreams, consults wise men, repents, and climbs to recover him. The Simurgh recognizes that human society is Zāl’s proper world and returns him, but gives him a feather: burning it will summon her. Zāl is placed “under the shadow” of her *farr*.

**Earliest recoverable written form:** lost *Šāhnāma-ye Abū Manṣūrī* tradition, reflected independently in Ferdowsi and Ṯaʿālebī; extant full canonical form ca. 1010.

**Comparative antecedent:** Aelian, *De natura animalium* 12.2, reports that Achaemenes was raised by an eagle. This demonstrates an Iranian royal fosterage motif in Greco-Roman testimony, not that Aelian’s eagle was called Simurgh.

**Function:** The episode reverses Sām’s social rejection of bodily difference and gives the Sistanian dynasty superhuman fosterage and *farr*. That interpretation arises from the narrative structure; it is not an explicit ritual charter.

### C. The birth and healing of Rostam

Rudāba cannot deliver her enormous child. Zāl burns the feather. The Simurgh instructs him to calm or anesthetize Rudāba with wine, summon a skilled man, open her side, remove the child, sew the incision, dress it with a specified herbal compound and milk, and pass the feather over it. Mother and child survive.

The episode is regularly called the first caesarean section or “Rostamian section.” It is certainly an early literary description of abdominal delivery, but claims of historical surgical priority exceed the evidence: it is an eleventh-century epic account preserving an undatable legendary tradition, not a medical case record. [Later canonical epic; modern medical commemoration.]

### D. Rostam, Esfandiyār, and the fatal tamarisk arrow

Esfandiyār wounds Rostam and his horse Raḵš almost beyond recovery. Zāl burns the remaining feather. The Simurgh descends, removes the arrows, sucks or extracts poisoned blood, and heals hero and horse.

She knows that the death of Esfandiyār will curse his killer in both worlds and initially warns against it. When conflict proves unavoidable, she carries or directs Rostam to a tamarisk tree and instructs him to make a double-pointed arrow and aim at Esfandiyār’s eyes, the vulnerable part of his otherwise invulnerable body. Rostam follows the counsel and kills him.

The Simurgh is thus neither merely benevolent nor omnipotent: she heals but cannot dissolve fate; she saves her foster-line at the price of foreknown catastrophe. Ferdowsi’s episode is morally tragic, not an uncomplicated dragon-slaying victory.

### E. Esfandiyār kills the hostile Simurgh

In Esfandiyār’s Seven Labors another Simurgh appears as a gigantic predator, dark as a mountain or cloud, capable of lifting crocodiles, panthers, and elephants. Esfandiyār prepares a wagon or chest armed with blades; when the bird attacks, the blades cut it apart. Its two giant offspring flee or are overcome.

Possible readings:

1. two different Simurghs, one benevolent and one monstrous;
2. a species name rather than an individual name;
3. an originally ambivalent raptor tradition split by epic composition;
4. hostile rhetoric about the same creature, since elsewhere Esfandiyār mocks Zāl as something the bird declined to eat.

The poem does not resolve the identity. “The good Simurgh’s mate was killed” is a later harmonizing interpretation occasionally repeated in summaries, not an unequivocal statement of Ferdowsi’s text.

### F. The Conference of the Birds

The birds seek a king. The hoopoe tells them that their true king is the Simurgh, beyond Mount Qāf and veils of light and darkness. One by one the birds raise excuses rooted in attachments, fears, and incomplete spiritual understanding. The hoopoe answers with parables, including the long story of Shaykh Ṣanʿān.

The company crosses seven valleys commonly rendered Quest, Love, Knowledge or Gnosis, Detachment/Independence, Unity, Bewilderment, and Poverty/Annihilation. Many die. Thirty reach the court, where their separate identities are reflected back: the thirty birds, *sī murḡ*, see that they are the Sīmorḡ. Yet the conclusion is not a simple modern slogan that “the Simurgh was imaginary.” The poem stages dependence before transcendent self-sufficiency, then the annihilation of the seekers’ separateness in their king.

**Sources and development:** ʿAṭṭār adapted earlier bird-assembly and journey frames attributed to Avicenna and the Ghazālīs. His named birds, seven-valley itinerary, extensive embedded tales, and final *sī murḡ/Sīmorḡ* mirror are his distinctive literary construction. The number thirty is a pun, not etymology.

### G. Folkloric rescue cycle

In Kurdish tales the hero saves Sīmīr’s nestlings from a serpent. The bird gives feathers that summon her or transports him to a remote land. In Armenian and Georgian parallels, the hero saves the bird’s young and is carried from the underworld, feeding the bird sheep fat and wine; when provisions fail, he cuts flesh from his leg. The bird refuses or preserves it and restores the wound at journey’s end.

These belong to widespread Eurasian tale-types. Their agreement with the Simurgh’s healing and transport functions suggests Iranian participation in the tale complex but does not prove that every “helpful giant bird” story descends from the Avesta.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No independent cult of the Simurgh is securely documented.

- No temple can be identified as a Simurgh sanctuary.
- No priesthood, festival, sacrifice, liturgical calendar, oracle, pilgrimage, or votive inscription is known.
- No Achaemenid, Parthian, or Sasanian inscription invokes Saēna/Sēnmurw as a recipient of worship.
- No securely identified theophoric personal name proves devotion to it; Avestan *Saēna* as a personal name may derive from the ordinary raptor-name.
- The Avestan references occur inside hymns to Rashnu and Vərəθraγna, not hymns addressed to Saēna.
- No evidence connects the being to human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, or a temple economy.
- No source records suppression of “Simurgh worship” during Islamization because no such institution is attested.

This is not evidence that the image lacked religious meaning. The all-seed tree narrative belongs to Zoroastrian cosmology, and the Sasanian composite may have signified *xwarrah*, prosperity, or protective power. But cosmological significance and auspicious decoration are not equivalent to cult.

The feather-burning in Ferdowsi is a literary summoning procedure, not evidence of an actual Zoroastrian rite. Likewise, modern charms or meditation practices advertised under the Simurgh’s name cannot be retrojected into ancient ritual.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Old Iranian oral period, approximately first millennium BCE

The Saēna bird enters the Younger Avestan mythic lexicon. Its recoverable attributes are narrow: greatness, enveloping cloud-like protection, rain or prosperity, and association with the all-healing all-seed tree. Its composition date is imprecise because the material was orally transmitted and linguistically redacted.

### Achaemenid period, 550–330 BCE

No Achaemenid inscription securely names or portrays Saēna. The eagle-fosterage of Achaemenes, recorded much later by Aelian, may preserve an Iranian royal legend but cannot be equated textually with the Simurgh. Winged creatures in Achaemenid art require individual analysis; none is self-labeled Saēna.

### Parthian and early Sasanian periods

Direct evidence remains thin. Middle Persian phonological development produced *Sēnmurw*. Zoroastrian priests systematized the cosmic fauna and the seed cycle, but the surviving books are later copies and compilations.

### Late Sasanian period, sixth–seventh centuries

The dog-headed, winged, peacock-tailed composite appears securely in late Sasanian art, above all Ṭāq-e Bostān. Its identification as Sēnmurw is plausible but debated. It may have conveyed prosperity or *xwarrah* and was used in elite visual culture rather than a separately attested cult.

Cross-cultural translation already equated Simurgh terminology with Indian Garuḍa. This is also the probable environment behind the contradictory Pahlavi attempt to classify the creature as bird, dog, muskrat, and bat.

### Early Islamic centuries, seventh–tenth centuries

Iranian composite motifs continued in Umayyad, Central Asian, Caucasian, Byzantine, and Islamic decorative art. Their survival does not prove covert Zoroastrian worship; portable luxury art transmitted motifs across religious boundaries.

Zoroastrian scholars committed older cosmology to the Middle Persian books. The Simurgh remained part of cosmological natural history while acquiring a more expansive literary career in New Persian.

### Epic canonization, tenth–eleventh centuries

Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma* made the Simurgh one of Persian literature’s defining beings: maternal, medically omniscient, prophetic, and inseparable from Zāl and Rostam. A hostile Simurgh survived beside this benefactor. The epic form became culturally dominant and shaped manuscript painting.

### Sufi transformation, twelfth–fourteenth centuries

Persian mystical writers elevated the Simurgh into a symbol of the hidden divine sovereign or ultimate unity. ʿAṭṭār’s punning conclusion became the most influential form. In Islamic cosmography and adab, Simurgh merged variably with ʿanqāʾ, Garuḍa, phoenix, and roc traditions.

### Mongol, Timurid, and Safavid art

Book painters rendered it as a spectacular bird rather than the late Sasanian canine composite. Illustrated *Šāhnāmas* fixed the scenes of Zāl’s rescue, the feather summons, and Rostam’s healing in the global visual canon. The 1487 *Manṭeq al-ṭayr* manuscript gave ʿAṭṭār’s poem an especially important Timurid visual life.

### Early modern folklore

Kurdish, Armenian, Georgian, Anatolian, Turkic, and Iranian tales retained the bird as rescuer, mount, nest-parent, or adversary of a serpent. These are regional transformations, not a single unchanged “Simurgh religion.”

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century rediscovery

European Avestan editions by Westergaard, Geldner, Darmesteter, and others reconnected literary Simurgh with Avestan Saēna. Archaeology and art history then identified the Ṭāq-e Bostān composite as Sēnmurw. This produced the now-familiar synthetic history—Avesta to Sasanian textile to Ferdowsi to ʿAṭṭār—but later scholarship has qualified its weakest visual links.

### Present

The Simurgh remains a major Iranian cultural symbol and a literary-spiritual image used across religious and secular settings. There is no quantitative evidence for a large organized Simurgh-centered neopagan religion. Modern individual invocation, reconstructionist practice, and occult correspondence systems exist online, but they are decentralized and generally modern inventions rather than continuations of ancient cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, literature, and art

Jorge Luis Borges and Margarita Guerrero included “El Simurg” in *Manual de zoología fantástica* (1957), expanded as *El libro de los seres imaginarios* (1967/1969). Borges compresses Ferdowsi and ʿAṭṭār, calling the bird immortal and placing it in the Tree of Knowledge; “Tree of Knowledge” is Borges’s literary formulation, not a literal Avestan title. Borges also wrote “El Simurgh y el águila” in 1948, later collected in *Nueve ensayos dantescos*, comparing collective, transcendent imagery in ʿAṭṭār and Dante. [Modern literary reception.]

In the requested Colegio connection: Mexican composer and member of El Colegio Nacional Mario Lavista composed the piano work *Simurg* after reading the Sufi poem; El Colegio Nacional has repeatedly programmed it in commemorative concerts. [Modern artistic institutional use.]

Peter Sís’s illustrated *The Conference of the Birds* (2011) introduced ʿAṭṭār’s allegory to a large anglophone children’s and art-book audience. Numerous theatrical, operatic, and choreographic adaptations treat the thirty birds as a parable of collective identity.

### Iranian national and political symbolism

The Simurgh has become a flexible emblem of Iran, Persian-language cultural continuity, healing, wisdom, feminism, diaspora, and collective liberation. Such uses usually select the nurturing Simurgh of Zāl or the plural unity of ʿAṭṭār. They are modern political readings, not recoverable Avestan doctrines.

The “Simorgh” name is used by airlines, publishing houses, cultural institutes, prizes, festivals, medical organizations, aircraft projects, and commercial brands. Iran’s annual film honors are called Crystal Simorghs. Commercial prevalence demonstrates recognition, not worship.

Assertions that the Simurgh was “the official national emblem of Sasanian Iran” or appeared on the *Derafš-e Kāvīān* are not supported by a securely identified surviving standard. The Sasanian composite’s royal associations are real; the specific flag reconstruction is modern.

### Esotericism and neopaganism

Unlike Egyptian, Greek, or Mesopotamian gods, the Simurgh had little distinct place in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or classical ceremonial grimoires. Broad occult references generally entered through Orientalist translations of Persian poetry, comparative mythology, Theosophical universalism, or modern fantasy.

Modern practices treating it as:

- a fire-phoenix that dies and resurrects;
- an angel corresponding to a planet or sephirah;
- a “goddess of healing”;
- a spirit summoned by burning feathers;
- an ancient patron of shadow work, chakras, or manifestation;

are modern inventions or syncretisms unless their practitioner explicitly presents them as new. No ancient ritual manual supplies those systems.

### Popular culture

The name and image occur widely in fantasy fiction, comics, animation, games, music, and speculative fiction. These representations normally combine the phoenix’s rebirth, Garuḍa’s martial scale, the *Šāhnāma*’s healer, and ʿAṭṭār’s collective mind. Such hybrids have materially reshaped public recognition but should not be cited as evidence for Iranian antiquity.

The Simurgh in Wildbow’s *Worm*, for example, is a precognitive, psychologically destructive superbeing: a deliberate modern inversion of the beneficent wise bird, not a survival of Iranian myth. Game creatures labeled Simurgh often function as fire-elemental mounts or bosses, importing phoenix conventions absent from the oldest sources.

### Extremism and appropriation

No major extremist movement is documented as maintaining a distinctive Simurgh cult. Nationalist discourse can, however, detach the bird from both Zoroastrian and Islamic-Persian textual histories and present it as a racially pure “Aryan” emblem. That is modern ideological selection. Conversely, pan-Turkic, pan-Iranian, and Slavic-nationalist claims of exclusive ethnic ownership commonly confuse historical borrowing, cognate motifs, and modern identity politics. The evidence supports circulation and adaptation, not a single modern group’s proprietary origin story.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Etymology

The mainstream derivation from Avestan *mərəγō Saēnō* and Indo-Iranian *śyena-/saēna-* is philologically strong. The “thirty birds” derivation is intentionally exploited by ʿAṭṭār but historically secondary. The dog-bird derivation is folk etymology.

### Dating the first evidence

Older handbooks sometimes date individual Yašt passages confidently to a particular dynasty. Current caution is preferable: Younger Avestan linguistic form and eastern geography show antiquity, but oral composition, performance, redaction, and manuscript copying occurred at different times. Jm4 (1352), F1 (1591), and E1 (1601) are witnesses to a much older text, not physical Achaemenid artifacts.

### Bird, bat, or composite?

The *Bundahišn* contradicts itself. Schmidt reconstructed an older bird tradition and a secondary bat/dog/bird rationalization. Trever connected that rationalization to the Sasanian artistic composite. The reconstruction is persuasive but not demonstrable in every stage.

### Is the Sasanian creature really Sēnmurw?

Older scholarship usually answered yes. More cautious recent art history emphasizes:

- there is no identifying inscription;
- composite quadrupeds circulated widely;
- alleged Sasanian textiles often have non-Iranian provenances;
- the image may have been associated with *xwarrah* without being the mythic bird;
- “pseudo-Simurgh” or “so-called senmurv” can be more accurate catalogue language.

The image’s presence at Ṭāq-e Bostān is secure; its name is an interpretation.

### Astral theory

Schmidt’s equation Sēnmurw = Aquila/Altair, Camrōš = Cygnus, and Tištar = Sirius elegantly explains seasonal seed and rain imagery. Only Tištar-Sirius is explicit and secure. The full celestial scheme remains reconstruction.

### Near Eastern and Indian origins

Anzû, Etana’s eagle, Garuḍa, the phoenix, roc, and griffin offer strong motif parallels: giant birds, aerial journeys, saved nestlings, cosmic or solar roles. Translation evidence directly links Simurgh and Garuḍa in Sogdian and Syriac. For Anzû and the phoenix, common heritage or borrowing is possible, but direct descent has not been established.

Fauth’s proposal that Simurgh, Garuḍa, phoenix, Tibetan khyung, and Melek Ṭāʾūs descend from a primordial creator-bird is a maximal comparativist hypothesis, not consensus.

### Gender

Avestan grammatical forms and Middle Persian passages do not support one timeless gender. The *Bundahišn*’s bird may be grammatically or conceptually male in one layer and female/mammalian in another. Ferdowsi’s nursing foster-parent is clearly treated as female; ʿAṭṭār’s king is conventionally masculine. English “it,” “she,” and “he” should therefore follow the source under discussion.

### Primary evidence and preservation

The evidence survives in:

- Avestan manuscripts held in Iranian, Indian, British, French, and German collections and increasingly represented in facsimile projects;
- medieval Pahlavi manuscripts, often copied late from damaged exemplars;
- Persian *Šāhnāma* and *Manṭeq al-ṭayr* manuscripts dispersed among institutions including the Metropolitan Museum, British Library, Bibliothèque nationale de France, and major Iranian collections;
- Ṭāq-e Bostān’s in-situ rock reliefs;
- metalwork, stucco, textiles, seals, and coins in museum collections, often with uncertain archaeological provenance.

Modern Avestan studies were transformed by comparative Indo-Iranian linguistics and Geldner’s manuscript collation. Art-historical certainty has moved in the opposite direction: expanding comparative material has made older, confident “Simurgh” labels less secure.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- The word literally and originally means “thirty birds.” **False as etymology; true as ʿAṭṭār’s pun.**
- The Avesta describes a dog-headed peacock. **Unsupported.**
- The Simurgh lives on the White Hōm/Gaokerena tree in every source. **Conflation.**
- It is a Zoroastrian goddess with temples and priestesses. **No evidence.**
- It dies by fire and rises from its ashes. **Phoenix borrowing.**
- It was certainly the Sasanian imperial flag emblem. **Unproven.**
- It performed a historically documented operation on a real Rostam. **Legend treated as clinical record.**
- Every Eurasian winged dog is a Simurgh. **Iconographic overreach.**
- Garuḍa, ʿAnqāʾ, roc, phoenix, and Simurgh are merely different names for one primordial bird. **Comparativist harmonization; historical equations occurred, but origins and corpora differ.**

## 11. Sources

### Principal primary texts and editions

- *Avesta*: *Rašn Yašt* 12.17; *Warahrān Yašt* 14.41; *Vidēvdād* 2.42. Karl F. Geldner’s critical edition remains foundational; Darmesteter’s translation is historically important but must be checked against modern Avestan philology. The Yašts are Younger Avestan, not Gathic.
- *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 27.50; 48.15; 62.37–41, editions/translations by T. D. Anklesaria and others.
- *Bundahišn* 13, 15–17, 24, especially B. T. Anklesaria’s *Zand-Ākāsīh: Iranian or Greater Bundahišn*.
- *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram* 3.39, 3.65, 23.2.
- Pahlavi *Rivāyat* accompanying the *Dādestān ī Dēnīg* 31c8.
- *Ayādgār ī Zarērān* 12.3.
- New Persian Rivāyats of Dārāb Hormazyār and Hormazyār Frāmarz, translated by B. N. Dhabhar.
- Ferdowsi, *Šāhnāma*, critically edited by Djalal Khaleghi-Motlagh; compare Jules Mohl, Arthur and Edmond Warner, and Dick Davis, with the recognition that English versions vary greatly in completeness.
- Ṯaʿālebī, *Ḡorar aḵbār molūk al-Fors*, for the parallel Zāl tradition.
- Farīd al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār, *Manṭeq al-ṭayr*, especially maqālas 3–15 and 45; consult Persian critical editions rather than abridged English adaptations.
- Aelian, *De natura animalium* 12.2, for Achaemenes’ eagle fosterage.
- Borges and Margarita Guerrero, “El Simurg,” *Manual de zoología fantástica* and *El libro de los seres imaginarios*; Borges, “El Simurgh y el águila.”

### Modern scholarship

The most important synthetic study is Hanns-Peter Schmidt’s “SIMORḠ” in *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, based on his “The Sēmurw: Of Birds and Dogs and Bats,” *Persica* 9 (1980). Its philological distinctions remain essential, though its artistic identification should be read beside Matteo Compareti’s more skeptical work on Sasanian textiles and the “so-called senmurv.” For Zoroastrian context and periodization, Mary Boyce’s *A History of Zoroastrianism*, Jean Kellens, Helmut Humbach, William Malandra, Prods Oktor Skjærvø, and John Hinnells remain central; none supplies evidence for an independent Simurgh cult. For epic and Iranian traditional history, Khaleghi-Motlagh, Ehsan Yarshater, and A. Shapur Shahbazi are fundamental. For ʿAṭṭār, Hellmut Ritter and the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article by Benedikt Reinert provide the necessary literary history.
