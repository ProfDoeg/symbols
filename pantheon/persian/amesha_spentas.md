# The Amesha Spentas: Research Dossier

**Class:** A collective of six supreme beneficent divine beings or hypostases in Zoroastrian tradition—later commonly rendered “archangels,” though that Christian-derived label is approximate. Together with Ahura Mazda, or in some enumerations with Spenta Mainyu, they form a sacred heptad.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The six constituent names occur throughout the Old Avestan *Gathas*, conventionally dated somewhere in the late second or early first millennium BCE, although they are not yet explicitly defined there as a closed group called “Amesha Spentas.” The collective expression first appears, in reversed order, as *spəṇtā aməšā* in Old Avestan *Yasna Haptanghaiti* 39.3. Both works survived orally; the oldest extant Avestan manuscripts are medieval, notably Copenhagen K7a, conventionally dated 1268 CE. Dating the compositions must therefore rest primarily on language, meter, relative chronology, and comparison with Vedic Sanskrit—not on contemporary manuscripts.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The collective

The standard Avestan expression is **Aməša Spəṇta** (𐬀𐬨𐬆𐬴𐬀 𐬯𐬞𐬆𐬥𐬙𐬀), plural *Aməša Spəṇtas*. Middle Persian forms include **Amahraspand**, **Amahraspandān**, and **Amešāspand**; New Persian has **امشاسپندان**, *Amešāspandān*. Older European spellings include *Amesha-Spenta*, *Ameshāspand*, and *Amshaspand*.

- *aməša-* means “immortal,” cognate with Vedic Sanskrit *amṛta-*.
- *spəṇta-* means “beneficent, life-furthering, strengthening,” hence contextually “holy” or “bounteous.” Translating it simply as “holy” loses its productive, increase-giving sense.
- Accordingly, defensible translations include “Bounteous Immortals,” “Beneficent Immortals,” and “Holy Immortals.”

**Securely attested early:** In *Yasna Haptanghaiti* 39.3 the order is reversed: “the beneficent immortals,” *spəṇtā aməšā*. The passage worships good beings “male and female,” ever-living and ever-benefiting. It does not enumerate a fixed six.

**Later canonical elaboration:** In the Younger Avesta the designation can be broad enough to encompass beneficent divinities generally: *Yasna* 1.2 calls Ātar, Fire, the most active of the Amesha Spentas, and *Visperad* 8.1 can use *aməša spəṇta* much like *yazata*, “being worthy of worship.” In normal later theological and scholarly usage, however, “the Amesha Spentas” means the following six.

### The six

| Old Avestan name | Middle/New Persian | Approximate meaning | Later material creation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vohu Manah** | Wahman, Bahman; بهمن | Good Mind, Good Purpose | Beneficent animals, especially cattle |
| **Aša Vahišta** | Ardwahišt, Ordībehešt; اردیبهشت | Best Truth, Best Order, Best Righteousness | Fire |
| **Xšaθra Vairya** | Šahrewar, Šahrīvar; شهریور | Desirable Dominion or Chosen Rule | Metal, and by learned extension the metallic/stone sky |
| **Spəṇta Ārmaiti** | Spandārmad, Spendarmad, Esfand; اسفند | Bounteous Devotion, Right-mindedness, Humble Receptivity | Earth |
| **Haurvatāt** | Hordād, Khordād; خرداد | Wholeness, Integrity, Health | Water |
| **Amərətāt** | Amurdād, Mordād; امرداد/مرداد | Immortality, Deathlessness | Plants |

The now-common form **Mordād** literally resembles “mortality”; traditionalists therefore favor **Amordād**, retaining the negative prefix and the meaning “immortality.”

### Gender and personhood

The first three names are grammatically neuter in Avestan; Ārmaiti, Haurvatāt, and Amərətāt are grammatically feminine. Grammatical gender does not map neatly onto later iconographic or theological gender. Ārmaiti is persistently understood as feminine and is called Ahura Mazda’s “daughter” in *Yasna* 45.4. Haurvatāt and Amərətāt, although grammatically feminine and occasionally represented as female in Middle Persian comparison, were often treated as male after Middle Persian lost grammatical gender.

The categories “abstract quality,” “personification,” “emanation,” “hypostasis,” “god,” and “angel” reflect different interpretive frameworks:

- **Securely attested early:** The Gathic terms can function both as ordinary nouns—good thought, truth, dominion, devotion, wholeness, immortality—and as agents whom Zarathustra addresses, approaches, sees, or receives gifts from.
- **Later canonical:** They are distinct *yazatas*, receive worship and offerings, have stations, assistants, creations, festivals, and demonic opponents.
- **Modern comparative label:** “Archangels” is useful for rank but misleading if it implies created messengers identical to Christian angels. The Amesha Spentas are also divine qualities, material creations, and modes of Ahura Mazda’s activity.
- **Modern Parsi reformist interpretation:** Many Parsis describe them chiefly as attributes or facets of the one God. This understanding was substantially reinforced by Martin Haug’s 1862 presentation of the Gathas as pure ethical monotheism during Parsi-Christian controversy in Bombay. It is now a genuine modern Zoroastrian interpretation, but should not be projected without qualification onto every Avestan and Pahlavi passage.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Old Avestan evidence

The *Gathas*, seventeen metrical hymns embedded in *Yasna* 28–34, 43–51, and 53, are the oldest stratum. Scholarly dates range roughly from 1500 to 900 BCE; around 1200–1000 BCE is a common working range, but no exact date is secure. The hymns’ eastern Iranian language and social world predate the western Old Persian inscriptions.

All six eventual members occur, but the Gathas do not say “there are six Amesha Spentas.” Particularly important passages include:

- **Yasna 28.2–3:** Zarathustra invokes Mazda, Vohu Manah, Aša, and Ārmaiti.  
- **Yasna 30.7:** Ārmaiti and Xšaθra participate in the saving or renewing order; the passage also supplies an early association between Ārmaiti and the earth.
- **Yasna 31.8:** Mazda is recognized as father of Vohu Manah and creator of Aša.
- **Yasna 34.11:** Haurvatāt and Amərətāt are paired and associated with sustenance, water, and plants.
- **Yasna 43.4, 44.3–4, 45.4:** Mazda’s relations to Aša, Vohu Manah, and Ārmaiti are expressed through fatherhood and creation.
- **Yasna 47.1:** The closest Gathic convergence of the later heptad: beneficent spirit, best thought, truth, words and deeds, dominion, wholeness, immortality, Mazda, and Ārmaiti are brought into one theological field.
- **Yasna 51.7:** Haurvatāt and Amərətāt again stand together.

Johanna Narten and Jean Kellens emphasize that this is still an **open constellation** rather than the later closed system: other entities, including Daēnā, Sraoša, and recompense, can occupy similarly central positions. Mary Boyce instead argued that the sixfold structure and creation correspondences are already intentionally, if allusively, present in the Gathas. That disagreement is fundamental.

The *Yasna Haptanghaiti* (*Yasna* 35–41), Old Avestan prose probably close in date to the Gathas, supplies the earliest collective title. *Yasna* 39.3 worships “the good beings, male and female, the beneficent immortals.” It establishes the category but still does not provide the later canonical list.

### Younger Avesta

The Younger Avestan corpus was composed and redacted over centuries, probably beginning well before the Achaemenid period and continuing into it; precise dates for individual passages are disputed.

- **Yasna 1 and later liturgical chapters:** invoke the heptad and individual members. *Yasna* 1.2’s description of Fire as the most active Amesha Spenta demonstrates that the term could remain taxonomically broad.
- **Yasna 12, the Fravarānē or creed:** the worshipper declares allegiance to the Mazda-worshipping religion, rejection of the daēvas, and praise and worship of the Amesha Spentas.
- **Yasht 2:** a short hymn devoted collectively to the Amesha Spentas.
- **Yasht 13.81–83:** describes Ahura Mazda as mingling himself with them and invokes their aid.
- **Yasht 19.15–18:** provides the mature collective portrait. The six and Spenta Mainyu are of one thought, one word, and one deed; they see one another’s souls; their father and ruler is Ahura Mazda; they are creators, makers, protectors, and guardians of Mazda’s creations.
- **Vendidad 10.9 and 19.43:** preserve the demonization of Indra, Saurva, and Nåŋhaiθya, important for comparisons between the Zoroastrian system and older Indo-Iranian gods.
- **Sīrōza:** orders the thirty day-divinities and their liturgical associates, institutionalizing the calendar theology.

### Achaemenid inscriptions and Greek witnesses

The Behistun inscription of Darius I, carved approximately 520–518 BCE in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian, invokes **Auramazdā**, condemns “the Lie,” and presents rightful kingship as divinely granted. It does **not** name the Amesha Spentas as a group or list the six. The Old Persian political vocabulary *arta-* and *xšaça-* is cognate with Avestan Aša and Xšaθra, but ordinary use of these concepts is not proof that the divinities were separately invoked.

The Naqš-e Rostam tomb relief of Darius shows the king, fire stand, winged symbol, and imperial throne-bearers. A proposal by A. Shapur Shahbazi interprets six prominent nobles around the king as an allusion to the six Amesha Spentas. This is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not an inscribed identification, and the figures remain historical courtiers in the image.

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, describes Persian sacrifice, elemental reverence, open-air worship, and the absence of Greek-style divine statues. He does not name the Amesha Spentas. His testimony is relevant to cultic setting, not direct attestation of the six.

Strabo, around the turn of the Common Era, names **Omanus** at Persian sanctuaries in Cappadocia. Omanus is widely identified with Vohu Manah/Wahman, but the equation is philological and historical, not explicit in Strabo.

Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 46–47, composed around the late first or early second century CE, says that Oromazes created six gods: Good Thought, Truth, Order, Wisdom, Wealth, and the maker of pleasure in honorable things. The first three transparently approximate Vohu Manah, Aša, and Xšaθra; the remaining renderings do not map exactly onto the canonical Iranian meanings. Plutarch is therefore valuable independent evidence for a sixfold Iranian system, but his account has passed through Greek interpretation.

### Middle Persian books

The Pahlavi texts were written or compiled chiefly in the ninth and tenth centuries CE, while preserving much older Sasanian exegesis and translations of Avestan works now lost.

- **Bundahišn:** gives the most extensive cosmogonic system. Ohrmazd produces the Amahraspands from his own selfhood; each accepts a material creation, assistants are assigned, and the good creation fights Ahriman’s counter-creation. Its surviving Iranian and Indian recensions differ.
- **Dēnkard:** preserves summaries of lost Avestan *nasks*, theological classifications, and the Sasanian-derived learned tradition.
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad**, probably compiled around the sixth century but surviving in much later manuscripts: says Ohrmazd created the Amesha Spentas from his own light and repeatedly joins “Amesha Spentas and yazads.”
- **Selections of Zādspram:** narrate creation, eschatological conflict, and the eventual defeat of evil; they also compare Zarathustra’s daughters to Spandārmad, Hordād, and Amurdād.
- **Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg:** compares Ohrmazd’s production of the Amesha Spentas to one torch being lit from another.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag:** belongs to the same cosmological world but is chiefly an otherworld journey; the collective is part of the heavenly order rather than the work’s principal narrative subject.
- **Zand ī Wahman Yasn:** a medieval apocalyptic work named for Vohu Manah, not a surviving Avestan Bahman Yasht; it should not be mistaken for an intact ancient yasht.

### Islamic-period Persian literature

Ferdowsi’s *Shahnameh*, completed around 1010 CE, preserves Iranian royal legend but does not offer a systematic dossier of the six. Names such as Bahman occur primarily as royal names, calendar terms, or lexical inheritances. Treating every *Shahnameh* “Bahman” as the Amesha Spenta Vohu Manah is erroneous.

### Manuscript record

The Avestan works are ancient compositions preserved through oral-liturgical transmission and a Sasanian written archetype. The surviving manuscript corpus is medieval and early modern:

- Copenhagen **K7a**, 1268? CE, is generally treated as the oldest extant Avestan manuscript.
- **J2** and **K5**, early fourteenth century, are among the oldest Pahlavi Yasna witnesses.
- Complete surviving Yasht collections are later still.
- The Avestan Digital Archive photographs and catalogs manuscripts dispersed among Copenhagen, Mumbai, Navsari, Tehran, London, Munich, and Paris.

Karl Friedrich Geldner’s late nineteenth-century *Avesta* remains foundational; modern work by Helmut Humbach, Jean Kellens and Éric Pirart, Prods Oktor Skjærvø, and the ritual-manuscript work associated with Alberto Cantera has substantially revised translation and textual method. Mills and Darmesteter remain historically important but cannot be treated as the last word.

## 3. Domains, Attributes, and Iconography

### The ethical-material correspondences

The mature system joins mental quality, divine person, ritual object, and material creation:

- **Vohu Manah / cattle:** Good Mind makes right discernment possible and is especially manifested in beneficent animals. Milk or meat offerings represent him in ritual.
- **Aša Vahišta / fire:** Aša is truth, cosmic order, correct ritual, and righteousness. Fire makes truth visible and functions in judgment and ordeal.
- **Xšaθra Vairya / metal and sky:** Xšaθra is rightful dominion and the power that protects the poor. Pahlavi theology makes it guardian of metals. To reconcile this with the older creation “sky,” priests classified the hard sky as stone, crystal, and metal.
- **Spəṇta Ārmaiti / earth:** Ārmaiti is responsive devotion, humility, and right-minded participation. Earth is simultaneously her material manifestation.
- **Haurvatāt / water:** Wholeness and health, paired with life-sustaining water.
- **Amərətāt / plants:** Immortality, paired with vegetation and food. With Haurvatāt, Amərətāt supplies the complementary food-and-drink pair.

Humans have the responsibility to keep these creations flourishing and free from pollution. This is not merely symbolic correspondence: in traditional ontology, spiritual and material aspects form a single reality.

### Calendar

The first seven days of every thirty-day Zoroastrian month are assigned to Ohrmazd and the six in their standard order:

1. Ohrmazd  
2. Bahman  
3. Ardwahišt  
4. Šahrewar  
5. Spandārmad  
6. Hordād  
7. Amurdād  

Each also names a month, although not in that order: Farvardīn is followed by Ordībehešt, Khordād, then later Šahrīvar, Bahman, and Esfand; Amurdād is the fifth month. When a divinity’s day and month coincide, a name-day festival is celebrated. This calendar is commonly reconstructed as a late Achaemenid development; direct documentation of its creation is absent.

The six are also correlated with the six seasonal **Gāhāmbārs**, while the concluding/all-souls observance completes the heptadic pattern. The precise historical priority of feast, creation, and divine correspondence remains debated.

### Plants, colors, and ritual objects

Middle Persian calendrical systems assign each day-divinity a flower and supporting divinities. These are **later learned correspondences**, not Gathic attributes. Modern tables that assign the six fixed gemstones, chakras, planets, rainbow colors, or magical seals are normally Theosophical, New Age, or internet-era inventions unless a specific Pahlavi or ritual source is supplied.

### Iconography

No securely identified ancient image depicts the six as six individually recognizable anthropomorphic beings.

- The natural creations—fire, earth, water, vegetation, cattle, metal—function as their visible presences.
- The Naqš-e Rostam proposal that Darius and six nobles mirror Ohrmazd and the six is plausible symbolism but unproven.
- The winged disk or winged human symbol over Achaemenid kings has variously been identified as Ahura Mazda, royal glory, or a fravaši. It is not securely “the Amesha Spentas.”
- Modern illustrations showing six winged, haloed humanoid archangels borrow Jewish, Christian, Islamic, or fantasy visual vocabulary. They are **modern invention**, not recovered Avestan iconography.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin and hierarchy

The Gathas call Ahura Mazda creator or father of several of the qualities. *Yasht* 19 makes him the one father and ruler of the united group. *Bundahišn* 1 says Ohrmazd produced them from his own selfhood; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 8.2 says from his own light; the *Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg* uses the image of torch from torch.

These are complementary in later theology but should not be collapsed into one biological genealogy. The language expresses dependence, consubstantiality, emanation, and distinct agency in different registers.

Two heptadic enumerations coexist:

- Ahura Mazda + the six.
- Spenta Mainyu + the six, with Spenta Mainyu functioning as Mazda’s creative or beneficent spirit and sometimes virtually identical with him.

No Avestan text presents all six as siblings born through sexual generation. Calling them “sons and daughters of Ahura Mazda” is an interpretive simplification.

### Documented divine interactions

The following entries cover specific collective encounters or joint acts in the principal extant corpora. Routine liturgical lists in which names merely stand beside one another are identified as joint invocation rather than narrative interaction.

- **Ahura Mazda — creates, fathers, rules, and “mingles himself” with the six.** *Yasna* 31.8, 44.3–4, 45.4; *Yasht* 13.81 and 19.16–18; Old and Younger Avestan. **Secure early relationship / later canonical elaboration.**
- **Spenta Mainyu — stands with the six as the active beneficent spirit and sometimes the seventh member.** *Yasna* 47.1; *Yasht* 19.16–18. **Early attested / later systematized.**
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — attacks the creation guarded by the Amesha Spentas; they and their creations resist him until the renovation.** *Bundahišn* 1–6. **Later canonical cosmogonic elaboration preserving Sasanian Zand.**
- **Vohu Manah — approaches or mediates revelation to Zarathustra and is repeatedly invoked with Mazda and Aša.** *Yasna* 28.2–6, 43.7–15. **Securely attested early.**
- **Aša — acts with Mazda and Vohu Manah as the order through which revelation, judgment, and gifts reach the worshipper.** *Yasna* 28–34 passim. **Securely attested early.**
- **Spəṇta Ārmaiti — is called Mazda’s daughter and cooperates with Aša, Xšaθra, and human devotion.** *Yasna* 45.4, 47.1. **Securely attested early.**
- **Haurvatāt and Amərətāt — form a recurrent dyad granting wholeness, immortality, food, and drink.** *Yasna* 33.8, 34.11, 51.7. **Securely attested early.**
- **Ātar — is called the most active Amesha Spenta and manifests Aša in ritual and judgment.** *Yasna* 1.2; Gathic fire passages including 34.4 and 43.4. **Early association / later taxonomic statement.**
- **Haoma — serves as swift sacrificer for Ahura Mazda and the Amesha Spentas.** *Mithra Yasht* 10.89–90. **Younger Avestan canonical.**
- **Mithra — receives sacrifice from Haoma in the same ritual order in which Haoma serves Mazda and the Amesha Spentas; later becomes a calendrical co-worker.** *Yasht* 10.88–90; *Bundahišn* 26. **Canonical joint cult, not a discrete mythic meeting.**
- **Sraoša — joins the beneficent divine order and its battle against demons; he is not one of the canonical six, although his early prominence made him a candidate in scholarly reconstructions of an originally open group.** *Yasna* 57; *Vendidad* 19. **Early divine associate / later canonical ally.**
- **Fravašis — assist the good creation and its rulers; they serve as collaborators of Haurvatāt in bringing water and rain.** *Yasht* 13; *Greater Bundahišn* 26.18. **Younger Avestan and Pahlavi canonical.**
- **Tištrya and Wāta — assist Haurvatāt in seizing, distributing, and raining water.** *Greater Bundahišn* 26.18. **Later canonical systematization.**
- **Rashnu, Aštād, Zamyād, and the Glories — serve Amurdād and take part in judgment and protection of plant-life.** *Greater Bundahišn* 26.19. **Later canonical systematization.**
- **Airyaman — joins Aša/Ardwahišt at the final renovation, when molten metal purifies creation and overcomes Až/Greed.** *Zādspram* 34.38–39 and Pahlavi eschatology. **Later canonical elaboration from lost Avestan tradition.**
- **Saoshyant — completes the renovation in the divine order protected by the Amesha Spentas.** Younger Avestan eschatological passages and Pahlavi books. **Canonical eschatology; surviving full narrative is late.**

### Demonic counterparts

The perfectly matched six-versus-six scheme is not Gathic. The Gathas know Aka Manah, Druj, Aēšma, and the concept *tarōmaiti*, but not a stable table of six archdemons.

Pahlavi systematization commonly pairs:

- Vohu Manah with **Aka Manah/Akōman**, Evil Mind.
- Aša Vahišta with **Indra** or the Lie.
- Xšaθra Vairya with **Saurva/Sāwar**, oppressive misrule.
- Spəṇta Ārmaiti with **Nåŋhaiθya/Tarōmāiti**, disobedience or arrogant wrong-mindedness.
- Haurvatāt with **Tauru/Tarīč**, destruction, disease, or thirst.
- Amərətāt with **Zairi/Zarīč**, hunger, poison, or decay.

Lists vary, and the correspondences are not uniform between the *Vendidad*, *Bundahišn*, and later summaries. A rigid chart presented as Zarathustra’s original teaching is therefore a **later learned systematization**.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

The Amesha Spentas have fewer independent adventure narratives than Greek or Norse gods. Their mythology is primarily cosmogonic, ritual, and eschatological.

### The creation of the divine heptad

**Early core:** The Gathas describe Mazda as father or maker of several divine qualities but do not narrate a sequential birth.

**Younger Avestan form:** *Yasht* 19 presents the group as a perfectly coordinated divine college: same thought, word, and deed, under one father and ruler.

**Pahlavi form:** Ohrmazd creates them from himself or his light before material creation. The torch analogy explains plurality without depletion: one light kindles another while remaining whole.

### Creation and guardianship of the world

In the *Bundahišn*, Ohrmazd first creates the spiritual prototypes and then the material world. The Amesha Spentas accept or guard creations that serve as fortifications against Ahriman:

- sky/metal and rightful dominion;
- water and wholeness;
- earth and devotion;
- plants and immortality;
- cattle and good mind;
- fire and truth;
- humankind, or the righteous priest, as Ohrmazd/Spenta Mainyu’s special creation.

The sources disagree on the order and even the count of creations. Some lists begin with sky and end with fire; others place fire throughout all creation. The metal-sky relationship is a particularly transparent example of priestly reconciliation between inherited schemes.

### The assault of Ahriman

In *Bundahišn* 3–6, Ahriman breaks into material creation, polluting water, earth, plants, cattle, humanity, and fire. The spiritual beings and material creations counterattack. For ninety days and nights the heavenly beings fight the demons and confine them within the world. Evil is not immediately annihilated because material history is the arena in which it will be exposed, exhausted, and finally rendered powerless.

This developed narrative is **Middle Persian canonical elaboration**, not a continuous story found in the Gathas.

### Final renovation

At the *frašō.kərətiš*/*frašgird*, the savior Saoshyant and the beneficent divine powers restore creation. Aša and Airyaman are associated in Pahlavi texts with the river of molten metal that purifies all beings; Haurvatāt and Amərətāt correspond to complete health and deathlessness. The universe becomes unaging, uncorrupted, and immortal.

Again, the Avesta supplies components, while the connected narrative survives chiefly in Middle Persian books.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No separate temple cult securely demonstrated

No archaeological structure can securely be identified as “a temple of the Amesha Spentas” collectively. They were normally worshipped through the Yasna liturgy, calendar, festivals, and care for their material creations rather than through six cult statues.

Classical reports of temples of Omanus may provide localized evidence for Vohu Manah in Cappadocia, but Omanus’s precise cult and image remain uncertain. The Amesha Spentas should not be assigned temples of Anāhitā, Mithra, or fire simply because all belong to Iranian religion.

### Yasna and offerings

The Yasna is a performed sacrifice, not merely a scripture. In traditional interpretation the heptad is present through:

- priest or righteous human — Ohrmazd/Spenta Mainyu;
- milk and animal products — Vohu Manah;
- fire — Aša Vahišta;
- metal vessels — Xšaθra Vairya;
- consecrated earth or ritual precinct — Ārmaiti;
- water — Haurvatāt;
- Haoma and other plants — Amərətāt.

The creed in *Yasna* 12 explicitly makes praise of the Amesha Spentas a marker of Zoroastrian identity.

### Sacrifice

Animal offerings existed in ancient Iranian and Zoroastrian ritual; cattle belong especially to Vohu Manah’s sphere. Sources emphasize correct, controlled sacrifice and condemn cruelty or predatory slaughter. Haoma’s role as “swift sacrificer” has been interpreted as requiring rapid killing to reduce suffering.

A dedicated search found **no evidence for a regular cult of human sacrifice to the Amesha Spentas**. Classical reports of Persian human sacrifice concern royal, funerary, military, or outsider narratives and are themselves disputed; they cannot be transferred to this collective without a source.

There is likewise no evidence of temple prostitution connected to them.

### Gāhāmbārs

The six seasonal festivals honor creation and communal cooperation. Later theology aligns each festival with an Amesha Spenta and a creation. Feasting, priestly recitation, charity, and shared meals remain central. The festivals are collective obligations, not dramatic reenactments of a single myth.

### Ethical cult

Care for the protected creation is itself worship:

- good treatment of cattle honors Vohu Manah;
- maintaining pure and useful fire honors Aša;
- just rule and responsible use of metal honor Xšaθra;
- cultivating rather than polluting earth honors Ārmaiti;
- safeguarding water honors Haurvatāt;
- tending plants and sharing food honors Amərətāt.

This ethical-material practice is more securely traditional than many modern occult correspondences.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Old Avestan period

The eventual six exist as ethically and ritually charged qualities with varying degrees of agency. They cluster around Mazda but do not yet form a plainly closed angelic bureaucracy. Abstract meaning and divine personhood coexist.

### Younger Avestan period

The category becomes explicit. The six acquire a stable collective identity, are praised and worshipped, and appear as creators and guardians under Ahura Mazda. The creed makes devotion to them confessional. Creation correspondences, collective epithets, and a heptadic structure become clearer.

### Achaemenid period, approximately 550–330 BCE

Royal inscriptions securely attest Auramazdā, truth versus lie, and divinely authorized dominion, but not the six by name. Avestan religion and Achaemenid ideology overlap linguistically and conceptually without permitting simple identification.

The thirty-day calendar and heptadic first week are often dated to the late Achaemenid period. That is a historical reconstruction, not an event documented by a dated decree.

### Hellenistic and Parthian periods

Iranian religion diversified regionally. Strabo’s Omanus and Plutarch’s six creations of Oromazes show that members and structure were intelligible to Greek observers. Their translations also demonstrate reinterpretation: the Iranian abstractions become Greek “gods” with Hellenized domains.

No evidence supports identifying the Amesha Spentas with the seven planetary gods as an ancient Zoroastrian norm.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

Priestly institutions organized Zoroastrian doctrine on an imperial scale. The six-creations, six-demons, calendar assistants, and eschatological roles received elaborate exposition in the lost Avestan canon and its Middle Persian Zand.

Christian polemicists accused Zoroastrians of worshipping elements. That allegation documents religious controversy, not an impartial definition of Zoroastrian theology. Zoroastrians understood the creations as manifestations and charges of the divine beings, not simply as autonomous material gods.

Sasanian rulers and priests also used Zoroastrian institutions politically. Kerdīr’s inscriptions boast of suppressing or converting adherents of other religions, and Yazdegerd II attempted to impose Zoroastrianism on Christian Armenians. These acts belong to the political history of the religious system, although no source makes the Amesha Spentas individually instigators of persecution.

### Manichaean adaptation

From the third century CE, Middle Persian Manichaean translations used **Amahraspandān** for Mani’s five Light Elements—ether, wind, light, water, and fire. This is a **syncretistic translation strategy**, not evidence that the Zoroastrian six were originally Manichaean or elemental pentads.

### After the Muslim conquest

The fall of the Sasanian state removed imperial support. Conversion to Islam proceeded unevenly between the eighth and thirteenth centuries through political defeat, taxation, social mobility, intermarriage, institutional decline, and at times coercion or destruction of clergy, temples, and texts. Zoroastrians were generally accorded protected *dhimmī* status but experienced variable restriction and persecution.

The Amesha Spentas survived through domestic ritual, priestly recitation, calendars, names, Gāhāmbārs, and Pahlavi literature. They did not simply disappear into Islamic angels, although Iranian angel vocabulary and individual names such as Soruš survived in Persian culture.

### Parsi India

Migrants from Iran established Zoroastrian communities in western India during the early medieval period. Liturgical devotion, calendar observance, and creation ethics continued.

In nineteenth-century Bombay, Christian missionaries attacked Zoroastrian “polytheism” and elemental worship. Martin Haug’s 1862 account argued that Zarathustra taught ethical monotheism and that the Amesha Spentas were originally abstract attributes. Parsi reformists adopted this as an effective theological answer. Mary Boyce criticized the projection of this modern abstraction-only reading back into the Gathas, while other philologists—such as Stanley Insler in his treatment of Vohu Manah—have independently favored strongly conceptual readings.

Modern Parsi views consequently range from:

- six divine beings worthy of reverence;
- hypostases or emanations of Ahura Mazda;
- modes of divine action;
- ethical attributes to be cultivated;
- symbolic guardians of the environment.

These positions coexist and cannot be reduced to a single “modern Parsi belief.”

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and reform

The Amesha Spentas became central to nineteenth-century disputes over whether Zoroastrianism was monotheistic, dualistic, or polytheistic. The classification often reflected Christian theological categories more than Iranian ones.

Haug’s abstraction/monotheism model profoundly shaped Anglophone Parsi catechisms. Dhalla’s *Zoroastrian Theology* and *History of Zoroastrianism* used “archangels” systematically, making the Christian analogy familiar while still treating the six as specifically Iranian.

### Theosophy and occultism

The Theosophical movement incorporated the Amesha Spentas by the 1880s. Blavatskian and successor literature equated them with:

- Dhyāni-Buddhas;
- Prajāpatis;
- Sephiroth;
- Elohim;
- Gnostic cosmocrators;
- planetary spirits;
- “builders” or occult intelligences.

These equations are **modern syncretistic inventions**. No Avestan text identifies the six with the planets, Buddhist celestial Buddhas, or the Kabbalistic Sephiroth.

Parsi esoteric movements, notably Behramshah Nowroji Shroff’s twentieth-century Ilm-e-Khshnoom, responded to both Western occultism and colonial modernity with their own mystical readings. Such systems are historically real modern Zoroastrian developments but should not be mislabeled ancient doctrine.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Small modern revivalist, conversion-oriented, and reconstructionist communities invoke the Amesha Spentas in English-language prayers and sometimes render them as gods, angels, or divine aspects. Reliable membership figures for Amesha-Spenta-centered neopagan worship do not exist.

Internet charts assigning them chakras, crystals, tarot cards, fixed angelic sigils, weekdays, or Western astrological planets generally lack premodern Iranian sources. They are best labeled **New Age or modern occult synthesis**.

### Environmental reception

The creation correspondences lend themselves naturally to ecological interpretation. Modern Zoroastrians often present protection of animals, fire, metal/resources, soil, water, and plants as an ancient environmental ethic. The correspondences and duty of care are genuinely premodern; framing them in the language of environmentalism and sustainability is modern.

### Literature and commercial culture

Individual names—especially Bahman, Ordībehešt, Šahrīvar, Khordād, and Esfand—remain Persian personal and month names, often without conscious invocation of a divine being. Fantasy art and games sometimes reuse “Amesha Spenta” as a name for angels, machines, monsters, or magical ranks. Such works have not produced a single dominant popular iconography comparable to the modern hammer-bearing Thor or jackal-headed Anubis.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Were they abstractions that became gods?

One model describes a linear development:

1. ordinary ethical nouns;
2. poetic personifications in the Gathas;
3. independent divine beings in the Younger Avesta;
4. fully developed archangels in Pahlavi theology.

This model is associated in different forms with Haug and later historical-critical scholarship.

Boyce and Lommel objected that ancient Iranian thought did not maintain a modern boundary between abstract and concrete. Aša could simultaneously be truth, cosmic structure, divine agent, fire, and a quality embodied by a righteous person. On this reading, “abstraction versus person” is a false binary.

The cautious conclusion is that grammatical nouns, theological qualities, and addressed divine agents coexist from the earliest texts; what demonstrably develops later is the closed list, administrative hierarchy, and systematic network of correspondences.

### Narten and the “open list”

Johanna Narten’s *Die Aməša Spəṇtas im Avesta* argued that the Gathas do not know the later closed group as such and that the Old Avestan divine constellation remains open. Kellens developed this approach. Boyce instead read Gathic allusions to creations as evidence that the mature structure was already largely present.

The surviving text cannot decisively settle how much unstated system the poets and audiences assumed.

### The Vedic Ādityas

Comparisons rest on genuine Indo-Iranian features:

- Iranian *aməša-* and Sanskrit *amṛta-* are cognate.
- Aša is cognate with Vedic ṛta, cosmic and ritual order.
- Ārmaiti has a Vedic cognate, Aramati.
- Vedic gods can be called “all the immortals.”
- Both traditions develop groups of moral-cosmic divinities.

But no Vedic text gives a six-member group matching the Amesha Spentas name for name.

Georges Dumézil’s 1945 *Naissance des archanges* reconstructed the six as a Zoroastrian transformation of Indo-European trifunctional theology. He related sovereign, warrior, and fertility functions to paired Amesha Spentas and compared Haurvatāt-Amərətāt with the Nāsatyas. Supporters included Widengren and Duchesne-Guillemin.

Objections include:

- the Mitanni treaty gods used by Dumézil are linguistically Indic, not proof of a fixed common Indo-Iranian pantheon;
- assignments can be selective or circular;
- Haurvatāt and Amərətāt do not securely correspond to the Nāsatyas;
- the Gathic group itself may not yet be closed.

The comparison remains a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not a demonstrated genealogy.

### Christian and Jewish archangels

Similarity is real at a structural level: a supreme god surrounded by a small number of exalted beneficent beings, sometimes seven, with cosmic functions. Persian-period contact makes influence historically possible.

Direct derivation is not proven:

- the Hebrew Bible does not present a canonical list of seven named archangels;
- seven principal angels become prominent in Second Temple works such as *Tobit* and *1 Enoch*;
- the Amesha Spentas are qualities and creations as well as beings;
- their six-plus-one enumeration does not correspond name-for-name with Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Uriel, and later angel lists;
- sevenfold heavenly groups also occur in Mesopotamian and broader Mediterranean traditions.

Darmesteter’s late theory reversed the influence, dating the Gathas extremely late and deriving Vohu Manah from Philonic Logos speculation. The linguistic antiquity of Old Avestan makes that chronology untenable. Claims that Christian archangels are simply “renamed Amesha Spentas” are therefore **unproven modern diffusionism**.

### Primary-evidence limitations

- No autograph or ancient manuscript survives.
- The earliest compositions were transmitted orally for centuries.
- Extant manuscripts derive from a narrow medieval transmission.
- Much of the Sasanian Avesta is lost; the *Dēnkard* preserves summaries.
- Pahlavi texts often identify themselves as Zand of lost Avestan revelation, but the age of any particular doctrine must be evaluated individually.
- No securely identified portrait cycle of the six survives.
- Greek authors translate Iranian ideas through Greek categories.
- Achaemenid inscriptions cannot be made to say “Amesha Spentas” merely because they praise Ahura Mazda, truth, and kingship.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| c. 1500–900 BCE, highly disputed | Probable composition range of the Gathas. The six eventual names occur, but neither a numbered group nor the collective title is explicit. |
| Near the Gathic period | *Yasna Haptanghaiti* 39.3 supplies the earliest collective phrase *spəṇtā aməšā*. |
| Early first millennium BCE onward | Younger Avestan composition develops the collective, its worship, and its relation to creation. |
| c. 520–518 BCE | Darius’s Behistun inscription invokes Auramazdā and opposes Truth to the Lie but does not name the six. |
| Late sixth–fourth centuries BCE, reconstructed | Formation of the thirty-day Zoroastrian calendar, with the heptad assigned the first seven days. |
| Fifth century BCE | Herodotus describes Persian worship and sacrifice but does not mention the collective. |
| Fourth century BCE | Achaemenid tomb imagery is reproduced; its possible heptadic symbolism remains disputed. |
| First century BCE–first century CE | Strabo records temples of Omanus, probably Vohu Manah, in Cappadocia. |
| Late first–early second century CE | Plutarch records Oromazes’s creation of six gods, the clearest Greco-Roman report of the collective structure. |
| Third century CE onward | Manichaean Middle Persian uses *Amahraspandān* for the Five Light Elements. |
| 224–651 CE | Sasanian priestly and imperial institutions elaborate the creation, calendar, demon, and eschatological systems preserved in the Zand and Pahlavi books. |
| Sixth century CE, approximate | Probable compilation period of *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, although surviving witnesses are much later. |
| Seventh century onward | Muslim conquest ends Sasanian state patronage; conversion and institutional decline proceed over centuries. |
| Ninth–tenth centuries | Principal redaction or compilation of the *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, *Zādspram*, and related Pahlavi works. |
| c. 1010 | Completion of Ferdowsi’s *Shahnameh*; divine names survive mainly as cultural, calendrical, and personal vocabulary rather than as a systematic sixfold theology. |
| 1268? | Copenhagen K7a, generally the oldest surviving Avestan manuscript. |
| Early fourteenth century | J2, K5, and related manuscripts preserve major Yasna and Pahlavi traditions. |
| 1771 | Anquetil-Duperron publishes the first major European translation of the Avesta, initiating sustained European study. |
| 1862 | Martin Haug publishes *Essays on the Sacred Language, Writings and Religion of the Parsis*; his attribute-centered monotheistic reading becomes influential among Parsi reformists. |
| 1875–77 | James Darmesteter publishes studies of Haurvatāt, Amərətāt, Ohrmazd, and Ahriman. |
| 1880–95 | Darmesteter, Mills, West, and Geldner publish translations and critical textual work that define early Western study. |
| 1880s onward | Theosophists equate the Amesha Spentas with planets, Sephiroth, Dhyāni-Buddhas, and other occult heptads. |
| 1916 | Bernhard Geiger publishes *Die Aməša Spəntas*. |
| 1945 | Dumézil publishes *Naissance des archanges*, interpreting the six through Indo-European trifunctionality. |
| 1982 | Narten’s study strengthens the case that the Gathic constellation was open rather than the later fixed six. |
| 1989 | Mary Boyce’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article synthesizes the immanent-divinity and creation-correspondence interpretation. |
| Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries | Manuscript digitization, especially the Avestan Digital Archive, shifts attention from abstract reconstructed texts toward ritual manuscripts and performance. |
| Present | Iranian Zoroastrian and Parsi communities continue to invoke the Amesha Spentas through liturgy, calendar observances, Gāhāmbārs, prayers, and ethical care for creation; understandings range from divine beings to attributes or aspects of Ahura Mazda. |
