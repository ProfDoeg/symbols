# Gayomart: Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** primordial human/protoplast and righteous mortal (*ašavan*) in the Avesta; primordial giant and sixth material creation in Middle Persian Zoroastrian cosmology; first prophet or recipient of revelation in learned Zoroastrian systematization; first king and culture hero in Iranian national epic; Adam-equivalent in Manichaean and some Islamic adaptations. He is not ordinarily classified as a *yazata* or god.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Young Avestan *Gaiia Marətan* in *Yasna* 26.10 and especially *Frawardīn Yašt* 13.87. The hymn’s precise composition date is disputed, but it belongs to the pre-Sasanian Young Avestan corpus, conventionally placed within the late second to first millennia BCE. No securely identified Achaemenid inscription, contemporary image, or datable archaeological cult object names him. The surviving Avestan manuscripts are medieval and early modern witnesses to a much older orally transmitted text.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Gayomart is the Iranian primordial human: the first embodied man, ancestor or generative source of humanity, and chronological counterpart to the final renovator Saošyant. In the sparse surviving Avesta he is remembered through his *fravaši* and as the first to receive or heed Ahura Mazda’s thought and teaching. The Middle Persian cosmological books turn this compressed notice into an elaborate cosmogony: Ohrmazd creates Gayomart radiant and enormous; Ahriman’s assault brings about his death; metals issue from his body and the first human couple eventually grows from his purified seed. Iranian national history transforms the protoplast into the first world-king. Manichaeism reassigns his Iranian name to Adam in an anti-cosmic creation narrative, while Islamic historians variously identify him with Adam, Adam’s son, Gomer, or a primordial Persian king.

### Names and linguistic history

- **Avestan:** *Gaiia Marətan*, commonly normalized *Gayō Marətan* or *Gaya Marətan*. The conventional meaning is “mortal life,” “living mortal,” or “mortal living being”: *gaiia-* concerns life, while *marətan-* is “mortal.” The two-word expression subsequently became a personal name. **Tier: securely attested early material.**

- **Middle Persian, Book Pahlavi:** *Gayōmard*, *Gayōmart*, reconstructed more precisely by Carlo Cereti as *Gayōmarθ* from Pahlavi, Persian, and Arabic reflexes. Pahlavi spellings are heterographic and do not alone determine the final consonant. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**

- **Manichaean Middle Persian:** *Gēhmurd/Gehmurd*. Although functionally equated with Gayomart and Adam, Cereti cautions that this form reflects a different secondary etymological analysis. **Tier: syncretistic identification.**

- **New Persian:** کیومرث, *Kayūmarṯ/Keyumars/Kayumars/Kaiumers*; also *Gayūmart*. The initial *g/k* variation and different treatments of the final spirant account for many modern spellings. **Tier: medieval transmission.**

- **Arabic:** جيومرت or variants transliterated *Jayūmart*, *Jiyūmart*, *Kayūmarṯ*. **Tier: medieval adaptation.**

### Titles

- **“Righteous” or “holy” Gayomart:** Avestan *aṣ̌aona-* in *Yasht* 13.87, marking him as aligned with *aša*, the right cosmic and ritual order. **Tier: securely attested early material.**

- **Garšāh, “mountain king”:** found in the Pāzand *Aogəmadaēcā* 85–87 and plausibly the older of two competing titles. It suits the mountain-dwelling king of epic tradition. **Tier: late/uncertain textual tradition.**

- **Gilšāh, “clay king”:** present in a secondary Pahlavi form and common in Islamic accounts. Scholars have proposed that *garšāh* was reshaped as *gilšāh* under the influence of Near Eastern stories of humanity created from clay. The direction of change is probable, not proven. **Tier: medieval syncretistic reinterpretation.**

- **Mahārāja, “great king”:** the Sanskrit *Aogəmadaēcā*, probably later than the twelfth century. **Tier: regional learned translation.**

- **First messenger/prophet:** the *Dēnkard* calls him the first envoy from Ohrmazd to embodied humanity. This is absent from the extant Gathas and expands *Yasht* 13.87. **Tier: priestly systematization.**

- **Pēšdādian founder:** Ferdowsi places him at the beginning of the first royal dynasty, though the epithet *pēšdād*—Avestan *para-dāta*, conventionally “first-established”—is more securely associated in Avestan passages with Hōšang. **Tier: national-epic systematization.**

### What he is not

Gayomart has sometimes been called a “god,” “demigod,” or “angel” in popular summaries. None is an exact native classification. The Avesta venerates his *fravaši*, as it does those of other righteous humans; Pahlavi cosmology makes him superhuman in scale and radiance but still the prototype of embodied humanity. “Primordial human” is the least misleading description.

No ancient source securely identifies Gayomart with a Greek or Roman deity. Proposed equations with Roman Mithraic figures belong to modern comparative scholarship and remain disputed.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Avestan evidence

#### *Frawardīn Yašt* 13.87

The central early notice reads approximately:

> “We worship the *fravaši* of righteous Gaiia Marətan, who first listened to the thought and teaching of Ahura Mazda, from whom [or through whom] Ahura Mazda fashioned the peoples/seed of the Aryan lands.”

The wording establishes four points: Gayomart is righteous; his *fravaši* receives cultic reverence; he is first in relation to Mazda’s teaching; and humanity or the Iranian peoples proceed from him. It does **not** narrate Ahriman’s attack, his thirty-year life, metals arising from his corpse, or the rhubarb birth of the first couple. Those details survive only in later texts.

*Yasht* 13 is composite. Its final redaction cannot be dated precisely; its mythic and liturgical materials are older than their medieval manuscript witnesses. Its list of righteous *fravašis* frames human sacred history from Gayomart to Saošyant. The hymn remains liturgically active in Zoroastrian ceremonies, including forms of the Āfrīnagān. **Tier: securely attested early material, with uncertain absolute date.**

#### *Yasna* 26.10

This passage reveres the *fravašis* of righteous men and women “from Gayomart to the victorious Saošyant.” Its importance lies less in narrative than in temporal architecture: Gayomart is humanity’s beginning and Saošyant its consummation. **Tier: securely attested early material.**

#### Other Avestan allusions

Young Avestan passages pair “the living/mortal man” with the sole-created Ox, suggesting that compilers knew a larger first-man/first-bovine cosmogony. The extant Avesta, however, preserves no continuous Gayomart narrative.

Gayomart does **not** appear as a character in the Gathas, the oldest linguistic layer of the Avesta. Claims that Zarathustra himself taught the full Bundahishn story therefore exceed the extant evidence. Nor does the *Vendīdād* preserve that story. **Tier: absence of evidence.**

### Lost Avestan material reported by the *Dēnkard*

Books 8–9 of the ninth-century *Dēnkard* summarize lost books of the Sasanian Avesta. Its account of the *Čihrdād Nask* says that this lost work narrated Gayomart’s bodily creation, the generation of Mašīa and Mašīānag, and Hōšang’s establishment of kingship. This is valuable testimony to a Sasanian Avestan/Zand corpus no longer extant, but it is indirect: the *Dēnkard* is centuries later and its summary cannot restore the lost wording. **Tier: learned preservation of lost canonical material.**

### *Bundahišn*

The *Bundahišn* (“Primal Creation”) is a Middle Persian compilation based substantially on Sasanian Zand traditions but redacted after the Islamic conquest, commonly in the ninth century. Two recensions survive: the shorter Indian and fuller Iranian or Greater *Bundahišn*. Important manuscripts are much later; the fuller Iranian recension became known to scholarship after a manuscript was brought from Iran to India around 1870.

Principal Gayomart passages include:

- 1a.13: creation, dimensions, radiance, location opposite the primordial Ox;
- 3.12: Gayomart among the creations and under Ohrmazd’s guardianship;
- 4.19–25: demonic assault, Sleep, and the ordained thirty-year survival;
- 6F: horoscope, death, seed, and the genesis of metals;
- 14: growth of Mašīa and Mašīānag and the descent of human races;
- 34: resurrection and Renovation.

This is the fullest Zoroastrian source, but its elaborated form should not simply be projected unchanged into the Achaemenid period. **Tier: later canonical elaboration grounded partly in lost Sasanian exegesis.**

### *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram*

Zādspram’s ninth-century selections preserve a parallel creation account. Gayomart is four average reeds in height and equally broad, a primordial giant rather than an ordinary human. Zādspram sometimes differs from the *Bundahišn* in enumeration and detail—for example, eight metals rather than seven may be associated with his body. **Tier: later canonical variant.**

### *Dēnkard*

The ninth-century compendium presents Gayomart as:

- first hearer or recipient of divine teaching;
- first messenger of Ohrmazd to embodied humanity;
- origin point in human sacred history;
- one of the religion’s pre-eminent heroes alongside Zarathustra and Saošyant;
- subject of the lost *Čihrdād Nask*.

It also preserves genealogical sequences running from Gayomart through Mašīa/Mašīānag, Sīāmak, Frawāk, and Hōšang. **Tier: priestly systematization.**

### *Mēnōg ī Xrad*

This Middle Persian wisdom dialogue, substantially late antique or early Islamic in form, lists the benefits bestowed by early heroes. Gayomart heads the sequence of primordial and royal figures, and the work affirms humanity’s descent from him. It is not an independent full creation narrative. **Tier: later ethical-historical systematization.**

### *Aogəmadaēcā*

This difficult Pāzand/Pahlavi text, of uncertain date, gives a compressed Gayomart legend at §§85–87. Its witnesses disagree over whether he is “mountain king,” “clay king,” or Sanskrit “great king.” The disagreement is important evidence for the medieval transformation of the figure. **Tier: late textual variant.**

### *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*

The *Book of Ardā Wīrāz* is relevant to the broader Zoroastrian eschatological setting, but it supplies no major independent Gayomart cycle. Including it among sources for his detailed biography would overstate its evidence. **Tier: contextual source; no substantial direct attestation established.**

### Achaemenid inscriptions and Classical authors

No occurrence of Gayomart is securely identified in the inscriptions of Darius or Xerxes at Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam, or Persepolis. These inscriptions name Ahuramazda and articulate royal ideology but do not supply a primordial-man genealogy.

Herodotus describes Persian sacrifice and religion but does not name Gayomart. Plutarch’s *On Isis and Osiris* preserves a Greek account of Oromazes and Areimanius but not Gayomart’s complete myth. These sources demonstrate that Greek testimony cannot fill the gap between the Avesta and Pahlavi books. **Tier: explicit negative finding.**

### Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*

Composed approximately 977–1010 CE from earlier Iranian epic-historical traditions, including the lost Middle Persian *Xwadāy-nāmag*, the *Šāhnāma* makes Kayumars the first ruler of the world. He lives in the mountains, wears leopard skin, establishes kingship, draws humans and animals around him, and reigns thirty years. His son Sīāmak is killed by the son of Ahriman; after mourning and divine warning, Kayumars sends his grandson Hōšang to defeat the demons.

The epic therefore retains the thirty-year figure and opposition to Ahriman but relocates death: Kayumars is not killed by Ahriman; Sīāmak is. **Tier: medieval national-epic transformation.**

### Manichaean corpus

Mani’s third-century religion adapted Iranian, Christian, Mesopotamian, and other materials. In Iranian-language Manichaean fragments, *Gēhmurd* is Adam and *Murdiyānag* is Eve. Fragments of the Middle Persian *Šābuhragān*, Mani’s work for Šābuhr I, include a chapter about their creation.

The Manichaean story is not the Zoroastrian rhubarb myth. Demonic beings concentrate swallowed light, reproduce, and generate the material first couple; Gēhmurd/Adam contains divine light but is imprisoned in corrupt matter. Mani has taken Gayomart’s name and first-human function while assigning Mašyānag’s transformed name to Eve and omitting Mašīa. **Tier: third-century syncretistic reconstruction.**

### Islamic historiography

From the ninth century onward, Yaʿqūbī, Ṭabarī, Balʿamī, Masʿūdī, Ḥamza Eṣfahānī, Bīrūnī, Ṯaʿālebī, Šahrastānī, and others preserve incompatible solutions to Gayomart’s place in universal history:

- Gayomart is Adam;
- Gayomart is a descendant or son of Adam;
- Gayomart is Gomer in the post-Noah genealogy;
- Gayomart is the first Persian king, but not the first human;
- Gayomart is both primordial human and king.

Ṭabarī and Balʿamī describe him as pious, peaceful, and civilizing; Balʿamī omits Frawāk from one genealogy preserved by Ṭabarī. Šahrastānī places his reign at Eṣṭaḵr and reports a *Kayūmarṯīya* doctrine. These are not mere corruptions: they document how Iranian sacred history was translated into an Abrahamic universal chronology. **Tier: medieval Islamic syncretism and historiography.**

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

Gayomart governs no specialized natural domain comparable to Mithra’s covenants or Anāhitā’s waters. His functions are structural:

- prototype and generative source of embodied humanity;
- opening member of human sacred history;
- first recipient of Mazda’s teaching;
- first combatant among humans against the Evil Spirit;
- source of metals and human seed in Pahlavi cosmology;
- first king and initiator of royal order in epic;
- first material human/Adam in Manichaean myth.

### Form

The *Bundahišn* calls him bright as the sun, four average reeds high and equally broad, with eyes, ears, tongue, and human sexual characteristics. “Round” or equally broad and tall should not automatically be converted into modern images of a spherical person; the text emphasizes primordial completeness and enormous, symmetrical dimensions.

He stands on the left bank of the Good Dāitī River in Ērān-wēz; the sole-created Ox stands on the right. Both are made from earth, while their reproductive seed is fashioned from the light or verdure of the sky. Sleep is created for his assistance as a radiant fifteen-year-old youth.

### Body, seed, and metals

At death, Gayomart emits seed. Two portions are purified by sunlight and entrusted to Nēryōsang; another is received by Spandārmad, the Earth. After forty years a rhubarb-like plant grows; after further development its joined stems become Mašīa and Mašīānag. Seven kinds of metal arise from his bodily members in the *Bundahišn*; Zādspram gives eight.

The precise mapping varies in manuscripts and editions. The general transformation is secure within the Pahlavi tradition; any claim for a particular metal should be checked against the chosen recension. **Tier: later canonical elaboration with textual variants.**

### Celestial and temporal correspondences

The *Bundahišn* gives Gayomart a horoscope. His death occurs under adverse planetary conditions: Saturn is in exaltation and Jupiter in fall or debility. This astrological detail belongs to late antique learned cosmology, not to the surviving Avestan notices. Zurwān/Time fixes his post-Assault survival at thirty years. **Tier: Sasanian or post-Sasanian learned systematization.**

### Iconography

No securely identified Avestan, Achaemenid, Parthian, or Sasanian image of Gayomart survives. In particular:

- no royal relief can safely be relabeled Gayomart merely because it depicts an Iranian king;
- Roman Mithraic tauroctonies are not depictions of Gayomart’s death;
- the image sometimes circulated online of Ahriman as a serpent strangling a bull while Gayomart emerges is a modern composite, not standard ancient Gayomart iconography.

The great visual tradition belongs to illustrated *Šāhnāmas*. Its masterpiece is Sultan Muhammad’s **Court of Gayumars**, made for the *Šāhnāma* of Shah Tahmasp, conventionally dated about 1522–25. Gayumars sits enthroned in a luminous mountain landscape, surrounded by courtiers in animal skins; humans, animals, rocks, plants, and fantastical vegetation create an image of the first harmonious kingship. This illustrates Ferdowsi’s king, not the naked cosmic giant of the *Bundahišn*. **Tier: early-modern epic iconography.**

### Sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers

- **Plant:** rhubarb/*rēwās*-like vegetation is the form from which the first couple grows, not Gayomart’s personal cult plant.
- **Animal:** the sole-created Ox is his cosmological counterpart, not securely his sacred animal.
- **Color/radiance:** white or solar brilliance characterizes his primordial body.
- **Numbers:** four reeds; thirty years after the Assault; forty years of seed in earth; seven or eight metals.
- **No securely attested personal day, month, temple color, weapon, or sacrificial animal is known.**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Origin and descendants

The *Bundahišn* says Ohrmazd makes Gayomart from earth through Spandārmad. Because Spandārmad is both Ohrmazd’s creation/daughter and Earth, priestly texts could interpret the sequence as a cosmic next-of-kin union: Ohrmazd and Spandārmad generate Gayomart, whose seed enters Spandārmad and produces humanity. This theological construction should not be simplified into an ordinary human genealogy.

The first couple is Mašīa and Mašīānag. They are at once his descendants and the transformed growth of his seed. Later genealogies continue through Sīāmak, Frawāk, and Hōšang, although epic and Islamic sources rearrange the generations.

Ferdowsi instead makes Sīāmak Kayumars’s immediate son and Hōšang Sīāmak’s son. The *Šāhnāma* does not recount Mašīa and Mašīānag’s vegetal birth in this episode.

No consort of Gayomart is named in the orthodox Pahlavi creation myth. Calling Spandārmad his “wife” is an over-literal harmonization; she is Earth, receiver of his seed, and in learned theology his creator/mother.

### Every documented interaction with a named divine or demonic being

- **Ahura Mazda/Ohrmazd — creates Gayomart and gives or reveals his teaching to him; promises him immortality at the Renovation.** *Yasht* 13.87; *Bundahišn* 1a; *Dēnkard* 3, 8–9. **Tier: early attestation, later canonical expansion.**

- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — attacks creation and seeks Gayomart’s destruction; Gayomart eventually dies from the Assault’s consequences.** *Bundahišn* 4 and 6F; Zādspram. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.** Ferdowsi transfers the immediate killing to Ahriman’s demon son. **Tier: medieval epic variant.**

- **Jeh/Jahī, the “Whore” demoness — revives the prostrate Ahriman and promises to afflict Gayomart and the good creation.** *Bundahišn* 4. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.** She does not meet Gayomart in a narrated face-to-face scene.

- **Astwihād — sent as death’s demon against Gayomart, accompanied by Want, Sloth, Lust, diseases, and demonic forces.** *Bundahišn* 4.19. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**

- **Sleep — created by Ohrmazd in the form of a radiant fifteen-year-old youth and brought over Gayomart, temporarily frustrating the assault.** *Bundahišn* 1a.13, 4. **Tier: later canonical personification.**

- **Zurwān/Time — fixes Gayomart’s lifespan at thirty years after the Assault.** *Bundahišn* 4.25. **Tier: learned cosmological systematization; not evidence that Gayomart belonged to a separately organized “Zurvanite church.”**

- **Spandārmad/Spenta Ārmaiti — as Earth participates in his creation and receives one part of his seed after death.** *Bundahišn* 1a.13, 6F. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**

- **Nēryōsang — receives and safeguards two purified portions of Gayomart’s seed.** *Bundahišn* 6F. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**

- **The Sun — its rays purify Gayomart’s seed; Gayomart’s ideal form is placed in the solar station.** *Bundahišn* 6F. The sun is a sacred creation and can be personified, though the passage is not necessarily a dialogue with a named solar god. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**

- **The sole-created Ox, Gāw ī ēwdād — created opposite Gayomart as the primordial animal counterpart; both suffer in the Assault and become generative through death.** Avestan allusions; *Bundahišn* 1a, 4–6. **Tier: early association, later narrative elaboration.** No conversation or combat between them is attested.

- **Warahrān/Bahrām — the fire of Warahrān safeguards Gayomart’s cause because human seed is associated with fire.** *Dēnkard*, Madan p. 538. **Tier: priestly systematization.**

- **Saošyant — Gayomart is first in human history and Saošyant last; Gayomart is raised first or among the first at the Resurrection and participates in Renovation.** *Yasna* 26.10; *Bundahišn* 34.6. **Tier: early structural pairing, later eschatological elaboration.**

- **Srōš/Soroush — in Ferdowsi, brings the divine command or warning that enables Kayumars’s house to act against the demons after Sīāmak’s death.** *Šāhnāma*, opening reign. **Tier: medieval epic elaboration.**

- **Ahriman’s unnamed or variably designated demon son — kills Sīāmak; Hōšang later destroys him.** *Šāhnāma*, reign of Kayumars. Because the demon is not consistently given an independent proper name, inventing one would violate the evidence. **Tier: medieval epic variant.**

- **Āz — in Manichaean cosmology, a demonic power participates in producing Gēhmurd and Murdiyānag from matter containing stolen light.** Iranian Manichaean creation texts and the *Šābuhragān* fragments. **Tier: third-century syncretistic reconstruction.**

- **Jesus the Splendor — descends to awaken or instruct Adam/Gēhmurd concerning his divine soul and material imprisonment.** Manichaean cosmological tradition. **Tier: Manichaean syncretistic identification.**

No documented encounter with Mithra, Anāhitā, Yima/Jamšīd, or Zarathustra has been established merely from their membership in Iranian tradition. Comparisons are not interactions.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The first righteous mortal

The oldest recoverable layer is not a creation epic but a liturgical memory. Gayomart is the first righteous human to hear Ahura Mazda and the ancestor or generative origin of the Iranian peoples. His *fravaši* is worshipped in a continuum extending to the final savior. **Tier: securely attested early material.**

### Creation, assault, and death

In the Pahlavi synthesis, Ohrmazd creates six or seven principal material creations against Ahriman; Gayomart is the human creation. He stands in Ērān-wēz opposite the primordial Ox. His *fravaši* voluntarily accepts combat after being shown the final victory and is promised perfection and immortality at the Renovation.

Ahriman lies stunned for 3,000 years until Jeh arouses him. During the Assault he releases demons, pollution, and disease. Astwihād assails Gayomart, but Gayomart cannot die before the thirty years fixed by Time. Sleep protects him temporarily. Eventually adverse cosmic conditions coincide with his death.

The story is explicitly dualist but not evenly dualist: Gayomart’s death is allowed within a predetermined history in which evil’s violence paradoxically releases further good creation. **Tier: later canonical elaboration.**

### Metals and humanity from the dead body

After Gayomart dies, metals develop from his members. His seed is purified by sunlight and conserved by Nēryōsang and Earth. After forty years, a single rhubarb-like growth appears. Its paired stems are so intertwined that sex cannot initially be distinguished. They separate into Mašīa and Mašīānag, receive souls, and become ancestors of humanity.

The source does not say that Gayomart himself grew as rhubarb. That error conflates him with his descendants. Nor does it securely say Ahriman physically “murdered” him in a single combat; the Pahlavi account describes a cosmic assault, disease, fate, and delayed death. **Tier: later canonical elaboration; popular simplification rejected.**

### First prophet and religious teacher

The *Dēnkard* reasons from *Yasht* 13.87 that Gayomart was the first recipient and messenger of Mazda’s revelation. This elevates him from first hearer to proto-prophet. It is meaningful within medieval Zoroastrian theology but not explicit in the oldest text. **Tier: learned priestly systematization.**

### First king in the *Šāhnāma*

Ferdowsi’s Kayumars establishes royal rule from a mountain dwelling. He and his people wear leopard skins; animals gather peacefully around his court. His glorious son Sīāmak is envied by Ahriman, whose demon offspring kills him in combat. Kayumars mourns for a year. Soroush communicates the command for vengeance, and Hōšang leads humans and animals against the demons, killing Sīāmak’s murderer. Kayumars then yields the royal succession to Hōšang.

This version reverses the Zoroastrian death pattern: Kayumars survives the demonic crisis, while the son dies. Kingship, mourning, dynastic succession, and legitimate revenge replace the cosmogony of seed and vegetation. **Tier: medieval national epic.**

### Manichaean Gēhmurd

Manichaean demons consume the light trapped in the bodies of their offspring and reproduce in an attempt to preserve it. The resulting Adam/Gēhmurd and Eve/Murdiyānag are material prisons modeled on divine forms. Gēhmurd therefore contains both luminous substance and the corruption of demonic manufacture. A saving revealer awakens him to knowledge.

This is neither a continuation of the *Bundahišn* nor evidence that Zoroastrians believed Gayomart was demon-created. It is Mani’s deliberate third-century recombination of Iranian nomenclature with Adamic and gnostic cosmology. **Tier: syncretistic identification.**

### Islamic Adamization and royal history

Muslim chroniclers faced incompatible inherited chronologies. Identifying Kayumars with Adam preserved his primacy; making him Adam’s descendant preserved Qurʾanic-Biblical genealogy; identifying him with Gomer moved Persian origins after Noah. Persian epic meanwhile retained him as first king without requiring theological first-human status. No single “Islamic version” became wholly canonical. **Tier: medieval historiographical adaptation.**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There is no evidence for a dedicated temple, priesthood, oracle, festival, sacrificial economy, or civic cult of Gayomart. No securely identified altar or dedicatory inscription addresses him alone. Calling him the center of an ancient “Gayomart cult” would go beyond the evidence.

What is documented is liturgical commemoration:

- his *fravaši* is invoked in *Yasna* 26;
- *Frawardīn Yašt* 13.87 worships his *fravaši*;
- that Yašt remains part of Zoroastrian ritual and is incorporated into Āfrīnagān observances;
- recitation places him within the collective communion of righteous dead and future renovators.

Thus Gayomart’s cultic existence is collective and textual rather than temple-centered. He is venerated as the first righteous human, not ordinarily petitioned as a specialist deity.

No evidence connects him with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or a historical violent cult. Comparative theories that reconstruct him as an Indo-European sacrificial victim concern mythic structure, not an attested rite in which humans impersonated and killed Gayomart.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Young Avestan period

The first recoverable figure is concise: righteous Gaiia Marətan, first hearer of Mazda and beginning of human sacred history. The creation-death story must have existed in some form because of his association with the primordial Ox and later testimony about lost Avestan books, but its exact early content cannot be reconstructed securely.

### Achaemenid through Parthian periods

There is no direct named attestation in imperial inscriptions or surviving Classical descriptions. This silence does not prove the tradition was absent; it prevents precise claims about royal sponsorship, temples, or images.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

Zoroastrian scholasticism organized older materials into a universal chronology and probably developed or codified much of the Zand underlying the *Bundahišn*. Gayomart became the sixth material creation, first combatant, first recipient of revelation, and source of humanity. Astrology, the 12,000-year cosmic scheme, and detailed correspondences reflect learned late-antique synthesis.

Mani, active in the third century, simultaneously appropriated the name as Gēhmurd/Adam. The Zoroastrian and Manichaean figures must be distinguished even when they share Iranian vocabulary.

### Early Islamic centuries

After the Arab conquest and the loss of Zoroastrian political supremacy, Middle Persian priests compiled the *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, Zādspram selections, and related works. This was preservation but also systematization under new conditions.

Muslim authors inserted Kayumars into universal history. The transformation from first man to first king was neither a simple Islamic erasure nor a single event: first-man, first-prophet, and royal traditions coexisted.

### Ferdowsi and Persian literary canon

By about 1010, Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma* gave the first king his most influential Persian literary form. Kayumars became a dynastic founder, mountain monarch, bereaved father, and ancestor of Iranian kingship. This narrative ultimately shaped visual culture more strongly than the Pahlavi giant.

### Mongol, Timurid, Safavid, and Mughal manuscript cultures

Illustrated *Šāhnāmas* made the court of Gayumars a standard subject. Painters visualized primordial kingship using the clothing, landscapes, court conventions, and aesthetic ideals of their own periods. Sultan Muhammad’s Safavid page is the most celebrated culmination, not evidence for ancient iconography.

### Modern period

Zoroastrian communities continue to transmit the Avestan and Pahlavi texts and to recite the *Frawardīn Yašt*. Gayomart survives as sacred ancestor rather than the center of an independent cult.

Modern Iranian literature, education, nationalism, and popular mythology generally emphasize Kayumars as the first Iranian king. The figure can symbolize antiquity, indigenous identity, or royal origins, but evidence for an organized modern Gayomart-worship movement is lacking.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and comparative religion

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars often reconstructed a “Primordial Man” complex spanning India, Iran, Gnosticism, Manichaeism, Mithraism, and Germanic myth. Comparisons with Vedic Mārtāṇḍa, Puruṣa, Norse Ymir, or Adam can be illuminating, but genetic relationships differ sharply in evidential strength.

Hoffmann argued for a significant relationship between Gayomart and Vedic Mārtāṇḍa; Cereti calls Gayomart “most probably” an Iranian counterpart. Christensen treated the Avestan expression as an abstraction personified by Iranian tradition. These proposals are scholarly reconstructions, not explicit ancient identifications.

### Mithraic claims

Older Mithraic scholarship sometimes identified Cautes and Cautopates, or elements of the tauroctony, with Gayomart, Yima, and Iranian cosmogony. Current scholarship stresses that Roman Mithraic cosmology is reconstructed mainly from images; the extent of Iranian continuity remains disputed, and the balance has shifted toward Roman reinvention.

No Iranian text says Mithra kills Gayomart. No Roman inscription labels a torchbearer “Gayomart.” Presenting the tauroctony as a picture of Gayomart’s sacrifice is therefore speculative and generally disfavored. **Tier: disputed modern scholarly reconstruction.**

### Occultism and neopaganism

The research pass found no major Renaissance grimoire, Golden Dawn ritual, Theosophical canon, or established chaos-magic current centered on Gayomart. Modern occult websites sometimes recast him as an “angel,” solar god, hermaphroditic deity, or initiatory archetype. Such classifications usually combine the *Bundahišn*, generic “cosmic man” symbolism, and modern esotericism without historical continuity. **Tier: modern invention or eclectic reinterpretation.**

Contemporary Zoroastrian revivalists and Iranian reconstructionists may honor him as the first human or king. Reliable demographic evidence for a distinct Gayomart cult is unavailable. The medieval *Kayūmarṯīya* reported by Šahrastānī cannot be equated automatically with modern game-based “Gayomarthianism.”

### Psychology and archetypal readings

Jungian and comparative treatments may classify Gayomart as a primordial Anthropos, sacrificed cosmic man, or Self-symbol. These are twentieth-century interpretive frameworks, not native Avestan categories.

### Popular culture

Kayumars appears in translations and adaptations of the *Šāhnāma*, museum exhibitions, fantasy compendia, games, and online myth encyclopedias. Games that turn “Gayomarthianism” into a selectable, coherent medieval denomination necessarily systematize extremely thin heresiographical evidence. **Tier: modern game design, not reconstructed institutional history.**

### Political appropriation

Kayumars can function in Iranian nationalist narratives as the first monarch and evidence of an ancient continuous Iranian civilization. This depends primarily on Ferdowsi’s literary-national history, not on archaeological proof of an actual prehistoric king. No well-documented extremist organization centered specifically on Gayomart emerged in the source search.

## 9. Historiography and Contested Questions

### Is the name an inherited person or a personified expression?

The conventional philology derives the name from Avestan “mortal life/living mortal.” Christensen argued that an expression became personified. Other comparativists link Gayomart with an inherited Indo-Iranian primordial figure, especially Mārtāṇḍa. These positions are not mutually exclusive: inherited mythic structures may have been expressed through an Iranian phrase that later became a proper name. **Status: unresolved reconstruction.**

### Gayomart and Mārtāṇḍa

Hoffmann’s comparison notes that both stand near the beginning of humanity and involve mortality and problematic primordial generation. The names are not straightforward cognates, and the narratives are not identical. “Iranian counterpart” is defensible as a comparative hypothesis; “the same god” is not. **Status: influential but debated scholarly comparison.**

### Was Gayomart originally androgynous?

The first couple’s undifferentiated vegetal growth and the concentration of human generative potential in Gayomart encouraged interpretations of him as bisexual or androgynous. Some scholars extend the explicitly or implicitly dual-sexed nature of primordial beings to both Gayomart and the first Ox. Yet the texts also use masculine grammar and depict sperm. “Androgynous primordial totality” is an interpretation, not an uncontested explicit statement in the earliest Avesta. **Status: scholarly reconstruction based on later cosmology.**

### Did Ahriman kill him?

Popular summaries say yes. More precisely, Ahriman initiates the Assault, Astwihād and diseases attack, and Gayomart dies when his allotted thirty years expire under adverse planetary conditions. The causal responsibility is Ahriman’s; the text is not a simple murder scene. Ferdowsi instead has Ahriman’s offspring kill Sīāmak. **Status: source-dependent variants.**

### Did a rhubarb plant become Gayomart?

No. The plant grows from Gayomart’s seed and becomes Mašīa and Mašīānag. Assertions that Gayomart himself emerged from or turned into rhubarb confuse generations. **Status: demonstrable modern/popular error.**

### Mountain king or clay king?

Pāzand *garšāh* and secondary Pahlavi *gilšāh* compete. The mountain reading fits Iranian epic geography; the clay reading fits Adamic creation motifs. Cereti considers *garšāh* probably earlier, but definitive proof is lacking. **Status: unresolved textual and etymological dispute.**

### First man or first king?

Both are historical stages of the tradition:

- Avesta: first righteous human/ancestor;
- Pahlavi cosmology: primordial man;
- Pahlavi learned history: emerging royal traits;
- Islamic historiography: first man and/or king;
- Ferdowsi: unequivocally first king.

Treating one answer as timelessly canonical erases the figure’s development.

### Gayomart and Adam

The equation is already central in third-century Iranian Manichaeism and becomes common, though not universal, in Islamic historiography. It is a datable syncretistic identification, not proof that the Avestan Gayomart originated from Genesis.

### Gayomart and Roman Mithraism

The proposed equation of Mithraic torchbearers or tauroctony elements with Gayomart lacks labels and direct textual confirmation. Roman Mithraism should not be used to manufacture Gayomart iconography. **Status: disfavored or highly contested comparativism.**

### Sacrifice

Gayomart’s death generates metals and humanity, allowing comparison with cosmic-body myths such as Puruṣa or Ymir. No source describes a historical human sacrifice performed for Gayomart, and no ritual reenactment is documented. Calling him a “sacrificed god” imports a comparative category into a story where demonic assault, not worshippers’ sacrifice, causes death.

### State of the evidence

The evidentiary chain is unusually uneven:

1. ancient oral composition represented by much later Avestan manuscripts;
2. lost Sasanian Avestan and Middle Persian exegesis summarized in ninth-century books;
3. post-conquest Pahlavi compilations surviving in late manuscripts;
4. Islamic chronicles negotiating Iranian and Biblical chronologies;
5. Ferdowsi’s literary reshaping;
6. illustrated manuscripts that visualize the epic rather than the ancient cult.

The *Bundahišn*’s late manuscripts do not make its entire content late invention, but neither do claims of ancient transmission date every detail to the Avestan period.

## 10. Chronology

- **Late second to first millennium BCE, imprecisely:** Young Avestan tradition names righteous Gaiia Marətan as first hearer of Ahura Mazda and ancestor/source of humanity (*Yasht* 13.87); *Yasna* 26.10 frames history from Gayomart to Saošyant. **Evidence: orally composed Avestan texts in medieval manuscripts.**

- **Sixth–fourth centuries BCE:** Achaemenid inscriptions invoke Ahuramazda but do not securely attest Gayomart. **Evidence: negative inscriptional finding.**

- **Fifth century BCE:** Herodotus describes Persian religion without Gayomart. **Evidence: negative literary finding.**

- **First–second centuries CE:** Plutarch transmits an Oromazes–Areimanius cosmogony without the developed Gayomart narrative. **Evidence: negative/indirect Classical testimony.**

- **Third century CE:** Mani adopts Gēhmurd as Adam in Manichaean cosmology; the *Šābuhragān* tradition contains a chapter on Gēhmurd and Murdiyānag. **Evidence: Iranian Manichaean fragments.**

- **Third–seventh centuries:** Sasanian priestly exegesis systematizes creation, cosmic chronology, and lost Avestan nasks. Exact stages in Gayomart’s expanded myth cannot be dated individually. **Evidence: later Pahlavi compilations drawing on the Zand.**

- **651 CE onward:** Zoroastrianism loses imperial status; priestly textual preservation continues under Islamic rule.

- **Ninth century:** Principal redactional period of the *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, and Zādspram selections. Gayomart appears as primordial giant, sixth creation, first recipient of revelation, ancestor of humanity, source of metals, and eschatological participant.

- **Ninth–eleventh centuries:** Yaʿqūbī, Ṭabarī, Balʿamī, Masʿūdī, Ḥamza Eṣfahānī, Bīrūnī, Ṯaʿālebī, and others harmonize or contrast Kayumars with Adamic and post-Noah genealogies.

- **Approximately 977–1010:** Ferdowsi composes the *Šāhnāma*; Kayumars becomes the first world-king, father of Sīāmak and grandfather of Hōšang.

- **Twelfth century or later:** Sanskrit *Aogəmadaēcā* calls him *mahārāja*, one witness to the story’s Indian Zoroastrian transmission.

- **Fourteenth–seventeenth centuries:** Illustrated *Šāhnāma* production establishes Kayumars’s enduring visual identity as mountain king.

- **About 1522–25:** Sultan Muhammad paints *The Court of Gayumars* for Shah Tahmasp’s *Šāhnāma*.

- **Nineteenth century:** European editions and translations of the Avesta and Pahlavi books make Gayomart central to comparative studies of the “Primordial Man.” The fuller Iranian *Bundahišn* manuscript becomes known in India around 1870.

- **1917:** Arthur Christensen publishes his major study of the earliest Iranian kings and develops the first-man/first-king transformation historically.

- **1920s–1970s:** Comparative studies connect Gayomart with Gnosticism, Manichaeism, Mārtāṇḍa, cosmic-man myths, and Mithraism; several of the broader equations later draw criticism.

- **2000:** Mansour Shaki’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article synthesizes Pahlavi, epic, and Islamic evidence.

- **2015:** Carlo Cereti’s revised *Iranica* treatment emphasizes linguistic history, textual development, the first-man/first-king transition, and competing medieval titles.

- **Present:** Gayomart remains part of living Zoroastrian liturgical memory and Iranian literary heritage. No independent ancient or modern institutional cult centered exclusively on him is securely documented.
