# Nasu: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the Younger Avestan tradition, **Nasu** is both *nasu-*—dead matter/carrion and the pollution proceeding from it—and, when personalized as **Druj Nasu**, a hostile female *druj*, “creature of the Lie” or demoness. Middle Persian sources classify **Nas/Nasuš/Nasrušt** as a *dēw*, demon. The grammatical and conceptual boundary between substance, pollution, and personification is not always sharp.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest identifiable textual witness is the **Young Avestan Vīdēvdād**, especially chapters 3, 5–10. Its component materials cannot be dated precisely. They are later than the Old Avestan Gāthās and were collected into a heterogeneous redaction probably during the late first millennium BCE or early first millennium CE; one influential reconstruction places the redaction in the Arsacid or possibly early Sasanian period. Recent manuscript research cautions that defective Young Avestan need not prove such a late composition, because errors also arose during oral and manuscript transmission. The surviving witnesses are medieval: the principal Pahlavi Vīdēvdād manuscripts L4 and K1 were copied in India in 1323 and 1324 from an Iranian line traceable by colophons to about 1205. No Achaemenid inscription securely names Nasu.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Nasu is the Zoroastrian personification of the contaminating power of dead matter. In the Vīdēvdād, the demoness arrives when a human or dog dies, takes possession of the corpse, and spreads “corruption, infection, and pollution” to persons, clothing, food, water, fuel, earth, and ritual objects. She can also invade a person contaminated through improper corpse handling. The mythic figure and ritual category are therefore inseparable: Nasu is at once carrion, its impurity, and the demon who propagates that impurity.

This is not a goddess with a cult, genealogy, temple, or beneficent portfolio. She belongs to the hostile order opposed to *aša*, truth and right order. Her “history” consists chiefly of regulations designed to contain, expel, or neutralize her.

### Names and forms

| Form | Language/period | Meaning and use | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|---|
| *nasu-*, nominative forms conventionally cited as *nasā̊/nasao-* and *nasuš* | Avestan | Corpse, carrion, dead matter; sometimes the personified pollution | **Securely attested Younger Avestan** |
| *druj nasuš* / Druj Nasu | Avestan | “Druj of dead matter,” the personalized corpse demon | **Securely attested Younger Avestan** |
| *nasā* | Middle Persian and New Persian | Corpse or dead matter | **Later canonical usage** |
| *Nas*, *Nasuš* | Middle Persian | The demon of pollution | **Later canonical systematization** |
| *Nasrušt*, *nasruštīh* | Middle Persian | Corpse-pollution, contamination; sometimes the demon’s name | **Later canonical systematization** |
| “Corpse Demon,” “Carrion Demon,” “Corpse Demoness” | Modern translations | Interpretive English equivalents | **Modern scholarly terminology** |
| “Nasa,” “Naush” | Modern popular spellings | Simplified or corrupt transliterations | **Modern variants**, not separate ancient beings |

Avestan *druj-* denotes deceit, falsehood, or a hostile being belonging to the Lie. Because *druj* is grammatically feminine and Vīdēvdād 7 treats the attacking figure with feminine reference, Nasu is commonly called a demoness. The noun *nasu-* itself, however, occurs with more than one grammatical gender. It is therefore too simple to say that every occurrence of *nasu-* denotes one consistently female individual.

The likely etymological starting point is Iranian *nasu-* “corpse, carrion/dead matter,” reflected in Middle and New Persian *nasā*. The demon’s name is thus transparent personification rather than an independent proper name of securely recoverable Indo-Iranian antiquity. Proposals deriving Nasu from unrelated mythic names—especially the Vedic Nāsatyas—lack linguistic and textual support.

No securely attested cult epithets comparable to those of a *yazata* exist. **Druj Nasu** is a description and classification, not evidence for a separate deity. *Bāj-e nasrūšt*, “utterance against corpse-pollution,” names a prophylactic prayer, not an epithet of Nasu.

### Identifications and non-identifications

Nasu is not securely identified in ancient sources with a Greek, Roman, Mesopotamian, Manichaean, or Mithraic being. Herodotus and later Greek authors describe Iranian funerary customs, but do not name or equate her with a Greek demon. No evidence establishes an interpretatio graeca or interpretatio romana.

Modern internet lists sometimes conflate Nasu with plague demons, flies associated with Beelzebub, Arabic *nasnās*, or generic ghouls. These are **modern comparisons or inventions**, not documented historical syncretisms.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Old Avesta and Achaemenid evidence

**Negative finding:** Nasu is not a named being in the Gāthās, the Old Avestan core of the Yasna. The Gāthās supply prayers later redeployed against her, but that ritual redeployment does not show that Zarathustra’s hymns originally concerned a corpse demon.

The Achaemenid inscriptions—Behistun and the royal tomb inscriptions at Naqš-e Rostam included—do not name Nasu or describe *sagdīd*, *barašnom*, or a tower of silence. Achaemenid royal tombs keep bodies away from direct contact with soil, which some historians interpret as broadly compatible with Iranian purity concerns. That is an inference; it does not prove that the kings followed the detailed Vīdēvdād code or believed in a named Druj Nasu.

### Vīdēvdād

The title *Vīdēvdād*, Avestan *vi-daēva-dāta-*, is conventionally interpreted as “law given against” or “law repudiating the demons.” The extant work is a composite Young Avestan dialogue in which Zarathustra asks questions and Ahura Mazda gives rules.

The passages most important for Nasu are:

- **Vd. 3.14:** Improper solitary corpse-carrying allows Nasu to invade the bearer. The text’s placement is composite, and scholars identify the verse as assembled from different material.
- **Vd. 5:** Specifies the distance through which pollution spreads among persons lying together and distinguishes classes of human and canine corpses.
- **Vd. 6.26–41:** Measures the area of still or flowing water contaminated by a corpse and regulates its recovery. Vd. 6.42–43 unusually exempts already consecrated Haoma from ordinary contamination.
- **Vd. 7.1–5:** Gives Nasu’s best-known manifestation. At ordinary death she arrives as soon as the soul leaves; after specified violent or premature deaths she arrives at the next watch. She comes from the north as a foul fly. Vd. 7.3, present in the *Vīdēvdād Sāde* tradition, says she remains until a dog sees or eats the corpse or carrion birds approach it.
- **Vd. 7.6–35:** Regulates contaminated clothing, food, fuel, and other materials. Its transmitted wording at 7.29–30 appears displaced or reversed; Darmesteter corrected it by comparison with Vd. 8.
- **Vd. 7.45–59:** Discusses polluted earth and *dakhmas*.
- **Vd. 8.14–22:** Orders a dog to be led over the route taken by the corpse. A yellow dog with “four eyes”—explained in the Pahlavi commentary as two spots above its eyes—or a white dog with yellow ears is preferred. Nasu then flees northward.
- **Vd. 8.23–25:** Describes the fate of a person who carries a corpse alone: Nasu enters progressively through the body’s openings. Such a person is treated as exceptionally and perhaps irredeemably contaminated in the strict wording of the law.
- **Vd. 8.35–72:** Prescribes bodily purification by repeated applications of cattle urine and water. As washing proceeds, Nasu is driven from body part to body part.
- **Vd. 9:** Describes the full nine-night *barašnom*, the principal purification for a person defiled by the dead. It regulates the purification enclosure, pits, washing sequence, isolation, purifier’s qualifications, and fees.
- **Vd. 9.47–57:** Condemns an incompetent purifier. Later exposition says such a purifier strengthens Nasu and increases sickness and death.
- **Vd. 10.1–17:** Answers how one should fight the demon that “flies from the dead upon the living.” Prescribed repetitions of passages from the five Gāthās drive her from the body.
- **Vd. 19:** Places purity practice within the larger struggle between Zarathustra and the demonic world.

The text is physically preserved through the medieval manuscript tradition. L4, completed at Navsari in 1323, and K1, completed at Cambay in 1324, are the principal early Pahlavi Vīdēvdād witnesses; both derive through earlier exemplars from Iran. The Iranian and Indian *Vīdēvdād Sāde* traditions preserve the Avestan work embedded in a liturgical arrangement.

James Darmesteter’s English and French translations remain valuable for their use of the Pahlavi commentary, but his conjectures must be distinguished from manuscript text. Fritz Wolff’s German translation relied heavily on Bartholomae’s lexicography. Modern work by Alberto Cantera and the Avestan Digital Archive emphasizes manuscript variation and the performative character of the transmitted text.

### Pahlavi and Persian corpus

- **Pahlavi Vīdēvdād**, substantially Sasanian and post-Sasanian exegesis preserved in medieval manuscripts: expands and rationalizes the Avestan purity rules. Named authorities including Āfrāg and Mēdyōmāh disagree about procedural details, such as whether a dog should traverse the corpse-route lengthwise or from side to side. **Later priestly systematization.**
- **Dēnkard 5, ch. 24:** Explains *sagdīd*: the dog’s gaze confines the corrupting demons in the body until corpse-eating animals transform it, protecting fire and earth. **Ninth-century learned systematization drawing on older material.**
- **Dēnkard 8.44:** Summarizes the Vīdēvdād’s lost or received Sasanian organization: the rushing of *druj ī nasuš*, corpse pollution, contaminated clothing, burial, water, and the production of disease through dead matter. **Ninth-century testimony to the Sasanian canon.**
- **Bundahišn 27.29** in the surviving redactions, substantially ninth century with later transmission: “The demon Nas” causes *rēmanīh* and *nasruštīh*, pollution and corpse-contamination, called *nasā*. **Later canonical demonology.**
- **Dādestān ī Dēnīg 16**, by the ninth-century priest Manuščihr: Nasu makes her place in the body after overcoming it; *sagdīd* reduces the corpse from the more dangerous *nasā* condition toward dry dead matter, *hixr*. **Later canonical legal theology.**
- **Saddar Nasr 35–36**, a post-Sasanian Persian Zoroastrian catechetical work: prescribes the *Kəm nā Mazdā* prayer and expands the *barašnom* into a broadly meritorious purification, including performance around religious majority. **Medieval elaboration.**
- **Persian Rivāyats**, principally fifteenth–eighteenth centuries: transmit Iranian priestly answers to Indian Zoroastrian questions; they preserve and sometimes intensify corpse-pollution regulations. One formulation states cautiously that *sagdīd* makes a corpse less polluting rather than literally annihilating Nasu. **Early-modern legal tradition.**
- **Srōš bāj / Bāj-e nasrūšt:** A daily protective recitation honoring Sraoša and guarding against corruption and death; its recorded form belongs to living priestly tradition. **Later liturgical elaboration.**

### Texts in which Nasu is absent

No identifiable Nasu narrative occurs in the Gāthās, major Yašts, Herodotus, Plutarch, Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, the *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag*, or securely identified Manichaean and Mithraic texts. These sources can illuminate Iranian death, demons, or funerary practice, but should not be represented as Nasu attestations.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Nasu’s primary domain is **corpse contamination**, especially from dead humans and dogs. The category extends to other dead matter and, through later legal interpretation, to blood, menstruation, stillbirth, hair, and nail clippings. Not every impurity is Nasu in an equally personalized sense: the sources move between a demon, a contaminating condition, and physical carrion.

Her characteristic actions are:

1. arriving when life departs;
2. occupying the corpse;
3. spreading pollution by touch or proximity;
4. invading an improper corpse-bearer;
5. retreating before a suitable dog, carrion-eating creatures, prescribed washing, and sacred recitation;
6. becoming stronger when rites are performed incompetently.

### The fly form

Vīdēvdād 7.2 and the repeated formula in 8.16–18 depict Nasu as *maxši.kəhrpa*, “fly-shaped,” with crooked or projecting knees, an emphasized rear or tail, and incessant foul sound or discharge. Translation varies because several descriptors are difficult. Darmesteter’s “raging fly, with knees and tail sticking out, droning without end” is an interpretive rendering, not a neutral word-for-word certainty.

The fly is therefore **securely textual**, but no securely identified ancient image of Nasu survives. Modern illustrations of a giant woman-fly hybrid are imaginative reconstructions. There is no ancient canonical color, weapon, number, celestial body, sacred plant, throne, or cult statue.

### Direction and environment

Nasu comes from and retreats toward the **north**, a direction associated in later Zoroastrian cosmography with the demonic realm. This is an attested feature of the Vīdēvdād narrative, although the fully mapped northern hell belongs to broader and later cosmological systematization.

### Powers and limitations

Nasu does not appear omnipotent. Her pollution varies by:

- manner and timing of death;
- species and religious status of the dead;
- distance and degree of contact;
- whether *sagdīd* has occurred;
- moisture or dryness of contaminated material;
- whether a ritual substance has already been consecrated;
- competence of the purifier.

Dryness, distance, exposure, consumption by carrion animals, cattle urine, water applied in regulated order, and sacred speech limit her. These distinctions show that the tradition did not treat impurity as a wholly undifferentiated supernatural force.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy and rank

No source supplies Nasu with parents, siblings, consort, children, mount, or independent household. She belongs collectively to the *druj/dēw* side of Zoroastrian dualism. Later demonological lists place Nas among the many demons produced or deployed by Ahriman, but she is not one of the consistently enumerated great archdemons.

It is safest to distinguish:

- **Younger Avestan layer:** a *druj* that embodies corpse contamination;
- **Middle Persian systematization:** Nas is a named *dēw* in Ahriman’s creation;
- **Modern reconstruction:** “daughter of Angra Mainyu,” “queen of flies,” and similar genealogical formulas have no identified primary source.

### Every documented divine interaction

| Other being | Interaction | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|

This is instruction about Nasu, not a narrated meeting between them. | Vīdēvdād 5–10; composite Young Avestan text, redaction disputed | **Securely attested Avestan** |
| **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman** | Nasu belongs to the hostile demonic creation and advances the death and corruption introduced by the destructive spirit. No surviving Avestan episode stages a personal conversation or joint action between the two. | Implicit in Avestan dualism; explicit classification in Middle Persian demonology, especially Bundahišn 27 | **Later canonical systematization** |
| **Sraoša/Srōš** | The *Srōš bāj*, also called *Bāj-e nasrūšt*, invokes Sraoša in a protective rite against decay, death, and corpse-pollution. No narrative combat scene survives. | Post-Sasanian liturgical tradition, recorded in priestly manuals | **Later liturgical elaboration** |

**Absence of evidence:** No securely attested interaction links Nasu personally with Mithra, Anāhitā, Haoma, Verethragna, the Aməša Spəntas, a Manichaean divinity, or a Mithraic figure. Haoma’s resistance to pollution in Vd. 6 is a rule about a consecrated substance, not a Nasu–Haoma narrative.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Nasu has no independent epic cycle. Her narrative record consists of compact ritual myths embedded in law.

### 5.1 The corpse-fly’s arrival

**Early attested form:** Vīdēvdād 7.1–5.

When a person dies naturally, Nasu flies from the north and attacks as soon as the soul leaves the body. She takes the shape of a repellent fly. If death is violent—through an animal, hostile action, execution, a fall, poison or related causes as differently interpreted by the commentary—her arrival is delayed until the next watch.

The myth explains why a living body is not intrinsically impure while its corpse rapidly becomes dangerous. The delay after violent death may encode an observation about uncertainty of death or decay, but such explanations are modern scholarly inference.

Vd. 7.3 says Nasu remains until the corpse has been seen or consumed by a dog or until carrion birds approach. Because the verse is specifically marked as coming from the *Vīdēvdād Sāde*, its exact textual status deserves notice: it belongs to the transmitted Avestan tradition but is not equally supported across every manuscript branch.

### 5.2 The assault on the solitary corpse-bearer

**Early attested form:** Vd. 3.14 and 8.23–25.

A person who carries a corpse alone permits direct, encompassing contact with dead matter. Nasu enters through successive bodily openings—from nose, eyes, tongue, and jaws down to the sexual organs and anus. The law accordingly requires corpse handlers to work in pairs. Later practice materialized their ritual bond through *peywand*, a cloth or sacred cord joining them.

This is not merely hygienic advice in its textual setting. It is a supernatural invasion myth expressing the idea that improper social and ritual handling converts a dangerous exterior pollution into an interior one.

### 5.3 The dog’s gaze: *sagdīd*

**Early attested form:** Vd. 7.3; 8.14–22.  
**Later explanation:** Dēnkard 5.24; Dādestān ī Dēnīg 16.

A suitable dog is brought to see the exposed face of the dead person. A dog with two markings above its eyes is called “four-eyed”; a white dog with yellow ears is an alternative. For a polluted roadway, the dog is led over it three times, or six or nine times if reluctant. Nasu then flies northward.

The Avestan text states the effect but does not provide a fully developed mechanism. The Dēnkard explains that the dog’s gaze confines the demons within the body and protects living creation until scavengers transform the flesh. The Dādestān says that *sagdīd* reduces the corpse’s polluting state. The Persian Rivāyats likewise generally promise reduced pollution, not necessarily the metaphysical extermination of Nasu.

The dog’s power reflects its elevated place in Zoroastrian law. The “four eyes” are markings, not evidence for a supernatural four-eyed breed. Popular claims that the dog “determines whether the person is really dead” are plausible modern rationalizations but are not the reason supplied by the primary theological texts.

### 5.4 Expulsion through the body

**Early attested form:** Vd. 8.35–72 and 9.

The purifier applies consecrated cattle urine and then water in a prescribed anatomical sequence. Each application drives Nasu to the next location—from the head and brows through the body and eventually to the extremities—until she is expelled. The later commentary compares this descent with the reverse of her invasion of Gayōmard.

The ritual turns contamination into a spatially measurable enemy. Its narrative is procedural: the demon’s route is mapped by the purifier’s actions.

### 5.5 Sacred speech against Nasu

**Early attested form:** Vd. 10.1–17.

Asked how to fight the demon that flies “from the dead upon the living,” Ahura Mazda prescribes repeated recitation of verses now situated in the Gāthās and Yasna. The passages are recited two, three, or four times. Ancient hymns not originally addressed to Nasu are thereby incorporated into a younger ritual program. This is an important distinction between the date of the prayer and the date of its application.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No cult of Nasu

There were no known temples, priests, sacrifices, votives, festivals, theophoric personal names, or devotional images belonging to Nasu. Ritual activity was apotropaic: one did not worship her but prevented or removed her contamination.

No evidence connects her with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, an oracle, or a dedicated magical cult. The requested contested-material search produced no credible primary or academic evidence for any of these.

### The *barašnom*

The *barašnom* described in Vd. 9 is a prolonged purification conducted in a specially prepared dry and clean enclosure. It employs multiple pits or stations, prescribed distances, consecrated cattle urine—Middle Persian *gōmēz*, ritually prepared as *nērang*—water, repeated washing, prayer, and nine nights of separation.

The purifier must be trained, truthful, and ritually qualified. Fees are graded. An unqualified purifier is presented as dangerous because faulty performance strengthens rather than disperses pollution.

The rite’s function expanded. In the Vīdēvdād it primarily addresses defilement by dead matter. In medieval and Parsi usage it became a broader high purification, required for some priestly functions and sometimes undertaken at religious majority. That enlarged application is **later ritual development**, not necessarily the original scope of Vd. 9.

### Corpse handlers

Professional corpse-bearers became known as *nasā-sālār*. They worked in pairs and maintained *peywand*. Washing and handling could also be carried out by Iranian *pākšūs*, “those who wash clean.” Special clothing, metal implements, cattle urine, prayers, and restricted routes limited transfer of pollution.

These institutions are documented principally in later Zoroastrian practice and ethnography; one should not project every modern Parsi detail unchanged into the Avestan period.

### Corpse exposure and towers of silence

The theological rationale for exposure is straightforward: burial contaminates earth, cremation contaminates fire, and immersion contaminates water. Exposure permits sun and carrion-eating dogs or birds to remove flesh while direct contact with the good creations is minimized. Once dry, bones are less polluting and can be collected in an ossuary.

The history of the architecture is less straightforward:

- Vīdēvdād references *dakhmas* and places for laying out bodies, but this does not prove the existence of the familiar high, circular “tower of silence.”
- Herodotus 1.140 reports that Persian bodies were exposed to dogs or birds before burial and associates the practice particularly with Magi. He does not mention Nasu.
- Archaeological ossuaries and exposure practices across Iranian and Central Asian regions demonstrate diverse corpse treatments; they cannot all automatically be labeled Vīdēvdād orthodoxy.
- Monumental, enclosed towers of the familiar type are not securely attested in Iran before the Islamic period. Enclosure probably became useful when a religious minority needed to protect exposure grounds.
- Medieval *dakhmas* combined exposure platforms with central bone repositories.
- Achaemenid royal entombment demonstrates that ancient Iranian practice was not uniform. Stone isolation of the corpse may have reconciled elite burial with pollution rules, but that remains interpretation rather than an explicit royal statement.

Thus it is correct to say that Nasu doctrine supplied a central **rationale** for Zoroastrian exposure. It is incorrect to claim that the Vīdēvdād itself describes every architectural feature of the later tower.

### Sacrifice, punishment, and alleged violence

Nasu receives no sacrifice. Vīdēvdād and medieval legal texts prescribe severe punishments for purity violations and incompetent ritual specialists; some prescriptions include death or exposure to dogs. Whether such penalties were routinely enforced is not established. They are normative religious law, not by themselves records of executions.

No documentary evidence links Nasu doctrine to human sacrifice. No reliable evidence was found for “temple prostitution,” sexual rites, or violence performed as worship of Nasu.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Phase I: pre-Avestan background — unreconstructable

Ancient Iranian communities certainly encountered corpse decay and practiced funerary rites, but no securely recoverable pre-Avestan Nasu figure exists. An Indo-Iranian origin for general concepts of death-pollution is possible; a named Proto-Indo-Iranian demoness Nasu is not demonstrable.

### Phase II: Young Avestan formulation

The Vīdēvdād converts dead matter into a highly articulated legal and supernatural category. Nasu is both measurable pollution and a fly-shaped aggressor. The rules link corpse management to the cosmic defense of earth, water, fire, plants, animals, and the faithful.

The date remains contested. A traditional relative chronology makes the Vīdēvdād later than the Gāthās and most major Yašts. Its heterogeneous redaction may be Arsacid or early Sasanian, but some component rules may be substantially older. Cantera’s work on liturgical Avestan challenges arguments that all anomalous grammar must be very late.

### Phase III: Sasanian canon and exegesis, 224–651 CE

The Vīdēvdād occupied the nineteenth place among the twenty-one Sasanian *nasks*. Its Avestan law was accompanied by a Middle Persian *zand*, initially a close word-for-word gloss and then layers of commentary. Priestly authorities debated procedures while integrating Nasu into systematic demonology.

The surviving text does not preserve an untouched Sasanian book. Its present form passed through post-Sasanian transmission, and chapter 12 was already lost before the construction of the extant composite liturgical traditions.

### Phase IV: early Islamic period, seventh–tenth centuries

With Zoroastrians now a religious minority, the Dēnkard, Bundahišn, and Dādestān ī Dēnīg collected, explained, and defended inherited doctrine. Nasu became more explicitly located in taxonomic lists of Ahriman’s demons. The reason for *sagdīd* was spelled out, and exposure acquired apologetic explanation as protection of the good creations.

The nocturnal Vīdēvdād ceremony in its surviving composite form appears to be a post-Sasanian or Islamic-period development. Ritual use helped the text survive when most other Sasanian *nasks* were lost.

### Phase V: migration and Parsi transmission, tenth–eighteenth centuries

Iranian Zoroastrian communities carried manuscript and ritual traditions to India. Medieval scribes at Navsari and Cambay produced the earliest surviving principal Vīdēvdād manuscripts. Parsi ritual specialists maintained *sagdīd*, corpse-bearers, *barašnom*, and exposure.

The Persian Rivāyats document active exchange: Parsis sent questions to Iranian priests about purity, burial, and pollution. These records show continuity but also historical adaptation, not a frozen survival from Achaemenid times.

### Phase VI: colonial scholarship and reform, eighteenth–twentieth centuries

European translators first encountered Nasu through priestly manuscripts. Anquetil-Duperron, Spiegel, Darmesteter, Geldner, Bartholomae, and Wolff successively established editions, translations, and comparative philology. Colonial-era reformers and modernist Zoroastrians sometimes rationalized Nasu as microbes, infection, or hygienic wisdom. Such readings can illuminate practical effects but are **modern reinterpretations**; the Vīdēvdād describes an intentional demon and ritual pollution, not germ theory.

In Iran, exposure gradually ceased during the twentieth century under legal pressure, urban development, and changing communal practice. Zoroastrians adopted graves isolated from earth by stone or cement. In India, *dakhma* use continued in some communities, but the catastrophic decline of vultures created practical and theological disputes. Some Parsis continued exposure; others adopted burial or cremation despite traditional objections.

### Phase VII: present

Nasu remains part of Zoroastrian scripture, priestly explanation, and funerary theology. Belief and interpretation vary:

- Traditionalists may understand corpse pollution in explicitly demonic and ritual terms.
- Modernists may treat Nasu symbolically or as a premodern account of decomposition and contagion.
- Reform communities may reject or minimize Vīdēvdād purity law.
- Online occult and fantasy sources increasingly detach Nasu from ritual context and recast her as an autonomous monster.

There is no reliable evidence of a continuous or revived devotional cult of Nasu.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and popular religion

Nasu entered European-language religious histories chiefly through nineteenth-century translations of the Vīdēvdād. The vivid corpse-fly became a compact symbol of Zoroastrian purity law in encyclopedias and comparative mythology.

The frequent modern statement that Zoroastrians invented towers because they “knew corpses carried disease” is reductive. Sanitary consequences may be real, but the attested explanation concerns cosmic and ritual pollution of creation.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no substantive Nasu tradition in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, ceremonial magic, or classical modern grimoires. Generic modern demon catalogues sometimes include her after extracting a few sentences from the Vīdēvdād.

No organized Nasu-centered neopagan movement or reconstructionist cult is documented. Individual online invocations cannot establish a historical tradition or measurable community.

### Fiction, games, and commercial use

“Nasu” appears sporadically in fantasy databases, role-playing material, monster lists, art, and horror writing, usually as a fly demon, corpse eater, or female demon of decay. These uses generally preserve the fly and corpse association while discarding purity law, manuscript history, and Zoroastrian dualism. Depictions with humanoid female anatomy, insect wings, magical weapons, sigils, or elaborate genealogies are **modern inventions** unless independently sourced.

### Political appropriation and suppression

No notable nationalist or extremist movement was found to have appropriated Nasu as a central symbol. Anti-Zoroastrian polemic has sometimes sensationalized corpse exposure, while modern apologetic writing has sometimes converted the entire system into ecological or hygienic science. Both are modern framing strategies.

Zoroastrian funerary practices were restricted or abandoned under changing governments and majority pressure, but no source documents a historical campaign directed specifically against belief in Nasu as distinct from broader pressure on Zoroastrian institutions.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Personification versus substance

The central interpretive problem is whether Nasu is best called a demoness or corpse matter. The sources support both:

- common-noun *nasu-* denotes corpse or contamination;
- *Druj Nasu* flies, attacks, occupies, and retreats;
- grammatical variation prevents every occurrence from being read as the same female personality.

A careful translation must decide passage by passage.

### Dating the Vīdēvdād

Earlier scholarship frequently placed the text very late because of corrupt grammar, composite structure, and supposed Hellenistic units of measure. The counterarguments are:

- scribal and oral transmission can produce grammatical deterioration;
- analogous measures existed in Iranian and Indo-Iranian vocabularies;
- liturgical variants sometimes preserve competent Avestan;
- the redaction and its ingredients need not have the same date.

The secure conclusion is relative, not absolute: the Vīdēvdād is later than Old Avestan composition, heterogeneous, and transmitted only in much later manuscripts.

### “Towers of silence” and ancient evidence

A second dispute concerns retrospective projection. Exposure is early and appears in Greek testimony, Avestan regulation, and archaeology. The standardized circular tower with concentric corpse platforms is medieval and later. Consequently:

- “Zoroastrians exposed corpses in antiquity” is defensible;
- “Achaemenid Persians used the surviving towers at Yazd” is not;
- individual archaeological deposits require local analysis.

### *Sagdīd*: supernatural act or practical death test?

Primary texts say the dog repels or confines pollution. Modern writers propose that a dog helped confirm death or detect life. That may be a secondary practical effect, but it is not the stated ancient rationale. The claim becomes misleading when presented as the ritual’s original scientific explanation.

### Nasu as disease germ

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century rationalists equated Nasu with putrefactive bacteria. The demon’s association with decay makes the analogy understandable, but bacteria are not what the Avestan text describes. Nasu contaminates ritually by proximity, religious classification, and contact, sometimes in ways unrelated to biological infection.

### State of the evidence

The greatest evidentiary gap lies between composition and manuscripts. No Vīdēvdād manuscript survives from Achaemenid, Arsacid, or Sasanian times. The earliest reconstructable manuscript ancestry reaches Iran around 1205; principal surviving copies date from the fourteenth century.

The Avestan Digital Archive has transformed study by allowing comparison of manuscript folios and ritual traditions. Geldner’s edition remains foundational, but modern work treats the manuscripts as records of performance rather than mere corrupt containers from which an imaginary single original can always be recovered.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

The following claims circulate but lack adequate evidence:

- Nasu was an Achaemenid goddess later demonized.
- She derives from or is identical to the Vedic Nāsatyas.
- She is Beelzebub, Lilith, a Mesopotamian plague demon, or the Arabic *nasnās*.
- Ancient statues or temple reliefs securely portray her.
- Zoroastrians sacrificed humans to her.
- She presided over temple prostitution.
- The fly was chosen because ancient priests possessed germ theory.
- Every Iranian ossuary or exposed body proves observance of the extant Vīdēvdād.
- Modern fantasy portraits reproduce ancient iconography.

All should be classified as **modern invention, unsupported comparison, or overstatement** unless new primary evidence is produced.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the attested history |
|---|---|
| Second millennium–early first millennium BCE | A specifically named Nasu cannot be securely reconstructed. General Indo-Iranian death-pollution concepts may predate the surviving texts, but this remains comparative reconstruction. |
| c. 1200–1000 BCE or later, under competing chronologies | Old Avestan Gāthās composed. They do not name Nasu; verses from them are later used to expel her. |
| Sixth–fourth centuries BCE | Achaemenid inscriptions and royal tombs attest Iranian religion and protected entombment but not Nasu by name. |
| c. 440 BCE | Herodotus reports Persian/Magian exposure of corpses to dogs or birds before burial; he does not mention Nasu. |
| Late first millennium BCE–early first millennium CE | Probable broad horizon for assembling/redacting major portions of the Vīdēvdād. Exact dating is disputed. |
| Sasanian period, 224–651 CE | Vīdēvdād classified as the nineteenth *nask*; Middle Persian translation and commentary develop. |
| After 651 | Chapter 12 is lost; later liturgical compilations reflect post-Sasanian transmission. |
| Ninth century | Dēnkard summarizes the Vīdēvdād and explains *sagdīd*; Bundahišn classifies Nas among demons; Manuščihr’s Dādestān ī Dēnīg discusses Nasu and corpse exposure. |
| c. 1205 | Colophons trace the ancestry of major Vīdēvdād manuscripts to an Iranian copy by Ardaxšīr Wahman. |
| 1269 onward | The Iranian manuscript lineage is carried to India. |
| 28 August 1323 | L4, one of the principal Pahlavi Vīdēvdād manuscripts, completed at Navsari. |
| 17 May 1324 | K1 completed at Cambay by Mihrābān Kay Khusrow. |
| Fifteenth–eighteenth centuries | Persian Rivāyats transmit Iranian rulings on Nasu, *sagdīd*, and corpse disposal to Parsis in India. |
| 1594 | Ml3, the sole complete direct transcript of K1, completed at Broach. |
| 1618 and 1638 | Important Iranian *Vīdēvdād Sāde* manuscripts Mf2 and Jp1 copied. |
| 1770s | Anquetil-Duperron’s publications introduce the Avesta and Zoroastrian purity texts widely to European scholarship. |
| 1853 | Spiegel publishes an edition of the Pahlavi Vīdēvdād. |
| 1880 | Darmesteter’s English Vīdēvdād translation appears in *Sacred Books of the East*. |
| 1886–1896 | Geldner publishes his critical edition of the Avesta, foundational for later textual work. |
| 1892–1893 | Darmesteter publishes his expanded French *Zend-Avesta*. |
| 1895–1898 | Revised English presentation of Darmesteter’s translation appears; it becomes the most widely available English version. |
| 1907 | Hoshang Jamasp publishes Avestan and Pahlavi Vīdēvdād materials using then-accessible manuscripts. |
| Twentieth century | Iranian exposure practice largely ceases; isolated burial becomes common. Parsi debates grow over reform, cremation, burial, and continued *dakhma* use. |
| Late twentieth century | Vulture decline in India severely disrupts traditional Parsi corpse exposure. |
| Twenty-first century | Avestan Digital Archive and Corpus Avesticum Berolinense projects make manuscript evidence more accessible; Nasu continues in Zoroastrian teaching and is increasingly detached from it in online fantasy culture. |
| 2026 | No documented independent cult of Nasu; the figure remains principally scriptural, ritual, scholarly, and popular-cultural. |
