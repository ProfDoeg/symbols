# Haoma: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the Younger Avesta, Haoma is a male **yazata**—a being “worthy of worship”—inseparably identified with a sacred mountain plant, its pressed juice, the consecrated drink made from that juice, and the divine power manifested through the rite. Middle Persian tradition calls him **Hōm Yazad**; modern Persian and Parsi usage generally has **Hōm/Hom**.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Haoma is securely attested in the orally composed Avestan corpus. The clearest extended witness is the Younger-Avestan **Hōm Stōm or Hōm Yašt, Yasna 9–11**, probably assembled from older inherited verses during the first millennium BCE, although no absolute year can be assigned. Possible references in the linguistically older Gāthās—especially Yasna 32.14 and 48.10—are disputed because neither passage unambiguously names *haoma*. The oldest independently datable written occurrence related to the name is Old Persian **Haumavargā**, an ethnonym for a Saka people in the Achaemenid inscriptions of Darius I, about 520–486 BCE; it proves knowledge of *hauma* as a word but is not an attestation of the personified god. Surviving complete Yasna manuscripts are much later, principally from the seventeenth century onward.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

Haoma is simultaneously:

- **A plant:** a yellow or golden-green, fragrant, many-branched plant growing on high mountains.
- **A ritual substance:** the juice or extract obtained by pounding its stems with water.
- **A consecrated preparation:** *parahaoma*, offered in the Yasna and ultimately returned to the waters.
- **A divine person:** a beautiful, radiant male yazata who appears to Zarathustra and speaks with him.
- **A priestly archetype:** the divinely appointed sacrificer or *zaotar*, girded by Ahura Mazda and serving the Amesha Spentas.
- **A power:** healing, alertness, victory, fertility, inspired knowledge, continuity of lineage, and resistance to death.
- **An eschatological plant:** in later Zoroastrian cosmology the earthly rite anticipates the White Hōm of the final renovation, whose preparation gives immortality to resurrected humanity.

These are not successive allegorical interpretations imposed on a merely botanical plant. The earliest full texts already move between plant, drink, ritual action, and speaking deity without separating them. [The Hōm Yašt](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hom-yast/) and the [Iranica survey of Haoma ritual](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/haoma-ii/) document that complex.

### Names and scripts

- **Avestan:** *haoma-*; conventionally **Haoma**. In Avestan script it is written approximately 𐬵𐬀𐬊𐬨𐬀. The spelling *Haōma* is sometimes used to make the vowel sequence explicit.
- **Reconstructed Proto-Indo-Iranian:** ***sauma-***, conventionally “the pressed extract.”
- **Vedic Sanskrit:** **sóma**, सोम, the exact regular cognate.
- **Old Persian:** **hauma-**, surviving most conspicuously in **Haumavargā**, the Achaemenid name of a Saka group.
- **Middle Persian:** **hōm**, written heterographically in Pahlavi, commonly represented as 𐭧𐭥𐭬.
- **Sogdian:** forms including **xwm/γwm**; the theophoric personal name **γwmdʾt**, “given by Hōm,” is recorded in the documents from Mount Mugh.
- **New Persian:** **هوم**, *hōm/hūm*.
- **Gujarati and Parsi English usage:** **Hom**, often in *Hom Yasht*, *Hom Yazad*, and *Hom-ni jashan*.

The accepted etymology connects *haoma/soma* with Avestan *hu-* and Sanskrit *su-*, “press, pound, extract.” It therefore meant approximately “the pressed thing” or “extract” before becoming, by metonymy, the name of the plant and deity. The Iranian change of Indo-Iranian initial *s* to Avestan *h* is regular; the relationship between *soma* and *haoma* is not a speculative resemblance. [The botanical and linguistic survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/haoma-i/) emphasizes, however, that the word’s derivation does not by itself identify the species.

### Principal epithets

The following are textually attested, principally in Yasna 9–11:

- **ašavan / ašaum**, “righteous,” “belonging to truth/order.”
- **zairi-**, “yellow,” “golden,” or “golden-green.” The color is botanical and radiant rather than a fixed iconographic costume.
- **bərəzant-**, “high, lofty, tall.”
- **dūraoša-**, traditionally interpreted in Middle Persian as “keeping death far away.” Its historical etymology is uncertain; interpretations such as “death-averting” are traditional or provisional, not established philology.
- **frāšmi-**, of disputed meaning, sometimes translated “fresh,” “vigorous,” or “flourishing.”
- **fradaṱ.gaēθa-**, approximately “furthering life” or “promoting the living world.”
- **baēšazya-**, “healing,” “medicinal.”
- **vərəθrajan-**, “resistance-smashing” or “victorious,” cognate in formation with Vedic *vṛtrahán-*.
- **hu-baoδi-**, “sweet-smelling” or “fragrant.”
- **swiftly sacrificing zaotar:** Haoma’s priestly title in the Mithra Yašt tradition.

The Middle Persian explanation of *dūraoša* as *dūr-ōš*, “keeping death far,” is a **later learned etymology**, valuable as evidence for Sasanian and medieval exegesis but not proof of the Avestan word’s original formation.

### Classification through time

- **Reconstructed Indo-Iranian phase — modern scholarly reconstruction:** a deified pressed plant/drink, ***sauma***, already central to sacrifice before the separation of Iranian and Indo-Aryan traditions.
- **Gāthic phase — disputed:** an established intoxicating or stimulating rite that Zarathustra may have criticized, regulated, or opposed in some form.
- **Younger Avesta — securely attested canonical classification:** a beneficent male yazata, plant, divine priest, and ritual power created or established by Ahura Mazda.
- **Middle Persian books — later canonical systematization:** Hōm Yazad, the earthly sacred plant, and the cosmic White Hōm/Gōkarn are integrated into creation and renovation.
- **Ferdowsi — medieval epic transformation:** Hōm appears as an extraordinarily strong, holy mountain hermit who captures Afrāsiāb. The deity has been euhemerized into a human ascetic.
- **Living Zoroastrian practice:** Hom remains a consecrated plant and yazata, not merely an ancient mythological figure.

There is no securely attested Greek or Roman equation of Haoma with Dionysus, Apollo, or another Classical god. Comparisons with Dionysian wine or ambrosia are modern comparative constructions. Vedic Soma is a **genetic cognate**, not an *interpretatio graeca*-type identification made by ancient worshippers.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The problem of dating

The Avesta was transmitted orally for centuries. Linguistic date, compositional date, redaction, ritual arrangement, and manuscript date must therefore be separated.

The Gāthās are in Old Avestan, linguistically close to the oldest Vedic Sanskrit, and are usually assigned broadly to the late second or early first millennium BCE. Younger Avestan compositions belong to a later but still pre-Sasanian oral culture, conventionally within the first millennium BCE. The extant written codices descend from a much later manuscript tradition. The Hōm Stōm is consequently ancient in language and poetic material but not preserved on an ancient physical manuscript. [Jean Kellens’s survey of the Avesta](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/) describes this textual situation.

### Possible Gāthic evidence

#### Yasna 32.14

This difficult Old Avestan stanza mentions *dūraoša*, the regular Younger-Avestan epithet of Haoma, in a hostile context involving oppressive ritual specialists and cattle. One interpretation finds a condemnation of the Haoma cult. Others question whether *dūraoša* here is a proper epithet of Haoma or whether the stanza can sustain the traditional reconstruction.

**Tier:** securely early text; interpretation disputed.

#### Yasna 48.10

Zarathustra asks when men will reject or restrain *maδa*, an intoxicating or maddening substance associated with bad ritual practice. The stanza does not say *haoma*. Mary Boyce and others have nevertheless taken it as evidence that Zarathustra objected to an old warrior use of Haoma that excited fury. Other interpreters understand it as criticism of drunken abuse, a particular mixture, or wrongful ritual rather than of Haoma itself.

**Tier:** securely early text; identification with Haoma is a modern scholarly reconstruction.

The common popular statement “Zarathustra banned Haoma” is therefore stronger than the evidence. Conversely, the assertion that he wholly endorsed the later Hom ritual is also unprovable.

### Hōm Stōm/Hōm Yašt: Yasna 9–11

This is the principal primary corpus. “Hōm Yašt” is conventional, but the text stands within the Yasna rather than in the separate collection of twenty-one Yašts. “Hōm Stōm,” “Praise of Hōm,” avoids that ambiguity.

- **Language:** Younger Avestan, with inherited formulae and imperfectly preserved metrical passages.
- **Composition:** first-millennium BCE oral material, assembled from older poetic units; precise dating is impossible.
- **Manuscripts:** much later than composition. Major witnesses to the liturgical Yasna tradition are early modern.
- **Content:**
  - Yasna 9: Haoma appears to Zarathustra; the four primordial pressers and their rewarded sons; priestly and fertility blessings; Haoma’s installation as first priest.
  - Yasna 10: praise of the plant’s mountain growth, fragrance, healing power, victory, alerting intoxication, ritual instruments, and moral confession.
  - Yasna 11: sacrificial portions and Haoma’s threat to bind a negligent sacrificer as he bound Fraŋrasyan.

Avestan text and public-domain translation are available in the [online Yasna](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/); a linguistic excerpt from Yasna 10 is supplied by the [University of Texas Indo-European lessons](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/aveol/60).

### Other Avestan texts

- **Yasna 1–8, 12, 22–27, 62, 65–72:** Haoma and the haoma-water occur within the liturgical architecture, invocations, consecration, and offering to the waters.
- **Visperad:** extends the Yasna for high festivals; explicitly presents haoma-water, mortar, pestle, barsom, fire, and other ritual components. [Visperad translation](https://www.avesta.org/visperad/vrsbe.htm).
- **Vendīdād:** its night-long liturgical performance incorporates the Yasna and supplies ritual directions relevant to the second parahaoma. The Vendīdād service in its complete form may be a relatively late, possibly Sasanian development.
- **Nērangestān:** preserves learned ritual prescriptions concerning offerings and sacrifice.
- **Mihr Yašt, Yasht 10.89–90:** Haoma is the swiftly sacrificing priest on Mount Harā; the passage associates the rite closely with Mithra.
- **Drvāsp Yašt, Yasht 9.17–20:** Haoma sacrifices to Drvāspā and asks to capture Fraŋrasyan.
- **Ard Yašt, Yasht 17.37–39:** a parallel appeal to Aši for the same boon.
- **Zamyād Yašt, Yasht 19:** preserves an alternative in which Haosravah himself binds or defeats Fraŋrasyan, demonstrating that the capture story was not transmitted in one consistent form.
- **Gōš Yašt, Yasht 9:** a figure named Haoma asks Drvāspā for the conquest of Fraŋrasyan; scholars have debated whether every occurrence of “Haoma” in these heroic lists refers to the deity, a legendary priest, or a partly euhemerized form.
- **Fravardīn Yašt:** names connected with Haoma occur among revered *fravašis*, including names whose relation to the god is not certain.

### Achaemenid evidence

Darius I’s imperial lists name the **Sakā Haumavargā**. Proposed translations include “Haoma-preparing Sakas,” “Haoma-revering Sakas,” and older “Haoma-drinking Sakas”; the second element remains debated. The label strongly implies a group publicly distinguished by a hauma-related practice, but it tells us nothing directly about Haoma’s mythology. [Iranica’s Haumavargā article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/haumavarga/) treats the philological dispute.

Neither the Behistun inscription nor the royal tomb inscriptions at Naqš-e Rostam name Haoma as a god. The throne-bearer reliefs include securely captioned Haumavargā delegates, but these are ethnic representations, not images of Haoma.

Green-chert mortars, pestles, and plates from the Persepolis Treasury, some bearing Aramaic inscriptions, were once confidently labeled Haoma equipment. A seal impression showing figures, fire, twigs, and a mortar encouraged that interpretation. The reading has been challenged: the inscriptions need not be religious and the utensils need not have crushed haoma. A recent reassessment calls the older “ritual capital” reconstruction into question. [The objects and controversy](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/8/965).

### Classical testimony

Herodotus, *Histories* 1.132, says a Magus sang a “theogony” at Persian sacrifice; he does not name Haoma. This is contextual evidence for priestly sacrificial performance, not direct evidence for the Hom rite.

Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* 46–47, reports that Persian priests pounded a plant called **ōmōmi** in a mortar with the blood of a sacrificed wolf while invoking Hades/Darkness and cast the mixture where sunlight did not reach. Since *ōmōmi* resembles *haoma*, it has been interpreted as a Greek rendering of the name. The rite, however, is directed against or toward Areimanios/Ahriman and conflicts with normative Zoroastrian treatment of Haoma as beneficent. It may preserve a hostile rite, misunderstood report, local practice, or Greek systematization. It cannot simply be inserted into the Yasna ritual.

### Middle Persian corpus

- **Bundahišn**, principally ninth-century redactions of older material: distinguishes ordinary Hōm from the cosmic White Hōm or Gōkarn in the world sea; the latter is guarded within the good creation and supplies immortality at renovation.
- **Dēnkard**, ninth–tenth century: summarizes lost Avestan nasks and Zarathustra’s sacred history. Hōm participates in the transmission of Zarathustra’s pre-existent glory and seed in later hagiographic systems.
- **Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram**, ninth century: elaborates the prophet’s conception and birth using plant, milk, and sacred substance motifs.
- **Dādestān ī Dēnīg** and the Pahlavi Rivāyat: at the final renovation, White Hōm and the fat of the primordial bull Hadayōš form the death-destroying drink for resurrected people.
- **Mēnōg ī Xrad**, ninth century in surviving form: supplies the wider cosmology of Afrāsiāb, immortality, and the final defeat of evil, but is not an independent narrative biography of Hōm.
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag:** its visionary journey belongs to the same ritual-literary culture; it does not provide an important independent Haoma myth.

### New Persian epic

Ferdowsi’s **Šāhnāma**, completed around 1010 CE, turns Hōm into a holy recluse descended from Fereydun. He hears Afrāsiāb’s lament from a cave, seizes and binds him, loses him temporarily when the captive escapes into a lake, and enables Kay Khosrow’s final revenge. This is a medieval epic recasting of the Avestan capture myth, not evidence that an historical ascetic named Hōm existed. [Public-domain translation of the episode](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Persian_Literature/Volume_1/The_Sh%C3%A1h_N%C3%A1meh/The_Death_of_Afr%C3%A1siy%C3%A1b).

### Manichaean and Roman Mithraic material

No surviving Manichaean scripture makes the Zoroastrian Haoma deity a significant divine actor. Iranian divine names were extensively repurposed in Manichaean translation, but similarity of Iranian vocabulary does not establish a Hōm cult.

The Roman mysteries of Mithras provide no secure representation, inscription, or liturgical text naming Haoma. Claims that the Mithraic communal cup was “the Haoma sacrament” are **modern comparative hypotheses**. The Iranian Mihr Yašt does associate Mithra with Haoma ritual, but it does not follow that Roman Mithraists preserved that rite.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

The Hōm Stōm attributes to Haoma:

- healing and the driving away of disease;
- alertness, wakefulness, inspired awareness, and ritual efficacy;
- strength, speed, victory, and the overcoming of enemies;
- fertility and the begetting of distinguished sons;
- aid in childbirth;
- religious knowledge, especially for students of the nasks;
- flourishing herds and the protection of beneficent animals;
- resistance to demonic forces;
- delay or overcoming of death;
- correct sacrificial priesthood;
- purification and strengthening of the waters.

Haoma is not simply “the Zoroastrian god of intoxication.” Yasna 10 distinguishes his condition from the violent wrath produced by other intoxicants: other forms of *mada* are accompanied by Aēšma, Wrath, while Haoma’s exhilaration is linked with truth. That is an internal theological distinction, not a pharmacological test.

### Form

In Yasna 9 Haoma manifests to Zarathustra “at the time of pressing” as a beautiful, radiant man. In the plant descriptions he is yellow-golden, fragrant, high-growing, many-branched, and flourishing on mountains. These are two modes of one presence.

There is no securely identified ancient cult statue of Haoma and no stable anthropomorphic iconography comparable to Greek Apollo or Egyptian Isis. Depictions of priests carrying barsom, mortars at Persepolis, Haumavargā delegates, or vegetation in Iranian art should not be relabeled “Haoma” without an inscription or decisive context.

### Symbols and ritual implements

Securely associated objects are:

- mountain twigs or stems of hōm;
- stone, metal, or iron mortar and pestle;
- water;
- a strainer or filter;
- cups and offering vessels;
- **barsom/barsman**, the ritual bundle;
- pomegranate twig in later and living practice;
- milk—traditionally goat’s milk among Parsis and cow’s milk in Iranian practice—for the second preparation;
- the sacred girdle, with which Haoma is installed as priest and binds Fraŋrasyan.

No canonical color, weekday, planet, zodiacal sign, weapon, temple animal, or numerical system belongs uniquely to Haoma. Modern charts assigning him a planet, chakra, tarot card, or elemental correspondence are modern invention unless they cite a distinct recent esoteric system.

### Botanical identification

#### Ephedra

The strongest historical-botanical case identifies ritual hōm with one or more **Ephedra** species, especially *E. gerardiana*, *E. intermedia*, or *E. major/procera*:

- Ephedra is a leafless, many-jointed mountain shrub consistent with Avestan description.
- Its active alkaloids are water-soluble, fitting repeated pounding with cold water.
- Iranian Zoroastrians around Yazd called ephedra *hōm/hum*.
- During the nineteenth century they supplied dried twigs to Parsis in India, where suitable ephedra does not grow.
- Ephedrine promotes wakefulness, endurance, attention, and physiological stimulation—effects Harry Falk considered closer to the texts than hallucination.
- The continuity of vernacular name and ritual use is unusually strong evidence.

This establishes Ephedra as the best-documented plant in the living Iranian-Zoroastrian transmission. It does not logically prove that every Proto-Indo-Iranian pressing used exactly one species.

#### Harmala/Syrian rue

Flattery and Schwartz proposed **Peganum harmala**, stressing Iranian drug traditions and harmaline’s psychoactivity. Against it:

- rue is not crushed as hōm in documented Zoroastrian ritual;
- the plant’s form fits the Avestan description poorly;
- the linguistic continuity of Ephedra as *hōm* is stronger.

The theory remains part of the history of scholarship but has not displaced Ephedra.

#### Amanita muscaria

R. Gordon Wasson proposed the fly agaric mushroom as Soma. Problems for Haoma include the Avestan language of stems, branches, mountain growth, pounding, and continuing Ephedra practice. Elaborate proposals involving filtered urine are much better suited to Wasson’s reconstruction than to the attested Iranian ritual.

#### Cannabis, poppy, mandrake, ginseng, and composite drinks

All have been proposed. None has comparably strong combined linguistic, botanical, and ritual evidence. Bronze Age residues alleged from Bactria-Margiana ritual installations were once reported as ephedra, cannabis, and poppy. Both the identifications and their connection to Soma/Haoma have been contested. Even if the residues were confirmed, they would attest local plant use, not by themselves the named god Haoma.

**Finding:** Ephedra is the most economical identification for historical Iranian hōm and a strong candidate for Proto-Indo-Iranian *sauma*. Absolute identity across every region and period remains unprovable.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other divine beings

Haoma has no securely attested parents, spouse, siblings, or biological offspring. The absence is significant: his “sons” in Yasna 9 are rewards granted to human pressers, not Haoma’s children. His origin is botanical-cosmic and liturgical rather than genealogical. Ahura Mazda is his creator or installer; the high mountains and waters sustain his plant form.

### Documented divine interactions

Each entry below records an actual encounter, action, petition, appointment, or joint ritual role. Mere membership in the same pantheon is excluded.

- **Ahura Mazda — appointment as priest.** Ahura Mazda invests Haoma with the sacred girdle and establishes him as the first or swift-sacrificing *zaotar*. **Source:** Yasna 9.26; Mihr Yašt 10.89–90; first-millennium BCE Younger Avestan material. **Tier:** securely attested canonical.
- **Ahura Mazda — creator and recipient.** Haoma is repeatedly called Mazda-made and serves as priest in the worship of Ahura Mazda. **Source:** Yasna 9–10. **Tier:** securely attested canonical.
- **The Amesha Spentas collectively — priestly service.** Haoma sacrifices for or serves Ahura Mazda and the Amesha Spentas as their divine priest. **Source:** Mihr Yašt 10.89–90. **Tier:** securely attested canonical.
- **Mithra — ritual association.** The Mihr Yašt situates the primordial Haoma preparation on high Harā in Mithra’s hymn and presents Haoma as the divine sacrificer. It does not narrate combat, kinship, or fusion between them. **Source:** Yasht 10.89–90. **Tier:** securely attested canonical association.
- **Drvāspā — petition and granted victory.** Haoma offers sacrifice to Drvāspā and asks for power to seize Fraŋrasyan and deliver him to Haosravah. **Source:** Drvāsp Yašt 9.17–20. **Tier:** securely attested canonical heroic myth.
- **Aši — petition for the same boon.** Haoma worships Aši and asks to capture Fraŋrasyan for Haosravah’s vengeance. **Source:** Ard Yašt 17.37–39. **Tier:** securely attested canonical parallel.
- **Vohu Manah — healing and animal-protection association.** Haoma’s medicines are connected with the delights of Vohu Manah; later tradition places Hōm among the powers protecting beneficent animals. No narrative meeting survives. **Source:** Yasna 10; Persian Rivāyats. **Tier:** early liturgical association plus later priestly systematization.
- **Gə̄uš Urvan/Gōšurun — overlapping protector and alternative captor.** Later Persian Rivāyat tradition makes Gōšurun, rather than Hōm, bind Afrāsiāb. This is a competing assignment, not a joint adventure. **Tier:** late regional/priestly variant.
- **Māh and Rāman — cultic association.** Hōm is grouped with Vohu Manah’s fellow-workers Māh, Gōšurun, and Rāman in protection of animals. No individual encounter is recorded. **Source:** Persian Rivāyats. **Tier:** later canonical systematization.
- **Aēšma — moral opposition.** Yasna 10 contrasts the wrath accompanying other intoxicants with Haoma’s truth-aligned exhilaration. This is an antithetical theological relation rather than a narrated combat. **Tier:** securely attested canonical.
- **The daēvas collectively — destructive opposition.** Praise and consumption of Haoma are said to strike down or repel daēvas. No individual daēva is named in the encounter. **Source:** Yasna 10. **Tier:** securely attested canonical.
- **White Hōm/Gōkarn — identity or cosmic counterpart.** Middle Persian cosmology relates ordinary ritual Hōm to the world-renovating White Hōm. Texts do not consistently explain whether these are one being in terrestrial and cosmic forms or two sacred plants. **Tier:** later canonical elaboration.
- **Soma — cognate counterpart, not a recorded meeting.** Iranian Haoma and Vedic Soma descend from a common Indo-Iranian divine plant/drink. No ancient narrative has Haoma meet Soma. **Tier:** modern historical-linguistic reconstruction based on ancient cognate traditions.

No documented interaction was found with Anāhitā, Ātar, Tištrya, Verethragna, Rashnu, Nairyō.saŋha, Spenta Ārmaiti, Haurvatāt, or Ameretāt as individual characters, beyond shared invocation or general pantheon membership.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Haoma appears to Zarathustra

At the morning pressing, a radiant man appears before Zarathustra. Asked who he is, he declares himself righteous Haoma *dūraoša*. He instructs Zarathustra to gather and press him so that he may be praised as earlier worshippers praised him.

The scene converts ritual action into revelation: every priest pressing the plant repeats Zarathustra’s primordial encounter. Haoma is at once the material being pounded and the teacher directing the rite.

**Earliest source:** Yasna 9.1–2.  
**Tier:** securely attested Younger-Avestan canonical myth.  
**Important caution:** some modern retellings say Zarathustra initially “refused to worship” Haoma. The text’s damaged and formulaic opening supports tension but not a developed conversion drama.

### The four primordial pressers

Zarathustra asks who first pressed Haoma and what reward each received.

1. **Vīvahvant** pressed Haoma and received the son **Yima Xšaēta**, the splendid king under whom there was abundance and, before his fall, freedom from death and decay.
2. **Āθwya** pressed and received **Θraētaona**, conqueror of the three-headed serpent Aži Dahāka.
3. **Θrita** pressed and received two sons: **Urvāxšaya**, just and law-abiding, and **Kərəsāspa**, the great dragon- and monster-slayer.
4. **Pourušāspa**, Zarathustra’s father, pressed and received **Zarathustra**, who first proclaimed the religion widely and drove daēvas into hiding.

The cycle links pressing with ideal male generation: king, dragon-slayer, warrior, and prophet are ritual rewards. It is also a compressed sacred history in which Haoma precedes Zarathustra and authorizes the prophet’s ancestry.

**Earliest source:** Yasna 9.3–15.  
**Tier:** securely attested canonical.  
**Comparative status:** the theme of Soma bringing heroic sons and vigor is inherited Indo-Iranian, but the exact Iranian sequence is not found as such in the Rigveda.

### Haoma as first priest

Ahura Mazda girds Haoma with the sacred belt, and Haoma becomes the primordial priest for the Amesha Spentas. The myth establishes divine precedent for human initiation, ritual dress, and sacrificial office.

**Source:** Yasna 9.26 and Mihr Yašt 10.89.  
**Tier:** canonical priestly theology.

### Defeat of Kərəsāni

Yasna 9.24 credits Haoma with casting down or removing **Kərəsāni**, who had arrogantly sought to prevent priests from teaching or moving freely in the world. The name resembles Vedic Kṛśānu, a guardian or opponent connected with Soma, but the relationship between the figures is debated.

**Tier:** securely attested but textually obscure inherited myth.  
**Later interpretation:** Haoma becomes defender of priestly transmission.

### Capture of Fraŋrasyan/Afrāsiāb

In the Drvāsp and Aši Yašts, Haoma petitions divine powers for the ability to seize the Turanian Fraŋrasyan and hand him to Haosravah, who must avenge Syāvaršan. Yasna 11 warns a negligent sacrificer that Haoma may bind him as he bound Fraŋrasyan.

The Zamyād Yašt gives a competing form in which Haosravah is the captor or victor. Later tradition apparently developed Fraŋrasyan’s inaccessible underground metal fortress, making a superhuman captor necessary.

In Ferdowsi, Hōm has become a powerful hermit. He captures Afrāsiāb with his sacred girdle; the captive escapes into a lake but is drawn out, captured again, and delivered for Kay Khosrow’s vengeance.

**Development:**

- Younger Avesta: Haoma is the divine captor.
- Parallel Avestan version: Haosravah is the victorious binder.
- Persian Rivāyat: Gōšurun may perform the binding.
- *Šāhnāma*: Hōm is euhemerized as a human holy man.

The versions must not be harmonized into a single canonical story.

### White Hōm and the final renovation

Middle Persian cosmology places the **White Hōm**, also called **Gōkarn**, in the cosmic sea. At the *frašegird*, the final renovation, the resurrected receive a preparation made from White Hōm and the fat of the primordial bull Hadayōš. It makes their bodies immortal; thereafter hunger, decay, and death cease.

The earthly parahaoma is thus a ritual anticipation of eschatological immortality.

**Sources:** Bundahišn, Dādestān ī Dēnīg, Pahlavi Rivāyat; mainly ninth–tenth-century redactions of older traditions.  
**Tier:** later canonical elaboration.  
**Caution:** identifying every Avestan reference to Haoma with Gōkarn retrojects the developed Middle Persian cosmology.

### Haoma and Zarathustra’s birth

Later Pahlavi hagiography divides Zarathustra’s pre-existent components—glory, guardian spirit, and bodily substance—and transmits them through cosmic and earthly media. A miraculous Hōm stalk participates in bringing the prophet’s glory or substance into the materials consumed by his parents.

**Sources:** Dēnkard VII and Zādspram; ninth-century redactions.  
**Tier:** later canonical hagiographic elaboration, not stated in the Gāthās or Hōm Stōm.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No independent Haoma temples

No securely identified temple was dedicated exclusively to Haoma. His cult is embedded in the **Yasna**, Zoroastrianism’s principal priestly liturgy. Its ritual focus is the consecrated precinct, fire, water, implements, recitation, and priestly performance—not an anthropomorphic image in a temple cella.

Accordingly, the absence of a temple inventory or statue is not evidence that Haoma was marginal. The ritual itself is his primary monument.

### The two parahaoma preparations

The developed ceremony employs two distinct preparations.

#### Preliminary preparation

During the **paragna**, the serving priest or *rāspī* purifies the ritual area and ingredients. In documented Parsi practice:

- three dried hōm twigs are washed and consecrated;
- a pomegranate twig is cut;
- the materials are placed in a metal mortar;
- water is added;
- the twigs are pounded and strained;
- Avestan formulae and the *xšnūman* of Hōm accompany the actions.

#### Main liturgical preparation

During recitation of the Yasna, the priests prepare the second parahaoma, traditionally adding milk. It is offered as *zaoθra* to the waters. The ceremony coordinates spoken text and physical action: the Hōm Stōm is not simply read about a remembered rite but recited while the rite is enacted.

The [Iranica Yasna article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasna/) describes the ritual table, mortar, pestle, barsom stands, knife, filter, cups, water, milk, and plant material.

### Consumption and offering

Ancient Indo-Iranian practice probably involved drinking the pressed preparation. Zoroastrian ritual redirected much of the consecrated liquid toward the waters. Priests may consume a small quantity at prescribed points; historical communal consumption varied.

In conservative Šarifābād near Yazd, lay and priestly participants were still reported in the twentieth century as partaking of the Nowruz parahaoma. In ordinary modern Parsi practice, the quantities are small and the ceremony is not an entheogenic gathering.

### Priesthood and access

The high ritual is performed by qualified priests, especially the **zōt** as principal celebrant and **rāspī** as assisting priest. Their actions, purity requirements, recitations, and manipulation of implements are closely regulated. The divine Haoma’s appointment as primordial *zaotar* supplies the mythic model.

### Calendar and occasions

Haoma does not possess a unique day-name in the standard thirty-day Zoroastrian calendar. The plant is used in:

- ordinary Yasna;
- Visperad services on major festivals;
- Vendīdād liturgies;
- some Jashan and commemorative rites;
- rites connected with water and communal purification;
- historically, Nowruz observance.

This differs from yazatas such as Mithra or Tištrya, who have calendrical days and major named festivals.

### Sacrifice and disputed violence

Animal sacrifice formed part of ancient Iranian worship and survived in different Zoroastrian environments, although later Parsi practice deemphasized or abandoned it. Haoma’s liturgy could accompany animal offerings, and Yasna 11 is concerned with rapidly cutting and assigning the sacrificial portion. The text’s concern with speed was later interpreted as compassion toward the victim.

There is **no evidence of human sacrifice to Haoma**. Interpretations connecting Haoma with human sacrifice arise from broad theories about Indo-European sacrificial substitution or from conflation with other Iranian rites. They are modern reconstruction, not documented Haoma cult.

Yasna 10.15 contains hostile sexual language against a polluting woman who improperly consumes Haoma’s offering. It witnesses gendered purity polemic within the ancient ritual system; it does not document “temple prostitution.” No Haoma temple prostitution is archaeologically or textually attested.

### Theophoric and ethnic evidence

Sogdian names such as **γwmdʾt**, “Hōm-given,” show that the name functioned in personal devotion. The Achaemenid **Haumavargā** ethnonym shows hauma practice could mark collective identity. Neither provides a census of worshippers.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Proto-Indo-Iranian background, approximately second millennium BCE

**Modern scholarly reconstruction:** the regular correspondence of Avestan *haoma*, Vedic *soma*, *yasna/yajña*, the pressing terminology, filters, water, milk, mountain provenance, priestly recitation, and deification establishes a shared *sauma* cult before the Iranian–Indo-Aryan separation. [The comparative evidence](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/indo-iranian-religion/) is stronger than any proposed identification with a particular archaeological complex.

### Zarathustra and the Gāthic community

The prophet’s relationship with inherited haoma practice remains disputed:

1. **Rejection theory:** Yasna 32.14 and 48.10 condemn Haoma and its intoxicating warrior-sacrificial cult.
2. **Reform theory:** Zarathustra rejected violent or wrath-producing use but retained a controlled offering to the waters.
3. **Abuse-only theory:** he condemned bad intoxication, not Haoma.
4. **Continuity theory:** Haoma’s life-giving function was absorbed conceptually into Amərətāt/Immortality and was never truly discarded.

Boyce proposed that conservative priests restored fuller consumption after Zarathustra had restricted it. This is a historical reconstruction, not an event narrated by a contemporary source.

### Younger-Avestan consolidation

Haoma was thoroughly incorporated into Mazdayasnian worship as a righteous, Mazda-made yazata. The Hōm Stōm represented him as precursor and supporter of Zarathustra, neutralizing any remembered opposition. His priestly role, healing, fertility, heroic history, and anti-demonic power were systematized.

### Achaemenid period, 550–330 BCE

Royal inscriptions do not name the deity. They do attest the Haumavargā Sakas. Mortars and ritual imagery at Persepolis may reflect haoma preparation, but the proposed identification is contested. Greek descriptions of Magian sacrifice provide indirect context.

It is therefore improper either to call Haoma an officially proclaimed Achaemenid imperial god or to say that Achaemenid religion suppressed him.

### Parthian and Sasanian periods

The Avesta was collected and reorganized under priestly institutions, although later accounts of a single complete Achaemenid archetype destroyed by Alexander are not straightforward history. Under the Sasanians, the Yasna and its extensions assumed forms ancestral to the surviving ritual tradition. Haoma was integrated into cosmology, eschatology, and the learned zand commentary.

No secure evidence shows an independent Manichaean Haoma cult. Roman Mithraism’s relationship to Iranian haoma remains conjectural.

### Early Islamic Iran

After the Arab conquest, Zoroastrian political and institutional power contracted. Islamization, taxation, loss of patronage, local persecution, emigration, and demographic change reduced the number of communities able to maintain elaborate priestly services. No known decree singled out Haoma for suppression; his decline in public visibility followed the contraction of Zoroastrian institutions generally.

The Pahlavi books of the ninth and tenth centuries demonstrate continued learned interest. Ferdowsi’s Hōm shows the figure surviving in Persian epic memory after the establishment of Islam.

### Parsi India

Iranian Zoroastrian migrants established communities in western India. Because ephedra does not naturally thrive in the relevant Indian lowlands, Parsis obtained dried hōm from Iran and Afghanistan. Communication recorded in the Persian Rivāyats helped maintain ritual detail. Colonial-era priests and scholars—especially Martin Haug’s informants and Jivanji Jamshedji Modi—documented the practice extensively.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

European publication of the Avesta turned Haoma into a major object of comparative religion and botanical speculation. Observation of Yazdi Zoroastrians using locally named ephedra materially changed the plant debate. Parsi ritual manuals documented continuity while also standardizing descriptions for print.

### Present

Yasna and related rites continue among Zoroastrian priests in India and Iran, although the small number of fully trained ritual priests places the most elaborate liturgies under demographic pressure. Hōm twigs remain ritually important. This is continuous Zoroastrian worship, not a twentieth-century neopagan reconstruction.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Comparative religion and “Aryan” discourse

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scholars used Soma/Haoma as evidence of an Indo-Iranian or “Aryan” religious past. The linguistic relationship is valid; racialized schemes that turned the drink into evidence for a biologically unified superior “Aryan race” are obsolete ideological appropriations.

Modern Iranian nationalist writing sometimes presents Haoma as a uniquely Persian elixir suppressed by foreign conquest. That erases both its Indo-Iranian prehistory and its continuous specifically Zoroastrian custodianship.

### Psychedelic reinvention

Wasson’s Amanita theory and Flattery and Schwartz’s harmaline theory helped create a popular image of Haoma as a lost psychedelic sacrament. Contemporary internet claims variously identify it with psilocybin, cannabis, ayahuasca analogues, Syrian rue plus acacia, or a secret multi-drug cocktail.

These are **modern reconstructions**, not transmitted Zoroastrian traditions. Some may be pharmacologically possible; possibility is not attestation. Living Parsi and Iranian practice points instead to Ephedra, and the ancient texts emphasize wakefulness, strength, victory, healing, and truth-aligned exhilaration more clearly than visions.

### Occultism

No evidence establishes a Haoma rite in the original Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Thelema, or a continuous Western grimoire tradition. Modern occult correspondence lists that assign Haoma to a planet, sephirah, tarot trump, or “Indo-European entheogenic current” are recent syntheses.

Theosophical and perennialist authors often treated Soma and Haoma as symbols of divine consciousness, the lunar principle, or an internal spiritual nectar. These are datable modern allegorical readings, not recoveries of a lost Avestan doctrine.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist use

Small online Iranian-reconstructionist, Indo-European, and eclectic pagan communities invoke Haoma or experiment with substitute beverages. Reliable population figures do not exist. Such practice should be distinguished from continuous Zoroastrian ritual, which operates through inherited priestly liturgy and communal rules.

### Literature, games, and commerce

The word appears sporadically in fantasy, games, music, botanical supplements, beverage branding, and psychedelic culture. No single modern fictional depiction has displaced the Avestan image in the way popular culture has transformed figures such as Thor or Anubis. Commercial products called “Haoma” are not evidence of an inherited recipe; many contain no Ephedra and use the name simply to suggest antiquity, energy, or sacred intoxication.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Early editions and translations

- **Anquetil-Duperron**, eighteenth century, first made substantial Avestan material available in Europe through Parsi manuscripts and instruction.
- **N. L. Westergaard** and **Karl Friedrich Geldner** established major nineteenth-century Avestan editions.
- **James Darmesteter**, *Le Zend-Avesta* and the *Sacred Books of the East*, integrated translation with Parsi ritual observation but often treated the Avesta as later than current scholarship does.
- **L. H. Mills** translated the Yasna into English, though many philological conclusions are now dated.
- **Jivanji Jamshedji Modi**, *The Religious Ceremonies and Customs of the Parsees* (1922), remains indispensable testimony to Parsi practice. [Modi’s description](https://www.avesta.org/ritual/rcc4.htm).

More recent philology by Humbach, Kellens, Pirart, Skjærvø, Hintze, Malandra, and others emphasizes ritual composition, oral transmission, and the difficulty of treating Avestan texts as prose narratives.

### Hōm Stōm as compilation

The hymn is not a single composition by one author. Its changes of meter, syntax, address, and subject indicate a collection of inherited praises placed into a ritual frame. The redactor may not always have fully understood the old material. This explains contradictions and obscure lines without requiring a lost perfectly coherent “original myth.”

### Zarathustra’s position

This is the major theological-historical dispute. The evidence permits confidence that Zarathustra criticized some established intoxicating or sacrificial practice; it does not permit confidence about whether he abolished Haoma, reformed it, or attacked only abuse. Later texts deliberately make Haoma an ally of Zarathustra, which may represent continuity, reconciliation, or both.

### Plant controversy

- **Ephedra:** supported by living name and usage, physical description, geography, water-soluble stimulant alkaloids, and wakefulness.
- **Peganum harmala:** supported by regional psychoactive traditions but weak on ritual continuity and morphology.
- **Amanita muscaria:** influential in Soma studies but poorly fitted to Iranian ritual evidence.
- **Cannabis/poppy/composite:** possible in some Central Asian rites, unproven as Haoma.
- **Substitution theory:** different plants may have replaced an inaccessible original. This is plausible but often invoked without evidence whenever a favored candidate conflicts with the texts.

The responsible conclusion is graded: Ephedra is certain for documented modern Iranian hōm, highly plausible for much earlier Iranian ritual, and a strong but not universally accepted identification for Proto-Indo-Iranian *sauma*.

### Archaeology

Proposed “Haoma rooms” and residue finds in the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex do not carry inscriptions naming Haoma. The Persepolis mortars are real Achaemenid objects but their religious use is contested. Archaeology can demonstrate plant processing and ritual installations more readily than it can name the god involved.

### Iconographic absence

No ancient image is securely inscribed “Haoma.” Modern illustrations of a green-robed man holding Ephedra are reconstructions. This negative finding matters: descriptions of Haoma’s “traditional appearance” often derive solely from Yasna 9–10 and modern artistic inference.

### Discredited or unsupported claims that still circulate

- Haoma was certainly cannabis: unsupported.
- Haoma was certainly psychedelic: unsupported.
- Haoma was certainly Amanita: unsupported.
- BMAC vessels scientifically proved an ayahuasca-like Haoma cocktail: overstated and disputed.
- Zarathustra unequivocally banned Haoma: stronger than the Gāthic evidence.
- Roman Mithraists drank Haoma: unproven.
- Haoma worship involved human sacrifice or temple prostitution: no evidence.
- Haoma is the same word as Chinese *huo-ma* “fire hemp”: incompatible with the secure Indo-Iranian etymology.
- Haoma was merely symbolic and never a physical plant: contradicted by ritual instructions and living practice.
- Today’s Ephedra automatically proves the exact species used in every ancient Soma and Haoma rite: excessive certainty.

### State of the sources

The most important primary witnesses are preserved in Avestan liturgical manuscripts held in Indian, Iranian, European, and North American collections. Digital photography and projects such as the Avestan Digital Archive and Multimedia Yasna have improved access, textual comparison, and study of ritual performance. Much of the Sasanian Avesta is lost; the Dēnkard’s summaries cannot fully recover it.
