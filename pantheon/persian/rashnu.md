# Rashnu: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the Younger Avesta, Rašnu is a *yazata*—a worship-worthy divine being—principally the divine judge and guardian of juridical truth. In Middle Persian Zoroastrian literature he remains a *yazad*, but his role narrows and intensifies into that of an impartial judge of the dead at the Činwad/Chinvat Bridge.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest independently datable evidence is probably not a narrative text but Iranian theophoric personal names reconstructed from Elamite spellings in the Persepolis Fortification archive—*Rašnuka*, *Rašnudāta*, and possibly *Rašnubar(a)*—written under Darius I, approximately 509–493 BCE. These names establish knowledge of a deity Rašnu in Achaemenid Persis, but do not establish what myths, rituals, or Zoroastrian theology their bearers associated with him. The oldest literary witnesses are passages in the Younger Avesta, whose components were orally composed and transmitted at uncertain dates during the first millennium BCE but survive only in manuscripts copied many centuries later. Rašnu is absent from the linguistically older Gāthās.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early attested]**, **[Later Avestan]**, **[Middle Persian elaboration]**, **[Learned systematization]**, **[Regional or manuscript variant]**, **[Syncretistic comparison]**, **[Medieval accretion]**, **[Modern reconstruction]**, and **[Modern invention/unverified]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Rašnu is the Iranian divine judge. His earliest recoverable sphere is not specifically postmortem judgment but the trial, oath, and ordeal: he is summoned to ensure that testimony is straight, to expose theft and falsehood, and to punish perjury. The familiar image of Rashnu weighing every dead person’s deeds on golden scales at the Chinvat Bridge is genuine Zoroastrian tradition, but it is **Middle Persian elaboration**, securely explicit in the *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag* and *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, not in the Gāthās or the *Rašn Yašt*.

### Forms of the name

- **Avestan:** 𐬭𐬀𐬱𐬥𐬎, conventionally transliterated **Rašnu**; older English works use **Rashnu**, **Rashnû**, or **Rashnou**.
- **Middle Persian:** **Rašn**, conventionally Rashn; written heterographically in Book Pahlavi.
- **New Persian:** **Rašn/Rašan**, رَشن or رشن in modern representations.
- **Sanskrit translation tradition:** **Satyapati**, approximately “lord/master of truth,” is a functional translation rather than an inherited Indo-Aryan form of the name.
- **Middle Parthian personal name:** *ršnwmtr*, interpreted as “Rašn-Mihr” or “Rashnu-Mithra,” preserves the divine pair in reverse order.
- **Greek and Latin:** No secure ancient Greek or Latin transcription or interpretatio of Rashnu has been established.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic consensus, with disputed semantic nuance]** Rašnu is normally derived from Iranian *raz-* “move/direct in a straight line; make straight; adjudicate,” ultimately Indo-European \**h₃reǵ-*. Related formations underlie concepts of straightness, rule, right, and rulership across Indo-European languages. The divine name may therefore be rendered:

- Bartholomae: “Justice,” “god of justice.”
- Gershevitch and Malandra: “Judge.”
- The standing epithet **razišta-**, Middle Persian **razistag**, is the superlative: “straightest,” hence “most just.”

Éric Pirart contests the conventional juridical interpretation of parts of *Yasht* 12. He translates the name-function approximately as “Orientation” and argues that Rashnu originally represented the “straight” or correct pronunciation and alignment of ritual speech. Pirart also interprets *varah-* in the hymn as a sacrificial space rather than an ordeal. Most earlier philologists understand *varah-* as “oath” or “ordeal.” The dispute cannot be resolved by simply substituting one English gloss for the Avestan: the hymn contains both ritual-performance language and unmistakably juridical epithets.

Claims that Rašnu is etymologically identical with Vedic Viṣṇu are not accepted in current specialist treatments. There is no Old Indic reflex of *Rašnu* as a divine name. Comparison with Vedic Varuṇa concerns function—especially divine supervision of oaths and association with Mitra—not a demonstrated identity of names.

### Principal epithets in *Yasht* 12.7–8

The hymn addresses Rašnu as:

- **aṣ̌āum** — “possessing truth/order,” “truthful,” or “righteous”;
- **razišta** — “straightest,” “most just”;
- **spəništa** — “most beneficent/bounteous”;
- **vaēδišta** — “wisest” or “most knowing”;
- **parakwistəma** — approximately “best knowing from afar”;
- **dūraēdarəštəma** — “seeing farthest into the distance”;
- **arəθəm bairišta** — approximately “best supporter/bearer of the trial”;
- **tāyūm nijagništa** — “best smiter of the thief”;
- destroyer of thief and bandit at the proceeding.

Middle Persian texts regularly call him **Rašn ī rāst** or **Rašn ī razist/razistag**, “Rashn the just/straightest.”

### Classification through time

- **Old Iranian/Achaemenid evidence:** a deity whose name could form personal names; further classification is unavailable.
- **Younger Avesta:** an individual male *yazata*, invoked in sacrifice and judicial ritual.
- **Pahlavi literature:** a spiritual *yazad* and eschatological judge.
- **Modern English summaries:** frequently called an “angel.” This follows nineteenth-century translation practice for *yazata* and should not be mistaken for a native classification as a Jewish, Christian, or Islamic angel.

No source makes Rashnu a demon, monster, jinn, Amesha Spenta, or independent creator-god.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Achaemenid onomastic evidence

**[Earliest secure physical evidence, c. 509–493 BCE]** Elamite administrative tablets from the Persepolis Fortification archive contain names reconstructed as:

- *Rašnuka*;
- *Rašnudāta*, “given by Rashnu”;
- possibly *Rašnubar(a)*.

The readings are onomastic reconstructions through Elamite orthography rather than alphabetic spellings of the divine name. William Malandra argues that these names are especially important because the Fortification archive reflects ordinary imperial administration and cannot simply be attributed to later Zoroastrian textual influence. They make an old, perhaps pre-Zoroastrian Iranian Rashnu probable. They do **not** demonstrate a Rashnu temple, priesthood, image, or doctrine of judgment after death.

Rashnu is not named in the royal Old Persian inscriptions at Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam, or elsewhere. Darius’s praise of *arta*—truth, right order—is conceptually relevant but is not an invocation of Rashnu.

### The Gāthās and Older Avesta

**[Negative finding]** Rašnu does not occur by name in the Gāthās. The Gāthic Chinvat crossing, judgment, *aša*, and consequences of moral choice form an older eschatological setting into which later texts placed named judges, but they cannot be cited as descriptions of Rashnu or his scales.

### Younger Avestan corpus

#### Yasna

Rašnu is invoked in *Yasna* 1.7, 2.7, 16.5, 65.12, and 70.3. These are Younger Avestan liturgical passages, not the Older Avestan Gāthās. In Yasna 1.7 and 2.7 he is closely linked with Arštāt, personified rectitude or justice.

#### *Mihr Yašt* (Yasht 10)

- **Yt. 10.41:** Mithra, Sraosha, and Rashnu pursue covenant-breakers.
- **Yt. 10.79:** *rašnu-* can function appellatively of Mithra, approximately “judge.”
- **Yt. 10.100 and 126:** Rashnu accompanies Mithra’s chariot, appearing on one side and then the other.
- **Yt. 10.139:** Rashnu and Arštāt occur together.

These passages preserve Rashnu’s connection with enforceable truth and covenant before the Pahlavi literature makes judgment of the dead his overwhelmingly prominent function.

#### *Fravardīn Yašt* (Yasht 13)

Yt. 13.3 and 13.47 pair Mithra and Rashnu in formulae comparable in structure—but not necessarily origin or identity—to the Vedic pairing Mitra–Varuṇa.

#### *Ard Yašt* (Yasht 17)

Yt. 17.16 supplies a theological “family”: Rashnu is a son of Ahura Mazda and Spənta Ārmaiti and brother of Sraosha, Mithra, Aši, and the Mazdayasnian Daēnā. This is a **later Avestan priestly genealogy**, not a birth story or biological narrative, and other texts do not elaborate it into a continuous family myth.

#### Vendidad/Vīdēvdād 4.54–55

Rashnu presides over, or is offended in connection with, an oath procedure involving *āpəm saokəṇtavaitīm zaranyāvaitīm vīθušavaitīm*. Older translations associated this with sulphurous or molten ordeal-water; Martin Schwartz argues that it means “oath-water,” rejecting an etymology of later Persian *sowgand xordan*, “swear an oath,” as literally “drink sulphur.” The perjurer is said to ward off Rashnu and deceive Mithra.

#### Sīrōza and Khordeh Avesta

Rashnu receives the eighteenth day of each thirty-day Zoroastrian month. Sīrōza 1.18 and 2.18 invoke him with Arštāt. Later Khordeh Avesta practice incorporates *Yasht* 12 into personal devotional recitation.

### *Rašn Yašt* (Yasht 12)

**[Later Avestan; redactionally composite]** The hymn consists of 39 numbered verses including its liturgical frame. It survives in thirteen identified manuscripts of Khordeh Avesta and “pure Yasht” types: F1, E1, Pt1, P13, O3, L18, K12, J18, J10, J19, W2, Ml2, and B27. No manuscript contains a Pahlavi translation of this Yasht.

Some manuscripts and Geldner’s edition present the hymn continuously; other manuscript families divide it into 31 *kardes*. Consequently, claims that it “has 31 sections” describe one arrangement of the transmitted text, not an uncontested original architecture.

Its contents are:

1. **Yt. 12.1–2:** An unnamed righteous interlocutor, probably Zarathustra, questions Ahura Mazda about the beneficent formula and correct legal or ritual pronouncement.
2. **12.3–8:** Instructions concerning the *varah*: barsom is spread along the sun’s path; fire, an overflowing cup, ghee or ordeal-oil, and vegetable oil appear; Ahura Mazda and Rashnu are called to attend. Rashnu’s judicial and omniscient epithets follow.
3. **12.9–37:** Rashnu is invoked successively in 29 places, producing the most extensive compact cosmographical itinerary in the extant Avesta:
   - the seven regions of the inhabited world;
   - the Sea Vourukaša, the Saēna Tree, and the Raŋhā;
   - the earth’s edge, center, and every place upon it;
   - Harā Bərəzaitī and associated peaks;
   - Vega, Sirius, Ursa Major, and stellar classes;
   - moon and sun;
   - the Endless Lights, Best Existence, and House of Welcome.
4. **12.38:** Standard concluding liturgy.

Malandra treats the text as a late redactional collection: material originally proper to Rashnu appears to have been reframed so that Ahura Mazda gives the instructions. Goldman likewise notes its traditionally “minor” and comparatively late classification. The cosmographical repetitions establish Rashnu’s universal availability: no litigant can evade the far-seeing judge by place or distance.

### Middle Persian corpus

#### *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.114–163

**[Middle Persian elaboration; probably compiled in late antiquity, transmitted in later manuscripts]** On the fourth dawn after death, Srosh, good Vāy, and Wahrām assist the soul, while Astwihād, evil Vāy, Frazišt, Nizišt, and Ēšm oppose it. At the Chinvat Bridge, Mihr, Srosh, and Rashn mediate. Rashn weighs the deeds with a spiritual balance which favors neither righteous nor wicked, lord nor monarch; it does not turn falsely by a hair’s breadth. At 2.163 Rashn detects the wicked soul’s wickedness.

#### *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag* 5

**[Middle Persian visionary elaboration]** Wīrāz reaches the Chinvat Bridge and sees Mihr, Srosh, Rashn, and other yazads. AWN 5.5 explicitly gives Rashnu **“yellow, golden scales”** (*tarāzūg ī zard ī zarrēn*) in which good and evil deeds are weighed. The work’s traditions may contain Sasanian material, but its surviving form and manuscripts are considerably later; it cannot be projected unchanged into Achaemenid belief.

#### *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*

The ninth-century high priest Manuščihr repeatedly places Rashn in the postmortem court. Chapters 14, 30, and 31 are cited in traditional editions for the fourth-day rites and judgment. A sacred cake or *drōn* is consecrated to Rashn during funerary observance.

#### *Dēnkard*

- Book 8 summarizes the lost legal *Duvasrūj/Duwasrud Nask*, including the exaltation of Rashnu and juridical matters.
- Book 9 associates Rashn with correct judgment, recompense, and ritual remembrance.
- A Pahlavi gloss on Yasna 46.5, quoted in the *Dēnkard*, retrospectively interprets a Gāthic passage as the work of Rashn at judgment. That is **Pahlavi exegesis**, not Rashnu’s occurrence in the Gāthic wording.

#### *Bundahišn*

The Iranian or Greater *Bundahišn* locates the pivot of Rashn’s cosmic balance on the peak of Dāitī: one pan reaches toward the northern base of Alborz and the other toward its southern summit. This monumental cosmic geography is **learned Middle Persian systematization**. Rashn is also assigned as a helper or co-worker within the organized hierarchy of creations, commonly under Amurdād/Ameretāt. He is not prominent in the *Bundahišn*’s resurrection narrative itself.

#### *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* 7.19–20

In the eschatological crisis, Mihr, Nēryōsang, Srosh, Rashn, Wahrām, Aštād, and Xwarrah assist Pēšyōtan. This is a late Middle Persian apocalyptic ensemble, not an Avestan Rashnu myth.

#### Ritual and Rivāyat literature

The *Šāyest nē Šāyest*, Pahlavi Rivāyats, and later Persian Rivāyats prescribe rites involving Rashn, Aštād, Vāy, and the fravašis during the first three nights and fourth dawn after death. These are significant evidence for lived funerary liturgy, although most surviving copies are post-Sasanian.

### Texts in which Rashnu is not securely present

- **Herodotus:** no named Rashnu.
- **Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris*:** valuable for Greek testimony about Iranian dualism, but no securely identifiable Rashnu.
- **Achaemenid royal inscriptions:** no named Rashnu.
- **Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*:** no active deity Rashnu corresponding to the Avestan judge.
- **Manichaean corpus:** no securely documented adoption of Rashnu comparable to Manichaean transformations of Mihr/Mithra.
- **Roman Mithraic inscriptions and tauroctonies:** no securely identified Rashnu.
  
Similarity between Rashnu’s balance and judgment imagery elsewhere is insufficient to establish direct borrowing or divine identity.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

1. **Human law, oath, and ordeal — [Early/later Avestan].** Rashnu attends the *varah*, detects false testimony, smites thieves, and shares enforcement of covenants with Mithra.
2. **Universal surveillance — [Later Avestan].** His far-seeing epithets and presence through every terrestrial, celestial, and heavenly region make concealment from judgment impossible.
3. **Judgment of the dead — [Middle Persian elaboration].** At the Chinvat Bridge he weighs accumulated good and evil deeds.
4. **Cosmic moral measurement — [Learned systematization].** The *Bundahišn* expands his balance to cosmic dimensions.
5. **Funerary protection and adjudication — [Late antique through modern liturgy].** Rashn is invoked during rites surrounding the soul’s fourth-day judgment.

### Powers and limitations

Rashnu knows and sees at a distance, supports legal procedure, detects thieves and falsehood, and measures deeds without regard for rank. He is not the source of moral law, sole ruler of the dead, executioner of every sentence, or psychopomp in the strict sense. Srosh commonly escorts and protects the soul; Mihr participates in judgment; Daēnā embodies or displays the moral quality of the person’s life; hostile spirits attempt to seize the wicked.

### Scales

- No scales occur in *Yasht* 12.
- The impartial balance is explicit in *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.119–22.
- Its “yellow, golden” character is explicit in *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag* 5.5.
- Its enormous geographical construction appears in the Greater *Bundahišn*.

Thus “Rashnu, god with the golden scales” is authentically traditional but belongs to the Middle Persian eschatological dossier, not the earliest attested layer.

### Iconography

**[Negative finding]** No securely identified Achaemenid, Parthian, or Sasanian anthropomorphic image can presently be labeled Rashnu by inscription or distinctive attribute. No surviving ancient statue, relief, coin type, or seal securely combines his name and image. Generic figures carrying scales, enthroned judges, or divine attendants cannot be assigned to him without independent evidence.

Illustrated manuscripts of the *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag* and modern Zoroastrian or popular-art depictions may show a male angelic judge with scales. These are witnesses to medieval or modern reception, not portraits preserving an authenticated ancient iconography.

### Animals, plants, colors, numbers, and celestial bodies

- **Animal:** none uniquely sacred to Rashnu is attested.
- **Plant:** none uniquely sacred; barsom and vegetable oil belong to the ritual apparatus of *Yasht* 12, not to a Rashnu botanical cult.
- **Color:** golden/yellow applies to his Pahlavi balance; it is not established as his universal cult color.
- **Number/day:** the eighteenth day of every Zoroastrian month is Rashn Rōz.
- **Celestial body:** none is exclusively his. *Yasht* 12 invokes him at named stars, moon, and sun to express ubiquity, not astral identity.
- **Weapon:** none. His “striking” or destroying thieves is verbal divine action, not evidence for a standard club or sword.
- **Principal emblem:** the balance, but only securely from Middle Persian sources onward.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Yasht 17.16 calls Rashnu a son of **Ahura Mazda** and **Spənta Ārmaiti**, and brother of **Sraosha**, **Mithra**, **Aši**, and the Mazdayasnian **Daēnā**. This is the only clear family schema. It should be read as theological classification: divine abstractions and ritual beings are arranged as members of Mazda’s household. No source narrates Rashnu’s conception, birth, childhood, marriage, or offspring.

No consort, child, mount, or individual temple-servant is securely attested.

### Every documented divine interaction or joint episode

- **Ahura Mazda — ritual instruction:** Ahura Mazda answers the righteous questioner and directs the *varah* ritual in which Rashnu is invoked; *Yasht* 12.1–6, Younger Avestan. **[Later Avestan/redactional]**
- **Spənta Ārmaiti — parentage:** named as Rashnu’s mother, with Ahura Mazda as father; *Yasht* 17.16. **[Later Avestan priestly genealogy]**
- **Mithra — sibling relation:** both children of Mazda and Ārmaiti; *Yasht* 17.16. **[Later Avestan genealogy]**
- **Mithra — chariot companionship:** Rashnu rides beside Mithra, on the right in one stanza and the left in another; *Mihr Yašt* 10.100, 126. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Mithra — joint punishment of covenant-breakers:** Mithra, Sraosha, and Rashnu pursue violators; *Mihr Yašt* 10.41. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Mithra — joint oath jurisdiction:** a perjurer “wards off Rashnu and deceives Mithra”; *Vendidad* 4.54–55. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Mithra — paired invocation:** formulaic Rashnu–Mithra pair; *Yasht* 13.3, 47. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Mithra — joint judgment of souls:** Mihr and Rashn await the soul at Chinvat, with Srosh mediating; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115–22. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Sraosha — sibling relation:** *Yasht* 17.16. **[Later Avestan genealogy]**
- **Sraosha — pursuit of covenant-breakers:** with Mithra and Rashnu; *Yasht* 10.41. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Sraosha — funerary/judicial cooperation:** Srosh escorts or protects the soul and participates with Mihr and Rashn at the bridge; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115–22. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Arštāt/Aštād — paired justice:** jointly invoked as Rashnu–Arštāt in Yasna 1.7, 2.7; Yasht 10.139; Yasht 12’s dedication; Sīrōza 1.18, 2.18. **[Later Avestan liturgical association]**
- **Arštāt/Aštād — bridge and funerary association:** appears with Rashn and other yazads in the *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag* and funerary texts. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Aši — sibling relation:** *Yasht* 17.16. **[Later Avestan genealogy]**
- **Daēnā — sibling relation:** *Yasht* 17.16. **[Later Avestan genealogy]**
- **Daēnā — judgment context:** the deceased encounters his or her embodied Daēnā in the afterlife complex within which Rashn judges, but the surviving sources do not give them a sustained dialogue. **[Avestan eschatology developed in Middle Persian literature]**
- **Victorious Winds (*Vāta vərəθrājanō*) — joint attendance at the ordeal:** *Yasht* 12.4, 6. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Dāmōiš Upamana — joint attendance at the ordeal:** *Yasht* 12.4, 6. **[Later Avestan; identity obscure]**
- **Kayanian Glory (*kauuaēm xᵛarənō*) — joint attendance:** *Yasht* 12.4, 6. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Mazda-created Radiance (*saoke mazdaδāite*) — joint attendance:** *Yasht* 12.4, 6. **[Later Avestan]**
- **Good Vāy — aids the soul approaching Rashnu’s court:** *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Evil Vāy — opposes the soul before judgment:** *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Wahrām/Verethragna — aids the soul on its fourth-day journey:** *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115; also joins Rashn in the Pēšyōtan apocalypse. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Astwihād — hostile watcher at the bridge:** Rashn judges while Astwihād seeks the soul; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115–22. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Ēšm/Aēšma — hostile opponent:** the spirit of wrath seeks to obstruct the soul at the court; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115–22. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Frazišt and Nizišt — hostile opposition:** demons opposing the soul’s passage; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.115. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Wizāreš — capture of the wicked:** binds the condemned soul whom Rashn detects as wicked; *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 2.161–63. **[Middle Persian elaboration]**
- **Nēryōsang — eschatological alliance:** joins Rashn and other yazads in support of Pēšyōtan; *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* 7.19–20. **[Late Middle Persian apocalypse]**
- **Xwarrah — eschatological alliance:** same episode; *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* 7.19–20. **[Late Middle Persian apocalypse]**
- **Amurdād/Ameretāt — hierarchical cooperation:** Rashn is assigned as a co-worker/helper in Middle Persian cosmological classification. **[Learned systematization]**

There is no securely attested personal combat, seduction, marriage, deception, or rivalry involving Rashnu. His narrative relations are overwhelmingly collegial, juridical, and liturgical.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The ordeal of truth

**Earliest narrative complex:** *Yasht* 12.3–8 and *Vendidad* 4.54–55.

A litigant or ritual officiant prepares the *varah*. Barsom, fire, a cup, ghee or ordeal substance, and vegetable oil are arranged. Ahura Mazda and then powerful Rashnu are invoked to attend. Rashnu’s capacity to perceive distant and hidden wrongdoing makes him the divine guarantor of the test. The thief and bandit cannot evade him; false swearing offends both Rashnu and Mithra.

The exact historical procedure is disputed. Older scholarship reconstructed ordeals involving fire, hot substances, or oath-water. Pirart’s interpretation makes *varah* the prepared sacrificial space rather than a forensic ordeal. The hymn’s legal vocabulary—correct law, proceeding, decision, thief, trial—nonetheless makes a juridical dimension difficult to remove completely.

### Rashnu everywhere

**Source:** *Yasht* 12.9–37.

This is not an adventure story but a ritualized cosmic progress. The invoker successively locates Rashnu throughout the seven continents, waters, world-mountains, stars, moon, sun, and highest heavens. Each location renews the appeal for him to attend. The effect is theological and juridical: Rashnu is available everywhere and sees across distance. The cosmography may have been incorporated because universal presence is essential to a judge who cannot be evaded.

### The soul at the Chinvat Bridge

**Early background:** Gāthic and Younger Avestan texts know the Chinvat crossing and postmortem consequences, but do not describe Rashnu operating golden scales.

**Full Middle Persian form:** After death, the soul remains near the body for three nights. At the fourth dawn it journeys toward the terrible, lofty Chinvat Bridge. Srosh and beneficent powers protect it; demons and destructive powers seek it. Mihr, Srosh, and Rashn constitute the judicial mediation. Rashn places the soul’s good and evil deeds in an impartial balance. Social status has no weight: king and least subject receive the same measurement. The righteous soul proceeds toward blessed existence; the wicked is exposed and seized; later systematic texts place an exactly balanced soul in Hamēstagān, the intermediate state.

The “three judges” formulation is a fair condensation of Middle Persian tradition, but their offices are not interchangeable. Rashn is the weigher and paradigmatic impartial judge; Srosh is prominently escort and protector; Mihr’s older domain is covenant and truth.

### Wīrāz’s vision

In the *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag*, the righteous priest Wīrāz undergoes an induced visionary journey to verify the truth of the religion. At the bridge he sees the divine court and Rashn’s golden scales. He then tours heaven and hell and witnesses rewards and punishments fitted to earthly conduct.

This is the source that most powerfully shaped later pictorial and popular presentations of Rashnu. It is a literary vision with a legendary Sasanian setting, not a contemporary court record from Ardašīr I’s reign.

### Rashnu and the last crisis

In the *Zand ī Wahman Yasn*, Rashn joins the collective of yazads assisting the immortal Pēšyōtan against forces hostile to the religion. Rashnu has no independent combat episode; his presence signals the restoration of divine justice and ordered religion. This is **late apocalyptic elaboration**.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers and temples

No temple excavated in Iran or Central Asia can be securely identified as a temple dedicated specifically to Rashnu. No foundation inscription, priestly archive, cult statue, or royal building inscription names such a sanctuary. The dossier therefore does not support claims for “great temples of Rashnu.”

His cult is predominantly textual and liturgical:

- invocation during sacrifice;
- juridical ordeal;
- calendrical observance;
- funerary ceremonies;
- personal recitation of the *Rašn Yašt*.

### Priesthood

There is no attested separate priesthood of Rashnu. Zoroastrian priests performing Yasna, Yasht, *drōn*, and funerary rituals invoke him within the general ritual system. References to “priests of Rashnu” in unsourced modern summaries should be treated as modern extrapolation.

### Calendrical worship

Rashn governs the **eighteenth day** of each Zoroastrian month. The sequence of days 16–18—Mihr, Srosh, Rashn—materializes the associated triad in the calendar just as Yashts 10–12 place their hymnic dossiers consecutively.

Modern Khordeh Avesta directions also prescribe or recommend the *Rašn Yašt* on days associated with Amurdād, Rashn, Aštād, and Zam. Practice varies among Shahanshahi, Kadmi, and Fasli communities because their civil dates diverge, not because Rashnu’s place within the thirty-day religious month differs.

### Ordeal and oath

*Yasht* 12 and *Vendidad* 4 are the principal evidence. The exact substances and mechanics remain debated; therefore it cannot be stated securely that worshippers routinely drank molten metal or sulphur in Rashnu’s name. Zoroastrian literature knows ordeals, including fiery ordeals elsewhere, but each claim must be tied to its particular text.

### Funerary rites

Pahlavi and later ritual sources prescribe continuous or repeated worship during the first three nights after death and ceremonies at the fourth dawn. A *drōn* or sacred cake may be consecrated to Rashn, often in association with Aštād, Vāy, or the fravašis. This practice corresponds to the belief that the soul then reaches judgment.

### Offerings

Attested or textually prescribed items include:

- barsom;
- fire;
- an overflowing ritual cup;
- ghee or an ordeal-associated fatty substance;
- vegetable oil;
- the funerary *drōn*.

These are components of Zoroastrian ritual, not evidence that Rashnu possessed an independent sacrificial cuisine.

### Sacrifice and contested allegations

No source reviewed documents human sacrifice to Rashnu, temple prostitution in his cult, a Rashnu-specific cult militia, or violent coercion conducted in his name. Ancient Iranian and Zoroastrian traditions contain violence, animal sacrifice, ordeal, punishment, polemic, and state coercion, but attaching human sacrifice or sexual cult practice specifically to Rashnu would be unsupported.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure and Cult

### Before or outside explicit Zoroastrian formulation

The Achaemenid theophoric names suggest an established Iranian deity Rašnu known in Persis by the late sixth or early fifth century BCE. Because these names arise outside the Avesta, Malandra regards Rashnu as inherited from older Iranian religion rather than created by Zoroastrian theologians.

This conclusion is probable, not absolute: a personal name proves devotion or cultural familiarity, not the precise theology of its bearer.

### Younger Avestan period

Rashnu entered the Mazdayasnian pantheon as a *yazata* subordinated within Ahura Mazda’s created order. His old judicial competence was preserved in oath and ordeal. Priestly redaction connected him genealogically to Mazda’s household and liturgically to Arštāt, Mithra, and Sraosha.

The *Rašn Yašt* appears comparatively late and composite. Its universal cosmography magnifies a specialist judicial deity without supplying heroic mythology.

### Achaemenid through Parthian history

Achaemenid onomastics prove the name’s currency, but royal inscriptions do not make Rashnu an imperial patron. Under Parthian conditions, the Middle Parthian name *ršnwmtr* indicates continued pairing with Mithra. Evidence is too sparse to reconstruct institutions, festivals beyond the calendar, or regional temples.

### Sasanian and late antique transformation

The major transformation was eschatological. Rashnu’s courtroom moved from human ordeal to the bridge of the dead. This was not a total replacement: the same principle—perfect detection and straight judgment—was applied to the soul’s account.

Sasanian-era scholastic organization also assigned him a place within the hierarchy of divine co-workers. The absence of secure Rashnu images contrasts with the rich textual elaboration of his office.

### After the Arab conquest

The seventh-century conquest ended Sasanian political support for Zoroastrian institutions. Conversion to Islam proceeded over centuries rather than through a single uniform decree. Communities contracted, migrated, paid discriminatory taxes in various periods, and sometimes experienced destruction or coercion. Rashnu’s worship was displaced as part of this general loss of Zoroastrian institutional power; no Rashnu-specific suppression campaign is documented.

Paradoxically, the ninth- to eleventh-century period produced or preserved much of the Middle Persian literature through which Rashnu is now known. Priests systematized inherited doctrine precisely while Zoroastrian communities had lost imperial dominance.

### Parsi and Iranian continuity

Rashnu remained present in:

- the thirty-day calendar;
- Sīrōza and Khordeh Avesta prayers;
- recitation of the *Rašn Yašt*;
- funerary ceremonies;
- teaching about the Chinvat judgment.

This is continuous Zoroastrian observance, not a twentieth-century revival, although particular printed prayer-book rubrics may be modern standardizations.

### Present

Rashnu continues to be invoked by Zoroastrians in India, Iran, and the diaspora. No reliable census measures how many individuals maintain Rashnu-specific devotional practice. He has no independent worldwide religious organization or demonstrable mass “cult revival.”

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Orientalist publication

European access was transformed by nineteenth-century editions and translations:

- Martin Haug and E. W. West’s Pahlavi work;
- James Darmesteter’s translation of the Avesta;
- Karl Geldner’s critical Avestan edition;
- E. W. West’s *Sacred Books of the East* translations;
- Bartholomae’s *Altiranisches Wörterbuch*;
- Lommel’s German translation of the Yashts.

These works established the spellings “Rashnu/Rashnû,” “angel of justice,” and “golden balance” in English-language reference literature. “Angel” often reflects Christianizing translation conventions rather than a native equation.

### Modern scholarship

Mary Boyce and William Malandra emphasized Rashnu’s roots in pre-Zoroastrian Iranian religion and his original jurisdiction over trial and oath. Goldman’s full study of *Yasht* 12 foregrounded manuscript transmission, ritual language, and cosmography. Pirart reopened the interpretation of the name and *varah*, shifting attention from courtroom ordeal toward correctly oriented ritual performance.

### Western occultism

The dedicated searches produced no primary Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Crowleyan, or early modern grimoire text in which Rashnu has a stable ritual office. General references to “Persian angels,” modern internet invocation lists, or newly written correspondences assigning Rashnu a planet, tarot card, crystal, incense, or magical seal are therefore **modern inventions unless individually sourced**.

No historical basis was found for claims that Rashnu belonged to Renaissance planetary magic, the canonical Golden Dawn grade system, or Roman Mithraic initiation.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Contemporary Iranian-religion reconstructionists and eclectic pagans sometimes honor Rashnu as a god of justice. Such practice is modern but may consciously reuse the Avestan hymns and Zoroastrian calendar. It should not be confused with the continuous Zoroastrian community, which understands him within Mazdayasnian worship rather than as an independently selectable pagan deity.

Claims that Rashnu is the “ancestral national god of all Kurdish peoples,” or any other modern ethnic totalization, exceed the evidence. Old Iranian heritage can legitimately be discussed across Iranian-speaking cultures, but the securely datable Rashnu evidence is Avestan, Achaemenid-Persian onomastic, Middle Persian, and Zoroastrian—not a documented ancient pan-ethnic institutional cult.

### Literature, games, and popular culture

Rashnu has a modest presence in mythology dictionaries, fantasy databases, games, and online god catalogues, normally reduced to “Persian god of justice who weighs souls.” These retellings commonly:

- project the golden scales into “ancient Persian” times without dating them;
- call him one of exactly three judges at every historical stage;
- give him a standardized winged appearance;
- assign modern magical correspondences;
- conflate Zoroastrian Yazatas with Christian angels.

Those are compressions or inventions, not the complete historical record.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Rashnu pre-Zoroastrian?

Malandra’s affirmative case rests on the Persepolis theophoric names and the apparently inherited juridical function shared structurally with Indo-Iranian oath theology. The argument is strong but reconstructive. The tablets do not state “this is the old pre-Zoroastrian god Rashnu,” and the religious affiliation of Achaemenid name-bearers is unknown.

### Rashnu and Varuṇa

The functional comparison is attractive:

- Rashnu and Varuṇa supervise truth and oaths;
- Rashnu pairs with Mithra as Varuṇa pairs with Mitra;
- both are associated with water and concealed wrongdoing.

But no Iranian cognate of the name Varuṇa survives as Rashnu, and Malandra’s “Varuna problem” concerns comparative reconstruction, not a textual Iranian statement identifying them. “Rashnu is the Persian Varuna” is therefore shorthand, not an ancient equation.

### “Justice,” “Judge,” or “Orientation”?

“Justice” emphasizes the abstract quality; “Judge” fits the deity’s activity and the appellative use of *rašnu-*; Pirart’s “Orientation” emphasizes straight ritual utterance and spatial correctness. The epithets concerning thieves and trials support jurisprudence, while the ritual construction of *Yasht* 12 prevents reduction to a secular courtroom god.

### What is the *varah*?

- Traditional view: oath or ordeal, perhaps using water, fire, fat, or oil.
- Pirart: prepared sacrificial area.
- Cautious conclusion: it is a solemn prepared ritual proceeding with legal-veridical vocabulary; its exact performance cannot be reconstructed confidently from the extant text.

### When did Rashnu acquire the scales?

No extant Avestan passage gives him scales. Their first explicit surviving descriptions are Middle Persian. Comparative parallels—Egyptian weighing of the heart, Greek judgment traditions, Christian and Islamic scales—have prompted diffusion theories, but formal similarity alone does not establish borrowing. A balance is also a natural symbol of impartial measurement. The direction and mechanism of any transfer remain unproved.

### Dating the corpus

The Avesta is linguistically ancient but materially late: composition, oral redaction, Sasanian canonization, and surviving manuscript copying are different events. *Yasht* 12 is linguistically Younger Avestan and considered comparatively late within that corpus; its extant manuscripts are medieval or early modern. Pahlavi books often preserve Sasanian learning but reached written forms and surviving recensions under Islam. Statements such as “the sixth-century *Mēnōg ī Xrad* says…” should therefore distinguish presumed compilation from extant witnesses.

### State of the evidence

- Persepolis tablets: excavated beginning in the 1930s; many remain under study.
- Avestan manuscripts: dispersed among Indian and European collections and increasingly digitized through projects such as the Avestan Digital Archive.
- Pahlavi manuscripts: often late, corrupt, and difficult because of heterograms and compressed script.
- Lost Avesta: *Dēnkard* summaries preserve notices of Nasks no longer extant, but summaries cannot substitute word-for-word for the lost originals.
- Iconography: effectively unattested for Rashnu.
- Archaeology of an independent cult: unattested.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- Rashnu is Vedic Viṣṇu under another name — unsupported.
- Rashnu is securely depicted on named Achaemenid or Sasanian reliefs — unsupported.
- Rashnu had a documented network of temples and a separate priesthood — unsupported.
- Golden Dawn magicians transmitted an ancient Rashnu ritual — unsupported.
- Rashnu’s scales occur in the Gāthās — false.
- The *Šāhnāma* contains a full Rashnu myth — unsupported.
- Roman Mithras’s attendants are Rashnu and Sraosha — not securely attested.
- Rashnu received human sacrifice or temple prostitution — no evidence found.
