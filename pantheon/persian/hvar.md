# Hvar Khshaeta: Research Dossier

**Class:** *yazata*—a worship-worthy beneficent divinity—and simultaneously the visible Sun itself, not an abstract “god behind” the solar body. In Middle Persian Zoroastrian texts the corresponding class is *yazd*. Hvar is not an Aməša Spənta (“Bounteous Immortal”), although the fixed formula calls the Sun *aməša*, “undying/immortal.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Sun, *huuar-* or *xᵛan-*, occurs already in the Old Avestan Gāthās, notably Yasna 44.3–5, where Ahura Mazdā is asked who established the paths of the Sun and stars and created light and darkness. These passages, conventionally placed around the late second or early first millennium BCE but not precisely datable, attest the Sun within Zarathustra’s cosmology; they do **not** yet securely present a named, independently characterized personality “Hvar Khshaeta.” The complete cult title *Huuarə Xšaēta*, “Radiant” or possibly originally “Mighty Sun,” is securely attested in the Younger Avestan liturgy and especially *Xwaršēd Yašt* 6. That oral composition belongs broadly to the Old Iranian period, probably the first millennium BCE, but cannot be dated to a particular century. Its first physical witnesses are medieval manuscripts: the surviving Avesta was copied long after its composition, and the earliest surviving Avestan codex of any kind, K7a, dates to 1288 CE. No Achaemenid inscription names Hvar Khshaeta.

## Evidentiary labels used below

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Old or Younger Avestan evidence, or closely dated classical testimony.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** authoritative Zoroastrian liturgy or doctrine compiled or redacted after the oldest strata.
- **[RS] Regional variant:** geographically restricted evidence.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** priestly cosmography, calendrics, scholastic interpretation, or deliberate harmonization.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically datable equation with another being.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or popular custom.**
- **[MR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern attribution.**

Dates assigned to orally transmitted Avestan material describe probable composition strata, not dates of surviving physical manuscripts.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

Hvar Khshaeta is the beneficent, visible Sun. The clearest statement of function is *Xwaršēd Yašt* 6.1–4: when the Sun shines, multitudes of spiritual *yazatas* gather and distribute *xᵛarənah*, prosperity or glory, over the Mazda-created earth; its rising purifies earth, flowing waters, springs, seas, and the creations of Spənta Mainyu; without sunrise the *daēvas* would destroy the seven regions; worship of the Sun repels darkness, demons, thieves, sorcerers, witches, death, and forgetfulness. Worshipping it is simultaneously worship of Ahura Mazdā, the Aməša Spəntas, one’s own soul, and the spiritual and terrestrial *yazatas*. **[EA/LC]** The text is available in Geldner’s Avestan edition and Darmesteter’s translation through the [Zoroastrian Archives](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt6.htm).

This is elemental rather than biographical theology. Hvar has no recorded parents, birth, spouse, offspring, anthropomorphic adventures, temple foundation myth, or death. It shines, rises, purifies, distributes glory, maintains the good creation, and excludes demonic darkness.

### Forms of the name

- **Avestan:** 𐬵𐬎𐬎𐬀𐬭𐬆 𐬑𐬱𐬀𐬉𐬙𐬀, conventionally transliterated *huuarə xšaēta*, *Huuarə Xšaēta*, *Hvarə Xšaēta*, or *Hvare Khshaeta*. Manuscripts and editions also join the words as *huuarəxšaēta-*.
- **Short Avestan designation:** *huuar-*; Old Avestan also has *xᵛan-*.
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** *xwaršēd*, *xwaršēt*, conventionally Khwarshēd or Xwaršēd.
- **New Persian:** خورشید, *ḵoršīd*, *khorshīd*, *khurshīd*; now the ordinary noun “sun” and also a personal name.
- **Parsi Gujarati/English usage:** Khorshed, Khurshed, Khwarshed.
- **Title of the hymn:** *Xwaršēd Yašt*, Khorshed/Khwarshed Yasht, Yasht 6.
- **Title of the daily litany:** *Xwaršēd Niyāyišn*, Khorshed Nyāyesh/Niyayesh.

The contraction from *huuarə-xšaēta-* to Middle Persian *xwaršēd* closely parallels *Yima Xšaēta* becoming Jamšēd/Jamshid. **[MR, linguistically secure]**

### Etymology and its dispute

*Huuar-* “sun” continues Proto-Indo-Iranian *súHar-* and is cognate with Vedic *svar-* “sunlight, heaven” and the solar name Sūrya. That Indo-Iranian relationship is secure.

The second element is less simple:

- The conventional translation is *xšaēta-* “shining, radiant, splendid,” reflected in traditional renderings such as “Radiant Sun.”
- Gershevitch proposed that it originated as a formation from Iranian *xšay-* “be powerful, rule”: therefore “Mighty/Ruling Sun.” Repeated use beside “Sun” then encouraged reinterpretation as “bright, radiant.” **[MR, disputed etymology]**
- Skjærvø consequently glosses the adjective cautiously as “radiant(?)”; Prods Oktor Skjærvø’s discussion of Yima observes that the word may instead concern the Sun’s luminous color. The same epithet is applied to Yima, heavenly lights, Tištrya, Apąm Napāt, the Aməša Spəntas, Aši, Anāhitā, and dawn, so it was not Hvar’s exclusive proper name. The problem is summarized in the [Iranica entry on Jamšid](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jamsid-i/).

These alternatives should not be collapsed. “Radiant Sun” is an excellent traditional and contextual translation; “Mighty Sun” is a serious historical-etymological proposal.

### Fixed epithets

Yasht 6 repeatedly calls Hvar:

- *aməša-*—“undying, immortal”;
- *raēva-*—“splendid, wealthy, glorious”;
- *auruuat̰.aspa-*—“swift-horsed” or “possessing swift horses”;
- *xšaēta-*—traditionally “radiant,” with the etymological qualification above.

“Swift-horsed” describes speed and possibly inherited Indo-Iranian solar imagery. Yasht 6 itself names no chariot and gives no number, color, or personal names for the horses. Modern depictions of Hvar in a Greek-style quadriga, or in a Vedic seven-horse chariot, are comparative artistic reconstructions rather than facts stated in Hvar’s hymn. **[MI when presented as canonical iconography]**

### Classifications and false equivalences

- **Hvar and Sūrya:** Their names and solar roles descend from shared Indo-Iranian religious vocabulary. This establishes cognacy, not proof that every Vedic Sūrya myth belonged to Iranian Hvar. **[MR]**
- **Hvar and Mithra:** Distinct in the Avesta. Yasht 10.13 says Mithra rises over Mount Harā before the swift-horsed immortal Sun. Yasht 6.5 praises “the best companionship between Moon and Sun” immediately after invoking Mithra, but it does not call Mithra the Sun. The later equation is discussed below. [Iranica’s Mithra study](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithra-i/) explicitly warns that the Avestan beings are not identical. **[EA distinction; SI equation]**
- **Hvar and Helios/Sol:** Greek and Roman writers translated Iranian solar worship into their own divine vocabulary, but no surviving Iranian text formally equates Hvar by name with Helios or Sol. “Greek equivalent: Helios” is a modern comparative shorthand, not an attested Iranian identification. **[MR/MI]**
- **Hvar and Šamaš:** Mesopotamian solar theology probably contributed to Mithra’s later solar and judicial iconography; direct identification of Hvar Khshaeta with Šamaš is not securely documented. **[MR, unestablished]**
- **Hvar and Ahura Mazdā:** The Sun is Ahura Mazdā’s created luminary, visible form or eye—not simply another name for Ahura Mazdā. Zoroastrian prayer directed toward the Sun need not imply that the solar disk is the supreme creator. **[EA/LC]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Old Avestan foundation

Yasna 44.3 asks who fixed the courses of Sun and stars; 44.5 asks who created light and darkness and ordained dawn, noon, and night. **[EA]** These are questions addressed to Ahura Mazdā and establish solar order as part of creation. They do not contain Hvar’s later short hymn or mythology.

The Gāthās are linguistically the oldest Avesta. Current dating remains approximate: many specialists place them around 1000 BCE, while proposals extend several centuries earlier or later. The crucial fact is relative chronology, not a false exact year. Jean Kellens’s [survey of the Avesta](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/) notes that precise dating cannot be justified, though a broad consensus places the best Younger Avestan compositions near the period of the Old Persian monuments.

### Younger Avestan corpus

#### Yasna

The Younger Avestan liturgy repeatedly includes the Sun among the worship-worthy creation:

- Yasna 1.30 and 2.30 invoke the radiant, swift-horsed Sun, the “eye of Ahura Mazdā,” along with stars, Tištrya, Moon, and Mithra.
- Yasna 3.13 and 7.13 similarly place the Sun between Moon and Mithra in the liturgical catalogue.
- Yasna 16.4 worships the Sun and other cosmic beings.
- Yasna 22.24, 25.4, 68.22, and related repetitions praise the swift-horsed Sun; Yasna 68.22 speaks of Ahura Mazdā’s eyes, which later interpretation commonly understands as Sun and Moon.

These passages are preserved in the ritual Yasna, whose layers vary in age. The nineteenth-century Mills translation is searchable in the [complete Yasna archive](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/). **[EA/LC]**

#### Xwaršēd Yašt, Yasht 6

This is the only surviving hymn devoted exclusively to Hvar. It has six substantive Avestan sections plus a concluding liturgical formula:

1. Solar light and heat cause spiritual *yazatas* to collect and distribute *xᵛarənah* and make the creations of *aša* prosper.
2. Sunrise purifies land, waters, seas, and Spənta Mainyu’s creation.
3. Without sunrise the demons would destroy the seven regions and no heavenly being could resist them in the material world.
4. Worship of the Sun counters darkness, darkness-born demons, thieves, raiders, male and female sorcerers, death, and forgetfulness; it simultaneously honors Ahura Mazdā, the Aməša Spəntas, one’s soul, and every spiritual and earthly *yazata*.
5. Mithra and the ideal companionship of Moon and Sun are invoked.
6. Hvar is worshipped with *haoma*, milk, *barsom*, thought, word, deed, libations, and truthful speech.
7. Later Pahlavi/Pazand and standard Avestan closing formulas complete the received service.

The exact date of Yasht 6 cannot be recovered. It is Younger Avestan and was transmitted orally before being incorporated into the written Sasanian Avesta. Yasht composition continued during the Old Iranian period; treating every Yasht as a unitary pre-Zoroastrian hymn or dating Yasht 6 precisely to the Achaemenid court exceeds the evidence. The history of the collection and competing models are surveyed in [Iranica’s Yašts entry](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/). **[EA with later liturgical framing]**

#### Xwaršēd Niyāyišn

The Sun litany is a nineteen-section daily prayer. Sections 11–16 reproduce Yasht 6.1–6; sections 1–10 invoke a larger divine company, and sections 17–19 are formulaic Avestan and Pazand conclusions. It is therefore not an independent ancient “second hymn” in its received form.

The Niyāyišn corpus is a late liturgical assemblage. Its Avestan material is largely excerpted from older texts; its introductory Pazand reflects New Persian and even Arabic vocabulary. The assemblage cannot be earlier than the late Sasanian period in its present form. Antonio Panaino’s study concluded that the Niyāyišns depend on the Yashts, including in the case of Yashts 6 and 7. The [Khordeh Avesta entry](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khordeh-avesta-2/) provides the manuscript and compositional analysis. **[LC]**

#### Sīrōza

The short and long Sīrōza organize invocations around the thirty named days of the Zoroastrian month. Xwaršēd governs the eleventh day. This calendrical position is securely traditional; claims assigning Hvar a unique “sacred number 11” in an occult numerological sense are a modern extrapolation. **[LC; MI for occult numerology]**

#### Vidēvdād/Vendidad

The purity code treats sunlight as an agent in the management of pollution. Vendidad 5 and 7 prescribe exposure of contaminated clothing or other materials to sunlight under specified conditions; corpse-related practice likewise uses sun and dryness within a much larger system of purity law. These prescriptions concern the physical-purificatory power associated with solar light, not a narrative episode involving an anthropomorphic Hvar. **[LC]**

### Middle Persian books

#### Bundahišn

The *Bundahišn* survives in Iranian and Indian recensions compiled and redacted after the Arab conquest, with material reaching its extant form between roughly the ninth and twelfth centuries, although it preserves older doctrine. A modern translation should be checked against Agostini and Thrope; the older West translation remains accessible in the [Zoroastrian Archives](https://avesta.org/mp/bundahis.html).

Its cosmology teaches:

- Ohrmazd creates the luminaries between earth and sky: fixed and wandering stars, then Moon, then the shining swift-horsed Sun.
- Sun and Moon are chiefs of the movable heavenly bodies.
- The Sun occupies a station above the Moon and below the throne of the Aməša Spəntas and Endless Light.
- Before Ahriman’s assault, Sun, Moon, and stars stood still and it was always noon. After the assault they began moving and will continue until cosmic restoration.
- The Sun’s motion is likened to an enormous three-feathered arrow shot by a mighty archer.
- Mount Alburz and its openings explain the solar circuit in the inherited geocentric cosmography.
- The luminaries belong to Ohrmazd’s organized army against demonic disorder.

This is **[LS/LC]**, not all explicit in Yasht 6. The detailed text is available in the [Greater Bundahišn](https://www.avesta.org/mp/grb1.htm).

#### Dēnkard

The ninth-century *Dēnkard* preserves summaries and Zand theology rather than a separate Hvar myth. It treats cosmic light, daily worship, and the Avesta’s transmission within a systematic defense of Mazdayasnian revelation. Its commentarial comparison of the good believer’s eye to the benevolent eye of the Sun shows how solar surveillance became an ethical metaphor. **[LS]**

#### Mēnōg ī Xrad

This probably ninth-century wisdom dialogue states that solar and lunar settings make calendrical and seasonal calculation possible; it urges repentance before Sun and Mithra, Moon, and Ohrmazd’s fire. The Sun is thus witness, devotional presence, and regulator of time. **[LC/LS]**

#### Šāyest nē Šāyest

This post-Sasanian priestly manual says the morning Sun may be reverenced until midday, the midday Sun until afternoon, and the afternoon Sun until night; each reverence of Sun, Moon, or fire is meritorious. It gives practical discipline rather than myth. The relevant text is available in the [online Pahlavi edition](https://www.avesta.org/mp/shayest.html). **[LC/LS]**

#### Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag

In the medieval vision of Wīrāz’s journey, the stations of stars, Moon, and Sun form ascending regions of the afterlife below the highest paradise. This is a developed celestial hierarchy, not a story of Hvar acting as judge. **[MA/LS]**

### Achaemenid inscriptions: negative evidence

The Behistun inscription and Darius’s tomb inscriptions at Naqš-e Rostam name Ahuramazdā and speak generically of “other gods,” while inscriptions beginning under Artaxerxes II name Mithra and Anāhitā. They do **not** name Hvar Khshaeta. Consequently:

- Behistun is not an attestation of Hvar.
- The winged symbol above Darius cannot securely be labeled Hvar.
- Achaemenid imperial solar worship is instead known chiefly from Greek testimony.

This absence matters because modern illustrations often attach Hvar’s name to Achaemenid royal symbols without inscriptional support.

### Greek and Roman testimony

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, says Persians sacrificed to the Sun, Moon, earth, fire, water, and winds and performed sacrifice without Greek-style temples, altars, or cult statues (*Histories* 1.131–32). His account is early evidence for Persian solar cult, but he neither records the name Hvar nor proves every Persian practice was Zoroastrian. See the [Greek text and translation](https://lexundria.com/hdt/1.131/mcly). **[EA, external ethnography]**

Xenophon reports sacrifice of horses to the Sun and distinguishes Zeus/Ahuramazdā from Sun in descriptions of Persian royal ceremony. Curtius Rufus later has Darius III invoke Sun, Mithra, and Fire separately. These witnesses support the distinction between Hvar and Mithra in at least some Iranian traditions. **[EA/RS, external testimony]**

Strabo, however, says Persians call the Sun Mithra. This is the first explicit surviving Mithra–Sun equation, around the turn of the Common Era. It may describe western Iranian practice or Greek interpretive compression, not the vocabulary of the Avesta. **[SI/RS]**

Plutarch, around 100 CE, reports a learned Zoroastrian dualism in which Oromazes resembles light and Arimanius darkness, with Mithra between them. He further describes Oromazes placing himself at a cosmological distance measured from the Sun. The report contains genuine Iranian names but is philosophically recast; it is not a missing Hvar myth. The text is available in [*Isis and Osiris* 46–47](https://sacred-texts.com/cla/plu/pte/pte04.htm). **[RS/SI/learned Greek reception]**

### Surviving manuscripts and editions

The Avestan corpus was transmitted orally for centuries, written in the special Avestan script in late antiquity, and preserved in medieval and early-modern Iranian and Indian manuscripts. K7a, dated 1288 CE, is the oldest surviving Avestan codex, but no surviving manuscript approaches the date of composition of Yasht 6. The [Avestan Digital Archive](https://ada.geschkult.fu-berlin.de/ada/) documents and images the manuscript tradition.

Principal modern editions and translations include:

- Karl F. Geldner, *Avesta: The Sacred Books of the Parsis* (1886–96), still the standard full diplomatic basis for many online texts.
- James Darmesteter, *Le Zend-Avesta* and *Sacred Books of the East* translations (late nineteenth century), historically important but philologically dated.
- L. H. Mills, translation of the Yasna in *SBE* 31.
- Christian Bartholomae, *Altiranisches Wörterbuch* (1904), foundational lexicography.
- Ilya Gershevitch, *The Avestan Hymn to Mithra* (1959), especially relevant to the Mithra–Sun distinction and *xšaēta-*.
- Mary Boyce, *A History of Zoroastrianism*, especially volume I for inherited Iranian nature divinities.
- Jean Kellens, Helmut Humbach, Prods Oktor Skjærvø, Almut Hintze, and Alberto Cantera for modern textual, linguistic, and ritual analysis.
- Domenico Agostini and Samuel Thrope, *The Bundahišn: The Zoroastrian Book of Creation* (2020), a modern translation replacing reliance on West where possible.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

Hvar’s securely attested domains are:

- visible sunlight and warmth;
- the daily defeat of darkness;
- purification of earth and water;
- protection of the good creation from *daēvas*;
- distribution of *xᵛarənah*, prosperity, and vitality;
- calendrical and seasonal measurement;
- an ethically watchful presence as Ahura Mazdā’s eye;
- a celestial station in the journey of righteous souls.

Calling Hvar “god of fire” is inaccurate: Ātar is the distinct divine Fire. Calling Hvar simply “god of justice” improperly transfers Mithra’s covenantal and judicial functions. Calling Hvar a weather god, fertility god, or psychopomp is unsupported, though sunlight promotes the flourishing of creation and the solar station forms part of later eschatological geography.

### The Sun as Ahura Mazdā’s eye

Yasna 1.30, 2.30, 3.13, and 7.13 call the brilliant Sun *Ahurahe Mazdā dōiθra-*—“the eye of Ahura Mazdā.” Yasna 68.22 praises Ahura Mazdā’s two eyes, later interpreted as Sun and Moon. **[EA/LC]**

The metaphor makes the Sun the visible organ of divine surveillance and illumination. It does not establish a separate myth in which Ahura Mazdā physically loses, creates, or appoints an anatomical eye. Comparison with Vedic descriptions of the Sun as the eye of Mitra-Varuṇa is a valid Indo-Iranian parallel, but a common poetic inheritance is more securely established than direct textual borrowing.

### Powers and limitations

Hvar purifies and repels darkness; it does not independently create the cosmos. It operates within Ahura Mazdā’s creation and with the assistance or companionship of other *yazatas*. Yasht 6’s hypothetical non-rising demonstrates dependence of the material world on solar presence but does not narrate an actual eclipse, imprisonment, or temporary death of the Sun.

The Sun is not omnipotent. In later cosmology its movement begins because evil has entered the world; it is one combatant within the ordered host of creation. At final renovation, the conditions produced by mixture and motion will be transformed.

### Horses and movement

*Auruuat̰.aspa-* establishes swift horses as Hvar’s defining attribute. The inherited image plausibly implies a vehicle, and later Iranian art knows solar charioteer imagery, but the hymn supplies neither a named mount nor a canonical iconographic scene. Claims that Hvar rides a chariot drawn by four horses are Greek-comparative; claims of seven rainbow-colored horses come from Indian Sūrya traditions. **[MR/MI]**

### Celestial placement

The *Bundahišn* places the Sun above the Moon and below the throne of the Aməša Spəntas and Endless Light. Souls who reach the solar station have ascended beyond the lower star and lunar stations. This tiered universe is a **[LS]** developed from Avestan ideas of luminous heavenly regions.

### Iconography: what can and cannot be identified

No surviving Achaemenid, Parthian, or Sasanian image is securely identified by inscription as Hvar Khshaeta. That is the central iconographic finding.

- The human figure in the Achaemenid winged disk has been interpreted as Ahuramazdā, royal *xᵛarənah*, a generic protective deity, or a combined royal-divine symbol. [Iranica’s survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-visual-reps/) presents the dispute. Calling it Hvar is unproved. **[MR]**
- Radiate deities on Kushan coins are usually identified as Mithra/Miiro or other named gods from their legends, not Hvar.
- The radiate figure at Tāq-e Bostān associated with Šāpūr II is conventionally Mithra, not Hvar. [Iranica’s iconographic study](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithra-2-iconography-in-iran-and-central-asia/) stresses the evidence.
- Solar disks, rosettes, halos, and royal crowns prove solar symbolism, not necessarily a portrait of Hvar.
- Bahrām I’s radiate crown is royal solar symbolism; it is not a securely labeled image of Hvar.
- The later lion-and-sun emblem of Iran developed through astrological, dynastic, and Shiʿite contexts. It is not an ancient cult image of Hvar. **[MA/MI when retrojected]**

Therefore Hvar lacks a securely reconstructable human form, gendered body, weapon, distinctive dress, or canonical image. Avestan grammar and later convention treat the Sun as masculine, but the textual figure remains overwhelmingly elemental.

### Animals, plants, colors, days, and numbers

Securely attested:

- Horses: swift, but not numbered or described by color.
- Day: the eleventh day of every thirty-day Zoroastrian month, Xwaršēd.
- Time of devotion: the three daylight watches.
- Associated substances: *haoma*, milk, water/libations, and *barsom* in Yasht 6.6.
- Associated quality: radiance, purity, *xᵛarənah*.

Not securely established:

- A unique sacred flower assigned exclusively to Hvar.
- A canonical color such as gold or yellow.
- A sacred number beyond the calendrical fact of day eleven.
- Lion, eagle, rooster, lotus, or sunflower as Hvar’s personal animal or plant.
- Sunday as his ancient weekly day; the Avestan calendar is not organized by the later seven-day planetary week.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy and hierarchy

No source gives Hvar parents, spouse, sibling, offspring, or a birth story. **Absence of evidence is the finding.** In Younger Avestan religion Hvar belongs among the *yazatas* within Ahura Mazdā’s good creation. Later cosmology says Ohrmazd created or established the Sun, but that is a creator–creation relationship, not biological parentage.

Hvar is neither one of the six/seven Aməša Spəntas nor an adversarial demon. The adjective *aməša* in its liturgical title means “immortal”; it does not promote Hvar into the corporate class “the Aməša Spəntas.”

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Hvar’s dossier is unusual because it contains cosmological propositions and recurring sacred actions, not an epic cycle.

### The threatened world without sunrise

**Source:** Yasht 6.1–4, Younger Avestan. **[EA]**

The hymn imagines the daily rising of the Sun as a cosmic defense. When it shines and heats, innumerable *yazatas* assemble the glory that sustains Ahura Mazdā’s earth and the embodiments of *aša*. Sunrise purifies all land and waters. If it failed to rise, demons would destroy everything in the seven regions, and the spiritual gods would be unable to resist effectively in the embodied world.

The passage does not say that the Sun once failed to rise. It is a counterfactual explanation of why solar worship sustains creation. Modern retellings that turn it into a lost “night when Hvar was captured” are inventions. **[MI]**

Its likely function is devotional and cosmological: the worshipper participates in renewing the ordered world by honoring the luminary whose daily appearance makes demonic concealment impossible.

### The first noon and the beginning of celestial motion

**Source:** *Bundahišn* 2–3, post-Sasanian redaction preserving older material. **[LC/LS]**

Before evil invades material creation, stars, Moon, and Sun remain motionless and the world experiences perpetual noon. Ahriman’s assault brings the mixed state in which celestial bodies move. Sun and Moon become commanders of the mobile heavens; the Sun speeds like a vast arrow. Its daily journey is thus not merely natural repetition but part of the finite cosmic war between mixture and final separation.

This is not in Yasht 6 and should not be projected automatically into its earliest meaning. It is the fullest surviving Pahlavi solar myth.

### The solar station of the righteous

**Sources:** Avestan references to luminous heavenly regions; *Ardā Wīrāz-nāmag* and related Pahlavi eschatology. **[LC/MA]**

The righteous soul ascends successively through stations associated with stars, Moon, and Sun, then toward the highest lights or Paradise. The solar station is superior to star and lunar regions but subordinate to the supreme realm. Hvar does not personally weigh the soul; Mithra, Sraoša, Rašnu, the Činwad Bridge, and the soul’s own *dēn* carry those narrative functions.

### The Sun as the eye of Ahura Mazdā

**Sources:** Younger Avestan Yasna passages; later Zand interpretation. **[EA/LC]**

This is a poetic-theological complex rather than a plot. The Sun makes the world visible and makes moral life visible to divine order. Pahlavi commentators extend it into an ethical analogy: the good believer should look benevolently on creation as the Sun does.

### Mithra and Hvar: convergence without an Avestan fusion myth

**Sources:** Yashts 6 and 10; Strabo; later Pahlavi prayer practice. **[EA/SI/LC]**

The early sequence is:

1. Hvar is the visible Sun.
2. Mithra rises before it and exercises wide surveillance.
3. Both are repeatedly placed together in prayer.
4. Western observers begin calling the Iranian Sun “Mithra.”
5. Middle Persian usage allows *Mihr* to mean the Sun.
6. Roman Mithras becomes *Sol Invictus Mithras* while also appearing beside a separate Sol.

This is historical convergence, not a surviving myth in which Mithra transforms into Hvar. Roman Mithraic iconography cannot be used to fill gaps in Hvar’s biography. [Iranica’s Mithraism survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithraism/) emphasizes how contested the Iranian contribution to Roman Mithraism remains.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult centers and temples

No temple can securely be identified as a temple exclusively of Hvar Khshaeta. No dedicatory inscription naming Hvar marks a sanctuary, priesthood, treasury, or landed estate. This sharply distinguishes Hvar from Anāhitā or Mithra in some imperial contexts.

Herodotus says fifth-century BCE Persians sacrificed to the Sun in open, ritually pure places and rejected Greek-style temples, altars, and statues. That testimony fits a cult of natural presences but is external and generalized. It does not prove that every Iranian community lacked sanctuaries.

Later Zoroastrian fire temples are not “Sun temples.” Their consecrated focus is fire; solar prayer may be performed outdoors or facing the Sun. The absence of a Hvar temple is not absence of worship.

### Priesthood

There is no separate priesthood of Hvar. The inherited Zoroastrian priesthood performs high liturgies; laypeople may recite Yashts and Niyāyišns. Yashts can in principle be recited by priest or layperson, male or female. Hvar’s cult is embedded in the general Mazdayasnian system rather than supported by an independent institution.

### Daily prayer

The Xwaršēd Niyāyišn is prescribed for the three daylight watches:

- *hāwan gāh*—morning;
- *rapiθβin/rapihwin gāh*—midday or early afternoon;
- *uzīrin gāh*—later afternoon/evening before night.

The reciter faces visible light—the Sun by day, or fire where appropriate—while observing purity and wearing the sacred cord if initiated. The Sun Niyāyišn is conventionally followed by the Mihr Niyāyišn, a ritual pairing that materially encouraged their later conceptual convergence. The Niyāyišn’s composition and schedule are documented in the [Khordeh Avesta study](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khordeh-avesta-2/). **[LC, continuing practice]**

### Offerings

Yasht 6.6 names:

- haoma;
- milk;
- *barsom* twigs;
- libations;
- thought, word, deed;
- truthful utterances.

These belong to the broader Avestan sacrificial system. The hymn does not establish a unique solar altar, sacred meal, or animal sacrifice.

### Animal sacrifice and horses

Greek sources report Iranian horse sacrifice to the Sun, reasoning that the swiftest mortal animal was offered to the swiftest god. This fits Hvar’s Avestan epithet “swift-horsed,” but the accounts do not use Hvar’s name and may reflect royal or western regional practice. **[EA/RS, externally attested]**

Zoroastrian sources do not prescribe human sacrifice to Hvar. The dedicated contested-material search found no primary or reliable scholarly evidence for a Hvar cult practicing human sacrifice, ritual murder, temple prostitution, or cultic sexual service. Those claims should be marked **unattested**, not euphemized and not invented.

### Purity and sunlight

Sunlight is materially important in Zoroastrian purity law. Exposure to sun and air can purify specified objects after prescribed preliminary treatment. This is neither a license for casual disposal of pollution nor evidence of germ theory. It expresses the opposition of light, dryness, and ordered life to corpse pollution.

### Calendar and festival

Xwaršēd is the eleventh day of each thirty-day month. When day and month names coincide, Zoroastrian calendars ordinarily create a name-feast, but there is no month named Xwaršēd; therefore there is no ancient annual “Khorshedgan” produced by a day–month coincidence.

Modern websites sometimes list “Khorram Ruz,” a sacred flower, or an annual Hvar festival. These claims require independent documentation. They do not occur in Yasht 6 and should not be reported as ancient facts. **[MI/unverified]**

### Votive and personal-name evidence

No substantial corpus of ancient votive inscriptions to Hvar is known. New Persian Khorshid became an ordinary personal name, including for women, and occurs in compound names and titles across Persianate societies. That linguistic afterlife demonstrates the vitality of the word “sun,” but a medieval or modern person named Khorshid is not automatically evidence of devotion to the Avestan *yazata*.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### 1. Indo-Iranian inheritance, before approximately 1200–1000 BCE

A solar divine being, linguistic ancestor of Iranian Hvar and Indic Sūrya, belonged to common Indo-Iranian religious culture. Shared features include the divine Sun, solar sight, horses or rapid movement, and the eye metaphor. Reconstruction cannot securely import later Vedic genealogies, the seven-horse chariot, Aruṇa, Saṃjñā, or Sūrya’s marriages into Iran. **[MR]**

### 2. Old Avestan reformulation, approximately late second to early first millennium BCE

The Gāthās subordinate cosmic phenomena to Ahura Mazdā’s creative wisdom and *aša*. The Sun’s ordered path is evidence of the creator’s work. A developed independent Hvar personality is not visible. **[EA]**

### 3. Younger Avestan cult, broadly first millennium BCE

Hvar becomes explicitly worship-worthy as the immortal, splendid, swift-horsed Sun. Its daily rising purifies creation and holds off demons. Yasht 6 records a compact cult theology without genealogy or narrative biography. **[EA]**

### 4. Achaemenid-period Persian practice, sixth–fourth centuries BCE

Royal inscriptions do not name Hvar, but Greek witnesses report Persian sacrifice to the Sun and sometimes horses. Ahuramazdā dominates royal epigraphy; Mithra and Anāhitā enter named royal inscriptions only under Artaxerxes II. Hvar’s absence from the inscriptions cautions against identifying imperial symbols or buildings with it. **[EA, negative epigraphy plus external testimony]**

### 5. Hellenistic, Parthian, and western Iranian convergence, fourth century BCE–third century CE

Greek interpretatio translated the Iranian solar sphere through Helios/Apollo. Mithra’s surveillance, radiance, and dawn association encouraged equation with the Sun; Strabo supplies the first explicit surviving statement. Commagene and other western settings joined Mithra with Apollo and Helios. This is chiefly Mithra’s history, not evidence that Hvar acquired Mithra’s myths. **[SI/RS]**

### 6. Sasanian canon and cosmological system, 224–651 CE

Priestly redaction and written transmission organized the Avesta; daily prayer collections took form; Pahlavi cosmology placed Sun, Moon, stars, and Endless Lights in a structured universe. Hvar/Mihr terminology increasingly overlapped, yet Xwaršēd remained the ordinary and liturgical Sun. No independent Hvar temple establishment or anthropomorphic iconographic program is documented. **[LC/LS]**

### 7. Manichaean Iran and Central Asia, third–tenth centuries CE

Manichaeism assigned the Sun and Moon vital roles as ships transporting liberated Light. Iranian Manichaean terminology identified Mihr-yazd with major redeemer figures, especially the Third Messenger in Parthian contexts. [Iranica’s Manichaean cosmology](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cosmogony-iii/) describes the Sun and Moon vessels; [its study of Mithra in Manichaeism](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithra-in-manicheism-1/) traces the Iranian nomenclature.

This is genuine solar syncretism, but it does not establish that the Manichaean Sun God is Hvar Khshaeta unchanged. The cosmology, salvific function, and divine dramatis personae are Manichaean. **[SI/RS]**

### 8. Islamic conquest and post-conquest contraction, seventh century onward

The Arab conquest ended Sasanian political support for the Zoroastrian priesthood. Conversion, taxation, legal disability, migration, local violence, and loss or abandonment of institutions progressively reduced Zoroastrian communities. The cult of Hvar did not undergo a separately documented suppression campaign; it contracted with Zoroastrian communal life.

At the same time *xwaršēd/khorshid* remained the normal Persian word for Sun. Solar imagery flourished in Muslim Persian literature without necessarily preserving worship of Hvar.

### 9. Medieval Persian literature

Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*, completed around 1010 CE, opens its cosmological discussion with the creation of the Sun. The Sun is a brilliant moving jewel from which day receives light, traveling from east to west. The Persian text is preserved online at [Ganjoor](https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/aghaz/sh5). Ferdowsi’s creator is the sovereign God of an Islamic Persian epic; *khorshid* here is ordinarily the created Sun, not consistently a personal Zoroastrian god. **[MA, literary reception]**

Daqiqi’s incorporated account of Goštāsp contains memories of solar prayer and Zoroastrian custom, but the poem’s religious history is mediated through tenth-century Persian epic and Sasanian tradition. **[MA]**

In Persian lyric poetry, *khorshid* becomes an inexhaustible metaphor for:

- the beloved’s face;
- kingly majesty and generosity;
- prophetic or saintly radiance;
- truth and spiritual illumination;
- the manifest divine;
- overwhelming beauty before which stars vanish.

*Mihr* simultaneously means Mithra, love/affection, covenant, and Sun, enabling deliberate poetic ambiguity. These poetic uses are Hvar’s linguistic and symbolic afterlife, not proof of continued personal cult.

### 10. Parsi and Iranian Zoroastrian continuity, medieval period to present

Communities in Iran and India preserved the Khordeh Avesta and the daily Xwaršēd Niyāyišn. The tradition is therefore continuous at the level of prayer, even though manuscripts, pronunciations, calendars, and theological explanations developed.

Modern Zoroastrians continue to face the Sun or sacred fire during appropriate prayers. Hvar’s present “cult” consists primarily in liturgical recitation within Mazdayasnian worship, not temples or priesthoods devoted exclusively to a Sun god.

### 11. Modern diaspora and revival

Migration created communities in North America, Europe, Australia, and elsewhere. FEZANA’s 2012 survey, reproduced in [Iranica’s modern historical review](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroastrianism-02-arab-conquest-to-modern/), counted approximately 61,000 Zoroastrians in India, 15,000 in Iran, and substantial diasporas, while emphasizing demographic uncertainty. Prayer books, recordings, and online teaching have made the Xwaršēd Niyāyišn more accessible.

Neo-Zoroastrian and Iranian identity movements sometimes emphasize solar imagery, ancient kingship, and pre-Islamic heritage. Their ideology varies from devotional reconstruction to secular nationalism. No reliable global count exists for people specifically worshipping Hvar outside established Zoroastrian communities.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and comparative religion

Nineteenth-century translations made “Hvare-khshaeta, the radiant Sun” available to European comparative mythology. Early writers frequently treated every Indo-European Sun as a local form of one reconstructed solar god. Modern philology is more cautious: linguistic cognacy and inherited formulas are distinguishable from identical myths or cults.

### Occultism and esotericism

The dedicated search found no substantial Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or early modern grimoire tradition centered specifically on Hvar Khshaeta. Zoroaster, Mithras, Ormuzd, Ahriman, and “Persian fire” appear widely in Western esotericism, but Hvar seldom does.

Modern occult correspondence tables that assign Hvar:

- Sunday;
- gold;
- the number six or eleven;
- the lion;
- sunflower or heliotrope;
- the astrological Sun;
- Apollo, Ra, Šamaš, and Sūrya as exact equivalents

are modern comparative systems unless they independently cite historical Iranian evidence. **[MI]**

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Small online pagan and reconstructionist circles invoke Hvar as a personal solar god, often using Yasht 6 alongside modern hymns. This is a modern devotional reception. It may consciously draw on ancient evidence, but additions such as a fixed human appearance, a Greek chariot, seven horses, seasonal Sabbats, tarot correspondences, or a reconstructed standalone priesthood are new ritual creations. Their modernity does not invalidate them as present religious practice; it does invalidate claims that they are documented ancient customs.

### National and political symbolism

The lion-and-sun emblem, the winged disk commonly called *faravahar*, and radiate royal crowns are sometimes presented as emblems of Hvar. Historically:

- the Achaemenid winged figure’s identification is disputed;
- *faravahar* as the popular name and personal Zoroastrian identity badge is modern;
- the lion-and-sun emblem developed through medieval astrology and early-modern monarchy;
- neither image is securely Hvar’s ancient cult icon.

Solar symbolism is important to Iranian nationalism and diaspora identity, but equating every Iranian Sun with Hvar erases the intervening Islamic, astrological, dynastic, and modern histories. **[MA/MI]**

### Popular media

Hvar Khshaeta appears sporadically in mythology databases, role-playing games, fantasy art, and online “god profiles.” These commonly supply a gendered body, armor, sun disk, chariot, sacred animal, flower, festival, Greek equivalent, and family relations. Most such details are synthesized from Hvar, Mithra, Sūrya, Helios, and generalized solar-god conventions. No major film, novel, comic, or game has yet reshaped Hvar’s public image on the scale achieved for Mithras or Anāhitā.

### The name Khorshid

Khorshid remains a Persian word and personal name throughout Iran, Afghanistan, Central and South Asia, and diaspora communities. Its modern use may be secular, poetic, Muslim, Zoroastrian, or simply familial. It is the most extensive living afterlife of the ancient title, but not by itself a confession of Hvar worship.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Hvar a personified god or the natural Sun?

Older summaries often call Hvar a “sun god,” while some historians emphasize how little personification Yasht 6 contains. Both observations are compatible if carefully stated: grammatically and ritually Hvar is a *yazata* who is worshipped, acts, and receives offerings; phenomenologically Hvar remains the luminous Sun rather than a richly anthropomorphic personality.

The claim that there is “neither personification nor myth” captures the absence of biography but can understate the hymn’s active language: Hvar purifies, protects, and enables divine distribution of glory.

### Did Zarathustra reject Hvar and other old gods?

One influential twentieth-century model held that Zarathustra rejected the traditional pantheon and that later priests restored pre-Zoroastrian gods to form a syncretic “Zoroastrianism.” Boyce and others argued for more continuity: traditional beneficent divinities could be subordinated to Ahura Mazdā without being repudiated. The Gāthās’ limited divine nomenclature and the Younger Avesta’s abundant pantheon leave room for debate. Hvar’s case supports both restraint and continuity: the Gāthās recognize the divinely ordered Sun, while the Younger Avesta explicitly worships it.

### How old is Yasht 6?

The hymn is linguistically Younger Avestan, but that label covers centuries of oral production. Its elemental theology may preserve ancient Indo-Iranian inheritance; its present liturgical form contains later framing. No archaeological context or early manuscript gives a year. “Composed in 1200 BCE” and “written in the sixth century BCE” are unjustifiably exact.

### “Radiant” or “ruling”?

Traditional translation favors “radiant.” Gershevitch’s derivation from *xšay-* favors an original sense “mighty/ruling,” followed by semantic shift through solar usage. The adjective’s wide distribution among luminous and exalted beings prevents certainty. Both readings should appear in serious work.

### Hvar and Mithra

This is the largest historical confusion:

- Avestan texts distinguish them.
- Their prayers are paired.
- Both see widely and oppose darkness.
- Greek observers sometimes call the Sun Mithra.
- Middle Persian *mihr* can mean Sun.
- Roman Mithras is simultaneously identified with and distinguished from Sol.

Franz Cumont’s once-dominant claim that Roman Mithraism was essentially Romanized Iranian Mazdaism has been substantially revised. Modern scholarship accepts Iranian inheritance but disputes its extent and the transmission mechanisms. None of this licenses replacing Hvar with the bull-killing Roman Mithras.

### Hvar and the winged disk

Interpretations include Ahuramazdā, royal *xᵛarənah*, protective genius, dynastic symbol, or composite divine kingship. Direct identification with Hvar lacks an identifying inscription. The image’s Egyptian and Mesopotamian antecedents make a purely Iranian solar reading inadequate.

### Sun and *xᵛarənah*

Yasht 6 closely associates Hvar’s shining with the distribution of *xᵛarənah*. The two words sound similar, encouraging ancient or later association, but modern linguists generally do not treat *huuar-* “Sun” and *xᵛarənah-* “glory, fortune” as straightforwardly identical roots. Iconographic arguments that every ray represents *xᵛarənah* remain interpretive.

### State of the evidence

What survives:

- oral-liturgical Avestan material in medieval manuscripts;
- Pahlavi cosmology and commentaries in later copies;
- external Greek and Roman accounts;
- general Iranian solar imagery;
- continuing prayer practice.

What does not survive:

- an ancient manuscript of Yasht 6;
- an inscription dedicated to Hvar Khshaeta;
- a securely identified temple;
- a securely labeled image;
- an independent priesthood archive;
- a developed narrative cycle;
- a genealogy;
- an indigenous Achaemenid explanation of solar symbols.

The history must therefore be reconstructed principally from liturgy and comparative textual strata, not monumental archaeology.

### Discoveries and editions that changed interpretation

- Anquetil-Duperron’s eighteenth-century publication introduced the Avesta to European scholarship, though his translations were soon challenged.
- Avestan–Vedic comparison established Avestan as an ancient Iranian language rather than fraudulent “Zend.”
- Geldner’s late nineteenth-century collation created the enduring critical foundation.
- Bartholomae’s dictionary systematized the lexical evidence.
- Study of oral transmission and ritual manuscripts shifted attention away from reconstructing a lost book alone toward actual performances.
- The Avestan Digital Archive and Multimedia Yasna projects now allow direct manuscript and ritual comparison.

### Discredited or unsupported ideas still circulating

- Hvar was the supreme god of Persia. **Unsupported.**
- Mithra and Hvar were always the same being. **Contradicted by the Avesta.**
- The Achaemenid winged disk is certainly Hvar or certainly a *fravaši*. **Unproved.**
- Roman bull-slaying Mithras is an image or myth of Hvar. **False conflation.**
- Hvar had seven rainbow horses. **Borrowed from later Sūrya iconography.**
- Khorshed Yasht describes a solar chariot, weapon, spouse, or children. **It does not.**
- The lion-and-sun was Hvar’s ancient emblem. **Anachronistic.**
- Ancient Hvar worship involved human sacrifice or temple prostitution. **No supporting evidence found.**
- Hvar possesses a canonically attested sacred flower and annual festival called Khorram Ruz. **Unestablished.**
- “Khorshid” in every Persian poem is a covert survival of pagan worship. **Philologically and historically untenable.**
