# Yima / Jamshid: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the oldest Iranian evidence, Yima is a **mythic mortal, primordial shepherd-king, culture hero, and possessor of royal Fortune**, not unequivocally a god. Younger Avestan tradition places him between humanity and divinity; Middle Persian tradition makes him the paradigmatic first great king of the Pēšdādian age; Islamic Persian epic makes Jamshid a human world-king. Only in Kushan Bactria is he securely represented and named as a cultic deity, **Iamšo**.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **Yasna 32.8**, an Old Avestan Gāthic verse conventionally dated around the turn of the second to first millennium BCE, though preserved through oral transmission and only in medieval manuscripts. It names Yima as a notorious precedent for an obscure offense but supplies no biography. The oldest surviving Avestan manuscripts are medieval—K7a is conventionally dated 1268—so “early” below denotes the linguistic and compositional layer, not the physical age of the manuscript. [Avesta survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/), [Codices Hafnienses](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/codices-hafnienses/)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Old or Younger Avestan evidence whose linguistic form belongs to the ancient Iranian corpus.
- **[Later canonical]** authoritative Middle Persian Zoroastrian exegesis or systematic literature, mainly redacted in the ninth–tenth centuries CE but often preserving Sasanian or older materials.
- **[Regional variant]** a localized Bactrian, Sogdian, Manichaean, Persian, or oral development.
- **[Learned systematization]** priestly, chronographic, or genealogical ordering not stated in the early sources.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** an explicit equation with another traditional figure.
- **[Medieval accretion]** Islamic-period historical, epic, romance, or folklore material.
- **[Folklore]** orally transmitted or storyteller material not securely datable to antiquity.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** a scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention]** a demonstrably recent attribution or presentation as ancient.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

Yima begins in the surviving record not as a fully described golden-age ruler but as a famous and morally ambiguous ancient figure invoked in the Gāthās. The Younger Avesta makes him the son of Vīuuaŋᵛhant, extraordinarily endowed with *xᵛarənah* or Fortune, ruler of a deathless, disease-free age, enlarger of the inhabited earth, and builder of the *vara* that preserves chosen life from a killing winter. Yašt 19 adds his “deceiving word,” loss of Fortune, and eventual cutting. Middle Persian books turn these fragments into a history of the first millennium, an eschatological refuge, a fall through pride or refusal of religion, kinship with Jamag and Tahmōraf, and struggle with Ahriman and Dahāg. Islamic Persian tradition transforms him into Jamshid, inventor of civilization and Nowruz, builder or legendary owner of monumental ruins, proud claimant to divine honors, fugitive, and victim of Zahhak.

### Names

- **Avestan:** *Yima*, commonly written **Yima Xšaēta**. The name is cognate with Sanskrit **Yama**, यम, probably continuing Indo-Iranian \**Yama/Yima*, from a root associated with “twin” or “pair.” **[Early attested; etymology broadly accepted]**
- **Avestan patronymic:** *Yima Vīuuaŋᵛhantō*, “Yima, son of Vīuuaŋᵛhant.” Vīuuaŋᵛhant corresponds to Vedic Vivasvant, a solar figure; the names mean approximately “shining far and wide.” **[Early attested: Yasna 9; Vidēvdād 2]**
- **Middle Persian:** *Jam*, conventionally reconstructed from Pahlavi spellings; also *Jamšēd*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Classical and modern Persian:** **جم** (*Jam*) and **جمشید** (*Jamšīd/Jamshēd*). The longer form continues *Yima Xšaēta*, not an original compound meaning “Jam plus sun.” **[Historical linguistic development]**
- **Bactrian, Greek script:** **ΙΑΜϷΟ**, *Iamšo/Yamšo*, on Kushan coinage. **[Regional, securely material]**
- **Sogdian:** *Yam*, in Manichaean fragments. **[Regional late antique]**
- **Sanskrit cognate:** **यम, Yama**. Yama is not simply an Indian spelling of the Iranian being but a cognate figure with an independent history as first mortal, ancestral king, and lord of the dead. **[Comparative reconstruction]**
- **Nuristani:** *Imra/Im-rō*, traditionally compared with *Yama-rāja*. The historical relationship is plausible but does not make every Imra myth evidence for Iranian Yima. **[Regional/comparative]**

### Epithets and disputed meanings

- **xšaēta**: traditionally rendered “radiant,” “shining,” or “splendid.” It also qualifies the sun, heavenly lights, Tištrya, Apąm Napāt, Aši, and Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā. Yima is “sun-looking among mortals” in Yasna 9.4, supporting a luminosity interpretation. Andreas and Henning instead connected it with rulership; Kellens regarded the matter as unresolved. **[Early term; disputed etymology]**
- **srīra**, “fair/beautiful,” and **huuaŋᵛha**, “having good flocks,” underlie traditional renderings such as “fair Yima, the good shepherd.” **[Younger Avestan]**
- **huuarə.darəsō mašiiānąm**, “sun-like/sun-looking among mortals,” Yasna 9.4. **[Early attested]**
- **xᵛarənaŋᵛhastəma**, approximately “most possessed of Fortune among those born.” **[Early attested: Yasna 9.4]**
- Persian **šīd** was explained by medieval authors as “brightness” or “radiance,” sometimes producing folk etymologies such as “moon-brightness” in contrast with *ḵoršīd*, “sun.” This is learned medieval etymology, not proof that *Jamšīd* was originally formed in New Persian. **[Medieval learned explanation]**

### Classification through time

1. **Old Avestan:** famous ancestral mortal and sinner or ritual transgressor.
2. **Younger Avestan:** superhuman mortal ruler and sacrificer, not explicitly a *yazata*.
3. **Kushan Bactria:** deity on royal coinage; this is the clearest historical deification.
4. **Middle Persian Zoroastrianism:** first-age king, semi-divine culture hero, sinner, ruler of an intermediate afterlife realm in some texts, and eschatological preserver.
5. **Islamic Persian epic:** human shah of legendary antiquity.
6. **Modern cultural usage:** national epic hero and symbol of ideal kingship, civilization, splendor, hubris, Nowruz, and pre-Islamic Iran.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[Early attested] Yasna 32.8.** The verse belongs to the Ahunavaiti Gāthā in Old Avestan. Its approximate composition is usually placed around 1000 BCE, with proposed chronologies extending several centuries on either side. It says, in effect, that “Yima, son of Vīuuaŋᵛhant, was renowned for these offenses,” followed by a difficult clause later understood in Pahlavi exegesis as his making meat available for human consumption. The grammatical and contextual problems prevent certainty that animal slaughter or meat-eating was his actual Gāthic offense. The verse does **not** mention a golden age, vara, winter, kingship, pride, Zahhak, or Nowruz.

Because the Avesta was transmitted orally, no first-millennium-BCE manuscript or inscription bearing Yima’s name survives. The earliest evidence is therefore a reconstructably ancient composition preserved in late witnesses, not a contemporary physical document.

### Avestan corpus

- **Yasna 9.1–5, Hōm Yašt:** Vīuuaŋᵛhant is the first mortal to press haoma; his reward is Yima, during whose reign there was neither cold nor heat, age nor death, envy nor poverty. Yima is sun-like and possesses the greatest Fortune among mortals. **[Younger Avestan; ancient oral corpus]**
- **Yasna 32.8:** the single Gāthic allusion to his offense. **[Earliest attested]**
- **Vidēvdād 2:** Ahura Mazdā offers Yima custodianship and proclamation of the religion; Yima declines, saying he was not fitted or taught for it, but accepts responsibility to enlarge and protect the world. After three expansions he is instructed to build the *vara* against a catastrophic winter. **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Yašt 5.25–26:** Yima sacrifices to Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā on Mount Hukairya and seeks sovereignty over humans and *daēvas* and deprivation of the demons’ prosperity. **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Yašt 9.8–10, Drvāsp Yašt:** his golden reign is recalled; he sacrifices to Drvāspā for power over creatures and demons. **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Yašt 15.15–17, Vāyu Yašt:** Yima sacrifices on golden Hukairya and requests rule over peoples and demons. **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Yašt 17:** his Fortune and comparison with Zarathustra occur within the hymn to Aši. **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Yašt 19.30–34:** Yima’s Fortune leaves when he speaks a *draogəm vāčim*, “deceiving word.” **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Yašt 19.46:** Spitiiura is called *yimō.kərəṇtəm*, “Yima-cutter”; Aži Dahāka is present in the opposing emissary-list but the text does not say that Dahāka kills Yima. **[Younger Avestan]**
- **Yašt 13:** Yima’s *fravaši* is invoked, demonstrating inclusion in the commemorated community of the righteous or powerful dead without making him a god. **[Younger Avestan]**

### Dating and transmission

Old Avestan and Younger Avestan are linguistic strata, not securely dated books. A broad modern consensus places the Gāthās near 1000 BCE and the strongest Younger Avestan compositions in the early first millennium BCE, while allowing later oral redaction. The extant text descends through a Sasanian written archetype and medieval Iranian and Indian manuscript families. Vidēvdād 2 cannot responsibly be assigned an exact year. Older scholarship often called it post-Alexandrian or Median-Magian; Skjærvø argues its language does not compel a date later than the rest of the Younger Avesta. [Yašts transmission](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/), [Vidēvdād edition and translation](https://www.avesta.org/vendidad/vd_eng.pdf)

### Middle Persian corpus

Most surviving Pahlavi books were compiled or redacted in the ninth–tenth centuries CE from Sasanian exegetical traditions and older oral or written materials.

- **Bundahišn:** Jam’s genealogy; Jam and Jamag; the location and eschatological function of Jam’s *war*; his reign length; his cutting by Spitūr and Dahāg; the fires associated with his reign. **[Later canonical/systematic]**
- **Dēnkard Books 3, 5, 7, 9:** Jam’s *paymān* or right measure; his recovery of wisdom from demons; the missed union of royal and religious Fortune; genealogy connecting Zarathustra to Jam; exegesis of the Gāthic offense; consequences of Jam’s cutting. **[Later canonical exegesis]**
- **Mēnōy ī xrad 26, 61:** a catalogue of Jam’s benefits and the underground location of the *war*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Dādestān ī dēnīg 36, 38:** the eschatological *war*, religious institutions attributed to Jam, and explanations of his fault. **[Later canonical]**
- **Pahlavi Rivāyat:** Jam and Jamag, demonic unions, incestuous restoration of the human lineage, Jam’s fall, hell, repentance, and eschatological traditions. **[Later canonical/learned]**
- **Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg:** reign chronology, exile with Jamī, the thousand-edged saw, and apocalyptic material. **[Late antique/medieval Zoroastrian]**
- **Māh ī Frawardīn Rōz ī Hordād:** associates Great Nowruz, ossuaries, and recovery of right measure with Jam. **[Later priestly tradition]**
- **Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag:** not a major Yima narrative source. Its importance is comparative, as a Middle Persian map of the afterlife. Claims that it makes Jam lord of the dead should not be imported from Indian Yama without a passage. **[Absence finding]**

### Manichaean corpus

Two Sogdian fragments of the **Manichaean Book of Giants**, from Central Asia and probably copied in the first millennium CE, call Yam “lord of the world.” One gives him five diadems; another says that the world’s rulers assembled before him on New Year’s Day. This is valuable independent evidence that the Yam–Nowruz association was circulating outside surviving Zoroastrian Pahlavi literature. It does not establish a Manichaean cult of Yam. **[Regional syncretistic reception]**

### Islamic and New Persian corpus

- **Ṭabari, History**, early tenth century: Jamshid’s inventions, rule over demons, pride, deception by Eblis, fall, and violent death. **[Medieval chronography]**
- **Balʿami’s Persian Ṭabari**, tenth century: expands Eblis’s temptation, Jamshid’s demand for worship, and punishment of dissenters. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Biruni, Āṯār al-bāqiya**, ca. 1000: calendar traditions linking Jamshid with Nowruz and Great Nowruz. **[Medieval antiquarian testimony]**
- **Ferdowsi, Šāhnāma**, completed ca. 1010: the dominant epic biography—civilization, classes, jeweled throne, Nowruz, hubris, loss of *farr*, hundred-year flight, and being sawn in two by Zahhak. **[Medieval canonical epic]**
- **Ṯaʿālebi, Ḡorar**, eleventh century: parallel prose royal legend and alternative death by wild beasts. **[Medieval variant]**
- **Asadi Ṭusi, Garšāsp-nāma**, eleventh century: Jamshid’s combat, flight through Zabulistan and India, marriage, descendants, and death in China. **[Medieval regional-epic variant]**
- **Ebn Balḵi, Fārs-nāma**, early twelfth century: Jamshid, Estakhr, Persepolis, inventions, and death traditions. **[Medieval regional historiography]**
- **Mojmal al-tawāriḵ**, twelfth century: genealogies and alternative death traditions, including burning at Babylon. **[Medieval learned systematization]**
- **Neẓāmi, ʿAṭṭār, Hafez, and later lyric poets:** develop the world-seeing **Cup of Jam**, although Ferdowsi assigns the world-revealing cup to Kay Khosrow, not Jamshid. **[Medieval literary accretion]**

### Classical Greco-Roman and imperial Persian evidence

Neither the Achaemenid royal inscriptions at Behistun and Naqš-e Rostam nor securely identified Achaemenid imagery names Yima. Darius’s opposition of truth (*hašiya*) and the Lie may be culturally comparable with Yima’s deceptive word, but a direct allusion is unprovable. Herodotus does not securely name Yima. Boyce proposed that Herodotus 7.114—where Persian victims are buried alive for an underworld god—might concern Yima, but the deity is unnamed, the rite does not correspond to an attested Yima cult, and Skjærvø considers an association with the Evil Spirit at least as plausible. Plutarch’s account of Persian dualism likewise supplies no secure Jam/Yima attestation. **[Absence finding; disputed modern identification]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Younger Avestan]** Yima’s primary domain is prosperous earthly sovereignty: population, livestock, fire, cultivated space, bodily health, freedom from destructive extremes, and the preservation of living creation. He is not initially lord of the dead in the Iranian sources.

**[Later canonical]** His *vara* becomes an eschatological reserve from which humans, animals, and plants will repopulate the world after Malkūs’s winter. He also becomes guardian or restorer of *paymān*, the right measure between excess and deficiency.

**[Medieval epic]** Jamshid governs civilization itself: metallurgy, armor, textiles, professional specialization, four social estates, brick-building, ships, mining, gems, perfume, medicine, and royal ceremony. Various historians add writing, glass, astronomy, sugar, wine, roads, mills, keys, elephants, and cities. These are cumulative medieval attributions, not all ancient features.

### Powers and limitations

- Keeps age, sickness, hunger, death, heat, and cold from his realm. **[Yasna 9; Vidēvdād 2]**
- Expands the earth three times by one-third. **[Vidēvdād 2]**
- Builds with clay and encloses ideal specimens in the *vara*. **[Vidēvdād 2]**
- Rules humans, animals, and demons and strips demons of prosperity. **[Yašts 5, 9, 15, 19]**
- Possesses exceptional *xᵛarənah*, but can lose it through falsehood. **[Yasna 9; Yašt 19]**
- Is not invulnerable in the earliest sources. Later folklore makes him repeatedly regenerate while being sawn inside a tree. **[Medieval/folklore]**
- His glory and perfect reign do not make him omnipotent: he cannot expand the earth indefinitely and requires Ahura Mazdā’s warning and instructions. **[Vidēvdād 2]**

### Regalia and symbols

- **Golden goad and suβrā:** Ahura Mazdā’s tools for Yima. The second object has been rendered dagger, pick, ring, seal, horn, or shepherd’s instrument. “Golden ring and dagger” reflects Pahlavi interpretation and older translations, not an uncontested lexical result. **[Early attested; philologically disputed]**
- **Jeweled aerial throne:** demons lift Jamshid’s throne, producing his sun-like Nowruz epiphany. **[Šāhnāma; medieval epic]**
- **Diadems/crowns:** five diadems in the Manichaean Book of Giants; royal crown throughout Persian art. **[Regional late antique onward]**
- **The Vārəγna bird:** form in which Fortune leaves him. It is not necessarily his familiar or permanent emblem. **[Yašt 19]**
- **Fire:** red-burning fires prosper in his realm; later authors make him founder of fire veneration. A dedicated Jamshid fire cult is not independently attested. **[Early motif; medieval elaboration]**
- **Cup of Jam:** the omniscient cup is a post-Ferdowsian transfer from Kay Khosrow, elaborated by Neẓāmi, ʿAṭṭār, Hafez, and later poets. Presenting it as securely Avestan or as Jamshid’s object in Ferdowsi is a modern error. [Cup of Jam](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jam-e-jam/)

No ancient source assigns Yima a canonical color, weekday, planet, sacred number, or exclusive animal. Gold, solar light, birds, cattle, and fire are recurrent associations, not a formal attribute-system.

### Surviving images

1. **Iamšo coin of Kushan king Huviška, second century CE.** The reverse legend names Iamšo; the standing figure carries a bird. This is the earliest secure named image and the clearest evidence that Yima was treated as a deity in Bactria. Identification of the bird specifically as the Fortune-bearing Vārəγna is plausible but interpretive. [Kushan study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1649632114C7D43297151F6CE8260E07/9788323394037c5_p107-114_CBO.pdf/king_huviska_yima_and_the_bird_observations_on_a_paradisiacal_state.pdf)
2. **Shahnameh manuscript painting, Shiraz, ca. 1435–40**, Fitzwilliam Museum MS 22-1948, fol. 11v: Jamshid enthroned with supporting demons. The throne is shown on land even though the textual episode places it in the air. **[Medieval visual interpretation]** [Fitzwilliam record](https://shahnameh.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/explore/objects/no-40-jamshids-throne-borne-by-divs)
3. **“Court of Jamshid,” Shah Tahmasp Shahnameh, Tabriz, 1520s:** Jamshid in Safavid dress presides over craftsmen, with demons marking his supernatural sovereignty. This is not a reconstruction of ancient Iranian clothing or ritual. **[Safavid reception]** [Smithsonian record](https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/shahnama/els2010-7-1/)
4. Numerous later manuscript cycles depict the throne, court, fall, flight, or killing. They follow the Šāhnāma and workshop conventions; they are not evidence for pre-Islamic iconography.

Achaemenid kings at Persepolis are not historical portraits of Jamshid. Their later identification as Jamshid reflects medieval loss of the ability to read cuneiform and legendary reinterpretation of the ruins.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogical variants

- **Father:** Vīuuaŋᵛhant in Yasna 9 and Vidēvdād 2. This is the only securely early parentage. **[Early attested]**
- **Mother:** no Iranian Avestan source names her. Importing Vedic Saraṇyū into Yima’s genealogy is comparative reconstruction, not Iranian attestation.
- **Tahmōraf/Tahmuras:** brother in Bundahišn 35.3 and priestly traditions; father and predecessor in Ferdowsi. The father-version became dominant through the Šāhnāma. **[Later canonical versus medieval epic]**
- **Brothers:** Bundahišn lists Jam, Tahmōraf, Spitūr, and Narseh as brothers. **[Later systematization]**
- **Twin sister:** Jamī/Jamag first appears explicitly in Pahlavi literature. They hide together; in one account they restore a proper close-kin union after demonic partners have produced harmful animals. **[Later canonical; absent from Avesta]**
- **Other “sisters”:** Šahrnāz and Arnavāz are Jamshid’s sisters in the Šāhnāma, taken by Zahhak and rescued by Fereydun. Their Avestan prototypes Saŋhauuācī and Arənauuācī are desired by Θraētaona, but the Avesta never calls them Yima’s sisters. **[Medieval genealogical reassignment]**
- **Children:** Pahlavi tradition gives Jam and Jamag the twins Āspīgān and *Zrēšom. The Garšāsp-nāma gives him Tur by a princess of Zabulistan; later texts make him ancestor of Garshasp and Rostam. Other chronicles supply Batuʾāl and Homāyun. **[Conflicting late traditions]**
- **Wife/consort:** no Avestan wife. Jamag, a female demon, and several human princesses appear in different later traditions. No single canonical consort exists.

### Every documented divine-being interaction

- **Ahura Mazdā/Ohrmazd —** offers Yima the religion, commissions him to protect and enlarge the world, gives him golden implements, warns of the winter, and dictates the *vara*. **Source:** Vidēvdād 2, Younger Avestan. **[Early attested]**
- **Haoma/Hōm —** tells Zarathustra that Vīuuaŋᵛhant’s pressing of Haoma earned the birth of Yima; the interaction is genealogical and sacrificial rather than a narrated meeting with Yima. **Source:** Yasna 9.1–5. **[Early attested]**
- **Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā —** receives Yima’s sacrifice on Hukairya and grants or is asked to grant sovereignty over mortals and demons. **Source:** Yašt 5.25–26. **[Early attested]**
- **Drvāspā —** receives his sacrifice and petition for dominion. **Source:** Yašt 9.8–10. **[Early attested]**
- **Vāyu —** receives his sacrifice on golden Hukairya and petition for rule. **Source:** Yašt 15.15–17. **[Early attested]**
- **Aši —** Yima’s Fortune is recalled within Aši’s hymn; no extended personal encounter survives. **Source:** Yašt 17. **[Early attested; joint textual appearance]**
- **Xᵛarənah/Fortune —** dwells with Yima and departs in bird form after his deceptive word. Fortune is a divine potency and, in hymnic contexts, an object of veneration. **Source:** Yašt 19.30–34. **[Early attested]**
- **Miθra/Mihr —** receives one departure or portion of Yima’s Fortune. **Source:** Yašt 19.35; later Persian division of Jam’s light. **[Early attested, later elaborated]**
- **Spənta Mainyu —** sends Good Thought, Best Order, and Fire in the contest for unseizable Fortune; Yima is background to the Fortune-cycle rather than an interlocutor. **Source:** Yašt 19.46. **[Early attested, indirect]**
- **Aŋra Mainyu/Ahriman —** may be the unnamed tempter behind Yima’s lie; later explicitly deceives Jam, swallows Tahmōraf, contests Jam’s rule, searches the *war*, and causes his pride. **Sources:** Yašt 19.46; Pahlavi Rivāyat; Persian Rivāyats. **[Early possible; later explicit]**
- **Vohu Manah/Good Thought —** appears as an emissary of the Beneficent Spirit in the Fortune contest, not in a separate meeting. **Source:** Yašt 19.46. **[Early attested, indirect]**
- **Aša Vahišta/Best Order —** likewise an emissary in the Fortune contest. **Source:** Yašt 19.46. **[Early attested, indirect]**
- **Ātar/Fire —** “son of Ahura Mazdā,” sent as an emissary in the contest; later Farrōbay fire saves or protects Jam from Dahāg in a difficult Bundahišn passage. **Sources:** Yašt 19.46; Bundahišn 18.9–10. **[Early attested; later canonical variant]**
- **Akō Manah/Bad Thought —** emissary of the Evil Spirit in the contest. **Source:** Yašt 19.46. **[Early attested, indirect]**
- **Aēšma/Wrath —** emissary of the Evil Spirit. **Source:** Yašt 19.46. **[Early attested, indirect]**
- **Aži Dahāka/Dahāg/Zahhak —** appears with Spitiiura among evil emissaries, but the Avesta does not say he kills Yima. Bundahišn makes Dahāg collaborate in cutting Jam; Ferdowsi makes Zahhak hunt and saw him in two. **Sources:** Yašt 19.46; Bundahišn 35.5; Šāhnāma. **[Early association; later canonical and epic combat]**
- **Spitiiura/Spitūr —** explicitly “Yima-cutter” in the Avesta; later Jam’s brother and collaborator with Dahāg. **Sources:** Yašt 19.46; Bundahišn 35.3–5. **[Early attested; later genealogical elaboration]**
- **Sraoša/Srōš —** instructs Jamshid how to recover Tahmōraf from Ahriman, using song and an offer of sodomy to make the demon turn around. **Source:** Persian Rivāyat tradition. **[Late Zoroastrian accretion]**
- **Daēvas/dēws collectively —** are deprived of prosperity under Yima; later build, carry his throne, teach crafts, seduce humanity, marry Jam and Jamag, and kill or cut Jam. **Sources:** Yašts; Dēnkard; Bundahišn; Šāhnāma. **[Early collective opposition; later servants and killers]**
- **Karšipta —** divine or marvelous bird that brings Ahura Mazdā’s religion into the *vara*. The text does not describe a direct conversation with Yima. **Source:** Vidēvdād 2.42. **[Early attested]**
- **Eblis/Satan —** persuades Jamshid that he is God or creator and, in variants, reveals his hiding place to Zahhak. **Sources:** Ṭabari, Balʿami, Persian Rivāyats. **[Islamic syncretistic identification/medieval accretion]**
- **Solomon —** Jamshid is equated or contrasted with Solomon because both command demons and possess marvelous kingship. This is an identification, not a narrated meeting. **Sources:** Ebn Qotayba, Maqdesi, Eṣṭaḵri, Ebn Ḥawqal, medieval geographical literature. **[Medieval syncretism]**

There is no documented personal encounter between Yima and Vedic Yama, Manu, Noah, Prometheus, Ymir, or Mithras of the Roman mysteries. Such pairings belong to comparative scholarship.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The golden age and expansion of the earth

**[Younger Avestan core]** Yasna 9 remembers Yima’s reign as free of cold, heat, old age, death, envy, and scarcity. Vidēvdād 2 dramatizes the reign. Yima refuses to become propagator of Ahura Mazdā’s religion but accepts the care of creation. Three times—after 300, 600, and 900 winters—the earth becomes overcrowded. Facing south along the sun’s path, he presses it with the golden implements and makes it expand by one-third.

The text’s population crisis is not moral punishment. It results from successful immortality and fertility. The older translation “bad corporeal world” encouraged comparisons with sinful humanity before Noah’s Flood, but the Avestan grammar is corrupt or poorly transmitted; “bad winters will come upon the corporeal world” better fits Avestan usage.

**[Later systematization]** Pahlavi cosmology assigns Yima’s perfect reign to the first millennium and makes *paymān*, right measure, its sustaining principle. Reign lengths vary: 616 years and six months in Bundahišn; 616 years, six months, and thirteen or sixteen days in Aogəmadaēcā; 717 years and seven months in Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg; 700 years in Ferdowsi.

### The vara and the destructive winter

At an assembly in Airyanəm Vaējah, Ahura Mazdā warns Yima that deadly winters will bring deep snow and subsequent floods. Yima must construct a *vara*, an enclosure the size of a riding-ground, with residential divisions, water, self-luminous windows or lights, and inexhaustible food. He admits “seeds”—reproductive specimens—of the finest humans, cattle, dogs, birds, plants, fruits, and red-burning fires. Diseased, malformed, sterile, deceitful, or otherwise defective persons are excluded. The discriminatory catalogue belongs to the ancient text and should not be softened into a modern universal rescue story.

Inside, a human pair produces two children every forty years. A year seems like a day; created and uncreated lights illuminate the enclosure. Karšipta brings the religion there, while Urvatat-nara and Zarathustra are named its temporal and spiritual authorities. The text does not explicitly say that Yima himself lives permanently inside it.

**[Later canonical]** The *war ī Jamkerd* is underground in Ērān-wēz or under Mount Čamagān in Fārs. At the end of Ušēdar’s millennium, Malkūs’s winter or rain will devastate life; inhabitants of the *war* will emerge to restore creation. This eschatological role is not explicit in Vidēvdād 2.

**[Scholarly dispute]** The *vara* has been understood as walled enclosure, cavern, subterranean paradise, celestial vault, ritual structure, or ideal microcosm. Comparison with Noah, Utnapishtim, and Manu is legitimate, but direct borrowing is unproved. The Iranian disaster is winter followed by snowmelt, not primarily divine flood-punishment; Yima preserves selected life but does not sail; the inhabitants are a continuing reserve rather than merely survivors.

### The lie and loss of Fortune

**[Earliest allusion]** Yasna 32.8 condemns Yima for an obscure precedent. Pahlavi exegesis makes the offense the introduction of meat-eating; Molé interpreted it as a ritual fault. Neither conclusion is secure.

**[Younger Avestan narrative]** Yašt 19 says that when Yima uttered a “deceiving word,” Fortune left him in the form of a Vārəγna bird. It passes to Miθra, Θraētaona, and Kərəsāspa. Whether Fortune leaves three times, in three portions, or with a rhetorically repeated finality is disputed. The actual proposition Yima lied about is lost or incomprehensible.

**[Later canonical]** Jam refuses Ohrmazd’s religion, obeys Ahriman, or proclaims himself creator. His royal Fortune consequently departs; he becomes mortal, loses the throne, and is condemned to hell before repentance gains him rule over *hamēstagān*, the intermediate place for evenly balanced souls.

**[Medieval epic]** Ferdowsi’s Jamshid gathers priests, nobles, and warriors and declares that every good thing proceeds from him. The divine *farr* visibly abandons him, rebellion follows, and the Iranians invite Zahhak to replace him. Ferdowsi turns the philologically obscure lie into a coherent political theology: legitimate civilization collapses when the ruler claims divine authorship of it.

### The cutting and death

**[Early attested fragment]** Yašt 19.46 calls Spitiiura “the Yima-cutter.” It does not describe how, why, or whether “cutting” means bisection, mutilation, or killing.

**[Later canonical]** Bundahišn makes Spitūr and Dahāg collaborate in cutting Jam apart. Another passage says demons cut him; Dēnkard 9.21 may instead preserve a castration story in which Dahāg’s mother damages Jam’s genitals and prevents descendants.

**[Šāhnāma]** After losing the throne, Jamshid hides for one hundred years. Zahhak finds him by the sea of Chin and saws him in two. This is the dominant Persian version.

**[Variants]**

- He hides inside a tree; Zahhak saws through tree and man. Jamshid twice regenerates overnight before the third attempt kills him. **[Persian Rivāyat/folklore]**
- Bivarasp tears out and eats his intestines before sawing him. **[Ṭabari]**
- Dahāg gives him to wild beasts. **[Ṯaʿālebi]**
- He is burned in Babylon. **[Mojmal al-tawāriḵ]**
- A thousand-edged or fishbone-like saw is used. **[Ayādgār ī Jāmāspīg and medieval chronicles]**

The often-repeated theory that Yima was a primordial man whose dismemberment created the cosmos is **not stated in any Yima text**. It is Bruce Lincoln’s comparative reconstruction from Puruṣa, Ymir, and first-man patterns.

### Jam, Jamag, demons, and renewed lineage

In the Pahlavi Rivāyat, Jam and twin sister Jamag hide in the *war*. A male demon and female *parīg* deceive them into cross-marriages, producing monkeys, bears, and other harmful creatures. During a drinking episode Jamag exchanges clothing with the female demon and sleeps with Jam, effecting *xwēdōdah*, close-kin marriage; its ritual merit drives the demons back to hell. Bundahišn gives the pair human twins who continue the lineage.

This is a late priestly myth, though its pairing of Jam/Jamag with Vedic Yama/Yamī suggests inherited Indo-Iranian material. The Rigvedic Yamī tries to persuade Yama to unite with her; he refuses. The Iranian consummation is therefore parallel but not identical.

### Rescue of Tahmōraf and discovery of gōmēz

Ahriman, previously ridden as a horse by Tahmōraf, swallows him. Srōš tells Jamshid that Ahriman especially loves song and being sodomized. Jamshid offers both; when Ahriman turns, Jamshid reaches into him and retrieves his brother’s corpse. His hand contracts leprosy and a foul dryness. A cow or bull urinates on it and cures him, revealing the purifying power of *gōmēz*, consecrated bovine urine.

This is **late Zoroastrian narrative**, preserved in Persian Rivāyats and related manuscripts. It should neither be projected into the Avesta nor omitted because of its sexual and bodily content.

### Nowruz

Ferdowsi says demons raise Jamshid’s jeweled throne; the world beholds him shining like the sun, scatters jewels, and calls the day **Nowruz**, “New Day.” Pahlavi and Persian Rivāyat traditions variously place the event on Ohrmazd day, 1 Farvardin, or Hordad day, 6 Farvardin, the Great Nowruz. Biruni preserves several explanations, including Jamshid’s restoration of blessing and sun-like reappearance. The Manichaean Yam fragment shows rulers assembling before him at New Year.

The association is therefore older than Ferdowsi, but no Achaemenid inscription says that Jamshid founded Nowruz. Nor is it certain that the Persepolis tribute reliefs portray a Nowruz ceremony. Calling Jamshid the historical inventor of the festival is traditional legend, not recoverable institutional history. [Nowruz study](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/nowruz-i/)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Direct cult evidence

The evidence for an Iranian cult dedicated specifically to Yima is extremely thin.

- No securely identified Achaemenid, Parthian, or Sasanian temple is dedicated to Yima.
- No Achaemenid royal inscription names him.
- No priesthood, festival office, sacrificial tariff, oracle, pilgrimage center, or temple economy devoted to him is documented.
- Avestan invocations of his *fravaši* establish ritual commemoration, not necessarily an independent cult.
- The fully material exception is Kushan Bactria: Huviška’s coinage names and depicts **Iamšo** among divine reverse types. Bactrian personal names such as *Iamšo-lado*, “given by Iamšo,” provide theophoric evidence. **[Regional cult/deification]**

Zaehner’s statement that non-Zoroastrians worshipped Yima as a god is a historical reconstruction, supported indirectly by the later Kushan evidence but not demonstrated for Zarathustra’s own milieu.

### Sacrifice and prayer

The Yašts depict Yima sacrificing, usually a conventional hundred stallions, thousand cattle, and ten thousand sheep, to Anāhitā, Drvāspā, and Vāyu. Such formulaic heroic sacrifices demonstrate Yima as worshipper, not recipient. Whether these quantities describe performed ritual, poetic magnificence, or ideal royal offering cannot be decided.

### Human sacrifice

Herodotus 7.114 reports that Persians buried victims alive for an unnamed subterranean deity. Boyce tentatively connected that deity with Yima because of Yama’s underworld role. The identification is unsupported by a name, sanctuary, or Iranian parallel and should not be reported as a documented Yima human-sacrifice cult. No securely identified sacrifice of humans to Yima survives.

### Religious institutions attributed to Jam

Late texts credit him with:

- the *kusti* or sacred girdle;
- *hamāzōr*, ritual solidarity;
- seasonal *gāhānbār* festivals;
- ossuaries and *daxmas*;
- fire veneration;
- four religious-social estates.

These are etiological attributions by later communities seeking primordial authorization. They do not date the institutions to a historical king Jamshid.

### Persepolis and Takht-e Jamshid

Persepolis was the Achaemenid city of Pārsa, principally built by Darius I and successors beginning around 518 BCE. Its later Persian name **Taḵt-e Jamšīd**, “Throne of Jamshid,” records medieval attribution, not Jamshid’s cult or construction. Ebn al-Balḵi saw Achaemenid figures as Jamshid and his court; other Muslim geographers called the site Solomon’s mosque or playground, reflecting the Jamshid–Solomon equation. Mirḵᵛānd later said Jamshid built the palace and instituted Nowruz there. The monuments themselves preserve no Jamshid inscription or identifiable cult space. [Persepolis](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/persepolis/)

## 7. Historical evolution

### ca. 1200–900 BCE: Old Avestan horizon

Yima is already sufficiently famous that Yasna 32.8 can allude to his offense without explanation. This indicates a pre-existing oral myth but does not reveal its contents. **[Secure textual inference]**

### Early first millennium BCE: Younger Avestan development

Yima becomes the ideal ruler of an undying age, son of the first Haoma presser, sacrificer to major deities, possessor and loser of Fortune, enlarger of the earth, and builder of the *vara*. Spitiiura cuts him. These themes may come from several poetic traditions rather than one original “life.”

### Achaemenid period, 550–330 BCE

The ideological vocabulary of truthful kingship, the Lie, divine favor, ordered peoples, royal splendor, and paradisal court landscapes is comparable to Yima’s mythic field. Nevertheless, Yima is absent from the surviving royal inscriptions, and no Achaemenid image is securely his. Claims that Darius modeled himself explicitly on Yima remain conjectural.

### Arsacid and Kushan centuries

The Iranian heroic sequence is increasingly organized into dynasties. In second-century Kushan Bactria, **Iamšo becomes an explicitly represented god** on Huviška’s coins, holding a bird; theophoric names show broader recognition. This is the peak of material cult evidence.

### Sasanian period, 224–651 CE

Priestly compilation integrates Jam into a universal Zoroastrian history: his reign belongs to a cosmic millennium; the *war* acquires an end-time function; *paymān* explains immortality; his refusal of religion and pride explain failure; genealogies connect him with Jamag, Tahmōraf, and Zarathustra. Sasanian-era written Avesta and *zand* traditions lie behind surviving Pahlavi books, although their extant manuscripts and redactions are later.

### Manichaean Central Asia, third–tenth centuries

Yam enters Manichaean heroic history as lord of the world and New Year monarch. This is adaptation, not straightforward preservation of an original Zoroastrian canon.

### Islamic conquest and early Islamic Iran

There is no documented campaign aimed specifically at suppressing a cult of Yima; there was apparently no major cult institution to suppress. The broader decline of Zoroastrian institutions after the seventh-century conquest changed the channels of transmission. Jam survived vigorously in Persian historiography, royal legend, calendar lore, place-names, and epic.

### Tenth–twelfth centuries: epic consolidation

Ṭabari, Balʿami, Biruni, Ferdowsi, Ṯaʿālebi, Asadi Ṭusi, and Ebn Balḵi produce the durable Jamshid. Ferdowsi’s version becomes dominant: civilizer, founder of Nowruz, over-proud ruler, loser of *farr*, and victim of Zahhak. Alternative Zoroastrian accounts of hell, the *war*, and Jamag remain less culturally visible.

### Later medieval and early modern Persianate world

Jamshid becomes a poetic yardstick for royal magnificence and impermanence. His cup symbolizes visionary or mystical knowledge. Rulers are praised as “second Jamshid”; ruins and monumental works are assigned to him; storytellers expand his adventures. Solomon syncretism reaches particular strength in Fars because both command demons and are associated with monumental ruins.

### Nineteenth–twenty-first centuries

Philological recovery of Avestan and Pahlavi sources separates Yima from Ferdowsi’s composite Jamshid. Simultaneously, modern Iranian nationalism elevates Ferdowsi, Persepolis, Nowruz, and pre-Islamic kingship. Under the Pahlavi monarchy, **Takht-e Jamshid** became a powerful state symbol; after 1979, some street names and official symbols associated with monarchic pre-Islamic nationalism were changed, while Nowruz and Shahnameh culture continued.

Traditional Zoroastrians continue to transmit and recite the Avestan and Pahlavi texts in which Yima appears, but there is no well-documented, continuous, separate modern Yima priesthood or temple cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship, translation, and comparative mythology

Anquetil-Duperron’s eighteenth-century publication of the Avesta introduced Yima to European scholarship. Darmesteter’s translations, Geldner’s critical Avesta, Christensen’s collection of Yima material, Boyce’s history, and later work by Kellens, Shaked, Hintze, Malandra, Humbach, Ichaporia, and Skjærvø successively complicated the Noah-like “Persian Flood hero” picture.

Popular summaries still regularly collapse four separate figures:

1. Gāthic Yima, the obscure offender;
2. Younger Avestan golden-age king and *vara* builder;
3. Pahlavi Jam, priestly and eschatological figure;
4. Ferdowsi’s Jamshid, civilizer and fallen shah.

That composite is useful for reception history but is not a single ancient biography.

### National and political reception

Jamshid functions as a symbol of pre-Islamic Iranian civilization, royal *farr*, and Nowruz. Takht-e Jamshid/Persepolis was central to Pahlavi state spectacle and the 1971 celebration of 2,500 years of monarchy, though the historical site is Achaemenid and the name legendary. After the Islamic Revolution, official culture sometimes treated such symbolism as monarchist or anti-Islamic, while the Shahnameh and Nowruz remained national institutions. Modern opposition rhetoric also uses Jamshid’s golden age and Zahhak’s tyranny as flexible political archetypes. Such use is reception, not evidence for ancient political doctrine. [Street-name politics](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sena.12393)

### Literature and art

Jamshid remains pervasive in Persian and Urdu literary allusion: his throne signifies majesty; his cup, universal vision; his ruined kingdom, worldly impermanence. Safavid and later painters clothed him as a contemporary monarch, turning the myth into courtly political reflection.

The Cup of Jam’s modern reputation as an ancient magical artifact is misleading. Its attachment to Jamshid is medieval literary development, not Avestan tradition.

### Film, animation, and popular narrative

Faridun Rahnama’s documentary **Takht-i Jamshid** (1960) and feature **Siyāvash dar Takht-i Jamshid** (1964) use Persepolis and Shahnameh memory to question historical and national identity. The Iranian animated film and graphic-novel project **The Last Fiction** reworks the Jamshid–Zahhak cycle for modern epic fantasy. These productions materially reinforce Jamshid as a tragic founder whose achievement turns to tyranny. [Cinema Iranica](https://cinema.iranicaonline.org/article/faridun-rahnamas-postmodernist-approach-in-siavash-in-persepolis/)

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated searches produced no reliable evidence that Yima/Jamshid occupied a defined role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or its standard ritual corpus. Modern occult websites sometimes assimilate him to solar kings, Yama, flood initiates, subterranean masters, or Indo-European sacrificed gods; these are modern syntheses unless a specific modern author and text are supplied.

Modern Zoroastrian, Iranian neopagan, and “Aryan” reconstructionist spaces sometimes honor Yima as an ancestral king or deity. Their scale and institutional continuity are not established by reliable demographic or ethnographic evidence. Claims of an unbroken secret Yima cult, a prescribed “Yima sigil,” ancient tarot correspondence, planetary rulership, or Golden Dawn invocation should be classified **[Modern invention]** unless supported by identifiable premodern texts.

### Commercial and personal use

Jamshid remains a common masculine personal name across Iran, Afghanistan, Tajikistan, South Asia, and diasporas. “Jamshid,” “Jamshed,” and “Takht-e Jamshid” are used commercially for restaurants, hotels, jewelry, cultural organizations, and media projects. Such naming testifies to cultural prestige, not worship.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Yima originally a god, first man, or king?

- **Textual minimum:** the Iranian texts call him a mortal, son, ruler, sacrificer, and exceptional possessor of Fortune.
- **First-man theory:** Roth, Windischmann, Christensen, Boyce, Lincoln, Kellens, and Shaked have differently reconstructed a first-man function from Yama, Jamag, incest, preservation, and dismemberment.
- **Objection:** no extant Iranian source plainly calls Yima the first human. Gayōmard occupies that role. Skjærvø stresses that the Iranian evidence makes Yima first addressed by Ahura Mazdā or first ideal king, not first man.
- **Deity:** Kushan Iamšo proves actual regional deification, but it does not establish that every Avestan community worshipped him as a god.

### The meaning of Yasna 32.8

Pahlavi exegetes read it as Yima making meat edible; Zaehner and Boyce related it to condemned animal sacrifice; Molé proposed ritual fault. The Old Avestan syntax remains obscure. “Yima lost immortality because he introduced meat-eating” is therefore a later exegesis or scholarly theory, not a secure translation.

### What was the lie?

Yašt 19 explicitly says “deceiving word” but does not securely preserve its content. Later traditions identify it as:

- refusal of Ahura Mazdā’s religion;
- claim to have created the world;
- demand for divine worship;
- submission to Ahriman;
- pride in his inventions.

These are interpretations of the lacuna, not equivalent witnesses to a single original sentence.

### Did Fortune leave three times?

Christensen and Humbach–Ichaporia read three departures; others read division into three portions; Hintze takes the repetition as emphasizing finality. The beneficiaries—Miθra, Θraētaona, and Kərəsāspa—are clear, but the mechanics are not.

### Vara, ark, paradise, or bunker?

Darmesteter and Moulton emphasized Noah and Mesopotamian borrowing. Lindner stressed independent Indo-Iranian development. Hertel called the *vara* the heavenly vault; Geldner an ideal microcosm; Boyce a transplanted underworld paradise; Kellens an engineered preserve linked to successive attempts at immortality. No archaeological structure can be identified as Yima’s *vara*.

### Yima and Vedic Yama

The names, fathers, royal vocabulary, formulas concerning a path or place, assembly of mortals, immortality, and sibling traditions establish cognacy. Yet their developed functions diverge:

- Vedic Yama becomes the first mortal to find the path to the Fathers and king of the dead.
- Iranian Yima rules a deathless earth and preserves life from winter.
- Iranian underworld rulership is late and limited.
- Yama’s explicit twin Yamī is Vedic; Yima’s Jamag appears only in Pahlavi texts.

The pair should be treated as inherited cognates, not one deity unchanged across India and Iran.

### Yima, Ymir, and sacrificed twins

Güntert and Lincoln linked Yima/Yama, Ymir, Puruṣa, and reconstructed Indo-European \**Yemo*, a “Twin” killed and dismembered by a brother. The broad comparative model remains influential, especially the Manu–Yemo reconstruction. The specific derivation of Old Norse *Ymir* from the same name is linguistically disputed, and no Iranian source says Yima’s body created the world. This is **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, not an attested Iranian myth.

### Mithraic claims

Zaehner explored transfer of Yima’s Fortune to Mithra and connected it with sacrificial themes. There is no Roman Mithraic text or image naming Yima, and no demonstrable chain from the Yima myth to the Roman tauroctony. A direct “Yima became Mithras” theory exceeds the evidence.

### Human sacrifice and underworld god

Boyce’s reading of Herodotus 7.114 remains conjectural. The victims, god, and ritual are unnamed in Iranian terms. The passage cannot securely document Yima worship, much less a normative human-sacrifice cult.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is structurally uneven:

- ancient compositions, medieval manuscripts;
- many ritual allusions, few continuous narratives;
- one major ancient named image, from Kushan Bactria;
- no securely excavated Yima sanctuary;
- rich ninth–twelfth-century literary elaboration;
- extensive manuscript art beginning many centuries after Ferdowsi.

The Avestan Digital Archive and critical manuscript work allow scholars to compare witnesses rather than treating nineteenth-century translations as a fixed “Zend-Avesta.” Darmesteter remains valuable, especially for Pahlavi interpretation, but his late dating and Biblical-dependence theories are not current consensus.
