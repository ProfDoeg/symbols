# Saoshyant: Research Dossier

**Class:** Human eschatological benefactor/restorer (*not* a god or yazata) in Zoroastrian tradition; in the oldest texts, *saoš́iiaṇt-* is primarily a common title for beneficent religious agents, sometimes plural, rather than the personal name of one supernatural being.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Old Avestan Gathas, especially Yasna 46.3 and 48.12, orally composed at an uncertain date, conventionally around the turn of the second to first millennium BCE, though proposed chronologies vary widely. There *saoš́iiaṇt-* means approximately “one who will bring benefit,” “benefactor,” or “helper.” The earliest securely identifiable *individual final savior*, Astvat̰.ərəta, occurs in the Younger Avesta, especially Yašt 13.128–129 and Yašt 19.89–96. No securely dated Achaemenid inscription names Saoshyant.

**Evidentiary key used below**

- **[Early attested]** Old Avestan/Gathic evidence.
- **[Later Avestan]** Younger Avestan material, older than its surviving manuscripts but not securely datable year by year.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** Middle Persian priestly literature, principally redacted in the Sasanian and early Islamic periods.
- **[Learned systematization]** Artificial chronology, genealogy, numerology, or harmonization produced by exegetes.
- **[Regional or popular tradition]** Localized expectation or non-systematic communal belief.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically made equation with another religious figure.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Philological or historical inference, not a statement directly supplied by an ancient source.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim first demonstrable in recent apologetic, occult, literary, or popular culture.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

Saoshyant is best understood as a title that became a proper name. In the Gathas it designates beneficent agents who advance Ahura Mazda’s order, perhaps including Zarathustra and his associates. In the Younger Avesta it becomes associated with unborn renovators and, preeminently, with one final victorious figure, Astvat̰.ərəta. In Middle Persian literature the title contracts to Sōšāns/Sōšyāns and normally denotes the last of three future sons of Zarathustra, who raises the dead and completes *frašegird*, the final renovation.

Calling Saoshyant a “god” is therefore misleading. Even in the most elaborate account he is a future human being, miraculously conceived but bodily born, acting under Ohrmazd’s authority with divine and human allies. Mary Boyce emphasized precisely this point: his extraordinary conception does not make him divine; Zoroastrian salvation remains something achieved through the participation of humanity as well as the divine world. [Later Avestan/later canonical elaboration] ([Astvat̰.ərəta](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/astvat-ereta-savior/))

### Names and scripts

- **Avestan:** 𐬯𐬀𐬊𐬴𐬌𐬌𐬀𐬥𐬙𐬀, conventionally transliterated *saoš́iiaṇt-* or normalized *saošyant-*; nominative and case forms vary in the texts.
- **Scholarly romanizations:** *Saošyant, Saoshyant, Saoshyans, Saoshyant-*, and older German *Saoschjant*.
- **Middle Persian/Pahlavi:** *Sōšāns, Sōšyāns*, with variant manuscript spellings.
- **New Persian:** سوشیانت, also سوشیانس (*Sūšiyānt, Sūšiyāns*).
- **Gujarati/Parsi English:** commonly *Soshiosh, Soshyosh,* or *Soshans* in older publications.
- **Specific Avestan personal name:** *Astvat̰.ərəta*.
- **First preliminary savior:** *Uxšyat̰.ərəta*, Middle Persian *Ušēdar/Hušēdar/Ōšēdar*.
- **Second preliminary savior:** *Uxšyat̰.nəmah*, Middle Persian *Ušēdarmāh/Hušēdarmāh/Ōšēdarmāh*.

### Etymology

**[Secure philology]** *Saošyant* is the active participle in *-ant-* from the future stem of Avestan √*sū/sau*. Its Indo-Iranian cognates convey swelling, strengthening, increase, prosperity, usefulness, and benefit. Hence:

- Darmesteter and Humbach/Faiss: “benefactor” or “one who will bring benefit.”
- Lommel: “helper.”
- Bartholomae and many older translations: “savior,” especially in eschatological passages.
- Insler emphasizes a semantic connection with “salvation.”

“Savior” is defensible in later eschatological contexts but misleading when retrojected into every Gathic occurrence because it imports the idea of a single Christ-like redeemer into passages that speak of plural human benefactors. The Middle Persian translators themselves normally glossed the word with *sūdōmand*, “useful, profitable, beneficial.” ([Malandra, “Saošyant”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/saosyant/))

### Astvat̰.ərəta

Yašt 13.128–129 supplies an indigenous etymology:

- he will be the victorious Saoshyant because he will “further” or benefit the whole material existence;
- he is *Astvat̰.ərəta* because, in the traditional explanation, he will cause embodied creatures to stand against destruction.

Older translations accordingly rendered the name “he who makes bodily creatures rise.” Modern philology more often understands it as “he who embodies righteousness/order,” connecting it with Gathic Yasna 43.16, *astvaṱ aṣ̌əm hyāṱ*, approximately “may truth/righteousness become embodied.” The contrast is not a contradiction so much as a difference between ancient priestly wordplay and historical linguistics. [Later Avestan plus modern scholarly reconstruction]

### Epithets and titles

- **“Victorious Saoshyant”** (*saoš́iiaṇt- vərəθrajan-*): Yasna 26.10, Yašt 13.129, Yašt 19.89–96. [Later Avestan]
- **Astvat̰.ərəta:** the individual final renovator. Yašt 13.128–129; 19.92. [Later Avestan]
- **“Unborn men who will accomplish the renovation”:** the future renovators collectively in Yašt 13.17 and 19.22. [Later Avestan]
- **“Restorer/raiser of the dead”:** Middle Persian descriptions in *Mēnōg ī Xrad*, *Bundahišn*, *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*, and related works. [Later canonical elaboration]
- **“Body-maker”:** attached in later tradition to his mother or her function in producing the final embodied savior; the exact allocation varies among translations and manuscripts. [Later canonical elaboration]
- **“Messiah”:** a comparative modern translation, not an Avestan or Pahlavi title. It is useful typologically but must not be treated as a lexical equivalence. [Modern scholarly/comparative usage]

There is no documented Greek or Roman *interpretatio* by which Saoshyant was equated with Heracles, Apollo, Mithras, or another classical god.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Old Avestan core

The Gathas are orally composed Old Avestan hymns embedded in the Yasna. Suggested dates range over many centuries; a common linguistic estimate places them near the turn of the second to first millennium BCE. Their transmission remained oral long before a Sasanian Avestan written archetype was created. Therefore “early attestation” means early linguistic composition, not an extant manuscript of that age. The oldest surviving Avestan codices are medieval.

#### Yasna 46.3

**[Early attested]** Zarathustra asks when the “dawns” will come through which the world advances toward what later tradition calls renovation, and when the saviors/benefactors will come with effective teachings. The number and identity of these agents are not fixed. Nothing here says that one is named Astvat̰.ərəta, has a virgin mother, or will be born from seed in a lake.

#### Yasna 48.9 and 48.12

Yasna 48.12 states that “the benefactors of the lands” will be those who follow Mazda’s teaching through actions governed by Good Mind and Truth and who oppose Wrath (*Aēšma*). The form is plural. This is the clearest control against treating every Gathic *saoshyant* as the personal name of the final savior. The Avestan text is preserved online alongside later translations. ([Yasna 48](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/y47to50.htm))

#### Yasna 45.11

A disputed and grammatically difficult passage applies *saoshyant* in relation to one who properly attends to the religion. Translations differ over syntax and whether a particular benefactor is envisaged. It cannot securely support the complete later savior biography. ([Yasna 43–46](https://www.avesta.org/yasna/y43to46.htm))

#### Yasna 30, 34, 43 and 53

These contain language about final recompense, defeating the Lie, fire as an instrument of judgment, making existence excellent, and embodying *aša*. Later exegesis connected these ideas to Saoshyant. They do not themselves narrate the three miraculous births or the complete Pahlavi apocalypse. [Early attested; later exegetical linkage]

### Younger Avestan corpus

The Younger Avesta is linguistically later than the Gathas. Its components accumulated orally; individual passages cannot ordinarily be assigned exact dates. Some older Yašt material may reach the early Achaemenid period, while other verses are later. Canonical writing is generally placed in the Sasanian period, perhaps not before the sixth century CE. The chief surviving pure Yašt manuscript is F1, copied at Navsari in 1591; J10 preserves an independent but often inferior branch. ([“Yašts”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/); [“Avesta”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/))

#### Yasna 9.2

Haoma asks Zarathustra to praise him as the other/future Saoshyants will. The title remains plural and need not identify Astvat̰.ərəta. [Later Avestan]

#### Yasna 26.10 and Yašt 13.145

The righteous fravašis are worshipped “from Gayōmard to the victorious Saoshyant.” This creates a total historical span from first man to last renovator. [Later Avestan]

#### Yašt 13, the Fravašis hymn

This is fundamental:

- 13.17 reveres the fravašis of unborn renovators.
- 13.62 associates 99,999 fravašis with protection of the future salvific process.
- 13.128 reveres the fravaši of Astvat̰.ərəta.
- 13.129 names him victorious Saoshyant and explains both names.
- 13.141–142 names the future mothers and supplies explanations for their names.
- 13.145 again spans history from Gayōmard to the final Saoshyant.

Darmesteter’s older translation is readily accessible, but its etymological renderings should be checked against Geldner, Lommel, Humbach, Hintze, Kellens, and current philology. ([Farvardin Yašt 128–129](https://www.avesta.org/ka/yt13sbe.htm?level=1))

#### Yašt 19, the Zamyād Yašt

This is the earliest extant text to present a developed final renovation around the individual victorious Saoshyant:

- 19.22 calls the future renovators unborn accomplishers of renovation.
- 19.89 says the Kayanian Glory will accompany the victorious Saoshyant and his companions when they make existence undecaying, imperishable, ever-living, and when the dead rise.
- 19.90–91 describes the defeat of the Lie and immortality.
- 19.92 places the final figure’s emergence at Lake Kąsaoya and names Astvat̰.ərəta.
- 19.93–96 describes his comrades and cosmic victory.

The text combines geographical, royal-legendary, and eschatological material; its final savior passage belongs to the hymn’s late historical reach, but its date within the Younger Avestan period cannot be fixed precisely. ([“Zamyād Yašt”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zamyad-yast/); [Kanga’s Avestan text and translation](https://www.avesta.org/kanga/kanga_yashts.pdf))

#### Vendidad 19.5

This passage connects Zarathustra’s semen with preservation under the guardianship of the fravašis. Popular summaries sometimes say that Vendidad 19.5 itself tells the complete virgin-bathing story. It does not: the familiar narrative results from reading this passage together with Yašts 13 and 19 and, especially, the later Pahlavi books. [Later Avestan; later harmonization]

### Middle Persian corpus

The Pahlavi books preserve Sasanian learning but are mostly extant in works redacted or copied under Islamic rule. Their late manuscript date does not prove that all content is late; nor does priestly attribution prove that every detail is ancient.

#### *Bundahišn*

The *Bundahišn* is a Middle Persian cosmological compilation. Its Iranian recension contains the fullest systematic account in chapters 33–34; older editions number the resurrection chapter 30.

It supplies:

- the three millennial saviors;
- exact world-years for their conceptions and appearances;
- the final fifty-seven-year ministry;
- resurrection in stages;
- recognition of resurrected kin;
- judgment;
- the molten-metal river;
- purification or punishment;
- the final sacrifice of the ox Hadayānš;
- white hōm mixed with the ox’s fat;
- universal immortality;
- the removal of mountains and restoration of creation.

This is **later canonical elaboration and learned systematization**, not a verbatim survival of one lost Gathic apocalypse. ([West’s translation](https://avesta.org/mp/bundahis.html); [modern overview of *frašō.kərəti*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/frasokrti/))

#### *Dēnkard*, Book VII, chapters 8–11

Compiled principally in the ninth–tenth centuries from older priestly materials, Book VII organizes sacred history as Zarathustra’s life followed by the successive millennia of Ušēdar, Ušēdarmāh, and Sōšyāns. Chapter 10 describes the final mother’s entry into the lake, miraculous conception, the child’s luminous body, possession of the Kayanian Glory, and his progressive renunciation of ordinary food. Chapter 11 covers his fifty-seven years and the renovation. ([*Dēnkard* VII](https://www.avesta.org/denkard/dk7.html); [Iranica, “Dēnkard”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/denkard/))

#### *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram*

Zādspram, active in the ninth century, relates the end-time chronology, the fifty-seven years, resurrection, and the cosmic battles. Chapters 34–35 are particularly important. ([Selections of Zādspram](https://www.avesta.org/mp/zadspram.pdf))

#### *Dādestān ī Dēnīg* and its accompanying Pahlavi Rivāyat

These works transmit detailed questions and answers about resurrection, bodily restoration, judgment, and the final fate of the wicked. The Pahlavi Rivāyat contains important variants: some passages imply ultimate purification; others allow annihilation or permanent exclusion for at least some wicked souls. [Later canonical elaboration with internal disagreement]

#### *Mēnōg ī Xrad*

Probably compiled in late antiquity, commonly assigned to around the sixth century with later transmission, it calls Saoshyant the triumphant “raiser/restorer of the dead.” It also makes Kay Khosrow one of his future assistants. ([*Mēnōg ī Xrad*](https://avesta.org/mp/mx.html))

#### *Zand ī Wahman Yasn*

Despite its title, this is a Middle Persian apocalypse rather than a surviving Avestan Yašt. Its present form reflects Islamic-era crises and retrospection, though it incorporates older material. It is much fuller on the calamities preceding Ušēdar than on Saoshyant’s own biography. Claims that it is a verbatim pre-Achaemenid prophecy are untenable. ([*Zand ī Wahman Yasn*](https://www.avesta.org/pahlavi/vohuman.html))

#### *Jāmāsp Nāmag*

This dialogue-prophecy supplies the three-savior sequence and a developed account of Sōšyāns performing yasna ceremonies, the melting of the mountains’ metal, resurrection, and immortal existence. Its layers and transmission are late and complex. ([*Jāmāsp Nāmag*](https://www.avesta.org/mp/jamaspi.htm))

#### *Abdīh ud Sahīgīh ī Sagastān*

This short Middle Persian praise of Sīstān lists the future birth and upbringing of Ōšēdar, Ōšēdarmāh, and Sōšyāns among the region’s wonders. It is significant evidence for the localization of the myth around the Helmand basin. ([Iranica, “Wonders of Sīstān”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abdih-ud-sahigih-i-sagastan-the-wonder-and-remarkability-of-sagastan-short-pahlavi-treatise/))

#### *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*

This vision of heaven and hell supplies the moral geography assumed by later eschatology but is not a principal source for Saoshyant’s identity or birth. It should not be cited as though it contained the complete Saoshyant legend.

### External witnesses and non-witnesses

#### Achaemenid inscriptions

Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam, Persepolis, and Artaxerxes’ inscriptions attest Ahuramazda, *arta*/*drauga*, royal divine favor, and eventually Mithra and Anāhitā. They do **not** name Saoshyant, Astvat̰.ərəta, the three saviors, Kąsaoya, or resurrection. Darius’s statement that Ahuramazda’s blessing attends a worshipper “while living and when dead” is not evidence for Saoshyant. ([Behistun passage](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/behistun-persian-text/behistun-t-46/))

#### Herodotus

*Histories* 1.131–140, written in the fifth century BCE, describes Persian sacrifice and the Magus’s recitation of a “theogony,” but never names Saoshyant or describes the final savior. The passage may witness ritual literature comparable to the Yašts; it cannot date the extant Saoshyant verses specifically. ([Herodotus 1.131–132](https://classics.andrewgadsden.com/library/herodotus/histories/1))

#### Plutarch

*De Iside et Osiride* 46–47, composed around the early second century CE, attributes to “Zoroaster” and the Magi a cosmic contest between Oromazes and Areimanius, after which evil disappears, the earth becomes level, and blessed humanity has one life and one language. Plutarch does not name Saoshyant. His account is, however, an important independent witness to an Iranian final-renovation complex in circulation by Roman times. Its exact Iranian source and the extent of Plutarch’s Hellenizing interpretation remain debated. ([Plutarch passage and scholarship](https://corpuscoranicum.de/de/verse-navigator/sura/20/verse/106/intertexts/1462))

#### Ferdowsi’s *Šāhnāma*

Completed around 1010 CE, the *Šāhnāma* preserves much Iranian royal legend but does not narrate the priestly Saoshyant cycle as a principal episode. Kay Khosrow, Sām, and other heroes later assigned end-time roles have extensive epic lives there, but one must not import their Pahlavi apocalyptic functions into Ferdowsi where the poem does not state them.

#### Manichaean and Roman Mithraic material

Manichaeism adopted and translated many Iranian divine names and eschatological ideas. Roman Mithraic interpretation has sometimes been linked to the final bull sacrifice, and older scholarship occasionally read the Mithraic tauroctony through the Saoshyant-Hadayānš episode. No surviving Mithraic inscription or icon labels Mithras as Saoshyant, however. The two sacrificial scenes differ fundamentally: Saoshyant’s sacrifice is a final restorative rite; Mithras’ tauroctony belongs to a separate iconographic and ritual system. Direct identity is unestablished. ([Iranica, “Mithraism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mithraism/))

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Saoshyant’s domain is not a natural phenomenon or a standing social institution. It is the future transformation of embodied existence:

- defeating the Lie and its agents;
- completing *frašō.kərəti/frašegird*;
- raising the dead;
- restoring bodies;
- presiding over final judgment under Ohrmazd;
- purifying creation;
- ending sickness, decay, hunger, thirst, and death;
- mediating the final immortality-producing sacrifice.

In the Gathas this domain is distributed among benefactors who oppose Wrath and advance Truth. In Yašt 19 it concentrates around the victorious final Saoshyant and his companions. In the Pahlavi books it becomes a precisely sequenced cosmic office.

### Physical form

No early source gives a stable portrait. The later books say:

- he is male and human;
- his body is radiant “like the sun”;
- the Kayanian Glory is with him;
- he progressively abstains from animal food, then plants, then water, finally living on spiritual nourishment;
- he remains embodied throughout the renovation.

These are literary attributes, not a canonical visual type.

### Iconography

There is no securely identified ancient or medieval image of Saoshyant.

No coin, relief, seal, manuscript painting, statue, or fire-temple image has been accepted by specialists as a named depiction. A winged disk, royal *xwarrah*, fire altar, fravaši symbol, solar halo, mounted Iranian hero, or bull-slaying Mithras must not be labeled “Saoshyant” without inscriptional or narrative evidence. Consequently:

- **standard pose:** none;
- **fixed clothing:** none;
- **weapon:** none;
- **sacred animal:** none peculiar to him;
- **planet, weekday, month, or calendar feast:** none securely assigned;
- **color or number:** no personal color; fifty-seven and the millennial chronology belong to later apocalyptic calculation, not cult iconography.

### Emblems and associated substances

These are narrative associations, not necessarily personal symbols:

- **Kayanian Glory (*xᵛarənah*):** accompanies Saoshyant and his companions in Yašt 19.89. [Later Avestan]
- **Lake Kąsaoya/Kayānsih:** place from which he arises or where Zarathustra’s seed is preserved. [Later Avestan and Pahlavi]
- **Fire and molten metal:** agents of universal ordeal and purification, produced by Ātar and Airyaman in later accounts. [Later canonical elaboration]
- **White hōm:** ingredient of immortality.
- **Ox Hadayānš:** final sacrificial animal.
- **Airyaman īšya prayer:** recited in connection with healing or restoration at the renovation.

Lake Kąsaoya was traditionally localized in Sīstān and identified with the Hamun/Helmand lake system. This is a historical-geographical identification, not a scientifically demonstrable location for a future birth.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogical versions

#### Gathic version

No parentage is given. The saoshyants are beneficent human agents. [Early attested]

#### Younger Avestan version

Astvat̰.ərəta is associated with Zarathustra’s preserved seed, Kąsaoya, the fravašis, and a future mother called:

- *Ǝrədāt̰.fəδrī*, approximately “she who brings fulfillment/prosperity to the father”;
- *Vīspa.taurvairī*, “all-overcoming.”

Whether these are two names for one woman or titles assembled during textual development is a philological question; later tradition treats them as belonging to the final mother. Yašt 13.142 explains “all-overcoming” by the son’s defeat of hostility.

#### Three-savior Pahlavi version

Zarathustra’s seed produces three sons at thousand-year intervals:

1. **Ušēdar**, Avestan *Uxšyat̰.ərəta*, “he who makes righteousness grow.”
2. **Ušēdarmāh**, Avestan *Uxšyat̰.nəmah*, “he who makes reverence grow.”
3. **Sōšyāns/Astvat̰.ərəta**, the final renovator.

Their mothers are respectively associated with the artificially parallel names *Srūtat̰.fəδrī*, *Vaŋhu.fəδrī*, and *Ǝrədāt̰.fəδrī*. Boyce regarded the triplication and parallel naming as priestly systematization: the first two savior and mother names appear modeled upon those of the final pair and could be relatively late additions to the formulaic list in Yašt 13. ([Astvat̰.ərəta](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/astvat-ereta-savior/))

Saoshyant has no attested consort or offspring. Ušēdar and Ušēdarmāh are his half-brothers through Zarathustra’s preserved seed, not sons of Saoshyant.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The following list separates actual narrative interaction from mere shared membership in the Zoroastrian cosmos.

- **Ahura Mazda/Ohrmazd — [Later canonical elaboration]:** Ohrmazd orders the resurrection and final rites; Saoshyant acts as his human agent and ritual assistant. *Bundahišn* 34; Pahlavi Rivāyat 48.
- **Haoma/Hōm — [Later Avestan]:** Haoma tells Zarathustra to praise him as the future saoshyants will praise him. Yasna 9.2.
- **The fravašis — [Later Avestan]:** 99,999 righteous fravašis guard Zarathustra’s seed and the future salvific birth. Vendidad 19.5; Yašt 13.62; later harmonization in Pahlavi texts.
- **The Kayanian Glory (*xᵛarənah*, personified sacred power) — [Later Avestan]:** accompanies the victorious Saoshyant and his companions during renovation. Yašt 19.89.
- **Airyaman — [Later canonical elaboration]:** with Ātar melts the mountains into the final river of metal; the Airyaman prayer is connected to restoration. *Bundahišn* 34; *Zādspram* 34–35; *Jāmāsp Nāmag*.
- **Ātar/Fire — [Later canonical elaboration]:** acts with Airyaman to melt the world’s metal for judgment and purification. *Bundahišn* 34.18.
- **Vohu Manah/Wahman — [Later canonical elaboration]:** participates in the final divine liturgy and defeats Aka Manah in the paired cosmic combat. *Bundahišn* 34.
- **Aša Vahišta/Ardwahišt — [Later canonical elaboration]:** participates in the final yasna and the defeat of evil; closely connected with Airyaman and fire. *Bundahišn* 34; Persian Rivayat.
- **Xšaθra Vairya/Šahrewar — [Later canonical elaboration]:** associated with metal and its final melting; assigned a priestly office in the final yasna. *Jāmāsp Nāmag*; Persian Rivayat.
- **Spəṇta Ārmaiti/Spandarmad — [Later canonical elaboration]:** participates in the final divine rite; as Earth she is freed from mountains and corruption. *Bundahišn* 34; Persian Rivayat.
- **Haurvatāt/Hordād — [Later canonical elaboration]:** participates in the final rite and defeats hunger. *Bundahišn* 34; Persian Rivayat.
- **Amərətāt/Amurdād — [Later canonical elaboration]:** participates in the final rite and defeats thirst; her domain of immortality is realized universally. *Bundahišn* 34.
- **Angra Mainyu/Ahriman — [Later canonical elaboration]:** principal cosmic adversary ultimately rendered powerless or expelled through the renovation that Saoshyant completes. *Bundahišn* 34 and related Rivayat texts.
- **Aēšma/Xēšm, Wrath — [Early attested/late personalized resolution]:** the Gathic saoshyants oppose Wrath in Yasna 48.12; later texts make Aēšma a demon defeated or driven away at the end.
- **Aka Manah/Akōman — [Later canonical elaboration]:** defeated by Vohu Manah in the cosmic battle accompanying Saoshyant’s work. *Bundahišn* 34.
- **Druj, the Lie — [Later Avestan]:** Astvat̰.ərəta stands against the Lie and its human and demonic brood. Yašt 13.129; Yašt 19.90–96.
- **Aži Dahāka/Dahāg — [Later canonical elaboration]:** not normally slain by Saoshyant personally; he escapes before the renovation and is killed by the revived hero Kərəsāspa/Sām. *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* and Pahlavi Rivayat.
- **Kay Khosrow — [Later canonical elaboration]:** an immortal human king rather than a god; he returns to assist Saoshyant in raising the dead. *Mēnōg ī Xrad* 26.57–63; related Pahlavi texts.
- **Sām/Kərəsāspa — [Later canonical elaboration]:** revived or awakened as an end-time champion and destroys Dahāg. Pahlavi Rivayat; *Zand ī Wahman Yasn*.
- **Nairyō.saŋha/Narsē — no specific interaction established:** both belong to Zoroastrian sacred tradition, but no securely attested Saoshyant episode joins them.
- **Mithra — no specific interaction established:** no Avestan or Pahlavi narrative records a meeting, alliance, combat, or identity between Mithra and Saoshyant.
- **Anāhitā/Arədvī Sūrā — no specific interaction established:** later writers sometimes confuse the lake mother with Anāhitā; the primary sources do not make Anāhitā Saoshyant’s mother.
- **Jesus, the Jewish Messiah, Maitreya, the Mahdi — no primary Zoroastrian interaction:** comparisons and identifications are comparative, syncretistic, or modern, not episodes in the Avestan-Pahlavi record.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The benefactors who oppose Wrath

**Earliest source:** Yasna 48.12.

The future belongs to those who, guided by Good Mind and Truth, enact Mazda’s teaching and oppose *Aēšma*, Wrath. The passage presents salvation as collective ethical action. There is no lone supernatural rescuer and no passive humanity. [Early attested]

**Development:** Later tradition retained companions around the final savior, but concentrated the title and narrative agency in Astvat̰.ərəta. The movement is from a functional plural to a named eschatological individual without completely eliminating collective human assistance.

### B. Astvat̰.ərəta and the Kayanian Glory

**Earliest developed source:** Yašt 19.89–96.

At the end of the hymn’s sequence of possessors of *xᵛarənah*, the Glory comes to the victorious Saoshyant and his companions. They make existence “excellent” or “wonderful”—ageless, deathless, undecaying, and ever-thriving. The dead rise; Druj is overcome; beings become immortal.

This is the earliest extant coherent core of the final-savior myth. It does not yet provide the Pahlavi chronology, detailed infant biography, or complete molten-metal drama.

### C. Zarathustra’s seed in Lake Kąsaoya

The familiar story is composite.

1. **[Later Avestan]** Vendidad 19.5 and Yašt 13.62 associate Zarathustra’s seed and the protecting fravašis.
2. **[Later Avestan]** Yašt 19.92 says the victorious Saoshyant will arise from Kąsaoya.
3. **[Later Avestan]** Yašt 13.142 names his future mother and explains her victory-bearing role.
4. **[Later canonical elaboration]** The Pahlavi books narrate a virgin entering or bathing in Lake Kayānsih, receiving the preserved seed, and conceiving without intercourse.
5. **[Learned systematization]** Three deposits or three luminous manifestations in the lake generate three saviors at thousand-year intervals.

Popular summaries that put the whole story into one ancient Avestan verse conflate these stages.

### D. The three Saoshyants

In the Pahlavi world chronology, Zarathustra’s revelation occurs around world-year 9000. His millennium is followed by three millennia:

- **9970:** the first virgin conceives Ušēdar; at age thirty, around world-year 10,000, he inaugurates his millennium.
- **10,970:** Ušēdarmāh is conceived; at age thirty he inaugurates the next millennium.
- **11,943:** Astvat̰.ərəta/Sōšyāns is conceived according to one principal reckoning.
- **11,973:** at age thirty his fifty-seven-year salvific career begins.
- **12,000:** the renovation is completed.

The odd 11,943 date accommodates the final fifty-seven years within the 12,000-year structure. Other texts and translations simplify the figures. They should not be harmonized into a false unanimity. [Learned systematization]

Under Ušēdar and Ušēdarmāh, the sun stands still for increasingly long periods, human life improves, and hunger diminishes, but evil returns. Only the third completes the irreversible renovation.

### E. Saoshyant’s birth and ascetic maturation

The final mother, descended in later genealogy from Zarathustra’s line, enters the lake as a sexually untouched maiden. The preserved seed impregnates her. Her son is radiant, endowed with victorious knowledge and *xwarrah*.

The *Dēnkard* describes a progressive discipline:

- seventeen years on vegetable food;
- thirty years on water;
- ten years on spiritual food.

The arithmetic totals fifty-seven years and mirrors the duration assigned to the final work. This is late priestly schematization, not found in the Gathas.

### F. Resurrection

Saoshyant and his assistants raise the dead over fifty-seven years. Later sources ask how bodies dispersed into earth, water, plants, animals, and other human beings can be restored. Ohrmazd replies by analogy with creation: the creator who originally formed the body can recollect its elements.

Versions differ on the order of resurrection. Some begin with Gayōmard and the primordial couple; others emphasize successive generations. The resurrected recognize spouses, parents, children, and companions. An assembly separates or evaluates the righteous and wicked.

The materiality of resurrection is important: this is not merely survival of an immaterial soul. Body and soul are reunited because material creation itself is to be healed.

### G. The river of molten metal

Airyaman and Ātar melt the metals in the mountains, producing a river that every resurrected human crosses.

- For the righteous it feels like warm milk.
- For sinners it is painful and burns away impurity.
- In universalist passages even the wicked emerge purified and join the praise of Ohrmazd.
- Other Pahlavi passages instead speak of permanent damnation or annihilation for some souls.

The contradiction is genuine. Shaul Shaked emphasized punitive and exclusionary texts; Mary Boyce stressed the universal restoration implied by the principal *Bundahišn* narrative. Zoroastrian sources do not yield one uncontested doctrine of universal salvation. ([“Frašō.kərəti”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/frasokrti/))

### H. Final combat

The good beings and their evil counterparts fight in paired confrontations. Humans and immortal heroes likewise eliminate monsters. Saoshyant is commander, ritual agent, and renovator, but not necessarily the personal killer of every demon. Ahriman’s fate varies in wording: defeated, made powerless, driven back into darkness, or removed from renewed creation.

### I. The last sacrifice and immortal food

Saoshyant and his helpers sacrifice the primordial/end-time ox Hadayānš. Its fat is mixed with white hōm to create an immortalizing preparation. Resurrected humanity consumes it and becomes permanently deathless.

This is presented as the last animal sacrifice because, once death and hunger end, further killing is unnecessary. It is not evidence for human sacrifice. No source attests that humans were sacrificed to Saoshyant.

### J. Flattening and perfecting the earth

Mountains melt or subside, hollows rise, and the earth becomes level and enlarged. Plutarch independently records the Magian expectation of a level earth and united blessed humanity but omits Saoshyant’s name. The image may represent restored accessibility, removal of Ahriman’s distortions, or the return of creation to its intended form. Interpretations beyond the texts are scholarly reconstruction.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No independent cult

There is no evidence for:

- a temple dedicated to Saoshyant;
- a hereditary Saoshyant priesthood;
- an altar or cult statue bearing his name;
- a festival exclusively his;
- sacrifices offered directly to him as a resident deity;
- an oracle, pilgrimage institution, or treasury under his patronage;
- theophoric personal names proving a mass Saoshyant cult.

This absence follows from his classification: Saoshyant is a future human restorer, not a continuously available cult god.

### Liturgical presence

He nonetheless appears in worship:

- Yasna 26.10 and Yašt 13 revere his future fravaši.
- Yasna liturgical passages invoke the victorious Saoshyant within the total sacred order.
- The Farvardin Yašt, still recited, venerates righteous fravašis from Gayōmard to the final Saoshyant.
- Later Afrinagān and commemorative prayer traditions preserve the same historical span.
- The final renovation is imagined as a perfect yasna, with Ohrmazd, Saoshyant, the Amesha Spentas, and assistants occupying ritual offices.

Thus Saoshyant is present in Zoroastrian liturgy without possessing an autonomous cult.

### Sīstān and the sacred geography

Lake Kąsaoya/Kayānsih was located in Sīstān, associated with the Helmand-Hamun basin. The *Wonders of Sīstān* celebrates the future saviors as regional glories. This is textual localization. No excavated lakeside shrine has been securely identified as a temple of Saoshyant, and archaeological structures near Lake Hamun cannot be assigned to him merely because of proximity.

### Sacrifice and ethically difficult material

- **Animal sacrifice:** the final ox Hadayānš is explicitly sacrificed in the Pahlavi renovation narrative.
- **Human sacrifice:** unattested in Saoshyant tradition.
- **Temple prostitution:** unattested.
- **Cult violence:** no Saoshyant cult institution is known that practiced it.
- **Religious suppression:** Zoroastrians endured conquest, taxation, legal disabilities, local persecution, conversion, and demographic contraction after the Arab conquest. Saoshyant hope functioned as communal consolation, but the evidence does not show organized violence committed in Saoshyant’s name.
- **Extremist appropriation:** Iranian nationalist discourse sometimes employs Zoroastrian good-versus-evil language, but no well-documented modern extremist movement centered specifically on worship of Saoshyant emerged in the sources reviewed.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Old Avestan stage

The term is functional and ethical: saoshyants are benefactors who enact Truth, Good Mind, and Mazda’s teaching. A final transformation is hoped for, but no complete biography of an individual savior survives. [Early attested]

### II. Younger Avestan concentration

The title becomes increasingly eschatological. Unborn renovators appear; Astvat̰.ərəta is named; his fravaši is worshipped; Kąsaoya and the final mother enter the complex; the Kayanian Glory accompanies him; bodily resurrection and immortal renovated existence become explicit. [Later Avestan]

### III. Achaemenid and Hellenistic contexts

Achaemenid inscriptions do not name him. Herodotus does not name him. The best historical inference is that some renovation doctrine existed in Iranian religious transmission, but its exact form cannot be recovered from royal inscriptions.

In the Hellenistic and Parthian eras Iranian, Babylonian, Jewish, Greek, and later Christian communities interacted extensively. Plutarch shows that a Magian final-renovation narrative was known outside Iran by the early second century CE. He does not establish the complete three-savior system.

### IV. Sasanian codification

The Avesta was canonically organized and written, a large zand exegetical tradition developed, and cosmic chronology was systematized. Much of the detailed architecture later preserved in Pahlavi books likely reflects Sasanian scholasticism, though the extant books were often redacted later.

The three saviors supplied symmetry to the final three millennia. The last fifty-seven years, demon-by-demon combats, resurrection mechanics, final liturgy, molten metal, and immortal food were coordinated into a comprehensive end-time sequence.

### V. Early Islamic-period redaction

Ninth- and tenth-century priests—among them Ādurfarnbag, Ādurbād Ēmēdān, Zādspram, and Manuščihr’s circle—preserved and reorganized Sasanian learning while living under Muslim rule. *Dēnkard*, *Bundahišn*, *Zādspram*, *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*, and Rivayat materials provide the fullest record precisely because Zoroastrians were now a vulnerable minority.

The savior expectation acquired obvious communal urgency. Iranian believers sometimes expected deliverance from the east, connected with hopes surrounding descendants or successors of the Sasanian royal house who had fled toward China. This was a historical adaptation of Saoshyant expectation, not part of the Avesta.

### VI. Medieval and early-modern Iran and India

Parsi migration created separate but communicating centers. Persian Rivayat correspondence between Iranian priests and Parsis in India transmitted doctrine and messianic expectations. The elaborate chronology persisted among learned priests, while ordinary expectation often reduced it to one future Sōšyāns who would end suffering.

No evidence demonstrates continuous institutional worship of Saoshyant as a god. Continuity consists instead in scripture, recitation, catechesis, and expectation.

### VII. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century reform

European philology, Christian missionary criticism, Parsi reform, and modern Iranian nationalism changed interpretation:

- philologists distinguished Gathic plural “benefactors” from the later individual savior;
- reformers privileged the Gathas and often minimized Pahlavi miracles;
- traditionalists defended literal miraculous conception, resurrection, and renovation;
- modern ethical readings made every righteous person a “saoshyant” contributing to world improvement;
- comparative religion popularized “the Zoroastrian Messiah.”

These are modern hermeneutical choices, not evidence that one ancient view displaced every other.

### VIII. Present

Saoshyant remains part of Zoroastrian scripture and theology. Present belief is diverse:

- some Zoroastrians expect a literal future savior and bodily renovation;
- some accept the figure but understand the details symbolically;
- Gatha-centered reformists emphasize the plural benefactor and collective human responsibility;
- cultural revivalists sometimes use Saoshyant as an emblem of Iranian renewal without affirming the priestly apocalypse.

Reliable figures for the number holding each view do not exist.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Comparative “Messiah”

The comparison rests on genuine structural similarities:

- future righteous deliverer;
- defeat of evil;
- resurrection;
- judgment;
- transformed world.

But major differences remain:

- *māšîaḥ* means “anointed” and emerges from Israelite royal language; *saošyant* means benefactor.
- The Gathic word is plural and functional.
- Saoshyant is neither a Davidic king nor an incarnation of God.
- The lake-seed conception is not the same narrative mechanism as the Christian conception of Jesus.
- In the three-savior system, preliminary renovators precede the final one.
- Saoshyant’s decisive work includes a Zoroastrian yasna and final hōm sacrifice.

Calling him “the Persian Messiah” is therefore a comparative shorthand, not a historical native title.

### Did Saoshyant influence Jewish and Christian messianism?

#### Case for Iranian influence

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Scholars including Mary Boyce and contributors to *Encyclopaedia Iranica* argue that Persian rule and long Iranian-Jewish contact offer a credible route for influence. Second Temple Jewish texts develop bodily resurrection, judgment, angelic and demonic opposition, a final adversarial crisis, and world renewal after centuries of Achaemenid and post-Achaemenid contact. The total complex resembles Iranian eschatology strongly. ([Iranica, “Eschatology”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/eschatology-i/))

Boyce argued that belief in a world savior was sufficiently developed in the Achaemenid period to influence Judeo-Christian thought. Plutarch proves that Iranian renovation traditions circulated beyond Zoroastrian scripture by Roman times.

#### Case for caution or reverse/reciprocal influence

The decisive evidentiary problem is dating. The Gathas attest *saoshyant* early but do not give the detailed Messiah-like biography. The most explicit Younger Avestan passages survive only in medieval manuscripts, and the three virgin-born saviors are fully narrated in Pahlavi works redacted after Christianity and in an Islamic environment.

Therefore scholars such as Shaul Shaked and many historians of Judaism reject simple one-way derivation. Iranian and Jewish traditions may have influenced each other repeatedly under Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sasanian rule. Greek cosmology, Babylonian schemes, and internal Jewish exegesis also contributed. Similarity cannot by itself determine direction.

The most defensible conclusion is:

1. an Iranian renovation tradition is genuinely pre-Christian;
2. its exact Achaemenid form is uncertain;
3. Iranian influence on some Second Temple Jewish eschatology is historically plausible and accepted by many specialists;
4. deriving “the Jewish Messiah” or Christian virgin birth wholesale from Saoshyant exceeds the evidence;
5. the elaborate three-savior birth cycle may itself reflect late antique systematization amid Jewish, Christian, Manichaean, and Iranian exchange.

### Jesus as Saoshyant

No Avestan, Pahlavi, early Christian, or mainstream Zoroastrian source identifies Jesus as Saoshyant. Such equations appear in modern interfaith speculation, Christian apologetic, universalist teaching, and online syncretism. They are **modern syncretistic identifications**, not ancient tradition.

The Gospel Magi are never called Zoroastrian priests in the text, and Matthew does not say they came because they recognized Jesus as Saoshyant. The popular story that Zoroastrian scripture predicted a star announcing Saoshyant and therefore sent the Magi to Bethlehem lacks a secure primary Zoroastrian source.

### Maitreya, Mithras, Mani, and the Mahdi

- **Maitreya:** Iranian influence on the development of future-Buddha expectation has been proposed, especially for Central Asia, but no text identifies Maitreya and Saoshyant as one being.
- **Mithras:** equation is unsupported; their bull sacrifices belong to different mythic structures.
- **Manichaeism:** Mani adopted Iranian vocabulary and apocalyptic structures, but there is no simple surviving “Saoshyant” counterpart. Manichaean Iranian names often translate distinct Manichaean entities.
- **Mahdi/Qāʾem:** Iranian religious culture contributed to the environment of Islamic messianism, especially in Iran, but direct derivation of the Mahdi from Saoshyant is not demonstrable. Interaction was multilateral.
- **Bahāʾī universalism:** occasional modern identifications treat successive saviors as manifestations of one principle. This is not traditional Zoroastrian exegesis.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no substantial premodern grimoire tradition invoking Saoshyant, no Golden Dawn grade or ritual centered upon him, and no established Theosophical Saoshyant cult. Modern occult websites sometimes assimilate him to Christ, Maitreya, Kalki, or an Aquarian world teacher. These equations are recent comparativism.

Modern reconstructionist or revivalist Zoroastrians may honor him as part of scripture, but evidence for a separate international “Saoshyant cult” is absent. Internet prayers, AI-generated portraits, solar warriors, winged Persian messiahs, and images labeled “ancient Saoshyant” are modern creations unless tied to an identified manuscript or monument.

### Literature and popular culture

Saoshyant appears less often as an individualized character than names such as Ahura Mazda, Ahriman, or Mithra. Modern fantasy, games, nationalist poetry, and esoteric writing use the name for a prophesied hero or world-renewer. Such works usually combine the final Saoshyant with generic “chosen one” conventions. They have not produced an agreed modern iconography.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major scholarly stages

- **Nineteenth century:** Martin Haug, James Darmesteter, Lawrence Mills, E. W. West, Karl Geldner, and Christian Bartholomae edited, translated, and lexicographically organized the Avestan and Pahlavi evidence. Their work remains indispensable, but translations often employ Christian vocabulary such as “Savior,” “Messiah,” “archangel,” and “resurrection” too freely.
- **Early twentieth century:** H. Lommel, Arthur Christensen, and others attempted detailed stratification and comparative reconstruction.
- **Mid-to-late twentieth century:** Mary Boyce produced an influential historical synthesis; Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin, Ilya Gershevitch, Jean Kellens, Helmut Humbach, William Malandra, John Hinnells, Shaul Shaked, and others revised philology, dating, and comparative claims.
- **Recent work:** Almut Hintze, Alberto Cantera, Antonio Panaino, Prods Oktor Skjærvø, Michael Stausberg, Yuhan Vevaina, and others emphasize manuscript traditions, ritual context, redaction, and reciprocal intercultural contact.

### Principal debates

#### 1. Does Gathic *saoshyant* already mean the final savior?

- **Maximal continuity:** Zarathustra already expected a particular future man who would complete the work.
- **Philological caution:** the Gathic forms are usually common and plural; the individual Astvat̰.ərəta belongs to Younger Avestan development.
- **Assessment:** the second position better fits the grammar. A general expectation of future benefactors and final transformation may be Gathic, while the full personal dossier is later.

#### 2. Is Astvat̰.ərəta’s name ancient or exegetically manufactured?

Boyce derived it from Yasna 43.16, “may righteousness be embodied,” suggesting theological formation from Zarathustra’s words. Kellens and others emphasize scholastic naming patterns. The name is securely Avestan but its narrative interpretation may be secondary.

#### 3. When did the three-savior system arise?

The three names occur in Yašt 13, but formulaic placement allows interpolation or late addition. Their parallel names and those of their mothers look artificially modeled. The complete thousand-year sequence is securely documented only in Pahlavi literature. The system may preserve Sasanian teaching; it cannot securely be projected into the Gathic period.

#### 4. Is the mother a virgin in the Avesta?

The Avesta provides the mother’s names, the lake, the seed, and future birth, but the detailed bathing-and-conception narrative is Pahlavi. “Virgin-born Saoshyant in ancient Avestan scripture” is therefore an overcompressed claim.

#### 5. Is final salvation universal?

- *Bundahišn* and related passages imply that sinners are painfully purified and creation becomes wholly good.
- Other Rivayat passages permit eternal damnation or annihilation.
- The evidence preserves competing doctrinal answers.

#### 6. Iranian influence on Judaism and Christianity

The debate turns on chronology and mechanism, not simply resemblance. Persian political rule and diaspora contact supply opportunity, but late written witnesses complicate priority. Modern scholarship increasingly speaks of contact, stimulation, adaptation, and reciprocal formation rather than a single act of borrowing.

#### 7. Localization of Kąsaoya

Identification with Lake Hāmūn in Sīstān is strong within later Iranian geography and local tradition. It is not certain that the oldest composers had exactly the modern lake complex in mind.

### State of the evidence

- The Avesta was orally transmitted for centuries.
- A Sasanian written archetype is generally accepted, possibly sixth century or later.
- The extant corpus may be only a fraction of earlier Zoroastrian literature, but “one quarter” is an estimate, not a measurable fact.
- The oldest principal Yašt codex is F1, 1591 CE.
- Geldner’s 1886–96 edition remains foundational but is being superseded in method by direct manuscript work.
- The Avestan Digital Archive and Corpus Avesticum Berolinense have made manuscript variation more visible.
- Pahlavi works survive in still later manuscripts and contain both old zand learning and Islamic-period redaction.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Saoshyant was an Achaemenid state god: **unsupported**.
- Darius or Cyrus explicitly proclaimed Saoshyant: **false**.
- Herodotus named the Persian savior: **false**.
- A known ancient statue depicts Saoshyant: **unsupported**.
- Anāhitā was his canonical mother: **false**.
- Saoshyant and Mithras are the same deity: **unsupported**.
- Roman Mithraic bull-slaying depicts Saoshyant’s final sacrifice: **unsupported**.
- The Avesta contains the complete Pahlavi three-virgin story: **false**.
- Saoshyant straightforwardly “is Jesus”: **modern syncretism**.
- Zoroastrianism unquestionably invented every Jewish and Christian end-time doctrine: **historically excessive**.
- Conversely, the entire Saoshyant doctrine was copied from Christianity: **also excessive**, because Gathic and Younger Avestan evidence demonstrates indigenous Iranian antecedents.
- Saoshyant requires or receives human sacrifice: **no evidence**.
- There is a continuous ancient occult order devoted to Saoshyant: **no evidence**.
