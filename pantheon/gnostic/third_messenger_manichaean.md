# The Third Messenger (Manichaean): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** a divine light-being, “god” or *yazad* in Iranian Manichaean terminology, “angel” in some Islamic descriptions, and an emanated salvific power rather than a created mortal spirit. He is the presiding deity of the **Third Evocation**—the third divine response to the invasion of Light by Darkness. “Messenger” here is a cosmological title, not simply “prophet.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** the figure belongs to Mani’s own third-century cosmological system. The earliest recoverable textual witness is material attributed to Mani’s lost *Treasure of Life* or *Treasury*, composed in Syriac in the third century and quoted in Latin by Augustine in *De natura boni* 44, written about 404–405. Independent Manichaean witnesses survive in fourth- or fifth-century Coptic codices from Medinet Madi; Middle Iranian fragments from Turfan preserve the corresponding Iranian names and hymns. The surviving manuscripts are later than the doctrine they transmit.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early/Mani]**, securely traceable to Mani or his immediate school; **[Early community]**, third–fourth-century Manichaean tradition; **[Canonical elaboration]**, authoritative later Manichaean exposition; **[Regional variant]**; **[Hostile witness]**; **[Syncretistic identification]**; **[Medieval report]**; **[Modern reconstruction]**; **[Modern invention/revival]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

The Third Messenger is the leading power of the final divine “evocation” in Manichaean cosmogony. After the First Man’s five light-elements have become mixed with Darkness and the Living Spirit has constructed the cosmos as a mechanism for separating them, the Father of Greatness evokes the Third Messenger. From the sun—or through luminous powers stationed in the celestial “ships”—the Messenger reveals beautiful male and female forms to the imprisoned demonic rulers. Their desire causes them to discharge or miscarry the Light held in their bodies. The released substance is partly purified and carried upward; the remainder falls into the terrestrial world, precipitating plant, animal, and human generation. The Messenger therefore combines solar sovereignty, beauty, and cosmic redemption with an unsettling reproductive episode deliberately preserved by both Manichaean texts and hostile witnesses.

### Principal names

- **Third Messenger / Third Envoy / Third Ambassador** — Coptic translations employ forms equivalent to Greek *tritos apostolos* or *presbeutēs*; Latin polemicists use **Tertius Legatus**. **[Canonical elaboration]** The adjective denotes his place in the three divine evocations, not the third member of a sequence of three human prophets. There is no normally attested “First Messenger” and “Second Messenger” corresponding exactly to him; Chinese terminology occasionally creates a “Second Messenger” title for another being as part of a local numerical system.

- **Syriac ܐܝܙܓܕܐ, īzgaddā / izgaddā**, “envoy, legate, ambassador.” **[Early/Mani, reconstructed through quotation]** Theodore bar Kōnay’s Syriac account, completed in 791–792 but drawing on older Manichaean material, calls him simply “the Messenger,” not necessarily “the Third Messenger.” The title is related to Iranian diplomatic terminology and should not be confused with Arabic *rasūl* as though it necessarily denoted a historical prophet.

- **Latin *Tertius Legatus***, “Third Legate/Envoy.” **[Western canonical terminology]** This is the standard scholarly label derived from Latin anti-Manichaean sources.

- **Middle Persian *Narēsahyazad / Narisahyazad***, conventionally **Narisah**, **Narisaf**, or **Narseh Yazad**. **[Syncretistic identification; Iranian regional terminology]** The second element *yazad* means a being worthy of worship, a god. The name continues Iranian **Nairyō.saŋha**, traditionally connected with “manly/potent utterance” or a divine herald. It is not an etymological translation of “Third Messenger”; it is an equation with an Iranian divine name.

- **Parthian *Narisaf Yazad***, written in Manichaean script as forms transliterated *nrysfyzd*; Sogdian adaptations include *nrʾyšβ yzδ* and the native-looking *nryšnx βγy*. **[Regional variant]**

- **Parthian *hridīg frēštag*** and Sogdian *aštīk(u) frēštāk*, approximately “Third Envoy/Messenger.” **[Regional translation]**

- **Mihr / Mihr Yazad**. **[Syncretistic identification; disputed development]** Parthian and Sogdian Manichaean texts can identify the Third Messenger with the Iranian Mithra, especially in his solar aspect. The terminology is notoriously unstable:
  
  - Werner Sundermann’s detailed account states that Middle Persian **Mihr** ordinarily names the Living Spirit, whereas Parthian and Sogdian traditions use Mihr for the Third Messenger.
  - Some searchable summaries reverse the Middle Persian side of this distribution. The primary philological tables and language of individual fragments therefore control any specific identification.
  - Mary Boyce argued that Parthian solar devotion to Mithra encouraged the equation and gave the Messenger Mithraic traits.
  - Ilya Gershevitch objected that Sogdian “Mithra as sun” is itself chiefly Manichaean evidence, so one cannot simply posit a pre-existing Sogdian solar Mithra as the cause.
  - Sundermann instead proposed divergent terminological development: Mani constructed one Middle Persian nomenclature, while Mār Ammō and the Parthian mission completed another at some distance from him.

- **Sun God / God of the Sun**, sometimes **Rōšnšahr Yazad**, “God of the Realm/City of Light.” **[Iranian learned systematization]** Solar language can describe either the Messenger himself or his residence and visible manifestation. It must not automatically be converted into identity with the physical sun.

- **Chinese “Sun-Light Buddha”** and titles corresponding to “Great Envoy of Beneficent Light.” **[Chinese Buddhist translation]** Chinese Manichaeism rendered the pantheon in Buddhist honorifics. “Buddha” in such texts is a translational and missionary classification, not evidence that the deity originated as a Buddhist figure.

### Classification through time

- **Manichaean internal texts:** a god or divine power of Light, emanated or “called forth” by the Father.
- **Coptic systematic texts:** a “father” or great light-power in the third divine evocation.
- **Iranian hymns:** a *yazad*, solar god, redeemer, and object of praise.
- **Christian polemicists:** a fictitious Manichaean divine “power,” sometimes ridiculed as part of a polytheistic mythology.
- **Islamic heresiography:** frequently an angel or named celestial power; this terminology partly suppresses the polytheistic appearance that “god” would have conveyed to Muslim readers.
- **Modern scholarship:** a mythological deity, hypostasis, emanation, or personified stage of salvation. These are analytical descriptions, not interchangeable indigenous titles.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest recoverable testimony

#### Mani’s *Treasure of Life*

**[Early/Mani, preserved by hostile quotation]** Augustine, *De natura boni* 44, explicitly says that the erotic extraction of Light stood in book seven of the Manichaean *Thesaurus*. His Latin quotation describes the “blessed Father who has the luminous ships as lodgings,” whose powers assume the forms of beautiful virgins before male powers and beardless boys before female powers. Desire loosens the captive “living substance,” which rises toward the light-ships; contaminated residue descends into vegetation.

The underlying book was composed by Mani in Syriac during the third century, but Augustine’s Latin witness dates to about 404–405. It is therefore an early composition in a later hostile transmission. Augustine’s hostile framing—“incredible obscenities”—must be separated from the cosmological extract he quotes.

A textual problem remains: Augustine’s quoted passage speaks of the “blessed Father” and his powers rather than naming *Tertius Legatus* in every sentence. Scholars identify the actor with the Third Messenger or his celestial retinue because the episode corresponds to independent Manichaean cosmogonies. It is unsafe to treat every grammatical subject in Augustine’s excerpt as a direct personal name for the Messenger.

#### The Coptic *Kephalaia*

**[Early community/Canonical elaboration]** The *Kephalaia of the Teacher* survives in Coptic papyrus codices from Medinet Madi, Egypt, normally dated palaeographically to the fourth or fifth century. Its contents probably derive from authoritative oral teaching and editorial work extending from Mani’s immediate circle into the early fourth century.

Important configurations include:

- **Kephalaion 7:** Father of Greatness → Third Messenger → Jesus the Splendour → Light-Nous → Light Form and three soul-guiding angels.
- **Kephalaion 16:** the third evocation includes the Third Messenger, the Column of Glory, and the powers of Light revealed by him.
- **Kephalaion 25:** a more elaborate and partly different arrangement of the third divine group.

The discrepancies are genuine evidence. They show that even authoritative Manichaean systematization did not preserve a single invariant genealogy.

The Medinet Madi codices were found or entered the antiquities market in 1929–1931. The *Kephalaia* are now divided principally between Berlin and the Chester Beatty Library. Their fragmentary condition means that several names and transitions remain uncertain.

### Other primary corpora

- **Mani’s *Šābuhragān*** — Middle Persian, composed for Šāpur I in the third century; surviving Turfan fragments are medieval manuscript witnesses. **[Early/Mani]** It presents the cosmic drama in Iranian terminology. The extent to which each surviving passage names Narisah directly varies with fragment and reconstruction.

- **Mani’s *Pragmateia* or Treatise** — lost in its original form; cosmological matter is preserved through Theodore bar Kōnay and other excerpts. **[Early/Mani through later quotation]**

- **Theodore bar Kōnay, *Book of Scholia* XI** — Syriac, 791–792. **[Medieval hostile witness preserving early material]** His account is among the most valuable connected summaries of Mani’s cosmogony and is often considered dependent on a Syriac Manichaean source close to the *Pragmateia*. It recounts the Messenger’s evocation and the Twelve Virgins.

- **Coptic Manichaean Psalm-Book** — fourth- or fifth-century manuscripts; compositions are earlier and layered. **[Canonical/liturgical]** Psalms address and praise the Third Messenger, demonstrating cultic devotion rather than merely scholastic cosmology.

- **Iranian hymns from Turfan**, including the “Hymn to the Third Messenger” and the “Hymn on the Third Messenger and the Archons” — Parthian or Middle Persian compositions surviving in fragmentary Central Asian manuscripts, principally ninth–eleventh century. **[Regional liturgical tradition]** These invoke Narisah or Mihr, celebrate the deity’s radiance, and retell the defeat of the demons.

- **The Manichaean Confessional Book (*Xwāstwānīft*) and benedictions (*āfrīwan/āfurišn*)** — Middle Iranian, Sogdian, and Old Uyghur witnesses. **[Regional liturgy]** Iranian benedictions explicitly address Narisah along with Jesus the Splendour, the Column of Glory, Mani, and the ecclesiastical envoys.

- **Augustine’s anti-Manichaean works** — Latin, chiefly 388–405. **[Hostile witness with insider knowledge]** Augustine had been a Manichaean Hearer for roughly nine years. *De natura boni* 44 is central; *De moribus Manichaeorum*, *Contra Faustum*, *Contra Fortunatum*, and related works supply the wider ritual and cosmological setting.

- **Ephrem the Syrian, *Prose Refutations*** — Syriac, fourth century. **[Hostile early witness]** Ephrem attacks the display of luminous images before demonic rulers. His presentation sometimes makes a single Maiden of Light, rather than a male Messenger plus twelve female powers, the immediate seducer.

- **Alexander of Lycopolis, *Against the Doctrines of Mani*** — Greek, late third or early fourth century. **[Early hostile philosophical witness]** Alexander renders the First Man as Soul, the Living Spirit as Demiurge, and the Third Messenger as “another power,” illustrating philosophical abstraction rather than Manichaean devotional nomenclature.

- **Acta Archelai** — Greek/Latin transmission of a fourth-century anti-Manichaean work. **[Hostile legendary witness]** Useful for Western reception but unreliable as a biography of Mani.

- **al-Nadīm, *Fihrist*** — Arabic, 987–988. **[Medieval report]** Preserves lists of Mani’s books and Islamic-period summaries of his doctrine. Its terminology sometimes calls Manichaean gods angels.

- **al-Bīrūnī, *Taḥqīq mā li-l-Hind*** — Arabic, about 1030. **[Medieval learned report]** Quotes or paraphrases Manichaean cosmogonic material concerning the light-powers’ displayed forms.

- **Šahrastānī, *Kitāb al-milal wa-l-niḥal*** — Arabic, twelfth century. **[Medieval systematization]** Valuable for reception but remote from Mani.

- **Chinese *Compendium of the Teachings of Mani, the Buddha of Light*** — presented to the Tang court in 731. **[Regional canonical adaptation]** It translates the hierarchy into Buddhist and Chinese cosmological language.

- **Chinese Hymnscroll and Dunhuang texts** — eighth–tenth centuries. **[Regional liturgy]** They preserve the sun and moon as great ships, Manichaean gods as buddhas, and the salvific transport of Light.

### The requested Gnostic and Mandaean corpora: a negative finding

The **Apocryphon of John**, **Hypostasis of the Archons**, **On the Origin of the World**, **Gospel of Truth**, **Tripartite Tractate**, **Gospel of the Egyptians**, **Zostrianos**, **Allogenes**, **Trimorphic Protennoia**, **Eugnostos**, **Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth**, Berlin Codex texts, *Pistis Sophia*, the *Books of Jeu*, and the *Gospel of Judas* do **not** name the Manichaean Third Messenger. Nor do Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Epiphanius, Tertullian, Clement’s *Excerpta ex Theodoto*, or the *Corpus Hermeticum* attest this being as such.

They are relevant only as **comparanda**:

- Sethian and related works furnish archons, heavenly archetypes, luminous humans, and sexualized cosmogony.
- *Eugnostos* and the *Sophia of Jesus Christ* offer terminology comparable to parts of the Manichaean pentadic soul and emanational hierarchy.
- Valentinian syzygies have been proposed as structural background for the Father–female power–son patterns.
- *Poimandres* offers a luminous archetypal Human desired by the lower nature, a notable comparison to the demonic desire for the Messenger’s image.
- None proves that the Third Messenger was borrowed directly from a surviving Nag Hammadi tractate.

The **Ginza Rabba** likewise does not attest the Third Messenger under that title. Mandaean *uthras*, the World of Light, salvific messengers, celestial Jordan imagery, and a hostile material cosmos show shared late-antique Aramaic religious environments. A direct equation with a named Mandaean being has not been demonstrated. “Mandaean cousin” is therefore a comparative description, not a documented identity.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

- **Liberation of imprisoned Light.** **[Early/Mani]** The Messenger activates the final great phase of cosmic purification.
- **Solar radiance.** **[Canonical/Regional]** He resides in, governs, or is manifested through the sun-ship. Texts do not always distinguish residence, vehicle, symbol, and identity.
- **Transformative beauty.** **[Early/Mani]** His powers—or the Virgins/Maiden acting for him—assume forms suited to the desire of male and female archons.
- **Cosmic revelation of the divine image.** **[Canonical elaboration]** The demonic creators later fashion Adam after the image they saw.
- **Ordering the machinery of salvation.** **[Canonical elaboration]** He sets the sun and moon in motion, commissions the construction of the New Paradise, and supervises recovery of Light.
- **Human redemption.** **[Iranian liturgical development]** Hymns emphasize him as helper, luminous guide, and redeemer, sometimes more than as the actor in the sexual cosmogony.
- **Final cosmic separation.** **[Eschatological elaboration]** He participates in the eventual withdrawal of the light-powers when the cosmic machine has completed its task.

### The sun and moon as ships

**[Early/Canonical]** The sun and moon are “luminous ships” or vessels that receive purified Light. The precise route varies by source:

1. liberated Light ascends through the Column of Glory;
2. the moon receives it and waxes;
3. the moon transfers its cargo to the sun and wanes;
4. the sun conveys it onward toward the New Paradise or World of Light.

Other descriptions assign uncontaminated Light directly to sun and moon while stars gather partially tainted material. Astronomical observation—the lunar phases, solar course, and Milky Way—was interpreted as the visible operation of salvation.

The Third Messenger is particularly linked to the **sun-ship**; Jesus the Splendour and other gods can be associated with the moon-ship. Lists of divine occupants differ regionally.

### The Column of Glory

**[Canonical]** The Column of Glory, Column of Praise, Perfect Man, or celestial route of ascent is commonly identified visually with the Milky Way. It is not simply another name for the Third Messenger. In *Kephalaia* 7 it can be an emanation or power produced in the third evocation; elsewhere it is listed beside the Messenger. Chinese texts render it through titles associated with Vairocana/Lushena and a “diamond column,” demonstrating Buddhist translation rather than original Buddhist identity.

### Form and gender

The Messenger’s own gender is usually grammatically masculine. The displayed salvific form, however, is sexually polyvalent:

- male archons see beautiful virgins;
- female archons see beautiful, beardless youths;
- some accounts attribute both transformations to the Messenger’s powers;
- others make the Twelve Virgins the agents;
- still others speak of one Maiden of Light with transient or androgynous manifestation.

**[Variant, not harmonizable]** These are not simply interchangeable descriptions. They reflect different enumerations of the third evocation and perhaps different strategies for explaining Genesis 1:27, “male and female.”

### Emblems and associations

Securely attested:

- the sun and sun-ship;
- radiant or naked beauty;
- twelve female powers or virgins;
- the zodiacal circuit in later systematization;
- the Milky Way/Column of Glory;
- light, fire, and celestial brilliance;
- divine images appearing to bound archons.

Not securely attested:

- an exclusive weapon, animal, plant, color, weekday, or numerical cult symbol belonging to the Messenger alone;
- a standardized individual portrait;
- a universally accepted sigil.

### Iconography

No surviving image can be identified as the Third Messenger with the certainty possible for a labeled classical deity. Manichaean art from Turfan, Dunhuang, and southern China includes solar and lunar disks, celestial courts, rows of light-deities, sermon scenes, elect wearing white, and elaborate cosmological diagrams. Some figures have been proposed as the Messenger, but most lack surviving captions.

The medieval **Manichaean Diagram of the Universe** and related Chinese paintings visualize the layered cosmos and its divine administration; identification of an individual solar Buddha as the Third Messenger remains an iconographic reconstruction unless a label survives.

Manichaean art itself is securely documented. Mani’s lost *Image* or *Arzhang* reportedly provided pictorial instruction, while surviving book fragments, textiles, and wall paintings chiefly date from the Uyghur and Chinese periods. Zsuzsanna Gulácsi’s reconstruction of a continuous Manichaean visual tradition is influential, but it does not produce a certain third-century portrait of this deity.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin and hierarchy

The Third Messenger is not born sexually. He is “called,” “evoked,” or emanated by the **Father of Greatness** after the Mother of Life, First Man, and Living Spirit appeal for further aid. He heads the third salvific deployment.

Three important arrangements survive:

1. **Theodore bar Kōnay:** Messenger → Twelve Virgins → Jesus the Splendour.
2. **Kephalaia 7:** Third Messenger → Column of Glory/Perfect Man, Jesus the Splendour, and a Virgin of Light, followed by Light-Nous, Light Form, and three angels.
3. **Kephalaia 16/25:** the Messenger appears with the Column and revealed light-powers, but the membership and order differ.

Calling the Twelve Virgins his “daughters” is widespread in summaries but should be treated as systematizing kinship language. “Emanations,” “powers,” or members of his evocation are more consistently secure.

There is no securely attested consort in the ordinary sense. The Virgin or Maiden of Light is a collaborating emanation, not demonstrably a wife. No ordinary biological offspring are attested.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Father of Greatness — emanation:** the Father calls forth the Third Messenger in response to the crisis of mixed Light. *Kephalaia* 7, 16, 25; Theodore bar Kōnay, drawing on older material. **[Early core/Canonical]**

- **Mother of Life — joint petition and cosmic alliance:** with the First Man and Living Spirit, she appeals to the Father for the next salvific intervention; the Messenger emerges as the answer. Theodore bar Kōnay’s cosmogony. **[Canonical preservation of early material]**

- **First Man — joint salvific system:** the First Man’s lost light-elements are the substance the Messenger helps recover; he participates in the appeal for the third evocation. Theodore; Coptic systematization. **[Canonical]**

- **Living Spirit — succession and cooperation:** the Living Spirit constructs the cosmos; the Messenger activates it as a machine of purification. They are adjacent but distinct heads of the second and third evocations. Theodore; *Kephalaia*. **[Early core]**

- **Twelve Virgins of Light — emanation/commission:** the Messenger brings them forth or directs their display before the archons. Theodore; later summaries. **[Canonical, with variants]**

- **Virgin or Maiden of Light — joint seduction of the archons:** she reveals beautiful forms, sometimes acting as the Messenger’s principal agent and sometimes apparently replacing the twelvefold group. Coptic and Syriac traditions; Ephrem; Iranian hymns. **[Regional/canonical variant]**

- **Jesus the Splendour — emanation and cooperation:** Jesus is commonly produced within or from the Messenger’s evocation and subsequently awakens Adam. *Kephalaia* 7; Theodore; Iranian and Coptic texts. **[Canonical]** Western Manichaeism sometimes magnified Jesus until he functionally displaced the Third Messenger.

- **Column of Glory / Perfect Man — emanation or coordinate power:** one *Kephalaia* scheme has the Messenger produce the Column; another lists them together. *Kephalaia* 7 and 16. **[Competing canonical systematizations]**

- **Light-Nous — downstream emanational relation:** in *Kephalaia* 7, Jesus the Splendour proceeds after the Messenger and the Light-Nous follows Jesus. **[Canonical systematization]**

- **Light Form — downstream relation:** the Light Form and three angels belong to the soul’s postmortem rescue sequence descending from the third evocation. *Kephalaia* 7. **[Canonical systematization]**

- **Great Architect — commission:** the Third Messenger commands the Great Architect, a deity of the second evocation, to construct the New Paradise and a prison for the enemies of Light. Coptic systematic tradition summarized in the Manichaean pantheon. **[Canonical elaboration]**

- **Male archons — erotic stratagem:** luminous female forms arouse them so that they emit seed containing Light. Mani’s *Treasure* as quoted by Augustine; Theodore; Iranian hymns. **[Early core]**

- **Female archons — erotic stratagem:** luminous youths arouse them; they discharge captive Light or miscarry their demonic fetuses. Same corpus. **[Early core with variant outcomes]**

- **King of Darkness / Prince of Darkness — indirect antagonism:** the Messenger’s action deprives the realm of Darkness of captured Light and prompts countermeasures leading toward human creation. Connected cosmogonies. **[Canonical]**

- **Āz, Concupiscence/Matter — antagonistic imitation:** Āz or the demonic female principle preserves and reincorporates fallen Light, driving generation and the creation of humanity. Iranian and heresiological accounts. **[Regional/canonical]**

- **Ašaqlūn / Saklas — imitation of the divine image:** the male human-forming demon makes Adam after the beautiful heavenly form displayed by the Messenger. Manichaean Genesis traditions. **[Canonical]**

- **Namrāʾēl / Nebrōel — joint human creation:** the female demon mates with Ašaqlūn and contributes to the formation of Adam and Eve as prisons for Light. **[Canonical]**

- **Adam — archetypal-image relation:** Adam’s body is formed in imitation of the Messenger’s displayed image. Coptic and Iranian Manichaean traditions. **[Canonical]** A competing early strand makes the First Man, not the Third Messenger, Adam’s archetype; Nils Arne Pedersen and John Reeves have stressed that this conflict may reflect ancient diversity rather than mere scribal confusion.

- **Eve — indirect archetypal relation:** human sexual differentiation and reproduction continue the demonic response to the Messenger’s male-and-female image. **[Canonical interpretation]**

- **Mithra/Mihr — regional equation:** Parthian and Sogdian communities identify the Messenger with Mihr, giving the figure solar and possibly judicial traits. **[Syncretistic identification]**

- **Nairyō.saŋha/Narisah — regional equation:** Iranian communities give the Messenger the name of the Zoroastrian divine herald. **[Syncretistic identification]**

No securely documented narrative meeting exists between the Third Messenger and Mandaean named beings such as Manda d-Hiia or Hibil Ziwa. Similarity is not interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Third Evocation

**Earliest layer: [Early/Mani].** Following the invasion of Darkness, the First Man’s descent, his rescue, and the Living Spirit’s construction of the cosmos, much Light remains trapped in demonic bodies. The Mother of Life, First Man, and Living Spirit appeal to the Father of Greatness. The Father evokes the Third Messenger.

The Messenger surveys the cosmic prison and sets its celestial machinery in motion. The sun and moon become ships; the Column of Glory provides an ascending route; subordinate light-powers carry and refine the recovered substance.

**Development:** The basic sequence is stable, but membership of the Third Evocation varies. Theodore gives the Messenger, Twelve Virgins, and Jesus; Coptic texts add or rearrange the Column, one Virgin, Light-Nous, Light Form, and angels.

### The seduction of the archons

This is the defining narrative.

1. Demonic rulers, male and female, are bound or suspended in the heavens.
2. They contain Light swallowed during the primordial conflict.
3. The Messenger or his powers displays forms of ideal beauty.
4. Male demons see radiant virgins; female demons see beautiful beardless youths.
5. Desire causes male archons to ejaculate and female archons to miscarry or discharge the contents of their bodies.
6. Purified Light rises toward the celestial ships.
7. Matter still contaminated with Darkness descends through heat or cold and enters vegetation, animals, and reproductive processes.
8. Demonic powers attempt to swallow or recapture the released Light.
9. The terrestrial cycle of eating, sex, birth, and death becomes the next battlefield of separation.

#### Source variants

- **Augustine, *De natura boni* 44:** plural “powers of Light” transform into both sexes; the passage is explicitly attributed to book seven of the *Treasure*. Purified souls ascend to the luminous ships, while contaminated residue descends into plants. **[Early/Mani through hostile quotation]**

- **Theodore bar Kōnay:** the Messenger and Twelve Virgins belong to the connected sequence. **[Medieval witness to early Syriac tradition]**

- **Ephrem:** the Maiden of Light is prominent, sometimes as a single gender-changing figure. **[Fourth-century hostile variant]**

- **Iranian hymns:** Narisah/Mihr and the powers of Light defeat or confuse the archons; the liturgical register can subdue the explicit physiology. **[Regional liturgical variant]**

- **al-Bīrūnī:** preserves a learned Islamic-period account of the displayed luminous forms. **[Medieval report]**

Augustine’s account is polemical but cannot be dismissed as invention: he quotes a named Manichaean book, and independent Eastern witnesses confirm the myth’s structure. His disgust is evidence about Christian reception, not a neutral explanation of Manichaean intent.

### Creation of humanity after the Messenger’s image

The demonic powers see the Messenger’s glorious form and seek to retain Light by reproducing that image. Ašaqlūn and Namrāʾēl consume or concentrate the offspring of the archons and generate Adam and Eve. Humanity thus bears a celestial image but is materially fashioned by demonic powers and functions as a reproductive prison for Light.

**Competing archetypes:**

- **Third Messenger version:** Adam is modeled on the radiant male-and-female image revealed in the third evocation. **[Dominant surviving Manichaean version]**
- **First Man version:** heresiological material suggests Adam was patterned after the First Man. **[Ancient variant]**

The second version should not be “corrected” automatically. It may preserve a rival early recension of the myth.

### Operation of the ships and Column

Recovered Light rises through the Column of Glory, is loaded into the moon, transferred to the sun, and conveyed beyond the visible cosmos. Lunar waxing and waning are the visible rhythm of loading and unloading. The Messenger’s solar work is regular, reliable, and cosmically beneficent; Iranian hymns praise these traits in language also associated with Mithra.

### Construction of the New Paradise

The Third Messenger orders the Great Architect to build the New Paradise and a prison for the powers of Darkness. This anticipates the end of mixture: purified souls and Light will dwell in safety while irredeemable Darkness is permanently enclosed. **[Canonical elaboration]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Was there a separate cult of the Third Messenger?

There is no evidence for an autonomous priesthood, temple institution, or civic festival devoted exclusively to him. His cult existed inside the Manichaean church.

Evidence includes:

- Coptic psalms addressed to the Third Messenger;
- Parthian and Middle Persian hymns to Narisah or solar Mihr;
- benedictions naming Narisah among the saving gods;
- prayers directed toward the sun and moon as visible residences and symbols of divinity;
- cosmological instruction through recitation and sacred pictures;
- the ritual meal of the Elect, understood as freeing Light from plant food.

### Prayer and astral orientation

Manichaean laity were encouraged to pray toward the sun and moon. This does not establish crude worship of physical objects: the luminaries were divine vehicles, manifestations, and stations in the saving apparatus. The distinction was clear internally but easily portrayed by opponents as sun worship.

### Ritual meal and liberation of Light

Elect consumed selected plant foods believed to contain abundant Light. Digestion, prayer, hymn-singing, and disciplined purity assisted liberation. Hearers supplied the food and received merit. Since the Third Messenger had initiated the large-scale extraction of Light, the ritual meal continued his cosmic work at the human level.

### Sexual discipline

Elect were celibate. Reproduction was understood as binding Light into additional bodies. Hearers could marry but were discouraged from procreation in stricter formulations. The erotic celestial myth therefore did not authorize ritual sex; its point was to turn demonic desire against Darkness.

Augustine and other opponents alleged obscene Manichaean rites, including consumption of sexual fluids. These accusations must be separated from the securely attested cosmogony:

- the sexualized celestial extraction is securely documented;
- mainstream Manichaean ascetic opposition to intercourse is securely documented;
- claims of a semen eucharist are polemical and associated with alleged splinter groups or confessions under hostile judicial conditions;
- no secure evidence establishes such a rite as ordinary Third Messenger worship.

### Temples and institutions

Manichaean congregations used churches, monasteries, and teaching halls from Mesopotamia to China. Archaeological remains are strongest in Central Asia and China:

- Uyghur temple complexes at Kocho/Turfan;
- illuminated manuscripts and wall paintings from the Turfan expeditions;
- Dunhuang manuscripts;
- the Cao’an sanctuary in Fujian, now famous for its statue of Mani as Buddha of Light;
- Xiapu ritual manuscripts preserving heavily sinicized Manichaean traditions.

None can presently be called a dedicated “Temple of the Third Messenger.”

### Sacrifice and contested material

No human sacrifice is documented in his cult. No animal sacrificial system is securely associated with him; Manichaean ascetic ethics generally opposed killing animals. No temple prostitution is documented. The seduction narrative is cosmological, not evidence of performed sacred prostitution.

Claims that Manichaeans practiced ritual sexual extraction are **hostile allegations**, not archaeological facts. Modern retellings that present the seduction as a liturgical sex rite are **modern distortion** unless tied to a specific, critically evaluated source.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure and Cult

### c. 240–276: formation under Mani

**[Early/Mani]** Mani formulated the three-stage cosmic drama in Sasanian Mesopotamia. The Third Messenger probably belonged to the system from an early stage because the figure is embedded in Mani’s *Treasure*, Iranian cosmology, and the Syriac tradition behind Theodore.

Translation began during Mani’s lifetime. Iranian names such as Narisah were not necessarily survivals of a pre-Manichaean cult inside Manichaeism; they were adaptations of the new cosmological role to recognizable Iranian divine vocabularies.

### Late third–fourth century: rapid expansion and polemical visibility

Manichaeism spread west into the Roman Empire and east through Iran and Central Asia. Alexander of Lycopolis already knew the third salvific “power.” Ephrem and Augustine show that the erotic extraction myth was prominent enough to become a primary polemical target.

In Egypt, Christian vocabulary and the prominence of Jesus reshaped the third evocation. In Latin Western Manichaeism, Jesus could functionally replace or absorb much of the Messenger’s role. **[Regional Christianization of terminology]**

### Fourth–sixth centuries: systematization and suppression

The Coptic *Kephalaia* stabilized several competing arrangements of the pantheon. Manichaean communities continued to sing hymns to the Messenger.

Roman and Byzantine laws treated Manichaeism as a dangerous foreign or heretical religion. Confiscation, exile, capital penalties, and destruction of books progressively eliminated its institutions in the West. These measures suppressed the entire church, not a distinct Third Messenger cult.

### Sasanian and Islamic Iran

Mani died under Bahrām I, traditionally in 276 or 277, following royal and priestly hostility. Later Sasanian persecution forced communities eastward and westward. Under Islamic rule, Manichaeans at first remained visible; Abbasid anti-*zindīq* campaigns in the eighth and ninth centuries brought interrogation, execution, and concealment.

Arabic heresiographers preserved the cosmology while recategorizing many gods as angels. The Messenger’s Iranian identities remained known but ceased to support a major public cult.

### Central Asia and the Uyghur state

Manichaean missions translated the pantheon into Parthian, Sogdian, Bactrian, and Old Uyghur. The conversion of the Uyghur qaghan at Luoyang in 762/763 gave Manichaeism unprecedented state patronage. Temples, monasteries, scribal institutions, and illuminated books flourished.

In this environment the solar Mihr/Narisah form of the Messenger received hymnic prominence. Surviving manuscripts are frequently products of this later Central Asian efflorescence even where the compositions are older.

The collapse of the Uyghur steppe empire in 840 weakened the church, although Manichaeism persisted in the Turfan kingdom for centuries.

### China

Manichaeism was known at the Tang court by 694 and formally summarized in 731. It was permitted primarily for foreigners, especially Sogdians and Uyghurs. Uyghur patronage led to monasteries in Chinese cities.

The anti-Manichaean measures of 843, followed by the broader Huichang persecution of 845, closed temples and exiled or executed foreign clergy. Chinese adherents survived clandestinely, increasingly adopting Buddhist and Daoist vocabulary. The Messenger became a “Buddha” or solar light-envoy within a transformed pantheon.

Southern Chinese traditions survived into the Song, Yuan, and perhaps Ming periods. Fujian ritual texts discovered in modern scholarship contain genuine Manichaean strata, but it is not established that the Third Messenger remained individually recognized without interruption.

### Present

There is no securely documented continuous, self-conscious institutional worship of the Third Messenger from antiquity to the global present.

Small modern groups identifying as Manichaean exist online and in diaspora contexts. A “Central Manichaean Temple” website presents the Third Messenger as a still-coming or continuously revealing divine messenger. Its teaching differs materially from the ancient cosmological deity: it can portray the title as eschatological or prophetological and cite newly organized “Gospel of Mani” material. This is **[Modern revival/reconstruction]**, not evidence for an uninterrupted ancient church.

Chinese local devotion connected with Mani, Lin Deng, Cao’an, and Xiapu deserves separate classification: it contains historically transmitted Manichaean elements but is deeply integrated with Buddhist, Daoist, and local ritual traditions. Evidence for a discrete surviving cult of Narisah/Third Messenger is absent.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

The Third Messenger has had less independent modern reception than Mani, “Manichaean dualism,” or the Maiden of Light.

### Scholarship and comparative religion

- Ferdinand Christian Baur’s nineteenth-century work treated Manichaeism largely through Christian heresiologists.
- Discovery of Turfan manuscripts from the beginning of the twentieth century transformed the Iranian and Central Asian picture.
- The Medinet Madi codices, discovered in 1929, supplied internal Coptic theology.
- The Cologne Mani Codex, acquired in 1969 and dated palaeographically to the fifth century, revolutionized understanding of Mani’s early life but adds little direct information about this deity.
- Hans Jonas used the seductive revelation of celestial form as a classic example of Gnostic cosmic drama.
- Geo Widengren emphasized Iranian and Mesopotamian backgrounds.
- Mary Boyce, Walter B. Henning, Ilya Gershevitch, and Werner Sundermann developed competing accounts of Mihr/Narisah terminology.
- John C. Reeves and Nils Arne Pedersen reopened the problem of whether Adam’s archetype was the First Man or Third Messenger.
- Zsuzsanna Gulácsi reconstructed Manichaean visual culture without claiming a securely identified portrait for every god.

### Esotericism and occultism

The being does not have a substantial documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the principal twentieth-century ceremonial-magical orders. Modern websites sometimes insert the Messenger into generic “Gnostic pantheons,” but these classifications usually derive from modern comparative tables.

No ancient grimoire, magical papyrus, planetary seal, invocation, or talisman of the Third Messenger has been identified. Internet “sigils” or ritual correspondences assigned to him are **[Modern invention]** unless their creator explicitly presents them as new.

### Literature, games, and popular culture

Manichaeism appears in historical fiction and strategy games; the Third Messenger is occasionally summarized in online lore as an androgynous seducer or solar god. These portrayals often collapse him with the Maiden of Light and treat “the seduction of the archons” as one uniform story. That composite is a **modern retelling** assembled from Augustine, Ephrem, Theodore, and Iranian fragments.

The generic use of “Manichaean” to mean starkly dualistic usually erases this being entirely.

### Political appropriation

No well-documented nationalist or extremist movement has made the Third Messenger a central emblem. Iranian cultural reclamation sometimes treats Narisah as evidence of a distinctively Persian religious heritage, but the underlying deity originated inside Mani’s transregional Aramaic-Iranian system.

Claims that Cathars or Bogomils continuously worshipped the Third Messenger are unproven. Similar dualist structures do not establish institutional or doctrinal continuity, and medieval anti-heretical sources do not normally name him.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is “Gnosticism” the correct category?

Hans Jonas and Kurt Rudolph placed Manichaeism within a broad Gnostic history. Michael Williams and Karen King challenged “Gnosticism” as an overly reified category. For this dossier, “Gnostic” is a modern comparative label; **Manichaean** is the securely historical identity.

The Third Messenger is absent from the Nag Hammadi corpus. Similarities with its luminous humans, archons, and emanations may reflect shared late-antique traditions, indirect borrowing, or parallel exegetical development.

### Origin of the seduction myth

Proposed backgrounds include:

- Genesis 1:26–27, especially the heavenly image as male and female;
- Genesis 6 and Enochic Watcher traditions;
- the luminous Anthropos desired by Nature in *Poimandres*;
- Valentinian and other Christian-Gnostic emanational systems;
- Iranian solar and divine-messenger traditions;
- Mesopotamian cosmology.

No single-source derivation commands consensus. Mani’s version is a synthetic construction whose surviving witnesses already show variation.

### Who performs the seduction?

Three models coexist:

1. the Third Messenger personally reveals male and female forms;
2. the Twelve Virgins act at his command;
3. a single Maiden/Virgin of Light performs a gender-variable display.

They should not be harmonized into a fictitious fixed scene. The common core is the strategic exhibition of divine beauty to extract captured Light.

### Narisah, Mihr, or both?

The terminology is the most important philological dispute.

- **Boyce:** popular Parthian solar Mithra encouraged identification with the Messenger.
- **Gershevitch:** the evidence does not permit that simple causal argument; Sogdian solar Mithra is itself especially Manichaean.
- **Sundermann:** divergent missionary terminologies developed—Mani’s Middle Persian system and Mār Ammō’s Parthian system—and were later combined in Sogdian.

“Narisah” is therefore not the deity’s universal ancient personal name. It is a regional Iranian equation.

### Adam’s heavenly model

Most surviving Manichaean texts make Adam resemble the Third Messenger. Some hostile witnesses give the First Man as model. Earlier scholarship often treated the latter as error; recent work takes seriously the possibility of genuinely competing early mythologies.

### Reliability of Augustine

Augustine is:

- hostile and rhetorically aggressive;
- a former Hearer with substantial practical knowledge;
- capable of conflating mainstream doctrine with allegations against dissidents;
- uniquely valuable when he quotes a named Manichaean book.

His chapter 44 quotation cannot simply be rejected, but neither should his moral language be mistaken for the Manichaeans’ interpretation.

### Primary evidence and losses

Lost or fragmentary:

- Mani’s original Syriac *Treasure*, *Pragmateia*, *Living Gospel*, and most letters;
- the original *Arzhang*;
- most Western Manichaean books destroyed during persecution;
- large portions of the Medinet Madi codices damaged after discovery;
- many Turfan fragments lacking joins or provenance.

Principal surviving repositories include Berlin collections, the Chester Beatty Library, Cologne’s papyrus collection, the British Library, St Petersburg collections, and Chinese, Japanese, and Central Asian museums and libraries.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- that the Third Messenger appears in the Nag Hammadi *Apocryphon of John*;
- that he is simply the Mandaean Manda d-Hiia under another name;
- that “Narisah” proves the whole figure was borrowed intact from Zoroastrianism;
- that the celestial seduction was enacted as routine temple prostitution;
- that a standardized ancient Third Messenger sigil survives;
- that Cathars preserved his cult unchanged;
- that modern online Manichaean scriptures automatically transmit otherwise lost verbatim books of Mani.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 216:** traditional year of Mani’s birth; no Third Messenger evidence yet.
- **c. 240:** Mani begins public preaching. **[Context]**
- **240s–270s:** Mani composes the Syriac *Treasure of Life*, *Pragmateia*, and other scriptures; the third evocation and extraction myth belong to this doctrinal corpus. **[Earliest reconstructable attestation]**
- **Third century:** Middle Persian and Parthian translation generates the Narisah and Mihr terminology. The exact sequence remains disputed.
- **c. 276/277:** Mani dies under Bahrām I; his scriptures and missionary church survive.
- **Late third/early fourth century:** Alexander of Lycopolis describes a third divine “power.”
- **Fourth century:** Ephrem attacks the luminous sexual stratagem; Manichaean teaching spreads through Syria, Egypt, and North Africa.
- **Late fourth century:** Coptic *Kephalaia* traditions probably receive substantial editorial formation.
- **c. 404–405:** Augustine writes *De natura boni*; chapter 44 quotes book seven of Mani’s *Treasure* on beautiful virgins, youths, captive Light, and luminous ships.
- **Fourth–fifth century:** surviving Medinet Madi codices are copied in Coptic.
- **Fifth century:** approximate palaeographic date of the Cologne Mani Codex; it does not supply a major independent Third Messenger narrative.
- **Fifth–sixth centuries:** Roman and Byzantine persecution progressively extinguishes public Western Manichaean institutions.
- **Seventh–eighth centuries:** Manichaeism expands through Sogdian networks into China; the Messenger is translated through Iranian and Buddhist vocabularies.
- **694:** conventional date for an early recorded Manichaean presence at the Chinese court.
- **731:** Chinese *Compendium of the Teachings of Mani, the Buddha of Light* is prepared for the Tang state.
- **762/763:** conversion of the Uyghur qaghan; Manichaeism receives state patronage.
- **Late eighth century:** Theodore bar Kōnay compiles the Syriac *Book of Scholia*.
- **Ninth–eleventh centuries:** many surviving Turfan manuscripts and paintings are copied; hymns praise Narisah/Mihr.
- **840:** collapse of the Uyghur steppe empire weakens state support.
- **843–845:** Chinese suppression closes temples and disperses clergy; Manichaeism becomes clandestine and increasingly sinicized.
- **987–988:** al-Nadīm’s *Fihrist* records Mani’s books and doctrine.
- **c. 1030:** al-Bīrūnī preserves Manichaean cosmological material.
- **Twelfth–fourteenth centuries:** Manichaean survivals continue in southern China; Cao’an develops as an important Mani sanctuary.
- **Sixteenth century:** possible terminus for recognizable organized Chinese Manichaean survival; the evidence is regional and disputed.
- **1902–1914:** German Turfan expeditions recover Iranian, Sogdian, and Uyghur Manichaean manuscripts and art.
- **1929–1931:** Medinet Madi codices are discovered and acquired.
- **1969:** Cologne Mani Codex is acquired from the antiquities market.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** new editions, joins, and Chinese ritual discoveries refine the picture; small modern revival groups reinterpret the Third Messenger, without demonstrable institutional continuity.
