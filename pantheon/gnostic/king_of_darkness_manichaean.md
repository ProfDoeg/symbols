# The King of Darkness (Manichaean): Research Dossier

**Class:** Primordial demonic ruler and personification of evil Matter; in some Manichaean witnesses a single arch-demon commanding five dark realms, in others the collective or composite embodiment of their five rulers. Manichaeans did not ordinarily classify him as a god worthy of worship. Christian opponents called Manichaeism “ditheist” because Darkness was uncreated, but the Manichaean bishop Faustus denied that the evil principle possessed divinity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The being belongs to Mani’s third-century cosmology. The earliest independent witness to its underlying doctrine is Alexander of Lycopolis’s Greek *Against the Doctrines of Mani*, composed in Egypt around 300 CE, which reports evil Matter/Darkness as an independent primordial principle. The earliest securely attributable wording about a savage ruler in the world of smoke comes from Mani’s lost Syriac *Fundamental Epistle*—composed in the mid-third century but preserved in Augustine’s Latin quotations of the late fourth and early fifth centuries. The complete title “King of Darkness” survives in fourth-century polemical summaries and in later witnesses drawing on Syriac Manichaean material. The earliest substantial internal manuscripts describing him directly are the fourth-/fifth-century Coptic Manichaean codices from Medinet Madi, especially *Kephalaia of the Teacher* 6 and 27.

**Evidentiary warning:** “The King of Darkness” is a dossier-heading, not an invariant ancient proper name. The sources alternately distinguish and merge (1) primordial Darkness or Matter; (2) female Hylē/Matter or Lust; (3) the chief of the smoke-world; (4) five animal-shaped kings; and (5) a pentamorphous collective ruler. “Ahriman” and “Šimnu” are eastern missionary translations or identifications, not proof that Mani simply imported the Zoroastrian Ahriman unchanged.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

**[Secure early doctrine]** Mani taught two primordial, uncreated “natures” or realms: Light above, governed by the Father of Greatness, and Darkness below, populated by hostile powers. Darkness is not a fallen portion of the good deity. It precedes the cosmos and invades Light from outside. The ensuing mixture of light and dark produces the present world and human condition. This radical external dualism distinguishes Manichaean cosmology from Sethian stories in which Yaldabaoth emerges through Sophia’s error within an ultimately derivative divine genealogy. The Syriac cosmology preserved by Theodore bar Konai summarizes the initial opposition as the Father of Greatness with five worlds against the King of Darkness with five worlds. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Cosmogony and Cosmology iii”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cosmogony-iii/)

**[Internal classification]** The King is an arch-demon, evil ruler, source or mind of the dark realm—not a recipient of Manichaean devotion. Darkness is eternal as a nature, but some Islamic-era accounts distinguish that eternal nature from the composite Satan generated out of its elements. Calling him an “evil god” describes the logical problem perceived by opponents; it is not a neutral translation of every Manichaean classification. The Manichaean pantheon article notes both the accusation that the Prince was an independent deity and the internal insistence that only the Father of Greatness is properly God. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “The Manichean Pantheon”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-parent/manicheism-ii-the-manichean-pantheon/)

### Names and linguistic forms

- **Syriac:** ܡܠܟ ܚܫܘܟܐ, *malkā/mlek ḥeššōkā*, “King/Ruler of Darkness.” The exact vocalization varies in modern transcription. Theodore bar Konai preserves the title in a Syriac account probably dependent on older Manichaean writing.

- **Coptic:** ⲡⲣ̅ⲣⲟ ⲙ̅ⲡⲕⲉⲕⲉ, *p-rro m-p-keke*, “the king of the darkness,” in the Coptic Manichaean tradition.

- **Greek:** βασιλεὺς τοῦ σκότους, “king of darkness,” or ἄρχων, “ruler”; ὕλη, *hylē*, “matter,” often personified as female; πεντάμορφος, *pentamorphos*, “five-formed,” used by the sixth-century Neoplatonist Simplicius for the Manichaean evil principle.

- **Latin:** *princeps tenebrarum*, “prince/ruler of darkness”; *gens tenebrarum*, “race of darkness”; *materia* or *hyle* for Matter. Augustine’s *Against the Fundamental Epistle* refers to “the savage character of the prince” dwelling in smoke. [Augustine, *Contra epistulam fundamenti*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1405.htm)

- **Middle Persian and Parthian:** ʾhrmyn, reconstructed *Ahreman/Ahriman*. This is a translation-equation with the Zoroastrian adversary, not the original Syriac name.

- **Sogdian and Old Turkic:** *šmnw/šimnu*, derived ultimately from Buddhist terminology for Māra through Iranian missionary usage. It functions as “demon/adversary.”

- **Arabic:** ملك الظلمة, *malik al-ẓulma*, “King of Darkness”; شيطان, *Šayṭān*, “Satan”; in some accounts إبليس القديم, *Iblīs al-qadīm*, “the ancient Iblis.”

- **Chinese:** titles translating “King of Darkness” or “dark king” occur within translated Manichaean cosmology, although surviving Chinese texts more often render whole cosmological structures than preserve a fixed transliteration of the Syriac title.

### Hylē, Āz, Satan, and Ahriman

**[Secure western formulation]** Greek and Latin witnesses frequently make evil “Matter,” ὕλη. In older-looking traditions Hylē is female and is closely associated with Lust or Desire. She produces or mobilizes dark powers and participates in anthropogony.

**[Later canonical redaction]** *Kephalaia* 6 transfers attributes and actions of Hylē to the male King of Darkness. *Kephalaia* 27 still preserves a distinction between male and female powers and calls Matter the “Thought of Death” and mother of the dark powers. Werner Sundermann therefore argued that the compiler of chapter 6 combined two previously distinguishable leaders: Hylē and the King. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “The Manichean Pandaemonium”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-parent/manicheism-pandaemonium/)

**[Iranian syncretistic identification]** Eastern Manichaeans called the King Ahriman, while they could call the First Man Ohrmazd. These are functional translations into an Iranian religious vocabulary. Manichaean and Zoroastrian cosmologies nevertheless differ: Manichaeans rejected the idea that Ohrmazd and Ahriman were brothers originating from one higher principle, and the Manichaean adversary’s five-world anatomy has no simple Zoroastrian equivalent. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Dualism”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/dualism/)

**[Comparative conclusion]** The Manichaean King “owes a good deal to Iran, including his name” in Iranian texts, but he cannot simply be identified with the Avestan Angra Mainyu across all periods. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Ahriman”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ahriman/)

### Comparison with neighboring “Gnostic” beings

**[Modern scholarly comparison—not ancient identity]** Yaldabaoth, Saklas, Samael, the rulers in the *Apocryphon of John*, *Hypostasis of the Archons*, and *On the Origin of the World*, and the demiurgic rulers of Valentinian texts are not names of the Manichaean King. Their myths share hostile rulers, stolen light, counterfeit creation, and salvific revelation, but their genealogies differ. Yaldabaoth is normally a deficient product of Sophia and constructs the lower world; Mani’s Darkness is an independent primordial nature, while the ordered cosmos is constructed by powers of Light from defeated dark bodies.

The *Gospel of Truth*, *Tripartite Tractate*, *Gospel of the Egyptians*, *Zostrianos*, *Allogenes*, *Trimorphic Protennoia*, *Eugnostos*, and *Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth* contain no securely identifiable “King of Darkness” as Mani’s five-world ruler. Nor do the Berlin Codex, *Pistis Sophia*, Books of Jeu, Gospel of Judas, or *Poimandres* supply direct attestations. They belong in the comparative history of late-antique demiurgy, ignorance, archons, and matter—not in this being’s primary corpus. Recent scholarship explores possible two-way relations between Manichaean readers and Nag Hammadi texts, but resemblance is not identity. [“Towards a Manichaean Reading of the Nag Hammadi Codices”](https://lockwoodonlinejournals.com/index.php/jcscs/article/view/97)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest recoverable layer

**[Secure early material, indirectly preserved]** Mani lived from 216 to 274 or 277 CE and wrote primarily in Syriac. His *Fundamental Epistle* explicitly described the Prince of Darkness and his allied princes discussing the great light rising before them. Augustine preserves a quotation in which those rulers plan anthropogony so that captured light cannot escape. The original manuscript is lost, but the attribution to Mani is stronger than that of a generic later heresiological summary. [Augustine’s quotation](https://biblehub.com/library/augustine/writings_in_connection_with_the_manichaean_controversy_/chapter_46_name_unspeakable_doctrine.htm)

**[Earliest independent external testimony]** Alexander of Lycopolis, writing in Greek around 300, reports two opposed principles, God and evil Matter. His work does not preserve the later full five-animal portrait, but it securely establishes the underlying dualistic figure within a generation of Mani. The *Acta Archelai*, composed in Greek during the fourth century and surviving completely in a late-fourth-century Latin translation, compares Light and Darkness to two kings with separate territories. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Archelaus”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/archelaus-author/)

### Internal Manichaean corpus

1. **Mani’s canonical Syriac books, third century.** The *Living Gospel*, *Treasure of Life*, *Pragmateia*, *Book of Mysteries*, *Book of Giants*, *Letters*, and *Psalms and Prayers* are fragmentary or known from translations and hostile quotations. The *Fundamental Epistle*, although sometimes counted separately from the seven-book canon, is the clearest Mani-attributed witness to the Prince and anthropogony.

2. **Šābuhragān, third century.** Mani’s Middle Persian presentation to Šābuhr I survives in Turfan fragments. It uses an Iranian lexicon for the cosmic adversary and dark powers. Composition is contemporary with Mani; surviving manuscripts are centuries later.

3. **The *Kephalaia of the Teacher*.** An evolving teaching-collection presented as Mani’s answers, preserved in a large Coptic papyrus codex from Medinet Madi, conventionally dated to the early fifth century. Chapters 6 and 27 are central for the King/Hylē problem. Chapter 6 describes the five worlds, dark trees and rulers, the King’s composite properties, internal war, speech, and attack. Chapter 27 retains gendered male and female powers. The genre developed in the early Sasanian church rather than constituting verbatim stenography of Mani. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Kephalaia”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kephalaia/)

4. **Dublin Kephalaia.** A second Coptic kephalaia collection, also from Medinet Madi, preserves related teaching but is textually distinct. Its existence warns against treating “the Kephalaia” as one fixed canonical book.

5. **Coptic Psalm-Book and Homilies.** Fourth-/fifth-century Egyptian codices contain liturgical poetry and narrative material about the conflict, captured Light, demons, eschatological separation, and Mani’s church. They attest lived recital of the cosmology, though the King is not the object of hymnic praise.

6. **Cologne Mani Codex.** A miniature Greek parchment codex, probably fifth century, recounting Mani’s youth within a Judaeo-Christian baptist community. It illuminates the prophet’s formative setting but is not a major direct source for the King.

7. **Turfan corpus.** Middle Persian, Parthian, Sogdian, Bactrian, and Old Turkic fragments, chiefly copied between approximately the eighth and eleventh centuries, preserve scriptures, hymns, confessions, doctrinal summaries, and the eastern names Ahriman and Šimnu. Their discovery in the German Turfan expeditions of 1902–14 transformed study from heresiology into manuscript-based philology. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Turfan Expeditions”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/turfan-expeditions-2/)

8. **Chinese Manichaean texts.** The Dunhuang *Compendium* or “Fragment Pelliot,” the *Traité* (a long Chinese cosmogonic and ethical work), hymnscroll, and related fragments translate the system into Buddhist and Chinese religious language. They attest continuity of the dualist cosmology but extensive terminological accommodation. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Dunhuang”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/dunhuang-1/)

### External witnesses

- **Alexander of Lycopolis, ca. 300:** Greek philosophical critique; earliest outside report.
- **Acta Archelai, fourth century:** hostile Christian anti-biography and cosmological report.
- **Ephrem the Syrian, fourth century:** Syriac refutations preserving eastern terminology and myths.
- **Titus of Bostra, fourth century:** Greek/partly Syriac anti-Manichaean work.
- **Epiphanius, *Panarion* 66, ca. 375:** depends substantially on the *Acta Archelai*.
- **Augustine, 388–405:** *On the Morals of the Manichaeans*, *Against the Fundamental Epistle*, *Against Faustus*, *On the Nature of Good*, and related works. Hostile but exceptionally informed: Augustine had been a Manichaean Hearer for roughly nine years.
- **Simplicius, sixth century:** Neoplatonic critique employing *pentamorphos*.
- **Theodore bar Konai, late eighth century:** *Book of Scholia* preserves a compact Syriac cosmogony with the King and five worlds, probably based at least partly on genuine older Manichaean material. [Translation and discussion](https://pages.charlotte.edu/john-reeves/publications/unpublished-lectures-and-fragmenta/theodore-bar-konai-on-mani-and-manichaeism/)
- **Ibn al-Nadīm, *Fihrist*, 987/988:** Arabic account based on earlier Muslim and Manichaean sources; detailed but already adapted to Islamic terminology. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Fehrest iii”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/fehrest-iii-representation-of-manicheism/)
- **al-Bīrūnī and al-Shahrastānī, eleventh–twelfth centuries:** later Islamic summaries useful for comparison but not independent third-century evidence.

### Manuscript and edition history

The Medinet Madi Coptic codices, acquired in Egypt in 1929–30, include the *Kephalaia*, Homilies, and Psalm-Book. Their poor preservation and dispersal complicated reconstruction. Standard work includes Polotsky and Böhlig’s Coptic editions; Iain Gardner’s *The Kephalaia of the Teacher*; and Gardner and Samuel N. C. Lieu’s *Manichaean Texts from the Roman Empire*. The Chester Beatty Library preserves major portions of the Coptic Psalm-Book. [Survey of the codices](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/coptic-manichean-texts/)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Secure early/core]** His domain is primordial Darkness: a spatially lower, uncreated realm characterized by turbulence, appetite, aggression, ignorance, foulness, and internally divided life. It has no harmonious divine administration comparable to the five peaceful greatnesses of Light.

**[Canonical fivefold system]** The five worlds or elements are:

1. Smoke  
2. Fire  
3. Wind  
4. Water  
5. Darkness

Their inhabitants correspond to five bodily classes:

1. Bipeds or demons  
2. Quadrupeds, represented by the lion  
3. Flying creatures, represented by the eagle  
4. Swimming creatures, represented by the fish  
5. Reptiles, represented by the dragon

The correlations vary by witness. Some lists put the animal rulers in a different order; “demon,” “man,” or “biped” can occupy the first position. Timothy Pettipiece argues that the *Kephalaia*’s pervasive pentads reflect editorial “pentadic redaction,” so not every fivefold detail should automatically be assigned to Mani. The five worlds themselves, however, occur across Coptic, Syriac, Latin, Arabic, and Iranian witnesses.

### Physical form

**[Canonical but variant]** The composite ruler has a lion’s head, bird/eagle wings, dragon or serpentine trunk, fish’s tail, and four animal feet. Other descriptions make him the bipedal smoke-world king who commands four other animal kings. It is therefore unsafe to speak of one universally fixed anatomy.

**[Kephalaia elaboration]** Chapter 6 also associates the dark ruler with five sensory or material qualities—darkness, stench, ugliness, bitterness, and consuming heat/fire—and presents his body as incorporating the five realms. This is theological anatomy, not naturalistic portraiture.

### Powers and limitations

**[Secure early/core]**

- He perceives or desires the distant Light and initiates the invasion.
- He commands dark rulers and hosts, though his kingdom is intrinsically factious.
- He speaks to allied princes in the anthropogonic council.
- He or his agents organize captured light into Adam and Eve.
- His beings reproduce through lust, devouring, abortion, and violence.
- He cannot create light or stable harmonious order.
- Darkness’s dependence on captured Light eventually makes its powers vulnerable.
- He is neither omniscient nor morally free in the manner of the Father; the *Kephalaia* emphasizes the dark powers’ mutual incomprehension and inability to speak one language.
- At the end of the cosmic process, Darkness and unrecoverable mixture are permanently confined rather than converted.

### Symbols and correspondences

**[Attested]** Smoke, fire, destructive wind, turbid water, abyssal darkness, the five hostile animal forms, lust, greed, bitterness, stench, and blackness belong to his semantic field.

**[Not established]** No securely attested sacred day, number beyond the doctrinal pentad, color code beyond darkness/blackness, dedicated plant, independent emblem, sacrificial implement, or astral body belongs specifically to him. Sun and Moon serve the extraction and transport of Light and are therefore opposed to, not emblems of, the King.

### Iconography

**[Absence of secure identification]** No surviving ancient or medieval image has been securely identified as a portrait of the King of Darkness. Manichaean art survives from Uygur Central Asia and southern China, including manuscript illuminations, temple banners, cosmological diagrams, and images of Mani and Jesus. Dark demons may appear in narrative or cosmological registers, but no known figure can be labeled the five-formed King without interpretive uncertainty.

The thirteenth-/fourteenth-century Chinese *Manichaean Diagram of the Universe* depicts a structured cosmos and hell realms, but identifying any single monster as the primordial King requires reconstruction rather than an inscription. The contrast is significant: Mani, Jesus, Elect, donors, and celestial beings received recognizable devotional images; the King did not possess a cult icon.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin and status

**[Secure early/core]** The King has no birth from the Father of Greatness and no place in the light pantheon. The dark nature is uncreated. In some accounts the King is likewise primordial; in the *Fihrist*’s Islamicized philosophical formulation, however, Satan’s constituent elements are eternal while the composite Satan is not eternal “in his own person.” These formulations should not be silently combined.

**[Hylē version]** Female Matter/Hylē, Lust, or the Thought of Death may be the parent or generative source of dark rulers.

**[King-centered redaction]** In *Kephalaia* 6 the King himself incorporates or displaces Hylē’s productive role.

**[No attested consort]** No stable named consort is securely assigned to him. Pairing him romantically with Hylē is a modern harmonization of sources that often treat them as alternatives or overlapping personifications.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Father of Greatness — hostile opposition:** The King’s intended ascent prompts the Father to evoke the Mother of Life and the First Man; they do not meet in a bodily duel. Theodore bar Konai, drawing on older Syriac material, late eighth century; *Kephalaia* and wider canonical tradition. **[Secure canonical interaction]**

- **Mother of Life — indirect opposition:** She is evoked by the Father in response to the invasion and produces/summons the First Man. No direct conversation with the King survives. *Kephalaia*; Theodore bar Konai. **[Canonical; indirect]**

- **First Man/Original Man — battle:** The First Man descends with his five sons, confronts the five dark powers, is defeated, and deliberately gives his light-elements to them like poison concealed in food. The King’s precise personal participation varies. *Acta Archelai*; Theodore bar Konai; Coptic and Iranian texts, third-century myth preserved from the fourth century onward. **[Secure early/canonical]**

- **The five Sons or Elements of the First Man — capture and consumption:** Air, Wind, Light, Water, and Fire are swallowed or bound by matching dark powers. Their mixture begins cosmic history. Same corpus. **[Secure early/canonical]**

- **Living Spirit — defeat and cosmic construction:** The Living Spirit enters after the First Man’s rescue, overcomes or flays dark archons, and constructs heavens and earth from their bodies. Sources differ on whether the King himself is captured or whether subordinate rulers supply the material. Coptic, Middle Iranian, Theodore bar Konai, and Islamic accounts. **[Canonical variant]**

- **Call and Answer — rescue opposed:** The Call descends from the Living Spirit; the Answer rises from the First Man. Their union helps rescue him from Darkness. The King is the contextual adversary, not a speaking partner. Manichaean canonical cosmogony. **[Canonical; indirect]**

- **Third Messenger — sexual deception of the archons:** The Messenger displays male and female beauty. Male dark archons emit seed; female archons abort embryos. The King is ruler of the affected host, but many accounts do not name him as the individual seduced. Coptic Psalm-Book, Ephrem, Augustine, Theodore bar Konai, Šābuhragān fragments. **[Canonical with variant dramatis personae]**

- **Maiden of Light — seduction/deception:** She displays herself to male archons so that they release swallowed Light. Some accounts divide this function between the Maiden and the Third Messenger. **[Canonical regional/textual variant]**

- **Jesus the Splendour — revelatory enemy:** Jesus reveals the salvific pattern to Adam and awakens him against the dark rulers’ design. No secure direct duel with the King occurs. Coptic and Iranian texts. **[Canonical; indirect]**

- **Light-Nous/Mind — continuing opposition:** The Light-Mind and its five members awaken souls trapped through dark matter and counter the King’s continuing power in humanity. *Kephalaia* and eastern doctrinal texts. **[Later canonical systematization]**

- **Āz/Lust — identity, subordinate, or female counterpart:** In western sources Hylē/Lust may lead Darkness; Iranian sources personify Āz as the binding appetite active in the body. The relation ranges from near identity to generative female power to subordinate demon. **[Canonical variants and Iranian syncretism]**

- **Ahriman — translation-equation:** Eastern texts use Ahriman’s name for the Manichaean adversary. This is not a narrative meeting between two beings. **[Iranian syncretistic identification]**

- **Māra/Šimnu — translation-equation:** Sogdian and Turkic terminology identifies the adversarial office with a Buddhist demon-name. No narrative meeting is recorded. **[Central Asian syncretistic identification]**

- **Adam — creator and jailer:** In Mani’s *Fundamental Epistle*, the Prince and allied rulers plan Adam as a microcosmic prison for concentrated Light. The actual production involves male and female demons and their offspring. **[Secure early/canonical]**

- **Eve — creator and instrument of reproduction:** Eve is fashioned within the same dark anthropogony so that procreation will keep Light divided and embodied. **[Secure early/canonical]**

- **Hibil Ziwa — no Manichaean interaction:** The Mandaean savior descends among dark rulers including Ur and Šdum, but no Manichaean text makes him encounter Mani’s King. **[Absence of evidence]**

- **Yaldabaoth, Saklas, Samael, Sophia, Barbelo, Protennoia, Poimandres — no documented interaction:** These figures belong to separate Sethian, Valentinian, or Hermetic narratives. **[Absence of evidence; modern comparisons only]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The two primordial realms

**[Secure early/core]** Before the present cosmos, Light and Darkness exist separately. Light is harmonious, knowing, fragrant, peaceful, and ordered. Darkness is turbulent and internally violent. The realms touch at a boundary but are not derived from one another.

**Variant:** Some sources treat the evil principle abstractly as Matter; others personify its initiative as Hylē, Lust, Satan, or the King. Islamic-era formulations can distinguish eternal dark elements from a composite Satan.

### B. Discovery and invasion of Light

**[Secure early/core]** During its own internal turmoil, Darkness reaches the border and perceives the Light. Desire replaces internecine fighting with an upward assault. The King assembles his five worlds or rulers and attacks.

**Function:** The myth accounts for evil without deriving it from the good God. Evil enters the mixed cosmos through aggression from an independent exterior principle.

**Variant:** In some witnesses the King himself looks upward and commands the attack. In others Hylē or dark Matter generates the invading powers. The *Acta Archelai* describes Darkness as rising “by chance,” while later summaries emphasize envy, desire, or conscious strategy.

### C. The First Battle and the sacrifice of the First Man

**[Secure early/canonical]** The Father does not send his peaceful five greatnesses. He evokes the Mother of Life, who evokes the First Man. The First Man equips himself with five light-elements and descends. Outnumbered or strategically self-sacrificing, he allows the dark powers to swallow his sons. Light becomes mixed with Matter; because the demons now contain Light, the poison-like divine substance ultimately weakens and exposes them.

The First Man is rescued by the second evocation, but part of his light remains dispersed. This “Living Soul” in the material world becomes the object of subsequent salvation.

**Variant:** Sources disagree over whether “defeat” was foreseen sacrifice, genuine military loss, or both. Polemicists stress divine incompetence; Manichaean texts stress salvific strategy.

### D. Construction of the cosmos from defeated demons

**[Canonical elaboration]** The Living Spirit and his sons defeat dark rulers. Their skins become heavens; their bodies or bones become earth and mountains. Light remains trapped throughout the resulting machinery. Sun and Moon become ships that collect liberated particles.

**King’s role:** He initiates the crisis but does not necessarily manufacture the ordered universe. Modern claims that “the Manichaean Devil created the world” are imprecise: dark powers provide much of its material, while beings of Light organize that material into a salvific machine.

### E. Seduction of the archons

**[Canonical, multiply attested]** The Third Messenger or Maiden of Light displays radiant forms to the male and female rulers. Male demons ejaculate; their seed falls to earth. Female demons miscarry. Plants, animals, and further demonic bodies arise through the resulting transfers of light and matter.

**Variant:** Coptic, Syriac, Iranian, and Islamic witnesses distribute the action differently among the Third Messenger, Maiden, male archons, female archons, and abortions. The King is not invariably the seduced individual. Describing the whole episode as “the King’s seduction” exceeds the evidence.

### F. Creation of Adam and Eve

**[Secure early/canonical]** Alarmed by celestial mechanisms removing Light, dark rulers concentrate swallowed light into human bodies modeled on the divine image they glimpsed. Adam and Eve are therefore double: their body and reproductive impulses belong to Darkness, while their imprisoned soul/light belongs to the divine realm.

The *Fundamental Epistle* has the Prince ask the assembled rulers what the rising light resembles and proceed with a “wicked invention.” Jesus the Splendour later awakens Adam, explains his condition, and gives saving knowledge.

**Variant:** The King may command the process; Hylē, Āz, Saklas-like rulers, male and female demons, or a particular pair of archons perform it. The resemblance to Sethian anthropogony is real, but shared motifs do not make the Manichaean King identical with Yaldabaoth.

### G. Continuing rule in the microcosm

**[Canonical ethical systematization]** The King’s primordial violence persists within bodies as lust, greed, anger, ignorance, and attachment. The Elect’s discipline and sacramental meal liberate light from food; Hearers support them and seek improved rebirth or eventual deliverance.

This is not a doctrine that all physical acts worship the King. It is a diagnosis of embodiment as mixture.

### H. Final separation

**[Canonical eschatology]** At the end, a great conflagration lasting 1,468 years in some sources separates most remaining Light. Irrecoverable matter, demons, and recalcitrant souls are enclosed in a final “bolos” or mass. A stone or seal prevents renewed invasion. Primeval coexistence is not restored in precisely its original form: Darkness is permanently confined and Light secured. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Manichean Eschatology”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/eschatology-ii/)

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Was the King worshipped?

**[Negative finding]** No temple, priesthood, festival, offering, prayer, votive inscription, theophoric name, oracle, or sacrifice dedicated to the King of Darkness is known. Manichaeans narrated, resisted, and ritually extracted themselves from his realm. “Cult of the King of Darkness” is therefore misleading.

### Manichaean institutions in which his myth operated

**[Documented church practice]** The church had five principal grades conventionally rendered Teacher, Bishop, Presbyter, Elect, and Hearer. Elect observed rigorous celibacy, dietary rules, nonviolence, poverty, prayer, and fasting; Hearers supplied food and material support.

**[Ritual meal]** Elect consumed specially presented plant foods. The rite was understood to release imprisoned Living Soul/light through the purified Elect. It was the reverse of an offering to Darkness: matter lost its captured light through the meal.

**[Prayer]** Communities prayed facing celestial lights as visible instruments or dwellings of divine power, not to the King. Opponents regularly characterized this as solar and lunar worship, but Manichaean explanations distinguished the luminaries from the ultimate Father.

**[Confession and fasting]** Eastern communities used extensive confessional formularies such as the Sogdian/Old Turkic *Xwāstwānīft*, confessing injury to the five light elements and failures of discipline. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Confessions”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/confessions/)

**[Bēma]** The annual Bēma festival commemorated Mani’s passion and judgment. It involved a month of observance culminating in readings, hymns, vigils, fasting, confession, a meal, and an empty raised seat representing Mani’s presence. It was not a festival of the King. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Bēma”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bema-festival-manicheans/)

### Temples and material settings

Manichaean meeting places and monasteries are documented at Kellis in Egypt, in the Turfan/Qocho region, and in China. Cao’an near Quanzhou became a famous southern Chinese sanctuary whose surviving image venerates Mani as the “Buddha of Light.” Its later Buddhist framing is evidence of adaptation and preservation, not a surviving shrine to the dark ruler.

### Sacrifice and alleged crimes

**[No evidence for human sacrifice]** Neither Manichaean internal texts nor credible archaeological evidence attest human sacrifice to the King or any other Manichaean being.

**[Polemical allegation]** Christian and later Muslim opponents accused Manichaeans of sexual rites, ritual semen consumption, infanticide, and magical crimes. Such allegations follow recurring heresiological stereotypes also directed at other minorities. They are not corroborated by Manichaean documentary, liturgical, or archaeological evidence and must not be reported as cult practice.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure and Religion

### 216–277: Formation

**[Secure historical setting]** Mani developed his movement in Sasanian Mesopotamia from a Judaeo-Christian baptist environment. His original language was Syriac; his theology engaged Christianity, Jewish apocalyptic traditions, Bardaisanite thought, Iranian religion, and, after travel eastward, Buddhist institutions and vocabulary.

The King’s earliest reconstructable form was probably the ruler/personification of an autonomous dark nature, accompanied by Hylē/Lust and five dark worlds. Exact allocation of gender and zoological form was already fluid.

### Late third–fourth centuries: Western spread and controversy

Missionaries carried Manichaeism through Mesopotamia, Syria, Egypt, Roman North Africa, and the Mediterranean. Alexander of Lycopolis, the *Acta Archelai*, Ephrem, Titus, and imperial legislation show that opponents regarded it as a serious competitor.

Diocletian’s anti-Manichaean rescript, conventionally dated 297/302, ordered severe punishment and destruction of writings, framing the religion as a Persian threat. Christian emperors later imposed confiscation, exile, civil disabilities, and capital penalties. Suppression helped make hostile quotations indispensable witnesses.

### Fourth–fifth centuries: Coptic and Latin systematization

The Medinet Madi codices preserve elaborate Coptic teaching, liturgy, and myth. In North Africa, Augustine’s former membership gave his polemics unusual detail. Here the title *princeps tenebrarum* and the dark land’s physical descriptions became famous in Latin Christianity.

The zoological pentad and Hylē/King conflation become especially visible in this phase. This may reflect genuine older teaching, editorial pentadic organization, or both.

### Third–seventh centuries: Iranian translation

In Iranian languages the King becomes Ahriman; First Man becomes Ohrmazd; the Father may become Zurwān. These names made the system intelligible in an Iranian environment, but functions were reassigned. Manichaean polemic expressly rejected Zurvanite claims that good and evil were brothers.

Sasanian authorities alternated between toleration and persecution. Mani died under Wahrām I; later tradition construed his imprisonment and death as a passion. The vehemence of Zoroastrian and Christian opposition likely reflects doctrinal proximity and competition as much as raw numbers. [Survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iran-ix1-religions-in-iran/iran-ix12-manicheism/)

### Seventh–tenth centuries: Islamic rule and Central Asian expansion

Under early Islamic governments Manichaeans sometimes operated openly and sometimes faced prosecution as *zanādiqa*. Arabic authors translated the King as Satan or ancient Iblis and reframed the system in kalām’s language of substances, bodies, and eternity.

Sogdian missionaries transmitted Manichaeism along the Silk Road. The Uygur ruler Bögü Qaghan adopted it around 762/763, giving the church exceptional political patronage. In Central Asian texts the adversary could be called Šimnu, drawing on Buddhist demonology.

### Ninth–fourteenth centuries: China

After the collapse of the Uygur empire in 840, Manichaean institutions lost major sponsorship, but communities continued in Central Asia and southern China. Chinese authorities periodically prohibited the “Religion of Light”; adherents sometimes operated within Buddhist or popular religious categories. Chinese art emphasizes Mani, salvific teachers, congregations, and the ordered cosmos more than the King’s monstrous image.

### Paulicians, Bogomils, and Cathars

**[Disputed medieval reception]** Medieval polemicists frequently called later dualists “Manichaeans.” Older scholarship consequently constructed a line:

Manichaeans → Paulicians → Bogomils → Cathars.

Similarities include opposition between good and evil powers, criticism of material creation, ascetic elites, and docetic tendencies. Contacts between eastern and western dualists are possible, and Bogomil influence on some western dualist texts remains defensible.

The continuous institutional genealogy is nevertheless unproved. “Manichaean” was a reusable heresiological label; Paulician primary evidence does not reproduce Mani’s five worlds, First Man, light-elements, or pentamorphous King. Recent work can even dissociate Paulician divinity from Manichaean, Marcionite, Bogomil, and Cathar systems. [Cambridge, “Paulician Dualism Revisited”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-ecclesiastical-history/article/paulician-dualism-revisited/5E5A8DEDBC87597E92714D235D6FE6A3); [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Cathars, Albigensians, and Bogomils”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cathars-albigensians-and-bogomils/)

Thus medieval European Satan figures are not later attestations of the specific King unless the distinctive Manichaean complex can be demonstrated.

### Fifteenth century to present

Organized Manichaeism disappears from the secure record after late-medieval China. Cao’an and paintings preserved in Buddhist settings are cultural survivals, not proof of an unbroken Manichaean congregation.

Small modern internet communities call themselves Manichaean and reconstruct practice from translations. Their scale, succession, and continuity are unverified. A scholarly survey already cautioned that an online “Manichaean Church” could not simply be accepted as historical continuity. [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Manicheism: General Survey”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-parent/manicheism-1-general-survey/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### “Manichaean” as a metaphor

**[Early-modern and modern semantic afterlife]** In European languages, “Manichaean” came to mean any rigid division into absolute good and evil. This usage usually has no knowledge of the King, five worlds, Hylē, or Manichaean salvation. It is a reception of Christian anti-heretical memory, not survival of the mythology.

### Scholarship and esotericism

Renaissance and early-modern authors knew Manichaeism chiefly through Augustine and church histories. The King was consequently assimilated to the Christian Devil. Nineteenth-century histories often treated Manichaeism as a Persian-Christian hybrid and overemphasized Zoroastrian derivation.

Theosophy, comparative occultism, Jungian writing, and modern “Gnostic” movements sometimes use Ahriman, Yaldabaoth, Satan, and the Manichaean King as interchangeable names for materialism, ego, or the shadow. Such equations are **[modern syncretism]**, not ancient Manichaean teaching. Rudolf Steiner’s “Ahrimanic” principle, for example, belongs to Anthroposophy’s own twentieth-century system rather than the Manichaean five-world myth.

### Popular culture

Generic “King/Prince of Darkness” characters in fantasy, horror, music, games, comics, and Satanic imagery overwhelmingly descend from Christian and literary titles for Satan. Unless a work includes Mani, the Father of Greatness, five dark elements, First Man, or captured Light, verbal coincidence alone does not establish Manichaean reception.

Games and online lore increasingly borrow Manichaeism as a selectable “dualistic” religion. They often turn the King into a worshipped evil deity, assign him standardized heraldry, or make him co-omnipotent with the Father. These are **[modern inventions]** unsupported by Manichaean cult evidence.

### Political appropriation

“Manichaean worldview” is routinely applied to political rhetoric that divides humanity into good and evil camps. This is an analogy, often pejorative, not evidence that political movements invoke the historical King.

### Modern worship

**[Unestablished]** There is no reliable census or institutional documentation demonstrating a substantial present-day cult of the King. Self-described reconstructed Manichaean groups generally side with Light and treat the King as the adversary. Claims that they continue an underground lineage from medieval China, Catharism, or antiquity remain unproved.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### From hostile witnesses to internal texts

Before the twentieth century, Manichaeism was reconstructed principally from Alexander, the *Acta Archelai*, Ephrem, Augustine, later Christian heresiologists, Zoroastrian polemic, and Arabic bibliographers. This encouraged readings through Christian categories such as “heresy,” “Devil,” and “two gods.”

The Turfan discoveries, followed by the Medinet Madi codices and Cologne Mani Codex, established that Manichaeans possessed a sophisticated multilingual scriptural, liturgical, artistic, and institutional culture. Friedrich W. K. Müller’s identification of Turfan fragments as Manichaean was decisive. [Research history](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/waldschmidt-ernst-02/)

### Principal debates

1. **Is the King one being or five?**  
   The evidence supports no single answer. One model makes him chief of five animal rulers; another makes the five collectively the Prince; another gives the chief all five anatomical forms. Pettipiece warns that the exact zoomorphic composition cannot be recovered securely from one privileged source.

2. **King versus Hylē.**  
   Sundermann’s reconstruction sees *Kephalaia* 6 merging an older female Hylē with the male King. Chapter 27 and external Greek material preserve the distinction more clearly. This is a redactional hypothesis with strong textual support, not an explicit Manichaean historical statement.

3. **Iranian or Mesopotamian origin?**  
   Hans Jonas’s influential “Iranian type of gnosis” stressed two opposed external principles. Later scholars emphasize Mani’s Syriac, Judaeo-Christian, Mesopotamian environment and comparisons with Bardaisan. Iranian names and motifs are real, but the original system cannot be reduced to Zoroastrianism.

4. **What does “Gnostic” mean?**  
   Jonas and Kurt Rudolph placed Manichaeism within a broad Gnostic religion of alienated divine substance. Bentley Layton, Birger Pearson, Michael Williams, Karen King, John Turner, and Einar Thomassen refined or challenged the category. Williams’s *Rethinking “Gnosticism”* and King’s *What Is Gnosticism?* show how taxonomic labels can impose false unity. The King is securely Manichaean; calling him generically “Gnostic” does not make every Nag Hammadi archon his cousin in a single pantheon.

5. **Dependence between Manichaean and Mandaean traditions.**  
   The Mandaean *Right Ginza* contains a striking pentamorphous dark ruler. Henri-Charles Puech argued that this description depends on Manichaean tradition. The broader relation among Mani’s baptist background, early Mandaeism, and surviving Mandaean literature remains debated because Mandaean manuscripts are late and compositions layered.

6. **Paulician–Bogomil–Cathar continuity.**  
   Similar dualist structures and possible routes of transmission support limited influence hypotheses. The absence of distinctively Manichaean mythic names and mechanisms prevents proof of a continuous church.

7. **Can hostile sources be trusted?**  
   They must be controlled against internal texts. Augustine and Theodore preserve genuine vocabulary and quotations, yet frame them polemically. Epiphanius frequently repeats the *Acta Archelai* and is not always an independent witness. The *Fihrist* is invaluable but incorporates Islamic-era philosophical adaptation.

### Mandaean comparison

**[Regional comparative tradition]** Mandaean texts speak of the World of Darkness and beings such as Ur and Šdum. The *Right Ginza* 12.6 resembles the *Kephalaia*’s five-formed ruler; Hibil Ziwa’s descents supply Mandaeism’s own combat-and-rescue pattern. These are parallels and perhaps literary borrowings, not evidence that Ur or Šdum is simply another name for Mani’s King. The surviving Mandaean manuscripts are much later than Mani, even where their traditions may be older.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- That the King is Yaldabaoth under another ancient name.
- That Mani copied the entire figure directly from Zoroastrian Ahriman.
- That the King was worshipped in black-magic rites.
- That Manichaeans practiced human sacrifice or ritual infanticide.
- That a continuous Manichaean church passed unchanged through Paulicians, Bogomils, Cathars, occult orders, and modern neopaganism.
- That all dualistic religion or rhetoric is “Manichaean” in a historically genealogical sense.
- That the Nag Hammadi codices form part of the Manichaean canon.
- That the five-animal body is uniformly described in every early source.

## 10. Chronology

- **216 CE:** Mani is born in Sasanian Mesopotamia. **[Historical context]**
- **ca. 228 and 240:** Revelatory experiences later reported in the Cologne Mani Codex; Mani begins an independent mission. **[Internal biographical tradition]**
- **240s–270s:** Mani composes Syriac scriptures and the Middle Persian *Šābuhragān*. The primordial dark ruler belongs to this system. **[Secure early attribution]**
- **274 or 277:** Mani dies in Sasanian custody. **[Historically secure event; circumstances vary]**
- **Late third century:** Manichaean missions reach Egypt and the Roman world. **[Documentary and polemical evidence]**
- **ca. 300:** Alexander of Lycopolis reports the dualism of divine Good and evil Matter. **[Earliest independent external attestation]**
- **297/302:** Diocletian’s anti-Manichaean rescript orders leaders burned with scriptures and severe punishment of adherents. **[Imperial suppression]**
- **Fourth century:** Greek *Acta Archelai* compares Light and Darkness to hostile kings; Ephrem, Titus, and Epiphanius attack the religion. **[External attestation]**
- **ca. 370s–400s:** Medinet Madi textual traditions develop; Augustine participates as Hearer and later writes extensive refutations. **[Internal and external evidence]**
- **Early fifth century:** Principal surviving Coptic *Kephalaia* manuscript is copied; chapters 6 and 27 preserve competing King/Hylē arrangements. **[Secure manuscript evidence]**
- **Fifth century:** Cologne Mani Codex is probably copied. **[Manuscript evidence]**
- **Sixth century:** Simplicius describes the Manichaean evil principle as *pentamorphos*. **[External philosophical reception]**
- **Later sixth century onward:** Sogdian becomes a major eastern missionary language; confessional and doctrinal texts transmit the dark ruler under translated names. **[Eastern development]**
- **Late eighth century:** Theodore bar Konai preserves a Syriac five-world cosmogony. **[External witness with older source material]**
- **762/763:** Bögü Qaghan adopts Manichaeism in the Uygur empire. **[Political patronage]**
- **840:** Uygur imperial collapse removes major institutional support; eastern communities continue. **[Historical transition]**
- **987/988:** Ibn al-Nadīm completes the *Fihrist*, preserving detailed Manichaean teaching in Arabic. **[Islamic-era witness]**
- **Tenth–thirteenth centuries:** Paulicians, Bogomils, and Cathars are called “Manichaeans” by opponents; direct continuity remains disputed. **[Medieval reception]**
- **Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries:** Southern Chinese Manichaean cosmological paintings and sanctuaries attest late survival and Buddhist accommodation. **[Material evidence]**
- **After the fourteenth century:** Secure evidence for an organized Manichaean church disappears. **[Negative historical finding]**
- **Late nineteenth–early twentieth centuries:** Central Asian expeditions recover Manichaean texts and art; Müller identifies their religious affiliation. **[Modern rediscovery]**
- **1929–30:** Medinet Madi Coptic codices come to scholarly attention. **[Manuscript discovery]**
- **1969:** Cologne Mani Codex is recognized and announced, illuminating Mani’s baptist upbringing. **[Manuscript discovery]**
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** Critical editions, Coptic reconstruction, Iranian philology, and art history replace the old dependence on heresiology. Small online revival groups appear, without demonstrated ancient institutional continuity. **[Modern scholarship and reinvention]**
- **2026:** The King remains a reconstructed figure known from fragmentary internal scriptures, hostile quotations, multilingual translations, and comparative scholarship; no dedicated cult or securely identified portrait is known. **[Current evidentiary state]**

## 11. Sources

### Primary sources and editions

The essential source base consists of Mani’s quoted *Fundamental Epistle*; the *Šābuhragān* fragments; the Coptic *Kephalaia*, Psalm-Book, and Homilies; the Cologne Mani Codex; Middle Persian, Parthian, Sogdian, and Old Turkic Turfan fragments; Chinese Dunhuang texts; Alexander of Lycopolis; the *Acta Archelai*; Ephrem; Titus of Bostra; Epiphanius; Augustine; Simplicius; Theodore bar Konai; Ibn al-Nadīm; al-Bīrūnī; and al-Shahrastānī.

For English access, Gardner and Lieu’s *Manichaean Texts from the Roman Empire* usefully juxtaposes internal and external witnesses; its table of contents identifies dedicated sections for Alexander, the *Acta Archelai*, and the five worlds. [Cambridge front matter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805215/60900/frontmatter/9780521560900_frontmatter.htm)

Gardner’s *The Kephalaia of the Teacher* remains central for the Coptic text in translation and commentary. The Coptic Gnostic Library is relevant for the Nag Hammadi comparanda, but those writings are not direct witnesses to this being.

### Selected modern scholarship

Foundational and major interpretive works include Hans Jonas, *The Gnostic Religion*; Kurt Rudolph, *Gnosis*; Geo Widengren on Iranian religion and Manichaeism; Henri-Charles Puech on Manichaean and Mandaean cosmology; Mary Boyce on Middle Iranian texts; Werner Sundermann on the pantheon and pandaemonium; Michel Tardieu, *Manichaeism*; Samuel N. C. Lieu, *Manichaeism in the Later Roman Empire and Medieval China*; Jason BeDuhn, *The Manichaean Body*; Iain Gardner’s editions; Timothy Pettipiece, *Pentadic Redaction in the Manichaean Kephalaia*; and Zsuzsanna Gulácsi on Manichaean art.

For the broader category problem: Bentley Layton, *The Gnostic Scriptures*; Birger Pearson, *Ancient Gnosticism*; Michael Williams, *Rethinking “Gnosticism”*; Karen King, *What Is Gnosticism?*; John Turner on Sethianism; and Einar Thomassen on Valentinianism. Their work supports treating “Gnostic” as a contested scholarly classification rather than a single historically unified pantheon.
