# Iusaaset: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Egyptian goddess (*nṯrt*), specifically a Heliopolitan creator-goddess and personification of the female generative power or “Hand” of the self-creating Atum.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Middle Kingdom, reign of Senusret I/Sesostris I (approximately 1965–1920 BCE), in evidence for her Heliopolitan cult or sanctuary. Coffin Text Spell 660, transmitted on Middle Kingdom coffins, independently attests “Iusaaset-town” and its sacred acacia north of Heliopolis. Claims that the goddess herself is already named in the Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts are not secure: Pyramid Text Utterance 519 contains the locality and a goddess associated with it, but not an unambiguous personal name “Iusaaset.”

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Old or Middle Kingdom textual or material evidence.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** New Kingdom or later Egyptian development of established theological material.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** A local or temple-specific formulation.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Priestly harmonization, especially in Ptolemaic and Roman temple texts.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** Explicit combination or functional equation with another deity.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A defensible synthesis not stated in precisely that form by an ancient source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**

Dates below are BCE unless otherwise specified.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Iusaaset was a comparatively minor but theologically significant goddess of Heliopolis. Her principal function was not to act in an extended narrative cycle but to supply a female formulation of the creative potency already contained in Atum. Egyptian creation accounts could say that Atum generated Shu and Tefnut alone—by masturbation, by spitting, or by expectoration—yet later theology could personify the hand that stimulated him as a goddess. Iusaaset, Nebethetepet, Hathor, and the more generic “Hand of the God” could all occupy this position, separately or in compound forms.

The most precise formulation is therefore not simply “Atum’s wife.” She could be his hand, daughter, mother, consort, or the feminine component of his own androgynous completeness, depending on text and period. These relations are theological predicates, not a consistent biological genealogy.

Her importance lies disproportionately in this conceptual role. Evidence for an actual sanctuary, divine estate, personnel, processional equipment, and offerings confirms that she was more than a literary abstraction, but nothing indicates a popular or nationally dominant cult comparable to those of Isis, Hathor, or Mut.

### Name and writing

The name appears in variable hieroglyphic spellings conventionally transliterated:

- **Jwi̯=s-ꜥꜣi̯=s**, normalized by the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae.
- **Jw=s ꜥꜣ=s**, a common older Egyptological transcription.
- **Iusaas, Iusâas, Iousâas, Jusaas, Juesaes, Ausaas**, modern transcription variants.
- **Iusaaset/Iusaast**, expanded modern forms influenced by the feminine ending or goddess determinative; these are conventional rather than a uniquely authoritative vocalization.

The name is normally interpreted as a verbal sentence: **“She comes and grows great,” “As she comes, she becomes great,”** or **“When she comes, she is great.”** The TLA analyzes the Roman-period spelling as *Jwi̯=s-ꜥꜣi̯=s*. A German study of Egyptian royal naoi likewise translates it approximately, “Wenn sie kommt, ist sie groß.” This supports a derivation from *jwi*, “come,” and *ꜥꜣi*, “be or become great,” but ancient Egyptian did not write most vowels; “Iusaaset” is therefore not a recoverable ancient pronunciation.

The interpretation that the name expresses the growth or activation necessary for creation is **[MSR]**, especially associated with modern readings by Stephen Quirke and other Egyptologists. It is plausible but should not be mistaken for an Egyptian prose explanation of the name.

### “Saosis”

Modern reference works often give **Saosis** as a Greek name for Iusaaset. This identification requires caution. In Plutarch’s *De Iside et Osiride* 15, *Saōsis* is one reported name of the queen of Byblos—other authorities called her Astarte or Nemanous; Plutarch says Greeks would call her Athenaïs. Plutarch neither identifies this queen as Iusaaset nor discusses Atum’s hand there. The similarity may preserve an Egyptian name or later interpretive association, but “Saosis = Iusaaset” is not securely established by Plutarch. It is best labeled **[MSR/disputed]**, not an ancient Greek equation.

### Documented titles and descriptions

The following are attested or closely supported:

- **“Mistress/Ruler of Heliopolis”** (*ḥnwt n Jwnw*): New Kingdom inscriptions, including the sarcophagus of Seti I and monumental scenes. **[LC]**
- **“She who is in/resides in Heliopolis”** (*ḥryt-jb Jwnw*): Temple of Amada, reign of Thutmose III. **[LC]**
- **“Hand of the God”** (*ḏrt nṯr*): explicitly attached to the compound Nebethetepet-Iusaaset in Roman-period Deir el-Shelwit; the same theology occurs earlier in magical and temple material. **[LC/LS]**
- **“Hand of Atum”**: a healing spell identifies the protective hand with Atum’s hand and then names the Iusaaset sanctuary. **[LC]**
- **“Eye of Re”** (*jrt Rꜥ*): Roman temple text at Deir el-Shelwit. **[LS/SI]**
- **“Mistress of heaven”** and **“ruler of all the gods”**: Deir el-Shelwit, between Galba and Domitian. **[LS]**
- **“Venerable”** and **“powerful”**: the same text. **[LS]**
- **“Mistress of Heliopolis-feminine”** or Hathoric mistress of the female Heliopolis: Dendera geographical-theological processions identify Hathor as Iusaaset in Heliopolis. **[LS/SI]**
- **“Mother of the god,” “mother of the sun,” “god’s mother,” “great mother,”** and **“grandmother of the gods”** occur in scholarly summaries of temple theology. Individual passages must be parsed carefully because *mwt=f*, “his mother,” *mwt=j*, “my mother,” and royal mother-language can be ambiguous. **[LC/LS; disputed in particular passages]**
- **“Lady/Mistress of the acacia”** is a reasonable description of her association with the sacred *šnḏt* tree and grove but is less securely established as a recurrent formal ancient title than popular accounts imply. **[EA/LC association; title itself uncertain]**

No secure evidence was found for colors, weekdays, numbers, zodiacal rulership, gemstones, or modern “correspondences” assigned specifically to Iusaaset. Such lists are **[MI]** unless tied to an identified Egyptian text.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Old Kingdom background: what the Pyramid Texts do and do not establish

The Pyramid Texts were first inscribed in the pyramid of Unas near the end of the Fifth Dynasty and continued in Sixth Dynasty royal pyramids. They are the earliest large Egyptian religious corpus. Their Heliopolitan creation passages are indispensable background:

- Pyramid Text Utterance 527 and especially Utterance 600 describe Atum’s production of Shu and Tefnut from himself.
- Other passages use masturbation, hand, spitting, or expectoration imagery.
- Utterance 519, §1212, situates the deceased at Hetepet beneath the “tresses” or tree-growth of a goddess associated with **Iusaaset-town**.

What this proves is that the locality or sacred complex underlying the later cult existed in Old Kingdom religious geography, and that a female divine figure was associated with it. It does **not** securely prove that the goddess’s personal name was already written there. James P. Allen’s critical translation of the Pyramid Texts does not present Iusaaset as an independently named actor in the principal creation passages.

Accordingly:

- **Old Kingdom roots of the locality and theological setting:** **[EA]**
- **Iusaaset explicitly named as Atum’s hand in the Pyramid Texts:** **[MI or at least unproven]**
- **Later identification of the unnamed hand or local goddess with Iusaaset:** **[MSR/LC]**

### Middle Kingdom

#### Senusret I and Heliopolis

Evidence summarized from the Heliopolitan monuments and later temple inventory places worship of Iusaaset/Nebethetepet at Heliopolis by the reign of Senusret I. A Ramesside inventory tablet attributes construction or equipment of the goddess’s sanctuary to Kheperkare Senusret I. Because the tablet is later than the claimed foundation, it is evidence for Ramesside memory or archival tradition concerning a Middle Kingdom sanctuary, not an intact foundation deposit recovered in situ.

Senusret I extensively monumentalized the Re-Atum temple at Heliopolis; one of his obelisks remains at al-Matariyya. The deity’s sanctuary itself has not been securely recovered archaeologically. The best formulation is therefore:

> **Earliest secure named cultic horizon:** reign of Senusret I, based on Heliopolitan textual and inventory evidence whose surviving witnesses are partly later.

#### Coffin Text Spell 660

Coffin Text 660, CT VI 283r–284i, names:

- “the acacia (*šnḏt*) of Iusaaset-town, north of the Souls of Heliopolis,” and
- “the acacia north of Iusaaset-town and south of the Souls of Heliopolis.”

This is the strongest early evidence connecting her name or named district with an acacia and a precise Heliopolitan sacred landscape. The wording may designate a town, estate, or shrine-quarter bearing her name. It does not by itself narrate a myth in which Iusaaset literally transforms into a tree.

### New Kingdom corpus

#### Temple of Amada

A column scene from the reign of Thutmose III depicts the goddess anthropomorphically, with vulture headdress, cow horns and solar disk, holding *wꜣs* and *ꜥnḫ*. Its legend calls her “Iusaaset, who resides in Heliopolis.” This is among the earliest securely identified surviving images. **[LC]**

#### Seti I monuments

Iusaaset appears repeatedly in Seti I’s monumental theology:

- At Abydos she embraces or supports Seti, placing an ankh at his nose and conferring life. Her label situates her “in the midst of the Mansion/Temple of Menmaatre.”
- She appears in the Book of Gates on Seti I’s sarcophagus as “Iusaaset, ruler of Heliopolis.”
- These are royal-ritual encounters, not surviving stories about her independent adventures.

#### Great Harris Papyrus, BM EA 9999

Compiled at the end of Ramesses III’s reign, around 1150, the Great Harris Papyrus records royal benefactions to major cults. Its Heliopolitan section and vignette place Iusaaset among Re-Horakhty, Atum, and Hathor-Nebethetepet. Ramesses III says, in a translation of the relevant passage, that he built great sacred barks for “your great daughter, Iusaaset-Nebethetepet.” Elsewhere the text mentions the king’s coming to Iusaaset in connection with Hetepet and the Great Temple at Heliopolis.

The papyrus is direct evidence for:

- state-supported worship;
- processional barks;
- a compound Iusaaset-Nebethetepet identity;
- her classification as the great daughter of the solar creator in this context.

It does not establish that she was invariably Atum’s daughter rather than mother, hand, or consort.

#### Heliopolitan inventory tablet

A schist inventory tablet, now Turin and conventionally dated to Ramesses III, schematically records the equipment and plan of the Hathor-Nebethetepet sanctuary. It mentions cult statues, scepters, and a large wooden figure of Atum six cubits high. It is crucial for reconstructing the otherwise archaeologically elusive female cult adjoining Atum’s.

#### Healing spell

A magical-medical text translated as Borghouts no. 145 invokes:

> “that hand of Atum” which drives away storm in heaven and disturbance in Heliopolis, the victorious combatant protecting her lord and defending Re on the day of a great conflict north and west of the House of the Uraeus-Iusaaset.

The spell then applies the goddess’s arrival and expulsion of enemies to removing illness or impurity from a patient. This is one of the few sources giving Iusaaset an active protective action rather than a static genealogical position. **[LC]**

#### Papyrus of Seramon

The Twenty-first Dynasty mythological papyrus of Seramon, BnF inv. 53.1, depicts Re-Horakhty enthroned and protected by the two Heliopolitan goddesses Iusaaset and Nebethetepet, both wearing Hathoric crowns. This is especially valuable because it distinguishes them visually while pairing their functions. **[LC]**

### Late, Ptolemaic, and Roman temples

Iusaaset or compound forms occur at:

- Edfu;
- Dendera;
- Deir el-Shelwit;
- Armant/Hermonthis, “Southern Heliopolis”;
- Karnak and the Theban region;
- Deir el-Medina;
- Qurna;
- Abydos;
- oases and local temples where Heliopolitan theology was incorporated.

A Deir el-Shelwit text, dated between Galba and Domitian, reads in TLA translation:

> “Words spoken by Nebethetepet-Iusaaset, the Hand of the God, who created the Ennead, venerable and powerful, who resides in Southern Heliopolis, Eye of Re, mistress of heaven, ruler of all the gods.”

This is a late but exceptionally explicit theological résumé. It shows that by the Roman period the goddess could receive creative agency otherwise assigned to Atum: she herself “created the Ennead.” **[LS]**

Dendera texts identify Hathor, mistress of the female Heliopolis, as Iusaaset in Heliopolis, “whose appearance is hidden from gods and humans.” Edfu likewise knows forms such as “Hathor-Nebethetepet-Iusaaset.” These are temple-specific equations, not proof that the names were always interchangeable. **[SI/LS]**

### Texts named in the research brief but not demonstrably part of her corpus

- **Book of the Dead:** No securely identified spell in the standard New Kingdom recension names Iusaaset as an independent deity. General Heliopolitan and Atum traditions are relevant background only.
- **Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** It discusses creation through Ptah and incorporates Atum and the Ennead, but does not securely name Iusaaset. Any attempt to insert her as Ptah’s female principle is modern harmonization.
- **Contendings of Horus and Seth:** Iusaaset has no demonstrable role.
- **Turin King List:** A royal list, not a source for Iusaaset.
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** Egyptian solar and creator gods appear abundantly, but no securely indexed occurrence of Iusaaset under that name was established.
- **Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*:** its Saosis is a reported name of the Byblian queen in the Osiris story; her identification with Iusaaset is unproven.
- **Philae:** The surviving inscriptions prominently elaborate Isis, Hathor, and other goddesses, but no central or independent Iusaaset cult has been established there.

Absence from these famous corpora is significant: Iusaaset’s documentary center is Heliopolitan cult and temple theology, not the Osiris-Horus literary narratives.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

Iusaaset’s documented spheres are:

1. **Female creative potency:** the activated female element within the ostensibly solitary creator Atum.
2. **The divine hand:** particularly the hand used by Atum in self-stimulation.
3. **Cosmic generation and growth:** suggested by her name and her late title as creator of the Ennead.
4. **Heliopolitan sovereignty:** mistress or ruler of Heliopolis and a local sanctuary quarter.
5. **Solar protection:** Eye of Re, defender of Re, victorious against disturbance and illness.
6. **Sacred vegetation:** association with an acacia grove north of Heliopolis.
7. **Royal life and legitimation:** she embraces kings and grants the lifespan of Re and years of Atum.

Her common modern description as a generic “goddess of creation and acacia trees” is broadly serviceable but less exact than the evidence: creation, Heliopolitan place, and divine-hand theology are primary; “tree goddess” is an inference from a specific sacred grove.

### Form and recognition

Iusaaset is normally depicted as a woman:

- wearing a close-fitting dress;
- holding an ankh and a *wꜣs* scepter;
- wearing a tripartite or vulture headdress;
- crowned with cow horns and solar disk, often with uraeus;
- occasionally crowned by or marked with a scarab.

The Hathoric crown does not by itself identify her, since Hathor, Isis, Nebethetepet, and numerous local goddesses use it. A hieroglyphic caption is normally necessary.

The scarab may evoke Khepri, solar renewal, or Atum’s self-generation, but ancient captions explaining it do not survive. Assertions that it specifically represents “resurrection” are **[MSR]**.

Other head emblems recorded in temple imagery include a sistrum-naos and, less commonly, an ankh-like emblem. These are late and local rather than universal.

### Important surviving depictions

- **Temple of Amada, Thutmose III:** one of the earliest securely identified complete anthropomorphic representations.
- **Temple of Seti I, Abydos:** Iusaaset touching or embracing the king and presenting life to his nostrils.
- **Seti I sarcophagus/Book of Gates:** captioned ruler of Heliopolis.
- **Great Harris Papyrus:** color vignette with the Heliopolitan gods.
- **Papyrus of Seramon, Twenty-first Dynasty:** Iusaaset and Nebethetepet protect Re-Horakhty.
- **Karnak Eighth Pylon scene:** Ramesses III presents Maat to Amun-Re-Horakhty in the presence of Iusaaset and Thoth.
- **Later Edfu, Dendera, Armant, and Deir el-Shelwit reliefs:** compound and syncretic forms.
- **Private and royal statues:** inscriptions name estates, priestly offices, or devotion to her; British Museum EA25, a reused kneeling statue of Shedsunefertem, cites Vandier’s corpus but is not itself an image of the goddess.

No securely identified freestanding cult statue of Iusaaset from her Heliopolitan sanctuary is presently known.

### Sacred acacia

The *šnḏt* acacia in CT 660 stood in the ritual geography north of Iusaaset-town and near the Souls of Heliopolis. Later summaries call it the “oldest tree” or a “tree of life.” Those phrases are interpretive. The secure facts are the named acacia and its topographic relationship to her estate or district.

No primary evidence assigns her a unique animal. The cow-horn crown expresses Hathoric-solar identity; it does not prove that she was originally a cow goddess.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogical configurations

| Configuration | Evidence and date | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Atum creates alone; no consort is required | Pyramid Text creation passages, Old Kingdom | **[EA]** earliest securely attested framework |
| Iusaaset is Atum’s hand or female component | New Kingdom magical/temple formulations, explicit in later texts | **[LC]** |
| Iusaaset is Atum’s consort | Heliopolitan cult pairing and later theological interpretation | **[LC/MSR]** |
| Iusaaset is Atum’s daughter | Great Harris Papyrus calls Iusaaset-Nebethetepet the creator’s “great daughter” | **[LC]** |
| Iusaaset is Atum’s mother | Some temple epithets and readings of ambiguous mother-language | **[LS/disputed]** |
| Iusaaset is mother of Shu and Tefnut | Logical result when the hand becomes a female co-creator; some later temple formulations support maternity | **[LC/LS]** |
| Iusaaset is grandmother of the gods | Derived from maternity of Shu and Tefnut, whose children Geb and Nut produce Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys | **[LS/MSR]**, the title is later theology, not an Old Kingdom narrative |
| Iusaaset emerges from Geb’s head | Reported in modern summaries citing late temple theology | **[LS]**, but the exact primary passage needs renewed verification |
| Iusaaset and Nebethetepet are one goddess | Compound names and overlapping roles, especially Iusaaset-Nebethetepet | **[LC/SI]** |
| Iusaaset and Nebethetepet are distinct paired goddesses | Papyrus of Seramon and other monuments depict both separately | **[LC]** |
| Iusaaset is Hathor | Hathor-Nebethetepet-Iusaaset equations at Edfu/Dendera and related temples | **[SI/LS]** |
| Iusaaset is Nut, Mehetweret, Tefnut, or another mother/solar goddess | Occasional functional equations in late temple theology | **[SI/LS]**, never a timeless identity |

Philippe Derchain challenged Jacques Vandier’s treatment of Iusaaset and Nebethetepet as actual mothers of Atum’s children. Derchain emphasized that Atum remains a solitary creator and that the goddess is the creator’s own hand or generative function rather than a second biological parent. This is the principal historiographic dispute behind “mother of Shu and Tefnut.”

### Every documented divine interaction

The list below separates encounters from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Atum — creative union/identity:** Iusaaset is his divine hand, female generative component, consort, daughter, or mother according to different temple formulations; together they account for creation without eliminating Atum’s self-generation. New Kingdom magical text; Great Harris Papyrus; Ptolemaic-Roman temple texts. **[LC/LS; variants conflict]**
- **Atum — cultic pairing:** Iusaaset receives worship within the Heliopolitan domain of Atum and appears near him in the Great Harris Papyrus vignette. Twentieth Dynasty. **[LC]**
- **Re — protection in battle:** as the powerful hand, she protects her lord and defends or avenges Re during a great conflict near the House of the Uraeus-Iusaaset. New Kingdom or later healing spell, Borghouts no. 145. **[LC]**
- **Re — bodily identity:** called the Eye of Re at Deir el-Shelwit. Roman period. **[LS/SI]**
- **Re-Horakhty — protective attendance:** she stands behind or beside the enthroned solar god in the Papyrus of Seramon. Twenty-first Dynasty. **[LC]**
- **Amun-Re-Horakhty — joint royal scene:** Ramesses III offers Maat to Amun-Re-Horakhty while Iusaaset and Thoth attend; Karnak Eighth Pylon. Twentieth Dynasty. **[LC]**
- **Shu — maternity/co-creation:** in later theological readings she is the female agency through which Atum produces Shu. Explicit maternal status varies by source. **[LC/LS; disputed]**
- **Tefnut — maternity/co-creation:** the corresponding relation to Shu’s sister. **[LC/LS; disputed]**
- **Geb — descendant relationship:** when Iusaaset is mother of Shu and Tefnut, Geb is her grandson; “grandmother of the gods” follows through him. This is a genealogical deduction rather than a surviving narrative meeting. **[LS/MSR]**
- **Nut — descendant or syncretic identification:** Nut is likewise a grandchild in the expanded genealogy; late temple theology can assimilate Iusaaset to great mother/sky goddesses. No independent narrative meeting is known. **[LS/SI]**
- **The Ennead — creation:** Deir el-Shelwit explicitly calls Nebethetepet-Iusaaset “the Hand of the God who created the Ennead.” Roman period. **[LS]**
- **Nebethetepet — fusion:** compound forms Iusaaset-Nebethetepet or Nebethetepet-Iusaaset occur in the Great Harris Papyrus and later temples. **[LC/SI]**
- **Nebethetepet — paired distinction:** the Papyrus of Seramon places the two goddesses separately behind Re-Horakhty. **[LC]**
- **Hathor — equation:** Edfu, Dendera, Deir el-Medina, and other temples employ Hathor-Nebethetepet-Iusaaset combinations or present Hathor in the local role of Iusaaset. **[SI/LS]**
- **Thoth — joint attendance:** both attend the royal offering to Amun-Re-Horakhty at Karnak; no dialogue or separate relationship is preserved. **[LC]**
- **Osiris, Isis, Seth, Nephthys, Horus:** they may become her later descendants through Shu–Tefnut–Geb–Nut, but no specific mythic meeting, combat, seduction, rescue, or judgment between Iusaaset and any of them is securely documented.
- **Ptah:** no direct interaction is attested on the Shabaka Stone.
- **Apep/Apophis:** no securely named direct encounter was found. General Eye-of-Re combat should not automatically be converted into an Iusaaset-versus-Apep myth.
- **Astarte/Athena:** Plutarch’s use of Saosis beside Astarte and Athenaïs concerns the queen of Byblos. It is not secure evidence that Iusaaset interacted with or was equated with those goddesses.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Iusaaset has no surviving extended narrative comparable to the Contendings of Horus and Seth. Her “myths” are compact theological episodes embedded in ritual texts and images.

### The hand of Atum

**Early core [EA]:** Atum arises from the primeval condition and generates Shu and Tefnut from himself. Pyramid Texts describe the production through bodily acts including masturbation, hand action, spitting, or expectoration.

**Later elaboration [LC]:** The hand becomes grammatically and theologically female. Iusaaset, Nebethetepet, Hathor, or a generic “Hand of the God” can personify it. This preserves Atum’s solitary creation while articulating sexual complementarity within him.

**Late systematization [LS]:** At Deir el-Shelwit, the compound Nebethetepet-Iusaaset is not merely present at creation but explicitly “created the Ennead.”

The widespread modern story, “Atum married Iusaaset and together they conceived Shu and Tefnut,” smooths a variable theology into a conventional divine marriage. It is **[MI when presented as the sole ancient account]**.

### Grandmother of the gods

The sequence is:

Atum + his female creative potency → Shu and Tefnut → Geb and Nut → Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys.

If Iusaaset is treated as mother of the first pair, she becomes grandmother or great ancestress of the remaining Heliopolitan gods. The label “grandmother of the gods” is therefore a compact theological inference. It should not be retrojected into the earliest Pyramid Text version, in which Atum creates without a separately named female parent.

### The acacia of Iusaaset-town

CT 660 locates an acacia near Iusaaset-town and the Souls of Heliopolis. The text makes the grove part of the deceased’s sacred itinerary. Later writers treat it as the place where the gods were born, a world-tree, or the “oldest tree.” Those elaborations are plausible developments of Heliopolitan sacred geography but are not all stated in CT 660.

No source says securely that Iusaaset herself was born from, imprisoned in, or transformed into the acacia.

### Defender of Re and healer

The healing spell makes the hand of Atum a victorious female combatant who suppresses cosmic disturbance and protects Re. Her mythic victory is ritually reapplied: as she expelled the hostile force at Heliopolis, so she is asked to drive illness and impurity from the patient. This is an example of an Egyptian magical *historiola*, where divine precedent effects a present cure.

The hostile being is not clearly named in the surviving cited passage. Identifying it automatically as Seth or Apep is unwarranted.

### Royal embrace and gift of life

At Abydos and elsewhere Iusaaset embraces, touches, or stands behind the king, conveys the ankh to his nose, and grants him “the lifetime of Re and the years of Atum.” This is repeated visual ritual rather than a single narrative episode. Its function is royal renewal, legitimation, and incorporation into solar eternity.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Heliopolis

Her principal cult center was **Iunu/Heliopolis**, specifically a sanctuary, estate, or district associated with Iusaaset and Hetepet. Sources mention:

- Iusaaset-town;
- an acacia grove;
- a “House of the Uraeus-Iusaaset”;
- a divine estate;
- a sanctuary remembered as built or equipped under Senusret I;
- royal restoration or building under Ramesses II;
- major endowments under Ramesses III.

A regnal-year-40 monument of Ramesses II places a sanctuary of Iusaaset in the Estate of Re. Because Heliopolis was heavily quarried and built over, the sanctuary’s plan and foundations have not been securely isolated. Claims that “her only temple stood west of Heliopolis” summarize textual geography, not a completely excavated building.

### Cult equipment

The Great Harris Papyrus and inventory evidence attest:

- great sacred barks for Iusaaset-Nebethetepet;
- cult images;
- scepters and temple furnishings;
- association with Atum’s cult image;
- royal donations and maintenance.

These imply festivals or processions, although no exclusive Iusaaset festival calendar survives.

### Priesthood and estates

Titles connected with her include an **overseer of works in the estate of Iusaaset**, borne by Amenemwia. Heliopolitan high priests and officials could incorporate her cult into their portfolios. Evidence does not disclose an autonomous, hierarchically elaborate Iusaaset priesthood comparable to that of Amun.

### Offerings and royal ritual

Temple scenes show:

- food offerings;
- incense and libation;
- presentation of Maat;
- royal approach to the goddess;
- her return gifts of life, dominion, health, jubilees, and solar longevity.

These are standard Egyptian temple exchanges, locally articulated through her Heliopolitan identity.

### Wider geographical spread

Her appearances at Amada, Abydos, Qurna, Karnak, Edfu, Dendera, Armant, Deir el-Shelwit, and oasis temples show diffusion of Heliopolitan theology. They do not necessarily establish independent local congregations devoted primarily to her.

### Sacrifice, sexuality, and contested practices

- **Animal and food offerings:** implied by ordinary temple ritual, but no sacrifice distinctive to Iusaaset is known.
- **Human sacrifice:** no textual, archaeological, or iconographic evidence associates it with her.
- **“Temple prostitution”:** no evidence associates it with her. Sexual creation imagery is theological and must not be converted into evidence for ritual intercourse or institutional prostitution.
- **Cult violence:** the healing spell employs divine combat imagery, but no historical violence carried out by her priesthood is documented.
- **Oracles, pilgrimage, incubation, personal votives:** no distinctive corpus has been established.
- **Theophoric personal names:** possible names invoking her occur much less frequently than names formed with Re, Amun, Ptah, or Isis; no quantitative study demonstrates broad popular penetration.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Old Kingdom: theological and topographic antecedents

The Pyramid Texts establish Atum’s self-generation, Heliopolitan divine geography, Hetepet, and a female divinity or sacred locale connected with what later becomes Iusaaset-town. An independently named Iusaaset cannot be demonstrated with equal security.

### Middle Kingdom: named cult and sacred landscape

By Senusret I’s reign, evidence places Iusaaset/Nebethetepet in the Heliopolitan cult complex. CT 660 documents Iusaaset-town and its acacia. This is the clearest emergence of her recoverable historical identity.

### New Kingdom: iconographic and institutional expansion

From Thutmose III onward:

- she appears in identifiable anthropomorphic images;
- she enters royal monumental programs;
- Seti I gives her prominent ritual scenes;
- Ramesses II records or supports a sanctuary;
- Ramesses III furnishes barks and endows the Heliopolitan gods;
- she appears separately from, and compounded with, Nebethetepet.

This is her best-documented period as a named cult figure.

### Third Intermediate and Late Period

Papyrus Seramon preserves her paired protective role around Re-Horakhty. Heliopolitan priests continued to invoke her; private statuary and divine estates provide indirect institutional evidence. Increasing assimilation to Hathor and other great goddesses makes her simultaneously more cosmically elaborate and less distinct.

### Ptolemaic and Roman periods

Temple theologians at Edfu, Dendera, Armant, and Deir el-Shelwit systematized her as:

- Hand of the God;
- Eye of Re;
- creator of the Ennead;
- Hathor-Nebethetepet-Iusaaset;
- sovereign of heaven and the gods.

This is not simply “decline.” Her name acquired unusually explicit cosmic predicates, though chiefly in learned temple liturgy.

### End of the temples

Egypt’s traditional temple institutions declined under Roman rule and lost imperial support during Christianization. Pagan public cults were progressively restricted in the fourth century CE; major temple institutions were closed, converted, or abandoned at different times. No decree or martyr narrative specifically names a suppression of Iusaaset’s cult. Her disappearance is visible as the disappearance of the temple system that transmitted her, not as a documented targeted persecution.

### Medieval to early modern period

No continuous medieval worship, folklore cycle, Islamicized local cult, magical survival, or demonization of Iusaaset has been established. Her name was effectively inaccessible until hieroglyphic decipherment and the publication of monuments. Claims of an unbroken secret tradition are **[MI]**.

### Nineteenth century to present

Modern Egyptology recovered her through inscriptions, museum collections, and temple publications. Jacques Vandier’s four-part 1964–1968 study remains the foundational collection. Contemporary Kemetic and eclectic pagan practitioners sometimes worship her, but there is no census or documented institutional lineage connecting those practices to the ancient cult. This is revival or reconstruction, not demonstrated continuity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Iusaaset never achieved the popular modern profile of Isis, Anubis, Horus, or Bastet. Her modern reception falls into several phases.

### Egyptological recovery

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century publications recorded her images and inscriptions at Abydos and other temples. Earlier scholars proposed uncertain translations of the name. Vandier assembled the first extensive dossier in *Revue d’Égyptologie* 16, 17, 18, and 20.

### Diffusion into comparative mythology

Gerald Massey’s *A Book of the Beginnings* (1881) treated Iusaaset through speculative linguistic comparison, linking her with Isis, Hathor, a coming “messiah,” and a supposed maternal chamber or womb encoded in her name. These etymologies do not meet modern Egyptological linguistic standards. **[MI/discredited comparativism]**

### Western occultism

No significant appearance of Iusaaset was found in the formative texts of Renaissance Hermetism, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Aleister Crowley’s Thelema, or the classic grimoire corpus. General Egyptian creator and solar symbolism later made it possible for eclectic occult writers to adopt her, but claims that she belonged to a continuous ancient mystery school or a canonical Golden Dawn pantheon are unsupported.

### Contemporary paganism and online religion

Modern devotees use spellings such as Iusaaset, Iusaas, Ausaas, or Iusaast and may treat her as:

- goddess of self-creation;
- divine feminine counterpart to Atum;
- goddess of acacia, fertility, personal growth, or ancestral motherhood;
- patron of autonomy or sexual creativity.

These are legitimate descriptions of modern devotional practice but are **[MI]** when represented as word-for-word ancient doctrine. Modern offerings, invocations, planetary correspondences, tarot associations, crystals, colors, and feast dates require contemporary attribution unless anchored in an identified Egyptian source.

### Popular misstatements

The following frequently circulate as ancient facts but require correction:

- **“She is named in the earliest Pyramid Texts.”** The locality and theological antecedents are early; the personal naming is uncertain.
- **“She was Atum’s wife and literally gave birth to Shu and Tefnut.”** One later configuration among several.
- **“Her Greek name was certainly Saosis.”** Possible but not proved by Plutarch.
- **“The acacia was universally the Egyptian Tree of Life belonging exclusively to her.”** Overgeneralization from a local sacred grove.
- **“She was worshipped continuously into modern times.”** No evidence.
- **“She appears in the Greek Magical Papyri or Golden Dawn rituals.”** Not securely demonstrated.
- **“Iusaaset is an ancient spelling.”** It is a modern vocalized transcription.

No documented nationalist, extremist, or major commercial appropriation specific to Iusaaset was found. Her name appears mainly in educational, neopagan, and mythology-reference contexts.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Vandier’s corpus

Jacques Vandier’s “Iousâas et (Hathor)-Nébet-Hétépet,” published in four installments between 1964 and 1968, remains the indispensable monument-by-monument collection. Its breadth also encouraged synthesis across texts of very different dates.

### Are Iusaaset and Nebethetepet one goddess?

Evidence supports both answers:

- Compound names and overlapping functions show fusion.
- Paired depictions show two distinguishable goddesses.
- Hathor can absorb either or both.
- Regional temple systems could select a single name or arrange them as complementary manifestations.

The history cannot be reduced to “originally one, later two” without qualification. The sources repeatedly alternate unity and plurality.

### Did she become the literal mother of Atum’s children?

Vandier accepted extensive maternal language. Derchain objected that Atum’s defining characteristic is solitary creation: the hand is a differentiated part of the creator, not a second reproductive parent. Later texts can nevertheless call the goddess mother or creator without respecting that philosophical distinction. The disagreement is partly about how much systematic consistency to impose on Egyptian temple theology.

### Etymology

“Coming and becoming great” is currently the most defensible linguistic analysis. Older explanations connecting the name with Isis, a womb, a messianic “coming son,” or Semitic words are obsolete or speculative.

### Goddess or place-name first?

The Pyramid and Coffin Text evidence leaves open whether Iusaaset first designated:

1. a goddess;
2. a locality or sanctuary;
3. a sacred tree/grove;
4. a phrase subsequently reanalyzed as a divine name.

The Middle Kingdom evidence already links place, tree, and deity closely enough that priority cannot be securely reconstructed. Statements that priests simply “invented” her to give Atum a wife are interpretive, not documented historical events.

### Evidence lost or inaccessible

Heliopolis suffered extensive stone-robbing, urban encroachment, and reuse of monuments. Much of the cult is known from:

- blocks found out of context;
- later copies of earlier building records;
- papyri now in European museums;
- references in temples far from Heliopolis;
- objects without secure findspots.

This survival pattern explains why literary theology is much clearer than the physical plan of her sanctuary.

### Important repositories and editions

- **British Museum:** Great Harris Papyrus BM EA 9999; Heliopolitan priestly statuary such as EA25.
- **Bibliothèque nationale de France:** Papyrus of Seramon, inv. 53.1.
- **Museo Egizio, Turin:** Heliopolitan inventory tablet and related material.
- **Louvre:** Late Period images and inscriptions of Hathor-Nebethetepet useful for the syncretic corpus.
- **TLA:** searchable transliterations, translations, dates, and metadata for Egyptian textual attestations.
- **IFAO:** temple publications, Heliopolitan fragments, and archaeological bibliographies.
- **Sethe, Faulkner, and Allen:** editions and translations of the Pyramid Texts.
- **de Buck and later translations/dictionaries:** Coffin Text corpus.
- **Grandet:** modern edition of the Great Harris Papyrus.
- **Leitz, *Lexikon der ägyptischen Götter und Götterbezeichnungen*:** systematic compilation of divine names and epithets.
- **Brunner, LÄ “Iu(e)s-aes”:** concise lexicographic treatment.
