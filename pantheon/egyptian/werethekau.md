# Werethekau: Research Dossier

**Class:** Egyptian goddess, initially discernible as a personified divine potency of royal crowns, uraei, and other ritually powerful objects; securely individualized as a goddess with images, priesthood, offerings, and cult places from the New Kingdom onward.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Pyramid Texts of King Unas, Saqqara, late Fifth Dynasty, approximately 2375–2345 BCE. In Unas’s Spell 153, *wrt-ḥkꜣw* (“Great of Magic”) addresses and personifies the Red Crown. This is the earliest secure occurrence of the expression, but not unambiguous proof that an autonomous goddess named Werethekau already existed.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Direct Old or Middle Kingdom evidence.
- **[New Kingdom attested]** Direct textual, iconographic, or institutional evidence from approximately 1550–1069 BCE.
- **[Later attested]** Third Intermediate, Late, Ptolemaic, Roman, or post-pharaonic evidence.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally specific manifestation or equation.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** An explicit compound, equation, or functional merger with another deity.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion inferred from the surviving evidence rather than stated by an Egyptian source.
- **[Modern invention/unverified]** A claim circulating in modern reference or devotional material for which no adequate ancient source has been located.
- **[Absence of evidence]** A requested feature for which the documented corpus presently supplies no secure example.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

Werethekau is Egyptian *Wr.t-ḥkꜣ.w*, conventionally vocalized **Weret-hekau**, **Werethekau**, **Urt-hekau**, or **Urethekau**. Grammatically, *wr.t* is the feminine “great one” or “great,” while *ḥkꜣ.w* is the plural or collective form of *ḥkꜣ*, the efficacious power usually translated “magic.” Accordingly, defensible translations include **“Great of Magic,” “Great One of Magic,” “Great in Magics,” “Great Enchantress,”** and **“She Who Is Great in Magical Power.”** “Great of Magic” best preserves the compact structure without implying that Egyptian *ḥkꜣ* was merely stage sorcery. **[Early attested]**

The expression is intrinsically ambiguous. It can designate:

1. a powerful object, especially a crown, uraeus, vulture or cobra amulet, Eye of Horus, or ritual implement;
2. an epithet of a king, deceased person, or another deity;
3. an autonomous goddess.

Ahmed Mekawy Ouda’s corpus-based work stresses that these uses cannot safely be collapsed. Writing, determinatives, context, accompanying epithets, priestly titles, and representation must decide each case. A limestone block such as Petrie Museum UC 16639, whose provenance and archaeological context are lost, can remain genuinely indeterminate between goddess, epithet, and object. [Ouda’s publication of UC 16639](https://student-journals.ucl.ac.uk/pia/article/id/153/) is the most explicit case study of this problem. **[Scholarly reconstruction]**

### Egyptian form and determinatives

The normalized transliteration is:

- **Wr.t-ḥkꜣ.w** — “Great of Magic.”
- Dual: **Wr.ty-ḥkꜣ.w** — “the Two Great-of-Magic ones,” commonly the paired crowns.
- Common older typographic forms include *Wrt-ḥk3w*, *Weret-Hekau*, and *Urt-Ḥekau*.

The name can be followed by determinatives of a seated woman, cobra, vulture, crown or paired crowns; it may also be written without a diagnostic determinative. Ouda identifies these graphic alternatives as a principal reason isolated attestations are difficult to classify. UC 16639 was once catalogued as mentioning Sobek because its damaged terminal sign was read as a crocodile; Ouda argues that it more closely resembles a cobra but still calls the reading uncertain. **[New Kingdom or later; disputed object]**

### Principal titles

Documented titles include:

- **nb.t ꜥḥ**, “Lady of the Palace.” Ouda argues that this was the characteristic epithet of Werethekau as an autonomous goddess. It appears with her on Tutankhamun’s shrine and probably on pendant JE 61952, in priestly titles, and in later monuments. Its significance is institutional: she belongs to the royal palace, regalia, accession, and bodily transformation of the ruler. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **nb.t ḥb-sd**, “Lady of the Sed-festival.” Attested on a statue base, Cairo N 127, probably from Amenhotep III’s jubilee complex at Kom el-Hitan. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **nb.t pt**, “Lady of Heaven.” Attested on Ramesside fragments from Aniba. **[Regional variant; New Kingdom]**
- **ḥnwt tꜣ.w nb.w**, “Mistress of all lands.” Also on the Aniba material. **[Regional variant; New Kingdom]**
- **nb.t ns.t tꜣ.wy**, “Lady of the Throne of the Two Lands,” or comparable throne title, on the Penamun stela and Amara West lintel. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- “Beautiful of face” and “sweet of love,” on the damaged Amara West lintel F9010. **[Regional variant; Ramesside]**
- “She who resides in the District of Kheput,” on the late Twenty-fifth/early Twenty-sixth Dynasty statue of Merymay from Kafr ed-Deir. **[Later regional attestation]**

### Werethekau as an epithet of other goddesses

The title was borne by numerous goddesses, including **Isis, Sekhmet, Mut, Wadjet, Hathor, Renenutet**, and locally **Pakhet** and **Bastet**. Such occurrences do not automatically prove that the named goddess “is” Werethekau in every context. They range from ordinary epithets to compound manifestations.

- **Isis-Werethekau:** Isis is repeatedly called “Great of Magic,” including in New Kingdom temple texts. This reflects her formidable ritual knowledge, but Ouda notes that the independent Werethekau’s title “Lady of the Palace” is not otherwise an Isis epithet. A monument that lists Isis and Werethekau separately therefore weighs against treating all Werethekau material as Isis. **[New Kingdom epithet; distinction securely attested]**
- **Sekhmet-Werethekau:** An inscribed statue from Amenhotep III’s Kom el-Hitan complex invokes “Sekhmet-Werethekau.” The combination unites leonine solar danger, protective power, and jubilee renewal. **[Syncretistic identification; New Kingdom]**
- **Pakhet-Werethekau:** At Speos Artemidos, Seti I’s revision of Hatshepsut’s decoration makes the local lioness goddess Pakhet-Werethekau perform coronation under Amun-Re’s authority. **[Regional syncretism; Eighteenth–Nineteenth Dynasty redaction]**
- **Bastet-Werethekau:** A Ramesside stela at Amara West shows Ramesses III offering wine to Amun, Bastet-Werethekau, and Horus of Buhen. **[Regional syncretism; Twentieth Dynasty]**
- **Wadjet/Werethekau:** The royal uraeus and Lower Egyptian crown make the two closely overlapping. The Pyramid Texts already call the Red Crown “Great of Magic,” but that does not mean every occurrence is a second name of Wadjet. **[Early functional overlap; later syncretistic interpretation]**
- **Nekhbet/Werethekau:** The vulture amulet and Upper Egyptian crown could likewise be “Great of Magic.” Dual crown language can bring Wadjet and Nekhbet into a single Werethekau-like conception without erasing their names. **[Early functional overlap]**
- **Mut-Werethekau:** Mut’s motherhood, crowns, and Theban queenship made “Great of Magic” an appropriate epithet. The equation becomes especially intelligible in royal nursing and coronation scenes. **[New Kingdom syncretistic identification]**
- **Renenutet-Werethekau:** The serpent form, nursing function, and plumes create overlap. Tutankhamun’s pendant has sometimes been described through Renenutet-like iconography, but its inscription identifies Werethekau. **[Iconographic affinity, not identity by default]**

No Greek or Roman author is known to supply a stable *interpretatio graeca* name for Werethekau comparable to the equation Isis–Demeter or Amun–Zeus. Greek **οὐραῖος**, “uraeus,” names the royal cobra-form, not Werethekau as a unique Greek goddess. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Pyramid Texts

The earliest secure physical witness is the inscribed pyramid of **Unas at Saqqara**, late Fifth Dynasty. In James P. Allen’s edition, Unas Spell 153 is headed “Commendation to the Red Crown.” The king comes to the Red Crown, addressed successively as Fiery One, Great One, and Great of Magic. The text later states that he comes as Horus “encircled by the aegis of his eye, the Great of Magic.” [Allen’s complete translation](https://www.siamcostumes.com/cutters_guides/pdf/the-ancient-egyptian-pyramid-texts-james-p-allen.pdf) preserves both the physical placement and textual sequence. **[Early attested]**

What this proves:

- *wrt-ḥkꜣw* was established royal-ritual language by the later third millennium BCE.
- The designation could personify and address the Red Crown.
- The crown’s power was linked to Horus’s Eye, fear, protection, and legitimate rule.

What it does **not** prove:

- that an independent goddess with her own sanctuary and clergy already existed;
- that every later Werethekau image descends from a single Old Kingdom anthropomorphic prototype;
- that the crown was exclusively Werethekau rather than a nexus through which several goddesses could act.

Later Pyramid Text witnesses expand the conception:

- In Merenre’s corpus, the Eye emerges on the royal head as the **Nile Valley Great-of-Magic Crown** and the **Delta Great-of-Magic Crown**. **[Early attested]**
- Pepi II’s texts call Horus’s two eyes the two Great-of-Magic crowns, refer to the Great-of-Magic uraeus and vulture, and assign Great-of-Magic crowns to Horus and Seth. **[Early attested]**
- Pepi II Spell 315 says Horus made the king’s magic great in his identity as Great of Magic. Here the expression is a transformed royal identity, not necessarily a separate female deity. **[Early attested]**
- Queen Neith’s Pyramid Texts repeat the dual-crown and Eye formulae. **[Early attested]**

These passages reveal the matrix from which the later goddess becomes legible: crown, cobra, vulture, Eye, magical efficacy, protection, and royal manifestation.

### Coffin Texts

The Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts reproduce and democratize much older royal mortuary language. Objects, eyes, crowns, and deceased persons can be *great of magic*. The corpus is important for the diffusion of the concept, but a securely individualized narrative role for a goddess Werethekau is much harder to establish than modern summaries imply. **[Early attested concept; autonomous goddess unproven]**

The most concrete Middle Kingdom institutional evidence consists of two holders of a title translated “god’s servant of the Werethekau-crown.” Ouda emphasizes that these officials handled and placed the royal crowns and belonged primarily to royal-cult administration. They are not secure evidence for a freestanding temple cult of the goddess. **[Middle Kingdom attested; classification disputed]**

### Book of the Dead

The Book of the Dead inherits the language of powerful eyes, uraei, crowns, amulets, and divine magical knowledge. Isis and other goddesses may be “great of magic.” No major, securely attributable Book of the Dead narrative centered on an autonomous Werethekau has been identified in the consulted critical material. Claims that she is a major guide through the Book of the Dead should therefore be treated cautiously unless a spell and manuscript are specified. **[Absence of evidence]**

### New Kingdom royal and temple texts

The decisive change occurs in the Eighteenth Dynasty.

- **Hatshepsut/Thutmose II, Karnak and Speos Artemidos:** Werethekau appears in coronation imagery, including the Red Chapel sequence and the lioness-form Pakhet-Werethekau. Ouda regards these as the earliest known depictions, around the early-to-mid fifteenth century BCE. The [TLA record for Hatshepsut’s coronation cycle](https://tla.digital/object/J4S3IRFDWJFZVKOWKIUIKWHEHY) names Werethekau among those attending the ritual. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Thutmose IV:** Ceremonial chair panels from KV43 place a lioness-headed Werethekau before the king, with Horus and Thoth also participating in the legitimating tableau. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Amenhotep III:** Statues and officials establish Werethekau’s cultic institutionalization. Her titles include “Lady of the Sed-festival”; Sekhmet-Werethekau appears among the king’s vast jubilee statuary. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Tutankhamun:** A gilded shrine repeatedly calls the king “beloved of Werethekau, Lady of the Palace.” Pendant JE 61952 depicts a human-headed serpent goddess nursing the king. Ouda’s study argues that the damaged base inscription should likewise be read as Werethekau “Lady of the Palace.” **[New Kingdom attested; reading partly reconstructed]**
- **Seti I, Karnak:** In the Great Hypostyle Hall, Werethekau places a crown on the king. The image provides one of the clearest representations of her coronation function. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Ramesses II:** She appears in royal inscriptions, at Abu Simbel, Wadi es-Sebua, and in the monuments of vizier Paser. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Ramesses III:** At Amara West the king offers wine to Amun, Bastet-Werethekau, and Horus of Buhen; an offering chapel at Bubastis also invokes her. **[Regional variant; New Kingdom]**
- **Ramesses XI:** Vizier Wennefer, shown or named in the rear chapel of Maat’s temple at Karnak, bears the title “first god’s servant of Werethekau.” **[New Kingdom attested]**

### Texts named in the brief that do not securely feature her

- **Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** No secure individual appearance of Werethekau has been established. Its theology of Ptah is relevant to her later Memphite associations but is not itself a Werethekau source. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Contendings of Horus and Seth:** The narrative does not give Werethekau a securely identifiable speaking or acting role. General connections with crowns and Horus’s Eye should not be inserted into the story. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Turin King List:** No direct Werethekau attestation; it is not evidence for her cult or myths. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*:** Plutarch discusses Isis, Osiris, Horus, Seth/Typhon, and Egyptian theology, but not an independently identifiable Werethekau. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** Isis and other Egyptian deities are called powerful magicians, yet no secure continuous cult or discrete Greek magical persona for Werethekau was located. Similar-sounding magical titles are insufficient identification. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Edfu, Dendera, Philae:** Their Graeco-Roman inscriptions contain extensive Isis, Hathor, Eye, crown, and uraeus theology, and the expression “Great of Magic” can function as an epithet. Each occurrence must be checked for whether it names Werethekau, another goddess, or an object. No major autonomous narrative cycle centered on her is presently established. **[Later learned systematization; classification contextual]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

Werethekau’s best-attested sphere is **the efficacious power of kingship embodied in crowns, uraei, palace regalia, coronation, royal appearances, jubilees, and divine nursing**. She does not merely “rule magic” in the generalized modern sense. Her *ḥkꜣ* is operative power: it protects the ruler, validates transformation into Horus, repels enemies, and makes regalia effective. **[Early and New Kingdom attested]**

Her domain developed in stages:

1. **Old Kingdom:** magically powerful crown, eye, uraeus, vulture, and royal condition.
2. **Middle Kingdom:** continued crown terminology and offices concerned with regalia.
3. **New Kingdom:** visible coronation and palace goddess with cult statues and priests.
4. **Ramesside period:** expanded offering cult, temples or temple departments, personal votive evidence, and spread into Nubia.
5. **Late and Graeco-Roman periods:** continued local cult and integration into increasingly elaborate crown and Eye-of-the-Sun theology.

### Forms

Documented forms include:

- **Crown itself**, especially the Red Crown and paired Upper/Lower Egyptian crowns. **[Early attested]**
- **Uraeus or rearing cobra.** **[Early concept; New Kingdom iconography]**
- **Full serpent with a human female head and torso**, as on Tutankhamun pendant JE 61952. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Cobra-headed woman**, as on Bristol Museum H 514 and Ramesside temple scenes. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Lioness-headed woman**, especially in coronation contexts and in overlap with Sekhmet or Pakhet. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Fully anthropomorphic nursing goddess**, as on statue Cairo CG 42002, identified by its back-pillar inscription. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Vulture or vulture amulet**, where “Great of Magic” qualifies the object or manifestation rather than necessarily depicting an autonomous goddess. **[Early attested]**

The cobra and lioness are not contradictory zoological biographies. Both belong to the Egyptian visual grammar of the fiery protective goddess and Solar Eye. A figure without an inscription cannot automatically be identified as Werethekau: Wadjet, Sekhmet, Mut, Renenutet, Meretseger, Pakhet, and other goddesses share these forms. **[Methodological conclusion]**

### Regalia and symbols

- **Red Crown, White Crown, Double Crown:** principal embodiments of her power.
- **Uraeus:** frontal royal cobra, capable of attacking enemies and protecting the brow.
- **Eye of Horus/Eye of Re:** theological mechanism by which protective female power emerges from or occupies the divine or royal forehead.
- **Sun disk, cow horns, and plumes:** worn in some images, reflecting affiliation with solar and maternal goddesses.
- **Papyrus or *wꜣḏ* sceptre:** held on the Penamun stela.
- **Ankh:** appears in standard scenes where she grants life.
- **Breast milk:** transfers divine vitality, kingship, and filial legitimacy to the ruler.
- **Crown chapel and palace:** characteristic institutional setting.

No securely attested sacred color, number, weekday, planet, or exclusive plant belongs to Werethekau. Modern lists assigning these should be marked modern correspondential systems unless they cite an Egyptian text. **[Absence of evidence]**

### Tutankhamun’s pendant, JE 61952

The pendant was recovered from KV62 and is dated to Tutankhamun’s reign, approximately 1332–1323 BCE. It is made from heavy gold plate and has suspension loops. The goddess rises in serpent form but has a female head, breasts, and arms; she nurses a small standing king. Her headdress comprises horns, disk, and plumes. [The object summary and inventory number](https://egypt-museum.com/pendant-of-tutankhamun-with-werethekau/) identify it as Cairo JE 61952. **[New Kingdom attested]**

Older descriptions sometimes identify the nurse as Isis or treat “Werethekau” merely as an Isis epithet. Ouda’s 2016 re-examination joins the pendant to Tutankhamun’s shrine, where “Werethekau, Lady of the Palace” occurs repeatedly. His proposed reading of the base makes the goddess Werethekau rather than an unnamed Isis-like cobra. Because parts of the inscription are damaged, “Lady of the Palace” on the pendant remains a reasoned epigraphic reconstruction; the shrine supplies strong contextual support. **[New Kingdom object; modern scholarly reinterpretation]**

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

No early Egyptian source supplies Werethekau with a stable birth story, parents, spouse, or offspring. Assertions that she was categorically the “daughter of Re” or “wife of Re-Horakhty” derive from treating her as a Solar Eye goddess or from particular syncretistic forms; they are not a universal genealogy. **[Absence of evidence; later theological inference]**

As an Eye or uraeus, she could be conceptualized as the emanated daughter and protective agent of a solar god. As a nursing goddess she could be the king’s divine mother. These are ritual relationships rather than a single family tree. **[New Kingdom and later theological construction]**

### Every documented named divine interaction or joint appearance

The following catalogue separates direct scenes from general association:

- **Horus —** The Unas Pyramid Texts identify the king approaching the Great-of-Magic Red Crown as Horus encircled by the aegis of his Eye. Later Pyramid Texts make Horus’s eyes the two Great-of-Magic crowns and say Horus magnifies the deceased king’s magic. *Pyramid Texts*, late Fifth–Sixth Dynasties. **[Early attested]**
- **Horus —** Werethekau appears with Horus in the royal tableau on Thutmose IV’s ceremonial chair; Horus grants life and dominion while Werethekau confronts or protects the king. KV43 furniture, approximately 1400–1390 BCE. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Horus of Buhen —** Ramesses III offers wine jointly to Amun, Bastet-Werethekau, and Horus of Buhen. Amara West stela, Twentieth Dynasty. **[Regional variant; New Kingdom]**
- **Seth —** Pepi II’s Pyramid Texts assign Great-of-Magic crowns to both Horus and Seth. This is an association of divine crowns, not a narrative meeting between an autonomous Werethekau and Seth. Sixth Dynasty. **[Early attested object/personification]**
- **Thoth —** Thoth joins Horus and Werethekau in the Thutmose IV chair scene, presenting millions of years of life and dominion. Approximately 1400–1390 BCE. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Amun/Amun-Re —** In Hatshepsut’s Red Chapel coronation cycle, Werethekau accompanies the royal installation conducted under Amun’s authority. Karnak, Eighteenth Dynasty. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Amun-Re —** At Speos Artemidos, Pakhet-Werethekau performs coronation under Amun-Re; Seti I altered part of Hatshepsut’s scene so that the goddess assumes the primary crowning action. Fifteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE, with Nineteenth Dynasty redaction. **[Regional syncretism]**
- **Amun —** Ramesses III offers wine to Amun, Bastet-Werethekau, and Horus of Buhen at Amara West. Twentieth Dynasty. **[Regional variant]**
- **Sekhmet —** Amenhotep III is named beloved of Sekhmet-Werethekau on a statue from Kom el-Hitan. Fourteenth century BCE. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Sekhmet —** On Paser’s stelophorous statue Louvre E 25980, Werethekau appears with Neith, Ptah, and Sekhmet; the gods receive paired offering formulae. Late Seti I–early Ramesses II. **[New Kingdom joint cultic appearance]**
- **Pakhet —** Pakhet-Werethekau crowns the king at Speos Artemidos. Hatshepsut/Seti I decoration. **[Regional syncretistic identification]**
- **Bastet —** Bastet-Werethekau receives wine from Ramesses III at Amara West. **[Regional syncretistic identification]**
- **Neith —** Paser’s Louvre statue gives a joint offering to Werethekau and “Neith the Great, Lady of Sais,” asking that they cause his name to prosper in their temple. Nineteenth Dynasty. **[New Kingdom joint cultic appearance]**
- **Ptah —** Paser’s statue places Werethekau in a Memphite group with Ptah, Sekhmet, and Neith. At Kafr ed-Deir, priests of Ptah serve “Ptah who resides in the temple of Werethekau.” At Wadi es-Sebua Ramesses II is beloved of Ptah and Werethekau in adjacent columns. **[New Kingdom–Late Period institutional association]**
- **Mut —** “Great of Magic” functions as an epithet or manifestation of Mut, especially where royal motherhood, crowns, and nursing converge. No discrete myth of a meeting between two separate goddesses is attested. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Wadjet —** The Lower Egyptian cobra and crown share Werethekau’s uraeus function; Middle Kingdom priestly titles can concern the Great-of-Magic crown together with the secrets of Wadjet and Nekhbet. **[Functional and institutional association]**
- **Nekhbet —** The Upper Egyptian crown and vulture can be Great of Magic; priestly crown titles explicitly connect the paired crowns with Wadjet and Nekhbet. **[Functional and institutional association]**
- **Isis —** Isis bears “Great of Magic” as an epithet, but the Abydos monuments of Padiaset list him separately as priest of Isis, Lady of the Birth House, and priest of Werethekau, Lady of the Palace. That juxtaposition is positive evidence that they could be regarded as distinct. Late Period. **[Epithet plus attested distinction]**
- **Osiris —** Werethekau and Osiris occur in the same priestly institutional milieu at Abydos, but no secure narrative episode shows Werethekau acting in the Osiris myth. **[Joint cultic context; absence of mythic interaction]**
- **Maat —** Vizier Paser held priestly titles for Maat and Werethekau; Wennefer’s Werethekau title is inscribed in Maat’s Karnak temple. These are personnel and location links, not myths. **[New Kingdom institutional association]**
- **Merit-Re —** The Merymay statue invokes Werethekau and Merit-Re together in the District of Kheput. Late Twenty-fifth/early Twenty-sixth Dynasty. **[Later regional joint invocation]**
- **Re/Re-Horakhty —** The daughter, wife, or Eye relationship appears in modern summaries and follows general Solar Eye theology, but a secure source naming Werethekau as “wife of Re-Horakhty” was not located. **[Unverified/possibly regional late theology]**

No attested combat, seduction, theft, judgment, or extended dialogue between Werethekau and another named god is known. Her documented interactions are overwhelmingly ritual: crowning, nursing, protecting, granting life, sharing offerings, or existing as another goddess’s epithet or compound form. **[Absence of narrative evidence]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Werethekau has no surviving independent myth cycle comparable to the Contendings of Horus and Seth, the Destruction of Humanity, or Isis and Re’s secret name. Modern retellings frequently transfer Isis’s deeds to her because Isis can bear the epithet “Great of Magic.” That procedure converts an epithet into a separate protagonist and is not textually warranted. **[Absence of evidence; modern conflation]**

Her “myths” are better described as recurring ritual narratives.

### The crown encounters the king

In Unas Spell 153, the doors of the horizon open and the deceased king approaches the Red Crown. She is addressed as Fiery One, Great One, and Great of Magic; the king is pure for her and feared because of her. He comes as Horus protected by his Eye. **[Early attested]**

This is the earliest narrative kernel. The crown is not inert equipment: it receives the king, inspires fear, and participates in his transformation. Later sources divide and multiply the power into Upper and Lower Egyptian crowns, the two Eyes, the cobra, and the vulture.

### The two Great-of-Magic crowns

Merenre, Pepi II, and Queen Neith’s texts describe the Eyes emerging or being placed on the royal head as the two Great-of-Magic crowns. The wearer becomes Dual King and gains control over gods and their *kas*. **[Early attested]**

This is not a tale of the goddess’s birth. It is a ritual explanation of how crowns become animate divine agencies and how the dead ruler resumes sovereign power.

### The coronation goddess

New Kingdom reliefs convert the older personified potency into an image of a goddess who places the crown upon the king. Hatshepsut’s monuments provide the earliest known depictions; Seti I’s Karnak relief is a particularly explicit example. Human chamberlains actually handled and adorned the king with regalia, while temple representation shows Werethekau performing the divine archetype of the same act. **[New Kingdom canonical elaboration]**

The ritual’s political function is direct: it presents accession not as mere inheritance or force but as transformation authorized and effected by the gods. The close correlation between Werethekau’s priesthood and palace chamberlains is the institutional counterpart of the scene.

### The nursing of the king

Tutankhamun’s pendant and statue CG 42002 show Werethekau nursing a royal or divine child. Milk makes the ruler the goddess’s child and transmits divine life and fitness to rule. The pendant combines serpent protection with maternal nourishment: the same being who is the dangerous royal cobra feeds the king. **[New Kingdom attested]**

Claims that the image narrates a specific lost story about Werethekau raising Tutankhamun are not established. It is a compact theological and ritual image, not evidence of an ordinary biography. **[Absence of evidence]**

### Solar Eye cycle

Because Werethekau can be cobra-, lioness-, and sun-disk-bearing, scholars situate her within the broader network of Solar Eye goddesses: the eye emanates from the sun god, becomes dangerous, destroys enemies, is pacified, and returns. This comparison explains her forms and functions but no surviving telling of the Destruction of Humanity names the independent Werethekau as its protagonist. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction from syncretistic evidence]**

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Was there a cult?

Older scholarship often said no. Erika Hoenes concluded that no independent cult or priesthood was known; the view was repeated by Ingrid Nebe and others. Ouda re-examined the material and demonstrated priests, statues, offering formulae, production departments, and temples or sanctuaries. His conclusion is that the New Kingdom evidence establishes Werethekau as at times “ontologically separate” from better-known goddesses. [The complete cult study](https://scholar.cu.edu.eg/sites/default/files/mekawy/files/09_-_mekawy_ouda.pdf) is the central modern reassessment. **[Historiographical dispute resolved in favor of a documented cult]**

### Cult statues

Ouda identifies three securely inscribed or attributable statues:

1. **Cairo N 127:** base of Werethekau, “Lady of the Sed-festival,” probably from Amenhotep III’s Kom el-Hitan complex.
2. **Taref magazine 4064:** Sekhmet-Werethekau, likewise from Kom el-Hitan.
3. **Cairo CG 42002:** statue from the Karnak Cachette showing Werethekau nursing a child; the back-pillar inscription identifies her.

No surviving precious-metal inner-sanctuary image is known. Absence of such a statue does not negate cult, since precious materials were particularly liable to reuse or loss. **[New Kingdom attested]**

### Priesthood

Four titles are securely documented:

- *wꜥb Wr.t-ḥkꜣ.w* — pure-priest of Werethekau.
- *ꜥꜣ n wꜥb.w n Wr.t-ḥkꜣ.w* — chief of Werethekau’s pure-priests.
- *ḥm-nṯr Wr.t-ḥkꜣ.w* — god’s servant/priest of Werethekau.
- *ḥm-nṯr tpy n Wr.t-ḥkꜣ.w* — first god’s servant/high priest of Werethekau.

The earliest institutionalization appears under Amenhotep III. Known officeholders include Paser the Elder, Amenhotep, Hori-djedefenre, Amenwahsu, the royal cupbearer Tjawy, vizier Paser, Padiaset, Sahptah, and Wennefer. Vizier Paser’s title occurs repeatedly on seven monuments and five times in TT106. **[New Kingdom attested]**

Werethekau’s priests are repeatedly also **chamberlains** or overseers of chamberlains. The connection is functional: chamberlains guarded, prepared, and placed royal crowns, while Werethekau embodied the regalia’s divine efficacy. **[New Kingdom attested; functional interpretation]**

No female priestess of Werethekau is securely documented. Ouda warns that this may reflect preservation or the male palace bureaucracy rather than a formal theological prohibition. **[Absence of evidence, not proof of exclusion]**

### Cult places

- **Thebes/Karnak:** Early imagery, statue CG 42002, Tjawy’s Theban monuments, Paser’s monuments, and Wennefer’s title establish strong Theban activity. A Werethekau production department or sanctuary probably stood within or near a larger temple estate. **[New Kingdom attested; precise location reconstructed]**
- **Kom el-Hitan:** Amenhotep III’s jubilee statuary includes Werethekau and Sekhmet-Werethekau. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Memphis:** Paser’s Louvre statue and Piay’s block statuette invoke her temple; Piay asks to remain daily within it until burial at Ankhtawy. Association with Ptah, Sekhmet, and Neith favors a Memphite location. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Kafr ed-Deir/District of Kheput:** Merymay’s statue names Ptah residing in the temple of Werethekau. Habachi located *Pr-Wr.t-ḥkꜣ.w* near modern Kafr ed-Deir. **[Late Period regional attestation]**
- **Abydos:** Priesthoods and votive monuments attest devotion, though they do not necessarily establish a freestanding temple. **[New Kingdom–Late Period attested]**
- **Bubastis/Tell Basta:** Bay’s Twentieth Dynasty offering chapel invokes her. **[Regional attestation]**
- **Aniba:** Ramesside temple jambs name her Lady of the Palace, Lady of Heaven, and Mistress of All Lands. **[Nubian regional attestation]**
- **Amara West:** Lintel F9010 and Ramesses III’s wine-offering stela demonstrate a local cultic presence. **[Nubian regional attestation]**
- **Abu Simbel and Wadi es-Sebua:** Royal monuments associate her with Ramesses II and Ptah. **[Nubian regional attestation]**
- **El-Qalʿa:** A Roman-period temple assigns her a sanctuary identified with the *Pr-wr*. **[Later attested]**

No foundation dates survive for most of these establishments, and few can be traced as independent excavated temple plans. “Temple of Werethekau” may sometimes mean a chapel or administrative unit inside a larger complex. **[Material limitation]**

### Offerings and ritual

Ouda catalogues at least eleven monuments with *ḥtp-dỉ-nsw* offering formulae for Werethekau, beginning in the Eighteenth Dynasty and becoming common in the Ramesside period. They include the statue of Hor from Dendera, statue EA 1459 from Deir el-Bahari, Paser’s Louvre statue, Abu Simbel, Piay’s statuette, Amara West lintel, Penamun stela, Aniba lintel, Bay’s Bubastis chapel, Merymay’s statue, and a Saqqara statue of Isis. **[New Kingdom–Late Period attested]**

Boston relief 1972.651 shows Tjawy burning incense and pouring libation before Werethekau as a cobra in a granary. His titles include overseer of her *šnꜥ*, a production and redistribution department that processed food, offerings, and textiles. This gives the cult an economic as well as symbolic footprint. **[New Kingdom attested]**

Known ritual acts include:

- crowning and adorning the king;
- nursing the king;
- presenting incense and libations;
- wine offering;
- receiving standard food offerings;
- jubilee protection;
- processional service, inferred from pure-priest duties;
- guarding and ritually handling crowns.

No festival exclusively dedicated to Werethekau has been securely reconstructed. Her participation in coronations and Amenhotep III’s Sed-festival is documented, but those were royal/state rites rather than necessarily annual festivals of her own. **[Absence of evidence]**

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

The dedicated search found **no evidence** that Werethekau’s cult practiced human sacrifice, “sacred prostitution,” or organized cult violence. Egyptian warfare imagery and the uraeus’s destruction of royal enemies belong to royal theology and should not be converted into evidence of literal sacrifices to this goddess. Likewise, older claims of widespread Egyptian “temple prostitution” are not valid evidence for Werethekau without a relevant document. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late Fifth–Sixth Dynasties: powerful crown and royal identity

The Pyramid Texts attest *wrt-ḥkꜣw* as the animate power of the Red Crown, Horus’s Eye, the paired crowns, cobra, and vulture. The expression can also qualify Seth or the deceased king. The evidence concerns royal funerary transformation and regalia, not a demonstrable temple goddess. **[Early attested]**

### Middle Kingdom: diffusion and administration of crowns

The formula persists in funerary texts and is attached to crown-handling offices. Two “priests” of the Werethekau-crown are known, but Ouda interprets them as royal officials serving the crown and king rather than an independent goddess’s cult. **[Early attested; disputed classification]**

### Early Eighteenth Dynasty: visualization as a goddess

Under Hatshepsut and Thutmose II/III, Werethekau enters surviving monumental coronation programs as an identifiable female deity. Lioness imagery and the Pakhet-Werethekau form connect the older crown power to the dangerous-protective Solar Eye complex. **[New Kingdom turning point]**

### Amenhotep III: institutionalization

Priestly titles appear; statues invoke Werethekau and Sekhmet-Werethekau; “Lady of the Sed-festival” integrates her into royal rejuvenation. Ouda dates the effective institutionalization of her priesthood to this reign. **[New Kingdom attested]**

### Tutankhamun: palace and nursing theology

The shrine and JE 61952 emphasize “Lady of the Palace,” serpentine form, divine nursing, and the king’s intimate bodily dependence upon the goddess. This is the most famous surviving portable representation. **[New Kingdom attested]**

### Ramesside period: high visibility and geographic spread

Seti I and Ramesses II placed Werethekau in monumental crowning scenes. Priests such as Paser held senior titles; offering formulae, cult installations, and references to temples multiply. Her worship is documented from Thebes and Memphis through Nubian centers such as Aniba and Amara West. **[New Kingdom peak]**

Calling this a “peak” is justified by the density and diversity of surviving evidence—images, priesthoods, offerings, and geographic spread—not by ancient membership statistics, which do not exist.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods

A Twenty-second Dynasty pure-priest is attested on Louvre IM 3141. Abydos documents distinguish the priesthoods of Isis and Werethekau. The Kafr ed-Deir statue indicates a temple substantial enough to contain a Ptah chapel. **[Later attested continuity]**

### Ptolemaic and Roman periods

The name and epithet continued in temple theology into the Roman period, especially where crown, uraeus, palace, and Solar Eye systems were elaborated. Ouda dates the overall corpus from the third millennium BCE through approximately 395 CE. El-Qalʿa supplies a Roman-period sanctuary reference. **[Later attested]**

### Christianization and disappearance

Egyptian temple institutions declined and were eventually closed or transformed under Roman Christian rule, especially during the fourth through sixth centuries CE. No edict, attack, or conversion specifically directed against Werethekau’s cult is documented. Her disappearance is therefore part of the dissolution of the hieroglyphic temple system, not a recorded individual suppression. **[Late antique historical inference]**

There is no securely demonstrated continuous folk cult under her name through medieval Islamic Egypt. Cobra protection and popular magical practices survived in many forms, but continuity with Werethekau must not be assumed solely from thematic resemblance. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

Werethekau remained obscure in comparison with Isis, Hathor, or Sekhmet. Egyptological lexica and museum publications brought her into modern reference literature; Tutankhamun’s tomb gave her a striking visual emblem, although the pendant was often subordinated to Isis or generic nursing-goddess interpretations.

Modern Kemetic and goddess-spirituality writers now invoke Werethekau as an independent deity. These practices are genuine modern religious phenomena, but no numerical estimate of her adherents is available. Modern devotees often reconstruct rites from Egyptian offering conventions or compose new prayers. Such practices should be dated to the modern revival rather than presented as an unbroken ancient liturgy. **[Modern religious reconstruction]**

Common modern additions include fixed altar colors, crystals, tarot correspondences, astrological rulerships, standardized feast days, and claims that she governs all witchcraft. No ancient documentation for a canonical system of this kind has been located. **[Modern invention/unverified]**

The web increasingly describes her as:

- wife of Re-Horakhty;
- daughter of Re in a fixed family tree;
- present at every pharaoh’s coronation;
- protector of pregnant women through “ivory knives”;
- an underworld guardian on weapons;
- protagonist of myths properly belonging to Isis.

Some of these statements arise from legitimate affinities but exceed the evidence. In particular, apotropaic wands with lioness and serpent figures do not identify every figure as Werethekau, and Isis’s magical myths do not become Werethekau myths merely because Isis is called *wrt-ḥkꜣw*. **[Modern conflation]**

No significant Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or early modern grimoire tradition centered on Werethekau has been identified. Nor is there evidence of nationalist, extremist, or violent political appropriation specific to her. Her modern reception remains chiefly Egyptological, museum-based, and small-scale Kemetic or goddess devotional. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The central debate: epithet, object, or deity?

Earlier reference works often treated Werethekau mainly as a crown-name, an epithet of major goddesses, or a minor personification without independent cult. Hoenes’s 1976 statement that no cult or priesthood was known influenced Nebe’s *Lexikon der Ägyptologie* article and later summaries.

Ouda’s work changed the evidentiary balance by assembling:

- three cult statues;
- four classes of priestly title;
- multiple named priests;
- at least eleven offering formulae;
- references to temples, a guardian, and a *šnꜥ* production department;
- cult locations in Thebes, Memphis, Kafr ed-Deir, Abydos, Bubastis, and Nubia.

The best present formulation is therefore diachronic: **the phrase began as a flexible designation of magically efficacious regalia and divine/royal power; by the early New Kingdom it could also name an autonomous goddess; thereafter the object, epithet, and goddess continued side by side.** **[Modern scholarly reconstruction strongly supported by primary evidence]**

### The uraeus question

Werethekau is commonly called “the uraeus goddess,” but the uraeus was not a single divine individual. It could be Wadjet, an Eye of Re, an Eye of Horus, one of paired goddesses, or another protective cobra. Werethekau describes the royal cobra’s magical efficacy and can personify it, yet an unlabeled uraeus is not automatically Werethekau. **[Scholarly caution]**

### Lioness versus cobra

Older summaries sometimes imply that her lioness form is late while her serpent form is original. The textual record makes crown/uraeus language early; surviving individualized images begin in the New Kingdom and include lioness-, cobra-, serpent-, and human forms. The lioness form particularly reflects the Solar Eye and syncretism with Pakhet or Sekhmet. **[Evidence-based distinction]**

### Tutankhamun pendant

The pendant’s nursing figure was often described through Isis, Mut, or Renenutet analogies. Ouda’s comparison of its inscription with the adjacent shrine’s ten references to “Werethekau, Lady of the Palace” argues for Werethekau as the named deity. The identification is strong; restoration of the damaged epithet remains partly conjectural. **[Modern epigraphic debate]**

### UC 16639

This unprovenanced Petrie Museum jamb fragment illustrates the hazards of decontextualization. Its inscription may contain an offering formula to Werethekau and probably dates to the Ramesside period by comparison with other formulae. Neither provenance nor date is securely recorded, and the terminal determinative remains damaged. It should not be used as an early or geographically specific attestation. **[Disputed object]**

### State of the evidence

The evidence is dispersed among:

- pyramid interiors at Saqqara;
- temple reliefs at Karnak, Luxor-region monuments, Abu Simbel, Wadi es-Sebua, Aniba, Amara West, and Bubastis;
- the Cairo Egyptian Museum and archaeological magazines;
- the Louvre;
- British Museum;
- Petrie Museum;
- Bristol Museum;
- Metropolitan Museum of Art;
- University of Pennsylvania Museum;
- Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek;
- Berlin and Boston collections;
- private or presently untraced objects.

Important publications include Sethe and Allen for the Pyramid Texts; De Buck for the Coffin Texts; Leitz’s *Lexikon der ägyptischen Götter und Götterbezeichnungen*; Nebe’s *Lexikon der Ägyptologie* entry; Wilkinson’s survey; Hornung and Assmann on Egyptian divine plurality and manifestation; and Ouda’s object- and corpus-based studies.

The greatest loss is contextual rather than merely textual: objects were excavated or acquired without provenance, buildings were dismantled, portable precious cult images disappeared, and older editions sometimes failed to distinguish crown, epithet, and goddess.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 2375–2345 BCE, Unas:** Pyramid Text Spell 153 addresses the Red Crown as Great of Magic; earliest secure attestation of the expression. **[Early attested]**
- **c. 2280–2200 BCE, Merenre and Pepi II:** Pyramid Texts develop the Nile Valley and Delta Great-of-Magic crowns, Great-of-Magic uraeus and vulture, and royal Great-of-Magic identity. **[Early attested]**
- **c. 2200 BCE, Queen Neith:** Her pyramid repeats dual-crown and Eye formulae. **[Early attested]**
- **Middle Kingdom, c. 2055–1650 BCE:** Funerary transmission continues; officials titled servants of the Werethekau-crown handle royal regalia. No secure independent cult yet. **[Early attested]**
- **Reign of Hatshepsut/Thutmose III, c. 1479–1425 BCE:** Earliest surviving monumental depictions; Werethekau participates in coronation imagery at Karnak and as Pakhet-Werethekau at Speos Artemidos. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Thutmose IV, c. 1400–1390 BCE:** Lioness-headed Werethekau appears on ceremonial chair panels from KV43 with Horus and Thoth. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Amenhotep III, c. 1390–1353 BCE:** Priesthood becomes institutionally visible; Kom el-Hitan statues name Werethekau, Lady of the Sed-festival, and Sekhmet-Werethekau. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Tutankhamun, c. 1332–1323 BCE:** Gilded shrine invokes Werethekau, Lady of the Palace; gold pendant JE 61952 depicts her nursing the king. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Late Eighteenth/early Nineteenth Dynasty:** Tjawy serves as chief of her pure-priests and administrator of her production department; relief shows incense and libation before her cobra image. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Seti I, c. 1290–1279 BCE:** Werethekau crowns the king in Karnak’s Great Hypostyle Hall; Seti revises the Speos Artemidos coronation scene. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Ramesses II, c. 1279–1213 BCE:** Vizier Paser repeatedly bears her high-priestly title; Memphite, Abydene, Abu Simbel, and Wadi es-Sebua evidence expands. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Ramesside period:** Offering formulae and temple references occur at Aniba and Amara West. **[Regional attestation]**
- **Ramesses III, c. 1186–1155 BCE:** Wine offered at Amara West to Amun, Bastet-Werethekau, and Horus of Buhen; Bay’s Bubastis chapel invokes her. **[Regional attestation]**
- **Ramesses XI, c. 1099–1069 BCE:** Vizier Wennefer bears the title first god’s servant of Werethekau at Karnak. **[New Kingdom attested]**
- **Twenty-second Dynasty, c. 943–716 BCE:** Louvre IM 3141 names a pure-priest of Werethekau. **[Later attested]**
- **Late Twenty-fifth/early Twenty-sixth Dynasty, seventh century BCE:** Merymay’s Kafr ed-Deir statue attests her temple in the District of Kheput and a Ptah chapel within it. **[Later regional attestation]**
- **Late Period:** Abydos sources distinguish priests of Isis from priests of Werethekau, Lady of the Palace. **[Later attested]**
- **Ptolemaic–Roman periods, 332 BCE–fourth century CE:** The name continues in temple theology; Roman el-Qalʿa preserves a sanctuary reference. **[Later attested]**
- **Fourth–sixth centuries CE:** The hieroglyphic temple system disappears under Roman and Byzantine Christian rule; no Werethekau-specific act of suppression is documented. **[Historical inference]**
- **1880s–1930s:** Excavation and museum publication bring her monuments, including Karnak statues and Tutankhamun’s pendant, into modern scholarship.
- **1976:** Hoenes’s study denies that an independent cult is known, shaping subsequent reference literature. **[Historiography]**
- **1986:** Nebe’s *Lexikon der Ägyptologie* entry summarizes Werethekau and repeats the absence-of-priesthood position. **[Historiography]**
- **2002:** Leitz’s divine-name lexicon assembles priestly and epithetic attestations, complicating that position. **[Historiography]**
- **2013:** Ouda publishes UC 16639 and formally distinguishes object, epithet, and goddess. **[Modern scholarship]**
- **2015:** Ouda publishes the Penamun stela and argues for an independent cult figure.
- **2016:** Ouda reinterprets Tutankhamun pendant JE 61952 through the epithet “Lady of the Palace.” **[Modern scholarship]**
- **2010s–present:** Corpus study establishes a New Kingdom priesthood and temple network; small modern Kemetic and goddess-devotional communities adopt Werethekau without evidence of institutional continuity from antiquity. **[Modern reception]**
