# Meretseger: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess; more specifically a local Theban necropolis goddess, sacred-landscape personification, and serpent/uraeus deity. Egyptian sources do not classify her as a demon, monster, or merely anonymous spirit.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently verifiable evidence belongs to the **Eighteenth Dynasty, probably its later part, c. fourteenth century BCE**: a named representation on Vatican stela no. 170, conventionally dated to the late Eighteenth Dynasty, shows Meretseger as a serpent-headed sphinx. An Eighteenth-Dynasty statue group of Amenhotep II with a protective cobra goddess has long been identified as Meretseger, but the exact dating and identification require caution because modern descriptions are not fully consistent. General-reference claims that her divine name is attested “from the Middle Kingdom” have not been tied to a securely contemporary Middle Kingdom cult object; they may partly reflect confusion with a purported queen Meretseger known only from later evidence. Her first abundant, unambiguous corpus is Ramesside, especially Dynasty 19.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[Early attested]** = Eighteenth Dynasty or earliest secure evidence; **[Ramesside attested]** = Dynasties 19–20; **[Regional variant]** = local form outside, or distinct within, the principal Deir el-Medina cult; **[Priestly/systematic]** = theological equation or composite identification rather than an independent story; **[Later ancient]** = after the New Kingdom; **[Modern reconstruction]** = scholarly inference; **[Modern invention]** = a claim not found in known ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Meretseger was the divine power of the Theban western mountain and necropolis, especially the escarpment and peak above the Valleys of the Kings and Queens and the royal-tomb workers’ settlement now called Deir el-Medina. She was simultaneously a place—the dangerous, silent desert mountain—and an anthropomorphic or serpentine agent who could hear prayer, strike an offender with illness or blindness, and become merciful when properly petitioned. Her highly local cult is unusually well documented through ordinary people’s stelae, ostraca, graffiti, chapels, and prayers rather than through a national mythological cycle.

### Name and writing

The normal Egyptological form is **Meretseger**, with variants **Mertseger, Meresger, Mersger, Mert Seger, Mert-Seger**, French **Méretseger/Mertséger**, German **Meretseger**, Italian **Mertseger**, and Spanish **Meretseger/Meretseger**. The Egyptian is conventionally transliterated:

- **Mr.t-sgr / Mr.t-sgr**: normally “She Who Loves Silence.”
- The feminine ending **-t** marks a female subject; *mr(i)* means “love/desire,” and *sgr* denotes silence, quieting, or causing silence.
- Monumental spellings vary and may include a seated-woman and cobra determinative. One published sequence is represented in modern font encoding as `mr:r:O34 W11:r B1 I12`; such encodings should not be mistaken for a single invariant ancient spelling.

**[Ramesside attested]** The translation “She Who Loves Silence” is supported by the ordinary Egyptian syntax and by *Wörterbuch der ägyptischen Sprache* II, 104. It probably refers to the silence appropriate to the sacred necropolis, not to a goddess incapable of speech or hearing. Egyptian prayers in fact depict her as hearing and answering.

**[Older scholarly interpretation; disputed]** “Beloved of Him Who Makes Silence,” with “him” identified as Osiris, occurs in older Egyptological and popular writing. This requires supplying an unexpressed male agent and is grammatically less straightforward. It may reflect theological association with Osiris and the silent dead rather than the name’s primary construction.

### Names of the landscape

Meretseger’s most important alternate designation is the mountain itself:

- **tꜣ dḥnt / ta dehenet**, “the Peak,” “the Summit,” or, in some translations, “the Scarp.”
- **dḥnt ỉmntt**, “Peak/Scarp of the West.”
- **pꜣ dḥnt n ỉmntt**, “the Peak of the West.”

The modern mountain name is **el-Qurn/al-Qurn**, Arabic “the Horn.” It rises above the royal valleys and has a pyramidal profile from some approaches. Identifying Meretseger exclusively with the literal summit rather than the wider western escarpment is too narrow: inscriptions and landscape studies allow *dḥnt* to encompass the sacred cliff or mountain region.

### Attested titles and descriptions

The Ramesside corpus gives her both local and generalized goddess-titles:

- “Peak of the West.”
- “Great Peak of the West.”
- “Great Peak of the Mountain of the West.”
- “Mistress/Lady of the West.”
- “Mistress of the Peak.”
- “Lady/Mistress of Heaven.”
- “Mistress of the Two Lands.”
- “Mistress of all the gods.”
- “Great of strength/power.”
- “Great and noble mistress.”
- “My mistress.”
- “Lady of food.”
- “The beautiful one.”
- “The beautiful lady, pacified.”
- “She who returns to kindness.”
- “She who is in this *djar(et)*,” apparently a local topographical designation.
- “Eye of Re,” in a composite ritual identification.
- “Daughter of Re,” in extended theological titulary.
- “Daughter of the West/Amentet,” in older readings of a royal funerary context.

“Lady of Heaven,” “Mistress of the Two Lands,” and “Mistress of all the gods” are not evidence that she headed Egypt’s national pantheon. They are expansive honorifics routinely bestowed on goddesses. Her actual monumental distribution remains strongly Theban.

### Equations and fusions

- **Hathor–Meretseger:** **[Regional variant; syncretistic identification]** A form called Hathor-Meretseger is reported at Deir el-Bahri and as far south as Esna. Both goddesses could be “Mistress of the West,” receive the dead, inhabit the western mountain, wear the horn-and-disk crown, and appear as a cobra. A New Kingdom graffito in Thutmose III’s Deir el-Bahri temple invokes Meretseger in a setting otherwise dominated by Hathor.
- **Renenutet–Meretseger:** **[Priestly/iconographic assimilation]** Both could be cobra goddesses and providers of food or prosperity. A Deir el-Medina stela, DB 291 in one scholarly catalogue, depicts Meretseger in a Renenutet-like role. This does not make the two permanently identical.
- **Eye of Re / uraeus goddesses:** **[Priestly/systematic]** In the tomb of Paser, Meretseger heads a sequence of identifications involving several fiery protective goddesses and the Eye of Re. Egyptian divine “syncretism” here is situational co-presence, not necessarily a declaration that all the named goddesses were always one being.
- **Hathor, Amentet, and the personified West:** **[Regional/systematic]** Fully anthropomorphic images of Meretseger often occur when she is performing a role normally associated with Hathor or the goddess of the West.

No secure Greek or Roman *interpretatio* assigning Meretseger to a named Greek or Roman goddess has been established.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest evidence problem

The often-repeated formula “known from the Middle Kingdom onward” is not equivalent to a securely documented Middle Kingdom cult.

1. **Purported Queen Meretseger:** A woman represented in later material as a wife of Senusret III bears the same name and has been credited with the title “Great Royal Wife.” No contemporary Dynasty 12 record securely establishes her. A personal name, especially one transmitted only in New Kingdom material, is not automatically proof of an active Middle Kingdom cult. **[Disputed evidence]**

2. **Amenhotep II group:** Cairo JE 39394 is commonly described as Amenhotep II protected by Meretseger. The king stands before a large cobra amid papyrus vegetation. If the goddess is named on the original monument and the piece is genuinely contemporary with Amenhotep II, it would provide evidence c. 1427–1401 BCE. Some modern publications, however, assign related material or its present inscriptional state to Dynasty 19. It is therefore better treated as probable early evidence, not the sole foundation for the chronology. **[Early attested, attribution requiring catalogue verification]**

3. **Vatican stela no. 170:** A serpent-headed sphinx identified as Meretseger, dated stylistically to the late Eighteenth Dynasty, is the earliest relatively secure named iconographic witness cited in recent specialist work. **[Early attested]**

4. **Ramesside explosion:** From early Dynasty 19 onward, named stelae, ostraca, graffiti, sanctuary dedications, and tomb scenes become numerous. This is the first secure basis for reconstructing her theology and lived cult.

### Principal primary corpus

#### Neferabu’s penitential stela, Turin Cat. 1593 / CGT 50058

**Language and support:** Middle Egyptian/Late Egyptian religious idiom in hieroglyphs on limestone.  
**Date:** Dynasty 19, 1292–1191 BCE; probably the Ramesside community’s major period of votive production.  
**Provenance:** Deir el-Medina.  
**Role:** Meretseger is “Peak of the West,” punisher, healer, and hearer of prayer.

The draftsman Neferabu says that he was ignorant of good and evil and committed a transgression against the Peak. She placed him “in her hand” night and day; he sat on bricks “like a woman in labor,” and his appeal to the wind initially failed. He warns the work crew that “a lion is within” the Peak and that she strikes like a savage lion. When he calls upon her, she comes “as a sweet breeze,” becomes merciful, and causes his malady to be forgotten. The exact offense and exact pathology are not stated.

This text must not be silently conflated with Neferabu’s separate British Museum stela EA 589, where he confesses to swearing falsely by **Ptah**, who made him “see darkness by day.” Popular accounts frequently transfer Ptah’s explicit blinding to Meretseger. The Turin Meretseger text describes severe illness but does not explicitly say “she blinded me.” The Museo Egizio supplies the object, date, provenance, and a museum translation; Lichtheim prints the fuller composition.

#### Deir el-Medina votive stelae and ostraca

The corpus includes:

- British Museum **EA8508**, a late Dynasty 19 painted limestone ostracon showing the workman Pennub adoring Meretseger as a woman-headed serpent.
- Metropolitan Museum **26.7.1438**, a Ramesside limestone ostracon or preliminary design for a Meretseger stela, with named Deir el-Medina workmen.
- Louvre **E 13084**, Dynasty 19, showing a kneeling woman adoring a cobra Meretseger wearing upright plumes and a solar disk.
- Liverpool/Mayer stela **M13832**, now lost since 1941, showing a seated cobra-headed woman with *ankh* and *was* and naming her “Meretseger, the Peak of the West, Lady of the Sky, Mistress of all the gods.”
- Turin stela Cat. 1451 bis / CGT 50033, Dynasty 19, dedicated by Parahotep to **Amun-Re of Ipet, Meretseger, and the deified Amenhotep I**.
- Further stelae of Pendua, Nefersenut, Paneb, and other workmen; figured ostraca; rock graffiti; and domestic serpent statuettes catalogued by Bruyère and subsequent museum publications.

The monuments are short acts of cult rather than episodes in a mythic biography: praise, offering, protection, thanksgiving, confession, and petition.

#### Rock sanctuary inscriptions

The rock-cut sanctuary between Deir el-Medina and the Valley of the Queens contains Ramesside royal and private stelae. It was excavated by Ernesto Schiaparelli’s Italian mission and, more systematically, by Bernard Bruyère in 1926. Its inscriptions join Meretseger to “Ptah of the Valley of the Queens,” kings, Hathor, and other deities. Recent spatial analysis cautions that Bruyère’s convenient name “Oratory of Ptah and Meretseger” may exaggerate a single unified dedication: the complex comprised several chapels with different divine emphases.

#### Royal tomb and sarcophagus corpus

- **Tomb of Tausret, KV14:** late Dynasty 19/early Dynasty 20 decoration includes a winged, undulating, woman-headed serpent Meretseger as “Mistress of the West.” This has been called her earliest surviving appearance on an official royal monument.
- **Sarcophagus lid of Ramesses III**, now Fitzwilliam Museum: a finely carved serpent form of Meretseger belongs to the king’s funerary protective program.
- **Valley of the Queens chapels:** In Chapel B a stela of Ramesses III shows anthropomorphic Meretseger presenting papyrus to Osiris. In Chapel C she nurses the young king. A badly damaged scene in Chapel E may repeat the nursing motif.

#### Tomb of Paser, TT 106

Pillar H contains an unusual lion-headed goddess whose caption begins with Meretseger and layers several other divine identifications into the same figure. The ritual concerns censing and the Eye of Re. This is key evidence for a local goddess being inserted into a broader network of royal-uraeus theology. It is not a free-standing myth in which Meretseger literally transforms into every listed goddess.

### Texts in which Meretseger does **not** securely appear

The tradition-specific survey requires an explicit negative result.

- **Pyramid Texts:** no secure Meretseger passage.
- **Coffin Texts:** no secure occurrence of Meretseger as this named Theban goddess. “Mistress of the West” and serpent deities elsewhere cannot automatically be reassigned to her.
- **Book of the Dead:** no standard spell centers on Meretseger, although later funerary imagery shares serpent-guardian forms.
- **Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** no Meretseger.
- **Contendings of Horus and Seth:** no Meretseger.
- **Turin King List:** no goddess Meretseger; the document is a royal list, not her cult corpus.
- **Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*:** no securely identifiable Meretseger. Plutarch’s discussion of silence and Harpocrates must not be retrojected onto her.
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** no securely established invocation of Meretseger under her Egyptian name.
- **Edfu, Dendera, Karnak, and Philae:** these temples contain many serpent, Eye-of-Re, and western goddesses, but no major Meretseger narrative has been demonstrated in the standard published corpora consulted for this dossier. Similar iconography alone is insufficient identification.

Thus Meretseger’s “scripture” is not the great funerary books or national myths. It is primarily the landscape-bound epigraphic and archaeological record of western Thebes.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Ramesside attested]** Meretseger governs or embodies:

- The Theban western mountain, peak, cliff, and necropolis.
- The protected space of royal tomb construction.
- Dangerous desert conditions, especially snakes and scorpions.
- Punishment for wrongdoing, oath-breaking, impiety, or violation of sacred boundaries.
- Hearing petitions, relenting, healing, and restoring the penitent.
- The safety and food supply of at least some members of the workmen’s community.
- Funerary protection of kings and the dead.
- In expanded theology, the fiery protective power of the solar Eye.

She is often called “goddess of the Deir el-Medina workmen,” but no source makes her a simple occupational patron comparable to Ptah as divine craftsman. Her special connection arises because the workers lived and labored within her sacred landscape.

### Powers and limitations

The Neferabu stela makes her punishment immediate and unavoidable—he is “in her hand” day and night—but also reversible. Calling upon her can make her “return appeased.” Her characteristic theological pattern is therefore not unqualified vengeance but **offense → divine affliction → confession/praise → mercy**.

Blindness is possible within the broader Deir el-Medina theology of divine punishment, but the best explicit blindness confession is addressed to Ptah. Snakebite, scorpion sting, desert accident, fever, and blindness are plausible ancient experiences behind her cult; assigning a particular diagnosis to Neferabu is **[Modern reconstruction]**.

### Forms

Documented forms include:

- A naturalistic cobra, erect or coiled.
- A woman-headed snake.
- A cobra-headed woman.
- A fully anthropomorphic goddess.
- A serpent-headed sphinx.
- A winged, undulating serpent with female head.
- A triple-headed serpent combining female, cobra, and vulture heads on the Neferabu stela.
- A rare scorpion or woman-headed scorpion form.
- A rare lion-headed form in a composite ritual context.
- A nursing anthropomorphic goddess.
- The mountain or Peak itself, without requiring a separate bodily image.

### Crowns and objects

She may wear:

- Double upright plumes.
- Solar disk and cow horns.
- Vulture headdress.
- Red Crown or uraeus-associated headgear in assimilated forms.
- A plain fillet or no elaborate crown.

She may carry:

- **Ankh**, sign of life.
- **Was** scepter, sign of divine dominion.
- Knives in protective or dangerous manifestations.
- Papyrus stems in a royal offering scene.

No ancient evidence assigns her a canonical color, sacred number, weekday, planet, or fixed festival date. Modern lists that provide such correspondences are **[Modern invention or occult correspondence]** unless they cite an ancient document.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Meretseger possesses no stable narrative genealogy comparable to Isis, Horus, or Osiris.

- **“Daughter of Re”: [Priestly/systematic]** An Eye-of-Re/uraeus classification, not an extant birth story.
- **“Daughter of the West/Amentet”: [Regional funerary formulation]** A topographical-theological relationship.
- **Consort of Osiris:** Popular summaries sometimes call her the companion or wife of the buried Osiris. No narrative marriage text has been identified. Association with Osiris arises from their shared necropolis and royal-funerary setting. **[Late scholarly harmonization unless a specific inscription is supplied]**
- No securely attested children, siblings, mount, or permanent divine spouse.
- “Mother” or nurse of the king is a ritual role shown in royal imagery, not biological genealogy.

### Every documented named god–god interaction or co-presence

Because Meretseger has no extended narrative cycle, most relations are ritual co-appearances or theological equations, not conversations or adventures.

- **Ptah — joint cult and sanctuary:** Meretseger and “Ptah of the Valley of the Queens” receive worship in the rock-cut chapel complex between Deir el-Medina and the Valley of the Queens; Ramesside inscriptions and votives. **[Ramesside attested]**
- **Ptah — parallel punishment theology:** Neferabu dedicates separate penitential hymns to Meretseger and Ptah. Ptah explicitly punishes his false oath with blindness; Meretseger inflicts an unspecified malady after a transgression. They do not meet in a narrative. **[Ramesside attested]**
- **Hathor — fusion/local equivalence:** A New Kingdom Deir el-Bahri graffito invokes Meretseger within Hathor’s cultic landscape; later iconography and titles overlap, and Hathor-Meretseger is reported at Thebes/Esna. **[Regional variant; syncretistic identification]**
- **Hathor — joint representation:** Deir el-Medina material includes scenes where Meretseger and Hathor appear together or where the image is interpreted as Hathor-Meretseger. **[Ramesside regional]**
- **Renenutet — functional/iconographic assimilation:** Meretseger adopts a Renenutet-like role, particularly as provider or serpent goddess, on a Deir el-Medina stela conventionally numbered DB 291. No narrative encounter is known. **[Ramesside regional; systematic]**
- **Osiris — papyrus offering:** In Valley of the Queens Chapel B, anthropomorphic Meretseger presents papyrus to Osiris on a stela of Ramesses III. **[Ramesside official cult]**
- **Osiris — funerary co-presence:** Royal funerary monuments place Meretseger within the protective world ruled by Osiris; this is not evidence of marriage. **[Ramesside attested]**
- **Re — Eye/daughter relationship:** The Paser ritual associates Meretseger with the Eye of Re and the uraeus-goddess complex. **[Priestly/systematic]**
- **Weret-Hekau and other uraeus goddesses — temporary composite identity:** In TT 106 the caption of the lion-headed goddess begins with Meretseger and layers other divine names or forms. The relationship is ritual cohabitation of one image, not a story of physical fusion. **[Ramesside priestly/systematic]**
- **Amun-Re of Ipet — joint reception of worship:** Parahotep’s Dynasty 19 Turin stela invokes Amun-Re, Meretseger, and Amenhotep I together. No action between the gods is stated. **[Ramesside attested]**
- **Amenhotep I, deified — joint reception of worship:** Same Parahotep stela; both are local divine patrons of the Deir el-Medina community. **[Ramesside attested]**
- **Mut of Isheru — cultic co-presence in the sanctuary complex:** Royal stelae from the Valley of the Queens chapel area include Mut, Hathor, Amun-Re, and Ptah alongside the wider setting identified with Meretseger. No direct Meretseger–Mut episode survives. **[Ramesside regional]**

No securely attested interaction has been found with Isis, Seth, Horus, Anubis, Thoth, Khonsu, or Nephthys. Their membership in Egyptian funerary religion does not itself create a relationship.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The confession and mercy of Neferabu

This is Meretseger’s fullest surviving narrative.

**[Ramesside attested]** Neferabu introduces himself as formerly truthful but also ignorant and foolish, unable to distinguish good from evil. He says that he committed “the transgression” against the Peak. The wording neither names the offense nor permits a detailed reconstruction. Meretseger seizes him with a grave affliction: he is helpless night and day, sits on bricks like a woman in labor, and cannot summon the wind. He makes libation to the Peak and to every god and goddess.

He then warns the entire work crew: a lion dwells within the Peak; she pursues and strikes whoever offends her. He calls on his mistress and experiences her coming “as a sweet breeze.” She becomes merciful, shows him her hand or power, returns appeased, and makes his sickness forgotten. He closes by telling every living ear to beware the Peak of the West.

The physical support and first-person form make this simultaneously a votive monument, hymn, public confession, warning, and record of restored relations.

### The dangerous and merciful Peak

There is no continuous epic, but many monuments participate in a common narrative structure:

1. The necropolis is sacred and dangerous.
2. A human violates divine or moral order.
3. The Peak, cobra, lion, scorpion, or goddess strikes.
4. The sufferer acknowledges divine agency.
5. Prayer, praise, libation, and public testimony follow.
6. Meretseger is pacified and may heal.

Calling this a formal “myth” is useful only with qualification. It is a repeated cultic theology inferred from individual testimonies, not a single canonical composition.

### Meretseger nurses the king

**[Ramesside official elaboration]** In the Valley of the Queens chapel program, Meretseger appears anthropomorphically and suckles the young king. The action presents her as royal mother/protector and assimilates her to Hathor and other nursing goddesses. It does not establish a lost story in which she literally gave birth to Ramesses III.

### The goddess of the mountain and the dead

**[Modern reconstruction from cultic evidence]** Meretseger’s mountain was probably understood as simultaneously her body, dwelling, and jurisdiction. The Qurn’s visual prominence and proximity to the royal valleys made the landscape itself protective. Claims that the peak’s pyramidal appearance was the decisive reason the Valley of the Kings was selected remain hypotheses, not Egyptian testimony.

### Stories absent from the ancient corpus

There is no known ancient story of:

- Meretseger’s birth.
- Her marriage to Osiris.
- Combat with a named god.
- A journey from or return to Nubia.
- Participation in the murder of Osiris.
- The Contendings of Horus and Seth.
- A cosmic creation act.
- A fixed judgment tribunal over the dead.

Modern narratives supplying these episodes are composites made from the myths of Hathor, Sekhmet, Wadjet, Renenutet, or Isis.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult geography

The central zone comprises:

- Deir el-Medina, the walled settlement of royal-tomb personnel.
- The paths to the Valleys of the Kings and Queens.
- The western escarpment and the Qurn.
- Small shrines and chapels near the mountain.
- The rock-cut sanctuary on the route between the village and Valley of the Queens.
- Royal tombs and Valley of the Queens chapels.

Attestations at Deir el-Bahri and Esna broaden the distribution, especially through Hathor-Meretseger, but do not make her a nationwide deity.

### The so-called Sanctuary of Ptah and Meretseger

The sanctuary occupies a topographically suggestive place under the western cliffs. A rock overhang has been compared to a cobra’s head, though whether ancient worshippers consciously selected it for that reason is an inference.

The complex contained several small rock-cut and constructed chapels, niches, royal stelae, and private dedications. Material ranges across the Ramesside period, with royal monuments of Setnakht and Ramesses III prominent. The Getty assessment describes it as a popular place of worship for royal-tomb workers and identifies its principal gods as Ptah and Meretseger.

Bruyère excavated and conserved the area in 1926 and published it in *Mert Seger à Deir el Médineh* (1930). Subsequent scholarship has warned that “the Sanctuary of Ptah and Meretseger” may be an overly unified name: individual chapels had distinct dedications, and Ptah or royal cults can dominate particular spaces.

### Priesthood

No autonomous, nationwide priestly hierarchy of Meretseger is documented. Worship appears to have been maintained by royal-tomb personnel, local ritual specialists, and officials participating in the broader temple system. Titles on votive monuments do not reveal a well-developed hereditary “high priest of Meretseger.” Absence is significant: her cult’s institutional profile differs from Amun’s or Ptah’s major temple establishments.

### Offerings and prayer

Attested or strongly indicated practices include:

- Prostration or “kissing the ground” before her *ka*.
- Praise-giving.
- Spoken petition and confession.
- Libation.
- Food and drink offerings on tables.
- Flowers and papyrus.
- Dedication of limestone stelae and painted ostraca.
- Installation of serpent statuettes or images.
- Public testimony after recovery.
- Worship at path-side and rock-cut shrines.

The archaeological record supports accessible local devotion, including dedications by women. Domestic serpent images may represent household access to Meretseger, although identifying every uninscribed snake figure specifically as her is unsafe.

### Festivals and oracle

No securely named annual festival exclusively for Meretseger survives. She may have participated in local royal, Hathoric, funerary, or procession calendars, but dates and liturgies cannot be reconstructed. The records do not establish a Meretseger oracle comparable to the well-documented oracular activity of deified Amenhotep I.

### Sacrifice and disputed practices

- **Animal and food offerings:** ordinarily expected and iconographically attested through offering tables.
- **Human sacrifice:** no textual, osteological, or archaeological evidence connects it with Meretseger.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence.
- **Cult violence:** no evidence for worshippers committing organized violence in her name. Texts describe divine affliction, not human enforcement squads.
- **Tomb robbery:** Meretseger is often imagined as punishing robbers, but surviving prayers speak more generally of offense and sacred order. The idea is plausible within her jurisdiction yet should not be converted into a documented police function.
- **Magical protection against snakes:** compatible with her iconography and local dangers, but no securely identified Meretseger spell in the Greek Magical Papyri has been demonstrated.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the New Kingdom

**[Unestablished]** No Old Kingdom evidence. No secure occurrence in the Pyramid Texts. Middle Kingdom claims remain uncertain. Hathor already possessed a western-Theban funerary presence, furnishing part of the religious landscape into which Meretseger later emerged.

### Early to later Dynasty 18, c. 1550–1292 BCE

The establishment and growth of the royal necropolis and its specialized workforce created the social and spatial setting of Meretseger’s cult. The probable Amenhotep II group and late Eighteenth-Dynasty sphinx stela suggest that the goddess existed before the Ramesside florescence. A graffito in Thutmose III’s Deir el-Bahri temple shows her entering a Hathoric cult environment.

Her origin may have been:

- A personification of the western cliff/Peak.
- A local snake power associated with hazards of the desert.
- A specialized form emerging from Hathor’s western-necropolis theology.
- A convergence of all three.

No source records a historical “foundation” of her cult, so choosing one origin is **[Modern reconstruction]**.

### Dynasty 19, c. 1292–1189 BCE: principal florescence

This is the most securely documented high point. Deir el-Medina stela production peaks particularly in the earlier reign of Ramesses II; one study counted 99 of a dataset of 264 village stelae in that period. Meretseger receives private dedications, women’s worship, illustrated ostraca, rock graffiti, and the Neferabu penitential hymn. Her forms proliferate: cobra, woman-headed snake, cobra-headed woman, sphinx, and composite serpent.

“Peak” becomes a powerful form of address. Her cult participates in the Ramesside phenomenon once termed “personal piety,” though scholars now resist treating it as an entirely unprecedented religion of the poor. The literate and skilled workmen were neither socially invisible nor uniformly impoverished.

### Dynasty 20, c. 1189–1077 BCE: royal and funerary elaboration

Meretseger enters more formal royal programs:

- Tausret’s tomb provides an official royal representation around the Dynasty 19–20 transition.
- Setnakht and Ramesses III monumentalize the chapel zone.
- Meretseger nurses the king and offers papyrus to Osiris.
- Ramesses III’s sarcophagus incorporates her as funerary protector.

Her local mountain identity is now capable of “synthesizing,” in Jean Yoyotte’s phrase, the roles of a great Egyptian goddess without losing its Theban center.

### Dynasty 21 and after, from c. 1077 BCE

The organized royal-tomb workforce dispersed as royal burial shifted away from western Thebes. The tightly localized social base of Meretseger’s worship consequently contracted. This is better described as institutional and demographic decline than as a documented act of suppression.

Later references and Hathor-Meretseger forms indicate some afterlife, but there is no evidence for an uninterrupted, large temple cult through the Ptolemaic and Roman periods. The Ptolemaic Hathor temple at Deir el-Medina should not be relabeled a Meretseger temple merely because it occupies the same sacred landscape.

### Christian and Islamic periods

The Ptolemaic temple at Deir el-Medina was reused as a Christian monastery, giving the site its Arabic name, “Monastery of the City.” No source documents a campaign directed specifically against Meretseger, the destruction of a surviving Meretseger priesthood, or forced conversion of her named devotees. By the Christian period her distinct institutional cult was apparently already long defunct.

There is likewise no evidence of a Meretseger cult surviving into Islamic folklore under another name. General local respect for mountains, snakes, tombs, or desert spirits cannot be assigned to her without a naming tradition.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Archaeological recovery

Early nineteenth-century collecting and looting removed many Deir el-Medina stelae from context. The Drovetti collection carried major pieces to Turin; others entered London, Paris, Liverpool, and smaller collections.

Systematic study developed through:

- Ernesto Schiaparelli’s Italian excavations and photography.
- Bernard Bruyère’s Deir el-Medina excavations from the 1920s onward.
- Bruyère’s 1930 monograph, still the foundational assemblage.
- Museum catalogues by Tosi and Roccati and the *Kitchen Ramesside Inscriptions* corpus.
- Modern archaeological, gender, domestic-religion, and landscape studies.
- Parker M. Anderson’s 2024, 450-page thesis, the first stated attempt since Bruyère to reassemble the expanded corpus comprehensively.

### Popular image

Modern books routinely call her:

- “Goddess of silence.”
- “Goddess of punishment and mercy.”
- “Guardian of tomb robbers.”
- “Cobra goddess of the Valley of the Kings.”

The first three are useful summaries but not all are ancient titles. “Silence” is in her name; punishment and mercy are documented actions; “guardian against tomb robbers” is largely an interpretive extrapolation from her territory and moral force.

### Occult and neopagan reception

Modern Kemetic and eclectic pagan practitioners sometimes address Meretseger in prayer, especially as guardian of sacred space, healer, enforcer of accountability, or patron of silence. Internet testimony documents individual worship but does not establish the scale of devotion or organizational continuity.

No demonstrable line connects such worship continuously to the New Kingdom cult. Modern rites are revivals or reconstructions built from translated stelae, general Egyptian offering practice, and personal religious experience. Claims that a ritual, planetary correspondence, tarot attribution, sigil, incense recipe, or initiation descends directly from Meretseger’s ancient priesthood are **[Modern invention]** unless an ancient text is produced.

The Golden Dawn, Theosophy, and classic nineteenth-century Hermetic orders do not appear to have given Meretseger a major documented role. Modern occult compendia that assign her generic “serpent goddess” magic are late synthetic reception.

### Literature, art, games, and commerce

Meretseger appears in modern mythology handbooks, museum exhibitions, educational art, goddess encyclopedias, online fiction, spiritual merchandise, and role-playing or fantasy-derived deity lists. No single novel, film, comic, or game has demonstrably reshaped her public image on the scale that modern media reshaped Isis or Anubis. Her reception remains principally Egyptological, museum-based, touristic, and small-scale religious.

No well-documented nationalist, extremist, or organized political appropriation specific to Meretseger was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Bruyère’s synthesis

Bernard Bruyère’s *Mert Seger à Deir el Médineh* collected the sanctuary evidence and known representations into the first dedicated monograph. Its importance is exceptional, but later evidence has exposed three limitations:

1. It predates nearly a century of excavation, cataloguing, and theoretical work.
2. It encouraged later summaries to repeat a compressed portrait without returning to individual objects.
3. Its label “Oratory of Ptah and Meretseger” can obscure the complex’s multiple chapels and dedications.

### “Personal piety”

Breasted and Battiscombe Gunn interpreted Ramesside penitential stelae as a new “age of personal piety” or “religion of the poor.” Miriam Lichtheim retained the concept but argued that it was the culmination of longer developments in individual self-awareness and prayer, not an abrupt religious revolution. The monuments belonged to skilled, literate, sometimes prosperous workers as well as less prominent family members.

### The meaning of silence

A literal reading makes Meretseger herself “silent.” More careful approaches understand *sgr* as sacred quiet, disciplined speech, or the silence proper to a necropolis. Her hymns depict her as hearing calls, responding, and arriving like a breeze. Thus “goddess who refuses to speak” is a modern simplification.

### Local goddess or cosmic Hathor?

Yoyotte distinguished a strongly local Meretseger, the western Peak, from a more cosmic or Hathoric Meretseger who absorbs titles and iconography of great goddesses. Other scholars emphasize Egyptian divine fluidity rather than two completely independent goddesses. The evidence favors a spectrum:

- Local mountain/serpent.
- Regional funerary mistress.
- Hathoric nursing and western goddess.
- Eye-of-Re/uraeus composite.

These aspects coexist but are not necessarily present in every monument.

### First attestation

The Middle Kingdom claim remains problematic. A queen’s name transmitted later and generalized statements in museum glossaries do not securely demonstrate cult. The safest present formulation is:

- Possible antecedents or later testimony referring to the Middle Kingdom.
- Probable Eighteenth-Dynasty evidence.
- Secure late Eighteenth-Dynasty named iconography.
- Abundant Dynasty 19–20 cult.

### Identity of images

Uninscribed cobras cannot automatically be called Meretseger. Wadjet, Renenutet, Hathor, Isis, Weret-Hekau, and other goddesses share serpent imagery. Identification requires name, find context, associated figures, or a distinctive local title.

### Neferabu and blindness

A persistent conflation joins two different stelae:

- Meretseger stela: transgression and unspecified severe malady, followed by mercy.
- Ptah stela: false oath and explicit darkness/blindness.

Saying “Meretseger blinded Neferabu for stealing from a tomb” adds both an offense and symptom not stated on her stela.

### State of the evidence

The corpus survives in:

- Museo Egizio, Turin.
- British Museum.
- Louvre.
- Metropolitan Museum of Art.
- Egyptian Museum, Cairo.
- Fitzwilliam Museum.
- Vatican Egyptian collection.
- Liverpool collections and records of objects lost in 1941.
- In-situ chapels, tombs, graffiti, and sanctuary remains in western Thebes.
- IFAO archives and excavation reports.

Provenance is uneven because early collecting severed numerous objects from their archaeological contexts.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Historical record |
|---|---|
| Old Kingdom, c. 2686–2181 BCE | No secure Meretseger attestation in the Pyramid Texts or material cult record. |
| Middle Kingdom, c. 2055–1650 BCE | General references sometimes claim the name is known; no securely contemporary divine monument has been established here. The supposed queen Meretseger is documented only retrospectively. |
| Early Dynasty 18, after c. 1550 BCE | Deir el-Medina and the royal-necropolis workforce create the social setting in which the cult becomes visible. |
| Reign of Amenhotep II, c. 1427–1401 BCE | Statue group JE 39394 is conventionally identified as Amenhotep II protected by Meretseger; exact identification and object history warrant caution. |
| Later Dynasty 18, fourteenth century BCE | Named serpent-sphinx stela Vatican no. 170: earliest comparatively secure iconographic attestation cited by specialist scholarship. |
| Dynasty 18 | Graffito in Thutmose III’s Deir el-Bahri temple invokes Meretseger within a predominantly Hathoric cult setting. |
| Late Dynasty 18–early Dynasty 19 | Local stelae and graffiti show an established mountain-serpent cult. |
| Early–middle reign of Ramesses II, thirteenth century BCE | Major peak in Deir el-Medina votive-stela production; Meretseger becomes one of the best-documented local deities. |
| Dynasty 19, 1292–1191 BCE | Neferabu’s Turin stela records transgression, affliction, prayer, mercy, and public warning. Pennub, Parahotep, Pendua, women devotees, and other villagers dedicate images and prayers. |
| Late Dynasty 19 | Meretseger appears in official royal funerary imagery in Tausret’s KV14 as winged woman-headed serpent and Mistress of the West. |
| c. 1189–1150 BCE | Setnakht and Ramesses III monumentalize the Valley of the Queens chapel zone; Meretseger offers papyrus to Osiris and nurses the king. |
| Reign of Ramesses III, c. 1186–1155 BCE | Meretseger appears on the king’s sarcophagus lid as a funerary protective serpent. |
| Dynasty 20 | Continued ostraca, domestic images, sanctuary dedications, and theological expansion into Hathoric and Eye-of-Re forms. |
| Dynasty 21, after c. 1077 BCE | Abandonment and dispersal of the royal-tomb workforce remove the cult’s principal community; distinct worship declines. |
| First millennium BCE | Sparse survival or absorption into Hathoric and general western-necropolis theology; no evidence of a major independent national cult. |
| Ptolemaic period, 305–30 BCE | Hathor’s temple at Deir el-Medina occupies the older sacred landscape, but should not automatically be classed as a Meretseger temple. |
| Late antiquity | Deir el-Medina temple reused as a Christian monastery. No documented targeted suppression of Meretseger. |
| 1820s onward | Deir el-Medina objects enter European collections, often with lost find context. |
| 1905–1914 | Italian mission photographs and investigates the rock sanctuary. |
| 1926 | Bruyère excavates the sanctuary area between Deir el-Medina and the Valley of the Queens. |
| 1930 | Publication of Bruyère’s *Mert Seger à Deir el Médineh*, the foundational monograph. |
| 1941 | Liverpool stela M13832, known from photographs and catalogue records, is lost during wartime destruction. |
| Later twentieth century | Museum catalogues, Ramesside inscription editions, and studies of personal piety refine the corpus. |
| 2010s–2020s | Landscape, gender, household-religion, and syncretism studies challenge a monolithic cult model. |
| 2024 | Parker M. Anderson completes *Meretseger: A Reexamination*, a 450-page reassessment incorporating evidence found since Bruyère. |
| Present | Meretseger remains an archaeological and museum subject and receives limited reconstructed worship among individual modern Kemetics; no continuous ancient institution survives. |

### Evidentiary conclusions

The securely recoverable Meretseger is not a nationally scripted goddess with a birth, marriage, and epic career. She is a place made divine and a divine person encountered in that place. Her strongest record is local and humanly immediate: cobra images scratched or painted on limestone, workers and women kneeling before her, royal protection in the necropolis, and Neferabu publicly warning that the mountain can strike yet will listen. Her variants—Peak, cobra, Hathoric mother, Eye of Re, punisher, healer—are not fragments of one lost canonical biography. They are the historical development of the goddess.

No evidence presently supports human sacrifice, temple prostitution, an organized violent cult, a continuous post-antique priesthood, or a major classical or modern occult lineage. No Pyramid Text, Coffin Text, Book of the Dead, Memphite Theology, *Contendings*, Plutarch passage, or Greek Magical Papyrus can presently be identified as a primary Meretseger text. These absences sharply distinguish the documented goddess from modern composite mythology.
