# Horus the Elder (Haroeris): Research Dossier

**Class:** Male Egyptian god; specifically a falcon/sky, celestial-light, royal, martial, and protective deity, later functioning as a local temple god and the head of several regional divine families.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The explicit divine name **Ḥr-wr** (“Horus the Great/Elder”) is securely attested in the **Middle Kingdom**, by approximately **1759 BCE** at the latest. The Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae dates the surviving record of the name from 1759 BCE onward. Coffin Text Spell 280 independently calls the deceased **Ḥr smsw**, “Horus the Elder/Eldest.” Claims that Haroeris himself is securely attested in Predynastic or Old Kingdom falcon imagery are modern reconstructions: early Horus, Horus of Nekhen, Horus of Letopolis, and unnamed falcon gods may contribute to his later identity, but an uninscribed prehistoric falcon is not securely identifiable as **Ḥr-wr**. The disputed Old Kingdom reading of Pyramid Text §116a as “Haroeris” is a translator’s interpretation rather than an unambiguous occurrence of the compound name.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA—early attested]** Secure Egyptian evidence through the Middle Kingdom.
- **[LC—later canonical elaboration]** New Kingdom or later Egyptian development built into widely transmitted religious literature.
- **[RV—regional variant]** Local theology, especially Letopolis or Kom Ombo.
- **[LS—learned systematization]** Priestly synthesis, king-list, temple-theological organization, or Greek antiquarian account.
- **[SI—syncretistic identification]** Explicit fusion or equation with another god.
- **[MA—medieval/early-modern accretion]**
- **[F—folklore]**
- **[MSR—modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **[MI—modern invention or modern religious/esoteric reinterpretation]**
- **[U—uncertain]** Evidence exists, but its attribution specifically to Haroeris cannot be established.

A governing caution applies throughout: Egyptian sources do not maintain a stable, modern taxonomic boundary between “Horus the Elder,” “Horus son of Isis,” Horus of Letopolis, Horus of Nekhen, Horus Behdety, and other local Horuses. A text saying only **Ḥr**, “Horus,” cannot automatically be assigned to Haroeris. Distinguishing these figures is sometimes ancient theology and sometimes modern analysis.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[EA/LC]** Haroeris is **Ḥr-wr**, literally “Horus the Great.” Because *wr* can denote greatness, seniority, or pre-eminence, “Horus the Elder” is a defensible contextual rendering, not the only literal translation. The god is an adult celestial Horus, commonly imagined as a falcon or falcon-headed man and differentiated—sometimes sharply, sometimes imperfectly—from **Ḥr-pꜣ-ẖrd**, Horus the Child, and **Ḥr-zꜣ-Ꜣst**, Horus son of Isis. The TLA registers these as separately attested names: Ḥr-wr from about 1759 BCE, Horus the Child from about 1400 BCE, and Horus son of Isis in an explicit form from the first millennium BCE, though the underlying filial tradition is substantially older. [TLA](https://thesaurus-linguae-aegyptiae.de/lemma/107500)

**[EA/MSR]** The broad conception behind Haroeris may be older than the compound name. Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts preserve an antagonistic and complementary Horus–Seth pairing, Horus’s damaged and restored eye, celestial Horus forms, and Horus of Letopolis. Those materials are ancestral to later Haroeris theology, but they do not prove that every early Horus was understood as the discrete god later called Ḥr-wr.

### Principal names

- **Egyptian:** **Ḥr-wr**, conventionally vocalized *Her-wer, Har-wer, Heru-wer,* or *Heru-ur*. “Horus the Great,” contextually “Horus the Elder.”
- **Egyptian epithet:** **Ḥr smsw**, “Horus the Eldest/Elder.” Coffin Text Spell 280 applies this identity to the deceased.
- **Greek:** **Ἁρουήρις / Haroëris**, also rendered **Aroueris** or **Arueris** in older translations. Plutarch’s Greek form reflects Egyptian Ḥr-wr.
- **Latinized/esoteric:** **Haroeris, Aroueris, Arouerist, Hoor-Ouer**. The last forms belong chiefly to modern occult transmission rather than Egyptological transliteration.
- **Arabic description:** **حور ور**, *Ḥūr Wer*, used by Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities for the god at Kom Ombo. [Egyptian Ministry, Arabic](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/ar/monuments/kom-ombo-temple)

### Etymology and disputed interpretation

**[EA]** Egyptian *wr* means “great,” “great one,” “chief,” or “elder/senior.” “Horus the Great” is therefore the safest literal translation.

**[LS/MSR]** “Horus the Elder” reflects theological differentiation: the adult or senior Horus is contrasted with the child Horus and, in Plutarch, is born before Seth. It should not be converted into a claim that the name literally and exclusively means “old Horus.”

**[MSR—disputed]** An older proposal treated the name as a compound or fusion of Horus with a separate primordial god **Wer/Ur**. This has not become the dominant explanation; the ordinary adjective *wr* and the parallel epithet *smsw*, “eldest,” provide a less speculative account.

### Major titles and descriptions

These are not all equally old:

- **“Horus the Great/Elder”** — **[EA]**, Middle Kingdom onward.
- **“Horus the Eldest”** — **[EA]**, Coffin Text Spell 280.
- **“Great god”** — **[LC/RV]**, common temple diction.
- **“Father of the gods”** — **[LC/RV]**, especially New Kingdom and later exaltation.
- **“Lord of Ombos/Kom Ombo”** — **[RV]**, Ptolemaic-Roman temple theology.
- **“Foremost of the Two Eyes”** — **[RV/SI]**, associated with Letopolitan ocular theology.
- **“Lord/Possessor of the *iit*-knife, who resides in Letopolis”** — **[RV]**, Kom Ombo inscription; the knife is presented as the weapon with which he attacks his father’s enemies. [IFAO study](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/publications/catalogue/?nif=BIFAO113_art_03.pdf&nv=0)
- **“Lord of Maʿat,” “king of the gods,” and “chief of the Ennead”** — **[RV/LS]**, late temple exaltation.
- **“Strong of arm,” “great of strength,” “lord of slaughter”** — **[RV]**, martial temple epithets.
- **“Horus-Khenty-irty” / “Horus foremost of the two eyes”** — **[SI/RV]**, Letopolitan fusion.
- **“Shu, son of Re”** — **[SI/RV]**, Kom Ombo theological equation.
- **Apollo** — **[SI/LS]**, Greek interpretatio; Plutarch explicitly says that Aroueris is called Apollo.

### Distinction from Horus the Child

**[EA–LC]** Haroeris is not intrinsically a child god. **Horus the Child** (*Ḥr-pꜣ-ẖrd*, Greek Harpocrates) is represented as a naked child with sidelock and finger to mouth; **Horus son of Isis** (*Ḥr-zꜣ-Ꜣst*, Harsiese) is the posthumous heir of Osiris in the Osirian cycle.

**[LC/LS]** These distinctions repeatedly collapse. An adult son of Isis can perform roles earlier belonging to the celestial Horus; Haroeris can be inserted into the family of Nut, become an ally or son of Re, or acquire an Osirian father. The *Contendings of Horus and Seth* explicitly calls its claimant “Horus son of Isis,” so it is not safe to relabel that literary protagonist Haroeris merely because he is an adult who fights Seth.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest securely identified evidence

**[EA]** The TLA’s Ḥr-wr lemma records the divine name between approximately **1759 BCE and 200 CE**, with references to *Wörterbuch der ägyptischen Sprache* and *Lexikon der ägyptischen Götter und Götterbezeichnungen*. This establishes a secure late Middle Kingdom terminus for the named form. [TLA](https://thesaurus-linguae-aegyptiae.de/lemma/107500)

**[EA]** **Coffin Text Spell 280**, preserved on Middle Kingdom coffins, addresses the deceased: “O this Osiris N, you are Horus the Elder,” and closes with the transformation rubric “Becoming Horus the Elder.” The spell places this Horus in Osiris’s house, mourning, while identifying the transformed body with Re, Atum, Sepa, and Iunmutef. It demonstrates that “Horus the Elder” was already a funerary transformation available to a deceased person, not merely a later Kom Ombo temple god. [Open scholarly analysis of CT 280](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/2002779/1/LandborgAnn_Feb2014.pdf)

### Pyramid Texts

**Physical and chronological setting:** Hieroglyphic religious utterances inscribed in royal pyramids beginning with Unas, late Fifth Dynasty, about the twenty-fourth century BCE; later copied and expanded in Sixth-Dynasty pyramids.

**[EA/U]** They contain:

- Horus and Seth as rival and complementary powers;
- the injured Eye of Horus and its restoration;
- the king’s assimilation to Horus;
- Horus of Nekhen and Horus of Letopolis;
- the “four children of Horus”;
- celestial and stellar Horus traditions.

**[MSR—contested]** Sethe and some subsequent translators rendered Pyramid Text §116a as “Haroeris.” Other scholarship observes that the compound name Ḥr-wr is not unambiguously written there. The passage can be read as Horus possessing or receiving something “great,” rather than naming Haroeris. Thus the Pyramid Texts are indispensable prehistory for the figure, but not the safest first attestation of his full name.

**[MSR]** Older histories sometimes spoke of a coherent “Horus-the-Elder stellar religion” visible throughout the Pyramid Texts. The texts are instead heterogeneous mortuary corpora combining solar, stellar, Osirian, royal, and local conceptions; assigning whole theological strata to Haroeris is reconstruction.

### Coffin Texts

**Date and support:** Primarily First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, especially approximately 2100–1650 BCE; painted or written on coffins and other funerary equipment.

- **Spell 280:** transformation into Horus the Elder. **[EA]**
- **Spell 50 and related ocular material:** Horus–Seth injury, sexual or bodily symbolism, and restoration traditions that later appear in fuller narrative form. **[EA/U]**
- **Spell 1143:** situates Horus the Elder among celestial stars. **[EA]**
- **Spell 148 and associated traditions:** connects Isis and Horus with the four protective sons; attribution of their father specifically to Haroeris varies by recension and modern translation. **[EA/U]**

### Book of the Dead

**Date and support:** Evolved from earlier mortuary corpora; conventionally used from the New Kingdom through the Ptolemaic and Roman periods on papyri, coffins, tomb walls, linen, and other supports.

**[LC/SI]** Chapter/Spell 17 is important for the Letopolitan complex. Its glosses identify forms of Horus and include **Horus-Khenty-irty**, sometimes translated “Horus the Eyeless.” It preserves the paradox of a celestial eye-god who is alternately sighted and blind, corresponding to waxing, waning, disappearance, and restoration of the luminaries.

**[LC/U]** General references to the Eye of Horus, Horus’s sons, Horus avenging Osiris, and Horus enthroned are not automatically references to Haroeris. Attribution must depend on an epithet or local context.

### The Shabaka Stone / “Memphite Theology”

**Support and date:** British Museum EA498, a conglomerate slab inscribed under Shabaka, about **710 BCE**, later reused as a millstone. The stone claims that its text was copied from a worm-eaten ancestral document. Modern dating remains debated, but the British Museum notes that the present composition is often assigned to the Twenty-fifth Dynasty; New Kingdom antecedents remain possible. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA498)

**[LS/U]** Geb first divides Egypt between Horus and Seth, then gives the whole inheritance to Horus as the son of his firstborn. Horus and Seth are reconciled in the House of Ptah. The Horus is embedded in an Osirian genealogy; the text does not label him Ḥr-wr. It documents the old Horus–Seth structure and its political use, but identifying him specifically as Haroeris is interpretive. [Lichtheim translation](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/shabaka_stone.html)

### The Contendings of Horus and Seth

**Support and date:** Papyrus Chester Beatty I, recto 1–16, hieratic; Twentieth Dynasty, approximately the twelfth century BCE, probably from Deir el-Medina. [Chester Beatty edition](https://chesterbeatty.ie/assets/uploads/2018/11/The-Library-of-A.-Chestera-Beatty-Description-of-a-Hieratic-Papyrus-with-a-Mythological-Story-Love-Songs-and-Other-Miscellaneous-Texts.pdf)

**[LC]** The claimant is repeatedly **Horus son of Isis**, seeking the office of his father Osiris. The work includes divine litigation, Isis’s deceptions, transformation of Horus and Seth into hippopotamuses, Horus decapitating Isis after she releases Seth, Seth tearing out Horus’s eyes, Hathor restoring sight, Seth’s sexual attempt against Horus, Isis’s counterplot with Horus’s semen, divine judgments, and Horus’s eventual installation.

**Critical finding:** It is a principal source for the broader Horus–Seth cycle but not a clean “Haroeris story.” Treating its Horus as Horus the Elder suppresses the papyrus’s own filial language.

### Turin King List

**Support and date:** Fragmentary hieratic papyrus, written on the reverse of an administrative tax document under Ramesses II, thirteenth century BCE.

**[LS/U]** Its opening columns list divine rulers before human kings. Reconstructions include Horus, Seth, Thoth, Maʿat, another Horus, and the *Shemsu-Hor*, “Followers of Horus.” One damaged entry is sometimes presented as “Horus the Elder,” but the name is incomplete. The list documents a learned scheme in which gods ruled Egypt; it is not historical evidence that Haroeris was remembered as a mortal prehistoric monarch. [Turin list analysis](https://austriaca.at/0xc1aa5576%200x0007e84f.pdf)

### Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*

**Language and date:** Greek, late first or early second century CE.

**[LS/SI]** In section 12, Plutarch says that Osiris was born on the first epagomenal day and **Aroueris** on the second; Aroueris is “called Apollo,” and some call him “the elder Horus.” Seth follows on the third day, Isis on the fourth, and Nephthys on the fifth. This makes Aroueris a child of Nut and a sibling of Osiris, Seth, Isis, and Nephthys—not the son of Isis and Osiris. [Plutarch, section 12](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0239%3Asection%3D12)

**[LS]** Plutarch is late, philosophically interpretive, and dependent on Egyptian priestly information filtered through Greek categories. His testimony is genuine evidence for Greco-Roman learned religion, not direct evidence that this exact five-birth sequence existed unchanged in the Old Kingdom.

### Kom Ombo temple inscriptions

**Date:** Earlier sanctuary activity is attested in the New Kingdom. The surviving double temple began under Ptolemy VI Philometor, 180/186–145 BCE; much decoration belongs to Ptolemy XII, 80–51 BCE; work continued under Roman emperors as late as Macrinus, 217–218 CE. [Egyptian Ministry](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/monuments/kom-ombo-temple) [Austrian Archaeological Institute](https://www.oeaw.ac.at/en/oeai/research/prehistory-wana-archaeology/archaeology-in-egypt-and-sudan/kom-ombo-city-and-hinterland)

**[RV/LS]** This is Haroeris’s richest surviving local corpus. It gives his epithets, family with Tasenetnofret and Panebtawy, solar and Shu identifications, weapons, festivals, offering rituals, enemies, and relationship with Sobek. The theology is late but should not be dismissed: it is primary evidence for what Kom Ombo’s priesthood asserted.

### Greek Magical Papyri

**Date and languages:** Mostly second century BCE through fifth century CE; Greek, Demotic Egyptian, and Old Coptic elements.

**[LS/SI]** Horus appears under Greek and voces-magicae forms; **AROUER** is a Graecized magical rendering of Ḥr-wr. Such occurrences belong to cosmopolitan temple-magical syncretism. They do not show that all solar youths or falcon invocations in the PGM are Haroeris. [Betz translation](https://mahabodhi.saranagamana.org/studies/texts/other/The_Greek_Magical_Papyri_in_Translation.pdf)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Sky and celestial eyes

**[EA/LC]** Haroeris is a sky falcon. His two eyes are understood as the sun and moon. Their injury, disappearance, and restoration account for varying luminosity, especially lunar phases. This conception overlaps with Khenty-irty of Letopolis and with the more general Eye of Horus complex.

**[MSR caution]** “Right eye = sun, left eye = moon” is a useful conventional summary but not an invariant rule in every period and source.

### Kingship and ordered rule

**[EA/LC]** Horus is the divine prototype of living kingship; Haroeris participates in this field as adult sovereign, victorious contender, and “lord of Maʿat.” The king’s Horus-name, however, invokes Horus broadly and cannot normally be narrowed to Haroeris.

**[LS]** The Shabaka Stone makes the Horus–Seth settlement a model for territorial division, reunification, and legitimate inheritance. Ptolemaic temple rulers used Haroeris to present themselves as legitimate Egyptian sovereigns.

### Combat and protection

**[EA–RV]** Haroeris fights Seth or unnamed enemies and protects divine and royal order. At Kom Ombo the *iit*-knife is both his weapon and a theological symbol connected with Letopolis and the returning solar eye. The hostile beings in protective texts are threatened with divine violence; this is apotropaic ritual language rather than evidence for a human military cult.

### Standard iconography

- **Falcon-headed standing man**, often in a short kilt, carrying a *was*-scepter and *ankh*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Falcon-headed man wearing the Double Crown**, emphasizing kingship and rule of the Two Lands. A Late Period lapis-lazuli example, British Museum EA20654, shows this form. [British Museum EA20654](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA20654)
- **Entire falcon**, seated or standing. **[LC/RV/U]** Without an inscription this is usually indistinguishable from other Horus forms.
- **Human form** or **lion form**, and a falcon-headed lion/hieracosphinx. **[RV/late]**
- **Solar disk and uraeus**, or other solar crowns. **[SI/RV]**
- **Mummiform falcon-headed god**, especially in funerary and ritual settings. **[LC/U]**

The British Museum indexes seventy-nine objects under Haroeris, mostly Late Period and Ptolemaic amulets and figures. This abundance demonstrates strong late material circulation but also reflects modern cataloguing decisions. [British Museum Haroeris collection](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/BIOG53586)

### Recognizing him securely

A falcon head alone is insufficient. Secure identification normally requires:

1. a written label **Ḥr-wr**;
2. a specifically Haroeris epithet;
3. a known Kom Ombo or Letopolitan context;
4. placement in his local triad; or
5. a museum record based on an inscription or documented find-context.

### Colors, numbers, days, and celestial correspondences

**[EA–RV]** Sun and moon are securely associated; falcon and lion are documented forms. No stable ancient universal “color of Haroeris,” sacred number, weekday, crystal, or modern astrological correspondence can be established.

**[MI]** Modern webpages assigning him particular crystals, tarot cards, chakras, weekdays, or personality traits generally offer modern occult invention unless they cite a specific temple calendar or ancient text.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

#### Celestial sibling model

**[EA/MSR]** The older Horus–Seth pattern treats the two as peers or brothers. Pyramid and Coffin Text materials preserve rivalry and complementary pairing without consistently making Horus the posthumous child of Osiris.

#### Plutarch’s five siblings

**[LS]** Plutarch makes Aroueris the child of Nut, born on the second epagomenal day, among Osiris, Seth, Isis, and Nephthys. Geb is implicitly the father in the associated Egyptian genealogy. This is the clearest surviving source for Haroeris as Seth’s brother.

#### Kom Ombo solar family

**[RV/SI]** Haroeris can be son of Re and identified with Shu. His principal local consort is **Tasenetnofret**, “the Good/Beautiful Sister,” herself identified in different contexts with Hathor or Tefnut. Their child is **Panebtawy**, “Lord of the Two Lands.”

#### Isis and the Sons of Horus

**[EA/LC, variant]** Coffin Text and funerary traditions can make the four sons of Horus—Imsety, Hapy, Duamutef, and Qebehsenuef—the offspring of Horus the Elder and Isis. Elsewhere Isis is their protector rather than biological mother, and their father is simply “Horus.” This must not be harmonized into the dominant Osiris–Isis–Horus family.

#### Osirian assimilation

**[LC]** When Horus is son and heir of Osiris, the figure is most precisely Harsiese. Later sources and modern summaries often merge this adult triumphant Horus with Haroeris. That merger is historically real, but it does not erase the separate sibling genealogy.

### Documented god–god interactions

The following inventory includes interactions securely attached to Haroeris or to an expressly “elder” Horus, followed by important but attributionally ambiguous Horus material.

#### Secure or locally explicit interactions

- **Seth — sibling, rival, and cosmic counterpart.** Haroeris is Seth’s elder brother in Plutarch’s epagomenal genealogy; broader Egyptian traditions make Horus and Seth combatants whose struggle wounds Horus’s eye. *De Iside* 12, c. 100 CE; Middle Kingdom ocular traditions. **[EA/LS]**
- **Nut — mother.** Nut gives birth to Aroueris on the second added day. Plutarch, *De Iside* 12. **[LS]**
- **Geb — father or ancestor.** Implied by the Nut–Geb family; Geb also judges the Horus–Seth dispute in the Shabaka Stone, though that Horus is Osiris’s heir. **[LS/U]**
- **Osiris — brother in Plutarch; father or funerary prototype in assimilated traditions.** Plutarch makes Osiris and Aroueris siblings; CT 280 makes the deceased simultaneously Osiris N and Horus the Elder. **[EA/LS]**
- **Isis — sister in Plutarch; consort and mother of his four sons in a funerary variant.** Plutarch, c. 100 CE; Coffin Text-derived tradition. **[EA/LS, conflicting roles]**
- **Nephthys — sister.** Plutarch’s epagomenal genealogy. **[LS]**
- **Re — source, father, or identity.** Kom Ombo calls Haroeris son of Re and can solarize him; his eyes are the luminaries. **[RV/SI]**
- **Shu — explicit theological equation.** Kom Ombo identifies Haroeris with Shu, fitting his role as celestial light/air and son of Re. **[RV/SI]**
- **Tasenetnofret — consort.** She forms the Haroeris triad with him at Kom Ombo. **[RV]**
- **Panebtawy — son.** Child of Haroeris and Tasenetnofret, representing rule of the Two Lands. **[RV]**
- **Sobek — co-resident, parallel sovereign, and theological complement.** The Kom Ombo temple is divided into parallel northern Haroeris and southern Sobek axes; their priestly theologies coexist rather than documenting a primordial personal feud. **[RV]**
- **Khenty-irty/Mekhenty-irty — fusion.** At Letopolis the sighted/blind local falcon-eye god is identified with Horus; later theology associates or fuses him with Haroeris. **[SI/RV]**
- **Hathor — identification with or theological background of Tasenetnofret; ocular healer in general Horus tradition.** Direct consortship varies by place. **[RV/SI]**
- **Tefnut — identified with Tasenetnofret and with the wandering solar eye whom a solar/warrior god retrieves.** Kom Ombo theology makes this relevant to Haroeris. **[RV/SI]**
- **Imsety — son/protective attendant in the Haroeris–Isis variant.** Coffin/funerary tradition. **[EA/LC]**
- **Hapy — son/protective attendant in the same variant.** **[EA/LC]**
- **Duamutef — son/protective attendant in the same variant.** **[EA/LC]**
- **Qebehsenuef — son/protective attendant in the same variant.** **[EA/LC]**
- **Apollo — Greek equation rather than narrative interaction.** Plutarch identifies Aroueris as Apollo. **[SI/LS]**

#### Important Horus interactions not securely assignable to Haroeris

- **Thoth — restores or presents the Eye and arbitrates the Horus–Seth dispute.** Pyramid/Coffin traditions and *Contendings*. **[EA/LC/U]**
- **Neith — advises that Horus receive Osiris’s office and that Seth be compensated.** *Contendings*. Its protagonist is Horus son of Isis. **[LC/U]**
- **Atum/Re-Horakhty — presides over the divine tribunal and initially questions Horus’s fitness.** *Contendings*. **[LC/U]**
- **Hathor — heals Horus’s blinded eyes with gazelle milk.** *Contendings*. **[LC/U]**
- **Babi — threatens or humiliates Horus in the tribunal.** *Contendings*. **[LC/U]**
- **Banebdjedet — consulted as divine judge.** *Contendings*. **[LC/U]**
- **Ptah-Tatenen — invoked in the divine legal dispute and creator in the Shabaka theology.** **[LC/U]**
- **Onuris — linked indirectly through the returning distant-eye myth and the *iit*-offering at Kom Ombo.** **[RV/SI]**

No primary text establishes a personal interaction between Haroeris and every member of the Egyptian pantheon. Shared appearance in a divine catalogue is not by itself an interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Horus and Seth as rival brothers or peers

**[EA]** The early recoverable core is not a single continuous tale but a network of ritual allusions. Horus and Seth injure one another: Horus loses or damages an eye, while Seth suffers injury, often to his testicles. The Eye is recovered, healed, filled, or presented, becoming the **wedjat**, the restored/whole eye and a paradigmatic offering.

**Development:** The conflict functions as cosmic opposition, political duality, and a model of injury followed by restoration. It should not be reduced to a remembered war between prehistoric Upper and Lower Egypt. Henri Frankfort criticized precisely that projection: the sources use Horus and Seth as symbolic antagonists within dual kingship and cosmic order, not as transparent records of tribal armies. [Frankfort](https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/kingship.pdf)

**[MSR—discredited when stated as fact]** “Horus was Upper Egypt, Seth was Lower Egypt, and Narmer’s conquest produced the myth” is too simple. Sources assign regions inconsistently, and royal ideology can require both gods.

### 5.2 The damaged eyes: sun, moon, blindness, and restoration

**[EA–RV]** A sky god whose eyes are sun and moon can become “without eyes” when the luminaries disappear and “with eyes” when they return. Letopolitan Khenty-irty/Mekhenty-irty theology exploits wordplay between “he who has two eyes” and “he who is without two eyes.” Horus’s restored eye becomes health, completeness, offering, and legitimate sovereignty.

**[SI/RV]** Haroeris’s association with Khenty-irty makes ocular theology central at Letopolis. At Kom Ombo an Eye appears within the *iit*-sign offered to Haroeris; the sign is simultaneously his weapon and a reference to Letopolis. [IFAO](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/publications/catalogue/?nif=BIFAO113_art_03.pdf&nv=0)

### 5.3 The divine inheritance and reconciliation

**[LS/U]** On the Shabaka Stone, Geb assigns Lower Egypt to Horus and Upper Egypt to Seth, bringing peace. He subsequently judges equal portions inappropriate and gives Horus the complete inheritance because Horus is the son of his firstborn, Osiris. Horus and Seth are then fraternized in Ptah’s house.

This is not the brother-Haroeris genealogy; it is an Osirian political theology. It nevertheless preserves the same fundamental opposition and reconciliation that underlie Haroeris’s relation to Seth.

### 5.4 The *Contendings*

**[LC/U]** The fullest Egyptian narrative survives on Papyrus Chester Beatty I. Horus son of Isis and Seth litigate for approximately eighty years. Neith endorses Horus but proposes compensation for Seth. Isis deceives Seth into condemning his own seizure of another’s inheritance. Horus and Seth compete as hippopotamuses; Isis harpoons first Horus and then Seth, but releases Seth after he appeals to their kinship. Horus, enraged, removes Isis’s head; Thoth replaces it with a bovine head in Gardiner’s reconstruction. Seth finds Horus, removes his eyes, and buries them; Hathor restores his sight. Seth then attempts to establish sexual dominance, but Horus catches Seth’s semen, while Isis places Horus’s semen on lettuce eaten by Seth. Thoth summons the semen; Seth’s claim of dominance is publicly reversed when Horus’s seed emerges from Seth. Further boat and stone-boat contests follow. Osiris threatens cosmic consequences from the underworld, and Horus finally receives the throne; Seth is taken into Re’s company.

**Attributional ruling:** The story is essential comparative material, but its protagonist is textually “Horus son of Isis,” not explicitly Haroeris. Modern retellings that title it “Horus the Elder versus Seth” conflate two stages of the tradition.

### 5.5 Birth on the epagomenal days

**[LS]** Plutarch tells how Hermes/Thoth won portions of moonlight and added five days to the 360-day year, permitting Nut to give birth despite a prohibition. Osiris is born first, Aroueris second, Seth third, Isis fourth, and Nephthys fifth. Aroueris is equated with Apollo.

This is the most explicit Haroeris birth story. It is late and Greek-mediated, though built from Egyptian calendar and genealogy traditions.

### 5.6 The distant solar eye

**[RV/SI]** The sun god’s eye, often a dangerous lioness such as Tefnut, departs to a foreign land and must be persuaded or compelled to return. At Kom Ombo Haroeris’s solar, Shu, and warrior identities allow him to participate in this complex, while the *iit*-offering may symbolize both his weapon and the returning lioness. Versions assigning the retrieval to Shu, Onuris, Thoth, or another deity should not be silently merged.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Letopolis

**Location:** Egyptian **Khem**, Greek Letopolis, modern Ausim, capital of the second Lower Egyptian nome.

**[EA/SI/RV]** Its primary falcon god was Khenty-irty/Khenty-khem, a cosmic ocular deity. Horus of Letopolis is attested in early mortuary religion, and later Haroeris theology absorbs or is identified with this god. The four sons of Horus have a particularly important Letopolitan affiliation.

**Cult functions:** celestial sight, sun and moon, restored vision, royal and funerary protection, knives and ritual weapons, and craftsmanship associated with divine bodily restoration.

**Evidence limitation:** The relationship “Haroeris = Khenty-irty” is strongly attested as later association/fusion; it does not prove that every early Khenty-irty reference originally meant Haroeris under another name.

### Kom Ombo

**Ancient name:** Nubt/Nebyt; modern Kom Ombo in Upper Egypt.

**[RV]** The visible temple is a highly unusual double sanctuary:

- northern/left axis: Haroeris;
- southern/right axis: Sobek;
- duplicated entrances, halls, and sanctuaries;
- Haroeris’s local family: Tasenetnofret and Panebtawy;
- Sobek’s parallel family: Hathor and Khonsu.

An earlier New Kingdom temple existed. The present monument began under Ptolemy VI; Ptolemy XII completed much decoration; Roman work continued through Macrinus. [Egyptian Ministry](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/monuments/kom-ombo-temple)

The temple’s inscriptions preserve:

- daily offerings and purification;
- calendrical festival lists;
- processions;
- presentation of food, drink, cloth, crowns, weapons, Maʿat, and divine-eye symbols;
- king-as-priest scenes;
- theological equations with Re and Shu;
- destruction of divine enemies in image and word.

An inscription on the shrine of Haroeris, conventionally OGIS 114, records Greek-era royal benefaction and is evidence for the temple’s institutional history. [OGIS 114](https://www.attalus.org/docs/ogis/s114.html)

### Priesthood and economy

**[RV/archaeological]** Kom Ombo had temple personnel serving the two divine houses within one architectural institution. Titles naming servants or priests of “Sobek and Horus” occur in late evidence. Like other major Ptolemaic temples, it controlled land, offerings, workshops, storage, ritual knowledge, and priestly offices; a Haroeris-specific economic archive has not survived intact enough to reconstruct its complete finances.

### Votive and magical practice

**[LC]** Falcon figurines, amulets, wedjat-eyes, and multi-deity protective objects attest personal religious use. British Museum EA20775, a fourth-century BCE wooden amulet from Akhmim, depicts Ptah, Min, Thoth, Haroeris, Isis, and Nephthys; its incantation threatens hostile beings and protects its wearer. [British Museum EA20775](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA20775)

### Sacrifice and contested practices

**[Documented]** Standard offerings included bread, beer, wine, incense, flowers, meat, fowl, produce, cloth, and symbolic presentations. Enemy-smashing and slaughter language is widespread in temple ritual.

**[Not established]** No surviving evidence demonstrates a regular cult of human sacrifice to Haroeris. Early Dynastic retainer deaths and Classical allegations about Egyptian human sacrifice are separate, contested questions. They cannot be assigned to Haroeris merely because he has martial titles. Jean Yoyotte’s review emphasizes how little many Greek and Roman allegations establish about actual Egyptian practice. [Yoyotte](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1980_num_93_89_18352)

**[Not established]** There is no credible evidence for “temple prostitution” in Haroeris’s cult. Neither Kom Ombo’s inscriptions nor its archaeology documents such an institution.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Predynastic and Early Dynastic background, before c. 2686 BCE

**[MSR/U]** Falcon gods, falcon standards, and royal Horus symbolism are securely present. Assigning them specifically to Haroeris is not secure because **Ḥr-wr** is not written. Modern claims that a Predynastic falcon is “the earliest depiction of Horus the Elder” overstate the evidence.

### Old Kingdom, c. 2686–2181 BCE

**[EA/U]** Pyramid Texts document Horus–Seth opposition, Horus of Nekhen and Letopolis, the Eye, royal Horus, and sons of Horus. These are major antecedents. The explicit Ḥr-wr reading remains disputed.

### First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, c. 2181–1650 BCE

**[EA]** This is the first securely documentable phase of Haroeris as a named or explicitly “elder” Horus. Coffin Text Spell 280 turns the deceased into Horus the Elder. Celestial, funerary, and Osirian roles coexist.

### New Kingdom, c. 1550–1069 BCE

**[LC]** Haroeris becomes more prominent and receives exalted titles. Earlier building activity existed at Kom Ombo. Ramesside texts expand Horus–Seth narrative and learned divine kingship, although not every Horus is Haroeris.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods, c. 1069–332 BCE

**[LC/SI]** Local Horus forms proliferate and fuse. Haroeris is increasingly visible in amulets and protective imagery; Letopolitan eye theology, funerary sons, solar identity, and royal imagery intersect. TLA records priestly titles connected with “Horus the Great” and dual uraei.

### Ptolemaic period, 332–30 BCE

**[RV/peak]** Haroeris’s best-documented institutional zenith is Kom Ombo. Ptolemy VI begins the surviving double temple; subsequent Ptolemies enlarge and decorate it. His local theology is systematized alongside Sobek’s, and his family with Tasenetnofret and Panebtawy becomes monumental.

Calling this his “peak” refers to the density of surviving inscriptions and architecture, not necessarily to the number of worshippers across Egypt.

### Roman period, 30 BCE–fourth century CE

**[RV/SI]** Temple work continues into the early third century. Plutarch records Aroueris and equates him with Apollo. Magical papyri transmit Egyptian divine names into Greek and multilingual ritual. Haroeris remains visually and ritually Egyptian even as Greek equations accumulate.

### Christianization and closure

**[Historical process]** Egyptian temple cults declined unevenly through loss of funding, imperial policy, changing local patronage, Christian opposition, and conversion of sacred spaces. Theodosius I’s late-fourth-century prohibitions and subsequent local actions accelerated closure, but no surviving decree records a unique empire-wide campaign specifically against Haroeris.

**[U]** Kom Ombo’s formal cult probably ended in Late Antiquity, but the exact final Haroeris ceremony and date are unknown. Claims that Christians personally “killed the worship of Haroeris” in a single documented event are not supported.

### Islamic period to early modern rediscovery

**[F/archaeological afterlife]** The temple remained a visible ruin within an inhabited landscape. No continuous medieval Islamic cult of Haroeris is documented. Classical and antiquarian literature preserved the name, while local knowledge preserved the monument without necessarily preserving the ancient theology.

### Modern archaeological recovery

- **1894–1909:** de Morgan, Bouriant, Legrain, Jéquier, Barsanti, and André published *Kom Ombos*.
- **1895:** the temple was cleared.
- **1953–61:** André Gutbub conducted five inscription-recording seasons.
- **1973:** Gutbub published *Textes fondamentaux de la théologie de Kom Ombo*.
- **From 2010:** renewed epigraphic work addressed deficiencies in older publication.
- **From 2017:** Austrian-Egyptian research expanded study from the temple to the ancient city and hinterland. [IFAO](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/archeologie/kom-ombo/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Egyptology and museum culture

**[MSR]** Haroeris became a standard named “form of Horus” in handbooks, museum catalogues, and comparative mythology. This classification is useful but can create sharper boundaries than Egyptian evidence warrants. British Museum indexing, for example, assigns dozens of late falcon amulets to Haroeris, sometimes on typological rather than inscribed grounds.

### Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn

**[MI]** Late nineteenth-century Golden Dawn ritual adopted **Aroueris/Hoor-Ouer** as a magical god-form. In the reconstructed temple, the Hierophant ordinarily bears the god-form of Osiris while enthroned and assumes Aroueris as an active, moving form. Modern Golden Dawn descriptions prescribe colors, garments, wand, crook, scourge, and ritual station.

These elaborate color correspondences are not recovered Kom Ombo liturgy. They are Victorian ceremonial-magic constructions using Egyptological names within Hermetic-Qabalistic ritual. Israel Regardie’s twentieth-century publication transmitted them widely. [Regardie text](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf)

### Thelema and successor occultism

**[MI]** Golden Dawn-derived orders retain Aroueris as an initiatory and protective god-form. Thelemic literature more often emphasizes Ra-Hoor-Khuit, Hoor-paar-kraat, and the “Crowned and Conquering Child”; equating every Thelemic Horus with Haroeris is incorrect.

### Kemetic reconstructionism and Kemetic Orthodoxy

**[MI with ancient-source engagement]** Modern practitioners worship **Heru-wer** as a living deity, commonly emphasizing sky, kingship, maturity, law, and the complementary opposition of Heru-wer and Set. Kemetic Orthodoxy includes Heru-wer among its “Names of Netjer” and uses Heru-wer and Set in modern royal symbolism. [Kemet.org](https://www.kemet.org/taxonomy/term/81)

This is genuine contemporary religion, but claims of unbroken institutional succession from Kom Ombo are not documented. Modern ritual necessarily reconstructs, selects, and innovates because no complete Haroeris liturgy survived in continuous practice.

### Popular culture

**[MI]** Haroeris appears less often under his own name than generic Horus. Games, comics, novels, tarot products, and fantasy encyclopedias routinely merge:

- Haroeris;
- Horus son of Isis;
- Horus the Child;
- Ra-Horakhty;
- Horus Behdety;
- the Eye of Horus.

A modern falcon warrior avenging Osiris is normally a composite popular Horus, even when labeled “Horus the Elder.”

### Political and racial appropriation

**[MI]** Generic Horus imagery has been enlisted in Egyptian nationalism, Afrocentric religious movements, Western esotericism, and commercial “ancient astronaut” narratives. No major extremist movement centered specifically on Haroeris could be securely established from the reviewed evidence. Assertions that Haroeris encodes a technologically advanced prehistoric ruler, alien visitor, or lost white/black master race are modern inventions unsupported by Egyptian texts.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Haroeris the “original Horus”?

**Position A:** Haroeris preserves the oldest cosmic Horus, later displaced or absorbed by Horus son of Isis.

**Evidence:** early Horus–Seth pairing; celestial eyes; royal falcon; Letopolitan material; sibling rather than filial genealogy.

**Problem:** the name Ḥr-wr is securely attested only in the Middle Kingdom, and the early sources speak of multiple Horuses without consistently distinguishing “elder” and “younger.”

**Assessment:** **[MSR]** Haroeris preserves old Horus concepts, but calling every early Horus “Horus the Elder” is circular.

### Meaning of Ḥr-wr

- “Horus the Great”: literal and philologically safest.
- “Horus the Elder”: contextually justified, especially against child Horus and through *smsw*.
- Horus fused with primordial Wer: possible older theory, weakly established.

### Haroeris and Khenty-irty

**Strong association:** both are falcon/sky gods whose eyes are sun and moon; Letopolis is central; later compounds explicitly join Horus and Khenty-irty.

**Open question:** whether Haroeris was simply a later name for Khenty-irty, or whether two theological identities converged. Current evidence supports fusion and functional overlap more securely than original identity.

### Which Horus fights Seth?

Different sources answer differently:

- an early peer/brother Horus;
- Horus son of Isis and heir of Osiris;
- a royal Horus embodying kingship;
- local martial Horuses in temple ritual.

There is no warrant for forcing these into one biography. The *Contendings* names the son of Isis; Plutarch’s Aroueris is Seth’s brother.

### Dating the Shabaka Stone’s content

Older scholarship accepted Shabaka’s assertion that he copied a remote Old Kingdom document. Linguistic and ideological studies have instead dated the composition variously to the New Kingdom or Shabaka’s own reign. The physical inscription is securely c. 710 BCE. Its archaizing claim is evidence for Twenty-fifth-Dynasty cultural politics, not proof of an Old Kingdom manuscript.

### Political-historical interpretations

Early Egyptologists sometimes treated the Horus–Seth conflict as a literal memory of dynastic wars. Later scholarship emphasized structural opposition, royal duality, legal ideology, and ritual reconciliation. Political readings remain possible in particular recensions, but no single prehistoric war explains the whole complex.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is unusually uneven:

- early royal and funerary texts are terse, allusive, and often omit local epithets;
- Middle Kingdom coffins give the first secure “elder” designation;
- New Kingdom literary texts expand narrative but usually concern Horus son of Isis;
- Ptolemaic temples offer immense theological detail, but it is late, local, and priestly;
- Greek writers preserve otherwise lost explanations but translate them into Greek philosophy and divine equivalence;
- many temple inscriptions remain incompletely published or require improved recording.

The IFAO notes that the original *Kom Ombos* publication contains errors and lacunae and lacked adequate photographs, explaining why Haroeris’s richest corpus remains less accessible than Edfu’s or Dendera’s. [IFAO](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/archeologie/kom-ombo/)

### Frequently repeated but unsupported claims

- **“Haroeris is securely depicted on Predynastic pottery.”** **[U/MSR]** Falcon imagery is secure; the named identity is not.
- **“He was always the brother of Seth.”** **False as a universal statement.** It is one major genealogy.
- **“He is simply Horus son of Isis grown up.”** **False as a universal statement.** Ancient sources both distinguish and merge them.
- **“The sun is his right eye and the moon his left in every source.”** **Over-systematized.**
- **“Haroeris had a cult of human sacrifice or temple prostitution.”** **Not established.**
- **“His Golden Dawn color scheme is ancient Egyptian.”** **Modern invention.**
- **“The surgical-instrument relief at Kom Ombo proves a hospital of Haroeris.”** **Unproven.** The objects’ medical interpretation remains debated, and the relief does not by itself establish a temple hospital.
