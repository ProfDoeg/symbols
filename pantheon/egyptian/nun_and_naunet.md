# Nun and Naunet: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Nun is both the impersonal primeval water or abyss and its male divine personification; Naunet is a goddess, the grammatically feminine counterpart of that water. In later Hermopolitan theology they form one male–female pair within a collective of eight primordial gods, the Ogdoad.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the late Old Kingdom *Pyramid Texts*, especially Utterance 301 (§§446–448), preserved in royal-pyramid copies of the later third millennium BCE. It invokes Nun and Naunet together as a primordial pair and “sources/parents of the gods.” The Pyramid Text corpus begins in the pyramid of Unas, about 2350 BCE, although individual utterances have different surviving distributions and may be older than their inscriptions. The fully enumerated, locally defined Hermopolitan Ogdoad is not comparably secure this early; its explicit monumental attestation belongs to the New Kingdom.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

**[Securely attested early material]** Egyptian sources distinguish—but also deliberately overlap—three things:

1. **Nun as a condition or substance:** the limitless, dark, still water existing before differentiated creation.
2. **Nun as a male god:** a speaking, acting personification who advises Atum, supports the renewed sun, and can be called “father of the gods.”
3. **Naunet as the feminine counterpart:** a goddess produced through the Egyptian habit of expressing primordial totality in male–female pairs. In some early passages *nnt* may instead denote a lower or counter-sky, so not every occurrence is unquestionably the personal name Naunet.

Creation does not eliminate Nun. Water beneath the earth, temple lakes, the Nile’s hidden source, the nocturnal solar realm, and the dangerous possibility of cosmic dissolution could all be construed as its continuing presence. This is why “chaos” is only an approximate translation: Nun is undifferentiated, unbounded, and outside ordered creation, but also fertile and necessary.

### Egyptian names

- **Nun:** conventionally transliterated *Nwn*, *Nnw*, *Nw*, or *Nūn*. Modern spellings include **Nun**, **Nu**, **Nuun**, **Nenu**, and older Egyptological **Noun**.
- **Naunet:** *Nwnt*, *Nnt*, or *Nūnet*; modern forms include **Naunet**, **Nunet**, **Nunet**, **Naune**, and **Nenet**.
- The feminine ending *-t* creates the female form, but the vocalizations “Nun” and “Naunet” are modern scholarly conventions because Egyptian hieroglyphic writing normally omits vowels.
- Hieroglyphic spellings vary by period. Water signs, plural water pots, sky signs, deity determinatives, and male or female determinatives may be employed. Font-independent Unicode strings are unreliable substitutes for the actual facsimiles and should not be treated as a single canonical spelling.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The name has been connected through Egyptian wordplay with *nnj*, “to be weary/inert.” That suits descriptions of Atum and Nun in precreation, but it is not a secure historical derivation. “The Inert One” is therefore an interpretive gloss, not a directly recoverable proper-name meaning. The Global Egyptian Museum describes the wordplay rather than presenting it as settled etymology.

### Titles and descriptions

The following are descriptions or titles actually grounded in Egyptian sources, rather than a timeless canonical list:

- **“Father of the gods”** — explicit in the Middle Kingdom antecedent of Book of the Dead Spell 17 and its New Kingdom descendants: “there is water; there is Nun, father of the gods.”
- **“Father of fathers” / “primeval father”** — a New Kingdom royal epithet in the Speos Artemidos inscription of Hatshepsut; whether every occurrence should be translated specifically as Nun depends on context.
- **Source, progenitor, or protector of the gods** — Pyramid Text Utterance 301 addresses Nun and Naunet as a pair associated with the gods’ origin and protective shade.
- **The abyss, flood, or primeval water** — translations of contextual uses of *nwn*; English editions vary between “Nun,” “the Waters,” “the Deep,” and “the Flood.”
- **“The Eight,” “fathers and mothers,” “primeval gods,” and “ancestors”** — collective titles applied to the Ogdoad in New Kingdom and especially Late and Graeco-Roman temple theology.

No securely attested fixed sacred color, weekday, number, festival day, or planet belongs uniquely to Nun or Naunet. Blue or blue-green skin is iconographic water symbolism; eight belongs to the collective Ogdoad, not exclusively to this pair.

### Distinctions that should not be collapsed

- **Nun is not Nut.** Nut is the female sky goddess, although the consonantal spellings and cosmological relations have encouraged confusion.
- **Naunet is not simply an early independent “wife of Nun.”** That description is convenient for the later Ogdoad but may over-personalize the early female/counter-sky term.
- **Nun is not the Nile god Hapy.** The Nile’s hidden source could be imagined in Nun, but Hapy personifies inundation and abundance in a different way.
- **Nun is not the biblical Noah, Hebrew *nun*, or the Mesopotamian *Anunnaki*.** Such equations are modern folk-etymological inventions without linguistic or documentary support.
- Greek and Roman writers did not establish a stable *interpretatio graeca* identifying Nun with Oceanus, Pontus, or Chaos. These are useful modern comparisons, not ancient equations unless a particular bilingual source says so.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Pyramid Texts

**Date and support:** Old Egyptian hieroglyphic spells carved on the walls and sarcophagi of royal pyramids at Saqqara, beginning with Unas near the end of Dynasty 5 and continuing through Dynasties 6–8, approximately 2350–2100 BCE. Later copies survive on Middle Kingdom and later monuments. UCL’s survey identifies Sethe’s edition, Faulkner’s translation, and James P. Allen’s modern translation as the principal points of entry.

**Principal evidence:**

- **Utterance 301, §§446–448:** invokes Nun with Naunet, then Amun with Amunet, and Atum with the Two Lions/Shu and Tefnut. An older translation calls Nun and Naunet “two sources of the gods” who protect the gods with shade. This is the clearest early paired occurrence.
- Other passages place the king or creator in, above, or emerging from primeval water, but the spelling is not always the later standard divine name *Nwn*. A modern study accordingly warns that it is unsafe to count every translated “Nun” as an identical orthographic divine person.
- The term *nnt* occurs in senses connected with a lower sky or counter-heaven. Only passages whose grammar and pairing demand a goddess should be confidently assigned to Naunet.

**What this proves:** late Old Kingdom theologians possessed a concept of preexistent water and could personify it as a male–female divine pair.

**What it does not prove:** that a standardized Ogdoad with exactly the later four pairs, frog-headed males, snake-headed females, a lotus, cosmic egg, and fixed Hermopolitan narrative already existed in its Ptolemaic form.

### Coffin Texts

**Date and support:** Middle Egyptian funerary compositions painted or carved mainly on nonroyal coffins, burial-chamber walls, and related objects, principally about 2100–1650 BCE. Adriaan de Buck’s seven-volume *Egyptian Coffin Texts* remains the fundamental hieroglyphic edition; Faulkner supplied the standard complete English translation.

**Key sequence: Spells 75–80, the “Shu theology.”**

- Spell 80 presents Atum before the establishment of sky, earth, Heliopolis, or a place to stand.
- Atum says that he is alone with Nun in weariness or inertness.
- Nun speaks to Atum and counsels him concerning Shu and Tefnut and the establishment of ordered existence.
- The spell invokes eight primordial beings, but one enumeration uses **Tenem/Tenemet**, “wandering” or “gloom,” rather than the later standardized Amun/Amunet pair.

This is essential evidence against treating the Ogdoad as an eternally fixed roster. The Middle Kingdom text knows an eightfold primordial complex, but its members and its relationship to Atum and Shu remain fluid.

### Book of the Dead

The so-called Book of the Dead is a variable collection of funerary spells, not a single canonical book. Its New Kingdom papyrus tradition begins around 1550 BCE, while many compositions derive from the Coffin Texts; manuscripts continue into the Ptolemaic and Roman periods.

#### Spell 17

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Spell 17 develops a glossed creation theology:

- The speaker identifies with a self-generating great god.
- The commentary explains: “there is water; there is Nun, father of the gods.”
- Ra creates his own body, and the gods in his following take shape.
- Another passage places the first rising of Ra at high ground in Khemenu/Hermopolis, amid the “supports of Nun.”

Because Spell 17 embeds multiple glosses and alternate explanations, it preserves theological commentary rather than a single seamless narrative. Its variations are evidence of active scribal interpretation.

#### Spell 175 and the end of creation

**[Later canonical elaboration, New Kingdom onward]** Atum tells Osiris that he will eventually destroy what he made:

> “This land shall return into the Deep, into the flood, as it was aforetime.”

Atum says that he and Osiris will remain after assuming serpent forms unknown to humans and unseen by gods. The “Deep” is Nun: ordered land returns to its precreative watery condition.

The spell does **not** explicitly say that a second creation follows. Since Nun is also the matrix from which creation emerged, modern interpreters sometimes infer cyclic regeneration, but this is an inference. The text’s stated event is dissolution and survival of Atum and Osiris.

### Memphite Theology: the Shabaka Stone

The British Museum Shabaka Stone, inscribed under Shabaka of Dynasty 25, about 710 BCE, claims to reproduce an older worm-eaten document. Its language contains archaisms, but the once-common claim that it is a straightforward Old Kingdom copy is disputed; many scholars locate the composition or substantial editorial form in the first millennium BCE.

The text identifies:

- **Ptah-Nun** as the father who generated Atum;
- **Ptah-Naunet** as the mother who bore Atum.

**[Learned priestly systematization]** This is not simply a record of an original universal Egyptian creed. It is Memphite theological appropriation: Ptah is placed behind the Heliopolitan creator by absorbing the generative roles of Nun and Naunet. The pairing can be expressed through male organ/father and female womb/mother imagery, while Ptah ultimately creates through heart and tongue.

### New Kingdom royal and underworld texts

- **Speos Artemidos inscription of Hatshepsut, fifteenth century BCE:** mentions “the Eight” and also invokes a “father of fathers” interpreted as Nun. The inscription is the earliest secure monumental reference to the Ogdoad as such, although its primary purpose is royal restoration ideology.
- **Book of Gates**, first securely monumentalized in the New Kingdom: at the conclusion of the sun’s nocturnal journey, a colossal anthropomorphic Nun raises the solar bark and renewed sun from the waters. Versions occur in the Osireion of Seti I and royal tombs, including KV9 of Ramesses V–VI.
- **Amduat, Book of Caverns, Book of the Earth, and Book of Night:** their cosmographies presuppose waters surrounding or underlying the created world, but Nun is not equally individualized in every scene.

### The Contendings of Horus and Seth

Papyrus Chester Beatty I, reign of Ramesses V, about the twelfth century BCE, preserves the principal version. Nun and Naunet do not play a substantive named role in the surviving narrative. The tale’s divine court, letters, sexual deception, transformations, and contests should not be retroactively described as actions by Nun merely because the world exists within the broader Egyptian cosmos.

**Absence finding:** no discrete Nun–Horus or Naunet–Seth encounter is documented in the principal text.

### Turin Royal Canon

The fragmentary hieratic papyrus dates to Dynasty 19, probably the reign of Ramesses II. It begins with gods and divine or semi-divine rulers before historical kings. Nun and Naunet are not securely preserved as reigning monarchs in its extant divine sequence. The document therefore supports Egyptian placement of divine rule before human kingship, but not the popular claim that Nun personally appears in the surviving king list.

### Late and Graeco-Roman temple texts

The fullest Hermopolitan narratives are scattered through inscriptions rather than preserved as one “Hermopolitan Book of Creation.” Significant witnesses occur at:

- **Karnak and Medinet Habu/Djeme:** Theban theology makes the Ogdoad ancestors of Amun, brings them from or returns them to Thebes, and locates their burial at Djeme.
- **Edfu:** texts connect the primeval water, the first mound, the creation of a sacred domain, and recurring temple foundation. Nun also appears in lotus-offering terminology.
- **Dendera:** Roman-period reliefs show frog-headed males and snake-headed females of the Ogdoad adoring Hathor and Ihy or participating in solar-birth symbolism.
- **Esna:** Khnum-centered theology absorbs and reorganizes primordial creation motifs.
- **Philae:** reliefs and inscriptions incorporate the Ogdoad into Isis-centered temple theology.
- **Deir el-Medina, Hathor-and-Maat temple:** Ptolemaic ritual scenes show royal offering to Ogdoad members.
- **Saft el-Henna/Bubastis naos of Nectanebo I:** fragmentary reliefs display unusual Ogdoad iconography, demonstrating that the sex-specific frog/snake rule was not mechanically uniform.

These inscriptions are late but primary. They should not be dismissed as degeneration; they document how Egyptian priestly culture continued to create internally learned syntheses.

### Plutarch

Plutarch’s *De Iside et Osiride*, composed in Greek around the late first or early second century CE, is indispensable for contemporary philosophical interpretation of Egyptian religion. Nevertheless, it does not preserve a full named narrative of Nun and Naunet comparable to the Egyptian temple texts. Its discussions of water, generative moisture, darkness, Osiris, Typhon, and Egyptian cosmogony should not automatically be assigned to Nun. Plutarch is evidence for Roman-period reception, not a substitute for hieroglyphic sources.

### Greek Magical Papyri

The *Papyri Graecae Magicae* are manuscripts and compositions from approximately the second century BCE through fifth century CE, mainly in Greek with Demotic and Coptic material.

- **PGM VII and XII** contain phrases translated or annotated as “I am he who came forth from Nun/the abyss.”
- **PGM XXI.1–29**, in scholarly interpretation, invokes the Hermopolitan pairs as eight guardians.
- Magical names are often corrupt, bilingual, or intentionally nonsemantic; an apparent divine name cannot always be securely restored.
- These texts attest continuing operative use of Egyptian cosmogony in syncretic ritual. They do not demonstrate a distinct mass cult of Nun.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Secure early core]**

- preexistent water;
- the undifferentiated condition before sky, earth, light, locality, and ordered time;
- the medium from which the creator and created land emerge;
- water beneath and beyond the created world;
- potential return of creation to undifferentiation.

**[Middle and New Kingdom elaboration]**

- fatherhood or parenthood of the gods;
- counsel to Atum;
- daily solar renewal;
- the nocturnal and subterranean waters;
- the cosmic support from which the sun bark rises.

**[Temple-theological extension]**

- sacred lakes as localized manifestations or analogues of Nun;
- temple foundation as a repetition of the first mound’s emergence;
- lotus, cosmic egg, child sun, and primeval island narratives;
- Nile-source and cataract associations.

A sacred lake was not necessarily a freestanding “temple of Nun.” Temple water enabled purification and materially represented the primeval waters surrounding the sacred mound that the temple itself reenacted.

### Iconography

1. **Anthropomorphic Nun:** a male deity, sometimes blue or blue-green, with divine beard and wig. In the final Book of Gates scene he rises from water and lifts the solar bark.
2. **Ogdoad form:** male members usually appear as frog-headed men or frogs; female members as cobra- or snake-headed women or snakes. This convention is especially prominent in Late, Ptolemaic, and Roman temples.
3. **Fully anthropomorphic Ogdoad:** some scenes show the pairs as ordinary male and female deities, identifiable principally by labels.
4. **Baboons:** the Ogdoad can appear as baboons greeting the newborn sun, transferring familiar dawn iconography into a primordial setting.
5. **Naunet:** individual depictions are uncommon outside collective Ogdoad scenes. Identifying an unlabeled snake-headed goddess as Naunet is unsafe because many Egyptian goddesses and protective beings use serpentine forms.
6. **Nun as environment rather than figure:** water lines, the submerged register, a lake, or the space beneath the solar bark may signify *nwn* without a labeled anthropomorphic god.

### Symbols and associated phenomena

- Water and water signs;
- the first mound or high ground;
- the lotus bearing the child sun, in late versions;
- the cosmic egg, also a variant rather than a universal element;
- frogs and snakes as liminal aquatic/chthonic creatures;
- solar bark and solar disk;
- temple lakes and purified water.

No primary evidence establishes a uniquely Nun-specific weapon, sacred plant, precious stone, weekday, planet, or standardized private altar equipment.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other divine beings

### Genealogical systems

| System | Position of Nun and Naunet | Evidence and date | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early mortuary theology | Primordial paired sources or protectors of gods | Pyramid Text Utterance 301, late third millennium BCE | Securely attested early |
| Heliopolitan creation | Atum exists or becomes active in Nun before producing Shu and Tefnut | Pyramid and Coffin Text traditions | Early core with Middle Kingdom elaboration |
| Coffin Text Spell 80 | Nun accompanies and advises lonely Atum; a variable eightfold group precedes order | c. 2100–1650 BCE | Middle Kingdom theological elaboration |
| Hermopolitan Ogdoad | Nun–Naunet, Heh–Hauhet, Kek–Kauket, and usually Amun–Amunet form four pairs | Explicitly monumental from New Kingdom; fullest in Late/Graeco-Roman temples | Regional learned systematization |
| Memphite Theology | Ptah as Ptah-Nun and Ptah-Naunet fathers/mothers Atum | Shabaka Stone, c. 710 BCE | Syncretistic priestly identification |
| Theban system | Amun/Kematef/Irta creates or precedes the Ogdoad; they become ancestral gods buried at Djeme | New Kingdom antecedents; fullest first-millennium and Graeco-Roman evidence | Regional systematization |
| Solar-child schemes | The Ogdoad collectively engender, release, or greet the child sun from egg, lotus, or mound | Principally late temple texts | Late canonical/regional elaboration |

Naunet is conventionally called Nun’s consort, but “counterpart” is more precise: the pair expresses sexually differentiated completeness. Sources do not give them named children in the manner of Osiris and Isis. Their collective parenthood of the sun or gods belongs to Ogdoad theology.

### Documented divine interactions

The following list separates actual textual or iconographic relationships from mere membership in the same pantheon. It is extensive, but no claim of absolute exhaustiveness across every unpublished temple fragment is possible.

- **Naunet — paired with Nun as male and female primordial sources/protectors of the gods.** Pyramid Text Utterance 301, late Old Kingdom. **Securely attested early.**
- **Atum — exists with Nun before creation and converses with him; Nun counsels Atum regarding Shu and Tefnut.** Coffin Text Spell 80, Middle Kingdom. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Atum — rises or becomes manifest from the primeval water.** Pyramid/Coffin Text creation language and later temple expositions. **Early core, repeatedly elaborated.**
- **Atum — predicts that ordered land will return to Nun while he and Osiris survive as serpents.** Book of the Dead Spell 175, New Kingdom onward. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Shu — born or established within the primordial setting of Nun; the variable Eight participate in the account.** Coffin Texts 75–80. **Middle Kingdom elaboration.**
- **Tefnut — appears with Shu in Atum’s movement from solitary precreation to differentiated creation.** Coffin Texts 75–80; the direct action of Nun toward her is less explicit than modern summaries imply. **Attested contextual relation.**
- **Amun — paired beside Nun in early invocations and later shares membership in the Ogdoad.** Pyramid Text Utterance 301; later Hermopolitan and Theban texts. **Early juxtaposition; later systematization.**
- **Amunet — paired beside Naunet and later grouped with her in the Ogdoad.** Same sources. **Early juxtaposition; later systematization.**
- **Heh and Hauhet — co-members of the standardized Ogdoad, embodying spatial or numerical endlessness.** New Kingdom and later temple theology. **Regional learned systematization.**
- **Kek and Kauket — co-members of the Ogdoad, embodying darkness.** New Kingdom and later temple theology. **Regional learned systematization.**
- **Tenem and Tenemet — replace Amun and Amunet in a Middle Kingdom eightfold enumeration associated with wandering, gloom, or non-direction.** Coffin Text Spell 80. **Regional/textual variant.**
- **Gereh and Gerehet — occur in variant late primordial rosters as night or cessation.** Temple texts. **Late regional variant.**
- **Niau and Niaut — substitute in some Theban lists, associated with void or negation.** Ptolemaic Theban evidence. **Late regional variant.**
- **Ra — creates his body or first rises in Nun; the Ogdoad greet, generate, or release the solar child in later traditions.** Book of the Dead Spell 17 and temple texts. **Canonical and late regional elaboration.**
- **Khepri — his renewed solar bark is lifted by anthropomorphic Nun at dawn.** Book of Gates final division, New Kingdom royal tombs and Osireion. **Canonical New Kingdom iconography.**
- **Osiris — remains with Atum after the world returns to Nun.** Book of the Dead Spell 175. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Ptah — identified as Ptah-Nun and Ptah-Naunet, making Ptah the father and mother of Atum.** Shabaka Stone, Dynasty 25. **Syncretistic identification.**
- **Thoth — linked to the Hermopolitan primeval system as local chief god; late accounts can make him an organizer or activator of the Eight.** Temple theology, principally first millennium BCE and later. **Regional systematization.**
- **Hathor — receives adoration from the Ogdoad in Dendera reliefs.** Ptolemaic/Roman Temple of Hathor. **Late regional cultic interaction.**
- **Ihy — appears with Hathor before the adoring Ogdoad, emphasizing divine-child and renewed-creation themes.** Dendera reliefs. **Late regional interaction.**
- **Khnum — absorbs or organizes primordial creation roles in Esna theology.** Ptolemaic/Roman Esna inscriptions. **Syncretistic regional elaboration.**
- **Isis — the Ogdoad is incorporated into the ritual and cosmogonic world of her Philae temple, but no early independent Nun–Isis narrative is securely attested.** Philae inscriptions. **Late regional association.**
- **Horus and Seth — no substantive direct encounter with Nun or Naunet occurs in the principal *Contendings* manuscript.** **Absence of evidence.**
- **Apophis/Apep — both belong to the uncreated or boundary-threatening cosmological field, but no early source makes Apep a child of Nun and Naunet.** Claims of a fixed genealogy are **modern harmonization**.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Atum in Nun

**Early core:** before earth and sky were established, the creator existed in or emerged from primordial water.

**Middle Kingdom fullest form:** Coffin Text Spell 80 dramatizes the situation. Atum is alone with Nun, weary and without a place to stand or sit. Heliopolis does not yet exist. Nun responds, directing or encouraging the creator in relation to Shu and Tefnut. Differentiation follows: air, moisture, sky, earth, locality, and divine society become possible.

This is not creation *ex nihilo*. Nun already “is,” though not as ordered matter in the later philosophical sense. Nor does Atum necessarily have an ordinary birth from two parents. Depending on the text, he self-develops, emerges, or is placed within a prior generative framework.

### B. The Hermopolitan Eight and the first sunrise

**Regional systematization:** four male–female pairs embody features of precreation:

- Nun–Naunet: water;
- Heh–Hauhet: boundlessness;
- Kek–Kauket: darkness;
- Amun–Amunet: hiddenness.

The pairs act collectively rather than through individualized adventures. Their interaction brings about, carries, or prepares the first dry land. The sun then appears:

- from a lotus;
- from a cosmic egg;
- upon the first mound;
- as a child greeted by the Eight;
- or through the agency of Thoth, Amun, another creator, or a local divine child.

These are parallel and sometimes competing temple variants, not episodes of one canonical epic. The polished modern story in which frogs and snakes “mix their qualities,” lay a single egg, and produce Ra is a synthesis assembled from late sources.

### C. Daily solar rebirth

In New Kingdom underworld books, the sun travels through night and is renewed at dawn. The final scene of the Book of Gates visualizes Nun as a huge male figure lifting the bark containing the renewed solar disk. The image makes a theological argument: each sunrise repeats the first emergence, while the ordered world remains supported by the uncreated water outside it.

### D. The return to Nun

Book of the Dead Spell 175 provides Egypt’s clearest surviving statement of cosmic dissolution. Atum will undo what he made; land will become the Deep and flood again. Atum and Osiris remain as unknown serpents.

The text contains no judgment of all souls, no final battle, and no explicit new world. A cyclical recreation is possible within Egyptian cosmological logic but is not narrated. Calling the passage an Egyptian “Noah flood” is misleading: it concerns the end of differentiated creation, not a historical deluge survived by a human family in a vessel.

### E. Temple as primeval mound

Late temple inscriptions and architecture repeatedly identify the sanctuary with land first emerging from Nun. Foundation, purification, opening the shrine, and presenting lotus or water offerings renew creation. Sacred lakes are functional reservoirs and ritual places, but also cosmological images: every temple can become the first island encircled by Nun.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult status

Nun and Naunet were theologically pervasive but rarely independent recipients of popular cult. There is no securely identified major temple devoted solely to either deity, no well-documented separate high priesthood, and little evidence for a broad corpus of personal votives or theophoric names.

The often-repeated assertion that “Nun had no cult” is too absolute. Better conclusions are:

- no independent cult center comparable to those of Amun, Ptah, Isis, or Horus is known;
- Nun and Naunet received offerings in collective and temple-theological contexts;
- images of the Ogdoad participate in royal cult scenes;
- invocations in funerary and magical texts constitute ritual use;
- sacred lakes materialized Nun without proving a resident independent cult statue.

### Hermopolis

Ancient **Khemenu**, “Eight-town,” later Greek Hermopolis Magna and modern el-Ashmunein, is the chief geographic association. The place-name is old, but a name meaning “Eight-town” by itself does not reconstruct the entire later Ogdoad myth. Thoth remained the city’s dominant historically visible deity.

Archaeological recovery is incomplete, and the fullest cosmogonic accounts come from temples elsewhere. This uneven evidence encouraged earlier scholars to reconstruct a lost ancient Hermopolitan doctrine more confidently than surviving local monuments warrant.

### Thebes and Djeme

Theban theologians incorporated the Eight into Amun’s ancestry. Their cultic resting or burial place was located at **Djeme**, in the Medinet Habu region. Periodic rites for dead creator-gods connected the living Amun cult at Karnak with the western bank.

This was political as well as theological: Thebes could claim that even the ancient Hermopolitan creators originated in or returned to Amun’s territory.

### Dendera, Edfu, Philae, Esna, and Deir el-Medina

- At **Dendera**, the Ogdoad adores Hathor and the child Ihy, embedding primordial creation in Hathoric festival and temple theology.
- At **Edfu**, Nun belongs to the vocabulary of primordial landscape, temple foundation, and lotus offerings.
- At **Philae**, the Ogdoad appears within Isis-centered liturgy.
- At **Esna**, Khnum theology reworks precreation.
- At the Ptolemaic **Hathor-and-Maat temple at Deir el-Medina**, offering scenes demonstrate ritual address to individual Ogdoad members.

### Sacred lakes

Temple lakes supplied water for purification, priestly bathing, ritual vessels, sacred processions, and certain festival dramas. Theologically, a lake could be called or construed as Nun. This does not mean every lake was a dedicated Nun sanctuary; its function was to place the temple’s ordered sacred mound within a controlled image of primeval water.

### Offerings and magical practice

Documented or reasonable direct evidence includes:

- food offering in Pyramid Text Utterance 301;
- funerary recitation identifying the deceased with creator gods in Nun;
- royal offerings before the Ogdoad in temple relief;
- lotus and water offerings in Ptolemaic temples;
- magical self-identification as one who “came forth from Nun”;
- invocation of the Eight as guardians in late magical texts.

### Contested harmful practices

A dedicated search found **no evidence tying Nun or Naunet specifically to human sacrifice, temple prostitution, sexual exploitation, cult violence, or extremist political appropriation**. General claims about Egyptian “temple prostitution” are themselves heavily disputed and cannot be assigned to this pair. No source authorizes reconstructing such practices from their connection with fertility or primeval water.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late Old Kingdom, c. 2350–2100 BCE

Nun/Naunet are securely present in royal funerary theology as a paired primordial source or protective power. Precreative water also functions as a cosmological environment. The later complete Hermopolitan system cannot yet be assumed.

### First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, c. 2100–1650 BCE

Coffin Texts expand royal traditions for nonroyal elites. Spell 80 provides the first sustained dialogue involving Nun and Atum and an eightfold primordial group. The roster’s variability shows theology still being configured rather than copied from one canonical creed.

### New Kingdom, c. 1550–1070 BCE

- Book of the Dead Spell 17 calls Nun “father of the gods.”
- Spell 175 presents eventual return to Nun.
- Hatshepsut’s inscriptions attest “the Eight” monumentally.
- Royal underworld books develop Nun’s visual role in solar rebirth.
- The figure becomes increasingly personified without ceasing to be a cosmic condition.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods, c. 1070–332 BCE

Memphite and Theban priesthoods build synthetic systems:

- Ptah absorbs Nun and Naunet’s reproductive roles;
- Amun is positioned before, within, or above the Ogdoad;
- the Eight become dead ancestral creator-gods;
- their burial at Djeme supports recurring rites.

Foreign rule did not simply turn Nun into an “evil chaos god.” That popular claim overstates Egyptian order/chaos rhetoric. Nun remains dangerous in its potential to dissolve order but also generative and indispensable.

### Ptolemaic and Roman periods, 332 BCE–fourth century CE

This is the richest period for explicit Ogdoad iconography and elaborate temple texts. Frog- and snake-headed forms, lotus and child-sun scenes, local creator identifications, and ritual offerings proliferate. Magical papyri carry “coming forth from Nun” language into multilingual, syncretic ritual.

The late evidence is not automatically a faithful transcript of Old Kingdom belief. It records sophisticated living traditions with long memories and contemporary innovations.

### Christianization, fourth–sixth centuries CE

Traditional Egyptian temples lost institutional support under Christian Roman rule; legislation, episcopal activity, local conflict, economic change, and conversion gradually ended sacrificial temple cult. There is no documented anti-Nun campaign or known violent destruction aimed specifically at a Nun sanctuary. Because the pair lacked a major independent institution, their disappearance cannot be separately charted.

Philae’s closure in the sixth century is often treated as a conventional endpoint for public Egyptian temple religion. Nun survived thereafter chiefly as an object of textual preservation and later antiquarian reconstruction, not as a demonstrably continuous named folk cult.

### Islamic and medieval Egypt

No continuous medieval cult of Nun or Naunet has been documented. Coptic preserved the late Egyptian language, but linguistic continuity is not evidence for continuous worship. Stories about hidden waters, sacred wells, or Nile origins cannot be labeled survivals of Nun unless the name or a traceable ritual connection is present.

### Nineteenth century to present

Decipherment and publication of the Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, papyri, and temple inscriptions reconstructed the figure for modern scholarship. Modern Kemetic religions, emerging particularly from the 1970s onward, sometimes honor Nun or contemplate the primordial waters. Kemetic Orthodoxy was founded by Tamara Siuda in 1988, but no reliable census isolates Nun/Naunet devotees.

In contemporary Egypt, only Islam, Christianity, and Judaism receive full constitutional provision for organized worship; this complicates public indigenous-polytheist revival. That is a modern legal context, not direct “suppression of Nun.”

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Egyptology and museum culture

Modern illustrations commonly show a blue male frog-headed Nun beside a snake-headed Naunet. Such images usually combine:

- late temple iconography;
- modern standardized color schemes;
- a single fixed Ogdoad list;
- generalized Egyptian regalia.

They should not be presented as copies of an Old Kingdom archetype.

Museum and educational summaries have made Nun “the Egyptian god of chaos” or “the ocean before creation.” The latter is serviceable; the former can falsely suggest moral evil or conflict with the gods.

### Occult and esoteric reception

No major Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, or Thelemic text gave Nun and Naunet the sustained individual importance assigned to Isis, Osiris, Horus, Thoth, or Nuit. Similarity between **Naunet/Nunet** and Crowley’s **Nuit** has encouraged modern conflation, but Nuit derives from the sky goddess Nut, not Naunet.

Late magical-papyrus invocations of the abyss are ancient, but contemporary claims that Nun and Naunet provide “fluid intelligence,” manifestation energy, subconscious abundance, or a ritual method for “conscious creation” are **modern New Age inventions**. A recent commercial “Ogdoad subliminal attunement,” for example, explicitly recasts the pair as an architecture of potential and manifestation; this is evidence for reception, not antiquity.

### Modern Kemetic practice

Modern practitioners may:

- honor Nun as the unmanifest source;
- pour water or maintain bowls representing primordial water;
- meditate on creation emerging from stillness;
- include the pair in Ogdoad invocations.

These practices can be historically inspired, but detailed personal liturgies usually reconstruct what the sparse evidence does not preserve. Online practitioners themselves frequently note that Nun is less often a personal devotional deity than a cosmological principle.

### Popular culture

Nun and Naunet remain comparatively minor in film, comics, novels, and games. They occur in modern mythology reference works, deity catalogues, independent art projects, occult media, and Egyptian-themed fantasy, usually as embodiments of water or chaos. No single major commercial portrayal has displaced the scholarly image as decisively as modern media have for Anubis, Set, or Isis.

A recurring internet invention equates the Ogdoad with modern physics—matter/antimatter, energy fields, dimensions, electricity, or quantum potential. Egyptian texts make no such claims. Likewise, attempts to derive alphabets, Greek philosophy, Noah’s flood, or Christian cosmology directly from Nun generally depend on resemblance rather than demonstrable transmission.

### Political and nationalist use

Egyptian creation traditions appear in broader Pharaonist, Afrocentric, and heritage discourse. However, no well-documented nationalist or extremist movement appears to have made Nun and Naunet particular emblems. Assertions that their cult was uniquely suppressed by a foreign ethnic group are unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major works and approaches

- **Kurt Sethe, *Amun und die acht Urgötter von Hermopolis* (1929):** foundational reconstruction of the Ogdoad and its relation to Amun.
- **Adriaan de Buck, *The Egyptian Coffin Texts* (1935–1961):** indispensable primary edition.
- **R. O. Faulkner:** standard translations of the Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, and Book of the Dead.
- **James P. Allen:** major grammatical and cosmological reassessment; his *Genesis in Egypt* and Pyramid Text translations stress the language of creation without forcing Egyptian categories into biblical or Greek molds.
- **Erik Hornung, *Conceptions of God in Ancient Egypt*:** emphasizes plurality, complementarity, and the inadequacy of treating Egyptian gods as fixed classical persons.
- **Jan Assmann:** analyzes Egyptian theological discourse, cultural memory, solar religion, and the historical construction of divine systems.
- **Richard H. Wilkinson, *The Complete Gods and Goddesses of Ancient Egypt*:** accessible synthesis of identity and iconography.
- **Geraldine Pinch, *Handbook of Egyptian Mythology*:** concise source-based synthesis.
- **Christiane Zivie-Coche:** challenges older assumptions about a single immemorial Ogdoad and emphasizes the historical “invention” or reconfiguration of its forms.
- **Christian Leitz, *Lexikon der ägyptischen Götter und Götterbezeichnungen*:** the largest systematic catalogue of Egyptian divine names and epithets.
- **Lexikon der Ägyptologie:** still valuable for research history but must be supplemented by newer epigraphy and linguistic work.

### Principal debates

#### Was there one ancient Hermopolitan creation myth?

Probably not in the form found in handbooks. The surviving record is distributed among mortuary texts and much later temples. Egg, lotus, mound, Thoth, Amun, and solar-child versions should remain distinct.

#### How old is the Ogdoad?

Individual pairs and an eightfold grouping have Old and Middle Kingdom roots. The explicit collective “Eight” is securely monumental in the New Kingdom. Much of the canonical roster, iconography, and narrative belongs to first-millennium and Graeco-Roman priestly elaboration.

#### Is Naunet a goddess or a lower sky?

Both readings occur in scholarship because *nnt* can function cosmographically and as a feminine divine name. Context, determinatives, and pairing must decide each case. Translators who render every instance “Naunet” risk over-personification.

#### Is Nun “chaos”?

Only with qualification. Nun lacks created boundaries and can overwhelm order, but is also the necessary reservoir of creation and renewal. Greek *chaos*, biblical “deep,” and modern scientific “nothingness” carry associations not inherent in Egyptian usage.

#### Is the Shabaka Stone an Old Kingdom text?

The stone dates securely to Shabaka. Its claim to copy an ancient damaged manuscript cannot independently date the composition. Archaism may be genuine preservation, deliberate archaizing, or both. Its Ptah-Nun theology should therefore be cited as a Dynasty 25 witness unless an argument for older material is explicitly marked as reconstruction.

#### Does Spell 175 promise another creation?

No explicit new creation appears in the surviving passage. Cyclic regeneration is a possible theological inference, not a narrated event.

#### Did Nun receive worship?

Nun received invocation, offering, and representation. Evidence for an autonomous popular cult, separate temple economy, or dedicated priesthood is lacking. Thus “no cult” is too strong, while “widely worshipped water god” is equally misleading.

### State of the evidence

- Pyramid texts remain on royal monuments at Saqqara and in published facsimiles.
- Coffin Texts are dispersed among museums and excavated collections.
- Book of the Dead manuscripts include British Museum papyri and many international holdings.
- The Shabaka Stone is in the British Museum.
- The Turin Royal Canon is in the Museo Egizio, Turin.
- Temple inscriptions remain in situ and in long-running publication projects for Edfu, Dendera, Philae, Karnak, and Esna.
- The Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae is essential for lexical checking, but spelling searches must include variants and determinatives.
- Hermopolis’s archaeological record is incomplete, making claims about its earliest doctrine unusually dependent on texts from elsewhere.

### Evidentiary conclusions

- Nun and Naunet are securely ancient; the late Old Kingdom pair is not a modern reconstruction.
- The familiar complete Ogdoad is historical but reached its most explicit form much later than the earliest Nun/Naunet evidence.
- Nun is simultaneously place, substance, condition, and god; choosing only one category falsifies the sources.
- Naunet’s individuality is weakest in the early evidence and strongest in later paired systems.
- There is no evidence for a continuous post-antique cult, human sacrifice, temple prostitution, uniquely violent suppression, a biblical Noah identity, a physics doctrine, or a direct Naunet–Nuit identity.
- Modern manifestation, psychological, and “quantum” accounts are modern inventions unless presented explicitly as contemporary religious interpretation.
