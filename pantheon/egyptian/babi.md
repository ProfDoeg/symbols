# Babi: Research Dossier

**Class:** Ancient Egyptian male **god** (*nṯr*), also functioning in mortuary and magical texts as a dangerous underworld power or guardian-like liminal being. Modern descriptions such as “demon” are interpretive and should not replace the Egyptian classification without qualification.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Pyramid Texts in the pyramid of King Unas at Saqqara, late Fifth Dynasty, approximately **2350–2320 BCE**. Pyramid Texts 313 and 320 name Babi and associate him respectively with an enormous phallus that opens the sky’s doors and with the title “lord of night” and the difficult expression conventionally translated “bull of the baboons.” The Pyramid Texts are the earliest Egyptian funerary corpus preserved extensively in writing; Unas’s pyramid contains their earliest monumental witness.

**Evidentiary summary:** Babi is securely attested but never becomes a major, institutionally visible temple god. His strongest record is textual: Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts, Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts, New Kingdom Book of the Dead manuscripts and *The Contendings of Horus and Seth*, and Ptolemaic priestly mythology, especially Papyrus Jumilhac. He can be protective, sexually generative, aggressive, transgressive, or sacrificially defeated. Claims that he presided over a major cult at Hermopolis, governed a formal judgment office beside a lake of fire, or was simply replaced by Thoth are not established by the surviving evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Babi is a forceful male deity whose identity is constructed around baboon imagery, darkness, physical dominance, sexual potency, and the dangerous thresholds of death and divine space. In some mortuary compositions the deceased becomes Babi or appropriates his phallus and protection; elsewhere the deceased needs protection from him. This is not an accidental contradiction. Egyptian ritual texts routinely turn dangerous divine powers into defenses by naming, identifying with, or subordinating them.

His animal manifestation is normally understood as a baboon, commonly specified in modern literature as the hamadryas baboon (*Papio hamadryas*). Rita Lucarelli’s study emphasizes that late texts also make him a red dog. The baboon and dog forms belong to different textual situations and should not be collapsed into one fixed iconography. [Secure early attestation; later regional elaboration.]

### Names and spellings

- **Bꜣbj / Bꜣby**, conventionally vocalized **Babi** or **Baby**. Because Egyptian writing normally omitted vowels, the precise ancient pronunciation is unrecoverable. [Securely attested Egyptian name.]
- **Bꜣbꜣ / Baba**, a frequent modern vocalization and possibly a genuinely different written form in some witnesses. Egyptological publications are not consistent in separating *Babi*, *Baba*, and *Babay*. [Attested variant; exact linguistic relationship disputed.]
- **Bebon**, Greek **Βέβων**, in Plutarch. Whether Plutarch’s Bebon is the same deity as Egyptian Babi is an old but unresolved identification rather than a fact. The nineteenth-century *Realencyclopädie* entry already warned that the equation was not demonstrated from hieroglyphic evidence. [Greco-Roman identification; disputed.]
- **Babawy**, apparently a dual or expanded form in some lexical and scholarly treatments. It must not automatically be treated as identical with every occurrence of Babi. [Learned/lexical classification; context-dependent.]

The commonly repeated etymology **“bull of the baboons”** derives most securely from an epithet in Pyramid Text 320, not necessarily from the bare name itself. The expression rendered by Faulkner as “bull of the baboons” is translated differently by different scholars; older translations even produce “the bull without whom life would cease.” Accordingly, “bull of the baboons” is a plausible title emphasizing primacy or sexual dominance, but it is not a securely established literal etymology of *Babi*. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 49, says that **Bebon** means “holding back” or “hindering.” He reports two opinions: some called Bebon a companion of Typhon/Seth; Manetho called Bebon another name of Typhon himself. Plutarch’s Greek explanation belongs to his philosophical interpretation of Egyptian religion and cannot independently establish the Egyptian etymology. A recent study of Plutarch’s translation practice likewise notes that Plutarch may be extending Manetho’s limited identification into his own natural-philosophical chain of explanation. [Greco-Roman learned systematization; etymology uncertain.]

### Attested titles and descriptions

- **“Lord of night”** — Pyramid Text 320, Unas §516. [Securely attested early.]
- **“Bull of the baboons/apes”** — the same passage under a widely accepted modern translation. [Secure phrase; translation debated.]
- **“Eldest/firstborn son of Osiris”** — Coffin Text 359. This is a ritual self-identification, not a universally accepted Egyptian genealogy. [Middle Kingdom funerary elaboration.]
- **“He who assembled every god within the circuit of his eye in Heliopolis”** — Coffin Text 359. [Middle Kingdom funerary elaboration.]
- **“He who lives on the entrails of the elders”** — Book of the Dead 125A in the prayer that the tribunal rescue the deceased from Babi. “Elders,” “great ones,” and “deceased” occur in modern translations; the wording should not be silently expanded into “all sinners.” [New Kingdom canonical mortuary elaboration.]
- **Red-eared and purple- or red-rumped** — Pyramid Text 549. The colors characterize a dangerous baboon and perhaps actual baboon anatomy; they do not prove a universal artistic color scheme. [Securely attested early.]
- **Red dog with yellow eyes** — Papyrus Jumilhac’s local priestly mythology. [Ptolemaic regional systematization.]

### Classification and overlap

Babi is called a god in Egyptian narrative and ritual sources. Lucarelli nevertheless treats him alongside “baboon demons” because he shares the dangerous, controllable, liminal functions of underworld guardians. Egyptian categories did not always reproduce the modern god/demon binary.

Babi must also be distinguished from:

- **Thoth as a baboon.** Thoth’s seated lunar baboon is extremely common in Egyptian art. An uninscribed baboon image cannot safely be labeled Babi.
- The four baboon guardians of the **Lake of Fire** in Book of the Dead 126. Lucarelli compares them with Babi, but the four are not thereby four manifestations of Babi.
- **Baba/Bau of Mesopotamia**, a goddess of Girsu. The similar Romanized name is coincidental; she has a separate language, sex, cult, and history.
- **Babi/Bābī** as a term relating to the nineteenth-century Bābī religion. It is unrelated.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Pyramid Texts

The earliest preserved Babi passages occur in Unas’s pyramid at Saqqara. The corpus was carved inside late Old Kingdom royal pyramids, beginning with Unas, and was copied and rearranged in subsequent royal monuments. UCL’s corpus overview dates the broader Old Kingdom to approximately 2686–2181 BCE and identifies Unas as the earliest monumental witness. [Securely attested early.]

Important occurrences include:

- **PT 313, §§502–503:** “The phallus of Babi is drawn” or “drawn back,” and the doors of the sky open. The writing stands in Unas’s exit corridor. Harold Hays cautions against assuming that the formula literally maps the adjacent architectural doorway: “doors of the sky” language occurs in many locations in the pyramid corpus. [Secure text; spatial interpretation disputed.]
- **PT 320, §§515–516:** the king orders the night and stars, is proclaimed as Babi, becomes “Babi, lord of night,” and receives the “bull of the baboons” description under one translation. The king’s assumption of Babi’s identity makes Babi a source of celestial and physical potency. [Securely attested early.]
- **PT 549:** Babi is driven back as a dangerous red-eared, colored-rumped being who has taken a portion of a goddess’s sacrificial meat. [Securely attested early.]
- Texts involving baboons but not naming Babi should remain separate. PT 315, for example, identifies Unas with several kinds of baboon; it illuminates the broader symbolic field but does not necessarily name this god.

The “Cannibal Hymn” says that the risen king eats divine bodies and entrails. It is frequently combined in popular accounts with Babi’s later entrail-eating. The hymn does **not**, merely by describing entrail consumption, prove that Babi performs the act there. [Correction of modern conflation.]

Critical foundations are Kurt Sethe’s *Die altägyptischen Pyramidentexte*, R. O. Faulkner’s *The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts* (1969), Alexandre Piankoff’s publication of Unas, and James P. Allen’s *The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts* (2005).

### Coffin Texts

The Coffin Texts occur mainly on Middle Kingdom coffins, sarcophagi, and tomb surfaces, approximately **2100–1650 BCE**. They reused Pyramid Text material and greatly expanded access beyond royal burials. [Secure Middle Kingdom transmission.]

Principal Babi passages include:

- **CT 359, “Having power over water in the realm of the dead”:** the speaker is the oar of Re and declares, “I am Babi, the eldest son of Osiris,” who assembled the gods and inherited or seized power. Faulkner notes manuscript variants, including “eldest son of Nut,” and grammatical uncertainty over which epithets apply to Babi. [Attested, with variant genealogy.]
- **CT 668:** “Becoming Babi in the realm of the dead.” This adapts the royal identification of PT 320 for a Middle Kingdom deceased owner. [Canonical transmission and social expansion.]
- **CT 822:** the deceased identifies his phallus with Babi. [Attested sexual-magical application.]
- **CT 945:** Babi is invoked as protection. [Attested protective application.]
- **CT 404–405:** ancestors of Book of the Dead 99’s ferry interrogation. Not all copies contain the same Babi passage later found in Book of the Dead witnesses. [Textual transmission.]

The standard English edition is Faulkner, *The Ancient Egyptian Coffin Texts*, with the underlying hieroglyphic corpus edited by Adriaan de Buck.

### Book of the Dead

The “Book of the Dead” is a modern collective name for individualized collections called in Egyptian the “Book/Spells of Going Forth by Day.” It emerged from earlier funerary textual traditions in the Second Intermediate Period and early New Kingdom; its numbered “chapters” derive from modern editions rather than an ancient canonical sequence. [Modern editorial classification.]

Babi appears chiefly in:

- **Spell 99, ferry interrogation:** in a version translated by T. G. Allen, the ferry lacks a wooden mast and the deceased is told to use “the phallus of Baba,” which produces youths and begets calves. Allen’s note explicitly identifies the phallus as the mast. Other witnesses, including the Book of the Dead of Nu as presented by UCL, instead give the mast another mythic identity. Thus “Babi’s phallus is the ferry’s mast” is an **attested manuscript reading**, not a universal text of Spell 99. The composition descends from Coffin Texts 404–405. [New Kingdom variant; earlier Middle Kingdom ancestry.]
- **Spell 125A:** the deceased asks the gods to rescue him from Babi, “who lives on the entrails of the elders,” on the day of the great reckoning. This is the firm basis for calling Babi an eater of entrails. The text does not expressly say that he routinely eats everyone whose heart fails the balance. [Canonical mortuary attestation; popular elaboration corrected.]
- Later descriptions placing him beside the Lake of Fire generally result from associating Babi with other baboon guardians and judgment imagery. This is plausible comparative interpretation, not a clear statement of Spell 125 itself. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

### Papyrus of Ani

The Papyrus of Ani, British Museum EA 10470, was made at Thebes around **1250 BCE**, Nineteenth Dynasty. Ani’s manuscript includes the judgment complex and Spell 125 material relevant to Babi. The famous small baboon atop the judgment balance is identified by the British Museum as **Thoth**, not Babi. Babi’s presence in the spell must not be used to relabel that image. [Secure museum identification.]

Accordingly:

- “Babi appears textually in the Papyrus of Ani’s mortuary tradition” — supported.
- “The balance-top baboon in Ani is Babi” — contradicted by the object’s inscriptions and museum identification.
- “Ani depicts Babi waiting to eat condemned souls” — not established.

### *The Contendings of Horus and Seth*

Papyrus Chester Beatty I, copied in the Twentieth Dynasty, probably during the reign of Ramesses V, preserves the only extensive manuscript of this Late Egyptian tale. Babi/Baba/Bebon briefly insults the solar god Pre-Harakhty: “Your shrine is vacant.” The god withdraws in distress; the Ennead expels Babi; Hathor exposes her genitals to her father and makes him laugh, restoring his willingness to resume judgment. The manuscript is in Dublin’s Chester Beatty collection. [New Kingdom literary attestation.]

Babi is a catalyst, not a principal contestant. He does not fight Horus or Seth in this episode.

### Papyrus Jumilhac

Louvre E 17110, a Ptolemaic mythological-geographical papyrus dated by the Louvre to **332–30 BCE**, preserves a developed conflict between Baba and Thoth. Its hieroglyphic text has Demotic marginal notes and concerns local Upper Egyptian cult geography. Jacques Vandier published the fundamental edition in 1961. [Ptolemaic regional priestly systematization.]

Baba, as a red dog, accuses Thoth before Re and the Great and Small Enneads of stealing and consuming Re’s property. The assembly rejects the accusation. After Baba continues his slander, Thoth magically affects Baba’s phallus while Baba is sleeping or coupling with an unnamed female; it swells and becomes trapped. Thoth calls the gods to witness Baba’s humiliation. Baba then attacks himself under Thoth’s magic. Re hands him over to Thoth, who kills him on a slaughter block. The text explains a red dog killed at a festival of Thoth. [Regional etiological myth.]

### Temple texts and other nominated corpora

- **Edfu:** a related Ptolemaic formula suppresses Baba’s sexual power in relation to a goddess or “God’s Wife.” It belongs to the same late ritual-mythological complex but is not evidence for a temple dedicated to Babi. [Regional temple attestation.]
- **Dendera, Karnak, and Philae:** these corpora contain extensive baboon, guardian, Sethian, and underworld material, but this investigation found no comparably secure, central Babi dossier in them. An uncaptioned baboon cannot be assigned to Babi. [Absence of securely identified evidence.]
- **Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** no secure Babi episode was identified. [Absence.]
- **Turin King List:** no relevant divine attestation; it is a royal list, not evidence for Babi’s cult. [Absence.]
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** no securely identified occurrence that can confidently be separated from similar divine names or dog-/baboon-faced magical beings was established here. [Unestablished.]
- **Plutarch:** possible late equation with Bebon, but identity remains disputed. [Greco-Roman interpretive witness.]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic powers

**Night and the celestial threshold.** PT 320 calls Babi lord of night and lets the king become him while ordering nocturnal celestial powers. PT 313 makes his phallus the agency or image by which the doors of the sky open. [Secure Old Kingdom material.]

**Sexual potency and generation.** The phallus is overwhelmingly prominent: door-bolt in PT 313, the deceased’s member in CT 822, and the mast that “creates youths and begets calves” in one Spell 99 tradition. The same potency becomes a liability in Papyrus Jumilhac when Thoth magically immobilizes it. [Continuous motif with changing valuation.]

**Aggression, appetite, and dangerous liminality.** PT 549 repels him after sacrificial appropriation; Book of the Dead 125 fears him as an eater of elders’ entrails; Papyrus Jumilhac makes him a slanderer and sacrificial victim. [Attested but heterogeneous.]

**Protection and empowerment.** CT 945 invokes him as protection, while PT 320 and CT 668 achieve transformation into him. Calling him simply an “evil god” therefore misrepresents the ritual evidence. [Attested counter-role.]

**Water and ferrying.** CT 359 associates identification with Babi with power over water; one Spell 99 version makes his phallus the ferry mast. Babi is not securely the ferryman himself. [Attested association; popular exaggeration corrected.]

### Physical form

The most defensible forms are:

1. **Baboon** or baboon-like male deity, often inferred from his title, color description, and name determinatives. [Secure general form.]
2. **Anthropomorphic baboon-headed male**, sometimes ithyphallic, in modern reconstructions and later iconographic classification. Individual ancient examples require inscriptions before identification. [Probable but object-specific.]
3. **Red dog with yellow eyes**, explicitly in the late Papyrus Jumilhac tradition. [Secure Ptolemaic regional form.]

Claims that every red baboon, every ithyphallic ape, or every baboon beside fire is Babi are unjustified. Most museum baboon statuettes with lunar discs or scribal contexts are Thoth. The British Museum identifies its baboon statuette EA 35401 and the balance baboon in Ani as Thoth.

### Emblems and substances

Secure or defensible attributes are:

- Erect phallus
- Door-bolt
- Ferry mast in one textual recension
- Oar of Re in CT 359
- Red ears and colored hindquarters
- Baboon body or head
- Red dog body in Papyrus Jumilhac
- Sacrificial meat or entrails
- Night and the mortuary realm

No secure ancient evidence establishes a special Babi color other than the contextual red/purple descriptions, a sacred number, calendrical day, planet, independent constellation, plant, or mineral.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

| Relation | Source and date | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| “Eldest son of Osiris” | Coffin Text 359, Middle Kingdom | Attested ritual genealogy, not universal |
| “Eldest son of Nut” | Variant noted by Faulkner in CT 359 | Manuscript variant |
| “Son of a mother who did not know she bore him” | PT 320, Old Kingdom; identity of the mother unclear | Secure wording, genealogy obscure |
| Companion or aspect of Seth/Typhon | Plutarch 49, late first/early second century CE, reporting divergent opinions and Manetho | Greco-Roman learned identification |
| Ally of Seth | Papyrus Jumilhac’s local classificatory mythology | Ptolemaic regional systematization |

No securely attested named consort or child has been established. The unnamed female in Papyrus Jumilhac is not necessarily a consort.

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Osiris — filial/inheritance relation:** Babi is “eldest son” and “heir” of Osiris and takes over powers in Coffin Text 359, Middle Kingdom. **Tier:** later funerary elaboration.
- **Re — oar and power relation:** the deceased as Babi is also the oar with which Re rows in Coffin Text 359. **Tier:** Middle Kingdom canonical mortuary text.
- **Re/Pre-Harakhty — insult:** Babi tells the solar god that his shrine is empty or vacant, causing him to withdraw in grief; *Contendings of Horus and Seth*, Papyrus Chester Beatty I, Twentieth Dynasty. **Tier:** New Kingdom literary attestation.
- **The Ennead — expulsion:** after Babi insults Pre-Harakhty, the Ennead denounces the offense and drives him from the assembly; *Contendings*, Twentieth Dynasty. **Tier:** New Kingdom literary attestation.
- **Hathor — indirect narrative opposition:** Babi’s insult depresses her father; Hathor exposes herself and makes the solar god laugh, undoing Babi’s disruptive effect. They do not directly converse; *Contendings*, Twentieth Dynasty. **Tier:** New Kingdom literary attestation.
- **Thoth — accusation:** Baba accuses Thoth before Re and the Enneads of stealing and eating Re’s property; Papyrus Jumilhac, Ptolemaic period. **Tier:** regional priestly systematization.
- **Thoth — sexual humiliation by magic:** Thoth uses his reed and spell against Baba’s phallus, trapping it during intercourse; Papyrus Jumilhac. **Tier:** regional priestly systematization.
- **Thoth — coerced self-violence and execution:** Thoth’s magic makes Baba turn his weapon upon himself; Re then gives Baba to Thoth, who slaughters him; Papyrus Jumilhac. **Tier:** regional etiological myth.
- **Re — judgment:** Re finds Baba wrong in his accusation and ultimately transfers him to Thoth for punishment; Papyrus Jumilhac. **Tier:** regional priestly systematization.
- **Great and Small Enneads — witnesses/jury:** they reject Baba’s charge against Thoth and witness his humiliation; Papyrus Jumilhac. **Tier:** regional priestly systematization.
- **Seth/Typhon — companion, ally, or identity:** Plutarch gives both “companion of Typhon” and Manetho’s “Typhon himself was called Bebon”; Papyrus Jumilhac separately makes the red dog Baba Seth-aligned. **Tier:** conflicting Greco-Egyptian and regional learned equations.
- **Unnamed goddess whose meat is taken:** PT 549 says Babi has taken a thigh or portion belonging to “your goddess.” She is not named; no identification should be supplied. **Tier:** securely attested early, relationship incomplete.

No direct Babi interaction with Horus, Isis, Nephthys, Anubis, Ptah, or Amun is securely documented merely because those gods occur in the same corpora.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The king becomes Babi

In PT 320 the dead king regulates night, dispatches or orders the stars, and is proclaimed Babi. Divine and social superiors are commanded to submit. The central action is not a biographical adventure of Babi but ritual identity: the king becomes the god’s dominating nocturnal and generative force. CT 668 later adapts this as a spell for a nonroyal deceased to become Babi in the necropolis. [Early core with Middle Kingdom democratization.]

### Babi’s phallus opens heaven

PT 313 says that Babi’s phallus is drawn back as the sky doors open. The analogy is simultaneously anatomical and mechanical: the member resembles or functions as a bolt. Because the formula occurs in Unas’s exit corridor, it has often been read as ritually unlocking the king’s architectural route; Hays argues that the widespread distribution of sky-door formulas makes an exact architectural mapping too rigid. [Secure text; ritual-spatial interpretation disputed.]

### The underworld ferry

Spell 99 requires the deceased to know the secret identities of every boat component before crossing. In the version translated by T. G. Allen, the boat has no wooden mast, so Babi’s generative phallus becomes its mast. The dialogue specifies the thighs or crotch as its setting and serpent-like ropes associated with Hemen. Other manuscripts assign a different mythic identity to the mast. [Attested New Kingdom variant, not universal canon.]

### The eater of entrails

Book of the Dead 125 places Babi among the dangers of judgment: the deceased asks to be rescued from the being who lives on elders’ entrails on the day of accounting. This establishes an intestinal appetite and judicial setting. It does not explicitly describe Babi standing beside the balance, eating every condemned soul, or replacing Ammit. Those details are modern synthesis from separate judgment and baboon-demon imagery. [Attested core; modern composite expansion.]

### Babi insults the solar judge

In *The Contendings of Horus and Seth*, the divine tribunal has failed for decades to resolve the succession to Osiris. Babi insults the solar god with the statement that his shrine is vacant. The god withdraws, the Ennead expels Babi, and Hathor restores her father’s spirits through sexual display and laughter. The episode presents Babi as disruptive speech personified and uses obscene humor to restart stalled authority. Its function has been interpreted as comic inversion, ritualized laughter, and critique of a deadlocked tribunal. [New Kingdom literary myth; scholarly functional interpretations.]

### Baba and Thoth

Papyrus Jumilhac preserves the fullest Babi-centered narrative. Baba, the red dog, falsely accuses Thoth. Thoth uses scribal and magical power against Baba’s sexuality, exposes him before the gods, and brings about his violent defeat. The account concludes etiologically: the red dog killed at Thoth’s festival is Baba. The story transforms an aggressive, sexually potent deity into the mythic prototype of a sacrificial enemy. It also exploits the opposition between Thoth’s controlled knowledge and Baba’s uncontrolled speech and body. [Ptolemaic regional priestly systematization.]

The killing is an **animal sacrifice**, not evidence for human sacrifice.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No temple securely dedicated primarily to Babi has been identified. No independent Babi priesthood, divine estate, oracle, pilgrimage center, or royal building program is securely documented. This sharply distinguishes him from Thoth, Osiris, Horus, or Hathor.

Claims that Babi was once the principal god of Hermopolis and was later displaced by Thoth lack adequate primary documentation. Hermopolitan baboon statues and mummies overwhelmingly belong to Thoth’s cult unless an inscription says otherwise. [Modern unsupported reconstruction.]

What is documented is mainly ritual use:

- Royal mortuary recitation in Pyramid Texts.
- Nonroyal funerary appropriation in Coffin Texts.
- Knowledge and protection spells in Book of the Dead manuscripts.
- Narrative deployment in a Ramesside literary papyrus.
- Local priestly etiologies in Ptolemaic Papyrus Jumilhac and Edfu texts.
- A festival sacrifice of a red dog explained as the killing of Baba in the Jumilhac tradition.

There is no evidence that Babi received human sacrifice. The threatening language of entrail-eating belongs to mortuary myth and must not be converted into a historical sacrificial practice.

The same restraint applies to “temple prostitution”: no evidence connects Babi with such an institution.

Theophoric personal names incorporating Babi have not been securely demonstrated in the reviewed material. Absence is significant because theophoric names are otherwise an important measure of popular devotion.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Late Fifth–Sixth Dynasties, approximately 2350–2180 BCE

Babi enters the surviving record in royal mortuary texts. He is already ambivalent: the king becomes him and uses his potency, but another formula drives him away as a dangerous thief of sacrificial meat. His phallus opens the sky. [Secure early phase.]

### First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, approximately 2100–1650 BCE

The Coffin Texts transmit and expand the figure beyond royal burials. Babi becomes a form available to the deceased, a source of protection, a personified phallus, an oar of Re, and “eldest son of Osiris.” Competing genealogical readings already appear. [Canonical funerary development.]

### New Kingdom, approximately 1550–1069 BCE

Book of the Dead traditions retain Babi in underworld protection and ferry formulas. Spell 125 develops the entrail-eater formulation; a Spell 99 recension makes his phallus the mast. *The Contendings of Horus and Seth* employs him as a disruptive divine speaker. The Papyrus of Ani attests his textual environment around 1250 BCE but depicts Thoth, not Babi, as the judgment baboon. [Canonical and literary elaboration.]

### Late and Ptolemaic periods, 664–30 BCE

Priestly manuals and temple texts elaborate local divine histories. Papyrus Jumilhac gives Baba a red-dog manifestation, a Sethian alignment, a formal conflict with Thoth, magical sexual humiliation, and sacrificial death. This is not necessarily a newly invented god, but it is a much later and more localized narrative than the Pyramid Text material. [Regional learned systematization.]

### Roman period

Plutarch’s *De Iside et Osiride* supplies Bebon as either Typhon’s companion or, according to Manetho, another name for Typhon. This may record a genuine late Egyptian equation, but the linguistic identification of Bebon with the earlier Babi is uncertain. [Greco-Roman syncretistic identification.]

### Christian and Islamic Egypt

No specific decree, temple closure, conversion narrative, demonization process, or folklore survival concerning Babi has been documented. General accounts of Egyptian Christianization cannot be used as evidence that a distinct Babi cult was forcibly suppressed. His absence from later Coptic and Arabic religious tradition is better described as disappearance from the presently recoverable record. [Absence of evidence.]

### Modern period

Babi reentered circulation through editions and translations of the funerary texts, especially Sethe, Budge, Faulkner, Allen, and popular dictionaries of Egyptian gods. He now appears in Kemetic and neopagan discussion, mythology databases, games, and fantasy proposals, but no measurable continuous ancient-to-modern cult exists. [Modern revival/reception.]

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Modern popular accounts often combine several authentic but separate elements into a single narrative:

1. Babi eats elders’ entrails in Spell 125.
2. Four baboons guard a Lake of Fire in Spell 126.
3. Ammit consumes the condemned in judgment imagery.
4. Thoth appears as a baboon on the balance in the Papyrus of Ani.

The resulting figure—“Babi waits beside the Lake of Fire and devours every sinful soul after the weighing”—is a **modern composite**, not a single ancient passage.

Other recurrent modern inventions or overstatements include:

- **“Babi was the Egyptian god of alpha-male dominance.”** This recasts ancient virility through modern social-media vocabulary.
- **“Babi was Thoth’s assistant.”** No general ancient office of this kind is established; the best-developed myth makes the two enemies.
- **“Babi ferried the dead.”** His phallus can be the mast, and CT 359 links him with an oar, but this does not make him the ferryman.
- **“Babi was worshipped at Hermopolis before Thoth replaced him.”** Unsubstantiated as a historical cult sequence.
- **“Babi is shown on Ani’s balance.”** The figure is Thoth.
- **“Babi is one of the four Lake of Fire baboons.”** Possible modern association, not secure identification.
- **“Babi was the universal Egyptian devil.”** Anachronistic; his protective uses contradict it.
- **“Babi’s name simply means bull of the baboons.”** The phrase is better treated as a translated epithet whose reading remains debated.

Kemetic practitioners sometimes worship or address Babi today. Online testimony establishes modern devotional interest, but not its scale, institutional continuity, or conformity with an ancient rite. Modern rituals should be described as reconstruction or new religious creation unless their elements can be tied to specific ancient formulas.

Babi has not acquired a substantial independent role in the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, classical Hermeticism, or Jungian literature comparable with Isis, Thoth, Horus, or Anubis. Assertions of an ancient “Babi current” in ceremonial magic lack documentary support. [Absence.]

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Identity of Babi and Bebon

This remains the principal onomastic dispute. Budge and other older writers accepted the equation of Egyptian Babi with Plutarch’s Bebon and therefore with Seth/Typhon. The older *Realencyclopädie* objected that the hieroglyphic equation was unproved. Modern work should therefore cite Plutarch as evidence for a **late Bebon tradition**, not automatically as a transparent account of Old Kingdom Babi.

### “Bull of the baboons”

Translations of PT 320 vary substantially. The now-popular title fits Babi’s broader sexual and dominant character, but the divergent renderings demonstrate that it cannot bear an entire etymological argument by itself.

### God or demon

Older reference works tend to call Babi simply a god; recent demonology scholarship emphasizes the permeability between minor gods, guardians, executioners, and demons. Lucarelli’s “Baba and the Baboon Demons” is especially important because it preserves Babi’s divine status while comparing his forms and functions with netherworld guardians.

### Babi and Thoth’s baboon

The enormous quantity of Thoth-baboon art has encouraged misidentification. Secure attribution requires a caption, lunar emblem, scribal context, or other specific evidence. The Papyrus of Ani is the most prominent corrective case: Babi occurs in the textual tradition, but the illustrated balance baboon is Thoth.

### Archaeological imbalance

Babi’s record is almost wholly textual. The lack of a securely attributable temple, priesthood, cult statue, or votive corpus may mean:

- he was principally a textual and ritual persona;
- his images were not normally labeled;
- he was embedded in broader baboon or Sethian imagery;
- or relevant evidence has not survived.

The available record does not permit a choice among these possibilities.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in Babi’s documented history |
|---|---|
| c. 2350–2320 BCE | PT 313 and 320 are carved in Unas’s pyramid: Babi’s phallus opens the sky; the king becomes Babi, lord of night. |
| Sixth Dynasty, c. 2320–2180 BCE | Additional Pyramid Text witnesses, including the hostile address in PT 549, transmit the figure. |
| c. 2100–1650 BCE | Coffin Texts adapt Babi for nonroyal mortuary use: CT 359, 668, 822, and 945 supply genealogy, transformation, sexual identification, and protection. |
| Early New Kingdom onward | Book of the Dead collections inherit and rearrange Coffin Text traditions. |
| Nineteenth Dynasty, c. 1250 BCE | Papyrus of Ani preserves Spell 125’s judgment tradition; Babi is feared textually as an entrail-eater. Its illustrated baboon is Thoth. |
| Twentieth Dynasty, c. 1160 BCE | Papyrus Chester Beatty I records Babi insulting Pre-Harakhty in *The Contendings of Horus and Seth*. |
| New Kingdom manuscript tradition | A recension of Spell 99 identifies the ferry mast with Babi’s generative phallus; other witnesses give another identification. |
| Ptolemaic period, 332–30 BCE | Papyrus Jumilhac systematizes Baba as a red dog, Sethian ally, accuser of Thoth, victim of sexual magic, and sacrificial enemy. |
| Ptolemaic temple phase | Edfu material uses a related formula for suppressing Baba’s sexual power. |
| Late first–early second century CE | Plutarch records Bebon as either Typhon’s companion or another name of Typhon, attributing the latter view to Manetho. |
| Late antiquity | No distinct continuation of a Babi cult is securely documented. |
| 1895 | Budge publishes the Papyrus of Ani with hieroglyphic transcription and translation, powerfully shaping English-language reception. |
| 1908–1922 | Sethe’s edition establishes the principal Pyramid Text reference system. |
| 1931 | Gardiner publishes Papyrus Chester Beatty I, making the Babi episode in *The Contendings* widely accessible. |
| 1961 | Vandier publishes *Le Papyrus Jumilhac*. |
| 1969 | Faulkner’s English Pyramid Text translation becomes a standard reference. |
| 1972–1978 | Faulkner’s Coffin Text translation gives broad access to CT 359 and related spells. |
| 1974 | T. G. Allen’s Book of the Dead translation documents the Spell 99 mast reading. |
| 2005 | James P. Allen publishes a major new Pyramid Text translation and arrangement. |
| 2020 | Rita Lucarelli publishes “Baba and the Baboon Demons,” reframing Babi within the study of Egyptian liminal beings. |
| Twenty-first century | Babi circulates in Kemetic devotional discussion, mythology databases, games, and online art; much modern material blends him with Thoth, Ammit, and unnamed Lake of Fire baboons. |

### Evidentiary conclusions

- The underworld entrail-eating is genuinely attested, but the standardized “devourer of all sinful souls at the Lake of Fire” biography is not.
- The phallus as ferry mast is genuinely attested in one Spell 99 recension, but not in every witness.
- “Bull of the baboons” is an important translation of PT 320, but should not be presented as an uncontested etymology.
- The Papyrus of Ani belongs centrally in Babi’s textual record, but its pictured judgment baboon is Thoth.
- Babi’s aggression and virility are securely grounded in early and later sources.
- No independent temple, priesthood, large cult, human sacrifice, temple prostitution, continuous survival, extremist appropriation, or major occult lineage has been demonstrated.
- The Babi–Seth equation is late and disputed; the Babi–Thoth relationship is hostile in the fullest surviving myth, not a timeless assistantship.
