# Bat: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*nṯrt*), specifically a bovine or cow-associated female deity in ancient Egyptian tradition. She is neither a bat-animal deity nor a demon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Bat is named in Pyramid Text Utterance 506, §1096b—“I am Bat with her two faces”—preserved in the late Sixth-Dynasty pyramids of Pepi I, Merenre, and Pepi II, approximately the twenty-third century BCE. Predynastic and Early Dynastic images resembling her later emblem, including the bovine faces on the Narmer Palette, are earlier but not inscribed with an unambiguous identification; they are therefore probable or contested attestations, not secure named ones. A possible First-Dynasty spelling of her name on a fragmentary stone vessel from Hierakonpolis is likewise disputed because the relevant fragments’ original positions are uncertain.

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early—secure]** Explicitly named or otherwise securely identified in contemporary Old Kingdom evidence.
- **[Early—probable/contested]** Predynastic or Early Dynastic material plausibly connected with Bat but not unambiguously labeled.
- **[Later canonical]** A later Egyptian religious formulation having broad institutional currency.
- **[Regional]** Evidence particular to a nome, town, temple, or local theological system.
- **[Syncretistic]** A historically observable fusion, borrowing, or identification between deities.
- **[Learned systematization]** A theological or antiquarian organization of older material, especially in later temple texts.
- **[Late accretion]** Greco-Roman, medieval, or early-modern material not documented in the early cult.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim or practice unattested before modern reception.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[Early—secure]** Bat was a female Egyptian deity whose name and double-faced character occur in royal funerary literature and whose cult personnel and provincial association are documented in Old Kingdom and Middle Kingdom inscriptions. Her emblem consisted, in its classical form, of a frontal human face with bovine ears and two elongated, inward-curving or spiral-ended horns or tendrils, commonly set upon a trapezoidal neck or support.

**[Regional]** By the reign of Senusret I, Bat is explicitly represented on that king’s White Chapel at Karnak as the goddess of the seventh nome of Upper Egypt. The nome lay immediately north of the Abydene/Thinite region and south of Hathor’s Denderite sixth nome. Its principal town was the settlement later called **Ḥwt-sḫm**, conventionally rendered Hut-Sekhem or “Mansion of Power,” and in later orthography reinterpreted as “Mansion of the Sistrum”; the modern site is Hu/Hiw, Greek **Diospolis Parva**.

**[Syncretistic]** During the Middle Kingdom Bat’s emblem entered Hathoric equipment, especially the sistrum and architectural “Hathor capital.” In New Kingdom evidence Hathor, not Bat, is “Mistress of Ḥwt-sḫm.” Bat’s independent divine personality consequently becomes nearly invisible, although the inherited emblem remained ubiquitous in Hathoric and other goddess imagery.

### Name and writing

The conventional Egyptological transliteration is **Bꜣt**, also printed **Bat**, **Bāt**, or, in older typography, **Bȝ.t/Bꜣ.t**. The final *-t* is the ordinary Egyptian feminine ending. “Bata” occasionally appears in popular literature, but it risks confusion with **Bata**, the male divine protagonist of the New Kingdom *Tale of the Two Brothers*. The two are not demonstrably the same being.

**[Modern reconstruction]** Henry George Fischer proposed that **Bꜣt** is the feminine form of **bꜣ**, a difficult Egyptian religious term conventionally translated “soul,” but more accurately encompassing a being’s mobile manifestation, power, or mode of presence. “Female *ba*” is consequently a plausible morphological gloss, not a securely attested ancient etymological explanation. The corresponding hieroglyphic spelling uses the jabiru/saddle-billed-stork sign with phonetic value *bꜣ*, feminine *t*, and a divine or emblematic determinative.

**[Early—probable/contested]** Fischer suggested that a jabiru sign beside the human-faced bovine emblem on a First-Dynasty diorite vessel from Hierakonpolis might together spell Bat’s name. Because the vessel is reconstructed from fragments and the original relation between bird and emblem is uncertain, this cannot serve as an incontrovertible written attestation.

**[Modern reconstruction]** Proposed Mesopotamian derivation of Bat’s face-and-horns design rests on visual parallels such as a human-faced bovine image from Ur. Fischer presented this only as a possibility and acknowledged that the foreign examples do not necessarily predate the Egyptian evidence. No Mesopotamian deity named Bat has been shown to be her source.

### Documented designations

- **Bꜣt ḥrwy.s snwy**, “Bat with her two faces.” **[Early—secure]** Pyramid Texts 506, §1096b, late Sixth Dynasty.
- **“Goddess of the seventh Upper Egyptian nome.”** **[Regional—secure]** Explicitly represented in the nome procession on Senusret I’s White Chapel at Karnak, early Twelfth Dynasty.
- **“Overseer of the *ḫnr* of Bat.”** **[Early—secure]** Title of the woman Nefret-Bat on Petrie Museum stela UC 14312, probably late Sixth Dynasty or First Intermediate Period. *Ḫnr* denotes an organized body of female cult performers or temple personnel; translating it mechanically as “harem” can impose misleading later associations.
- **“Overlord/chief of the Bat nome.”** **[Early—secure as a geographical title]** On Berlin stela 7765, probably late Sixth Dynasty. The emblem names the province; it does not itself supply a myth about the goddess.
- **“Human-faced.”** **[Middle Kingdom object terminology]** A Middle Kingdom coffin labels a related emblem “human-faced.” Fischer cautioned that this generic description could also be applied to other human-headed forms, so it does not make every such object an image of Bat.

No securely established ancient epithets identify Bat as “Lady of the Milky Way,” “Mother of Souls,” “Goddess of Fertility,” “Mistress of Music,” or “Uniter of the Two Lands.” These are modern extrapolations from bovine imagery, the word *bꜣ*, her emblem’s later sistrum use, or compositions in which she appears between political opposites.

### Equations and non-equations

- **Bat–Hathor:** **[Syncretistic—secure process]** Their images and cultic equipment became associated during the early Twelfth Dynasty. By the Eighteenth Dynasty Hathor had taken Bat’s place as mistress of Hu. This was a historical absorption, not evidence that the two names had always denoted one goddess.
- **Bat as an “aspect of Hathor”:** **[Later canonical/modern shorthand]** Reasonable for post-Middle-Kingdom imagery, but anachronistic when applied to all Predynastic and Old Kingdom Bat emblems.
- **Bat–Isis, Bat–Bastet, Bat–Tefnut:** **[Later iconographic diffusion]** Bat-derived frontal faces and sistrum forms could adorn cult objects of these goddesses, but shared equipment does not establish an independent composite deity “Bat-Isis,” “Bat-Bastet,” or “Bat-Tefnut.”
- **Bat–Hathor under Greek or Roman interpretation:** No Classical author is known to identify Bat separately with a Greek or Roman goddess. Plutarch discusses Hathor-related and Isis-related theology but supplies no independent Bat narrative.
- **Bat–Bata:** **[Unsupported]** The similarity of modern spellings does not establish identity with the male Bata.
- **Bat and the zoological bat:** **[False association]** The English homonym is accidental. Her Egyptian image is bovine and human, not chiropteran.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Predynastic and Early Dynastic candidates

**Gerzeh or “Cow-Head” Palette, Cairo JE 43103, Naqada II, approximately 3500–3200 BCE.**  
**[Early—probable/contested]** The palette bears an abstract head-like form with bovine ears or horns and five stars touching its extremities. Fischer connected its outline with the later Bat emblem. Earlier commentators variously saw a woman with raised arms or a cow head. Even if it represents a celestial bovine power, it carries no name; “Bat” remains an attribution.

**Hierakonpolis celestial-cow imagery, Naqada IID–III.**  
**[Early—probable/contested]** Pottery and seal material from Hierakonpolis and Abydos contains closely related horned, frontal, and sometimes star-associated emblems. Such finds establish the antiquity of a celestial-cow sign-complex, but not the name or full personality of Bat. The 2005 *Nekhen News* report treats a newly discovered emblem as Bat; that identification is typological rather than inscriptional.

**Narmer Palette, Cairo JE 32169/CG 14716, approximately 3100–3000 BCE.**  
**[Early—contested]** Excavated by James Quibell and Frederick Green in the “Main Deposit” of the Horus temple at Nekhen/Hierakonpolis in 1897–1898, this siltstone ceremonial palette places a human-faced bovine head on either side of Narmer’s serekh on both faces. A related head decorates the king’s apron. The [Egyptian Museum’s record](https://egyptianmuseumcairo.eg/artefacts/narmer-palette-collection/) calls them bovine heads without identifying a goddess.

The principal readings are:

1. **Bat:** Fischer and many later scholars compare the frontal face, projecting ears, incurving horns, double placement, and later “two faces” formula.
2. **Hathor:** Other scholars, including Lana Troy, connect the emblem with Hathor and royal costume; this assumes an earlier Hathoric bovine iconography than her securely named evidence.
3. **A generic celestial-cow or divine emblem:** This avoids assigning a name not written on the object.
4. **Royal bulls or royal power:** A minority interpretation treats the heads as embodiments of the king’s bovine vigor.

The image is thus the earliest famous **possible** Bat, not her earliest secure attestation. The palette’s political scene also should not be treated as a literal record of one moment of national unification without qualification.

**First-Dynasty Hierakonpolis stone vessel.**  
**[Early—probable/contested]** The vessel rim carries a Bat-like human-faced bovine emblem and a bird possibly read *bꜣ*. Fischer proposed that the group spells Bat. The uncertain joining of the fragments prevents certainty.

**First-Dynasty ivory from Semerkhet’s tomb at Abydos.**  
**[Early—probable]** Paired bovine emblems continue the doubled royal motif, but again lack an explicit divine caption.

### Pyramid Texts

**Pyramid Text Utterance 506, §1096b.**  
**[Early—secure]** This is Bat’s oldest unambiguous textual occurrence. Versions occur in pyramids of Pepi I, Merenre, and Pepi II; Merenre’s is especially clear. James P. Allen translates the sequence:

> “I am Blessing, I am Awe. I am Bat with her two faces. I am the one who saves myself, and I have saved myself from everything bad.”

The speaker is the deceased king identifying himself successively with divine and numinous powers. The passage is not a narrative hymn to Bat and does not state her parentage, consort, mythology, or cult. “Two faces” is explicit; its connection to double-sided emblems or sistra is interpretive. The full corpus is accessible in Allen’s [critical translation of the Pyramid Texts](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/AncientEgyptianPyramid_Allen_SBL.pdf), while UCL’s [Pyramid Texts overview](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/pyramid.html) documents the corpus’s monuments, chronology, and major editions.

### Coffin Texts

**Coffin Text IV, 181.**  
**[Middle Kingdom—secure but obscure]** A difficult sequence associates “the sun disk on the two horns” with “the face of Bat.” It is important evidence that Bat’s face and horned symbolism remained intelligible in the Middle Kingdom, but it does not amount to a narrative myth.

**Coffin Text V, 98.**  
**[Middle Kingdom—technical/contested]** Fischer interpreted a divine name or title in this passage as evidence for reading an official or cultic title connected with the Bat emblem. The text is philologically difficult and should not be expanded into a biography.

The [UCL Coffin Texts overview](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/coffin.html) dates the principal corpus to approximately 2025–1700 BCE and notes its relationship to both Pyramid Texts and the later Book of the Dead.

### Book of the Dead and later corpora

**Book of the Dead:** **[Absence of named evidence]** No standard Book of the Dead spell provides Bat with a distinct narrative, genealogy, or cult role. Bat-derived faces may occur in later divine or sistrum iconography, but these cannot automatically be read as appearances of Bat as an independent goddess. The [UCL corpus overview](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/bdfront.html) traces the collection from late Second Intermediate Period attestations through Roman-period use.

**Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** **[Absence]** No distinct Bat episode is securely attested.

**Contendings of Horus and Seth:** **[Absence]** Bat is not a character in the surviving New Kingdom narrative. A Middle Kingdom pectoral showing her or Hathor’s emblem between Horus and Seth is an iconographic composition, not evidence that Bat participated in the literary *Contendings*.

**Turin King List:** **[Absence]** The king list contributes nothing specific to Bat’s history.

**Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*:** **[Absence]** Plutarch does not preserve a separate Bat cult or myth.

**Greek Magical Papyri:** **[Absence]** No securely identified independent Bat invocation has been established. Hathor, Isis, and composite solar or animal forms occur; similarity of imagery is insufficient to rename them Bat.

**Edfu, Dendera, Karnak, and Philae temple inscriptions:**  
Karnak supplies the decisive Middle Kingdom nome-procession evidence on Senusret I’s White Chapel. Later Dendera and related temple material preserves the Bat-derived frontal goddess face within Hathoric theology and sistrum architecture. It does not demonstrate an uninterrupted independent Bat priesthood. Edfu and Philae contain elaborate Horus, Hathor, and Isis systems but no securely separable late cult of Bat.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### What is securely known

**[Early—secure]** Bat was a goddess, was described as two-faced, possessed an identifiable face/emblem, had cult personnel, and was associated with the seventh Upper Egyptian nome.

**[Early—probable]** Her doubled frontal face had a protective or encompassing royal function. Its placement above Narmer’s name and on royal costume suggests a relationship to royal authority, but no caption explains it.

**[Modern reconstruction]** She may have had celestial significance. The five stars touching the Gerzeh emblem and related early designs support a celestial reading if those objects are Bat-related. They do not specifically identify the Milky Way.

**[Modern reconstruction]** The Middle Kingdom pectoral placing a Bat/Hathor emblem between Horus and Seth may express equilibrium, totality, kingship, or reconciliation of opposed regions and forces. It does not contain a written statement that Bat “unified Egypt.”

### Form

The classical Bat emblem has:

- A frontal human female face.
- Projecting bovine ears.
- Originally heavy, ribbed, incurving bovine horns.
- In later Old Kingdom forms, long rope-like appendages ending in inward spirals.
- A trapezoidal neck, collar, pendant, knot, shrine, or sistrum support beneath the face.
- Frequently, a second face on the reverse of an object.

Frontal presentation is notable because most Egyptian human figures in relief show the head in profile. It made the face effective as a confronting emblem and allowed symmetrical architectural, jewelry, and musical-instrument applications.

### The sistrum

**[Corrected chronology]** Bat was not originally “the goddess of the sistrum” on presently available evidence. Old Kingdom sistra associated with Hathor lack the Bat face. Fischer found the first explicit combination in early Twelfth-Dynasty tombs at Meir, where female votaries of Hathor of Cusae carry Bat-faced sistra and *menit* necklaces. Under Senusret III the motif appears as sistrum, freestanding fetish, and architectural capital. An Amenemhat III jewel box from Lahun combines Bat faces with Hathor’s outward-curving horns and solar disk.

**[Syncretistic]** The Bat face and Hathoric sistrum then became so thoroughly joined that later museum catalogues properly use compound labels such as “Hathor/Bat emblem.” The Metropolitan Museum’s [New Kingdom ivory emblem](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/554645) explicitly describes the wig, collar, and shrine acquired when the Bat emblem entered the Hathoric sistrum. Its [Late Period faience fragment](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/550961) illustrates the mature naos-sistrum form.

The sistrum was shaken in temple ceremonies and in the approach to deities, especially great goddesses. Its rustling sound could be associated with papyrus and pacification. These are securely Hathoric and general temple functions; assigning all of them retrospectively to independent Bat is unwarranted.

### Other objects

- **Pendant and elite regalia:** Bat emblems appear on Old Kingdom officials’ clothing and jewelry. The emblem could signal office or court identity without proving personal devotion to Bat.
- **Amulets:** Old Kingdom and Middle Kingdom examples are known. Their protective function is probable from form and context, although individual amulets are often uninscribed.
- **Architectural capitals:** The Bat-derived frontal face becomes the familiar “Hathor capital.” Calling every such capital a depiction of Bat erases its later Hathoric setting.
- **Royal apron:** A related emblem appears on Narmer’s apron and on later royal fragments, including material from Djoser.
- **Nome emblem:** Its use could denote the seventh nome rather than depict a divine encounter.
- **Sistrum handles:** Later examples frequently preserve the face while their inscriptions address Hathor, Isis, Bastet, Tefnut, or another goddess.

### Unsupported attributes

No primary source securely assigns Bat a characteristic weapon, sacred plant, canonical color, sacred number, calendar day, planet, formal festival date, or divine mount. Cattle symbolism is intrinsic to the emblem, but no surviving text institutes a sacred herd of Bat. Claims that she was specifically goddess of fertility, sexuality, childbirth, motherhood, music, dance, the Milky Way, or the stars generally derive from analogy with Hathor or other cow goddesses.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

**[Absence of evidence]** No surviving Egyptian source names Bat’s parents, siblings, consort, or children. No birth narrative is preserved. She is not securely placed in the Heliopolitan Ennead, Hermopolitan Ogdoad, Memphite genealogy, or a documented family triad.

Modern family trees that make Bat a daughter of Ra, wife of Min, mother of Horus, form of Nut, or sister of Hathor are reconstructions or inventions unless they explicitly refer to a late syncretistic Hathor theology rather than Bat herself.

### Every documented or claimed god–god interaction

The record is extremely short. Mere co-presence in a nome list is not an interaction.

- **Hathor — progressive iconographic and cultic assimilation.** **[Syncretistic—secure]** Bat’s emblem is incorporated into Hathoric sistra at Meir during the first two reigns of Dynasty XII; under Senusret III it appears in Hathoric architectural and ritual settings; an Amenemhat III jewel box combines the Bat face with Hathor’s horns and solar disk. By Dynasty XVIII Hathor is “Mistress of Ḥwt-sḫm.” This is Bat’s only extensively documented divine relationship.
- **Horus — juxtaposition on a Middle Kingdom pectoral.** **[Iconographic, identification contested]** A probably Dahshur pectoral of Dynasty XII places a Bat/Hathor emblem at the center, with a falcon-headed sphinx interpreted as Horus on one side. No surviving text describes speech or action between them.
- **Seth — juxtaposition on the same pectoral.** **[Iconographic, identification contested]** Seth in animal form occupies the opposing side. The symmetric composition implies balance or royal totality; it is not a narrative in which Bat adjudicates the Horus–Seth dispute. The object is catalogued by [Eton College](https://collections.etoncollege.com/online-exhibition/ancient-beings/gods/).
- **Min — emblematic association on an Early Dynastic ivory plaque from Abu Rawash.** **[Early—contested]** A Bat-like emblem occurs with “thunderbolt” signs associated with Min. Some modern scholarship calls Bat Min’s female counterpart. The object documents juxtaposed signs, not marriage, sexual union, or genealogy.
- **Triphis/Repyt — shared object composition.** **[Early—indirect/contested]** An archaic carrying-chair image of Repyt includes a Bat emblem at the front. Fischer considered analogies with royal-birth and Hathoric symbolism but stressed that the connection is indirect; this is not a narrated interaction.
- **Sekhmet — later title containing a Bat-emblem term.** **[New Kingdom—technical, not personal interaction]** A Nineteenth-Dynasty title on the statue of the vizier Paser refers to an office or emblem “in the Mansion of Sekhmet.” It shows migration of Bat-related terminology, not a mythic meeting.
- **Isis, Bastet, Tefnut, Mut, and other sistrum-bearing goddesses — use of inherited Bat/Hathor emblem.** **[Later canonical iconographic diffusion]** These goddesses can carry or inhabit Bat-faced sistrum imagery. No separate myth records them meeting Bat.
- **Ra — no direct interaction securely attested.** The stars, solar disk, and later Hathoric solar associations do not by themselves establish a Bat–Ra genealogy.
- **Osiris — no direct interaction securely attested.** Osirian offering formulae on stelae belonging to Bat’s personnel concern the deceased person’s funerary cult, not a meeting of Bat and Osiris.
- **Bata — no interaction or identity securely attested.**
- **Nekhbet, Wadjet, Neith, Ptah, Thoth, Anubis, Sobek, Amun, and the named deities of the major narrative corpora — no documented personal interaction with Bat.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### “Bat with her two faces”

**[Early—secure formula; later interpretation]** The sole compact verbal image approaching a mythic statement is Pyramid Text 506. The deceased king moves through a sequence of identifications—Blessing, Awe, Bat, the four Sons of Horus, and other powers—to secure salvation from harm. Bat contributes her doubled or comprehensive face to that process.

The text does not explain why she has two faces. Proposed explanations include:

1. A front-and-back emblem or pendant.
2. The two sides of a sistrum.
3. Two faces guarding opposite directions.
4. Upper and Lower Egypt.
5. Nile valley and desert.
6. General divine totality.

Only the first—double-sided material form—is closely supported by surviving objects. The sistrum explanation is chronologically problematic because secure Bat-faced sistra appear much later than the Pyramid Text formulation. Political readings remain plausible symbolism, not ancient exegesis.

### Bat and royal totality

**[Modern reconstruction]** The Narmer Palette and the Dynasty XII Horus–Seth pectoral encouraged a reconstructed “cycle” in which Bat protects kingship or unifies opposed regions. The artifacts support royal and symmetrical associations, but no continuous narrative survives. It is better described as a recurring visual function than a myth.

### Bat and the celestial cow

**[Early—probable/modern reconstruction]** Predynastic horned emblems accompanied by stars may represent a celestial bovine power. Later Hathor and Nut are securely sky-associated, and the *Tale of Sinuhe* calls Hathor “Mistress of the Stars.” These parallels do not prove that Bat personified the Milky Way. Modern claims that the Milky Way was imagined specifically as Bat or as a pool of cow’s milk lack a cited Egyptian text. Recent astronomical scholarship instead emphasizes how uncertain the Egyptian name and personification of the Milky Way remain.

### No surviving narrative cycle

There is no securely attested Bat equivalent of:

- the Osiris cycle;
- the Contendings of Horus and Seth;
- the Distant Goddess;
- the Destruction of Humanity;
- the Eye of Ra cycle;
- a divine marriage;
- a descent, combat, death, resurrection, or creation narrative.

Her absence is not evidence that oral stories never existed, only that the documented corpus does not preserve them.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult center and geography

**[Regional—secure]** Bat belonged to the seventh nome of Upper Egypt. Its capital was in the Hu/Hiw area, Greek Diospolis Parva. The precise history of the town’s name is important: Fischer argued that the full Middle Kingdom form referred to Senusret I’s throne name Kheperkare and originally meant approximately “the Mansion ‘Kheperkare-is-powerful.’” Reinterpretation of *sḫm* as “sistrum” is first securely visible in Dynasty XIX spelling. Thus the attractive phrase “Bat’s town was always called Mansion of the Sistrum” is anachronistic.

### Temple

**[Early—secure institutional existence; archaeology absent]** Stela UC 14312 names an overseer of Bat’s *ḫnr*, implying a temple or cult establishment. Senusret I’s White Chapel confirms her provincial status. No securely identified monumental temple of Bat has been excavated at Hu, and no architectural history comparable to Hathor’s Dendera temple can be reconstructed.

Claims about a great Predynastic “Temple of Bat” at Hierakonpolis exceed the evidence. Bat-like emblems occurred in temple deposits, but the known sanctuary there was later associated with Horus and does not bear Bat’s name.

### Personnel

**Nefret-Bat on UC 14312.**  
**[Early—secure]** The stela, probably late Old Kingdom or early First Intermediate Period, names the owner’s wife Nfr-Bꜣt and gives her the title “overseer of the *ḫnr* of Bat.” The provenance may be Abydos or the neighboring seventh nome; Fischer considered both possibilities. The title is the clearest direct evidence for organized female personnel.

**Nome governors.**  
**[Regional—secure]** Berlin stela 7765 calls its owner “overlord of the Bat [nome].” This is administrative and geographical, not necessarily priestly.

**Priesthood after assimilation.**  
**[Syncretistic]** By the New Kingdom personnel at Hu bear Hathoric titles. A Twenty-Second-Dynasty Dakhla stela may show worship of the old fetish, but its priestly title belongs to Hathor and the emblem is flanked by figures of Hathor. Fischer therefore rejected it as proof that Bat had recovered independent status.

### Ritual objects and practice

- **Emblem and pendant:** worn by officials and deposited as amulets.
- **Sistrum:** securely linked with the Bat emblem only from Dynasty XII onward, in Hathoric cult settings.
- **Menit necklace:** appears beside Bat-faced sistra at Meir as Hathoric ritual equipment.
- **Columns and furniture:** the emblem decorates bed canopies, poles, carrying chairs, jewelry boxes, and capitals.
- **Votive or funerary stelae:** document personnel and provincial titles but preserve no Bat-specific prayer liturgy.
- **Offerings:** A temple establishment would have required ordinary Egyptian offerings, but no surviving Bat ritual manual specifies a distinctive sacrificial regime.

### Violence and disputed practices

**Human sacrifice:** No source documents human sacrifice to Bat. Early royal subsidiary burials and violent imagery on the Narmer Palette are wider historical problems; neither is labeled a sacrifice to her.

**Temple prostitution:** No evidence links Bat’s *ḫnr* to commercial or sacred prostitution. Older translations of *ḫnr* as “harem” helped generate misleading sexualized pictures of Egyptian goddess cults; the title more safely denotes an organized female performance or cult body.

**Cult violence:** No text attributes ritual violence, persecution, or military action to Bat’s priesthood. Narmer’s smiting scene is royal political iconography and cannot be treated as violence ordered by Bat merely because bovine faces appear above it.

**Economics:** The existence of staff and a local temple implies land, offerings, and administration, but no surviving account identifies Bat’s estates, revenue, workshops, or pilgrimage economy.

**Festivals and oracles:** None can be securely reconstructed for Bat independently. Later Hathoric festivals at Dendera and elsewhere cannot simply be transferred to her.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### Naqada II–III, approximately 3500–3000 BCE

**[Early—probable/contested]** Frontal bovine or human-bovine emblems, sometimes joined with stars, appear at Gerzeh, Hierakonpolis, and Abydos. These constitute the visual ancestry of the classical Bat emblem. Whether every example already denoted a goddess named Bat is unknowable.

### Dynasty 0–First Dynasty, approximately 3100–2800 BCE

**[Early—probable/contested]** The Narmer Palette gives the emblem extraordinary royal prominence. First-Dynasty vessels, ivories, and seal material continue the form. A possible hieroglyphic spelling at Hierakonpolis remains uncertain. The emblem’s geographic distribution beyond the seventh nome warns against treating it as merely local at this early stage.

### Old Kingdom, approximately 2686–2181 BCE

**[Early—secure]** The emblem enters court dress, amulets, furniture, architecture, and provincial signs. Pyramid Text 506 supplies the first certain name and double-faced description. Stela UC 14312 supplies cult personnel; Berlin 7765 records the Bat nome. The evidence reveals institutional worship but no narrative mythology.

Fischer’s influential reconstruction treats Bat as the seventh nome’s local goddess. Andrea Rodríguez Valls’s 2022 dissertation, based on 142 emblem-related documents, challenges both the automatic equation of emblem with goddess and the prevailing description of Bat as simply local. This revisionist position underscores that an emblem may serve royal, geographical, administrative, and decorative functions without always personifying Bat.

### First Intermediate Period and early Middle Kingdom

**[Regional]** Sparse provincial inscriptions continue. Bat remains a recognized name and face, while neighboring Hathor of Dendera expands in prominence. The absence of a rich textual record at Hu may reflect archaeological loss and later assimilation rather than instantaneous cultic extinction.

### Dynasty XII, approximately 1985–1773 BCE

**[Syncretistic—secure turning point]**

- Senusret I’s White Chapel explicitly names Bat as goddess of nome seven.
- Early Dynasty XII Meir tombs incorporate the Bat face into Hathoric sistra.
- Senusret III-period Meir material uses it in Hathoric sistra and capitals.
- An Amenemhat III object joins it with Hathor’s outward-curving horns and disk.
- Coffin Text material preserves “the face of Bat.”

This is better described as progressive appropriation and fusion of emblematic systems than as one decree merging two gods.

### New Kingdom, approximately 1550–1069 BCE

**[Later canonical]** Hathor is mistress of Hu/Ḥwt-sḫm. Bat ceases to be well attested as an independently addressed deity. Her face survives as part of the Hathoric sistrum, capital, and cult emblem. The Metropolitan Museum’s ivory object of approximately 1550–1479 BCE visually embodies this transformed form.

The shift was not documented as suppression, prohibition, or violent replacement. It appears to be absorption within Egyptian theological and cultic practice.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods, approximately 1069–332 BCE

**[Later canonical]** Bat-derived faces remain widespread on sistra, amulets, aegises, and capitals. The Metropolitan Museum’s faience sistrum fragment of 664–332 BCE preserves the frontal face, collar, uraei, and naos. Museum labels use “Bat/Hathor” because the morphology descends from Bat while the functioning cult object is commonly Hathoric.

A Dynasty XXII representation from Dakhla may give special prominence to the fetish, but the accompanying figures and priesthood are Hathoric. It does not prove restoration of a separate Bat cult.

### Ptolemaic and Roman Egypt, 332 BCE–fourth century CE

**[Later canonical/learned systematization]** Sistra with the inherited Bat/Hathor face continue in temples of Hathor, Isis, and other goddesses. The Met’s [Ptolemaic silver sistrum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/551361) illustrates the standard form. Late temple theologians multiplied local equations among goddesses, but no evidence demonstrates a major independent Bat sanctuary, pilgrimage, or priesthood.

### Christianization and Islamization

**[Historical absence]** No decree or martyr narrative names Bat’s cult for special suppression. Egyptian temples generally lost resources, were closed, abandoned, or converted during Late Antiquity, but Bat had long since ceased to be independently prominent. It is therefore misleading to narrate a discrete Christian or Islamic “persecution of Bat.”

No securely documented medieval Egyptian folklore preserves Bat by name. The sistrum itself survived differently in Coptic liturgical instruments, but direct continuity from Bat’s cult is not established.

### Modern revival

**[Modern reception]** Modern Kemetic and neopagan practitioners can and sometimes do address Bat as an individual deity, usually using published Egyptological reconstructions and the Pyramid Text formula. Such worship represents revival after a long documentary discontinuity. No reliable census establishes its scale; Bat is far less prominent than Isis, Hathor, Bastet, Anubis, or Sekhmet.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Egyptology and museums

Bat’s modern visibility comes principally from three things:

1. identification of the Narmer Palette heads;
2. Fischer’s 1962 synthesis of her cult and nome;
3. museum catalogues distinguishing the older Bat face from the Hathoric sistrum that absorbed it.

The British Museum’s [Bat authority record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/BIOG236852) classifies her as an Egyptian female deity generally represented by a cow head with curling horns. The Metropolitan Museum uses the more cautious designation “Hathor/Bat emblem” where the object belongs to the merged iconographic tradition.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

**[Absence/modern invention]** Bat is not a distinct major power in the Renaissance Hermetica, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Picatrix tradition, Golden Dawn grades, Aleister Crowley’s principal Egyptian syncretisms, or standard early-modern grimoires. Modern websites that give her planetary correspondences, tarot cards, chakras, crystals, moon phases, colors, or magical “portfolios” are constructing new systems.

**[Modern invention]** Personal gnosis that Bat governs “all-seeing awareness,” “shadow work,” “soul retrieval,” “divine femininity,” or the Milky Way can be meaningful within modern religion but is not recoverable ancient doctrine.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

**[Modern revival]** Contemporary Kemetic reconstruction creates independent devotional practice for gods whose original liturgies are lost. For Bat, reconstruction may employ:

- Pyramid Text 506;
- bovine and double-faced images;
- modern replicas of sistra;
- offerings customary in broader reconstructed Egyptian religion;
- the historic geography of Hu.

Such practice should not be represented as an unbroken survival. The attachment of the sistrum is historically defensible for the merged Middle Kingdom and later form but not demonstrably part of Bat’s earliest cult.

### Popular confusion

- **Bat as a bat-winged goddess:** **[Modern invention]** Based on the English word “bat,” not Egyptian evidence.
- **Bat as “the original Hathor”:** **[Overstatement]** Bat-like imagery may predate secure Hathor attestations, but the relationship is not a simple one-way evolutionary sequence.
- **Bat as the Milky Way:** **[Unsupported modern reconstruction]** Celestial associations are possible; specific identification with the Galaxy is unproven.
- **Bat as goddess of fertility and sexuality:** **[Transferred attribute]** Commonly borrowed from Hathor and generalized cow symbolism.
- **Bat as ruler of Upper Egypt while Hathor ruled Lower Egypt:** **[False]** Both principal cult regions were neighboring Upper Egyptian nomes.
- **Bat in *True Blood*:** **[Misidentification]** The series’ bull-headed statue belongs to its fictional Maenad/Dionysian plot, not a documented representation of Egyptian Bat.
- **Bat and Bastet:** **[Modern confusion]** Their names and animals are unrelated.

There is no major ancient-source-based Bat tradition in modern film, novels, comics, or games comparable to the reception of Isis, Anubis, Set, or Bastet. Her chief popular image remains the retrospectively labeled Narmer Palette face.

### Political appropriation

**[Modern reconstruction/absence]** The Narmer Palette is often presented in nationalist or popular histories as the founding image of Egyptian unity, and Bat is sometimes made its divine guarantor. Because both the palette’s event and the bovine heads’ identification are debated, claims that Bat was historically “the goddess who united Egypt” overstate the evidence.

No substantial modern extremist movement appears to have adopted Bat as a central emblem. Superficial online association with “Aryan cow cults,” universal prehistoric mother-goddess theories, or racialized diffusionism is not supported by the Egyptian record.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Fischer’s reconstruction

Henry George Fischer’s “The Cult and Nome of the Goddess Bat” remains the foundational focused study. It assembled the Pyramid Text, stelae, nome evidence, emblem history, and Middle Kingdom sistrum material. Its key conclusions were:

- Bat’s secure written evidence begins in Dynasty VI.
- Her emblem probably appears earlier.
- Bat and Hathor should not be assumed to have been identical from the beginning.
- The Bat face did not securely enter the Hathoric sistrum before the Middle Kingdom.
- Hathor had eclipsed Bat independently by the New Kingdom.

The complete article is available through the [University of Geneva copy](https://www.unige.ch/Hout-Sekhem/application/files/4217/2995/9531/Fischer_-_The_Cult_and_Nome_of_the_Goddess_Bat.pdf).

### The Narmer Palette dispute

The Bat identification is attractive because of the later emblem’s human face, ears, incurving horns, and doubleness. Its weaknesses are the absence of a caption and chronological distance from the explicit Pyramid Text name.

The Hathor identification relies on Hathor’s later royal and bovine prominence but faces the problem that secure Hathor evidence is later and her mature horns normally curve outward. A generic celestial-cow reading avoids overprecision. A royal-bull reading challenges the assumption that the faces are female or divine.

The proper catalogue statement is therefore: “human-faced bovine heads, frequently identified as Bat, alternatively Hathor, a generic celestial-cow deity, or royal bovine power.”

### Emblem versus goddess

Fischer already warned that not every occurrence of the emblem necessarily evoked Bat. A Middle Kingdom coffin could call it simply “human-faced”; it served as pendant, office badge, architectural decoration, nome sign, and later Hathoric equipment.

Andrea Rodríguez Valls’s 2022 Spanish dissertation, *El emblema bat. Permanencia y cambio de un elemento religioso*, pushes this distinction further. Its [abstract and bibliographic record](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/tesis?codigo=320509) report a corpus of 142 documents and reject both the prevailing definition of Bat as merely a local goddess and the conventional claim of a straightforward Middle Kingdom assimilation. Because the full thesis is not openly available at that record, its detailed arguments cannot be evaluated here; its conclusion nevertheless demonstrates that Fischer’s model is not uncontested.

### Origin and etymology

The derivation from *bꜣ* plus feminine *-t* is linguistically plausible but not explained by an ancient text. Claims that the term means “female power,” “female soul,” or “feminine principle” are interpretive glosses. Proposed Mesopotamian iconographic influence remains speculative.

### Celestial interpretation

Five stars around early bovine forms establish some relation to stars if those forms are correctly reconstructed. They do not tell us whether the image is Bat, Hathor, a constellation, Sirius, the whole sky, or a decorative celestial sign. Fischer expressly allowed that the five-star arrangement could represent an unidentified constellation.

Modern statements that Bat “was the Milky Way” are especially weak. Research on the Galaxy in Egyptian thought notes that its ancient name and role are unclear; recent studies concentrate instead on possible relations with Nut and the “Winding Waterway.”

### “Absorption” versus coexistence

“Absorbed by Hathor” is a convenient summary but can suggest that Bat simply disappeared at one moment. The evidence shows stages:

1. separate or not-yet-combined emblems;
2. Bat-face plus Hathoric ritual equipment;
3. composite horns, disk, face, collar, and shrine;
4. Hathor assuming Bat’s local title and town;
5. continued use of a Bat-derived emblem after Bat’s personal name becomes rare.

Egyptian syncretism did not necessarily annihilate constituent deities philosophically. Nevertheless, Fischer found no persuasive evidence for an independently functioning Bat cult after the beginning of the New Kingdom.

### State of the evidence

Principal objects are dispersed among:

- Egyptian Museum, Cairo: Narmer Palette, Gerzeh Palette, and other early material.
- Petrie Museum, University College London: UC 14312 and related finds.
- Neues Museum/Staatliche Museen zu Berlin: Berlin 7765 and a Sahure temple relief with Bat emblem.
- Metropolitan Museum of Art: Bat amulet, New Kingdom Hathor/Bat emblem, Late Period sistrum fragments, and later instruments.
- British Museum: Middle Kingdom bangle/amulet, scarabs, inlays, and related emblem objects.
- Eton College Myers Collection: Dynasty XII Horus–Seth pectoral.
- Karnak Open-Air Museum: reconstructed White Chapel of Senusret I.

The primary problem is not total absence but ambiguity: an abundant sign survives, while the goddess’s own narratives, liturgy, temple archive, and genealogy do not.

## 10. Chronology

- **Naqada IIC–D, approximately 3600–3300 BCE:** **[Probable/contested]** Frontal bovine and star-associated images emerge; later comparison connects them with the Bat emblem.
- **Approximately 3500–3200 BCE:** **[Probable/contested]** Gerzeh Palette JE 43103 bears a stylized horned, five-star form.
- **Naqada IID–III:** **[Probable/contested]** Bat-like emblems occur at Hierakonpolis and Abydos.
- **Approximately 3100–3000 BCE:** **[Contested]** Narmer Palette displays paired human-faced bovine heads and an apron emblem; Bat is a leading but unproven identification.
- **First Dynasty:** **[Probable]** Bat-like paired emblems appear on royal ivories and vessels; a Hierakonpolis vessel may spell her name.
- **Third Dynasty:** **[Material continuity]** Related emblem appears on Djoser-period royal imagery.
- **Fourth Dynasty:** **[Material continuity]** Bat emblems occur in elite regalia and architectural decoration; Hathor’s distinct named and solar-bovine evidence becomes clearer.
- **Fifth–Sixth Dynasties:** **[Secure institutional/geographical evidence]** Emblem appears on court dress, amulets, temple reliefs, and nome signs.
- **Late Sixth Dynasty, approximately twenty-third century BCE:** **[Earliest secure named attestation]** Pyramid Text 506 declares the king “Bat with her two faces.”
- **Late Sixth Dynasty/First Intermediate Period:** **[Secure cult evidence]** UC 14312 names Nefret-Bat, overseer of Bat’s *ḫnr*.
- **Late Old Kingdom:** **[Secure geographical evidence]** Berlin 7765 records an overlord of the Bat nome.
- **Eleventh–early Twelfth Dynasty:** **[Transition]** Bat emblem remains distinct but begins appearing in Hathoric contexts.
- **Reign of Senusret I, approximately 1965–1920 BCE:** **[Secure regional attestation]** White Chapel at Karnak identifies Bat with nome seven; early Hathoric use of Bat-faced sistra appears at Meir and Beni Hasan.
- **Reign of Senusret III, approximately 1872–1854 BCE:** **[Syncretistic]** Meir tombs show Bat motif as Hathoric sistrum, fetish, and column capital.
- **Reign of Amenemhat III, approximately 1854–1806 BCE:** **[Syncretistic]** Lahun jewel box combines Bat faces with Hathor’s horns and solar disk.
- **Middle Kingdom:** **[Textual continuity]** Coffin Text IV, 181 mentions “the face of Bat.”
- **Early New Kingdom, after approximately 1550 BCE:** **[Later canonical]** Hathor is mistress of Hu; Bat is scarcely attested as an independent goddess.
- **Eighteenth Dynasty:** **[Iconographic survival]** Bat-derived face remains in Hathoric sistra, pendants, and columns.
- **Nineteenth Dynasty:** **[Learned reinterpretation]** Orthography of Ḥwt-sḫm encourages “Mansion of the Sistrum”; Bat-related terminology appears in wider priestly contexts.
- **Twenty-Second Dynasty:** **[Ambiguous survival]** Dakhla stela venerates a Bat-derived fetish within an explicitly Hathoric framework.
- **664–332 BCE:** **[Later canonical]** Elaborate faience and metal sistra preserve the Hathor/Bat face.
- **332–30 BCE:** **[Later canonical]** Ptolemaic sistra retain the emblem in multi-goddess temple worship.
- **Roman Period:** **[Iconographic afterlife]** Sistrum and frontal goddess-face imagery continue, especially in Isis and Hathor religion; independent Bat cult remains unattested.
- **Late Antiquity:** Egyptian temple cults decline or close; no Bat-specific suppression is documented.
- **1897–1898 CE:** Quibell and Green recover the Narmer Palette at Hierakonpolis, creating the principal modern debate over Bat’s earliest image.
- **1958 CE:** Reconstructed First-Dynasty Hierakonpolis vessel prompts Fischer’s proposed early spelling of Bat.
- **1962 CE:** Fischer publishes “The Cult and Nome of the Goddess Bat,” establishing the standard modern historical reconstruction.
- **1975 CE:** Fischer’s *Lexikon der Ägyptologie* entry consolidates Bat’s reference history.
- **2003–2005 CE:** Wilkinson, Hart, and Hierakonpolis publications bring the Bat identification into widely used modern reference literature.
- **2009 CE:** M. G. Rashed publishes “The Goddess Bat and the Confusion with Hathor,” catalogued by the IFAO [Early Egypt Bibliography](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/bases/beo/beo15112?id=15112&lang=en).
- **2022 CE:** Rodríguez Valls completes a 142-document reassessment challenging the standard local-goddess and assimilation model.
- **Present:** Bat has a modest museum, popular, and reconstructed Kemetic presence; no uninterrupted cultic continuity or reliable count of modern worshippers is established.
