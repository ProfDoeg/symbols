# Ihy: Research Dossier

**Class:** male Egyptian god; more specifically a divine child, ritual musician, solar-renewal figure, funerary guide, and protective intermediary. He is never securely classified as a demon, monster, collective, or deified human.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the Fifth-Dynasty royal annals preserved on the Palermo Stone, under Neferirkare Kakai, approximately 2475–2455 BCE. The entry records the fashioning and Opening-of-the-Mouth ritual of an electrum statue of Ihy for a procession to Hathor of the Sycamore. It attests an anthropomorphic divine image, ritual animation, and association with Hathor; it does **not** explicitly call Ihy her child, identify Horus as his father, or establish a Dendera triad.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** directly documented Old- or Middle-Kingdom evidence.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** established New-Kingdom or later Egyptian religious material.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** local theology or iconography, especially Dendera.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** priestly synthesis, theological wordplay, or scholarly reconstruction.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically situated equation or overlap with another deity.
- **[MA] Medieval/early-modern accretion:** post-antique reception.
- **[F] Folklore:** popular tradition not demonstrably inherited from an ancient cult.
- **[MR] Modern reconstruction or revival.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern claim.**
- **[Ø] Absence of evidence:** specifically searched-for material that is not securely attested.

Dates are BCE/CE unless otherwise indicated. Transliterations use a normalized Egyptological system; fonts and editorial conventions differ among publications.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[EA–LC]** Ihy is best understood not as a god with one continuous narrative biography but as the divine personification and performer of the ecstatic, protective, and regenerative force associated with Hathoric music—especially the sistrum and *menat*. Middle-Kingdom funerary texts make him a child of Hathor, a form assumed by the deceased, a guide through dangerous regions, and a figure of rebirth. New-Kingdom art fixes his recognizable child-and-sistrum iconography. Ptolemaic and Roman Dendera then embeds him in an explicit local family: Hathor, Horus of Edfu, and their child Ihy.

Calling him simply “the god of music and joy” is serviceable but reductive. The sources also make him a divine acolyte, protector, solar child, ritual heir, conductor of the deceased, and participant in royal regeneration. Conversely, modern descriptions such as “god of childhood,” “god of pleasure,” or “god of lust” are generally interpretive expansions rather than translated ancient titles.

### Name and writing

The usual modern forms are **Ihy**, **Ihi**, **Ahy**, and occasionally **Ehi**. The normalized Egyptian form is commonly written:

- **jḥj / Iḥy** — conventional Egyptological transliteration.
- Hieroglyphically, spellings vary; a commonly reproduced consonantal spelling is 𓇋𓎛𓇌 or variants expanded with phonetic complements, plural strokes, a divine determinative, or a child/standing-god determinative.
- Greek has no securely established independent cult-name for him comparable to Ἁρποκράτης for Horus-the-Child.

Two principal etymologies compete:

1. **“Sistrum-player,” “musician,” or “he who plays/makes jubilation.”**  
   **[EA/LS]** This interpretation fits Ihy’s persistent relation to the sistrum and Egyptian words connected with rejoicing or jubilation. Egyptian temple theology itself connected his name with sounding the instrument. It is therefore more than a purely modern guess, although the precise morphology remains debated.

2. **“Calf.”**  
   **[LS]** This derives the name from Egyptian *jḥ*, “calf,” and interprets Ihy as the young of cow-shaped Hathor. It gains circumstantial support from a Sixth-Dynasty cattle-crossing scene in Kagemni’s mastaba where the divine name is written beside a calf. That scene is unique and disputed: it may identify the calf as Ihy, invoke Ihy to guide it through the water, or pun on the name. It cannot by itself prove that Ihy originated as a calf god.

The name is therefore not securely reducible to one English word. “Sistrum-player” best matches his historically dominant role; “calf” remains a plausible ancient pun or secondary interpretation.

### Documented titles and descriptions

The following are representative rather than an exhaustive concordance of every orthographic variant:

- **“Son of Hathor” (*zꜣ Ḥwt-Ḥr*)** — **[EA]** repeatedly in Coffin Texts spell 334 and spell 563.
- **“Whom she loves”** — **[EA]** CT 563: Ihy is “son of Hathor, whom she loves.”
- **“Child/baby, son of Hathor”** — **[EA]** CT 334.
- **“Ihy of Hathor”** — **[EA]** CT 146, where he is the deceased’s protection for life.
- **“Protector”** — **[EA]** CT 334; not merely inferred from iconography.
- **“Lord of hearts”** — **[LC]** Book of the Dead 149, in a petition that he reassemble the deceased’s bones and establish the crown.
- **“Jackal of the sunshine”** — **[EA/LS]** CT 334; its exact theological implication is disputed.
- **“Bull of turbulence”** — **[EA]** CT 334, within its sexually generative and solar language.
- **“Great god”** — **[RV]** exceptional at Dendera rather than Ihy’s normal rank. A Dendera festival celebrated the moment when Hathor gave him the sistrum and he assumed divine authority; scholarship rightly treats this as local late theology rather than evidence that he had always been a major national god.
- **“Sistrum-player” / divine musician** — **[LC/RV]** pervasive functional identity in temple scenes, especially Dendera.
- **“Uniter of the Two Lands”** — **[RV]** expressed iconographically by his Double Crown in Dendera reliefs; it assimilates the divine child to royal succession.

### Identifications and distinctions

- **Ihy and Horus-the-Child/Harpocrates:** **[SI]** Their child iconography and maternal relationships overlap increasingly in the Late and Greco-Roman periods. This is not proof that Ihy was originally Harpocrates. Coffin Texts already give Ihy protective and child-renewal functions before the flourishing of Harpocrates’s independent cult. The often-repeated claim of a simple “Late Period assimilation” requires case-by-case inscriptional demonstration.
- **Ihy and Harsomtus (Horus-Sematawy):** **[RV/SI]** Both can serve as the child of Horus of Edfu and Hathor in the Dendera–Edfu system. They are sometimes alternatives or overlapping child manifestations, but Dendera texts also enumerate them separately. “Ihy is another name for Harsomtus” is therefore too absolute.
- **Ihy and Khonsu:** **[EA/SI]** CT 334 gives the speaker acclaim in the name Ihy and in the name Khonsu; the comparison rests partly on their shared child, traveling, and guidance functions.
- **Ihy and Re:** **[EA/SI]** The deceased can bear both names in CT 368. In CT 334 Ihy is also solarized and grows “high like my father.”
- **Ihy and Hu:** **[EA/LS]** CT 51 says Horus “creates” or “acts the part of Ihy in/with Hu,” depending on the disputed verb. Scholars associate both with efficacious sound or authoritative utterance, but they are not simply the same god.
- **Ihy and the king:** **[LC]** The king can act as Ihy by playing Hathor’s instruments, while Tutankhamun’s funerary statuettes embody the royal deceased as, or under the aspect of, Ihy.
- **Greek or Roman equation:** **[Ø]** No secure equation of Ihy with Apollo, Eros, Dionysus, or another classical god is known. Greek descriptions of Dendera more readily identify Hathor with Aphrodite; Ihy retained Egyptian forms and nomenclature.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Palermo Stone

**[EA]** The Royal Annals survive in several fragments, the best known being the basalt-like stone now in Palermo. The inscription itself was compiled during the Fifth Dynasty, although it retrospectively records earlier reigns. Its provenance is unknown; Heliopolis has been proposed but is not established. Modern imaging has improved readings, while the exact relationship among all purported annal fragments remains debated. [Hsu’s study](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1524/aofo.2010.0006/html) and the [Palermo Stone research project](https://cegu.ff.cuni.cz/en/new-research-and-insights-into-the-palermo-stone/) summarize these issues.

The Ihy entry, conventionally cited as *Urkunden I*, 247, belongs to Neferirkare’s reign:

> *nswt-bjt Nfr-jr-kꜣ-Rꜥ … ms(t) wpt-rꜣ ḏꜥm twt Jḥj šms r Ḥwt-Ḥr nht Mrt-Snfrw*

A cautious translation is: “The Dual King Neferirkare: he made as his monument the fashioning and Opening of the Mouth of an electrum statue of Ihy, to accompany/proceed to Hathor of the Sycamore in ‘Beloved-of-Sneferu.’”

The determinative represents a standing anthropomorphic statue. The text establishes:

- Ihy was treated as a god with an animated cult image.
- The image was made of electrum or adorned with it.
- His image accompanied a Hathoric procession.
- Royal resources supported the rite.

It does **not** establish a Dendera temple for Ihy, his later naked-child iconography, or Horus’s paternity.

### Old Kingdom material

- **Palermo Stone, Fifth Dynasty:** described above.
- **Theophoric personal names:** **[EA]** Ihy occurs in Old-Kingdom names, corroborating recognition of the divine name but not disclosing doctrine.
- **Kagemni mastaba, Saqqara, Sixth Dynasty:** **[EA/disputed]** a cattle-fording scene puts the divine spelling of Ihy near a calf. Herman Altenmüller interpreted it as invoking Ihy’s aid for safe passage; other readings see the calf as an embodiment or rebus of Ihy. It is not secure evidence of routine animal worship.
- **Pyramid Texts:** **[Ø]** Ihy is not explicitly named in the surviving Old-Kingdom Pyramid Texts. This negative finding is important: the Palermo entry predates them, but he did not enter their preserved royal mortuary repertoire under his recognizable name.

### Coffin Texts

The Coffin Texts were written chiefly on Middle-Kingdom coffins, approximately 2055–1650 BCE, while incorporating older material. Adriaan de Buck’s seven-volume edition remains the basic text; Raymond Faulkner’s translation is still widely used, though individual readings require checking against de Buck and newer lexical work. The University of Chicago makes volumes of [De Buck’s edition](https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/oip/egyptian-coffin-texts-1-texts-spells-1-75) accessible.

Documented Ihy passages include:

- **CT 36 (I, 140f):** **[EA]** the deceased brings Osiris “the water of life which is in the two hands of Ihy.”
- **CT 51 (I, 236h–237a):** **[EA/disputed]** Horus son of Osiris “creates” or “acts the part of” Ihy in/with Hu. The verb is textually and lexically uncertain.
- **CT 146 (II, 199b):** **[EA]** “Ihy of Hathor is the protection of this N for life.”
- **CT 326 (IV, 161d–f):** **[EA]** the deceased takes possession of Ihy “for eternity” and places Sia/Perception at his feet. The relevant wording is confined to one witness, Sq6C.
- **CT 334 (IV, 179–183):** **[EA]** the central Ihy composition; a transformation or self-identification in which the deceased becomes Ihy.
- **CT 368 (V, 30b–c):** **[EA]** “Your name is not Excrement; Re is your name, Ihy is your name.”
- **CT 483–484 (VI, 54 and following):** **[EA]** the speaker follows Ihy and declares, “Ihy is in my belly, the pure flesh of his mother.”
- **CT 495 (VI, 76i–j):** **[EA]** one who has fled with Ihy reaches the horizon as a great falcon.
- **CT 544 (VI, 139d):** **[EA]** reference to the “mansion of Ihy.”
- **CT 563 (VI, 162s–v):** **[EA]** “I shall see the road with my two eyes like Ihy, son of Hathor, whom she loves.”
- **CT 588 (VI, 209j–m):** **[EA]** the deceased becomes a follower of Hathor with the liturgical cry “Ihy, Ihy”; this underlies Book of the Dead 103.
- **CT 698 (VI, 332e–f):** **[EA]** “Ihy has driven you off,” addressing a hostile netherworld being.
- **CT 746:** **[EA]** Seshat and Ihy are juxtaposed in the speaker’s bodily powers: “My hands are those of Seshat, who is in my mouth as Ihy.” Interpretation as writing plus performed sound is plausible but scholarly.
- **CT 939 (VII, 151a):** **[EA/uncertain]** mentions “the son of Ihy”; the passage is obscure and provides no usable genealogy.

#### Coffin Texts spell 334

**[EA]** This spell is the richest pre-Dendera account. Its speaker identifies with Ihy and combines several parentages and divine conditions rather than presenting a clean narrative birth:

- Hathor is repeatedly “my mother.”
- Isis also functions as mother and says she has generated or ejaculated him.
- Nephthys occurs as mother in one passage.
- Re is the companion and likely solar father; the speaker grows “high like my father.”
- Ihy is a child in Nun, a newborn solar-renewal being.
- Heaven trembles before his radiance.
- He can be in heaven and on earth without perishing.
- His decay becomes Hathor’s myrrh, incense, and sacred oil.
- He fraternizes or unites humans and gods.
- He receives acclaim as Ihy and as Khonsu.

Representative passages include:

> “I will not perish in the sky with Re and my mother Hathor; I will not perish on earth with Re and my mother Hathor … for I am Ihy, son of Hathor.”

> “I am the baby, son of Hathor. I am the inert one in Nun.”

> “My putrefaction is myrrh which my mother Hathor places upon her head; my odor is incense which my mother Hathor places in her censer; my efflux is sacred oil which my mother Hathor puts upon her flesh.”

The last passage is not a sanitized hymn to innocent childhood: the god’s child-renewal is produced through mortuary decomposition transformed into divine fragrance.

Spell 334 does not give the later Dendera story of a conventional child born to Hathor and Horus in a mammisi. It supplies older ingredients—Hathoric sonship, solar generation, rebirth, protection, sound, fragrance, and multiple maternal identities—which Dendera theology later reorganized.

### Middle-Kingdom monumental evidence

- **Tomb of Senet, mother of Antefoker, Thebes, Twelfth Dynasty:** **[EA]** an Ihy-like divine sistrum player appears in the Hathoric ritual program.
- **Serabit el-Khadim, Sinai, reign of Amenemhat IV, approximately 1815–1806:** **[EA]** a relief published by Gardiner, Peet, and Černý shows Ihy beside Hathor, extending his hand to an official.
- **Stela of Wahankh Intef II, MMA 13.182.3, approximately 2112–2063:** **[EA/disputed]** its hymn or ritual song to Hathor has been connected with Ihy’s function, although whether a particular word names the god or means music/rejoicing requires philological caution.

### New-Kingdom sources

- **Deir el-Bahari, temple of Hatshepsut, Eighteenth Dynasty:** **[LC]** Ihy accompanies Hatshepsut before Hathor’s sacred bark, wearing the sidelock and *menat* and shaking a sistrum. This is the earliest secure monumental presentation of his mature child-musician iconography.
- **Karnak, reign of Thutmose III:** **[LC]** Ihy son of Hathor presents a sistrum to the king with the words, “It is for you, young god, lord of the Two Lands, so that you may rejoice” (*Urkunden IV*, 580.5–6).
- **KV62, tomb of Tutankhamun, late Eighteenth Dynasty:** **[LC]** two black-resin-coated wooden statues, JE 60731–60732, show the king in Ihy’s role holding a gilded sistrum. Black signifies fertile regeneration as well as the transformed state of the deceased. The [Global Egyptian Museum record](https://www.globalegyptianmuseum.org/detail.aspx?id=14824) identifies the subject as Ihy, son of Hathor.
- **Tomb of Seti II, KV15, Nineteenth Dynasty:** **[LC]** unfinished figures of sistrum-bearing Ihy belong to the royal funerary program.
- **Book of the Dead 103:** **[LC]** adapts CT 588: the deceased seeks to be a follower of Hathor and cries “Ihy, Ihy.”
- **Book of the Dead 149:** **[LC]** invokes “Ihy, lord of hearts” to reconstruct the bones and establish the crown.

### Dendera corpus

**[RV/LC]** The overwhelming later corpus is monumental, not a single narrative scripture. It is distributed through the Hathor temple, subsidiary rooms, exterior walls, roof chapels, the ruined earlier mammisi, and the Roman mammisi. Émile Chassinat, François Daumas, and Sylvie Cauville published the inscriptions in the *Dendara* series and specialist studies.

The present great temple was laid out under Ptolemy XII on 16 July 54 BCE; the sanctuary was completed and entered under Augustus. A dedicatory record states that work lasted thirty-four years. An Augustan multilingual land dedication of 12 BCE names a “prophet of Horus, prophet of Hathor, prophet of Ihy, prophet of Isis,” direct evidence that Ihy had an institutional priesthood within the Dendera establishment. [The translated inscriptions](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/dendera.html) preserve these details.

Ihy’s Dendera appearances include:

- birth, nursing, presentation, purification, coronation, and enthronement;
- receiving a sistrum from Hathor and thereby assuming his characteristic office;
- playing sistrum and *menat* for Hathor;
- wearing the Double Crown as divine and royal heir;
- appearing with Hathor and Horus of Edfu as the local family;
- appearing separately from Harsomtus in enumerations of Dendera’s gods;
- embodying the newborn sun and renewed kingship;
- being celebrated in a festival of “taking power” as “the great god.”

The University of Memphis’s documented exterior-wall relief shows Hathor, Horus, and Ihy together; Ihy is naked, side-locked, double-crowned, and holds sistrum and *menat*. [The institutional description and image](https://www.memphis.edu/egypt/resources/colortour/dendara4.php) are especially useful for identification.

### Sources named in the brief but not actually part of Ihy’s secure corpus

- **Pyramid Texts:** **[Ø]** no explicit Ihy.
- **Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** **[Ø]** no securely identified Ihy episode.
- **Contendings of Horus and Seth:** **[Ø]** Ihy does not participate. Hathor’s exposure of her genitals to make the offended solar judge laugh helps explain Hathoric joy and pacification, but it is not an Ihy myth.
- **Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*:** **[Ø]** no secure independent Ihy account.
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** **[Ø]** no securely isolated Ihy cult-name or episode. Egyptian child gods and Horus/Harpocrates appear, but retroactively renaming them Ihy is unwarranted.
- **Turin King List:** **[Ø]** no cultic or mythic Ihy entry.
- **Edfu:** **[RV]** crucial indirectly through Horus of Edfu, the Sacred Marriage visit to Dendera, and the parallel Harsomtus theology; no evidence makes Edfu Ihy’s independent principal sanctuary.
- **Philae:** **[RV/SI]** child-god and Hathor-Isis theology offers parallels, but Ihy must not automatically be substituted for Horus-the-Child.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and characteristic powers

**[EA–LC]**

1. **Hathoric music and jubilation.** Ihy performs music rather than merely patronizing human musicians. His sounding of the sistrum activates joy and pacifies divine wrath.
2. **Divine mediation.** As Hathor’s acolyte he moves between goddess, king, dead, and divine assembly.
3. **Protection and guidance.** CT 146, 334, 495, 563, and 698 make this explicit. He sees the road, traverses sky and earth, repels hostile beings, and protects the deceased.
4. **Rebirth and regeneration.** The child form signifies recurrent creation, solar dawn, royal succession, and mortuary renewal.
5. **Fragrance and transformed decay.** CT 334 converts putrefaction into Hathor’s perfumes and oils.
6. **Royal legitimation.** By the Ptolemaic-Roman period his birth and coronation model the perpetual renewal of legitimate kingship.
7. **Fertility:** **[LS/RV]** reasonably inferred from divine birth and Hathoric regeneration, but “fertility god” is less precise than child-renewal god.
8. **Bread, beer, and coffins:** **[uncertain]** popular summaries sometimes call him lord of bread, controller of beer, or god of coffins. These formulations require specific temple or funerary citations and should not be treated as securely established core domains.

### Standard iconography

**[LC/RV]** Ihy is normally:

- a naked boy;
- wearing the side-lock of youth;
- sometimes with a finger at his mouth, the Egyptian sign of childhood rather than secrecy;
- holding or shaking a sistrum;
- wearing or holding the *menat* counterpoise-necklace;
- occasionally wearing the White Crown or Double Crown;
- sometimes enthroned with crook and flail under assimilation to the royal Horus-child;
- sometimes represented through the king performing Ihy’s office.

**[EA]** The Palermo Stone’s standing-statue determinative indicates an anthropomorphic form but does not preserve detailed iconography.

**[EA/disputed]** A bovine or calf form is possible in Kagemni’s mastaba but remains unique.

**[LC]** The Tutankhamun figures are blackened wooden child-musicians. They have been interpreted either as Ihy guiding and protecting the king or as the regenerated king himself in Ihy’s form. The two readings are compatible but not identical.

**[RV]** At Dendera, the child may wear the Double Crown and stand in royal posture. One exterior scene gives him both sistrum and *menat*. Other late representations can adopt a mummiform or lion-headed child configuration, but these are rare, local, and potentially entangled with other child gods; they should not displace the standard naked sistrum-player.

### Instruments and emblems

- **Sistrum:** defining emblem; an apotropaic and pacifying rattle associated above all with Hathor.
- **Menat:** heavy beaded collar and counterpoise, moved rhythmically in Hathoric ritual.
- **Side-lock:** childhood and potential succession.
- **Finger to mouth:** childhood; the later Greek misreading of Harpocrates as the god of silence should not automatically be transferred to Ihy.
- **Double Crown:** local/late royal and unificatory meaning.
- **Crook and flail:** late royal-child imagery.
- **Black color:** on funerary statues, death transformed toward regeneration, not an invariant sacred color of Ihy.

### Animals, plants, planets, numbers, and days

- **Calf:** **[EA/disputed]** possible pun or exceptional manifestation.
- **Jackal:** **[EA/metaphorical]** CT 334’s “Jackal of the sunshine”; not evidence for a jackal cult.
- **Lotus:** **[MI unless sourced]** modern pages sometimes show Ihy emerging from a lotus, apparently importing Nefertem/Harpocrates imagery. No early defining Ihy source establishes the lotus as his standard plant.
- **Celestial body:** **[EA/LS]** solar character is secure; no independent star, planet, or constellation is demonstrably his.
- **Sacred number, weekday, gemstone, color correspondence:** **[Ø/MI]** no ancient system securely assigns these. Modern occult lists doing so are inventions unless they cite an Egyptian text.
- **Dedicated festival days:** Dendera’s ritual calendars contain days connected with his birth and accession, but conversion into a universally valid modern birthday is unsafe because Egyptian local calendars and regnal dating varied.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Competing genealogies

| Version | Parents or origin | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hathoric funerary | Hathor is repeatedly his mother; father not consistently named | CT 146, 334, 563; Middle Kingdom | **[EA]** |
| Solar-child | Re or an unnamed solar father; Ihy rises from Nun and grows high like his father | CT 334, 368 | **[EA]** |
| Isiac | Isis generates him; Hathor and Isis overlap | CT 334 | **[EA/SI]** |
| Nephthyan | Nephthys is named as mother | CT 334 IV 180b | **[EA/SI]** |
| Horus creates/fathers Ihy | Horus son of Osiris “creates” or “acts the role of” Ihy | CT 51; verb disputed | **[EA/disputed]** |
| Dendera family | Hathor and Horus of Edfu are parents; Ihy is their child | Ptolemaic-Roman Dendera reliefs and inscriptions | **[RV/LC]** |
| Primeval child | self-manifesting child in Nun, linked to Heh and cosmic first creation | CT 334 | **[EA/LS]** |
| “Son of Ihy” | unnamed descendant | CT 939 | **[EA/uncertain]** |

The Hathor–Horus–Ihy triad became dominant in modern summaries because Dendera preserves exceptionally rich architecture and relief. Historically, however, it is a late local consolidation. Middle-Kingdom sources emphasize mother and child; a stable father completing the triad is absent or fluid.

### Divine hierarchy

**[EA]** Ihy was recognized by the royal court in the Fifth Dynasty but was not prominent in the Pyramid Texts.  
**[EA]** The Coffin Texts gave him broad regenerative and protective capacity without making him chief of a national pantheon.  
**[LC]** New-Kingdom kings used his role ritually and funerarily.  
**[RV]** At Ptolemaic-Roman Dendera he became the divine heir and occasionally “great god,” completing the local family.  
**[Ø]** There is no evidence that he displaced Re, Amun, Ptah, Osiris, or Horus as a national supreme god.

- **Hathor — his decay becomes the myrrh, incense, and oil she uses.** CT 334. **[EA]**
- **Hathor — he is her beloved son whose sight guides the deceased.** CT 563. **[EA]**
- **Hathor — Ihy is the deceased’s protection “for life.”** CT 146. **[EA]**
- **Hathor — he appears beside her and reaches toward an official.** Serabit el-Khadim relief, Amenemhat IV. **[EA]**
- **Hathor — he accompanies Hatshepsut before her bark, shaking the sistrum.** Deir el-Bahari, Eighteenth Dynasty. **[LC]**
- **Hathor — she gives Ihy the sistrum, enabling him to assume his musical office and authority.** Dendera temple texts, Ptolemaic-Roman period. **[RV]**

- **Horus son of Osiris — Horus “creates” or “acts the part of” Ihy through Hu.** CT 51; translation disputed. **[EA/disputed]**

- **Re — Ihy accompanies Re in heaven and earth and shares solar identity.** CT 334 and 368. **[EA]**
- **Isis — she functions as his mother and generates him.** CT 334. **[EA/SI]**
- **Nephthys — she is named as mother.** CT 334 IV 180b. **[EA/SI]**
- **Khonsu — the speaker is acclaimed in the names of Ihy and Khonsu.** CT 334 IV 181i–j. **[EA/SI]**
- **Khonsu — Ihy, Khonsu, and Nefertem appear sequentially as models for seeing, breathing, and walking.** CT 563. **[EA; joint liturgical appearance]**
- **Nefertem — joint appearance with Ihy and Khonsu as youthful regenerative models.** CT 563. **[EA]**
- **Osiris — the deceased brings Osiris life-water held by Ihy.** CT 36. **[EA; mediated interaction]**
- **Osiris — the deceased as Osiris takes possession of Ihy.** CT 326, one textual witness. **[EA/variant]**
- **Sia — the deceased secures Ihy and places Sia/Perception at his feet.** CT 326. **[EA/variant]**
- **Seshat — her hands and Ihy’s presence in the mouth are combined in the speaker.** CT 746. **[EA]**
- **Hu — Ihy is connected with authoritative utterance in CT 51.** **[EA/disputed]**
- **Nun/Nu — Ihy exists as newborn or inert child in the primeval water.** CT 334. **[EA/cosmological]**
- **Heh/Huh — Ihy bears or invokes a name related to Heh while seeking his place.** CT 334. **[EA/wordplay]**
- **Khnum — Khnum fashions the divine child for Hathor in the Dendera birth cycle.** Roman mammisi. **[RV/LC]**
- **Heqet — she accompanies Khnum and Hathor in the divine-birth sequence.** Roman mammisi. **[RV/LC]**
- **Harsomtus — the two occur as parallel or distinct child gods within the Dendera–Edfu system.** Ptolemaic-Roman temple lists and processions. **[RV/SI]**
- **Harpocrates/Horus-the-Child — later iconographic and theological convergence.** Late and Greco-Roman child-god religion. **[SI]**
- **Bes — appears in the mammisi’s protective birth environment.** Roman Dendera mammisi. **[RV; joint architectural program, not a surviving narrative encounter]**

**[Ø]** No securely attested episode has Ihy fighting Seth, accompanying Isis through the marshes, judging in the *Contendings*, defeating Apophis, meeting Greek Dionysus or Apollo, or appearing in Plutarch’s Osiris narrative.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The transformation into Ihy

**Earliest source:** CT 334, Middle Kingdom. **[EA]**

This is Ihy’s closest surviving equivalent to a major myth, although it is a mortuary transformation spell rather than a third-person story. The deceased speaks as Ihy, claims multiple mothers, passes between earth and sky, becomes a solar child in Nun, terrifies the cosmos with radiance, takes divine names, protects beings from gods, and turns decomposition into Hathor’s fragrant cult substances.

Its purpose is performative: the dead person does not merely learn facts about Ihy but assumes his identity and capacities. Childhood supplies inexhaustible potential; solarity supplies cyclical rebirth; musical and fragrant relations with Hathor supply admission to divine society.

**Variants and development:** Other Coffin Texts extract individual functions—protection in CT 146, possession in CT 326, naming in CT 368, guidance in CT 495 and 563, repelling danger in CT 698. Later Book of the Dead spells retain Ihy as guide and restorer but do not reproduce CT 334’s entire theology.

### B. Ihy as Hathor’s musician

**Earliest nucleus:** Palermo Stone procession, Fifth Dynasty. **[EA]**  
**Developed form:** New-Kingdom temple images and Ptolemaic-Roman Dendera. **[LC/RV]**

The core action is Ihy’s attendance upon Hathor through music. The sistrum’s rattling pacifies a potentially dangerous solar goddess and generates joy. In royal performance, the king assumes Ihy’s role toward the goddess; in funerary use, the musician becomes guide and protector.

At Dendera, Hathor formally hands Ihy the instrument. This turns a longstanding functional association into a local accession narrative: by receiving and sounding the sistrum, the child becomes *Ihy*, the sistrum-player, and can be hailed as a great god.

This is not a story in which Ihy pacifies Hathor during the Destruction of Mankind; no surviving early version inserts him into that narrative. Linking his function to the broader Egyptian pattern of soothing dangerous goddesses is contextual interpretation.

### C. The divine birth at Dendera

**Sources:** Roman mammisi reliefs and inscriptions, with precedents in the Nectanebo/Ptolemaic birth house. **[RV/LC]**

The fully developed local cycle includes:

1. divine intention and conception;
2. Khnum’s fashioning of the child;
3. Heqet’s life-giving and childbirth role;
4. Hathor’s labor and delivery;
5. divine nursing and presentation;
6. recognition of Ihy as solar child and heir;
7. bestowal of crowns and royal authority;
8. reception of the sistrum;
9. public manifestation and festival procession.

The cycle is both theological and political. Every divine birth renews the cosmos, while every imperial donor shown participating in it presents Roman rule in Egyptian royal form.

The Dendera cycle should not be back-projected intact to the Old Kingdom. Its ancient ingredients are real—Hathor, child-renewal, divine fashioning, royal birth, solar rebirth—but their architectural arrangement is a Late and Roman temple synthesis.

### D. Primeval and solar birth

**Source:** CT 334. **[EA]**

Ihy exists as a baby in Nun and as a manifestation of the new sun. He grows to his father’s height and produces cosmic trembling. This is not a linear creation myth like the Heliopolitan genealogy; it is identification theology in which a divine child embodies the first dawn repeatedly.

At Dendera the same logic becomes architectural: the mammisi is the secluded place in which the sun, god, and legitimate ruler are born anew.

### E. Guidance through danger

**Sources:** CT 146, 334, 495, 563, 698; New-Kingdom funerary images. **[EA–LC]**

Ihy sees the path, guides flight to the horizon, protects the deceased, and drives away hostile underworld beings. Altenmüller interpreted Kagemni’s cattle-crossing appeal through this guiding function: just as a herder conducts cattle through crocodile-haunted water, Ihy conducts the dead through dangerous regions. The analogy is attractive but not stated in a surviving myth.

### F. Alleged Golden Calf connection

**[Modern scholarly speculation, not attested tradition]** Some modern writers have suggested that the biblical golden calf in Exodus 32 might reflect Ihy as Hathor’s calf or young bull. The proposal depends on the disputed “calf” etymology and generalized Egyptian bovine religion. Exodus does not name Ihy; no Egyptian text connects him with Israelites or Sinai’s golden calf. Broad calf symbolism cannot establish direct borrowing. This remains speculative and should not be reported as an ancient Ihy myth.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Dendera

Dendera, ancient *Iunet* and Greek Tentyra, was Ihy’s principal documented late cult center. The monumental precinct visible today is predominantly Ptolemaic and Roman, although inscriptions and reused blocks preserve claims and remains of earlier construction.

#### Main Hathor temple

**[RV/archaeologically attested]**

- Foundation ceremony under Ptolemy XII: 16 July 54 BCE.
- Sanctuary entered under Augustus.
- Construction and decoration continued under Roman emperors.
- Ihy appears in the exterior family relief, interior ritual scenes, divine lists, and calendars.
- The cult establishment included a **prophet of Ihy**, directly attested in a 12 BCE dedication.
- There is no reason to imagine a wholly independent Ihy priesthood divorced from Hathor’s estate; the title occurs within a man’s accumulation of Hathoric, Horus, Isis, and administrative offices.

The economy was that of a large temple estate holding land, directing offerings, employing priests and artisans, and negotiating with royal and Roman authority. The same 12 BCE text that names Ihy’s prophet concerns the dedication of land near the temple avenue, connecting cult personnel to property administration.

#### The mammisis

Dendera contains two birth houses:

1. **Earlier mammisi:** begun under Nectanebo I, 379/378–361/360 BCE, enlarged and decorated under Ptolemaic kings. Its building history was disrupted by the construction of the great temple’s court.
2. **Roman mammisi:** begun under Augustus; decoration and associated inscriptions continued under Trajan, Hadrian, and Antonine emperors. Quarry marks from Gebel el-Silsila plausibly identify stone extraction for sections of this structure, although that reconstruction is explicitly preliminary. [The archaeological quarry study](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/032346b6-78e5-4ab2-ab4d-774f133bd31f) documents the evidence.

A mammisi is not simply a public maternity clinic. It is a temple building for the periodic birth, appearance, and enthronement of the divine child. Its reliefs connect divine maternity, royal succession, cosmic rebirth, and festival performance.

### Festivals

**[RV]** Dendera celebrated the divine child’s birth and emergence. A festival studied as the “taking of power of Ihy the great god” involved Hathor proceeding to the mammisi, the child’s manifestation, his reception of the sistrum, and public recognition. The surviving texts indicate a liturgical sequence, but reconstruction of a literal dramatic performance in every detail is uncertain.

**[RV]** The annual meeting of Hathor of Dendera and Horus of Edfu—the “Beautiful Reunion”—created the divine setting in which a child of their union could be celebrated. At Edfu, Harsomtus more commonly occupies this role; at Dendera, Ihy may do so. The relationship is complementary and locally variable.

### Offerings and rites

Documented or securely contextual practices include:

- sistrum and *menat* performance;
- hymns and acclamations;
- processions of divine images;
- Opening-of-the-Mouth animation of a cult statue;
- incense, myrrh, oils, food, beer, clothing, and other ordinary temple offerings;
- presentation of crowns and royal insignia;
- carrying the child’s image or revealing it at festival;
- funerary invocation for guidance and skeletal reconstitution.

### Votive and personal evidence

Old-Kingdom theophoric names containing Ihy indicate personal use of the god’s name. Monumental and funerary images show elites and kings approaching Hathor through Ihy’s musical role. The available online museum evidence is much stronger for royal and institutional objects than for a mass corpus of inexpensive private Ihy votives.

### Human sacrifice, sacred prostitution, and cult violence

- **Human sacrifice:** **[Ø]** no Ihy cult text, temple deposit, or archaeological report documents it.
- **Temple prostitution:** **[Ø]** no evidence associates prostitution with Ihy’s priesthood or mammisi. Broad claims that Egyptian music, sexuality, or Hathor worship automatically entailed “sacred prostitution” are obsolete generalization.
- **Cult violence:** **[Ø]** Ihy can repel underworld enemies, but there is no documented historical violence conducted by an Ihy cult.
- **Sexual theology:** **[EA]** CT 334 contains explicit generative language, and Hathoric mythology elsewhere uses erotic display to restore divine equilibrium. That is not evidence of institutional prostitution.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Fifth and Sixth Dynasties, approximately 2490–2181 BCE

**[EA]** Ihy is securely recognized in royal ritual under Neferirkare. His electrum statue accompanies Hathor, suggesting that the essential god–goddess relationship already existed. Theophoric names and the Kagemni scene confirm wider knowledge. No Pyramid Text spells or independent temple cult can yet be demonstrated.

### First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, approximately 2181–1650 BCE

**[EA]** This is the decisive theological phase. Coffin Texts convert Ihy from a sparsely attested Hathoric attendant into a powerful mortuary identity: divine child, protector, guide, solar rebirth, fragrance, and bridge between humans and gods. Parentage remains plural and fluid. Serabit el-Khadim supplies monumental imagery beside Hathor.

### New Kingdom, approximately 1550–1069 BCE

**[LC]** His mature iconography becomes visible: naked or youthful, side-locked, carrying sistrum and *menat*. Hatshepsut’s Deir el-Bahari and Thutmose III’s Karnak place him in royal temple ritual. Tutankhamun’s and Seti II’s funerary images make him a guide and manifestation of royal rebirth. Book of the Dead spells preserve his Hathoric and restorative roles.

He nevertheless remains subsidiary. He has no known national temple network comparable with Amun, Osiris, or Horus.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods, approximately 1069–332 BCE

**[LC/SI]** Egyptian child-god theology expands considerably. Ihy’s image increasingly shares features with Horus-the-Child, Khonsu-the-Child, Nefertem, and other local divine heirs. Dendera’s earlier mammisi, begun under Nectanebo I, materializes divine birth as an architectural cult.

### Ptolemaic period, 332–30 BCE

**[RV]** Dendera systematizes Ihy into the child of Hathor and Horus of Edfu. The great temple’s foundation under Ptolemy XII and extensive inscriptions give him far more monumental visibility than earlier evidence does. His birth, music, solar renewal, and kingship become one coordinated local theology. Harsomtus remains a parallel child and is not simply erased.

### Roman period, 30 BCE–fourth century CE

**[RV/LC]** Roman emperors construct and decorate the mammisi while representing themselves as Egyptian pharaohs. Ihy’s birth legitimates order under a new regime. A 12 BCE administrative inscription attests his prophet. Trajanic and later scenes continue the Egyptian-language cult even when the rulers themselves were absent from Egypt.

### Christianization and temple closure

**[LC/historical inference]** There is no surviving edict aimed specifically at Ihy. His institutional cult disappeared as Egyptian temples lost state support and traditional sacrifice was prohibited or abandoned during Late Antiquity. Dendera contains Christian occupation and crosses, and portions of the complex were reused. The process should not be narrated as a documented single “destruction of Ihy’s temple”: the evidence indicates economic decline, legal suppression of sacrifice, Christian reuse, and eventual discontinuity of priestly transmission.

### Islamic period to the nineteenth century

**[Ø/F]** No evidence demonstrates an unbroken folk cult of Ihy under Islam. Dendera’s monuments remained locally known, but knowledge of Ihy’s name and theology depended on undeciphered inscriptions. Later local stories about temple ruins cannot be assumed to preserve his ancient worship.

### Rediscovery and modern scholarship

European travelers recorded Dendera before hieroglyphic decipherment, but systematic identification of Ihy followed nineteenth-century philology and publication. Chassinat, Daumas, Cauville, and other Egyptologists progressively made Dendera’s enormous inscriptional corpus usable. Tutankhamun’s discovery in 1922 added two celebrated royal-Ihy statuettes.

### Present

**[MR]** Ihy may be honored by individual Kemetic reconstructionists and eclectic pagans. Kemetic Orthodoxy itself was founded in the late 1980s and openly describes itself as a modern religious practice based on ancient Egyptian religion. [Its institutional account](https://www.udjat.org/about) is evidence for modern revival, not ancient continuity.

No reliable census isolates Ihy’s worshippers, and there is no evidence of a large independent Ihy denomination. Modern devotion is revival, reconstruction, or personal religious innovation—not an uninterrupted hereditary cult from Dendera.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Egyptology and museum culture

Ihy’s modern visibility comes primarily from:

- illustrations of the Dendera triad;
- the Dendera mammisi;
- Tutankhamun’s two sistrum-bearing statuettes;
- compact encyclopedia descriptions derived from Wilkinson and earlier handbooks.

Because these images are visually accessible, modern summaries often project the late Dendera family backward across all Egyptian history.

### Occult and esoteric reception

**[Ø/MA]** Ihy is conspicuously marginal in Renaissance Hermetism, nineteenth-century ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, and standard modern grimoires. Isis, Thoth, Horus, Osiris, and Harpocrates dominate those systems. No major early-modern occult text appears to give Ihy a distinctive seal, planetary office, invocation, or grade attribution.

Accordingly:

- planetary correspondences assigned to Ihy are **[MI]** unless explicitly presented as modern creations;
- claims that a Golden Dawn temple secretly initiated people into Ihy’s mysteries are **[MI/Ø]**;
- a “traditional spell of Ihy” circulating without a papyrus, coffin, or temple citation is not ancient evidence.

### Neopagan and Kemetic reception

**[MR]** Modern practitioners may invoke Ihy for music, joy, childhood, creativity, protection, or relationship with Hathor. These choices draw selectively on genuine ancient traits but often reorganize them into modern devotional categories.

A widely circulated English poem beginning “I am the perfume of my Mother” is sometimes presented as if it were an ancient hymn spoken by Ihy. It is actually a modern composite or adaptation assembled from CT 334 and Dendera motifs. Its individual images have ancient precedents; the English poem as a unified composition does not. Labeling it “translated from an ancient hymn” is **[MI]**.

Modern offerings of playlists, candy, toys, colored candles, crystals, or assigned weekdays may be meaningful contemporary practice but are not documented ancient ritual unless supported independently.

### Popular culture and commerce

Ihy remains minor in global film, comics, and games compared with Anubis, Isis, Ra, or Seth. His principal commercial presence is in deity dictionaries, replica Egyptian art, prints, tourism material, and online spiritual content. No major modern fictional franchise has demonstrably reshaped his image on the scale seen with Anubis or Bastet.

### Political or extremist appropriation

**[Ø]** No significant nationalist, racialist, or extremist movement centered on Ihy was found. Egyptian religious imagery can be appropriated broadly, but attributing such appropriation specifically to Ihy without documentation would manufacture a history he does not have.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major scholarly foundations

- **Adriaan de Buck, *The Egyptian Coffin Texts*:** hieroglyphic base edition.
- **Raymond O. Faulkner, *The Ancient Egyptian Coffin Texts*:** standard English translation, still useful but not final.
- **Émile Chassinat, François Daumas, Sylvie Cauville, *Dendara* publications:** indispensable temple text and relief documentation.
- **Hermann Altenmüller:** major studies of Ihy as musician, guide, and protector.
- **Sami Allam, *Beiträge zum Hathorkult*:** Ihy’s Hathoric setting and textual evidence.
- **Hellmut Brunner, “Kindergötter,” *Lexikon der Ägyptologie*:** influential typology of divine children.
- **Richard H. Wilkinson, *The Complete Gods and Goddesses of Ancient Egypt*:** concise synthesis; useful entry point, not a substitute for the primary corpus.
- **Geraldine Pinch, *Handbook of Egyptian Mythology*:** broader mythological synthesis.
- **Erik Hornung, *Conceptions of God in Ancient Egypt*:** crucial for understanding why overlapping identities do not require exclusive equations.
- **Jan Assmann:** ritual, solar theology, cultural memory, and temple religion.
- **Toby Wilkinson, *Royal Annals of Ancient Egypt*:** Palermo Stone.
- **Heidi Roberts, *Joy of the Divine Child*:** child-god interpretation, including some more speculative comparative suggestions.
- **Daehyun Kim’s study of Egyptian child deities:** detailed collation of Ihy’s Coffin Text passages, iconography, and historical development, accessible in searchable form [here](https://www.scribd.com/document/374528898/etd1161-20110721165628-1).

### Central disputes

#### Was Ihy originally a calf?

The “calf” reading explains his relation to cow-Hathor and the Kagemni scene. The “musician/rejoicer” reading explains his name’s ritual interpretation and dominant iconography. The evidence allows ancient punning and convergence; it does not require a single original essence.

#### Was Hathor his “genuine” mother?

This question is misleading if “genuine” means a stable biological genealogy. Coffin Texts emphatically call her his mother, but also make Isis and Nephthys maternal and solarize his father. Some scholars interpret “son of Hathor” functionally: he is her acolyte and instrument-player before he becomes a member of a formal family triad. Dendera later literalizes and monumentalizes the kinship.

#### When did the Dendera triad form?

The Fifth-Dynasty Palermo evidence joins Ihy and Hathor but not Horus. The Middle-Kingdom texts preserve mother-and-child theology. A fully articulated Hathor–Horus of Edfu–Ihy triad is clearest in the Ptolemaic-Roman temple. Claims that the triad was already complete in the Fourth Dynasty exceed the evidence.

#### Is Ihy Harpocrates?

Late iconography and mother-child theology overlap, but Ihy has an earlier independent record. Harpocrates should be treated as a partial syncretistic convergence, not the explanation of Ihy’s origin.

#### Did music itself magically guide the dead?

Texts explicitly make Ihy both musician and guide. The proposition that the soothing power of music is specifically what enables underworld guidance is a plausible modern interpretation; no surviving passage states the causal theory so directly.

#### Does “Ihy” in every text name the god?

Egyptian spellings can overlap with words for rejoicing, cries, personal names, and unrelated persons named Ihy. Each occurrence requires determinatives and context. Search-engine results frequently confuse the god with officials or tomb owners called Ihy.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is unusually uneven:

- one terse Old-Kingdom annal;
- scattered names and images;
- a substantial but internally fluid Middle-Kingdom funerary corpus;
- New-Kingdom ritual and funerary iconography;
- an enormous late local Dendera corpus.

This distribution creates a danger of letting the best-preserved late evidence define every earlier period.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- Ihy was universally Egypt’s “god of joy, pleasure, lust, beer, bread, and childhood”: **over-composite**.
- The Dendera triad existed unchanged from the Fourth Dynasty: **unsupported**.
- Ihy appears in the Pyramid Texts: **not demonstrated**.
- He plays a role in the *Contendings of Horus and Seth*: **not demonstrated**.
- Plutarch describes him: **not demonstrated**.
- He was the biblical golden calf: **speculative modern comparison**.
- He had a continuous secret cult through medieval Egypt: **no evidence**.
- Modern English devotional poems are literal ancient translations: **false unless accompanied by a specific ancient text and edition**.
- Lotus, crystals, zodiac signs, chakras, weekdays, or tarot cards were ancient Ihy correspondences: **modern invention**.

### Conclusions of the contested-material pass

The explicit searches for etymology, origin, disputed genealogy, syncretism, sacrifice, prostitution, suppression, occultism, and neopaganism produced the following defensible results:

- etymology remains divided between music/rejoicing and calf interpretations;
- Ihy’s earliest evidence is Old Kingdom, but his child-protector theology is first extensively visible in Middle-Kingdom Coffin Texts;
- the Dendera nuclear family is a later local systematization;
- equivalence with Harpocrates, Harsomtus, or Khonsu is partial and contextual;
- no human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, or Ihy-specific persecution is documented;
- no substantial medieval, Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, or grimoire tradition devoted to Ihy is documented;
- modern Kemetic worship exists as revival and reconstruction, not demonstrated continuous transmission;
- popular correspondences and composite “ancient hymns” frequently blend authentic motifs with modern composition.
