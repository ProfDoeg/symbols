# Montu: Research Dossier

**Class:** god (*nṯr*) in ancient Egyptian tradition; principally a Theban-region solar, royal, martial, and bull-associated male deity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Montu is named in the late Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts, certainly by the Sixth Dynasty. Pyramid Text §1378b, in Utterance 555, says that the deceased king “ascends to heaven like Montu.” The surviving inscriptional witnesses belong to royal pyramids of the later third millennium BCE; this establishes Montu’s name and celestial potency, but not yet a documented Theban war cult or genealogy. Claims that his cult is archaeologically demonstrable in predynastic Egypt, or that a specific “primitive temple” at Medamud proves an Old Kingdom cult there, remain unestablished.

## Evidence labels used

- **Securely attested early:** primary evidence from the Old or Middle Kingdom.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** recurrent New Kingdom or later Egyptian formulation.
- **Regional variant:** local theology of Armant, Medamud, Tod, Karnak, or another center.
- **Learned systematization:** chiefly priestly combinations and genealogies, especially in Ptolemaic and Roman temple texts.
- **Syncretistic identification:** explicit fusion or equation with another deity.
- **Late accretion:** a development first securely documented in the Late, Ptolemaic, or Roman periods.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from dispersed evidence, not an ancient statement.
- **Modern invention or reception:** a modern religious, occult, literary, or commercial development.

Dates are approximate unless an inscription supplies a regnal year.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

Montu was not originally one immutable “god of war.” The oldest text presents him as a divine model for the king’s ascent. Middle Kingdom evidence places him at the center of Theban royal theology, especially under the Eleventh-Dynasty Mentuhotep rulers. New Kingdom royal texts increasingly make “Montu” a metaphor or divine identity for the charging, conquering pharaoh. Later temple theology integrates solar, falcon, bull, creator, and family roles, while the Buchis cult gives Montu a succession of living bovine manifestations.

The common description “the falcon war-god of Thebes before Amun” is broadly useful but requires qualification:

- **Securely attested early:** Montu appears in the Pyramid Texts before Thebes became Egypt’s political capital.
- **Securely attested Middle Kingdom:** Theban kings made Montu a major royal patron; the name *Mentuhotep* is direct evidence.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** Montu was probably the most politically prominent divine patron of the early Theban state.
- **Not established:** that Montu was the single, exclusive “chief god of Thebes” in every context before Amun.
- **Historical development:** Amun’s royal and imperial prominence increased sharply from the Twelfth Dynasty and especially after the beginning of the New Kingdom. Montu was not erased; he remained “lord of Thebes,” received temples and priesthoods, and continued as a model of royal aggression.

### Egyptian name

The conventional Egyptological form is **Montu**, with older publications frequently using **Mentu, Menthu, Monthu, Mont, Month**, or French **Montou**. The underlying consonantal writing is normally transliterated **Mnṯw**; Egyptian vowels were not normally written, so none of the modern vocalizations reproduces a securely known pharaonic pronunciation.

Greek and Roman-period forms include **Μονθ / Μονθου** and related spellings. The Greek name of Armant, **Hermonthis**, incorporates the deity’s name: roughly “city of Montu.” Coptic and Arabic place-name developments ultimately produced modern **Armant/Ermant**.

### Etymology

The popular gloss “nomad” or “the nomad” is frequently repeated but is not securely established. It rests on connecting *Mnṯw* with a similarly written Egyptian lexical root. The phonetic and semantic history is insufficient to make this certain.

- **Secure fact:** the divine name is written *Mnṯw*.
- **Disputed scholarly proposal:** derivation from a verb or noun connected with roaming, moving, or nomadism.
- **Modern invention when overstated:** “Montu literally means Nomad” presented as settled fact.

### Principal forms and titles

The following are representative rather than an exhaustive concordance of the *Lexikon der ägyptischen Götter und Götterbezeichnungen*:

- **Montu, lord of Thebes** — *Mnṯw nb Wꜣst*. Attested in monumental and priestly texts over many periods.
- **Montu-Re** — *Mnṯw-Rꜥ*. A solar fusion securely prominent by the Middle Kingdom.
- **Montu-Re, lord of Thebes** — a frequent expanded form.
- **Montu, lord of Armant** — local Armant/Hermonthis form.
- **Montu, lord of Tod** — local cult form.
- **Montu, lord of Medamud** — local cult form.
- **“Montu, lord of Thebes, the bull who resides in Medamud”** — *Mnṯw nb Wꜣst, kꜣ ḥry-jb Mꜣdw*, documented in the Medamud temple material. The formula joins his pan-Theban lordship with specifically local bull theology, as discussed in the IFAO’s re-examination of the site.
- **Bull in Medamud / raging bull** — regional and later temple epithets expressing virility and destructive force.
- **Lord of the Theban nome** — administrative-sacral title reflecting the fourth Upper Egyptian nome.
- **Lord of victory / giver of valor / smiter of enemies** — New Kingdom and later royal-temple language.
- **Montu of the Two Lands** — particularly important in Ramesside royal image-cults, including cult statues of Ramesses II assimilated to Montu.
- **The four Montus** — late collective conception of Montu in the four principal Theban-region sanctuaries: Armant, Tod, Medamud, and Thebes/Karnak. This is a geographically structured “palladium” theology, not four originally independent biographies.

### Interpretatio graeca and related equations

- **Montu–Ares:** Greek equation based on martial character. This is a historical act of interpretation, not proof that the Egyptian and Greek gods were originally identical.
- **Montu–Apollo:** less intuitive but attested or inferred in Graeco-Egyptian interpretation through Montu’s solar and falcon aspects. It did not wholly replace the Ares equation.
- **Montu–Re:** an indigenous Egyptian syncretistic fusion, already important in the Middle Kingdom.
- **Montu–Horus:** not a complete universal identification. Falcon iconography, kingship, and later genealogical relations produced overlaps, while individual inscriptions could coordinate or assimilate their functions.
- **Montu–Buchis:** Buchis was not simply another spelling of Montu. The sacred bull could be termed Montu’s *ba*, herald, image, repetition, or living manifestation according to particular late formulations.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Old Kingdom

**Pyramid Texts, Utterance 555, §1378b — securely attested early.** The king is sound and “ascends to heaven like Montu.” The comparison demonstrates that Montu was already understood as a powerful ascending or celestial deity. It does not call him a war-god, identify a temple, name a consort, or associate him with Buchis. The online Mercer translation preserves the passage, though modern study should consult Allen’s edition and the pyramid witnesses themselves.

A second Pyramid Text association sometimes cited is §1081, where published discussions identify a Montu reference. Because numbering and readings differ among editions and pyramid witnesses, claims based on it should be checked against Sethe and Allen rather than detached quotation.

### First Intermediate Period and Eleventh Dynasty

**Theophoric royal names — securely attested early.** The name **Mentuhotep**, conventionally interpreted “Montu is satisfied/content,” was borne by multiple Theban rulers. The series shows Montu’s political importance more securely than speculative claims about his prehistoric origin.

The rulers conventionally numbered Mentuhotep I–IV do not all have the same documentary status. Mentuhotep II, III, and IV are historical kings; “Mentuhotep I” is partly reconstructed from later dynastic and genealogical evidence and may initially have been a Theban ancestor granted royal status posthumously. The Turin king list is relevant to the later ordering of this dynasty but is not a contemporary Eleventh-Dynasty record.

**Tod temple blocks and inscriptions — securely attested early.** Blocks associated with the early Theban dynasty document royal construction and Montu’s cult at Tod. Two blocks naming Intef I were erected or reinscribed under Mentuhotep II, illustrating the difficulty of distinguishing contemporary commemoration from retrospective dynastic construction.

**Mentuhotep II’s Deir el-Bahri complex — securely attested Middle Kingdom.** The mortuary temple served the deceased king and included Montu-Re; Amun-Re entered its later cultic arrangement. The Metropolitan Museum’s archaeological summary dates much surviving material to approximately 2030–2000 BCE and emphasizes the temple’s successive divine associations.

### Middle Kingdom literary and funerary evidence

**Coffin Texts — securely attested Middle Kingdom.** Montu occurs in spells that extend royal divine identifications to nonroyal coffin owners. Individual occurrences require citation by spell and coffin witness; statements that “the Coffin Texts describe the full Montu mythology” are false. They preserve ritual identifications and divine allusions, not a continuous biography.

**The Story of Sinuhe — securely attested Middle Kingdom composition.** In Sinuhe’s combat with the champion of Retjenu, martial success is expressed through royal-divine language in which Montu belongs to the conceptual field of valor. The work is literary and survives through later copies; it is evidence for Middle Kingdom ideology, not a record that Montu historically intervened in Syria-Palestine.

### New Kingdom royal corpus

**Annals and victory inscriptions of Thutmose III — later canonical elaboration.** The king is characterized as Montu in battle or as possessing Montu’s strength. Such phrases are royal ideology: they identify the king’s manifested violence with divine force.

**Gebel Barkal Stela of Thutmose III — later canonical elaboration.** The king is represented as a valiant Montu. The text helps document the export of Theban royal theology into Nubian imperial settings.

**Kadesh inscriptions of Ramesses II, regnal year 5, conventionally 1274/1275 BCE — later canonical elaboration.** The “Poem,” “Bulletin,” and relief captions survive in multiple monumental copies and in hieratic manuscripts, including Papyrus Sallier III. They describe Ramesses as having strength like Montu, arising like Montu, or assuming the adornments of his father Montu. The British Museum identifies Papyrus Sallier III, EA 10181,10, as a Nineteenth-Dynasty hieratic copy of the Kadesh Poem.

This does **not** mean that the Kadesh narrative makes Montu the principal rescuer of Ramesses. In the decisive prayer scene, Ramesses calls upon **Amun**, who answers him. Montu supplies the king’s martial identity and simile; Amun provides the narratively explicit divine response. “Ramesses II became Montu at Kadesh” is therefore accurate as royal rhetoric, not as a statement of permanent theological identity or literal metamorphosis.

The Egyptian claim of overwhelming solitary victory is itself ideologically shaped. Hittite records and the later treaty show that Kadesh did not produce the unambiguous conquest proclaimed in Ramesses’ temple program.

### Book of the Dead and priestly funerary material

The Book of the Dead is not a single authored scripture but a changing collection of spells, developing from the Second Intermediate Period/New Kingdom and copied into the Ptolemaic period. Montu appears less centrally than Re, Osiris, Horus, Thoth, or Anubis. His importance is often indirect through owners who held Montu priesthoods.

The painted funerary stela of **Ankh-ef-en-Khonsu**, usually dated to the Twenty-Fifth or early Twenty-Sixth Dynasty, identifies its owner as a priest of Montu, lord of Thebes. Its spells and image concern the deceased, Re-Harakhty, Nut, and the winged solar disk; it is not a “scripture of Montu.” Its later Thelemic importance is a modern reception history.

### Temple texts

The largest Montu corpus is dispersed through inscriptions and reliefs rather than a single narrative book:

- Armant/Hermonthis temple and Bucheum inscriptions.
- Medamud temple blocks from the Middle Kingdom through the Roman period.
- Tod temple reliefs, dedications, and foundation material.
- North Karnak’s Montu precinct.
- Royal scenes elsewhere at Karnak, Luxor, Medinet Habu, Edfu, Dendera, and Philae.
- Buchis birth, installation, death, and burial stelae.
- Priestly statues and funerary monuments.

At Medamud, the IFAO records Middle- and New-Kingdom buildings modified by Ptolemaic construction and Roman embellishment. Excavation began in 1924–25 and continues through modern reanalysis.

### Sources named in the brief that do not supply a major Montu myth

Absence is significant:

- **Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** no major narrative role for Montu. Its principal theology concerns Ptah, the Ennead, Horus, Seth, and the ordering of Egypt.
- **Contendings of Horus and Seth:** Montu is not a principal contestant or narrative actor in the surviving Ramesside papyrus.
- **Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*:** preserves Greek interpretations of Egyptian religion but no substantial independent Montu cycle.
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** the corpus is highly syncretistic, but Montu is not one of its consistently central named powers. Similar solar, falcon, Ares, and bull imagery cannot automatically be relabeled Montu.
- **Edfu, Dendera, and Philae:** Montu may occur in divine lists, processions, geographical theology, or offering scenes, but those temples are not equivalent to his four principal Theban-region cult centers.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Early evidence:** celestial ascent, royal vitality, and Upper Egyptian/Theban patronage.

**Middle Kingdom development:** solar power, royal legitimacy, political unification, and aggressive kingship.

**New Kingdom canonical usage:** battlefield fury, chariot warfare, archery, smiting foreigners, protection of the king, and the destructive heat or force of the sun.

**Late regional theology:** bull fertility and potency, creator functions, oracular presence, temple protection, and the maintenance of the Theban sacred landscape.

Reducing Montu to “war” obscures his solar and royal foundations. Conversely, presenting him as originally a fully developed universal sun-god overstates the earliest evidence.

### Forms

1. **Falcon-headed anthropomorphic god.** Usually male, wearing a close-fitting garment or kilt and holding *was* scepter and *ankh*.
2. **Human-headed god.** Less diagnostic unless named.
3. **Bull-headed man.** Especially prominent in later Medamud imagery.
4. **Complete bull.** Most concretely realized in the living Buchis animal and its cult image.
5. **Falcon.** Local manifestations and emblematic representation.
6. **Royal manifestation.** The king shown or described “as Montu,” rather than Montu necessarily appearing as a separate figure.

### Crown and emblems

Montu’s most recognizable crown consists of a solar disk surmounted or flanked by two tall feathers, often with one or two uraei. Its visual overlap with other Egyptian divine crowns means the inscription and context remain important.

His characteristic equipment includes:

- *Was* scepter.
- *Ankh* sign.
- Solar disk.
- Double plumes.
- Uraei.
- Spear, bow, scimitar, or other royal-martial weapons in particular contexts.
- The “Nine Bows,” symbolizing Egypt’s conventionally ordered foreign enemies.

A Ptolemaic limestone statue from Medamud in the Louvre, E 12922, depicts bull-headed Montu seated above or crushing the Nine Bows, wearing the solar disk, double feathers, and double uraeus. It was found in 1926. The object is secure evidence for Ptolemaic iconography, not for the form Montu necessarily had in the Old Kingdom.

### Sacred animals and celestial associations

- **Falcon:** securely linked to Montu’s visible form.
- **Bull:** securely linked by temple epithets and, from the Late Period onward, the institutional Buchis cult.
- **Buchis bull:** a selected living sacred bull at Armant; later texts present it as Montu’s living manifestation, soul, herald, or renewed form.
- **Sun:** securely expressed by Montu-Re and the solar disk.
- **Specific planet:** popular claims identifying Montu exclusively with Mars should be treated cautiously. The Ares equation encouraged later planetary association, but Egyptian evidence does not make “planet Mars” his single original domain.
- **Colors, numbers, weekdays, crystals, incense recipes:** modern occult tables often assign these to Montu. No stable ancient canonical scheme is documented.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogical systems

Egyptian theology did not preserve one universally binding family tree for Montu.

#### Armant

**Regional variant:** Montu is associated with **Tjenenet** and **Iunyt**. Their precise relation can vary: consorts, companion goddesses, or members of a local triadic arrangement. Later texts may combine Tjenenet and Raet-Tawy.

#### Medamud

**Regional variant and later systematization:** Montu-Re, **Raet-Tawy** (“female Re of the Two Lands”), and **Harpocrates/Horus-the-Child** form a triad. The surviving Ptolemaic and Roman complex strongly represents this family, although its components have earlier histories.

#### Tod

**Regional variant:** Montu is frequently paired with Tjenenet. Other deities enter offering scenes according to the temple’s broader theology.

#### Karnak/Thebes

Montu operates beside the increasingly dominant Amun complex. Later theological systems sometimes make him part of an extended Theban divine family or describe him in relation to Amun, Mut, and Khonsu, but he was not consistently their biological son. The frequently repeated claim that “Amun and Mut first had Montu as their son, then replaced him with Khonsu” is a modern simplification of shifting cultic arrangements, not a single surviving Egyptian narrative.

### Documented divine interactions

The surviving Montu corpus is principally cultic and royal rather than narrative. Consequently, many “interactions” are joint appearances, offerings, fusions, or family groupings rather than episodes with dialogue and plot.

- **Re — fusion as Montu-Re.** Indigenous solar syncretism, prominent by the Middle Kingdom. **Tier:** securely attested early syncretistic identification.
- **Amun — Montu-Re and Amun-Re share Mentuhotep II’s Deir el-Bahri cultic landscape; later Amun becomes the dominant state god while Montu retains martial and regional functions.** Archaeological and dedicatory evidence, Middle Kingdom onward. **Tier:** securely attested institutional relation; later canonical development.
- **Amun — in the Kadesh Poem, Ramesses is characterized through Montu but prays to Amun, who responds.** Kadesh inscriptions, Year 5 of Ramesses II. **Tier:** later canonical royal theology; coordinated divine roles rather than a direct Montu–Amun conversation.
- **Tjenenet — paired with Montu as consort or companion at Tod and Armant; receives offerings with him.** Middle Kingdom foundations with fuller later temple evidence. **Tier:** regional variant.
- **Iunyt — appears with Montu and Tjenenet in Armant theology.** Primarily temple evidence. **Tier:** regional variant/learned systematization.
- **Raet-Tawy — consort of Montu-Re and female solar counterpart in Medamud and related Theban theology.** Best documented in later temple scenes. **Tier:** regional variant and late systematization.
- **Horus-the-Child/Harpocrates — child of Montu and Raet-Tawy in the Medamud triad.** Ptolemaic and Roman temple evidence. **Tier:** regional variant/learned systematization.
- **Horus — functional overlap through falcon form and kingship; Montu can be integrated with Horus traditions without universal identity.** Temple and royal texts. **Tier:** syncretistic or analogical identification, context-dependent.
- **Hathor — shares the Deir el-Bahri cultic complex and royal legitimation of Mentuhotep II; no surviving Montu–Hathor narrative is securely known there.** Middle Kingdom archaeological context. **Tier:** securely attested joint cultic setting.
- **Satis — appears with Montu and Mentuhotep II on a fragmentary lintel from Tod.** Eleventh Dynasty. **Tier:** securely attested joint appearance; the nature of the relation beyond the scene is uncertain.
- **Atum — coordinated with Montu in royal temple imagery and in the Kadesh Poem’s sequence of divine comparisons: Ramesses has strength like Montu and beauty like Atum.** New Kingdom. **Tier:** later canonical royal rhetoric, not genealogy.
- **Seth — both can embody dangerous force controlled for kingship, but no major surviving myth of personal combat between Montu and Seth is securely attested.** **Tier:** absence of documented interaction.
- **Osiris — Montu priests participate in Osirian funerary religion, but no principal mythic Montu–Osiris episode is securely preserved.** Third Intermediate/Late Period funerary evidence. **Tier:** institutional conjunction, not narrative.
- **Re-Harakhty — appears on the funerary stela of the Montu priest Ankh-ef-en-Khonsu; the deceased worships Re-Harakhty, not Montu in the depicted scene.** c. eighth–seventh century BCE. **Tier:** attested priestly-funerary conjunction.
- **Buchis — living bull manifestation, herald, *ba*, or renewed form of Montu according to late theology.** Bucheum stelae and temple sources, securely institutionalized from the fourth century BCE. **Tier:** late regional manifestation/syncretistic identification.
- **Ares — Greek martial equation.** Graeco-Roman interpretive tradition. **Tier:** interpretatio graeca.
- **Apollo — occasional solar equation.** Graeco-Roman interpretive tradition. **Tier:** interpretatio graeca; less comprehensive than the Ares association.
- **Maat — offered to Montu by the king in temple scenes; this represents the king sustaining divine-cosmic order.** Ptolemaic and Roman temple reliefs. **Tier:** learned temple ritual, not genealogy.
- **Sekhmet — both express solar destructive power, but a specific Montu–Sekhmet narrative is not securely documented.** **Tier:** absence of evidence.
- **Min — occasional iconographic and theological convergence in ithyphallic or generative settings should not be converted into a universal identity.** **Tier:** local syncretistic development where explicitly inscribed.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The king ascends like Montu

**Earliest source:** Pyramid Texts, Utterance 555, §1378b, late Old Kingdom.

The deceased king’s intact body and clothing accompany an ascent “like Montu.” This is the earliest recoverable Montu “episode,” but it is a ritual analogy rather than a narrated myth. It implies a known divine pattern now largely lost.

**Development:** Middle Kingdom funerary texts extend royal divine identifications beyond the pyramid king. Later descriptions that turn this line into a detailed myth of Montu physically carrying the sun or defeating a named cosmic enemy go beyond the text.

### Montu and the Theban reunification

No surviving ancient epic narrates Montu personally giving Mentuhotep II victory over Herakleopolis. The historical reconstruction rests on:

- Mentuhotep theophoric royal names.
- Temples and dedications at Theban-region centers.
- Montu-Re’s presence in the Deir el-Bahri complex.
- Later royal warrior ideology.

Thus “Montu enabled the reunification of Egypt” is best classified as **royal theological implication and modern reconstruction**, not a preserved narrative like the Contendings of Horus and Seth.

### Sinuhe’s combat

In the Middle Kingdom *Story of Sinuhe*, divine and royal martial language frames Sinuhe’s single combat with the champion of Retjenu. Montu functions as the exemplary force of victory.

**Function:** literary legitimation of Egyptian prowess and the protagonist’s restoration to royal order.

**Limitation:** retellings that describe Montu appearing bodily on the battlefield are embellishments.

### Pharaoh as Montu in battle

New Kingdom inscriptions repeatedly describe kings as Montu:

- Thutmose III is a Montu against foreign lands.
- Amenhotep II’s athletic and military image uses comparable divine-warrior rhetoric.
- Seti I and Ramesses II are represented as divinely empowered smiters.
- Ramesses III continues the pattern at Medinet Habu.

This is a broad royal narrative cycle rather than one myth: chaos and foreign resistance arise; the king manifests Montu’s violent power; enemies fall; temples receive the proceeds; Maat is restored.

### Ramesses II at Kadesh

The fullest Montu-connected royal story is the Kadesh cycle.

1. Ramesses marches toward Kadesh.
2. Hittite deception leads him into a vulnerable position.
3. The Hittite chariot force attacks the Egyptian camp.
4. Egyptian troops scatter.
5. Ramesses presents himself as abandoned.
6. He calls on Amun.
7. Amun answers, assuring the king of divine support.
8. Ramesses charges, rhetorically transformed into an irresistible divine warrior.
9. The text compares his strength and appearance to Montu and other gods.
10. The enemy is routed in the Egyptian account.

**Evidence tier:** later canonical royal inscription, not neutral battle reportage.

**Montu’s role:** the divine type of the king’s ferocity. The text’s explicit savior is Amun. Modern capsule summaries often merge these roles.

### The four Montus and the Theban palladium

Late temple theology conceptualized four localized Montus guarding the Theban sacred region:

- Montu of Armant.
- Montu of Tod.
- Montu of Medamud.
- Montu of Thebes/Karnak.

This arrangement is sometimes called the “Theban palladium.” It is a geographical and ritual system, not an early myth of four brothers. Its developed form belongs especially to Late and Graeco-Roman priestly theology.

### Buchis cycle

The Buchis cult supplied Montu with a repeated embodied life-cycle:

1. A bull bearing the proper signs was recognized.
2. Priests authenticated and installed it.
3. It lived as a sacred manifestation associated with Montu.
4. At death it received elaborate mummification and burial.
5. A funerary stela recorded birth, installation, reigns survived, death, and interment.
6. A successor renewed the manifestation.

The cycle is historical because individual bulls and burials are documented, while the claim that the bull embodied Montu belongs to the cult’s own theology. The earliest securely excavated Bucheum burial dates to Nectanebo II, although earlier regional bull cults probably contributed to the institution.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Armant/Hermonthis

Armant, ancient **Iunu-Montu**, was a principal cult center southwest of Thebes. The temple’s documented history extends from the Middle Kingdom into the Roman period, though later quarrying destroyed much of it. A mammisi associated with Cleopatra VII and Caesarion formed part of the late complex.

The **Bucheum**, on the desert margin, contained the catacombs of sacred Buchis bulls and burials of their mothers. Excavations by Robert Mond and Oliver Myers in the 1920s–30s recovered stelae and animal burials. The Egypt Exploration Society describes more than six centuries of use from the Late Period into the Roman era.

The long-accepted terminal date around 295 CE arose from a burial under Diocletian. Reanalysis of Bucheum Stela 20 established a later bull: born in the regnal reckoning called “year 33,” dying in “year 57,” interpreted as the era of Diocletian and corresponding to 340 CE under Constantius II. The “last bull in 305” statement is therefore obsolete or refers only to earlier excavators’ chronology.

### Medamud

Medamud, ancient **Madu**, lay northeast of Karnak. Archaeology documents:

- Middle Kingdom construction, especially under Senwosret III.
- New Kingdom rebuilding.
- Major Ptolemaic reconstruction.
- Roman additions, including the Tiberian gate.
- Workshops and settlement around the sanctuary.

The “primitive temple” excavated in 1938–39 was once assigned great antiquity and used to argue for an Old Kingdom Montu cult. Archival re-evaluation calls its character and dating controversial. It should not be presented as secure proof of a prehistoric or Old Kingdom temple.

Medamud emphasized Montu as a bull and as member of the Montu–Raet-Tawy–Harpocrates triad. The IFAO has excavated there since 1924 and continues renewed fieldwork and archival study.

### Tod

Tod, ancient Djerty, southeast of Thebes, possessed a Montu sanctuary with Eleventh-Dynasty royal blocks and extensive later rebuilding. Senwosret I’s and later kings’ activity confirms continuity. The Tod Treasure, found in foundation deposits associated with Senwosret I, illuminates royal temple wealth and long-distance exchange, though its foreign metal vessels are not themselves a narrative about Montu.

### North Karnak

The Montu precinct lies north of the Amun-Re precinct. Its major remains include:

- A temple whose history reaches into the Middle and early New Kingdom.
- Construction or reconstruction associated with Amenhotep III.
- Additions by Taharqa.
- Late Period enclosure work.
- The monumental gateway of Ptolemy III and Ptolemy IV.
- A temple of Harpre/Harpocrates.
- A Maat chapel.
- A sacred lake.

The precinct’s location beside the much larger Amun establishment illustrates coexistence, not complete replacement.

### Deir el-Bahri

Mentuhotep II’s mortuary complex combined royal mortuary cult, Montu-Re, and eventually Amun-Re. The surviving material is fragmentary because later quarrying removed much of its decorated stone. This remains one of the clearest monuments connecting Montu with the Theban reunification regime.

### Priesthood

Titles include:

- Prophet or god’s servant of Montu.
- High priest of Montu.
- Monthly priest assigned to a rotating phyle.
- Stolist or priest responsible for divine clothing and cult-image service.
- Singer of Montu.
- Door-opener and secret-keeper offices associated with Karnak ritual.
- Priests responsible for Buchis recognition, maintenance, burial, and records.

Priestly families are particularly visible in Third Intermediate and Late Period Theban funerary material. Their members could simultaneously hold offices for Montu, Amun, Osiris, Khnum, or other gods. This is evidence for an interconnected temple economy, not mutually exclusive denominations.

### Ritual

Secure or strongly supported practices include:

- Daily awakening, purification, clothing, feeding, and censing of the cult image.
- Presentation of food, drink, incense, linen, oils, and Maat.
- Royal foundation ceremonies and temple endowments.
- Processions by road and canal among Theban sanctuaries.
- Oracular consultation in later periods.
- Votive stelae and statues.
- Sacred-bull installation, maintenance, mummification, funeral, and burial.
- Funerary invocation by Montu’s priests.
- Military invocations and royal assimilation to Montu.

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

**Human sacrifice:** no secure evidence establishes a regular human-sacrificial rite in Montu’s historic temple cult. Egyptian smiting scenes and claims that the king gives captives to a god are ideological and sometimes accompany real warfare, execution, or enslavement, but they cannot by themselves prove sacrificial killing at Montu’s altar.

**Temple prostitution:** no reliable evidence establishes “sacred prostitution” as an institution of Montu’s temples. Applying the older generalized theory of temple prostitution to Montu is unsupported.

**Animal killing:** bulls were not ordinarily killed as routine disposable offerings merely because they were Buchis. A recognized sacred bull was maintained until death, mummified, and buried. Other animal and food offerings formed part of temple practice.

**Political violence:** Montu’s imagery explicitly legitimized pharaonic warfare, domination, captive-taking, and the smiting of foreign or rebellious enemies. This political use is abundantly documented even though the inscriptions’ victory claims remain partisan royal representations.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late Old Kingdom, c. 24th–23rd centuries BCE

Montu appears in the Pyramid Texts as a divine model for royal ascent. His geography, family, and war function are not fully expressed in that evidence.

### First Intermediate Period and Eleventh Dynasty, c. 2150–1990 BCE

The Theban ruling house’s Mentuhotep names and construction at Tod, Armant, and Deir el-Bahri mark Montu’s rise with the expanding Theban state. Mentuhotep II’s reunification gave his divine patron national political significance.

Whether Montu originated specifically at Armant, Medamud, Tod, or in a wider Upper Egyptian falcon/solar complex remains debated. Egyptian texts do not preserve an origin narrative settling the matter.

### Twelfth Dynasty, c. 1991–1802 BCE

Amun’s prominence increases under the Amenemhat kings, but Montu remains active. Senwosret III builds substantially at Medamud. Rather than a simple fall, the evidence shows functional differentiation: Amun expands as dynastic and imperial god; Montu retains Theban, solar, martial, and local importance.

### Second Intermediate Period and early New Kingdom

Fragmentary temple evidence suggests continuity. The Theban wars against Hyksos rule intensified the political usefulness of warrior theology, although surviving texts more often foreground Amun and royal ancestors than a unique Montu intervention.

### Eighteenth Dynasty, c. 1550–1295 BCE

Montu becomes a standard divine model for the imperial warrior king. Thutmose III, Amenhotep II, and other rulers are compared to him. North Karnak receives major development; priests of Montu remain visible.

During Akhenaten’s reign, images and names of traditional gods, including Montu, were attacked in some contexts. The Chicago Epigraphic Survey has documented a scene in which Montu’s image and name were hacked out during the Amarna period and restored afterward. This is secure evidence of suppression of his representation, though not proof that every Montu sanctuary ceased functioning.

### Ramesside period, c. 1295–1069 BCE

Montu’s martial rhetoric reaches exceptional visibility. Ramesses II’s Kadesh program repeatedly identifies the king with Montu-like power. Royal statues and cults could bear titles such as “Montu in the Two Lands.” Ramesses III continued Montu offerings and warrior imagery at Medinet Habu.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods, c. 1069–332 BCE

Montu priestly families remained influential at Thebes. The Twenty-Fifth Dynasty’s Nubian rulers patronized Theban sanctuaries, including North Karnak. Nectanebo I added to the precinct; the earliest securely excavated Buchis burial dates to Nectanebo II.

The cult was therefore neither dead nor a fossil after Amun’s rise. Its institutions adapted and, in the Buchis cult, acquired one of their most archaeologically visible forms.

### Ptolemaic period, 332–30 BCE

Armant, Medamud, Tod, and North Karnak were rebuilt or embellished. Priests systematized the four-Montu geography, solar genealogies, and local triads. Greek rulers represented themselves performing traditional offerings to Montu.

The bull-headed Montu crushing the Nine Bows and the monumental development of the Buchis cemetery belong prominently to this period.

### Roman period, 30 BCE–fourth century CE

Roman emperors appeared as pharaohs in temple reliefs and continued work at Montu centers. Buchis burials continued. The latest presently identified Buchis funerary record dates to 340 CE, showing that the cult survived well into the Christian imperial period.

### Christianization

The end was gradual and regionally uneven. Imperial restrictions on sacrifice and temple funding, the growth of Christian institutions, economic change, reuse of buildings, and local conflict all contributed.

Coptic occupation or structures occur at former temple sites. Later evidence from the Bucheum includes disturbance and Christian-period material, but a dramatic single episode in which Christians killed “the last Buchis” is not securely documented. Archaeology establishes abandonment, reuse, and looting more readily than a single forced-conversion event.

### Islamic period to modern archaeology

No demonstrable continuous popular cult of Montu survived into Islamic Egypt. The ancient place-name endured indirectly in Armant. Temples were quarried; the Armant temple suffered extensive destruction in the nineteenth century, including stone reuse associated with industrial construction.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century archaeology recovered Montu’s dispersed record. Excavations at Deir el-Bahri, Armant, the Bucheum, Medamud, Tod, and Karnak transformed a name known from classical and hieroglyphic texts into a documented regional cult system.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Egyptology and museums

Montu’s modern public image as “the Egyptian god of war” was standardized through dictionaries, museum displays, and popular mythological handbooks. This captures an important New Kingdom function but often suppresses his solar, royal, geographical, and bull dimensions.

Objects central to the modern image include:

- The Louvre’s falcon- and bull-headed Montu figures from Medamud.
- British Museum stela EA 926, showing a woman offering to Montu and Mentuhotep II.
- Kadesh papyri and temple reliefs.
- Buchis stelae and excavated bull burials.
- Montu-priest coffins and stelae from Thebes.

### Thelema

The strongest indirect occult connection comes through the funerary stela of the Montu priest Ankh-ef-en-Khonsu. In Cairo in 1904, Aleister Crowley and Rose Kelly connected the museum object then numbered 666 with the reception of *Liber AL vel Legis*, the *Book of the Law*. Crowley called it the “Stele of Revealing.”

This did not revive an ancient Montu rite. The central Thelemic reading transforms the stela’s Re-Harakhty, winged disk, and Nut into Ra-Hoor-Khuit, Hadit, and Nuit. Montu is principally present through the deceased owner’s priestly title. Claims that *The Book of the Law* is a recovered scripture of Montu are **modern invention**.

### Golden Dawn and modern ceremonial magic

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and successor currents used Egyptian divine names, color scales, planetary correspondences, and dramatic ritual personae. Montu was never as central as Isis, Osiris, Horus, Thoth, or Ra. Modern correspondence tables assigning Montu rigidly to Mars, Tuesday, iron, red gemstones, a tarot trump, or a zodiacal sign are modern occult systematizations unless an ancient source is separately cited.

### Kemetic reconstruction and neopaganism

Modern Kemetic and reconstructionist practitioners may honor Montu as a war, courage, protection, athletic, solar, or military deity. These communities are real modern religious traditions, but their scale is not reliably measurable and their rituals vary widely.

Historically responsible reconstruction may draw on offering formulae and temple practice. Personal revelations, internet epithets, devotional calendars, and ethical commands should be identified as modern practice rather than passed off as translated ancient doctrine.

### Popular culture

Montu appears in games, comics, fantasy reference works, and online deity catalogs, usually as a falcon-headed “war god.” Some modern fiction merges him with Khonshu because both are Theban and their names look similar in English; they are distinct Egyptian gods. Marvel’s moon god Khonshu is not Montu.

### Political appropriation

Pharaonic nationalist imagery sometimes invokes ancient warrior gods, but no major modern state ideology centered specifically on Montu is documented. Nor is there good evidence that Montu has become a stable symbol of a major extremist movement. Isolated internet use of a war deity does not establish organized appropriation.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Origin and original nature

Three emphases recur:

1. **Armant-origin theory:** Montu began as the local deity of Armant and spread with Theban power.
2. **Wider Theban-nome theory:** the four cult centers reflect a regional network whose early history cannot be reduced to a single point of origin.
3. **Solar-before-war reconstruction:** Montu’s earliest celestial and Montu-Re evidence suggests that his solar character preceded or underlay the fully developed warrior image.

The available evidence favors caution. The Pyramid Texts show celestial power; Eleventh-Dynasty evidence shows Theban royal patronage; explicit battlefield language becomes particularly abundant later. That sequence does not prove that Montu was “not a war-god” earlier—only that the earliest surviving line does not say so.

### Montu’s supposed replacement by Amun

Older histories often present a simple succession: Montu was Thebes’ god, then Amun displaced him. The evidence instead supports:

- Amun’s dramatic promotion.
- Continued Montu temples and priesthoods.
- Division and overlap of royal functions.
- Later cooperation in theological and ritual systems.
- A major Late and Graeco-Roman revival or transformation around Buchis.

“Displacement” describes relative state prominence, not extinction.

### Dating Medamud’s earliest sanctuary

Early excavation reports interpreted an unusual brick structure as a “primitive temple,” sometimes assigning it to the Old Kingdom. Modern IFAO archival and archaeological study treats the building as problematic. Its layout, context, and date do not securely establish an Old Kingdom Montu temple.

### Origins of Buchis

The institutional Bucheum sequence is securely visible from Nectanebo II. Scholars infer that Buchis consolidated several earlier Montu-associated bull traditions. The inference is plausible but should not be transformed into a detailed undocumented merger ceremony.

### Last Buchis burial

Earlier excavation chronology placed the cult’s end around Diocletian. Jean-Claude Grenier’s re-reading of the last funerary stela, supported in later scholarship, places the latest known bull’s death in 340 CE. This materially extends the documented history of the cult.

### Etymology

The “nomad” derivation remains common in popular works but is not certain. Egyptian divine names often resist transparent lexical derivation, particularly when their first written attestations postdate the name’s formation.

### Primary evidence and archives

Important holdings and publications include:

- Pyramid inscriptions in situ and in Sethe’s and Allen’s editions.
- Coffin Text manuscripts published by de Buck and subsequent projects.
- Ramesside Kadesh copies at Karnak, Luxor, the Ramesseum, Abydos, and Abu Simbel, plus papyri including Sallier III.
- Armant/Bucheum records from Egypt Exploration Society excavations.
- IFAO archives and publications for Medamud.
- Karnak epigraphy and the Chicago Oriental Institute/ISAC survey publications.
- Cairo’s Egyptian Museum and Grand Egyptian Museum holdings.
- Louvre Medamud material.
- British Museum stelae and papyri.
- Metropolitan Museum Deir el-Bahri excavation objects.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Montu’s name certainly means “Nomad.”
- He was exclusively a war-god from the beginning.
- He was permanently abolished when Amun rose.
- He was universally the son of Amun and Mut.
- He was identical with Horus, Ares, Apollo, or Mars in all periods.
- Buchis worship is documented from predynastic times.
- Montu’s priests practiced institutional temple prostitution.
- Montu required routine human sacrifice.
- The Kadesh texts say Montu personally answered Ramesses’ prayer; the answering deity is Amun.
- Crowley recovered an ancient Montu scripture.
- Modern crystal, chakra, tarot, or weekday correspondences are ancient Egyptian doctrine.

The most important methodological conclusion is that Montu’s “life” is not a continuous mythic biography. It is a sequence of transformations: an Old Kingdom divine comparator, an Eleventh-Dynasty royal patron, a Middle Kingdom solar and regional lord, a New Kingdom embodiment of the warrior king, a late protector of a four-city Theban sacred geography, a god made recurrently present in Buchis bulls, and finally an archaeological, museum, reconstructed-religious, and occultly refracted figure.
