# Apep (Apophis): Research Dossier

**Class:** Primordial hostile being; a male, serpent-shaped cosmic enemy, variously classified in modern scholarship as a demon, monster, antigod, or personification of *isfet*—disorder, rebellion, and the dissolution of created existence. Egyptian sources do not consistently treat him as a *nṯr*, “god,” in the positive cultic sense. He received rites of execration, not beneficent worship.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts, early second millennium BCE, especially Coffin Text Spell 414, “Repelling Apep from the Barque of Re.” No securely identified occurrence of the named being *ꜥꜣpp* has been demonstrated in the Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts. Those texts contain hostile snakes and anti-serpent spells, but equating every such serpent with Apep is a modern retrospective harmonization.

**Evidence tiers used below:** **[Early attested]**, **[Later canonical]**, **[Regional variant]**, **[Learned systematization]**, **[Syncretistic]**, **[Late accretion]**, **[Folklore]**, **[Modern reconstruction]**, and **[Modern invention]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Apep is the recurrent enemy of the solar god Re and of the ordered cosmos sustained through *maat*. In Middle Kingdom funerary texts he is already the named “Rebel” who must be repelled from Re’s boat. New Kingdom royal netherworld books transform this compact formula into a detailed nocturnal drama: Apep obstructs the solar barque, is immobilized by magic, bound, pierced, hacked apart, burned, and nevertheless returns to threaten the next cycle. The apparent contradiction is structural rather than accidental. Ritual and myth repeatedly announce his total annihilation, while the continuation of night, danger, rebellion, and ritual makes his renewed appearance inevitable. He is therefore not a ruler of a morally administered “hell,” nor originally an Egyptian equivalent of Satan. He is the force that would arrest cosmic process and return differentiated creation toward nonexistence.

### Names and scripts

- **Egyptian:** *ꜥꜣpp*, with variants *ꜥpp* and later Demotic *ꜥpp/ꜥpꜥp*. The writing commonly terminates in a serpent determinative. In hostile contexts the serpent sign may be mutilated by painted or carved knives, so that writing the name simultaneously disables its bearer.
- **Reconstructed vocalizations:** “Apep” is conventional Egyptological English, not a preserved pharaonic pronunciation. Reconstructions such as *ʿAʿpāp* or *ʿApāp* remain provisional because Egyptian ordinarily omitted vowels.
- **Coptic:** Bohairic ⲁⲫⲱⲫ, *Aphōph*, also a common noun meaning “giant.” This late lexical evidence partly underlies translations of the ancient name as “giant” or “giant serpent.” [The Coptic forms and standard dictionary references are collected here.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E2%B2%81%E2%B2%AB%E2%B2%B1%E2%B2%AB)
- **Greek:** Ἄπωφις or Ἄποπις, conventionally Latinized **Apophis** or **Apopis**. The Greek form may have been reshaped under the influence of Greek ὄφις, “snake,” but that explanation is not certain.
- **Distinguish the royal name:** The Hyksos king conventionally called Apepi/Apophis bore Egyptian *jppj*, not the securely identical spelling *ꜥꜣpp*. Their convergence in Greek and modern writing does not prove that the king was named for the cosmic serpent. The Turin King List concerns the king, not a cult or biography of Apep.

### Etymology

The etymology is unresolved.

- “Giant” or “giant serpent” is supported indirectly by Coptic *aphōph*, “giant,” and is used by the Global Egyptian Museum, but it may reflect a later semantic development rather than the original derivation.
- Connections with a root meaning “to slither” have been proposed but are not securely established.
- “He who was spat out” is a popular modern etymology retrojected from a late cosmogony involving divine bodily efflux. It is not a demonstrated linguistic derivation.
- The safest statement is that *ꜥꜣpp* is a proper name of uncertain etymology whose later Coptic descendant could mean “giant.” The underlying lexical forms and references to the *Wörterbuch* and Faulkner’s dictionary are summarized in the [TLA-linked lexical entry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%EA%9C%A5%EA%9C%A3pp).

### Titles and hostile designations

The texts more often deny dignity than confer stable cult titles. Among recurrent descriptions are:

- **“Enemy of Re”** (*ḫftj n Rꜥ* or equivalent formulations): central in Coffin Texts and the Bremner–Rhind ritual.
- **“Rebel”** (*sbj*): Coffin Text Spell 414 and later ritual language.
- **“Evil one,” “fallen one,” “one of evil character,”** and comparable execratory circumlocutions.
- **“Great serpent”** or a serpent measured at 120 cubits: explicitly systematized in the Roman-period Esna cosmogony.
- Names catalogued in the terminal section of Papyrus Bremner–Rhind, “The Names of Apep,” are ritual handles meant to make the enemy vulnerable, not honorific litany in the manner of Re or Amun.

“Lord of Chaos,” frequent in modern summaries, expresses Apep’s function but should not automatically be presented as a securely translated ancient cult title.

### Classification through time

- **Middle Kingdom:** named supernatural enemy or rebel-serpent in funerary magic.
- **New Kingdom:** personalized cosmic opponent in royal solar theology; still not a recipient of positive cult.
- **Late and Ptolemaic periods:** universalized ritual enemy whose image can encompass cosmic, royal, civic, and political opponents.
- **Greek and Roman interpretation:** “Apopis” enters Greek explanatory mythology, but is not simply identical with Typhon. Greek writers more regularly identify **Seth** with Typhon; collapsing Seth, Typhon, and Apep into one being erases the Egyptian tradition in which Seth can defend Re by spearing Apep.
- **Modern occult and popular media:** frequently called a “god of chaos,” “snake god,” demon, primordial dragon, or Satanic figure. These are modern taxonomic conveniences or reinventions, not a single ancient classification.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Absence from the Pyramid Texts

**[Early evidence: negative finding]** The Pyramid Texts begin in the pyramid of Unas in the late Fifth Dynasty, around the twenty-fourth century BCE. They contain hostile snakes and spells for neutralizing them, but the named *ꜥꜣpp* is not securely attested. Statements that Apep appears in the Pyramid Texts commonly confuse:

1. unnamed or differently named snake enemies;
2. later reuse of Pyramid Text anti-serpent formulas;
3. later copies in which Apep imagery influenced the writing or interpretation.

The absence matters. Apep’s later cosmic role should not be projected unchanged into the Old Kingdom.

### Coffin Texts

**[Earliest attested, c. 21st–17th centuries BCE]** The Coffin Texts, written principally on Middle Kingdom coffins, provide the first secure named evidence.

- **CT Spell 414:** titled “Repelling Apep from the Barque of Re.” The speaker declares, in effect, “I have repelled the Rebel; I have made Apep powerless.” A flame comes from the sky into the rebel’s cavern or hole. This is the clearest early nucleus of the solar conflict.
- Other allusive references occur in CT 80, 284, 473, 644, 681, 752, 1033, 1053, 1069, 1089, 1094, and 1179, though manuscripts and interpretation vary. These establish a larger Middle Kingdom complex of repelling, disabling, or rescuing Re from hostile serpent power.
- The early texts do not yet supply the full popular narrative of a gigantic serpent attacking at a fixed nightly hour while an established company of gods executes an elaborate battle sequence. That is a New Kingdom development.

A modern study reproducing CT 414’s title and key passage describes it as the most complete Middle Kingdom statement of the fight, while emphasizing how allusive the Coffin Text language remains ([text and discussion](https://files.core.ac.uk/download/pdf/79623221.pdf)).

### Book of the Dead

**[Later canonical, from c. 1550 BCE; individual manuscripts later]** The Book of Going Forth by Day developed from Coffin Text traditions but remained an open, variable corpus.

Relevant compositions include:

- **Spell 7:** protection from a hostile serpent, frequently identified as Apep in textual traditions.
- **Spell 15 and variants:** solar hymns celebrating Re’s victory and safe passage.
- **Spell 17:** cosmological declarations with internal glosses. It identifies the “Great Cat” as Re and explains a night of battle at Heliopolis. Illustrated manuscripts increasingly show the cat cutting a serpent.
- **Spell 39:** explicitly “repelling a serpent in the realm of the dead,” with Apep as the principal adversary.
- **Spells 99–100:** participation in or protection of the solar bark.
- **Spell 108:** describes the western mountain and the defeat of Apep.
- Further references occur in 127, 130, 134, 136B, 140, 144, 147, 149, and 181, depending on recension. Thomas George Allen’s concordance indexes the relevant occurrences ([critical translation and index](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc37.pdf)).

Spell numbers are modern editorial devices, and no one papyrus necessarily contained the entire corpus.

### Amduat

**[Later canonical, early Eighteenth Dynasty; earliest complete royal witnesses in the tombs of Thutmose III and Amenhotep II]** The *Book of What Is in the Duat*, or Amduat, maps the sun’s twelve nocturnal hours.

In the **seventh hour**, Apep lies across the route of the solar barque. Isis stands at the prow and uses effective speech or magic; the “Eldest Magician” joins her; Selket binds Apep’s head; another divine figure secures his lower body. Knives penetrate the serpent. The scene explains that knowledge and ritual performed on earth correspond to the divine action below. Apep’s defeat allows the solar journey to proceed.

The evidence does not support the simplified claim that Re alone engages in a nightly duel. Re is the threatened and ultimately victorious sovereign, but subordinate deities perform much of the disabling action. A comparative table of the underworld books identifies Isis, the Eldest Magician, Selket, and associated spirits as the seventh-hour executioners ([Kousoulis, table and discussion](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3174997/1/420908.pdf)).

In the **twelfth hour**, fiery powers destroy renewed enemies at the eastern threshold. This completes the nocturnal sequence but does not permanently remove the possibility of Apep’s return.

### Book of Gates

**[Later canonical, late Eighteenth/early Nineteenth Dynasty]** This royal netherworld book develops several Apep scenes:

- In the **third hour**, Atum confronts and repels the coiled serpent.
- In the **tenth hour**, divine executioners restrain and attack Apep in an extended battle composition.
- In the **eleventh hour**, he is bound, dismembered, and rendered powerless while the barque is towed toward rebirth.
- Ramesses I’s tomb, KV16, preserves an important early royal version.

As in the Amduat, the repeated mutilation is performative: representation, caption, and anticipated ritual cooperate to make the desired cosmic result present.

### Litany of Re

**[Later canonical, Eighteenth Dynasty]** The *Book of Adoring Re in the West* associates the dead king’s victory with Re’s chastisement of Apep. Portions occur with Thutmose III and his vizier Useramun; a fuller early witness was placed on Amenhotep II’s shroud for Thutmose III. Its concern is primarily Re’s forms and the royal union with the sun, so Apep is an oppositional element rather than the text’s central protagonist.

### Papyrus Bremner–Rhind

**[Late canonical/learned systematization, manuscript dated 305 BCE]** British Museum EA 10188 belonged to the priest Nesmin and was found at Thebes. Its colophon dates it to 305 BCE. The papyrus contains:

1. *Songs of Isis and Nephthys*;
2. *Ritual of Bringing in Sokar*;
3. *Book of Overthrowing Apep*, columns 22–32;
4. *Names of Apep*, columns 32–33.

The museum record identifies the material, date, ownership, and column divisions ([British Museum EA 10188](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10188-10)). Faulkner published the principal modern edition and translation in installments in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology* and as *The Papyrus Bremner-Rhind*.

The “Book” is a compilation of nine major units rather than a narrative biography. Its rubrics prescribe daily temple performance against Apep and, by extension, enemies of Re, Osiris, the king, temple, and country. Actions include:

- drawing Apep in green ink on new papyrus;
- fashioning him in red wax;
- naming the image;
- spitting on it;
- trampling it with the left foot;
- stabbing or cutting it with a flint knife;
- binding it;
- putting it into fire;
- repeating the destruction at prescribed moments.

The surviving manuscript is Ptolemaic, but CT 414 and Ramesside ritual parallels demonstrate older antecedents. It is therefore wrong both to call the entire work a Middle Kingdom manuscript and to regard it as a wholly Ptolemaic invention.

### Temple corpora

**[Regional variants and learned systematization, mainly Ptolemaic–Roman]**

Ritual and mythological texts at Edfu, Dendera, Esna, Kom Ombo, Medamud, Karnak, Hibis, and Philae integrate Apep into local theology. The evidence includes nineteen relevant ritual scenes at Edfu and six at Dendera, while Late Period Karnak sources provide related sequences. The order of operations varies, warning against reconstruction of a single universal ritual.

- **Edfu:** Horus and the king destroy serpent, crocodile, hippopotamus, and human-shaped enemies. Apep can function as the cosmic identity behind local and political adversaries.
- **Dendera:** Hathoric and solar ritual similarly turns image destruction into protection of temple, crown, and renewed year.
- **Esna:** a Roman-period Neith cosmogony gives Apep an explicit origin from the discarded umbilical cord of Re.
- **Karnak:** the Bremner–Rhind title says that its rite was performed in the temple of Amun-Re at Ipet-sut “in the daily cycle.”
- **Philae:** late inscriptions preserve solar and Osirian enemy traditions, but Philae should not be called an Apep temple.

The University of Würzburg’s Edfu project emphasizes that these compositions occur beside protection-of-the-year and Evil Eye rituals and must be studied as integrated text-image programs ([project description](https://www.phil.uni-wuerzburg.de/en/aegyptologie/forschung/horus-behedety-project/apotropaic-rituals/)).

### Greek Magical Papyri

**[Syncretistic/late survival, Roman Egypt]** A Greek magical spell breaks a palm branch so that the serpent—identified with Apep—will split. It adapts pharaonic image-mutilation logic to practical protection against snakes. It documents transmission, not an independent Greek cult of Apophis. Ritner discusses this survival in *The Mechanics of Ancient Egyptian Magical Practice* ([open edition](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc54.pdf)).

### Sources named in the brief that do not supply an Apep dossier

- **Memphite Theology/Shabaka Stone:** important for Ptah’s creation theology, but not a primary narrative source for the developed Apep conflict.
- **Contendings of Horus and Seth:** essential for Horus–Seth relations, but not the textual foundation for Seth’s defense of Re against Apep; that role belongs chiefly to solar and netherworld material.
- **Turin King List:** its “Apophis” concerns a historical Hyksos ruler whose Egyptian name is distinct.
- Their presence in a general Egyptian corpus must not be converted into evidence that Apep appears substantially in them.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Early attested → later canonical]** Apep threatens:

- Re and the solar bark;
- sunrise and cosmic recurrence;
- the ordered distinction between created things;
- divine, royal, civic, and personal security;
- the deceased’s passage through the next world.

Calling him “chaos” is broadly justified, but Egyptian *isfet* also includes rebellion, falsehood, wrongdoing, and socially destructive action. Apep is the paradigmatic cosmic rebel, not merely random disorder.

### Characteristic actions

Sources variously say that he:

- approaches, blocks, or attacks the bark;
- coils across the waterway like a sandbank;
- uses his gaze or roar to overpower the crew;
- drinks or swallows the water so that the bark runs aground;
- attempts to swallow or extinguish the sun;
- must be made to disgorge water;
- lies in a cavern or on a sandbank in the Duat;
- returns despite repeated declarations of annihilation.

Not every action occurs in the earliest texts. The complete nightly-barque scenario is principally New Kingdom and later.

### Form and size

- Standard form: an enormous undulating or tightly coiled serpent.
- Less common hostile forms or ritual substitutions include turtle, crocodile, and generalized enemy figures, but these are functional equations rather than proof that Apep had one fixed composite body.
- The Esna cosmogony specifies a serpent of **120 cubits**.
- Human-headed or otherwise composite enemy images may represent assimilated foes; identification must follow the caption rather than visual resemblance alone.

### Recognition in art

A secure Apep image is normally:

1. captioned with his name;
2. shown beneath, before, or obstructing the solar bark;
3. bound, speared, cut, or covered with knives;
4. attacked by a cat, king, or divine executioners.

A large snake without caption or narrative context cannot automatically be identified as Apep. Egyptian religion contained many protective and beneficial serpents, including Mehen and uraei.

### Principal depictions

- **Amduat, seventh hour:** the bound serpent before the solar boat; royal tomb witnesses beginning in the Eighteenth Dynasty.
- **Book of Gates, third and tenth/eleventh hours:** Atum and executioner gods confront and dismember Apep; prominently preserved in Ramesses I’s tomb.
- **Book of the Dead Spell 17:** the Great Cat at the *ished* tree cutting a serpent. The Nineteenth-Dynasty Book of the Dead of Hunefer, British Museum EA 9901, frame 8, explicitly catalogs “a cat slaying a snake by a tree” and associates the subject with Apophis ([museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA9901-8)).
- **Deir el-Medina tombs:** Sennedjem, Nakhtamun, and Inherkhau preserve Ramesside cat-and-serpent versions.
- **Edfu:** Apep’s name written with a serpent pierced by multiple knives; Ptolemaic ritual reliefs show king or Horus destroying the enemy.

### The cat of Re

**[Later canonical iconographic elaboration]** Book of the Dead Spell 17 calls the Great Cat Re himself through wordplay on *miw*, “cat.” The base text speaks of the Cat and the night when enemies were destroyed; the familiar picture of the cat holding a knife against a serpent makes the hostile victim explicit visually. The Hunefer papyrus is a secure Nineteenth-Dynasty example.

Modern captions sometimes identify the cat as Bastet, Hathor, Ra, or “the cat of Ra.” The internal gloss identifies the Cat as Re; Hathoric or Bastet associations can exist in broader feline theology, but should not replace the manuscript’s own identification.

### Colors, plants, numbers, and celestial bodies

- **Colors:** ritual prescriptions use green ink for a papyrus image and red wax for the figure. These belong to the mechanics of the rite, not to a securely attested “sacred color of Apep.”
- **Plant:** the *ished* tree at Heliopolis belongs to Spell 17’s battlefield and renewal symbolism. It is not evidence that Apep had a sacred tree.
- **Numbers:** 120 cubits at Esna and measurements in netherworld compositions describe the monster or his domain; no stable sacred numerology or festival number for Apep is documented.
- **Celestial associations:** darkness, eclipses, storms, and obstructions of sunlight could be interpreted through the Apep complex. No planet or constellation was his universally fixed ancient celestial body.
- **Modern attribution:** asteroid 99942 Apophis is a twenty-first-century naming event, not the rediscovery of an ancient astronomical identification.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin traditions

Apep has no consistent early genealogy.

1. **No stated parentage** — **[Early attested]**. Coffin Texts present him as an already-existing rebel. They do not name parents, consort, siblings, or offspring.
2. **Primordial residual chaos** — **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**. Because he opposes differentiation and *maat*, Hornung and others interpret him as an aspect of nonbeing that was never fully incorporated into creation. This is explanatory scholarship, not a surviving ancient birth narrative.
3. **Neith and Re’s umbilical cord** — **[Regional variant/late learned systematization]**. In Roman-period Esna text 206, Neith bears Re, removes his umbilical cord, and casts it into Nun; it becomes the 120-cubit serpent called Apep, whose heart conceives rebellion against Re. The current translation preserves the sequence explicitly ([Esna III, column 2, §§14–15](https://shemanefer-esna3.share.connect.posit.cloud/column-2-204-213.html)). The story makes Apep derivative from creation’s expelled material without necessarily making Neith a willing mother of evil.
4. **“Born of Nun”** — **[Late theological shorthand]**. Since the cord is placed in Nun and the serpent emerges there, later summaries call Apep “born from Nun.” Nun is the medium or primordial condition in the Esna account, not unambiguously a biological father.
5. **Neith’s saliva** — **[Modern misstatement/variant confusion]**. Popular books sometimes say that Neith spat Apep into being. The published Esna translation instead makes him from Re’s discarded umbilical cord; spittle later produces gods in the same cosmogony. The two bodily-efflux episodes have often been conflated.
6. **Brother of the Sun** — **[Greco-Roman learned accretion]**. Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 36, reports “another tale” in which Apopis, brother of Helios, wars against Zeus; Osiris helps Zeus and is adopted as Dionysus. This is a Greek-language interpretive myth of the late first/early second century CE, not evidence for an early Egyptian sibling genealogy ([Greek and English text](https://www.astrologicon.org/plutarch/plutarch-on-isis-and-osiris.html)).

No securely attested consort or offspring belongs to Apep. Claims that he fathered a race of demons generally expand collective “children,” rebels, or enemy bands beyond what the sources say.

### Every documented named divine interaction

The entries below distinguish direct episodes from broad theological opposition.

- **Re (Ra):** Apep attacks or obstructs Re’s barque; Re’s company repeatedly repels and destroys him. CT 414, early second millennium BCE; Book of the Dead, Amduat, Book of Gates, and Bremner–Rhind. **[Early attested; later canonical elaboration]**
- **Isis:** from the prow of Re’s bark, Isis uses spells that deprive Apep of strength in Amduat hour 7; she remains prominent in later anti-Apep ritual. Early Eighteenth Dynasty onward. **[Later canonical]**
- **Selket/Serqet:** binds Apep’s head with a lasso after Isis and the Eldest Magician disable him; Amduat hour 7. **[Later canonical]**
- **The Eldest Magician (*Hkꜣ-smśw*):** joins Isis in neutralizing Apep’s strength; Amduat hour 7. This named divine personification should not casually be replaced by “Heka” without regard to the caption. **[Later canonical]**
- **Atum:** stands before, controls, or repels the coiled Apep in Book of Gates hour 3; royal tomb copies beginning in the late Eighteenth/early Nineteenth Dynasty. **[Later canonical]**
- **Seth:** spears or otherwise repels Apep from the solar bark in New Kingdom and later solar traditions, including later temple iconography. This is one of Seth’s principal protective roles and proves that Seth and Apep were not originally identical. **[Later canonical; regional continuation]**
- **Horus of Edfu:** destroys serpentiform and assimilated enemies in the Great Horus Myth and associated Ptolemaic ritual scenes; Apep becomes a cosmic identity of the defeated foe. **[Regional variant/learned systematization]**
- **Neith:** creates Re and discards his umbilical cord into Nun, from which Apep comes into being; Esna 206, Roman period. **[Regional variant/late learned systematization]**
- **Aker:** guards or restrains Apep’s limbs in creation and overthrowing material preserved in the Bremner–Rhind complex. **[Late canonical compilation]**
- **The Great Cat/Re:** the Cat, identified by Spell 17’s gloss as Re, cuts the hostile serpent beside the Heliopolitan tree in illustrated Book of the Dead witnesses. **[Later canonical iconographic elaboration]**
- **Osiris:** Apep is declared enemy of Osiris/Wennefer as well as Re in the Bremner–Rhind ritual; destroying Apep protects both solar and Osirian renewal. **[Late canonical compilation]**
- **Zeus:** Apopis wars against Zeus in Plutarch’s reported Egyptian tale. This is not an Egyptian temple-text equation and cannot be backdated. **[Greco-Roman learned accretion]**
- **Osiris/Dionysus:** in the same Plutarchan episode, Osiris aids Zeus against Apopis and is adopted as Dionysus. **[Greco-Roman syncretistic accretion]**
- **Helios:** Plutarch calls Apopis Helios’s brother, preserving an inverted kinship between solar power and its enemy. **[Greco-Roman learned accretion]**

Named gods who merely appear elsewhere in the same composition have not been counted as interacting with Apep. In particular, no direct Apep episode should be manufactured from the general membership of a deity in Re’s entourage.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Repelling the rebel from Re’s boat

**Earliest source:** CT 414, Middle Kingdom.  
**Tier:** **[Early attested]**

The speaker identifies with an effective divine power who has repelled “the Rebel” and rendered Apep powerless. A flame enters his cavern. The action rescues or clears Re’s bark. This compact spell contains the essential relationship: solar passage is endangered by a named rebel and restored through powerful utterance.

The text does not yet narrate a nightly battle hour by hour, name a full divine crew, or describe Apep swallowing the sun. Those are later elaborations.

### B. The nightly attack on the solar barque

**Earliest detailed sources:** Amduat and Book of Gates, New Kingdom.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical]**

After sunset Re enters the Duat. The journey is necessary for regeneration: the aged sun encounters Osiris and renews the powers of life. Apep interrupts this process. In Amduat hour 7 his immense body occupies the route; Isis and the Eldest Magician use spells, while Selket and other divine agents bind him. The immobilized serpent is pierced. Once his power is removed, the barque advances.

Later descriptions add or emphasize:

- drinking the underworld river;
- creating a sandbank;
- hypnotizing or terrifying the crew;
- swallowing the solar disk;
- being forced to regurgitate water;
- dismemberment and burning.

Apep never gains a final canonical victory. Eclipses or storms could make his threat experientially immediate, but the surviving texts do not present every eclipse as a unique mythic triumph followed by rescue in a standardized story.

### C. Atum and the bound serpent

**Source:** Book of Gates, especially hours 3 and 10–11.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical variant]**

Atum confronts the coiled rebel and ensures Re’s progress. Later hours expand the punishment: gods grasp ropes, bind Apep, stab him, cut him, and prevent his movement. His body can fill an entire register, making obstruction visible. The Book of Gates distributes the conflict across the night rather than confining it to the Amduat’s seventh-hour tableau.

### D. The Great Cat at Heliopolis

**Textual source:** Book of the Dead Spell 17; pictorial witnesses especially Ramesside.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical and iconographic elaboration]**

The deceased declares identity with the Great Cat beside the *ished* tree on the night when the enemies of the Lord of All were destroyed. An internal gloss says the Cat is Re. Illustrated copies make the victim a serpent and show the Cat wielding a knife.

The British Museum’s Hunefer papyrus is direct material evidence. The accompanying Spell 17 text identifies Re, while the image supplies the familiar cat-and-snake combat. “Bastet cuts Apep” is therefore not the most exact formulation for this manuscript. The Great Cat is Re; broader feline identifications are secondary.

### E. The ritual annihilation of Apep

**Fullest source:** Papyrus Bremner–Rhind, 305 BCE, drawing on older spells.  
**Tier:** **[Late canonical systematization]**

The practitioner creates ritually vulnerable bodies for Apep in papyrus and wax. Naming, spitting, trampling, binding, cutting, and burning transfer the desired fate to the cosmic enemy. Recited claims announce that Apep’s body, soul, shadow, magic, name, and allies have been destroyed. The acts protect Re, Osiris, Pharaoh, temple, city, and Egypt.

The ritual’s claim of permanent annihilation conflicts deliberately with its daily repetition. The performance is not a failed attempt to kill a mortal animal. It renews the conditions under which ordered existence continues.

The same logic can encompass political enemies. Cosmic rebellion and opposition to the reigning order are ritually assimilated; this is a documented political use of Apep theology.

### F. Apep’s birth from Re’s cord

**Source:** Esna 206, Roman period.  
**Tier:** **[Regional variant/late learned systematization]**

Neith creates and bears Re. She removes his umbilical cord and casts it into Nun. It becomes a 120-cubit serpent named Apep. His heart then conceives rebellion against Re and his companions.

This story answers a theological problem left open by earlier sources: how can destructive power arise in a creation made by deity? It locates Apep in discarded divine material without making Neith intentionally create evil. It cannot be used as the original, pan-Egyptian genealogy.

### G. Apopis, Zeus, and Osiris

**Source:** Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 36, c. 100 CE.  
**Tier:** **[Greco-Roman learned accretion]**

Apopis, called brother of the Sun, wars against Zeus. Osiris supports Zeus, who defeats the enemy and adopts Osiris as Dionysus. The account belongs to Plutarch’s comparative, allegorical treatment of Egyptian religion. Its Greek genealogy and divine equations should be reported, not harmonized with Esna or Coffin Text theology.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No positive cult

No temple dedicated to Apep, beneficent priesthood of Apep, offering cult, oracle, pilgrimage, or securely ancient community of Apep worshippers has been found. Apep appears inside temples as the object of ritual hostility. Consequently, “cult of Apep” is misleading unless “cult” is being used broadly for ritual attention.

No credible evidence connects Apep with temple prostitution. No documented human sacrifice was offered to him. Claims of sacrificial murder, secret Apep priesthoods, or an ancient antinomian cult require evidence not presently known.

### Anti-Apep priestly performance

The Bremner–Rhind rubric places performance in Amun-Re’s Karnak temple as part of a daily cycle. Comparable rites occur in Ptolemaic temples. The officiants were ordinary Egyptian temple specialists—lector priests and related ritual personnel—not members of a separate “priesthood of Apep.”

The ritual combined:

- spoken incantation;
- divine role assumption;
- lists of vulnerable names and body parts;
- image manufacture;
- physical mutilation;
- fire;
- repeated declarations that the enemy and his human analogues were destroyed.

Kousoulis traces the practice from Middle Kingdom funerary formulas to fully developed Late and Ptolemaic temple liturgy ([study abstract and full thesis](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3174997/)).

### Wax figures and burning

The instruction to use a red-wax Apep and a green-ink image on fresh papyrus is secure in the Bremner–Rhind tradition. Wax was suitable because it could receive form, names, damage, and heat. Burning the figure was not a symbolic human sacrifice; it was image magic directed at a supernatural and political enemy.

Because the ritual could name Pharaoh’s enemies, it participated in royal ideology. The equation does not prove that identified prisoners were executed as part of the Apep rite. Egyptian execution and prisoner-smashing imagery must be evaluated independently.

### Calendar and frequency

- Bremner–Rhind describes a **daily** temple application.
- Related rites protected the New Year, temple, city, crown, and festival cycle.
- Popular statements about one universal “annual festival of burning Apep” compress varied evidence. Some performances were calendrical; the best-preserved handbook explicitly supports repeated daily action.

### Archaeological and epigraphic evidence

- **Karnak:** textual claim of daily performance in Amun-Re’s temple.
- **Edfu:** extensive carved ritual program; the present temple was constructed under the Ptolemies between 237 and 57 BCE.
- **Dendera:** anti-enemy and renewal texts embedded in the Hathor temple’s ritual topography.
- **Esna:** Roman-period cosmogony and local theological synthesis.
- **Magical statues and Horus cippi:** extend anti-serpent and enemy-repulsion techniques into healing and private protection.
- **Papyri:** show movement between institutional and private possession; Bremner–Rhind, though compiled for temple use, belonged to the priest Nesmin.

Apep’s images survive because Egyptians represented him in a state of defeat. There is no paradox in drawing a being whose image could be dangerous: captions, knives, bonds, and ritual context controlled the representation.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Old Kingdom, c. 2686–2181 BCE

Hostile snakes and solar enemies are present, but named Apep is not secure. Later Apep theology inherited and reorganized this older anti-serpent vocabulary. **[Prehistory of the motif, not attestation of the being]**

### First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, c. 2181–1650 BCE

Named Apep emerges in Coffin Texts. Some scholars relate this development to expanding solar theology or memories of political disorder, but a direct historical causation cannot be proved. CT 414 already makes him Re’s opponent aboard the solar boat. **[Earliest attested phase]**

### Second Intermediate Period

The Hyksos king Apepi must not be used as evidence for devotion to Apep. The names are written differently. No “Hyksos cult of the chaos serpent” is documented. **[Contested-material finding]**

### Early New Kingdom, c. 1550–1300 BCE

Royal tomb cosmographies give Apep his monumental form. The Amduat fixes the celebrated seventh-hour confrontation; the Book of Gates distributes his capture and execution through several hours. Book of the Dead traditions make his defeat available to nonroyal deceased owners. **[Canonical elaboration]**

### Ramesside period, c. 1292–1070 BCE

Apep imagery becomes especially elaborate in royal and private funerary art: Ramesses I’s Book of Gates, Books of the Dead, and Deir el-Medina tombs. Seth’s role as defender of the bark coexists with his more dangerous roles in Osirian mythology. **[Expansion and popularization within literate funerary culture]**

### Third Intermediate and Late periods, c. 1070–332 BCE

Anti-Apep formulas circulate across funerary, healing, magical, and institutional media. Mutilated determinatives and executable enemy images make written form itself ritual action. The opposition between Apep and political enemies becomes increasingly explicit. **[Ritual intensification]**

### Ptolemaic period, 332–30 BCE

Temple scriptoria systematize inherited traditions. Bremner–Rhind, dated 305 BCE, preserves the fullest handbook. Edfu and Dendera integrate anti-Apep actions into local divine victories and protection of crown, town, temple, and year. **[Learned systematization and regional synthesis]**

### Roman period, 30 BCE–fourth century CE

Esna gives Apep a local cosmogonic birth. Greek magical texts preserve practical anti-serpent adaptations. Plutarch reports a Hellenized story of Apopis, Helios, Zeus, and Osiris. These are multiple, nonidentical receptions. **[Regional, magical, and syncretistic developments]**

### Christianization and the end of temple institutions

As Egyptian temple institutions lost patronage and closed between Late Antiquity and the early medieval period, the specialist ritual context supporting Apep texts disappeared. This was not a documented empire-wide campaign aimed uniquely at Apep; he had no independent temple cult to suppress. Dragon-slaying images in Christian Egypt invite comparison, but a direct line from Apep to Saint George cannot be assumed from visual resemblance alone.

The Coptic word *aphōph*, “giant,” preserves the name lexically. This does not establish continuous worship or ritual.

### Medieval and early-modern reception

Apep played little independent role in medieval European learned demonology. Renaissance Hermeticism appropriated “Egypt” chiefly through Greco-Roman Hermetica, Isis, Osiris, Thoth/Hermes, and later hieroglyphic speculation. Claims of a continuous secret Apophis order descending from pharaonic priests are unsupported. **[Absence of evidence]**

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century rediscovery

Decipherment of hieroglyphs, publication of royal tombs, Coffin Texts, Books of the Dead, and the Bremner–Rhind papyrus reconstructed Apep’s documentary history. Older writers sometimes treated him as “the Egyptian devil” or conflated him with Seth; more recent scholarship emphasizes the distinct ritual roles and diachronic evidence.

### Present

Modern Kemetic reconstructionists usually preserve Apep’s ancient role as a being to be rejected or ritually neutralized. Some communities avoid writing or speaking the name without mutilating, parenthesizing, or striking it through. These are modern devotional applications inspired by ancient execration practices, not proof of uninterrupted transmission.

Small occult or “left-hand path” circles sometimes revere Apep as liberation, entropy, anticosmic force, or adversarial consciousness. Such Apep devotion is a modern reversal of the ancient evidence. Its scale cannot be reliably quantified.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Egyptology and popular mythology

Victorian and early twentieth-century writers often translated Apep into Christian moral language as “god of evil,” “devil,” or “Satan.” These comparisons made the figure accessible but encouraged three errors:

1. treating Egyptian religion as a dualism of equally sovereign good and evil gods;
2. merging Apep with Seth;
3. presenting Apep as head of an organized evil cult.

Modern handbooks by Erik Hornung, Jan Assmann, Geraldine Pinch, Richard H. Wilkinson, and Robert K. Ritner instead situate him within Egyptian theories of *maat*, *isfet*, nonbeing, solar renewal, and ritual performance.

### Occult reception

Apep is peripheral in the classical Golden Dawn and Thelemic systems compared with Isis, Osiris, Horus, Thoth, Nuit, Hadit, and Set. Later anticosmic and dragon-oriented occult authors developed “Apophian” or “Apepian” currents.

- Michael W. Ford and Michael Kelly are among modern writers using Apep/Apophis within adversarial or left-hand-path systems.
- These systems draw selectively on Egyptology, Thelema, Setianism, chaos magic, and modern Satanism.
- They do not reproduce the ancient Egyptian rite, whose purpose was Apep’s destruction.
- The Temple of Set, founded by Michael Aquino in 1975, centers **Set**, not Apep. Ancient iconography of Set spearing Apep underscores the distinction. Claims of an official ancient-derived Apep cult inside Setianism are modern and marginal.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Modern Kemetic communities commonly perform adapted “execration of Apep” rites for cosmic, personal, or social protection. Some use translations of Bremner–Rhind; others create new liturgy. The underlying model is ancient, but each modern adaptation must be dated to its author or community. There is no demonstrable institutional continuity from a pharaonic temple.

A smaller countercurrent venerates Apep positively. This is **[Modern invention/reversal]**, even when practitioners sincerely understand it as recovered spirituality.

### Psychology and New Age interpretation

Apep has been interpreted as:

- the Jungian shadow;
- unconscious fear;
- depression or self-sabotage;
- entropy;
- trauma;
- the cyclical return of destructive impulses.

These are modern psychological metaphors. Ancient texts treat the being as an objectively operative cosmic and ritual enemy; they do not describe him as an archetype within an individual psyche.

### Literature, television, games, and comics

- **Stargate SG-1:** Apophis, played by Peter Williams, is a Goa’uld system lord and major early antagonist. He is essentially a science-fiction tyrant using an Egyptian name, not a close adaptation of the serpent.
- **Gods of Egypt** (2016): Apep appears as a colossal space-serpent threatening Re’s solar vessel, visually amplifying the nightly-barque myth.
- **Assassin’s Creed Origins** (2017): Apep appears in a visionary or underworld confrontation, adapting Egyptian imagery for combat.
- **Yu-Gi-Oh!**, role-playing games, fantasy fiction, and trading-card systems frequently turn Apep into a summonable dragon, boss monster, or chaos deity.
- **John Langan’s *The Fisherman*:** uses an immense world-serpent called the Fisherman’s captive, invoking Apophis-like cosmic horror without reproducing an Egyptian narrative.
- Numerous modern works call Apep an “elder god.” That phrase belongs to modern fantasy and Lovecraftian vocabulary, not Egyptian classification.

### Asteroid 99942 Apophis

**[Modern naming event]** Roy A. Tucker, David J. Tholen, and Fabrizio Bernardi discovered provisional object 2004 MN4 in 2004. Its early impact estimates generated exceptional public attention. It was subsequently named **99942 Apophis** after the Egyptian figure.

NASA records that Tholen searched for an Egyptian name and chose Apep/Apophis as a being of destruction; contrary to a widespread story, NASA says it was not named for the *Stargate SG-1* villain. The asteroid will pass Earth safely on **13 April 2029**. Current calculations exclude an impact for at least the next century ([NASA facts](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/apophis-facts/)). ESA likewise explains that Earth’s gravity will substantially alter its orbit during the close pass ([ESA planetary-defense page](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Planetary_Defence/Apophis)).

The asteroid has materially reshaped the modern resonance of “Apophis”: for many nonspecialists it now signifies a potential planet-killer. That association is twenty-first-century reception, despite drawing rhetorically on Apep’s ancient cosmic danger.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No major ancient nationalist movement or modern mass political ideology centered on Apep has been documented. Online anticosmic, Satanic, and accelerationist subcultures occasionally appropriate him as a symbol of annihilation or opposition to order. Evidence is scattered, usually pseudonymous, and insufficient to infer a unified movement. Statements about organized “Apep cults” or terrorist networks require case-specific documentation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Foundational editions and studies

- Adriaan de Buck, *The Egyptian Coffin Texts*: fundamental hieroglyphic edition for the Middle Kingdom evidence.
- Raymond O. Faulkner, *The Ancient Egyptian Coffin Texts*: standard English translation, to be checked against de Buck and later philology.
- Raymond O. Faulkner, *The Papyrus Bremner-Rhind*: foundational edition and translation.
- Thomas George Allen and Raymond O. Faulkner, Book of the Dead translations; Allen’s edition is especially valuable for manuscript variants and indices.
- Erik Hornung, *Das Amduat*, *The Ancient Egyptian Books of the Afterlife*, and *Conceptions of God in Ancient Egypt*: central to the distinction between created being, nonbeing, and the recurring enemy.
- Jan Assmann, especially work on New Kingdom solar religion and Egyptian theologies of order and memory.
- Robert K. Ritner, *The Mechanics of Ancient Egyptian Magical Practice*: decisive for understanding image destruction as operative ritual rather than “mere symbolism.”
- Panagiotis Kousoulis, study of the Ritual of Overthrowing Apep from funerary antecedents to temple cult.
- John Gwyn Griffiths, work on Plutarch and the Horus–Seth conflict.
- Richard H. Wilkinson and Geraldine Pinch: reliable synthetic orientations, though handbook compression should be checked against primary texts.
- *Lexikon der Ägyptologie* entries remain important for German-language research history and bibliography.

### Principal debates

#### Was Apep present in the Pyramid Texts?

The secure answer is no, not by name. Hostile serpent material supplies antecedents, but identifying an unnamed snake as Apep requires argument. Older or popular works often blur motif history with named attestation.

#### What does the name mean?

“Giant,” “giant serpent,” “slitherer,” and “spat-out one” circulate. Only the later Coptic meaning “giant” has direct lexical support; the original Egyptian derivation remains disputed.

#### Is Apep a god?

Egyptian supernatural categories do not map neatly onto “god versus demon.” Apep has name, agency, speech, body, magic, and cosmic presence, but lacks beneficent cult and is treated as what ritual must negate. “Antigod,” “demon,” or “cosmic monster” may be analytically clearer than “god,” provided none is mistaken for a native fixed category.

#### Is Apep identical with Seth or Typhon?

No timeless identity is supportable.

- Seth can be Re’s defender against Apep.
- In late Egyptian theology Seth may acquire Apep-like traits or be ritually assimilated to defeated enemies.
- Greek Typhon is commonly identified with Seth.
- Plutarch separately names Apopis in another narrative.

The relationships are historical transformations, not proof of an original Seth–Apep–Typhon unity.

#### Did Egyptians worship Apep?

No positive cult is documented. Extensive ritual attention is directed toward knowing, representing, and destroying him. Modern Apep worship reverses this orientation.

#### Is the myth a memory of eclipses or political collapse?

Apep could explain or ritually contain frightening interruptions—eclipses, storms, failed sunrise, political rebellion—but no single natural or historical event is established as his “origin.” Attempts to derive him solely from the First Intermediate Period or from eclipse observation are modern hypotheses.

#### Why does he always return?

Some interpretations treat recurrence as an inconsistency. Egyptian ritual logic instead distinguishes a successful present annihilation from the permanent structural possibility of disorder. Daily cosmic renewal requires repeated defense.

### State of the evidence

- Coffin Text material survives on dispersed coffins in museums and archaeological publications.
- Royal netherworld books remain in the Valley of the Kings and in facsimiles and critical editions.
- Book of the Dead witnesses are distributed across Cairo, London, Paris, Turin, Berlin, Leiden, New York, and many other collections.
- Bremner–Rhind is British Museum EA 10188.
- Temple inscriptions remain *in situ* and in large photographic and epigraphic corpora, many still incompletely translated.
- TLA supplies searchable Egyptian text and lemma infrastructure; the Bremner–Rhind text is available through the [TLA digital corpus](https://tla.digital/text/A2PTGDCYB5E4XK6OORW6V4QHOE?page=122).

The surviving record is rich but uneven. It disproportionately represents royal tombs, temples, priestly manuscripts, and elite burials. Ordinary oral belief is much harder to recover.

### Discredited or unsupported claims that remain popular

- Predynastic pottery snakes are “the earliest portrait of Apep.”
- Apep is named in the Pyramid Texts.
- Hyksos king Apepi was certainly named in devotion to Apep.
- Apep and Seth were always the same.
- Bastet is invariably the cat that kills Apep.
- Apep had temples, priests, sacrifices, and worshippers analogous to Re’s.
- The anti-Apep rite required human sacrifice.
- Neith spat Apep into existence in the securely translated Esna narrative.
- An occult “Order of Apep” survived continuously from ancient Egypt.
- Asteroid Apophis remains on a predicted collision course with Earth.

All are false, unproved, or materially oversimplified.
