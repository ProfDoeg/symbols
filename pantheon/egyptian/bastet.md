# Bastet: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*nṯrt*) of the ancient Egyptian pantheon; initially a royal and solar lioness-power, later most visibly a feline protector, festival goddess, and recipient of cat-votive cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Bastet’s name is securely present on Early Dynastic–Old Kingdom stone vessels recovered from the galleries beneath Djoser’s Step Pyramid at Saqqara. Some vessels are residual objects probably made in the Second Dynasty, approximately the late twenty-ninth or early twenty-eighth century BCE, although their original archaeological contexts were lost when they were deposited beneath Djoser’s pyramid. Her earliest securely localized connection with Bubastis is later: Sixth-Dynasty monuments of Pepi I and offices connected with her temple, approximately the twenty-fourth–twenty-third centuries BCE. The Pyramid Texts of Pepi I and Pepi II then supply the earliest substantial sentences about her: “my mother Bastet has nursed me.” The distinction matters—the Early Dynastic vessels attest her name and cult, not the later domestic-cat goddess, mythology, or full Bubastite theology.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Name

The conventional Egyptological form is **Bastet**, transliterated variously as **Bꜣstt**, **Bȝstt**, **Bastet**, or, in older typography, **Ubasti/Wbastit**. Coptic descendants include forms such as Ⲟⲩⲃⲁⲥⲧⲉ; Greek authors wrote **Βούβαστις**, *Boubastis*, both for the goddess and, by metonymy, her city. Greek **Boubastis** reflects Egyptian **Pr-Bꜣstt**, “House/estate of Bastet,” rather than an independent Greek divine name.

The common modern contrast “Bast was the original name, Bastet a later feminine form” is too simple. Egyptian spelling varied, final consonants were increasingly weak in speech, and *-t* was already the normal feminine ending. Greek *Boubastis* and Coptic forms confirm that the fuller name was not merely invented by nineteenth-century Egyptologists. “Bast” remains a convenient normalized form, especially in older literature, but should not be treated as a distinct early goddess.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed etymology]** Two principal explanations compete:

1. **“She of Bast.”** The goddess’s name is a nisbe or locality-name, “the one belonging to Bꜣst,” with the goddess and city defined reciprocally.
2. **“She of the *bꜣs*-jar.”** The hieroglyphic writing employs a vessel sign conventionally identified as an ointment or perfume jar, encouraging “she of the ointment jar.”

Neither meaning is demonstrated beyond dispute. Egyptian divine names could be written with rebuses or cultic symbols that did not preserve transparent historical etymology. The claim that *Bastet* means “soul of Isis” from *bꜣ* + *ꜣst* is a modern folk etymology: it does not match the historical spelling or morphology and is not an ancient explanation. The early-vessel evidence and etymological problem are summarized in Francesco Raffaele’s publication of an Early Dynastic inscription, which explicitly leaves open whether goddess, town, or jar-name came first. [Early inscription study](https://www.francescoraffaele.com/egypt/hesyra/Bastet.htm)

### Classification and cross-cultural identifications

- **Egyptian:** a goddess, initially lioness-like and solar/royal, not a demon or merely an animal spirit.
- **Greek:** Herodotus identifies her as **Artemis** (*Histories* II.59, 60, 67, 137). This is fifth-century BCE *interpretatio graeca*, not evidence that Bastet and Artemis had always been one being. The comparison probably drew upon femininity, childbirth, dangerous wild nature, and a major festival, though Herodotus does not explain his full reasoning. [Herodotus II.60](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D2%3Achapter%3D60)
- **Late Egyptian syncretism:** Bastet could be compounded or coordinated with **Sekhmet, Mut, Wadjet, Hathor, Isis**, and other Eye goddesses. Egyptian syncretism did not necessarily abolish the component deities; a being could be invoked as “Sekhmet-Bastet” in one ritual while both retained separate temples, images, and priesthoods elsewhere.
- **Greco-Roman Isis religion:** Bastet could enter Isis-centered divine networks, but “Bastet is simply Isis” is an overstatement. Joint titles and composite images are historically situated syncretisms.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Early Dynastic and Old Kingdom

**[Secure early evidence]** Stone vessels from the Step Pyramid galleries preserve early spellings of Bastet’s name. Because Djoser’s subterranean galleries contained many heirloom vessels made for earlier kings, their findspot supplies a terminus for deposition but does not automatically fix the date of manufacture. The inscriptions establish a goddess called Bastet and an elite or royal cultic setting; they do **not** establish a domestic-cat form, motherhood portfolio, sistrum, or the later Bubastis festival. [Early Dynastic inscription discussion](https://www.francescoraffaele.com/egypt/hesyra/Bastet.htm)

At Bubastis, Sixth-Dynasty monuments and titles of cult administrators attest an institutional cult by the reign of Pepi I. This is firmer evidence for “Bastet of Bubastis” than attempts to project the city-cult back into the First or Second Dynasty.

**Pyramid Texts.** In Utterance 508, first preserved in Sixth-Dynasty royal pyramids, the deceased king says: “My mother Bastet has nursed me.” She participates in the king’s regeneration and ascent alongside goddesses of Nekheb and Dep. Elsewhere she may be associated with the king’s heart or embodied protective force. The text makes her maternal toward the king, but it is not a domestic story about a mother cat. [Translation of Utterance 508](https://www.touregypt.net/featurestories/pyramidtext.htm); modern critical English editions include James P. Allen, *The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts*, and R. O. Faulkner, *The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts*. [Allen edition record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt14jxv34)

### Middle Kingdom: Coffin Texts and expanding access

The **Coffin Texts**, approximately 2100–1650 BCE, transfer and rework royal mortuary language for nonroyal elites. Bastet occurs in protective, transformative, and divine-identification formulae, but there is no single “Bastet cycle” within them. Their evidence shows diffusion of her protective power beyond royal mortuary ritual. De Buck’s *The Egyptian Coffin Texts* remains the base hieroglyphic edition; Faulkner’s translation is standard but must be checked against individual witnesses because spells differ between coffins. [Coffin Text overview](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/Publications/SAC/sac3.pdf)

### New Kingdom and Third Intermediate Period

**Book of the Dead.** The corpus developed from the Second Intermediate Period/New Kingdom and remained in use into the Roman period. Bastet appears chiefly in divine lists and composite protective forms rather than as the protagonist of a continuous narrative.

Particularly important is **Spell 164**, a late addition not found in every Book of the Dead. It addresses **“Sekhmet-Bastet, Eye of Re”**, calls her mistress of the gods and of the red and white crowns, locates her at the prow of Ra’s bark against an evil being, and declares that “strife and peace” are in her grasp. The spell demonstrates an explicitly fused Sekhmet-Bastet and her aggressive solar aspect; it refutes the modern simplification that Bastet was only gentle while Sekhmet alone was violent. [University of Chicago critical translation, Spell 164](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc37.pdf)

**Temple and royal texts.** The Third Intermediate Period is pivotal because rulers of the Twenty-second Dynasty came from the eastern Delta and monumentalized Bubastis. Osorkon II’s granite festival gate records his *sed*-festival, with Bastet presiding in her city. Édouard Naville’s excavation publication remains a primary archaeological record, though its methods and reconstructions require correction by later fieldwork. [Naville, *Festival-Hall of Osorkon II*](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/naville1892/0001)

### Late Period local mythology: Papyrus Brooklyn 47.218.84

**[Regional variant; surviving manuscript approximately seventh–sixth century BCE, with older materials possible]** This hieratic “mythological handbook” of Lower Egyptian nomes preserves local cult explanations. In the Bubastite section, Seth possesses or has carried away the injured Eye of Horus; Bastet recovers or protects it at Bubastis. A sacred water procession displays her triumph. This is the clearest narrative in which Bastet directly intervenes in the Horus–Seth conflict.

It must not be retrojected into the Ramesside **Contendings of Horus and Seth**. Papyrus Chester Beatty I, copied under Ramesses V around 1147 BCE, does not give Bastet a narrative role. The Brooklyn papyrus supplies a later, specifically Delta localization of the conflict. Dimitri Meeks’s *Mythes et légendes du Delta d’après le papyrus Brooklyn 47.218.84* is the principal edition. [Corpus and bibliography](https://oraec.github.io/corpus/oraec2.html); [discussion of the Bubastite episode](https://www.herder.de/wbg-magazine/wissenswert/antikenforschung/herrin-des-himmels-auge-des-re/)

### Texts named in the research brief but not actually Bastet narratives

- **Shabaka Stone/Memphite Theology:** the extant Twenty-fifth-Dynasty monument, probably made under Shabaka in the late eighth century BCE, presents Ptah’s creation through heart and tongue and a Memphite settlement of Horus and Seth. Bastet has no major speaking or narrative role. Later Memphite theology associates her with Ptah, but this should not be inserted into the Shabaka text. [Shabaka Stone overview](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA498)
- **Contendings of Horus and Seth:** Papyrus Chester Beatty I, Ramesses V, contains no securely identifiable Bastet episode. The later Brooklyn local myth must remain distinct. [Gardiner’s publication](https://chesterbeatty.ie/assets/uploads/2018/11/The-Library-of-A.-Chestera-Beatty-Description-of-a-Hieratic-Papyrus-with-a-Mythological-Story-Love-Songs-and-Other-Miscellaneous-Texts.pdf)
- **Turin King List:** a Ramesside royal list, not a Bastet myth or cult document. Its relevance is chronological context, not testimony about her.
- **Edfu, Dendera, Karnak, and Philae:** Ptolemaic and Roman inscriptions incorporate Bastet into learned lists and Eye-of-Ra theology. These are valuable late systematizations but cannot, without an older parallel, prove that each relationship existed nationally in the Old Kingdom.

### Classical sources

**Herodotus, fifth century BCE.** Book II is indispensable but outsider testimony filtered through Greek equivalences and ethnographic interests.

- II.59–60: pilgrimage and festival at Bubastis.
- II.67: embalmed cats are taken to Bubastis for burial.
- II.137–138: description of the sanctuary and its low-lying, tree-lined enclosure.
- II.66: houses are protected during fires because agitated cats may leap into the flames; households mourn dead cats by shaving their eyebrows.

His figure of **700,000 adult pilgrims** is explicitly attributed to what local people said. It is not a census and is logistically improbable if read literally. The procession, music, ritual mockery, exposure, sacrifice, and heavy wine consumption are nevertheless substantive eyewitness-era evidence, although Ian Rutherford stresses Herodotus’s Greek framing and the rarity of native accounts of popular pilgrimage. [Herodotus II.60](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D2%3Achapter%3D60); [Rutherford’s study](https://academic.oup.com/book/10869/chapter-abstract/159088776)

**Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*, first–second century CE.** Plutarch preserves learned Greco-Egyptian equations, including Bubastis/Artemis, within a philosophical treatment of Isis and Osiris. His testimony is important for Roman-period reception, not a transparent archive of predynastic doctrine.

### Magical papyri

The Greek and Demotic magical papyri, principally second century BCE–fifth century CE, preserve operative, syncretistic religion. In **PGM III.1–164**, a ritual involving the drowning of a cat invokes a cat-faced **Sekhmet-Bastet** in a coercive chariot-race spell. This is neither ordinary temple worship nor proof of a universal Egyptian cat-sacrifice rite; it is a specialized magical operation in a late multilingual milieu. [PGM III text](https://hermetic.com/pgm/pgm-iii1-59); [PGM collection database](https://berlpap.smb.museum/pgm/)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Development of form

**[Secure early and later iconographic evidence]**

1. **Early phase:** Bastet was a leonine goddess or woman with a lioness head, comparable in visual grammar—but not automatically identical—to Sekhmet, Tefnut, Menhit, and other dangerous Eye goddesses.
2. **New Kingdom transition:** domestic cats became increasingly visible in household and tomb art. Some small amulets plausibly identify Bastet with a domestic cat, but isolated feline images cannot always be named securely.
3. **Third Intermediate and Late Period:** Bastet’s domestic-cat form becomes unmistakable and abundant. She appears as a seated cat; a cat-headed woman; or a woman holding a sistrum, aegis, basket, and sometimes kittens.
4. **Modern reception:** the seated black or bronze cat has eclipsed her lioness and anthropomorphic forms, producing the modern shorthand “Egyptian cat goddess.”

The change was not an overnight replacement of lioness by cat. Late theology could still invoke Bastet as a raging lioness, while cat imagery expressed pacified power, fertility, maternal abundance, musical celebration, and approachable protection.

### Recognizing Bastet

Typical Late Period objects include:

- A poised seated cat, often with pierced ears and jewelry.
- A wedjat-eye, scarab, or divine figure on the chest.
- A cat-headed woman in a close-fitting dress.
- A **sistrum**, linking her to music, appeasement, festival, and Hathoric ritual.
- An **aegis**, usually a divine head and broad collar held before the body.
- A cylindrical or handled basket.
- Kittens at her feet, emphasizing fecundity and maternal care.
- Bronze, faience, wood, or stone votive manufacture; some hollow bronzes contained animal remains.

The British Museum’s **Gayer-Anderson Cat**, probably around 600 BCE, is a hollow-cast bronze cat with gold earrings and nose ring and a silver wedjat pectoral. Scientific analysis found a copper-tin-arsenic-lead alloy; modern repairs and green paint altered its appearance before conservation. It is an exceptional elite dedication, not a generic household idol. [British Museum object study](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/animals/gayer-anderson-cat)

The Metropolitan Museum’s inlaid bronze Bastet, dated broadly 664–30 BCE, illustrates the enormous Late/Ptolemaic market in divine feline statuettes. [Metropolitan Museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/551774)

### Domains

| Domain | Evidence and qualification |
|---|---|
| **Royal nourishment and protection** | Pyramid Texts: Bastet nurses the deceased king |
| **Solar heat, royal violence, and destruction of enemies** | Eye-of-Ra theology; Sekhmet-Bastet invocations; lioness form |
| **Protection and health** | Amulets, votives, funerary spells, healing/protective contexts |
| **Motherhood and fertility** | Maternal language, nursing kittens, royal motherhood; intensified in Late Period imagery |
| **Music, dance, sexuality, joy, intoxication** | Sistra, festival descriptions, Hathoric affinities; not a complete early portfolio |
| **Household protection** | Reasonable inference from amulets and domestic feline symbolism, but “goddess of the home” is largely a modern categorical formulation |
| **Perfume/ointment** | Depends partly upon the disputed jar etymology; not securely established as her primordial domain |
| **Cats** | Securely dominant from the first millennium BCE, especially through votive breeding, mummification, and bronze dedications |
| **Moon** | Late and secondary, sometimes produced through Artemis or Wadjet associations; Bastet was primarily solar in Egyptian theology |
| **War** | Her early leonine and Eye aspects are violent, but the modern title “goddess of war” risks importing Sekhmet’s better-attested institutional role |

No ancient canonical system securely assigns Bastet a unique color, number, weekday, planet, or modern crystal. Black cats, the moon, Monday, the number nine, essential oils, and particular gemstones in present-day devotional lists are normally **modern inventions or modern occult correspondences** unless a specific ancient object or text is cited.

### The cat killing Apophis: a frequent misidentification

Book of the Dead Spell 17 illustrates a cat cutting the serpent Apophis beside the *ished*-tree. The accompanying gloss identifies that “Great Cat” as **Ra himself**, not Bastet. Modern illustrations and websites often relabel the animal Bastet because she is the best-known Egyptian cat goddess. Bastet can indeed defend Ra and oppose chaotic enemies in other texts, but the famous Spell 17 vignette is not secure evidence that she personally decapitates Apophis.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

Egyptian genealogy was a language of cult, place, and function, not one immutable family tree.

### Genealogical variants

- **Mother of the king:** Pyramid Texts Utterance 508; early royal theology.
- **Daughter of Ra/Re:** standard Eye-of-Ra relationship in later solar theology.
- **Daughter of Atum:** local or learned variant reflecting the equation of Ra and Atum.
- **Consort of Atum:** sometimes assigned in Bubastite systematization, though less universal than the daughter relationship.
- **Consort of Ptah:** Memphite and late systematization, facilitated by Bastet’s convergence with Sekhmet, Ptah’s usual Memphite consort.
- **Mother of Mahes/Mihos:** particularly important in Bubastis and Leontopolis; the lion god is son of a lioness/cat goddess, with Nefertum or Atum sometimes filling the paternal position.
- **Mother of Nefertum:** occasional late/local variant produced through substitution for Sekhmet in the Memphite triad.
- **Relation to Anubis:** modern lists sometimes call Anubis her son. This is not a stable ancient genealogy and usually arises from indiscriminate compilation of local variants.
- **Relation to Isis:** joint or fused forms occur late, but the popular derivation “Bastet = soul of Isis” is modern and philologically unsound.

### Every securely or plausibly documented named divine interaction

The following list separates narrative events from cultic coordination.

- **Ra/Re — mother–daughter and defensive relationship.** Bastet is called Ra’s daughter and Eye and stands at the prow of his bark against an evil power; Book of the Dead Spell 164, first millennium BCE. **Tier: later canonical/learned systematization.**
- **Ra/Re — nursing or identifying relation mediated through the king.** The deceased king is nursed by “my mother Bastet”; Pyramid Texts Utterance 508, Sixth Dynasty. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Atum — daughter, partner, or local co-deity.** Bubastite and late temple arrangements variously relate her to Atum; no single genealogy prevails. **Tier: regional variant/learned systematization.**
- **Horus — rescue of his injured Eye.** Bastet recovers or protects Horus’s Eye and triumphantly displays it at Bubastis; Papyrus Brooklyn 47.218.84, surviving Late Period manuscript. **Tier: regional variant.**
- **Seth — adversarial encounter.** Seth carries or controls the injured Eye; Bastet defeats his purpose by recovering it; Papyrus Brooklyn 47.218.84. **Tier: regional variant.**
- **Ptah — consort or coordinated Memphite deity.** Late Memphite cult places Bastet beside Ptah, partly through her convergence with Sekhmet. **Tier: regional syncretistic identification.**
- **Sekhmet — fusion and complementary pairing.** “Sekhmet-Bastet” in Book of the Dead Spell 164 and late magical texts; both wrath and pacification are contained in the composite goddess. **Tier: securely attested late syncretism.**
- **Mahes/Mihos — mother and son.** Bubastite/Leontopolitan temple theology makes the lion god Bastet’s son. **Tier: regional canonical relationship.**
- **Nefertum — mother and son in some systems.** Memphite substitution aligns Bastet with Sekhmet and makes Nefertum her son. **Tier: regional/late systematization.**
- **Hathor — coordinated Eye goddess and ritual analogue.** Sistrum, intoxication, music, return/pacification, and solar femininity encourage joint forms and overlapping rites. **Tier: syncretistic identification, especially late.**
- **Mut — fusion as Mut-Bastet or larger composite goddesses.** Temple litanies combine solar maternal powers. **Tier: late learned systematization.**
- **Wadjet — coordinated Delta and Eye goddess.** Both are protective solar females of Lower Egypt; compounds occur in late theological texts. **Tier: regional syncretism.**
- **Isis — joint cult and Isis-Bastet forms.** Late Egyptian and Greco-Roman religion place Bastet in Isis-centered networks. **Tier: late syncretism.**
- **Artemis — Greek equation.** Herodotus II.59–60 and later Greek usage identify Bubastis/Bastet with Artemis. **Tier: fifth-century BCE onward, interpretatio graeca.**
- **Apophis/Apep — generalized enemy relation.** Sekhmet-Bastet protects Ra’s bark from an evil being in Spell 164, but the famous cat cutting Apophis in Spell 17 is Ra, not explicitly Bastet. **Tier: partly attested; specific popular image attribution is modern invention.**
- **The deceased king — divine-human interaction.** Bastet nurses and enables the king’s ascent; Pyramid Texts. **Tier: securely attested early.**

There is **no documented Bastet encounter in the Ramesside *Contendings of Horus and Seth***. There is likewise no secure ancient narrative in which Bastet is transformed from Sekhmet after Ra intoxicates her; that popular account blends Bastet into the **Destruction of Mankind/Heavenly Cow** tradition, whose principal raging goddess is Hathor-Sekhmet.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Bastet nurses the ascending king

**Earliest source:** Pyramid Texts Utterance 508, Sixth Dynasty.

The dead king rises toward the sky with the assistance of several maternal and territorial goddesses. Bastet has nursed him; the lady of Nekheb reared him; the goddess of Dep places her hands upon him. The passage integrates Lower and Upper Egyptian protective powers into royal resurrection.

This is not a birth narrative for Bastet and does not give the king a literal biological feline mother. Divine maternity expresses authorization, nourishment, and incorporation into divine society.

**Development:** Later sources generalize Bastet’s maternity to children, fertility, and protection, but the earliest text is specifically royal and funerary.

### 5.2 Bastet recovers the Eye of Horus at Bubastis

**Source:** Papyrus Brooklyn 47.218.84; surviving manuscript generally placed in the seventh–sixth century BCE, probably preserving older Delta traditions.

Seth injures or seizes the Eye of Horus and brings it into the Bubastite region. Bastet retrieves and protects it. Her sacred bark travels upon the local waterways, and the goddess appears in triumph. The myth explains Bubastis as a place where order and legitimate kingship were restored and provides an aetiology for a waterborne festival procession.

**Variant history:** The older Pyramid Texts already know the damaged Eye of Horus, and the Ramesside *Contendings* tells a long Horus–Seth struggle, but neither makes Bastet’s Bubastite intervention part of its surviving narrative. The Brooklyn version is therefore a local reterritorialization of a national mythic complex.

### 5.3 Bastet as the pacified Eye of Ra

There is no single early narrative titled “Bastet and the Eye of Ra.” Instead, temple hymns, divine epithets, funerary spells, and ritual litanies map her onto a recurring structure:

1. A solar daughter or Eye embodies the god’s dangerous heat.
2. She rages against enemies or withdraws.
3. Music, drink, cooling water, or ritual praise pacifies her.
4. The pacified goddess returns as beneficent protection, fertility, and joy.

For Hathor, Sekhmet, Tefnut, Mut, and Bastet, the structure could be shared and locally reassigned. Bastet’s lioness-to-cat imagery made her especially suitable as the “appeased” feline power, but “Sekhmet literally turned into Bastet” is not the wording of a securely identified pharaonic source. It is a modern harmonization of fluid Eye-goddess theology.

### 5.4 The “Distant Goddess”

Late temple theology associates several Eye goddesses with the story of a daughter of Ra who departs to Nubia and is brought back by Thoth, Shu, or another male deity. Bastet can occupy this role in local liturgies. The fuller narrative is better attested for Tefnut/Hathor/Mehit and should not be made uniquely hers. **Tier: late regional assignment within a shared mythic pattern.**

### 5.5 Taboubu

In the Demotic tale commonly called **Setne II**, Setne encounters the alluring Taboubu, a priestess or daughter of a priest of Bastet. She demands property and finally the killing of his children before intercourse; the horror is then revealed as illusion or divine punishment.

Geraldine Pinch and others have suggested that Taboubu may function as a manifestation or punitive agent of Bastet. The surviving text does not straightforwardly say “Taboubu is Bastet.” This is a **modern scholarly interpretation**, not an explicit ancient identification.

### 5.6 The cat and Apophis

The familiar scene of a knife-wielding cat killing Apophis belongs to Book of the Dead Spell 17, but its gloss identifies the cat with Ra. Bastet’s broader solar-defender role made the later reassignment attractive. The story “Bastet personally beheaded Apophis every night” should be labeled **modern conflation**, not securely attested canonical myth.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Bubastis/Tell Basta

Bubastis, modern Tell Basta near Zagazig in the eastern Nile Delta, was the principal cult center. Its Egyptian name *Pr-Bꜣstt*, “House of Bastet,” became Greek *Boubastis*.

The site contains:

- Late Old Kingdom elite cemetery and officials connected with Bastet’s administration.
- Sixth-Dynasty royal building activity under Teti and Pepi I.
- Middle Kingdom royal monuments, including material from Amenemhat III.
- New Kingdom construction and reused blocks.
- Major Twenty-second-Dynasty building, especially the *sed*-festival complex of Osorkon II.
- Late Period shrines and extensive animal-votive activity.
- Ptolemaic and Roman occupation.

The temple’s architectural history is difficult to reconstruct because later kings reused earlier stone, the Delta’s water table and agriculture damaged remains, and nineteenth-century removal dispersed objects. Archaeology nevertheless confirms a monumental sanctuary surrounded by canals or water features broadly compatible with Herodotus’s topographical description. [Geoarchaeological study](https://egqsj.copernicus.org/articles/70/73/); [excavation-history database](https://egyptartefacts.griffith.ox.ac.uk/node/1070/)

Claims that Persians completely destroyed Bastet’s temple around 350 BCE are not securely supported. Surviving architectural fragments and later cult activity contradict a clean one-event destruction model. A late sanctuary may have suffered military damage, spoliation, rebuilding, and gradual abandonment rather than a single annihilation. [Study of the Bubastis naos](https://egittolizzando.altervista.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/The-Naos-of-Bastet-Lady-of-the-Shrine-from-Bubastis.pdf)

### Herodotus’s festival

Herodotus II.60 describes boats crowded with men and women traveling to Bubastis. Travelers sing and clap; women play rattles or sistra and flutes, shout ritual abuse at women in towns along the route, dance, and expose themselves. At Bubastis great sacrifices occur and, he says, more wine is consumed than during the rest of the year. Local informants gave attendance as 700,000 adults.

The description attests pilgrimage, inversion of normal decorum, music, sexuality, intoxication, sacrifice, and intercity performance. Calling the festival an “orgy” imports a modern implication not required by the text. Ritual exposure and abuse are documented; mass sexual intercourse or temple prostitution is not.

The native Brooklyn papyrus adds a sacred bark procession through Bubastis’s waters, connecting festival landscape to Bastet’s recovery of Horus’s Eye.

### Priesthood and economy

Old Kingdom titles show administrators and servants of Bastet’s estate; later evidence includes prophets, *wab*-priests, scribes, sistrum-players, and female musical personnel. The temple controlled land, animals, workshops, offerings, and labor like other major Egyptian institutions. Men and women could hold Bastet-related titles, but surviving titles do not permit reconstruction of one unchanging priestly hierarchy across three millennia.

Theophoric names incorporating Bastet—such as “Bastet has given,” “Bastet is gracious,” or “servant of Bastet”—attest personal devotion and the geographical reach of her cult. Their frequency is useful but varies with survival and administrative fashions.

### Cat breeding, killing, mummification, and votive exchange

From the first millennium BCE, enormous numbers of cats were embalmed and deposited at Bubastis, Saqqara’s Bubasteion, Beni Hasan, and other sites. Their functions were not all identical:

- Some were beloved pets buried with care.
- Many were **votive animals**, purchased by pilgrims and presented to Bastet.
- Some embodied or conveyed the goddess’s power.
- Some bundles contain complete cats; others contain partial animals, mixed bones, padding, or no cat.
- Radiography and osteological study show that many young animals were deliberately killed—by neck breaking, strangulation, or blunt trauma—to supply the votive economy.

This was not “Egyptians worshipped every cat and never killed one.” Sacred-species protection, household affection, temple breeding, ritual killing, and commercial production coexisted. The British Museum notes that limbs could be broken to obtain the customary mummy shape and that most surviving cat mummies are Greco-Roman. [British Museum cat mummy](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA6753); [Zivie and Lichtenberg, “The Cats of the Goddess Bastet”](https://academic.oup.com/cairo-scholarship-online/book/23151/chapter-abstract/184022466)

### Saqqara Bubasteion

New Kingdom elite tombs at Saqqara were later reused as cat catacombs associated with Bastet. Excavations have recovered cat coffins, wooden and bronze feline figures, amulets, papyrus fragments, and at least one lion burial, showing that the sacred-feline category was not restricted to domestic cats. [Lion discovery](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14724625/); [Bubasteion excavation report](https://pes.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2020/03/Mostafa_Waziri_Mohammad_M_Youssef_83-91.pdf)

### Beni Hasan and the fertilizer shipment

In 1888–1890 enormous cat deposits at Beni Hasan were commercially excavated. Contemporary reports describe approximately **nineteen tons** of mummified animal material shipped to Liverpool, where it was auctioned and ground for fertilizer. Exact numbers of cats—often printed as 180,000 or many hundreds of thousands—depend on estimates rather than an archaeological count. The episode is well documented as destruction of archaeological material under colonial-era antiquities and commodity practices. [Contemporary 1890 report](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-mummy-cats-of-beni-hassan/)

### Sacrifice, sexuality, and contested claims

- **Animal sacrifice/killing:** securely documented for the votive-mummy industry.
- **Human sacrifice:** no credible evidence links human sacrifice to Bastet’s historical temple cult.
- **Temple prostitution:** no secure Egyptian administrative, legal, or temple evidence demonstrates an institution of sacred prostitution serving Bastet. Herodotus’s ritual exposure and erotic license do not establish it.
- **Cat sacrifice in magic:** PGM III instructs the ritual drowning of a cat in a specialized coercive spell. This is documented late magical practice, not evidence for ordinary Bubastite liturgy.
- **Cult violence:** the killing of animals for votives is materially documented. Stories that Persian soldiers conquered Egyptians by carrying cats before them come from late hostile literary traditions and should not be treated as straightforward military history.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Approximately 2900–2350 BCE: emergence

Bastet appears by name in Early Dynastic/early Old Kingdom elite material. Her precise place of origin remains debated. The evidence supports an early leonine goddess connected with kingship and the Delta; it does not yet support the complete later image of a gentle domestic-cat deity.

### Approximately 2350–2050 BCE: royal mother and Bubastite institution

Sixth-Dynasty Pyramid Texts make her the king’s nursing mother. Monuments and offices at Bubastis establish a functioning local cult. Her power is protective, maternal, territorial, and royal.

### Approximately 2050–1550 BCE: broader mortuary and regional theology

Coffin Texts extend protective divine identifications to nonroyal elites. Bubastis receives Middle Kingdom royal construction. Bastet remains predominantly leonine in formal divine iconography; the domestic cat is not yet her exclusive emblem.

### Approximately 1550–1070 BCE: Eye-goddess integration

New Kingdom texts and images integrate her more visibly into solar Eye theology and the overlapping network of Sekhmet, Hathor, Tefnut, Mut, and Wadjet. Domestic cats become prominent in elite households and tomb scenes. Some feline amulets may represent Bastet, but identification must be object-specific.

### Approximately 1070–664 BCE: Bubastite ascendancy and cat iconography

The Twenty-second Dynasty’s Delta origins elevated Bubastis politically and monumentally. Osorkon II’s festival hall is central evidence. Bastet’s domestic-cat and cat-headed forms grow common, but her lioness and solar-dangerous aspects continue.

Calling this her “peak” is justified by royal building at Bubastis and the growing number of votives, but the largest surviving animal deposits often date later.

### 664–332 BCE: massive votive cult and learned local mythology

Saite and later first-millennium Egypt saw increasingly standardized bronze cats, cat-headed Bastet figures, mummies, and pilgrimage economies. Papyrus Brooklyn records Bubastite myth. Herodotus supplies the fullest outside description of her festival and temple.

### 332 BCE–fourth century CE: Greco-Roman internationalization

Bastet was identified with Artemis and entered Isis-centered religious networks. Greek, Demotic, and magical papyri used forms such as Sekhmet-Bastet in ritual technology. Cat mummification continued on an industrial scale. Figurines and cult traces circulated beyond Egypt in the Mediterranean.

### Fourth–sixth centuries CE: contraction and Christianization

Imperial restrictions on sacrifice, conversion of urban populations, changing temple economies, episcopal expansion, and local conflict progressively ended state-supported Egyptian temple cult. No surviving decree specifically records “the suppression of Bastet at Bubastis,” and the exact closing date of her temple is unknown. The process should not be reduced to a single destruction by Christians, Persians, or Muslims.

Bubastis remained inhabited, but active institutional worship of Bastet disappears from the record in Late Antiquity. Claims of an unbroken secret cult through the medieval Islamic period are unsupported.

### Medieval to eighteenth century: antiquarian survival

Bastet survived indirectly in Greek and Roman literature, descriptions of Egyptian animal worship, local place names, collected antiquities, and European representations of Egypt. Medieval European hostility toward cats did not derive simply from a deliberate Christian inversion of Bastet worship; that popular genealogy lacks documentary support.

### Nineteenth century: rediscovery and dispersal

Egyptology, museum collecting, and excavations restored Bastet to modern visibility. Naville excavated Bubastis in 1887–1889. Meanwhile animal cemeteries were looted, traded, exported, and—in the Beni Hasan case—processed as fertilizer. Scholarly recovery and colonial destruction occurred simultaneously.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries: “the cat goddess”

Museum bronzes, tourism, popular Egyptology, fiction, and modern paganism made Bastet globally recognizable as the elegant black cat or cat-headed protector. This form is genuinely ancient but disproportionately Late Period. Contemporary Kemetic practitioners worship Bastet, although there are no reliable global membership statistics and no institutional continuity from the ancient priesthood.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Egyptology and museum culture

The Gayer-Anderson Cat, donated to the British Museum in 1939, became an archetype for modern Bastet. Museum shops and commercial reproductions convert an exceptional Late Period bronze into a universal symbol of Egyptian religion. The predominance of cat objects in collections reflects both ancient mass production and modern collectors’ tastes.

### Occultism

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century occult movements adopted Egyptian divine names through translations, museum art, Hermetic texts, and the Isis-centered imagination. Bastet was less central than Isis, Thoth, Horus, or Anubis in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, but later ceremonial magic, Thelema-derived eclecticism, Wicca, and goddess spirituality made her a patron of sensuality, independence, protection, sexuality, and cats.

Modern correspondence tables connecting Bastet with a planet, tarot card, chakra, crystal, incense, or fixed ritual day are generally **modern occult constructions**. They may be meaningful within those traditions but should not be described as pharaonic evidence.

### Kemetic reconstruction and neopagan worship

Modern Kemetic religions emerged principally during the late twentieth century, ranging from historically oriented reconstruction to eclectic solitary devotion. Bastet is among their most popular goddesses. Common practices include water, beer, wine, perfume, music, cat imagery, candles, food, and care for living cats.

Some offerings have ancient analogues—beer, wine, incense, music, votive images—but today’s home altars, online rituals, deity “personality profiles,” and direct personal revelation belong to modern religious history. There is no demonstrated apostolic succession from Bubastis.

### Psychological and feminist readings

Jungian, archetypal, and feminist writers interpret Bastet as an image of integrated sexuality, maternal ferocity, feminine autonomy, or the boundary between domesticity and wildness. These are modern interpretive systems, not recovered Egyptian doctrines.

### Literature, comics, film, and games

Materially influential reinventions include:

- Paul Gallico, *Thomasina, the Cat Who Thought She Was God* (1957), and Disney’s *The Three Lives of Thomasina* (1963).
- Neil Gaiman’s fiction and comics, where Egyptian divinities enter modern fantasy.
- Rick Riordan’s *Kane Chronicles*, which presents Bast as a protective cat goddess.
- Marvel’s **Bast**, patron goddess of Wakanda and the Black Panther—an invented transposition linking Egyptian feline divinity with a fictional African nation.
- *Stargate SG-1*, where Bastet belongs to a science-fictional alien-god framework.
- *Smite*, *Age of Mythology*, and related games, which emphasize agility, cats, fertility, or combat.
- Google Arts & Culture’s 2023 *Return of the Cat Mummy*, in which Bastet revives and guides a mummified cat. [Google Arts & Culture](https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/arts-culture/journey-to-ancient-egypt-in-return-of-the-cat-mummy/)
- Contemporary fantasy and science fiction generally use the cat form as immediate visual shorthand. A scholarly reception study notes the recurrent reduction of Egyptian gods to animal-headed icons, including “the cat Bastet.” [Modern reception study](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/aegyp/article/view/105658/104370)

### Commercial and political appropriation

Bastet appears in jewelry, cosmetics, perfume, pet products, tattoos, décor, and branding. The perfume association is often justified by the disputed ointment-jar etymology. Black-cat imagery commonly owes more to modern graphic design than to ancient color conventions.

Unlike some Egyptian symbols, Bastet has not acquired a major, well-documented extremist political appropriation. Unsourced claims that she was a universal prehistoric “Great Goddess,” an extraterrestrial, or proof of a racially exclusive civilization belong to modern ideological or pseudohistorical systems, not the ancient record.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major questions

**1. How early is Bastet?**  
Her name appears on early vessels, but their secondary deposition complicates precise dating. The securely localized Bubastite institution is Old Kingdom. Scholars distinguish the date of an object, the date of its inscription, and the date of its archaeological deposit.

**2. Does the name mean “ointment jar”?**  
Possible, not proven. The jar sign may be phonetic, cultic, or etymological. “Soul of Isis” is not accepted historical linguistics.

**3. When did she become a cat?**  
The older model described a straightforward lioness-to-cat transition, sometimes dated to the Middle Kingdom. Current caution emphasizes ambiguous small felines, gradual domestication, regional overlap, and the survival of leonine Bastet after cat imagery became common. The unmistakable explosion of cat-form Bastet belongs to the first millennium BCE.

**4. Is Bastet the peaceful side of Sekhmet?**  
Ancient texts can fuse or contrast them, but there is no universal doctrine of two halves of one goddess. Bastet herself can be wrathful; Sekhmet can heal and protect. “Sekhmet is anger, Bastet is gentleness” is a useful modern mnemonic, not a full historical account.

**5. Were all Egyptian cats sacred?**  
No. Cats could be pets, vermin-control animals, manifestations, commodities, sacrificial votives, and mummies. Zivie and Lichtenberg explicitly warn that the modern notion that every cat was worshipped and embalmed projects later Western ideas onto Egypt. [Animal-mummy study](https://academic.oup.com/cairo-scholarship-online/book/23151/chapter-abstract/184022466)

**6. Can Herodotus be trusted?**  
His festival report is near-contemporary and partly corroborated by landscape and Egyptian processional evidence, but his divine equation, rhetoric, huge attendance figure, and focus on sexually transgressive behavior require source criticism. He is neither worthless nor a neutral transcription of Egyptian theology.

**7. Did Persians destroy Bubastis?**  
A single complete Persian destruction is not established. Archaeology indicates multiple phases of damage, rebuilding, reuse, and later cult.

**8. Was there temple prostitution or human sacrifice?**  
Neither is documented for Bastet’s institutional cult. Sexualized festival behavior is not the same institution as commercial or sacred prostitution. Animal killing is extensively documented.

### Evidentiary conclusions

The record supports an early leonine royal goddess whose documented history begins long before the familiar cat image. It supports a major Old Kingdom cult at Bubastis, royal maternity in the Pyramid Texts, assimilation into the Eye-of-Ra complex, a dramatic first-millennium expansion of domestic-cat imagery and votive animal production, and extensive Greek and Roman reinterpretation. It does not support a timeless, uniform identity as merely “goddess of cats, home, women, perfume, and the moon”; a literal ancient doctrine that Sekhmet transforms into Bastet; the “soul of Isis” etymology; human sacrifice; a Bastet institution of temple prostitution; or uninterrupted worship from antiquity to modern Kemetic religion.
