# Jarilo: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest secure evidence, *Jarilo* is the name of a seasonal popular festival and its costumed human personification. In nineteenth-century ethnography it also names an effigy or embodied ritual figure associated with sexuality, vegetation, spring–summer transition, and ceremonial burial. Classification as an ancient Slavic **god** is a scholarly and later religious interpretation, not something demonstrated by a medieval text.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Bishop Tikhon of Zadonsk’s 1765 admonition concerning a Voronezh gathering locally called **Ярило** (*Jarilo/Yarilo*). It describes a disorderly post-Trinity festival and a decorated human performer, but supplies no mythology, temple, priesthood, image of an ancient god, or claim of continuous worship from pre-Christian Rusʹ.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The documented figure

Jarilo is best treated as a layered historical object:

1. **Secure eighteenth-century evidence:** a Russian seasonal festival called *Jarilo* and a costumed performer who represented it.
2. **Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century folklore:** spring or early-summer processions, erotic festivities, mock funerals, and effigies named *Jarilo*, *Jarila*, or related forms, especially in parts of Russia and Belarus.
3. **Nineteenth-century mythography:** Jarilo reclassified as a pre-Christian god of the sun, spring, love, vegetation, or fertility.
4. **Twentieth-century comparative reconstruction:** identification with South Slavic *Juraj/Jurij* and sometimes the medieval Pomeranian god Jarovit; reconstruction of a seasonal divine biography and marriage to Mara/Morana.
5. **Modern Rodnovery and popular mythology:** a substantially elaborated god with stable genealogy, iconography, festivals, prayers, and annual death-and-rebirth cycle.

The evidence does not justify silently merging these layers.

### Name forms

Attested and conventional forms include:

- Russian **Ярило**, **Ярила**, transliterated *Jarilo, Iarilo, Yarilo, Yarila*.
- Belarusian **Ярыла**, *Jaryla*.
- Ukrainian **Ярило**, *Jarylo*.
- Polish scholarly and revival usage **Jaryło**.
- South Slavic reconstructed correspondences **Jarilo**, **Juraj**, **Jura**, **Đuro**, and **Zeleni Juraj** (“Green George”). Only the last group is directly attested in Christian-era St George songs and customs; its identity with Jarilo is a scholarly reconstruction.

The suffix *-ilo* can form an agent or personification. The stem belongs to a securely Slavic word-family: Russian *ярый* “fierce, ardent”; *ярь* and agricultural words referring to spring growth or spring-sown grain; cognates of “spring” such as Czech *jaro*. Max Vasmer records *Jarilo* as an effigy or ritual figure and connects it to this *jar-* complex. Thus “ardent one,” “spring-force,” or “vigorous/fertile one” are defensible semantic glosses. A precise ancient theonymic meaning cannot be recovered from the name alone. [Vasmer’s dictionary entry](https://lexicography.online/etymology/vasmer/%D1%8F/%D1%8F%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BB%D0%BE)

Older mythographers proposed remote Sanskrit, Greek *Eros*, solar, and equine derivations. These are products of nineteenth-century comparativism and are not accepted simply because similar sounds can be assembled across Indo-European languages.

### Names that must not be treated as automatic synonyms

- **Jarovit/Gerovit/Herovith:** a securely medieval Pomeranian or Polabian god named in twelfth-century lives of Otto of Bamberg. The shared *jar-* stem and springtime setting have encouraged identification with Jarilo. Gieysztor, Michal Téra, and Roman Zaroff have favored some relationship; Henryk Łowmiański, Stanisław Urbańczyk, Jerzy Strzelczyk, and others regarded identity as unproved or unlikely. Jarovit is earlier evidence for a related divine name, not an early attestation of Jarilo itself.
- **Juraj/Jurij/St George:** South and East Slavic St George customs mark the opening of spring, pasture, and herding seasons. Katičić and Belaj interpret ritual songs about *Zeleni Juraj* as Christianized descendants of a Jarilo complex. The name *George* derives from Greek **Γεώργιος**, not etymologically from *Jarilo*; any relationship is ritual or interpretive, not a linguistic derivation.
- **Kostroma/Kostrubonko/Kostrub:** names of East Slavic ritual figures or effigies subjected to mock death and burial. They are analogues and were sometimes interpreted as local forms of one vegetation spirit, but the performers did not leave a source declaring them identical.
- **Kupalo/Kupala:** a midsummer festival and personification sometimes placed beside Jarilo by mythographers. Similar calendar rites do not establish one divine identity.
- **Svetovid:** a well-attested Rugian god whose temple and white divinatory horse are described by Saxo Grammaticus. Jarilo’s supposed white horse cannot be transferred from Arkona without independent evidence.
- **Dazhbog, Khors, or “the Sun”:** modern and older mythographic equations exist, but no primary text equates any of them with Jarilo.

No securely transmitted ancient epithets or cult titles are known. “God of spring,” “god of fertility,” “young sun,” “lord of vegetation,” and “dying-and-rising god” are scholarly or modern descriptive titles.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 The 1765 Voronezh admonition

The first secure named record is connected with Tikhon, bishop of Voronezh and Elets from 1763 to 1767. His admonition says that near the Moscow Gate a festival occurred “which the inhabitants here call *Jarilo*.” Men, women, older people, youths, and children assembled. A person selected for the gathering was decorated with flowers, ribbons, bells, a tall colored paper cap, painted cheeks, and rattling objects.

The ecclesiastical document attacks drunkenness, dancing, fighting, obscenity, and sexual disorder. It therefore establishes:

- the local festival name;
- a mass gathering;
- a costumed human embodiment or leader;
- a date in the movable post-Trinity season;
- active episcopal suppression.

It does **not** establish:

- a pre-Christian temple or idol;
- prayers addressed to a god;
- a theology of spring resurrection;
- a white horse, grain, skull, Morana, Perun, or Veles;
- continuity from the tenth century.

Later hagiography says that Tikhon confronted the gathering and that it did not recur. The historical core is his 1765 intervention; dramatic details in saintly biographies require the usual source criticism. [1889 compilation of recollections concerning Tikhon](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/36/%D0%97%D0%B0%D0%B4%D0%BE%D0%BD%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B9_%D1%87%D1%83%D0%B4%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B5%D1%86_%D0%B2_%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%8F%D1%85_%D0%92.%D0%A7%D0%B5%D0%B1%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2_%D0%98.%D0%95%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BC%D0%BE%D0%B2_1889.pdf)

### 2.2 Kostroma testimony

Priest and local historian Mikhail Diev wrote in 1836 that Kostroma elders remembered a custom about sixty years earlier, approximately 1766. A ragged old man carried in a small coffin a male doll adorned with ribbons and possessing a conspicuously enlarged phallus. Women sang, lamented, and buried it. This is retrospective oral testimony written roughly seventy years after the supposed event; it is important but less direct than Tikhon’s text.

Later nineteenth-century notices describe *Jarilovka* as a public outing or fair on Jarilo Field in Kostroma, Galich, or Kineshma, sometimes after the burial performance had disappeared. An 1854 provincial report retained the name; an 1899 newspaper described vendors and crowds rather than a functioning ancient cult. Local evidence therefore shows transformation from obscene or fertility-charged mock funeral to ordinary seasonal fair. [Collection of Kostroma descriptions](https://kostromka.ru/zontikov/yarilo/1.php)

V. I. Smirnov’s *Narodnye pokhorony i prichitaniia v Kostromskom krae* (1920) belongs to the regional ethnographic corpus and helps contextualize mock funerals and lamentation; its existence and publication details are catalogued by the Russian State Library. [RSL catalogue record](https://search.rsl.ru/ru/record/01009160623)

### 2.3 Pavel Shpilevsky’s Belarusian account

Pavel Shpilevsky, writing as P. Drevliansky in 1846, published a celebrated description of Belarusian *Jaryla*. A girl was dressed in white, crowned with spring flowers, placed barefoot on a white horse, and given a human head or skull in one hand and rye ears in the other. Other girls danced and sang around her.

This account is the source of most familiar modern iconography: white clothing, bare feet, wreath, horse, grain, and head. It is not an independently preserved ancient image.

Shpilevsky is a problematic witness. His *Belorussian Popular Traditions* blended folklore, literary elaboration, speculative mythology, and invented or systematized beings. The article was published in 1846 rather than recorded in a field notebook whose informants, wording, and circumstances can be checked. Consequently:

- the publication itself is authentic nineteenth-century evidence;
- the described performance may preserve real customary material;
- its exact staging and “deity portrait” cannot be accepted uncritically;
- it is not proof that pagan Belarusians possessed this fully formed iconography.

[Publication history and identification of “P. Drevliansky”](https://www.bestiary.us/books/belorusskiya-narodnyya-predaniya)

### 2.4 Afanasyev and the mythological school

Alexander Afanasyev’s *Poetic Views of the Slavs on Nature* (1865–1869) assembled songs and customs through the nature-myth framework then dominant in European folklore studies. Jarilo became a personification of the fructifying spring sun and male generative force. Afanasyev preserved valuable references, but his equation of ritual characters with solar or vegetation gods is interpretation, not wording supplied by medieval sources.

James Frazer subsequently placed the Kostroma and Jarilo burials within his worldwide “dying vegetation spirit” model in *The Golden Bough*. He explicitly treated the death of the effigy as necessary for vegetal renewal. This became extremely influential in modern retellings, but Frazer’s universal category is not a primary Slavic tradition. [Frazer text](https://www.metrici.com/ebta/titles/the_golden_bough_a_study_of_magic_and_religion/the_golden_bough_a_study_of_magic_and_religion_pdf.pdf)

### 2.5 Medieval corpus: the significant absence

Jarilo does **not** appear in the principal medieval sources for pre-Christian Slavic religion:

- Procopius, *Wars* VII.14, sixth century;
- Thietmar of Merseburg, *Chronicon*, early eleventh century;
- the *Primary Chronicle*, including Vladimir’s 980 Kyiv pantheon;
- the Novgorod birch-bark letters;
- Helmold of Bosau, *Chronica Slavorum*, twelfth century;
- the lives of Otto of Bamberg, except for the distinct Jarovit;
- Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, with its account of Svetovid at Arkona;
- the Polish and Czech medieval chronicles;
- the bylina corpus and Afanasyev’s fairy-tale collection as ordinarily edited.

The absence matters. It does not prove that no related pre-Christian figure existed; it means the named god and his mythology cannot be presented as textually attested medieval doctrine.

Álvarez-Pedrosa’s source anthology assembles the known medieval evidence in Greek, Latin, Old Church Slavonic/East Slavic, Czech, Old Norse, and Arabic. Its scope and critical review reinforce the indirect, late, Christian or Muslim framing of the corpus; Jarilo is not recovered as a member of Vladimir’s pantheon. [Petrukhin’s review](https://slavicworld.ru/index.php/jsw/article/download/361/369)

### 2.6 Długosz, the Zbruch figure, and other false leads

Jan Długosz’s fifteenth-century list of supposed Polish gods is learned interpretatio romana: he aligned names with Roman deities and helped create a systematized “Polish pantheon.” It contains no Jarilo and cannot supply his genealogy.

The Zbruch four-faced stone, normally dated to the ninth or tenth century but disputed in date and interpretation, has no identifying inscription. Calling one face Jarilo is conjecture.

No known birch-bark letter names Jarilo. No medieval icon, temple inscription, theophoric princely name, or excavated idol is securely identifiable as his.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Securely attested functions

The secure sources support a seasonal and social complex rather than a defined theological portfolio:

- late spring or early summer;
- public assembly, dancing, drinking, courtship, and sexual license;
- vegetative and reproductive symbolism;
- an embodied festival leader or effigy;
- mock death, lamentation, burial, or disposal in some regions;
- gradual conversion into a fair or recreational outing.

Calling Jarilo a “fertility god” is a compact interpretation of this cluster. It should not be mistaken for a statement found in a pagan hymn.

### White horse and barefoot youth

The white horse, white garment, bare feet, flower wreath, grain, and severed head or skull all derive primarily from Shpilevsky’s 1846 Belarusian description. The rider was reportedly a girl representing a male figure. Modern artists changed the ritual embodiment into a conventionally handsome young male god.

South Slavic *Juraj* songs sometimes describe a youth arriving on a “green horse.” In dialectal horse terminology *zelenko* can mean a pale gray or dappled horse, hence Katičić’s interpretation as effectively a white horse. Some songs have Juraj walking beside it rather than riding it. [Study quoting and discussing the song variants](https://darhiv.ffzg.unizg.hr/id/eprint/9273/1/Lukec%2C%20Katarina_Usmena%20knji%C5%BEevnost%20u%20Sjevernoj%20Moslavini%20i%20njene%20funkcije.pdf)

This yields three distinct layers:

- **Folklore:** Shpilevsky’s white horse and bare feet.
- **Comparative reconstruction:** the *Zeleni Juraj* horse as Jarilo’s.
- **Modern iconography:** Jarilo permanently portrayed as a barefoot blond youth riding a white horse.

### Phallus

Diev’s account of the Kostroma effigy explicitly gives it an enlarged penis. That is strong evidence for sexual and reproductive symbolism in that local mock funeral, not proof of a pan-Slavic canonical image.

### Grain, greenery, wreath, and dew

Rye ears and the flower wreath occur in the Shpilevsky account. Green branches, fresh growth, dew, and gifts to households occur in St George processional songs. Their assembly into Jarilo’s fixed “attributes” is interpretive.

### Human head or skull

Shpilevsky gives the represented Jarilo a human head, often retold as a skull. Nineteenth-century writers interpreted it as defeated winter, night, or the old year. Comparative scholars have also related it to sacrificial heads or equine rites. No source explains its meaning from within the community, and no actual human killing is documented.

### Attributes without evidence

No ancient source gives Jarilo a canonical weapon, color system, sacred number, planet, zodiac sign, formal temple statue, priestly regalia, or sacred species. Modern lists assigning him gold, red, Sunday, Mars, the sun, oak, wheat, ram, horse, or wolf combine etymology, broader Slavic custom, and modern ritual creativity.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

No primary or medieval source supplies Jarilo with parents, siblings, consort, offspring, servants, enemies, or a rank in a pantheon. Everything resembling a family tree is reconstruction or modern theology.

### Reconstructed Jarilo–Morana genealogy

Radoslav Katičić and Vitomir Belaj reconstructed a seasonal myth from Slavic ritual songs:

- Jarilo/Juraj is a son of the thunder god Perun.
- At birth or early in the year he is carried or taken across the water into the realm of Veles.
- He returns in spring from a distant, watery, or otherworldly land.
- He meets Mara/Morana, interpreted as his twin sister.
- Their incestuous sacred marriage at the summer threshold makes the earth fertile.
- His betrayal or subsequent death ends the fertile phase.
- Morana becomes an aged winter figure and is herself expelled or killed before his next return.

Katičić’s formulation is a philological reconstruction assembled from recurrent phrases and narrative fragments in ritual songs; Belaj integrated it with ritual calendars and sacred landscape. It is not a preserved song that names all four figures and tells this continuous story. A Croatian review accurately describes it as a reconstruction of fragmentary sacred texts and highlights the proposed marriage of Perun’s children Jarilo and Morana. [Review of Katičić’s reconstruction](https://www.matica.hr/vijenac/441/o-mitu-s-pokricem-1162/)

Critics object that the method can select widely separated songs, assume common Proto-Slavic ancestry where borrowing or ordinary wedding diction may suffice, identify Christian Juraj with Jarilo, and assign anonymous “Mara” figures to Morana. The reconstruction is learned, productive, and testable, but it is not equivalent to direct attestation.

### Every documented or proposed god–god interaction

Because no source directly narrates Jarilo meeting another named god, the list necessarily separates reconstruction from documentation.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 The arrival of spring

**Folkloric base:** A youth, girl, or green-clad procession embodies returning spring. Songs announce that greenery, crops, or fertility follow the visitor. In the Belarusian description Jarilo appears on horseback with grain.

**Reconstructed myth:** Jarilo returns from across the sea or from Veles’s otherworld, bringing dew, growth, sexual vitality, and pasture fertility. The famous formula that grass grows wherever his foot falls is consistent with the song complex but should be cited as folklore or reconstruction, not an ancient scripture.

**Modern retelling:** Jarilo is born at the winter solstice, kidnapped by Veles, and rides back through the world on a white horse each spring. The exact birth date, kidnapping scene, and annual itinerary are reconstructions.

### 5.2 The sacred wedding

**Folkloric base:** Spring and midsummer songs contain encounters between a young man and woman, wedding preparations, green groves, water, keys, horses, apples, wreaths, and erotic imagery.

**Katičić–Belaj reconstruction:** Jarilo/Juraj and Mara/Morana are divine twins, children of Perun. Their reunion and marriage join complementary cosmic realms and release fertility. The incest is therefore sacral and cosmological.

**Critical status:** no surviving traditional narrative explicitly says, “Jarilo, son of Perun, married his sister Morana.” The coherent story is the scholars’ reconstruction from distributed fragments. It should be preserved as an important historiographical object, not backdated as a textual myth.

### 5.3 Death and burial

The most securely documented Jarilo “narrative” is enacted rather than told:

- a male effigy is carried in a coffin;
- women sing or lament;
- the figure is buried or otherwise disposed of;
- obscene and phallic elements emphasize generative power;
- the celebration occurs around the end of spring or beginning of summer.

Interpretations differ:

- farewell to spring;
- disposal of exhausted vegetal power;
- transfer of fertility from effigy to fields;
- expulsion of death or harmful force;
- parody of Christian funeral practice;
- youth entertainment gradually detached from ritual belief.

Frazer’s “dying and reviving vegetation god” became dominant in popular literature, but the sources show funeral performance, not a doctrinal claim that Jarilo literally resurrected.

### 5.4 Morana’s revenge and the annual cycle

Modern retellings often say Jarilo is unfaithful, Morana kills him, his body becomes grain, and she ages into winter before being burned or drowned. This is an elegant joining of Jarilo burial customs to Marzanna expulsion rites. Its details are not directly attested together. It is best labeled **twentieth-century comparative reconstruction expanded by modern retelling**.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No institutional cult securely demonstrated

There is no identified Jarilo temple, sanctuary, altar, priesthood, inscription, canonical hymn, oracle, treasury, sacred estate, or votive deposit. The evidence is communal and customary rather than institutional.

### Calendar

Reported observances vary considerably:

- Wednesday or other days after Trinity in Voronezh;
- All Saints’ Sunday or the beginning of the Apostles’ Fast in Kostroma and neighboring regions;
- around 27 April in Shpilevsky’s Belarusian description;
- late June or around the summer turning point in other ethnographic reports;
- overlap with St George’s Day, Semik, Trinity, Kupala, or St Peter’s season.

Variation argues against a single preserved pan-Slavic liturgical calendar. It may reflect regional rites drawn toward movable Christian feasts.

### Ritual forms

Attested or reported forms include:

- selection and decoration of a human representative;
- female processions;
- white or festive clothing;
- flowers, ribbons, bells, greenery, and grain;
- circular dancing and songs;
- public feasting and drinking;
- courtship and sexual license;
- carrying a phallic male effigy in a small coffin;
- mock lamentation and burial;
- transformation into a fair or public outing.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Food, drink, greenery, grain, and the effigy itself may function as ritual offerings in a broad sense. No surviving record establishes a formal animal-sacrifice rule to Jarilo.

There is **no documentary evidence for human sacrifice to Jarilo**. The “human head” in Shpilevsky’s account is a carried object; the Kostroma burial uses a doll; Stravinsky’s maiden is a modern theatrical invention. General medieval reports of human sacrifice among some Slavs cannot be reassigned to Jarilo without a named connection.

### Sexuality and obscenity

The Voronezh condemnation and the Kostroma phallic effigy make erotic and obscene conduct part of the documented record. These rites likely connected seasonal fertility, courtship, and festive license. They do not prove “temple prostitution,” for which there is no evidence.

### Suppression

Tikhon’s 1765 intervention is the clearest documented suppression. Clerical denunciations and administrative pressure helped reduce some events to fairs or extinguish their ritual components. The claim that this was the final destruction of an unbroken pagan cult goes beyond the document.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1765: hypothetical prehistory

The *jar-* vocabulary is ancient, and related seasonal customs may preserve pre-Christian structures. Jarovit’s twelfth-century name shows that a *jar-* divine name existed among western Slavs. Neither fact securely proves a Proto-Slavic god *Jarilo* with the modern biography.

Jarilo’s absence from Vladimir’s 980 pantheon, medieval Rusʹ anti-pagan sermons, and the major western-Slavic chronicles is a major limitation.

### 1765–early nineteenth century: named custom

The Voronezh record identifies a living festival. Diev’s later Kostroma testimony suggests that a phallic mock funeral existed at approximately the same time. Here Jarilo is most securely a rite, festival name, and embodied figure.

### 1840s–1900: ethnography becomes mythology

Shpilevsky supplied the striking equestrian portrait. Afanasyev and other mythologists classified Jarilo as an ancient natural deity. Regional fairs and funerals continued or were remembered, while scholars linked them to Kostroma, Kupala, Kostrub, St George, and comparative vegetation gods.

### 1890–mid-twentieth century: the “dying god”

Frazer’s comparative model internationalized Jarilo as a dying-and-rising spirit of vegetation. Russian and Soviet folklorists alternately interpreted the rites through solar mythology, agrarian magic, class history, survivals, or ritual theater.

### Soviet scholarship

Vyacheslav Ivanov and Vladimir Toporov’s structuralist work sought an underlying Proto-Slavic myth in linguistic oppositions and folklore, especially the Perun–Veles conflict. Boris Rybakov offered ambitious reconstructions of Slavic paganism from archaeology, chronicles, and folklore. Rybakov’s syntheses were influential but are widely criticized for overidentifying artifacts, treating late folklore as direct ancient survival, and building elaborate systems from ambiguous evidence.

Aleksander Gieysztor’s *Mitologia Słowian* offered a more historically comparative synthesis while still accepting that Jarilo may preserve an old divine figure. Later source criticism has emphasized how little direct evidence supports a full cult.

### Katičić and Belaj

From the late twentieth century onward, Katičić reconstructed formulaic Proto-Slavic sacred poetry from cognate ritual songs, and Belaj placed the results in the ritual year and landscape. Their Jarilo–Morana wedding is one of the most sophisticated reconstructions, but also one of the most frequently converted by popularizers from hypothesis into “the Slavic myth.”

### Contemporary revival

Rodnovery emerged through several twentieth-century nationalist, émigré, ecological, folkloric, and esoteric currents rather than by continuous transmission from medieval priesthoods. Contemporary communities differ sharply. Some celebrate *Jarilovo* near the spring equinox or St George’s season, invoke Jarilo in agricultural and erotic fertility rites, create idols, offer bread or drink, and dramatize the marriage to Morana. Others omit him because the historical evidence is late.

Modern representations commonly show a blond, barefoot youth in white riding a white horse, wearing a flower wreath, and holding grain. This image is a revival standardization of Shpilevsky, comparative reconstruction, and modern fantasy art. It is not copied from an ancient statue.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and visual culture

Sergey Gorodetsky’s collection *Yarʹ* and poem “Yarila” participated in the Russian modernist rediscovery of an imagined pagan antiquity. His Jarilo is a literary deity shaped by Symbolist and neo-folkloric aesthetics rather than recovered scripture. [Study of Gorodetsky’s Jarilo](https://czasopisma.uwm.edu.pl/index.php/apr/article/view/4403)

Modern paintings, calendars, fantasy encyclopedias, and online art stabilized the youthful solar rider. Claims that this is “traditional Slavic iconography” are modern inventions.

### Stravinsky’s *The Rite of Spring*

Igor Stravinsky and Nicholas Roerich conceived *Le Sacre du printemps*, premiered in Paris on 29 May 1913, as “Pictures of Pagan Russia.” Its second part culminates in a chosen maiden dancing herself to death before elders. Roerich’s 1913 exposition says the elders hand the victim over to the sun god Jarilo. [Roerich’s 1913 libretto/exposition](https://etikavomne.ru/trydu-rerihov/nk-rerih/work/vesna)

This is historically important reception, not evidence of an ancient Jarilo sacrifice. Stravinsky’s initial vision, Roerich’s archaeological and ethnographic interests, Russian modernism, and contemporary primitivism created a synthetic pagan rite. Taruskin traced the ballet’s materials to folk-song collections and modern scholarly notions of Semik and Kupala, including the burning of an image interpreted as Jarilo. [Taruskin discussion](https://files.cercomp.ufg.br/weby/up/269/o/TARUSKIN_-_Oxford_History_of_Western_Music__Richard_Taruskin_Vol.4a.pdf)

A recent music-theory account describes the creators’ intended victim as a propitiation to the sun god Yarilo; that statement accurately reports the modern dramatic program, not ancient documentary evidence. [Music Theory Online](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.4/mto.22.28.4.straus.html)

The ballet materially altered global perceptions of Slavic religion by associating Jarilo and spring with collective ecstatic violence and maiden sacrifice.

### Occult and esoteric reception

Jarilo did not possess a major Renaissance Hermetic, grimoire, Golden Dawn, or Theosophical tradition comparable to classical, Egyptian, or biblical beings. Modern occult correspondences assigned to him are generally derived from neopagan elemental systems, solar symbolism, and Wiccan seasonal categories. They are modern constructions.

### Political and nationalist appropriation

Slavic neopagan symbolism has been used across incompatible political positions: cultural revival, environmentalism, ethnic nationalism, pan-Slavism, and, in some groups, racialist or far-right ideologies. Jarilo’s image can appear in these settings, but there is no evidence that he functions as a universal political emblem of Rodnovery. Individual organizations and publications must be assessed separately.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Jarilo an ancient god?

Three broad positions exist:

1. **Substantial continuity:** Jarilo is a genuine pre-Christian deity preserved in Christian-era folk rites.
2. **Historical core with reconstruction:** the rites probably preserve an old seasonal sacral figure, but his genealogy and narrative must be reconstructed cautiously.
3. **Late personification:** the evidence securely establishes a festival or ritual effigy; elevation to an ancient god may be a product of nineteenth-century mythography.

The third position best describes what can be demonstrated, while not excluding the second as a hypothesis.

### Jarilo and Jarovit

The argument for identity rests on:

- shared *jar-* “spring/force/ardor” vocabulary;
- divine or ritual male figures;
- spring dates;
- possible agricultural and martial vigor.

Objections are:

- different forms and regions;
- a gap of more than six centuries;
- Jarovit’s medieval martial and civic cult versus Jarilo’s late agrarian folk rites;
- no primary source equating them.

The safest formulation is “possibly related names and functions,” not “the same god.”

### Jarilo and St George

The correspondence is stronger at the level of ritual calendar than name etymology. St George’s spring opening of pasture, horses, wolves, dew, and vegetation provided a Christian framework in which older agrarian customs could persist. Katičić’s equation of *Juraj* with Jarilo is a reconstruction, not a folk gloss preserved in the songs.

### The status of Shpilevsky

Because the white-horse portrait depends so heavily on one romantic-era writer, every use of it should identify him and the date. Repetition in later encyclopedias is not independent corroboration.

### Frazer’s vegetation-god model

Frazer successfully drew attention to ritual death and seasonal renewal but tended to classify diverse customs under one universal drama. Modern scholarship is less willing to infer a dying-and-resurrecting god whenever an effigy is buried, burned, drowned, or mourned.

### Archaeology

No archaeological object is securely attributable to Jarilo. Proposed identifications involving the Zbruch figure, horse imagery, solar signs, or phallic objects are iconographic guesses unless accompanied by an inscription or controlled contextual argument.

### State of the evidence

The record consists principally of:

- one eighteenth-century ecclesiastical denunciation;
- retrospective local testimony;
- nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century ethnography;
- ritual songs recorded after Christianization;
- comparative linguistic and mythological reconstruction;
- modern religious and artistic elaboration.

No manuscript is a “Book of Jarilo,” and no archive preserves a continuous cult dossier.

## 11. Conclusions by evidentiary tier

### Securely attested

- A Voronezh festival called Jarilo existed in 1765.
- It included a decorated human representative and conduct condemned by the bishop.
- Kostroma testimony and later reports document or remember a phallic mock funeral and a *Jarilovka* outing.
- Nineteenth-century writers documented or constructed additional Belarusian imagery.
- The name belongs naturally to the Slavic *jar-* semantic field of ardor, spring, vigor, and spring growth.

### Folklore and regional variant

- Effigy burial and lamentation.
- White garments, flowers, grain, a horse, bare feet, and a head or skull.
- Seasonal courtship, dancing, obscenity, and reproductive symbolism.
- Parallels with Kostroma, Kostrub, Kupala, and St George customs.

### Disputed historical reconstruction

- Jarilo as an actual Proto-Slavic god.
- Identity with Jarovit.
- Survival as St George/Juraj.
- Sonship of Perun and upbringing in Veles’s realm.
- Marriage to Morana.
- A single annual death-and-return myth behind all the rites.

### Modern invention or elaboration

- A fixed canonical genealogy and pantheon rank.
- Ancient temples, priesthoods, scriptures, and standardized idols dedicated to Jarilo.
- A canonical portrayal as a blond young man on a white horse.
- Fixed correspondences with planets, weekdays, colors, runes, or zodiac signs.
- Historical human sacrifice to Jarilo.
- Treating *The Rite of Spring* as a reconstruction of an attested ancient ceremony.

The fullest defensible conclusion is therefore not that Jarilo is “fake,” nor that the modern composite biography is ancient. A seasonal being or personified force is genuinely documented in Russian and Belarusian tradition, but the documentation begins in the eighteenth century. The divine personality now called Jarilo was built by successive acts of interpretation: village ritual, clerical opposition, romantic ethnography, comparative mythology, structural philology, modernist art, and religious revival. Those layers, kept distinct, constitute Jarilo’s actual historical record.
