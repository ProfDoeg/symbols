# Veles (Volos): Research Dossier

**Class:** male god in medieval East Slavic sources; specifically *скотий богъ* (*skotii bogŭ*), “god of cattle/livestock,” in the Rusʹ–Byzantine oath formulas. In later Christian literature the name may denote a pagan idol or demon; in modern reconstruction and Rodnovery, Veles is commonly a chthonic god of cattle, wealth, the dead, poetry, magic, and the underworld.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Povestʹ vremennykh let* (“Tale of Bygone Years” or Primary Chronicle), compiled in Kyiv in the early twelfth century, reports oaths sworn by **Volos, the cattle god**, under the annalistic years 907 and 971. The 971 formula probably preserves tenth-century diplomatic material, but no tenth-century original survives: its earliest manuscript witnesses are medieval chronicle copies, notably the Laurentian codex of 1377 and the early-fifteenth-century Hypatian codex. The 907 “treaty” is partly or wholly a chronicle construction and therefore less secure as an independent tenth-century document. The earliest surviving physical witness is consequently fourteenth-century, while the formula it transmits may be tenth-century. The form **Veles** first appears securely in later medieval literary and ecclesiastical texts.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** the medieval text explicitly says it, although its report may concern an earlier period.
- **[LE] Later canonical or ecclesiastical elaboration:** medieval Christian transmission or reuse.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** geographically limited evidence.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian, chronicler, theologian, or scholar organizes fragmentary evidence into a system.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically proposed or practiced equation with another figure.
- **[F] Folklore:** ethnographic or oral material recorded substantially later than the pre-Christian period.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an explicit ancient or medieval statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** a recent claim, image, ritual, genealogy, or text presented as old without adequate evidence.

The distinction is essential. No surviving pre-Christian Slavic hymn, mythological epic, temple archive, priestly manual, or contemporary image identifies itself as belonging to Veles.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

The documentary core is exceptionally small. The Primary Chronicle twice calls **Volos** a “cattle god” and places him beside Perun in oath-taking. The *Tale of Igor’s Campaign* calls the poet or singer Boyan “Veles’s grandson.” Medieval anti-pagan texts repeat the name among condemned gods; later Czech texts use *Veles* for a devil or malign being. Almost everything now defining the popular image of Veles—lord of a watery underworld, serpent or dragon, opponent of Perun, master of magic and poetry, guardian of the dead, husband or abductor of a goddess—is a comparative reconstruction from those notices, Baltic evidence, Christian saints’ cults, place-names, and much later folklore.

### Documented forms

- **Волосъ — Volosŭ/Volos**: the form in the Primary Chronicle’s oath passages. **[EA]**
- **Велесъ — Velesŭ/Veles**: found in the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign* and medieval ecclesiastical texts. **[LE]**
- **Волос / Велес** in Russian and Ukrainian scholarship; **Велес** in Bulgarian, Macedonian, and Serbian; **Veles** in Czech, Slovak, Slovenian, and Croatian; Polish **Weles** is primarily a scholarly or revival form rather than the name of a securely documented medieval Polish cult. **[LS/modern reception]**
- **Vlos/Wlos**: early-modern Polish and Ruthenian spellings, particularly in chronicle-derived antiquarian accounts. **[LS]**
- **Власий/Vlasii, Vlas, Vlaho**: forms of Saint Blaise’s name. Their resemblance to *Volos* helped facilitate an equation, but Blaise derives from Greek **Βλάσιος, Blásios**; the saint’s name is not simply a linguistic continuation of *Veles*. **[SI]**

The 2024 Croatian study arguing that *Volos* was a Varangian designation imposed on an older Veles is a recent onomastic proposal, not consensus. It usefully reopens the distinction between the names but cannot convert that proposal into an attested tenth-century fact.

### Epithets and titles

- **скотий богъ — skotii bogŭ**, “cattle/livestock god”: the sole securely early functional title. Old East Slavic *skotŭ* could mean livestock and, by extension, movable property or wealth; “god of wealth” is therefore plausible but remains an interpretation of the epithet, not a second explicit title. **[EA/MSR]**
- **Велесовъ внуче — Velesovŭ vnuče**, “grandson/descendant of Veles”: applied to Boyan in the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*. This associates Veles literarily with a singer or poet but does not itself say that Veles is “god of poetry.” **[LE]**
- “Lord of Nav,” “shepherd of the dead,” “god of magic,” “god of merchants,” “god of music,” “shape-shifter,” and “lord of the forest” are modern scholarly, popular, or Rodnover descriptions assembled from inference and reception. None is an early cult title. **[MSR/MI according to formulation]**

### Etymology

There is no agreed etymology.

1. **Baltic and Indo-European death/underworld comparison.** Veles has been compared with Lithuanian *vėlė* “soul of the dead,” *velnias* “devil,” Latvian *velis* “spirit of the dead,” and a wider Indo-European root associated with death, pasture, or the otherworld. This supports a chthonic reconstruction but depends on disputed linguistic relationships. **[MSR]**

2. **Wool/hair/fleece.** Comparison with Slavic *volosŭ* “hair” and *vьlna* “wool” has been connected to shaggy animals, cattle, or fleece. Phonological and semantic routes are disputed. **[MSR]**

3. **Cattle/wealth.** Because *skotŭ* could encompass property, scholars have read Volos as a protector of economic wealth and oath-bound transactions. That function follows reasonably from the sources but does not explain the name by itself. **[MSR]**

4. **Power or possession.** Comparisons with roots behind “rule,” “authority,” or “possess” have been proposed. They have not displaced the competing explanations. **[MSR]**

5. **Veles and Volos as one name versus two figures.** Most mythological scholarship treats them as variants of one god. Some philologists have questioned whether the forms are truly equivalent or whether *Veles* and *Volos* accumulated separate literary meanings. The fact that the oath texts consistently have **Volos**, while the poetic and some ecclesiastical witnesses have **Veles**, is real; a prehistoric unity is reconstructed, not directly demonstrated. The Indiana University discussion explicitly argues for some functional differentiation—Veles as poet/seer-magician and Volos as cattle god—while acknowledging the long scholarly convention of treating them together. **[Historiographical dispute]**

### Cross-cultural identifications

- **Saint Blaise/Vlasii:** cattle protector and likely functional successor in portions of East and South Slavic folk Christianity. Similarity of names strengthened the identification, but Blaise was associated with animals beyond the Slavic world, so not every animal-related Blaise custom is inherited from Veles. **[SI]**
- **Saint Nicholas:** Boris Uspensky argued that Russian Nicholas inherited several functions assigned by reconstruction to Volos/Veles, including livestock and agrarian protection. This is functional succession, not a medieval statement that Nicholas “is Veles.” **[SI/MSR]**
- **Pan/Faunus:** Mikołaj Stryjkowski’s late-sixteenth-century comparison of Vlos with classical pastoral and woodland gods is Renaissance interpretatio, not evidence that pre-Christian Slavs made the equation. **[LS/SI]**
- **Lithuanian Velnias:** a central comparison in Baltic-Slavic reconstruction, especially in Ivanov and Toporov. It is a comparative relationship, not a recorded historical syncretism. **[MSR]**
- **Vedic Vala/Vṛtra or Varuṇa:** comparativists have proposed several mutually incompatible equations. Vala/Vṛtra supports an adversary-of-the-thunderer theory; Varuṇa supports sovereignty, oath, or cosmic-order functions. None is an attested Slavic identification. **[MSR]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Rusʹ–Byzantine oaths

#### The annalistic settlement of 907

The Chronicle says that the Byzantine emperors kissed the cross while Oleg and his men swore according to Rusʹ custom by their weapons, Perun, and **“Volos, the cattle god”**:

> *кляшася оружьемь своимъ, и Перуномъ, богомъ своимъ, и Волосомъ, скотьимъ богомъ*

“they swore by their weapons, and by Perun their god, and by Volos, the cattle god.”

**Tier:** **[EA as an early-twelfth-century chronicle report; disputed as a 907 document].**

The notice is indispensable but not a surviving treaty original. Modern study observes that the supposed separate treaty of 907 may be a chronicle compilation constructed from genuine treaty material, especially the treaty of 911. The Primary Chronicle’s translator likewise noted that the textual history of the treaties is obscure and that the preserved Slavonic versions may be later translations from Greek documents. [The Russian Primary Chronicle](https://www.mgh-bibliothek.de/dokumente/a/a011458.pdf) and modern treaty scholarship therefore require two dates to be kept separate: **907, the event-date claimed by the chronicle; early twelfth century, the date of the extant chronicle compilation.**

#### The oath of Sviatoslav, 971

After Sviatoslav’s defeat in the Byzantine war, the Chronicle preserves an oath promising peace and threatening violators:

> *да имѣемъ клятву от бога, въ его же вѣруемъ, въ Перуна и въ Волоса, скотья бога, и да будемъ золоти, яко золото, и своимъ оружьемь исѣчени будемъ*

In substance: may violators be accursed by the god in whom they believe, by Perun and by Volos the cattle god; may they become “yellow/golden as gold” and be cut down by their own weapons.

**Tier:** **[EA, probably preserving a tenth-century oath formula, transmitted only in later manuscripts].**

“Golden as gold” has been read as jaundice or another wasting discoloration. The curse shows that Volos could guarantee elite political agreements and punish perjury. It does not describe a temple, image, priesthood, animal form, or myth.

#### The treaty of 945

The 945 treaty uses Perun in the pagan oath but does not name Volos. Suggested explanations—different social constituencies, military versus mercantile oath groups, or compositional variation—remain hypotheses. **Absence is significant:** Volos was not an obligatory name in every Rusʹ diplomatic oath.

### Vladimir’s pantheon, 980

The Primary Chronicle says that Vladimir established idols of Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh on a hill outside the princely court. **Volos/Veles is absent.** This is one of the most important negative findings in the dossier.

Later texts sometimes place a Volos idol elsewhere in Kyiv, often “below” near the Podil or Pochaina district. Such spatial opposition—Perun above, Volos below—became a major support for chthonic interpretation, but it is not supplied by the canonical 980 list itself. Accounts describing a Volos idol thrown into the Pochaina belong to later recensions of Vladimir’s Life and require separate dating. **[LE/MSR]**

### The *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*

The Old East Slavic poem, generally dated to the late twelfth century and narrating Igor Sviatoslavich’s campaign of 1185, addresses Boyan as:

> *вѣщии Бояне, Велесовь внуче*

“prophetic Boyan, grandson of Veles.”

**Tier:** **[LE, medieval literary evidence].**

The passage is the strongest basis for associating Veles with song, inspired poetry, prophecy, or magic. Yet its grammar establishes only a metaphorical or mythic descent. Other beings in the poem also have collective “grandsons”; “grandson” can signify affinity, inheritance, or poetic lineage. The original manuscript disappeared in the Moscow fire of 1812; the text survives through the 1800 edition and prior copies. Modern linguistic work strongly favors medieval authenticity, although the poem’s date and transmission have generated a long authenticity controversy. The textual commentary and history are surveyed in the [Fundamental Digital Library of Russian Literature](https://feb-web.ru/feb/slovo/critics/aps/aps-045-.htm?cmd=p) and [Arzamas’s account of the authenticity debate](https://arzamas.academy/materials/154).

### *Word of a Certain Christ-Lover*

The anti-pagan sermon conventionally called *Slovo někojego christoljubca, revnitelja po pravoi věre* is normally assigned in its early form to approximately the eleventh or twelfth century but survives in later manuscripts and redactions. Some copies condemn people who pray to or honor fire, spirits, Mokosh, Perun, and **Volos the cattle god**.

**Tier:** **[LE].**

Its evidentiary difficulty is dependence: the phrase may echo the Primary Chronicle rather than independently report living Volos worship. The text proves that medieval Christian scribes included Volos in an authoritative catalogue of pagan error; it does not automatically prove that every named cult remained active when each manuscript was copied.

### *Walk of the Mother of God through the Torments*

In Slavonic versions of this apocryphon, the damned include those who made gods of “Troyan, Khors, Veles, [and] Perun.” The work is translated and redacted through medieval Slavonic manuscript traditions.

**Tier:** **[LE].**

It confirms Veles as a named pagan divinity in Christian literary memory. It supplies no independent mythology.

### Lives of Vladimir

Later recensions of Vladimir’s Life report that after baptism an idol of Volos was cast into the Pochaina River. The recensions and their relationships are complex; some may preserve lost chronicle material, while others expand the conversion narrative.

**Tier:** **[LE; possible older tradition, not independently datable to 988].**

This is the main literary basis for a Kyiv idol outside Vladimir’s hilltop six-god ensemble.

### Life of Abraham of Rostov

The hagiographic tradition says that Abraham destroyed an idol of Veles at Rostov and built a church, usually of the Epiphany, on its site. The narrative retrospectively situates Abraham in the Christianization era, but its surviving forms are much later and its chronology is unreliable. A published recension explicitly says a church stood “where formerly the idol Veles was.” [The text of the recension](https://www.sedmitza.ru/lib/text/8968692/) demonstrates the medieval/early-modern hagiographic claim, not archaeological proof of a Rostov sanctuary.

**Tier:** **[LE/legend].**

### Czech texts

In the early-fifteenth-century dialogue *Tkadleček*, *veles* is paired with “devil” and “serpent” in an insult or accusation: essentially, “what devil, what Veles, what serpent incited you?” Later Czech proverbial and biblical-adaptation material includes dispatching someone “over the sea to Veles” or asking “what Veles whispered it?”

**Tier:** **[RV/LE].**

The surviving *Tkadleček* witnesses are fifteenth-century manuscripts. These usages show demonization or lexical survival in Czech, but they do not provide a description of a pre-Christian Czech god or cult. [The Czech manuscript description](https://mecz.kreas.ff.cuni.cz/publication/the-weaver/) dates the two principal witnesses.

### Polish and early-modern chronicles

Jan Długosz’s late-fifteenth-century “Polish Olympus” does not securely attest Veles. It systematizes alleged Polish gods through Roman equations and has generated a continuing dispute over which names preserve genuine vernacular material. Mikołaj Stryjkowski, writing in the later sixteenth century, names **Vlos** as a cattle and woodland deity comparable to Pan or Faunus. His notice is chronicle antiquarianism, probably dependent on East Slavic material and classical models.

**Tier:** **[LS].**

It cannot establish a medieval Polish cult of Veles.

### Novgorod birch-bark letters

The published Novgorod birch-bark corpus supplies exceptional evidence for everyday medieval language, belief, charms, and literacy. It does **not** presently provide a secure direct attestation of the god Veles/Volos. Assertions that a particular birch-bark letter proves his cult require a document number and accepted reading; none has become standard evidence. The searchable [birch-bark corpus](https://ruscorpora.ru/en/corpus/birchbark) is therefore important chiefly as a negative control.

### Byliny, dumy, fairy tales, and Afanasyev

East Slavic byliny and the fairy tales collected by Alexander Afanasyev contain dragons, serpents, cattle theft, underground kingdoms, magical musicians, water spirits, forest masters, and saints who protect livestock. None automatically names or preserves a Veles myth. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century mythologists often interpreted these figures as transformations of Veles.

**Tier:** **[F as tales; MSR when assigned to Veles].**

The same caution applies to Ukrainian *dumy*. Similarity of motif is evidence for comparison, not proof that an unnamed character “is Veles.”

### Sources in the item brief that do not attest Veles

- **Procopius, *Wars* VII.14.23–24, sixth century:** describes the Sclaveni and Antes as honoring one god, usually translated as maker of lightning, and sacrificing cattle and other victims. He never names Veles. Identifying that god as Perun is itself an inference; identifying sacrificed cattle with Veles would be still less justified. The manuscript reading and translation are debated. [A dedicated textual study](https://czasopisma.uni.lodz.pl/sceranea/article/download/13159/12758/32304) examines the passage.
- **Thietmar of Merseburg, early eleventh century:** describes cults such as that at Riedegost/Rethra; no Veles.
- **Helmold, *Chronica Slavorum*, twelfth century:** describes Polabian gods and a heavenly “god of gods”; no Veles.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, late twelfth/early thirteenth century:** gives the destruction and cult of Svetovid at Arkona; no Veles.
- **Długosz, fifteenth century:** no secure Veles in his Polish list.
- **The Zbruch idol:** no inscription identifies any face or register as Veles. Assignments to Veles are modern iconographic interpretation. The object itself has been variously dated and interpreted, including as a medieval Slavic image, a Turkic-influenced sculpture, or even a Romantic-era creation. [Slavia Antiqua’s review of the controversy](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/sla/article/view/9903) illustrates how unsettled attribution remains.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Cattle, property, and wealth

**Cattle protection is the only explicitly early domain. [EA]** Because livestock functioned as wealth, exchange value, and movable property, scholars extend the title to wealth and commerce. The oath context strengthens the association with property and contractual order. It does not prove that Veles was a specialized “merchant god.”

### Oaths and punishment

The 971 oath makes Volos a guarantor of political fidelity. The paired curses—yellowing like gold and death by one’s own weapons—have been interpreted as differentiated divine sanctions, perhaps disease from Volos and violent death from Perun.

**Tier:** the oath and its curses are **[EA]**; assigning each curse separately to one god is **[MSR]**.

### Poetry, prophecy, and magic

Boyan’s title “Veles’s grandson” supports an association with inspired song or prophetic performance. Calling Veles the formal “god of poetry, music, sorcery, and wisdom” goes beyond the poem.

**Tier:** Boyan’s association **[LE]**; generalized portfolio **[MSR/modern reception]**.

### Death and the underworld

No early East Slavic text directly calls Veles lord of the dead or names an underworld kingdom belonging to him. This reconstruction draws on:

- proposed Baltic cognates connected with souls of the dead and the devil;
- the later demonic meaning in Czech;
- spatial readings of Veles “below” and Perun “above”;
- pastoral images of the afterlife as a meadow;
- later folklore about cattle, ancestors, household spirits, water, serpents, and saints.

The result is coherent but remains comparative.

**Tier:** **[MSR].**

### Earth, moisture, and agricultural fertility

The underworld reconstruction often makes Veles master of earth, roots, water, rain released after combat, crops, and fertility. North Russian harvest customs called *Volosova boroda* (“Volos’s beard”) or paralleled by *Nikolina borodka* (“Nicholas’s beard”) are important late evidence, but their age and original referent are debated.

**Tier:** recorded customs **[F]**; attribution to pre-Christian Veles **[MSR]**.

### Animals

- **Cattle:** explicit. **[EA]**
- **Serpent/dragon:** central to Ivanov–Toporov but never explicitly attached to Veles in an early source. **[MSR]**
- **Bear:** attached through pastoral, forest, shaggy, or wealth symbolism and modern cult, but no early source calls Veles a bear. **[MSR/MI when presented as certain]**
- **Wolf:** primarily modern or comparative. **[MSR/MI]**
- **Bull:** plausible from cattle patronage; no surviving image identifies a bull as his emblem. **[MSR]**

### Appearance and iconography

There is **no securely identified pre-Christian image of Veles**.

Common modern depictions—an antlered or horned bearded man, a bear-headed god, a serpent coiled among tree roots, a black-robed magician holding a staff, or a horned “Slavic Cernunnos”—are modern visual synthesis. Neither horns nor antlers are documented attributes.

The Radziwiłł Chronicle’s miniatures are sometimes enlisted as iconographic evidence, including a serpent near an oath scene. The manuscript is a fifteenth-century illustrated chronicle; its miniatures are not contemporary tenth-century portraits, and identifying a depicted serpent as Veles is interpretive.

The Zbruch idol cannot be assigned to Veles by inscription. Claims that its lower kneeling figure represents Veles depend on a reconstructed three-level cosmos.

### Colors, days, planets, sacred plants, and numbers

No early source securely gives Veles:

- a color;
- a weekday;
- a planet or star;
- a sacred tree;
- a ritual number;
- horns, staff, key, cauldron, or musical instrument;
- a fixed annual feast.

Modern associations with black, green, brown, Wednesday, Mercury, Saturn, the Moon, the Pleiades, willow, oak, hazel, mushrooms, or the number three belong to modern reconstruction or individual Rodnover systems. **[MSR/MI]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

No medieval source supplies Veles with parents, siblings, consort, or divine children.

Boyan is called his “grandson,” but Boyan is a human singer and the phrase is poetic. It is not a full genealogy. Modern family trees making Veles a son of Rod, brother of Perun, husband of Mokosh, consort of Mara, or father of Yarilo are unattested.

### Pantheon rank

The oath pairing indicates high importance among at least some Rusʹ elites or oath groups. It does not make Veles a universally recognized “second god” of all Slavs. His absence from Vladimir’s 980 list argues against a simple standardized hierarchy.

Ivanov, Toporov, Gieysztor, and others reconstructed a binary organization:

- Perun: sky, height, weapons, princely and military power.
- Veles: earth or lowland, cattle, wealth, magic, and the dead.

This scheme explains several scattered oppositions elegantly but remains **[MSR]**, not a surviving Slavic theology.

### Every documented god–god interaction or joint appearance

1. **Perun — joint oath witnesses.** Volos and Perun are invoked together in the Chronicle’s reports under 907 and 971; violators of the 971 agreement are threatened with divine curses. Source: Primary Chronicle, compiled early twelfth century, preserving or claiming tenth-century formulas. **[EA]**

2. **Perun — adversarial cosmic combat.** Veles steals cattle, wealth, people, or the thunderer’s wife; Perun pursues him with lightning; Veles hides in animals, trees, stones, or water; his defeat releases rain and restores order. No medieval Slavic text tells this episode. It is Ivanov and Toporov’s twentieth-century reconstruction from Baltic folklore, Slavic charms and tales, place and ritual oppositions, and Indo-European comparisons. **[MSR]**

3. **Mokosh — alleged wife, lover, or abducted woman in the combat myth.** The woman in reconstructed variants is not securely named in early evidence. Naming her Mokosh is a further reconstruction. **[MSR]**

4. **Troyan and Khors — joint condemnation, not narrative interaction.** Slavonic versions of the *Walk of the Mother of God through the Torments* list Troyan, Khors, Veles, and Perun among beings whom people made into gods. No action between them is related. **[LE]**

5. **Other members of Vladimir’s 980 pantheon:** no recorded interaction. Veles is not in the list. **[Negative finding]**

6. **Svetovid, Triglav, Svarog, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, Rod, Yarilo, Morana/Mara, and Belobog/Chernobog:** no securely transmitted medieval story depicts an interaction with Veles. Modern pantheon charts do not constitute primary evidence. **[Negative finding]**

### Relations to saints

Saints are not Slavic gods, but their relation to Veles is historically important.

- **Saint Blaise/Vlasii:** functional overlap in livestock protection, reinforced by phonetic resemblance. Icons show cattle gathered around the saint; folk rites include prayers or bread for livestock. **[SI/F]**
- **Saint Nicholas:** in Uspensky’s interpretation, absorbed northern Russian agrarian, animal-protective, and wealth functions. **[SI/MSR]**
- **Saint George:** cattle and spring-pasture customs sometimes overlap with the reconstructed Veles complex, but no early text equates them. **[F/MSR]**
- **Elijah:** often interpreted as Perun’s Christian successor and thereby the Christianized opponent of Veles-like demons or serpents. The resulting Elijah-versus-demon pattern is comparative, not a direct medieval naming of Veles. **[SI/MSR]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The oath-god narrative

The closest thing to an early Veles “story” is historical rather than mythic. Rusʹ rulers make international agreements; pagan participants swear by Perun and Volos; violation will bring discoloring disease and death by their own weapons.

**Earliest source:** Primary Chronicle’s accounts under 907 and 971.

**Function:** divine enforcement of elite agreements.

**Development:** later scholarship distinguishes Perun as weapon-god of the princely retinue and Volos as cattle/wealth god of other social or economic groups. The text itself does not allocate worshippers by class.

**Tier:** oath formula **[EA]**; social bifurcation **[MSR]**.

### B. Boyan, “Veles’s grandson”

The *Tale of Igor’s Campaign* invokes Boyan as an inspired singer capable of ranging in thought like a creature across earth, tree, and sky. Addressing him as Veles’s grandson suggests Veles conferred or embodied his extraordinary poetic knowledge.

**Earliest source:** late-twelfth-century poem, surviving through the lost early-modern manuscript and 1800 edition.

**Variants:** interpretations range from literal mythic ancestry to poetic patronage or conventional heroic descent.

**Development:** Romantic, folklorist, and Rodnover authors expanded one phrase into Veles’s full patronage of poets, musicians, magicians, priests, and storytellers.

**Tier:** phrase **[LE]**; expanded portfolio **[MSR/MI]**.

### C. The “basic myth”: Perun against Veles

The best-known modern Veles myth was reconstructed by Vyacheslav Ivanov and Vladimir Toporov in the 1960s–1970s.

#### Reconstructed form

Veles, placed below or near water and cattle, steals Perun’s cattle, wealth, family member, or wife. Perun attacks from above with thunderbolts. The fugitive hides successively under a human, horse, cow, tree, stone, or in water. Perun strikes the hiding places. Veles is driven into the watery or subterranean realm; rain falls, cattle or fertility return, and cosmic order is restored.

#### Evidence used

- the Chronicle’s joint naming of Perun and Volos;
- Volos’s cattle title;
- Baltic Perkūnas–Velnias narratives;
- East and South Slavic thunder charms against a serpent or demon;
- folklore about God, Elijah, Nicholas, devils, dragons, and cattle;
- place-name and vertical oppositions;
- Indo-European comparisons, especially Indra against Vala or Vṛtra.

#### Criticism

Lev Klejn and other critics emphasized that no source calls Veles a serpent, says he stole cattle, or narrates combat with Perun. The theory can turn a widely distributed thunderer-versus-serpent motif into a specifically Veles myth by circular identification. Christian folklore recorded many centuries later may preserve old structures, but it also contains biblical, hagiographic, and local transformations.

The theory’s strongest claim is structural: a thunderer’s adversary is associated with below, water, livestock, and chthonic power. Its weakest claim is nominal: that the adversary was necessarily called Veles in the pre-Christian Slavic narrative.

**Tier:** wholly **[MSR]**, though built from genuine early and folkloric materials.

### D. Veles as ruler or shepherd of the dead

In this reconstructed cycle, the deceased enter a green, moist pasture in the otherworld, where Veles tends them like cattle. Ancestors, fertility, and wealth arise from the same underground reservoir.

No medieval Veles text tells this story. It derives from Baltic linguistic comparisons, Indo-European pastoral afterlife imagery, and later folk belief.

**Tier:** **[MSR].**

### E. Veles’s beard and the harvest

Ethnographic accounts record the leaving or binding of the last sheaf, sometimes called “Volos’s beard,” alongside analogous names such as “Nicholas’s beard” or “God’s beard.” Scholars interpreted the offering as survival of Veles’s agrarian cult.

The custom is real where documented, but its divine recipient varies, and resemblance of a late name cannot establish uninterrupted practice from the tenth century.

**Tier:** custom **[F]**; pre-Christian continuity **[MSR]**.

### F. The Yaroslavl bear and Volos sanctuary

A late local narrative, recorded or published in the eighteenth–nineteenth century, tells of a Volos sanctuary near Yaroslavl where priests kept a sacred bear; Yaroslav allegedly killed the bear and displaced the cult.

This is not contemporary evidence for Yaroslav’s eleventh-century activity. It is a late foundation legend that influenced popular accounts of Veles as a bear god.

**Tier:** **[medieval/early-modern accretion or late local legend]**, not **[EA]**.

### G. Creation myths and “the three worlds”

Modern narratives frequently assign *Prav* (divine order), *Yav* (manifest world), and *Nav* (world of the dead) to a pre-Christian Slavic cosmology and place Veles over Nav. This tripartite system in its familiar terminology owes much to the forged *Book of Veles* and modern systematization.

**Tier:** **[MI]** in its standard codified form.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Kyiv

The oath passages demonstrate cultic authority but not a sanctuary. Later Vladimir Lives say a Volos idol was thrown into the Pochaina during Christianization. This supports a medieval belief that an idol had existed in Kyiv, possibly in the lower commercial district, but the location and form remain uncertain.

No securely excavated Kyiv structure bears an inscription dedicating it to Veles.

### Rostov

The Life of Abraham of Rostov claims destruction of a Veles idol and replacement with a church. The account belongs to hagiography with unreliable early chronology. No archaeological inscription confirms the dedication.

### Yaroslavl

The sanctuary, priests, and sacred bear belong to late local tradition. A later church of Saint Blaise in a purportedly meaningful location may reflect Christian animal-protection cult, but it does not independently prove the exact pagan predecessor.

### Temples and priesthood

There is no securely documented priestly title unique to Veles, no list of privileges, no temple treasury, and no Veles oracle. Generic Slavic *volkhvy* (“sorcerers,” “magi,” or ritual specialists) appear in Rusʹ sources, but deriving *volkhv* from Volos or treating all *volkhvy* as his priests is disputed.

### Sacrifice and offerings

The Veles oath texts mention no sacrifice. Procopius reports cattle and other animal sacrifice among sixth-century Sclaveni and Antes but does not name the recipient as Veles. Later livestock customs around Saint Blaise, Nicholas, or George may preserve pastoral ritual patterns without providing direct evidence for a Veles rite.

Modern offerings of milk, beer, mead, wool, coins, grain, mushrooms, tobacco, black bread, or animal figurines are contemporary practice, not documented medieval liturgy.

### Human sacrifice

**Dedicated contested-material finding:** no source documents human sacrifice to Veles. Medieval authors allege or describe human sacrifice in certain West Slavic cults, especially in political-religious contexts, but none assigns a victim to Veles. Any statement that Veles’s cult practiced human sacrifice is unsupported.

### Sexual rites or “temple prostitution”

No source documents sexual rites, sacred marriage, or temple prostitution in a Veles cult. Modern erotic or phallic interpretations based on fertility, livestock, supposed links to Norse *Völsi*, or archaeological phalli are speculative and should not be reported as cultic fact.

### Festivals

No pre-Christian calendar date for Veles survives. February dates linked to Saint Blaise, winter “Veles days,” modern February 11/24 observances, and autumn ancestors’ festivals are reconstructions or modern Rodnover conventions.

### Theophoric names and place-names

Toponyms such as **Veles** in North Macedonia, Velesnica, Velestovo, Volosovo, and related formations have been used to argue for broad cult diffusion. Each requires independent linguistic history: some may derive from personal names, common nouns, saints, landscape terms, or unrelated roots. A map of look-alike names is not by itself a cult map.

No securely datable pre-Christian personal name transparently means “devotee of Veles” in the way an explicit dedication would.

### Economics and politics

The oath setting connects Volos to state diplomacy, movable wealth, and sanctions. Beyond that, claims that merchants worshipped him while warriors worshipped Perun are plausible social reconstruction, not stated institutional history.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the tenth century

There is no named contemporary attestation. Procopius’s sixth-century description of Slavic sacrifice and a supreme lightning-making god belongs to the background of Slavic religion but cannot be assigned to Veles.

Linguistic reconstruction may place *Velesŭ* in Proto-Slavic or compare it with Baltic vocabulary, but linguistic ancestry is not the same as documented cult.

### Tenth century: oath deity

The chronicle tradition associates Volos with the political agreements of 907 and 971. The 971 passage is the strongest evidence that a god called Volos functioned as oath guarantor and cattle deity among the Rusʹ.

The ethnic composition of “Rusʹ” in these episodes complicates interpretation. Scandinavian-named elites, Slavic-speaking populations, Finnic groups, and Byzantine diplomatic practice interacted. Calling Volos simply the timeless god “of all Slavs” goes beyond the evidence.

### 980–988: reform and conversion

Vladimir’s 980 pantheon excludes Veles. Possible explanations include local variation, different social constituency, a sanctuary elsewhere, or deliberate selection by the prince. None is proved.

After Vladimir’s conversion, the Chronicle reports destruction of the official idols. Later Lives add Volos to the conversion narrative. This is the beginning of Veles’s documented transformation from worshipped oath-god into defeated pagan idol.

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: Christian literary memory

Anti-pagan sermons catalogue Volos/Veles; the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign* preserves a positive poetic allusion through Boyan. These sources demonstrate that the name remained culturally intelligible, but they do not prove an organized underground cult.

### Later Middle Ages: demonization and localization

Czech *veles* becomes a devilish or malign being. East Slavic hagiography localizes destroyed idols at Rostov and elsewhere. This is Christian interpretatio and foundation legend, not a transparent window onto pagan institutions.

### Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries: learned pantheons

Chroniclers such as Długosz and Stryjkowski created systematic national pantheons using classical analogies, medieval notices, folk material, and authorial inference. Stryjkowski’s Vlos as Pan/Faunus-like cattle and forest god belongs to this phase.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: Romantic reconstruction

Antiquarians, folklorists, and national philologists sought lost Slavic epics behind songs, tales, saints’ customs, and chronicles. Afanasyev’s nature-mythology and later comparative work dramatically enlarged Veles’s domain. Late local legends, including the Yaroslavl bear story, entered reference literature as if they were early records.

### Twentieth century: structural reconstruction

Ivanov and Toporov formulated the Perun–Veles “basic myth.” Roman Jakobson, Aleksander Gieysztor, Boris Rybakov, and others developed related linguistic, Indo-European, archaeological, and functional accounts.

Rybakov’s large syntheses were influential but have been criticized for treating ambiguous archaeology and very late folklore as components of a recoverable unified pantheon. Gieysztor presented the dual organization more cautiously but still relied heavily on comparative structure. His *Mitologia Słowian* remains a major historiographical landmark; the revised Polish edition explicitly situates his arguments in later scholarship. [The University of Warsaw Press edition](https://www.wuw.pl/product-pol-6783-Mitologia-Slowian-EBOOK.html) documents that publication history.

### Late twentieth century to present: Rodnovery

Slavic Native Faith movements emerged from several national, émigré, occult, ecological, folkloric, and nationalist currents and expanded visibly during the late Soviet and post-Soviet periods. Veles became one of the most widely honored deities, often patron of magic, wisdom, poetry, commerce, ancestors, the wilderness, and the underworld.

There is no single Rodnover canon. Groups differ over whether Veles is benevolent, morally ambivalent, a “dark” god, Perun’s complementary partner, or his adversary. They also differ over ritual dates and the authority of the *Book of Veles*. Kaarina Aitamurto notes that Perun, Veles, and Mokosh have deity-specific festival days in contemporary communities, while emphasizing the diversity of Rodnover organization and belief. [Her discussion of modern Rodnoverie](https://ethandoylewhite.blogspot.com/2021/07/interview-with-dr-kaarina-aitamurto.html) and her monograph *Paganism, Traditionalism, Nationalism* are useful modern-religion sources.

Some Rodnover communities are cultural, ecological, nonpolitical, or pluralist; others have intersected with ethnic nationalism, racial ideology, antisemitism, or militant politics. Victor Shnirelman’s research treats those political strands as documented parts of Russian neopagan history, not as characteristics of every practitioner. [*Ancestral Wisdom and Ethnic Nationalism*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250013345_Ancestral_Wisdom_and_Ethnic_Nationalism_A_View_from_Eastern_Europe) provides the comparative argument.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### The *Book of Veles*

The *Book of Veles*—also *Vles Kniga* or the “Isenbek planks”—purports to be a pagan historical-religious text carved on wooden tablets, commonly assigned by its advocates to the ninth century.

The alleged tablets were said to have been found after the Russian Civil War and seen by Yuri Mirolyubov in the possession of Fyodor Isenbek. They were never submitted to an archive or laboratory and reportedly disappeared after Isenbek’s death. Mirolyubov circulated transcriptions; publications began in the émigré milieu in the 1950s. The title itself was assigned in modern publication.

Professional historical linguists classify the work as a modern forgery because its language is an irregular mixture of modern Slavic forms, pseudo-archaic spelling, incompatible phonology and morphology, and inventions inconsistent with the documented development of Slavic languages. Andrey Zaliznyak regarded the linguistic diagnosis as decisive: the text behaves as a modern author imagined antiquity, not as an early medieval language would. [Zaliznyak’s analysis](https://azbyka.ru/otechnik/books/34644-o-velesovoj-knige/) explains the argument.

The work is historically important not as a pagan source but as a modern religious and nationalist artifact. It helped popularize:

- an ancient Slavic sacred scripture;
- the system *Prav–Yav–Nav*;
- expansive pseudo-histories of prehistoric Slavs;
- the idea of an indigenous alphabet predating Christian literacy;
- Veles as custodian of a revealed national wisdom.

**Classification:** **[MI: documented twentieth-century forgery].**

### Occult and esoteric reception

Unlike Egyptian, Greek, or Solomonic beings, Veles has little established place in Renaissance hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, or pre-twentieth-century ceremonial grimoires. Claims for an ancient continuous “Velesian occult order” are unsupported.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century neopagan, witchcraft, chaos-magic, and internet-occult communities have assimilated him to horned gods, tricksters, psychopomps, shamanic masters, Odin, Cernunnos, Hermes, and Hades. These are modern cross-traditional correspondences.

### Literature, music, art, and games

Romantic and nationalist literature made Boyan the archetypal ancestral bard and thereby broadened Veles’s poetic identity. Modern fantasy illustration standardized the horned, antlered, bearded, bear- or serpent-associated form for which there is no securely ancient iconographic model.

Veles appears widely in Slavic-themed fantasy fiction, folk-metal and black-metal music, role-playing games, online mythology projects, tattoos, jewelry, and reconstructed idols. Commercial symbols marketed as “the seal of Veles” or “Veles’s rune” are normally modern designs. No medieval corpus provides a canonical Veles sigil or rune.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

Veles can function as a symbol of ethnic rootedness or pre-Christian sovereignty. The *Book of Veles* has been especially useful to nationalist pseudo-history because it purports to prove great antiquity, literacy, unity, and an “Aryan” Slavic religion. Some extremist uses combine Veles with racialized anti-Christian or antisemitic narratives.

That appropriation is documented, but it must not be generalized to all Rodnovers. Modern Slavic paganism contains competing liberal, conservative, nationalist, apolitical, ecological, reconstructionist, and eclectic currents.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The central methodological problem

The corpus is a handful of medieval notices written or copied by Christians, followed by late folklore and comparative evidence. Scholarship must decide how far scattered items can reconstruct a lost system.

Two broad tendencies recur:

1. **Minimalist/source-critical:** retain cattle, oath, and Boyan associations; treat serpent, underworld, and cosmic combat as unproved.
2. **Comparative-reconstructive:** use Baltic parallels, Indo-European mythology, folklore, linguistic evidence, and Christian substitutions to recover a coherent Perun–Veles opposition.

Neither approach can manufacture a missing pre-Christian narrative text.

### Ivanov and Toporov

Their “basic myth” is the most consequential modern interpretation. Its virtues are explanatory breadth and attention to repeated oppositions: above/below, dry/wet, weapon/cattle, ruler/wealth, thunderer/serpent. Its danger is circularity: once every thunderer’s opponent is identified as Veles, every serpent tale becomes evidence for Veles.

### Jakobson

Roman Jakobson emphasized linguistic, poetic, and comparative evidence and helped establish the image of Veles as more than a livestock specialist. His work remains influential but belongs to the same reconstructive tradition.

### Gieysztor

Aleksander Gieysztor incorporated Dumézilian comparative structures and the Perun–Veles polarity into a broad history of Slavic mythology. His synthesis is foundational but not a catalogue of directly attested facts.

### Rybakov

Boris Rybakov attempted an expansive reconstruction integrating archaeology, chronicles, folklore, seasonal ritual, and the Zbruch idol. Critics argue that he often identified anonymous images too confidently, projected late ethnography backward, and turned possibilities into an organized ancient theology.

### Uspensky

Boris Uspensky studied the Christian transformation of the Veles/Volos complex through Saint Nicholas and related pastoral traditions. His model is valuable for explaining functional continuity, though continuity is not always demonstrable case by case. [Uspensky’s study](https://www.gumer.info/bibliotek_Buks/Culture/usp/02.php) presents the argument.

### Klejn and skeptical criticism

Lev Klejn rejected or sharply limited the “basic myth,” noting that sources never identify Veles as a serpent and never narrate conflict with Perun. His criticism targets the difference between structural probability and textual attestation.

### Álvarez-Pedrosa

Juan Antonio Álvarez-Pedrosa’s *Sources of Slavic Pre-Christian Religion* is especially valuable because it returns attention to the medieval texts in original languages and translation rather than presenting a synthetic mythology as if it were transmitted scripture. [The institutional bibliographic record](https://produccioncientifica.ucm.es/documentos/616e4d71af0fea379e25d59f?lang=en) describes its coverage. His later work with Enrique Santos Marinas likewise distinguishes documented ritual from expansive reconstruction.

### Recent etymological proposals

Milorad Ivanković interprets Volos/Veles from a Vedic perspective as a deity of order and oath rather than a demonic cattle thief, arguing that the oath sources present Perun and Volos as complementary, not hostile. This correctly emphasizes what the treaties actually do, though the Vedic etymological reconstruction remains contested. [The published article](https://www.dlib.si/stream/URN%3ANBN%3ASI%3ADOC-VK5NPCPV/ac29867c-4e0c-454b-a7a9-335dbe1d1a7f/PDF) provides the full case.

### Archaeology and iconography

No archaeological object carries a readable dedication “to Veles.” The Zbruch idol, Radziwiłł miniatures, phallic objects, animal figurines, and alleged sanctuary sites are interpretively mobile. Naming an anonymous figure Veles usually reflects the reconstruction being tested.

### Lost and surviving evidence

- No pre-Christian Slavic Veles manuscript survives.
- No treaty original survives.
- The Primary Chronicle survives in later manuscripts.
- The sole known manuscript of the *Tale of Igor’s Campaign* was destroyed in 1812, though copies and the 1800 edition remain.
- The supposed *Book of Veles* tablets are unavailable and were never scientifically documented.
- Hagiographic Veles narratives survive in later recensions whose textual histories must be reconstructed.
- Archaeological evidence remains anonymous unless supported by inscriptions or unusually specific context.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Veles had a securely known horned or antlered appearance.
- The Zbruch idol certainly depicts him.
- He was explicitly called lord of Nav in an ancient text.
- Mokosh was certainly his wife.
- A complete Perun–Veles epic survives in folklore.
- Every Saint Blaise or Saint Nicholas custom derives from Veles.
- All *volkhvy* were priests of Veles.
- He possessed a historical rune or sigil.
- The *Book of Veles* is an early medieval scripture.
- *Prav–Yav–Nav* is a securely ancient Slavic theological triad.
- Human sacrifice or temple prostitution formed part of his cult.
- A continuous secret Veles priesthood survived Christianization.

All are **[MSR]**, **[MI]**, or unsupported, depending on formulation.

## 11. Sources and Critical Assessment

### Primary and near-primary witnesses

- *Povestʹ vremennykh let* / Primary Chronicle: oath notices under 907 and 971; Vladimir’s 980 pantheon and conversion.
- *Slovo o polku Igoreve* / *Tale of Igor’s Campaign*: Boyan as “Veles’s grandson.”
- *Slovo někojego christoljubca*: anti-pagan catalogue including Volos.
- *Walk of the Mother of God through the Torments*: catalogue including Veles.
- Lives and chronicle lives of Vladimir: later Volos-idol tradition.
- Life of Abraham of Rostov: later claim of a destroyed Veles idol.
- *Tkadleček* and later Czech texts: demonized Veles.
- Stryjkowski: Renaissance comparison of Vlos with Pan/Faunus.
- Procopius, Thietmar, Helmold, Saxo, Długosz, the birch-bark corpus, and the Zbruch idol: essential comparative or negative evidence, but no secure direct Veles attestation.

### Principal modern scholarship

- Juan Antonio Álvarez-Pedrosa, *Sources of Slavic Pre-Christian Religion*.
- Juan Antonio Álvarez-Pedrosa and Enrique Santos Marinas, *Rituals in Slavic Pre-Christian Religion*.
- Aleksander Gieysztor, *Mitologia Słowian*.
- Vyacheslav Ivanov and Vladimir Toporov, studies of the reconstructed “basic myth.”
- Roman Jakobson, work on Veles/Volos, language, poetry, and comparative mythology.
- Boris Uspensky, work on Saint Nicholas and Christian transformations of the Veles complex.
- Boris Rybakov, large archaeological-mythological syntheses, to be read with critics.
- Lev Klejn, skeptical criticism of the Perun–Veles combat reconstruction.
- Andrey Zaliznyak, linguistic demonstration that the *Book of Veles* is a modern forgery.
- Kaarina Aitamurto and Scott Simpson, scholarship on contemporary Rodnovery.
- Victor Shnirelman, research on neopaganism and nationalism.
- Milorad Ivanković and recent onomastic studies, representing ongoing alternative etymological interpretation.

### Final evidentiary judgment

The fullest defensible reconstruction begins with three facts:

1. **Volos was called a cattle god and invoked beside Perun in the Rusʹ oath tradition.**
2. **Veles was remembered in medieval literature as the divine “grandfather” of Boyan and in Christian texts as a condemned god or demon.**
3. **No surviving early source tells a Veles myth, describes his appearance, records his genealogy, or explicitly makes him Perun’s enemy or ruler of the underworld.**

The richer figure familiar today is historically real as a history of interpretation: medieval demonization, saintly substitution, folklore, comparative mythology, Romantic nationalism, structuralist reconstruction, Rodnovery, fantasy, and political appropriation. Its elements should not be discarded, but they must remain attached to the periods and sources that actually produced them.
