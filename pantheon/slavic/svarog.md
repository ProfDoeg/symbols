# Svarog: Research Dossier

**Class:** male deity in the medieval East Slavic gloss that names him; possibly a divine progenitor inferred from the patronymic/diminutive *Svarozhich*, but not securely the universally recognized “supreme creator,” “sky father,” or “chief god” of a historical pan-Slavic pantheon.

**Earliest secure attestation of the exact name:** the East Slavic interpolation identifying Hephaestus as **Сварогъ / Svarog** in the annalistic entry for 1114 preserved in the Hypatian tradition. The compilation underlying that entry is twelfth-century, but the principal surviving Hypatian manuscript is fifteenth-century. Earlier evidence, beginning in the early eleventh century, names **Zuarasici/Zuarasiz**, conventionally normalized as *Svarozhich*; whether this is Svarog’s son, Svarog under a diminutive title, or an independently developed fire/war deity remains disputed.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely or directly attested:** stated by a datable primary source.
- **[LE] Later elaboration:** medieval or early-modern interpretation substantially later than the religion described.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** evidence particular to one Slavic-speaking region.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian or scholarly effort to create an orderly pantheon.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[F] Folklore:** recorded oral tradition, normally many centuries after Christianization.
- **[MI] Modern invention or revival:** unattested as ancient, although potentially meaningful in a modern religion.
- **[X] Syncretistic or comparative identification:** an equation made by a source or scholar, not proof of original identity.

The controlling fact is the poverty of direct evidence. The exact name *Svarog* occurs in one medieval textual complex. No securely identified pre-Christian inscription, temple, idol, prayer, hymn, festival, priesthood, sacrifice, or image of Svarog survives.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Svarog

**[EA]** The relevant East Slavic forms are **Сварогъ**, conventionally transliterated *Svarogŭ* or *Svarog*. Modern forms include Russian and Ukrainian **Сварог**, Belarusian **Сварог**, Polish **Swaróg**, Czech and Slovak **Svarog**, and South Slavic **Svarog/Сварог**. Most are modern normalized forms rather than independent medieval attestations.

In the Hypatian text Svarog is not introduced through a native hymn or myth. He occurs inside a Slavic adaptation of John Malalas’ sixth-century Greek *Chronographia*: the Egyptian ruler-god Hephaestus is glossed as the god “whom the Egyptians call Svarog.” The same passage makes the Sun—Malalas’ Helios—Svarog’s son and glosses him as Dazhbog. The result is an indigenous-looking genealogy embedded in a Christian chronographic account of pagan Egyptian kings.

**[EA/X]** The sole direct classification therefore is:

- a god;
- equated by a medieval East Slavic glossator with Hephaestus;
- father of the deity Dazhbog/Sun;
- associated, through the Hephaestus narrative, with the first forging of weapons and with the imposition of monogamous marriage and penalties for adultery.

Whether those functions were independently remembered properties of a Slavic Svarog or merely consequences of translating Malalas is the central unresolved question.

### Svarozhich

The forms include Latin **Zuarasici** in Thietmar of Merseburg and **Zuarasiz** in Bruno of Querfurt; Old East Slavic **Сварожичь**, modern Russian **Сварожич**, Polish **Swarożyc**, and scholarly *Svarožič/Svarozhich*.

Morphologically, Slavic *-ičь* commonly forms a patronymic, “son of X,” but it can also function as a diminutive or derivative. Thus *Svarozhich* can naturally mean “son of Svarog,” yet grammar alone does not determine whether medieval worshippers understood it genealogically.

The sources attest two distinguishable complexes:

- **[EA/RV—Polabian]** Thietmar’s *Zuarasici*, the principal armed idol at Riedegost/Rethra, connected with priestly divination and war.
- **[EA/RV—Rus’]** the fire called *Svarozhich* in Christian homilies condemning people who “pray to fire, calling it Svarozhich.”

**[SR]** Scholars have variously treated these as one god, as cognate regional manifestations, as “the son of Svarog,” or as separate cultic developments sharing a divine title. None of these solutions is demonstrable.

### Proposed etymologies

No etymology has universal acceptance.

1. **Native Slavic “heat/fire” theory [SR].** Franc Miklošič connected the name with a stem *svar-* meaning heat, burning, or radiance plus a suffix *-ogъ*. Support has been sought in Romanian *sfarog*, “scorched, dried, charred,” probably borrowed from a South Slavic source. The difficulties are that the relevant Slavic lexical family is limited and that the direction and age of the formations are uncertain.

2. **Slavic *svarъ*, “quarrel” [SR].** Comparison with Russian *свара* and related forms is phonologically possible but semantically weak unless one hypothesizes thunder, noise, conflict, or a secondary development. It does not independently explain the god’s supposed sky or smith character.

3. **Indo-Aryan/Sanskrit comparison [SR, disputed].** Sanskrit **svar** denotes sunlight, brilliance, or heaven, while **svarga** denotes heaven or the abode of the gods. The resemblance encouraged derivations of *Svarog* from Indo-Iranian or specifically Indo-Aryan material. A direct historical route from Sanskrit is not demonstrated. Constantine Borissoff argues that Sanskrit *svar/svarga* is phonologically closer than Iranian forms and invokes prehistoric Indo-Aryan remnants in the Pontic region; this is a speculative contact model, not a consensus result.

4. **Iranian derivation [SR, disputed].** Older comparativists frequently invoked Scythian, Sarmatian, or Iranian influence on Slavic religious vocabulary. A major objection is the Iranian change of inherited *s* to *h/x*: Sanskrit *svar-* corresponds to Iranian *hvar-/xvar-*, which does not straightforwardly produce initial Slavic *sv-*. An unspecified Iranian intermediary is consequently insufficient without a demonstrated form and borrowing chronology.

5. **From *sъvariti*, “to weld/forge” [SR].** Modern presentations often derive Svarog directly from a verb meaning “weld” and infer “the Smith.” The semantic fit is attractive, but some relevant verb forms and their technical senses are late, and the argument is vulnerable to circularity: Svarog is called a smith because of Hephaestus, then the name is explained through smithing.

6. **Comparison with Rarog/Raróg [SR].** The fiery falcon or whirlwind being *Rarog* has been connected with Svarog through sound, fire symbolism, or hypothesized loss of initial *s-*. No medieval source identifies them.

### Epithets and titles

There are no securely transmitted native epithets of Svarog. “Heavenly Smith,” “Father of the Gods,” “Lord of Celestial Fire,” “Creator of the World,” “God of Law,” and similar titles are **[SR/MI]**, distilled from the Malalas gloss, comparative mythology, or modern Rodnover theology.

No ancient source gives him a sacred day, color, number, animal, plant, rune, star, planet, or solar emblem.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### John Malalas and the Hypatian interpolation

John Malalas composed his Greek *Chronographia* in the sixth century. The Greek text narrates a euhemerized succession of Egyptian rulers: Hephaestus follows Hermes; iron weapons are introduced; laws governing marriage and adultery are imposed; Hephaestus is succeeded by his son Helios.

**[EA]** The Greek Malalas does **not** contain the names Svarog or Dazhbog. Those names belong to the Slavic transmission.

Under the annalistic year 1114, the Hypatian tradition reports extraordinary objects falling from the sky and then quotes chronographic precedents. Its Malalas material says, in substance:

> After Hermes, Feosta—that is, Hephaestus—reigned; the Egyptians called him Svarog. During his reign smiths’ tongs fell from heaven, weapons were first forged, and before that people fought with clubs and stones. He established a law that each man should have one wife and each woman one husband, punishing adulterers. After him reigned his son named Sun, whom they call Dazhbog. “Sun the king, son of Svarog, who is Dazhbog,” was a mighty man.

**[EA]** This establishes that a medieval East Slavic writer or textual tradition knew the two names and considered Svarog an intelligible equivalent for Hephaestus and Dazhbog for the Sun.

It does **not** securely establish that pre-Christian Slavs told the whole Egyptian story about Svarog. The fallen tongs, invention of weapons, Egyptian kingship, and adultery law all derive from Malalas’ narrative framework. The indigenous contribution may be limited to the divine equations and father-son relationship.

The dating of the gloss is disputed:

- Aleksey Shakhmatov associated the chronographic insertion with the later redactional history of the *Primary Chronicle*, often placed around 1118.
- Viljo Mansikka argued for a later, possibly thirteenth-century layer associated with the western Rus’ or Lithuanian transmission.
- Henryk Łowmiański rejected parts of Mansikka’s reconstruction and treated the glosses as earlier.
- The oldest principal physical witness, the Hypatian Codex, is generally dated to around the 1420s; the Khlebnikov manuscript is sixteenth-century.

Accordingly, “first attested in 1114” is shorthand for the annalistic location and probable twelfth-century textual layer, not the date of an extant autograph.

### Thietmar of Merseburg

**[EA/RV]** Thietmar wrote his Latin chronicle between 1012 and 1018. In Book VI, chapter 23, he describes the stronghold he calls **Riedegost** in the land of the Redarii. Modern scholarship conventionally associates it with Rethra, though its precise archaeological location remains unresolved.

Thietmar says that:

- the stronghold was triangular and had three gates;
- a sacred grove surrounded it;
- a wooden sanctuary rested on animal horns;
- carved images of gods and goddesses appeared outside;
- anthropomorphic idols stood inside, with their names inscribed;
- they wore helmets and cuirasses;
- the foremost was **Zuarasici**;
- banners were carried from the sanctuary into war;
- priests guarded the sanctuary and conducted divination;
- a sacred horse was used in oracular procedure;
- captured standards or enemy spoils could be deposited there.

The passage is direct evidence for an organized Polabian cult of *Zuarasici*. It is not direct evidence that the idol was Svarog, a domestic hearth fire, a smith, or a creator.

### Bruno of Querfurt

**[EA/RV]** In a letter to Emperor Henry II, normally dated to 1008, missionary bishop Bruno of Querfurt contrasts the Christian martyr-soldier Maurice with the pagan **Zuarasiz**. The polemical juxtaposition confirms that a deity of this name mattered politically and religiously among the Liutizi. It supports a martial setting but supplies no genealogy.

### Old East Slavic anti-pagan sermons

**[EA, but difficult to date]** The *Slovo někoego khristoljubca* (“Sermon of a Certain Lover of Christ”) and related compilations condemn Christians who worship Perun, Khors, Mokosh, Simargl, the vila, and fire. A frequently cited reading says:

> “They pray to fire, calling it Svarozhich”  
> *огневи молятся, зовуще его Сварожичемъ.*

The homiletic traditions survive in later manuscripts and exist in multiple recensions, commonly assigned across the twelfth through fifteenth centuries. They prove that Christian authors associated the term *Svarozhich* with illicit reverence for fire. They do not describe a temple, icon, mythology, or priesthood of Svarog.

### Sources that do not name Svarog

These are indispensable controls against over-reconstruction:

- **Procopius, *Wars* VII.14, c. 550–551 [EA]:** the Sclaveni believe in one god, described in the usual reading as maker of lightning and lord of all, and sacrifice cattle and other victims. He does not name the deity. Identifying him as Perun or Svarog is **[SR]**. A textual argument has also been made for construing the Greek as “creator of all” rather than “maker of lightning.”
- **Primary Chronicle, pantheon of 980 [EA]:** Vladimir’s Kiev idols are Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh. **Svarog is absent.**
- **Primary Chronicle, conversion of Rus’ [EA]:** it recounts the destruction of Vladimir’s idols in 988 but does not mention an idol, temple, or suppression of Svarog.
- **Helmold, *Chronica Slavorum*, c. 1167–1172 [EA]:** describes West Slavic cults, including Prove, Chernobog, and the high god of the heavens whose subordinate gods descend from him. It does not name Svarog.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, late twelfth/early thirteenth century [EA]:** describes Svetovid’s sanctuary at Arkona, its priest, horse oracle, treasury, harvest rite, and destruction in 1168. Svarog is absent.
- **Novgorod birch-bark corpus [EA negative finding]:** no accepted Svarog text has emerged.
- **The *Slovo o polku Igoreve* [EA for Dazhbog]:** calls the Rus’ “Dazhbog’s grandsons,” but does not name Svarog.
- **Byliny, Russian fairy tales, Ukrainian *dumy*, and Afanasyev’s nineteenth-century collections [F]:** preserve smiths, fire beings, solar heroes, heavenly marriages, and figures such as Svyatogor. None supplies a securely continuous Svarog narrative.
- **Długosz’s *Annales*, c. 1455–1480 [LS]:** constructs a Polish pantheon by Roman equations. Svarog is absent. Długosz is important evidence for fifteenth-century learned reconstruction, not transparent evidence for an ancient national pantheon.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Smithing and technology

**[EA/X]** Svarog’s smith character derives from his identification with Hephaestus and from the Malalas story that smiths’ tongs fell from heaven and iron weapons began to be forged.

**[SR]** A genuinely native smith god is possible: a glossator normally chooses a familiar equivalent because some semantic overlap exists. But it is equally possible that Svarog was selected because of a broader association with fire, after which Hephaestus supplied the smith detail.

No source says that Svarog forged the earth, sun, gods, humans, wedding rings, Perun’s axe, or a cosmic chain. Those are later reconstructions and modern myths.

### Fire

**[EA for Svarozhich; SR for Svarog]** East Slavic sermons explicitly equate Svarozhich with worshipped fire. If the word means “son of Svarog,” Svarog may have been the progenitor or celestial form of fire. If *Svarozhich* is simply a title of fire, the evidence need not imply a separate father deity.

The common distinction “Svarog = heavenly fire, Svarozhich = earthly fire” is an elegant **[SR]** classification, not a sentence found in a medieval source.

### Sky, sun, and creation

**[SR]** The sky domain rests mainly on the proposed comparison with Sanskrit *svar/svarga*, on Dazhbog’s solar identity, and on comparative models of an inactive Indo-European sky father.

**[EA]** The Hypatian gloss makes the Sun/Dazhbog Svarog’s son. It never calls Svarog the sky.

**[MI]** Claims that Svarog forged or dreamed the universe, created Earth, placed the sun in the heavens, or withdrew after creation are not documented in medieval Slavic sources.

### Marriage and law

**[EA within translated narrative]** The Malalas passage credits Hephaestus/Svarog with instituting monogamy and punishing adultery. This is textual evidence that the medieval compilation applied the story to the glossed figure.

**[SR/F]** Scholars have compared the episode with later East Slavic smiths who magically weld or bind couples and with Saints Cosmas and Damian in folk wedding culture. Such analogies may illuminate a smith–marriage complex, but they do not prove an ancient Svarog wedding cult.

### War

**[EA for Polabian Zuarasici]** Thietmar’s armed idol, standards, war expeditions, priests, and horse oracle place Zuarasici in a martial civic cult.

**[SR]** If Zuarasici is Svarog’s son, this is an interaction of divine genealogy and regional specialization. If he is Svarog under another form, Svarog himself acquired a war cult. The evidence cannot decide.

### Physical appearance and images

No securely identified image of Svarog survives.

Thietmar’s *Zuarasici* was an armed anthropomorphic idol wearing helmet and cuirass, with its name reportedly inscribed on the pedestal. The idol and sanctuary were destroyed or disappeared; there is no surviving drawing.

The **Zbruch idol**, recovered in 1848 and usually dated to the ninth or tenth century, is a four-sided stone figure whose identification remains contested. Rybakov and others assigned its faces and tiers to particular Slavic gods; no inscription names Svarog, and identifying any face with him is **[SR]**. Doubts have also been raised about the monument’s context and even its medieval authenticity, though other specialists defend its early-medieval date.

Nikos Čausidis has proposed that Balkan radiate-headed fibulae and cultic plates express an anthropomorphic firmament and may represent Svarog. This is an explicitly comparative **[SR]**, not an accepted identification.

Modern images of an elderly bearded smith with hammer, anvil, flames, solar wheel, or eight-pointed emblem are **[MI]**.

### Symbols

No medieval source assigns Svarog:

- an eight-pointed “Star of Rus’” or “Square of Svarog”;
- the *kolovrat*;
- a hammer;
- a swastika-like fire emblem;
- an oak, falcon, horse, bull, or ram;
- Tuesday, Sunday, a solstice, or a zodiacal “Circle of Svarog.”

The horse is securely associated with **Zuarasici’s sanctuary and divination**, not identified as Svarog’s personal mount. Animal horns supported the sanctuary but are not called his emblem.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other divine beings

### Genealogy

The only explicit genealogy is:

**Svarog → Dazhbog/Sun**

The Hypatian passage calls the Sun “son of Svarog” and identifies that Sun as Dazhbog. No mother is named.

**[SR]** *Svarozhich* may mean “son of Svarog,” but no narrative states “Svarog begat Svarozhich,” and some scholars identify Svarozhich with Dazhbog. It is therefore unsafe to present Dazhbog and Svarozhich as two securely distinct sons.

No early source names:

- Svarog’s parents;
- a wife or consort;
- Perun, Veles, Stribog, Svetovid, Khors, Lada, or Mokosh as his child or sibling;
- a divine enemy;
- a mount, servant, or priestly messenger.

Modern genealogies pairing him with **Lada**, **Mother Earth**, **Priparchis**, or a goddess called **Svarog’s wife** are **[MI]** unless specifically presented as modern theology.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The reign of Hephaestus-Svarog

**[EA/X]** In the fullest preserved account, Hephaestus—glossed as Svarog—rules Egypt after Hermes. Smiths’ tongs fall from heaven; weapons are forged for the first time; former fighters had used stones and clubs. Hephaestus establishes exclusive marriage and orders punishment for adultery. The Egyptians honor him as a god. His son Sun/Dazhbog succeeds him and reigns as a mighty king.

This is not a free-standing Slavic epic. Its narrative skeleton is Malalas’ Christian euhemerism: pagan gods are represented as mortal ancient rulers. The specifically Slavic layer consists most securely of the substitutions or glosses *Svarog* and *Dazhbog* and their father-son relation.

### The withdrawal of the smith god

**[SR/LE]** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century summaries often state that Svarog created civilization and then withdrew, leaving the world to his sons. The “retired sky god” model owes much to comparative history of religions and to the fact that the source makes his son succeed him. No source describes a voluntary divine retirement or continuing *deus otiosus* theology.

### Svarog forging the sun or world

**[MI]** Numerous modern accounts say Svarog forged the sun, placed it in the sky, hammered the world into shape, or created the first humans. These episodes have no medieval textual source. They creatively expand the Hephaestus equation and Dazhbog genealogy.

### Svarog and the Alatyr stone

**[MI/F conflation]** Modern mythographies sometimes have Svarog strike the *Alatyr* stone, producing divine sparks or gods. The Alatyr motif belongs to later charms and folklore; no historical text joins it to Svarog.

### Cosmic egg, celestial smithy, and world tree

**[MI/SR]** These are comparative mythic constructions assembled from widespread cosmogonic motifs. They may function in contemporary Slavic Native Faith but are not recoverable Svarog narratives.

### The fire Svarozhich

The Rus’ homilies preserve practice rather than story: people pray to fire and call it Svarozhich. No creation, theft-of-fire, sacrifice, marriage, or combat narrative accompanies the statement.

### Rethra’s war oracle

Thietmar preserves a ritual complex rather than a myth. Priests divine by drawing lots and leading a sacred horse across buried spears before military undertakings. Zuarasici presides over the sanctuary as its foremost god. No surviving myth explains how he obtained the horse, armor, banners, or authority.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Direct evidence for Svarog

There is none beyond the literary gloss. No temple, priest, sacrifice, festival, or image is explicitly assigned to *Svarog* by name.

### Rethra/Riedegost

**[EA/RV]** If Zuarasici belongs in Svarog’s dossier, Rethra is the only major institutional cult complex:

- **Location:** territory of the Redarii/Liutizi, probably in present northeastern Germany; exact site disputed.
- **Date:** documented by Thietmar in the early eleventh century and by Bruno in 1008.
- **Structure:** wooden sanctuary in a fortified center and sacred grove.
- **Images:** named, armored anthropomorphic idols.
- **Priesthood:** specialist priests conducted ritual and divination.
- **Oracle:** lots and a sacred horse crossing spears.
- **Politics:** the sanctuary’s banners accompanied war; the cult served the Liutizian federation.
- **Offerings and spoils:** Thietmar mentions gifts and war booty in the sanctuary complex.
- **Destruction:** the center disappears from reliable description amid the political and military collapse of organized Polabian paganism during the eleventh and twelfth centuries. No securely identified remains of the temple have been excavated.

The famous four-horned cup, harvest divination, white horse, and 1168 Danish destruction belong to **Svetovid at Arkona**, not to Svarog or Zuarasici. Combining the Rethra and Arkona ritual systems creates a false composite.

### Household or ritual fire in Rus’

The homiletic evidence suggests offerings or prayer to fire under the name Svarozhich. The sources are condemnatory and terse. They do not establish whether this was:

- a household hearth ritual;
- a drying-barn or granary fire cult;
- sacrificial fire;
- a personified god;
- or a Christian author’s generalized label for several practices.

### Sacrifice and human sacrifice

Procopius reports cattle and “all kinds of victims” offered to the unnamed supreme deity of the Sclaveni. This cannot be assigned to Svarog.

Thietmar and other German chroniclers report sacrifice, warfare, and in some Polabian contexts the killing of Christian captives, but no primary source securely states that humans were sacrificed specifically to Zuarasici or Svarog. Claims of a regular human-sacrifice cult of Svarog are therefore **unestablished**.

There is no evidence of temple prostitution associated with Svarog.

### Sacred calendar and festivals

No premodern source names a feast of Svarog. Modern “Svarog Day,” “Forge Day,” “Day of the Heavenly Smith,” or November observances are **[MI]**, sometimes constructed by aligning Svarog with the folk-Christian smith saints Cosmas and Damian.

### Theophoric names and place-names

Place-names such as Polish *Swarożyn* and similar forms have been cited as evidence of cult. Individual cases are contested because they may derive from personal names such as *Swarota/Swarysz* rather than the deity. They cannot presently carry the weight of proving a widespread Svarog cult.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Sixth century: unnamed comparative background

Procopius describes Sclavene religion, sacrifice, spirits, rivers, and an unnamed sovereign deity. This is the earliest substantial literary account of Slavic religion, but it does not attest Svarog.

### Tenth century: Svarog absent at Kiev

Vladimir’s pantheon of 980 names six deities but not Svarog. This absence is important. It may mean that Svarog was not a major public god at Kiev, that the list was selective, or that the name survived in another form. It cannot prove that he was “too transcendent for an idol.”

### c. 1008–1018: Zuarasici at the center of Liutizian religion

Bruno and Thietmar independently place *Zuarasiz/Zuarasici* in the political religion of the Polabian Liutizi. This is the strongest evidence for a historically active cult related by name to Svarog.

### Twelfth to fifteenth centuries: literary Svarog and condemned fire cult

The Hypatian chronographic interpolation preserves Svarog as Hephaestus and Dazhbog’s father. Anti-pagan sermons condemn prayer to fire called Svarozhich. These sources may retain pre-Christian vocabulary, but they were transmitted and reshaped in Christian literary settings.

### Twelfth century: eradication of organized Polabian cult

German and Danish conquest, Christian missions, and the destruction or conversion of Slavic sanctuaries ended the public temple systems documented by Thietmar, Helmold, and Saxo. No continuous institutional worship of Svarog can be traced through this transition.

### Fifteenth to eighteenth centuries: antiquarian pantheons

Długosz, later Polish and Czech chroniclers, and early-modern antiquarians attempted to match Slavic gods to Roman ones and construct national pantheons. They often imported names across regions, mistook place-names for gods, or expanded short notices into systems. Svarog’s later image developed in this environment even where he was not central to the earliest antiquarian lists.

### Nineteenth century: comparative mythology and folklore

Romantic nationalism and comparative philology transformed the Hypatian notice into the profile of a major “Slavic Vulcan” or celestial smith. Aleksandr Afanasyev interpreted tales and customs as remnants of nature mythology. Miklošič and other linguists debated the name. This period generated many claims still repeated as ancient fact.

### Twentieth century: reconstructed pantheon

Vyacheslav Ivanov and Vladimir Toporov attempted structural reconstruction of Slavic mythology, especially a thunderer-versus-chthonic-opponent pattern. Svarog remained peripheral because he has no preserved role in that central myth.

Aleksander Gieysztor treated Svarog comparatively and with greater awareness of the thin textual record. Boris Rybakov offered ambitious syntheses linking texts, archaeology, embroidery, idols, and seasonal ritual; his reconstructions influenced popular presentations but have been criticized for assigning names and theological systems to ambiguous objects.

### Late twentieth century to present: Rodnovery

**[MI as historical revival; living religion in the present]** Slavic Native Faith movements developed in several countries, especially during the late Soviet and post-Soviet periods, although Ukrainian and Polish precedents are earlier. They are diverse and generally small but publicly visible. Modern communities variously understand Svarog as:

- supreme creator or celestial father;
- divine smith;
- personification of the ordered heavens;
- source or father of other gods;
- transcendent unity behind a polytheistic pantheon;
- one deity among many without supreme rank.

Some Ukrainian Native Faith circles use **Kolo Svarozhe**, “Svarog’s Circle,” for a ritual calendar or cosmological order. The journal *Svaroh* began publication in the Ukrainian revival milieu in 1995. A Russian organization opened a modern “Temple of the Fire of Svarozhich” at Krasotinka in 2015. These are evidence of modern religious creativity, not proof of continuous institutions from antiquity.

Rodnovery ranges from ecological, cultural, and reconstructionist groups to ethnic-nationalist, racialist, or antisemitic currents. Scholarship warns both against denying the documented far-right appropriations and against reducing every adherent or organization to them.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### The *Book of Veles*

**[MI—forgery]** The *Book of Veles* purports to reproduce ancient wooden tablets, but the text becomes traceable only through émigré publication in the mid-twentieth century. Linguistic analysis finds an artificial mixture incompatible with the claimed early-medieval date. It is treated by the relevant academic disciplines as a modern forgery.

Some Native Faith believers nevertheless regard it as scripture, inspired modern myth, or a vessel of tradition. Its use is a real modern religious fact; its testimony is not evidence for ancient Svarog worship.

### Supreme creator theology

The definition “Svarog is the creator of the universe and father of all gods” is **[MI/SR]**. It develops from:

- the Malalas father-son relation;
- proposed “heaven” etymologies;
- comparative *deus otiosus* theories;
- Helmold’s unnamed heavenly high god;
- the desire for a coherent pan-Slavic pantheon.

No source joins those elements explicitly.

### “Svarog’s Square” and modern symbols

The interlaced eight-pointed device sold as **Квадрат Сварога**, “Svarog’s Square,” or “Star of Rus’” has no demonstrated medieval attribution to Svarog. Similar geometric ornaments can occur historically without carrying the modern name or theology. Its identification as his ancient emblem is **[MI]**.

The modern *kolovrat* likewise lacks a historical source assigning it to Svarog.

### Literature and art

Modern fantasy, games, metal music, online mythographies, and nationalist art have standardized Svarog as a muscular or elderly bearded smith carrying a hammer. This visual language derives from generic blacksmith iconography and the Hephaestus comparison.

Svarog appears in modern popular culture including fantasy novels and games, where creators often supply a wife, sons, weapon, cosmic forge, and struggle against chaos. These should be catalogued as individual modern adaptations, not synthesized back into reconstructed antiquity.

### Esotericism and occultism

Svarog has entered post-Soviet “Slavic Vedic,” New Age, Traditionalist, and esoteric systems. Frequent elements include:

- a tripartite cosmos of *Prav, Yav,* and *Nav*;
- divine emanation from an absolute Rod;
- astrological “Circles of Svarog”;
- pseudo-runic sigils;
- assertions of an ancient “Slavic-Aryan Vedic” civilization.

These systems are modern. Some borrow from the *Book of Veles*, Theosophy, Hindu vocabulary, Germanic runic occultism, and twentieth-century racial myth. There is no evidence that the medieval Svarog had a zodiac, runic alphabet, or doctrine of racial descent.

### Political appropriation

Svarog’s name and solar-smith imagery can represent ethnic strength, male craft, sovereignty, or supposed Aryan antiquity. Far-right users coexist with apolitical, ecological, artistic, and pluralistic practitioners. Particular organizations and publications must be evaluated individually.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Svarog a real pre-Christian god?

Three principal positions recur:

1. **Minimalist:** the Hypatian Svarog may be a learned gloss, scribal invention, or obsolete name with no recoverable cult. The absence from Vladimir’s pantheon and the lack of independent East Slavic evidence support caution.

2. **Moderate:** the glossator probably used a genuine traditional name. Its association with Hephaestus and the independent term Svarozhich suggest a deity connected with fire, possibly smithing, and fatherhood of the sun.

3. **Maximalist:** Svarog was an ancient pan-Slavic supreme sky and creator god whose diminished documentation results from withdrawal from active cult or Christian suppression. This model can accommodate much comparative material but exceeds direct evidence.

The moderate position best explains why a glossator could expect the name to communicate meaning, while still recognizing that almost the entire modern biography is reconstructed.

### Svarog and Svarozhich

Arguments for a close relationship include transparent Slavic morphology, the fire associations of the Rus’ Svarozhich, and Svarog’s Hephaestan setting.

Objections are that:

- *-ič* need not preserve a literal mythic genealogy;
- the Polabian god is martial and civic, not demonstrably a smith or domestic fire;
- East and West Slavic sources are separated geographically and institutionally;
- no text says that Zuarasici is Svarog’s son.

### Svarozhich and Dazhbog

Because the Hypatian text makes Dazhbog Svarog’s son, some scholars equate Dazhbog with “Svarozhich,” understood as a title rather than a separate name. This produces a coherent system—Svarog father, Dazhbog/Svarozhich solar-fire son—but no source explicitly says “Dazhbog is Svarozhich.”

### Iranian and Indic dispute

The broader Iranian-influence thesis is plausible in principle because Slavic-speaking populations interacted with Scythian, Sarmatian, and Alan groups and because religious words such as *bogъ* have long figured in contact debates. For *Svarog*, however, an Iranian derivation must overcome the mismatch between initial Slavic *s-* and Iranian *h-/x-*.

The Sanskrit *svarga* comparison has a better superficial sound-and-meaning resemblance but lacks a secure borrowing route. Proto-Indo-European inheritance, prehistoric Indo-Aryan contact, and chance convergence remain competing explanations. Etymology cannot by itself establish a cult, domain, or myth.

### The smith-god reconstruction

The Hephaestus equation is real evidence, but scholars disagree about its direction:

- the glossator selected Svarog because both were smith gods;
- the glossator selected a fire or sky god as the nearest available equivalent;
- later readers turned Svarog into a smith solely because of Hephaestus.

The existing text cannot decide among them.

### Ivanov and Toporov

Their structuralist work reconstructed a Common Slavic conflict between a thunder god, normally Perun, and a chthonic opponent associated with Veles. Svarog has no securely attested place in that narrative. Attempts to insert him as father, judge, cosmic smith, or retired creator are secondary extensions.

### Rybakov

Rybakov’s large syntheses made Slavic paganism intelligible as a developed religious system and were highly influential. Critics object that he frequently converted ambiguous archaeological motifs and late folklore into named ancient gods, sometimes with insufficient control of chronology and region. His identification of figures on the Zbruch idol and other objects should be cited as hypotheses, not archaeological labels.

### Zaroff and institutional cult

Roman Zaroff’s studies emphasize the political and institutional sophistication of Polabian sanctuaries and warn against imagining a uniform pan-Slavic religion. This regional approach is particularly important for Zuarasici: Rethra’s god belonged to a historically specific Liutizian federation.

### Álvarez-Pedrosa

Juan Antonio Álvarez-Pedrosa’s source collection is methodologically important because it presents the medieval testimonies in their original languages and English translation rather than beginning from a synthetic pantheon. Its arrangement makes the documentary limits visible: most Slavic gods are known from hostile, external, or late witnesses, and Svarog from exceptionally little.

### State of the evidence

- The Hypatian Codex is preserved by the Library of the Russian Academy of Sciences; digital editions reproduce the 1908 *Polnoe sobranie russkikh letopisei* text.
- Thietmar’s chronicle survives in a damaged authorial manuscript tradition and later copies and has modern critical editions.
- The original Rethra idols and sanctuary are lost.
- No accepted inscription naming Svarog survives.
- No securely attributed image survives.
- The Zbruch idol is in the Archaeological Museum in Kraków, but its divine identifications remain disputed.
- The *Book of Veles*’ alleged original tablets are unavailable and were never independently authenticated.

## 11. Sources and documentary assessment

### Primary sources

- John Malalas, *Chronographia*, sixth century: source narrative for Hephaestus and Helios, but without the names Svarog and Dazhbog.
- Hypatian Chronicle, entry under 1114: sole extended text naming Svarog.
- Bruno of Querfurt, letter to Henry II, c. 1008: *Zuarasiz*.
- Thietmar of Merseburg, *Chronicon* VI.23, 1012–1018: *Zuarasici* and Riedegost.
- East Slavic anti-pagan homilies: fire called Svarozhich.
- Procopius, *Wars* VII.14, c. 550–551: comparative but unnamed Sclavene deity.
- *Primary Chronicle*, entries for 980, 988, and 1114 in the Hypatian tradition.
- Helmold, *Chronica Slavorum*, c. 1167–1172: comparative West Slavic religion, not Svarog.
- Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum* XIV, late twelfth/early thirteenth century: Arkona and Svetovid, not Svarog.

### Major modern works

- Aleksander Gieysztor, *Mitologia Słowian*.
- Vyacheslav Ivanov and Vladimir Toporov, structural reconstructions of Slavic mythology.
- Boris Rybakov, *Iazychestvo drevnikh slavian* and *Iazychestvo drevnei Rusi*, influential maximal archaeological syntheses.
- Roman Zaroff, studies of Polabian cult and political religion.
- Juan Antonio Álvarez-Pedrosa, ed., *Sources of Slavic Pre-Christian Religion*.
- Juan Antonio Álvarez-Pedrosa and Enrique Santos Marinas, *Rituals in Slavic Pre-Christian Religion*.
- Studies of modern Native Faith by Kaarina Aitamurto, Scott Simpson, Victor Shnirelman, Mariya Lesiv, and later regional scholars.

### Final assessment

The smallest defensible reconstruction is this:

**[EA]** A medieval East Slavic tradition knew a male divine name Svarog, equated him with Hephaestus, and called him the father of the solar Dazhbog. Earlier sources independently attest a deity or sacred fire called Svarozhich/Zuarasici among both Polabian and East Slavic communities.

**[SR]** These facts make an old fire-related divine complex plausible and a literal “son of Svarog” interpretation possible. A connection with smithing may be genuine, but the evidence cannot distinguish inherited function from consequences of the Hephaestus gloss.

**Unestablished:** a pan-Slavic supreme-cult status, a creator myth, a wife, a large divine family, a canonical iconography, a temple explicitly belonging to Svarog, a festival, an ancient emblem, or any narrative conflict with another deity.

**[MI]** The full celestial smith, world creator, father of the pantheon, lawgiver, zodiacal ruler, and bearer of “Svarog’s Square” is principally a product of modern reconstruction and living Rodnover religious creativity. That modern Svarog is historically important, but he is not identical with the extremely thin medieval record.
