# Morana (Marzanna): Research Dossier

> **Class:** Disputed. In Jan Długosz’s fifteenth-century learned system she is a Polish **goddess**, identified with Roman Ceres. In securely documented Czech, Polish, and Slovak customary practice, *Marzanna/Morena/Mařena* is more conservatively classified as a **female personification or effigy of death, winter, disease, and the exhausted season**. Modern Slavic Native Faith generally worships her as a goddess of winter and death.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The underlying rite of carrying “Death” out of a settlement is documented in Czech ecclesiastical prohibitions from the fourteenth century and in the Polish provincial statutes associated with Bishop Andrzej Łaskarz’s synod of 1420. The earliest secure surviving source to name **Marzyana/Marzanna explicitly as a deity** is Jan Długosz’s Latin *Annales seu cronicae incliti Regni Poloniae*, composed from about 1455 until his death in 1480. The frequently cited “thirteenth-century” *Mater Verborum* gloss identifying Morana with Hecate and Proserpina is a nineteenth-century interpolation associated with Václav Hanka, not secure medieval evidence.
>
> **Central evidentiary finding:** A late-medieval spring-expulsion rite is securely documented; a pre-Christian goddess standing behind it is possible but not directly demonstrated. No surviving pre-Christian inscription, temple, idol, theophoric name, or contemporary narrative names Morana. Her familiar modern biography—queen of winter, wife or sister of Jarilo, daughter of Lada, adversary of Vesna, annual traveler to an underworld—is principally comparative reconstruction or modern myth-making.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[SEA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary documentary evidence.
- **[MCA] Medieval Christian-era attestation:** genuine medieval evidence, but produced after conversion and usually by clerics.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an antiquarian or scholarly arrangement that may preserve evidence but interprets it through an imposed scheme.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally documented form or meaning.
- **[F] Folklore:** ethnographically recorded custom, song, expression, or belief.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from comparative linguistics, ritual, or folklore rather than an explicit early text.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern elaboration.**
- **[NR] Modern religious reception:** belief or practice within Rodnovery or another new religious movement.
- **[SYN] Syncretistic identification:** an equation with a foreign deity, dated to the source making it.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identification

The documentary nucleus is not a divine biography but a ritual complex: villagers fashion a usually female figure from straw, rags, or a sheaf; carry it beyond the inhabited boundary; strip, beat, stone, burn, drown, dismember, or abandon it; and return, sometimes carrying evergreen branches, a decorated sapling called *gaik*, *májiček*, or *létečko*. The expelled figure is called “Death,” “Winter,” or a member of the *Marzanna/Morena* name-family. In later Polish historiography and modern mythology, that figure becomes an ancient goddess.

Długosz does not call Marzanna a death goddess. He identifies **Marzyana** with **Ceres**, assigns her agricultural abundance, and places her in a classicizing “Polish Olympus.” Her now-standard dominion over death and winter comes primarily from the effigy’s ritual identity, its names, folk language, comparative interpretation, and modern synthesis.

### Etymology

**The death-root explanation [MSR, strongly supported linguistically].** The main comparison joins the names to the Indo-European and Slavic family represented by Proto-Slavic reconstructions such as *merti* “die,” *mьrtvъ* “dead,” *sъmьrtь* “death,” and *morъ* “pestilence, mass death.” Cognates include Latin *mors* and *mori*, Lithuanian *mirtis* and *mirti*, Polish *śmierć* and *mór*, Czech *smrt* and *mor*, and Russian *смерть*. The reconstructed name forms vary among dictionaries and authors: *Mara*, *Marěna*, *Morěna*, *Morana*, and suffixed developments are proposed. Giuliano Gajetti’s phraseological study usefully assembles the forms but ranges beyond what historical attestation alone can prove.

**The dream/nightmare connection [MSR].** Slavic *mara* can denote a dream, apparition, oppressive nightmare-being, or illusion. Germanic *mare* in English *nightmare* belongs to a comparable supernatural vocabulary. Whether Marzanna and the nightmare-*mara* descend from one personified being, merely converged semantically, or represent several homonymous traditions cannot be established.

**The cold/frost explanation [MSR, weaker].** Polish popular etymology sometimes connects *Marzanna* with *marznąć* “to freeze.” This fits her modern winter character but is generally less persuasive than the death/*mor-* complex as historical etymology.

**Mary derivation [scholarly dispute].** Henryk Łowmiański and others entertained influence from the Christian name Maria/Mary and western European Lent customs. Marian influence on the forms or later ritual setting is possible, especially after centuries of Christian performance, but it does not adequately explain the whole comparative *mor-/smrt-* vocabulary.

**Mars/Mamurius Veturius comparison [MSR, speculative].** The Slovak reduplicated *Marmuriena/Marmoriena* has encouraged comparison with Roman Mars, Mamers, or Mamurius Veturius and Roman “expulsion” rituals. Phonetic resemblance and functional analogy are insufficient to demonstrate borrowing or common origin.

### Syncretistic identifications

- **Ceres — [SYN/LS], Długosz, c. 1455–1480:** Marzyana is the Polish equivalent of the Roman grain goddess. This is the only secure medieval divine equation, but it is a clerical antiquarian interpretation, not evidence that worshippers themselves equated the two.
- **Hecate and Proserpina — [MI presented as medieval]:** “*Morana: Ecate, trivia vel noctiluca, Proserpina*,” attributed to the Czech *Mater Verborum*, belongs to the manuscript’s forged mythological glosses. It entered scholarship after Václav Hanka’s 1827 “discovery.”
- **Demeter/Persephone — [MSR/NR]:** Later writers combine Długosz’s Ceres with the destroyed-and-returning season to produce a Persephone-like cycle. No medieval Slavic source narrates such a myth.
- **Vesna, Kostroma, Kupala, Lada, Mokosh, Baba Yaga — [MSR or MI]:** Modern charts frequently make these beings Morana’s opposite, seasonal form, mother, rival, or cognate. Such equations are not supplied by an early source.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest ritual evidence

**Fourteenth-century Czech lands [MCA].** Ecclesiastical texts condemn the carrying of an image of death out of a settlement during Lent. The Czech Ethnological Encyclopedia dates the earliest claimed parallel to Eisenach in 1286 but warns that the evidence may be forged; Czech attestations become secure in the fourteenth century. These sources attest an *imago mortis* rite, not necessarily a goddess named Morana.

**Poland, 1420 [MCA].** Statutes connected with Bishop Andrzej Łaskarz’s provincial synod instruct clergy not to allow an inherited custom on Laetare Sunday in which an effigy or “idol” is carried out and thrown into water. The wording is hostile clerical evidence but confirms that the practice was sufficiently established to require prohibition. It does not furnish a mythology, priesthood, temple, or genealogy for Marzanna.

### Długosz

**Jan Długosz, *Annales seu cronicae incliti Regni Poloniae*, composed c. 1455–1480 [MCA/LS].** In the opening ethnographic account, Długosz constructs a pre-Christian Polish pantheon through Roman equivalences. He names:

- Jesza as Jupiter;
- Łada as Mars;
- Dzidzilela as Venus;
- Nya as Pluto;
- Marzyana as Ceres;
- Dziewanna as Diana;
- Pogoda as a deity of temperate weather;
- Żywie as a deity of life.

Marzyana is credited with crops or agricultural abundance. Elsewhere Długosz explains the annual drowning of figures as a memorial of the destruction of pagan idols after Poland’s baptism. That etiological explanation is not contemporary with the tenth-century conversion and is unsupported by tenth- or eleventh-century evidence. It is best classified **[LS]**, although it witnesses the rite in Długosz’s own cultural horizon.

Modern evaluation is divided. Aleksander Brückner regarded much of Długosz’s pantheon as a literary manufacture from refrains, common nouns, and classical analogy. Gieysztor, Ivanov and Toporov, and some later Polish scholars considered at least part of the list capable of preserving genuine tradition. Recent work by Leszek Kolankiewicz argues that Długosz may reflect genuine late-medieval non-Christian or syncretistic material while still projecting fifteenth-century phenomena backward into pre-Christian Poland. For Marzanna specifically, the independently attested effigy custom gives Długosz a real vernacular datum; it does not independently validate his Ceres equation or an organized ancient cult.

### Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century continuators

- **Maciej of Miechów, *Chronica Polonorum* (1519/1521) — [LS]:** Repeats and modifies Długosz’s Polish gods and associates Marzanna with Ceres/agriculture.
- **Marcin Kromer, *De origine et rebus gestis Polonorum* (1555) — [LS]:** Repeats the classicizing pantheon and the expulsion/drowning tradition.
- **Maciej Stryjkowski, *Kronika Polska, Litewska, Żmudzka i wszystkiej Rusi* (1582) — [LS]:** Enlarges the synthetic pagan system; useful for reception, not independent proof of antiquity.
- **Jakub Wujek and other Counter-Reformation authors, sixteenth century — [LS/polemical reception]:** “Marzannas” occur in catalogues of demons or former Polish idols.
- **Abraham Frencel/Frenzel, seventeenth century — [LS]:** The reported form *Marzava, dea mortis* belongs to early-modern antiquarian system-building, not an early cult record.

### Sources named in the broader Slavic corpus that do **not** attest Morana

Absence here is significant:

- **Procopius, *Wars* VII.14, Greek, c. 550s — [SEA]:** Describes Sclaveni and Antes worshipping a single maker of lightning, sacrificing cattle and other victims, venerating rivers, nymphs, and other divinities, and making vows in danger. No Morana.
- **Thietmar of Merseburg, *Chronicon*, Latin, 1012–1018 — [SEA]:** Describes the Liutician sanctuary at Riedegost/Rethra, named idols including Zuarasici, priests, lots, a sacred horse, standards, and sacrifice. No Morana.
- **Primary Chronicle, Old East Slavic, compiled early twelfth century; earliest complete witness Laurentian Codex, 1377 — [MCA reporting 980]:** Vladimir’s Kyiv idols are Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh. Morana is absent.
- **Helmold of Bosau, *Chronica Slavorum*, Latin, c. 1160s–1170s — [MCA]:** Records western Slavic cults including Svantevit, Triglav, Prove, Chernobog, and Siwa/Živa. No Morana.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, Latin, late twelfth/early thirteenth century — [MCA]:** Gives the extensive account of Svantevit at Arkona and its destruction in 1168. No Morana.
- **Novgorod birch-bark documents, eleventh–fifteenth centuries — [SEA]:** No accepted Morana theonym.
- **Zbruch idol, usually dated ninth–tenth century but with authenticity and interpretation disputed — [archaeological object]:** No inscription identifies any face as Morana; such an identification would be modern speculation.
- **Byliny, Afanasyev’s nineteenth-century Russian tale collections, and Ukrainian *dumy* — [F]:** They preserve female figures named Mara, Marena, or Marinka in heterogeneous roles, but no textually secure chain makes them the western Slavic Marzanna.
- **Old Russian anti-pagan sermons — [MCA]:** They name or condemn other figures and rites, but supply no secure Morana cult.

### Material support and manuscript situation

There is no Morana inscription, papyrus, coin, dedicated altar, temple inventory, or indisputable pre-Christian image. The evidence is transmitted through Latin ecclesiastical manuscripts, Długosz’s manuscript chronicle tradition, later printed chronicles, early-modern antiquarian works, and nineteenth- and twentieth-century ethnographic documentation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Securely documented domains

**Death and the departing season [MCA/F].** The effigy is expressly called “Death” in Czech, Polish, and Slovak practice. It is removed from the community at the end of winter or during Lent.

**Winter, hunger, and disease [F].** Later songs and ethnographic explanations identify the figure with winter, scarcity, illness, or communal misfortune. These meanings are stronger documentary claims than a fully articulated “goddess of the underworld.”

**Agricultural abundance [MCA/LS].** Długosz’s Ceres equation makes Marzyana a grain/agricultural goddess. It may interpret the spring rite’s obvious agrarian setting, but no independent medieval cultic document confirms her receipt of crop offerings.

**Regeneration [F/MSR].** The removal of death is frequently followed by bringing greenery into the village. This ritual sequence supports a death/life opposition. It does not itself prove that participants believed the same goddess died and was reborn.

### Iconography

The only securely attributable iconography is the **ritual effigy**:

- straw or an unthreshed sheaf formed around a pole or wooden cross;
- female clothing, often old garments or local costume;
- a kerchief or cap;
- necklaces of shells, blown eggs, beads, paper, or ribbons;
- sometimes a painted or textile face;
- in some areas a male companion or male counterpart called “grandfather” or another local name.

Its destruction varies: immersion in running water, burning followed by immersion, dismemberment, beating, stoning, hanging, depositing beyond the boundary, or casting it into another village’s territory. Participants were formerly adults in some reports; girls and children increasingly became the performers.

There is **no securely identified ancient statue or temple image of Morana**. Modern depictions as a pale queen with black hair, a sickle, skulls, ravens, wolves, snowflakes, moon, black dress, scythe, or crystal crown are **[MI/NR]**, though sometimes inspired by the genuine straw figure or by generalized European death imagery.

### Alleged correspondences

No early source assigns Morana:

- a sacred animal;
- a sacred color;
- a celestial body;
- a weekday, month, or number;
- a weapon;
- a canonical emblem;
- a temple form;
- an individual priesthood.

Black, white, silver, crows, ravens, wolves, owls, yew, willow, and the waning moon occur in modern devotional systems but should not be reported as ancient attributes without a practitioner-specific citation.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No medieval or early folkloric source supplies Morana with parents, siblings, consort, or children.

Claims that she is:

- daughter of Lada;
- daughter of Mokosh;
- daughter of Chernobog;
- sister and wife of Jarilo;
- wife of Veles;
- consort of Chernobog;
- mother of winter demons;
- one seasonal face of Mokosh;

are **[MSR]** where derived from a named comparative scholar and otherwise **[MI/NR]**.

### Every documented divine interaction

The early record contains **no narrative in which Morana meets, speaks with, marries, fights, deceives, judges, rescues, or is punished by another named Slavic deity**.

Later and reconstructed relations must therefore be listed separately:

- **Ceres — equivalence, not narrative interaction.** Długosz identifies Marzyana as the Polish Ceres; *Annales*, c. 1455–1480. **[SYN/LS]**
- **Hecate — equivalence in a forged gloss.** “Morana” is glossed as Hecate/trivia/noctiluca in the interpolated *Mater Verborum* material publicized in the nineteenth century. **[MI presented as medieval]**
- **Proserpina — equivalence in the same forged gloss.** No narrative is supplied. **[MI presented as medieval]**
- **Jarilo/Jaryło — seasonal marriage, death, or opposition.** Reconstructed by Ivanov, Toporov, Radoslav Katičić, Vitomir Belaj, and later popularizers from spring and wedding-song complexes; not present as a complete Morana–Jarilo story in a medieval source. **[MSR]**
- **Vesna — winter-versus-spring combat or succession.** Popular in modern Czech, Slovak, and internet retellings; no early text records a meeting or battle. **[MI/NR]**
- **Dziewanna/Devana — seasonal opposition.** Both occur in Długosz’s list, but he records no interaction between them. Pairing them as winter and spring is later. **[MI]**
- **Mokosh — identity, mother-daughter relation, or seasonal aspect.** Comparative or devotional construction; no source records an interaction. **[MSR/NR]**
- **Veles — marriage or underworld partnership.** Modern reconstruction or devotional theology; no early narrative. **[MI/NR]**
- **Perun — opposition or punishment.** Unsupported as a Morana episode. **[MI]**
- **Lada — mother-daughter relation.** Modern synthetic pantheon genealogy. **[MI/NR]**
- **Chernobog — consortship.** Modern occult and fantasy elaboration. **[MI/NR]**
- **Kostroma or Kupala — corresponding seasonal figure or successor.** Comparative juxtaposition, not an attested encounter. **[MSR/MI]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The expulsion and destruction of Death

**Documented ritual narrative [MCA/F].** On a Sunday in Lent—commonly Laetare or “Death Sunday,” with regional variation—a group constructs and dresses a figure. The figure is carried through or out of the settlement while songs announce that Death, winter, hunger, or an unpleasant season is being taken away. Beyond the village boundary it is stripped and destroyed. Participants may run home without looking back. Contact with the figure, falling behind, or stumbling is sometimes treated as dangerous. In some regions the procession returns with green branches or a decorated tree, marking life or summer’s arrival.

**Variants:**

- **Poland [RV]:** *topienie Marzanny*, drowning Marzanna; burning and drowning can be combined. The following *gaik* procession is especially important in Silesian documentation.
- **Czech lands [RV]:** *vynášení smrti*, “carrying out Death”; names include Smrtka, Mařena, Mořena. Green *létečko* or *májíček* may be carried back.
- **Slovakia [RV]:** *vynášanie Moreny*; Morena/Muriena is carried by singing girls, then burned, dismantled, stoned, or cast into water. Central and eastern Slovak performances were commonly on Death Sunday; western and southern ones could occur on Palm Sunday. In some districts a girl put on the effigy’s clothes and collected eggs, bacon, or other food for a communal feast.
- **Gender variant [RV]:** Some communities used a male figure or paired male and female figures. Therefore the custom is not uniformly evidence for a goddess.

**Meaning:** Removal of death, winter, hunger, or disease and invitation of spring are meanings attested in songs and ethnographic testimony. James Frazer’s interpretation as the killing of a vegetation spirit is a comparativist theory, not the participants’ universal explanation.

### Długosz’s conversion story

Długosz connects the drowning of effigies with Mieszko I’s conversion in 966: images of old gods were supposedly broken or drowned, and the annual rite commemorated the event. **[LS/late etiological legend]** No tenth-century source records this command. The rite’s wider central-European distribution also makes a single origin in Mieszko’s baptism unlikely.

### The Jarilo–Morana cycle

In its fullest modern form, Jarilo returns from the land of the dead in spring, marries his sister Morana, is unfaithful or slain, and Morana becomes a terrible old woman who dies at winter’s end. This yields an annual cycle of fertility, betrayal, death, and regeneration.

The cycle is an ambitious **[MSR]** assembled by Ivanov and Toporov and developed in South Slavic scholarship from scattered ritual songs, agricultural language, Indo-European comparison, and figures such as Green George/Juraj. Its individual motifs may be old; the complete Morana biography is not preserved in any medieval manuscript or single folk narration.

### The winter queen and underworld ruler

Modern retellings describe Morana as ruling winter, night, dreams, witchcraft, fate, pestilence, and an underworld called Nav, often alongside Veles or Chernobog. This is **[MI/NR]** unless explicitly presented as the theology of a named modern group. The forged Hecate/Proserpina gloss materially encouraged this expansion.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Temples and cult centers

None is securely known. No site excavated at Kyiv, Rethra, Arkona, Wolin, the Zbruch region, or elsewhere bears an inscription or assemblage identifying Morana. No medieval author names a sanctuary, idol-house, oracle, sacred horse, or permanent priesthood belonging to her.

### Ritual specialists

The documented rite is communal rather than sacerdotal. Girls, youths, adults, schoolchildren, local associations, and folklore ensembles perform it. There is no evidence for an ancient order of “priestesses of Morana.”

### Offerings

Later processional collections of eggs, bacon, coins, and food are documented, but these are commonly provisions or rewards for participants. Calling them sacrifices “to Morana” goes beyond the evidence unless a particular modern community says so.

### Sacrifice and violence

**Effigy destruction [secure]:** burning, drowning, stoning, beating, tearing apart, or abandonment is extensively documented.

**Animal sacrifice [not documented for Morana]:** Procopius and other sources attest sacrifice among early Slavs, but none assigns it to Morana.

**Human sacrifice [not documented for Morana]:** Suggestions that the effigy replaces a drowned maiden, captive, elderly person, or “king of winter” are comparativist conjectures. No text, grave, forensic assemblage, or dedicatory evidence establishes a human sacrifice to Morana. General evidence or allegations of sacrifice elsewhere in Slavic religion cannot be transferred to her.

### Suppression

Medieval church statutes sought to prohibit the carrying out and drowning of Death. This is documented suppression of a customary rite. It is not proof that authorities encountered an intact temple cult. The rite survived partly by occupying the Christian calendar—Laetare, Death Sunday, or Palm Sunday—and by being redescribed as custom, children’s play, seasonal pageantry, or local heritage.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the fourteenth century: unattested figure

Comparative vocabulary and the wide distribution of expulsion rites permit the hypothesis of an older Slavic or central-European background. Yet Morana herself is absent from the sixth- through thirteenth-century narrative sources that name Slavic gods. **Period status: [MSR], not direct attestation.**

### Fourteenth to early fifteenth century: Death-expulsion rite

Church prohibitions in Czech lands and Poland establish the carrying out of an *imago mortis*. The custom belongs to the Lenten calendar but is condemned as inherited superstition. **Period status: [MCA].**

### Later fifteenth century: Marzanna becomes a goddess in writing

Długosz names Marzyana as Ceres and retrospectively inserts her into pre-Christian Polish religion. The real folk rite, classical education, Christian polemic, and national historiography meet in his account. **Period status: [MCA/LS].**

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: canonical antiquarian pantheon

Miechów, Kromer, Stryjkowski, Wujek, and others reproduce and alter Długosz’s list. Marzanna becomes increasingly detachable from the local effigy and available as a literary goddess. Identifications vary among Ceres, Diana, Venus, death, and winter, showing that no stable canonical theology existed. **Period status: [LS].**

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: folklore, nationalism, and forgery

Folklorists document local customs while Romantic nationalism seeks a systematic Slavic mythology comparable to Greece and Rome. Václav Hanka’s forged additions to *Mater Verborum* provide an apparently medieval Morana–Hecate–Proserpina equation. This false witness profoundly enlarges her supposed antiquity and underworld character.

Ethnographers such as Oskar Kolberg and compilers including Alexander Afanasyev collect vernacular materials, but comparative mythographers often combine unlike figures on etymological grounds. **Period status: [F/LS], with major [MI] contamination.**

### Twentieth century: reconstruction and folklorization

Brückner’s criticism sharply reduces confidence in the Długosz pantheon. Gieysztor and structuralists Ivanov and Toporov restore a qualified place for comparative reconstruction. Boris Rybakov proposes elaborate ancient systems from archaeology and folklore; critics fault his readiness to assign uncertain artifacts and customs to specific gods.

Meanwhile, industrialization and urbanization weaken village performance. Slovakia’s traditional-culture encyclopedia reports progressive decline from the 1930s. Schools, museums, tourism bodies, and folklore ensembles then revive or preserve the custom as seasonal heritage. **Period status: [F/heritage revival].**

### Late twentieth century to 2026: religious re-enchantment

Rodnovery/Rodzimowierstwo reinterprets the effigy as a goddess and the rite as explicitly pagan. Some practitioners regard destruction as ritually releasing winter; others object to “killing” a deity and reinterpret the effigy as an unwanted seasonal manifestation rather than the goddess herself. Academic work describes this process as the re-enchantment of folk custom.

No reliable global number of Morana worshippers exists. Slavic Native Faith is decentralized and internally diverse; participation in public Marzanna customs cannot be counted as religious adherence.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Rodnovery

Modern organizations describe Marzanna as a goddess of winter and death and perform burning or drowning at a spring festival, often called *Jare Święto*. This theology combines:

- the documented effigy custom;
- the *mor-/death* etymology;
- Długosz’s divine classification;
- Frazerian seasonal interpretation;
- structuralist Jarilo reconstruction;
- contemporary ecological and cyclical spirituality.

It is a modern religious synthesis, not fraudulent merely because it is new. Its historical claims, however, must be distinguished from its present devotional authority.

### Occultism and witchcraft

Morana has no substantial documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the *Clavicula Salomonis*, the Golden Dawn, classical Theosophy, or early Wicca. Claims of an ancient “Morana grimoire tradition,” fixed planetary correspondences, or continuous covens are unsupported. Her appearance in contemporary witchcraft, deity work, tarot, and online spell systems is predominantly twenty-first-century eclectic paganism.

### Nationalism and politics

Romantic reconstruction of Slavic gods participated in Polish, Czech, Slovak, and wider Slavic national awakenings. Hanka’s manuscript interventions are an especially clear instance of mythology being supplied to meet nationalist expectations.

Rodnovery encompasses apolitical, environmental, liberal, conservative, ethnic-nationalist, and extremist currents. Morana herself is not a documented ideological emblem of one political faction. A general association of Rodnovery with nationalism cannot be converted into evidence that every Marzanna celebration is nationalist or extremist.

### Schools, festivals, and commerce

Today Marzanna is widely made by Polish schoolchildren and local cultural institutions. Czech and Slovak museums stage or document the carrying out of Death. The figure appears in tourism campaigns, craft workshops, fantasy art, novels, music, games, jewelry, and seasonal merchandise. These modern images have standardized her as a beautiful dark-haired “winter queen,” often replacing the grotesque straw figure of ethnographic practice.

### Common modern inventions

The following should not be presented as ancient facts:

- a canonical marriage to Chernobog, Veles, or Jarilo;
- combat with Vesna;
- a daughter named something fixed across Slavic tradition;
- rulership of Nav as queen;
- a sacred raven/wolf;
- a black-and-silver liturgical color scheme;
- a festival fixed to the astronomical equinox;
- resurrection as Kostroma, Lada, or Vesna;
- identity with Baba Yaga;
- worship by a pan-Slavic priestesshood;
- an unbroken organized cult from antiquity to the present.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Marzanna an ancient goddess?

**Affirmative or qualified position:** Her archaic-looking name, broad geographic distribution, personification of Death, agricultural setting, and Długosz’s deity classification may preserve an older divine figure. Gieysztor and some structuralist scholars accept cautious reconstruction.

**Skeptical position:** The earliest secure evidence is for a late-medieval folk effigy; Długosz classicized contemporary custom into a pagan goddess; later writers then elaborated his inference. Brückner’s critique remains important.

**Best-supported conclusion:** The rite and named personification are secure. A goddess is historically possible and was certainly believed in by late-medieval learned writers and modern practitioners, but her pre-Christian cult is unproved.

### Długosz’s credibility

Brückner treated the “Polish Olympus” as largely artificial. Later scholars observed that wholesale dismissal is also unsafe: Długosz knew living customs, and some names have independent linguistic or folkloric support. Recent scholarship tends toward source-by-source discrimination: Długosz can preserve fifteenth-century evidence while remaining unreliable about the tenth-century past.

### *Mater Verborum*

This is the dossier’s clearest case of false antiquity. The codex itself is medieval, and some Czech glosses are genuine. The mythological glosses, including Morana’s identification with Hecate and Proserpina, are products of Hanka’s nineteenth-century activity. Schaeken states that there is no doubt the numerous mythological names are products of Hanka’s imagination. Any work calling this a secure thirteenth-century Morana attestation is methodologically obsolete.

### Etymological expansion

Linguistic relationship does not equal divine identity. *Mara* the nightmare-being, Morana the ritual figure, words for pestilence, and female names may be related without belonging to one coherent prehistoric goddess. Ivanov and Toporov’s structural method is valuable for identifying patterns but can transform analogy into narrative.

### Rybakov and archaeological overidentification

Rybakov’s reconstructions greatly influenced popular Slavic mythology, but critics object to his assignment of ambiguous archaeological motifs to named gods and to treating late folklore as a transparent prehistoric archive. No face of the Zbruch idol can responsibly be labeled Morana on present evidence.

### Gieysztor, Ivanov, Toporov, Katičić, and Belaj

These scholars shifted attention from finding a Greek-style pantheon to reconstructing oppositions and ritual language: upper/lower, wet/dry, summer/winter, life/death, thunderer/chthonic opponent, and seasonal wedding. Their models explain patterns but remain reconstructions. A reconstructed “Proto-Slavic text” is not a recovered manuscript.

### State of the evidence

The strongest sources are:

1. genuine medieval statutes describing the expulsion of Death;
2. Długosz’s fifteenth-century account;
3. sixteenth- and seventeenth-century reception;
4. nineteenth- and twentieth-century ethnographic records;
5. linguistic comparison.

The weakest are:

- forged manuscript glosses;
- unsourced internet genealogies;
- modern fantasy encyclopedias;
- iconographic identifications without inscriptions;
- treating every female *Mara/Marena/Marinka* as one deity;
- projecting modern Rodnover theology backward unchanged.
