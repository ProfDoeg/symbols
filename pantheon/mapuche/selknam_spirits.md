# The Selk'nam (Ona) spirits: Temáukel and the Hain: Research Dossier

> **Tradition and class:** Selk’nam—also written Selk'nam, Shelk'nam, or Shelknam—of Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego. The language belongs to the Chonan family. **Temáukel** is an incorporeal supreme being in the ethnographic record; **Kénos** is a primordial culture-shaper or *howenh*; **Hain** is an initiation institution and ceremonial complex, not a single god; its named figures are spirits, mythic beings, and roles embodied by human performers.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Carlos R. Gallardo’s *Los onas* (Buenos Aires, 1910) securely documents the initiation institution under the name *Klóketen*, although Gallardo denied that the Selk’nam possessed gods or religion in the sense he expected. Antonio Coiazzi’s 1914 account records the myth of former female ascendancy and the initiation ceremony. The earliest securely attributable detailed evidence for **Temáukel by name**, his relation to Kénos, and the full Hain spirit system comes from Martin Gusinde’s Selk’nam fieldwork of 1919–1923, published preliminarily in expedition reports and comprehensively in *Die Feuerland-Indianer, I: Die Selk’nam* (1931). Gusinde photographed the Hain performed for him in 1923.  
> **Critical correction to the supplied brief:** These are **not Mapuche, Huilliche, or Chiloé beings**. Selk’nam and Mapuche are distinct peoples with unrelated languages and different religious corpora. The requested Ercilla–Rosales–Febrés–Havestadt–Augusta–Lenz–Coña–Foerster–Bacigalupo sequence documents Mapuche traditions and cannot serve as primary evidence for Temáukel or the Hain. Comparisons between Temáukel and Mapuche Ngünechen are modern comparative constructions, not evidence of identity, borrowing, or a shared “pantheon.”

## Evidentiary labels

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[EA—early attestation]** Documentary evidence preceding Gusinde’s mature synthesis.
- **[FE—field ethnography]** Testimony, observation, image, or recording collected directly from Selk’nam participants.
- **[LS—learned systematization]** An ethnographer’s organization or interpretation of the material.
- **[RV—regional or informant variant]** A version tied to a locality, territorial division, or particular narrator.
- **[MSR—modern scholarly reconstruction]** A later synthesis based on historical records.
- **[MR—modern reception or retelling]** Artistic, educational, political, or commercial reuse.
- **[MI—modern invention or unsupported claim]** A recent attribution not established in the documentary corpus.
- **[AE—absence of evidence]** A proposition for which the examined sources provide no secure support.

## 1. Identification, names, and classifications

### Selk’nam and “Ona”

**Selk’nam** is the people’s own name in the documentary tradition, subject to considerable variation in European transcription: *Selk'nam, Selknam, Shelk'nam, Shelknam,* and older *Shelk'nam*. **Ona** is an exonym, generally traced to Yaghan usage. Because most historical publications called the people “Ona,” both terms remain important for bibliographic searching. Neither term means “Mapuche.” **[FE/MSR]**

The Selk’nam occupied most of Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego; the culturally and linguistically related Haush or Mánekenk occupied its southeastern extremity. Yaghan and Kawésqar communities inhabited neighboring maritime zones. Relations and cultural parallels among these peoples do not justify combining their mythologies into one pantheon.

### Temáukel

Recorded forms include **Temáukel, Témaukel, Timáukel, Temaukl, Timauk**, and **Pémaukl/Pémaulk**. Orthography varies because Selk’nam had no precolonial writing system and collectors used inconsistent Spanish- and German-based transcription. **[FE/LS]**

Gusinde reported that informants treated *Temáukel* as a proper name whose lexical derivation they did not explain. Consequently, confident internet etymologies should not be accepted. Circumlocutions attributed to Selk’nam speakers include expressions translated as “the one who dwells above/in heaven.” These are better understood as reverential descriptions than as securely fixed cult titles. **[FE; etymology unresolved]**

Classification varies with analytical vocabulary:

- In Gusinde’s account, Temáukel is the **highest being** (*Höchstes Wesen*): eternal, bodiless, omniscient or all-seeing, morally authoritative, and removed from ordinary human affairs. **[FE filtered through LS, 1931]**
- Later museum and popular descriptions often call him the single “god” of a “monotheistic” Selk’nam religion. **[LS/MSR]**
- Anne Chapman describes a more complex field in which Temáukel is the remote source of existence, while the narratively active beings are *howenh* and Hain spirits. Calling that field simply monotheistic or polytheistic imposes categories that Selk’nam terminology does not map neatly. **[MSR]**
- “Supreme creator god of the Selk’nam pantheon,” though common online, is potentially misleading: there was no documented temple pantheon, and Kénos—not Temáukel—performs most acts of earthly formation. **[MR/MI when presented without qualification]**

### Kénos

Forms include **Kénos/Kenos**. He is the first or principal **howenh**, a being of the primordial *hóowin* epoch. A *howenh* is better rendered as mythic ancestor, primordial person, or transforming being than automatically as “god.” According to Gusinde’s synthesis, Temáukel sent Kénos to order the unfinished earth, establish norms, and generate or form the ancestors of humanity. **[FE/LS]**

Kénos is not securely described as Temáukel’s biological son. “Son of Temáukel” is a modern genealogizing simplification. Their connection is commission or delegation, not attested parentage. **[AE/MI]**

### Hain and Klóketen

**Hain** names the great Selk’nam initiation ceremony, its ceremonial lodge, and by extension the ritual complex enacted there. **Klóketen/Kloketen/Klóketn** properly designates an initiate or the initiate’s status, although early observers—including Gusinde in some early usage—sometimes applied it to the whole ceremony. Chapman preferred *Hain* as the indigenous designation for the institution. **[EA/FE/MSR]**

The Hain was simultaneously:

- a male initiation;
- instruction in hunting, endurance, bodily discipline, social duties, kinship, and secrecy;
- a gathering of otherwise dispersed territorial groups;
- a theatrical cycle involving masked and painted embodiments;
- a renewal of the men’s control of ritual knowledge;
- a commemoration of the myth in which men appropriated a ceremony formerly controlled by women;
- a field of humor, erotic display, terror, parody, singing, and women’s active responses—not merely a solemn “religious service.”

### Principal Hain beings

Spellings vary among Gusinde, Chapman, Spanish editions, and modern catalogues.

- **Xalpen/Jálpen/Alpen:** voracious female subterranean being, conventionally treated as mistress of the Hain and wife of Shoort.
- **Shoort/So’ort:** the most active recurring male spirit; seven principal territorial or directional forms are reported, accompanied by subordinate Shoort.
- **Tanu:** towering female being associated with Xalpen, often called her sister in later summaries.
- **Kulan/Kula:** alluring or sexually independent female sky-being whose appearances generate courtship and comic jealousy.
- **Koshménk/Kosmenk:** Kulan’s jealous husband; multiple directional forms could appear.
- **K’terrnen/K’ternen:** the “baby” produced after Xalpen’s announced relations with initiates; the recorded role could be male or female.
- **Matan:** a powerful painted spirit associated with a shamanically controlled appearance.
- **Ulen:** elegant comic or clown-like figure.
- **Halaháches:** paired or multiple beings appearing in a dramatic episode.
- **Olum:** a beneficial figure involved in restoring initiates after their represented killing.
- **Hashé:** messenger or herald of Xalpen.
- **Hayílan, Hóshtan, Wakus/Wácus, Waash-Heuwan, Kotaix** and others: less uniformly described figures or performance roles.

These names do not form a stable “pantheon list” comparable to a classical divine genealogy. Their documentary identities depend on particular performances, narrators, and ethnographers. **[FE/RV]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and documentary corpus

### Before 1910

European voyagers encountered Fuegian peoples from the sixteenth century onward, but early descriptions usually failed to distinguish Selk’nam ritual systems precisely. They contain observations about bodies, fires, camps, hunting, and appearance, not secure attestations of Temáukel or named Hain spirits. Archaeology establishes a long Indigenous history in Tierra del Fuego, but it cannot independently identify a site, pigment, structure, or artefact as belonging to “Temáukel” or “Xalpen.” **[AE]**

No precolonial inscriptions, scriptures, temples, coins, or indigenous manuscripts name these beings. The tradition was transmitted orally and performatively.

### Gallardo, 1910

Carlos R. Gallardo’s Spanish-language *Los onas: Tierra del Fuego* is the first substantial published Selk’nam ethnography securely available for this dossier. It describes the *Klóketen* institution and initiation traditions. Its importance is sharpened by an interpretive failure: Gallardo wrote that the Selk’nam had no religion, gods, supreme power, or genuine belief in the spirits used to frighten women. Gusinde later quoted and explicitly rejected this conclusion. **[EA]**

Gallardo therefore proves both the ceremony’s early documentation and the danger of treating an observer’s conceptual denial as an Indigenous statement.

### Coiazzi, 1914

Salesian Antonio Coiazzi’s *Los indios del archipiélago fueguino*, published in the *Revista Chilena de Historia y Geografía* in 1914, records the female-dominance myth and Klóketen material. It is an early printed witness to the story later elaborated by Bridges, Gusinde, and Chapman. Coiazzi’s position within the missionary enterprise requires source criticism, but his account precedes Gusinde’s field synthesis. **[EA]**

### Gusinde’s expeditions, 1919–1924

Martin Gusinde, a Catholic priest and ethnologist connected with the Vienna school of Wilhelm Schmidt, made four Fuegian expeditions in 1919, 1920, 1922, and 1924. His Selk’nam work included linguistic elicitation, mythology, social organization, ritual description, photography, and participation in the 1923 Hain. His preliminary expedition reports already challenged Gallardo’s denial of Selk’nam religion. **[FE]**

The decisive publication is *Die Feuerland-Indianer: Ergebnisse meiner vier Reisen in den Jahren 1918 bis 1924*, vol. I, *Die Selk’nam* (Mödling, 1931). Nearly 300 pages concern the initiation complex. It is the fullest single primary ethnographic record, but it is not a transparent transcript: Gusinde selected, translated, classified, and theologized testimony. **[FE/LS]**

### The 1923 Hain and photographs

The Hain Gusinde attended and photographed in 1923 lasted approximately fifty days; Chapman reports that a preceding ceremony lasted around four months and that older Hains could continue, with changes of site, for far longer. The 1923 lodge reportedly measured about eight metres in diameter and six metres high, with a broad east-facing entrance and seven principal posts aligned with cosmological directions. **[FE]**

The surviving photographs show named participants embodied as Shoort, Koshménk, Tanu, Kotaix, Ulen, Matan, Hashé, K’terrnen, and other figures. They are the earliest extensive visual corpus of the Hain.

They must nevertheless be read as **collaboratively staged ethnographic images**, not photographs of immaterial beings. Some poses were arranged for Gusinde’s camera; particular people were repainted or posed outside the ceremony; captions and dates were not always recorded consistently on the surviving prints. A 2025 archival study has found discrepancies between Gusinde’s itineraries, later captions, and dates assigned to some photographs. The images document bodies, paint, masks, setting, performance, and ethnographic mediation. **[FE plus modern archive criticism]**

### Lothrop, 1928

Samuel Kirkland Lothrop’s *The Indians of Tierra del Fuego* (1928) combines literature review with approximately three months of 1924–1925 fieldwork. It is especially useful for material culture, settlement, comparative Fuegian ethnology, and bibliography. It is less detailed than Gusinde or Chapman for the Hain. **[FE/LS]**

### Gusinde, 1931

Gusinde’s 1931 volume remains the indispensable textual corpus for Temáukel, Kénos, the *howenh*, cosmology, ritual practices, Hain staging, and spirit nomenclature. Its German original is the critical historical text; later Spanish editions and extracts differ in transcription and pagination. **[FE/LS]**

### Chapman, 1960s–2000s

Anne Chapman worked with surviving tradition-bearers, notably **Lola Kiepja** and **Ángela Loij**, and re-read Gusinde against women’s testimony and social practice. Her principal works include:

- the field recordings *Selk’nam (Ona) Chants of Tierra del Fuego*;
- *Drama and Power in a Hunting Society: The Selk’nam of Tierra del Fuego* (1982);
- Spanish works on Selk’nam life and the end of their autonomous social world;
- *Hain: ceremonia de iniciación selknam* (2002; later editions/translations).

Chapman’s recordings preserve forty-one Hain chants sung by Lola Kiepja, along with shamanic, mourning, cradle, and other songs. These are primary sound documents, though recorded outside a functioning full Hain. **[FE]**

Chapman reported that the last Hain known to her was presented in **1933**, with only a few families. Consequently, “the 1923 Hain was the last ever” is incorrect. It was the last extensively photographed and described full-scale example, not necessarily the final performance. **[FE/MSR]**

### What the corpus cannot supply

There is no indigenous written canon, fixed liturgy, temple archive, epigraphy, or iconographic manual. Almost all detailed documentation was collected during demographic catastrophe, displacement, missionization, and the collapse of the social conditions that sustained the ceremony. The corpus is therefore extraordinarily rich but late, colonial, translated, and concentrated in a few collectors. **[AE/MSR]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Temáukel

Gusinde’s Temáukel is eternal, incorporeal, unseen, unrepresented, remote, and associated with the upper or eastern celestial realm, often rendered **Wintek**. He does not descend to earth or participate in colorful episodic adventures. He establishes moral order, delegates earthly ordering to Kénos, and may punish wrongdoing through misfortune, sickness, or death. **[FE/LS]**

No securely identified traditional image, statue, mask, shrine, sacred animal, weapon, color, number, or celestial body belongs to Temáukel. Modern illustrations of him as a humanoid sky god are inventions. **[AE/MI]**

Gusinde reports minor offerings or gestures interpreted as directed to the supreme being: throwing out a piece of meat during a nocturnal meal and women casting a glowing coal outside a dwelling, as well as exceptional appeals when a child was gravely ill. These reports do not establish a priesthood or regular sacrificial cult. **[FE/LS]**

### Kénos

Kénos belongs to narrative rather than cult image. He shapes or orders terrain, establishes human forms and laws, and appears as an exemplary primordial person. One strand identifies his celestial transformation with Aldebaran, but celestial equations should be cited to the particular collected version rather than treated as universal Selk’nam doctrine. **[FE/RV]**

No securely proven precolonial depiction of Kénos survives. **[AE]**

### Hain lodge as cosmogram

The lodge was built of Nothofagus trunks and hides or vegetation. Its eastward orientation and principal posts mapped territorial-cosmological directions. It was simultaneously architecture, backstage area, teaching space, and image of the Selk’nam world. The posts were associated with directions and particular Shoort forms. **[FE]**

The Hain was not a permanent temple. Its temporary, organic construction and the mobile Selk’nam way of life explain the absence of monumental temple remains. **[AE]**

### Spirit embodiment

The standard visual language consisted of:

- naked or nearly naked human bodies;
- mineral, charcoal, fat, and earth pigments;
- red, white, and black grounds, stripes, bands, dots, and contrasting panels;
- tall conical or rounded masks;
- hides, bark, feathers, or other locally obtainable materials;
- tightly controlled movement, posture, sound, entrance, disappearance, and distance.

A “spirit” was recognizable through the total performance, not just a mask. Photographs detach the painted body from its songs, fear, jokes, relations, and movement. **[FE/MSR]**

### Selected iconographies

- **Shoort:** often tall and conically masked, with regional forms distinguished by patterns and color arrangements. He rushed into camp, disciplined initiates, terrified spectators, and appeared repeatedly. **[FE/RV]**
- **Xalpen:** normally remained unseen within or beneath the lodge. Her presence was produced through roars, messages, demands for meat, staged violence, and the actions of her attendants. Modern images giving her a fixed humanoid body are speculative. **[FE/AE]**
- **Tanu:** an imposing, often red-white-black, broadly shaped figure, moving slowly and creating awe through size and controlled presentation. **[FE]**
- **Koshménk:** very tall conical head; red and white vertical designs vary by directional form. His jealousy and search for Kulan produced comic action. **[FE/RV]**
- **Kulan:** a beautiful female role, embodied by a carefully selected male performer, who appeared from the sky-world and attracted male admirers. **[FE]**
- **Ulen:** elegant, comic, and socially transgressive; his performance depended heavily on gesture and interaction. **[FE]**
- **K’terrnen:** painted as an extraordinary infant and carried from the Hain after Xalpen’s supposed delivery. Sex varied in reports or performances. **[FE/RV]**
- **Matan:** a potent painted figure whose appearance could involve a shaman’s ritual action. **[FE]**

No evidence associates these beings with tarot, planetary magic, European occult correspondences, fixed “energy frequencies,” zodiac signs, or universal color symbolism. **[AE/MI]**

## 4. Genealogy, hierarchy, and documented interactions

### Cosmological hierarchy

A cautious reconstruction is:

1. **Temáukel:** remote source and moral authority.
2. **Kénos:** commissioned earthly organizer; first great *howenh*.
3. **Howenh:** primordial persons whose deeds and transformations produced landscape, animals, weather, and celestial phenomena.
4. **Hain beings:** figures tied to the mythic former women’s ceremony and its later male enactment. Some overlap conceptually with transformed ancestors; the sources do not reduce them to one taxonomic category.
5. **Xon:** shamans, living humans with acquired power, not gods.

This hierarchy is a scholarly diagram, not a preserved Selk’nam creed. **[LS/MSR]**

### Documented being-to-being interactions

Each line records an actual source relationship rather than proximity within a modern “pantheon.”

- **Temáukel — Kénos:** Temáukel sends or commissions Kénos to the primordial earth to order it and establish human life; Gusinde, fieldwork 1919–1923, published 1931. **[FE/LS]**
- **Temáukel — other howenh:** no individual face-to-face episode is securely documented in the principal corpus; authority is mediated through Kénos. **[AE]**
- **Kénos — primordial humans/howenh:** Kénos forms, renews, instructs, or orders the first beings; Gusinde 1931. **[FE/LS; versions vary]**
- **Kénos — Kwanip/Kwányip:** both belong to the primordial cycle, with Kwanip’s deeds helping bring irreversible death and transformation into a world Kénos had ordered; Gusinde 1931. Their exact encounter varies and should not be reduced to a simple rivalry. **[FE/RV]**
- **Kran/Krren (Sun) — Kré/Kreeh (Moon):** Moon is Sun’s wife in the Hain-origin cycle; he discovers the women’s deception, attacks her, and pursues her into the sky; Coiazzi 1914, Bridges’s recollections, Gusinde 1931, Chapman 1982. **[EA/FE/RV]**
- **Moon — the women of the first Hain:** Moon leads the women’s lodge and directs the spirit masquerade that controls men; Coiazzi 1914; Gusinde 1931; Chapman 1982. **[EA/FE]**
- **Sun — the men of the first Hain:** Sun reveals the women’s secret to the men, precipitating the overthrow of the women’s ritual order; same corpus. **[EA/FE]**
- **Men/Sun — Moon:** the men kill most initiated women; Moon escapes after being struck or burned, bearing marks visible on the lunar face; variants in Coiazzi, Bridges, Gusinde, and Chapman. **[EA/FE/RV]**
- **Xalpen — Shoort:** husband-and-wife relation; Shoort acts as the most active visible enforcer associated with Xalpen; Gusinde 1931; Chapman 1982. **[FE]**
- **Xalpen — Klóketen initiates:** she is said to summon, consume, kill, have sexual relations with, or return initiates, depending on the scene; the men stage cries, wounds, disappearance, and restoration; Gusinde 1931; Chapman 1982. **[FE/ritual drama]**
- **Xalpen — K’terrnen:** Xalpen supposedly gives birth to K’terrnen after relations with initiates; the infant’s appearance marks a major ritual scene; Gusinde 1931; Chapman 1982. **[FE]**
- **Xalpen — Hashé:** Hashé functions as her messenger or emissary; Gusinde’s 1923 description and photographs. **[FE]**
- **Xalpen — Tanu:** Tanu is identified in later summaries as Xalpen’s sister or close female associate; the kinship should be treated as a collected Hain classification rather than a universal genealogy. **[FE/RV]**
- **Xalpen — Olum:** Olum is implicated in restorative action after Xalpen’s ritual “killing” of initiates; Gusinde 1931; Chapman 1982. **[FE]**
- **Kulan — Koshménk:** spouses; Kulan’s sexual freedom or infidelity and Koshménk’s jealous search generate comic scenes; Gusinde 1931; Chapman 1982. **[FE]**
- **Kulan — male admirers/other Koshménk:** Kulan is courted while her husband reacts with frustration; Chapman’s reconstruction from Gusinde and surviving informants. **[FE/MSR]**
- **Matan — a xon/shaman:** a shaman ritually causes or controls Matan’s emergence; Gusinde 1931. **[FE]**
- **K’terrnen — women of the camp:** women receive, inspect, celebrate, or mourn the extraordinary infant according to the scene; Gusinde and Chapman. **[FE]**
- **Shoort — initiates:** Shoort disciplines, chases, frightens, and physically tests them; Gusinde 1931; Chapman 1982. **[FE]**
- **Shoort — women and children:** Shoort’s approaches to camp enforce fear and ritual tension; the audience responds through flight, song, resistance, or staged belief. **[FE]**
- **Temáukel — Xalpen, Shoort, Kulan, Koshménk, Tanu, or K’terrnen:** no securely documented direct meeting, combat, marriage, command, or shared scene. Modern charts that place all under Temáukel’s personal government infer a system not narrated in the sources. **[AE/MI]**
- **Temáukel — Ngünechen:** no traditional interaction. Their comparison is modern comparative religion and possible discussion of Christian influence, not an Indigenous myth. **[MSR; no attested equation]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Creation and ordering

In Gusinde’s collected synthesis, Temáukel exists before the present ordered world. Rather than directly performing a sequence of manual creations, he sends Kénos to the primordial earth. Kénos shapes an initially unfinished world, creates or generates primordial people, establishes conduct, and begins the *hóowin* age. **[FE/LS]**

The *howenh* initially possess fluid bodies and extraordinary powers. Through conflict, death, or chosen transformation, they become mountains, animals, winds, stars, and other enduring features. This is why “genealogy of gods” inadequately describes Selk’nam mythology: much of the corpus is a history of metamorphosed primordial persons.

Later summaries that make Temáukel create every animal and landform directly flatten this distinction. **[MR]**

### Renewal and the arrival of irreversible death

Some Kénos narratives describe an early capacity for bodily renewal: aged people could be restored or renewed. In the wider *howenh* cycle, Kwanip’s acts or errors help make death permanent and establish the present condition. Versions differ in sequence, agency, and the relations among Kénos, Kwanip, and other ancestors. **[FE/RV]**

It is therefore unsafe to assign a single “god of death” title. The myths explain how a mutable primordial world became the fixed world of mortal humans.

### The women’s Hain

In the mythic past, women controlled a great lodge. Led by Moon, they painted and masked themselves as terrifying spirits and convinced men that subterranean and celestial beings entered the lodge. The spirits compelled male obedience and secured women’s collective authority. **[EA/FE]**

Sun—or a young Sun in some versions—overhears women discussing the deception or sees a performer without her disguise. He informs the other men. The men attack the lodge and kill most initiated women. Girls and infants who did not know the secret are spared, thereby allowing future wives and families. Moon escapes to the sky. Sun follows, and their continuing separation or pursuit explains their celestial motions. Marks on Moon’s face are attributed in variants to Sun’s blows, burning, or the violence of her escape. Women killed in the upheaval may transform into birds or other beings. **[EA/FE/RV]**

The men then adopt the women’s ceremony, reverse its political direction, and teach initiates that the visible “spirits” are embodied by men. The male Hain continually reenacts this transfer of ritual power.

This is a myth of a former female ritual government, not historical proof that Selk’nam society once passed through an archaeological age of matriarchy. Chapman calls the past order “mythical” while analyzing how its narration worked within actual gender relations. **[MSR]**

### Women’s knowledge: deception versus double belief

A simplistic account says men fooled completely credulous women. Chapman’s testimony complicates this:

- women may have recognized particular performers or suspected male embodiment;
- Ángela Loij and others nevertheless emphasized that the beings did not simply appear to be ordinary men;
- women participated through song, alarm, mockery, demands, and strategic maintenance of the secret;
- men themselves believed in the reality or power of the spirits they embodied and observed ritual precautions before performing them.

Thus the Hain involved layered knowledge: theatrical construction, guarded male privilege, women’s probable awareness, and shared recognition that ritual embodiment made something more than an everyday man present. Calling it merely a “hoax” loses this ambiguity. **[FE/MSR]**

### Xalpen’s cycle

Xalpen remains chiefly within or beneath the lodge. Her hunger and rage are announced by roaring and by messengers. Men carry meat into the lodge, ostensibly to feed her. She demands initiates, kills or devours them in staged episodes, or has relations with them. Blood, screams, wounds, and death are simulated. The initiates later return, sometimes through Olum’s restorative intervention. **[FE]**

K’terrnen’s “birth” follows one such sequence. A painted performer representing a supernatural infant is carried out and shown to the camp. The infant can be affectionate, dangerous, comic, or pathetic within different descriptions.

No evidence establishes actual cannibalism, ritual killing, or human sacrifice in these performances. The deaths are dramatic representations. **[AE]**

### Shoort cycle

Shoort appears repeatedly, unlike figures restricted to a particular episode. Directional Shoort are related to territorial divisions and lodge posts. The performer’s identity and preparation are guarded; his movements combine speed, threat, discipline, and display. Initiates endure ordeals associated with him, while women and children react visibly to his incursions. **[FE/RV]**

### Kulan and Koshménk

Kulan’s beauty and erotic availability invite courtship. Koshménk searches for her, suspects infidelity, attacks or circles the lodge, and becomes an object of laughter. The cycle temporarily reverses the otherwise threatening atmosphere: jealousy, sexuality, and marital tension become comedy. **[FE]**

### Halaháches, Ulen, Matan, and other scenes

The Hain did not follow one invariant script. Apart from recurring appearances such as Shoort and the initiatory core, scenes could be selected, repeated, or omitted. Performers’ skills, local tradition, food supply, weather, audience, and the ritual leaders’ decisions shaped the sequence. This flexibility explains inconsistencies among spirit lists. **[FE/RV]**

## 6. Cult, ritual, and social practice

### No temple cult

Temáukel had no documented permanent temple, idol, hereditary priesthood, pilgrimage center, taxation system, or monumental altar. The Hain lodge was temporary. Archaeological absence here accords with mobile hunter-gatherer social organization and should not be mistaken for absence of religious life. **[AE/MSR]**

### Organization of the Hain

A Hain required sufficient food and an unusual concentration of families. A stranded whale or other abundance could make an extended gathering possible. Older men with ceremonial knowledge supervised construction, initiates, performance, secrecy, and discipline. Skilled younger men embodied physically demanding roles. Shamans participated in particular scenes but did not constitute a centralized priesthood. **[FE]**

The candidate, the **klóketen**, underwent seclusion, instruction, physical testing, food restrictions, exposure to fear, and instruction in the secret identities of performers. He learned social obligations as well as ritual knowledge.

Women were not passive outsiders. They supplied labor and food, sang, responded to spirits, challenged men’s explanations, judged performances, and preserved knowledge of the cycle. Girls had their own puberty observances, although the documentation is much thinner than for male initiation. **[FE/MSR]**

### Masks and secrecy

Masks and costume materials were manufactured and concealed from uninitiated people. Performers entered and exited through controlled routes. After use, materials could be hidden or destroyed. Revealing the secret was represented as gravely dangerous. These practices explain why masks are less common in museum collections than the photographic image might suggest.

### Song

Chant structured entrances, lament, ridicule, supernatural presence, and collective response. Chapman’s recordings with Lola Kiepja are crucial because written transcription cannot preserve timbre, rhythm, breathing, or vocal technique. They document remembered performance after the institution could no longer be staged at its earlier scale. **[FE]**

### Sacrifice and violence

No reliable source documents human sacrifice to Temáukel, Xalpen, or any Hain being. Apparent killings, consumption, sexual assault, and resurrection inside the lodge were dramatic representations. Animal meat was consumed communally and offered within the drama; small pieces of meat or coals were reportedly cast outside in gestures interpreted by Gusinde as offerings. **[FE/AE]**

The mythology itself contains mass killing: men kill the initiated women after discovering the first Hain’s secret. That violence must be reported as mythic narrative, not converted into evidence for an actual prehistoric massacre. **[FE/MSR]**

No evidence supports temple prostitution, organized sacred prostitution, or an occult blood cult. **[AE]**

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before sustained colonial occupation

The Hain probably existed in multiple regional forms before detailed writing. Selk’nam and Haush versions shared broad structures but differed in spirits and performance details. Its age cannot be securely calculated from surviving evidence. Claims that the ceremony is “9,000 years old” confuse the antiquity of human occupation with the demonstrated age of a named ritual. **[AE]**

### 1880s–1910: dispossession and genocide

After the 1881 Chile–Argentina boundary settlement, gold extraction, state concessions, and large sheep ranches transformed Isla Grande. Guanaco grounds were occupied or fenced; Selk’nam people killed sheep replacing or excluding their prey; ranchers, employees, police, and hired killers retaliated. Archival evidence includes the Chilean inquiry *Sumario sobre vejámenes inferidos a indígenas de Tierra del Fuego* initiated in 1895 after reports of abuses and killings.

Modern genocide scholarship characterizes the process as settler-colonial and substantially civilian-driven, while recognizing the enabling role of states, concessions, police, missions, deportation, and extinction discourse. Mission settlements offered refuge from direct shooting in some circumstances but also imposed confinement, religious transformation, diet change, crowding, and exposure to epidemic disease. **[EA/MSR]**

Genocide is essential to the Hain’s history: it destroyed performers, territorial networks, food security, transmission chains, and the demographic scale required for extended ceremonies.

### 1910–1933: late documentation during collapse

Gallardo documented Klóketen while denying “religion.” Coiazzi recorded myths. Gusinde then worked with communities already affected by violence, missionization, wage labor, disease, and population loss. His 1923 Hain was made possible partly through outside provisioning—Chapman reports that approximately 300 sheep were supplied for participants. It was not an untouched survival outside history, but neither was it simply invented for the ethnographer. Selk’nam participants mobilized genuine knowledge under altered circumstances. **[EA/FE]**

A smaller Hain reportedly occurred in 1933. Thereafter, the institution ceased to function as a large community initiation, although stories, songs, images, ancestry, and personal knowledge survived.

### Mid-twentieth century: recording survivors

The disappearance of the large Hain did not mean the disappearance of Selk’nam people. “Extinction” became a colonial and scholarly narrative that obscured descendants, intermarried families, mission survivors, and people whose ancestry had been concealed. Chapman’s work with Lola Kiepja, Ángela Loij, Federico Echelaite, and others recovered testimony that Gusinde could not or did not record, particularly women’s perspectives. **[FE/MSR]**

### Late twentieth century to present

Photographs, recordings, books, museum collections, oral family memory, and community organizations became the principal vehicles of cultural recovery. Argentina legally recognizes the **Rafaela Ishton** Selk’nam community. In Chile, the Covadonga Ona and related descendants campaigned against the official fiction of extinction.

Chile’s **Law 21.606**, published in 2023, amended the Indigenous Law to recognize the Selk’nam among the country’s principal Indigenous peoples. This is a political and legal development in the history of reception, not evidence that ancient practice remained unchanged. It does, however, decisively invalidate descriptions of the Selk’nam as an extinct people.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Photography as global icon

Gusinde’s 1923 photographs have become among the most reproduced images of any Indigenous South American ritual. Their visual force has detached them from names, performers, songs, colonial circumstances, and ethnographic staging. Commercial objects, tattoos, murals, fashion, tourism, advertising, and digital art often reproduce Tanu, Shoort, Koshménk, Kotaix, or Ulen merely as anonymous “Patagonian spirits.” **[MR]**

Dánae Fiore’s systematic study of 43 historical-ethnographic texts and 130 visual sources shows that contemporary reproduction disproportionately selects Hain spirits because their forms appear visually distinctive or “exotic.” Copies range from close reproductions to free designs with little historical fidelity. **[MSR]**

### Theatre, dance, and public commemoration

Contemporary performances inspired by the Hain may function as memorial art, Indigenous reclamation, intercultural education, or tourism. Unless performed and identified by Selk’nam communities as a renewed traditional act, they should not be described as an uninterrupted continuation of the initiation institution. **[MR]**

### Literature, film, and visual arts

Anne Chapman participated in documentary work including *The Ona: Life and Death in Tierra del Fuego*. Modern literature and cinema concerning the Fuegian genocide frequently use painted Hain bodies as a visual shorthand for the Selk’nam. This can restore public visibility while also repeating the false idea that the people exist only as beautiful images from a vanished past. **[MR]**

### Music and occult appropriation

Names such as **Xalpen** and **K’terrnen** have been adopted by modern musical projects, including metal and experimental music. This is reception, not preservation of an ancient musical cult. **[MR]**

The contested-material search found **no documented Renaissance hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, ceremonial-magic, chaos-magic, or historically organized neopagan tradition centered on Temáukel or the Hain**. Modern websites that assign occult correspondences, extraterrestrial origins, universal “shamanic energies,” or magical powers to individual paint patterns are modern invention. One circulating pseudo-historical text explicitly interprets the Hain figures as extraterrestrials; it supplies no archaeological or ethnographic evidence and contradicts the documented performance context. **[AE/MI]**

### Political use

The imagery now appears in campaigns about Indigenous recognition, genocide, territorial memory, repatriation, and decolonization. Unlike decorative commercialization, community-directed use can function as political reclamation. Even so, the modern legal and activist meaning should not be projected backward as the ceremony’s sole traditional function. **[MR]**

No documented extremist political cult of Temáukel was found. **[AE]**

## 9. Historiography and scholarly disputes

### Did the Selk’nam have religion?

Gallardo’s 1910 denial reflects a narrow model requiring temples, idols, formal prayer, or visible worship. Gusinde’s data decisively demonstrate mythology, ritual, supernatural beings, shamanism, moral cosmology, and offerings. The proper correction, however, is not automatically to force everything into European theological categories. **[EA/LS]**

### Temáukel and “primitive monotheism”

Gusinde belonged to an intellectual network associated with Wilhelm Schmidt’s theory of **Urmonotheismus**, according to which very ancient or technologically simple peoples preserved knowledge of a primordial high god. Schmidt’s research program helped fund or motivate Fuegian work. That context creates a genuine methodological concern: Gusinde was predisposed to recognize Temáukel as a morally perfect supreme being.

Two extremes should be rejected:

- Gusinde’s theoretical commitments do **not** prove that Temáukel was invented by him; his reports rest on Indigenous testimony and terminology.  
- His systematic portrait should not be accepted as an unmediated Indigenous theology; selection, leading questions, Christian categories, and translation may have sharpened the resemblance to a monotheistic God.

Temáukel is securely attested in the twentieth-century field corpus. The exact pre-contact form and centrality of the concept remain unrecoverable. **[FE/LS/MSR]**

### Christian influence

Some Selk’nam speakers equated or compared Temáukel with the Christian God after missionary contact. Similarities—remoteness, celestial location, moral judgment, creation—may reflect Indigenous tradition, Christian reinterpretation, missionary translation, Gusinde’s categories, or a combination. No pre-contact document is detailed enough to settle the proportions. **[contested]**

Kénos’s role as an intermediary has sometimes been rendered with Christian language such as “angel” or “son.” The ethnographic record supports a commissioned intermediary, not a secure Christian-style genealogy. **[LS/MI when literalized]**

### Was the first Hain a historical matriarchy?

The sources securely attest a **myth of women’s former ritual power**. They do not supply archaeological proof of a historical matriarchal social stage. Chapman analyzed the myth as a structure through which male authority was legitimated, while also criticizing portrayals of actual Selk’nam women as wholly submissive. Women could leave abusive husbands, hunt when necessary, oppose shamans, and manipulate ritual expectations. **[FE/MSR]**

### Did women know the secret?

Gusinde often emphasizes male secrecy and women’s supposed belief. Chapman’s female informants reveal ambiguity: women could recognize enactment while treating spirits as real and maintaining the ceremonial fiction. There is no reason to impose a single yes/no answer on every woman, period, or performance. **[FE/RV]**

### Was 1923 the final Hain?

No. It was the best-photographed ceremony. Chapman’s informants placed the last known small Hain in 1933. Claims that 1923 was categorically the last reflect the fame of Gusinde’s photographs. **[FE/MSR]**

### Were the photographs neutral records?

No. They are invaluable primary evidence but include posed and reconstructed images. Archival captions and dates can conflict, and photographs remove motion, sound, spectators, secrecy, and duration. Their later reproduction has created a visual canon more rigid than the original performances. **[FE/MSR]**

### Extinction discourse

Older scholarship and popular writing routinely called the Selk’nam extinct and described Lola Kiepja, Ángela Loij, or Virginia Choquintel as “the last Selk’nam.” Such descriptions generally meant last known fluent speaker, last person raised in a particular autonomous lifeway, or last person classified as “racially pure”—not last descendant. Contemporary communities and Chile’s 2023 law expose the political and scientific inadequacy of the extinction claim. **[discredited theory]**

### Mapuche comparison

Temáukel has been compared with Mapuche **Ngünechen**, especially in discussions of celestial supreme beings and Christian influence. The comparison may be heuristically useful, but no source establishes identity, historical syncretism, or a shared cult. The Mapuche chroniclers and dictionaries named in the brief belong in a Ngünechen or Mapuche dossier, not as primary sources here. **[MSR; AE for identity]**

## 11. Conclusions and limits of reconstruction

The securely reconstructable Temáukel is a remote, incorporeal supreme being recorded principally by Gusinde from Selk’nam testimony gathered between 1919 and 1923. Kénos, not Temáukel, is the active shaper of the terrestrial world. Whether Gusinde’s unusually systematic and monotheistic presentation preserves pre-contact theology, reflects Christian-era reinterpretation, or was sharpened by the Vienna school’s search for primordial monotheism cannot now be conclusively resolved.

The Hain is much more densely documented. It was a variable, extended initiation and social-drama complex whose lodge mapped the cosmos, whose masked beings embodied mythic powers, and whose foundational narrative recalled the violent male appropriation of an earlier women’s ceremony. Its “spirits” cannot be reduced either to frauds or to a fixed list of gods: they were simultaneously narrated beings, painted human embodiments, territorial forms, social roles, and objects of layered belief.

The record’s richness is inseparable from catastrophe. Gallardo, Coiazzi, Gusinde, Lothrop, and Chapman documented the tradition during and after genocide, missionization, demographic collapse, and territorial dispossession. Gusinde’s photographs preserve otherwise lost visual knowledge, but they also helped transform living participants into icons of a supposedly extinct people. Present Selk’nam communities, legal recognition, provenance research, and cultural reclamation are therefore not an epilogue external to the dossier; they are the latest historical phase in how Temáukel and the Hain are remembered, represented, and claimed.

The dedicated contested-material pass found:

- no evidence of human sacrifice in Hain practice;
- no evidence of temple prostitution;
- no precolonial writing or monumental temple cult;
- no documented traditional identification of Temáukel with Ngünechen;
- no historically established occult, Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, or neopagan cult;
- no evidence that the painted beings represent extraterrestrials;
- no evidence that 1923 was absolutely the final Hain;
- no basis for calling the Selk’nam extinct;
- and no basis for classifying Temáukel and the Hain as Mapuche, Huilliche, or Chiloé religion.
