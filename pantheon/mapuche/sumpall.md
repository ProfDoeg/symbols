# Sumpall: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest secure source, *Shompallwe* is a singular male **water-owner or water numen**—“*ŋen·ko, fùchá wentru*,” “owner of the water, great/famous man”—approached ritually by a fisherman. Later Mapuche oral narratives and ethnographic literature use *Shumpall/Sumpall* more broadly for male or female aquatic beings, sometimes a collective category. “God,” “spirit,” “merman,” “mermaid,” and “monster” are translations imposed by different collectors; none captures every regional form.

**Earliest secure attestation:** A Mapudungun fisherman’s *ŋillatun* recorded in the Panguipulli district by the Capuchin Sigifredo de Fraunhäusl and published by Félix José de Augusta in *Lecturas Araucanas* (Valdivia, 1910), p. 401. It invokes Shompallwe with a jar of *mudai* and asks him to drive his “animals”—explicitly glossed as fish—toward the fisherman. No secure occurrence of the name has been demonstrated in the principal sixteenth- through nineteenth-century chronicles or missionary dictionaries.

**Evidentiary warning:** Much now presented online as an ancient, uniform, pan-Mapuche mythology—an entire race of blond, fish-tailed Sumpall descended from flood victims—is a synthesis of sources collected at different dates and places. Helmut Schindler’s source-critical reconstruction argues that *Shompallwe* was initially a local Panguipulli name and became a widespread generic label partly through ethnographic and educational circulation during the later twentieth century. That argument is important but remains a modern scholarly reconstruction, not a fact independently stated by the 1910 source.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Documented forms

- **Shompallwe** — Augusta and Fraunhäusl’s 1910 spelling, from Panguipulli. This is the earliest secure form.
- **shompallwe** — Augusta’s 1916 dictionary: “*Ser fabuloso que domina en el agua (ŋen’ko), p. ej. en un lago, tal vez de figura humana*,” marked **Pangui**, i.e. Panguipulli usage.
- **Sompallwe** — spelling used in Augusta’s editorial introduction to the songs.
- **Shompalwe / Sompalhue / Shompalhue** — variants in later Capuchin and ethnographic literature.
- **Shumpall / Sumpall / Chumpall** — shortened forms increasingly common in later-twentieth-century collections.
- **Sumpallwe / Sumpalwe** — modern expanded variants.
- **Sumpay** — a popular modern form, but weakly documented in the early linguistic corpus and not safely projectable backward.
- **sumpallwe**, as a place or collective destination, is sometimes interpreted in modern retellings as “place of the Sumpall.” No secure early lexical demonstration of that analysis was located.

The suffix **-we** ordinarily forms instrumental or locative nouns in Mapudungun, but the internal analysis of *shompall-* is uncertain. No reliable etymology is offered in Augusta’s dictionary.

Moesbach later proposed **rompü/shompü** (“curly”) + **alwe** (“dead person’s spirit, apparition”), hence “curly soul.” This generated his picture of a small, dark, curly-haired lake being. Schindler judges the derivation dubious or erroneous: Augusta wrote double *ll*, not *l*; *alwe* belongs to the semantic field of the dead and is not normally the term for a territorial water-owner; and later field descriptions do not consistently give Sumpall curly hair. Therefore:

- **“Curly soul”** — **learned twentieth-century etymology, disputed**.
- **Derivation from *alwe*** — **unestablished**.
- **Any confident translation of the name itself** — **unestablished**.

### Attested titles

The 1910 prayer supplies the only secure early titles:

- **Ŋen·ko / ngen ko** — “owner/master of the water.”
- **Fücha wentru** — literally “old/great man,” rendered by Augusta “hombre famoso”; an honorific male designation.
- **Owner of the animals/fish** — implicit in repeated petitions for *mi kulliñ*, “your animals,” which Augusta’s note identifies as fish.

Later literature adds:

- **Rey del lago**, “king of the lake” — a regional Spanish designation documented in later ethnography around lakes such as Panguipulli.
- **Sirena / sireno / tritón** — colonial-language iconographic comparisons, increasingly prominent after the mid-twentieth century.
- **Kallfümalen**, “blue maiden” — a female aquatic/ritual figure sometimes brought into the Sumpall complex; not an early synonym proved by the 1910 text.
- **Chumpall**, a blue-clad girl symbolizing sea and water in a late-twentieth-century rain ceremony at Mono Paine — **regional, recent ritual application**.

### Classification through time

1. **1910–1916, securely attested:** a local male *ngen ko*, a water-owner approached by fishermen.
2. **1920s–mid-century elaboration:** lake genius or aquatic divinity; a being that abducts people and compensates their relatives with fish.
3. **Late twentieth-century oral and scholarly corpus:** male or female water person, often generic rather than individual; abducting spouse, giver of fish, regulator of reciprocity.
4. **Popular-modern synthesis:** a species of merpeople guarding all rivers, lakes, and seas. This is partly grounded in modern oral tradition but falsely presented when called a uniformly ancient canonical taxonomy.

The Mapuche religious field is not organized as a single classical “pantheon” with universally fixed divine offices. Sumpall is better understood through the category of locally situated *ngen*—owners or presences of places—than as a departmentally defined “god of water.”

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial sources: a consequential silence

The item brief names Ercilla, Núñez de Pineda, Rosales, Febrés, and Havestadt. None supplies a presently demonstrable occurrence of *Shompallwe/Sumpall*:

- Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana* (1569, 1578, 1589): no secure Sumpall attestation.
- Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz* (written in the seventeenth century; printed 1863): no secure attestation.
- Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del Reino de Chile* (seventeenth-century manuscript; nineteenth-century publication): extensive religious and ethnographic notices, but no demonstrated occurrence of the name.
- Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* (1765): no securely located entry.
- Bernardo Havestadt, *Chilidúgú* (1777): no securely located entry.
- Rodolfo Lenz’s late-nineteenth-century *Estudios araucanos*: not the earliest named record.
- Tomás Guevara, the early works of Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach, Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s early published collections, Oreste Plath, and other major collectors likewise appear on Carrasco’s list of works that did not initially use the name.

This absence does **not** prove that no comparable water-being was known. Colonial and missionary records are selective and use unstable translations. It does establish that claims for an ancient, continuously documented being specifically named Sumpall cannot presently be supported.

### The 1910 Panguipulli prayer

Augusta and Fraunhäusl printed:

> *Fərenean mai, Shompallwe,*  
> *Kechanpəramelpaiaqen mi kulliñ.*  
> *Təfa mai ŋillatueimi*  
> *Kiñe metawe mudai*  
> *Mi pətokoyáqel, Shompallwe.*  
> *Eluaqen mi kulliñ, fərenean,*  
> *Ŋen·ko, füchá wentru!*

In Augusta’s Spanish: “Be favorable to me, Shompallwe; drive your animals upward for me. Here I invoke you with a jar of *mudai* for you to drink … Give me your animals … owner of the water, great/famous man.”

The prayer continues by offering the drink and asking that the fish be driven in at dawn or by noon. Its informant was **Juan de Dios Pəraiantù/Püraiantü**. Augusta explicitly classed it as the *ŋillatun* or prayer of a fisherman. His note called Shompallwe “a kind of god of the waters, perhaps of human form”; the “perhaps” matters. The text itself does not mention:

- a fish tail;
- blond or long hair;
- a female counterpart;
- abduction;
- drowned girls;
- marriage;
- bride-price;
- descent from flood victims;
- genealogy.

The source is nonetheless stronger than a dictionary gloss because it documents ritual speech, an offering, the being’s title, and the desired transaction.

### Augusta’s dictionary, 1916

Augusta’s entry reads in substance:

> **shompallwe**, noun, Panguipulli: fabulous being who rules in the water (*ngen’ko*), for example in a lake, perhaps human in form.

This confirms the geographical restriction and the collector’s uncertainty about physical appearance. The revised National Library edition preserves the original dictionary’s content while modernizing its presentation.

### Latcham and Moesbach: early expansion

Ricardo E. Latcham’s *La organización social y las creencias religiosas de los antiguos araucanos* (1924) belongs to the first generation of synthetic treatments. In the ensuing literature Shompallwe becomes connected with the taking of a woman and the driving of fish ashore.

Moesbach’s *Voz de Arauco*—first issued in 1944, later editions including 1959—offers two influential developments:

1. the disputed “curly soul” etymology and a small, dark, curly-haired male figure;
2. the proposition that when the water-being takes a woman, it compensates her family with a shoal of fish.

Schindler identifies this as the first ethnographic statement of compensation that would later be interpreted specifically as **mafün**, marriage payment or bridewealth. Moesbach’s wording also closely resembles the 1910 prayer’s request that the being “drive” fish toward shore. Direct dependence, a common source, or independent local testimony cannot be conclusively distinguished.

### Faron

Louis C. Faron’s *Hawks of the Sun* and related work records a Liumalla/Liumilla account of a woman who drowned and was understood by many local Mapuche to have been taken as wife by a *shompalwe*. This is significant testimony to belief attached to an actual death, not merely to a formally told *epew*. It does not, however, prove ritual human sacrifice or consent to drowning.

### Carrasco’s corpus, 1979–1986

Hugo Carrasco collected material in 1979–80 and used twenty-one versions in his 1981 thesis, followed by:

- “Sumpall: un relato mítico mapuche” (1982);
- “El ser mítico Sumpall en la cultura mapuche” (1984);
- “El mito de Sumpall en la cultura mapuche” (1986).

His 1986 article presents two narratives in Spanish and Mapudungun and situates them within the larger corpus. Carrasco’s research established that by 1979–80 many communities across Araucanía knew *Shumpall* and the marriage-payment narrative. It also exposed substantial variation:

- the being may be male or female;
- it may appear directly, as an attractive person, or metonymically as wave, mist, whirlpool, or water;
- disappearance may be narrated as abduction, seduction, marriage, drowning, or transformation;
- the returning woman may carry a child;
- the child may become water when unwrapped;
- compensation may be fish, shellfish, marine produce, or the gift of fishing itself.

### Gaete, 1989

Amelia Gaete analyzed three variants for their social treatment of marriage:

- In Juan Porma’s version, the girl disappears, tells her mother in a dream that she married Shumpall, returns carrying a baby that becomes water when exposed, and instructs her family to attend the payment ceremony. A great wave leaves fish on the beach.
- José Catripi’s Deuco version similarly begins with sudden disappearance and later return.
- The wider comparison relates the narrative to Mapuche marriage norms, exchange, and the transformation of an initially wrongful taking into a socially acknowledged union.

This is **late-twentieth-century recorded oral tradition**, not evidence that one immutable narrative existed before European contact.

### Foerster and Montecino

Rolf Foerster and Sonia Montecino read the fisherman’s prayer and marriage myth together: *mudai* offered in the prayer substitutes for the woman or feminine product, while Sumpall reciprocates with fish. This is a **structural and sacrificial interpretation**, not something explicitly stated by the 1910 informant.

Foerster later grouped Sumpall with Mankián and Huilliche Abuelito Huenteao in a broader complex of watery or petrified transformations. The proposed derivation of Sumpall stories from the Trentren–Kaikai flood cycle is likewise a **modern reconstruction**.

### Schindler, 2004/2006

Helmut Schindler’s “Shumpall: ¿nombre propio o término genérico?” made the principal historiographical intervention. His thesis is:

- the earliest name is local to Panguipulli;
- it remained limited in early literature;
- later sources suddenly display a wide distribution of the shortened *shumpall*, the Spanish term *sirena*, blond aquatic women, and the bride-payment story;
- books, schools, ethnography, and educated Mapuche intermediaries may have helped recirculate and standardize those features;
- consequently, twentieth-century oral testimony may be authentically Mapuche while also reflecting feedback from ethnographic print.

This is not an argument that the narratives are “fake.” It is a model of cultural circulation: local oral tradition enters print and later returns to oral use in new regions.

### Patagonia: Neuquén and Río Negro

Bertha Koessler-Ilg collected extensively around San Martín de los Andes from approximately 1920 until 1965. Her initially published *Cuentan los araucanos* does not securely establish an early Sumpall entry. Schindler reports that the name appears only in the then-unpublished third volume of her larger collection, in divergent portrayals reflecting different narrators.

Later Argentine ethnobiological fieldwork records *nwenko/ngen ko* and uses Shumpall as a generic personification of water. In thirty-six interviews, aquatic beings could appear as people, stones, sheep, bulls, pigs, horses, hides, snakes, “sirens,” or saints. The post-contact domestic animals and Christian saints demonstrate ongoing incorporation rather than a sealed precolonial canon.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Secure early domain

The 1910 Shompallwe:

- inhabits or governs water;
- owns fish as livestock;
- can direct fish toward humans;
- receives *mudai*;
- responds to *ŋillatun*;
- is addressed as male.

The transaction is reciprocal rather than simply devotional: drink is presented; fish are requested.

### Later domains

Later Sumpall traditions make the being:

- owner or guardian of a particular lake, river, spring, lagoon, or sea sector;
- source or keeper of fish;
- taker and spouse of humans;
- regulator of compensation when something is taken;
- source of fog, whirlpools, waves, abundance, danger, or prophetic appearance;
- embodiment or personification of water itself.

The widespread claim that water “never dries up” where a Sumpall lives is familiar in modern popular accounts but lacks support in the 1910 source.

### Form

No single iconography is canonical.

- **1910:** possibly human; male by grammatical and titular context.
- **Moesbach:** small dark man with curly hair; dependent on a disputed etymology.
- **Later Araucanía testimony:** male or female human figure, sometimes golden- or blond-haired.
- **Narrative form:** wave, mist, whirlpool, or unseen underwater spouse.
- **Modern folklore:** upper human body and fish tail.
- **Patagonian ethnobiology:** person, sheep, hide, bull, pig, horse, stone, snake, saint, or siren.

Therefore the familiar fishtail is a **late and regionally variable image**, probably strengthened by the Spanish category *sirena*. Schindler notes that the European image of a woman combing long hair may also have entered local narration through print and schooling. That does not establish that all attractive aquatic-human imagery is European; it identifies particular iconographic details as historically mobile.

### Associated substances and objects

Securely attested:

- water;
- fish as *kulliñ*, “animals/livestock”;
- a ceramic jar (*metawe*);
- wheat *mudai*;
- fishing at dawn or toward midday.

Later:

- fish and shellfish deposited by a wave;
- baskets of aquatic food;
- baby or child who liquefies;
- golden comb;
- blue clothing;
- *kallfü*, blue, as an aquatic and ritual color;
- waves, whirlpools, mist, rocks, and shoreline caves.

No secure ancient association with a planet, weekday, sacred number, weapon, temple image, or standardized emblem has been found.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogy

The earliest evidence gives no parents, siblings, consort, or children. The marriage narratives supply human wives or husbands and sometimes hybrid or watery children, but these relationships differ by telling.

A common modern claim says that **Trengtreng Vilu and Kaikai Vilu transformed flood survivors or drowned people into the first Sumpall**. The source trail is much weaker than popular summaries imply. Carrasco and Foerster proposed that Sumpall narratives developed from the Trentren–Kaikai motif of humans transformed into fish and fish fecundating women. That is **comparative scholarly reconstruction**, not an explicit genealogy in the 1910 prayer.

### Every documented divine interaction

Because the tradition does not supply a fixed divine family narrative, the checkable list is short:

- **Trengtreng / Trentren and Kaikai / Kai-Kai** — No primary text located records a face-to-face meeting with a named Sumpall. Carrasco and Foerster relate the cycles genealogically or structurally through flood victims transformed into fish. **Tier: modern scholarly reconstruction, not securely attested interaction.**
- **Mankián / Mankian** — Foerster compares his transformation into stone with Sumpall’s aquatic transformations. No specific episode has Sumpall meeting Mankián. **Tier: modern comparative systematization; no documented interaction.**
- **Abuelito Huenteao / Huentecao** — Foerster places Huilliche Huenteao mythology in the same mythic field. No direct meeting or exchange with Sumpall is documented. **Tier: modern comparative systematization.**
- **Gürüfilu / Ngürüfilu** — A recent Botxolwe community publication treats Gürüfilu as a local aquatic divinity and invokes Augusta’s definition of Shompallwe; this is an equation or overlap, not a narrated meeting. **Tier: contemporary regional identification.**
- **Kallfümalen, “Blue Maiden”** — Some late regional discussions align female aquatic Sumpall with blue-maiden figures or give *chumpall* to a blue-clad ceremonial girl. No independent myth narrating two distinct beings interacting has been located. **Tier: late regional identification, not interaction.**
- **Ngen ko** — Not a separate being in the earliest prayer but Shompallwe’s title/classification: “owner of the water.” **Tier: securely attested identity/title.**

**Finding:** No securely attested narrative interaction between Sumpall and another separately named divine being has been found. Modern genealogical charts that make Sumpall the “child” of Trentren and Kaikai convert a comparative theory into narrative fact.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The fisherman’s reciprocal prayer

**Tier: securely attested early material, 1910.**

A fisherman brings a jar of *mudai* and addresses Shompallwe respectfully. The water-owner is asked to drink, be favorable, and herd his fish toward the petitioner by dawn or noon. This is the only early text showing an actual ritual relationship. Its logic is gift and counter-gift, but it does not narrate marriage.

### B. The taken girl and marriage under the water

**Tier: mid- to late-twentieth-century oral corpus, with earlier components in Latcham/Moesbach.**

A young woman goes to a river, lake, lagoon, or coast to bathe, wash, gather shellfish, or look into the water. A handsome aquatic being, wave, mist, or whirlpool takes her. Depending on the version, she is forcibly abducted, willingly attracted, enchanted, or simply disappears. She lives beneath or within the water as Sumpall’s wife.

Her family mourns. In some variants they hold a *ŋillatun* or receive a dream. The girl eventually returns or communicates that she is alive, married, and not to be mourned.

The interpretive danger is to call every disappearance “consensual.” Schindler stresses that testimonies associated with actual drownings do not describe consent or complacency; relatives may understand a water woman or man to have killed or taken the victim.

### C. Bride-price in fish

**Tier: canonical late oral form; first explicit ethnographic precursor in Moesbach, then extensively collected after 1979.**

The woman tells her parents to prepare for the marriage payment. Her family goes to the shore with food or holds a gathering. A huge wave arrives and leaves fish, shellfish, or other marine produce. Sometimes a female figure brings a basket. In other versions the payment is not one catch but the lasting gift of successful fishing.

The story converts an improper taking into an exchange conforming to social reciprocity: what is taken must be compensated. Gaete therefore reads the tale through Mapuche marriage practice; Carrasco identifies the deep opposition as **taking/giving**, expressed as deprivation/possession, unmarried/married, death/life, and harm/benefit.

The story must not be simplified into approval of kidnapping. Individual variants disagree about consent, and the narrative’s very need for reparation marks the taking as socially problematic.

### D. The watery child

**Tier: regional variant, collected in the late twentieth century.**

The returned woman brings a baby, sometimes concealed in a basket or wrapping. When curious relatives uncover it, it becomes water, or occasionally reveals an aquatic or marine-animal form. The episode dramatizes the child’s incompatibility with ordinary land life and the boundary between human and water households.

### E. Transformation into a bird

**Tier: regional folklore.**

In some Budi-area material the taken girl becomes a **wala/huala**, a grebe-like diving bird. Its poor flight and skilled diving are explained by Sumpall’s continuing hold. This is an etiological variant, not part of the 1910 prayer.

### F. Female Sumpall who takes men

**Tier: modern and regional oral tradition.**

Gender may be reversed: an attractive female aquatic being calls a man, who later drowns, dies, or becomes her underwater spouse. Late accounts near Huichahue describe a female Sumpall seen by men whom she subsequently calls away. Schindler’s Melipeuco material includes a father’s belief that his drowned son was taken by a water woman.

### G. Flood-origin theory

**Tier: modern scholarly reconstruction, later popularized as mythic fact.**

Carrasco linked Sumpall to the Trentren–Kaikai cycle, specifically humans transformed into fish after the flood. Foerster extended the comparative field to Mankián and Huenteao. Popular retellings transformed this hypothesis into a canonical creation story in which the two great serpents directly created the Sumpall. The underlying comparison is plausible; the confident genealogy is not securely attested.

## 6. Cult, places, and ritual practice

### No temple institution

No temple, permanent shrine, hereditary Sumpall priesthood, cult statue, calendrical festival, or centralized cult organization is securely documented. Applying Mediterranean temple categories would misdescribe the evidence.

### Panguipulli fishing rite

The 1910 prayer is the chief cultic document. Its elements are:

- an individual fisherman;
- direct invocation;
- *mudai* presented in a *metawe*;
- a request for fish;
- a specified expected time;
- recognition of the being as *ngen ko*.

Whether the offering was poured, left, or consumed communally is not stated.

### Marriage-payment ceremony in narrative

Later stories describe families taking food to the shore and waiting for the aquatic spouse’s payment. Some versions include communal prayer. These are narrated ritual actions inside an *epew*; they should not automatically be treated as descriptions of repeatedly performed historical ceremonies.

### Rain ritual

At Mono Paine, a late report describes a barefoot virgin called **chumpall**, blue-clad and riding beside a standard-bearer during a *ngillatun* chiefly concerned with rain. A local longko explained her as a sea siren symbolizing sea and water. Schindler considers the terminology probably recent, adopted after wider diffusion of *shumpall*. This is valuable evidence for living ritual innovation, not proof of an ancient Sumpall priestess.

### Actual drownings and belief

Accounts in Liumalla/Liumilla, Melipeuco, and Huichahue show Sumpall functioning as an explanation of deaths or disappearances in water. That is evidence of belief attached to experience. It is not evidence that victims were deliberately offered.

### Human sacrifice

No source located documents sacrifice **to Sumpall**. Foerster and Montecino interpret the myth structurally as substitution or sacrifice—the woman in the myth, *mudai* in the prayer—but this is analytical language.

Colonial Mapuche sacrificial practices and the documented killing of a child after the 1960 Valdivia earthquake belong to broader Mapuche religious history, but no responsible source found connects those events specifically with Sumpall. To make that connection would be guilt by pantheon rather than evidence.

### Economics

Fish constitute wealth, food, and the aquatic owner’s “livestock.” In the marriage cycle they become compensation, transforming natural abundance into social payment. The dossier therefore joins subsistence fishing to rules of reciprocity without establishing a separate temple economy.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1910: possible antecedents, no named record

Mapuche communities plainly possessed traditions about local water owners, dangerous waters, animal transformations, and flood beings. Yet the name *Shompallwe* has not been securely found in the principal colonial corpus. The evidentiary status is therefore **possible older oral antecedent; named continuity unproved**.

Spanish colonization, missionization, military conquest, land reduction, and ecological displacement radically altered Mapuche religious life. None of the documentation reviewed records a targeted suppression of a distinct Sumpall cult.

### 1910–1916: local Panguipulli water-owner

The earliest record is narrow and coherent: male water-owner, fisherman, fish, and *mudai*. Augusta geographically labels the word Panguipulli.

### 1924–1959: ethnographic synthesis

Latcham and Moesbach broaden the figure. Abduction, human spouse, compensation in fish, “genius of lakes,” and the disputed curly-haired iconography enter published synthesis. The term is also associated with Budi despite its initial Panguipulli documentation.

### Mid-century: uneven distribution

Faron records belief in a *shompalwe* spouse of a drowned woman. Yet many major ethnographers and collectors do not use the name. This unevenness argues against a uniform pan-Mapuche category.

### 1979–1989: major collection and canon formation

Carrasco’s twenty-one narratives establish the late-twentieth-century geographical spread of *Shumpall*. His thesis and articles give the cycle a stable scholarly identity. Gaete, Foerster, and Montecino connect it with marriage norms, reciprocity, sacrifice, and historical dispossession.

Schindler proposes that ethnographic literature or a school text helped cause this diffusion. This feedback thesis is supported by:

- abrupt increase in geographic distribution;
- prevalence of shortened *shumpall* rather than the earlier *shompallwe*;
- adoption of *sirena*;
- learned repetition of Moesbach’s “curly soul” etymology by a narrator outside Budi;
- later introduction of Sumpall into works by authors whose earlier works omitted it.

It remains possible that collectors had previously missed a wider oral distribution.

### Late twentieth century: gender and image expand

Sumpall becomes male, female, or collective; blond mermaid, merman, blue maiden, wave, whirlpool, animal, or generic water person. The name enters poetry, schooling, popular mythology, ecological discourse, and occasional ritual symbolism.

### Twenty-first century

Contemporary fieldwork in Argentine Patagonia continues to record water owners while showing incorporation of post-contact animals and Christian saints. Community publications reclaim Shumpall and related local beings as parts of territorial memory. This is living tradition, but not necessarily institutional “revival” after total extinction.

No reliable census of present-day Sumpall worship exists. It is safer to speak of continuing stories, place-respect, personal belief, artistic invocation, and localized ritual adaptation than of an organized Sumpall denomination.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and poetry

Roxana Miranda Rupailaf’s *Shumpall* and *Invocación al Shumpall* (2011) made the being central to an erotic, gendered, and Mapuche poetic project. Here the woman is not merely passive prey: desire, seduction, bodily autonomy, water, colonial violence, and linguistic hybridity are reworked from the inherited story. This is **modern literary reinvention**, not a transcription of an ancient hymn.

Recent criticism treats Shumpall as:

- an aquatic or “hydropoetic” figure;
- a vehicle for female and queer desire;
- a figure of ecological crisis and water depletion;
- a symbol of Mapuche cultural recovery and resistance.

### Visual representation

Modern illustrations commonly depict a conventionally European mermaid or muscular merman. Some academic and popular publications have even used fantasy art unrelated to Mapuche material culture. Such images document modern reception, not traditional iconography.

### Environmental discourse

Modern community and scholarly works present Sumpall as guardian of water and a figure of ecological ethics. This accords broadly with *ngen ko* conceptions but often systematizes them into a generalized environmental doctrine more explicit than the early sources.

### Popular mythology

Web summaries commonly add:

- a fixed half-fish anatomy;
- universal blond or shining hair;
- an organized species of male and female Sumpall;
- permanent residence in a named underwater realm;
- descent from victims of the Trentren–Kaikai flood;
- punishment of all polluters;
- a single canonical bride-abduction plot.

These items range from later oral tradition to unsourced synthesis. They should not be labeled simply “ancient Mapuche belief.”

### Science and commerce

The fossil rodent **Luantus sompallewei** was named for the aquatic being associated with Laguna del Laja. This is a modern scientific commemoration and incidentally records the popularized mermaid interpretation.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no well-documented Sumpall tradition in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, Wicca, chaos magic, or historic grimoires. Nor was an organized international neopagan Sumpall cult established. Generic internet pages that absorb Indigenous beings into “water-spirit,” mermaid, or elemental catalogues are **modern cross-cultural appropriation**, not evidence of an inherited occult lineage.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Sumpall appears in Mapuche cultural and decolonial literature, but no specific, documented extremist cult or sustained nationalist appropriation centered on Sumpall was found. Claims beyond that should be marked unestablished.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Sumpall local or pan-Mapuche?

- **Carrasco:** by 1979–80 the narrative was widely known; his large variant corpus justifies treating Sumpall as an important Mapuche mythical being.
- **Schindler:** the name was originally local to Panguipulli and became generic through twentieth-century ethnographic feedback, schooling, and literary circulation.
- **Balanced conclusion:** wide late-twentieth-century distribution is documented; wide pre-1910 distribution is not.

### Is the being singular or collective?

The prayer addresses one male individual. Later narrators use the name for different localized male and female beings. Hence “Sumpall” shifted from local name/title toward generic class—precisely the history rather than a contradiction to erase.

### Is Sumpall a *ngen ko*?

The earliest text explicitly says so. Later system-builders sometimes describe Sumpall as a subtype of *ngen ko*, while others treat it as the generic personification encompassing many water forms. Both are later taxonomic formulations; the secure point is the 1910 apposition.

### Is Sumpall a mermaid?

Only in a qualified sense. The 1910 source says “perhaps human.” The fish-tail image is late and likely strengthened by the Spanish *sirena*. Human, wave, fog, animal, and stone forms remain documented alternatives.

### Was the girl kidnapped or consenting?

Both forms exist. Some stories reinterpret disappearance as marriage; others stress violent taking. Accounts applied to actual drownings give no warrant for assuming consent. A harmonized “voluntary abduction” is therefore misleading.

### Is fish payment ancient bride-price?

Moesbach supplies the first clear published compensation in fish; the full *mafün* structure is prominent in late collections. Carrasco and Gaete show that it accords closely with Mapuche marriage exchange. Schindler cautions that publication may have helped standardize the connection. Ancient continuity is possible but unproved.

### Did Sumpall originate in the Trentren–Kaikai flood?

This is Carrasco’s developmental hypothesis, endorsed and elaborated by Foerster, not an early source statement. Popular presentations frequently erase that distinction.

### Is “curly soul” the etymology?

Probably not. It depends on altering Augusta’s spelling and conflating a water-owner with *alwe*. It should be retained as part of the history of scholarship, not as accepted linguistic fact.

### Siren influence

The aquatic human spouse is not by itself proof of European derivation; comparable beings can develop independently. More specific motifs—the fish tail, golden comb, blond woman, and the label *sirena*—are plausible colonial or modern borrowings. Schindler’s print-feedback model explains their accelerated distribution, but individual motifs require separate source histories.

### Limits of the archive

The record is unusually dependent on missionaries and outside ethnographers. Augusta and Fraunhäusl nevertheless named informants and printed Mapudungun parallel texts, giving their work substantial documentary value. Carrasco likewise identified narrators and communities. Still absent are precolonial images, inscriptions, Indigenous manuscripts, and an archaeological object securely identifiable as Sumpall.

Dillehay’s archaeological and political histories, Bacigalupo’s work on *machi*, Grebe’s cosmological studies, and general *nguillatun/machitun* ethnographies illuminate the broader religious world but do not independently push named Sumpall evidence behind 1910.

## 10. Chronology

- **1569–1589:** Ercilla publishes the three parts of *La Araucana*. No Sumpall attestation established.
- **Seventeenth century:** Núñez de Pineda and Rosales describe Mapuche society and religion; no secure occurrence of the name located.
- **1765:** Febrés’s Mapudungun grammar and vocabulary; no secure entry located.
- **1777:** Havestadt’s *Chilidúgú*; no secure entry located.
- **1895–1897:** Lenz publishes *Estudios araucanos*. Important oral and linguistic corpus, but not the earliest demonstrated Sumpall record.
- **1910:** Augusta and Fraunhäusl publish Juan de Dios Pəraiantü’s Panguipulli fisherman’s prayer to Shompallwe. **Earliest secure attestation.**
- **1916:** Augusta’s dictionary defines *shompallwe*, marked Panguipulli, as a fabulous water-ruling being or *ngen ko*, perhaps human.
- **1920–1965:** Koessler-Ilg collects Mapuche traditions in Neuquén; the name appears only in later/unpublished portions reported by Schindler, not securely in her first published collection.
- **1924:** Latcham incorporates Shompallwe into a synthetic account of Mapuche religion.
- **1930:** Moesbach publishes Pascual Coña’s testimony; no secure named Sumpall entry establishes an earlier tradition in that text.
- **1934:** Second edition of *Lecturas Araucanas* expands the editorial gloss: male siren/water god whose animals are fish.
- **1944/1959:** Moesbach’s *Voz de Arauco* advances the disputed “curly soul” etymology and published fish-compensation motif.
- **Mid-twentieth century:** Faron records belief that a drowned woman became the wife of a *shompalwe*.
- **1979–1980:** Carrasco and collaborators collect a large corpus across Araucanía; *Shumpall*, *sirena*, blond aquatic women, and bride-payment narratives are widely known.
- **1981:** Carrasco completes *El mito del Shumpall en relatos orales mapuches*, using twenty-one variants.
- **1982:** Carrasco publishes “Sumpall: un relato mítico mapuche.”
- **1984:** Carrasco publishes on the mythical being Sumpall.
- **1986:** “El mito de Sumpall en la cultura Mapuche” presents bilingual narratives and comparative analysis.
- **1989:** Gaete analyzes three variants through marriage norms and reciprocity.
- **1990:** Foerster and Montecino connect the 1910 fishing prayer and marriage myth through sacrificial/substitution analysis.
- **1993:** Foerster’s *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche* relates Sumpall to Mankián, Huenteao, and the Trentren–Kaikai complex.
- **Late twentieth century:** Sumpall becomes a widespread generic water-being in scholarship, schools, poetry, oral narration, and some rituals.
- **2004:** Schindler publishes “Shumpall: ¿nombre propio o término genérico?”, arguing for local origin and ethnographic feedback.
- **2006:** Schindler republishes/extends the study in *Acerca de la espiritualidad mapuche*.
- **2011:** Roxana Miranda Rupailaf publishes *Invocación al Shumpall*.
- **2014:** Ethnobiological work relates Shumpall to *filoko*, fishing, marriage payment, and contemporary ecological knowledge.
- **2016:** Patagonian field research documents *nwenko* and a broad, hybrid repertoire of aquatic forms.
- **2020s:** Shumpall remains active in Indigenous territorial publications, ecocritical scholarship, poetry, and popular mythology; no centralized cult or authoritative canon exists.
