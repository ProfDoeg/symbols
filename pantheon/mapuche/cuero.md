# Cuero (Trelquehuecuve): Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the securely documented Mapuche record, the Cuero is an aquatic **mythic animal/monster** and, in some testimony, the corporeal aquatic form of a harmful **wekufü**. It is not securely attested as a god, goddess, ancestor, or individually worshipped *ngen*. Chilean folklore generally classifies it as a monster or supernatural animal.  
> **Earliest secure attestation under the specific name and recognizable form:** Tomás Guevara, *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* (Santiago, 1908), pp. 322–23: **Trelquehuecufe**, an aquatic constrictor shaped like a calf hide and armed around its edge with claws. Guevara also published the earliest presently located image.  
> **Earlier possible antecedent:** late-eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century descriptions of the **ngürüfilu/guirivilo** sometimes compare that distinct water monster to a rounded ox hide or *manta*. Because those texts name another being and mix incompatible morphologies, they are antecedents to the Cuero complex, not secure attestations of Trelquehuecufe itself.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Secure early attestation]** Direct, named evidence from the earliest documentary layer, principally Guevara in 1908.
- **[Early folklore collection]** A directly collected or lexicographically registered version from approximately 1900–30.
- **[Regional variant]** A localized Mapuche, Huilliche, Chilote, central-Chilean, Neuquén, or Río Negro form.
- **[Learned systematization]** A collector’s synthesis or classification rather than a single traditional testimony.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** An equation produced through contact between traditions.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An analytical interpretation proposed by a modern scholar.
- **[Modern retelling/invention]** A feature found only in recent popular, cryptozoological, commercial, or internet accounts.
- **[Unestablished]** A claim for which no adequate primary or scholarly source was located.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**[Secure early attestation]** Guevara describes the *Trelquehuecufe* as inhabiting deep, quiet parts of rivers and lagoons. It is approximately the size of a calf’s hide, has claws around its perimeter, seizes humans or animals entering the water, wraps or contracts around them, and kills them. In a later synthesis he calls it a *mito* and a “constrictor acuático”; a whirlpool marks its dwelling. His collected narratives from Quepe and Cholchol connect particular drownings and dangerous river pools with it.

**[Early folklore collection]** Julio Vicuña Cifuentes’s *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* (1915) assembled Guevara’s versions, Rodolfo Lenz’s lexicographical evidence, Francisco J. Cavada’s Chilote *Manta*, and Vicuña Cifuentes’s own southern informants. His synthesis made “El Cuero” a broadly Chilean folklore figure: a brown or mottled bovine-looking hide, edged by many short clawed appendages, which folds over prey.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** It is therefore better understood not as one canonical personality but as an overlapping complex:

1. Mapuche **Trülke/Trelque-wekufü**, the hide-shaped harmful aquatic entity;
2. the Chilote **Manta**, a folding, engulfing skin;
3. central-Chilean **Cuero** or **Cuero del agua** traditions;
4. Argentine Patagonian **lafken trülke**, **cuero vivo**, and related water-hide narratives;
5. occasional confusion with the distinct **ngürüfilu**, the “fox-snake.”

### Names and spellings

Mapudungun was historically transcribed through several incompatible missionary and scholarly alphabets. The following forms should not automatically be treated as separate beings:

- **trülke wekufü**: modernized Mapudungun spelling, literally “hide/skin–wekufü.”
- **Trelquehuecufe / Trelquehuecú / Trelquehuecuve / Trelquehuecuvu / Threquelhuecuvu / Trilkewecufe / Trelwekufe**: historical or Spanish orthographic variants.
- **trelke / trülke / trilque / tilque**: hide, skin, or pelt.
- **wekufü**, historically *huecufe, huecuve, huecuvu, huecú*: a harmful force, agency, spirit, or entity. Translating it simply as “devil” reflects Christian and ethnographic vocabulary and can falsely imply identity with the Christian Satan.
- **Cuero**, **Cuero del agua**, **Cuero de agua**, **Cuero vivo**: Spanish names emphasizing its hide-like body or an animated hide.
- **Manta**, **Manta del agua**, **Manta del Diablo**: especially Chilote and broader Chilean names based on its blanket-like expansion and folding.
- **lafken trülke / lafquén trilque**: “lake/sea hide,” recorded in Argentine Mapuche literature.
- **trülke leufü / trelque leufu**: “river hide,” reported in later compilations.
- **Chueiquehuecú / Chueiquehuecu**: Lenz’s lexicographical headword for a harmful aquatic animal; his supplement also records **triuquehuicú** and **pelatregua**.
- **huecú / huecúo**: a Coquimbo regional name that Lenz connected with *chueiquehuecú*.
- **tanta**: an Argentine regional name reported second-hand by Oscar Barreto in 1992 for a mimetic lake hide.
- **Xulke**: a contemporary Argentine spelling glossed “cuero” by the Biblioteca Nacional Mariano Moreno in 2024.

**[Unestablished]** Modern lists sometimes add *uñ*, *cuerito*, *cuero unudo*, and *El Bien Peinado*. Their geographical distributions and historical pedigrees have not been demonstrated in the early corpus examined here.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic core]** The first element is transparently Mapudungun *trülke* “hide, skin, pelt.” The second is *wekufü*, but its semantic treatment is disputed.

- **[Early lexicography]** Lenz proposed a compound of the word for hide, citing the older missionary forms *tilque/trilque*, and *huecú*.
- **[Learned systematization]** Mid-twentieth-century *Enciclopedia Chilena* defined *huecufe/huecuve* as “the principle of evil.” This is a theological abstraction, not a literal dictionary equivalence.
- **[Modern scholarly caution]** Contemporary Mapuche studies often treat *wekufü* as harmful power or agency, sometimes embodied, rather than a fixed zoological species or the Christian Devil. “Demon hide” is convenient but culturally overdetermined; “hide-shaped wekufü” is safer.

The name **Cuero** is not an independent indigenous etymology but a Spanish translation or descriptive equivalent. **Manta** is likewise Spanish and refers to shape and action, not necessarily to the zoological manta ray.

### Classification shifts

- **1908 Guevara:** a *pulpo* (“octopus”) and aquatic constrictor, but this is the ethnographer’s zoological analogy.
- **1914 Cavada:** the Chilote *Manta*, a skin that rises and folds around prey.
- **1915 Vicuña Cifuentes:** a Chilean mythic animal, sometimes said by informants to be the visible bodily form of a *Huecuve*.
- **Late twentieth century:** catalogued as a “fantastic being,” “water monster,” or particular manifestation of *wekufü*.
- **Twenty-first century:** additionally reclassified by popular writers as a cryptid, giant freshwater ray, octopus, bryozoan colony, or unidentified animal. None of those proposed biological identifications is established evidence about the tradition’s historical origin.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earlier colonial comparanda

**[Possible antecedent; not a secure identification]** Joseph de La Porte’s eighteenth-century account, transmitted in Spanish translation by Pedro Estala in 1798, reports that some Chilean descriptions of the **guruvilu** resembled a rounded or swollen ox hide. Juan Ignacio Molina’s expanded 1810 *Saggio sulla storia naturale del Chili* repeated a similar report about the **ghyryvilu**, comparing one reported form to an extended bovine hide and treating the story skeptically.

These passages show that a hide-shaped aquatic-monster motif circulated before 1800. They do **not** securely establish the name *Trelquehuecufe*, nor do they prove that the hide-shaped being and fox-serpent were originally one entity. Molina wrote from exile and compiled memories and earlier reports; the 1810 edition was extensively revised from his 1782 work. [Memoria Chilena’s publication history](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-93689.html) documents that textual situation.

**[Possible colonial evidence]** Vicente Carvallo y Goyeneche’s *Descripción histórico-geográfica del Reino de Chile*, completed in 1796 but first published in 1875–76, records an aquatic predator called a *manta* in connection with children disappearing in a lagoon near Talcamávida. The chronology of the manuscript is secure, but the identification with the later Cuero is inferential. Carvallo’s work’s completion and delayed publication are documented by [Memoria Chilena](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-3352.html).

### The first unambiguous record: Guevara, 1908

**[Secure early attestation]** Tomás Guevara, *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* (Santiago: Imprenta Cervantes, 1908), pp. 322–23, names *Trelquehuecufe* and gives the defining morphology and predatory behavior. Page 323 includes a simple line illustration: a flattened, irregular hide-like body with many clawed projections around the edge. The surviving digital reproduction identifies the source, page, and date and preserves the [1908 image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Guevara(1908)-Psicoloj%C3%ADa-p323-Fig42.5-Trelquehuecufe.jpg).

Guevara’s evidence appears to derive from contemporary Mapuche informants rather than an older written source. It proves that the named being and its recognizable form were recorded by 1908; it cannot by itself date the oral tradition’s origin.

### Guevara’s Mapuche-language narratives

**[Secure early oral testimony]** In *Folklore araucano*, Guevara prints Mapudungun and Spanish versions attributed to named informants:

- **“Un Trelquehuecufe en Quepe,” from Manqueo:** a man dives into a still reach of the Quepe River, disappears, and cannot be recovered despite a four-day search. After other drownings, the community concludes that a *trelquehuecufe* inhabits the pool and ceases bathing there.
- **“Un Trelquehuecufe en Cholchol,” from Lorenzo Manquian:** the narrator sees on a stony riverbank something like a calf hide, brown with whitish spots and “surrounded by nails/claws.” He runs to tell his father. They return cautiously because a whirlwind might propel them toward it; it has disappeared. The narrator adds that many men are said to have been devoured by this strong, elastic hide.

The bilingual Cholchol text begins: *Küne trelkenekufü Cholchol meu* and describes *kiñe trelke waka*—“a cow/calf hide.” The digitized original is preserved as [Guevara’s *Folklore araucano*](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0008877.pdf); an accessible transcription appears in the [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/el-pueblo-mapuche--0/html/ff91c804-82b1-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_23.html).

### Lenz

**[Early lexicography]** Rodolfo Lenz’s *Diccionario etimolójico de las voces chilenas derivadas de lenguas indígenas americanas* records *chueiquehuecú* as a fabulous aquatic animal harmful to bathers and passers-by, catchable only with a *lleivún* fiber rope. His supplement describes it as a many-legged skin that attracts animals from afar and sucks their blood, and records regional forms in Los Ángeles and Coquimbo.

This is the important source for the “drinks its prey” thread. It does not say that all Trelquehuecufe versions drink blood; that belongs specifically to the *chueiquehuecú* lexical variant. See the [dictionary supplement entry](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/P%C3%A1gina%3ADiccionario_etimol%C3%B3jico_de_las_voces_chilenas_derivadas_de_lenguas_ind%C3%ADgenas_americanas.djvu/889).

### Cavada and Chiloé

**[Regional variant]** Francisco J. Cavada, *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914), p. 104, calls the being **la Manta**. Islanders imagine it as an extended skin that folds over its prey. When a person or animal enters the water, it rises, envelops the victim, drags it down, and devours it. Cavada emphasizes its use as a terror or warning for bathing children.

Cavada supplies the clearest early evidence for the Chiloé *Manta*. He does not establish an independent temple, cult, genealogy, or relation to the island’s organized witchcraft mythology.

### Vicuña Cifuentes

**[Early folklore collection / learned synthesis]** Julio Vicuña Cifuentes, *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* (1915), pp. 38–40, brings together Cavada, Guevara, Lenz, and his own southern testimony. His informants described a bovine hide, brown with large whitish patches, with numerous short legs ending in claws. Some told him it was the bodily form taken by a *Huecuve* in water and that the indwelling force sucked blood and caused wasting illness.

Vicuña Cifuentes also made an undisciplined comparison with the medieval European succubus. That comparison is the collector’s comparativism, not Mapuche testimony and not evidence for European origin. The complete volume is available in a [public-domain scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3a/Mitos_y_supersticiones_recogidos_de_la_tradici%C3%B3n_oral_chilena_con_referencias_comparativas_a_los_de_otros_paises_latinos_%28IA_mitosysuperstici00vicu%29.pdf).

### Saunière

**[Early folklore collection]** Sperata R. de Saunière’s *Cuentos populares araucanos y chilenos*, assembled from oral tradition in the late 1910s, repeats Guevara’s documentary descriptions and reports independent southern testimony. Informants identified the Cuero as a brown, white-spotted bovine skin with many short clawed legs; some classified it as the bodily appearance of a harmful *Huecuve*. The volume also contains “El Indio y el Cuero,” narrated by Ramón Trincau of Río Bueno in 1918. Its digital scan is [held by Memoria Chilena](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0076045.pdf).

### Later corpus

- **[Learned systematization]** Ricardo Latcham, *La organización social y las creencias religiosas de los antiguos araucanos* (1924), places such harmful beings inside a reconstructed Mapuche religious system. Its value is comparative; its tendency to reconstruct a single “ancient Araucanian” system must be treated cautiously.
- **[Ethnographic republication]** Guevara’s later *El pueblo mapuche* repeats the classification of the being as a malicious water myth and preserves the Quepe and Cholchol accounts.
- **[Argentine regional tradition]** Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s collections, made around San Martín de los Andes from the 1920s onward and published in *Cuentan los araucanos* (1954) and *Tradiciones araucanas* (1962), place hide-like water beings in Neuquén lakes, including warnings that the Cuero drags children under. Her 1954 book’s bibliographic record is available through [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Cuentan_los_Araucanos.html?id=brJQAAAAMAAJ).
- **[Modern scholarly analysis]** Verónica Contreras Hauser, “El Trülkewekufe: espacio textual mítico-simbólico,” *Revista de Lenguas y Literatura Indoamericanas* 6 (1994; web publication 1996), analyzed a corpus of twenty versions as a transformation myth and as a narrative about rupture between human and supernatural spheres. The [journal record](https://lli.ufro.cl/index.php/lli/article/view/134) documents its corpus and publication.
- **[Contemporary ethnography]** Aigo and Ladio’s 2011–12 fieldwork among 36 families in three Neuquén Mapuche communities found the *cuero del agua / Trelquehuecufe / Trelwekufe* to be the most frequently cited individually named sacred or mythic water figure. Their peer-reviewed article was published in 2016. It situates the Cuero within contemporary ideas of *nwenko*, the force or owner of water, without proving that the being was historically identical to every *nwenko*. See the [open-access study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5143445/).

### Sources named in the brief but not direct Cuero attestations

A targeted examination did not locate the Cuero under this name in Ercilla’s *La Araucana*, Núñez de Pineda’s *Cautiverio feliz*, Rosales’s *Historia general*, Febrés’s or Havestadt’s dictionaries, Augusta’s *Diccionario araucano* or *Lecturas araucanas*, or the principal discussions by Faron, Foerster, Bacigalupo, Grebe, and Dillehay. Those works remain essential for Mapuche history, language, ritual, *machi*, and cosmology, but they must not be cited as though each directly described Trelquehuecufe.

Absence in searchable editions is not proof that no overlooked spelling occurs anywhere in those works. It does establish that claims of continuous documentation from Ercilla or Rosales onward require evidence not presently supplied.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Secure early attestation]** The Cuero belongs to still, deep, or dangerous waters: river pools, lakes, lagoons, and later canals, reservoirs, and coastal waters. Guevara associates it especially with remansos and whirlpools. It is not securely documented as lord of all water, rain, fishing, or the sea.

**[Contemporary regional interpretation]** In Neuquén, Aigo and Ladio’s informants placed *cuero del agua* among beings associated with the *nwenko*, the water’s owner or force. This supports a guardian/owner interpretation in some present communities, but it should not be retrojected as the sole ancient classification.

### Form

The principal documented forms are:

- **[Secure early attestation]** A flattened calf- or cow-hide-sized body with claws around its edge.
- **[Early oral testimony]** Brown or coffee-colored, mottled with whitish patches, powerful and elastic.
- **[Regional variant]** A skin or blanket that floats or lies on the shore and folds itself around prey.
- **[Early lexicographic variant]** A many-legged hide that attracts animals and drinks their blood.
- **[Learned analogy]** An “octopus,” because its edge or short appendages grasp and its body contracts.
- **[Modern popular elaboration]** A distinct mouth beneath the body and two eyestalks. These details are common online but are not the morphology given in the earliest sources.

### Eyes on the rim

**[Early twentieth-century elaboration]** A French account published in *Mysteria* in July 1913 describes the Cuero as an extended ox hide edged by innumerable eyes, with four larger eyes in the portion corresponding to the head. It also calls the creature a freshwater octopus and says it devours its victims. The passage is accessible in the [1913 French periodical](https://iapsop.com/archive/materials/mysteria/mysteria_v3_n7_jul_1913.pdf).

**[Later popular consolidation]** Carlos Keller and subsequent popular retellings repeat the eyes-around-the-margin and four-large-eyes arrangement. Because Guevara’s 1908 description and drawing show marginal claws rather than eyes, the “eyes on the rim” are not securely the earliest core. They may represent a regional description, a confusion between claws and eyes, or an early literary elaboration.

### Mouth, feeding, and “drinking”

- **[Secure early attestation]** It kills by irresistible contraction and is blamed for people and animals disappearing underwater.
- **[Regional variant]** Cavada says it devours victims.
- **[Early lexicographic variant]** Lenz’s *chueiquehuecú* attracts animals and sucks their blood.
- **[Early folklore synthesis]** Vicuña Cifuentes’s informants connect the embodied Cuero with a *Huecuve* that enters bodies through breath, sucks blood, and produces wasting.
- **[Modern retelling]** A large toothed ventral mouth and eye-tipped tentacles are vivid but inadequately sourced to the early corpus.

### Claws, “thorns,” and defensive vegetation

The early body is edged with **uñas/garras**—nails or claws—not thorns.

**[Early folklore]** The thorns belong to the human countermeasure: a branch or whole plant of **quisco**, a spiny cactus, is thrown into the water. The Cuero instinctively wraps it as if it were prey, tears itself, bleeds, and dies.

**[Regional variants]** Later southern and Argentine versions substitute calafate, other thorny shrubs, or a cactus trunk wrapped in animal viscera or skin to make it smell like prey. Some require a *machi* to attract or enchant the monster first. The version in which the Cuero itself possesses defensive thorns is not supported by the early sources examined.

### Motion and weather

**[Secure early attestation]** The Cuero’s strongest recurrent atmospheric sign is a sudden whirlpool or whirlwind. Guevara says a whirlpool in the water reveals its residence; the Cholchol narrator feared that a whirlwind could sweep a person toward it. In another early summary the creature suns itself ashore and raises a wind when returning to the water.

### Earliest image

**[Secure early iconography]** Guevara’s 1908 figure is the oldest located depiction: an irregular flattened skin with multiple hooked or claw-like projections around its edge. It is an ethnographic line drawing, not an archaeological or Indigenous cult image. No securely identified precolonial Cuero image, petroglyph, textile motif, sculpture, mask, or ritual object has been established.

### Unattested attributes

No early evidence establishes sacred colors, numbers, days, stars, planets, plants, priestly insignia, temples, weapons, regalia, or a fixed gender.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

**[Absence of evidence]** No source examined assigns the Cuero parents, siblings, consort, offspring, creator, mount, servant, or dynastic position. It does not occupy a documented place in a genealogically organized pantheon.

**[Regional origin tale]** A contemporary Argentine presentation says hides discarded after animals were butchered entered rivers and lakes, came alive, and remained below feeding on animals and humans. This is an etiological folk explanation, not a genealogy and not securely dated before the modern period. It is presented by the [Biblioteca Nacional Mariano Moreno](https://www.bn.gov.ar/micrositios/multimedia/pueblos-originarios/cuero-del-agua).

### Relation to *wekufü*

**[Early folklore testimony]** Some of Vicuña Cifuentes’s informants said the Cuero is the physical form in which a *Huecuve/Huecufe* becomes visible in water. This is a classification or embodiment relation, not an encounter between two independently acting named beings.

**[Variant]** Other sources simply call the animal *Trelquehuecufe*, without narrating an external *Wekufü* entering it. The evidence therefore permits two readings: “a hide-shaped harmful being” and “a hide body assumed by harmful power.”

### Relation to *Shumpall* and *nwenko*

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Aigo and Ladio cite scholarship in which *Shumpall* personifies water and can appear as a human, sheep, hide, bull, pig, horse, or stone. Their own fieldwork grouped the Cuero among manifestations associated with *nwenko*. This is a structural equation in modern ethnographic analysis, not an attested myth in which a named Shumpall meets the Cuero.

### Relation to *ngürüfilu*

**[Historical conflation]** The *ngürüfilu* (“fox-snake”) is a different aquatic monster in Guevara: it inhabits rivers, betrays its presence through a whirlpool, and drags victims under. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century authors sometimes gave the *ngürüfilu* a rounded hide-like shape, and later compilers consequently conflated it with the Cuero.

**[Source-critical conclusion]** Shared habitat and behavior explain the conflation, but Guevara lists and illustrates them separately. They should remain separate unless a particular source explicitly equates them.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

No securely documented narrative interaction—meeting, combat, alliance, kinship, seduction, judgment, theft, rescue, or joint adventure—between Cuero/Trelquehuecufe and another individually named supernatural being was located.

- **Wekufü — embodiment/classification, not an episode.** Some informants identify the Cuero as the aquatic bodily form of a *wekufü*. Vicuña Cifuentes, 1915. **[Early folklore variant]**
- **Shumpall — modern classificatory relation, not a meeting.** Modern scholarship treats a hide as one possible water-personification and groups water beings under *Shumpall/nwenko*. Aigo and Ladio, 2016, drawing on Kuramochi and Mora Penroz. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Ngürüfilu — comparison and conflation, not interaction.** Guevara lists it immediately after the Cuero as a similar but distinct being; earlier and later authors sometimes merge their descriptions. 1908 onward. **[Secure comparison / learned conflation]**
- **Machi — human ritual specialist, not a divine being.** Later narratives have a *machi* lure or defeat the Cuero with thorny bait. **[Regional folklore / later elaboration]**

No interaction with Ngünechen, Pillan, Antü, Küyen, Kai Kai, Tren Tren, Trauco, Caleuche, Camahueto, Pincoya, or Invunche is securely attested.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

The Cuero possesses no surviving epic cycle. Its narrative corpus consists of short legends, encounter reports, etiological explanations, and cautionary tales.

### A. The fatal bathing place at Quepe

**[Secure early oral testimony]** A Mapuche man enters a still pool in the Quepe River and immediately disappears. Searchers fail to find his body after four days. Because other people are also drowned there, the community concludes that a Trelquehuecufe inhabits the pool. People thereafter avoid bathing there.

The narrative’s mechanism is diagnostic: repeated unexplained drownings establish the monster’s presence. It does not describe eyes, a mouth, blood-drinking, or a ritual killing.

### B. Lorenzo Manquian’s sighting at Cholchol

**[Secure early oral testimony]** At dusk, Lorenzo Manquian sees on the stones beside the Cholchol River an object resembling a brown calf hide with whitish spots, surrounded by claws. He runs home and returns with his father. They approach cautiously, believing a whirlwind could carry them close enough to be seized. The being has vanished. Manquian explains that many men have been devoured by this strong, elastic Cuero.

This is the earliest detailed first-person morphology. The creature neither attacks nor speaks; its identity is inferred from appearance and inherited knowledge.

### C. The Manta of Chiloé

**[Regional variant; 1914]** Cavada’s islanders describe an extended skin concealed in water. When a human or animal enters, it rises, folds around the victim, drags it to the bottom, and eats it. Its narrative function includes warning children away from dangerous water.

The Chiloé Manta has the Cuero’s predatory folding action but is reported under a Spanish regional name. Treating it as “the same being” is a historical folkloristic identification, not proof of a single unchanged precolonial myth.

### D. Death by thorny bait

**[Early folklore]** The Cuero can be killed by throwing a quisco into its water. Mistaking the spiny plant for prey, it wraps itself around it; its contraction drives the spines through its body, and it tears itself apart or bleeds to death.

Variants change the plant and ritual:

- quisco or cactus in central Chile;
- calafate or another thorny shrub farther south;
- thorny wood wrapped in animal skin or viscera;
- a *machi* chanting or preparing a potion that makes the bait attractive.

The simple quisco version is securely early twentieth century. The fully staged *machi*-and-potion version is later and should not be presented as the universal Mapuche form.

### E. The deceptive skin on shore

**[Regional folklore]** Some versions have the Cuero mimic a harmless discarded hide on a beach. A person who steps or lies upon it is wrapped up and carried into the water. Argentine descriptions emphasize its ability to blend into a shoreline.

**[Modern retelling]** A widely circulated episode has a woman place her baby on an apparently ordinary hide while washing clothes; the hide awakens and carries the child away. The motif is plausible within the tradition but was not located in the earliest Guevara, Cavada, or Vicuña Cifuentes passages. It should be dated to later oral or popular transmission unless a specific older collection is supplied.

### F. The animated discarded hide

**[Modern/undated regional etiological tradition]** Animal hides thrown into rivers after slaughter acquire life and become Cueros. This explains the monster through a familiar pastoral practice. Because cattle and sheep are post-contact animals, its present form cannot be demonstrated as an unchanged pre-Hispanic narrative.

### G. The water-hide that abducts young women

**[Argentine regional variant]** Some Neuquén material calls the being *lafken trülke* and describes it as abducting young women or children. A modern song collected near Junín de los Andes adapts this narrative. The motif belongs to regional performance and should not be retrojected onto the 1908 Cholchol material.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult and institutions

**[Absence of evidence]** There is no documented Cuero temple, shrine, altar, priesthood, festival, pilgrimage, calendrical feast, sacrificial economy, theophoric personal name, or political cult. No archaeological assemblage has been securely identified as devoted to it.

The being is therefore not a “deity with a cult” in the institutional sense. Its historical record is one of belief, avoidance, oral narrative, and protective action.

### Ritual specialists

**[Later regional folklore]** Some versions require a *machi* to remove a Cuero. The *machi* attracts it to the shore or enchants thorny bait, after which its own contraction destroys it. This represents the general role of *machi* as specialists able to diagnose or oppose harmful agencies. It does not establish a dedicated Cuero priesthood.

**[Unestablished]** No primary description was located of Cuero rites within *machitun* or *nguillatun*. General ethnographies of those ceremonies should not be used to imply the being’s participation without a direct textual link.

### Offerings and sacrifice

**[Absence of evidence]** No offering to, feeding of, or propitiatory sacrifice for the Cuero is securely documented. The animal flesh, skin, or viscera attached to thorny bait is a trap, not worship.

No human sacrifice to the Cuero is documented. Human deaths in the narratives are predation or explanations for drowning, not ritual killings.

### Practical religious behavior

The most securely documented practices are:

- avoiding water believed to contain it;
- interpreting whirlpools, sudden wind, and unexplained drowning as signs;
- warning children away from dangerous pools;
- approaching a suspected sighting cautiously;
- killing or expelling it with thorny vegetation;
- in later accounts, consulting a *machi*.

These are better termed apotropaic and risk-management practices than cult.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before European documentation

**[Unestablished]** An Indigenous oral antecedent is likely because the core name is Mapudungun and early twentieth-century collectors obtained accounts from Mapuche narrators. Its precise precolonial form and antiquity cannot be reconstructed.

The modern cow-hide image incorporates cattle, an animal introduced after Spanish conquest. This does not prove the entire being is post-contact: older aquatic-agent traditions could have adopted a new visible form. Aigo and Ladio’s contemporary data show exactly such incorporation of post-Hispanic animals into Mapuche water knowledge.

### Late eighteenth century: hide-like aquatic monsters

**[Possible antecedent]** La Porte, Vidaurre, Carvallo, and Molina preserve reports of aquatic predators described either as *ngürüfilu/guirivilo* or *manta* and sometimes likened to a hide. The categories are unstable. This is evidence for the motif, not yet for the securely named Trelquehuecufe.

### Nineteenth-century publication and national folklore

Carvallo’s manuscript was published in 1875–76; Molina circulated in Italian and translation; regional accounts increasingly entered Chilean natural history, local history, and folklore. Descriptions were often filtered through European zoological categories—ray, octopus, fabulous animal—and through skeptical language about “superstition.”

### 1908–19: consolidation of the documentary type

Guevara’s 1908 description and image establish the type: calf-hide body, marginal claws, deep water, constriction, drowning, and whirlpool. Lenz registers related names and the blood-drinking variant. Cavada documents the Chiloé Manta. Vicuña Cifuentes and Saunière synthesize and expand the distribution.

This is the decisive documentary period. It does not necessarily mark the legend’s creation; it marks its entry into a recoverable corpus.

### 1920s–60s: ethnographic systematization and trans-Andean record

Latcham incorporated the being into a reconstruction of “Araucanian religion.” Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collecting documented analogous water-hide narratives east of the Andes. The figure became part of Argentine as well as Chilean Mapuche folklore collections.

At the same time, national folklore works generalized “El Cuero” from Coquimbo to Chiloé. The unpublished mid-century *Enciclopedia Chilena* calls it one of Chile’s most widespread beliefs and identifies the Chiloé form as Manta. Its [entry](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Enciclopedia_Chilena/Folclore/Cuero,_El) describes its national distribution and quisco trap.

### Late twentieth century: literary and structural interpretation

Collectors such as Oreste Plath incorporated it into geographical catalogues of Chilean myth. Contreras Hauser’s twenty-version analysis treated it as a transformation narrative organizing relations between human and supernatural worlds.

The figure also entered schoolbooks, illustrated myth anthologies, dictionaries of fantastic Mapuche beings, and local heritage publications. In this stage, versions were frequently normalized into one composite creature.

### Twenty-first century: living knowledge and media folklore

**[Contemporary ethnography]** Aigo and Ladio show that the Cuero remained known in Neuquén Mapuche communities in 2011–12, not merely as an archival fossil. Their data also show cultural change: horses, cattle, sheep, saints, and other post-contact figures have been integrated into water cosmology.

**[Public folklore]** Chilean and Argentine museums, libraries, schools, heritage sites, podcasts, blogs, cryptozoology pages, and social media continue to retell the legend. This is continuity of narrative recognition, not evidence of a centralized revival cult.

### Suppression and conversion

Mapuche and Huilliche religious life was profoundly affected by Spanish warfare, missions, settlement, Chilean occupation of Araucanía, Argentina’s Conquest of the Desert, schooling, and Christianization. Nevertheless, no source located documents a campaign specifically against Trelquehuecufe belief, destruction of a Cuero sanctuary, or a formal ecclesiastical prohibition naming it.

Christian vocabulary did affect classification: *wekufü* was repeatedly translated as “diablo,” encouraging the names “Cuero del Diablo” and “Manta del Diablo.” That is a demonstrable interpretive overlay, but not proof that the being derives from Christianity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Folklore and education

The Cuero is now one of the most recognizable Chilean water monsters. It appears in school readers and collections of national myth, commonly as a safety tale explaining drownings and warning children away from deep or turbulent water. The *Enciclopedia Chilena* explicitly recommended pedagogical use to prevent rural bathing and river-crossing accidents.

That practical reading is plausible and documented, but it is not necessarily the legend’s original or only function.

### Cryptozoology

**[Modern reinvention]** Cryptozoological writers reinterpret the Cuero as:

- an unknown giant freshwater ray;
- a giant octopus;
- a surviving or anomalous aquatic species;
- a bryozoan colony;
- floating livestock hide, vegetation, or debris;
- a misidentified whirlpool.

These proposals explain selected physical features but have not established the existence of a zoological Cuero or the legend’s origin. South American freshwater stingrays are real, but no historical evidence connects a particular species to the formation of the Mapuche name and narrative. The colonial word *manta* also need not mean “manta ray.”

### Visual reinvention

Modern artists commonly give the creature:

- a circular ray-like body;
- a ventral toothed mouth;
- two long eye-bearing tentacles;
- numerous red eyes around the rim;
- spikes or thorns;
- a glossy black or blood-red surface.

Only the flattened hide, marginal grasping structures, brown/white mottling, and sometimes numerous eyes have early documentary support. The rest are modern attempts to turn a variable oral image into a stable monster design.

### Occultism and neopaganism

**[Negative finding]** The dedicated search found no significant Renaissance hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, ceremonial-magic, chaos-magic, or modern grimoire tradition centered on Cuero. No established neopagan or reconstructionist organization devoted specifically to it was identified.

Generic internet assertions that it is a “demon,” summonable entity, or ancient god are modern extrapolations unless tied to a named community or text.

### Political and extremist appropriation

**[Negative finding]** No documented nationalist, fascist, racialist, extremist, or state cult appropriation specific to Cuero was found. Its classification as “Chilean mythology” can participate in national folklore-building, sometimes obscuring Mapuche provenance, but that is different from organized political worship.

### Commercial culture

The being appears in bestiaries, children’s books, tourist material, tabletop and digital-game monster lists, videos, podcasts, and merchandise. Such uses generally combine features from Guevara, Cavada, Keller, modern illustrations, and online summaries rather than preserve one locally attested version.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is it Cuero, Manta, or Ngürüfilu?

This is the central identification problem.

- Colonial sources describe a *guirivilo/ngürüfilu* whose morphology fluctuates between fox-serpent and rounded hide.
- Guevara distinguishes the hide-shaped Trelquehuecufe from the fox-snake Ngürüfilu.
- Cavada’s Manta behaves like the Cuero.
- Later encyclopedias treat *Cuero*, *Manta*, *chueiquehuecú*, and *Trelquehuecufe* as regional equivalents.

The safest conclusion is that several aquatic-monster traditions overlapped and influenced one another. A single timeless taxonomy cannot be reconstructed.

### Indigenous category or Christian “demon”?

The compound’s *wekufü* element supports association with harmful supernatural agency. But “demon” and “devil” are missionary/Christian translations that imply a theology of absolute evil not necessarily present in Mapuche categories.

Vicuña Cifuentes’s testimony is especially useful because it records variation: **some**, not all, informants said the Cuero was a bodily form of *Huecuve*. Therefore the being can be an animal-like predator in one account and an embodiment of harmful force in another.

### Pre-Hispanic or post-contact?

The Mapudungun name and Indigenous testimony argue against treating it as merely a recent Chilean invention. Yet the cow-, calf-, and sheep-hide imagery depends on post-contact pastoral life. Modern ethnobiology shows that introduced livestock now occupy a major place in Mapuche water-being discourse.

The evidence supports historical transformation: an older water-danger complex may have absorbed the cow-hide form. It does not allow reconstruction of the being’s pre-contact appearance.

### Octopus, ray, or hide?

“Octopus” first appears as an ethnographer’s analogy for contraction and multiple grasping appendages. “Manta” can mean blanket and only secondarily invites comparison with a ray. Neither term is a secure Indigenous zoological classification.

No specimen, bone, preserved skin, photograph, or biological description has validated either identification. Cryptozoological naturalization is modern speculation.

### Eyes versus claws

Guevara’s earliest picture and text emphasize claws. The 1913 French account and later Keller tradition emphasize innumerable eyes plus four large eyes. Some popular images combine both.

The discrepancy should be preserved as variant history. There is no basis for declaring the many-eyed form primordial.

### Thorns

The tradition securely gives thorns to the trap, not the monster. Claims that the Cuero naturally bears defensive thorns probably result from inversion or modern monster design.

### Source criticism

Early collectors made the tradition recoverable but also reshaped it:

- Guevara sometimes rationalized beings as misperceived animals or natural phenomena.
- Lenz filtered oral vocabulary through historical lexicography.
- Vicuña Cifuentes favored broad cross-cultural comparisons, including an unsupported European succubus analogy.
- Latcham reconstructed unified ancient systems from geographically and chronologically diverse material.
- National folklore collections often translated distinct Mapuche, Huilliche, Chilote, and rural Chilean accounts into one “Chilean myth.”

Contemporary scholarship is more attentive to named narrators, place, language, environmental knowledge, and historical hybridization.

### State of the evidence

The most important surviving primary materials are printed ethnographies and folklore collections rather than precolonial artifacts:

- Guevara’s 1908 description and drawing;
- Guevara’s Mapudungun/Spanish Quepe and Cholchol narratives;
- Lenz’s dictionary and supplements;
- Cavada’s 1914 Chiloé description;
- Vicuña Cifuentes’s 1915 synthesis;
- Saunière’s southern oral collections;
- Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén corpus.

Digitized copies survive through the Biblioteca Nacional de Chile/Memoria Chilena, Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes, Wikimedia repositories, university journals, and Argentine collections. No manuscript, inscription, or archaeological discovery has pushed the secure named attestation earlier than the modern ethnographic period.

## 11. Conclusions

The reconstructable Cuero is not a neglected high god with a lost temple cult. It is a place-bound aquatic danger whose documentary force lies in oral testimony: dangerous pools, missing bodies, sudden wind, whirlpools, and something on the stones that resembles a mottled calf hide.

The secure core established by 1908–15 is a living or supernatural hide that inhabits water, grasps with marginal claws or appendages, folds around humans and animals, and kills by constriction, drowning, or devouring. The Chilote Manta is an early regional cognate. Blood-drinking belongs particularly to Lenz’s *chueiquehuecú* and *Huecuve* interpretations. Innumerable marginal eyes appear early, by 1913, but not in Guevara’s foundational description. Thorny plants are its vulnerability, not an securely attested part of its body.

The Cuero’s historical development is a record of transformation: Indigenous Mapudungun classification, post-contact livestock imagery, Chiloé and central-Chilean localization, trans-Andean survival in Neuquén, national folklore systematization, and modern cryptozoological and visual reinvention. Its variants—not a harmonized composite—are the history.
