# Küyen: Research Dossier

**Class:** A celestial power/personified Moon in Mapuche, Huilliche, and Puelmapu traditions; in different records Küyen is an astral being, a female or gender-composite deity, one member of a fourfold lunar family (*Meli Küyen*), or simply the visible Moon and lunar month. Calling Küyen a discrete “moon goddess” is convenient but can falsely impose a fixed Greco-Roman-style pantheon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Luis de Valdivia’s Mapudungun–Spanish vocabulary, printed with his *Arte y gramática general de la lengua que corre en todo el Reyno de Chile* at Lima in **1606**, records *cuyen* “moon” and compounds for new, full, and waning moon. This securely establishes the name and lunar-phase vocabulary, but not a goddess, mythology, genealogy, temple cult, or marriage to the Sun. The earliest detailed narratives presently identifiable are oral texts recorded in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, especially the Pampa/Neuquén materials of Robert Lehmann-Nitsche and Bertha Koessler-Ilg.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The problem of identity

Küyen is first of all Mapudungun for “Moon” and, by extension, “month.” The sources do not consistently distinguish the astronomical body, its living agency, and a named supernatural person. Consequently:

- **[Secure early attestation]** *cuyen* is the Moon in Valdivia’s 1606 vocabulary.
- **[Later ethnographic attestation]** prayers and cosmological accounts can address Küyen as an agent, life-giver, elder, mother, or member of a divine family.
- **[Regional narrative]** Puelmapu stories personify Küyen as Antü’s wife and explain lunar markings through marital conflict.
- **[Modern systematization]** encyclopedic descriptions commonly turn this variable complex into a single “principal Wangülen,” “queen of the stars,” or “moon goddess.” Those precise ranks are not established by the seventeenth-century record.

This variability is not terminological noise: it is the historical evidence. Mapuche religious thought frequently treats powers relationally and plurally rather than as permanently individuated gods.

### Names and spellings

The relevant Mapudungun forms appear in several alphabets:

- **Küyen / küyen** — common modern spelling.
- **Küyeṉ** — spelling with final interdental nasal in some linguistic conventions.
- **Küyeñ / küyen’ / küyeh** — variants reflecting different analyses of the final nasal.
- **Cuyen, Cuyén, Cüyen, Kuyén, Kueyen** — missionary, Spanish, popular, and international transcriptions.
- **Küyen** also means a lunar month or month-like interval. Modern dictionaries preserve sentences describing the Moon’s rising and post-full delay, confirming that the word remains ordinary astronomical vocabulary as well as a culturally charged name.

The form has no separate indigenous script. Mapudungun was transmitted orally before colonial alphabetization; Latin-letter spellings are competing representations of its sounds.

### Phase and age terms

Valdivia’s 1606 vocabulary already gives:

- *huecuyen* — “new moon,” modernized as *we küyen*.
- *purcuyen* — “full moon”; later sources normally use *apon küyen*, “filled/full Moon.”
- *chomeycümeyün cuyen* — waning Moon.

Later recorded terms include:

- *we küyen* — new Moon.
- *apon küyen* — full Moon.
- *wüñoñküyen* — returning or post-full Moon.
- *lan Küyen* — “dead Moon,” lunar eclipse.
- *wütre küyen* — “cold Moon,” used in a seasonal designation.
- *kadü küyen* — “gray/ashen Moon,” a seasonal name.
- *Küyen ülcha* — maiden/young Moon.
- *Küyen ñuke* — mother Moon.
- *Küyen kuse* — old-woman Moon.

The last three circulate widely as a neat tripartite sequence of maiden, mother, and old woman. **[Caution]** Although the age vocabulary accords with genuine Mapuche classificatory language, a fully standardized three-phase “Triple Goddess” scheme is not securely attested in the early missionary corpus. Its resemblance to modern European neopagan Maiden–Mother–Crone theology makes unsourced modern presentations especially suspect.

### Gender and titles

Attested or reported designations include:

- **Ñuke Küyen / Madre Luna** — Mother Moon.
- **Küyen kuse** — old-woman Moon.
- **Küyen fucha** — old-man Moon.
- **Meli Küyen** — the four Moon beings/powers.
- **Küyen ülcha** — young-woman or maiden Moon.

Faron’s 1950s fieldwork, published as *Hawks of the Sun* in 1964, and later work by Sonia Montecino record prayers in which both Sun and Moon receive masculine and feminine age aspects: *Antü fucha*, *Antü kuse*, *Küyen fucha*, and *Küyen kuse*. María Ester Grebe, Sergio Pacheco, and José Segura’s 1972 cosmological model instead organized major divinities as four-person families: old man, old woman, young man, and young woman. Thus “Küyen is a goddess” captures one strand but suppresses the documented masculine, plural, and tetradic formulations.

### Etymology

Two levels must be separated:

1. **[Secure]** Within Mapudungun, *küyen* is the inherited conventional word for Moon/month, productive in compounds concerning phases, menstruation, and time.
2. **[Disputed historical reconstruction]** Tomás Guevara proposed derivation from Quechua *killa*, “Moon.” More recent comparison has connected Mapudungun *küyen*, Quechua *killa*, Aymara *phaxsi* only indirectly, or proposed a Puquina source for some central-Andean and Mapuche celestial vocabulary. Phonetic resemblance and Andean contact make borrowing possible, but no surviving document dates or demonstrates a particular transfer. The claim that *küyen* is simply “a Quechua word” is therefore stronger than the evidence permits.

The shared Sun–Moon marriage pattern in Mapuche and central-Andean traditions likewise does not prove that Mapuche Küyen is merely Inca Mama Killa. Parallel symbolism, borrowing, and independent development remain competing possibilities.

### Cross-cultural identifications

- **Mama Killa/Quilla:** **[Modern scholarly comparison; possible older syncretism]** The Inca Moon was female and paired with the Sun, making comparison natural. No colonial Mapuche document found explicitly equates Küyen with Mama Killa as a formal interpretatio.
- **Virgin Mary:** **[Christian-era syncretism]** Some twentieth- and twenty-first-century machi identify or closely associate the Moon with Mary. Bacigalupo records a machi for whom Mary, Moon, and Jesus were inseparable. This is lived syncretism, not evidence that pre-contact Küyen was “really” Mary.
- **Tehuelche/Selk’nam Moon:** **[Regional transculturation]** Rodrigo Moulian and collaborators argue that some Puelmapu stories collected by Koessler-Ilg partially calque Chon-family myths of the estranged Sun–Moon couple. This identifies a shared Patagonian narrative field, not a timeless identity of all the beings involved.

No defensible source identifies Küyen with the *añchümalleñ/anchimallén*. Modern websites sometimes do so, but the latter belongs to a different class of luminous or dangerous spirit.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

Valdivia’s 1606 work is the earliest secure written attestation currently established. It records the noun and lunar phases. Its physical form is a missionary grammar and vocabulary printed at Lima in Latin characters. It proves that the lunar term and a differentiated lunar vocabulary existed at the beginning of the seventeenth century. It does **not** call Küyen a goddess, describe worship, or supply a myth.

No pre-contact Mapuche manuscript corpus exists. Archaeology can establish observation of celestial cycles or solar orientations only indirectly; an object bearing a circle or crescent cannot be assigned to Küyen without contextual evidence.

### Colonial and early republican witnesses

- **Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana* (three parts, 1569, 1578, 1589):** literary evidence for Spanish representations of the Arauco War, not a dependable inventory of Mapuche religion. No secure Küyen narrative has been located in it.
- **Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz* (written 1673; first published 1863):** important eyewitness material on seventeenth-century Mapuche life. It does not securely attest the later Küyen–Antü myth cycle.
- **Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del Reino de Chile, Flandes indiano* (completed circa 1674; printed 1877–1878):** indispensable but Christian and missionary in framework. Again, no full Küyen biography or temple cult emerges.
- **Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* (Lima, 1765):** lexical evidence for *cuyen* and related forms.
- **Bernhard Havestadt, *Chilidúgú* (Münster, 1777):** records the word and embeds it in Christian catechetical polemic. In one hymn he denies that Pillan, Antü, thunder, hot water, or Cüyen are gods and says God created them. This is evidence both for the vocabulary and for missionary suppression/reclassification—not an indigenous declaration that Küyen lacked agency.
- **Félix José de Augusta, *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) and *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916):** extensive early twentieth-century Mapudungun documentation. The dictionary fixes lunar, calendrical, and bodily vocabulary but should not be mistaken for a pre-contact theological canon.
- **Rodolfo Lenz, *Estudios araucanos* (1895–1897):** Mapudungun dialogues, songs, tales, historical narratives, and descriptions collected in several dialect areas. Its importance is methodological: it preserves named speakers and vernacular text, while also reflecting late nineteenth-century colonial conditions.
- **Tomás Guevara, *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* (1908) and related studies:** records religious and social interpretations but frequently advances diffusionist etymologies now treated cautiously.
- **Ricardo Latcham, *La organización social y las creencias religiosas de los antiguos araucanos* (1924):** influential reconstruction of an “ancient” system from heterogeneous materials. It is secondary synthesis, not direct pre-contact testimony.
- **Pascual Coña, narrated to Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach, *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX* (1930):** an exceptional indigenous life-history and cultural record. Its seasonal vocabulary describes periods by rain, cold Moon, sowing, fruits, and harvest. Because Moesbach edited and translated Coña’s Mapudungun, both voices and editorial layers matter.
- **Robert Lehmann-Nitsche, “El diluvio según los araucanos de la Pampa” (1919):** publishes the “Texto de Chimpay,” an important Puelmapu account connecting Sun and Moon to creation/flood traditions.
- **Bertha Koessler-Ilg, *Cuentan los araucanos* (1954; expanded/posthumous editions):** principal source for narrative Küyen: marriage, pursuit, domestic conflict, creation, and Mother Moon material collected around Neuquén and Lake Lácar.
- **Louis Faron, *Hawks of the Sun* (1964):** documents ritual kinship, prayer, and divine age/gender pairs in Chilean Mapuche communities.
- **Grebe, Pacheco, and Segura, “Cosmovisión mapuche” (1972):** formulates the highly influential seven-platform cosmos and fourfold divine-family model. It is a fieldwork-based ethnomodel, but not a universally accepted Mapuche creed.
- **María Ester Grebe, “El kultrún mapuche: un microcosmo simbólico” (1973):** foundational documentation of drum construction, performance, and cosmographic designs.
- **Rolf Foerster, *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche* (1993):** reviews colonial and modern evidence and the effects of Christian categories on descriptions of Mapuche religion.
- **Ana Mariella Bacigalupo, *Shamans of the Foye Tree* (2007), following earlier articles and *La voz del kultrun en la modernidad* (2001):** documents twentieth- and twenty-first-century machi theology, gender, healing, Catholic and evangelical relations, and political modernity.
- **Rodrigo Moulian and collaborators (2018, 2022):** reassess Sun–Moon symbolism comparatively and distinguish Mapuche material from Patagonian narrative calques.

### Chiloé and Huilliche records

Francisco Javier Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914), Oreste Plath, Vicuña Cifuentes, and later Chiloé collectors document an intensely syncretic folklore combining Huilliche, Chono, Spanish Catholic, maritime, and later literary materials. They show that lunar timing survived in fishing, planting, livestock, weather lore, and household practice. They do not establish a separate Chiloé temple cult of a goddess named Küyen. Claims that every nocturnal Chilote female being is an aspect or daughter of Küyen are unsupported.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Secure lexical and ethnographic complex]** Küyen governs or marks:

- Night light and lunar phases.
- The month and the recurrence of time.
- Menstruation, female fertility, pregnancy, and maturation.
- Germination, planting, sap, growth, and harvest timing.
- Healing and machi ritual symbolism.
- In some narratives, care for humankind jointly with Antü.
- In regional stories, marriage, estrangement, and cosmic pursuit.

The Moon’s cycle is often said to contain twenty-eight days, but exact calendrical schemata in modern educational literature should not automatically be projected into antiquity. Mapuche seasonal reckoning used ecological signs—rain, cold, flowering, ripening, harvest, animal behavior—alongside Sun and Moon. It was not necessarily a single, pan-Mapuche, numerically standardized calendar.

### Mother and mistress of women

The strongest evidence is linguistic and ritual:

- *Küyen* means Moon/month.
- *peküyen*—literally “see the Moon”—means to menstruate or reach menarche.
- *küyentun/cüyentun* and *cuyenpen* are recorded for menstruation.
- Ritual discourse can say a premenstrual girl *petu peküyenlay*, “has not yet seen the Moon.”
- *Apon Küyen*, the full Moon, is described as carrying the light, warmth, energy, and vigor needed for germination.

Margarita Calfio records María Raguileo’s story that menstruation began when a girl exposed her vulva while urinating under the full Moon; Küyen’s powerful gaze caused her to bleed. **[Modern-recorded oral tradition]** This is a particular etiological account, not a universal doctrine. It presents the Moon as a disciplining female power and makes explicit the link between lunar light, sexuality, bodily cycles, and social regulation.

Calling Küyen “mistress of all women” goes beyond the secure formulations unless tied to a particular speaker or text. “Mother Moon,” fertility power, and controller or marker of women’s cycles are better attested.

### Planting and seasonal time

Pascual Coña’s recorded seasonal vocabulary includes a rainy/cold/sowing period designated by terms including *ketrawüngen* (“time of sowing”) and *wütre küyen* (“cold Moon”). Later ethnography states that planting during *apon Küyen* promotes even, vigorous germination. Farmers also read the orientation and appearance of the new Moon as a forecast of rain, cold, dryness, or heat.

These practices vary by crop, ecology, and community. Chiloé’s maritime climate, central Araucanía, and the eastern Andean valleys cannot be assigned one universal planting rule. Modern claims that Mapuche people planted every crop only at full Moon are overgeneralizations.

### The kultrun

The *kultrun* is the shallow ceremonial drum of the machi, constructed and consecrated through procedures that can include prayer, song, breath, offerings, and the placement of substances inside it. Its membrane may bear:

- A cross or four-part division for *meli witran mapu*, the four directions/quarters.
- Sun, Moon, and stars.
- Additional individual signs meaningful to the owning machi.

Grebe interpreted the kultrun as a microcosm: its bowl can represent the world and its painted skin the ordered terrestrial and celestial field. In some designs Küyen occupies one quadrant while Antü and stars occupy others. **[Important limitation]** There is no single mandatory “Küyen symbol” or universal diagram. Not every circle on a kultrun is the Moon, and not all drums reproduce Grebe’s published schema. Bacigalupo’s work emphasizes that each machi’s kultrun is biographically and spiritually specific.

### Appearance and material associations

No securely identified pre-contact anthropomorphic image of Küyen is known. Her primary visible “image” is the Moon itself. Later depictions employ crescents, disks, female figures, silver ornaments, or paired Sun–Moon designs.

Silver (*lüqen* in color symbolism; *plata* in colonial material culture) is strongly associated in modern and historical Mapuche women’s adornment with lunar whiteness, fertility, protection, and female status. Yet attributing every item of Mapuche silverwork directly to Küyen is interpretive rather than epigraphically demonstrable.

No secure ancient sacred animal, plant, fixed color code, weekday, numerical talisman, weapon, or temple statue belongs to Küyen. The *foye* tree is central to machi practice generally, but is not exclusively Küyen’s plant. The number four belongs to a broad Mapuche cosmological grammar, not solely to the Moon.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

### Genealogical variants

1. **Sun–Moon spouses:**  
   **[Puelmapu regional tradition, documented 1919–1954]** Küyen is Antü’s wife. In some stories they are primordial parents or creators; in others they are estranged celestial spouses.

2. **Two names/aspects of a creator:**  
   **[Regional theological variant]** Koessler-Ilg recorded testimony that older people called the being above Antü and “mother” Luna because the pair alternated in lighting Earth and caring for humanity; some used “Sun and Moon” instead of *Rey Chau*. This blurs polytheistic spouses into complementary manifestations of a high creator.

3. **Fourfold lunar family:**  
   **[Twentieth-century ethnographic systematization]** Grebe’s *Meli Küyen* comprises elder male, elder female, young male, and young female lunar powers. Küyen is therefore not necessarily one female individual.

4. **Mother of stars or first woman:**  
   Some popular narratives make Küyen ruler or mother of the *wangülen* and say she selected one of her daughters to become the first earthly woman. **[Late collected/modern retelling]** These claims require story-specific citation and do not occur in the 1606 lexical evidence.

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Antü — spouses and complementary sky powers.** They alternate in illuminating Earth and caring for people. Source: Koessler-Ilg, material published 1954. **[Regional oral tradition; later ethnographic attestation]**
- **Antü — pursuit after marital separation.** Antü eternally follows Küyen but cannot catch her. Source: Koessler-Ilg, recorded in Neuquén/Lake Lácar and published 1954. **[Regional variant]**
- **Antü — domestic violence and lunar markings.** Antü struck Küyen, leaving her face stained and one eye bruised; she refuses reconciliation while the marks remain. Source: Koessler-Ilg 1954, analyzed as a likely Patagonian/Chon-area calque by Moulian and colleagues. **[Regional transculturation]**
- **Antü — marriage at the world’s end.** A collected Puelmapu story has the pair marry where Earth ends. Source: Koessler-Ilg corpus, mid-twentieth-century publication. **[Regional variant]**
- **Antü — joint creation or primordial governance.** Sun and Moon appear before the serpent conflict and may create or oversee beings. Sources: “El Dios del cielo y sus rebeldes hijos” and “Sobre Adán y Eva,” Koessler-Ilg 1954. **[Regional oral tradition with evident Christian-era vocabulary]**
- **Treng Treng — creator/creature relation.** In Koessler-Ilg narratives Sun and Moon precede and create or govern the saving serpent. **[Regional, Christian-era collected cosmogony]**
- **Kay Kay — creator/creature relation.** The destructive flood serpent likewise appears as subordinate to the primordial astral beings in those versions. **[Regional variant]**
- **Ngenechen/Rey Chau — identity or incorporation rather than episode.** Some accounts merge the divine family, creator, Sun, and Moon; others keep them distinct. Sources: Koessler-Ilg 1954; Marileo 1995; Bacigalupo’s late twentieth-century fieldwork. **[Syncretistic/variable theology]**
- **Virgin Mary — identification or intimate association.** A modern machi account reported by Bacigalupo linked Mary, Moon, and Jesus. **[Modern lived Catholic–Mapuche syncretism]**
- **Jesus — association through the same machi’s Catholic cosmology.** Source: Bacigalupo, fieldwork published 2007. **[Modern syncretism]**
- **Wangülen — superior, mother, or member of the astral family.** Later syntheses call Küyen the principal Wangülen and ruler of stars, while Grebe treats Moon and star families as adjacent ranks. No early narrative securely documents Küyen issuing commands to named star beings. **[Modern scholarly/popular systematization]**

No securely sourced episode was found in which Küyen meets Pillan as a named individual, battles a *wekufe*, interacts with the Chiloé figures Millalobo or Pincoya, or participates directly in a human sacrifice. Pantheon membership is not interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Antü and Küyen, the estranged spouses

In the best-documented Puelmapu version, Antü pursues his wife across the sky. He cannot reach her because she withdraws while showing the stained face and bruised eye left by a blow he gave her. She will not reconcile until the marks disappear. Their unending separation explains their celestial movement and the markings visible on the lunar disk.

- **Earliest located source:** oral material published by Koessler-Ilg in 1954, collected in the Neuquén/Lake Lácar region.
- **Variant:** the couple marry at the edge of the world but repeatedly fail to meet.
- **Comparative finding:** Moulian and colleagues judge the dysfunctional-marriage motif to be partially calqued from, or circulating with, Selk’nam/Tehuelche traditions. It should not be projected without qualification across all Mapuche territory.
- **Modern softening:** children’s and internet retellings often convert the violence into a sentimental separation or jealousy story.

### B. “The tears of the Moon”

A widely circulated modern story says Antü and Küyen married under Ngenechen, cared respectively for men and for women and children, then quarreled and separated. Antü took a beautiful maiden to the sky as the golden star or morning/evening star. Seeing them, Küyen wept; her tears became southern Chile’s lakes.

The readily traceable forms are twenty-first-century educational and online retellings. Their use of a single supreme “God,” sharply gendered administrative duties, romantic dialogue, and a named rival star suggests literary reshaping. **[Modern retelling; ancient status unestablished]** It should not be cited as a securely pre-contact myth unless an older collector and speaker are identified.

### C. Küyen and the origin of menstruation

A girl urinates outdoors in full moonlight and exposes her genitals. The Moon’s gaze or punishment causes bleeding, establishing *kutran küyen* and the recurring menstrual condition. The version was narrated by María Raguileo and transmitted through Sonia Montecino and Margarita Calfio.

- **Tier:** modern-recorded oral etiological tradition.
- **Function:** relates menarche, modesty, fertility, light, and lunar periodicity.
- **Caution:** it is one account, not a universal Mapuche doctrine that menstruation is divine punishment.

### D. Sun and Moon as primordial creators

Koessler-Ilg’s “El Dios del cielo y sus rebeldes hijos,” “Sobre Adán y Eva,” “Por qué Antü, el padre sol tiene grandes penas en el corazón,” and “Kuyen, la madre luna se venga de una maldición” place the pair in primordial history. In some, they are creators before the Treng Treng–Kay Kay flood; in others their identity overlaps the high creator.

Christian vocabulary such as “Adam and Eve,” “God,” and *Rey Chau* makes colonial-era rearticulation explicit. This does not make the stories inauthentic; it dates their recoverable form as a product of Mapuche–Christian encounter.

### E. Cosmic rebellion, preferred wife, and dimmed stars

Popular mythographies tell that Antü chose the brightest Wangülen, Küyen, as his preferred wife; jealous stars joined Peripillán’s rebellion; after victory Antü dimmed them, leaving Küyen brightest. The story often continues with fallen spirits, volcanoes, and the creation of humanity.

**[Modern systematized mythology]** The accessible versions descend largely from late compilations and Alberto Trivero’s twentieth-century synthetic reconstruction rather than a cited early Mapudungun text. Until a named speaker, collection date, and original-language version are supplied, its elaborate divine hierarchy should not be presented as early canonical religion.

## 6. Cult, ritual, and material practice

### No temple cult is documented

There is no secure archaeological or colonial evidence for a permanent temple dedicated exclusively to Küyen, a hereditary Küyen priesthood, a temple treasury, or an image cult comparable to Inca Mama Killa’s Cuzco institution. Mapuche ceremonial organization centered on communities, sacred landscapes, *rewe*, *nguillatun/kamarikun*, healing rites, and machi practice.

Absence of a temple is not absence of religion. It means “cult” must be reconstructed from address, timing, gesture, image, and ritual use rather than from monumental institutions.

### Machi practice

Küyen appears in the ritual universe of machi through:

- Lunar timing of healing or initiation in some communities.
- Kultrun cosmograms.
- Invocation of *Meli Küyen* among upper-world powers.
- Fertility and menstrual language.
- Modern Catholic associations with Mary.
- Personal visions and spirit relations that vary by machi.

The *machitun* is a healing complex, not a standardized “Moon ceremony.” Likewise, the *nguillatun* or eastern *kamarikun* may invoke celestial and ancestral powers, but is not properly reduced to worship of Küyen.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Animal sacrifice, food, drink, blood, smoke, and prayer occur in Mapuche ceremonies, but no examined source securely assigns a distinctive sacrificial species or offering exclusively to Küyen.

The documented killing of José Luis Painecur after the 1960 Valdivia earthquake belongs to a catastrophic maritime/flood ritual context. It is not evidence of a Küyen cult and should not be attached to her merely because the rite occurred within a Mapuche community.

No evidence was found for temple prostitution, ritual sexual service to Küyen, or cult violence committed in her name.

### Ordinary observance

The material record consists primarily of:

- Speech: lunar names, forecasts, phase terminology, and menstrual expressions.
- Practice: timing sowing, harvesting, cutting, animal treatment, fishing, and household work by lunar phase.
- Images: lunar disks or crescents on some kultrun membranes, flags, textiles, silverwork, and contemporary art.
- Oral testimony: myths and instructions transmitted within families and communities.

### Suppression and Christianization

Missionaries translated Mapudungun but simultaneously reclassified celestial powers as creations of the Christian God. Havestadt’s eighteenth-century hymn explicitly denies divinity to Antü and Cüyen. Catholic missions, settlement, schooling, land reduction, and the Chilean and Argentine military occupations disrupted transmission. Later evangelical churches have sometimes opposed machi practice more categorically; Bacigalupo records both conflict and adaptation.

Küyen was not eliminated. The name survived ordinary speech, women’s bodily vocabulary, agriculture, story, ritual imagery, and syncretic theology.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1606: unrecoverable directly

Astronomical observation and lunar timing must long predate writing, but no document securely identifies a pre-contact image, sanctuary, or canonical Küyen narrative. Archaeological orientations and circular motifs cannot independently supply the name.

### 1606–1777: lexical attestation and missionary reclassification

Valdivia fixes *cuyen* and phase terms in writing. Febrés and Havestadt continue the lexical record. Havestadt’s polemical hymn shows Christianity actively denying independent divinity to the Moon while adopting Mapudungun to teach doctrine.

### Nineteenth century: conquest and textual collection

After Chile’s Occupation of Araucanía and Argentina’s Conquest of the Desert, Mapuche communities were dispossessed, concentrated, and subjected to state institutions. Lenz’s 1890s texts emerge from this changed landscape. Küyen remains part of everyday language, environmental time, and oral narrative rather than a publicly monumental cult.

### 1900–1954: ethnographic consolidation

Augusta, Guevara, Latcham, Coña/Moesbach, Lehmann-Nitsche, Cavada, and Koessler-Ilg create the recoverable corpus. This period produces the first richly documented myths but also introduces editorial selection, translation, diffusionist theory, Catholic categories, and attempts to build a single “Araucanian mythology.”

The strongest Küyen narratives come from eastern, highly intercultural Patagonia. Their regionality is essential.

### 1954–1993: cosmological models

Faron, Grebe, and Foerster move from myth collecting toward social, ritual, and structural interpretation. Grebe’s *Meli Küyen* and seven-platform cosmos become extremely influential. They are supported by fieldwork but sometimes reproduced as if they were an ancient universal catechism.

### 1993–present: indigenous scholarship, gender, revival, and plurality

Bacigalupo, Montecino, Calfio, Mapuche writers, and community scholars foreground women’s knowledge, machi authority, colonial trauma, and living variation. Küyen becomes a symbol of female fertility, cultural continuity, territorial identity, and decolonizing knowledge. Contemporary teaching reconstructs lunar calendars and phase terminology, sometimes combining inherited practice with modern astronomical and pan-indigenous schemata.

There is continuing observance within Mapuche lifeways, but no reliable census of people who “worship Küyen.” The category assumes a bounded membership model that the evidence does not provide.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Astronomy

The European Southern Observatory named the VLT’s second 8.2-metre Unit Telescope **Kueyen**, “the Moon.” Its first light occurred in 1999. The other units were named Antu, Melipal, and Yepun. This is an official technological commemoration, not evidence of cult continuity.

### Literature and art

Küyen is widely used as a personal and artistic name. The Mapuche poet Rayen Küyen uses lunar imagery for women’s power, land, memory, exile, and resistance; her works include *Luna de Cenizas*, *Cometas Azules*, and *Luna de los primeros brotes*. Contemporary visual artists place Sun–Moon and kultrun imagery in explicitly political and territorial contexts.

Chile’s Subdirectorate of Indigenous Peoples has presented *Antü ka Küyen* as a children’s *piam*, with narration by Roberto Cayuqueo and collage-based table theatre. This is documented state-sponsored contemporary transmission, not a facsimile of a pre-contact performance.

### Popular mythology

Modern websites, schoolbooks, trading-card games, and myth encyclopedias commonly present Küyen as:

- “principal Wangülen”;
- sole queen of all stars;
- a universally female Moon goddess;
- Antü’s preferred wife;
- ruler of oceans and winds;
- mother of the first woman;
- a Maiden–Mother–Crone triple goddess.

Some elements may derive from collected oral material, but the complete package is a modern composite. The frequent equation of Küyen with Anchimallén is erroneous.

### Esotericism and neopaganism

The research located contemporary magical and spiritual websites invoking “Mother Moon” or adopting Küyen into generalized goddess spirituality. It did **not** locate an established premodern occult current, Renaissance hermetic identification, Golden Dawn correspondence, classical grimoire tradition, or demonstrably large organized neopagan Küyen cult.

Claims assigning her tarot cards, zodiac signs, crystals, universal “divine feminine” functions, or a European-style triple-goddess theology should be labeled **modern invention** unless their creator and publication date are supplied.

### Political use

Lunar names and imagery participate in Mapuche cultural resurgence and women’s activism. This differs from extremist appropriation: no documented far-right or violent organization centered on Küyen was found. Nor is every political use an invention; the language and lunar symbolism are old, while particular activist meanings are modern and datable.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Küyen originally a goddess?

The 1606 evidence proves only the word “Moon.” Later prayers, myths, and ritual diagrams prove personification and agency, but not one universal divine individual. The best conclusion is that Küyen occupies a continuum from celestial body and calendrical process to ancestor-like or divine person. “Goddess” is defensible as translation in specific contexts, not as an exhaustive class.

### Indigenous system or Christianized pantheon?

Early missionaries interpreted Mapuche categories through “God,” “devil,” “idol,” and “superstition.” Later ethnographers sometimes reversed the procedure by assembling all named powers into a European-style pantheon. Foerster and Bacigalupo show that Ngenechen, Christian God, Mary, Jesus, ancestors, and astral powers have been distinguished, merged, or strategically translated by different speakers.

### Inca or Puquina borrowing?

Guevara’s simple derivation from Quechua *killa* is insufficiently demonstrated. Broader Andean comparison and possible Puquina mediation remain hypotheses. Linguistic similarity must be evaluated separately from shared Sun–Moon marriage symbolism.

### Mapuche or Patagonian marriage myth?

Moulian’s comparative study is decisive for caution. The estranged-spouse story recorded by Koessler-Ilg occurs in a contact region and resembles Selk’nam/Tehuelche patterns. It may be an adopted and Mapuchized story. This does not make it “false”; it identifies transculturation as part of Küyen’s documented history.

### How universal is Grebe’s fourfold cosmos?

Grebe’s 1972–1973 publications preserve valuable field testimony. Yet later Mapuche researchers and ethnographers warn that the seven stacked platforms and four-person divine families are not uniformly recognized. They should be cited as Grebe’s fieldwork-based ethnomodel, not “the” timeless Mapuche cosmology.

### State of the evidence

Surviving evidence is dispersed among:

- Early printed grammars and dictionaries in European and Chilean libraries.
- The Biblioteca Nacional de Chile and Memoria Chilena.
- The Anales de la Universidad de Chile.
- Museo de La Plata publications and archives.
- Koessler-Ilg’s Argentine collecting legacy.
- Published indigenous testimony and modern community archives.
- Museum collections of kultrun, silverwork, textiles, and ritual objects—often poorly provenanced.

The principal loss is not a destroyed Mapuche scripture but the enormous body of oral knowledge never recorded, together with the contexts broken by war, epidemic disease, missionization, dispossession, schooling, and language shift.

## 11. Sources and conclusions

The documentary record supports a strong but carefully bounded reconstruction. Küyen is securely ancient as the Mapudungun Moon and measure of months. Her connection with phases, women’s cycles, plant growth, and seasonal time is linguistically and ethnographically substantial. She appears on some machi kultrun as part of a four-direction celestial microcosm. She can be Mother Moon, male and female elder, a fourfold lunar family, Antü’s wife, or one face of a creator complex.

What the evidence does **not** support is equally important: no ancient Küyen temple, fixed priesthood, canonical icon, universal genealogy, exclusive sacrificial cult, or single internally consistent mythology is documented. The elaborate “queen of the Wangülen” biography popular online is a late synthesis. The Sun–Moon marriage is genuinely recorded, but its fullest dysfunctional-marriage form belongs to twentieth-century Puelmapu collections and may reflect Mapuche–Tehuelche–Selk’nam narrative circulation. The variants, rather than a harmonized pantheon, constitute Küyen’s recoverable history.
