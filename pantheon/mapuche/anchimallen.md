# Anchimallen (Anchimayén): Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest evidence, a named supernatural apparition or spirit associated with alleged Indigenous sorcery—not securely a “god” or “goddess.” Later sources variously classify Anchimallén as a female cave-guardian, familiar spirit, pigmy duende, child-sized being, luminous omen, harmful *wekufü*-like entity, or the magical servant of a *kalku*. The twentieth-century classification “moon goddess/wife of the Sun” is a scholarly reconstruction, not the earliest attested form.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** **1693**, in the colonial prosecution of alleged Mapuche sorcerers associated with Boroa/Vilcún, Chile. A witness described beings called **Anchimalguén** that made whirlwinds in the air and appeared birdlike. This survives in a Spanish judicial record—not a pre-Conquest Mapudungun manuscript—and therefore documents what a witness said under colonial legal conditions, not an independently verified ritual or an undisturbed pre-Hispanic theology.
>
> **Evidence warning:** Mapudungun was transmitted primarily through speech, performance, and material practice. Absence from sixteenth- and seventeenth-century missionary chronicles is not proof that a belief did not exist; conversely, later assertions that every feature is pre-Conquest cannot be accepted without evidence.

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[Early attestation]** Colonial or earliest recoverable documentary evidence.
- **[Later ethnographic attestation]** Material recorded from Mapuche or regional informants in the nineteenth or twentieth century.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally recorded form, not automatically generalizable to all Mapuche communities.
- **[Learned systematization]** An interpretation imposed or organized by an antiquarian, folklorist, missionary, or scholar.
- **[Colonial/syncretistic formulation]** Material demonstrably filtered through Christian demonology, colonial courts, or mixed Indigenous–European folklore.
- **[Modern oral tradition]** Recent testimony presented as living belief rather than ancient documentary evidence.
- **[Modern retelling/invention]** A recent elaboration for literature, popular mythology, paranormalism, tourism, games, or the Internet.
- **[Unestablished]** A claim for which no adequate primary or scholarly documentation was located.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Anchimallén is best understood not as one stable “deity” but as a historically changing cluster of Mapuche and southern Chilean–Argentine supernatural representations. Its documented forms include:

1. an aerial, birdlike or whirlwind-producing apparition;
2. a very small female guardian of a sorcerers’ cave;
3. a beautiful young female protective spirit;
4. an alleged solar or lunar female power;
5. a pigmy, duende, or child-sized being;
6. a moving light, will-o’-the-wisp, spark, or ball of fire;
7. an omen of illness or death;
8. an auxiliary or manufactured servant of a *kalku*;
9. a protector of a person, property, or livestock;
10. a dangerous being encountered by children and travellers.

The variants should not be collapsed. The tiny cave-woman of 1749, Guevara’s reconstructed solar woman, Augusta’s mischievous luminous pigmy, and contemporary accounts of a ball of fire are related historically by name and reception, but they are not interchangeable descriptions from a single period.

### Documented spellings

Spanish-language and Mapudungun records employ unstable missionary and popular orthographies:

- **Anchimalguén / Anchimalgén** — colonial judicial form, 1693 and 1749.
- **Anchimallén / anchimallen** — common Chilean scholarly and popular spelling.
- **Añchimallen / añchimalleñ** — phonologically closer modern dictionary forms.
- **Anchimalén / Anchümalen / Anchümallen** — modern variants.
- **Anchimalhuén / Anchimalwén / Anchimaluén** — variants reflecting older spellings of the second component.
- **Anchimayén** — a widespread modern Spanish/English form; the change from *-ll-* to *-y-* reflects yeísmo rather than a securely distinct ancient name.
- **Auchimalguén / Auchimalhuén / Chimalguén / Chimalen** — secondary transcription or popular variants.

Félix José de Augusta’s 1916 *Diccionario araucano-español* defines **anchimallen** as “*trasgo, duende que aparece en figura de un pigmeo*”—“goblin, duende appearing in the form of a pigmy.” The modern Biblioteca Nacional dictionary expands the Pangui-area form **añchimallen** to a pigmy duende seen as a light on the open country and responsible for mischievous disorder in houses. These lexicographic descriptions securely establish the word and the pigmy/light complex by the early twentieth century.

### Etymology

#### Solar-woman derivation

**[Learned systematization]** Tomás Guevara proposed that the name derived from **anchü/antü**, “sun” or “to shine,” plus **malwen/mallen**, “woman, maiden,” yielding “woman of the Sun,” “solar maiden,” or “shining woman.” In *Folklore araucano* (1911, p. 231), he used that reading to conjecture a former lunar or astral being.

The first element can plausibly be associated with the Mapudungun family represented by **antü**, “sun/day,” and **anchülen**, “to shine, give light.” **Malen** does mean a girl or young woman in Mapudungun usage. A folk or historical reinterpretation as “shining maiden” is therefore intelligible.

#### Problems with the derivation

**[Unestablished]** No colonial Mapudungun text has been identified that analyzes *Anchimalguén* into those two components. Augusta treated **anchimallen** as a lexical entry but did not derive it from *antü* plus *mallen*. The modern Biblioteca Nacional dictionary labels it a compound but preserves no certain internal analysis.

Sound correspondences also make a direct *antü + malen* derivation less automatic than popular summaries imply. Guevara’s etymology supplied the foundation for his lunar-wife hypothesis; consequently, using that hypothesis as independent proof of the etymology would be circular.

The responsible conclusion is:

- association with light is strongly supported by later descriptions;
- association with a small female form appears already in 1749;
- the exact historical morphology is unresolved;
- “wife of the Sun” is not a literal translation established by the earliest record.

### Classification changes

- **1693 [Early attestation]:** aerial “fiction” or apparition, producing whirlwinds and apparently birdlike.
- **1749 [Early attestation]:** *una mujer muy pequeñita*, a very small woman guarding a cave.
- **1908–1911 [Learned systematization]:** Guevara reconstructs a formerly benign female familiar and possible lunar/solar figure.
- **1916 [Later lexicographic attestation]:** Augusta calls it a pigmy duende.
- **Mid-twentieth century onward [Folklore/regional variants]:** child-being, moving light, fireball, dangerous omen, livestock guardian, and *kalku*’s servant.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries [Modern systematization]:** frequently placed in a standardized “Mapuche pantheon,” or classified as a fire elemental, demon, cryptid, or moon goddess. Those categories generally come from comparative mythology, fantasy taxonomy, or Western esotericism, not from the earliest sources.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The 1693 proceeding

**[Early attestation]** In proceedings against fourteen Indigenous people from the Boroa/Vilcún region, one deponent reported seeing in a cave, besides *imbunches*, “*otras ficciones que llamaban Anchimalguén que por el aire hacían remolinos y que parecía ave*”: other apparitions called Anchimalguén which made whirlwinds through the air and appeared to be a bird.

This evidence establishes by 1693:

- the name *Anchimalguén*;
- a plural or class-like usage;
- association with an alleged gathering place of sorcerers;
- aerial motion, whirlwind action, and birdlike appearance.

It does **not** establish:

- a goddess;
- relation to the Moon or Sun;
- a ball of fire;
- manufacture from a child’s corpse;
- feeding with blood, milk, or honey;
- participation in *nguillatun*;
- a temple, priesthood, or communal cult.

The deposition emerged from coercive colonial proceedings in which Mapuche concepts were translated into Spanish judicial and Christian-demonological categories. It remains a primary attestation, but its vocabulary—especially “fictions,” caves, pacts, and demons—cannot be treated as transparent transcription of Mapuche theology.

### The 1749 Chillán proceeding

**[Early attestation; colonial/syncretistic formulation]** During the prosecution of alleged sorcerers at San Bartolomé de Chillán, Agustina Aillanca’s notarized declaration of 27 October 1749 described another cave at a stony hill named Guechuqueu. It contained a *chivato pillán*, a great serpent, and “*una mujer muy pequeñita que cuidaba la cueva a quien llamaban Anchimalguén*”—a very small woman who guarded the cave and was called Anchimalguén.

Another modern Archivo Nacional synopsis calls the guardian an old Indigenous woman who kept a *chuico* of chicha full. The discrepancy may reflect different depositions or later compression; it should not be harmonized into a single portrait.

The case ran to 169 folios. The court’s investigators did not find the caves or supernatural occupants, and the accused were ultimately released. Thus the record demonstrates a narrated belief under prosecution, not material proof of the being.

### Absence from the requested early chronicles

No verified passage naming Anchimallén was located in:

- Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (1569–1589);
- Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán’s *Cautiverio feliz* (written in the seventeenth century; first printed in the nineteenth);
- Diego de Rosales’s *Historia general del Reino de Chile* (seventeenth-century manuscript);
- Andrés Febrés’s eighteenth-century Mapudungun dictionary;
- Bernardo Havestadt’s *Chilidúgú* (1777).

**[Finding from absence]** These works are important for the history of European representations of Mapuche religion, but they cannot presently be cited as direct Anchimallén attestations. Guevara himself conceded that the chroniclers gave no information about worship of his reconstructed benign/astral Anchimallén.

### Rodolfo Lenz

Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos* (1895–1897) is foundational as the first major printed collection of Mapudungun oral texts across several dialect areas, with close Spanish translation and named contributors. It documents the linguistic and narrative environment in which later Anchimallén material was collected.

**[Unestablished]** Searchable evidence did not verify a discrete Anchimallén narrative in the published *Estudios araucanos*. Modern websites often claim that Lenz documented the being or its entry into rural Chilean speech, but without volume and page these claims remain inadequate. Lenz should not be cited as a direct source for a particular Anchimallén episode until that passage is produced.

### Tomás Guevara

In *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* (1908), Guevara discussed contemporary magical belief, including harmful or protective familiars. A passage later cited by folklorists says that such a being could be bought from sorcerers to protect its owner and animals and had to be fed with the blood of the owner’s relatives.

In *Folklore araucano* (1911, p. 231), he wrote that the word derived from *anchü/anti* “Sun” plus *malwen/mallen* “woman.” He proposed that it referred to the Moon, although the ordinary Mapudungun term was *küyen*. He described an alleged older form as a beautifully dressed young woman and protective familiar, while contemporary Mapuche feared it as a will-o’-the-wisp or faint light moving among trees, entering houses through cracks, or passing between a traveller’s horse’s legs.

Guevara’s work preserves valuable material but is methodologically complicated. His publications depended on Mapuche collaborators such as Manuel Manquilef, Lorenzo Koliman, Felipe Reyes Millán, and Ramón Lienan, whose roles were not adequately acknowledged. His evolutionary framework also treated Mapuche society through assimilationist and racial hierarchies. His statements about an “ancient” form are therefore hypotheses, not direct colonial evidence.

### Augusta’s dictionaries and texts

Félix José de Augusta’s 1916 dictionary supplies the clearest early lexicographic definition:

> *anchimallen, s., trasgo, duende que aparece en figura de un pigmeo.*

This supports the pigmy-duende form but not Guevara’s moon-goddess reconstruction. Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* is essential contextual evidence for contemporary Mapudungun speech and narrative, but no securely located Anchimallén narrative from that work was found in this investigation.

### Pascual Coña/Moesbach

Pascual Coña’s Mapudungun life-history, edited and translated by Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach and published in 1930 as *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX*, is indispensable primary testimony for Mapuche social and religious life.

**[Finding from absence]** No specific Anchimallén passage was verified in the searchable material consulted. It should therefore be used as cultural context, not silently cited as a source for the fireball or child-corpse legends.

### Koessler-Ilg and Argentine Patagonia

Bertha Koessler-Ilg collected Mapuche oral narratives around San Martín de los Andes, Neuquén. Her publications include *Cuentan los araucanos* (1954), *Indianermärchen aus den Kordilleren* (1956), and *Tradiciones araucanas* (1962). Her collection circumstances—asking Mapuche visitors and patients connected with her physician husband’s household to recount narratives—make the works mediated records rather than untouched transcripts.

**[Regional variant]** Neuquén testimony describes Anchimallén in terms close to the *chonchon*: something flying as a “*pelotón de fuego*,” a great moving lump or ball of fire. Other Neuquén syntheses call it a harmful dwarf associated with livestock guardianship. This Argentine material firmly supports east-of-the-Andes circulation of the name and the light/fire form, but not every online detail can be traced to a specific Koessler-Ilg page.

### Later scholarship and educational collections

- Louis C. Faron’s works, including *The Mapuche Indians of Chile* (1968) and *Hawks of the Sun*, situate malicious powers and sorcery within Mapuche kinship, morality, and ritual rather than an Olympian-style pantheon.
- Else María Waag’s *Tres entidades Wekufu en la cultura mapuche* (1982) compares Anchimallén with *witr’analwe* and other dangerous beings and is central to the modern analytical category.
- Rolf Foerster’s *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche* (1993) traces missionary and anthropological constructions of Mapuche religion.
- Modern bilingual and community educational collections preserve living reports: a blinking light at night; at times a humanlike head; a presence that plays with children at dusk; a light attached to trees or rocks; and stories pairing Anchimallén with Mankián.
- A community medicinal collection records **trafentui anchimallen**, “encountering the Anchimallén,” as a condition affecting children who go out around midday. Symptoms include nocturnal fright, crying, screaming, and vomiting; heated *huevil* and tobacco leaves are used as a *contra* or countermeasure.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

#### Cave guardian

**[Early attestation, 1749]** Anchimalguén guards a cave associated in testimony with alleged sorcerers, a serpent, and a *chivato pillán*. Guarding rather than attacking is its explicit task.

#### Familiar and property guardian

**[Later ethnographic attestation]** Guevara describes a spirit possessed or purchased for protection. Later Chilean and Argentine reports make it guardian of livestock, land, or a household. A fire-sphere may patrol fields and confront thieves.

#### Omen

**[Folklore]** A sudden light near a house, tree, road, mountain, or field can presage illness, misfortune, or death. Later syntheses say a red Anchimallén foretells the death of an important person. That color-specific rule has not been located in the colonial record.

#### Agent of a *kalku*

**[Later folklore/learned systematization]** The most widespread modern account makes the Anchimallén a servant sent by a *kalku* to spy, guard, frighten, introduce illness, or kill. Some sources distinguish it from an autonomous *wekufü*; others place it within the *wekufü* category. No single classification is universal.

#### Danger to children and travellers

**[Regional folklore]** It may appear at midday to play with children, frighten them, or cause subsequent sickness. At night it follows travellers, slides among trees, enters through cracks, or moves beneath a horse, frightening or unhorsing the rider.

### Physical forms

- birdlike aerial apparition in a whirlwind (1693);
- very small woman (1749);
- beautifully dressed young woman (Guevara’s reconstructed “older” form);
- sex-indeterminate dwarf or pigmy;
- child or infant-sized companion;
- grotesque, thin, red-faced, or underdeveloped figure;
- luminous human head;
- finger-sized ember or light resembling a glowing cigarette;
- will-o’-the-wisp;
- faint light;
- spark, meteor-like flash, or fireball;
- a ball or mass of fire flying close to the ground.

These may represent transformations attributed to one being, different local beings joined by a shared name, or successive reinterpretations. The evidence does not decide among those possibilities.

### Sounds

**[Folklore]** Some accounts give it a cry like a newborn infant or a mournful child. The sound belongs chiefly to the child-corpse legend and is absent from the two colonial descriptions.

### Powers and limitations

Later narratives attribute:

- rapid flight;
- transformation between child and fireball;
- invisibility or selective visibility;
- entry through narrow cracks;
- production of sparks or flame;
- guarding property;
- causing fright, breathing difficulty, illness, or death;
- continuing injury until expelled by a healer;
- magical linkage to its owner, so that injury to the familiar rebounds on the *kalku*.

It may allegedly be lured with milk, honey, or blood. Some stories say a neglected or greedy Anchimallén can be stolen. The proverb “*por goloso pierde la vida el Anchimallén*”—“through gluttony the Anchimallén loses its life”—belongs to this late folklore complex.

### Countermeasures

Recorded or popularly reported measures include:

- treatment by a *machi*;
- a *machitun* or related healing performance;
- community members lending spiritual strength;
- heated *huevil* and tobacco leaves as a *contra* for an affected child;
- loud metallic noises, particularly spurs;
- avoidance of solitary travel or play at dangerous hours.

There is no evidence of one pan-Mapuche canonical expulsion rite.

### Iconography

No pre-Hispanic image, colonial cult statue, temple relief, or securely identified archaeological representation of Anchimallén is known.

**[Modern art]** A modern Chilean artwork catalogued as **Anchimalgüen** depicts a stylized black anthropomorphic figure against a grey-to-black background. It is reception history, not evidence of ancient iconography.

Modern illustration commonly depicts a glowing child, red dwarf, spectral girl, or flaming orb. These images consolidate divergent oral variants into a recognizable fantasy creature.

### Unattested correspondences

No securely traditional color system, sacred number, weekday, plant, weapon, planetary correspondence, festival date, or fixed emblem was found. Assigning “fire,” a magical direction, tarot correspondence, or elemental rank is modern esoteric taxonomy unless independently documented.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No early source gives Anchimallén parents, siblings, consort, offspring, birth genealogy, or fixed place in a ranked pantheon.

The principal “origin” accounts are instead technological or transformational:

- **[Folklore]** a *kalku* manufactures it from a bone stolen from a child’s grave;
- **[Folklore]** sorcerers steal or kill an infant, place or feed it beneath an inverted pot, and nourish it with blood;
- **[Regional folklore]** it is the soul of a sacrificed child appointed to guard land or livestock;
- **[Learned systematization]** it was originally a female astral spirit or the Moon/wife of the Sun;
- **[Modern popular synthesis]** it is a species of servant-spirit created when a *kalku* lacks a sufficiently powerful *wekufü*.

None of these should be presented as the single canonical origin.

### Documented interactions with named or classified supernatural beings

Because the record often uses classes rather than personal names, both are retained and explicitly identified.

- **Kalku — owner, maker, purchaser, or commander.** Later ethnography and folklore describe Anchimallén as a familiar used by a *kalku* for guarding, spying, or harm. The *kalku* is a human ritual specialist/sorcerer, not a god. **Source tier:** later ethnographic attestation and folklore, especially Guevara (1908–1911) and twentieth-century syntheses.

- **Imbunche — co-presence in a cave.** The 1693 testimony places Anchimalguén apparitions in a cave “besides the imbunches.” No combat, kinship, or command relationship is stated. **Source tier:** early attestation, 1693.

- **Chivato Pillán — joint cave scene.** The 1749 Chillán testimony places a small Anchimalguén guardian in a cave also containing a *chivato pillán*. No genealogy or alliance beyond co-presence is explicit. **Source tier:** early attestation filtered through colonial demonological questioning.

- **The cave serpent/culebrón — joint cave scene.** The same proceeding describes a great serpent that crawled over initiates and another serpent associated with the second cave. Anchimalguén guards the latter place. **Source tier:** early attestation, 1749; colonial/syncretistic formulation.

- **Antü — alleged astral relation.** Guevara’s etymological reconstruction makes Anchimalhuén “woman of the Sun” and therefore connects her with Antü. No early narrative of a meeting, marriage ceremony, dialogue, or joint action was found. **Source tier:** learned systematization, 1911.

- **Küyen — proposed identity or displacement.** Guevara speculated that Anchimalhuén represented the Moon even though *küyen* was the ordinary Mapudungun name for the Moon. Later popular works alternately identify, separate, or confuse the two. No early text narrates them interacting as separate beings. **Source tier:** learned systematization and modern popular conflation.

- **Wekufü — categorical association, not a discrete meeting.** Later scholarship frequently treats Anchimallén as one type of harmful power or *wekufü*, or as a *kalku*’s auxiliary comparable to one. Other traditions make it an independent guardian. **Source tier:** later ethnographic analysis; classification disputed.

- **Chonchon — functional comparison.** Neuquén testimony says Anchimallén has the same purpose as the *chonchon*, and both may appear as flying nocturnal manifestations associated with sorcery. No narrative of the two meeting was verified. **Source tier:** Argentine regional variant.

- **Cherufe — visual confusion.** Modern informants and compilers say the ember-like or meteor-like Anchimallén may be mistaken for *Cherufe*. This is comparison, not an interaction. **Source tier:** modern folklore synthesis.

- **Laftrache — visual confusion.** Both may be described as small humanoids, but no shared episode or kinship is attested. **Source tier:** modern comparative folklore.

- **Mankián — joint appearance in a modern community narrative.** A recent educational collection reports a story told by Norma Huentén in which Anchimallén and Mankián appear in an admonitory narrative about women travelling alone at certain hours. **Source tier:** modern oral tradition; the full episode and collection date should be consulted before inferring a wider theological relationship.

**Finding from absence:** There is no securely documented divine council, combat cycle, marriage myth, creation scene, or genealogical episode involving Anchimallén and another named deity.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Anchimallén has no surviving epic cycle comparable to a written creation epic. Its “myths” are short encounter traditions, origin explanations, judicial narratives, and memorates.

### The cave apparitions

**Earliest form: 1693.** Anchimalguén beings move through the air as whirlwinds and appear birdlike in an alleged sorcerers’ cave.

**1749 transformation:** Anchimalguén becomes singular and anthropomorphic—a tiny woman guarding a cave occupied by other extraordinary beings. In the colonial record, the scene is translated into the language of demonic pacts and witches’ assemblies.

The two proceedings show continuity of name and cave/sorcery association but substantial formal variation. The evidence does not permit reconstructing which form is older in oral tradition.

### The benign solar maiden

**[Learned systematization]** Guevara describes an earlier Anchimalhuén as a young, beautiful, finely dressed, benign female familiar. On etymological grounds he proposes that she represented the Moon as “woman of the Sun.”

The story’s weakness is documentary: Guevara supplies no early hymn, prayer, cult image, or narrative in which Antü marries Anchimalhuén. His language—“it would not be rash to maintain”—signals conjecture. Later encyclopedias converted this proposal into a declarative story that Anchimallén “was originally the moon goddess,” thereby erasing its hypothetical status.

### Degeneration into the feared light

Guevara’s historical scheme presents a transition from benign feminine familiar to frightening rural light:

- it glides among trees;
- enters houses through cracks;
- moves among a traveller’s horse’s legs;
- its presence foretells death.

This “degeneration” may preserve actual historical change, but it may also reflect an evolutionist model in which a supposedly elevated astral deity decays into superstition. Without intermediate texts it remains a reconstruction.

### The manufactured child-familiar

**[Folklore; possibly colonial/syncretistic]** In its fullest modern form:

1. a sorcerer steals or obtains a child or a child’s remains;
2. the child is killed, buried, or taken after burial;
3. a bone or the remains are ritually manipulated beneath an inverted pot;
4. the new familiar is fed human blood, sometimes milk or honey;
5. it manifests as a dwarf, child, ember, or ball of fire;
6. it guards the owner or livestock and may be sent to injure enemies;
7. neglecting it creates danger for owner and familiar.

The unbaptized-child motif, inverted pot, blood feeding, and corpse manufacture have close structural affinities with colonial and Chilote witchcraft narratives. They cannot be safely projected into pre-Conquest religion. Nor do such stories prove that children were actually killed.

### Balls of fire in conflict

**[Later folklore]** Where several Anchimallén exist, they may fight as two low-flying fireballs that collide and cast sparks. No colonial or early ethnographic source for this particular episode was verified. It should be cited as late popular tradition.

### The traveller’s light

**[Regional folklore]** A mobile light follows travellers, passes beneath horses, or appears on roads and in woodland. In some accounts it merely alarms; in others it causes a fall, sickness, or death. Argentine versions emphasize a flying fire-mass; Chilean versions often emphasize a faint, darting, will-o’-the-wisp-like light.

### The child at midday

**[Modern oral tradition]** Anchimallén emerges at the hottest part of the day and plays with children who should not be outdoors. An encounter is diagnosed retrospectively through fear, nocturnal crying, vomiting, and disturbed sleep. Treatment uses plant-based countermeasures. This version functions both as illness explanation and as a warning controlling children’s movements.

### Guardian of livestock and land

**[Regional variant]** A person may put livestock, land, or a household under Anchimallén’s protection. The light patrols the property and opposes thieves. The guardian can therefore be beneficial to its possessor while remaining dangerous to outsiders. This moral ambiguity is more faithful to the documented variants than calling it simply “evil.”

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No documented institutional cult

No evidence was found for:

- a temple dedicated to Anchimallén;
- a hereditary Anchimallén priesthood;
- communal hymns;
- an annual festival;
- public sacrifice;
- votive inscriptions;
- pilgrimage;
- a securely identified idol or archaeological cult image;
- theophoric personal names.

The word “cult” is therefore inappropriate if it implies an organized religious institution.

### Possession and feeding traditions

**[Folklore]** Owners allegedly fed the familiar milk, honey, or blood. Guevara’s harshest form says it was fed the blood of the owner’s relatives. These are reports about feared secret practice, not archaeological proof.

### Human sacrifice claim

Stories of a stolen, killed, or sacrificed infant are widespread in later descriptions. The evidentiary status is crucial:

- the stories are genuine folklore and must not be omitted;
- no documented sacrificial killing performed to create an Anchimallén was found;
- no excavated ritual context has been securely identified with this being;
- no judicial conviction based on a recovered child victim was found;
- colonial witchcraft accusations often transformed Indigenous ritual categories into demonological crime.

Thus “human sacrifice” is **attested as narrative accusation and folklore**, not as an established cult practice.

### Healing

A *machi* may diagnose or expel harmful influence attributed to an Anchimallén. A modern community remedy for affected children uses *huevil*, tobacco, heat, and sometimes strong urine in a bodily rub. This is a directly recorded therapeutic practice, although it does not demonstrate a universal Mapuche rite.

### Nguillatun and machitun

Anchimallén does not have a documented central role in the *nguillatun*. Claims that it belongs intrinsically to that communal rite are unsupported.

Its association with *machitun* is remedial: a healer may address illness attributed to the entity. It is not evidence that Anchimallén is invoked or worshipped during every healing ceremony.

### Witchcraft prosecutions and political economy

The clearest colonial “ritual” record comes from hostile judicial investigation. In 1693 officials prosecuted Indigenous leaders, including caciques and a *machi*, for alleged secret assemblies and magical attacks on chiefs aligned with Spanish power. In 1749 more than eighteen people were drawn into the Chillán case. Accusations of sorcery thus intersected with frontier politics, Indigenous rivalries, colonial discipline, property, gender, and translation.

The Chillán court did not find the supernatural caves described in testimony. This failure is essential evidence against reading the dossier as proof of an operative secret cult.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1693: unrecorded background

No securely datable pre-Hispanic depiction, inscription, or text names Anchimallén. A pre-Conquest oral antecedent is possible but cannot be reconstructed securely. Assertions of a fully developed ancient “moon-goddess cult” exceed the evidence.

### 1693–1749: colonial judicial figure

The first documents associate the name with criminalized Indigenous sorcery: first as plural aerial apparitions, later as a tiny female cave-guardian. Spanish scribes, interpreters, prosecutors, and Christian demonology mediate both records.

### Nineteenth century: oral continuity amid conquest and missionization

The state occupation of Mapuche territory, reduction into reservations, Catholic and later Protestant missionization, and settlement transformed the settings in which religious knowledge circulated. No dated nineteenth-century Anchimallén text was verified here, but the term’s appearance in early twentieth-century fieldwork demonstrates continuity into communities that had undergone those disruptions.

### 1895–1916: ethnographic and lexicographic codification

Lenz established methods for publishing Mapudungun oral texts. Guevara integrated Anchimallén into an evolutionary account of Mapuche “magic,” proposing a shift from solar/lunar female protector to feared will-o’-the-wisp. Augusta recorded the living lexical sense “pigmy duende.”

This period created the principal printed forms on which later dictionaries and popular mythologies depend.

### 1914–mid-century: incorporation into Chilean and Chilote folklore

Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, and later collectors catalogued regional folklore in a nation-building context. Chilote witchcraft traditions—already a mixture of Huilliche, Spanish Catholic, maritime, and rural materials—favored the elaboration of corpse-made familiars, cave guardians, blood feeding, secret brotherhoods, and magical property protection.

Anchimallén is not among the best-attested distinctively Chilote figures in Cavada’s searchable material; its modern labeling as specifically “Chilote” should therefore be used cautiously. Its inclusion in twentieth-century Chiloé syntheses may reflect south-Chilean circulation and cross-fertilization with the *Recta Provincia* witchcraft complex.

### 1950s–1980s: Argentine corpus and comparative systematization

Koessler-Ilg’s publications made Neuquén oral tradition available internationally. Argentine material emphasizes flying fire and livestock protection. Later scholars, particularly Else Waag, compared Anchimallén with other *wekufü*-associated entities.

Folklorists increasingly standardized spelling variants and combined Chilean and Argentine material into one catalogue entry. This preserved information but often obscured locality and chronology.

### 1990s–present: living tradition and cultural heritage

Contemporary Mapuche and intercultural educational publications continue to record Anchimallén as a living subject of oral narrative: a light that blinks, a headlike form, a being attached to trees, a danger to children, and a source of death-related stories. These accounts show continuity of belief and storytelling, though not necessarily unchanged survival from pre-Conquest times.

The figure is now also circulated through school materials, tourism, music, fantasy bestiaries, paranormal websites, and Indigenous heritage projects.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Heritage and education

Modern bilingual and community publications reframe Anchimallén as intangible Mapuche heritage rather than merely “superstition.” They preserve named narrators and contemporary versions, an important corrective to earlier works that obscured Indigenous contributors.

A 2025 Chilean national children’s-story competition awarded a special Indigenous Peoples prize to **“Don Agustín y el Anchimallén.”** This demonstrates continued literary use rather than ritual cult.

### Literature and music

Anchimallén appears in contemporary poems, songs, children’s narratives, educational guides, and regional horror. The musical title **“Anchimayen”** by Futhan is a modern cultural use; without further documentation it should not be treated as a ritual hymn.

### Visual art

The catalogued artwork **Anchimalgüen** turns the being into a modern anthropomorphic image. Contemporary illustrators typically choose one of four motifs—glowing child, spectral maiden, dwarf, or fireball—thereby producing a fixed iconography absent from early evidence.

### Paranormal and UFO reinterpretation

**[Modern invention]** Websites have compared Anchimallén to ball lightning, atmospheric ghost lights, “energy beings,” or UFOs. Natural luminous phenomena may have influenced individual reports, but no general physical explanation can be established from the texts. Conversely, calling the being an extraterrestrial imports a twentieth-century category unsupported by the colonial and ethnographic record.

### Fantasy taxonomies

Modern encyclopedias call Anchimallén:

- a “fire elemental”;
- a cryptid;
- a zombie-like being created from a corpse;
- a demon;
- a moon goddess;
- a species of flying fire creature.

All are selective recodings. “Fire elemental,” especially, derives from Western occult element theory rather than documented Mapuche classification.

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search found no documented Anchimallén-centered organization, initiatory order, temple, stable neopagan denomination, or traceable ritual lineage. Nor was it found in the major historical currents of Renaissance Hermetism, Theosophy, Golden Dawn ritual, or early chaos magic.

Individual online invocations or “spirit guides” may exist, but no evidence supports a continuous esoteric Anchimallén cult. Presenting reconstructed offerings or elemental correspondences as ancient would be modern invention.

### Political appropriation

Anchimallén appears principally in heritage, education, regional identity, and popular horror, not as a documented emblem of a major nationalist or extremist movement. Broader disputes over Mapuche identity and territory should not be projected onto this particular being without evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Anchimallén originally a moon goddess?

The theory rests chiefly on Guevara:

1. he divides the name into a light/Sun element and “woman”;
2. he identifies the resulting “woman of the Sun” with the Moon;
3. he treats the benign young woman as older than the frightening light.

Against it:

- the 1693 being is aerial and birdlike, not lunar;
- the 1749 being is a tiny cave guardian;
- the standard Mapudungun word for Moon is *küyen*;
- no early marriage narrative with Antü is known;
- no lunar cult image or prayer names Anchimalguén;
- Augusta records a pigmy duende without a lunar meaning.

**Conclusion:** the lunar identity is a significant historiographical theory, not an established early tradition.

### One being or several merged beings?

Else Waag and later compilers note the semantic instability of the name. The record may preserve:

- one highly transformable familiar;
- several local spirit categories merged by Spanish transcription;
- an old female guardian reinterpreted through duende and will-o’-the-wisp traditions;
- a learned conflation of an astral female and a sorcerer’s servant.

The evidence supports historical layering but does not uniquely identify the mechanism.

### Beneficent or malevolent?

Both are attested:

- guardian of cave, animals, owner, land, or household;
- omen and bringer of fear, sickness, or death;
- mischievous household duende;
- harmful emissary of a *kalku*.

“Ambivalent relative to ownership and social relation” describes the corpus better than “evil demon.”

### Indigenous category or Christian demonization?

The name and some underlying conception are Indigenous; the earliest written records, however, emerge only inside colonial prosecutions. Christian language of demons, pacts, witches’ caves, unbaptized children, and diabolical worship may have reshaped testimony and later folklore.

It would be equally mistaken to declare the entire figure a Spanish invention or to strip the colonial documents of their coercive and syncretistic setting.

### Human sacrifice

Popular accounts often state sacrifice as fact. Scholarship and archives support only:

- stories and accusations involving child death, stolen remains, and blood;
- actual colonial prosecution of alleged sorcery;
- no verified Anchimallén sacrificial victim or archaeological cult installation.

### State of the evidence

Important surviving materials are held or reproduced through:

- Chile’s Archivo Nacional and Fondo Real Audiencia;
- Biblioteca Nacional de Chile/Memoria Chilena;
- Museo de La Araucanía and regional heritage collections;
- Universidad de Chile repositories;
- Argentine university and provincial collections preserving Koessler-Ilg’s reception;
- published dictionaries and ethnographies.

The evidence is disproportionately filtered through colonial notaries, missionaries, non-Mapuche ethnographers, translators, and folklorists. Modern community publications partly redress this imbalance.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Anchimallén was certainly the ancient Mapuche moon goddess.”
- “Its pre-Conquest human-sacrifice cult is archaeologically proven.”
- “Ercilla or Rosales described the fireball in detail.”
- “Every Mapuche community classifies it identically.”
- “It is simply another name for Küyen.”
- “It is objectively a UFO, ball lightning, elemental, or cryptid.”
- “Its feeding ritual is part of the *nguillatun*.”
- “The modern Internet list of spellings proves multiple ancient regional deities.”

## 11. Sources

### Primary and near-primary evidence

The central primary sources are the 1693 and 1749 colonial proceedings, surviving through Chilean archival records and Holdenis Casanova Guarda’s transcription and analysis. Augusta’s dictionary provides direct lexical evidence. Guevara records early twentieth-century field material but also supplies his own reconstruction. Modern community publications are primary evidence for contemporary oral tradition, not automatically for antiquity.

### Critical source assessment

The requested chroniclers and ethnographers are not equally informative about Anchimallén. Some are essential to Mapuche religious history but apparently silent on this name. Direct citation must remain tied to passages actually found. Modern anonymous mythology sites were useful only for identifying claims requiring verification; unsupported assertions from them have not been promoted to historical fact.
