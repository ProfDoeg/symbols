# Kai-Kai Vilu and Tren-Tren Vilu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Paired primordial serpent-beings or landscape spirits in Mapuche, Huilliche, Lafkenche, and related Chiloé traditions. The earliest source calls both *culebras* (“serpents”), treats Tren-Tren as a protective, quasi-divine power inhabiting a sacred mountain, and calls Kai-Kai the hostile “lord of the sea.” Their classification as a neatly opposed “sea god” and “earth goddess,” or as children of named *pillanes*, belongs to later systematization and popular retelling rather than the earliest record.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del reino de Chile, Flandes indiano*, book I, chapter 1, manuscript completed in 1674 and first printed in 1877–1878. Rosales records *Tenten* and *Caicai-Vilu*, multiple sacred “Tenten” mountains, the rising sea and rising mountain, transformations into fish and stone, varying survivor counts, and a child sacrifice that ends the inundation. No earlier presently identified text securely names both beings. Assertions that Rosales documented the myth “in 1606” confuse his approximate lifetime or an unsupported date with the completion of his manuscript. [Memoria Chilena’s manuscript and publication history](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-3356.html); [Rosales, book I, chapter 1](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Historia_general_de_el_Reyno_de_Chile/Libro_I/I).

## Evidence labels used

- **[Early attested]** Rosales’s 1674 manuscript or another comparably early documentary witness.
- **[Later ethnographic]** Material collected from Mapuche, Huilliche, or Chilote narrators in the nineteenth or twentieth century.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally documented form, not presumed to represent every Mapuche-speaking community.
- **[Learned systematization]** A reconstruction or synthesis made by an ethnologist, theologian, folklorist, educator, or modern compiler.
- **[Syncretistic]** Material demonstrably or plausibly reshaped through sustained contact with Christianity or another religious system.
- **[Folklore]** Oral tradition recorded relatively late, with no claim that every element is precolonial.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An academic interpretation rather than a statement directly made by the primary source.
- **[Modern invention/retelling]** A recent addition presented in literature, education, tourism, popular mythology, occultism, or entertainment without early documentation.
- **[Unestablished]** A circulating claim for which no adequate primary evidence was located.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identification

**[Early attested]** Rosales does not give a single abstract “pantheon” in which two universal gods permanently govern earth and sea. His report is topographically plural. Every province, he says, possessed a revered high place called a *Tenten*, believed to have saved local ancestors from an ancient inundation. A serpent of the same name inhabited its summit, warned people, protected humans and animals, and made the mountain rise. *Caicai-Vilu* was its enemy, associated either with low ground or, in a variant Rosales records, with those same mountains. It raised the sea to destroy people, Tenten, and the refuge itself.

The pair are therefore most securely described as antagonistic serpent-powers embedded in a network of sacred mountains, waters, remembered catastrophes, kin origins, and ritual obligation. “God,” “goddess,” “monster,” “demon,” and “nature spirit” each capture only some historical interpretations.

Rosales’s Christian framing must not be mistaken for Indigenous classification. He repeatedly calls the beings demonic deceptions, but simultaneously reports that Tenten was regarded as “como cosa divina” (“as something divine”) and that its mountain was sacred and “of great religion.” His demonization is evidence for Jesuit interpretation, not proof that Mapuche narrators classified Tren-Tren as a devil.

### Names and spellings

**[Early attested]**

- **Tenten** — Rosales’s spelling, applied both to the serpent and the sacred mountain.
- **Caicai-Vilu** — Rosales’s spelling of the hostile serpent.
- **Caicai** and **Tenten** — the cries Rosales says made the water and mountain rise: *Cai, cai* and *Ten, ten*. His text explicitly presents these calls as explanations of the names.

**[Later ethnographic/regional]**

- **Tren-Tren, Trentren, Treng-Treng, Trengtreng, Tenten, Ten-Ten**
- **Kai-Kai, Kaikai, Kay-Kay, Cai-Cai, Caicai, Kaikai Filu**
- Modern Mapudungun spellings include **Trengtreng Filu** and **Kaykay Filu**. Orthographic variation reflects both dialectal realization and competing Mapudungun alphabets; it is not evidence for distinct beings.
- In some modern writing, particularly with the Unified Alphabet or Azümchefe-derived conventions, *filu* replaces Spanish-influenced *vilu*.

### Etymology

**[Secure lexical component]** Mapudungun *filu* means “snake” or “serpent.” Spanish colonial spelling commonly represents it as *vilu*.

**[Early source’s folk etymology]** Rosales derives *Caicai* and *Tenten* from the powers’ repeated cries. This is a seventeenth-century recorded explanation, but it is not independently established historical linguistics.

**[Disputed/modern reconstruction]** Modern summaries often gloss *Tren-Tren* as “earth” and *Kai-Kai* as “water.” These are useful descriptions of their functions but should not be treated as secure morpheme-by-morpheme etymologies. A modern linguistic study cautiously defines Kai-Kai as a water force and Tren-Tren as the power-cry of the hill, again reflecting traditional semantics rather than proving a derivation.

**[Later regional etymology]** Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s Argentine material glosses the *Tren-Tren* as “mountains of fire.” This belongs to her specific, highly elaborated narrative and is not the meaning supplied by Rosales. It may connect the refuge hills with volcanism.

### Gender and number

**[Early attested]** Rosales’s Spanish grammar makes both serpents grammatically feminine because *culebra* is feminine; that does not securely establish personified female gender. The text supplies no spouses, sexed bodies, or gendered personal names.

**[Regional/modern]** Modern Chiloé and popular sources sometimes call one or both female; other retellings use masculine pronouns. Neither gender scheme is universally canonical.

**[Early attested plurality]** Rosales’s “in every province there is some Tenten” is important. *Tenten* initially functions not only as one cosmic individual but as a class or network of local sacred refuges, each associated with a serpent. The now-familiar singular world-serpent Tren-Tren is partly the result of later narrative consolidation.

### Epithets and titles

- **“Lord of the sea”** — *señor del mar*, applied to Caicai by Rosales in the sacrificial conclusion. **[Early attested]**
- **Protectress/protector of humans and animals** — descriptive rather than a fixed title; Rosales says Tenten sheltered them. **[Early attested]**
- **Mistress of lakes and seas** — applied to Kai-Kai in Koessler-Ilg’s twentieth-century Argentine version. **[Regional/later ethnographic]**
- **Sea serpent / earth serpent** — standard modern descriptive pair. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Serpent of water / serpent of land** — modern pedagogical form. **[Modern retelling]**

There is no securely attested precolonial inscriptional epithet, divine office, standardized litany, or theophoric personal-name series for either being.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Rosales record

Rosales, a Jesuit active in Chile from approximately 1629, completed his history in 1674. Its manuscript remained unpublished until Benjamín Vicuña Mackenna’s three-volume edition of 1877–1878. The textual distance between collection, composition, and print matters: 1674 is the date of the source as a manuscript, while 1877 is the date at which the wider learned public could read it.

**What Rosales securely documents:**

- an already-transmitted Indigenous account of an ancient sea inundation;
- sacred mountains called *Tenten* in multiple provinces;
- a warning delivered first by a humble man and also by the Tenten serpent;
- disbelief followed by flight;
- competition between two serpents;
- transformation of the overtaken into whales, swordfish, mullets, sea bass, tuna, and other fish;
- transformation into rocks;
- a Chiloé rock resembling a woman carrying children;
- fish ancestors later fathering children with women gathering seafood;
- animal survival;
- a mountain rising toward the sun;
- deaths from heat, hunger, and cannibalism among those on the summit;
- differing survivor counts;
- survival of seeds and specimens of animals;
- sacrifice of a survivor’s child, quartered and cast into the sea;
- consumption of the offering by fish-kings and mermaids;
- recession of the water and the mountain’s return to its place.

**What Rosales does not establish:**

- an absolute date for the supposed catastrophe;
- that one historical tsunami caused every version;
- that all Mapuche communities shared an identical account;
- that Kai-Kai and Tren-Tren were children of named gods or *pillanes*;
- that their combat created the Chiloé Archipelago in its modern narrated form;
- fixed divine genders;
- standardized images or temples;
- continuous performance of the child sacrifice from precolonial times to 1960.

His record is filtered through a polemical Christian scheme. Rosales attempts to subordinate the Indigenous account to Genesis and to explain fossils and shells as evidence of Noah’s flood. He nevertheless preserves details—especially localized Tenten hills, fish clan origins, and competing survivor totals—that resist a simple biblical reduction.

### Earlier chroniclers and missionary works

**[Negative finding]** No named Kai-Kai/Tren-Tren episode has been securely located in Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (published in parts, 1569–1589) or Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán’s *Cautiverio feliz* (completed in the seventeenth century). Their silence does not prove that the narrative was absent; it shows only that they do not provide an earlier secure textual attestation.

The Mapudungun works of Luis de Valdivia, Andrés Febrés, and Bernardo Havestadt are indispensable evidence for language and missionary categorization. They do not, on present evidence, replace Rosales as the first secure full narrative witness. Early dictionaries were designed for evangelization and cannot be treated as exhaustive catalogues of Indigenous sacred beings.

### Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century corpora

- Rodolfo Lenz, *Estudios araucanos* (1895–1897), collected oral and linguistic material in several Mapuche regions. His work was methodologically innovative but relied on a limited number of informants, often one for a dialect. [Memoria Chilena on Lenz’s fieldwork and method](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-97582.html).
- Lenz, *Tradiciones e ideas de los araucanos acerca de los terremotos* (1912), records earthquake prophecy and Kai-Kai’s cry in a setting where catastrophe can punish *wingka* while Mapuche people suffer innocently. **[Later ethnographic/political reinterpretation]**
- Félix José de Augusta, *Lecturas araucanas* and *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916), preserve early twentieth-century language and narratives. A searchable reproduction of the dictionary confirms its publication and scope. [Augusta dictionary corpus](https://aldoberrios.cl/corpus/1916_Augusta/index.html).
- Tomás Guevara and Ricardo Latcham incorporated the pair into broad ethnological syntheses. Their evolutionary and diffusionist assumptions require caution; they are evidence for early-twentieth-century scholarship, not transparent windows onto a timeless precolonial religion.
- Pascual Coña’s Mapudungun testimony, transcribed and translated by Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach and published in 1930 as *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX*, is a primary Indigenous-language source for social life, *machi*, *machitun*, and *nguillatun*. Its importance does not mean every later story about Kai-Kai appears in it. [Bibliographic record and digitized book](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-8190.html).
- Julio Vicuña Cifuentes, *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* (1915; later editions), and Oreste Plath’s geographical folklore collections helped move the narrative into Chilean national folklore. [Digitized Vicuña Cifuentes volume](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3a/Mitos_y_supersticiones_recogidos_de_la_tradici%C3%B3n_oral_chilena_con_referencias_comparativas_a_los_de_otros_paises_latinos_%28IA_mitosysuperstici00vicu%29.pdf).
- Francisco J. Cavada’s work on Chiloé and later collectors preserve insular forms in which the struggle explains islands, channels, hills, and rock formations. These are **regional folklore**, not automatically the earliest Mapuche form.
- Bertha Koessler-Ilg, *Cuentan los araucanos* (1954), followed by *Tradiciones araucanas* (1962) and later editions, preserves Argentine Neuquén material gathered around San Martín de los Andes. The bibliographic record identifies *Tradiciones araucanas* as a three-volume collection. [UNLP catalogue](https://memoria.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/library?a=d&c=libros&d=Jpm121).

### Koessler-Ilg’s distinctive cycle

**[Regional variant; later ethnographic; syncretistic elements possible]** Koessler-Ilg supplies an elaborate cosmogony absent from Rosales:

1. The elder children of Sun and Moon rebel.
2. Moon intercedes, but they rebel again.
3. Sun hurls them to earth, breaking the landscape.
4. Moon’s tears form lakes.
5. The children are revived in nonhuman form as Kai-Kai Filu.
6. Kai-Kai becomes mistress of lakes and seas and hates the creator and humanity.
7. A good serpent, made by God from special clay, dwells on the Tren-Tren “mountains of fire” and warns humanity.
8. Volcanic fire, thunder, mountain uplift, and flood form one catastrophe.

The version’s “good God,” moral rebellion, resurrection, and in some tellings Adam-and-Eve survivors strongly suggest long Christian contact. That does not make the account inauthentic as twentieth-century Mapuche oral tradition; it prevents its projection unchanged into precolonial antiquity. [Text and comparison in a University of Chile geological study](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/bitstream/handle/2250/175020/Influencia-de-los-procesos-geologicos-en-la-cosmovision-Mapuche-entre-Concepcion-y-Chiloe.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=4).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Kai-Kai

**[Early attested]**

- Associated with lowlands, sea, inundation, and hostility toward humans.
- Makes the sea rise by crying *Cai, cai*.
- Attempts to drown Tenten, the refuge mountain, and its human dependents.
- Receives part of the child sacrifice as “lord of the sea.”
- Is an adversary, but Rosales does not say that Kai-Kai is killed.

**[Later elaboration]**

- Sleeps beneath or within the sea.
- Strikes water with an enormous tail.
- Governs marine beings and all waters.
- Appears as a horned being or a horse-serpent hybrid in some Argentine records.
- Becomes a moral punisher angered by human ingratitude toward the sea. This familiar environmental ethic is not stated in Rosales and is a later narrative framing.

### Tren-Tren

**[Early attested]**

- Is simultaneously sacred hill, serpent resident of that hill, warning power, and protector.
- Raises the mountain in proportion to the rising sea.
- Enables transformations into fish or stone.
- Protects humans, animals, seeds, and future reproduction.
- Returns the mountain to its original position as the sea recedes.
- May speak first through or in the form of a poor, humble man; Rosales leaves the relationship between the warning man and serpent somewhat ambiguous.

**[Later elaboration]**

- Becomes a single global “earth serpent.”
- Is assigned control of earth, mountains, volcanoes, fertility, plants, and animals.
- Is sometimes said to cause earthquakes while Kai-Kai causes tsunamis.
- Is regularly cast as absolute good opposed to absolute evil. The early account supports protective versus destructive roles but is more ambivalent: both beings require appeasement, and Tenten itself commands the sacrifice.

### Limits

The powers are reciprocal rather than unlimited. Kai-Kai can raise the water but cannot overtake a mountain continually lifted by Tren-Tren. Tren-Tren saves only some people; others drown, transform, burn, starve, or eat one another. Neither serpent achieves total victory. This unresolved equilibrium explains why later communities could treat earthquakes and tsunamis as renewals of the contest.

### Iconography

**[Secure early finding]** No precolonial image is securely inscribed or otherwise identified as Kai-Kai or Tren-Tren. There is no ancient canonical portrait.

A star-headed stone mace in the Museo Chileno de Arte Precolombino bears a serpent and lizard. The museum asks whether it *could* represent the pair, explicitly presenting the identification as a possibility, not a demonstrated attribution. It must therefore be classified **[modern iconographic hypothesis]**, not “the earliest image.” [Museum catalogue entry](https://museo.precolombino.cl/caicai-y-trentren/).

Américo Gordon’s 1986 comparison of more than 300 *trariwe* belts proposed that paired serpentiform and human-fish motifs encode the flood narrative. This is a significant **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**, but the textiles lack captions naming Kai-Kai or Tren-Tren. [Summary of Gordon’s interpretation](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0716-27902022000100197&script=sci_arttext).

Blue Kai-Kai and brown or green Tren-Tren, now common in schoolbooks and illustration, are **[modern visual conventions]**. No early source assigns colors, crests, wings, horns, or fixed sexual dimorphism.

### Sacred animals, plants, numbers, and celestial associations

- Serpent form is secure.
- Fish, whales, marine mammals, and mermaids belong to the Rosales narrative as transformed ancestors or recipients of sacrifice, not necessarily as cult animals.
- Sun is an environmental danger in Rosales, burning survivors when the mountain rises too high.
- Sun and Moon become parents of Kai-Kai in Koessler-Ilg’s regional cycle.
- Fourfold symbolism is widespread in later descriptions of Mapuche cosmology, but a fixed doctrine of “four elders” as the only survivors was not verified in the earliest account. Rosales instead gives **competing totals**: two men and two women with their children, or one man and one woman called *Llituche*. Claims of precisely “four elders” should be labeled **[later or unestablished variant]** unless tied to a named narrator and edition.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogies

**[Early attested]** Rosales gives neither serpent parents, siblings, consorts, nor offspring. Their relationship is enmity, not a stated sibling bond. Calling them sisters, brothers, twins, or children of two antagonistic *pillanes* is not supported by the earliest text.

**[Koessler-Ilg regional cycle]** Kai-Kai originates from rebellious children of Sun and Moon. Tren-Tren is separately fashioned by a creator from special clay. They are therefore not siblings in this version.

**[Modern popular synthesis]** Online and school retellings often describe both as children or embodiments of rival *pillanes*. No early primary source located in this research establishes that genealogy. It should be marked **[modern invention or unattributed synthesis]** until a specific oral source is supplied.

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Kai-Kai ↔ Tren-Tren:** Kai-Kai raises the sea to drown humans, Tenten, and its mountain; Tren-Tren raises the mountain and protects refugees. Rosales, *Historia general*, book I, chapter 1, completed 1674. **[Early attested]**
- **Tren-Tren → Kai-Kai:** Tren-Tren requires survivors to sacrifice a child to appease both itself and Kai-Kai; the sea then recedes. Rosales, 1674. **[Early attested]**
- **Kai-Kai ↔ Sun:** In Koessler-Ilg’s version, rebellious children of Sun are cast down and later become Kai-Kai; Kai-Kai inherits hostility toward the heavenly parent. Collected in Argentine Patagonia and published in *Cuentan los araucanos* (1954). **[Regional later ethnographic; possible syncretistic elaboration]**
- **Kai-Kai ↔ Moon:** Moon intercedes for the rebellious children and weeps when Sun destroys them; her tears form lakes from which Kai-Kai’s aquatic domain develops. Koessler-Ilg, 1954. **[Regional later ethnographic]**
- **Tren-Tren ↔ creator/“good God”:** The creator forms the protective serpent from special clay and commands it to warn humanity when Kai-Kai stirs the waters. Koessler-Ilg, 1954. **[Regional later ethnographic; syncretistic terminology]**
- **Kai-Kai ↔ fish-kings and mermaids:** Rosales says the quartered child is thrown into the sea for the “kings of the fishes and the mermaids” to eat. The text presents these as recipients, but does not name individual beings. Rosales, 1674. **[Early attested collective interaction]**
- **Kai-Kai/Tren-Tren ↔ Ngünechen:** Later theologized accounts make the flood the origin of a renewed relationship between survivors and *Chau Ngünechen*. Armando Marileo’s formulation, quoted in Rolf Foerster’s 1990s discussion, says the episode grounds the Mapuche people and *ngillatun*. **[Modern Indigenous theological systematization]**
- **Kai-Kai ↔ Pillanes:** Later stories make *pillanes* Kai-Kai’s assistants, ancestors, or parents. **[Regional/modern retelling]** No such interaction appears in Rosales.
- **Tren-Tren ↔ Sumpall:** Foerster and later interpreters relate the transformed aquatic survivors and fish ancestry to the *Sumpall* cycle. This is a structural or genealogical interpretation, not a direct named meeting in Rosales. **[Modern scholarly/Indigenous systematization]**
- **Kai-Kai/Tren-Tren ↔ Wekufü:** Some modern pantheon charts place Kai-Kai among destructive *wekufü* and Tren-Tren among beneficent *ngen*. **[Learned systematization]** A direct narrative encounter was not located.
- **Kai-Kai/Tren-Tren ↔ Chiloé beings such as Millalobo, Pincoya, or Caleuche:** No early or securely cited episode of direct interaction was found. Co-membership in modern “Chilote mythology” lists is not evidence of interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Rosales’s inundation

**[Early attested, 1674]**

People know that the sea once covered the land but do not know when. Every province possesses a revered Tenten hill because ancestors survived there and because people expect to flee there if another inundation comes. A poor man warns of the disaster but is ignored. The serpent dwelling on the mountain also warns people to seek refuge.

Before the water arrives, some people plan that, if overtaken, they will become whales, swordfish, mullets, sea bass, tuna, or other fish. Kai-Kai, enemy of Tenten and humanity, makes the sea rise. Tenten raises the mountain to match it. Families run uphill carrying children, spouses, and what food they can seize. Few reach the summit. Those caught by water become fish. Others become stone so the current cannot carry them away.

Rosales connects this to a Chiloé formation resembling a mother with children. He also reports that the fish-people later come ashore to have sexual relations with women gathering seafood, thereby founding lineages named for marine species.

Animals reach safety more quickly than humans. As Tenten rises toward the sun, survivors shelter their heads beneath pottery vessels, but many burn; baldness is explained as an effect of the heat. Hunger produces cannibalism. Survivors preserve selected animals and seeds.

Rosales then explicitly acknowledges variant survivor counts: one version has two men and two women with their children; another has a single man and woman called *Llituche*, “beginning of the generation of people.” Tenten commands them to sacrifice one child. They quarter the child and cast the pieces into the sea for Kai-Kai, the fish-kings, and mermaids. The sea subsides, and Tenten settles back into place.

This is not a simple tale in which “good Tren-Tren defeats evil Kai-Kai.” Tren-Tren saves life but also demands sacrificial payment; Kai-Kai is checked, not annihilated.

### B. Fish and stone ancestries

**[Early attested/regional]** The transformed survivors are not merely casualties. They generate continuing lineages. Rosales reports families bearing names associated with whales, sea lions, mullets, and other marine beings, and offers transformed fish-men as the tradition’s explanation.

The Chiloé stone woman similarly turns landscape into historical testimony. The rock is not an illustration added by Rosales; he identifies it as a locally shown place where a mother and children became stone. The motif belongs to an older pattern of reading unusual landforms as bodies of flood survivors.

### C. Koessler-Ilg’s “Sun and his rebellious children”

**[Regional later ethnographic]** This Argentine cycle creates a complete cosmic prehistory for the serpents. Rebellion in the celestial family causes geological rupture; Moon’s grief creates lakes; rebellious children become Kai-Kai; the creator makes Tren-Tren as a guardian. Kai-Kai’s wingbeats or tail movements raise water and mountains, while Tren-Tren mountains emit volcanic fire, thunder, and lightning.

This cycle is fuller than Rosales but not therefore older. Its heavenly family, creator terminology, resurrection motif, and occasional Adam-and-Eve survivors reveal centuries of intercultural transformation.

### D. Chiloé creation of the archipelago

**[Regional folklore/late elaboration]** In widely circulated Chilote versions, Kai-Kai floods an originally more continuous land while Tren-Tren raises it. The surviving high ground becomes Chiloé’s islands, hills, and broken coastline; channels mark the sea’s incursion. Later collectors including Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, Plath, and local Chiloé writers made this the best-known insular form.

Rosales already knew a Chiloé flood-rock tradition, so the islands version did not arise from nothing. Nevertheless, his account does not say explicitly that the combat created the entire archipelago. That complete etiological geography should be labeled later Chiloé elaboration.

### E. The “second birth” or founding of the Mapuche

Hugo Carrasco’s influential 1980s interpretation calls Tren-Tren/Kai-Kai a myth of the Mapuche “second birth”: the first population is transformed or largely destroyed, and survivors found a renewed people. [Article record](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=652130).

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction grounded in traditional narrative]** The phrase “founding story of the Mapuche” is defensible if it means that many twentieth- and twenty-first-century narrators and intellectuals understand the survivors as ancestors of today’s Mapuche. It is too strong if used to claim that Rosales documented one universally canonical ethnogenesis. His source reports local ancestral refuges and competing survivor numbers.

Armando Marileo’s 1989 interpretation explicitly makes the catastrophe the beginning of Mapuche culture, the *ngillatun*, and renewed relationship with *Chau Ngünechen*. Foerster observes that such cosmogonic accounts show Christian influence while remaining coherent Mapuche responses to colonization and evangelization. [Foerster discussion](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0009042.pdf).

## 6. Cult, sacred places, and ritual practice

### Tenten hills

**[Early attested]** Rosales’s strongest cultic evidence is not a temple but a sacred landscape. He says each province had a highly venerated Tenten mountain, regarded as ancestral refuge and future sanctuary. This establishes:

- local sacred topography;
- collective memory attached to named hills;
- anticipated reuse during future catastrophe;
- belief in a resident speaking serpent;
- a dispersed, noncentralized network rather than a single temple institution.

A modern University of Chile dataset maps 36 identified Tenten, Trentren, and Caicai places in Chile and Argentina. The dataset documents enduring toponymy, not the prehistoric age of each identification. [University of Chile Tenten/Caicai map](https://datos.uchile.cl/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi%3A10.34691%2FUCHILE%2FQSFITR&version=2.10).

### Priesthood and temples

**[Negative finding]** There is no evidence for a dedicated Kai-Kai or Tren-Tren temple, permanent priesthood, image-house, liturgical calendar, temple economy, or exclusive sacrificial office. Mapuche religion historically centered on kin territories, *rewe*, *machi*, *ngen*, ancestors, community ceremonies, and sacred landscape rather than masonry temples of the Mediterranean or Mesoamerican type.

*Machi* can interpret earthquakes, illness, dreams, and imbalance, but they are not simply “priests of Kai-Kai.” Treating every *nguillatun* after an earthquake as Kai-Kai worship would be an unjustified conflation.

### Ngillatun and machitun

The extensive ethnographies of *nguillatun* and *machitun* document prayer, dance, offerings, healing, and the restoration of balance. Pascual Coña’s testimony contains separate chapters on the *machi*, *machitun*, and *nguillatun*. These provide context for how catastrophic imbalance could be ritually addressed, but they do not establish a universal Kai-Kai liturgy.

**[Later Indigenous theological systematization]** Marileo interprets the flood episode as the origin of *ngillatun*. This is an important modern Mapuche theological formulation, not a statement found in Rosales.

### Sacrifice in the myth

**[Early attested]** Rosales unambiguously records a child killed, divided into four portions, and offered to the sea. The act is indispensable to his version’s resolution. It cannot be omitted as an embarrassing colonial accusation, but its evidentiary limits also matter:

- Rosales does not say he witnessed it.
- He reports it as an event within the ancient narrative.
- His polemical aim was to depict Indigenous religion as demonic error.
- The account nevertheless predates the 1960 Collileufu case by nearly three centuries and cannot have been invented to explain that modern event.
- It establishes a sacrificial motif, not the frequency of historical human sacrifice.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1674: oral and landscape history

The tradition necessarily predates Rosales’s written account in some form, because he reports inherited beliefs, sacred places, local variants, and ancestral lineages. Its absolute antiquity is unknowable. Geological interpretations linking it to a particular prehistoric earthquake or tsunami remain hypotheses.

**[Modern geological reconstruction]** South-central Chile’s repeated subduction earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, coastal subsidence, and uplift supply an exceptionally plausible environment for intergenerational catastrophe traditions. They do not date the narrative or prove that a single event generated it.

### 1674–1877: manuscript without broad print circulation

Rosales completed the text in 1674. During the next two centuries the narrative continued orally and in local sacred geography, while his manuscript remained largely unavailable. Therefore not every later parallel can be explained as borrowing from the printed Rosales text.

### Late nineteenth to early twentieth century: ethnographic fixation

Lenz, Guevara, Latcham, Augusta, Coña/Moesbach, Vicuña Cifuentes, Cavada, and others recorded language, ritual, prophecy, folklore, and local variants amid Chilean and Argentine territorial incorporation of Mapuche lands, missionization, reduction, and schooling.

This period changed the narrative’s documentary form:

- oral performances became bounded printed “myths”;
- local Tenten hills became one generalized cosmic mountain or serpent;
- collectors compared the story with Noah, Deucalion, American flood traditions, volcanism, and solar myths;
- “Araucanian mythology” was organized into pantheon-like systems not necessarily recognized by individual narrators.

### 1954 onward: the Koessler-Ilg synthesis

Koessler-Ilg’s dramatic Argentine cosmogony gave later popular writers a genealogy and moral universe absent from Rosales. Its widespread reuse sometimes erases its date and regional provenance.

### 1960: earthquake, tsunami, and Collileufu

On 22 May 1960 the magnitude 9.5 Valdivia earthquake and resulting tsunamis devastated southern Chile. On 5 June, during continuing tremors and maritime danger, a *nguillatun* was held at Collileufu near Puerto Saavedra. A child—named in records as Luis Quimén Painecur or José Luis Painecur Painecur, with reported age varying around five or six—was taken from the care of his grandfather and killed or cast into the sea.

The police account reproduced in later scholarship states that Juan Paiñán Huechumán went to obtain the boy while the community performed *nguillatun* “to appease the tempests of the sea.” The accused included the *machi* Juana Namuncura Añen and men alleged to have carried out her direction. [Contemporary act as reproduced by Memoria Chilena](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0009042.pdf).

### The trial

Proceedings culminated in the Nueva Imperial court decision, role no. 24,228, dated 2 October 1962. Later legal scholarship reports acquittal on the ground of overwhelming fear or an irresistible force associated with catastrophe and ancestral custom. [Modern legal study of the Painecura case](https://ojs.uc.cl/index.php/rldr/article/download/44011/37247/123697).

Important evidentiary cautions:

- The original regional case file was for years reported missing; Sonia Montecino found that neither the judicial archive nor the principal repositories she checked could produce it.
- Later writers report obtaining a copy, and modern legal scholarship supplies a role number and decision date.
- Press accounts sensationalized the event and frequently assigned sole agency to the *machi*, while the ceremony involved a wider community.
- Names, ages, and details vary between police, press, judicial, and secondary accounts.
- The child’s body was not recovered.
- The trial did not judicially prove an unbroken precolonial practice. It addressed responsibility for one death under extraordinary circumstances.

Montecino argues that the event, media coverage, anthropological intervention, and Indigenous organizational responses exposed a threefold political discourse: supposed universality of sacrifice, social abandonment and poverty, and the existence of a culturally distinct people within the Chilean state. Her dossier treats Kai-Kai and Tren-Tren not as a mechanical “cause” but as part of the symbolic field through which catastrophe and payment were interpreted.

### After 1960

The sacrifice became the most controversial modern episode attached to the serpents. It has been variously:

- cited as survival of the Rosales sacrificial motif;
- treated as a unique crisis reaction;
- sensationalized as proof of timeless “savagery”;
- reinterpreted as a legal-cultural-defense precedent;
- remembered in theatre, documentary, journalism, and scholarship.

The existence of Rosales’s motif makes total disconnection implausible, but it does not prove institutional continuity. The responsible formulation is: **a seventeenth-century mythic sacrifice and a documented twentieth-century ritual killing occupy the same catastrophe-and-appeasement complex, while the exact chain of transmission cannot be reconstructed.**

### Present

The narrative remains active in Mapuche cultural teaching, literature, environmental politics, community memory, museums, schools, public art, and Chilean folklore. Sacred hills and the paired names remain geographically embedded. This is continuity of narrative and place-memory, not evidence for a centralized modern cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and Indigenous cultural thought

Foerster, Grebe, Faron, Bacigalupo, Carrasco, Dillehay, and others have interpreted the pair through complementary opposition, sacred landscape, *machi* practice, cosmology, social reproduction, catastrophe, and colonial transformation.

Ana Mariella Bacigalupo’s work on Ngünechen warns against casually identifying distinct beings—Ngünechen, *ngen*, Tren-Tren, Kai-Kai, *wekufü*, and the Christian God—as one timeless deity. [Bacigalupo article record](https://revistaschilenas.uchile.cl/handle/2250/160655?show=full).

### Literature, music, and performance

Víctor Jara’s unfinished or less widely known “Cai Cai Vilú” project has been analyzed in relation to magical thought and the Nueva Canción Chilena. Modern political and musical treatment turns the serpents into figures of historical conflict, class, Indigenous survival, and ecological balance. [Musical-historical analysis](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0716-27902022000100197&script=sci_arttext).

Miguel Serrano’s *Ni por mar ni por tierra* (1950) linked the Chiloé remnant to Lemuria. **[Modern esoteric invention]** The lost-continent interpretation is not Indigenous early evidence. Serrano’s later esoteric and political associations make it especially important not to retroject his synthesis into Mapuche tradition.

Eduardo Luna’s play *Painecur* (premiered 2017) revisits the Collileufu child and the ethics of representation. **[Modern dramatic reception]**

### Museums and education

Museum, school, and tourism versions usually simplify the cycle:

1. humans become ungrateful;
2. Kai-Kai strikes the sea;
3. Tren-Tren commands the hills to rise;
4. both tire;
5. the struggle creates Chile’s geography.

This is a coherent modern teaching narrative, but several components—the ingratitude motive, the single uniform geography, and mutual exhaustion—are not in Rosales. Chile’s Education Ministry now presents the paired serpents as sea and terrestrial powers, demonstrating their incorporation into official intercultural education. [Education curriculum example](https://bibliotecadigital.mineduc.cl/bitstream/handle/20.500.12365/19249/ProgMapuche-3ero-2022web.pdf?sequence=1).

### Neopagan, occult, and New Age use

**[Negative finding]** No well-documented, sizable, continuous neopagan denomination centered on Kai-Kai and Tren-Tren was found. No historical connection to Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, ceremonial magic, or classical grimoire traditions is attested.

Occult websites and universalist “serpent wisdom” treatments exist, but they generally detach the beings from named Mapuche narrators and documentary history. Claims that they encode Atlantis, Lemuria, chakras, kundalini, extraterrestrials, or a universal dragon war are **modern inventions** unless presented explicitly as contemporary creative interpretations.

### National and political appropriation

The myth has been used as:

- a Chilean national explanation for an earthquake-prone, island-fragmented landscape;
- a Mapuche ethnogenesis and survival narrative;
- an environmental allegory opposing extractive ingratitude to protective earth;
- a political image for colonial flooding, land loss, and Indigenous resurgence;
- a rhetoric of catastrophe directed against *wingka* domination.

These uses are historically real modern receptions. None is automatically “the original meaning.”

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is it a precolonial myth or a Christianized flood story?

Rosales’s account is already written inside a Noahic polemic. He explicitly tries to make the Mapuche story a corrupted memory of Genesis. Yet its sea surge rather than rain, localized rising hills, serpent antagonism, fish transformation, marine lineage formation, rock bodies, solar burning, and reciprocal landscape powers are not reducible to Genesis.

The best-supported conclusion is **layering**:

- a Mapuche catastrophe-and-landscape complex was present by 1674;
- Rosales interpreted it through Christian universal history;
- centuries of missionization encouraged further exchange;
- later versions could incorporate creator-God, sin, Adam and Eve, moral punishment, and resurrection without ceasing to be Mapuche oral productions.

### One myth or a family of myths?

The evidence favors a family:

- local Tenten refuges;
- a general sea-versus-mountain contest;
- fish and rock transformations;
- alternative survivor counts;
- Chiloé island geography;
- Argentine solar and volcanic cosmogony;
- prophecies targeting colonizers;
- modern ecological and national narratives.

A smooth “canonical version” is mainly the product of folklore anthologies and school texts.

### Good versus evil?

Rosales does contrast destructive Kai-Kai with protective Tenten, but Tenten orders the child sacrifice, and both must be appeased. Grebe and later interpreters place the opposition within broader Mapuche dualism. The absolute moral binary is therefore a useful later interpretation, not an exhaustive early description.

### Did a specific paleo-tsunami originate the story?

Geologists and archaeologists reasonably compare the myth with repeated megathrust earthquakes, tsunamis, uplift, subsidence, and volcanism. A University of Chile study maps the traditions against geological processes. This supports environmental plausibility, not a unique date. Oral tradition cannot currently identify whether the narrative remembers the 1575 Valdivia earthquake, an earlier event, multiple events, or a cosmological pattern continually renewed by catastrophe.

### Are there ancient images?

No secure identification exists. The museum mace and Gordon’s textile reading are hypotheses. Assertions that a particular pre-Hispanic serpent image “is Kai-Kai” should be rejected unless supported by provenance, dating, and an explicit chain of interpretation.

### Was human sacrifice customary?

Rosales documents it narratively; Collileufu documents a modern death under ritual circumstances. Colonial sources also allege sacrifices in war and crisis, but they require source criticism because accusations of cannibalism and sacrifice served colonial polemic.

The evidence does not support either extreme:

- “Mapuche religion never contained human sacrifice” ignores Rosales and Collileufu.
- “Mapuche regularly sacrificed children to Kai-Kai” extrapolates far beyond the record.

### State of the archives

- Rosales’s manuscript history and first edition are documented by Chile’s National Library.
- Many folklore, linguistic, and ethnographic works survive in the Biblioteca Nacional de Chile, Memoria Chilena, university repositories, and Argentine collections.
- Koessler-Ilg’s collections are institutionally catalogued, but not every volume is digitally available.
- The Collileufu file’s reported disappearance severely complicates reconstruction; modern publications use surviving copies, reports, press records, and legal summaries.
- Much primary testimony remains mediated by missionaries, translators, folklorists, and Spanish-language editors. Even bilingual editions require attention to who selected, transcribed, translated, and arranged the material.

## 11. Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction begins not with two neatly classified gods but with Rosales’s plural sacred landscape: every province had a Tenten refuge, and the serpent, mountain, ancestors, animals, seeds, fish lineages, and future catastrophe formed one complex. Kai-Kai’s sea and Tenten’s rising land opposed one another without final annihilation.

Later history progressively singularized and systematized the beings. Chiloé tradition made their contest the origin of the archipelago. Argentine narration supplied Sun, Moon, rebellion, resurrection, volcanism, and a creator-fashioned guardian. Ethnologists interpreted the pair through dualism; Indigenous intellectuals made the catastrophe a second birth and source of *ngillatun*; schools transformed them into accessible sea and earth deities; artists and political writers made them figures of ecology, colonization, and survival.

The sacrifice cannot be treated as a marginal embarrassment. It is explicit in the earliest text and reappeared, under historically exceptional circumstances, at Collileufu in 1960. But neither occurrence licenses the claim of a stable sacrificial institution. The responsible record is narrower and more unsettling: the narrative had long linked cosmic restoration with the death of a child, and a twentieth-century community facing an unprecedented earthquake-tsunami crisis performed a killing interpreted through catastrophe, ritual payment, and ancestral authority. The subsequent trial, missing archive, press sensationalism, anthropological intervention, and cultural-defense argument are themselves part of Kai-Kai and Tren-Tren’s documented modern history.

The pair have no single canonical genealogy, icon, gender, temple, or final victory. Their persistence resides instead in landscape, seismic recurrence, oral variation, historical memory, and the continuing possibility that the sea may rise and the land answer.
