# Chonchón: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A nocturnal supernatural being or monstrous manifestation associated with sorcery—not a god or goddess. In Mapuche-language and ethnographic sources it is variously a large ominous night-bird, the bird-form of a *kalku* (harmful sorcerer), or a detached flying human head propelled by its ears. In Chilean, Chilote, and Argentine folklore it becomes the *chonchón*, *chonchoñ*, *chon-chon*, or onomatopoeic *tué-tué*. It is sometimes classified as a *wekufü*, but the evidence more securely presents it as a form or instrument of the *kalku*, not consistently as an autonomous spirit.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Miguel de Olivares, writing in the eighteenth century, names the *chonchón* simply as a nocturnal bird resembling an owl. That passage does **not** describe a flying human head. The earliest presently located explicit printed description of the supernatural head—“con cabeza humana” and ears serving as wings—is Pedro Lautaro Ferrer’s *Historia general de la medicina en Chile*, vol. I (Talca, 1904), based, according to its note, on personal information collected in Arauco. Tomás Guevara’s independently important Mapuche ethnography, *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* (1908), supplies the earliest extensive narrative and an illustration. Earlier chronicles document Indigenous sorcerers’ animal transformations, but not securely the named flying-head being.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY CONTEXT]** Colonial or eighteenth-century evidence preceding the fully documented Chonchón complex.
- **[EARLIEST SECURE]** The earliest located explicit documentation of the supernatural being.
- **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]** Belief or narrative recorded from identifiable communities or consultants.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Chilean, Chilote, Huilliche, or Argentine oral tradition as collected by folklorists.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A geographically restricted form not safely projected onto all Mapuche tradition.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** An ethnologist’s, folklorist’s, lexicographer’s, or popular mythographer’s ordering of heterogeneous evidence.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]** A historically conditioned equation with Christian demonology or another tradition.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** A scholarly interpretation assembled from dispersed evidence.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/UNVERIFIED]** A recent claim for which no older documentary basis was located.

## 1. Identification, names, and classifications

### Chonchón, *choñchoñ*

The standard Spanish spelling is **Chonchón**, with older or variant forms **chonchon**, **chon-chon**, and Mapudungun-oriented **choñchoñ**. Félix José de Augusta’s *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916), p. 25, records *choñchoñ* only as “cierto pájaro grande, nocturno”—“a certain large nocturnal bird.” A later linguistic study correctly warns that this lexical gloss alone does not establish the flying-head mythology; its Mapuche informants, however, explicitly defined the being as a sorcerer’s severed head that acquires the properties of an ominous bird ([Salas and related oral-text study, 2000](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0071-17132000003500002&script=sci_arttext&tlng=pt)). **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**

The etymology is unsettled. Rodolfo Lenz connected Chilean *chonchón* with Mapuche bird-names, citing Febrés’s *chuchu*, “a bird” and “grandmother,” but this is a historical proposal, not a demonstrated derivation. The related forms *chucho*, *chuncho*, and *quilquil* name actual owls or owl-like birds in different collections; phonetic resemblance and overlapping ominous associations encouraged their conflation. Claims that *chonchón* transparently “means flying head” are unsupported. The semantically transparent expression for a departing or flying head is instead **tripanlongko**: *tripan*, “to go/come out,” plus *longko*, “head.” The 2018 study *Katulongkon* uses *tripanlongko* for the head that leaves the body ([Moulian and colleagues, 2018](https://scielo.conicyt.cl/article_plus.php?lng=es&pid=S0718-10432018000400213&tlng=es)). **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION grounded in Mapudungun]**

### Tué-tué

**Tué-tué**, **tue-tue**, **tuetué**, and occasionally **totué** imitate the attributed cry. The naming is therefore onomatopoeic, but the antiquity of the exact cry is uncertain: it is prominent in twentieth-century folklore and contemporary testimony, while Augusta’s 1916 dictionary defines *choñchoñ* without mentioning it. The cry is variously heard as a repeated bird-call, sinister laughter, or words. **[FOLKLORE; REGIONAL VARIATION]**

The terms *Chonchón* and *Tué-tué* often designate the same entity in central and southern Chile. They should not be made timeless synonyms: *Chonchón* also denoted an actual nocturnal bird, while *Tué-tué* is a vernacular name generated by the cry and appears especially prominently in non-Indigenous rural Chilean narration.

### Tripanlongko

**Tripanlongko**, “the head that goes out/leaves,” is the analytically clearest Mapuche designation for the flying-head phenomenon. It emphasizes bodily separation rather than a zoological species. In the documented conception, the sorcerer’s vital or spiritual agency travels materially in the head; the abandoned trunk lies inert. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**

### Chucho, chuncho, quilquil, chaihue

These are bird-names or regional equivalents rather than securely universal names of the supernatural head.

- **Chucho/chuncho:** Frequently connected with the pygmy owl, generally *Glaucidium nanum* in older Chilean ornithology. Its call and nocturnal or crepuscular behavior generated death-omen traditions.
- **Quilquil/quitquil:** Another owl-name in Chilean and Chilote collections. The equation *quilquil = Chonchón* is regional, not universal.
- **Chaihue:** Alejandro Cañas is cited as applying this Chilote name to the Chonchón. Later surveys found it poorly attested.
- **Deñ/deñi:** In eastern Chiloé, a dark bird into which witches may transform. It belongs to the larger bird-sorcery complex but is not automatically the detached head.

A review of birds and plants in Chilote witchcraft preserves these distinctions and notes that Chonchón extended from Mapuche tradition into central Chile ([Romo Sánchez’s survey](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0033460.pdf)). **[REGIONAL VARIANTS; LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Classification through time

1. **Eighteenth-century natural-history usage:** a nocturnal owl-like bird. **[EARLY CONTEXT]**
2. **Early twentieth-century Arauco evidence:** a human head with wing-like ears, blood-drinking and disease-producing. **[EARLIEST SECURE]**
3. **Guevara’s Mapuche corpus:** particularly a woman’s head with wings, traveling at night to places frequented by sorcerers; also a category of dangerous sorcerer. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**
4. **Broader Chilean folklore:** a witch or wizard transformed into, or projecting, an ominous nocturnal bird or flying head. **[FOLKLORE]**
5. **Chilote lore:** a bird-like manifestation within a larger complex of witches, the *Voladora*, the *macuñ*, and the Cueva or organization of sorcerers. The Chonchón should not simply be substituted for each of those figures. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
6. **Popular internet mythology:** often presented as a discrete “demon,” “vampire,” or species of monster. These classifications simplify the earlier evidence. **[MODERN INVENTION/LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial sources: precursors, not attestations of the complete being

Neither Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (three parts, 1569–1589) nor Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán’s *Cautiverio feliz* (written in the seventeenth century; first printed 1863) securely names or describes the flying-head Chonchón. They matter for the colonial record of Mapuche society and Spanish representations of Indigenous religion, but citing them as direct Chonchón sources would be misleading.

Diego de Rosales’s *Historia general del reino de Chile, Flandes Indiano*, written in the seventeenth century but first published in 1877–1878, says of people on Mocha Island that they practiced magic and apparently transformed into foxes, dogs, “lions,” sea-lions, and other animals. Rosales supplies early evidence for a **sorcerous metamorphosis complex**, but neither the name *Chonchón* nor a detached flying head occurs in the cited passage. **[EARLY CONTEXT]**

Felipe Gómez de Vidaurre, writing in the eighteenth century, says witches transform into nocturnal birds, fly, and discharge invisible projectiles against enemies. This brings together witchcraft, nocturnal avian transformation, and magical attack, but still does not securely identify the Chonchón as a detached head. These passages are conveniently assembled in *Diablos, brujos y espíritus maléficos* ([Memoria Chilena edition](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0037757.pdf)). **[EARLY CONTEXT]**

### Olivares: the name as a bird

Miguel de Olivares’s eighteenth-century history states that the *mucu* and *chonchón* are birds resembling owls in color and shape and that they avoid light. This is the earliest located exact-name reference, but it is zoological or natural-historical, not an explicit supernatural flying-head account. It may attest the bird-name from which the mythical figure developed or with which it became identified. It does not prove that Olivares knew the later complex. **[EARLY CONTEXT; EARLIEST NAME]**

### Missionary dictionaries

Andrés Febrés’s *Arte de la lengua general del reyno de Chile* (1765) and Bernardo Havestadt’s *Chilidúgú* (1777; nineteenth-century reprint 1883) document relevant Mapudungun religious and anatomical vocabulary but have not yielded a secure entry describing the named Chonchón as a flying head. Their silence matters: the later being cannot simply be read back into every colonial vocabulary.

Félix José de Augusta’s *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916) records *choñchoñ* as a large nocturnal bird. It does not itself give “sorcerer’s flying head.” The distinction between lexeme and narrative complex is explicitly discussed in the 2000 oral-text study ([Scielo full text](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0071-17132000003500002&script=sci_arttext&tlng=pt)). **[LEXICAL ATTESTATION]**

### Ferrer, 1904: earliest explicit flying-head description located

Pedro Lautaro Ferrer’s *Historia general de la medicina en Chile*, vol. I (1904), says:

> “El chonchón, con cabeza humana y cuyas orejas le servían de alas, chupaba la sangre de los enfermos.”

That is: “The Chonchón, with a human head and whose ears served it as wings, sucked the blood of the sick.” The note attributes the surrounding information to personal reports collected in Arauco ([digitized page](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/P%C3%A1gina%3AHistoria_general_de_la_medicina_en_Chile,_tomo_I.pdf/28)). This is the earliest presently located unambiguous printed convergence of name, human head, ears-as-wings, and harmful supernatural action. **[EARLIEST SECURE]**

### Guevara, 1908 and 1911

Tomás Guevara’s *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* (1908) is the foundational extended record. Its “Concepciones míticas” chapter includes a small illustration labelled *chonchoñ* and narratives in which the Chonchón is a winged woman’s head moving by night. One tale concerns a husband or lover who discovers the apparently headless body of a woman; when the body is displaced, the returning head cannot reattach, flies frantically about the room, and can return only after the body is restored to its proper position. The surviving illustration is identified as Guevara 1908, p. 323 ([Wikimedia provenance page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGuevara%281908%29-Psicoloj%C3%ADa-p323-Fig42.2-Choncho%C3%B1.jpg)). **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**

Guevara’s *Folklore araucano* (1911) records the saying *Melei ta chonchón*—“There is a Chonchón” or “It seems [like] a Chonchón”—applied to a suspicious, prying, tale-bearing person. Guevara explains the Chonchón as “a myth of a woman’s head, with wings,” traveling at night to places frequented by witches or sorcerers. Marriage to a woman who is secretly a Chonchón is presented as a danger and legitimate reason for separation ([digitized volume](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0008877.pdf)). **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**

### Vicuña Cifuentes, 1915

Julio Vicuña Cifuentes’s *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* (1915) systematized Chilean oral material. He describes the Chonchón as a mythical human head with enormously enlarged ears used as wings and treats Chonchones as witches or a special class of witches. His corpus helped shift the figure from locally recorded Mapuche material into a national Chilean folklore canon ([digitized volume](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3a/Mitos_y_supersticiones_recogidos_de_la_tradici%C3%B3n_oral_chilena_con_referencias_comparativas_a_los_de_otros_paises_latinos_%28IA_mitosysuperstici00vicu%29.pdf)). **[FOLKLORE; LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Cavada and Chiloé

Francisco J. Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914) describes Chilote witches, bird-transformations, the *Voladora*, and supernatural flight. Cavada compares the Voladora’s punishment of an unfulfilled promise with conduct attributed to the Chonchón. Yet the Voladora is a distinct figure: a woman who removes or deposits her entrails before traveling, serves as a messenger for the witches’ organization, and resumes human form by day. Similarity does not establish identity. The relevant Cavada passage is reproduced by Vicuña Cifuentes in his 1915 collection. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**

### Later ethnographic and linguistic corpus

- Ricardo Latcham’s works on Mapuche religion and mythology linked *chucho/chuncho* owl names with the winged-head legend. This is an origin hypothesis, not direct proof.
- Wilhelm de Moesbach’s publication of Pascual Coña’s life history, commonly cited as *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos* (first edition 1930), is foundational for Mapuche social and religious life. It does not make the Chonchón a central pantheon figure.
- Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collections, later issued in forms including *Cuentan los araucanos* and *Tradiciones araucanas*, document the cross-Andean circulation of Mapuche narratives and include Chonchón vocabulary and comparative notes. Their publication history is complicated: material gathered earlier was edited and published across several decades.
- Ewald Waag’s *Tres entidades wekufu en la cultura mapuche* (1982) interprets the Chonchón in relation to *wekufü* and compares earlier authors.
- Carrasco, Contreras, and García’s “Elementos constitutivos de la matriz simbólica en tres relatos míticos mapuches” (1998) analyzes a narrative semantic field structured by head, body, Chonchón, *kalku*, death, flight, hair, night, sleep, illness, and countermeasures ([article](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0071-17131998003300012&script=sci_arttext)).
- The 2000 oral-text study records contemporary Mapuche informants’ explicit flying-sorcerer-head definition.
- The 2018 *Katulongkon* study places *tripanlongko* within broader Mapuche understandings of the head as a locus of identity and spiritual vitality, while comparing analogous Andean flying-head traditions.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and characteristic actions

The Chonchón’s domain is not divine governance. It belongs to the dangerous nighttime boundary between human society, sorcery, illness, death, and ominous birds.

Documented actions include:

- leaving a sorcerer’s inert body as a detached head;
- flying at night with the ears functioning as wings;
- taking bird form, especially an owl-like form;
- traveling to gatherings or places frequented by sorcerers;
- spying and gathering information;
- announcing death, disease, or misfortune through its cry;
- attacking sick or sleeping persons;
- sucking the blood of the ill in Ferrer’s Arauco account;
- being unable to rejoin the body if the trunk is displaced;
- revealing its human identity if a verbal invitation is made and subsequently claimed.

Not every source contains every attribute. The modern composite—detached head, feathered body, talons, enormous ears, grey plumage, blood-drinking, death prophecy, magical ointment, and compulsory breakfast visit—is an accumulation from different periods and regions.

### Form

Three overlapping forms are documented:

1. **Night-bird:** The oldest named description—Olivares—and Augusta’s dictionary.
2. **Human head with ears as wings:** Ferrer 1904 and later folklore.
3. **A sorcerer assuming an owl or bird form:** colonial metamorphosis accounts and later Mapuche ethnography.

The alternation is structural rather than an easily harmonized zoology. The 2018 synthesis observes that anatomical and ornithomorphic forms “alternate, combine, and transpose”: the head may flap its ears, or the *kalku* may assume the form of a *choñchoñ/tuetué* owl ([*Katulongkon*](https://scielo.conicyt.cl/article_plus.php?lng=es&pid=S0718-10432018000400213&tlng=es)). **[MODERN SCHOLARLY SYNTHESIS]**

### Earliest image

The earliest securely identified image located is the small drawing published by Guevara in 1908, p. 323. It depicts the *chonchoñ* as a horizontally oriented head or compact body with lateral wing-like extensions. It is an ethnographic illustration, not a pre-Columbian cult image. No securely identified archaeological image of the Chonchón is known.

### Colors, animals, objects, calendar

Grey or ash-colored plumage occurs in later bird folklore, but it is not an invariant early attribute. The relevant natural referents include owls and other nocturnal birds; *Glaucidium nanum* is often proposed for the *chuncho/chucho*, but this does not mean every Chonchón account identifies that species.

There is no securely attested sacred color, number, weekday, celestial body, plant, temple emblem, or standardized regalia. Modern lists assigning such correspondences are inventions or occult-style extrapolations.

The magical neck ointment often found in present-day summaries is plausible within wider European and Chilean witchcraft motifs, but no early Chonchón source examined here supplies a stable recipe. It should be labeled **[LATE FOLKLORE/MODERN RETELLING]** rather than “ancient Mapuche ritual.”

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

The Chonchón has no documented divine genealogy, parents, consort, offspring, court, mount, or stable pantheon rank. That absence is not a gap to be filled by reconstruction: the being is principally a transformed person or detachable aspect of one.

### Documented being-to-being relations

- **Kalku — transformation or manifested agency.** A harmful sorcerer travels as the Chonchón or sends out the flying head. Attested clearly in twentieth-century Mapuche ethnography and oral testimony; anticipated by earlier accounts of sorcerous bird-transformation. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**
- **Wekufü — association with harmful power.** Later ethnologists and popular accounts call the Chonchón a *wekufü* or say the *kalku* operates through *wekufü*. Sources do not consistently make a named *wekufü* personally meet or converse with the Chonchón. **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION; NO DISCRETE NARRATIVE INTERACTION]**
- **The Christian Devil — power attributed to him in colonialized Chilean/Chilote witchcraft discourse.** Christian narrators and collectors often explain supernatural flight as diabolical. Cavada explicitly says the Voladora is carried by the Devil; comparable language surrounds the Chonchón and witches. This is a historical syncretistic interpretation, not evidence that a pre-contact Chonchón served the Devil. **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Voladora — comparison, not demonstrated encounter.** Cavada compares their response to promises, but records no episode in which a named Voladora and a Chonchón meet. **[REGIONAL COMPARISON; ABSENCE OF INTERACTION]**
- **Machi — structural opposition, not a single canonical episode.** A *machi* may diagnose or counter harmful sorcery attributed to a *kalku*, but no stable early narrative was located in which an individually named machi combats an individually named Chonchón. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC CONTEXT; ABSENCE OF NAMED INTERACTION]**
- **Anchimallén, Imbunche, Caleuche, Pillán, Ngenechén, Trauco, Fiura, Camahueto, Pincoya — no documented Chonchón interaction located.** Their co-occurrence in modern “Mapuche/Chilote pantheon” lists is not a narrative relationship.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The absent body and returning head

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION: Guevara 1908]**

A man unexpectedly approaches or enters the house of a woman—sometimes his wife, sometimes a lover or prospective partner—and finds her body lying supine without its head. He moves or turns the body. The head returns before dawn, flying about the room and trying unsuccessfully to settle upon the neck. It strikes the walls or flutters in distress. When the body is restored to the original position, head and trunk reunite and the woman resumes ordinary form.

The episode supplies several rules:

- the flying head and inert trunk remain one person;
- correct bodily orientation is required for reintegration;
- the transformation is vulnerable while the head is away;
- discovering the secret exposes an intimate partner’s identity as a sorcerer.

Later versions may punish the discoverer or leave the sorcerer dead when dawn arrives. Those endings require individual sourcing; they are not present in every version.

### The nocturnal sorcerers’ journey

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION/FOLKLORE]**

The Chonchón travels at night to gatherings of sorcerers, spies on households, or seeks a victim. Guevara describes a woman’s winged head making excursions to places frequented by witches. Later accounts expand the journey into attendance at a cave, *reni*, or “Salamanca.” These cave and diabolical-school elements belong partly to Hispanic and Chilote witchcraft systems. A generalized internet account that every Chonchón flies to the same coven is therefore over-systematized.

### Blood attack and illness

**[EARLIEST SECURE: Ferrer 1904]**

The Chonchón approaches a sick person and sucks blood, worsening illness or causing death. Later narratives describe entry into a room and struggle with the patient’s spirit. Only the simpler blood-sucking statement is securely present in Ferrer. The elaborate “astral combat” version appears largely in later retellings and should not be projected backward.

### The “tué-tué” death omen

**[FOLKLORE]**

A persistent cry near a house announces death, illness, or misfortune. Some Argentine material adds marriage as a possible portent, showing that “always predicts death” is too absolute. The omen probably converged with long-standing beliefs about actual owl calls. Olivares’s and Augusta’s bird definitions support the avian background but do not themselves document the complete omen narrative.

### The invitation and claimant

**[LATER CHILEAN FOLKLORE]**

On hearing the cry, a person invites the unseen being to return for food—usually breakfast, tea, salt, yerba, or grain. The next day a seemingly ordinary neighbor or stranger arrives to claim the invitation, thereby revealing the Chonchón’s human identity. Refusing what was promised risks retaliation.

This widespread contemporary motif is useful as folklore but was not located in the earliest Arauco descriptions. Elaborations involving a Seal of Solomon drawn in animal blood are especially late and reflect European ceremonial-magic imagery. **[LATE ACCRETION/MODERN RETELLING]**

### Countermeasures or *contras*

No universal anti-Chonchón rite exists. Reported measures include:

- commanding it to continue on its way;
- throwing salt into the fire;
- placing scissors open in a cross beneath a seat or pillow;
- marking or reversing a sweaty saddlecloth or *pelero*;
- reciting a formula involving repeated Tuesdays;
- making—and scrupulously keeping—an invitation;
- moving, overturning, or otherwise obstructing the abandoned body;
- invoking Christ, Solomon, Saint Cyprian, or other Christian powers.

The combinations differ by locality and era. Salt, crosses, scissors, Solomon, Cyprian, and “the Devil” show the incorporation of Iberian and Christian magical vocabulary. They cannot be confidently reconstructed as a single pre-contact Mapuche rite. A twentieth-century national folklore card records throwing salt on the fire ([Biblioteca Nacional Digital](https://www.bibliotecanacionaldigital.gob.cl/colecciones/BND/00/AE/AE0027325.pdf)). **[FOLKLORE; SYNCRETISTIC ACCRETION]**

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is no evidence of a Chonchón cult in the usual sense.

- No temple, shrine, priesthood, formal festival, pilgrimage, sacred calendar, or votive corpus is known.
- No archaeological structure has been securely identified as dedicated to the Chonchón.
- No theophoric personal names based on Chonchón are securely documented.
- No communal sacrificial liturgy to the Chonchón is attested.
- It is feared, diagnosed, repelled, narrated, or occasionally addressed; it is not ordinarily worshipped.

The relevant institutions are instead Mapuche healing and anti-sorcery practice and, in Chiloé, the reputed organization of witches. The *machi*’s healing work and the *machitun* concern illness and harmful agencies broadly. They should not be redescribed as a Chonchón priesthood or Chonchón ritual.

The *nguillatun* is likewise a major communal Mapuche ceremony, but no evidence makes the Chonchón its recipient. Faron, Grebe, Foerster, Bacigalupo, and Dillehay are vital for the surrounding religious system precisely because they show how misleading a Greco-Roman-style “pantheon” model can be. Mapuche religious agency is relational, localized, and distributed among ancestors, masters or owners, spirits, shamans, sorcerers, natural forces, and Christian figures.

### Sacrifice and violence

No human sacrifice to the Chonchón is documented. Colonial and archaeological discussions of Mapuche war captives, decapitation, trophy heads, and ritual killing concern a much broader historical complex. The 2018 *Katulongkon* study argues that flying-head narratives, trophy heads, ancestral skulls, and *longko* symbolism all participate in a conception of the head as a container of identity or vital force. That is an interpretive cultural relationship, not proof that war captives were sacrificed “to Chonchón.”

Likewise, there is no documented temple prostitution, cult militia, or political office attached to the being.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before and during early colonial documentation

No pre-Columbian inscription, image, or securely dated artifact names the Chonchón. Mapuche tradition was oral, and Spanish chroniclers selectively recorded practices through Christian categories such as “demon,” “witch,” and “idolatry.” Rosales documents apparent animal transformations by sorcerers; this is relevant background, but not the complete Chonchón. **[EARLY CONTEXT]**

### Eighteenth century

Olivares names *chonchón* as an owl-like nocturnal bird. Gómez de Vidaurre records witches transforming into night-birds and shooting invisible projectiles. The two motifs coexist in the historical record but are not yet explicitly joined into the named flying head. **[EARLY CONTEXT]**

### Nineteenth century

The colonial histories were printed, missionary dictionaries republished, and Chilean lexicography began treating *chonchón* as a vernacular bird or popular term. Mapuche territory was simultaneously subjected to military incorporation, settlement, missionization, land loss, and coercive reorganization in Chile and Argentina. These processes altered the settings in which oral knowledge was transmitted and in which collectors obtained it.

No evidence supports a formal governmental “suppression of Chonchón worship,” because no such cult is documented. Christian missions and state assimilation instead stigmatized Mapuche religious specialists and translated Indigenous harmful agencies into witchcraft and demonology.

### 1904–1916: documentary consolidation

Ferrer (1904) provides the first located explicit flying-head-and-wing-ears description. Guevara (1908, 1911) supplies narrative, illustration, Mapuche phrases, and social applications. Cavada (1914) documents the neighboring Chilote witchcraft complex; Vicuña Cifuentes (1915) organizes Chonchón lore within Chilean folklore; Augusta (1916) gives the restrained lexical gloss “large nocturnal bird.”

This juxtaposition shows why no single definition is sufficient: at virtually the same moment the term meant bird, flying head, female sorcerer, omen, and metaphor for a prying person.

### Mid-twentieth century

Latcham, Moesbach, Koessler-Ilg, folklorists, and regional collectors expanded the record. The figure spread in print as a “Mapuche myth” and a Chilean national legend. Chilote material was sometimes indiscriminately merged with continental Mapuche material; Argentine versions demonstrated that the tradition also crossed the Andes into Neuquén and Río Negro.

### Late twentieth century

Faron, Grebe, Foerster, Bacigalupo, and others developed more relational accounts of Mapuche religion, healing, and harmful power. Waag classified the Chonchón within studies of *wekufü*. Carrasco and colleagues treated the narrative as a structured symbolic complex rather than a zoological belief. Folklorists such as Oreste Plath popularized the Tué-tué as a national legend.

### Twenty-first century

The Chonchón remains present in Mapuche and rural Chilean oral testimony, schools, folklore festivals, internet horror, podcasts, illustration, games, and film. Continuity is real at the level of story and reported belief, but its forms are historically layered. Contemporary narrators may retain the *kalku*, replace it with the generic Spanish *brujo*, invoke the Devil or Christian protections, or treat the creature as an entertainment monster.

There is no reliable evidence for an organized modern Chonchón cult, reconstructionist denomination, or sizeable neopagan worship community.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and folklore publishing

Guevara and Vicuña Cifuentes transformed locally collected materials into a print canon. Plath’s *Geografía del mito y la leyenda chilena* and *El lenguaje de los pájaros chilenos* further nationalized the figure. School anthologies commonly retell it as an uncomplicated “Mapuche legend,” often blending sources and omitting the differences between bird, sorcerer, and detached head.

### Film

*La Ñecla y el Chonchón* (2014) is a Chilean film built around a boy, his absent mother, and the world of Chilean kites; its title plays on the double Chilean use of *chonchón*, including a kind of simple paper kite. It is a modern artistic work, not an ethnographic transmission ([Cinechile catalogue](https://cinechile.cl/pelicula/la-necla-y-el-chonchon/)). **[MODERN REINVENTION]**

### Popular horror and games

Contemporary horror illustration standardizes the Chonchón as a grotesque severed head with giant ears, feathers, talons, and glowing eyes. That visual synthesis is indebted to early descriptions but usually more anatomically elaborate than Guevara’s image or Ferrer’s sentence.

Games and bestiaries frequently turn it into a combatable flying “demon” or vampire species. No early source supports fixed statistics, elemental affinities, ranks, summoning procedures, or populations of biologically reproducing Chonchones. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

### Occult and neopagan claims

The dedicated search found no evidence that Renaissance hermetists, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or classical European grimoires knew the Chonchón. Claims attaching the Seal of Solomon, Saint Cyprian, “astral projection,” or universal demonological taxonomies to it are later syncretistic interpretations.

The phrase “astral projection” is particularly anachronistic when applied to early Mapuche tradition. In the documented *tripanlongko* conception, the traveling agency remains materially embodied in the head, rather than becoming the disembodied subtle body of modern Western occultism.

No well-documented Chonchón-centered neopagan movement was located. Individual contemporary spiritual or artistic appropriations may exist, but their scale and continuity cannot presently be established.

### Political and nationalist appropriation

The figure has been incorporated into national Chilean folklore, sometimes stripped of Mapuche language, history, and colonial context. Conversely, modern Indigenous cultural projects may reclaim Mapuche stories from generic “Chilean mythology.” Neither process proves a single original form.

No sustained extremist appropriation specifically centered on Chonchón was located.

## 9. Historiography and contested questions

### Is the Chonchón originally an owl or a flying head?

The evidence supports competing possibilities rather than a definitive solution.

- **Bird-first hypothesis:** Olivares and Augusta define *chonchón/choñchoñ* as a nocturnal bird; ominous owl traditions could have acquired a witchcraft explanation.
- **Flying-head tradition independently old:** the broad Andean distribution of animated or flying heads suggests that the motif may long predate its twentieth-century documentation among Mapuche communities.
- **Convergence hypothesis:** an older flying-head/sorcery complex and an actual owl-name converged, with the bird’s cry generating *tué-tué*.

The third is a reasonable scholarly model, but remains reconstruction rather than direct proof.

### How old is the myth?

Carrasco and colleagues call it one of the oldest Mapuche myths on grounds of diffusion and recurrence. That is an inference from distribution, not a securely dated pre-contact attestation. The first explicit documentary description located is 1904. “Ancient” should therefore mean “argued to preserve an old oral complex,” not “recorded in a pre-Columbian text.”

### Mapuche or pan-Andean?

Flying heads occur widely in Andean traditions: Quechua *uma-waqya*, *umaphawa*, *uma puriq/purej*, *uman tak-tak*, and Aymara *khate-khate*. González Holguín’s early colonial Quechua dictionary records *humapurik*, witches encountered at night in the form of a human head. Comparative scholars such as Morote and Ansión therefore view flying-head lore as pan-Andean or at least widely distributed.

Similarity does not establish direct borrowing into Mapuche tradition. The 2018 study argues for a broad Andean-Amazonian co-tradition involving heads as containers of vital force. That is stronger than treating the Chonchón as an isolated invention, but weaker than demonstrating one traceable line of descent. **[SCHOLARLY DEBATE]**

### Is it a *wekufü*?

Some later authors describe it as a *wekufü*. Other evidence makes it the form of a human *kalku*. These are not identical propositions. A *kalku* may work with harmful agencies, but the flying head remains the sorcerer’s own vulnerable embodiment in major narratives. “A demon servant of the kalku” is therefore one systematization, not the uncontested original definition.

### Gender

Guevara’s major descriptions make the Chonchón a woman’s head, and discovery-by-husband tales are gendered accordingly. Other sources speak generically of male or female sorcerers. The universal claim that only women become Chonchones is false; the equally universal claim that gender never matters erases Guevara’s material.

### Chonchón and Tué-tué

They are commonly synonymous in twentieth- and twenty-first-century folklore, but the names highlight different strata: *choñchoñ* is also a bird-name, whereas *tué-tué* names the cry. Some local traditions distinguish the unseen omen-bird from the anatomically explicit flying head. National folklore collections tend to collapse them.

### Chonchón and the Chilote Voladora

They share night flight, witchcraft, bird transformation, and the danger of unfulfilled promises. Cavada compares them but does not make them one being. The Voladora’s removal of her intestines and work as a messenger are her distinctive features. Their total identification is a later flattening of Chilote lore.

### Contested-material findings

- **Etymology:** disputed; no single derivation is demonstrated.
- **Origin:** Mapuche attestation is strong, but a pan-Andean flying-head background is plausible.
- **Syncretism:** Christian demonology, Solomon, Cyprian, crosses, and the Salamanca complex are demonstrably layered onto Indigenous and rural traditions.
- **Human sacrifice:** none documented to Chonchón.
- **Suppression:** no specific suppression of a Chonchón cult; broader repression and missionization of Mapuche religion are historically documented.
- **Occultism:** no premodern European occult tradition concerning Chonchón; modern occult terminology is retrospective.
- **Neopaganism:** no organized Chonchón worship established.
- **Extremism:** none established.
- **“Ancient images”:** none authenticated. Guevara’s 1908 illustration is the earliest securely located depiction.
- **Fixed transformation recipes:** modern retellings present recipes and ointments more confidently than the primary ethnographic record permits.
- **Taxonomic claims such as “vampire demon”:** modern classificatory shorthand, not traditional consensus.

## 11. Sources and source criticism

The strongest direct sources are Ferrer (1904), Guevara (1908, 1911), Augusta (1916), Vicuña Cifuentes (1915), and later recorded oral narratives. Colonial authors are indispensable for antecedent transformation beliefs but should not be quoted as if they had already described the complete flying-head figure.

The principal methodological limitations are:

1. oral traditions were documented late;
2. collectors frequently translated Mapuche categories into “witch,” “demon,” and “superstition”;
3. Mapuche, Huilliche, Chilote, central-Chilean, and Argentine variants were often merged;
4. dictionaries record bird-names without necessarily recording associated narratives;
5. modern popular accounts copy one another and rarely identify their informants;
6. broad works on Mapuche religion establish context but do not always discuss Chonchón directly;
7. survival of a motif does not prove every modern detail is ancient.

Ercilla, Núñez de Pineda, Rosales, Febrés, Havestadt, Lenz, Moesbach, Faron, Grebe, Foerster, Bacigalupo, and Dillehay are essential controls on the surrounding culture and its historiography. Their prestige should not be used to attribute Chonchón claims they do not actually make. The most defensible reconstruction is therefore narrow at its chronological base and increasingly ramified after 1904: an owl-like name and older sorcerous-transformation background converge in the documented winged human head, which then accumulates the Tué-tué cry, death-omen narratives, Chilote comparisons, Christian counter-magic, national folklore, and modern horror imagery.
