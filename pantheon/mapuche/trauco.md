# The Trauco: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest documentary record, a **Chilote folkloric being or “mythological being,” a deformed supernatural forest man and harmful sorcerous presence**; in later literature, variously a *duende*, monster, demon, satyr-like sexual aggressor, incubus, forest spirit, or *wekufü*. He is **not securely attested as a god receiving worship**, nor as a member of a formally organized Mapuche pantheon.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Alejandro Cañas Pinochet’s *Estudios de la lengua veliche*, communicated in the context of the Fourth Scientific Congress at Santiago in 1908–09 and printed in 1910/1911. Cañas describes a being called **Trauco** in Chiloé, about a vara high, inhabiting caves and attacking or deforming healthy children. The first full surviving ethnographic dossier is Francisco J. Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914), pp. 102–105 of the modern Museo Regional de Ancud edition.  
> **Critical qualification:** No Trauco has been located in the major sixteenth- through nineteenth-century sources named in the brief—Ercilla, Núñez de Pineda, Rosales, Febrés, Havestadt, Augusta, or Lenz’s principal Araucanian texts. Their silence does not prove that no oral antecedent existed, but it prevents securely projecting the documented Trauco back into pre-conquest Mapuche religion or treating him as an ancient pan-Mapuche deity.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

The Trauco is most securely a being of the oral culture of the Chiloé archipelago, where Williche-Mapuche, Chono and Spanish colonial populations and traditions interacted. The documentary Trauco begins not as a divinity with temples, prayers and genealogy, but as a small, deformed and dangerous male being associated with caves, old forest, trees, bodily deformation, illness, frightening noises and attacks on women or children.

The now-standard popular portrait—a man under one metre tall, clothed in quilineja vines, with feet ending in stumps, carrying a crooked staff and sometimes a stone axe, whose gaze twists or kills men and whose sexual power overwhelms unmarried women—is composite. Its securely traceable nucleus appears in Cañas (1910/11), Cavada (1914) and Vicuña Cifuentes (1915). Further narrative particulars, including an increasingly choreographed seduction scene and the stone axe as a constant identifying object, were consolidated by twentieth-century collectors, especially Evaristo Molina, Nicasio Tangol, Bernardo Quintana, Narciso García Barría and Renato Cárdenas.

### Recorded forms

- **Trauco** — the usual modern Spanish spelling.
- **Thrauco** — Cavada’s spelling, reflecting an older Chilean transcription convention for a Mapuche-like dental or retroflex consonant. **[Secure early attestation: 1914]**
- **Trauko / Trawko** — modern orthographic adaptations intended to look or function more like Mapudungun spelling. **[Modern learned usage]**
- **Chauco / Chawko** — regional or modern Williche-oriented form. Some contemporary interpreters analyze it as *chaw* “father” + *ko* “water,” “father of the water.” David Núñez reports this interpretation but rejects its equation with an *ngen’ko*, observing that collected Trauco narratives make the being unable or unwilling to cross water. **[Contested modern reconstruction]**
- **Fiura** — Cavada recorded this as one of several insulting substitute names for the Trauco. Later collections ordinarily differentiate **the Fiura** as a female counterpart, wife, or occasionally daughter-wife. The historical shift from alias to distinct being must not be concealed. **[Early alias; later separate character]**
- **Thrauca / Trauca** — Cavada’s female counterpart. **[Secure early attestation: 1914]**
- **Huella / huelli** — Cavada gives *huella* for the Thrauca and *huelli* among the Trauco’s substitute names. **[Secure early regional vocabulary]**
- **Pompón del monte** — Cavada’s insulting or apotropaic circumlocution. **[Secure early regional vocabulary]**
- **Ruende, Cusme** — names appearing in later compilations, but not established in the earliest descriptions consulted. **[Later folklore/systematization]**
- **“Hijo del Trauco”** — not an epithet of the being but a social expression for a child whose paternity was unknown or concealed. Its circulation is well documented in twentieth- and twenty-first-century Chilean culture. **[Folklore/social usage]**

### Etymologies

No proposed etymology is secure.

1. **Indigenous derivation generally.** The spelling and Chiloé setting make an indigenous or indigenized form plausible, but neither Febrés’s 1765 dictionary nor Augusta’s early twentieth-century Mapudungun lexicography supplies a demonstrated source word for the being. **[Unestablished]**

2. **From *traw-/trau-* “join, gather.”** Narciso García Barría surveyed a proposed Williche root meaning “to join” or “bring together.” This may be retrospective folk etymology; no early text derives the name this way. **[Modern speculation]**

3. **From *trau-trau*, a plant name.** García Barría also considered the name of a myrtaceous shrub. Phonetic resemblance alone does not establish derivation. **[Modern speculation]**

4. **From Chono *chau*, “small.”** Also reported by García Barría. The proposal is attractive because of the being’s size, but the documentation of Chono is extremely limited and no historical derivational chain has been demonstrated. **[Modern speculation]**

5. ***Chaw-ko*, “father-water.”** Promoted in some contemporary Williche reinterpretations as evidence that the figure was originally an *ngen’ko*. Núñez argues that the narrative evidence contradicts this because water functions as a barrier to the Trauco. **[Contested modern Indigenous reconstruction]**

6. **European *duende*, faun, satyr or incubus ancestry.** Cavada himself compared the Trauco with the northern Chilean *duende* and classical fauns and satyrs. Such comparison demonstrates how Cavada interpreted the figure, not that the Trauco historically descends from Greece or Rome. Sonia Montecino’s more cautious hybrid model—Williche forest-being plus Iberian *duende* traditions—fits Chiloé’s colonial cultural setting but remains historical reconstruction rather than proof of a single origin. **[Modern comparative reconstruction]**

### Classification through time

- **Cañas, 1910/11:** a terrifying, cave-dwelling supernatural aggressor or monster.  
- **Cavada, 1914:** *mito*, monster and analogue of the *duende*, faun, satyr and basilisk.  
- **Vicuña Cifuentes, 1915:** a *brujo* in some islands, a deformed satyr-like being in Castro, and elsewhere a monster also called Fiura.  
- **Mid-century folklore:** a Chilote “myth,” monster or forest *duende*.  
- **Late twentieth-century popular systematization:** an incubus-like “spirit of fecund love,” seducer and father of natural children.  
- **Recent Williche-oriented interpretation:** possibly a hostile *wekufü* or *wedake newen*, rather than an *ngen* or benevolent owner-spirit.  
- **Tourist culture:** a standardized “mythological character” or mascot of Chiloé.

Calling the Trauco a “Mapuche god” therefore overstates the evidence. “Chilote forest being with Williche-Mapuche and European folkloric elements” is the most defensible classification.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Colonial and early republican silence

The following works are essential controls because they document Araucanian/Mapuche religion or language but do not provide a secure Trauco attestation:

- Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana* (1569, 1578, 1589): epic poetry, not systematic ethnography; no Trauco.
- Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz* (written 1673): extensive colonial account of Mapuche life; no identified Trauco.
- Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del Reino de Chile* (seventeenth-century composition): abundant religious and ethnographic notices; no identified Trauco.
- Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* (1765): no lexical entry securely naming the being.
- Bernhard Havestadt, *Chilidúgú* (1777): no secure Trauco.
- Rodolfo Lenz, *Estudios araucanos* (1895–97) and *Diccionario etimológico de las voces chilenas derivadas de lenguas indígenas americanas* (1905–10): crucial for Mapuche language and narratives, but not the first description of a Trauco tradition.
- Félix de Augusta, *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) and *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916): no secure evidence for the developed Chilote figure.
- Pascual Coña/Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach, *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos* (1930): no basis for making the Trauco part of Coña’s continental Mapuche cosmology.

This pattern matters: the evidence does not support an uninterrupted written history from pre-conquest Mapuche religion to the tourist-era Trauco.

### Cañas Pinochet: the earliest secure record

Cañas’s *Estudios de la lengua veliche* was associated with the Fourth Scientific Congress of 1908–09 and appeared in print in 1910/1911. The volume mixes linguistic materials, comparisons and ethnographic observations; modern scholarship has criticized some of Cañas’s linguistic reconstruction and translation practices.

His Trauco is:

- about one vara tall;
- a terror to mothers and children;
- resident in caves;
- moving by jumps;
- hunting healthy children;
- breaking limbs, spine and ribs;
- disfiguring the face and cutting off ears before abandoning the victim.

This earliest recoverable description is strikingly different from the now-dominant romanticized “irresistible lover.” It emphasizes child attack and mutilation, not fecundity. **[Secure earliest material]**

### Cavada, 1914

Francisco J. Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes*, based on information he said he gathered patiently among Chilote rural people, is the first extensive documentary account. His “El Thrauco” occupies pp. 102–105 in the Museo Regional de Ancud edition.

Cavada records:

- comparison with the *duende*, but greater perversity;
- residence in trunks and sometimes treetops;
- conical hat and clothing of quilineja;
- feet without heels or toes, ending in shapeless stumps;
- a gaze that kills or grotesquely deforms, while the Trauco dies if a human sees him first;
- aliases or insulting names;
- erotic dreams in which he appears as a handsome young man or clergyman;
- a female Thrauca;
- assault on maidens in the forest;
- pursuit of a woman into the sea, where he can drown;
- noises like stampeding animals, tree-felling or mocking repetitions of workers’ calls and axe blows;
- excrement as a dangerous substance;
- deformity, paralysis, torticollis, weakness and death among his harms;
- fourteen named countermeasures.

Cavada’s account is indispensable but not neutral. His Latin aside asks whether “cunning girls” invent nocturnal violation to escape parental anger, and he calls the belief conducive to vice. That is Cavada’s early twentieth-century clerical-moral judgment, not an indigenous explanation.

The **crooked pahueldún** is securely in Cavada, but as a thick, twisted stick found in the forest and sympathetically linked to the Trauco: blows to the stick hurt the being. Cavada does **not** make a carried stone axe the constant visual emblem familiar today. He says the being produces or imitates the sounds of an axeman. **[Secure early staff; later standardized axe]**

### Vicuña Cifuentes, 1915

Julio Vicuña Cifuentes’s *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* gives explicitly localized reports:

- **Valdivia:** an informant thought the Trauco was a *brujo* but could not define him.
- **Castro:** a deformed, satyr-like being whose lust was invoked when mothers warned daughters and when women explained an otherwise visible “novelty,” meaning pregnancy.
- **Caguach, Apiao, Alao and Chaulinec:** a *brujo* who could twist or hunch children and adults; twelve piles of fine beach sand distracted him through compulsive counting.
- A cited version described him as a small, deformed man clad in fibrous plants.
- Another version treated **Fiura** as a name for the same monster.

Vicuña explicitly admitted that he had obtained no further oral information. His candour shows how fragmentary and geographically variable the record remained in 1915.

### Later corpus

- **Evaristo Molina Herrera, “Mitología chilota” (1950):** a major mid-century synthesis that helped nationalize the Chiloé corpus. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Carlos Munizaga, “Mito y pequeñas comunidades rurales: el Trauco en Chiloé” (1964):** sociological/anthropological treatment of belief in rural communities. **[Modern scholarship]**
- **Nicasio Tangol, *Chiloé: mitos y leyendas* (1972):** popular-national folklore presentation. **[Later collection]**
- **Bernardo Quintana Mansilla, *Chiloé mitológico* (first associated with the early 1970s; later editions including 1987):** the most influential full narrative rendering. It calls the Trauco a spirit of fecund love, specifies approximately eighty centimetres in height, the stone axe, the pahueldún, his watch from a tique tree, three blows, magnetic fascination and the pregnancy sequence. **[Late literary-folkloric systematization]**
- **Narciso García Barría, *Tesoro mitológico del archipiélago de Chiloé* (1985):** comparative compilation and etymological speculation. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Renato Cárdenas and Catherine Hall, *Chiloé: manual del pensamiento mágico y la creencia popular* (1985), and Cárdenas, *El libro de la mitología de Chiloé* (1998):** important later oral-history syntheses. **[Late folklore collection]**
- **David Núñez, “El sustrato indígena de los seres mitológicos de Chiloé” (2021/22):** recent Mapuche-Williche comparative reinterpretation based on museum collections, literature and contemporary testimony. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

Koessler-Ilg’s Argentine *Cuentan los araucanos*, Faron’s *Hawks of the Sun*, Grebe, Foerster, Bacigalupo and Dillehay are valuable for broader Mapuche religion and ontology. None, however, supplies evidence that the documented Chilote Trauco possessed a pan-Mapuche cult, canonical genealogy or ancient institutional office.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

The Trauco’s most durable domain is the **old, dark and humid forest close to human settlement**. His functions vary:

- attacker and mutilator of children in Cañas;
- forest monster, disease-agent and sexual aggressor in Cavada;
- witch-like producer of deformity in the eastern islands;
- explanation, idiom or socially available narrative for nonmarital pregnancy;
- hostile forest force in contemporary Williche interpretation;
- emblem of Chilote regional identity and tourist folklore.

He is not securely the “god of fertility,” “god of the forest,” or “father of water.” Those are modern interpretive elevations.

### Appearance

**Secure early composite, 1910/11–1915:**

- male and anthropomorphic;
- very short, approximately a vara or less;
- deformed or repellent;
- legs or feet defective, sometimes stump-like;
- resident in caves, trunks or treetops;
- clothed in quilineja in Cavada and related collections;
- dangerous gaze;
- moves, in one early version, by jumping.

**Later standard image:**

- sixty to ninety centimetres tall;
- oversized head and coarse features;
- conical quilineja hat and coat;
- no feet, only stumps;
- exaggerated virility;
- crooked wooden staff in the presence of women;
- magical stone axe used to fell a tree in one or three blows.

The precise “eighty centimetres,” the alternating staff and axe, the tique-tree lookout and the elaborately described seductive gaze belong principally to Quintana’s influential narration, not to a demonstrably ancient iconographic canon.

### Powers and limitations

**Powers:**

- crippling or killing gaze;
- twisting necks, causing humps, facial paralysis, dislocation and wasting illness;
- producing erotic dreams;
- appearing in dreams as a handsome youth or priest;
- sexual fascination or loss of the victim’s volition;
- mimicking voices, cries and axe blows;
- creating deafening noises;
- spoiling charcoal;
- contaminating objects or places through footprints, gaze, breath or excrement;
- sympathetic connection with the pahueldún;
- in late versions, cutting trees with a few blows of a stone axe.

**Limitations and countermeasures:**

- if a human sees him before he sees the human, he may die;
- he can drown pursuing a victim;
- recent accounts say that he cannot cross water;
- insulting substitute names drive him away, while speaking his proper name summons him;
- garlic repels him;
- crosses made with two knives repel him;
- ash or seaweed placed at the house corners protects it;
- axe blows to the corners protect the dwelling;
- whistling through kelp repels him;
- recounting the dream breaks its force;
- smoke treats a person struck by his gaze;
- dragging and beating a pahueldún injures him;
- burning his excrement counteracts him;
- a handful or twelve piles of sand compel him to count;
- in Cavada, capturing and hanging him over the hearth turns him into a stick exuding curative oil.

These practices are household magic or defensive folklore, not evidence of worship.

### Material representations

No securely pre-Columbian image has been identified as the Trauco. Small anthropomorphic archaeological figures cannot be assigned to him merely because they are small, deformed or sexually explicit.

The earliest documented visual canon is therefore modern. In the 1970s, CORFO, museum personnel and Chiloé craftspeople produced stone and woven figures for a “mythological map” and museum display. Interviews recorded in *La fijación del mito en Chiloé* show that artisans initially disagreed about what the Trauco should look like. A commissioner reportedly supplied Bernardo Quintana’s book after an artisan’s first figure did not match the expected image. This is unusually direct evidence that print and cultural administration **fixed** a formerly variable oral being into a standard visual type.

The Museo Regional de Ancud now preserves anthropomorphic and partly zoomorphic Trauco sculptures and cestería figures. These are important evidence for twentieth-century heritage production, not ancient cult images.

No secure traditional colors, sacred number, weekday, celestial body, formal emblem or sacred animal are attested. The recurring natural substances are quilineja, forest wood, stone, sand, garlic, smoke, ash, kelp and water.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

The earliest sources supply no parents, birth, siblings or offspring.

- **Thrauca:** Cavada’s female counterpart, also called *huella*. He records only an obscure sexual dream interpretation concerning her. **[Secure early counterpart]**
- **Fiura:** Cavada treats *fiura* partly as a name or insult for the Trauco; Vicuña also records a version in which they are the same monster. Later collectors distinguish Fiura as his wife or female equivalent. **[Early identity; later differentiation]**
- **La Condená:** late systematized genealogies make the Fiura the daughter of La Condená and the Trauco, sometimes also the Trauco’s wife. This incestuous genealogy is not present in the early Cañas–Cavada–Vicuña record. **[Late accretion]**
- **Human children:** the belief that unexplained children were “children of the Trauco” is early twentieth-century folklore. Quintana narrativized it as literal supernatural paternity. Núñez reports that detailed case narratives rarely produce a child and that one described offspring was half human and half rotten trunk. **[Folklore with variant literalization]**

### Every documented interaction with another named supernatural being

- **The Thrauca / Trauca** — Female counterpart: a man dreaming of her is assigned a sexual omen in Cavada. *Source:* Cavada, *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914), p. 103. **[Secure early attestation]**
- **Fiura** — Cavada lists *fiura* as an alternative or insulting name for the Trauco; Vicuña records identity between them. *Sources:* Cavada (1914); Vicuña Cifuentes (1915). **[Secure early identity variant]**
- **Fiura** — Later literature makes her his wife or female partner, sharing forest habitat and powers of twisting or sickening through gaze or breath. *Sources:* Tangol (1972), Quintana (1970s/1987), Cárdenas (1998). **[Later canonical Chilote systematization]**
- **Fiura** — Some later genealogies make her simultaneously his daughter and wife. *Source tradition:* late twentieth-century popular compilations ultimately associated with Quintana/Plath-derived accounts. **[Late accretion; absent from earliest sources]**
- **La Condená** — Later genealogy says the Trauco couples with La Condená and fathers Fiura. *Source tradition:* late twentieth-century popular Chilote mythology. **[Late accretion]**
- **The Christian Devil/Satan** — Some later Christianized tellings call the Trauco an offspring or servant of Satan. No such genealogy occurs in the earliest documentation. *Source:* later popular folklore repertories. **[Christianized late accretion]**
- **Basilisk** — Cavada compares, but does not narrate a meeting between, the Trauco and the basilisk because both kill through sight and die if seen first. *Source:* Cavada (1914). **[Learned comparison, not interaction]**
- **Fauns and satyrs** — Cavada compares their forest dwelling and lust. No narrative encounter or historical syncretistic cult identification is documented. *Source:* Cavada (1914). **[Learned comparativism, not interaction]**
- **Duende** — Cavada calls the Trauco analogous to the northern *duende*. Again, this is classification, not an episode between two beings. **[Learned comparison]**
- **Ngen-mawida / forest owner-spirit** — Modern comparative interpretation sometimes treats the Trauco as a transformed Williche forest *ngen*. No story records a meeting between the two as separately named beings. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Ngen’ko** — Some contemporary etymology identifies Chawko as “father of water”; Núñez rejects the equation. No discrete interaction exists. **[Contested modern identification]**
- **Wekufü** — A class rather than an individual. Núñez interprets Trauco and Fiura as hostile forces belonging with *wedake newen* or *wekufü*, supported by disease concepts and defensive use of steel knives. **[Modern Indigenous-comparative classification]**

There are no securely documented meetings, battles or alliances between the Trauco and Caleuche, Pincoya, Camahueto, Invunche, Tentén Vilu or Caicai Vilu. Their joint appearance in modern lists and tourist displays is not a mythic interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

The Trauco has no ancient epic cycle. His dossier consists of encounter narratives, warnings, illness explanations and increasingly literary set pieces.

### A. The mutilator of children

**Earliest form:** Cañas, 1910/11.

The cave-dwelling dwarf jumps in search of healthy children. If he catches one, he breaks limbs, spine and ribs, disfigures the face, cuts the ears and leaves the child in a deserted place.

This is the earliest secure narrative profile and therefore cannot be dismissed merely because later summaries emphasize sexuality. It may overlap functionally with other Chilote explanations for deformity and childhood illness, but Cañas identifies the aggressor specifically as Trauco.

**Tier:** securely attested early folklore.

### B. The fatal or deforming encounter

**Full early form:** Cavada, 1914.

The Trauco inhabits trees. If he sees a person before being noticed, his gaze kills or leaves the victim grotesquely twisted and expected to die within a year. If the human sees him first, the danger reverses and the Trauco dies. His presence is also recognized through terrifying movement, imitated voices and axe sounds, damaged charcoal or dangerous excrement.

Vicuña’s eastern-island material emphasizes twisting and hunching both children and adults. Later traditions transfer a similar “crooked” illness to the Fiura’s breath, producing the diagnostic categories *aire de Fiura* and *aire de Trauco*.

**Tier:** securely attested early folklore; subsequently elaborated as a culture-bound illness complex.

### C. The erotic dream and forest assault

**Earliest full form:** Cavada, 1914.

Before arriving, the Trauco sends erotic dreams and may appear within them as a handsome young man or religious figure. He assaults maidens wandering in the forest. Cavada’s account contains both the belief and his skeptical, moralizing interpretation that women used it to escape parental anger.

**Castro variant:** Vicuña Cifuentes, 1915.

Mothers warn daughters sent away from home: “Beware of the Trauco.” When a pregnancy becomes impossible to hide, the young woman attributes it to him.

**Quintana’s expanded form:** twentieth century.

The Trauco watches from a tique tree, notices a desirable unmarried woman, descends, strikes the tree three times with his axe, confuses her, touches or blows upon her with the pahueldún, fixes her with a magnetic gaze and induces a dreamlike sexual surrender. She later awakens with disordered clothes and returns home. Pregnancy follows, but mother and child are supposedly spared social dishonour because no ordinary man could resist or equal the supernatural assailant.

This expanded scene is the best-known modern version, but it should be read as a literary-folkloric synthesis. The language of irresistible attraction can romanticize what the narrative itself depicts as incapacitation and sexual assault.

**Tier:** early attested sexual-danger tradition; later canonical elaboration.

### D. Pursuit across the water

Cavada says that the Trauco will throw himself into the sea while pursuing his victim and may drown like a new Leander before Hero. Recent testimony instead makes water a firm boundary: a family can escape an obsessively pursuing Trauco by moving across a watercourse, which he cannot cross.

The versions are compatible only at the broad level that water defeats him; one has him enter and die, the other makes crossing impossible.

**Tier:** early regional anecdote and modern regional variant.

### E. Capturing and reducing the Trauco

Cavada records that a captured Trauco may be hung over the hearth, where he becomes a stick that drips medicinal oil. The oil cures his victims. This is not a sacrifice or offering: it is hostile capture, transformation and therapeutic appropriation.

Later versions connect the same logic to finding and beating his pahueldún or burning his excrement. These are instances of contagious or sympathetic magic: the being’s object or residue transmits harm back to him.

**Tier:** securely attested early household magic.

### F. The half-human, half-rotten-tree child

Núñez reports a case tradition in which Trauco-induced pregnancy produces a being half human and half rotten trunk. He interprets the image through forest humus and decomposition, strengthening the case for a hostile old-forest entity rather than a generalized fertility god.

**Tier:** regional oral variant documented in modern scholarship.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No institutional cult

There is no evidence for:

- temples or shrines dedicated to the Trauco;
- a priesthood;
- festivals or a liturgical calendar;
- hymns or formal prayers;
- sacrifice to him;
- votive offerings;
- pilgrimage;
- theophoric personal names;
- institutional or royal patronage;
- an oracle;
- continuous pre-Christian worship.

“Cult of the Trauco” is consequently a misleading phrase unless used very loosely for belief and protective custom.

### Defensive and healing practice

What does exist is a substantial repertoire of apotropaic action:

- two knives arranged as a cross;
- steel knives carried for protection;
- garlic kept on the body or crushed in the hands;
- ash or sargassum at the four corners of a house;
- striking the house corners with an axe;
- whistling through kelp;
- smoke treatment;
- insulting the being with substitute names;
- publicly recounting an erotic dream;
- beating a presumed pahueldún;
- burning dangerous excrement;
- casting sand so the being must count it;
- placing twelve small piles of fine beach sand on a table;
- crossing water or moving the household beyond it;
- urinating in a cross, according to a 2019 Llanco-Ancud testimony.

These practices combine Catholic signs, household materials, forest knowledge and Mapuche-associated ideas about harmful power. Their existence does not imply a single coherent theology.

### Healing specialists

The *aire de Fiura* or *aire de Trauco* has been treated by machi or other local specialists. A physician’s reminiscence concerning the healer Machi Teca of Compu around 1932 describes treatment of *aire de Fiura* using *contra*. Modern health research lists bodily twisting and “bad air” syndromes while distinguishing indigenous explanations from biomedical diagnoses.

This is the strongest connection between the Trauco complex and Mapuche ritual medicine. It remains treatment of harmful contact, not veneration of the being.

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

The dedicated search found **no credible evidence of human or animal sacrifice to the Trauco**. Cavada’s capture-and-burning practices concern the supernatural being or its excrement, not a human victim. Claims of ancient sacrificial worship should be classified as unsupported modern invention unless new primary evidence is produced.

No documented “temple prostitution,” political priesthood, formal cult suppression or extremist appropriation is associated with the Trauco.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1900: possible oral antecedents, no secure named record

Chiloé underwent prolonged interaction among Williche-Mapuche, Chono and Spanish populations, followed by Catholic evangelization and the retreat of insular Mapudungun. This provides a credible setting for hybrid folklore, but it is not a date for the origin of the Trauco.

The absence of the name from colonial chronicles and missionary dictionaries prevents claims that a fully formed Trauco was a pre-conquest deity.

### 1908–1915: entry into print

Cañas records a child-mutilating cave dwarf. Cavada produces the first large dossier, adding forest habitat, quilineja clothing, stump feet, gaze, sexuality, sounds, excrement and counter-magic. Vicuña demonstrates regional variance across Castro, Valdivia and the eastern islands.

This period supplies the earliest secure core:

- small deformed male being;
- Chiloé localization;
- harmful effects on bodies;
- caves, forests or trees;
- *brujo/duende/satyr* classifications;
- danger to women and children;
- sexual narrative;
- domestic countermeasures.

### 1915–1960s: folklorization and comparative classification

Collectors increasingly placed the Trauco in a bounded “Chilote mythology” beside Caleuche, Pincoya, Invunche and other beings. Classical comparison—satyr, faun, basilisk—made him intelligible to national readers but risked replacing local categories with European ones.

Molina’s 1950 synthesis and Munizaga’s 1964 community study helped move the figure from scattered testimony into Chilean folklore scholarship. The unfinished *Enciclopedia Chilena* recorded strong belief in Chiloé and weaker diffusion into Llanquihue, Osorno and Valdivia; it also claimed that court cases concerning rape had invoked the Trauco. That latter statement requires examination of the original case files before individual legal events can be reconstructed.

### 1970s–1990s: narrative and visual canonization

Tangol, Quintana, García Barría and Cárdenas expanded and organized the mythology. Quintana’s highly visual account became especially influential: eighty-centimetre body, tique-tree lookout, stone axe, three blows, magnetic gaze, pahueldún and socially absolving pregnancy.

At the same time, state and regional cultural projects commissioned physical figures and a “mythological map.” Museum research shows that artisans’ initial mental images did not necessarily match administrators’ expectations; printed books were used to standardize the desired representations. The modern image is therefore not simply an unchanged ancient icon handed down in wood or stone. It was materially stabilized through books, museums, CORFO projects and craft markets.

### 1990s–present: heritage, identity and tourism

The Trauco now appears on routes, in parks, museums, school readers, websites, sculpture, souvenirs and publicity. The Municipality of Ancud advertises a “Ruta del Trauco,” and Chile’s official tourism portal presents him as one of Chiloé’s most feared myths, complete with dwarf stature and stone axe.

Scholarship on contemporary Chiloé describes mythology as a language of cultural difference amid neoliberal modernization, aquaculture, migration and tourism. In this setting the Trauco simultaneously functions as:

- inherited local narrative;
- shorthand for Chiloé;
- marketable monster;
- environmental forest image;
- subject of ironic or skeptical speech;
- vehicle for discussing sexuality, paternity and abuse;
- object of Williche cultural reinterpretation.

Belief has not simply disappeared, but evidence differs by household and community. Contemporary testimony may speak of personal encounters or inherited family stories; commercial display does not measure literal belief.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Tourism and heritage

The “tourist Trauco” is visually consistent: short, ugly, quilineja-clad, carrying an axe and inhabiting picturesque forest. This version is easier to sculpt, illustrate and narrate than the diffuse early complex of dreams, childhood mutilation, excrement, disease and household counter-magic.

A 2009 study of cultural mutation argues that Chiloé’s myths now help mark regional difference while being incorporated into a commercialized island image. The official Chile Travel site in 2024 calls the Trauco Chiloé’s most feared myth and depicts the now-standard axe-bearing dwarf. Ancud’s municipal tourist route offers narrated mythological stories. **[Modern heritage formation]**

### Film and theatre

- **Hijo de Trauco** (Alan Fischer, released in Chile in 2014): a fourteen-year-old mocked as “the Trauco’s son” investigates the concealed truth of his paternity. The film uses the social meaning of supernatural paternity rather than merely reproducing a monster tale. **[Modern cinematic reinterpretation]**
- **Trauko: el encuentro de las aguas:** a recent musical reframes the name positively as “meeting of the waters” and uses the being as a metaphor for encounter and diversity. That etymology is artistically productive but philologically unproven. **[Modern invention/reinterpretation]**
- Educational adaptations and dramatic retellings regularly reproduce Quintana’s set-piece seduction.

### Literature, comics and popular culture

The Trauco appears in Chilean folklore anthologies, children’s readers, horror and fantasy. A webcomic, *El Hijo del Trauko*, recasts him within an action-fantasy category as a powerful *kurü*. The Chilean countercultural comics magazine *Trauko* used the name as a regional and rebellious cultural sign rather than as a source-critical mythological claim.

Modern internet summaries commonly call him an “incubus,” “goblin” or “gnome.” These are cross-cultural translations, not indigenous titles.

### Gender and sexual politics

The old formula that the Trauco “explained” unmarried pregnancy has several possible social uses:

- protecting a woman and child from public stigma;
- concealing consensual relations;
- disguising the identity of a socially forbidden partner;
- concealing rape or incest;
- expressing an experience understood within a supernatural worldview;
- disciplining girls by warning them away from forests or unsupervised travel.

No single function should be imposed on every case. Cavada’s blanket suspicion of women is itself historical evidence of gendered elite attitudes. Conversely, the modern claim that the myth was simply “invented to hide rape” is too reductive: Cañas’s earliest profile concerns mutilated children, and Núñez notes that detailed encounter narratives seldom culminate in pregnancy.

The responsible conclusion is that the pregnancy motif was real and socially consequential, but neither its origin nor every invocation can be reduced to one explanation.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no documented Trauco tradition in Renaissance magic, ceremonial Hermeticism, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, classical grimoires or established international neopagan organizations. Modern practitioners may individually adopt him, but no evidence located establishes a continuous cult or a substantial organized revival.

Claims that the Trauco is an ancient fertility god, a horned god, an elemental king or a traditional occult initiator are **modern inventions unless independently sourced**.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Indigenous substrate versus colonial hybrid

Three broad positions appear:

1. **Indigenous survival:** the Trauco is an altered Williche forest being or *ngen*. Its old-forest habitat, quilineja, disease effects, *trafentun/topantun*-like encounter illness and steel-knife defenses support indigenous structural connections.

2. **European transfer:** the figure descends primarily from the Iberian *duende*, incubus or wild man. Sexual pursuit, nocturnal dreams, compulsive counting and comparisons with fauns or satyrs support European parallels.

3. **Chilote synthesis:** indigenous forest ontology and European demonological or fairy motifs combined locally. This is the most cautious model, but the absence of early transition documents means the exact contribution of each stream cannot be measured.

Cavada’s comparisons establish early scholarly framing, not historical descent. Similarity is not genealogy.

### “Father of water” dispute

Contemporary Williche-oriented accounts derive *Chawko* from *chaw* + *ko* and classify him as an *ngen’ko*. Núñez rejects this because collected narratives place him in dark forest and make water a barrier. He tentatively situates Trauco and Fiura with hostile *wekufü/wedake newen* instead.

Both interpretations are modern reconstructions. No early native-language prayer, myth or lexical gloss settles the question.

### Trauco and Fiura

The source history contradicts a single timeless genealogy:

- Cavada: Fiura can be a substitute name or insult for Trauco; Thrauca is the female form.
- Vicuña: one version equates Trauco and Fiura.
- Later collectors: Fiura is an independent female being, usually his wife.
- Still later popular genealogy: she is his daughter-wife through La Condená.

The separate married pair now familiar in tourist mythology is therefore a historical development.

### Pregnancy versus disease

Popular summaries frequently make seduction and pregnancy the being’s essence. The chronology is more complicated:

- earliest Cañas account: attacks and mutilates children;
- Cavada: bodily illness and sexual assault both prominent;
- Vicuña: regional variation between childhood disease and pregnancy explanation;
- Quintana: seduction and supernatural paternity dominate;
- contemporary indigenous-focused research: harmful encounter illness and forest decomposition return to the foreground.

The “incubus” reading is genuine but not exhaustive.

### The stone axe

Cañas does not establish it. Cavada records axe-like sounds and household axe defenses, while the Trauco’s personal object is the twisted pahueldún. Quintana and later iconography make the small stone axe a defining possession and describe three magical blows.

Thus:

- **axe sound:** secure in 1914;
- **carried magical stone axe:** later canonical elaboration;
- **archaeological stone axes identified as Trauco objects:** unsupported.

### Quality of the early evidence

Cañas, Cavada and Vicuña were educated male collectors writing within evolutionary, Catholic and comparativist frameworks. Their texts preserve invaluable testimony but mediate it through Spanish transcription, moral judgment and European categories. Informants are often unnamed, and exact collection dates are seldom supplied.

Later compilations frequently repeat one another without making transmission visible. The museum study of visual “fixation” demonstrates how a printed synthesis can return to the community as if it were an immemorial standard.

### Evidence repositories

Important materials survive in:

- Biblioteca Nacional de Chile and Memoria Chilena;
- Museo Regional de Ancud and its cestería and stone collections;
- Biblioteca del Congreso Nacional’s unfinished *Enciclopedia Chilena*;
- university journal repositories including Universidad de Chile and SciELO;
- published oral-history collections.

The most urgent documentary task would be to locate and publish the original court files behind the *Enciclopedia Chilena* assertion that rape proceedings invoked the Trauco. Until that is done, the general assertion should not be expanded into invented individual cases.

### Final assessment by evidentiary tier

**Securely attested early material, 1910/11–1915:** a small, deformed male supernatural being of Chiloé; caves, forest, trunks or treetops; bodily mutilation and deformation; dangerous sight; sexual threat; erotic dreams; quilineja clothing; stump-like feet; frightening and imitative noises; dangerous excrement; household protections; a female Thrauca; locally variable identification as monster, *brujo*, *duende*-analogue or Fiura.

**Later canonical elaboration:** fixed height around eighty centimetres; stone axe as constant attribute; three blows against the tree; tique-tree lookout; alternation between axe and pahueldún; fully staged magnetic seduction; “spirit of fecund love”; standardized wife Fiura.

**Regional variants:** Trauco as child-mutilator; Trauco as *brujo*; Fiura as the same being, female equivalent or wife; inability to cross water versus drowning in pursuit; twelve piles of sand; half-human, half-rotten-tree offspring; *aire de Trauco*.

**Learned systematization:** organization of multiple local beings into a single “Chilote pantheon”; comparison with satyrs, fauns, incubi, basilisks and European *duendes*; genealogical charts involving Fiura and La Condená.

**Syncretistic or historical reconstruction:** mixture of Williche forest ontology with Iberian fairy, wild-man or incubus motifs. Plausible, but no source documents the moment of fusion.

**Modern reinvention:** “father of water,” ancient fertility god, tourist mascot, environmental emblem, theatrical symbol of diversity, action-comic *kurü*. These may be culturally meaningful but are not early attestations.

**Unsupported:** pre-Columbian temples, priesthood, formal cult, sacrifice, sacred calendar, canonical scripture, archaeological images securely identifiable as the Trauco, organized occult orders, or a demonstrable ancient pan-Mapuche god by this name.

The most defensible historical reconstruction is consequently not that an ancient god persisted unchanged. It is that an orally variable Chilote forest being entered print around 1910, was rapidly expanded through folklore scholarship, acquired a powerful sexual-paternity narrative, was given a standardized body and equipment by twentieth-century writers and heritage institutions, and now circulates simultaneously as living belief, regional identity, cautionary legend, discourse about sexual violence and paternity, museum object and tourist commodity.
