# Elal (Tehuelche): Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the securely documented Aónikenk tradition, Elal is principally a **male mythic culture hero, ancestral creator, protector, and world-orderer**. Some collectors call him a *dios-héroe* (“god-hero”) or “young god,” but the evidence does not establish a formal divine rank, priesthood, or organized cult comparable to those of temple-based religions.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Ramón Lista, *Los indios tehuelches: una raza que desaparece* (Buenos Aires, 1894), pp. 16–27 of the original edition. Lista attributed his account to the elder Aónikenk narrators Papón and Jatachuena. No earlier securely identified inscription, manuscript, image, or colonial text names Elal. [Lista’s complete 1894 edition](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/da/Los_indios_tehuelches_-_Ramon_Lista.pdf)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Lista’s 1894 record, the earliest presently secure written source.
- **[Collected oral variant]** A narrative documented from a named or localized Indigenous narrator after 1894.
- **[Later ethnographic synthesis]** A scholarly assembly or analysis of several oral records.
- **[Regional variant]** A version associated with another Aónikenk or broader “Tehuelche-complex” community.
- **[Mapuche-contact layer]** Material recorded in a context of Aónikenk–Mapuche contact or from “Araucanized Tehuelche” narrators.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** An analytical connection proposed by an ethnologist, linguist, or historian rather than stated by a narrator.
- **[Modern retelling]** Literary, educational, tourist, artistic, or commercial adaptation.
- **[Unsupported modern claim]** A claim circulated without a traceable primary or ethnographic source.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[Early attested]** Lista introduces Elal as the Tehuelches’ *mito heroico* and describes him in Spanish as *“el sér fuerte, sabio, benéfico, autor del Cosmos”*—“the strong, wise, beneficent being, author of the cosmos.” In Lista’s narrative he makes the Tehuelche people, clears the land of dangerous beings, discovers fire, invents weapons, provides clothing and shelter, and institutes morality. Lista alternates *Dios*, *Dios-héroe*, and *Héroe*, but these are the collector’s comparative categories, not demonstrated translations of a single Aónikenk theological title. He explicitly compares Elal to Hercules and Nóshtex to Saturn, comparisons which must not be mistaken for Indigenous equations. [Lista, pp. 16–27](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/da/Los_indios_tehuelches_-_Ramon_Lista.pdf)

**[Collected oral variant]** Luis Cuaterno/Má:ger of Camusu Aike, recorded by Ana Fernández Garay and Martine Delahaye before his death in 1985, called Elal *“nuestro jefe”* (“our chief”), *“el hacedor de las cosas”* (“maker of things”), the one who made the people long ago, and their protector. This is exceptionally important because the episode was published in Aónikenk with interlinear linguistic analysis, rather than only as a Spanish literary paraphrase. [Fernández Garay, “Un relato mítico tehuelche: Elal y el cóndor”](https://www.scribd.com/document/669008725/Dialnet-UnRelatoMiticoTehuelche-6914184)

**[Later ethnographic synthesis]** Bórmida and Siffredi’s corpus places Elal in a sequence of mythic epochs: primordial cosmic formation, an age of giants and animal-ancestors, and Elal’s ordering of the differentiated human world. “Culture hero” is therefore the safest comparative class. “Creator god” is only partly accurate: Elal creates or transforms human society and parts of the inhabitable world, but in later cosmogonic versions Kóoch already exists and creates the primordial cosmos.

### Recorded forms

- **El-lal** — Lista’s principal spelling in 1894.
- **Elal / E:lal / ’e:lal** — later Spanish and linguistic transcriptions.
- **ʔeːlal** — a modern phonological rendering frequently reproduced in reference works; it should not be projected backward as a historical Indigenous orthography.
- **El-tal** — apparently a typographical or OCR-corrupted form at the beginning of Lista’s discussion; the same pages consistently use *El-lal*.
- **Erral** — a secondary spelling in modern summaries, not a separately attested being.

Aónikenk was an oral language and had no pre-contact manuscript script. All spellings are scholarly or popular Latin-alphabet transcriptions.

### Etymology

**[Early scholarly speculation; discredited]** Lista associated *El-lal* with the Mesopotamian Ea and Semitic *Ilu, El, Elohim,* and *Allah*. This was impressionistic nineteenth-century comparative mythology. He supplied no phonological or historical transmission argument, and the comparison has no standing as a demonstrated etymology.

**[Collected Indigenous gloss]** Manuel Llarás Samitier reported in 1950 that southern Tehuelche narrators said the Swan named Elal and called the name at daybreak, while northern narrators glossed it as “the son” in the old language of the *Chónek*. This is evidence for an Indigenous interpretation, although it was recorded decades after Lista.

**[Modern linguistic proposal]** A modern argument connects *Elal* with reconstructed Chon forms for “son/child” and compares Selk’nam *yilal* and Mapudungun *yal* “son.” The resemblance is worth recording, but the proposed Proto-Chon derivation and especially attempts to derive Mapuche names of *gualicho* from it remain hypotheses, not consensus etymologies. The absence of a demonstrated sound history prevents treating “Elal means ‘the son’” as certain. [Discussion preserving the Llarás Samitier gloss](https://es.scribd.com/document/681050878/Elal-El-Heroe-Tehuelche-y-Otros-Nombres)

### Titles and descriptors

No fixed liturgical epithets are securely documented. The following are collectors’ descriptions or translations:

- *Dios-héroe* — “god-hero,” Lista, 1894.
- *Héroe* — “hero,” Lista, 1894.
- *Hacedor de las cosas* — “maker of things,” Luis Cuaterno’s account, published 1993.
- *Nuestro jefe* — “our chief,” Cuaterno.
- *Protector de nuestros paisanos* — protector of the Aónikenk people, Cuaterno.
- *Héroe cultural* — modern ethnological category used by Siffredi, Bórmida, Hernández, and others.
- “Creator of the Tehuelches” — recurrent ethnographic description, but not evidence that he created the primordial universe in every version.

### Tehuelche, Mapuche, Huilliche, and Chiloé: a necessary correction

**[Secure historical distinction]** Elal is an Aónikenk or southern Tehuelche figure, not a securely attested member of the Mapuche, Huilliche, or Chiloé pantheons. The Mapuche name *tehuelche* was applied broadly to Patagonian peoples; it should not be mistaken for proof that every “Tehuelche” tradition is Mapuche.

The named Mapuche sources—Ercilla’s *La Araucana*, Núñez de Pineda’s *Cautiverio feliz*, Rosales’s *Historia general*, the dictionaries of Febrés, Havestadt, and Augusta, Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos*, Pascual Coña’s testimony, Faron, Grebe, Foerster, Bacigalupo, and the Chiloé collectors—are indispensable for Mapuche religious history and contact, but they do not constitute an early corpus for Elal. Lenz’s work, for example, was assembled from Mapuche-language fieldwork of 1894–97 and belongs to a different linguistic archive. [Biblioteca Nacional de Chile on Lenz’s corpus](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-97582.html)

Rosales completed his Mapuche-centered history in 1674; it was not printed until 1877–78. Its age does not make it evidence for Elal when Elal is absent from the work’s documented supernatural nomenclature. [Biblioteca Nacional de Chile on Rosales](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-3356.html)

The same caution applies to Chiloé folklore. Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, Plath, and later collectors document figures such as the Caleuche, Pincoya, Trauco, and Camahueto, not an indigenous Chiloé cult of Elal. Modern Chilean children’s books may present Elal alongside Mapuche, Selk’nam, Yámana, or Chiloé stories as “Chilean Indigenous mythology”; that is editorial juxtaposition, not ancient pantheon membership.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Ramón Lista, 1894

**[Early attested]** Lista’s account occupies approximately pp. 16–27. Its evidentiary setting is unusually explicit: he says he heard the story from the elderly Papón and Jatachuena, who had themselves learned it as children early in the nineteenth century. That statement provides a claimed oral genealogy but does not create a written attestation earlier than 1894.

Lista records:

1. Elal as maker of the Tehuelches and civilizer of Patagonia.
2. Nóshtex killing Elal’s mother and cutting the fetus from her womb to eat it.
3. The rodent Térguerr saving and raising the infant underground.
4. Elal inventing bow and arrow.
5. The first appearance of fire.
6. The etiological punishment of the condor.
7. Conflict and temporary submission to Nóshtex.
8. Defeat of the child-eating giant Goshge by entering him as a horsefly.
9. Combat with Sintalk’n.
10. The deceptive substitution of a servant for the daughter of Sun and Moon.
11. Elal’s departure eastward over the sea on a swan, with islands rising where his arrows strike.

Lista’s language is not a verbatim Aónikenk transcription. It is polished Spanish prose, supplemented by his own classical and diffusionist commentary. His declaration that the version is *“rigurosamente exacta”* means that he claimed fidelity, not that modern source criticism can recover the exact Indigenous wording.

### Later core corpus

**[Collected oral variants]**

- **Manuel Llarás Samitier, 1950 and 1956:** “Primer ramillete de fábulas y sagas de los antiguos patagones.” His material includes Elal’s discovery of fire, the naming of Elal by Swan, and northern/southern interpretations of the name.
- **Alejandra Siffredi, fieldwork 1963–67; publications 1968 onward:** “El ciclo de Elal, héroe mítico de los aonik’enk” and “Algunos personajes de la mitología tehuelche meridional.” Siffredi compared several southern and neighboring versions and examined the courtship of Sun’s Daughter.
- **Marcelo Bórmida and Alejandra Siffredi, 1969–70:** “Mitología de los Tehuelches meridionales,” *Runa* 12, pp. 199–245. This is the largest foundational scholarly corpus, documenting numerous tellers and variants rather than a single “canonical” narrative.
- **Siffredi, “Hierofanías y concepciones mítico-religiosas de los Tehuelches meridionales,” 1969–70:** analysis of sacred places, celestial beings, and mythic geography.
- **Rodolfo Casamiquela, field recording in 1975:** Nemesio Chongle of the Gobernador Gregores/Lago Cardiel area told a version in which Elal and Puma hunt a guanaco and negotiate the human/animal division over fire.
- **Molina, 1976:** Feliciana Velásquez’s testimony includes Elal’s manufacture of guanaco-hide cloaks.
- **Martine Delahaye and Ana Fernández Garay, early 1980s:** Luis Cuaterno’s Aónikenk-language account of Elal and Condor; published by Fernández Garay in 1993.
- **Ana Fernández Garay, 1997:** *Testimonios de los últimos tehuelches meridionales*, preserving late linguistic and narrative material.
- **Fernández Garay and Graciela Hernández, 1998 fieldwork/1999 publication:** a bilingual fire-origin narrative from Alberto Quilchamal in Chubut. In that regional story the matuasto lizard, Hare, and Rhea carry the action; Elal is not necessarily the direct thief in every telling.
- **Johannes Wilbert and Karin Simoneau, 1984:** *Folk Literature of the Tehuelche Indians*, an English comparative collection drawing together Lista, Borgatello, Hughes, Llarás Samitier, Bórmida, Siffredi, and other records. It is an anthology of previously recorded oral texts, not an ancient scripture.

### Earlier-looking but later-published records

Some traditions are attributed to collectors or missionaries earlier than Bórmida and Siffredi but survive chiefly through later anthologies:

- **Borgatello:** a version in which Elal creates the Tehuelches and grants them Patagonia.
- **Hughes, 1927:** a version in which Elal marries one of the two daughters of Sun and Moon and the Tehuelches descend from them.
- **Tomás Harrington manuscripts:** later scholars used unpublished or incompletely published Patagonian records.
- **Payró:** helped circulate Elal in literary descriptions of Patagonia, but is not earlier than Lista’s foundational account.

### No pre-1894 physical corpus

There are no known Elal inscriptions, codices, bark manuscripts, carved cult images, coins, temple archives, or securely identifiable pre-contact depictions. The corpus begins as oral tradition mediated through Spanish ethnographic writing. That is a limitation of the surviving archive, not evidence that Elal was invented in 1894.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic actions

**[Early attested]** Lista assigns Elal the following functions:

- creator or maker of the Tehuelche people;
- clearer of a monster-filled landscape;
- controller or tamer of puma, fox, condor, and other animals;
- inventor of bow and arrow;
- discoverer of fire;
- provider of skin clothing and shelter;
- founder of human morality;
- transformer able to assume bird or horsefly form;
- maker of islands by shooting arrows into the sea.

**[Later collected variants]** The corpus expands these functions:

- giver or thief of fire rather than its first discoverer;
- teacher of hide-working, sewing, hunting, and the gendered division of tasks;
- regulator of dangerous animals and landscape features;
- creator of human beings from birds in some versions;
- institutor of exogamy, marriage rules, or incest prohibitions in later syntheses;
- intermediary between earthly, subterranean, marine, and celestial zones;
- candidate husband of Sun’s Daughter, identified in some records with the morning or evening star.

The distinction between “discoverer,” “creator,” and “thief” of fire is fundamental. Lista has Elal gather stones and wood and make the first fire. Llarás Samitier similarly records discovery. Bórmida and Siffredi record Elal taking fire from Armadillo, Skunk, and Pampas Cat; the animals are punished or permanently separated from cooked food. Casamiquela’s Nemesio Chongle instead tells of Elal and Puma choosing different lifeways. [Hernández’s comparison of the fire variants](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/73522/CONICET_Digital_Nro.c5f7eb9d-89a3-4e2a-9818-1c253615c32a_X.pdf?sequence=5)

### Form

No stable physiognomy is recorded.

- He is an extracted fetus and rapidly growing child.
- He becomes a youthful hunter armed with bow and arrows.
- Lista describes a period in which his hair is held by an Indigenous headband and he carries stone axe and dart.
- He can become a horsefly to enter Goshge.
- At departure he becomes a small bird and rides on a swan.
- Other versions make the rodent grandmother transform into the transporting swan.

No source establishes a fixed skin color, eye color, beard, halo, ceremonial costume, or canonical bodily type.

### Implements and emblems

Secure narrative implements include:

- bow;
- arrows;
- condor feathers used on arrows;
- bolas in later variants;
- stone tools and scrapers;
- guanaco-hide mantle;
- awl, sinew thread, and hide-working equipment;
- swan as transporter;
- rodent burrow as refuge.

Claims that Elal had fixed sacred colors, numbers, weekdays, planetary correspondences, zodiacal signs, incense, crystals, or ritual seals are unsupported modern elaborations.

### Landscape and rock art

**[Collected oral variants]** Elal’s cycle attaches mythic events to named or recognizable Patagonian places: the spring near Tecka/Ay-aike and the Senguerr route in Lista; Chaltén/Fitz Roy in later retellings; mountains, bogs, caves, rock dwellings, and the Atlantic horizon.

Bórmida and Siffredi’s narrators associated marks on stone with the dwelling, footprints, handprints, and bodily traces of Sun, Moon, or figures in Elal’s cycle. Modern archaeology rightly distinguishes this Indigenous interpretation of already existing rock art from proof that the images depict Elal or were made by the narrators’ ancestors. A recent study notes both the mythic association and the absence of direct historical testimony that Aónikenk people made the ancient images. [Rock art and Elal traditions in Santa Cruz](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0752/14/2/42)

There is therefore no securely identified ancient “image of Elal.” Twentieth- and twenty-first-century illustrations are modern reception.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Parentage and birth variants

#### Lista’s version

**[Early attested]** Nóshtex is Elal’s father. The mother is not named by Lista. Nóshtex kills her, cuts open her belly with a flint, and extracts Elal to eat him. Térguerr, a rodent, rescues and raises the infant. The wounded mother’s body produces a spring. Lista does not name her Teo or explicitly identify her as a cloud.

#### Cloud-woman version

**[Later ethnographic synthesis/collected variant]** In the Bórmida–Siffredi cycle, the mother is commonly described as a Cloud Woman, often written **Teo**. Nóshtex abducts and rapes or forcibly impregnates her. A prophecy or curse states that the child will surpass his father; Nóshtex consequently kills the pregnant mother and tries to consume the fetus. The Tuco-tuco/Rat grandmother rescues Elal.

This fuller “Nóshtex + Teo” genealogy should not be retrojected into Lista. It is a later-documented variant or elaboration.

#### Other maternal ambiguity

Some summaries confuse Elal’s mother with the rodent who saves him. The secure distinction in the expanded cycle is:

- Cloud Woman/Teo: biological mother;
- Térguerr/Terr-werr/Tuco-tuco: maternal grandmother, rescuer, fosterer, or tutor.

Lista calls the murdered woman merely Nóshtex’s wife and the rescuer a rodent; he does not provide the later complete family vocabulary.

### Consort and descendants

**[Early attested]** Lista records an unsuccessful proposal to the daughter of Sun and Moon. Her parents substitute a servant wearing the daughter’s clothes and name. Elal detects the deceit and leaves without completing the marriage.

**[Collected variant]** In other versions Elal undergoes lethal tests imposed by Sun and Moon and wins their daughter. Hughes’s 1927 version makes the Tehuelches descendants of this union.

**[Collected variant]** Some Bórmida–Siffredi narratives again frustrate the marriage: after discovering deception, Elal refuses the daughter, sends Sun and Moon to the sky, or departs permanently.

The daughter appears as **Pet’n/Pe’l’n**, “Daughter of Sun,” or **kalemen kengenken**, literally “Sun’s daughter,” and is sometimes identified with the morning or evening star. The designation **Teluj** is widespread in school and literary retellings but should not be treated as the single ancient canonical name. Comparative research connects the solar-lunar genealogy with other Chon-language traditions. [Study of Sun–Moon lineage in Chon traditions](https://www.scielo.cl/article_plus.php?lng=es&pid=S0718-68942022000200011&tlng=es)

### Position relative to Kóoch

**[Later collected systematization]** Kóoch is the remote primordial creator in an influential cosmogonic synthesis: Kóoch’s tears form the sea, his breath the wind, and his acts the celestial order. Elal belongs to a later epoch and makes the earth socially inhabitable. Lista’s Elal is himself “author of the cosmos” and Lista does not introduce Kóoch in the Elal chapter. The neat hierarchy “Kóoch creates the universe; Elal creates culture” is useful but reflects the later assembled corpus, not a uniform doctrine attested in 1894.

### Every documented interaction with another named supernatural being

Animals who speak or act as primordial persons are included where they have individualized mythic roles. Dates refer to publication or field record, not the supposed mythic time.

- **Nóshtex/Nosjthej — father, intended devourer, temporary master, and pursuer.** He cuts Elal from the murdered mother, later claims paternal authority, and chases him until Elal raises forest as a barrier. Lista, 1894. **[Early attested]**
- **Tkaur — transformed or alternative name of Nóshtex during Lista’s confused transitional episode.** Lista, 1894. **[Early attested; identity uncertain]**
- **Térguerr/Terr-werr/Tuco-tuco — rescuer, grandmother, foster mother, and teacher.** She hides Elal underground and teaches him plant food and mountain routes; in later versions she may transform into Swan or arrange his escape. Lista, 1894; Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Early attested with later variants]**
- **Teo/Cloud Woman — mother and victim whose death precipitates Elal’s rescue.** Bórmida–Siffredi corpus, 1969–70. **[Later collected variant]**
- **Kóoch — primordial creator whose already-formed universe precedes Elal; in modern syntheses Kóoch curses Nóshtex or ordains Elal’s superiority.** Bórmida–Siffredi-derived cosmogonic syntheses. **[Later systematization; direct encounter variably attested]**
- **Goshge/Góos — anthropophagous giant or land-whale whom Elal defeats by becoming a horsefly and entering his mouth.** Lista, 1894. Later children’s versions turn Góos explicitly into Whale and have Elal force her into the sea. **[Early attested; later literary transformation]**
- **Sintalk’n/Shintaukel — powerful warrior who fights Elal, is defeated, imprisoned, and devoured.** Lista, 1894. **[Early attested]**
- **Sun, Xáleshen/Kéenyenken — prospective father-in-law, imposer of trials, deceiver, and object of Elal’s threat.** Lista, 1894; Siffredi, 1968; Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Early attested with collected variants]**
- **Moon, Kéenguenkon/Kéenyenkon — prospective mother-in-law, co-imposer of trials, and deceiver.** In some versions she enchants a mountain that throws stone scrapers or requests hides and tools; Elal completes the tasks. Lista, 1894; Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Early attested with collected variants]**
- **Daughter of Sun/Pet’n/kalemen kengenken — desired bride.** Elal either wins, rejects, or never obtains her, depending on the version. Lista, 1894; Hughes, 1927; Siffredi, 1968; Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Early attested with conflicting variants]**
- **The substituted servant — receives the daughter’s dress and name and is sent to Elal as a counterfeit bride; he detects the substitution.** Lista, 1894. **[Early attested; personal name not preserved]**
- **Swan/Kelfü/Kóokne — transporter and, in Lista, one of Elal’s “brothers.”** Carries bird-transformed Elal east over the sea; in other versions carries the infant west to Chaltén and may be the transformed grandmother. Lista, 1894; later collected versions. **[Early attested with directionally opposite variants]**
- **Condor — reluctant feather-giver, informer, or antagonist.** Elal shoots him and plucks his head, explaining condor baldness. Lista, 1894; Luis Cuaterno text, published 1993. **[Early attested and independently recollected]**
- **Puma/“Lion” — first an animal Elal subdues; in a 1975 version his hunting companion who declines fire, thereby separating animal from human life.** Lista, 1894; Nemesio Chongle/Casamiquela, 1975. **[Early attested with regional variant]**
- **Fox — animal submitted to Elal and companion on his journeys.** Lista, 1894. **[Early attested]**
- **Armadillo/Piche/Aano — possessor who withholds fire; Elal takes it and slashes the shell, explaining its markings.** Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Collected oral variant]**
- **Skunk/Chingue — co-possessor of fire who denies having it; loses fire and receives or retains foul smell and degraded food.** Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Collected oral variant]**
- **Pampas Cat — co-possessor of fire, less severely punished than Armadillo and Skunk.** Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Collected oral variant]**
- **Terrible Guanaco/Guanaco Male — trial-animal killed by Elal, sometimes by arrow and sometimes after being trapped in a bog.** Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Collected oral variant]**
- **Dangerous Rhea egg — an animate or magically lethal trial-object containing an item required by Sun and Moon; Elal destroys it with an arrow.** Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Collected oral variant]**
- **Matuasto — owner of fire in the Quilchamal/Chalía regional version.** Hare rather than Elal steals the fire, demonstrating that not every Tehuelche fire story centers Elal. Alberto Quilchamal, recorded 1998. **[Regional variant]**

No securely documented interaction has been located between Elal and the major named Mapuche/Huilliche beings Ngünechen, Pillan, Antü, Küyen, Kai Kai, Tren Tren, Anchimallén, or the Chiloé figures Caleuche, Pincoya, Millalobo, Trauco, and Camahueto. Modern lists combining them are cross-cultural compilations.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Birth, rescue, and accelerated childhood

**[Early attested]** Nóshtex kills his wife, cuts out the fetus, and prepares to eat it. An earthquake-like disturbance distracts him. Térguerr takes Elal into the deepest part of her burrow. Nóshtex cannot reach him. A spring flows from the dead mother’s wound near Tecka, and generations of Tzóneka children bathe there.

The rodent raises Elal in a guanaco-wool nest, feeds him herbs, and teaches him mountain paths. He grows rapidly, makes bow and arrow, and brings back birds. The grandmother warns him that the dangerous beasts are daughters of darkness.

**[Later canonical elaboration]** The mother becomes Teo, Cloud Woman; Nóshtex abducts and violates her. Kóoch’s curse or a prophecy says the child will surpass the giant. Animals organize Elal’s rescue. Swan carries him from the eastern island to Chaltén, where he remains before descending into Patagonia.

The elaborate animal council, named birds, three-day stay on Chaltén, and precise Kóoch–Teo genealogy are not in Lista and must be labeled later collected or literary forms.

### Making Patagonia habitable

**[Early attested]** Elal fights pumas and subjects animals to his authority. His actions convert a dangerous landscape into human territory. The cycle is simultaneously cosmological, etiological, and territorial: features of animals and places preserve traces of his acts.

**[Collected oral variants]** He creates forests to obstruct Nóshtex, neutralizes dangerous guanacos, and provides hides, tools, shelters, and hunting procedures. In some accounts he creates humans; in others humans already exist and receive culture from him. In still others birds become the first people.

It is therefore inaccurate to choose a single proposition—“Elal created all humans”—as canonical. The narrower recurring claim is that he creates or organizes the Tehuelche human world.

### Origin of bow and arrow

**[Early attested]** Lista says that, while growing in Térguerr’s care, Elal invents bow and arrow. The first game he brings home consists of small birds. His contest with Condor explains both feathered arrows and the bird’s bald head.

Later depictions that add metal arrowheads, European dress, or a standardized feather crown are modern visualization.

### Elal and Condor

Elal asks Condor for an attractive wing feather for an arrow. Condor refuses, saying he needs his feathers for warmth and flight. Elal shoots him down. When Condor offers a feather after being wounded, Elal seizes him and plucks his head, declaring that it will remain bald.

**[Early attested]** Lista gives this etiological story in 1894.

**[Collected oral variant]** Luis Cuaterno’s Aónikenk text retains the exchange’s humor and hostility. Elal initially identifies himself as a wanderer; Condor insults him and refuses; Elal shoots, summons him, and plucks his head, decreeing the permanent condition. Cuaterno simultaneously describes Elal as maker, chief, and protector. This independent later recollection confirms the episode’s importance. [Fernández Garay text](https://www.scribd.com/document/669008725/Dialnet-UnRelatoMiticoTehuelche-6914184)

### Origin of fire

At least four distinguishable structures survive.

1. **Discovery:** Elal gathers stones and wood, and fire shines for the first time. Lista, 1894. **[Early attested]**
2. **Discovery by the culture hero:** Llarás Samitier, 1950. **[Collected oral variant]**
3. **Theft from animal owners:** Armadillo, Skunk, and Pampas Cat conceal fire; Elal detects and takes it for humans, punishing the selfish owners. Bórmida–Siffredi, 1969–70. **[Collected oral variant]**
4. **Human–puma separation:** Elal wants to make fire after hunting a guanaco, but Puma refuses because fire would expose animals to human persecution. Nemesio Chongle/Casamiquela, 1975. **[Regional variant]**
5. **Matuasto–Hare–Rhea story:** Matuasto owns fire; Hare steals it; Rhea participates in the pursuit and a dispute over winter. Alberto Quilchamal, recorded 1998. Elal is not central. **[Regional, partly Mapuche-contact context]**

Hernández interprets the fire narratives as marking the separation between raw animal nature and cooked human culture while emphasizing their continued historical alteration. That is a modern structural and historical reading, not an explanation supplied by every narrator.

### Goshge, the devouring giant

**[Early attested]** Goshge terrorizes the people and eats children and lost hunters. Elal’s arrows cannot wound him. Elal becomes a horsefly, enters his mouth, penetrates his stomach, and stings him to death.

**[Modern retelling]** In “the legend of the whale,” Goshge/Góos becomes a terrestrial whale who inadvertently swallows people, animals, plants, tents, and tools. Elal enters as a horsefly, makes the whale cough everything up, changes its legs into fins, and sends it to sea. This is an attractive etiological children’s narrative, but its gentle resolution differs materially from Lista’s killing of an anthropophagous giant. [A modern educational version](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/sites/default/files/media/document/2017/10/27/a96b1a345570b5db35879f1797ea74aed1322d2f.pdf)

### Courtship of Sun’s Daughter

**[Early attested]** Elal asks for the daughter of Sun and Moon. Her parents fear refusing him and substitute a servant dressed and named as their daughter. Elal detects the deception and threatens Sun with arrows.

**[Collected oral variants]** Sun and Moon demand dangerous tasks:

- killing a petrifying guanaco;
- obtaining an object from a deadly rhea egg;
- killing a male guanaco for hide or sinews;
- surviving a mountain that throws sharp stone scrapers;
- bringing materials Moon needs for hide-working.

A guanaco-hide mantle protects Elal. In some tellings he wins the daughter; in others the deception causes him to reject her and send the celestial couple skyward. One version says the couple’s stone dwelling retains their prints. [Material-culture analysis quoting the variants](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/bitstream/handle/10915/24989/Documento_completo.pdf?sequence=1)

The myth supports competing genealogies:

- the Tehuelches descend from Elal and Sun’s Daughter;
- Elal completes the tests but does not marry her;
- Elal never receives the true daughter;
- the daughter is a star and the episode orders celestial motion.

### Departure over the sea

**[Early attested]** After completing his work, Elal transforms into a small bird, assembles his swan “brothers,” rides on the strongest, and flies eastward over the sea. Islands rise where his arrows strike the water. Papón tells Lista that Elal and the swans disappeared where the sea vapors move.

**[Collected variant]** In other narratives the decisive swan-flight occurs at the beginning: infant Elal is carried westward from an eastern island and set down on Chaltén.

**[Collected variant]** Some tellers place Elal’s final destination in the sky rather than across the sea. Ana Montenegro de Yebes described his ascent as spiral or wheel-like. The contradiction is real: eastern oceanic departure, celestial ascent, and earlier westward arrival must remain distinct.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrated Elal institution

No temple, shrine building, altar, hereditary priesthood, festival calendar, tithe, oracle, pilgrimage administration, or sacrifice specifically to Elal has been securely documented. “Cult of Elal” is therefore too strong unless *cult* is being used loosely for narrative reverence.

Lista records Elal in story, not as the recipient of prayer or offering. Siffredi’s fieldwork documents mythic importance but does not establish an Elal priesthood. Cuaterno calls him maker, chief, and protector, but the surviving story is not a ritual invocation.

### Sacred geography

Places associated with Elal could function as mnemonic or sacred landmarks:

- the spring associated with his dead mother;
- Chaltén in later versions;
- caves and burrows;
- rock surfaces bearing attributed traces;
- the Atlantic horizon and islands;
- dangerous mountains, bogs, and guanaco ranges.

Such associations demonstrate mythic geography, not necessarily pilgrimage or worship.

### Broader Aónikenk ritual life

Aónikenk communities had shamans/healers, birth and puberty ceremonies, mortuary observances, divination or magical practice, and rites involving animals. These should not automatically be assigned to Elal.

Horse and dog sacrifice at funerals is well documented in historical descriptions: animals could be killed so their souls would transport or accompany the dead. This was a mortuary practice, not a sacrifice to Elal. [Overview of Tehuelche belief and funerary practice](https://chileprecolombino.cl/pueblos-originarios/tehuelche/culto-y-funebria/)

### Human sacrifice and sexual institutions

No reliable source examined documents human sacrifice, temple prostitution, sacred sexual service, or ritual killing offered to Elal. Nóshtex’s murder of Elal’s mother, Goshge’s child-eating, Sintalk’n’s devouring, and Elal’s killing of monsters are mythic violence, not descriptions of human ritual practice.

### Moon observance

Siffredi considered whether supplications to the full moon preserved evidence of an earlier lunar cult. This is a scholarly possibility concerning Moon, not proof of an Elal cult. The courtship myth relates Elal to Moon, but relation in narrative does not establish joint worship. [Siffredi publication and fieldwork record](https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/cultures/sh05/documents/010)

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1520: unrecorded Indigenous history

Elal’s tradition almost certainly preceded Lista, since he heard it from elders who said they had learned it in childhood. How far it preceded European contact cannot presently be dated. Archaeological rock art and ancient settlement demonstrate deep human history in Patagonia, but no excavated object bears an identifiable label “Elal.”

### 1520–late eighteenth century: European contact without named attestation

Pigafetta recorded the first European encounter conventionally dated to 1520, but not Elal. Later missionaries, navigators, and travelers described Patagonian peoples and beliefs without securely recording this name. Silence may reflect limited access, language barriers, selective missionary interests, or geographical differences; it cannot prove absence from Indigenous tradition.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: Mapuche contact and “Araucanization”

Mapuche language and culture spread eastward through trade, marriage, migration, warfare, diplomacy, and the equestrian economy. Northern and central Patagonian communities often became Mapudungun-speaking; southern Aónikenk retained their Chon language longer but were not isolated from contact. Fernández Garay emphasizes both Mapuche influence and the distinct survival of Aónikenk south of the strongest Araucanizing zone.

This history permits borrowing in either direction, but it does not justify reclassifying Elal as a Mapuche god. Casamiquela and later writers proposed Patagonian roots for some beliefs found among Araucanized groups; each motif requires separate demonstration.

### Nineteenth-century colonial disruption

Argentine and Chilean territorial expansion, military campaigns, missions, ranching, introduced disease, confinement, labor exploitation, and land loss transformed Aónikenk life. The Argentine Conquest of the Desert and subsequent settlement intensified cultural and linguistic displacement. Communities were confined or concentrated; Camusu Aike was formally established in the late nineteenth century.

The historical suppression of Aónikenk autonomy is securely documented, but no order specifically banning “worship of Elal” has been identified. The disruption operated through dispossession, missionization, demographic collapse, schooling, and language replacement rather than a documented Elal-specific persecution.

### 1894: literary ethnographic stabilization

Lista’s publication transformed one transmitted oral cycle into a fixed Spanish text. He preserved otherwise endangered testimony but also imposed:

- “god” and “hero” classifications;
- classical comparisons;
- speculative Semitic etymology;
- a progressive theory of religion;
- a smooth literary sequence where he himself admitted contradiction and transition.

The publication became a major source for subsequent popular retellings.

### 1920s–1950s: collecting and comparison

Hughes, Borgatello, Llarás Samitier, Lehmann-Nitsche, and others recorded or republished episodes. Scholars compared Elal with culture heroes elsewhere in the Americas and with neighboring Chon traditions. Comparative work preserved variants but sometimes over-systematized a “Tehuelche pantheon.”

### 1960s–1970s: field-based corpus formation

Siffredi and Bórmida’s work with Santa Cruz elders created the central modern documentary corpus. The fuller stories of Cloud Woman, the animal owners of fire, Sun and Moon’s tests, stone traces, and social institutions enter scholarship securely at this stage.

Bórmida’s phenomenological and “historical-cultural” anthropology belongs to its period and should not be treated as theoretically neutral. His and Siffredi’s transcriptions remain valuable primary ethnographic evidence even where later scholars reject rigid cultural typologies.

### 1980s–1990s: linguistic documentation and historical interpretation

Fernández Garay and Delahaye documented late speakers and produced analyzable Aónikenk texts. The Elal–Condor story demonstrated that a narrative known from Lista still circulated in the Indigenous language, although with its own wording and emphases.

Siffredi’s later historical work challenged portrayals of Aónikenk mythology as timeless and unchanging. She argued that the Elal cycle incorporated intercultural contact and Indigenous historical consciousness—processes she described as mythologization of history and historicization of myth. [Siffredi’s historical analysis](https://revistas.inapl.gob.ar/index.php/cuadernos/article/view/476)

### Twenty-first century: heritage and educational circulation

Elal now circulates predominantly through:

- Indigenous and regional heritage work;
- museum interpretation;
- school curricula;
- children’s books;
- tourism around Chaltén and Patagonia;
- internet myth encyclopedias;
- popular fiction and card games.

This is continued cultural presence, but not evidence for an unbroken institutional cult. Contemporary Aónikenk individuals and communities may value, teach, or narrate Elal; no reliable census of active devotional worship exists.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and children’s publishing

Ana María Pavez and Constanza Recart’s *Elal y los animales* (2007), illustrated by Carmen Cardemil, retells Elal as the threatened child of Nóshtex rescued by animals and brought to Patagonia. Chile’s national curriculum describes it as an Aónikenk creation myth. [Currículum Nacional entry](https://www.curriculumnacional.cl/recursos/elal-animales)

Lilia García Bazterra’s *Elal y los misterios tehuelches* (2014) turns the corpus into a continuous literary narrative of cosmic origins, conflict, love, and heroism. Its seamless plot is a modern novelistic synthesis, not a primary oral text. [Publisher description](https://www.casadellibro.com/ebook-elal-y-los-misterios-tehuelches-ebook/9789877030051/2281655)

Modern books and teaching materials increasingly attach Elal to Chaltén, name numerous helper birds, identify the bride as Teluj, and narrate Kóoch’s creation before Elal’s birth. These details may derive from genuine later variants, but the finished unified sequence is modern editorial construction.

### Games and commercial art

Elal appears as a character in the Chilean trading-card game *Mitos y Leyendas*, including cards titled “El-lal,” “Elal – Orígenes,” and “Elal – Hijos del Sol.” Game statistics, factions, powers, costume, and card art are wholly modern inventions. They should never be used as evidence for traditional iconography.

### Scientific nomenclature

The dinosaur **Elaltitan lilloi**, named in 2012, incorporates Elal’s name with Greek *Titan*. This is modern honorific nomenclature, not evidence of ancient association between Elal and dinosaurs or giants.

### Tourism and national framing

Patagonian tourism frequently presents Chaltén as Elal’s landing place. That tradition is genuinely present in later Aónikenk narratives, but commercial accounts often suppress the source history and present one literary synthesis as “the Tehuelche creation myth.”

Elal is also enlisted into national or regional heritage narratives in both Argentina and Chile. Such inclusion can acknowledge Aónikenk history, but it may also detach the figure from living communities or subsume him under generic “Mapuche mythology.”

### Occultism, neopaganism, and New Age claims

The contested-material search found no securely documented Elal tradition in:

- Renaissance hermeticism;
- ceremonial magic;
- theosophy;
- Golden Dawn-derived systems;
- historical grimoires;
- chaos-magic manuals;
- established international neopagan organizations;
- Jung’s own published archetypal catalogues.

Modern websites may call Elal an elemental, solar deity, shamanic master, “Patagonian Prometheus,” or assign him correspondences. These are modern analogies or inventions unless accompanied by an identifiable Aónikenk source.

No reliable evidence establishes the scale of organized modern Elal worship. Heritage narration, literary interest, or private spiritual identification must not automatically be called a neopagan cult.

### Political appropriation

Elal can appear in arguments that oppose “authentic Tehuelches” to supposedly foreign Mapuche people. Such rhetoric transforms complex histories of contact, marriage, multilingualism, and shared communities into mutually exclusive nationalist categories. The Elal corpus does demonstrate Aónikenk distinctiveness; it does not support modern claims that Mapuche people as a whole are recent impostors or that present Mapuche–Tehuelche identities are fraudulent.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Elal a god or culture hero?

- **Lista:** god-hero, god, hero, author of the cosmos.
- **Cuaterno:** chief, maker, protector.
- **Siffredi/Bórmida:** culture hero or young ordering deity.
- **Modern popular works:** creator god or demigod.

“Culture hero” preserves the breadth of the evidence with the fewest unsupported institutional implications. Calling Elal “the supreme Tehuelche god” conflates him with Kóoch in later cosmologies and ignores the absence of a documented cult.

### One cycle or many narratives?

Lista presents a long sequence but admits a “vague and mysterious” transitional section in which identities and chronology change. Bórmida and Siffredi document numerous episodes and incompatible outcomes. Wilbert and Simoneau classify 49 Elal-related narratives.

The “Cycle of Elal” is therefore an ethnographic grouping of related stories, not a fixed epic with an authoritative original text.

### Indigenous continuity versus contact history

Older scholarship sometimes treated mythology as a fossil of an untouched past. Siffredi’s historical analysis instead shows episodes responding to relations with outsiders and changing social circumstances. Hernández similarly stresses fragmentation, language loss, and the incorporation of new meanings.

Neither extreme is justified:

- contact-era change does not make the stories inauthentic;
- Indigenous transmission does not make every collected detail prehistoric.

### The Kóoch–Elal system

Modern presentations often construct a clean theology:

1. Kóoch creates the universe.
2. Nóshtex and the giants inhabit the first island.
3. Elal is born and brought to Patagonia.
4. Elal creates Tehuelche humanity and culture.

This synthesis is narratively coherent and draws on collected material, but Lista’s earliest text calls Elal author of the cosmos and does not establish that hierarchy. The system is best labeled a later ethnographic synthesis rather than a universally attested creed.

### Selk’nam comparisons

Elal has been compared with Selk’nam Kénos and the remote Temáukel. Shared Chon-language ancestry and regional exchange make comparison legitimate. Direct identity does not follow. Statements that “Temáukel sent Elal through Kénos” or that Kóoch and Kénos are simply alternate spellings commonly collapse separate traditions and different mythic offices.

### Mapuche influence

Mapuche influence on Patagonian language, religion, trade, and social life is documented. Specific Elal episodes may bear contact layers. However:

- Elal is absent from the early Mapuche corpus;
- his best texts are Aónikenk;
- similarity to a Mapuche figure is not proof of borrowing;
- a possible Mapudungun cognate for his name is not proof that he is Mapuche;
- “Tehuelche,” “Puelche,” “Patagon,” “Aónikenk,” and “Mapuche–Tehuelche” are historically shifting labels, not interchangeable timeless ethnic categories.

### Iconography debate

Rock markings can be traditional evidence about how later Aónikenk narrators interpreted a landscape. They are not self-identifying portraits of Elal. Attempts to label prehistoric petroglyphs “Elal symbols” exceed the evidence.

### Source loss and archival condition

The archive is shaped by:

- the late arrival of sustained collectors;
- Spanish-language mediation;
- loss of Aónikenk speakers;
- unpublished field manuscripts;
- dispersed museum and private collections;
- missionary and governmental classifications;
- editions that excerpt rather than reproduce original speech.

Fernández Garay’s bilingual text is therefore particularly valuable. It preserves phonological transcription, segmentation, grammar, and translation rather than only plot.

## 11. Sources and Assessment

### Principal primary and near-primary records

- Ramón Lista, *Los indios tehuelches: una raza que desaparece* (1894), especially pp. 16–27. Earliest secure written attestation.
- Manuel Llarás Samitier, Patagonian fables and sagas, collected/published 1950–56.
- Alejandra Siffredi, fieldwork 1963–67 and publications beginning in 1968.
- Marcelo Bórmida and Alejandra Siffredi, “Mitología de los Tehuelches meridionales” (1969–70).
- Nemesio Chongle’s 1975 narrative, recorded by Casamiquela.
- Feliciana Velásquez’s testimony, published by Molina in 1976.
- Luis Cuaterno/Má:ger’s Aónikenk-language Elal–Condor account, published by Fernández Garay in 1993.
- Alberto Quilchamal’s 1998 fire narrative, published by Fernández Garay and Hernández.
- Johannes Wilbert and Karin Simoneau, *Folk Literature of the Tehuelche Indians* (1984), comparative anthology.

### Overall finding

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not that Elal was the universally worshipped “supreme god of a Tehuelche pantheon.” He is the central protagonist of a broad and changing Aónikenk narrative cycle: miraculous child, monster-killer, transformer, maker or ancestor of the people, inventor and teacher, controller of animals, celestial suitor, and departing protector.

The earliest source already contains contradictions and collector interpretation. Later oral records do not merely ornament it: they change Elal’s mother, his place in cosmic hierarchy, the source of fire, his marriage’s outcome, the direction and nature of his swan-flight, and even whether certain etiological stories require him. Those differences are the documented history of Elal.

Claims that make him an ancient Mapuche or Chiloé deity, give him a temple cult, identify prehistoric rock art as his portrait, equate him directly with Kénos or Temáukel, or install him in occult correspondence systems cannot presently be established.
