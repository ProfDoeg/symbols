# Cherufe: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest record, a named fiery atmospheric phenomenon or portent; in nineteenth- and twentieth-century Mapuche and Pehuenche narrative, a class of dangerous superhuman beings, volcanic ogres, anthropophages, shape-shifters, or animate talisman-stones. “God” is appropriate only in one documented modern Ranquel interpretation; “demon” and “lava monster” are chiefly missionary, folkloristic, or modern popular classifications.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Luis de Valdivia’s Mapudungun vocabulary, printed in 1606, records *cheurvue* (also transcribed *cheurove*) as “la cometa o ayre encendido”—“the comet or fiery air.” This proves the word and its association with a luminous aerial phenomenon by 1606, but does **not** attest a humanoid lava body, volcanic residence, maiden-eating, or an organized cult.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY]** Secure colonial lexical or narrative evidence.
- **[ATTESTED ORAL]** A statement or narrative recorded from an identified Indigenous speaker or community.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A locally bounded form not to be generalized to all Mapuche traditions.
- **[LATER FOLKLORE]** Nineteenth- or twentieth-century oral or literary folklore.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** An ethnographer’s, missionary’s, or scholar’s synthesis.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC]** An equation with another being or category.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** A scholarly explanation not itself part of the recorded tradition.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/RETELLING]** A recent elaboration unsupported by the early record.
- **[UNESTABLISHED]** A claim for which no adequate primary documentation was located.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Documentary identification

“Cherufe” does not designate one stable personality with a canonical genealogy. The dossier instead reveals a historical semantic complex:

1. **A comet, fireball, or nocturnal fiery exhalation** in colonial dictionaries.
2. **A sentient fiery portent** announcing death, sickness, or an approaching crisis.
3. **A volcanic or mountain-dwelling superhuman being**, sometimes giant and sometimes seven-headed.
4. **An anthropophagous ogre** who holds women or water and is killed by a human hero.
5. **A movable red or human-shaped stone**, owned by a powerful person, diviner, *kalku*, chief, or warrior; it flies as fire, foretells events, brings wealth, or assists in war.
6. **A folkloric substitute for the European dragon or Devil**, explicitly recognized as such by Rodolfo Lenz.
7. In one late Ranquel testimony, **“dios” and Ngenechén**, an exceptional identification rather than a pan-Mapuche doctrine.

The evidence therefore supports “being/class of beings and manifestations,” not a single volcano god.

### Recorded forms

Documented spellings include:

- *cheurvue*, *cheurove* — Valdivia, 1606.
- *cheruvoe*; misprinted *cheruvoc* in one edition — Febrés, 1765.
- *cheurvoe* — Havestadt, *Chilidúgú*, 1777.
- *cherruve*, *cheruve* — common nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Hispanicized forms.
- *cheurfe*, *cheurfue*, *cheurpue* — Félix José de Augusta, 1916, with dialect labels Huapi and Panguipulli.
- *chewürpue* — modern retranscription of an early-twentieth-century phonographic text.
- *cherüwfe*, *chewürfe*, *chw’fe* — modern normalized or author-specific spellings.
- *Cheburbue* — Diego de Rosales’s spelling for the being in Caupolicán’s dream. Its identification with Cherufe is traditional in later scholarship but not philologically beyond dispute.

No independent cult epithets comparable to the formal titles of a temple deity are documented.

### Etymology

The oldest lexicographers give definitions rather than a morphological etymology:

- Valdivia: “comet or fiery air.”
- Febrés: “the comet and the fiery exhalations seen at night.”
- Havestadt: Latin *cometa* and *exhalationes igneae quae quandoque videntur noctu*—“fiery exhalations which are sometimes seen at night.”
- Augusta: “fenómeno ígneo conocido con el nombre de bola de fuego”—“fiery phenomenon known as a fireball.”

A modern derivation connects the name with Mapudungun *chewpüdün*, “to reduce to ashes; exterminate,” producing glosses such as “the incinerator,” “the one who destroys by fire,” or “the exterminator.” Augusta’s modern dictionary does place *chewpüdün* beside the Cherufe entries, so the linguistic association is possible; nevertheless, the fully segmented popular etymology *cherüwfe* = “he who reduces to ashes” is later than the colonial attestations and should be labeled **[MODERN/CONTESTED ETYMOLOGY]**, not treated as Valdivia’s or Febrés’s explanation. The relevant historical entries can be compared in [Lenz’s dictionary entry](https://diccionariodechile.cl/entrada/lenz-1904/381-cherruve) and the [modernized Augusta dictionary](https://mapuchediario.cl/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Diccionario-mapudungun-espanol-espanol-mapudungun.pdf).

### Classifications through time

- **[EARLY, 1606–1777]:** comet, fiery air, nocturnal fiery exhalation.
- **[LATER FOLKLORE, 1890s]:** monster, giant, volcanic householder, father, husband, employer, water-holder, anthropophage.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION, Lenz]:** “personification of the force of fire,” perhaps lightning or volcanic glow reflected on clouds.
- **[REGIONAL, Argentina/Neuquén–Pampa]:** animate stone and warrior’s familiar or talisman.
- **[ATTESTED ORAL, Ranquel]:** in one testimony, “a god,” specifically identified with Ngenechén.
- **[MODERN POPULAR]:** magma-bodied “lava demon” or cryptid. The crystalline-rock-and-magma anatomy is not found in the colonial dictionaries or the early texts examined.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Valdivia, 1606

Luis de Valdivia’s *Arte y gramática general de la lengua que corre en todo el Reyno de Chile, con un vocabulario y confesionario* records *cheurvue* as “la cometa o ayre encendido.” **[EARLY]**

What it establishes:

- A Mapudungun word recognizably ancestral to later *Cherufe*.
- Its referent was understood by the missionary lexicographer as a luminous aerial phenomenon.
- The term was in use by the opening of the seventeenth century.

What it does not establish:

- A humanoid or serpentine body.
- Residence inside volcanoes.
- Control of earthquakes.
- Consumption of maidens.
- Sacrifice as historical ritual.
- Worship, temples, priesthood, or genealogy.

#### Diego de Rosales, composed circa 1674

Rosales’s *Historia general del Reino de Chile* says that a being calling itself *Cheburbue*, “the announcer of future things,” appeared to Caupolicán in a dream, urged him to attack Tucapel, and promised victory if its name were invoked. Caupolicán awoke and offered tobacco incense to Pillán. **[EARLY NARRATIVE; CONTESTED IDENTIFICATION]**

Later authors identify *Cheburbue* with *Cherruve*. The phonetic resemblance and divinatory function support the comparison, but the text does not explicitly say that Cheburbue is a comet, volcanic monster, or Cherufe in the later folkloric sense. Rosales’s account was written in the seventeenth century but published much later; the familiar citation is to the 1877 edition.

#### Febrés, 1765, and Havestadt, 1777

Both repeat the fiery-phenomenon field:

- Febrés: comet and nocturnal fiery exhalations.
- Havestadt: comet; fiery exhalations seen by night.

Their agreement strengthens the conclusion that the stable colonial meaning was celestial or atmospheric, not “magma giant.”

### Nineteenth-century textual turning point: Lenz

Rodolfo Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos* was issued in installments in 1895–1897. Its seventh study presents bilingual Mapudungun–Spanish tales attributed to Calvún, also called Segundo Jara, in what Lenz classified as Chilean Pehuenche. The texts were written down partly by Lenz and partly by Chiappa, then corrected and translated with Calvún’s help. Lenz himself warned that most displayed recent elements and that several were affected by European folktales. The complete section identifies seven tales, of which nos. 3–7 involve Cherufe in some fashion: [*Estudios araucanos*, VII](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Estudios_araucanos/VII).

The important texts are:

- **“Las apuestas: cuento de un Cherruve.”** A man enters Cherufe’s house on a volcano and survives or outwits a dangerous host. **[ATTESTED ORAL; PEHUENCHE; LITERARILY MEDIATED]**
- **“Los dos perritos.”** A seven-headed Cherufe has stopped the water. A father gives or sells his naked daughter to it so that water may return; a young man and his dogs kill it, and the river runs again. **[ATTESTED ORAL; REGIONAL; POSSIBLE EUROPEAN DRAGON-TALE INFLUENCE]**
- **“Las transformaciones.”** Cherufe appears as a dangerous employer or antagonist amid shape-shifting and arduous tasks. Lenz notes discontinuities in the narration and at one point calls it a sea monster revived when placed in water. **[ATTESTED ORAL; COMPOSITE]**
- **“La hija del Cherruve.”** A young man reaches Cherufe’s volcanic house, works for him, forms a relationship with Cherufe’s daughter, and escapes with her; the wife of Cherufe pursues them. **[ATTESTED ORAL; REGIONAL; EUROPEAN FOLKTALE STRUCTURE]**
- **“Huenchumir, el hijo del oso.”** Cherufe functions as a dragon-like monster. Lenz explicitly judged this Cherufe to be the European/Indo-European dragon transplanted into Mapuche-language narrative. **[SYNCRETISTIC FOLKTALE]**

Lenz’s methodological importance is double: he preserves Mapudungun texts rather than only Spanish summaries, but he also edited them, acknowledged uncertain readings, and supplied interpretations such as “human sacrifice.”

### Early twentieth-century corpus

- **Sperata de Saunière, “Cuentos populares araucanos y chilenos” (1916–1917):** records further Cherufe material, including descriptions from people near Villarrica. Informants described a tall thin luminous man on the summit, or a clawed giant whose legs span volcanoes and whose weight breaks rocks. Saunière states expressly that Cherufe was not an object of worship. Her discussion and tales are available in the [digitized volume](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0076045.pdf). **[LATER FOLKLORE]**
- **Félix José de Augusta, *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916):** defines *cheurfue/cheurpue* first as a fireball, then summarizes Lenz’s tales: a superhuman man living on volcanic summits, eating Mapuche girls, drying rivers if parents do not surrender them, and causing earthquakes by displacing rocks. This is explicitly a lexicographic summary of Lenz, not independent proof of those practices. **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Félix Outes, “La materialización del cherrufe araucano” (1917):** identifies an engraved plaque from Chimpay, Río Negro, as a representation or materialization of Cherufe and attempts to unite comet and monster. The identification is interpretive, not secured by an inscription naming the figure. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Lehmann-Nitsche recordings, 1903:** Regina, recorded in La Plata on 28 January 1903, described *Chewürpue* as a bad “thing of fire” that appeared when trouble or “news” was about to occur. The text survives in Lehmann-Nitsche’s archive and has been critically retranscribed in *Mongeleluchi zungu*. **[ATTESTED ORAL]**
- **Bertha Koessler-Ilg, *Tradiciones araucanas* (1962):** preserves extensive Neuquén material about Cherufe stones, including traditions connected with Calfucurá and Namuncurá. **[ATTESTED ORAL/LATER COMPILATION]**
- **Graciela Hernández, 1995 and 2006:** analyzes Ranquel memories of warriors Huentulaf and Curruhuil owning a human-shaped Cherufe stone whose red-spotted mouth foretold victory. Her 2006 study is based partly on the La Pampa oral archive and distinguishes the fireball, anthropomorphic ogre, and talisman-stone complexes: [“En tiempos del malón”](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/87728/CONICET_Digital_Nro.07375688-17bb-41cc-bd7e-850765c90e74_Y.doc.pdf?sequence=5). **[ATTESTED ORAL; MODERN ANALYSIS]**

### Named corpora in which no clear Cherufe dossier was established

Ercilla’s *La Araucana* and Núñez de Pineda’s *Cautiverio feliz* are indispensable colonial sources for Spanish representations of Mapuche society, but no secure Cherufe occurrence was established in this research. They must not be cited as Cherufe attestations merely because they concern Araucanía.

Likewise, the searchable forms examined of Pascual Coña/Moesbach, Faron, Grebe, Foerster, Bacigalupo, Dillehay, Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, and general *nguillatun* and *machitun* ethnographies do not yield evidence for a distinct Cherufe cult. Their value lies principally in contextualizing Mapuche cosmology, ritual, colonial disruption, and the danger of forcing fluid beings into a rigid “pantheon.”

No persuasive Cherufe entry was located in Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes*. Cherufe should therefore not be presented as a major member of the specifically Chilote mythology documented alongside the Caleuche, Trauco, Pincoya, or Imbunche. Its occurrence in pan-Chilean mythology books does not itself demonstrate Huilliche or Chiloé provenance.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and actions

#### Fire, luminous flight, and portent

**[EARLY/ATTESTED]** Cherufe’s most durable core is fiery luminosity: comet, meteor-like body, fireball, nocturnal exhalation. Regina’s 1903 testimony says the fiery apparition foretold a coming disturbance. Guevara and later Argentine sources make its inclination or direction significant: the place toward which it leaned could expect death or epidemic.

#### Volcanoes and mountains

**[LATER FOLKLORE]** In Lenz’s tales Cherufe owns a house at a volcano’s summit. Villarrica-area reports place a luminous giant on the summit or allow it to stride between volcanoes. This is well attested by the late nineteenth century, but colonial definitions do not place the being in magma chambers.

#### Earthquake and rockfall

**[LATER FOLKLORE]** Augusta’s summary says the giant causes tremors by sitting on or displacing rocks. Informants recorded by Saunière similarly said its enormous weight breaks or overturns rock. This is a documented belief, though not proof that all Mapuche earthquake traditions centered on Cherufe. Pillán and other mountain powers occupy overlapping explanatory territory.

#### Water control

**[REGIONAL FOLKTALE]** The seven-headed being in “Los dos perritos” has stopped the rivers. When killed, water runs again. The mechanism belongs to the dragon-and-water-hoard folktale pattern and is not in the colonial lexical core.

#### Anthropophagy

**[REGIONAL FOLKTALE]** Cherufe eats humans, especially girls, in some Lenz and later Argentine narratives. Other narratives make it the owner or father of women rather than their eater. These variants must not be collapsed.

#### Wealth, divination, and war

**[ARGENTINE REGIONAL VARIANT]** The Cherufe stone can:

- Foretell victory.
- Fly like a ball of fire.
- Drink blood, especially the blood of Christians in one Koessler-Ilg tradition.
- Make its owner wealthy.
- Act as the ally or materialized power of a renowned chief or warrior.

Hernández records the tradition that Calfucurá owed military success to such a stone, citing Guevara, Casamiquela, and Koessler-Ilg. This is a political and martial form of Cherufe, quite different from the volcano ogre.

### Forms

No single canonical image exists. Recorded forms include:

- Fireball or comet with a trail.
- Fiery aerial “thing.”
- Red stone left after a fiery descent.
- Human- or child-shaped stone.
- Tall, thin human-like figure surrounded by red light.
- Giant with claws and extremely long legs.
- Seven-headed monster.
- Human-headed serpent, in Guevara’s regional synthesis.
- Shape-shifting ogre.
- Husband, father, or householder with wife and daughter.
- Marine monster capable of revival in water.
- Modern lizard-man or magma giant.

### Colors, materials, and objects

Red is associated with fire and with reported Cherufe stones; one Ranquel stone had red spots around its mouth. Silver appears in accounts in which a privately kept stone consumes silver objects or filings and enriches its owner. Blood is its food in some Neuquén accounts. None of these constitutes a universal sacred color or standardized offering system.

### Iconography

No securely identified precolonial image bears an inscription naming Cherufe. Outes’s Chimpay plaque is the most important proposed material depiction, but it is an attribution based on formal comparison. **[CONTESTED]**

Modern depictions as a massive humanoid made of black volcanic rock, glowing crystal, or exposed magma are visual standardizations created by illustrators, encyclopedias, television, and games. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Cherufe has no securely attested primordial birth, parents, siblings, or fixed place in a divine hierarchy.

In Lenz’s “La hija del Cherruve,” one Cherufe has:

- A wife.
- A daughter.
- Livestock and a house.
- The social position of a dangerous father and employer.

This is household genealogy inside one tale, not a cosmic genealogy of “the god Cherufe.”

The plural or class character is important: divergent Cherufe figures can die in stories without extinguishing the category.

### Syncretistic relationships

- **Pillán:** Lenz compared the two because both could represent fiery force. The colonial dream narrative has Caupolicán encounter Cheburbue but offer tobacco to Pillán. This is an association, not proof that Cherufe is Pillán’s offspring, servant, or enemy.
- **Wekufü:** Some twentieth-century ethnographers classify Cherufe among harmful *wekufü* beings or forces. This is a categorical inclusion, not a personal genealogy.
- **Ngenechén:** One modern Ranquel narrator called Cherufe “dios” and identified it with Ngenechén. Hernández presents this as the narrator’s testimony; it should not be retrojected into colonial or pan-Mapuche theology.
- **European dragon/Devil:** Lenz explicitly argued that some Cherufe tales substitute Cherufe for the European dragon. Spanish-language retellings also call it a demon. These are processes of narrative translation and syncretism, not ancient equations.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Pillán — ritual juxtaposition:** After Cheburbue appears in Caupolicán’s dream and counsels the attack on Tucapel, Caupolicán offers tobacco incense to Pillán. Source: Rosales, *Historia general*, composed circa 1674. **[EARLY; CHEBURBUE–CHERUFE IDENTIFICATION CONTESTED]**
- **Ngenechén — identification:** Ranquel narrator Macedonio Carripí says “el cherufe es dios” and identifies Cherufe as Ngenechén. Source: oral testimony analyzed by Hernández, published 2006. **[REGIONAL VARIANT; LATE IDENTIFICATION]**
- **No other named deity interaction securely established.** Lenz’s comparisons with Pillán are comparative classifications, not narrated encounters. No secure episode was found in which Cherufe fights, marries, deceives, or jointly acts with Antü, Küyen, Trentren Vilu, Caicai Vilu, Anchimallén, or a named Chilote being. Similarity between Cherufe and Anchimallén as moving lights does not constitute an interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The fireball that announces calamity

**Earliest core:** Colonial dictionaries identify a comet or fiery air.  
**Oral development:** Regina’s 1903 narrative says the bad fiery thing appeared when trouble was coming. Guevara’s collected material links its trajectory with impending death and epidemic.

This is probably the least literarily elaborated Cherufe complex. It has neither a fixed protagonist nor a complete plot: apparition, interpretation, consequence.

### Caupolicán’s dream

Rosales reports that before an attack, Caupolicán dreams of Cheburbue, who identifies itself as an announcer of future things, counsels an assault on Tucapel, and promises victory through invocation. Caupolicán then burns tobacco for Pillán.

- **Earliest source:** Rosales, composed circa 1674.
- **Function:** Divinatory authorization for warfare.
- **Problem:** Rosales writes as a Christian missionary and calls such beings demonic; his terminology cannot simply be taken as Indigenous taxonomy.
- **Status:** **[EARLY; CONTESTED AS CHERUFE]**.

### “Los dos perritos”: water, maiden, and seven-headed monster

A young man encounters a naked girl. She explains that her father has sold or surrendered her to a seven-headed Cherufe in exchange for drinking water. The monster approaches to eat her. The young man’s two dogs, Norte and Sur, kill it. Water flows again. A secondary impostor episode follows: another man removes the monster’s heads and claims the victory, but the hero possesses the seven tongues and proves his deed, after which he marries the rescued girl.

Lenz interpreted this explicitly as a human-sacrifice myth. The text does describe an intended human offering, but it is a folktale event, not evidence that communities historically threw virgins into volcanoes.

- **Source:** Calvún/Segundo Jara, recorded and edited by Lenz and Chiappa, published 1895–1897.
- **Regional character:** Pehuenche/Pampas-associated.
- **Comparative structure:** Seven-headed dragon, captive maiden, water withheld, canine helpers, false claimant, tongues as proof.
- **Evidentiary conclusion:** **[ATTESTED FOLKTALE; SACRIFICE LEGEND]**, not documented ritual practice.

### The volcanic household and escaping daughter

In “La hija del Cherruve,” a young man climbs a volcano, reaches Cherufe’s house, and asks for work. Cherufe assigns difficult or supernatural tasks. The young man becomes attached to Cherufe’s daughter; she assists him, and they flee on extraordinary horses. Cherufe’s wife pursues them.

The Cherufe here is not merely an eater of anonymous maidens but a father, husband, employer, and wealthy householder. The story belongs to the international “magical flight” and “daughter of the ogre/Devil” complex.

- **Source:** Calvún through Lenz/Chiappa, 1895–1897.
- **Lenz’s judgment:** European origin.
- **Status:** **[MAPUCHE-LANGUAGE FOLKTALE; SYNCRETISTIC]**.

### “Las apuestas”

A hunter goes to the volcanic summit and enters Cherufe’s house. Cherufe is a dangerous host or opponent; the story proceeds through contests, tricks, and exploits. It shows the being’s mountain residence, noise, fire, and household wealth but contains loosely connected episodes.

- **Source:** Lenz, 1895–1897.
- **Status:** **[ATTESTED ORAL; COMPOSITE FOLKTALE]**.

### “Las transformaciones”

The hero acquires animal powers or transformation herbs and enters a sequence of contests and relationships involving Cherufe. Lenz notes textual discontinuities and an episode in which Cherufe behaves as a sea monster revived by water. The narrative warns against constructing a single natural-history description from each incident.

- **Source:** Lenz, 1895–1897.
- **Status:** **[ATTESTED ORAL; COMPOSITE AND PARTLY EUROPEANIZED]**.

### The Cherufe stone of chiefs and warriors

In Neuquén and La Pampa traditions, Cherufe becomes or inhabits an animate stone:

- Namuncurá’s mother reportedly finds a child-shaped stone while washing by a river.
- It flies as a ball of fire.
- It feeds on blood, especially Christian blood.
- Calfucurá’s possession of a Cherufe stone explains his military success.
- Ranquel warriors Huentulaf and Curruhuil possess a human-shaped stone that tells them whether they will win.

This narrative complex links Indigenous memory of warfare and resistance to a sacred or animate object. Hernández interprets it within the historical memory of the “Conquest of the Desert,” while noting that the object is both divine presence and guarded political property.

- **Sources:** Guevara 1908/1929; Koessler-Ilg 1962; La Pampa oral archive; Hernández 1995/2006.
- **Status:** **[ARGENTINE REGIONAL TRADITION; POLITICAL-MARTIAL]**.

### The lava monster and thrown burning heads

The now-common story says that a crystalline magma monster lives inside volcanic lava, causes eruptions and earthquakes, demands virgin maidens, devours them, ignites their heads, and throws those heads from the crater as meteors.

The components have unequal histories:

- Volcano or summit residence: attested in nineteenth-century oral tales.
- Eating girls: attested in Lenz.
- Maiden surrendered to obtain water: attested in Lenz.
- Fireballs/comets: attested since 1606.
- Throwing human heads amid volcanic fire: reported in early-twentieth-century scholarly summaries of eastern traditions.
- Body literally made of magma and rock crystal: no early source located.
- Regular communal practice of throwing virgins into volcanic craters: no historical or archaeological evidence located.
- Combination of all elements into one fixed “canonical” story: **[MODERN SYNTHESIS]**.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No documented institutional cult

No Cherufe temple, sanctuary, hereditary priesthood, festival, calendar day, pilgrimage center, or standardized liturgy is documented. Saunière states directly that Cherufe, unlike Pillán in her comparison, was not an object of cult.

No securely identified Cherufe image survives from an excavated shrine. No votive inscription names it. No theophoric personal names based securely on Cherufe were established.

### Domestic and personal dealings

The closest evidence to “cult” concerns privately held stones:

- A chief, wealthy owner, warrior, or ritual specialist keeps a Cherufe stone.
- It may be fed silver or blood.
- It gives wealth, intelligence, protection, or victory.
- It may leave its owner as a fiery projectile and return.

These practices belong to the logic of a powerful familiar, talisman, or animate sacred object, not a public temple cult. Accounts differ on whether ownership belongs especially to *kalku*, chiefs, or exceptional warriors.

### Sacrifice

Two bodies of evidence must be separated:

1. **Narrative sacrifice:** In “Los dos perritos,” a father gives a girl to the seven-headed Cherufe so that water may return. This is securely documented as a tale.
2. **Historical human sacrifice to Cherufe:** **[UNESTABLISHED]**. No colonial judicial record, eyewitness testimony, archaeological context, or ritual ethnography examined here documents a recurring practice of throwing maidens into volcanoes for Cherufe.

Claims that “the Mapuche sacrificed virgins to Cherufe” transform a folktale motif into an ethnographic assertion. That move is not evidentially justified.

### Nguillatun and machitun

General Mapuche ritual ethnographies document *nguillatun*, *machitun*, offerings, healing, prayer, and communication with powers and ancestors. No dedicated Cherufe rite was established within these ceremonies. Rosales’s Caupolicán episode links tobacco incense to Pillán, not explicitly to Cherufe.

### Politics and warfare

The stone traditions give Cherufe a political history:

- Sacred possession legitimates exceptional martial success.
- Calfucurá and Namuncurá become linked to Cherufe stones in remembered accounts.
- Ranquel testimony frames the stone as an ally in resistance to Argentine forces.
- Ownership creates asymmetry: this is guarded power, not a communal object freely circulated.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1606: unattested prehistory

The word and some associated beliefs almost certainly predate Valdivia’s dictionary, but their age cannot be measured. No pre-Hispanic inscription names Cherufe. Archaeological images cannot securely be assigned to it without circular comparison.

### 1606–1777: fiery lexeme and portent

Valdivia, Febrés, and Havestadt consistently give comet/fire/exhalation meanings. Rosales’s possibly related Cheburbue adds divination and warfare. This is the earliest documentary core.

### Nineteenth century: narrative personification

By the material collected in the 1890s, Cherufe is thoroughly personified: it owns a volcanic house, has family and livestock, withholds water, eats people, conducts contests, and may have seven heads. Yet Lenz already recognized extensive European narrative influence.

### 1895–1917: folkloristic canon formation

Lenz, Saunière, Guevara, Augusta, and Outes turn dispersed oral materials into a printed Cherufe corpus. During this phase:

- The fireball and monster become systematically associated.
- Volcano and earthquake attributes are consolidated.
- “Human sacrifice” becomes an ethnological gloss.
- The European-dragon resemblance is both acknowledged and incorporated.
- The Chimpay plaque is proposed as physical iconography.

### 1903–1962: Argentine and trans-Andean documentation

Phonographic and oral records preserve the portent form. Neuquén and Pampas collections emphasize the animate stone, war, wealth, blood, and chiefs. The material demonstrates trans-Andean variation rather than one Chilean norm.

### Later twentieth century: symbolic and structural interpretation

Casamiquela, Waag, Carrasco, Hernández, and others analyze Cherufe within *wekufü* classifications, symbolism of fire, fragmented-ogre patterns, embodied sacred objects, and Indigenous memories of conquest.

Hernández, following a suggestion from Lévi-Strauss, proposes that the fiery meteor can be understood as the still-potent flying head of a fragmented ogre. She notes that Outes had independently tried to unite monster and comet through the Chimpay plaque. This is a productive **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**, not an Indigenous doctrine universally stated by informants.

### Twenty-first century: global monster

English-language encyclopedias and online summaries stabilize one vivid composite: an evil humanoid of magma and crystal living in a lava pool, causing eruptions, eating virgins, and throwing burning heads. Television and role-playing games then visualize it as a giant reptilian or rocky fire creature. This version has become globally recognizable but is much narrower—and in parts more recent—than the documentary corpus.

### Continuity and revival

Cherufe remains present in Mapuche-themed cultural materials, schoolbooks, folklore collections, fantasy art, and games. No reliable evidence was located for a distinct organized contemporary Cherufe-worship movement, temple, reconstructionist denomination, or continuous public cult. Individual spiritual interpretations cannot be excluded, but their existence or scale is **[UNESTABLISHED]**.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Chilean school and children’s literature

Blanca Santa Cruz Ossa’s *Leyendas y cuentos araucanos* (1938) belongs to the formative period of Chilean children’s folklore publishing. Such collections moved orally collected and philologically presented material into polished Spanish stories for schools and families. They materially shaped “the Chilean Cherufe,” often suppressing dialect, named informants, textual uncertainty, and the distinction between Mapuche narrative and European folktale structures.

Oreste Plath’s later geographical compilations likewise helped canonize regional beings as a national inventory of “Chilean mythology.” This cultural work is important, but the resulting catalogue should not be mistaken for an Indigenous theological canon.

### International popular mythology

Recent encyclopedias and websites frequently repeat:

- A magma-and-rock-crystal body.
- Residence in underground lava pools.
- Direct causation of eruptions and earthquakes.
- A specific appetite for virginal maidens.
- Igniting and throwing victims’ heads.

The construction draws selectively from Lenz, Augusta, eastern many-headed-monster traditions, and later visual fantasy. The exact crystalline magma anatomy appears to be **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless an older source emerges.

### Television

The animated series *The Secret Saturdays*, season 1 episode 8, “Twelve Hundred Degrees Fahrenheit” (2008), portrays a volcanic eruption releasing a fiery lizard-man called Cherufe. The episode’s official retail synopsis preserves that formulation: [Apple TV episode listing](https://tv.apple.com/do/episode/seiscientos-cincuenta-grados-centigrados/umc.cmc.6j3ozupy68amq7thacqmxtfrm?l=en-GB). This version helped internationalize the lizard/magma-monster image. **[MODERN RETELLING]**

### Games

*Pathfinder Roleplaying Game: Bestiary 5* turns Cherufe into a challenge-rating 13 evil fire magical beast: a huge clawed mountain creature with fire healing, burning attacks, thrown rocks, and a monthly earthquake power. Its rules entry is indexed by [Archives of Nethys](https://aonprd.com/Monsters.aspx?Letter=C); the full French rendering identifies *Bestiaire 5*, p. 59. **[MODERN COMMERCIAL ADAPTATION]**

Other tabletop and digital games have used the name for fire monsters or playable species. Their genealogies, alignments, statistics, invented tribal religions, and fantasy allies are not Mapuche tradition.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated contested-material search located no primary evidence that Cherufe entered Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, theosophy, the Golden Dawn, classical grimoire traditions, or a documented organized neopagan current. Claims placing it in universal occult demonologies are therefore **[UNESTABLISHED/MODERN INVENTION]** unless supported by a named modern publication.

### Political appropriation

The historically significant political use is not an extremist cult but the Argentine oral memory of Cherufe stones as sources of Indigenous military power during resistance. Conversely, colonial and later national literature could recast Indigenous beings as “demons,” “superstitions,” or picturesque monsters—categories reflecting missionary and nation-building agendas.

No documented modern extremist appropriation specific to Cherufe was located.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Cherufe fundamentally a meteor or a monster?

- **Lexical position:** Valdivia, Febrés, Havestadt, and Augusta’s first definition favor comet/fireball.
- **Narrative position:** Lenz’s tales and later folklore favor an anthropomorphic or many-headed being.
- **Integrative position:** Outes, Casamiquela, Hernández, and structuralist comparison interpret the moving fire as the monster’s head, manifestation, or material form.
- **Pluralist conclusion:** The corpus need not be reduced to one zoological identity. Fireball, being, and stone are related forms whose exact relationship varies among speakers and regions.

### Indigenous being or European dragon?

Lenz was unusually explicit: the Cherufe of “Huenchumir, son of the bear” is essentially a European dragon, while the tales generally contain recent and imported material. The seven-headed water-hoarder and maiden-rescue story also has close Old World analogues. Yet the name, fiery-portent meaning, and local volcanic geography are independently Mapuche and early colonial.

The evidence supports **syncretic composition**, not the alternatives “wholly European” or “unchanged pre-Columbian myth.”

### Were maidens historically sacrificed?

- **For:** Lenz’s tale clearly depicts a girl being surrendered to the monster, and Lenz called it a human-sacrifice myth.
- **Against extrapolation:** The source is one late folktale with dragon-story features. No corroborating ritual documentation or archaeology was found.
- **Conclusion:** Maiden sacrifice is **attested as legend**, not demonstrated as practiced Cherufe cult.

### Does the name mean “incinerator”?

Casamiquela and modern popular accounts offer “the one who exterminates with fire” or “the incinerator.” The semantic fit is plausible, especially beside Mapudungun *chewpüdün*. Colonial dictionaries, however, simply gloss the already-formed noun. The derivation remains **plausible but not securely demonstrated**.

### Is Cheburbue the same being?

Lenz and later writers accept the identification. Both names concern foreknowledge and extraordinary power. Nevertheless, Rosales’s spelling, Christian demonological framing, and lack of fireball detail leave room for doubt. It is the earliest possible narrative appearance, not the earliest unquestionable fully developed Cherufe story.

### Is Cherufe a god?

Most early sources do not call it a god, and Saunière explicitly denies a cult. One Ranquel narrator identifies it as God/Ngenechén. The correct historical answer is therefore: **usually not classified as a high god in the documentation, but divinity is explicitly asserted in one regional testimony.**

### State of the evidence

The strongest textual witnesses are:

- Colonial bilingual dictionaries.
- Rosales’s seventeenth-century chronicle.
- Lenz’s bilingual Mapudungun–Spanish texts.
- Lehmann-Nitsche’s recorded testimony.
- Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collections.
- La Pampa oral archives analyzed by Hernández.

Their limitations include missionary translation, late publication, editorial normalization, uncertain dialect transcription, European folktale circulation, and the enormous gap between pre-Hispanic material culture and named textual evidence.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1606 — [UNESTABLISHED PREHISTORY]:** No securely named precolonial Cherufe inscription or image.
- **1606 — [EARLY]:** Luis de Valdivia records *cheurvue*, “comet or fiery air.”
- **Circa 1674 — [EARLY/CONTESTED]:** Rosales completes his *Historia general*; Cheburbue appears to Caupolicán in a prophetic dream.
- **1765 — [EARLY]:** Febrés defines *cheruvoe* as comet and nocturnal fiery exhalations.
- **1777 — [EARLY]:** Havestadt records *cheurvoe* as comet and fiery nocturnal exhalation.
- **1877 — [PUBLICATION EVENT]:** Rosales’s seventeenth-century history appears in print in the edition generally cited by later scholars.
- **1895–1897 — [ATTESTED ORAL/LITERARY]:** Lenz publishes Calvún’s bilingual tales: “Las apuestas,” “Los dos perritos,” “Las transformaciones,” “La hija del Cherruve,” and “Huenchumir.”
- **1899/1908 — [SYSTEMATIZATION]:** Guevara discusses Cherufe in histories and psychology of the Araucanian people, including meteor, anthropomorphic, serpentine, and stone traditions.
- **28 January 1903 — [ATTESTED ORAL]:** Regina dictates “Chewürpue/Historia del hombre que sabía volar” in La Plata; Cherufe is a fiery omen of trouble.
- **1904 — [LEXICOGRAPHIC SYNTHESIS]:** Lenz’s etymological dictionary consolidates the colonial spellings, Rosales, Guevara, Molina, and the fire-spirit interpretation.
- **1912 — [SCHOLARLY SYNTHESIS]:** Lenz develops the association with fire, volcanoes, lightning, and earthquake traditions.
- **1916 — [LEXICOGRAPHIC/FOLKLORE]:** Augusta defines *cheurfue/cheurpue* as fireball and summarizes the volcanic girl-eating giant from Lenz.
- **1916–1917 — [FOLKLORE]:** Saunière publishes Cherufe tales and Villarrica-area descriptions; she states that Cherufe receives no cult.
- **1917 — [ARCHAEOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION]:** Outes publishes the Chimpay engraved plaque as “the materialization of the Araucanian Cherrufe.”
- **1938 — [CHILDREN’S LITERATURE]:** Blanca Santa Cruz Ossa publishes *Leyendas y cuentos araucanos*, contributing to school-age canonization.
- **1954–1962 — [NEUQUÉN COLLECTIONS]:** Koessler-Ilg publishes Mapuche oral traditions; *Tradiciones araucanas* includes extensive Cherufe-stone material.
- **1964 — [ETHNOGRAPHIC CONTEXT]:** Faron publishes *Hawks of the Sun*; later popular writers cite it while presenting Cherufe, though it does not establish a Cherufe temple cult.
- **1982 — [CLASSIFICATION]:** Else María Waag studies Cherufe among three *wekufü* entities.
- **1983 — [REGIONAL THEORY]:** Casamiquela publishes “El mito del cherrufe,” arguing for Patagonian roots and giving the “exterminator by fire” etymology.
- **1994/1996 — [SYMBOLIC ANALYSIS]:** Hugo Carrasco analyzes Cherufe within Mapuche fire symbolism.
- **1995 — [STRUCTURAL/HISTORICAL ANALYSIS]:** Hernández publishes *Nuevos estudios sobre el cherrufe araucano*.
- **2006 — [ORAL HISTORY]:** Hernández connects Cherufe-stone traditions with Ranquel memories of warfare and the “Conquest of the Desert.”
- **2008 — [TELEVISION]:** *The Secret Saturdays* depicts Cherufe as a fiery lizard-man released by a volcanic eruption.
- **2015 — [GAME ADAPTATION]:** *Pathfinder Bestiary 5* publishes Cherufe as a huge fire magical beast.
- **Present — [MODERN RECEPTION]:** Cherufe circulates internationally as a magma monster, while scholarship retains the older fireball, omen, folktale-ogre, and animate-stone variants. No documented organized modern cult has been established.
