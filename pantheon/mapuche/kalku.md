# The Kalku: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Human ritual specialist and accusatory category—“sorcerer/witch,” not a god, goddess, demon, monster, or single named supernatural being. A *kalku* may be male or female.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana*, part I (1569), canto VIII, names the fictionalized Mapuche diviner **Puchecalco**—a compound containing *calco/calcu*—as a *hechicero* and *agorero*. The first unambiguous lexical definition of the independent category is Luis de Valdivia’s Mapudungun vocabulary of **1606**, “*Calcu—hechizero … calcun/calcutun—hechizar*.”  
> **Controlling conclusion:** “The Kalku” is a misleading reification. The documented Mapuche expression is normally **kalku**, a common noun for a person accused of or reputed to practise harmful occult aggression (*kalkutun*). There is no biography, genealogy, temple cult, canonical image, or single mythic personality called “the Kalku.” Chilote *brujos*, the Recta Provincia, and the *invunche* belong to a related but historically hybrid Huilliche–Mapuche–Spanish island tradition and must not be treated as straightforward synonyms for the mainland category.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attestation]** Sixteenth- or seventeenth-century documentation.
- **[Later lexicographic/ethnographic attestation]** Missionary dictionaries, recorded narratives, or ethnography from the eighteenth through twentieth centuries.
- **[Regional variant]** A geographically particular Mapuche, Huilliche, Chilote, Puelmapu, Neuquén, Río Negro, or Rankülche account.
- **[Colonial interpretation]** A missionary or colonial translation through Christian demonology or European “witchcraft.”
- **[Folklore]** Reported legend whose existence as a story is documented, without treating its supernatural content as an ordinary historical event.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An analytical synthesis by historians, linguists, or anthropologists.
- **[Modern reinvention]** A recent fictional, occult, commercial, or internet elaboration not established in the older record.
- **[Unestablished]** Repeated in popular summaries but not securely demonstrated by the sources examined.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

### What a *kalku* is

**[Securely attested; seventeenth century to present]** Mapudungun *kalku* denotes a person believed or alleged to cause injury through occult means. The associated verb or action noun is *kalkun/kalkutun*, harmful magical aggression. Luis de Valdivia’s 1606 vocabulary gives “*Calcu—hechizero*” and *calcutun* “to bewitch”; Andrés Febrés’s 1765 *Calepino* is more explicit: “*Calcu—brujo, bruxa: calcun, calcutun—embruxar, haciendo daño*.” The gender-neutral Mapudungun noun was thus translated into both masculine and feminine Spanish forms. Joaquín García Insausti’s historical study emphasizes that *kalkutun*, rather than European “witchcraft,” is the more exact native analytical term for the alleged aggression ([García Insausti 2020](https://revistas.uns.edu.ar/csh/article/view/2850); [Valdivia facsimile](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AArte_y_gram%C3%A1tica_general_de_la_lengua_que_corre_en_todo_el_Reyno_de_Chile_%281606%29.pdf)).

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A *kalku* is therefore simultaneously:

1. an attributed type of ritual agent;
2. the supposed author of a misfortune;
3. an accusation applied to a neighbor, rival, healer, captive, outsider, or political enemy;
4. in narrative, a stock antagonist who dispatches harmful powers.

Because people generally do not publicly self-identify as *kalku*, most evidence concerns what others said about alleged witches. This asymmetry is fundamental: the archive documents accusations, diagnoses, missionary classifications, judicial statements, and folklore far more securely than it documents a self-described profession.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic fact]** *Kalku* is a Mapudungun lexical item documented since the colonial period. No transparent derivation from productive Mapudungun morphemes has been securely demonstrated.

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** Rodrigo Moulian, María Catrileo, and Pablo Landeo have proposed an Andean comparison, linking Valdivia’s *calcu* with Quechua *kawchu*, “one who bewitches/casts the evil eye,” and treating it among possible Quechua or wider Central-Andean affinities in early Mapudungun vocabulary ([Moulian, Catrileo, and Landeo 2015](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-48832015000200004&script=sci_arttext)). Some popular summaries transform this into the stronger assertion that the word is “a Puquina loan” dating from Tiwanaku. That conclusion is **not securely established** by the lexical resemblance alone: the direction, intermediary language, and date of transmission remain uncertain. It should not be presented as settled etymology.

### Is the *kalku* the “opposite of the machi”?

**[Later ethnographic systematization]** Louis Faron’s *Hawks of the Sun* (fieldwork 1952–54; publication 1964) describes a moral contrast between the healing *machi* and destructive *kalku*: the former restores health and social order; the latter exploits disturbed relations between living people, ancestors, and dangerous powers. Faron calls the *kalku* an embodiment of evil within his structural account of Mapuche morality ([Faron preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780822975571_A49442892/preview-9780822975571_A49442892.pdf)).

**[Modern ethnography; qualification]** Ana Mariella Bacigalupo likewise records the opposition in contemporary ritual discourse, but her work shows that it is not a division between two wholly different technologies. A *machi* must recognize, negotiate with, expel, redirect, or defeat the very forces attributed to a *kalku*. Knowledge capable of curing can be suspected of harming. Accusations may consequently fall on unsuccessful, rival, politically inconvenient, socially ambiguous, or feared healers. The boundary is moral and relational, not a simple opposition between “priest” and “demon” ([Bacigalupo, *Shamans of the Foye Tree*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Shamans_of_the_Foye_Tree.html?id=ORvUAAAAQBAJ)).

**[Colonial interpretation]** Missionaries often mapped both *machi* and *kalku* onto “diabolical” categories. Valdivia’s, Febrés’s, Rosales’s, and later clerical vocabulary therefore preserves valuable native terms while simultaneously distorting them through Christian demonology. “Black magic,” now common in summaries, is a modern Western gloss, not a literal translation of a native color-coded theological category.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 1569: Ercilla and Puchecalco

**[Earliest literary attestation]** In canto VIII, octaves 39–44, of part I of Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (Madrid, 1569), an aged indigenous *curaca hechicero* and *agorero* named **Puchecalco** predicts eventual Spanish domination. Tucapel kills him for the unwelcome prophecy. The name has traditionally been analyzed as containing *calcu*, “sorcerer”; later annotations compare Valdivia and Febrés. The passage proves that an expression recognizable as *calcu* was circulating in the earliest colonial encounter, but it does **not** supply an ethnographic definition of an institution. Ercilla’s episode is a Spanish epic construction shaped by classical prophecy scenes ([text and historical annotations](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-araucana-ilustraciones--0/html/016456e2-82b2-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_59.html); [study locating the passage](https://www.revistahipogrifo.com/index.php/hipogrifo/article/download/436/pdf/3493)).

There is no firm basis for treating Puchecalco as “the first Kalku,” a canonical individual, or evidence for an organized witch cult. He is an early literary *hechicero* whose name preserves the lexeme.

### 1606: Valdivia’s vocabulary and confessional

**[Earliest unambiguous lexical attestation]** Luis de Valdivia’s *Arte y gramática general de la lengua que corre en todo el Reyno de Chile, con un vocabulario y confessionario* (Lima, 1606) records *calcu* as *hechizero* and the harmful act as *calcutun*. Its physical support is a printed missionary grammar, vocabulary, catechism, and confessional. Its purpose matters: the lexicon was compiled for evangelization, and the confessional sought indigenous equivalents for prohibited religious and magical acts. It proves the word and the missionary’s semantic equation; it does not prove that Mapuche speakers understood *kalku* as a worshipper of Satan.

### Seventeenth-century chronicles and cases

**[Early attestation; colonial interpretation]** Diego de Rosales’s *Historia general del reino de Chile, Flandes Indiano*, completed in 1674 but first printed in 1877–78, describes indigenous “hechiceros” and “médicos,” instruction in caves, and alleged communication with the devil. Rosales had extensive frontier experience, but his account fuses observation, hearsay, polemic, and European demonological explanation. The manuscript’s long delay to publication must be retained in citation: evidence composed in 1674 reached print two centuries later ([Memoria Chilena](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-3356.html)).

Rosales’s famous description says that children were taught to become “*hechiceros y médicos que curan por arte del diablo*” in concealed schools or caves. **[Colonial interpretation]** The cave-school motif has an early source; “by the devil’s art” is Rosales’s Christian causal explanation, not an independently recorded Mapuche confession. Later Chilote cave traditions may resemble it, but direct institutional continuity has not been demonstrated.

**[Early colonial documentary cases]** Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century mission and administrative records describe illnesses, deaths, divination, accusations, retaliations, and political uses of magical aggression. Oriol Ambrogio argues that communities employed *machi* and *dugul* to identify alleged *kalku*, while local authorities could use accusations as political and social instruments ([Ambrogio 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353202237_Accusing_and_Identifying_theKalku_The_Perception_of_Sorcery_in_Mapuche_Society_Seventeenth_to_Eighteenth_Centuries)).

### Núñez de Pineda, Ovalle, and González de Nájera

**[Early colonial corpus]** Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán’s *Cautiverio feliz* was composed around 1673 from memories of his 1629 captivity but printed only in 1863. Alonso de Ovalle’s *Histórica relación del Reyno de Chile* appeared in Rome in 1646. Alonso González de Nájera’s early-seventeenth-century account says friendly Indigenous people called certain deceptive intermediaries *calcos*, “hechiceros.” These works attest Spanish efforts to classify diverse ritual specialists; they do not consistently preserve native distinctions among *machi*, diviners, herbalists, poisoners, prophets, and alleged aggressors.

### Eighteenth-century lexicography

**[Later lexicographic attestation]** Andrés Febrés’s *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* (Lima, 1765) distinguishes:

- *calcu*: “*brujo, bruxa*”;
- *calcun/calcutun*: “*embruxar, haciendo daño*”;
- *machi*: professional healer;
- *llihuatu/dugul*: forms associated with divination.

This is important evidence against reducing every indigenous ritual practitioner to one office. It is also a missionary document whose derisive description of *machitun* as “disparates y ridiculezas” reveals authorial hostility ([digital text](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/Ancillary/BeagleLibrary/1765_Febres_A709.html)).

Bernhard Havestadt’s *Chilidúǵu* (1777; later edition 1883) belongs to the same missionary lexicographic corpus. It supports the existence of a developed vocabulary of ritual practice but supplies no “Kalku mythology” comparable to a scripture or divine cycle.

### Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century recorded Mapuche material

**[Later ethnographic attestation]** Rodolfo Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos* (1895–97), Tomás Guevara’s *Folklore araucano* (1911), Félix José de Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) and *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916), and Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach’s publication of Pascual Coña’s testimony—*Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX* (1930)—preserve vocabulary and narratives from named Mapuche speakers.

Guevara prints a bilingual tale headed “*Una mujer calcu*,” collected at Pichún, Galvarino: a woman’s harmful knowledge is disclosed in domestic and social circumstances. Such texts are evidence for a narrative type and vocabulary, not biographies of a single supernatural Kalku ([Guevara, *Folklore araucano*](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0008877.pdf)).

**[Regional Argentine material]** Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s *Cuentan los araucanos* records twentieth-century oral narratives in Neuquén. Rankülche, Puelmapu, Río Negro, and Neuquén sources show that accusations of occult aggression crossed the modern Chile–Argentina border with Mapuche social networks. They also contain local vocabulary and narrative differences; they are not evidence for a centrally standardized pantheon.

### Modern ethnographic corpus

- **Louis C. Faron, *Hawks of the Sun* (1964):** moral and social structure of reservation communities; *machi*, *kalku*, ancestry, death, kinship, and ritual form an interdependent system.
- **María Ester Grebe:** structural accounts of Mapuche cosmology, especially oppositions among beneficent and harmful powers. These are influential scholarly models, not revealed native dogma.
- **Rolf Foerster, *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche* (1993):** religion under colonial and Christian contact, including *machi*, *kalku*, and evangelical transformations.
- **Ana Mariella Bacigalupo, *La voz del kultrun en la modernidad* (2001), *Shamans of the Foye Tree* (2007), and later work:** long-term ethnography of gender, healing, accusation, politics, and relations with spirits.
- **Joaquín García Insausti (2020 and later):** historical reconstruction of *kalkutun* across Araucanía, Pampas, and northern Patagonia, with close attention to archival accusations.
- **Oriol Ambrogio (2021):** political and social production of accusations in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Mapuche society.

### What the corpus does not contain

**[Absence of evidence]** There is no known Kalku scripture, hymn, prayer, temple inscription, priest list, sacred biography, canonical revelation, or iconographic cycle. No text presents “Kalku” as a singular proper name with a stable personality. Narrative evidence concerns particular alleged witches, anonymous witch figures, ritual diagnoses, supernatural assistants, and the consequences of accusation.

## 3. Domain, attributes, powers, and iconography

### Domain

**[Securely attested across colonial and ethnographic sources]** The attributed domain of a *kalku* is intentional harm: sickness, wasting, unexplained death, reproductive or household misfortune, damage to crops or animals, interpersonal vengeance, and occasionally wider political danger.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** These attributions operate as explanations of why misfortune struck a particular person. They also identify agency and liability, turning illness or death into a problem of damaged social relations.

### Characteristic methods

The sources attribute several methods, not all equally old or universal:

- **Sending or manipulating *wekufe/weküfe*.** **[Later ethnographic systematization]** Harmful, disordering, dangerous, or predatory nonhuman forces are sent toward a victim.
- **Intrusion into the body.** **[Ethnographic attestation]** The victim is said to contain an intrusive object or force, which a *machi* may extract during *machitun*.
- **Poison.** **[Colonial and nineteenth-century allegation]** Food, drink, plants, or prepared substances may be blamed. Judicial records sometimes collapse pharmacological poisoning and supernatural bewitchment into the same accusation.
- **Contact substances.** **[Modern ethnographic attestation]** Hair, nail clippings, clothing, belongings, grave earth, or places where the victim sits or walks may mediate aggression.
- **Soul capture or manipulation of the dead.** **[Later ethnographic attestation]** Restless or improperly separated spirits may be exploited.
- **Animal familiars or transformations.** **[Folklore/regional variants]** Owls, foxes, snakes, night birds, *chonchon/tue-tue*, or other beings may serve as disguises, omens, vehicles, or companions.
- **Nocturnal travel and luminous manifestations.** **[Folklore]** Lights such as the *anchimallén* and calls of night birds announce danger in some twentieth-century accounts.
- **Envy and commissioned revenge.** **[Ethnographic attestation]** A client may allegedly engage a harmful specialist against an enemy; jealousy, unpaid obligations, disputes over marriage, inheritance, land, livestock, prestige, and political allegiance recur as motives.

### Plants and substances

**[Regional ethnobotanical tradition]** *Latua pubiflora*, the toxic and psychoactive plant called *latúe* and sometimes *kalku-mamüll* (“kalku tree/wood”), is associated in southern Chilean lore with poisoning, altered states, and witches. Its toxicity is pharmacological fact; its universal use by *kalku* is not. Popular claims that every *kalku* prepares a standardized latúe potion exceed the evidence.

**[Unestablished]** There is no canonical list of sacred colors, numbers, weekdays, planets, metals, or ritual regalia belonging to all *kalku*. Assertions that black clothing is a required uniform are late folklore or schematic ethnographic generalization.

### Physical form and iconography

**[Absence of evidence]** A *kalku* is human and has no mandatory nonhuman anatomy. No securely identified pre-Columbian statue, rock image, textile, mask, or archaeological object depicts “the Kalku.”

**[Folklore]** Particular witches may appear as ordinary neighbors, nocturnal birds, flying heads, lights, or transformed animals. The *chonchon/tue-tue* is sometimes a witch’s transformed head or emissary, but this is a regional narrative complex, not the invariable physical form of a *kalku*.

**[Modern invention]** Horned demons, black-robed necromancers, raven masters, standardized staffs, pentagrams, or European grimoire costumes in modern art are not established Mapuche iconography.

### Limits

A *kalku* is not omnipotent. **[Ethnographic tradition]** Harm may be detected by a *machi* or diviner, extracted or redirected, countered through ritual, or defeated through protective social and ceremonial action. Accusations can also be challenged, redirected, or politically contested. The harmful agent’s dependence on secrecy is a recurrent premise.

## 4. Relations and documented interactions

### Genealogy

**[Absence of evidence]** A category of human ritual practitioner has no divine parentage, consort, offspring, siblings, mount, or fixed place in a pantheon. No early source gives a genealogy of “the Kalku.”

**[Folklore/ethnography]** Knowledge may be described as inherited within a family, learned from another practitioner, obtained through dangerous nonhuman contact, or acquired by a healer who turns toward harmful use. These are alternative vocation narratives, not biological genealogies of a supernatural being.

### The *machi* relationship

**[Securely documented relationship]** The *machi* diagnoses and combats injuries attributed to a *kalku*. Yet the two categories overlap in knowledge and capacity: a powerful healer is believed to understand harmful forces and may therefore become vulnerable to accusation. “Failed machi becomes a *kalku*” is a reported explanatory tradition, not an inevitable career path.

### Every documented interaction with named or classed nonhuman beings

Because *kalku* is a class rather than an individual, these lines record recurrent attributed relationships, not episodes from one biography.

| **Anchimallén / anchimallen** | Luminous being or familiar associated with nocturnal danger and, in some accounts, dispatched by witches. | Faron, 1964, and Chilean folklore collections | Regional folklore |

**Finding:** No documented “god–god interaction” exists because Kalku is neither a named god nor a single agent. Assigning it adventures with Mapuche deities would manufacture a mythology the sources do not supply.

## 5. Narrative complexes

### A. The unseen attack and the healer’s counteraction

**[Early attested social narrative]** A person becomes ill or dies; ordinary causes do not satisfy relatives; a *machi* or diviner is consulted; ritual diagnosis identifies occult aggression and sometimes names or describes an aggressor; kin then seek cure, restitution, counter-sorcery, expulsion, or vengeance.

This is the central Kalku “story,” but it is a recurring social sequence rather than a fixed myth. Colonial records and later ethnography vary concerning who diagnoses, what evidence counts, whether an intrusive object is extracted, and what sanction follows.

Ambrogio’s study of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century materials argues that identifying a *kalku* was a socially productive act. Authorities and ritual specialists could use accusations to explain disaster, manage factional conflict, discipline local enemies, or consolidate authority. García Insausti similarly treats *kalkutun* as embedded in obligations and retaliation across the Araucanía–Pampas–Patagonia region rather than as an isolated “superstition.”

### B. The accused healer

**[Colonial and modern attestation]** The *machi* and *kalku* are not stable hereditary castes with mutually exclusive technologies. The healer’s ability to recognize occult aggression implies knowledge of its operation. Failed cures, deaths, unusual gender presentation, rivalries, payment disputes, or political conflict can turn admired power into suspected harmful power.

Bacigalupo shows that colonial and national ideologies gendered this suspicion. Spanish writers represented male *machi*, especially *machi weye*, through European categories of effeminacy, sodomy, witchcraft, and devil worship. Women healers were likewise exposed to the imported figure of the witch. These representations changed the social field; they were not neutral translations of an unchanged precolonial binary ([Bacigalupo working paper](https://mapuche.info/wps_pdf/baciga03030E.pdf)).

### C. Cave instruction

**[Early colonial report; later folklore]** Rosales’s 1674 narrative describes hidden cave schools in which “sorcerers and doctors” instructed the young. Later mainland folklore speaks of *renü* or hidden places where ritual experts assemble.

**[Regional Chilote elaboration]** The Cave of Quicaví becomes the headquarters of the Chilote brotherhood and dwelling of the *invunche*. Similarity does not by itself prove that one continuous organization existed from Rosales’s seventeenth-century mainland account to the nineteenth-century island society.

### D. The Moraleda–Chillpila contest

**[Late Chilote foundation legend]** José Manuel de Moraleda y Montero surveyed Chiloé between 1787 and 1796. In later tradition, the Huilliche *machi* Chillpila challenges him to a contest of powers, defeats or matches him, and receives a European book of magic. From this conjunction of indigenous power and imported occult learning the Chilote brotherhood is said to arise.

The documentary Moraleda was skeptical about *machitun* and wrote that local people avoided demonstrating it to him because they considered him an even greater *machi*, diviner, or witch. The duel and gift of the magic book do not occur as a verified event in that observation. They are a later narrative appropriation of a real visitor, explaining the mixed indigenous-European character of Chilote witchcraft.

### E. Becoming a Chilote *brujo*

**[Folklore; collected chiefly in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries]** Initiation narratives require secrecy, repudiation of Christian sacraments, ordeals involving cold water or waterfalls, attendance at a cave, acquisition of a *macuñ* or magical garment, and proof that the candidate can fly or perform harm. Details vary sharply by collector and should not be retrojected wholesale into 1880 testimony.

### F. Making and using the *invunche*

**[Chilote folklore; documented by 1880/1914]** A stolen or surrendered infant is allegedly deformed: one leg is fastened behind the neck, joints are twisted, and the child is confined in a cave. It guards or advises the witches and may be beaten or driven toward a target as an instrument of harm. Francisco Cavada’s 1914 account calls it *Invunche/Ivunche*, also *Vuta* or *Vuta-macho*, and expressly says it is a consultant and instrument rather than chief of the coven ([Cavada 1914](https://www.museodeancud.gob.cl/sites/www.museodeancud.gob.cl/files/images/articles-77081_archivo_01.pdf)).

No physical specimen, victim record, or independent corroboration establishes the manufacture of such a being. Its documented reality is as judicial testimony and enduring legend. Claims of infant flesh as its diet belong to the same folkloric dossier and are not verified criminal facts.

## 6. Cult, ritual, and institutional practice

### No cult of “the Kalku”

**[Absence of evidence]** There are no temples, priesthoods, festivals, public sacrifices, votive inscriptions, pilgrimages, canonical prayers, or recognized communal worship of a being called Kalku. The noun refers to a feared human agent, usually identified by others.

### Ritual setting

**[Ethnographic attestation]** Kalku belief appears most visibly in healing and divination:

1. sickness or misfortune is interpreted;
2. a *machi* or other specialist enters altered ritual consciousness, prays, sings, drums, or divines;
3. the injury may be personified as *wekufe* or embodied as an intrusive object;
4. the healer extracts, expels, fights, or redirects it;
5. kin deliberate about the alleged human source and appropriate response.

A *machitun* is a healing rite, not worship of a *kalku*. A *nguillatun* is a collective ceremony of prayer, renewal, reciprocity, and communal order; it is not a witches’ festival. Kalku imagery enters these settings as the counterforce against which healing and social integration are enacted.

### Sacrifice and violence

**[No documented Kalku cult sacrifice]** Warfare killings, colonial reports of execution or sacrifice, and the 1960 Lago Budi child-sacrifice case cannot be assigned to a “cult of Kalku” without evidence. The relevant Kalku violence is instead accusatory and punitive: alleged witches could be beaten, expelled, or killed.

**[Historical attestation with source bias]** Colonial and nineteenth-century observers report executions of suspected witches. Such acts belong to systems of kin liability, retaliation, diagnosis, and political authority. Missionaries and officials also prosecuted Indigenous ritual practitioners under Christian and state categories. Both indigenous punishment of alleged aggressors and colonial persecution of ritual experts must be kept in view.

### The Recta Provincia

**[Historical attestation]** Chiloé’s strongest institutional evidence is the 1880–81 judicial record. In April 1880 Intendant Luis Martiniano Rodríguez ordered arrests of persons reputed to be *machis* or witches. Witnesses described a body called **La Mayoría**, **Recta Provincia**, or **Tribunal de la Raza Indígena**, with kings, subdelegates, territorial divisions, initiation, healing, punishment, and the Cave of Quicaví. The investigation began in 1880; Judge Beytía issued a first-instance judgment on 2 February 1881. Ramón Espech copied important portions for Benjamín Vicuña Mackenna; these copies, not an untouched complete archive, underlie the best-known publication ([*Proceso de los brujos de Chiloé*](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0008651.pdf)).

**[What is securely historical]**

- arrests and interrogations occurred;
- named witnesses testified;
- some witnesses described offices and territorial organization;
- officials investigated poisonings, killings, intimidation, illicit association, and concealment;
- the proceedings became the foundation of much later Chilote witch lore.

**[What remains contested]**

- whether testimony was voluntary, coerced, strategically shaped, or mutually accusatory;
- how centralized and durable the organization actually was;
- whether “Recta Provincia,” “La Mayoría,” and “Tribunal de la Raza Indígena” always denoted precisely the same thing;
- which alleged deaths were ordinary poisoning or homicide, which were rumor, and which accusations could be proved;
- whether officials encountered a criminal society, an indigenous parallel system of justice and healing, a loose reputation network, or some combination.

Gonzalo Rojas Flores argues that the judicial discourse itself helped constitute the Recta Provincia as a legible organization: the tribunal translated reciprocal obligations, ritual authority, and island networks into the modern state’s categories of conspiracy and crime ([Rojas Flores, chapter “El kalku o agente maléfico”](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0037757.pdf)). This does not mean the organization was invented from nothing; it means the record is an interaction between island testimony and state prosecution.

### Economics and politics

**[1880 testimony]** Healers and alleged witches received payment, answered requests, adjudicated conflicts, and were accused of charging for cures or injuries. Titles such as “King of Quicaví” and the mapping of island districts onto names such as Lima, Santiago, Peru, or Buenos Aires combined parody, alternative sovereignty, memory, and administrative imitation.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The Recta Provincia can be read as an indigenous or subaltern institution operating where formal courts, medicine, communications, and policing were distant or mistrusted. It can also have contained coercion, extortion, poisoning, and personal vendetta. These readings are not mutually exclusive.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1569: reconstructable limits

**[Absence of direct evidence]** Archaeology establishes long indigenous occupation and complex ritual landscapes in south-central Chile, but it cannot securely identify a pre-Columbian object or structure as “Kalku.” Tom Dillehay’s work on monuments, political landscapes, and resistance supplies broad Mapuche historical context, not a material cult of the witch.

The existence of the lexeme by the early contact period makes a wholly post-Conquest invention unlikely. The precise precolonial meanings and institutional boundaries remain unrecoverable.

### 1569–1606: first textual visibility

Ercilla uses a *calcu*-bearing personal name in epic literature; Valdivia fixes *calcu* and *calcutun* in missionary print. The category is already associated by Spaniards with divination and harmful enchantment.

### Seventeenth century: demonological translation and political accusation

Missionaries translate indigenous ritual practice through the Devil, witchcraft, superstition, and idolatry. Indigenous communities continue to diagnose harm through their own specialists. Accusations enter negotiations among kin groups, chiefs, missionaries, and colonial officers.

The important historical change is not simply “Christianity replaces paganism.” Christian demonology supplies new language and sanctions, while Mapuche interpretations absorb, resist, or repurpose Christian figures. *Wekufe* may be glossed “demon,” but the equivalence is imperfect.

### Eighteenth century: lexical differentiation and frontier cases

Febrés distinguishes healer, diviner, and harmful practitioner. Mission records and legal cases show occult aggression implicated in factional politics and relations with colonial settlements. Historians find accusations particularly valuable because they expose otherwise poorly documented local conflicts.

### Late eighteenth to nineteenth century: Chiloé hybridization and trans-Andean reach

Moraleda’s real voyages become the seed of a foundation legend. In Araucanía and across the Andes, *kalkutun* remains embedded in illness, death, warfare, captivity, alliance, and vengeance. Argentine materials record accusations among Rankülche and other Mapuche-connected peoples.

The Chilean and Argentine campaigns of territorial occupation disrupted autonomous political systems and altered the authority of *lonko*, ritual experts, and kin groups. Missionization, settlement, schooling, policing, and biomedicine did not simply erase occult explanations; they changed the settings in which accusations could be made and punished.

### 1880–81: state prosecution in Chiloé

The mass arrests and trial made the Recta Provincia a documentary object. The state sought to suppress an allegedly autonomous authority and prosecute ordinary crimes. Subsequent newspapers, historians, folklorists, and writers selected the most sensational testimony—flight, magic garments, poison, cave, and *invunche*—thereby transforming a legal archive into a national mythology.

### 1890s–1930s: salvage ethnography and printed folklore

Lenz, Guevara, Augusta, Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, and Moesbach/Coña recorded vocabularies and narratives during intense territorial dispossession and assimilation. Their works preserve otherwise vulnerable testimony but also reflect missionary, racial, evolutionary, and folklorist assumptions.

Cavada’s 1914 synthesis helped stabilize the popular Chilote image of the *invunche*. Oreste Plath and later collectors nationalized regional material as “Chilean mythology,” sometimes smoothing distinctions among Mapuche, Huilliche, Chilote, Spanish, and wider European traditions.

### 1950s–1990s: anthropological system-building

Faron interpreted *machi* and *kalku* within a moral, kinship, and ancestral system. Structural cosmologies associated good/bad, upper/lower, health/illness, and social integration/disintegration. Such models clarified recurrent oppositions but could over-systematize variable local discourse.

Foerster foregrounded colonial Christianity and contemporary religious change. Bacigalupo’s work shifted attention toward lived practice, gender, agency, political history, and the unstable boundary between healing and harming.

### Twenty-first century

**[Living belief]** Sorcery remains meaningful to some Mapuche and Chilote people, while others interpret it as folklore, reject it as Christian error, understand it psychologically, or use the vocabulary metaphorically. No single modern Mapuche position exists.

**[Modern accusations]** Accusations remain potentially dangerous. A 2007 statement associated with a Mapuche territorial conflict publicly accused named people of using *kalkutun* to cause a *machi*’s death, demonstrating that the category can still enter political disputes ([Ragintulewfu statement](https://mapuche.info/archivo/mapu/ragintulewfu070519.html)). Such statements document the accusation, not the alleged supernatural causation.

**[Modern legal and ethical debate]** Bacigalupo and Fabien Le Bonniec examine conflicts between Mapuche shamanic justice, state law, discrimination, and colonial history. The central difficulty is avoiding two reductions: treating supernatural causation as a proven forensic fact, or treating indigenous legal-religious concepts as meaningless irrationality ([Bacigalupo and Le Bonniec](https://nomadias.uchile.cl/index.php/NO/article/download/77893/78855/305648)).

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Folklore publishing

Twentieth-century folklore collections transformed locally variable accusations into a catalog of named “mythological beings.” In that process *kalku* was increasingly presented as if it were a monster species or pantheon entry rather than a human and relational category.

### Occult and esoteric appropriation

**[Unestablished as tradition]** Searches for a distinct historical Kalku cult within Renaissance hermeticism, theosophy, Golden Dawn ritual, ceremonial magic, or pre-twentieth-century European grimoires produce no reliable evidence. Modern sites that give the Kalku sigils, planetary correspondences, tarot cards, ceremonial ranks, or a universal “dark path” are modern inventions unless they cite recent practitioners who explicitly created them.

**[Modern hybridization]** Chilean and international occult writers sometimes merge Mapuche terms with European witchcraft, demonology, neo-shamanism, New Age energy language, and generic “black magic.” This is a datable modern reception, not recovery of an ancient secret order.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist use

**[Evidence insufficient]** Individual practitioners may adopt *kalku* imagery, but no large, documented “Kalku religion” or continuous public worship organization is established. Mapuche religious revitalization generally centers community, land, language, ancestors, *machi*, *ngillatun*, and territorial relations—not veneration of *kalku*.

### Literature, comics, cinema, and games

**[Modern reinvention]** Germán Acuña’s Chilean-Brazilian animated feature *Nahuel y el libro mágico* premiered at Annecy in 2020 and received its Chilean theatrical release in 2022. Its villain **Kalku** is a singular sorcerer who commands crows and seeks a magic book. This is a deliberate fantasy character built from the category, not an ancient named being ([Latido Films](https://latidofilms.com/films/nahuel-and-the-magic-book/); [director interview](https://remezcla.com/features/film/interview-nahuel-libro-magico-mapuche-animation/)).

The 2023 graphic novel *Cazador de Kalku: la ira de Quetralco* likewise turns the category into adventure-fantasy vocabulary. Role-playing supplements and internet bestiaries increasingly classify “Kalku” as a villain class or monster, often pairing it automatically with an *invunche*. These are modern genre conventions.

### Political and nationalist uses

Chilean national folklore has marketed Chiloé as a mysterious “land of witches,” useful for tourism and regional identity. The trope can preserve local memory but can also exoticize Huilliche and rural people.

Colonial missionaries used the “witch” label to subordinate indigenous religious authority; later national writers used superstition as evidence of backwardness; contemporary revitalization may reverse that judgment and treat occult knowledge as resistance. None of these ideological uses is a neutral description.

### Commercialization

The Kalku name appears in fiction, illustration, games, tourism, crafts, and online “mythology” content. Commercial images usually lack historical iconographic authority. A raven-controlling wizard, for example, owes more to modern fantasy design than to a standard Mapuche representation.

## 9. Contested-material audit

### Origin

- **Supported:** the word existed by the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries.
- **Plausible but unprovable:** related concepts predate Spanish invasion.
- **Disputed:** an Andean lexical affinity.
- **Not established:** a specifically Puquina loan from the Tiwanaku period.
- **False framing:** “Kalku was an ancient Mapuche god.”

### Syncretism

- **Documented:** missionaries equated harmful specialists and sometimes *machi* with European witches or servants of the Devil.
- **Documented:** Chilote lore combines Huilliche–Mapuche concepts, Spanish Catholicism, European witch vocabulary, colonial administration, and island history.
- **Not established:** exact equivalence of *wekufe* with Christian demons or of *kalku* with Satanists.

### Human sacrifice

- **No evidence:** there was no documented institutional “Kalku cult” demanding human sacrifices.
- **Folklore:** manufacture and feeding of an *invunche* involves abducted or surrendered children.
- **Historically documented but distinct:** executions of people accused as witches; occasional ritual killings elsewhere in Mapuche history must be treated case by case and not assigned automatically to *kalku*.

### Poisoning

- **Documented allegation:** colonial and Chiloé sources repeatedly associate witches with poison.
- **Possible ordinary mechanism:** some plants are genuinely toxic, and some deaths could have resulted from poisoning.
- **Not demonstrated wholesale:** testimony that a death was caused by a *kalku* does not establish either poison or supernatural action.
- **1880 evidentiary caution:** defendants and witnesses had incentives to shift blame, repeat rumor, satisfy interrogators, or redescribe local disputes.

### Suppression

- **Documented:** missionary condemnation, church discipline, colonial proceedings, Chilean state policing, the 1880 arrests, and broader campaigns of assimilation constrained indigenous specialists.
- **Qualification:** colonial Chile did not simply reproduce the mass European witch hunts. Prosecution was uneven and often intertwined with frontier policy, evangelization, local homicide, poison allegations, and indigenous accusation.

### Occultism and neopaganism

- **No evidence:** Kalku appears in Renaissance or classical Western occult orders.
- **Modern invention:** sigils, planetary tables, demon rankings, internet spell systems, and “ancient Kalku initiations” without primary citations.
- **Living tradition:** belief in harmful agency and ritual counteraction persists among some communities; that should not be confused with commercial occult branding.

### Modern accusations

- **Documented fact:** people are still called *kalku* or accused of *kalkutun*.
- **Potential consequences:** stigma, fractured kinship, political accusation, threats, or violence.
- **Methodological rule:** report who made the accusation, when, against whom, and in what conflict; do not repeat the alleged occult act as established fact.

## 10. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Translation: witchcraft, sorcery, or *kalkutun*?

Anthropology traditionally distinguishes “witchcraft” as innate psychic power from “sorcery” as learned technique. Mapuche evidence does not reliably conform to that imported distinction. *Kalku* may be learned, inherited, attributed, commissioned, spirit-mediated, poisonous, or transformative. García Insausti therefore retains *kalkutun* to avoid forcing the material into European categories.

### Functionalist and structural accounts

Faron read sorcery within kinship, morality, ancestral relations, and social integration. Grebe’s cosmological models emphasized balanced oppositions. Their enduring contribution is to show that Kalku belief was not a disconnected curiosity. The criticism is that tidy diagrams can turn contingent, contested local claims into a fixed theology.

### Historical-anthropological accounts

Ambrogio and García Insausti treat accusations as historically situated acts. Rather than asking only “what did people believe witches could do?”, they ask who accused whom, which specialist authenticated the charge, which authority enforced it, and how invasion, missions, warfare, and state formation changed those processes.

### Gender and sexuality

Bacigalupo demonstrates that colonial and national observers sexualized ritual difference. Missionaries’ descriptions of male *machi* as effeminate witches or diabolic agents reveal European gender politics as much as Mapuche categories. Modern scholarship therefore rejects a timeless equation of male healer, homosexuality, deviance, and *kalku*.

### Chiloé: secret government or judicial construction?

One position treats Recta Provincia testimony as evidence of a strongly organized parallel government with defined offices and territorial jurisdiction. A more critical position emphasizes interrogation, rumor, incomplete archival survival, and the state’s need to render heterogeneous practices as an “illicit association.” The most defensible conclusion is intermediate: the record supports organized networks and recognized offices, but the perfectly centralized occult state of later popular literature is an elaboration.

### Primary-evidence condition

The major evidentiary problems are:

- missionary sources translate native categories polemically;
- chronicles mix eyewitness material with literary conventions;
- oral texts were transcribed through collectors’ orthographies and editorial choices;
- the 1880 archive survives through selections and copies;
- alleged witches rarely speak outside hostile or coercive settings;
- material culture cannot securely identify an invisible practice;
- later encyclopedias recycle one another and often promote late folklore into “ancient mythology.”

The greatest discoveries were therefore textual rather than archaeological: Valdivia’s and Febrés’s vocabularies, publication of Rosales’s manuscript, collection of Mapudungun narratives by Lenz, Augusta, Guevara, and Moesbach, and recovery/publication of the Chiloé proceedings.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

1. **“Kalku is a god/demon.”** Unsupported; it is principally a human category.
2. **“Every machi is benevolent and every kalku belongs to a separate evil priesthood.”** Over-systematized.
3. **“The word is definitively Puquina and proves Tiwanaku control of Mapuche religion.”** Unproven.
4. **“All kalku have an invunche.”** Chilote folklore generalized to the entire Mapuche world.
5. **“The Recta Provincia ruled Chiloé unchanged for three centuries.”** Later traditional chronology unsupported by continuous records.
6. **“The 1880 trial proved flying garments and cave monsters.”** It proved that testimony about them entered a judicial proceeding.
7. **“Black robes, crows, occult books, and fixed sigils are ancient Kalku attributes.”** Modern fantasy or esoteric invention.
8. **“Kalku belief disappeared with Christianity or biomedicine.”** Contradicted by twentieth- and twenty-first-century ethnography.
9. **“Any present accusation represents all Mapuche people.”** False and potentially defamatory.

## 12. Final assessment

The fullest defensible history is not the life of a supernatural individual. It is the history of a word, a feared agency, a recurrent accusation, and the changing institutions that interpreted it.

An early Mapudungun category became visible through Spanish epic and missionary lexicography. Colonial writers translated it as witchcraft and devilry, while indigenous communities used diagnoses of *kalkutun* within illness, kinship, retaliation, and authority. Later ethnographers placed the *kalku* opposite the healing *machi*, although close study reveals a porous boundary grounded in shared knowledge and disputed moral use. Across the Andes, the category travelled with Mapuche and related peoples without becoming a uniform office.

In Chiloé, Huilliche–Mapuche concepts entered a distinctive island synthesis with Catholicism, European witch lore, colonial administration, poisoning allegations, and local systems of authority. The 1880–81 proceedings securely establish arrests, testimony, named offices, and belief in La Mayoría or Recta Provincia. They do not independently verify magical flight, the physical *invunche*, or a centuries-old centralized occult government. Later collectors and popular culture converted the archive into one of Chile’s most durable bodies of folklore.

The historical Kalku is therefore neither merely imaginary nor a factually demonstrated supernatural operator. Its documented existence lies in language, belief, accusation, ritual response, social consequence, judicial intervention, ethnography, and modern art. That record is extensive; the singular divine being called “The Kalku” is modern reification.
