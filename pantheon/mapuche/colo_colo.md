# Colo Colo: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A dangerous folkloric creature or monstrous animal, not a god, goddess, ancestor, *ngen*, or securely attested *wekufü*. Mapuche attribution is common in twentieth-century folklore literature, but the earliest detailed records come from Chilean Spanish-speaking rural tradition and must not automatically be treated as pre-contact Mapuche religion.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation of the supernatural creature:** José Silvestre, “Algo de mitología zoológica en Ovalle,” published in the newspaper *El Obrero* of Ovalle on **19 February 1904**, preserved through Julio Vicuña Cifuentes’s transcription. It describes a house-dwelling, elongated mouse-like being with a piglike snout that consumes spittle, induces salivation, and slowly kills the inhabitants. Rodolfo Lenz’s approximately contemporary lexicographical entry of 1904 records *colocolo* only as a wildcat and a historical chief, not as this monster. Claims of a colonial attestation of the creature remain unproved.
>
> **Evidentiary conclusion:** The rat-serpent-rooster-egg composite familiar today is not one stable ancient account. It is a twentieth-century synthesis of several regional Chilean traditions: an unseen saliva-drinker; mouse, bird, bat, or lizard forms; an animal developing from a supposed rooster’s egg; and the European-derived basilisk/cockatrice complex. The human Mapuche leader Colocolo and the football club named for him are distinct from the creature.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early folklore attestation]** Printed or reported testimony from 1904–1915.
- **[Later folklore]** Subsequent oral collection or folklore synthesis.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally recorded version not safely generalizable to all Mapuche, Huilliche, Chilote, or Chilean traditions.
- **[Learned systematization]** A collector’s organization, explanation, or cross-cultural comparison rather than an informant’s own statement.
- **[Syncretistic interpretation]** A historically proposed mixture or equation with another tradition.
- **[Literary elaboration]** An author’s reshaping of folklore in fiction.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern attempt to infer origins or relationships.
- **[Modern invention or unsupported claim]** A proposition not established in the accessible primary record.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

### Identity

**[Early folklore attestation]** The Colo Colo is a harmful domestic or peri-domestic creature believed to cause wasting illness and death. Its central act is not battle, judgment, or governance but predation by contagious or sympathetic contact: it drinks a sleeper’s saliva, consumes spittle or food remnants, or—less commonly—sucks blood. Victims waste away, become feverish, or die of what informants identified with *tisis*, consumption.

**[Early folklore attestation]** Julio Vicuña Cifuentes’s geographically labeled testimonies show that the creature had no universally recognized body:

- La Serena: a lizard that sucks sleepers’ blood.
- Santiago and Talagante: a fierce little mouse living near houses; whoever loses saliva to it becomes thin, disfigured, and dies unless the animal is killed.
- Malloco: a black bird about the size of a *chercán*, or house wren.
- Quella: a batlike bird entering bedrooms by night.
- Coihueco de Chillán: a mouse whose drinking of saliva or eating of leftovers causes fatal illness.
- Coelemu: an unseen “very evil animal” whose cry says *colo-colo* and whose saliva-drinking causes fatal fever or “consumption.”

Vicuña said that another twenty-seven reports collected between Valparaíso and Concepción agreed about its lethal nature, although only five included the cry. His conclusion was therefore cautious: for most informants it was not a visible zoological species but “*un animal muy malo que nadie ha visto*”—“a very evil animal that nobody has seen.” The complete passage is preserved in his [*Mitos y supersticiones*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3a/Mitos_y_supersticiones_recogidos_de_la_tradici%C3%B3n_oral_chilena_con_referencias_comparativas_a_los_de_otros_paises_latinos_%28IA_mitosysuperstici00vicu%29.pdf).

### Names and spellings

**[Attested orthographic variants]**

- *Colocolo*
- *Colo colo*
- *Colo-colo*
- Modern Mapudungun-style normalization occasionally gives *kolo-kolo*, but no early creature text examined here is written in a standardized Mapudungun orthography.

The repeated form lends itself to an onomatopoeic explanation: the creature cries “*colo-colo*.” This explanation is explicitly present in the Coelemu testimony and later reference literature. It does not prove that the word originated with the monster.

### Etymology and homonyms

Three historically distinct referents must be separated.

1. **Wildcat.** **[Secure colonial lexicography]** Andrés Febrés’s eighteenth-century Mapudungun vocabulary gave *codcod* or *colocolo* as “gato montés.” Lenz repeated this in 1904, with variants *codcod* and *coll-coll*, and referred to the animal later classified as *Felis colocolo*. See the transcribed [Lenz entry](https://diccionariodechile.cl/entrada/lenz-1904/201-colocolo).

2. **Human leader.** **[Secure sixteenth-century historical-literary attestation]** Colocolo is the name of a Mapuche leader in early colonial chronicles and, most influentially, Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana*. He is a human political and literary figure. Ercilla’s wise elder, the chronicle traditions about a chief of the same name, and later nationalist representations belong to that person’s dossier, not to the monster’s.

3. **Folkloric creature.** **[Early folklore attestation]** The lethal creature is securely documented only at the opening of the twentieth century. The creature may have inherited a pre-existing animal name; alternatively, its name may have been explained through its cry. The evidence does not demonstrate a single original derivation.

**[Disputed etymology]** Later lexicographers proposed that the monster arose from popular interpretation of a real small mammal whose call sounded like *col-col-col*, or from confusion among the colocolo wildcat, the kodkod/güiña, burrowing rodents, and small marsupials. These are naturalistic explanations of a belief, not primary traditions about the creature. Manuel Antonio Román distinguished a calling mouse from Molina’s wildcat, while later writers variously identified the supposed animal with a *yaca* or mouse opossum.

**[Modern invention or unsupported claim]** Exact translations such as “goddess of fever and death,” “Mapuche vampire god,” or a derivation from a Mapudungun verb meaning “to crawl” are not supported by the early corpus located here.

### Classification through time

- **1904–1915:** malignant animal, hidden house pest, or uncanny creature.
- **1920s folklore and fiction:** supernatural rat-bird-serpent, sometimes born through an anomalous egg.
- **Mid-century folklore systematization:** “myth,” “monster,” and sometimes a being of “Araucanian origin.”
- **Contemporary popular summaries:** frequently described as a Mapuche demon, monster, cryptid, or *wekufü*.

**[Modern scholarly caution]** *Wekufü* is a genuine Mapuche category of harmful force or agent, but no early Colo Colo testimony found here explicitly calls it *wekufü*. Applying that category retroactively may be interpretively reasonable in some modern Mapuche contexts, but it is not a documented early classification.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Before the monster: colonial sources

**[Secure negative finding]** The creature does not appear under this name in the searchable evidence from the standard early corpus examined for this dossier:

- Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (parts published 1569, 1578, and 1589) contains the human Colocolo.
- Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán’s *Cautiverio feliz* (written in the seventeenth century) supplies extensive information about Mapuche society and religion but no verified Colo Colo monster episode.
- Diego de Rosales’s *Historia general del Reino de Chile* (seventeenth century) records *cod-cod/coll-coll* as an animal name in later lexicographical citation, not the saliva-drinking monster.
- Febrés’s *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* and vocabulary (1765) records the wildcat word.
- Bernardo Havestadt’s *Chilidúgú* (1777) does not furnish a verified monster narrative.
- Félix José de Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) and *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916) provide an exceptionally important Mapudungun corpus but no securely located early narrative establishing the modern creature complex.

Absence from a dictionary cannot prove absence from oral tradition. It does, however, prevent presenting the complete modern legend as securely pre-contact or colonial Mapuche doctrine.

### 1904: José Silvestre

**[Earliest secure folklore attestation]** José Silvestre’s “Algo de mitología zoológica en Ovalle,” *El Obrero*, Ovalle, 19 February 1904, describes the Colo Colo as living indoors like mice, cackling like a laying hen, shaped like an elongated mouse with a pig’s snout and sparse hair, eating spittle, and provoking salivation until household members waste away and die. An unhealthy house could consequently be said to “have Colo Colo.”

This version contains the saliva, domestic hiding place, wasting illness, mouse form, porcine snout, and avian vocalization. It does **not**, in the quotation preserved by Vicuña, say that a rooster lays its egg.

### 1904: Rodolfo Lenz

**[Secure lexicographical evidence]** Lenz’s *Diccionario etimológico de las voces chilenas derivadas de lenguas indígenas americanas* gives *colocólo* as the popular name of a wildcat and as the name of the famous leader. It cites Febrés’s *codcod o colocolo—gato montés*. The failure to include the monster is significant because Lenz was deeply engaged with Chilean popular speech and Mapudungun. It suggests that the creature meaning was then regional or not yet firmly lexicalized.

### 1908: Tomás Guevara

**[Early ethnographic systematization]** Guevara’s *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* (Santiago: Imprenta Cervantes, 1908) gives the important metamorphic account later quoted by Vicuña: the creature begins in an anomalous egg attributed by popular belief to a rooster; solar heat produces a snake or lizard; this later becomes an animal like a feathered rat. It lives in caves near houses, licks spittle and eating utensils, and through sympathetic magic causes wasting and death. Suspect “rooster eggs” are therefore burned.

Guevara is the first securely dated source located here that joins the egg, reptile, rat, feathers, household proximity, indirect ingestion, and death complex. The digitized volume is catalogued by Chile’s National Library at [Memoria Chilena](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-9870.html).

His work must be read critically. It was written immediately after the Chilean occupation and colonization of Mapuche territory, within an evolutionary and racialized anthropology. A modern historiographical assessment shows how Guevara’s categories served a colonial “national convenience” project; see Rodolfo Mardones’s [critical study](https://www.scielo.cl/article_plus.php?lng=es&pid=S0719-05812017000200165&tlng=es).

### 1910/1915: Julio Vicuña Cifuentes

**[Early folklore corpus]** Vicuña first circulated related folklore studies around 1910 and published the enlarged *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* in 1915. His six localized summaries and twenty-seven additional reports are the strongest evidence for geographic variation.

Vicuña also supplies an Angostura de Paine version in which the creature is very ugly and ratlike, and preserves Silvestre’s 1904 Ovalle report. His collection demonstrates that the belief was not confined to Mapuche-speaking communities: it circulated broadly through central Chilean rural and urbanizing popular culture.

### 1914: Francisco J. Cavada and the Chilote basilisk

**[Regional Chilote parallel]** Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes* documents the Chilote basilisk complex rather than an independently named Colo Colo cult. The work’s first edition appeared in 1914; its bibliographic and digitization history is documented by [Chiloé Omeka](https://chiloe.omeka.net/items/show/4).

Cavada explains that the supposed rooster’s egg is really a small, yolkless egg from an old or exhausted hen, popularly attributed to a rooster. The resulting basilisk belongs to a European-derived monster tradition reshaped in Chiloé. Later authors compare or conflate it with the Colo Colo.

### 1923: Ricardo E. Latcham

**[Learned systematization]** Latcham’s “Mitos araucanos” argues that the animal name *colocolo* was transferred to another myth and relates the being to an animal used by sorcerers. This is an important stage in presenting dispersed folklore as an “Araucanian” mythological system, but it postdates the earliest collections and should not be treated as independent ancient testimony. The digitized article is available from the [Revista Chilena de Historia Natural](https://rchn.biologiachile.cl/pdfs/1923/1/Latcham_1923.pdf).

### 1926: Manuel Rojas, “El colocolo”

**[Literary elaboration]** Rojas’s story appeared in *La Nación* on 19 September 1926. Its publication information and complete text are preserved by [Lecturia](https://lecturia.org/cuentos-y-relatos/manuel-rojas-el-colocolo/5947/).

In the embedded narrative:

- A family moves into a house already said to “have colocolo.”
- The father develops fever, sunken eyes, severe coughing, and wasting.
- He says a feathered, sharp-snouted mouse sits on his face and drinks his saliva.
- A knowledgeable old woman says the creature resembles neither rat nor bird, cries like a newborn, and is born from a bad hen’s egg carried off and incubated by a snake.
- Flour is spread to discover its tracks.
- Its hole is to be filled with paraffin mixed with holy water.
- The sons wound something matching the description, but it escapes; the father dies.
- The frame narrative ends by undercutting certainty through fear, drink, and mistaken perception.

This is fiction, not a field transcript. It nevertheless became one of the creature’s most influential textual forms and probably helped standardize details that popular summaries now present as anonymous antiquity.

### Later folklore corpora

**[Later folklore]** Oreste Plath’s *Folklore chileno* and *Geografía del mito y la leyenda chilenos* reorganized earlier collections into a national folklore geography. The unfinished mid-century *Enciclopedia Chilena* summarizes the creature from Atacama to Llanquihue as a largely unseen bringer of domestic misfortune, recognizable by a clocklike *colo-colo* sound, and explicitly compares its rooster-egg birth with the basilisk. Its article is available through [Wikisource](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Enciclopedia_Chilena/Folclore/Colocolo%2C_El).

**[Argentine regional material]** Argentine and Neuquén compilations record a feather-covered rat and, in some traditions, a nocturnal bird associated with a *kalku* whose call foretells a death within a year. These variants should not be collapsed into the Chilean saliva-drinker without an exact text and informant history. The accessible search evidence did not verify that Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s *Cuentan los araucanos* contains the full rooster-egg/saliva narrative often attributed to it online.

### Major Mapuche ethnographies: a negative corpus finding

**[Secure negative finding]** In the accessible indexes and searchable versions consulted, Colo Colo is not a central being in:

- Pascual Coña’s testimony edited by Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach;
- Louis Faron’s *Hawks of the Sun*;
- María Ester Grebe’s major writings on Mapuche cosmology;
- Rolf Foerster’s *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche*;
- Ana Mariella Bacigalupo’s studies of *machi* practice;
- Tom Dillehay’s work on monuments, landscape, and resistance.

These sources are crucial for Mapuche religion, ritual, healing, social organization, and sacred landscape, but their importance cannot be transferred automatically to Colo Colo. Modern webpages claiming that Bacigalupo documents continuous contemporary Colo Colo belief require a page-level citation; none was located.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and characteristic activity

**[Early folklore attestation]** The creature’s domain is the unhealthy house: cracks, walls, roof spaces, floors, nearby burrows, and caves. It is an intimate domestic threat rather than a wilderness sovereign.

Its characteristic activities are:

- drinking saliva from sleepers;
- consuming discarded spittle;
- licking eating utensils or consuming leftovers;
- less commonly sucking blood;
- causing fever, emaciation, disfigurement, coughing, and death;
- crying its name, cackling, or sounding like an infant;
- remaining unseen or escaping into cracks.

The belief offered a supernatural language for household clusters of wasting disease. Vicuña’s Coelemu informant explicitly glossed fatal fever as *tisis*. This does not reduce the legend to tuberculosis; it shows that consumption supplied one historical interpretation of the symptoms.

### Forms

**[Regional variants]**

| Place/source | Form |
|---|---|
| Ovalle, 1904 | Elongated, sparsely haired mouse with piglike snout |
| La Serena | Lizard |
| Santiago–Talagante | Fierce little mouse |
| Malloco | Small black bird |
| Quella | Batlike bird |
| Coelemu | Unseen animal known by its cry |
| Guevara, 1908 | Egg → snake/lizard → feathered rat |
| Rojas, 1926 | Ratlike animal with feathers, bald diseased head, pointed snout, red snake-like tongue |
| Later national summaries | Underground lizard capable of becoming rat or wildcat |

There is consequently no single “canonical” anatomy.

### Symbols and correspondences

**[No evidence]** No securely attested sacred color, number, weekday, celestial body, plant, weapon, regalia, priestly emblem, or calendar correspondence belongs to Colo Colo.

**[Folklore objects]**

- anomalous small egg;
- flour used to reveal footprints;
- fire used to destroy the egg;
- paraffin and holy water in Rojas’s literary version;
- food vessels and saliva as vectors of sympathetic contamination.

### Iconography

**[No premodern iconography]** No archaeological sculpture, rock image, ritual mask, colonial devotional image, or securely identified Indigenous visual representation of the creature was found. Modern rat-serpent-rooster illustrations are artists’ composites derived from verbal variants.

**[Modern invention]** Images presenting a fixed feathered rat with rooster head and serpent tail as “the traditional Mapuche depiction” lack an ancient prototype.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Origin rather than genealogy

Colo Colo has no parents, consort, offspring, siblings, divine court, servants, or stable pantheon position in the documented corpus.

Its “birth” has several forms:

- **[Early folklore attestation, Guevara 1908]** A small anomalous egg popularly attributed to a rooster is heated by the sun; a snake or lizard forms and later becomes a feathered rat.
- **[Literary elaboration, Rojas 1926]** A snake carries off and incubates a bad hen’s egg, nurses the hatchling, and teaches it to drink sleepers’ saliva.
- **[Later folklore]** A rooster or old hen lays and sometimes incubates a small rough gray-white egg.
- **[Modern unstable retelling]** A serpent lays the egg or a toad feeds the hatchling. These formulations were not established in the early core examined here.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

**None securely attested.**

The following distinctions matter:

- A generic snake appears as incubator and teacher in Rojas; it is not named and is not classified as a deity.
- The Basilisco chilote is compared or equated with Colo Colo by collectors, but no traditional narrative depicts the two meeting.
- No episode with Ngünechen, Pillan, Antü, Küyen, Tren Tren, Kai Kai, Anchimallén, Chonchon, Piuchén, Trauco, Pincoya, Millalobo, or another named being was located.
- Shared membership in modern “Mapuche mythology” lists is not an interaction.

### Relation to sorcery

**[Later learned systematization/regional variant]** Latcham connects the creature with an animal employed by witches or *kalku*. Some Argentine lore likewise associates a *colo-colo* bird with a *kalku*. This is not enough to classify every Colo Colo as a sorcerer’s familiar, still less as a universal *wekufü*.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Colo Colo has no epic cycle. It has a cluster of short belief narratives.

### A. The fatal house

**[Earliest core, 1904–1915]**

1. A dangerous creature inhabits or approaches a house.
2. It consumes saliva, spittle, leftovers, or blood.
3. A resident begins to waste away.
4. If the creature is not killed, the victim dies.
5. Its presence may explain why successive occupants of a house become ill.

The important early invariant is not anatomy or supernatural birth but the transfer of bodily substance and progressive wasting.

### B. The rooster’s egg and metamorphosis

**[Early elaboration, Guevara 1908]**

1. A hen produces an anomalous, infertile-looking egg popularly said to have been laid by a rooster.
2. Solar incubation generates a snake or lizard.
3. It metamorphoses into a feathered ratlike animal.
4. It lives near houses and obtains human substance indirectly.
5. Suspect eggs must be burned.

This narrative combines anomalous poultry reproduction, reptilian incubation, metamorphosis, and sympathetic magic.

### C. The snake foster-parent

**[Literary elaboration, Rojas 1926]**

Rojas replaces solar incubation with a snake that retrieves, broods, nurses, and instructs the hatchling. He also integrates diagnostic flour, a domestic exorcistic mixture of paraffin and holy water, a direct bedside sighting, and skeptical framing. These details should be cited to Rojas unless independently collected elsewhere.

### D. The cry of death

**[Regional variant]** In Coelemu it cries *colo-colo*. Later descriptions make the sound clocklike. Other versions say it cackles like a hen or cries like a newborn. Argentine variants transform it into a large nocturnal bird whose call foretells death.

### E. Colo Colo and the basilisk

**[Syncretistic interpretation]** The closest structural analogue is the European basilisk or cockatrice: an unnatural rooster-associated egg produces a serpentlike lethal creature. Chiloé’s basilisk lives beneath houses, feeds on saliva or blood, and causes wasting; some versions also retain the lethal gaze and death by mirror.

The direction of borrowing cannot be demonstrated simply from resemblance. Three possibilities must remain separate:

1. an Indigenous house-creature acquired European rooster-egg and basilisk motifs;
2. a European basilisk complex acquired local saliva-drinking, wasting, animal, and household features;
3. collectors fused partly distinct central-Chilean, Mapuche, Huilliche, and Chilote traditions.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The third model best fits the surviving documentary sequence: early twentieth-century sources already show unstable forms, while “Colo Colo = Mapuche basilisk” becomes progressively cleaner in later summaries.

The [Museo Regional de Ancud](https://www.museodeancud.gob.cl/galeria/seres-mitologicos-objetos-de-la-coleccion-cesteria-de-chiloe) describes the Chilote basilisk as a crested snake living in houses and consuming inhabitants’ saliva until they dry up. That museum interpretation cites Oreste Plath and illustrates the mature conflation, not an ancient equation.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult

**[No evidence]** Colo Colo had no documented temple, shrine, priesthood, festival, pilgrimage, hymn, formal sacrifice, votive offering, oracle, theophoric naming system, sacred image, or cult economy.

Calling it a “divinity” or speaking of its “worship” misrepresents the record.

### Household precautions

**[Early folklore practice]**

- destroy or burn anomalous poultry eggs;
- kill the creature before the victim dies;
- avoid or abandon a house believed to harbor it.

**[Literary elaboration]**

- spread flour to identify tracks;
- fill the burrow with paraffin and holy water.

Rojas’s combination joins practical vermin-tracking, combustible destruction, and Catholic protection. It is unsafe to describe this as a traditional *machi* rite.

### Nguillatun and machitun

**[Secure negative finding]** No reliable account was found placing Colo Colo in a *nguillatun* ceremony or making it a standard object expelled in *machitun*. General descriptions of Mapuche healing cannot be used as proof that *machi* routinely diagnosed Colo Colo attacks.

### Sacrifice and sexual practice

**[Contested-material finding: absent]** No evidence connects Colo Colo with human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, or institutional coercion. Internet descriptions that insert it into generalized accounts of “dark Mapuche ritual” are unsupported.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1765: no securely named monster

Colonial chronicles contain the leader Colocolo and extensive reports of Mapuche beings, rituals, and specialists. No secure Colo Colo monster passage was identified.

### 1765–1904: animal word and human name

Febrés records *codcod/colocolo* as a wildcat. Natural histories apply *colocolo* to a South American cat. Histories and literary commentary continue the fame of the leader. These meanings predate the monster’s secure printed record.

### 1904–1915: emergence in documentation

Silvestre, Guevara, and Vicuña record a broad central-Chilean belief complex. The geographical range extends from La Serena and Ovalle through Santiago, Talagante, Angostura de Paine, Malloco, Coihueco, Coelemu, and Quella. It is already plural:

- rural and popular rather than exclusively Mapuche;
- animal and supernatural;
- visible in some versions, unseen in others;
- saliva-drinking in most, blood-drinking in a minority;
- egg-born only in some accounts.

### 1914 onward: Chilote comparison

Cavada’s description of Chiloé’s basilisk supplies a closely related but separately named southern tradition. Later compilers increasingly treat Colo Colo and basilisk as regional names or parent traditions in a syncretic monster.

### 1923–1926: systematization and literary canonization

Latcham places the creature in “Araucanian myths” and sorcery. Rojas gives it its fullest influential story. Literary circulation stabilizes the feathered-rat form, snake foster-parent, infant cry, flour test, and holy-water remedy.

### Mid-twentieth century: national folklore

Plath and the *Enciclopedia Chilena* map the creature across much of Chile, while naturalistic authors associate sightings with real marsupials or rodents. The creature becomes an entry in a national bestiary.

### Late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries

School anthologies, children’s books, illustrated myth collections, card games, websites, and “cryptid” catalogues compress variants into a standard biography: rooster lays egg; serpentlike creature hatches; it becomes a rat-bird hybrid; it drinks saliva and kills sleepers.

**[Modern reinvention]** This is a convenient composite, not the text of a single old Mapuche narrative.

### Present status

**[Folklore survival]** Colo Colo remains recognizable in Chilean folklore, horror writing, educational material, and internet mythology. The record located does not establish an organized revival cult or measurable contemporary worship.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Manuel Rojas

Rojas’s 1926 story is the most important literary reception. Its achievement is not merely repetition: it stages competing belief and skepticism and makes tuberculosis-like illness, poverty, unsafe housing, oral narration, religious protection, and fear part of the legend’s social world.

### Encyclopedias and children’s literature

Mid- and late-century compilers converted locally inconsistent testimonies into a nationally legible monster. Illustrators then supplied the stable visual body absent from early testimony.

### Games and popular culture

**[Modern reinvention]** The Chilean card game *Mitos y Leyendas* issued a Colo Colo ally card in the *Vendaval* expansion of the *Guerrero Jaguar* era in 2005. Such depictions belong to fantasy adaptation, not recovered Indigenous iconography.

### Football club: the crucial distinction

The Club Social y Deportivo Colo-Colo was founded in Santiago on **19 April 1925**. Luis Contreras proposed the name, and the founders chose it in honor of the Mapuche leader memorialized in national history and *La Araucana*. ESPN’s account of the club’s centenary identifies Contreras and the intended values of rebellion and courage: [“Con empuje y coraje”](https://www.espn.com.mx/futbol/chile/nota/_/id/15068493/origen-del-club-social-y-deportivo-colo-colo).

**[Secure reception finding]** The football club is named after **Colocolo the human toqui/leader**, not after the saliva-drinking creature. The club’s Indigenous head emblem likewise represents the leader.

**[Modern confusion]** Search results, school materials, and popular articles sometimes imply that the club was named for the monster or wildcat. Contemporary reporting occasionally repeats a story that the founders knew a monster called Colo Colo, but the historical club tradition identifies the human chief. The names’ homonymy has nevertheless increased the creature’s visibility.

### National and political symbolism

The club transformed the human Colocolo into a mass symbol of Chilean popular identity, struggle, and “Araucanian” courage. Scholarship on football and national memory treats this as appropriation of an Indigenous emblem into Chilean nationalism. That political reception primarily concerns the chief, but it also overwhelms the monster in modern searches and public recognition.

### Occultism and neopaganism

**[Contested-material finding: absent]** No historical role was found for Colo Colo in Renaissance occultism, ceremonial magic, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, classical grimoires, New Age angelology, Jungian literature, or established chaos-magic traditions.

**[Modern invention]** Recent occult or cryptid webpages may call it an energy vampire, demon, familiar, astral parasite, or ancient death god. These labels extrapolate from saliva-drinking and wasting; they are not attested early classifications.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was it originally Mapuche?

There are two levels of evidence.

- **For Indigenous association:** the name has documented Mapudungun animal use; Guevara and Latcham explicitly classify the belief as Araucanian; some southern and Argentine versions occur in Mapuche-associated settings.
- **Against simple attribution:** the earliest detailed testimony is from Ovalle popular culture; Vicuña’s corpus is largely Spanish-speaking central Chilean folklore; early Mapudungun text collections do not securely preserve the familiar narrative; the rooster-egg complex closely parallels European basilisk lore.

The defensible formulation is therefore: **a Chilean and Mapuche-associated folklore complex, extensively creolized and regionally variable**, not a demonstrably unchanged pre-contact Mapuche god.

### Animal-name theory

Lenz securely documents the wildcat meaning. Later scholars and popular naturalists proposed various real animals as the substrate:

- *Leopardus colocolo*, the pampas/colocolo cat;
- *Leopardus guigna*, the kodkod or güiña;
- a burrowing rodent identified through its call;
- a *yaca* or small marsupial;
- the monito del monte, *Dromiciops gliroides*.

These identifications conflict and belong to naturalistic reconstruction. None explains by itself the egg, serpent metamorphosis, saliva-drinking, or sympathetic death.

### Basilisk syncretism

The rooster’s impossible egg is the strongest evidence of European influence. European basilisk and cockatrice traditions reached colonial Latin America through Iberian folklore and learned literature. Chiloé preserved its own localized basilisk. Yet the assumption that one fully formed myth simply replaced another is too neat. The record shows reciprocal recombination.

### Collector bias

Guevara, Latcham, and some later compilers sought coherent “Araucanian mythology” at a time when Chilean anthropology commonly treated Mapuche culture as a survivance to be classified after military incorporation of Araucanía. Their texts preserve valuable testimony but also impose outsider categories. Mardones’s analysis of Guevara is particularly important for this context.

### Disease interpretation

Informants linked the creature with *tisis*, coughing, fever, emaciation, and houses in which several people became sick. Scholars may reasonably compare this pattern with tuberculosis, crowding, contaminated interiors, or rodent infestation. But diagnosing individual legendary deaths retroactively is impossible. The historical fact is that Colo Colo supplied a culturally intelligible agent for wasting illness.

### Claims not established

The following widely circulating propositions lack adequate early evidence:

- that Colo Colo is a goddess of fever and death;
- that every Mapuche community recognized it;
- that it was worshipped or propitiated;
- that *machi* universally treated its victims;
- that it is securely an ancient *wekufü*;
- that it had a single rooster-headed serpent-rat form;
- that the Chilote basilisk descended unidirectionally from Colo Colo;
- that the football club took its name from the monster;
- that Bacigalupo, Faron, Grebe, Foerster, or Dillehay document a central Colo Colo cult;
- that Koessler-Ilg is the secure source for the full modern composite without a volume and page citation.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Sixteenth century | Colonial chronicles and Ercilla attest Colocolo the human leader; no monster is securely identified. |
| 1646 | Alonso de Ovalle publishes *Histórica relación del Reyno de Chile*. Later webpages sometimes claim a tangential precedent, but no named Colo Colo creature passage was verified. |
| 1765 | Febrés records *codcod/colocolo* as “gato montés.” |
| 1777 | Havestadt publishes *Chilidúgú*; no verified saliva-drinking Colo Colo narrative located. |
| Late eighteenth–nineteenth centuries | Natural histories use *colocolo* for a South American wildcat; the chief’s literary-national reputation continues. |
| 1898–1902 | Guevara publishes *Historia de la civilización de Araucanía*, later cited by national folklore summaries; exact priority for the monster requires page-level verification. |
| 19 February 1904 | José Silvestre publishes the earliest securely identified detailed creature description in *El Obrero* of Ovalle. |
| 1904 | Lenz’s dictionary records the wildcat and chief meanings, not the monster. |
| 1908 | Guevara’s *Psicolojía del pueblo araucano* records the rooster-attributed egg, reptile stage, feathered-rat metamorphosis, spittle/utensil contact, wasting death, and egg-burning precaution. |
| 1910 | Augusta and Sigifredo de Fraunhäusl publish *Lecturas araucanas*; no secure Colo Colo monster narrative located. |
| 1914 | Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes* documents the Chilote basilisk tradition and the anomalous hen/“rooster” egg. |
| 1915 | Vicuña Cifuentes publishes the enlarged *Mitos y supersticiones*, documenting multiple localized forms and twenty-seven additional reports. |
| 1916 | Augusta’s dictionary appears; it does not establish a standard Mapudungun monster entry corresponding to the modern composite. |
| 1923 | Latcham systematizes the creature within “Araucanian myths” and relates it to sorcery. |
| 19 April 1925 | Club Social y Deportivo Colo-Colo is founded and named after the human Mapuche leader. |
| 19 September 1926 | Manuel Rojas publishes “El colocolo” in *La Nación*, giving the legend its most influential literary narrative. |
| 1948–1971 | The *Enciclopedia Chilena* project summarizes the creature as a national folklore item distributed from Atacama to Llanquihue. |
| 1954–1962 | Koessler-Ilg’s *Cuentan los araucanos* volumes appear; online claims that they contain the central full narrative remain to be verified by volume and page. |
| 1960s–1990s | Plath’s folklore compilations consolidate regional testimonies into a standard Chilean mythography. |
| 1971–1976 | Nicasio Tangol publishes major Chilote collections, helping stabilize the archipelago’s mythological canon; the basilisk remains the relevant named Chilote analogue. |
| 1993 | Verónica Contreras publishes “El kolo-kolo y el colo-colo: diálogo intercultural,” comparing Mapuche oral traditions and Rojas’s literary recreation. |
| 2005 | *Mitos y Leyendas* includes a Colo Colo card, a fantasy-commercial reinterpretation. |
| 2015 | Sonia Montecino’s *Mitos de Chile* places the creature within a modern national encyclopedia of beings and enchantments. |
| 2020s | Online summaries increasingly present a standardized rat-serpent-rooster hybrid and often blur early testimony, the Chilote basilisk, the wildcat, and the chief. No organized cult is documented. |
