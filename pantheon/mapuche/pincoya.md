# The Pincoya: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A female maritime nonhuman being of Chiloé folklore. In modern ethnographic interpretation she is most plausibly classified as an intertidal **ngen**—an owner, guardian, or custodian of shellfish grounds—not securely as a pan-Mapuche “goddess.” “Sea goddess,” “nymph,” and “mermaid” are later folkloristic or popular labels.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation located:** Julio Vicuña Cifuentes’s entry “La Pincoya,” first published in *Mitos y supersticiones* in **1910** and republished in the enlarged 1915 edition, p. 83. Francisco J. Cavada’s fuller *Chiloé y los chilotes* followed in **1914**. No occurrence has been established in the sixteenth- through nineteenth-century Mapuche sources named in the brief.
>
> **Geographical center:** The Chiloé Archipelago and adjacent maritime cultural area of southern Chile, especially beaches, tidal flats, fishing grounds, shellfish beds, corrales de pesca, and the Cucao–Huelde region.
>
> **Evidentiary caution:** The familiar genealogy making the Pincoya a daughter of Millalobo and Huenchula, sister-wife of Pincoy, sibling of the Chilote Sirena, and conductor of drowned sailors to the Caleuche is substantially better documented in twentieth-century folklore syntheses than in the earliest printed notice. It should not be projected uncritically into pre-Hispanic Mapuche religion.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** an explicit pre-1920 printed record.
- **[LF] Later folklore:** twentieth- or twenty-first-century oral testimony or folklore collection.
- **[RV] Regional or local variant:** geographically limited testimony.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** a collector’s organized pantheon, genealogy, or structural interpretation.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** an analytical proposal rather than an informant’s explicit doctrine.
- **[SY] Syncretistic interpretation:** proposed Mapuche–Huilliche–Chono–European synthesis.
- **[MR] Modern retelling or reception.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported recent claim.**
- **[NE] No evidence established.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

The Pincoya is the female guardian of the littoral abundance on which Chilote fishing and shellfish-gathering households depend. She appears on or near a beach and dances. In the best-known account, dancing while turned toward the sea promises or produces abundance; turning toward the land announces scarcity or the removal of fish and shellfish elsewhere. Her response is moral and reciprocal: waste, quarrels, excessive extraction, or failure to cultivate the land may cause her to withhold marine resources. Joy, music, sociability, and responsible use attract her favor. **[EA/LF]**

A particularly valuable recent testimony separates her from the generic siren: “**La pincoya es la dueña de los mariscos, y la sirena es la dueña de los pescados**”—“The Pincoya is the owner of shellfish, and the siren is the owner of fish” (Rosa Chiguay, Caylin Island, 2013). David Núñez therefore interprets her as an intertidal *ngen*, while expressly distinguishing that interpretation from older literary “mythology” catalogues. [Núñez, *El sustrato indígena*, pp. 12–14](https://www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/sites/www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/files/2022-04/El%20sustrato%20ind%C3%ADgena%20de%20los%20seres%20mitol%C3%B3gicos%20de%20Chilo%C3%A9.pdf). **[LF/SR]**

### Name and spelling

- **La Pincoya / Pincoya:** the normal Spanish-Chilote form. No independent traditional script exists; Mapudungun was historically transmitted orally and has several modern orthographies.
- **Pincoy:** a masculine counterpart, frequently represented in later compilations as her brother, husband, or both. Similarity of name does not itself prove an ancient paired cult. **[LF/LS]**
- **“La Sembradora”:** a modern descriptive title used among or about artisanal fishers, referring to her “sowing” shellfish and marine fertility. It is not documented as an ancient cult title. **[LF/MR]**
- **Nereida, ninfa, sirena, diosa del mar, princesa del mar:** Spanish and Europeanizing classifications employed by folklorists, educators, tourism material, and children’s literature. They are analogies, not indigenous epithets. Oreste Plath called her a siren or nymph; modern summaries frequently call her a goddess. **[LS/MR]**
- **Ngen’lafken / ngen-ko:** not recorded as ancient names of the Pincoya. These are analytical Mapuche categories—owner or guardian of sea/water—within which recent scholarship places her. **[SR]**

### Etymology

The etymology is unresolved.

1. **Mapudungun derivation:** Popular accounts sometimes derive *Pincoya* from Mapudungun compounds, but no derivation has been demonstrated through Febrés, Havestadt, Augusta, or another historical lexicographical sequence. **[NE/MI where asserted as certain]**

2. **Pun-küyen, “night-moon”:** A published hypothesis connects the name with *pun* “night” and *küyen* “moon.” Trivero objects that *küyen* may be a relatively late Quechua-related form and that southern usage retained *ale* for moon. The proposal is speculative and does not explain all phonological features. [Trivero, discussion of the Pincoya](https://mapuche.info/wps_pdf/Trivero181010.pdf). **[SR, disputed]**

3. **Chono or other canoe-people substrate:** Because the Pincoya belongs to a maritime complex unlike much of the better-recorded inland Mapuche material, some writers propose a Chono substrate. This is possible but unprovable: the Chono language and religious corpus are too fragmentarily documented to furnish a direct cognate. Trivero explicitly calls the identification of Pincoya with a Chono sea spirit only a hypothesis. **[SY/SR]**

4. **Inca/Aymara or *coya* derivations:** Comparisons with Quechua *quya/coya*, “queen” or high-ranking woman, occur in secondary literature. They lack a secure early form, transmission history, and local attestation. **[SR, unestablished]**

The responsible conclusion is therefore **etymology unknown**.

### Classification through time

- **1910–1915:** a being in Chilean oral “myth” or “superstition,” the terminology of contemporary folklore studies. **[EA]**
- **Mid-twentieth century:** a nymph, nereid, siren, or member of a systematically arranged “Chilote pantheon.” **[LS]**
- **Current popular culture:** goddess, sea princess, mermaid, protector of fishers. **[MR]**
- **Recent indigenous-centered analysis:** an intertidal *ngen*, a relational owner or guardian rather than an Olympian-style goddess. **[SR]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### What the early colonial sources do—and do not—say

The following works are indispensable for reconstructing Mapuche vocabulary and religion, but no secure Pincoya passage has been established in them:

- Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana* (1569, 1578, 1589): epic treatment of the Arauco war, not a Pincoya source. **[NE]**
- Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz* (written 1673; first printed 1863): no verified Pincoya. **[NE]**
- Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del Reino de Chile, Flandes Indiano* (seventeenth-century manuscript; printed 1877–78): no verified Pincoya. **[NE]**
- Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* (1765), and Bernhard Havestadt, *Chilidúgú* (1777): no verified lexical form *Pincoya*. **[NE]**
- Félix de Augusta, *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916) and *Lecturas araucanas* (1910): no verified Pincoya narrative. **[NE]**
- Rodolfo Lenz, *Estudios araucanos* (1895–97): no secure Pincoya text located. **[NE]**
- Tomás Guevara, Ricardo Latcham, Martín Gusinde, Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach/Pascual Coña, Louis Faron, María Ester Grebe, Rolf Foerster, Ana Mariella Bacigalupo, and Tom Dillehay provide essential Mapuche religious context but do not constitute early evidence for this specifically Chilote figure. **[NE for early attestation]**
- Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collection *Cuentan los araucanos* does not establish the Pincoya in Argentine Mapuche tradition. **[NE]**

This documentary silence does not prove that the being was created in 1910. It establishes only that an earlier written attestation has not been demonstrated.

### First printed records

**Julio Vicuña Cifuentes, “La Pincoya,” 1910/1915.** The enlarged 1915 *Mitos y supersticiones: recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* lists “La Pincoya” at p. 83. Its express claim to derive from oral tradition makes it evidence for a belief circulating before publication, though not for its antiquity. [Digital copy and contents](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0056546.pdf). **[EA]**

**Francisco J. Cavada, *Chiloé y los chilotes*, 1914.** Published in Santiago by Imprenta Universitaria, 448 pages, this is the principal early ethnographic-folkloric source. Its surviving digital record confirms the 1914 edition and the work’s combined treatment of folklore, language, custom, history, and mythology. [Bibliographic record](https://chiloe.omeka.net/items/show/4); [page-image index](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/%C3%8Dndice:Chilo%C3%A9_y_los_chilotes_-_MC0008648.pdf). **[EA]**

Cavada’s interpretive vocabulary—“superstition,” “ignorance,” and “mythology”—belongs to an early-twentieth-century clerical and folkloristic frame. Recent museum scholarship warns that such cataloguing both preserved traditions and recast lived relationships with nonhuman beings as picturesque fiction. [Museo Regional de Ancud discussion](https://www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/bajo-la-lupa/coleccion-de-seres-mitologicos-de-chiloe-del-museo-regional-de-ancud). **[historiography]**

### Later corpus

- **Evaristo Molina, “Mitología Chilota” (1950):** an influential effort to treat the named figures as a coherent mythology rather than miscellaneous superstition. **[LS]**
- **Constantino Contreras (1961):** structural and folkloristic analysis of Chilote myth, emphasizing recurring concrete manifestations. **[LS]**
- **Oreste Plath, *Geografía del mito y la leyenda chilenos*:** consolidates the now-familiar dance, Cucao/Huelde birthplace, Pincoy companion, scarcity, abundance, and joyful-fisher motifs. [Online chapter](https://web.uchile.cl/cultura/oplath/antologia/geogmitos/geogmitoyleyenda15c.html); [digital volume](https://www.bibliotecanacionaldigital.gob.cl/visor/BND%3A666512). **[LF/LS]**
- **Bernardo Quintana Mansilla, *Chiloé mitológico* (1987):** a major popular synthesis responsible for much of the standardized pantheon and genealogy. **[LS/MR]**
- **Renato Cárdenas Álvarez, *El libro de la mitología de Chiloé* (1998):** explicitly presents Chilote myth as a worldview rooted in Mapuche tradition but strongly conditioned by Christianity and Western cultures. **[LF/LS/SY]**
- **Jaime Blume, “Narraciones míticas de Chiloé”:** structural classification places Pincoy/Pincoya as a pair and Huenchula/Pincoya in an owner/servant or superior/subordinate relation. This is an analytical schema, not proof of a fixed indigenous pantheon. [Article](https://revistachilenadederecho.uc.cl/index.php/RAIT/article/download/8684/8126). **[LS]**
- **David Núñez, *El sustrato indígena de los seres mitológicos de Chiloé*:** incorporates testimonies collected in 2012–18, including explicit understandings of the Pincoya as guardian of shellfish, and relocates her within Mapuche *kimün*. **[LF/SR]**

No scripture, liturgy, ancient inscription, temple archive, theophoric-name corpus, or indigenous manuscript devoted to the Pincoya is known.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Her primary domain is the **intertidal shore**: shellfish, algae, beach resources, and the local abundance or depletion experienced by mariscadores and fishing households. Núñez’s data distinguish her from the Sirena, guardian of fish in open water. **[LF/SR]**

Her power is not unlimited sovereignty over the ocean. Rather, she redistributes or “sows” resources and mediates reciprocity:

- generosity and a well-tended shore-side garden invite abundance;
- selfishness and fighting over beach resources offend her;
- repeated extraction from one place causes abandonment and sterility;
- music, happiness, companionship, and laughter attract her. **[LF]**

This ecological interpretation is consistent with the narratives but should not be passed off as an ancient conservation doctrine formulated in modern terms.

### Form

There are two significant forms:

1. **Beautiful human woman:** youthful, fair or lightly bronzed, usually with long golden or blond hair, sometimes dancing naked on the beach. **[LF/LS]**
2. **Mermaid form:** woman above the waist and fish below it. Plath’s Huelde version uses this form, but contemporary Chiloé testimony cited by Núñez explicitly distinguishes the fully female Pincoya from the hybrid Sirena. **[RV]**

Thus “the Pincoya is a mermaid” is not universally valid. The mermaid image may reflect European visual and narrative influence, while a human female manifestation fits broader Mapuche accounts of aquatic owners appearing anthropomorphically. **[SY/SR]**

### Recognizing her

Her most distinctive iconographic action is the dance:

- arms extended;
- on the beach or tidal margin;
- face toward sea or land;
- long loose hair;
- sometimes accompanied by Pincoy;
- sometimes shown with fish, shellfish, waves, nets, or a fish tail.

No color, number, weekday, planet, weapon, sacred animal, or standardized regalia is securely attested in traditional material. Gold hair, moonlight, nudity, shells, and sea foam are recurrent modern artistic conventions, not formal cult attributes.

### Surviving images

No pre-Columbian or colonial image has been securely identified as the Pincoya.

A documented stone sculpture in the Museo Regional de Ancud, inventory **22-1470**, was carved circa **1980**, attributed to José Segundo Ampuero Maldonado. It is a 147-cm anthropomorphic female in cancagua stone, made for the museum’s Patio de la Mitología after an earlier sculpture was damaged. [SURDOC catalogue](https://www.surdoc.cl/registro/22-1470). **[MR]**

The museum also holds Pincoya figures in plant fiber, wood, and stone. Such pieces document twentieth-century heritage representation and craft, not ancient sacred iconography. The museum itself dates from 1976. [Museo history](https://www.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/multimedia/museo-regional-de-ancud). **[MR]**

Public statues in Ancud, Castro, Achao, and elsewhere have made the mermaid or dancing-woman form visually canonical. A public artwork at the Liceo de Achao explicitly uses the figure as a marker of regional culture. [Public-art record](https://cna.mop.gob.cl/project/liceo-de-achao-comuna-de-quinchao/). **[MR]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

**Minimal early form:** The early record establishes a Pincoya associated with marine abundance; it does not securely establish an elaborate divine dynasty. **[EA]**

**Standardized later pantheon:** Millalobo, king of the sea, and the human Huenchula are made parents of Pincoya, Pincoy, and the Chilote Sirena. Pincoya subsequently marries her brother Pincoy. **[LS/MR]**

**Huelde–Cucao birth variant:** She is born at Laguna Huelde near Cucao and once inhabits a large rocky cavern, bathing in the lagoon and Río Puchanquin. This local landscape narrative is preserved by Plath. **[RV/LF]**

**Ngen/shumpall reconstruction:** Pincoya, Sirena, Millalobo, Caleuche, and other sea figures are interpreted as differentiated manifestations of a broader aquatic-owner or *shumpall* complex rather than members of a literal monarchical family. **[SR]**

### Every documented named interaction

- **Millalobo — father/daughter:** Later systematized tradition makes Pincoya the daughter and subordinate helper of the sea king. Twentieth-century folklore syntheses, especially Quintana and Cárdenas. **[LS]**
- **Huenchula — mother/daughter or superior/helper:** Later genealogy makes the human Huenchula her mother; structural analysis also lists Huenchula/Pincoya as an amo/servidor relation. Blume, late twentieth century. **[LS]**
- **Pincoy — sibling and spouse:** Pincoy accompanies or prompts her dance; some narratives make him sing before she dances. The incestuous sibling-marriage belongs to the standardized later genealogy. Plath; Quintana; Cárdenas; Blume. **[LF/LS]**
- **Chilote Sirena — sisters and complementary custodians:** Later genealogy makes them sisters. A 2013 Caylin testimony assigns shellfish to Pincoya and fish to Sirena. **[LF/LS]**
- **Caleuche — connection through drowned sailors:** Popular later retellings claim Pincoya, Pincoy, and Sirena carry or guide drowned sailors to the ghost ship. The recent indigenous-centered record instead treats the Caleuche as independently recruiting people through shipwrecks and disappearances; it does not confirm that Pincoya transports the drowned. **[LS/MR, disputed]**
- **Caicai/Coicoi or Kaykay — shared maritime classification:** Modern pantheon diagrams place both within the sea realm, but no narrative meeting or action between Pincoya and Caicai has been securely located. **[NE interaction]**
- **Tentén — shared pantheon only:** No specific encounter securely attested. **[NE]**
- **Huenchur — no direct interaction established:** Appears elsewhere in Chilote mythic networks; not a securely attested Pincoya partner. **[NE]**
- **Named Christian saint or Virgin — no direct identification:** Chilote Catholicism coexists with these narratives, but the Pincoya is not securely equated with the Virgin Mary or a saint. **[NE]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The dance of abundance and scarcity

**Core narrative [EA/LF]:** The Pincoya appears at the shore and dances. Facing seaward signifies that fish or shellfish will approach and be abundant; facing landward signifies that they will leave or become scarce. Some versions turn this from omen into causation: her dance actually “sows” or moves marine life.

**Behavioral elaboration [LF]:** She abandons an overworked shore if people harvest too frequently. The community must seek another place until the depleted location recovers. She is attracted by contentment, music, singers, laughter, and festive companions. The Chilean encyclopedia project of 1948–71 records fishers proceeding joyfully with music and song, believing she follows beneath the water, then concentrating celebration at the desired site. [Enciclopedia Chilena entry](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Enciclopedia_Chilena/Folclore/Pincoya%2C_La).

**Recent reciprocity version [LF]:** A well-kept garden makes her generous with shellfish; quarrelling on beaches violates the reciprocal order. This version links sea and cultivated land, not simply extraction and magical reward.

**Directional caution:** The dominant printed formula is sea = abundance and land = scarcity. Retellings sometimes reverse or muddle the signs. Such reversal should be treated as variant or error, not silently harmonized.

### Birth at Huelde

In Plath’s recorded local version, the Pincoya was born in Laguna Huelde, approximately four kilometres from Cucao. She is beautiful, fair or bronzed, golden-haired, and fish-tailed below the waist. She once lived in a great cavern shaped like a rocky hall and bathed in the lagoon and Río Puchanquin. [Plath’s Chiloé chapter](https://web.uchile.cl/cultura/oplath/antologia/geogmitos/geogmitoyleyenda15c.html). **[RV/LF]**

This story localizes the being in western Isla Grande. It should not be merged automatically with the Millalobo genealogy; the two are separable narrative strands.

### Daughter of Millalobo and Huenchula

The widely circulated modern story says Huenchula bore a daughter to Millalobo, lost or surrendered the infant to the sea, and later saw her returned as the supernaturally matured golden-haired Pincoya. She becomes a princess or helper in Millalobo’s marine kingdom and marries Pincoy. **[LS/MR]**

The story is narratively elaborate but lacks demonstrable colonial attestation. Its status is later folklore or systematization, not securely pre-Hispanic scripture.

### Drowned sailors and the Caleuche

The popular composite says Pincoya, Pincoy, and Sirena collect drowned sailors and carry them to the Caleuche, where they continue an enchanted maritime existence. **[LS/MR]**

Evidence is weaker than popular repetition suggests. Núñez’s recent field-based account states that the Caleuche itself recruits men by causing shipwrecks or disappearances and that Pincoya and Millalobo did not display recruitment agency in the narratives heard by the researcher. The “Pincoya rescues every drowned sailor for the Caleuche” motif should therefore be labelled a later composite, possibly created by connecting independently circulating figures in the organized “pantheon.” **[disputed]**

### The ethic of sufficiency

A recurring narrative logic punishes excessive harvesting, selfishness, and conflict while rewarding cultivated land, sharing, and joy. This can be interpreted as an ecological charter for moving between grounds and respecting regeneration. **[LF/SR]** It is not evidence for a formal ancient environmental law or priestly doctrine.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Temples and priesthood

No temple, sanctuary building, permanent altar, hereditary Pincoya priesthood, festival calendar, oracle, inscription, or theophoric personal-name series is known. **[NE]**

The absence matters: “cult” here denotes localized offerings, rules of conduct, narratives, and fishing practices, not an institution comparable to a state temple cult.

### Offerings

A testimony from a southern Chiloé community records that during *ngillatun* ceremonies in the **1940s**, offerings were made separately to the Caleuche and to Pincoya/Sirena. Food for the Caleuche was taken to a beach facing the Gulf of Corcovado; offerings to Pincoya and Sirena were deposited on a rock near a fish corral inside the estuary or bay. The testimony was supplied by Rosa Chiguay. [Núñez, pp. 17–18](https://www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/sites/www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/files/2022-04/El%20sustrato%20ind%C3%ADgena%20de%20los%20seres%20mitol%C3%B3gicos%20de%20Chilo%C3%A9.pdf). **[LF/RV]**

This is the clearest evidence for ritual attention directed to her. It does not establish an archipelago-wide cult or uninterrupted pre-Hispanic continuity.

### Fishing rites

Ethnography of the coast records the smoking or fumigation of fishing gear and corrals with aromatic plants, striking the equipment with branches, and burying horticultural produce, toys, perfume, and occasionally a tethered lizard as a living offering. [Álvarez and collaborators, coastal cosmovision](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0719-26812016000100014&script=sci_arttext). **[LF]**

These rites belong to the wider maritime ritual complex. Unless an informant explicitly names Pincoya, they must not all be reassigned to her.

The music-and-laughter expedition described in the *Enciclopedia Chilena* is a more explicit Pincoya-directed practice, although its documentary chain requires caution: it is a mid-century encyclopedia report, not an archaeological record. **[LF]**

### Sacrifice and violence

- **Human sacrifice to Pincoya:** no evidence established. **[NE]**
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence established. **[NE]**
- **Cult violence:** no evidence established. **[NE]**
- **Animal offering:** the tethered-lizard rite is documented within coastal fishing ritual but not securely as a Pincoya-specific sacrifice. **[LF, association unproved]**

### Christian context and suppression

Chiloé underwent prolonged Catholic evangelization, particularly through Jesuit missions and the lay office of *fiscales*. The mythology coexisted with Catholic observance rather than surviving as a clearly bounded persecuted Pincoya religion. No trial, extirpation order, destroyed idol, or forced conversion episode specifically naming Pincoya has been located. General statements that missionaries “suppressed the cult of Pincoya” are therefore unsupported. **[NE/MI]**

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1567: undocumented antecedents

Chiloé had millennia of maritime occupation and, at European contact, Huilliche and Chono-related populations. Archaeology establishes long dependence on coastal resources, but no archaeological object or image can be identified securely as Pincoya. A pre-Hispanic origin remains possible but unproved. **[SR]**

### Colonial period, 1567–1826

Spanish settlement, Catholic missions, Huilliche language and knowledge, European mermaid lore, and the traditions of maritime canoe peoples interacted over centuries. This provides a plausible setting for syncretism but no contemporary text naming Pincoya has been located. **[SY/NE]**

The lack of the name in Ercilla, Rosales, Febrés, Havestadt, and other early sources cautions against calling her a securely documented “ancient Mapuche goddess.”

### Nineteenth century

Darío Cavada’s *Chiloé* (1896) was apparently the first work to apply “mitología de Chiloé” to the island’s beings, but its listed examples include Caleuche, Camahueto, Caballo Marino, Ciudad de los Césares, Invunche, and Buta—not demonstrably Pincoya. [Núñez’s historiographical survey](https://www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/sites/www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/files/2022-04/El%20sustrato%20ind%C3%ADgena%20de%20los%20seres%20mitol%C3%B3gicos%20de%20Chilo%C3%A9.pdf). **[NE for Pincoya]**

### 1910–1915: entry into print

Vicuña Cifuentes’s 1910 collection supplies the earliest secure explicit printed attestation located. Cavada’s 1914 work expands the ethnographic record. The figure enters national folklore studies through texts that label island belief “myth” and “superstition.” **[EA]**

### 1950–1998: pantheon formation

Molina, Contreras, Plath, Quintana, Blume, and Cárdenas collect, compare, classify, and narratively connect the named beings. During this period Pincoya’s now-standard family tree and relation to the Caleuche become culturally canonical. **[LS]**

This is not necessarily fabrication; collectors may preserve older oral material. But the printed system—a king of the sea, queen, prince, princesses, defined jurisdictions, and regular family relations—reflects the systematizing hand of folklorists.

### Late twentieth century to present: heritage figure and renewed indigenous reading

Pincoya becomes a visual emblem of Chiloé in museums, public sculpture, crafts, school material, tourism, environmental discourse, and regional identity. Simultaneously, recent scholarship resists treating the beings merely as fantasy and interprets them through *ngen*, reciprocity, and Mapuche *kimün*. **[MR/SR]**

Continuous household belief is locally attested, but no reliable census of active “worshippers” exists.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Folklore, tourism, and education

Pincoya is now among Chiloé’s most marketable cultural figures. She appears on signs, souvenirs, illustrated books, school resources, museum displays, public statuary, and tourism websites. The common image—an attractive blond mermaid—is more visually standardized than the oral evidence warrants. **[MR]**

The Museo Regional de Ancud collection demonstrates deliberate twentieth-century materialization: named beings were commissioned in fiber and stone as a finite heritage set. **[MR]**

### Literature and visual art

Oreste Plath’s popular anthology strongly shaped the national version. Later illustrated children’s books such as *Pincoya, princesa de los mares de Chiloé* (Miguel Jiménez Colin and Marcy Lanfranco Orlandini, 2007) amplify the princess genealogy and benevolent-protector image. **[MR]**

Public works include the circa-1980 Ancud museum sculpture and later civic statues. These are representations of folklore, not surviving cult images.

### Astronomy

On **17 December 2019**, the International Astronomical Union assigned the proper name **Pincoya** to the star HD 164604 through the NameExoWorlds campaign; its planet HD 164604 b received the name **Caleuche**. [IAU star-name list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_stars/). This reverses their familiar narrative scale—the star rather than the ship bears Pincoya’s name—but permanently joins the two figures in international scientific nomenclature. **[MR]**

### Popular media

The name acquired national popular visibility as the nickname of Jennifer Galvarini, a Chilote participant in Chile’s *Gran Hermano* in 2023. This is nominal and regional identification, not a religious reception. **[MR]**

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no documented Golden Dawn, Theosophical, ceremonial-magical, chaos-magical, or established reconstructionist Pincoya tradition. Individual internet invocations or deity lists may exist, but they do not demonstrate a lineage or organized movement. **[NE]**

Claims that Pincoya possesses an ancient initiatory cult, a surviving priestesshood, prescribed lunar rites, chakras, crystals, tarot correspondences, or universal “divine feminine” doctrine are modern inventions unless separately documented. **[MI]**

### Politics

Pincoya functions chiefly as a regional and ecological emblem, not a documented nationalist or extremist symbol. No extremist appropriation was found. The Santiago neighborhood La Pincoya acquired the name in the modern era; its naming is civic history rather than evidence for cult diffusion. **[MR]**

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was she originally Mapuche, Chono, or European?

Three positions recur:

1. **Mapuche/Huilliche core:** Her role resembles *ngen’lafken* and *shumpall*: aquatic owners enter reciprocal relations with humans, control abundance, and may appear anthropomorphically. Núñez’s interpretation is the strongest modern statement of this case. **[SR]**

2. **Chono maritime substrate:** Her unusually central maritime role may preserve beliefs of Chono or other canoe-oriented peoples absorbed into Chilote society. Linguistic and textual evidence is insufficient to identify a specific Chono prototype. **[SR]**

3. **European mermaid influence:** Blond hair, fair skin, fish tail, “nereid” terminology, and a monarchical sea court parallel European folklore. These features may be colonial additions without exhausting the figure’s indigenous relational logic. **[SY]**

A mixed formation is historically plausible, but the layers cannot be dated individually.

### Is she a mermaid?

Popular iconography says yes. Recent Caylin testimony says no: Pincoya is a woman and guardian of shellfish; Sirena is the woman-fish and guardian of fish. This is a genuine regional and historical variation, not a problem to be harmonized. **[RV]**

### Does facing the sea mean abundance?

This is the dominant collected form. Reversed versions exist in retellings but have less documentary weight. **[LF/MR]**

### Does she rescue drowned sailors?

Popular summaries say that she and her siblings conduct them to the Caleuche. Recent field-based analysis finds no such agency for Pincoya and instead attributes recruitment and disappearance to the Caleuche itself. The rescue narrative is therefore a later, contested component. **[LS/MR]**

### Is she a goddess?

“Goddess” communicates supernatural power to general readers but imports a theological category not required by the testimony. *Ngen*, “owner/guardian,” is the more precise recent reconstruction. Calling her a “Mapuche goddess” without qualification falsely suggests colonial documentation, a temple cult, or a unified pan-Mapuche pantheon. **[SR]**

### Problems in the archive

The tradition was transmitted orally, recorded mostly by outsiders or literate folklorists, and frequently stripped of informant names, collection dates, exact locations, and linguistic originals. Printed repetition can therefore look like independent confirmation when later authors are copying Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, Plath, or one another.

The central methodological change has been from:

1. “superstition”;
2. to picturesque national folklore;
3. to a coherent mythological pantheon;
4. to lived relationships with nonhuman guardians interpreted through indigenous knowledge.

Each frame preserves something while also reshaping the evidence.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1567:** Maritime occupation of Chiloé is archaeologically established; no identifiable Pincoya evidence. **[NE]**
- **1569–1589:** Ercilla’s *La Araucana* appears; no verified Pincoya.  
- **Seventeenth century:** Núñez de Pineda and Rosales record Mapuche life and religion; no verified Pincoya.
- **1765:** Febrés’s Mapuche grammar and dictionary; no verified name.
- **1777:** Havestadt’s *Chilidúgú*; no verified name.
- **1895–97:** Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos*; no secure Pincoya text located.
- **1896:** Darío Cavada applies “mitología de Chiloé” to island beings, but Pincoya is not demonstrably among the examples.
- **1910:** Vicuña Cifuentes publishes the earliest secure explicit entry, “La Pincoya.” **[EA]**
- **1914:** Francisco J. Cavada publishes *Chiloé y los chilotes*, the major early folklore and linguistic collection. **[EA]**
- **1915:** Enlarged edition of Vicuña Cifuentes’s *Mitos y supersticiones* lists “La Pincoya,” p. 83.
- **1916:** Augusta’s *Diccionario araucano-español* appears; it does not establish the name’s etymology.
- **1950:** Evaristo Molina publishes “Mitología Chilota,” contributing to systematic pantheon-building.
- **1961:** Contreras advances structural folklore analysis.
- **1940s, recorded later:** Southern-Chiloé testimony places offerings to Pincoya and Sirena near a fish corral during *ngillatun*. **[LF]**
- **1948–71:** The *Enciclopedia Chilena* project records music-and-joy practices intended to attract Pincoya to a fishing ground.
- **Late twentieth century:** Plath popularizes the dance and Huelde birth versions.
- **1976:** Museo Regional de Ancud is founded.
- **Circa 1980:** José Segundo Ampuero Maldonado carves the documented cancagua-stone Pincoya for the museum.
- **1987:** Quintana’s *Chiloé mitológico* helps standardize the family-centered pantheon.
- **1998:** Cárdenas’s *El libro de la mitología de Chiloé* interprets the tradition as Mapuche-rooted but strongly transformed by Christianity and Western culture.
- **2007:** *Pincoya, princesa de los mares de Chiloé* gives the figure a modern illustrated children’s-book form.
- **2011 onward:** Civic sculpture projects further monumentalize her in Ancud and elsewhere.
- **2013:** Rosa Chiguay’s Caylin testimony distinguishes Pincoya, owner of shellfish, from Sirena, owner of fish.
- **2019:** IAU names HD 164604 “Pincoya” and its planet “Caleuche.”
- **2022:** Núñez publishes an indigenous-centered reassessment identifying her as an intertidal *ngen* and documenting offerings, reciprocity, and local classifications.
- **2023:** The nickname “Pincoya” receives national media prominence through *Gran Hermano Chile*.
- **Present:** Pincoya remains an active emblem of Chilote identity, maritime reciprocity, folklore, tourism, environmental education, and public art; the scale of literal devotional belief remains unmeasured.

## 11. Sources and Assessment

The most defensible reconstruction begins not with a supposed prehistoric divine biography but with two early-twentieth-century folklore records. Everything earlier is comparative reconstruction. The enduring narrative core is the female shore-being whose orientation and dance disclose or govern littoral abundance. Her exact form, genealogy, and relationship to other named beings vary.

The Millalobo dynasty and Caleuche rescue cycle are culturally real as later Chilote tradition, but their antiquity is not established. The recent *ngen* interpretation better accounts for reciprocity, localized ownership, shellfish grounds, offerings, and resource ethics than the popular label “sea goddess”; nevertheless, it too is an explicit modern scholarly reconstruction.

No evidence was found for a temple cult, ancient iconography, human sacrifice, temple prostitution, a named historical suppression campaign directed specifically at Pincoya, organized occult orders, an established neopagan denomination, or extremist appropriation. Their absence from the record should not be replaced by imaginative reconstruction.
