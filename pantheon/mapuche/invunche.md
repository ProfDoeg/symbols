# Invunche (Imbunche): Research Dossier

> **Class:** Not a god or independently worshipped divine being. In the earliest Mapudungun record it is a cave-dwelling, humanlike being raised by *kalku* (“sorcerers”) as an adviser; in nineteenth- and twentieth-century Chilean folklore it becomes a deliberately deformed human monster, captive, guardian, oracle, and instrument of witchcraft.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del reyno de Chile*, printed at Lima in **1765**, Mapudungun–Spanish *Calepino*, p. 523, s.v. **Ivumche**. Febrés defines the word as those whom sorcerers consult in their caves and raise from childhood for enchantments. This entry establishes the cave, childhood capture or upbringing, sorcerers, and advisory function—but not yet the famous leg sewn or fastened to the back. The original book is extant in digitized copies.  
> **Evidence key:** **[EA]** securely attested early material; **[LE]** later elaboration; **[RV]** regional variant; **[LS]** learned systematization; **[SY]** syncretistic interpretation; **[F]** collected folklore; **[MR]** modern reconstruction or interpretation; **[MI]** modern invention or creative retelling.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

The Invunche is best understood as a history of related figures rather than one stable “being.” The eighteenth-century Mapudungun evidence describes a class of humanlike cave-consultants kept by *kalku*. Juan Ignacio Molina subsequently calls them sorcerers’ “disciples.” The 1880 Chiloé judicial testimony presents two long-lived, forcibly confined human captives in the Cueva de Quicaví—one called **Ibunche**, the other **Chibato**. Twentieth-century Chilote collectors consolidate the now-familiar singular monster: a stolen child whose body is twisted, one leg fixed to the back, neck, or nape, and whose mutilation turns him into guardian and counsellor of the witches’ cave. Central-Chilean variants instead describe children whose bodily openings are sewn shut and who guard treasure or are abandoned in marshes.

These forms overlap, but they are not interchangeable. In particular, “deformed guardian with one leg sewn to the back” should not be projected without qualification into Febrés’s 1765 entry.

### Names and spellings

- **Ivumche / Ivùmche** — Febrés’s 1765 spelling. His entry reads: “*los que consultan los bruxos en sus cuevas, donde los crian desde chiquitos para sus hechizerias, ò encantos*” (“those whom the sorcerers consult in their caves, where they raise them from very young for their sorceries or enchantments”). He adds that Indigenous women call them *ivùm coñi*. **[EA]** [Febrés entry](https://diccionariodechile.cl/entrada/febres-1765/1693-ivumche)
- **Ivum coñi / ivùm coñi** — literally treated by early lexicographical tradition as a monstrous or animal-like child: *koñi* is “child”; the interpretation of *ivüm* is disputed. **[EA/contested etymology]**
- **Ivunche** — frequent nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century learned or folkloric spelling and regarded by Cavada as closer to Chilote pronunciation. **[LE/F]**
- **Invunche** — the dominant Chilean Spanish spelling in much folklore writing and literary reception. **[LE/F]**
- **Imbunche / imbumche / ibunche** — phonetic and orthographic variants reflecting assimilation of /n/ before /b/ and unstable colonial transcription. **[LE/F]**
- **Ifünche** — a modern normalized form often glossed “deformed person,” sometimes analyzed from *ifün/ivün* (“swollen, hypertrophied”) plus *che* (“person”). This convenient etymology is later than the first attestations and should not be treated as unanimously established. **[MR]**
- **Machucho / Machucho de la Cueva** — Chilote regional name. **[RV/F]**
- **Vuta Macho / Butamacho** — interpreted in Spanish–Mapudungun folk etymology as “large male animal,” from *füta/vuta* (“great, large”) plus Spanish *macho*. **[RV/F]**
- **Chivato / Chivato de la Cueva / chivo brujo** — caprine form or alternative cave creature. In the 1880 testimony **Chibato and Ibunche are separate beings**, although later compilers often collapse “Chivato” into an epithet of the Invunche. **[EA for distinction; LE for equation]**
- **Chinchín roca, Vuta, Trabutre**, and similar names occur in later folklore dictionaries and compilations, but their equivalence is not demonstrable in the eighteenth-century record. **[LS/F]**

### Etymological dispute

1. **Febrés–Cavada interpretation:** *ivùm*, “small animal, monster,” plus *che*, “person,” hence Cavada’s Latinizing gloss *homo-bestia*, “man-beast.” **[EA lexical element; LS compound analysis]**
2. **Moesbach/Plath interpretation:** *ivün* or *ifün*, “swollen, hypertrophied,” plus *che*, “person,” hence “swollen/deformed person.” **[LS]**
3. **Alternative historical hypothesis:** later scholarship reports a proposal that *ivumche* originally denoted a sorcerer’s animal ally or shape-shifting familiar and became confused with a similar expression for a malformed being. This is a scholarly reconstruction, not something stated by Febrés. **[MR]**
4. Popular internet glosses such as “person transformed,” “secret person,” or a fully standardized ancient form *ifünche* frequently suppress the instability of the early spellings and meanings. **[MI or oversimplified MR]**

The word subsequently entered Chilean Spanish metaphorically for a monstrous or deformed person, a hopelessly tangled affair, a malefic construction, or an institution prevented from developing normally.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Colonial antecedents and significant silences

Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (1569–1589), Núñez de Pineda’s *Cautiverio feliz* (composed in the seventeenth century; first printed in the nineteenth), Diego de Rosales’s *Historia general del Reino de Chile* (seventeenth century), and Luis de Valdivia’s early Mapudungun works do not supply the securely identifiable Invunche dossier. They document colonial representations of Mapuche religion, divination, sorcery, caves, and spirits, but those topics cannot by themselves be counted as attestations of this being.

That silence matters. No evidence located establishes an ancient temple cult, pre-Columbian image, theophoric name, inscription, or archaeological Invunche object.

### Febrés, 1765

Andrés Febrés’s missionary grammar and dictionary was printed at Lima in 1765 in Spanish and Mapudungun. Its *Ivumche* entry is the first secure, name-bearing record now identifiable. The plural sense—“those whom the sorcerers consult”—suggests a class rather than a unique monster. It says they are raised from childhood in caves for magical consultation. It does **not** mention Quicaví, a sewn leg, reversed head, goat form, cemetery flesh, or a gatekeeping office. **[EA]** The complete 682-page linguistic work survives digitally. [Bibliographic record and scan information](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL24360916M/Arte_de_la_lengua_general_del_reyno_de_Chile_con_un_dialogo_chileno-hispano_muy_curioso)

### Havestadt, 1777

Bernhard Havestadt’s Latin *Chilidúgú sive tractatus linguæ Chilensis*, printed at Münster in 1777 from knowledge acquired during his Chilean mission, gives a substantially fuller report:

> *Fama est inter Indos, veneficos suos in quadam specu nutrire hominum genus…*

In paraphrase: it was reported among Indigenous people that sorcerers raised a kind of human in a cave; while the children were still nursing, their eyes, mouth, and anus were sewn, so that as they grew they degenerated into another form or species; sorcerers assembled in caves called *renu* to seek their counsel.

This is the earliest located description of bodily sewing and imposed deformation. It still does not specify the leg fixed to the back. “*Fama est*” marks the account explicitly as reported belief, not eyewitness observation. **[EA]** Havestadt’s book is a grammar, vocabulary, catechetical collection, music book, map, and travel record—not an Indigenous-authored scripture. [Corpus of Historical Mapudungun description](https://benmolineaux.github.io/bookshelf/en/core-texts/havestadt/)

### Molina, 1787/1795

Juan Ignacio Molina’s *Saggio sulla storia civile del Chili* (Bologna, 1787; Spanish translation, 1795) says the Mapuche feared *calcús*, who lived by day in caves with disciples called **Ivunches**, glossed “hombres animales,” and by night changed into nocturnal birds to shoot invisible arrows at enemies. Molina’s wording turns the Invunches into disciples and embeds them in a larger account of magical warfare. Whether the bird transformation applies grammatically to the *calcús*, the disciples, or both must not be resolved beyond the wording. **[EA/LS]** [Quotation in historical scholarship](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339350182_Mover_la_guerra_Resistencia_indigena_hechiceria_y_negocios_coloniales_Sur_del_Reyno_de_Chile_fines_del_siglo_XVII)

### The 1880 Chiloé proceedings

The most important documentary episode is the judicial investigation of the organization called the **Recta Provincia**, **La Mayoría**, or, in later interpretation, the “Tribunal de la Raza Indígena.” In April 1880 Intendant Luis Martiniano Rodríguez ordered the arrest of persons reputed to be *machis*, witches, or sorcerers. Depositions were taken at Ancud in a coercive criminal proceeding; they are testimony about belief and organization, not independent proof that a cave or monsters existed. Copies circulated and portions were printed in 1908.

Mateo Coñuecar testified that the Cueva de Quicaví was an underground, timber-lined chamber containing a table, chairs, and benches. Approximately twenty years earlier, he said, he had brought it the meat of a slaughtered kid. Two disfigured beings emerged:

- one crawled and resembled a **Chibato**;
- the other was a naked man with white hair and beard reaching halfway down his body, called **Ibunche**.

Both were said to be about fifty years old and to be replaced when they died. According to the deposition, the council selected unwilling persons, abducted and confined them, trained them to leap, rubbed them with *picochihuín* to produce hair, and fed them goat meat, kid meat, and the flesh of dead children stolen from the cemetery. **[EA as 1880 testimony; not established fact]**

Crucially, this Ibunche is not described as a stolen infant with one leg sewn to his back. He is a forcibly recruited adult or older human captive. The deposition also distinguishes him from the Chibato. [The reproduced judicial record](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0008651.pdf)

The proceeding is historically secure; the supernatural claims are statements made within it. The prosecution’s charges concerned illicit association, usurpation of authority, poisoning, intimidation, and alleged killings. The record also shows the state criminalizing Indigenous healers and authorities under the joined labels *machi*, *brujo*, and *hechicero*. [Biblioteca Nacional overview](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-95073.html)

### Early-twentieth-century collectors

- **Francisco J. Cavada, *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914):** gives the canonical Chilote anatomy: backward-facing head; one leg fixed behind the neck or nape; locomotion on one remaining leg; advisory and cave-associated functions. Cavada derives the name from *ivùm* and *che*. **[F/LE]** [Modern critical reedition](https://www.museodeancud.gob.cl/sites/www.museodeancud.gob.cl/files/images/articles-77081_archivo_01.pdf)
- **Julio Vicuña Cifuentes, *Mitos y supersticiones* (1915):** records geographically distinct versions. At Talagante, sorcerers steal boys aged six to twelve months, stop their bodily openings, and employ them to guard buried treasure. At Melipilla the openings are “covered”; at Coihueco de Chillán they are sewn and the children cast naked into marshes. Vicuña separately quotes the Chilote form with face turned backward and one leg attached to the neck or nape. **[RV/F]** [Digitized text](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3a/Mitos_y_supersticiones_recogidos_de_la_tradici%C3%B3n_oral_chilena_con_referencias_comparativas_a_los_de_otros_paises_latinos_%28IA_mitosysuperstici00vicu%29.pdf)
- **Alejandro Cañas Pinochet:** defines the Invunche as an imagined rational being or animal raised by sorcerers in caves and consulted about magic. **[F/LS]**
- **Oreste Plath, *Geografía del mito y la leyenda chilenos*:** synthesizes the stolen-child, mutilated body, cave-guardian, consultant, and malefic-instrument traditions, listing several regional names. **[LS]**
- **Evaristo Molina and later Chilote collectors, including Quintana:** further stabilize the guardian of the “Casa Grande” or Quicaví cave. **[F/LS]**

### Mapuche ethnographies and absence

Rodolfo Lenz, Tomás Guevara, Ricardo Latcham, Félix de Augusta, Pascual Coña/Moesbach, Louis Faron, María Ester Grebe, Rolf Foerster, Ana Mariella Bacigalupo, and Tom Dillehay are indispensable for Mapuche language, ritual, *machi*, *kalku*, *wekufü*, healing, landscape, and colonial transformation. They should not all be cited as if they supplied Invunche narratives. The modern linguistic survey notes the entry in Febrés, later dictionaries, and Cavada but does not find it as a central element of Pascual Coña’s life history. **Absence finding:** the Invunche is not securely shown to have a role in *nguillatun* or *machitun*, nor is it prominent in the best-known twentieth-century Mapuche religious ethnographies. [Historical lexical survey](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-93032010000200008&script=sci_arttext)

Likewise, no securely verified Invunche narrative was located in the published descriptions of Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collection. Assertions that it is uniformly current throughout Chilean and Argentine Mapuche territory overstate the documentation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Functions by stratum

- **Cave oracle or consultant:** present already in Febrés and Havestadt. **[EA]**
- **Sorcerer’s disciple:** Molina. **[EA/LS]**
- **Confined cave being and trained performer:** 1880 deposition. **[EA as testimony]**
- **Guardian or porter of the Cueva de Quicaví/Casa Grande:** fully developed in Chilote folklore collectors. **[RV/LE]**
- **Instrument of harm:** later folklore says sorcerers take it out, beat it, or use its cries and presence to cause fright, illness, or malefic effects. **[F]**
- **Guardian of buried treasure:** Talagante version recorded by Vicuña Cifuentes. **[RV]**
- **Adviser to inexperienced witches:** twentieth-century Chilote systematization. **[F/LS]**

### Physical forms

The corpus offers no single canonical body:

1. **Sewn infant:** Havestadt reports sewn eyes, mouth, and anus, producing degeneration into another form. **[EA]**
2. **Hairy captive:** the 1880 Ibunche is naked, with very long white hair and beard. Herbs allegedly produce hair over the Chibato’s body. **[EA as testimony]**
3. **Three-limbed crawler:** later descriptions fix one leg to the spine, neck, or nape; it travels on its hands and remaining foot. **[RV/LE]**
4. **Backward head:** Cavada and later collectors. **[RV/LE]**
5. **Universally twisted features:** later syntheses add twisted ears, mouth, nose, arms, and fingers. **[LS]**
6. **Goatlike, woolly, or bristly being:** some Chilote informants describe a large kid or goat, with baleful bleating. **[RV]**
7. **Mute versus screaming:** some versions make it mute because of mutilation or isolation; others give it piercing cries or goatlike bleats. These traditions conflict. **[RV]**

No securely proven pre-twentieth-century image or conventional Indigenous iconography survives. Modern illustrations showing a naked, hairy, three-legged humanoid with one foot behind its neck visualize the collector-era composite. They are not ancient sacred images.

No securely attested sacred color, number, calendrical day, celestial body, weapon, temple emblem, or sacrificial symbol belongs to the figure.

### Food and substances

- Goat or kid meat and *picochihuín* water: 1880 testimony. **[EA as testimony]**
- Flesh of children’s corpses stolen from cemeteries: same deposition. This is an allegation in a witchcraft trial, not archaeological proof of cannibalism. **[EA as allegation]**
- Human infant flesh, black-cat or black-goat milk, or cemetery flesh: later folklore compilations. **[F/LE]**
- Herbs rubbed onto or administered to the captive: 1880 testimony and subsequent dictionaries. **[EA/F]**

There is no evidence of “human sacrifice to the Invunche.” The relevant traditions concern alleged abduction, mutilation, corpse violation, imprisonment, and feeding; the being is victim, captive, servant, or monster, not recipient of a cult sacrifice.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

The Invunche has no divine genealogy. Its “origin” is manufactured rather than procreated: a child or adult human is acquired by sorcerers and transformed. No named parents, consort, offspring, or sibling are securely attested.

### Origin variants

| Tier | Origin | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **[EA]** | Raised from childhood by sorcerers in caves | Febrés, 1765 |
| **[EA]** | Nursing child mutilated by sewing bodily openings | Havestadt, 1777 |
| **[EA as testimony]** | Person selected, abducted, confined, conditioned, and eventually replaced at death | Coñuecar deposition, 1880 |
| **[RV]** | Boy aged six months to one year stolen by witches | Vicuña Cifuentes’s Talagante, Melipilla, and Chillán material, 1915 |
| **[RV/LE]** | First-born son stolen or purchased from his father | later Chilote popular tradition |
| **[MI when presented as ancient]** | A precisely prescribed nine-day-old firstborn subjected to an invariant initiation recipe | modern popular syntheses; no such uniform rule appears in the earliest corpus |

### Every documented interaction with another named supernatural being

Strictly speaking, there are **no securely documented interactions with a named god or goddess**. The figure inhabits a world of human sorcerers and other folkloric creatures, not a genealogical pantheon.

- **Chibato — co-resident but distinct cave being.** Two disfigured beings emerge together from the Cueva de Quicaví; one is Ibunche, the other Chibato. *Proceso de los brujos de Chiloé*, Mateo Coñuecar deposition, 1880. **[EA as testimony]**
- **Kalku / brujos — creators, jailers, masters, and consultants.** Febrés 1765; Havestadt 1777; Molina 1787; 1880 testimony; later collectors. These are human ritual specialists or witches, not gods. **[EA through F]**
- **Chillpila — no direct Invunche interaction attested.** In the 1880 foundation legend she defeats the Spanish pilot José de Moraleda in magic and carries his alleged book to Quicaví; the deposition does not place her in a scene with Ibunche. **[Absence finding]**
- **Voladora — no early shared episode found.** Later systematizations place both among servants or associates of Chilote witches, but co-membership in a bestiary is not an interaction. **[Absence finding]**
- **Caleuche, Trauco, Fiura, Pincoya, Camahueto, Tenten, Caicai, Anchimallén, Wekufü, Ngünechen — no named narrative interaction securely attested.** Modern charts that present these beings as one interacting “pantheon” are learned systematizations. **[Absence finding]**
- **Satan/Devil — later collectors sometimes Christianize witches’ caves as Satanic assemblies or “Salamanca” caves, but neither Febrés nor the 1880 Ibunche passage identifies the creature with Satan or records a meeting between them.** **[SY/LE]**

## 5. Major Legends and Narrative Cycles

### A. The cave consultants

**[EA: 1765–1777]** Sorcerers raise children or childlike human beings in caves. Through confinement and, in Havestadt, deliberate mutilation, the captives become physically other. Sorcerers meet in the cave and ask their counsel. This is the oldest recoverable narrative core.

Its social setting is Mapudungun vocabulary for *kalku* and *renu*, recorded by Catholic missionaries. The texts are filtered through missionary categories such as “sorcery” and “enchantment”; they are not transcripts of an Indigenous narrator’s complete myth.

### B. The nocturnal magical-war complex

**[EA/LS: Molina, 1787]** *Calcús* live by day in caves with their Ivunche disciples, then travel as night birds and launch invisible arrows. The episode connects the beings with Indigenous explanations of occult aggression, illness, and magical warfare. Later works often detach the Ivunche from this context and make it a generic monster.

### C. The Recta Provincia and Quicaví

**[EA as testimony: 1880]** The Recta Provincia is represented in depositions as an island-wide authority with officers, judges, healers, and punishments. Its foundation legend says the Indigenous sorceress Chillpila defeated Spanish hydrographer José de Moraleda in a magical contest; he surrendered a book of magic, which she brought to Quicaví. Historical Moraleda did survey the region, but his own record does not corroborate this encounter. The magical contest is therefore an oral foundation legend recorded in 1880, not a documented event of 1786–1788.

Within this cycle, the cave is a concealed council chamber and prison. Ibunche and Chibato are maintained there, fed, trained, and replaced. The Ibunche’s role is evidence of institutional terror and secrecy in testimony, but the proceeding does not verify the cave archaeologically.

Modern scholarship offers competing interpretations of the Recta Provincia:

- survival or transformation of Indigenous authority and healing;
- an anticolonial or ethnic tribunal;
- an organization emerging after evangelization disrupted older shamanic structures;
- a rural medical and conflict-resolution network criminalized by the state;
- an illicit coercive association that sometimes employed poison and intimidation.

The record supports neither romanticizing it as purely anticolonial resistance nor accepting every prosecutorial allegation literally. A modern study argues that witchcraft and healing operated as alternative medicine and community conflict resolution, while another analyzes the organization as a performative Indigenous tribunal formed through colonial transformation. **[MR]** [Medical and cultural-resistance study](https://www.magallania.cl/index.php/magallania/article/view/817); [Tribunal study](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-23762014000100003&script=sci_arttext)

### D. The stolen and twisted child

**[RV/LE: Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, later Chilote collectors]** Witches steal or buy a young boy. A specialist progressively twists him: one leg is broken or dislocated and fastened to his back; the head is turned; limbs and facial features are deformed; sometimes the tongue is split or bodily openings sealed. He is fed abnormal food and denied ordinary language and society. When transformation is complete, he guards the cave and advises its masters.

This is now the dominant popular story, but it is a composite. The stitched openings derive from the early Havestadt tradition; the peculiar leg and backward head are prominent in collector-era Chilote versions; tongue-splitting, black-cat milk, a compulsory firstborn, and detailed timetables vary by modern reteller.

### E. Journeys outside the cave

**[F]** On rare occasions witches move the Invunche to another assembly or take it to harm a victim. It advances painfully on hands and one foot, sometimes beaten by its escorts. Its cries terrify listeners or cause *espanto*. Other versions say it cannot speak and only bleats. The contradiction is genuine: muteness and destructive cries belong to different tellings.

### F. Central-Chilean treasure guardians

**[RV: Vicuña Cifuentes, 1915]** Talagante informants said Imbunches guarded buried wealth that passed to witches if not recovered within a year. In Melipilla and Coihueco, the emphasis falls on stopping or sewing bodily openings. These are not merely defective versions of the Quicaví myth; they show geographic diffusion and local repurposing.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There is no evidence of a public cult of the Invunche:

- no temple;
- no priesthood dedicated to it;
- no festival;
- no votive inscription;
- no prayer or hymn;
- no pilgrimage;
- no theophoric personal name;
- no securely identified archaeological image;
- no historical community self-identifying as its worshippers.

The cave is a legendary or testimonial ritual location, not a temple. The Invunche is consulted, feared, confined, or used; it is not clearly worshipped. Later statements that witches kiss its posterior reproduce European Sabbath motifs and may reflect colonial or postcolonial demonological influence rather than an early Mapuche rite. **[SY/F]**

The 1880 record supplies allegations of feeding, herbal treatment, initiation oaths, magical books, councils, healing, poison, and internal jurisdiction. It does not document an “Invunche ritual” in the manner of an observed ethnographic ceremony. No connection to *nguillatun* is attested, and none should be inferred. Any relationship to *machitun* is indirect, through colonial authorities’ conflation of *machi*, healer, witch, and criminal sorcerer.

The state’s 1880 action is the principal documented suppression event. Circular No. 294 ordered the arrest not only of “brujos” and “hechiceros” but also of *machis*. The proceeding must therefore be read both as evidence about a feared organization and as an exercise of state power over Indigenous and rural healing institutions.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1765: unattested prehistory

Elements of the figure may be older than their recording, but no named pre-Columbian attestation is known. Neither archaeological remains nor the earliest conquest chronicles establish an Invunche cult. **[Unknown]**

### 1765–1795: Mapudungun cave-being

Febrés records cave-raised consultants; Havestadt adds infant mutilation; Molina makes them disciples within magical warfare. The being is plural or class-like, connected to *kalku*, and not yet the standardized Chilote guardian. **[EA]**

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: Huilliche–Chilote transformation

In the culturally mixed, missionized archipelago—Huilliche, Chono, Spanish, and mestizo—the cave-consultant becomes embedded in narratives of organized witchcraft. European demonological motifs, Christian language, Indigenous healing, rural politics, and colonial administrative forms combine without allowing each detail to be assigned neatly to one source. **[SY/historical reconstruction]**

### 1880: documentary crystallization

The Ancud proceeding preserves first-person judicial testimony naming Ibunche, Chibato, Quicaví, the Recta Provincia, and Chillpila. It is the earliest secure Chiloé-specific documentary complex, although not the first attestation of the being generally. The arrests disrupt the alleged organization and publicize its lore. **[EA as historical record]**

### 1908–1950s: print folklore

The proceedings are printed; Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, Cañas, Lenz-associated scholarship, Molina, and other collectors classify variants. During this phase the leg-on-back anatomy, stolen-child plot, goat traits, guardian office, and long lists of names become the public composite. **[F/LS]**

### Later twentieth century: decline as living Mapuche belief, expansion as national symbol

A modern survey reports that the figure was no longer readily found among Mapuche informants in Araucanía, while variants remained vigorous in Chiloé. The Invunche simultaneously becomes a general Chilean metaphor for arrested development, mutilated institutions, secrecy, and cultural deformity. **[MR]**

### Present

The being persists strongly in heritage presentation, tourism, school folklore, fiction, comics, music, visual art, podcasts, and internet bestiaries. This is cultural continuity of a story, not evidence of an organized modern cult. No reliable evidence was located for continuous or revived devotional worship, Invunche neopaganism, or a reconstructionist priesthood.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Joaquín Edwards Bello and “invunchismo”

From the 1920s onward, Joaquín Edwards Bello used **invunchismo** for a perceived Chilean tendency to deform, obstruct, or destroy what might otherwise develop beautifully or freely. His cultural metaphor detached the figure from literal witchcraft and made it an image of institutional and national self-mutilation. A 1959 article connected it to the “cult of ugliness.” **[MI as metaphor grounded in folklore]** [Scholarly analysis](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-22952009000200012&script=sci_arttext)

### José Donoso, *El obsceno pájaro de la noche* (1970)

Donoso’s novel is the most consequential literary reinvention. Its narrator Humberto Peñaloza/Mudito is progressively enclosed, reduced, sewn, bundled, deprived of speech and identity, and transformed into a “packet.” The novel does not merely retell the Quicaví legend. It turns **imbunchamiento** into a structural principle:

- bodies and identities fold into one another;
- houses become caves, wombs, prisons, and labyrinths;
- servants, old women, monsters, masks, sacks, and sealed rooms participate in systems of enclosure;
- authority manufactures deformity and then hides it;
- the Mudito is simultaneously victim, guard, narrator, secret, and object.

Thus Donoso universalizes the legend into a figure for repression, class power, monstrous creation, self-erasure, and the impossibility of a stable identity. Scholarship explicitly identifies the myth of the Imbunche, alongside the Chonchón and Minotaur, as one of the novel’s organizing mythic structures. **[MI/modern literary retelling]** [Literary study](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-22952012000100009&script=sci_arttext)

The novel’s composition extended for more than a decade and joined initially separate narrative projects. This matters because its Invunche pattern is an achieved literary synthesis, not the transcription of one oral tale. [Manuscript study](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-04622008000100009&script=sci_arttext)

### Later literature and cyberpunk

Jorge Baradit’s *Ygdrasil* relocates the Imbunche into a cyberpunk system of technological, bodily, and economic exploitation. Scholarship reads the borrowed monster as subordinate to a critique of globalized enslavement rather than as an ethnographic reproduction. **[MI]** [Study of Baradit’s bestiary](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-71812011000100010&script=sci_arttext)

### Comics

Alan Moore and Rick Veitch introduced an **Invunche** into DC’s *Swamp Thing* milieu in 1985. The comics version helped internationalize the name but belongs to DC occult fiction, not Chilean oral tradition. Its powers, continuity, and encounters with DC supernatural characters are modern invention. **[MI]**

Chilean comics and games have also adapted the being as an intelligent ally, generic cave monster, or collectible creature. A version that speaks fluently or knowingly rejects the traditional role is creative inversion, not a recovered variant.

### Film and visual culture

Roberto Aschieri’s documentary/essay film *Invunches* (Chile, 2013) used the figure and “invunchismo” to examine Chilean identity, distortion, and mutilation. Its published festival synopsis explicitly links the film to Edwards Bello, Jorge Edwards, Donoso, and Carlos Franz. **[MI/critical reception]** [Festival catalogue](https://www.museofigari.gub.uy/innovaportal/file/48729/1/catalogo.pdf)

Modern illustrations, sculptures, animations, tattoos, and tourist objects usually show the leg fixed behind the neck. That visual formula reflects Cavada and later syntheses rather than a surviving precolonial iconographic canon.

### Occultism, neopaganism, and politics

The dedicated contested-material search found:

- no documented Renaissance, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or classical grimoire tradition involving the Invunche;
- no established Invunche-centered neopagan or reconstructionist religion;
- no reliable evidence of ritual human sacrifice offered to it;
- no documented extremist political cult centered upon it.

Contemporary occult pages that insert the being into universal demonology, Satanism, “ancient shamanic genetics,” or a fixed pan-American monster hierarchy are modern syntheses. Claims that witches literally engineered hybrids, revived stillborn infants, or used an ancient genetic technology belong to modern speculative writing, not the early corpus. **[MI]**

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Indigenous core versus Chilote synthesis

The eighteenth-century Mapudungun attestations make an entirely European origin untenable: cave-kept consultants called *ivumche* were recorded before the 1880 Chiloé proceedings. Conversely, the fully developed Chilote monster contains traits comparable to European witch-Sabbath lore: goat imagery, the obscene kiss, Satanic assemblies, caves of Salamanca, and Christian counter-magic. The best-supported model is historical layering, not “purely Mapuche” or “purely Spanish.”

### Is the sewn leg ancient?

No early source located says that one leg is sewn to the back. Febrés knows cave-raised consultants; Havestadt knows sewn bodily openings; the 1880 deposition knows a hairy human captive and separate crawling Chibato. Cavada supplies the iconic leg and backward head. The anatomy may preserve older oral material, but its antiquity cannot be established before its collection. **[Unresolved]**

### Child or adult?

- child raised from infancy: Febrés and Havestadt;
- approximately fifty-year-old confined persons chosen by a council: 1880 testimony;
- stolen infant or young boy: twentieth-century folklore.

These are competing traditions. Treating the adult Ibunche of the trial as if the deposition described a stolen baby with a sewn leg is inaccurate.

### Guardian, oracle, or chief?

The oracle/consultant role is oldest. Guardian or porter is prominent in Chiloé. Some informants make the creature owner or chief of the cave; others explicitly deny it is head of the witches. The dominant modern statement that it is both guardian and supreme oracle merges variants.

### Ibunche and Chibato

The 1880 deposition separates them. Later sources call the Invunche “Chivato de la Cueva” or make it goatlike. This may represent folkloric fusion, but the date and mechanism of fusion are not known.

### The Recta Provincia as fact and representation

The court record proves that authorities investigated and prosecuted people accused of belonging to such an organization and that defendants used or acknowledged a complex administrative vocabulary. It does not validate every supernatural or criminal allegation. Confessions were elicited in custody under an openly hostile campaign; evidentiary caution is essential.

### State suppression and medicine

Recent historiography interprets Chilote witchcraft in relation to medical pluralism, Indigenous resistance, community justice, and state formation. The 1880 attack on *machis* cannot be reduced to enlightened suppression of superstition: it also displaced competing healers and local authorities. Conversely, allegations of poisoning and coercion should not be erased by an exclusively resistance-centered interpretation.

### Condition of the evidence

The foundation rests overwhelmingly on words:

- missionary dictionaries and descriptions;
- a learned civil history;
- court copies and later printings;
- folklore collections;
- literary transformations.

There is no agreed archaeological object, temple, ancient image, or Indigenous manuscript devoted to the figure. The physical supports nevertheless matter: Febrés’s 1765 printed dictionary survives; Havestadt’s 1777 printed volumes survive; the 1880 depositions survive through copied and published judicial material; twentieth-century oral reports survive as collector-mediated print.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| **Before 1765** | No securely named attestation presently established. Earlier chronicles discuss Mapuche religion and sorcery but do not securely document the Invunche. |
| **1765** | Febrés prints *Ivumche*: beings raised from childhood in sorcerers’ caves and consulted in magic. Earliest secure attestation. |
| **1777** | Havestadt reports children mutilated by sewing eyes, mouth, and anus and kept as cave consultants. |
| **1787** | Molina’s Italian *Saggio* calls Ivunches “hombres animales” and disciples of cave-dwelling *calcús*. |
| **1795** | Spanish translation of Molina circulates the description more widely. |
| **c. 1786–1788, recorded 1880** | Oral foundation legend places José de Moraleda’s magical contest with Chillpila in this period; Moraleda’s own record does not confirm it. |
| **c. 1860, according to 1880 testimony** | Mateo Coñuecar later claims to have taken kid meat to the Quicaví cave and seen Ibunche and Chibato. |
| **7 April 1880** | Circular No. 294 orders arrests of persons reputed to be *machis*, witches, or sorcerers. |
| **1880–1881** | Ancud proceedings document testimony about the Recta Provincia, Quicaví cave, Ibunche, Chibato, Chillpila, alleged poisonings, healing, and local jurisdiction. |
| **1908** | Depositions are printed as *Los brujos de Ancud: declaración de los reos*, widening public access. |
| **1914** | Cavada publishes the major Chilote synthesis in *Chiloé y los chilotes*: backward head and one leg fixed behind the neck or nape. |
| **1915** | Vicuña Cifuentes publishes central-Chilean and Chilote variants, including sewn bodily openings and treasure guardians. |
| **1920s onward** | Joaquín Edwards Bello develops *invunchismo* as a metaphor for Chilean cultural and institutional deformation. |
| **1930** | Pascual Coña/Moesbach’s major Mapuche life-history corpus appears without making the Invunche a central religious figure—important negative evidence. |
| **1950s** | Folklorists further systematize names, anatomy, cave duties, cries, and goat traits. |
| **1959** | Edwards Bello’s “cult of ugliness” treatment renews *invunchismo*. |
| **1970** | José Donoso publishes *El obsceno pájaro de la noche*, transforming the Imbunche into a major modern literary structure of enclosure and mutilated identity. |
| **1985** | Alan Moore and Rick Veitch introduce a fictional Invunche into *Swamp Thing*. |
| **Late twentieth century** | Ethnographic assessment suggests decline or absence in much of Araucanía while Chilote folklore and national metaphor remain productive. |
| **2011** | Scholarship analyzes the cyberpunk Imbunche in Baradit’s *Ygdrasil*. |
| **2013** | Roberto Aschieri’s film *Invunches* treats the figure as a national-cultural metaphor. |
| **2018** | The blind-winged Chilean cockroach *Austroectobius invunche* is named after the twisted Chilote monster—scientific nomenclature as modern reception. |
| **2026** | The Invunche remains active in literature, music, comics, podcasts, tourism, and digital folklore, but no organized historic or revived cult is documented. |

### Critical and contextual scholarship used

The modern linguistic work on Mapudungun loanwords is especially useful because it compares Febrés, Havestadt, Cañas, Cavada, Augusta, Coña, and later dictionaries. Recent historical work on the Recta Provincia is valuable for separating healing, community authority, criminal accusation, and state suppression. Literary scholarship establishes the difference between collected folklore and the deliberately metaphorical Imbunche of Edwards Bello, Donoso, and Baradit.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest defensible reconstruction begins with an eighteenth-century Mapudungun category of humanlike cave consultants raised by sorcerers. Mutilation through sewing appears by 1777. Chiloé’s 1880 testimony introduces a named Ibunche as one of two long-confined beings at Quicaví, but not yet the exact popular anatomy. The backward head and leg fixed to the back are securely visible in early-twentieth-century collection and become definitive through folklore books and modern art. There is no documented Invunche cult, temple, genealogy, public ritual, or pantheon of divine relationships. Its historical “life” is instead a movement from cave oracle, to alleged captive of a rural magical tribunal, to composite national monster, and finally to an unusually powerful metaphor for bodies, persons, institutions, and societies deformed by the powers that enclose them.
