# Peuchén (Piuchén): Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** A non-human predatory being—variously classified as a fabulous animal, flying serpent, blood-drinking monster, shapeshifter, *wekufü*-like harmful force, or local *ngen* (“owner/controller” spirit). It is not securely attested as a god or goddess and has no documented organized cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Andrés Febrés’s Mapudungun–Spanish *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* (Lima, 1765), under the form conventionally transcribed *pimuychen/pihuchen*: a serpent said to fly and whistle, whose beholder dies. Bernhard Havestadt’s Latin *Chilidúgú* (Münster, 1777) independently gives *Pihuichen*, “a flying serpent” whose sight or whistle brings death. Neither earliest entry says that it drinks blood.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

### Orientation

The Peuchén belongs to a historically layered complex of Mapuche, central-Chilean, Huilliche, and Chilote traditions. Its most stable early features are serpentine form, flight, whistling, dangerous perception, and elusiveness. Blood-drinking, livestock predation, bat-like anatomy, amphibious existence, metamorphosis, and employment by ritual specialists enter the record in distinct later witnesses and should not be projected wholesale into 1765.

There is no single canonical Peuchén. At least four documentary configurations must be distinguished:

1. **Early missionary-lexicographical Peuchén:** an unseen flying and whistling serpent that kills whoever sees or hears it.
2. **Late-eighteenth-century naturalized Piguchén:** a purported winged quadruped or large bat-like animal described by Juan Ignacio Molina.
3. **Nineteenth- and twentieth-century rural vampire:** a nocturnal predator blamed for bloodless livestock and sometimes human illness or death.
4. **Chilote Piguchén/Puchén:** a radically mutable or “degenerated” being—serpent, bird, quadruped, frog, bat, transformed rooster, or failed sorcerer—sometimes associated with rivers, lakes, marshes, or Chilote witchcraft.

### Names and orthography

Documented spellings include:

- **Pimuychen** — attributed by Rodolfo Lenz to Febrés’s 1765 printing.
- **Pihuchen** — found in later revisions of the Febrés lexicon.
- **Pihuichen / Pihuichén** — Havestadt and several nineteenth-century lexicographical sources.
- **Pihuychen** — a historical Spanish transcription used in etymological dictionaries.
- **Piwichen / Piwüchen / Piwüchën** — modern Mapudungun-oriented spellings.
- **Piuchén** — common Chilean and folkloristic spelling.
- **Peuchén / Pewchen** — regional or phonetic variants.
- **Piguchen / Piguchén** — Molina, Latcham, Cavada, and much Chilean popular literature.
- **Puchén** — documented in Chiloé.
- **Pinuychén** — recorded in older Argentine literature.

The forms are not names for demonstrably separate species. They principally reflect dialect, Spanish adaptation, and competing transcription systems.

### Etymology

**Modern lexical analysis / disputed:** Modern Mapudungun dictionaries commonly analyze *piwüchen* as *piwün/piwü-* (“to dry, become dry”) plus *che* (“person, people”): “the one that dries people.” A modern Mapudungun dictionary accordingly defines it as a vampiric entity that sucks blood and leaves people dried out.

This is semantically attractive but cannot automatically be treated as the historical derivation. The spelling *pimuychen* in the earliest lexicographical tradition is unstable, and early sources do not offer a morphological explanation. Another proposal treats the name as onomatopoeic or associates it with the call of a bird such as the *pidén*. No secure precolonial etymological testimony decides the question.

### Classification through time

- **1765–1777, securely attested early material:** *culebra/serpens volatilis*—a flying serpent.
- **1782, learned naturalization:** an alleged winged quadruped or large bat-like mammal.
- **Nineteenth-century folklore:** a nocturnal vampire or harmful fabulous animal.
- **Early-twentieth-century Chiloé, regional variant:** a metamorphic or physically degenerated being rather than one fixed species.
- **Pascual Coña corpus, Indigenous testimony published in 1930:** a blood-sucking bat or vampire; some machi are said to possess or employ one.
- **Contemporary Mapuche interpretation:** in some communities a dangerous being; in others a *gen/ngen*, an owner or controlling force of wet, vegetated places. José Quidel Lincoleo cautions against translating every such force automatically as an evil “demon.” [Quidel’s discussion](https://mapuche.info/wps_pdf/quidel2012.pdf)

“Demon” is therefore an outsider’s or context-dependent classification, not a universally valid Mapuche category.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Before 1765

No securely identified reference to the being has been established in Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana* (1569–1589), Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán’s *Cautiverio feliz* (written in the seventeenth century), Diego de Rosales’s *Historia general del Reino de Chile* (completed approximately 1674), or Luis de Valdivia’s early Mapudungun lexicography. These works remain important for the colonial representation of Mapuche religion, but they should not be cited as Peuchén attestations without a locatable passage.

Claims on modern websites that Rosales recorded the Peuchén have generally not supplied a book, chapter, or quotation. Pending such evidence, this dossier treats them as **unverified modern assertions**.

### Febrés, 1765

**Securely attested early material.** Andrés Febrés, a Jesuit missionary, printed the earliest presently locatable lexical notice. Rodolfo Lenz reports the entry as *pimuychen*, “a serpent which they say flies when it whistles, and whoever sees it dies.” The later Hernández revision of Febrés reads, in substance, “another [serpent] which they say flies” and “a snake which they say flies and whistles, *pihuchen*.”

This entry establishes:

- the name by 1765;
- classification as a serpent;
- flight;
- a whistle or hiss;
- lethal seeing.

It does **not** establish blood-drinking, bat wings, livestock predation, shapeshifting, a machi’s remedy, a cult, genealogy, or relations with other supernatural beings.

### Havestadt, 1777

**Securely attested early material.** Bernhard Havestadt’s Latin *Chilidúgú*, part IV, section 804, p. 745, gives:

> *Pihuichen: coluber, anguis, serpens volatilis, quem qui videt, vel audit sibilare, moritur: addo & qui non videt; uti visus est nemini.*

That is: “A snake, serpent, flying serpent; whoever sees it or hears it whistle dies. I add: also whoever does not see it, since it has been seen by nobody.” The final sentence is Havestadt’s skeptical joke, valuable because it shows a missionary lexicographer recording but distancing himself from the report. [Text and source reference](https://diccionariodechile.cl/entrada/havestadt-1777/1461-piwichen)

### Molina, 1782

**Learned systematization / disputed naturalization.** In *Saggio sulla storia naturale del Chili* (Bologna, 1782), Juan Ignacio Molina presents the *Piguchen* as a possible zoological “link” between birds and mammals: rabbit-sized, cinnamon-furred, sharp-snouted, with shining eyes, inconspicuous ears, membranous wings, short five-toed legs, and a tail that becomes broad like a fish’s. It hisses like a snake, flies like a partridge, lives in tree hollows, emerges at night, and—significantly—harms no one, feeding on insects.

This is incompatible with the lethal serpent of Febrés and Havestadt and with the later livestock vampire. Molina signals hearsay and conditional existence rather than presenting a collected myth narrative. Later Chilean writers alleged that he had described an unusual carcass passed off as the Piguchén, but the precise circumstances remain historiographical conjecture.

José Pérez García subsequently mocked the notion while noting a Santiago belief that a *piguchen* entered drains, killed poultry, and sucked their blood without eating the bodies. That passage is important because it documents blood-draining belief around the turn of the nineteenth century, rather than in Febrés’s 1765 entry. [Pérez García text](https://acspm.cl/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/HISTORIA-tomo-1.pdf)

### Nineteenth-century documentation

**Folklore and natural-history debate.**

- Naturalists attempted to identify the belief with a real vampire bat, especially a *Desmodus* species. This may explain part of the blood-sucking complex, but it does not explain the older flying serpent, lethal gaze, or magical transformations.
- José Toribio Medina’s literary sketch “El Piuchén,” first published in *Sud-América* in 1874, describes rural reports of sheep and mules found bloodless. Its narrator contrasts popular certainty with a naturalistic explanation involving *Desmodus*. This is literature built from contemporary folklore, not a verbatim Indigenous myth. [Medina text](https://www.bibliotecanacional.gob.cl/sites/www.bibliotecanacional.gob.cl/files/2026-02/JTMedina%20en%20breve%20Opusculos%20facticios.pdf)
- The English traveler Richard Longville Vowell supplied a widely repeated description of thick leathery bat wings, lizard-like claws, and a broad fish-like tail. Later Chilean dictionaries quoted this description as folklore.
- Lenz’s late-nineteenth-century work preserved and compared Febrés, Havestadt, and contemporary pronunciations. Lenz is crucial for textual transmission but is not evidence that every later trait existed in 1765.

### Twentieth-century ethnographic and folkloristic corpus

**Regional variants and folklore.**

- Tomás Guevara and Ricardo Latcham treat the Piuchén/Piguchén among Araucanian beliefs, usually as a fabulous vampire or anomalous animal.
- Félix José de Augusta’s *Diccionario araucano-español* records a *piwichen* form; his works are linguistic witnesses rather than a full narrative cycle.
- Pascual Coña’s Mapudungun testimony, written down and edited by Wilhelm de Moesbach as *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos* (1930), identifies two blood-drinking bats or vampires and states that they suck blood from animals and “Christians”; it adds that some machi possess or use a *piuchén*. The accompanying zoological notes try to map the names onto bats, illustrating editorial naturalization layered over Indigenous testimony. [Digitized Coña edition](https://es.scribd.com/document/610689179/1930-Pascual-Cona-rescaned)
- Francisco Javier Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914) gives the fullest early Chilote systematization. It explicitly says the being’s “true nature” had not been fixed and enumerates mutually incompatible forms. [Cavada edition](https://www.museodeancud.gob.cl/sites/www.museodeancud.gob.cl/files/images/articles-77081_archivo_01.pdf)
- Julio Vicuña Cifuentes, Oreste Plath, and later Renato Cárdenas consolidated oral and printed variants into Chilean folklore collections.
- Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s Argentine collections preserve Neuquén material, including later cosmogonic explanations that should be treated as locally collected variants, not as the primordial origin of every Piuchén tradition.
- Modern ethnographies by Faron, Grebe, Foerster, Bacigalupo, and Dillehay explain Mapuche ritual specialists, landscapes, illness, and sacred power. They provide interpretive context but do not collectively establish one canonical Piuchén doctrine.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

The being has no securely documented divine portfolio. Its recurring spheres are:

- nocturnal danger;
- serpents, bats, birds, and anomalous animals;
- livestock loss and unexplained wasting;
- blood and bodily depletion;
- dangerous seeing or hearing;
- wetlands, rivers, lakes, forests, ravines, tree hollows, and thickets;
- magical aggression or control in some regional accounts.

### Form

**Secure early core:** a flying serpent.

**Later and regional forms:**

- winged snake;
- giant lizard with bat wings;
- rabbit-sized winged quadruped;
- long-tailed bat;
- bird as large as a young turkey;
- rooster or hen with reptilian features;
- large frog with short wings;
- composite of serpent, bird, quadruped, and fish;
- human transformed through failed sorcery;
- an indefinitely mutable being without stable zoological form.

Cavada’s Chiloé chapter is particularly explicit that informants disagreed: serpent-bird-quadruped composite, frog, snake, and bat were all reported. Consequently, any polished modern image of “the” Piuchén as a dragon with red eyes and bat wings is a composite reconstruction.

### Whistle and lethal perception

The whistle is among the oldest securely attested traits. Febrés connects flight and whistling; Havestadt says death follows either seeing the creature or hearing it whistle.

The commonly repeated modern formula “its gaze paralyzes the victim so that it can drink the victim’s blood” combines two historically separable strands:

- **early:** sight or hearing causes death;
- **later folklore:** the gaze stupefies, immobilizes, or kills while the creature attacks.

“Paralysis” is thus a reasonable later folkloric formulation, not the exact claim of the 1765 or 1777 sources.

### Blood-drinking

Blood-sucking is securely documented by the later colonial or early republican period in Pérez García’s account of poultry and becomes central in nineteenth-century rural reports, Coña, Guevara, Cavada, Plath, and popular dictionaries. Typical victims are sheep, goats, cattle, horses, poultry, and sometimes sleeping humans.

Reported signs include wasting animals, animals found dead without having been eaten, and blood-like traces beneath a tree where the creature supposedly rested. Claims that it leaves exactly two punctures, always drains a body completely, or spares white animals occur in recent popular retellings but lack equivalent support in the earliest corpus.

### Powers and limitations

Attested across different sources—not as one universal inventory—are:

- flight;
- whistling or hissing;
- killing, stunning, or immobilizing through sight or sound;
- blood consumption;
- extreme longevity or immortality;
- metamorphosis;
- residence in water or wet ground;
- concealment in trees or ravines;
- use by a machi or sorcerer;
- resistance to ordinary capture because it is rarely or never seen.

No ancient or colonial source supplies canonical colors, numbers, holy days, celestial bodies, weapons, or regalia.

### Iconography

There is no securely identified precolonial or colonial image of the Peuchén and no iconographic canon comparable to that of a temple deity. Archaeological serpent imagery cannot be labeled Peuchén without an inscription, narrative context, or reliable chain of testimony.

Modern illustrated books, websites, role-playing games, and bestiaries favor the bat-winged reptile. Those are modern visualizations of literary folklore, not reproductions of an ancient image.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

### Genealogy

No secure early source gives parents, consort, offspring, siblings, or a fixed pantheon position.

Later origin accounts include:

- **Chilote regional variant:** a red rooster that lays the egg from which the Basilisk arises is left physically transformed into a Piguchén.
- **Chilote witchcraft variant:** an unsuccessful candidate for sorcery is thrown into a river as punishment and becomes a Piguchén.
- **Argentine regional variant:** Piuchenes are said to have arisen from pieces of a vast serpent shattered in a primordial or lunar catastrophe. This comes from modern oral collection and is not attested in Febrés or Havestadt.
- **Naturalistic reconstruction:** sightings of vampire bats or other nocturnal animals generated the legend. This is a scholarly hypothesis, not a traditional genealogy.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The surviving corpus is strikingly sparse.

| Other being | Interaction | Source and date | Evidentiary tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basilisco/Basilisk of Chiloé** | Cavada reports that when a red rooster lays the small egg from which the Basilisk is born, the rooster itself becomes *piguchén*—a new, repulsive, transformed being. This is an origin adjacency rather than dialogue or combat. | Francisco Javier Cavada, *Chiloé y los chilotes*, 1914, p. 109 | Regional Chilote folklore |
| **Unnamed primordial great serpent** | In material attributed to Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collection, individual piwichen are fragments of a great serpent broken apart during a cosmic catastrophe associated with the moon. | Oral material collected in the twentieth century; published in Koessler-Ilg editions from 1954/1962 onward | Regional Argentine variant / modern ethnographic record |

No secure source documents a meeting, combat, marriage, contest, or alliance between the Peuchén and **Ngünechen, Antü, Küyen, Pillan, Kai-Kai, Tren-Tren, Anchimallén, Chonchon, Invunche, Trauco, Pincoya,** or **Caleuche**. Co-membership in modern “Mapuche” or “Chilote mythology” lists is not an interaction.

A **machi** is a human ritual specialist, not a divine being. A **kalku** or Chilote brujo is likewise a human or human-coded harmful specialist in the relevant accounts; neither belongs in a god-to-god interaction table.

## 5. Major myths and narrative complexes

### The unseen flying serpent

**Early attested, 1765–1777.** A serpent flies and whistles; seeing it—or, in Havestadt, even hearing its whistle—causes death. Havestadt adds that nobody has seen it. This is the entire surviving early narrative nucleus. It has no named protagonist, genealogy, moral resolution, or ritual conclusion.

### The zoological Piguchén

**Learned systematization, 1782.** Molina converts the reported being into a hypothetical nocturnal animal. It becomes rabbit-sized, furry, winged, insectivorous, and harmless. This is not merely another visual variant: it reverses the monster’s moral and predatory character.

Later naturalists and lexicographers treated Molina’s account skeptically or connected the Piguchén with the common vampire bat. The vampire-bat theory explains why blood-draining became zoologically plausible in learned discourse, but the documentary sequence does not show that a bat alone generated the 1765 flying-serpent belief.

### The livestock vampire

**Folklore, late eighteenth century onward.** The creature enters henhouses, sheepfolds, or grazing areas at night and removes blood while consuming little or no flesh. Its presence explains animals that are suddenly dead, wasted, or bloodless. Medina’s 1874 story dramatizes how a rural community converts unexplained livestock death into certainty that the Piuchén has struck.

This complex became the dominant national Chilean image and later facilitated comparisons with the twentieth-century “Chupacabras.” Such comparison is a modern analogy, not evidence of continuous identity.

### The Peuchén of Tocohue/Tocoihue

**Chilote regional folklore.** A narrative preserved in modern Chilote collections describes a Peuchén associated with water and physical attack. The broader Chilote record places such beings in rivers, lakes, marshes, and deep pools. Versions vary in whether the creature is a vampire, a metamorphosed human, or a grotesque amphibious composite. [Chilote compilation](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0008652.pdf)

### The failed sorcerer

**Regional Chilote variant, documented 1914.** A candidate who fails to advance in the magical arts is thrown into a powerful river and becomes Piguchén—neither human nor any determinate animal. This account makes “Piguchén” a condition of supernatural deformation rather than the name of a zoological species.

### The transformed rooster

**Regional Chilote variant, documented 1914.** When a red rooster lays the Basilisk’s egg, it is itself left *piguchén*: changed into an unnatural and repellent form. Here the word functions almost adjectivally, denoting magical degeneration.

### The machi’s Piuchén

**Indigenous testimony published in 1930; interpretively difficult.** Pascual Coña’s text says some machi possess or make use of a *piuchén*. It does not justify a universal doctrine that all machi command one, nor does it establish that the machi and the creature are invariably enemies.

Later popular accounts often assert the opposite—that only a machi can identify, repel, or destroy the being through ceremony and herbs. These strands may reflect local differences: the ritual specialist can possess a dangerous helper in one account and counteract harmful power in another.

### Remedies and avoidance

The most recurrent later remedies are:

- leaving the place where the being feeds;
- interposing or crossing running water;
- calling a machi who knows the appropriate ceremony;
- ritual diagnosis, smoke, prayer-song, drumming, and plant medicines in the broader *machitun* complex.

No early lexical source specifies a remedy. No reliable primary account located for this dossier identifies one universally prescribed “Peuchén herb” or a fixed formula. Modern lists that name particular plants without an informant, locality, and recorded ceremony should therefore be treated as **modern elaboration**.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is no evidence of a Peuchén cult in the institutional sense:

- no temple;
- no priesthood dedicated to it;
- no festival;
- no altar securely identified as its own;
- no inscription or votive deposit naming it;
- no theophoric personal names securely derived from it;
- no sacrificial calendar;
- no oracle;
- no documented pilgrimage;
- no securely identified precolonial image.

The being belongs primarily to oral belief, environmental interpretation, illness discourse, lexicography, storytelling, and magical accusation.

Some later accounts connect it with machi or kalku practice. These are not evidence of worship. Possessing, deploying, diagnosing, or expelling a being is not equivalent to venerating it.

The wider *machitun* healing tradition may involve drumming, song-prayer, trance, plant medicines, diagnosis of spiritual aggression, and extraction or expulsion of harmful forces. General descriptions of those rites cannot automatically be relabeled “Peuchén rituals.” The Chilean Museum of Pre-Columbian Art describes machi practice as locally variable and assisted by music, songs, dancing, medicinal plants, and specialized helpers. [Machi Kimün overview](https://museo.precolombino.cl/chamanismo/11-machi-kimun-ayer-y-hoy/)

No evidence links human sacrifice, temple prostitution, institutional cult violence, or sacrificial killing specifically to the Peuchén. Searches combining the name with those subjects produced no primary documentation. Mapuche sacrifice as a broader subject has its own debated ethnography, but it must not be attached to this being by association.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1765: unattested background

Serpents, dangerous landscape powers, animal transformations, and ritual specialists were part of Mapuche worlds before the surviving Peuchén entry. Nevertheless, no pre-1765 object or text securely names this being. A precolonial origin is plausible as oral tradition but not directly documentable.

### 1765–1777: missionary lexical fixation

Febrés and Havestadt reduce an oral report to short dictionary entries. The being is a flying, whistling, lethal serpent. Missionary skepticism is already visible in Havestadt’s joke that nobody has seen it.

### 1782–early nineteenth century: naturalization and vampirization

Molina recasts it as a hypothetical winged mammal. Pérez García records a popular Santiago explanation of poultry deaths involving blood-sucking. The record therefore divides into learned zoology and vernacular predator belief.

### Nineteenth century: national rural folklore

The Piuchén becomes a vehicle for discussing peasant credulity, unexplained animal deaths, vampire bats, and the limits of natural history. Medina’s 1874 literary treatment demonstrates that the name was broadly intelligible to a Chilean readership.

### 1890s–1930s: salvage ethnography and regional differentiation

Lenz, Guevara, Latcham, Augusta, Cavada, and Moesbach/Coña collect or systematize language and oral material. This period produces much of the corpus now presented as timeless mythology.

It also reveals that Chiloé did not preserve a single stable animal. Cavada records *piguchén* as a category of unnatural transformation, as well as a bat-, serpent-, frog-, bird-, or composite-like creature.

### Mid- to late twentieth century: folklore canon

Vicuña Cifuentes, Koessler-Ilg, Oreste Plath, the projected *Enciclopedia Chilena*, and regional collectors canonized the Piuchén as a Chilean blood-drinking monster. The *Enciclopedia Chilena* describes a vampire feeding especially on sheep, developing from a small snake into either a large frog with weak wings or a turkey-sized bird with grotesque eyes and a powerful beak. It also preserves the Chilote sense of a magically deformed object or person. [Encyclopedia entry](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Enciclopedia_Chilena/Folclore/Piguch%C3%A9n%2C_El)

### Contemporary Mapuche interpretation

Current Indigenous scholarship resists treating every dangerous or ambivalent force as a Christian-style demon. Quidel describes *piwüchen* as appearing in bird or reptile form and, in some accounts, as a *gen* associated with wet or densely vegetated places. Some machi encounter such powers as part of their visionary landscape. This interpretation does not erase blood-vampire traditions; it demonstrates that “malevolent monster” is not exhaustive.

### Present folklore

The being remains current in Chilean and Argentine storytelling, dictionaries, education, fantasy illustration, tourist literature, and internet bestiaries. This is cultural continuity of a name and narrative complex, not proof that every present detail descends unchanged from precolonial tradition.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and folklore publishing

Oreste Plath’s *Geografía del mito y la leyenda chilenos* and similar anthologies gave the Piuchén national visibility. Children’s and regional books continue to adapt local narratives. A 2013 publication titled *El piuchén*, by Jorge Araya Cortés and Yuliana Tabilo Ramírez, depicts a strange nocturnal bird attacking people and animals near the river at Fundina, Río Hurtado. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/El_piuch%C3%A9n.html?id=cSswzwEACAAJ)

### Fantasy gaming

Modern role-playing adaptations turn the being into a standardized combat monster. One fifth-edition fantasy treatment assigns armor class, hit points, “vampiric touch,” charm and paralysis magic, poisonous bite, alignment, and a challenge rating. These are demonstrably modern game mechanics, not Mapuche attributes. [Modern game adaptation](https://www.5esrd.com/database/creature/peuchen/)

### “Chupacabras” comparison

Modern authors often call the Chupacabras a revived Piuchén or describe the Piuchén as an ancestral Chupacabras. The comparison rests on reported livestock exsanguination. No evidence establishes historical identity or direct descent; the Chupacabras phenomenon was named and mediated in a very different late-twentieth-century context.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated contested-material search found no reliable evidence that the Peuchén entered Renaissance hermeticism, theosophy, the Golden Dawn, a historical grimoire tradition, Wicca, or an organized neopagan cult. Modern occult webpages may list or invoke it, but no dated initiatory lineage or stable liturgy was located.

Claims that “ancient Mapuche priests worshipped the Peuchén,” that it had a temple cult, or that modern invocations reproduce an ancient rite should therefore be classified as **modern invention unless independently documented**.

### Political and commercial uses

No sustained nationalist, extremist, or party-political Peuchén appropriation was found. The name does occur commercially and geographically—including livestock breeding, localities, and the Cordillera del Piuchén—but a shared name does not prove religious significance.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was it originally a serpent or a bat?

The earliest evidence favors a flying serpent. Molina’s 1782 account favors a winged mammal; later naturalists proposed a vampire bat as the empirical nucleus. The correct historical conclusion is not that one side “wins,” but that serpent and bat interpretations are successive and partly interacting layers.

### Is “the one who dries people” the true etymology?

It is a credible modern Mapudungun analysis and fits vampiric narratives. It is not explicitly supplied by the eighteenth-century witnesses, whose spellings vary. It should be reported as a strong modern interpretation, not an uncontested ancient gloss.

### Monster, *wekufü*, or *ngen*?

Older ethnologists and popular literature often translate such beings as “evil spirits” or demons. Contemporary Indigenous scholarship emphasizes that *newen*, *ngen*, and *wekufü* are not simple equivalents of Christian angels and demons. A dangerous appearance may be an owner or controlling force of a place rather than metaphysical evil.

### Did the Chiloé tradition simply preserve the Mapuche original?

No. Chiloé inherited substantial Huilliche/Mapuche language and belief, but also underwent Spanish Catholic settlement and local development. Cavada’s Piguchén is unusually plastic and embedded in the island’s witchcraft complex. It cannot be used uncritically to reconstruct a unitary precolonial Mapuche being. A modern study of Chilote supernatural beings stresses the historical mixture and the biases of early collectors, who often labeled island traditions “superstition.” [Study of the Indigenous substrate](https://www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/sites/www.investigacion.patrimoniocultural.gob.cl/files/2022-04/El%20sustrato%20ind%C3%ADgena%20de%20los%20seres%20mitol%C3%B3gicos%20de%20Chilo%C3%A9.pdf)

### Source problems

The record is unusually vulnerable to circular citation:

- modern encyclopedias repeat Plath or Cavada without page references;
- internet articles merge Mapuche, Chilote, and central-Chilean versions;
- Molina’s speculative zoology is quoted as though it were direct Indigenous testimony;
- general machi healing procedures are converted into a specific Peuchén exorcism;
- modern fantasy illustrations are mistaken for traditional iconography.

The principal witnesses survive in printed books held and digitized by institutions including Chile’s Biblioteca Nacional/Memoria Chilena, Museo Regional de Ancud, and historical language projects. The underlying oral performances are mostly unrecoverable; collectors’ spelling, translation, selection, and Christian or natural-scientific categories mediate them.

| **1900–1910s** | Guevara, Latcham, and lexicographers systematize the being as Araucanian and Chilean folklore. |
| **1914** | Cavada publishes the major early Chilote account: multiple animal forms, extreme longevity, transformed rooster, and failed sorcerer. |
| **1930** | Moesbach publishes Pascual Coña’s testimony that blood-drinking vampire-like beings attack animals and humans and that some machi use a Piuchén. |
| **1954–1962** | Koessler-Ilg publishes Argentine Mapuche/Neuquén oral collections, including later cosmogonic material associated with the being. |
| **1948–1971** | The Chilean national encyclopedia project synthesizes the vampire, developmental, and Chilote transformation variants. |
| **1960s–1980s** | Oreste Plath and other folklorists establish the Piuchén in the national geography of Chilean myth. |
| **Late twentieth century** | Popular comparisons with the Chupacabras arise; these are analogical modern retellings. |
| **2012** | José Quidel Lincoleo’s Indigenous critique emphasizes the *piwüchen* as a potentially ambivalent *gen* rather than automatically a demon. |
| **2013** | A Chilean illustrated book, *El piuchén*, adapts a Río Hurtado version. |
| **Twenty-first century** | Internet bestiaries and tabletop games standardize the being as a bat-winged, blood-drinking, paralyzing fantasy monster. No organized modern cult is documented. |
| **2026** | The being remains active in folklore, education, popular art, fantasy gaming, and Mapuche discussions of landscape powers; its historical variants remain irreducible to one canonical form. |

### Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not a biography of one immutable monster. It is the history of a name attached successively—and sometimes simultaneously—to an invisible lethal serpent, a hypothetical winged mammal, a vampire bat, a livestock predator, a magically deformed being, a sorcerer’s familiar, and an ambivalent owner of wet places. Flight, serpent form, whistling, and lethal perception constitute the oldest documented layer. Blood-draining appears later but becomes dominant. Fixed bat wings, standardized paralysis, elaborate combat powers, and a single universal machi remedy are products of regional elaboration or modern synthesis.
