# Pillan: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the oldest colonial evidence, *Pillañ/Pillan* is not securely one anthropomorphic “god” but a polyvalent Mapuche term for a powerful sacred being or force, thunder and lightning, volcanic manifestations, and—especially in later descriptions—the potent dead ancestors of lineages and chiefs. Both singular *Pillan* and plural or collective *pillanes* are historically attested. “God,” “spirit,” “ancestor,” “volcano spirit,” and “demon” represent different periods, regions, speakers, and translations rather than interchangeable definitions.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana*, Part I (Madrid, 1569), repeatedly calls the Mapuche commander Lautaro “hijo de Pillán,” but the epic’s literary and Christianizing conventions make its ethnographic meaning uncertain. The earliest unequivocal documentary evidence that Mapuche people invoked and honored Pillan is Luis de Valdivia’s bilingual missionary sermons, printed in 1621: he condemns “adorar al Pillan y Huecuvoe,” naming Pillan reverentially, invoking him in sickness, and drawing blood while naming him. These passages describe practices while polemically reclassifying their recipient as the Christian devil.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

Pillan is best reconstructed as a historically changing sacred category centered on extraordinary power (*newen*), dangerous atmospheric and volcanic manifestations, and potent ancestry. Early missionaries encountered invocations addressed to Pillan and translated the name as “devil.” Eighteenth-century lexicographers alternated between “devil,” “first cause,” and the superior cause of thunder, lightning, and volcanic eruptions. Colonial historians also reported that eminent dead Mapuche—and sometimes dead Spanish enemies—became friendly or hostile pillanes who continued warfare in the air. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century ethnographers variously systematized these data into “thunder god,” “volcano god,” “high god,” “tribal ancestor,” or a class of ancestral spirits. Contemporary usage remains regional: it may denote an ancestral or volcanic power, a sacred mountain such as Pillan Mawiza/Lanín, a Christianized demon, or a name retained primarily in narratives and place-names.

No evidence establishes a universal, centralized “pantheon” headed by a single Pillan across all Mapuche, Williche, Pehuenche, Pikunche, and Argentine communities. That model is chiefly a scholarly or popular synthesis.

### Names and spellings

- **Pillañ / Pillaŋ / Pillan / Pillán.** Orthographies reflect different analyses of the final Mapudungun nasal and Spanish spelling conventions. *Pillañ* is common in Febrés and Augusta; Spanish normally writes *Pillán*.
- **pu pillañ / pillanes.** Plural or collective “pillanes.” Spanish *pillanes* is a Hispanicized plural; Mapudungun normally marks animate plurality with *pu*.
- **Fücha Pillañ / Futa Pillan / Buta Pillan**, “Great/Old Pillan.” This appears in later ethnographic systematizations and in the Christian-influenced creation narrative collected from Ñamco. It must not automatically be projected into the sixteenth century.
- **Wenu Pillañ / Guenu-pillan**, “Pillan of heaven” or “heavenly Pillan.” Juan Ignacio Molina’s late-eighteenth-century theological account uses comparable titles while presenting Pillan as a supreme creator. Molina’s formulation is a learned synthesis strongly shaped by European natural religion.
- **Ruka Pillañ / Rukapillan**, “house of the pillañ,” the Mapudungun name associated with Villarrica volcano. Modern translations include “house of the spirit(s),” “house of the ancestors/guardians,” and the missionary-derived “devil’s house.” Only the first element, *ruka* “house,” is etymologically transparent; the rest depends upon how *pillañ* is understood.
- **Pillan Mawiza**, “Pillan mountain,” used for Lanín in contemporary Neuquén Mapuche political-spiritual discourse.
- **Quetrupillán**, traditionally analyzed as *ketru* plus *pillañ*, conventionally “mutilated/truncated Pillan,” a volcano west of Lanín.
- **Pillan-leufu**, “Pillan river”; **Pillan-lelbún**, a place-name; **Melipilla**, popularly interpreted as “four pillanes,” although place-name etymologies require caution.
- **Pillantoki**, “Pillan axe.” Augusta’s texts record invocation of a descending *pillantoki*. Lenz compared it with the widespread thunder-axe motif, but the comparative reconstruction is not itself an indigenous explanation.
- **Pillantun / pillantún.** In older lexicographic and ethnographic usage, invocation or ceremony directed to Pillan, especially for weather. Later authors sometimes treated it as an antecedent or variety of *ngillatun*; that historical derivation remains debated.

### Etymology

**Secure:** *Pillañ* is a Mapudungun word documented from the sixteenth century. Febrés records the derivative *pillañn*, “to do all the aforesaid”—to thunder or produce the associated manifestations.

**Disputed reconstruction:** Molina derived *Pillan* from *püllü/pulli* “soul, spirit.” Later writers proposed a compound of *püllü* and *am*, two terms used in discussions of the person and the dead. Tadashi Yanai’s critique of Erich Böning emphasizes that this decomposition is hypothetical, not demonstrated historical morphology, and that Böning’s meanings for *püllü* and *am* were not adequately supported. Similarity to *püllü* is suggestive but does not establish derivation.

**Unsupported popular etymology:** Claims that the word intrinsically means “devil,” “evil god,” or one named volcano spirit reproduce colonial translation, not Mapudungun etymology.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The first attestations

#### *La Araucana* — Alonso de Ercilla, 1569, 1578, 1589

Ercilla’s Castilian epic was printed in three parts. It calls Lautaro *hijo de Pillán* repeatedly. Part I simultaneously says that the Araucanians honored the fallen celestial being and called him *Eponamón*. This is literary representation written within Renaissance epic and Christian demonology, not a verbatim indigenous account.

What it establishes is modest but important: by 1569, *Pillán* was recognizable in Spanish writing as a Mapuche-associated name denoting superhuman force or ancestry, sufficiently intelligible for “son of Pillan” to function as an exalted epithet. It does **not** establish an indigenous genealogy in which Lautaro was literally the child of a singular god.

#### Luis de Valdivia’s sermons — printed 1621

Valdivia’s *Sermón en lengua de Chile* provides the earliest clear cluster of ritual references:

- Sermon II condemns “adorar al Pillan y Huecuvoe.”
- It says people honored and named Pillan reverentially.
- Sermon IV says elders and ritual specialists advised people to name Pillan and Huecuvoe in sickness and necessity.
- The confessional asks whether the penitent drew blood from the body during drinking assemblies while naming Pillan.
- Sermon V denies that separate gods cause thunder, rain, crop growth, and healing.

The sermons therefore attest invocation, reverential naming, bodily bloodletting, ritual drinking, association with specialized powers, and joint mention with *Wekufü/Huecuvoe*. Valdivia’s claim that both are the devil is his evangelizing interpretation. A modern analysis calls this an “ethnocentric interpretation of indigenous otherness,” noting that Valdivia’s theological explanation cannot simply be substituted for Mapuche categories.

### Principal corpus

- **Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz***, composed 1673 from his 1629 captivity. Mapuche speakers are represented saying “our Pillan” had granted success in raids; another passage associates machi or ritual specialists with consultation of Pillan in caves. A proposed Spanish captive is described as intended “to sacrifice to our Pillan” for continued military success. The text is unusually rich but remains a retrospective Spanish literary construction.
- **Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del reino de Chile, Flandes Indiano***, completed 1674, first printed 1877–1878. Rosales reports thunder, lightning, volcanoes, ritual invocation, warfare, and the transformation of dead warriors into pillanes. Because publication occurred two centuries after composition, citations must distinguish manuscript date from print date.
- **Miguel de Olivares**, *Historia militar, civil y sagrada de Chile*, written around 1762. Olivares says Mapuche souls became friendly pillanes and Spanish souls hostile pillanes that weakened Mapuche fighters.
- **Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile***, Lima, 1765. His dictionary defines *Pillañ* as the devil “or a superior cause” said to make thunder, bolts, lightning, and volcanic eruptions—and says the manifestations themselves also bear the name.
- **Bernhard Havestadt, *Chilidúgú***, Münster, 1777, based on earlier missionary work. Havestadt’s Latin definition calls Pillan a *causa prima* to which thunder, lightning, thunderbolts, and comparable natural effects are attributed.
- **Juan Ignacio Molina, *Compendio de la historia geográfica, natural y civil del Reino de Chile***, Italian 1776/1787 editions. Molina systematizes Pillan as a supreme being with titles such as heavenly spirit, great being, thunderer, creator, omnipotent, and eternal. This is a major late-colonial source but an unusually harmonized natural-theological presentation.
- **Rodolfo Lenz, *Estudios araucanos***, 1895–1897. Provides Mapudungun texts, translations, comparison of early dictionaries, and narratives involving *pillantoki*, volcanic beings, and *Cherruve*. His theory of a thunder deity and thunder-axe includes comparative reconstruction beyond what informants explicitly said.
- **Félix José de Augusta, *Lecturas araucanas***, 1910, and *Diccionario araucano-español*, 1916. Augusta preserves indigenous-language narratives and an important appendix on Pillan. An informant at Panguipulli explained the word through fiery phenomena—volcanic fire, lightning, strange lights—by which spirits show anger. Another insisted that Pillan was powerful but “not God” and emphatically “not the devil.”
- **Tomás Guevara**, *Historia de la civilización de Araucanía* and related works, early twentieth century. Guevara systematizes Pillan as a thunder power but depends heavily on colonial texts and evolutionist religious categories.
- **Ricardo Latcham, *La organización social y las creencias religiosas de los antiguos araucanos***, 1924. Latcham develops an influential ancestor-cult interpretation: each group’s great ancestor was its Pillan, later tending toward a national ancestor. His additional totemic scheme is a scholarly reconstruction, not established indigenous doctrine.
- **Pascual Coña / Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach, *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX***, 1930. This bilingual/autobiographical corpus is central for Mapuche life and ritual, although editorial mediation by Moesbach and Lenz must be remembered.
- **Bertha Koessler-Ilg**, collections made principally in Neuquén during the twentieth century and published in *Tradiciones araucanas* and *Cuentan los araucanos*. These include Lanín and Tronador narratives in which Pillan is a volcanic ancestor or great spirit. Their Argentine provenance must not be generalized to Chile.
- **Louis C. Faron, *Hawks of the Sun***, 1964. Interprets ritual and morality through lineage, ancestors, and social structure; important for the claim that powerful ancestors continue as effective beings.
- **María Ester Grebe**, studies of Mapuche cosmology from the 1970s onward. Her influential vertically layered and quadripartite cosmological diagrams are ethnographic scholarly models, not ancient maps.
- **Rolf Foerster, *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche***, 1993. Traces the historical reclassification of Pillan and the development of Ngünechen under evangelization and Mapuche reinterpretation.
- **Ana Mariella Bacigalupo**, works on machi from the 1990s onward. These show that Mapuche ritual authority and spirits cannot be reduced to a fixed Western pantheon.
- **Lucía Golluscio and Lucas Curapil, “Pillañ Ngütram”**, 2023. A contemporary traditional narrative presented with attention to Mapudungun verbal art; it identifies Pillañ physically with the volcano and its sacred power.
- **Thomas Dillehay, *Monuments, Empires, and Resistance***, 2007. Archaeological reconstruction of Mapuche political and ceremonial landscapes; useful for resisting the assumption that absence of masonry temples means absence of organized sacred space.

### Negative findings

Ercilla does not provide a systematic Pillan theology. No pre-Hispanic inscription naming Pillan has been identified. Mapuche knowledge was transmitted primarily orally; the surviving early corpus was written by Spaniards, overwhelmingly missionaries or military authors. No manuscript, image, or archaeological object securely identifiable as a pre-contact depiction of Pillan is known.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**Securely attested early and colonial domains:**

- Thunder, thunderbolts, lightning, storms, and extraordinary celestial fire.
- Volcanic explosions and eruptions.
- Military fortune and the continuing power of dead warriors.
- Aid or danger invoked in illness and communal crisis.
- The potency of ancestors, particularly prominent chiefs and warriors.

Febrés’s definition is especially important because it applies *pillañ* both to the superior cause and to the phenomena. This cautions against imagining a Western-style person standing behind the thunder: manifestation and agent may not be categorically separate.

### Beneficial and harmful action

The record does not sustain a simple good/evil classification. Pillan may grant victory, rain, safe travel, healing help, or protection; anger may appear as storms, eruption, earthquake, drought, or other catastrophe. An ancestor may protect descendants while being hostile to enemies. The modern dictionary definition “maleficent divinity” therefore captures only one regional and Christianized development.

### Volcanoes

Pillan is associated above all with the Andes and volcanoes:

- **Villarrica/Rukapillan:** “house of Pillan.” Late-nineteenth-century and early-twentieth-century reports mention pouring drops of chicha or liquor toward Villarrica.
- **Lanín/Pillan Mawiza:** a living sacred mountain in Neuquén traditions and contemporary political-spiritual claims.
- **Tronador/Amun Kar:** Koessler-Ilg records a narrative in which a famous ancestral Pillan inhabits the crater and protects the community.
- **Llaima:** later oral and popular traditions place a court of pillanes there, but documentation is thinner and often comes through recent retellings.
- **Osorno, Quetrupillán, Mocho-Choshuenco:** occur in folklore and landscape narratives; traditions differ on which is protective, hostile, defeated, or silent.

The proposition “every volcano contains one pillan” is a modern generalization. Some sources distinguish the *ngen* or owner of a mountain from pillanes associated with it.

### Form and iconography

No standard anthropomorphic form is securely attested. Pillan may be:

- An unseen voice or force in thunder.
- Fiery light, lightning, eruption, or smoke.
- A dead ancestor dwelling in or beyond a mountain.
- A collective of warriors fighting in the sky.
- A singular “great spirit” in later theological narrative.

There are no securely identified ancient cult statues, masks, or temple images of Pillan. The lack of iconography is itself significant. Modern pictures of muscular fire gods, winged beings, or horned demons are illustrations, not recovered Mapuche canonical images.

### Emblems and associated objects

- **Thunder and lightning:** earliest consistent signs.
- **Volcanic fire, stones, smoke, and eruption:** consistent from the dictionaries onward.
- **Pillantoki, the Pillan axe:** securely a lexical/narrative object in Augusta; interpretation as a prehistoric thunder-god weapon is Lenz’s comparative hypothesis.
- **Yellow Pillan flag:** Augusta records a yellow cloth tied to a cane, suggesting solar or fiery color symbolism; his color explanation is interpretive.
- **Tobacco smoke:** Valdivia and later summaries associate tobacco offerings with Pillan.
- **Blood:** bodily bloodletting while naming Pillan; animal sacrifice; and colonial warfare accounts of human captives offered for victory.
- **Chicha or liquor:** libations, including drops cast toward a volcano.
- **East/Andes:** later cosmological diagrams often locate Pillan toward the eastern mountains. This is an ethnographic regional pattern, not a universal ancient rule.

No securely documented sacred animal, fixed number, weekday, or planet belongs exclusively to Pillan.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The early sources give Pillan no parents, birth, spouse, or offspring. That absence is important. The best-attested “genealogy” is social: distinguished human ancestors become pillanes, and descendants invoke them.

Later popular pantheon charts make Antü the chief Pillan, Kuyén his wife, Wangülen female counterparts of pillanes, and Peripillán a rebellious rival. These schemes draw on scattered twentieth-century folklore and synthetic retellings. They should not be presented as a pre-contact canonical family tree.

The Christian-influenced creation account told by Ñamco and published through twentieth-century folklore collection introduces a Great Pillan, subordinate pillanes, rebellion, divine punishment, and confinement in volcanoes. Its collector explicitly recognized biblical influence. It is valuable evidence for syncretic tradition, not for an unchanged ancient cosmogony.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The corpus does not contain a large stable cycle of named god-to-god episodes. The following are the interactions that can be tied to an identifiable source or documented collection:

- **Wekufü/Huecuvoe — jointly invoked or jointly condemned.** Valdivia says elders and ritual specialists advised naming Pillan and Huecuvoe in sickness and necessity; his sermons identify both with the devil while still using distinct indigenous names. *Sermón en lengua de Chile*, 1621. **Secure early joint attestation; colonial demonization.**
- **Ngünechen — functional displacement and partial identification.** Early-twentieth-century scholars report that prayers formerly directed to Pillan were increasingly addressed to Ngünechen/Ngenemapun or Christian God. This is not a narrated meeting but a historically documented relationship of theological replacement and overlap. Latcham, 1924; Augusta; Foerster, 1993. **Modern scholarly reconstruction of a colonial/postcolonial transformation.**
- **Cherruve/Cherufe — analogous fiery power, not a securely narrated encounter.** Lenz compares the volcanic and fiery functions of Pillan and Cherruve but provides no early story in which they meet. *Estudios araucanos*, 1895–1897. **Scholarly comparison; no interaction attested.**
- **Kai-Kai — Pillan supplies catastrophic rain in one collected flood narrative.** In the narrative attributed to Ñamco and published by Sperata de Saunière, Pillan causes rain that assists Kai-Kai’s inundation. Early-twentieth-century collection. **Regional folklore; collector-mediated.**
- **Trentren — indirect opposition during the same flood.** In that version Pillan’s rain aids the inundating side while Trentren protects survivors; they do not converse or fight directly. **Regional folklore; indirect interaction only.**
- **Trauco/Thrauco — Pillan’s rain assists him and Kai-Kai in Saunière’s version.** This combination is unusual and not corroborated as an early canonical episode. **Regional folklore, early twentieth century.**
- **Mocho-Choshuenco’s spirit — Rukapillan defeats it with a thunderbolt.** A field narrative recorded in 2015 says hostile Mocho-Choshuenco sent illness until protective Villarrica struck and truncated its crater. **Contemporary regional oral tradition.**
- **Quetrupillán — conflict or reunion with Rukapillan.** Recent Villarrica educational and popular narratives tell of combat between the two volcanic spirits. No early source for the fully developed plot was located. **Modern local retelling; antiquity unestablished.**
- **Antü — identified as a chief or solar Pillan in modern syntheses.** No early source examined here narrates Pillan meeting Antü as two distinct beings. **Learned/popular systematization, not a securely attested interaction.**
- **Peripillán — rebellion against Antü in online and recent popular pantheon narratives.** The elaborate celestial war, with defeated pillanes thrown into volcanoes, lacks a demonstrated colonial or early ethnographic source in the materials located. **Modern synthesis or invention unless a specific oral provenance is supplied.**
- **Wangülen — described as female companions or counterparts of pillanes in modern summaries.** No early dated episode of interaction was found. **Late systematization.**

Thus the widespread online “war of Antü and Peripillán” cannot presently be treated as an ancient canonical Mapuche myth.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The chiefs and warriors become pillanes

**Early core:** Rosales and later Olivares report that dead warriors continue to fight in the sky. The souls of Mapuche are friendly pillanes who strengthen their people; Spanish dead may become hostile pillanes aiding Spain. Thunder can consequently be understood as ancestral warfare.

This tradition explains several features at once: thunder as the sound of battle, Pillan’s interest in military victory, the formula “son of Pillan,” and lineage claims upon protective ancestors. Latcham converted these reports into a theory that every lineage possessed an ancestral Pillan and that the category later expanded into a national god. The sources support ancestral pillanes; they do not prove all of Latcham’s evolutionary sequence.

### Pillan and the volcanoes

Febrés and Havestadt provide the firm lexical core: eruption, thunder, and lightning are Pillan’s manifestations or effects. Later traditions locate ancestral spirits within particular volcanoes. Rukapillan becomes the most prominent named landscape.

In one twentieth-century Panguipulli explanation, fiery phenomena themselves are Pillan’s signs of anger. In the Tronador narrative collected in Neuquén, a renowned ancestor inhabits the crater and will protect the people. Contemporary Rukapillan narratives emphasize reciprocity: the volcano is not inert terrain but a powerful participant to be greeted, petitioned, and treated respectfully.

### The battle of volcanic beings

A 2015 field narrative from communities near Villarrica says Mocho-Choshuenco was a harmful volcano that spread disease. People prayed for relief; Villarrica’s spirit struck its opponent with lightning and cut its crater, explaining Mocho-Choshuenco’s form and inactivity. Recent retellings substitute Quetrupillán or make Villarrica fight multiple volcanoes.

This is a living etiological cycle, but its individual versions must be dated to collection. It is unsafe to reconstruct a single ancient “volcano war” from them.

### Trentren and Kai-Kai

The flood myth is old in Mapuche and Williche tradition, but Pillan’s part varies. Some versions concern the two serpents without Pillan. In Saunière’s collected version, Pillan produces rain to help Kai-Kai and Trauco flood the world. Later synthetic accounts make the serpents children of rival pillanes or consequences of a primordial celestial war. Those genealogies are late and poorly sourced.

The 1960 Collileufú sacrifice was interpreted by Jorge Araya in relation to the Trentren–Kai-Kai complex: earthquake and tsunami made mythic catastrophe immediately present. That historical ritual did not necessarily address Pillan by name and must not be used to prove that every flood-myth sacrifice was offered to Pillan.

### The Great Pillan and rebellious spirits

Ñamco’s creation account, collected and published in the early twentieth century, describes a Great Pillan and lesser beings who rebel and are confined within volcanoes. The collector considered the account a mixture of Mapuche and biblical material. Its rebellion, supreme creator, and fallen subordinate spirits closely resemble Christian cosmology. It is therefore a prime example of **syncretic folklore**, not material to discard and not material to backdate.

### Pillan helps brothers pass a sorcerer’s tests

A story summarized in Chilean folklore literature makes Pillan a benefactor who helps brothers complete tasks imposed before they may marry two fox women. This counters the reduction of Pillan to a destructive demon. It is, however, a collected folktale rather than early ritual theology.

### Pillañ Ngütram

The narrative published by Golluscio and Curapil in 2023 identifies Pillañ with the volcano in its physical manifestation and sacred power. Its importance lies partly in presentation as Mapudungun oral art rather than as a fragment extracted to fit a comparative pantheon.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Sacred places rather than temples

No Pillan temple, permanent priestly institution, or securely identified cult image is known. Sacred action occurred at communal ritual grounds, mountains, volcanoes, caves, stones, homes, and open-air assembly places. Archaeological monumentality in Mapuche territory includes mounds, fields, cemeteries, rehue, and politically meaningful landscapes, but none can presently be labeled a “Temple of Pillan.”

### Ritual practitioners

- **Machi:** healing and divinatory authorities. Colonial writers associate machi with consultation of Pillan; modern machi practice varies greatly and is more often framed through Ngünechen, spirit helpers, ancestors, and territorial beings.
- **Longko and other lineage authorities:** chiefs possessed special ancestral relationships, and eminent chiefs could become pillanes.
- **Ritual elders and speakers:** Valdivia attributes instruction about naming Pillan to “old people and sorcerers,” pejorative missionary terminology that should not be accepted as neutral classification.
- No hereditary “priesthood of Pillan” is securely documented.

### Practices

- Reverential naming and invocation.
- Naming Pillan during illness and necessity.
- Bloodletting during drinking assemblies, as recorded by Valdivia’s confessional.
- Tobacco smoke offerings.
- Libations of chicha or liquor; late-nineteenth-century observers report drops cast toward Villarrica.
- Animal sacrifice and communal consumption during *ngillatun*. Whether every such rite once addressed Pillan is Latcham’s reconstruction, not established fact.
- Weather petitions, including requests for rain or cessation of destructive weather.
- Invocations for military success.
- Respectful avoidance of, or protocol toward, volcanic summits.
- Consultation at caves, according to Núñez de Pineda’s account.

### Human sacrifice and violence

The evidence must be separated into distinct categories:

1. **Colonial warfare testimony:** Núñez de Pineda represents Mapuche speakers as intending to sacrifice a Spanish captive to Pillan for military success. Rosales describes the killing of a captive, extraction of the heart, blooding of weapons, distribution of the heart, and an oath of military unity. These sources document colonial authors’ testimony about wartime rites; they do not establish a regular temple-sacrifice institution.

2. **1960 Collileufú:** After the Valdivia earthquake and tsunami, a Lafkenche community at Collileufú sacrificed the child José Luis Painecur during a *ngillatun* intended to stop the catastrophe. The event is historically documented and has been studied by Jorge Araya and Natalia Caniguan. It was exceptional, not evidence of routine twentieth-century Mapuche practice. Sources relate it particularly to the sea, earthquake, and Trentren–Kai-Kai myth; a direct dedication to Pillan is not securely established.

3. **Animal sacrifice:** Sheep, goats, chickens, and historically camelids or horses appear in ritual accounts. Blood and chicha mediate reciprocity among people, animals, and sacred powers. Modern Pehuenche ethnography shows animal blood’s role in relations with Ngünechen; it should not automatically be reassigned to Pillan.

No evidence supports “temple prostitution” in Pillan worship.

### Politics and economy

Pillan’s ancestral character connected ritual to leadership and warfare: invoking the ancestor affirmed descent, territorial right, and the moral continuity of the group. Spanish missionary denunciation was also political. Reclassifying Pillan as Satan delegitimized Mapuche ritual authorities and made conversion part of frontier governance.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 1569: unattested pre-contact history

Archaeology demonstrates long-lived ceremonial and political landscapes in south-central Chile, but no pre-contact written name or securely identified Pillan image exists. The word and associated practices were almost certainly older than Ercilla’s publication, yet their pre-contact form cannot be directly recovered.

### 1569–1621: literary recognition and missionary confrontation

Ercilla turns Pillan into heroic genealogy—“son of Pillan”—while embedding Mapuche religion in Christian demonology. Valdivia documents actual invocation more clearly and attacks it as sinful worship of the devil. This begins a durable semantic struggle: missionaries use an indigenous sacred name while insisting that its bearer is Satan.

### 1621–1674: ritual, warfare, and ancestry

Núñez de Pineda and Rosales show Pillan in political-military life, divination, sacrifice, and postmortem ancestral power. Singular and plural meanings coexist. The evidence is richest here but wholly mediated through colonial male authors.

### 1762–1787: lexicography and theological systematization

Febrés preserves the remarkably broad semantic range: cause, devil, thunder, lightning, and eruption. Havestadt elevates Pillan to *causa prima*. Molina constructs a near-monotheistic creator bearing multiple titles. These are not simply descriptions of one unchanged doctrine; they are products of missionary translation, Enlightenment theories of natural religion, and indigenous explanations supplied in colonial situations.

### Nineteenth century: demonization, displacement, and ethnographic survival

Missionaries increasingly promoted Ngünechen and Christian God-language. Some communities retained Pillan for volcanic or ancestral power; elsewhere the term acquired a negative “demonic” sense. Travelers continued to report libations toward volcanoes. The Chilean and Argentine states occupied Mapuche territory through the “Pacification of Araucanía” and the “Conquest of the Desert,” violently transforming ritual landscapes, mobility, and political institutions.

### 1895–1930: the araucanist archive

Lenz, Guevara, Augusta, Latcham, Coña, and Moesbach recorded language and tradition during profound dispossession. Their work preserved indispensable evidence but also imposed evolutionist stages—animism, totemism, high-god religion—or missionary categories. Pillan became alternatively “thunder god,” “tribal ancestor,” or a formerly supreme deity displaced by Ngünechen.

### 1930–1970: folklore collections and structural anthropology

Koessler-Ilg recorded Argentine narratives of mountain ancestors; Faron emphasized lineage, morality, and ritual structure. Folklorists incorporated Pillan into national “Chilean mythology,” often detaching narratives from named communities and tellers. The category increasingly resembled a pantheon entry.

### 1970–2000: cosmology, shamanism, and historical critique

Grebe’s cosmological models and Bacigalupo’s work on machi restored complexity but could still be simplified in popular diagrams. Foerster treated the movement from Pillan toward Ngünechen as a historical transformation involving evangelization rather than a timeless system.

### 2000–present: territorial personhood and revitalization

Contemporary scholarship increasingly treats volcanoes and mountains as living relational beings rather than “symbols of nature.” Communities around Rukapillan articulate reciprocity with the mountain; Neuquén communities have sought recognition of Pillan Mawiza/Lanín as a sacred site. Current ritual life is diverse and frequently interwoven with Catholic or Protestant Christianity. There is no census establishing a separate organized “Pillan religion.”

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### National folklore

Schoolbooks, tourist literature, and mythology encyclopedias commonly present Pillan as “the Mapuche god of thunder, fire, earthquakes, and volcanoes.” This is a useful mnemonic but collapses ancestor, manifestation, class of beings, and singular deity.

Oreste Plath’s *Geografía del mito y la leyenda chilena* and the collections of Vicuña Cifuentes and Cavada helped nationalize regional oral traditions. Chiloé material is particularly syncretic: Williche concepts intermingle with Iberian Catholic saints, devils, witches, and maritime beings. A Pillan episode appearing in a Chilote compilation should therefore be labeled regional rather than “the Mapuche version.”

### Popular pantheon building

Modern websites often supply a complete divine dynasty:

- Antü rules all pillanes.
- Kuyén is his wife.
- Peripillán rebels.
- Pillanes and Wangülen divide into opposing armies.
- Defeated spirits become volcanoes.
- Trentren and Kai-Kai are children of rival divine factions.

Parts may derive from oral motifs, but the integrated system is not found in the early sources reviewed here. Until a narrator, locality, collection date, and publication can be supplied, the complete pantheon should be classified as **modern systematization**, and some details as possible **modern invention**.

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search found no evidence that Pillan entered Renaissance Hermeticism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or established European grimoires. Claims placing Pillan in an “ancient occult hierarchy” are modern inventions.

Contemporary Mapuche spiritual and territorial practice should not be indiscriminately labeled neopaganism. Small non-Mapuche esoteric and internet communities may invoke Pillan, but no reliable evidence establishes their scale or historical continuity.

### Politics and sacred-land claims

Pillan Mawiza/Lanín is central to an explicitly contemporary Mapuche project seeking recognition of the volcano as a sacred living place. This is not merely folklore marketing: it concerns jurisdiction, park management, summit access, tourism, and indigenous authority. Claims of ancient continuity and present political formulation can both be true; the documented institutional campaign belongs chiefly to the twenty-first century.

### Commercial and technological use

The Chilean state-owned aircraft manufacturer ENAER named its trainer the **T-35 Pillán**. It entered Chilean Air Force training service in 1985 and was exported to several states. The name turns a sacred term associated with atmospheric power into a national military-technical brand. ENAER continues to use it in the Pillán II program.

Rukapillan and Pillan are also used in tourism, outdoor recreation, educational programs, festivals, and commercial branding. Such uses often translate the word as “spirit” without its ancestral and colonial history.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Pillan a single god or a class?

Both usages are documented. Singular grammar, missionary “supreme cause” language, and some narratives support a singular great Pillan. Rosales, Augusta, and later sources support multiple ancestral pillanes. The safest conclusion is historical polysemy rather than a choice between mutually exclusive definitions.

### High god or ancestor?

Molina, Havestadt, and some later writers present Pillan as a supreme cause or creator. Latcham and Faron emphasize ancestry. These need not describe identical communities or periods. Missionaries also tended to seek either “God” or “devil,” producing artificial singularity.

### Pillan and Ngünechen

Scholars dispute whether Ngünechen was an ancient supreme being, a lineage/category term transformed into God under evangelization, or a historically layered Mapuche-Christian formation. Casamiquela and Dowling questioned simple identity between Pillan and Ngünechen; other writers proposed that attributes passed from an ancestral Pillan to Ngünechen. The evidence shows overlap and displacement, but not a clean one-time replacement.

### Pillan and püllü

The derivation from *püllü* “spirit” is plausible-looking but unproved. Böning’s *püllü + am* theory has been criticized for weak linguistic and ethnographic grounding. It must not be cited as established etymology.

### “Devil” as translation

Valdivia, Febrés, later missionaries, and some modern communities use devil-language. Historically, however, “devil” first appears as Christian classification. Augusta’s informant explicitly rejected it. Later Mapuche speakers may genuinely use Pillan with demonic meaning, but that is a product of semantic history, not evidence that sixteenth-century meaning was “Satan.”

### Thunder god and Indo-European comparison

Lenz proposed that *pillantoki* was a thunder-god axe comparable to thunder weapons elsewhere. The Mapuche word and narrative are attested; the universal thunder-god reconstruction belongs to late-nineteenth-century comparativism.

### Evidence limits

- No indigenous pre-contact writing.
- No securely identified image.
- Early sources are hostile or assimilating.
- Many twentieth-century collections omit exact narrator, language, date, or recording circumstances.
- Missionary dictionaries are indispensable but encode Catholic categories.
- Modern web mythology repeatedly circulates unsourced elaborations.
- The archives are distributed among Chilean and Argentine libraries, missionary collections, national archives, museums, and later editions; the original oral performances are largely irrecoverable.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1569:** Archaeologically documented Mapuche ceremonial landscapes exist, but Pillan is not named in recoverable pre-contact evidence.
- **1569:** Part I of Ercilla’s *La Araucana* prints the formula “hijo de Pillán.” **Earliest secure written occurrence.**
- **1578 and 1589:** Later parts of *La Araucana* repeat the epithet and deepen its literary association with Lautaro.
- **1621:** Valdivia’s sermons condemn worship, reverential naming, invocation in sickness, and bloodletting while naming Pillan. **Earliest unambiguous cultic attestation.**
- **1629:** Núñez de Pineda experiences captivity among Mapuche; his later account supplies dialogue about Pillan, war, machi, and sacrifice.
- **1640–1641:** Major volcanic activity is remembered near the period of the Parliament of Quilín. Later writers link eruptions with pillanes, but a contemporaneous Mapuche interpretation of a particular 1640 volcano is not securely preserved.
- **1673:** Núñez de Pineda completes *Cautiverio feliz*.
- **1674:** Rosales completes *Historia general del reino de Chile*.
- **1762:** Olivares describes friendly Mapuche and hostile Spanish pillanes continuing the colonial war after death.
- **1765:** Febrés defines *pillañ* as devil or superior cause of thunder, lightning, and eruptions, and also as those effects.
- **1777:** Havestadt publishes *Chilidúgú*, calling Pillan a first cause to which extraordinary natural phenomena are attributed.
- **1776–1787:** Molina develops the supreme-being interpretation and provides multiple theological titles.
- **1877–1878:** Rosales’s seventeenth-century history is first printed.
- **Late nineteenth century:** Mission and traveler reports document libations and belief in a powerful volcano-dwelling Pillan while Christian demonization continues.
- **1895–1897:** Lenz publishes *Estudios araucanos*.
- **1910:** Augusta publishes *Lecturas araucanas*, including indigenous-language narratives and comparative notes on Pillan.
- **1914:** Cavada publishes *Chiloé y los chilotes*, documenting the syncretic folklore environment of Chiloé.
- **1916:** Augusta publishes his dictionary and description of ancestral pillanes and volcanic/fire manifestations.
- **1924:** Latcham formulates the lineage-ancestor theory and proposes continuity between older Pillan-directed rites and modern *ngillatun*.
- **1930:** Moesbach publishes Pascual Coña’s testimony with Lenz’s revision.
- **Mid-twentieth century:** Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collections preserve Pillan as mountain ancestor and great spirit.
- **1960:** José Luis Painecur is sacrificed at Collileufú after the Valdivia earthquake and tsunami. The rite is linked in scholarship chiefly to catastrophe and the Trentren–Kai-Kai myth, not securely to a named Pillan.
- **1964:** Faron’s *Hawks of the Sun* interprets Mapuche morality and ritual through kinship and ancestors.
- **1970s–1990s:** Grebe, Foerster, and Bacigalupo reshape scholarship through cosmology, historical religious change, and machi-centered ethnography.
- **1985:** The T-35 Pillán enters Chilean Air Force training service.
- **1993:** Foerster publishes *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche*.
- **1998/1999:** Jorge Araya publishes a major analysis of the Collileufú sacrifice.
- **2007:** Dillehay’s *Monuments, Empires, and Resistance* emphasizes Mapuche ceremonial landscapes and resistance.
- **2015:** Fieldwork near Villarrica records a narrative of Rukapillan defeating harmful Mocho-Choshuenco.
- **2022:** Scholarship documents the political-spiritual project to recognize Pillan Mawiza/Lanín as a sacred Mapuche site.
- **2023:** Golluscio and Curapil publish *Pillañ Ngütram*, a contemporary Mapudungun traditional narrative.
- **2024–2026:** Participatory research and arts projects around Rukapillan continue to treat the volcano as a living social and sacred presence rather than merely a geological hazard.
