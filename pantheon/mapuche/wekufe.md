# Wekufe (Huecuve): Research Dossier

**Class:** A Mapuche category of harmful power or harmful beings: variously a spirit, a class of spirits, an embodied pathogenic intrusion, the illness or misfortune produced by such an intrusion, and—under Christian influence—a “demon” or “Devil.” It is not securely reconstructable as one personal god with a fixed biography.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Alonso de Ovalle’s *Histórica relación del Reyno de Chile*, printed in Rome in **1646**, reports *guecubus* as subordinate beings commanded by *Güenupillán*. Ovalle says that he obtained this information from Francisco Almendras, a Spaniard held for decades among Mapuche communities. This is the earliest presently verified printed occurrence found in this survey. Luis de Valdivia’s 1606 vocabulary may contain the related spelling *huecuvoe*, as later scholars report, but that entry has not been directly verified here; it therefore cannot displace Ovalle as the earliest secure attestation. Contrary to repeated modern claims, *La Araucana* (1569–1589) does not provide a verified occurrence of *wekufe*: Ercilla’s Fitón is a literary diviner, and later authors—not Ercilla—compare him with *huecubuyes*.

**Evidentiary labels used below**

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** seventeenth- or eighteenth-century primary evidence.
- **[LEX] Lexicographic attestation:** missionary or modern dictionary evidence.
- **[ET] Ethnographic attestation:** recorded testimony or observed practice.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** materially localized Mapuche, Huilliche, Chiloé, Neuquén, Río Negro, or Chilean-folklore form.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically produced equation, especially with the Christian Devil.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an ethnographer’s or theologian’s ordered model, not necessarily an indigenous consensus.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an analytical interpretation of older evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern claim.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identification

*Wekufe* is best treated as a **relational category of harm**, not as the proper name of a single deity. Depending on speaker, period, and context, it can denote:

1. a harmful incorporeal agency;
2. one member of a plurality, *pu wekufü*;
3. an object or creature through which harm enters a body;
4. the “dart,” splinter, straw, tooth-like object, reptile, insect, hair, or other substance extracted during curing;
5. the illness itself;
6. misfortune, crop failure, drought, pestilence, accident, or destructive disorder;
7. a power directed by a *kalku*;
8. under colonial and later Christian usage, the Devil or a demon.

That semantic mobility is already visible in Andrés Febrés’s eighteenth-century definition: the word meant both the objects supposedly extracted by machi, “any illness,” and a “deity or being of reason” regarded as the cause of death, illness, and affliction. Félix José de Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* explicitly asks what kind of being *Wekufù* is, demonstrating that no single definition had become universally authoritative even by 1910. [EA, LEX, ET]

### Documented forms

The extensive spelling variation largely reflects attempts to represent Mapudungun sounds in Spanish, Latin, German-influenced, or modern orthographies:

- **wekufe / wekufü** — common modern spellings;
- **Wekufù** — Augusta’s spelling;
- **huecufu / huecufú / huecufe** — nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Hispanic spellings;
- **huecuvu / huecuvú / uecuvu** — common historical and Argentine forms;
- **guecubo / guecubu / güecubo / güecubu** — Rosales and related colonial spellings;
- **guecubus / güecubes** — Ovalle’s Hispanicized plural;
- **huecuvoe** — a form attributed in later scholarship to Luis de Valdivia;
- **Huekúfu** — a spelling in Argentine compilations;
- **huecú** — Argentine or north-central Chilean abbreviation and folkloric derivative.

Several much longer Internet lists—*watuku, huecovoe, giiecubu, güecugu*, and others—circulate without adequate provenance. They may represent transcription errors, OCR corruption, unrelated regional vocabulary, or one-off orthographies. They should not be presented as equivalent ancient names without a traceable source. [MI]

### Etymology

A popular modern explanation derives *wekufü* from an expression meaning “the one from outside” or “being outside.” This explanation is repeated widely online but was not securely established in the historical dictionaries examined here. It must therefore be marked **disputed or unverified**, not stated as settled etymology. [MI/MSR]

Rodolfo Lenz catalogued the historical spellings but did not resolve the word into a secure transparent compound. The variation between final *-e, -u,* and *-ü* is better explained first as transcriptional and dialectal variation than as evidence for separate beings. [LEX, MSR]

The resemblance alleged in modern occult literature between *huecuvu* and Latin *succuba/succubus* has no defensible linguistic basis. A modern esoteric work quoted and rejected this comparison: the sounds and histories differ, and the purported form “súcubu” was itself manufactured to increase the resemblance. [MI]

### Titles and classifications

Wekufe has no securely attested set of temple epithets or formal cult titles. Descriptive combinations include:

- **weda/wedá wekufü** — “bad/evil wekufe” or “bad spirit,” recorded by Augusta;
- **trülke wekufü** — “hide/skin wekufe,” the dangerous animated hide or “Cuero” of a narrative tradition;
- **pu wekufü** — plural/class expression, “the wekufe beings”;
- **Wekufe Mapu / Huecuvu Mapu** — “land or country of wekufe,” applied in Argentina to harsh or dangerous terrain;
- compounds connected with expelling, burning, or medicating against wekufe, including the place-name **Ləpemwekúfùwe**, interpreted by Augusta as “place where the wekufe was burned.”

“God of evil,” “Mapuche Satan,” and “lord of demons” are misleading modern titles. The primary record supports neither an exclusive divine office nor a stable individual sovereign named Wekufe. [SI, MI]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 Alonso de Ovalle, 1646

Ovalle’s *Histórica relación del Reyno de Chile* was printed in Spanish and Latin at Rome in 1646. It is therefore the earliest secure printed witness identified here. Quoting Francisco Almendras, Ovalle says that the Mapuche believed in *Güenupillán*, “their God,” who had many *guecubus*, described as his *ülmenes* or great chiefs and placed alongside volcanoes among the beings under his command. [EA]

This passage differs sharply from the later dominant representation of wekufe as exclusively hostile. Ovalle’s beings appear as subordinate supernatural chiefs in a hierarchy. Since the statement comes through a Spanish captive and a Jesuit author, its vocabulary—“God,” hierarchy, subordinates—may partly reflect translation into European political and theological categories. It nevertheless proves that a cognate plural was circulating by the mid-seventeenth century.

### 2.2 Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz*

Written in the seventeenth century and first printed in 1863, *Cautiverio feliz* reports *huecubuyes*, also called *renis*, as old-time specialists comparable to priests. They wore long garments and long or artificial hair, lived apart in mountains and caves, and consulted *Pillán*. [EA]

The lexical relationship is contested. Lenz and later writers asked whether *huecubu-ye/buye* meant “lord or specialist of the huecubu,” while other scholars identify Núñez’s term as a corruption of *boquibuye*, an office associated with the *foye* or canelo. It cannot safely be used as proof that a priesthood worshipped Wekufe. [EA, MSR]

### 2.3 Diego de Rosales, completed c. 1674; printed 1877–1878

Rosales supplies the richest early account. He records *Gucuibu/Guecubo/Guecubu* as:

- a cause of crop failure, drought, pests, and lack of fish;
- an agency of earthquakes;
- something that tires a horse by riding on it;
- a cause of sickness and death in people and livestock;
- an invisible dart sent by an enemy;
- the harmful object which a healer appears to extract from the patient.

Rosales reports the accusation that an enemy “threw a Guecubo and an invisible arrow” into a victim’s heart or painful body part. He also records the social consequence of diagnosis: death could lead a diviner to identify an alleged human perpetrator, provoking retaliatory killing. His presentation is polemical—he repeatedly attributes indigenous practices to the Christian Devil and accuses healers of deceit—but the concrete vocabulary of darts, objects, illness, diagnosis, and counteraction is indispensable early evidence. [EA, SI]

### 2.4 Miguel de Olivares, composed 1762; printed 1864

Olivares says that people did not regard death from illness as natural but attributed it to *Huecubu*, “sorcery inserted into the body.” He adds a revealing colonial contact episode: baptism or confession followed by death could be interpreted as this kind of dangerous intrusion. That testimony documents not merely a static belief but the translation of Christian ritual into an existing idiom of harmful insertion. [EA, SI]

### 2.5 Andrés Febrés, 1765

Febrés’s *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* and its dictionary define *huecufù* as:

- the small arrows, splinters, and tooth-like things that machi say they remove by sucking;
- any illness;
- a certain deity or imagined entity held to cause deaths, illnesses, and hardships.

This is the clearest eighteenth-century lexicographic proof that “the spirit,” “the projectile,” and “the disease” were not separate senses imposed only by twentieth-century anthropology. [LEX]

### 2.6 Bernardo Havestadt, 1777

Havestadt’s Latin *Chilidúgú* reportedly gives essentially the same semantic field as Febrés. Augusta explicitly says Havestadt speaks “exactly in the same sense.” Direct collation of the relevant original entry remains desirable; the present claim rests on Augusta’s comparison and later lexicographic scholarship. [LEX]

### 2.7 Nineteenth-century historical synthesis

Diego Barros Arana rejected attempts to make *huecuvu* a sharply personal demon. He described it as the indeterminate cause of bad harvests, failed fishing, disease, poison, the animals or darts removed by machi, and broadly everything adverse. His conclusion—that it was closer to an expression of adverse fortune than a defined person—is interpretation, not a recovered indigenous creed, but it usefully resists Christian personification. [LS]

### 2.8 Lenz, Augusta, and early ethnographic texts

Rodolfo Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos* (1895–1897) and Félix José de Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) preserve Mapudungun texts and commentary gathered from speakers. Augusta describes Wekufù as a spiritual being capable of taking material residence in animals and small objects. He records:

- a speaker calling a loica with a red breast *Wedá kurəf Wekufù*;
- special fear of the lizard as possessed or influenced by Wekufù;
- fragrant *kelleñlawen* placed near house openings to repel it;
- a cure in Wapi in which machi collected and burned lizards, producing the place-name *Ləpemwekúfùwe*;
- denial by speakers that they sacrificed to Wekufù, together with horror at its name.

Augusta’s own language is overtly missionary and derogatory toward the machi. His observations and quoted speech should therefore be separated from his Christian moral commentary. [ET, SI]

### 2.9 Pascual Coña / Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach, 1930

*Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX* is a bilingual Mapudungun–Spanish account dictated by Pascual Coña and organized by Moesbach, with review by Augusta and Lenz. It uses *wekufü* in explanations of destructive events; one passage says volcanic smoke, ashes, and stones were believed caused by *pillán* or *wekufü*. Its importance lies in presenting Mapudungun testimony rather than only a Spanish chronicler’s paraphrase, though the editorial and missionary mediation remains significant. [ET]

### 2.10 Argentina: Kössler-Ilg and later collections

Bertha Kössler-Ilg collected oral narratives around San Martín de los Andes from the 1920s onward; *Cuentan los araucanos* appeared in 1954, and *Tradiciones araucanas* in 1962. Her texts retain *Huekúfu* as a harmful or maleficent presence among narratives of named beings, landscape, animals, and sorcery. They document the category’s trans-Andean life but should not be retrojected wholesale into sixteenth-century Chile. [ET, RV]

### Corpus limits

No indigenous precolonial inscription, manuscript, temple text, or archaeological label names Wekufe. All surviving early verbal attestations are colonial alphabetic texts mediated by Europeans. There is likewise no securely attributable precolonial image labelled as Wekufe. This materially limits claims about pre-contact antiquity, appearance, hierarchy, and mythic genealogy.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The strongest cross-period domain is **harm made present**. Documented effects include illness, premature death, bodily pain, livestock disease, crop pests, drought, scarcity of fish, accident, exhaustion, earthquake, volcanic danger, and socially destructive suspicion. [EA, ET]

“Evil” is an imperfect translation. Early evidence also treats wekufe as an implementable force, an intrusion, or the harmful quality of an event. Twentieth-century dualistic diagrams that place Wekufe opposite Ngünechen often clarify one regional theology but risk converting situational forces into two metaphysical sovereigns. [LS]

### The pathogenic object

One of the most stable motifs is the small material intrusion. It can be a splinter, straw, thorn, miniature dart, tooth-like object, insect, reptile, hair, or other matter. A *kalku* sends it; a patient sickens; a machi identifies and extracts it, often through sucking, manipulation, song, drumming, or trance. The object is simultaneously evidence of attack, embodiment of the harmful agency, and ritual proof that treatment has succeeded. [EA, LEX, ET]

Colonial authors routinely called the extraction fraudulent. That judgment is evidence of missionary medicine and polemic, not proof of how participants understood the rite. Conversely, the ethnographic fact that a healer displayed an object does not establish a biomedical cause. The historically secure claim is that such objects functioned as material signs in diagnosis and treatment.

### Form and manifestation

No fixed anthropomorphic form is attested. Wekufe can be:

- invisible;
- a projectile or minute object;
- an animal or a force occupying an animal;
- a humanlike apparition;
- a reptile, insect, bird, or hair;
- a moving skin in the *trülke wekufü* tradition;
- a destructive environmental condition;
- the illness itself.

This variability is defining. Modern illustrations of a horned humanoid demon are Christianizing or commercial interpretations, not recovered Mapuche iconography. [SI, MI]

### Colors, plants, and substances

Black is associated in some ethnographic and scholarly syntheses with destructive or inauspicious forces and therefore with wekufe. Colonial accounts of sacrifices sometimes describe black animals offered in dealings with dangerous powers. Scholars caution, however, that black was also an ordinary clothing color and that Mapuche symbolism is contextual rather than a universal color code. [ET, MSR]

The **foye/canelo** is associated mainly with machi authority, peace, healing, and protection. Burning or deploying aromatic plant matter to repel harmful presence is regionally recorded. This does not make the canelo Wekufe’s sacred tree. [ET]

No secure ancient sacred number, weekday, planet, standardized weapon, heraldic emblem, or canonical color belongs to Wekufe. Claims assigning such correspondences are modern occult systematizations unless independently sourced. [MI]

### Iconography

There is no canonical cult statue or ancient iconographic type. Tomás Guevara’s early twentieth-century publication includes an illustration labelled *Trelquehuecufe/Threquelhuecuvu*, the animated hide, but it is an ethnographic representation of a narrated monster—not a precolonial portrait of Wekufe as such. [ET]

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No securely attested source provides parents, spouse, offspring, or birth story for “Wekufe.” This absence follows from its categorical rather than biographical character.

Ovalle’s 1646 hierarchy places plural *guecubus* under Güenupillán. Later materials place wekufe under Pillán, under a *kalku*, in destructive cosmic regions such as *anka wenu* or *minche mapu*, or simply loose in the human world. These are variant models, not fragments of one recoverable family tree. [EA, RV, LS]

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or supernatural being

Because Wekufe is frequently a class, the entries below concern explicit relational claims rather than presumed co-membership in a “pantheon.”

- **Güenupillán — subordination:** Ovalle reports that Güenupillán commands many *guecubus*, compared with his chiefs or *ülmenes*. *Histórica relación*, 1646. **[EA]**
- **Pillán — consultation through specialists:** Núñez de Pineda’s *huecubuyes/renis* consult Pillán in secluded caves. *Cautiverio feliz*, seventeenth-century composition; 1863 printing. The morphology of *huecubu-ye* is disputed. **[EA, disputed]**
- **Pillán — alternative or associated cause:** Pascual Coña’s account attributes volcanic emissions to Pillán or Wekufü. Oral testimony concerning nineteenth-century life, published 1930. **[ET]**
- **Pillán — superior or controller:** some nineteenth- and early twentieth-century syntheses call Huecubu dependent on Pillán. This conflicts with models treating pillán as ancestral power and wekufe as pathogenic harm. **[LS]**
- **Ngünechen/Ngünemapun — opposition:** twentieth-century ethnographic and theological syntheses often contrast life-giving Ngünechen with destructive Wekufe. This is an ordered cosmological opposition, not a surviving early narrative of personal combat. **[LS, ET]**
- **Filew/Fileu — contrast in possession:** Foerster’s synthesis describes *filew* as the positive possessing power associated with becoming a machi, and *wekufe* as the negative possession producing the patient. *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche*, 1993. **[LS]**
- **Sumpall — narrative contrast with Trülke Wekufü:** comparative analysis of Mapuche tales contrasts Sumpall’s abduction of a woman into an affinal/marital aquatic family with Trülke Wekufü’s predatory attack, punishable by vengeance. This is a structural comparison across tales, not necessarily a scene in which Sumpall and Wekufe meet. **[RV, MSR]**
- **Alwe/püllü — possible capture or enslavement:** later summaries state that a wekufe or kalku can capture the soul of a recently dead person and employ it. The precise primary provenance is uneven; it should not be treated as a universal doctrine. **[ET/LS, uncertain]**

**Absence finding:** no securely identified primary narrative records Wekufe personally fighting, marrying, seducing, judging, or conversing with Antü, Küyen, Tren Tren, Kai Kai, Mankean, Millalobo, Pincoya, or the Chiloé Imbunche. Later lists that place all these figures in one “Mapuche pantheon” do not establish interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Wekufe has no single canonical epic cycle. Its narratives are usually short etiological, diagnostic, cautionary, or local stories.

### 5.1 The invisible dart and its extraction

**Early core:** Rosales and Febrés provide the earliest detailed evidence. A hostile person or *kalku* sends an invisible dart or embodied harm into a victim. The victim becomes ill. A healer determines the source and extracts a minute object, sometimes by sucking the painful location. [EA]

**Later ethnographic form:** the machitun dramatizes a struggle against the intrusive agency. Songs and invocations order it to leave; the cure may culminate in displaying or disposing of the extracted material. Foerster characterizes its ritual language as warfare or exorcism. [ET, LS]

**Function:** diagnosis converts unexplained suffering into a socially intelligible attack, identifies a cause, and authorizes therapeutic action. It can also identify an alleged human enemy, explaining Rosales’s concern about retaliatory violence. [MSR]

### 5.2 The Wekufe as animal or object

Augusta’s accounts show Wekufù inhabiting or acting through seemingly insignificant objects and animals. A red-breasted loica may be denounced as a bad Wekufù; lizards are especially feared. In the Wapi episode, machi gather and burn lizards during a chief’s illness, giving the location a commemorative name. [ET]

The story does not imply that all loicas or lizards are intrinsically evil. The animal is dangerous in a particular diagnostic or omen-bearing situation.

### 5.3 Trülke Wekufü, the animated hide

The *trülke wekufü* or “hide wekufe” belongs to the wider southern tradition of the predatory living hide, commonly Spanish *Cuero*. In Mapuche-language narrative it waits in or near water and attacks people. Analysis contrasts its assault with a Sumpall’s marriage-oriented abduction: the former destroys social and bodily integrity; the latter can establish aquatic affinity and descendants. [RV, ET]

The Chile-wide “Cuero” of later folklore should not be collapsed completely into Wekufe. *Trülke wekufü* is one named manifestation or classification; “Wekufe” is not simply the proper name of the Cuero.

### 5.4 The landscape Wekufe

In Argentine Patagonia, *Huecuvu Mapu* names dangerous, barren, exhausting, or death-associated terrain. Dead animals at passes, poisonous pasture, and journey failure can be attributed to Huecuvú. The name makes environmental hazard intelligible as active malevolence. [RV]

In Coquimbo and Atacama folklore, *Huecú* became both an indeterminate force known among miners and, in one account, an amphibious creature blamed for drowning. Vicuña Cifuentes observed that the aquatic description resembles the Cuero, while the miners’ unseen working noises and omens preserve the sense of an undefined force. These are northward Chilean folkloric transformations, not proof of an unchanged precolonial Mapuche doctrine. [RV]

### 5.5 The harmful force directed by the kalku

A widespread ethnographic complex makes the *kalku* the human director and the wekufe the instrument or servant. The kalku sends the force, object, or creature; the machi identifies and combats it. Yet the moral identities are not always absolute. Casanova and later historians note that a respected machi within one community could be feared as a *kalku machi* outside it. The opposition is partly positional and political. [ET, MSR]

### 5.6 Volcanic and cosmic destruction

Coña’s attribution of smoke, ash, and stones to Pillán or Wekufü indicates overlapping causal vocabularies for volcanic activity. Modern diagrams often sharpen this into “Pillán/Ngünechen good, Wekufe evil,” but older evidence is less tidy. Extraordinary powers can protect, punish, terrify, or destroy according to context. [ET, LS]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No institutional Wekufe cult

No securely attested temple, permanent sanctuary, priesthood, cult statue, calendrical festival, or theophoric personal-name series belongs specifically to Wekufe. Ovalle himself observed no indigenous temple or idol system of the kind missionaries expected. Augusta’s informants denied sacrificing to Wekufù and reacted to the name with horror. [EA, ET]

Accordingly, “cult of Wekufe” is usually the wrong analytical category. The evidence concerns **avoidance, diagnosis, expulsion, coercion, and occasional propitiation of dangerous forces**.

### Machitun

The machitun is the central relevant curing complex. Its form varies by community and period, but documented elements can include:

- patient examination and diagnosis;
- dreams, visions, pulse or bodily reading, and other divinatory procedures;
- singing and drumming on the *kultrun*;
- invocation of helping powers;
- warfare imagery directed against the intruder;
- suction or manipulation of the affected body;
- extraction and display of an intrusive object;
- expulsion or disposal of the harmful agent;
- medicines, including plant preparations.

Foerster interprets the patient as negatively possessed by Wekufe and the rite as dispossession. This is an analytical synthesis rather than a universal native formula. [ET, LS]

### Nguillatun

The collective *nguillatun* is not primarily worship of Wekufe. It is a communal rite concerning fertility, weather, health, reciprocity, and collective order. Harmful forces can be named, warded off, or balanced within it, but the ceremony should not be reduced to demon-exorcism. [ET]

### Sacrifice and offerings

Colonial Mapuche rites included animal sacrifice, and scholarship documents sacrificial equivalences in warfare, peace-making, funerary, and communal settings. Some researchers report black animals offered to dangerous beings or forces. Yet there is no secure evidence of a distinct, regular Wekufe temple sacrifice. [EA, MSR]

**Human sacrifice:** colonial southern Chilean sources contain allegations and descriptions of human killing in war, vengeance, funerary, and sacrificial contexts. The evidence must be evaluated episode by episode. No source examined here establishes an institutional cult in which humans were sacrificed specifically to Wekufe. Claims of “human sacrifices to the god Wekufe” are therefore unsupported. [EA, contested]

### Social and political consequences

Wekufe diagnosis could have serious consequences. If illness or death was attributed to a kalku’s attack, the alleged perpetrator might be assaulted or killed, and feuds could follow. Colonial authorities and missionaries prosecuted indigenous ritual specialists as witches, while indigenous political actors could use ritual specialists in warfare, protection, and resistance. A late seventeenth-century judicial dossier from the Chilean frontier shows Spanish officials translating these practices into criminal “witchcraft,” whereas indigenous participants could understand them as methods of warfare and political agency. [EA, MSR]

### Chiloé and Huilliche contexts

Chiloé folklore preserved Mapuche-derived vocabulary within a transformed colonial island complex of *brujos*, the Caleuche, Imbunche, flying garments, caves, and Spanish demonology. The island system should not be projected backward as a pristine “Wekufe cult.” Cavada’s derivation of a Chilote incantation from *alwe* as “devil” was later criticized because *alwe* more properly denotes a soul or spirit of the dead, illustrating how Christian demon vocabulary distorted multiple indigenous categories. [RV, SI]

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1646: reconstructable only indirectly

The wide Mapudungun distribution and the early colonial semantic complexity make a pre-contact category probable. Nevertheless, no precolonial inscription, labelled image, or indigenous manuscript survives. Any detailed reconstruction of its pre-Hispanic form is inferential. [MSR]

### 1646–1670s: colonial recording and Christian translation

Ovalle records plural *guecubus* under Güenupillán. Rosales records harmful environmental and pathogenic powers, magical darts, and extraction. Both write within Jesuit demonology and repeatedly translate indigenous beings or practices through “God,” “Devil,” “sorcery,” and “superstition.” [EA, SI]

This period already contains incompatible models:

- Ovalle: subordinate supernatural chiefs;
- Rosales: generalized harmful agency and pathogenic intrusion;
- missionary framing: operations of Satan;
- indigenous therapeutic framing, imperfectly recoverable: an attack requiring diagnosis and removal.

### Eighteenth century: lexical consolidation

Febrés and Havestadt stabilize a broad dictionary field encompassing projectile, extracted object, disease, and causal being. Olivares documents the reinterpretation of Christian sacraments within a harmful-insertion model when illness or death followed baptism or confession. [LEX, SI]

### Nineteenth century: conquest, reduction, and scholarly abstraction

Military occupation and state incorporation in Chile and Argentina disrupted territorial and ritual institutions. Missionaries, administrators, doctors, and historians increasingly translated *huecuvu* as “evil spirit,” “demon,” or “cause of disease.” Barros Arana moved in the opposite analytical direction, arguing that it lacked a stable personality and denoted adversity itself. [LS]

The term also spread or persisted in Argentine place-lore, pastoral disease terminology, and hazardous-landscape narratives. [RV]

### 1895–1930: philological and ethnographic documentation

Lenz, Augusta, Guevara, and the Coña–Moesbach corpus preserved vocabulary, narratives, place-names, curing customs, and speaker testimony. Their work is invaluable but embedded in evolutionist, missionary, and medical judgments. Augusta simultaneously records indigenous explanations and overlays them with Christian conclusions about “the demon.” [ET, SI]

### Mid-twentieth century: pantheon and dualism models

Latcham, Guevara, Faron, Grebe, and other ethnographers constructed ordered accounts of Mapuche religion. Especially influential was the presentation of life-promoting and destructive forces as a dual structure. Wekufe became the negative pole, the kalku’s power, and the ritual antagonist that makes machi healing necessary. [LS]

These models clarify patterned oppositions but can overstate coherence. The early corpus does not reveal a universally fixed pantheon with Wekufe as a single anti-god.

### 1950s–1980s: Argentine narrative preservation and entity classification

Kössler-Ilg’s Neuquén collections documented *Huekúfu* in trans-Andean oral narrative. Else María Waag’s *Tres entidades “wekufü” en la cultura mapuche* (1982) analyzed particular figures—including Anchimallén, Chonchon, and Wichalalwe-type beings—through the broader category. This is scholarly classification, not evidence that every community always called every dangerous being a wekufe. [ET, LS]

### 1990s–present: relational and decolonizing interpretation

Foerster emphasized possession, ritual boundaries, and the historical effects of evangelization. Bacigalupo’s work with machi treats illness, spirit relations, embodiment, gender, performance, and power as living and changing practices rather than remnants of a closed pre-Christian system. Current scholarship is correspondingly cautious about translating Wekufe simply as “Devil.” [MSR]

Mapuche religious and therapeutic practices continue, but no evidence located establishes a distinct modern organization devoted to worshipping Wekufe. The category survives primarily in language, diagnosis, narrative, cultural education, literature, and popular representation.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and visual art

Wekufe functions in modern Chilean and Mapuche writing as a figure of destructiveness, colonial rupture, illness, alienation, or imbalance. A study of Clemente Riedemann’s poetry, for example, notes that Wekufe becomes “the diabolical” and figures the violence of intercultural encounter. This is a modern literary deployment, not an ancient myth recovered unchanged. [modern reception]

Modern illustrations often give Wekufe a singular monster body. Such depictions materially shape public imagery but conflict with the older evidence’s fluidity and lack of a fixed icon.

### Folklore publishing

Chilean and Argentine folklore dictionaries routinely condense Wekufe into “genio del mal,” subordinate to Pillán, able to assume any form, poisoning pasture and killing animals. These summaries preserve genuine regional motifs but harmonize sources from different periods and locations. [RV, LS]

### Games, fantasy, and Internet mythology

Modern mythology websites commonly present Wekufe as:

- a named demon species;
- a single supreme evil god;
- an “energy” manipulable like occult force;
- creator or commander of every Mapuche monster;
- captor of souls in a fully schematized afterlife;
- member of a stable encyclopedia-style pantheon.

Some elements have ethnographic analogues, but the seamless system is modern. Internet lists often copy one another without page-level references and amplify speculative etymologies. [MI]

### Occultism and neopaganism

The requested contested-material search found no reliable evidence that Wekufe played a role in Renaissance Hermetism, European grimoires, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, or early Wicca. Those movements did not possess a documented historical Wekufe tradition.

Modern South American esoteric writing has compared Huecuvu with elementals, incubi, and succubi, sometimes on pseudo-etymological grounds. These equations are modern inventions and lack linguistic or historical support. [MI]

There are contemporary attempts to recover or practice Mapuche spirituality, but it would be misleading to label living Mapuche ceremonial life “neopagan.” No verifiable organized Wekufe-worship movement or measurable Wekufe-centered cult was found.

### Political appropriation

Colonial authorities used the labels Devil, witch, and sorcery to delegitimize ritual specialists and indigenous political resistance. Conversely, modern literature can use Wekufe as a metaphor for colonial or state violence. Neither process reveals a timeless theological identity; both are historically situated appropriations.

No well-documented extremist movement centered on Wekufe was found in the sources consulted.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Wekufe a being, force, object, or disease?

The strongest answer is **all four are historically attested usages**. Attempts to choose only one discard evidence:

- Ovalle: plural subordinate beings;
- Rosales: misfortune, environmental harm, disease, projectile;
- Febrés: extracted objects, illness, and causal entity;
- Augusta: spiritual agency embodied in animals or objects;
- Barros Arana: generalized adversity;
- later ethnography: manipulable pathogenic power.

The variations are chronological, regional, contextual, and translational.

### Did Catholicism create the idea of evil Wekufe?

A strong version of that claim is untenable: Rosales and Febrés document harmful disease-causing senses, even though their descriptions are Christian-mediated. What Catholic translation clearly did was **personalize and moralize** the category by equating it with Satan and demons, and then feed that equation back through mission teaching and bilingual dictionaries. [EA, SI]

Thus both propositions can be true:

- harmful wekufe concepts did not originate simply with missionaries;
- the equation “Wekufe = the Devil” is a colonial syncretistic transformation.

### Does Wekufe mean “outside being”?

Possible, but not demonstrated securely by the historical lexicons consulted. It should remain a proposal. [contested etymology]

### Is Wekufe subordinate to Pillán?

Ovalle makes *guecubus* subordinate to Güenupillán; later sources sometimes make Huecuvu dependent on Pillán. Rosales’s pathogenic usages need no such hierarchy, while later Mapuche testimony can name Pillán and Wekufe as alternative causes. The relationship is therefore a variant, not canonical doctrine.

### Did Wekufe receive sacrifice?

Augusta’s informants denied worship or sacrifice. Other scholarship reports offerings or black animals to dangerous powers. Fear-propitiation may have occurred locally, but a regular cult is not established. Human sacrifice specifically to Wekufe is unverified.

### Are Anchimallén, Chonchon, Piuchén, Imbunche, and Caleuche all wekufe?

They may be classified as harmful *wekufü* in particular modern or ethnographic schemes, but each has its own regional history. Treating “wekufe” as an encyclopedia taxon containing every sinister southern being is learned systematization. [LS]

### Problems in the source record

1. **Missionary mediation:** most early authors translated unfamiliar categories into Catholic demonology.
2. **Genre:** dictionaries compress contextual words into Spanish headwords such as *diablo*.
3. **Publication gaps:** Rosales and Núñez wrote in the seventeenth century but were printed much later.
4. **Dialect and orthography:** spelling differences obscure whether forms are cognate, compounds, or scribal errors.
5. **Ethnographic present:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century researchers sometimes projected contemporary observations backward.
6. **Composite “pantheons”:** popular works combine Chilean Mapuche, Huilliche, Chilote, Pehuenche, Puelche, and Argentine narratives.
7. **Christian feedback:** “Devil” became not merely an outsider’s mistranslation but a term some Mapuche speakers themselves could adopt.
8. **Colonial violence:** witchcraft testimony was sometimes produced under accusation, imprisonment, or judicial threat.

### Material record

The central evidence is textual and ethnographic rather than archaeological. There are no verified Wekufe temples, inscriptions, coins, votive statues, or precolonial labelled images. Important surviving witnesses are held or digitized by the Biblioteca Nacional de Chile/Memoria Chilena, Biblioteca Nacional Digital de Chile, Universidad de Chile, Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes, Universidad Nacional de La Plata, and other repositories.

## 11. Sources and Critical Bibliography

### Primary and near-primary sources

- Alonso de Ovalle, *Histórica relación del Reyno de Chile* (Rome, 1646). Earliest secure printed witness; its *guecubus* are subordinate to Güenupillán.
- Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz* (seventeenth-century manuscript; printed 1863). Source for the disputed *huecubuyes*.
- Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del Reyno de Chile, Flandes Indiano* (completed c. 1674; printed 1877–1878). Principal early evidence for environmental harm, disease, invisible darts, and extraction.
- Miguel de Olivares, *Historia militar, civil y sagrada del Reino de Chile* (1762; printed 1864). Evidence for bodily insertion and colonial sacramental reinterpretation.
- Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* (1765). Foundational dictionary definition.
- Bernardo Havestadt, *Chilidúgú* (1777). Comparative missionary lexicography.
- Rodolfo Lenz, *Estudios araucanos* (1895–1897). Linguistic and oral-text documentation.
- Félix José de Augusta, *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) and *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916). Extensive lexical, narrative, and ethnographic evidence.
- Pascual Coña and Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach, *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX* (1930). Bilingual indigenous testimony with editorial mediation.
- Julio Vicuña Cifuentes, *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena* (1915). Chilean regional transformations.
- Bertha Kössler-Ilg, *Cuentan los araucanos* (1954) and, with Clemente Hernando Balmori, *Tradiciones araucanas* (1962). Neuquén oral traditions.

### Major scholarship

- Tomás Guevara’s early twentieth-century studies preserve important narratives and illustrations but frequently employ evolutionist categories.
- Louis C. Faron, *Hawks of the Sun* (1964), relates religion, morality, kinship, and ritual but encourages a highly ordered systemic reading.
- Else María Waag, *Tres entidades “wekufü” en la cultura mapuche* (1982), analyzes named harmful beings under the category.
- Holdenis Casanova Guarda’s work on devils, witches, and harmful spirits is central to understanding colonial demonization and judicial violence.
- Rolf Foerster, *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche* (1993), explicitly presents the historical discourses of chroniclers, missionaries, and anthropologists rather than claiming unmediated access to timeless belief.
- Ana Mariella Bacigalupo’s studies of machi are essential for modern practice, shamanic authority, illness, gender, embodiment, and historical transformation.
- Joaquín García Insausti’s study of late seventeenth-century witchcraft and indigenous warfare usefully connects Rosales’s descriptions with colonial judicial archives.
- Modern linguistic evaluation of Augusta emphasizes the dictionary’s empirical value while situating it after Valdivia, Febrés, Havestadt, Lenz, and other documentary traditions.

### Conclusions of the contested-material pass

- **Etymology:** “outside being” remains insufficiently demonstrated.
- **Origin:** pre-contact existence is probable but not directly documented.
- **Syncretism:** equation with Satan is securely colonial; it transformed rather than simply invented the category.
- **Human sacrifice:** no secure Wekufe-specific human sacrifice is documented.
- **Suppression:** colonial and republican persecution targeted machi, alleged kalku, and other indigenous specialists, but no separate Wekufe church or temple was suppressed.
- **Occultism:** incubus/succubus and elemental equations are modern pseudo-comparative inventions.
- **Neopaganism:** no documented Wekufe-centered neopagan organization was found.
- **Iconography:** no canonical ancient image exists.
- **Divine interactions:** only a small number of explicit relationships are attested; most modern “pantheon” connections are categorical co-listings, not narratives.
