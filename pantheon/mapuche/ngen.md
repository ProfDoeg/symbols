# The Ngen: Research Dossier

**Class:** A plural and open-ended class of place-, element-, species-, and activity-specific “owners,” “masters,” “keepers,” or guardian beings in Mapuche, Pewenche, Lafkenche, Williche, and related traditions. Individual *ngen* may be described as spirits, persons, forces, or owners, but “the Ngen” is not securely attested as one god, goddess, or collective personality with a single biography.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The underlying Mapudungun word *gen/ngen* is documented by the Jesuit Luis de Valdivia in his 1606 grammar and vocabulary, where it means an owner or person responsible for something: *gen ruka*, “owner/master of the house”; *gen mapu*, “lord/owner of the land”; *gen boye*, “owner of the canelo,” a human ceremonial office. This proves the term and its relational “owner/master” construction by 1606, but not yet the fully elaborated modern ethnographic taxonomy of nature spirits. Secure, explicit documentation of multiple *ngen* as owners or guardians of natural places becomes substantially clearer in nineteenth- and especially early-twentieth-century linguistic and ethnographic records; María Ester Grebe’s fieldwork and 1992 synthesis provide the most influential modern classification.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY-LEXICAL]** Secure colonial documentation of the word or construction, not necessarily a supernatural being.
- **[EARLY/ETHNOHISTORICAL]** Colonial testimony about Mapuche religion whose author, translation, and missionary framing require scrutiny.
- **[RECORDED-TRADITION]** Oral testimony or practice documented by an ethnographer or Mapuche narrator.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A locally attested formulation, not safely generalizable to all Mapuche territories.
- **[SCHOLARLY SYSTEMATIZATION]** An analytical taxonomy produced by an ethnographer, historian, linguist, or theologian.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC]** A demonstrable or probable product of interaction with Christianity or another religious vocabulary.
- **[FOLKLORE]** A collected legend or customary account whose historical depth cannot be fixed.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern attempt to synthesize dispersed traditions.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/RETELLING]** A recent attribution lacking adequate older documentation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### What *ngen* means

**[EARLY-LEXICAL]** Valdivia’s 1606 *Arte y gramática general de la lengua que corre en todo el Reyno de Chile* derives the noun *gen*, “owner,” from the existential/possessive verb *gen*, which he renders through meanings including “to be,” “to have,” and “to be born.” His examples include *gen ruka* and *gen mapu*. This is the earliest presently secure printed attestation found in the researched corpus. It establishes that *ngen* was a productive relational term: an owner is the owner **of something**. It does not establish a fixed pantheon called “the Ngen.”

Valdivia also records *gen boye*—in modern spelling approximately *ngen foye*, “owner of the canelo”—for the principal person responsible for supplying a whole canelo tree in a *kawin*. Here the bearer is human and ceremonial. That usage is essential: not every occurrence of *ngen* denotes a supernatural being.

**[EARLY-LEXICAL]** Andrés Febrés’s 1765 *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* and Bernardo Havestadt’s 1777 Latin *Chilidúgú* continue the missionary grammatical tradition. They preserve *gen/ngen* both as part of the verb “to be” and in possessive or master/owner constructions. Orthography differs because early missionaries represented Mapudungun sounds through Spanish, Latin, and German conventions.

**[RECORDED-TRADITION / SCHOLARLY SYSTEMATIZATION]** Félix José de Augusta’s *Diccionario Araucano-Español* (1916), Rodolfo Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos* (1895–1897), and Wilhelm de Moesbach’s edition of Pascual Coña’s testimony, *Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX* (1930), greatly expand the contextual record. Augusta and Moesbach support a general meaning such as “owner,” “master,” “possessor,” or the person responsible for an entity. Grebe therefore distinguishes:

1. a **generic/profane domain**, in which *ngen* means an ordinary owner, master, responsible person, or possessor; and  
2. a **religious/cosmological domain**, in which it designates nonhuman owners or guardians of places and natural phenomena.

Grebe’s division is analytically useful but is a **1992 scholarly formulation**, not a demonstrated pre-Columbian creed.

### Forms and spelling

Attested scholarly and popular spellings include:

- *gen* — common in early missionary transcription.
- *ngen* — widespread modern spelling.
- *nguen* — older Spanish-influenced spelling.
- *ngen’* — a Williche/Chiloé representation used by some recent writers.
- *pu ngen* — plural, “the ngen.”
- *ngen + noun* — productive compound meaning the owner, master, keeper, or responsible being associated with that noun.

Mapudungun has no ancient native manuscript tradition comparable to cuneiform, Greek epigraphy, or medieval codices. The relevant spellings are Latin-alphabet transcriptions devised or standardized after European contact. Consequently, there is no precontact indigenous written form to reproduce.

### Principal documented compounds

The compounds are not an exhaustive divine register. Because the construction is productive and local landscapes differ, different speakers and researchers enumerate different sets.

- **Ngen-ko / ngen-kó / gen-ko** — “owner/keeper of water.” *Ko* means water. Applied to springs, streams, wells, rivers, lagoons, lakes, and sometimes other bodies of water. **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-trayenko** — keeper of a waterfall or spring-water place. **[RECORDED-TRADITION; LOCAL]**
- **Ngen-lafken** — keeper of the sea, lake, or large body of water, depending on dialect and locality. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Ngen-mapu** — owner or keeper of land/territory. The expression may denote a human landholder in lexical contexts, a place-specific nonhuman owner, or—especially in related forms such as *Ngenemapun/Ngünemapun*—a much higher religious being. These meanings must not be collapsed. **[EARLY-LEXICAL / RECORDED-TRADITION / CONTESTED]**
- **Ngen-winkul** — keeper of a hill or mountain. Local accounts distinguish keepers of large mountains or volcanoes from those of smaller hills. **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-mawida / ngen-mawiza** — owner of forest, wooded mountain country, or native woodland. Dialect and translator determine whether *mawida* is rendered “forest” or “mountain.” **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-kütral** — owner or keeper of fire. **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-kurra / ngen-kura** — owner of stone or rock. **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-kürüf** — owner of wind or air. **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-kullin** — owner or keeper of animals. **[SCHOLARLY SYSTEMATIZATION based on recorded terminology]**
- **Ngen-foyentu** — owner of a grove of *foye*, the sacred canelo tree. **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-pewen** — keeper of the monkey-puzzle tree and its piñones, particularly important in Pewenche country. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Ngen-rüpü** — keeper or owner of a path. **[RECORDED-TRADITION]**
- **Ngen-wenu** — “owner/master of the sky”; in some historical ethnographies, a maker or controller of rain. It can be an office or powerful being rather than simply another nature spirit. **[REGIONAL / CONTESTED]**
- **Ngenpin** — literally the owner/master of speech or word; a ritual speaker and authority, normally human. It should not be listed uncritically as a nature spirit. **[EARLY-LEXICAL continuing into modern practice]**
- **Ngen foye/gen boye** — master or ceremonial owner of the canelo; historically an office. Bacigalupo warns that older scholars, especially Ricardo Latcham, conflated *gen boye* with the *boquibuye*. **[EARLY-LEXICAL; HISTORIOGRAPHIC DISPUTE]**
- **Ngenünkan/ngenüncán** — a protecting or sheltering being in Latcham’s reconstruction, distinguished by him from an ancestral *pillan*. The derivation and alleged place in an ancient totemic system are theory-laden. **[EARLY-20TH-CENTURY SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**

### “Owner” is not identical to Western property ownership

**[MODERN ETHNOGRAPHIC INTERPRETATION]** “Owner” is the usual translation, but it can mislead if it suggests unrestricted private property. In many narrated encounters, the *ngen* is the party empowered to permit, withhold, regulate, or punish human use. The relationship is closer to mastery, custody, responsibility, and prior authority than to a transferable title deed. Magnus Course notes that Mapudungun *ngen* relations can be mutually constitutive: expressions such as “master of husband” and “master of wife” describe reciprocal relations rather than a simple dominant proprietor.

### Classification and gender

**[RECORDED-TRADITION]** Ngen may appear anthropomorphically, zoomorphically, phytomorphically, through sound, movement, dream, or unusual natural phenomena. They need not have a permanent body.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT / GREBE’S SYSTEMATIZATION]** Grebe describes some ngen through a fourfold pattern—elder man, elder woman, young man, young woman—also found in descriptions of major Mapuche powers. This should not be converted into a universal doctrine that every local ngen is literally four persons. Chiloé and Williche accounts may instead give a keeper a distinct visible form, such as a woman associated with shellfish or a hybrid aquatic being.

Mapudungun nouns are not grammatically gendered like Spanish nouns. A given ngen may manifest as male, female, plural, animal, plant, or indeterminate. Translation as “god” or “goddess” is usually less accurate than “owner-being,” “master,” or “guardian spirit.”

### Ngen and Ngünechen are not synonyms

**[HISTORIOGRAPHIC DISPUTE]** Ngen must be distinguished from *Ngünechen/Ngenechen*, commonly glossed “owner/governor of the people.” Ana Mariella Bacigalupo traces several competing historical explanations for Ngünechen:

1. an older creator who declined and later re-emerged;
2. a generalization of nature owners, ancestor beings, and regional powers;
3. a being whose omniscient, omnipotent, creator attributes were intensified through Christian translation.

Missionaries’ equation of Ngünechen with the Christian God encouraged later presentations of a single “Mapuche supreme god.” The linguistic presence of *ngen* inside a name does not prove that all ngen form emanations of one deity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What can securely be said about the beginning

There is no presently known precontact inscription, codex, statue inscription, or archaeological label naming a *ngen*. Archaeology can identify Mapuche ritual landscapes, *kuel* mounds, cemeteries, ceremonial grounds, and long-term occupation, but it cannot independently attach the spoken name *ngen* to them.

The evidence therefore begins in colonial writing:

- **1558–1589: Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana*.** **[EARLY/ETHNOHISTORICAL]** The poem is an indispensable early representation of the colonial war but not a controlled ethnography of Mapuche religion. It does not securely document a developed class of nature spirits called *pu ngen*. Its silence cannot prove absence.
- **1606: Luis de Valdivia, grammar, vocabulary, confession manual, and catechetical texts.** **[EARLIEST SECURE LEXICAL ATTESTATION]** Documents *gen* as “owner” and in compounds including *gen mapu*, *gen ruka*, and *gen boye*. Because the book was made for evangelization and analyzes Mapudungun through Latin and Spanish categories, its translations are evidence filtered through missionary purposes.
- **1673: Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz*.** **[EARLY/ETHNOHISTORICAL]** Based partly on his 1629 captivity. It contains valuable observations on Mapuche society and religion but does not supply a securely organized ngen taxonomy.
- **1674 manuscript: Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del reino de Chile: Flandes indiano*.** First printed in 1877–1878. **[EARLY/ETHNOHISTORICAL]** Rosales records religious beings and practices through a Jesuit demonological and providential frame. The two-century gap between composition and printing matters. A mention in an 1877 edition remains seventeenth-century testimony if the manuscript reading is secure.
- **1765: Andrés Febrés, *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile*.** **[EARLY-LEXICAL]** Continues and expands lexical evidence; includes catechism and confessional materials whose Christian vocabulary cannot automatically be read as native theology.
- **1777: Bernardo Havestadt, *Chilidúgú*.** **[EARLY-LEXICAL]** A major Mapudungun–Latin linguistic source based on missionary experience, including travel in southern Chile.
- **1895–1897: Rodolfo Lenz, *Estudios araucanos*.** **[RECORDED-TRADITION]** An early scientific-linguistic corpus of narrated Mapudungun, with Spanish translation and commentary. It is more useful for actual discourse than decontextualized word lists, though collection and translation still shaped the result.
- **1903 and 1910: Félix José de Augusta, *Gramática araucana* and *Lecturas araucanas*.** **[RECORDED-TRADITION]** The latter preserves Mapudungun texts with translations, including prayers, narratives, and customary knowledge.
- **1916: Augusta, *Diccionario Araucano-Español y Español-Araucano*.** **[LEXICOGRAPHIC CORPUS]** The most extensive early dictionary in this tradition. Its definitions often preserve examples and regional qualifications absent from colonial dictionaries.
- **Early twentieth century: Tomás Guevara and Ricardo Latcham.** **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Both gathered important material but built evolutionary schemes—animism, totemism, ancestor cult, “higher” religion—whose categories are now contested. Their documentation must be separated from their theories.
- **1914/1921: Francisco Cavada’s Chiloé studies.** **[FOLKLORE / REGIONAL]** Important for Chilote custom and legend, although the material often appears already hybridized through Williche, Spanish, Catholic, seafaring, and commercial society.
- **1915: Julio Vicuña Cifuentes, *Mitos y supersticiones recogidos de la tradición oral chilena*.** **[FOLKLORE]** A large collector’s corpus, valuable for distribution and narrative forms but not proof that every recorded motif is ancient or pan-Mapuche.
- **1930: Pascual Coña, dictated to Wilhelm de Moesbach, *Vida y costumbres…*.** **[INDIGENOUS TESTIMONY / EDITED ETHNOGRAPHY]** Coña’s account is a central source for late-nineteenth-century Mapuche lifeways. It is not a transparent transcript: narration, missionary relationship, transcription, translation, and editorial arrangement all matter.
- **Mid-twentieth century: Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collections, later published in forms including *Cuentan los araucanos*.** **[REGIONAL FOLKLORE]** Important evidence for eastern-Andean Mapuche and Argentine narrative, not a universal canon.
- **1964: Louis Faron, *Hawks of the Sun*.** **[ETHNOGRAPHY]** Analyzes social organization, morality, ancestors, ritual, and the *ngillatun*. It does not justify reducing all religious practice to ngen worship.
- **1970s–1990s: María Ester Grebe and collaborators.** **[ETHNOGRAPHY / SYSTEMATIZATION]** Grebe’s work on cosmology, music, medicine, and nature spirits culminates in “El concepto de ngen en la cultura mapuche” (1992; sometimes cited through 1993–1994 issue dating) and later popular restatements. Her taxonomy is the principal modern source for lists such as *ngen-ko, ngen-winkul,* and *ngen-mawida*.
- **1993: Rolf Foerster, *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche*.** **[HISTORICAL-ANTHROPOLOGICAL SYNTHESIS]** Especially important for comparing missionary and ethnographic evidence and for examining Christian interaction.
- **1995 onward: Ana Mariella Bacigalupo.** **[ETHNOHISTORY / ETHNOGRAPHY]** Her work on Ngünechen and the machi exposes the dangers of conflating nature owners, ritual offices, creator beings, Christian God-language, ancestors, and *wekufü*.
- **2007: Tom Dillehay, *Monuments, Empires, and Resistance*.** **[ETHNOARCHAEOLOGY]** Documents Mapuche political and ritual landscapes from approximately 1550–1850. It provides material context for territorial sacredness but does not archaeologically “prove” individual ngen names.
- **2010s–2020s:** Environmental anthropology and Indigenous-rights research increasingly documents ngen in disputes over forestry, water, hydropower, roads, tourism, and conservation. **[CONTEMPORARY TESTIMONY]**

### Limits of the textual record

The absence of a clearly articulated nature-spirit taxonomy in Ercilla, Valdivia, Núñez de Pineda, or Rosales cannot be treated as proof that the beings did not exist. Missionaries concentrated on what served confession and conversion; soldiers emphasized warfare; European writers often folded distinct beings into “devil,” “sorcerer,” “idol,” or “superstition.” Conversely, the existence of the word *gen* in 1606 does not prove that every compound listed by Grebe in 1992 existed with identical meaning four centuries earlier.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[RECORDED-TRADITION]** Ngen regulate relations between human communities and more-than-human places. Their characteristic activities are:

- inhabiting or manifesting through a particular place, species, element, or resource;
- maintaining its vitality, continuity, fertility, or dangerous power;
- granting or withholding permission;
- indicating acceptable quantities or behavior;
- warning through sounds, dreams, illness, fright, disorientation, weather, animal behavior, or unusual appearances;
- punishing disrespect, noise, waste, contamination, or excessive extraction;
- receiving speech, acknowledgment, and offerings;
- mediating human access to medicine, water, wood, food, and ceremonial places.

These actions are not consistent “laws” across Wallmapu. They recur as a family of relations in local testimony.

### Permission before taking

**[RECORDED-TRADITION; STRONGLY ATTESTED]** Before entering a forest, gathering medicinal plants, cutting wood, taking water, fishing, collecting shellfish, or crossing a charged place, a person may address the relevant ngen through a short prayer or *ngellipun*. The person explains the purpose, asks permission, takes only what is needed, and gives thanks. Depending on community and occasion, an offering may include *muday*, bread, unsalted cooked food, tobacco, coins, or an animal offering.

The underlying rule is reciprocity and measured use, but modern ecological slogans should not be mistaken for verbatim ancient commandments. Current Pewenche testimony says that outsiders’ failure “to know how to enter” a place without asking permission is a central difference between Mapuche and *wingka* conduct.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT]** In contemporary Williche Chiloé testimony, entering forest without requesting permission may be followed by dizziness or sudden illness. Such an episode is interpreted as a damaged relation with the local ngen; it is not reducible to a generalized abstract “nature god.”

### Powers and limits

A ngen’s power is normally localized or relational. A water keeper acts through its water; a mountain owner through a mountain and those entering it. No early evidence makes every ngen omnipotent, omniscient, or creator of the universe.

A site can lose vitality when its ngen departs. Contemporary testimony associates disappearance of ngen with plantation forestry, dried springs, channelization, damming, pollution, and desecration. This is a religious explanation and an ecological-political diagnosis at once.

### Forms

Grebe records anthropomorphic, zoomorphic, and phytomorphic manifestations. Reported forms include:

- an elder, young person, woman, or man;
- a small or unusually dressed person;
- a serpent, fish, bull, horse, bird, or other locally significant animal;
- a tree or visibly animated vegetal form;
- a whirlpool, wave, voice, song, rushing water, light, wind, or dream-presence.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT]** In Chiloé, recent scholarship interprets the Pincoya as an owner of shellfish or the intertidal zone, the Sirena as owner of fish, and figures including Millalobo, Caleuche, sea-horse, Camahueto, and Kai-Kai as beings that can be understood within the broader ngen system. That is a modern Indigenous-knowledge classification of hybrid Chilote lore—not proof that all those figures historically bore the title *ngen* in Cavada’s period.

### Iconography

There is no canonical ancient iconography of “the Ngen,” no standard cult statue, no securely identified archaeological image, and no fixed emblem equivalent to Zeus’s thunderbolt or Athena’s owl.

Local natural features are often the relevant material presence: a spring, rock, waterfall, old-growth grove, hill, volcano, *foye*, *pewen*, or shoreline. Offerings, ritual speech, and rules of approach identify the relationship more reliably than a portrait.

Modern paintings, museum exhibitions, school illustrations, and internet images that show “Ngen” as a humanoid forest deity are **[MODERN RETELLING]** unless tied to a named community account. Jaime Díaz’s 2022 documentary *Ngen*, centered on a machi from Fin Fin Boroa, is modern visual testimony about relations with ngen, not ancient iconography.

### Colors, numbers, days, planets

No reliable evidence establishes a universal ngen color, sacred number, weekday, planet, or zodiacal correspondence. Online occult tables assigning such properties are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless independently traced to a documented local teaching.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

### Genealogy

Ngen do not possess one stable family tree. Most are defined by relation to their domain, not by descent.

**[RECORDED-TRADITION: GREBE]** In an origin account published by Grebe, *Füta Chachai* (“Great/Old Father”) and *Ñuke Papai* (“Mother/Old Mother”) make the world and leave an ngen in each thing as caretaker. The account names guardians of hill, water, forest, stone, wind, fire, and land. This securely establishes what Grebe’s consultants narrated in the late twentieth century. It does not independently date the narrative to precontact antiquity.

A later popular version claims that *Pu-am*, an alleged universal soul, created the ngen after a battle of the *pillan* so they would maintain *admapu*. That synthesis circulates widely online but is poorly anchored to an identifiable early primary text. Pending a precise source, it should be marked **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION, possibly invention in its present form]**.

### Position in the spiritual order

Ngen coexist with, but are not automatically identical to:

- *newen* — force, potency, capacity;
- *püllü* — spirit or soul, in context;
- *pillan* — powerful beings often associated with ancestors, mountains, volcanoes, thunder, or historic persons;
- *wekufü* — dangerous or harmful agencies/beings;
- *wangülen* — star or celestial beings in some accounts;
- ancestors;
- Ngünechen and related high-being names;
- machi spirits and helpers.

The boundaries are neither universally fixed nor well represented by a European pantheon chart. One community may call a being an ngen where another stresses its status as ancestor, *newen*, *pillan*, or named local person.

### Every securely documented named divine-being interaction

Because ngen are a category rather than one narrative protagonist, explicit god-to-god episodes are uncommon. The following list separates recorded relations from mere coexistence.

- **Füta Chachai — creator/commissioner:** In Grebe’s late-twentieth-century recorded creation account, Füta Chachai and Ñuke Papai make the world and leave ngen as its caretakers. Source: Grebe’s field material, published in her 1980s–1990s discussions of ngen. **[RECORDED-TRADITION; LATE DOCUMENTATION]**
- **Ñuke Papai — creator/commissioner:** Jointly with Füta Chachai, creates or appoints the ngen in the same recorded account. **[RECORDED-TRADITION; LATE DOCUMENTATION]**
- **Ngünechen — contested hierarchical relation:** Some modern speakers and writers treat Ngünechen as creator or superior of nature owners; other ethnographic formulations treat Ngünechen as one *ngen* among many or as a Christian-influenced high god. No single canonical encounter is securely attested. Source: Foerster 1993; Bacigalupo 1995; Course 2012. **[CONTESTED / SYNCRETISTIC]**
- **Ngenemapun/Ngünemapun — overlap rather than episode:** Names meaning approximately owner/governor of the land are sometimes merged with Ngünechen, Elmapun, or a high creator. The evidence concerns historical equation, not a story in which two independently named gods meet. **[SYNCRETISTIC / HISTORIOGRAPHIC]**
- **Elchen — syncretistic equation:** “Maker/creator of people” becomes associated or merged with Ngünechen and related high-being names in missionary and modern theological syntheses. No secure narrative of Elchen personally interacting with a nature ngen was found. **[SYNCRETISTIC]**
- **Elmapun — syncretistic equation:** “Maker/creator of the land” is likewise joined to Ngünechen/Ngenemapun in some syntheses. No securely early episode with a specific ngen was found. **[SYNCRETISTIC]**
- **Pillan collectively — modern origin-cycle relation:** Popular accounts say the ngen were instituted after destructive conflict among the pillan. A securely identified early text for that precise episode was not located. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION / UNESTABLISHED]**
- **Kai-Kai/Coicoi — regional classificatory relationship:** Recent Chiloé scholarship classes Kai-Kai among maritime ngen; this is a categorical identification, not a narrated encounter between Kai-Kai and a separate Ngen. **[REGIONAL MODERN INTERPRETATION]**
- **Tren-Tren — no secure direct interaction:** Although Tren-Tren and Kai-Kai form a major flood narrative, no secure source was found in which a generic or specifically named nature ngen independently meets Tren-Tren. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **Wekufü — opposition by classification, not a universal mythic battle:** Ngen commonly protect or regulate; *wekufü* commonly harm or disorder. No single canonical combat between “the Ngen” and “Wekufü” is securely attested. **[ABSENCE OF A SPECIFIC EPISODE]**
- **Pincoya — regional identification:** Contemporary Chiloé testimony identifies Pincoya as owner of shellfish/intertidal abundance and therefore an ngen-like being. There is no second Ngen with whom she interacts in the cited account. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Sirena — regional identification:** In the same contemporary Chiloé classification, Sirena owns fish. This is identity-by-function rather than a god-god episode. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Millalobo — regional classification:** Sometimes interpreted as a maritime ngen in modern Chiloé analysis; no early, securely sourced interaction with a separately named ngen was established. **[REGIONAL MODERN INTERPRETATION]**
- **Taita Huenteyao — local Williche relation:** Huenteyao is described as a powerful coastal ngen at Pucatrihue from whom Williche ritual participants seek permission before *ngillatun*. Sources document approach and authorization, not interaction with another named god. **[REGIONAL WILLICHE TRADITION]**

The negative finding is substantial: unlike Mediterranean epic gods, ngen usually appear in narrated human–place relations, not in a densely interconnected divine drama.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The emplacement of the ngen

**[RECORDED-TRADITION]** Grebe’s principal origin narrative says that when the world was made, the creator pair fashioned things and placed them where they belonged. In each they left an ngen to watch over it. This explains both plurality and localization: hill, water, native forest, stone, wind, fire, and land each receive a custodian.

The narrative’s early core cannot be independently reconstructed. Its creator-pair language resembles the fourfold and kin-based descriptions of high Mapuche power but was recorded after centuries of Christian mission. It should neither be dismissed as “Christian” nor projected unchanged into precontact time.

### Encounters with water owners

**[RECORDED-TRADITION / FOLKLORE]** Ngen-ko narratives describe water as alive, inhabited, audible, and subject to a prior owner. Rushing or abundant water may be its voice or song. Whirlpools, waves, serpentine manifestations, or sudden danger may disclose the being.

A person who approaches correctly asks permission and avoids contamination. One who behaves loudly, takes excessively, or enters without respect may suffer fright, illness, drowning danger, loss of direction, or a disturbing dream. These are a recurrent narrative structure, not one canonical tale.

**[SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION]** A 2018 study connects water-spirit imagery—*filu, filoko*, whirlpool, great wave, and fish transformations—with the Tren-Tren/Kai-Kai origin complex. This establishes a thematic and cosmological relationship, not necessarily a single original myth.

### Forest and medicine narratives

**[RECORDED-TRADITION]** Ngen-mawida or ngen-mawiza controls access to native forest and its medicines. A machi or *lawentuchefe* may receive knowledge or authorization through dreams and signs. Plants are not inert substances simply available for taking; efficacious medicine depends partly on maintaining relations among healer, plant, place, and owner.

Contemporary Chiloé testimony about dizziness after cutting wood without permission follows this pattern. It is living regional tradition, not evidence for a word-for-word ancient legend.

### Mountain-owner narratives

**[RECORDED-TRADITION]** Ngen-winkul and related mountain owners govern hills, volcanoes, passes, grazing grounds, and places used in Pewenche seasonal movement. Entering may require controlled speech, restraint, and prayer. Mountain weather, animal encounters, and dreams convey approval or warning.

In Pewenche transhumance, summer grazing is not only economic movement. Testimony collected in 2015 describes hills, forests, rivers, lagoons, ceremonial sites, and paths as having ngen and *newen*. The person entering must “know how” to enter.

### Chiloé maritime cycle

**[REGIONAL FOLKLORE]** Chilote mythology contains named beings—Pincoya, Millalobo, Sirena, Caleuche, Camahueto, sea-horse, and others—collected by Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, Oreste Plath, and local narrators. These traditions are historically layered: Williche concepts, Spanish-Catholic imagery, maritime work, colonial settlement, and print folklore all contributed.

Recent Williche-oriented analysis argues that Pincoya and Sirena are best understood as visible forms of *ngen’* governing intertidal shellfish and marine fish. This is a strong functional interpretation supported by current testimony, but not proof that early twentieth-century collectors understood the beings through precisely that category.

### Punishment and reciprocity stories

**[FOLKLORE / RECORDED-TRADITION]** A widespread narrative grammar runs as follows:

1. a person enters or takes from a place;
2. the owner is ignored or offended;
3. the person experiences illness, fear, lost direction, bad weather, scarcity, or supernatural encounter;
4. a knowledgeable elder, machi, or dream identifies the breach;
5. apology, prayer, offering, withdrawal, or moderated use restores the relation.

These stories are etiological, ethical, and practical. They explain danger and teach protocols of resource use. Their recurrence does not demonstrate a centralized priestly canon.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No central temple institution

There is no evidence for a pan-Mapuche temple system dedicated collectively to “the Ngen,” no centralized priesthood of ngen, and no canonical idol cult. Sacred geography itself is the principal cultic field.

Relevant places include:

- springs and waterfalls;
- rivers, lakes, wetlands, and seashores;
- wooded hills and old-growth groves;
- significant rocks and caves;
- volcanoes and high mountains;
- *foye* and *pewen* stands;
- *ngillatuwe* ceremonial grounds;
- *rewe* and local healing sites;
- burial grounds and *kuel* landscapes.

Dillehay’s study of monumental and ceremonial landscapes demonstrates long-lived material organization of ritual and political space. Archaeology, however, cannot assign every mound or monument to an individual ngen without independent testimony.

### Ritual specialists

- **Machi:** Healer, diviner, ritual expert, and mediator with spirits and powers. A machi may negotiate with place owners, obtain medicinal knowledge, diagnose a breach, or petition for restoration. The machi is not simply a “priest of the ngen.”
- **Ngenpin:** Ritual speaker, “master of the word,” associated with authoritative speech and communal ceremony. Normally a human office.
- **Longko:** Community authority who may have ritual as well as political responsibilities.
- **Ngillatufe and related regional offices:** Leaders or officiants in communal prayer.
- **Ngen foye/gen boye:** Historically recorded canelo-related ceremonial responsibility. Bacigalupo’s work shows why this office should not be merged with every similarly named specialist.

### Prayer and offerings

**[RECORDED-TRADITION]** The most characteristic act is verbal acknowledgment: asking permission, giving the reason for entry, requesting safe passage, or thanking the owner. A brief personal *ngellipun* differs from a large communal *ngillatun*.

Offerings can include *muday*, bread, cooked unsalted meat, tobacco, or other locally appropriate substances. Some accounts mention sacrificing a chicken or another small animal as propitiation. Animal sacrifice also belongs to larger communal ceremonies, but not every animal offered in a *ngillatun* is specifically “to a Ngen.”

### Nguillatun

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Faron describes the *ngillatun* as a communal agricultural and protective rite associated with fertility, harvest, animals, prosperity, and defense from harmful forces. Invocations vary regionally and historically. Some address high beings, ancestors, *pillan*, local powers, or a Christian-inflected creator language. It is inaccurate to call the ceremony solely a “cult of the Ngen.”

Williche participants at Pucatrihue reportedly approach Taita Huenteyao before the main *ngillatun* to obtain permission. This is a regional example of a local owner’s authority over ceremonial access.

### Machitun

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC]** The *machitun* is a healing complex involving diagnosis, song, drumming, plants, spirit communication, and removal or redirection of harmful agency. Ngen may supply medicines or appear in the causal history of illness, but the ritual is not uniformly directed to them.

### Human sacrifice

**[CONTESTED-MATERIAL FINDING]** Searches found no secure evidence for a regular cult in which human victims were offered to the class of ngen.

After the 1960 Valdivia earthquake and tsunami, a child was killed at Collileufu in a ritual intended to stop or appease the sea. The event is documented in legal and journalistic history, though sensational retellings disagree about its details. It is evidence for an exceptional catastrophe ritual within a particular community, not evidence that “the Ngen” ordinarily received human sacrifice. Without the trial record and testimony explicitly naming an ngen-ko or other ngen as recipient, attaching the killing directly to a formal Ngen cult would exceed the evidence.

### Temple prostitution and systematic cult violence

No credible evidence was found for temple prostitution, a hereditary Ngen priesthood conducting systematic violence, or state-sponsored Ngen sacrifice. Such claims should be treated as unsupported.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1606: archaeologically deep landscapes, unnamed beings

Mapuche occupation, landscape modification, burial practice, mound construction, and ritual gathering long predate the first written occurrence of *gen*. These furnish context for territorial religion but do not securely name particular ngen. **[ARCHAEOLOGICAL CONTEXT, NOT DIRECT ATTESTATION]**

### 1606–1777: colonial lexical record and missionary translation

Valdivia, Febrés, and Havestadt document the linguistic root and owner/master constructions. Missionary confession manuals sought equivalents for God, devil, sin, soul, priest, and possession, inevitably reorganizing Indigenous vocabulary. The period supplies firm lexical evidence but only fragmentary evidence for the later systematized “nature-spirit” class.

Rosales and Núñez de Pineda describe seventeenth-century religion but foreground *pillan*, machi, divination, warfare, healing, and what Christian authors called superstition or diabolism. This may reflect actual emphasis, observer selection, or both.

### Nineteenth century: military occupation and territorial rupture

The Chilean occupation of Araucanía and Argentine “Conquest of the Desert” radically reduced and fragmented Indigenous territory. Reservation or *reducción* systems, settler agriculture, mission expansion, and state schooling changed access to forests, rivers, mountains, burial places, and ceremonial grounds.

For a relational system centered on local owners, territorial dispossession was also religious disruption. Written sources increasingly preserved traditions at the same time that state programs endangered their material settings.

### 1890s–1930s: documentation and imposed systems

Lenz, Augusta, Guevara, Latcham, Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, Coña/Moesbach, and others created the major early modern corpus. This period preserved invaluable words and narratives, but scholars frequently arranged them into European evolutionary categories such as animism, totemism, ancestor worship, and “supreme being.”

Latcham’s treatment of *ngenünkan*, *pillan*, totems, and ritual offices illustrates the difficulty: the documentary details may be valuable even where the encompassing theory is outdated.

### Mid-twentieth century: social anthropology

Faron studied Mapuche morality, social organization, ancestors, and communal ritual. His conclusion that missionary Christianity had not simply displaced Mapuche practice challenged linear conversion narratives, although regional and denominational diversity remained substantial.

Koessler-Ilg’s Argentine material demonstrated that narrative traditions continued east of the Andes and developed in distinct Neuquén and Río Negro settings.

### 1970s–1990s: “cosmovision” and Grebe’s model

Grebe and collaborators created the most influential schematic descriptions of Mapuche cosmology. Her ngen model emphasized guardianship, balance, continuity, preservation, and reciprocal conduct. It made local nature owners intelligible within anthropology and environmental education.

That influence also produced a risk: later summaries often present Grebe’s classification as an ancient, uniform, closed pantheon. A 1992 analytical table cannot by itself establish the exact form of sixteenth-century belief.

Foerster and Bacigalupo introduced stronger historical criticism, especially concerning Ngünechen, Christianity, ritual offices, gender, and machi practice.

### 1990s–2004: Pangue and Ralco

The Pangue dam began operation in 1996; construction of the Ralco hydroelectric project followed in the upper Bío-Bío. Ralco flooded more than 3,000 hectares and displaced roughly one hundred Pewenche families. Opposition joined Pewenche women and families, Mapuche organizations, environmentalists, lawyers, and international human-rights advocates.

The conflict was not merely about abstract “environment.” Reservoir inundation and relocation altered cemeteries, paths, river relations, grazing circuits, medicinal sites, and places possessing ngen and *newen*. Nevertheless, public and legal documents more often framed the dispute through land, relocation, consultation, compensation, and Indigenous rights than through a named Ngen claimant.

In 2002, affected families petitioned the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights. A framework agreement was signed in February 2003, followed by a definitive friendly settlement on 13 October 2003; the IACHR approved it in Report 30/04. The United Nations Special Rapporteur reported irregularities acknowledged by Chilean institutions and noted the eventual agreement permitting completion. Ralco began operating in 2004.

**Finding:** The ngen framework helps explain why flooding a river valley can constitute injury to a network of persons and owners rather than merely loss of economically substitutable acreage. But claims that “Ralco was opposed specifically because of Ngen-ko” need a named testimony or document; the formal settlement itself does not make Ngen-ko a legal party.

### Twenty-first century: water, forestry, and hydropower

Current testimony connects the disappearance or withdrawal of ngen with:

- industrial pine and eucalyptus plantations;
- drying or diversion of water;
- wetland damage;
- road construction;
- mining;
- intensive agriculture;
- tourism in ceremonial or medicinal places;
- large and small hydroelectric projects.

A recurrent expression is that when water leaves, “the essence of the ngen-ko” leaves with it. In some accounts, stopping pollution does not guarantee the being’s return: a broken relation requires more than engineering repair.

At Neltume, resistance to an Endesa/Enel hydroelectric proposal drew on community organization, sacred landscape, and the memory of Ralco. The project was withdrawn and finally abandoned in 2018. Research cautions against imagining a single Mapuche position: neighboring communities adopted different strategies ranging from opposition to negotiation.

### Present status

Ngen remain part of living Mapuche and Williche religious language, healing, environmental ethics, political advocacy, education, film, and cultural revitalization. This is neither an untouched survival nor simply a recent revival. It is a continuing tradition repeatedly translated, regionalized, Christianized, contested, and publicly reformulated.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Environmental discourse

Ngen now appear in environmental humanities, Indigenous-rights advocacy, conservation programs, and intercultural education. This use has strong foundations in contemporary testimony but may compress complex place relations into a general slogan that “nature has spirits.”

The most defensible formulation is not that Mapuche tradition teaches a modern scientific conservation policy, but that many Mapuche testimonies describe land and water as socially and morally inhabited, making unrestricted extraction illegitimate.

### Museums, film, and education

Jaime Díaz’s 2022 film *Ngen* follows Rosa, a machi from Fin Fin Boroa, and presents her relations with medicine and owners of nature. Museum exhibitions and school materials increasingly use ngen to introduce Mapuche ecological knowledge.

These works document and shape modern reception. Their stylized visuals are not archaeological reconstructions.

### Chiloé revitalization

Recent Williche scholarship reclassifies familiar “Chilote mythological creatures” through Indigenous categories: Pincoya and Sirena as owners of shellfish and fish, and other maritime figures as ngen or *wekufü*. This challenges folklore collections that detached beings from Indigenous territorial knowledge.

It is also a modern act of intellectual recovery. Each equation should be attributed to its contemporary researchers and consultants rather than retrojected indiscriminately into all earlier Chiloé lore.

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search found no well-documented Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Renaissance Hermetic, or historical European grimoire tradition centered on ngen. Websites presenting them as elementals equivalent to European sylphs, undines, gnomes, or salamanders are **[MODERN COMPARATIVE INVENTION]**.

There is contemporary Indigenous revitalization and personal spiritual practice, but reliable demographic evidence for a separate institutional “Ngen neopagan religion” was not found. Generic internet rituals, planetary correspondences, sigils, invocation formulas, and claims that ngen are a closed set of elemental gods should be treated as recent inventions unless sourced to named Mapuche authorities.

### Nationalism and politics

Mapuche movements invoke ngen, *itrofill mongen* and territorial spirituality in land and water defense. Chilean institutions also use “cosmovisión mapuche” in museums, tourism, health programs, and environmental consultation. This can create recognition, but it may also domesticate political land claims into nonthreatening cultural symbolism.

No credible evidence was found for a stable modern extremist organization organized around Ngen worship. Assertions equating Mapuche militancy with a “cult of nature spirits” are polemical, not scholarship.

### Commercial use

“Ngen” appears in titles for films, cultural projects, ecological initiatives, tourism, crafts, and businesses. Commercial naming demonstrates the term’s modern visibility, not continuity of ritual authorization.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Debate 1: lexical owner or supernatural spirit?

The colonial evidence unmistakably supports “owner/master.” The historical question is when particular owner constructions became—or were first documented as—nonhuman place-persons.

Grebe argues for two domains, profane and cosmological. Course offers a more relational reading that resists turning *ngen* into a simple noun denoting a bounded supernatural species. The best conclusion is that “owner” is the linguistic base, while supernatural reference depends on context.

### Debate 2: ancient pantheon or ethnographic system?

Popular presentations often list dozens of ngen as if they formed a precontact canonical pantheon. The primary record does not support a fixed official list. Grebe’s taxonomy is well-grounded modern ethnography, but its neatness partly reflects scholarly ordering.

The plurality and productivity of the compounds are more securely established than the antiquity of any exhaustive classification.

### Debate 3: Ngen and Ngünechen

Bacigalupo’s three hypotheses remain valuable because no single origin theory fits all evidence. Christian mission clearly affected later descriptions of Ngünechen as an omnipotent creator. It does not follow that every feature of Ngünechen is a missionary invention, nor that nature ngen were created by Christianity.

The crucial error is phonetic conflation: *ngen*, *Ngünechen*, *Ngünenchen*, *Ngenemapun*, and related names are historically connected but not automatically interchangeable.

### Debate 4: animism, totemism, or ontology?

Guevara and Latcham employed early anthropological evolutionism. Their “totemic” reconstructions are no longer adequate as neutral descriptions. More recent work analyzes reciprocity, personhood, force, landscape, and political ecology.

Even “animism” can overgeneralize if it implies that every object contains the same kind of spirit. Local testimony concerns particular owners, powers, histories, and protocols.

### Debate 5: ecology versus romanticization

Ngen relations can support limits on extraction, protection of water, and restraint in forests. But representing all Mapuche people as timeless ecological guardians erases farming, livestock, wage work, urban life, internal disagreement, and historical adaptation.

The Ralco and Neltume cases show both a genuine collision of ontologies and politically diverse community responses.

### Debate 6: missionary suppression and transformation

Missionaries translated Indigenous terms into Christian categories, denounced machi and local powers as demonic or superstitious, and taught equations between Christian and Mapuche high-being language. State schooling and territorial dispossession further weakened ritual transmission.

Yet Faron found in the mid-twentieth century that Christian dissemination had not straightforwardly erased Mapuche practices. Present religion ranges from traditional practice and Catholic syncretism to Pentecostal or evangelical rejection, selective coexistence, and revitalization.

### Debate 7: human sacrifice

The 1960 Collileufu killing is a documented exceptional catastrophe response, not evidence of an ordinary Ngen institution. Colonial allegations of Indigenous sacrifice require case-by-case scrutiny because conquest literature frequently used atrocity claims polemically. No secure evidence found in this research establishes recurrent human sacrifice to ngen.

### State of the evidence

Important holdings and publication sites include:

- Biblioteca Nacional de Chile and Memoria Chilena: digitized colonial chronicles, early ethnography, folklore, dictionaries, and missionary history.
- Chilean Archivo Nacional: manuscript of *Cautiverio feliz*.
- University and journal archives: Grebe, Bacigalupo, Foerster-related studies, contemporary environmental anthropology.
- Community testimony preserved in books, interviews, documentary film, and Indigenous organizations.
- Archaeological collections concerning *kuel*, settlement, and ceremonial landscapes.

Major losses include unrecorded oral teachings, places destroyed by agriculture, dams, forestry, roads, and settlement, and meanings filtered or omitted by missionary authors.

## 11. Conclusions

The Ngen cannot responsibly be reconstructed as one deity. The secure record begins with a seventeenth-century Mapudungun word for an owner, master, possessor, or responsible party. Over the later documentary record, that productive relation is abundantly attested in nonhuman form: owners of waters, forests, hills, stones, wind, fire, paths, animals, and particular places.

What persists is not a canonical divine genealogy but a protocol of relations. A place is not ownerless simply because no human holds a deed. Entry, gathering, healing, and extraction may require speech, permission, moderation, reciprocity, and thanks. Illness, scarcity, fear, or ecological loss can register a broken relation.

Grebe’s work remains indispensable, but her taxonomy must be dated as late-twentieth-century ethnography rather than quoted as a timeless indigenous catechism. Colonial sources establish the language early while leaving the cosmological system fragmentary. Chiloé, Williche, Pewenche, and Argentine records demonstrate real regional variation. Modern environmental conflicts, particularly Ralco, did not invent the territorial logic of ngen, but they gave it new public, legal, and ecological expression.

No secure evidence supports a fixed Ngen pantheon, canonical images, planetary correspondences, regular human sacrifice, temple prostitution, European occult transmission, or a single universal creation narrative. Those absences are part of the documented history.
