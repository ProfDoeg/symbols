# The Machi's spirits (the filew): Research Dossier

**Class:** Spirit or collective ancestral spirit associated with Mapuche *machi*; depending on speaker and context, also the *machi*’s spiritual gift or power, an individual possessing/tutelary spirit, an ancestral *machi*, or—in a Christian-influenced theological systematization—the “Spirit of Power and Wisdom” of Ngünechen.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Andrés Febrés’s printed Mapudungun–Spanish vocabulary, issued with his *Arte de la lengua general del Reyno de Chile* in Lima in **1765**, defines *Fileu* as “los machis curanderos.” This attests the word, but apparently as a designation for human ritual specialists rather than an independently described spirit. The earliest unambiguous documentary descriptions of *filew/fileu* as a spirit or power belonging to the *machi* are much later, principally twentieth-century linguistic and ethnographic sources. Félix de Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) still says that certain Indigenous persons are called “*machi ó fileu*,” showing that person, office, power, and spirit remained lexically entangled.

> **Evidentiary warning:** “The Machi’s spirits” is not the name of one god with a fixed personality, genealogy, and mythology. *Filew* belongs to a relational complex involving the living *machi*, deceased ancestral machi, an individual *machi püllü*, the *rewe*, dreams and visions, Ngünechen, and other powers. Sources do not define these relationships uniformly. Treating Filew as a single Mapuche equivalent of a Christian angel, Holy Spirit, “spirit guide,” or patron deity would erase the principal finding of the evidence: its contextual variability.

## 1. Identification, names, and classifications

### Names and spellings

- **Fileu** — spelling used in eighteenth- through twentieth-century Spanish-language dictionaries and ethnographies. Febrés’s 1765 vocabulary has “*Fileu los machis curanderos*.” Augusta’s 1910 *Lecturas araucanas* similarly calls certain healers “*machi ó fileu*.”  
  **Tier:** securely attested lexeme; its early referent is a human practitioner or office, not demonstrably a separate supernatural being.

- **Filew** — spelling in contemporary Mapudungun orthographies and in Ana Mariella Bacigalupo’s English-language ethnography. Fernando Zúñiga’s modern glossary defines it as “*espíritu de machi antigua*,” the spirit of a former female *machi*. Bacigalupo glosses it as the “generic ancestral spirit of all machi,” literally “the knowledgeable one.”  
  **Tier:** modern linguistic and ethnographic documentation.

- **Fileo / filen** — Hispanized or unstable scholarly spellings occurring in secondary works. A 1920s ethnological publication, citing Augusta, refers to persons called “*machi o filen*.”  
  **Tier:** transcriptional variants; they should not automatically be treated as distinct beings.

- **Machi püllü** (“machi spirit”) — frequently used for the particular ancestral or possessing spirit associated with an individual *machi*. Some practitioners distinguish it from the generic *filew*; others use the terms as overlapping or contextually identical.  
  **Tier:** living and recent ethnographic terminology.

- **Machi gen / machi ngen** — a tutelary “owner” or spirit associated with the *machi*. Contemporary cultural presentations distinguish this from *machi püllü*, but usage is not standardized across Mapuche territory.  
  **Tier:** regional and contemporary explanatory vocabulary.

### Etymology

Bacigalupo gives “the knowledgeable one” as the literal sense of *filew*. This is influential but should be treated as an ethnographic gloss, not a settled historical derivation. Zúñiga’s linguistic glossary conservatively supplies only “spirit of a former machi.” No reviewed historical-linguistic source establishes a universally accepted segmentation or Proto-Mapudungun etymology.

A further ambiguity is semantic rather than merely etymological. *Filew* can refer to:

1. a *machi* or the condition of being one;
2. the vocation or gift that makes a person a *machi*;
3. a deceased ancestral *machi*;
4. an individual spirit possessing or guiding a practitioner;
5. a generic ancestral spirit shared by machi;
6. divine power conveyed through Ngünechen.

Hans Schindler warned that ethnologists have sometimes reified Mapuche terms for qualities, capacities, inspiration, or power into Western-style “spirits.” In his field material, *filew* could designate a *virtud*, a capacity possessed by a *machi*, while comparable speakers also used *wekufü*, *wentru*, *kona*, or other relational terms.  
**Tier:** scholarly dispute; no single definition exhausts documented usage.

### Classification by source and period

| Source tradition | Classification |
|---|---|
| Febrés, 1765 | A name for *machi* healers |
| Augusta, 1910 | A human ritual-healing specialist: “*machi ó fileu*” |
| Mid-/late twentieth-century Mapuche theology represented by Martín Alonqueo | Ngünechen’s “Spirit of Power and Wisdom,” which selects and sustains machi |
| Bacigalupo’s 1990s–2000s Araucanía ethnography | Generic ancestral spirit of machi; sometimes also the possessing spirit or spirit-husband of a particular practitioner |
| Zúñiga, modern linguistic glossary | “Spirit of a former machi” |
| Recent ritual ethnography | A complex ancestral entity that may coincide with or remain distinct from the individual *machi püllü* and Ngünechen |

It is therefore safer to classify *filew* as a **spirit/power complex**, usually ancestral and tutelary, rather than as a discrete god.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Before the word *filew*: sixteenth- and seventeenth-century evidence

Alonso de Ercilla’s *La Araucana* appeared in three parts in 1569, 1578, and 1589. It contains the literary magician Fitón and descriptions of divination, but Fitón is a classical-epic construction and not evidence for a being called Filew. Later scholars disputed how much reliable Reche/Mapuche religion could be recovered from Ercilla.  
**Finding:** no secure Filew attestation.

Seventeenth-century chronicles document *machi* and ritual specialists without clearly isolating a *filew* spirit:

- Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán’s *Cautiverio feliz*, written in 1673 about his 1629 captivity, mentions *machi* consulted concerning illness and sorcery. It is valuable eyewitness and retrospective testimony, but its Catholic moral vocabulary—especially around male *machi weye*—must not be mistaken for neutral Indigenous classification.
- Diego de Rosales’s *Historia general del Reino de Chile*, composed in the seventeenth century but printed much later, describes Indigenous ritualists through Christian categories such as sorcery and demonic intervention.
- These chronicles establish the historical depth of the *machi* office, not an independently named Filew.

**Tier:** securely attested colonial descriptions of practitioners; no secure early Filew ontology.

### First lexical attestation: Febrés, 1765

Andrés Febrés’s missionary grammar and dictionary gives:

> “*Fileu los machis curanderos*.”

The physical witness is an eighteenth-century printed missionary linguistic work. It proves the term was known by 1765. It does **not** say that Fileu is a god, ancestral spirit, possessing husband, inhabitant of a *rewe*, or emissary of Ngünechen. Those are later documented meanings.

Bernardo Havestadt’s *Chilidúgú* (Münster, 1777), based upon earlier missionary work in Chile, includes *machi* songs. Rodolfo Lenz later observed that these were the only apparently genuine Mapuche ritual songs known to him before his own collections. They are evidence for a ritual vocal corpus but do not furnish a narrative biography of Filew.

### Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century vernacular records

Rodolfo Lenz’s *Estudios araucanos* (1895–1897) printed Mapudungun dialogues, narratives, historical accounts, letters, and songs with literal Spanish translations. Lenz recorded a *machi* song and sought contemporary oral materials rather than simply repeating missionary dictionaries. His collection nevertheless relied on only one principal consultant for each dialect represented; its regional coverage must not be universalized.

Félix de Augusta and Sigifredo de Fraunhäusl’s *Lecturas araucanas* (1910) contains the major early vernacular description “Cómo cura la machi,” ritual speech, songs, and explanatory commentary. Its opening definition—“*Se da el nombre de machi ó fileu á ciertas personas indígenas*”—still identifies *fileu* with human specialists. Augusta’s *Diccionario araucano-español y español-araucano* followed in 1916.

Tomás Guevara and Ricardo Latcham attempted broader reconstructions of “Araucanian religion.” Their syntheses preserved information but often organized fluid local relations into evolutionist schemes—animism, shamanism, totemism, or a pantheon—more rigid than the underlying testimony.

Pascual Coña’s life narrative, recorded and edited through the Capuchin missionary Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach and first published in 1930, is indispensable Indigenous testimony concerning Mapuche life and religion. It is, however, a mediated, translated text produced in a Catholic mission setting, not an untouched precolonial transcript.

### Later ethnographic corpus

- Louis C. Faron, *Hawks of the Sun* (1964), emphasized moral order, ancestors, ritual congregations, and the relation between social organization and religion.
- María Ester Grebe and collaborators’ work from the 1970s systematized a vertically and horizontally ordered Mapuche cosmos. Its diagrams became widely reproduced, but their neatness should not be projected backward as a timeless creed.
- Rolf Foerster’s *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche* (1993; second edition 1995) deliberately studies the succession of chroniclers’, missionaries’, anthropologists’, and Mapuche intellectuals’ discourses rather than claiming direct access to one immutable theology.
- Ana Mariella Bacigalupo’s articles and *Shamans of the Foye Tree* (2007) supply the fullest sustained documentation of Filew as ancestral/possessing spirit in contemporary Araucanía.
- Recent ethnomusicological work on *ulutun* explicitly reports that the boundaries among *filew*, *machi püllü*, and Ngünechen can shift with practitioner and ritual context.

### Huilliche, Chiloé, Neuquén, and Río Negro

The Huilliche and Chiloé records document *machi*, ritual callings, the *kultrun/cultrunca*, curing, weather knowledge, and Catholic-Indigenous syncretism. They do **not**, in the sources reviewed, securely establish that every Huilliche or Chilote *machi* conceptualized a spirit called Filew in the Araucanía sense.

Chiloé underwent intensive Jesuit and Franciscan missionization. Catholic chapels were sometimes placed at former *ngillatuwe* sites, while Indigenous offices and spatial practices were transformed rather than simply erased. Later Chilote folklore also fused Huilliche *kalku/machi* concepts with European witchcraft. Filew should not be retroactively inserted into every story collected by Cavada, Vicuña Cifuentes, Quintana, or Plath.

Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s Neuquén collections, assembled during decades of work beginning after her arrival in San Martín de los Andes in 1920, preserve Mapuche tales and traditions through named consultants such as Antonio Kinchauala and Francisco Kolüpán. Their mythic corpus is important comparative evidence, but no securely indexed Filew narrative has been established here.  
**Finding:** absence of evidence for a standardized trans-Andean Filew mythology.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

The Filew’s securely documented sphere is the making and work of the *machi*:

- issuing or embodying the call to become a *machi*;
- causing or interpreting *machi kutran*, the illness associated with a resisted vocation;
- communicating through *pewma* (dreams) and *perimontun* (extraordinary visions or apparitions);
- possessing, “mounting,” or settling in the practitioner’s head during trance;
- transmitting ritual speech, diagnosis, healing knowledge, and power;
- enabling journeys to or communication with *wenu mapu*;
- residing in, being fed at, or being protected through the *rewe*;
- renewing its marriage or alliance with the *machi* in periodic rites.

These are functions, not a centralized divine portfolio. Filew has no securely attested responsibility for a celestial body, weekday, number, season, or universal natural domain.

### The calling and the *perimontun*

Bacigalupo’s consultants describe callings through recurrent dreams, visions, encounters with extraordinary beings or phenomena, lightning/thunder, illness, bleeding, paralysis, altered consciousness, and apparent death and return. These signs are interpreted retrospectively by established machi and kin.

In a 2020 interview, machi Jorge Quilaqueo described childhood *perimontun* between roughly five and ten years of age, followed in adolescence by *kisu kutran machi*, an illness culminating in a deathlike crisis. He called Filew both the *don* of the *machi* and a *compa püllü*, a companion spirit from *wenu mapu*. This is important living testimony, not proof that every historical Mapuche community held the same staged model.

Refusing the vocation may, in ethnographic accounts, prolong illness or cause the Filew to punish the candidate. Acceptance initiates apprenticeship with a senior *machi*, construction or installation of a *rewe*, and ritual consecration.

### The head and possession

During *küymi/küymün*—altered ritual consciousness—the Filew or *machi püllü* may enter or “mount” the head. A process has been recorded as *longko luupan*. The *trarilongko* headband, headscarf, and silver ornaments consequently protect and adorn the place of reception. One testimony recorded by Bacigalupo calls the Filew “*la machi del cielo*,” the heavenly machi who notices and desires the ancient plant-jewels.

This language permits several interpretations without resolving them:

- the Filew is a deceased machi;
- it is an ancestral collectivity personified as one heavenly machi;
- it is a possessing partner;
- it is a capacity made present through ritual objects.

### The *rewe*

A domestic *machi rewe* is ordinarily a notched upright tree trunk or pole, commonly laurel or oak, with branches of *foye* (Drimys winteri), *triwe*, *klon*, or *coligüe*. It faces east in many documented cases. It is simultaneously:

- a ritual altar and material sign of office;
- a vertical route or axis between terrestrial and upper realms;
- the Filew’s dwelling;
- the place at which the spirit is fed and invoked;
- the structure mounted by the *machi* to enact visionary ascent.

Foods, drink, medicinal plants, *kopiwe* flowers, and other objects coded as nourishing or feminine may be placed around it. Knives, volcanic stones, spears, or *chueca* sticks—objects with martial or masculine associations—may be placed on its steps or summit to guard the Filew from hostile powers.

The Filew has no independent anthropomorphic statue. Photographs and museum installations of machi beside a *rewe* document practitioners and ritual apparatus, not portraits of the spirit.

### The *kultrun*

The *kultrun* is a shallow bowl drum covered with hide and played during curing, divination, initiation, renewal, and communal ritual. Its painted membrane may map the four directions and cosmological divisions, though designs differ among practitioners. It:

- summons or pleases the spirit;
- regulates dance and trance;
- accompanies the spirit’s speech and the *machi*’s ascent;
- materializes an individual *machi*’s cosmological knowledge.

The widespread four-part “Mapuche cosmogram” is therefore not one canonical icon of Filew. Grebe’s structural diagrams and popular reproductions systematize variable drum designs.

### The *machitun* and ascent

In healing, the *machi* diagnoses whether illness arises from social transgression, spirit action, witchcraft, intrusive matter, or other causes. Ritual elements may include drumming, chanting, prayer, herbal remedies, massage, suction, smoke, the removal or display of a pathogenic object, interrogation by a *dungumachife*, and confrontation with a harmful agency.

The ritual ascent of the *rewe* announces altered consciousness and travel or communication between levels of the cosmos. Filew supplies knowledge and power; the *dungumachife* may interpret speech uttered in trance. Not every healing ceremony contains an identical ascent, and *machitun*, *datun*, and *ulutun* should not be treated as interchangeable labels.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to divine beings

### Genealogy

No primary source supplies biological parents, siblings, birth, consort, or offspring for Filew.

Bacigalupo’s phrase “generic ancestral spirit of all machi” supplies a corporate ancestry, not a family tree. An individual *machi püllü* can be associated with a deceased grandmother or other former practitioner, while Filew can be understood as the continuity of ancestral machi power across generations.

The *machi* is frequently described as Filew’s “spiritual bride,” while Filew is at once husband and master. This is ritual kinship, not a mythological marriage between two pantheon gods. Both female and male machi may occupy the bride position. Their dress, flowers, jewelry, headscarves, and relations with the *foye* express this position.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being or spirit class

- **Ngünechen / Chaw Ngünechen — source and transmission of power.** Alonqueo, quoted in Foerster (1979 testimony; Foerster 1993/1995), describes Fileu as Ngünechen’s “Spirit of Power and Wisdom,” sent to preserve a succession of machi. Recent practitioners may invoke Ngünechen and an ancestral *machi püllü* jointly.  
  **Tier:** modern Mapuche theological systematization, with probable Christian conceptual influence; living religious interpretation.

- **Machi püllü — identity, overlap, or differentiation.** Bacigalupo (1990s–2007 fieldwork) and recent *ulutun* ethnography report that Filew may be the generic ancestral machi spirit while *machi püllü* is the particular spirit of one practitioner; in other contexts they coincide.  
  **Tier:** securely attested modern ethnographic variation.

- **Ngen / gen — selection and delegated power.** Some contemporary testimony says a *machi* is selected by *ngen* or by a particular spirit; “machi gen” can mean the tutelary being of the practitioner. Jorge Quilaqueo describes the *lawen ngelu* as a *ngen* sent through Filew.  
  **Tier:** contemporary regional account; not a universal genealogy.

- **Wekufü — adversarial encounter.** Foerster characterizes Filew possession as the positive pole producing the *machi*, opposed to harmful *wekufü* possession producing the sick person. During healing the Filew empowers the *machi* to identify, negotiate with, or expel the harmful agency. Schindler’s evidence complicates this binary: some speakers used *wekufü* for a morally ambiguous power or capacity rather than an intrinsically evil demon.  
  **Tier:** attested ritual opposition; the good/evil system is a modern scholarly and theological simplification contested by field evidence.

- **Kalku — indirect conflict through sorcery.** A *kalku* may send harmful forces or intrusive substances; Filew empowers the *machi* who diagnoses and counters the attack. No independent narrative meeting between a personified Filew and a named individual kalku has been located.  
  **Tier:** widespread ethnographic pattern, not a single myth.

- **Wenu püllüam / ancestral sky spirits — association.** Filew belongs to the ancestral upper-world complex and may be described as arriving from *wenu mapu*. The evidence does not yield a discrete narrated encounter with a named *püllüam*.  
  **Tier:** cosmological association only.

- **Meli Küyen / Meli Wangülen — no demonstrated direct interaction.** These fourfold moon and star powers appear in Bacigalupo’s ritual vocabulary, but no reviewed source documents a specific Filew episode with them.  
  **Tier:** absence of evidence.

- **Pillan — no securely documented direct Filew interaction.** *Pillan* are invoked in broader Mapuche religion and ancestral politics, but membership in the same cosmological field is not a narrated meeting.  
  **Tier:** absence of evidence.

- **Chaw Wenteyao/Huenteao — no documented Filew interaction.** This Huilliche coastal mediator is central around San Juan de la Costa, but no source reviewed describes Filew meeting or contesting him.  
  **Tier:** regional separation; absence of evidence.

- **Christian Holy Spirit — proposed analogy, not an attested mythic encounter.** Calling Fileu the Spirit of Ngünechen resembles Christian pneumatology, especially in Alonqueo’s explicitly monotheistic comparison between Ngünechen’s four persons and the Trinity. This may represent Christian-Mapuche translation and creative theology; it does not establish that Filew was historically “the Holy Spirit under another name.”  
  **Tier:** syncretistic identification or theological analogy.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Filew has no surviving epic cycle comparable to Tren-Tren and Kai-Kai, Mankian, Sumpall, or the narratives of named Chilote beings. Its “myths” are vocational and ritual narratives embedded in practitioners’ lives.

### Cycle I: the spirit chooses a future *machi*

**Narrative:** A child or adolescent experiences dreams, *perimontun*, lightning, unusual animals or people, celestial travel, repeated illness, or unexplained powers. Kin seek established machi, who recognize a calling. The candidate may resist from fear of poverty, stigma, celibacy, gender expectations, or the burdens of ritual service. Resistance worsens *machi kutran*. Acceptance and initiation restore health and transform illness into vocation.

**Earliest evidence:** colonial texts document *machi* but not this full Filew-centered sequence. It is clearly documented in twentieth- and twenty-first-century ethnography.

**Variants:**

- the caller is a deceased grandmother or named ancestral machi;
- the caller is generic Filew;
- Ngünechen sends the power;
- thunder directly initiates the candidate;
- a *ngen* chooses the candidate;
- Filew is the gift rather than the giver.

**Tier:** securely attested living and modern ethnographic tradition; antiquity of the complete sequence cannot be demonstrated.

### Cycle II: spirit marriage and possession

**Narrative:** During initiation the candidate becomes a bride of Filew. Through dress, song, scent, plants, jewelry, and dance, the practitioner attracts the spirit-husband. Filew possesses the head and conveys knowledge. Periodic renewal restores this relationship.

Male and female machi alike may be symbolically feminized in relation to Filew. Bacigalupo interprets the *foye*—a ritually bisexual or co-gendered tree—as enabling movement across gender, generation, and cosmic space.

Colonial Spanish authors sexualized male *machi weye* as effeminate “sodomites” and demon worshippers. Bacigalupo argues that this polemic distorted a Reche co-gendered ritual office and later affected Mapuche self-presentation.  
**Tier:** colonial evidence for hostile Spanish classification; modern ethnographic reconstruction of Mapuche ritual gender; living variants.

### Cycle III: dwelling at the *rewe* and ascent

**Narrative:** Filew dwells at or in the *rewe*. It is nourished with plants, flowers, food, drink, and blood or life-power from sacrificed domestic animals. Protective objects guard it. In trance the *machi* climbs the notched pole, journeys upward, receives information, and returns to diagnose or heal.

The ascent is sometimes interpreted as literal spirit travel, sometimes as possession, and sometimes as performative disclosure to the assembled community. These interpretations may coexist.

**Tier:** well-attested modern ritual complex; no datable ancient narrative text.

### Cycle IV: death, transmission, and return

A *machi*’s spirit may survive death, select a relative or successor, and reappear in dreams or visions. Bacigalupo’s later work on “the machi of thunder” shows how spirits allow practitioners and descendants to produce histories in which the dead comment on colonial violence, land, kinship, and the state.

No single rule governs inheritance: succession may follow family lines, but Filew can choose outside them. “Ancestral spirit of all machi” and “my grandmother’s machi spirit” are compatible in some accounts and distinct in others.

**Tier:** attested oral-history and ethnographic narrative.

## 6. Cult, ritual practice, and material setting

### No independent temple cult

There are no securely documented temples, priesthoods, festivals, offerings, or pilgrimages devoted to Filew as an autonomous deity. Its cultic setting is the *machi* institution.

Relevant places are:

- the practitioner’s house and healing room;
- the personal *rewe*;
- the collective *ngillatuwe* or ceremonial field;
- medicinal wetlands, springs, forests, and *menoko*;
- graves or mounds associated with former machi;
- in some regional traditions, mountains, rocks, lakes, and coastal sacred places.

The distinction between personal *machi rewe* and collective *rewe/ngillatuwe* is essential.

### Initiation and renewal

The initiate receives instruction in medicinal plants, songs, prayers, drum rhythms, diagnosis, spirit speech, bodily techniques, and ritual ethics. A senior *machi* conducts consecration. Domestic animals—often sheep or fowl—may be dedicated, circulated around the *rewe*, painted, or sacrificed. Bacigalupo describes “spirit animals” such as the *karre-karre* hen and *keñeyken* sheep in particular ritual contexts.

Blood sacrifice is documented; **human sacrifice to Filew is not**. Claims about Mapuche human sacrifice belong to other historical or legendary contexts and cannot be transferred to Filew without evidence.

Renewal rites called *ngeykurewen* or *machi pürun* renew the practitioner, altar, community relationship, and spirit marriage. Failure to renew may weaken both practitioner and *rewe*.

### Healing

In *machitun*, *datun*, or *ulutun*, depending on region and diagnosis, Filew may speak through the entranced *machi*. A *dungumachife* repeats or interprets speech. The spirit identifies causes, prescribes remedies, or negotiates with afflicting powers. The ceremony joins pharmacology, kin participation, music, prayer, performance, and cosmological diagnosis; reducing it either to herbal medicine or to supernatural exorcism misrepresents the documented whole.

### Collective ritual

Machi can officiate at *ngillatun*, but in many communities the *ngenpin* or another authority leads it. Filew is therefore not necessarily the object of communal prayer. The machi’s possessing spirit may enable ritual action while prayers are directed to Ngünechen, ancestors, *ngen*, or locally relevant powers.

### Chiloé and Huilliche transformations

Chilote sources remember male *machi* as community healers and *ngillatun* leaders, with women entering the office later according to some collectors. Modern summaries report that the office was reduced in some locations to herbal healing or advice about witchcraft. Catholic missionaries incorporated Indigenous personnel and sometimes built chapels on older ceremonial sites. The result was neither unaltered continuity nor total disappearance, but layered Catholic-Huilliche practice.

Filew itself is poorly documented in these regional materials. A generic Araucanía model should not be imposed on Chiloé.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1550: archaeological horizon

Dillehay’s study of *kuel* mounds and *rewe-kuel* landscapes argues for long-lived ceremonial and political landscapes in south-central Chile. Archaeology can demonstrate monumental gathering places, feasting, burial, and territorial memory; it cannot identify a being called Filew without inscriptions or secure continuity of terminology.

### 1550s–1600: early colonial observation

Spanish war and missionary texts encounter Reche ritual specialists. Ercilla turns Indigenous divination into Renaissance epic; later chroniclers characterize specialists as sorcerers or servants of the devil. The *machi* office is visible, but Filew is not.

### Seventeenth century

Núñez de Pineda and Rosales document machi curing, divination, political counsel, and colonial demonization. Male *machi weye* appear prominently. Colonial writers merge Indigenous gender difference, anti-Spanish ritual power, witchcraft, and sodomy into a polemical category.

### 1765–1777: lexical threshold

Febrés prints *Fileu* as a designation for “machi healers.” Havestadt prints ritual songs. This is the earliest secure textual horizon for the word, but not for the later spirit ontology.

### Nineteenth century: conquest and reduction

The Chilean occupation of Araucanía and Argentina’s campaigns in Patagonia destroyed autonomy, seized land, concentrated communities, and disrupted ceremonial landscapes. Missionization, schools, policing, and medical regulation stigmatized machi practice. Political functions formerly held by some male machi declined; female healers became increasingly prominent.

### 1895–1930: ethnographic textualization

Lenz, Augusta, Guevara, Latcham, Moesbach/Coña, and regional collectors converted oral practice into printed corpora. *Fileu* could still denote the practitioner. The same period produced systematic “Araucanian religion” models, often under Christian and evolutionist assumptions.

### Mid-twentieth century: pantheon and cosmology models

Faron, Grebe, and others framed Mapuche religion as a coherent moral and cosmological system. Mapuche intellectuals such as Martín Alonqueo presented Ngünechen as one sovereign creator and Fileu as his Spirit of Power and Wisdom. Some scholars regard Ngünechen’s apex and the Fileu–divine-spirit formulation as strongly shaped by Christian mission translation; practitioners may nonetheless regard them as authentically Mapuche. Historical influence does not make a living theology insincere.

### 1990s–2000s: person-centered ethnography

Bacigalupo documented substantial disagreement among individual machi and treated their cosmologies as creative, situational, and politically engaged. *Shamans of the Foye Tree* established the now influential distinction between generic Filew and individual *machi püllü*, while also documenting their overlap.

### 2009–present: territorial rights and public politics

Machi Francisca Linconao’s successful 2009 protection action against Forestal Palermo concerned native forest, springs, medicinal plants, and a sacred *menoko*. Chile’s courts applied ILO Convention 169, making her case an important legal recognition of Indigenous spiritual relations with territory.

Bacigalupo cautions that Chilean political actors and Mapuche movements both turn female machi into icons of authenticity. Individual machi respond variously: some avoid partisan activity, some appear at rallies, some defend territories, and some interpret political conflict through dreams and spirits. There is no single “machi political position.”

The imprisonment and hunger strikes of machi Celestino Córdova made access to his *rewe* a public human-rights and prison-policy question. Supporters argued that separation from the *rewe* imperiled his spiritual health; opponents centered his conviction in the fatal Luchsinger-Mackay arson. Both are facts in the public record and should not be collapsed into a theological judgment.

In Argentina, conflict around machi Betiana Colhuan Nahuel and the Lafken Winkul Mapu community likewise made the relation between *machi*, *rewe*, territorial recovery, and state force nationally contentious.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and museums

Museums commonly present the *rewe*, *kultrun*, silver jewelry, sacred plants, and spirit possession as a coherent system. These displays increase public visibility but risk fixing the vocabulary of one region or practitioner as a universal Mapuche creed. Claims that machi knowledge is unchanged for “14,000 years” exceed what archaeology or textual history can demonstrate.

### Health care

Chile’s intercultural-health programs employ or cooperate with machi and herbal specialists. Modern centers use names such as “Filew Lawen.” This is a living institutional and commercial application of the term, not evidence of an ancient temple organization.

### Literature, art, and popular media

Modern fiction, visual art, tourism, games, and fantasy often transform the *machi* into a generic spellcaster and Filew into a “familiar” or guardian spirit. Such representations owe as much to global fantasy and “shamanism” discourse as to documented Mapuche ritual.

No reviewed source establishes a significant Filew-specific tradition in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, chaos magic, or historical European grimoires. Online lists that assimilate Filew to a universal occult “spirit guide” are modern inventions unless they cite identifiable Mapuche testimony.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Mapuche cultural and religious revitalization is well documented, but it is not accurately classified as neopaganism merely because it is non-Christian. No evidence located establishes a numerically significant, independent Filew-centered neopagan cult. Non-Mapuche appropriation of the *kultrun*, *rewe*, or “machi initiation” in commercial workshops should be distinguished from community authorization.

### Political symbolism

Machi have become symbols of territorial defense, Indigenous medicine, ecological protection, gender nonconformity, and resistance to the Chilean state. Bacigalupo shows that these public images can empower practitioners while also constraining them to perform “tradition” for activists, courts, journalists, or state agencies.

Filew remains relevant because the practitioner’s authority is not represented as self-invented: it is grounded in a call, ancestral transmission, dreams, illness, and obligations to the *rewe* and community.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### Is Filew one spirit or many?

- **Generic-one position:** Bacigalupo’s glossary calls Filew the generic ancestral spirit of all machi.
- **Individual-many position:** practitioners describe a particular ancestral *machi püllü*, sometimes a relative, who accompanies one *machi*.
- **Contextual position:** recent ethnography concludes that Filew, *machi püllü*, and Ngünechen may be identical in one utterance and distinct in another.
- **Capacity position:** Schindler’s evidence suggests *filew* can name a gift, virtue, vocation, or efficacy rather than a Western-style bounded being.

**Assessment:** the contextual position best fits the total evidence.

### Is Filew ancient?

The word is documented in 1765, but then denotes healers. A fully articulated Filew spirit is not securely documented until modern ethnography. It may preserve older concepts, but “unchanged pre-Columbian deity” is not demonstrable.

### Christian syncretism

Alonqueo’s Fileu as the “Spirit of Power and Wisdom of God-Ngenechen” resembles Christian language about the Holy Spirit. Missionaries explicitly equated Ngünechen with the Christian Father, Ngünechen Kushe with Mary, the Tren-Tren flood with the biblical Flood, and *ngillatun* sacrifice with Old Testament sacrifice.

Competing readings are:

1. missionaries imposed Christian categories;
2. Mapuche theologians used Christian vocabulary to explain an Indigenous structure;
3. centuries of interaction produced a genuinely new Mapuche-Christian religious form.

The evidence supports historical interaction; it does not permit a simple ruling that one side is “real” and the other “corruption.”

### Good spirit versus evil spirit

Foerster’s geometric opposition—Filew as positive possession, *wekufü* as negative possession—is analytically powerful. Schindler and Magnus Course caution that Mapuche beings’ morality can be relational and ambiguous. “Good Filew versus evil demon” is therefore a systematization, not a universally attested dogma.

### Human sacrifice

Animal sacrifice is documented in machi consecration and renewal. Historical and legendary claims of human sacrifice exist in broader Mapuche-Chilean literature, including debates over emergency sacrifices after disasters, but no reviewed primary evidence connects human sacrifice specifically to Filew.  
**Finding:** unsupported for this being.

### Temple prostitution and sexual initiation

No evidence supports temple prostitution. Colonial “sodomy” accusations against male *machi weye* arose in conquest and missionary polemic. Bacigalupo reconstructs a co-gendered ritual role and spiritual-bride relationship but does not justify turning every male machi into a modern sexual-identity category.  
**Finding:** temple prostitution is unattested; colonial sexual allegations require source criticism.

### Hallucinogens

Some modern accounts report *miyaya/chamico* or *palo de bruja* in ritual. Bacigalupo also records machi who reject such substances and characterize users as illegitimate practitioners unable to trance unaided. No evidence makes hallucinogens a necessary means of contacting Filew.

### Suppression

Suppression operated through mission demonology, territorial conquest, schooling, racial stigma, medical regulation, and state policing. It was not one dated edict abolishing Filew worship. The spirit persisted because practice was domestic, embodied, transmitted orally, and tied to living practitioners rather than monumental temples.

### Source limitations

- Colonial observers wrote through Christian demonology and imperial politics.
- Missionary dictionaries extracted words from relational situations.
- Early ethnologists often sought a single pantheon.
- Modern ethnographies are regionally concentrated.
- Individual machi can disagree without one being inauthentic.
- Sacred knowledge may be intentionally withheld.
- Chiloé, Huilliche, Lafkenche, Pewenche, Nagche, Wenteche, Neuquén, and Río Negro evidence cannot be flattened into one system.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Event in the evidentiary record |
|---|---|
| Before European contact | Archaeology documents long-term ceremonial landscapes, mounds, feasting, burial, and territorial memory; no inscription identifies Filew. |
| 1569–1589 | Ercilla publishes the three parts of *La Araucana*. Its magician Fitón is not a Filew attestation. |
| 1629 / 1673 | Núñez de Pineda experiences captivity among Mapuche in 1629 and writes *Cautiverio feliz* in 1673, documenting machi practice and colonial judgments. |
| Seventeenth century | Rosales records Mapuche ritual specialists through Catholic categories; Filew is not securely isolated. |
| **1765** | Febrés prints “*Fileu los machis curanderos*”: earliest secure attestation of the term. |
| 1777 | Havestadt publishes *Chilidúgú*, including machi songs collected earlier in Chile. |
| 1860s–1880s | Chilean and Argentine territorial campaigns transform Mapuche political, economic, and ritual life. |
| 1895–1897 | Lenz publishes *Estudios araucanos*, including vernacular songs and narratives. |
| 1910 | Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* calls certain people “*machi ó fileu*” and documents curing. |
| 1916 | Augusta publishes his major Mapudungun dictionary. |
| 1920 onward | Koessler-Ilg begins decades of collecting oral traditions in Neuquén. |
| 1930 | Moesbach publishes Pascual Coña’s mediated autobiographical and ethnographic testimony. |
| 1964 | Faron’s *Hawks of the Sun* analyzes ritual, ancestors, and social order. |
| 1970s | Grebe and collaborators popularize structural models of Mapuche cosmology. |
| 1979 | Alonqueo presents Fileu as Ngünechen’s Spirit of Power and Wisdom. |
| 1993/1995 | Foerster historicizes Mapuche religious representations and analyzes Filew possession in machi consecration. |
| 1995 | Bacigalupo emphasizes variation among individual machi cosmologies and publishes on renunciation of the calling. |
| 2004 | Bacigalupo analyzes colonial and modern struggles over male machi gender and sexuality. |
| 2007 | *Shamans of the Foye Tree* supplies the fullest Filew-centered ethnography: ancestral spirit, spirit marriage, head possession, *rewe*, and renewal. |
| 2009 | Francisca Linconao wins legal protection involving sacred and medicinal territory; the judgment applies ILO Convention 169. |
| 2010 | Bacigalupo publishes on machi spiritual power, resistance, and the Chilean state. |
| 2018 | Historical research synthesizes head symbolism and Filew possession as *longko luupan*. |
| 2020 | Machi Jorge Quilaqueo publicly describes Filew as gift and companion spirit arriving from *wenu mapu*. |
| 2020s | Imprisoned or prosecuted machi, access to *rewe*, territorial recovery, intercultural medicine, and state recognition keep machi spirituality in Chilean and Argentine politics. |
| 2024 | Ethnomusicological research on *ulutun* reiterates that Filew, *machi püllü*, and Ngünechen may merge or separate according to practitioner and context. |
| Present | Filew remains a living, variable category; there is no centralized doctrine or independent Filew cult. |
