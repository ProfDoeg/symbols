# Antü: Research Dossier

**Class:** solar being/deity or sacred celestial power in some Mapuche, Pewenche, Puelche, and Williche traditions; elsewhere simply the personified Sun, one member or manifestation of a divine family, or the ordinary Sun addressed as a ritual agent. The widespread modern classification “supreme Pillán and king of all Pillanes” is not securely attested in the early sources.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **1606**, in Luis de Valdivia’s *Arte y gramática general de la lengua que corre en todo el Reyno de Chile*. Valdivia records **antü** as the Mapudungun word for “sun/day” and as a lineage or *küga* name. This proves the word and its social-symbolic use, but does **not** establish a named supreme god with the elaborate biography circulated online. The earliest securely documented explicit divine or parental personifications belong chiefly to nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century ritual prayers and narratives.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Secure colonial-period linguistic or historical evidence.
- **[ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Recorded from Mapuche or related Indigenous informants, principally nineteenth–twentieth century.
- **[REGIONAL]** Local or subgroup tradition; not safely generalizable to all Mapuche communities.
- **[MISSIONARY]** Evidence mediated by Christian missionary collection, translation, or polemic.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern analytical synthesis, not an Indigenous text in itself.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Oral narrative as collected, without a claim that it is pre-Columbian or universal.
- **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION]** A recent harmonization of distinct beings or narratives.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/UNVERIFIED]** A claim presently lacking a traceable early or ethnographic source.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

**[EARLY]** Mapudungun *antü* means both **“sun” and “day.”** Febrés states in 1765, “*antù—el sol o día*.” The semantic range extends naturally into compounds referring to sunlight, weather, time of day, seasons, years, and solar direction. Consequently, not every occurrence of *antü* is a divine name.

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC; REGIONAL]** In recorded prayers and myths, the Sun can be addressed or narrated personally as **Chaw Antü**, “Father Sun,” or qualified through the paired titles **Antü Rey Fucha**, “Old Man/King Sun,” and **Antü Rey Kuse**, “Old Woman/Queen Sun.” In some Pewenche and Williche systems these form the senior pair of a divine family. In other central-Araucanian accounts the senior pair is called Wenumapu Fucha and Wenumapu Kuse, with solar and lunar associations rather than a simple identity.

The safest formulation is therefore:

> Antü is first the Mapudungun Sun/day; in multiple documented regional traditions the Sun is a sacred, animate, parental, or divine being, sometimes male, sometimes internally male-and-female, sometimes paired with the Moon, and sometimes incorporated into a fourfold divine family.

### Forms and orthographies

- **Antü** — common modern unified spelling.
- **Antú** — Spanish-influenced spelling and one used in some linguistic literature.
- **Antù, antú, antu** — colonial and modern typographic variants.
- **Aṉtü** — spelling occasionally encountered in modern popular literature; not a separate name.
- **Chaw Antü / Chau Antü** — “Father Sun.”
- **Antü Fucha / Antü Fücha** — “Old Man Sun,” “Ancient/elder male Sun.”
- **Antü Kuse / Antü Kushe / Antü Kuche** — “Old Woman Sun,” “Ancient/elder female Sun.”
- **Antü Rey Fucha** — “King/royal Old Man Sun.”
- **Antü Rey Kuse** — “Queen/royal Old Woman Sun.”
- **Mareupu Antü / Mari Epu Antü** — literally interpretable as “Twelve Suns/Days”; a difficult early expression connected by some writers with a “son of the Sun,” but the identification is disputed.
- **Antüñamku / Antipaiñanko and related personal names** — theophoric or solar personal names containing *antü*; they establish the prestige of the solar word, not necessarily worship of a god with a fixed biography.

### Etymology

**[EARLY]** Valdivia in 1606 already records *antü* in Mapudungun. Febrés (1765), Havestadt (1777), Augusta, and subsequent dictionaries consistently gloss it as “sun/day.”

**[DISPUTED SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Havestadt and later scholars noted the resemblance between Mapudungun *antü*, Quechua *inti* “sun,” and *anti* “east.” One modern line of argument treats Mapudungun *antü* as an Andean loan, ultimately perhaps from Puquina rather than Proto-Quechua. Contact is historically plausible: Inka expansion reached central Chile, and linguistic and calendrical parallels exist. Resemblance, however, does not by itself establish direction or date of borrowing. Claims that the similarity proves that Mapuche solar religion was imported wholesale from the Inka are reductive; the documented Mapuche material has its own grammar of duality, kinship, direction, and ritual.

### Classification through time

1. **[EARLY, 1606–1777]** Primarily a lexical Sun/day and lineage designation in dictionaries and grammars.
2. **[COLONIAL REPORT, disputed]** Object or direction of reverence according to some Spaniards, though “worship of the Sun” is often an outsider’s interpretation of eastward prayer.
3. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Father, mother, senior divine couple, celestial caretaker, creator, or source of light in different prayers and narratives.
4. **[REGIONAL]** One or two figures within the fourfold divine family.
5. **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION]** A singular male “sun god,” supreme Pillán, husband of Küyen, commander of a fixed pantheon.
6. **[MODERN INVENTION/UNVERIFIED]** A fully standardized Olympian-style king with a fixed genealogy, war narrative, symbols, and rank accepted by all Mapuche communities.

No precontact inscription naming Antü is known. Mapudungun had no precontact manuscript tradition comparable to Maya codices or Mesopotamian tablets; the record begins with colonial transcription and later ethnography.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest secure evidence

Luis de Valdivia’s *Arte y gramática* (Lima, 1606) records *antü* linguistically and in a list of *küga*, social or lineage names. Valdivia explains that some people bore group names meaning “suns,” as others bore names associated with animals or rivers. **What this establishes:** the word, its solar meaning, and its socially potent use by 1606. **What it does not:** that Antü was then a supreme deity, a Pillán king, or Küyen’s husband.

Valdivia’s 1621 Mapudungun sermon attacks worship of “false gods,” explicitly listing the Sun and Moon. This is evidence that missionaries feared or alleged reverence for celestial bodies. It is not a neutral Indigenous description: its purpose is Christian instruction, and the passage contrasts the Christian God with peoples who worship “some the Sun, others the Moon.”

### Early chroniclers

#### Alonso de Ercilla, *La Araucana* (parts published 1569, 1578, 1589)

**[EARLY LITERARY SOURCE]** Ercilla supplies descriptions of warfare, prophecy, sorcery, and Indigenous resistance but no secure biography of a named god Antü. His epic classicizes and dramatizes Mapuche life. Later mythographic claims should not be attributed to Ercilla without canto-and-line evidence.

#### Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, *Cautiverio feliz* (written 1673; first printed 1863)

**[EARLY COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** Based partly on his 1629 captivity, the work is important for ritual speech, daily practice, and Spanish-Mapuche religious comparison. It does not securely furnish the modern Antü–Peripillán cosmic war.

#### Diego de Rosales, *Historia general del Reino de Chile* (composed chiefly in the seventeenth century; printed 1877–78)

**[EARLY MISSIONARY ETHNOHISTORY]** Rosales discusses Mapuche ritual and supernatural beings through a Jesuit demonological and providential framework. His testimony must be separated from his identifications of Indigenous powers with the Devil. He is relevant to the history of Spanish claims about solar observance, but not a transparent transcription of a standardized Antü cult.

### Missionary dictionaries and grammars

- **Valdivia, 1606:** earliest secure lexical attestation.
- **Febrés, 1765:** *antù*, “sun or day”; extensive compounds.
- **Havestadt, 1777:** confirms the lexical field and comments on possible Andean correspondences.
- **Augusta, *Diccionario araucano-español* (1916):** preserves extensive compounds and examples.
- **Augusta and Fraunhäusl, *Lecturas araucanas* (1910):** bilingual narratives, prayers, ritual accounts, and linguistic commentary from named or localized informants.

The historical Mapudungun corpus provides digital access to these materials and should be preferred to modern unsourced pantheon charts.

### *Lecturas araucanas* and ritual prayer

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC, collected before 1910]** Augusta records a coastal *ngillatun* account from Pascual Segundo Painemilla Ñamcuchen. Sheep are killed; a right ear is cut off and smeared with blood; a ritual speaker raises his voice toward the sunrise/east. The prayer is directed to **Ngünemapun**, “Ruler/Master of the land,” and to a heavenly Father, later called **Rey Chau** and **Rey Kushe**. The text asks for good weather, rain, crops, health, and protection.

This is crucial negative evidence: orientation toward the sunrise and requests for *küme antü*—“good day/weather”—do not prove that the sacrificial recipient is a distinct god named Antü. The prayer explicitly names Ngünemapun and the royal senior pair instead.

### Pascual Coña / Ernesto Wilhelm de Moesbach

*Vida y costumbres de los indígenas araucanos en la segunda mitad del siglo XIX* was published bilingually in 1930 from the testimony of Pascual Coña, mediated and translated by the Capuchin Moesbach and reviewed by Lenz. It documents time-reckoning, ritual, *machi*, *machitun*, and *ngillatun*. Because Coña was a specific Budi-area Mapuche man who had prolonged contact with missions, his testimony is invaluable but neither precontact nor automatically universal.

### Lehmann-Nitsche

**[REGIONAL; Puelmapu]** Robert Lehmann-Nitsche’s “El diluvio según los araucanos de la Pampa” (1919) records a Chimpay-area account. It is evidence for eastern, Argentine Mapuche/Puelche narrative development, not a template for all Wallmapu.

### Koessler-Ilg

Bertha Koessler-Ilg’s *Cuentan los araucanos* was first published in 1954 from Neuquén oral material. It contains some of the fullest solar and lunar stories:

- “El Dios del cielo y sus hijos rebeldes”
- “Sobre Adán y Eva”
- “Por qué Antü, el padre sol, tiene grandes penas en el corazón”
- “Küyen, la madre luna, se venga de una maldición”
- stories of the marriage or estrangement of Sun and Moon.

**[FOLKLORE; REGIONAL; CHRISTIANLY ENTANGLED]** Titles involving God, Adam, and Eve show that some narratives already occupy a Mapuche-Christian narrative world. Their value is not diminished, but they cannot be projected unchanged into precontact antiquity.

### Major modern studies

- Louis C. Faron, *Hawks of the Sun* (1964): fieldwork chiefly in Cautín, 1952–54; structural-functionalist account of morality, ritual, ancestors, and celestial symbolism.
- María Ester Grebe, Sergio Pacheco, and José Segura, “Cosmovisión mapuche” (1972): influential account of dual and fourfold cosmological structures.
- Grebe, “El kultrún mapuche: un microcosmo simbólico” (1973): detailed analysis of the drum as cosmogram, while recognizing variation among drums and *machi*.
- Hans Gundermann, *Análisis estructural de los ritos mapuche: nguillatún y pintevún* (1981): particularly important for Pewenche prayer and the paired Antü Rey Fucha/Antü Rey Kuse.
- Rolf Foerster, *Introducción a la religiosidad mapuche* (1993/1995): historical analysis of missionary reporting, ritual, Ngünechen, and Christianization.
- Ana Mariella Bacigalupo’s work on *machi*: emphasizes historical transformation, gender plurality, colonial interaction, and the danger of reducing Mapuche beings to a fixed pantheon.
- Tom Dillehay, *Monuments, Empires, and Resistance* (2007): ceremonial landscapes, mounds, political aggregation, memory, and resistance; it does not provide archaeological proof of an ancient temple cult devoted specifically to Antü.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[SECURE LEXICAL CORE]**

- The visible Sun.
- Daylight and daytime.
- Weather expressed through solar/day terminology.
- Measurement of days, years, seasons, and age.
- The direction of sunrise, especially the ritual east.
- Warmth, ripening, agricultural recurrence, and calendrical change.

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC]**

- Parental care: *Chaw Antü*, Father Sun.
- Illumination and watching over dispersed people.
- Senior authority within a celestial family.
- Fertility and renewed growth through the returning annual cycle.
- In some narratives, creator or co-creator with Küyen.
- In some prayers, both male and female seniority.

There is no secure evidence for a single universal portfolio equivalent to a classical god “of war, kingship, justice, and fire.” Those domains belong to modern harmonizations.

### Gender and form

Antü cannot be assigned one universal gender.

- **[REGIONAL]** Chaw Antü is grammatically and relationally paternal.
- **[REGIONAL]** Antü Rey Fucha and Antü Rey Kuse form an elderly male/female pair.
- **[ETHNOGRAPHIC SYSTEMATIZATION]** Some classifications subdivide a celestial being into elder man, elder woman, young man, and young woman.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Narrative Antü is often a husband pursuing or quarrelling with Küyen.
- **[MODERN POPULARIZATION]** Antü is reduced to a single male solar god.

The ethnographic evidence therefore supports complementary gender, shifting personification, and internal plurality more strongly than a fixed masculine anthropomorphic body.

### The fourfold god

The frequently cited four persons are:

1. **Fucha/Fücha** — elder man.
2. **Kuse/Kushe** — elder woman.
3. **Weche Wentru** — young man.
4. **Ülcha Domo** — young woman.

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC; SCHOLARLY DEBATE]** They may be treated as one divine family, four heavenly powers, four aspects of Ngünechen, or a classificatory pattern applied to several celestial entities. Christian observers and later Indigenous Christian theologians sometimes compared the pattern to the Trinity, but the fourfold form is not simply a defective Christian Trinity. Its generational and gender complementarity correspond to broader Mapuche social and cosmological structures.

The precise equation “Antü = all four persons of Ngünechen” is not universal. In Gundermann’s Pewenche material, the senior solar pair occupies the highest position; in central Araucanian material the divine family bears Wenumapu titles; in modern accounts Ngünechen can encompass the family.

### Kultrun iconography

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC]** The *kultrun* is a *machi*’s shallow ritual drum and a microcosm. Its membrane may carry:

- a cross or quartered design;
- four directions;
- the terrestrial and celestial fields;
- Sun and Moon;
- stars or Wünelfe;
- family or gendered powers;
- routes of ritual movement.

Grebe’s study stresses that the drum symbolizes both cosmos and *machi*. **There is no single mandatory painted “Antü glyph.”** A circle, radiate disk, or solar placement may represent the Sun, but interpretation belongs to the particular maker or *machi*. Claims that every central cross is exclusively Antü, or that every drum preserves one prehistoric solar emblem, go beyond the evidence.

The fourfold pattern should not be read automatically as European heraldry or Christianity. Conversely, the possibility of historical Christian influence on particular painted crosses cannot be categorically excluded.

### Colors, metals, animals, weapons

- **Gold:** Solar-gold associations occur in Andean comparison and later literature, but a standardized sacred metal of Antü is not securely documented across Mapuche communities.
- **Yellow/red:** plausible modern solar color associations, not a securely ancient liturgical canon.
- **Hawk:** Faron’s title *Hawks of the Sun* derives from Mapuche social-moral symbolism; it does not establish a universal sacred animal belonging to a god Antü.
- **Weapons, throne, crown:** no securely early Antü iconographic corpus.
- **Sacred number four:** securely important in Mapuche ritual-cosmological organization, but it belongs to a larger system and is not Antü’s private number.

There are no securely identified precontact statues, temple reliefs, or coins depicting Antü.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical systems

#### Solar-lunar couple

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC; REGIONAL]** Antü and Küyen form a husband-and-wife or father-and-mother pair in Puelmapu and other recorded narratives. The visible separation of Sun and Moon, lunar markings, eclipses, and alternating illumination are explained through marital pursuit, conflict, injury, or division of labor.

#### Fourfold celestial family

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC; REGIONAL]** Elder male and female powers are paired with a young man and young woman. Depending on place and interpreter, these may be children, younger counterparts, or inseparable aspects rather than genealogically born offspring.

#### Ngünechen

**[LATE ETHNOGRAPHIC; SYNCRETISTIC]** Antü may be identified with, contained within, or distinguished from Ngünechen. Ngünechen’s prominence as a supreme creator is especially visible in nineteenth- and twentieth-century sources and is deeply entangled with missionization, colonial state pressure, and Indigenous reworking. It is unsafe to say simply that “Antü later became Ngünechen” everywhere.

### Every documented named interaction

The following list distinguishes source-bearing relations from modern combinations.

- **Küyen — spouse and alternating illuminator.** Antü and Mother Moon alternate in giving light and caring for people. Source: Neuquén traditions collected by Koessler-Ilg, published 1954. **[REGIONAL FOLKLORE; CHRISTIANLY ENTANGLED]**

- **Küyen — pursuit and estrangement.** Father Sun suffers because he cannot catch his wife Moon; when he approaches, she retreats and shows the bruised or marked face caused by a blow from him. Source: Koessler-Ilg collection, published 1954 and later editions. **[REGIONAL FOLKLORE]**

- **Küyen — marriage.** Stories collected in Neuquén describe the marriage of Sun and Moon. Source: Koessler-Ilg, twentieth-century collection. **[REGIONAL FOLKLORE]**

- **Küyen — co-creator or primordial divine pair.** Some Koessler-Ilg narratives place Sun and Moon before the conflict of Trengtreng and Kaykay and represent them as creators or caretakers. **[REGIONAL FOLKLORE; LATE RECORDED]**

- **Ngünechen — partial equation or incorporation.** Some twentieth-century accounts identify the solar-lunar family with the fourfold Ngünechen; others distinguish the names. Sources: Faron, Gundermann, Marileo, Foerster, and Bacigalupo. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC SYSTEMATIZATION; SYNCRETISTIC]**

- **Ngünemapun/Ngenemapun — ritual adjacency, not demonstrated identity.** In Augusta’s *ngillatun* text, prayer is oriented eastward and asks Ngünemapun for good weather. Antü is present lexically and directionally, but the text does not explicitly state “Antü is Ngünemapun.” **[ETHNOGRAPHIC; ABSENCE OF EXPLICIT INTERACTION]**

- **Wenumapu Fucha and Wenumapu Kuse — structural equivalents or alternative senior celestial pair.** Central Araucanian systems use these rather than Antü Rey Fucha/Kuse. The relation is comparative, not a surviving mythic meeting. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**

- **Weche Wentru and Ülcha Domo — younger members/aspects of the divine family.** They occur with the senior pair, but no universally attested birth episode from Antü is known. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC; GENEALOGICAL STATUS VARIABLE]**

- **Trengtreng and Kaykay — creatures or later actors in a world established by Sun and Moon.** Certain Neuquén accounts put the solar pair before the serpent flood struggle. A direct personal encounter with Antü is not consistently narrated. **[REGIONAL FOLKLORE]**

- **Mareupu Antü / “Son of the Sun” — disputed relation.** Missionary material mentions *Mareupu Antü* and Augusta published a story titled “El hijo del sol.” Scholars have disputed whether these refer to the same being or whether *mari epu antü* has been misunderstood. **[EARLY LINGUISTIC PUZZLE; LATER FOLKLORE]**

- **Peripillán — alleged celestial rival.** The elaborate story in which Antü fights Peripillán, buries defeated Pillanes beneath mountains, dims the Wangülen, and has a son transformed into Trengtreng is traceable in its currently circulating form chiefly to Alberto Trivero’s late-twentieth-century reconstruction and derivative websites. No colonial text or standard early ethnography located in this investigation documents this complete episode. **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION; ANCIENT STATUS UNVERIFIED]**

- **Elmapu/Elchen — alleged collaborator or subordinate creator.** Popular summaries place these figures within the reconstructed Pillán war. A securely dated primary narrative establishing Antü’s command over them was not found. **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION/UNVERIFIED]**

This record is strikingly sparse compared with the large pantheon biographies circulating online. Membership in the same cosmological vocabulary is not itself an interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Sun and Moon as caretakers

**[REGIONAL FOLKLORE; Koessler-Ilg, published 1954]**

A heavenly God or divine power is called Antü, while the maternal counterpart is called Moon. They alternate in illuminating the earth and looking after scattered human beings. Some elders, according to the collected testimony, said “Sun and Moon” rather than Rey Chau and a corresponding maternal title.

This narrative is significant because divine names and celestial bodies are not rigidly separated: the solar being, heavenly father, and supreme caretaker may overlap without forming one uniform theological definition.

### 5.2 The troubled marriage of Antü and Küyen

**[REGIONAL FOLKLORE; Neuquén, twentieth-century collection]**

Antü pursues Küyen across the sky but cannot overtake her. Her marked face is associated with a blow from her husband. Their separation explains why the celestial pair do not ordinarily travel together.

Variants change the emotional center:

- Antü suffers because he cannot reach her.
- Küyen retreats and displays her injured face.
- Küyen exacts revenge for a curse.
- Their movement is a marital pursuit rather than simple alternation.
- Modern children’s retellings soften the violence into a romance or have Ngünechen solemnize a wedding.

The online story “Antü and Küyen: a legend of love,” in which Ngünechen notices their love and marries them, is a modern pedagogical retelling, not an early attestation.

### 5.3 Creation before the serpent flood

**[REGIONAL FOLKLORE]**

In some Koessler-Ilg materials, the Sun and Moon belong to the primordial period and precede the struggle between Trengtreng and Kaykay. They may create or care for the world and its inhabitants. Other Mapuche flood accounts concentrate on the serpents and do not give Antü an active role. The absence of Antü in a version is not an omission to be repaired.

### 5.4 The son of the Sun

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC; Augusta, 1910; interpretation disputed]**

Augusta’s *Lecturas araucanas* includes “El hijo del sol.” Older scholarship attempted to connect this figure with *Mareupu Antü*, a term already surrounded by missionary speculation. The evidence does not support a stable universal genealogy. The title documents a solar-filial narrative as collected around 1910; it does not prove that “Son of Antü” was a pan-Mapuche god.

### 5.5 The fourfold solar family

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC; twentieth century]**

The Sun appears not merely as an individual but through elder-male and elder-female forms and, in wider divine-family systems, young male and young female forms. The “story” is often enacted in prayer and classification rather than told as a narrative with a birth and sequence of events. Its function is relational: senior/junior, male/female, sky/earth, east/west, and four directional divisions.

### 5.6 The war of Antü and Peripillán

**[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION; source-critical warning]**

The now-common account runs as follows: Antü, brightest and most powerful Pillán, chooses Küyen; jealous Wangülen and Pillanes rebel under Peripillán; Antü and Peripillán first punish their own rebellious sons; a second war follows; Antü casts defeated spirits to earth; their bodies form mountains and lakes; Wangülen tears become waters; Peripillán is buried beneath the highest mountains or Osorno; the sons become Trengtreng and Kaykay.

The narrative is coherent, attractive, and widely reproduced. Its evidentiary problem is that derivative pages cite Alberto Trivero’s 1999 *Trentrenfilú* project rather than an identifiable colonial manuscript or classic ethnographic transcription. Trivero appears to have woven separate Mapuche materials into a continuous cosmogony. Until an earlier informant text is produced, the complete war should be labeled a **late reconstruction**, not “the ancient Mapuche creation myth.”

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrated Antü temple system

No archaeological complex can presently be securely identified as a temple built specifically for Antü. Mapuche religious organization historically centered on community landscapes, *rewe*, *ngillatuwe*, cemeteries, mounds, mountains, waters, household rites, and the practices of ritual specialists rather than permanent urban temples with inscribed dedications.

Dillehay’s work establishes the antiquity and political importance of ceremonial landscapes and monumentality. It does not permit any particular mound or field to be labeled “Temple of Antü” without supporting evidence.

### Eastward orientation

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC]** In Augusta’s coastal *ngillatun* account, the officiant raises his voice toward *tripawe antü*, the place from which the Sun emerges—the east. Offerings and prayer address the ruler of the land and heavenly parental powers. Eastward orientation gives the Sun a ritual role, but it is not equivalent to exclusive solar worship.

### Ngillatun

The *ngillatun* or *nguillatun* is a communal rogation and renewal ceremony varying greatly by region. Documented elements can include:

- erection or use of a *rewe*;
- animal sacrifice;
- blood placed or displayed ritually;
- prayers for rain, favorable weather, crops, health, livestock, and communal continuity;
- circular movement and mounted *awün*;
- orientation toward the east;
- food, drink, kinship, and political assembly.

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Augusta’s text documents sheep sacrifice and an ear smeared with blood. The recipient is not explicitly “Antü alone.” Assertions that every *ngillatun* was historically a sacrifice to a solar god are unsupported.

### Machitun and the machi

The *machitun* is a healing complex led by a *machi*. The *kultrun* helps structure ritual movement, altered states, song, and cosmological communication. Solar designs may appear on the drum, but a *machi* is not thereby a “priest of Antü.” Bacigalupo’s work shows that *machi* practice is negotiated through spirits, dreams, gender, history, community, and political conditions.

### We Tripantü / Wiñoy Tripantü

**[ETHNOGRAPHIC; contemporary living practice]**

Names include:

- *We Tripantü* — commonly interpreted “new emergence/year.”
- *Wiñoy/Wiñol Tripantü* — “return of the emergence/year.”
- *Wiñon Antü* — “the Sun returns.”

It is celebrated around the austral winter solstice, though the date and calendrical method differ among communities. Detailed Pewenche fieldwork at Trapa documents four non-exclusive methods of determining it. The heliacal reappearance of the Pleiades (*Ngau*) is especially important; observers also attend to sunrise position, lunar reckoning, and day counts. Dates may fall between approximately 17/18 and 24 June depending on local horizon and convention.

Calling it simply “the birthday of the god Antü” is a modern simplification. The documented meaning is the renewal or return of the solar and seasonal cycle, inseparable from stars, Moon, water, cold, agriculture, and community.

### Human sacrifice

**[CONTESTED-MATERIAL FINDING]**

No source located documents a regular institutional cult of human sacrifice to Antü.

The 1960 Collileufu case, in which a child was killed after the Valdivia earthquake and tsunami, is a documented modern atrocity interpreted by participants or observers in relation to catastrophic water and ritual restoration. It is not evidence of a standing Antü cult and should not be retrojected into ordinary *ngillatun* practice.

Animal sacrifice is securely documented. Human sacrifice specifically to Antü is **not established**.

### “Temple prostitution”

No evidence was found for temple prostitution connected to Antü or to historical Mapuche religion. The category presupposes an urban temple institution absent from the evidence.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1600: archaeological horizon

Mapuche-speaking or ancestral southern-central Chilean societies left settlements, ceramics, burials, mounds, and ceremonial landscapes, but no readable precontact texts naming Antü. Solar alignments proposed for sites such as Antükura may illuminate calendrical observation, yet the recently advanced interpretation of Antükura as a thirteenth-century solar calendar remains a modern archaeoastronomical proposal, not proof of a temple or personalized god.

### 1606–1777: lexical and missionary record

Valdivia records *antü* as Sun/day and a *küga* designation. His later preaching condemns celestial worship. Febrés and Havestadt consolidate the lexicographic record. Missionaries translate Christian concepts through Mapudungun, creating a documentary archive that is both indispensable and interventionist.

### Seventeenth century: chroniclers and Christian classification

Spanish observers describe prayers, divination, Pillanes, celestial reverence, and ritual through categories such as idolatry, superstition, demon, and “worship.” Reports of “Sun worship” cannot be discarded, but their vocabulary does not show whether participants understood the visible Sun as a god, direction, ancestor, sign, animate person, or manifestation of a larger power.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: frontier transformation

Missionization, trade, warfare, treaties, livestock expansion, and movement across the Andes reshaped religious language. Christian “God,” heavenly fathers and mothers, Mapuche territorial powers, and solar-lunar beings increasingly entered the same translated discourse.

The Chilean occupation of Araucanía (principally 1861–83) and Argentine campaigns in Patagonia violently reduced Indigenous political autonomy, displaced communities, and transformed ritual geography. The effects include loss of land, disruption of ceremonial fields, mission schooling, and intensified Christianization.

### Late nineteenth–early twentieth century: the ethnographic archive

Lenz, Augusta, Fraunhäusl, Guevara, Moesbach/Coña, Lehmann-Nitsche, and others recorded oral texts and ceremonies. Their work preserved irreplaceable evidence but was shaped by transcription systems, translators, editorial selection, racial theory, folklore categories, and missionary goals.

This is the period in which explicit Father-Sun narratives and detailed solar-lunar stories become securely documentable.

### Mid-twentieth century: structural pantheons

Latcham, Faron, Grebe, and other scholars increasingly described a Mapuche “pantheon,” cosmology, dualism, and quadripartition. These models clarified real patterns but could over-systematize locally variable ritual vocabularies. Faron himself found the supremacy of a Christian-like Ngünechen inconsistent with much Mapuche belief.

### Late twentieth century: Indigenous revitalization and synthetic mythology

Mapuche intellectuals and communities articulated cosmology in education, political organization, intercultural health, poetry, and public ceremony. We Tripantü became increasingly visible in schools, municipalities, and Indigenous movements. Some presentations understandably standardize terminology for public communication.

At the same time, popular mythology projects assembled unified creation cycles. The Antü–Peripillán war belongs to this environment unless earlier documentation emerges.

### Present

Antü remains:

- the ordinary Mapudungun word for Sun/day;
- a living sacred referent in prayer, seasonal observance, teaching, art, and naming;
- Father Sun or a senior solar power in some communities;
- part of the fourfold divine-family interpretation in others;
- a symbol of Mapuche endurance and renewal;
- a highly simplified “sun god” in commercial and internet mythology.

There is no reliable census of exclusive Antü worshippers. Contemporary Mapuche religious life commonly combines Indigenous practice with Catholic, Evangelical, secular, or other commitments.

---

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Indigenous cultural and political renewal

Solar imagery, the return of the Sun, the four directions, and the *kultrun* have become prominent signs of Mapuche identity. This is not merely “invented tradition”: it draws on documented ritual and cosmology. Yet particular public graphics, festival scripts, and educational summaries may be newly standardized.

### Literature and oral-story publishing

Koessler-Ilg’s collections strongly influenced the Spanish-language image of Father Sun and Mother Moon. Children’s books and websites often transform conflictual marriage narratives into gentle celestial romances. These are **modern retellings**, even when based on genuine oral motifs.

### National and commercial use

*Antü* is widely used in Chile and Argentina as:

- a personal name;
- a name for schools, cultural organizations, tourism ventures, and products;
- a symbol of warmth, Indigeneity, and southern identity;
- the name of a Chilean “popular television” set produced during the Allende period.

These uses draw on the lexical meaning “Sun” more securely than on a specific divine biography.

### Occult and neopagan material

The dedicated search found no historical Antü tradition in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, classical grimoires, or established European occult lineages.

Modern eclectic pagan pages may invoke Antü as a solar god, but no evidence establishes an ancient initiatory cult, magical seal, planetary correspondence table, canonical incense, tarot attribution, or grimoire. Such correspondences are **modern inventions** unless their creators explicitly present them as new practice.

### Political appropriation

Solar and *kultrun* imagery appears in Indigenous rights movements and state multicultural programs. That is modern political symbolism grounded in living culture, not evidence that Antü was historically a centralized national god. No well-documented extremist Antü cult or systematic far-right appropriation was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was there “Sun worship”?

**Position A:** Colonial accusations, eastward ritual orientation, offerings raised toward sunrise, solar names, and Father-Sun prayers together indicate solar worship.

**Position B:** The visible Sun is a direction, temporal agent, and manifestation within a relational cosmos; prayers may actually address Ngünemapun, heavenly parents, ancestors, or a divine family. Spanish authors imposed an “idolatry of the Sun” category influenced by knowledge of the Inka.

**Assessment:** Sacred attention to the Sun is well documented. A uniform, exclusive Antü cult is not.

### Is Antü borrowed from Quechua *Inti*?

**For:** close phonological and semantic resemblance; Inka-Mapuche contact; other Andean loans; Havestadt’s early observation.

**Against/cautions:** direction and chronology are difficult; the proposed ultimate Puquina origin complicates a simple Quechua-to-Mapudungun equation; religious systems cannot be derived from one lexical resemblance.

**Assessment:** An Andean contact etymology is plausible and widely discussed, not finally demonstrated in every historical detail.

### Is Antü male?

Father-Sun narratives support masculinity. Antü Rey Fucha and Antü Rey Kuse demonstrate a paired male/female solar expression. Fourfold theology further resists singular gender. The answer is regional and contextual.

### Is Antü Ngünechen?

Some modern accounts merge them; others treat Antü as a manifestation, title, or senior member of a divine family; still others distinguish them. The late prominence of Ngünechen and its Christian entanglements argue against timeless equation.

### Is Antü the supreme Pillán?

The claim is common online, but the early dictionaries and classic prayer texts do not securely establish it. Pillán historically has ancestral, thunderous, volcanic, and powerful-spirit dimensions, themselves distorted by missionary translation as “devil.” The fixed rank “most powerful Pillán” belongs chiefly to modern synthetic mythology.

### The kultrun’s “painted Sun”

Grebe securely establishes the *kultrun* as a multivalent microcosm, and ethnography documents solar and lunar motifs. The dispute concerns exclusivity: the design is not a universal pictographic code in which one form always means Antü.

### Chiloé and Huilliche material

Chiloé’s mythology is a colonial and postcolonial synthesis of Indigenous—especially Williche and Chono-related—Spanish, maritime, and Catholic traditions. Cavada’s *Chiloé y los chilotes* (1914), Vicuña Cifuentes, Plath, and later collectors preserve this corpus. Antü is not a dominant named actor in the canonical Chilote cycles of the Caleuche, Trauco, Pincoya, Camahueto, or Recta Provincia. Solar vocabulary and Williche sacred cosmology remain relevant, but importing the modern Peripillán war into Chiloé as its canonical pantheon would be unjustified.

### State of the evidence

The archive consists of:

- colonial grammars, dictionaries, sermons, and chronicles;
- nineteenth- and twentieth-century manuscripts and printed bilingual texts;
- ethnographic field notes and books;
- oral traditions that continued changing after collection;
- ritual objects, especially *kultrun*, textiles, *rewe*, and ceremonial landscapes;
- no precontact readable inscription bearing Antü’s name;
- no securely identified ancient statue or temple dedicated exclusively to Antü.

## 11. Findings from the dedicated contested-material pass

- **Etymology:** an *antü–inti–anti* connection is plausible but disputed in origin and direction.
- **Origin:** no evidence permits a date for the “birth” of Antü as a divine person; the word is documented from 1606.
- **Syncretism:** strong and demonstrable, especially in equations among heavenly Father, Ngünechen, Sun, Moon, and Christian God.
- **Human sacrifice:** no institutional Antü cult of human sacrifice is documented.
- **Animal sacrifice:** securely documented in *ngillatun*, though not necessarily to Antü alone.
- **Suppression:** Spanish missionization and nineteenth-century state conquest transformed Mapuche ritual, landholding, and divine terminology.
- **Occultism:** no historical grimoire or Western esoteric Antü tradition was found.
- **Neopaganism:** scattered modern eclectic invocation exists, but no ancient initiatory continuity is established.
- **Peripillán war:** complete form appears to be modern systematization unless an earlier primary text is produced.
- **Chiloé pantheon:** Antü is not securely a principal character of the best-known Chilote narrative corpus.
- **Iconography:** solar images occur on some *kultrun*, but there is no universal standardized portrait of Antü.
- **“Supreme Pillán”:** widespread modern claim; early attestation not established.
- **Absence of evidence:** no temple inscriptions, canonical scripture, ancient statue, priesthood exclusively devoted to Antü, fixed feast solely for Antü, sacred weapon, or universally accepted genealogy is documented.
