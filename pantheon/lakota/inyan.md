# Inyan: Research Dossier

**Class:** primordial *wakȟáŋ* (“sacred, mysterious, spiritually potent”) being or divine power embodied in rock and stone; in James R. Walker’s Oglala synthesis, a male “Superior God.” “God” is an approximate English classification, not an exact equivalent of *wakȟáŋ*.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the ordinary Dakota word *inyan* “stone/rock” appears in Stephen Return Riggs’s Dakota lexicography, published in 1852. The earliest unambiguous documentary description of **Tunkan/Inyan as a named sacred or divine being** is James W. Lynd’s account, collected among Dakota people before his death in 1862 and published in 1865: western Dakota groups, he wrote, chiefly addressed Tunkan/Inyan, “the Stone God,” said to dwell in stones and to be the oldest god. The elaborate cosmogony in which Inyan opens his veins to create Maka and the waters is not securely documented until James R. Walker’s Oglala materials, collected principally in 1896–1914 and later fashioned into his literary cycle.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Nineteenth-century linguistic, ethnographic, or material evidence predating Walker’s synthesis.
- **[OGLALA TESTIMONY]** Material traceable to named Oglala consultants in Walker’s papers, 1896–1914.
- **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]** Walker’s organized theology or literary narrative, built from Oglala testimony but shaped, joined, translated, or expanded by him.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** Dakota-, Lakota-, or community-specific material not safely generalizable to all Oceti Sakowin peoples.
- **[LATER TRADITION]** Twentieth- or twenty-first-century Indigenous testimony or practice.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** An analytical conclusion rather than an Indigenous source’s explicit formulation.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/UNVERIFIED]** A recent assertion for which no older documentary basis has been established.

“Sioux” is retained only in historical titles or quotations. Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota traditions are related but not interchangeable.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Lynd distinguished the sacred being **Tunkan** from the physical word **Inyan**, yet presented the two together: “Tunkan (Inyan), the Stone God.” He reported that this power dwelt in stones or rocks, was regarded as oldest because stone was hardest, received everyday devotion, and was especially important among Titonwan/Teton and other western groups. His language—“god,” “worship,” “lingam,” and comparisons with Hindu religion—is that of a nineteenth-century Euro-American observer and must not be mistaken for Indigenous technical vocabulary. Lynd’s own evidence nevertheless securely establishes a named sacred stone power before Walker.

**[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]** Walker’s cosmology makes **Inyan, Rock**, the first being and first of four *Wakáŋ Akáŋtu*, “Superior” or “Above” sacred beings. In Walker’s literary cycle, Inyan is initially living, soft, primordial matter containing all powers in his blood. By creating Earth and water he becomes shrunken, hard, and largely powerless: ordinary rock is thus the exhausted body or material continuation of the primordial being.

### Names

- **Íŋyaŋ / Inyan / Inyan** — Lakota “stone, rock.” Modern Standard Lakota orthography writes **Íŋyaŋ**. Earlier sources omit nasalization and diacritics.
- **Iŋyaŋ / Įyan** — Dakota or older linguistic spellings vary with dialect and transcription.
- **Tȟuŋkáŋ / Tunkan / Toŋkan / Toon-kan** — sacred-language or reverential designation for Stone, often glossed “Grandfather,” “Venerable One,” or the spirit residing in stone. Walker’s consultants treated *Tunkan* as a sacred name; ordinary stone was *inyan*.
- **Tȟuŋkášila / Tunkashila**, “Grandfather” — a broader reverential address used for sacred powers, ancestors, and sweat-lodge stones. It is not exclusively an epithet of the cosmogonic Inyan.
- **Rock; Stone; Stone God** — English ethnographic translations. “Stone God” is useful historically but risks implying a bounded Greco-Roman-style deity.
- **Grandfather of all things** — explicitly reported in later nineteenth-century summaries and compatible with Walker’s claim that Inyan precedes the other beings.
- **First of the Superior Gods** — specifically Walker’s organized Oglala theology, not a securely pan-Lakota creed.

The word is not demonstrably related to the English personal name *Inyan* or to unrelated names elsewhere. No responsible linguistic source derives Íŋyaŋ from Sanskrit, Persian, or Indo-European roots.

### Classification and its changes

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Lynd described Tunkan/Inyan both as a being dwelling “in” stones and as the sacred stone itself. This instability is not necessarily confusion: Lakota ontology does not require the rigid Western separation of symbol, material object, indwelling spirit, and divine person.

**[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]** Walker regularized Inyan into a masculine divine person with genealogy, intentional speech, and a place in a fourfold hierarchy. His frequent capitalization and word “God” make the system look more like a European pantheon than the earlier evidence warrants.

**[LATER TRADITION]** In contemporary teaching, *Íŋyaŋ* may mean rock as a living relative; *Tȟuŋkáŋ* or “Grandfather stones” may designate the heated stones of *inípi*. Speakers do not necessarily mean that every stone is a miniature personification of Walker’s creator.

### No secure foreign equation

No ancient *interpretatio graeca*, Roman equation, or historically Indigenous identification with a foreign deity exists. Lynd’s “Lingam” analogy is **[COLONIAL COMPARATIVISM]**, not an attested Lakota–Hindu syncretism. Modern equations of Inyan with Gaia, Saturn, a “crystal elemental,” or a generic neopagan Earth god are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless explicitly presented as new personal practice.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **Stephen Return Riggs, *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language* (1852).**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED—LINGUISTIC]** A missionary-produced Dakota grammar and lexicon based on approximately eighteen years of language work. It establishes the word for stone but, by itself, does not establish Walker’s creation narrative. The printed book is the physical witness; its limitations include missionary orthography and a mainly eastern-Dakota base.

2. **James W. Lynd, “The Religion of the Dakotas” (information gathered before 1862; published 1865).**  
   **[EARLIEST SECURE DIVINE ATTESTATION]** Lynd says western Dakota people gave principal devotion to Tunkan/Inyan; describes a round, head-sized stone painted red and ornamented with dyed swan’s down; calls Tunkan the oldest god; and says everyday prayers and sacrifices were directed to it. He also records diversity: people attached themselves to different sacred powers, so there was no uniform dogmatic pantheon. His evolutionist and Christian judgments are not evidence from consultants.

3. **James Owen Dorsey, *A Study of Siouan Cults* (1894).**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Dorsey reproduced and compared Lynd, Riggs, and Dakota-language testimony including Bushotter’s. He reports mysterious pebbles that found lost property, exposed horse thieves, moved, delivered messages, and were painted, wrapped, and furnished with medicine bundles. Dorsey repeats the heading “Tunkan or Inyan, the Stone God or Lingam”; “lingam” remains an observer’s analogy.

4. **James R. Walker, field papers (Pine Ridge, 1896–1914), and “The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala” (1917).**  
   **[OGLALA TESTIMONY plus WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]** Walker, physician at Pine Ridge, worked with approximately thirty-eight consultants. The most consequential included George Sword/Miwakan, Bad Wound, Finger, Ringing Shield, Short Bull, No Flesh, and Thomas Tyon. Sword and Tyon were especially influential; Sword wrote extensive Lakota texts. Walker did not command Lakota well enough to eliminate mediation by translators.

5. **Frances Densmore, *Teton Sioux Music* (1918).**  
   **[EARLY TWENTIETH-CENTURY TESTIMONY]** Based on recordings and testimony from Teton/Lakota singers. Densmore reports that sacred stones speak in dreams because, among substances in the earth, they frequently appear and communicate with people. Her corpus documents songs, ritual practice, and Indigenous explanations, but does not independently attest the whole vein-opening cosmogony.

### Walker’s posthumously published corpus

- ***Lakota Belief and Ritual* (edited by Raymond J. DeMallie and Elaine Jahner, 1980).** Preserves raw narratives, consultant biographies, translations, ritual descriptions, and Walker’s syntheses. It is more valuable for source criticism than a seamless summary.
- ***Lakota Society* (edited by DeMallie, 1982).** Context for kinship, camps, offices, ritual societies, and the historical conditions of Walker’s consultants.
- ***Lakota Myth* (edited by Elaine Jahner, first issued 1983; later edition 2006).** Prints Walker’s planned literary cycle. Jahner explicitly identifies it as a literary construction. The sentence “Inyan had no beginning, for he was when there was no other” belongs to this cycle, not to an ancient manuscript or a single verbatim Oglala performance.

The surviving evidence therefore has layers:

> Oglala oral narration → Lakota writing or interpreter’s rendering → Walker’s notes and translations → Walker’s literary synthesis → posthumous editorial publication.

The cycle is indispensable evidence for early-reservation Oglala intellectual history, but its polished continuity cannot be projected unchanged into a timeless “Sioux creation myth.”

### Other major corpora

- **Ella Cara Deloria, *Dakota Texts* (1932).** Dakota- and Lakota-language narratives with close and free translations. Its Stone Boy material is evidence for a distinct cycle, not proof that Stone Boy and primordial Inyan were originally identical.
- **John G. Neihardt, *Black Elk Speaks* (1932).** Black Elk’s life and great vision as interpreted, selected, and literary shaped by Neihardt. It is crucial for Ghost Dance and Oglala sacred history but not a primary source for Walker’s Inyan cosmogony.
- **Raymond J. DeMallie, *The Sixth Grandfather* (1984/1985).** Publishes the 1931 and 1944 interview transcripts and demonstrates Neihardt’s omissions, additions, reframing, and construction of Black Elk as an aged tragic spokesman. It is the necessary control on *Black Elk Speaks*.
- **Black Elk/Joseph Epes Brown, *The Sacred Pipe* (1953).** An account of seven Oglala rites based on Brown’s 1947–49 work with Black Elk. It places heated stones and water centrally in *inípi*, but its tidy set of “seven rites” is a late systematization as well as living Oglala teaching.
- **Lame Deer and Richard Erdoes, *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972).** A collaborative and editorially mediated modern Lakota voice.
- **William K. Powers, *Oglala Religion* (1975/1977).** A major ethnography stressing contemporary practice and warning against treating belief as a fixed creed.
- **DeMallie and Douglas Parks, eds., *Sioux Indian Religion* (1987).** Comparative historical and ethnographic essays.
- **D. M. Dooling, *The Sons of the Wind* (1984).** A modern collection and retelling of sacred stories; valuable for circulation history but later than the Walker papers.
- **Winter counts.** Pictographic annual records are vital for Lakota political and social chronology. No examined winter-count evidence independently dates the primordial Inyan creation narrative.
- **Arthur Amiotte.** His visual and written work interprets Lakota sacred history from within a modern Lakota artistic tradition. It is reception and continuing tradition, not a substitute for nineteenth-century textual witnesses.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]**

- Stones and rocks as loci of sacred power.
- Stability, age, hardness, endurance, and primordiality.
- Divination: some sacred stones locate lost or stolen things, identify thieves, move, or communicate.
- Prayer, sacrifice, and personal ritual assistance.
- Purification, because stones are essential to the steam lodge.

**[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]**

- Primordial matter.
- Source of Earth and terrestrial water.
- Ultimate material ancestor of the world.
- First bearer of undifferentiated divine powers.
- Support of Maka, although progressively subordinate to Skan after creation.

“Inyan governs geology,” “rules crystals,” or “controls the earth element” is modern occult vocabulary, not language found in the early corpus.

### Form

In Walker’s cycle Inyan first exists as living, soft, potentially anthropomorphic matter with blue blood. After bleeding, he shrinks and hardens into rock. The story explains both water’s blue appearance and the solidity of stone.

Lynd’s cult object was round and roughly head-sized, painted red, adorned with colored swan’s down, and approached through prayer. Dorsey also records white or translucent mysterious pebbles and ordinary-looking sacred stones. These are not known portraits with a standardized face or body.

There is no securely identified pre-contact statue of Inyan. Nor is there a canonical color image, weapon, costume, sacred number, weekday, or planet. Blue belongs to the blood/waters in Walker’s cosmogony; red belongs to historically documented stone offerings and ritual paint. Neither constitutes a universal heraldic color.

### Inípi stones

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED and CONTINUING PRACTICE]** Stones heated in the fire and carried into the lodge are addressed reverentially as ancient relatives or Grandfathers. Water poured upon them produces cleansing steam. Black Elk’s account interprets the lodge as the universe and the heated stones as ancient and sacred; later practitioners offer related but non-identical explanations.

The evidence establishes a profound association between stone and *inípi*. It does **not** establish that every historical participant consciously reenacted Walker’s detailed story of Inyan opening his veins. That linkage may be taught in some communities, but ritual meanings are plural and often restricted.

### Sacred stones and bundles

Sacred stones could be individually dreamed, painted, wrapped, hung with medicines, feasted, and consulted. In Yuwipi-related traditions, stones and the moving power *Tákú Škaŋškáŋ* could be connected with revelation and finding hidden things. A sacred pebble, a sweat-lodge stone, Tȟuŋkáŋ, and Walker’s primordial Inyan overlap semantically but should not automatically be collapsed into one object or personality.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Genealogical schemes

**Walker literary cycle [WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]:**

- Inyan has no parent and “had no beginning.”
- Maka is made from Inyan’s own substance and receives the spirit Maka-akan.
- Water is Inyan’s blood.
- The powers leaving the water form the blue dome and Skan.
- Subsequent beings arise through decisions of Inyan, Skan, or the growing divine order.
- In portions of Walker’s system Inyan is connected genealogically with Wakinyan and Iktomi.

This is not the only Lakota origin structure. Emergence narratives centered on Wind Cave, Buffalo People, Tokahe, or subterranean humanity do not necessarily begin with an anthropomorphic Inyan. Black Elk’s ritual theology centers Wakan Tanka and White Buffalo Calf Woman rather than reciting Walker’s genealogy.

### Every documented interaction with another named being

The following list separates actual narrative interaction from mere membership in the same cosmological scheme.

- **Maka / Maka-akan — creation from Inyan’s substance.** Inyan spreads part of himself as the earth-disc and gives it a spirit. Later Maka complains of darkness, cold, heat, or dependence. *Walker, Oglala papers 1896–1914; literary cycle published in* Lakota Myth. **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Skan / Škaŋ — emergence from Inyan’s released powers.** Inyan’s powers cannot remain in water; they assume the blue-dome form and identify themselves as Skan. Skan thereafter becomes judge and active supreme power, effectively displacing the exhausted Inyan. *Walker,* Lakota Myth. **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Han / Haŋ, Darkness — primordial coexistence and later displacement.** Darkness surrounds Inyan before the ordered world; subsequent rulings confine or alternate Darkness after disputes involving Maka. *Walker,* Lakota Myth. **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION; variant details within Walker materials]**
- **Anpo / Aŋpétu, Dawn or Daylight — created in response to Maka’s complaint.** Some Walker versions assign the act to Inyan, others make the ordering chiefly Skan’s work. *Walker,* Lakota Myth. **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION/INTERNAL VARIANT]**
- **Wi / Aŋpétu Wi, Sun — brought forth to give Maka heat and light.** Agency varies between Inyan and Skan in summaries and versions. *Walker,* Lakota Myth. **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION/INTERNAL VARIANT]**
- **Hanwi / Haŋwí, Moon — part of the later regulation of light and darkness.** Inyan appears in some modern retellings as direct creator, but the securely accessible Walker text gives Skan the dominant ordering function. **[LATE RETELLING unless tied to a specific Walker passage]**
- **Wakinyan / Wakíŋyaŋ, Thunder-being — genealogically descended from or created by Inyan in Walker’s organized pantheon.** No independent early episode of personal meeting is securely established here. **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Iktomi / Iktómi — presented in Walker-derived genealogies as Inyan’s son or descendant.** No secure early, separately recorded encounter between them has been found. **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Unk / Uŋk — Earth-related female power or contention arising in the developed cycle.** Some summaries make her offspring of Maka and Inyan or a being produced through Maka’s dissatisfaction; the variants are unstable and should not be harmonized. *Walker cycle.* **[WALKER SYSTEMATIZATION/VARIANT]**
- **Tate / Tȟaté, Wind — belongs to the later divine genealogy.** No securely isolated early Inyan–Tate encounter is established beyond genealogical placement in Walker’s scheme. **[ABSENCE OF DIRECT INTERACTION]**
- **Wakan Tanka / Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka — relationship disputed.** Walker at times treats the sixteen named powers collectively as Wakan Tanka; popular retellings instead make Wakan Tanka a single creator who first makes Inyan. Lynd treated Wakan Tanka and Tunkan as distinguishable. The second model must not be projected backward as uncontested. **[HISTORICAL VARIATION and CHRISTIAN-ERA SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Taku Skanskan / Tákú Škaŋškáŋ — sacred stones are described as friends or associates of the moving power in divinatory practice.** Ringing Shield testimony in Walker materials; compare Densmore and Dorsey. **[OGLALA TESTIMONY]**
- **Stone Boy / Íŋyaŋ Hokšíla — names share “stone,” but identity with primordial Inyan is not established.** Stone Boy is a culture hero born after a woman swallows a stone. Treating him as an incarnation or son of the cosmogonic Inyan without a particular source is **[MODERN CONFLATION]**.
- **White Buffalo Calf Woman / Ptesáŋwiŋ — no direct interaction with Inyan is documented in the examined primary corpus.**
- **Unktehi / Uŋktéȟi — both occur in Siouan religious records, but no direct Inyan–Unktehi episode has been securely identified.**
- **Wohpe — no direct interaction securely attested.**
- **Wazi, Wakanka, and the Four Winds — no direct interaction with Inyan securely attested outside Walker’s generalized genealogy.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The sacrifice of Inyan

**Earliest full witness:** Walker papers, 1896–1914; polished form in *Lakota Myth*.

Before time or the differentiated world, Inyan exists alone within darkness. He possesses powers in his blood and desires something over which or toward which those powers can operate. He takes substance from himself and spreads it as a great disk, Maka. He gives Maka a spirit, Maka-akan.

The act costs too much. Inyan’s veins open; his blue blood flows out and becomes the waters upon Earth. Emptied, he shrinks and hardens. Matter—Inyan, Maka, and water—cannot retain the mobile sacred powers; they separate, rise, and become the blue dome and Skan. Skan declares itself the source of energy or motion and becomes the effective ruler and judge. Inyan remains first in age but last or diminished in active power.

The myth thus explains:

- why stone is ancient, hard, and apparently inert;
- why water and sky appear blue;
- why Earth and rock are physically joined;
- why creative self-expenditure can produce both life and loss of power;
- why Skan, though later, governs motion and judgment;
- why rock remains sacred despite its stillness.

**Source-critical judgment:** The narrative’s components may preserve older Oglala teachings, especially stone’s primacy and its relationship to water, power, and Earth. Its continuous cosmogonic architecture, explicit hierarchy, and quasi-philosophical matter/spirit distinction are inseparable from Walker’s literary labor. Calling it simply “the ancient Lakota creation myth” overstates the evidence.

### The complaints of Maka and ordering of the cosmos

In the Walker cycle Maka complains that the world is dark and cold. Dawn/daylight and Sun are produced. When heat or light becomes excessive, Darkness and the alternation of day and night are established. In another strand, Maka wants independence from Inyan or taunts his depleted condition; Skan judges that Earth must remain bound to Rock, explaining why stone lies within the ground.

The variants disagree over whether Inyan or Skan creates particular lights and who initiates the dispute. They should remain separate. Popular web retellings often splice them into one sequence and silently transfer all agency to Inyan or Wakan Tanka.

### Stone Boy

**[REGIONAL VARIANT; DISTINCT CYCLE]** In Deloria and other Dakota/Lakota collections, a woman swallows or is impregnated by a stone and gives birth to Stone Boy. He grows rapidly and performs heroic or world-ordering deeds. Versions differ sharply: in some he is a culture hero; modern summaries sometimes credit him with creating directions, Earth, or the sweat lodge.

The shared stone vocabulary and generative imagery invite comparison with Inyan, but no early source proves that Stone Boy is primordial Inyan in human form. Their merger is a scholarly or popular inference.

### Emergence and Buffalo-People narratives

Lakota emergence traditions connect humanity or the Buffalo Nation with a subterranean world and the Black Hills/Wind Cave region. Tokahe may lead the people outward. These narratives can coexist with, supplement, or stand apart from Walker’s Inyan cosmogony. A single canonical sequence—Inyan creates Earth, then humans emerge at Wind Cave—is a modern harmonization unless a named narrator explicitly combines them.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No temple institution

There are no documented temples, permanent priestly estates, idols in monumental sanctuaries, or temple economies devoted exclusively to Inyan. Sacred power was engaged through household, camp, lodge, bundle, dream, vision, feast, and specialist practice. “Cult of Inyan” is therefore an analytical shorthand.

### Stone devotion

Lynd reports round stones painted red, decorated with swan’s down, and addressed in prayer. Sacred objects could receive cloth, skins, food, smoke, paint, and feasts. Dorsey’s compilation records privately owned mysterious pebbles used as helpers and oracles.

This is material cult evidence in the ethnographic sense, although few surviving museum objects can be securely identified as a particular consultant’s Tunkan without collection documentation. Sacred bundles and ceremonial knowledge also present ethical restrictions on display and disclosure.

### Divination and Yuwipi

Certain dreamers of Tȟuŋkáŋ or Taku Skanskan performed binding and singing rites. Ringing Shield told Walker that Tunkan was a stone that fell from the sky, knew secrets, and could disclose lost or stolen property; it was a friend of Taku Skanskan. This is a regional Oglala ritual statement, not necessarily the origin of primordial Inyan. “Fell from the sky” may describe a particular sacred stone or class of stones, possibly including meteorites, but identifying all Tunkan stones as meteorites is unproved.

### Inípi

Heated stones are placed in the central pit; water, prayer, songs, medicines, and steam effect purification. The lodge precedes vision-seeking, Sun Dance, and other solemn undertakings. The stones’ age, endurance, heat, and transformation make them central sacred participants, not mere equipment.

There is no fixed archaeological “temple of Inyan.” Sweat lodges leave limited material traces, and continuity cannot normally be assigned to this named being from archaeology alone.

### Sacrifice

Offerings to sacred powers included food, tobacco, cloth, paint, animal portions, fasting, vows, and bodily suffering. Sun Dance piercing is self-offering, not homicide.

**Contested finding:** Lynd explicitly says deliberate religious killing of prisoners was not Dakota custom and that Dakota people viewed a Pawnee captive-sacrifice story with horror. No reliable source found documents human sacrifice to Inyan. Claims that Inyan’s own cosmic bleeding proves an institution of human blood sacrifice confuse mythic divine self-expenditure with human ritual practice.

No evidence supports “temple prostitution,” sexual rites for Inyan, or a dedicated violent priesthood.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1850: oral and material history

Rock, sacred stones, steam purification, and stone-associated powers necessarily predate their written documentation, but no surviving dated Indigenous manuscript or artifact securely names the primordial Inyan before the nineteenth century. Claims of a precisely datable “thousands-year-old Inyan myth” exceed the evidence.

### 1852–1894: first documentary record

Riggs fixed the vocabulary in print. Lynd documented Tunkan/Inyan’s religious importance among western Dakota communities and the diversity of sacred allegiances. Dorsey assembled a comparative Siouan account, adding Dakota-language testimony about mobile and oracular stones.

This era’s evidence was recorded amid missionization, warfare, land loss, forced reservation settlement, epidemics, and translation into Christian categories. It nevertheless precedes Walker’s pantheon and proves that sacred Stone was not his invention.

### 1877–1890: land seizure and ceremonial repression

The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty recognized the Black Hills within Lakota territory; after the 1874 gold discovery, the United States failed to exclude intruders and took the Hills in 1877. Because Pahá Sápa is a sacred landscape, this was religious as well as territorial dispossession.

In 1883 the federal Code of Indian Offenses declared the Sun Dance and related ceremonies punishable by ration withholding or imprisonment. The suppression affected the ceremonial world in which stones, purification, vows, and sacred powers operated, even though the order did not name Inyan individually.

The Ghost Dance reached Pine Ridge and Rosebud in 1890. Officials treated it as a threat; agent Daniel Royer sought military intervention. On December 29 the Seventh Cavalry killed hundreds of Lakota men, women, and children at Wounded Knee. Inyan is not the named god of the Ghost Dance, so presenting the massacre as an episode in an “Inyan cult” would be false. It belongs here because it catastrophically altered the transmission and public practice of Lakota religion.

### 1896–1917: Walker’s collection

Walker began work at Pine Ridge in 1896. At first holy men resisted disclosure. George Sword reportedly argued that knowledge should be recorded before its holders died. Sword had also become a Christian deacon, illustrating that Christian affiliation and Lakota intellectual authority were not mutually exclusive.

Walker converted a heterogeneous archive into an ordered theology. By 1915 both Sword and Tyon, his key consultants, were dead. The first published synthesis appeared in 1917.

### 1918–1953: parallel documentation

Densmore documented songs and sacred-stone explanations. Deloria preserved narratives in Dakota/Lakota with translations. Neihardt transformed Black Elk’s testimony into a literary spiritual autobiography. Brown later recorded Black Elk’s seven-rite account, including *inípi*. These works expanded public knowledge but did not independently confirm every part of Walker’s Inyan narrative.

### 1920s–1978: revival under restriction

Sun Dances resumed in some places before formal policy liberalization; piercing remained contested or prohibited longer. Commissioner John Collier’s 1934 reforms reduced federal administrative hostility, though they did not erase local repression or Christian opposition. Public and private continuities varied by reservation.

The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 declared federal policy protecting Indigenous religious exercise, though later litigation showed its weak enforceability. Its 1994 amendment specifically strengthened protections concerning peyote, not Inyan.

### 1970s–present: reclamation and living practice

Lakota ceremonial renewal, language teaching, repatriation, and sacred-site advocacy restored public visibility. The Densmore/Lakota Song Repatriation Project now frames recorded songs as the intellectual and cultural property of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe.

Pahá Sápa remains central to land and religious-rights struggles. In *United States v. Sioux Nation* (1980), the Supreme Court affirmed compensation for the unlawful taking; Sioux nations have refused the monetary settlement because acceptance would extinguish the land claim.

At Mato Paha/Bear Butte, Native people continue prayer and vision practices. At Mato Tipila/Bear Lodge, officially “Devils Tower,” more than twenty-six tribes maintain sacred relationships; the National Park Service requests a voluntary climbing closure each June. These places are not documented “temples of Inyan,” but they show continuing sacred relations with stone and landscape.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Anthropology and comparative religion

Walker’s fourfold and sixteenfold schemes made Lakota religion legible as a “pantheon.” Later reference works repeated the scheme as fixed doctrine, often dropping consultant names and Walker’s mediation. This is the principal mechanism by which Inyan became an internet “creator god.”

Mircea-Eliade-style or perennialist readings often emphasize primordial sacrifice, center symbolism, and cosmic renewal. Such comparisons can be illuminating but are interpretations, not Lakota attestations.

### New Age and neopagan use

Since the later twentieth century, sweat lodges, “medicine wheels,” crystal spirituality, and generic “Native American shamanism” have been marketed to non-Native audiences. Inyan sometimes appears as an Earth-element deity or stone elemental.

No historical evidence was found for:

- an ancient initiatory “Order of Inyan”;
- standardized Inyan sigils;
- planetary or zodiacal correspondences;
- crystal grids dedicated to Inyan;
- a pre-contact “Inyan priesthood”;
- equivalence with Saturn, Geb, Gaia, or the Hindu lingam;
- a traditional Inyan feast day;
- demonological or ceremonial-magic seals.

These are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless practitioners plainly identify them as new creations.

Lakota people disagree about non-Lakota participation in ceremonies. The disagreement itself is contemporary evidence; neither unrestricted universalism nor a single absolute exclusion rule should be projected backward as unanimous tradition.

### Literature, art, and popular databases

Walker’s cycle has supplied encyclopedias, mythology handbooks, fantasy works, role-playing games, and deity registries. Common distortions include making Inyan omnipotent, saying he created humans directly, calling him “god of crystals,” or merging him with Stone Boy.

Arthur Amiotte and other Lakota artists offer modern Indigenous interpretation rather than archaeological illustration. Their works should be dated and attributed, not treated as anonymous survivals.

### Political meaning

Stone and sacred landscape appear in sovereignty, land-back, anti-mining, and sacred-site activism. These uses are not merely secular appropriations: land, religion, kinship, and political jurisdiction are not cleanly separable in Lakota thought. Still, not every defense of the Black Hills invokes Inyan by name.

No documented extremist appropriation centered specifically on Inyan was found. Generic white-supremacist or occult claims to “Native earth powers” should not be imputed to Lakota tradition.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Walker recording or inventing?

The answer is neither simple transcription nor wholesale invention.

Evidence for authenticity includes named Oglala consultants, Lakota-language manuscripts, parallel nineteenth-century documentation of Tunkan/Inyan, and recurrent concepts—stone’s age, power, sacredness, and ritual agency.

Evidence for shaping includes Walker’s limited Lakota, dependence on interpreters, arrangement of diverse accounts into a single cycle, European theological vocabulary, comparative interests, and literary transitions not traceable to one performance. Jahner’s description “Walker’s Literary Cycle” is therefore essential.

The responsible formulation is: **the Inyan cosmogony is a Walker-shaped Oglala archive whose motifs are Indigenous but whose surviving continuous epic form is early-twentieth-century literary ethnography.**

### “Great Spirit” and monotheism

Lynd portrayed Wakan Tanka as a remote supreme creator while Tunkan received practical devotion. Walker’s George Sword material includes the corrective that formerly people did not know “a Great Spirit” in the Christian sense. Elsewhere Walker interpreted Wakan Tanka collectively through sixteen powers.

Thus “Wakan Tanka created Inyan” may represent later monotheizing interpretation rather than the oldest recoverable Oglala formulation. Conversely, asserting that Wakan Tanka is purely a missionary invention is also too strong: *wakȟáŋ tȟáŋka* is Indigenous language with a complex history.

### Is Inyan identical with every stone?

Sources oscillate between:

- the primordial Rock;
- a sacred being dwelling in stone;
- stone as manifestation;
- particular dreamed or meteor-like stones;
- heated Grandfather stones;
- the ordinary material category.

This is evidence of multiple registers, not a problem to be “solved” by choosing one.

### Etymology

The secure etymology is lexical: Íŋyaŋ means stone/rock. Tȟuŋkáŋ is the reverential or sacred designation and is associated with kinship language. Claims that *Inyan* literally means “blue-blooded creator,” “foundation of the universe,” or “sacrifice” confuse narrative attributes with etymology.

### Human sacrifice and sexual cult claims

No source found establishes human sacrifice, ritual murder, temple prostitution, or sexual service to Inyan. Lynd expressly distinguishes Dakota self-offering and ordinary sacrifice from deliberate captive killing. Sun Dance piercing was bodily sacrifice but normally not an intention to kill.

### Archives and surviving witnesses

Important holdings include:

- Walker papers and George Sword manuscripts in History Colorado and related archival repositories;
- American Museum of Natural History materials connected with Clark Wissler and Walker;
- National Anthropological Archives/Smithsonian records, including Densmore materials;
- American Philosophical Society holdings for Ella Deloria;
- Neihardt interview papers published through *The Sixth Grandfather*;
- sound recordings and song documentation now involved in tribal repatriation projects.

The most consequential recoveries were the editorial reunification of Walker’s scattered primary materials in the late 1970s–1980s and DeMallie’s publication of Neihardt’s underlying Black Elk transcripts.

### Discredited or unsupported theories

- Inyan as a phallic “lingam”: colonial comparison, not an Indigenous equation.
- A single uniform “Sioux pantheon”: contradicted by Lynd’s and later ethnographers’ evidence of personal and regional variation.
- Walker’s cycle as a verbatim ancient epic: unsupported.
- Inyan and Stone Boy as automatically identical: unsupported.
- Blue sky literally being Inyan’s blood in every Lakota account: Walker-cycle detail generalized beyond its evidence.
- A continuous organized modern “cult of Inyan”: no evidence.
- Human sacrifice to Inyan: no evidence.
- All sacred Lakota mountains being shrines specifically to Inyan: no evidence.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Before written documentation | Oral traditions and stone-centered practices existed, but their age and exact Inyan narratives cannot be securely dated. |
| 1852 | Riggs publishes *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language*, establishing early printed linguistic evidence for *inyan*, stone/rock. |
| Before August 1862 | James W. Lynd gathers information on Dakota religion. |
| 1865 | Lynd’s “Religion of the Dakotas” is published, the earliest secure description of Tunkan/Inyan as oldest Stone power and major recipient of western Dakota prayers. |
| 1868 | Fort Laramie Treaty recognizes the Black Hills within Lakota territory. |
| 1874–1877 | Gold invasion is followed by United States seizure of the Black Hills. |
| March 30, 1883 | Code of Indian Offenses criminalizes participation in the Sun Dance and related ceremonies. |
| 1890 | Ghost Dance spreads at Pine Ridge and Rosebud; federal suppression culminates in the Wounded Knee massacre on December 29. |
| 1894 | Dorsey publishes *A Study of Siouan Cults*, preserving Lynd and accounts of mysterious, oracular stones. |
| 1896 | Walker begins work at Pine Ridge. |
| 1896–1914 | Walker collects testimony and Lakota texts from George Sword, Bad Wound, Finger, Tyon, Ringing Shield, and others. |
| 1911–12 | George Sword dies, according to the No Ears winter count cited in later scholarship. |
| 1914–15 | Thomas Tyon dies. |
| 1917 | Walker publishes his Oglala ceremonial synthesis, including organized theology. |
| 1918 | Densmore publishes *Teton Sioux Music*. |
| 1932 | Deloria’s *Dakota Texts* and Neihardt’s *Black Elk Speaks* appear. |
| 1934 | Collier-era policy formally reduces Bureau opposition to traditional ceremonies; public revival expands unevenly. |
| 1953 | *The Sacred Pipe* publishes Black Elk’s account of seven Oglala rites, including *inípi*. |
| 1970s | Ceremonial, political, and cultural reclamation becomes increasingly public; Powers publishes *Oglala Religion*. |
| 1978 | American Indian Religious Freedom Act declares federal protection policy. |
| 1980 | *Lakota Belief and Ritual* makes Walker’s underlying materials more accessible; Supreme Court decides *United States v. Sioux Nation*. |
| 1982–83 | *Lakota Society* and *Lakota Myth* appear; the latter exposes Walker’s literary-cycle form. |
| 1984–85 | DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* publishes the Black Elk interview record beneath Neihardt’s book. |
| 1987 | DeMallie and Parks publish *Sioux Indian Religion*. |
| 1994 | AIRFA amendment strengthens statutory protection for Indigenous peyote use. |
| 1995 | Devils Tower Climbing Management Plan establishes the voluntary June climbing closure. |
| 2006 | Updated Devils Tower plan continues the closure; a new edition of *Lakota Myth* appears. |
| 2016 | Pe’ Sla is placed into federal Indian trust status for participating tribes. |
| 2024 | A Native-led organization purchases land adjoining Bear Butte, part of continuing restoration of access to sacred landscapes. |
| 2026 | Íŋyaŋ remains present in Lakota-language teaching, ceremonial understandings of stone, published mythology, and living sacred-land relations; no centralized Inyan denomination or exclusive temple cult is documented. |
