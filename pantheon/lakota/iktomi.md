# Iktomi: Research Dossier

**Class:** Lakota/Dakota *wakȟáŋ* (“sacred/mysterious”) trickster figure or spirit, characteristically anthropomorphic despite his name’s association with the spider. Calling him simply a “spider god” is reductive.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** nineteenth-century ethnographic and linguistic documentation; the earliest substantial, source-attributed Oglala theological accounts were recorded by James R. Walker at Pine Ridge principally from George Sword, Bad Wound, Finger, and Thomas Tyon between 1896 and 1914. The narratives demonstrably predate their recording, but no pre-contact inscription or securely dated image of Iktomi is known.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

**[Secure linguistic attestation]** Lakota **Iktómi**, also **Iŋktómi**, means “spider”; Dakota cognates include **Uŋktómi/Unktómi**. Modern lexicography also records the figurative meaning “trickster, swindler, cheat.” Compounds include *iktómi tȟawókaške* (“spider web”), *iktómi sápa* (“black widow”), and *iktómi húhaŋskaska* (“daddy longlegs”). No securely demonstrated deeper etymology appears in the accessible linguistic evidence.

Variants such as **Ikto**, **Unktomi**, and older transcription **Ictomi** partly reflect dialect and partly inconsistent colonial orthographies. **Ictinike** belongs more properly to related Dhegiha traditions and should not automatically be treated as an alternative Lakota name.

**[Oglala learned systematization, recorded 1896–1914]** Walker’s informants distinguish or connect:

- **Ksa**, primordial wisdom and first son of Inyan;
- **Iktomi**, Ksa after his degradation, whose wisdom becomes self-defeating cunning.

That theology is important Oglala evidence, but it must not be projected backward as a universal “Sioux canon.” Dakota narratives collected by Ella Deloria ordinarily present Iktomi directly as a trickster protagonist without requiring Walker’s Ksa cosmology.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

The evidentiary record is oral-textual rather than epigraphic. No securely identifiable ancient temple, inscription, cult statue, or pre-contact manuscript names Iktomi.

Major corpora are:

- **Walker manuscripts, 1896–1914** — Lakota and English material collected at Pine Ridge. Their principal modern editions are *Lakota Belief and Ritual* and *Lakota Myth*. Walker preserves named and anonymous Oglala testimony, but his orderly pantheon may partly reflect editorial synthesis.
- **Zitkala-Ša, 1901, *Old Indian Legends*** — literary English retellings including Iktomi stories. These are Indigenous-authored adaptations for print, not verbatim ritual transcripts.
- **Frances Densmore, *Teton Sioux Music* (1918)** — songs, ceremonial evidence, and ethnographic testimony; valuable context but not an Iktomi scripture.
- **Ella Cara Deloria, *Dakota Texts* (1932)** — sixty-four Dakota/Lakota narratives, many printed in Indigenous-language text with translation. Its Iktomi cycle includes “Iktomi Conquers Iya,” “Iktomi Takes His Mother-in-Law on the Warpath,” “Iktomi Marries His Daughter,” “Iktomi Tricks the Pheasants,” “Iktomi Kills the Deer Boy,” “Iktomi and the Buffalo,” “Iktomi and the Raccoon-Skin,” and “Iktomi in a Skull.” Deloria collected across Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Standing Rock, and related communities. The Smithsonian preserves associated Boas-collection manuscripts.
- **Black Elk testimony, 1931 and later** — *Black Elk Speaks* is John Neihardt’s literary construction from interviews; Raymond DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* restores transcripts and demonstrates Neihardt’s omissions and reshaping. Black Elk is indispensable for Lakota sacred history but not the central source for the Iktomi tale corpus.
- **John Fire Lame Deer and Richard Erdoes, *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972)** — late twentieth-century mediated testimony containing living Lakota interpretation.
- **William K. Powers, *Oglala Religion* (1977)**; Raymond DeMallie and Douglas Parks, *Sioux Indian Religion*; Mark Dooling, *The Sons of the Wind* — modern analytical and documentary syntheses.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

**[Broadly attested folklore]** Iktomi deceives, imitates, disguises himself, violates social and sexual boundaries, manipulates appetite, and is frequently trapped by his own scheme. He is therefore simultaneously:

- a warning against greed, vanity, lust, boastfulness, and bad judgment;
- a comic entertainer;
- a culture-transforming agent whose misconduct can nevertheless produce present conditions;
- a dangerous mediator among humans, animals, and *wakȟáŋ* beings.

The tales usually portray a manlike traveler rather than a literal zoological spider. “Spider” chiefly supplies the image of entanglement and cunning. Modern illustrations depicting a humanoid spider are interpretive, not an ancient canonical iconography.

No fixed traditional color, planet, weekday, weapon, priesthood, or standardized cult image is securely attested. Internet lists assigning such correspondences are generally **modern invention** unless tied to specific Lakota testimony.

## 4. Genealogy and Divine Relations

**[Oglala system recorded by Walker]** Inyan fathers Ksa, the embodiment of wisdom. Ksa misuses wisdom in the affair involving Anpetu Wi and Ite; Skan condemns him, and he becomes Iktomi—clever but foolish, isolated, and repeatedly ensnared by his own designs.

**[Dakota narrative tradition]** Deloria’s Iktomi often enters stories without genealogy. This absence is evidence against treating Walker’s account as universally canonical.

### Documented interactions

- **Inyan —** father of Ksa, Iktomi’s prior identity; Walker’s Oglala accounts, recorded 1896–1914. **[Regional learned systematization]**
- **Skan —** judges and degrades Ksa after his misuse of wisdom; Walker corpus. **[Regional learned systematization]**
- **Anpetu Wi —** Iktomi/Ksa facilitates or exploits the improper relationship involving Sun and Ite; Walker corpus. **[Regional variant]**
- **Ite —** implicated in the transgression leading to Ksa’s punishment; Walker corpus. **[Regional variant]**
- **Iya —** Iktomi defeats the devouring being; Deloria, *Dakota Texts* (1932). **[Securely recorded Dakota narrative]**
- **Wakinyan —** some later-recorded Oglala cosmological narratives place Iktomi among beings acting in the ordering or disruption of the world; exact episodes vary by informant. **[Regional variant; no universal interaction established]**

No interaction should be inferred merely because Iktomi and another being appear in modern pantheon charts.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Ksa becomes Iktomi

In Walker’s Oglala synthesis, Ksa, Inyan’s first-born and possessor of wisdom, uses that wisdom destructively in a scandal involving Ite and Anpetu Wi. Skan’s judgment converts wisdom into cunning: Ksa becomes Iktomi, socially excluded and destined to be defeated by his own plots.

**Historical status:** securely recorded from Oglala authorities around 1900; its pre-contact antiquity is plausible but cannot be dated. It is not the organizing premise of every Dakota Iktomi tale.

### Iktomi conquers Iya

Deloria’s 1932 text presents Iktomi against Iya, the eater. Unlike tales ending in humiliation, this episode permits trickster cunning to overcome a destructive power. It demonstrates why “fool” alone is insufficient: Iktomi may be socially dangerous yet cosmologically useful.

### Appetite and self-entrapment tales

In “Iktomi Tricks the Pheasants,” “Iktomi and the Buffalo,” “Iktomi and the Raccoon-Skin,” and “Iktomi in a Skull,” desire initiates a scheme whose reversal exposes him. The tales differ in incident but share a durable narrative mechanism: cleverness detached from restraint becomes stupidity.

### Sexual and kinship violations

“Iktomi Marries His Daughter” and “Iktomi Takes His Mother-in-Law on the Warpath” use gross breaches of kinship decorum as comic and cautionary material. Sanitized children’s adaptations often omit precisely this aspect.

### Winter storytelling

**[Living traditional protocol]** Iktomi narratives belong prominently to **ohúŋkakaŋ**, the old-time story category traditionally narrated in winter, commonly after snowfall and while snakes or other beings are inactive. The protocol is culturally significant: these are not merely portable “folktales,” although publication detached them from season and performance.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There is no secure evidence for an institutional Iktomi cult, dedicated temple, specialist priesthood, annual Iktomi festival, human sacrifice, or temple prostitution. Iktomi’s primary locus is narrated tradition and moral-intellectual reflection.

This distinguishes him from beings addressed centrally in pipe ceremony, vision practice, or the Sun Dance. Storytelling itself is the relevant practice, governed by season, audience, language, humor, and narrative ownership.

The sacred geography of Paha Sapa, Bear Butte, and Mato Tipila remains central to Lakota religion, but none is securely established as an exclusive “Iktomi cult center.” Claims to that effect are unsubstantiated.

## 7. Historical Evolution

- **Pre-contact period:** Iktomi stories certainly circulated orally, but individual episodes cannot be securely dated.
- **Nineteenth century:** reservation confinement, missionization, schooling, military violence, and ethnographic collection altered the settings in which narratives were transmitted.
- **1882–1883:** federal policy targeted “heathenish dances,” including the Sun Dance. This suppressed Lakota ceremonial life broadly; it was not an Iktomi-specific prohibition.
- **1890:** Lakota Ghost Dance practice was met by military intervention culminating in the Wounded Knee massacre. No reliable evidence makes Iktomi a central Ghost Dance figure.
- **1896–1914:** Walker recorded the elaborate Oglala Ksa–Iktomi theology.
- **1901:** Zitkala-Ša transformed oral materials into English-language Indigenous literature.
- **1918:** Densmore documented Teton music and ceremony.
- **1932:** Deloria published the strongest bilingual textual corpus of Iktomi narratives.
- **1934 onward:** changes in federal Indian policy eased some official restrictions, though local pressure continued.
- **1978:** the American Indian Religious Freedom Act formally declared federal protection for Native religious exercise; it did not by itself repair every practical restriction.
- **Late twentieth century–present:** Iktomi appears in Lakota literature, education, visual art, and language revitalization, both as inherited trickster and as a figure for colonial entanglement, survival, and Indigenous intellectual sovereignty.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Zitkala-Ša’s work is the decisive early modern Indigenous literary transformation. Later Lakota and Dakota writers employ Iktomi without freezing him into Walker’s cosmology: he can signify appetite, comic survival, dangerous adaptability, and the snares of colonial modernity.

Generic “trickster archetype” readings are **modern comparative or Jungian interpretation**, not Lakota doctrine. Equating Iktomi with Loki, Anansi, Coyote, Hermes, or a universal occult spider deity is **comparativism**, not an attested syncretism. No documented historical Iktomi tradition in the Golden Dawn, ceremonial magic, or Renaissance hermetism has been established. Contemporary neopagan invocations outside Lakota communities are modern adoption, frequently without evidence of continuity or authorization.

## 9. Historiography and Contested Material

The central scholarly problem is mediation. Walker preserves extraordinary Oglala testimony but organizes it into a systematic theology. Neihardt reshaped Black Elk’s words for literary purposes. Deloria, trained by Boas yet herself Dakota, offers unusually strong linguistic and cultural competence, though publication still fixes performances that were contextual and seasonal.

Contested findings:

- **“Spider god”:** convenient but misleading; “spider/trickster *wakȟáŋ* being” better reflects the evidence.
- **Ksa identity:** securely recorded in Walker’s Oglala corpus, not universal across Dakota/Lakota sources.
- **Ancient spider iconography:** no securely identified pre-contact Iktomi image.
- **Human sacrifice:** no evidence tied specifically to Iktomi.
- **Ghost Dance:** no evidence that Iktomi was a principal Ghost Dance divinity.
- **Syncretism:** comparisons with world tricksters are scholarly or popular analogies, not documented historical equations.
- **Occult/neopagan antiquity:** unsupported.
- **Sun Dance “disappearance”:** too simple; suppression was real, but practice and knowledge did not uniformly vanish, and histories differ over clandestine continuity and revival.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before European documentation:** oral circulation; no individually datable Iktomi text survives.
- **1882–1883:** federal suppression of Native ceremonies intensifies.
- **1890:** Ghost Dance crisis and Wounded Knee; major context for Lakota religious history, not an Iktomi episode.
- **1896–1914:** Walker collects Oglala religious accounts.
- **1901:** Zitkala-Ša publishes literary Iktomi tales.
- **1918:** Densmore publishes *Teton Sioux Music*.
- **1928–1938:** Deloria records language, stories, and ethnography among Dakota and Lakota communities.
- **1932:** *Dakota Texts* appears.
- **1934:** Indian Reorganization era brings a partial federal policy shift.
- **1972:** *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions*.
- **1977:** Powers’s *Oglala Religion*.
- **1978:** American Indian Religious Freedom Act.
- **1984:** DeMallie publishes *The Sixth Grandfather*, exposing Neihardt’s editorial reshaping.
- **2006:** *Dakota Texts* reissued with DeMallie’s introduction.
- **Present:** Iktomi remains active in storytelling, literature, education, art, and Lakota-language work.
