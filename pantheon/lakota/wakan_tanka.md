# Wakan Tanka: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Lakota classification, **Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is the encompassing sacred or mysterious reality and, in one Oglala priestly formulation, the collective unity of sixteen wakȟáŋ beings or powers**. It is not securely reducible to a single anthropomorphic “god.” Its treatment as one personal Supreme Being is especially prominent in missionary translation, some later Lakota Christian discourse, and popular retellings.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely dated printed documentation located for this dossier is Stephen Return Riggs’s missionary **_Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language_**, accepted for Smithsonian publication in 1851 and published in **1852**. It records *wakáŋ* and related Dakota religious vocabulary from a missionary environment already engaged in translating Christian scripture. This is an attestation of the terminology—not proof that the later sixteen-power system, a unitary creator-god concept, or any single theology existed unchanged at that date. The earliest detailed Lakota/Oglala descriptions of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as the **Tóbtob**, “four-times-four,” derive from James R. Walker’s Pine Ridge investigations, principally **1896–1914**, especially the testimony of George Sword and Finger.

**Evidentiary labels used below**

- **[Early documentary attestation]**: nineteenth-century evidence preceding Walker’s synthesis.
- **[Walker/Oglala attestation]**: testimony gathered principally at Pine Ridge in 1896–1914.
- **[Regional or individual variant]**: an account attributable to a particular speaker, community, or ceremonial lineage.
- **[Missionary-Christian syncretism]**: translation or reinterpretation involving Christian categories.
- **[Later canonical presentation]**: an influential twentieth-century text often treated as authoritative.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**: an analytical conclusion, not itself an Indigenous revelation.
- **[Modern reinvention or appropriation]**: a recent representation unsupported as an old Lakota tradition.
- **[Not established]**: a claim for which adequate documentary evidence was not found.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identification

**Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka** belongs to a field of meanings rather than to a stable Western category such as “high god.” The expression may refer to:

1. the great or encompassing *wakȟáŋ*—the sacred, potent, inexplicable, extraordinary, or set-apart;
2. the collectivity of sacred powers;
3. in the Walker material, sixteen powers acting as a unity;
4. in Christian Lakota usage, God, God the Father, or the Trinity;
5. in some twentieth-century Native explanations, a pervasive creative power or mystery within which all beings participate.

These meanings overlap historically but are not interchangeable.

### Lakota and Dakota forms

- **Lakota standard orthography:** **Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka**.
- **Older ethnographic and missionary spellings:** *Wakan Tanka*, *Wakantanka*, *Wakan-Tanka*, *Wakan’tanka*, *Wakonda Tonka*, and phonetic variants.
- **Dakota:** cognate forms generally written *Wakáŋ Táŋka* or, in older missionary spelling, *Wakan Tanka*.
- **Common English renderings:** “Great Spirit,” “Great Mystery,” “Great Mysterious,” “Great Sacred,” “Great Holy,” “great incomprehensibility,” and “sum of all things unknown.”

The form is adjectival: *tȟáŋka* means “large” or “great”; *wakȟáŋ* has a semantic field including sacredness, potency, mystery, incomprehensibility, and extraordinary efficacy. “Great Mystery” better preserves the epistemic dimension than “Great Spirit,” but no short English phrase reproduces the entire field.

Claims that *wakȟáŋ* can be transparently divided into *wa-* plus *kan* and thereby given a simple primordial etymology are **[Not established]** by the historical sources examined. Such explanations occur in popular literature, but synchronically meaningful word segmentation is not necessarily historical etymology. The comparative Siouan cognates—Omaha-Ponca and related *wakóⁿda/wakanda* forms—show an old Siouan religious vocabulary, but they do not make all Siouan concepts identical.

### “Great Spirit” and its history

**[Missionary-Christian syncretism]** “Great Spirit” is not a neutral word-for-word translation. European and Euro-American writers widely used “Great Spirit” as a cross-tribal category, flattening distinct Algonquian, Siouan, and other concepts into an assumed Indigenous monotheism.

Riggs’s 1852 dictionary emerged from the Dakota Mission and therefore belongs simultaneously to linguistic documentation and Christian translation history. Dakota Bible translators used forms of *Wakantanka* for the biblical God. The full Dakota Bible appeared in the nineteenth century; an American Bible Society edition was published in **1883**. The title *Wowapi Wakan*—“Sacred/Holy Book”—likewise placed *wakáŋ* inside Christian print vocabulary.

Walker later observed this semantic transformation directly. In a letter of **20 March 1912**, he wrote that younger Lakotas used *Wakan Tanka* for “Jehovah,” while older religious specialists associated it with *Taku Škaŋškaŋ* and, in an older ceremonial register, simply **Škaŋ**. That is valuable testimony to change, but it is Walker’s historical inference based on age differences among speakers—not a transcript from a pre-contact period.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Raymond DeMallie’s analysis is therefore cautious: before about **1880**, the evidence is insufficient to reconstruct one uniform Lakota doctrine of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka. What can be reconstructed is a religious world organized through *wakȟáŋ* power, relations with named other-than-human beings, dreams, visions, pipes, stones, animals, winds, thunder, and place. A single personal creator called Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka cannot simply be projected backward from twentieth-century Christian-influenced texts.

### Titles and related expressions

- **Tóbtob kiŋ / Tobtob kin**, “the Four-Times-Four”: the sixteen powers in Sword’s account. **[Walker/Oglala attestation]**
- **Tȟuŋkášila**, “Grandfather”: a relational address in prayer, also applicable to revered ancient beings, stones, or ancestors; it is not always a proper name synonymous with Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka.
- **Táku Škaŋškaŋ**, approximately “That-which-moves/makes-move”: associated by Walker with an older conception of the animating power Škaŋ. **[Walker’s historical reconstruction]**
- **Škaŋ:** movement, sky, or cosmic animation in Walker’s materials; in his system it is one of the superior powers, not simply a universally attested alternative proper name for one supreme god.
- **Wakȟáŋpi:** sacred or mysterious beings/powers, the plural form appropriate to many members of the sacred world.
- **Wičháša Wakȟáŋ:** a sacred or holy man, commonly but inadequately rendered “medicine man” or “shaman.”

“Creator,” “Father,” “Lord,” “Supreme Being,” and “God” are historically important translations, but their capitalization and personal grammar often import Christian assumptions.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 What the earliest evidence establishes

Riggs’s **1852** dictionary is a printed book compiled through Dakota speakers and missionaries. It securely establishes that *wakáŋ* belonged to Dakota religious language by the mid-nineteenth century. Because missionary activity had already begun, it cannot by itself disentangle inherited meanings from emerging Christian usage. Nor does a dictionary gloss establish systematic theology.

No securely dated pre-contact inscription, manuscript, temple text, or Indigenous alphabetic document naming Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is known. Lakota sacred knowledge was principally oral, performative, visionary, ceremonial, and place-based. The absence of an earlier written record is therefore a condition of colonial documentation, not evidence that the underlying concepts began in 1852.

Winter counts are crucial Indigenous historical records, but the surviving counts generally register years through memorable events rather than offering theological treatises. They contextualize epidemics, warfare, migrations, the disappearance of buffalo, missions, the Ghost Dance, and Wounded Knee; they do not independently attest Walker’s sixteen-part system.

### 2.2 Walker corpus

James R. Walker was a physician at Pine Ridge who collected Oglala religious material from approximately **1896 to 1914**. His principal consultants included:

- **George Sword / Mičháŋ Máza or related recorded forms** (1846–1910), an Oglala leader and Christian convert with extensive knowledge of older ritual;
- **Bad Wound**;
- **No Flesh**;
- **Thomas Tyon**;
- **Finger**;
- **Ringing Shield**;
- **Short Feather**;
- other ceremonial specialists.

The resulting documents are heterogeneous. Some are dictated statements; some are answers to Walker’s questions; some are translations; some are Walker’s syntheses. They must not all be treated as verbatim scripture.

#### _The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota_ (1917)

Published in the American Museum of Natural History’s *Anthropological Papers*, this contains Sword’s explicit statement:

> “Wakan Tanka is like sixteen different persons; but each person is kan. Therefore, they are all only the same as one.”

Sword says a holy man addresses this totality as **Tobtob kin**. He distinguishes the “four-times-four gods” from **Tob kin**, the Four Winds, which serve as *akíčhita*, messengers or enforcers of the other powers. **[Walker/Oglala attestation]**

This is the earliest detailed published source located for the sixteen-power doctrine. It dates the documentation, not the doctrine’s origin.

#### _Lakota Belief and Ritual_ (Walker material edited by Raymond DeMallie and Elaine Jahner, 1980)

This volume prints the primary documents with editorial context rather than hiding variations under one synthesis. Relevant components include:

- “Foundations,” by George Sword;
- accounts of ceremonies;
- teachings on *kan*, *nagí*, *niyá*, and other components of personhood;
- Finger’s narrative of Wóȟpe and the Sacred Pipe;
- testimony concerning Wakíŋyaŋ, buffalo, bears, wolves, toads, ghosts, sacred stones, visions, and ritual specialists.

It is indispensable because it shows individual disagreements and because DeMallie and Jahner identify Walker’s hand in the material.

#### _Lakota Myth_ (Walker material edited by Elaine Jahner, 1983)

This gathers Walker’s mythological narratives and reconstructions. Walker’s polished creation sequence—Íŋyaŋ, Makȟá, Škaŋ, Wí, the secondary gods, the fall of Ksa/Iktómi, and subsequent ordering of the world—is influential but is partly a **learned ethnographic synthesis**. Walker stated that he combined teachings from several specialists. Finger’s final long interview probably affected the result, but a full transcript does not survive. The synthesis cannot be treated as a single ancient “Lakota Bible.”

### 2.3 Densmore

Frances Densmore’s **_Teton Sioux Music_** (Bureau of American Ethnology Bulletin 61, **1918**) is a 561-page documentary corpus based on early recording work. It covers:

- the White Buffalo Calf Pipe;
- spirit keeping;
- the Sun Dance;
- dream obligations;
- sacred stones;
- healing;
- buffalo, elk, bear, wolf, and bird powers;
- military and social societies;
- ceremonial and personal songs.

Its importance lies in performances and testimony rather than systematic cosmology. A prayer recorded from Red Weasel addresses Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as a truthful power “from above” who hears and watches. This sounds personal in English but does not, by itself, decide whether the referent is one anthropomorphic god.

Densmore’s wax-cylinder recordings and papers are material witnesses as important as the printed transcriptions. Modern Lakota-led repatriation work has re-recorded and culturally contextualized more than seventy songs while stating that the traditional knowledge remains the intellectual and cultural property of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe.

### 2.4 Ella Deloria

Ella Cara Deloria’s **_Dakota Texts_** (**1932**) presents Dakota-language narratives with translations. It is especially valuable because the Indigenous-language text constrains English theological paraphrase. Its stories preserve multiple sacred beings and trickster narratives rather than a neat monotheistic system. Because most contributors are Dakota rather than Oglala Lakota, the collection should not be used to erase dialectal, regional, and tribal distinctions.

### 2.5 Black Elk and Neihardt

#### _Black Elk Speaks_ (1932)

John G. Neihardt interviewed Black Elk in **1931**, through interpreters and with shorthand transcription by his daughter Enid. Neihardt then shaped the material into literary English. Black Elk’s Great Vision includes the Six Grandfathers, powers of the directions, horses, a sacred tree, and the hoop of the nation.

**[Later canonical presentation]** The book became perhaps the most influential modern representation of Lakota religion.

**[Textual caution]** Neihardt omitted, condensed, rearranged, and poeticized material. His published text is not a verbatim transcript, and the framing reflects his own literary and universalizing purposes.

#### _The Sixth Grandfather_ (edited by Raymond DeMallie, 1984)

DeMallie published the interview transcripts and compared them with Neihardt’s book. The comparison shows, among other things, that Neihardt sharply shortened the Six Grandfathers sequence and omitted gifts, songs, and explanations of particular powers.

DeMallie argues that Black Elk’s later thought cannot be separated from his Catholic life. Black Elk converted in **1904**, took the name Nicholas, and became a catechist. His language could join Lakota and Christian referents without simply abandoning either.

DeMallie notes that Black Elk sometimes appears to construe the Six Grandfathers as a “Six-in-One,” with the fifth, the Sky, resembling a supreme creator. That has no exact parallel in the older Walker corpus. Yet DeMallie explicitly declines to assert with certainty that Black Elk simply converted Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka into the Christian Godhead.

#### _The Sacred Pipe_ (Black Elk as told to Joseph Epes Brown, 1953)

This presents seven rites associated with the Sacred Pipe:

1. keeping of the soul;
2. purification lodge;
3. crying for a vision;
4. Sun Dance;
5. making relatives;
6. girl’s puberty ceremony;
7. throwing the ball.

Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is frequently addressed as “Grandfather,” “Father,” or the power to whom voices travel through the pipe. Brown’s Traditionalist or perennialist interests and Black Elk’s Catholic formation require critical attention. The rites themselves are not inventions of Brown, but their organization as exactly seven and the universal metaphysics of the published exposition are **[Later canonical presentation]**, not an uncontested transcript of nineteenth-century consensus.

### 2.6 Later Native and ethnographic sources

- **John (Fire) Lame Deer and Richard Erdoes, _Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions_ (1972):** presents Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as both unity and multiplicity. It is a collaborative autobiography, not a transparent oral transcript.
- **William K. Powers, _Oglala Religion_ (1975 and later editions):** a major ethnography of ritual and social organization, heavily engaged with Walker but based also on twentieth-century Pine Ridge fieldwork.
- **D. M. Dooling, _The Sons of the Wind_ (1984):** an English collection of Lakota sacred stories. It is a literary compilation and must not be mistaken for a single ancient canonical text.
- **Raymond DeMallie and Douglas Parks, _Sioux Indian Religion: Tradition and Innovation_ (1987):** explicitly treats plurality and change. Important contributors include Arvol Looking Horse on the Sacred Pipe and Arthur Amiotte on the Sun Dance.
- **Arthur Amiotte:** his writing and art interpret ritual, kinship, history, Catholic encounter, and contemporary Lakota life from an Oglala perspective. His work is modern Indigenous interpretation, not a fossilized survival from an ahistorical past.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is associated with:

- the total sacred order;
- the sources and circulation of life and power;
- vision and revelation;
- the forces manifested in sky, earth, stone, sun, moon, wind, thunder, buffalo, bear, and the human soul;
- prayer carried through pipe smoke;
- truth, kinship, reciprocity, and right relation;
- creation in some later or systematized narratives.

The expression is not restricted to a specialized domain like war, rain, fertility, or death.

### Powers and limitations

In Sword’s account, the constituent sacred persons possess **tóŋ**, the capacity to perform extraordinary acts. Some possess physical properties; others do not. Some are ordinarily visible; others invisible. Invisible powers appear in visions particularly to religious specialists. The system therefore describes modes of manifestation rather than the anatomy of one supernatural body.

Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is not consistently portrayed as omnipotent in the later Christian philosophical sense. Individual powers have offices, relationships, rivalries, and limitations. Creation may proceed through Íŋyaŋ’s expenditure of his own substance, Škaŋ’s judgments, Makȟá’s needs, and the activities of winds, thunder beings, animals, and tricksters.

### Iconography

**No stable traditional anthropomorphic iconography of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as a single figure is securely attested.** This negative finding matters. There is no Lakota counterpart to a standard statue of Zeus, Śiva, or Athena by which the collective sacred reality can always be recognized.

Material and visual forms associated with prayer toward the sacred include:

- the **čhaŋnúŋpa**, sacred pipe;
- pipe bowl and stem, joined ceremonially;
- the Sun Dance pole and lodge;
- the sacred tree;
- stones used in *inípi*, *yuwípi*, and other rites;
- buffalo and buffalo imagery;
- eagle feathers;
- the circle or hoop;
- the four directions and direction colors, whose exact arrangements vary;
- medicine wheels and contemporary diagrammatic representations.

These are relational and ceremonial media, not necessarily “symbols of Wakan Tanka” in the sense of a divine logo.

**[Modern invention]** Images depicting Wakan Tanka as a bearded “Native American sky god,” a single giant warrior, or a fantasy-game character are modern personifications. They lack early Lakota iconographic warrant.

### Sacred numbers, directions, colors, and beings

Four is structurally important in directions, winds, ritual repetitions, and Walker’s four groups of four. Seven becomes prominent in Black Elk/Brown through the seven rites and the four directions plus sky, earth, and center. Neither number should be imposed on every Lakota community or period as a universal code.

Colors associated with directions vary by source and ceremony. Modern posters often standardize yellow, red, black, and white into a fixed “medicine wheel”; historical Lakota evidence shows more contextual variation.

### Animals and natural manifestations

Buffalo, eagle, bear, wolf, horse, elk, and birds may convey or manifest sacred power. They are not merely heraldic “sacred animals belonging to” a master god. The relational model is better supported: human beings receive songs, knowledge, healing, obligations, and names through encounters with other-than-human persons.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### 4.1 Is there a genealogy of Wakan Tanka?

Not in the ordinary sense. As an encompassing reality or collective, Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka has no securely attested parents, spouse, or biological offspring.

Walker’s mythology instead supplies genealogies among the constituent powers. His synthesized creation account begins with **Íŋyaŋ** and darkness. Íŋyaŋ creates or differentiates **Makȟá**, expending his blue blood and hardening into stone. **Škaŋ** encloses or orders the world; **Wí** supplies light. Subsequent beings emerge through their relations.

This should be classified as **[Walker/Oglala learned systematization]**, not “the canonical Lakota family tree.”

### 4.2 The sixteen powers

Sword’s published list is not identical to every later chart, partly because names, suffixes, and categories were normalized differently. In the older transcription it includes:

- **Wí / Wí-káŋ** — Sun;
- **Haŋwí / Haŋwí-káŋ** — Moon;
- **Škaŋ / Táku Škaŋškaŋ** — movement, sky, animating power;
- **Tȟaté** — Wind;
- **Tȟaté Tȟópa / Tob Kin** — Four Winds;
- **Yumní / Yumnikáŋ** — whirlwind;
- **Makȟá** — Earth;
- **Wóȟpe** — beneficent feminine power, identified in Finger’s account with the Pipe Woman;
- **Íŋyaŋ** — Stone/Rock;
- **Wakíŋyaŋ** — Thunder Beings;
- **Tȟatȟáŋka** — Buffalo;
- **Húŋuŋpa / Hunonpa** — a two-legged or bear-related category whose translation varies in the secondary literature;
- **Nagí** — spirit;
- **Niyá** — breath, life, or ghost-like component;
- **Nagíla** — spirit-like component;
- **Wašíčuŋpi / sicuŋ-related power** — a power or indwelling potency, difficult to translate.

Later four-by-four tables often organize these as superior powers, their associates, subordinate powers, and “god-like” beings. The tables are useful representations of Walker’s archive, but exact English labels vary because Walker and his editors were translating an esoteric vocabulary.

### 4.3 Every documented divine interaction

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Creation through Íŋyaŋ and the ordering powers

**[Walker/Oglala learned systematization]**

Walker’s fullest reconstruction begins when only Íŋyaŋ and darkness exist. Íŋyaŋ is soft and possesses creative potency in his blue blood. To make another being, he opens himself and creates Makȟá, spilling his blood and losing fluid power. He hardens into stone. Makȟá is initially shapeless or incomplete. Škaŋ orders or surrounds the emerging world. Wí is established as light. Wind, Moon, and other powers follow as the cosmos becomes differentiated.

This is not a creation “by Wakan Tanka” if that phrase is imagined as one separate craftsman. It is creation through relations among powers that collectively belong to Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka.

The story’s earliest full documentary form is Walker’s turn-of-the-century archive. No equivalent pre-1850 text has been found. Its polished genealogy and fourfold ranks may reflect Walker’s desire for a coherent theology, the systematizing interests of particular *wičháša wakȟáŋ*, or both.

### 5.2 Ksa’s fall and transformation into Iktómi

In Walker’s mythic history, **Ksa**, wisdom, becomes implicated in deception. He induces Anóǧ Ite to seek Haŋwí’s place beside Wí. Škaŋ judges the conspirators. Anóǧ Ite receives two faces; Ksa becomes **Iktómi**, whose remaining intelligence is cunning that repeatedly traps its possessor.

This myth accounts for disorder within an ordered sacred cosmos. It also explains why intelligence is morally ambiguous. The familiar Iktómi tale corpus is broader than Walker’s divine genealogy; Dakota and Lakota trickster stories often function independently of the sixteen-power framework.

### 5.3 The Pipe Woman

**[Regional variant becoming later canonical]**

Finger told Walker that **Wóȟpe** was the woman who brought the sacred Pipe. In the widely circulated form, two hunters see a beautiful woman. One approaches with predatory desire and is destroyed or reduced to bones; the respectful hunter returns to his people with her instructions. She brings the pipe, teaches right relations and prayer, and departs, changing into buffalo of successive colors. She promises continuing relation with the people.

Variants differ on:

- the woman’s name;
- whether she is explicitly Wóȟpe;
- the exact buffalo transformations;
- which ceremonies she teaches immediately;
- whether the seven rites are all enumerated;
- the antiquity and succession of Pipe Keepers.

Densmore documented Pipe traditions by 1918. Black Elk and Brown’s 1953 presentation made the seven-rite system internationally canonical. Treating every detail in Brown as immemorial and universally Lakota is unwarranted.

### 5.4 Emergence and the creation of the present world

Lakota and Dakota sources preserve several creation and emergence narratives. Some describe humanity living beneath the earth before emerging through a passage or cave; some make trickster figures responsible for bringing people into an unfinished world; some feature earth-diver animals recovering mud from primordial water.

Modern retellings sometimes insert a singular Wakan Tanka as the speaking creator in every episode. That wording may be a legitimate contemporary narration, but it is not evidence that all older versions featured one named personal creator. Dooling’s _The Sons of the Wind_ is a useful story collection but a twentieth-century English literary presentation.

### 5.5 Black Elk’s Great Vision

At about nine years old, Black Elk experienced a prolonged vision in which he encountered the Six Grandfathers, received powers associated with the directions, saw horses and peoples, and was charged with restoring the nation’s sacred hoop and flowering tree.

The vision is not a myth of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka in the narrow sense; it is a visionary disclosure of an ordered sacred world. Neihardt’s **1932** version universalized its imagery. The **1984** transcripts reveal omitted songs, objects, and explanations.

Black Elk later interpreted the sixth grandfather—the earth or humanity—as represented through himself. DeMallie detects a Christ-like universalism but does not reduce the vision to either “pure Christianity” or untouched pre-Christian religion.

### 5.6 Ghost Dance revelation

In **1889–1890**, the Northern Paiute prophet Wovoka’s movement spread to Lakota communities. Its Lakota forms promised reunion with the dead, restoration of buffalo and land, and transformation of the colonial order. Christian resurrection language, Wovoka’s prophetic authority, older circle dancing, and Lakota visionary practice converged.

Black Elk participated and incorporated Ghost Dance imagery into his account. The movement was not merely “worship of Wakan Tanka,” and it was not a military conspiracy. Federal agents and newspapers construed it as a threat. Attempts to arrest Sitting Bull and disarm Miniconjou Lakotas culminated in the U.S. Seventh Cavalry’s killing of hundreds at **Wounded Knee on 29 December 1890**.

The claim that ghost shirts universally made wearers invulnerable is an outsider simplification. Beliefs and interpretations differed, and accounts were reshaped after the massacre.

## 6. Cult, Sacred Places, and Ritual Practice

“Cult” is used here in the historical-religions sense. Lakota religion did not center on permanent temples, cult statues, or a bureaucratic priesthood.

### Religious specialists

**Wičháša wakȟáŋ** acquired knowledge through visions, apprenticeship, ceremonial authority, and relations with sacred powers. Their fields included healing, divination, dream interpretation, weather, warfare, and ceremonial leadership. Authority was plural and lineage-based rather than governed by one church.

The Walker archive distinguishes public knowledge from restricted ceremonial knowledge. Sword persuaded specialists to disclose some teachings because elders were dying and he feared the knowledge would be lost. This colonial context shaped what was revealed, withheld, translated, and systematized.

### The Pipe

The **čhaŋnúŋpa** joins bowl and stem and provides a material means of making relation. Tobacco smoke bears the participants’ voice; the pipe may represent the whole cosmos and the joining of differentiated beings.

The pipe is not a passive “idol.” It is treated as a sacred participant that knows, witnesses, and entails obligations. Black Elk said that when one prays with the Pipe, one prays “with everything,” because all beings are joined.

### Sun Dance

The Lakota name is commonly given as **Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi**, “They Dance Looking at the Sun.” Practices include:

- construction of a circular lodge around a central tree or pole;
- fasting and dancing;
- songs and eagle-bone whistles;
- vows made for kin or community;
- in some lineages, piercing and flesh offerings;
- prayer toward Sun, sky, earth, directions, buffalo, and the encompassing sacred order.

Self-piercing is documented sacrifice, but not human sacrifice in the sense of killing a victim for Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka. No reliable evidence was found for institutional killing of human victims to this being.

### Other rites

- **Inípi:** purification in a lodge with heated stones, water, steam, songs, and prayer.
- **Haŋbléčheyapi:** “crying for a vision,” involving isolation, fasting, prayer, and awaiting a sacred encounter.
- **Wanáǧi Yuhápi:** keeping of a deceased person’s spirit for a prescribed period.
- **Huŋkalowaŋpi / Huŋká:** making relatives or establishing ritually binding relationships.
- **Išnáthi Awíčhalowaŋpi:** puberty or coming-of-age rite for a girl.
- **Tȟápa Waŋkáyeyapi:** throwing the ball, interpreted in Black Elk/Brown as humanity’s difficult effort to reach the sacred.
- **Yuwípi:** healing rite involving binding the ceremonial leader in darkness while songs and spirit helpers are invoked.

Dog or puppy sacrifice and ceremonial consumption are documented in some Lakota/Dakota rites, including forms associated with dream societies and Heyókȟa practice. They should neither be concealed nor generalized into a universal “cult of Wakan Tanka.” No evidence supports temple prostitution.

### Suppression

On **2 December 1882**, Interior Secretary Henry M. Teller condemned Sun Dances and related ceremonies as obstacles to federal “civilization.” The **1883 Rules Governing the Court of Indian Offenses** made the Sun Dance and related feasts punishable by withholding rations or agency imprisonment.

This was coercive religious suppression tied to reservation dependency, missionization, boarding schools, allotment, and the destruction of the buffalo economy. Ceremonies continued selectively, privately, or in modified public forms.

The policy shift commonly associated with John Collier and the **1934 Indian New Deal** reduced federal opposition and allowed more open revival. The **American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978** declared a federal policy of protecting Indigenous religious exercise but created limited enforceable rights. Peyote use received clearer statutory protection for Native American religious practice in **1994**.

### Sacred places

#### Pahá Sápa / He Sápa, the Black Hills

The Black Hills are central to Lakota sacred geography and contemporary political claims. The **1868 Fort Laramie Treaty** reserved the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Hills, for the Sioux Nation’s “absolute and undisturbed use and occupation.” After gold discoveries, the United States imposed the **1877** taking without the treaty-required consent.

In **United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians** (1980), the Supreme Court affirmed compensation for the taking. Sioux nations have refused the money because accepting compensation would treat the land as sold. The continuing claim is for land and sovereignty, not merely financial value.

#### Matȟó Pahá / Bear Butte

Bear Butte is a place of prayer, fasting, vision, and offerings for Lakota and Cheyenne people, among others. Modern land purchases near the state park seek to restore ceremonial access obstructed by dispossession and commercial development.

#### Matȟó Thípila / Bear Lodge, officially Devils Tower

The formation is sacred to multiple Plains nations. Lakota names include Bear Lodge and related forms. NPS records identify vision quests, ceremonies, and healing traditions there. The English name “Devils Tower” arose from a nineteenth-century mistranslation or hostile reinterpretation and remains contested. A voluntary June climbing closure seeks to reduce disruption during a major ceremonial season.

Sacred-place rites do not necessarily invoke Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as a separate resident deity. Place itself participates in *wakȟáŋ* relations.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before sustained written documentation

**[Reconstruction with major limits]** Lakota religious life included named sacred powers, animal-persons, dreams, bundles, pipes, stones, thunder beings, healing societies, kinship rites, and place-based practice. The comparative age of *wakȟáŋ* vocabulary is supported by related Siouan languages. A standardized sixteen-god pantheon or solitary creator named Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is not securely reconstructable.

### Missionary era, approximately 1830s–1880

Missionaries and Dakota collaborators created an extensive Christian vocabulary. *Wakantanka* became an equivalent for “God,” while *wowapi wakáŋ* became scripture or “holy writing.” Native translators were active agents, but the translation encouraged readers to hear a proper name for one Supreme Being.

This is the period in which “Great Spirit” became entrenched in English. It often served missionaries, government officials, and comparative religionists seeking an Indigenous anticipation of Christianity.

### Colonial crisis, 1860s–1890

War, confinement, buffalo destruction, land seizure, rations, mission schools, and bans on ceremonies transformed religious life. The Ghost Dance offered restoration and reunion. Wounded Knee became a traumatic historical and sacred site.

Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka language during this period could express inherited sacred relations, Christian God-language, or both. Speakers did not necessarily experience those layers as mutually exclusive.

### Walker’s Pine Ridge documentation, 1896–1914

Walker’s consultants produced the most detailed record of the four-times-four system. Several were Christian converts who retained older religious expertise. Their evidence proves that the system existed as an articulated Oglala teaching around 1900; it does not prove that every Lakota community shared it or that Walker’s final genealogy was unchanged from pre-contact antiquity.

### Densmore and Black Elk, 1911–1953

Densmore documented songs and rites at Standing Rock and elsewhere. Black Elk’s accounts then supplied a globally influential synthesis:

- _Black Elk Speaks_ presented visionary history and national tragedy;
- _The Sacred Pipe_ presented seven rites and an encompassing metaphysics;
- Black Elk’s Catholic identity complicated any division between “traditional” and “Christian.”

### Ceremonial revival and Indigenous activism, 1930s–1970s

Sun Dances and other ceremonies became increasingly public. This was not simply the restoration of an unchanged past: communities reconstructed rites after decades of suppression, negotiated Christian participation, and responded to tourism and anthropological attention.

The American Indian Movement, the **1973 occupation of Wounded Knee**, language revitalization, and sacred-land struggles made Lakota spirituality central to political renewal.

### Protection and boundary-making, 1978–present

AIRFA gave federal recognition to Indigenous religious freedom, though courts continued to permit burdens on sacred practices. Contemporary Lakota leaders have also drawn boundaries around ceremonies.

At the **Lakota Summit V in June 1993**, representatives adopted a “Declaration of War Against Exploiters of Lakota Spirituality,” condemning non-Native commercialization and misrepresentation. In **2003**, Arvol Looking Horse announced restrictions on non-Native participation within the central ceremonial space of the seven rites.

Contemporary practice remains plural:

- traditional ceremonial communities;
- Catholic-Lakota and other Christian-Lakota syntheses;
- Native American Church practice;
- individuals who participate across traditions;
- secular or cultural understandings of *wakȟáŋ*;
- language and land-based revitalization.

No reliable statistic establishes the number of exclusive “worshippers of Wakan Tanka,” because the category itself misconstrues how many practitioners identify their religion.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Black Elk as universal mystic

Neihardt’s prose, Joseph Epes Brown’s Traditionalism, comparative religion, environmental writing, and countercultural spirituality transformed Black Elk into a universal mystic. This greatly increased respect for Lakota thought but often detached teachings from Oglala kinship, sovereignty, language, and ceremonial authority.

Carl Jung did not establish an ancient “Wakan Tanka archetype.” Jungian and archetypal readings are twentieth-century interpretive overlays.

### New Age and neopagan reception

From the 1960s onward, non-Native workshops marketed sweat lodges, medicine wheels, vision quests, pipe ceremonies, “Native shamanism,” and prayers to the “Great Spirit.” Some instructors claimed Lakota authorization; others fabricated lineages.

**[Modern invention or appropriation]** A generic Wakan Tanka assembled from Lakota vocabulary, pan-Indian stereotypes, environmental spirituality, occult correspondences, chakras, astrology, or ceremonial practices sold by fee is not evidence of an old Lakota esoteric order.

No historical evidence connects Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka to Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, classical grimoires, Thelema, or pre-twentieth-century European witchcraft. Occult charts making such correspondences are modern constructions.

### Political and nationalist use

Lakota activists invoke sacred vocabulary in defense of:

- the Black Hills;
- Bear Butte;
- Pe’ Sla;
- Bear Lodge;
- water and treaty rights;
- repatriation of objects and ancestors;
- protection of ceremonies from commercial exploitation.

This is not merely “religion used for politics.” In Lakota testimony, land, kinship, treaty obligations, and sacred relations are not separable domains.

No substantial documented white-supremacist or other extremist cult centered on Wakan Tanka was found. Generic misuse of “Great Spirit” in settler nationalism and romantic “vanishing Indian” narratives is much better attested.

### Literature, music, games, and commercial imagery

Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka and “Great Spirit” appear widely in Western fiction, children’s books, popular music, New Age recordings, fantasy deity lists, role-playing games, anime, and mobile games. These generally personify the term as a male god or use it as atmospheric shorthand for “Native spirituality.”

Such works have shaped public recognition but have little evidentiary value for historical Lakota religion. Commercial feathers, dreamcatchers, “medicine wheels,” and mass-produced images labeled Wakan Tanka are likewise modern commodities unless made and contextualized by identifiable Native artists.

### Living Indigenous art

Artists such as Arthur Amiotte use collage, ledger-art references, family photographs, Catholic imagery, and Lakota cosmology to represent historical layering. This is contemporary Lakota religious and intellectual production, not a less authentic substitute for “ancient art.”

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Debate 1: One god or a sacred collectivity?

**Monotheistic reading:** Missionaries, some earlier anthropologists, Black Elk/Brown readers, and Åke Hultkrantz’s Supreme-Being framework have interpreted Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as a personal or delimited high god.

**Collective or relational reading:** Sword explicitly says the referent is like sixteen persons who are nevertheless one. DeMallie, Powers, and later scholars stress that *wakȟáŋ* is distributed across beings, objects, places, and relationships.

**Conclusion:** Both usages are historically real, but they belong to different speakers and periods. The evidence does not support projecting the fully personal Christian-like “Great Spirit” unchanged into pre-contact Lakota religion.

### Debate 2: How old is the sixteen-power system?

For antiquity:

- Sword and Finger regarded the teachings as old sacred knowledge.
- The system uses unquestionably Lakota beings and categories.
- Fourfold organization is deeply embedded in ritual.

Against treating it as an immemorial universal canon:

- no earlier source records the same sixteenfold table;
- the evidence comes largely through Walker and a small Oglala circle;
- Walker sought a coherent system and produced syntheses;
- later scholars often repeated Walker rather than obtaining independent confirmation.

The best classification is **a genuine Oglala priestly system documented around 1900, of uncertain earlier age and uncertain distribution**.

### Debate 3: What existed before 1880?

DeMallie’s position is methodological: sources earlier than the reservation and mission crisis are too thin to define a single doctrine. The safest reconstruction is not “Lakotas had no Wakan Tanka,” nor “Lakotas always worshipped one Great Spirit,” but that *wakȟáŋ* organized a plural sacred world whose later compression into a proper name was intensified by translation.

### Debate 4: What did Neihardt change?

Comparison with the transcripts demonstrates that Neihardt:

- condensed speech;
- omitted songs and ceremonial detail;
- rearranged material;
- heightened literary unity;
- universalized Black Elk’s vision;
- foregrounded tragedy and apparent failure.

But it does not follow that _Black Elk Speaks_ is fraudulent. It is a collaborative literary work based on real interviews. The transcripts permit readers to separate Black Elk’s recorded words, interpreter mediation, and Neihardt’s composition.

### Debate 5: Was Black Elk “really” traditional or Christian?

The binary is inadequate. Black Elk was a respected visionary and ritual practitioner, converted Catholic and catechist, and later expositor of the Pipe rites. His accounts show continuity, reinterpretation, and coexistence. Claims that he secretly rejected all Christianity or abandoned all Lakota tradition depend on late, disputed recollections.

### Debate 6: “Great Mystery” versus “Great Spirit”

“Great Mystery” is often the more cautious translation because it avoids assuming a separable spirit-person. Yet it, too, can become a New Age abstraction. Translation should be contextual:

- missionary Bible: “God” is historically what translators intended;
- Sword’s Tóbtob: “Great Sacred/Mysterious totality” is closer;
- contemporary prayer: the speaker’s own explanation should govern;
- popular deity list: “Great Spirit” is likely over-personalized.

### Archives and material evidence

Important repositories and editions include:

- Walker papers and edited volumes;
- American Museum of Natural History publications;
- National Anthropological Archives and Smithsonian holdings;
- Densmore’s recordings and transcriptions;
- Library of Congress digitized books;
- Neihardt interview transcripts as edited by DeMallie;
- winter counts in Smithsonian, tribal, and regional collections;
- tribal cultural-preservation programs.

Colonial collecting removed pipes, bundles, human remains, recordings, and ceremonial objects from communities. Repatriation under NAGPRA and community-led digital return projects are part of the present history of the evidence.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka was always a single male creator exactly equivalent to Yahweh: **unsupported**.
- All Native North American peoples worshipped the same “Great Spirit”: **false homogenization**.
- Walker recorded a universally accepted Lakota Bible: **unsupported**.
- The sixteen powers are Walker’s complete fabrication: **also unsupported**; Sword’s testimony is explicit.
- Wakan Tanka has a standard human appearance: **unsupported**.
- Lakota religion taught chakras, Atlantis, extraterrestrial Pleiadians, or European witchcraft: **modern invention unless presented explicitly as recent personal syncretism**.
- “Sioux” names a single homogeneous religion: **misleading**; Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota communities have regional and historical differences.
- Human victims were sacrificed to Wakan Tanka: **not established**.
- Lakota religion was legally secure after 1934 or even after AIRFA in 1978: **incorrect**; land access, eagle feathers, peyote, sacred objects, and state regulation remain contested.
