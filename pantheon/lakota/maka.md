# Maka: Research Dossier

**Class:** sacred personification and indwelling power of the Earth; in James R. Walker’s Oglala theological vocabulary, a female “Superior God” and one of the four primary manifestations of *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka*. In less systematized Lakota discourse, *makȟá* is simply earth, ground, or soil, addressed relationally as mother or grandmother rather than necessarily imagined as an anthropomorphic goddess.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the word belongs to the inherited Siouan lexicon, but its antiquity as a word cannot date a personified divine being. The earliest securely documented extended theology of Maka comes from Oglala testimony collected by physician-ethnographer James R. Walker at Pine Ridge between approximately 1896 and 1914, especially from George Sword, Thomas Tyon, Bad Wound, Finger, and other Oglala authorities. Parts appeared in Walker’s 1917 *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota*; the fuller creation cycle remained in manuscripts until Elaine Jahner’s edition, *Lakota Myth* (1983). Thus the earliest surviving record is late nineteenth-/early twentieth-century ethnography preserving an older oral tradition, not a precontact inscription or manuscript. Walker’s highly ordered pantheon may preserve genuine specialist Oglala teachings, but its literary and scholastic arrangement is partly Walker’s and must not automatically be projected unchanged into remote antiquity.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Name and language

The normalized New Lakota Dictionary spelling is **Makȟá**, conventionally written **Maka** in Walker, Densmore, Brown, Powers, and much popular literature. Its ordinary lexical range is “earth, ground, soil, land.” Comparative Siouan evidence connects Lakota *makȟá* with cognates meaning earth, soil, clay, or ground in other Siouan languages; consequently the name is not plausibly borrowed from Greek *Gaia*, Sanskrit, Akkadian, or another Old World divine name. Those comparisons are modern inventions or accidental resemblances, not historical etymologies. The Comparative Siouan Dictionary records Lakota *makhá* alongside such cognates as Osage forms for “earth, soil, clay, mud.”

The distinction between the following usages is essential:

- **makȟá:** earth, soil, ground, or the terrestrial world.
- **Makȟá:** Earth spoken of as a personal sacred power.
- **Makȟá Íŋa / Maka Ina:** “Mother Earth,” literally Earth-Mother or Mother Earth.
- **Uŋčí Makȟá / Unčí Maka:** “Grandmother Earth.”
- **Makȟá-akáŋl / Maka-akan:** a form appearing in modern summaries as “the spirit over/on the Earth” or “Earth spirit.” It is much less securely documented than plain *Maka* and should not be treated as the universally canonical name of a separate goddess.
- **“All-mother,” “mother of all,” “grandmother of all things”:** English renderings of explanatory and ceremonial statements, especially in Walker’s material.
- **“The Great God, the Earth”:** Walker’s English terminology for Earth’s potency in ritual.
- **“Grandmother”:** a frequent ritual address. Walker states that Oglala shamans “usually addressed the Goddess, the Earth, as Grandmother.”

The spelling **Maka Ina** circulates widely in songs and contemporary religious English. The underlying expression is legitimate Lakota, but not every modern song labeled “traditional Maka Ina song” has a demonstrable pre-1900 pedigree. A song’s language and traditional imagery do not themselves establish its age.

### Classification

- **Securely attested Oglala specialist teaching, recorded c. 1896–1914:** Maka is one of four primary *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka*: Wi, Škaŋ, Íŋyaŋ, and Maka. In one dialogue, the Sun/Moon, Škaŋ/Wind, Rock/Winged One, and Earth/Beautiful Woman form paired manifestations; all eight are nevertheless “only one,” a mystery known fully by shamans.
- **Walker’s learned systematization:** Maka is ranked third among the Superior Gods, after Wi and Škaŋ and before Íŋyaŋ, and is assigned the lands except mountains and high hills.
- **Broader ceremonial language:** Earth is Mother and Grandmother, the source, support, nourisher, receiver of bodies, and one of the relatives addressed through the pipe.
- **Modern popular taxonomy:** “Earth goddess.” This is usable shorthand, but “goddess” can falsely suggest an Olympian-style discrete anthropomorphic individual. “Sacred Earth power/personification” is safer.

There is no documented Lakota equivalent of Greek or Roman *interpretatio* identifying Maka historically with Gaia, Terra, Pachamama, Pṛthvī, or another named Earth deity. Modern spiritual writers sometimes place these figures in a universal “Earth Mother” category; that is comparative system-building, not an attested Lakota equation.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### What can and cannot be dated

No precontact Lakota writing system, inscriptional corpus, temple archive, coinage, or indigenous manuscript names Maka as a goddess. Oral antiquity may be considerable, but an oral narrative’s date of origin cannot be derived from the date of its first transcription.

The noun itself was recorded in nineteenth-century Dakota/Lakota lexicography and place-names. Such evidence proves the word “earth,” not necessarily Walker’s complete cosmogony. The personified theological record becomes secure in the ethnographic era.

### Walker corpus

James R. Walker served as agency physician at Pine Ridge. He began systematic collaboration with Oglala religious authorities in the late 1890s and worked under Clark Wissler’s anthropological encouragement after 1902. George Sword and Thomas Tyon were especially important collaborators; Walker reported in 1915 that both had died. A modern study places Sword’s death in 1911–12 and Tyon’s in 1914–15 according to the No Ears winter count.

#### *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota* (1917)

Language: Walker’s English, incorporating translated Lakota terms and statements.  
Physical form: printed paper in the American Museum of Natural History’s *Anthropological Papers*.  
Evidentiary tier: **secure early ethnographic attestation**, with the qualification that the English is mediated through Walker.

The text explicitly states:

- “There are four, Wi, Skan, Inyan, and Maka. These are the Wakan Tanka.”
- Earth is “grandmother of all things.”
- Earth and the Beautiful Woman are, in an esoteric sense, “the same.”
- In ritual, the potency of Earth may inhabit the excavated earthen altar.
- Earth, Rock, and Buffalo may be declared present together in the ceremonial lodge.
- Shamans address Earth as Grandmother.
- The altar is made by removing living and growing things, digging a square in the soil, pulverizing and replacing the earth, and consecrating it through song or invocation.

These statements establish Maka’s ceremonial presence more securely than later encyclopedia-style lists of “Sioux gods.”

#### *Lakota Belief and Ritual* (Walker; edited by Raymond J. DeMallie and Elaine Jahner, 1980)

This volume publishes Walker manuscripts, correspondence, ceremonial accounts, and translated teachings. It is indispensable for separating testimony attributed to named Oglala collaborators from Walker’s prose synthesis.

Evidentiary tier: **early ethnographic records in a modern critical edition**.

#### *Lakota Myth* (Walker; edited by Elaine Jahner, 1983)

This publishes the extended mythic material and Walker’s “literary cycle.” In its fullest creation sequence:

1. Íŋyaŋ exists within primordial darkness.
2. Seeking to use or manifest his power, he releases his blood.
3. The blood or substance becomes water and Earth; Íŋyaŋ becomes drained, hard, and comparatively powerless.
4. Maka is therefore materially derived from Íŋyaŋ and remains bound to Rock.
5. Released power becomes or contributes to Sky/Škaŋ.
6. Maka complains of darkness, cold, nakedness, or insufficient distinction, depending on the passage and recension.
7. Dawn and Sun are instituted; excessive heat then requires an ordered alternation of light and darkness.
8. Škaŋ assigns domains, colors, and ranks: green to Maka, terrestrial land to her, high hills and mountains to Íŋyaŋ.
9. Maka is ancestress of growing things and the material mother of terrestrial life.

Evidentiary tier: **Oglala material recorded early but heavily arranged as a literary and theological cycle**. Its exact narrative joins and systematic rankings should not be assumed to have been universally told by all Lakota communities in that form.

### Frances Densmore, *Teton Sioux Music* (1918)

Densmore’s Bureau of American Ethnology Bulletin 61 contains 240 transcribed songs, singers’ names, ceremonial explanations, photographs, and musical analysis. The Smithsonian preserves the publication and related manuscript transcriptions; the Library of Congress also supplies page images.

Densmore’s collection is crucial evidence for actual early twentieth-century song practice, but it does **not** provide independent proof for every modern song advertised as a “Maka Ina song.” In the accessible cataloguing and indexed material examined for this dossier, no securely identifiable standalone song entitled “Maka Ina” could be established. Earth occurs in song texts and prayers, but that is different from documenting a fixed ancient hymn under that modern title.

Evidentiary finding: **“Maka Ina in the songs” is real as a living mode of address, but a specific ancient canonical song-cycle under that title remains unestablished here.**

### Ella Cara Deloria, *Dakota Texts* (1932)

Deloria, a Yankton Dakota scholar and native speaker, published 63 bilingual or glossed narratives. More than half of her collected texts remained unpublished in the Boas Collection at the American Philosophical Society; Indiana University’s Lakota Texts project later transcribed and annotated selections.

Deloria’s corpus is essential comparative evidence because it shows that Dakota/Lakota sacred narratives exist as particular performances rather than as a single canon. It does not simply reproduce Walker’s ranked pantheon. This absence cautions against treating Walker’s system as a universal “Sioux creed.”

### Black Elk and his editors

#### *Black Elk Speaks* (John G. Neihardt, 1932)

Black Elk spoke Lakota; his son Ben translated; Enid Neihardt took shorthand; John Neihardt produced the literary book. Earth is fundamental to Black Elk’s vision—the sixth grandfather represents Earth and humankind—but the published prose is collaborative literature, not a verbatim transcript.

#### *The Sixth Grandfather* (edited by Raymond J. DeMallie, 1985)

DeMallie published the 1931 and 1944 interview transcripts and supplied comparison, historical notes, and analysis. The transcripts show that Neihardt generally sought to communicate Black Elk’s worldview but selected, rearranged, expanded, and omitted material. In particular, he muted Black Elk’s Catholic history to preserve a more exclusively “traditional” literary persona. Maka claims derived from *Black Elk Speaks* should therefore be checked against these transcripts wherever exact wording matters.

#### *The Sacred Pipe* (Black Elk and Joseph Epes Brown, 1953)

This is a separate collaboration, centered on the seven rites. It repeatedly calls Earth Mother and Grandmother. In the pipe narrative, the bowl is Earth; the wooden stem represents all that grows; feathers represent the winged peoples; smoking joins the peoples of the universe in prayer. Earth is sacred, every step upon her should be prayerful, human bodies come from and return to her, and she nourishes all beings.

Evidentiary tier: **mid-twentieth-century canonical elaboration grounded in Black Elk’s testimony but edited into Brown’s perennialist theological idiom**.

### Later testimony

- John Fire Lame Deer and Richard Erdoes, *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972): living Lakota religious and political testimony, but also a collaborative, literary, and sometimes deliberately provocative book rather than an unmediated transcript.
- William K. Powers, *Oglala Religion* (1977): participant-observation and analysis of continuing Oglala ceremonial life.
- DeMallie and Douglas Parks, eds., *Sioux Indian Religion* (1987): comparative historical and contemporary studies.
- D. M. Dooling, ed., *The Sons of the Wind* (1984/1987 editions): accessible retellings primarily derived from Walker’s papers; influential but later than the underlying manuscripts.
- Winter counts: invaluable for dating social, ceremonial, epidemic, and political events, but they do not furnish a continuous precontact “biography” of Maka.
- Arthur Amiotte’s writing and art: modern Lakota interpretation in which land, kinship, sacred geography, colonial rupture, and continuity remain interwoven.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Maka is Earth both as substance and as living relation:

- soil and land;
- the terrestrial world;
- support of humans, animals, and plants;
- fertility and growth;
- nourishment;
- the material source and eventual receiver of bodies;
- terrestrial sacred space;
- the earthen altar;
- healing through physical and spiritual contact with the land.

In Walker’s system, Škaŋ contributes movement, spirit, and ordering power while Maka supplies terrestrial substance and generative capacity. This division is a theological exposition, not a modern matter/spirit dualism that can safely be generalized to all Lakota thought.

### “Mother of all”

Walker calls Maka the ancestress or grandmother of growing things and of terrestrial beings. Black Elk/Brown describes her as both Mother and Grandmother: mother in active generation and nourishment, grandmother as the abiding ground and potentiality from which forms arise. Later Lakota teachers likewise speak of Earth as the first or real mother of all life.

This language is **securely attested**, but its English abstraction into the capitalized universal figure “Mother Earth” can obscure specific kinship terms and ceremonial contexts.

### Form and image

There is no securely established canonical anthropomorphic image of Maka. She is encountered in the land, soil, vegetation, stone-earth relation, and altar rather than chiefly represented by a statue.

Consequently:

- No ancient cult statue can be identified.
- No fixed face, dress, weapon, or animal attribute is documented.
- Modern images of a woman whose body becomes a landscape are contemporary artistic interpretations unless tied to a named Lakota artist and explanation.
- Generic “Native American Earth goddess” images sold online are usually modern commercial inventions.

### Colors and symbols

- **Green:** assigned to Maka in Walker’s literary cycle because she asks for beauty and becomes associated with growing things. This is an **early recorded specialist teaching**, not necessarily a universal Lakota color chart.
- **Earth/soil:** her direct material presence.
- **Circularity:** the encompassing world, camp circle, pipe, and sacred hoop; not Maka’s exclusive emblem.
- **Pipe bowl:** in Black Elk/Brown, the red stone bowl is Earth and includes the four-legged peoples.
- **Earthen altar:** the potency of Maka resides in the exposed and consecrated ground.
- **Plants and food:** products of Earth rather than exclusive heraldic attributes.
- **Buffalo:** profoundly related to land, food, ceremony, and the pipe but not simply “Maka’s sacred animal.”
- **No securely attested exclusive planet, weekday, number, weapon, or zodiacal correspondence.** Lists assigning such correspondences are modern occult constructions.

### Powers and limitations

Maka nourishes, supports, heals, receives offerings, bears growing beings, and can be ritually present in an altar. Walker’s mythic Maka also complains, asks, boasts, and can be deceived. She does not exercise unlimited sovereignty: Škaŋ adjudicates cosmic disputes and fixes domains; she remains bound to Íŋyaŋ; mountains and high hills belong to Rock in Walker’s apportionment.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin and kinship

#### Walker creation account

- **Source/origin:** Íŋyaŋ releases blood or substance from himself, producing Earth and water. Maka is therefore made “of” him.
- **Relation to Íŋyaŋ:** creator/created, source/derivative, and permanently joined Earth/Rock. Some later summaries call her his lover; that term is less secure than the underlying statements of origin and attachment.
- **Cosmic rank:** third of four Superior Gods in Walker’s ordered account.
- **Descendants:** ancestress of terrestrial and growing life, though Walker does not always present biological genealogies in a single consistent tree.
- **Equation:** Earth and Wohpe/Beautiful Woman are “the same” in one esoteric eightfold/fourfold explanation. This is a theological pairing or manifestation, not a narrated mother-daughter relationship.
- **Consort:** no universally attested husband should be assigned. Modern charts naming Father Sky as Maka’s fixed consort often conflate general Earth-Sky complementarity with particular Walker passages.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The making of Earth from Íŋyaŋ’s blood

**Earliest surviving source:** Walker’s Oglala materials, collected c. 1896–1914.

In the most familiar recension, primordial Íŋyaŋ possesses active, fluid power. To create or manifest a world, he opens himself or sends forth his blood. The outflow becomes water and the substance of Maka. Because the power has left his body, Íŋyaŋ shrivels and hardens into rock. The liberated power rises or participates in the formation of Sky/Škaŋ.

The myth explains several observable relations at once:

- water’s fluidity and blue color;
- rock’s hardness;
- soil’s attachment to stone;
- Earth’s derivative but generative power;
- the transfer of cosmic authority from primordial Rock to Škaŋ.

**Variants:** Some modern tellings say half the disk became land and half water; others make blue sky and green Earth emerge together; some say Íŋyaŋ deliberately creates Maka as part of himself, while others emphasize uncontrolled loss. These should not be conflated into a single original text.

**Historical caution:** “Blood sacrifice” is a defensible description of Íŋyaŋ’s mythic self-emptying, but there is no evidence that Lakota people ritually reenacted this by killing humans for Maka.

### Maka’s complaint of darkness, cold, and nakedness

Maka finds herself dark, cold, insufficiently adorned, or “naked.” Aŋp or Dawn is produced, but dawn’s red illumination lacks sufficient heat. Wi, the Sun, is therefore made or commissioned to give light and warmth. When unrelieved heat becomes intolerable, darkness and light are ordered into succession.

The variations matter:

- In some Walker-derived retellings, **Íŋyaŋ** creates Dawn and Sun.
- In others, **Škaŋ** performs the ordering after Íŋyaŋ’s power is spent.
- Some distinguish Aŋp, Wi, Haŋ, and Haŋwí carefully.
- Simplified modern versions collapse Dawn into Sun and Darkness into Moon.

The tale is etiological: it accounts for daylight, heat, night, seasonal growth, and the balanced succession of opposites. The “nakedness” motif explains vegetation and Earth’s green covering; it is not securely a separate pan-Lakota myth independent of the Walker cycle.

### Earth and Rock bound together

Maka wants independence or resents her dependence upon exhausted Íŋyaŋ. Škaŋ adjudicates and rules that Earth and Rock remain joined. The physical fact that soil rests on, surrounds, or adheres to stone becomes the visible continuation of the primordial judgment.

This is a strong example of why variants must remain separate: some versions make Maka an ungrateful or contentious figure; others omit that characterization and emphasize mutual constitution. The harsher characterization may reflect one storyteller’s dramatic theology or Walker’s literary shaping.

### The color green and the domains

In Walker’s ordered council, Maka complains that Sun is red and Škaŋ blue while Earth is drab. She receives green. Her jurisdiction is the land, while mountains and high hills belong to Íŋyaŋ. This creates a mythic taxonomy of terrain and color.

Tier: **learned Oglala systematization**, not demonstrably prehistoric universal dogma.

### The deceptive white fruits

Gnas persuades Maka that fruits from below the world can be planted above. They grow quickly as white, leafless, flowerless, seedless things. Maka invites the gods to a feast and boasts of her produce, but the fruits smell and taste vile. The episode displays deception, misplaced pride, and the difference between natural terrestrial growth and counterfeit production.

Tier: **Walker literary cycle**. Its identification with mushrooms is a reasonable interpretation, not always made explicit by the text.

### Earth in the pipe narrative

White Buffalo Calf Woman presents the pipe and explains that:

- the bowl is Earth;
- the buffalo image represents the four-legged peoples;
- the stem represents growing things;
- feathers represent winged beings;
- prayer through the pipe joins all relatives.

This narrative gives Earth a liturgical rather than chiefly dramatic role. Its best-known wording comes from Black Elk through Joseph Epes Brown in 1953. It should not be retroactively quoted as a verbatim ancient text, though it transmits Lakota teaching.

### Earth in Black Elk’s great vision

In the Neihardt tradition, the six grandfathers include the power of Earth and humankind. The sacred hoop, flowering tree, four directions, and restored land are inseparable. Earth is not a side character: the health of land and the health of the people mirror one another.

Because Neihardt shaped the book, exact literary sentences belong to the collaborative 1932 publication. The underlying visionary structure is supported by the interview transcripts published by DeMallie.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No temple cult or priestly bureaucracy

No freestanding temple dedicated solely to Maka, temple estate, hereditary “priestess of Maka,” divine statue, or centralized sacrificial economy is documented. Lakota sacred practice was and is embedded in kinship, camps, land, visions, bundles, pipes, medicine people, and collective ceremonies.

Calling the Black Hills “Maka’s principal temple” would therefore be metaphor, not an indigenous institutional category.

### Earthen altars

Walker’s 1917 account gives unusually concrete evidence. For an altar, vegetation and living material are removed from a square space. Soil is excavated, purified, pulverized, replaced, leveled, and consecrated. Buffalo skull, stone, paint, fire, smoke, and offerings create a meeting place of powers. Walker’s explanation says Earth’s potency is in the altar and Rock’s in its stone.

This is direct ritual treatment of Maka, even where no independent prayer to a goddess-statue occurs.

### Pipe rites

In *The Sacred Pipe*, Earth receives tobacco, prayer, and acknowledgment. The pipe bowl materially represents her. Participants pray while touching or directing the pipe toward the ground; bodies come from Earth and return to her.

Offerings commonly include tobacco, food, smoke, meat, cloth, and the physical disposition of ceremonial objects. It is misleading to isolate a “Maka cult” from the larger network of relations invoked.

### Sweat lodge

The sweat lodge is built on Earth, uses stones from Earth, and includes a central pit and path formed from soil. Black Elk’s account addresses Mother Earth directly during purification. The rite renews relations among Earth, stone, fire, water, breath, directions, and participants.

### The Sun Dance

Earth’s role in the Oglala Sun Dance includes:

- supporting the circular lodge;
- receiving the central pole;
- forming the altar;
- receiving dancers’ blood;
- sustaining the plant and animal relatives represented in the lodge;
- standing in complement with Sun, Sky, Buffalo, Rock, and the directions.

Walker explains piercing and bloodletting as demonstrations of sincerity, bravery, and sacrifice before the Sun and people. It is not evidence of human sacrifice to Maka: dancers endured wounds but were not killed as offerings.

The last “old-time” Rosebud Sun Dance is commonly dated to 1883. Federal officials suppressed the ceremony under the Courts of Indian Offenses regime. Some ceremonies continued privately or in attenuated form. Public revival occurred under John Collier’s reforms around 1934; Pine Ridge observance became regular again by the later 1950s.

### Ghost Dance, 1889–1890

The Ghost Dance reached Pine Ridge by late 1888 or early 1889 through letters and delegations connected to Wovoka’s movement. Lakota versions promised renewal, return of deceased relatives and buffalo, removal or transformation of colonial conditions, and restoration of the land. Earth therefore mattered profoundly as the place to be renewed.

However, the available Lakota Ghost Dance evidence does **not** establish Maka as a separate presiding goddess of the movement. Earth is danced upon, renewed, and reunited with the dead; that is not the same as a discrete “Maka cult.”

The movement divided Lakota communities and alarmed agents and the Army. On December 29, 1890, the U.S. Seventh Cavalry killed perhaps 250–300 Lakota men, women, and children at Wounded Knee. The massacre followed disarmament of Big Foot’s band and cannot responsibly be described as a ritual act or a battle between equal forces. Government representations of the Ghost Dance as military conspiracy were central to the violence.

### Human sacrifice, sexuality, and cult violence

- **Human sacrifice:** no credible evidence of humans killed as offerings to Maka.
- **Sun Dance piercing:** documented voluntary ritual suffering and bloodletting, not human sacrifice.
- **Temple prostitution:** inapplicable; no temples or supporting evidence.
- **Cult violence:** warfare could be prayed over within Lakota religion, but no evidence identifies a Maka-specific institution of sacred violence.
- **Political violence against the cult:** well documented in military conquest, land seizure, ceremony bans, reservation policing, boarding schools, and coercive Christianization.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before written documentation

The Lakota language and Earth-directed ceremonial relationships long predate Walker. Exact reconstruction is limited by the absence of precontact written texts. Archaeology can establish human occupation and material practices but cannot by itself label an object “Maka.”

### Nineteenth-century colonial transformation

U.S. expansion, destruction of buffalo herds, reservation confinement, the 1877 taking of the Black Hills, ration dependency, missionization, and compulsory education changed the circumstances in which Earth relations were practiced. The seizure of land was simultaneously material, political, and religious because land was not an interchangeable commodity.

The 1883 Code of Indian Offenses explicitly targeted “heathenish dances,” including the Sun Dance, and empowered reservation courts to punish religious practice. Later regulations renewed or tightened suppression.

### Ethnographic documentation, 1890s–1930s

This era produced the main textual archive:

- Walker’s Pine Ridge manuscripts;
- winter counts;
- Densmore’s recordings and musical transcriptions;
- Deloria’s texts;
- Neihardt’s interviews with Black Elk.

These records preserved knowledge while also translating it into Christian, anthropological, literary, and “mythological” categories. “God,” “goddess,” “Great Spirit,” and “pantheon” are therefore partly intercultural vocabulary.

### Reform and public revival, 1934 onward

Commissioner John Collier ended official Bureau opposition to Native ceremonies in 1934. Sun Dances resumed publicly, though piercing and other elements were sometimes omitted, altered, or contested. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 recognized a federal policy favoring protection of Native religious exercise, but it did not automatically restore sacred lands or guarantee access.

### Late twentieth-century revitalization

The 1960s–1970s brought renewed language, ceremonial, political, and land-based activism. John Fire Lame Deer and others linked religious renewal with the Black Hills claim and Indigenous sovereignty. Some Lakota practiced traditional rites alongside Catholicism, Episcopalianism, or the Native American Church; such combinations are lived religious histories, not necessarily a formal equation of Maka with the Virgin Mary or another Christian figure.

### Present

Maka remains a living relation in prayer, environmental ethics, healing, land defense, ceremony, education, and ordinary Lakota speech. Contemporary invocations such as *Uŋčí Makȟá* are not merely revivals of a dead goddess.

At the same time, living use cannot prove that every modern formulation is ancient. Statements framed through ecology, climate activism, legal testimony, or global Indigenous solidarity are modern historical developments, even when grounded in inherited relationships.

## 8. Sacred Geography

### Pahá Sápa / He Sápa, the Black Hills

The Black Hills are a living sacred geography, connected to emergence traditions, star knowledge, ceremony, origin, and national identity. “Heart of everything that is” is a widely used interpretive description. The Hills should not be reduced to a single sanctuary belonging exclusively to Maka; their sacred relations involve many beings, places, stories, and communities.

The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty recognized the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills. Congress took the Hills in 1877 after military pressure and the gold rush. In *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians* (1980), the Supreme Court held that the taking required compensation. Sioux nations have generally rejected monetary settlement in favor of land restoration.

### Matȟó Pahá / Bear Butte

South Dakota identifies Bear Butte as a sacred site used by multiple nations. Lakota people continue to fast, pray, seek visions, leave offerings, and undertake sacrifice there. It is a place of Earth relationship but not exclusively a “Maka temple.”

### Matȟó Thípila / Bear Lodge (“Devils Tower”)

The National Park Service recognizes Bear Lodge as sacred to the Lakota and many other nations and documents continuing prayer, fasting, vision quests, and offerings. Lakota names include Bear Lodge and related translations. “Devils Tower” derives from nineteenth-century colonial naming and is not a translation of Maka.

### Pe’ Šla and contemporary protection

Pe’ Šla is a ceremonial landscape in the central Black Hills associated with Oceti Sakowin star knowledge and renewal. Tribal and nonprofit acquisitions have restored some access, while mining and drilling proposals remain contested. Contemporary defense of such places often invokes Earth as mother and relative, but the disputes concern sovereignty, treaty law, water, ecology, and ceremony together.

## 9. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Black Elk’s international reception

*Black Elk Speaks* became especially influential after its 1960s republication. Readers in environmental, countercultural, Christian, perennialist, and New Age movements treated it as a universal ecological scripture. That reception helped make “Mother Earth” central to the popular image of Native spirituality, sometimes at the cost of Lakota linguistic and historical specificity.

Joseph Epes Brown’s *The Sacred Pipe* likewise gave non-Lakota audiences an integrated symbolic theology. Brown’s framing was influenced by perennialism. Its ritual material is important, but the book should not be read as an untouched transcript from antiquity.

### “Gaia” and universal Earth-goddess systems

Equating Maka with Gaia, Terra, Pachamama, or a Jungian Great Mother is a **modern comparative or psychological interpretation**. No historical channel of Lakota-Greek syncretism is documented.

Modern songs combining Lakota words with “Gaia,” global chakras, crystal healing, or generic goddess spirituality are new compositions unless a traceable Lakota ceremonial history is supplied. Denean’s “Gaia’s Prayer,” for example, uses *Uŋčí Maka* within a consciously modern world-spiritual musical setting; it is not an ancient Lakota hymn.

### Neopagan and occult material

Internet deity lists increasingly assign Maka:

- elemental correspondence “Earth”;
- green candles;
- Taurus or another zodiacal sign;
- planetary associations;
- ritual sigils;
- invocation formulas;
- equivalence with Gaia;
- solitary “devotional” festivals.

No such fixed system appears in Walker, Densmore, Deloria, or Black Elk. These are **modern inventions or modern eclectic practice**, not reconstructed Lakota antiquity.

No historical Maka tradition was found in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy’s foundational texts, or pre-twentieth-century European grimoires. Later Theosophical and New Age authors sometimes retell Walker-derived cosmology, but this is reception, not continuity.

### Popular media and commerce

Maka is less visible as a named game or film character than generic “Mother Earth.” Online mythology databases often copy one another, give her a fixed genealogy, or label her “Earth goddess and lover of Inyan” without distinguishing Walker from wider Lakota belief. Commercial prints depicting an anonymous Indigenous woman merged with a landscape are not evidence of traditional iconography.

### Political and environmental use

Lakota activists’ references to Maka or Uŋčí Makȟá in land and water defense are contemporary expressions of living tradition, not simply New Age borrowing. Congressional testimony has explicitly invoked “Unci Maka, Grandmother Earth” in arguments for religious liberty and sacred-land protection.

No documented white-nationalist or other extremist Maka cult emerged in the dedicated search. Generic appropriation of Indigenous environmental language occurs, but a distinct extremist use of Maka could not be established.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Walker’s pantheon authentically Lakota?

The false choice is “entirely authentic” versus “invented by Walker.” The evidence supports a more precise judgment:

1. Walker worked with named Oglala authorities.
2. Core concepts—Earth, Rock, Sun, Motion, *wakȟáŋ*, sacred directions, pipe, altar, Buffalo—are independently embedded in Lakota language and ceremony.
3. Walker translated them through English words such as “God,” “goddess,” “Supreme,” and “pantheon.”
4. He arranged disparate accounts into an unusually coherent literary cycle.
5. His collaborators were themselves intellectuals capable of theological systematization and were speaking amid missionization and colonial change.
6. Therefore the documents are primary evidence for turn-of-the-century Oglala religious thought, but not a transparent fossil of a single unchanged prehistoric creed.

### Mother or Grandmother?

Both are documented. They are not mutually exclusive names requiring harmonization. Usage changes with relational and ritual context. Brown’s explanatory distinction—mother as active producer, grandmother as underlying ground—is influential, but it should not be treated as a grammatical rule governing every speaker.

### “God,” “spirit,” or Earth itself?

All are imperfect English glosses. Walker’s “God” emphasizes personal sacred agency. “Spirit” can wrongly detach Maka from material land. “Personification” can imply that a nonliving object was merely imagined as human. “Earth power/person” best signals that land and sacred agency are not easily separable in the evidence.

### Neihardt’s alterations

DeMallie’s publication of the transcripts established that *Black Elk Speaks* is a shaped literary work. Neihardt:

- selected and reordered material;
- heightened literary symbolism;
- mediated Black Elk through Ben’s translation and Enid’s shorthand;
- omitted or minimized Catholic elements.

Nevertheless, DeMallie did not conclude that the book was wholesale fabrication. It remains evidence when used together with the transcripts.

### Primary evidence and archives

Important surviving bodies include:

- Walker papers and associated American Museum of Natural History publications;
- Densmore’s printed bulletin, manuscripts, and sound recordings at Smithsonian repositories and the Library of Congress;
- Deloria’s published texts and unpublished Boas Collection material at the American Philosophical Society;
- Neihardt interview transcripts and papers;
- winter counts held in museums, archives, and tribal contexts;
- continuing oral and ceremonial knowledge, some of which is not public and should not be treated as an extractable archive.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Maka is a universally standardized “Sioux goddess” with a fixed Olympian biography.
- *Makȟá-akáŋl* is unquestionably her single ancient full name.
- Maka had temples and professional priestesses.
- Lakota people practiced human sacrifice to her.
- Sun Dance piercing was human sacrifice.
- Maka was anciently equated with Gaia.
- Every modern “Maka Ina” song is an ancient traditional hymn.
- Walker’s ranked system is either a verbatim prehistoric catechism or entirely Walker’s fabrication.
- “Mother Earth” exhausts the meaning of actual land, kinship, jurisdiction, and ceremony.

## 11. Chronology

|---|---|
| **Undatable precontact period** | The Siouan word ancestral to Lakota *makȟá* and Earth-directed practices predate written documentation; no exact beginning can be assigned. |
| **Early–mid nineteenth century** | Dakota/Lakota lexicography and place-names document words for earth/ground; this proves the lexeme, not Walker’s complete divine genealogy. |
| **1851** | First Fort Laramie Treaty establishes territorial arrangements involving the Lakota and neighboring nations. |
| **1868** | Fort Laramie Treaty recognizes the Great Sioux Reservation and Lakota rights in the Black Hills region. |
| **1874–1877** | Gold expedition and invasion of the Black Hills; Congress takes the Hills in 1877 under coercive conditions. Sacred geography becomes a central treaty and religious issue. |
| **1883** | Federal Code of Indian Offenses targets the Sun Dance and other ceremonies; the last old-style Rosebud Sun Dance is conventionally dated to this year. |
| **1888/1889** | News of Wovoka’s Ghost Dance reaches Lakota reservations; the movement is adapted to Lakota hopes for restored people, buffalo, and land. |
| **December 29, 1890** | U.S. troops massacre Lakota people at Wounded Knee amid the suppression of the Ghost Dance. |
| **c. 1896–1914** | James R. Walker records Oglala religious testimony at Pine Ridge, including the earliest secure extended documentation of Maka as a Superior God and participant in creation. |
| **1911–1914** | Frances Densmore conducts major Teton/Lakota musical fieldwork, recording named singers and ceremonial contexts. |
| **1917** | Walker publishes *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota*. |
| **1918** | Densmore publishes *Teton Sioux Music*, BAE Bulletin 61. |
| **1931** | Black Elk gives his principal interviews to John Neihardt through Ben Black Elk’s translation. |
| **1932** | *Black Elk Speaks* and Ella Deloria’s *Dakota Texts* are published. |
| **1934** | John Collier’s policy change ends formal Bureau hostility toward Native ceremonies; major public ceremonial renewal follows. |
| **1944** | Neihardt conducts additional interviews later published in *The Sixth Grandfather*. |
| **1953** | Black Elk and Joseph Epes Brown publish *The Sacred Pipe*, making Earth-as-Mother/Grandmother and pipe symbolism internationally influential. |
| **Late 1950s onward** | Pine Ridge Sun Dance becomes regular again, with local variation over piercing, leadership, access, and ritual form. |
| **1960s–1970s** | Broad Lakota ceremonial, political, language, and land-rights revitalization; Mother Earth language enters global environmental and countercultural discourse. |
| **1972** | *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* publishes contemporary Lakota religious and political testimony. |
| **1977** | William K. Powers publishes *Oglala Religion*. |
| **1978** | American Indian Religious Freedom Act declares federal policy protecting Native religious practice, though enforcement and sacred-land protection remain limited. |
| **1980** | *Lakota Belief and Ritual*, edited by DeMallie and Jahner, publishes core Walker documents; the Supreme Court decides *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*. |

| **1990s–present** | Lakota educators, artists, ceremonial leaders, and land defenders continue to articulate relations with Uŋčí Makȟá while contesting commercialization and non-Native appropriation. |
| **Twenty-first century** | Bear Butte, Bear Lodge, Pe’ Šla, and the Black Hills remain sites of prayer, pilgrimage, land recovery, environmental conflict, mining disputes, and legal action. Maka remains a living relation rather than solely a mythological archive entry. |

## 12. Sources

### Primary and critical-source hierarchy

The central witnesses are Walker’s documents, Densmore’s recordings and publication, Deloria’s texts, Black Elk’s two differently mediated corpora, winter counts, and continuing Lakota testimony. None is neutral:

- Walker supplies the fullest named Maka narratives but the strongest systematic editing.
- Densmore supplies dated songs and singers but interprets them through early ethnomusicology.
- Deloria supplies linguistically rigorous Indigenous scholarship and narrative plurality.
- Neihardt supplies a powerful literary text whose alterations are documented.
- Brown supplies detailed ritual theology shaped by perennialist editing.
- Contemporary Lakota testimony establishes living use but does not automatically date every formulation to antiquity.

### Dedicated contested-material findings

Searches were conducted for Maka with etymology, origin, dispute, syncretism, sacrifice, suppression, occultism, and neopaganism. The resulting evidentiary conclusions are:

- **Etymology:** inherited Siouan “earth/soil”; no credible Old World derivation.
- **Origin dispute:** the extended blood-creation narrative is securely recorded only in the Walker corpus; its oral age is unknown.
- **Syncretism:** no ancient Gaia/Terra identification; universal Earth-goddess equations are modern.
- **Human sacrifice:** no evidence.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence and no applicable temple institution.
- **Suppression:** extensively documented through the 1883 Code, reservation enforcement, missionization, and schooling.
- **Occultism:** no historical role in Western ceremonial magic; modern correspondence systems are inventions.
- **Neopaganism:** individual eclectic devotion exists, but it is not evidence of Lakota authorization or historical continuity.
- **Extremist appropriation:** no Maka-specific movement established.
- **Modern invention:** fixed statues, sigils, zodiacal signs, feast days, and universal goddess genealogies lack support in the primary corpus.
