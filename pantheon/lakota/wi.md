# Wi: Research Dossier

> **Tradition and class:** In Lakota usage, **Wí** is the sun and, when personified in ceremonial speech and Oglala sacred narratives, a male **wakȟáŋ being**—a sacred or mysterious power. “God” is a convenient English gloss, but it should not imply a category exactly equivalent to a classical Mediterranean deity or the Christian God.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The word *wi* for the sun is securely documented in the Dakota-language missionary lexicographic record published by Stephen Return Riggs in **1852**. The earliest detailed documentary description located for the sun as a prayer-receiving, vision-giving being and addressee of the Sun Dance appears in S. R. Riggs’s account published in **1867**. The first extended Indigenous-language description of the ceremony is George Bushotter’s Teton Dakota text, collected in the late nineteenth century and published by James Owen Dorsey in **1894**. Wi’s elaborate rank as “chief of the gods,” his marriage to Haŋwí, and the Ite episode are most fully documented in the **Oglala corpus collected and partly systematized by James R. Walker at Pine Ridge, principally 1896–1914**, published from 1917 onward.
>
> **Evidence warning:** The celestial sun and its ritual address are considerably earlier and more broadly documented than Walker’s coherent divine genealogy. The latter is important Oglala testimony, especially that of George Sword and Walker’s other Lakota authorities, but it must not automatically be projected backward as a uniform, prehistoric “Sioux pantheon.”

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attestation]** Nineteenth-century language, ritual, pictorial, or testimonial evidence predating Walker’s synthesis.
- **[Walker-Oglala corpus]** Oglala accounts written or collected by James R. Walker and his Lakota collaborators, chiefly 1896–1914.
- **[Later canonical formulation]** A modern work that became especially influential in presenting Lakota religion, notably Black Elk’s published accounts.
- **[Regional variant]** Evidence particular to an Oglala, Sicangu, Hunkpapa, Dakota, or other community or narrator.
- **[Learned systematization]** A deliberately ordered theological synthesis, whether principally an Indigenous intellectual’s formulation, an ethnographer’s arrangement, or both.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically identifiable conjunction with Christian, Ghost Dance, Native American Church, or other religious language.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An analytical conclusion rather than an Indigenous narrative claim.
- **[Modern invention/unverified]** A claim circulating without support in the relevant primary corpus.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**[Early attestation]** *Wí* means “sun.” In the historical orthographies it appears as **Wi**, *wi*, or with diacritics omitted. Lakota commonly distinguishes the visible daytime sun as **Aŋpétu Wí**—literally “day sun”—from the moon, **Haŋwí**, “night sun,” also expanded as **Haŋhépi Wí**. Riggs’s later dictionary gives *anpetu-wi* as “the sun, literally day-sun,” distinguished from *haŋyetu-wi*, “night-sun” or moon. This lexical evidence establishes the word but does not by itself establish Walker’s divine biography.

In Standard Lakota Orthography:

- **Wí** — Sun.
- **Aŋpétu Wí** — Day-Sun; the solar body distinguished from the moon.
- **Haŋwí / Haŋhépi Wí** — Moon or Night-Sun; a distinct female being in the Walker-Oglala narratives.
- **Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi**, with historical spellings such as *wiwanyag wacipi* and *wiwaŋyaŋ wačipi* — “they dance gazing at the sun,” conventionally “Sun Dance.”

The spelling *Wi* should not be confused with English “we,” nor treated as an exotic proper name wholly separate from the ordinary Lakota noun. The sacred being and the perceptible sun share the term.

### Titles and status

**[Early attestation]** Riggs reported that the sun was addressed with the honorable title **Huŋkáyapi** in Dakota prayer. The precise social and ritual implications of this title require care: it belongs to the semantic field of the *huŋká* relationship and ceremonial adoption, not simply “Lord” in a European sense.

**[Walker-Oglala corpus; learned systematization]** In Walker’s presentation, Wi is:

- the Sun;
- male;
- one of the four **Superior Gods** or superior wakȟáŋ powers;
- “chief of the gods” in the feast narrative;
- husband of Haŋwí;
- father of Wóȟpe;
- the divine addressee most directly connected with the Sun Dance vow.

Walker’s ordered scheme places **Iŋyaŋ, Maka, Škáŋ, and Wí** among the superior powers and distinguishes associate or subordinate beings. This taxonomy is not independently attested in the same completeness in the earlier Dakota record. Ella Deloria and later William K. Powers questioned treating Walker’s arrangement as a transparent transcription of a single traditional creed. Raymond DeMallie and Elaine Jahner nevertheless showed that much of Walker’s archive preserves genuine Lakota texts and named Lakota intellectual contributions. The correct conclusion is not “Walker fabricated it,” but that his books combine testimony, translation, editorial arrangement, and—in *Lakota Myth* especially—literary synthesis.

### Etymology

**[Early attestation]** *Wí* is an inherited Siouan lexical term for a luminary, used in compounds for both sun and moon. No secure evidence was found that it derives from a personal name, from English, or from a hidden esoteric phrase.

**[Modern invention/unverified]** Internet claims that Wi has an occult numerical correspondence, an ancient transcontinental equivalent, a fixed “totem,” a zodiacal rulership, or a secret color are unsupported by the Lakota textual and ritual corpus reviewed here.

### Cross-cultural equations

No precolonial equation of Wi with Apollo, Helios, Ra, Mithra, or another Eurasian solar deity is documented. Such comparisons are modern comparative analogies, not Lakota identifications.

**[Syncretistic identification]** Christian missionaries translated Christian theological vocabulary into Dakota and Lakota, while Lakota Christians variously related the Christian creator to Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka. This did not yield a stable identification “Wi = Christ” or “Wi = God.” Walker’s highly ordered pantheon and some creation-story motifs have been suspected of Christian influence, but influence cannot be demonstrated equally for every component.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 1852: the lexical record

**[Early attestation]** Riggs’s *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language*, compiled with Dakota Mission colleagues and speakers and published by the Smithsonian in 1852, securely records the relevant solar vocabulary. It is linguistic evidence, produced in a missionary environment. It establishes the name’s nineteenth-century use but says little about genealogy.

### 1867: prayer, visions, and Sun Dance

**[Early attestation]** In the Minnesota Historical Society’s 1867 publication, Riggs states that Dakota people:

- appealed to the sun as witness;
- prayed to it;
- addressed it as *Huŋkáyapi*;
- received communications from it in dreams and visions;
- might vow to perform the Sun Dance in return for success in hunting or war.

The account’s openly missionary language—calling practitioners “unenlightened” and “deluded”—is the observer’s polemic, not Lakota self-description. The behavioral observations remain important when read against that bias.

### George Bushotter and Dorsey

**[Early attestation]** George Bushotter, a Teton Dakota/Lakota writer, supplied several Indigenous-language descriptions of the Sun Dance. James Owen Dorsey translated and arranged them for *A Study of Siouan Cults* (published in the Bureau of American Ethnology’s 1894 report). Dorsey explicitly says that the scattered texts were Bushotter’s but their order was his.

Bushotter explains the title as “Sun-looking-at they-dance.” People vowed during famine, epidemic, war, or need for horses and food to pray in the following summer. The vow was addressed through the ceremony to Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka and the sun. This is strong evidence that “Sun Dance” is not merely an outsider’s picturesque label, although reducing the whole ceremony to “solar worship” is equally inadequate.

### Walker’s archive, 1896–1914

**[Walker-Oglala corpus]** James R. Walker, physician at Pine Ridge, worked with more than thirty Oglala consultants. Especially important were:

- **George Sword**, an Oglala leader, ritual authority, literate Lakota author, and Walker’s principal theological teacher;
- **Bad Wound**;
- **Finger**;
- **Thomas Tyon**, writer and interpreter;
- **Little Wound**;
- **American Horse**;
- **Short Bull**, a Ghost Dance leader;
- other named ceremonial authorities.

Walker’s surviving manuscripts include Lakota, bilingual, translated, and English texts. Relevant publications are:

- “The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota” (1917);
- *Lakota Belief and Ritual*, edited by Raymond J. DeMallie and Elaine A. Jahner (1980);
- *Lakota Myth*, edited by Elaine A. Jahner (1983; later Nebraska edition);
- *The Sons of the Wind*, edited by D. M. Dooling (1984), presenting the Tate cycle while exposing Walker’s literary shaping.

The archive distinguishes two kinds of evidence too often conflated:

1. accounts attributed to individual Lakota narrators; and
2. Walker’s “literary cycle,” assembled into a continuous creation-and-emergence narrative.

Only the first can straightforwardly be cited as a particular person’s testimony. The second is evidence for Walker-era systematization and subsequent reception.

### Densmore

**[Early twentieth-century ethnographic corpus]** Frances Densmore conducted her principal Teton Sioux musical research in 1911–14 and published *Teton Sioux Music* as Bureau of American Ethnology Bulletin 61 in 1918. It documents 240 songs and ceremonial testimony, including:

- the White Buffalo Calf Pipe;
- spirit-keeping;
- the Sun Dance;
- cutting the sacred pole;
- fair-weather prayer;
- dancers’ and elders’ testimony.

Its musical transcriptions and named consultants are independent evidence that the Sun Dance was a complex communal offering, not simply the enactment of Walker’s divine family narrative. The surviving publication and recordings must also be read within the asymmetry of early ethnographic collecting. A Lakota-led Densmore Repatriation Project now reconnects the recordings and documentation with descendant communities.

### Ella Deloria

**[Regional and linguistic corpus]** Ella Cara Deloria, a Dakota/Lakota scholar working with Franz Boas, published “The Sun Dance of the Oglala Sioux” in 1929 and *Dakota Texts* in 1932. The latter contains more than sixty Lakota/Dakota narratives gathered at Pine Ridge, Rosebud, and Standing Rock. More than half her collected texts remained unpublished in the Boas Collection at the American Philosophical Society; an Indiana University project later transcribed and annotated a substantial selection.

Deloria’s work is crucial because it shows the multiplicity of ordinary narrative genres and because she did not endorse Walker’s complete theological system as a universally held Lakota canon. Her criticism particularly concerns the conversion of variable oral material into a seamless literary cycle.

### Black Elk’s accounts

#### *Black Elk Speaks* (1932)

**[Later canonical formulation]** Nicholas Black Elk spoke Lakota in interviews conducted by John G. Neihardt in 1931; Black Elk’s son Ben interpreted, and Neihardt’s daughter Enid made notes. Neihardt then composed *Black Elk Speaks*. The book’s great vision is indispensable evidence for Black Elk’s visionary world and twentieth-century Oglala history, but the published prose is not a verbatim transcript.

Raymond DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* (1984) published the interview materials and demonstrated Neihardt’s selection, compression, rearrangement, literary elevation, and occasional alteration. Neihardt emphasized tragedy and the apparent death of a people’s sacred tree more strongly than the transcripts warrant, and he minimized Black Elk’s later Catholic career. Therefore claims sourced only to Neihardt’s finished text should be labeled “Black Elk through Neihardt.”

#### *The Sacred Pipe* (1953)

**[Later canonical formulation]** Joseph Epes Brown’s *The Sacred Pipe* presents Black Elk’s account of seven rites. It calls the Sun Dance *wiwáŋyaŋg wačhípi*, “dance looking at the sun,” says that it came long after White Buffalo Calf Woman brought the pipe, and places it around the full moon in June or July. The ceremony addresses Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka through a total sacred relationship; the sun is a visible focus, witness, and channel, not necessarily the sole recipient.

The neat list of exactly seven rites became highly influential. It should be treated as Black Elk’s twentieth-century organization of Oglala ceremonial knowledge, mediated by Brown—not automatically as an immemorial enumerated canon.

### Lame Deer, Powers, DeMallie, Parks, and Amiotte

**[Modern Indigenous testimony and scholarship]**

- John (Fire) Lame Deer and Richard Erdoes’s *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972) conveys twentieth-century Miniconjou/Lakota ceremonial testimony through a collaborative literary form.
- William K. Powers’s *Oglala Religion* (1977) and related work emphasize local ceremonial practice, experience, and the dangers of reifying Walker’s pantheon.
- Raymond DeMallie and Douglas Parks’s *Sioux Indian Religion: Tradition and Innovation* (1987) foregrounds historical change and community diversity.
- Arthur Amiotte’s “The Lakota Sun Dance: Historical and Contemporary Perspectives” documents the ceremony as a living, changing Lakota institution rather than a fossilized survival.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

**[Early attestation]** Wi is first of all the physical sun: source of daylight and warmth, regulator of days and seasons, and an omnipresent witness. Prayer to the sun was associated with:

- hunting success;
- success or protection in war;
- relief from famine or epidemic;
- horses and food;
- the fulfillment of a vow;
- visions and sacred instruction.

**[Walker-Oglala corpus]** Wi illuminates the world and presides at the feast of divine beings. His authority is great but not absolute: Škáŋ can expose his misconduct, judge him, and reorder the movements of sun and moon. This limitation is important. “Chief” does not mean omnipotent creator.

### Form

Wi is predominantly anthropomorphic in Walker’s narratives—male, married, capable of desire, shame, feasting, speech, and judgment—while simultaneously being the visible sun. No securely identified precontact Lakota cult statue of Wi is known.

### Iconography

**[Early attestation]** Solar disks, circles, radiating forms, and painted sun signs occur in Plains pictography and ceremonial art. Identification must be contextual. A disk cannot automatically be catalogued as a “portrait of the god Wi.”

In Sun Dance settings, the sun’s presence is conveyed through:

- the dancers’ sustained gaze toward it;
- orientation and movement in the lodge;
- the central pole or tree;
- eagle-bone whistles;
- painted bodies and ritual equipment;
- buffalo imagery and the buffalo skull;
- the pipe and tobacco offering.

The central pole may connect earth, sky, people, buffalo, and the sacred powers. It is not simply an idol of Wi.

### Winter counts

**[Early attestation]** Lakota *waníyetu wówapi*, “winter counts,” are pictorial year records. A keeper selected a memorable event as the name and emblem of each winter; the image was a mnemonic cue for an oral historical account. Surviving counts cover eighteenth- and nineteenth-century sequences, although many extant supports are later copies on cloth or paper.

Sunlike circles can mark eclipses, unusual celestial appearances, extreme heat, ceremonies, or an individual/event described through a solar emblem. They are historical evidence for observation and mnemonic convention, but they do not independently prove Walker’s genealogy. The Rosebud Winter Count, probably collected at Rosebud, contains 136 pictographs covering approximately 1752–1887. Other counts memorialize the 1833 Leonid meteor storm as “the year the stars fell.” The lesson is methodological: each solar-looking glyph must be read with its keeper’s year-name and explanation.

### Associations not securely established

No pan-Lakota canonical evidence was found for a unique sacred plant, metal, gem, weekday, occult number, or zodiac sign belonging exclusively to Wi. Buffalo, eagle, pipe, tobacco, cottonwood, and the four directions are central to broader Lakota ceremonial relations, especially the Sun Dance; they are not necessarily Wi’s private “attributes.”

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing origin structures

**[Walker-Oglala corpus; learned systematization]** In one Walker creation arrangement, Iŋyaŋ’s self-expenditure and the activity of Škáŋ and Maka precede the ordered world; Wi is brought forth to provide light and warmth. Elsewhere Walker’s categorical scheme simply places Wi with Iŋyaŋ, Maka, and Škáŋ among the four superior gods. These are not necessarily identical propositions: one concerns narrative sequence, the other theological rank.

**[Regional variation]** Later summaries sometimes say Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka directly “created” four superior gods. That wording often turns a relational sacred totality into a creator-person and may reflect Christianized or popular systematization.

### Household

**[Walker-Oglala corpus]**

- **Spouse:** Haŋwí, the Moon/Night-Sun.
- **Daughter:** Wóȟpe, often glossed Falling Star or Beautiful One.
- **Would-be substitute/lover:** Ite, wife of Tate.
- **No secure sons of Wi are named** in the central Walker cycle.
- The Four Winds are the children of Tate and Ite, not of Wi.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The creation and ordering of light

**[Walker-Oglala corpus; learned systematization]** Walker’s literary creation cycle begins with primordial sacred powers and cosmic differentiation. Iŋyaŋ, Maka, and Škáŋ participate in the emergence or ordering of the world; Wi supplies light and heat. The exact sequence varies between Walker materials and later retellings.

The secure historical conclusion is limited: Oglala intellectuals around 1900 discussed Wi within a sophisticated relational cosmology. It is not secure that every Oglala—or all Lakota divisions—recited one fixed creation narrative.

### “When the People Laughed at Haŋwí”

**[Walker-Oglala corpus]** This is Wi’s fullest preserved divine drama.

Wazí and Kȟáŋka live below the world. Their beautiful daughter Ite is married to Tate and has borne the Four Winds. Iktómi exploits the older couple’s ambition and Ite’s pride. He gives or teaches a beauty charm and repeatedly suggests that Ite deserves a place among the gods. He separately tells Wi of her beauty.

At a feast of the gods, Ite occupies the place belonging to Haŋwí beside Wi. Haŋwí arrives and is humiliated before the assembled beings—hence the title “When the People Laughed at Haŋwí.” The breach is not merely adultery in a modern domestic sense; it overturns rank, kinship obligations, hospitality, and cosmic seating order.

Škáŋ judges the participants. Ite becomes **Aŋúŋg Ite**, Double-Face: one side beautiful, the other terrible. Her parents are punished, and Ite is separated from Tate and her children. Haŋwí and Wi no longer travel together as before. The moon’s changing face is explained through Haŋwí’s shame: she covers her face as she approaches her estranged husband and uncovers it as she moves away, or vice versa depending on the retelling.

#### Variants and development

- Some summaries describe an “affair”; Walker’s feast scene centers on seduction, wrongful seating, and public exposure.
- Some modern retellings make Ite wholly villainous. Walker’s cycle distributes responsibility among Iktómi, Ite, her parents, and Wi.
- Some versions make Škáŋ the unequivocal creator-judge; others use broader Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka language.
- Modern websites frequently turn Ite’s transformation into a simple beauty-and-jealousy fable. That strips away the kinship and cosmic-order dimensions.
- The story’s eclipse or lunar-phase interpretation is plausible as an etiological function, but it should not be asserted as the only meaning.

### Origin of the Sun Dance

There is no single origin myth.

**[Early attestation]** In the Riggs and Bushotter record, an individual promises the ceremony after a prayer or vision, often amid danger, illness, hunger, or warfare. The vow is fulfilled publicly the following summer.

**[Walker-Oglala corpus]** Thomas Tyon and other Walker contributors supplied origin accounts in which the ceremony is disclosed by sacred beings or buffalo and given for the people. Different ritual authorities emphasize different revelations.

**[Later canonical formulation]** Black Elk’s account makes the Sun Dance one of the rites anticipated when White Buffalo Calf Woman brought the pipe. It was revealed long after the pipe itself. The buffalo, pipe, sun, tree, dancers, kin, and Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka form one ritual field.

These versions need not be harmonized. One explains why a particular dancer vows; another why the people possess the rite; another situates it within a seven-rite theology.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No temple institution

There is no evidence for temples, permanent cult statues, a centralized priestly bureaucracy, or a tax-supported “church of Wi.” Lakota ceremonial life was mobile, kin-based, visionary, and organized through ritual specialists, sponsoring families, societies, and the camp circle.

### Sun Dance

The Lakota name means approximately “they dance gazing at the sun.” The English “Sun Dance” is conventional but incomplete.

Documented elements include:

- a vow prompted by danger, illness, grief, communal need, or a vision;
- selection and ritual cutting of a central tree;
- construction of a circular lodge;
- pipe prayer and offerings;
- fasting and thirst;
- sustained dancing and gazing;
- eagle-bone whistles;
- songs held by knowledgeable singers;
- buffalo symbolism;
- gifts and reciprocal obligations;
- flesh offering or piercing in some forms;
- prayer for relatives and the people.

### Piercing

**[Early attestation and continuing practice]** In the best-known nineteenth-century Oglala forms, skewers were inserted through folds of skin, commonly on the chest or back, and attached by thongs to the central pole or buffalo skulls. Dancers pulled until the flesh released or completed another prescribed ordeal.

Calling this “self-torture” reproduces the language of suppressing officials. Calling it “human sacrifice” without qualification is also misleading. It was a voluntary personal offering of flesh, endurance, prayer, and suffering—not the killing of a human victim. In some interpretations it is related to renewal, warrior discipline, kinship obligation, and Iŋyaŋ’s primordial self-expenditure. No securely documented Lakota sacrifice in which a person was killed as an offering to Wi was found.

Piercing was not necessarily required of every dancer in every period or community. During the early twentieth-century revival, ceremonies sometimes continued without it because of government restrictions. Later communities restored it under their own protocols.

### Ritual officials

Named roles vary by community and translation, but documented functions include:

- the pledger or sponsor;
- a ceremonial leader or holy person;
- singers and drum keepers;
- pole cutters and lodge builders;
- women and relatives responsible for food, gifts, and support;
- attendants who prepare and pierce dancers;
- pipe keepers and knowledgeable elders.

These offices were founded on demonstrated ceremonial knowledge, visions, kinship, apprenticeship, and authorization—not membership in a permanent “priest caste.”

### Offerings and economics

Offerings included tobacco, cloth, food, valued property, horses historically, bodily endurance, and gifts distributed to participants and relatives. The ceremony mobilized substantial labor and wealth. Gift exchange affirmed relationships rather than paying a fee to Wi.

### Sacred geography

- **Pahá Sápa / He Sápa, the Black Hills:** the sacred center of numerous Lakota narratives and contemporary ceremonial claims. The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty protected the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Hills; Congress imposed the 1877 taking after the gold rush. In *United States v. Sioux Nation* (1980), the Supreme Court held that compensation was owed. Sioux nations have declined the monetary award because acceptance would extinguish the land claim.
- **Matȟó Pahá, Bear Butte:** a living prayer and vision site. South Dakota’s park authority acknowledges its sacred status.
- **Matȟó Thípila, Bear Lodge/Devils Tower:** sacred to Lakota and numerous other Plains nations. National Park Service documentation connects it with Lakota Sun Dance tradition and White Buffalo Calf Woman. A voluntary June climbing closure seeks to accommodate contemporary ceremonies.
- These places belong to a broader sacred geography. They should not be reduced to “temples of Wi,” though solar prayer and summer ceremonies occur within it.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before sustained written documentation

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Sun-directed prayer and the Plains Sun Dance almost certainly predate their nineteenth-century descriptions. Comparative scholarship proposes that Sun Dance complexes spread and changed among Plains peoples during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. No securely dated precontact Lakota text names Wi as “chief of the superior gods.” Archaeological solar motifs cannot establish that title.

### 1850s–1883: documentary visibility and ceremonial florescence

Language work records *wí*; missionaries describe prayer and vows to the sun; Indigenous and non-Indigenous observers describe increasingly large summer ceremonies. During the reservation crisis, the Sun Dance remained a major means of communal renewal. Reports say more than forty dancers took part in the 1881 Pine Ridge ceremony.

### 1883: federal prohibition

On December 2, 1882, Interior Secretary Henry M. Teller instructed the Indian Office to suppress what he called “heathenish dances,” expressly including the Sun Dance. Commissioner Hiram Price’s March 30, 1883 rules for Courts of Indian Offenses authorized punishments including loss of rations, hard labor, fines, and imprisonment.

This was an administrative regime, not an act of Congress, and its reach and enforcement varied. Nevertheless it furnished agents with coercive machinery against Lakota religion. The last “old-time” Rosebud Sun Dance is commonly dated to 1883, but ceremonies, prayers, and knowledge did not simply disappear; some continued privately or under altered forms.

### 1889–1890: Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee

**[Syncretistic and intertribal movement]** Wovoka’s Ghost Dance originated among Northern Paiute people and spread rapidly. Lakota adopters transformed it according to their own visions, material practices, kinship, and expectations. Its growth occurred amid hunger, epidemics, ration cuts, land loss, and the prior suppression of ceremonies including the Sun Dance.

Ghost Dance shirts were sacred protective objects. The claim that every wearer understood them as mechanically “bulletproof” oversimplifies heterogeneous testimony; recent scholarship emphasizes existential and spiritual protection.

On November 20, 1890, President Benjamin Harrison ordered military action against the movement. Indian police killed Sitting Bull during an attempted arrest on December 15. On December 29 the Seventh Cavalry surrounded and attempted to disarm Spotted Elk’s Miniconjou band at Wounded Knee. Firing followed; soldiers killed at least 162 Lakota according to the National Park Service’s conservative named figures—84 men, 62 women, and 16 children—and likely more. This was not a mythic action by Wi, but it belongs to Wi’s historical dossier because suppression of Lakota ceremonial life, replacement and transformation of communal rites, and the catastrophic policing of Indigenous religion shaped subsequent practice.

### 1896–1918: salvage conditions and textual canon formation

Walker collected Oglala religious knowledge after the public Sun Dance had been suppressed. His consultants were consciously preserving, translating, explaining, and sometimes ordering knowledge for a non-Lakota recorder. The resulting image of Wi as chief of an articulated pantheon emerged under these historical conditions.

Densmore likewise recorded songs after coercive disruption. Her archive preserves otherwise endangered performances while also removing recordings and knowledge into federal collections.

### 1934–1958: return

Under Commissioner of Indian Affairs John Collier, federal policy changed during the 1930s. Public Sun Dances returned around 1934, often without piercing. Performance was intermittent. At Pine Ridge, annual continuity is generally dated from about 1958.

The 1934 date marks a public revival, not the resurrection of a religion that had been completely dead. Families had retained songs, prayers, pipes, visions, and ritual competence throughout prohibition.

### 1960s–1978: revitalization and legal protection

Lakota ceremonial revitalization accelerated amid broader Indigenous political movements. The American Indian Movement and traditional leaders drew renewed attention to treaty rights, sacred lands, and religious freedom. Congress enacted the American Indian Religious Freedom Act in 1978, declaring federal policy to protect Native religious exercise. AIRFA initially supplied limited judicial remedies, but it marked a formal reversal of suppression policy.

### 1980–present

Sun Dances are held in multiple Lakota communities under different leaders and protocols. Some admit invited non-Lakota supporters; others restrict participation. In 2003 Arvol Looking Horse, keeper of the Sacred White Buffalo Calf Pipe, called for protection of sacred ceremonies from unauthorized access and exploitation. Other Lakota ceremonial leaders have taken different positions. There is no central institution empowered to impose one practice on all Lakota communities.

Wi therefore persists not principally through temples or devotional images but through language, sunrise prayer, seasonal knowledge, pipe prayer, Sun Dance, oral narrative, and relationships with land and other sacred beings.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and scholarship

*Black Elk Speaks* became one of the most influential representations of Native spirituality in the twentieth century. Its success helped international readers encounter Oglala visionary thought, but also encouraged the mistaken idea that Neihardt’s literary voice was an unmediated transcript.

Walker’s works have similarly become recursive: material collected during suppression later aided Lakota cultural recovery and ceremonial teaching. That does not make the archive false; it means the archive became an actor in the tradition it documented.

### Art

Lakota artist and scholar Arthur Amiotte has treated the Sun Dance and cultural continuity through narrative art, collage, and scholarship. Contemporary Lakota art may use solar forms without presenting a standardized portrait of Wi.

### New Age and neopagan appropriation

**[Modern invention/unverified]** Popular deity directories sometimes detach Wi from Lakota language and community and assign:

- neopagan altar correspondences;
- crystals, tarot cards, planetary hours, or zodiac signs;
- universal “solar masculine” psychology;
- equivalence with Helios, Apollo, Ra, or Mithra;
- invented prayers;
- unrestricted self-initiation into Sun Dance practice.

None of those systems is documented in Riggs, Bushotter, Walker’s named Lakota texts, Densmore, Deloria, Black Elk, Powers, or DeMallie and Parks. They are modern eclectic constructions.

There is no documented Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or medieval European cult of Wi. Claims to the contrary are anachronistic.

### Political appropriation and sacred-site conflict

Lakota solar and ceremonial imagery appears in pan-Indian activism, tourism, sports branding, and commercial “Native spirituality.” The clearest political contexts are not abstract nationalism but treaty claims, protection of the Black Hills, access to Bear Butte and Bear Lodge, and resistance to mining or climbing during ceremonial periods.

Commercially staged “Sun Dances” and fee-based workshops led by persons without community authorization are not evidence of ancient practice. They are modern appropriation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Wi “chief of the gods”?

- **Evidence for:** Walker’s Oglala feast narratives and theological classification explicitly give Wi exceptionally high rank.
- **Qualification:** Earlier Dakota descriptions establish solar prayer and divine agency but not this complete hierarchy.
- **Assessment:** “Chief of the superior gods” is secure as a statement within the Walker-Oglala corpus; it is not secure as a timeless doctrine of every Lakota and Dakota community.

### Did Walker record or invent the pantheon?

Two extreme answers are inadequate.

- Walker did not invent every name, rite, and episode: Lakota contributors wrote and dictated substantial texts, and independent records corroborate major concepts and practices.
- Walker did arrange variable accounts into a coherent literary cycle. His Christian and Euro-American categories affected terms such as “god,” “supreme,” and “worship.”
- George Sword may himself have been an innovative Oglala theologian presenting sacred knowledge systematically for Walker. Indigenous systematization is still Indigenous intellectual history, but it is not the same as anonymous timeless consensus.
- Deloria’s skepticism and Powers’s practice-centered critique warn against treating Walker as scripture.
- DeMallie and Jahner’s editions allow individual documents and authors to be separated from Walker’s synthesis.

### Is the Sun Dance “sun worship”?

The answer depends on what “worship” means.

- Riggs and Dorsey explicitly describe prayer to the sun as a sacred being.
- The ceremony’s Lakota name explicitly includes gazing at the sun.
- Yet its ritual field includes Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka, the pipe, buffalo, tree, directions, earth, sky, kin, visions, vows, and communal renewal.
- Therefore “not related to solar reverence” is wrong, but “a cult devoted only to the sun god” is equally wrong.

### Was piercing human sacrifice?

It was a bodily sacrifice in the sense of voluntarily offering flesh, pain, blood, fasting, and endurance. It was not documented human sacrifice in the narrower sense of killing a person for Wi. Federal descriptions of “self-torture” were politically interested language used to justify prohibition.

### Christian influence

Possible Christian influence is strongest where:

- Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka is translated as a single omnipotent “Great Spirit”;
- creation stories acquire a biblical flood and rainbow structure;
- a neat hierarchy resembles a theological summa;
- missionary interpreters control English vocabulary.

But resemblance is not proof. Floods, sacred totalities, moral judgments, and hierarchical relations need not derive from Christianity. Each passage requires a transmission history.

### State of the evidence

Important holdings include:

- Walker manuscripts and photographs, substantially represented in published critical editions;
- Densmore recordings and papers in Smithsonian and Library of Congress collections;
- Ella Deloria’s unpublished texts in the Franz Boas collection at the American Philosophical Society;
- winter counts in the Smithsonian’s National Anthropological Archives and museums;
- oral and ceremonial knowledge retained by Lakota families, much of which is not public research material.

Absence from publication does not mean absence from the living tradition. Conversely, a modern claim cannot be called ancient merely because it is spiritually plausible.

## 10. Dedicated Contested-Material Audit

### Etymology

Secure: *wí* is the Lakota/Dakota luminary term.  
Disputed or unsupported: elaborate occult derivations, Indo-European cognates, or claims that it secretly means “male solar creator.”

### Origin

Secure: solar language and prayer are documented by the mid-nineteenth century.  
Uncertain: when a fully personified Wi narrative first emerged.  
Not established: a prehistoric date for Walker’s complete pantheon.

### Syncretism

Documented: Christian translation practices, the Ghost Dance, Native American Church participation, and some Lakota Catholic ceremonial conjunctions.  
Not established: a formal equation of Wi with Christ or the Christian Father.

### Sacrifice

Documented: fasting, thirst, flesh offerings, and piercing.  
Not found: killing a human victim for Wi; institutional temple prostitution; animal sacrifice conducted as a regular blood cult exclusively for Wi.

### Suppression

Securely documented: the 1882–83 federal directive and Courts of Indian Offenses; punishments; interrupted public performance; later revival.  
Qualification: suppression was uneven, and private continuity complicates any simple “ban until 1978” formula.

### Cult violence

No evidence was found of a priesthood of Wi conducting coercive violence. Violence central to the historical record came chiefly from colonial dispossession, policing, military action, and suppression of Lakota religion. Piercing was voluntary ritual offering, although individual experiences and social pressures cannot be reconstructed universally.

### Occult and neopagan material

No historical Lakota basis was found for ceremonial-magic seals, planetary tables, tarot correspondences, or eclectic invocations of Wi. These are modern inventions or cross-cultural adaptations and should be labeled as such.

## 12. Findings in Brief

Wi has two documentary histories that overlap but should not be collapsed.

The broader and earlier history is of **the sun as Wí**: named in the language, addressed in prayer, encountered in visions, invoked as witness, and gazed upon during a communal ceremony of vows and sacrifice. That history is securely visible by 1852–67 and in Indigenous-language testimony by the late nineteenth century.

The narrower history is of **Wi as the male chief of an ordered divine hierarchy**, husband of Haŋwí, father of Wóȟpe, and participant in Ite’s attempted displacement of the Moon. That identity is richly documented but principally in the Walker-Oglala corpus recorded around 1900. Its fullest continuous form reflects both Lakota intellectual testimony and Walker’s literary systematization.

The Sun Dance is genuinely solar but not reducible to a one-god solar cult. It joins the sun, Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka, pipe, buffalo, tree, directions, land, visions, kinship, bodily offering, and communal renewal. The United States attempted to suppress it through coercive administrative regulation beginning in 1883. Its later public return was a revitalization of retained ceremonial relationships, not the modern invention of a vanished religion.

Claims about Wi’s occult correspondences, Eurasian equivalents, sacrificial killings, temple organization, or universal “solar archetype” are unsupported. The strongest modern continuity lies instead in Lakota language, ceremonies governed by living communities, and struggles to protect the lands in which those relations are practiced.
