# Tate: Research Dossier

**Class:** *wakȟáŋ* spirit/divine power; specifically Wind personified. Walker renders Tate as an “immaterial God,” but “god” is his English classificatory term, not an exact equivalent of Lakota *wakȟáŋ*.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The word *tȟaté*, “wind,” is documented in nineteenth-century Dakota/Lakota lexicography, including Stephen R. Riggs’s 1852 dictionary tradition. Tate as a named, personified divine being is securely documented in James R. Walker’s Oglala materials collected at Pine Ridge between 1896 and 1914 and first published systematically in *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota* in 1917. The elaborate connected biography—husband of Ite, father of the Four Winds and Yumni, companion of Škáŋ, guardian of the spirit-road, and regulator of the year—must be treated as the Walker/Sword Oglala system, not as a demonstrably pan-Lakota canon of remote antiquity.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early documented]** Directly recorded in nineteenth- or early twentieth-century Lakota/Dakota testimony or practice.
- **[Walker system]** Found in Walker’s theological arrangement or literary cycle; partly based on Oglala consultants but editorially systematized.
- **[Independent traditional element]** Corroborated outside Walker’s continuous literary narrative.
- **[Regional variant]** Particular to an identifiable Lakota/Dakota community or narrator.
- **[Later Indigenous interpretation]** A later Lakota account, teaching, or adaptation.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An academic inference or analytical model.
- **[Modern retelling]** A modern literary or educational reshaping.
- **[Modern invention/unverified]** A claim presented as traditional but unsupported by the located documentary record.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

Tate is the personification of wind in the Oglala Lakota religious materials preserved by James R. Walker. In Walker’s 1917 theological exposition, Tate is an invisible, spirit-like being; the associate of Škáŋ, the Sky or cosmic power of motion; father of the directional winds; ruler of the year and seasons; and custodian of the entrance to the road taken by spirits after death. In the accompanying mythic cycle, he is the husband of Ite, later Aŋóg Ité, and the father of four elder wind-sons and the anomalously born whirlwind Yumni. **[Early documented/Walker system]**

The ordinary noun and divine name are not separate words. Modern standard Lakota writes **tȟaté**, “wind.” Walker normally printed **Tate**, without modern diacritics. This is therefore a transparent personification—“Wind”—rather than a personal name with a separate obscure etymology. The modern *New Lakota Dictionary* confirms *tȟaté* as the ordinary noun “wind”; forms such as *tȟaté-tȟáŋka* mean a strong or great wind. **[Secure linguistic evidence]**

### Documented names and titles

- **Tȟaté / Tate** — “Wind.” Modern standard and Walker spelling respectively.
- **Tatekan / Taté-kaŋ** — a ceremonial or esoteric form printed by Walker in material concerning the “four times four” powers. The exact morphology and orthography vary in older records; Walker explains that *-kan* forms belong to shamans’ special language. **[Early documented, specialist vocabulary]**
- **“The Wind”** — Walker’s normal English gloss.
- **“Associate of Škáŋ”** — Tate’s position in Walker’s four-by-four theological classification. **[Walker system]**
- **One component of the “Great Spirit” grouping** — Walker groups Škáŋ, Tate, Bear, and Ghost as four individualities treated as one “Great Spirit.” This should not be converted into a simple equation “Tate = the Great Spirit”; it belongs to Walker’s disputed sixteen-power schema. **[Walker system/learned systematization]**
- **Father of the Four Winds** — directly stated in Walker’s published doctrinal account. **[Early documented/Walker system]**
- **Keeper of the spirit-road entrance** — Walker’s doctrinal exposition says that Tate hides the entrance from humanity and admits or excludes spirits according to Škáŋ’s judgment. **[Early documented/Walker system]**
- **Governor of the fourth time** — the year, and therefore of the passage of the four seasons. **[Early documented/Walker system]**

No secure Lakota corpus located here supplies Tate with a set of independent local cult epithets comparable to the temple titles of a Mediterranean deity. Modern phrases such as “master of the four directions,” “lord of storms,” or “wind father” are explanatory English labels, not demonstrated historical Lakota titles.

### Classification

Walker distinguishes:

1. **Wakȟáŋ Aŋkáŋta**, “Superior” powers;
2. **Wakȟáŋ Kólaya**, “Associate” powers;
3. **Wakȟáŋ Kúya**, subordinate powers;
4. **Wakȟáŋlapi**, “god-like” or sacred-like beings.

Tate is placed among the four associates, paired with Škáŋ. His sons, collectively Tȟaté Tópa/Tȟaté Tób, belong to the subordinate group. Walker calls all of these “Gods,” but Lakota *wakȟáŋ* has a semantic range including sacred, powerful, mysterious, incomprehensible, or extraordinary. “Wind spirit” or “sacred power Wind” is therefore less misleading than assuming a Greco-Roman kind of god. **[Early documented terminology; Walker’s English translation is historically conditioned]**

There is no documented *interpretatio graeca*, Roman equation, or traditional identification with Aeolus, Boreas, or another Eurasian wind deity. Such comparisons are modern typology, not syncretism.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Linguistic attestation

Stephen R. Riggs’s *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language* appeared in 1852. It establishes the Dakota word *tate* for wind, but lexical attestation alone does not prove the whole later divine biography. Riggs worked principally with eastern Dakota dialects; later editions added Teton/Lakota material. **[Early lexical evidence; limited theological content]**

The modern spelling **tȟaté** reflects phonemic distinctions that Walker’s “Tate” does not show. The form is Indigenous and lexical; proposed exotic etymologies or derivations from European names have no basis.

### Walker’s collection, 1896–1914

James R. Walker was physician at Pine Ridge from 1896 to 1914. His principal religious consultants included George Sword, Bad Wound, Finger, Thomas Tyon, No Flesh, Ringing Shield, Little Wound, and other Oglala specialists. Walker’s papers contain three different evidentiary kinds that must not be collapsed:

- texts or explanations attributed to named Lakota speakers;
- Walker’s own ethnographic/doctrinal summaries;
- Walker’s deliberately composed literary cycle constructed from the material he had gathered.

The first two can preserve direct Oglala testimony, although mediated by translation, transcription, questioning, and Walker’s categories. The third is expressly a literary synthesis. The 1983/2006 edition *Lakota Myth* includes both source narratives and “Walker’s Literary Cycle,” and identifies them separately. **[Primary collection plus editorial reconstruction]**

### *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies* (1917)

Walker’s 1917 monograph in the American Museum of Natural History’s *Anthropological Papers* is the earliest securely published extensive description of Tate. It states:

- Tate is invisible and spirit-like;
- he is father of the Four Winds by Aŋóg Ité;
- he governs the year and four seasons;
- he guards the entrance of the spirit-road;
- he acts according to Škáŋ’s judgment;
- sacrifice and ceremony cannot influence him, and his potency cannot be transferred to an object.

The same work classifies Tate as Škáŋ’s associate and gives the Four Winds ritual precedence and a fixed order of address: west, north, east, south. **[Early documented/Walker system]**

Because Walker’s system is highly regular—four classes of four powers, grouped again into four composite aspects of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka—scholars have questioned how far the arrangement reproduces a shared pre-reservation theology and how far it reflects George Sword’s philosophical synthesis, Christian catechetical habits, Walker’s questions, or Walker’s own system-building.

### *Lakota Belief and Ritual* (1980)

Edited by Raymond J. DeMallie and Elaine A. Jahner, this volume publishes Walker’s rawer papers and named testimony. Its particular value is that it permits comparison of individual speakers instead of presenting one harmonized “Lakota creed.” George Sword’s “Foundations,” material on secret knowledge, ritual directions, spirit terminology, and ceremonial practice supply the historical matrix in which Walker placed Tate. **[Primary testimony in a modern critical edition]**

### *Lakota Myth* (1983; reissued 2006)

Elaine Jahner’s edition contains:

- “A Myth of the Lakotas as It Is Told in Their Winter Camps,” attributed to George Sword;
- “The Feast by Tate”;
- “Wooing Wohpe”;
- “The Pte Cycle”;
- “The Founding of the Four Directions”;
- Walker’s own introductory comments and literary cycle.

The table of contents itself makes clear that Tate’s material is dispersed through different documentary strata. George Sword’s winter-camp account is not identical in status to Walker’s continuous epic. **[Early narrated material plus Walker literary synthesis]**

### *The Sons of the Wind* (1984; later edition 2000)

D. M. Dooling selected and reshaped Walker materials into a compact continuous narrative: “The First Two Times,” “Tate and Ite,” “The Four Directions,” and “The Coming of the Ikce Oyate.” It has strongly influenced popular presentations of Lakota mythology.

Dooling’s book is not an independent nineteenth-century oral source. It is a **modern literary retelling of Walker’s already mediated corpus**, omitting much of the edition’s source-critical apparatus. Its smooth narrative should not be cited as proof that every episode circulated together in that form before Walker. **[Modern retelling]**

### Ella Deloria

Ella Cara Deloria’s *Dakota Texts* (1932) is a major bilingual corpus, principally Dakota rather than Oglala Lakota. It does not independently reproduce Walker’s entire Tate epic. More importantly, in correspondence with Franz Boas in 1938–39, Deloria and her consultants criticized Walker’s mythology as displaying the systematic workmanship of a European mind and challenged parts of its supposed traditionality.

Elaine Jahner later argued more cautiously that George Sword may himself have created or recomposed explanatory narratives from traditional motifs—particularly Tate and his sons—to make implicit Lakota spatial and social symbolism explicit. Thus the choice is not simply “authentic Indigenous myth” versus “Walker fabrication.” Possible stages include traditional motifs, Sword’s philosophically and Christian-inflected recomposition, Walker’s transcription and expansion, and later editorial condensation. **[Contested transmission]**

### Black Elk materials

John G. Neihardt’s *Black Elk Speaks* (1932), Joseph Epes Brown’s *The Sacred Pipe* (1953), and DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* (1984) are indispensable for Oglala religious history but do not provide an independent full Tate biography equivalent to Walker’s cycle.

*The Sixth Grandfather*, based on the stenographic transcripts behind *Black Elk Speaks*, demonstrates that Neihardt selected, reordered, interpreted, and sometimes elevated Black Elk’s account into his own literary work. This warning applies by analogy to Dooling and Walker, although the editorial histories are different. Black Elk’s vision prominently organizes powers by directions, colors, horses, weather, and the sacred hoop; that does not by itself authenticate every named genealogical relation in Walker’s Tate system. **[Independent Oglala cosmological evidence; no full independent Tate cycle]**

### Densmore, Powers, DeMallie and Parks, and Lame Deer

- Frances Densmore’s *Teton Sioux Music* (1918) records songs and ceremonial contexts in which directions, winds, weather, and sacred powers are important, but it is not an independent biography of Tate. **[Contextual primary documentation]**
- William K. Powers’s *Oglala Religion* (1977/1982) discusses Tate, the Four Winds, time, space, and Walker’s theology but treats Oglala religion as a continuing social and ritual system, not a fossilized “pantheon.” Powers is skeptical of treating Walker’s ordered metaphysics as an uncontested creed. **[Modern scholarship]**
- Raymond DeMallie and Douglas Parks’s *Sioux Indian Religion* situates such accounts within diverse Dakota/Lakota/Nakota traditions and the history of ethnographic mediation. **[Modern scholarship]**
- *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972), mediated by Richard Erdoes, testifies to living Lakota sacred experience and colonial disruption. It does not independently establish the whole Tate genealogy. **[Later Indigenous testimony with editorial mediation]**

### Winter counts

Lakota winter counts are pictographic annual histories, not systematic divine genealogies. They are vital evidence for epidemics, battles, astronomical events, religious movements, and colonial disruption, but no located winter-count entry securely supplies Tate’s complete mythic dossier. Their absence from this role is significant: the Walker cycle should not be described as though it were chronologically recorded in winter counts. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and functions

In Walker’s published doctrine Tate governs:

- wind itself;
- the “fourth time,” the year;
- the arrival and departure of the four seasons;
- the directional winds through his sons;
- access to the spirit-road after Škáŋ’s judgment.

In the literary cycle, he also functions as a patriarch and organizer. He commands his sons to establish the world’s spatial quarters and their circuit measures the year. **[Walker system]**

A distinction is necessary between Tate and Tȟaté Tópa, the Four Winds. Tate is their father; the sons directly occupy and express the cardinal directions, control weather, circle the world, and receive ritual address. Modern summaries often transfer all their functions to Tate and call him “master of the four directions.” That is a simplification.

### Form and visibility

Walker says Tate is immaterial and never visible, “as a Spirit.” Consequently:

- no stable anthropomorphic form is documented;
- no canonical face, costume, weapon, or sacred animal is assigned to him;
- he does not appear in vision as a fixed embodied figure in Walker’s account;
- no securely identified pre-reservation image of Tate is known from the consulted record.

Images on modern mythology websites are imaginative illustrations, not ancient or traditional portraits. **[Modern invention if presented as canonical iconography]**

### Symbols

Tate’s strongest symbolic associations are indirect:

- the circle or circuit of the year;
- breath and moving air;
- fourfold directionality through his sons;
- the lodge and its directional seating in the founding narrative;
- the entrance to the spirit-road.

Walker assigns symbolic colors to certain superior beings but supplies no comparably secure exclusive color for Tate. Contemporary charts that give him a universal color, crystal, zodiacal sign, weekday, or “elemental correspondence” are unsupported by the historical Lakota sources located here. **[Modern invention/unverified]**

### Power and limitations

Walker explicitly says Tate cannot be influenced by sacrifice or ceremony and cannot impart his potency to an object. That description argues against imagining an independent Tate cult with transferable talismans. His actions remain subordinate to or coordinated with Škáŋ: he admits spirits according to Škáŋ’s judgment and acts as Škáŋ’s associate. **[Early documented/Walker system]**

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Genealogical versions

#### Walker’s doctrinal and literary version

- **Creator/companion relation:** Škáŋ creates or appoints Tate as his associate and messenger/agent. Some passages emphasize creation; the 1917 classification says most powers except the Four Winds had no beginning, creating an internal theological tension. **[Walker system; internally non-literal classification]**
- **Wife:** Ite, daughter of Wazi and Wakanka in the Pte/Buffalo people. After her attempted union with the Sun and judgment by Škáŋ, she becomes Aŋóg Ité, Double Face. **[Walker/Sword cycle]**
- **Children:** Yata, Eya, Yanpa, and Okaga, born together; and the later anomalously born Yumni, Whirlwind. **[Walker system]**
- **Grandparents of the winds:** Wazi/Waziya and Wakanka through Ite. **[Walker system]**
- **Later household relation:** Wohpe comes among Tate’s family and becomes associated particularly with Okaga; she teaches Yumni games and courtship. **[Walker cycle]**

The sources do not establish a biological parent for Tate in the ordinary genealogical sense. Statements on popular sites that Tate is simply “son of Škáŋ” flatten the more ambiguous creator-associate relationship.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Škáŋ — companion and subordinate executor:** Tate is Škáŋ’s associate; Škáŋ’s message directs Tate to send his sons to establish the directions, and Tate guards the spirit-road according to Škáŋ’s judgments. Walker 1917; Walker’s literary cycle, collected 1896–1914 and published in *Lakota Myth*. **[Early documented/Walker system]**
- **Ite/Aŋóg Ité — marriage and separation:** Tate marries Ite and fathers the winds. Her attempted elevation through the Sun’s seat brings judgment; she becomes Double Face, is separated from Tate, and her children cease to know her as mother. Walker narratives and literary cycle. **[Walker/Sword cycle]**
- **Yata — paternal command:** Tate sends the first-born northern wind to help establish directions; Yata later loses the senior birthright because of his disposition. *Founding of the Four Directions* and Walker 1917. **[Walker system]**
- **Eya — paternal command and transferred seniority:** Tate sends Eya westward; Eya receives the first-born precedence displaced from Yata. Same sources. **[Walker system]**
- **Yanpa — paternal command:** Tate sends Yanpa to the eastern quarter, associated with the place where the Sun begins its journey. Same sources. **[Walker system]**
- **Okaga — paternal command:** Tate sends Okaga to the southern quarter, beneath the Sun’s midday pause; Okaga’s lodge later houses Wohpe and Yumni. Same sources. **[Walker system]**
- **Yumni — father and anomalously born child:** Yumni emerges after Ite’s punishment, remains little, and is not counted among the four directional sons. Walker 1917. **[Walker system]**
- **Wohpe — messenger and guest:** Wohpe, appearing as a falling star and carrying Škáŋ’s message, comes to Tate; Tate later holds a feast when she stays with him. “The Feast by Tate,” “Wooing Wohpe,” and the founding cycle in *Lakota Myth*. **[Walker/Sword narrative complex]**
- **Wi, the Sun — opposed purposes:** Walker’s doctrine says the Sun’s will prevails “though the Wind thwart his purposes.” In the Ite episode, Tate is the wronged husband when Ite attempts to take the Moon’s place beside Wi. Walker 1917 and literary cycle. **[Walker system]**
- **Haŋwí/Wí-wiŋ, the Moon — linked through Ite’s transgression:** Tate and Moon are not direct antagonists, but Ite’s attempt to occupy Moon’s place disrupts both marriages and results in cosmic judgment. Walker cycle. **[Walker system; indirect interaction]**
- **Wakíŋyaŋ, the Winged/Thunder power — joint ceremonial status:** Tate and Wakíŋyaŋ appear together in the classified sacred order; Tate’s son Eya assists Wakíŋyaŋ in cleansing the world. No direct Tate–Wakíŋyaŋ narrative encounter was located. **[Co-classification only; absence of a direct episode]**
- **Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka — constituent relation:** Walker’s schema treats Tate as one individuality within the sixteenfold Great Mysterious. This is theological inclusion, not a meeting between two anthropomorphic characters. Walker 1917. **[Learned/Walker systematization]**
- **Nági, the spirit of a dead person — gatekeeper:** Tate admits or excludes the spirit from the spirit-road after Škáŋ’s judgment. Walker 1917. **[Walker system]**
- **Síčhuŋ/wandering spirit — delayed admission:** A rejected spirit wanders until Tate deems it fit to pass. Walker 1917. **[Walker system]**
- **Taku Wakȟáŋ, the sacred powers collectively — host:** Tate gives a feast for the sacred powers when Wohpe comes to stay; the guest list includes Wi, Haŋwí, Škáŋ, Iŋyaŋ, and others. “The Feast by Tate.” **[Recorded narrative/Walker corpus]**
- **Iktomi — no securely documented direct Tate encounter:** Iktomi engineers Ite’s attempted liaison with the Sun, gravely affecting Tate, but the located text does not require a face-to-face Tate–Iktomi scene. **[Indirect action; absence of direct interaction]**
- **Wazi/Waziya and Wakanka — affinal relationship:** They are Ite’s parents and thus Tate’s parents-in-law; their conspiracy produces the crisis in his household. A direct encounter with Tate is not securely isolated. **[Walker genealogy/indirect interaction]**
- **Iŋyaŋ, Maka, Tatanka, Hunonpa — joint appearance at Tate’s feast or in the classified sacred order:** These are documented co-presences, not evidence of separate personal adventures. **[Walker system/narrative co-appearance]**

No secure interaction was found between Tate and White Buffalo Calf Woman as a distinct figure, Black Elk’s Six Grandfathers, Wovoka, or a Christian saint. Modern charts that merge Wohpe with White Buffalo Calf Woman create a relation not uniformly present in the early sources.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The marriage of Tate and Ite

In the Walker/Sword complex, Ite is the beautiful daughter of Wazi and Wakanka among the Pte people. Tate marries her, connecting sacred powers and the Buffalo people. They have four sons at one birth. **[Walker/Sword cycle]**

Iktomi and Ite’s parents conspire to raise her status. She attempts to occupy the Moon’s place beside the Sun. Škáŋ judges the offenders. Ite is transformed into Aŋóg Ité, with one beautiful and one terrible face; her relation to Tate is broken, and her unborn child becomes the permanently little Yumni. **[Walker/Sword cycle]**

This episode explains several later conditions: separation of Sun and Moon, Double Woman’s frightening visionary form, disruption of Tate’s household, and Yumni’s anomalous status. It should not be universalized as the single Lakota account of cosmic origins; its fullest continuous form belongs to Walker’s corpus.

### Founding the four directions and the year

At first the world lacks fixed directions. Tate lives in a round lodge with his four elder sons and little Yum. A place in the lodge corresponds to each son, but cosmic space itself remains unordered.

Wohpe falls or descends as a star carrying Škáŋ’s command: Tate must send the four elder sons around the world to establish the directions. The sons travel to the world’s margins. Yata is associated with the north, Eya with the west, Yanpa with the east, and Okaga with the south. Their placements correspond to stellar and solar observations: northern circumpolar stars, sunset or the western mountain, sunrise in the east, and the Sun’s midday position toward the south. **[George Sword/Walker material, expanded in Walker’s literary cycle]**

Their complete circuit takes twelve moons. On the last evening of the twelfth moon they return to the first established point, completing the year. Tate decrees that the directional winds will continue circling the world. Thus space and annual time arise together. Walker’s 1917 doctrinal account compresses the result: Tate governs the fourth time, the year, while the Four Winds’ circuit marks it. **[Early documented motif; literary elaboration in Walker]**

A modern geographical study has attempted to map the brothers’ journey onto Plains geography and archaeological “medicine wheels.” That is an explicitly modern reconstruction, not an interpretation demonstrated by Walker’s consultants.

### The Feast by Tate

When Wohpe comes to remain with Tate, he consults his sons and gives a feast to the sacred powers. Wi, Haŋwí, Škáŋ, and Iŋyaŋ receive precedence as Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka; the narrative arranges the powers and explains ritual hierarchy. **[Recorded Walker-corpus narrative]**

The story’s conspicuously classificatory function supports two readings: it may preserve sacred-order teaching in narrative form, or it may reflect Sword’s and Walker’s efforts to turn flexible relations into a systematic theology. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

### Wooing Wohpe

Tate’s sons become involved in the competition or ordering surrounding Wohpe. Yata desires her, but Okaga becomes her companion. Yumni remains in Okaga’s lodge and learns games, contests, courtship, and related powers from Wohpe. **[Walker corpus]**

The narrative explains differences among the winds: cold, contentious Yata; pleasing southern Okaga; forceful western Eya; and less active eastern Yanpa. It also embeds social themes—birth order, household position, courtship, hospitality, and the directional arrangement of a lodge.

### Spirit-road judgment

This is more doctrine than developed tale. Škáŋ judges a deceased person’s spirit; Tate controls the entrance to the road leading to the spirit world. Worthy spirits enter with their endowed spirit-properties; rejected spirits wander until Tate permits passage. **[Walker 1917 doctrinal account]**

No independent funerary hymn or archaeological object securely depicting “Tate’s gate” was located. The claim is textual, not material.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No independent temple cult

There is no evidence for:

- a temple dedicated solely to Tate;
- a permanent priesthood of Tate;
- a cult statue or canonical icon;
- a Tate-specific pilgrimage center;
- an exclusive annual festival;
- a distinct animal or human sacrificial cult.

Walker expressly says sacrifice and ceremony cannot influence Tate. It is therefore misleading to reconstruct a temple-style “cult of Tate.” **[Documented negative finding]**

### Directional rites

Tate’s sons are much more directly present in ceremony. Walker prescribes that a lighted pipe be offered toward the four directions in the order west, north, east, south. The square altar’s sides face the four winds; the directional powers receive equal consideration. The Four Winds are invoked after Wohpe and have high ceremonial precedence. **[Early documented ritual instruction]**

These rites attest sacred wind-directionality, not necessarily direct worship of Tate as an individual personality. Tate supplies the genealogical and cosmological explanation for the directional winds.

### Sun Dance

Walker’s Sun Dance description integrates the Four Winds into altar orientation, camp organization, invocations, and petitions for favorable weather. Before the dance the leader may offer the pipe to the Four Winds and ask for a clear, temperate day. Tate himself is not the principal recipient.

The 1883 federal Code of Indian Offenses classified the Sun Dance and related ceremonies as “Indian offenses,” punishable by withheld rations and imprisonment. This was coercive suppression, not evidence that Lakota ceremonial life voluntarily disappeared. A Smithsonian manuscript recorded in 1894 describes an Oglala Sun Dance performed in 1883 by Little Wound’s band, demonstrating practice at the moment federal suppression was being formalized. **[Documented colonial history]**

Ceremonies continued, changed, went underground, or were publicly revived. Administrative hostility eased under John Collier in the 1930s; the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 formally recognized a federal policy of protecting Indigenous religious exercise, although it did not automatically resolve access to sacred land or all legal restrictions.

### Human sacrifice and “temple prostitution”

The dedicated search found no evidence that Tate received human sacrifice or that a Tate cult practiced ritual or “temple” prostitution. The absence should be stated, not filled with analogy to unrelated ancient wind gods.

Lakota Sun Dance piercing was condemned by federal officials as “self-torture,” but it is not human sacrifice to Tate. Accounts of warfare, sacrifice, flesh offerings, or sexual danger elsewhere in Lakota ethnography should not be transferred to him without a source. **[Contested-material finding: unsupported]**

### Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee

The Ghost Dance reached Lakota communities in 1890 and was adapted under leaders including Kicking Bear and Short Bull. Walker recorded Short Bull’s testimony. The movement’s doctrines, songs, and hopes belong to a distinct historical revelation originating with the Northern Paiute prophet Wovoka; they are not an episode in the Tate myth.

Federal alarm over the movement culminated in military intervention, Sitting Bull’s killing, and the massacre of Lakota men, women, and children by the Seventh Cavalry at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890. This violence formed the immediate historical background of Walker’s later Pine Ridge collecting. Tate is not securely documented as the Ghost Dance’s presiding deity. The coincidence of “whirlwind” language in modern book titles must not be treated as a theological identification. **[Documented historical context; no direct Tate role established]**

### Sacred places

The Black Hills (*Pahá Sápa/He Sápa*), Bear Butte (*Matȟó Pahá*), Bear Lodge/Devils Tower, Wind Cave, and other places remain central to Lakota sacred geography and religious exercise. Wind Cave is identified in a contemporary National Park Service presentation as *Makȟá Oníya*, “the earth breathing,” and its emergence narrative includes Ite as the wife of Tate.

These places are not documented as “temples of Tate.” Their relevance lies in living sacred geography, emergence traditions, vision seeking, prayer, offerings, and the movements of wind, weather, animals, and stars. Bear Butte continues to receive prayer cloths and tobacco offerings. Bear Lodge remains a site of ceremonies and contested recreational access. **[Living Indigenous tradition; no exclusive Tate cult]**

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the documentary record

Wind, breath, weather, four-direction symbolism, and circular spatial organization are deeply embedded in Lakota life, but the age of each specific Tate narrative cannot be fixed. Oral antiquity cannot be dated merely by calling a story “timeless.” **[Undatable traditional background]**

### 1852–1890: lexical and ethnographic visibility

Missionary linguists recorded *tate* as “wind.” Dakota and Lakota writers and ethnographers began producing extensive texts. Colonial warfare, reservation confinement, buffalo destruction, missionization, and federal pressure transformed the setting in which religious knowledge was transmitted.

No pre-1890 document located here gives the complete later Walker biography. **[Documentary limit]**

### 1890–1914: crisis and Walker’s collecting

The Ghost Dance crisis and Wounded Knee preceded Walker’s tenure at Pine Ridge. From 1896 to 1914, he worked with Oglala holy men who were preserving and explaining knowledge under assimilationist conditions. George Sword was both a Lakota intellectual and an Episcopalian catechist. His accounts may therefore preserve traditional structures while also employing Christian and systematic explanatory forms. **[Early documented transmission under colonial pressure]**

### 1917: first major publication

Walker’s *Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies* publicly fixed Tate’s place in a sixteenfold theological system. It also converted flexible Lakota categories into English labels such as “God,” “Great Spirit,” “Creator,” and “Executive.” **[Publication and systematization]**

### 1917–1970s: suppression, adaptation, and partial revival

Public ceremonies were restricted while Christianity and the Native American Church became important alongside continuing Lakota practices. Tate remained more visible in ethnographic texts and directional ritual than as an independently invoked cult personality. Sun Dances and other ceremonies returned publicly in changing forms.

### 1980–1984: critical republication

DeMallie and Jahner’s editions restored named consultants, manuscript strata, and editorial context. *Lakota Myth* exposed the difference between primary narratives and Walker’s literary composition. *The Sixth Grandfather* likewise made editorial intervention in *Black Elk Speaks* visible. **[Modern scholarly correction]**

Dooling’s *The Sons of the Wind* simultaneously made Tate’s cycle accessible to a broad audience but re-created a smooth sacred epic. Its cultural influence is real; its form remains modern.

### Late twentieth century to present

Lakota religious life has continued and revived through family teaching, Sun Dance communities, pipe ceremonies, language work, sacred-site protection, and land-return movements. Contemporary presentation at Wind Cave repeats a Tate–Ite emergence narrative, showing that the story now participates in public Indigenous heritage.

This does not prove uninterrupted universal transmission of Walker’s entire literary cycle. Continuity exists at several levels: the Lakota language word *tȟaté*; wind and directional sacredness; stories preserved by Oglala narrators; and modern reappropriation of published records.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literary and educational reception

Dooling’s *The Sons of the Wind* is the most consequential modern retelling. It is frequently cited online as though it were a verbatim ancient scripture. In fact, it is a twentieth-century selection and arrangement of Walker material. **[Modern retelling]**

National Park Service and educational programs now present parts of the emergence and direction narratives for broad audiences. These are public heritage interpretations, sometimes developed with tribal consultation, rather than evidence of an ancient fixed text.

### Neopagan, occult, and commercial use

The contested-material search found scattered internet lists describing Tate as a “wind god,” “master of the four directions,” or Native American elemental deity. Some sites attach generic candles, crystals, magical correspondences, offerings, or comparative wind gods. No historical Lakota source was found for a Tate-centered Western occult cult, Renaissance Hermetic identification, Golden Dawn correspondence, chaos-magic sigil tradition, or established neopagan denomination.

Accordingly:

- Tate’s inclusion in generic “pantheon lists” is a modern classificatory practice.
- Claims of canonical colors, gemstones, zodiac signs, incense, or altar designs are **modern inventions unless independently sourced**.
- Comparative equations with Aeolus, Fujin, Vayu, or Gaoh are typological comparisons, not traditional Lakota syncretisms.
- Commercial “Native deity” merchandise should not be treated as evidence of living Lakota ritual.

### Political and nationalist appropriation

No documented extremist appropriation specifically centered on Tate was located. Broader misuse of Indigenous spirituality—uncredentialed “shamans,” pay-to-pray ceremonies, and decontextualized medicine-wheel systems—is well documented as a modern phenomenon, but assigning all such practices to Tate would be unsourced.

Conversely, contemporary defense of the Black Hills, Bear Butte, Bear Lodge, and other sacred places is not “neopagan revival” in the ordinary Euro-American sense. It is Indigenous religious, political, legal, and territorial continuity.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The Walker problem

The central debate is not whether Tate “really existed,” but who composed the surviving form of his mythology.

1. **Traditional-core position:** Walker preserved teachings from knowledgeable Oglala men, especially George Sword, at a moment when those teachings were endangered.
2. **Systematization critique:** Walker imposed a rigid, comparative-religion structure on diverse beliefs and translated *wakȟáŋ* beings as a pantheon of “gods.”
3. **Sword-as-author argument:** Sword himself philosophically recomposed traditional motifs into explanatory narratives, influenced in part by his work as a Christian catechist.
4. **Layered-production model:** Traditional language and motifs, individual Lakota interpretation, colonial dialogue, translation, Walker’s literary composition, and later editing all contributed.

The layered model best fits the surviving record. It neither dismisses the material as fabrication nor treats Walker’s final prose as an untouched pre-contact scripture.

### Ella Deloria and Elaine Jahner

Deloria’s criticism is often paraphrased too simply as “Walker invented it.” Jahner notes that Deloria’s evidence also suggests Lakota storytellers could creatively use traditional plots and motifs. Jahner interprets Tate’s lodge and sons as condensed symbols of spatial order, social relations, and the tipi’s organization. George Sword may have “made up” an explanatory story in the sense of composing with culturally meaningful materials—not inventing every constituent from nothing. **[Scholarly debate]**

### Christian influence

Sword’s Episcopalian commitments and Walker’s concepts of “Great Spirit,” divine hierarchy, judgment, and theological classification raise legitimate questions of Christian framing. Yet resemblance is not proof that every element is borrowed. Directional winds, lodge orientation, sacred circles, kinship symbolism, and differentiated powers have Indigenous contexts. Each element requires separate examination.

### Primary evidence and archives

Walker’s original papers are associated with the Colorado Historical Society/History Colorado collections; critical selections appear in the University of Nebraska editions. American Museum publications preserve his 1917 text. Smithsonian archives contain extensive Dakota/Lakota linguistic and ethnographic manuscripts, including George Bushotter materials and the 1894 account of the 1883 Oglala Sun Dance.

The evidence is overwhelmingly textual and ethnographic. No ancient inscription, temple archive, securely identified cult statue, or pre-contact manuscript names Tate. That difference from literate ancient pantheons is a property of the source record, not evidence that the traditions lacked antiquity.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

- **“Tate is the universally supreme Lakota god.”** Unsupported; even Walker makes him one associate power.
- **“Tate created the four directions alone.”** Overcompressed; his sons establish them under Škáŋ’s directive and Tate’s command.
- **“The four winds are merely Tate’s aspects.”** Walker treats them collectively as one power but genealogically as four sons distinct from Tate.
- **“Yumni is one of the four cardinal winds.”** Incorrect in Walker; he is the little fifth son, Whirlwind.
- **“Walker transcribed a single ancient Lakota Bible.”** Incorrect; his corpus contains different narrators, raw documents, summaries, and his own literary cycle.
- **“Dooling is an independent traditional source.”** Incorrect; Dooling retells Walker.
- **“Tate had temples, idols, sacrifices, sacred prostitutes, or an occult priesthood.”** No supporting evidence located.
- **“Black Elk independently confirms every Tate episode.”** Unsupported.
- **“Tate is the deity of the Ghost Dance.”** Unsupported.
- **Fixed gemstone, chakra, zodiacal, tarot, planetary, or European elemental correspondences.** Modern invention unless separately documented.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Before the nineteenth century | Wind, directionality, weather, circular organization, and oral sacred narratives existed in Lakota/Dakota life, but particular Tate episodes cannot be securely dated. |
| 1852 | Riggs’s Dakota grammar and dictionary tradition documents the language lexically, including *tate*, wind; it does not furnish Walker’s complete divine biography. |
| 1883 | The federal Code of Indian Offenses targets the Sun Dance and related ceremonies, beginning an especially explicit phase of coercive religious suppression. |
| 1883 | Little Wound’s Oglala band performs a Sun Dance later described in a Smithsonian manuscript recorded in 1894. |
| 1888–90 | Dakota writer George Bushotter produces a large body of Dakota-language ethnographic texts; these demonstrate Indigenous textual scholarship but do not yield a securely identified complete Tate cycle in the material located here. |
| 1890 | Lakota adoption of the Ghost Dance; federal military intervention culminates in the Wounded Knee massacre on December 29. No direct role for Tate in the movement is securely attested. |
| 1896–1914 | Walker gathers religious, ritual, linguistic, and mythic materials at Pine Ridge from George Sword and other Oglala authorities. This is the earliest secure documentary stratum for Tate’s developed theology. |
| 1913 | Densmore’s fieldwork appears as *Teton Sioux Music*, preserving ceremonial songs and contexts involving directions, weather, and sacred powers. |
| 1917 | Walker publishes *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies*, including the first extensive systematic account of Tate, the Four Winds, and the sixteen powers. |
| 1918 | The full Bureau of American Ethnology edition of Densmore’s *Teton Sioux Music* circulates widely. |
| 1932 | Ella Deloria publishes *Dakota Texts*; Neihardt publishes *Black Elk Speaks*. Neither is an independent full Tate epic. |
| 1934 | Federal Indian policy becomes less overtly hostile to traditional ceremonial practice under John Collier; public revivals expand, though restrictions and interference continue. |
| 1938–39 | Deloria’s correspondence with Boas criticizes Walker’s systematic mythology and reports Indigenous doubts about parts of it. |
| 1953 | Joseph Epes Brown publishes Black Elk’s *The Sacred Pipe*, documenting seven Oglala rites but not independently establishing Walker’s entire Tate genealogy. |
| 1972 | *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* presents living Lakota religious testimony through Lame Deer and Richard Erdoes. |
| 1977/1982 | Powers’s *Oglala Religion* analyzes ritual continuity, space, time, and identity while challenging simple acceptance of Walker’s theological system. |
| 1978 | American Indian Religious Freedom Act declares federal protection for Indigenous religious exercise; sacred-site access remains contested. |
| 1980 | DeMallie and Jahner publish *Lakota Belief and Ritual*, restoring named narrators and manuscript layers. |
| 1983 | Jahner publishes *Lakota Myth*, explicitly separating traditional narratives, consultant materials, and Walker’s literary cycle. |
| 1984 | DeMallie publishes *The Sixth Grandfather*, exposing Neihardt’s reshaping of Black Elk’s testimony; Dooling publishes *The Sons of the Wind*, a popular continuous retelling of Walker. |
| 2000/2006 | New editions of Dooling and Walker broaden public access and substantially increase Tate’s internet-era visibility. |
| Twenty-first century | Lakota language revitalization standardizes the spelling *tȟaté*. Public heritage interpretation at Wind Cave presents Tate and Ite in the emergence narrative. Ceremonies and sacred-site use continue at the Black Hills, Bear Butte, Bear Lodge, and elsewhere. |
| 2024 | A Native-led organization purchases land adjacent to Bear Butte, illustrating continuing efforts to restore practical access to sacred landscapes. |
| 2026 | Tate circulates widely in online “pantheon” catalogs; their claims frequently derive from Walker through Dooling, often without acknowledging the contested transmission. |
