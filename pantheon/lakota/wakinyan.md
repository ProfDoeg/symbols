# The Wakinyan (Thunder Beings): Research Dossier

> **Traditional class:** a collective or race of powerful *wakȟáŋ* (“sacred, mysterious, incomprehensible”) spirit beings, commonly manifested as great winged beings and as thunder, lightning, storm, and rain. Some accounts speak of one great Wakíŋyaŋ, others of four primary manifestations, and still others of numerous old and young Thunder Beings. “God,” “Thunderbird,” and “monster” are imperfect English classifications.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Mary Henderson Eastman’s *Dahcotah; or, Life and Legends of the Sioux around Fort Snelling* (1849), which names **Wakinyan, “Man of the West,”** and includes “Unktahe and the Thunder Bird.” Frank Blackwell Mayer’s diary from 1851 independently records Dakota explanations of thunder birds, while Stephen R. Riggs’s *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language* (1852), based on Dakota Mission linguistic work begun in 1834, supplies an early printed lexical record. These are the earliest presently located documentary attestations, not the beginning of the tradition.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

The Wakíŋyaŋ are the Thunder Beings of Dakota and Lakota religious tradition: powers associated especially with the west, storms, lightning, rain, warfare, purification, visionary revelation, and the ceremonial vocation of the *heyókȟa*. The plural English “Thunder Beings” often represents them better than the singular “Thunderbird.” Historical sources disagree about whether they constitute a single being, four differentiated forms, an elder with numerous offspring, or a populous class. That disagreement should not be resolved artificially: plurality and instability of visible form are recurrent features of the evidence.

### Names

- **Wakíŋyaŋ** is the modern standardized Lakota spelling; older ethnographies print *Wakinyan*, *Wakinyan*, *Wah-keen-yon*, *Wahkeah*, *Waukheon*, and similar forms.
- The securely supported etymology is from **wa-** plus **kiŋyáŋ**, “to fly”: “that which flies,” “the flyer,” or “flying one.” Gideon Pond explicitly said the name signifies “flyer.” The modern expansion “sacred wing” or “sacred flying being,” derived by separating the word into *wakȟáŋ* plus “wing/fly,” is popular but linguistically less secure.
- “Thunderbird” is an English comparative label. Lakota sources also translate the name as **Thunder Being**, **Winged One**, or **Flying One**. “Bird” describes a prominent manifestation; it should not be taken as an exhaustive biological classification.
- **Wakíŋyaŋ Tȟáŋka**, “Great/Big Thunder Being,” appears in nineteenth-century descriptions as the father or chief of the Thunder Beings.
- **Man of the West** is Eastman’s 1849 rendering and reflects the being’s directional association rather than a stable personal name.
- Thunder is described in older Dakota material as the being’s **voice**; lightning is the opening or flashing of its eyes.
- George Sword’s Oglala theological materials, recorded by James R. Walker around 1896–1914, classify Wakíŋyaŋ as Inyan’s active associate and messenger. This placement belongs to Walker’s highly systematized Oglala corpus and must not automatically be imposed on all Dakota or Lakota communities.

### Classification over time

- **Nineteenth-century Dakota accounts:** a class of “gods,” “thunder gods,” or great birds. “God” reflects missionary-era English terminology.
- **Walker’s Oglala system:** an Associate God or *wakȟáŋ* power, created by Inyan and participating in a structured divine hierarchy.
- **Ritual and autobiographical accounts:** conscious Thunder Beings capable of calling, teaching, warning, punishing, and empowering human dreamers.
- **Modern Lakota usage:** sacred spirit-beings whose manifestations are thunder and lightning. Many speakers prefer “Thunder Beings” to the pan-Indigenous commercial image of “the Thunderbird.”

No Lakota evidence equates Wakíŋyaŋ historically with Zeus, Jupiter, Thor, Indra, or another Eurasian thunder god. Such comparisons are modern comparative mythology, not Lakota syncretistic identifications.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest documentation

**Secure early attestation — 1849.** Eastman’s *Dahcotah*, written after seven years around Fort Snelling, lists “Wakinyan, or Man of the West” and contains a story titled “Storms in Life and Nature; or, Unktahe and the Thunder Bird.” It establishes by 1849 the western association, the thunder-bird manifestation, and opposition to the water power Unktahe. Eastman wrote in English and mediated Dakota narratives through a settler-military household, so her wording is not a verbatim Dakota transcript. The digitized volume confirms both the date and contents ([Eastman, 1849](https://preview.wellcomecollection.org/works/r4bnyqa7/items?canvas=17&page=3&shouldScrollToCanvas=true)).

**Secure early attestation — 1851.** Frank Blackwell Mayer recorded that thunder was caused by one or two great birds, “Wahkeah,” whose flight or voice produced thunder. The later scholarly edition notes Gideon Pond’s account of four Wakinyan and their war with two Unktehi. This is independent evidence that plurality varied even within mid-century testimony ([Mayer diary](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/lhbum/17122/17122.pdf)).

**Secure linguistic attestation — 1852.** Riggs’s *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language*, assembled with Dakota Mission collaborators from vocabulary collected over eighteen years, is the first major printed Dakota lexicon. It is a linguistic artifact produced in a missionary project, not a neutral indigenous theological treatise ([Smithsonian record](https://repository.si.edu/items/607ec97e-1d61-4472-987f-1a2799595147)).

**Expanded nineteenth-century ethnography.** Gideon H. Pond’s “Dakota Superstitions and Dakota Gods,” later printed in the *Minnesota Historical Society Collections*, gives the earliest extensive classification: three or four outward varieties, numerous members of each race, old and young Thunder Beings, the western dwelling, weapons, war functions, and perpetual conflict with Unktehi. Pond’s term “superstitions” records his missionary judgment, not the Dakota classification.

**Comparative compilation — 1894.** James Owen Dorsey’s *A Study of Siouan Cults* collates Pond, Riggs, de Smet, and other reports. It records Thunder Being–Unktehi combats, lightning weapons, war paint, sacrificial offerings, spring observances, and Wakinyan songs sung by the Heyoka order. It is valuable as a source-preserving compilation but frequently merges material from different Sioux divisions and even neighboring Siouan peoples ([Dorsey scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AA_study_of_Siouan_cults_%28IA_studyofsiouancul00dors%29.pdf); [relevant text](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7c0fedc9-7dfe-4fd0-be48-1bebff8f7fcf/content)).

### Walker’s Oglala corpus

James R. Walker, physician at Pine Ridge, collected religious accounts from Oglala authorities between 1896 and 1914. His principal consultants included George Sword, Bad Wound, No Flesh, Finger, Thomas Tyon, Ringing Shield, Short Bull, Thunder Bear, Little Wound, American Horse, and others. The papers survive in manuscript and typescript form; the modern critical publications are *Lakota Belief and Ritual* (edited by Raymond J. DeMallie and Elaine A. Jahner, 1980) and *Lakota Myth* (edited by Jahner, 1983).

Relevant items include:

- George Sword’s theological “Foundation” account, in which Wakíŋyaŋ is Inyan’s associate and messenger.
- Seven Rabbits’s “Wakinyan.”
- Thomas Tyon’s “The Wakinyan Are Wakan.”
- Thunder Bear’s instruction concerning *haŋbléčheyapi*, the vision quest.
- Red Cloud’s speech “Defying the Wakinyan.”
- accounts of the Sun Dance by Thomas Tyon, George Sword, Little Wound, American Horse, Lone Star, and Bad Heart Bull.
- Walker’s literary myth cycle, including “Tempest,” the war with Unk, Iya, and the water powers.

The edition’s table of contents and editorial history confirm these separate testimonies ([*Lakota Belief and Ritual*](https://books.google.je/books?id=PYwV956P_UgC)). Walker is unusually valuable because named Lakota authorities supplied much of his material. He is also hazardous: he wanted a coherent “Oglala pantheon,” asked leading comparative questions, rearranged material, and composed a literary cycle from several accounts. Statements found only in that synthesis are therefore labeled **learned/ethnographic systematization**, not universal Lakota doctrine.

### Music, oral texts, and first-person testimony

- **Frances Densmore, *Teton Sioux Music* (1918):** 245 songs and extensive contextual testimony, including “Song Concerning a Dream of the Thunderbirds.” The book preserves notation, translations, performers, and ceremonial explanations ([Smithsonian edition](https://repository.si.edu/items/47c6ed45-63a3-455e-8006-a71fe7e12cd2); [digital song index](https://github.com/craigsapp/densmore-teton-sioux)).
- **Ella Cara Deloria, *Dakota Texts* (1932):** Dakota-language narratives with close translations. These are indispensable for distinguishing Dakota narrative vocabulary from Walker’s Oglala theological synthesis, although Wakíŋyaŋ is not the organizing subject of the volume.
- **Black Elk and John G. Neihardt, *Black Elk Speaks* (1932):** Black Elk’s great vision, horse dance, Ghost Dance experiences, and ascent of Harney Peak. It is a literary collaboration, not a stenographic transcript.
- **Black Elk and Joseph Epes Brown, *The Sacred Pipe* (1953):** Black Elk’s later explanation of seven rites, recorded in 1947. It reflects both long-lived Lakota practice and Black Elk’s own later Catholic/traditional religious world.
- **Raymond DeMallie, *The Sixth Grandfather* (1984):** publishes the surviving Neihardt interview materials and demonstrates substantial condensation, rearrangement, universalizing language, and strategic omission in *Black Elk Speaks*. DeMallie shows that Black Elk’s great vision was specifically a western Thunder-Being vision and that Neihardt reduced the visibility of that structure ([digitized text](https://dokumen.pub/the-sixth-grandfather-black-elks-teachings-given-to-john-g-neihardt-9780803216648-0803216645.html)).
- **John (Fire) Lame Deer and Richard Erdoes, *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972):** a vivid modern oral-literary account. Lame Deer calls the Wakinyan “sacred flying ones,” explains their mountain in the west, cloud robes, enormous egg, backward motion, and the calling of heyoka. The prose is co-produced with Erdoes and should not be backdated to antiquity ([excerpt](https://www.sacredhoop.org/Articles-Extra/LameDeer-ThunderBeings.pdf)).
- **William K. Powers, *Oglala Religion* (1977):** ethnography of twentieth-century Oglala religion and ritual renewal.
- **DeMallie and Douglas Parks, eds., *Sioux Indian Religion: Tradition and Innovation* (1987):** Lakota and Dakota religious leaders and scholars address continuity, innovation, Christian encounter, and contemporary ceremonial life ([publisher description](https://www.oupress.com/9780806121666/sioux-indian-religion/)).
- **Dooling, ed., *The Sons of the Wind* (1984):** presents Oglala mythic materials concerning Tate and the winds, useful for the broader directional cosmology but not evidence for a single fixed pantheon.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and powers

**Securely attested nineteenth-century material:**

- thunder is the voice or wing-noise of the Wakinyan;
- lightning flashes from their eyes or is cast as a projectile;
- they inhabit or arrive from the west;
- they fight Unktehi and other dangerous water or serpentine powers;
- they give warriors spears, tomahawks, protective paints, and war power;
- they may kill humans, animals, and other *wakȟáŋ* beings;
- rain is simultaneously life-giving, purifying, and dangerous;
- old Thunder Beings are described as wise; young ones may be reckless and destructive.

**Walker systematization:** Wakíŋyaŋ gives growth and increase to vegetation, acts as Inyan’s messenger, wars against filth and malevolent beings, and observes decrees issued by Škan. These claims are richly attested in Walker’s corpus but not all appear in earlier Dakota reports.

**Visionary and ritual testimony:** Thunder Beings can summon a dreamer, prescribe songs and ceremonies, confer healing or war power, require an inverted heyoka performance, and punish disobedience with lightning.

### Form

Gideon Pond’s nineteenth-century account gives three or four principal manifestations:

1. **Black:** long-beaked, with four joints in each wing.
2. **Yellow:** without a beak, four-jointed wings, six quills.
3. **Scarlet:** extremely long wings, each with eight joints.
4. **Blue:** globular, faceless, eyeless, and earless; an inverted crescent stands where the face would be, two chains of lightning descend from it, and two downy plumes serve as wings.

Each manifestation was said to represent a numerous race. This is the “four Wakinyan” account preserved through Pond and Dorsey. Similar fourfold descriptions occur in the Walker materials associated with George Sword’s larger cosmological teaching, but the surviving record does not justify assigning every detail exclusively to Sword.

Lame Deer gives a different interpretive principle: no one sees a Thunder Being whole; its image is assembled from partial dreams. The beings wear clouds as robes, lightning is their emblem, and they move counterclockwise or contrary to ordinary order. This explains why visual descriptions need not form a naturalistic bird.

### Symbols and material representations

- zigzag or forked lightning;
- dark cloud, storm, rainbow, rain, and the western quarter;
- winged or birdlike figures on shields, drums, tipi covers, and painted hides;
- thunder-derived protective paint;
- “thunder stones” or dark stones understood as spent missiles;
- cedar, which several accounts say the Thunder Beings will not strike;
- weapons—especially bow, lightning-arrow, spear, tomahawk, and “mysterious irons.”

Clark Wissler warned that the apparently conventional thunderbird image on modern shields could not simply be projected unchanged into deep antiquity; his study found the historical iconographic evidence indirect ([Wissler](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50589/50589-h/50589-h.htm)). A late-nineteenth-century Lakota rawhide shield bearing thunderbird and moon designs illustrates documented historic-period iconography, not a timeless canonical portrait ([Lame Deer excerpt and shield](https://www.sacredhoop.org/Articles-Extra/LameDeer-ThunderBeings.pdf)).

No universal sacred color, weekday, planet, or numerical correspondence comparable to European occult tables is documented. Black, yellow, red, and blue belong to one account’s forms; they should not be converted into a universal “Wakinyan color system.”

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other sacred beings

### Competing genealogical and hierarchical versions

**George Sword/Walker system:** Inyan creates Wakíŋyaŋ as his active associate or companion. Wakíŋyaŋ is therefore placed among the second rank of powers. In some Walker-derived tables, Ksa/Iktomi is described as Wakíŋyaŋ’s son. This is a learned Oglala systematization, not a universally attested genealogy.

**Pond/Dorsey Dakota material:** the Wakinyan are a race or class with elders and offspring. Wakinyan Tanka is the great or father Thunder Being. No elaborate divine parentage is required.

**Black Elk’s visions:** the western Grandfather represents or commands Thunder power, but Black Elk does not present a genealogical family tree. The Six Grandfathers are directional powers revealed in vision, not simply six humanlike gods.

No securely attested consort of Wakíŋyaŋ is consistent across the corpus.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The western mountain and the race of Thunder Beings

**Early attested core:** Wakinyan live in or beyond the west, commonly on a high mountain at the world’s edge. Thunder comes from their voices or wings. The great elder is wise; young Thunder Beings cause destructive strikes.

**Pond variant:** the outward forms are black, yellow, scarlet, and blue, each representing a race. The blue form is the least birdlike and most explicitly a personification of lightning.

**Later Lame Deer form:** the Thunder Beings occupy a mountain “where the sun goes down,” build an immense nest, and possess an egg greater than South Dakota. Their true form remains concealed by cloud robes. This is important twentieth-century Lakota testimony, but the giant egg and modern geographical comparison must not be silently backdated to 1849.

### The war with Unktehi

This is the most consistently documented narrative complex.

In the common structure, Unktehi are horned, tailed, serpentine, or quadrupedal water beings inhabiting lakes, rivers, marshes, and underground waters. Their movement causes waves, floods, drowning, disease, or the corruption of water. Wakinyan attack from above with lightning. Both sides possess lethal *tóŋwaŋ*; neither is invulnerable.

Dorsey preserves an account of a lake where people saw a deadly shining eye. During the night Thunder Beings fought the monster amid deafening crashes; the next morning enormous fish and serpents lay dead along the shore. Another tradition describes a Wakinyan with a wingspan of roughly twenty-five to thirty yards being killed near a river. These stories deny the popular simplification that Thunderbird always wins effortlessly.

In Lame Deer’s flood account, Unktehi floods the world and Wakinyan attacks; the red pipestone deposits are connected with the catastrophe and its victims. The National Park Service explicitly presents this as Lame Deer’s told version, while warning that related horned-water-being traditions differ among Plains nations ([NPS account](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/unktehi-and-the-flood-as-told-by-lame-deer.htm)).

The sky-versus-water polarity is early and robust. Claims that it is an Ice Age memory, a dinosaur-battle record, or proof of a universal worldwide “dragon war” are modern speculation.

### Wakinyan, Unk, and Iya

Walker’s “Tempest” gives a literary-theological narrative: Wakíŋyaŋ learns of Inyan’s love for Unk and rages when Iya is born. Because Wakíŋyaŋ is contrary, laughter expresses anger and praise expresses hatred. Hidden in cloud robes, he hunts Unk and Iya with his eye and beak. Škan decrees that he cannot enter water, injure cedar, or harm those beneath cedar branches. Unk therefore takes refuge in the water; Wakíŋyaŋ declares war on filth and casts it into the water.

This story elegantly explains storm, pollution, cedar protection, water refuge, and contrary speech. Its fullest surviving form is Walker’s composed myth cycle, however. It is not securely attested in this exact literary form before Walker.

### Black Elk’s great vision

At age nine, according to Black Elk’s account, two men came from the clouds amid thunder and summoned him. He was carried into a visionary world of horses, powers, and the Six Grandfathers. The first lodge was the cloud-tipi of the western Thunder Beings. The western Grandfather gave him a cup of water—power to make live—and a bow—power to destroy. Later the Thunder Beings gathered beneath the rainbow and rejoiced as Black Elk’s visionary mission succeeded.

Neihardt’s *Black Elk Speaks* universalized the vision into a drama of the “hoop of the world.” DeMallie’s comparison with the notes establishes that its ritual grammar was more specifically Lakota: it was initially a Thunder-Being vision of the west and therefore potentially a heyoka vocation. Neihardt deemphasized this, partly because Black Elk later narrated a second, more explicit Thunder vision and heyoka performance.

Black Elk said he feared the Thunder Beings through adolescence because a recipient who failed to enact their demand could be killed by lightning. At seventeen he told Black Road, who advised a horse dance rather than the ordinary heyoka performance for the first vision. After a later vision quest—Neihardt’s “dog vision”—Black Elk enacted the heyoka obligation.

At the close of *Black Elk Speaks*, Black Elk and Neihardt ascend Harney Peak. Black Elk prays for rain, and rain falls. This literary conclusion connects the mountain, his failed and continuing vocation, and the western storm powers. It is both autobiographical testimony and a scene deliberately shaped by Neihardt.

### Stone Boy and the four Thunder brothers

Dakota narrative variants associate Stone Boy with four Wakinyan brothers. In different tellings he cures them, awakens them from enchanted sleep, joins them against massed enemies, or provokes later hostility. These tales belong to Dakota heroic narrative rather than Walker’s creation system. Details vary enough that they should not be condensed into a single canonical episode.

## 6. Cult, sacred places, and ritual practice

“Cult” is potentially misleading here. No permanent Wakinyan temple, centralized priesthood, idol-house, or priestly bureaucracy is documented. Relations with Thunder Beings occur through dreams, songs, personal bundles, offerings, painted objects, communal dances, vision quests, the Sun Dance, and the vocation of ritual specialists.

### Heyoka

A person who dreams of the Thunder Beings may be obliged to become or act as a *heyókȟa*. The role is not a personality type voluntarily adopted by someone who is humorous or empathic. Traditional features include:

- performing actions backward or contrary to expectation;
- treating heat as cold and cold as heat;
- speaking in inversions;
- moving counterclockwise;
- clowning, parody, sexual or bodily humor, and public self-humiliation;
- using comedy to expose fear, arrogance, or improper conduct;
- singing Thunder-Being songs;
- fulfilling exact instructions received in a dream.

The office is simultaneously feared, comic, healing, and dangerous. Failure to honor the vision exposes the dreamer to illness, fear, or lightning. Black Elk’s account is the best-documented individual case.

### Vision quest

In *haŋbléčheyapi*, a person fasts and prays in isolation to receive knowledge or power. A Thunder Being may appear directly, through storm, through a humanlike emissary, or through symbolic animals and objects. The meaning is not self-authenticating: a holy person interprets it and determines obligations. Walker’s Thunder Bear testimony and Black Elk’s second Thunder vision document this institutional process.

### Sun Dance

The Sun Dance is not exclusively a Wakinyan rite, but Thunder power participates in it:

- the west and West Wind are invoked;
- rain can purify dancers and ground;
- songs and painted objects may derive from Thunder dreams;
- visionary power authorizes particular ritual roles;
- lightning, cloud, and rainbow imagery belong to the ceremonial cosmology.

George Sword explained that the pipe is offered to West Wind because West Wind lives in Wakíŋyaŋ’s lodge and is his friend. Walker’s accounts by Thomas Tyon, Little Wound, American Horse, Lone Star, and others show local variation rather than one fixed script.

### Offerings and protective practices

Nineteenth-century sources report songs and sacrifices to Wakinyan, especially at the first thunder of spring. Tobacco, cloth, food, painted objects, or personal vows could serve as offerings. Cedar was burned or used as protection because the Thunder Beings were said not to strike it. Warriors carried Wakinyan-derived paints or shield designs.

No reliable source documents human sacrifice to Wakinyan. Sun Dance piercing is voluntary bodily offering or sacrifice by the dancer, not human killing. Claims that Lakota people sacrificed human victims to a Thunderbird are unsupported.

### Black Hills and named sacred places

**Pahá Sápa, the Black Hills.** The Hills form a living sacred landscape rather than merely a set of “myth locations.” Their seizure by the United States after the 1874 Black Hills expedition and the 1876–77 war severed communities from places of prayer and vision. Wakinyan’s western and mountain associations are part of this larger sacred geography.

**Black Elk Peak / Hinhan Kaga.** Formerly officially Harney Peak, the mountain was renamed Black Elk Peak in 2016. Black Elk’s great vision was later understood from its summit, and contemporary tribal members make spring pilgrimages connected with welcoming the Thunder Beings ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/people/black-elk.htm)). The popular claim that the vision originally occurred physically on the summit is imprecise: the childhood experience was visionary; the later ascent framed its public narration.

**Matȟó Pahá, Bear Butte.** A major site of prayer, fasting, offerings, and vision seeking. Its Lakota significance is broader than Wakinyan alone. Contemporary prayer cloths and tobacco bundles attest continued practice.

**Matȟó Thípila, Bear Lodge / Devils Tower.** Sacred to Lakota and numerous neighboring nations. NPS documentation records Lakota use for vision quests, camps, and ceremonies, with several Lakota names and distinct tribal narratives ([NPS cultural history](https://www.nps.gov/deto/learn/historyculture/americanindians.htm)). No primary evidence located makes it exclusively Wakinyan’s dwelling. Associating every dramatic Black Hills formation with Thunderbird is a modern overgeneralization.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1849: oral antiquity without a datable beginning

Language, mythic integration, and the distribution of thunder-versus-water narratives demonstrate that the tradition predates its written record. No surviving inscription, precontact manuscript, or securely attributable archaeological image names Wakíŋyaŋ. Rock art and bird designs may be old, but assigning a particular image to Wakinyan without contextual testimony is speculative.

### 1849–1883: documentation under invasion and dispossession

Eastman, Pond, Riggs, Mayer, and later Dorsey documented Dakota religious language during accelerating settler occupation, warfare, treaty violation, removal, missionary pressure, and ecological collapse. Their writings preserve early evidence but often call it superstition, idolatry, or primitive religion. This was not passive description: documentation occurred amid campaigns to Christianize Dakota and Lakota people.

During this period Wakinyan appears prominently as warrior power, western Thunder Bird, opponent of Unktehi, and giver of protective paint and weapons. The sources show substantial internal variation before Walker’s system was written.

### 1883–1890: federal criminalization

The Office of Indian Affairs’ 1883 rules declared participation in the Sun Dance and related ceremonies an “Indian offense,” punishable by withholding rations or imprisonment. The text explicitly names the Sun Dance ([1883 Code](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Code_of_Indian_Offenses)). Because vision seeking, ritual specialists, and communal dance were interconnected, suppression affected the social settings in which Thunder dreams were publicly interpreted and fulfilled.

Enforcement varied and ceremonies did not disappear. Some were shortened, concealed, reframed, or conducted beyond agents’ observation. It is therefore incorrect to posit a complete break in 1883.

### Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee, 1890

The Ghost Dance was a new intertribal prophetic movement originating with Wovoka, not a Wakinyan cult. Lakota participants translated it through existing ritual concepts, songs, visionary authority, sacred clothing, and expectations of renewal. Short Bull and Kicking Bear were important Lakota leaders; Walker later recorded Short Bull’s testimony.

Federal officials and sensational newspapers interpreted the movement as military rebellion. On December 29, 1890, the Seventh Cavalry killed Lakota men, women, and children at Wounded Knee. This was state violence against a community already disarmed and confined, not a battle between “Thunderbird worshipers” and the army. The Library of Congress summarizes the Ghost Dance’s rapid spread and its suppression after the massacre ([Library of Congress](https://guides.loc.gov/chronicling-america-ghost-dance-movement)).

Wounded Knee forms part of Wakinyan’s documentary history indirectly: it transformed the communities, ceremonial conditions, and autobiographical memories from which Walker and Black Elk’s testimony emerged.

### 1896–1932: ethnographic salvage and indigenous testimony

Walker collected systematic Oglala testimony at Pine Ridge. Densmore recorded songs. Ella Deloria recorded Dakota-language narratives. Neihardt interviewed Black Elk. Their work preserved material endangered by coercive assimilation, but editorial control generally remained with non-Native collectors. The surviving notebooks, recordings, and bilingual texts permit unusually close study of mediation.

### Mid-twentieth century: persistence and public return

The final “old-time” Rosebud Sun Dance is often dated to 1883, but domestic rites, visions, songs, and ritual knowledge persisted. Public Sun Dances returned during the twentieth century. Black Elk’s 1947 teachings to Joseph Epes Brown documented seven rites, while Lame Deer, Frank Fools Crow, and other religious leaders later articulated Lakota practice publicly.

The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 supplied federal policy recognition but did not itself restore land or eliminate local interference.

### Late twentieth century to present

Religious revitalization has been especially visible at Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Cheyenne River, and Standing Rock. Sun Dances, vision quests, sweat lodges, seasonal prayer, sacred-site pilgrimages, and Thunder-dream responsibilities continue. “Revival” is only partly adequate: public scale and legal conditions changed, but testimony indicates transmission through the suppression period.

Protection of Black Hills sacred sites, opposition to unwanted climbing at Bear Lodge, the restoration of indigenous place names, and the 2016 renaming of Black Elk Peak are part of the contemporary documentary history.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and art

*Black Elk Speaks* made Thunder imagery—western clouds, lightning, rainbow, Six Grandfathers, horses, and the sacred hoop—internationally recognizable. Its influence is inseparable from Neihardt’s editorial choices. DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* later made the Lakota-specific Thunder-Being structure more visible.

Lakota artists, including Arthur Amiotte, have used ledger-art methods, collage, and narrative imagery to address Black Elk, the sacred hoop, colonial history, and the continuity of Lakota cosmology. Such art is contemporary indigenous interpretation, not a reproduction of an unchanging ancient iconographic canon.

A 2016 storm film titled *Wakíŋyaŋ* by Martin Lisius explicitly honored the Lakota thunder spirit. It is a modern artistic dedication, not traditional ritual documentation.

### Pan-Indian “Thunderbird”

Popular books, tattoo culture, sports logos, tourist jewelry, fantasy games, and online mythology lists commonly merge Lakota Wakinyan with Anishinaabe Animikii, Algonquian thunderbirds, Kwakwaka’wakw crest beings, and many other distinct traditions. Visual resemblance does not make these one transcontinental deity. The singular, eagle-shaped “Native American Thunderbird” is largely a modern pan-Indian and commercial abstraction.

### New Age “heyoka empath”

The internet category **“heyoka empath”**—a supposedly rare personality who mirrors emotions and heals others through disruption—is a modern invention, emerging especially in online spirituality during the 2010s. It has no basis in Walker, Densmore, Deloria, Black Elk, Powers, or the older Dakota corpus. Traditional heyoka status arises from a Thunder dream and community-recognized ceremonial obligation, not self-identification through a personality checklist. Lakota practitioners explicitly reject “heyoka empath” as a tribal category ([Lakota practitioner statement](https://heyokalife.com/about/sacredpathofheyoka/)).

### Occultism and neopaganism

No documented Renaissance Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, or pre-twentieth-century European occult cult of Wakinyan was located. Modern occult websites sometimes place “Thunderbird” in elemental tables or invoke Wakinyan as a generic air or storm spirit. These are recent eclectic borrowings.

There is likewise no well-documented, historically continuous non-Lakota “Wakinyan neopagan” tradition. Individual practitioners may adopt the name, but that constitutes modern eclectic religion rather than reconstruction from an ancient liturgy.

### Political use

Thunderbird imagery can mark Native sovereignty, warrior identity, environmental protection, and connection to sacred land. It has also been detached from tribal authority for commercial branding. The decisive documentary question is not whether a design “looks Native,” but who produced it, which community authorized it, and whether the image derives from a particular dream or ceremonial right.

No substantial modern extremist appropriation of Wakinyan comparable to white-nationalist use of some European gods was found.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Wakinyan one or many?

The sources do not support a single answer:

- Eastman uses a singular directional “Man of the West.”
- Pond records three or four manifestations, each a race.
- Dorsey distinguishes elder and younger Thunderers.
- Walker sometimes writes Wakinyan as a singular associate god.
- Black Elk describes crowds of Thunder Beings.
- Lame Deer alternates between the great Thunderbird and numerous beings.

This is not merely collector error. Lakota sacred powers need not obey European numerical individuality.

### Bird or being?

“Thunderbird” is visually convenient but can distort the sources. Some forms are avian; the blue form is a faceless globe with lightning chains. Thunder Beings also appear as humanlike grandfathers or messengers in visions. “Thunder Being” best preserves the category’s range.

### How systematic was the pantheon?

Walker’s hierarchy of superior, associate, and subordinate gods resembles a theological summa. George Sword and other Oglala intellectuals undoubtedly supplied sophisticated explanations, but Walker selected and synthesized them. Scholars therefore distinguish:

- direct consultant texts;
- Walker’s prose summaries;
- Walker’s composite literary cycle;
- later popular charts derived from that cycle.

Calling Wakinyan “the son of X, husband of Y, and father of Z” without naming the Walker passage turns one systematized account into false canon.

### Etymology

The early and linguistically direct explanation derives the word from *kiŋyáŋ*, “fly.” “Sacred wing,” *wakȟáŋ* + “wing,” and similar renderings circulate widely but are folk segmentation unless supported by a specific Lakota linguistic analysis. The modern spelling preserves nasal vowels and stress more accurately than “Waukheon.”

### Fossils and “dinosaur memory”

Lakota narratives associate giant bones, stone remains, monsters, and ancient combats in some contexts. Modern claims that Wakinyan and Unktehi stories are eyewitness records of dinosaurs, pterosaurs, or prehistoric humans are not supported by chronological evidence. Indigenous interpretation of fossils is historically important; converting it into cryptozoology is modern invention.

### Source ethics

The record is unusually rich but structurally unequal. Missionaries and ethnographers often controlled publication; sacred knowledge was elicited under colonial conditions; English translations obscured grammatical and ritual nuances. Conversely, dismissing the corpus as nothing but outsider invention erases the named Lakota thinkers who used those encounters to preserve teachings.

Relevant archives include Walker’s papers and photographs, Densmore’s recordings and notes in Smithsonian/Library of Congress collections, Dorsey’s papers at the National Anthropological Archives, Neihardt’s interview notes published by DeMallie, and the textual legacy of Ella Deloria. Repatriation projects now reconnect Densmore’s songs with descendant communities ([Densmore Repatriation Project](https://www.lakotasongs.com/book)).

## 11. Sources and evidentiary conclusions

The stable historical core is not a single bird-god biography but a complex of relationships:

- Thunder Beings are western, winged or flying *wakȟáŋ* powers.
- Their voice and movement are thunder; their gaze or weapons are lightning.
- They bring both destructive danger and life-giving rain.
- Their most persistent supernatural opponents are Unktehi, the water powers.
- They call human dreamers into demanding ceremonial obligations, especially the heyoka vocation.
- They participate in vision quest, war power, healing, song, the Sun Dance, and the sacred geography of the Black Hills.
- Their number, appearance, genealogy, and moral character legitimately vary among accounts.
- Walker’s elegant divine hierarchy is one important Oglala intellectual construction preserved under ethnographic conditions, not a pan-Sioux creed.
- Black Elk’s vision is essential first-person evidence, but its famous published form was reshaped by Neihardt.
- No evidence supports human sacrifice, an ancient Eurasian occult correspondence system, a universal pan-Indigenous Thunderbird identity, or the New Age “heyoka empath.”
- Wakinyan remains part of a living religion; its documentary history did not end with the nineteenth-century ethnographers.
