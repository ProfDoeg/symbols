# Unktehi: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A class or nation of powerful *wakȟáŋ* water beings—variously classified as water spirits, water monsters, horned serpents, giant horned quadrupeds, or, in some nineteenth-century English accounts, a “water god.” Unktehi is not consistently a single deity. Some sources speak collectively; others personify a chief, a female flood-maker, or a named male being.  
> **Earliest secure published attestation:** Mary Henderson Eastman’s *Dahcotah; or, Life and Legends of the Sioux around Fort Snelling* (New York, 1849), based on her residence near Fort Snelling in 1841–48 and especially on stories learned from the Dakota woman Checkered Cloud. Eastman prints forms including **Unktahe** and **Unk-a-tahe**, calls the being “the god of the waters,” locates its dwelling near the Minnesota and Mississippi rivers, and records both a horned apparition and warfare with the Thunder Beings. Her book is early documentary evidence, but it is an English literary rendering by a settler author with an explicitly Christian interpretive program, not a verbatim Dakota transcript. [Eastman’s complete 1849 text](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d9/Dahcotah%3B_or%2C_Life_and_legends_of_the_Sioux_around_Fort_Snelling_%28IA_dahcotahlife00eastrich%29.pdf)

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early attestation]** Nineteenth-century documentation based on Dakota or Lakota testimony.
- **[Later primary testimony]** A named Indigenous narrator recorded in the late nineteenth or twentieth century.
- **[Regional variant]** A Dakota-, Lakota-, band-, or locality-specific account that must not be universalized.
- **[Collector’s systematization]** An arrangement or synthesis substantially shaped by an ethnographer or editor.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical or comparative inference, not itself an Indigenous primary statement.
- **[Modern reception/invention]** Recent literature, art, games, cryptid lore, or generalized “mythology” material not demonstrably inherited as an older tradition.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

The documentary record does not justify a single, timeless definition. In the eastern Dakota material around the Minnesota and Mississippi rivers, Unktehi is often the singular or collective power inhabiting active water—rivers, lakes, springs, falls, whirlpools, and subterranean passages. It can appear as an immense horned aquatic animal, a “great fish,” a buffalo- or ox-like monster, or a horned serpent. It causes waves, floods, ice breakups, drowning, and transformations, but also gives sacred knowledge and ceremonial bundles.

In Oglala Lakota testimony collected by James R. Walker at Pine Ridge between 1896 and 1914, *unktehi* are a class of four-legged, horned water beings with long neck hair and powerful tails. They are perpetually at war with the *Wakíŋyaŋ*, the Thunder Beings. In Walker’s more synthetic mythological manuscripts, Unktehi may also be connected to **Unk**, a female embodiment of quarrelsomeness or contention banished into the waters. That genealogy should not be projected backward into the earlier Dakota river traditions.

A Walker text summarizes the class:

> “They have four legs and horns which they can draw in or extend them to the skies.… Their tails are strong.… They are always at war with the Wakinyan.”

The passage appears in the Walker corpus as edited in *Lakota Belief and Ritual*; a later scholarly discussion reproduces it and connects the Unktehi to the Mystery Dance and sacred bundles. [Walker corpus scan](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~rfrank/class_web/ES-110/ETHN110articles/Plains/walker_lakota_ps.pdf) [scholarly discussion](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/bitstreams/ba3f8abe-5c0a-425e-8711-862f2e018c00/download)

### Recorded forms

Orthographies vary because nineteenth-century writers lacked a standardized system and because Dakota and Lakota dialect forms differ.

- **Unktahe, Unk-a-tahe, Unksa-tahe:** Eastman’s 1849 forms.
- **Unktehi / Unktehee:** common missionary and ethnographic spelling.
- **Uŋktéȟi / Uŋktéhi:** modern Lakota orthographies.
- **Uŋktéȟipi / ųktéĥipi:** plural or collective forms found in modern linguistic and religious scholarship.
- **Unktehila / Unktehi-la:** sometimes treated popularly as a diminutive or as the horned serpent; often confused with **Uŋčéǧila/Unhcegila**, a distinct or overlapping terrestrial monster in different narratives.
- **Unk:** the female figure in Walker’s constructed Oglala divine genealogy. Its equation with the whole class of Unktehi is source-specific, not universally attested.
- **Miniwatu / Mni-watu:** another water-monster term recorded by George Bushotter and James Owen Dorsey. Dorsey himself questioned whether this was the same being under a different name. The evidence permits comparison but not automatic identity.

James Owen Dorsey wrote in 1894 that “Unktehi” resisted confident morphological analysis; older translations such as **“One Who Kills”** are therefore traditional or conjectural glosses, not a settled etymology. Dorsey’s treatment distinguishes linguistic evidence from interpretive translation and describes subordinate beings and rituals associated with Unktehi. [Dorsey, *A Study of Siouan Cults*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/48635)

The modern *New Lakota Dictionary* provides a standardized orthographic framework, but standard spelling does not resolve the historical etymology. [Lakota Language Consortium dictionary](https://www.dictionary.lakhota.org/)

### Titles and classifications

Attested English descriptions include:

- “god of the waters” and “water-god” — Eastman, 1849.
- “great fish of the water” and “monster of the deep” — Eastman’s narrative dialogue, 1849.
- “Water God or Guardian Spirit” — nineteenth-century missionary and ethnological glosses.
- “water monster” — Walker, Dorsey, and most later scholarship.
- *wakȟáŋ* being — Lakota classification: powerful, mysterious, and capable of helping or harming, rather than simply “evil.”
- “protector of the waters” — a contemporary interpretive formulation in Marlena Myles’s 2022 Dakota Spirit Walk; this is modern public art, not a securely attested ancient epithet. [Minnesota DNR account](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/mcvmagazine/issues/2023/jan-feb/dispatch.html)

“Demon” is inappropriate unless reporting a missionary’s polemical vocabulary. “Monster” translates its dangerous morphology but does not exhaust its sacred and pedagogical roles.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 1849: Mary Henderson Eastman

Eastman’s *Dahcotah* supplies the earliest presently located, securely dated printed descriptions by name.

Her geographical notice calls Unktahe “the god of the waters,” says it was greatly revered, and identifies a bluff near Fort Snelling—later called Morgan’s Bluff or Taku Wakan Tipi—as its residence. A subterranean passage supposedly connected the hill to the Minnesota River.

In another episode, a Dakota chief sees Unk-a-tahe expose its horns, an omen of calamity. An elder recalls the being breaking river ice near St. Anthony Falls; the resulting surge uprooted trees, stripped islands, destroyed a house, and drowned a settler. Eastman’s account treats this as an interpretation of an actual ice-jam flood.

Her most extensive Unktahe narrative describes a vision-speaker joining forty Thunder Beings against Unktahe and sixty water spirits. Storm wind, rain, waves, lightning, spears, and shields become the physical form of the battle. The narrator kills Unktahe’s son; the water spirits retreat, and a rainbow marks the Thunder party’s victory. This is the earliest securely published named interaction between Unktehi and Wakinyan presently identified.

Eastman’s husband Seth supplied illustrations to the volume, but no securely identified Unktehi illustration in the book can be established from the accessible catalogue evidence.

### 1852: Riggs’s dictionary

Stephen Return Riggs’s *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language*, compiled with members of the Dakota Mission and published by the Smithsonian in 1852, is the earliest lexicographic witness. It represents roughly eighteen years of missionary language collection rather than a single informant’s testimony. Its importance lies in establishing that the name belonged to a living Dakota vocabulary by the mid-nineteenth century. It does not establish a single canonical mythology. [Smithsonian edition record and scan](https://repository.si.edu/items/607ec97e-1d61-4472-987f-1a2799595147)

### Gideon H. Pond and the Minnesota Dakota record

Gideon Pond’s “Dakota Superstitions” or “Dakota Superstitions and Dakota Gods,” eventually printed in the Minnesota Historical Society collections, gives a more organized account of Unktehi. Pond says springs are the being’s “breathing places,” associates it with river catastrophes and sacred localities, and describes the water power as horned and immensely large. The publication history is awkward: Pond’s observations reach back to the mission period beginning in the 1830s, but the commonly cited printed form is 1889. Observation date and publication date must therefore remain distinct.

Pond’s account of the Fort Snelling ice-jam catastrophe closely parallels Eastman’s 1849 narrative. This may reflect a shared Dakota account, circulation within the Fort Snelling mission community, or both; it should not be counted as two independent traditions without qualification. [Coldwater ethnographic study quoting Pond](https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/miss/coldwater_spring.pdf)

### 1894: Dorsey and George Bushotter

James Owen Dorsey’s *A Study of Siouan Cults*, published in the Bureau of Ethnology’s Eleventh Annual Report, assembles older Dakota missionary sources and information from Lakota writer George Bushotter.

Dorsey reports that:

- Unktehi made or helped shape earth and human beings in one account.
- Unktehi gave the Dakota the sacred or “mystery” sack.
- It instituted the *Wakȟáŋ Wačhípi*, the Mystery Dance.
- Associated beings included serpents, lizards, frogs, ghosts, owls, and eagles.
- Sacred pigments and animal-skin bundles were connected with its instructions.
- Bushotter’s *miniwatu* had a buffalo-like body and a horn on its forehead.

These are valuable early records but already composite: Dorsey combined dialects, bands, informants, and earlier publications under comparative headings. [Dorsey text](https://repository.si.edu/bitstream/handle/10088/91659/Study%20of%20the%20Siouan%20Cults.pdf)

### 1896–1914: Walker’s Pine Ridge collection

James R. Walker, physician at Pine Ridge, collected Oglala material from George Sword, Bad Wound, No Flesh, Finger, Thomas Tyon, Ringing Shield, Short Bull, and others. The collection contains both Indigenous-authored or dictated documents and Walker’s own reconstructions.

The editors of *Lakota Belief and Ritual* distinguish “raw narratives” from Walker’s synthesis. George Sword began instructing Walker in 1896; other documents followed through Walker’s departure in 1914. The corpus describes Unktehi’s anatomy, war with Wakinyan, disease-causing power, relation to water, and ceremonial authority. [Publication record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Lakota_Belief_and_Ritual.html?id=PYwV956P_UgC)

Walker’s *Lakota Myth* is even more textually layered. It contains separately attributable narratives, manuscript fragments, and Walker’s literary “cycle” constructed from what he had learned. Statements from the synthesized cycle—especially a tightly organized pantheon and divine genealogy—must be labeled as Walker’s systematization unless independently attested. [*Lakota Myth* record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Lakota_Myth.html?id=LrM4QvpwnHoC)

D. M. Dooling’s *The Sons of the Wind* (2000) retells the Walker mythic cycle in polished narrative form. It is an important modern transmission but not an independent primary source. [Publication record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Sons_of_the_Wind.html?id=DE-ALbKSkQEC)

### Densmore, Deloria, Black Elk, and related corpora

- **Frances Densmore, *Teton Sioux Music* (1918):** a major corpus of songs, ritual testimony, and musical notation recorded principally at Standing Rock. It contextualizes Lakota sacred practice but does not provide a comparably central Unktehi cycle. Its silence cautions against claiming that Unktehi possessed a standardized public cult or universally performed song repertoire. [Complete Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/unk83014444/)
- **Ella Deloria, *Dakota Texts* (1932):** sixty-three Dakota and Lakota-language narratives, carefully transcribed and translated. Its published contents emphasize Iktomi, Double Face, culture heroes, animal persons, monsters, and social narratives; no major independent Unktehi cycle is listed. More than half of Deloria’s collected texts remained unpublished in the Boas Collection at the American Philosophical Society, so absolute absence cannot be claimed. [Smithsonian catalogue](https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_795227) [Indiana corpus description](https://zia.aisri.indiana.edu/resources/texts.html)
- **Black Elk Speaks (1932):** Black Elk’s account to John G. Neihardt is essential for the Ghost Dance and Oglala sacred history, but Unktehi is not a principal figure in the published narrative. Raymond DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* shows how Neihardt reordered, condensed, and literary-shaped the interviews. Therefore, neither silence nor wording in *Black Elk Speaks* should be treated as an untouched transcript.
- **The Sacred Pipe (1953):** Black Elk’s accounts to Joseph Epes Brown foreground White Buffalo Calf Woman and seven rites, not an Unktehi cult. This is evidence of selective ritual emphasis, not proof that Black Elk rejected Unktehi.
- **Lame Deer, *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972):** records a major flood narrative told in 1969. It is explicit later Indigenous testimony but also a collaborative English publication with Richard Erdoes.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and powers

Across the sources, Unktehi belongs to deep or active water: lakes, rivers, springs, marshes, falls, whirlpools, and subterranean waterways. Its characteristic powers include:

- making waves by bodily movement;
- extending horns to enormous size;
- striking or grasping with the tail;
- causing floods and destructive ice breakups;
- drowning people or consuming their spirits;
- carrying people beneath the water;
- transforming captives into aquatic animals;
- polluting or “spoiling” water in Walker’s cycle;
- causing suppuration in wounds by placing saliva or pith in them;
- granting ceremonial and medicinal power;
- revealing bundles, pigments, and ritual procedures.

Walker stated that a sufficiently powerful holy person could subdue or drive away an Unktehi and reverse its magic. His 1917 ceremonial monograph also attributes infected wounds to Unktehi. [Walker, *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies*](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/native-american/great-plains/oglala-lakota-ceremony/the-sun-dance-and-other-ceremonies-of-the-oglala-division-of-the-teton-dakota-jr-walker)

### Form

No single zoological form is canonical.

- **Horned aquatic quadruped:** four legs, retractable or extensible horns, long hair on head and neck, immense tail.
- **Buffalo or ox form:** especially strong in Minnesota Dakota descriptions; mammoth or mastodon bones were sometimes interpreted as Unktehi remains.
- **Horned fish:** Eastman’s “great fish of the water.”
- **Horned serpent:** widespread in later retellings and overlapping Great Lakes/Plains iconography.
- **Female flood monster:** Lame Deer uses feminine pronouns for the flood-making Unktehi.
- **Collective nation:** numerous beings, with chiefs and offspring, as in Eastman’s sixty water spirits and “son of Unktahe.”

The now-common internet image of a dragon-like snake should therefore be labeled a selective visualization, not the sole traditional form.

### Fossils

Pond, Dorsey, and later ethnographers connect unusually large fossil bones with Unktehi. Mammoth and mastodon remains found in wet ground supplied a plausible material referent for the buffalo-like form. Farther west, Badlands fossil beds were understood in some Lakota accounts as remains of primordial monsters killed or petrified during the conflict with the Thunder Beings.

Lame Deer’s 1969 narrative says Unktehi was turned to stone and that her backbone and vertebrae can be seen as ridges and colored formations in the Badlands. This is an attested Indigenous interpretation of fossils and landforms, not evidence that the tradition originated as an attempt at paleontological explanation.

### Images

Securely identified early Unktehi images are uncommon. A nineteenth-century Sioux painted hide in Berlin has been interpreted as showing aquatic monsters beside a large ceremonial pipe and radiating disks, but attribution is scholarly and the precise artist and narrative are uncertain. Tipi and hide designs elsewhere on the Plains include horned underwater beings, yet tribal and narrative identification must be established object by object.

Modern depictions include Marlena Myles’s **Dakota Spirit Walk** at Bruce Vento Nature Sanctuary, inaugurated in 2022: an augmented-reality blue water serpent with antler-like horns and a spiked tail. It presents Unktehi as a guardian warning humans to respect water. This is a documented Dakota artist’s contemporary interpretation, not a reproduction of a nineteenth-century icon.

No securely attested traditional color, number, weekday, planet, or unique plant belongs to Unktehi. Claims assigning such correspondences are modern occult-style systematization unless a specific Indigenous source is supplied.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Competing genealogical models

**Dakota river tradition [early attestation]:** Unktehi is a water power or nation with offspring and companions. Eastman mentions a “son of Unktahe” but gives no parents or consort.

**Walker’s Oglala system [collector’s systematization built from Oglala testimony]:** Maka creates Unk as a companion. Because Unk produces contention, she is banished beneath the waters and becomes associated with Unktehi. Some versions make Unk the mother or source of water monsters. This tidy place in a pantheon appears most clearly in Walker’s synthetic cycle and should not be treated as pan-Sioux dogma.

**Class model [later primary testimony]:** Unktehi are a species or people with males and females, leaders, children, and local dwellings. Gender varies by narrator and locality.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The war of Water and Thunder

**Earliest form:** Eastman, 1849.

A visionary recalls following the Thunder Birds through black clouds and heavy rain. Unktahe rises with sixty companions. Forty Thunder allies confront them. The atmospheric violence of the storm is narrated as battle: winds uproot trees and overturn lodges, waters surge, and the beings fight with shields and spears. The narrator kills Unktahe’s son. The water party withdraws, rain subsides, and a rainbow spans sky and earth.

**Walker variant:** Unktehi and Wakinyan are always at war. Unktehi spoil waters and destroy living things; the Thunder Beings attack from above. Walker’s literary cycle makes the opposition a structural cosmic polarity.

**Interpretation:** Scholars commonly read the cycle as an opposition of below/above, water/fire or lightning, and dangerous aquatic power/cleansing storm. That is a useful structural interpretation, not necessarily an explanation stated by every narrator.

### The river monster and the ice-jam flood

**Earliest form:** Eastman, 1849; paralleled by Pond’s later publication.

After a chief sees Unk-a-tahe’s horns—an omen—an elder recalls the being entering beneath St. Anthony Falls and breaking the ice. The river rises violently; islands are stripped, trees fall, and a settler dies when his house is swept away. Dakota witnesses interpret the disaster as Unktehi’s passage.

This account preserves the coexistence of observable event and sacred causation. The ice breakup is not evidence “against” the story; it is the event whose meaning the story records.

### The primordial flood and Pipestone

**Later primary testimony:** Lame Deer, told at Winner, South Dakota, in 1969.

Unktehi fights humanity and raises a flood. Lame Deer offers alternatives rather than dogma: perhaps Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka was angry, perhaps Unktehi was allowed to prevail so that a better humanity could come. People take refuge on the hill beside the future pipestone quarry, but waves and falling rocks kill them. Their pooled blood becomes red pipestone; the pipe bowl is their flesh and blood, its stem their backbone, and its smoke their breath.

Unktehi herself becomes stone. Her body forms a Badlands ridge, with exposed rock imagined as vertebrae. A beautiful woman survives or is rescued by Spotted Eagle; their twin children renew the people.

This version joins several themes that earlier sources do not necessarily combine: flood, moral destruction, Pipestone’s red stone, Badlands fossils, petrification, eagle rescue, twins, and ethnogenesis. It is a valid twentieth-century Lakota narrative, but not proof that the complete sequence existed unchanged in 1849. [NPS transcription](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/unktehi-and-the-flood-as-told-by-lame-deer.htm)

A current Smithsonian educational presentation gives a Dakota formulation: the Creator sent the Unktehi to flood the earth after people forgot how to behave as human beings; their blood became the red pipestone used for prayer pipes. [National Museum of the American Indian](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/plains-belonging-homelands/oceti-sakowin)

### Creation and the Mystery Dance

Dorsey reports a Dakota account in which Unktehi made the earth and people, then gave the mystery sack and instituted the *Wakȟáŋ Wačhípi*. The prescribed bundle incorporated skins and medicines representing animal and plant powers.

Walker’s Oglala materials likewise connect Unktehi to sacred bundles, but some narrators credit Taku Skanskan. A modern historical hypothesis interprets the Unktehi version as preserving an older eastern, lake-oriented period and the Taku Skanskan version as developing after westward movement onto the Plains. This is **modern reconstruction**, plausible but unproved.

### Local landscape narratives

- **Taku Wakan Tipi/Morgan’s Bluff:** residence of Unktehi, with a subterranean route to the Minnesota River; recorded by Eastman in 1849.
- **Oȟéyawahe/Pilot Knob:** Unktehi’s movement through the Mississippi–Minnesota confluence raised or shaped the bluff in Dakota oral history. Contemporary Dakota leaders continue to identify the place as sacred. [Carleton University exhibit](https://religionsmn.carleton.edu/exhibits/show/pilotknob/significance)
- **Coldwater Spring/Mni Owe Sni:** springs are Unktehi’s breathing or manifestation places. A 2005–06 ethnographic study records testimony from Dakota cultural experts while carefully noting that Coldwater’s specific historical documentation is thinner than that of Taku Wakan Tipi. [NPS ethnographic study](https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/miss/coldwater_spring.pdf)
- **Mni Wakan/Spirit Lake:** contemporary Spirit Lake tradition locates water spirits in the lake; “Devils Lake” is a colonial mistranslation of a sacred-water name, not evidence of a Dakota devil concept.

## 6. Cult, sites, and ritual practice

Unktehi had no documented temple system, permanent priestly corporation, cult statue, or exclusive festival comparable to an ancient Mediterranean state cult. Religious relations were embedded in landscape, visions, bundles, medicine practice, offerings, and ceremonies.

### Mystery Dance and bundles

Dorsey says Unktehi instituted the Mystery Dance and prescribed its animal-skin medicine sack, pigments, and contents. Walker’s Oglala collection describes bundles as animate manifestations of sacred power. Holders required visions, songs, formulas, and correct ritual obligations. This is the closest evidence for an institutional Unktehi cult, though even here the ceremony concerned the education and empowerment of holy people rather than worship of one exclusive god.

### Propitiation and healing

People gave gifts to spirits both to obtain help and to prevent harm. Water beings could cause disease, drowning, and wounds; holy people with sufficiently potent fetishes or bundles could repel them. Sage and sweetgrass are reported in later summaries as substances used against dangerous beings generally, but no exclusive Unktehi incense can be securely established.

Human sacrifice to Unktehi is **not documented** in the examined Dakota/Lakota primary sources. Stories of drowned persons being taken or consumed by the water being describe supernatural predation or interpretation of accidental death, not a verified institution of sacrificial drowning. Claims of an Unktehi “human-sacrifice cult” should be treated as modern invention unless accompanied by a specific primary record.

### Sacred sites

The strongest site record lies in Minnesota Dakota country:

- St. Anthony Falls/Owámniyomni;
- the Mississippi and Minnesota rivers;
- Taku Wakan Tipi;
- Oȟéyawahe/Pilot Knob;
- Coldwater Spring;
- Lake Waconia and other active waters;
- Mni Wakan/Spirit Lake.

Paha Sapa, Bear Butte, and Mato Tipila/Devils Tower are central Lakota and Northern Plains sacred landscapes, but the reviewed sources do not establish them as exclusive Unktehi sanctuaries. They belong in the broader living sacred geography within which Thunder, emergence, prayer, fasting, and Sun Dance traditions continue. The National Park Service recognizes Mato Tipila as sacred and supports a voluntary June climbing closure, while noting that “Bear Lodge” translates a Lakota name. [NPS Devils Tower FAQ](https://home.nps.gov/deto/faqs.htm)

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1849: oral and material tradition

The tradition necessarily predates its first English publication, but no datable pre-contact inscription or securely identified archaeological image names Unktehi. Fossils, painted hides, landscape names, bundles, and oral narratives may preserve older forms; none supplies an absolute date for the figure’s origin.

### 1849–1894: eastern Dakota documentation

Eastman, Riggs, Pond, and Dorsey document a powerful river-and-lake being closely tied to Minnesota geography. The being is simultaneously dangerous, creative, medicinal, and ceremonial. English terms such as “god,” “demon,” and “superstition” reflect missionary and settler taxonomies and should not replace *wakȟáŋ*.

### 1890 and the Ghost Dance

The Ghost Dance came from the Northern Paiute prophet Wovoka and reached Lakota communities in 1890. Lakota leaders and visionaries adapted it to local sacred geography, expectations of returning relatives and buffalo, and resistance to colonial destruction.

No reliable primary source located makes Unktehi the founder, patron, or central actor of the 1890 Ghost Dance. The defensible connections are contextual:

1. Unktehi belonged to the sacred world from which Lakota Ghost Dancers drew symbols and expectations.
2. Badlands landscapes associated with Unktehi fossils also became places of refuge and spiritual expectation during the crisis.
3. Flood or earth-renewal themes could resonate with Ghost Dance renewal, but direct dependence is unproved.
4. Black Elk, Short Bull, and other Ghost Dance witnesses appear in collections that also preserve older Lakota religion; shared informants do not prove a specific Unktehi role.

Federal agents treated the movement as a threat. Sitting Bull was killed during an attempted arrest on December 15, 1890. On December 29 the Seventh Cavalry killed Lakota men, women, and children at Wounded Knee. Wounded Knee was not a mythical battle and must not be folded into an Unktehi legend; it was documented state violence occurring during suppression of a living religious movement. [PBS historical account](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/american-oz-lakota-ghost-dance-massacre-wounded-knee/)

### Suppression of ceremony

The federal Code of Indian Offenses of March 30, 1883 classified the Sun Dance and related ceremonies as offenses, punishable through withheld rations or imprisonment. This struck the larger ritual system in which sacred beings, bundles, visions, and ritual specialists were transmitted, even though the edict did not name Unktehi. [1883 text](https://commons.und.edu/indigenous-gov-docs/131/)

Ceremonies continued privately or in altered forms. Public Sun Dances reappeared unevenly during the 1920s and especially after John Collier’s 1934 policy change; broader statutory recognition came with the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978. It is misleading to describe a single clean “ban lifted” date: administrative discouragement weakened in the 1930s, while legal protection remained incomplete and sacred-site conflicts continued.

### Twentieth-century documentation and renewal

Walker’s papers, Densmore’s recordings, Deloria’s linguistic texts, Black Elk’s testimonies, and Lame Deer’s narrative moved Lakota sacred knowledge into print and archives. Each involved selection, translation, and editorial mediation. Publication both preserved material threatened by assimilation and detached some narratives from their ceremonial and kinship controls.

### Present

Unktehi remains present in Dakota and Lakota storytelling, language work, sacred-site protection, education, visual art, and water-centered environmental interpretation. This is continuity through adaptation, not a “neopagan reconstruction.” Traditional Lakota and Dakota religion is a living Indigenous tradition, not a branch of modern Western Paganism.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Indigenous reception

Contemporary Dakota artists and educators increasingly present Unktehi in relation to water responsibility, cultural survival, and Indigenous presence in urban landscapes. Marlena Myles’s Dakota Spirit Walk is a prominent example.

Arthur Amiotte’s scholarship and art have been fundamental to understanding continuity and transformation in Lakota art, ceremony, and reservation life, but no specific Amiotte work securely centered on Unktehi was established in the reviewed catalogues. Invoking him as an “Unktehi artist” without an identified work would exceed the evidence.

### Literature and popular mythology

Modern mythology dictionaries and websites often collapse:

- Unktehi with Unhcegila;
- Dakota buffalo-like water beings with a generic pan-Indigenous “horned serpent”;
- the singular with the plural;
- Walker’s Unk genealogy with all Dakota and Lakota traditions;
- fossils with literal dinosaurs;
- sacred beings with “cryptids.”

These are modern harmonizations. “Unktehi was a dinosaur,” “Wakinyan was a pterosaur,” and similar claims are speculative paleomythology, not traditional identifications established by the sources.

Cryptid sites and games tend to turn Unktehi into a fixed dragon species. Such depictions can be documented as modern reception but should not be cited as evidence of pre-reservation belief.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no evidence of:

- a historical Unktehi current in Renaissance Hermeticism;
- theosophical, Golden Dawn, or established grimoire rites to Unktehi;
- a documented non-Indigenous reconstructionist denomination devoted to Unktehi;
- an ancient system of planetary, zodiacal, elemental, or numerological correspondences.

Isolated internet invocations may exist, but treating them as “Lakota neopaganism” would erase the difference between living Indigenous religion and outsider eclecticism.

### Political appropriation

Unktehi is more often mobilized in Indigenous land-and-water interpretation than in nationalist or extremist politics. No documented extremist appropriation was located. The absence should be stated rather than padded with unrelated examples.

## 9. Historiography and debates

### Singular god or class of beings?

Early English writers favored singular “god of waters.” Indigenous testimony and later linguistic work often favor a class or nation of beings. The variation may reflect grammar, regional traditions, individual chiefs within a class, or translators’ preference for a Greco-Christian category. The evidence does not permit one answer for every period and community.

### Buffalo monster or serpent?

Both are attested. The buffalo/ox form is especially visible in Minnesota and fossil accounts; reptilian and serpent forms become prominent in western Lakota and comparative horned-serpent treatments. A historical change from woodland/lake quadruped to Plains fossil-serpent has been proposed, but the overlapping evidence is too complex for a linear evolutionary sequence.

### Unk versus Unktehi

Walker’s cycle makes Unk a divine female cast into the waters and closely associates her with Unktehi. Earlier Dakota sources do not supply that genealogy. Scholars have therefore debated whether Walker preserved esoteric Oglala teaching, whether George Sword translated Lakota concepts into a systematic theology for Walker, or whether Walker imposed too much coherence. The safest practice is to cite the exact Walker document and identify whether it is an attributed Indigenous statement or Walker’s literary reconstruction.

### Fossils and myth

Adrienne Mayor and other scholars have treated Unktehi traditions as Indigenous interpretations of fossil remains. The material association is well attested; the causal claim that fossils “created” the tradition is not. Existing sacred categories could interpret fossil discoveries, and discoveries could in turn enrich stories and imagery.

### Missionary bias

Eastman, Riggs, Pond, and some later compilers preserved invaluable information while describing Dakota religion through Christian categories such as “heathenism,” “superstition,” “demon,” or deficient monotheism. Their hostility is part of the documents’ provenance, not a reason to discard all their reported material. Comparison between independent narrators, Dakota-language texts, and later Indigenous testimony is essential.

### Black Elk and editorial mediation

*Black Elk Speaks* is not a stenographic transcript. Neihardt organized Black Elk’s interviews into a literary autobiography and ended before much of Black Elk’s later Catholic life. DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* permits comparison with the interview records. Brown’s *The Sacred Pipe* is a different collaborative project with a different subject and editorial theology. Neither should be mined as an all-purpose encyclopedia of Lakota religion.

### State of the archive

Important holdings include:

- Walker papers and associated Lakota-language manuscripts;
- the American Philosophical Society’s Boas/Deloria materials;
- Smithsonian Bureau of American Ethnology publications and collections;
- Densmore’s wax-cylinder recordings and field documentation;
- winter counts in Smithsonian, tribal, state, and private collections;
- museum hides, bundles, and painted objects, many with incomplete provenance.

Winter counts are annual mnemonic histories, not theological scriptures. No securely identified winter-count glyph documenting Unktehi by name was found in the accessible catalogues. That negative finding matters: the figure’s record is primarily oral, linguistic, ethnographic, ritual, and geographical.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| **Before European documentation** | Oral, ritual, landscape, and fossil traditions necessarily predate surviving publications, but no absolute origin date is recoverable. |
| **1830s–1840s** | Gideon and Samuel Pond and other Dakota Mission workers collect language and religious information in Minnesota; later publications preserve Unktehi accounts. |
| **1841–1848** | Mary Eastman resides around Fort Snelling and hears Dakota narratives, notably through Checkered Cloud. |
| **1849** | *Dahcotah* publishes the earliest securely dated named accounts: horned omen, St. Anthony Falls flood, Morgan’s Bluff dwelling, and battle with Thunder Beings. |
| **1852** | Riggs publishes *Grammar and Dictionary of the Dakota Language*, establishing an early lexicographic record. |
| **1860s–1889** | Pond’s Dakota religious accounts circulate and are published/reprinted in Minnesota Historical Society collections. |
| **1883** | Federal Code of Indian Offenses criminalizes the Sun Dance and related ceremonies, disrupting the wider ceremonial setting in which sacred-being knowledge was transmitted. |
| **1890** | Lakota Ghost Dance reaches its crisis; Sitting Bull is killed December 15 and the Wounded Knee massacre occurs December 29. No secure source makes Unktehi a central Ghost Dance being. |
| **1894** | Dorsey publishes *A Study of Siouan Cults*, including Unktehi’s ceremonial, creative, and bundle-giving roles and Bushotter’s water-monster evidence. |
| **1896–1914** | Walker collects Oglala testimony at Pine Ridge from George Sword, Bad Wound, Finger, Thomas Tyon, and others. |
| **1917** | Walker’s *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota* appears. |
| **1918** | Densmore publishes *Teton Sioux Music*; invaluable ritual context but no central Unktehi corpus. |
| **1920s–1930s** | Public and semi-public ceremonial renewal accelerates despite earlier suppression. |
| **1932** | Deloria publishes *Dakota Texts*; Neihardt publishes *Black Elk Speaks*. |
| **1934** | Collier-era policy formally reduces federal administrative hostility to Native ceremonies. |
| **1947–1950** | Black Elk gives Joseph Epes Brown the ritual accounts later published as *The Sacred Pipe*. |
| **1953** | *The Sacred Pipe* published. |
| **1969** | Lame Deer records the flood, Pipestone, Badlands, and eagle-survivor narrative at Winner, South Dakota. |
| **1972** | *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* publishes that account. |
| **1977–1980** | Powers’s *Oglala Religion* and the edited *Lakota Belief and Ritual* place the Walker materials in modern anthropological context. |
| **1978** | American Indian Religious Freedom Act declares federal policy protecting Indigenous religious exercise, though enforcement and sacred-site protection remain limited. |
| **1983/2006** | Walker’s *Lakota Myth* appears in edited form and later reissue, explicitly distinguishing attributed narratives from Walker’s synthesis. |
| **1987** | DeMallie and Parks’s *Sioux Indian Religion* emphasizes internal diversity, historical change, and living practice. |
| **2000** | Dooling’s *The Sons of the Wind* publishes a polished modern retelling of Walker’s cycle. |
| **2002–2006** | Dakota advocacy protects Oȟéyawahe/Pilot Knob from development; ethnographic investigations document continuing Unktehi associations at Minnesota sacred sites. |
| **2022** | Marlena Myles’s Dakota Spirit Walk gives Unktehi a new Indigenous digital-art form in St. Paul. |
| **Present** | Unktehi remains a living Dakota/Lakota sacred and narrative being, a figure in language and land education, and a frequently distorted subject of popular “cryptid” culture. |

## 11. Conclusions

The most defensible reconstruction is not of one horned-serpent god with a fixed biography. It is of a historically changing complex of Dakota and Lakota water powers:

1. In the earliest published Minnesota evidence, Unktehi is a horned, river-dwelling sacred power associated with floods, falls, subterranean passages, omens, and combat with Thunder.
2. Nineteenth-century Dakota ethnology also makes Unktehi a creator and giver of sacred bundles and the Mystery Dance.
3. Oglala testimony collected by Walker describes a class of horned quadrupedal water beings and preserves competing accounts of ceremonial revelation.
4. Walker’s organized genealogy of Unk, Maka, and the other divine powers is valuable but partly systematized and cannot be treated as a universal ancient creed.
5. Lame Deer’s 1969 account joins Unktehi to flood, Pipestone, Badlands fossils, petrification, eagle rescue, and human renewal in the fullest surviving narrative.
6. The direct historical link between Unktehi and the 1890 Ghost Dance is unestablished. Their worlds overlap, but similarity of renewal or flood imagery is not documentary proof.
7. There is no evidence for an exclusive temple cult, institutional human sacrifice, Renaissance occult lineage, or ancient neopagan correspondence system.
8. Contemporary Dakota and Lakota uses are not merely “revivals” of a dead mythology. They belong to living communities negotiating language revitalization, sacred-site protection, artistic innovation, and the consequences of colonial suppression.
