# Skan: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** Sacred power or divine being (*wakȟáŋ*/*wakȟáŋpi*), specifically the Sky and the power by which things move; in James R. Walker’s Oglala materials, one of four “Superior” sacred beings and sometimes the supreme judge or governing power. “God” is a serviceable but potentially misleading English shorthand.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest located printed reference is Stephen R. Riggs’s *Tah-koo Wah-kan; or, The Gospel among the Dakotas* (1869), which records **Takú Škaŋškáŋ** as the “motion god” addressed as “father” in a Dakota prayer-song. Samuel W. Pond’s *The Dakotas or Sioux in Minnesota as They Were in 1834* preserves related nineteenth-century observations but was published posthumously in 1908. James Owen Dorsey’s *A Study of Siouan Cults* (1894), using earlier Dakota testimony, describes Takú Škaŋškáŋ as “the Moving deity.” The shorter, esoteric name **Škáŋ**, its identification with the sky, and its elaborate position as cosmic judge are most securely documented in Oglala testimony collected by James R. Walker at Pine Ridge between 1896 and 1914 and first published in 1917.

**Evidentiary warning:** There is no pre-contact written Lakota corpus. Every written attestation postdates sustained colonial and Christian contact. The fullest “pantheon” is a historical Oglala theological formulation transmitted through particular religious specialists—especially George Sword, Finger, Bad Wound, Thomas Tyon, and Little Wound—to Walker. It must not automatically be treated as an invariant theology of every Lakota, Dakota, or Nakota community.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Škáŋ

Modern standardized Lakota spelling is **Škáŋ**; Walker printed forms including *Skan* and *Škan*. Walker’s collaborators associated it with both **the sky** and an omnipresent power that gives motion and life. Walker eventually wrote that several informants, including George Sword, approved “sky” as a translation while insisting that Škáŋ was also a spirit everywhere present, giving life and movement to whatever lives or moves.

- **[Securely attested, Oglala, 1896–1914]** Škáŋ is the sky.
- **[Securely attested, Oglala, 1896–1914]** Škáŋ is the pervasive cause or principle of motion.
- **[Regional theological systematization]** Škáŋ is a distinct personal member of a ranked divine council and adjudicates disputes among other sacred beings.
- **[Translation convention]** “Sky god,” “God of Motion,” “Great Spirit,” and “Supreme God” each translate only part of the recorded semantic field.

The stem *škaŋ* is connected with action, movement, or doing. This supports “that which moves” or “the moving power,” but English nominalization makes the expression sound more like a discrete person than Lakota grammar necessarily requires. The repetition in *škaŋškáŋ* is distributive or iterative: movement occurring throughout or continually.

### Takú Škaŋškáŋ

**Takú Škaŋškáŋ** appears in older orthographies as *Taku Skanskan*, *Taku Skan-skan*, *Takuskanskan*, and *Takushkanshkan*. A cautious gloss is **“that which moves/makes movement”** or **“what moves continually.”** *Takú* means “what?” or “something/that which,” depending on construction.

- **[Early printed Dakota evidence, 1869–1894]** A moving, ubiquitous sacred power invoked in prayer, visionary practice, war-related objects, winds, stones, and the vapor bath.
- **[Walker’s Oglala explanation, early twentieth century]** The public or ordinary name corresponding to the more esoteric *Škáŋ*.
- **[Later scholarly reconstruction]** “Cosmic energy” or “life force.” These phrases can clarify the concept but are not literal translations and may import modern metaphysical vocabulary.

Walker wrote in 1912 that younger Lakotas commonly used *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* for the Christian God, whereas older people used it for the being formerly called *Takú Škaŋškáŋ*, and holy men used *Škáŋ*. This is valuable historical testimony but also Walker’s own evolutionary reconstruction of terminology, not a dated indigenous creed.

### Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka and related identifications

*Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* is commonly translated “Great Spirit,” but “Great Mystery,” “Great Sacred,” or the totality of great sacred powers better represents many historical usages. George Sword reportedly told Walker that “Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka are many,” although the powers could also be spoken of as one.

- **[Oglala systematization]** Škáŋ is one constituent of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka.
- **[Variant within Walker’s archive]** Škáŋ is effectively the unity or governing power of all the *wakȟáŋpi*.
- **[Christian-era syncretism]** Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka, Škáŋ, and the Christian God could be equated, partially equated, or kept conceptually distinct.
- **[Not established]** A universal pre-contact Lakota belief that Škáŋ was a monotheistic creator identical to the Christian God.

George Sword’s account reportedly placed Škáŋ or the corresponding supreme sky power highest. Elsewhere in the Walker scheme Wí, the Sun, is ranked first among the four Superior beings and Škáŋ second. This is a genuine conflict in the surviving evidence, not something to harmonize.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Nineteenth-century evidence

#### Riggs, 1869

Riggs printed a Dakota prayer-song addressed to *Takú Škaŋškáŋ*, called the “motion god” and “father.” The song invokes the sacred power in connection with east or west and shooting an arrow. This proves that the name and a cultic address to it circulated in the nineteenth century. It does **not** establish Walker’s later sixteen-power hierarchy.

**Tier:** securely attested early printed Dakota material, filtered through a missionary linguist.

#### Lynd/Pond materials

Nineteenth-century Dakota observers describe Takú Škaŋškáŋ as an invisible or mobile power associated with the four winds, consecrated weapons, stones, the sweat lodge, and release from ritual binding. One source simultaneously says no single deity was universally supreme: different Dakota religious specialists privileged different powers.

**Tier:** early ethnography; regional Dakota evidence, not automatically Oglala doctrine.

#### Dorsey, 1894

Dorsey’s *A Study of Siouan Cults* includes a discrete section on “Takuskanskan, the Moving deity.” It describes the power as ubiquitous and influential over human fortune while preserving evidence that Dakota worship was decentralized and variable.

**Tier:** securely published comparative ethnography assembled from earlier testimony.

### Walker corpus, 1896–1917 and later editions

Walker was agency physician at Pine Ridge from 1896 to 1914. His principal collaborators included George Sword, Finger, Bad Wound, No Flesh, Thomas Tyon, Little Wound, and other Oglala authorities. His first synthesis appeared as “The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota” in the American Museum of Natural History’s *Anthropological Papers* 16(2), 1917.

The archival materials were later edited as:

- *Lakota Belief and Ritual*, edited by Raymond J. DeMallie and Elaine A. Jahner (1980).
- *Lakota Myth*, edited by Elaine A. Jahner (1983; later reprints).
- *Lakota Society*, edited by Raymond J. DeMallie (1982).

The important distinction is between:

1. Lakota statements recorded or translated by Walker;
2. Walker’s analytical summaries;
3. Walker’s composite literary “mythic cycle.”

The third is not an ancient, unitary Lakota scripture. Jahner’s edition shows that Walker assembled and connected episodes from multiple contributors, sometimes in his own prose. It remains evidence for the traditions he received, but not for a verbatim indigenous canon.

Within *Lakota Belief and Ritual*, the documents “Foundations,” “The Secret Knowledge of Shamans,” “Kan and Its Derivatives,” and “Ni, Ini, and Initi” are especially relevant. They connect Škáŋ with cosmic order, movement, breath, spirit, and the distinctions among *ní*, *naǧí*, *naǧíla*, and *šičúŋ*.

### Densmore

Frances Densmore’s *Teton Sioux Music* (BAE Bulletin 61, 1918) documents Teton songs, prayers, ritual testimony, the sacred pipe, spirit keeping, and the Sun Dance. On page 201 she cites Takú Škaŋškáŋ as “the moving deity” and the most powerful or most feared of the Dakota gods, dwelling in the four winds and affecting human welfare. Her formulation relies partly on earlier ethnographic literature and should not be mistaken for a new independent creed from one named singer.

Densmore’s recorded songs are nevertheless indispensable evidence for lived ritual practice. The modern Densmore Repatriation Project has re-recorded many songs while emphasizing that the knowledge remains the cultural property of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe.

### Ella Deloria

Ella Cara Deloria’s *Dakota Texts* (1932) presents sixty-three Dakota-language narratives. More than half of her collected Lakota texts remained unpublished in the Franz Boas collection at the American Philosophical Society; Indiana University’s Lakota Texts project has transcribed and glossed a substantial selection.

Deloria’s corpus confirms that Dakota/Lakota sacred narrative was plural, performative, linguistically specific, and not reducible to Walker’s arrangement. Škáŋ is not everywhere foregrounded as a separately narrated god. That absence is important evidence against treating Walker’s ranked pantheon as universal.

### Black Elk

*Black Elk Speaks* (1932) is John G. Neihardt’s literary reconstruction of interviews with Nicholas Black Elk, mediated through translation. Raymond DeMallie’s *The Sixth Grandfather* (1984) publishes the interview records and demonstrates that Neihardt rearranged material and added passages, including framing language. Consequently:

- Black Elk’s visions and ritual explanations are primary testimony in mediated form.
- Neihardt’s polished cosmic narrative is a modern literary text, not a verbatim transcript.
- Black Elk’s *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* cannot automatically be replaced with Walker’s Škáŋ.
- Black Elk’s simultaneous Catholic and Lakota commitments make his testimony evidence for creative religious continuity and dialogue, not simple “corruption.”

Joseph Epes Brown’s *The Sacred Pipe* (1953) presents Black Elk’s account of seven rites and explicitly invokes *Takú Škaŋškáŋ* or Škáŋ as an all-present power. It reflects Black Elk’s later theological teaching, Brown’s editorial mediation, and a mid-twentieth-century effort to present Lakota religion systematically.

### Later ethnography

William K. Powers’s *Oglala Religion* (1977), *Yuwipi* studies, DeMallie and Douglas Parks’s *Sioux Indian Religion* (1987), Richard Erdoes and John Lame Deer’s *Lame Deer: Seeker of Visions* (1972), and D. M. Dooling’s *The Sons of the Wind* (1984) document later interpretations and narrative collections. None provides grounds for retrojecting one fixed Škáŋ theology into all earlier periods.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

- **Sky:** Walker’s collaborators explicitly identified Škáŋ with the sky.
- **Movement:** Takú Škaŋškáŋ is whatever moves or makes movement possible.
- **Life and animation:** Škáŋ supplies the animating condition of living and moving things.
- **Breath:** In Walker’s analysis, **ní** is breath or life; the relationship of Škáŋ to *ní* makes Škáŋ a source of animation, though “creator of every individual soul” is a stronger and less secure formulation.
- **Cosmic law and judgment:** Walker’s mythic system makes Škáŋ the hearer of disputes, issuer of decrees, assigner of stations, and judge among sacred beings.
- **Direction and celestial order:** Škáŋ places or governs Wí, day and night, shadows, stars, and cosmic movements.
- **Postmortem judgment:** Some Walker-derived summaries make Škáŋ judge the dead or direct spirits along the Milky Way. This belongs to the Walker complex and should not be universalized.
- **Human fortune:** Earlier Dakota descriptions stress Takú Škaŋškáŋ’s unpredictable influence over health, war, success, and misfortune.

### Form and visibility

No stable anthropomorphic form is securely documented. Nineteenth-century sources call Takú Škaŋškáŋ invisible and ubiquitous. Walker’s narratives employ personal speech and kinship, but grammatical personification is not proof of an envisioned human body.

**Iconographic finding:** There is no established ancient statue type, temple image, canonical color, or unique emblem by which Škáŋ can be securely identified. Modern depictions of a male “sky god” are artistic interpretation.

Associated manifestations or media include:

- the blue sky or sky-dome;
- moving air and the four winds;
- stars as companions, messengers, or sources connected with spirit;
- consecrated spears and tomahawks in some Dakota accounts;
- stones or boulders;
- steam and motion in the sweat lodge;
- breath.

These are associations, not necessarily “symbols” in the Western heraldic sense.

### Powers and limitations

In Walker’s literary cycle, Škáŋ can issue cosmic decrees, assign places and functions, create or appoint companions, and punish disobedience. Yet he is not always an unlimited creator: Íŋyaŋ, Makȟá, Wí, and other primordial beings possess their own agency. One feast narrative even gives Takú Škaŋškáŋ power over moving things only after the assembled powers grant his request; Iktómi then makes him sleepy. This comic limitation conflicts with presentations of Škáŋ as straightforwardly omnipotent.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Competing structures

#### Walker’s four Superior powers

- Wí — Sun
- Škáŋ — Sky/motion
- Makȟá — Earth
- Íŋyaŋ — Rock

The corresponding “Associate” powers vary in presentation but generally include Haŋwí, Tȟaté, Wóȟpe, and Wakíŋyaŋ. Walker and later interpreters arrange sixteen sacred powers in four groups of four.

**Tier:** documented Oglala theological systematization; historically important but not universal canon.

#### George Sword’s supreme-power account

Sword’s “Foundations” and related teachings place a supreme sky or moving power at the origin or summit, associated with breath and life. Depending on translation and editorial framing, that power is called Škáŋ, Naǧí Tȟáŋka, or Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka.

**Tier:** named Oglala authority’s account, recorded after Christian contact.

#### Feast tradition

In Little Wound’s “Feast by Tȟaté,” Wí, Haŋwí, “Wakan-skan,” and Íŋyaŋ appear among the senior powers. Takú Škaŋškáŋ attends the feast and asks for authority over everything moving so that he can protect and benefit it. This makes his jurisdiction a conferred office rather than an eternal attribute.

**Tier:** regional narrative variant recorded by Walker.

### Every documented divine interaction located

The following are discrete interactions in Walker-associated materials. The list is intentionally limited to interactions for which a narrative or doctrinal statement was located.

- **Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka — Škáŋ:** Škáŋ is described either as one aspect/member of the Great Sacred totality or as its operative unity. Walker, materials collected 1896–1914; published 1917/1980. **[Oglala systematization; Christian-era terminology disputed]**
- **Íŋyaŋ — Škáŋ:** In Walker’s creation sequence, the loss or release of Íŋyaŋ’s power in creating Makȟá contributes to the emergence of water and the moving/animating power identified with Škáŋ. Walker, *Lakota Myth*, collected 1896–1914, edited 1983. **[Regional creation variant; editorially synthesized]**
- **Makȟá — Škáŋ:** Makȟá complains of darkness, cold, heat, or cosmic discomfort; Škáŋ responds by arranging light, shadow, day, and night. Walker’s literary cycle, early twentieth century. **[Editorially connected Oglala mythic material]**
- **Wí — Škáŋ:** Škáŋ places Wí above the sky, orders him to illuminate material things and give them shadows, and later regulates his alternation with night. Walker’s literary cycle. **[Regional/systematized]**
- **Haŋ/Anp — Škáŋ:** Škáŋ commands Night and Dawn/Day to follow one another, establishing temporal alternation. Walker’s literary cycle. **[Regional/systematized]**
- **Tȟaté — Škáŋ:** Tȟaté, Wind, is Škáŋ’s companion, associate, manifestation, or creation in different Walker formulations. *Lakota Belief and Ritual* and *Lakota Myth*. **[Oglala systematization; versions vary]**
- **Wóȟpe — Škáŋ:** Some Walker texts make Wóȟpe Škáŋ’s daughter, made from his essence and sent downward as mediator; other genealogies make her the daughter of Wí and Haŋwí or connect her with the Buffalo people. *Lakota Myth*. **[Conflicting regional/editorial genealogies]**
- **Stars/Wičháȟpi — Škáŋ:** Škáŋ creates or appoints stars as companions and messengers, fixes a chief star, and orders their motion. Walker, *Lakota Myth*, especially the celestial-order material. **[Regional/systematized]**
- **Kšá — Škáŋ:** Škáŋ commissions wise Kšá to order or teach; after Kšá’s transgression and corruption through Gnaski, Škáŋ judges and demotes him. Walker mythic cycle. **[Regional/systematized]**
- **Iktómi — Škáŋ:** Škáŋ’s judgment transforms or condemns the fallen Kšá into Iktómi, whose wisdom becomes trickery. Walker mythic cycle. **[Regional/systematized]**
- **Iktómi — Takú Škaŋškáŋ:** At Tȟaté’s feast, Iktómi counteracts the gifts granted to the guests; when Takú Škaŋškáŋ receives power over moving things, Iktómi makes him sleepy. Little Wound’s “Feast by Tȟaté,” published by Walker in 1917. **[Named regional narrative]**
- **Tȟaté and assembled sacred beings — Takú Škaŋškáŋ:** Tȟaté invites him to the divine feast; the council hears his origin and grants his desired jurisdiction over moving things. Little Wound/Walker, 1917. **[Named regional narrative]**
- **Wakíŋyaŋ — Škáŋ:** Both participate in the larger sacred order, but no direct dyadic encounter securely isolated beyond council membership and structural pairing systems. **[Absence of specific interaction]**
- **Haŋwí — Škáŋ:** Joint council/cosmic-order appearances are documented; no securely isolated personal episode between them was located. **[Absence of specific interaction]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The ordering of the cosmos

**[Walker systematization]** In the composite cycle, primordial material and sacred powers exist before the ordered world. Íŋyaŋ expends his vitality in bringing forth or shaping Makȟá; water and mobile power enter creation; Škáŋ encompasses the world as sky and orders the motions of celestial beings. Makȟá repeatedly complains about cosmic conditions. Škáŋ places Wí above, creates or regulates shadows, and orders day and night to alternate.

The central meaning is not creation from nothing but the transformation, distribution, and ordering of already sacred powers. Walker’s transitions produce an unusually coherent cosmogony, and that coherence itself must be treated as partly editorial.

### The assignment of stations

**[Walker systematization]** Škáŋ acts as judge. He hears disputes and assigns each sacred being a position, office, or limitation. Wí travels his course; day and night alternate; stars receive ordered paths; other beings receive domains. This is the chief textual basis for “judge of the gods who gave each his place.”

No evidence establishes that every Lakota community held a formal myth in precisely this shape. It is safest to call it the **Walker Oglala cycle**.

### The fall of Kšá and origin of Iktómi

**[Regional/systematized]** Kšá is wisdom or knowledge, initially a respected sacred being. Through misconduct, deception, or entanglement with Gnaski, he violates the sacred order. Škáŋ judges him, excludes him from the feasts or council of the gods, and reduces his wisdom to cunning. He becomes Iktómi, the spider-trickster.

This cycle explains how intelligence becomes morally ambiguous and why the trickster retains supernatural cleverness while repeatedly trapping himself. Popular summaries often omit that the narrative’s coherent “fall” structure comes especially from Walker.

### The feast of Tȟaté

**[Named regional narrative: Little Wound]** Tȟaté gives a feast when Wóȟpe comes to stay with him. Numerous sacred beings are invited, including Takú Škaŋškáŋ. They recount their origins and powers. The guests receive desired gifts, while Iktómi adds counterbalancing defects. Takú Škaŋškáŋ asks for power over everything moving in order to protect it and do good; the request is granted, but Iktómi makes him sleepy.

This story provides a markedly different theology from the claim that Škáŋ was inherently omnipotent from the beginning.

### Breath, spirit, and death

**[George Sword/Walker doctrinal material]** Walker’s documents distinguish breath (*ní*), ghost or surviving personal spirit (*naǧí*), spirit-like diminutive or aspect (*naǧíla*), and an immanent potency often written *šičúŋ*. Škáŋ is connected with the animation that makes living things breathe and move. Stars, the Milky Way, and the return or journey of spirits are incorporated into some explanations.

The terminology is difficult, contributors did not always agree, and English “soul” obscures the distinctions. Claims that Škáŋ manufactures a single Western-style immortal soul should therefore be rejected.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No temple cult

No dedicated temple, permanent cult image, hereditary “priesthood of Škáŋ,” or excavated sanctuary exclusively attributable to Škáŋ is known. Lakota religion was practiced through kinship, sacred bundles, pipes, songs, visions, offerings, specialist knowledge, and ceremonial places rather than temple institutions.

### Prayer and naming

Takú Škaŋškáŋ was addressed as “father” in nineteenth-century prayer-song. Such address establishes devotional relationship but not exclusive worship. Lakota sacred powers were commonly approached through kin terms such as *Tȟuŋkášila* (“grandfather”).

### Inípi and ritual steam

Nineteenth-century Dakota accounts connect Takú Škaŋškáŋ with the vapor bath or sweat lodge. Movement, heated stones, water transformed into steam, breath, and prayer make the association conceptually appropriate. The ceremony is not, however, exclusively a “cult of Škáŋ.”

### Binding and release

An early account describes a bound ritual specialist emerging freed from knots after praying for an encounter with Takú Škaŋškáŋ. This is evidence for a specific ritual complex involving sacred power, not proof that invisible divine hands physically performed the release.

### Sun Dance

The Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi, often translated “Gazing-at-the-Sun Dance,” principally addresses the Sun and the sacred totality. Škáŋ participates through sky, breath, motion, winds, and cosmic order but is not necessarily the sole recipient.

The United States suppressed Plains ceremonies through agency rules. The last “old-time” Rosebud Sun Dance is often dated to 1883, but practice did not disappear uniformly; ceremonies persisted, changed form, or resumed locally before federal policy formally softened. John Collier’s 1934 circular ended many Bureau objections to Native ceremonies, and the American Indian Religious Freedom Act followed in 1978.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Documented offerings include tobacco, smoke, food, flesh offered through Sun Dance piercing, cloth, pipes, and prayer. Animal sacrifice and food distribution occurred in broader Lakota ritual contexts.

**Contested-material finding:** No credible evidence located documents human sacrifice specifically to Škáŋ. Sun Dance piercing is voluntary ritual suffering, not human sacrifice. Claims that Lakota people sacrificed humans to a “sky god” are unsupported conflations.

**Temple prostitution:** No evidence. The category is foreign to the documented institutional setting.

### Sacred geography

Škáŋ, as sky and moving sacred power, is not confined to a single shrine. Broader Lakota religious practice remains inseparable from sacred places:

- **Pahá Sápa (Black Hills):** sacred landscape, ceremonial and historical center.
- **Matȟó Thípila/Bear Lodge (“Devils Tower”):** site of prayer, fasting, offerings, sweat lodges, vision seeking, and narratives of bears and celestial transformation. The National Park Service requests voluntary restraint from climbing during June because of ongoing ceremonies.
- **Matȟó Pahá/Bear Butte:** sacred to several Plains nations and used for prayer and vision seeking.

These are not securely documented as exclusive “temples of Škáŋ.”

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before written attestation

Linguistic distribution and nineteenth-century testimony make the moving-power concept older than Walker. Its precise antiquity cannot be dated. Claims of an unchanged theology “from time immemorial” are traditional statements, not archaeologically datable propositions.

### 1660s–mid-nineteenth century: contact and missionization

Catholic and later Protestant contact introduced Christian vocabulary and comparison with a single creator God. Missionaries used *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* to translate “God.” By the time detailed ethnography began, interpretation was already dialogical.

### 1869–1894: first printed record

Riggs, Lynd/Pond, and Dorsey documented Takú Škaŋškáŋ as a moving, pervasive power. These sources emphasize ritual address, wind, weapons, stones, vapor-bath practice, and variable personal devotion. They do not present Walker’s complete hierarchy.

### 1883–1890: suppression and crisis

Federal agents sought to end the Sun Dance and other ceremonies. The destruction of buffalo economies, compulsory schooling, land seizure, confinement, and missionary pressure profoundly altered religious transmission.

The Ghost Dance reached Lakota communities in 1889–1890. Its central prophetic source was Wovoka, not Škáŋ. Lakota adherents reinterpreted it through their own sacred world. On December 29, 1890, the Seventh Cavalry killed more than 250 Lakota people at Wounded Knee while attempting to disarm Spotted Elk’s band. It is misleading to portray the massacre as a “cult event” of Škáŋ; it belongs to the documented history of state violence against Lakota religious and political life.

### 1896–1917: Walker’s documentation

Walker worked with Oglala religious authorities during a period when ceremonies were suppressed and specialist knowledge was in danger of loss. His collaborators consciously entrusted teachings to writing. Their circumstances help explain both the urgency and the systematic quality of the resulting record.

### 1917–1953: publication and theological reframing

Walker and Densmore made sacred concepts available to a global readership. Black Elk, Neihardt, and Joseph Epes Brown later presented Lakota religion through visionary, perennialist, and comparative-religion frameworks. Škáŋ increasingly appeared in English as a universal “Great Spirit,” “cosmic power,” or “energy.”

### 1934–1978: ceremonial renewal

Federal policy became less overtly prohibitive after 1934, and public Sun Dances revived or expanded. Renewal was not the recovery of a frozen pre-1883 form; communities adapted ceremony while maintaining claims of continuity. The 1978 American Indian Religious Freedom Act recognized a federal policy of protecting Native religious exercise, although it initially supplied weak judicial remedies.

### Late twentieth century–present

Škáŋ survives primarily within a living network of Lakota language, prayer, ceremonial teaching, kinship, landscape, and sacred-power discourse—not as a church-like cult with membership statistics. Contemporary educators and artists sometimes represent Škáŋ as “movement” or the sky among the powers of Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka. Such representations can be indigenous cultural renewal without proving that every detail of Walker’s pantheon was universally ancient.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Perennialist and New Age readings

*The Sacred Pipe* helped make Lakota spirituality legible to comparative religion and perennial philosophy. Subsequent readers translated Škáŋ as “universal energy,” “vibration,” “cosmic consciousness,” or an indigenous equivalent of *qi*, Brahman, or the Holy Spirit.

**Tier:** modern comparative interpretation. No historical evidence establishes those exact equivalences.

### Neopagan and occult reception

Websites and popular mythology handbooks increasingly list Škáŋ as a discrete male “god of the sky and motion,” often with a clean genealogy, fixed rank, and fantasy-style powers. This presentation usually derives from Walker through tertiary summaries.

**Dedicated contested-material finding:**

- No documented Lakota-derived Western occult order centered on Škáŋ was located.
- No significant role in the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, Renaissance Hermetism, or historical grimoire magic was found.
- Modern ritual invocations by non-Lakota occultists are modern appropriation or invention unless tied to accountable Lakota teachers.
- “Škáŋ as chaos deity” is a distortion, probably produced by conflating unpredictable motion with moral chaos.
- Claims connecting Škáŋ to human sacrifice, Satanism, or a global prehistoric sky-god cult are unsupported.

### Literature, games, and popular reference

Škáŋ has had less independent mass-media reception than Iktómi, White Buffalo Calf Woman, or Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka. Modern deity databases nevertheless amplify the Walker figure by presenting a short, internally consistent encyclopedia entry. That format suppresses local variation and source mediation.

### Contemporary Lakota art

Keith BraveHeart’s *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* portrays young people embodying eight sacred powers, including Skaŋ as movement or sky. This is a contemporary Lakota artistic interpretation and an important form of cultural transmission, not an archaeological reconstruction of an ancient idol.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Škáŋ a supreme being?

Evidence for:

- early identification of Takú Škaŋškáŋ as exceptionally powerful;
- George Sword’s high or supreme formulation;
- Walker’s description of an everywhere-present source of life and motion;
- Škáŋ’s judicial role in the Walker cycle.

Evidence against universal supremacy:

- nineteenth-century testimony says different Dakota people honored different powers as highest;
- Walker’s own hierarchy can rank Wí before Škáŋ;
- Little Wound’s feast makes Škáŋ’s jurisdiction a granted gift;
- other Lakota corpora do not consistently isolate Škáŋ as the central personal deity.

**Conclusion:** Škáŋ’s supremacy is securely documented as one historical Oglala theological position, not as an uncontested pan-Sioux dogma.

### Person or principle?

The sources alternate between personal agency—Škáŋ speaks, judges, creates, punishes—and abstract function—sky, motion, animation. Lakota sacred ontology does not require the Western choice between “person” and “impersonal force.” Translating Škáŋ as either merely a weather god or merely scientific “energy” loses evidence.

### Christian influence

Missionary influence is undeniable because the records postdate contact and Christian vocabulary had entered Lakota speech. Yet it does not follow that Škáŋ was invented by missionaries. The 1869 prayer, ritual associations, linguistic structure, and testimony from multiple specialists demonstrate an indigenous moving-power complex.

The debated issue is how far the **supreme, unitary, morally governing** presentation was reshaped through Christian dialogue. George Sword himself knew Christianity, and Walker used theological categories familiar to Euro-American readers. Scholars therefore treat the sixteen-power scheme as real historical Lakota testimony while questioning its universality and pre-contact form.

### Walker’s authorship

Walker’s achievement was exceptional access to named Oglala specialists and preservation of extensive testimony. His weakness was synthesis: he sought a consistent religious system and composed connective narrative. Jahner and DeMallie’s editions allow readers to distinguish documentary strata more carefully.

### Neihardt and Black Elk

DeMallie demonstrated that *Black Elk Speaks* is a literary collaboration shaped substantially by Neihardt. Its worldwide influence makes it part of Lakota religious history, but it cannot be cited as an unedited transcript. Black Elk’s later Catholicism likewise cannot be erased to create a pristine “traditional” informant.

### Etymology

The association of Škáŋ with movement is linguistically and contextually strong. More speculative claims—that it literally means “energy,” “consciousness,” “ether,” or “quantum vibration”—are modern interpretive expansions.

### Iconography and archaeology

There are no securely identified pre-contact images of Škáŋ. Rock art, solar signs, spirals, thunderbirds, and sky motifs cannot be assigned to Škáŋ without independent contextual evidence.

### Archives and surviving evidence

Important repositories include:

- Walker papers associated with the Colorado Historical Society/History Colorado;
- American Museum of Natural History publications and collections;
- Densmore papers and recordings at the Smithsonian/National Anthropological Archives;
- Ella Deloria materials in the Boas collection at the American Philosophical Society;
- Black Elk–Neihardt interview records edited in *The Sixth Grandfather*.

Some ceremonial knowledge was never disclosed, some remains community-restricted, and some was lost through deaths, suppression, boarding schools, language decline, and editorial omission. A documentary dossier cannot be assumed to exhaust the living tradition.

## 10. Chronology

- **Pre-contact:** A Lakota/Dakota moving-power concept is linguistically probable, but its form cannot be dated from written evidence. **[Reconstruction]**
- **1830s–1850s:** Missionaries and observers record Dakota sacred-power concepts; later-published accounts mention Takú Škaŋškáŋ. **[Early ethnographic testimony, publication dates later]**
- **1869:** Riggs publishes a prayer-song to Takú Škaŋškáŋ, the moving power addressed as father. **[Earliest located secure printed attestation]**
- **1883:** Often cited as the last old-form Rosebud Sun Dance before sustained federal suppression. **[Documented historical convention; local continuity varied]**
- **1893:** Riggs’s *Dakota Grammar, Texts, and Ethnography* appears posthumously.
- **1894:** Dorsey publishes *A Study of Siouan Cults*, including “Takuskanskan, the Moving deity.”
- **1889–1890:** Ghost Dance spreads among Lakota communities. **[Distinct prophetic movement, not a Škáŋ cult]**
- **December 29, 1890:** U.S. troops massacre more than 250 Lakota at Wounded Knee.
- **1896–1914:** Walker collects Oglala testimony at Pine Ridge, including Sword’s, Finger’s, Bad Wound’s, Tyon’s, and Little Wound’s accounts of Škáŋ and Takú Škaŋškáŋ.
- **1912:** Walker writes that younger speakers associate Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka with Jehovah while older/esoteric terminology points to Takú Škaŋškáŋ/Škáŋ. **[Walker’s historical interpretation]**
- **1917:** Walker publishes *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota*.
- **1918:** Densmore publishes *Teton Sioux Music*.
- **1932:** Ella Deloria publishes *Dakota Texts*; Neihardt publishes *Black Elk Speaks*.
- **1934:** Federal Indian policy formally becomes more tolerant of Native ceremonies; public ceremonial renewal expands.
- **1953:** Joseph Epes Brown publishes *The Sacred Pipe*, presenting Black Elk’s seven-rites theology and identifying the pervasive presence as Takú Škaŋškáŋ/Škáŋ.
- **1972:** *Lame Deer: Seeker of Visions* contributes a modern Lakota voice distinct from Walker’s system.
- **1977:** Powers publishes *Oglala Religion*, critically systematizing ethnographic evidence.
- **1978:** American Indian Religious Freedom Act declares federal protection of Native religious practice.
- **1980:** DeMallie and Jahner edit *Lakota Belief and Ritual*.
- **1982:** DeMallie edits *Lakota Society*.
- **1983:** Jahner edits *Lakota Myth*, exposing the documentary and literary strata behind Walker’s cycle; Buechel’s Lakota dictionary appears in a major posthumous edition.
- **1984:** DeMallie publishes *The Sixth Grandfather*, enabling direct comparison between interview records and Neihardt’s literary text.
- **1987:** DeMallie and Parks edit *Sioux Indian Religion*.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first century:** Lakota language renewal, repatriation projects, ceremonies, scholarship, and art continue to interpret Škáŋ as sky, motion, and sacred animation.
- **Present:** No evidence supports a separately organized “Church of Škáŋ”; the concept remains embedded in living Lakota religious and linguistic worlds and in modern scholarly and popular reconstructions.

### Central conclusions

1. **Škáŋ is securely historical, not a recent invented deity.** Takú Škaŋškáŋ is printed by 1869 and appears across nineteenth-century Dakota ethnography.
2. **The equation “Škáŋ = Sky = Motion” is genuinely attested**, although those terms describe overlapping dimensions rather than exact synonyms.
3. **The supreme judge who assigns every god a place belongs principally to the Walker Oglala corpus.**
4. **George Sword’s high-god account is an important named Lakota theology, not proof of universal Lakota monotheism.**
5. **The sixteen-being pantheon is documented but disputed as a universal or pre-contact system.**
6. **Breath and animation are central**, but English “soul” and “energy” can erase important distinctions among *ní*, *naǧí*, *naǧíla*, and *šičúŋ*.
7. **There is no canonical ancient iconography or temple cult of Škáŋ.**
8. **No credible evidence was found for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, a historical occult cult, or the standard modern fantasy presentation of Škáŋ as a neatly bounded male sky god.**
9. **The variants are the record:** pervasive power and personal judge, second-ranked superior and supreme unity, inherent mover and feast-guest granted motion—each is attested within a different documentary stratum.
