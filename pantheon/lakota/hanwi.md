# Hanwi: Research Dossier

> **Class:** *Wakáŋ*—a sacred/superhuman being, specifically the female Moon or Moon-person in Oglala Lakota materials. Walker’s classificatory system calls her one of the four **Wakáŋ Kȟoláya**, “Associate Gods”; “goddess” is a serviceable English gloss but imports a Western category.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The word *haŋwí* “moon” occurs in nineteenth-century Dakota/Lakota lexicography. The earliest securely recoverable extended divine narrative is the Lakota-language text conventionally attributed to the Oglala writer George Sword, written before his death in 1910 and collected within James R. Walker’s Pine Ridge corpus. Walker published a substantially edited English version, “When the People Laughed at Hanwi,” in 1917. No securely identified pre-contact inscription, manuscript, or image of Hanwi exists.
>
> **Core evidentiary conclusion:** Hanwi as the Moon is linguistically and culturally broader than Walker’s elaborate “pantheon.” Her classification as Wi’s wife, a member of a sixteen-being theological hierarchy, and protagonist of a continuous Lakota “genesis” is documented most fully in the Walker–Sword corpus and must not automatically be projected backward as a uniform, timeless belief of every Lakota, Dakota, or Nakota community.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century Lakota/Dakota-language or first-generation ethnographic evidence.
- **[WALKER–SWORD SYSTEM]** Material in Walker’s Pine Ridge corpus, especially texts associated with George Sword and Walker’s later synthesis.
- **[REGIONAL/ORAL VARIANT]** A documented community, family, speaker, or band version differing from Walker.
- **[LATER TRADITION]** Later Lakota testimony, ceremony, teaching, or published Indigenous interpretation.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** An academic analysis rather than a tradition’s own explicit claim.
- **[MODERN REINVENTION]** A modern literary, neopagan, occult, commercial, or internet claim not demonstrated in the earlier record.
- **[ABSENCE]** A claim for which the searched record yielded no adequate evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** *Haŋwí* is the ordinary Lakota/Dakota name for the Moon and, in narrative contexts, the Moon treated as a female personal being. Modern Lakota teaching materials also give *haŋhépi wí*, “night sun,” for the moon. Lakota does not necessarily impose the Western opposition between a material celestial body and a personal “deity”: the same expression can name the visible moon and the sacred person manifested by it.

**[WALKER–SWORD SYSTEM]** Walker renders Hanwi as “the Moon,” wife or “companion” of Wi, the Sun. His 1917 doctrinal table places:

- Wi, Sun, among the four **Wakáŋ Aŋkanta**, “Superior Gods”;
- Hanwi, Moon, as Wi’s associate among the four **Wakáŋ Kȟoláya**, “Associate Gods.”

The other associates are Tate, Wind; Wohpe, the Feminine/Mediator; and Wakinyan, the Winged or Thunder Being. This elegantly balanced four-by-four hierarchy is firmly attested in Walker’s materials, but its antiquity and universality are disputed.

### Forms

- **Haŋwí / Hanwi / Han-wi / Hąwí** — “Moon.” Modern standardized Lakota orthography writes *Haŋwí*. Older publications generally omit nasal and stress marking.
- **Haŋhépi Wí** — “Night Sun,” from *haŋhépi*, “night,” plus *wí*, “sun.” This is an ordinary descriptive name for the moon.
- **Haŋyétu Wí** — another analyzable “night sun” form, using *haŋyétu*, “night.”
- **Wi-wiŋ / Wi-win** — Walker-era form glossed as “female Wi” or “Sun-female.” This belongs especially to Walker’s theological synthesis and should not be silently substituted for ordinary *Haŋwí*.
- **Moon / Moon Woman / Moon Spirit / Moon Goddess** — English translations. “Moon Goddess” is common in comparative mythology and commercial material but is not a literal indigenous office-title.

Buechel’s posthumously published lexicon and the modern New Lakota Dictionary preserve the living lexical tradition; the latter uses a systematic orthography and contains more than forty thousand entries. Current Lakota educational resources explicitly list *haŋhépi wí* and *haŋwí* as “moon” ([Lakota Language Consortium](https://lakhota.org/product/new-lakota-dictionary-3rd-edition/); [WoLakota glossary](https://www.wolakotaproject.org/lakota-pronunciation-glossary/)).

### Etymology

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED / LINGUISTIC]** The transparent phrase *haŋhépi wí* means “night sun.” A common explanation similarly analyzes *Haŋwí* as “night-sun,” connecting *haŋ-* with night and *wí* with sun. This is semantically persuasive and is found in later linguistic and ethnographic discussion.

**[CAUTION]** “Hanwi means goddess of the night,” “silver guardian,” or “she whose light governs fertility” are not translations supported by Lakota dictionaries. They are interpretive expansions.

### Classification and titles

**[WALKER–SWORD SYSTEM]**

- Moon;
- Wi’s companion or wife;
- first-listed member of the Associate Gods;
- ruler or measurer of the lunar interval after separation from Wi.

“Queen of the Night,” “guardian of women,” “fertility goddess,” “dream goddess,” and “triple goddess” were not found as securely attested traditional titles.

### Cross-cultural equations

**[ABSENCE]** No early source identifies Hanwi with Selene, Luna, Artemis, Hecate, Isis, Ishtar, or another Eurasian lunar deity. Modern websites sometimes group them as “moon goddesses”; that is comparativist classification, not Lakota syncretism.

**[MODERN OFFICIAL NAMING]** The International Astronomical Union assigned **Hanwi Chasma** on Venus in 2006, describing Hanwi as an Oglala moon-and-sky goddess. **Hanghepiwi Chasma** had been named in 1997 from a Dakota designation for moon/night. These are modern astronomical commemorations, not evidence for an ancient Venus association ([USGS Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature](https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/SearchResults?Feature+Type=6_Chasma%2C+chasmata&Target=15_Venus)).

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Lexical evidence

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Missionary dictionaries demonstrate that a Dakota/Lakota moon-word cognate with *haŋwí* was recorded in the nineteenth century. Stephen R. Riggs’s dictionary originated in the 1852 Smithsonian publication and was expanded in 1890. It establishes the word’s age in writing, but a dictionary gloss “moon” alone does not establish the full feast narrative, genealogy, or Walker hierarchy.

Buechel collected Lakota vocabulary and texts among Sicangu and Oglala speakers in the early twentieth century. His dictionary appeared posthumously in 1970 and was reorganized by Paul Manhart in 2002 ([Buechel and Manhart](https://books.google.com/books/about/Lakota_Dictionary.html?id=FoLuyDwQAmEC)).

### Walker’s Pine Ridge collection

James R. Walker was physician at Pine Ridge from 1896 to 1914. His principal Lakota collaborators included George Sword, Bad Wound, Little Wound, Finger, Thomas Tyon, and other Oglala authorities. Several wrote in Lakota; Walker also used interpreters and later reorganized material.

The textual stages must be separated:

1. **Before 1910 — George Sword manuscript.**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** The Lakota text “When the People Laughed at the Moon” is conventionally attributed to George Sword and must have been written before Sword’s death in 1910.

2. **1917 — Walker, “When the People Laughed at Hanwi.”**  
   **[EDITED EARLY PUBLICATION]** Walker printed an English narrative at pages 164–67 of *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota*. The publication does not identify its speaker on the page and smooths the account into polished English narrative. Its text is directly accessible in the [1917 edition](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-sun-dance-and-other-ceremonies-of-the-oglala-division-of-the-teton-dakota/shell/when-the-people-laughed-at-hanwi).

3. **1980 — *Lakota Belief and Ritual*.**  
   **[CRITICAL RECOVERY]** Raymond J. DeMallie and Elaine A. Jahner published Walker’s less-synthesized papers, biographical information about contributors, and evidence needed to distinguish informant texts from Walker’s constructions ([University of Nebraska Press](https://www.nebraskapress.unl.edu/nebraska/9780803298866/lakota-belief-and-ritual/)).

4. **1983 — *Lakota Myth*.**  
   **[CRITICAL RECOVERY]** Jahner edited the mythic corpus, including a closer version of Sword’s Hanwi text, Walker’s later “epic,” manuscript relationships, and commentary. The critical Hanwi narrative appears at pages 52–57; Walker’s synthetic cosmogony and “Pte cycle” occupy separate later sections ([Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Lakota_Myth.html?id=LrM4QvpwnHoC)).

### The attribution dispute

**[SCHOLARLY DEBATE]** Jahner considered but rejected the possibility that “When the People Laughed at Hanwi” was actually composed by Thomas Tyon, a knowledgeable Oglala interpreter and associate of Sword. Attribution to Sword remains conventional, not absolutely demonstrated.

A further complication is that Walker apparently did not translate—or perhaps even see—the Sword manuscript until after retiring in 1914. Walker added an ending to Sword’s unfinished account and later joined separate narratives into a continuous “Lakota mythology.” Consequently:

- the pre-1910 Lakota text is the strongest early evidence;
- the 1917 English story is an edited publication;
- Walker’s 1920s grand cosmogonic synthesis is a tertiary reorganization within the corpus.

A detailed critique of this transmission, including the Sword/Tyon question and Walker’s additions, appears in Peter Jones and Robert White’s study of “myths about American Indian mythology” ([PDF](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/u.osu.edu/dist/c/53874/files/2017/10/1999-Jones-White-Myths-about-American-Indian-Mythology-1d0jc3z.pdf)).

### Other major corpora

- **Frances Densmore, *Teton Sioux Music* (1918).**  
  **[EARLY DOCUMENTED, INDIRECT]** Based on recordings and testimony gathered principally in 1911–14. It documents songs, sacred-pipe rites, spirit keeping, Sun Dance, healing, societies, and ceremonial life. It does not furnish an independent full Hanwi feast-cycle comparable to Walker’s. Its silence cautions against treating Hanwi as the center of all Lakota public ritual ([Smithsonian edition](https://repository.si.edu/items/47c6ed45-63a3-455e-8006-a71fe7e12cd2)).

- **Ella Deloria, *Dakota Texts* (1932).**  
  **[REGIONAL COMPARISON]** A major Dakota-language narrative corpus. Deloria later examined Walker’s texts for Franz Boas in 1937–39 and judged portions linguistically or culturally questionable. Her criticism is central to the historiography, though it does not prove that Sword’s individual Hanwi text is fabricated.

- **Black Elk / John G. Neihardt, *Black Elk Speaks* (1932).**  
  **[INDIRECT]** Essential for Oglala religious history, the Ghost Dance, visions, and Wounded Knee, but not a primary independent source for Walker’s Hanwi feast myth. Neihardt made a literary book from interviews conducted through Black Elk’s son Ben as interpreter.

- **Joseph Epes Brown, *The Sacred Pipe* (1953).**  
  **[LATER TRADITION]** Black Elk’s account of seven rites; crucial for twentieth-century Lakota ceremonial teaching but not an independent textual witness to Hanwi’s humiliation.

- **Raymond DeMallie, *The Sixth Grandfather* (1984).**  
  **[CRITICAL RECOVERY]** Publishes the 1931 and 1944 Black Elk interview records, revealing details omitted, rearranged, or literary-shaped in *Black Elk Speaks*. The interviews are open access through [Fulcrum](https://www.fulcrum.org/concern/monographs/cj82kb32v?locale=en) and [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.32942796).

- **William K. Powers, *Oglala Religion* (1975; later editions).**  
  **[SCHOLARLY SYNTHESIS]** Summarizes the Hanwi narrative but warns against assuming that Walker’s systematic theology is a simple transcript of universally shared Oglala doctrine.

- **DeMallie and Parks, eds., *Sioux Indian Religion* (1987).**  
  **[SCHOLARLY/INDIGENOUS INTERPRETATION]** Elaine Jahner’s “Lakota Genesis: The Oral Tradition” analyzes the Hanwi and emergence narratives as developing oral-literary works.

- **Richard Erdoes and John Fire Lame Deer, *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* (1972).**  
  **[LATER TESTIMONY]** Important for twentieth-century ceremonial experience and critique of colonial religion; its English literary mediation must be acknowledged. It supports the continued ordinary term *hanwi* for moon but is not a secure source for all details of Walker’s divine hierarchy.

- **Dooling, ed., *The Sons of the Wind* (1984).**  
  **[LATER EDITION OF EARLIER MATERIAL]** Collects Densmore, Walker, Deloria, and other versions concerning Tate and the Winds. It situates Hanwi’s feast episode within the broader wind/emergence cycle but does not eliminate contradictions between the source texts.

- **Winter counts.**  
  **[ABSENCE]** Winter counts are vital Lakota historical records, usually marking one memorable event per year. No securely identified winter-count glyph has been shown to depict Hanwi’s feast, marriage, or shame. Moon-shaped marks may date seasons or months and cannot automatically be read as portraits of Hanwi.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Hanwi is the Moon. Lunar cycles provide measures of time, and named moons organize seasonal knowledge. This lexical and calendrical function is broader and more securely rooted than Walker’s formal theology.

**[WALKER–SWORD SYSTEM]** Before the feast, Hanwi and Wi are companions associated together with the ordering of light and time. Afterward, Škaŋ separates their paths:

- Wi is associated with the Sun and day;
- Hanwi travels independently;
- the interval from departure from Wi until return becomes a third period of time—the lunar month;
- proximity to Wi produces concealment; distance permits her full face to appear.

Walker’s text says that she hides her face when near Wi and uncovers it when far away. Astronomically, this maps new moon to conjunction and full moon to opposition.

### Moon phases

**[WALKER–SWORD SYSTEM]** The phases are Hanwi’s successive covering and uncovering of her face. The covering is explicitly connected to shame inflicted at the feast. Popular summaries often say she hides because *she* behaved shamefully. The source says instead that Wi allowed Ite to usurp her seat and that other beings laughed at Hanwi. She is the injured party, although Škaŋ’s permanent arrangement obliges her to reenact the concealment.

### Appearance and emblems

The early narrative gives remarkably little physical description:

- Hanwi is implicitly female;
- she wears or uses a robe to cover her head and face;
- her visible “face” is the lunar disk;
- fullness and concealment are her chief visual signs.

**[ABSENCE]** There is no securely established ancient or pre-reservation canonical anthropomorphic image of Hanwi, no fixed number of arms, special weapon, crown, sacred animal, cult statue, temple image, or prescribed color. A crescent-bearing “Native moon goddess” in feathers or Plains-style clothing is normally modern illustration.

### Celestial and calendrical associations

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** The moon is a temporal marker. Lakota seasonal moon names vary by speaker, locality, and translation; treating one internet list as a fixed “Lakota zodiac” is unsound.

**[MODERN INDIGENOUS ART]** Spirit Lake Dakota/Mohegan artist Marlena Myles depicts Hanwi in contemporary Indigenous visual culture and interprets her through female power and motherhood. This is a living artistic interpretation, not evidence for a pre-contact iconographic canon ([Marlena Myles, “Hanwi”](https://marlenamyl.es/project/hanwi/)).

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Family and hierarchy

**[WALKER–SWORD SYSTEM]**

- **Spouse/companion:** Wi, Sun.
- **Daughter:** Wohpe, the Feminine, Beautiful Woman, Mediator, or Falling Star.
- **Superior associate:** Hanwi is paired with Wi as Tate is paired with Škaŋ, Wohpe with Maka, and Wakinyan with Inyan.
- **Parents:** No parentage for Hanwi is stated in the early feast text.

**[WALKER LATE SYNTHESIS]** In Walker’s constructed cosmogony, Wi produces or emanates a female counterpart variously called Wi-wiŋ, creating a quasi-genealogical origin for Moon. That formulation should not be treated as identical with the older and ordinary name *Haŋwí*, nor as universally accepted Lakota cosmogony.

**[REGIONAL/ORAL VARIANT]** A contemporary Cheyenne Creek/Pine Ridge emergence account places Ite’s downfall in a different divine configuration. Gnaskinyan induces her to dress as Hanwi; Takuskanskan judges the participants, and Woksape becomes Iktomi. The feast-seat episode is absent from that presentation. The National Park Service explicitly identifies it as one Cheyenne Creek community version rather than the single Lakota account ([Wind Cave NPS](https://www.nps.gov/wica/learn/historyculture/the-lakota-emergence-story.htm)).

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The humiliation of Hanwi

**Earliest recoverable form:** Sword manuscript, before 1910.  
**First publication:** Walker, 1917.

Wazi, leader of the underground people, wants divine powers. His wife Kanka is a seer; their daughter Ite is Tate’s wife and mother of four supernatural sons. Iktomi exploits the family’s ambition. He grants Wazi and Kanka powers, teaches them to make a beautifying charm, and repeatedly suggests that Ite deserves a place among the gods.

Iktomi praises Ite to Wi and tells Ite that Wi has tired of Hanwi. Kanka supplies the charm, while Ite neglects her lodge, hungry children, and husband. Wi sees her, admires her, and invites her to the divine feast.

Iktomi then goes to Hanwi and tells her that Wi considers Ite the most beautiful being and has invited her. Hanwi remains behind to adorn herself. Ite arrives early and takes the vacant seat beside Wi. Wi neither objects nor remembers his companion. When Hanwi enters, she finds her place occupied, covers her head with her robe, and stands behind Ite. The assembled people laugh; Iktomi laughs “loudest and longest.”

After the feast Hanwi stands before Škaŋ, still hiding her face. She explains that Wi allowed another woman into her seat and that the people ridiculed her. Škaŋ conducts a judicial inquiry:

- Wi admits that beauty made him forget Hanwi.
- Ite blames the prediction of her mother, Iktomi’s representations, Wi’s invitation, and the vacant seat.
- Kanka invokes her prophetic claim and powers.
- Wazi says that he sought power to do good.
- Iktomi ultimately boasts that he wanted to shame beings who had laughed at him.

Škaŋ’s rulings transform the cosmos. Wi must lose Hanwi as his companion. Hanwi may travel her own path and rules the interval between leaving and returning to Wi. Because Wi caused her to hide her face, she will conceal it near him and uncover it far away. The ruling explains the lunar month and phases.

Ite is separated from Tate and her children, becomes Anog Ite, and receives a second terrifying face. Wazi and Kanka are exiled and transformed into Wizard and Witch figures. Iktomi is condemned to the world, where he turns to animals and humans as objects of his tricks.

### What the myth explains

**[WALKER–SWORD SYSTEM]**

- why Sun and Moon do not normally traverse the sky together;
- why the Moon waxes and wanes;
- why a month is measured by return;
- the differentiation of day, night, and lunar time;
- Ite’s transformation into Double Face;
- Iktomi’s exile and antagonistic trickster role;
- the reorganization of relations between divine beings and the world.

### Was “night and day” created as Hanwi’s punishment?

The common summary requires qualification. Walker’s wording says Wi and Hanwi had already ruled “the two periods of time, day and night.” Škaŋ’s sentence does not create light and darkness from nothing. It separates their companionship and institutes a **third temporal interval**, the lunar cycle. “The separation of night and day” is therefore a reasonable shorthand for the new celestial arrangement but not the exact cosmological claim of the 1917 text.

### Whose shame?

Hanwi hides because others shame and ridicule her. She is not convicted of adultery, usurpation, or vanity. Škaŋ punishes Wi and the conspirators, but the visible consequence is permanently borne on Hanwi’s face. Modern retellings that call the phases “Hanwi’s shame for her own wrongdoing” reverse the narrative’s moral allocation.

### Development and variants

**[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Jahner reads the story as a narrative of disrupted place and restored cosmic order. The movement from an older configuration into a new one resembles an origin story but should not be forced into Genesis-derived categories.

**[CONTESTED CHRISTIAN INTERTEXT]** George Sword was a baptized Christian as well as a defender and interpreter of Oglala religion. One scholarly reading treats the Hanwi story’s succession of world orders and anticipated future transformation as evidence of creative Lakota-Christian dialogue. That is an interpretation of Sword’s authored text, not proof that the narrative is simply Christian fabrication. Conversely, claims that the corpus is an untouched pre-contact “Lakota Bible” disregard its authorial and colonial setting.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT]** The Cheyenne Creek version makes Gnaskinyan the evil instigator, Woksape the future Iktomi, and Ite an aspirant who dresses as Hanwi. Takuskanskan judges them. This is not merely a shortened Walker story; it rearranges agencies and transformations.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No temple cult

**[ABSENCE]** No Hanwi temple, permanent priesthood, cult statue, sanctuary treasury, or exclusive annual “Festival of Hanwi” is securely documented. Lakota religious life was not organized like the civic temple systems of Greece, Rome, or Mesopotamia. Sacred powers were approached through kinship, prayer, vision, pipe ceremony, songs, offerings, vows, fasting, dance, and particular places.

### Moon-related practice

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** The moon was used in reckoning time and naming seasonal periods. Its presence belongs to a lived celestial environment, but not every use of a lunar calendar is “worship of Hanwi.”

**[ABSENCE]** The searched Walker, Densmore, Deloria, Black Elk, Powers, Lame Deer, and related records do not securely establish:

- a sacrifice offered uniquely to Hanwi;
- a specialist “priestess of Hanwi”;
- a prescribed Hanwi altar;
- human sacrifice to Hanwi;
- temple prostitution;
- a lunar oracle institution;
- an ancient secret initiatory order devoted to her.

Lakota ceremonies did include animal offerings in some contexts and bodily sacrifice in the Sun Dance, but these must not be reassigned to Hanwi without evidence. The 1883 federal code’s attack on “medicine men” and the Sun Dance documents colonial suppression of Lakota religion generally, not a targeted ban on a Hanwi cult.

### Sun Dance and the celestial order

The Oglala Sun Dance, *Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi*—often explained as “They Dance Looking at the Sun”—centers on the Sun rather than Hanwi. Walker’s theology nonetheless places Sun and Moon within one ordered sacred totality. Densmore’s 1918 work provides extensive testimony about Sun Dance songs and procedure but no comparable public Moon Dance institution.

### Sacred places

Hanwi has no securely documented exclusive shrine among the major Lakota sacred places. Those places nevertheless sustain the religious world within which celestial persons remain meaningful:

- **Pahá Sápa, the Black Hills:** sacred homeland and landscape of visions, origin traditions, pilgrimage, and ceremony. The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty promised to exclude settlers, but the United States seized the Hills following gold intrusion and war. The Supreme Court later held that the taking required compensation; Lakota nations have refused the monetary settlement as a substitute for land.
- **Matȟó Pahá, Bear Butte:** an important place of fasting, prayer, and vision. Modern acquisition of nearby land by Native organizations seeks to restore practical access.
- **Matȟó Thípila, Bear Lodge/Devils Tower:** sacred to numerous Plains peoples. “Devils Tower” arose from an 1875 mistranslation or renaming; earlier federal maps used Bear Lodge. NPS requests voluntary climbing restraint during June because of ceremonies ([NPS name history](https://www.nps.gov/deto/learn/historyculture/aboutthename.htm)).
- **Wind Cave / Makȟá Oŋíya, “breathing earth”:** associated with Lakota emergence traditions.

No source examined makes these places “temples of Hanwi.” Their relevance is contextual rather than proprietary.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before sustained written documentation

**[ORAL TRADITION / UNDATABLE]** The moon-word, lunar timekeeping, and personification certainly precede the surviving Walker manuscript, but no responsible year can be assigned. Oral antiquity cannot be converted into a fabricated archaeological date.

### 1852–1890: lexical recording and colonial disruption

Missionary lexicography placed Dakota celestial vocabulary in writing. During the same period, military conquest, confinement to reservations, destruction of buffalo herds, missionization, allotment, and boarding-school regimes transformed the conditions under which sacred narratives and language were transmitted.

### 1883: criminalization of ceremony

The federal **Rules for Courts of Indian Offenses**, issued March 30, 1883, declared the Sun Dance, Scalp Dance, War Dance, and similar feasts “Indian offenses.” A first conviction could mean withholding rations; later convictions could bring longer ration deprivation or agency imprisonment. The code also attacked religious specialists. Its wording is preserved in the [1883 transcription](https://commons.und.edu/indigenous-gov-docs/131/) and [full text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Code_of_Indian_Offenses).

This did not name Hanwi, but it altered the entire religious environment in which Moon narratives circulated.

### 1889–1890: Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee

The Northern Paiute prophet Wovoka’s Ghost Dance was adapted among Lakota communities living under hunger, land loss, epidemic disease, and coercive administration. Federal authorities construed it as a military danger.

On December 29, 1890, the Seventh Cavalry killed Lakota men, women, and children at Wounded Knee Creek after detaining Spotted Elk’s Miniconjou band and attempting to disarm it. The massacre is essential to the historical setting of later Oglala religious documentation, but no primary evidence makes Hanwi a presiding deity of the Ghost Dance. The connection is historical context, not cultic identity ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/immigration/native-american/disaster-at-wounded-knee/)).

### 1896–1914: Walker’s collection

At Pine Ridge, Walker developed working relationships with Oglala religious authorities. His informants were not fossil survivals: they were intellectuals responding to reservation rule, Christianity, censorship, and demands to explain Lakota categories to an English-speaking outsider. Sword’s Hanwi story belongs to this creative documentary moment.

### 1917–1932: publication and textual stabilization

Walker’s 1917 publication turned an individually transmitted Lakota text into an apparently anonymous tribal myth. Densmore’s 1918 collection documented a much broader ceremonial world. Deloria’s 1932 *Dakota Texts* demonstrated the sophistication of Dakota-language oral literature without reproducing Walker’s pantheon as a universal catechism.

### 1934 onward: relaxation and revival

John Collier’s Indian New Deal administration ended the Bureau’s formal hostility to many traditional ceremonies. Sun Dances and other practices had never vanished completely; some continued discreetly or in modified forms. Public revivals therefore combined continuity and reconstruction.

### 1978: religious-freedom legislation

The American Indian Religious Freedom Act declared federal policy to protect Native access to sacred places, objects, and ceremonies. It did not itself provide a strong judicial cause of action, and conflicts over sacred land and ceremonial access continued.

### Late twentieth century to present

Lakota ceremonial life, language revitalization, art, scholarship, and seasonal knowledge remain living practices. Hanwi continues foremost as the Moon and as a narrative person. The Walker story circulates through books, classrooms, NPS interpretation, mythology websites, and artistic work. The circulation is continuous at the lexical level but textually mediated at the level of the elaborate feast myth.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and education

Walker’s story has become a standard “Lakota creation myth” in comparative-mythology collections. This gives Hanwi greater visibility but often erases Sword, the Lakota manuscript, Walker’s editorial work, and variant community accounts.

The Wind Cave National Park presentation is more careful: it explicitly calls its account one Cheyenne Creek community version and warns that families and bands transmit differing emergence stories.

### Indigenous visual reception

Marlena Myles’s contemporary Hanwi is an Indigenous artistic interpretation linking moon, motherhood, and feminine power. It materially contributes to Hanwi’s modern image while remaining distinguishable from an excavated or nineteenth-century cult image.

### Neopagan and occult material

The dedicated contested-material search found modern pages calling Hanwi:

- a “moon goddess” governing fertility and renewal;
- a guardian of dreams, intuition, motherhood, or all women;
- a Native equivalent of Selene, Artemis, or Hecate;
- a power to invoke in eclectic lunar ritual.

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** These claims were not substantiated from Walker, Densmore, Deloria, Black Elk, Buechel, or Powers. No evidence was found for Hanwi in the Golden Dawn, Theosophy’s foundational canon, Renaissance Hermetism, classical grimoires, or a historically continuous Euro-American occult order.

Because many Lakota ceremonies are community-governed and some knowledge is not public, modern outsiders’ ritual “work with Hanwi” should not be represented as reconstruction merely because it uses her name.

### Popular art and commerce

Prints and online merchandise depict Hanwi as a generic Art Nouveau or fantasy “Native American moon goddess,” commonly with a feathered headdress, wolves, dreamcatchers, turquoise ornaments, or a crescent crown. Those objects combine pan-Indigenous and European lunar-goddess motifs. They are commercial iconography, not documentary reproductions.

### Literature, film, comics, and games

**[ABSENCE]** No single major film, novel, comic, or game adaptation was found that transformed Hanwi’s public image on the scale achieved for better-known Greco-Roman or Norse beings. She appears mainly in mythology lists, educational retellings, spiritual websites, art, and commercial prints.

### Nationalist or extremist appropriation

**[ABSENCE]** No well-documented nationalist or extremist movement centered specifically on Hanwi was found. Broader appropriations of “Native spirituality,” racialized claims of invented Indigenous ancestry, and anti-institutional occult syncretism exist, but assigning all such activity to a Hanwi movement would exaggerate the evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Walker’s pantheon authentic?

The question is badly framed if “authentic” means either pure timeless tradition or complete invention.

Evidence supports the following:

1. Walker worked with named Oglala authorities.
2. George Sword and others produced substantial Lakota-language texts.
3. Hanwi is independently a genuine Lakota/Dakota name for the Moon.
4. The feast story existed in a Lakota manuscript before 1910.
5. Walker edited translations, added connecting material, and attempted a systematic synthesis.
6. His sixteen-being hierarchy is unusually symmetrical and may reflect the explanatory interests of Walker and particular collaborators rather than universal Oglala consensus.
7. Ella Deloria questioned portions of Walker’s mythology.
8. Christian and comparative-classical concepts influenced the setting in which Walker, Sword, and later readers interpreted the texts.

The strongest method is therefore source stratification, not wholesale acceptance or dismissal.

### Sword’s Christianity

Sword’s Christian identity has produced two opposite distortions:

- that anything written by a Christian convert is no longer Lakota;
- that any Biblical resemblance proves missionary invention.

Both exceed the evidence. Sword could participate in Christianity while deliberately writing Lakota sacred history and interpreting each through the other. His authorship makes the text historically situated, not invalid.

### Walker’s “Lakota Genesis”

Walker compared Lakota sacred beings with Greek, Roman, and Egyptian personifications and sought a coherent system. His later epic joined texts that had separate manuscript histories. Jahner’s editorial work shows that “When the People Laughed at the Moon” should be read first as its own authored narrative and only secondarily as a chapter in Walker’s constructed cycle.

### Black Elk and Neihardt

*Black Elk Speaks* is not a stenographic transcript. Neihardt selected, arranged, and literary-shaped the account. *The Sixth Grandfather* supplies the interview records and shows both the extent of Neihardt’s mediation and the genuine Lakota material underlying the book. Neither text independently verifies every feature of Walker’s Hanwi system.

### “Sioux pantheon”

“Sioux” is an imposed umbrella term covering related but distinct Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota peoples. The phrase “Sioux pantheon” encourages false uniformity. The fullest Hanwi theology is specifically Oglala and specifically tied to the Pine Ridge Walker corpus; ordinary moon terminology and celestial knowledge have wider distribution.

### Archaeology and images

No excavated object can presently be securely labeled “Hanwi” solely because it bears a crescent, circle, or female figure. Plains pictorial signs may denote the moon, night, a personal name, a calendrical event, or decoration. Identification requires provenance and accompanying testimony.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Hanwi was worshipped in stone temples — **unsupported**.
- She had a fixed ancient idol — **unsupported**.
- She was universally chief goddess of all “Sioux” peoples — **unsupported**.
- Her phases express guilt for her own adultery — **contradicted by Walker’s narrative**.
- She is identical with White Buffalo Calf Woman — **not established**.
- She rules wolves, silver, psychic magic, and the zodiac — **modern accretion unless separately sourced**.
- Human beings were sacrificed to her — **no evidence found**.
- Her story is either verbatim pre-contact scripture or entirely Walker’s fiction — **both positions ignore the layered manuscript record**.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| **Pre-contact, undatable** | Lakota/Dakota use of lunar time and oral personification necessarily predates surviving documentation, but no precise date is recoverable. |
| **1852** | Riggs’s Dakota grammar and dictionary places Dakota celestial vocabulary in print; later expanded editions preserve the lexicographic record. |
| **1883** | Federal Rules for Courts of Indian Offenses criminalize the Sun Dance and attack Native religious specialists, transforming the context of Lakota ceremonial transmission. |
| **1889–90** | Ghost Dance spreads among Lakota communities. No special Hanwi cult is documented within it. |
| **29 December 1890** | U.S. troops massacre Lakota people at Wounded Knee. |
| **1896–1914** | Walker collects Oglala religious, linguistic, and ceremonial materials at Pine Ridge. |
| **Before 1910** | George Sword writes the Lakota text conventionally identified as “When the People Laughed at the Moon.” |
| **1910** | Death of George Sword, supplying the latest possible date for his manuscript. |
| **After 1914** | Walker translates or gains full access to the Sword Hanwi manuscript; the precise earlier extent of his knowledge remains unclear. |
| **1917** | Walker publishes “When the People Laughed at Hanwi” and the four-by-four divine classification in *The Sun Dance and Other Ceremonies of the Oglala Division of the Teton Dakota*. |
| **1918** | Densmore publishes *Teton Sioux Music*, documenting ceremonial life but no independent equivalent Hanwi cult. |
| **1920s** | Walker develops a continuous literary “Oglala mythology,” joining previously separate materials and adding connective passages. |
| **1932** | Ella Deloria publishes *Dakota Texts*; Neihardt publishes *Black Elk Speaks*. |
| **1934** | Federal administrative policy becomes more tolerant of Native ceremony; public ceremonial revival expands. |
| **1937–39** | Deloria evaluates Walker’s Lakota texts in correspondence and commentary for Franz Boas, questioning elements of the corpus. |
| **1953** | *The Sacred Pipe* publishes Black Elk’s account of seven rites; important to modern Lakota religious reception but not an independent Hanwi narrative. |
| **1970** | Buechel’s Lakota dictionary is published posthumously. |
| **1972** | *Lame Deer, Seeker of Visions* contributes to the international reception of Lakota religion. |
| **1975** | Powers’s *Oglala Religion* analyzes Walker’s system while questioning its universal status. |
| **1978** | American Indian Religious Freedom Act declares protection of Native religious practice and sacred-site access as federal policy. |
| **1980** | DeMallie and Jahner publish *Lakota Belief and Ritual*. |
| **1983** | Jahner publishes *Lakota Myth*, restoring manuscript relationships and a closer Sword Hanwi text. |
| **1984** | DeMallie publishes *The Sixth Grandfather*; Dooling publishes *The Sons of the Wind*. |
| **1987** | *Sioux Indian Religion* publishes major scholarly and Indigenous discussions, including Jahner’s analysis of Lakota genesis traditions. |
| **1997** | IAU names Hanghepiwi Chasma on Venus. |
| **2002** | Revised Buechel–Manhart *Lakota Dictionary* appears. |
| **2006** | IAU names Hanwi Chasma on Venus. |
| **2010s–present** | Lakota-language revitalization, Indigenous star-knowledge projects, contemporary art, and community interpretation keep *Haŋwí* current; mythology and commercial websites simultaneously create unsupported “moon goddess” attributes. |
| **2024** | A Native-led organization purchases land near sacred Bear Butte, illustrating continuing efforts to restore access to Lakota sacred landscapes. |
| **Present** | Hanwi remains the living Lakota name/person of the Moon. The elaborate feast-and-shame narrative remains documented chiefly through the layered Sword–Walker textual tradition and its later retellings. |
