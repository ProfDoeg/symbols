# Mummu: Research Dossier

**Class:** A male Mesopotamian deity in the securely contextualized Akkadian evidence, usually a primordial god and *sukkallu* (“minister, vizier”) of Apsû. The word *mummu* also functioned independently as a theological epithet or abstract noun connected with knowledge, craft, form, and creative power. Consequently, not every written *mummu* denotes the individual god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest possible evidence consists of Old Akkadian and Ur III personal names—*Mu-mu-sa-tu* and *Mu-mu-ilum*—and a Mari mace-head dedication to divine *Mu-mu*. Whether these name the same Mummu later known as Apsû’s minister is disputed. The earliest unambiguous contextual identification is Kassite: an inscription on a *kudurru* of Nazi-Maruttaš, conventionally dated c. 1307–1282 BCE, names Mummu beside the *suḫurmašû* (“goat-fish”) as symbols of Ea. The best-preserved narrative evidence is the later *Enūma eliš*, probably composed in the late second millennium BCE and surviving mainly in first-millennium tablets.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Mummu is not securely reconstructable as a major city god with a stable temple establishment, family, and anthropomorphic iconography. He emerges instead at the intersection of three bodies of evidence:

1. a sparsely worshipped divine Mummu known from names, a dedication, a Kassite monument, rituals, and learned lists;
2. Apsû’s counselor Mummu in *Enūma eliš*, who urges the destruction of the younger gods and is captured by Ea;
3. the common or technical noun *mummu*, applied to Tiamat, Marduk, Ea, Ištar, Nabû, and possibly buildings or workshops as a term relating to knowledge and creative craftsmanship.

These uses overlap deliberately in Babylonian learned theology, but they should not be collapsed into one personified “spirit of creation.”

### Names and writings

Attested spellings include:

- **Mummu**, normalized Akkadian *Mummu*.
- Syllabic **mu-mu**, **mu-um**, **mu-um-mu**, and inflected **mu-um-ma**.
- Divine forms with the determinative: **𒀭𒈬𒌝𒈬**, conventionally transcribed *d*mu-um-mu. The divine determinative is not used consistently.
- The logogram **dDÉ** is reported in learned material.
- Greek **Μωϋμίς / Mōymis** or **Moymis**, in the Babylonian cosmogony attributed to Eudemus of Rhodes and preserved by Damascius.
- “Mummu-Tiamat” represents Akkadian *mummu Tiʾāmat*, not necessarily a compound personal name.

ORACC’s proper-name glossary records eleven occurrences under Mummu, including *d*mu-um-mu, *d*mu-um-ma, and the nondeterminative expression *mummišu*, “his mummu.” Its lexical corpus cautiously glosses common *mummu* as “life-giving force?”—the question mark is significant. [ORACC proper-name evidence](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/armep/cbd/qpn/M.html); [ORACC lexical entry](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/cams/akno/cbd/akk/x000022000.html)

### Etymology and semantic dispute

No single derivation commands universal acceptance.

- **Knowledge, skill, or workshop:** The strongest lexical connection is with Sumerian *umun₂*, “knowledge; workshop,” which the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary equates with Akkadian *mummu*, *ummatu*, and *ummuqu*. This supports “knowledge,” “skill,” or the place where skilled creation occurs. [ePSD2, *umun₂*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0041537.html)

- **Craftsman or expert:** Akkadian *ummânu/ummiānu*, “expert, scholar, craftsman,” is related in sense, though it should not simply be treated as the same word or as a literal translation of the god’s name. The Sumerian loanword *ummia*, “expert, master craftsman,” is abundantly attested from the Early Dynastic through Hellenistic periods. [ePSD2, *ummia*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0041517.html)

- **Form, model, or creative idea:** Older translations include “form,” “mould,” “pattern,” “creative spirit,” and “creative power.” Thorkild Jacobsen derived it from Sumerian *umun*, understood as “mould/form”; Lambert emphasized a development from knowledge or skill toward creative power. These are scholarly interpretations, not glosses supplied by *Enūma eliš* itself.

- **Mist:** The widespread description “mist rising where fresh Apsû and salt Tiamat mingle” is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not an ancient gloss. It attempts to give the third member of the primordial scene a physical cosmological function. It has no explicit lexical or iconographic confirmation.

- **Logos or speech:** Frans de Liagre Böhl connected Mummu with a participial interpretation of *amû*, “to speak,” and with the Greek/Hellenistic *logos*. Alexander Heidel rejected the proposed etymology. Damascius’s “intelligible world” is his Neoplatonic interpretation, not proof that Babylonian Mummu meant Logos.

- **Multiple scribal wordplays:** Yoon Jong Yoo argues that *Enūma eliš* exploits several possible learned associations rather than preserving one recoverable etymology: *MU* with “speak,” “son,” “create,” “noise,” Akkadian *ummu* “mother,” and *mû* “water.” This is best classified as **modern reconstruction of ancient scribal etymologizing**. [Yoo’s study and abstract](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/landing/article.kci?arti_id=ART002035148)

Two homonyms—a word for noise and a wooden object—should not automatically be joined to the divine name.

### Epithets and title-like uses

- **Sukkal Apsî**, “minister/vizier of Apsû”: **later canonical elaboration**, *Enūma eliš* I.
- **Mummu Tiamat, “she who bore them all”**: **later canonical wording**, *Enūma eliš* I.4. Whether *mummu* means “creative power,” “matrix,” or something else remains disputed.
- **Mummu as a name of Marduk:** **learned systematization**, among Marduk’s concluding names in *Enūma eliš* VII. Here the name is transferred to the victorious creator.
- **Mummu as an epithet of Ea, Ištar, or Nabû:** **learned theological usage**, expressing supreme wisdom or creative expertise; it does not make these gods identical with Apsû’s vizier.

The evidence therefore permits both a distinct deity and a transferable learned term. Treating every occurrence as the same god produces false genealogies.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Early possible attestations

#### Old Akkadian personal name

Lambert cited *Mu-mu-sa-tu*, conventionally interpreted “Mummu is a mountain.” A divine name in a personal name normally indicates that its bearer or family recognized a deity so named. Krebernik nevertheless cautioned that the identity of this early *Mu-mu* with the later primordial Mummu cannot be demonstrated. **Tier: securely attested name; identification with the later god uncertain.**

#### Ur III personal name

*Mu-mu-ì-lum*, “Mummu is a god,” occurs on a document dated to year 4 of Šu-Sîn, approximately 2034 BCE under the middle chronology. It is strong evidence that *Mumu/Mummu* could be understood as a god by the Ur III period. It does not establish the Apsû narrative. **Tier: securely attested divine name; later identity probable but unproved.**

#### Mari mace head

A mace head from the *šakkanakku* period at Mari bears a dedication to *d*Mu-mu. Its divine determinative makes the status as a deity explicit. The object supplies cultic evidence, but no genealogy, mythology, sex, or iconography beyond the dedication. **Tier: securely attested deity; equation with the later Mummu debated.**

### Kassite evidence

A *kudurru* from the reign of Nazi-Maruttaš identifies Mummu and the *suḫurmašû*, the goat-fish, as two symbols associated with Ea. This gives Mummu a place in Ea’s symbolic and theological sphere by the thirteenth century BCE. It is the earliest unambiguous evidence connecting Mummu to the Ea/Apsû complex. It does not by itself narrate Mummu’s defeat.

### *Enūma eliš*

The Babylonian creation epic is the chief narrative source. Its date remains debated. Lambert argued for composition under Nebuchadnezzar I, c. 1125–1104 BCE; other scholars allow earlier Kassite development or substantial later shaping. The surviving exemplars are predominantly Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian tablets from sites including Nineveh, Aššur, Babylon, Kish, and Sultantepe.

Relevant passages include:

- **I.3–5:** Apsû is “their begetter,” while *mummu Tiʾāmat* is “she who bore them all”; their waters mingle.
- **I.30s–50s:** Apsû summons Mummu, his minister, and consults him about the younger gods’ noise.
- **I.47–54:** Mummu advocates destroying the younger generation and embraces Apsû in approval.
- **I.60s–70s:** Ea learns the plan, enchants Apsû into sleep, kills or defeats him, and confines Mummu. The text portrays captivity rather than an explicit killing of Mummu.
- **I.73 onward:** Ea establishes his dwelling on Apsû; Marduk is conceived or born in the transformed Apsû.
- **Later recollection:** Tiamat is reminded of Apsû and the defeated Mummu.
- **VII:** Mummu is bestowed or interpreted as one of Marduk’s exalted names, “creator of heaven and underworld” in common translations.

An extant Sultantepe witness preserves “Mummu answered, advising Apsû” and later “he confined Mummu.” [ORACC edition of STT 1, 001](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/cams/gkab/P338317/)

### Ritual lists of defeated beings

First-millennium ritual and explanatory texts group Mummu with vanquished or primordial figures such as Qingu, Tiamat, Enmešarra, or other beings thought to be confined below. Lambert inferred that a lost tradition may have described Mummu and Qingu being defeated together. That is an inference from list placement, not a surviving myth. **Tier: securely attested ritual association; joint narrative modern reconstruction.**

### River-opening ritual

A fragmentary first-millennium tablet from Nineveh prescribes silver discs for Mummu, Qingu, and Ešret-nabnīssu and offerings including flour, dates, *mirsu* preparations, a libation vessel, juniper incense, and a sheep. This is direct ritual evidence that Mummu could receive offerings. Because the rite handles primordial or subdued powers, it need not imply a free-standing popular cult.

### Kettledrum ritual and commentary

In the learned exegesis surrounding the manufacture or consecration of the divine *lilissu* kettledrum, Mummu appears among defeated primordial powers. The ritual universe connects the underworld, craft production, Ea’s Apsû, and the *bīt mummi*, the workshop or “house of craft/creation.” Modern scholarship emphasizes that the workshop’s artisans and scribal experts made “knowledge,” “craft,” and “creation” mutually reinforcing senses. [Study of the *bīt mummi* and ritual craft](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/79691de9-e512-4d19-b66c-eda8cb696fd4/683-162-PB.pdf)

### God lists and learned theology

Mummu occurs in learned catalogues of primordial or defeated beings. He is not a major household deity in the surviving portions of *An = Anum*. The great god list was a scholarly taxonomy, not a census of popular worship; absence from a prominent position is nevertheless consistent with Mummu’s marginal cultic profile.

### Texts named in the research brief: negative findings

- **ETCSL Sumerian literary corpus:** No secure appearance of the individual Apsû-vizier Mummu in *Inanna’s Descent* or *Enki and Ninmaḫ*.
- **Atraḫasīs:** The individual Mummu is not a character. Nintu/Mami in some translations must not be confused with Mummu.
- **Epic of Gilgameš:** No narrative appearance of Mummu in Andrew George’s standard edition.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP:** The corpus does not establish a royal cult of Mummu. ORACC does record *Bīt-Mummu*, “temple/house of Mummu,” once as a place or building name, but a single lexical occurrence does not disclose its location, history, or ritual establishment. [ORACC *Bīt-Mummu* entry](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/armep/cbd/qpn-x-temple/B.html)

These absences matter: Mummu belongs principally to Babylonian creation theology and learned ritual, not to the major Sumerian narrative cycles.

### Greek transmission: Mōymis, not securely “Berossus’s Moymis”

The prompt’s “Berossus Moymis” requires correction. The surviving explicit Mōymis passage is not attributed to Berossus. It occurs in Damascius’s sixth-century CE *Problems and Solutions Concerning First Principles*, which attributes a Babylonian theology to **Eudemus**, normally Eudemus of Rhodes, a student of Aristotle in the fourth century BCE.

Damascius reports Tauthē and Apasōn—Tiamat and Apsû—as two principles, with Apasōn as Tauthē’s husband and Tauthē as mother of the gods. From them comes an only-begotten **Mōymis**, whom Damascius interprets as the “intelligible world.” Further generations correspond, imperfectly, to Laḫmu/Laḫamu, Anšar/Kišar, Anu/Enlil/Ea, and finally Bel/Marduk.

This version differs materially from *Enūma eliš*: Mummu is explicitly the couple’s son, not Apsû’s minister. Proposed explanations include:

- Eudemus had access to a Babylonian cosmogony related to but distinct from *Enūma eliš;
- a translator or transmitter turned the epithet *mummu Tiʾāmat* into a separate offspring;
- Damascius imposed Neoplatonic generational structure and the category of “intelligible cosmos” on the Babylonian names.

Berossus, a Babylonian priest writing Greek under Antiochus I in the early third century BCE, transmitted other Babylonian cosmogonic material, but the extant fragments do not securely make him the source of the Mōymis genealogy. Calling Mōymis “Berossus’s” is therefore **unsupported modern conflation**. [Damascius passage in translation](https://ordoabchao.ca/resources/chaldean-magi/damascius); [modern study of Damascius’s interpretation](https://philarchive.org/archive/SONDNI)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

No source assigns Mummu an ordinary portfolio such as storms, agriculture, war, healing, or a city’s protection. His documented spheres are:

- **Counsel and administration:** as Apsû’s *sukkallu* in *Enūma eliš*.
- **Primordial existence:** as a being associated with Apsû, Tiamat, and defeated cosmic powers.
- **Knowledge and craft:** through the semantic field of common *mummu* and Ea’s creative wisdom.
- **Creative power or pattern:** as an epithet and as a name transferred to Marduk.
- **Chthonic confinement:** in rituals treating vanquished primordial beings as resident below the present divine order.

The often-repeated domain “mist” is unattested as an explicit ancient doctrine.

### Form

No surviving text supplies Mummu with a stable bodily description. *Enūma eliš* makes him sufficiently anthropomorphic to speak, advise, embrace, be seized, tied, and imprisoned. These are narrative actions, not a formal iconographic description.

### Symbols

The Nazi-Maruttaš *kudurru* associates Mummu with Ea’s symbols. The wording distinguishes Mummu from the goat-fish, but it is unclear whether “Mummu” there names a depicted emblem, a personified symbol, or the divine force represented by one. No image can presently be identified as Mummu with confidence.

### Sacred animals, colors, numbers, days, and celestial bodies

None is securely attested for the individual god. Assignments circulating in modern esoteric tables are **modern inventions or transfers from Ea/Marduk**, unless an independent ancient source is supplied.

### Powers and limitations

- He gives counsel and can influence Apsû.
- He does not independently execute the plan against the younger gods.
- Ea’s incantation and magical strategy overcome him.
- He is confined rather than clearly annihilated.
- As a transferred divine name, *Mummu* expresses creative or cosmic ordering power in Marduk; that should not be retrojected as an action performed by captive Mummu.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

*Enūma eliš* gives Mummu no explicit parents, spouse, siblings, or offspring. Apsû addresses him affectionately and Mummu calls Apsû “my father” in some translations, but courtly kinship language need not establish biological descent. Vitali Bartash has argued for implied sonship; Krebernik and others remain more cautious.

Damascius/Eudemus explicitly makes Mōymis the only-begotten son of Apasōn and Tauthē. That is a **Hellenistic or late-antique transmitted variant**, not the genealogy stated by the cuneiform epic.

No consort or child of Mummu is documented.

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Apsû — counselor and subordinate:** Mummu serves as Apsû’s *sukkallu*, supports his proposal to destroy the younger gods, urges him to act, and embraces him. *Enūma eliš* I, probably late second millennium BCE, surviving first-millennium copies. **Tier: later canonical Babylonian narrative.**

- **Ea/Enki — capture and subordination:** Ea discovers the plot, enchants Apsû, defeats him, seizes Mummu, and confines him; Ea then occupies Apsû. *Enūma eliš* I. **Tier: later canonical Babylonian narrative.**

- **Tiamat — joint primordial context but no direct dialogue:** *mummu Tiʾāmat* appears in I.4, and Tiamat is later reminded that Mummu has been defeated. No direct conversation or action between distinct Mummu and Tiamat survives. *Enūma eliš* I. **Tier: canonical association; direct interaction absent.**

- **Marduk — name appropriated by the victor:** Mummu becomes one of Marduk’s learned names in *Enūma eliš* VII. Marduk does not meet the captive Mummu in a surviving episode. **Tier: learned canonical systematization.**

- **Qingu — ritual co-listing:** Mummu and Qingu receive parallel ritual treatment and occur together among defeated beings. A shared defeat story is inferred but lost. First-millennium ritual tablets. **Tier: securely attested ritual association; interaction unestablished.**

- **Ešret-nabnīssu — joint recipient:** Silver discs and offerings are prescribed for Mummu, Qingu, and Ešret-nabnīssu in the Nineveh river-opening ritual. No narrative interaction is recorded. **Tier: securely attested ritual co-appearance.**

- **Ištar — no meeting:** Ištar can receive *mummu* as an epithet in a *šuila* prayer comparing her with Ea. This is an epithet, not an encounter with the god. **Tier: learned theological comparison.**

- **Nabû — no meeting:** A text may describe Nabû with *mummu* in the sense of creative wisdom or expertise. This is not a narrative interaction. **Tier: learned epithet.**

- **Laḫmu, Laḫamu, Anšar, Kišar, Anu, Enlil, and other younger gods — intended victims collectively:** Mummu recommends suppressing or destroying the noisy younger generation, but the epic does not give him a discrete encounter with each named member. **Tier: canonical collective hostility; individual interaction absent.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The conspiracy of Apsû and Mummu

**Later canonical Babylonian narrative: *Enūma eliš* I.**

Before heaven and earth possess names, Apsû and Tiamat mingle their waters and generate successive divine generations. The activity and noise of the younger gods disturb Apsû. He consults Mummu, his minister. Tiamat opposes destroying their own offspring, but Mummu encourages Apsû’s plan and praises it as beneficial. Apsû rejoices and embraces him.

The younger gods learn of the threat. Ea, master of incantation and wisdom, devises a spell, pours sleep over Apsû, and disables him. Ea removes Apsû’s crown or radiance, kills or establishes victory over him, and binds Mummu. The wording describing Mummu’s treatment varies with tablet preservation and translation: he is seized, tethered, confined, or locked away. Popular retellings that say Ea kills Mummu go beyond the clearest surviving wording.

Ea makes his dwelling in or upon the defeated Apsû. There Marduk is conceived and born. Mummu therefore occupies a structural position between the old primordial regime and Ea’s appropriation of its waters, space, and creative authority.

The myth’s political-theological function is to subordinate older Eridu theology to Marduk’s Babylonian supremacy. Ea first defeats Apsû and appropriates his realm; Ea’s son Marduk then defeats Tiamat and appropriates universal kingship.

### Mummu-Tiamat

**Canonical ambiguous phrase: *Enūma eliš* I.4.**

The line *mummu Tiʾāmat muallidat gimrišun* is often rendered “Mummu-Tiamat, she who bore them all,” “creative Tiamat,” or with *mummu* left untranslated. Three main interpretations exist:

1. *mummu* is an epithet meaning creative power, matrix, form, or generative principle;
2. Mummu and Tiamat are both named, with damaged or compressed syntax;
3. scribes deliberately exploited ambiguity between the common noun and personal deity.

Because the following narrative clearly distinguishes Mummu as Apsû’s counselor, the opening need not mean that counselor Mummu and Tiamat are identical. Damascius/Eudemus may preserve or create a resolution in which Mummu becomes the couple’s son.

### Mōymis in the Greek cosmogony

**Hellenistic tradition preserved through late-antique Neoplatonism.**

Mōymis proceeds from Apasōn and Tauthē and is interpreted as the intelligible cosmos. The genealogy then unfolds through paired generations to Bel, the demiurge. The source is valuable evidence for Greek reception of Babylonian learned cosmology, but its philosophical gloss cannot be read straight back into second-millennium Babylonian belief.

### Defeat beside Qingu

**Ritual association; lost myth hypothesized.**

Mummu and Qingu repeatedly belong to groups of subdued cosmic opponents. Lambert proposed a missing tradition in which the two were defeated together. No surviving narrative permits its reconstruction. It should therefore be catalogued as a **modern scholarly inference from ancient list structure**, not told as a recovered myth.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers and buildings

No city can securely be called Mummu’s principal cult center. Mari supplies the early dedication; Babylonia supplies the Kassite monument and learned theology; Nineveh preserves ritual and literary copies.

The single *Bīt-Mummu* entry demonstrates a “House/Temple of Mummu” name but does not by itself establish a durable, independently administered temple cult. The term also risks confusion with *bīt mummi*, a workshop associated with divine manufacture and ritual craft.

### Priesthood

No priestly office uniquely serving Mummu is securely documented. No evidence establishes hereditary clergy, temple estates, or a Mummu-specific prebend.

### Offerings

The Nineveh river-opening ritual is the clearest evidence:

- silver discs for Mummu and associated beings;
- flour and dates;
- *mirsu* food preparations;
- a libation vessel;
- juniper fumigation;
- a sheep.

This is ritual propitiation or management of a primordial power, not proof of a public festival.

### Festivals

No annual festival dedicated specifically to Mummu is securely attested. *Enūma eliš* became associated with Babylon’s Akītu festival, but that made Mummu a character in a recited or ritually meaningful cosmic narrative, not necessarily the recipient of an independent festival.

### Votive evidence and popularity

The early personal names and Mari mace head show devotion or acknowledgment. Their extreme rarity indicates that Mummu was never among the best-attested household or civic gods. No substantial corpus of votive statues, seals, plaques, or dedicatory inscriptions survives.

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

No evidence connects Mummu’s cult with human sacrifice or so-called “temple prostitution.” The sheep in the river ritual is the only explicit sacrificial victim presently associated with him. His advocacy of divine destruction in *Enūma eliš* is mythic violence, not evidence for human ritual violence.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Third millennium BCE: an obscure divine name

Old Akkadian, Ur III, and Mari evidence indicates a deity *Mumu/Mummu*, but provides almost no mythology. The Mari dedication proves a ritual recipient; personal names suggest limited devotional use. Continuity with the later figure remains probable rather than demonstrable.

### Kassite period: incorporation into Ea theology

The Nazi-Maruttaš *kudurru* places Mummu among Ea’s symbols. This is the first firm contextual bridge to the Apsû-Ea complex. Mummu may already have represented divine skill, a primordial force, or a subordinated power.

### Late second millennium BCE: literary crystallization

In *Enūma eliš*, Mummu becomes Apsû’s counselor and a defeated representative of the old cosmic order. The poem simultaneously uses *mummu* of Tiamat and later grants the name to Marduk. This suggests deliberate theological capture: the old power of counsel or creativity is removed from Apsû’s regime and incorporated into Marduk.

### First millennium BCE: ritual and scholarly afterlife

Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian scholars copied *Enūma eliš*, placed Mummu in lists of defeated beings, and used the word in learned epithets. The river-opening and kettledrum materials show that he remained ritually usable as a primordial, chthonic power. His profile is more scholarly than popular.

RINAP’s lack of a developed Mummu cult in royal inscriptions is meaningful. Assyrian kings invoked major gods extensively; Mummu does not emerge as a royal patron.

### Hellenistic period and late antiquity

Eudemus’s Babylonian theology, preserved by Damascius, turns Mōymis into an offspring of the first cosmic pair and an intelligible principle. Whether this transformation occurred in a Babylonian Greek-speaking milieu, in Peripatetic interpretation, or during late-antique transmission cannot be decided.

Berossus’s lost *Babyloniaca*, composed c. 290–278 BCE, is relevant to the broader Greek transmission of Babylonian cosmology, but no preserved fragment securely attributes Mōymis to him. [Oxford overview of Berossus](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548510466)

### Medieval and early-modern periods

No continuous medieval cult or folklore of Mummu is documented. Damascius’s philosophical text survived in manuscript transmission, but this is learned preservation rather than worship. Renaissance and early-modern occultists did not develop a demonstrable Mummu tradition comparable with their reception of planetary gods, angels, or demons.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries: rediscovery

The decipherment and publication of cuneiform creation tablets allowed scholars to identify Damascius’s Apasōn, Tauthē, and Mōymis with Apsû, Tiamat, and Mummu. Early translators variously rendered Mummu “chaos,” “son,” “mist,” “form,” or “Logos.” Later lexical work made “knowledge/creative skill” more plausible while exposing the uncertainty.

### Present

Mummu appears in Mesopotamian reconstructionist religion and general neopagan reference material, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes a large, continuous, or separately organized cult. Modern invocations are revivals based on published scholarship and translated texts, not survival from antiquity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship-derived popular image

Modern encyclopedias commonly call Mummu “god of mist,” “personification of the fog over primordial waters,” or a third primordial element. This is a visually effective but weakly attested reconstruction. Ancient texts never plainly say “Mummu is mist.”

Another common scheme makes Apsû fresh water, Tiamat salt water, and Mummu the vapor between them. The first two identifications are textually grounded; the third is not.

### The Simon *Necronomicon*

The 1977 *Simon Necronomicon* combined names and translated material from Mesopotamian scholarship with H. P. Lovecraft’s fictional mythology and modern ceremonial magic. It helped circulate “MUMMU TIAMAT” as an occult entity or formula.

This is a **documented modern invention**, not an ancient grimoire or direct survival of Sumerian ritual. Its Mummu material belongs to twentieth-century Lovecraftian occultism. The book’s alleged ancient manuscript pedigree is unsupported; analyses have identified close dependence on modern published translations. [History and textual-authenticity summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Necronomicon)

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Modern Mesopotamian polytheists can acknowledge or invoke Mummu, often using *Enūma eliš* and ritual scholarship. Such practice is a legitimate modern religious development but cannot be documented as uninterrupted transmission. Online claims that reconstruct elaborate correspondences, sigils, planets, colors, or feast days for Mummu lack ancient support unless separately sourced.

### Literature, games, and entertainment

The name has entered fantasy and game catalogues, usually as a chaos monster, water demon, or primordial evil. These portrayals derive from *Enūma eliš* filtered through modern “chaoskampf,” occult, and role-playing conventions. Ancient Mummu is a counselor and captive god, not a tentacled monster.

The Finnish Moomins’ name is unrelated despite superficial spelling in some languages.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No well-documented nationalist or extremist movement centered on Mummu was found. General appropriations of “Sumerian” or “Aryan” antiquity should not be attributed to this deity without specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal debates

#### Is the third-millennium Mumu the same deity?

Lambert treated the personal names and Mari dedication as early Mummu evidence. Krebernik cautioned that phonetic identity does not prove continuity of character. The prudent conclusion is: the deity-name is early; the Apsû-vizier profile is not.

#### What does *mummu* mean?

Proposals have included:

- chaos;
- mist or vapor;
- mother;
- word or Logos;
- form, mould, or model;
- knowledge or skill;
- craftsman;
- creative spirit or life-giving force.

Current lexical evidence most strongly supports a semantic cluster around knowledge, skilled design, workshop practice, and creative power. It does not establish a single English equivalent in every context.

#### Are the god and the noun one entity?

The divine determinative’s inconsistent use and *Enūma eliš*’s multiple applications create purposeful ambiguity. A named counselor, Tiamat’s epithet, and Marduk’s title coexist in the same work. Modern translations that capitalize every instance or none conceal this complexity.

#### Was Mummu Apsû’s son?

Damascius/Eudemus explicitly says so; *Enūma eliš* does not. “My father” may indicate kinship, hierarchy, or respectful address. The evidence supports a competing Greek-transmitted genealogy, not a single canonical answer.

#### Is Mōymis from Berossus?

No surviving attribution demonstrates this. The explicit chain is Damascius reporting Eudemus. Associating it with Berossus is possible only as speculation about lost channels of transmission.

#### Was Mummu a Logos?

Damascius calls Mōymis the intelligible world; Böhl connected this with speech and Logos. Heidel rejected the Akkadian etymology. The “Babylonian Logos” remains a history-of-scholarship hypothesis, not a secure ancient Babylonian doctrine.

#### Was Mummu mist?

This remains an attractive naturalistic interpretation without direct textual confirmation.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is fragmented among:

- cuneiform literary tablets, especially first-millennium copies of *Enūma eliš*;
- early administrative tablets preserving personal names;
- the Mari mace head;
- the Nazi-Maruttaš *kudurru*;
- ritual tablets from Nineveh;
- lexical and god lists;
- a Greek philosophical text preserved through manuscript transmission.

No securely identified cult statue, temple archive, priestly dossier, or canonical image survives.

Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths* remains the essential synthesis for the literary and ritual material. Earlier works by Heidel and Jacobsen remain influential but must be read against newer lexical and textual editions. Black and Green offer a compact iconographic orientation, though Mummu’s own image is uncertain. ORACC and CDLI now make individual witnesses and lexical distributions more checkable than older composite translations allowed.
