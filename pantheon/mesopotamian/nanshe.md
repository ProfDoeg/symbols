# Nanshe: Research Dossier

**Class:** goddess (*diĝir*, Akkadian *iltu*) in Sumerian and later Babylonian learned and cultic traditions.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Early Dynastic IIIa, approximately 2600–2500 BCE. The ePSD2 corpus records Nanše from ED IIIa onward; the oldest evidence consists of god-list/administrative and onomastic attestations rather than a surviving narrative biography. By ED IIIb, approximately 2500–2340 BCE, royal inscriptions from the state of Lagash securely document her as a major territorial goddess. A clay inscription of Enannatum I from Lagash, now Baghdad IM 76644, names her among the gods and dates archaeologically to ED IIIb.

**Evidentiary warning:** Most substantial literary descriptions survive on Old Babylonian school tablets, roughly 1900–1600 BCE, even where the compositions preserve earlier Lagashite traditions. “Nanshe the goddess of social justice” is a defensible modern summary of *Nanshe A* and the Gudea corpus, but it is not a literal ancient job title. The often-repeated identifications of her bird as a pelican and of particular uninscribed female figures as Nanshe remain disputed.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Nanshe was above all the city-goddess of Niĝin—also conventionally read Nina—within the Lagash polity of southern Mesopotamia. Her sanctuary was É-Sirara. Her oldest recoverable identity joins the ecology and economy of the southern marshes and Gulf—water, fish, waterfowl, boats, fisheries and abundance—with institutional management: offerings, storerooms, personnel, measures, boundaries and judgment. Literary theology developed these functions into protection of orphans, widows, debt-slaves, refugees and the otherwise weak. She was also a divine interpreter of dreams and oracles, most famously for Gudea.

This is not a miscellaneous collection of unrelated “domains.” Fish, reed beds, water transport, institutional redistribution, weights, boundary control and vulnerable dependants all belonged to the economic and juridical world of a southern Mesopotamian temple household. *Nanshe A* expressly links them.

### Name and writings

**[Securely attested]** The standard divine name is conventionally normalized **Nanše**, commonly Anglicized **Nanshe** or formerly **Nanse**. Its usual logographic writing is:

- **𒀭𒀏**, transliterated **^dNANŠE** or **^dAB×ḪA**.
- The prefixed **^d** is the divine determinative.
- The compound sign contains ḪA, “fish,” within AB, but this visual composition does **not** establish that the spoken name meant “lady of fish” or anything comparable.

The name’s etymology is unknown. Modern scholarship generally finds no convincing Sumerian derivation. Treating the sign’s graphic form as a transparent etymology is therefore a modern folk explanation, not an established ancient one.

**[Regional/orthographic variants]**

- **^dna-zi / Nazi**: syllabic form attested in third-millennium Ebla material, Old Babylonian lexical traditions, *Enki and Ninhursag*, *An = Anum*, and especially First Sealand texts.
- **^dna-áš**, **na-an-še**, **na-aš-še**: less common syllabic spellings.
- A proposed underlying form *Nassi* and the suggestion that *Nazi* might be an Emesal form are scholarly hypotheses, not demonstrated etymologies.
- **Šarrat-Nina**, “Queen of Nina,” in a Sealand deity list may designate Nanshe, but an identification with Ištar of Nineveh has also been proposed. The text does not settle the matter.
- In the Canonical Temple List, **Sirara** may sometimes have been reanalyzed as a name of the goddess. Ordinarily it designates her sanctuary or sacred locality.

The ePSD2 search records 1,166 indexed occurrences under Nanše across ED IIIa, ED IIIb, Old Akkadian, Lagash II, Ur III, Old Babylonian, Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, Persian and Hellenistic text classifications. Individual late occurrences must nevertheless be checked: lexical survival is not automatically evidence of an active local cult.

### Attested titles and descriptions

**[Early/Lagashite and Old Babylonian literary witnesses]**

- **Lady/mistress of Niĝin and Sirara**: local tutelary identity.
- **“Child born in Eridug” / “daughter of Eridug”**: *Nanshe A* and the Gudea Cylinders; identifies her with Enki’s city and theological family.
- **“Child of Enki”**: *Nanshe A* 59–64.
- **“Interpreter of dreams among the gods”**: Gudea Cylinder A.
- **“Lady of all the lands”**: Cylinder A’s prayer to her; hymnic universalization, not evidence that her cult displaced the great imperial gods.
- **“Lady of abundance”**: *Nanshe A*; connected to full storehouses, offerings and water.
- **“Lady of the storerooms”**: functionally supported by the hymn’s closing section and later scholarship.
- **One who “like Enlil determines fates”**: *Nanshe A* and Cylinder A. This is exalted hymnic language, not proof that she occupied Enlil’s supreme rank.
- **“Guarantor of boundaries”**: *Nanshe A* 223–31.
- **Bēlet kudurri**, “Lady of the boundary stone”: later Akkadian *Gula Hymn* of Bulluṭsa-rabi, where Nanshe is one manifestation or equation of Gula.
- **Mother Nanshe**: recurring cultic address. It need not signify biological motherhood.
- **Matriarch of Enlil**: an obscure exalted expression in *Nanshe A*; it should not be converted into a literal genealogy.

No secure classical *interpretatio graeca* or *interpretatio romana* of Nanshe is known. Equations with Greek Themis, Dike, Tyche, Aphrodite or Egyptian Isis on modern websites are comparisons or inventions, not ancient identifications.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[Securely attested, ED IIIa, ca. 2600–2500 BCE]** Nanshe appears in the Early Dynastic documentary world, including lexical/god-list and administrative evidence. This establishes the theonym and divine status but does not by itself establish her later full portfolio of social justice and dream interpretation.

**[Securely attested, ED IIIb, ca. 2500–2340 BCE]** Lagash royal inscriptions place Nanshe inside the state pantheon. Enannatum I’s clay inscription from Lagash, IM 76644, names Nanshe alongside Hendursag, Ningirsu, Inanna, Nindara and other local gods. The text is a contemporary monumental source, not a later mythological reconstruction.

Royal onomastics are also significant. **Ur-Nanše**, founder of the first historically documented Lagash dynasty, bore the theophoric name “servant/man of Nanshe.” His building inscriptions credit him with work for her sanctuary. A ruler’s name is strong evidence of the goddess’s prestige but is not itself a myth about her.

### Principal primary corpus

#### Early Dynastic royal and administrative texts

**Language/support:** Sumerian cuneiform on stone, plaques, tablets, cones and other dedicatory objects.  
**Date:** principally twenty-fifth–twenty-fourth centuries BCE.

Nanshe appears as:

- a divine patron who chooses or favors rulers;
- a territorial guarantor whose boundary land must not be violated;
- recipient of buildings, offerings and votives;
- member of the Lagash divine family.

An Enmetena inscription calls him “chosen by Nanshe in the heart” and invokes both Ningirsu’s and Nanshe’s boundary dikes against Umma. A votive inscription records Ninkagina dedicating a human-headed bull to Nanshe for the life of Nam-maḫni. These are direct cultic-political records.

#### Gudea Cylinders A and B

**Language/support:** Sumerian, written on two large terracotta cylinders.  
**Date:** reign of Gudea of Lagash, conventionally about 2144–2124 BCE; the cylinders are contemporary or nearly contemporary monumental literary inscriptions.  
**Modern edition:** Dietz Otto Edzard, *Gudea and His Dynasty*, RIME 3/1 (1997); ETCSL 2.1.7 supplies an accessible composite and translation.

Nanshe is the decisive interpreter of Gudea’s temple-building dream. Gudea sails to Niĝin, offers bread and cold water, and invokes her as the gods’ dream-interpreter. She identifies:

- the cosmic warrior as her brother Ningirsu;
- the rising daylight as Ningishzida;
- the woman studying a star tablet as her sister Nisaba;
- the figure with a lapis tablet and building plan as a divine architect;
- the impatient donkey as Gudea himself.

She confirms that Ningirsu demands construction of the Eninnu and gives practical ritual advice. Later, Enki fixes foundation pegs while Nanshe attends to oracular decisions. The cylinders also connect the ruler’s justice toward orphan and widow with “the justice of Nanshe and Ningirsu.”

This is not merely a myth about private dream analysis. It legitimates a ruler’s construction program through a chain of divine authorization.

#### *Nanshe A* (“Hymn to Nanshe”)

**Language/support:** Sumerian; reconstructed from many clay-tablet witnesses, predominantly Old Babylonian school copies from Nippur and related collections.  
**Composition/transmission:** likely preserves late-third-millennium Lagashite institutions and royal memory, but the extant manuscript corpus is mainly early second millennium BCE.  
**Critical study:** Wolfgang Heimpel, “The Nanshe Hymn,” *Journal of Cuneiform Studies* 33 (1981), 65–139; Heimpel’s translation in *Context of Scripture* I; Jacobsen, *The Harps That Once…*; ETCSL 4.14.1.

Its major subjects are:

- Niĝin and the return/presence of Nanshe;
- abundance and full temple storerooms;
- aid to orphan, widow, debt-slave, refugee and weak person;
- legitimation of Ur-Nanshe and Gudea;
- temple musicians, cooks, food and drink offerings;
- inspection and appointment of personnel at New Year;
- Nisaba’s record keeping and Haia’s registration;
- dismissal of negligent priests;
- river ordeal and oracular procedure;
- condemnation of violence, boundary alteration, false weights and measures, concealed consumption and abuse;
- adjudication by Hendursag;
- standardization of silver weights, baskets and the *ban* measure;
- divine assembly at Sirara.

The “widow and orphan” material is therefore securely textual. Claims that the hymn proves the existence of a modern-style welfare state, asylum law or universal human-rights program go beyond the evidence. It describes the ideal order of a divine temple household and its rulers.

#### *Nanshe B* (*balbale* to Nanshe)

**Language/support:** Sumerian clay-tablet witnesses.  
**Date:** surviving witnesses Old Babylonian; original date uncertain.

This fragmentary song clothes Nanshe in fish imagery: fish serve as staff, sandals and royal garment; fish illuminate and stir the sea; she rides home in a gold-and-cedar boat. Nindara is called her husband and “tax collector of the sea.” The composition is the strongest literary evidence for her maritime/fish character.

#### *Nanshe C* (“Nanshe and the Birds”)

**Language/support:** Sumerian clay tablets.  
**Date:** Old Babylonian witnesses; earlier origin possible but not securely datable.

Nanshe associates herself with a **u₅ bird**, descends or moves upon the water, sets the holy bird at her feet, and commands birds of heaven and earth to assemble and bring offerings. The poem becomes an extensive catalogue of birds, habitats, calls and behaviors. Its last preserved lines praise her as one “who loves the fishes and birds.”

“Pelican” in ETCSL is printed with a question mark. Proposed species include goose, swan, cormorant, gull and pelican. The safe designation is **u₅ waterbird**.

#### *Enki and Ninhursag*

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Surviving manuscripts:** chiefly Old Babylonian; the composition probably draws on earlier traditions.

After Enki consumes forbidden plants and suffers in eight body parts, Ninhursag produces eight healing deities. **Nazi** is one of them and is assigned to marry Nindara. Identification of Nazi with Nanshe is supported by lexical lists and the same spouse. This gives her a mythic “birth,” but it is a literary episode, not necessarily the original cultic genealogy.

#### Sumerian Temple Hymns

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Transmission:** Old Babylonian copies of a collection traditionally associated in its closing rubric with Enheduana; the collection’s formation and attribution remain debated.

The Sirara/Niĝin section celebrates Nanshe’s watery, elevated sanctuary. It is important testimony to the temple’s literary prestige, but the surviving copies are much later than Enheduana’s lifetime.

#### God lists

Nanshe occurs in the Nippur and related lists, in Old Babylonian lexical traditions and in the first-millennium canonical list **An = Anum**. In *An = Anum* tablet III, Nindara, Nanshe and associated figures stand near the circle of Sîn. The placement is learned systematization; it does not prove that ordinary worshippers regarded Nanshe as Ningal or as the moon god’s wife.

#### First Sealand administrative corpus

**Language:** Akkadian, with Sumerograms.  
**Date:** later Old Babylonian/First Sealand period, approximately seventeenth–fifteenth centuries BCE, depending on chronological scheme.

Nanshe/Nazi is exceptionally prominent in surviving palace offering lists. She receives *nindabû* offerings, possibly linked with the full moon, and valuable animals, especially ewes. The archive makes her a royal or dynastic patron. Whether this was uninterrupted survival from Lagash or a deliberate Sealand revival/importation is disputed.

#### *Tintir = Babylon* / *Topography of Babylon*

**Language:** scholarly Akkadian.  
**Probable compilation:** late second millennium BCE, often associated with the twelfth century BCE; surviving manuscripts are later.

It assigns Nanshe a cultic seat, **Ešbanda**, “Little Chamber,” in the Esagil complex at Babylon. This is learned topography backed by a real cultic setting, but not evidence that Esagil became her principal temple.

#### *Gula Hymn* of Bulluṭsa-rabi

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Date:** usually placed in the late second millennium BCE.

The hymn identifies or assimilates many goddesses to Gula. Nanshe appears as **bēlet kudurri**, “lady of the boundary stone.” This is a learned syncretistic equation, not proof that Nanshe and Gula had always been one goddess.

### Negative corpus findings

The requested corpus check produces an important negative result:

- Nanshe has no material narrative role in **Enūma eliš**.
- She has no material role in **Atrahasis**.
- She has no material role in the standard Babylonian **Epic of Gilgamesh** as edited by Andrew George.
- She has no material role in **Inanna’s Descent**.
- She has no role in **Enki and Ninmah**; this title is sometimes confused with *Enki and Ninhursag*, where Nazi does appear.
- The Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP do not supply a major Nanshe myth or royal cult comparable to those of Aššur, Ištar or Marduk.

Mentioning her alongside these works merely because they belong to “Mesopotamian mythology” creates a false corpus.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Water, marshes, fish and birds

**[Secure early core]** Nanshe’s city lay in the alluvial and marsh environment of southern Lagash. *Nanshe B* makes the sea and fish her court; *Nanshe C* makes birds assemble before her; *Nanshe A* links her temple to waterways, fish deliveries, gardens, reed beds and vessels of ever-flowing water.

She is consequently better described as a goddess of the marsh-sea economy than simply “goddess of the ocean.” Sumerian **a-ab-ba**, often translated “sea,” could designate the lower waters/Gulf environment relevant to southern Mesopotamia.

### Abundance and institutional administration

*Nanshe A* credits her with bulging storehouses and properly supplied offerings. Her household inspects workers, registers personnel, audits food and drink, controls rationing, dismisses negligent officeholders and standardizes measures. Her concern with abundance is thus distributive and administrative, not merely vegetative fertility.

### Justice and social protection

**[Secure literary development]** The hymn says she cares for the orphan and widow, advises the debt-slave, protects refugees and finds a place for the weak. It condemns violence, boundary tampering and commercial fraud. She “sees into the heart of the Land.”

The judicial mechanism is partly delegated:

- Nanshe establishes the ordinances and moral field.
- Nisaba records.
- Haia registers.
- Hendursag examines testimony, separates just from wicked and promulgates decrees.
- The river ordeal provides divine judgment.

Calling Nanshe a “social justice goddess” is therefore accurate shorthand, provided it is not mistaken for an ancient formal title.

### Divination and dreams

**[Secure, ca. 2100 BCE]** Cylinder A calls her the interpreter of dreams among the gods. She decodes Gudea’s dream figure by figure and attends to oracular decisions during construction. Her expertise is interpretive, institutional and political.

No source establishes the popular modern claim that Nanshe grants all human “psychic powers” or that she is a generalized patron of clairvoyants.

### Boundaries and measurement

*Nanshe A* names her a guarantor of boundaries and associates her assembly with standardized silver weights, reed baskets and capacity measures. Enmetena’s inscription invokes her territorial boundary dike. The later Gula hymn retains the title “lady of the boundary stone.”

### Iconography

No surviving image carries an inscription saying unequivocally “this is Nanshe.” Identifications depend on textual attributes and find context.

**[Probable identifications]**

- A goddess seated or standing with a flowing vase, accompanied by waterfowl and fish.
- A goddess enthroned upon or associated with a large bird.
- Morgan Seal 260, Post-Akkadian in date, shows a worshipper led toward a goddess seated on a bird; swans or waterbirds appear below. It is a plausible Nanshe image but not certain.
- A Louvre terracotta plaque traditionally described as a “goddess with geese” has been associated with Nanshe.
- Ur III/Post-Akkadian seals showing a female deity, flowing water, fish and birds are candidates.

The older proposal that any goddess with flowing water must be Nanshe is unsafe: Enki/Ea, Ningal and other divine figures can share aquatic imagery. A recent study of waterbird-shaped weights warns that cylinder-seal imagery cannot always distinguish Nanshe from Ningal.

### Emblems and animals

- **u₅ waterbird:** securely associated; precise species uncertain.
- **Fish:** securely associated in hymns and offerings.
- **Flowing vessel/water:** plausible iconographic marker but not exclusive.
- **Boat:** prominent in *Nanshe B* and Gudea’s journey.
- **Reed beds:** environmental and hymnic association.
- **Boundary markers, measuring vessels, tablets and stylus:** functional associations, though tablets and stylus especially belong to Nisaba in Nanshe’s household.
- No securely attested sacred color, weekday, planet or canonical number is known.
- The claim that a pelican symbolized self-sacrifice because it fed its young with its blood imports a medieval Christian bestiary motif. It is not Sumerian evidence.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical traditions

**[Lagashite canonical tradition]**

- **Father:** Enki. *Nanshe A* calls her “child of Enki”; Gudea calls her daughter/child of Eridug.
- **Mother:** usually reconstructed as Damgalnuna from Enki’s standard household, though early Nanshe texts do not consistently name her.
- **Brother:** Ningirsu, explicitly so in Cylinder A.
- **Sister:** Nisaba, explicitly so in Nanshe’s dream interpretation.
- **Husband:** Nindara, explicitly in *Nanshe B*, *Enki and Ninhursag* and god-list traditions.
- **Daughter:** Nin-MAR.KI is treated as Nanshe’s daughter in some traditions; this relationship is not maintained consistently in *An = Anum*.

**[Narrative variant]** In *Enki and Ninhursag*, Nazi/Nanshe is produced by Ninhursag as a healing deity from Enki’s afflicted body and then assigned to Nindara. This makes Ninhursag her immediate generator. It should not be harmonized into an ordinary sexual parentage story.

**[Unsupported popular harmonizations]** Claims that Nanshe was a daughter of Enlil, sister of Inanna and Ereshkigal, sister of Ninurta in every tradition, or spouse of Haia commonly arise from conflating divine households, shared titles and administrative association. Haia serves as registrar in *Nanshe A*; the text does not call him her husband. Nindara is the securely attested spouse.

### Every documented divine interaction or joint episode

The following list separates genuine interactions from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Enki — father who determines her destiny.** In *Nanshe A* 251–55, “Father Enki” determines a fate for her; in the Gudea Cylinders Enki fixes foundation pegs while Nanshe oversees oracles. **Source/date:** Gudea Cylinders, ca. 2100 BCE; *Nanshe A*, Old Babylonian witnesses preserving earlier tradition. **Tier:** securely attested Lagashite tradition.

- **Ninhursag — produces Nazi to heal Enki.** After asking where Enki hurts, Ninhursag brings forth Nazi, who is then assigned to Nindara. **Source/date:** *Enki and Ninhursag*, Old Babylonian manuscripts. **Tier:** later literary/narrative variant.

- **Ningirsu — brother and partner in dynastic justice.** Nanshe identifies Ningirsu as the colossal figure in Gudea’s dream and explains his demand for the Eninnu; Gudea later attends to “the justice of Nanshe and Ningirsu.” **Source/date:** Gudea Cylinders A–B, ca. 2100 BCE. **Tier:** securely attested Lagashite canonical tradition.

- **Nindara — husband.** She calls Nindara “my husband” and “tax collector of the sea”; *Enki and Ninhursag* assigns Nazi to marry him. **Sources/dates:** *Nanshe B* and *Enki and Ninhursag*, Old Babylonian witnesses. **Tier:** canonical marital relation.

- **Nisaba — sister and chief scribe.** Nanshe identifies the dream woman with golden stylus and star tablet as “my sister Nisaba”; in *Nanshe A*, Nisaba places tablets on her knees and records personnel for Nanshe. **Sources/dates:** Cylinder A, ca. 2100 BCE; *Nanshe A*, Old Babylonian witnesses. **Tier:** securely attested/canonical.

- **Hendursag — herald, household judge and promulgator.** He distinguishes good and evil, examines testimony and issues Nanshe’s decrees. **Source/date:** *Nanshe A*, Old Babylonian witnesses; he and Nanshe also co-occur in ED IIIb Lagash inscriptions. **Tier:** canonical household relation.

- **Haia — registrar serving her administration.** He enters or deletes personnel on a tablet at Nanshe’s inspection. **Source/date:** *Nanshe A* 94–112. **Tier:** canonical administrative interaction. **Caution:** not securely her spouse.

- **Utu — Hendursag is called Utu’s child.** This is an indirect genealogical link within Nanshe’s household, not a preserved meeting between Nanshe and Utu. **Source/date:** *Nanshe A* 83–91. **Tier:** learned/cultic genealogy; no direct interaction.

- **An — approves her in the divine assembly.** An looks joyfully at Nanshe as she sits with Enlil at the fate-determining dais. **Source/date:** *Nanshe A* 251–55. **Tier:** hymnic exaltation.

- **Enlil — shares the fate-determining dais.** Nanshe sits with Enlil and is repeatedly said to determine fates “like Enlil.” **Source/date:** *Nanshe A* and Cylinder A. **Tier:** hymnic/theological exaltation, not equality of office.

- **Ĝatumdug — co-founder/co-protector within Lagash theology.** *Nanshe A* describes Nanshe as founding Lagash with Ĝatumdug; in the Gudea Cylinders, Ĝatumdug receives Gudea’s prayer before his journey and later gives birth ritually to the temple bricks while Nanshe oversees oracles. **Sources/dates:** Gudea Cylinders, ca. 2100 BCE; *Nanshe A*. **Tier:** securely attested local joint action.

- **Bau — joint temple-building scene.** Bau sprinkles the Eninnu bricks with oil and cedar essence while Nanshe handles oracular messages. A possible fragment of *Nanshe C* also mentions Bau bathing, but its placement may belong to a different version. **Sources/dates:** Gudea Cylinders; *Nanshe C*, Old Babylonian witnesses. **Tier:** securely attested joint appearance; fragmentary regional/literary variant in *Nanshe C*.

- **Ningishzida — interpreted by Nanshe.** She identifies the dream’s rising daylight as Gudea’s personal god Ningishzida. He also appears in the Sirara divine assembly in fragmentary *Nanshe A*. **Sources/dates:** Cylinder A, ca. 2100 BCE; *Nanshe A*. **Tier:** securely attested interpretive interaction/joint assembly.

- **Ištaran — participant in her Sirara assembly.** He appears as a righteous lawsuit-decider when the gods establish standardized measures. **Source/date:** *Nanshe A* 232–50. **Tier:** canonical joint assembly; the exact damaged syntax remains uncertain.

- **Anuna gods — acclaim and serve within her sphere.** *Nanshe C* calls her praised by the Anuna and has an Anzud bird decide destinies with them; the Gudea Cylinders place the Anuna in admiration during temple building. **Sources/dates:** *Nanshe C*, Old Babylonian witnesses; Gudea Cylinders, ca. 2100 BCE. **Tier:** canonical collective interaction.

- **Nin-MAR.KI — daughter in some local lists.** This is a genealogical placement, not a surviving narrative interaction. *An = Anum* places her nearby but does not preserve the daughter equation. **Tier:** regional genealogy subsequently weakened in learned systematization.

- **Gula — Nanshe assimilated as one of Gula’s forms.** **Source/date:** *Gula Hymn* of Bulluṭsa-rabi, late second millennium BCE. **Tier:** learned syncretistic identification.

- **Ningal/Sîn — cultic proximity, not demonstrated identity.** Old Babylonian Ur offerings place Nanshe near Ningal; *An = Anum* positions Nanshe’s group near Sîn and equates Nindara with Sîn. It does not directly equate Nanshe with Ningal. **Tier:** regional cultic association and learned systematization.

- **Kiki/Ninkiki — proposed fusion.** Krebernik and Lisman suggest that the Early Dynastic waterbird goddess Kiki, who disappears after the early period, may have merged with Nanshe. **Tier:** modern scholarly reconstruction; no surviving ancient equation proves it.

No specific interactions with Inanna, Ereshkigal, Marduk, Tiamat or Gilgamesh are securely documented merely from their belonging to the wider Mesopotamian pantheon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Dream of Gudea

**[Securely attested, ca. 2100 BCE]**

Ningirsu appears to Gudea in an overwhelming dream and commands construction of his Eninnu temple, but Gudea cannot understand the signs. He decides to consult Nanshe, his “mother” and divine sister from Sirara. Traveling by ceremonial boat toward Niĝin, he makes offerings and approaches her court.

Gudea recounts a sequence of images: a cosmic warrior with Anzud-like wings and lions; sunrise; a woman consulting a star-marked tablet with a metal stylus; a powerful male figure holding a lapis architectural plan; a brick mold and carrying basket; and an agitated donkey.

Nanshe interprets the dream systematically. Ningirsu is the colossal warrior; Ningishzida is the light; Nisaba announces the auspicious celestial sign; the plan concerns the Eninnu; Gudea himself is the laboring donkey. She advises Gudea to fashion Ningirsu’s chariot, emblem, weapons and drum to secure favorable divine attention. Gudea acts on her counsel and builds the temple.

The cycle’s function is political and ritual: it presents royal construction not as arbitrary expenditure but as a correctly interpreted divine command. Nanshe mediates between divine revelation and executable state policy.

### Nanshe and the moral-temple order

**[Canonical Old Babylonian literary form preserving earlier institutions]**

*Nanshe A* is not a continuous adventure story. It dramatizes the goddess’s return, the abundance of her city, the inspection of her personnel and the exposure of wrongdoing.

At New Year, workers appear before her. Nisaba records them; Haia verifies registration. Priests responsible for food, ritual cleanliness, water and song can lose office for negligence. The ordeal river cleanses or judges. Nanshe’s law rejects force, altered boundaries, false weights and covert consumption. She sees the conditions of orphan, widow and waif, while Hendursag investigates cases and executes her ordinances. The gods then establish standardized measures in Sirara.

The poem’s “myth” is therefore the manifestation of just institutional order. Modern accounts sometimes extract only its compassionate lines and omit its disciplinary, audit and ordeal machinery.

### Nanshe and the fish

**[Fragmentary canonical composition]**

In *Nanshe B*, marine abundance becomes royal pageantry. Fish form Nanshe’s staff, footwear and garment, illuminate the sea, play instruments and gather around her boat. She proclaims her voyage home; Nindara administers the sea’s dues.

The poem may sacralize fisheries, collection of aquatic produce and transport to the temple. Because only fragments survive, a complete plot cannot be reconstructed.

### Nanshe and the birds

**[Fragmentary canonical composition]**

The poem opens with the u₅ bird emerging from sacred reed beds. Nanshe associates herself with it, moves upon or descends to the water, erects a lapis shrine and sets the bird at her feet. She summons birds of heaven and earth, including unnamed and uncaught birds, to attend and bring offerings. Long catalogues characterize species through color, call, habitat and behavior.

This is simultaneously a divine myth, lexical catalogue and observation of marsh fauna. Some segments may derive from different versions; ETCSL expressly warns that Segment D may not belong to the same recension.

### The healing birth of Nazi

**[Later canonical narrative variant]**

In *Enki and Ninhursag*, Enki’s consumption of plants brings bodily illness. Ninhursag creates a deity corresponding to each pain. Nazi is one of these deities and receives Nindara as husband.

The episode supplies a literary origin for Nanshe/Nazi but does not explain her principal Lagashite functions. It may be secondary incorporation of a pre-existing goddess into the Dilmun healing sequence.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Niĝin/Nina and É-Sirara

Nanshe’s primary cult center was **Niĝin**, modern **Tell Zurghul** in Dhi Qar, Iraq, within the Lagash state. Older scholarship often read the city name Nina; Niĝin is now common.

Her principal sanctuary was **É-Sirara**. “Sirara” may designate the temple, precinct or an intimately connected settlement; sources do not always distinguish these possibilities. Translating it with a confident literal meaning is unsafe.

Ur-Nanshe and later Lagash rulers claimed construction or restoration there. Gudea’s inscribed cones and bricks commemorate work on Nanshe’s temple.

Modern Italian excavations beginning in 2015 investigated Mound A. The evidence establishes occupation and repeated artificial elevation from the early third millennium BCE. Gudea-inscribed building material was recovered, and production/storage areas likely served the nearby sanctuary. Heavy erosion, ancient destruction and Robert Koldewey’s 1887 trenching compromised upper levels. The excavators therefore distinguish between:

- textual certainty that É-Sirara stood at Niĝin;
- strong contextual evidence placing it on Mound A;
- incomplete survival of the temple’s actual plan.

Recent excavations also recovered clay goose/waterbird figurines and Early Dynastic material near the presumed temple zone. Calling each figurine an image “of Nanshe” is possible but not epigraphically certain.

### Other sanctuaries and cult places

- **Girsu:** Nanshe possessed a shrine or temple in the Lagash capital and appears throughout its institutional records.
- **Lagash/Al-Hiba:** royal inscriptions and cult materials associate her with the wider state.
- **Ur:** she received offerings, particularly in association with Ningal, during the Isin-Larsa/Old Babylonian period.
- **Umma:** “Nanshe of Umma” received regular offerings under the Ur III administration.
- **Nippur, Adab and Uruk:** lesser textual attestations occur, without evidence that she became the dominant city goddess.
- **Babylon:** *Tintir* locates her seat Ešbanda inside Esagil.
- **Sealand palace cult:** offering archives show strong royal sponsorship, though the capital and exact cult installation remain debated.

### Personnel and offices

*Nanshe A* names or alludes to:

- **šita-abba**, **šusbu**, **sagga**, **enkum** and **ninkum** personnel;
- a dream interpreter;
- cooks, musicians and carriers of fat, milk, fish and firewood;
- Nisaba as divine chief scribe;
- Haia as divine registrar;
- Hendursag as herald and judge.

Several titles remain philologically obscure. It is unjustified to translate the personnel collectively as a modern “charitable clergy,” though the institution’s ideal duties included feeding dependants and protecting the weak.

### Festivals and offerings

**[Textually attested]** *Nanshe A* describes a New Year inspection “on the day of rites,” with water libation, ration bowls, registration, purification, songs and abundant food. It also mentions a continuing first-fruit observance.

Offerings included bread, beer, wine, meat, milk, fat, fish, eggs, plants and water. First Sealand archives record animal sacrifice, notably valuable ewes, and *nindabû* offerings.

### Divination, ordeal and law

Gudea approaches Nanshe for dream interpretation after offerings and prayer. *Nanshe A* refers to oracular messages emerging from the *abzu* and to a river ordeal at her house.

The text does not prove that every pilgrim was thrown into a river before seeing Nanshe, as some modern retellings claim. It establishes an ordeal institution connected with her temple and justice, but its precise frequency and participants are not recoverable.

### Human sacrifice, “temple prostitution” and violence

The dedicated contested-material search found:

- **No evidence of human sacrifice specifically to Nanshe.**
- **No evidence for “sacred prostitution” or institutional temple prostitution in her cult.**
- Animal slaughter and meat offerings are directly attested.
- River ordeal could expose accused persons to injury or death, but the surviving hymn does not describe its exact implementation.
- Her political cult supported Lagashite territorial claims: Enmetena invokes punishment against anyone violating Nanshe’s boundary dike.
- Her justice is coercive as well as compassionate. Officials are dismissed, offenders denied sacred food, and malefactors described as physically ruined or socially rejected.

Claims of human sacrifice or sexual priesthood should not be imported from generalized and often outdated pictures of “Mesopotamian religion.”

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before ca. 2600 BCE

Tell Zurghul was occupied before Nanshe’s first written appearance, but archaeology cannot assign every early building or waterbird object to her. A prehistoric cult of Nanshe is possible, not demonstrable.

### ED IIIa–IIIb, ca. 2600–2340 BCE: emergence into documentation

Nanshe appears in the written pantheon and becomes a central state goddess of Lagash. Ur-Nanshe’s theophoric name and building program, royal dedications, land and boundary formulas, and the city of Niĝin establish a mature institutional cult.

Her political position complemented that of Ningirsu: he was the great warrior and chief god of Girsu; she anchored Niĝin, waterways, resources and territorial/juridical order.

### Akkadian period, ca. 2334–2154 BCE

The cult continued despite imperial reorganization. Evidence is less narratively rich, but the theonym and cult institutions survived. There is no documented episode of Akkadian authorities suppressing Nanshe.

### Lagash II/Gudea, ca. 2144–2124 BCE: literary and architectural peak

This is the best-documented phase. Gudea monumentalized Nanshe as:

- sister of Ningirsu;
- divine dream interpreter;
- controller of oracles;
- guarantor of just kingship;
- goddess of Sirara and Niĝin.

Gudea restored É-Sirara, and his inscribed building material remains a principal archaeological marker at Tell Zurghul.

### Ur III, ca. 2112–2004 BCE: integration and diffusion

Administrative documentation shows offerings within and beyond Lagash, including “Nanshe of Umma.” Her cult participated in the redistributive system of the Ur III state. This is diffusion, not evidence that she became universally supreme.

### Isin-Larsa and Old Babylonian periods, ca. 2000–1595 BCE

Her political prominence in Lagash declined, while scribal transmission expanded her literary afterlife. Most manuscripts of *Nanshe A–C* and *Enki and Ninhursag* belong to this period. At Ur she entered Ningal’s cultic circle; sporadic personal and offering evidence persists.

This divergence matters: declining autonomous city cult did not mean disappearance from learned culture.

### First Sealand dynasty, approximately seventeenth–fifteenth centuries BCE

Nanshe/Nazi became one of the most frequently attested recipients in a Sealand palace archive and evidently a dynastic goddess.

Two interpretations remain viable:

- **Continuation/revival:** Sealand rule inherited or deliberately revived the old southern marsh goddess.
- **Court importation:** Odette Boivin notes that her traditional Lagashite retinue is largely absent, suggesting that the court adopted Nanshe in a new configuration rather than simply continuing Niĝin’s cult.

The documentary sample may not represent the entire Sealand pantheon.

### Late second millennium BCE: scholarly systematization

*An = Anum*, the *Gula Hymn* and *Tintir* reorganized Nanshe:

- her household was fitted into canonical divine lists;
- Nindara was associated/equated with Sîn;
- Nanshe was assimilated to Gula as boundary goddess;
- she received a seat within Marduk’s Esagil complex.

These are historical acts of theological classification, not timeless truths about her identity.

### First millennium BCE

Nanshe remained textually and cultically known in Babylonia. Evidence extends into the Neo-Babylonian sixth century BCE; ePSD indexing includes Persian and Hellenistic material. Her scale was modest compared with Marduk, Nabû, Ištar, Sîn or Šamaš.

No Assyrian royal campaign is known to have suppressed her specifically. Nor is there evidence of a discrete Christian or Islamic destruction campaign against a functioning Nanshe temple. Her cult faded as the old temple systems and cuneiform institutions contracted over the first millennium BCE and early centuries CE.

### Late antiquity to nineteenth century CE

There is no documented continuous worship, folklore lineage or hidden transmission of Nanshe. Her name survived on buried tablets, not in an identifiable living oral cult.

### Nineteenth century to present: rediscovery

Excavation, cuneiform decipherment, publication of the Gudea Cylinders and recovery of Nippur literary tablets reconstructed her identity. Koldewey investigated Tell Zurghul in 1887; twentieth-century Assyriologists edited the hymns; renewed Italian excavations since 2015 have materially refined the setting of Niĝin and É-Sirara.

Any contemporary worship is revivalist or reconstructionist, not demonstrably continuous.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Nanshe’s modern profile is shaped primarily by translated Sumerian literature. Samuel Noah Kramer, Thorkild Jacobsen and Wolfgang Heimpel made the orphan-and-widow passage accessible; reference works by Black and Green and surveys of Mesopotamian goddesses popularized her aquatic and judicial characteristics.

### Modern devotional reception

Small-scale Mesopotamian reconstructionist and eclectic pagan communities invoke Nanshe as a goddess of justice, dreams, refugees, waterways and ecological stewardship. Online prayers and devotional pages are genuine evidence of modern worship but not evidence for ancient ritual details.

No reliable demographic estimate for Nanshe-specific worship was found. Claims of a large organized modern cult cannot be established.

### Modern inventions and distortions

- **“Pelican of maternal self-sacrifice”:** combines an uncertain translation of *u₅* with a medieval Christian pelican legend. Modern invention.
- **Fish/pelican symbols taken over directly by Christianity from Nanshe:** no historical chain demonstrates this. Fish and bird symbolism is widespread; resemblance is not transmission.
- **Haia as her usual husband:** a modern conflation of his registrar role with spousal status. Nindara is the attested husband.
- **Universal psychic patroness:** extrapolation from her single well-documented role as divine dream interpreter.
- **Goddess of modern human rights, socialism or democracy:** metaphorical reception, not an ancient classification.
- **Continuous survival into Christian or Islamic folk religion:** no evidence found.
- **Ancient spells, planetary correspondences, tarot cards, colors or “Nanshe sigils” circulating online:** unless derived transparently from cuneiform signs or attested texts, these are modern occult constructions.
- **A fixed portrait of Nanshe as a woman dancing over water between winged Anuna:** interpretation of an uninscribed image, not certain identification.

### Literature and popular culture

Compared with Inanna/Ištar, Ereshkigal, Enki or Marduk, Nanshe has had limited major literary, cinematic or gaming reception. Her modern visibility is strongest in mythology websites, devotional art, educational retellings and social-media reconstructionism. No major Renaissance, Golden Dawn, Theosophical or early-modern grimoire tradition centered on her was found.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No documented major nationalist or extremist movement centered on Nanshe emerged from the dedicated search. General uses of Sumerian religion in nationalist or racialized pseudo-history should not be imputed to this goddess without specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major scholarship

- **Wolfgang Heimpel, “The Nanshe Hymn,” JCS 33 (1981):** fundamental edition and commentary on *Nanshe A*.
- **Thorkild Jacobsen, *The Harps That Once…* (1987):** influential literary translation, sometimes freer and more interpretive than later philological renderings.
- **Gebhard J. Selz, *Untersuchungen zur Götterwelt des altsumerischen Stadtstaates von Lagaš* (1995):** major German study of the early Lagash pantheon.
- **Dietz Otto Edzard, *Gudea and His Dynasty* (1997):** standard RIME edition of Gudea.
- **Edzard, “Nanše,” *Reallexikon der Assyriologie*:** concise critical synthesis.
- **Gonzalo Rubio, Jeremy Black and ETCSL collaborators:** electronic composite texts and translations.
- **Niek Veldhuis, work on religion, classification and *Nanshe and the Birds*:** crucial for understanding the bird catalogue as learned literature.
- **Julia M. Asher-Greve and Joan Goodnick Westenholz, *Goddesses in Context* (2013):** gendered divine history and later cultic development.
- **Odette Boivin, *The First Dynasty of the Sealand in Mesopotamia* (2018):** fundamental reassessment based on the new Sealand archives.
- **Davide Nadali, Andrea Polcaro and collaborators:** renewed archaeology of Tell Zurghul/Niĝin and É-Sirara’s setting.
- **Grégory Chambon and Adelheid Otto:** reassessment of waterbird imagery and warning against easy iconographic identifications.

### Principal disputes

#### Meaning of the name

The sign contains “fish,” but no plausible Sumerian etymology of *Nanše* is accepted. Graphic interpretation must not be confused with linguistic derivation.

#### Nina, Niĝin and Sirara

The city sign formerly read Nina is now often read Niĝin. Sirara may be its temple, sacred precinct, closely adjacent place, or a locality eventually merged with it. Ancient texts sometimes blur these categories.

#### Identity of the u₅ bird

Pelican, goose, swan, cormorant and gull have all been proposed. “Pelican” is not settled. Recent finds of goose figurines strengthen a waterfowl association but do not determine every lexical occurrence.

#### Identification of images

A goddess with birds, fish or flowing water is a plausible Nanshe, especially in a southern/Post-Akkadian context, but these motifs are not exclusive. Earlier art-historical identifications, including those influenced by Henri Frankfort’s seal studies, require case-by-case caution.

#### Origin of the social-justice function

One view treats protection of the weak as a defining ancient essence of Nanshe. A more historical reading notes that the richest evidence comes from *Nanshe A* and Gudea’s ideology: marsh economy, temple audit, correct measures, boundaries and vulnerable dependants form one institutional system. The ethical image may be an elaboration of administrative sovereignty rather than a separate original “domain.”

#### Kiki/Ninkiki

Krebernik and Lisman’s proposed absorption of the waterbird-associated Early Dynastic Kiki by Nanshe is attractive but circumstantial. There is no explicit ancient statement “Kiki is Nanshe.”

#### Sealand continuity or revival

Westenholz allows continuity or revival; Boivin emphasizes a courtly import or reconfiguration because Nanshe’s Lagashite entourage is mostly missing. New archives transformed this debate: older histories that end her significant cult around Ur III are obsolete.

### State and location of evidence

Relevant objects and tablets are dispersed among:

- the Iraq Museum, Baghdad;
- the Louvre, including the Gudea Cylinders;
- University of Pennsylvania Museum collections;
- Istanbul/Nippur collections represented by “Ni” numbers;
- Yale, Chicago and other cuneiform collections;
- Morgan Library seal collections;
- excavation stores and publications for Tell Zurghul.

ETCSL provides legacy composites and translations but has not been substantively revised for many years. ORACC/ePSD2 and CDLI provide broader, continually curated lexical and object-level access. A rigorous study should consult tablet photographs and modern editions rather than treating a composite translation as a single manuscript.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- transparent etymology “lady of fish”;
- secure identification of the sacred bird as pelican;
- Nanshe as wife of Haia;
- direct borrowing of her fish/pelican symbols by Christianity;
- temple prostitution;
- human sacrifice;
- empire-wide supremacy in the third millennium;
- uninterrupted worship into modern times;
- all water-vase goddesses as Nanshe.
