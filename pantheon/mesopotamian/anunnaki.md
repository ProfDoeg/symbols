# The Anunnaki: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A collective of gods, not a single god, species, race, demon, or monster. Sumerian texts call them the **Anuna/Anunna**, often “the great gods”; Akkadian texts use **Anunnakū/Anunnaki**. The collective’s membership, number, rank, and cosmic location vary by period and composition.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Post-Akkadian Sumerian inscriptions associated with Gudea of Lagaš, approximately the late twenty-second or early twenty-first century BCE, followed by several Ur III administrative attestations. The earliest evidence calls the Anuna a divine collective but does not identify them as extraterrestrials, associate them with a planet named Nibiru, or give them a fixed roster. The securely attested history begins with the Anuna as eminent gods associated with divine assembly, fate, and the pantheons of particular cities. [ORACC-AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/Anunna/index.html)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** third-millennium or Old Babylonian textual or administrative evidence.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** an influential Babylonian or Assyrian literary formulation, principally late second- or first-millennium BCE.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant:** a formulation limited to a city, recension, genre, or manuscript tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** scribal lists, commentaries, numerological classifications, or theological organization.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically situated equation or fusion.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** a post-cuneiform interpretation predating modern Assyriology.
- **[F] Folklore:** vernacular transmission not demonstrably derived from a surviving ancient text.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** conclusions reached by comparing incomplete, damaged, or composite evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention:** a modern claim absent from the ancient sources, even if presented as ancient tradition.

Dates of compositions must be distinguished from dates of surviving manuscripts. Many Sumerian works may preserve older traditions but survive principally on Old Babylonian school tablets of approximately 1900–1600 BCE.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### 1.1 Identity

**[EA/MSR]** The Anuna were a category or assembly of eminent Mesopotamian gods. In Sumerian literary usage the name can encompass the “great gods” generally, the divine assembly, or a local pantheon—hence references to the Anuna of Eridu, Lagaš, or another city. No single ancient document supplies a universally valid membership list. The collective is instead defined relationally: gods gathered around An, Enlil, Enki, Inanna, a city’s principal deity, or a deliberative assembly.

**[EA]** Their recurrent early functions are:

- determining or confirming destinies;
- witnessing and ratifying divine or royal decisions;
- inhabiting temples and consuming offerings;
- admiring, fearing, acclaiming, or petitioning a principal deity;
- appearing as the leading gods of a city or the whole land;
- in one important Sumerian composition, acting as seven judges in the netherworld.

**[LC]** Akkadian literature increasingly uses *Anunnakū* for the gods of the earth or netherworld, sometimes in contrast to the heavenly **Igīgū**. This opposition never became perfectly consistent. Other passages use *Anunnakū* broadly for gods of heaven and earth, while *Igīgū* may mean the heavenly gods or simply the gods assembled. Ancient scribes inherited overlapping classifications rather than a single systematic cosmology. [ORACC Anunna](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/Anunna/index.html) [ORACC Igigi](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/igigi/)

### 1.2 Spellings and normalized forms

**Sumerian forms [EA]:**

- `𒀭𒀀𒉣` — conventionally transliterated `ᵈa-nun`
- `𒀭𒀀𒉣𒈾` — `ᵈa-nun-na`
- `ᵈa-nun-na-ke₄-ne`
- `ᵈa-nun-ke₄-ne`
- logographic forms including `ᵈA.NUN`, `ᵈA.NUN.NA`, and `ᵈA.NUN.NA.KE₄.NE`

Normalized English spellings include **Anuna**, **Anunna**, and occasionally **Anunak**. The initial superscript `d` in transliteration represents the divine determinative *diĝir*; it is a classifier, not normally pronounced as part of the name.

**Akkadian forms [LC]:**

- *Anunnakū*
- *Anunnaki*
- *Anunnakku*
- syllabic writings including `ᵈa-nun-na-ki`
- cryptographic `ᵈGÉŠ.U`, particularly in learned and royal writing

RINAP’s Neo-Assyrian glossary records both `ᵈa-nun-na-ki` and `ᵈGÉŠ.U`. [RINAP glossary](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/rinap3/cbd/qpn-x-divine/onebigfile.html)

The widespread modern singular “an Anunnaki” is not the ordinary ancient classification. The ancient term normally denotes a collective.

### 1.3 Etymology

**[MSR; disputed]** The most influential scholarly interpretation understands Sumerian *a-nun-na* approximately as **“offspring of the prince”** or **“those of princely seed.”** Adam Falkenstein associated *a* with seed or progeny, *nun* with princely status, and the formation with descent from An. Dietz-Otto Edzard offered a closely related interpretation. The translation is plausible rather than mechanically certain: Sumerian compound analysis, spelling variation, and the uncertain history of the name prevent absolute confidence.

**[MI]** The popular expansion “those who from heaven to earth came” is not a valid parsing of *a-nun-na* or *Anunnakū*. A Sumerian statement “from heaven to earth” would require grammatical elements not present in the divine name. Neither final **-ki** in the conventional Akkadian spelling nor the Sumerian sign sequence establishes “Earth” as the destination of extraterrestrial travelers. [Falkenstein, “Die Anunna in der sumerischen Überlieferung”](https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/as16.pdf)

### 1.4 Titles and descriptions

The following are descriptions rather than permanent personal epithets:

- **“The great gods”** (*diĝir gal-gal-e-ne*) — frequent Sumerian apposition. **[EA]**
- **Gods who determine destinies** — especially in compositions centered on Enki, Enlil, or a royal investiture. **[EA]**
- **Children/offspring of An** — genealogical or honorific formulation; not an invariant roster. **[EA/RV]**
- **Fifty Anuna of Eridu** — local and numerical grouping reported in the Sumerian tradition. **[EA/RV]**
- **Seven judges** — *Inanna’s Descent*, where seven Anuna judge Inanna. **[EA/RV]**
- **Great Anunnakū** — conventional Akkadian designation. **[LC]**
- **Anunnakū of heaven and earth** — inclusive cosmic formulation in *Enūma eliš*. **[LC]**
- **Anunnakū of the netherworld/earth** — later classificatory usage, particularly when contrasted with heavenly Igīgū. **[LC/LS]**
- **“Judge of the Anunnaki”** — a damaged title of Gilgameš in Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš* VIII; probably connected to Gilgameš’s netherworld judicial role. **[LC; interpretation uncertain]**

### 1.5 What the name does not mean

No known cuneiform text identifies “Anunnaki” as:

- a biological species;
- reptilian beings;
- inhabitants of a planet called Nibiru;
- astronauts arriving in rockets;
- giants nine or twelve feet tall;
- genetic engineers using hominid DNA;
- miners sent to Earth for gold;
- the biblical Nephilim as a linguistic equivalence.

All of those are **[MI]**, principally developments from Zecharia Sitchin’s *The 12th Planet* (1976) and its reception.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 Earliest evidence

**[EA] Gudea and post-Akkadian Lagaš, approximately 2144–2124 BCE.** The earliest secure occurrences are in inscriptions of the Gudea period. The Gudea cylinders, clay foundation documents from Girsu/Tello describing construction of Ningirsu’s Eninnu temple, invoke or describe the Anuna as a divine assembly. Cylinder B opens in a hymnic and dedicatory setting; elsewhere the Anuna react with wonder to divine and royal temple-building. The cylinders themselves are monumental ritual texts, not historical annals in the modern sense. Their evidence establishes that the term was intelligible by the Gudea period and belonged to temple-centered theology. It does not establish a fixed number, universal membership, or independent Anuna sanctuary. [ETCSL Gudea line 825](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslgloss.php?charenc=&lookup=c217.825&sn=OFF)

**[EA] Ur III evidence, approximately 2112–2004 BCE.** A small number of administrative documents appear to record offerings to the Anuna. This revises Falkenstein’s older conclusion that the name occurred only in literary texts and therefore reflected no cultic practice. The evidence remains sparse—ORACC notes only three administrative attestations—so it demonstrates offerings but not a large independent institution. [ORACC Anunna](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/Anunna/index.html)

### 2.2 Principal Sumerian corpus

#### *Enki and Ninmaḫ* — ETCSL 1.1.2

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Composition/transmission:** probably composed in the late third or early second millennium BCE; surviving witnesses are mainly Old Babylonian.  
**Role:** The poem begins when the Anuna have been born. Senior gods supervise work while junior gods carry it; their complaint leads Namma to awaken Enki and propose human creation. Enki and Ninmaḫ then fashion humanity, followed by their contest over malformed humans.

**Significance:** The Anuna belong to the divine society within which the labor crisis occurs, but the text does not say that extraterrestrial Anunnaki genetically engineered human beings. Creation is expressed through divine birth-making, clay, and assigned social functions. [ETCSL, *Enki and Ninmaḫ*](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?charenc=j&text=t.1.1.2)

#### *Enki and the World Order* — ETCSL 1.1.3

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Transmission:** principally Old Babylonian manuscripts.  
**Role:** The Anuna inhabit Sumer’s shrines, receive food, determine destinies, approach Enki’s shrine with prayers, and receive dwellings and fields through Enki’s ordering of the world. Enki confirms or administers their functions.

**Significance:** This is a strong witness to the Anuna as the organized great gods of the inhabited divine world, with cultic sustenance and territorial placements—not a separate extraterrestrial species. [ETCSL search corpus](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?charenc=gcirc&lists=&searchword=&simplesearch=translation&simplesearchword=anuna)

#### *Enki’s Journey to Nippur* — ETCSL 1.1.4

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Role:** At Enki’s banquet, An sits at the head, Enlil beside him, Nintur in the place of honor, and the Anuna in adjacent places. They drink beer and liquor.

**Significance:** The scene represents hierarchy through banquet seating. The Anuna are the assembled divine elite.

#### *The Debate between Grain and Sheep* — ETCSL 5.3.2

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Role:** An begets the Anuna before Grain and Sheep have been created. The Anuna initially do not know these forms of subsistence; Grain and Sheep are produced for the gods’ food and clothing.

**Significance:** Here An is progenitor and the Anuna are dependent divine consumers. The text explicitly calls them “the great gods.” [ETCSL, Grain and Sheep line 11](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslgloss.php?charenc=&lookup=c532.11&sn=OFF)

#### *Inanna’s Descent to the Netherworld* — ETCSL 1.4.1

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Transmission:** Old Babylonian manuscripts; the composition’s antecedents may be older.  
**Role:** After Inanna displaces Ereškigal from her throne, “the Anuna, the seven judges” decide against her. Their gaze is death, their speech anger, and their cry condemnation; Inanna becomes a corpse hung on a hook.

**Significance:** This is the clearest Sumerian evidence connecting Anuna with netherworld judgment, but it does not prove that every Anuna in every period was an underworld deity. It is a scene-specific group of seven. [ETCSL, *Inanna’s Descent* line 167](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslgloss.php?charenc=gcirc&lookup=c141.167)

#### Royal hymns and laments

**[EA/RV]** Numerous Sumerian royal hymns describe the Anuna blessing a king, bowing before a deity, confirming destiny, inhabiting shrines, or reacting in fear to catastrophe. In Iddin-Dagan A, Inanna is foremost or most awesome among them, and the great Anuna bow and make supplication while she determines judgments with Enlil. These texts frequently exalt the patron deity or reigning king above the collective.

### 2.3 Akkadian corpus

#### *Atraḫasīs*

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Principal witness:** a three-tablet Old Babylonian recension copied by the scribe Ellēt-Aya in the reign of Ammī-ṣaduqa; British Museum tablet 78943 is dated to approximately 1635 BCE and came from Sippar. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1889-0426-236)

**Role:** The opening distinguishes the governing “great Anunnakū” from the laboring Igīgū. The junior gods dig canals and perform severe work until they rebel and surround Enlil’s dwelling. The assembly’s solution is the creation of humans from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god, often restored as Wê/Ilawē, under the direction of Enki/Ea and the mother goddess Bēlet-ilī/Mami. Humans inherit divine labor.

**Textual problem:** The syntax and group labels in the opening lines are difficult, and translations differ over whether “seven” qualifies the Anunnakū or the laboring Igīgū. The contrast between senior directing gods and burdened junior gods is nevertheless clear. [SEAL composite text](https://seal.huji.ac.il/index.php/node/1515) [Dalley translation](https://omnika.org/assets/translations/66-atrahasis-stephanie-dalley/Atrahasis-Stephanie-Dalley-Translation-Mesopotamia.pdf)

**Limits:** No mining of gold, space travel, Nibiru, DNA, or Homo erectus occurs in the poem. Its concern is irrigation labor, divine noise and fatigue, overpopulation, plague, drought, flood, and mortality.

#### *Enūma eliš*

**Language:** Standard Babylonian Akkadian.  
**Composition:** uncertain, commonly placed in the later second millennium BCE and often associated with Babylon’s theological ascendancy; proposals range around the Kassite and post-Kassite periods. No exact composition date is secure.  
**Manuscripts:** first-millennium copies from Babylonian and Assyrian sites, including Nineveh; later copies continued into Persian and Seleucid times. Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths* is the principal modern critical edition. [Lambert edition](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5325/j.ctv18r6rfr)

**Role in Tablet VI:** After Marduk defeats Tiamat and humanity is created from Kingu’s blood, Marduk allocates stations to the gods. The Anunnaki request a sanctuary in gratitude. They spend a year making bricks and then raise Esagila and Babylon. The text refers to 600 divine stations—300 in heaven and 300 in the earth/Apsû—and subsequently to 300 heavenly Igīgū. Fifty great gods take their seats, while seven destiny gods establish decrees.

**Crucial correction:** The text does **not** say that “fifty Anunnaki came from space.” Fifty great gods participate in the concluding assembly; the Anunnaki collectively build Babylon; 300 + 300 are stationed cosmically. Sitchin combines distinct numbers and scenes into a new narrative. [Tablet VI text](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/225/enuma-elish---the-babylonian-epic-of-creation---fu/)

#### Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgameš*

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Formation:** developed from Old Babylonian and earlier Sumerian Gilgameš traditions; the Standard Babylonian edition is first-millennium in its surviving form. Andrew George’s 2003 edition remains fundamental. [George publication record](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/446250/the-epic-of-gilgamesh-a-new-translation)

Occurrences include:

- **Tablet X:** the Anunnaki and Mammitum establish death and life, while the day of death remains undisclosed.
- **Tablet XI:** during the Flood, the Anunnaki raise torches or lightning that illuminate the land and subsequently weep with Ištar as the gods cower from the storm.
- **Tablet VIII:** the damaged title “judge of the Anunnaki” is applied to Gilgameš, probably anticipating his posthumous underworld office.
- **Tablet XII:** an Akkadian translation/adaptation of the Sumerian *Gilgameš, Enkidu and the Netherworld*, presenting netherworld conditions; its relationship to the eleven-tablet epic is secondary.

The flood passage demonstrates that “Anunnaki” could still be used within a general gathering of gods rather than exclusively for immobile underworld judges. [SOAS, Tablet XI](https://www.soas.ac.uk/baplar/recordings/epic-gilgames-standard-version-tablet-xi-lines-92-139-read-martin-west)

#### *Erra and Išum*

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Date:** probably early first millennium BCE.  
**Role:** The poem distinguishes Igīgū from Anunnakū in several passages; the latter are associated more strongly with the lower cosmic realm. The opposition is clearer here than in many earlier works.

#### Incantations, curses, prayers, and royal inscriptions

**[LC/RV]** The Anunnakū occur in curse formulas as divine enforcers, in incantations and apotropaic texts, and in royal inscriptions as members of the cosmic assembly. Neo-Assyrian inscriptions sometimes conceal the name behind `ᵈGÉŠ.U`. These occurrences generally invoke collective authority rather than an autonomous cult narrative.

### 2.4 God lists

**[LS]** *An = Anum*, the great Babylonian god list, reached a seven-tablet form probably in the Middle Babylonian/Kassite period and contains about two thousand entries. It arranges divine households, ranks, relatives, courts, and explanatory identities. It does not supply a timeless “official list” of all Anunnaki; its classifications are products of learned scribal theology. The new critical edition is W. G. Lambert and Ryan D. Winters, *An = Anum and Related Lists* (2023). [Mohr Siebeck edition](https://www.mohrsiebeck.com/en/book/an-anum-and-related-lists-9783161613838/) [ORACC technical terms](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Technicalterms/index.html)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### 3.1 Domains

No single domain belongs to the collective in all periods.

| Domain or function | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|
| Divine assembly and high gods | **[EA]** Predominant Sumerian usage |
| Determination or ratification of destinies | **[EA]** Recurrent in literary and royal texts |
| Local pantheon of a city | **[EA/RV]** “Anuna of Eridu,” Lagaš, and comparable formulations |
| Receipt of temple food and offerings | **[EA]** Literary descriptions plus sparse Ur III administrative evidence |
| Netherworld judgment | **[EA/RV]** Seven judges in *Inanna’s Descent* |
| Gods of earth/netherworld opposed to heavenly Igīgū | **[LC/LS]** Increasingly common after the Old Babylonian period |
| Construction of Babylon and Esagila | **[LC]** *Enūma eliš* VI |
| Governance over laboring Igīgū | **[EA/LC]** *Atraḫasīs* |
| Cosmic agents in the Flood | **[LC]** Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš* XI |
| Extraterrestrial colonization and gold mining | **[MI]** Sitchin, 1976 onward |

### 3.2 Form and appearance

**[EA/LC]** The collective has no single described body. Individual constituent gods are anthropomorphic in literature and art, but the Anuna as a group are not described as sharing a uniform height, complexion, anatomy, clothing, or species.

### 3.3 Iconography

**[MSR; negative finding]** No surviving ancient image can securely be identified as “the Anunnaki” as a collective. There is no standard Anunnaki emblem, sacred animal, weapon, color, crown, number, or planetary symbol. Images circulated online under that name commonly show:

- individual gods;
- protective winged genies from Neo-Assyrian palaces;
- the bird-headed apkallu;
- a god in a horned crown;
- cylinder-seal scenes whose participants are unnamed;
- modern fantasy art.

Horned crowns signify divinity broadly, not membership in an Anunnaki species. Winged Assyrian genies are not labeled Anunnaki, and the bucket-and-cone ritual implements do not depict genetic manipulation. ORACC states explicitly that no identified depiction of the collective is known. [ORACC Anunna](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/Anunna/index.html)

### 3.4 Numbers

Numbers are contextual, not a stable census:

- **Seven:** the Anuna judges in *Inanna’s Descent*. **[EA/RV]**
- **Fifty:** the Anuna of Eridu in one tradition; fifty great gods in *Enūma eliš*. **[EA/RV; LC]**
- **Six hundred:** 300 heavenly and 300 terrestrial stations in *Enūma eliš* VI. **[LC/LS]**
- **Unspecified multitude:** the usual condition.

The separate appearances of seven, fifty, and six hundred show that numerical groupings served narrative, local, and theological purposes.

### 3.5 Celestial body and Nibiru

**[MI]** No celestial body is the permanent planet of the Anunnaki. Akkadian *nēberu/nibiru* means a “crossing” or “crossing point” and can designate different astronomical objects in different contexts, prominently Jupiter in some learned texts. *Enūma eliš* assigns Marduk an astral Nēberu identity; it does not make Nibiru the Anunnaki’s home world.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### 4.1 Collective genealogy

**[EA/RV]** An is frequently their father or progenitor. *The Debate between Grain and Sheep* explicitly says An engendered the Anuna. Other texts call them siblings or imply common membership without giving literal parentage.

Because “Anuna” can mean the great gods generally, individual members possess incompatible genealogies. It is therefore misleading to draw a single family tree in which every major Mesopotamian god is biologically descended from An in the same manner.

### 4.2 Hierarchical position

- **Early Sumerian:** eminent gods, divine assembly, or leading gods of a locality. **[EA]**
- **Old Babylonian *Atraḫasīs*:** senior divine administrators over against working Igīgū. **[EA/RV]**
- **Later Babylonian cosmology:** often terrestrial or netherworld gods contrasted with heavenly Igīgū. **[LC/LS]**
- ***Enūma eliš*:** subjects and builders in Marduk’s newly ordered cosmos, though still “great gods.” **[LC]**
- **Neo-Assyrian theology:** retained as an authoritative class while Aššur could appropriate Marduk’s cosmic supremacy. **[LC/SI]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 Birth of the gods and provisioning of the Anuna

**[EA]** In *The Debate between Grain and Sheep*, An begets the Anuna before the necessary forms of civilized subsistence exist. Grain and Sheep are created because the gods need food and clothing. Their products are first consumed by the gods and only later made available to humanity.

The myth’s structure makes the Anuna dependent recipients of the ordered world. It provides no account of technologically advanced beings importing agriculture from another planet.

### 5.2 Divine labor and human creation: Sumerian version

**[EA/RV]** *Enki and Ninmaḫ* opens after the Anuna’s birth. Senior gods supervise while lesser gods perform labor and complain. Namma wakes Enki, who devises humanity from clay associated with the Abzu, with divine birth goddesses assisting. Ninmaḫ subsequently fashions a series of impaired persons, and Enki assigns each a social livelihood. Enki then produces a being Ninmaḫ cannot sustain, reversing the contest.

This is not merely a cosmogony: it concerns labor allocation, dependence, disability, social place, and the powers and limits of divine makers. It differs substantially from *Atraḫasīs* and should not be silently merged with it.

### 5.3 Divine labor and human creation: *Atraḫasīs*

**[EA]** In *Atraḫasīs*, the working Igīgū dig waterways for 3,600 years, burn their tools, and confront Enlil. The great gods investigate the grievance rather than annihilate the rebels. Ea proposes creating humanity to carry the work. A god is killed, and Bēlet-ilī mixes clay with divine flesh and blood; birth goddesses produce seven males and seven females.

The poem then moves through population growth, noise, divine attempts at plague and famine, Ea’s repeated assistance to Atraḫasīs, and the Flood. Afterward, mortality and social restrictions on reproduction prevent renewed overpopulation.

**Variant discipline:** Sumerian *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, Akkadian *Atraḫasīs*, and *Enūma eliš* all make humans assume divine work, but differ in creators, sacrificial victim, motivation, procedure, and theology. The genetic-engineering synthesis is **[MI]**.

### 5.4 The seven judges and Inanna’s death

**[EA/RV]** Inanna prepares for the netherworld, instructs Ninšubur to seek help if she does not return, and passes through seven gates, losing an item of regalia at each. Naked and deprived of office, she faces Ereškigal. After attempting to take the throne, seven Anuna judges condemn her. Their deathly gaze, angry speech, and guilty cry turn her into a corpse, which is hung on a hook.

Enki later fashions rescuers who obtain Inanna’s corpse. The food and water of life revive her, but the netherworld demands a substitute. After refusing to surrender mourners loyal to her, Inanna selects Dumuzi, who was seated in splendor. Subsequent traditions vary over Dumuzi’s capture, escape, substitution, and seasonal sharing of the netherworld with Geštinanna.

The seven judges are central to this particular narrative but cannot be projected backward or outward as the only Anuna.

### 5.5 Marduk, Babylon, and the building Anunnaki

**[LC]** In *Enūma eliš*, Marduk defeats Tiamat, constructs the cosmos from her body, receives supreme names, and arranges divine stations. Humanity is created from Kingu’s blood to take over divine service. The Anunnaki then ask to build a resting place for Marduk. At his direction they mold bricks for a year, construct Babylon, raise Esagila, and establish their own shrines. The gods feast and affirm Marduk’s sovereignty.

This is political theology: Babylon’s cult center becomes the divinely built cosmic capital. The story does not preserve a neutral pan-Mesopotamian creation account; it elevates Marduk and Babylon using older divine motifs.

The item-specific expression “the fifty who built Babylon” requires correction. *Enūma eliš* distinguishes:

1. the Anunnaki who perform construction;
2. fifty great gods seated in assembly;
3. seven destiny gods;
4. 300 heavenly and 300 terrestrial stations.

They overlap conceptually but are not a textually explicit party of fifty extraterrestrial builders.

### 5.6 The Flood

**[LC]** In *Gilgameš* XI, Uta-napišti tells Gilgameš how the gods sent the Flood. During the storm, Adad thunders, other storm gods devastate the land, and the Anunnaki lift torches whose glare sets the country ablaze. The gods themselves become terrified and withdraw upward. Ištar laments, and the Anunnaki weep with her. Afterward Enlil is angered that a human survived; Ea rebukes indiscriminate destruction.

The Anunnaki are participants in a divided divine assembly, not uniform villains. Their weeping also conflicts with modern portrayals of them as a single, coordinated ruling species.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### 6.1 Sanctuaries

**[MSR; negative finding]** No temple can presently be securely identified as a sanctuary dedicated exclusively to “the Anuna” or “the Anunnaki.” Individual gods encompassed by the designation possessed major temples—Enlil’s Ekur at Nippur, Enki’s E-abzu at Eridu, Inanna’s Eanna at Uruk, Ningirsu’s Eninnu at Girsu, Marduk’s Esagila at Babylon—but these were not generic Anunnaki temples.

### 6.2 Offerings

**[EA]** Sumerian literary texts repeatedly say the Anuna eat in temples or receive divine provisions. A few Ur III administrative records apparently document offerings directly designated for the Anuna. These furnish limited evidence of collective ritual recognition.

The evidence does not yet establish:

- a specialized Anuna priesthood;
- an independent annual Anuna festival;
- an oracle institution;
- a pilgrimage center;
- a permanent temple treasury;
- a standard sacrificial calendar.

### 6.3 Priests and ritual officials

**[MSR; absence finding]** No office securely equivalent to “priest of the Anunnaki” is documented as a major enduring institution. Temple personnel served particular gods and temples. Scribes, lamentation priests, diviners, exorcists, singers, brewers, and offering personnel could invoke divine collectives, but that does not make them Anunnaki clergy.

### 6.4 Sacrifice and food offerings

**[EA/LC]** Mesopotamian temple cult regularly supplied gods with grain, bread, beer, livestock, incense, garments, and precious materials. Literary passages about the Anuna consuming food fit this institutional world.

**Human sacrifice:** No evidence establishes a normal cult of human sacrifice to the Anunnaki. The killing of a god in *Atraḫasīs* or Kingu in *Enūma eliš* is a mythic divine execution, not archaeological evidence for human victims offered to an Anunnaki cult.

**Temple prostitution:** No source connects the Anunnaki collectively to a documented institution of “sacred prostitution.” Older scholarship often treated Mesopotamian sexual and temple vocabulary too freely; those debates concern particular goddesses, personnel, and texts, not an Anunnaki institution.

### 6.5 Magic and curses

**[LC]** Akkadian incantations and curse clauses invoke the Anunnakū as powerful divine witnesses or enforcers. Their collective authority makes them useful in formulas intended to bind, condemn, or protect. Invocation is evidence of ritual language, not necessarily a separate cult.

### 6.6 Political economy

The group’s political significance lies chiefly in legitimation:

- assemblies of Anuna approve divine and royal destinies;
- royal hymns place a king under their collective blessing;
- Gudea’s temple program is staged before their admiring assembly;
- *Enūma eliš* has them construct Babylon and ratify Marduk’s supremacy;
- imperial inscriptions invoke their authority in curses.

Thus the Anuna functioned as a divine analogue to an assembly or court whose consensus validated hierarchy.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late third millennium BCE: emergence in the surviving record

**[EA]** The name is securely attested in the post-Akkadian/Gudea period and Ur III records. It designates great gods, frequently in relation to Lagaš, Eridu, Nippur, temples, assemblies, and the determination of destinies. Evidence for collective offerings exists but is meager.

### Early second millennium BCE: literary expansion

**[EA]** Old Babylonian scribal schools copied major Sumerian compositions in which the Anuna were born from An, consumed offerings, inhabited shrines, deliberated, and judged Inanna. In Akkadian *Atraḫasīs*, the governing Anunnakū were contrasted with laboring Igīgū.

This is not a simple transition from “sky gods” to “underworld gods.” Multiple meanings coexisted.

### Later second millennium BCE: systematization and Babylonian theology

**[LC/LS]** Learned god lists such as *An = Anum* organized thousands of divine names and relationships. *Enūma eliš* placed the Anunnaki within Marduk’s new cosmic hierarchy and made them builders of Babylon and Esagila. The collective’s identity became increasingly available for cosmic classifications.

### First millennium BCE: heaven/underworld contrast

**[LC/LS]** Later Babylonian and Assyrian texts more often contrast heavenly Igīgū with terrestrial or netherworld Anunnakū, though not consistently. *Erra and Išum*, incantations, royal inscriptions, and the Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš* preserve several meanings.

Neo-Assyrian scholars copied Babylonian compositions at Nineveh. Ashurbanipal’s library, excavated in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, contained more than 30,000 tablets and fragments, including copies central to modern reconstruction. [British Museum library project](https://www.britishmuseum.org/research/projects/what-was-ashurbanipals-library)

### Persian, Hellenistic, and Seleucid periods

**[LC/LS]** Akkadian scholarly culture continued to copy and interpret ancient texts. The name remained attested into the Seleucid period. Late Babylonian theologians reorganized pantheons and equated gods in increasingly elaborate ways, but there is no evidence of a newly dominant independent Anunnaki cult.

### Late antiquity through the early modern period

**[MSR; absence finding]** As cuneiform literacy disappeared, the technical term ceased to function as a continuously transmitted cult name. Greek-language reports about Babylonian religion, most importantly the fragments attributed to Berossus, preserved some Mesopotamian cosmogony but did not transmit the modern Anunnaki narrative.

No demonstrable medieval occult lineage carried detailed Anunnaki worship from ancient Mesopotamia into Europe, the Islamic world, or modern ceremonial magic. Claims of such continuity are **[MI]** unless supported by specific manuscripts.

### Nineteenth-century rediscovery

Excavation and decipherment restored the name to modern knowledge. Tablets from Nineveh, Babylon, Nippur, Sippar, and southern Mesopotamia allowed scholars to reconstruct *Gilgameš*, *Enūma eliš*, lexical lists, hymns, and rituals. George Smith’s nineteenth-century publications of the Babylonian creation and flood texts made them internationally famous.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship

Falkenstein’s 1965 study distinguished flexible Sumerian usage from later Akkadian classification. Kienast studied the Anunnakū–Igīgū terminology. Modern corpora—ETCSL, CDLI, ORACC, RINAP, ePSD, and SEAL—make transliterations, translations, images, and lexical data searchable, while new fragments continue to refine readings.

### Present-day religious reception

**[MI/modern revival]** Some contemporary Mesopotamian reconstructionists honor individual Sumerian and Akkadian gods and may use “Anuna” collectively in prayer. These are modern revivals informed by scholarly texts, not demonstrably continuous cults.

New Age, occult, UFO-religious, and conspiracy communities use “Anunnaki” far more often, usually in Sitchin-derived meanings. Their beliefs are historically real modern religious phenomena, but their extraterrestrial narrative is not an ancient attestation.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### 8.1 Precursors

Nineteenth-century diffusionism and later “lost civilization” writing created a market for claims that advanced outsiders founded ancient cultures. Charles Fort’s anomalistic collections and Erich von Däniken’s *Chariots of the Gods?* (1968) helped popularize ancient-astronaut explanation before Anunnaki became its principal Mesopotamian name. Academic analysis places ancient-astronaut discourse between purported alternative science and modern esotericism. [Richter, “Ancient Astronaut Narrations”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/FABL.2007.018/html)

### 8.2 Zecharia Sitchin

**[MI]** Zecharia Sitchin’s *The 12th Planet* (New York: Stein and Day, 1976) supplied the foundational modern synthesis. He claimed that:

- Nibiru is an undiscovered planet on an approximately 3,600-year orbit;
- the Anunnaki were humanoid inhabitants of it;
- they arrived on Earth hundreds of thousands of years ago;
- they required gold, sometimes explained as material for Nibiru’s atmosphere;
- they established bases in Mesopotamia or Africa;
- they genetically engineered humanity as a labor force;
- biblical Nephilim preserved the same beings;
- Mesopotamian gods, biblical narratives, astronomy, and monumental architecture recorded literal extraterrestrial history.

These claims constitute a documented modern mythology. They are “source-driven” only in the sense that Sitchin reinterpreted ancient texts; they are not translations accepted by Assyriology.

Specific philological failures include:

- parsing *Anunnaki* as “those who from heaven to earth came”;
- treating Nibiru as one permanent trans-Neptunian planet;
- conflating distinct numbers of gods;
- interpreting divine labor myths as records of gold mines;
- presenting cylinder-seal symbols as a heliocentric solar system with twelve planets;
- equating Anunnaki with Nephilim without linguistic evidence.

Sitchin’s own book is the primary evidence for the modern claims. [1976 book record](https://www.divinityatlas.com/sources/the-12th-planet) [digital scan](https://d2wuvg8krwnvon.cloudfront.net/media/user_space/3d2185217e7f/ebook/ebook_1659044139_6065.pdf)

### 8.3 Spread of the modern narrative

**[MI]** Sitchin expanded the narrative through the *Earth Chronicles*. Later authors, UFO media, websites, radio programs, social platforms, and television detached “Anunnaki” from the uncertainties of cuneiform scholarship. Recurrent additions include:

- reptilian Anunnaki;
- competing Enki and Enlil alien factions;
- royal bloodlines controlling contemporary governments;
- suppressed archaeological artifacts;
- secret bases, pyramids, and lunar installations;
- imminent return of Nibiru;
- channeling and past-life memories;
- racialized claims that ancient peoples could not build cities without aliens.

These elements do not all originate with Sitchin, but his 1976 synthesis supplied their central vocabulary. The Nibiru-cataclysm prediction was further popularized through Nancy Lieder’s Internet claims beginning in the 1990s, despite differing from Sitchin on important details.

### 8.4 Occult and neopagan use

**[MI/modern revival]** Modern ritual manuals and online communities sometimes construct Anunnaki invocations by combining translated Mesopotamian hymns, ceremonial magic, planetary correspondences, and Sitchin-derived cosmology. No ancient tablet supplies a generalized “Anunnaki summoning ritual.” Authentic-looking cuneiform, reconstructed Sumerian names, and invocations of individual gods do not by themselves demonstrate ancient continuity.

There is no evidence that Renaissance Hermetists, the Theosophical Society, or the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn possessed a tradition specifically devoted to the Anunnaki. Claims of an unbroken secret order are **[MI]**.

### 8.5 Literature, film, television, comics, and games

Modern entertainment commonly uses “Anunnaki” as a compact signifier for ancient aliens, vanished high technology, or predatory creator gods. Works sometimes influenced directly or indirectly by Sitchin include ancient-alien television, conspiracy fiction, comics, role-playing settings, and games. *Stargate* (1994) uses an alien explanation for Egyptian gods rather than a faithful Anunnaki tradition; *Cowboys & Aliens* (2011) drew upon the gold-mining-alien motif. Such works materially reinforce the modern image while remaining fiction.

### 8.6 Political and extremist appropriation

**[MI; documented modern use, heterogeneous]** Anunnaki narratives appear in conspiracy ecosystems involving secret elites, “bloodlines,” New World Order claims, antisemitic tropes, apocalyptic survivalism, and racial pseudohistory. Not every believer accepts those additions, and “Anunnaki belief” is not inherently one political ideology. Where coded claims about a hidden nonhuman cabal reproduce older antisemitic narratives, the relationship must be evaluated from the speaker’s actual language rather than assumed.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### 9.1 Major works

- Adam Falkenstein, “Die Anunna in der sumerischen Überlieferung” (1965): foundational study of Sumerian attestations, local groups, numbers, hierarchy, and functions.
- Burkart Kienast, “Igigū und Anunnakkū nach den akkadischen Quellen” (1965): principal study of the Akkadian classification.
- Dietz-Otto Edzard, “Anunna/Anunnakū,” *Reallexikon der Assyriologie*: concise philological synthesis.
- Thorkild Jacobsen, *The Treasures of Darkness* (1976): historically influential interpretation of Mesopotamian religion; valuable but not a substitute for current editions.
- Jeremy Black and Anthony Green, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia* (1992): iconographic and religious reference work.
- Andrew R. George, *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic* (2003): critical edition and translation.
- W. G. Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths* (2013): critical edition of *Enūma eliš* and related creation literature.
- W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard, *Atra-ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood* (1969; reprints and revisions): foundational edition.
- Lambert and Ryan D. Winters, *An = Anum and Related Lists* (2023): current critical edition of the major god list.

### 9.2 Central debates

#### Meaning of the name

“Those of princely seed” is the leading interpretation, but the compound is not beyond dispute. Sitchin’s “from heaven to earth came” is not a competing academic etymology.

#### Membership

There is no recoverable universal roster. Attempts to name exactly seven, fifty, or several hundred Anunnaki generally promote one textual configuration into a timeless canon.

#### Anunnaki versus Igigi

The most defensible conclusion is historical variability:

- early Sumerian Anuna: eminent gods and local pantheons;
- *Atraḫasīs*: directing Anunnakū versus laboring Igīgū;
- some later systems: earth/netherworld Anunnakū versus heavenly Igīgū;
- other texts: partially synonymous or overlapping collectives.

#### Underworld identity

The seven judges in *Inanna’s Descent* are secure early evidence, but “the Anunnaki were always and only underworld gods” is too strong. Conversely, denying all underworld association ignores both that composition and later Akkadian classifications.

#### Dating *Enūma eliš*

Proposed dates range within the later second millennium BCE. Association with Nebuchadnezzar I and Marduk’s post-Kassite ascendancy is influential but not conclusively proven. The date of the surviving tablets, many from the first millennium, must not be confused with the composition date.

#### Myth and ritual

The claim that *Enūma eliš* was recited at Babylon’s Akītu festival rests on ritual texts and later associations, but the precise history and regularity of that performance remain debated. The Anunnaki’s construction of Babylon is literary theology even if embedded in real festival ideology.

### 9.3 State of the evidence

The record is incomplete because:

- tablets are broken;
- compositions survive in multiple school copies;
- readings change when fragments join;
- findspots were sometimes poorly recorded in early excavation;
- numerous tablets remain unpublished;
- “composite texts” reconstruct a work from witnesses separated by place and century;
- many cult practices were never written down or have not survived.

Major holdings include the British Museum’s Nineveh and Babylonian collections, the Louvre’s Gudea cylinders, the Vorderasiatisches Museum collections, Istanbul collections, the Iraq Museum, Yale Babylonian Collection, University of Pennsylvania Museum, and many smaller tablet collections. CDLI supplies object records and images; ORACC provides annotated corpora; ETCSL provides composite Sumerian editions.

### 9.4 Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- Anunnaki means “those who came from heaven to Earth.” **[MI]**
- The `KI` in “Anunnaki” proves “Earth.” **[MI]**
- The Anunnaki were a uniform alien species. **[MI]**
- All Mesopotamian gods were Anunnaki in every period. **Unsupported harmonization.**
- Seven Anunnaki is the universal roster. **Contradicted by variable counts.**
- Fifty Anunnaki landed in spacecraft. **[MI], constructed from unrelated passages.**
- Assyrian winged genies are photographic portraits of Anunnaki. **[MI]**
- Bucket-and-cone reliefs portray genetic engineering. **[MI]**
- *Atraḫasīs* describes gold mines. **[MI]**
- Nibiru was always a planet beyond Neptune. **[MI]**
- The Anunnaki are linguistically identical with the Nephilim. **[MI]**
- Archaeologists conceal the relevant tablets. **Unsupported:** the primary corpora, transliterations, photographs, and critical disputes are extensively published.

## 10. Dedicated contested-material findings

### Origin and etymology

The collective is Mesopotamian and securely attested in cuneiform by the Gudea/Ur III period. “Princely offspring” is a scholarly reconstruction; “from heaven to Earth came” is modern invention.

### Syncretism

The name itself was transferred from Sumerian into Akkadian religious vocabulary, but its semantic range changed. Later scholarly systems placed it opposite the Igīgū or within Marduk-centered cosmology. There is no ancient syncretistic equation “Anunnaki = Nephilim = fallen angels.”

### Human creation

Ancient sources do connect divine labor, a slain god, clay, blood, and humanity. They do not contain genetics, DNA laboratories, gold-mining colonialism, or Homo erectus. The modern account is a literalizing technological reinterpretation of mythic ingredients.

### Human sacrifice

No established Anunnaki cult of human sacrifice is documented. Divine killing within creation myth is not evidence of historical human ritual.

### Sexual institutions

No collective Anunnaki institution of temple prostitution is documented. Modern claims typically generalize controversial interpretations of particular Mesopotamian goddess cults.

### Suppression

The ancient cultic system declined through long political, linguistic, and religious transformations under Persian, Hellenistic, Parthian, Roman, Christian, and later Islamic conditions. No source records a targeted state campaign to suppress an independent “Anunnaki religion.”

### Occult continuity

There is no documented medieval-to-modern initiatory transmission. Modern occult practice is revival, reconstruction, or invention.

### Extraterrestrials

The extraterrestrial system begins in recognizable form with Sitchin’s 1976 publication. Earlier ancient-astronaut literature provided a genre, but the specific Anunnaki–Nibiru–gold–genetic-engineering synthesis is Sitchin’s.

### Documentary conclusion

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not the story of a single stable group. It is the history of a name repeatedly repurposed within Mesopotamian religious language:

1. eminent Sumerian gods and local divine assemblies;
2. authorities who assign fates and receive temple sustenance;
3. seven judges in one netherworld myth;
4. senior gods contrasted with laboring Igīgū in *Atraḫasīs*;
5. earthly or underworld gods contrasted with heavenly Igīgū in some later classifications;
6. builders and subjects within Marduk’s Babylonian cosmic state;
7. divine witnesses, judges, and literary actors through first-millennium scholarship;
8. after cuneiform’s decipherment, raw material for a modern extraterrestrial mythology beginning decisively in 1976.

The variation is not corruption of an original fixed doctrine. It is the documented history of the Anuna/Anunnakū.
