# Gilgamesh (deified): Research Dossier

**Class:** deified king, heroic god, ancestral royal spirit, and underworld judge. In literary narrative he is also a mortal or partly divine king of Uruk. These classifications coexist and should not be forced into a single chronological biography.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the divine name **Pabilgames/Bilgames**, written with the divine determinative, occurs in an Early Dynastic IIIa god list from Šuruppak/Fara, approximately the twenty-sixth century BCE. This is earlier than the surviving narratives and probably earlier than any securely datable historical reference to a king Gilgamesh. The evidence therefore does **not** establish the often-repeated sequence “historical king first, posthumously deified later.” It establishes that Gilgamesh was already classified as a god in the earliest surviving record bearing his name.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Early Dynastic through Ur III evidence, approximately 2600–2004 BCE.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** chiefly Old Babylonian through first-millennium scholarly or literary tradition.
- **[RV] Regional or manuscript variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** god lists, lexical texts, commentaries, scholarly catalogues, or formal theology.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[LA] Late antique, medieval, or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or popular tradition whose transmission cannot be demonstrated continuously.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or reception.**
- **[U] Unestablished or disputed.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Gilgamesh occupies several overlapping evidentiary identities:

1. **A god in the earliest god lists** and a recipient of offerings.
2. **A legendary king of Uruk** placed in the First Dynasty of Uruk by the Sumerian King List.
3. **A heroic protagonist** in five principal Sumerian compositions and in the evolving Akkadian epic.
4. **A dead king installed as an underworld authority**, judge, or governor.
5. **An exemplary ancestor of kingship**, invoked by rulers who called themselves his “brother.”
6. **A modern literary and cultural figure**, reconstructed after the nineteenth-century decipherment of cuneiform.

The proposition that he was a historical Early Dynastic king remains plausible but unproved. No contemporary royal inscription securely attributable to him is known, and the King List manuscripts are many centuries later than the reign they assign him.

### Names and scripts

- **Pabilgames**, conventionally reconstructed as *Pabilgameš* or *Pabilgames*: probably the oldest form. The Early Dynastic spelling has been analyzed as a personal name meaning approximately “the ancestor/elder was a young man” or “the forebear was a prince.” The exact segmentation and the value of the final element remain disputed. **[EA/U]**
- **Bilgames / Bilgameš**, Sumerological normalization of the common Sumerian literary form: **𒀭𒄑𒂆𒈦**, conventionally transliterated *dBil-ga-meš* or *dBil₂-ga-meš*. The superscript *d* represents the divine determinative *DINGIR*. **[EA/LC]**
- **Gilgameš**, Akkadian form, eventually shown by syllabic spellings and the Greek *Gilgamos* to begin with /g/, not /b/. This is the basis of English “Gilgamesh.” **[LC]**
- Logographic writings include **dGIŠ.BIL.GA.MEŠ**, **dGIŠ.GÍN.MAŠ**, and numerous learned or abbreviated spellings. ORACC’s ePSD2 presently indexes twenty-four spellings and attestations from Early Dynastic IIIa through the Old Babylonian period. The sign sequence was historically difficult to read; early Assyriologists used forms such as **Izdubar**, **Gizdubar**, and **Gishdubar**. **[LS/MSR]**
- Greek **Γίλγαμος**, *Gilgamos*, appears in Aelian’s *De natura animalium* 12.21, written in the Roman imperial period, probably around the early third century CE. **[LA]**

The attractive translation “the ancestor is a young man” is a current philological proposal, not an ancient gloss. Older translations connecting the name with fire, axes, or “the fire-god” resulted from obsolete sign readings or speculative etymology. Comparisons with Kurdish *gelgameş*, Greek names, or Turkic words are modern folk etymologies without demonstrated historical sound correspondences. **[MI/U]**

### Principal titles and descriptions

- **“Lord of Kulaba”** (*en Kulaba*) in Sumerian poems. Kulaba was the old sacred district of Uruk. **[LC, drawing on an older Uruk tradition]**
- **King/lord of Uruk**, especially “Uruk-the-Sheepfold” in the Babylonian epic. **[LC]**
- **“Wild bull,” “mighty man,” “man of battle,” “perfect in strength,”** and similar heroic descriptions in the Sumerian poems and Babylonian epic. These are literary epithets rather than evidence of a separate bull cult. **[LC]**
- **Two-thirds god and one-third human**, in Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh* I. This mathematically unusual genealogy is a canonical literary claim, not a doctrine demonstrably present in the Early Dynastic cult. **[LC]**
- **Son of Ninsun**, regularly in literary tradition; **son of Lugalbanda** in later genealogy. **[LC]**
- **“Judge” or “governor of the netherworld.”** The Sumerian *Death of Gilgamesh* gives him a postmortem judicial position; later Akkadian texts call him, among other formulations, *šāpir qaqqari/erṣeti*, “governor of the earth/netherworld.” **[EA?/LC]**
- **A divine king or heroic ancestor** in god lists, incantations, funerary texts, and royal ideology. **[EA–LC]**

No securely attested body of evidence assigns him a planet, zodiacal sign, weekday, canonical color, or single sacred animal. Modern lists that give such correspondences are usually occult or neopagan constructions. **[MI]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

The earliest secure occurrence is in the **Fara/Šuruppak god list**, Early Dynastic IIIa, approximately 2600 BCE. The name bears the divine determinative and belongs to a list of gods. It does not narrate deeds, identify parents, call him king of Uruk, or explain whether worshippers imagined an earlier human ruler. **[EA]**

Further Early Dynastic and later lexical evidence confirms that the divine name was not an isolated scribal accident. ePSD2 records Early Dynastic IIIa and IIIb instances, followed by Lagash II, Ur III, and especially Old Babylonian attestations.

A frequently cited archaic personal name or formula translated “Gilgamesh is the one whom Utu chose” has been used to argue for a near-contemporary memory of the king. Its reading, date, and relationship to the literary Gilgamesh require caution. It is not the equivalent of a surviving royal inscription by Gilgamesh. **[EA/U]**

### Sumerian King List

The Sumerian King List, in its Old Babylonian and related recensions, places Gilgamesh in the First Dynasty of Uruk after Dumuzi the fisherman and before Ur-Nungal. Depending on the manuscript, Gilgamesh is described as:

- a king of Uruk;
- a ruler for 126 years;
- son of a **lillû**, a term variously understood as a spirit, phantom, or supernatural being;
- associated genealogically with Lugalbanda in later tradition.

The 126-year reign is legendary numerology, not usable as a literal chronology. The list’s early dynasties combine political theology, city succession, mythic rulers, and retrospective historiography. It may preserve names of real rulers, but it was repeatedly edited to present kingship as a single institution transferred among cities. **[LC/LS]**

### The five principal Sumerian Gilgamesh compositions

The extant manuscripts are overwhelmingly Old Babylonian school tablets, approximately nineteenth–eighteenth centuries BCE, although the compositions or constituent traditions may be older.

1. **Gilgamesh and Akka**  
   Gilgamesh rejects the demand of Akka of Kish after consulting first Uruk’s elders and then its able-bodied men. Uruk resists; Gilgamesh’s appearance and intervention secure victory or submission. The poem is political and heroic, not primarily theological. It may preserve memories of Early Dynastic rivalry, but it is not contemporary reportage. **[LC/MSR]**

2. **Gilgamesh and Huwawa, Versions A and B**  
   Gilgamesh seeks lasting fame in the Cedar Mountain. Enkidu warns him to consult Utu, who grants helpers or winds. Gilgamesh overcomes Huwawa/Humbaba, sometimes through deceptive promises, and kills him despite pleas for mercy. Version A and Version B differ materially in sequence and detail. Enlil later distributes Huwawa’s terrifying auras. **[LC/RV]**

3. **Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven**  
   Inana conflicts with Gilgamesh and receives the Bull from An. The Bull devastates Uruk; Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill it. In one preserved passage Gilgamesh throws a haunch at Inana and threatens her. The conclusion nevertheless praises Inana, indicating that the poem cannot be reduced to simple anti-Inana polemic. **[LC/RV]**

4. **Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld**  
   The poem begins with a cosmogonic prologue and Inana’s ḫalub tree. Gilgamesh drives hostile beings from it and makes furniture or implements for Inana; later his *pukku* and *mēkku* fall into the netherworld. Enkidu descends, violates Gilgamesh’s instructions, and is detained. Gilgamesh appeals first to Enlil, then Suen, then Enki. Enki orders Utu to open a fissure so Enkidu’s shade can rise and report the conditions of the dead. A translation of the latter portion became Standard Babylonian Tablet XII, although it conflicts with Enkidu’s death in Tablets VII–VIII. **[LC/RV]**

5. **The Death of Gilgamesh**  
   Gilgamesh lies dying. A divine assembly reviews his achievements and the exceptional favor once granted to the flood survivor Ziusudra, but mortality cannot be revoked for Gilgamesh. He is promised high status in the netherworld, where he will judge the dead. The Me-Turan recension describes or evokes a monumental river-bed burial and offerings to netherworld powers. The poem survives in divergent Nippur and Me-Turan witnesses and is textually damaged. **[EA?/LC/RV]**

Some scholars have proposed a Ur III date for the composition of *The Death of Gilgamesh* or for significant portions of its tradition; its surviving tablets are later. Composition date and manuscript date must therefore remain separate.

### The Babylonian Epic

#### Old Babylonian phase

Old Babylonian fragments, approximately eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE, show that a connected Akkadian epic already existed. The Pennsylvania and Yale tablets preserve the encounter with Enkidu, the civilizing role of the prostitute Šamkatum, and preparations for the Cedar Forest expedition. The Sippar tablet preserves Siduri’s advice and Gilgamesh’s search for Uta-napišti. The opening incipit was probably **“Surpassing all other kings”** (*šūtur eli šarrī*).

This version was not merely a translation of the five Sumerian poems. It reorganized inherited episodes into a sustained reflection on kingship, companionship, death, fame, and human limitation. **[LC]**

#### Peripheral and translated traditions

Fragments or adaptations were copied outside Babylonia:

- Akkadian tablets at **Boğazköy/Hattusa**, **Ugarit**, **Emar**, **Megiddo**, and elsewhere.
- A **Hittite-language adaptation** and Hurrian fragments at Hattusa.
- Manuscripts differ in wording and arrangement, showing that the epic remained fluid through much of the second millennium. **[RV]**

#### Standard Babylonian version

The eleven-tablet core beginning **“He who saw the deep”** (*ša naqba īmuru*) attained a comparatively fixed form in the late second millennium BCE. Mesopotamian scholarly catalogues attribute the series to **Sîn-lēqi-unninnī**, probably a scholar of the late second millennium, but “author” in the modern sense is too strong: he or his tradition likely reshaped and stabilized older material.

The fullest manuscripts come from the seventh-century BCE Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. They are late copies of an older composition, not the date of the epic’s first creation.

The eleven-tablet sequence is:

1. Gilgamesh’s oppressive rule; creation of Enkidu; Enkidu’s socialization.
2. Gilgamesh and Enkidu wrestle and become companions.
3. Preparations for the Cedar Forest.
4. Journey and dreams.
5. Defeat and killing of Humbaba.
6. Ištar’s proposal; Gilgamesh’s rejection; Bull of Heaven killed.
7. Divine decision that Enkidu must die.
8. Lament and funeral for Enkidu.
9. Gilgamesh’s journey in fear of death.
10. Siduri, Ur-šanabi, and the Waters of Death.
11. Uta-napišti’s flood account; failed sleep test; plant of rejuvenation lost to a snake; return to Uruk.

**Tablet XII** is an Akkadian translation/adaptation of the latter part of *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld*. Its Enkidu is alive enough to descend bodily, so it is an erudite appendix, not a seamless continuation of Tablet XI. **[LS/LC]**

### Other primary texts

- **Early Dynastic, Ur III, and Old Babylonian god lists:** classify Gilgamesh among gods. **[EA/LS]**
- **An = Anum**, especially Tablet VI: places Gilgamesh and Enkidu among underworld-related divine figures in a learned first-millennium recension whose materials are older. **[LS]**
- **The Death of Ur-Namma:** the dead Ur-Namma presents gifts to netherworld gods, including Gilgamesh, confirming Gilgamesh’s established status below. **[EA/LC]**
- **Incantations and ritual texts:** invoke Gilgamesh among chthonic powers, sometimes with Ningishzida, Dumuzi, Namtar, or other underworld beings. **[LC]**
- **Royal inscriptions and court literature:** later kings invoke heroic kinship with Gilgamesh and Lugalbanda. This is ideological ancestry, not biological documentation. **[LC]**
- **Aelian, *De natura animalium* 12.21:** King Seuechoros of Babylon is warned that his daughter’s child will depose him. He confines her; she bears Gilgamos, guards throw the infant from a tower, an eagle catches him, and a gardener raises him. Gilgamos becomes king. The story resembles the Greek Acrisius–Danaë–Perseus pattern and is unattested in cuneiform Gilgamesh literature. It may derive from a late Babylonian popular tradition, Hellenistic adaptation, or conflation. **[LA/U]**

Neither *Enūma eliš*, *Atrahasis*, *Inana’s Descent*, nor *Enki and Ninmah* makes Gilgamesh a principal actor. They remain essential comparative sources for cosmology, flood tradition, and the underworld, but their contents must not be silently added to his dossier.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

#### Underworld judgment

Gilgamesh’s clearest divine office is chthonic. *The Death of Gilgamesh* promises him a position among the great underworld authorities, hearing or pronouncing cases of the dead. Later god lists, incantations, and epithets continue this role. He is not consistently the supreme ruler of the underworld: that position belongs to Ereškigal, and later often to Nergal beside her. Gilgamesh is better described as a royal judge, governor, or senior official within that realm. **[EA?/LC]**

#### Royal ancestry and dead kings

Offerings to Gilgamesh and his appearance in funerary contexts suggest a powerful ancestral or royal-dead function. The Ur III state’s strong interest in Uruk’s legendary kings made Gilgamesh useful as a predecessor and legitimator. **[EA/MSR]**

#### Heroic strength, civic kingship, and fame

In literature he protects Uruk, defeats wilderness monsters, builds or possesses its wall, excavates wells, establishes fame, and ultimately learns the limits of mortal kingship. These are narrative domains, not necessarily cult offices. **[LC]**

#### Protection and magic

Later incantations can invoke him against ghosts or as an authority capable of controlling beings of the netherworld. This is consistent with, but not identical to, his literary heroism. **[LC]**

### Physical form

The Babylonian epic presents a gigantic, exceptionally beautiful and powerful human king. Standard Babylonian Tablet I describes his dimensions and makes him two-thirds divine. The Sumerian poems call him black-bearded, well-proportioned, bull-like, and splendid. None of this establishes literal bovine anatomy. **[LC]**

### Iconography: what is secure and what is not

There is **no universally secure ancient image bearing an inscription that identifies the depicted figure as Gilgamesh**.

A frontal, nude, bearded hero with six curls, often grappling a lion or bull, and a bull-man grappling animals appear frequently in Early Dynastic, Akkadian, Ur III, and later glyptic art. Older scholarship routinely labeled them “Gilgamesh and Enkidu.” Modern iconographic study is more cautious:

- the motifs predate surviving Gilgamesh narratives;
- the figures occur in scenes without narrative markers;
- the bull-man is more securely identifiable as a generic *kusarikku* or related hybrid;
- the nude hero may be a conventional heroic or protective figure rather than a named individual;
- some later artists or viewers may nevertheless have associated such types with Gilgamesh and Enkidu.

Accordingly, museum labels such as “possibly Gilgamesh” are appropriate; categorical identification is not. **[MSR/U]**

The monumental Assyrian figure from Sargon II’s palace at Khorsabad, a hero holding a lion, is popularly called Gilgamesh but is uninscribed and cannot securely be named. **[MI/MSR]**

### Symbols and associated objects

Textually grounded objects include:

- the walls of Uruk;
- the axe and sword used against Humbaba or the Bull;
- the Cedar Forest and felled cedar;
- Humbaba’s severed head;
- the Bull of Heaven’s horns, made into oil vessels in the Sumerian poem;
- the *pukku* and *mēkku*, whose exact forms remain disputed;
- the plant of rejuvenation;
- Enkidu’s funerary statue and grave goods;
- the lapis-lazuli and carnelian objects offered in funerary scenes.

The lion, bull, cedar, snake, and scorpion-beings are important narrative or iconographic associates, but no evidence makes all of them “sacred animals of Gilgamesh” in a formal cultic sense.

No ancient evidence securely assigns Gilgamesh a canonical color, metal, number, day, zodiac sign, or planet. **[U/MI]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical traditions

#### Ninsun

Ninsun, “Lady Wild Cow,” is consistently Gilgamesh’s mother in the mature literary tradition. In the Babylonian epic she interprets his dreams, adopts Enkidu, and petitions Šamaš for protection. **[LC]**

#### Lugalbanda

Lugalbanda, himself a legendary king of Uruk and deified hero, is Gilgamesh’s father in the dominant later tradition. The Standard Babylonian epic calls Gilgamesh the offspring of “Wild Cow Ninsun” and associates him with Lugalbanda; other texts treat Lugalbanda and Ninsun as a divine couple. **[LC/LS]**

#### The *lillû* tradition

Some Sumerian King List manuscripts call Gilgamesh’s father a *lillû*, possibly a phantom, spirit, or supernatural being. Whether this reflects a damaged name, an older independent genealogy, or a description of Lugalbanda is disputed. It must not be harmonized automatically with the Lugalbanda genealogy. **[RV/U]**

#### Siblings

The Sumerian *Bull of Heaven* composition mentions **Peštur/Pectur**, Gilgamesh’s “little sister.” She is sparsely attested and not prominent in later epic tradition. **[LC/RV]**

No secure ancient tradition gives Gilgamesh a canonical wife or divine children. *The Death of Gilgamesh* can enumerate wife, child, concubine, musicians, and household as part of the royal funerary sphere, but the damaged text does not provide a stable divine genealogy. **[U]**

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

Dates below refer to the approximate composition or surviving textual phase, not to mythical time.

- **An/Anu — adversarial consequence and divine judgment.** An gives Inana/Ištar the Bull of Heaven; after its death, Anu participates in the council deciding that Enkidu must die. Sumerian *Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven*, Old Babylonian manuscripts; Standard Babylonian Tablets VI–VII, late second-millennium form. **[LC/RV]**
- **Aruru/Bēlet-ilī — creator of his counterpart.** At Anu’s command Aruru creates Enkidu to check Gilgamesh. Standard Babylonian Tablet I, with Old Babylonian antecedents. **[LC]**
- **Aya — indirect joint ritual action.** Ninsun appeals to Šamaš and his wife Aya concerning Gilgamesh’s expedition. Gilgamesh does not conduct a substantial independent encounter with Aya. Standard Babylonian Tablet III. **[LC]**
- **Bull of Heaven/Gugalanna — combat and killing.** Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill the divine bull released against Uruk. Sumerian *Bull of Heaven*; Standard Babylonian Tablet VI. **[LC/RV]**
- **Dumuzi — joint underworld/funerary setting.** Both receive or are associated with funerary offerings in chthonic texts; direct dialogue is not securely attested. *Death of Gilgamesh*, ritual and incantation traditions. **[EA?/LC]**
- **Ea/Enki — rescue and counsel, usually indirect.** In *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld*, Enki responds to Gilgamesh’s appeal and orders Utu to release Enkidu’s shade. In the flood story Ea is Uta-napišti’s covert savior, whose action makes Gilgamesh’s later quest possible, but he does not meet Gilgamesh directly there. Old Babylonian Sumerian witnesses; Standard Babylonian Tablets XI–XII. **[LC]**
- **Enkidu — companion, opponent, adopted brother, fellow divine-list figure.** They wrestle, become companions, kill Humbaba and the Bull, and are paired in god lists. Enkidu’s death transforms Gilgamesh. Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian epic; Sumerian poems; *An = Anum*. **[LC/LS]**
- **Enlil — divine authority, adversary in consequence, and underworld addressee.** Gilgamesh appeals unsuccessfully to Enlil for Enkidu’s release; Enlil had appointed Humbaba and later condemns the heroes’ killing or redistributes Humbaba’s auras. He participates in the decision that one hero must die. Sumerian *Huwawa* and *Netherworld* compositions; Standard Babylonian Tablets V and VII. **[LC/RV]**
- **Ereškigal — hierarchical underworld relation.** Gilgamesh’s postmortem judgeship operates in the realm ruled by Ereškigal. Direct dialogue is not preserved securely. *Death of Gilgamesh*, god lists, and chthonic texts. **[EA?/LC/LS]**
- **Humbaba/Huwawa — deceptive negotiation and killing.** Gilgamesh overcomes the divinely appointed guardian of the Cedar Forest, offers gifts or marriages in Sumerian versions, and kills him with Enkidu. Sumerian Versions A and B; Babylonian Tablets IV–V. Humbaba’s precise classification varies between monstrous guardian and demonized divine being. **[LC/RV]**
- **Inana/Ištar — service, conflict, rejection, and violence.** In the *ḫalub*-tree episode Gilgamesh clears Inana’s tree and makes furniture for her. In the Bull story they are antagonists. In the Babylonian epic he rejects Ištar’s sexual or marital proposal by cataloguing the fates of her former lovers; she retaliates with the Bull. Sumerian *Netherworld* and *Bull* compositions; Standard Babylonian Tablet VI. **[LC/RV]**
- **Lugalbanda — paternal and ideological relation.** Later tradition makes the deified Lugalbanda Gilgamesh’s father; no substantial narrative meeting survives. God lists and Babylonian epic genealogy. **[LC/LS]**
- **Namtar — underworld authority named in relation to Enkidu.** The Sumerian *Netherworld* poem explicitly says Namtar did not seize Enkidu; he is detained by the netherworld itself. Gilgamesh’s direct interaction with Namtar is not preserved. **[LC]**
- **Nergal — underworld relation.** The *Netherworld* poem says Nergal’s *udug* did not seize Enkidu. Later chthonic theology places Gilgamesh within Nergal’s underworld administration; direct narrative conversation is absent. **[LC/LS]**
- **Ningishzida — joint funerary/chthonic context.** Gilgamesh offers to or is listed among underworld powers including Ningishzida; no independent narrative encounter is securely preserved. *Death of Gilgamesh* and incantation traditions. **[EA?/LC]**
- **Ninsun — mother, interpreter, intercessor, and adopter of Enkidu.** She interprets Gilgamesh’s dreams, petitions Šamaš, and ritually takes Enkidu as her son. Old Babylonian fragments and Standard Babylonian Tablets I–III. **[LC]**
- **Suen/Sîn — unsuccessful appeal.** Gilgamesh appeals to Suen to release Enkidu from the netherworld; Suen does not grant the request. Sumerian *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld*. **[LC]**
- **Šamaš/Utu — patron, counselor, and rescuer.** Utu authorizes or assists the Cedar expedition; Šamaš sends winds against Humbaba, receives Ninsun’s appeal, counsels the heroes, and later argues in Enkidu’s favor. Utu also opens the underworld fissure at Enki’s command. Sumerian *Huwawa* and *Netherworld* poems; Babylonian Tablets III–VII and XII. **[LC]**
- **Uta-napišti/Ziusudra — meeting with a divinized immortal.** Gilgamesh reaches the remote flood survivor, hears the flood story, fails a sleep test, and learns of the rejuvenating plant. Uta-napišti is an immortal human admitted to a godlike mode of existence, not consistently a normal pantheon god. Old Babylonian antecedents; Standard Babylonian Tablets X–XI. **[LC]**
- **Bēlet-ṣēri — underworld scribe in the Tablet XII tradition.** She is associated with the accounting of the dead, but a direct dialogue with Gilgamesh is not clearly preserved. **[LC/LS]**

Gods appearing only inside Uta-napišti’s flood narrative—Adad, Ninurta, Ennugi, Bēlet-ilī, and others—do not thereby have a direct interaction with Gilgamesh. That absence matters.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The king checked by Enkidu

**Earliest connected evidence:** Old Babylonian Akkadian epic, eighteenth century BCE. **[LC]**

Gilgamesh exercises royal power oppressively. The precise abuses are partly obscure, but the people complain to the gods. Aruru creates Enkidu from clay as an equal force. Enkidu initially lives with animals until Šamkatum’s sexual and social instruction separates him from the herd and introduces him to food, clothing, and human society.

Enkidu travels to Uruk and blocks Gilgamesh. They wrestle; neither destroys the other, and companionship replaces rivalry. Ninsun subsequently adopts Enkidu.

The text is often described as a movement from nature to culture, but that is a modern analytical model. The poem itself stresses altered knowledge, bodily transformation, social incorporation, and friendship. Psychoanalytic claims that Enkidu is Gilgamesh’s “id,” “shadow,” or repressed sexuality are modern readings. **[MSR/MI]**

### The Cedar Forest and Humbaba

The independent Sumerian poems and the Babylonian epic preserve substantially different tellings.

In the Sumerian tradition Gilgamesh seeks a name because human life ends. Enkidu directs him to Utu; divine assistance makes the journey possible. Gilgamesh uses stratagems and promises to deprive Huwawa of his protective auras. Huwawa is killed, and Enlil responds angrily or redistributes the auras.

In the Babylonian epic, Gilgamesh initially frames the expedition as heroic fame-making. Enkidu, who knows the wilderness, is afraid. Gilgamesh dreams repeatedly; Enkidu interprets the dreams favorably. Šamaš sends winds that immobilize Humbaba. Humbaba pleads for life and curses Enkidu after Enkidu urges execution. The heroes cut cedars and fashion an enormous door for Enlil.

The episode can be read in relation to Mesopotamian royal timber expeditions and ideological conquest of distant mountain resources. That historical function is plausible, but the Cedar Forest should not be identified confidently with one modern forest or country. Lebanon, the Amanus, and Iranian mountain regions have all been proposed. **[MSR/U]**

### Ištar and the Bull of Heaven

The Sumerian poem begins amid fragmentary circumstances and portrays a pre-existing conflict over judgment or authority in Inana’s precinct. Inana obtains the Bull from An; it devastates Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill it, and Gilgamesh hurls a haunch at Inana.

The Standard Babylonian epic supplies a new immediate cause: after the Cedar victory Ištar proposes to Gilgamesh. He rejects her and enumerates former lovers whom she harmed. She demands the Bull from Anu and threatens to raise the dead if refused. The Bull opens pits that kill men, but the heroes kill it. Enkidu throws a portion at Ištar.

The divine council then determines that one of the two heroes must die. Anu and Enlil favor punishment; Šamaš protests, but Enkidu is doomed.

The Sumerian and Babylonian accounts are not merely duplicate versions. The marriage proposal and catalogue of lovers are defining elements of the later epic’s characterization of Ištar and Gilgamesh. **[LC/RV]**

### Enkidu’s death and Gilgamesh’s transformation

Enkidu dreams of the divine council and then of the underworld. He curses the cedar door, the trapper, and Šamkatum, then modifies his curse of Šamkatum after Šamaš reminds him of what he gained through her. He dies after prolonged illness.

Gilgamesh’s lament recalls Enkidu through animals, landscapes, artisans, citizens, and family. He commissions a funerary image and provides grave goods. Fear that he too will die drives him into the wilderness.

This is the emotional center of the Standard Babylonian epic. The independent Sumerian *Netherworld* poem instead has Enkidu detained below while still functioning as Gilgamesh’s servant; Standard Tablet XII preserves that incompatible tradition as an appendix. **[LC/RV]**

### Quest for immortality

Gilgamesh crosses the mountains of Māšu past scorpion-beings, travels through darkness, and reaches a jeweled garden. Siduri initially bars her door. The Old Babylonian Siduri passage may have included advice to accept ordinary human pleasures, though the popular “eat, drink, and be merry” formulation often blends separate reconstructions and later parallels.

Ur-šanabi ferries Gilgamesh across the Waters of Death to Uta-napišti. Uta-napišti recounts the flood: Ea covertly warns him; he builds a vessel, preserves life, survives the storm, releases birds, sacrifices, and is granted remote immortal existence with his wife. Gilgamesh fails to stay awake and thereby fails a test more modest than immortality.

At Uta-napišti’s wife’s prompting, he learns of a prickly plant that renews youth. He retrieves it but delays using it. A snake steals it while he bathes and sheds its skin. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk and directs attention to its walls.

The flood narrative draws heavily on the older Akkadian *Atrahasis* tradition, while the Sumerian Ziusudra story represents a related but distinct stream. Direct dependence, shared tablets, and scribal reworking are more defensible than a single timeless “Mesopotamian flood myth.” **[LC/MSR]**

### Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld

The poem combines at least two narrative movements:

1. Inana rescues a ḫalub tree and plants it in Uruk. A snake, the Anzud bird, and a female wilderness being occupy it. Gilgamesh drives them away and cuts the tree, furnishing Inana.
2. Gilgamesh’s *pukku* and *mēkku* fall below. Enkidu volunteers to retrieve them but ignores instructions governing contact with the dead. The netherworld holds him. Enki and Utu enable his shade to emerge, and Gilgamesh questions him about the conditions of different dead persons.

The poem supplies unusually detailed Mesopotamian reflections on burial, descendants, funerary offerings, and the fate of neglected corpses. It does not present a simple moral judgment system comparable to later heaven-and-hell schemes. **[LC]**

### Death and enthronement below

In *The Death of Gilgamesh*, the hero’s supernatural ancestry and achievements cannot annul mortality. The gods recall that only the flood survivor received exceptional life. Gilgamesh is compensated with a high underworld office.

The Me-Turan recension appears to describe diversion of the Euphrates and construction of a stone tomb in its bed, after which the river returns. Because the text is broken, claims of a literal archaeological “tomb of Gilgamesh” under the Euphrates are unjustified.

The poem’s references to wives, children, retainers, and palace personnel have been compared with mass burials in the Royal Cemetery at Ur. It remains disputed whether the narrative describes living attendants killed for burial, already dead members of the household represented through offerings, or literary idealization. The Royal Cemetery supplies independent evidence that some Early Dynastic royal burials included numerous attendants, but it does not identify any grave as Gilgamesh’s. **[EA/LC/U]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### God-list and offering evidence

Gilgamesh’s cultic reality is clearest in administrative and lexical evidence:

- he occurs in the Early Dynastic Fara god list;
- Ur III records document offerings to him;
- Gudea-era and Ur III material associates him with funerary observance and the royal dead;
- Old Babylonian god lists retain him;
- *An = Anum* embeds him in systematic underworld theology.

This is direct evidence that he was treated as a deity, not merely admired as a fictional hero.

### Uruk, Ur, Nippur, Umma, and Puzriš-Dagan

Uruk is his mythic and political home, but surviving evidence does not establish a monumental, independently excavated “Temple of Gilgamesh” comparable to Eanna or Ekur.

Ur III administrative archives from Ur, Umma, Girsu, Nippur, and Puzriš-Dagan record distributions to many deities and royal ancestors, including Gilgamesh. The evidence often consists of livestock, grain, beer, flour, oil, or prepared foods assigned in offering schedules. It shows institutional provision but rarely reveals the spoken liturgy or personal theology of worshippers.

### Festivals

A festival connected with Gilgamesh is attested in the Sumerian calendrical tradition, commonly associated with the month later called **Abu**, when torches, funerary observances, and contests or wrestling have sometimes been connected with his cult. The sources and local calendars vary. Claims that a single pan-Mesopotamian “Gilgamesh festival” existed unchanged are excessive. **[EA/LC/U]**

### Funerary and magical rites

Gilgamesh received *kispum*-like offerings to the dead and appears in underworld ritual contexts. His authority could be invoked in incantations concerning ghosts and the dead. This fits his judicial office: the deceased king had become a power capable of regulating other dead beings.

No surviving ritual manual provides a complete initiation, priestly theology, or daily cult sequence devoted solely to Gilgamesh.

### Priests and personnel

Offices associated with the cult of deified rulers—including *gudu₄* priests and *nin-dingir* women—are relevant comparative evidence, but a securely reconstructable hereditary “priesthood of Gilgamesh” is not presently documented. Claims of such an institution should therefore be marked inferred or unestablished.

### Sacrifice and alleged human sacrifice

Animal and food offerings are securely documented. Human sacrifice specifically “to Gilgamesh” is not.

The mass graves at Ur and the funerary household in *The Death of Gilgamesh* have prompted comparison with retainer sacrifice. Archaeology indicates that attendants in some Royal Cemetery burials died in connection with elite interment, though the exact mechanisms remain debated. No inscription identifies those rites with Gilgamesh, and no evidence supports a recurring public human-sacrifice cult dedicated to him. **[EA/U]**

“Temple prostitution” is not documented for Gilgamesh’s cult. Šamkatum’s role in the epic has often been translated “sacred prostitute,” but her exact social category is disputed, and the narrative does not make her a priestess of Gilgamesh. Older claims of a universal Mesopotamian institution of sacred prostitution have been substantially criticized. **[MSR/U]**

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### c. 2600–2350 BCE: divine name and possible heroic ancestor

Gilgamesh appears as a deity in Early Dynastic god-list evidence. A historical ruler may stand behind the tradition, perhaps in Early Dynastic Uruk, but this cannot be recovered as an ordinary biography. **[EA/MSR]**

### c. 2350–2112 BCE: heroic and royal memory

Sparse evidence suggests continued divine recognition. The legendary kings of Uruk—Enmerkar, Lugalbanda, and Gilgamesh—became an increasingly important heroic sequence. **[EA/U]**

### 2112–2004 BCE: Ur III intensification

The kings of Ur promoted divine kingship and cultivated connections with Uruk. Gilgamesh and Lugalbanda were useful royal ancestors; offerings and literary activity placed Gilgamesh within state cult and funerary ideology. Some scholars date formative stages of the Sumerian poems to this environment, although the surviving copies are Old Babylonian. **[EA/MSR]**

### c. 2000–1600 BCE: Old Babylonian literary expansion

Sumerian Gilgamesh poems became scribal-school classics. An Akkadian connected epic reorganized selected traditions. God lists and rituals maintained the divine Gilgamesh, while the epic foregrounded a king forced to confront mortality. The divine cult figure and the mortal literary protagonist were not mutually exclusive. **[LC]**

### c. 1600–1000 BCE: international transmission and standardization

The epic traveled to Syria-Palestine and Anatolia and was adapted into Hittite and Hurrian. The Standard Babylonian version took shape, traditionally associated with Sîn-lēqi-unninnī. Gilgamesh became a canonical exemplar of ancient kingship and human mortality. **[LC/RV]**

### c. 1000–539 BCE: first-millennium scholarly canon

Assyrian and Babylonian libraries copied the epic, god lists, incantations, and lexical texts. Ashurbanipal’s library preserved the fullest surviving witnesses. Royal and scholarly ideology continued to remember Gilgamesh as a primordial king and underworld power. **[LC/LS]**

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Cuneiform scholarship continued into the Seleucid and Parthian periods, but evidence for an active popular cult of Gilgamesh becomes increasingly thin. Aelian’s *Gilgamos* birth story shows that a transformed name and royal legend survived into Greek-language learned tradition. It does not demonstrate continuous knowledge of the twelve-tablet epic. **[LA/U]**

### Late antiquity through the early nineteenth century

No secure continuous medieval transmission of the epic is known in Jewish, Christian, Islamic, Persian, or Arabic literature. Proposed identifications with **Nimrod**, **Heracles**, **Samson**, **Alexander/Dhu al-Qarnayn**, or figures in the *Arabian Nights* are comparisons or hypotheses, not demonstrated identity.

The name and story effectively disappeared from readable cultural memory because cuneiform ceased to be understood. Local communities continued to live among Mesopotamian ruins, but no documented chain connects medieval folklore directly to the cuneiform Gilgamesh tradition. **[U/F]**

### 1849–1872: archaeological recovery

Excavations at Nineveh recovered tablets from Ashurbanipal’s library. The texts were initially unread or fragmentary. Gilgamesh was first called Izdubar because of uncertain sign values.

In 1872 George Smith identified and translated the flood narrative on British Museum tablet **K.3375**, now known as Tablet XI. He presented his “Chaldean Account of the Deluge” to the Society of Biblical Archaeology on 3 December 1872. The discovery transformed biblical and comparative scholarship because the cuneiform flood story was demonstrably ancient.

The popular story that Smith removed all his clothes in excitement depends on later anecdotal reporting and is not a secure contemporary record. **[F/U]**

### 1870s–present: reconstruction without restored continuity

Successive discoveries recovered Sumerian poems, Old Babylonian fragments, peripheral versions, and additional Standard Babylonian pieces. The ancient cult itself did not survive continuously. Present-day devotional practice is revivalist or reconstructive and depends on modern editions.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and world literature

George Smith initially framed the flood tablet in biblical terms. Early reception accordingly concentrated on Genesis. By the twentieth century the work was increasingly read as autonomous Mesopotamian literature.

Important literary milestones include:

- Rainer Maria Rilke’s enthusiastic reading of the epic;
- Hans Henny Jahnn’s dramatic and musical engagement;
- Guido Bachmann’s *Gilgamesch* (1966), influential in queer reception;
- numerous poems, novels, plays, children’s books, operas, visual works, and dance productions;
- Theodore Ziolkowski’s documentation of the epic’s modern reception across Europe, North America, and elsewhere.

Modern portrayals of Gilgamesh and Enkidu as explicitly romantic or sexual partners are interpretive developments. The ancient texts portray intense love, grief, physical intimacy, dream symbolism, and kinship language but do not unambiguously narrate a sexual relationship between them. Both queer and nonsexual readings are modern hermeneutic positions. **[MSR/MI]**

### Psychology and comparative mythology

Frazerian, Freudian, Jungian, structuralist, existentialist, ecological, and queer readings have each remade the epic:

- Enkidu as “natural man” or Gilgamesh’s double;
- Humbaba as repressed shadow;
- the forest expedition as ecological destruction;
- the quest as individuation;
- the friendship as same-sex love;
- the return to Uruk as political maturation.

These may be productive criticism but are not recovered Babylonian theology. **[MI]**

### Occultism and neopaganism

Gilgamesh has no major documented place in Renaissance Hermetism, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or the foundational Theosophical canon comparable to Egyptian, Greek, Hebrew, or Indian figures. Claims of an ancient Western esoteric “Gilgamesh current” are therefore modern constructions.

Contemporary Mesopotamian reconstructionists and eclectic pagans sometimes honor him:

- as a heroic ancestor;
- as judge of the dead;
- in *kispum*-inspired ancestor rites;
- together with Enkidu;
- as a patron of strength, grief, kingship, or queer devotion.

Online rituals invoking Gilgamesh for curses, sexuality, “shadow work,” planetary magic, or generalized masculine power are individually created modern practices unless they explicitly reconstruct a documented ancient rite. There is no reliable census demonstrating a large organized Gilgamesh religion. **[MI]**

### National and political appropriation

Modern Iraqi nationalism has used ancient Mesopotamia to articulate territorial depth and national antiquity. Baʿthist cultural policy under Saddam Hussein strongly appropriated Babylonian and Assyrian symbols. Saddam and regime-sponsored literature sometimes invoked Gilgamesh or ancient kingship as analogues of heroic national leadership.

This was political reception, not a restoration of his ancient cult. Gilgamesh has also served non-Baʿthist Iraqi, Assyrian, Kurdish, and diaspora cultural identities; exclusive ethnic ownership claims exceed what the multilingual ancient transmission supports. **[MI]**

No substantial, well-documented extremist cult of Gilgamesh comparable to modern appropriations of certain European pagan symbols has been established.

### Popular culture and commerce

The name now appears in novels, comics, manga, anime, television, music, role-playing games, and video games. Particularly influential modern reinventions include the superhuman golden king in the Japanese *Fate* franchise and multiple fantasy-game characters. Such portrayals often detach him from the underworld judge and the mortality lesson, emphasizing treasure, arrogance, or magical weapon collections. **[MI]**

Commercial images frequently reproduce an Assyrian lion-holding hero or generic cylinder-seal contest scene as a “portrait of Gilgamesh.” That identification remains unproved. **[MI/MSR]**

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was there a historical Gilgamesh?

The cautious position is:

- a historical king behind the tradition is plausible;
- his supposed reign is conventionally placed in Early Dynastic II or III, roughly 2800–2500 BCE;
- no contemporary inscription securely establishes his acts, dates, parentage, or reign;
- the King List is too late and ideological to serve as a straightforward chronicle;
- the earliest secure written evidence presents the name as divine.

The statement “Gilgamesh was certainly a historical king later deified” is stronger than the evidence permits. **[MSR/U]**

### God first or king first?

Because the earliest attestation is a god list, scholars must consider several models:

1. a historical king was deified before the earliest surviving record;
2. a pre-existing god or heroic ancestor was historicized as a king;
3. divine and royal traditions developed together;
4. chance of preservation has erased earlier royal evidence.

The archive cannot presently choose decisively among them. **[MSR/U]**

### Name and etymology

Older readings moved from Izdubar/Gizdubar to Gilgamesh as syllabic evidence accumulated. Modern work reconstructs an early form *Pabilgames*, but the precise meaning remains debated. “The ancestor was a young man/prince” is plausible, not certain.

### Sîn-lēqi-unninnī and authorship

The first-millennium *Catalogue of Texts and Authors* associates the Gilgamesh series with Sîn-lēqi-unninnī. Andrew George argues that this scholar gave the poem its final fixed form. Others stress that attribution may indicate editorship, lineage, authority, or authorship in a Mesopotamian sense rather than sole composition. **[LS/MSR]**

### Dating the Sumerian poems

Their extant manuscripts are Old Babylonian. Linguistic, ideological, and intertextual arguments sometimes place composition or prototypes in Ur III. Exact dates remain disputed. A manuscript’s date must not automatically be called the poem’s date, but neither may a hypothetical oral predecessor be treated as documented text.

### The epic and the Bible

The flood account is related to *Atrahasis* and ultimately to older Mesopotamian flood traditions. Genesis shares significant motifs with Mesopotamian accounts, but the exact route of transmission and degree of direct borrowing remain debated.

Early pan-Babylonian claims by Peter Jensen and others that nearly the whole Hebrew Bible, Jesus tradition, or world mythology derived from Gilgamesh are discredited. Specific literary comparison is not the same as universal derivation. **[MSR]**

### Greek parallels

Parallels have been proposed between Gilgamesh and Heracles, Achilles, Odysseus, and Alexander. Some Near Eastern-to-Greek transmission is historically plausible, but motif resemblance alone cannot establish a direct borrowing. The Heracles equation is not an ancient interpretatio graeca securely attested for Gilgamesh.

### Iconographic dispute

Henri Frankfort and earlier scholars frequently used the labels Gilgamesh and Enkidu for nude-hero and bull-man contest figures. Later iconographic scholarship emphasizes that these are conventional types without name labels. The prudent catalogue term is “nude hero,” “bull-man,” or “possibly Gilgamesh,” depending on context.

### Human sacrifice and royal burial

*The Death of Gilgamesh* and the Royal Cemetery at Ur have been linked, sometimes dramatically. The comparison is important but cannot prove that the poem records Gilgamesh’s burial or that his cult practiced human sacrifice. Archaeological and literary datasets must remain separate.

### Sexuality and Šamkatum

Older translations called Šamkatum a temple prostitute or sacred harlot. More recent scholarship distinguishes her literary function from uncertain institutional categories and questions the broad reconstruction of Mesopotamian sacred prostitution. Likewise, the intimacy between Gilgamesh and Enkidu supports serious queer interpretation but does not settle their ancient sexual classification.

### State of the evidence

Principal holdings include:

- British Museum tablets from Nineveh, especially K.3375;
- Vorderasiatisches Museum/Berlin collections;
- Iraq Museum tablets;
- Yale Babylonian Collection;
- University of Pennsylvania Museum;
- Louvre and other European collections;
- Hattusa materials in Turkish and German collections;
- tablets catalogued through CDLI, ORACC, and museum databases.

The corpus remains incomplete. Tablets were broken in antiquity, lost through excavation history, dispersed across collections, or looted. New joins and newly published fragments continue to change readings.

The so-called “Gilgamesh Dream Tablet,” an Old Babylonian fragment acquired through the modern antiquities market and associated with Hobby Lobby’s Museum of the Bible, was forfeited by the United States and returned to Iraq in 2021. Its history illustrates how illicit trade destroys archaeological context even when the inscribed text survives.

## 11. Sources

### Primary corpora and editions

The indispensable primary-text foundation comprises:

- ETCSL editions of *The Death of Gilgamesh*, *Gilgamesh and Huwawa*, *Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven*, and *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld*.
- Andrew R. George, *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic: Introduction, Critical Edition and Cuneiform Texts*.
- Cavigneaux and al-Rawi, *Gilgameš et la Mort*.
- ORACC/ePSD2 lexical and name attestations.
- CDLI tablet records and images.
- Sumerian King List manuscripts.
- Old Babylonian, peripheral, and Standard Babylonian epic tablets.
- *An = Anum* and related god lists.
- *The Death of Ur-Namma* and funerary administrative records.
- Aelian, *De natura animalium* 12.21.

### Principal modern scholarship

Particularly important are Andrew George’s philological edition and studies; Thorkild Jacobsen on Mesopotamian religion and death; Jeremy Black, Anthony Green, and ORACC/AMGG for cautious divine taxonomy; Dina Katz on the Mesopotamian underworld and funerary tradition; W. G. Lambert on Babylonian mythology; Henri Frankfort and later revisions of his iconographic identifications; Theodore Ziolkowski on modern reception; and the *Reallexikon der Assyriologie* articles on Gilgameš, god lists, the underworld, and related deities.

### Contested-material findings

- **Etymology:** disputed; *Pabilgames* is preferable to confident popular translations.
- **Historical king:** plausible, not demonstrated by contemporary royal inscriptions.
- **Deification sequence:** unproved; earliest secure evidence already treats him as divine.
- **Syncretism:** no secure ancient equation with Heracles, Nimrod, Samson, or Alexander.
- **Human sacrifice:** possible comparative relevance of royal retainer burial, but no documented recurring sacrifice to Gilgamesh.
- **Temple prostitution:** not established for his cult; Šamkatum’s classification remains debated.
- **Suppression:** no documented campaign specifically suppressing a Gilgamesh cult; institutional disappearance accompanied the broader end of Mesopotamian temple and cuneiform culture.
- **Occult tradition:** no demonstrated Renaissance-to-Golden-Dawn Gilgamesh lineage.
- **Neopaganism:** small, decentralized, modern reconstruction rather than continuous worship.
- **Extremist appropriation:** no substantial documented Gilgamesh-centered extremist movement.
- **Iconic “portrait”:** the lion-holding hero and seal contest figures are not securely named.
- **Modern magical correspondences:** planets, colors, tarot cards, chakras, and similar systems are modern inventions unless explicitly marked as such.
