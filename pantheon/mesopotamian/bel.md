# Bel: Research Dossier

**Class:** male god when *Bel* is used as a divine name; originally and continuously also an Akkadian title, *bēlu* “lord/master,” applicable to gods and humans. It is not the name of one immutable deity. Historically its principal divine referents were **Enlil of Nippur**, then especially **Marduk of Babylon**, while **Bel of Palmyra** was a locally developed Syrian god whose identity combined indigenous Bol/Bel, Babylonian, West Semitic, and Greco-Roman elements.

**Earliest secure attestation:** this depends on what is being dated. The underlying Sumerian god **Enlil**, later called *Bēl*, is securely attested in Early Dynastic god lists and cultic sources of the mid-third millennium BCE, including the Fara tradition. The Akkadian common noun/title *bēlum*, “lord,” belongs to the early Semitic written record, but an unqualified divine **Bēl** functioning consistently as Marduk’s proper or role-name is principally a first-millennium BCE phenomenon. Marduk himself is attested by the later third millennium BCE. Palmyrene **Bel** is epigraphically secure by 44 BCE; the monumental temple later dedicated to Bel, Yarhibol, and Aglibol was consecrated on 6 April 32 CE.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary or near-contemporary inscription, tablet, archaeological object, or early manuscript.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a literary or theological form that became authoritative but is later than the earliest cult.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** local configuration not automatically transferable elsewhere.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** god lists, theological equations, commentaries, or priestly reinterpretation.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** an explicitly historical equation between originally distinguishable gods.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an ancient declaration.
- **[MI] Modern invention or demonstrably modern conflation.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The title and its changing referents

Akkadian **bēlu(m)**, conventionally normalized *bēlu* and written syllabically or logographically with Sumerian **EN**, means “lord,” “owner,” or “master.” Its feminine is *bēltu*, “lady/mistress.” Like English “the Lord,” it may be an ordinary noun, an epithet, or—where context makes the referent obvious—a substitute for a name. The construction *bēl X*, “lord of X,” is common and cannot by itself establish a separate god named Bel. A Neo-Assyrian occurrence may mean Marduk, Enlil, another deity, or a human superior; context decides. A study of first-millennium royal inscriptions finds that **Marduk** remains the broadly usable personal name, whereas **Bēl** is especially favored when Marduk acts as Babylon’s patron deity. [EA/MSR] ([Luukko](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/aebp/downloads/luukko_iraq74_2012.pdf); [Sleeman’s study](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/483ef7a7-e875-4eff-ade9-fbf6931b78e9))

The title’s major historical sequence is therefore:

1. **Enlil/Ellil as “the Lord”**: the sovereign god of Nippur, fate, kingship, and the divine assembly. In Akkadian literary usage he could be *Bēl*, and several titles later associated with “Bel” originated with him. [EA]
2. **Marduk as Bēl**: Babylon’s city god appropriated Enlil’s cosmic kingship, number fifty, and such titles as *bēl mātāti*, “Lord of the Lands.” By the first millennium BCE, unqualified **Bēl** normally means Marduk in Babylonian contexts. [LC/SI]
3. **Aššur as an Enlil-type sovereign**: Assyrian theology calls Aššur “the Enlil of the gods”; this is a transfer of office, not proof that every *Bēl* is Aššur. [LS/SI]
4. **Palmyrene Bel**: a Syrian civic and cosmic god formed from local **Bol** and the prestigious Babylonian name/title Bel, worshipped with Yarhibol and Aglibol. [RV/SI]
5. **Greek Βῆλος (*Bēlos*) and Latin Belus**: Greek and Roman forms used both for Babylonian Bel-Marduk and for Syrian gods interpreted as Zeus/Jupiter. [SI]
6. **Biblical Bel**: Hebrew **בֵּל** (*Bēl*) in Isaiah 46:1 and Jeremiah 50:2; Greek **Βήλ** in *Bel and the Dragon*. In these anti-idolatry texts Bel represents the chief god and cult image of Babylon, conventionally Marduk. [LC]

### Names and writings

- **Akkadian:** *Bēlu*, commonly shortened in modern writing to **Bēl/Bel**; cuneiform **EN**, sometimes with the divine determinative, **ᵈEN**.
- **Sumerian title:** **en**, “lord,” although Sumerian *en* and Akkadian *bēlu* have their own lexical histories and should not be treated as perfectly interchangeable in every context.
- **Enlil:** Sumerian **ᵈEN.LÍL₂**, Akkadian **Ellil/Enlil**; Emesal **Mullil**. The familiar rendering “Lord Wind/Air” is disputed because the value and meaning of *líl* are problematic. Proposed connections with *líl* “ghost/phantom” do not fit his documented character neatly. [MSR]
- **Marduk:** **ᵈAMAR.UTU**, syllabic *Mar-du-uk* and variants; biblical **Merodach**; Greek *Mardochaios/Marōdak* in different traditions. The logogram can be parsed “calf of Utu/the sun,” but whether it preserves the historical etymology is disputed. The alternative “calf of the storm” lacks firm evidence. [EA/MSR] ([ORACC, Marduk](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/marduk/))
- **Palmyrene:** Aramaic **בל**, normally vocalized **Bel**; an earlier or underlying **Bol** is reconstructed from names and early evidence. The change is usually explained as assimilation to Babylonian Bel, but precisely how complete the identity was remains debated. [RV/MSR]
- **Greek:** **Βῆλος**, “Belos”; **Ζεὺς Βῆλος**, “Zeus Belos.”
- **Latin:** **Belus**, **Jupiter Belus**.

### Principal epithets and offices

The following titles are not all simultaneously or universally Bel’s:

- **“Great Lord”** (*bēlu rabû*)—especially Marduk in first-millennium royal and liturgical language. [LC]
- **“Lord of Babylon”** (*bēl Bābili*)—Marduk as civic patron. [EA]
- **“Lord of the Lands”** (*bēl mātāti*)—originally a characteristic Enlil title; transferred explicitly to Marduk in *Enūma eliš* VII. [SI]
- **“King of the gods”**—Enlil in older theology, Marduk in Babylonian imperial theology, Aššur in Assyrian reformulations. [SI]
- **“Creator of humankind”**—applied to Marduk through his identification with Asalluhi/Ea’s magical-creative sphere and in *Enūma eliš*. [LC/SI]
- **“Sage of the gods,” “merciful lord,” “one who revives the dead”**—Marduk’s hymnic and prayer language, prominent in first-millennium penitential traditions. [LC]
- **Palmyrene “Bel, the good and compassionate”**—a civic and devotional characterization in local religious culture; it should not be projected backward onto Enlil. [RV]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Enlil, the earliest principal “Bel”

Enlil appears among the highest gods in Early Dynastic lexical and cultic lists, securely by the mid-third millennium BCE. The Fara and later Nippur traditions place him near An, with Ninlil and the gods of his household. Old Babylonian copies preserve older Sumerian compositions, but the date of a surviving school tablet is not automatically the date of the story it transmits. [EA]

His cult center was **Nippur**, with the **Ekur**, “Mountain House.” Royal inscriptions show rulers rebuilding and provisioning the sanctuary; a brick stamp of Šar-kali-šarri, about 2200 BCE, names him as builder of Enlil’s temple. Excavations beginning in 1889 recovered the temple and ziggurat as well as thousands of literary and administrative tablets. [EA] ([Penn Museum](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/the-thrill-of-discovery/))

### Marduk and the emergence of unqualified Bel

Marduk begins as the local god of Babylon. His name occurs in late-third- and early-second-millennium material, but his rise accelerated under the Old Babylonian dynasty. Hammurabi’s law collection still has Anu and Enlil grant Marduk authority over humanity: Marduk’s supremacy is presented as delegated “Enlilship,” not yet as the disappearance of Enlil. By the first millennium, Marduk was routinely called Bel. [EA/SI]

The surviving evidence is uneven: much of the literature that gives Marduk universal sovereignty survives in first-millennium copies even where composition is assigned to the later second millennium. This manuscript gap is crucial.

### Principal texts

#### Sumerian Enlil corpus

- **Early Dynastic god lists**, including the Fara tradition, mid-third millennium BCE: establish Enlil’s high rank but do not contain a continuous biography. [EA]
- **Old Babylonian Nippur and Weidner god lists**, early second millennium copies: place An/Antum, Enlil/Ninlil, and their household in systematic order. [LS] ([ORACC god-list witnesses](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/Q000263.7))
- **An = Anum**, compiled in its standard form in the Middle Babylonian period, seven tablets: makes Enlil a descendant within a chain headed by Enki/Ninki and lists divine names, spouses, children, courtiers, and equations. This is learned taxonomy, not necessarily popular narrative. [LS] ([ORACC technical summary](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/technicalterms/))
- **“Enlil and Ninlil,”** known chiefly from Old Babylonian manuscripts, probably drawing on earlier tradition: Enlil impregnates Ninlil, is banished, and encounters her in successive disguises, generating Nanna, Nergal, Ninazu, and Enbilulu in the poem’s own scheme. Its sexual politics and genealogy differ from simplified modern family trees. [LC]
- **“Enlil and Sud,”** Old Babylonian copies: narrates Enlil’s courtship of Sud, her identification with Ninlil, and the incorporation of Šuruppag’s goddess into Nippur theology. [SI/LC]
- **“Nanna-Suen’s Journey to Nippur,”** Old Babylonian copies: Nanna brings offerings to his father Enlil and receives blessing. [LC]
- **“Ninurta’s Return to Nippur,” “Ninurta’s Exploits/An-gim/Lugal-e,”** Old Babylonian corpus: present Ninurta as Enlil’s heroic son and agent. [LC]
- **“Inanna’s Descent,”** Old Babylonian manuscripts: Inanna appeals unsuccessfully through Ninšubur to Enlil before Enki supplies a rescue. The text calls Inanna Enlil’s daughter in one tradition. [LC]
- **“Enki and Ninmah,” “Enki and the World Order,” “Song of the Hoe,” “Hymn to the Ekur,” and royal hymns:** construct Enlil as cosmic organizer, giver of kingship, and master of Nippur. [LC]

The ETCSL supplies composite transliterations and translations while visibly marking variants and damaged passages; its catalogue should be used as a gateway to editions, not as though each composite were one ancient tablet. ([ETCSL corpus](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=all))

#### Akkadian flood and heroic literature

- **Atrahasis**, Old Babylonian version around the seventeenth century BCE with later fragments: Enlil orders successive population checks and finally the flood because human noise disturbs the gods; Enki/Ea covertly preserves Atrahasis. [EA/LC]
- **Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh**, its canonical edition conventionally associated with Sîn-lēqi-unninni and preserved chiefly in first-millennium manuscripts: Enlil participates in divine decisions, including the deaths connected with Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven; Utnapishtim recounts the flood conflict in a version related to *Atrahasis*. Andrew George’s critical work distinguishes the Old Babylonian poems from the Standard Babylonian recension. [LC]
- **Anzû**, Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian forms: Anzû steals the Tablet of Destinies from Enlil; Ninurta defeats him and restores divine order. [EA/LC]

These texts concern **Enlil**, whom Akkadian readers could call *Bēl*; translating every “Enlil” as “Bel,” however, would erase the linguistic and historical distinctions.

#### Marduk-Bel corpus

- **Old Babylonian hymns to Marduk**, including compositions for Abī-ešuḫ: document growing royal and liturgical importance but do not yet give the complete first-millennium theology. [EA]
- **Enūma eliš**, seven Akkadian tablets: composition is disputed. Older proposals ranged from the Old Babylonian to Kassite periods; W. G. Lambert argued for the age of Nebuchadnezzar I, late twelfth century BCE, after Marduk’s statue returned from Elam. The earliest extant manuscripts are first-millennium. The poem is less a neutral “creation myth” than a narrative of Marduk’s accession. [LC/MSR]
- **Ludlul bēl nēmeqi**, “I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom,” probably late second millennium BCE, known from first-millennium witnesses: a sufferer abandoned by society and apparently by his god is ultimately restored by Marduk. Here “Lord of Wisdom” is Marduk. [LC]
- **Marduk prayers, incantations, and bilingual hymns:** represent him as healer, exorcist, merciful sovereign, and heir to Asalluhi’s magical competence. [EA/LC]
- **Akītu ritual texts**, chiefly first millennium: prescribe the movement of Marduk’s image, royal humiliation and reinstatement, processions, offerings, and recitation of *Enūma eliš*. The epic’s ritual use is secure; that it was originally composed specifically for this festival is disputed. [EA/MSR]
- **Royal inscriptions:** Assyrian and Babylonian kings employ “Marduk” and “Bēl” selectively. RINAP indexes identify explicit occurrences of **Bēl (Marduk)** in Sennacherib-period material. [EA] ([RINAP 3/2](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/downloads/Grayson_Novotny_RINAP_3_2.pdf))
- **Cyrus Cylinder**, Babylonian, after Cyrus’s conquest in 539 BCE: claims Nabonidus offended the gods, Marduk searched for a just ruler, chose Cyrus, and led him into Babylon. This is Babylonian legitimating theology produced under Persian rule, not an impartial chronicle or a modern human-rights charter. [EA] ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1880-0617-1941))
- **Babylonian astronomical and scholarly texts:** associate Marduk with Jupiter; the equation is secure in first-millennium learned astronomy. [LS/SI]
- **Herodotus, *Histories* 1.181–183**, fifth century BCE: describes the precinct and staged tower of “Zeus Belos,” a top sanctuary with a couch and golden table, and a lower sanctuary with a large golden image. His account mixes recognizable Babylonian institutions with Greek interpretation and claims not corroborated by cuneiform documentation. [SI/external testimony] ([Greek-text tradition and translation](https://lexundria.com/hdt/1.181/mcly))
- **Isaiah 46:1 and Jeremiah 50:2**, exilic or post-exilic Hebrew prophetic traditions: announce Bel’s bowing and Merodach’s dismay or destruction. These are polemical witnesses to Babylon’s chief divine symbols, not Babylonian self-description. [LC]
- **Bel and the Dragon**, probably Hellenistic, surviving in the Old Greek and Theodotionic forms: the king says Bel consumes daily offerings; Daniel scatters ashes, reveals priests and families entering by concealed doors, and destroys the idol and temple. The story then turns to a living dragon and Daniel’s lions’ den. The Old Greek and Theodotion versions differ; the narrative is Jewish anti-idolatry literature, not an archival record of an actual temple exposure. [LC] ([NETS edition](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/41-bel-nets.pdf))

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Enlil-Bel

Enlil governs divine command, destiny, kingship, political legitimacy, wind/storm-like force, agricultural abundance, and destructive flood. He is neither merely an “air god” nor simply a weather god. In royal ideology, possession of Enlil’s favor legitimizes rule over Sumer and Akkad. His command is proverbially unalterable. [EA/LC]

Enlil’s anthropomorphic iconography is difficult to isolate securely because horned divine figures are often unnamed. His strongest emblem is the **horned crown**, sometimes resting on a throne or shrine. That emblem could also represent Anu and the general office of supreme divinity, so identification must depend on inscription and context. [EA]

### Marduk-Bel

Marduk’s accumulated domains include:

- patronage of Babylon;
- divine kingship and decree of destinies;
- creation and cosmic ordering;
- warfare against chaos;
- exorcism, incantation, and healing, largely through Asalluhi’s absorption;
- royal legitimacy;
- irrigation, abundance, and civic protection;
- Jupiter in learned astral theology. [EA/LC/SI]

His primary emblem is the **marru**, generally interpreted as a triangular **spade**, often mounted on a shrine. Whether it primarily signifies agriculture, canal work, temple construction, or a learned pun is not securely established. [MSR]

His animal is the **mušḫuššu**, a composite “fierce snake” or dragon with horned serpent head, scaled body, feline forelegs, birdlike hind feet, and long tail. It had earlier associations with other gods, including Tišpak and Ninazu, before becoming Marduk’s and Nabû’s emblematic beast. Neo-Babylonian glazed-brick examples lined Babylon’s Processional Way and Ishtar Gate. [EA/SI]

A limestone *kudurru* of about 1156–1025 BCE juxtaposes the mušḫuššu and Marduk’s spade with astral symbols and the horned crown associated with Anu/Enlil, visually encoding a differentiated pantheon rather than one featureless “Bel.” ([Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327048)) A British Museum lapis cylinder similarly shows the spade on a reclining mušḫuššu. ([British Museum seal](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_N-1204))

Certain depictions of a bearded, horn-crowned god can be Marduk, but securely identified anthropomorphic representations are rare. It is unsafe to label every Mesopotamian enthroned god “Marduk/Bel.”

### Palmyrene Bel

Palmyrene Bel is a bearded, mature sovereign, often dressed in military costume with a **calathos/modius**, radiate or celestial elements, and sometimes an eagle. In the great temple he belonged to a triadic configuration with **Yarhibol** and **Aglibol**, conventionally solar and lunar gods. Reliefs and architectural sculpture also connect his sanctuary with planets and zodiacal/cosmic imagery. [RV]

The Temple of Bel combined an eastern ritual plan—an enclosed sanctuary, bent-axis approach, multiple cult niches—with Corinthian and other Greco-Roman architectural forms. This hybridity is historical Palmyrene religion, not evidence of a timeless “East-West mystery faith.” [RV] ([Palmyra archaeological portal](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/palmyre/en/temple-bel))

No securely ancient universal correspondences with a fixed color, weekday, number, crystal, or magical seal belong to “Bel” across all periods. Marduk’s **number fifty** derives from his appropriation of Enlil’s rank; **Jupiter** is a first-millennium learned equation. Modern tables assigning Bel a single color, element, tarot card, or demonological seal are [MI].

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

#### Enlil

- **An as father:** common in third-millennium and literary tradition; Lugalzagesi’s inscription calls Enlil An’s firstborn. [EA]
- **An and Ki:** later schematic presentations may make Enlil offspring of heaven and earth. [LS]
- **Enki/Ninki ancestry:** *An = Anum* places him within a succession of paired ancestors beginning with Enki/Ninki—not the god Ea and his consort in the ordinary narrative sense, but primordial name-pairs. [LS]
- **Consort:** Ninlil; in “Enlil and Sud,” Sud becomes or is identified with Ninlil. [EA/SI]
- **Children:** Nanna/Sîn and Ninurta are especially stable. Nergal, Ninazu, Enbilulu, Namtar, Inanna, Utu, Iškur, Ningirsu, and others appear as children in particular texts. No single genealogy contains all these without contradiction. [LC/RV]
- **Vizier/messenger:** Nusku. [EA]

#### Marduk

- **Father:** Ea/Enki.
- **Mother:** Damkina/Damgalnuna.
- **Consort:** Ṣarpānītu/Zarpanītu; Erua can function as her aspect or learned counterpart.
- **Son:** Nabû, especially from the later second millennium onward; Tašmētu is Nabû’s consort.
- **Absorbed or equated gods:** Asalluhi, Tutu, and numerous gods whose names become aspects in *Enūma eliš* VII. This is theological incorporation rather than literal biological genealogy. [SI/LS]

#### Palmyrene Bel

Palmyrene inscriptions do not supply a Mesopotamian-style genealogy. Yarhibol and Aglibol are associates or temple companions, not securely Bel’s sons. Calling the group a “trinity” can mislead if it implies later Christian metaphysics. [RV]

### Documented divine interactions

The following are discrete interactions, not mere membership in a pantheon.

#### Enlil strand

- **An/Anu — conferral and shared sovereignty:** An and Enlil jointly decree fates and legitimate kings; later sources make An Enlil’s father. Royal hymns and god lists, third–second millennia BCE. **[EA/LS]**
- **Ninlil/Sud — courtship, intercourse, pursuit, and marriage:** *Enlil and Ninlil* and *Enlil and Sud*, Old Babylonian manuscripts. **[LC/SI]**
- **Nanna/Sîn — father receives son’s offerings and grants blessing:** *Nanna-Suen’s Journey to Nippur*, Old Babylonian copies. **[LC]**
- **Ninurta — father commissions or benefits from son’s martial victories:** *Anzû*, *Lugal-e*, and *An-gim*, second-millennium forms. **[LC]**
- **Anzû — theft from Enlil:** Anzû steals the Tablet of Destinies while Enlil bathes; Ninurta recovers it. *Anzû*, Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian versions. **[EA/LC]**
- **Enki/Ea — flood conflict:** Enlil seeks human destruction; Ea warns Atrahasis/Utnapishtim and preserves life. *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh* XI. **[EA/LC]**
- **Enki/Ea — rescue disagreement:** Enlil refuses aid to dead Inanna; Enki creates rescuers. *Inanna’s Descent*, Old Babylonian manuscripts. **[LC]**
- **Inanna/Ištar — divine daughter or subordinate petitioner:** Inanna receives powers from Enlil in hymns but is denied rescue in *Descent*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Nusku — service as vizier and messenger:** *Enlil and Ninlil*, god lists, hymns. **[EA/LC]**
- **Nergal, Ninazu, Enbilulu — generation through disguised encounters:** *Enlil and Ninlil*. **[LC]**
- **Humbaba — authorization and aftermath:** Enlil had assigned Humbaba to guard the Cedar Forest; after Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill him, divine anger follows. Sumerian Gilgamesh poems and Akkadian *Gilgamesh*. **[LC]**
- **Šamaš — opposed counsel regarding Enkidu’s death:** in the Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh*, Šamaš protests the death decision associated with Enlil and Anu. **[LC]**
- **Marduk — transfer of “Enlilship”:** Marduk receives the office and titles formerly centered on Enlil; Hammurabi’s prologue and *Enūma eliš*. **[SI/LC]**
- **Aššur — functional equation:** Assyrian texts call Aššur “Enlil of the gods.” This is ideological identification, not a surviving narrative encounter. **[SI/LS]**

#### Marduk-Bel strand

- **Ea/Enki — father trains and advances son:** Ea recognizes Marduk’s power and sends him against Tiamat. *Enūma eliš* I–III. **[LC]**
- **Damkina — mother-son relation:** god lists and *Enūma eliš*. **[LS/LC]**
- **Apsû — inherited victory:** Ea kills Apsû; Marduk is born or enthroned in Apsû’s domain. *Enūma eliš* I. **[LC]**
- **Tiamat — combat and killing:** Marduk traps her with winds, shoots her, splits her body, and constructs heaven and earth. *Enūma eliš* IV–V. **[LC]**
- **Qingu — judgment and execution:** Marduk identifies Qingu as rebel leader; Ea makes humanity from his blood. *Enūma eliš* VI. **[LC]**
- **Anšar — authorization:** Anšar and the assembly accept Marduk’s condition that his command become supreme before he fights Tiamat. *Enūma eliš* II–III. **[LC]**
- **Anu — bestowal of authority:** the gods declare Marduk’s word equivalent to Anu’s. *Enūma eliš* IV. **[LC/SI]**
- **Enlil — succession by title:** Marduk receives number fifty and the office “Enlil of the gods.” *Enūma eliš* VI–VII and learned lists. **[SI/LS]**
- **Lahmu and Lahamu — assembly support:** the primordial pair joins the banquet and recognition of Marduk. *Enūma eliš* III. **[LC]**
- **Asalluhi — fusion:** Asalluhi’s exorcistic identity is absorbed into Marduk, already developing in the Old Babylonian period. Some sources still distinguish them. **[SI]**
- **Tutu — fusion:** Borsippa’s Tutu becomes a name/aspect of Marduk, while Borsippa increasingly belongs to Nabû. **[SI]**
- **Ṣarpānītu — marriage and joint cult:** Babylonian god lists, offerings, and temple ritual. **[EA/LS]**
- **Nabû — father-son procession:** Nabû travels from Borsippa to Babylon for Akītu and joins Marduk’s assembly. First-millennium ritual texts. **[EA/LC]**
- **Nergal/Erra — temporary transfer of rule:** in *Erra and Išum*, Marduk’s withdrawal for restoration of his cult image creates space for Erra’s destructive activity. **[LC]**
- **Tišpak/Ninazu — appropriation of mušḫuššu:** iconographic and theological transfer, with no securely preserved combat narrative in which Marduk personally captures the animal. **[SI/MSR]**

#### Palmyrene strand

- **Yarhibol — joint residence and worship:** named with Bel in the Temple of Bel dedication and represented in the local triad. 32 CE and later. **[RV/EA]**
- **Aglibol — joint residence and worship:** likewise named in the dedication. **[RV/EA]**
- **Baalshamin — parallel civic sovereignty:** both were important Palmyrene high gods, but no surviving myth records a combat, meeting, or genealogy between them. **[RV; absence of narrative evidence]**
- **Zeus/Jupiter — interpretive equation:** Greek and Roman inscriptions render Bel as Zeus Belos or Jupiter Belus. **[SI]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Enlil’s separation of heaven and earth

Sumerian cosmological passages credit Enlil with separating An, heaven, from Ki, earth, establishing habitable space and ordering the world. There is no single complete “Book of Enlil’s Creation”; the motif is dispersed through hymns, debates, and introductions. [EA/LC] Modern retellings that present one seamless creation epic from these fragments are [MSR/MI].

### Enlil and Ninlil

Ninlil bathes in a canal after her mother’s advice. Enlil sees and impregnates her. The gods judge Enlil’s act and banish him from the city. Ninlil follows. Enlil assumes the identities of gatekeeper, river-man, and ferryman; in encounters with Ninlil he fathers underworld-associated sons who can substitute for or accompany Nanna. The poem combines divine genealogy, geography around Nippur, and a descent/substitution scheme. Calling it simply a romantic courtship suppresses its coercion and banishment; calling it a transcript of actual ritual overstates the evidence. [LC]

### Anzû and the Tablet of Destinies

Anzû steals the tablet that embodies executive authority from Enlil. Gods refuse or fail to confront him until Ninurta, aided by Ea’s strategy in the Standard version, defeats him. The story reaffirms Enlil’s office while allowing Ninurta heroic prominence. Different recensions vary in the hero and details; an older Sumerian Lugalbanda version and Akkadian Ninurta version should not be collapsed. [EA/LC/RV]

### The flood

In *Atrahasis*, Enlil is disturbed by multiplying humanity’s noise. Plague, drought, and famine fail because Enki helps humans; Enlil orders the flood, but Enki covertly instructs Atrahasis through a reed wall. After the catastrophe, the gods regret losing offerings. Enki and Enlil establish mortality and demographic controls. *Gilgamesh* XI retells the flood through Utnapishtim, compressing and altering the divine conflict. Enlil first rages that anyone survived, then grants Utnapishtim and his wife remote immortal life. [EA/LC]

This myth records ancient explanations of mortality and catastrophe; it does not establish a historical global flood. Conversely, its legendary status does not make its textual history unimportant.

### Marduk versus Tiamat

In *Enūma eliš*, Apsû plans to destroy younger gods whose noise troubles him; Ea kills Apsû. Tiamat then raises an army, installs Qingu, and gives him the Tablet of Destinies. Marduk agrees to fight only if the assembly makes his word supreme. Armed with bow, net, mace, winds, storm chariot, and divine radiance, he traps Tiamat, drives wind into her, shoots her, and splits her corpse. From it he structures heaven and earth, assigns celestial stations, organizes the calendar, and founds Babylon. Qingu is executed; humanity is fashioned from his blood to bear divine labor. The gods build Esagila and proclaim Marduk’s fifty names. [LC]

The poem reworks older combat, creation, and kingship traditions. Marduk inherits Enlil’s rank, Ninurta’s warrior pattern, and Ea/Asalluhi’s wisdom. It is not evidence that ordinary Babylonian belief everywhere abandoned the older gods.

### Marduk the suffering person’s rescuer

In *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*, Šubši-mešrê-Šakkan loses office, health, friends, and intelligibility before the gods. Dreams announce help; Marduk restores him, and the sufferer praises the lord’s inscrutable anger and mercy. The poem does not assert a universal doctrine that suffering always results from sin; its force lies precisely in the sufferer’s inability to discover a proportionate offense. [LC]

### Marduk’s disappearance and Erra’s violence

*Erra and Išum*, probably early first millennium BCE, has Erra persuade Marduk that his cult statue and regalia need restoration. Marduk’s temporary withdrawal destabilizes order; Erra unleashes devastation before Išum checks him. The poem reflects anxiety about cult-image maintenance, urban catastrophe, and divine absence. [LC]

### Bel and the Dragon

The first episode attacks a temple economy. Bel’s priests claim the image consumes prodigious food and wine. Daniel seals the temple after secretly covering its floor with ashes. Footprints reveal hidden entrances used by priests, wives, and children. The enraged king kills the priests and gives Daniel authority to destroy the image and temple. The dragon episode is separate in subject: Daniel kills a living cult animal with a cake of pitch, fat, and hair. [LC]

The narrative’s Bel is literary **Babylonian idolatry**, not a faithful Babylonian myth about Marduk. Proposed derivation of the dragon’s bursting from Marduk’s destruction of Tiamat is ingenious but unproved. The two Greek recensions and their different framing demonstrate continuing textual development.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Nippur and the Ekur

Nippur’s Ekur was Enlil’s central sanctuary and for long periods a supraregional source of legitimacy. Kings who did not rule from Nippur still rebuilt its temple, supplied offerings, and sought Enlil’s recognition. The institution included priests, administrators, scribes, agricultural estates, workshops, dependents, and redistributive accounts. “Temple” here means a major economic household as well as a ritual building. [EA]

The Ekur’s remains were excavated by University of Pennsylvania and later Chicago–Penn expeditions. A Parthian fortress was eventually built over the sacred area, illustrating transformation rather than a single dramatic moment of abolition. [EA]

### Babylon: Esagila, Etemenanki, and Akītu

Marduk’s principal temple was **Esagila**, “House whose top is lofty,” with the neighboring ziggurat **Etemenanki**, “House, foundation of heaven and earth.” Textual and archaeological evidence identifies Esagila as the cultic center and Etemenanki as the monumental temple tower; they are related but not interchangeable. ([Babylon archaeological portal](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/babylone/fr/le-complexe-cultuel-de-marduk))

The **Akītu** or New Year festival in Nisannu involved:

- purification and offerings;
- Nabû’s arrival from Borsippa;
- Marduk’s procession from Esagila through the Processional Way to the extramural Akītu house;
- the king’s temporary stripping of insignia, ritual striking or humiliation before Marduk, confession, and reinstatement;
- divine assembly and confirmation of destinies;
- recitation of *Enūma eliš* in documented first-millennium ritual;
- return procession and renewal of kingship. [EA/LC]

Descriptions of the rite are incomplete. Reconstructions of a staged sacred marriage, a literal ritual battle, or an annually performed full passion drama go beyond the surviving prescriptions. [MSR]

The cult image was treated as an embodied divine presence: washed, dressed, adorned, fed, processed, and sometimes captured by conquering armies. Removal of Marduk’s statue constituted divine and political absence; restoration allowed rulers to present themselves as restorers of cosmic order.

### Offerings, divination, and prayer

Daily offerings included bread, beer, wine, meat, incense, textiles, and precious objects. Hymns and penitential prayers address Marduk as healer and judge. Exorcists invoked Marduk/Asalluhi together with Ea in incantation traditions. Babylonian divination was not solely “Bel’s oracle”; extispicy and celestial interpretation belonged to broader learned institutions.

Theophoric names—Marduk-apla-iddina, Marduk-zākir-šumi, Bēl-šarru-uṣur, Bēl-ibni, and many others—document personal and dynastic devotion. The element **Bēl** can require contextual identification and must not automatically be assigned to Marduk in every place.

### Palmyra

A sanctuary of Bel existed by at least 44 BCE. Construction of the monumental complex began about 19 CE and was financed in part by wealthy Palmyrenes and merchants; it was consecrated to **Bel, Yarhibol, and Aglibol** on 6 April 32 CE, with work continuing into the second century. An inscription preserves the dedication and benefactor’s commemoration. [EA/RV] ([inscription and discussion](https://philipharland.com/greco-roman-associations/building-sanctuary-of-bel-i-bce/))

The sanctuary included:

- a vast walled court and porticoes;
- altar and basin;
- banquet spaces;
- a cella with two opposed cult niches or *thalamoí*;
- reliefs of gods, celestial bodies, and caravan scenes;
- donor inscriptions, honorary statues, and civic benefaction.

Priests and tribal or civic groups participated in administration and ritual. Sacrifice and communal banqueting are materially and epigraphically supported. Claims that the temple practiced human sacrifice are not supported by secure Palmyrene evidence.

### Human sacrifice and “temple prostitution”

**Human sacrifice:** no secure evidence demonstrates a regular sacrificial cult of humans to Enlil, Marduk-Bel, or Palmyrene Bel. Myths involving divine killing—Qingu’s execution, Tiamat’s dismemberment, or flood destruction—are not records of ritual human sacrifice. [Absence of evidence]

**Temple prostitution:** Herodotus 1.199 claims every Babylonian woman once had intercourse with a stranger in Aphrodite/Mylitta’s precinct. This concerns a goddess, not Bel, although it appears in the same Babylonian ethnography. No Babylonian administrative, legal, or ritual corpus verifies such a universal institution. Modern scholarship ranges from categorical rejection to cautious proposals that Herodotus distorted a narrower ritual. It must not be reported as established Babylonian practice. [External allegation/MSR] ([Beard and Henderson](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/1468-0424.00072); [Anagnostou-Laoutides and Charles](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Herodotus-on-sacred-marriage-and-sacred/991012821136802368))

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Mid-third millennium BCE: Enlil’s supremacy

Enlil stands near the summit of Early Dynastic pantheons. Nippur’s political importance derives less from imperial control than from its god’s ability to confer kingship. [EA]

### Akkadian and Ur III periods

Akkadian kings and especially Ur III rulers rebuilt the Ekur and tied universal kingship to Enlil. Nippur became a major scribal and administrative center. [EA]

### Old Babylonian period, approximately 2000–1600 BCE

Enlil remained supreme in literary and royal ideology, while Babylon’s Marduk grew with the fortunes of the First Dynasty of Babylon. Hammurabi’s prologue shows the transition: Anu and Enlil assign Babylon and magnify Marduk. Marduk also absorbs Asalluhi’s magical identity. [EA/SI]

### Kassite and later second millennium

Marduk’s national importance increased, but Enlil was not simply discarded; Kassite rulers continued to honor both. God lists rationalized divine families and equations. Marduk’s statue was carried away during foreign conquests; its return, especially under Nebuchadnezzar I, became a focus of restoration ideology. Lambert places *Enūma eliš* in this setting, though its precise composition date remains disputed. [LS/MSR]

### First millennium BCE: Bel normally means Marduk

Marduk became Babylon’s king of gods and inherited Enlil’s number fifty and major titles. Assyrian rulers alternately patronized, appropriated, or attacked Babylon’s cult. Sennacherib destroyed Babylon in 689 BCE; Esarhaddon rebuilt it. Assyrian texts could elevate Aššur by applying Enlil-like language while still acknowledging Marduk/Bel. [EA/SI]

Nebuchadnezzar II’s building program monumentalized Esagila, Etemenanki, the Processional Way, Ishtar Gate, and the mušḫuššu imagery. The cult’s prominence was simultaneously theological, civic, economic, and imperial. [EA]

### Achaemenid rule

After 539 BCE, Cyrus adopted Marduk’s legitimating language. The Cylinder presents conquest as Marduk’s choice. Later Persian-Babylonian relations were more variable than the binary “tolerant Cyrus versus suppressive successors” suggests. Evidence for Xerxes destroying Esagila or permanently removing a solid-gold statue is debated; classical reports and later reconstruction do not yield a single secure event matching the popular story. [EA/MSR]

### Seleucid and Parthian Babylonia

Esagila remained active, and Babylonian scribal, astronomical, and ritual culture persisted for centuries. Marduk was identified with Zeus and Jupiter in Greco-Roman interpretive systems. The cult declined gradually with demographic, political, linguistic, and institutional change; no single dated edict ended it. [EA/SI]

### Palmyra, first century BCE–third century CE

The local Bel cult became Palmyra’s monumental civic center. Its triadic organization and cosmic art differed from Babylonian Esagila. Merchants, tribes, and civic elites funded the sanctuary. Palmyrene communities carried related cults abroad, but individual expatriate temples must be read from their own inscriptions. [RV]

### Christianization and Islamization

The Temple of Bel was adapted into a Christian church in Late Antiquity. Later, the cella became part of a mosque and settlement. This is documented architectural reuse; sources do not establish uninterrupted worship of Bel beneath Christian or Islamic names. [EA]

Mesopotamian cults likewise disappeared through a long process of temple decline, language shift, loss of institutional funding, and expansion of Christianity, Manichaeism, Judaism, and later Islam. “Forced conversion” is too simple as a universal explanation, although coercion and temple closure occurred in some late-antique settings.

### Archaeological rediscovery and 2015 destruction

European and Ottoman-era visitors recorded Palmyra; modern excavations documented its architecture and inscriptions. In August 2015, Islamic State militants deliberately demolished the Temple of Bel’s cella and much of its monumental fabric with explosives. UNESCO confirmed the destruction on 1 September 2015. The gateway and portions of the precinct survived. [EA] ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/1341); [Getty documentation](https://www.getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/palmyra/temple_of_bel.html))

The attack was modern extremist iconoclasm directed against cultural heritage; it is not evidence of a continuing ancient cult or of a special modern worship of Bel.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Biblical and European reception

*Bel and the Dragon* kept Bel visible in Catholic and Orthodox biblical canons and in Protestant Apocrypha. In Western art and commentary, Bel became a stock image of exposed priestcraft: an idol “fed” by fraudulent clergy. This reception derives from Greek Daniel, not cuneiform evidence. [LC/MA]

Early modern biblical chronology often equated Bel with Nimrod, Baal, or the first deified human king. Such genealogies were speculative harmonizations produced without readable cuneiform and are [MA], not ancient Babylonian tradition.

### Assyriology

The nineteenth-century decipherment of cuneiform transformed Bel from a biblical/classical idol into a historically differentiated title. George Smith’s discoveries of the flood and creation tablets produced intense comparison with Genesis. Early scholars sometimes spoke as though “Bel” were one pan-Mesopotamian god; lexical lists and contextual philology have progressively replaced that model.

### Occultism and demonology

The goetic demon **Bael/Baël**, first spirit of Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* and the later *Lesser Key of Solomon*, belongs to the early-modern demonization of **Baal**, not demonstrably to a continuous transmission of Babylonian Marduk-Bel. Confusing Bael’s seal, invisibility power, three heads, or infernal legions with ancient Bel is [MI/MA].

Likewise, **Belial**, **Beelzebub**, and **Belenus** are linguistically and historically distinct. Similar spelling does not establish identity. Modern occult tables that assign “Bel” fixed planetary seals or make him a demon are eclectic constructions, even though Marduk’s ancient learned association with Jupiter is genuine.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Modern Mesopotamian polytheist and reconstructionist groups sometimes worship Marduk, Enlil, or Bel using translated hymns and reconstructed offerings. These are documented modern revivals, not continuous priestly lineages from Esagila or Nippur. Reliable membership numbers are unavailable. A historically careful reconstruction distinguishes Marduk from Enlil and Palmyrene Bel; eclectic practice may deliberately combine them. [MI/MSR]

### Literature, games, and popular culture

Modern fantasy frequently uses “Bel” for demons, devils, kings, or vaguely Near Eastern gods. Such characters usually descend from biblical “false god” imagery, Baal/Bael demonology, or invented fantasy taxonomies rather than Marduk’s cuneiform dossier. Specific fictional Bels should not be retroactively treated as ancient attributes. [MI]

### Political reception

The Cyrus Cylinder is often marketed as the “first charter of human rights.” Its actual text is a royal foundation inscription in which Marduk chooses Cyrus and validates his rule. Modern human-rights language is a twentieth-century political reception, not the document’s ancient literary genre. [MI] The Cylinder remains crucial evidence for Persian use of Bel-Marduk’s authority.

The destruction of Palmyra generated digital reconstruction projects, replicas, exhibitions, and debates over rebuilding. These projects memorialize lost heritage but do not reproduce the ancient building’s material history or revive its cult.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Bel a god or a title?

Both, depending on text. The obsolete extreme positions are:

- “Bel is always Enlil”;
- “Bel is always Marduk”;
- “Bel/Baal names one universal ancient god.”

The first preserves an older usage but fails for first-millennium Babylon; the second fails in earlier and regional contexts; the third collapses several languages and cult histories. Contextual philology is the current defensible method.

### Enlil’s etymology

“Lord Wind” remains popular, but the reading and semantics of **EN.LÍL₂** do not yield a secure transparent etymology. The lexical meaning “ghost/phantom” for *líl* complicates rather than settles the question. Functional descriptions of Enlil should therefore be derived from texts, not from an uncertain name translation. ([ORACC, Enlil](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/enlil/))

### Marduk’s origins

Marduk’s third-millennium beginnings remain obscure. The logographic “calf of Utu” need not be a historical etymology or proof of solar descent. His documented Old Babylonian merger with Asalluhi explains much of his magical character more securely than hypothetical original storm-god theories.

### Dating *Enūma eliš*

Proposals range from the Old Babylonian to Kassite and post-Kassite periods. The principal constraints are Marduk’s developed supremacy, the transfer of Enlil’s number fifty, references to Babylon’s cult topography, and the date of surviving manuscripts. Lambert’s late-twelfth-century setting under Nebuchadnezzar I is influential but not epigraphically proven by a dated authorial tablet. [MSR]

### Myth and ritual

That *Enūma eliš* was recited during first-millennium Akītu is documented. That it was composed for Akītu, or that every episode was enacted annually, is not. Earlier “myth-and-ritual” models frequently reconstructed a complete sacred drama from fragmentary rubrics; current work is more cautious.

### Herodotus

Herodotus’s “Zeus Belos” is valuable external testimony, but his Babylonian dimensions, customs, and priestly reports require comparison with excavation and cuneiform texts. His couch-and-chosen-woman account may reflect knowledge of divine sleeping arrangements, Greek ethnography, or both. His “sacred prostitution” report remains disputed and is not supported as a universal Babylonian law.

### Palmyrene Bol and Bel

The common reconstruction is that indigenous **Bol** was renamed or fused with Babylonian **Bel** by the Hellenistic period, perhaps by about 213 BCE. The evidence supports historical convergence, but statements that Palmyrene Bel was simply “Marduk transplanted intact” go too far. His associates, temple form, dress, and civic role are specifically Palmyrene.

### Primary evidence and collections

- **ETCSL:** composite Sumerian literary texts and translations.
- **ORACC/AMGG:** contextual deity dossiers and linked corpora.
- **DCCLT and An = Anum:** lexical and god-list evidence.
- **RINAP:** Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions.
- **CDLI:** tablet catalogues, images, provenience, and transliterations.
- **Penn Museum:** major Nippur excavation archive and tablet collection.
- **British Museum:** Cyrus Cylinder, Babylonian tablets, seals, and *kudurrus*.
- **Iraq Museum, Vorderasiatisches Museum, Louvre, Metropolitan Museum, Yale Babylonian Collection, Istanbul Archaeological Museums:** major dispersed holdings.
- **Palmyra Museum and site archives:** gravely affected by war, looting, and deliberate destruction.

The nineteenth-century removal and division of finds dispersed contexts among Istanbul, Philadelphia, Jena, London, Berlin, Paris, and other collections. Some tablets lack reliable provenience because of the antiquities market. Modern looting after the 1991 and 2003 Iraq wars further damaged archaeological context.

### Foundational scholarship

- Thorkild Jacobsen, *The Treasures of Darkness*: influential literary reconstruction of changing Mesopotamian divine personalities, but sometimes more synthetic and psychological than the surviving evidence warrants.
- Henri Frankfort, work on cylinder seals and ancient Near Eastern religion: foundational iconographic comparison, now supplemented by larger contextual corpora.
- Jeremy Black and Anthony Green, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*: reliable concise iconographic orientation, not a substitute for editions.
- Wilfred G. Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths*: central critical edition and study of *Enūma eliš* and related texts.
- Andrew R. George, *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*: indispensable critical edition distinguishing manuscripts and recensions.
- Walter Sommerfeld, *Der Aufstieg Marduks*: fundamental study of Marduk’s rise through the Old Babylonian period.
- *Reallexikon der Assyriologie*: essential detailed articles, whose publication dates and older hypotheses must be checked individually.

## 11. Conclusions

The historically defensible subject “Bel” is a **title-history and succession of identities**, not one god’s timeless biography. Its earliest recoverable divine center is Enlil’s sovereignty at Nippur. Its most influential ancient form is Marduk-Bel, produced by Babylon’s political ascent, Marduk’s absorption of Asalluhi, and the deliberate transfer of Enlil’s titles and rank. Its most monumental western regional form is Palmyrene Bel, whose sanctuary embodied a specifically Syrian and Greco-Roman civic religion.

Three findings are especially secure:

1. **“Bel” means “Lord”; context, period, and place determine the god.**
2. **First-millennium Babylonian Bel is normally Marduk, but earlier Enlil usage and Palmyrene regional development remain distinct.**
3. **Many familiar claims—one universal Bel/Baal, a goetic demon’s seal, routine human sacrifice, temple prostitution under Bel, or an unbroken modern cult—are not supported by the primary record.**
