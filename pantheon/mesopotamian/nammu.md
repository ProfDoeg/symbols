# Nammu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Mesopotamian goddess; more precisely, a primordial creator and divine mother associated with Eridu, subterranean/cosmic water, creation, and Sumerian incantation. Some modern scholars describe her primordial aspect as asexual or self-generating, but the texts normally write and grammatically treat her as a goddess.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the divine name **𒀭𒇉**, conventionally normalized *Namma* or *Nammu*, occurs in the Early Dynastic IIIa god list **SF 1**, excavated at Šuruppak/Tell Fāra and conventionally dated about **2600–2500 BCE** (obverse viii 10). This entry proves that the deity’s name was recognized by Early Dynastic scribes; by itself it says nothing about creation, water, genealogy, or cult. Approximately contemporary evidence from the Abu Ṣalābīkh *zà-mì* hymns associates her with a settlement whose reading is uncertain. The first surviving narrative account of her is much later: *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, preserved principally on Old Babylonian school tablets of the early second millennium BCE.

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[Early attestation]**: third-millennium evidence, or evidence securely preserving a third-millennium cultic fact.
- **[Old Babylonian literary tradition]**: Sumerian compositions preserved mainly in approximately 1900–1600 BCE manuscripts; these may conserve older traditions, but their surviving wording is Old Babylonian.
- **[Later canonical/learned systematization]**: canonical god lists, bilingual texts, first-millennium incantations, commentaries, or theological compilations.
- **[Regional variant]**: a tradition attached to a particular city, cult, or scribal center.
- **[Syncretistic or functional succession]**: one deity assumes another’s position or function; this does not necessarily mean that ancient scribes equated them.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**: an inference from the ancient record rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[Modern reception/invention]**: post-cuneiform religious, occult, literary, artistic, or popular reinterpretation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Nammu belongs to the oldest named stratum of documented Mesopotamian divinity, but the familiar modern portrait—“the primordial sea-goddess who existed before everything and parthenogenetically gave birth to the universe”—combines evidence of different dates. Her name is already present in an Early Dynastic god list. The explicit characterization “first mother who gave birth to all the gods” occurs in the Old Babylonian witnesses of *Enki and Ninmaḫ*. Her title “mother who gave birth to heaven and earth” belongs to Old Babylonian and later learned god-list traditions. Her identification as the cosmic ocean is a defensible scholarly interpretation of the ENGUR writing and her cosmogonic titles, but surviving texts can also distinguish Nammu from *engur* and *abzu* by calling her their mother. She is therefore connected with primordial or groundwater, not simply interchangeable with every Mesopotamian word for “sea.”

### Name and writings

The normal cuneiform writing is:

- **𒀭𒇉**, transliterated **^dNAMMA**, **^dNAMMU**, or **^dENGUR**. The first sign, 𒀭, is the divine determinative; the second is the complex sign **LAGAB×ḪAL**, conventionally ENGUR.
- Attested syllabic or variant writings include **^dnam-ma**, **^dnam-mu**, **^dla-am-mu**, **na-am-ma**, **na-am-na-am-mi**, and a longer form represented as **Namnamu/Nannama**. A Tell Harmal bilingual lexical text treats a short and a reduplicated form as corresponding Akkadian and Sumerian forms.
- **Namma** is now the more usual Assyriological normalization. Earlier scholarship commonly used **Nammu**, and that spelling remains widespread outside specialist publications. Miguel Civil argued for *Namma* in 1985; the change concerns vocalization, not a newly discovered deity. The historical pronunciation probably varied, and the final vowel is not recoverable with complete certainty. [ORACC’s lexical entry records 53 occurrences spanning Early Dynastic IIIa through Hellenistic scholarly contexts.](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0046549.html)

### The ENGUR problem

**[Secure writing; disputed interpretation]** Lexical texts give **engur** as a reading of the same sign and equate *engur* with Akkadian **apsû**, the subterranean fresh-water deep. Older scholarship sometimes treated the sign’s lexical value and the goddess’s name as identical: “Nammu = the engur.”

That is too simple:

1. The sign used for a divine name can simultaneously be a logogram for a cosmic substance without proving that every occurrence denotes the goddess.
2. An Old Babylonian incantation calls Nammu mother of the **abzu**; a Neo-Babylonian incantation calls her mother of **engur**. Those formulations grammatically distinguish the goddess from the waters.
3. *Enki and Ninmaḫ* places Enki asleep “in the deep engur” and has Nammu approach him there. This permits, but does not compel, the conclusion that she is the place itself.
4. Manuel Ceccarelli has argued more specifically for **groundwater**, criticizing an undifferentiated “salt sea” interpretation. Jan Lisman instead regards her primordial-ocean role as a second-millennium development, possibly influenced by Tiamat. Frans Wiggermann calls her the “Cosmic Ocean” while retaining the distinctions made by particular texts.

The careful formulation is therefore: **Nammu is a primordial water-associated creator whose name is written with ENGUR; some texts and scholars closely identify her with the cosmic waters, while other texts distinguish her from engur and abzu.**

### Etymologies

No proposed etymology is secure.

- **“Creatrix.”** A traditional analysis connects the name with a creation-related element also found in *Ninimma*, explained by Akkadian *nabnītu* or *bunnannû*, “formation/creation.” Wiggermann notes that ancient scholars themselves associated the name with **nig₂-nam-ma**, “creativity,” “totality,” or “everything.” This may be learned folk etymology rather than historical derivation.
- **“Lady of the cosmic river.”** A proposed genitive compound *(e)n + amma(k)* yields approximately “lady of the cosmic river/water.” The phonology and morphology are disputed.
- **Connection with Akkadian *mummu*.** Older comparativist scholarship connected Nammu with *mummu*, “creative force/form,” but this is not established and should not be used to identify her with the god Mummu of *Enūma eliš*.
- **Current conclusion:** the name’s original etymology is unknown. Ceccarelli explicitly criticizes Lambert’s derivation, while modern summaries that translate the name straightforwardly as “creation” overstate the evidence.

### Documented titles and descriptions

- **ama-palil u₃-tu-diĝir-šar-šar-ra-ke₄**, “first/primeval mother who gave birth to all the gods”: *Enki and Ninmaḫ* 17–19, Old Babylonian manuscripts. **[Old Babylonian literary tradition]**
- **ama-tu-an-ki**, “mother who gave birth to heaven and earth” or “mother, begetter of the totality heaven-and-earth”: TCL XV 10 and *An = Anum* I 28. The wording need not mean two independently personified children; *an-ki* can denote the cosmos collectively. **[Old Babylonian/later learned systematization]**
- **ama ^dEnki**, “mother of Enki”: *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, *An = Anum* I 27, and later bilingual magical texts. **[Old Babylonian through later canonical]**
- **dam An-ra**, “wife/spouse of An”: the Early Dynastic inscription of Lugal-kisalsi. This is the only unambiguous ancient source making them spouses. **[Early regional variant]**
- **ama-arḫuš**, “compassionate mother”: the Samsu-iluna B inscription, TCL XVI 43:33. **[Old Babylonian]**
- **nin-a-gub-ba / bēlet egubbê**, “mistress of the holy-water basin”: a later bilingual incantation, CT XVI 7:255 and duplicate from Emar. The title usually belongs to Ningirima, who precedes Nammu in the text. **[Later magical/learned transfer]**
- **agrig-zi-Ekur-ra**, “true housekeeper/stewardess of Ekur”: *An = Anum* I 27. Wiggermann and later scholars warn that this may arise from confusion with Ninimma, an established member of Enlil’s Nippur court. **[Later learned systematization; possibly scribal confusion]**
- “Mother of Eridu,” “mother of abzu,” and “mother of engur”: lamentational and incantation traditions of the second and first millennia BCE. **[Regional/later magical traditions]**

### Identifications and non-identifications

- **Tiamat:** Nammu is not named Tiamat in the extant sources. In the late-second-millennium Babylonian *Enūma eliš*, Tiamat occupies the primordial maternal-water position that Nammu occupies in some Sumerian learned traditions. Lambert therefore describes *Enūma eliš* as substituting Tiamat for Nammu. This is **functional succession**, not proof that worshippers regarded the two names as exact equivalents. Tiamat is paired with Apsû, raises a monster army, is killed by Marduk, and becomes the material of the cosmos; no such combat or dismemberment is recorded for Nammu. **[Syncretistic/functional succession]**
- **Ninmaḫ/Ninḫursaĝa/Mami/Bēlet-ilī:** these mother-goddess figures inherit Nammu’s anthropogonic labor in *Atrahasis* and related creation accounts, but Wiggermann emphasizes that Nammu is **never explicitly equated** with Ninmaḫ. **[Functional succession, not identity]**
- **Ningirima:** Nammu receives a holy-water-basin epithet normally associated with Ningirima, but the same incantation lists them separately. **[Shared magical title, not identity]**
- **Uraš:** Uraš is normally An’s earth-associated spouse and, in one tradition, mother of Enki. Nammu and Uraš are distinct, although Wiggermann suggests they could fill comparable positions in alternative local cosmogonies. **[Competing regional genealogy]**
- No secure interpretatio graeca or interpretatio romana is known. Equations with Greek Thalassa, Oceanus, Gaia, Egyptian Nun/Nut, Vedic Danu, or a universal “Great Mother” are modern comparisons, not ancient identifications.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Early Dynastic documentation

#### SF 1, Fāra god list

**Date and support:** Early Dynastic IIIa, approximately 2600–2500 BCE; clay lexical tablet from Šuruppak/Tell Fāra.

**Evidence:** ^dNamma at obverse viii 10.

**What it establishes:** the divine name was part of an Early Dynastic scribal inventory.

**What it does not establish:** the entry supplies no domain, parentage, narrative, iconography, offerings, or city. Its organization is partly lexical, so neighboring names cannot automatically be treated as a divine family or cultic circle. **[Earliest secure attestation]**

#### Abu Ṣalābīkh *zà-mì* hymns

The Early Dynastic IIIa hymnic sequence from Abu Ṣalābīkh praises cities and their deities. Lines 140–141 assign Namma to a place written **ŠU.EŠ.GI**, whose reading and location remain uncertain. The passage is difficult, and no other source securely identifies her as this settlement’s tutelary goddess. It is nevertheless early evidence that she could receive a city/temple hymn. **[Early attestation; uncertain locality]**

#### Lugal-kisalsi building inscription

A set of Early Dynastic IIIb foundation pegs, probably from Uruk but with imperfect provenance, bears the Sumerian inscription:

> ^dnamma / dam an-ra / lugal-kisal-si / lugal unu^ki-ga / lugal urim₅^ki-ma / e₂ ^dnamma / mu-du₃

“For Namma, spouse of An, Lugal-kisalsi, king of Uruk and king of Ur, built the temple of Namma.”

The [CDLI record](https://cdli.earth/artifacts/247730/history/atf) and [ETCSRI composite](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/etcsri/Q003669/) preserve the reading. The inscription is usually placed around the twenty-fourth century BCE. It establishes a built sanctuary and the An–Namma marriage in this particular royal theology. Because the objects’ provenance is not wholly secure, it does not securely locate the temple. **[Early attested regional cult]**

### *Enki and Ninmaḫ*

**Language and witnesses:** Sumerian; a composition of approximately 140 lines preserved primarily in multiple Old Babylonian school manuscripts, approximately 1900–1600 BCE, with later bilingual fragments from first-millennium Nineveh. The traditional suggestion that the poem was first composed under Ur III rule is possible but cannot be demonstrated from an extant Ur III manuscript.

**Role of Nammu:** central to the first, anthropogonic portion. She is the “first mother,” receives the tears and complaints of the laboring gods, wakes Enki, proposes a human substitute, receives his instructions, kneads clay from above the abzu, gives birth with divine assistants, decrees humanity’s fate, and is honored at Enki’s feast. The later contest between Enki and Ninmaḫ largely proceeds without her.

The [ETCSL composite text](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.1.1.2) is the principal open transliteration and translation. Major modern editions or treatments include Carlos Benito’s 1969 dissertation, Thorkild Jacobsen’s translation in *The Harps That Once…*, Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths*, and Manuel Ceccarelli’s recent work on the manuscripts.

**Textual caution:** the crucial execution of creation lies partly in a damaged passage. The poem explicitly assigns Nammu the plan, clay-working, birth, and fate-decreeing; it does not provide a continuous anatomical account of how clay became flesh. **[Old Babylonian literary tradition]**

### God lists and lexical texts

- **SF 1:** Early Dynastic IIIa; bare name.
- **Old Babylonian Nippur god list:** Namma is entry 107.
- **TCL XV 10/AO 5376:** Old Babylonian god list; contains the title “mother who bore heaven and earth.” The [CDLI record dates the tablet to approximately 1900–1600 BCE](https://cdli.earth/P345354).
- **Forerunner to *An = Anum*:** Namma occurs with Enki’s spouse and family circle.
- ***An = Anum* I 27–28:** canonical god list compiled from Middle Babylonian materials and transmitted in first-millennium copies. It calls her mother of Enki, “true housekeeper of Ekur,” and “mother who gave birth to heaven and earth.” **[Later canonical systematization]**
- **Bilingual lexical texts:** give the syllabic readings *na-am-ma*, variants, and the equation between the divine sign and its pronunciation. These are essential evidence that ^dENGUR was not meant simply to be read “the deep” in every divine-name context.

### Incantations and magical texts

Nammu’s magical record is more extensive than her narrative corpus.

- **Ur III Sumerian incantations:** she is invoked with Enki and sometimes Asalluḫi; one formula praises “holy Enki” and “holy Namma.” Tablet P124628 is an Ur III prayer-incantation that names Enki and Namma.
- **Old Babylonian formula:** *ĝe₂₆ lu₂ ^dNamma-me-en*, “I am the person/man of Namma,” identifies the incantation specialist with her divine authority; another version pairs Namma with Nanše.
- **CT XLII pl. 41b:** Old Babylonian incantation possibly calls her “the pure one of An,” but the reading is epigraphically uncertain.
- **VS XVII 30:3:** calls Nammu mother of the abzu.
- **TCL XVI 43:33, Samsu-iluna B:** calls her “compassionate mother.”
- **CT XVI 7 and the Emar duplicate:** “mistress of the holy-water basin.”
- **CT XVI 13:36–41 and Šurpu VIII 19:** later demon-averting sequences invoke Nammu, Nanše, and other gods.
- **PBS I/2 112 i 30:** Neo-Babylonian incantation calls her mother of engur.
- **First-millennium explanatory text:** equates a ritual container with Nammu, following an equation of the holy-water basin with Ningirima.

Her activity is therefore specifically prominent in **Sumerian or bilingual white-magic traditions**. She is nearly absent from Akkadian medical-magical literature considered independently of bilingual antiquarian transmission. [Wiggermann’s RlA dossier collects these references.](https://dokumen.pub/reallexikon-der-assyriologie-und-vorderasiatischen-archologie-band-9-nab-nuzi-9783110886771-9783110172966.html)

### Lamentations

Canonical Sumerian lamentations and related bilingual material call Nammu mother of Eridu and preserve her place in the Eridu divine circle. These are liturgical survivals rather than new independent myths. Their first-millennium tablets often preserve compositions with older second-millennium antecedents. **[Canonical liturgical transmission]**

### Texts named in the item brief in which Nammu does not appear

Absence must not be converted into identity:

- ***Enūma eliš*:** Nammu is not a character. Tiamat and Apsû occupy an analogous primordial-water position. The poem was probably composed in the later second millennium BCE; the precise date remains debated. Lambert favored a late-second-millennium setting. **[Functional replacement, not textual appearance]**
- ***Atrahasis*:** Nammu is not named. Ea and the womb-goddess Mami/Bēlet-ilī create humanity from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god. Combining this blood episode with Nammu’s clay-work in *Enki and Ninmaḫ* creates a modern composite account unsupported by either text.
- ***Epic of Gilgamesh*:** Nammu does not appear in the Old Babylonian or Standard Babylonian epic as edited by Andrew George. The “deep” in the title *ša naqba īmuru* is not the divine name Nammu.
- ***Inanna’s Descent*:** the [ETCSL text](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr141.htm) does not name Nammu.
- **Sumerian Gilgamesh poems:** no securely identified Nammu episode.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP:** Nammu has no meaningful role in royal narrative. RINAP’s references to Tiamat concern *Enūma eliš*-derived imperial imagery, not Nammu.
- **The Sumerian flood/Eridu Genesis:** Nammu is not a securely preserved actor in the extant text.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

1. **Primordial motherhood and cosmic generation.** Explicit in *Enki and Ninmaḫ* and the god lists, not stated by the bare Early Dynastic attestations.
2. **Anthropogenesis.** She proposes humanity, works the clay, gives birth, and decrees fate in *Enki and Ninmaḫ*. No other surviving narrative gives her this full role.
3. **Subterranean or cosmic water.** Inferred from the ENGUR writing, her presence in the deep, and her relationship to abzu/engur. Whether specifically freshwater, groundwater, or an undifferentiated primordial sea remains debated.
4. **Eridu.** She belongs to Enki’s local divine circle and can be mother of the city.
5. **Beneficent magic, purification, and ritual water.** Explicit in incantations, especially Sumerian and bilingual texts.
6. **Compassionate motherhood.** The Samsu-iluna B title and her response to the gods’ tears support this characterization.
7. **Fate-decreeing.** In *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, she is to decree the created human’s fate.

### Characteristic actions and limitations

Nammu receives petitions, carries them to Enki, wakes and exhorts him, plans, kneads clay, gives birth, decrees fate, accepts a feast, supports incantation specialists, and legitimates ritual water. She is never shown waging war, judging the dead, ruling the divine assembly, or combating another deity. No text records her death, defeat, imprisonment, or physical dismemberment.

Calling her “queen of all gods” is an extrapolation from motherhood, not a documented title. Likewise, there is no ancient text making her omnipotent, infinite, morally perfect, or the metaphysical substance of everything.

### Iconography

**No securely identified ancient image of Nammu is known.** This is the principal iconographic finding.

The Lugal-kisalsi foundation peg has sometimes been loosely described as “from Nammu’s temple,” but its human figure represents the royal dedicator, not a securely identified portrait of Nammu. Generic nude goddesses, flowing-water goddesses, fish-tailed women, snake goddesses, and female figures holding water vessels cannot be assigned to Nammu without an inscription or a unique attribute.

Henri Frankfort’s seal studies illuminate Mesopotamian divine imagery generally, especially water gods and presentation scenes, but they do not establish a Nammu iconographic type.

Consequently, the following are **not anciently attested for Nammu**:

- a standard bodily form;
- a horned-crown configuration unique to her;
- serpentine, mermaid, fish-tailed, or dragon form;
- a weapon;
- a sacred animal or plant;
- a fixed color, number, weekday, month, constellation, or planet;
- a documented emblem equivalent to Enki’s flowing-water streams or Tiamat’s later dragon-like representations.

The cuneiform ENGUR sign is a writing, not proof of a pictorial cult emblem.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Relationship | Source and date | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nammu as self-sufficient primordial mother | She gives birth to all the gods/cosmos without a named partner | *Enki and Ninmaḫ* 17–19; OB manuscripts | **[Old Babylonian literary tradition]**; parthenogenesis is a scholarly inference from the missing partner |
| Nammu → Enki | Mother and son | *Enki and Ninmaḫ*; *An = Anum* I 27; bilingual magic | **[Well-attested from OB onward]** |
| An + Nammu | Spouses | Lugal-kisalsi temple inscription, ED IIIb | **[Early regional variant]** and the only explicit marriage |
| An + Nammu → Enki | An is father and Nammu mother | Combination supported indirectly by texts naming An as Enki’s father and Nammu as mother; the marriage inscription joins them | **[Reconstruction from multiple sources]**, not one surviving birth narrative |
| Nammu → An and Ki / an-ki | Mother of heaven and earth/the cosmos | TCL XV 10; *An = Anum* I 28 | **[OB/later learned cosmogony]** |
| An + Uraš → Enki | Alternative parentage | Išme-Dagan E 11, 13 | **[Regional/literary variant]** |
| primordial Enki–Ninki ancestors → Enki’s theological line | Alternative learned ancestry | god-list traditions | **[Learned systematization]** |
| Nammu → abzu, engur, Eridu | Maternal relationship to cosmic places or substances | OB and NB incantations; lamentations | **[Poetic/magical genealogy]** |

There is no securely attested father or mother of Nammu, no securely attested sibling, and no named daughter. Describing every goddess helping at humanity’s birth as her daughter is unsupported.

### Every documented named divine interaction

The surviving inventory is small enough to list individually:

- **An:** Nammu is called his spouse in Lugal-kisalsi’s temple inscription, approximately twenty-fourth century BCE. No narrative meeting, courtship, or sexual episode survives. **[Early regional attestation]**
- **Enki:** Nammu receives the laboring gods’ complaint, approaches and wakes her sleeping son, urges him to create a substitute, follows his anthropogonic instructions, decrees humanity’s fate, and receives a feast from him; *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, Old Babylonian manuscripts. Later god lists and magical texts continue to call her his mother. **[Old Babylonian canonical literary interaction]**
- **Ninmaḫ:** Enki appoints Ninmaḫ as Nammu’s assistant during humanity’s creation; Enki later prepares a feast for both Nammu and Ninmaḫ. They are not equated. *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, approximately 1900–1600 BCE manuscripts. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction]**
- **Ninimma:** directed to stand by Nammu as Nammu gives birth to humanity; *Enki and Ninmaḫ* 33–37. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction]**
- **Šuzianna:** assists or stands by Nammu at humanity’s birth; same source. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction]**
- **Ninmada:** assists or stands by Nammu at humanity’s birth; same source. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction]**
- **Ninbarag:** assists or stands by Nammu at humanity’s birth; same source. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction]**
- **Ninmug:** assists or stands by Nammu at humanity’s birth; same source. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction]**
- **ŠAR.ŠAR.GABA:** name damaged or uncertain in some witnesses; assists or stands by Nammu during creation; same source. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction; uncertain reading]**
- **Ninguna:** assists or stands by Nammu at humanity’s birth; same source. **[Old Babylonian literary interaction]**
- **Nanše:** paired with Nammu in incantations, demon-averting god lists, and the Sealand cult, where a *šangû* priest served both. An Old Babylonian incantation has the practitioner declare himself a person of Nammu and Nanše. No independent narrative encounter survives. **[Cultic/magical association]**
- **Asalluḫi:** appears with Nammu and Enki in Sumerian magical invocations belonging to the Eridu incantation circle. No narrative dialogue between Asalluḫi and Nammu survives. **[Ur III and later magical association]**
- **Ningirima:** precedes Nammu in a bilingual incantation; Nammu receives a holy-water-basin title normally Ningirima’s. They are listed separately. **[Later magical association]**
- **Nergal:** a hemerology assigns both Nergal’s “playing ceremony” and Nammu’s festival to day 27; the king sacrifices before them. This is calendrical conjunction, not mythology. **[Later ritual association]**
- **Tiamat:** no ancient text stages a meeting. Tiamat replaces Nammu’s cosmogonic position in *Enūma eliš* according to modern textual comparison. **[Functional succession; no interaction]**
- **Mami/Bēlet-ilī:** no surviving meeting; Mami assumes the human-creator role in *Atrahasis*. **[Functional succession; no interaction]**
- **Uraš:** no meeting; she supplies a competing spouse-of-An and mother-of-Enki genealogy. **[Competing tradition]**

The unnamed laboring gods and collective birth-goddesses interact with Nammu in *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, but they cannot be turned into additional named relationships.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The creation of humanity in *Enki and Ninmaḫ*

**[Old Babylonian literary tradition]**

At the beginning of the poem, the gods exist and labor is already apportioned. Senior gods supervise while lesser gods dig canals and perform burdensome work. Their toil produces protest and tears. Enki, possessor of wisdom and design, is asleep in the deep **engur**, in a chamber inaccessible to other gods.

Nammu, called the first mother who bore the senior gods, receives the workers’ tears. She carries the complaint to Enki and addresses him as her son. Her proposal is pragmatic rather than a cosmic battle: apply wisdom and create a substitute so that the gods may be released from labor.

Enki wakes, deliberates in Ḫal-an-kug, his chamber of designs, and accepts “the creature you planned.” He instructs Nammu to:

1. impose on the new being the gods’ basket-carrying labor;
2. knead clay from above the abzu;
3. work with the birth-goddesses;
4. bring the form into existence;
5. give birth with Ninmaḫ and seven named goddesses attending;
6. decree the creature’s fate.

The following lines are damaged, but the text appears to describe birth purification and completion of humanity. Enki rejoices and gives a banquet for Nammu and Ninmaḫ. The poem then shifts to a contest: Ninmaḫ creates humans with various disabilities or limitations, and Enki assigns each a social livelihood. Enki creates a being Ninmaḫ cannot accommodate, producing the text’s final dispute. Nammu is not the antagonist and does not take part in this latter contest.

**Function:** The story explains why humans bear institutional labor formerly imposed on gods; it also distributes creative agency among planning, clay, gestation, birth, fate, and social assignment. It validates Enki’s wisdom and the mother-goddess complex without reducing creation to one actor.

**Variant and contamination:** *Atrahasis* supplies the slain god’s blood; *Enki and Ninmaḫ* supplies Nammu’s abzu clay. Modern retellings frequently fuse them by saying Nammu mixed clay with We-ilu’s blood. That combined scene is **modern invention**.

### Cosmic motherhood

There is no surviving continuous “Nammu creates heaven and earth” epic. The story is reconstructed from titles:

- “first mother who gave birth to all the gods” in *Enki and Ninmaḫ*;
- “mother who gave birth to heaven and earth” in TCL XV 10 and *An = Anum*;
- mother of Enki, abzu, engur, and Eridu in different texts.

Modern accounts often narrate a sequence—Nammu alone in the void, giving birth first to An, then Ki, after which An and Ki produce Enlil—as though copied from one tablet. No such tablet is known. This is a **modern synthetic cosmogony**, assembled from distinct Sumerian compositions and god lists. Earlier third-millennium cosmogonic passages commonly begin with heaven and earth already paired and do not name Nammu as their origin.

### Magical historiolae

In Sumerian incantations, Nammu belongs to the authoritative circle of Enki, Asalluḫi, Nanše, and purification water. Some texts make her the divine patron under whose identity the exorcist speaks; others call her mother of abzu or engur. These are compressed ritual myths rather than developed narratives. They attest continuing practical power after her independent literary profile had diminished.

### Nammu and Tiamat: transformation, not one myth

*Enūma eliš* begins with Apsû and Tiamat mingling waters and generating gods. Ea kills Apsû; Tiamat later arms monsters, gives Qingu the Tablet of Destinies, and is killed and divided by Marduk. Nothing comparable is narrated about Nammu.

The two traditions share primordial maternity and water imagery, so Lambert could say that *Enūma eliš* “substituted Tiamat” for Nammu. But the substitution transforms a beneficent mother and ritual-water power into an armed cosmic opponent. The [French Ministry of Culture overview dates *Enūma eliš* broadly to the end of the second millennium BCE](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/proche-orient/en/glossary/creation-epic-enuma-elis); Lambert’s edition remains the standard critical reference. **[Later Babylonian theological replacement]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Early temple

Lugal-kisalsi’s inscription records construction of an **e₂ ^dNamma**, “temple of Namma.” Its ceremonial name is lost, and the foundation objects’ uncertain provenance prevents a secure location. Uruk is plausible because the builder was king of Uruk and the inscription pairs Nammu with An, Uruk’s great god, but “temple at Uruk” should remain qualified. **[Early archaeological/epigraphic attestation]**

### Uncertain Early Dynastic cult center

The Abu Ṣalābīkh *zà-mì* hymn associates Namma with ŠU.EŠ.GI. The toponym is not securely read or located. Calling Eridu her exclusive or demonstrable principal temple center exceeds this evidence, although her later theological affiliation with Eridu is strong.

### Eridu

Nammu is repeatedly integrated into the Eridu circle through Enki, abzu, magic, and the title “mother of Eridu.” Yet no excavated building at Eridu has been securely identified by inscription as her independent temple. “Cult center: Eridu,” common in handbooks, is principally a theological inference. **[Strong textual association; archaeologically unlocated cult]**

### Old Babylonian Ur

Administrative tablets attest:

- a temple or cult establishment of Nammu;
- prebends and temple personnel;
- **gudu₄** purification priests;
- offerings;
- a field bearing her name.

Relevant UET V texts include 90, 107, 274, and 503. This is the best evidence for an operating institutional cult beyond the Early Dynastic building inscription. It still represents a small establishment compared with the cult of Ur’s moon god Nanna. **[Administrative attestation]**

### Sealand

A kudurru examined by Wiggermann indicates that a temple of Nammu existed somewhere in the Sealand from at least the reign of **Gulkišar**, conventionally in the later Old Babylonian/First Sealand period, and remained active under **Enlil-nādin-apli** of the Second Dynasty of Isin, early eleventh century BCE. Nammu and Nanše shared a **šangû** priest. The document preserves a legal memory of cult property over several centuries, although its retrospective chronology must be treated as a claim in a later legal instrument. **[Cultic/legal attestation with retrospective element]**

### Nippur, Babylon, and Bāṣ

- **Nippur:** an Enlil liturgy mentions a shrine **ki-uš-^dNamma**, “footstep/foundation of Namma,” in Ekur.
- **Babylon:** Nabonidus, 556–539 BCE, mentions a shrine of the same name within Esagil, Marduk’s temple complex. This is Nammu’s latest clearly datable royal reference. [ORACC’s AMGG summary cites George’s *House Most High*, no. 641.](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/namma/)
- **Bāṣ:** a document probably belonging to Esarhaddon’s reign, 680–669 BCE, places her worship in **E-DUR-gi-na**, the temple of Lugal-asal.

These attestations show antiquarian and ritual survival; they do not demonstrate mass popular worship.

### Festival and offerings

A first-millennium hemerology assigns the twenty-seventh day of a month to Nergal’s *melultu*, “playing ceremony,” and Nammu’s **isinnu**, “festival.” The king presents cereal, performs sacrifice before Nergal and Nammu, and expects divine response. The exact month and wider public observance are unclear.

Animal and food offerings are plausible within ordinary Mesopotamian temple economy, but no source documents a distinctive Nammu sacrifice, pilgrimage, oracle, sacred marriage, or mystery rite.

### Theophoric names

The royal name **Ur-^dNamma**, traditionally *Ur-Nammu* and increasingly *Ur-Namma*, is by far the most important theophoric occurrence.

- **ur** in personal names can mean “servant,” “man,” “warrior,” “dog,” or metaphorically “lion,” depending on period and context. Translations such as “servant of Namma” or “man/warrior of Namma” are preferable to treating “dog of Namma” as certain.
- The founder of the Third Dynasty of Ur reigned approximately **2112–2095 BCE** in the middle chronology. His inscriptions spell the name with the divine determinative before Namma: **ur-^dnamma**.
- Nineteenth-century scholarship read the king’s name **Ur-Engur**, because the sign was read by its lexical value. Syllabic evidence corrected this to Ur-Nammu/Namma. The [British Museum explicitly identifies “Ur-Engur” as the obsolete reading](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/BIOG63102).
- His name establishes personal or dynastic invocation of Namma; it does not establish that she was Ur III’s state goddess. Ur-Namma’s own building inscriptions overwhelmingly honor Nanna, Enlil, Enki, Inanna, and other major gods. The name is important precisely because independent Ur III cult evidence for Namma is sparse.

Other theophoric names are rare. Wiggermann concludes that the deity occurs as a personal-name element practically only in the royal name. Claims of widespread popular Nammu devotion based on names are therefore unsupported.

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

The dedicated contested-material search found:

- **No evidence for human sacrifice to Nammu.**
- **No evidence for “temple prostitution” in her cult.**
- **No Nammu-specific cult violence, coercive conversion, or punitive law.**
- **No securely identified sexual rite, sacred marriage, or priestess acting as her sexual representative.**

Generic claims about “Sumerian mother-goddess sex cults” cannot be transferred to Nammu. Likewise, royal tomb retainers at Ur are not evidence of sacrifice to her merely because the king Ur-Namma’s name contains hers.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### c. 2600–2400 BCE: emergence

Namma appears in the Fāra god list and Abu Ṣalābīkh hymns. Lugal-kisalsi builds a temple for her and calls her An’s spouse. At this stage she is a recognized goddess with at least one sanctuary, but the surviving early sources do not yet call her creator, ocean, or mother of Enki. **[Early attestation]**

### c. 2334–2112 BCE: Old Akkadian and post-Akkadian survival

Lexical databases register Old Akkadian attestations, but little narrative or institutional evidence survives. Her cult did not become prominent in Akkadian royal ideology. **[Sparse attestation]**

### c. 2112–2004 BCE: Ur III period

Namma occurs in Sumerian incantations associated with Eridu’s magical circle. The name Ur-Namma gives her unusual royal onomastic visibility, yet no large state cult or securely identified Namma temple-building program is known. The evidence therefore supports ritual-magical continuity, not peak institutional dominance.

### c. 2000–1600 BCE: Old Babylonian transformation

This is the decisive period for the Nammu known today:

- *Enki and Ninmaḫ* gives her a central creation role.
- TCL XV 10 and related theology call her mother of heaven and earth.
- Incantations call her compassionate mother, mother of abzu, and patron of the ritual specialist.
- Administrative tablets document temple personnel, land, and offerings at Ur.
- God lists organize her within Enki’s family.

The peak is literary and theological rather than demonstrably demographic. **[Old Babylonian elaboration]**

### c. 1700–1100 BCE: Sealand, Kassite, and learned continuity

A Sealand temple and priesthood survive in legal memory; an unprovenanced Kassite-period dedication names her. God-list transmission increasingly fixes divine genealogies. Namma remains marginal in Akkadian narrative.

### Late second millennium BCE: displacement by newer creation systems

In *Atrahasis*, Mami/Bēlet-ilī performs maternal human creation. In *Enūma eliš*, Tiamat and Apsû become primordial waters and Marduk dominates creation. Namma is not polemically defeated; she is largely omitted and functionally superseded. **[Theological displacement]**

### First millennium BCE: antiquarian and ritual survival

Namma persists in:

- canonical *An = Anum*;
- bilingual magical texts;
- lamentations;
- hemerologies;
- shrines at Nippur and Babylon;
- a probable Esarhaddon-period Bāṣ cult;
- Nabonidus’s Esagil description.

This is not “extinction” but restricted survival in learned, magical, and embedded temple contexts.

### Hellenistic cuneiform afterlife

The ePSD lexical corpus registers the name in Hellenistic scholarly transmission. This indicates scribal knowledge, not necessarily an autonomous public cult. No Hellenistic narrative renaissance of Nammu is documented.

### End of cuneiform cult

There is no recorded imperial decree specifically suppressing Nammu. Her cult disappears with the long contraction of Mesopotamian temple institutions and cuneiform scholarship under Seleucid, Parthian, and early Sasanian rule. No evidence traces her as a named being through Syriac Christian, Mandaean, Jewish Babylonian, or Islamic folklore. “Christianization” or “Islamization of Nammu” would therefore be an invented narrative; the documentary trail had already become scribal and fragmentary.

### Present

Modern Mesopotamian polytheists and eclectic pagans sometimes worship Nammu, but no continuous community or hereditary ritual lineage connects these practices with an ancient temple. They are revivals constructed from publications and translations. Their scale cannot presently be quantified from reliable demographic research.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Rediscovery

Nammu returned to modern awareness through nineteenth- and twentieth-century publication of cuneiform god lists, royal inscriptions, incantations, and *Enki and Ninmaḫ*. The history of the royal name illustrates the process: **Ur-Engur** yielded to **Ur-Nammu/Namma** when lexical and syllabic evidence improved.

Samuel Noah Kramer popularized Sumerian creation literature; Thorkild Jacobsen integrated Nammu into a historically developing Mesopotamian theology; Wiggermann’s *Reallexikon* article supplied the most concentrated source inventory. Digital projects—ETCSL, ETCSRI, CDLI, ePSD, and ORACC/AMGG—then made the documentation broadly accessible.

### Goddess spirituality and comparative mythology

Late twentieth-century Goddess spirituality often presented Nammu as an original supreme Great Mother displaced by patriarchal gods. The ancient sources support primordial motherhood in Old Babylonian theology, but do not demonstrate:

- a prehistoric universal Nammu religion;
- political matriarchy;
- exclusive female supremacy;
- a documented historical campaign to overthrow her;
- identity with every water or mother goddess.

Comparisons with Egyptian Nun or Nut, Greek Gaia, Vedic Danu, and a Jungian “Great Mother” are modern comparative frameworks. They may be interpretively meaningful, but are not ancient syncretisms.

### Occultism

No evidence located places Nammu in Renaissance planetary magic, early modern grimoires, nineteenth-century Theosophy, or the original Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. Claims of a continuous “Nammu current,” inherited initiatory ritual, sigil, planetary correspondence, or secret grimoire tradition are **modern inventions** unless a modern author and publication can be named.

Contemporary occult sites sometimes assign her:

- blue or black candles;
- shells, saltwater, moon phases, serpents, chalices, or womb symbolism;
- a planetary or elemental correspondence;
- invocations represented as reconstructed Sumerian ritual.

None of these systems is documented in the ancient cult. Saltwater is particularly problematic because the philological debate includes freshwater/groundwater, and Nammu is not simply Tiamat.

### Modern reconstructionist religion

Modern Mesopotamian reconstructionism developed as part of late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century polytheist revival. Online practitioners explicitly distinguish source-based reconstruction from eclectic revival. Nammu appears as a primordial mother and magical-water power, normally through *Enki and Ninmaḫ* and Wiggermann. Modern prayers, home altars, candle colors, annual festivals, and personal revelations belong to contemporary religion, even where inspired by ancient texts.

The existence of modern devotees is documented, but no reliable evidence establishes large organizations, stable membership totals, or a universally recognized priesthood.

### Literature, art, games, and online culture

Nammu’s direct commercial footprint remains modest. Notable modern uses include:

- **Fate/Grand Order:** the name appears in the phrase **Nammu Duranki**, connected with the franchise’s reinterpretation of Tiamat. This deliberately merges or layers Nammu and Tiamat and has materially encouraged the popular claim that they are one goddess. **[Modern fictional syncretism]**
- **Nammu**, an independent downloadable game by Lucas Ograssi, uses the name for a modern interactive work. **[Modern commercial/artistic reuse]**
- Contemporary visual artists depict Nammu as a blue woman, mermaid, serpent, dragon, starry womb, or literal ocean. Because ancient iconography is unknown, all such visual types are modern.
- Recent ecological and Goddess-spirituality events present Nammu as an “ocean mother” symbolizing environmental interdependence. This is modern reception, not recovered cult doctrine.

### Ancient-astronaut and internet reinvention

Ancient-astronaut material often combines *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, *Atrahasis*, and modern genetics: Nammu allegedly directs extraterrestrial Enki to manufacture humans as slave labor by genetic engineering. The texts speak of divine skill, clay, birth goddesses, and labor substitution; they contain no spacecraft, extraterrestrial species, laboratories, DNA, gold-mining program, or genetic technology.

Recent “powerscaling” discussions call Nammu an infinite, “outerversal,” or “boundless” fictional being because she supposedly existed alone as all reality. This is a modern category mistake built from a synthetic cosmogony. No cuneiform source applies such metaphysics or rankings to her.

### Political and extremist appropriation

The search found no documented Nammu-centered nationalist movement, fascist or racist cult, terrorist appropriation, or state ideology. General pseudo-Sumerian and “Anunnaki bloodline” discourse can acquire racial or conspiratorial forms, but assigning those specifically to Nammu without a documented user would be unjustified.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Early decipherment and naming

The chief historical problem was separating the sign’s lexical value **engur** from the deity’s proper name. The obsolete royal reading **Ur-Engur** reflects an early stage of decipherment. Syllabic spellings supported *Nammu/Namma*, and Civil’s 1985 note helped establish *Namma* as the preferred modern normalization.

### Jacobsen

Thorkild Jacobsen’s *The Treasures of Darkness* framed Mesopotamian gods historically as changing religious metaphors. His account was influential in presenting Nammu as primordial water and cosmic mother. Its broad developmental reconstructions remain valuable but should not be mistaken for direct translations of one source.

### Wiggermann

Frans A. M. Wiggermann’s “Nammu,” *Reallexikon der Assyriologie* 9 (1998–2001), 135–140, remains the indispensable dossier. It distinguishes:

- name from ENGUR;
- early cult from later cosmogonic titles;
- Nammu from Ninmaḫ;
- Sumerian magical prominence from Akkadian absence;
- direct evidence from reconstruction.

### Lambert

Wilfred G. Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths* analyzes the combination and displacement of older theogonies in *An = Anum* and *Enūma eliš*. His formulation that Tiamat was substituted for Nammu captures functional history, but should not be simplified into “Nammu’s Babylonian name was Tiamat.” [The British Academy’s account of Lambert’s posthumously published edition explains its reconstruction from his collations and drawings.](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/documents/1524/14_Lambert_1820.pdf)

### Lisman and Ceccarelli

Jan Lisman’s *Cosmogony, Theogony and Anthropogeny in Sumerian Texts* questions how early the full creator-ocean concept can be projected. Manuel Ceccarelli’s “Bemerkungen zu Namma und weiteren Wassergottheiten” argues for personified groundwater and critiques Lambert’s etymology. These studies sharpen the distinction between:

- a third-millennium divine name;
- Old Babylonian creator theology;
- later cosmic-ocean interpretation.

### Major debates

#### Was she literally the primeval sea?

- **For:** ENGUR writing, cosmic maternal titles, location in the deep, relation to Enki.
- **Against an unqualified equation:** texts make her mother of engur and abzu; no source simply narrates “Nammu was the sea”; the water may be groundwater rather than salt sea.
- **Best conclusion:** strong water-cosmogonic association, exact ontology unresolved.

#### Did she give birth to An and Ki?

The title “mother who gave birth to heaven and earth” is secure. Whether it means two anthropomorphic children named An and Ki or the total cosmos *an-ki* is debated. Detailed modern family trees go beyond the grammar.

#### Did she create humanity?

Yes, in *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, but creation is collaborative. Nammu proposes, kneads, bears, and decrees fate; Enki designs and directs; Ninmaḫ assists and imposes work; other birth goddesses attend. Statements that either Nammu alone or Enki alone created humanity flatten the poem.

#### Was she An’s normal wife?

No. One Early Dynastic inscription says so. Uraš and Antum are much better established as An/Anu’s spouses in other periods. Nammu’s marriage is a real but isolated regional tradition.

#### Is Nammu Tiamat?

No explicit ancient equation survives. They are historically comparable and Tiamat assumes a related structural role. Identity is a modern simplification.

#### Was Ur-Namma named for her?

The spelling strongly favors a theophoric construction with the goddess. The exact semantic value of *ur*—servant, man, warrior, dog, or lion—is debated. The royal name cannot prove a powerful state cult.

### State of the evidence

Primary evidence is distributed among:

- CDLI images and catalog records;
- ORACC projects, including ePSD, ETCSRI, and AMGG;
- ETCSL literary composites;
- museum tablets in Philadelphia, London, Istanbul, Paris, and other collections;
- UET tablets from Ur;
- published copies such as CT, TCL, PBS, VS, and god-list editions.

Many crucial claims depend on school-tablet composites rather than a single complete manuscript. Several cult texts are administrative fragments. Nammu’s iconography is unidentified. These limitations make negative conclusions—no weapon, no sacred animal, no independent epic, no Tiamat equation—substantive findings.
