# Pazuzu: Research Dossier

**Class:** A named male *demon* or demonic divinity—more specifically a royal wind-demon (*šar lilê lemnūti*, “king of the evil *lilû*-demons”)—in first-millennium BCE Assyrian and Babylonian magical tradition. Some inscriptions write his name with the divine determinative 𒀭 (*d*), but this does not turn him into a temple god in the institutional sense; Mesopotamian categories allowed supernatural beings conventionally translated “demons” to possess divine status and numinous power.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Pazuzu’s earliest securely identified images come from the tombs of the Assyrian queens at Kalḫu/Nimrud, deposited near the end of the eighth century BCE. The earliest presently identified written reference is a Neo-Assyrian letter conventionally dated about 670 BCE. No secure second-millennium depiction or textual attestation is known. The first-millennium date is therefore not merely the date of surviving examples: on present evidence, Pazuzu appears suddenly, already bearing a developed name, iconography, and function. [Nils Heeßel, *Iconography of Deities and Demons: Pazuzu*](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_pazuzu.pdf)

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested:** stated or depicted in an identifiable ancient source.
- **Regional or object variant:** ancient but confined to particular objects, places, or recensions.
- **Learned systematization:** interpretation generated by ancient scribes or modern scholarship rather than a narrative myth.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned historical explanation not stated by the ancient evidence.
- **Modern invention:** a claim first documented in modern fiction, occultism, popular reference works, or online religion.
- **Unestablished:** possible, but the surviving evidence does not demonstrate it.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### 1.1 Orientation

Pazuzu is an Assyro-Babylonian wind-demon whose frightening force could be directed against other frightening forces. His own inscriptions call him the son of Ḫanbu or Ḫanbi and king of the evil *lilû*-demons. Incantations describe a destructive wind that harms land, animals, and human beings; amulets employ his head or whole figure to repel supernatural danger. He became especially associated with protection from Lamaštu, who was blamed for miscarriage, puerperal illness, infant disease, and death. That apparent contradiction is structural, not accidental: the dangerous being controls or intimidates dangers similar to himself.

There is no surviving narrative “biography” of Pazuzu, no temple mythology, and no cycle comparable to the stories of Marduk, Ištar, Enki, or Gilgameš. His dossier consists principally of incantations, ritual prescriptions, letters, amulets, plaques, seals, fibulae, pendants, molds, and statuettes.

### 1.2 Name and spellings

The normalized Akkadian form is **Pazūzu**, conventionally written *Pazuzu* in English. Attested syllabic spellings include:

- 𒉺𒍪𒍪, *pa-zu-zu*;
- 𒀭𒁀𒍪𒍪, *dPa-zu-zu*;
- 𒀭𒅆𒊒𒍪𒍪, a scribal spelling conventionally normalized *dPazūzu*;
- the variant *Pa-sa-su*, cited in etymological discussion;
- English and older European variants **Pazuza** and **Fazuzu**.

The opening self-identification appears in Akkadian as *anāku Pazūzu*, “I am Pazuzu,” and in bilingual or Sumerianized learned form as *gá-e Pa-zu-zu*. One clay head makes identification explicit with *annû qaqqad Pazuzu*, “This is the head of Pazuzu.” These inscriptions establish that the distinctive head and full-bodied monster are not anonymous “devils.” [Heeßel, IDD](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_pazuzu.pdf)

### 1.3 Etymology

**Unestablished:** No proposed etymology commands consensus.

Proposals include:

1. **Aramaic *pzz*, “to be agile, impetuous, or move rapidly.”** This would suit a mobile wind-being. The problem is chronological and linguistic: the derivation is possible but not demonstrated by an ancient gloss.

2. **Akkadian *pasāsu*, “to erase, obliterate, destroy.”** The spelling *Pa-sa-su* has encouraged this interpretation, which would suit the demon’s destructive aspect. It remains conjectural.

3. **Babylonian *pessû*, “dwarf.”** This has been used to connect Pazuzu to the broader family of apotropaic dwarf figures and, indirectly, Egyptian Bes. Phonological and historical proof is lacking.

4. **Bes or Bazi-related hypotheses.** Wiggermann explored a construction involving the early royal names Anbu, Bazi, and Zizi—yielding a chain from Anbu to Ḫanbu and from Bazi-Zizi to Pazuzu. This is an ingenious modern reconstruction, not an ancient etymology. The parallel proposal that Pazuzu and Egyptian Bes share a name or prototype is likewise disputed.

The appropriate conclusion is “etymology unknown,” followed by the proposals rather than a translated name.

### 1.4 Titles and classifications

- **“King of the evil *lilû*-demons”**—Akkadian *šar lilê lemnūti*; securely attested in his standard self-introduction.
- **“Son of Ḫanbu/Ḫanbi”**—securely attested genealogy.
- **Wind-demon**—secure description of function, although translating *lilû* simply as “wind-demon” can conceal the wider Mesopotamian category of roaming, disease-bearing spirits.
- **Divine demon/demonic divinity**—a useful modern description reflecting use of the divine determinative and his supernatural kingship.
- **“Demon of the southwest wind”**—traditional modern description, but historically problematic. It derives partly from François Lenormant’s nineteenth-century interpretation of an incantation.
- **Cold northeasterly wind**—the interpretation favored by Pascal Charlier and Nils Heeßel because the texts associate Pazuzu with mountains, cold, frost, and winter destruction. It is a modern scholarly reconstruction, not an ancient meteorological label written beside his name.
- **West-wind personification**—too categorical. His standard inscription says that winds encountered by him had their faces set westward, not simply “I am the West Wind.”

No ancient source calls him Satan, Lucifer, a fallen angel, a jinn, or a “god of locusts.” Those are modern translations or inventions. No secure ancient epithet makes him Lamashtu’s husband.

### 1.5 Cultural location

Calling Pazuzu “Sumerian” is misleading. Some Pazuzu spells employ Sumerian or bilingual learned language, but all secure attestations are first-millennium BCE Assyrian or Babylonian, long after Sumerian ceased to be vernacular. He is absent from the preserved third- and second-millennium Sumerian literary corpus.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 Earliest material evidence

The earliest securely datable objects are from the royal tombs at Nimrud:

- two Pazuzu heads;
- a fibula;
- a necklace bearing fifteen small heads and three small statuettes.

Their funerary contexts date near the end of the eighth century BCE. These objects prove that Pazuzu’s image was already recognizable, portable, and protective. They do not establish an earlier oral myth, a public cult, or a temple. Attempts to identify a figure in the second-millennium temple at Tell al-Rimah as a Pazuzu precursor were challenged by Anthony Green; the identification is not accepted as secure. [Heeßel, IDD, chronological range](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_pazuzu.pdf)

### 2.2 Earliest textual evidence

The oldest presently identified textual reference is a Neo-Assyrian letter of approximately 670 BCE. A later Neo-Assyrian letter concerning instruction given to a goldsmith’s son mentions placing black-wood amulets, a neck-stone, and “Pazuzu-heads” on a person’s head. This is invaluable because it locates Pazuzu manufacture and ritual use within a professional network of scholars, diviners, exorcists, and metalworkers rather than a hypothetical congregational cult. [ORACC/SAA 16, “Divination Taught to a Goldsmith’s Son”](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/saa16/P334816)

### 2.3 Standard Pazuzu inscriptions

The central corpus is conventionally divided into standard inscriptions or incantations, with recensional variants often called Standard A and Standard B. They survive on amulets and in tablet copies from such centers as Nineveh, Kalḫu, Sultantepe, Babylon, and Uruk.

A representative self-introduction reads:

> *anāku Pazūzu mār Ḫanbi šar lilê lemnūti*  
> “I am Pazuzu, son of Ḫanbu, king of the evil *lilû*-demons.”

A longer recension continues, approximately:

> “I ascended the mighty mountain that shook. The winds among which I traveled had their faces set toward the west; one by one I broke their wings.”

Grammatical and manuscript variants matter. Some copies read “king of the evil *lilûs*”; another permits “king of my evil *lilûs*.” The mountain may be described as mighty, raging, or made to tremble depending on recension and translation. The inscription presents Pazuzu as a speaker exercising dominance over winds. It is neither a creation myth nor an account of his worship.

The Louvre statuette MNB 467, approximately seventh century BCE, carries a Sumerian or Sumerianized cuneiform version on the backs of its wings. Its provenance is not an excavated Mesopotamian context: it was acquired in Alexandria in 1872 from the Demetrio collection and is assigned stylistically to Assyria. [French Ministry of Culture object record](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/orient-cuneiforme/en/work-factsheet)

Two inscribed head-amulets studied by Zoltán Niederreiter carry versions of Standard B. One entered the Bibliothèque nationale de France collection in 1906; the other, Louvre AO 1197, was bought in Baghdad and donated by Gaston Maspero in 1883. Their textual variants help distinguish Assyrian and Babylonian production. [Niederreiter, “Two Pazuzu-head amulets”](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=6279228)

### 2.4 Ritual texts

The ritual corpus includes:

- prescriptions to fashion or wear a bronze Pazuzu;
- instructions for placing a Pazuzu head or pendant near a patient;
- a Late Babylonian prescription to make a Pazuzu necklace and hang it around a person threatened by Lamaštu;
- Uruk ritual material in which a bronze Pazuzu object protects a pregnant woman against miscarriage;
- rituals against illness, nightmares, ghosts, and the nonspecific category *mimma lemnu*, “anything evil.”

These texts support a wider apotropaic function than protection against Lamaštu alone. The British Museum notes that a “bronze Pazuzu” was prescribed for a laboring woman and that a stone example occurred in a grave necklace. [British Museum 132964](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1962-0514-1)

The surviving Lamaštu series contains only two textual passages explicitly associating Pazuzu with Lamaštu, although their iconographic association is much more frequent. This difference warns against turning a strong amuletic convention into a large lost narrative without evidence.

### 2.5 Omens, letters, and late texts

Incantations, letters, ritual prescriptions, and omens mention Pazuzu. Material and literary attestations continue through the Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid periods and into Seleucid Uruk. Objects from Parthian contexts have been proposed, but the dating and identification are ambiguous.

### 2.6 Texts in which Pazuzu does not occur

A corpus search must distinguish contextual literature from evidence about Pazuzu:

- **ETCSL/Sumerian literature:** no Pazuzu.
- **The Sumerian King List:** no mythic Pazuzu; Bazi and Zizi enter only a modern etymological hypothesis.
- **Inanna’s Descent:** no Pazuzu.
- **Enki and Ninmaḫ:** no Pazuzu.
- **Atrahasis:** no Pazuzu.
- **Enūma eliš:** no Pazuzu.
- **Gilgameš:** no Pazuzu. The epic’s winds and the apotropaic head of Ḫumbaba provide comparative material, not an encounter.
- **An = Anum:** no secure genealogy or pantheon entry establishing a developed Pazuzu theology.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP:** no royal campaign, temple foundation, or state cult devoted to Pazuzu.

The absence is historically important. Pazuzu belongs primarily to first-millennium ritual and material religion, not the canonical narrative epics.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### 3.1 Domain and powers

**Securely attested:**

- destructive wind;
- damage to cultivated land, livestock, and humans;
- disease-bearing atmospheric force;
- command over or defeat of other winds;
- power to repel Lamaštu;
- broader protection against illness and “anything evil”;
- protection of bedrooms, houses, pregnant women, mothers, infants, and patients.

His efficacy rested on controlled danger: a terrifying face confronts the approaching threat, while his royal authority over similar demons gives the image coercive force.

### 3.2 Standard form

Elaborate full-bodied representations combine:

- rectangular, partly canine head;
- open jaws, bared teeth, and protruding tongue;
- large, round, deep-set eyes;
- thick eyebrows;
- horns rising near the brow;
- human ears and horizontally cut beard;
- wrinkled or striated throat;
- emaciated canine or human torso with visible ribs;
- human shoulders and arms ending in predatory claws;
- animal or human thighs terminating in raptor talons;
- two pairs of wings;
- erect penis ending in a snake head;
- scorpion tail.

The common attacking pose raises the right arm and advances the left leg. Standing, sitting, kneeling, and crouching forms also occur. One variant raises the left arm. [Heeßel, IDD typology](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_pazuzu.pdf)

The image is often described loosely as lion-, dog-, or jackal-headed. “Canine jaws combined with a rectangular hybrid face” is safer than assigning it to one zoological species.

### 3.3 The head as *pars pro toto*

Detached heads greatly outnumber full statuettes. Suspension loops, pierced ears, attached fibulae, seals, and necklaces demonstrate bodily use. The outward-facing gaze transformed the wearer’s body or the room into defended space.

The Metropolitan Museum’s bronze pendant, 1993.181, dates to the eighth–seventh century BCE. Its suspension loop would make the face look outward when worn. [Metropolitan Museum 1993.181](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327485)

The British Museum’s copper-alloy head 132964, 10.55 cm high and dated 800–600 BCE, is much larger than a normal pendant and may have hung beside a bed or laboring woman. [British Museum 132964](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1962-0514-1)

Clay molds from Babylon and duplicate impressions show that some Pazuzu heads were mass-produced. Surviving materials include bronze, copper, gold, clay, limestone, gypsum, marble, serpentine, jasper, quartz, magnetite, chalcedony, lapis lazuli, hematite, bone, obsidian, and glazed composition.

### 3.4 Janus and four-faced forms

A small group has two opposed Pazuzu faces: a head, three seals, and a statuette. A bronze mace-head from Samos bears a face on four sides. These multiplied gazes plausibly extend protection in every direction; that explanation is interpretive rather than supplied by an inscription.

### 3.5 Geographic distribution

Heeßel’s catalogue contained 164 representations. Among provenanced objects, the largest groups came from Babylonia and Assyria. Outlying finds include:

- Susa and Nush-i Jan in Iran;
- Zincirli, Carchemish, and Sultantepe;
- Beth-Shean and Megiddo;
- the Edomite shrine at Ḥorvat Qitmit;
- Samos.

Objects reportedly obtained in Egypt do not prove Egyptian findspots. The Ashmolean statuette AN 1892.43 was allegedly from Tanis, but that provenance is not secure. Its later Aramaic inscription nevertheless shows adaptation of a Pazuzu figure in a Northwest Semitic magical setting. [Brandeis record of the Aramaic-inscribed statuette](https://scholarworks.brandeis.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Aramaic-inscription-of-the-Ashmolean/9924086493801921)

### 3.6 Non-attested correspondences

No ancient source securely assigns Pazuzu:

- a sacred color;
- a sacred number;
- a weekday;
- a planet or constellation;
- a festival date;
- a canonical plant;
- an independent animal cult.

Modern claims that his number is eight, his direction is southwest, or that particular incense and candles “belong” to him are modern occult correspondences unless supported by a separately identified ancient source.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### 4.1 Parentage

Pazuzu repeatedly calls himself **“son of Ḫanbu/Ḫanbi.”** That is the only securely attested genealogy.

Later reference works sometimes inflate Ḫanbu into “lord of all evil,” a primordial ruler of the underworld, a shadow-being, or an analogue of Satan. The Pazuzu inscriptions do not say this. No secure ancient narrative about Ḫanbu’s own parentage, deeds, spouse, or cult is known.

Pazuzu has no securely attested mother, spouse, sibling, or offspring.

### 4.2 Divine hierarchy

His title makes him king of evil *lilû*-demons. This is a demonic hierarchy expressed through royal language, not evidence that he headed the gods of an Assyrian city. No temple staff, divine household list, state offering schedule, or royal titulary places him among the major institutional gods.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Pazuzu has no surviving epic cycle. Three compact mythic structures can nevertheless be reconstructed from inscriptions and images.

### 5.1 The mountain and the broken wings

**Securely attested early material:** In his standard self-introduction, Pazuzu ascends or rages against a powerful mountain. The winds among which he travels face west; he breaks their wings one at a time.

The passage defines authority through movement, ascent, and mutilation of rival winds. Mountains mark the eastern or northeastern margin of the Mesopotamian world and the source of cold winter weather. On the older southwest-wind interpretation, Pazuzu emerged from arid lands and brought hot, crop-destroying winds. On the Charlier–Heeßel interpretation, he is associated more convincingly with cold winds descending from the Zagros.

The spell does not narrate why the contest began, name the defeated winds, or state that Pazuzu permanently became one of four cardinal winds. Attempts to supply those details are reconstruction.

### 5.2 Pazuzu and Lamaštu

**Securely attested, mainly iconographic:** Lamaštu threatens pregnant women, newborns, children, and patients. Pazuzu’s head is placed above or beside her image; on some plaques his full figure confronts her. Ritual offerings, incantations, protective gods, exorcists, and a boat combine to remove her to the wilderness or underworld.

A common modern retelling says that Pazuzu “defeated Lamashtu in battle.” The objects justify “confronts,” “repels,” or “compels her departure” more securely than a detailed combat narrative. Only two known Lamaštu-incantation passages explicitly invoke the association, while the visual evidence is abundant.

The Metropolitan Museum’s explanation of such plaques notes that many show Pazuzu driving Lamaštu back toward the netherworld. [Metropolitan Museum, Lamashtu amulet](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/326961)

### 5.3 The dangerous protector

This is not a single story but the theological logic uniting the corpus. Pazuzu harms fields, cattle, and humans as destructive wind; the same overwhelming nature makes him suitable to frighten or command other demons. The malformed, staring head is the active locus of protection.

Some amulets place Ugallu and Lulal behind or beside him. Heeßel suggests that these beneficent guardians may check Pazuzu himself. Crouching figures and deliberately broken clay faces may likewise represent containment or ritual neutralization. This interpretation explains why the user could invoke Pazuzu without treating him as benevolent.

### 5.4 Stories not belonging to the ancient corpus

The following are modern accretions:

- Pazuzu’s rebellion against named Mesopotamian gods;
- a marriage, sexual affair, or dynastic feud with Lamaštu;
- descent from Tiamat;
- identification as one of seven demon princes;
- an ancient possession of a girl comparable to Regan MacNeil;
- a command that speaking his name three times summons him;
- ancient sacrifices of humans to awaken him;
- membership in the *Ars Goetia*;
- a surviving secret cult extending from Assyria to modern demonolatry.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### 6.1 Was there a cult?

No temple securely dedicated to Pazuzu is known. No priesthood, festival, estate, tithe, regular sacrificial calendar, royal building inscription, or city patronage is attested. “Cult” is therefore potentially misleading if it implies an institution comparable to Marduk’s Esagila or Ištar’s Eanna.

What is abundantly attested is **apotropaic and healing practice**:

- personal pendants;
- necklaces;
- fibulae;
- stamp seals;
- bedroom plaques;
- freestanding or suspended heads;
- statuettes;
- ritual manufacture prescribed by exorcistic texts;
- household and funerary deposits.

### 6.2 Practitioners and production

Texts connect Pazuzu objects with ritual specialists and goldsmiths. The *āšipu* or exorcist supplied ritual knowledge; craftsmen translated it into bronze, stone, glazed composition, or clay. Molds and near-identical products show organized manufacture and broad consumption.

A letter about a goldsmith’s son mentions Pazuzu heads among amulets placed on the head. This demonstrates instruction and professional transmission, but not a hereditary Pazuzu priesthood. [ORACC/SAA 16](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/saa16/P334816)

### 6.3 Pregnancy and childbirth

Pazuzu objects were placed around the neck, pinned to clothing, or suspended near women in labor. Their function was to bar Lamaštu and related causes of miscarriage or infant sickness. The interpretation is supported both by ritual prescriptions and by the object forms.

One should not infer that all female burials containing Pazuzu objects belonged to women who died in childbirth, nor that every head was exclusively obstetric. The *mimma lemnu* and house-protection evidence demonstrates broader use.

### 6.4 House and sickroom

Lamaštu plaques show a sick person on a bed, ritual officiants, protective symbols, and the removal of the demoness. Pazuzu appears at the top or on the reverse, surveying the scene. His image could also be installed against supernatural beings attempting to enter a house.

### 6.5 Offerings

Objects shown around Lamaštu—comb, spindle, pin, food, and animals—are gifts intended to appease or equip her for departure. They are not necessarily offerings to Pazuzu. No regular sacrificial menu for Pazuzu is securely documented.

### 6.6 Burial

Pazuzu heads and figures occur in graves, most famously the Nimrud royal tombs. Their presence supports continued protection of the dead or the burial assemblage. It does not prove a funerary theology in which Pazuzu judged or escorted souls.

### 6.7 Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and violence

**Absence of evidence:** No text, burial, iconographic scene, or archaeological context documents human sacrifice to Pazuzu. There is likewise no evidence for temple prostitution attached to him, cult warfare in his name, or state persecution conducted by his worshippers.

Animal substances could occur in Mesopotamian healing and anti-demonic rituals, but that general fact must not be converted into a Pazuzu sacrificial cult without a specific prescription.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### 7.1 Before approximately 750 BCE

No secure Pazuzu. Mesopotamia already possessed ancient traditions about winds, *udug/utukku*, *lilû*-spirits, Lamaštu, lion-demons, grotesque guardians, and the apotropaic head of Ḫumbaba. These are the cultural materials from which Pazuzu could emerge, not earlier attestations of him.

### 7.2 Late eighth century BCE: sudden appearance

Pazuzu appears fully formed in the Nimrud royal tomb assemblages. His image already includes the characteristic face and portable protective function. This argues against a slow iconographic evolution visible in surviving evidence, though an undocumented development remains possible.

### 7.3 Seventh–sixth centuries BCE: maximum visibility

This is the best-attested phase:

- large numbers of heads and full figures;
- Assyrian and Babylonian production;
- Standard A and B inscriptions;
- letters and ritual prescriptions;
- Lamaštu plaques;
- distribution to Iran, the Levant, Anatolia, and Samos;
- manufacture in numerous materials and molds for repeated production.

Calling this the “peak” is justified by the surviving concentration of objects, not by population statistics.

### 7.4 Achaemenid and Seleucid periods

Pazuzu heads continue in Achaemenid and Achaemenid–Seleucid contexts. Seleucid scholarly texts at Uruk retain Pazuzu traditions. The latest secure examples demonstrate several centuries of endurance after Assyria’s political fall in 612 BCE.

### 7.5 Possible Parthian survival

Some objects have been attributed to Parthian contexts, but disturbed deposits, residual objects, and uncertain identification make continuity ambiguous. No securely demonstrated Parthian temple or organized Pazuzu cult is known.

### 7.6 Disappearance

The record thins as cuneiform scholarly culture and the associated amulet industries disappeared. There is no documented episode of Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, or Muslims specifically suppressing a Pazuzu temple. Nor is there a demonstrated chain carrying his name through Syriac, Mandaean, Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, or Islamic magical manuscripts.

Late-antique incantation bowls preserve many Mesopotamian magical structures and demonological continuities, but continuity of genre is not continuity of the named Pazuzu.

### 7.7 Nineteenth-century rediscovery

Excavation, antiquities collecting, and decipherment returned Pazuzu objects to European scholarly attention. Important landmarks include:

- Louvre MNB 467 acquired in Alexandria in 1872;
- early publication by François Lenormant in 1874;
- Louvre AO 1197 acquired in Baghdad and donated in 1883;
- the Ashmolean statuette acquired in 1892;
- Scheil’s publication of an inscribed head in 1894;
- Thureau-Dangin’s 1921 work on Labartu/Lamaštu rituals and amulets;
- Karl Frank’s 1941 *Lamaštu, Pazuzu und andere Dämonen*.

### 7.8 Present-day revival

Small numbers of modern pagans, reconstructionists, demonolaters, and occultists invoke or worship Pazuzu. Online testimony establishes contemporary practice, but not scale, institutional continuity, or derivation from an unbroken ancient community. These are modern revivals built from archaeology, popular culture, and new ritual composition.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### 8.1 *The Exorcist*

William Peter Blatty’s novel *The Exorcist* appeared in 1971. Its opening at an Iraqi excavation establishes an ancient adversary recognized by Father Merrin. In the book, the possessing demon is identified as Pazuzu, “demon of the southwest wind.” Blatty combines Mesopotamian imagery with Roman Catholic possession theology.

William Friedkin’s film appeared in 1973. A monumental winged statue confronts Merrin at Hatra, and brief shots of the demon’s white face appear during Regan’s possession. The statue was a modern production prop, not a reconstruction of a colossal ancient cult statue. Ancient Pazuzu figures are ordinarily small and portable.

Friedkin later described adding the shot of the “Mesopotamian devil called Pazuzu” to connect the Iraqi prologue with Regan’s bedroom. [Directors Guild of America interview](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0803-fall-2008/shot-to-remember-the-exorcist)

The adaptation reverses the ancient protective emphasis:

| Ancient corpus | Blatty/Friedkin |
|---|---|
| Dangerous wind-demon deployed against other demons | Possessing demon cast within Christian evil |
| Protects women and children from Lamaštu | Possesses and torments a girl |
| Small amulets and statuettes | Colossal desert idol |
| No Catholic or Satanic identity | Adversary overcome through Catholic exorcism |
| No ancient Regan narrative | Modern fictional narrative |

This is not simply a “mistake.” It is a documented modern reinvention that became the dominant global image of Pazuzu. The film itself never clearly speaks the name “Pazuzu”; recognition depends on the novel, production history, sequels, and commentary.

Blatty’s and Friedkin’s success generated *Exorcist II: The Heretic* (1977), where Pazuzu becomes entangled with locust symbolism; later novels, films, television adaptations, and *The Exorcist: Believer* expanded or altered the demonology. Locusts are especially a development of the film franchise, not Pazuzu’s primary ancient emblem.

### 8.2 Earlier visual reception

A Pazuzu-like ancient image appears among historical “evil spirits” in Benjamin Christensen’s *Häxan* (1922), although it is not developed as a named character. Thus 1973 was the decisive popular revival, not necessarily the first cinematic reproduction of the image.

### 8.3 Games, animation, comics, and music

Post-*Exorcist* popular culture includes:

- **Dungeons & Dragons:** Pazuzu becomes a demon lord or prince with a wholly new fantasy genealogy and cosmology.
- **Futurama:** Professor Farnsworth’s gargoyle is named Pazuzu, turning the name into comedy.
- **The Simpsons, “Treehouse of Horror XXVIII” (2017):** a Pazuzu statue possesses Maggie in a direct parody of *The Exorcist*.
- **DC and Marvel-related occult fiction, manga, anime, and videogames:** the name is repeatedly assigned to demons, monsters, or summonable beings.
- **Gorillaz:** the Pazuzu figure became part of Jamie Hewlett’s visual vocabulary and live-stage imagery.
- **Extreme-metal and industrial music:** the name and face function as shorthand for ancient demonic power.

These uses normally descend from *The Exorcist* plus museum photography, not directly from the cuneiform corpus.

### 8.4 Modern occultism

Pazuzu is absent from the medieval and early-modern Solomonic grimoires and from the seventy-two spirits of the *Ars Goetia*. He is absent from Renaissance planetary magic, the foundational Golden Dawn tables, and classical Thelemic demon lists.

Modern “sigils of Pazuzu,” cardinal correspondences, planetary rulerships, enn chants, candle colors, summoning formulas, and claims that he grants wishes are therefore modern inventions unless an author clearly labels them as newly constructed practice.

Books marketed as *Pazuzu Rising*, a *Grimoire of Pazuzu*, or similar titles belong to contemporary demonolatry. The “Simon *Necronomicon*” and Lovecraftian magic freely combine Mesopotamian names with invented systems; they are not translations of ancient tablets.

### 8.5 Neopagan reconstruction

A historically cautious reconstruction could use translated incantations and replicas of protective heads, but it would still be a revival because:

- the ancient ritual system is fragmentary;
- no initiating lineage survives;
- the relevant professional institutions disappeared;
- modern practitioners often approach Pazuzu as a personal deity, whereas the ancient evidence primarily concerns coercive/apotropaic deployment.

### 8.6 Political and criminal namesakes

Individuals and groups have adopted “Pazuzu” as an alias because of *The Exorcist*. The American murderer John Alexander Lawson legally adopted the name Pazuzu Illah Algarad; this is a modern criminal self-fashioning, not evidence of ancient Pazuzu ritual. No major documented ancient or modern nationalist movement centers on Pazuzu.

### 8.7 Commercial use

Museum replicas, jewelry, tattoos, horror merchandise, role-playing miniatures, album art, and Halloween décor usually reproduce the Louvre statuette or Friedkin’s statue. Commercial products frequently advertise him as a “Sumerian god,” “demon of the southwest wind,” or Satanic entity; each description compresses or distorts the evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 9.1 Discovery and early scholarship

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century writers often treated Mesopotamian demons through Biblical or Christian categories. Lenormant’s 1874 work helped popularize the hot southwest-wind interpretation. Thureau-Dangin’s study of Labartu/Lamaštu rituals and amulets established a stronger philological basis.

Karl Frank’s *Lamaštu, Pazuzu und andere Dämonen* (1941) treated the interconnected demonological corpus. Henri Frankfort’s work on seals and later handbooks by Jeremy Black and Anthony Green placed the figure within Mesopotamian visual and religious systems.

### 9.2 Modern corpus studies

The decisive monograph is Nils P. Heeßel, *Pazuzu: Archäologische und philologische Studien zu einem altorientalischen Dämon* (2002). It combined textual and archaeological evidence and catalogued 164 representations. Heeßel’s later IDD entry offers a concise typology and chronology. [Digital IDD entry](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_pazuzu.pdf)

Frans Wiggermann’s *Mesopotamian Protective Spirits: The Ritual Texts* (1992) situated Pazuzu within apotropaic practice. His RlA article and “The Four Winds and the Origins of Pazuzu” developed the problem of Pazuzu’s sudden appearance and possible antecedents. [Digital edition of *Mesopotamian Protective Spirits*](https://sites.dlib.nyu.edu/viewer/books/brill_awdl000079/14)

### 9.3 Hot wind versus cold wind

- **Older interpretation:** hot southwest wind, desert heat, crop failure.
- **Charlier and Heeßel:** cold northeasterly wind from the Zagros, producing frost, disease, and winter destruction.
- **Best conclusion:** Pazuzu is securely a destructive wind-being, but reducing him to one modern compass label oversimplifies variable poetic descriptions.

### 9.4 Origin debate

Competing reconstructions include:

1. development from the imagery of the four winds;
2. succession to the apotropaic role of Ḫumbaba’s head;
3. influence from Egyptian Bes;
4. synthesis of existing Mesopotamian lion-demon, bird, serpent, scorpion, wind, and grotesque-guardian elements;
5. name formation from Bazi/Zizi and Ḫanbu/Anbu traditions.

The evidence favors a first-millennium synthesis but does not identify a single parent figure. The complete iconography’s sudden appearance is a problem to be explained, not permission to select an attractive precursor.

### 9.5 Bes comparison

Pazuzu and Bes share frontal grotesqueness and domestic protection. Some Pazuzu forms even display a curly, Bes-like beard. Conversely:

- their standard bodies are different;
- Pazuzu has four wings, raptor feet, scorpion tail, and serpent phallus;
- no bilingual equation identifies them;
- eastern finds of Bes and possible Egyptian finds of Pazuzu demonstrate contact, not derivation.

Egyptian influence remains possible but unproven.

### 9.6 Identification problems

Not every horned or winged demon is Pazuzu. Secure identification depends on inscription or the distinctive combination of rectangular canine face, bulging eyes, horns, striated throat, four wings, talons, scorpion tail, and serpent-ended phallus.

A figure on several Lamaštu plaques resembles Pazuzu but lacks key features and appears beneath a separate Pazuzu head. Heeßel interprets it as a human exorcist, perhaps wearing a lion skin or mask. Another anomalous figure has been called a local Pazuzu variant by Moorey and a deviant *kusarikku* by Wiggermann. The disagreement should remain visible.

### 9.7 State of the evidence

Major holdings include the Louvre, British Museum, Metropolitan Museum of Art, Vorderasiatisches Museum Berlin, Ashmolean Museum, Bibliothèque nationale de France, and collections in Iraq, Jerusalem, and archaeological repositories.

The corpus is uneven:

- many objects lack excavated provenance;
- antiquities-market histories complicate dating and authenticity;
- small heads can lose diagnostic features;
- old publications sometimes misidentify materials or duplicate molds;
- late contexts may contain heirlooms;
- texts are often broken and survive in several recensions.

The British Museum’s reassessment of object 117941, for example, corrected both its reported material and an asserted duplication. [British Museum 117941](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1926-0410-6)

### 9.8 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Pazuzu was a major Sumerian god.
- He appears in *Gilgameš*, *Enūma eliš*, *Atrahasis*, or *Inanna’s Descent*.
- He is the son of Satan or an ancient form of Lucifer.
- Ḫanbu was securely “lord of all evil.”
- Lamashtu was his wife.
- He demanded human sacrifice.
- An organized cult continuously survived into modern demonolatry.
- He belongs to the *Ars Goetia*.
- The giant statue in *The Exorcist* reproduces an excavated Assyrian cult statue.
- His ancient sacred number was eight.
- He was exclusively evil or exclusively benevolent.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Historical attestation |
|---|---|
| Third–second millennia BCE | Winds, *lilû*-spirits, Lamaštu, Ḫumbaba heads, and apotropaic monsters are attested, but Pazuzu is not. |
| Late eighth century BCE | Earliest secure Pazuzu images deposited in the royal tombs at Nimrud: heads, fibula, and necklace ornaments. |
| Approximately 700–670 BCE | Pazuzu imagery spreads in Assyria; first textual references appear. |
| Approximately 670 BCE | Earliest presently identified written reference in a Neo-Assyrian letter. |
| Seventh century BCE | Standard self-identifying inscriptions, ritual texts, amulets, and plaques proliferate at Nineveh, Assur, Nimrud, Sultantepe, Babylonian cities, and neighboring regions. |
| Seventh–sixth centuries BCE | Principal period of surviving Pazuzu objects; widespread use against Lamaštu and “anything evil.” |
| 612 BCE | Fall of Nineveh; Pazuzu practice continues in Babylonian contexts and is not dependent on the Assyrian state. |
| Sixth–fourth centuries BCE | Continued Late Babylonian and Achaemenid production; molds and portable amulets attest ongoing use. |
| Third–second centuries BCE | Seleucid Uruk scholars copy or preserve Pazuzu-related magical material; late objects continue. |
| Parthian period | Possible survival, but evidence is ambiguous. |
| Late antiquity | Mesopotamian magical traditions continue in new languages and media, but the named Pazuzu is not securely continuous. |
| 1872 | Louvre MNB 467 acquired in Alexandria from the Demetrio collection. |
| 1874 | Lenormant discusses Pazuzu and helps establish the southwest/hot-wind interpretation. |
| 1883 | AO 1197 enters the Louvre through Gaston Maspero after purchase in Baghdad. |
| 1892 | Ashmolean AN 1892.43 acquired; purported Tanis provenance remains uncertain. |
| 1894 | Scheil publishes an inscribed Pazuzu head. |
| 1921 | Thureau-Dangin publishes foundational work on Labartu/Lamaštu ritual and amulets. |
| 1941 | Karl Frank publishes *Lamaštu, Pazuzu und andere Dämonen*. |
| 1962 | British Museum acquires the large bronze head now numbered 132964. |
| 1971 | William Peter Blatty publishes *The Exorcist*, recasting Pazuzu as a possessing demon in Catholic horror. |
| 1973 | William Friedkin’s film makes the modern Pazuzu globally recognizable. |
| 1977 | *Exorcist II: The Heretic* elaborates the locust association. |
| 1992 | Charlier argues for identification with cold rather than hot wind; Wiggermann publishes *Mesopotamian Protective Spirits*. |
| 2002 | Heeßel publishes the comprehensive archaeological and philological monograph. |
| 2004 | Wiggermann’s RlA article synthesizes the evidence. |
| 2007 | Wiggermann publishes “The Four Winds and the Origins of Pazuzu”; Heeßel’s IDD entry summarizes 164 representations. |
| 2017 | Niederreiter publishes two Standard-B-inscribed head amulets and analyzes Assyrian/Babylonian variants. |
| 2017 | *The Simpsons* parodies *The Exorcist* with a Pazuzu statue possessing Maggie. |
| 2018–present | Scholarship increasingly studies materiality, mass production, bodily use, visual agency, and the interaction of inscription and image. |
| Present | Museum display, horror media, games, merchandise, neopaganism, and demonolatry sustain multiple modern Pazuzus, none demonstrably continuous with an ancient institutional cult. |
