# Girra (Gibil): Research Dossier

**Class:** male Mesopotamian god—or, more precisely, two originally distinguishable male gods of fire and light, Girra and the conventionally read “Gibil,” who were merged by the later second millennium BCE.

**Earliest secure attestation:** divine-name writings conventionally normalized **Gibil** occur in Early Dynastic III administrative and offering texts from Šuruppak/Fara, approximately 2600–2500 BCE, and in the roughly contemporary *Zame Hymns* from Abu Ṣalabikh. The pronunciation *Gibil* is not independently secure: the written form is principally **𒀭𒉈𒄀** (*d*NE.GI, also inverted *d*BIL.GI). Girra and Gibil remain separate in an Old Babylonian god list; their equation is first explicit in the Assyrian recension of the Weidner god list. Thus “Girra (Gibil)” is historically convenient but not an original, timeless identity. [ORACC’s synthesis and bibliography](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/girra/) identifies Early Dynastic III evidence from Fara and Telloh and traces the cult through Seleucid times.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Girra/Gibil is the divine embodiment and administrator of controlled and uncontrolled fire: hearth and furnace, kiln and metallurgical fire, torchlight, ritual purification, the destruction of harmful ritual objects, and the burning of fields and cities. He is not merely an abstract “elemental.” In incantations fire is an active divine judge that discovers, binds, burns, and annihilates witchcraft; in construction inscriptions it bakes bricks; in royal war poetry it consumes enemy territory; in myth it can kill a hostile being and transform her place in the cosmos.

The equation “Gibil = blazing reed/torch,” once repeated confidently from sign-by-sign etymology, is a learned interpretation rather than a demonstrated historical etymology. The older *Reallexikon* discussion connected GI “reed” and BIL “burn,” hence “torch”; present caution is warranted because cuneiform divine names cannot automatically be etymologized from whichever sign values suit a translation.

Akkadian *Girra* is ordinarily related to *girru*, “fire,” ultimately to a Semitic root conventionally represented as \**ḥrr*, “burn/scorch.” A proposed connection between Girra and **Erra**, whose name has likewise been explained through scorching, remains disputed. The writing *d*GÌR.RA has been interpreted as a Sumerian rendering of an Erra-name, but functional resemblance and etymological resemblance do not establish that Girra and Erra were originally one god.

### Historical identifications

- **Girra and Gibil:** **syncretistic identification**, probably completed in or shortly after the Old Babylonian period. TCL 15, 10 distinguishes them; the Assyrian Weidner-list recension equates *d*BIL.GI with Girra.
- **Girra and Nuska:** **later syncretism/functional overlap**. Both concern light, fire, lamps, night, and purification. Girra shared Nuska’s Nippur sanctuary, but texts can still invoke them separately, so “equation” must not erase their separate identities.
- **Girra/Gibil and Marduk:** **learned theological absorption** in *Enūma eliš* VII: Gibil/Girra becomes one of Marduk’s fifty names.
- **Girra and Šamaš:** **close ritual association, not complete identity**. Incantations call Girra Šamaš’s associate; fire assists the sun god’s judicial work.
- **Girra and Erra:** a proposed etymological or conceptual relationship, not a secure equation.
- **Hephaestus/Vulcan or Agni:** modern comparative analogies. No surviving Mesopotamian source performs an *interpretatio graeca*, Roman equation, or Indo-Mesopotamian identification.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Early Dynastic evidence, approximately 2600–2350 BCE

The earliest written evidence is not narrative. Early Dynastic III administrative and offering lists from **Šuruppak/Fara** place *d*NE.GI among gods receiving institutional recognition. The name also occurs at Telloh/Girsu; at Lagash the evidence includes the theophoric personal name **Ur-Gibil**, “man/servant of Gibil.” Adab supplies an Old Akkadian offering-list occurrence and such names as **Geme-Gibil** and **Ur-Gibil**.

The forty-third *Zame Hymn*, preserved in Early Dynastic copies from **Abu Ṣalabikh**, is addressed to *d*NE.GI. Its cult-center line is disputed:

- Piotr Michalowski read it as an association with **Eridu**.
- Manfred Krebernik and Jan Lisman argue that **NE.GI nun ki** means approximately “NE.GI, pure place of the prince,” with NE.GI being an otherwise unknown city—not NUN^ki, Eridu.

This dispute matters: there is no independent temple archaeology that compels an Eridu reading. The hymn securely attests the god; it does not securely establish Eridu as his original city.

### Ur III and Old Babylonian evidence, approximately 2100–1600 BCE

An Ur III god list from Nippur and an Ur III text associating the god with Utu/Šamaš show continued scribal knowledge. Old Babylonian god lists are decisive for identity history: **TCL 15, 10** lists Girra and Gibil separately, showing that later interchangeability cannot be projected backward without qualification.

Old Babylonian Sumerian literary manuscripts include:

- **Inana and Ebiḫ**: Inana declares that she will make “Gibil, the purifier” work at Ebiḫ’s watercourses; fire ravages the mountain. The ETCSL translation preserves both the role and epithet.
- **Kusu A**: Gibil appears in a hymn centered on the purification deity Kusu, evidence for a ritual-purificatory divine network.
- Other Sumerian hymnic and lexical occurrences are generally brief; there is no surviving long, self-contained “Hymn to Gibil” comparable with the major hymns to Enlil or Inana.

The Old Babylonian Akkadian **Myth of Girra and Elamatum**, BM 78962, probably from Sippar or nearby Tell ed-Der, is the only surviving narrative centered on Girra. Its colophon indicates that the fragment belonged to the seventh section or tablet of a much larger work, perhaps originally about 350 lines. Only its conclusion is intelligible. Christopher Walker first edited it as “The Myth of Girra and Elamatum” in *Anatolian Studies* 33 (1983), 145–152; that title is modern because the ancient title is lost.

### Middle and first-millennium ritual corpora

**Maqlû**, “Burning,” is the principal corpus for Girra. Its Standard Babylonian recension consists of eight incantation tablets plus a ritual tablet, compiled from older material and transmitted chiefly through first-millennium libraries. Figurines representing a male and female witch—made from wax, tallow, bitumen, dough, clay, or occasionally metal, depending on the rite—are exposed to fire, mutilated, dissolved, or otherwise destroyed. The burning is juridical and apotropaic: it transfers the witch’s intended fate back to the figurine and alleged aggressor.

One incantation addresses:

> *Girra bēlu gitmālu, nannārāta, nabî šumka*  
> “Girra, perfect lord, you are radiant; your name is invoked.”

Another calls him:

> *Girra qāmû, qālû, kāsû, kāšid ša kaššāpāti*  
> “Girra, burner, scorcher, binder, vanquisher of witches.”

The practitioner says that two bronze figures of the warlock and witch were made through Girra’s power. In the rite’s culmination, Ea and Asalluḫi cleanse and release the patient, while Girra and Nuska protect and avenge. This is ritual violence in effigy, not evidence that accused human witches were burned alive. [ORACC’s *Maqlû* text](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/cmawro/maqlu/Q002706) preserves the invocation and bronze-figurine passage; a companion text calls Girra the burner and vanquisher of witches.

**Šurpu**, “Burning,” employs fire in rites against curses, impurity, and ominous misfortune. Tablet IX associates Girra with the torch. Here too the evidence is for ritual burning and purification, not human sacrifice.

Other corpora include:

- Fire-god prayers and incantations preserved in Neo-Assyrian library tablets, including a prayer calling Girra the great lord and “associate of Šamaš.”
- The anti-witchcraft and therapeutic corpora catalogued in the Corpus of Mesopotamian Anti-Witchcraft Rituals.
- Late Babylonian rituals, including the Seleucid-period **SpTU 2, 20**, which proves that the cultic figure still functioned in learned ritual.
- God lists, especially **An = Anum** and the Weidner list.
- The Canonical Temple List, which locates Girra in the Nippur sanctuary Emelamḫuš.
- Royal inscriptions: Sargon II invokes Girra’s role in brick firing, while a literary account of Shalmaneser III’s Urartian campaign makes Girra and Nergal accompany the king.

### Presence and absence in the broader canonical corpus

Several works named in the brief do **not** contain a major independent Girra/Gibil episode:

- **Atrahasis:** no secure independent Girra episode.
- **The Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgameš**, in Andrew George’s edition: fire is narratively important, but no secure major appearance of Girra as an acting character.
- **Inana’s Descent:** no Girra/Gibil episode. Confusion may arise because Šara, who interacts with Girra in *Anzû*, appears in the Sumerian descent story.
- **Enki and Ninmaḫ:** no secure Girra episode.
- These absences should not be filled by assuming that every mythic fire is Girra.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Securely attested functions**

- physical fire and light;
- torchlight and nocturnal illumination;
- furnace and metallurgical refining;
- brick firing and thus monumental construction;
- preparation or conveyance of divine food portions;
- purification of persons, places, and ritual objects;
- destruction of witchcraft and curse-bearing figurines;
- judicial exposure and punishment of evildoers;
- burning of vegetation, fields, hostile mountains, and enemy cities.

These functions encompass beneficial and destructive fire without making Girra morally dualistic. The same capacity that purifies a patient destroys the witch’s surrogate; the kiln enables civilization, while wartime flame destroys it.

### Characteristic instruments and substances

The **torch** is the only securely textual emblem. The flame, kiln, furnace, crucible, brazier, and burning figurine belong to his operational sphere. Ashwood is used to stir the fire in a *Maqlû* rite. Wax, tallow, bitumen, dough, clay, and bronze may become figurine materials, but none is a unique “sacred substance of Girra.”

No secure sacred animal, plant, color, number, weekday, or permanent celestial body is known. Claims assigning him salamanders, lions, red stones, Sunday, Mars, or a particular tarot card are modern correspondential constructions.

### Celestial aspect

Old Babylonian prayers to the Gods of the Night include Girra in an astral context. The *Girra and Elamatum* myth ends with Elamatum placed in the middle of heaven, and later scholarship therefore treats the story as partly astral. A later learned scheme paired Girra and Nuska with morning and evening aspects of Mercury before Nabu monopolized Mercury’s identity. This is a learned astral system, not the earliest definition of either god.

### Images

No anthropomorphic representation can be securely identified as Girra. The torch may denote him, but an isolated torch or flame on a seal or boundary stone cannot invariably be assigned to him; Nuska and fire symbolism overlap. Older handbooks occasionally assign a torch-bearing figure or lamp symbol to Gibil, but the identification is inferential. Frankfort’s seal studies remain useful for visual grammar, not for producing a securely labeled portrait.

Accordingly, there is no defensible canonical description of his face, clothing, beard, posture, or body. Modern images of a red-skinned, flame-haired smith god are inventions.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Competing genealogies

1. **Anu and Šala/Šalaš:** *Maqlû* II 136–137 calls Girra the son or firstborn of Anu and Šalaš. This is the clearest first-millennium ritual genealogy.
2. **Anu and Anunitu:** the Standard Babylonian *Anzû* calls Girra “Anunitu’s son.” Anunitu is an Ištar-like goddess especially associated with Akkad and Sippar-Anunitum.
3. **Enlil as father:** an Old Babylonian Akkadian text, BM 29383, and possibly a Sumerian literary passage support this variant.
4. **Abzu/Ea affiliation:** some incantatory traditions call him a son or product of the Apsû or closely attach him to Ea. This expresses purificatory and ritual theology; it must not be harmonized automatically with the Anu genealogy.
5. **An and Ki, An and Šala, or Iškur and Šala:** these combinations circulate in modern reference works, but the evidence is uneven. “An and Ki” is especially liable to arise from systematized family-tree construction rather than an explicit early birth narrative.
6. **Spouse Ninirigal:** *An = Anum* II 341 lists Ninirigal as his wife. This is a learned god-list relation; no surviving courtship or marriage myth accompanies it.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Girra and Elamatum

**Early attested narrative.** BM 78962 dates to the Old Babylonian period and probably to the reign of Ammisaduqa, in the seventeenth century BCE. The tablet was reportedly from Sippar or Tell ed-Der. Its colophon associates it with a scribe named Ilšu-iqiša and shows it was only the last portion of a substantially longer composition.

The recoverable conclusion says that Girra killed **Elamatum**, “the Elamite woman.” Enlil then fixes the consequences: she is to come forth and stand in the middle of heaven. The surviving text therefore presents death followed by transformation, restoration, or astral installation.

Interpretations remain disputed:

- Elamatum may personify political **Elam**, making Girra’s victory a Mesopotamian ideological triumph.
- She may embody famine, illness, or witchcraft, adversaries appropriate to Girra’s destructive-purifying role.
- She may be primarily an asterism, with the myth explaining its location.
- She has been connected with an “Elamite Bow” and with Ištar of Elam, but astronomical lists indicate that Elamatum and the Bow could be separate entries.

The missing six preceding parts prevent a full reconstruction. Claims that she caused a famine, that Ea ordered Girra to kill her, or that she received a particular annual festival depend on restoration and secondary synthesis and should be labeled **scholarly reconstruction**, not continuous surviving narrative.

### Inana’s destruction of Ebiḫ

In the Sumerian poem *Inana and Ebiḫ*, Inana attacks the insubordinate mountain. She announces that she will set fire to its forests and make “Gibil, the purifier” work at its watercourses. She then pours fire over the mountain’s flanks, thickening the smoke. Gibil is not an independently speaking character: he is the divine agency of her fire. This episode is crucial evidence that purification and devastation were not separate portfolios.

### The Anzû refusal

After Anzû steals Enlil’s Tablet of Destinies, Anu offers extraordinary honor to whichever god recovers it. Adad, Girra, and Šara are nominated in sequence. Each refuses because the stolen tablet allows Anzû to nullify hostile commands. Girra’s refusal is not simple cowardice in the text’s theology: it establishes the cosmic impossibility of the task and magnifies Ninurta’s eventual achievement.

This is a first-millennium canonical elaboration, although the Anzû tradition itself has older roots. A modern illustrated retelling that makes Girra immediately pursue and fight Anzû reverses the tablet’s plot and is a **modern invention**.

### Girra in *Erra and Išum*

The poem, conventionally dated to the early first millennium—often the eighth century BCE—and attributed in its own colophon to Kabti-ilāni-Marduk, concerns Erra’s catastrophic violence and Išum’s attempt to contain it. Girra is not its protagonist. His significant place is the purification of Marduk’s regalia: Marduk’s ornaments have become soiled, and their radiance must be renewed by the fire god.

The thread is important because it distinguishes three figures modern summaries often blur:

- **Erra** is the destructive warrior/plague god.
- **Išum** is Erra’s counselor and nocturnal herald, figuratively called a firebrand.
- **Girra** is the specialist whose fire purifies and restores divine brilliance.

### Gibil as a name of Marduk

In *Enūma eliš* VII, after Marduk defeats Tiamat, creates an ordered cosmos, and receives universal kingship, the gods proclaim fifty names. **Gibil/Girra** is the forty-sixth in common modern numbering. The accompanying explanation concerns maintaining or hardening the sharp point of weapons, presumably through furnace heat, though the Akkadian wording is damaged or difficult.

This is not a story in which Gibil helps Marduk fight Tiamat. It is late Babylonian systematic theology: Marduk absorbs the expertise and name of another god. Wilfred Lambert accordingly normalizes the name as Girra in his critical treatment.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult centers

**Šuruppak/Fara:** the earliest secure institutional context. Offering lists place the god alongside prominent local deities. This establishes cultic recognition but not a surviving temple plan.

**NE.GI or Eridu:** the *Zame Hymn* cult-center line is disputed. Eridu should be presented as a possible reading, not a settled archaeological fact.

**Adab and Lagash/Girsu:** sparse administrative and theophoric evidence.

**Nippur:** the best-documented later cultic setting. Girra shared **Emelamḫuš**, “House of Awesome Radiance,” with Nuska. The Canonical Temple List, usually dated in its formation to the Kassite period, is the principal evidence. This is the only specifically named sanctuary securely associated with Girra.

**Assyria:** first-millennium incantations, royal rhetoric, and library copies gave him an active learned and ritual presence, but there is no known independent monumental Girra temple comparable with the great temples of Aššur, Ištar, or Nabu.

**Late Babylonian Uruk:** ritual texts, including Seleucid copies, attest continuing scholarly-liturgical use.

### Personnel and offerings

No independent priestly college of Girra is securely documented. His rites belonged largely to the work of the **āšipu**, the ritual expert or exorcist, and to temple personnel managing lamps, offerings, furnaces, and purification. The surviving evidence does not establish hereditary “priests of Gibil,” distinctive vestments, or initiation rites.

Offering lists prove receipt of food and cultic goods. Fire’s description as giver of portions to the Igigi reflects both practical cooking/combustion and theological mediation.

### Anti-witchcraft ritual

In *Maqlû*, the patient and ritual expert fashion representations of the alleged witch and warlock. The sequence unfolds mainly at night and culminates toward dawn. Figurines are arraigned, burned, crushed, melted, or disposed of while Girra is asked to bind and consume the hostile spell. Ea and Asalluḫi cleanse; Šamaš judges; Nuska illuminates and protects; Girra burns.

The “witches” in these texts are accused invisible or human aggressors within Mesopotamian explanatory systems. The tablets document belief and ritual, not the factual guilt of any accused person.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

- **Human sacrifice:** no secure evidence connects Girra’s cult with human sacrifice.
- **Burning alleged witches alive:** not established. *Maqlû* burns figurines, not documented human victims.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence associates Girra with it, and the broader model of institutionalized Mesopotamian “sacred prostitution” is itself heavily disputed.
- **Animal sacrifice:** ordinary temple offerings may have occurred wherever he received cult, but no distinctive Girra-specific animal sacrifice is documented.
- **Cult violence:** royal texts could invoke his flame as an image of destroying enemy settlements. This is documented political theology, not proof of a standing cultic command to commit violence.

### Royal and economic uses

Kiln fire made monumental brick architecture possible. Sargon II’s construction rhetoric praises Girra in connection with brick firing. War inscriptions and royal poems transform burning cities and fields into divine participation. Girra thereby legitimates two opposed royal acts: building the king’s sacred capital and annihilating the king’s enemies.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Early Dynastic formation, ca. 2600–2350 BCE

*d*NE.GI/Gibil appears in the Fara religious economy, the *Zame Hymns*, offering lists, and personal names. Girra and Gibil cannot yet safely be treated as interchangeable. No complete narrative or recognizable image survives.

### Akkadian and Ur III continuity, ca. 2350–2000 BCE

Sparse offering-list and personal-name evidence shows persistence rather than major political promotion. Association with Utu/Šamaš becomes visible. The god remains below the highest pantheon tier.

### Old Babylonian differentiation and merger, ca. 2000–1600 BCE

TCL 15, 10 still distinguishes Girra and Gibil. Meanwhile Sumerian literary tradition calls on Gibil as purifier, and Akkadian narrative makes Girra the hero of the Elamatum myth. The Weidner-list equation belongs either to this period’s close or to a subsequent Assyrian reshaping. This is the critical merger phase.

### Kassite and Middle Babylonian systematization, ca. 1600–1155 BCE

Girra’s place at Nippur is formalized through the shared Nuska temple Emelamḫuš and canonical list traditions. The developing *An = Anum* system assigns spouse and family relations. Functional overlap with Nuska intensifies.

### First-millennium canonical florescence, ca. 1000–539 BCE

This is not necessarily the peak of popular worship, but it is the richest surviving textual period. Girra appears in *Maqlû*, *Šurpu*, *Anzû*, *Erra and Išum*, prayers, commentaries, royal literature, and *Enūma eliš*. The god becomes simultaneously:

- a highly active ritual power;
- a subordinate participant in canonical epic;
- an aspect absorbed into Marduk;
- a learned astral figure;
- a partner or partial double of Nuska.

Assyrian libraries preserved much of this evidence. The abundance reflects both ritual use and archival survival.

### Achaemenid, Hellenistic, and Seleucid survival, 539–ca. 100 BCE

Late Babylonian ritual tablets continue to invoke Girra; **SpTU 2, 20** supplies Seleucid-period evidence. There is no evidence of an abrupt Persian suppression of his rites. Instead, the cult survives inside increasingly specialized temple scholarship.

### Disappearance

There is no document recording a temple of Girra being forcibly closed by a named Christian or Muslim ruler. His institutional cult disappeared with the contraction and eventual cessation of cuneiform temple culture in late antiquity. General narratives of Christianization or Islamization cannot be attached specifically to Girra without evidence.

No medieval folklore securely preserves him under another name. Similar fire spirits or demons in later Near Eastern cultures are not automatically survivals.

### Present

There is no demonstrated continuous community of Girra worship. Individual modern pagans and occult practitioners sometimes address Gibil or Girra, but these are modern reconstructions based on translations and online reference works.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

Girra’s modern recovery began within Assyriology: excavation, museum acquisition, cuneiform decipherment, critical publication, and translations of ritual texts. Unlike Ištar, Tiamat, Gilgameš, or Pazuzu, he has not acquired a major literary, cinematic, or commercial identity.

### Scholarly and popular reception

- Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholarship classified him straightforwardly as a “nature god,” the personification of fire.
- Phenomenological handbooks expanded this into a unified elemental biography.
- More recent work asks how particular texts construct fire as divine agency and stresses that Girra and Gibil were initially distinguishable.
- Fantasy and myth-reference sites commonly describe him as “the Sumerian god of fire and forge,” often adding unverified imagery.
- Modern deity databases sometimes assign correspondences—red, sulfur, salamanders, Mars, Sunday, particular crystals or tarot cards—that have no ancient documentary basis.

### Occultism and neopaganism

No significant role for Girra has been established in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the classic European grimoire tradition. Claims that he possessed a continuous Western occult lineage are **modern inventions**.

Contemporary invocations of Gibil in eclectic paganism, Mesopotamian reconstructionism, or chaos magic are documented as modern religious creativity. They may draw authentically on *Maqlû* language, but:

- a solitary modern practitioner does not demonstrate an ancient-to-modern lineage;
- ritual correspondences absent from cuneiform sources are new;
- identifying Girra with Agni, Hephaestus, Brigid, Loki, djinn, or the alchemical element fire is comparative or syncretic innovation.

No reliable population statistics exist for modern worshippers.

### Political appropriation

Mesopotamian antiquity has been used by modern nationalisms, but Girra has not been shown to possess a sustained extremist or nationalist cult of his own. The absence of evidence should be stated rather than filling the category with general claims about Iraqi, Assyrian, occult, or “Anunnaki” movements.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The Girra–Gibil problem

Rintje Frankena’s *Reallexikon* entry “Girra und Gibil” long provided the standard synthesis. The decisive evidence now emphasized is:

1. early *d*NE.GI evidence conventionally called Gibil;
2. the Old Babylonian list preserving Girra and Gibil separately;
3. the Weidner-list interpretation equating *d*BIL.GI with Girra;
4. later interchangeability.

Johanna Tudeau dates the merger either within the Old Babylonian period or shortly afterward, perhaps in the context of Assyria’s political and religious rise. The exact moment remains uncertain because the relevant god-list recensions are copies, not transparent records of a single editorial event.

### Is “Gibil” the ancient pronunciation?

Jeremiah Peterson, Ryan Winters, Manfred Krebernik, and Jan Lisman have stressed that the conventional reading is not securely confirmed in primary sources. It remains serviceable as a scholarly label but should not be presented as unquestionably what Early Dynastic worshippers said.

### Nature god or textual role?

Peter Westh’s historiographical study uses Girra to question the apparently self-evident sentence “Girra is the fire god.” The sources unquestionably connect him with fire, but “nature god” can conceal differences between physical flame, ritual agent, legal executioner, divine personality, and scribal classification.

### Etymology and Erra

Roberts connected Girra’s name, and possibly Erra’s, with a Semitic root “scorch.” Lambert and others challenged aspects of the Erra etymology. Similar semantics do not prove common origin. Girra and Erra remain separate in first-millennium literature.

### Cult center

The *Zame Hymn* line is divided between an Eridu interpretation and an otherwise unknown NE.GI settlement. No archaeological discovery has settled the reading.

### Astral interpretation

Walker’s edition established the *Girra and Elamatum* fragment. Later astronomical study strengthens an astral dimension, but attempts to identify Elamatum precisely with the Bow, Ištar of Elam, famine, or sorcery remain reconstructions.

### Iconography

Earlier iconographic handbooks could assign flame or lamp symbols to fire gods too confidently. The present secure conclusion is negative: no anthropomorphic Girra image has been positively identified. The torch attribution is textual.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is distributed among:

- tablets from Fara, Abu Ṣalabikh, Nippur, Sippar/Tell ed-Der, Nineveh, Assur, and Uruk;
- the British Museum, including BM 78962;
- CDLI tablet records and photographs;
- ORACC’s AMGG, anti-witchcraft, lexical, literary, and royal-inscription projects;
- ETCSL’s editions of Sumerian literature;
- printed editions such as Walker’s *Girra and Elamatum*, Litke’s *An = Anum*, Abusch’s studies and Abusch–Schwemer’s *Maqlû*, Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths*, and George’s editions of temple lists and Babylonian literature.

Much has been lost: most of *Girra and Elamatum*; the context of many early administrative entries; almost all possible cult images; and the practical oral knowledge surrounding the rites.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Event in the documentary record |
|---|---|
| ca. 2600–2500 BCE | *d*NE.GI appears in Early Dynastic III offering and administrative texts from Šuruppak/Fara; roughly contemporary *Zame Hymn* 43 addresses the deity. |
| Early Dynastic III | Attestations at Telloh/Girsu and the theophoric name Ur-Gibil at Lagash. |
| ca. 2350–2200 BCE | Old Akkadian Adab offering list and Gibil theophoric names. |
| ca. 2100–2000 BCE | Ur III Nippur god-list attestation; textual association with Utu/Šamaš. |
| ca. 2000–1800 BCE | Old Babylonian god lists continue to attest Gibil; TCL 15, 10 distinguishes Girra from Gibil. |
| ca. eighteenth century BCE | Old Babylonian manuscripts of Sumerian literature preserve Gibil as purifier in *Inana and Ebiḫ* and in Kusu’s ritual sphere. |
| Seventeenth century BCE | BM 78962, *Girra and Elamatum*, copied probably near Sippar/Tell ed-Der; the surviving portion records Girra’s victory and Elamatum’s astral placement. |
| Late Old Babylonian or afterward | Weidner-list tradition explicitly interprets *d*BIL.GI as Girra, documenting merger. |
| ca. 1600–1155 BCE | Kassite-period temple and god-list systematization; Girra is associated with Nuska’s Emelamḫuš at Nippur. |
| Late second millennium BCE | Development or consolidation of *An = Anum*; Ninirigal is assigned as spouse. |
| Late second/early first millennium BCE | *Enūma eliš* VII absorbs Gibil/Girra as a name of Marduk. |
| Early first millennium BCE | Standard *Maqlû* and *Šurpu* traditions define Girra’s central role in burning witchcraft figurines and purification. |
| Probably eighth century BCE | *Erra and Išum* invokes the fire god’s power to renew Marduk’s regalia. |
| Ninth–seventh centuries BCE | Neo-Assyrian royal literature connects Girra with brick firing, enemy destruction, and Nergal’s accompaniment of Shalmaneser III. |
| Seventh century BCE | Nineveh library copies preserve major ritual and epic attestations. |
| Sixth–fourth centuries BCE | Continued Late Babylonian ritual and scholarly transmission. |
| Seleucid period, third–second centuries BCE | Uruk ritual tablets, including SpTU 2, 20, still invoke Girra. |
| Late antiquity | Cuneiform temple institutions cease; no specific decree suppressing Girra is known. |
| 1870s onward | Publication and reconstruction of Mesopotamian myth begins to restore the god to modern scholarship. |
| 1937 | Gerhard Meier publishes a foundational edition of *Maqlû*. |
| 1957–1971 | Frankena’s “Girra und Gibil” consolidates the earlier scholarly account. |
| 1983 | Christopher Walker publishes BM 78962 as “The Myth of Girra and Elamatum.” |
| 1990s–2000s | New temple-list, god-list, ritual, and literary editions refine the evidence; ETCSL and CDLI make primary material digitally accessible. |
| 2002 onward | Tzvi Abusch and later Abusch and Daniel Schwemer provide major historical and critical treatments of Babylonian witchcraft and *Maqlû*. |
| 2010s–2020s | ORACC integrates deity, ritual, lexical, and royal corpora; recent scholarship stresses the original Girra/Gibil distinction and uncertainty of the reading “Gibil.” |
| 2026 | No continuous cult is documented; small-scale modern reconstruction and eclectic invocation occur, without reliable demographic measurement. |
