# Inanna / Ishtar: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Goddess (*diĝir* in Sumerian; *iltu* in Akkadian), with numerous locally differentiated manifestations. She is associated above all with political sovereignty, warfare, sexuality, desire, celestial radiance, and the planet Venus.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The reed-bundle or ring-post sign conventionally read as Inanna occurs in proto-cuneiform administrative texts and seal imagery from the Eanna precinct at Uruk IV–III, approximately 3300–3000 BCE. This proves an institution or divine name associated with the sign, probably the tutelary power later called Inanna; it does not establish that all later myths and attributes already existed. The archaic Uruk corpus consists of administrative, lexical, and cult-economic records rather than narrative mythology. The earliest anthropomorphic images confidently associated with her belong to the later fourth and third millennia BCE, although individual identifications remain disputed. [Nissen, “The Archaic Texts from Uruk”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00438243.1986.9979973); [ORACC, Inana/Ištar](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/inanaitar/)

## Evidence labels

The labels used below concern the historical status of a tradition, not whether a divine event “really happened.”

- **[Early attestation]** Contemporary or near-contemporary evidence from the fourth or third millennium BCE.
- **[Later canonical]** A composition or theological formulation preserved principally in Old Babylonian or first-millennium scholarly transmission.
- **[Regional variant]** A city-, kingdom-, or language-specific form not automatically transferable to every Inanna or Ishtar.
- **[Learned systematization]** A god list, commentary, astronomical series, or priestly synthesis.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically traceable equation between formerly distinguishable deities.
- **[Late accretion]** Hellenistic, Roman, medieval, or early-modern development.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient assertion.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim demonstrably created or substantially reshaped in modern reception.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

Inanna was the paramount goddess of Uruk and one of the most important deities of Sumer. Ištar was an Akkadian-speaking Mesopotamian goddess whose name is related to West Semitic ʿAṯtar/ʿAštart but whose prehistoric formation remains disputed. By the later third millennium BCE, royal theology and bilingual scribal practice increasingly equated Inanna and Ištar. Thereafter the names commonly designate one translingual goddess, but local forms—Inanna of Uruk, Inanna of Zabalam, Ištar of Agade, Ištar of Nineveh, Ištar of Arbela, Annunītu, and others—could retain separate temples, cult images, personnel, genealogies, and political functions. The singular “Inanna-Ishtar” is therefore a useful scholarly shorthand, not proof of complete cultic identity at every date. [ORACC, Inana/Ištar](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/InanaIshtar/index.html)

### Names and writings

- **Inana / Inanna**, Sumerian: commonly normalized **Inana** by Sumerologists, conventionally written Inanna in general works. Spellings include `dINANNA`, `dINNIN`, `dIn-nin`, `dIn-ni-na`, `nin-an-na`, and phonetic variants. The archaic sign is derived from a twisted reed bundle or ring-post.
- **𒀭𒈹**, a common later cuneiform writing for Inanna: the divine determinative `𒀭` plus the goddess’s logogram.
- **Ištar**, Akkadian: written syllabically `dIš-tar`, `dEš₄-tár`, or logographically `dINANNA`; in first-millennium texts frequently `d15`.
- **Eštar, Ishtar, Issar**, modern or dialectal renderings. Assyrian phonology produced forms conventionally represented as Issar in proper names.
- **ʿAṯtar / ʿAštar**, cognate Semitic forms. Linguistic relationship does not by itself prove that every deity bearing a cognate name had identical mythology.
- **Annunītu**, “the skirmisher” or “martial one,” originally an epithet or manifestation of Ištar, especially at Agade and later Sippar-Amnānum; in some contexts she functioned as a distinct goddess.
- **Ninsianna**, Sumerian “Divine Lady of Heaven” or “Red Lady of Heaven” under competing analyses: the planet Venus personified. Ninsianna could be female or, in some later contexts, male, and was assimilated to Inanna-Ištar without being reducible to her in every early text. [ORACC, Ninsianna](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/ninsianna/)
- **Ninegalla / Bēlet-ekalli**, “Lady of the Palace,” sometimes an epithet or palace manifestation of Inanna-Ištar, elsewhere a distinguishable goddess.
- **Urkayītu**, “the Urukean,” a late cultic form.
- **Bēlet-Uruk**, “Lady of Uruk.”
- **Bēlet šamê**, “Lady of Heaven.”
- **Šarrat šamê**, “Queen of Heaven.”
- **Šarrat nipḫi**, “Queen of the Rising/Light.”
- **Ištar kakkabtu**, “Ištar the Star.”
- **Ištar ṣābittu / qarradtu**, martial formulations: “Ištar the warrior.”
- **Mullissu:** properly the Assyrian spouse of Aššur, derived from Ninlil rather than simply another name for Ištar. Modern summaries sometimes collapse her into Ištar because royal texts invoke both great goddesses.
- **Mylitta:** Herodotus’s Greek rendering in *Histories* 1.199, probably reflecting a title such as *Mulliltu*. It is not the normal Babylonian name for Ištar and cannot be used without qualification as evidence for her cult.

### Etymologies

**Inanna.** A traditional ancient or scholarly parsing is *nin-an-a(k)*, “Lady of Heaven.” Thorkild Jacobsen proposed “Lady of the Date Clusters,” connecting the archaic reed emblem with the date storehouse. The latter has not won general acceptance. Because archaic spellings predate the fully phonetic form, “Lady of Heaven” may be a secondary Sumerian interpretation rather than the historical origin of the name. **[Disputed modern reconstruction]**

**Ištar.** The name belongs to the Semitic *ʿṯtr* complex, represented by South Arabian ʿAthtar and Northwest Semitic ʿAštart, but the root’s ultimate meaning and the gender history of the deity are unresolved. Masculine Venus deities occur in some Semitic traditions; Mesopotamian Ištar is ordinarily feminine. It is safer to speak of cognate names and historically interacting cults than of one unchanged pan-Semitic goddess. **[Historical linguistics; origin disputed]**

### “Queen of Heaven”

Sumerian hymns call Inanna *nin-an-na* and related expressions, while Akkadian prayers use *šarrat šamê* and *bēlet šamê*. In the *Exaltation of Inanna*, the goddess is the mistress of the divine powers and possesses authority extending over heaven and earth. The title is securely ancient, but its use for other Near Eastern goddesses means that every occurrence of “Queen of Heaven” outside Mesopotamia cannot automatically be assigned to Ištar. **[Early/later canonical title; cross-cultural identification must be demonstrated case by case]** [Hallo and van Dijk, *The Exaltation of Inanna*](https://babylonian-collection.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/Hallo_Van%20Dijk%20%281968%29%20-%20Exaltation%20of%20Inanna_YNER%203.pdf)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Archaic and Early Dynastic evidence

**Uruk IV–III, c. 3300–3000 BCE.** Proto-cuneiform tablets from the Eanna precinct contain the sign later read as Inanna and document an economy centered on large institutional households. Seal impressions and monumental imagery include the reed-bundle emblem. These sources establish a cultic institution and name but provide no surviving Descent, marriage cycle, genealogy, or developed Venus theology. **[Early attestation]**

**Warka Vase, c. 3300–3000 BCE.** The carved alabaster vessel shows registers of water, plants, livestock, naked offering bearers, and a climactic encounter at the goddess’s reed-bundle emblems. The female figure is often identified as Inanna or her priestly representative. The vase securely documents offerings to the Eanna institution; whether the human figure is the goddess, a priestess, or a ruler’s ritual counterpart remains debated. **[Early iconography; identification qualified]**

**Early Dynastic god lists and inscriptions, c. 2900–2350 BCE.** Inanna appears in lists from Fara/Šuruppak and Abu Ṣalabikh, administrative offerings, royal inscriptions, and theophoric names. Her cults at Uruk and Zabalam are distinct enough to be named separately. Political invocations and military language are already present before the Akkadian dynasty. **[Early attestation]**

### Sargonic evidence and Enheduanna

**Old Akkadian period, c. 2334–2154 BCE.** Sargon and his successors prominently invoked Ištar, especially martial Ištar-Annunītu. Enheduanna, Sargon’s daughter and *en*-priestess of Nanna at Ur, is traditionally credited with Sumerian hymns exalting Inanna and linking the goddess to dynastic restoration. This period was crucial to the political and scribal assimilation of Sumerian Inanna and Akkadian Ištar. **[Early imperial syncretism]**

**The *Exaltation of Inanna* (*Nin-me-šara*).** The poem speaks in Enheduanna’s first person: Lugal-Ane expels her, Nanna remains unresponsive, and she appeals to Inanna, whose power restores the legitimate order. Its attributed setting is the late twenty-fourth or early twenty-third century BCE, but the more than one hundred surviving manuscripts are overwhelmingly Old Babylonian school tablets, c. nineteenth–eighteenth centuries BCE. Authorship by the historical Enheduanna is plausible and traditional, not demonstrable from an autograph. **[Tradition set in the Sargonic period; textually attested in Old Babylonian copies]** [Modern score-based translation](https://enheduana.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/exaltation_of_inana-2022.pdf)

Other compositions associated with Enheduanna include *Innin-šagurra*, *Inanna and Ebih* in some modern attributions, and the Temple Hymns. Attribution, especially beyond *Nin-me-šara*, remains debated.

### Principal Sumerian literary corpus

Most complete witnesses date to the Old Babylonian period, c. 2000–1600 BCE, even where the language, royal setting, or tradition is older.

- ***Inanna and Enki***, ETCSL 1.3.1: Inanna goes from Uruk to Eridu; Enki, made drunk during a banquet, gives her numerous *me*, socially constitutive powers or offices. His minister Isimud later tries unsuccessfully to recover them. [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.1.3.1)
- ***Inanna and Ebih***, ETCSL 1.3.2: the goddess demands submission from the mountain Ebih; An refuses or cautions her; she destroys it and celebrates her victory.
- ***Inanna and Šukaletuda***, ETCSL 1.3.3: after the gardener Šukaletuda sexually violates the sleeping goddess, she afflicts the land and pursues him until he is surrendered and killed. The text is evidence for divine judgment and bodily vulnerability, not for temple prostitution.
- ***Inanna and Gudam***, ETCSL 1.3.4: the destructive Gudam ravages Uruk; Inanna and other figures confront the crisis.
- ***Inanna and An***, ETCSL 1.3.5: fragmentary narrative concerning her acquisition of the Eanna sanctuary.
- ***Inanna’s Descent to the Netherworld***, ETCSL 1.4.1: approximately 400 lines in the composite text; Inanna descends, is stripped at seven gates, killed, revived through Enki’s intervention, and gives Dumuzi as substitute.
- ***Dumuzi’s Dream***, ***Dumuzi and Geshtinanna***, ***Dumuzi and his Sisters***, and ***Inanna and Bilulu***: connected but non-identical traditions of Dumuzi’s capture, death, lamentation, substitution, and vengeance.
- ***Inanna and Iddin-Dagan***, ETCSL 2.5.3.1: royal praise poem involving the goddess’s procession, ritual performance, and union with the king.
- The **Dumuzi–Inanna love songs**, ETCSL 4.08.01–33: courtship, erotic dialogue, bridal preparation, marriage, agricultural imagery, and royal ideology. [ETCSL hymns catalogue](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/catalogue/catalogue4.htm)
- ***The Exaltation of Inanna*** and the hymns conventionally called Inanna A–I: praise, warfare, cosmic power, gender reversal, kingship, and cultic titles.
- Royal hymns for Šulgi, Išme-Dagan, Iddin-Dagan, and other kings invoke Inanna as lover, patron, and distributor of kingship.

The ETCSL catalogue is the most convenient overview, but it is a composite electronic edition, not a substitute for collating individual manuscripts. [Complete ETCSL catalogue](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslfullcat.php)

### Akkadian corpus

- ***Descent of Ištar to the Netherworld*.** Akkadian adaptation, known principally from first-millennium tablets from Nineveh and Aššur, although the composition is likely older. Ishtar demands entry, is stripped at seven gates, and her confinement causes sexual reproduction to cease. Ea creates Aṣušu-namir to secure her release. Unlike the Sumerian narrative, the abbreviated ending alludes to Dumuzi and ritual mourning without making his seizure the fully narrated price of Ishtar’s return. **[Later canonical adaptation, not a direct translation]**
- ***Epic of Gilgamesh*, Tablet VI.** In the Standard Babylonian recension, assembled by the late second millennium and preserved mainly in first-millennium Nineveh tablets, Ishtar proposes marriage to Gilgamesh. He lists the disastrous fates of her former partners. She asks Anu for the Bull of Heaven, threatens to raise the dead if refused, and sends the Bull against Uruk; Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill it. [Andrew George publication record](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/446250/the-epic-of-gilgamesh-a-new-translation)
- ***Epic of Gilgamesh*, Tablet I.** Ištar is connected with Uruk and Eanna; the goddess Aruru, not Ištar, creates Enkidu in the Standard Babylonian text.
- **Akkadian hymns and prayers to Ištar:** include *Ištar 1*, the Great Prayer to Ištar, bilingual hymns, penitential prayers, incantations, and royal petitions. They emphasize radiance, judgment, victory, love, reversal of status, and the goddess’s terrifying anger.
- **Venus Tablet of Ammiṣaduqa / *Enūma Anu Enlil* Tablet 63.** First-millennium copies preserve Venus omens framed as observations from the reign of Ammiṣaduqa, conventionally seventeenth century BCE depending on chronology. The text documents the mature equation of Venus with Ištar/Ninsianna; its data cannot be used as a simple modern observational diary without textual criticism.
- ***An = Anum*.** The canonical god list, compiled from Old Babylonian precursors and stabilized in the later second millennium, devotes a long section to Ištar and her names, manifestations, family, and court. It is priestly classification rather than a narrative or a transparent map of everyday belief. [Krebernik, *An = Anum and Related Lists*](https://www.mohrsiebeck.com/en/book/an-anum-and-related-lists-9783161613838/)
- **Neo-Assyrian prophecies**, especially SAA 9: ecstatic speakers, many apparently women, transmit first-person words of Ištar of Arbela and related goddesses promising protection, legitimacy, or victory to Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal. [SAA 9 introduction](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/knpp/downloads/parpola_saa9intro.pdf)
- **Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions:** Ištar of Nineveh and Ištar of Arbela accompany Aššur in claims of conquest. Ashurbanipal describes a dream in which Ištar of Arbela, armed with bow, quivers, and sword, embraces him and goes to fight Teumman. [RINAP text](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/rinap5/Q003703)
- ***Enūma eliš*.** Ištar is part of the divine world of the poem but is not a principal actor in Marduk’s combat with Tiamat. Using *Enūma eliš* as a central Inanna myth is therefore misleading.
- ***Atrahasis*.** Its major divine actors are Enlil, Enki/Ea, the birth goddess, and the collective gods. Ištar is not the narrative center.
- ***Enki and Ninmah*.** This Sumerian composition concerns human creation and bodily conditions. Inanna is not a principal participant; modern discussions sometimes import her because another hymn credits her with changing men into women and women into men.

### Material supports and repositories

Evidence survives on clay tablets, stone inscriptions, foundation deposits, cylinder seals, plaques, votive sculptures, boundary stones, glazed bricks, and monumental reliefs. Major holdings include the Iraq Museum, Vorderasiatisches Museum Berlin, British Museum, Louvre, Penn Museum, Yale Babylonian Collection, Morgan Library, and collections holding Nippur tablets. Digital access is dispersed across ETCSL, ORACC/ePSD2, CDLI, RINAP, and SAAo.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Political sovereignty.** Inanna does not simply “rule kingship”; she grants favor, victory, and legitimacy, and may withdraw them. Enheduanna’s poem and royal hymns make her an arbiter of dynastic power. **[Third- and second-millennium attestation]**

**Warfare.** Martial epithets, weapons, roaring, storms, and the destruction of mountains occur in Sargonic inscriptions and Sumerian hymns. Assyrian rulers intensified this aspect, especially through Ištar of Arbela and Nineveh. Her warfare is therefore not a late masculine overlay, although its imperial uses changed. **[Early attested; regionally intensified]**

**Sexual desire and attraction.** Love songs, hymns, incantations, and the Gilgamesh epic make her a source of erotic power. “Goddess of fertility,” however, is too imprecise: she does not consistently govern grain, childbirth, and motherhood in the manner of Nisaba, Ninhursaga, or birth goddesses. Her texts emphasize desire, potency, allure, and destabilizing sexual force. **[Securely attested; popular label requires qualification]**

**Venus.** Inanna-Ištar is Venus as morning and evening star. The planet’s alternating visibility, disappearance, and movement furnished language for radiance, duality, transformation, and possibly descent, but no surviving source says the Descent is merely an astronomical allegory. That interpretation is modern. **[Ancient equation; allegorical reduction is modern reconstruction]**

**Reversal and transformation.** Hymns credit her with turning man into woman and woman into man, changing status, arming women, and giving feminine work to men. These passages belong to her capacity to overturn ordered categories. Mapping ancient cult personnel directly onto modern identities is historically hazardous, but erasing their gendered ambiguity is equally unwarranted.

### Iconography

- **Reed bundle/ring-post:** earliest emblem, visible in Uruk-period art and writing. **[Early attestation]**
- **Rosette:** associated with Inanna from early Mesopotamian art; individual rosettes are not always exclusive identifiers.
- **Eight-rayed star:** the clearest later astral emblem of Ištar/Venus. Numbers of rays vary in actual art. [Morgan Library seal](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/she-who-wrote/cylinder-seal-ninishkun-ishtar)
- **Lion:** vehicle, pedestal, companion, or symbol of martial and royal power. The lion reliefs on Babylon’s Processional Way signal Ištar, although the gate itself also displayed mušḫuššu and bulls associated with Marduk and Adad.
- **Weapons rising from the shoulders:** characteristic Old Akkadian and later martial iconography.
- **Horned crown:** general mark of Mesopotamian divinity, not unique to her.
- **Nudity:** nude female plaques are frequently labeled “Ishtar” in popular literature, but most lack decisive attributes; they may represent protective figures, worshippers, sexuality, or multiple goddesses.
- **Rod and ring:** occurs in divine investiture iconography but is not uniquely hers.
- **Adda Seal, c. 2300 BCE:** a frontal armed goddess standing on or near mountains is conventionally identified as Ištar; the weapons and frontal pose support, but do not absolutely prove, the identification.
- **“Burney Relief” / “Queen of the Night,” early second millennium BCE:** proposed identities include Ištar, Ereshkigal, or another goddess. Calling it a certain portrait of Lilith or Inanna is unsupported. **[Contested identification]**
- **Ishtar Gate, Babylon, reign of Nebuchadnezzar II, 604–562 BCE:** the gate bore Ištar’s name but its surviving animal program is not a portrait of her. The Berlin reconstruction combines excavated glazed bricks with modern restoration; Babylon underwent repeated rebuilding. [UNESCO documentation](https://whc.unesco.org/document/168481)

### Numbers, days, colors, substances

The number **15** was used logographically for Ištar because Venus is linked to the lunar god’s numerical system; `d15` became a standard writing of her name. Claims that Friday, copper, lapis lazuli, red, blue, particular incense recipes, or modern crystals were universally “sacred to Inanna” require source-by-source proof. Lapis lazuli, carnelian, cosmetics, jewelry, wool garments, beer, dates, bread, meat, oil, and incense appear in literary or offering contexts, but this does not create a single timeless correspondence chart.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Parentage or position | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|
| Uruk/early Sumerian | Closely associated with An; sometimes styled his daughter, favorite, or possessor of his Eanna | **[Early and Old Babylonian traditions]** |
| Nanna-Suen tradition | Daughter of Nanna/Sîn and Ningal; sister of Utu/Šamaš | **[Most widespread later Sumerian and Akkadian genealogy]** |
| Enki tradition | Enki appears as elder, patron, adversarial benefactor, or rescuer, not consistently as father | **[Narrative relationship; modern family trees overstate it]** |
| Akkadian royal tradition | Daughter of Anu in *Gilgamesh* VI; Antu appears as her mother | **[Later canonical]** |
| Learned lists | Multiple equations, names, courts, and local manifestations arranged under Ištar | **[Learned systematization]** |

Her principal spouse or lover is **Dumuzi/Tammuz**, but the relationship ranges from erotic courtship and sacred marriage to accusation, substitution, mourning, and vengeance. She is also paired locally with **Zababa** at Kish. No single, consistently attested body of children belongs to her. Claims that she is universally a “mother goddess” are therefore misleading.

**Ninshubur**, her minister in Sumerian texts, is ordinarily female in Inanna’s circle. The corresponding Akkadian minister **Papsukkal** is male; later scribal equation did not preserve every feature of Ninshubur. **[Syncretistic and learned transformation]**

### Documented god–god interactions

The following covers discrete interactions in the principal published literary and royal corpora. Thousands of ritual lists and fragmentary tablets make literal corpus-wide exhaustiveness impossible; mere co-presence in an offering list is excluded.

- **An:** Inanna demands authorization to destroy Ebih; An cautions or refuses her, and she acts independently. *Inanna and Ebih*, Old Babylonian witnesses. **[Later canonical preservation of an older Sumerian tradition]**
- **An:** Inanna acquires or claims Eanna in a fragmentary narrative. *Inanna and An*, Old Babylonian. **[Fragmentary canonical tradition]**
- **An:** He is praised as favoring her and appears as senior divine authority in hymns. *Exaltation of Inanna* and Inanna hymns, Old Babylonian copies. **[Later canonical]**
- **Anu:** Ishtar complains after Gilgamesh rejects her and demands the Bull of Heaven; she threatens release of the dead if he refuses. *Gilgamesh* VI, Standard Babylonian recension. **[Later canonical]**
- **Antu:** Ishtar appeals in the heavenly family scene following Gilgamesh’s rejection. *Gilgamesh* VI. **[Later canonical]**
- **Enki:** Inanna visits him at Eridu; while intoxicated, he grants her the *me*. *Inanna and Enki*, Old Babylonian witnesses. **[Later canonical]**
- **Enki:** He regrets the gifts and repeatedly orders Isimud and aquatic guardians to recover Inanna’s boat; she reaches Uruk with the *me*. Same text. **[Later canonical]**
- **Enki:** Hearing Ninshubur’s appeal, he creates the *kurgarra* and *galatur* and provides the life-giving substances that revive Inanna. *Inanna’s Descent*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Ea:** Creates Aṣušu-namir and sends him to obtain Ishtar’s release from Ereshkigal. Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar*, second-millennium composition/first-millennium witnesses. **[Akkadian canonical variant]**
- **Nanna/Sîn:** Appealed to by Ninshubur during Inanna’s death but refuses assistance; named as her father in widespread genealogy. *Inanna’s Descent* and hymns. **[Later canonical]**
- **Nanna:** Enheduanna appeals to him before turning decisively to Inanna; his silence heightens Inanna’s judicial supremacy. *Exaltation*. **[Sargonic setting; Old Babylonian manuscripts]**
- **Utu/Šamaš:** In courtship songs, Utu advises or converses with his sister about marriage and Dumuzi. Dumuzi–Inanna cycle. **[Later canonical]**
- **Utu:** Helps Dumuzi evade the demons by transforming him in some Dumuzi traditions. *Dumuzi’s Dream* and related texts. **[Variant tradition]**
- **Utu:** Inanna appeals for assistance after Šukaletuda’s assault. *Inanna and Šukaletuda*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Ereshkigal:** Inanna enters her realm, is stripped, judged, killed, and hung on a hook; after revival Ereshkigal must release her subject to a substitute. *Inanna’s Descent*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Ereshkigal:** Threatened by Ishtar’s demand to enter; orders the gatekeeper to admit and strip her, then later yields under Ea’s stratagem. Akkadian *Descent*. **[Regional/language variant]**
- **Neti:** Gatekeeper who admits Inanna and removes one item of regalia at each gate. *Inanna’s Descent*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Namtar:** Executes or imposes Ereshkigal’s commands and diseases against Ishtar in the Akkadian *Descent*. **[Later canonical variant]**
- **The Anunna judges:** Pronounce judgment against the naked Inanna in the netherworld. *Inanna’s Descent*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Ninshubur:** Receives Inanna’s instructions, publicly mourns her, petitions Enlil, Nanna, and Enki, and is spared as substitute because of loyal mourning. *Inanna’s Descent*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Enlil:** Refuses Ninshubur’s request, arguing that Inanna sought the great heaven and netherworld. *Inanna’s Descent*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Dumuzi:** Courts and marries Inanna in the love songs; she praises his body, produce, and kingship. Dumuzi–Inanna songs, mostly Old Babylonian copies. **[Later canonical/royal ritual poetry]**
- **Dumuzi:** Inanna selects him as her substitute after finding him richly dressed and insufficiently mourning. *Inanna’s Descent*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Dumuzi:** She mourns him or participates in traditions about his periodic return. Akkadian *Descent* ending and lamentation corpus. **[Variant traditions]**
- **Dumuzi:** She asks Bilulu’s punishment after his death and establishes a lament. *Inanna and Bilulu*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Geshtinanna:** Inanna participates in the arrangement by which Geshtinanna shares Dumuzi’s underworld term in some versions. *Dumuzi and Geshtinanna*. **[Regional/canonical variant]**
- **Ama-ušumgal-ana:** Appears as a divine or royal form of Dumuzi in love poetry; interaction is erotic and marital. **[Theological identification]**
- **Iškur/Adad:** Appears among the powers or divine figures connected with storms and royal order; direct personal episodes with Inanna are limited. Joint invocation does not establish intimacy.
- **Ebih:** Inanna demands the mountain’s submission and destroys it. *Inanna and Ebih*. Ebih is a deified mountain rather than an ordinary anthropomorphic god. **[Later canonical]**
- **Bilulu:** Inanna kills or transforms Bilulu and her son Girgire after Dumuzi’s death, establishing their ritual fate. *Inanna and Bilulu*. **[Later canonical]**
- **Gudam:** Inanna confronts the destructive bull-like Gudam and determines the outcome of his rampage. *Inanna and Gudam*. **[Fragmentary canonical]**
- **Zababa:** Paired with Inanna/Ištar at Kish in cult and learned theology; no major surviving narrative cycle describes their marriage. **[Regional cultic pairing]**
- **Nanaya:** Appears in Ištar’s circle and increasingly absorbs erotic and Urukean functions in first-millennium and Hellenistic theology. Their relationship is primarily cultic and classificatory, not a surviving narrative rivalry. **[Learned/regional development]**
- **Aššur:** Ištar of Arbela and Ištar of Nineveh support his chosen Assyrian kings, frequently appearing jointly in royal inscriptions. **[Neo-Assyrian state theology]**
- **Marduk:** Ištar joins the divine order headed by Marduk in Babylonian theology, but no major extant *Enūma eliš* episode pits them against one another. **[Learned/cultic hierarchy; absence of narrative interaction]**
- **Tiamat:** No securely attested individual combat between Ištar and Tiamat occurs in *Enūma eliš*. Modern art and summaries that assign Ištar Marduk’s dragon battle are erroneous. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Astarte/ʿAštart:** Cognate and sometimes equated or translated across Akkadian and West Semitic environments; no single Mesopotamian myth narrates their “meeting.” **[Syncretistic identification, not narrative interaction]**
- **Aphrodite:** Hellenistic communities could interpret Ištar or Astarte through Aphrodite, including syncretized Ishtar-Aphrodite figurines from Babylonia. No ancient Mesopotamian myth makes Aphrodite Inanna’s daughter or reincarnation. **[Hellenistic syncretism]** [Langin-Hooper, Ishtar-Aphrodite figurines](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/723488)

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Descent to the Netherworld

**Sumerian form.** Inanna turns her attention from “great heaven” to the “great below.” She abandons temples in Uruk, Bad-tibira, Zabalam, Adab, Nippur, Kish, and Agade; takes the seven *me* and regalia; and instructs Ninshubur to seek aid if she does not return. At each of Ereshkigal’s seven gates Neti removes one item. Naked and bent low, Inanna is judged by the Anunna, killed, and hung on a hook.

Enlil and Nanna refuse Ninshubur’s appeal. Enki creates the *kurgarra* and *galatur* from dirt under his fingernails and gives them life-giving food and water. By sympathizing with Ereshkigal’s bodily anguish, they secure Inanna’s corpse and revive her. The laws of the netherworld require a substitute. Inanna spares mourners, including Ninshubur, but finds Dumuzi seated in splendor and gives him to the *galla*. Related compositions describe his flight, transformations, capture, and Geshtinanna’s sharing of his fate. [ORACC/ePSD2 composite text](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/literary/Q000343)

This is not straightforwardly a voluntary death-and-resurrection fertility drama. Inanna does not descend to rescue Dumuzi in the Sumerian text; he becomes the consequence of her return. The motive for descent is never fully explained. Astronomical, initiatory, political, and ritual interpretations are modern hypotheses.

**Akkadian form.** Ishtar threatens the gatekeeper, is admitted under Ereshkigal’s rules, and loses an ornament or garment at each gate. With her trapped below, sexual activity ceases above. Ea creates Aṣušu-namir, whose intervention forces Ereshkigal to release her. The ending invokes Dumuzi, music, and mourning. It compresses and alters the Sumerian substitution narrative. **[Later canonical adaptation]**

### Inanna and Enki: acquisition of the *me*

Inanna goes to Eridu, where Enki welcomes her with food and beer. In his intoxication he grants her a long list of *me*: kingship, priestly offices, crafts, sexuality, truth and falsehood, music, weapons, judgment, lamentation, and other constituents of civilization. She loads them onto the Boat of Heaven. Once sober, Enki tries to retrieve them through Isimud and a succession of guardians, but Ninshubur defends the boat. Inanna arrives triumphantly at Uruk and distributes or displays the powers.

The *me* are not simply “laws,” “tablets,” or “superpowers.” They are divinely grounded capacities, offices, norms, artifacts, and modes of behavior. The narrative legitimates Uruk’s cultural centrality while acknowledging Eridu as an older source of wisdom. Whether Inanna “stole civilization” is interpretive: Enki repeatedly and formally gave the powers before trying to reverse himself. **[Old Babylonian canonical text; possible older city theology]**

### The Exaltation of Inanna

The poem praises Inanna as bearer of the *me*, terrifying warrior, storm, judge, and beloved of An. Enheduanna recounts expulsion by Lugal-Ane, ritual pollution or removal from office, and failed appeal to Nanna. She then addresses Inanna as the power capable of overturning the usurper. The goddess accepts the poem; Enheduanna’s restoration and Inanna’s exaltation coincide.

The historical rebellion is usually connected to unrest late in the reign of Naram-Sin, but the poem is not an annal. It is liturgical-political self-presentation transmitted through the Old Babylonian school curriculum. It helped make Inanna the divine guarantor of Akkadian-Sumerian imperial integration. **[Sargonic claim mediated by later copies]**

### Sacred marriage and Dumuzi

The love songs portray desire between Inanna and Dumuzi through shepherding, dairying, date cultivation, cloth, beer, gardens, and ploughed-field metaphors. Some associate the goddess with a named king—Šulgi, Iddin-Dagan, or others—thereby making erotic divine favor a language of legitimate rule.

Evidence supports performances, processions, beds, statues, songs, and royal participation. It does **not** prove that every king annually had intercourse with a high priestess. Some scholars reconstruct literal ritual intercourse; others read the texts as literary, symbolic, or performed before divine statues. “Sacred marriage” is a modern umbrella term covering several genres and centuries rather than the name of one invariant ceremony. **[Cultic practice partly attested; sexual enactment disputed]**

The traditions also resist a smooth fertility romance. The bride who chooses and praises Dumuzi in the songs later hands him to demons in the Descent; other texts mourn him or avenge him. These are variant theological uses of the relationship, not necessarily stages of a single canonical biography.

### Gilgamesh’s rejection of Ishtar

After Gilgamesh returns from defeating Humbaba, Ishtar offers him royal gifts and marriage. He rejects her and lists earlier lovers harmed or transformed: Dumuzi, the allallu-bird, lion, horse, shepherd, gardener Ishullanu, and others. Ishtar appeals to Anu, receives the Bull of Heaven, and sends it against Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill it; Enkidu throws part of it at her. Ishtar and a group of women mourn the Bull.

The speech belongs to the epic’s characterization and should not be converted into an objective catalogue of Ishtar’s “crimes.” Several lovers’ stories are otherwise lost or divergent. The episode stages conflict between royal male heroism and the city’s goddess while retaining her cosmic power. **[Standard Babylonian canonical elaboration]**

### Inanna and Ebih

Inanna praises her own powers, complains that Mount Ebih has not shown respect, and asks An to authorize its destruction. When An cautions her, she arms herself and annihilates the mountain. The poem presents warfare, self-authorizing sovereignty, and perhaps imperial domination of highland regions. Attribution to Enheduanna is possible but contested. **[Old Babylonian textual attestation; proposed Sargonic political background]**

### Inanna and Šukaletuda

While Inanna sleeps beneath a tree after surveying the land, Šukaletuda violates her. She discovers the assault and turns waters to blood or otherwise afflicts the country, refusing to relent until the offender is produced. Enki advises Šukaletuda to hide among the cities, but Inanna ultimately finds and condemns him. The damaged ending praises her.

Modern summaries have sometimes romanticized the encounter. The text depicts an assault, divine rage, collective calamity, and punishment. It cannot serve as evidence for consensual ritual sex. **[Old Babylonian canonical]**

### Inanna and Bilulu

After Dumuzi’s death, Inanna attacks Bilulu and Girgire, transforming or assigning them a ritual condition associated with water skins and funerary offerings. The composition, an *ulila* lament, connects revenge narrative to pastoral mourning rites. **[Old Babylonian canonical; text difficult and partially uncertain]**

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Major cult centers

**Uruk—Eanna, “House of Heaven.”** The cultic institution appears in the late fourth millennium BCE and became the paradigmatic home of Inanna. Building phases repeatedly replaced one another; later Eanna served both Anu and Ištar, with their relative prominence changing. Its household controlled land, labor, herds, crafts, storage, and offerings. **[Archaeological and administrative attestation]**

**Zabalam—Giguna.** An important early Inanna cult, distinct enough for “Inanna of Zabalam” to appear beside other local Inannas. **[Early administrative and literary attestation]**

**Agade—Eulmaš.** Center of Ištar-Annunītu and Sargonic dynastic devotion. Agade itself has not been securely located, limiting archaeological reconstruction. **[Textual attestation]**

**Ur.** Inanna had cultic presence alongside Nanna; Enheduanna’s office belonged primarily to Nanna, although her poetry elevated Inanna. **[Epigraphic and literary]**

**Kish—Eme-urur and cult with Zababa.** Regional pairing emphasized kingship and warfare.

**Nineveh—Emašmaš.** Ištar of Nineveh had an old and prestigious Assyrian cult; the sanctuary dominated Kuyunjik and was repeatedly rebuilt. [Cambridge, “The Ishtar Temple at Nineveh”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iraq/article/abs/ishtar-temple-at-nineveh/E47F34D815C5EC97D7046E3B22667766)

**Aššur.** Multiple architectural phases of an Ištar temple yielded significant sculptures and deposits. The sanctuary’s chronology illustrates the adoption and transformation of southern forms in Assyria.

**Arbela—Egašankalamma.** A center of royal festivals, landholding, prophecy, and Assyrian military ideology. Its archaeological remains are poorly accessible beneath modern Erbil, so the reconstruction is principally textual. [Nissinen and Mattila, “The Temple of Ištar of Arbela”](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/the-temple-of-i%C5%A1tar-of-arbela-2/)

**Babylon.** The monumental Ishtar Gate, Processional Way, and local temples made her part of the city’s ceremonial landscape under Marduk’s supremacy. The gate’s name demonstrates divine patronage of the entrance, not that the gate was her principal temple.

Other attested centers include Adab, Badtibira, Girsu, Isin, Kazallu, Larsa, Nippur, Sippar, Šuruppak, Umma, and numerous Assyrian provincial cities.

### Personnel

- **En / entu:** high priestly office, often filled by a royal woman in major lunar cults; Enheduanna’s case cannot be generalized to all Inanna temples.
- **Gala / kalû:** lamentation specialists using music and Emesal liturgical language. Their office could be prestigious and hereditary.
- **Assinnu, kurgarrû, kuluʾu, pilipili:** personnel especially associated with Ištar, performance, martial display, healing, prophecy, and gendered transformation. Their biological sex, social identity, and sexual activity cannot be reduced to one modern category.
- **Šangû and administrative clergy:** temple administrators overseeing property, offerings, and labor.
- **Raggintu / maḫḫû:** female and male prophetic or ecstatic speakers, conspicuous in Neo-Assyrian Ištar prophecy.

Older scholarship routinely called several of these groups eunuchs, transvestites, homosexual prostitutes, or castrated priests. Some texts do portray gender reversal or ambiguous presentation; none makes all these translations interchangeable. Castration and prostitution must be proved for each office rather than inferred from nonconforming gender. [Peled-related overview](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0752/13/1/20)

### Rituals and offerings

Documented practices include daily food offerings, beer, bread, dates, meat, oil, incense, garments and jewelry for the cult statue; lamentation; music; processions; divine journeys; purification; royal prayers; victory celebrations; omen consultation; love magic; healing incantations; and votive gifts. The cult image was treated as a living divine presence through washing, dressing, feeding, procession, and enthronement.

Royal inscriptions make warfare itself a field of Ištar’s agency. Ashurbanipal credited her with delivering Teumman into his hands and paraded the defeated king’s severed head at Arbela. This is political theology tied to documented Assyrian violence; it is not evidence that Ištar’s temples ordinarily practiced human sacrifice. [RINAP Ashurbanipal inscription](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/rinap5/Q007573/)

### Prophecy at Arbela

Neo-Assyrian prophecies present Ištar speaking maternally and martially to the king: he is her “calf,” she raised or protected him, and she will defeat his enemies. Reports identify named female prophets as well as other ecstatic personnel. The texts served royal legitimation but cannot be dismissed as court fiction; they preserve an institutional practice of collecting, authenticating, and archiving divine messages. **[Seventh-century BCE documentary evidence]**

### Temple prostitution: dedicated contested-material finding

**The ancient claim.** Herodotus, *Histories* 1.199, written in the fifth century BCE, says every Babylonian woman must once sit in the sanctuary of “Mylitta,” accept a stranger’s coin, and have intercourse outside the sanctuary. Later Greek and Roman authors report analogous customs in the eastern Mediterranean.

**Problems.**

1. No extant Babylonian administrative or ritual text describes Herodotus’s universal obligation.
2. The described institution would have required substantial organization, yet Babylonian temple archives do not document it.
3. Akkadian terms once translated “sacred prostitute”—*qadištu*, *ištarītu*, *nadītu*, *kulmašītu*, and others—designate heterogeneous legal and cultic statuses; their etymology does not prove commercial sex.
4. Erotic literature, sex work near temples, sacred-marriage imagery, and cultic prostitution are distinct phenomena.
5. Herodotus’s “Mylitta” is itself philologically problematic.

Stephanie Budin argues that sacred prostitution as a cross-cultural institution is a modern scholarly myth built by combining hostile classical ethnography with mistranslated occupational terms. Other scholars, including Julia Assante, criticize overly absolute dismissal and allow ritualized sex or sexualized cult performance in narrower settings. The strongest conclusion is: commercial sex certainly existed in Mesopotamian cities; Ištar’s cult was deeply sexualized; specific ritual-sex interpretations remain possible; but institutional temple prostitution, especially Herodotus’s once-in-a-lifetime duty for every Babylonian woman, is not corroborated by native evidence. **[Classical allegation; modern dispute; not established as Babylonian institution]** [Budin, *The Myth of Sacred Prostitution in Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/myth-of-sacred-prostitution-in-antiquity/last-myths/35E9790623BE65F028CEB125714F2E25)

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

No secure evidence establishes a regular human-sacrificial cult of Inanna-Ištar. Royal warfare, execution, ritual self-wounding, animal sacrifice, mythic killing, and the deaths in the Royal Cemetery of Ur are separate evidentiary problems. The *kurgarrû* may perform with weapons and possibly blood, but contested passages do not securely demonstrate routine self-castration or human sacrifice. Claims that children were sacrificed to Ishtar, particularly in modern Easter polemics, are modern inventions.

## 7. Historical evolution

### c. 3300–2900 BCE: formation at Uruk

The archaic record reveals the Eanna institution, the reed emblem, offerings, and a goddess-linked political economy. Her later mythology cannot safely be projected into this period. The very early relationship between the divine names Inanna, the place Eanna, and the temple household remains philologically contested.

### c. 2900–2350 BCE: regional Inannas

Early Dynastic god lists, offerings, royal inscriptions, and personal names show multiple cult centers. Uruk and Zabalam were especially important. Martial and sovereign features emerge in documentary and literary precursors. Local manifestations were neither wholly separate gods nor perfectly interchangeable.

### c. 2334–2154 BCE: Akkadian imperial synthesis

Sargon’s dynasty promoted Ištar/Annunītu as a patron of conquest. Enheduanna’s literary persona connects Inanna with imperial restoration and Sumerian cultic geography. This is the decisive documented phase of Inanna–Ištar assimilation, not necessarily its beginning.

### c. 2112–2004 BCE: Ur III court theology

Kings used Inanna-Dumuzi erotic language and divine favor to articulate kingship. Temple households remained major economic institutions. Hymns associated rulers with Dumuzi, but modern reconstructions of a compulsory annual sexual rite go beyond the evidence.

### c. 2000–1600 BCE: Old Babylonian literary canon

Scribal schools copied the great Sumerian myths, hymns, and royal songs. Akkadian literature developed corresponding Ishtar traditions. This period supplies much of what modern readers call “Sumerian mythology,” although the manuscripts postdate the settings they portray.

### c. 1600–1000 BCE: canonization and international spread

God lists, bilingual scholarship, omen series, prayers, and the Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh* stabilized major formulations. Cognate and interacting goddesses across Syria and the Levant included Šauška, ʿAštart, and others. The Hurrian **Šauška** was equated with Ištar in bilingual and diplomatic contexts but had her own cultic history.

### c. 1000–609 BCE: Neo-Assyrian apogee

Ištar of Nineveh and Ištar of Arbela became pillars of state ideology. Prophecy, dreams, royal prayers, temple festivals, and military inscriptions document extraordinary prominence. This was not simply “the same cult” as Uruk’s: Assyrian Ištars were armed national patrons with their own sanctuaries and personalities.

### 626–539 BCE: Neo-Babylonian monumentalization

Babylonian restoration and construction placed Ištar within Marduk-centered civic ceremonial. Nebuchadnezzar II’s Ishtar Gate is the best-known material survival. Uruk’s cult continued, while scholarly theology increasingly elevated Anu and Antu.

### 539–331 BCE: Achaemenid continuity and change

Mesopotamian temples continued under Persian rule, although political and economic structures shifted. Herodotus’s report belongs to this era but is external Greek ethnography, not a Babylonian temple record.

### 331 BCE–third century CE: Hellenistic and Parthian transformations

Late cuneiform scholarship continued at Uruk and Babylon. Nanaya, Antu, and other goddesses could assume functions formerly dominant in Ištar’s profile. Greek-speaking environments identified Near Eastern goddesses with Aphrodite or Artemis. Ishtar-Aphrodite imagery in Seleucid-Parthian Babylonia documents actual syncretism, but not a simple genealogical evolution “Inanna became Aphrodite.”

Cuneiform temple culture dwindled over the first centuries CE. The last dated cuneiform astronomical texts belong to the late first century CE; cultic decline was gradual rather than one recorded empire-wide suppression.

### Late antiquity and medieval transmission

Christianization, changing urban institutions, and later Islamization ended public temple cults. There is no demonstrated continuous secret lineage of Inanna priesthood from antiquity to modern paganism. Names and motifs survived indirectly through classical authors, biblical polemic against Astarte/Ashtoreth, Syriac and Arabic traditions about ancient idols, astrological Venus lore, and the demonological transformation of **Astarte/Astaroth**. Astaroth’s masculine demon form is a medieval Christian development from the name Astarte, not an ancient Mesopotamian classification of Ištar as a demon. **[Late accretion]**

### Present

Modern devotees worship Inanna, Ishtar, or both in reconstructionist, Mesopotamian polytheist, Goddess-spirituality, queer-spirituality, and eclectic occult settings. These communities are real contemporary religious movements, but their institutional continuity with ancient temples is not documented. Their scale cannot be established reliably from available census data.

## 8. Astarte, Aphrodite, and wider identifications

### Astarte

Akkadian Ištar and Northwest Semitic ʿAštart/Astarte have cognate names and overlapping associations with sexuality, warfare, kingship, and Venus. They interacted over centuries. Nevertheless, Ugaritic ʿAṯtart has a much smaller surviving mythological role than Anat, while Phoenician Astarte developed local forms in Sidon, Tyre, Cyprus, Egypt, and the western Mediterranean. “Astarte is simply Ishtar under another spelling” suppresses those histories. **[Linguistic relationship plus historical syncretism; not timeless identity]**

### Aphrodite

Near Eastern influence on Aphrodite is strongly supported by cult geography, iconographic transfer, titles such as *Ourania*, and Greek testimony connecting her with Phoenician Astarte. The mechanism and date remain debated. Aphrodite did not appear as a direct translation of a Babylonian goddess in one event; transmission likely ran through multiple Levantine and Cypriot contexts. By the Hellenistic period explicit Ishtar-Aphrodite combinations are archaeologically visible in Babylonia. **[Cultural transmission and later explicit syncretism]**

Claims that Aphrodite and Athena are two halves of Ishtar, or that every armed Aphrodite proves direct descent from Inanna, are modern simplifications. Similarity, influence, and cultic equation are different propositions.

## 9. Modern reception and reinvention

### Archaeological rediscovery

Nineteenth-century excavations at Nineveh, Aššur, Babylon, Nippur, and Ur recovered cuneiform tablets and temples. Early translations of the Akkadian Descent encouraged comparisons with Persephone, Orpheus, Tammuz, Christ, and seasonal vegetation. Such comparison made the goddess famous but often rearranged the evidence into a universal dying-and-rising-god scheme.

The Warka Vase, excavated at Uruk in 1933–1934, became central to reconstructions of early ritual. It was looted from the Iraq Museum in 2003 and recovered, damaged, soon afterward. Destruction, looting, illicit antiquities markets, and poorly provenanced tablets continue to compromise contextual knowledge.

### Literature and psychology

- **James Frazer and fertility-cult comparativism:** encouraged the treatment of Inanna-Dumuzi as one version of a universal sacred marriage and dying vegetation god. **[Modern theory, now substantially qualified]**
- **Samuel Noah Kramer and Diane Wolkstein, *Inanna: Queen of Heaven and Earth* (1983):** enormously influential literary retelling combining translated texts into a continuous cycle. It is not a single ancient book.
- **Jungian and feminist readings:** interpret descent as individuation, confrontation with the shadow, loss of social identities, or recovery of feminine power. These are twentieth-century psychological traditions, not ancient exegesis.
- **Goddess spirituality:** authors and ritualists recast Inanna as a great mother, autonomous sexual woman, wounded healer, or model of female sovereignty. Ancient texts support some ingredients but not this unified archetype.
- **Queer and transgender reception:** Inanna’s hymnic gender reversals and gender-ambiguous cult personnel provide genuine ancient points of engagement. Identifying particular ancient offices with exact modern identities remains interpretive.

### Occult reception

Renaissance and early-modern magic knew Astarte/Astaroth more often than cuneiform Ištar, since cuneiform had not yet been deciphered. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists then recombined Astarte, Ishtar, Venus, Isis, and ceremonial-magic correspondences. The Golden Dawn’s broad syncretistic tables helped normalize such equations, but there was no Golden Dawn recovery of an intact Babylonian rite.

Aleister Crowley’s **Babalon** draws primarily on the biblical Whore of Babylon, Revelation, and Thelemic revelation, not directly from a cuneiform cult of Ishtar. Later occult writers sometimes link Babalon, Astarte, and Ishtar; that chain is modern syncretism.

### Popular culture

Inanna and Ishtar appear in poetry, opera, visual art, comics, anime, games, fantasy fiction, and science fiction. Modern images often combine the Burney Relief, eight-pointed star, lions, exposed wings, horned crown, and Ishtar Gate into an anachronistic but recognizable icon.

### Modern false traditions

- **“Easter comes from Ishtar.”** False. The claim was popularized in anti-Catholic form by Alexander Hislop’s *The Two Babylons* (1853) and survives in internet memes. English *Easter* and German *Ostern* do not derive from Akkadian *Ištar*; most Christian languages use names derived from Greek/Latin *Pascha*. **[Modern invention]** [Bad Ancient analysis](https://www.badancient.com/claims/easter-ishtar/)
- **“Ishtar was pronounced Easter.”** False.
- **“Eggs and rabbits were Ishtar’s symbols.”** No Mesopotamian evidence establishes the modern Easter package as an Ishtar cult.
- **“Inanna descended to rescue Dumuzi.”** Contrary to the surviving Sumerian Descent, in which she hands him over as substitute.
- **“The Burney Relief is certainly Lilith/Ishtar.”** Identification remains disputed.
- **“Every nude female figurine is Ishtar.”** Unsupported.
- **“Her priests were all castrated transgender prostitutes.”** A conflation of distinct offices, ambiguous evidence, and modern categories.
- **“Ishtar demanded infant sacrifice.”** Not established by Mesopotamian evidence.
- **“Inanna, Astarte, Aphrodite, Isis, Cybele, and the Virgin Mary are one continuous goddess.”** A modern universalizing genealogy; ancient peoples did make specific equations, but not this timeless total identity.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Ištar has been used as an emblem of Iraqi and Assyrian heritage, women’s power, queer antiquity, and pre-monotheistic identity. Those appropriations range from scholarly informed to romantic-nationalist. Conversely, conspiracist and sectarian polemics invoke “Ishtar worship” as a label for Catholicism, feminism, sexual minorities, celebrity culture, or alleged elite ritual. These narratives generally descend from Hislop-style anti-Catholicism and modern conspiracy culture, not cuneiform evidence. No documented modern organization possesses institutional descent from the Eanna or Arbela priesthood.

## 10. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major interpretive phases

**Early decipherment, nineteenth century.** Scholars first encountered Ištar through biblical Astarte, classical authors, and newly translated Akkadian texts. This encouraged premature identification with Aphrodite and fertility religion.

**Pan-Babylonian and comparativist period.** Astral and seasonal readings treated myths as coded astronomy or vegetation ritual. Some insights—especially the Venus identification—are textually secure; totalizing explanations are not.

**Thorkild Jacobsen.** *The Treasures of Darkness* reconstructed historical layers of Mesopotamian religion through changing metaphors and divine functions. It remains influential but often presents elegant developmental narratives more confidently than fragmentary evidence allows.

**Henri Frankfort.** Work on cylinder seals established iconographic sequences and criteria, while later discoveries have qualified individual identifications.

**William Hallo and J. J. A. van Dijk.** Their 1968 edition of *The Exaltation of Inanna* transformed the study of Enheduanna. Annette Zgoll, Paul Delnero, Pascal Attinger, Benjamin Foster, and Sophus Helle have refined manuscripts, grammar, translation, and literary interpretation.

**Rivkah Harris.** Her analysis of Inanna-Ištar as paradox and a conjunction of opposites emphasized that warfare, sexuality, gender reversal, and social disruption should not be reduced to “fertility.”

**Joan Goodnick Westenholz and Tikva Frymer-Kensky.** Developed historically sensitive studies of goddess traditions, gender, and divine typology.

**Wilfred G. Lambert.** His editions of Babylonian mythology and god lists clarified late learned theology; *Babylonian Creation Myths* is essential for preventing Ištar from being assigned roles belonging to other deities.

**Andrew George.** His critical work on *Gilgamesh*, Babylonian topography, and temple names anchors the textual and cultic record.

**Julia Assante, Stephanie Budin, and subsequent critics.** Reopened the temple-prostitution question by challenging occupational translations, classical ethnography, and assumptions about erotic art.

### Live disputes

1. **Origin of Inanna:** whether her earliest identity was primarily storehouse, date-palm, celestial, political, or another form cannot be resolved from the archaic administrative evidence.
2. **Inanna–Ištar merger:** certainly advanced by the Sargonic era, but whether it began earlier and how completely it affected local cults remain debated.
3. **Authorship of Enheduanna:** ancient attribution and first-person voice are strong evidence for a historical authorial tradition; the surviving manuscripts are many centuries later.
4. **Sacred marriage:** performance and royal ideology are secure; literal intercourse, participants, frequency, and calendar are not.
5. **Temple prostitution:** Herodotus and later classical testimony versus the absence of corroborating native institutional evidence.
6. **Gender of personnel:** ancient gender variance is real; exact modern labels and claims of universal castration or sex work are not demonstrable.
7. **Descent interpretation:** political, initiatory, ritual, astronomical, and seasonal readings illuminate aspects but none exhausts the text.
8. **Icon identification:** Warka Vase female, Burney Relief, nude plaques, and armed goddesses often lack inscriptions.
9. **Aphrodite’s eastern formation:** strong evidence for eastern influence and later equation, but no simple linear biological genealogy.
10. **Continuity:** motifs and names survived; an uninterrupted initiatory cult from ancient Uruk to modern paganism is unattested.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is abundant but radically uneven. Archaic material is early but usually non-narrative; literary texts are detailed but often survive in copies centuries later than their presumed formation; cult records are local; monumental art is frequently uninscribed; tablets may be fragmentary or unprovenanced. The goddess’s apparent contradictions are partly intrinsic to ancient praise and partly produced by combining different cities and centuries.

| 1991 | Rivkah Harris publishes “Inanna-Ishtar as Paradox and a Coincidence of Opposites.” |

| 1998–2006 | ETCSL makes composite Sumerian texts and translations widely accessible online. |
| 2003 | Warka Vase looted from and then returned to the Iraq Museum, damaged. |
| 2006–2008 | Budin’s work intensifies debate over the alleged institution of sacred prostitution. |
| 2010s–2020s | ORACC, RINAP, SAAo, CDLI, and ePSD2 greatly expand open access to tablets, editions, and royal inscriptions. |
| 2020s | Modern reconstructionist and eclectic worship continues; no reliable census or demonstrated institutional continuity from antiquity exists. |
| 2023 | New study of Seleucid-Parthian Ishtar-Aphrodite figurines reframes their funerary and religious contexts. |
| 2026 | Research increasingly emphasizes local manifestations, manuscript history, gender-methodological caution, and separation of native evidence from modern universal-goddess narratives. |
