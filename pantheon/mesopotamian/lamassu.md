# Lamassu (Shedu): Research Dossier

**Class:** A class of benevolent tutelary deities or protective spirits, not one consistently individualized god. In early Sumerian evidence, *Lamma* is normally female and anthropomorphic; Akkadian *lamassu* continues that category. Sumerian *alad* and Akkadian *šēdu* normally designate the corresponding male protective spirit. In first-millennium Assyria, these names and the compound *aladlammû* were applied to monumental human-headed winged bulls and lions guarding palaces, temples, and city gates. “Demon” is defensible only in the older Assyriological sense of a superhuman intermediary; “monster” is misleading if it implies hostility.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The ePSD/ORACC corpus records divine *Lamma/Lammar* in Early Dynastic IIIa sources, conventionally about **2600–2500 BCE**, and ED IIIb evidence follows. Those early attestations establish a female tutelary deity but not the later winged-bull body. The earliest securely contextualized monumental Assyrian human-headed bull-and-lion guardians are those installed by **Ashurnasirpal II at Kalḫu/Nimrud, 883–859 BCE**. The distinction between these two beginnings is fundamental.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early attested]**, **[Later canonical/systematized]**, **[Regional variant]**, **[Syncretistic identification]**, **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, **[Modern reception]**, and **[Modern invention/unsubstantiated]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The historical identity

“Lamassu” is now commonly used as though it named one species of Assyrian winged bull. The documentation is more complicated:

1. **Third millennium BCE:** Sumerian *Lamma* is a usually female, human-shaped protective and intercessory deity who accompanies a worshipper, leads that person into a greater god’s presence, or embodies the favorable protection of a god, temple, city, or ruler.
2. **Second millennium BCE:** Akkadian *lamassu* and *šēdu* appear as paired favorable spirits attached to persons, kings, buildings, and gods. A person can “have” a lamassu; favorable šēdu and lamassu can accompany, preserve, or abandon someone.
3. **Neo-Assyrian period:** Royal texts use *šēdu*, *lamassu*, and the written compound **dALAD.dLAMMA** for apotropaic statues, especially human-headed winged bulls and lions. Modern museums consequently call nearly every such colossus a “lamassu,” although the ancient terminology was neither so uniform nor restricted to that shape.
4. The tradition therefore does **not** provide a single biography, genealogy, or stable personality. It provides a long history of protective offices, manifestations, and images.

### Documented names and writings

- **Sumerian:** **𒀭𒆗**, conventionally transliterated **dLAMA**, **dLAMMA**, or *Lammar*. The final *-r* in modern dictionary normalization reflects the reconstructed Sumerian form, not a separate deity. ORACC records ED IIIa, ED IIIb, Ur III, and Old Babylonian occurrences and defines it as a “(female) tutelary deity.” **[Early attested]**
- **Sumerian:** **dALAD / alad₂**, a protective spirit corresponding lexically to Akkadian *šēdu*. **[Early/later lexical attestation]**
- **Akkadian:** **lamassu**, with spellings including *la-ma-su*, *la-ma-as-su*, *la-ma-sa*, and writings with **dLAMA/dLAMMA**. Grammatical and contextual evidence usually treats it as female, although first-millennium applications to male-faced colossi complicate any absolute gender rule.
- **Akkadian:** **šēdu** (older publications often *šêdu*), conventionally masculine; written syllabically or with the Sumerogram **ALAD**.
- **Neo-Assyrian learned/monumental term:** **aladlammû**, represented by **dALAD.dLAMMA(.MEŠ)**. This compound is especially associated with colossal protective figures. Its exact reading and the extent to which every written occurrence denotes the familiar winged bull rather than a broader class remain philological questions. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Plural:** Akkadian plurals depend on form and context; the popular English plural “lamassus” is modern English, not ancient usage.
- **Lamašaga/Lamma-saga:** “Beneficent/Good Lamma,” especially the protective goddess attached to Bau/Baba. This can be a title, a particular hypostasis, or an independently listed divine attendant depending on period and text. **[Regional and learned systematization]**

### Etymology

The relation between Sumerian *Lamma* and Akkadian *lamassu* is secure at the level of scribal equivalence, but ultimate derivation is disputed.

- One position treats Akkadian *lamassu* as a borrowing or adaptation of Sumerian *Lamma*.
- W. G. Lambert argued that the morphology and personal-name evidence may point instead to a Semitic formation subsequently written with the Sumerian logogram.
- Proposals connecting *alad* with a Semitic root concerning generation or procreation are suggestive rather than settled.
- Popular explanations assigning the human head to “intelligence,” bull body to “strength,” and wings to “freedom” are **modern symbolic exegesis**. Assyrian sources emphasize divine, terrifying, protective, and evil-repelling appearance, not that tidy three-part allegory.

### Titles and functional descriptions

The sources use functional phrases more often than a fixed catalogue of epithets:

- “good/favorable *šēdu*” — *šēdu damqu*;
- “good/favorable *lamassu*” — *lamassu damiqtu*;
- “beneficent protective goddess of Bau”;
- attendant, minister, guide, or intercessor;
- guardian of a person, king, palace, temple, gate, road, or city;
- image “whose appearance repels evil,” in Neo-Assyrian royal building descriptions.

### False or overbroad equations

- **Lamaštu/Lamashtu:** not Lamassu. Lamaštu is a hostile female disease and infant-threatening demoness with a different name, corpus, and iconography. Confusion is modern and orthographic.
- **Bull of Heaven:** not a lamassu. The bull killed by Gilgamesh and Enkidu is a distinct mythic being.
- **Cherubim:** comparative study may legitimately place Assyrian composite guardians beside Levantine throne and gate guardians, but no ancient source simply equates *lamassu* with Hebrew *kerūb*. **[Modern comparison, not ancient identification]**
- **Ezekiel’s living creatures or the Christian tetramorph:** iconographic comparison is possible, direct identity is unproved.
- **Sphinx:** a useful modern morphological label for some human-headed winged lions, but not a timeless native equivalence.
- **Papsukkal and Išum:** internet claims that Papsukkal “is the lamassu” and Išum “is the shedu” overstate associations between divine messengers and protective offices. No general canonical equation is established.
- **Inara:** Hittite scribes used Sumerographic LAMMA for Anatolian tutelary deities, sometimes including a deity identified with Inara. This is a scribal and functional translation, not proof that Mesopotamian Lamassu and Inara were originally one being. **[Syncretistic/lexical identification]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

ORACC’s ePSD records *Lammar* nine times across Early Dynastic IIIa and IIIb, Ur III, and Old Babylonian documentation. A property inscription from Šuruppak reads, in translation, “(This bowl) is the property of Lama.” Such short administrative or dedicatory notices prove that a divine name or title existed but disclose almost nothing about form, narrative, or doctrine.

**What the earliest record establishes:**

- a divine or divinized protective entity;
- normally female grammatical treatment;
- association with ownership, favorable protection, divine retinues, and eventually intercession.

**What it does not establish:**

- wings;
- a bull or lion body;
- five legs;
- a standardized Assyrian gate-colossus;
- a universal zodiacal or astral identity;
- a single named personality with parents, spouse, and myths.

### Principal textual corpora

#### Gudea Cylinders A and B

**Language/date/support:** Sumerian, inscribed on two large clay cylinders, reign of Gudea of Lagash, approximately 2144–2124 BCE.

In the temple-building composition, Gudea asks the goddess Ĝatumdug: “May your friendly guardian go before me, and may your friendly protecting genius walk with me” on the road to Niĝin. The line distinguishes an *alad* and a *lamma*, already showing the paired male/female protective pattern. **[Early attested]**

This is not a story about a winged bull. It documents divine escort and personal protection within royal temple construction.

#### Presentation scenes and seal formulae

Ur III and Old Babylonian cylinder seals frequently show a minor goddess grasping a human worshipper’s wrist and leading that person before an enthroned major deity. Scholarship conventionally identifies this intermediary as a Lamma goddess where inscriptions or context permit. The identification is strongest when text and image coincide; an uninscribed introducing goddess should not automatically be called “Lamassu.” **[Modern scholarly reconstruction grounded in repeated iconography]**

#### *A Hymn to Bau’s Beneficent Protective Goddess* (“Bau A”)

**Language/composition:** Sumerian; surviving Old Babylonian school-tablet witnesses, early second millennium BCE, preserving a composition whose exact original date is uncertain.

The fragmentary hymn praises Bau’s “Beneficent Protective Goddess” as a minister who hears prayer, guides speech and intention, stands beside a favored person, and has connections to the “joyous symbol of Inana.” The surviving fragments do not yield a complete narrative. **[Old Babylonian attestation; regional Lagash/Bau theology]**

#### Royal hymns and prayers

An Old Babylonian prayer to Nanna for Rīm-Sîn includes “serving deities, protective goddesses, [and] good mother goddesses” receiving offerings so that Nanna and Ningal will accept the king’s worship. Here *lamma* belongs to a plural temple retinue rather than denoting one unique goddess.

A Samsu-iluna hymn presents male and female protective deities accompanying royal power; its female guardians speak as “the beneficent protective goddesses of Inana.” **[Later royal elaboration]**

#### Incantations and therapeutic ritual

A first-millennium anti-witchcraft prescription asks that “a good šēdu-spirit and a good lamassu-spirit always accompany” the patient while his personal god and goddess stand beside him. The ritual involves purification, offerings, and divinatory resolution. This demonstrates that favorable šēdu/lamassu were still living ritual categories outside monumental royal art. **[Later canonical ritual tradition]**

#### Royal inscriptions of Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, and successors

Sennacherib’s palace inscriptions at Nineveh describe stone and cast-metal colossi: bull colossi, lions, sphinxes, and “twin lamassu-statues.” Another inscription invokes Aššur and Ištar so that “the good šēdu and the good lamassu” may remain forever in the palace and never leave it. The terms can therefore refer both to supernatural protection and to installed embodiments of it.

Esarhaddon’s inscriptions describe repairing temple *šēdu*, *lamassu*, and *rābiṣu* figures and positioning evil-repelling gate guardians. RINAP’s catalogue also identifies inscriptions written directly on bull colossi. **[Neo-Assyrian royal and architectural systematization]**

#### Adad-guppi inscription

In the sixth-century BCE autobiographical inscription attributed to Adad-guppi, mother of Nabonidus, she tells Sîn that the good šēdu and lamassu entrusted to her brought her to extreme old age and asks that they now be entrusted to her son. This is unusually explicit evidence for protective spirits transmitted through divine favor and dynastic succession. **[Neo-Babylonian royal theology]**

#### God lists

The great lexical-theological list *An = Anum*, compiled through the second millennium and transmitted in first-millennium copies, assigns named Lamma figures to divine households. Such entries are priestly cataloguing: they locate protective goddesses in retinues, but should not be mistaken for narrative myths or universal popular belief. **[Learned systematization]**

### Texts in which Lamassu is not securely present

The requested corpus check yields an important negative result:

- **Enūma eliš:** no secure narrative role for Lamassu/Šēdu.
- **Atraḫasīs:** no secure role.
- **The Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh:** no Lamassu character. The Bull of Heaven and scorpion people are different beings.
- **Inana’s Descent:** no securely named Lamassu/Šēdu protagonist.
- **Enki and Ninmaḫ:** no Lamassu role; its birth goddesses must not be silently relabeled.
- Claims that Lamassu and an “Aspasu” appear in *Gilgamesh* and *Enūma eliš* as the zodiac or “parent stars” are **[Modern invention/unsubstantiated]**. They do not survive comparison with standard editions and corpus searches.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

Across the documented history, the principal functions are:

- accompanying and protecting an individual;
- mediating a worshipper’s approach to a greater god;
- representing the favorable protective power delegated by a god;
- guarding roads, thresholds, doors, palace chambers, temples, and cities;
- repelling hostile humans and supernatural evil;
- preserving royal authority, longevity, prosperity, and legitimate succession;
- monumentalizing imperial power while simultaneously performing apotropaic protection.

No source makes Lamassu a creator god, storm god, underworld ruler, zodiac sovereign, or autonomous head of a pantheon.

### Early human form

In Ur III and Old Babylonian art, the Lamma type is an anthropomorphic goddess:

- long flounced garment;
- horned divine headdress;
- one or both hands raised in intercession;
- often physically leading a human worshipper;
- subordinate placement before a major seated deity.

This image explains the early textual verbs of going before, accompanying, presenting, and interceding.

### Assyrian composite form

The iconic Neo-Assyrian colossus combines:

- male human head, usually bearded;
- elaborate hair and squared beard;
- horned divine crown or high tiara;
- bull or lion body;
- bird-of-prey wings;
- enormous scale;
- placement in symmetrical pairs;
- inscriptions across the body or between the legs.

Bull and lion bodies are both documented. Modern writers sometimes reserve “lamassu” for bulls and “shedu” for lions, but ancient usage does not support that clean zoological division.

### The five legs

Some, not all, monumental colossi have five legs. Seen frontally, two forelegs make the guardian stand immobile; seen from the side, four legs make it stride. The “fifth” leg is therefore a solution to two intended viewpoints at a doorway, not evidence that the spirit was imagined anatomically as a five-legged species. Louvre and Chicago descriptions explicitly document this optical construction. **[Secure Neo-Assyrian iconography]**

### Principal surviving and recorded monuments

- **Kalḫu/Nimrud, Northwest Palace of Ashurnasirpal II, ca. 883–859 BCE:** human-headed winged bulls and lions flanked throne-room and other entrances. Examples are now in the British Museum and Metropolitan Museum; others remained on site until their destruction.
- **Dur-Šarrukin/Khorsabad, palace of Sargon II, 721–705 BCE:** monumental bulls, some about four metres high and approximately 28–30 tonnes. Major examples are in the Louvre and the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures in Chicago.
- **Nineveh, Southwest Palace of Sennacherib, ca. 704–681 BCE:** colossi guarded major entrances. Palace reliefs also show quarrying and transporting colossal bulls, an exceptional visual record of their manufacture.
- **Nergal Gate, Nineveh:** a pair of guardians survived in damaged and reconstructed condition until ISIS attacked the site in 2015.
- **Arslan Tash/ancient Ḫadattu:** basalt bull colossi inscribed under Tiglath-pileser III.
- **Metal versions:** Sennacherib’s inscriptions describe paired lamassu cast in shining copper. Few or none of those full-size metal statues survive, illustrating the difference between textual corpus and surviving stone corpus.

### Materials, colors, and physical setting

Surviving colossi are commonly gypsum/alabaster or limestone; basalt examples also occur. The palaces were painted, so their current bare-stone appearance is incomplete. No stable ancient canonical color, sacred number, weekday, plant, or planet is documented for Lamassu as a class. The number five belongs to the sculptural viewpoint system, not demonstrably to numerology.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

There is no securely attested birth myth, parentage, spouse, offspring, or sibling set for Lamassu/Šēdu as a class. This absence follows from their character: they are divine offices, attendants, or manifestations of protection rather than a single genealogically defined personality.

Named Lamma figures can belong to a greater deity’s household. God lists sometimes individualize them, but that is retinue theology, not proof that every lamassu shared the same identity.

### Every documented divine interaction or relation recovered

- **Ĝatumdug — delegates or supplies protective escort to Gudea.** In Gudea Cylinder A, Gudea asks that her favorable *alad* go before him and her favorable *lamma* accompany him to Niĝin; ca. 22nd century BCE. **[Early attested; regional Lagash tradition]**
- **Bau/Baba — possesses a Beneficent Lamma as minister and intercessor.** *Bau A*, surviving in Old Babylonian manuscripts. **[Regional cult theology/later literary attestation]**
- **Inana — commands or is attended by beneficent protective goddesses.** *Samsu-iluna and Inana*, Old Babylonian royal hymn. **[Later royal elaboration]**
- **Inana — her “joyous symbol” is compared with or attributed to the Beneficent Protective Goddess.** *Bau A*, fragmentary Old Babylonian witnesses. The exact relation is obscure. **[Regional/fragmentary]**
- **Nanna — serving and protective goddesses receive offerings so that Nanna accepts the royal offering.** Prayer to Nanna for Rīm-Sîn, early second millennium BCE. **[Later royal liturgy]**
- **Ningal — receives the same offering alongside Nanna through the temple’s serving and protective goddesses.** Same source and date. **[Later royal liturgy]**
- **Aššur — commands that good šēdu and lamassu remain in Sennacherib’s palace.** Nineveh bull inscription, ca. 693–691 BCE. **[Neo-Assyrian royal theology]**
- **Ištar — jointly with Aššur authorizes the palace’s good šēdu and lamassu.** Same inscription and date. **[Neo-Assyrian royal theology]**
- **Sîn — entrusts good šēdu and lamassu to Adad-guppi and is asked to pass them to Nabonidus.** Adad-guppi inscription, sixth century BCE. **[Neo-Babylonian royal theology]**
- **Ninkura — divine craft is credited with perfecting the forms of Sennacherib’s bull colossi and sphinxes.** Sennacherib royal inscription, seventh century BCE. Ninkura does not create the supernatural class but patronizes the craftsmanship of its images. **[Neo-Assyrian royal elaboration]**
- **Bilulu and Ĝirĝire — transformed by Inana into the female and male protective deities of the desert.** *Inana and Bilulu*, surviving Old Babylonian literary tradition. The English “protective goddess/god” corresponds functionally to the Lamma/Alad category, but the passage does not make them the universal Lamassu and Shedu. **[Narrative regional variant]**

No securely attested combat, seduction, marriage, rivalry, judgment, or theft involving a singular Lamassu was found. Claims of such adventures generally confuse Lamassu with Lamaštu, the Bull of Heaven, or unrelated composite beings.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Absence of an independent cycle

Lamassu has no recoverable equivalent of the Marduk–Tiamat, Inana–Dumuzid, or Gilgamesh cycles. Its documented “story” is functional and historical:

1. A protective goddess accompanies a person or introduces a petitioner.
2. The office is paired with a male *alad/šēdu*.
3. Gods and kings deploy these favorable beings to protect lives, dynasties, temples, and palaces.
4. Assyrian rulers embody them in colossal composite statues.
5. Modern reception mistakes those embodiments for a single mythic animal with a standardized biography.

That absence is a finding, not a gap to fill with modern folklore.

### The closest narrative episode: *Inana and Bilulu*

Inana kills or curses Bilulu and her son Ĝirĝire in vengeance connected with Dumuzid. She transforms them into ritual fixtures of desert commemoration: Bilulu becomes the protective goddess and Ĝirĝire the protective god of the desert, calling for libations and flour offerings for the dead wanderer. This is an etiological story for desert mortuary ritual and the creation of a paired protective office. It illustrates how *Lamma/Alad*-type functions could be assigned, but it is not an origin myth for every lamassu.

### Modern pseudo-myths

The following are not established in ancient sources:

- Lamassu as a zodiac composed of “parent stars”;
- Lamassu as a personal mount of Marduk;
- a primordial war between Lamassu and Lamaštu;
- Lamassu as a species created in *Enūma eliš*;
- Lamassu teaching civilization in *Gilgamesh*;
- a canonical symbolism of intelligence, freedom, and strength;
- direct descent of Christian angels or evangelists from Lamassu.

These are **[Modern invention]** or, at best, unsourced comparative interpretations.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Was there a Lamassu cult?

There is no evidence for a pan-Mesopotamian, independently organized “cult of Lamassu” with a central temple, high priesthood, annual festival, and canonical mythology. Worship and ritual attention occurred chiefly in three settings:

1. **Divine retinues:** named or generic Lamma goddesses attached to Bau, Inana, and other major deities.
2. **Personal religion:** favorable šēdu and lamassu accompanying an individual.
3. **Building protection:** statues and ritual deposits installed at thresholds and entrances.

A hymn specifically addresses Bau’s Beneficent Protective Goddess, and royal year-names mention fashioning Lamma statues for major sanctuaries. That is real cultic evidence, but it remains embedded within larger temple institutions.

### Offerings and prayer

Texts attest prayer to protective goddesses, favorable-hand formulas, requests for escort, and offerings made to plural serving and protective deities. The *Bau A* hymn depicts the Lamma as hearing prayer and guiding speech. The Rīm-Sîn prayer includes food, incense, and temple service.

No specific sacrifice uniquely required by Lamassu is securely documented. Ordinary Mesopotamian offerings—food, drink, incense, garments, and manufactured images—are the relevant context.

### Apotropaic and magical practice

First-millennium ritual texts invoke a “good šēdu” and “good lamassu” in counter-witchcraft, purification, and restoration of divine favor. Protective figures could be stationed at architecturally vulnerable places or represented in small images and foundation deposits. Text, image, ritual action, and building fabric worked together.

### Monumental installation and royal politics

At Assyrian capitals, paired colossi:

- guarded entrances physically and magically;
- carried royal inscriptions and curses;
- made visitors pass beneath or between images of divinely sanctioned power;
- linked supernatural protection to the empire’s ability to quarry, transport, and carve stones weighing up to thirty tonnes;
- protected the king while displaying the labor and resources available to him.

The palace guardian is therefore simultaneously religious image, architectural support, imperial performance, and inscribed political document.

### Human sacrifice, prostitution, and cult violence

The dedicated search found:

- **No evidence of human sacrifice to Lamassu or Šēdu.**
- **No evidence of temple prostitution forming part of their cult.**
- **No Lamassu-specific sacrificial violence.**

Older claims of ubiquitous Mesopotamian “sacred prostitution” are themselves heavily disputed and cannot be imported into this dossier without Lamassu-specific evidence. Assyrian royal violence is graphically recorded elsewhere in palace art and inscriptions, but the guardian figures do not thereby become recipients of human sacrifice.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### c. 2600–2350 BCE: Early Dynastic emergence

*Lamma* appears as a female tutelary deity or title. The evidence is short and administrative enough that her full theology remains unrecoverable. No hybrid bull form is securely attached to these earliest attestations.

### c. 2112–2004 BCE: Ur III elaboration

Texts and seals increasingly document protective intercession. The human Lamma goddess leads worshippers into divine presence. Gudea’s slightly earlier/contemporary Lagash tradition pairs the favorable *alad* and *lamma* as escorts.

### c. 2000–1600 BCE: Old Babylonian literary and personal religion

Hymns, royal prayers, seal scenes, and lexical traditions elaborate Lamma as minister, guide, and protective goddess. Named hypostases such as Bau’s Beneficent Lamma emerge. Royal ideology makes male and female protective spirits companions of kings and peoples.

### Late second millennium BCE: lexical and theological systematization

God lists such as *An = Anum* organize minor deities into divine households. Akkadian *šēdu* and *lamassu* become stable equivalents of *alad* and *lamma*, although spellings and classifications remain fluid.

### c. 883–859 BCE: monumental transformation at Nimrud

Ashurnasirpal II uses colossal human-headed winged bulls and lions at the Northwest Palace. Museum guidance identifies this as the first Assyrian royal use of both monumental forms. This is the decisive creation of the image now popularly treated as “the Lamassu.”

### Ninth–eighth centuries BCE: spread through Assyrian architecture

Guardian colossi occur at royal and provincial sites, including Arslan Tash. They combine older supernatural protection with Assyrian palace architecture and imperial ideology.

### 721–705 BCE: Sargon II at Dur-Šarrukin

Khorsabad receives especially monumental examples. Long inscriptions recount Sargon’s titles and building works. Their horned crowns identify divinity; their placement marks vulnerable gateways. Sargon’s death in 705 BCE led to abandonment of the unfinished capital, preserving much of its program under collapse and burial.

### 704–681 BCE: Sennacherib at Nineveh

Sennacherib’s inscriptions describe stone, white-limestone, and cast-copper colossi; reliefs show their manufacture and transport. The terms *šēdu*, *lamassu*, and *aladlammû* become closely tied to palace guardians while retaining the abstract sense of favorable protective powers.

### 680–669 BCE: Esarhaddon

Esarhaddon repairs or reinstalls temple šēdu, lamassu, and rābiṣu figures. His texts describe evil-repelling guardians, confirming that the beings were active components of temple as well as palace protection.

### 612 BCE and aftermath

The sack of Nineveh and collapse of the Neo-Assyrian state end the royal building system that produced the greatest colossi. Many monuments were broken, burned, buried, or incorporated into ruins. This was political destruction of Assyrian capitals, not a documented theological suppression aimed specifically at Lamassu.

### Sixth century BCE: continued personal theology

Adad-guppi still speaks of good šēdu and lamassu as divine gifts securing longevity and dynastic favor. Protective terminology thus survives beyond the Assyrian Empire.

### Achaemenid and later antiquity

Human-headed winged bulls remained influential in imperial art, most famously at Persepolis. Calling the Persian examples “lamassu” is modern comparative nomenclature; they belong to an Achaemenid visual and political system and are not proven continuations of a Lamassu cult.

As cuneiform institutions disappeared in the late first millennium BCE and first centuries CE, the textual tradition ceased. No evidence establishes an unbroken Lamassu priesthood or worshipping community through Christianization or Islamization. Composite guardian imagery persisted more broadly, but continuity of form is not automatically continuity of name or cult.

### Nineteenth-century rediscovery

Paul-Émile Botta excavated Khorsabad in 1843–1844. Reliefs reached Paris in 1847, prompting the Louvre’s Assyrian museum. Austen Henry Layard excavated Nimrud and Nineveh in 1845–1851; their display in London created a powerful European “Assyrian” visual vocabulary. Removal was entangled with Ottoman permissions, European imperial competition, destructive cutting, and unequal archaeological division.

### Twentieth century

Iraqi and foreign missions excavated further examples. The Oriental Institute’s 1928–1935 Khorsabad expedition reconstructed a monumental bull from more than a dozen ancient fragments in Chicago. Lamassu became both a museum icon and a modern emblem of Iraqi and Assyrian heritage.

### 2015: ISIS destruction

On 26 February 2015, ISIS released video of destruction in the Mosul Museum, including attacks on guardian sculptures and the Nergal Gate lamassu. In March and April, it systematically bulldozed, hammered, drilled, and exploded structures at Nimrud. UNESCO satellite and field assessments documented large-scale deliberate destruction. Some museum objects shown in propaganda were replicas, but the Nergal Gate and site monuments were authentic; the blanket claim that “everything destroyed was fake” is false.

The attacks were ideological iconoclasm, propaganda, territorial violence, and an assault on Iraqi and especially Assyrian cultural memory. They were not evidence that Lamassu worship had survived to provoke a cultic conflict.

### 2016–present: recovery and reconstruction

After Nimrud’s recapture, UNESCO’s December 2016 mission documented systematic destruction and began planning emergency protection. Conservation, fragment recording, digital reconstruction, and renewed Iraqi and international excavation continue. Modern reconstructions raise unresolved questions about authenticity, displaced originals, museum ownership, and who controls the public memory of destroyed monuments.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Museums and nineteenth-century design

The arrival of Assyrian sculpture in Paris and London transformed European knowledge of Mesopotamia. Lamassu casts appeared in the Crystal Palace’s “Nineveh Court”; porcelain bookends and domestic objects followed. The guardian became a portable Victorian emblem of biblical antiquity and imperial archaeology. **[Modern reception]**

### Modern Assyrian identity

Modern Assyrians use the lamassu as an emblem of ancient heritage, survival, and attachment to the homeland. Academic interviews with Assyrians in Iraq, Europe, and the United States document pride in the image and the importance of museums as sites of connection. This is a genuine modern cultural tradition, but it should not be described as continuous pagan worship: most modern Assyrians belong to Syriac Christian traditions. **[Modern ethnic and heritage reception]**

### Iraqi national heritage

The image also operates as a broader Iraqi and Mesopotamian emblem in public art, museums, tourism, currency, institutional design, and heritage campaigns. Assyrian and Iraqi uses overlap but are not identical; reducing the image to either one alone erases real modern constituencies.

### Michael Rakowitz

Iraqi-American artist Michael Rakowitz began *The Invisible Enemy Should Not Exist* in 2007, reconstructing lost or looted Iraqi objects from Arabic newspapers and Middle Eastern food packaging. His 2018 Fourth Plinth sculpture in Trafalgar Square recreated the lamassu destroyed at Nineveh’s Nergal Gate using date-syrup cans. It was explicitly a counter-monument to disappearance, war, looting, and museum displacement, not a devotional idol. **[Modern artistic reinvention]**

### Gaming and fantasy

*Dungeons & Dragons* turned “lammasu” and “shedu” into distinct fantasy monster species with alignments, spell-like powers, telepathy, and game statistics. The Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures notes the game’s “lawful good” lamassu. These beings derive visually and nominally from Assyria but their taxonomy and powers are **[Modern invention]**.

Other games, fantasy fiction, comics, and films use lamassu-like forms as gatekeepers, monsters, mounts, or ancient magical machines. Such portrayals have materially reinforced the false idea that “Lamassu” was an individually defined mythological animal.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The search found small-scale contemporary Mesopotamian polytheist and demonolatry communities placing lamassu figures on altars or treating Lamassu and Shedu as personal guardians. This is documented as present-day practice in online testimony but not as institutional continuity from antiquity.

No major Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or classical grimoire tradition centered on Lamassu was found. Claims of an ancient Lamassu sigil, planetary correspondence, summoning rite, angelic rank, or initiatory current are therefore **[Modern invention]** unless supported by a specific modern author and publication. The being is conspicuously less important in historical Western occultism than Pazuzu, Lilith/Lilītu, or modernized “Sumerian” versions of major gods.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The central historiographical problem

Older and popular writing often begins with the Assyrian colossus and projects it backward into Sumer. Current philological evidence requires the reverse procedure:

- start with the female anthropomorphic Lamma;
- track her pairing with *alad/šēdu*;
- distinguish divine class, personal protective power, small ritual image, and architectural colossus;
- only then explain why modern museums use “lamassu” for the monumental hybrid.

### Principal scholarly resources

- **Chicago Assyrian Dictionary, L and Š volumes:** lexical attestations of *lamassu* and *šēdu*.
- **ePSD/ORACC:** Sumerian forms, dates, and corpus occurrences.
- **ETCSL:** literary translations, especially Gudea, *Bau A*, royal hymns, and *Inana and Bilulu*.
- **RINAP:** critical editions of Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions and bull-colossus texts.
- **Wolfram von Soden, “Die Schutzgenien Lamassu und Schedu in der babylonisch-assyrischen Literatur” (1964):** foundational philological study of the pair.
- **Frans Wiggermann and the Reallexikon der Assyriologie:** classification of protective spirits and apotropaic traditions.
- **Jeremy Black and Anthony Green, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*:** accessible but cautious iconographic synthesis.
- **Henri Frankfort:** influential study of seals and presentation scenes, though individual identifications require rechecking against later corpora.
- **Thorkild Jacobsen:** broad theological interpretation useful for intellectual history but not a substitute for object-level attestations.
- **Andrew George’s *Gilgamesh* and W. G. Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths*:** crucial partly because they expose popular claims of Lamassu appearances in those epics as unsupported.

### Disputed questions

#### Is *lamassu* Sumerian or Semitic?

The Sumerian–Akkadian equivalence is certain; ultimate etymology is not. Borrowing from Sumerian is plausible, while Lambert and others noted evidence for a Semitic formation. The question remains open enough that categorical internet etymologies should be avoided.

#### Are Lamassu and Šēdu separate beings?

Often yes: grammar, pairing, and Sumerian equivalents support female Lamma/lamassu versus male alad/šēdu. But first-millennium monumental terminology overlaps, and modern object labels cannot reliably reconstruct the ancient name of every uninscribed colossus. They are related classes, not universally interchangeable synonyms and not always two rigid biological sexes of one animal species.

#### What does **dALAD.dLAMMA** mean?

Royal inscriptions clearly apply it to monumental guardians, but “aladlammû” may be a learned compound or rebus-like designation. Its history cautions against treating a museum reading as a transparent folk name used identically in every period.

#### Are all introducing goddesses Lamma?

No. The pose is strongly associated with protective/intercessory goddesses, but many uninscribed figures cannot be individually named. “Lamma-type goddess” is often safer than “the goddess Lamassu.”

#### Did the colossi support arches?

They could function structurally or visually at massive doorways, and Khorsabad descriptions mention supporting brick vaulting. Their primary documented ideological function, however, is apotropaic guardianship; purely structural explanation is insufficient.

### State and location of the evidence

Relevant originals and fragments are distributed among:

- Iraq Museum, Baghdad;
- Mosul Cultural Museum and archaeological stores;
- Nimrud, Nineveh, and Khorsabad;
- British Museum, London;
- Louvre, Paris;
- Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York;
- Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures, Chicago;
- Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin;
- other European, American, and Middle Eastern collections.

The dispersal reflects excavation partage, diplomatic gifts, purchase, colonial power, destructive removal, loss in transport, looting, and modern war. Khorsabad fragments remain incompletely catalogued in Chicago; material at Iraqi sites was destroyed or scattered in 2015. The corpus is consequently large but profoundly uneven.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- Lamassu appears as a zodiac in *Gilgamesh* and *Enūma eliš*.
- Every Assyrian human-headed winged bull was anciently called “Lamassu.”
- The bull body always means strength, wings freedom, and head intelligence.
- Lamassu is an angel directly inherited by Judaism and Christianity.
- Lamassu is identical with the cherub, sphinx, Papsukkal, Išum, or Inara.
- Lamassu had a continuous secret occult cult.
- The five legs indicate an actual five-legged mythical anatomy.
- Human sacrifice or sacred prostitution belonged to Lamassu worship.
- All objects attacked by ISIS in 2015 were replicas.
- Lamassu and Lamaštu are good and evil forms of one goddess.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the attested record |
|---|---|
| c. 2600–2500 BCE | **ED IIIa:** earliest dictionary-dated attestations of divine *Lamma/Lammar*, a female tutelary deity. |
| c. 2500–2350 BCE | **ED IIIb:** further Lamma evidence in southern Mesopotamian documentation. |
| c. 22nd century BCE | Gudea Cylinders pair a favorable *alad* and *lamma* as escorts supplied through Ĝatumdug. |
| c. 2112–2004 BCE | Ur III seals and texts develop the anthropomorphic introducing/protective goddess type. |
| c. 2000–1600 BCE | Old Babylonian hymns and prayers attest Bau’s Beneficent Lamma, plural protective goddesses, and royal protective retinues. |
| Second millennium BCE | Lexical traditions stabilize Sumerian *Lamma/alad* and Akkadian *lamassu/šēdu* correspondences. |
| Later second millennium BCE | *An = Anum* and related lists systematize named Lamma figures within divine households. |
| 883–859 BCE | Ashurnasirpal II installs monumental human-headed winged bulls and lions at the Northwest Palace, Nimrud. |
| Ninth–eighth centuries BCE | Guardian-colossus program expands to other Assyrian royal and provincial sites. |
| 744–727 BCE | Tiglath-pileser III’s inscribed basalt bulls stand at Ḫadattu/Arslan Tash. |
| 721–705 BCE | Sargon II installs monumental five-legged bulls at Dur-Šarrukin/Khorsabad. |
| 704–681 BCE | Sennacherib installs stone and metal colossi at Nineveh; inscriptions invoke enduring good šēdu and lamassu. |
| c. 693–691 BCE | Late Sennacherib bull inscriptions explicitly associate palace permanence with good šēdu and lamassu. |
| 680–669 BCE | Esarhaddon repairs and reinstalls temple protective figures. |
| 612 BCE | Nineveh falls; Assyrian monumental production and palace cultic administration collapse. |
| Sixth century BCE | Adad-guppi invokes good šēdu and lamassu as Sîn’s gifts of longevity and dynastic protection. |
| Sixth–fourth centuries BCE | Achaemenid imperial art adopts related human-headed winged-bull forms; “lamassu” for them is modern comparative usage. |
| Late first millennium BCE–first centuries CE | Decline of cuneiform institutions ends the recoverable textual tradition; continuous Lamassu worship is not documented. |
| 1843–1844 | Botta excavates Sargon II’s palace at Khorsabad. |
| February–May 1847 | Khorsabad finds reach Paris; Louvre opens its Assyrian museum. |
| 1845–1851 | Layard excavates Nimrud and Nineveh; colossal guardians enter the British Museum and European public culture. |
| 1850s | Casts and reconstructions in the Crystal Palace “Nineveh Court” popularize the image. |
| 1928–1935 | Oriental Institute excavates Khorsabad; a reconstructed monumental bull eventually becomes a Chicago museum icon. |
| 1932 | Rockefeller gift brings the Metropolitan Museum’s Nimrud bull into its collection. |
| 2003 | Looting of the Iraq Museum stimulates documentation and reconstruction projects, including Rakowitz’s later work. |
| 2007 | Michael Rakowitz begins *The Invisible Enemy Should Not Exist*. |
| 26 February 2015 | ISIS video documents destruction at Mosul Museum and attacks associated with Nineveh’s guardians. |
| March–April 2015 | ISIS systematically destroys large portions of Nimrud, including surviving palace guardians and reliefs. |
| December 2016 | UNESCO conducts a rapid post-liberation assessment at Nimrud. |
| 28 March 2018 | Rakowitz’s reconstructed Nergal Gate lamassu is unveiled on Trafalgar Square’s Fourth Plinth. |
| 2020 | Ramsen Shamon publishes interviews documenting the lamassu’s importance to modern Assyrian identity. |
| 2020s | Iraqi and international projects document fragments, reconstruct destroyed monuments, and reassess colonial museum histories. |
| 2023 | Iraqi-French work re-exposes a large Khorsabad bull whose body had remained buried; renewed attention illustrates that the archaeological record is still developing. |
| Present | The lamassu remains an Assyrian and Iraqi heritage emblem, museum icon, reconstructed monument, fantasy creature, and object of small-scale neopagan reinterpretation—but no continuous ancient cult is demonstrable. |
