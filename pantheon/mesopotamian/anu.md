# An / Anu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male god; personified sky/heaven; ancestral “father of the gods”; divine king and source of legitimate authority in Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, and Assyrian traditions.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The divine name **An** is securely attested in Early Dynastic Sumerian texts of the third millennium BCE, including the god-list tradition represented at Fara/Šuruppag, Abu Salabikh, and Uruk, approximately the twenty-sixth century BCE. Claims that the fourth-millennium “White Temple” at Uruk was already An’s temple remain archaeological inference, not a contemporary written identification. The building itself is substantially earlier than the first unambiguous written evidence for the god.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Secure third-millennium evidence.
- **[Later canonical]** Widely transmitted second- or first-millennium literary theology.
- **[Regional variant]** A city-, kingdom-, or period-specific formulation.
- **[Learned systematization]** Scribal god lists, commentaries, numerical theology, or antiquarian synthesis.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically datable equation or fusion.
- **[Late accretion]** Late Babylonian, Hellenistic, medieval, or early-modern development.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim not present in ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

An is the Sumerian sky god and the divine personification of **an**, “sky” or “heaven.” Akkadian speakers adopted the god as **Anu** or **Anum**. Across more than two millennia he remained conceptually supreme—“father of the gods,” convener or senior member of the divine assembly, donor of kingship and divine offices—even when Enlil, Marduk, Aššur, or other gods exercised the more active leadership of particular political theologies.

That distinction is fundamental. Anu’s position at the head of lists or genealogies does not prove that his cult was everywhere the largest or politically dominant. Early god lists could begin with Enlil, while narrative and theological texts reserved a remote seniority for An. In many compositions he authorizes, judges, or delegates rather than acting directly. ORACC’s synthesis appropriately describes him as an authority figure, decision-maker, and progenitor whose prerogatives could be shared with or transferred to rising gods while his formal status remained high ([ORACC AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/an/)).

### Names and writings

- **Sumerian:** **An**, conventionally written 𒀭, the sign **AN**.
- **Akkadian:** **Anu**, nominative **Anum**; syllabic writings include *da-nu*, *da-num*, and occasionally *an-nu*.
- **Logographic:** 𒀭 or **dAN** in Akkadian contexts.
- **Learned numerical writing:** **d60**, since 60 was Anu’s divine number.
- **Feminine counterpart:** **Antu/Antum**, grammatically and theologically formed as the feminine of Anu in Akkadian tradition.

The AN sign also serves as **DINGIR**, “god,” and as the divine determinative placed before divine names. An’s own Sumerian name normally does not require a separate divine determinative because the sign is already his name and the general divine classifier. This identity between “sky,” “god,” and An’s written name made the sign exceptionally fertile for ancient scribal theology, but it does not mean every occurrence of AN in an archaic document designates the personal god.

### Etymology

**[Early attested]** Sumerian *an* means “sky, heaven.” The simplest historical interpretation is that the god is the sky personified. The Akkadian form *Anu(m)* is a borrowing or adaptation of the Sumerian divine name.

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** Broader attempts to connect An genetically with Semitic *ʾil/El*, Indo-European sky gods, or distant Asian divine names rely on typological similarities—high gods, father gods, or sky deities—not demonstrable linguistic descent. No ancient bilingual text equates Anu with the West Semitic El as a universal identity.

### Titles and epithets

Attested or well-supported titles include:

- **“Great An”** (*An gal*): an elevated designation in Sumerian literary texts.
- **“Father of the gods”**: widespread Akkadian theological title.
- **“King of the gods”** and **“king of the Igigi and Anunnaki”**: especially visible in first-millennium hymnic and royal language.
- **“Supreme one”** or senior god: Assyrian royal invocations can list Anu among the highest divine authorities; one RINAP text calls him “the supreme one” ([RINAP text](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/Q003812)).
- **“Lord of the heavens”**: functional title rather than an exclusive fixed epithet.
- **Holder of the *anūtu***: “Anu-ship,” the condition or authority of being Anu. Other gods’ elevation could be described as receiving this sovereignty.
- **“Father An”**: a relational and honorific description in hymnic tradition.

The expression “your word is Anu” in *Enūma eliš* signifies that Marduk’s command has become as unalterable as Anu’s. It is not evidence that Marduk literally became the same personal god.

### Identifications and equations

- **An = Anu:** A Sumerian–Akkadian lexical equivalence, not foreign interpretatio. The equation eventually supplied the title of the great god list *An = Anum*.
- **Anu–Enlil–Ea:** Not one triune being but a recurrent hierarchical grouping corresponding broadly to heaven, inhabited/cosmic earth, and subterranean fresh water.
- **Anu and Anšar:** **[Syncretistic/learned]** Late Assyrian theology could substitute Aššur for Anšar, and some learned texts equate or parallel Anu and Anšar. This served Assyrian claims that Aššur possessed primordial seniority; it is not the genealogy of every period.
- **Anu and Amurru:** **[Regional/learned equation]** Attested in some god-list theology, but the two gods retained independent identities and cult histories.
- **Anu and Enmešarra/Dumuzi:** **[Late Uruk systematization]** Seleucid Urukian scholarship could absorb or correlate these figures within an Anu-centered pantheon.
- **Anu and Greek Zeus:** Greek observers and modern writers naturally compare them as senior sky gods, but direct evidence for a universally institutionalized Anu–Zeus identification is limited. Greek names used by elite Uruk families show participation in Hellenistic society, not automatic replacement of Anu by Zeus.
- **Anu and Saturn:** **[Modern invention when presented as a timeless identity]** Anu was associated with the highest or equatorial heaven, and late astronomical theology made many complex divine–planetary correlations. “Anu was Saturn” is not a generally valid Mesopotamian equation.
- **Anu as a monotheistic creator:** **[Modern overstatement]** Some texts grant him creative or supreme power, but the corpus remains persistently polytheistic.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

The first unambiguous evidence is textual, not monumental.

**[Early attested, ca. twenty-sixth century BCE]** Early Dynastic god lists from Fara/Šuruppag and related copies at Abu Salabikh and Uruk record established pantheons. The great Fara list contained hundreds of divine names. Richard Litke cautioned that the archaic Fara lists do not form a simple direct textual ancestor of *An = Anum* and noted that some begin with Enlil rather than An ([Litke, *A Reconstruction of the Assyro-Babylonian God-Lists*](https://babylonian-collection.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/Litke%2C%20Richard%20L_%20-%20A%20Reconstruction%20of%20the%20Assyro-Babylonian%20God-Lists_%20TBC%203%2C%201998.pdf)). Thus:

- The lists prove that An was an established named deity.
- Their ordering documents scribal classification, not a universal measure of popular devotion.
- They do not establish that every earlier AN sign referred to him.
- They do not authenticate the White Temple’s dedication.

The Early Dynastic **zà-mì hymns** from Abu Salabikh provide another window into the organized divine geography of Sumer; the surviving corpus is catalogued in ORACC’s early-literature materials ([ORACC Early Literature](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/earlylit/withatf)).

### The White Temple problem

**[Archaeologically secure building; divine attribution uncertain]** The White Temple stood on a high, repeatedly enlarged terrace in Uruk’s Kullaba sector during the later fourth millennium BCE. It was a white-plastered, tripartite monumental building with altar and offering installations. Its cultic character is highly probable.

Calling it the **“Anu ziggurat”** is nevertheless retrospective. Excavators associated the district with Anu because a securely identified Anu sanctuary occupied the area thousands of years later. No surviving fourth-millennium dedicatory inscription names An as the temple’s owner. Accordingly:

- “White Temple at Uruk”: secure.
- “Monumental cult building”: strongly supported.
- “Temple of An”: possible but unproved.
- “Earliest secure attestation of An”: incorrect.

The distinction is emphasized in modern studies of the late Anu cult: even where the terrace is conventionally named for Anu, the historical attribution remains uncertain ([Krul, historical background](https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/9789004364943/BP000002.xml)).

### Principal corpus

#### Early royal and hymnic material

**[Early attested, third millennium BCE]** Royal inscriptions invoke An among the gods who grant authority, fix destiny, or legitimate rule. Lugalzagesi of Uruk, late Early Dynastic period, appears in a political theology involving An, Enlil, Enki, and other major gods; the surviving inscription is available through ETCSRI ([Lugalzagesi inscription](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/etcsri/Q001381)).

Ur III and Old Babylonian copies preserve Sumerian hymns and narratives in which An is a remote sovereign, father, or member of the assembly. The compositions often survive in school tablets centuries later than their presumed initial composition, so “Sumerian” does not automatically mean fourth- or early-third-millennium.

#### *Enki and Ninmaḫ*

**[Old Babylonian manuscript tradition, probably incorporating older theology]** Humanity is created to relieve the gods of labor. An, Enlil, and Enki/Nudimmud occupy the senior divine level; later in the feast “An, Enlil, and lord Nudimmud” roast sacred kids. The actual contest of human formation belongs chiefly to Enki and Ninmaḫ, not An. The ETCSL translation preserves the composition and its textual uncertainties ([ETCSL, *Enki and Ninmaḫ*](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr112.htm)).

#### *Inana and Ebiḫ*

**[Old Babylonian copies of Sumerian literature]** Inana appeals to An before attacking Mount Ebiḫ. An does not grant the authorization she wants; she proceeds independently, thereby dramatizing both his formal supremacy and her aggressive autonomy. Inana also says An has made her terrifying throughout heaven. The text survives as a composite assembled from multiple tablets ([ETCSL composite](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/c132.htm)).

#### *Inana’s Descent to the Netherworld*

**[Old Babylonian Sumerian witnesses]** An is principally embedded in the cosmic and genealogical order rather than acting as rescuer. The seven Anuna serve as judges in the netherworld. Ninšubur’s appeals move through major divine authorities, with Enki ultimately providing the successful rescue. The text should not be paraphrased as a simple “An rescues his daughter” story.

#### *Gilgameš and the Bull of Heaven*

**[Sumerian, Old Babylonian manuscript witnesses]** Inana demands the Bull of Heaven from her father An to kill Gilgameš. An initially refuses, warning that the bull grazes only at the horizon and that releasing it will cause devastation. After her threat to make an uproar reaching heaven and earth, he relinquishes the bull. The bull is killed by Gilgameš and Enkidu. The text explicitly calls him “Great An” and has him address Inana as “my child” ([ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr1812.htm); [manuscripts and bibliography](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/b1812.htm)).

#### Akkadian *Epic of Gilgameš*, Tablet VI

**[Later canonical Akkadian elaboration; Standard Babylonian form mainly first-millennium witnesses]** Ištar asks Anu for the Bull after Gilgameš rejects and insults her. Anu questions whether Gilgameš’s charges are justified and warns that the bull will produce seven years of famine unless provisions exist. Ištar claims she has stored grain; Anu releases the bull. This version enlarges the dialogue and famine calculation. Andrew George’s critical edition is the necessary modern control; the Sumerian and Akkadian episodes are related but not identical versions of one fixed text.

#### *Atraḫasīs*

**[Old Babylonian canonical myth, principal copy associated with the reign of Ammi-ṣaduqa]** The cosmos is apportioned: Anu receives heaven, Enlil the earth, and Enki the subterranean waters. Anu belongs to the senior assembly governing divine labor and human creation. In the flood sequence Enlil drives the destruction; Ea/Enki circumvents the decision to save Atraḫasīs. The birth goddess later blames Enlil and Anu for the catastrophe. Anu is therefore implicated in collective rule, but calling him the sole sender of the flood would distort the text. The Old Babylonian and later Assyrian recensions must be distinguished ([overview and witnesses](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/anet/104-106-the-epic-of-atrahasis/); [SOAS discussion](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/398056)).

#### *Enūma eliš*

**[Later canonical Babylonian theology, probably composed or consolidated in the late second millennium BCE; chiefly first-millennium manuscripts]** Anu is the son and heir of Anšar and Kišar and father of Nudimmud/Ea. When Tiamat threatens the gods, Anšar sends Anu against her; Anu sees that he cannot overcome her and withdraws. Ea also fails to resolve the crisis, preparing for Marduk’s elevation. Marduk’s command is subsequently declared equivalent to Anu’s, a transfer or concentration of supreme authority in the Babylonian city god. This genealogy is important but late; it must not be projected back into Early Dynastic belief. Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths* is the modern critical standard; an older complete translation illustrates the Tablet I sequence ([University of Chicago, *The Babylonian Genesis*](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/misc_genesis.pdf)).

#### *Erra and Išum*

**[First-millennium Akkadian epic]** Anu gives the seven destructive gods, the Sebetti, to Erra as weapons. Their deployment enables massacre and urban devastation. This is literary divine violence, not evidence that Anu’s temple conducted human sacrifice.

#### *An = Anum*

**[Learned systematization, Middle Babylonian formation with first-millennium copies]** This seven-tablet list organizes roughly two thousand divine names, spouses, courtiers, offspring, titles, and equations. It opens from the equivalence An = Anum and places Anu at the summit of an encyclopedic pantheon. It is evidence for scholarly taxonomy, not a scripture accepted in one invariant form throughout Mesopotamia. The edition remains Litke’s reconstruction; ORACC supplies the tablet sequence and explains its Middle Babylonian compilation ([ORACC technical terms](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/technicalterms/index.html); [modern study of the list tradition](https://cdn.mohrsiebeck.com/66%2C6c930660ee40b7b9d254c7d9c8e2e9.pdf)).

#### *Enūma Anu Enlil*

**[Learned divination series]** Its title, “When Anu and Enlil…,” invokes the two gods as cosmic authorities over celestial signs. The series of celestial omens developed over the second and first millennia and should not be confused with *Enūma eliš*. Late Urukian specialists bore the title *ṭupšar Enūma Anu Enlil*, “scribe of *Enūma Anu Enlil*,” linking Anu’s sanctuary with astral scholarship.

#### Assyrian royal inscriptions

**[Regional imperial theology]** Neo-Assyrian kings invoked Anu within long divine lists even where Aššur was politically supreme. Such texts preserve Anu as king of the Igigi and Anunnaki or senior heavenly power, while relocating effective imperial patronage to Aššur. ORACC/RINAP makes these inscriptions searchable and exposes the actual divine sequences rather than harmonized summaries ([RINAP example](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/riao/ria4/Q004474)).

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

An’s primary domain is the **sky as the highest cosmic realm**. In tripartite schemes:

- Anu rules heaven.
- Enlil rules the inhabited terrestrial or atmospheric sphere.
- Enki/Ea rules the subterranean fresh-water Apsû.

Other functions include:

- Convening or presiding over the divine assembly.
- Fixing destinies or ratifying decisions.
- Conferring kingship.
- Assigning divine offices and powers.
- Increasing another god’s rank.
- Generating gods and destructive beings.
- Authorizing weapons, monsters, or punishments.
- Serving as the ultimate point of appeal.

His decisions are frequently represented as immutable, yet myths complicate that formula: Inana can defy or coerce him; he retreats from Tiamat; Enlil or Marduk can exercise more immediate power. “Supreme” therefore describes rank and legitimating authority more consistently than narrative omnipotence.

### Cosmic location

**[Later learned cosmography]** One three-heaven model assigns Anu the uppermost heaven, made of reddish *luludānītu* stone. This is a first-millennium learned cosmology, not necessarily the model presumed in every Sumerian composition.

### Symbols

- **Horned crown:** Anu’s principal emblem; it can rest on a throne rather than appear on a securely identified anthropomorphic body.
- **Bull:** His animal and an emblem of potency and celestial force. The Bull of Heaven belongs to him or is at his disposal.
- **Throne:** Symbol of remote sovereignty.
- **Number 60:** Highest value in a sexagesimal hierarchy of divine numbers; used as the learned writing **d60**.
- **Heaven and star sign:** The AN/DINGIR sign’s later star-like graphic form encourages modern star symbolism, but one should not turn every eight-pointed star into an exclusive emblem of Anu. The star was also a generic divine sign and had specific associations with Ištar.

### Images

There is **no anthropomorphic image universally and securely identifiable as Anu**. Older art-historical identifications of enthroned, horned male figures can be plausible without being demonstrable. Mesopotamian art often distinguishes divinity through horned crowns but does not label every figure.

The strongest specific emblematic representation is the horned crown above a throne. On the stele of Šamši-Adad V, ca. 815 BCE, the king stands before divine symbols; ORACC identifies the upper horned crown as Anu’s ([ORACC AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/an/)). This is an emblem, not a portrait.

### Colors, plants, days, and substances

No universally attested ancient system assigns Anu one fixed color, weekday, herb, crystal, incense, or magical ingredient. Modern charts that confidently give him blue, lapis lazuli, a weekday, or a chakra usually synthesize later Western occult correspondences. The reddish stone of his highest heaven is a cosmographic material, not necessarily a cult color.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

#### No stated origin

**[Early attested]** The oldest sources do not supply An with parents. He functions as a primordial or already-present heavenly power. Absence of a genealogy is not evidence for a lost version in which he was “self-created.”

#### Heaven and Earth

**[Sumerian cosmic formulation]** An and Ki, Heaven and Earth, form a primordial pair. In several cosmogonic allusions heaven and earth begin united and are subsequently separated, often through Enlil. Ki is not consistently developed as an independent goddess with a uniform cult biography.

#### Uraš as consort

**[Third-millennium regional tradition]** Uraš, a female earth deity distinct from the male agricultural god Uraš in other contexts, appears as An’s wife and mother of particular deities in Lagashite and other traditions.

#### Antu as consort

**[Akkadian and especially late Uruk canonical form]** Antu/Antum becomes Anu’s principal spouse. Her name is effectively the feminine of his. The pairing reaches exceptional institutional importance in Achaemenid and Seleucid Uruk.

#### Anšar and Kišar as parents

**[Later canonical, *Enūma eliš*]** Anu is the firstborn or heir of Anšar and Kišar; Anu then begets Nudimmud/Ea. This is a late Babylonian literary genealogy rather than the earliest tradition ([ORACC, Anšar and Kišar](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/AnsharandKishar/index.html)).

### Children

Texts call numerous gods Anu’s sons or daughters, but “son/daughter” can express rank, dependence, local genealogy, or patronage as well as biological descent.

Documented children include, in one or more traditions:

- **Inana/Ištar**, daughter.
- **Enlil**, son in some genealogies, though other theogonies treat the relationship differently.
- **Enki/Ea/Nudimmud**, son in *Enūma eliš* and related systems.
- **Iškur/Adad**, son in the dominant Akkadian genealogy; some Sumerian traditions instead make Iškur the son of Enlil.
- **Nanna/Sîn**, occasionally son, although the better-known Nippur genealogy makes him Enlil and Ninlil’s son.
- **Nergal**, son in some literary traditions.
- **Ningirsu**, Baba, and Gatumdug in third-millennium Lagashite sources.
- **Nidaba, Nanaya, Ninisina, Ninkarrak, Ninmug, Ninsumun, Nungal**, and other goddesses in particular texts or lists.
- **Lamaštu, Asag, and the Sebetti**, generated or deployed by Anu in destructive-demon traditions.

These cannot be combined into one literal family tree without erasing local theology.

### Every documented named divine interaction in the principal surviving corpus

The following entries separate discrete interactions. “Joint appearance” means co-presence in one textual scene or formal divine sequence, not proof of a narrated personal relationship.

- **Antu — spouse and ritual partner.** Anu and Antu receive paired offerings and ceremonies at late Uruk’s Bīt Rēš. *Late canonical/Seleucid cult.*
- **Uraš — consort.** Third-millennium traditions pair heavenly An with terrestrial Uraš. *Early/regional.*
- **Ki — cosmic counterpart.** An and Ki form Heaven-and-Earth; their separation establishes differentiated cosmos. *Sumerian cosmogonic tradition.*
- **Anšar — father and commander.** In *Enūma eliš* I, Anšar fathers Anu and later sends him against Tiamat. *Later canonical.*
- **Kišar — mother.** *Enūma eliš* makes Anu the offspring of Anšar and Kišar. *Later canonical.*
- **Nudimmud/Ea — son.** Anu begets Nudimmud in *Enūma eliš* I. *Later canonical.*
- **Tiamat — failed confrontation.** Anu approaches but cannot overcome her and retreats. *Later canonical.*
- **Marduk — transfer of supreme authority.** Marduk’s command is proclaimed equivalent to Anu’s; Marduk receives comprehensive kingship after defeating Tiamat. *Later Babylonian canonical.*
- **Enlil — co-ruler and successor in active sovereignty.** They jointly allocate domains and appear together in assemblies, invocations, and *Enūma Anu Enlil*. *Early through late canonical.*
- **Enki/Ea — co-ruler.** Anu, Enlil, and Ea repeatedly form the supreme triad; in *Atraḫasīs*, their cosmic portions are distinguished. *Old Babylonian canonical.*
- **Ninmaḫ/Nintu/Bēlet-ilī — assembly relationship.** An belongs to the senior order within which humanity’s creation is authorized; the actual forming work is assigned to the mother goddess and Enki. *Old Babylonian/Sumerian literary.*
- **Inana/Ištar — father and daughter; authorization and conflict.** She appeals to An concerning Ebiḫ, claims power from him, and coerces the Bull of Heaven from him. *Sumerian and Akkadian canonical.*
- **Gilgameš — indirect lethal conflict.** An releases the Bull of Heaven at Inana/Ištar’s demand to attack him. No direct meeting is recorded. *Sumerian and Akkadian canonical.*
- **Bull of Heaven/Gugalanna — owner or controller.** An keeps the bull at the horizon and eventually hands it to Inana/Ištar. *Canonical.*
- **Iškur/Adad — father-son relation.** The dominant Akkadian genealogy makes Adad Anu’s son; the competing Sumerian tradition makes Iškur Enlil’s son. *Regional variants.*
- **Nanna/Sîn — father in limited genealogies or co-listed deity.** This conflicts with the standard Nippur parentage from Enlil and Ninlil. *Regional/learned variant.*
- **Nergal — father or senior authority.** Some traditions call Nergal his son; in destructive theology Anu’s decisions help authorize violence later executed by underworld or warrior gods. *Later/regional.*
- **Erra — grants weapons.** Anu gives the Sebetti to Erra in *Erra and Išum*. *First-millennium canonical.*
- **Sebetti — creator/controller.** The seven are created or commissioned by Anu and given to Erra as destructive weapons. *First-millennium canonical.*
- **Išum — joint narrative setting.** Išum witnesses and moderates Erra’s campaign in an epic whose divine armament derives from Anu. No independent Anu–Išum encounter is securely preserved. *Absence finding.*
- **Lamaštu — progenitor.** Incantation traditions call her daughter of Anu, despite her hostility to humans. *Later magical/ritual.*
- **Asag — progenitor or origin-point.** Some learned traditions connect the demon with Anu. *Learned/ritual.*
- **Gatumdug — father.** Lagashite inscriptions call An her father. *Early regional.*
- **Baba — father.** Lagashite tradition makes Baba a daughter of An. *Early regional.*
- **Ningirsu — father in some Lagashite material.** Other traditions give different relational structures. *Early regional.*
- **Šara — father.** Some Sumerian literary or god-list traditions call Šara a son of An. *Regional.*
- **Nanaya — father.** Late Urukian and god-list traditions place her among his daughters or descendants. *Learned/regional.*
- **Nidaba — father.** Attested in particular genealogical formulations. *Regional.*
- **Ninisina/Ninkarrak — father.** Healing-goddess traditions sometimes define them as daughters of Anu. *Regional/learned.*
- **Ninsumun — father.** Some genealogical texts call Gilgameš’s divine mother An’s daughter. *Regional.*
- **Nungal — father.** Literary and list traditions can call the prison goddess An’s daughter. *Learned.*
- **Papsukkal — vizier and gatekeeper.** In Seleucid Uruk, Papsukkal serves as Anu’s chief minister and guards the Bīt Rēš. *Late Uruk canonical.*
- **Ninšubur — ministerial relation through equation with Papsukkal.** Late theology assimilated aspects of Ninšubur into Papsukkal’s office. This does not erase Ninšubur’s earlier independent and often female identity. *Late learned syncretism.*
- **Anunnaki/Anuna — head or progenitor.** Anu presides over or fathers the high divine collectivity; membership and number vary. *Canonical but variable.*
- **Igigi — king or senior authority.** First-millennium titles call him king of the Igigi. In *Atraḫasīs*, the Igigi rebel against labor assigned within the senior gods’ order. *Later canonical.*
- **Aššur — parallel and appropriation.** Assyrian theology gives Aššur supreme political functions and sometimes identifies him with Anšar, thereby placing him above the Babylonian genealogy in which Anu is descended from Anšar. *Imperial syncretistic theology.*
- **Amurru — equation in lists.** No major narrative encounter is required by the learned identification. *Learned syncretism.*
- **Enmešarra — late equation or assimilation.** Seleucid Urukian theological ordering could place his identity or attributes within Anu’s expanded sovereignty. *Late learned.*
- **Dumuzi — late equation or correlation.** Certain Urukian systems relate him to Anu; older narratives keep them distinct. *Late learned.*
- **Ishtaran — proposed relationship.** Modern scholars have suggested late coordination between Uruk and Der theology, but direct evidence for a complete Anu–Ishtaran equation is inadequate. *Unestablished.*

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The ordering of Heaven and Earth

**[Early Sumerian cosmogonic complex]** Many compositions presuppose that heaven and earth once formed a unity and were separated as cosmic order emerged. An represents heaven; Ki or Earth is its counterpart; Enlil is frequently credited with effecting or maintaining the separation.

There is no single surviving “Book of An” narrating the event from beginning to end. Modern retellings often combine allusions from different texts into a synthetic creation epic. The secure core is the conceptual pair **an-ki**, “heaven and earth,” and the later differentiation of divine domains.

### Creation of humans

In *Enki and Ninmaḫ*, laboring gods complain, and human beings are fashioned so that they can assume work. An belongs to the senior divine order but is not the hands-on sculptor. In *Atraḫasīs*, the gods kill a divine victim and combine divine flesh/blood with clay; the mother goddess and Ea organize the birth process. Anu participates in governance and cosmic allocation rather than personally molding humanity.

**Modern claim rejected:** “Anu genetically engineered Homo sapiens” is absent from every cuneiform recension. It derives from twentieth-century ancient-astronaut literature.

### Inana and Ebiḫ

Inana resolves to destroy the mountain Ebiḫ because it does not submit to her. She approaches An and presents her complaint. An cautions or refuses rather than enthusiastically authorizing the attack. Inana proceeds, overwhelms the mountain, and celebrates her victory.

The myth reveals:

- An’s position as a formal source of authorization.
- The possibility that another god may act without his consent.
- Inana’s martial independence.
- A literary contrast between institutional hierarchy and charismatic power.

### The Bull of Heaven

#### Sumerian version

Inana demands the bull from her father. An objects that it belongs at the horizon and would lack pasture in Uruk. She threatens to make her cry heard across heaven and earth. He yields. The bull attacks; Gilgameš and Enkidu kill it.

#### Standard Babylonian version

After Gilgameš rejects Ištar’s proposal and lists the ruin of her former lovers, Ištar asks Anu for the bull. Anu asks whether she provoked the insult and warns of seven lean years. She claims to have stored sufficient provisions. He gives her the bull. It opens pits that kill men before Gilgameš and Enkidu defeat it. Enkidu’s insulting response to Ištar contributes to the gods’ decision that he must die.

The Akkadian version is not merely a translation. It gives Anu a more developed prudential role and integrates the episode into the epic’s mortality arc.

### The revolt of the gods and the Flood

In *Atraḫasīs*, junior gods labor on canals and earthworks, rebel, and surround Enlil’s residence. The senior gods negotiate rather than simply annihilate them. Humanity is created to take over labor. Human multiplication and noise later disturb Enlil, who sends successive population checks and finally a flood. Ea’s covert warning saves Atraḫasīs.

Anu’s role is collective and constitutional:

- He holds the heavenly lot.
- He sits with Enlil and Ea among the senior rulers.
- He participates in the assembly whose decisions affect humanity.
- The birth goddess implicates him with Enlil in the flood disaster.

The motif of divine hunger after sacrifices cease during the flood underscores cult’s reciprocal economics: humans feed the gods through offerings; destroying humanity imperils divine sustenance.

### The Tiamat crisis and Marduk’s elevation

In *Enūma eliš*, primordial generations lead from Apsû and Tiamat through Laḫmu/Laḫamu and Anšar/Kišar to Anu and Ea. After Tiamat prepares war:

1. Anšar commissions Anu.
2. Anu approaches but cannot face her power.
3. Ea likewise cannot solve the crisis.
4. Marduk agrees to fight if granted unrivaled command.
5. The gods confer kingship.
6. Marduk defeats Tiamat and constructs the ordered cosmos.
7. His word is declared equal to Anu’s.

The scene does not necessarily “humiliate” Anu. Structurally, the failure of older gods creates the legal and narrative necessity for Marduk’s promotion. It is Babylonian political theology expressed through divine succession.

### Anu and the Sebetti

In *Erra and Išum*, Anu provides Erra with seven terrifying divine weapons. Erra unleashes devastation while Išum attempts to limit or redirect the catastrophe. The myth reflects urban warfare, epidemic terror, and the instability of divine protection. Anu’s role is that of armorer and authorizer, not battlefield protagonist.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Uruk and Eanna

**[Secure from the third millennium onward]** Uruk was An’s principal city, but its sacred geography was never exclusively his. **Eanna**, “House of Heaven,” is overwhelmingly associated with Inana/Ištar in surviving administrative and literary evidence. An was worshipped there with her during portions of the third and later millennia.

Claims that Eanna began as “Anu’s temple” and was later usurped by Inana exceed the evidence. The temple’s name can invoke heaven without proving sole ownership by the god An.

### The White Temple and terrace

The monumental fourth-millennium complex in Kullaba is archaeologically real. Its conventional designation as Anu’s precinct derives from later cultic geography. No offering list naming An has securely tied the White Temple itself to him. It therefore cannot be used without qualification as proof of an organized cult of An ca. 3400 BCE.

### Der

Der, east of the Tigris near the Iranian frontier, was also called a “city of Anu.” The local pantheon centered strongly on Ishtaran, and relations among Anu, Ishtaran, and other local gods remain incompletely understood. Der demonstrates that Anu’s cult was not confined to Uruk.

### Aššur

Šamši-Adad I, early second millennium BCE, transformed or rebuilt the temple of Adad at Aššur as a double temple of **Anu and Adad**. The pairing expressed the dominant genealogy in which the storm god was Anu’s son. It also embedded the sky/storm relationship within royal building policy.

### Bīt Rēš at Uruk

**[Late Babylonian and Seleucid securely attested]** Anu’s most spectacular institutional revival occurred at the **Bīt Rēš** or Rēš temple in Uruk. Anu and Antu became the city’s principal divine couple, displacing Ištar and Nanaya from the top of the local offering hierarchy without eliminating their worship.

The complex included:

- Monumental courtyards and sanctuaries.
- A ziggurat dedicated to Anu.
- Residences and working areas for temple personnel.
- A learned tablet collection.
- Ritual connections with Irigal, Eanna, and the extramural akītu house.

Greek administrative documents translated into Akkadian on YOS 20, 87 concern the rebuilding of the sanctuary in the early Seleucid period, showing direct interaction between Seleucid government and Uruk’s priestly establishment ([official correspondence study](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/aofo-2023-0007/html?lang=en)).

### Priests and scholars

Late Uruk’s Anu establishment employed:

- **ēreb bīti**, temple-enterers authorized to enter restricted sacred space.
- **mašmaššu/āšipu**, ritual experts or exorcists.
- **kalû**, lamentation priests.
- Singers, brewers, sacrificial personnel, and administrators.
- The **aḫu rabû**, “Elder Brother,” a high-ranking office.
- Astronomer-scribes associated with *Enūma Anu Enlil*.

The Ekur-zākir and Sîn-lēqi-unninni families transmitted ritual, astronomical, omen, lexical, and commentary texts. A room in the Rēš yielded approximately 140 fragmentary tablets, including scholarly works and archival records, documenting a learned community operating across generations ([Robson, *Ancient Knowledge Networks*](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10085697/1/Ancient-Knowledge-Networks.pdf)).

### Rituals and offerings

Late ritual tablets prescribe:

- Daily food and drink offerings.
- Bulls, rams, and other animal sacrifices.
- Purification.
- Processions of divine images.
- Garment and crown ceremonies.
- Gate-opening rites.
- Temple rebuilding rites.
- Great offerings and royal sacrifices.
- Paired worship of Anu and Antu.
- A nocturnal fire ceremony conducted on the ziggurat roof.

One ritual directs mašmaššu-priests, the high priest, temple-enterers, and brewers to greet the god: “Great Anu, may heaven and earth greet you” ([ORACC ritual tablet](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/cams/gkab/P296523)). The fire ceremony’s movement to the ziggurat roof joined terrestrial temple ritual with the heavenly aspects of Anu and Antu ([study of the ceremony](https://escholarship.org/content/qt38d086ct/qt38d086ct.pdf)).

### Cult images

The normal Mesopotamian temple presumption is that the god was present through a consecrated cult statue maintained, clothed, fed, and processed by priests. No surviving object can be identified with certainty as the principal statue of Anu from Bīt Rēš. Ritual texts are therefore stronger evidence for the image’s treatment than surviving sculpture is for its appearance.

### Votive and personal-name evidence

Anu appears in royal dedications, prayers, offering accounts, and theophoric names. Late Urukian names such as **Anu-uballiṭ**, “Anu has kept alive,” became particularly conspicuous. Some individuals also bore Greek names, demonstrating bilingual elite accommodation rather than abandonment of the cult.

### Human sacrifice

**Finding:** No secure evidence demonstrates institutional human sacrifice to Anu at Uruk, Der, or Aššur.

The slaughter of a god in *Atraḫasīs*, the release of the Bull of Heaven, and the Sebetti’s massacre in *Erra and Išum* are mythic violence, not cult records. Early Mesopotamian elite burials and controversial seal imagery raise broader questions about retainer death or ritual killing, but they cannot be assigned to Anu without direct evidence.

### “Temple prostitution”

**Finding:** No secure documentation links an institution of compulsory or commercial “sacred prostitution” to Anu’s cult. Older literature often translated several priestly or gender-diverse offices as prostitutes without adequate lexical support. The issue is especially associated with Ištar and Herodotus’s account of Babylon, not with demonstrable Bīt Rēš practice. It should not be smuggled into Anu’s dossier merely because he shared sacred space with Inana/Ištar.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Fourth millennium BCE: monumental prehistory

Uruk possessed the White Temple and vast ceremonial architecture. The later “Anu district” stood in Kullaba. A cult of a sky power may have existed, but identifying that power specifically as the later An is unproved.

### Early Dynastic period, ca. 2900–2350 BCE

An is securely named in texts. He participates in royal legitimation and the organization of the pantheon. However, Enlil is already extraordinarily prominent; the model of An beginning as universally dominant and then suddenly being “replaced” is too simple.

### Akkadian and Ur III periods, ca. 2334–2004 BCE

The Sumerian god is incorporated into Akkadian-speaking imperial religion as Anu. Divine kingship, cosmic hierarchy, and triadic formulas develop further. Ur III administrative evidence documents offerings and temple economies, while Sumerian literary compositions later copied in Old Babylonian schools preserve An as senior divine authority.

### Old Babylonian period, ca. 2000–1595 BCE

This is the manuscript horizon for much surviving Sumerian literature and for the major Old Babylonian *Atraḫasīs*. Anu is remote but juridically supreme, often paired with Enlil and Ea. The Anu–Adad temple at Aššur institutionalizes a father–son theology.

### Middle Babylonian and Middle Assyrian periods

God-list scholarship becomes increasingly systematic. The great *An = Anum* tradition classifies the pantheon around equivalences, households, and courts. Babylonian Marduk theology and Assyrian Aššur theology appropriate supreme functions while retaining Anu as ancestral source.

### First millennium BCE: Assyrian and Babylonian empires

Anu remains in royal invocations, omen scholarship, incantations, and canonical epics. Aššur in Assyria and Marduk in Babylonia command the political center. Anu’s high rank continues, but active popular cult is difficult to measure.

### Achaemenid transition

Older scholarship often claimed that Xerxes destroyed Babylonian temples after revolts in 484 BCE and that this directly caused Anu’s rise at Uruk. The evidence is more difficult. Administrative disruption and changes in priestly families are real; a single securely documented royal decree abolishing Eanna’s cult is not extant. “Xerxes’ suppression” should therefore be treated as a contested reconstruction, not a settled narrative.

### Late Achaemenid and Seleucid revival

Between the fourth and third centuries BCE, Urukian priests reorganized the city’s religious system around Anu and Antu. This was not invention ex nihilo: Anu had an ancient relationship with Uruk. But the scale, hierarchy, paired cult with Antu, expanded Bīt Rēš, new rituals, and learned use of *An = Anum* constitute a deliberate revival and reconstruction.

A ritual colophon tells how Kidin-Anu claimed to recover old cultic ordinances from tablets carried to Elam and copied them under Seleucus I and Antiochus I. Whether every recovered rite was genuinely ancient cannot be independently proved, but the story itself documents the priests’ ideology of restoration ([Seleucid correspondence and ritual tradition](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/aofo-2023-0007/html?lang=en)).

Seleucid royal support, local elite patronage, Greek–Babylonian bilingualism, mathematical astronomy, and conservative cuneiform scholarship all converged in the revival. Bīt Rēš was not a fossil untouched by Hellenism; it was a functioning Hellenistic Babylonian institution.

### Destruction and final cuneiform phase

Parts of Bīt Rēš suffered fire and rebuilding. The exact end of ritual continuity is poorly documented. Cuneiform scholarship at Uruk faded during the Parthian period; Babylonian scholarly writing survived elsewhere into the first century CE and perhaps later. No record proves that Anu’s public cult continued uninterrupted into medieval Iraq.

### Christianization and Islamization

There is no securely documented episode in which a Christian bishop or Muslim ruler specifically “suppressed the cult of Anu.” The cult disappeared amid the long contraction of cuneiform institutions, political changes under Parthian and Sasanian rule, and the growth of Aramaic-speaking religious communities. By the Islamic conquest of Iraq in the seventh century CE, an operating Anu temple is not documented.

Claims of direct survival under the names of angels, jinn, Yazidi beings, or Mandaean figures require evidence that has not been supplied. Formal resemblance between a high heavenly god and later celestial beings is not continuity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Assyriological rediscovery

Nineteenth-century decipherment of cuneiform restored Anu to modern historical awareness. George Smith’s publication of Babylonian creation and flood fragments, subsequent excavations at Uruk, and twentieth-century editions of god lists and epics made it possible to distinguish Sumerian An, Akkadian Anu, and later theological systems.

### Literature, reference works, and games

Anu appears as a senior or creator god in modern retellings of Mesopotamian mythology, fantasy reference books, role-playing settings, comics, animation, and games. These works commonly:

- Make him a visibly anthropomorphic old king.
- Present one fixed family tree.
- Treat him as the uncontested ruler of every Mesopotamian era.
- Conflate Anu with all Anunnaki.
- Give him elemental “sky magic” in a modern game taxonomy.

These are legitimate adaptations but are not ancient iconography or theology.

### Occult and ceremonial magic

**Finding:** Anu did not become a major, continuously transmitted spirit of the Renaissance grimoire, Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or classical Theosophical ritual systems. Modern invocations of Anu in ceremonial magic are largely products of twentieth- and twenty-first-century access to Assyriological translations.

General occult interest in Babylonian antiquity does not establish a specific Anu lineage. Correspondence tables assigning him Saturn, Kether, the crown chakra, a tarot trump, blue candles, or a particular crystal are **modern syncretisms** unless an author documents the modern source.

### Contemporary paganism

Small contemporary Sumerian, Mesopotamian, and “Kemet/Mesopotamian reconstructionist” communities honor An or Anu. Their historically informed practices may draw on hymns, food offerings, libations, divine names, calendars, and temple texts. Nevertheless:

- There is no demonstrated unbroken initiatory lineage from Bīt Rēš.
- Household practice must reconstruct procedures designed for large staffed temples.
- Modern ethical, legal, linguistic, and economic conditions substantially transform the rites.
- Reliable membership figures are unavailable.

The worship is real as a modern religious practice; its claim to be an exact survival of ancient cult is not historically demonstrable.

### Ancient-astronaut mythology

**[Modern invention]** Zecharia Sitchin’s *The 12th Planet* (1976) popularized a narrative in which the Anunnaki were extraterrestrials from Nibiru who came to Earth for gold and engineered humans. In this system Anu becomes an alien ruler or off-world monarch.

The cuneiform record does not say:

- that Anu was an extraterrestrial;
- that Nibiru was the Anunnaki’s home planet on a long eccentric orbit;
- that the gods mined African gold to repair an atmosphere;
- that they genetically modified Homo erectus;
- that DINGIR means “pure ones of the blazing rockets.”

The Anunna/Anunnaki are variable groups of gods, often high gods or divine judges; the term is not an ancient ethnonym for extraterrestrials. Sitchin’s construction has nonetheless materially shaped television, internet occultism, UFO religions, fiction, and popular visual culture. It is among the most influential modern reinventions of Anu’s divine family ([discussion of Sitchin’s mistranslations](https://www.ancient-origins.net/human-origins-religions/zecharia-sitchin-and-mistranslation-sumerian-texts-009442)).

### Nationalist and racial appropriation

Internet ideologies sometimes describe Anu or the Anunnaki as white, Nordic, Black, reptilian, Aryan, or members of a modern ethnic group. No cuneiform source assigns Anu any of these modern racial identities. Such claims are modern political or esoteric appropriations.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was An originally the supreme god?

Older syntheses often reconstructed a neat sequence: An ruled first, then Enlil, then Marduk. The evidence supports shifts in active political prominence, but not a universal chronological replacement.

- Early texts already foreground Enlil.
- An retains formal seniority after Enlil’s rise.
- Local cult prominence differs from list rank.
- Marduk’s promotion is particularly Babylonian.
- Aššur’s promotion is Assyrian.
- Seleucid Uruk later returns Anu to active local supremacy.

The better model is overlapping hierarchies, with divine powers repeatedly reassigned without erasing older ranks.

### Was the White Temple An’s?

The archaeological complex lies in the district later associated with Anu and is conventionally called the Anu ziggurat. Against secure identification:

- No contemporary dedication names An.
- The chronological gap to securely identified Anu worship in the precinct is enormous.
- The sign AN in archaic documents can mean sky, a divine classifier, or the god and is not always phonetically recoverable.

The responsible verdict is **possible, traditional, but unproved**.

### Did Inana displace An from Eanna?

Eanna means “House of Heaven,” and An and Inana were closely related in Uruk theology. Yet the earliest recoverable Eanna evidence strongly associates the institution with Inana. A clean narrative of An’s original ownership followed by feminine “usurpation” is not documented.

### Was Anu an inactive *deus otiosus*?

Anu often delegates and is remote, making “otiose high god” a useful comparative description. But he:

- fixes or authorizes destinies;
- grants the Bull of Heaven;
- sends or equips destructive beings;
- receives appeals;
- legitimates kingship;
- heads active ritual at Seleucid Uruk.

“Remote” is more accurate than “irrelevant.”

### Meaning of *An = Anum*

Scholars debate how prescriptive god lists were. They undoubtedly preserve learned theology, but temples and narratives did not always conform to their equations. Paul-Alain Beaulieu proposed that the late Uruk hierarchy was significantly shaped by *An = Anum*. The revival’s close correspondence to list theology is persuasive, although ritual politics, priestly family interests, and Seleucid patronage also mattered.

### The late Uruk revival

Was it conservative continuity or innovative reconstruction? Both:

- Anu’s association with Uruk was ancient.
- Continuous worship in some form is plausible.
- The supremacy of Anu and Antu, monumental Bīt Rēš, and list-based hierarchy were late reorganizations.
- Priests represented innovation as recovery of ancient ordinances.
- Greek political support and Babylonian antiquarianism operated together.

### Iconographic uncertainty

Henri Frankfort and later art historians classified divine figures on seals by crown, posture, attendant animals, weapons, and scene type. But without inscriptions, identifying a generic enthroned high god as Anu remains uncertain. The emblematic horned crown is safer than supposed portraits.

### State of the evidence

Evidence is dispersed across:

- Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin.
- British Museum.
- Iraq Museum.
- Yale Babylonian Collection.
- University of Pennsylvania Museum.
- Louvre.
- Istanbul collections.
- Excavated and unprovenanced tablets in many smaller collections.

CDLI supplies images and catalogue data for large numbers of tablets; ORACC supplies lemmatized editions and translations; ETCSL remains valuable for Sumerian literature, though individual readings should be checked against newer editions. Looting, early excavation practices, unpublished fragments, uncertain provenance, and wartime destruction continue to constrain reconstruction.

### Major modern scholarship

- Thorkild Jacobsen, *The Treasures of Darkness*: influential literary-historical synthesis; often evocative, but some evolutionary reconstructions are more confident than current evidence permits.
- Henri Frankfort, work on cylinder seals and ancient kingship: foundational for iconography and political theology.
- Jeremy Black and Anthony Green, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*: concise iconographic reference.
- Richard Litke, *A Reconstruction of the Assyro-Babylonian God-Lists, AN: dA-nu-um and AN: Anu ša amēli*: primary modern reconstruction of the list.
- Andrew George, critical work on *Gilgameš* and temple names.
- W. G. Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths*: critical edition and study of *Enūma eliš* and related texts.
- Paul-Alain Beaulieu, *The Pantheon of Uruk during the Neo-Babylonian Period*: essential for the transition behind the late cult.
- Julia Krul, *The Revival of the Anu Cult and the Nocturnal Fire Ceremony at Late Babylonian Uruk*: central study of the Achaemenid–Seleucid revival.
- *Reallexikon der Assyriologie*: indispensable article-level historiography, though volumes span many decades and must be dated individually.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- The White Temple is certainly Anu’s: **unproved**.
- Anu was a historical human king: **unsupported**.
- Anu came from Nibiru as an extraterrestrial: **modern invention**.
- AN/DINGIR means rocket or alien: **false philology**.
- Anu was simply Saturn: **overgeneralized late/modern correspondence**.
- All Anunnaki are Anu’s biological children in one fixed family: **harmonization**.
- The Anu cult practiced temple prostitution or human sacrifice as a defining rite: **unsupported**.
- Anu worship survives continuously in a named modern Middle Eastern religion: **not established**.
- Anu, El, Zeus, Dyaus, and every sky father are one historically continuous god: **comparativist speculation, not demonstrated transmission**.

### Source-critical conclusion

The recoverable history does not reveal one changeless “Anu mythology.” It reveals a divine name anchored in the sky; an Early Dynastic high god without a securely attested birth narrative; a remote source of authority beside the more active Enlil; an Akkadian father and assembly ruler; a genealogical link in Marduk’s Babylonian ascent; an imperial-list god under Aššur; and finally the deliberately restored sovereign of Seleucid Uruk. His oldest supposed temple is uncertain, while his latest ancient cult is unusually well documented. His modern alien kingship is wholly recent. The differences among these forms are not defects to be reconciled: they constitute Anu’s documented historical existence.
