# Anshar and Kishar: Research Dossier

**Class:** Anšar is a male primordial god; Kišar is a female primordial goddess. In *Enūma eliš* they form a primeval divine pair and genealogical generation between Laḫmu–Laḫāmu and Anu. They are not independently well-developed personalities or securely attested objects of a joint cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The oldest recoverable tradition making Anšar an ancestor or father of Anu occurs in an Old Babylonian forerunner to the god list later standardized as *An = Anum*, probably in the first half of the second millennium BCE. Anšar also appears ambiguously in Old Babylonian Sumerian literature, including a hymn associated with Rim-Sîn I. The pairing **Anšar–Kišar as preserved divine characters** is securely documented principally in first-millennium manuscripts of *Enūma eliš*. The surviving epic manuscripts do not themselves prove how early its present wording was composed. [AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/AnsharandKishar/index.html), [Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths*](https://enenuru.net/pdfs/Lambert%2C%20W.%20G._Babylonian%20Creation%20Myths_2013.pdf)

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Old Babylonian or otherwise demonstrably early evidence.
- **[LC] Later canonical formulation:** the standard first-millennium scholarly or literary tradition.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a geographically specific recension or equation.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** god lists, commentaries, astronomical interpretation, or antiquarian theology.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically attested equation between deities.
- **[LA] Late accretion:** a development not demonstrably present in the earliest evidence.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation proposed by modern Assyriologists.
- **[MI] Modern invention or retelling:** a claim without ancient textual support.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Anšar

The normalized name is **Anšar**, conventionally Anglicized **Anshar**, and occasionally rendered Anshur or Ansar. Principal writings include **an-šár**, **AN.ŠAR₂**, and, when explicitly divine, **ᵈAN.ŠAR₂**. The compound consists of Sumerian *an*, “sky” or “heaven,” and *šar/šár*, “totality” or “whole.” The safest literal rendering is therefore **“totality of heaven”** or **“the whole heaven.”**

Translations such as “upper horizon,” “sky-horizon,” or “axis of heaven” are interpretive. They reflect attempts to make the pair cosmographically symmetrical; they are not independent ancient epithets. **[MSR]** The common description “horizon of heaven” should consequently be treated as an explanatory gloss, not as a uniquely established dictionary meaning. [AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/AnsharandKishar/index.html)

The name could also function as a learned writing for other gods:

- **Anšar = Anu/Anum. [LS/SI]** A first-millennium list beginning *Anšar = Anu* and equations in *An = Anum* assimilate the primeval ancestor to Anu.
- **AN.ŠÁR = Aššur. [RV/SI]** Neo-Assyrian theology exploited the graphic resemblance between Anšar and the national god Aššur. In the Assyrian recension of *Enūma eliš*, AN.ŠÁR can denote the primordial Anšar and the victorious Aššur who replaces Marduk, producing deliberate but narratively awkward overlap.
- **Anšar as an ancestor or defeated primeval deity. [LS/LA]** Late lists group him with dead or superseded divine figures such as Enmešarra, Lugaldukuga, Asag, and Qingu. These classifications do not describe an independent narrative preserved about Anšar’s defeat.

Anšar has no securely attested, stable cult epithet comparable to the extensive titulary of Enlil, Marduk, or Ištar. Descriptions such as “king of the gods” are contextual summaries of his temporary authority in *Enūma eliš*, not documented recurring cult titles.

### Kišar

The normalized name is **Kišar**, conventionally **Kishar**, written **ki-šar₂** and divinely **ᵈKI.ŠAR₂**. It combines *ki*, “earth/land,” with *šar*, “whole” or “totality”: **“totality of earth”** or **“the whole earth.”**

The common noun *kišar* is much older than the securely identified goddess. It occurs from Early Dynastic IIIb onward with senses including “the whole earth,” “totality,” and, once in the lexical record, “horizon.” Those occurrences cannot automatically be counted as attestations of the goddess. The divine name in ORACC’s name corpus is presently represented by only a handful of Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, and Hellenistic attestations. **[EA negative finding]** [ePSD](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0032294.html), [ORACC divine-name corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/names/cbd/qpn-x-divine/x000014130.html)

Documented equations include:

- **Kišar = Antu/Antum. [LS/SI]** This follows the corresponding equation Anšar = Anu and is explicit in late scholarly texts and a Seleucid bilingual composition.
- **Kišargal, “Great Kišar/Great Whole Earth.” [LS/LA]** A learned expanded form used in late Uruk theology.
- **Spouse of Gudanna. [LS/RV]** The astronomical commentary BM 68593 calls Kišar the spouse of the star/constellation Gudanna, “Bull of Heaven,” characterized there as the “Enlil of Šuruppak.” This is a late astral equation, not part of the narrative of *Enūma eliš*.

Descriptions such as “earth mother” are reasonable modern shorthand for her cosmographic and genealogical place, but no surviving source gives her an active fertility, agricultural, maternal, or chthonic cult comparable to Ninhursag, Uraš, or Ereshkigal. **[MSR]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Old Babylonian and possibly early evidence

1. **Old Babylonian forerunner of *An = Anum*. [EA/LS]**  
   This is the earliest recoverable evidence for the genealogy in which Anšar stands before Anu. The relevant material is lexical-theological rather than narrative: it systematizes divine succession but does not supply the plot later known from *Enūma eliš*.

2. **“A hymn to Haya for Rim-Sîn,” ETCSL 2.6.9.2. [EA, uncertain identity]**  
   The composition belongs to the cultural setting of Rim-Sîn I of Larsa, conventionally dated 1822–1763 BCE under the middle chronology. It contains the written name Anšar, but the reference is too isolated to demonstrate that the full *Enūma eliš* character or the paired cosmological god was already intended.

3. **“Enlil and Sud,” ETCSL 1.2.2. [EA or later copy of early composition; uncertain identity]**  
   A passage calls Enlil an offspring begotten by Anšar. The surviving scholastic witnesses and textual history complicate precise dating. It shows that Anšar could be inserted into Enlil’s ancestry, but it does not mention Kišar or reproduce the genealogy of *Enūma eliš*. The equation may represent learned expansion rather than early popular mythology.

### *An = Anum* and related god lists

The canonical god list *An = Anum*, assembled from older lists and standardized in the later second millennium or early first millennium BCE, is a primary witness to Mesopotamian learned theology. Its first tablet organizes generations associated with Anu. At or near the opening, Anšar and Kišar participate in equations connecting primeval pairs with Anu and Antu. A related recension explicitly begins with **Anšar = Anum**, followed by **Kišar = Antum**. **[LC/LS]**

The lists do not prove that ordinary worshippers regarded these equations as literal or that an Anšar–Kišar cult existed. God-list equivalence is a scribal method for arranging names, functions, and theological identities. Julia Krul and other scholars caution that equations between a god and his supposed father can remain intellectual constructions without cultic consequences. [*An = Anum and Related Lists* preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783161613838_A47326675/preview-9783161613838_A47326675.pdf)

### *Enūma eliš*

**Language:** Standard Babylonian Akkadian.  
**Form:** approximately 1,100 lines on seven tablets.  
**Composition:** disputed. Proposals range from an Old Babylonian nucleus or composition under Hammurabi to a late-second-millennium formulation, especially around Nebuchadnezzar I, c. 1125–1104 BCE. Lambert favored the latter historical setting while acknowledging inherited material.  
**Surviving witnesses:** overwhelmingly first millennium BCE.

Lambert catalogued witnesses from Nineveh, Assur, Sultantepe/Huzirina, Nimrud, Kish, Uruk, Sippar, Tell Haddad, and pieces of uncertain provenance. The seven pieces from Assur are the oldest surviving fragments identifiable in his corpus; most Babylonian-script witnesses are later. Clay tablets, not later manuscripts on perishable media, preserve the text. [Lambert](https://enenuru.net/pdfs/Lambert%2C%20W.%20G._Babylonian%20Creation%20Myths_2013.pdf), [CDLI Sultantepe witness](https://cdli.earth/artifacts/338321), [British Museum Tablet I](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1881-0701-3289)

Anšar appears prominently in Tablets I–III and briefly in the name-conferral section near the end. Kišar appears principally in the opening genealogy; she has no speeches or independent action in the surviving epic.

#### Relevant passages

- **Tablet I, approximately lines 10–16. [LC]** Laḫmu and Laḫāmu precede Anšar and Kišar; Anšar begets Anu “like himself,” and Anu begets Nudimmud/Ea.
- **Tablet II. [LC]** Ea reports Tiamat’s mobilization to Anšar. Anšar is distressed, sends Anu to confront or appease Tiamat, and receives him back after his failure. Ea arranges for Marduk to present himself as champion. Anšar accepts Marduk’s offer, conditional on the divine assembly granting him unrivalled authority.
- **Tablet III. [LC]** Anšar instructs his vizier Gaga to summon Laḫmu, Laḫāmu, and the divine assembly. Gaga repeats Anšar’s account of Tiamat’s army and Marduk’s demand. The gods feast and confer kingship on Marduk.
- **Tablets IV–V. [LC]** Anšar is not a combatant. Marduk defeats Tiamat, creates the ordered cosmos, and establishes celestial stations.
- **Tablet VI, line 101 and following. [LC/LS]** Anšar bestows or inaugurates Marduk’s exalted name **Asalluḫi**; Anšar, Laḫmu, and Laḫāmu participate in proclaiming groups of Marduk’s names.
- **Tablet VII. [LC/LS]** Marduk’s fifty names complete the theological elevation that began with Anšar’s authorization.

The epic is therefore not principally “the myth of Anšar and Kišar.” They supply a deep genealogy and, through Anšar, a provisional sovereign who transfers authority to Marduk.

### Assyrian recension

An Assyrianized recension is preserved on incomplete tablets from Assur and Nineveh, generally assigned to the Sargonid period and often associated with the ideological aftermath of Sennacherib’s destruction of Babylon in 689 BCE. **[RV/SI]** It replaces Marduk with Aššur, written AN.ŠÁR, while retaining the primordial Anšar of the genealogy. Lambert aptly observed that this substitution leaves parts of the plot confused: the conquering protagonist can become graphically identical with his own great-great-grandfather.

The recension’s purpose was not the neutral preservation of an ancient equivalence. It appropriated Babylon’s cosmological poem to make Aššur the cosmic victor and source of universal kingship. Only three principal tablets preserve this directly Assyrianized version—two from Assur and one from Nineveh—so its extent must not be exaggerated. [Modern study of the cuneiform reception](https://oa-fund.ub.uni-muenchen.de/id/eprint/1536/1/b-9781350297425.pdf)

### Seleucid and late scholarly material

- **Bilingual “Exaltation of Ištar,” Seleucid Uruk. [LA/LS/SI]** In the Sumerian column, Anšar and Kišar correspond respectively to Anu and Antu in Akkadian. This is explicit evidence of late Uruk syncretistic theology.
- **TCL 6, 51. [LA/LS]** A late text addresses “exalted Kišar/Antu,” again documenting the Kišar–Antu equation. [CDLI P363723](https://cdli.earth/P363723)
- **BM 68593. [LA/LS]** An astronomical commentary makes Kišar the spouse of Gudanna.
- **Esoteric ritual commentary. [LA/LS]** Anšar’s dispatch of Anu against Tiamat is correlated with Mandanu’s ritual journey to Ḫursagkalamma. The interpretation is commentary on the epic, not an independent myth.

### Named corpora in which the pair is absent

A dedicated review of the corpora named in the brief produces an important negative result:

- **Atrahasis:** no Anšar or Kišar in the preserved creation-and-flood narrative.
- **Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh*:** no role for either figure in Andrew George’s edition; the cosmic assembly and Flood story employ other divine actors.
- **Inanna’s Descent:** no Anšar or Kišar.
- **Enki and Ninmah:** no Anšar or Kišar.
- **Most ETCSL literature:** Anšar’s two uncertain passages are exceptional; Kišar is absent as an active goddess.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions:** references to AN.ŠÁR normally require contextual determination because the writing can designate Aššur, not primordial Anšar.
- **RINAP:** consequently valuable for Aššur ideology but not evidence of an independent Anšar–Kišar cult unless the context explicitly distinguishes the primeval figure.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[LC]** Anšar and Kišar embody or name a comprehensive heaven-and-earth pair within a sequence of expanding primordial generations. Their role is primarily:

- cosmographic;
- genealogical;
- classificatory;
- legitimating, because Marduk’s kingship is authorized through an older divine generation.

Anšar’s narrative functions are governance, consultation, dispatching messengers or champions, and transferring supreme authority. Kišar has no preserved independent function beyond forming the terrestrial half of the pair and participating in the genealogy.

Neither deity is securely shown governing the visible sky or cultivated earth in everyday religion. Those spheres already belonged to figures such as An/Anu, Enlil, Uraš, Ki, Ninhursag, and others. Calling Anšar simply “the sky god” or Kišar simply “the earth goddess” erases this distinction.

### Iconography

**No image can presently be securely identified as Anšar or Kišar.** AMGG explicitly records their iconography as unknown. No diagnostic crown, weapon, animal, plant, color, number, sacred day, or independent divine emblem is attested. [AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/AnsharandKishar/index.html)

Cylinder seals illustrating divine assemblies, cosmic mountains, or combat with a monster have sometimes been used as general illustrations of *Enūma eliš*. Frankfort’s studies of cylinder seals remain fundamental for Mesopotamian iconographic method, but resemblance to a creation story is not sufficient identification. No known seal inscription labels a figure Anšar or Kišar.

The winged-disc and related symbols of Aššur must likewise not be transferred automatically to primordial Anšar. They belong to Aššur’s iconographic history; their relevance to Anšar exists only after and where the equation Aššur = Anšar is demonstrable.

### Powers and limitations

Anšar can convene gods, issue instructions, send Anu and Gaga, and approve a champion. He cannot personally overcome Tiamat and eventually accepts Marduk’s authority above his own. Kišar displays no individually narrated power. The sources do not make either immortal couple creators of humanity, rulers of an underworld, planetary beings, or teachers of civilization.

The pair’s description as brother and sister is a reasonable inference from common primeval-pair structures but is not stated in the opening as plainly as their pairing and succession. “Consort” is safer than building an elaborate incest narrative unsupported by the text.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The succession of primordial generations

**[LC]** Before heaven and earth have received names and before destinies are fixed, Apsû and Tiamat mingle their waters. Laḫmu and Laḫāmu appear, followed after an interval by Anšar and Kišar, who surpass the earlier pair. Anšar generates Anu in his own likeness; Anu generates Nudimmud/Ea, who exceeds his forebears in wisdom and strength.

The passage creates a graduated ancestry culminating first in Ea and then, beyond the opening genealogy, in Marduk. Kišar’s role is structural: she completes a male–female cosmic pair but disappears from the plot. Anšar remains because the poet needs an elder sovereign able to authorize Marduk’s elevation.

**Development:** Earlier Sumerian cosmogonies do not preserve this exact sequence. Attempts to derive each pair from a physical stage—silt, horizons, celestial spheres—are modern reconstructions. The sequence is most securely understood as a learned Babylonian genealogy assembled from older divine names to legitimate Marduk. AMGG accordingly describes the pair as part of an “invented mythology” serving the poem’s new cosmology. “Invented” here means scholarly composition from inherited materials, not that the names were fabricated without precedent.

### Anšar and Tiamat’s rebellion

After Ea kills Apsû, Tiamat raises an army, creates monstrous beings, appoints Qingu commander, and gives him the Tablet of Destinies. Ea reports the crisis to Anšar. Anšar is stunned and initially reproaches or questions Ea. He sends Anu to confront Tiamat, but Anu cannot withstand her and returns.

Ea then maneuvers his son Marduk into the role of champion. Marduk tells Anšar that he will fight only if the assembled gods make his command absolute and his decrees irreversible. Anšar agrees and orders Gaga to summon Laḫmu, Laḫāmu, and the assembly. Gaga repeats the crisis narrative; the gods feast and grant Marduk kingship.

Marduk defeats Tiamat, captures her allies, takes the Tablet of Destinies from Qingu, organizes the cosmos from Tiamat’s body, and creates humanity after Qingu’s execution. The result “establishes Anšar’s supremacy” in the immediate dynastic sense, but the larger outcome is Anšar’s displacement: Marduk now possesses authority greater than his ancestor’s.

### The bestowal of Marduk’s names

Near the end of Tablet VI, Anšar gives Marduk the name Asalluḫi. Laḫmu and Laḫāmu join the proclamation of additional names. The episode incorporates older local gods and divine functions into Marduk’s identity. Anšar is consequently a theological legitimizer: his antiquity authorizes Marduk’s absorption of older cultic traditions. [Discussion of Asalluḫi](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/bitstream/123456789/8016/1/reading-akkadian-prayers-hymns-introduction.pdf)

### Assyrian rewriting

**[RV/SI]** In the Assyrian recension, Aššur takes Marduk’s place and is written AN.ŠÁR. This is not evidence that the oldest epic originally starred Aššur. It is an imperial rewriting of a Babylonian work. Because the primordial Anšar remains in the ancestry, the substitution creates genealogical and grammatical confusion. The imperfection is itself evidence of the historical process: a prestigious canonical text was altered selectively rather than recomposed from the beginning.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The pair proper

The clearest finding is negative:

- no securely identified joint temple;
- no independent priesthood;
- no festival dedicated specifically to the pair;
- no securely identified statue or cult image;
- no offering list securely assigning food, livestock, incense, or garments to the pair;
- no oracle or divination practice conducted in their names;
- no evidence of pilgrimage;
- no securely attested human sacrifice;
- no evidence of “temple prostitution” associated with either deity;
- no documented cult violence conducted for them;
- no securely identified theophoric personal names invoking Kišar as an independently worshipped goddess.

AMGG therefore states that there is no evidence for cult places or worship of Anšar and Kišar. That conclusion concerns the primordial pair proper. It must be qualified by the later equation of AN.ŠÁR with Aššur. [AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/AnsharandKishar/index.html)

### Babylonian New Year context

A late ritual tablet associates recitation of *Enūma eliš* with the Babylonian *akītu* festival. Thus Anšar and Kišar could be heard in a cultic performance without possessing their own cult. The recitation glorified Marduk and renewed the theological-political order of Babylon; it was not a festival for the primordial couple.

Modern descriptions often turn the recitation into an elaborate public dramatic reenactment. Formal recitation is documented; the extent of dramatic staging remains debated. The safest claim is that the poem became ritual literature in Marduk’s cult.

### AN.ŠÁR/Aššur at Uruk

Eanna administrative records from Neo-Babylonian Uruk mention a small temple or sanctuary of **AN.ŠÁR**. Paul-Alain Beaulieu argued that its deity was Aššur under the learned writing AN.ŠÁR, not a separate revival of the primordial Anšar. The evidence includes Assyrian-style personal names, references connected with “the City” in the Assyrian sense of Assur, and the political presence of pro-Assyrian elements at Uruk.

The establishment’s exact foundation is unknown. References belong principally to the sixth century BCE; Beaulieu proposed that the cult may have entered Uruk during the seventh century through Assyrian residents or sympathizers. This is the strongest material for something called an Anšar cult, but historically it is better classified as **the cult of Aššur in Babylonia under the writing AN.ŠÁR**. [Beaulieu bibliographic record](https://ccp.yale.edu/cult-ansar-assur-babylonia-after-fall-assyrian-empire), [article reproduction](https://www.scribd.com/document/473463587/The-Cult-of-AN-SAR-Assur-in-Babylonia-After-the-Fall-of-the-Assyrian-Empire-1997)

No comparable sanctuary for Kišar is documented.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Third millennium BCE: lexical prehistory

The common Sumerian term *kišar*, “whole earth/totality,” is attested by Early Dynastic IIIb. This establishes the antiquity of the linguistic material but not the goddess. Neither member of the pair has a demonstrable third-millennium cult. **[EA negative finding]**

### Old Babylonian period, early second millennium BCE

The Anšar-before-Anu genealogy is recoverable in a forerunner to *An = Anum*. Literary compositions associated with this period mention an Anšar, including the Rim-Sîn hymn and *Enlil and Sud*, but their precise theological relationship to the later primordial god is uncertain.

This period supplies the most defensible lower boundary for the tradition. It does not securely supply the complete *Enūma eliš* narrative.

### Later second millennium BCE

Babylon’s political and theological ascent encouraged the arrangement of inherited gods into a genealogy culminating in Marduk. Scholarly disagreement remains over whether *Enūma eliš* was composed substantially under Hammurabi, developed gradually, or reached its present form around Nebuchadnezzar I. **[MSR dispute]**

The latter dating relates the epic to Marduk’s renewed prestige after his statue was returned from Elam. The earlier dating emphasizes Babylon’s rise under Hammurabi and possible Old Babylonian antecedents. Surviving manuscripts cannot settle the question because they are later copies.

### Early first millennium BCE

The standard epic circulated in Assyria and Babylonia. Anšar became a recognizable primordial sovereign; Kišar remained a genealogical counterpart. God lists systematized their relation to Anu and Antu.

### Sargonid Assyria, eighth–seventh centuries BCE

Assyrian scholars identified Anšar with Aššur and rewrote *Enūma eliš* accordingly. This was a political-theological promotion of Aššur: the Assyrian national god became older than and superior to Marduk while appropriating Marduk’s cosmic victory.

The rewriting should be placed against Assyria’s contested rule over Babylonia, particularly under Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, and Ashurbanipal. It is not evidence of peaceful timeless equivalence.

### Neo-Babylonian period, 626–539 BCE

After Assyria’s fall, a sanctuary of AN.ŠÁR/Aššur continued or appeared at Uruk. This complicates any simple story of total suppression: an Assyrian cultic identity could survive in Babylonian territory. At the same time, no evidence shows a revival of Kišar or an independent cult of the primeval couple.

### Achaemenid, Seleucid, and Hellenistic Babylonia

Late Uruk scholars intensified equations among Anšar, Anu, Aššur, Kišar, and Antu. The *Exaltation of Ištar*, astronomical commentary, and related texts demonstrate antiquarian system-building. Kišar’s identity was thereby preserved primarily in scholarship rather than popular worship.

### Late antiquity to early modernity

No documented continuous cult of Anšar and Kišar survives the disappearance of Mesopotamian cuneiform scholarship. They do not acquire a substantive role in securely dated Syriac, Arabic, Jewish, Christian, or Islamic folklore. No medieval grimoire tradition centered on them has been identified.

### Nineteenth century to the present

The recovery and decipherment of *Enūma eliš* restored the pair to modern knowledge. They now appear in translations, reference works, neopagan lists, fiction, games, occult compilations, and ancient-astronaut literature. These receptions are discontinuous reconstructions based on Assyriology, not survivals of an unbroken cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Assyriological recovery

Fragments of *Enūma eliš* were recovered principally from the ruins of Nineveh and other Assyrian sites during nineteenth-century excavations. George Smith publicized the Babylonian creation material in the 1870s after his famous work on the Flood tablet. Early translations frequently interpreted the text through comparison with Genesis and used now-obsolete spellings such as Anshar, Kishar, Lakhmu, and Lakhamu.

Later editions by P. Jensen, L. W. King, René Labat, Alexander Heidel, W. G. Lambert, Benjamin Foster, and others progressively reconstructed the text. Lambert’s 2013 *Babylonian Creation Myths* is the fundamental modern critical edition and synthesis.

### Occult and neopagan reception

No evidence was found that Anšar or Kišar occupied a distinctive place in Renaissance magic, the *Arbatel*, Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, early Theosophy, or major ceremonial-magic systems. Generic modern lists sometimes insert them into “Sumerian” pantheons, but this normally derives from modern translations of *Enūma eliš*.

Contemporary Mesopotamian reconstructionists may honor them as primordial gods. Such devotion is a modern reconstruction and is not evidence of an uninterrupted lineage. Its historical scale cannot presently be established from reliable demographic evidence.

Claims that an ancient rite, invocation, color correspondence, sigil, planetary hour, crystal, or offering regimen belonged specifically to Anšar or Kišar should be labeled **[MI]** unless accompanied by a cited cuneiform source.

### Ancient-astronaut reinterpretation

Zecharia Sitchin’s *The 12th Planet* (1976) transformed *Enūma eliš* into a supposed account of Solar System history. In his scheme, **Anšar is Saturn and Kišar is Jupiter**, Gaga becomes a planetary body or emissary, and the divine genealogy becomes a sequence of planetary formation or collision.

This is **[MI]**, not an ancient interpretation. No cited cuneiform astronomical source identifies the primordial pair with Saturn and Jupiter. Mesopotamian scholars had well-attested names for those planets, notably Kajamānu for Saturn and Marduk/Sagmēgar associations for Jupiter. Sitchin’s mappings are created by replacing mythic actors with modern planetary objects, not by translating the Akkadian text. [Sitchin-derived presentation](https://www.bibliotecapleyades.net/sitchin/planeta12/12planeteng_07.htm), [modern description of the hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_objects_proposed_in_religion%2C_astrology%2C_ufology_and_pseudoscience)

### Literature, games, and commercial naming

Anshar and Kishar occur sporadically as names for fictional cosmic beings, spacecraft, locations, or fantasy characters. These uses usually retain only the sky/earth contrast or “primordial” status. No single modern adaptation appears to have displaced *Enūma eliš* as the dominant public source for their identities. Commercial uses are modern name-borrowing, not evidence of cult transmission.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Aššur has modern importance in discussions of Assyrian identity, but primordial Anšar and Kišar do not have a comparably documented nationalist or extremist cult. Conflating modern ethnic uses of Aššur with worship of Anšar would be historically irresponsible. No securely documented extremist appropriation specific to the pair was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Were they ancient popular gods or learned creations?

The central debate is not whether their names existed, but what sort of existence they had.

- The linguistic elements *an*, *ki*, and *šar* are ancient.
- Anšar occurs before the securely preserved epic.
- The paired characters have virtually no independent cult, iconography, or mythology.
- Their fullest presentation occurs in a literary genealogy designed to culminate in Marduk.

Consequently, many scholars regard the pair as products of learned theological construction using older names and cosmic vocabulary. This does not make them “fake”; it identifies their principal social setting as scribal literature rather than temple cult.

### “Horizon” versus “totality”

Older scholarship often translated the pair as upper and lower horizons. The lexical evidence more directly supports “totality of heaven” and “totality of earth.” Because *kišar* can carry a horizon sense and because the pair is cosmographically complementary, “horizon” remains possible as interpretation. It should not be presented as the uniquely literal etymology.

### Relation to Anu and Antu

The genealogy makes Anšar Anu’s father; late lists make Anšar identical with Anu. Kišar is correspondingly both ancestral partner and equivalent of Antu. This apparent contradiction is characteristic of Mesopotamian god-list reasoning. The lists organize theological relationships through equation; they do not preserve a single biography.

### Relation to Aššur

The identification is historically demonstrable but secondary. It was favored by:

- the graphic writing AN.ŠÁR;
- the desire to provide Aššur with primeval antiquity;
- imperial competition with Babylon;
- the adaptability of a literary recension in which a supreme champion could replace Marduk.

The proposal that *Enūma eliš* was originally Assyrian is not supported by the text’s Babylon-centered culmination or by current critical scholarship.

### Dating *Enūma eliš*

Principal positions include:

- an Old Babylonian composition connected with Hammurabi’s elevation of Babylon;
- a text developed from Old Babylonian materials but substantially edited later;
- a late-second-millennium composition around Nebuchadnezzar I.

The first-millennium date of every securely preserved substantial witness prevents certainty. Linguistic dating, Marduk’s position, political setting, and relations to older myths provide arguments, not an explicit ancient composition date.

### Ritual performance

A late ritual text associates the epic’s recitation with Babylon’s New Year festival. This establishes cultic use at that period. It does not prove that the poem was originally composed for the festival or that every episode was acted dramatically.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- **Anšar = Saturn and Kišar = Jupiter as ancient doctrine:** modern Sitchinite invention.
- **The pair were extraterrestrial rulers:** no cuneiform support.
- **They had major temples and priesthoods in early Sumer:** no evidence.
- **Their images are recognizable on unnamed cylinder seals:** unproven.
- **Kišar was a major agricultural earth-mother cult:** inference from her name, not documented worship.
- **Anšar and Kišar occur throughout *Atrahasis*, *Gilgamesh*, and Sumerian creation literature:** false; their distribution is narrow.
- **The Assyrian recension seamlessly preserves the original epic:** false; the substitutions visibly disrupt the genealogy.
- **Modern invocations or magical correspondences are translations of ancient rites:** generally unsupported unless a tablet and edition are supplied.

### State of the evidence

The textual witnesses are distributed among the British Museum, Vorderasiatisches Museum and other collections, with many pieces entered in CDLI and ORACC. Their archaeological value varies: only a minority of Babylonian-script fragments came from controlled excavations, while Assyrian libraries provide better provenience. The primary evidence remains fragmentary, duplicated, and sometimes known through joins identified long after excavation.
