# Gula: Research Dossier

**Class:** goddess (*iltu* in Akkadian; *diĝir* marked by the divine determinative 𒀭 in Sumerian writing), not a demon, monster, or collective. In the mature Babylonian and Assyrian tradition she is the pre-eminent divine healer, patron of physicians, mistress of medicinal plants, and both giver and remover of disease.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Ur III period, approximately 2112–2004 BCE, principally in administrative and offering records from Umma, Puzriš-Dagan, Ur, and perhaps Lagaš. A proposed Old Akkadian occurrence from Adab is possible but not secure because the written form may designate a different deity, Gu₂-la₂. The claim sometimes encountered that “Gula” is securely attested in the Early Dynastic period depends on merging differently written divine names and should remain disputed. The electronic Sumerian dictionary records relevant spellings across Early Dynastic through Hellenistic material, but corpus-wide lexical indexing is not by itself proof that every early occurrence denotes the later healing goddess. [ePSD2 entry](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0044836.html); [ORACC/AMGG synthesis](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/GulaNinkarrak/index.html).

**Evidentiary labels used below:**

- **[EA]** securely attested early material: generally Ur III–Old Babylonian evidence.
- **[LC]** later canonical or widely received Mesopotamian formulation.
- **[RV]** regional or local variant.
- **[LS]** learned scribal or priestly systematization.
- **[SI]** syncretistic identification between originally distinguishable beings.
- **[LA]** late ancient accretion.
- **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction or inference.
- **[MI]** modern invention or modern devotional interpretation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

Gula was a Mesopotamian healing goddess whose independent cult first becomes clearly visible in the late third millennium BCE. During the second millennium, and especially in Kassite and first-millennium scholarly religion, she became the dominant member of a constellation of originally local healing goddesses: Ninisina of Isin, Ninkarrak, Nintinugga of Nippur, Bau/Baba of Lagaš, and Meme. Their functions, spouses, children, epithets, temples, and even names could be transferred among them, but they never formed a perfectly uniform or universally interchangeable figure. *An = Anum*, for example, preserves several of them as distinct goddesses with distinct entourages even while other texts equate them.

The mature Gula is compassionate toward the petitioner but not intrinsically benign. She diagnoses, grows or reveals medicines, restores damaged bodies, and intercedes for life; conversely, her “hand” can denote disease, her dogs can pursue oath-breakers, and curse formulae ask her to place persistent sores or incurable illness upon violators. Healing and punitive disease are two aspects of the same divine jurisdiction, not separate deities. [ORACC/AMGG](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/GulaNinkarrak/index.html); [Böck bibliographic record and contents](https://emu.tind.io/record/254148).

### Names and writings

- **Gula:** conventionally normalized *Gula*; written principally 𒀭𒄖𒆷, **ᵈgu-la**, with variants **ᵈgú-la**, **ᵈgú-lá**, and occasionally the learned logogram **ᵈME.ME**.  
  **Etymology:** ancient scholars and many modern treatments connect the name with Sumerian *gula*, “great,” “greater,” “principal,” or “elder.” In Ur III texts *gula* can be appended to other divine names—Inanna-gula, Ninhursaĝ-gula, and Ninisina-gula—so one theory makes Gula an epithet, “the great one,” which became an autonomous divine name.  
  **Dispute:** Gu₂-la₂, written differently, may be a separate early goddess whose name is related to the verb *gu₂—la₂*, “to lean over/embrace.” Separate cult geography and positions in god lists warn against automatically reading every Gu₂-la₂ as Gula. **[EA/MSR]** [ePSD2](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0044836.html); [ORACC/AMGG](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/GulaNinkarrak/index.html).

- **Ninisina / Ninisinna:** Sumerian **ᵈnin-isin₂-na**, “Lady of Isin.” Ninisina was the civic healing goddess of Isin, not merely Gula’s timeless alternate name. A hymn for Išbi-Erra calls her both “holy Ninisina” and “Lady Gula,” demonstrating association by the early Isin dynasty while retaining the local name. **[EA/SI]** [ETCSL, Išbi-Erra D](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?charenc=j&display=Crit&lineid=c2514.10&text=c.2.5.1.4).

- **Ninkarrak:** Akkadianized spelling **ᵈnin-kar-ra-ak**, with variants. Probably “Lady of Kar,” not “Lady of Karrak”; neither the place *Kar* nor the etymology is completely settled. Ninkarrak is already an autonomous healing goddess in Old Babylonian and western Syrian sources. Later bilingual texts sometimes translate Ninisina as Ninkarrak, and Kassite Nippur documents may alternate Gula and Ninkarrak in what appears to be the same person’s theophoric name—particularly concrete evidence of contemporary equation. **[EA/SI]** [Devecchi, “Gula as Ninkarrak in the Middle Babylonian Onomastics”](https://iris.unito.it/bitstream/2318/2090211/1/Devecchi%202025_KASKAL%20NS%202.pdf).

- **Nintinugga / Nintilaʾuga:** approximately “Lady who revives the dead.” Originally Nippur’s own healing goddess. Her replacement or absorption by Gula was gradual and incomplete. **[EA/SI]**

- **Bau/Baba:** major goddess of Lagaš and wife of Ningirsu. Her healing profile and marriage to a warrior god made her available for equation with Gula once Ningirsu and Ninurta were themselves assimilated. She continued to possess an independent identity in some sources. **[SI/RV]**

- **Meme:** an independent early healing goddess whose cult appears to have been more completely absorbed into Gula after the Old Babylonian period than those of Ninisina, Bau, or Ninkarrak. **[SI]**

- **Ninigizibara / Ningizibara:** in some learned texts a healing goddess or manifestation assigned to Gula. **[LS/SI]**

- **Ninnibru:** “Lady of Nippur.” This title may denote Gula in late sources, but it could also belong to Nintinugga or Ninimma. It cannot be treated as an unambiguous name of Gula in every occurrence. **[RV/LS]**

- **Bēlet-balāṭi:** Akkadian “Lady of Life/Health.” A frequent medical and devotional title; at late Uruk it could also function as the name of a locally distinct goddess, so the equation with Gula is contextual rather than automatic. **[LC/RV]**

- **Azugallatu:** “Great female physician/healer,” the feminine of *asû rabû* or a comparable professional title. Shared with or related to Damu’s healing role. **[LC]**

- **Asītu / asâtu:** “female physician,” in literary praise.

- **Muballiṭat mīti:** “she who revives the dead,” rhetorically expressing restoration from near-fatal illness, not a consistent doctrine that Gula universally resurrected the deceased. **[LC]**

- **“Great healer of the black-headed people,” “great healer of the land,” “herb-grower,” “she who makes the broken whole,” “mother compassionate,” and “mistress of life”:** hymnological and medical titles attested chiefly in second- and first-millennium Akkadian literature. **[LC]**

### Foreign equations

No Greek or Roman identification of Gula became standard. Modern comparisons with Asklepios, Hygieia, Sekhmet, Hekate, or the Hindu Śītalā are comparative analogies, not ancient *interpretatio*. Kassite lexical lists gloss the poorly known Kassite goddess Ḫala by Gula; this is an ancient bilingual scholarly equation, not proof that Ḫala originated as Gula. **[LS/SI]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest record

Ur III economic documents furnish the first secure evidence: offerings to “Gula of Umma” and “Gula of KI.AN,” and appearances beside Damu and Gunura. Several persons making offerings to her were *asû*, physicians, which suggests—without by itself proving—that her medical identity was already recognized. Her cult at Umma was active, yet she was not the city’s principal tutelary goddess; Šara and Ninura held that position. Her ultimate place of origin therefore remains unresolved. **[EA/MSR]**

An alleged Old Akkadian Adab attestation is plausible but cannot presently establish an earlier cult securely because Gula and Gu₂-la₂ may be different names. The most defensible summary is therefore: **possible Old Akkadian precursor; secure Ur III goddess**.

### Sumerian literary corpus

Most surviving literary tablets are Old Babylonian school copies, even when compositions concern Ur III or early Isin-dynasty rulers.

1. **A hymn to Ninisina for Išbi-Erra (Išbi-Erra D), Sumerian.** Probably composed in the early Isin dynasty, early twentieth century BCE; surviving copy CBS 12604 is Old Babylonian. It calls Ninisina “Lady Gula,” locates her in Isin’s Egalmaḫ, and asks her to favor King Išbi-Erra. This is primary evidence for early linkage of the two names, not proof that every worshipper everywhere regarded them as identical. **[EA/SI]** [ETCSL text and witnesses](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?charenc=j&display=Crit&lineid=c2514.10&text=c.2.5.1.4).

2. **Ninisina A–F, Sumerian hymns and narratives.** The ETCSL corpus contains *A šir-gida to Ninisina*, *A šir-namšub to Ninisina*, *Ninisina’s Journey to Nibru*, *A Hymn to Ninisina*, *An adab to Ninisina*, and *Ninisina and the Gods*. They document the Isin goddess’s healing arts, journey to Enlil’s Nippur, relations with An, Enlil, Enki, Pabilsaĝ, Damu, and Gunura, and equations with other city goddesses. They belong in Gula’s dossier because later bilingual and syncretistic tradition transfers much of this profile to Gula; they are not direct evidence that the earliest Gula performed every narrated act. **[EA/SI]** [ETCSL Ninisina corpus](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=c.4.22%2A).

3. **Ninisina’s Journey to Nippur.** The surviving text is severely broken. Ninisina travels by boat, enters Enlil’s house, presents or prepares offerings, and is welcomed. Later bilingual recensions use Ninkarrak in the Akkadian version. **[EA/RV/SI]** [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.22.3).

4. **Ninisina and the Gods.** An and Enlil confer divine powers and destiny; Enki receives and adorns her in Eridu; Pabilsaĝ is her beloved spouse; the poem maps her onto goddesses including Ĝatumdug and Bau in their local cities. This is unusually direct evidence for an Old Babylonian-era theology of cross-city equivalence. **[EA/LS/SI]** [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.22.6).

5. **Damu laments, especially “In the Desert by the Early Grass.”** Ninisina or a corresponding healing mother and Gunura mourn Damu’s disappearance/death. Ur III Umma festival records associate Gula with mourning Damu, but direct familial language for Gula is later and less secure. **[EA/RV]**

### Akkadian corpus

1. **Bulluṭsa-rabi’s Hymn to Gula**, Akkadian, commonly catalogued as *Gula Hymn* or eBL L 3/7. The date of composition remains broad—often placed somewhere between approximately 1400 and 700 BCE—while surviving manuscripts are chiefly first millennium. A Mesopotamian catalogue explicitly attributes it to Bulluṭsa-rabi, a rare ancient author attribution. Gula speaks in the first person through much of the poem and claims a sequence of divine identities. Lambert’s 1967 edition remains foundational; new manuscripts and fragments have since improved the text. **[LC/LS]** [electronic Babylonian Library edition](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/3/7).

   Its sequence does not merely list synonyms. It constructs a learned universal goddess by identifying Gula, stanza by stanza, with such figures as Bau, Nanshe, Ninsun, Ninkarrak, Ninigizibara, and in some readings Ninlil. Some identifications arise from shared healing functions; others from analogous marriages, local theology, or learned wordplay. Notably, Ninisina is not plainly included among the named identifications, despite the common modern assertion that the hymn equates them all. **[LS/SI]**

2. **Šuilla and petitionary prayers to Gula.** First-millennium copies address her as exalted lady, compassionate mother, heavenly resident, healer, and intercessor when the petitioner is threatened by divine anger, omens, dreams, or disease. These prayers are personal ritual texts, not narrative myths. **[LC]**

3. **Medical incantations and therapeutic rituals.** Gula supplies plants, blesses bandages or preparations, and is invoked with her dog. Some formulas identify a remedy as “Gula’s plant” or ask her pure hand to restore the patient. Böck’s corpus study shows that her presence is important but not ubiquitous: much Mesopotamian medicine functions without invoking her, and surviving evidence does not justify describing every physician as her priest. **[LC]** [Böck record](https://emu.tind.io/record/254148).

4. **Diagnostic and prognostic handbook *Sakikkû*.** Certain diagnoses are described as “the hand of Gula”; this usually identifies supernatural agency or nosological classification, not a clinical observation that her temple caused the illness. **[LC/LS]**

5. **Incantations against Lamaštu.** Dogs speaking as Gula’s dogs threaten to mutilate the infant-killing demon. This juxtaposes the protective healer/midwife with a destroyer of pregnancies and infants. It does not constitute a narrative battle between Gula herself and Lamaštu. **[LC]**

6. **Curse clauses on kudurrus, treaties, grants, and royal inscriptions.** Gula and her dogs are invoked to inflict sores, disease, or relentless pursuit on anyone violating an oath or monument. On Nebuchadnezzar I’s kudurru tradition she may appear jointly with Ninurta in a destruction curse. **[LC]**

7. **God lists, especially *An = Anum*.** The mature list places healing goddesses and their families in a learned sequence but preserves Gula, Ninisina, Ninkarrak, and Nintinugga as distinguishable entries or courts. Gula’s attendants include Urmašum/Urmaš, a deity written ᵈUR who is equated with him, and Uršabiduga. **[LS]**

8. **Astral and lexical texts.** Gula can be associated with an asterism written **MUL.GU.LA**, but this does not make a planet or star her primary identity. Late astrological equations are scholarly mappings. **[LS/LA]**

9. **Royal inscriptions.** Assyrian and Babylonian kings record building, restoring, or provisioning her temples. Particularly important are Aššurnaṣirpal II at Kalḫu, Adad-nērārī II’s retrospective notice concerning Tukultī-Ninurta I at Aššur, Ashurbanipal’s restoration of Esabad at Babylon, and Neo-Babylonian building texts. Ashurbanipal’s Babylonian work began before 639 BCE and was completed by approximately 638/637. **[LC]** [RINAP 5/3](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/downloads/0RINAP5_3_final.pdf).

### Negative findings in the named major epics

No securely identified Gula has a narrative role in the standard recensions of **Enūma eliš**, **Atraḫasīs**, **The Epic of Gilgameš**, **Inanna’s Descent**, or **Enki and Ninmaḫ**. A temple-name or goddess-equation in a late commentary must not be converted into an episode featuring Gula. She is also not a participant in the Sumerian *Descent of Inanna*. Her dossier is built principally from hymns, prayers, medical-magical texts, god lists, administrative records, curse formulae, royal inscriptions, and material culture—not the famous creation and flood epics. **Absence here is significant.**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

- **Healing and bodily restoration:** her central mature domain. She treats wounds, fever, skin disease, internal illness, and conditions classified by diviners and physicians. **[EA/LC]**
- **Patronage of healers:** offerings by Ur III physicians and later title *azugallatu* support this relationship, but no evidence establishes a universal professional guild formally governed by her temples. **[EA/MSR]**
- **Medicinal plants:** hymns call her an herb-grower; therapeutic texts connect her with *buʾšānu/lišan kalbi* and *sasuntu* plants. **[LC]**
- **Birth and life preservation:** inherited partly from Ninisina and other mother/healing goddesses. **[SI/LC]**
- **Dream healing or incubation:** Neo-Babylonian evidence shows petitioners seeking or reporting nocturnal revelation. Evidence for institutional incubation like that known from later Asklepian sanctuaries is limited. **[LA]**
- **Punitive disease:** oath-breakers may be given incurable sores, and her divine “hand” can diagnose affliction. **[LC]**
- **Vegetation and regeneration:** a secondary interpretation derived from plant-growing language and connections with Damu; it should not replace the much better attested healing profile. **[MSR]**

### The dog

The dog is Gula’s defining animal from the Old Babylonian period onward, but explanations for the association remain hypotheses. Proposed interpretations include observation of licking wounds, the transfer of illness to an animal, dogs’ scavenging and liminal relation to death, or inheritance from older healing goddesses. No surviving Mesopotamian text supplies a single origin story.

Dogs appear:

- reclining beneath or beside the enthroned goddess;
- alone as her emblem on kudurrus;
- as clay, bronze, stone, gold, or silver dedications;
- in inscribed prayers;
- as threatening agents in curses;
- in ritual deposits;
- and as actual burials at Isin.

Seraina Nett’s archaeological-textual survey emphasizes that these uses span votive, protective, emblematic, and ritual contexts; “dog goddess” is convenient but Gula is normally anthropomorphic, not canine-bodied. **[EA/LC/MSR]** [Nett, “The Dogs of the Healing Goddess Gula”](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/e7e56308-2165-44c4-97b3-3cf66300a1b2).

### Recognizable images

- **Old Babylonian terracotta plaques:** seated goddess with a dog; attribution is probable when the dog and healing context coincide but not every seated goddess beside an animal is securely Gula. **[EA]**
- **Kassite kudurrus:** her most characteristic monumental iconography. She sits frontally or in profile on a shrine/throne, with a reclining dog. A Louvre fragment from Susa, made in Babylonia under Marduk-apla-iddina I, 1171–1159 BCE, bears both the figure and identifying context. It reached Susa as war booty. **[LC]** [Louvre SB 33](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010174463).
- **Nebuchadnezzar I-period and other twelfth-century kudurrus:** sometimes label Gula directly, making them especially important controls for otherwise ambiguous figures. [Israel Museum study](https://museum.imj.org.il/journal/archive/2004/images/The%20Goddess%20Gula%20and%20Her%20Tallay%20Oman.pdf).
- **British Museum kudurru 90835, 954 BCE:** Gula is enthroned with her dog among divine symbols used to witness a property settlement. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1882-0522-1799).
- **Neo-Assyrian stamp seal BM 130814:** Gula’s throne rests upon her recumbent dog; stars decorate the throne back. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1940-0210-8).
- **Dog figurines:** objects from Nippur, Dūr-Kurigalzu, Babylon, Aššur, and other sites may bear dedications or derive their identification from archaeological context. A Neo-Assyrian bronze dog from Nimrud illustrates the broader canine tradition but is not necessarily a cult statue of Gula. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/323812).

A scalpel occasionally appears in modern reconstructions as Gula’s fixed emblem. The dog is far more securely and consistently attested. No canonical ancient color, weekday, or sacred number can be assigned to her. Claims such as “blue is Gula’s color,” “Friday is her day,” or a fixed numerological correspondence are **[MI]** unless tied to a particular late astrological text.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Competing genealogies

There is no single original Gula family.

- **Independent early Gula:** Ur III records do not establish secure parents or spouse. **[EA]**
- **Ninisina tradition:** daughter of An and Uraš; wife of Pabilsaĝ; mother of Damu and Gunura, sometimes also Šumaḫ. **[EA/RV]**
- **Nippurian Gula:** wife of Ninurta, evidently through assimilation to local Nintinugga/Ninnibru and through the analogy Pabilsaĝ–Ninurta. **[SI/RV]**
- **Lagašite equation:** as Bau, wife of Ningirsu. **[SI/RV]**
- **Babylonian local arrangement:** Mandanu can occupy the warrior-husband’s role alongside Gula. **[RV/LA]**
- **Children:** Damu and Gunura become Gula’s children in later theology. The widespread statement that Ninazu was her son is not securely supported and should be marked **unestablished**.
- **Father:** An/Anu becomes standard through the Ninisina/Ninkarrak complex. Uraš appears as mother in some Ninisina traditions; she is not securely the mother of the earliest Gula.
- **Attendants:** Urmašum, ᵈUR, and Uršabiduga in *An = Anum*. **[LS]**

### Documented divine interactions and co-appearances

The following are discrete textual interactions or formally meaningful joint appearances. Mere membership in one pantheon is excluded.

- **An/Anu — grants or grounds her divine status; later treated as father.** *Ninisina and the Gods*, Old Babylonian Sumerian composition; transferred to Gula through equation. **[EA/SI]**
- **Uraš — mother of Ninisina in the Isin genealogy.** God lists and theological texts, second millennium BCE; applicable to Gula only through later identification. **[RV/SI]**
- **Enlil — determines Ninisina’s fate and receives her at Nippur.** *Ninisina and the Gods* and *Ninisina’s Journey to Nippur*, Old Babylonian manuscripts. **[EA/SI]**
- **Enki/Ea/Nudimmud — receives Ninisina in Eridu, seats her on his knees, adorns her, and determines her fate.** *Ninisina and the Gods*, Old Babylonian. The language creates a paternal or patronal relationship that sits uneasily beside the poem’s calling her child of An. **[EA/RV]**
- **Pabilsaĝ — beloved spouse of Ninisina; the pair lie together joyfully.** *Ninisina and the Gods*, Old Babylonian. Later made Gula’s spouse at Isin. **[EA/SI]**
- **Ninurta — spouse or cultic partner at Nippur; jointly invoked with Gula in curses and housed with her.** Kassite and first-millennium evidence. This is a theological marriage, not a surviving courtship myth. **[LC/RV/SI]**
- **Ningirsu — husband when Gula is identified with Bau in Lagaš.** Learned and regional theological equations, second millennium onward. No independent myth narrates Gula meeting Ningirsu under her own name. **[SI/RV]**
- **Mandanu — associated with Gula in Babylon in a role analogous to Ninurta.** First-millennium cultic evidence. **[RV/LA]**
- **Damu — paired with Gula in Ur III offerings and mourning rites; later made her son and co-healer.** Ur III administrative texts; later god lists and hymns. Maternal status for Gula is later than for Ninisina. **[EA/LC/SI]**
- **Gunura — appears beside Gula in Ur III records; later treated as her daughter through assimilation with Ninisina.** **[EA/SI]**
- **Ninisina — called “Lady Gula” and later translated or absorbed into Gula, but still appears separately in some first-millennium evidence.** Išbi-Erra D and subsequent lists. **[EA/SI]**
- **Ninkarrak — alternating or equivalent name in bilingual, lexical, and onomastic contexts; remains separately listed elsewhere.** Old Babylonian onward, especially Kassite Nippur. **[SI/LS]**
- **Nintinugga — functionally equated with Gula in Nippur yet retains a separate cult and genealogy.** Ur III through first millennium. **[SI/RV]**
- **Bau/Baba — identified as Gula’s Lagaš manifestation in hymnic theology.** *Bulluṭsa-rabi Hymn* and god-list tradition. **[LS/SI]**
- **Meme — absorbed into Gula more completely after the Old Babylonian period.** God lists and theological texts. **[SI]**
- **Nanshe — claimed as one of Gula’s identities in the Bulluṭsa-rabi hymn.** The basis is learned theological expansion, not a healing narrative. **[LS/SI]**
- **Ninsun — claimed as an identity in the Bulluṭsa-rabi hymn or related scholarship.** **[LS/SI]**
- **Ninlil/Sud — linked to Gula in learned lists and the hymn, possibly through an Old Babylonian Weidner-list equation.** **[LS/SI]**
- **Ninigizibara — a healing identity or manifestation claimed by Gula.** Bulluṭsa-rabi hymn and medical tradition. **[LS/SI]**
- **Lamaštu — Gula’s dogs threaten the demon in an anti-Lamaštu incantation.** First-millennium ritual tradition. Gula herself does not conduct a narrated combat. **[LC]**
- **Urmašum, ᵈUR, and Uršabiduga — listed as Gula’s sukkals/attendants.** *An = Anum*, canonical god-list tradition. **[LS]**
- **Kurunnam, Kusu, Nuska, Ninimma, Šuzianna, Bēlet-ṣēri, the Sebitti, Bēl-āliya, Siraš, and Ninĝirzida — receive cultic accommodation in or association with the late Gula temple at Nippur.** These are documented co-residents, not narrative interactions. **[LC/LA]**
- **Ḫala — Kassite lexical lists gloss Ḫala as Gula.** **[LS/SI]**

There is no documented personal encounter between Gula and Marduk, Ištar/Inanna, Tiāmat, Gilgameš, Enkidu, Atraḫasīs, or Ereškigal in the standard major narratives. Their joint occurrence in lists or pantheon-wide invocations is not an episode.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Gula has no surviving epic cycle comparable to those of Inanna or Ninurta. Her “mythology” survives largely through the inherited Ninisina corpus and theological autobiography.

### Ninisina’s journey to Nippur

**[EA/RV]** Ninisina travels by boat on the Euphrates toward Nippur, stopping en route and approaching Enlil’s sanctuary. She brings or prepares offerings and receives recognition in the divine center. Unfortunately, extensive lacunae prevent a continuous reconstruction; modern summaries that supply elaborate dialogue or a complete itinerary exceed the text. In a bilingual variant Ninkarrak replaces Ninisina in Akkadian, showing active translation among healing goddesses. Gula’s inclusion is indirect, through their later equation. [ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.22.3).

### Ninisina and the Gods

**[EA/LS]** The goddess possesses powers granted by An; Enlil determines her destiny. She proceeds to Eridu, where Enki receives, seats, cherishes, clothes, and adorns her. Pabilsaĝ is assigned as beloved spouse. The composition then identifies “my lady” with local goddesses including Ĝatumdug, Bau, and Nungal in different cities. It is less a plot-driven myth than a theological geography: one exalted healing/mother goddess manifested through local cults. This provides a second-millennium precedent for the still more expansive Bulluṭsa-rabi hymn. [ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.22.6).

### Damu’s disappearance and lamentation

**[EA/RV/SI]** Damu dies, disappears, or descends to the underworld in a body of fragmentary laments; his mother and sister mourn him. Ninisina is the best-attested mother, Gunura the sister. Ur III Umma ritual links Gula with mourning Damu, and later theology makes him Gula’s son. The exact narrative sequence cannot be reconstructed as a single canonical myth. Interpretations as a seasonal vegetation drama are possible but should not be stated as the text’s explicit doctrine.

### Bulluṭsa-rabi’s theological autobiography

**[LC/LS]** The hymn’s “story” is Gula’s self-disclosure. Through successive stanzas she declares herself in the forms of multiple goddesses while praising corresponding male partners and cities. Her range embraces cosmic authority, maternity, political power, and healing. Only a minority of the hymn concentrates narrowly on medicine; its purpose is to raise Gula to comprehensive rank by subsuming prestigious local female divinities. It is evidence of learned synthesis, not an early creation myth explaining that all these goddesses were always one.

### Healing and punishment as micro-narratives

Prayers and incantations repeatedly enact a small narrative: a sufferer, often uncertain which deity was offended, turns to Gula; she examines, intercedes, applies her hand or plant, loosens affliction, and restores life. Curse texts reverse the pattern: the violator is handed to Gula’s diseases or dogs. These are ritual plots rather than myths in the epic sense. **[LC]**

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Isin

Isin was principally the city of **Ninisina**, later increasingly identified with Gula. The main sanctuary was **Egalmaḫ**, “Exalted Palace.” A subsidiary building named **E-ur-gi₇-ra**, “House/Temple of the Dog,” is associated with King Enlil-bāni, 1860–1837 BCE.

Excavation beneath or beside the temple ramp recovered more than thirty dog burials. These are secure material evidence of special canine treatment in the healing-goddess precinct. Their exact purpose is unresolved. Suggestions include sacrificial offerings, honored temple animals, therapeutic substitutes, or animals maintained during life; pathological remains do not prove the temple was a veterinary hospital. The phrase “dog goddess of Isin” is therefore serviceable only if one remembers that the city’s older mistress was Ninisina. [LMU archaeological overview](https://www.en.vorderas-archaeologie.uni-muenchen.de/research/from-mesopotamia-with-love/dogs1/index.html); [ORACC/AMGG](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/GulaNinkarrak/index.html).

### Nippur

Excavations in Area WA uncovered successive monumental temple levels. The exposed Neo-Babylonian building was estimated at roughly 100 by 40 meters; underlying levels include seventh-century, Kassite, and Old Babylonian construction. Dog figurines, clay humans holding throat, head, or abdomen, and a lapis fragment inscribed **a-na ᵈGu-la**, “to Gula,” established the identification.

The excavators caution that the Gula sanctuary may be a sector of a larger Ninurta complex rather than a wholly independent temple. Its earliest strata may originally have belonged to another healing goddess, perhaps Bau or Nintinugga; calling all levels “the Temple of Gula” projects the later dedication backward. Excavation planned for 1991 was interrupted by the Gulf War, leaving major stratigraphic questions open. [ISAC Nippur report](https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/projects/nippur-sacred-city-enlil-0).

### Umma, Ur, Lagaš, and Uruk

- **Umma:** strongest early Gula cult, with offerings and a rite mourning Damu. No proof makes her Umma’s civic patroness. **[EA]**
- **Ur:** attested in Ur III records; later temple traditions mingle Gula and Ninisina. **[EA/SI]**
- **Lagaš:** Gula appears sporadically and is interpreted through Bau, wife of Ningirsu. **[RV/SI]**
- **Uruk:** Ur III evidence for Gula, disappearance from presently known Old Babylonian records, then later reappearance. Egalmaḫ and Bēlet-balāṭi traditions are debated; not every late “Lady of Life” is certainly Gula. **[RV]**

### Babylon and Borsippa

At Babylon, **Esabad**, “House of the Open Ear,” stood in the Tuba quarter. Ashurbanipal rebuilt it around 639–638 BCE. The name evokes a deity attentive to petition, though it is not unique proof of an oracle. Neo-Babylonian tradition reports precious-metal dogs placed at gateways, monumentalizing her emblem. [RINAP 5/3](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/downloads/0RINAP5_3_final.pdf).

Borsippa possessed three Gula shrines in late records. A local form, **Gula ša abbi**, probably “Gula of the ancestors,” may preserve a household/ancestral cult or an otherwise unknown local goddess assimilated to Gula. The interpretation remains disputed. [ORACC Borsippa corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/borsippa/P413004).

### Assyria

Gula entered Assyrian state cult comparatively late, in the latter second millennium. Adad-nērārī II credits Tukultī-Ninurta I with building her temple at Aššur, but this is retrospective evidence. Aššurnaṣirpal II established or renewed her cult and temple at Kalḫu when creating his capital in the ninth century BCE. Other northern centers included Tabetu and Mardaman. **[LC/RV]**

### Rituals and personnel

Documented practices include:

- animal and food offerings recorded in administrative accounts;
- hymns, laments, *šuila* prayers, and petitionary rites;
- clay or metal dog dedications;
- anthropomorphic ex-votos marking an afflicted body part;
- medicinal and incantatory rituals invoking her hand, plants, or dogs;
- curse formulae;
- dream petitions;
- royal rebuilding and endowment;
- and processional or calendrical participation with related deities.

There is no good evidence that Gula temples operated as hospitals in the modern institutional sense. The concentration of healing votives proves petition and thanksgiving, not ward-based treatment. Physicians’ devotion to her does not prove that all *asû* were temple employees.

No evidence specifically connects Gula’s cult with human sacrifice. The Isin dog burials are animal deposits, not human victims. No securely documented “temple prostitution” institution belongs to Gula’s cult; generic claims about Mesopotamian sacred prostitution cannot be transferred to her without a Gula-specific text. These are **negative findings**, not sanitized omissions.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Possible formation before Ur III

A healing-divinity background existed in Early Dynastic Mesopotamia through Bau, Nintinugga, and other local goddesses. Whether Gula herself existed then is uncertain because the readings Gula and Gu₂-la₂ cannot always be united. **[MSR]**

### Ur III, ca. 2112–2004 BCE

Gula is securely attested at Umma/KI.AN, Puzriš-Dagan, Ur, and possibly Lagaš. Associations with physicians, Damu, and Gunura already suggest healing and regenerative functions. Ninisina, Nintinugga, and Ninkarrak remain distinct regional goddesses. **[EA]**

### Isin-Larsa and Old Babylonian periods, ca. 2004–1595 BCE

The kings of Isin promote Ninisina and Egalmaḫ. Literary compositions develop her genealogy, journey, medical powers, and marriage to Pabilsaĝ. Gula spreads to Nippur, Uruk, Larsa, Sippar, and other cities, while names begin to overlap. Hammurabi’s political consolidation probably aided movement and equation of cults, but no single royal decree merged the goddesses. Dog iconography becomes recognizable. **[EA/SI]**

### Kassite and Middle Babylonian periods, ca. 1595–1155 BCE

This is Gula’s great ascent. She becomes the leading supraregional healing goddess, is married to Ninurta at Nippur, receives cult at Dūr-Kurigalzu, and appears prominently on kudurrus. Gula/Ninkarrak interchange in personal names shows syncretism operating in administration, not only abstract theology. The Bulluṭsa-rabi hymn may originate within this broad period, though a later date remains possible. **[LC/SI]**

### Middle and Neo-Assyrian periods, ca. 1400–612 BCE

Her cult is introduced to Aššur and then installed at Kalḫu. Assyrian royal patronage and scholarly copying integrate her into the imperial pantheon. Seals, prayers, medical series, and curse clauses flourish. **[LC]**

### Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid periods, 626–331 BCE

Temples at Babylon, Borsippa, Nippur, and other cities remain active; royal and private archives document personnel, property, offerings, and rebuilding. Gula is increasingly a learned universal healer while local manifestations remain viable. **[LC/LA]**

### Seleucid and later Mesopotamia, after 331 BCE

Late cuneiform scholarship continues divine equations and medical traditions into the Hellenistic period. Evidence becomes thinner and locally uneven. There is no recorded single act abolishing Gula’s cult. Mesopotamian temple institutions declined through changing patronage, urban conditions, imperial structures, and the eventual disappearance of cuneiform scholarly transmission.

Christianization and later Islamization transformed the religious landscape, but no known source documents a targeted campaign specifically against Gula, a forced conversion of her priesthood, or the conversion of a securely identified Gula temple into a church or mosque. Statements that “Christianity suppressed Gula” are broad historical inference, not a documented Gula-specific event.

### Modern period

Her ancient cult was not continuously transmitted through a demonstrable hereditary community. Present worship belongs to modern Mesopotamian reconstructionism or eclectic Paganism. Practitioners do exist and describe Gula as healer, divine physician, or consort of Ninurta, but no reliable census establishes their number. Online testimony documents practice, not continuity from antiquity. **[MI]**

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

Gula had little independent role in Renaissance hermeticism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, or classical planetary magic. Modern claims that she possessed a continuous occult correspondence system of planets, tarot cards, colors, chakras, or weekdays are recent constructions unless they cite an actual cuneiform astral text. She does not occur as a standard demon or spirit in the major early-modern grimoires.

Her recovery was primarily Assyriological:

- excavation and publication of Babylonian and Assyrian monuments in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries;
- identification of the dog as her emblem through labeled kudurrus;
- editions of hymns and god lists;
- Lambert’s 1967 publication of Bulluṭsa-rabi’s hymn;
- renewed archaeological attention after the Nippur discoveries in 1990;
- and modern syntheses by Böck, Asher-Greve, Westenholz, Sibbing-Plantholt, Tsouparopoulou, and Nett.

Contemporary uses include:

- **Reconstructionist devotion:** historically informed prayers, offerings, and healing petitions. This is a modern religious revival, not documented cult continuity. **[MI]**
- **Eclectic Pagan and occult practice:** invocation as a generic “Sumerian healing goddess,” often combining Gula, Ninisina, Bau, and Ninkarrak more completely than ancient evidence permits. **[MI]**
- **Popular mythology:** repeated claims that dog saliva directly explains her cult, that her temples were hospitals, or that she invented medicine. These are hypotheses or simplifications, not explicit ancient testimony.
- **Commercial imagery:** dog amulets, altar prints, and statues often derive from a small set of Kassite kudurru images.
- **Literature and games:** Gula remains relatively marginal compared with Inanna/Ištar, Ereshkigal, Marduk, or Tiamat. No single modern franchise has displaced the ancient healing-and-dog profile.
- **Political appropriation:** no documented extremist or major nationalist movement centers on Gula. General invocations of “Sumerian heritage” in Iraqi or diasporic identity should not be mistaken for a Gula cult.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major scholarship

- W. F. M. Henkelman and earlier *Reallexikon der Assyriologie* traditions established the basic lexical and cultic dossier.
- W. G. Lambert, “The Gula Hymn of Bulluṭsa-rabi” (1967), produced the foundational edition of the great Akkadian hymn.
- Andrew R. George’s work on temple names remains essential for identifying Esabad, Egalmaḫ, and other sanctuaries.
- Hector Avalos examined the relation between temples and healthcare, importantly questioning the uncritical label “hospital.”
- Joan Goodnick Westenholz and Julia Asher-Greve emphasized historical differentiation among goddesses and gendered divine traditions.
- Barbara Böck’s *The Healing Goddess Gula* systematically studied Gula in diagnostic, incantatory, and pharmacological literature.
- Irene Sibbing-Plantholt has refined the formation of Gula’s identity, the Gu₂-la₂ problem, and the distinct careers of healing goddesses.
- Christina Tsouparopoulou and Seraina Nett have integrated administrative, visual, and canine evidence.
- Elena Devecchi’s recent onomastic analysis demonstrates Gula/Ninkarrak variation within Kassite documentary practice.

### Principal controversies

1. **Was Gula originally an epithet?**  
   Evidence: ordinary Sumerian *gula*, “great,” appears after divine names, including Ninisina. Counterevidence: Gula is independently localized and may coexist with similarly named Gu₂-la₂. Most cautious conclusion: epithet-to-theonym development is plausible but not proved. **[MSR]**

2. **Are Gula, Ninisina, and Ninkarrak one goddess?**  
   No at origin; sometimes functionally yes in later texts. Separate cults, lists, and genealogies coexist with bilingual substitution and hymnological equation. “Syncretism” describes a process, not an original identity.

3. **Where did Gula originate?**  
   Umma is the conventional answer because of strong Ur III documentation, but she was not Umma’s chief goddess and may have arrived from KI.AN or another settlement. The proposed Old Akkadian Adab evidence remains uncertain.

4. **Why dogs?**  
   Healing saliva, transference ritual, protection, death/scavenging, and inheritance from other goddesses have all been proposed. No ancient etiological text settles the matter.

5. **Were Gula temples clinics?**  
   Body-part votives, physician dedications, and healing prayers show cultic medicine. They do not establish inpatient care, medical teaching, or temple employment for every practitioner. The hospital model is an attractive but unproved modern analogy.

6. **Were the Isin dogs sacrificed?**  
   Deliberate burial near the sacred ramp is certain; sacrificial killing is not. Zooarchaeological condition, burial pattern, and context allow several interpretations.

7. **Was Gula once a death goddess?**  
   This older theory inferred an original chthonic identity from dogs, punitive disease, and Damu’s death cycle. It remains speculative and is not required by the earliest secure evidence.

8. **Did Christianity destroy her cult?**  
   There is no Gula-specific destruction narrative. Her disappearance belongs to the long decline of cuneiform temple culture. Claims of a named Christian suppression are unsubstantiated.

### State of the evidence

Thousands of references potentially occur in administrative, lexical, medical, and literary tablets distributed among the Iraq Museum, British Museum, Vorderasiatisches Museum, Louvre, University of Pennsylvania Museum, Yale Babylonian Collection, Istanbul collections, and excavated archives. CDLI and ORACC improve discovery but cannot eliminate uncertainties caused by damaged tablets, unpublished collections, uncertain provenience, variant readings, and legacy excavation records.

The 1990 Nippur identification was especially important: the inscribed lapis dedication converted an iconographic hypothesis into a secure temple attribution. The Gulf War’s interruption of excavation left the earlier levels incompletely explored.

### Evidentiary conclusions

- Secure: Gula was a goddess; her independent cult is securely visible by Ur III; healing and dogs are ancient, not modern additions.
- Secure: the Isin dog burials and Nippur dog/body-part figurines are archaeological facts.
- Secure: Gula, Ninisina, Ninkarrak, Nintinugga, Bau, and Meme could be equated.
- Equally secure: they were not everywhere or originally one undifferentiated goddess.
- Probable, not proved: Gula began as an epithet meaning “the Great One.”
- Unresolved: exact origin city, meaning of the earliest Gu₂-la₂ forms, and the original reason for the dog.
- Unsupported: Gula-specific human sacrifice, temple prostitution, institutional hospitals, a continuous occult lineage, or targeted Christian suppression.
- Modern invention unless independently documented: fixed tarot, chakra, color, weekday, and universal planetary correspondences.
