# Utnapishtim (Ziusudra, Atrahasis): Research Dossier

**Class:** Primarily a legendary human king, priest, sage, and flood survivor—not an originally worshiped god. In the Sumerian flood narrative Ziusudra receives “life like a god” and eternal life; in *Gilgamesh* Utnapishtim and his wife are made “like gods” and removed from ordinary humanity. This is apotheosis or exceptional immortality, not evidence of an independent cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest unambiguous attestations belong to the Old Babylonian period. Ziusudra appears in Old Babylonian Sumerian literary and king-list traditions, probably by approximately 1900–1800 BCE; the securely provenanced Sumerian flood tablet CBS 10673 from Nippur is dated palaeographically to about 1650 BCE. The principal Old Babylonian *Atrahasis* manuscript was copied in Ammi-ṣaduqa’s years 11–12, approximately 1635–1634 BCE under one conventional chronology. Earlier, mid-third-millennium manuscripts of the *Instructions of Shuruppak* prove that the composition itself is very old, but scholars must not automatically project every name and line of the later Old Babylonian recension—including Ziusudra’s name—back into every Early Dynastic copy.

**Evidentiary synopsis:** “Ziusudra,” “Atrahasis,” “Utnapishtim,” and Berossus’s “Xisuthros” are historically related literary realizations of the Mesopotamian flood survivor. Their equivalence is supported by shared narrative structure, Shuruppak provenance, kinship traditions, bilingual lexical equations, and learned name translation. They are not interchangeable at every date: each belongs to a particular language, work, and redactional setting.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The figure

The dossier concerns a Mesopotamian survivor of the primeval Flood. In the Sumerian narrative he is Ziusudra, king and *gudug* priest, warned through a wall after the divine assembly decrees humanity’s destruction. His boat survives seven days and nights; he sacrifices after Utu reappears; An and Enlil grant him godlike and eternal life and settle him in Dilmun.

In the Akkadian *Atrahasis*, the “exceedingly wise” hero is Enki/Ea’s human devotee. He survives the last of Enlil’s population-destroying measures and preserves a human remnant. In the Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*, Utnapishtim recounts that flood in the first person to Gilgamesh, explains how he and his wife became immortal, tests Gilgamesh’s capacity to resist sleep, and reveals a rejuvenating plant. Berossus’s Hellenistic Xisuthros survives a flood, deposits writings at Sippar, disappears among the gods, and addresses the surviving boat party as a disembodied voice.

### Names

- **Zi-ud-sura / Zi-u₄-sud-ra**, conventionally **Ziusudra** or **Ṣiusudra**: Sumerian, written in such forms as `zi-ud-su₃-ra₂`, `zi-u₄-sud-ra`, and `zi-sud-da`. The probable sense is “life of distant/long days” or “life prolonged in days.” “He who lengthened life’s days” is an interpretive paraphrase. The spelling and grammatical segmentation have been debated since Poebel, Zimmern, Albright, Kramer, and Jacobsen.

- **Atram-ḫasīs / Atra-ḫasīs**: Akkadian, “exceedingly wise,” “surpassingly sage.” The Old Babylonian form commonly shows the accusative-looking ending *Atram-ḫasīs*. It can function as a personal name, an epithet, or a type-name for the paradigmatically wise man. Its appearance as an epithet in flood-related material helps explain scribal movement between hero names, but it does not prove that every “atra-ḫasīs” in Akkadian literature is the flood hero.

- **Ūta-napišti(m), Uta-napišti, Utnapištim**, conventional English **Utnapishtim**: Akkadian learned rendering of the Sumerian name. Proposed analyses include “he found life,” “I found my life,” and Lambert’s “I shall find life.” Andrew George’s *Reallexikon der Assyriologie* treatment stresses that the form probably arose through learned interpretation of Ziusudra rather than through ordinary name formation. The name is sometimes spelled with final *-m* in modern transcription although Akkadian case endings vary by context.

- **Ūta-napišti rūqu**, “Utnapishtim the Far-Away” or “the Distant”: the characteristic designation in Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh*. It marks his removal beyond normal human geography and society.

- **Awīl Šuruppak**, “man of Shuruppak”: Utnapishtim identifies himself with the city in *Gilgamesh* XI.

- **Atra-ḫasīs**, “the exceedingly wise”: used inside the *Gilgamesh* flood account in a passage where Ea denies having directly betrayed the gods’ counsel and says he caused the “exceedingly wise” to see a dream. This is a deliberate allusion to the older *Atrahasis* tradition.

- **Xísouthros / Xisuthros / Sisithros**, Greek **Ξίσουθρος** and variant transmitted spellings: Berossus’s Hellenized continuation of Ziusudra. The variants result partly from the multiple-stage transmission Berossus → Alexander Polyhistor/Abydenus → Eusebius → Syncellus and Armenian witnesses.

- **Atnabiš / Atambīš**, Middle Persian `ʾtnbyš`: a name in Manichaean *Book of Giants* fragments that many scholars derive from Utnapishtim. This is a late transformation inside an Enochic-Manichaean giant tradition, not evidence that ancient Babylonians considered Utnapishtim a giant.

- **Noah:** not another ancient spelling of Utnapishtim. Noah is the Hebrew flood survivor of Genesis 6–9. The narratives are historically comparable and probably connected through Mesopotamian literary tradition, but no cuneiform source equates the personal names.

### Classification through time

- **Old Babylonian Sumerian:** mortal king and *gudug* priest; afterward recipient of godlike and eternal life.
- **Old Babylonian Akkadian:** exceptionally wise human, apparently Enki’s devotee; whether the damaged ending of the Old Babylonian poem explicitly grants him immortality remains less secure than in the Sumerian and Gilgamesh versions.
- **Standard Babylonian:** immortalized human ancestor living apart with his wife.
- **Berossus:** antediluvian king translated to the gods.
- **Manichaean reception:** transformed or recontextualized in giant literature.
- **Modern popular usage:** frequently called a “god,” “demigod,” or “Sumerian Noah.” “God” is imprecise; “Sumerian Noah” is a comparative nickname, not an ancient title.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Early Sumerian witnesses

#### *Instructions of Shuruppak*

The composition is attested in Early Dynastic manuscripts from Abu Salabikh, approximately 2500 BCE, and in later Old Babylonian recensions. In its familiar composite form, Shuruppak, son of Ubara-Tutu, instructs his son Ziusudra. Because the composite incorporates manuscripts separated by many centuries, the antiquity of the composition cannot automatically be used as the date of every restored personal name. The safe conclusion is that the wisdom work is Early Dynastic, while the full Ziusudra genealogy is unambiguous in the later recension. [ETCSL’s literary-period overview](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/literature.php) distinguishes the early witnesses from eighteenth-century copies.

**Tier:** securely attested early composition; Ziusudra’s precise presence in the earliest surviving witness requires manuscript-level caution.

#### W-B 62 and the antediluvian king tradition

W-B 62, generally dated around the end of the third or beginning of the second millennium BCE, gives Ziusudra a fantastically long reign of 36,000 years in the Shuruppak sequence. It appears to misread “man of Shuruppak” as a genealogical link, inserting “Shuruppak” as though it were a person between Ubara-Tutu and Ziusudra. The more famous Weld-Blundell prism W-B 444, approximately 1800 BCE, ends its antediluvian dynasty with Ubara-Tutu and “the Flood swept over”; it does not name Ziusudra in that position. Thorkild Jacobsen therefore warned against making one smooth king list from divergent recensions. [Jacobsen’s critical edition](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/as11.pdf) lays out these differences.

**Tier:** securely attested Old Babylonian learned historiography; not ordinary political chronology.

#### Sumerian flood narrative, “Eridu Genesis”

The unique principal tablet, CBS 10673 + CBS 10867, was excavated at Nippur during the University of Pennsylvania expedition of 1896. It is baked clay, Sumerian, six columns, preserving roughly the lower third of an original text of perhaps 260 lines. The Penn Museum dates it to about 1650 BCE. Arno Poebel published it in 1914.

Its preserved sequence includes creation and establishment of cities; the divine decision to destroy humanity; Ziusudra listening beside a wall; a large lacuna where the boat instructions presumably stood; the seven-day-and-night flood; Utu’s appearance; sacrifice; and the grant of eternal life in Dilmun. The reason for the Flood is lost. Calling the text “Eridu Genesis” is Thorkild Jacobsen’s modern title, not an ancient title.

The tablet explicitly calls Ziusudra king and *gudug* priest. It says An and Enlil “granted him life like a god” and settled him “in an overseas country, in the land Dilmun, where the sun rises.” [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?charenc=j&text=t.1.7.4); [Penn Museum object record](https://www.penn.museum/collections/object/97591).

**Tier:** securely attested Old Babylonian Sumerian narrative, probably preserving older traditions; the amount and form of its pre-Old-Babylonian ancestry remain reconstructed.

### Akkadian *Atrahasis*

The best-preserved Old Babylonian recension occupied three tablets. The manuscripts’ colophons associate their copying with Ammi-ṣaduqa’s years 11–12, conventionally about 1635–1634 BCE, and with the scribe Ipiq-Aya/Ku-Aya; readings of the scribe’s name have varied. The poem begins *inūma ilū awīlum*, “When the gods were man/like men,” and supplies a complete primeval history:

1. Junior gods labor at canals.
2. They rebel.
3. Enki and the birth goddess arrange humanity’s creation from clay and the flesh and blood of a slain god.
4. Human multiplication and noise disturb Enlil.
5. Enlil sends plague and famine/drought.
6. Enki instructs Atrahasis how to redirect worship and defeat each measure.
7. The gods bind themselves to a flood decision.
8. Enki communicates through a reed wall and saves Atrahasis.
9. After the Flood, the divine order introduces mortality, infertility, infant death, and consecrated women as population controls.

The flood ending is damaged and reconstructed through multiple manuscripts. The standard critical edition remains W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard, with the Sumerian text edited by Miguel Civil. Its surviving witnesses include Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, Middle Assyrian/Neo-Assyrian, Late Babylonian, and an Akkadian fragment from Ugarit; finds or textual relatives from Hattusa demonstrate wide curricular transmission. [Penn State/Eisenbrauns edition description](https://www.eisenbrauns.org/books/titles/978-1-57506-184-9.html); [Sources of Early Akkadian Literature manuscript table](https://seal.huji.ac.il/index.php/node/1515).

**Tier:** securely attested Old Babylonian Akkadian epic with later regional recensions.

### Old Babylonian *Gilgamesh*

Old Babylonian fragments from the early second millennium already show Gilgamesh undertaking a journey connected with the distant survivor and human mortality. The surviving Old Babylonian material is too fragmentary to establish that it contained the full flood narration later inserted into Tablet XI. It demonstrates that the journey to the remote wise survivor was not simply invented by the first-millennium Nineveh copyists.

**Tier:** securely attested predecessor narrative; the form of its flood episode is uncertain.

### Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*

The Standard Babylonian epic, conventionally associated with the learned author-editor Sîn-lēqi-unninni, took form in the late second millennium BCE and survives mainly in first-millennium copies. Utnapishtim appears principally in Tablets IX–XI:

- **Tablet IX:** Gilgamesh seeks Utnapishtim, his ancestor, because he “found life” in the gods’ assembly.
- **Tablet X:** Siduri and Urshanabi frame the impossible journey across the Waters of Death; Gilgamesh reaches Utnapishtim.
- **Tablet XI:** Utnapishtim tells the Flood, tests Gilgamesh with sleeplessness, and reveals the plant “The Old Man Becomes Young.”

The chief manuscript of Tablet XI is British Museum K.3375, from Ashurbanipal’s library at Kuyunjik/Nineveh, seventh century BCE. It contains the square or cubic boat, craftsmen and wealth, family, animals and “seed of all living things,” a six-day storm with calm on the seventh, grounding on Mount Nimuš, dove/swallow/raven tests, sacrifice, divine quarrel, and Utnapishtim’s immortality. The standardized narrative adapts language and scenes from the *Atrahasis* tradition.

Andrew George’s 2003 *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic* is the foundational modern critical edition. A portion of Tablet XI is available with Akkadian transcription and English translation through [SOAS’s Babylonian and Assyrian Poetry and Literature in Audio Recordings](https://www.soas.ac.uk/baplar/recordings/epic-gilgames-standard-version-tablet-xi-lines-1-34-read-nathan-wasserman). The [British Museum record for K.3375](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_K-3375) supplies material, date, provenance, and excavation data.

**Tier:** later canonical elaboration, based on older Akkadian flood literature.

### The late *Atrahasis* witness with Utnapishtim’s name

A Neo-Babylonian or Achaemenid-period tablet in the Metropolitan Museum preserves the end of the *Atrahasis* tradition while naming the survivor Ut-napishtim. This is direct evidence that the story-names were not sealed into mutually exclusive textual compartments. [Metropolitan Museum object record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321999).

**Tier:** late scribal harmonization or cross-fertilization within Mesopotamian scholarship.

### Berossus, *Babyloniaca*

Berossus, a Babylonian priest writing in Greek under Antiochus I in the early third century BCE, placed Xisuthros as the tenth antediluvian king. Cronus warns him in a dream that humanity will be destroyed on the fifteenth of Daisios. He is to bury the “beginnings, middles, and ends” of writings at Sippar, build a ship, and embark with family, friends, animals, and provisions. After three bird releases and landing in Armenia, Xisuthros, his wife, daughter, and pilot disappear; his voice declares that he has gone to dwell with the gods. Survivors return to Babylon, recover the writings at Sippar, and rebuild cities and temples.

Berossus’s original is lost. The account survives through epitomes and quotations, especially Alexander Polyhistor, Abydenus, Eusebius, Armenian transmission, and George Syncellus around 800 CE. Thus it is a Hellenistic Babylonian witness preserved in late antique and Byzantine textual chains, not a verbatim extant Babylonian manuscript. [Berossus flood translation and transmission notice](https://www.livius.org/articles/misc/great-flood/flood3_t-berossus/); [Burstein’s study of the fragmentary transmission](https://prajnaquest.fr/blog/wp-content/uploads/Babylonaica-of-Berossus.pdf).

**Tier:** Hellenistic learned systematization and Greek-language regional adaptation.

### Manichaean *Book of Giants*

Middle Persian, Sogdian, and Old Turkic Manichaean fragments preserve transformed Gilgamesh-associated names. Middle Persian `ʾtnbyš`, Atnabiš, is widely read as descending from Utnapishtim. Here the name has entered Enochic giant lore and no longer functions simply as the Babylonian immortal flood sage. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/manicheism-parent/manicheism-pandaemonium/).

**Tier:** late antique syncretistic transformation.

### Texts named in the brief that do not attest him

- **Enūma eliš:** does not name Ziusudra, Atrahasis, or Utnapishtim.
- **Inanna’s Descent:** no appearance.
- **Enki and Ninmah:** no appearance, although its human-creation theology is relevant comparatively.
- **An = Anum:** not evidence for his membership in the divine pantheon; lexical equations elsewhere are more relevant.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP:** no known royal cult or campaign centered on him.
- **Inanna/Ishtar cycle:** no independent encounter with him outside the gods’ participation in the Gilgamesh flood scene.

These absences matter: the survivor belongs overwhelmingly to literary primeval historiography, not the ordinary god lists, temple lists, and royal cult apparatus.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

He has no securely attested divine “domain” comparable to Enki’s freshwater or Utu’s sun and justice. His functions are narrative:

- survivor and preserver of human and animal life;
- possessor of antediluvian knowledge;
- wise client of Enki/Ea;
- boundary figure between mortality and divine life;
- proof that immortality was once exceptionally granted but is unavailable to Gilgamesh;
- in Berossus, custodian and transmitter of written civilization.

### Powers and limitations

Before the Flood, he possesses no superhuman power beyond wisdom, piety, wealth, and receptivity to divine counsel. He requires a constructed vessel and divine warning. Afterward, he is immortal or possesses “life like a god,” but remains geographically isolated.

In *Gilgamesh*, he cannot bestow immortality by personal fiat. The divine assembly made his case unique. He can conduct a test—Gilgamesh must remain awake for six days and seven nights—but Gilgamesh immediately sleeps. Utnapishtim’s wife persuades him to give the traveler a compensatory gift, the rejuvenating plant. Even that gift is lost to a serpent.

### Geography

- **Dilmun “where the sun rises”:** Sumerian flood narrative. Historical Dilmun was centered in the Persian Gulf, especially Bahrain and adjacent regions, but a literary “place of sunrise” should not be mapped mechanically onto a single archaeological site.
- **“At the mouth of the rivers”:** *Gilgamesh*. This is mythic terminal geography beyond the Waters of Death, not a securely locatable residence.
- **Armenia/Corcyraean mountains:** Berossus. This northern landing belongs to the Hellenistic version and should not be retrojected into the Sumerian text.

### Iconography

No ancient image is securely identified as Ziusudra, Atrahasis, or Utnapishtim. Cylinder seals showing boats, storms, sacrifice, sages, or enthroned figures cannot be assigned to him without an identifying inscription or uniquely diagnostic scene. Standard handbooks of Mesopotamian divine iconography therefore do not give him a fixed horned crown, animal, weapon, star, number, color, day, or emblem.

The famous artifacts are textual objects, not portraits:

- CBS 10673 + 10867, Sumerian flood tablet;
- the Ipiq-Aya *Atrahasis* tablets;
- K.3375, *Gilgamesh* Tablet XI;
- W-B 62 and related king-list witnesses.

Modern illustrations of a bearded “Utnapishtim,” square ark, or round coracle are reconstructions. The round coracle belongs specifically to the Middle Babylonian “Ark Tablet” version of the wider *Atrahasis* tradition; it should not replace the vessel described in every recension.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

### Genealogical variants

| Tradition | Father/ancestry | Wife and descendants | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Later *Instructions of Shuruppak* | Ziusudra is son of Shuruppak; Shuruppak is son of Ubara-Tutu | None named | Old Babylonian recension of older wisdom tradition |
| W-B 62 as literally copied | Ziusudra follows an apparent “Shuruppak” generation after Ubara-Tutu | None | Probably scribal misdivision of “man of Shuruppak” |
| Sumerian flood tablet | Preserved portion does not securely state his father | No wife named in extant lines | King and *gudug* priest |
| *Gilgamesh* XI | Utnapishtim is son of Ubar-Tutu and man of Shuruppak | Wife present but unnamed; family boards boat | Later canonical Akkadian form |
| Berossus | Xisuthros succeeds Otiartes/Opartes, reflecting the Ubara-Tutu tradition | Wife and daughter accompany him; neither named | Hellenistic learned system |

The “Shuruppak as father” variant is probably not a separate mythology in which a city literally fathered him. Jacobsen followed earlier philological work in treating it as a scribal reinterpretation of “Ziusudra, man of Shuruppak.”

Utnapishtim is called Gilgamesh’s “ancestor” or forefather, but no surviving genealogy provides the intervening generations. It denotes remote ancestral relationship, not a checkable father-to-son chain.

### Every documented interaction with a named divine being

The entries distinguish direct contact from action merely witnessed or narrated.

- **Enki/Nudimmud — warning through the wall:** Enki communicates the divine flood decision to Ziusudra beside a wall. Sumerian flood tablet, ca. 1650 BCE. **Securely attested Old Babylonian Sumerian.**

- **Enki/Ea — patron and adviser during plague:** Enki tells Atrahasis how humanity can redirect worship to Namtar so plague ends. *Atrahasis* I–II, ca. 1635 BCE copy. **Securely attested Old Babylonian Akkadian.**

- **Enki/Ea — adviser during drought/famine:** Enki supplies countermeasures against Enlil’s extermination policy. *Atrahasis* II. **Securely attested Old Babylonian Akkadian, text partly reconstructed.**

- **Enki/Ea — flood warning through reed wall:** Because bound by the gods’ oath, Enki addresses Atrahasis’s house/reed wall and orders him to dismantle his house and build a boat. *Atrahasis* III. **Securely attested Old Babylonian Akkadian.**

- **Enki/Ea — warning and defense:** Ea tells Utnapishtim of Shuruppak to build a boat and later defends his conduct before Enlil, claiming that the “exceedingly wise” learned through a dream. *Gilgamesh* XI, Standard Babylonian, first-millennium manuscripts of a late-second-millennium edition. **Later canonical elaboration.**

- **Enki/Ea — post-flood mediation:** In the Sumerian narrative Enki is part of the divine resolution that culminates in Ziusudra’s survival and exaltation, though damaged lines obstruct the precise sequence. **Securely attested but fragmentary.**

- **An — participant in destruction oath:** An joins Enlil, Enki, and Ninhursag in binding the gods to the flood decision that Ziusudra overhears. Sumerian flood tablet. **Securely attested early material.**

- **An — grant of immortal life:** Ziusudra prostrates himself before An and Enlil; they treat him kindly, give him “life like a god,” and settle him in Dilmun. Sumerian flood tablet. **Securely attested early material.**

- **Anu — assembly authority:** Anu participates in the great-god assembly behind the *Atrahasis* measures against humanity; Atrahasis is affected rather than shown conversing personally with him. **Securely attested Old Babylonian Akkadian, indirect interaction.**

- **Enlil — persecutor through plague, drought, and flood:** Enlil repeatedly seeks to reduce or destroy humanity; Atrahasis survives through Enki’s counsels. *Atrahasis* I–III. **Securely attested early Akkadian.**

- **Enlil — post-flood confrontation:** Enlil is enraged that a human survived; Enki rebukes the indiscriminate flood and advocates proportionate punishments. Atrahasis is the object of their dispute. *Atrahasis* III. **Securely attested early Akkadian.**

- **Enlil — apotheosis:** Enlil boards or approaches Utnapishtim’s boat, leads him and his wife out, touches their foreheads, blesses them, and declares them like gods, assigning them a distant residence. *Gilgamesh* XI. **Later canonical elaboration.**

- **Ninhursag/Nintur — divine oath and lament:** She participates in the Sumerian divine assembly; Nintur laments humanity in the damaged flood text. Ziusudra is the beneficiary of the surviving remnant. **Securely attested, indirect and fragmentary.**

- **Nintu/Mami/Belet-ili — creator and post-flood regulator:** In *Atrahasis* the birth goddess helps create humanity and, after the Flood, establishes new reproductive limits. Atrahasis belongs to the humanity she preserves under altered conditions. **Securely attested Old Babylonian Akkadian, mainly indirect.**

- **Belet-ili — lament and oath-token:** In *Gilgamesh* XI she laments the destruction and raises her lapis-lazuli fly necklace, swearing to remember the event and excluding Enlil from the sacrifice. Utnapishtim hears or narrates this. **Later canonical elaboration.**

- **Utu — sunlight and sacrifice:** After seven days and nights Utu emerges, illuminates the boat, and enters it with his rays; Ziusudra prostrates himself and sacrifices cattle and sheep. Sumerian flood tablet. **Securely attested Old Babylonian Sumerian.**

- **Shamash — no secure personal encounter under that Akkadian name:** Solar action is Utu’s in the Sumerian account; attempts to supply an additional Shamash episode conflate languages. **Absence finding.**

- **Adad/Iškur — storm agent:** Adad roars in the cloud and leads the destructive weather in *Gilgamesh* XI; Utnapishtim survives his storm. **Later canonical elaboration, indirect conflict.**

- **Shullat and Hanish — storm heralds:** These named divine heralds advance over hill and plain before the deluge in *Gilgamesh* XI. **Later canonical elaboration, indirect conflict.**

- **Erragal/Erra — destructive agent:** Erragal tears out mooring or dam posts during the *Gilgamesh* flood. **Later canonical elaboration, indirect conflict.**

- **Ninurta — destructive agent:** Ninurta causes the dikes or waters to overflow in *Gilgamesh* XI. **Later canonical elaboration, indirect conflict.**

- **Anunnaki — divine illumination/destruction:** The Anunnaki raise torches, setting the land ablaze before or during the storm. *Gilgamesh* XI. **Later canonical collective interaction.**

- **Namtar — plague reversed through worship:** Atrahasis follows Enki’s strategy of directing offerings to the plague deity, who relents. *Atrahasis*. **Securely attested early Akkadian.**

- **Adad — drought reversed through worship:** Atrahasis and humanity redirect cult to the weather god in the famine sequence, according to reconstructed portions of *Atrahasis*. **Securely attested but textually damaged.**

- **Cronus — dream warning:** Berossus’s Cronus warns Xisuthros of the flood and orders preservation of writings. Cronus is the Greek interpretive identification of Ea/Enki in this episode, not an independent Sumerian meeting. **Hellenistic syncretistic identification.**

- **Gilgamesh — meeting, instruction, and test:** Gilgamesh is partly divine but remains mortal in the epic. Utnapishtim recounts the Flood, tests his wakefulness, orders cleansing and new clothing, and reveals the rejuvenating plant. Tablets X–XI. **Later canonical interaction with a hero of mixed divine-human status.**

No text names a divine consort, divine child, sacred mount, servant-demon, or permanent divine enemy for Utnapishtim.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Ziusudra and the Sumerian Flood

**Early attested form:** CBS 10673 + 10867, ca. 1650 BCE.

Civilization begins with kingship and the founding of Eridu, Bad-tibira, Larak, Sippar, and Shuruppak. After a large lacuna, the gods decide to destroy “the seed of mankind.” Ziusudra, king and *gudug* priest, is humble and reverent. Enki communicates through a wall. The boat-building instructions are lost.

When the text resumes, storms have engulfed the land. The huge boat is tossed for seven days and seven nights. Utu emerges, Ziusudra opens an aperture, prostrates himself, and sacrifices oxen and sheep. After further broken lines, animals disembark. Ziusudra prostrates himself before An and Enlil. They grant him godlike and eternal life and settle him in Dilmun.

The text does not preserve:

- a reason for the Flood;
- the boat’s shape or measurements;
- a named wife;
- bird releases;
- a landing mountain;
- an explicit statement that every other human died.

Supplying those details from *Atrahasis*, *Gilgamesh*, or Genesis would create an unattested composite.

### B. Atrahasis and the History of Humanity

**Early attested form:** Old Babylonian three-tablet recension copied ca. 1635–1634 BCE.

The junior Igigi gods dig the Tigris and Euphrates and maintain irrigation for 3,600 years. They burn their tools and surround Enlil’s dwelling. Enki proposes creating humans to assume divine labor. The birth goddess mixes clay with the flesh and blood of a slain intelligent or rebellious god, variously read We-ilu/Aw-ilu/Geshtu-e.

Humanity multiplies. Every 1,200 years its noise deprives Enlil of sleep. Enlil orders plague, then drought and famine. Atrahasis asks Enki for help. Enki’s strategies redirect cult to the responsible deity and break the collective embargo.

Finally the gods swear to use a flood. Enki avoids a literal breach of oath by addressing a reed wall: Atrahasis is to abandon possessions, hate property, and build a roofed boat. The assembly of the vessel, sealing with bitumen, entry of family and animals, and storm are preserved unevenly across manuscripts. Atrahasis offers sacrifice when the water recedes; the starving gods gather.

The lesson is not simply that Atrahasis’s personal goodness saves him. The poem is a myth of labor, population, subsistence, sacrifice, and mortality. Enki rebukes indiscriminate annihilation, while the post-flood settlement creates structural checks: barren women, a child-snatching demon, infant mortality, and classes of women barred from ordinary motherhood.

Whether Atrahasis himself is explicitly made immortal in the damaged Old Babylonian conclusion is less certain than popular harmonized retellings imply. Immortality is explicit for Ziusudra and for Utnapishtim.

### C. Utnapishtim and Gilgamesh

**Later canonical form:** Standard Babylonian Tablets IX–XI.

After Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh realizes that he too must die. He crosses mountains, reaches Siduri, kills the Stone Ones in anger, and crosses the Waters of Death with Urshanabi. Utnapishtim appears physically ordinary, prompting Gilgamesh to ask how he obtained divine life.

Utnapishtim’s first lesson is universal mortality: no one sees Death’s face or hears its voice, yet it cuts humanity down. He then narrates the secret counsel of the great gods. Ea speaks to a wall and commands him to demolish his house and build a boat. He deceives Shuruppak’s citizens with an explanation tailored to Enlil’s hostility. He loads silver, gold, family, craftsmen, animals, and the “seed of every living thing.”

The boat’s breadth and length are equal; it has multiple decks and compartments and is sealed with bitumen. At the appointed signal he boards and gives the vessel to the boatman Puzur-Enlil. The storm gods overwhelm the land so completely that the gods themselves panic and cower.

After six days and seven nights the storm ceases. Humanity has become clay. The vessel rests on Mount Nimuš. Utnapishtim sends a dove and swallow, which return, then a raven, which does not. He releases everything to the four winds and sacrifices. Belet-ili swears remembrance; Enlil is furious; Ea condemns collective punishment. Enlil then blesses Utnapishtim and his wife and removes them to the mouth of the rivers.

Utnapishtim tests Gilgamesh: if he seeks immortality, let him conquer sleep. Gilgamesh sleeps for six days and seven nights. Utnapishtim’s wife bakes a loaf for each day so the aging bread proves the passage of time. After Gilgamesh is washed and reclothed, she urges a parting gift. Utnapishtim reveals a thorny deep-water plant capable of rejuvenating an old person. Gilgamesh obtains it but, while he bathes, a serpent steals it and sheds its skin. He returns to Uruk mortal.

The flood episode thus performs two functions: it preserves an older flood myth and establishes that Utnapishtim’s immortality was a singular divine decision, not a reproducible technique.

### D. Xisuthros in Berossus

**Hellenistic learned systematization:** early third century BCE.

Xisuthros is warned by Cronus on a precisely calendrical date. He must preserve both biological life and written knowledge. The vessel lands in Armenia; birds are sent out repeatedly; Xisuthros and a select group are translated to the gods. Remaining survivors recover the buried tablets from Sippar and restore civilization.

Distinctive developments include:

- a Greek divine name, Cronus;
- an Armenian landing;
- the burial and recovery of books;
- Xisuthros’s post-translation voice;
- explicit rebuilding of Babylon and temples.

These are not preserved features of the Sumerian tablet and may reflect Berossus’s priestly concern for the antiquity and continuity of Babylonian scholarship.

### E. Noah as comparison

Genesis 6–9, in Hebrew and conventionally analyzed as an interwoven Priestly and non-Priestly narrative, shares a sequence with the Mesopotamian stories: divine decision, favored survivor, vessel, family and animals, destruction by water, grounding, birds, release, sacrifice, divine response, and a post-flood order.

Important differences include:

- one God both decrees destruction and saves Noah, whereas Enlil and Ea are opposed;
- Genesis makes human violence/corruption the stated reason, not noise and overpopulation;
- Noah is selected as righteous;
- Noah is not made immortal;
- the covenant and rainbow replace Belet-ili’s necklace and polytheistic bargaining;
- Genesis’s vessel, chronology, and bird sequence differ;
- Genesis universalizes the event through biblical genealogy.

The safest historical conclusion is not “Noah was Utnapishtim” and not necessarily direct copying from the extant Nineveh Tablet XI. Genesis participates in a much older West Asian flood tradition whose clearest earlier written witnesses include *Atrahasis*. Direct dependence, indirect scribal transmission, and common inherited tradition remain distinguishable hypotheses. Comparative scholarship specifically warns against “parallelomania.” [Oxford discussion of method](https://academic.oup.com/book/35373/chapter-abstract/301258660); [analysis of why *Atrahasis*, not only *Gilgamesh*, matters](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-mesopotamian-origin-of-the-biblical-flood-story).

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No independent cult

There is no secure archaeological, epigraphic, administrative, or liturgical evidence for:

- a temple of Ziusudra, Atrahasis, or Utnapishtim;
- a priesthood serving him;
- a festival in his name;
- regular sacrifices addressed to him;
- an oracle;
- a divine standard or cult statue;
- theophoric personal names invoking him as a god;
- a place in the major god lists as a normal cult deity.

The title *gudug* in the Sumerian flood tablet classifies Ziusudra as a priest, not as the god served by such priests.

### Ritual action inside the myth

The hero performs sacrifice after the Flood. This is narrative evidence about imagined primeval ritual, not direct proof of a historical Utnapishtim cult. The gods’ dependence upon sacrificial aroma is especially explicit in *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh*.

In *Atrahasis*, Enki advises humanity to concentrate offerings upon Namtar or Adad to end particular afflictions. Again, this belongs to the plot’s theory of divine-human reciprocity; it does not establish a cult of Atrahasis.

### Human sacrifice and cultic abuse

No source documents human sacrifice offered to Ziusudra/Utnapishtim. *Atrahasis* includes the killing of a god so that humanity can be formed from divine flesh, blood, and clay. That is divine sacrifice in a primordial creation myth, not evidence that Mesopotamian temples historically killed people in Atrahasis’s honor.

No source associates this figure with “temple prostitution.” No source documents cult violence, suppression of his worship, or forced conversion of his adherents, because no historical institutional cult is known.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before ca. 2000 BCE: formation of the primeval tradition

Flooding was a recurring fact of southern Mesopotamian life. Archaeologists found flood deposits at Ur, Kish, Shuruppak, and other sites, but these layers differ in date and extent. They do not constitute evidence for one simultaneous worldwide deluge. Attempts to identify a particular stratum as “Ziusudra’s Flood” remain modern reconstructions.

Early Dynastic *Instructions of Shuruppak* witnesses show that antediluvian wisdom attached to Shuruppak was already old. Whether the named flood hero stood in the earliest recoverable form is less secure.

### Ca. 2000–1600 BCE: Old Babylonian crystallization

This is the central documentary period:

- antediluvian king-list traditions place the Flood as the hinge between primeval and historical kingship;
- the Sumerian flood narrative names Ziusudra and grants him eternal life;
- *Atrahasis* integrates the Flood into a theological anthropology of labor and population;
- Old Babylonian *Gilgamesh* traditions connect the distant survivor with the hero’s search against death.

Ziusudra is not demonstrably “absorbed” by Atrahasis through a single datable event. The manuscripts reveal related literary traditions in Sumerian and Akkadian whose exact oral and written prehistory is unrecoverable.

### Ca. 1600–1000 BCE: international scribal transmission

Akkadian flood and *Gilgamesh* texts circulated beyond Babylonia—to Assyria, Ugarit, Hattusa, and other scribal centers. Local spelling and recension differences emerged. Utnapishtim’s name is attested in forms from Ugarit and Hattusa, demonstrating the reach of the learned tradition.

### Ca. 1200–600 BCE: Standard Babylonian canonization

The Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh* epic recast the survivor as Utnapishtim the Far-Away and placed his autobiography at the climax of Gilgamesh’s search. The Flood became evidence for the unrepeatability of immortalization.

Ashurbanipal’s seventh-century library preserved the best-known Tablet XI witness. This is the form rediscovered in the nineteenth century and consequently the form most influential in the modern world.

### Third century BCE: Berossus

Berossus translated Babylonian primeval history for a Greek-reading Seleucid environment. Ziusudra became Xisuthros; Ea became Cronus; scribal continuity at Sippar became central. This was neither the disappearance of the Babylonian tradition nor simple Greek corruption: it was priestly cultural translation.

### Late antiquity and medieval transmission

Mesopotamian cuneiform literacy disappeared during late antiquity, ending direct copying of *Gilgamesh* and *Atrahasis*. Berossus’s Xisuthros survived indirectly in Jewish and Christian chronography.

The possible Utnapishtim-derived Atnabiš entered Manichaean *Book of Giants* traditions in Iranian and Central Asian languages. This is a transformed survival, not continuous worship. No medieval Mesopotamian folk cult of Utnapishtim is documented.

### 1872 to the present: rediscovery

In 1872 George Smith identified the Flood account among British Museum tablets from Nineveh and presented it to the Society of Biblical Archaeology. The publication electrified Victorian audiences because of its parallels with Genesis. Smith’s [1873 paper](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ATransactions_of_the_Society_of_Biblical_Archaeology%2C_Vol_2_%281873%29.pdf/238) states that the account belonged to Tablet XI of a twelve-tablet series.

The *Daily Telegraph* sponsored Smith’s 1873 expedition to Nineveh to seek more fragments; he found material filling part of the tablet’s lacuna. The British Museum’s [George Smith record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/BIOG62990) documents his 1873–74 work.

Poebel’s 1914 publication of the Sumerian flood tablet established Ziusudra as a separate, earlier-language witness. Lambert and Millard’s 1969 edition reconstructed *Atrahasis* as a major independent epic, shifting scholarship away from treating every Mesopotamian flood fragment merely as a Gilgamesh or biblical parallel. George’s 2003 edition consolidated the manuscript history of *Gilgamesh*. Irving Finkel’s publication of the “Ark Tablet” renewed attention to a circular coracle version, but that vessel belongs to its own manuscript, not to every flood account.

No continuous worship is documented. Modern veneration, where individuals adopt the name or treat Utnapishtim as an immortal master, is revivalist or personal practice.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Assyriology and biblical controversy

The modern Utnapishtim was initially produced for the public through biblical comparison. Victorian discussion alternately treated the tablet as confirmation of Genesis, evidence that Genesis borrowed from Babylon, or proof of a common historical catastrophe. Modern textual scholarship separates three questions:

1. literary relationship;
2. theological transformation;
3. whether any narrative preserves cultural memory of regional flooding.

Agreement on literary kinship does not establish a global geological event or identify one excavated flood layer as the story’s cause.

### Literature and philosophy

Modern translations turned Utnapishtim into a figure of existential reflection: he possesses what Gilgamesh desires but lives at the margin of the world. Writers and critics commonly use him to discuss whether endless life is a gift, isolation, or narrative impossibility.

Direct influence on Jorge Luis Borges’s “The Immortal” is plausible at the thematic level but should not be asserted as a simple character adaptation without documentary evidence. Borges’s immortal is not Utnapishtim under another name.

### Games and popular media

- **Ys VI: The Ark of Napishtim** uses “Napishtim” in the title for a fantastical ancient mechanism; it is a modern commercial borrowing, not a Mesopotamian retelling.
- **Fate/Grand Order** uses “Ziusudra” as an identity or title in a modern Japanese fantasy cosmology. Its equations with other characters are franchise inventions.
- General fantasy, role-playing, comics, music, and online mythology sites often portray Utnapishtim as a wizard, demigod, antediluvian king, alien contactee, or secret immortal still alive. Only the first three have partial ancient analogues; extraterrestrial and present-day-survivor claims are modern inventions.

### Occult and neopagan material

A dedicated search produces scattered occult encyclopedia entries and web pages, but no historically continuous Utnapishtim cult and no major pre-twentieth-century Western magical order centered on him. He is not a standard named power in the *Greek Magical Papyri*, Hermetic corpus, Solomonic grimoires, Golden Dawn grade rituals, or classical Theosophical hierarchy.

Modern invocations or “Sumerian reconstructionist” rites addressed to Ziusudra should therefore be labeled modern composition. They may be religiously meaningful to present practitioners, but they do not reproduce an attested ancient cult.

### Pseudo-history

Recurring modern claims include:

- Ziusudra, Utnapishtim, Atrahasis, Noah, Deucalion, Manu, and every flood hero were one historical man;
- he was an Anunnaki extraterrestrial;
- cuneiform gives the vessel advanced technology;
- he ruled a global civilization;
- the myth records sea-level rise at the end of the Ice Age;
- archaeology has found his precise Flood;
- “Atrahasis” proves ancient human genetic engineering.

None is established by the named texts. “Anunnaki” in the sources designates a class of Mesopotamian gods, not extraterrestrials. The scientific-sounding genetic interpretation is a twentieth-century reading of mythic clay-and-divine-blood anthropology.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major discoveries and editions

- **George Smith, 1872–73:** decipherment and first publication of the Nineveh Flood account.
- **Arno Poebel, 1914:** publication of the Nippur Sumerian flood tablet.
- **Thorkild Jacobsen, 1939:** critical analysis of the Sumerian King List; later coined or popularized “Eridu Genesis.”
- **W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard, 1969:** critical edition of *Atrahasis*, with Miguel Civil’s Sumerian flood text.
- **Andrew George, 2003:** comprehensive critical edition of the Babylonian *Gilgamesh* epic.
- **Irving Finkel, 2014:** publication and popular exposition of the circular-ark tablet.
- Ongoing digital work by ETCSL, CDLI, ORACC-related projects, SEAL, and museum databases makes manuscripts and lexical data more accessible.

### Identity debate

The figures are traditionally equated because:

- Ziusudra and Utnapishtim have corresponding “long/found life” names;
- both are associated with Shuruppak and Ubara-Tutu;
- both survive the Flood and receive immortality;
- lexical texts explicitly equate forms of Ziusudra and Utnapishtim;
- *Gilgamesh* itself uses *atra-ḫasīs* inside Utnapishtim’s narrative.

Yet “Atrahasis = Utnapishtim” is a historical-literary equation, not proof that one fixed character biography existed behind all manuscripts. Atrahasis’s poem gives a different narrative frame and does not securely preserve every element of Utnapishtim’s fate.

### Etymological debate

Ziusudra’s name has been parsed as “life of long days,” “he who prolonged life,” and related formulations. Utnapishtim has been read “he found life,” “I found my life,” or “I shall find life.” These differences arise from uncertain verbal form, case ending, dialectal spelling, and learned bilingual wordplay. [George’s RlA entry](https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/20637/1/Utanapishti.pdf) is the central concise survey.

### Cause and function of the Flood

- In *Atrahasis*, overpopulation expressed as intolerable noise is central.
- In the Sumerian tablet, the causal passage is lost.
- In *Gilgamesh*, the reason is not explained in the same sustained way; the focus is the secret divine decision.
- In Genesis, corruption and violence are explicit.
- In Berossus, the surviving epitome gives a prediction rather than a developed moral cause.

Claims that “the Mesopotamian Flood happened because humans sinned” import Genesis. Claims that “all versions say humans were too noisy” import *Atrahasis* into broken or differently focused witnesses.

### Boat-shape debate

- Sumerian instructions are lost.
- Old Babylonian *Atrahasis* is damaged.
- The separate Ark Tablet describes a vast circular coracle.
- *Gilgamesh* XI describes equal length and breadth, conventionally reconstructed as square or cube-like.
- Genesis describes an elongated rectangular vessel.
- Berossus gives enormous dimensions through Greek transmission.

No single “original ark blueprint” can be recovered by combining them.

### Geological historicization

Leonard Woolley interpreted the deep flood layer at Ur dramatically; later comparisons showed that deposits at Ur, Kish, and Shuruppak are of different dates and local extent. Max Mallowan explored whether a major early-third-millennium regional flood lay behind tradition, but the evidence does not identify one universal event. Flood deposits establish floods, not Ziusudra.

### Noah dependence

Most historical-critical scholars accept some relationship between Genesis and older Mesopotamian flood tradition. Debate concerns the route:

- direct use of *Gilgamesh*;
- closer literary dependence upon an *Atrahasis*-type tradition;
- indirect oral or scribal transmission;
- a shared long-lived West Asian repertoire;
- combinations of these.

The presence of both Priestly and non-Priestly strata in Genesis complicates claims of one author copying one tablet.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- Ziusudra’s reign of 36,000 years as literal chronology.
- Every flood myth descending from one eyewitness.
- A global flood proven by Mesopotamian silt.
- Utnapishtim as an extraterrestrial or technologically advanced geneticist.
- Atlantis, Manu, Deucalion, and Noah as philologically identical names.
- A historical cult of Utnapishtim suppressed by Judaism, Christianity, or Islam.
- A fixed ancient portrait recognizable in arbitrary seal imagery.

### Evidentiary conclusions

The reconstructable figure is not one god with a stable biography. It is a changing Mesopotamian literary office occupied by closely related named heroes:

- Ziusudra embodies the Sumerian king-priest saved and granted eternal life.
- Atrahasis embodies the wise human client through whom Enki defeats total annihilation and establishes a revised human order.
- Utnapishtim embodies exceptional, unrepeatable immortality in Gilgamesh’s meditation on death.
- Xisuthros embodies Hellenistic Babylonian continuity, writing, and translation to the gods.
- Atnabiš represents late antique migration into Manichaean giant lore.
- “The Mesopotamian Noah” is a modern comparative label.

The continuous thread is the flood survivor; the differences are the historical record.
