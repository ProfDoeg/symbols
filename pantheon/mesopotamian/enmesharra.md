# Enmesharra: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male Mesopotamian god; more specifically a primordial, defeated, and usually dead or underworld-confined deity in Sumerian and Akkadian scholarly theology. He is not securely classified as a demon, monster, or collective, although late texts associate him with defeated divine enemies and with a group of seven sons.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Ur III-period Sumerian administrative and incantation evidence, approximately the twenty-first century BCE. The ePSD2 corpus currently registers three Ur III occurrences. Ur III offering lists associate Enmešarra with the sacred mound or *Duku*, but the early records do not yet narrate his defeat or establish him as a universal ruler. The familiar story of his seizure of “Enlilship” first survives in Old Babylonian copies of the Sumerian composition *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara*.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** Ur III or Old Babylonian textual evidence.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** first-millennium narrative or ritual material that develops earlier theological themes.
- **Regional variant:** a tradition confined to, or characteristically represented by, a particular city or scribal center.
- **Learned systematization:** god lists, commentaries, ritual exegesis, and antiquarian theology.
- **Syncretistic identification:** explicit equation with another deity rather than a merely similar function.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference advanced by modern Assyriologists rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **Modern invention:** a modern assertion not recoverable from ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Name and writing

The normal scholarly form is **Enmešarra** or **Enmešara**. Attested cuneiform spellings include:

- 𒀭𒂗𒈨𒊹𒊏, ^dEN.ME.ŠAR₂.RA or ^den-me-šar₂-ra.
- ^den-me-en-šar₂-ra.
- en-me-šar₂-ra, without the divine determinative.
- ^den-me-ša-ra.
- Shortened ^den-me-šar₂.

The ePSD2 lexical record presently collects thirteen occurrences distributed across Ur III, Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, Hellenistic, and undated material. Absence of the divine determinative in a few witnesses is orthographic evidence, not proof that the writer regarded Enmesharra as a human or demon.

The usual Sumerian analysis is **en** “lord” + **me** “divine powers, ordinances, or constitutive essences” + **šár/šara** “totality”: “Lord of all the *me*” or “Lord of all powers.” This is plausible and traditional, but its theological implications should not be read automatically into every occurrence. No surviving early text states that Enmesharra possessed a numbered set of “one hundred *me*.” That formulation in popular summaries is a modern expansion of the name.

### Titles and descriptions

- **“Lord of the Netherworld”**: found in an Akkadian temple-foundation incantation. **[Later canonical/ritual attestation]** It places Enmesharra among several gods who can bear underworld rulership titles; it does not prove a large independent cult.
- **“The Splendour”**, Akkadian *zīmū* or *zīmû*: the distinctive designation used in *Enmešarra’s Defeat*. **[Late Babylonian elaboration]**
- **Captured or bound god**: *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara* calls him a captive after his appropriation of Enlilship. Late narratives place him among the bound or seized gods. **[Early literary attestation with later elaboration]**
- **Ghost**, *eṭemmu/GIDIM*: Neo-Assyrian cultic exegesis treats him as dead and speaks of his ghost. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Primeval/ancestral deity**: his position beside the ancestors of Enlil, the *Duku*, and “conquered Enlils” makes this an accurate functional description, although “primeval god” is principally a modern analytical category.

### Equations and proposed equivalents

- **Qingu = Enmesharra:** an explicit first-millennium scholarly equation survives in a god-list/commentary fragment. **[Syncretistic identification/learned systematization]** It reflects the tendency to equate defeated predecessors of the reigning divine order; it does not make Enmesharra an original character in *Enūma eliš*.
- **Nergal:** nineteenth- and early twentieth-century descriptions of Enmesharra as an “Akkadian Pluto,” sometimes effectively identifying him with Nergal, are obsolete. Both can be underworld gods, but the texts distinguish them and make Nergal Enmesharra’s jailer or escort.
- **Enlil:** a late commentary equates the “Chariot of Enmesharra” with Enlil. This is an exegetical association, not a general equation of the two gods.
- **Dagan:** in a fragment where Enmesharra is guarded “by Dagan’s authority,” Dagan may function as a name or equivalent of Enlil. Lambert regarded both readings as possible. **[Disputed learned systematization]**
- **Hurrian Namšara/Napšara:** Alfonso Archi has proposed that the Hurrian primeval divine name Namšara is a transformed borrowing from Enmešarra. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction; possible, not demonstrated]**
- **Anzû:** Frans Wiggermann interprets Assyrian ritual exegesis and seal imagery as assimilating Enmesharra’s revolt to Anzû’s theft of divine authority. The texts associate them but do not simply state “Enmesharra is Anzû.” **[Later analogy and modern reconstruction]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Ur III evidence, circa 2112–2004 BCE

The earliest presently indexed evidence consists of three Ur III occurrences. At least one is an incantation, CBS 8231/PBS 1/2 107, while offering records place Enmesharra next to the *Duku*. **[Securely attested early material]**

What this establishes:

- Enmesharra was already recognized as divine in the late third millennium BCE.
- He belonged to a ritual or theological environment associated with Enlil’s primeval kin and the sacred mound.
- He could receive offerings.

What it does **not** establish:

- An independent temple or civic cult.
- The complete “defeated ruler” narrative.
- Possession of all cosmic ordinances merely because his name contains *me*.
- Seven sons, unless a particular list explicitly supplies them.

### *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara*

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Composition/copies:** Known through Old Babylonian school-tablet witnesses, approximately the early second millennium BCE; an earlier oral or written form is possible but unproved.

Enlil, disguised as a raven, asks the human Nam-zid-tara how he recognized him. Nam-zid-tara answers:

> “When your uncle Enmešarra was a captive, after taking for himself the rank of Enlil, he said: ‘Now I shall know the fates, like a lord.’”

This compact passage supplies the oldest surviving narrative core:

- Enmesharra is Enlil’s paternal uncle.
- He appropriated *nam-en-líl*, “Enlilship,” the office or authority of Enlil.
- His aim involved knowledge or declaration of destinies.
- He was subsequently captured.

The text does not say who captured him, describe a battle, or name seven sons. Lambert and Wiggermann infer an unlawful seizure comparable to Anzû’s theft of the Tablet of Destinies. That comparison is illuminating but remains **modern reconstruction**.

### God lists and theological genealogies

#### Old Babylonian forerunners

TCL 15, 10 and related Nippur material place Enmesharra within the succession of older divine generations associated with Enlil. He is adjacent to, rather than necessarily one of, the standard paired Enkis and Ninkis. **[Learned systematization based on early-second-millennium material]**

#### *An = Anum*

**Language:** Sumerian-Akkadian scholarly list.  
**Formation:** Based on Old Babylonian precursors; its standard form belongs to later second-millennium and first-millennium scribal transmission.

Tablet I places Enmešarra and Ninmešarra before their children. Some recensions insert Lugaldukuga between the couple and the seven children. This ordering created complex genealogical interpretations: Lugaldukuga can be Enlil’s father or grandfather, while *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara* calls Enmesharra Enlil’s uncle. The sources should not be forced into one family tree.

### *The Death of Gilgamesh*

**Language:** Sumerian.  
**Surviving witnesses:** chiefly Old Babylonian.

Enmesharra appears among ancient or underworld divine beings associated with Enlil’s ancestry. His presence supports the conclusion that he was imagined as a deceased god residing below. **[Secure early literary evidence]**

No equivalent role belongs to Enmesharra in the standard Akkadian *Epic of Gilgamesh* edited by Andrew George. References in discussions of Gilgamesh defeating “seven sons” concern variant or comparative material and should not be imported into the Standard Babylonian epic without a manuscript citation.

### Temple-building incantations

An Akkadian foundation incantation calls Enmesharra “lord of the Netherworld.” Such incantations summon cosmic and primordial beings to authorize or purify temple construction. **[Ritually attested, date dependent on manuscript; first-millennium copies preserve older material]**

### Assyrian cultic commentary on Enmesharra’s death

A cultic commentary from Aššur explains ritual acts through a myth in which Ninurta conveys Enmesharra’s corpse in a chariot drawn by the ghost of Anzû. Other passages describe Enmesharra as burned and existing as a ghost. **[Neo-Assyrian learned systematization]**

The commentary is important because it joins three themes:

1. Enmesharra’s challenge to divine sovereignty.
2. His defeat and death.
3. Ritual treatment of defeated primeval gods.

It is not a transparent transcript of an early popular myth. Its exegesis is characteristically Assyrian and presupposes scribal comparison with the Anzû cycle.

### *Enmešarra’s Defeat*

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Manuscript:** One badly damaged Late Babylonian tablet; its script and orthography permit a date in the Late Babylonian or Persian period, while the composition’s precise date is uncertain.  
**Modern edition:** W. G. Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 281–298.

The preserved narrative begins after an earlier revolt or confrontation:

1. Enmesharra and seven sons are prisoners.
2. Marduk orders Nergal to tell them that they must die.
3. Nergal enters the prison and announces the judgment.
4. Enmesharra laments that Bēl’s judgments mean death for both himself and his “fledglings.”
5. After a lacuna, Nergal escorts Enmesharra and his sons before Marduk.
6. Marduk accuses Enmesharra of hostile intent; the damaged line may concern begetting or swallowing something belonging to Marduk.
7. Marduk beheads the seven sons and draws or installs their representations on a wall.
8. Enmesharra is bound.
9. His *zīmū*, “splendour” or luminous appearance, is transferred to Šamaš.
10. The text proceeds into a redistribution of divine powers and cities: Nabû receives power associated with Ninurta, Nergal with Erra, and Marduk assumes Enlil’s authority.
11. The conclusion exalts the Babylonian divine order.

The title is modern; the ancient tablet’s original title is not preserved. The work reuses structures familiar from *Enūma eliš*: rebellion, capture, execution, redistribution of cosmic offices, and Marduk’s supremacy. **[Late canonical elaboration]**

Whether Enmesharra himself is killed is uncertain. The preserved tablet clearly kills his sons and binds him, whereas other texts say his life was extinguished or his corpse transported. Another fragment has him surrender his sons to save himself. These are genuine conflicting versions.

### *The Defeat of Enutila, Enmešarra, and Qingu*

**Language:** Akkadian.  
**Evidence:** Two Late Babylonian fragments, from Borsippa and Sippar.  
**Title:** Modern, assigned by Lambert.

The surviving sequence combines several defeated figures:

- Ninurta kills Enutila and takes his rites.
- A damaged subject “extinguishes the life” of Enmesharra.
- Enmesharra’s seven sons are released from bonds at the Market Gate, then apparently refurbished or reinstalled and stationed at Ganṣir.
- Ištar-of-Babylon guards or holds them.
- Qingu’s offspring or faction is destroyed.
- Dumuzi is bound.
- Ninzaginna goes before Ea.

Because the surviving text crowds together figures from distinct myths, Lambert considered it possibly a learned compilation rather than a coherent inherited narrative. **[Late learned synthesis]**

### Bird Call Text

A fragmentary learned composition relates birds to gods and states that the cock is Enmesharra’s bird. It also alludes to Tutu/Marduk’s conflict with him. **[First-millennium learned systematization]**

The cock association is textually real but does not establish an ancient iconographic convention in which every cock represents Enmesharra.

### Hemerologies and ritual calendars

The “Offering Bread Hemerology” directs an offering to Lugaldukuga, Enki, and Enmesharra facing west on the twenty-ninth of Tašrītu. Earlier scholarship sometimes construed the final writing as the god Amurru; Enrique Jiménez argues convincingly that it denotes the direction west. In Mesopotamian symbolism the west was associated with the dead. **[First-millennium ritual attestation; disputed reading now clarified]**

A late Babylonian cult calendar says Gula instituted lamentation for defeated Enmesharra. **[Regional late cultic elaboration]**

### Kettledrum-making rituals

Neo-Assyrian and Hellenistic ritual manuscripts involved in manufacturing a sacred kettledrum invoke twelve gods: Anu, Enlil, Ea; the underworld twins Lugalgirra and Meslamtaea; and the seven sons of Enmesharra. The sons may be represented by seven heaps of flour. They receive bread, confection, beer, wine, a sheep, and colored wool.

The killing of the bull whose hide became the drum is an animal sacrifice. Nothing in these texts constitutes human sacrifice.

### Texts named in the brief that do not attest Enmesharra directly

Absence is important:

- **Enūma eliš:** Enmesharra is not a narrative character. Qingu’s later equation with him is secondary scholarly interpretation.
- **Atra-ḫasīs:** no secure Enmesharra episode.
- **Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh*:** no direct Enmesharra narrative.
- **Inanna’s Descent:** no direct appearance.
- **Enki and Ninmaḫ:** no direct appearance.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP:** no securely identified royal campaign, temple construction, or major royal cult dedicated to Enmesharra. His best Assyrian evidence is learned ritual and commentary, not royal historiography.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

Enmesharra’s securely reconstructable domains are:

- **Primeval divine authority:** his seizure of Enlilship links him to the power to determine destinies. **[Old Babylonian literary evidence]**
- **The underworld and divine dead:** explicit in foundation incantations, funerary offerings, and commentaries. **[Ritually attested]**
- **Defeated earlier sovereignty:** a recurrent theological role rather than a natural domain. **[Early literary core; late elaboration]**
- **Light or splendour:** *Enmešarra’s Defeat* gives him transferable *zīmū*. **[Late narrative attestation]**
- **Astral association:** astronomical texts identify him with a star or constellation called the “Chariot of Enmesharra”; one modern catalogue places this in Perseus. **[Late learned astronomy]**

“God of order,” “keeper of the cosmic law-book,” and “possessor of the Tablet of Destinies” are overstatements. He sought or held the authority denoted by Enlilship, but no surviving text gives him a literal “book of cosmic law.”

### Emblems and animals

- **Cock:** assigned to him in the Bird Call Text. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Suššuru bird:** lexical or ritual sources associate this bird, sometimes loosely translated “pigeon,” with Enmesharra. The zoological identification is uncertain.
- **Chariot:** central to Assyrian exegesis of his corpse’s conveyance and to astronomical nomenclature.
- **Splendour/rays:** a narrative attribute removed from him and given to Šamaš.

No color, weekday, number, plant, or metal is securely established as his exclusive emblem. Seven belongs primarily to his sons.

### Proposed depictions

No surviving ancient image bears an inscription securely identifying Enmesharra.

Lambert proposed that certain cylinder-seal gods with rays rising from their shoulders might represent Enmesharra rather than Šamaš. He also suggested that the Tell Asmar/ancient Ešnunna stone relief dated approximately 2100–1900 BCE—showing a god stabbing a one-eyed or “Cyclops” adversary with a broad knife—might depict Enmesharra’s defeat.

This is **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not established identification. Alternative proposals identify the scene as Marduk versus Tiamat or Ninurta versus Asag. Anthony Green cautioned that it may illustrate an otherwise lost myth. Omen evidence, moreover, links a one-eyed being called *igitelû* more explicitly with Nergal. Wiggermann’s proposal that seal “bird-men” represent Enmesharra rests on their rebellious position and on analogy with Anzû; no text says Enmesharra had avian anatomy.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Family traditions

#### Ninmešarra

*An = Anum* pairs Enmešarra with **Ninmešarra**, “Lady of all the *me*.” This is normally treated as a spouse or female counterpart. Ninmešarra is also an epithet of Inanna in other contexts, so not every occurrence of the title denotes Enmesharra’s wife. Lambert tentatively connected Enmesharra’s luminous aspect with this Inanna/Venus title. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

#### Relationship to Enlil

*Enlil and Nam-zid-tara* calls Enmesharra Enlil’s **uncle**, literally the brother of his father. It does not identify that father. Later genealogical lists variously make Anu or Lugaldukuga Enlil’s father or place Lugaldukuga one generation farther back. Thus Enmesharra cannot be fitted into a single uncontested pedigree.

#### Seven sons

The most stable tradition gives seven sons, although lists vary in spelling and occasionally in membership. A reconstructed *An = Anum* group includes forms commonly normalized as:

1. Zisummu.
2. Ibgirḫuš or Adgirḫaš.
3. Šenbarimime/Šegbarimime.
4. Urbadda or Urbaddumu.
5. Urbadḫumḫum/Urbadagubgubu.
6. Gubbararae/Gubagarae.
7. Abarralaḫ or Abara-DU.DU.

The readings and meanings of several names remain uncertain. Ritual texts identify the group with, or place it beside, the **Sebettu**, “the Seven.” This is not consistent enough to erase the distinction between every group of Seven in Mesopotamian religion.

#### Additional or variant children

- **Šuzianna:** explicitly “daughter of Enmesharra whom Enlil married” in one tradition and included in some lists of his children. **[Regional/learned variant]**
- **Papsukkal:** called “offspring of Enmesharra” in a prayer, while normally connected with Anu. This may imply an otherwise poorly preserved genealogy linking Enmesharra to Anu’s line. **[Late learned variant]**
- **Ennugi:** sometimes assigned to Enlil and sometimes to Enmesharra in god-list traditions. **[Variant genealogy]**
- Lists can yield seven, eight, or more descendants depending on whether paired, secondary, or interpolated figures are counted.

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Ninmešarra — paired as spouse or female counterpart.** *An = Anum*, Tablet I; standard list compiled from second-millennium predecessors. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Enlil — Enmesharra, called Enlil’s uncle, takes Enlilship and is later captive.** *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara*, Old Babylonian Sumerian witnesses. **[Secure early literary attestation]**
- **Enlil — Enmesharra hands over or loses sovereign insignia associated with Enlil’s order.** Akkadian incantation and later theological texts. **[Later canonical elaboration; voluntariness disputed]**
- **Lugaldukuga — receives offerings beside Enmesharra and is placed near him in genealogical lists.** Ur III offering tradition; *An = Anum*. **[Early ritual association/learned genealogy]**
- **Anu — receives royal insignia in a mythic incantation involving Enmesharra.** First-millennium ritual witness preserving older theology. **[Later attestation]**
- **Enki/Ea — receives or shares transferred authority with Anu and Enlil; receives offerings beside Enmesharra.** Incantation and hemerology. **[Ritual and learned association]**
- **Ninurta — transports Enmesharra’s corpse after his defeat.** Cultic commentary from Aššur, first millennium BCE. **[Neo-Assyrian learned systematization]**
- **Ninurta — may be the deity who defeated or “laid to rest” Enmesharra at Šuruppak.** Fragmentary Nippur source; restoration between Enlil and Ninurta is uncertain. **[Disputed fragment]**
- **Anzû — his ghost draws the chariot bearing Enmesharra’s corpse.** Assyrian cultic commentary. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Dagan — authorizes or presides over Enmesharra’s perpetual confinement.** Fragmentary first-millennium text; Dagan may stand for Enlil. **[Disputed learned identification]**
- **Marduk — orders Enmesharra’s death, judges him, executes his sons, binds him, and redistributes his power.** *Enmešarra’s Defeat*, Late Babylonian/Persian-period manuscript. **[Late canonical elaboration]**
- **Nergal — serves as jailer, announces Marduk’s sentence, and escorts Enmesharra and his sons to judgment.** *Enmešarra’s Defeat*. **[Late canonical elaboration]**
- **Šamaš — receives Enmesharra’s confiscated splendour.** *Enmešarra’s Defeat*. **[Late canonical elaboration]**
- **Nabû — receives powers in the redistribution following Enmesharra’s defeat.** *Enmešarra’s Defeat*. **[Late canonical elaboration]**
- **Erra — powers associated with Erra are assigned to Nergal in the post-defeat settlement.** *Enmešarra’s Defeat*. This is indirect but occurs in the same redistribution. **[Late canonical elaboration]**
- **Girra — called “the light before Enmesharra.”** *Marduk’s Address B* commentary, copied in the fifth or fourth century BCE. **[Achaemenid learned systematization]**
- **Gula — establishes mourning for defeated Enmesharra.** Late Babylonian cult calendar. **[Regional late elaboration]**
- **Šuzianna — identified as Enmesharra’s daughter and wife of Enlil.** God-list/theological tradition. **[Variant genealogy]**
- **Papsukkal — called Enmesharra’s offspring.** Late prayer. **[Variant genealogy]**
- **Ennugi — assigned Enmesharra as father in one genealogical tradition.** God lists. **[Variant genealogy]**
- **Enutila — Enmesharra’s defeat is juxtaposed with Ninurta’s killing of Enutila.** *Defeat of Enutila, Enmesharra, and Qingu*, Late Babylonian fragments. No direct conversation or combat between Enutila and Enmesharra survives. **[Late learned compilation]**
- **Qingu — defeated in the same narrative complex and explicitly equated with Enmesharra elsewhere.** Late Babylonian fragments and commentary/god list. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Ištar-of-Babylon — guards or holds Enmesharra’s released sons at Ganṣir.** *Defeat of Enutila, Enmesharra, and Qingu*. **[Regional late elaboration]**
- **Dumuzi — is bound during the same learned sequence.** Same text; no direct personal exchange with Enmesharra survives. **[Late compilation]**
- **Ninzaginna — proceeds before Ea within the same compilation.** No direct exchange with Enmesharra survives. **[Late compilation]**
- **Tiamat and Apsû — occur in the damaged opening context of the same composite text.** No preserved direct interaction with Enmesharra. **[Late compilation]**

There is no securely documented interaction between Enmesharra and Ereškigal, despite both belonging to underworld theology.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The usurpation of Enlilship

**Earliest source:** *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara*, Old Babylonian witnesses.

This is the oldest reconstructable story. Enmesharra seized the office of Enlil so that he could know or determine destinies. He was captured. Nothing more is narrated in the surviving composition.

Its likely theological function was to distinguish legitimate cosmic authority from an older claimant’s appropriation. The comparison with Anzû is strong: both obtain Enlil’s power over destinies and are defeated. Yet the surviving Old Babylonian Anzû traditions do not name Enmesharra, so direct dependence is unproved.

### The death and conveyance of Enmesharra

**Source:** Neo-Assyrian cultic commentary from Aššur.

Enmesharra is dead; Ninurta conveys his body in a chariot pulled by Anzû’s ghost. This version transforms the older seizure story into ritual mythology. It may explain a procession, chariot, burning, or funerary rite. The source’s commentarial form means the narrative was already being reconstructed through liturgical symbolism.

A Nippur fragment instead says Enmesharra was “laid to rest” following a confrontation at Šuruppak, but the victor’s name is damaged or disputed between Enlil and Ninurta.

### Marduk’s judgment and the execution of the seven sons

**Source:** *Enmešarra’s Defeat*, one Late Babylonian manuscript.

In the preserved account Marduk has already won. Enmesharra and his sons are imprisoned, Nergal announces their sentence, and Marduk beheads the sons. Enmesharra loses his radiance to Šamaš and is bound. The cosmos is reorganized around Marduk, Nabû, and Nergal.

This is not the earliest Enmesharra myth. It is a Babylonian recasting of the defeated-old-ruler motif in the ideological vocabulary of *Enūma eliš*. The underlying uncle-versus-nephew succession has become Marduk’s judgment over a primordial rebel.

### The composite defeat of Enutila, Enmesharra, and Qingu

**Sources:** two Late Babylonian fragments from Borsippa and Sippar.

Here Enmesharra’s life is extinguished, but his sons are released, refurbished, and placed under Ištar-of-Babylon’s control. This conflicts with *Enmešarra’s Defeat*, where Marduk beheads them. The discrepancy may reflect different ritual outcomes: mythic enemies can be killed in narrative but materially represented and ritually reinstalled as controlled powers.

The accumulation of Enutila, Qingu, Dumuzi, and others points toward antiquarian synthesis. It is unsafe to reconstruct a single ancient epic joining all of them.

### The surrendered sons variant

A fragment says Enmesharra “to save his own life, handed over his sons.” **[Later regional or learned variant]** This reverses the pathos of *Enmešarra’s Defeat*, in which he laments their doom. It may be independent or may preserve a different interpretation of their delivery to Marduk.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cultic scale

There is no evidence that Enmesharra headed a major city pantheon comparable to Enlil at Nippur, Enki at Eridu, Nanna at Ur, or Marduk at Babylon. No securely identified temple exclusively dedicated to him is known. His cultic footprint is predominantly:

- Offerings within larger divine lists.
- Incantations.
- Funerary or west-facing rites.
- Ritual representation of his sons.
- Learned explanations of cult acts.

This favors Saggs’s and Lambert’s view that his mature profile was substantially a product of theological speculation around Enlil’s ancestry. It does not prove that he was invented all at once or lacked all popular observance; Ur III offerings show real ritual recognition.

### Nippur and the *Duku*

Ur III texts list Enmesharra with the *Duku*, the sacred mound associated with primeval gods and Enlil’s ancestry. Nippur remained the principal intellectual setting for his genealogy. The name “Enmešara temple of Ninurta” occurs in modern descriptions of a Nippur sanctuary; the phrasing does not necessarily mean a temple owned by Enmesharra.

### Tašrītu offerings

On the twenty-ninth of Tašrītu an officiant offered to Lugaldukuga, Enki, and Enmesharra while facing west. This is consistent with funerary *kispu* and the seasonal opening of the netherworld gate. It is direct evidence of ritual remembrance of Enmesharra as one of the divine dead.

### Kettledrum ritual

The seven sons received offerings and were materially represented by flour heaps during the preparation of a sacred kettledrum. The ritual includes:

- Bricks or stations for divine recipients.
- Bread and confection.
- Beer and wine libations.
- Sheep offerings.
- Colored wool.
- A bull whose hide was used for the drum.
- Mouth-washing and whispered incantations.

These rites controlled and integrated dangerous or defeated powers into temple liturgy. They do not demonstrate a congregational cult of Enmesharra.

### Death, burning, and funerary offerings

References to burning Enmesharra and to his ghost probably belong to ritual dramatization or commentary on the treatment of primeval defeated gods. Whether an image was literally burned in a regularly performed rite is difficult to establish from the fragmentary sources.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and cult violence

The dedicated searches found:

- **No evidence of human sacrifice to Enmesharra.**
- **No evidence of temple prostitution associated with him.**
- **No priestly office devoted specifically to him.**
- **No documented cult violence committed by worshippers in his name.**

His sons’ execution is divine violence within myth. The slaughter of a bull in the kettledrum rite is animal sacrifice. Neither should be converted into evidence for human ritual killing.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Ur III period, twenty-first century BCE

Enmesharra is a minor divine recipient in offerings and incantation. His association with the *Duku* places him near Enlil’s primeval family. **[Secure early evidence]**

### Old Babylonian period, approximately 2000–1600 BCE

The surviving mythic core becomes explicit in *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara*: he is Enlil’s uncle, usurper of Enlilship, and captive. God-list forerunners place him in the genealogy of older deities. *The Death of Gilgamesh* treats him as one of the divine dead.

### Middle and later second millennium BCE

God-list transmission regularizes Enmešarra, Ninmešarra, and their children. His personality remains chiefly genealogical and underworld-oriented rather than civic or political.

### Neo-Assyrian period, 911–609 BCE

Assyrian scholars expand him through cultic commentary:

- He is a dead god or ghost.
- His body is carried by Ninurta.
- Anzû’s ghost is subordinated to the procession.
- His sons become ritually significant among groups of Seven.
- Cosmic and cultic phenomena are read as traces of his defeat.

This does not amount to royal promotion. He is useful precisely as the defeated predecessor whose fate validates the reigning divine order.

### Neo-Babylonian, Achaemenid, and Late Babylonian periods

Babylonian scholars recast him as an adversary of Marduk. *Enmešarra’s Defeat* and the composite defeat text subordinate him to Babylon’s theology. His splendour and powers are redistributed, while his sons are killed in one text and ritually reinstalled in another.

The Achaemenid-period copy of the commentary on *Marduk’s Address* retains him in astral and esoteric scholarship: Girra is his light; a celestial chariot bears his name.

### Seleucid and Parthian Babylonia

Hellenistic ritual manuscripts continue to invoke his sons during kettledrum construction. This is evidence for continuity of temple scholarship and liturgy, not necessarily a surviving autonomous Enmesharra cult.

### After the cuneiform tradition

No medieval chain of worship, folklore, magical practice, or continuous cult of Enmesharra is documented. His name disappeared with the specialist cuneiform tradition and re-entered modern awareness through Assyriological publication.

There is consequently no documented episode of targeted Christian or Islamic suppression of his cult. Mesopotamian temples declined or were abandoned through broad late-antique political, economic, and religious changes, but no source records an anti-Enmesharra conversion campaign.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

Enmesharra has had a much smaller modern reception than Inanna, Enki, Marduk, Lilith, or Pazuzu.

### Assyriological recovery

His modern identity was reconstructed from published tablets, especially:

- Old Babylonian literary texts.
- *An = Anum* and its forerunners.
- Assyrian ritual commentaries.
- Late Babylonian myth fragments.
- Lambert’s 2013 critical editions.

### Popular mythology

Modern websites commonly call him:

- “God of order.”
- “Keeper of the *me*.”
- Possessor of a cosmic law-book.
- A Sumerian equivalent of Pluto.
- Original ruler of exactly “one hundred divine powers.”

Only “lord of all the *me*” follows directly from the conventional name analysis. The law-book, fixed number of one hundred, and direct Pluto equation are **modern inventions or obsolete harmonizations**.

### Neopagan and occult use

Contemporary Mesopotamian reconstructionist communities exist and openly describe their practice as reconstructed religion. Search results reveal occasional modern devotional or occult discussion of Enmesharra, but no evidence establishes:

- A sizeable organized Enmesharra cult.
- An unbroken initiation lineage.
- A Renaissance, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or traditional grimoire current devoted to him.
- A standard chaos-magical system centered on him.

Any present-day rite constructed for Enmesharra is therefore a **modern reconstruction or invention**, even when responsibly based on translated cuneiform ritual. That classification concerns historical continuity, not the practitioner’s religious validity.

### Political appropriation

No well-documented nationalist, extremist, or state-political movement has made Enmesharra a significant symbol. Claims of ancient secret orders or continuous blood cults attached to him are unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Origin: old cult god or scribal construction?

H. W. F. Saggs proposed that Enmesharra arose from theological speculation rather than surviving as an inactive god of formerly widespread popular religion. Lambert similarly regarded him as generated within the “theogony of Enlil.”

Evidence supporting that view:

- No major temple or civic cult.
- Heavy concentration in god lists, genealogies, commentaries, and ritual exegesis.
- His name’s schematic “Lord of all *me*” form.
- Late narratives built from succession-theology motifs.

Evidence requiring qualification:

- Ur III offerings and incantations show that he was ritually recognized before the fullest known lists.
- A sharp opposition between “real worship” and “scribal invention” is misleading in Mesopotamia; scholarly theology itself operated inside temple religion.

The most economical conclusion is that Enmesharra was an early but minor divine figure whose profile was enormously expanded and systematized by temple scholars.

### Who defeated him?

- **Enlil:** implied by Enmesharra’s seizure of Enlilship and captivity in *Enlil and Nam-zid-tara*.
- **Ninurta:** transports his body in the Assyrian commentary and may be the victor in a damaged Nippur fragment.
- **Marduk:** judges and binds him in the late Babylonian myth.
- **Unspecified agent:** extinguishes his life in the composite defeat text.

These are historical transformations, not missing pieces of one canonical episode.

### Did he die?

- Corpse and ghost traditions say yes.
- “His life was extinguished” says yes.
- *Enmešarra’s Defeat* preserves the execution of his sons but only binding of Enmesharra.
- The surrender variant says he saved himself by handing over his sons.

No single answer is textually universal.

### Seven sons and the Sebettu

The “seven sons of Enmesharra” are well attested, but “Sebettu” could describe several heptads with different origins and functions. Their identification with Enmesharra’s sons is strong in Assyrian ritual material but should not be imposed on every occurrence of seven gods, seven demons, or the Pleiades.

### Iconography

Lambert’s radiant figures and Tell Asmar relief, and Wiggermann’s bird-man seals, remain ingenious hypotheses. The lack of inscriptions makes all visual identifications uncertain. A museum label describing a god killing a Cyclops is a visual description, not identification of that being as Enmesharra.

### Qingu

The equation Qingu = Enmesharra is genuinely ancient but late and scholastic. It cannot be used to insert Enmesharra retroactively into the composition of *Enūma eliš*. It shows how Babylonian scholars grouped defeated holders of illegitimate cosmic authority.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is badly fragmented:

- The earliest administrative attestations give little context.
- The oldest narrative is only twenty-seven lines long.
- *Enmešarra’s Defeat* survives in one damaged manuscript.
- The composite defeat story survives in two fragments.
- Assyrian commentaries paraphrase myths that are otherwise lost.
- No depiction is securely inscribed.

Consequently, much of the apparently coherent modern “biography” of Enmesharra is a reconstruction assembled from incompatible sources separated by more than a millennium.
