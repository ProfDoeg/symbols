# Adapa: Research Dossier

**Class in the sources:** Adapa is primarily a **mortal human sage, ritual expert, fisherman, and servant or priest of Ea/Enki at Eridu**. He is not securely presented as an independently worshipped god. Old Babylonian ritual texts let an exorcist assume his identity; first-millennium learned traditions assimilate him to the primeval *apkallū*, beings occupying an intermediate or semidivine category. The equation of Adapa specifically with Uanna/Oannes, the first fish-sage, is late and textually uneven.

**Earliest secure attestation:** an **Old Babylonian Sumerian incantation from Nippur, approximately 1800–1600 BCE**, in which the ritual speaker declares, “I am Adapa, sage of Eridu.” The earliest surviving extended Adapa narrative is represented by approximately contemporary Old Babylonian Sumerian tablets from Me-Turan, modern Tell Haddad. The best-preserved Akkadian narrative witness, EA 356 from Akhetaten/el-Amarna, dates to the fourteenth century BCE.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early attested]**, **[Later canonical]**, **[Regional/textual variant]**, **[Learned systematization]**, **[Syncretistic identification]**, **[Late accretion]**, **[Folklore]**, **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, and **[Modern invention]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Adapa belongs to the learned mythology of Eridu, the southern Mesopotamian cult center of Enki, called Ea in Akkadian. In the surviving narratives he prepares Eridu’s ritual provisions, pilots a boat, and fishes for Ea’s household. Ea grants him extraordinary intelligence and effective speech but not immortality. When the South Wind overturns his boat, Adapa curses it and breaks its “wing.” Summoned before the sky god An/Anu, he obeys Ea’s warning to refuse heavenly food and water. In the Amarna recension these turn out to be the food and water of life; Adapa therefore loses—or is protected from—a possible transformation into an immortal.

This familiar synopsis is not the whole tradition. The Old Babylonian Me-Turan composition embeds the episode in a long post-flood cosmological and medical narrative and appears chiefly concerned with restoring the South Wind and removing illness. First-millennium Assyrian fragments magnify Adapa’s wisdom and sometimes appear to give him a more favorable fate. Ritual specialists identified themselves with him, kings compared their learning to his, and late scholarly lists drew him into the genealogy of the seven antediluvian sages.

### Name

- **Adapa**, normally transliterated *Adapa*; syllabic spellings include forms equivalent to **A-da-pa**.
- **Adapu** is a grammatical or dialectal form found especially in royal descriptions such as “the sage Adapu.”
- An Akkadian lexical item **adapu**, “wise,” is attested in a late lexical text. The direction of derivation is disputed: it may be an old adjective underlying the personal name, or, as the major dictionaries allow, an adjective created secondarily from the famous sage’s name. **[Disputed etymology]**
- A lexical equation connects *adapu* with Sumerian **ù-tu-a-ab-ba**, “born in the sea.” This is probably a scholarly or folk etymology reflecting the later fish-sage tradition, not proof of the name’s original meaning. **[Learned systematization]**
- Another lexical equation, **a-da-ab = amīlu**, “human being/man,” encouraged older comparisons with Adam. It does not establish that *Adapa* and Hebrew *ʾādām* are cognate. The proposed *m/p* interchange, resemblance to *ʾădāmâ* “ground,” and Sumerian “recovered from the water” analysis are speculative. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction, disputed]**
- The original etymology is therefore **not securely recoverable**. Shlomo Izre’el’s conclusion—that it cannot be determined whether the word began as a name or an epithet—remains methodologically sound.

### Titles and descriptions

Attested descriptions include:

- **“Sage of Eridu”**: Sumerian incantation, Old Babylonian Nippur; later ritual tradition. **[Early attested]**
- **“Wise man of Eridu”** or **“sage, his command none could alter”**: Akkadian narrative prologue, preserved in Neo-Assyrian fragments. **[Later canonical]**
- **“Son of Ea”**: Amarna EA 356, in Ilabrat’s report to Anu. Whether biological descent is meant is doubtful; patronage, creation, priestly affiliation, or adoptive/functional sonship are all possible. **[Middle Babylonian attested]**
- **“Son of Eridu”**: a variant or parallel designation in the wider tradition. **[Regional/textual variant]**
- **“Seed of humankind”**: Neo-Assyrian Fragment D, K 8214, in a damaged context. **[Later canonical]**
- **Apkallu**, “sage”: especially first-millennium scholarly, ritual, and royal sources. In some passages this is a category; in others merely a wisdom-title. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Ummânu**, “expert/master scholar,” is associated with the learned sage tradition, though it is not consistently Adapa’s personal title.
- **Purification priest/exorcist of Eridu**: reconstructed from ritual traditions and scholarly lists; the terminology varies with the text.

### Adapa, Uanna, and Oannes

The equation must be stated in layers:

1. In the Adapa narrative, **Adapa** is a human or humanlike fisherman and ritual servant created or instructed by Ea.
2. First-millennium *Bīt mēseri* material names seven primeval *apkallū*. The first is **Uanna/U-An**, while **Utuabzu**, the seventh, is the sage said to have “ascended to heaven.”
3. Some learned texts produce the compound or juxtaposition **Uanna-Adapa**. Here *Adapa* may function as an epithet, “the wise.”
4. The Seleucid Uruk List of Kings and Sages, W 20030,7, dated precisely to **165 BCE**, names **Uanna** as sage under the antediluvian king Ayalu; it does not simply identify the myth’s protagonist as “Adapa.” [The published table and date are reproduced here.](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004520264/BP000013.pdf)
5. **Oannes** is the Greek form of Uanna in the lost *Babyloniaca* of Berossus, composed in the early third century BCE and preserved only in excerpts by later authors. Berossus’s amphibious culture-bringer emerges from the sea and teaches writing, agriculture, laws, and the arts.
6. Identifying the fisherman Adapa, Uanna the first *apkallu*, Utuabzu the heaven-ascending seventh *apkallu*, and Berossus’s Oannes as one timeless person erases genuine differences. Rykle Borger explicitly questioned the equation; W. G. Lambert explained some occurrences by taking *adapa* as “wise.” Later scholarship treats their convergence as a historical process in Babylonian learned tradition. **[Syncretistic identification]**

Thus “Adapa-Oannes, first of the seven sages” is a valid description of one late learned synthesis, not of every source.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Old Babylonian evidence, approximately 1800–1600 BCE

#### Nippur incantation

A Sumerian forerunner of the *Udug-hul* or Evil Demons incantation series contains the reconstructed declaration:

> “I am Adapa, [sage of Eridu];  
> I am [the man/exorcist of] Asalluhi.”

The tablet is Old Babylonian and comes from Nippur. It is the earliest secure surviving use of Adapa’s name. Its context shows Adapa functioning as an identity of ritual authority, not as a recipient of cult. The speaker takes on the power or persona of the primeval sage in order to expel dangerous beings. Sara Milstein summarizes the witness and its edition as FAOS 12, no. 22, lines 60–61. [Her discussion appears here.](https://zoraweb.com/files/zw/books/Adapa_the_origins.pdf)

#### Me-Turan/Tell Haddad composition

Two Old Babylonian Sumerian tablets from Me-Turan in the Diyala region preserve a long composition conventionally treated as a Sumerian Adapa myth. They were recovered in the Hamrin salvage excavations. The wider Tell Haddad library contains Old Babylonian economic, lexical, magical, and literary tablets. [Cavigneaux and al-Rawi describe the find and corpus here.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iraq/article/abs/new-sumerian-literary-texts-from-tell-haddad-ancient-meturan-a-first-survey/906DFA81FC91D8EF1440EE87954D0B5C)

The composition’s surviving first hundred lines concern the post-flood world, the gods’ need for food, rivers, grain, and winds. Adapa’s story then emerges:

- he fishes for Enki;
- the South Wind overturns or threatens his boat;
- he curses it and breaks its wing;
- An summons him;
- Enki prepares him for the audience;
- Dumuzi and Ningishzida are gatekeepers;
- the ending returns attention to the South Wind and an illness-removing incantation.

The tablets use imperfect or archaizing Sumerian, apparently written by Akkadian-speaking scribes. This does not make them translations of the later Akkadian version; they preserve an independently shaped ritual narrative. A tiny Old Babylonian Nippur fragment may belong to another Sumerian version but is too small to reconstruct.

Milstein argues that the Me-Turan recension may be earlier in literary form than the compact Amarna story: the former makes Adapa a component of a South-Wind healing myth; the latter makes the South Wind the mechanism for a story about Adapa’s wisdom and fate. That direction of development is persuasive but remains **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, not a dated authorial record.

### Middle Babylonian Akkadian: EA 356

EA 356, museum number VAT 348, is a clay literary tablet excavated at Akhetaten/el-Amarna and now in Berlin’s Vorderasiatisches Museum. CDLI classifies it as Middle Babylonian, approximately 1400–1100 BCE; its archaeological setting places it in the fourteenth-century Amarna archive. It is written in Akkadian and has red or dark reading points characteristic of several Amarna scholarly tablets. Its ductus resembles the Babylonian diplomatic letters, although it is uncertain whether it was copied in Egypt or imported. [CDLI provides its object data, transliteration, photograph, and publication history.](https://cdli.earth/artifacts/270855)

EA 356 is a scribal or educational tablet, not evidence for an Egyptian cult of Adapa. Its presence in an international diplomatic center shows circulation of Babylonian scholarship and Akkadian scribal training.

Its preserved narrative includes:

- Adapa’s curse and the South Wind’s broken wing;
- the wind’s seven-day absence;
- Anu’s inquiry through Ilabrat;
- Ea’s instructions;
- Dumuzi and Gizzida/Ningishzida at heaven’s gate;
- Adapa’s defense before Anu;
- Anu’s astonishment that Ea disclosed “the heart of heaven and earth” to humanity;
- an offer of the food and water of life;
- Adapa’s refusal;
- acceptance of clothing and oil;
- Anu’s laughter or astonishment and a damaged concluding order.

The opening, portions of the journey, and the ultimate disposition of Adapa are lost or disputed. It is therefore imprecise to say that EA 356 alone preserves every line of the popular retelling.

### Neo-Assyrian manuscripts from Nineveh

Five principal fragments conventionally labeled **A, A₁, C, D, and E** came from the libraries of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, principally seventh century BCE. K 9994 is Fragment E; K 8743 is commonly identified as C; K 8214 is D. Other proposed joins or attributions, including K 10147, are uncertain. [Izre’el’s manuscript history and cautions are summarized here.](https://therealsamizdat.com/2015/09/14/izreel-listing-the-fragments/)

These are not merely duplicate shards of EA 356:

- **Fragment A** contains the famous prologue: Ea perfected Adapa in intelligence, gave him wisdom but not eternal life, and made him keeper of Eridu’s rites and provisions.
- **A₁ and C** preserve smaller, partly divergent passages.
- **Fragment D, K 8214**, calls Adapa a “seed of humankind” and preserves a conclusion involving the South Wind, human disease, and the healing goddess Ninkarrak.
- A debated reading of K 8214 has been taken to mean that Adapa remained or was established in heaven “triumphantly,” contrary to the common return-to-earth ending. Other reconstructions instead stress the fixing of the South Wind’s destiny and protection of humanity. Because the tablet is damaged, “Adapa became immortal and stayed in heaven” is a possible recension or interpretation, not a secure universal ending. **[Regional/textual variant]**

A Neo-Assyrian literary catalogue, Rm 618, lists an incipit possibly reading “Adapa in the midst of heaven…,” suggesting that the work may once have occupied more than one tablet.

### Ritual and incantation corpus

Adapa appears repeatedly in exorcistic literature:

- Old Babylonian *Udug-hul* forerunner: “I am Adapa.”
- First-millennium *Udug-hul/Utukkū Lemnūtu* traditions: the exorcist invokes or assumes Adapa’s identity.
- Other healing and protective incantations associate the practitioner with Adapa’s wisdom, ritual purity, and access to Ea.
- Some texts coordinate Adapa with Asalluhi/Marduk, Ea’s divine ritual agent.

These declarations do not mean that a priest claimed ordinary genealogical descent from a historical Adapa. Seth Sanders interprets them as adoption of a powerful ritual persona: “I am Adapa” makes the exorcist present as the paradigmatic expert. [The monograph’s structure and evidence are outlined here.](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783161547270_A30023991/preview-9783161547270_A30023991.pdf)

### Royal inscriptions and court scholarship

- **Sennacherib, 704–681 BCE:** a building inscription credits the god Ninšiku/Ea with giving the king “wide understanding equal to that of the sage Adapu.” This authorizes royal technical ingenuity by comparison with primeval wisdom.
- **Esarhaddon’s and Ashurbanipal’s courts:** scholarly letters call the king an “offspring of a sage and Adapa,” or compare his understanding with Adapa.
- **Ashurbanipal, 668–c. 631 BCE:** inscriptions claim mastery of “the craft of the sage Adapa, the secret lore of all scribal learning,” including difficult and antediluvian texts. This is royal ideology, but it also proves Adapa’s status as patronal ancestor of learned culture. [The relevant royal-library discussion quotes this formula.](https://knowledgebasedsociety.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/4200582.pdf)

No surviving royal inscription says that the king worshipped Adapa as an independent god.

### Sage lists and author catalogues

- *Bīt mēseri*, a first-millennium apotropaic ritual series, lists seven antediluvian *apkallū* and directs the making and burial of protective sage figurines.
- The first sage is normally **Uanna**; the seventh, **Utuabzu**, is the one explicitly said to ascend to heaven.
- In some catalogues the form **Uanna-Adapa** appears.
- The **Uruk List of Kings and Sages**, W 20030,7, copied in 165 BCE by Anu-bēlšunu at Uruk, pairs Uanna with Ayalu and post-flood scholars with later kings. Its purpose is to place contemporary scholarship in an unbroken pedigree descending from primeval revelation. **[Learned systematization]**
- Late author catalogues attribute canonical works to primeval sages, including Uanna-Adapa. Such attributions are scholarly claims of authorship, not contemporaneous records of composition.

### Berossus and Oannes

Berossus, a Babylonian priest writing in Greek under the early Seleucids, composed his *Babyloniaca* around 290–280 BCE. The work is lost and survives through later excerpts. Its Oannes is a fish-bodied, human-headed being who comes from the Persian Gulf and teaches humanity writing, agriculture, city building, law, and other arts. Later six comparable beings appear.

Oannes is historically related to Uanna. Connecting him to Adapa reflects the late Uanna-Adapa learned synthesis. Berossus does **not** retell the surviving South Wind episode.

### Texts named in the wider pantheon brief that do not contain Adapa

A dedicated check produces an important negative result: Adapa is not a character in the surviving text of the *Enūma eliš*, *Atrahasis*, the Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*, *Inanna’s Descent*, *Enki and Ninmah*, or the god list *An = Anum*. Those works illuminate Ea, creation, mortality, Eridu, or Mesopotamian divine taxonomy, but they are not primary Adapa witnesses. Conflating their plots with Adapa produces modern composite mythology.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

Adapa’s securely attested functions are:

- keeper or practitioner of Eridu’s rites;
- preparer of bread, water, and the divine table;
- fisherman and boatman for Ea;
- possessor of extraordinary intelligence;
- effective ritual speaker whose curse changes the natural order;
- model and ritual persona of the exorcist;
- primeval authority for scribal knowledge;
- late member, epithet, or counterpart of the antediluvian *apkallū*.

He does not govern a cosmic domain in the manner of Anu, Ea, Šamaš, or Ištar. “God of wisdom,” “god of fishing,” and “god of immortality” are therefore misleading modern classifications.

### Powers and limitations

- **Perfected intelligence:** granted by Ea.
- **Knowledge of cosmic or ritual ordinances:** Anu complains that Ea disclosed the “heart” or plans of heaven and earth to unfit or impure humanity.
- **Performative speech:** his spoken curse physically breaks the South Wind’s wing. The tale assumes that the properly empowered sage’s utterance cannot simply be reversed.
- **Ritual purity:** the prologue calls him clean-handed, anointed, and observant of ordinances.
- **Access to heaven:** exceptional for a human, but achieved under summons and divine sponsorship.
- **Mortality:** Ea gives him wisdom but not eternal life.
- **Obedience and limited foresight:** he understands rites yet cannot independently recognize the food of life. The myth distinguishes learned knowledge from divine omniscience.
- **No securely attested martial weapon:** his instrument is speech, not a mace, bow, or sword.

### Material attributes

Objects tied to him by the narrative include:

- fishing boat, oar or rudder, and fishing equipment;
- ritual bread, water, table, clothing, and anointing oil;
- mourning clothing and disheveled or deliberately afflicted hair in the Sumerian version;
- the food and water of life;
- tablets and stylus only indirectly, through first-millennium scribal ideology.

No ancient source assigns Adapa a sacred color, number, weekday, planet, plant, or independent animal emblem.

### Iconography

There is **no surviving image securely identified by inscription as Adapa**.

Neo-Assyrian palace reliefs, seals, and buried protective figurines depict fish-cloaked and bird-headed *apkallū*. These figures carry purification cones and buckets and perform protective or fertilizing gestures. Because Adapa was integrated into the sage tradition, such images are often used to illustrate him. But an unlabelled fish-cloaked *apkallu* cannot be identified individually as Adapa or Uanna.

Berossus’s Oannes reportedly had a fish body or fish covering combined with a human body and head. That Hellenistic literary description helps explain the fish-sage interpretation but does not retrospectively turn the fisherman of EA 356 into a fish-man.

Accordingly:

- **Adapa as ordinary or exceptional human:** textually secure.
- **Adapa as fish-cloaked *apkallu*:** possible within late learned identification.
- **Every Assyrian fish-apkallu image as Adapa:** unsupported.
- **Winged Adapa:** modern confusion with the winged South Wind or bird-headed sages.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

| Relation | Evidence | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Ea/Enki as creator, patron, lord, or “father” | Narrative prologue; EA 356 calls Adapa “Ea’s son” | Secure relationship; literal biological paternity is not |
| Eridu as parent-like origin | “Son/sage of Eridu” formulations | Civic and cultic origin |
| Mother | None named | No evidence |
| Consort | None named | No evidence |
| Children | None securely named | No evidence |
| Human descendants | First-millennium scholars metaphorically claim his lineage or persona | Professional/ideological descent, not family genealogy |
| Uanna/Oannes | Late lists and learned equations | Syncretistic or epithetic association |
| Utuabzu | Both linked with heavenly ascent in scholarly interpretation | Similarity, not secure identity |

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Ea/Enki — creation and endowment:** Ea creates or perfects Adapa, gives him broad understanding, but withholds eternal life; Neo-Assyrian prologue, reflecting an older narrative tradition. **[Later canonical; probably older core]**
- **Ea/Enki — cult service:** Adapa prepares food and water, maintains the table, and fishes for Ea at Eridu; Neo-Assyrian Fragment A and Old Babylonian Me-Turan tradition. **[Early-attested narrative complex]**
- **Ea/Enki — summons preparation:** Ea learns that Anu has summoned Adapa, puts him into mourning, supplies a strategy for Dumuzi and Ningishzida, and tells him what to accept or refuse; Me-Turan tablets and EA 356. **[Early attested/Middle Babylonian canonical]**
- **Ea/Enki — possible deception or protection:** Ea calls the offerings “food and water of death,” although Anu later offers life. The text never explicitly explains whether Ea deceives Adapa, anticipates a hostile offer that Anu then changes, or correctly protects him within a dangerous divine trial. **[Canonical ambiguity]**
- **An/Anu — summons and judgment:** Anu notices the South Wind’s seven-day absence, learns that Adapa caused it, and has him summoned; Me-Turan and EA 356. **[Early attested/Middle Babylonian canonical]**
- **An/Anu — interrogation:** Anu asks why Adapa broke the South Wind’s wing; Adapa explains the capsizing of his fishing boat; EA 356 and variants. **[Middle Babylonian canonical]**
- **An/Anu — offer of life:** Anu orders food and water of life, clothing, and oil brought to Adapa; Adapa refuses the first two but accepts the latter two; EA 356. **[Middle Babylonian canonical]**
- **An/Anu — altered fate:** damaged conclusions variously imply laughter, return to earth, public display, or favorable installation in heaven; EA 356 and K 8214. **[Conflicting textual variants]**
- **Ilabrat — report:** Anu’s *sukkal* Ilabrat explains that “Adapa, Ea’s son,” broke the South Wind’s wing; EA 356. Ilabrat does not otherwise confront Adapa directly in the preserved text. **[Middle Babylonian canonical]**
- **Dumuzi — gate encounter:** Adapa tells Dumuzi that he mourns because Dumuzi and Gizzida have disappeared from the land; Dumuzi is pleased or astonished and supports him before Anu; EA 356 and Me-Turan. **[Early-attested/Middle Babylonian canonical]**
- **Ningishzida/Gizzida — gate encounter:** the same episode as Dumuzi; he and Dumuzi repeat or commend Adapa’s speech and help calm Anu. **[Early-attested/Middle Babylonian canonical]**
- **Ninkarrak — healing aftermath:** Neo-Assyrian Fragment D invokes or predicts Ninkarrak’s healing of the disease associated with the South Wind’s restored blowing. Direct dialogue with Adapa is not preserved. **[Regional/textual variant]**
- **Asalluhi — ritual identification:** an Old Babylonian incantation places “I am Adapa, sage of Eridu” beside a damaged claim to be the man or ritual representative of Asalluhi. This is an exorcistic alignment, not a narrative meeting. **[Early ritual attestation]**
- **The Anunna/Anunnaki — assembly context:** the Me-Turan and later prologue situate Adapa amid or in comparison with the great gods, but no individually recoverable exchange survives. **[Damaged collective interaction]**

The South Wind is anthropomorphized and has a “wing,” but it is not consistently written or treated as a named god. Its encounter is nevertheless central:

- **South Wind — combat by curse:** it overturns Adapa’s boat; Adapa curses it and breaks its wing; it cannot blow for seven days. Its eventual restoration is part of the Sumerian and Neo-Assyrian ritual endings. **[Early narrative core]**

No interaction is attested between Adapa and Enlil, Marduk, Ištar/Inanna, Gilgamesh, Atrahasis, Utnapishtim, Tiamat, or the biblical Adam.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Old Babylonian South Wind and healing narrative

**[Early attested]**

The Me-Turan composition opens after a flood or world-restoring event. Humanity, rivers, grain, fish, divine provisions, and the gods’ dependence on offerings occupy the damaged prologue. Adapa enters not as the first man but as a figure performing duties for Enki.

While he fishes, the South Wind blows violently and overturns or endangers the boat. Adapa’s angry curse breaks its wing. An notices the interruption and summons him. Enki prepares Adapa by making him look mournful—disheveling his hair and, in one reading, putting lice into it—and teaches him how to amuse or win over Dumuzi and Ningishzida. He forbids Adapa the food and water and, in this recension, apparently warns him about clothing while allowing oil.

In heaven the gatekeepers respond favorably. The divine assembly asks about the South Wind, but Adapa himself has little preserved agency. Enki fixes the wind’s destiny. The text turns into an incantation asking that the South Wind not touch the patient’s skin and that its departure carry illness away.

The composition’s practical function is therefore likely medical or exorcistic. Breaking the wind’s wing may explain why harmful wind-borne illness lingers; restoring its movement removes disease. Earlier climatic theories that the wind’s absence halted vegetation are plausible environmental readings but are not stated in the text.

### The Amarna tale of lost or refused immortality

**[Middle Babylonian canonical]**

EA 356 compresses the cosmological and medical framework and centers Adapa’s personal fate.

The South Wind upsets his boat while he is fishing for Ea. Adapa declares that he will break its wing, and because his word has divine efficacy, the wing breaks. Seven days pass without the wind. Anu asks Ilabrat why; Ilabrat identifies Adapa, “Ea’s son,” as the cause.

Ea anticipates the summons. He dresses Adapa in mourning and explains that Dumuzi and Gizzida guard Anu’s gate. When they ask whom he mourns, he must say that those very gods have vanished from the land. Flattered, moved, or amused, they will commend him.

Ea warns that Anu will give him bread and water of death, which he must refuse, but that he should accept clothing and oil. Before Anu, Adapa explains that he was fishing for his lord when the South Wind capsized him. The gatekeepers intervene favorably.

Anu then asks why Ea disclosed the inner knowledge of heaven and earth to humanity. Faced with an extraordinarily wise mortal, Anu orders the food and water of life brought. Adapa refuses them but accepts clothing and oil. Anu laughs or expresses astonishment and asks why he did not eat. Adapa answers that Ea told him not to.

The ending is damaged. The traditional reconstruction has Anu send him back to earth, preserving human mortality. What the extant tablet securely establishes is narrower: Adapa refuses something Anu calls life-giving because he trusts Ea.

### Was Ea deceiving Adapa?

Four principal readings exist:

1. **Deliberate withholding:** Ea does not want his uniquely useful servant to become immortal or escape his control. **[Modern scholarly interpretation]**
2. **Protection against execution:** Anu originally intended punitive food and water; Dumuzi and Ningishzida’s intervention changed the offer after Ea had issued his warning. **[Modern scholarly interpretation]**
3. **Cosmic boundary maintenance:** Ea knows that divine immortality is inappropriate for humanity; his advice preserves the proper order even if it deprives Adapa individually. **[Modern scholarly interpretation]**
4. **Adapa’s faithful victory:** the point is his perfect obedience and survival, not a “Fall”; interpreting the text solely as a tragedy imports later expectations. **[Modern scholarly interpretation]**

The text supplies no narrator’s verdict. Calling Ea either a liar or a benevolent savior as an established fact exceeds the evidence.

### Neo-Assyrian ending: disease and possible heavenly elevation

**[Regional/textual variant]**

K 8214/Fragment D redirects the conclusion toward humanity. Adapa is “seed of humankind”; the South Wind’s blowing is connected with illness, and Ninkarrak can bring healing. A disputed passage may place Adapa triumphantly in heaven. If that reading is correct, the recension does not end with a simple failure and expulsion.

This variant is important because it disproves the claim that Mesopotamian tradition possessed only one canonical moral: “Adapa foolishly lost immortality for everyone.” The narratives differ over whether the focus is mortality, ritual healing, the South Wind’s regulation, or Adapa’s elevation.

### Adapa and the seven sages

**[First-millennium learned systematization]**

Separate traditions about a wise fisherman of Eridu and about seven primeval sages converged. Adapa became a paradigmatic *apkallu*, sometimes the first, sometimes an epithet of Uanna. His journey to heaven also resembled the explicitly stated ascent of the seventh sage Utuabzu. Scholarly catalogues then made the sages founders or authors of canonical learning.

### Oannes the culture-bringer

**[Hellenistic syncretistic identification]**

Berossus’s Oannes spends his days teaching humanity letters, sciences, arts, law, surveying, agriculture, and city life, returning to the sea at night. The story preserves the Babylonian idea that civilization was revealed by antediluvian sages. It does not contain the broken South Wind or refused food of life. “Adapa-Oannes” is thus a fusion of related but distinguishable cycles.

### Adapa and Adam

**[Modern comparativist reconstruction]**

The parallels are real but limited:

- both are primeval humans associated with extraordinary knowledge;
- both face divine food connected with immortality;
- both remain mortal;
- the names look superficially similar in European transliteration.

The differences are decisive:

- Adapa is already wise and refuses the food; Adam acquires forbidden knowledge by eating;
- Adapa obeys his patron god; Adam violates a prohibition;
- no woman, serpent, garden, tree, creation from soil, or expulsion from Eden appears in Adapa;
- Adapa’s action may concern his own fate, disease, or a sage’s status rather than a hereditary human “Fall”;
- no demonstrated linguistic derivation connects *Adapa* with Hebrew *ʾādām*.

Direct literary dependence is possible only as a broad cultural-historical hypothesis. “Adapa was the original Adam” is not established.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Eridu and Ea’s temple

Adapa’s narrative setting is Eridu and the household of Ea, conventionally associated with the E-abzu temple. Archaeology documents Eridu as one of southern Mesopotamia’s earliest and most enduring sacred centers, but no excavated shrine is demonstrably “Adapa’s temple.”

The texts portray Adapa as temple personnel or an idealized primordial servant:

- he makes bread with Eridu’s bakers;
- provides food and water;
- prepares and clears the divine table;
- pilots the boat;
- hunts or fishes for the temple;
- observes purity and divine ordinances.

These are literary depictions consistent with real temple economies, not personnel records proving that an office formally titled “Adapa priest” existed.

### Independent cult

No securely identified evidence establishes:

- a temple dedicated to Adapa;
- a priesthood serving him as its god;
- an annual festival for him;
- a sacrificial tariff in his name;
- votive statues offered to him;
- an oracle of Adapa;
- a month, day, or city officially belonging to him;
- theophoric personal names invoking him as a divine patron.

That absence accords with his normal classification as a sage rather than a god.

### Exorcistic practice

Adapa’s strongest ritual presence lies in the practitioner’s assumed identity. “I am Adapa” authorizes the exorcist as heir or embodiment of primeval expertise. The formula is comparable to other Mesopotamian ritual identifications in which a human operator speaks as a god or mythical expert.

First-millennium *apkallu* figurines were manufactured under ritual prescriptions and buried in buildings to avert evil. Because Uanna-Adapa belonged to the learned sage system, these installations are related to his tradition. But individual uninscribed figurines cannot be assigned personal names without a corresponding ritual text.

### Sacrifice, sexuality, and violence

A dedicated contested-material search found **no evidence for human sacrifice to Adapa**, no temple-prostitution institution attached to him, and no cult violence carried out in his name. His story contains supernatural violence against the South Wind and the threat of divine punishment, but not human sacrificial practice.

The *Atrahasis* creation myth’s killing of a god to make humanity is unrelated to Adapa and must not be imported into his dossier.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before approximately 1800 BCE: possible antecedents

Eridu, Enki, ritual wisdom, fishing, and dangerous winds are much older than the surviving Adapa tablets. Linguistic and thematic archaism may point to earlier oral or written forms. No third-millennium tablet yet securely names Adapa, however. A third-millennium “Adapa cult” is **unestablished**.

### Approximately 1800–1600 BCE: ritual expert and South-Wind protagonist

The Nippur incantation already assumes that Adapa is a recognizable sage of Eridu. The Me-Turan narrative embeds him in a post-flood restoration and healing composition. At this stage he is human, exceptionally powerful in speech, and closely subordinate to Enki.

### Fourteenth century BCE: international scribal literature

EA 356 reached or was copied at Akhetaten. The narrative has become a compact Akkadian story about divine knowledge, obedience, and life-giving food. Its presence in Egypt reflects scribal education in Akkadian, not Egyptian popular devotion.

### First millennium BCE: canonization and professional ancestry

At Nineveh the story survives in several recensions. Adapa becomes:

- the fully elaborated “wise man of Eridu”;
- an exemplar of knowledge for Assyrian kings;
- a ritual persona for exorcists;
- an ancestor of learned professions;
- a participant in catalogues organizing primeval revelation.

Royal comparison with Adapa had political force: it portrayed Sennacherib’s engineering and Ashurbanipal’s literacy as continuations of wisdom reaching back before the flood.

### First millennium BCE: assimilation to the *apkallū*

The seven sages were increasingly systematized as antediluvian beings paired with kings. Uanna was normally first; Utuabzu ascended to heaven. The title or name Adapa migrated into this scheme, producing Uanna-Adapa and subsequent scholarly conflation.

This was not a “promotion to godhood” in a simple sense. The *apkallū* are liminal: primeval sages, divine or semidivine beings, ritual powers, and ancestors of human scholars.

### Seleucid period

The Uruk list of 165 BCE anchors contemporary Urukean scholars in a chain from antediluvian Uanna through famous literary authors to the copyist’s own milieu. Berossus translates the sage system into Greek historiography through Oannes.

### Late antiquity and medieval transmission

Berossus’s original work disappeared, but Oannes survived in Greek and Christian chronographic excerpts, especially through Alexander Polyhistor, Abydenus, Eusebius, and George Synkellos. This is literary transmission, not evidence of continued worship.

There is no documented continuous Adapa cult through Christianization or Islamization. No source records a targeted suppression of an Adapa temple. The broader closure and transformation of Mesopotamian temple institutions ended the scribal environments that preserved his tradition, but an Adapa-specific persecution is unattested.

Proposed continuities into Mandaean, Gnostic, Islamic, or local Iraqi folklore are comparative hypotheses unless a text explicitly names or unmistakably describes him.

### Nineteenth century to present: rediscovery

The Amarna tablet was found in 1887 and published in the early 1890s. Nineveh fragments were progressively joined to it. The discovery of Old Babylonian Me-Turan tablets in the twentieth century overturned the assumption that the Amarna version was the earliest recoverable form and restored the story’s Sumerian ritual dimension.

No continuous modern worship of Adapa is demonstrable. Individual Mesopotamian reconstructionists may honor him or the *apkallū*, but publicly visible practice is small, decentralized, and historically modern.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Early Assyriology and biblical comparison

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scholarship quickly read Adapa beside Genesis. Robert William Rogers, E. A. Wallis Budge, and collections of “cuneiform parallels” popularized the label “Babylonian Fall of Man.” That comparison made the story widely known but also imposed a unified moral of lost immortality on fragmentary, divergent texts.

Modern critical editions, especially Izre’el’s *Adapa and the South Wind* (2001), shifted attention toward manuscript differences, language, and performative speech.

### Theosophy and esotericism

Helena Blavatsky’s *Isis Unveiled* treated Oannes as an emblem of priestly esoteric wisdom emerging from the symbolic deep. A 1928 Theosophical article explicitly compared Adapa with Adam and accused Ea of deception. [The periodical preserves that interpretation.](https://www.ultindia.org/s/thy/THYApril1928.pdf)

These are **[Modern occult reinterpretations]**, not continuations of Babylonian ritual. They combine Berossus’s fish-sage, biblical typology, and modern esoteric symbolism.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist reception

Modern devotional websites and reconstructionist communities retell Adapa as:

- “Lord Adapa”;
- a semidivine priest of Ea;
- an archetype of fidelity, wisdom, or initiation;
- Adapa-Oannes, revealer of sacred rites.

Such practice may be religiously meaningful to participants, but titles, prayers, and ethical readings not supported by cuneiform sources are modern compositions. One representative modern devotional interpretation describes him as a positive metaphor of faith and identifies him freely with Oannes. [It is documented here.](https://www.gatewaystobabylon.com/gods/lords/lordadapa.html)

No reliable census establishes the scale of Adapa-specific worship.

### Popular ancient-astronaut material

Modern internet and fringe literature sometimes converts Adapa into:

- a genetically engineered servant of extraterrestrial “Anunnaki”;
- the historical Adam;
- an immortal reptilian, alien, or member of a hidden brotherhood;
- a king placed in a fabricated universal genealogy.

These claims do not derive from EA 356, the Me-Turan tablets, *Bīt mēseri*, or Berossus. They are **[Modern inventions]** created by combining modern UFO mythology, speculative biblical harmonization, and mistranslations of “Anunnaki.”

### Psychology, fiction, and commercial culture

Adapa has attracted archetypal readings as the human who possesses knowledge without immortality, or as the obedient initiate who fails to question authority. These are modern philosophical or psychological applications; no ancient source supplies a Jungian theory.

Unlike Gilgamesh, Ištar, or Tiamat, Adapa has not acquired a single dominant mass-media image. Modern poems, adaptations, encyclopedic videos, games, and fantasy works usually reproduce the Amarna synopsis or the fish-man conflation. Their cumulative effect is to make “first apkallu who lost immortality” appear anciently uniform when it is a modern composite.

No documented nationalist or extremist movement has made Adapa a major emblem. Searches for political, racialist, or extremist appropriation produced scattered fringe claims rather than a coherent movement.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Discovery and editing

The scholarly world first recognized the myth after EA 356’s discovery at Amarna in 1887. Harper and Scheil published early treatments in 1891; further Nineveh fragments followed. A. T. Clay published another fragment in 1923. [Clay’s early edition and manuscript discussion remain accessible here.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d1/A_Hebrew_deluge_story_in_cuneiform_-_and_other_epic_fragments_in_the_Pierpont_Morgan_library_%28IA_hebrewdelugestor00clay_0%29.pdf)

Important modern studies include:

- Giorgio Buccellati, “Adapa, Genesis and the Notion of Faith”;
- Piotr Michalowski, “Adapa and the Ritual Process”;
- Shlomo Izre’el, *Adapa and the South Wind: Language Has the Power of Life and Death*;
- Antoine Cavigneaux and Farouk al-Rawi’s publications of the Me-Turan tablets;
- Sara J. Milstein, “The Origins of Adapa”;
- Seth L. Sanders, *From Adapa to Enoch*;
- Amar Annus, *The Overturned Boat*;
- Jørgen Kvanvig’s studies of Adapa, apkallū, and Enochic traditions.

### Principal debates

#### Is Adapa a god?

The narrative’s mortal status, dependence on Ea, and loss or lack of immortality argue against classifying him as a god. His *apkallu* identity and ritual embodiment give him superhuman or semidivine status in later systems. “Deified sage” is defensible for some contexts but should not replace the earlier human classification.

#### Did he lose humanity’s immortality?

The Amarna recension makes Adapa refuse life-giving food, but it does not explicitly say that all humans had previously been immortal or that his descendants inherit a curse. The Me-Turan version focuses on illness and the wind. The universal “Fall of Man” interpretation is therefore broader than the tablets.

#### Did Ea deceive him?

Possible, but not explicit. The intervention of Dumuzi and Ningishzida may have changed Anu’s disposition. The surviving versions support multiple readings.

#### Did Adapa return to earth or remain in heaven?

The ordinary reconstruction sends him back. K 8214 has been interpreted as granting heavenly elevation. Damage and divergent recensions prevent certainty.

#### Was Adapa the first *apkallu*?

Late learned sources can attach *Adapa* to Uanna, but the normal sage lists name Uanna first, while Utuabzu is the one who ascends. “Adapa was always the first of the seven” is anachronistic.

#### Was Oannes Adapa?

Oannes securely continues Uanna. Uanna-Adapa is an attested learned combination, but the South-Wind fisherman and Berossus’s amphibious civilizer remain narratively different figures.

#### Is the story originally Sumerian?

The earliest extended surviving form is Sumerian and Old Babylonian. The Akkadian text preserves possible Sumerian stylistic features. This strongly supports a Sumerian literary history but does not allow recovery of an unattested third-millennium original.

#### Is Adapa Adam?

The comparison is historically important but etymological identity and direct derivation are unproved. Similarity of name is insufficient, and the narrative logic is substantially different.

#### What does the South Wind mean?

Proposals include:

- a dangerous squall threatening fishermen;
- the humid or fertilizing wind necessary for date cultivation;
- a carrier of disease;
- a force whose movement removes illness;
- a ritual image for the exorcist’s control of harmful air.

The Sumerian and Fragment D medical endings securely support an illness association. Specific meteorological reconstructions remain modern hypotheses.

### State of the evidence

The central narrative survives only in fragmentary clay tablets:

- EA 356 in Berlin;
- Nineveh pieces largely in the British Museum;
- Me-Turan tablets in Iraqi collections;
- Nippur ritual material dispersed among excavated tablet collections;
- W 20030,7 from Uruk, historically associated with the Iraq Museum.

Modern editions necessarily combine witnesses separated by language, place, date, and purpose. Any seamless “complete epic” is an editorial composite.

### Discredited or unsupported claims that remain popular

- Adapa was certainly a god worshipped throughout Sumer.
- Adapa was the historical Adam.
- Ea was Satan and Anu the biblical God.
- Adapa was genetically created by extraterrestrials.
- Every fish-apkallu relief depicts Adapa.
- The tablets say that reptiles or aliens denied humans immortality.
- Adapa appears in *Enūma eliš*, *Atrahasis*, or the *Epic of Gilgamesh*.
- His cult practiced human sacrifice.
- A secret Adapa priesthood survived continuously into modern occult orders.

None is established by the primary corpus.
