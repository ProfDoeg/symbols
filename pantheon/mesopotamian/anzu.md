# Anzu (Imdugud): Research Dossier

> **Class:** A singular divine or numinous giant bird in Sumerian tradition; a lion-headed eagle and divine emblem in Early Dynastic art; in Akkadian literary tradition, a supernatural monster or demon (*rābiṣu* and related hostile classifications occur in scholarship and translations), though still divine enough to bear a divine determinative in some writings. “God,” “storm-bird,” “monster,” and “demon” describe different contexts and periods, not one stable taxonomy.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The lion-headed-eagle figure securely associated with Imdugud/Anzud is documented in Early Dynastic III Lagash, approximately 2600–2400 BCE, on inscribed cult objects dedicated to Ningirsu. The Mesilim mace-head from Girsu is probably the earliest securely contextualized example, conventionally dated around 2600 BCE; the Enmetena vase, ca. 2420–2400 BCE, combines the image with an inscription and cultic context. Earlier lion-eagle images exist, but identifying every prehistoric or Early Dynastic composite bird as Anzu is a modern attribution, not something their inscriptions establish.

## 1. Identification, names, and classifications

Anzu is the great Mesopotamian bird whose record divides into two overlapping but non-identical traditions. In third-millennium Sumerian art and literature, Anzud/Imdugud is a tremendous mountain and storm bird, a master of animals, a guardian or emblem associated especially with Ningirsu and Lagash, and—in *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*—a beneficent being capable of decreeing destiny. In the Akkadian *Epic of Anzu*, first securely preserved in Old Babylonian manuscripts and elaborated in first-millennium recensions, Anzu serves Enlil, steals the Tablet of Destinies, paralyzes divine government, and is killed by Ningirsu or Ninurta. These profiles cannot safely be collapsed into a biography in which one initially benevolent individual “turned evil”; that developmental story is a modern reconstruction.

### Names and writings

- **Sumerian:** conventionally **Anzud**, **Anzûd**, or **Imdugud**. The logographic writing is commonly represented as **AN.IM.DUGUD**, with the bird determinative *MUŠEN* in some texts. Unicode representation varies, but ORACC records the compound as 𒀭𒅎𒂂 and lists the normalized readings *anzu* and *anzud*. The initial AN sign was formerly taken as the divine determinative, producing the old reading **dZû** or **Zu**.
- **Akkadian:** **Anzû**, with manuscript forms including *Anza, Anzi, Anzu,* and emphatic or enclitic forms such as *Anzuma/Anzumma*. ORACC lexical data document syllabic writings including *an-za-a, an-zi-i, an-zu-u,* and *an-zu-u₂*.
- **Obsolete scholarly name:** **Zû**. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Assyriologists read the first sign as the unpronounced divine determinative DINGIR and the name as “Zu.” By the 1960s–70s the reading **Anzu** prevailed because the first sign belongs to the phonetic name. “Zu bird” survives in older translations and popular literature but should not be presented as a separate ancient being.
- **Imdugud / An-im-dugud:** a conventional reading of the Sumerian logographic spelling. Since *im* can denote wind, storm, or weather and *dugud* “heavy,” renderings such as “heavy storm,” “heavy wind,” “thundercloud,” or “storm-cloud” are interpretive possibilities, not a settled ancient gloss.
- The popular etymology “he who knows heaven,” obtained by parsing AN “heaven” and ZU “to know,” is a modern wordplay and is not an established historical etymology.
- Jacobsen argued that the graph could preserve an older pronunciation related to *Imdugud*, with *Anzu(d)* developing as a phonetic form. The direction and linguistic mechanism remain debated. No surviving Mesopotamian etymological commentary settles the issue.

### Epithets and descriptions

The being lacks the stable repertory of cult titles possessed by major temple gods. Its descriptions are source-specific:

- “Bird with sparkling eyes, born in this district” — *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*, Old Babylonian copies of a Sumerian composition.
- “Prince who decides the destiny of rolling rivers” — the same poem.
- Guardian who bars the mountain entrance and follows Enlil’s counsel — the same poem.
- Possessor of shark-like teeth and eagle’s claws, whose cry shakes the Lulubi mountains — the same poem.
- In Akkadian epic, a bird with a saw-like beak, extraordinary wings, and terrifying supernatural radiance or *melammu*.
- “Evil Anzu” is the polemical designation of Ninurta-centered victory literature. It is not a neutral label for every Sumerian appearance.

### Classification by period

- **Early Dynastic Lagash:** divine or heraldic lion-eagle; emblem and associate of Ningirsu; possibly an independently numinous being.
- **Sumerian literary tradition:** a speaking, reproductive, destiny-fixing supernatural bird. Its wife and chick show that *anzud* can describe a kind or family as well as one unique individual.
- **Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian Anzu epic:** cosmic rebel and monster, but one admitted into Enlil’s service and embedded within the divine household.
- **Lexical and magical texts:** a named supernatural creature whose body parts, notably a claw, can carry magical efficacy.
- **Modern fantasy taxonomy:** frequently reduced to “demon,” “griffin,” or generic monster. These are approximations, not exact equivalents of Mesopotamian categories.

There is no documented Greek or Roman interpretatio identifying Anzu with a named classical deity. Comparisons with the griffin, Garuda, the Iranian Sīmurgh, the Jewish Ziz, or the thunderbird are comparative constructions, not ancient equations.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest material evidence

The earliest securely contextualized evidence comes from the cultic art of Early Dynastic Lagash:

1. **Mesilim mace-head, Girsu, ca. 2600 BCE.** Dedicated to Ningirsu while Lugalshaengur governed Lagash. It bears a large bird/lion-eagle motif among lions. Its identification as the earliest Anzu image is strong but still iconographic: the inscription dedicates the object to Ningirsu rather than naming the creature.
2. **Ur-Nanshe and Dudu plaques, Lagash/Girsu, Early Dynastic III.** The lion-headed eagle appears frontally with outspread wings above or between lions. These establish the motif’s institutional place in Ningirsu’s cult.
3. **Enmetena’s silver vase, Girsu, ca. 2420–2400 BCE, Louvre AO 2674.** Four lion-headed eagles bind pairs of lions, ibexes, and deer. Its Sumerian inscription says the vessel belonged to Ningirsu’s table service and names the priest Dudu. The museum record securely dates and contextualizes the object.
4. **Tell al-ʿUbaid copper relief, ca. 2500 BCE, British Museum 114308.** A monumental copper-alloy lion-headed eagle holds two stags over the entrance of Ninhursag’s temple. Because the inscription does not name it, calling it “the Imdugud Relief” is a persuasive iconographic identification, not a textual label on the object itself.

These images demonstrate that the lion-eagle was not originally only an image of defeated evil. It could occupy temple architecture and votive objects as a protective, authoritative, or ordering emblem.

### Sumerian literary corpus

#### *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird* — ETCSL 1.8.2.2

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Surviving witnesses:** principally Old Babylonian school tablets, ca. nineteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE; CDLI’s composite is Literary 000368/P469673.
- **Probable compositional background:** traditions about the legendary Uruk ruler Lugalbanda may be older, but no extant third-millennium manuscript proves the poem existed in its recovered form that early.
- **Role:** Anzud lives with a wife and chick in remote mountains near Aratta. Its cry shakes the Lulubi mountains; it carries wild bulls; it calls itself a prince empowered by Enlil to guard the mountains and decree destinies. Lugalbanda feeds and adorns its chick. In gratitude Anzud grants him supernatural speed and advises secrecy. This is securely attested beneficent Anzud material, incompatible with a uniformly demonic classification.

#### *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld* — ETCSL 1.8.1.4

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Manuscripts:** chiefly Old Babylonian, though the tradition may have earlier roots.
- **Role:** In the poem’s huluppu-tree prologue, an uncharmable serpent nests in the roots, an Anzud bird places its young in the branches, and a female being occupies the trunk. Gilgamesh kills the serpent; the bird takes its young and flees to the mountains. Anzu does not steal a Tablet here and is not killed.
- The separate modern title “Inanna and the Huluppu Tree” designates this opening episode; it is not an independent ancient tablet title.

#### *Ninurta and the Turtle*

- **Language:** Sumerian; surviving Old Babylonian witnesses.
- **Role:** The story presupposes Ninurta’s recovery of the Tablet of Destinies after Anzu let it fall into the Abzu. Ninurta challenges Enki over possession and is defeated or humbled through Enki’s turtle. The composition preserves a different custody narrative: Enki, not simply Enlil, emerges as possessor of the Tablet.
- This is a variant sequel or alternative theological appropriation, not evidence that the Akkadian epic ended identically.

#### Hymnic and royal allusions

Sumerian Ninurta/Ningirsu praise poetry mentions the hero’s defeat of “evil Anzud” and recovery of divine powers or destiny. These allusions show that the combat tradition was broader than the surviving Akkadian epic, but no complete Sumerian “Anzu epic” has been recovered.

Gudea’s inscriptions, ca. twenty-second century BCE, call Ningirsu’s Eninnu temple the “White/Shining Anzud.” In Gudea’s dream vision, Ningirsu’s upper body or wings are compared to Anzud while his lower body resembles a flood. This does not make Ningirsu and Anzu universally identical; it shows deliberate assimilation of the bird’s storm form and prestige to the Lagash god.

### Akkadian *Epic of Anzu*

Ancient incipits and modern editions variously call the composition *Bīn šar dadmē* (“Son of the king of inhabited places…”) or the *Myth/Epic of Anzu*.

#### Old Babylonian recension

- **Language:** Old Babylonian Akkadian.
- **Date of manuscripts:** early second millennium BCE, approximately eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE.
- **Principal provenance:** a major witness came from Susa; other fragments extend the transmission.
- **Hero:** conventionally reconstructed as Ningirsu rather than Ninurta, reflecting a Lagash-oriented version.
- **State:** fragmentary. It proves the Tablet-theft narrative existed long before Ashurbanipal’s library, but many details commonly retold come only from later witnesses.

#### Standard Babylonian recension

- **Language:** Standard Babylonian Akkadian.
- **Form:** three tablets.
- **Composition/redaction:** probably second millennium BCE in substantial part; surviving manuscripts are mainly first millennium BCE.
- **Witnesses:** Nineveh, Assur, Sultantepe/Huzirina, Borsippa, and Tarbiṣu. The Nineveh copies belong substantially to the seventh-century BCE library of Ashurbanipal.
- **Hero:** Ninurta.
- **Modern critical edition:** Amar Annus, *The Standard Babylonian Epic of Anzu* (2001), following major work by Blahoslav Hruška, Marianna Vogelzang, W. G. Lambert, and others.

#### Late Assyrian textual variation

Some Sultantepe witnesses diverge in Tablet III. The epic therefore did not exist as one perfectly uniform canonical text. “Standard Babylonian” names a learned literary register and recension, not proof that every line was fixed throughout Mesopotamia.

### Related corpora: presence and absence

The corpora specified for comparison must be separated from texts that actually feature Anzu:

- **Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh*:** Anzu occurs as a terrifying image in a dream on the Cedar Forest journey: a lion-headed or monstrous bird soars like a cloud, with dreadful face, fiery maw, and deadly breath. Shamash’s intervention in the dream anticipates rescue. This is a simile or dream apparition, not the Tablet-theft story.
- **Enūma eliš:** Anzu does **not** appear as an actor in the extant epic. Tiamat gives the Tablet of Destinies to Qingu, and Marduk takes it after victory. The Tablet motif and Marduk’s elevation have often been compared with, and perhaps literarily shaped by, the Anzu tradition. Qingu is not Anzu.
- **Atrahasis:** Anzu is not an actor in the extant text. The shared divine assembly and labor/crisis vocabulary are contextual, not appearances.
- **Inanna’s Descent:** no Anzu episode occurs in the extant Sumerian or Akkadian descent narratives. Confusion arises from the huluppu-tree prologue of *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld*.
- **Enki and Ninmah:** Anzu does not occur in the secure ETCSL text. Modern “translations” in which Ninmah revives Anzu or employs technological apparatus derive from Zecharia Sitchin-inspired fiction, not this ancient composition.
- **An = Anum:** the great god list is valuable for pantheon structure and scribal equations, but Anzu’s best-documented identity is literary and iconographic rather than that of a major temple god with a large divine household.
- **RINAP:** Assyrian royal inscriptions employ Ninurta’s monster-slaying ideology and cosmic-battle imagery; they do not constitute a continuous independent Anzu cult history.

## 3. Domain, attributes, powers, and iconography

### Domains

**Secure Sumerian evidence:**

- mountains and their entrances;
- storms, thunderous sound, wind, and overwhelming atmospheric force;
- wild animals, especially powerful hoofed animals and lions;
- guardianship;
- the ability to fix or pronounce destiny;
- river power in the title “prince who decides the destiny of rolling rivers.”

**Akkadian epic elaboration:**

- usurped divine authority;
- interruption of ritual and cosmic administration;
- command made effective through possession of the Tablet of Destinies;
- control over the ontology of manufactured weapons: Anzu’s command makes an arrow revert to its constituent reed, wood, feathers, and sinew.

The claim that Anzu specifically personifies the **south wind** is a scholarly synthesis and popular formulation. Storm and wind associations are well supported, but no dossier should treat “god of the south wind” as an uncontested ancient office.

### Physical form

The Early Dynastic visual type is a frontal eagle with an unmistakably leonine head, spread wings, and large talons. It commonly grasps paired animals. Texts add features not always visible in art:

- eagle’s claws;
- shark-like teeth;
- saw-like beak;
- vast wings resembling a net across heaven;
- frightening eyes and *melammu*;
- a voice that shakes mountains;
- in later descriptions, fiery mouth or deadly breath.

This creates an important iconographic problem: the Standard Babylonian epic’s saw-like beak does not neatly match a lion muzzle. The identification of every ancient lion-eagle with the literary Anzu is therefore strong in some Lagash contexts but not mechanically certain everywhere.

### Principal depictions

1. **Mesilim mace-head**, Girsu, ca. 2600 BCE: lion-eagle in Ningirsu’s cult setting.
2. **Shell inlay of lion-eagle attacking a human-headed bull**, ca. 2600–2500 BCE, Metropolitan Museum 1984.77: unprovenanced within Mesopotamia but stylistically Early Dynastic IIIa.
3. **Tell al-ʿUbaid relief**, ca. 2500 BCE: monumental copper-alloy lion-eagle holding stags, once fixed over Ninhursag’s temple entrance.
4. **Enmetena vase**, ca. 2420–2400 BCE: four lion-eagles mastering alternating animals.
5. **Lagash votive plaques and mace-heads:** the motif functions as Ningirsu’s emblem and, arguably, a civic emblem of Lagash.
6. **Nimrud/Kalhu relief pair**, ca. 865–860 BCE, from Ashurnasirpal II’s Temple of Ninurta: one panel depicts the armed god, the other the fleeing lion-bird. Now in the British Museum, BM 124571–124572. The placement at the temple entrance made Ninurta’s victory part of Assyrian royal and military ideology.

The Nimrud pair was long mislabeled as Marduk fighting Tiamat. Its current interpretation as Ninurta pursuing Anzu grew from improved readings of the epic and from twentieth-century iconographic study.

### Symbols and associations

- **Animals:** lions, deer/stags, ibexes, bulls. Their appearance does not necessarily make every species “sacred to Anzu.” On the Enmetena vase, scholars have interpreted the animals as symbols of Ningirsu and his divine relatives, with the lion-eagle imposing unity or mastery over them.
- **Weapons:** Anzu normally relies on its body, roar, wings, and the Tablet rather than a conventional weapon.
- **Object:** the Tablet of Destinies, *ṭuppi šīmāti*, is an object Anzu steals, not an intrinsic emblem in the early visual record.
- **Color, day, number, planet:** no secure ancient system assigns Anzu a canonical color, weekday, sacred number, or celestial body. Modern correspondence tables are inventions or occult extrapolations.
- **Claw:** a first-millennium ritual directs an incantation to be recited seven times over an Anzu claw, then held so that the practitioner will receive what is desired. Whether this meant an alleged relic, a fossil, a large bird’s talon, or a manufactured amulet is uncertain.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Origins and family

No single genealogy became canonical.

- In *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*, Anzud calls Enlil “my father,” but the phrase may indicate patronage or appointment as much as biological descent. Anzud has an unnamed wife and at least one chick.
- The Standard Babylonian epic’s damaged opening associates Anzu’s generation with the waters or cosmic environment and may connect him with the earth/mountain and the birth-goddess tradition. Reconstructions naming Abzu and Mami as literal parents should be marked uncertain because the relevant lines are fragmentary.
- A late or isolated tradition makes Anzu a child of the beer or wine goddess Siris. This is not the dominant genealogy and may reflect lexical or learned association.
- No secure ancient text gives Anzu a named consort.
- The plural or reproductive implication is real: Sumerian narrative presents a mate and offspring, so not every *anzud* reference need denote the unique thief of the Tablet.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Lugalbanda and the mountain Anzud

**Tier: securely attested Sumerian narrative; Old Babylonian manuscripts.**

Stranded in distant mountains during Enmerkar’s campaign toward Aratta, Lugalbanda decides that Anzud may help him find the army. He discovers the bird’s nest and chick, feeds the chick rich cakes, meat, and sheep fat, paints its eyes with kohl, scents its head with cedar, and decorates the nest.

The returning parents panic when the chick initially fails to answer. Their cries reach heaven and the Abzu and terrify the mountain gods. Finding the chick safe and magnificently treated, Anzud promises the unknown benefactor friendship and a destiny without mountain rivals.

Lugalbanda reveals himself and praises the bird’s cosmic proportions. Anzud offers military prowess, abundance, and other gifts; Lugalbanda refuses until asking for tireless, lightning-like speed. Anzud grants it, enabling his return to the army. The bird instructs him not to divulge the source of the power. Lugalbanda promises statues that will make Anzud’s name famous in Sumer and in the temples of the great gods.

This Anzud is no criminal. It is a parent, guardian, destiny-giver, and reciprocal patron. The episode probably participates in royal legitimation: Lugalbanda’s superhuman capacity comes from correct ritual hospitality and secrecy.

### B. The huluppu-tree bird

**Tier: securely attested regional Sumerian narrative.**

Inanna transplants a tree to her Uruk garden, intending eventually to make furniture from it. Three beings take possession: a serpent in the roots, Anzud with its young in the branches, and a female figure in the trunk. Gilgamesh kills the serpent. The bird gathers its young and goes to the mountains; the female figure also departs. The tree is cut down for Inanna.

Nothing in the episode calls the bird the Tablet thief. Modern presentations that make this a chronological sequel in one individual Anzu’s “life” go beyond the text.

### C. Theft of the Tablet of Destinies

**Early tier:** Old Babylonian Akkadian recension, fragmentary, with Ningirsu as champion.  
**Canonical elaboration:** three-tablet Standard Babylonian recension, especially first-millennium Assyrian manuscripts, with Ninurta.

Anzu is brought into Enlil’s household and repeatedly sees Enlil’s Tablet, crown, robes, and authority. He resolves to seize the Tablet, control the divine rites, command the Igigi, and occupy the throne. While Enlil bathes and lays aside his crown, Anzu takes the Tablet and flees to a mountain.

Enlil becomes powerless or speechless; divine activity and ritual order stop. Anu summons champions. Adad, Girra, and Šara decline because the Tablet makes Anzu’s command unalterable.

Ea devises another strategy. Through Bēlet-ilī/Mami he calls Ninurta, her son, to battle. Ninurta attacks with bow and arrows, winds, and divine radiance. Anzu commands:

- shaft, return to reed thicket;
- bow stave, return to the forest;
- bowstring, return to the animal’s sinews;
- feathers, return to the birds.

The missile consequently disassembles in flight. The episode does not straightforwardly describe modern “time reversal”; it dramatizes authoritative speech returning a crafted object to its prior materials.

Ea’s counter-plan is transmitted to Ninurta: exploit the moment when Anzu’s wings are extended, cut or dislodge them, and let the ensuing confusion carry the attack home. Textual details vary, but Ninurta ultimately kills Anzu, pierces vital organs, recovers the Tablet, and returns in triumph. Tablet III celebrates the hero with names and honors.

The story makes divine authority juridical and material: the Tablet resembles a sealed document conferring command. It also legitimates Ninurta/Ningirsu as the victorious defender of Enlil’s order and, in Assyria, offered an analogue for the king who defeats rebels through both force and learned counsel.

### D. Ninurta, Enki, and the recovered Tablet

**Tier: Old Babylonian theological variant.**

In *Ninurta and the Turtle*, Anzu’s defeat lies in the background, but the recovered Tablet enters Enki’s Abzu. Ninurta tries to retain or obtain it; Enki creates a turtle that digs a pit and traps him. The narrative relocates ultimate wisdom and custody to Enki. It should not be fused silently with the Standard Babylonian ending in which restoration to Enlil is paramount.

### E. Anzu in Gilgamesh’s dream

**Tier: later canonical imagery, not an independent Anzu cycle.**

On the Cedar Forest journey, Gilgamesh dreams of a dreadful Anzu-like monster rising like a cloud, with fiery maw and deadly breath. A powerful youth—interpreted as Šamaš—intervenes. The dream uses Anzu as an established image of overwhelming danger and divine rescue; it does not narrate another historical act by the Tablet thief.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Independent cult

Evidence for a large, autonomous Anzu cult is weak. No secure record rivals the organized priesthoods, festival calendars, landholdings, or offering systems of Enlil, Inanna, Ningirsu, or Ninurta. Anzu is best documented as:

- a supernatural literary being;
- a divine emblem;
- a component of Ningirsu’s temple theology;
- a protective or authoritative temple image;
- an object of limited magical use.

The absence of an extensive cult dossier matters. Modern claims that Anzu had a standardized priesthood, sacred calendar, planetary correspondence, or widespread temples are unestablished.

### Lagash and Girsu

The densest evidence is institutional:

- Mesilim’s mace-head was dedicated to Ningirsu.
- Ur-Nanshe-period plaques place the lion-eagle in temple-building and offering contexts.
- Enmetena’s silver vase formed part of Ningirsu’s table service.
- Gudea’s inscriptions call Eninnu the temple of the shining or white Anzud and assimilate Ningirsu’s epiphany to the bird.

This was not necessarily “worship of Anzu instead of Ningirsu.” The bird articulated Ningirsu’s storm, mastery, military power, and control of the animal world.

### Tell al-ʿUbaid

The monumental relief over Ninhursag’s temple entrance shows that the motif was not exclusive to Lagash or Ningirsu. Its architectural position suggests protection, divine domination, or heraldic authority. The absence of an identifying inscription prevents certainty about the local name and theology.

### Umma

Ur III administrative evidence refers to a temple or sanctuary written with IM.DUGUD at Umma and to the placement of deified royal statues there. This is stronger evidence for an actual institutional locus connected with the name than the general statement that Anzu “had no temples,” but the sanctuary’s theology and whether IM.DUGUD denotes the bird, a divine name, or an inherited temple designation require further prosopographic and lexical study.

### Nimrud/Kalhu

Ashurnasirpal II built Ninurta’s temple early in the ninth century BCE. Monumental panels at its entrance depicted Ninurta’s pursuit of Anzu. The scene aligned:

- Ninurta’s mythic victory;
- Assyrian military kingship;
- divinatory scholarship and the gods’ decrees;
- the suppression of rebellion.

The temple remained standing until Kalhu’s destruction in 612 BCE. Its cult was Ninurta’s, not an Anzu cult; Anzu occupied the ritual architecture as defeated chaos.

### Offerings, sacrifice, and priesthood

No secure evidence links Anzu specifically to:

- human sacrifice;
- “temple prostitution”;
- a hereditary Anzu priesthood;
- a named annual Anzu festival;
- an Anzu oracle;
- gendered initiations.

The Enmetena vase demonstrates offerings and divine table service in which Anzu imagery participated, but not offerings made directly to Anzu. *Lugalbanda* describes hospitality to the chick—cakes, honey, meat, fat, cosmetics, and cedar scent—in narrative rather than documented human ritual.

### Magic

A first-millennium ritual tablet instructs the practitioner to recite an incantation seven times over “Anzu’s claw,” take it in hand, and thereby obtain desired gifts. This is direct evidence of Anzu’s entry into magical materia, though the physical identity of the claw is unknown.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before ca. 2600 BCE: iconographic precursors

Lion-eagle and master-of-animals compositions occur in early Mesopotamian art. Attribution to Anzu is possible but not secure without inscription or continuous context. There is no warrant for pushing the named god back into prehistory merely because a composite bird appears.

### Ca. 2600–2350 BCE: Lagash emblem and temple power

The lion-eagle becomes especially prominent in Lagash and Girsu. It appears on royal and priestly objects dedicated to Ningirsu and on cult vessels. In this period its role is predominantly prestigious and protective, not that of a defeated demon.

### Late third millennium BCE: theological assimilation

Gudea’s Eninnu inscriptions and dream narrative bind Anzud imagery tightly to Ningirsu. The temple itself can bear the bird’s name or epithet. This is a period of deliberate temple theology rather than evidence that two gods had always been identical.

### Early second millennium BCE: literary diversification

Old Babylonian schools copy *Lugalbanda*, the huluppu-tree episode, *Ninurta and the Turtle*, and the Akkadian Anzu combat story. The same name now covers:

- benevolent mountain parent and destiny-giver;
- tree-nesting bird;
- defeated cosmic rebel.

The variants coexist. There is no textual evidence that scribes felt obliged to reconcile them.

### Later second millennium BCE: canon formation

The Anzu combat poem develops into a learned Akkadian classic. Ningirsu and Ninurta, originally distinct city gods later extensively identified, exchange the champion’s role. The myth increasingly serves Ninurta theology and provides a model for divine promotion through monster combat.

### Ninth–seventh centuries BCE: Assyrian monumental and scholarly peak

Ashurnasirpal II monumentalizes the myth in Ninurta’s Kalhu temple. First-millennium copies circulate at Assur, Nineveh, Sultantepe, Tarbiṣu, Borsippa, and elsewhere. Court scholarship associates the Tablet of Destinies with the negotiability and interpretation of the divine future.

Royal inscriptions compare enemy kings and rebels to monsters overcome by the king under divine sponsorship. This is political use of the myth, not evidence of real Anzu worship.

### 612 BCE and afterward

Kalhu’s temples were plundered when the Assyrian imperial center fell. Babylonian scribal tradition nevertheless continued transmitting learned mythology into the late first millennium BCE. Direct evidence for Anzu diminishes with the end of cuneiform scholarship.

No securely documented chain carries Anzu worship through late antiquity, medieval Christianity, Islam, or local Iraqi folklore. Later giant-bird traditions may invite comparison, but continuity must be demonstrated, not assumed.

### Nineteenth–twentieth-century rediscovery

Excavation at Nimrud produced the battle reliefs before scholars understood them. Layard published them in *The Monuments of Nineveh*. George Smith’s 1876 *Chaldean Account of Genesis* knew the criminal “divine Zu” and inferred from another tablet that it was birdlike. For decades the Nimrud scene was identified as Marduk versus Tiamat.

Improved cuneiform readings, newly joined fragments, and iconographic analysis—especially Fuhr-Jaeppelt’s 1972 study and Hruška’s and Annus’s editions—established the reading Anzu and the identification of the reliefs as Ninurta and Anzu.

### Present

Anzu survives principally through museums, Assyriology, translations, fiction, games, reconstructionist interest, and scientific nomenclature. There is no evidence for a numerically significant continuous Anzu cult. Individual modern polytheists may honor the being, but such practice is revival or personal reconstruction.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Occultism and esotericism

Unlike Ishtar, Pazuzu, Lilith, or the names in the so-called *Simon Necronomicon*, Anzu had little securely documented role in Renaissance magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or early modern grimoires. Claims of an ancient continuous “Anzu current,” planetary seal, fixed sigil, enn, ceremonial color, or demon rank are modern inventions unless tied to a demonstrable cuneiform source.

Modern demonology websites usually draw on the Akkadian theft story and then supply correspondence systems unknown to Mesopotamia. The ancient claw ritual is genuine evidence for magical use, but it does not validate later occult systems.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Mesopotamian reconstructionist communities have made primary texts and offerings newly relevant, but there are no reliable demographic data for Anzu-specific devotion. Reconstructionist treatment varies: some approach Anzud as a dangerous but honorable mountain power on the model of *Lugalbanda*; others emphasize the rebel of the Akkadian epic. Both are modern selections among ancient variants.

### Ancient-astronaut claims

Zecharia Sitchin’s *The Twelfth Planet* (1976) recast Mesopotamian gods as extraterrestrials from Nibiru. Later derivative literature inserts Anzu into narratives of spaceships, genetic engineering, mining, or advanced weapons. These are **modern inventions**. Neither *Enki and Ninmah* nor the Anzu epic describes spacecraft or laboratory technology. “Ninmah revived Anzu with pulsers and emitters” belongs to modern pseudo-translations and fiction, not an ancient tablet.

Anzu must also not be confused with the **Anunnaki** merely because the names begin similarly.

### Literature and games

- The *Shin Megami Tensei* franchise uses Anzu as a recruitable demon. This reinforces the modern taxonomic category “demon” and the lion-bird visual type.
- *Dragon* magazine no. 329 (2005) reinvented “anzu” as a class of four-winged Abyssal demons serving Pazuzu. The servitude to Pazuzu, poisonous breath, oil-coated feathers, and generic species status are modern game design.
- *World of Warcraft* uses Anzu as a bird deity or loa-like game figure. Its taxonomy and narrative are modern fantasy.
- Faith Hunter’s *Jane Yellowrock* novels reinterpret anzu as a shape-changing magical species associated with vampires.
- Mai K. Nguyen’s *Anzu and the Realm of Darkness* (2024) concerns a Japanese-American girl whose given name is Anzu and Japanese underworld traditions; it is not a reception of the Mesopotamian being despite the identical romanization.

### Scientific nomenclature

In 2014 Matthew Lamanna and colleagues named the large feathered caenagnathid dinosaur **Anzu wyliei**. The genus name explicitly honors the feathered Mesopotamian demon and alludes to the animal’s unusual, presumably feathered appearance. The press nickname “chicken from hell” strengthened Anzu’s popular identity as a bizarre infernal bird. This is the most visible modern secular reuse of the name.

### Political or extremist appropriation

The Early Dynastic lion-eagle is sometimes described loosely as the “Eagle of Lagash” and reused as a heritage emblem. No well-documented modern extremist movement has made Anzu a central symbol. Claims equating it directly with later imperial double-headed eagles, the Nazi eagle, the Faravahar, or every heraldic griffin are diffusionist speculation unless supported by an independently traceable chain of transmission.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The “Zu” problem

Early scholars interpreted AN as a divine determinative and read the name dZu. Once syllabic spellings and textual structure were better understood, Anzu became standard. The old error still generates false etymologies and the impression that “Zu” and “Anzu” are different beings.

### Is the lion-headed eagle always Anzu?

Fuhr-Jaeppelt’s diachronic iconographic work made the equation persuasive, especially in Lagash contexts. Nevertheless:

- many images are uninscribed;
- texts sometimes describe a saw-like beak rather than a lion’s head;
- similar master-of-animals compositions could carry different local meanings;
- the Tell al-ʿUbaid relief belongs to Ninhursag’s temple, not Ningirsu’s.

Accordingly, “lion-headed eagle” is an iconographic type; “Anzu” is secure where text, provenance, and comparative sequence converge.

### Beneficent deity versus evil monster

Jacobsen and other historians emphasized a development from storm manifestation or numinous bird to monster defeated by a warrior god. The evidence does show different valuations, but it cannot supply a continuous biography. The benevolent *Lugalbanda* bird and the rebel epic may represent parallel genres and local traditions, not chronological stages in one universal doctrine.

### Ningirsu versus Ninurta

The Old Babylonian recension’s champion is associated with Ningirsu; the Standard Babylonian recension uses Ninurta. Because the two warrior gods were extensively syncretized, older scholarship sometimes treats the names as interchangeable. Historically, however, the substitution records the transfer and standardization of a local Lagash victory tradition within Ninurta’s Nippur and Assyrian theology.

### Tablet of Destinies

The singular Akkadian expression is better translated **Tablet of Destinies**, not “Tablets of Destiny.” Andrew George argued from Sennacherib’s inscriptions and legal-document analogies that it functioned like a sealed writ conferring divine rule. Whether it literally contained all future events, embodied executive authority, or did both differs by text.

The popular description of the Tablet as a machine that “reverses time” overstates the arrow episode. Anzu’s authoritative command returns each manufactured component to its origin. Temporal reversal is a possible modern conceptualization, not the poem’s technical explanation.

### Influence on *Enūma eliš*

Both poems feature:

- divine crisis and assembly;
- failed or reluctant champions;
- a young warrior’s elevation;
- wind-assisted monster combat;
- the Tablet of Destinies;
- celebratory names after victory.

Many scholars see literary dependence or shared Ninurta-combat tradition behind *Enūma eliš*. The direction and extent remain debated. The extant creation epic never identifies Anzu with Tiamat or Qingu.

### Archaeological and manuscript state

Key material is distributed among:

- British Museum: Tell al-ʿUbaid relief; Nimrud battle reliefs; many Nineveh tablets;
- Louvre: Enmetena vase and Lagash plaques;
- Iraq Museum: Lagash material including early mace-heads;
- Vorderasiatisches Museum and other collections: additional Early Dynastic material;
- CDLI: tablet images, metadata, and composites;
- ORACC/CAMS: transliterations, glossary data, contextual essays;
- ETCSL: Sumerian compositions;
- electronic Babylonian Literature: continuing critical reconstruction of Akkadian texts.

The physical corpus is fragmentary and dispersed. Several reconstructions depend on joining fragments excavated at different times. The antiquities market has further obscured provenance for some objects.
