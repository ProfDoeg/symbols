# Ninazu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male Mesopotamian god (*diĝir*), variously classified as an underworld, agricultural, snake/dragon, martial, protective, and—more tenuously—healing deity. He was the tutelary god of Enegi in southern Mesopotamia and, at least in Sumerian tradition, of Ešnunna in the Diyala region.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Early Dynastic IIIa, approximately 2600–2500 BCE, in the Fara/Šuruppak god list and the *zà-mì* hymns from Tell Abu Ṣalābīkh. The Abu Ṣalābīkh god list writes approximately **[ᵈnin]-a-zu**. These sources establish that Ninazu was already a recognized god in the mid-third millennium BCE but do not by themselves establish his later genealogy, healing function, or complete mythology. Contemporary or slightly later Early Dynastic administrative records from Lagaš document offerings and theophoric names associated with him. [ORACC AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/ninazu/); [ePSD2 corpus entry](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0046736.html); [Kobayashi 1992](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/orient1960/28/0/28_0_75/_article/-char/en)

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Name and writing

The standard Sumerian form is:

- **Ninazu**, conventionally normalized **Nin-azu**
- Cuneiform: most commonly **𒀭𒊩𒌆𒀀𒍪**, transliterated **ᵈNIN.A.ZU**
- Attested orthographies include **ᵈnin-a-zu**, **ᵈnin-azu**, **ᵈnin-a-zu₅**, **ᵈnin-azu(ZU₅)**, and **ᵈnin₉-a-zu**.
- The divine determinative **𒀭 / d** classifies the name as divine but was not pronounced.
- In *An = Anum*, **ᵈMUŠ**, “Divine Snake,” can function as a learned logographic writing of Ninazu. [ePSD2](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0046736.html)

The usual etymology is **nin + a-zu**, “Lord Healer” or “Lord Physician.” Sumerian *nin* is not intrinsically feminine: in divine names it can mean “lord,” “lady,” or sovereign possessor. Wiggermann understands the two elements as appositional rather than as a genitive construction.

This translation must not be converted automatically into a complete medical theology. Ninazu is rarely depicted performing clinical healing, and his surviving cultic profile is less medical than those of Ninisina, Gula, Nintinugga, Bau, or Damu. “Healing god” is therefore **partly securely lexical, partly a modern scholarly reconstruction from the name and limited hymn material**, not his uniformly dominant ancient classification.

Thorkild Jacobsen proposed reading the name **nin-a-sud**, “Lord Who Pours Water.” That interpretation connects Ninazu with chthonic water, libation, irrigation, and Enegi’s characterization as a pipe to the underworld. It remains a minority proposal rather than an established reading. [ORACC AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/ninazu/)

### Principal titles and descriptions

The following are textually documented; English formulations vary by translator:

- **“Sacred one of the great underworld”** — *Sumerian Temple Hymns*, Enegi hymn, lines 179–185; Ur III/Old Babylonian textual tradition.
- **“Seed of the great lord,” “given birth by Ereškigal”** — same hymn.
- **“Ninazu of the words of prayer”** — same hymn.
- **“Steward of the underworld”** — later literary and lexical descriptions; securely chthonic.
- **“King of the snakes”** — Ur III and Old Babylonian incantational tradition.
- **“King who stretches measuring lines over the fields”** — *Enlil and Ninlil*, line 95/near lines 91–116 in ETCSL’s arrangement; Old Babylonian manuscripts of a Sumerian composition.
- **“Shepherd of the city which thrives like cattle”** — *Ninazu A*, lines 1–6.
- **“Reed-worker” and “herdsman”** — *Ninazu A*, lines 1–6.
- **“God of the Land”** — *Ninazu A*, line 31.
- **“Towering lion spitting venom over hostile lands”** — *Temple Hymns*, Ešnunna hymn, lines 425–446.
- **“Snarling like a dragon”** — same hymn.
- **“Storm enveloping the disobedient”** and **“vigour of battle”** — same hymn.

The Enegi titles are chthonic, pastoral, musical, agricultural, and funerary. The Ešnunna titles are royal and military. This divergence is primary evidence for historical variation, not merely poetic ornament.

### Foreign and learned equations

- **Tišpak:** At Ešnunna, Ninazu and Tišpak were partially identified but normally remained separate entries in god lists. Tišpak inherited Ninazu’s temple, divine household, dragon associations, and position as city god. This was a historical displacement or partial assimilation, not a timeless equivalence.
- **Ninurta:** A first-millennium version of the *Anzû* tradition reportedly gives “Ninazu” as an equation or name of Ninurta. This is **late learned systematization**, not evidence that the gods had always been identical.
- **Hurrian Širuḫi:** The Late Bronze Age trilingual Weidner god list from Ugarit equates Ninazu with **ši-ru-ḫi**, whose meaning and identity remain uncertain. The Ugaritic column is fragmentary; the sometimes-repeated restoration *il mutema*, “god of death,” is not secure enough to present as settled text. [ePSD2](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0046736.html)

There is no securely attested Greek or Roman interpretatio of Ninazu.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Early Dynastic evidence, c. 2600–2350 BCE

1. **Fara/Šuruppak god list.** A lexical-list attestation establishes Ninazu’s place in the divine inventory. It does not furnish a myth.
2. **Tell Abu Ṣalābīkh *zà-mì* hymns.** These Early Dynastic praise texts associate deities with cult centers; Ninazu’s inclusion is his earliest literary attestation. Identification with the god of Enegi is probable.
3. **Early Dynastic Lagaš economic documents.** Rations or offerings to Ninazu and theophoric personal names show an operative cult rather than only scribal knowledge. Toshiko Kobayashi’s study remains the focused publication. [Kobayashi 1992](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/orient1960/28/0/28_0_75/_article/-char/en)
4. **A-Anepada dedication.** A king of the First Dynasty of Ur dedicated a clay tablet to Ninazu, probably in the twenty-fifth century BCE. This is securely cultic but does not preserve a narrative.

### Late third millennium BCE

- **Gudea inscriptions, c. 2144–2124 BCE:** Gudea records building a sanctuary for Ninazu at Girsu. One scholarly argument further identifies Ninazu as a personal deity of Gudea, but this is not universally accepted.
- **Ur III administrative archives, c. 2112–2004 BCE:** Offerings occur at Enegi, Ešnunna, Ur, Lagaš/Girsu, Nippur, Umma, and the Puzriš-Dagan redistribution center.
- **The Sumerian Temple Hymns:** The collection is attributed in its colophon to Enheduana, but its textual history is composite; the surviving witnesses are substantially Ur III and Old Babylonian. The two Ninazu hymns are essential because they place distinct theological portraits side by side:
  - Enegi, lines 179–185: underworld conduit, child of Ereškigal, prayer and music.
  - Ešnunna, lines 425–447: warrior, son of Enlil and Ninlil, dragon-like destroyer of rebels. [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/tr4801.htm)

### Old Babylonian Sumerian literary corpus, c. 2000–1600 BCE

- **Enlil and Ninlil** (*ETCSL* 1.2.1): Enlil, banished toward the underworld after impregnating Ninlil with the moon god Suen, assumes the identities of underworld-bound officials. At the man-eating river Id-kura he again has intercourse with Ninlil and engenders Ninazu, “the king who stretches measuring lines over the fields.” Ninazu is one of three substitute underworld sons generated so that Suen may escape confinement below. The tablets are Old Babylonian school manuscripts; the date of the underlying composition is disputed. [ETCSL annotated line](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslgloss.php?charenc=gcirc&lookup=c121.95)
- **How Grain Came to Sumer** (*ETCSL* 1.7.6): Ninazu proposes to his brother Ninmada that they travel to the mountain where barley and flax grow and introduce grain into Sumer. Ninmada refuses because Enlil has not commanded it. The surviving composition is fragmentary, so modern retellings claiming that the brothers straightforwardly “brought agriculture to humanity” overstate the preserved outcome.
- **A balbale to Ninazu / Ninazu A** (*ETCSL* 4.17.1): A 32-line Sumerian hymn surviving principally on CBS 14214 and CBS 15205. It calls him shepherd and herdsman; says Nanna engendered him, then names Enlil as the father who engendered him; calls him born at Ur but enthroned at Enegi; and names Ningirida as his wife. The internally layered genealogy is evidence of synthesis, not a coherent biological account. [Text and manuscripts](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/b4171.htm); [translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/tr4171.htm)
- **Šulgi D and Šulgi X:** Royal praise poetry invokes Ninazu in contexts involving divine power, health, agricultural order, or royal legitimacy. These are sources for his place in Ur III learned theology, though the extant tablets are mostly Old Babylonian copies.
- **Lamentation literature:** In *In the Desert in the Early Grass*, under the name Umun-azu, he belongs to a group of lamented chthonic or disappearing gods.
- **Incantations:** Ur III and Old Babylonian incantations call him “king of snakes,” providing the clearest textual basis for his ophidian domain.

### Scholarly god lists and late texts

- **An = Anum**, principally second-millennium in compilation with first-millennium witnesses: places Ninazu, Ningirida, Ningišzida, and a large divine household in a systematic genealogy. It includes **ᵈMUŠ** as a learned writing of Ninazu. These entries are **priestly systematization**, useful evidence for learned theology but not necessarily for popular belief.
- **Trilingual Weidner list from Ugarit**, thirteenth century BCE: Ninazu = Širuḫi; the Ugaritic equivalent is damaged.
- **First-millennium incantations, cult lists, and literary texts:** Ninazu remains known but is no longer a major independent state god.
- **Late *Anzû* tradition:** equation with Ninurta, a scholarly assimilation.

### Explicit absences from the requested major corpora

Ninazu is **not an actor in the standard surviving texts** of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, *Atraḫasīs*, *Enūma eliš*, or *Enki and Ninmaḫ*. He does not participate in the narrative action of *Inanna’s Descent*. That poem mentions Gugalana as Ereškigal’s dead husband, which later genealogy can connect indirectly to Ninazu, but it does not itself narrate Ninazu as their son. The standard Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions in RINAP likewise provide no major Ninazu cult narrative. These absences matter: Ninazu’s dossier must not be inflated by importing every Mesopotamian underworld or creation myth. [RINAP corpus description](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/projectlist.html)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Underworld and funerary domain

At Enegi he is securely chthonic. The *Temple Hymns* calls the city a “great libation pipe” to Ereškigal’s underworld and a **Gudua**, an entrance to the netherworld. Ninazu is born from Ereškigal and is “sacred” in the great underworld. His Ur festival included offerings to dead kings and deceased *entu* priestesses. This is the strongest continuously documented aspect of the southern god.

He is not, however, depicted as universal king of the dead. Ereškigal, Nergal, and other deities occupy that rank in different periods.

### Agriculture and vegetation

The measuring line over fields in *Enlil and Ninlil*, the grain-and-flax expedition proposed in *How Grain Came to Sumer*, the pastoral language of *Ninazu A*, and the cult’s funerary/agricultural seasonality support an agricultural aspect. Calling Ninazu a fully developed “dying-and-rising vegetation god” is a **modern comparative reconstruction**. Lamentations and underworld associations exist, but no surviving myth gives a complete death-and-resurrection narrative for Ninazu.

### Snakes and dragons

Ninazu is “king of snakes”; *An = Anum* can write his name as Divine Snake; the Ešnunna hymn likens him to a venom-spitting lion and snarling dragon. His best-supported emblematic creature is the **mušḫuššu**, the composite snake-dragon later famous as Marduk’s animal.

Wiggermann reconstructs the animal’s history as follows:

1. **Ninazu at Ešnunna:** probable original divine master.
2. **Tišpak:** inherited the animal when he displaced Ninazu.
3. **Marduk and Nabû:** acquired it after Babylon’s domination of Ešnunna, particularly from the Middle Babylonian period onward.
4. **Aššur:** appropriated it in Assyrian imperial theology after Sennacherib’s conquest of Babylon.

This is a scholarly reconstruction grounded in changing divine associations. No surviving ancient narrative says in these words that Tišpak “stole” Ninazu’s dragon. A myth in which Tišpak defeats a serpent monster may encode the transition, but that interpretation remains historical inference. [Wiggermann discussion](https://mc.dlib.nyu.edu/files/books/brill_awdl000079/brill_awdl000079_lo.pdf)

### Healing

The name’s conventional interpretation and Ninazu’s appearance near deities of health justify “healing” as a secondary domain. There is no substantial surviving medical ritual corpus centered on him. Modern claims that he invented medicine, was patron of physicians, or directly originated the modern caduceus are **unsupported modern inventions**.

### Iconography

No image bears a surviving caption securely identifying its figure as Ninazu. Proposed identifications include:

- An Early Dynastic cylinder seal in the British Museum, **ME 1933,0408.1**, showing a trident-bearing god standing on a lion whose body terminates in a snake. AMGG cautiously suggests Ninazu; this is **disputed iconographic attribution**, not certainty.
- Snake-dragon figures in Ešnunna glyptic may belong to Ninazu or Tišpak depending on date and context.
- The famous libation vase of Gudea, with entwined serpents and mušḫuššu creatures, belongs securely to **Ningišzida**, not Ninazu. Identifying one serpent or attendant as Ninazu is speculative.

No securely attested sacred color, number, weekday, planet, or star can be assigned to Ninazu. Online tables supplying such correspondences are modern occult constructions.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Parentage | Source and status |
|---|---|---|
| Enegi/chthonic | Ereškigal + “Great Lord,” probably Gugalana | *Temple Hymns* 179–185; later god-list theology. **Secure regional variant**; the father’s identification is reconstructed. |
| Ešnunna/Nippur | Enlil + Ninlil | *Temple Hymns* 425–447; *Enlil and Ninlil*. **Secure regional/literary variant.** |
| Ur lunar synthesis | Nanna/Suen as engenderer; Enlil also called father | *Ninazu A* 7–22. **Layered Old Babylonian synthesis.** |
| Anonymous animal parents | “Mighty cow” + “untamable bull” | Lexical/genealogical traditions. **Learned variant.** |
| Mami as mother | Late hymn/aretalogical theology | Probably dependent on identification with Ninurta, whom *Anzû* calls Mami’s son. **Late learned systematization.** |
| Gula as mother | Sometimes repeated in modern summaries | Rejected by current scholarship as unsupported. **Modern error.** |

His standard consort is **Ningirida**. Ereškigal and **(U)kulla(b)**, Tišpak’s spouse, occasionally occupy the consort position through local or syncretistic rearrangement. His best-attested son is **Ningišzida**. A healing goddess **Nintinugga** is called his daughter in an incantation. *An = Anum* supplies further children and siblings, but several names are obscure and belong to learned cataloguing rather than narrative myth.

### Documented divine interactions

The following list distinguishes actual episodes from genealogical or cultic juxtaposition:

- **Ninazu—Enlil:** Enlil begets Ninazu while disguised as the man of the Id-kura; *Enlil and Ninlil*, Old Babylonian manuscripts. **Later canonical Sumerian literary tradition.**
- **Ninazu—Ninlil:** Ninlil conceives Ninazu in the same episode and follows Enlil toward the netherworld; same source and date. **Later canonical.**
- **Ninazu—Nanna/Suen:** Nanna is alternately Ninazu’s brother and engendering father; he rejoices over Ninazu, illuminates his sceptre, and straightens his path in *Ninazu A*. **Old Babylonian learned/cultic synthesis.**
- **Ninazu—Ninmada:** Ninazu invites his brother to obtain barley and flax from the mountain; Ninmada refuses without Enlil’s authorization; *How Grain Came to Sumer*, Old Babylonian manuscript tradition. **Canonical but fragmentary.**
- **Ninazu—Ereškigal:** She gives birth to him in the Enegi *Temple Hymn*. No surviving narrative conversation occurs. **Secure regional genealogy.**
- **Ninazu—Gugalana/“Great Lord”:** Ninazu is called the seed of the Great Lord. Identification of that father with Gugalana is probable but not explicit in the hymn. **Regional genealogy plus modern reconstruction.**
- **Ninazu—Ningirida:** Husband and wife in *Ninazu A* and god lists; she approaches him in prayer. **Secure cultic genealogy.**
- **Ninazu—Ningišzida:** Father and son in god lists and hymnic references; no surviving extended meeting or adventure between them. **Secure genealogy; no narrative interaction.**
- **Ninazu—Nintinugga:** Father and daughter in an incantation. **Regional/incantational genealogy.**
- **Ninazu—Nergal/Meslamtaea:** Brothers in *Enlil and Ninlil*; Ninazu of Enegi could already be described as “Nergal of Enegi” in an Early Dynastic Šuruppak context. No surviving combat or dialogue. **Early functional association and later sibling genealogy.**
- **Ninazu—Enbilulu:** Brothers born as substitute underworld sons in *Enlil and Ninlil*. No direct dialogue. **Later canonical genealogy.**
- **Ninazu—An:** An gives or establishes Ninazu’s throne and determines a good destiny for him in *Ninazu A*. **Old Babylonian hymnic interaction.**
- **Ninazu—Tišpak:** Tišpak replaces Ninazu as Ešnunna’s patron and inherits his temple, family milieu, and dragon. This is documented cult history rather than a narrated divine encounter. **Historical syncretistic displacement.**
- **Ninazu—Ninurta:** Equated in a first-millennium *Anzû* recension or commentary. No narrative meeting: the equation collapses their identities for learned purposes. **Late learned systematization.**
- **Ninazu—Širuḫi:** Equivalent columns in the Ugaritic trilingual god list. **Late Bronze Age scribal equation; no interaction.**
- **Ninazu—Gula:** Possible association through the Tišpak–Ukulla/Gula complex or Ninurta assimilation; no secure episode or original mother-son relation. **Late theological inference.**
- **Ninazu—Inanna/Ištar:** No documented direct interaction. Inanna’s descent concerns Ereškigal and Gugalana but does not introduce Ninazu.
- **Ninazu—Enki/Ea:** Joint listing or thematic proximity in healing and creation contexts does not yield a documented personal interaction.
- **Ninazu—Marduk, Nabû, or Aššur:** Their later possession of the mušḫuššu reflects transmission of an emblem; no surviving narrative has them meet Ninazu.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The descent of Enlil and Ninlil and the begetting of Ninazu

**Later canonical Sumerian literary tradition.** After Enlil impregnates Ninlil with Suen, the assembly expels him from Nippur. Ninlil follows. Enlil successively assumes the identities of figures stationed along the route below—the gatekeeper, the man of the netherworld river, and the ferryman. Through repeated sexual unions he engenders underworld-associated sons capable of taking Suen’s place below.

At the Id-kura, the “man-eating river,” Ninlil tells the disguised Enlil that her womb contains Suen’s bright seed. The disguised figure proposes that his own seed descend in place of his master’s. He then begets Ninazu, “the king who stretches measuring lines over the fields.” The myth connects Ninazu simultaneously with descent, substitution for a celestial god, and agricultural measurement.

The text’s sexual dynamics should not be sanitized. Ninlil initially refuses Enlil’s advances; subsequent encounters occur within a plan articulated in dialogue, but the poem is not a modern account of uncomplicated consensual romance. Interpretations differ over whether its central purpose was theological genealogy, an explanation of lunar escape from the underworld, ritual geography around Nippur, or seasonal fertility.

### How grain was to come to Sumer

**Fragmentary Old Babylonian Sumerian narrative.** Grain and flax exist in a distant mountain region while Sumer lacks them. Ninazu urges Ninmada to travel there and make barley known in Sumer. Ninmada objects that neither their father nor Enlil has issued the command. Because the remainder is damaged, it cannot securely be said that Ninazu completes the journey or personally invents agriculture. Modern summaries often silently supply a successful ending.

The story nevertheless confirms an agricultural and civilizing dimension distinct from the underworld and martial profiles.

### Tišpak and the serpent monster

**Old Babylonian Akkadian/local Ešnunna tradition, interpretation disputed.** A fragmentary myth, conventionally associated with Tišpak, recounts his defeat of a monstrous serpent or dragon. Wiggermann interpreted this as a mythic expression of Tišpak’s takeover of the mušḫuššu, formerly Ninazu’s creature. The monster is not necessarily Ninazu, and the text does not explicitly narrate Tišpak defeating Ninazu. “Tišpak killed Ninazu’s dragon and took his throne” is therefore a **modern historical reconstruction**, not the literal preserved plot.

### Lamented or disappearing Ninazu

Lamentation texts group Ninazu/Umun-azu with chthonic young gods such as Damu, Dumuzi, and Ningišzida. This supports ritual mourning and seasonal disappearance. It does not preserve a full Ninazu resurrection myth. Classification as a “dying-and-rising god” belongs to older comparativist scholarship and should be used only with that qualification.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Enegi

Enegi—probably located between Ur and Uruk, though its precise archaeological identification remains uncertain—was Ninazu’s principal southern center. His temple was:

- **E-gida / E-gidda**, **é-gíd-da**, “Long House,” sometimes interpreted as “Storehouse.”
- The *Temple Hymns* describes Enegi as a libation pipe to Ereškigal’s realm and a gathering point for humanity at the entrance to the underworld.
- Early Dynastic Lagaš records document offerings to Ninazu of Enegi.
- Ur III Puzriš-Dagan tablets record cultic transfers and offerings.
- Old Babylonian references to temple prebends suggest continuing institutional operation, although Enegi becomes difficult to trace as an inhabited city.
- A first-millennium Egidda at Ur may reflect transfer of the cult from Enegi, but the identification is not certain.

No excavated building has been securely identified as Enegi’s Egidda.

### Ešnunna

Ninazu’s northern temple was:

- **E-sikil / E-sikilla**, **é-sikil(.la)**, “Pure House.”

The site is Tell Asmar. Excavators searched for the Esikil but did not securely identify it archaeologically. A bilingual building tradition from Šulgi’s time is decisive: the **Sumerian version names Ninazu**, while the **Akkadian version names Tišpak** as the deity for whom Esikil was built. This directly documents equivalence-in-office without proving total identity. [Tell Asmar excavation report](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oic16.pdf)

By the Old Babylonian period Tišpak dominates Ešnunna’s civic theology. After Hammurabi’s conquest of the city, Ninazu disappears from its routine cult documentation.

### Other centers

- **Ur:** Major Ur III and Old Babylonian cult; a festival in the sixth month; continued personal names through Achaemenid rule.
- **Girsu/Lagaš:** Early offerings and a temple built by Gudea.
- **Nippur and Umma:** Ur III offering evidence.
- **Puzriš-Dagan:** Administrative documentation of offerings connected with Enegi and the royal cult.

### Festival and funerary rites

At Ur, a major sixth-month Ninazu festival included:

- offerings to Ninazu;
- **id-a-nag**, “water-drinking” or funerary-libation rites;
- nighttime offerings;
- commemoration of deceased Ur III kings, including Šulgi;
- offerings for dead *entu* priestesses such as Enmegalana and Enanatuma.

This evidence securely joins Ninazu’s festival to elite ancestor cult and funerary libation. It does not show human sacrifice.

### Sacrifice, priesthood, and contested practices

Animal, grain, beer, oil, and other food offerings are documented through administrative records. Temple personnel and holders of prebends are attested, but a complete Ninazu-specific priestly hierarchy cannot presently be reconstructed.

There is **no credible evidence for human sacrifice in Ninazu’s cult**. There is likewise **no evidence for “temple prostitution” dedicated to Ninazu**. Neither should be inferred merely because Mesopotamian temples employed gendered personnel or because Ninazu was an underworld god. No oracle or pilgrimage institution specific to him is securely documented.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### c. 2600–2350 BCE: emergence in the written record

Ninazu appears in god lists and hymns at Fara and Abu Ṣalābīkh and in Lagaš’s economic documentation. The evidence already points toward Enegi. An Early Dynastic epithet resembling “Nergal of Enegi” suggests that his chthonic classification was early.

### c. 2350–2112 BCE: Akkadian and post-Akkadian transformations

His cult remains active in the south. At Ešnunna, Ninazu and the probably non-Sumerian Tišpak enter a competitive or assimilative relationship. Whether southern Ninazu was imported to Ešnunna or whether two originally separate gods bearing the same name were later combined remains disputed.

Van Dijk argued for two Ninazus because the Enegi and Ešnunna characters and genealogies differ sharply. Wiggermann treated them as one deity whose local profiles diverged. The surviving record cannot conclusively decide between separate origin and early regional differentiation.

### c. 2112–2004 BCE: Ur III expansion

This is the widest securely documented geographic distribution: Enegi, Ešnunna, Ur, Girsu, Nippur, Umma, and administrative centers. Gudea and Ur III kings patronize him. His funerary festival develops at Ur; the literary traditions connect him with fields, snakes, the netherworld, Nanna, Enlil, and Ereškigal.

### c. 2000–1600 BCE: literary codification and displacement

Old Babylonian schools copy the principal Sumerian myths and hymns. *Ninazu A* synthesizes incompatible genealogies. At Ešnunna, Tišpak becomes dominant, inheriting Esikil, the divine household, and dragon symbolism. After Hammurabi’s conquest, Ninazu effectively vanishes as a living Ešnunna civic god.

### c. 1600–1000 BCE: learned survival

Independent cult contracts, offerings, and royal patronage diminish. God lists conserve and enlarge his family. The Ugaritic trilingual list carries his name into a multilingual scribal environment. The mušḫuššu passes into the iconographic systems of Marduk and Nabû.

### c. 1000–539 BCE: antiquarian and local persistence

Ninazu occurs sporadically in incantations, literary texts, and learned lists. At Ur, cultic memory and theophoric names continue. Elsewhere Nergal, Ninurta, Marduk, and other gods absorb much of his former conceptual territory.

### Achaemenid period, 539–331 BCE

The latest probable evidence for active attachment consists of personal names at Ur invoking Ninazu. Such names establish continuing familial or local devotion but not necessarily a functioning independent temple establishment.

### Hellenistic period to modern rediscovery

No continuous cult is documented after the cuneiform temple system disappeared. There is no securely traceable medieval folklore, Islamic-period survival, Renaissance magical tradition, or inherited community worship of Ninazu. His modern existence derives from deciphered tablets, museum collections, academic publication, and recent revivalist selection.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Ninazu never acquired the large modern cultural profile of Inanna, Marduk, Tiamat, or Nergal. His recovery followed the nineteenth- and twentieth-century decipherment of Sumerian and excavations at Tell Asmar, Telloh, Nippur, Ur, Fara, and Abu Ṣalābīkh.

Modern Mesopotamian reconstructionists and eclectic polytheists sometimes worship Ninazu as an underworld guardian, ancestor god, snake god, healer, or agricultural deity. Online first-person testimony establishes that such devotion exists, but no reliable census establishes its scale. It is a **modern religious revival after a gap of more than two millennia**, not documented institutional continuity.

The following popular claims are modern or unsupported:

- Ninazu was the direct historical inventor of medicine.
- His emblem was the modern medical caduceus.
- The Gudea vase directly depicts Ninazu.
- He was a uniformly benevolent “reiki,” herbal, kundalini, or chakra deity.
- He had fixed modern occult correspondences—planet, tarot card, crystal, color, weekday, or zodiac sign.
- He belonged to a continuous secret Mesopotamian magical order.
- He was an extraterrestrial “Anunnaki” technician.
- He died and rose annually in a fully documented Passion narrative.

There is no substantial Ninazu reception in the Golden Dawn, Thelema, Renaissance Hermetism, or early modern grimoires. Modern rites published under those labels are new compositions. No documented extremist or nationalist appropriation specific to Ninazu emerged in the searched record.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal disputes

1. **One Ninazu or two?**  
   The contrasting Enegi and Ešnunna profiles led van Dijk to posit distinct gods. Wiggermann instead reconstructed one snake-underworld deity differentiated by local theology. Current summaries generally treat them together while marking uncertainty.

2. **Meaning of the name.**  
   “Lord Healer” is the majority analysis; Jacobsen’s “Water-Pouring Lord” connects better with libation and chthonic water but requires a different segmentation.

3. **How medical was he?**  
   Older and popular reference works often infer a strong healing portfolio from the name. Recent treatments emphasize that direct medical evidence is sparse.

4. **Who were his parents?**  
   Ereškigal and a Great Lord belong to the Enegi tradition; Enlil and Ninlil to Ešnunna/Nippur; Nanna and Enlil coexist in the composite *Ninazu A*. Gula as mother is unsupported; Mami is late assimilation.

5. **What happened at Ešnunna?**  
   The bilingual Šulgi building evidence establishes a close Ninazu–Tišpak relation. Whether this represents translation, political replacement, foreign introduction, or merger remains debated. Their continued separation in god lists argues against complete syncretism.

6. **Was the mušḫuššu originally Ninazu’s?**  
   Wiggermann’s transmission sequence is influential and fits the historical succession at Ešnunna, but the earliest images are not inscribed with divine names.

7. **Was he a dying-and-rising god?**  
   Lamentation and underworld evidence is real; a complete death-and-return myth is absent. The strong formulation owes much to twentieth-century comparativist categories.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is dispersed among administrative tablets, god lists, school manuscripts, incantations, royal inscriptions, seals, and excavation publications. Important manuscripts are held in the University of Pennsylvania Museum/Babylonian Section, the Iraq Museum, Yale Babylonian Collection, British Museum, and collections preserving the Fara and Abu Ṣalābīkh finds.

ETCSL provides composite Sumerian literary texts and translations; ORACC supplies lexical, divine-name, and royal-inscription corpora; CDLI supplies object-level metadata and images where available. A composite text is a modern philological reconstruction from multiple manuscripts and must not be mistaken for a single intact ancient “book.”

The major modern treatments include Thorkild Jacobsen’s *The Treasures of Darkness*; Jeremy Black and Anthony Green’s *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*; Frans Wiggermann’s RlA articles on Ninazu and the mušḫuššu; Wilfred G. Lambert’s studies of Babylonian mythology; Ernst Weidner and Richard Litke on god lists; Andrew George on temple names; Mark Cohen on cult calendars; and Toshiko Kobayashi on Early Dynastic Lagaš documentation.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Event and evidence |
|---|---|
| c. 2600–2500 BCE | Ninazu appears in the Fara god list and Abu Ṣalābīkh *zà-mì* hymns: earliest secure attestation. |
| c. 2500–2350 BCE | Offerings and theophoric names in Early Dynastic Lagaš; A-Anepada dedicates a tablet to him. |
| ED IIIa–IIIb | A Šuruppak text may call Enegi’s god “Nergal of Enegi,” early evidence for chthonic classification. |
| c. 2144–2124 BCE | Gudea builds a Ninazu sanctuary at Girsu. |
| c. 2112–2004 BCE | Ur III cult documented at Enegi, Ešnunna, Ur, Lagaš/Girsu, Nippur, Umma, and Puzriš-Dagan. |
| Reign of Šulgi, c. 2094–2046 BCE | Bilingual building tradition associates Esikil with Ninazu in Sumerian and Tišpak in Akkadian. |
| Late third/early second millennium BCE | *Temple Hymns* traditions preserve the contrasting Enegi and Ešnunna forms. |
| c. 2000–1800 BCE | Old Babylonian manuscripts preserve *Enlil and Ninlil*, *How Grain Came to Sumer*, and *Ninazu A*. |
| Early second millennium BCE | Incantations call Ninazu “king of snakes”; Ur festival integrates royal funerary libations. |
| c. 1900–1762 BCE | Tišpak dominates Ešnunna; Ninazu recedes from routine local documentation. |
| 1762 BCE, middle chronology | Hammurabi conquers Ešnunna; Ninazu subsequently disappears there as an independently documented civic god. |
| Later second millennium BCE | *An = Anum* systematizes Ninazu’s family; Ugaritic trilingual list equates him with Širuḫi. |
| Middle Babylonian onward | Mušḫuššu increasingly belongs to Marduk and Nabû. |
| First millennium BCE | Sporadic survival in incantations, god lists, and literary theology; learned equation with Ninurta. |
| Neo-Assyrian period | Aššur acquires mušḫuššu symbolism; Ninazu has no major role in preserved Neo-Assyrian royal ideology. |
| 539–331 BCE | Theophoric names at Ur provide the last probable evidence of living devotion. |
| Hellenistic and later antiquity | No continuous independent Ninazu cult securely documented. |
| 1930–1937 CE | Tell Asmar excavations clarify Ešnunna’s religious history, though Esikil is not securely located. |
| 1960 | J. van Dijk publishes the fundamental edition of *Ninazu A* in *Sumerische Götterlieder*. |
| 1987 | Jacobsen publishes the “Water-Pouring Lord” interpretation in *The Harps That Once*. |
| 1992 | Kobayashi publishes the focused study of Ninazu in Early Dynastic Lagaš economic texts. |
| 1990s–2000s | ETCSL, CDLI, and ORACC make literary, lexical, and tablet evidence digitally accessible. |
| Twenty-first century | Small-scale reconstructionist and eclectic worship appears online; no evidence supports institutional continuity from antiquity. |
| 2026 | Ninazu remains principally an object of Assyriological research and limited modern religious revival. |
