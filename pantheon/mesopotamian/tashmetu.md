# Tashmetu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*iltu*) in Akkadian-speaking Mesopotamian traditions; never securely classified as a demon, monster, spirit, or collective.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Nineteenth century BCE, in Old Assyrian evidence associated with Aššur and the merchant colony at Kaneš. A supposedly earlier Ur III occurrence, VAT 6563, is not secure: the tablet is lost and may actually have been Old Babylonian. The earliest presently identifiable evidence connecting Tashmetu with Nabû is a late-eighteenth-century BCE copy of the Weidner god list from Tell Taban.

**Historical orientation:** Tashmetu was an Akkadian-named goddess whose identity centered on hearing, favorable response, intercession, intelligence, beauty, and erotic attraction. Her pairing with Nabû was not primordial. The available evidence indicates that the two once had independent cultic histories and became a recognized couple during the Old Babylonian period. By the first millennium BCE she was Nabû’s established wife in Assyrian theology and ritual, most visibly at Kalḫu, while in Babylonia she coexisted and partly competed with Nanaya as Nabû’s female partner. The exceptionally intimate Neo-Assyrian “Love Lyrics of Nabû and Tashmetu” document the ceremonial union of their cult statues.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Name

The normalized Akkadian name is **Tašmētu**, conventionally anglicized **Tashmetu**, **Tashmetum**, or older **Tashmit/Tashmitum**. Cuneiform spellings include:

- `dtaš-me-tum`
- `dtaš-me-tu₂`
- `dtaš-me-tu₄`
- Occasionally abbreviated or logographic writings in lexical and cultic texts.

The final *-m* in *Tašmētum* is the Old Babylonian nominative ending and is normally omitted in modern normalized citation.

### Etymology

**Secure linguistic basis, disputed exact interpretation:** The name is built from the Akkadian root **šemû**, “to hear.” Older and modern translations include “Hearing,” “She who listens,” “Compliance,” “Reconciliation,” or “Answering.” The difficulty is that *tašmētu* is not independently well attested as an ordinary Akkadian abstract noun: it is chiefly a divine and personal name.

Zachary Rubin therefore proposes understanding it verbally as “she hears.” He further suggests that Tashmetu and Nabû may have been paired partly because their names formed a theological complement: Nabû, connected with *nabû*, “to call/name,” and Tashmetu, connected with “to hear.” This is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not an ancient explanatory text. Francesco Pomponio’s translation “intelligence” depends on her partnership with the learned god Nabû; it describes her developed character well but lacks direct lexical support for *tašmētu* meaning “intelligence.” [ORACC-AMGG](https://oracc2.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/Listofdeities/Tashmetu/index.html) [Rubin 2024](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380095362_The_Adoption_of_Nabu_and_Tasmetu_into_the_Babylonian_Pantheon)

### Sumerian and learned equivalents

- **Ningutešasiga**, approximately “Lady who hears favorably” or “Lady of favorable hearing,” occurs as a Sumerian equivalent in bilingual learned texts beginning in the Middle Babylonian period. This is a **learned systematization**, probably constructed to explicate the Akkadian name rather than evidence for an independent early Sumerian goddess.
- **Ninsiga**, “Good/Favorable Lady,” occurs in an inscription from Sippar. Elsewhere that name can designate a figure in Ninisina’s circle, so identity cannot be assumed outside the relevant context.
- **Emagar-qabûša**, “Her speaking showed favor,” occurs in a *lipšur* litany. It may be an epithet or alternate name of Tashmetu, but it could instead denote an otherwise unknown attendant goddess. The identification is **uncertain**.
- A Nuzi divine name written `dU-te-eš-me-tu₄`, known from the name of a gate at Zizza, has been treated as Tashmetu. Rubin argues that it may instead mean “Ištar is the one who hears” and relate to Ištar-tašmê. The supposed Nuzi Tashmetu is therefore **disputed**, not a secure regional form.
- **Tešmit-māti**, appearing in Sealand offering lists, is probably unrelated. It may be the divinized wife of King Gulkišar or a deity in Šamaš’s circle.
- A Hittite text, KUB 60.147, uses Tashmetu’s written name logographically for an unidentified deity of Ištaḫara. This is a **scribal equation**, not proof that Tashmetu herself possessed an Anatolian cult.

### Titles and descriptions

Attested or textually grounded titles include:

- **bēlet tešmê u salīme**, “Lady of hearing/obedience and peace/reconciliation.”
- **šarrat Borsippa**, “Queen of Borsippa.”
- “Lady whose command is heard.”
- “She whose word is heard by the great gods.”
- “Mistress of the lovers of the inhabited world.”
- “Goddess of allure/sexual attractiveness.”
- Nabû’s **ḫīrtu naramtu**, “beloved wife.”
- “Mountain of our trust,” in the love lyrics.
- “She of the wall,” in the same composition; its precise topographical or theological reference is uncertain.

These titles do not all belong to one period. The “hearing” vocabulary belongs to the interpretation of her name and intercessory function; “Queen of Borsippa” belongs to first-millennium Borsippan theology; and the overtly erotic language is particularly prominent in Neo-Assyrian prayers and the love ritual. [ORACC prayer text](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/cams/gkab/P363582) [SAA 3 no. 14](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/saa03/P223388)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Possible Ur III occurrence

Anne Löhnert identified VAT 6563 as a possible Ur III attestation. The object is now lost, and its dating is uncertain; it may be Old Babylonian. It cannot anchor a securely third-millennium history.

#### Old Assyrian evidence, nineteenth century BCE

The earliest secure horizon is Old Assyrian. Evidence includes:

- References to Tashmetu having a cultic place or installation associated with the cellae of Aššur and Ištar-Aššurītu at Aššur.
- A Kaneš letter reporting a votive gift to her by the merchant Pūšu-kēn.
- Theophoric names such as **Ikūn-pî-Tašmētim**, “Tashmetu’s word is firm,” and **Šāt-Tašmētim**, “She who belongs to Tashmetu.”

This evidence establishes worship, personal devotion, and a northern Mesopotamian presence. It does **not** yet securely establish Nabû as her husband. Joan Goodnick Westenholz consequently proposed an Assyrian origin; Rubin’s more recent reconstruction treats the goddess’s early history as independent and her later incorporation into Babylonian divine families as a process. [Rubin 2024](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380095362_The_Adoption_of_Nabu_and_Tasmetu_into_the_Babylonian_Pantheon)

#### Old Babylonian evidence

Tashmetu entered northern Babylonian documentation at Sippar, Babylon, Borsippa, and Dilbat. She is absent from the Old Babylonian Nippur god list, a meaningful indication that her integration into southern learned pantheons remained incomplete.

The oldest presently identified conjunction of Nabû and Tashmetu occurs in a late-eighteenth-century BCE Weidner god-list manuscript from Tell Taban. This is the first secure evidence for the pair, not proof of an eternally established marriage.

In Hammurabi’s forty-first regnal year—approximately 1752 BCE under the middle chronology—the royal administration dedicated red gold and a precious stone to Tashmetu. This shows her incorporation into Babylon’s cult, possibly within Esagil, although the precise shrine is not preserved. An Old Babylonian Sippar letter invokes Ištar and Tashmetu together in a blessing.

### Major textual witnesses

#### God lists

- **Weidner god list, Tell Taban manuscript:** late eighteenth century BCE; earliest identified placement connecting Nabû and Tashmetu.
- **An = Anum:** standard Babylonian god list compiled through the second millennium, with first-millennium transmission. It assigns divine relationships and equivalents but is a learned taxonomy, not narrative mythology.
- **Bilingual lists and explanatory texts:** Middle Babylonian onward; establish Ningutešasiga as Tashmetu’s Sumerian equivalent.
- **An = Anu ša amēli:** late scholarly list; its equation of Inzak with Nabû led Manfred Krebernik to infer a parallel Meskilak–Tashmetu equation. That parallel is **modern reconstruction**, not an explicit surviving equation.

#### Royal and dedicatory inscriptions

- Old Babylonian administrative/royal evidence from Hammurabi’s reign records offerings to her.
- A Middle Assyrian-period seal dedication by the royal scribe Aššur-išmanni, under Tiglath-pileser I, invokes Tashmetu.
- Kassite-period kudurru evidence, especially a monument of Marduk-apla-iddina I, 1171–1159 BCE, groups Nabû, Tashmetu, and Nanaya as “lords of judgment and decision.”
- Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions invoke Nabû and Tashmetu jointly as protectors and guarantors. One inscription directs a future ruler who discovers the inscribed object to anoint it and make an offering before Nabû and Tashmetu. [ORACC-RIAO](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/riao/Q003868/)
- Aššurbanipal credits Nabû and Tashmetu with broad understanding and scribal knowledge in a library inscription. This is royal theological language and political self-presentation as well as evidence of devotion.

#### “Love Lyrics of Nabû and Tashmetu,” SAA 3 no. 14

The principal manuscript is **TIM 9 54 = IM 3233 = CDLI P223388**, recovered in the textual world of Aššurbanipal’s library and copied in the seventh century BCE. Its language is Akkadian. The composition may be older than the surviving tablet, but no securely dated earlier manuscript establishes how much older.

The text alternates divine or choral voices. Worshippers declare trust in Nabû and devotion to Tashmetu. She is asked to enter the cella amid juniper fragrance. Nabû asks why she is adorned; Tashmetu answers that she wishes to go with him into the garden. Images of fruit, birds, perfume, dress, the bedchamber, and the garden give the song unmistakably erotic and nuptial dimensions. “May my eyes see the plucking of your fruit” employs deliberate erotic ambiguity. The text probably accompanied the movement and ceremonial union of the divine statues.

Calling this an “Assyrian Song of Songs,” as Martti Nissinen did comparatively, is a modern interpretive analogy. It does not establish direct dependence between this work and the biblical Song of Songs. [Complete transliteration and translation](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/saa03/P223388)

#### Aššurbanipal’s Hymn to Tashmetu and Nabû, KAR 122

KAR 122, edited as SAA 3 no. 15, is a damaged seventh-century BCE hymn. It describes Tashmetu as attractive and wise and addresses her as Nabû’s “beloved wife.” The tablet links royal praise, divine marriage, beauty, and learned theology. [ORACC text](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/P336226)

#### Šuʾillakku prayer from Kalḫu

CTN 4 no. 168, found in Nabû’s temple at Kalḫu, contains prayers to several goddesses connected with Nabû: Nisaba, Ištar, Tashmetu, and Nanaya. Tashmetu is:

- “Queen of Borsippa.”
- A goddess whose commands and words are heard.
- A recipient from Anu of *tašmû* and *salīmu*, hearing/obedience and reconciliation.
- A figure of erotic attraction and the “mistress of lovers.”

The same tablet’s juxtaposition of several female partners of Nabû shows that scribal theology preserved overlapping relationships rather than enforcing one perfectly exclusive marriage system. [ORACC text](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/cams/gkab/P363582)

#### Neo-Assyrian ritual correspondence

SAA 13 contains priestly letters from the reigns of Esarhaddon and Aššurbanipal. They document preparations, offerings, personnel, movement of statues, and the divine couple’s ceremonial seclusion. One report says Tashmetu entered the bedroom and would emerge after the prescribed stay; related evidence describes the pair as spending **six days and seven nights** in the bedchamber while elaborate food was served. These are administrative witnesses to performed state cult, not free-standing romantic stories. [SAA 13 no. 70](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/saa13/P334242) [SAA 13 introduction](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/knpp/downloads/cole_machinist_saa13intro.pdf)

### Negative corpus finding

Tashmetu has no identifiable role in the standard surviving texts of **Enūma eliš**, **Atrahasis**, the **Gilgameš Epic**, **Inanna’s Descent**, or **Enki and Ninmaḫ**. ETCSL is correspondingly of little direct use because she is an Akkadian-named goddess with no securely demonstrated third-millennium Sumerian literary mythology. Her omission from those compositions is not evidence that she was unimportant everywhere; it shows that her surviving profile is cultic, hymnic, lexical, and administrative rather than epic.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Hearing and favorable response

Tashmetu is regularly described as a goddess who hears prayers, causes words to be heard, and secures favorable response. “Goddess of hearing” is a useful shorthand, but it should not be mistaken for an explicit ancient bureaucratic assignment of a single natural domain. The vocabulary includes listening, obedience, concord, reconciliation, and intercession.

In prayer she can carry a petitioner’s request into the divine council. This complements Nabû’s roles as divine speaker, scribe, recorder, and authoritative name-giver.

### Wisdom

Her association with intelligence is strongest in first-millennium learned and royal texts, where she accompanies Nabû and grants understanding. It may partly be secondary to their marriage. Pomponio’s translation of her very name as “intelligence” remains unsupported lexically.

### Love, fertility, and sexuality

The love lyrics and Kalḫu prayers securely associate Tashmetu with:

- Erotic adornment.
- Beauty and sexual attraction.
- Gardens and fruit.
- Conjugal intimacy.
- Lovers.
- Fertility expressed through the flourishing of the royal house.

She is nevertheless not simply another name for Ištar or Nanaya. Erotic functions overlapped among Mesopotamian goddesses without requiring total identity.

### Intercession and political protection

Royal invocations ask Nabû and Tashmetu for long life, dynastic continuity, health, and stable rule. At Kalḫu their marriage rite was performed partly for the king and his descendants, embedding divine sexuality in Neo-Assyrian state ideology.

### Iconography

No independent anthropomorphic image can presently be identified as Tashmetu solely from a unique symbol or attribute. Her principal image was the cult statue housed beside Nabû’s at Kalḫu; the statue itself has not survived as an identifiable object.

The twin cellae and architectural pairing supply stronger identification than portable art. A goddess shown beside Nabû cannot automatically be labeled Tashmetu, because Nanaya and other goddesses also entered his theological circle. Likewise, the very old theory that a prehistoric or early cylinder-seal figure with prominent ears represents “the hearer” Tashmetu is uncontrolled iconographic speculation. A unique attribute—animal, plant, weapon, number, color, weekday, or celestial body—has not been securely established for her.

Modern claims that Capricorn, a particular color, crystal, or magical seal belongs to Tashmetu are not supported by the evidence reviewed here.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Parentage

First-millennium material can present **Uraš of Dilbat** as Tashmetu’s father. This Uraš is the male tutelary god of Dilbat and must not be confused with the Sumerian earth goddess Uraš.

Löhnert regards this as preserving an older Dilbat tradition. Rubin cautions that Tashmetu is poorly represented in Dilbat’s theophoric names and proposes that the relationship may have formed only under Samsu-iluna or later. No surviving Old Babylonian text straightforwardly says “Uraš begot Tashmetu.”

### Marriage

Tashmetu’s best-attested relationship is with **Nabû**. The partnership first becomes visible in the late eighteenth century BCE and is explicit in later hymns, god lists, inscriptions, temple architecture, and ritual correspondence.

At Borsippa, Tashmetu could be treated as Nabû’s official wife while **Nanaya** occupied the place of beloved or mistress. Elsewhere, especially Uruk, Nanaya could function as Nabû’s principal partner. These arrangements are regional and historical, not two versions of a single universal canon.

### Children

No divine child of Nabû and Tashmetu is securely attested. Fertility language in the marriage ritual concerns cosmic, agricultural, or dynastic prosperity and does not establish named offspring.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Nabû — marriage and erotic union.** Tashmetu enters the sanctuary and bedchamber with Nabû; the pair converse and proceed to the garden. *Love Lyrics of Nabû and Tashmetu*, surviving seventh-century BCE manuscript; **later canonical Assyrian cultic form**.
- **Nabû — joint reception of worship.** Royal inscriptions, prayers, seals, and administrative letters invoke or provide offerings to them together, second to first millennia BCE; **securely attested cultic relationship**.
- **Nabû — joint bestowal of wisdom.** Aššurbanipal states that Nabû and Tashmetu granted him understanding, seventh century BCE; **Neo-Assyrian royal theology**.
- **Uraš of Dilbat — father and daughter.** First-millennium genealogy, possibly reflecting an older local association; **late-attested genealogy, antiquity disputed**.
- **Marduk — court relationship and later father-in-law relationship.** Tashmetu belonged to Marduk’s circle at Old Babylonian Sippar, perhaps before her marriage to Nabû was standardized; after Nabû became Marduk’s son she became Marduk’s daughter-in-law. **Historical assimilation and learned genealogy**.
- **Ṣarpānītu/Zarpanitu — shared divine household.** Tashmetu entered the Marduk–Ṣarpānītu circle at Sippar and later appeared with them in royal divine sequences. No independent narrative meeting survives; **cultic co-occurrence**.
- **Nanaya — co-wife, rival, or complementary partner of Nabû.** A kudurru of Marduk-apla-iddina I groups Nabû, Tashmetu, and Nanaya; later Borsippan and Kalḫu texts preserve both goddesses in Nabû’s circle. **Kassite and first-millennium theological arrangement**.
- **Nanaya — partial syncretism.** Both bear “Queen of Borsippa,” and a late litany transfers Tashmetu’s epithets to Nanaya; Beaulieu and Westenholz caution that shared titles do not prove full identity. **Learned syncretism, incomplete**.
- **Ištar — joint invocation.** An Old Babylonian Sippar letter blesses its recipients by Ištar and Tashmetu. This is **cultic co-occurrence**, not a narrative interaction.
- **Ištar — equation.** A late syncretic hymn identifies Tashmetu with Ištar among many goddess equations. **Late learned systematization**, not evidence of original identity.
- **Ištar-Aššurītu — possible early court association.** Old Assyrian evidence places Tashmetu in or near her cultic household. The exact office is reconstructed; **early cultic association, role uncertain**.
- **Aššur — early cultic association.** Old Assyrian documentation situates Tashmetu in Aššur’s temple environment. No narrative episode survives.
- **Anu — grant of authority.** The Kalḫu *šuʾillakku* says Anu bestowed hearing/obedience and reconciliation upon Tashmetu. **First-millennium prayer theology**.
- **Dagan — joint appearance.** A damaged prayer mentions Anu and Dagan in the divine hierarchy around a goddess passage; this is **liturgical co-occurrence**, not a recoverable narrative encounter.
- **Šērūa — ritual procession.** In a Neo-Assyrian Assur ritual the king escorts Šērūa, Kippat-māti, and Tashmetu into Anu’s temple. **Securely attested ritual interaction**, SAA 20, first millennium BCE. [ORACC ritual](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/P336139)
- **Kippat-māti — ritual grouping or replacement.** She appears with Tashmetu and Šērūa in Assur ritual texts. Rubin has proposed that under Sennacherib Kippat-māti may have replaced rather than merely accompanied Tashmetu. **Disputed Neo-Assyrian cultic reorganization**.
- **Meskilak — proposed equation.** Inferred from the equation of their husbands Inzak and Nabû in a late list; **modern scholarly reconstruction with no explicit surviving Tashmetu–Meskilak equation**.
- **Nisaba — shared prayer collection.** CTN 4 no. 168 places prayers to Nisaba and Tashmetu in one sequence because both could be related to Nabû and learned culture. **Liturgical juxtaposition**, not a narrative interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Tashmetu has no surviving epic cycle comparable to Ištar’s descent, Marduk’s combat with Tiamat, or Ninurta’s battles. Her one substantial narrative is cultic and erotic.

### The courtship, garden, and bedchamber

**Earliest full witness:** SAA 3 no. 14, seventh century BCE copy.

The composition opens with communal allegiance to Nabû and Tashmetu. Tashmetu is summoned to the cella; juniper perfumes the sanctuary. She appears elaborately adorned. Nabû asks the purpose of her dress. Her answer is that she wishes to go with him to the garden.

The garden is simultaneously a cultic location, a field of natural fertility, and erotic imagery. Fruit is to be plucked; birds are heard; the divine pair move toward private union. Fragmentary lines refer to seating, harnessing, and a bedchamber. Related ritual letters show that the statues remained together for a prescribed period and received food.

The narrative’s likely enacted sequence was:

1. Purification and preparation of sanctuary and statues.
2. Dressing and adornment of Tashmetu.
3. Procession or installation.
4. Dialogue or choral recitation.
5. Entry of the statues into garden or *akītu* quarters.
6. Conjugal seclusion for six days and seven nights.
7. Offerings and feasts.
8. Tashmetu’s emergence.
9. Blessing of king, dynasty, temple, and land.

The surviving song should not be converted into a claim that a human king or priest necessarily had ritual intercourse with a priestess. The evidence describes divine images and their ceremonial union. A human sexual enactment is not documented.

### Development and function

The marriage story appears to have developed after Nabû and Tashmetu were paired in the Old Babylonian period. Its most detailed form belongs to Neo-Assyrian Kalḫu, where Babylonian scholarly religion was appropriated into imperial court culture.

Functions supported by the record include:

- Celebrating the couple’s marriage.
- Renewing fertility and abundance.
- Securing the king’s health and longevity.
- Ensuring dynastic succession.
- Displaying the learned and Babylonian credentials of Assyrian kingship.
- Integrating temple personnel and royal representatives into a shared ritual calendar.

Any attempt to reconstruct a lost third-millennium “original sacred marriage myth” behind it is speculative.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Aššur and Kaneš

Old Assyrian texts document personal devotion, votive gifts, and theophoric names. Tashmetu may have occupied a position within the temple households of Aššur and Ištar-Aššurītu. No free-standing Old Assyrian temple dedicated solely to her is securely known.

### Babylon, Sippar, and Dilbat

By Hammurabi’s late reign she received valuable royal offerings in Babylon. In Sippar she belonged to the extended court of Marduk and Ṣarpānītu and was invoked alongside Ištar. At Dilbat she became genealogically related to Uraš, but the chronological development of that relationship remains disputed.

### Borsippa

Ezida at Borsippa was Nabû’s principal Babylonian sanctuary and one of Babylonia’s most important temples. Tashmetu was “Queen of Borsippa” and belonged to its divine household, but the location of a dedicated Tashmetu chapel within the excavated complex remains unknown. French archaeological documentation explicitly cautions that her chapel has not been identified. [Borsippa archaeological overview](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/nimrud/fr/sanctuaire-de-nabu-borsippa)

Nanaya was also worshipped in Ezida. Both could bear “Queen of Borsippa,” and their status shifted over time. In Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid Babylonia, Nanaya often overshadowed Tashmetu without erasing her.

The Borsippa temple archives illuminate priestly prebends, offerings, property, and institutional administration. They terminate abruptly in Xerxes’ second regnal year, 484 BCE, in the context of Babylonian revolts and Persian repression. The documentary break does not itself prove that all worship of Tashmetu ended in that year. [Waerzeggers, *The Ezida Temple of Borsippa*](https://www.nino-leiden.nl/publication/the-ezida-temple-of-borsippa)

### Kalḫu

Kalḫu supplies the fullest archaeological context.

Aššurnasirpal II founded or established Nabû’s temple precinct in the ninth century BCE. Adad-nērārī III, 810–783 BCE, undertook the decisive reconstruction around 800 BCE. The southern portion contained adjoining monumental cellae for Nabû and Tashmetu. Inscribed guardian statues commissioned by the governor Bēl-tarṣi-ilumma for Adad-nērārī and Šammuramat stood at the entrances.

The paired shrines faced rooms in which scholarly tablets were stored and copied. Thus the architecture placed Nabû and Tashmetu physically before the learned personnel whose knowledge they patronized.

The northwestern rooms were rebuilt around 700 BCE as an **akītu suite**. Early-seventh-century letters call it the *bēt akiāti*. One room may have been the *bēt ūmē sebetti*, “house/room of seven days,” plausibly the couple’s ritual bedroom. [Kalḫu temple account](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/nimrud/ancientkalhu/thecity/nabustemple/)

### Festival

The marriage festival took place in **Ayyāru**, the second month, approximately April–May. It lasted several days. Offerings were made for the king and royal descendants. When the king was absent, the temple’s *ḫazannu*, conventionally “mayor,” represented him. Senior temple officials supervised the proceedings; letters to the king reported that prescribed actions had been completed.

Offerings included food, drink, aromatics, and probably garments and ornaments for the statues. Juniper appears in the lyrics as sanctuary incense.

### Prayer and votive practice

Tashmetu received:

- Precious metals and stones.
- Seals and inscribed dedications.
- Food and drink.
- Incense.
- Garments and adornment for the cult statue.
- *Šuʾillakku* prayers, literally “raised-hand” prayers.
- Petitions for favorable hearing, health, wisdom, protection, and dynastic stability.

Theophoric personal names demonstrate private acknowledgment but are much rarer than names built with Nabû.

### Sacrifice and contested sexual claims

Animal sacrifice and food offerings are documented in the normal temple system. **No human sacrifice to Tashmetu is documented.**

Her erotic marriage ritual is not evidence for “temple prostitution.” No text identifies a class of prostitutes serving Tashmetu or says that women had intercourse with worshippers in her sanctuary. The old habit of treating every Mesopotamian sacred-marriage text as human ritual sex is a discarded or heavily contested reconstruction. What the Kalḫu evidence securely documents is the union and seclusion of divine statues.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before approximately 2000 BCE

No secure evidence. The lost VAT 6563 cannot establish a Ur III cult, and no Tashmetu appears in securely identified third-millennium Sumerian literary mythology.

### Nineteenth century BCE: Old Assyrian goddess

Tashmetu is attested in northern Assyrian cult and among Assyrian merchants at Kaneš. She appears to be independent of Nabû. Personal names and votive gifts establish actual worship.

### Eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE: adoption into Babylonia

Her cult appears at Babylon, Sippar, Borsippa, and Dilbat. The Tell Taban Weidner-list manuscript associates her with Nabû. Hammurabi’s court makes valuable offerings to her.

Rubin interprets this period as the gradual adoption of both Nabû and Tashmetu into Marduk’s expanding circle. Their marriage was a historical theological construction, perhaps encouraged by the semantic pairing “call” and “hear.”

### Middle Babylonian and Kassite periods

Learned texts provide Sumerian equivalents and formal genealogies. Nabû rises in importance. Nanaya joins his circle; a kudurru under Marduk-apla-iddina I invokes Nabû, Tashmetu, and Nanaya jointly. Tashmetu becomes a principal goddess of Borsippa.

### Middle Assyrian period

Royal scribes invoke her. Nabû’s connection with scholarship makes the couple increasingly relevant to learned elites.

### Ninth–eighth centuries BCE: monumentalization at Kalḫu

Assyrian kings establish and rebuild Ezida. Around 800 BCE, adjoining shrines monumentalize the marriage of Nabû and Tashmetu. Their cult becomes entangled with court scholarship, imperial ideology, royal health, and succession.

### Seventh century BCE: best-documented phase

The love lyrics, KAR 122, *šuʾillakku* prayers, priestly letters, royal inscriptions, and temple architecture document a mature marriage theology. Aššurbanipal claims the couple’s gift of learning. Ritual practice includes the annual Ayyāru marriage and the statues’ multi-day seclusion.

Under Sennacherib there may have been a temporary reorganization in which Kippat-māti displaced Tashmetu in some Assur rituals. This remains disputed.

Kalḫu fell during the collapse of Assyria, conventionally dated 614 BCE for the sack of Nimrud. Ezida was looted and burned; treaty tablets and furnishings were smashed. There were limited later repairs or reuse, but no direct evidence establishes a restored post-Assyrian cult of Tashmetu there. [Archaeological synthesis](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10085697/1/Ancient-Knowledge-Networks.pdf)

### Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid periods

Borsippa remained the principal southern cultic setting. Tashmetu continued beside Nabû, but Nanaya held increasing prominence. The disappearance of the Ezida archive in 484 BCE marks a major institutional rupture.

### Seleucid and later periods

First-millennium lexical, liturgical, and theological manuscripts continued to transmit her name and epithets into the Seleucid period. Direct evidence for an independent living cult becomes sparse. Nabû’s cult spread west and east, but Tashmetu did not everywhere accompany him; local female partners could replace her.

There is no documented continuous Tashmetu cult through late antiquity, medieval Mesopotamia, or the Islamic period. Her ancient worship appears to have disappeared through the broader dissolution of the cuneiform temple system, not through a recorded campaign directed specifically at her.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Rediscovery

Tashmetu re-entered modern knowledge through the decipherment, excavation, and publication of cuneiform sources from the nineteenth century onward. Excavations at Nimrud/Kalḫu exposed the paired cellae and temple tablets; publication of the Neo-Assyrian letters and literary texts revealed the marriage ritual’s administrative and poetic dimensions.

### Comparative literature

Nissinen’s comparison of the love lyrics to the biblical Song of Songs helped make the work known outside Assyriology. The comparison is literary and phenomenological. Direct borrowing has not been demonstrated.

### Occult and neopagan reception

The dedicated search produced no evidence that Tashmetu formed part of Renaissance Hermeticism, early modern grimoires, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or a documented pre-twentieth-century occult lineage. She has no established traditional planetary seal, angelic correspondence, magical color, crystal, or ritual day in those systems.

Individual modern Mesopotamian reconstructionists and eclectic polytheists may honor her, usually through modern adaptations of the love lyrics or raised-hand prayers. Such practice is **modern religious reconstruction or invention**, however sincere, because no continuous initiating community or ritual lineage from Borsippa or Kalḫu survives. Its scale cannot presently be established.

Internet descriptions of Tashmetu as a “manifestation goddess,” “goddess of communication,” “patroness of active listening,” or fixed astrological ruler modernize genuine ancient hearing imagery but are not themselves ancient titles. Assertions connecting her securely with Capricorn are likewise unsubstantiated in the reviewed primary corpus.

### Popular culture

Tashmetu has not acquired a major independent profile in mass-market fiction, film, comics, or games. Most modern references occur in mythological dictionaries, academic translations, online deity catalogs, and modern devotional material. No documented nationalist, extremist, or major commercial appropriation was identified.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Origin

- **Assyrian-origin model:** Westenholz emphasizes nineteenth-century Old Assyrian evidence and her initial northern setting.
- **Babylonian integration model:** Rubin agrees that her early cult was independent but focuses on how she and Nabû entered Marduk’s circle during the Old Babylonian period.
- **Ur III possibility:** Löhnert’s reliance on VAT 6563 would move her earlier, but the tablet’s loss and disputed date prevent certainty.

The secure conclusion is that she is attested first in the Old Assyrian world; a third-millennium origin remains unproved.

### Meaning of the name

- “Hearing” or “she hears”: strongest etymological basis.
- “Reconciliation”: possible extension through *salīmu* and favorable hearing.
- “Intelligence”: theologically appropriate but lexically unverified.
- Ningutešasiga: probably a learned Sumerian rendering, not proof of an original Sumerian name.

### Relationship with Nabû

Older handbooks often present them as an eternal pair. Current historical analysis treats the marriage as an Old Babylonian formation. The earliest couple evidence is late eighteenth century BCE; earlier Old Assyrian evidence does not securely connect them.

### Relationship with Nanaya

Three interpretations must be separated:

1. Tashmetu as wife and Nanaya as mistress or beloved at Borsippa.
2. Regional substitution, with Tashmetu dominant in Assyria and Nanaya in some Babylonian settings.
3. Partial syncretism through shared titles and attributes.

Full identity is not demonstrated. A shared epithet or appearance in one prayer series does not erase their separately named cults.

### “Sacred marriage”

The phrase is a modern analytical designation. The ancient records securely establish decorated cult images, procession, a bedchamber, seclusion, offerings, and erotic poetry. They do not securely establish human intercourse, royal intercourse, or prostitution.

### Iconography

Black and Green and earlier art-historical handbooks necessarily rely on textual context because Tashmetu lacks a unique surviving emblem. Any unlabeled goddess beside Nabû remains an attribution rather than a certainty. Frankfort-style readings of seals by generalized divine “types” cannot override inscriptions.

### State of the evidence

Principal objects and corpora include:

- TIM 9 54/IM 3233/CDLI P223388, “Love Lyrics.”
- KAR 122, Aššurbanipal’s hymn.
- CTN 4 no. 168, the Kalḫu goddess-prayer tablet.
- SAA 13 priestly letters.
- RINAP/RIAO royal inscriptions.
- Old Assyrian Kaneš letters and personal names.
- God lists, including Weidner and *An = Anum* traditions.
- Borsippa’s Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid archives.
- Kalḫu’s architectural remains and inscribed statues.

Tablets from Nimrud are divided chiefly between the Iraq Museum and British Museum collections. The loss of VAT 6563 and the fragmentary state of many prayers prevent a complete reconstruction.
