# Enkidu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Heroic human or humanlike literary being, not securely an independently worshipped god. In Sumerian poems he is usually Gilgameš’s servant or companion; in the Akkadian epic he is a divinely fashioned wild man, equal and friend of Gilgameš. Some manuscripts write his name with the divine determinative, especially Tablet XII, but Enkidu is absent from the standard Mesopotamian god lists and has no securely identified temple, priesthood, festival, or independent cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The name *En-ki-du₁₀* occurs in Early Dynastic personal-name material, including Fara-period evidence conventionally dated around the twenty-sixth century BCE, but identification with the later literary Enkidu is uncertain. The earliest secure narrative attestations are the Sumerian Gilgameš poems preserved principally on Old Babylonian tablets, approximately 1900–1700 BCE. They probably preserve traditions older than their surviving manuscripts, but no presently known third-millennium tablet narrates Enkidu’s adventures.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[EA]** securely attested early material; **[LC]** later canonical elaboration; **[RV]** regional or manuscript variant; **[LS]** learned scribal systematization; **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction; **[MI]** modern invention or reception.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Enkidu is the nonroyal counterpart of Gilgameš. The Sumerian poems generally present him as Gilgameš’s subordinate—often literally his “servant,” Sumerian *arad*—already familiar with wilderness routes and Ḫuwawa’s country. The unified Akkadian epic radically enlarges him: the gods create him to check Gilgameš’s oppressive strength; he begins among animals, is separated from them through intercourse and instruction by Šamḫat, becomes Gilgameš’s equal and friend, joins him in killing Ḫumbaba and the Bull of Heaven, and dies when the divine assembly decrees that one hero must pay for those acts. His death precipitates Gilgameš’s quest for immortality.

### Names and spellings

- **Sumerian:** normally **En-ki-du₁₀**, conventionally normalized *Enkidu*. The spelling can be represented 𒂗𒆠𒂊 or with values corresponding to *EN.KI.DU₁₀*; actual manuscript spellings vary.
- **Akkadian:** *Enkidu*, commonly written syllabically or logographically from the Sumerian spelling. Some late manuscripts prefix the divine determinative **𒀭** (*d*), which classifies a name graphically but does not by itself establish cultic godhood.
- Older publications give **Eabani**, **Engidu**, or **Enkidu(g)**. *Eabani* resulted from obsolete nineteenth-century sign readings and is not a separate ancient character.
- The Sumerian element *du₁₀(g)* can mean “good/sweet/pleasant.” Accordingly, the name has been parsed as “lord of the good place” or “lord of the pleasant place.” **[MSR]** This remains uncertain: ancient sources supply no explicit etymology, and apparent segmentation may be scribal reinterpretation rather than historical derivation.
- “Creature of Enki” is a popular modern etymology. **[MI/MSR disputed]** It fits neither every spelling nor the Standard Babylonian account, where Aruru fashions Enkidu. A Middle Babylonian recension may have involved Ea more directly, which could have encouraged a learned association with Enki/Ea, but it does not prove the name originally meant “Enki’s creation.”
- A proposed relation to the pastoral deity **Enkimdu** is speculative. Enkimdu is a distinct god, best known as Dumuzi’s agricultural rival; no ancient equation *Enkidu = Enkimdu* is established.

### Titles and descriptions

These are literary descriptions rather than cult epithets:

- Gilgameš’s **servant** or follower in the Sumerian Ḫuwawa poems. **[EA]**
- A being “born in the wilderness,” massively strong and covered in hair, in the Akkadian epic. **[LC]**
- Gilgameš’s equal or counterpart, created in response to Uruk’s complaints. **[LC]**
- “Offspring of silence,” a difficult expression in Standard Babylonian Tablet I. **[LC; translation disputed]**
- A person whose strength resembles that of Ninurta or other heroic divinities. Such similes do not identify him with those gods.
- Herdsman and protector of shepherds after his encounter with Šamḫat. **[LC]**
- Ghost or returning spirit in *Gilgameš, Enkidu and the Netherworld* and Akkadian Tablet XII. **[EA/LC]**

There is no securely attested interpretatio graeca, interpretatio romana, or ancient equation with Heracles, Pan, Esau, Adam, Nimrod, or the Indian Ṛśyaśṛṅga. These are comparative proposals or modern analogies, not ancient identifications.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Early name evidence

The sequence *En-ki-du₁₀-ga* appears among Early Dynastic names from Fara/Šuruppak. **[EA, identity uncertain]** A personal name is evidence that the name existed, not that the epic character, a god, or Enkidu’s later biography was already known.

### Sumerian corpus

The surviving witnesses are predominantly Old Babylonian school tablets, approximately 1900–1700 BCE, from Nippur, Ur, and other sites.

1. **Gilgameš and Ḫuwawa, Version A** — Sumerian. Enkidu is Gilgameš’s servant, advises consulting Utu, guides the expedition, urges Ḫuwawa’s death, and in some manuscripts cuts his throat. Enlil condemns the killing and redistributes Ḫuwawa’s terrifying auras. **[EA with RV]**

2. **Gilgameš and Ḫuwawa, Version B** — Sumerian. A shorter and substantially independent recension. Enkidu again acts as adviser and companion. **[EA/RV]**

3. **Gilgameš and the Bull of Heaven** — Sumerian. Fragmentary. Enkidu assists Gilgameš against the celestial bull, including seizing its tail in one manuscript. The preserved poem does not contain the elaborate death sequel familiar from the Akkadian epic. **[EA]**

4. **Gilgameš, Enkidu and the Netherworld** — Sumerian. Inanna plants or receives a ḫuluppu tree; Gilgameš later makes objects conventionally rendered *pukku* and *mikkû* from it. They fall into the netherworld. Enkidu volunteers to retrieve them, disregards Gilgameš’s instructions, and is detained below. Enki and Utu enable his ghost to rise through a fissure so he can describe the condition of the dead. **[EA]**

5. **The Death of Gilgameš** — Sumerian. Enkidu is already among the dead. The dying Gilgameš is promised a judicial position in the netherworld alongside figures including Enkidu. **[EA]**

6. **Gilgameš and Aga** contains no substantial Enkidu role in its surviving text. This absence matters: Enkidu did not automatically accompany Gilgameš in every early tradition.

### Babylonian epic

The earliest unified Akkadian poem is the Old Babylonian composition conventionally called **“Surpassing All Other Kings,”** surviving on tablets of approximately the eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE.

- The Pennsylvania tablet preserves Enkidu’s encounter with Šamḫat and entry into human society.
- The Yale tablet continues with Uruk and preparations for the Cedar Forest.
- Additional fragments—including one published by Andrew George as *Enkidu and the Harlot*—show that the taming episode circulated in differing forms.
- The Old Babylonian epic was already a composite literary work; the precise relation between its wild-man and experienced-woodsman materials remains debated.

Middle Babylonian and peripheral witnesses, approximately 1500–1000 BCE, include manuscripts from Babylonia, Assyria, Ugarit, Megiddo, Emar, Ḫattuša, and probably the Sealand. Hittite and Hurrian adaptations demonstrate international scribal transmission, but their fragmentary state prevents a continuous reconstruction.

The **Standard Babylonian Epic**, incipit *ša naqba īmuru*, “He who saw the deep,” was probably shaped late in the second millennium BCE and traditionally attributed to the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni. **[LC/LS]** The attribution occurs in a later catalogue, not in a contemporary authorial colophon; his precise contribution is unknowable. The principal witnesses are first-millennium tablets, especially seventh-century BCE copies from Ashurbanipal’s library at Nineveh, plus Babylonian manuscripts from Babylon and Uruk. The latest dated witness is approximately 130 BCE.

Tablets I–VIII contain Enkidu’s creation, taming, friendship, expeditions, condemnation, death, and funeral. Tablets IX–XI concern Gilgameš’s grief and immortality quest after Enkidu’s death. **Tablet XII is not their narrative sequel:** it translates part of the independent Sumerian *Gilgameš, Enkidu and the Netherworld*, reintroducing a living Enkidu without reconciling this with Tablet VII. **[LS]** Its contradiction is evidence of compilation, not a hidden resurrection.

### Texts named in the brief but not Enkidu sources

- **Enūma Eliš:** Enkidu does not appear.
- **Atrahasis:** Enkidu does not appear; its clay-and-divine-substance anthropology supplies comparative background only.
- **Inanna’s Descent:** Enkidu does not appear. Similarities between its underworld restrictions and Enkidu’s instructions are thematic, not an interaction.
- **Enki and Ninmaḫ:** Enkidu does not appear; it is comparative evidence for divine manufacture of humans.
- **An = Anum:** no secure independent deity Enkidu appears in the standard god list.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP:** no documented royal cult, temple construction, or campaign undertaken for Enkidu has been established.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

Enkidu has no securely established divine domain. His recurring literary spheres are wilderness, animals, physical strength, companionship, herding, heroic warfare, death, and knowledge of the netherworld.

### Form and powers

In Standard Babylonian Tablet I, Aruru pinches off clay, casts it in the steppe, and creates Enkidu: hairy, long-haired, unclothed, grazing and drinking with animals. The text calls him extraordinarily strong but does not make him half bull. His animal affinity allows him to fill hunters’ pits and free captured beasts. After intercourse with Šamḫat, the animals reject him; he gains enlarged understanding while losing some former running power. Bread, beer, clothing, grooming, and pastoral labor complete his social transition.

His chief limitation is mortality. Even divine manufacture does not make him immortal. He cannot defeat the divine verdict, and his dream depicts death as seizure by a leonine or eagle-taloned being and transport to the “house of dust.”

### Iconography

**No surviving ancient image is securely captioned “Enkidu.”** Mesopotamian seals frequently show:

- a nude, bearded hero grappling with lions;
- a bull-man joining a contest scene;
- pairs of heroes subduing animals;
- heroes confronting the monstrous Ḫumbaba face.

Older scholarship routinely named the nude hero “Gilgamesh” and the bull-man “Enkidu.” **[MSR disputed]** This is unsafe: the literary Enkidu is never described as bull-legged or horned, and contest figures existed independently of the epic. The British Museum accordingly labels examples only “possibly Enkidu.” Plaques and seals showing two heroes with Ḫumbaba are more plausibly connected with the narrative cycle, but even these are ordinarily uninscribed.

No ancient evidence assigns Enkidu a sacred color, number, weekday, planet, star, plant, standardized weapon, or emblem. Modern lists doing so are inventions unless explicitly presented as contemporary devotional systems.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

Enkidu has no conventional biological genealogy.

- **Standard Babylonian:** Aruru fashions him from clay after Anu directs that Gilgameš’s counterpart be created. No father, consort, sibling, or offspring is named.
- **Middle Babylonian variant:** a fragment associates his creation or civilizing more closely with Ea. **[RV]**
- **Sumerian poems:** no creation account survives; he simply appears as Gilgameš’s servant or companion. **[EA]**
- Šamḫat is sexual initiator and teacher, not wife.
- Gilgameš is friend, social equal, and in Sumerian texts also master; no Mesopotamian source calls them biological brothers, although the Akkadian poem uses kinship language for their bond.
- Modern descriptions of them as an explicitly married couple are interpretive receptions, not ancient legal or genealogical claims.

### Every documented interaction with a named divine being

- **Aruru** — Creates Enkidu from clay as Gilgameš’s counterpart. *Standard Babylonian Gilgameš* I, late-second-millennium composition; first-millennium witnesses. **[LC]**
- **Anu/An** — Hears Uruk’s complaints and prompts Aruru’s creation; later participates in the divine deliberation over the Bull episode. Tablets I and VI–VII. **[LC]**
- **Šamaš/Utu** — In the Sumerian Ḫuwawa poems, Enkidu urges Gilgameš to seek Utu’s approval; Utu supplies supernatural helpers. In the Akkadian epic Šamaš aids the heroes against Ḫumbaba, rebukes Enkidu’s deathbed curse of Šamḫat, and receives funerary gifts. Tablet XII/Sumerian Netherworld has Utu open a fissure through which Enkidu’s ghost rises. **[EA/LC]**
- **Enlil** — In Sumerian Version A, angrily condemns Ḫuwawa’s killing and distributes his auras. In the Akkadian epic, Enkidu recalls Enlil’s appointment of Ḫumbaba and identifies offending him as perilous. **[EA/LC]**
- **Ninlil** — Enkidu and Gilgameš bring Ḫuwawa’s head before Enlil and, in one manuscript, Ninlil. **[RV]**
- **Ḫuwawa/Ḫumbaba** — Enkidu guides Gilgameš to him, rejects his pleas, and either kills him or helps Gilgameš kill him. Sumerian Ḫuwawa poems and Akkadian Tablets III–V. Ḫumbaba bears divine marking in some traditions but is fundamentally a divinely appointed guardian. **[EA/LC/RV]**
- **Ištar/Inanna** — After Gilgameš rejects Ištar, Enkidu helps kill her Bull and throws the bull’s haunch at or before her while insulting her. Akkadian Tablet VI. In the Sumerian Bull poem the episode survives differently and fragmentarily. **[EA/LC]**
- **Bull of Heaven** — Enkidu grapples with and helps kill this divine or divinely controlled being. Sumerian Bull poem; Akkadian Tablet VI. **[EA/LC]**
- **Ninsun** — Gilgameš’s divine mother adopts or accepts Enkidu as kin before the Cedar Forest expedition and entrusts him with Gilgameš’s protection. Akkadian Tablet III. **[LC]**
- **Ea/Enki** — Ea appears in a Middle Babylonian civilizing recension; in the Sumerian Netherworld poem Enki’s intervention leads to Utu releasing Enkidu’s ghost. **[RV/EA]**
- **Ereškigal** — Enkidu enters her netherworld realm and observes its social order; no securely preserved direct conversation occurs. Sumerian Netherworld poem/Akkadian XII. **[EA/LC]**
- **Namtar** — Appears among netherworld powers in traditions surrounding death and the underworld; no distinct personal exchange with Enkidu is securely preserved. **[EA; absence noted]**
- **Ningiszida and Dumuzi** — Named in Enkidu’s death dream as figures serving in the underworld. This is a witnessed joint scene, not friendship or genealogy. Akkadian Tablet VII. **[LC]**
- **Sîn/Namraṣit** — Funerary gifts may be dedicated to the moon god during rites for Enkidu; the damaged text makes details uncertain. Tablet VIII. **[LC, restoration disputed]**

There is no documented interaction between Enkidu and Marduk, Tiamat, Apsu, Ninmaḫ, the gods of *Atrahasis*, or most deities catalogued in *An = Anum*.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Creation, Šamḫat, and civilizing

**[LC]** Uruk’s people complain about Gilgameš’s abuses. Anu calls for a matching being; Aruru makes Enkidu from clay in the steppe. A hunter sees him undoing traps and reports him. Šamḫat is sent to expose herself and have intercourse with him. After six days and seven nights in the Standard version—variant fragments complicate the duration—his animals flee. Šamḫat tells him he has become wise “like a god,” clothes him, introduces him to bread and beer, and brings him into pastoral and urban society.

The word describing Šamḫat, *ḫarimtu*, has often been translated “harlot” or “prostitute.” Calling her a **temple prostitute** is not established by this episode: it never says a temple ordered the intercourse as ritual service. **[MSR disputed]** Modern reassessment treats *ḫarimtu* as a socially independent woman or a category whose exact sexual and juridical implications varied. The scene documents literary sex used for social transformation; it does not document an institution of “sacred prostitution.”

### Wrestling and friendship

**[LC]** Enkidu hears that Gilgameš intends to exercise royal or sexual prerogatives at a wedding and blocks his entrance. They wrestle violently. The broken text makes the exact victor uncertain; older smooth translations often make Gilgameš win, while some reconstructions leave the outcome unresolved. The struggle converts rivalry into friendship. This episode is not preserved in the extant Sumerian poems and probably belongs to the Babylonian integration of the wild-man story with the Gilgameš cycle.

### Cedar Forest and Ḫumbaba

**[EA/LC]** In the Sumerian versions, Gilgameš seeks imperishable fame; Enkidu knows the terrain and warns him, but accompanies him. Utu grants aid. Ḫuwawa’s supernatural auras are progressively neutralized. When the guardian pleads for life, Enkidu urges execution. Manuscripts differ over whether Enkidu alone cuts his throat or both heroes do so.

The Akkadian epic enlarges the expedition through dreams, Ninsun’s ritual support, Šamaš’s winds, and moral hesitation. The heroes kill Ḫumbaba and cut cedars despite his divine commission. A newly recovered Tablet V fragment emphasizes the forest’s luxuriant, almost courtlike life, sharpening the destructive character of their act.

### Bull of Heaven

**[EA/LC]** Ištar proposes marriage to Gilgameš; he rejects her by cataloguing her former lovers’ ruin. She obtains the Bull of Heaven from Anu. The Bull devastates Uruk, but Enkidu seizes it while Gilgameš kills it. Enkidu insults Ištar and casts the haunch. The Sumerian version differs in framing and remains fragmentary; one cannot assume every Akkadian detail belonged to the early poem.

### Divine judgment, death, and underworld dream

**[LC]** In a dream Enkidu hears the gods debate punishment: because Ḫumbaba and the Bull were killed, one hero must die. Enlil selects Enkidu. He curses the cedar door and Šamḫat; Šamaš persuades him to bless her instead. A terrifying dream carries him to the house of dust, where kings surrender crowns and the dead eat clay. After a prolonged illness he dies. Gilgameš conducts an elaborate lament and funeral, commissions an image, offers grave gifts, and then wanders in fear of sharing Enkidu’s fate.

The divine verdict is not found in the surviving Sumerian Bull poem. It is part of the Babylonian epic’s architecture linking heroic transgression to mortality.

### Enkidu in the netherworld

**[EA/LC]** This is an independent Sumerian tale, not the continuation of Enkidu’s Babylonian death. Enkidu descends bodily to retrieve Gilgameš’s lost objects but violates instructions: he wears clean clothing, anoints himself, carries weapons, and behaves in ways that disclose him as living. The netherworld holds him. Enki and Utu allow his ghost to emerge, and he reports the differing fates of the dead, especially the importance of descendants, burial, and offerings.

The Akkadian Tablet XII closely translates part of this poem and was appended to the eleven-tablet epic by learned scribes. Its Enkidu should not be harmonized with the already dead friend of Tablets VII–VIII.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No temple, cult center, priesthood, festival, regular sacrifice, oracle, or pilgrimage belonging to Enkidu is securely documented. AMGG’s omission of him from its deity catalogue and his absence from *An = Anum* support literary rather than cultic status.

Possible qualifications:

- An Old Babylonian incantation for quieting a baby apparently invokes Enkidu and his nighttime watch. **[EA, interpretation uncertain]** Invocation in magic does not establish a temple cult.
- Tablet VIII describes offerings at Enkidu’s funeral and to underworld or celestial gods on his behalf. These are narrative mortuary rites, not worship of Enkidu.
- *The Death of Gilgameš* places Enkidu among netherworld figures and associates Gilgameš with postmortem judgment. This provides no evidence that Enkidu received an official funerary cult.
- Theophoric-looking early names containing *Enkidu* are ambiguous because *en* and *ki* are ordinary Sumerian elements and the literary identity is uncertain.
- There is no evidence of human sacrifice to Enkidu.
- Šamḫat’s sexual role is not proof of temple prostitution or ritual sex in Enkidu’s cult; no such cult is known.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Early Dynastic period, approximately 2600–2350 BCE

The name sequence is attested, but the character’s existence cannot yet be demonstrated. Contest iconography is already developing; connecting it to Enkidu is retrospective. **[EA/MSR]**

### Ur III and Old Babylonian periods, approximately 2100–1600 BCE

Sumerian Gilgameš poems circulate in scribal education. Enkidu is chiefly a servant, guide, fighter, and underworld traveller. The Old Babylonian Akkadian epic synthesizes a wild-man civilizing story with the Gilgameš-Ḫuwawa tradition and elevates him toward equality with the king.

### Middle and Late Bronze Age, approximately 1600–1000 BCE

The poem spreads through international scribal networks to Ugarit, Megiddo, Emar, and Ḫattuša; Hittite and Hurrian adaptations appear. Variant tablets rework Enkidu’s creation and civilizing. Toward the end of this period, the Standard Babylonian edition takes shape, traditionally associated with Sîn-lēqi-unninni.

### First millennium BCE

Assyrian and Babylonian scribes copy the standardized epic. The Nineveh library preserves major witnesses. Learned compilation adds the discordant Tablet XII. Enkidu remains a literary figure, not an imperial cult recipient. Manuscript copying continues in Babylonia into the Parthian period.

### After approximately 130 BCE

Cuneiform literary transmission eventually ends. There is no demonstrated continuous medieval cult or folklore tradition preserving Enkidu by name. Proposed survival through biblical, Greek, Syriac, Arabic, or Persian figures is comparative speculation, not a documented chain of transmission.

### Nineteenth century to present

Excavation and decipherment recover the epic. Enkidu becomes central to modern presentations of Mesopotamian literature, environmental thought, sexuality, friendship, and queer reception. Contemporary individuals may honor him devotionally, but no evidence establishes an organized, continuous, or numerically substantial Enkidu cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

George Smith’s nineteenth-century work on the Nineveh tablets initiated modern recovery, though early readers concentrated on the Flood. Obsolete readings produced “Eabani,” and early art historians confidently labeled bull-men as Enkidu. Both conventions have since been corrected or qualified.

Modern literature, theatre, opera, visual art, and children’s retellings routinely make Enkidu:

- nature opposed to civilization;
- humanity’s ecological conscience;
- Gilgameš’s moral educator;
- an archetypal shadow or instinctual self;
- Gilgameš’s lover.

These readings may be productive reception, but they are not explicit ancient doctrines. The epic unquestionably depicts intense bodily affection, lamentation, and kinship between the heroes; it does not explicitly define a sexual relationship between them.

The *Fate* franchise—especially *Fate/strange Fake* and *Fate/Grand Order*—reimagines Enkidu as a gender-ambiguous, shape-changing divine weapon or “Chain of Heaven.” **[MI]** That ontology, appearance, and weapon identity are modern Japanese fiction, not Mesopotamian evidence. It has nevertheless become one of the most influential contemporary images of Enkidu.

Modern occult and neopagan web materials sometimes assign Enkidu planets, elements, offerings, sigils, feast days, or divine portfolios. **[MI]** No continuous ancient authority supports these systems. Scattered contemporary devotional practice is real as modern religion but should not be backdated.

No substantial evidence was found for Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, medieval magical, nationalist, extremist, or state-political appropriation centered specifically on Enkidu. Gilgameš and Mesopotamia more generally have such receptions, but transferring them automatically to Enkidu would be misleading.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

The modern textual foundation is Andrew George’s two-volume *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic* (2003), which catalogues manuscripts, reconstructs the Akkadian recensions, prints cuneiform texts, and distinguishes the Sumerian poems from the Babylonian epic. ETCSL supplies accessible Sumerian composites and translations. CDLI and ORACC provide tablet metadata, transliterations, lexical resources, and historical corpora.

Major disputes include:

- **Name and origin:** whether the Early Dynastic name denotes the later hero; whether *En-ki-du₁₀* is transparent Sumerian; and whether Enki/Ea belongs to the name’s origin. No consensus establishes “creature of Enki.”
- **Literary synthesis:** whether the wild man of the taming episode and the knowledgeable herdsman or forest guide originated as separate characters or narrative strata. Their tension is real; reconstruction of separate tales remains hypothetical.
- **Victory in the wrestling match:** the damaged text does not justify absolute confidence.
- **Šamḫat’s status:** “temple prostitute” is an older reconstruction, not an explicit textual fact.
- **Sex and civilization:** readings range from simple domestication to initiation, gendered power, loss of animal communion, and acquisition of human mortality. These are interpretations of the episode, not separate ancient variants.
- **Enkidu’s divinity:** divine manufacture and occasional divine determinatives do not outweigh his mortality, absence from god lists, and lack of cult evidence. “Demigod” is convenient modern language but not his stable Mesopotamian classification.
- **Iconography:** the nude hero and bull-man cannot normally be named without inscriptions. Frankfort and later seal specialists documented the motifs; current museum language is more cautious.
- **Tablet XII:** now generally treated as a learned Akkadian adaptation of an independent Sumerian poem appended to the eleven-tablet epic, not an integrated sequel.
- **Authorship:** Sîn-lēqi-unninni is a traditional attribution and useful label for the Standard version’s editor, not a securely documented sole author.
- **Comparative mythology:** comparisons with Adam, Esau, Heracles, Ṛśyaśṛṅga, medieval wild men, and initiation myth can illuminate motifs, but direct borrowing requires historical transmission evidence. Similarity alone is insufficient.

Claims that Enkidu was an ancient bull god, zodiacal Taurus, a routinely worshipped deity, a surviving Nephilim, or a historical extraterrestrial are unsupported modern constructions.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 2600 BCE:** *En-ki-du₁₀-ga* or comparable name forms appear in Early Dynastic records. Connection to the hero uncertain.
- **c. 2100–1800 BCE:** Gilgameš traditions probably circulate; exact oral and written prehistory unrecoverable.
- **c. 1900–1700 BCE:** principal surviving Sumerian Gilgameš poems copied on Old Babylonian tablets; Enkidu appears as servant, companion, fighter, and netherworld traveller.
- **c. eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE:** Old Babylonian Akkadian epic unites the wild-man story with Gilgameš traditions.
- **c. fifteenth–twelfth centuries BCE:** peripheral Akkadian, Hittite, and Hurrian recensions circulate across the Late Bronze Age Near East.
- **c. thirteenth–eleventh centuries BCE:** probable formation of the Standard Babylonian edition; traditional association with Sîn-lēqi-unninni.
- **ninth–seventh centuries BCE:** earliest surviving Standard-version manuscripts.
- **seventh century BCE:** major copies enter Ashurbanipal’s library at Nineveh.
- **first millennium BCE:** Tablet XII is transmitted as an appendix translating the Sumerian Netherworld episode.
- **c. 130 BCE:** latest dated Babylonian manuscript of the epic.
- **1849 onward:** tablets from Nineveh reach modern collections.
- **1872:** George Smith publicly identifies the Gilgameš Flood account, accelerating recovery of the epic.
- **late nineteenth–early twentieth centuries:** Enkidu is read as “Eabani”; contest-scene bull-men are confidently identified with him.
- **1930s–1980s:** improved Sumerian and Akkadian philology revises the name, textual sequence, and relation between the Sumerian poems and Babylonian epic.
- **1990s–2003:** electronic corpora and George’s critical edition consolidate manuscript evidence.
- **2007–2018:** newly published fragments refine the civilizing episode and Tablet V.
- **twenty-first century:** Enkidu becomes prominent in ecological, queer, literary, neopagan, gaming, manga, and anime reception; these receptions are culturally consequential but historically modern.
