# Ninlil: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Goddess (*diĝir*, Akkadian *iltu*) in Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, and Assyrian traditions; divine queen, mother, and consort.  
> **Earliest attestation:** The sign-complex later identified with Ninlil occurs in Uruk IV–III proto-cuneiform documentation, approximately 3350–3000 BCE, conventionally read **E₂.NUN**. Because proto-cuneiform signs do not always denote named anthropomorphic gods, this is the earliest probable attestation, not an unambiguous narrative identification. Secure divine-name attestations—written **dNIN.KID**, **dNIN.LÍL₂**, and related spellings—belong to the Early Dynastic third millennium BCE.  
> **Principal identity:** Wife and co-sovereign of Enlil at Nippur; mother of Nanna/Suen and, in varying traditions, Ninurta, Nergal, Ninazu, Enbilulu, and other gods; identified with Sud of Šuruppak; later represented in Akkadian as Mulliltu and in Assyria as Mullissu, wife of Aššur, sometimes overlapping with Šerua and local Ištar goddesses.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Direct third-millennium evidence, or the probable late-fourth-millennium precursor.
- **[Old Babylonian literary]** Known principally from manuscripts of approximately 1900–1600 BCE, even when the composition may preserve older traditions.
- **[Later canonical]** Second- or first-millennium material institutionalized in learned lists, ritual series, or state theology.
- **[Regional variant]** A city- or kingdom-specific form not safely universalized.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A datable equation of previously distinguishable deities.
- **[Classical reception]** Greek or Roman testimony about Mesopotamian religion.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference rather than an explicit ancient assertion.
- **[Modern invention or revival]** A recent attribution or religious practice without evidence of ancient continuity.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Ninlil in orientation

Ninlil is principally the divine wife of Enlil, the pre-eminent god of Nippur and head of much of the Sumerian pantheon. Her identity was consequently relational but not negligible: hymns call her a great mother, enthroned queen, judge of destinies, possessor of *me*—divinely constituted powers or offices—and female head of the divine household. Her temples, estates, festivals, processions, and cult image formed part of Nippur’s institutional religion. In the literary myth *Enlil and Sud*, the goddess Sud of Šuruppak becomes Ninlil through marriage to Enlil; historically, this is best understood as a theological explanation of an already established syncretism. In Assyria, Enlil’s equation with Aššur carried his consort with him: Mulliltu/Mullissu became Aššur’s wife and acquired traits or identities belonging to Šerua and several Ištar manifestations.

Calling Ninlil simply a “wind goddess” is not secure. Her name’s second element, **LÍL₂**, was explained in a Neo-Assyrian commentary as “breeze,” with Ninlil assigned the north wind, but the explanation belongs to a late speculative system. Earlier sources characterize her much more consistently as queen, mother, spouse, distributor of destiny, and guarantor of prosperity.

### Etymology

**NIN** normally means “lady,” “mistress,” or “queen.” The problem lies in **LÍL₂**.

1. **“Lady of the breeze/wind.”**  
   **[Later learned systematization]** A first-millennium commentary, STT 2, 400 reverse 12, assigns Ninlil the north wind and interprets her as “Lady of the breeze.” This is real ancient exegesis but is not proof of the name’s fourth- or third-millennium etymology.

2. **Parallel formation with Enlil.**  
   **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Ninlil may simply be the feminine or complementary name formed beside Enlil: “Lady corresponding to Enlil,” whatever the original meaning of *lil*.

3. **“Lady of the open country.”**  
   Older translations sometimes understand *lil* as open space or air. This is possible but not demonstrable from the earliest spellings.

4. **Mulliltu as “purified one.”**  
   Akkadian *Mullil/Mulliltu* can be analyzed secondarily through *elēlu* D, “to purify.” This may be an Akkadian learned reinterpretation. The historical derivation from Enlil’s Emesal name remains linguistically more direct.

5. **Sud.**  
   Sud was originally an independent city goddess of Šuruppak. The name is sometimes connected with “distant” or “remote,” but a secure functional etymology is lacking. The equivalence Sud = Ninlil is historically more certain than Sud’s name meaning.

### Principal epithets and titles

- **“Mother Ninlil” / “Great Mother”**—frequent in Sumerian hymns and royal compositions. This is a title of senior divine status, not proof that she was originally a generalized prehistoric “Mother Goddess.”
- **“Righteous/true wife of Enlil”**—*Ninlil A*, Old Babylonian manuscript.
- **“Lady of Kiur”**—links her to her Nippur sanctuary and private divine quarters.
- **“Queen of heaven and earth,” “Queen of the lands”**—later hymnic and theological titles expressing shared sovereignty with Enlil.
- **“Lady of the gods,” “foremost lady of the Anunna gods”**—formal divine-hierarchy epithets.
- **“She who decides destinies”**—*Enlil and Sud* and *Ninlil A*.
- **“Mother who bore the divine powers”**—Isin-Larsa royal hymnography.
- **Egi-Tummal**, “Lady/Princess of Tummal”—cult title tied to the Tummal sanctuary.
- **“Lady on high”**—reflected in the ceremonial temple name **Egašanantagal**, “House of the Lady Who Is Exalted,” at Dūr-Kurigalzu.
- **Mullissu who resides in Nineveh**—Neo-Assyrian royal usage.
- **Mother/creator of the king**—Neo-Assyrian royal ideology, as when Ashurbanipal calls himself the “creation of Aššur and Mullissu.”

### Identifications

- **Sud = Ninlil:** Early third-millennium association, fully narrated in Old Babylonian *Enlil and Sud*. Secure syncretism.
- **Nisaba/Nanibgal and Nunbaršegunu:** *Enlil and Sud* calls Sud the daughter of Haia and Nisaba/Nanibgal; *Enlil and Ninlil* calls Ninlil’s mother Nunbaršegunu. *An = Anum* treats Nunbaršegunu as a name or manifestation of Nisaba. This is a learned harmonization, not necessarily the original form of both myths.
- **Ninlil = healing or mother goddesses:** Various second- and first-millennium god lists associate her with Nintur/Ninmah-type figures and local healing goddesses. These are secondary expansions.
- **Ninlil/Mullissu = Šerua:** Assyrian theology could identify Aššur’s established spouse Šerua with Mullissu after Aššur acquired Enlil’s position. Sources do not always distinguish equation, co-spousal status, and overlapping titles.
- **Mullissu = Ištar manifestations:** First-millennium sources associate or identify her particularly with Ištar of Kiš and Ištar of Arbela; at Nineveh the boundary between “Mullissu residing in Nineveh” and Ištar of Nineveh is context-dependent.
- **Mylitta = Aphrodite:** Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131 and 1.199, calls Mylitta the “Assyrian” name of Aphrodite. This is Greek *interpretatio*, not evidence that Ninlil had always been a love goddess.
- **Ningal = Ninlil:** Some late texts use **dNIN.LÍL** as an epithet of Ningal or reflect a Ningal–Ninlil syncretism, presumably facilitated by equations between their spouses, Sîn and Enlil. It is not the normal third-millennium genealogy.
- **Šalaš or Athirat:** Modern comparative tables sometimes present these West Semitic goddesses as Ninlil’s “equivalents” because each is the wife of a senior weather or creator god. Direct ancient equation is limited or absent; typological similarity must not be turned into identity.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Uruk IV–III: approximately 3350–3000 BCE

The proto-cuneiform sign conventionally named **NINLIL** or read **E₂.NUN** occurs in the earliest administrative writing. Its material support is clay tablets written in proto-cuneiform, especially from Uruk. The sign’s later lexical history permits identification with Ninlil, but the tablets do not provide a myth, genealogy, anthropomorphic image, or explicit title “wife of Enlil.” The safest statement is:

> A sign later used for Ninlil is present in late-fourth-millennium accounting; a fully individualized goddess cannot be reconstructed from those occurrences alone.

The sign’s attested palaeographic forms are catalogued by the [Proto-Cuneiform Sign List](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/pcsl/signlist/l0078/o0901578/index.html).

#### Early Dynastic period: approximately 2900–2350 BCE

By Early Dynastic III, Ninlil is securely a divine name. Spellings such as **dNIN.KID** and later **dNIN.LÍL₂** occur in lexical, offering, and administrative contexts. Sud appears as Šuruppak’s goddess in the Early Dynastic tradition, including the city sequence represented by the *zà-mì* hymns. The sources establish a goddess receiving cult and occupying a position beside Enlil; they do not yet give the complete surviving narratives known from Old Babylonian school tablets.

The ePSD royal-text index records Ninlil in Early Dynastic IIIb, Old Akkadian, Ur III, Old Babylonian, and Neo-Assyrian corpora: [ePSD Ninlil divine-name entry](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/royal/cbd/qpn/x000000510.html).

### Major primary corpus

#### *Enlil and Ninlil* — ETCSL 1.2.1

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Surviving manuscripts:** Primarily Old Babylonian, approximately 1900–1600 BCE; several derive from Nippur’s scribal culture.
- **Probable composition:** Possibly late third millennium or early second millennium, but the surviving text should not be automatically projected into Early Dynastic religion.
- **Role:** Ninlil is the young woman approached by Enlil; after she refuses his request for sex and kissing, intercourse occurs, she conceives Suen, and Enlil is expelled as ritually impure. She follows him toward the underworld, where further disguised unions produce Nergal/Meslamtaea, Ninazu, and Enbilulu as substitutes allowing Suen to ascend.
- **Text:** [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.1.2.1).

The composition’s surviving wording matters: Ninlil says no to Enlil’s stated wishes, and the narrator subsequently states that he had intercourse with her. It does not describe her consenting before that first act. Whether to label the episode “rape,” “defloration,” or illicit premarital intercourse has been disputed in modern scholarship; the textual refusal and Enlil’s subsequent ritual expulsion must remain visible.

#### *Enlil and Sud* / *The Marriage of Sud* — ETCSL 1.2.2

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Surviving manuscripts:** Mainly Old Babylonian tablets from Nippur and other collections; one later Susa witness preserves a variant.
- **Critical edition:** Miguel Civil, “Enlil and Sud,” *JAOS* 103 (1983), 43–66.
- **Role:** Sud, daughter of Haia and Nisaba/Nanibgal, rejects Enlil’s insulting or overly direct approach. Enlil sends Nuska with a formal proposal and lavish bride-gifts. After marriage, Sud is enthroned in the Ekur and renamed Ninlil.
- **Text and witnesses:** [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr122.htm); [ETCSL bibliography and tablet list](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/b122.htm).

The story differs fundamentally from *Enlil and Ninlil*: it stages negotiation, maternal authorization, bridewealth, procession, marriage, and consensual conjugal union. It should not be harmonized into a single continuous biography with the river-bank narrative.

#### *Ninlil A* — an *adab* to Ninlil, ETCSL 4.24.1

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Manuscript:** JRL E 4 + 26; Old Babylonian literary tablet.
- **Role:** Direct praise of Ninlil as possessor of divine powers, co-decider of destiny with Enlil, great mother, source of prosperity, and resident of the Kiur/Ekur.
- **Edition history:** Claus Wilcke, “Sumerische literarische Texte in Manchester und Liverpool,” *AfO* 24 (1973), 1–17.
- **Text:** [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/tr4241.htm); [manuscript bibliography](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/b4241.htm).

#### *Nanna-Suen’s Journey to Nippur* — ETCSL 1.5.1

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Manuscripts:** Old Babylonian.
- **Role:** Nanna travels to the parental cult center. Ninlil appears as the “great mother,” richly clothed and receiving or participating in the divine household’s abundance. This composition reinforces the genealogy Nanna = son of Enlil and Ninlil.

#### Hymns to Nanna

An Old Babylonian hymn has Nanna request his mother to speak to his father; Ninlil intercedes with Enlil for their son. This is a rare narrative action outside the two principal myths: [ETCSL hymn to Nanna](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.13.a).

#### *Hymn to the Ekur* — ETCSL 4.80.4

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Role:** Describes the houses and courts of Enlil, Ninlil, and Ninurta; calls Ninurta or another princely son Ninlil’s offspring; documents the cultic architecture imagined around the Ekur.
- **Text:** [ETCSL translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/tr4804.htm).

#### *Tummal Chronicle*

- **Language:** Sumerian.
- **Form:** Historiographic or antiquarian chronicle, compiled from earlier traditions.
- **Role:** Successive rulers rebuild Enlil’s Nippur temple and “make the Tummal splendid,” then “bring Ninlil into it.” It ties Ninlil’s cultic installation to Enmebaragesi, Aga, Mesannepada, Meskiagnuna, Gilgamesh, Ur-Nungal, Ur-Namma, and Šulgi.
- **Historical caution:** It cannot be used as a simple contemporary record of all the Early Dynastic rulers it names. Its value lies in how later Mesopotamian historiography remembered cycles of temple restoration.
- **Text:** [Tummal Chronicle translation](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/cm-7-tummal-chronicle/).

#### Royal hymns, laments, and building inscriptions

Ur III and Isin-Larsa compositions repeatedly invoke Enlil and Ninlil as a ruling pair:

- *Ur-Namma B*: the king gladdens Ninlil and provides a banquet for Enlil and Ninlil.
- *Lament for Nippur*: the pair return to their restored seats, deliberate for the welfare of the population, and reconstitute cultic order.
- *Išme-Dagan W*: they are father and mother of the Anunna, set terrestrial boundaries, and install powers in Ninurta’s hands.
- A prayer for Sîn-iddinam has Nanna appeal to Enlil and Ninlil for the king’s longevity: [ETCSL Sîn-iddinam prayer](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.6.6.1).
- Ur III administrative tablets record offerings, temple personnel, fields, textiles, animals, boats, and festivals associated with Ninlil and Tummal.

#### God lists

- **Early Dynastic lists:** Establish early placement and orthographies but contain little narrative.
- **Weidner god list:** Sud can occur among healing goddesses rather than simply beside Enlil, preserving an alternative functional placement.
- **Proto-*An = Anum* and *An = Anum*:** The great god list was shaped across the second millennium. Its Enlil section places Ninlil as Enlil’s wife and catalogues names, manifestations, relatives, and court personnel. It identifies Nunbaršegunu with Nisaba and systematizes Sud/Ninlil.
- **Evidentiary status:** **[Later learned systematization]**. These lists are indispensable evidence for Babylonian theology but are not neutral transcripts of a single prehistoric pantheon. A modern edition and synopsis is available in [*An = Anum and Related Lists*](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783161613838_A47326675/preview-9783161613838_A47326675.pdf).

#### Astronomical lists and commentaries

First-millennium scholarly texts associate Ninlil with:

- **Ursa Major**, the Wagon constellation, *MUL.MAR.GÍD.DA* / Akkadian *ereqqu*.
- **Lyra**, represented through the Goat constellation.
- The north wind in a speculative divine-wind commentary.
- An “Asterism of Abundance,” called Ninlil’s messenger in *MUL.APIN*.

These are **[Later learned systematizations]**, not proof that Ninlil began as a stellar deity.

#### Assyrian royal inscriptions

Neo-Assyrian inscriptions provide the strongest late institutional evidence:

- Esarhaddon calls himself beloved of Aššur and Mullissu and presents their protection as the source of kingship: [RINAP, Esarhaddon 58](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/rinap4/Q003287/).
- Ashurbanipal calls himself “the creation of Aššur and Mullissu”; another royal text lists Mullissu beside Aššur in the command authorizing royal action: [RINAP, Ashurbanipal 11](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/rinap5/Q003710).
- Other royal texts call her “Mullissu who resides in Nineveh,” while separately naming Ištar of Nineveh and Ištar of Arbela. This proves coexistence in some contexts, not complete identity in every text.
- The RINAP catalogue preserves a specific “Mullissu Inscription” from Nineveh among Ashurbanipal’s building texts: [RINAP score catalogue](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/scores/pager).

### The named corpora that do **not** center Ninlil

Absence is important:

- **The Akkadian *Atrahasis Epic*** centers Enlil, Enki/Ea, the birth goddess Mami/Nintu/Bēlet-ilī, and the lesser gods. Ninlil is not a principal actor. Identifying Bēlet-ilī automatically with Ninlil would collapse distinct goddesses.
- **The canonical *Enūma eliš*** centers Apsu, Tiamat, Ea, Marduk, and the promotion of Babylon. Ninlil is not a major narrative figure. Her omission reflects the work’s Marduk-centered theology, not evidence that her cult had vanished.
- **The Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh***, in Andrew George’s critical edition, includes Enlil, Ea, Šamaš, Sîn, Ištar, Ninsun, and others, but Ninlil is not a significant narrative agent.
- **Sumerian *Inanna’s Descent*** has Ninšubur appeal to Enlil, Nanna, and Enki. Ninlil does not intervene. The composite’s Old Babylonian provenance and text can be checked in [CDLI P468903](https://cdli.earth/artifacts/468903).
- **Enki and Ninmah** concerns human creation by Enki and Ninmah/Namma-associated mother-goddess theology. Ninmah should not be renamed Ninlil without a source-specific equation.
- **The Enūma Anu Enlil omen series** contains Enlil in its title and astral theology but is not thereby a corpus about Ninlil.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

#### Divine queenship

**[Early and continuing]** Ninlil’s most securely attested sphere is queenship beside Enlil. She shares his dais, receives homage from rulers and gods, decides destinies, and supervises the divine household. In *Enlil and Sud*, marriage transforms the local goddess Sud into the queen Ninlil and grants her authority over the Anunna’s divine powers.

#### Motherhood and legitimate succession

She is repeatedly “Mother Ninlil.” Her motherhood covers:

- Biological or mythic maternity of major gods.
- Institutional maternity toward the Anunna.
- Royal maternity in Assyrian ideology.
- Mediation between divine father and son, as in the hymn where she petitions Enlil for Suen.

This should not be generalized into an undocumented domain of “all fertility.” Agricultural prosperity appears in hymns and in the Sud complex, but her clearest function is senior divine maternity and legitimate transmission of authority.

#### Destiny, judgment, and sovereignty

*Ninlil A* calls her a co-decider of destinies; *Enlil and Sud* grants her authority to apportion the *me* among the Anunna. Royal texts place her in the Kiur “rendering verdicts with grandeur.” These are not merely borrowed honorifics: they reflect her operative place in the Nippur state cult.

#### Prosperity, grain, textiles, and household abundance

*Enlil and Sud* associates Sud/Ninlil with:

- Grain and flax.
- Agricultural measurement and boundary-making.
- Canals and irrigation.
- Scribal tools, accounting, and surveying.
- Marriage wealth and the ordered household.

Some of this derives from Sud’s mother Nisaba, goddess of grain and writing. The poem itself dramatizes the transfer and accumulation of functions; these should not all be assumed to belong to an original Ninlil.

#### Healing

Sud’s position among healing goddesses in some lists and her later equations allowed Ninlil to acquire healing associations. This is **[Syncretistic and secondary]**, not her most characteristic Nippur role.

#### Wind and air

Ninlil’s widely repeated description as “goddess of air” depends chiefly on translating her name parallel to “Enlil, Lord Wind.” The only explicit ancient north-wind system is late. Therefore:

> Ninlil was capable of wind association, especially in learned first-millennium theology, but the evidence does not securely establish “air goddess” as her original or defining function.

### Characteristic powers and actions

- Determines fates with Enlil.
- Apportions divine offices or *me*.
- Confers legitimacy and long reigns.
- Gives birth to astral, chthonic, warrior, and irrigation gods.
- Intercedes between son and spouse.
- Receives royal offerings and temple construction.
- Travels ceremonially by boat and chariot.
- In Assyria, protects the king, forms him in the womb, and authorizes imperial rule.
- In *Enlil and Ninlil*, follows Enlil beyond Nippur and participates in the generation of underworld substitutes.
- In *Enlil and Sud*, undergoes a formal status transformation from city goddess and daughter to pantheon queen.

### Iconography

Very little securely identified Ninlil imagery survives.

#### Anthropomorphic form

As a Mesopotamian goddess she would normally be represented in a horned divine headdress and long robe, seated or standing. A few Ur III seals have been proposed to show an enthroned Ninlil receiving a worshiper, especially where an inscription appeals to her mediation with Enlil. Without an identifying caption, however, an enthroned goddess cannot be securely named.

An inscribed Old Babylonian seal fragment invokes “Ninlil, lady of the lands” to intercede with Enlil, documenting her intercessory function even if the pictured goddess remains subject to iconographic caution: [Smithsonian discussion of Mesopotamian seal inscriptions](https://publications.asia.si.edu/seals/mesopotamian-cylinder-seal-inscriptions.php).

#### Assyrian imagery

After Mullissu’s overlap with Ištar, her imagery could borrow:

- The lion.
- Radiance or a star-like divine aura.
- Armed or enthroned goddess types.
- Royal-couple imagery pairing a god and goddess.

Yet these features usually identify Ištar more readily than Mullissu. A scene of Aššur beside a goddess does not automatically prove the goddess is Mullissu unless inscription or architectural context supports it.

#### Symbols and constellations

- **Ursa Major / Wagon:** Explicit first-millennium learned association.
- **Lyra / Goat:** Another astronomical identification.
- **Horned crown:** Generic divine regalia, not Ninlil-specific.
- **Throne/dais:** Strong textual association but not a uniquely identifying emblem.
- **Temple, bedchamber, processional boat, textiles, grain and flax:** Cultic associations, not fixed iconographic attributes.

#### Unsupported modern identifications

The Burney/“Queen of the Night” relief has been variously called Ištar, Ereškigal, Lilith, and—in some modern polytheist circles—Ninlil. No inscription names the figure. A Ninlil identification is **[Modern speculation]**, not a recognized secure attribution.

No ancient evidence assigns Ninlil a fixed sacred color, weekday, universal number, or modern-style sigil. Lists circulating online that supply these are modern inventions unless tied to a cited omen, ritual, or lexical tablet.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Parentage

| Tradition | Parents | Source and date | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ninlil of *Enlil and Ninlil* | Mother: Nunbaršegunu; father not named in the surviving narrative | Old Babylonian manuscripts | **[Old Babylonian literary]** |
| Sud/Ninlil of *Enlil and Sud* | Haia and Nisaba/Nanibgal | Old Babylonian manuscripts; may preserve older Šuruppak/Ereš theology | **[Regional tradition, later literary form]** |
| Learned harmonization | Nunbaršegunu is a name of Nisaba | *An = Anum* and related lexical theology | **[Later learned systematization]** |
| Broad mother-goddess equations | Parentage can disappear when Ninlil is treated as Nintur/Ninmah-like | God lists and syncretic texts | **[Syncretistic]** |

The two myths should not be silently reconciled by saying that “Nunbaršegunu/Nisaba” is simply her mother in all periods. That identity belongs to learned theology and may have been created precisely to harmonize distinct traditions.

### Spouses

- **Enlil:** Universally dominant southern Mesopotamian tradition.
- **Aššur:** Assyrian development following Aššur’s appropriation of Enlil’s rank and characteristics.
- No separate mortal consort or alternative husband is securely attested for Ninlil proper.

### Children

| Child | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Nanna/Suen/Sîn** | *Enlil and Ninlil*; hymns and cult tradition | Most stable and widespread son. |
| **Nergal/Meslamtaea** | *Enlil and Ninlil* | Born as an underworld substitute for Suen. |
| **Ninazu** | *Enlil and Ninlil*; Temple Hymns and other traditions | One genealogy among several; elsewhere Ninazu has different parents. |
| **Enbilulu** | *Enlil and Ninlil* | Irrigation/canal god born in the final disguised union. |
| **Ninurta** | Numerous Nippur hymns and theological texts | Frequently son of Enlil and Ninlil, but other traditions associate his counterpart Ningirsu with a local mother such as Bau or Ninhursag. |
| **Nergal** | Also generalized in later genealogical summaries | Secure in the specific myth, not exclusive across all Nergal traditions. |
| **Išum** | Single Old Babylonian fragment concerning Išum’s origin | Probably caused by confusion between Sud and Sudaĝ, rather than a stable genealogy. |
| **Pabilsag** | Through equations of Ninlil with mother-goddess figures | Secondary syncretistic genealogy. |
| Other “children of Enlil” | God lists and local hymns | Do not automatically make Ninlil the mother unless a source says so. |

### Divine hierarchy

In third-millennium Nippur theology, Ninlil stands immediately beside Enlil. She is not merely one goddess among his anonymous household. Her cult estate, Kiur residence, throne, and power to decide destinies make her the female head of the pantheon. Under Babylon’s elevation of Marduk, the Nippur couple lost political centrality but retained canonical antiquity. In Assyria, Mullissu regained active royal prominence as Aššur’s consort.

### Every documented named divine interaction

The following list separates an interaction from mere co-membership in a pantheon.

- **Enlil — first river-bank sexual encounter and conception of Suen.** Ninlil refuses Enlil’s request; he has intercourse with her; the divine assembly expels him as ritually impure. *Enlil and Ninlil*, Old Babylonian manuscripts. **[Old Babylonian literary; interpretation contested]**

- **Enlil — pursuit into the underworld and three disguised unions.** Ninlil follows him; he impersonates gatekeeper, underworld river-man, and ferryman; she speaks with each disguise and conceives three chthonic substitute gods. *Enlil and Ninlil*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Enlil — courtship and negotiated marriage.** Sud rebukes him, he sends a formal proposal and bride-gifts through Nuska, and after marriage he renames and enthrones her as Ninlil. *Enlil and Sud*. **[Old Babylonian literary account of syncretism]**

- **Enlil — co-rule and shared judgment.** They decide destinies, banquet together, deliberate for humanity, and occupy the Ekur/Kiur. *Ninlil A*, royal hymns, Nippur laments, and cult texts, late third to early second millennium. **[Secure cultic/literary]**

- **Nanna/Suen — conception and maternal relationship.** Ninlil conceives Suen in the first union with Enlil. *Enlil and Ninlil*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Nanna/Suen — intercession with Enlil.** Suen asks his mother to speak to his father; Ninlil addresses Enlil on their son’s behalf. Hymn to Nanna, Old Babylonian. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Nanna/Suen — filial visit and cultic reception.** Nanna journeys to Nippur, his parents’ center; Ninlil appears as great mother. *Nanna-Suen’s Journey to Nippur*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Nergal/Meslamtaea — conception as underworld substitute.** Conceived when Enlil, disguised as the Nippur gatekeeper, has intercourse with Ninlil. *Enlil and Ninlil*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Ninazu — conception as underworld substitute.** Conceived when Enlil appears as the man of the man-eating underworld river. *Enlil and Ninlil*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Enbilulu — conception as underworld substitute.** Conceived in the ferryman episode. *Enlil and Ninlil*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Nuska — assists Enlil’s first approach.** In *Enlil and Ninlil*, Enlil orders Nuska to take him by boat toward Ninlil. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Nuska — marriage envoy.** In *Enlil and Sud*, Nuska carries the proposal and gifts to Sud’s mother and reports her acceptance. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Nunbaršegunu — maternal instruction.** She warns Ninlil about bathing or walking by the canal and about Enlil’s intentions. *Enlil and Ninlil*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Nisaba/Nanibgal — mother and marriage negotiator.** She raises Sud, receives Nuska, accepts the marriage after compensation for Enlil’s insult, and blesses her daughter. *Enlil and Sud*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Haia — father of Sud.** He impregnates Nisaba, resulting in Sud’s birth; he does not otherwise act directly with the adult Ninlil in the surviving narrative. *Enlil and Sud*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Aruru — escort and sister-in-law.** Aruru leads Sud into the Emah/Ekur and is assigned a household role; Enlil renames or titles her Nintud. *Enlil and Sud*. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Ninmah — participates in the wedding procession.** A fragmentary passage places Ninmah with Nuska and the bridewealth convoy. *Enlil and Sud*. **[Old Babylonian literary; damaged context]**

- **Enki and Ninki deities — perfect Ninlil’s divine powers.** *Ninlil A* says the ancestral Enki-Ninki group perfects her powers throughout the lands. **[Old Babylonian hymnic]**

- **Ninurta — son empowered by the parental couple.** Enlil and Ninlil place heaven’s and earth’s divine powers in Ninurta’s hands and install him in the Ekur. *Išme-Dagan W* and related Nippur hymns. **[Late third-/early second-millennium royal theology]**

- **Ninurta — entry into the Tummal.** *Song of the Hoe* places Ninurta before Enlil in the inner chamber of Ninlil’s Tummal sanctuary. Ninlil herself is named as owner/mother but does not speak. **[Old Babylonian literary cult-topography]**

- **Ninisina — offering at Tummal.** The same composition has Ninisina enter before Enlil with kids and fruit in Ninlil’s Tummal. Direct conversation with Ninlil is not attested. **[Old Babylonian literary]**

- **Sud — identity transformation.** Sud does not “meet” Ninlil as a separate character; Enlil declares that Sud will bear the name Ninlil. *Enlil and Sud*. **[Explicit syncretistic transformation]**

- **Nisaba — learned identity with Nunbaršegunu.** God-list theology treats Ninlil’s two maternal figures as names of one goddess. **[Later learned systematization]**

- **Aššur — divine marriage and joint royal patronage.** Assyrian inscriptions invoke Aššur and Mullissu together as parents, creators, protectors, and legitimators of kings. Middle and Neo-Assyrian evidence, especially first millennium BCE. **[Regional Assyrian state theology]**

- **Šerua — identification or convergence as Aššur’s spouse.** As Mullissu assumed the Enlil-consort’s place beside Aššur, she overlapped with Šerua. **[Assyrian syncretistic identification]**

- **Ištar of Arbela — identification or functional overlap.** First-millennium theological texts associate Ninlil/Mullissu with this Ištar, while royal inscriptions can also list them separately. **[Regional syncretism, not universal identity]**

- **Ištar of Nineveh — residence and iconographic overlap.** Mullissu may be described as resident at Nineveh, but some royal lists distinguish Mullissu from Ištar of Nineveh. **[Regional convergence with continuing distinction]**

- **Ištar of Kiš — equation.** First-millennium lexical/theological tradition identifies Ninlil with the Kiš goddess. **[Later syncretistic identification]**

- **Ningal — occasional equation or epithet.** Late royal or learned texts can write Ningal as *dNIN.LÍL* or merge their status through spouse-equations. **[Late syncretism]**

- **Išum — alleged maternity.** A single damaged Old Babylonian tradition may make Ninlil/Sud his mother; scholars suspect confusion with Sudaĝ. **[Isolated variant; disputed]**

- **Aphrodite — Greek equation.** Herodotus equates Mylitta with Aphrodite; there is no Mesopotamian narrative of a meeting between them. **[Classical interpretatio]**

No direct interaction between Ninlil and Inanna in *Inanna’s Descent*, between Ninlil and Marduk in *Enūma eliš*, or between Ninlil and the protagonists of the Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh* epic is securely recorded.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 *Enlil and Ninlil*

#### Narrative

The story opens in Nippur, naming the city, its canals, quay, well, and cultivated territory. Enlil is one of its young men; Ninlil is one of its young women; Nunbaršegunu is an older woman.

Nunbaršegunu warns her daughter against bathing in the holy river or walking along the Idnunbir-tum canal because Enlil will see her, desire intercourse, impregnate her, and leave. Ninlil nevertheless bathes and walks beside the canal.

Enlil sees her and asks for sex and a kiss. Ninlil refuses: her body is young, she does not know pregnancy or kissing, and she fears her parents’ reaction. Enlil tells Nuska about her. Nuska transports his master by boat. The narrator then states that Enlil grasps Ninlil and has intercourse with her; Suen is conceived.

The fifty great gods and seven destiny-deciding gods arrest Enlil in the Kiur and expel him from Nippur as ritually impure. Ninlil follows him.

At three boundary stations Enlil assumes the identities of:

1. The city gatekeeper.
2. The man of the Idkura, the man-eating river.
3. The ferryman SI.LU.IGI.

Ninlil asks after Enlil. In each episode the disguised god says that Enlil’s seed may ascend to heaven while his own seed should descend. The unions produce Meslamtaea/Nergal, Ninazu, and Enbilulu. These children function as underworld counterparts or substitutions so that Suen may rise to the sky.

The poem ends not by condemning Enlil but by praising his immutable commands and “Ninlil the mother.”

#### Dating and witnesses

The surviving manuscripts are Old Babylonian; the geographic specificity of Nippur and the cultic vocabulary suggest older local traditions, but no complete Early Dynastic version survives.

#### Consent dispute

- **Alhena Gadotti:** The refusal followed by intercourse is intelligible as rape, and Sumerian literature possesses ways of conceptualizing sexual coercion.
- **Jo Ann Scurlock:** The issue is illicit defloration or social “ruination” rather than rape in the modern legal sense.
- **Gwendolyn Leick and others:** Caution against mechanically applying modern legal categories to divine myth.
- **Textual finding:** Whatever terminology is chosen, Ninlil explicitly refuses Enlil’s first request; the text does not record her subsequent consent before the intercourse. Enlil, not Ninlil, is expelled as impure. The later three encounters are differently staged: Ninlil recognizes the logic of substitution and offers or accepts further intercourse in the dialogue, though Enlil conceals his identity.

A synopsis of the modern dispute is preserved in [Gadotti’s “Why It Was Rape” abstract](https://law-journals-books.vlex.com/vid/conceptualization-sumerian-literature-198518643).

#### Function

Proposed functions are not mutually exclusive:

- Explains why the moon god rises to heaven while three associated gods remain below.
- Establishes a Nippur-to-underworld geography.
- Connects sexuality, ritual impurity, exile, and return.
- Constructs the divine family.
- Explains substitution in the organization of cosmic offices.
- Represents the transition of Enlil and Ninlil from unmarried youth to parental authority.

### 5.2 *Enlil and Sud*

#### Narrative

Sud is a beautiful young goddess raised in the house of Nisaba at Ereš. Enlil sees her and speaks in a manner Sud considers insulting or threatening to her reputation. She rejects him and goes inside.

Enlil then follows socially legitimate procedure. He sends Nuska to Nisaba/Nanibgal with a marriage proposal, jewelry, and promises:

- Sud will be renamed Ninlil.
- She will live with Enlil in the Ekur and Kiur.
- She will sit on an august dais.
- She will determine destinies.
- She will apportion divine powers among the Anunna.
- Her mother will be entrusted with human lives.

Nisaba accepts, treating the bride-gifts as amends for Enlil’s earlier offense. A vast convoy follows: livestock, wild animals, dairy produce, honey, fruit, precious stones, metals, and other wealth. Aruru escorts Sud into the Ekur. Enlil and Sud consummate the marriage. Enlil then confers names and functions; Sud becomes Ninlil, queen beside Enlil.

#### Variants

Version B, represented by a later manuscript from Susa, condenses the enthronement and naming. It explicitly says: “From now on, Sud … Ninlil,” and contrasts Enlil as king with Ninlil as queen.

#### Historical interpretation

The poem is not evidence that a human woman literally became a goddess. It is a literary charter for integrating:

- Sud of Šuruppak.
- Nisaba’s grain-and-writing complex.
- Enlil’s Nippur theology.
- Formal marriage, bridewealth, and divine sovereignty.

The syncretism was already theological history when the Old Babylonian copies were written.

### 5.3 The Tummal restoration cycle

The *Tummal Chronicle* repeatedly pairs:

1. A king building or restoring Enlil’s structure.
2. His successor embellishing Tummal.
3. Ninlil’s ceremonial entry.

This sequence makes Ninlil’s procession and installation a marker of restored kingship. Its concern is cultic history rather than mythic birth, but it functions as a narrative cycle in which repeated temple renewal reconstitutes the divine couple’s presence.

### 5.4 Nanna’s return to the parental center

*Nanna-Suen’s Journey to Nippur* presents the moon god traveling by boat with gifts to Nippur. Ninlil belongs to the parental household that receives abundance. The myth maps political and cultic ties between Ur, Nippur, and their divine dynasties. The journey also supports the idea that local gods could acknowledge Nippur’s senior authority without losing their home cults.

### 5.5 Assyrian royal parenthood

Neo-Assyrian inscriptions turn the divine marriage into an imperial birth narrative: Aššur and Mullissu create, protect, and enthrone the king. This is not a single continuous mythic poem, but a repeated ideological narrative. The king becomes the earthly child or product of the divine ruling pair, and imperial conquest is authorized as their command.

### 5.6 The alleged Išum-birth myth

One Old Babylonian fragment appears to connect Ninlil or Sud with Išum’s birth and abandonment. Because Sudaĝ is otherwise known as Išum’s mother or as a goddess in his familial sphere, Manfred Krebernik interprets the Ninlil connection as scribal or theological confusion between **Sud** and **Sudaĝ**. It must remain an isolated variant, not part of a canonical Ninlil biography.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Nippur

#### Ekur and Kiur

The Ekur was Enlil’s principal sanctuary and the symbolic “bond of heaven and earth.” Ninlil possessed her own spaces within or adjacent to its complex:

- **Kiur / Ekiur:** Her principal Nippur sanctuary or residential cult complex.
- **Ĝaĝiššua:** Storehouse or chamber promised to Sud in *Enlil and Sud* and used in royal hymnography as a place where Ninlil decrees destinies.
- **Ešutumkiagga:** “House, beloved storeroom,” associated with Ur-Namma’s building activity.
- **Emi-Tummal:** A Tummal-related shrine.
- **Abzu-Ninlil:** A structure or basin attested in Ur III documentation.
- Additional chapels known from administrative texts but incompletely named.

The archaeological plan of Nippur is imperfectly matched to the many ceremonial names in literary and topographical texts. A named “house” may be a freestanding temple, chapel, court, storehouse, or ritual station.

### Tummal

Tummal was Ninlil’s major processional center near Nippur, though its exact archaeological location has not been established. Ur III documentation suggests:

- A major festival in the eighth month.
- Processions of Ninlil, Enlil, and Ninurta.
- Transport by barge or canal.
- Animal, grain, dairy, beer, and textile offerings.
- Regular deliveries from royal and temple estates.
- Lustrations and offerings associated with other goddesses, including Nintinugga.

The cult statue’s “entry” into Tummal was a politically charged act commemorated in royal year names and the *Tummal Chronicle*.

### Šuruppak

Sud was Šuruppak’s patron goddess. Early Dynastic evidence from Fara documents the city’s religious and administrative system, though reconstructing a specific excavated “Sud temple” remains difficult. The later claim that Sud became Ninlil reflects absorption into Nippur’s wider theological order, not the disappearance of every local feature.

### Ereš

*Enlil and Sud* places Sud in Ereš, city of Nisaba, rather than Šuruppak. This reflects her genealogical integration with Nisaba and may preserve a learned displacement or fusion of local cult traditions. Ereš’s exact archaeological identification remains disputed.

### Dūr-Kurigalzu

Kassite king Kurigalzu I, in the fourteenth century BCE, constructed a temple sector for Ninlil at Dūr-Kurigalzu, modern ʿAqar Qūf:

- Enlil’s main sanctuary stood in the central portion.
- Ninlil’s **Egašanantagal**, “House of the Exalted Lady,” occupied the northeastern sector.
- Ninurta’s sanctuary occupied another portion.
- Inscribed bricks and architectural remains confirm the divine-family arrangement.

This was a deliberate reproduction of Nippur theology in the Kassite royal capital.

### Assur

When Aššur was identified with Enlil, Mullissu received cult beside him. Assyrian sanctuary nomenclature sometimes reproduced Nippur names, creating an architectural claim that Assur was the new cosmic center. Esarhaddon’s inscriptions describe restoration of Aššur’s temple and continued devotion to Mullissu.

### Nineveh and Arbela

Mullissu was worshiped or said to reside at Nineveh. Her presence interacted with the much older Ištar of Nineveh cult. At Arbela she could overlap with Ištar of Arbela. Royal inscriptions sometimes list these goddesses separately, proving that theological equation did not necessarily erase cultic plurality.

### Other cult places

Textual attestations place Ninlil or her sanctuaries at:

- Isin.
- Kiš.
- Ur.
- Babylonian provincial centers.
- Assyrian royal capitals and temple complexes.

In many cases the evidence is a shrine name, offering list, or royal inscription rather than a fully excavated temple.

### Priesthood and personnel

The evidence does not reveal a single empire-wide “priesthood of Ninlil.” Her institutions employed personnel familiar from Mesopotamian temples:

- Administrative officials and scribes.
- Brewers, bakers, herders, textile workers, gardeners, and boatmen.
- Lamentation and musical personnel.
- Purification specialists.
- Temple servants of varying legal and economic status.
- Female religious personnel whose exact offices changed by period.

Assertions that Ninlil’s temples maintained a class of “sacred prostitutes” are not supported by the cuneiform administrative evidence.

### Offerings and ritual

Documented or strongly evidenced practices include:

- Sheep, goats, cattle, birds, fish, grain, bread, beer, dates, oils, aromatics, and textiles.
- Cult-statue clothing and ornamentation.
- Processions between Nippur and Tummal.
- Ritual meals shared conceptually by Enlil and Ninlil.
- Temple renovation and dedication of inscribed objects.
- Royal prayers for long life, legitimacy, and victory.
- Lustration and purification.
- Presentation of first fruits and agricultural produce.
- Festival journeys by boat.

### Theophoric names

Names invoking Ninlil are attested but never rival the enormous popularity of names built with Sîn, Šamaš, Ištar, Marduk, or Nabû in their peak periods. Their distribution nevertheless confirms that she was not merely a literary figure. Names and dedications occur in Early Dynastic, Akkadian, Ur III, Old Babylonian, and Assyrian documentation.

### Economy and politics

Ninlil’s temples owned or administered:

- Agricultural land.
- Herds.
- Grain stores.
- Textile production.
- Labor obligations.
- Canal transport and boats.
- Offerings transferred from royal centers.

Royal sponsorship of Ninlil was political because control of Nippur’s divine household helped validate rule over Sumer and Akkad. In Assyria, invocation of Aššur and Mullissu made the same structure explicitly imperial.

### Human sacrifice

No securely identified ritual of human sacrifice to Ninlil is known. Mesopotamian archaeology does contain debated cases of retainer death—most famously the Royal Cemetery at Ur—but those cannot be assigned to Ninlil without direct evidence. Her myths of underworld substitution concern divine births, not a documented human sacrificial liturgy.

### “Temple prostitution” and Mylitta

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, reports that every Babylonian woman once sat in Aphrodite/Mylitta’s precinct and had intercourse with a stranger who threw money into her lap: [*Histories* 1.199](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D1&force=y).

The evidence must be separated:

- **Documented:** Herodotus made this claim and named Mylitta.
- **Not documented:** No Babylonian ritual tablet, law, administrative account, or temple archive corroborates a universal requirement.
- **Identification problem:** Herodotus’ Mylitta may reflect Mulliltu, but the love-goddess setting also suggests Ištar/Aphrodite interpretatio; it cannot automatically be assigned to the historical Nippur cult of Ninlil.
- **Current scholarly trend:** Ancient Near Eastern and Classical scholarship increasingly rejects “sacred prostitution” as a coherent institution. Stephanie Budin argues that the Babylonian account is an ethnographic or political construction and that later authors amplified it. A review of the evidence and its present reception is available in [Budin, “How Sacred Prostitution Is Faring”](https://www.lockwoodonlinejournals.com/index.php/jaos/article/view/2176).

Accordingly, “Ninlil’s temple prostitution” is an allegation in Greek reception and modern historiography, not an established Mesopotamian cult practice.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### I. Proto-cuneiform background, approximately 3350–3000 BCE

A sign later tied to Ninlil appears in the earliest writing. No recoverable myth, image, or genealogy accompanies it. Whether this denotes a sanctuary, institutional household, or named goddess is unresolved.

### II. Early Dynastic formation, approximately 2900–2350 BCE

Ninlil becomes securely recognizable as a goddess beside Enlil. Sud is prominent at Šuruppak. The separate histories of Ninlil and Sud begin to converge, probably through political and theological relations among Nippur, Šuruppak, and northern Sumerian scribal centers.

Nippur’s growing supralocal prestige raised both Enlil and his consort. The Early Dynastic material still preserves regional alternatives: Sud’s healing associations and local identity should not be erased.

### III. Akkadian period, approximately 2334–2154 BCE

Enlil retained high status under Akkadian kings, and Ninlil continued in Nippur cult. Royal inscriptions and administrative texts invoke the couple. Akkadian imperial patronage did not replace them with Semitic deities; instead, bilingual divine nomenclature expanded.

### IV. Ur III apogee, approximately 2112–2004 BCE

This is one of Ninlil’s best-documented institutional periods:

- Nippur functioned as a major ideological center.
- Tummal received royal rebuilding and festival investment.
- Administrative tablets document offerings and temple economy.
- Royal year names commemorated the making or installation of cult objects.
- Ur-Namma and Šulgi associated their dynastic legitimacy with Enlil and Ninlil.
- Processions linked the royal redistribution center near Puzriš-Dagan with Nippur’s divine household.

Calling this Ninlil’s “peak” is justified for the southern institutional record, though her later Assyrian political profile was also very high.

### V. Isin-Larsa and Old Babylonian literary canon, approximately 2000–1595 BCE

Nippur’s political control changed hands, but its scribal schools preserved and elaborated Ninlil’s mythology. Most manuscripts of *Enlil and Ninlil*, *Enlil and Sud*, *Ninlil A*, Nanna hymns, and related compositions belong to this period.

The contrast between the two marriage narratives demonstrates active theological plurality:

- One explains cosmic substitution through illicit or coercive sex and exile.
- The other presents formal courtship, bridewealth, maternal approval, and enthronement.

### VI. Kassite and Middle Babylonian period, approximately 1595–1155 BCE

Kassite rulers cultivated Nippur and replicated its divine-family theology at Dūr-Kurigalzu. Ninlil’s temple there shows that she remained institutionally significant despite Marduk’s growing prominence in Babylon.

God-list theology increasingly catalogued her names and equations. Sud’s independent identity was progressively subordinated to Ninlil in canonical scholarship.

### VII. Middle and Neo-Assyrian transformation, approximately 1400–609 BCE

Aššur acquired Enlil’s status; Mulliltu/Mullissu consequently became his spouse. This was not simply a phonetic renaming but a political relocation of cosmic sovereignty.

In Neo-Assyrian inscriptions Mullissu:

- Protects kings.
- Creates them in conjunction with Aššur.
- Receives temples and inscriptions.
- Resides at Nineveh.
- Overlaps with Šerua and Ištar manifestations.
- Is invoked in treaties, building inscriptions, and royal titulary.

The development from *Mulliltu* to *Mullissu* reflects Assyrian phonology. Her independent visibility sometimes increased because the imperial king represented Aššur and Mullissu as his divine parents.

### VIII. Neo-Babylonian and Persian periods, 626–331 BCE

Marduk and Zarpanitu dominated Babylonian civic theology, but Ninlil persisted in lists, rituals, local cult, and antiquarian learning. Herodotus’ Mylitta testimony belongs to the Persian-period reception of Babylonian religion. It is evidence of Greek ethnography, not a transparent window into earlier Nippur ritual.

### IX. Hellenistic, Parthian, and Roman-period reception

The name Mylitta/Mulitta continued in Greek and learned writing. Ninlil’s distinct temple cult is harder to trace than those of major late Babylonian urban gods. Astronomical, lexical, and antiquarian traditions preserved her.

Herodian’s second-century CE *Peri Orthographias* mentions Mylitta. Hesychius, whose lexicon survives through much later manuscript transmission, may preserve still later learned forms. These attestations demonstrate persistence of the name, not continuous organized worship into the sixth century CE.

### X. Displacement and cessation

There is no single recorded decree abolishing Ninlil’s cult. Mesopotamian temple religion declined unevenly through:

- Collapse or depopulation of older cult centers.
- Loss of temple estates and royal funding.
- Changes under Seleucid, Parthian, and Sasanian rule.
- Increasing prominence of Christianity, Judaism, Mandaeism, and later Islam.
- Disappearance of cuneiform literacy by late antiquity.

Claims of a continuous secret Ninlil cult surviving through medieval Iraq are unsupported.

### XI. Rediscovery

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century excavations at Nippur, Fara, Ur, Nineveh, Assur, and Dūr-Kurigalzu recovered tablets and temples that made reconstruction possible. Early scholarship tended to translate Ninlil confidently as “Lady Wind” and smooth Sud, Mullissu, Mylitta, and Ištar into a single figure. Later philology has made the distinctions sharper.

### XII. Present

Small modern Mesopotamian-polytheist and reconstructionist communities honor Ninlil, usually alongside Enlil, Nanna, or the Nippur pantheon. Their rituals are revivals reconstructed from translations, offering lists, and comparative practice; there is no institutional continuity from an ancient priesthood.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Assyriological rediscovery

Ninlil’s modern image emerged from publication of Nippur tablets and translations by scholars such as Hermann Hilprecht, Stephen Langdon, Samuel Noah Kramer, Thorkild Jacobsen, Miguel Civil, and later Manfred Krebernik and Nicole Brisch.

Important shifts include:

- Recognition that Sud was originally distinct.
- Replacement of a single smooth “air-goddess” biography with a history of syncretism.
- Attention to the first sexual encounter as coercive or contested.
- Recognition that Mullissu’s relationship with Ištar varies by source.
- Rejection of uncritical sacred-prostitution narratives.

### Comparative mythology and psychology

Popular and Jungian writing occasionally interprets Ninlil as:

- The “young maiden” entering sexuality.
- An earth or air anima paired with Enlil.
- A model of descent, initiation, pregnancy, or feminine transformation.
- An embodiment of unconditional devotion because she follows Enlil underground.

These are **[Modern interpretive traditions]**. Ancient texts do not call her an archetype, and the psychological reading can obscure the first episode’s coercion and the myth’s genealogical purpose.

### Occultism

Unlike Ištar, Lilith, Nergal, or Marduk, Ninlil had little independent role in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, or major early modern grimoires. Modern occult correspondences assigning her:

- An elemental quarter.
- A planet.
- A Tarot trump.
- A color.
- A crystal.
- A sigil.
- A “divine feminine frequency.”

are generally twentieth- or twenty-first-century inventions.

Ninlil is sometimes confused with **Lilith** because both names contain romanized *lil*. This is linguistically and historically unsound. Ninlil is a goddess and queen; Akkadian *lilītu* belongs to a class of harmful or restless female spirits. No ancient source identifies Ninlil with Lilith.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Modern practitioners have composed hymns, altar instructions, offering calendars, and devotional narratives for Ninlil. Historically grounded elements include bread, beer, grain, dates, dairy produce, clean water, textiles, and joint worship with Enlil. Modern elements include personalized sigils, crystal correspondences, generalized “air magic,” and treating her as a Wiccan Maiden/Mother/Crone form.

Online devotional communities provide evidence that worship exists today, but not reliable measurements of scale. A modern discussion of reconstructed practice appears in [“Any advice on worship of Ninlil?”](https://www.reddit.com/r/Sumer/comments/180yd3n); it is evidence for modern reception, not ancient ritual.

### Modern retellings

A circulated story titled “Ninlil’s Descent” expands her underworld journey into a first-person initiatory drama involving menstruation, female rites, and conversations absent from the tablets. It is a modern literary retelling, not a translation: [modern “Ninlil’s Descent”](https://www.gatewaystobabylon.com/myths/texts/retellings/ninlild.htm).

### Literature, games, and commercial imagery

Ninlil appears much less often than Inanna/Ištar, Tiamat, or Ereshkigal. Uses generally fall into three forms:

- Reference works and myth anthologies.
- Independent games or digital artwork borrowing her name.
- Occult or “goddess spirituality” products.

No single modern film, novel, or major game has established a dominant popular Ninlil image. This comparative obscurity has made her especially vulnerable to conflation with Inanna, Ninhursag, Lilith, or the “Queen of the Night.”

### Political appropriation

There is no documented major modern extremist movement centered on Ninlil. General nationalist uses of Sumerian and Assyrian heritage occasionally employ pantheon imagery, but Ninlil has not acquired a political profile comparable to Aššur in Assyrian nationalism or Marduk in Babylon-themed Iraqi state symbolism.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Name and origin

The central philological dispute concerns **LÍL₂** and the archaic spellings. Earlier scholarship confidently translated Enlil and Ninlil as “Lord Wind” and “Lady Wind.” Modern work emphasizes:

- Uncertain archaic sign readings.
- Possible place-name origins.
- Late folk etymology.
- The distinction between the historical origin of a name and an ancient commentator’s later explanation.

Robert Englund, Piotr Steinkeller, Thorkild Jacobsen, Piotr Michalowski, Xianhua Wang, and Manfred Krebernik have contributed to this broader debate. A CDLI discussion of the KID sign and its implications warns against overly confident reconstructions: [CDLI, “Notes on KIDₐ”](https://cdli.earth/articles/cdln/2011-6).

### Sud’s relationship to Ninlil

Older summaries often treated Sud as merely “another name” of Ninlil. The evidence supports a historical process:

1. Sud was a regional goddess.
2. She had healing, grain, and local civic associations.
3. She became associated with Enlil.
4. Scribal theology identified her with Ninlil.
5. *Enlil and Sud* narrated the merger as marriage and renaming.

The timing and political mechanism remain debated. One proposal connects the myth with Nippur’s absorption of Šuruppak traditions; this is plausible but not directly narrated in a royal inscription.

### Rape, seduction, or defloration

The interpretive camps differ because they ask different questions:

- Philological: what verbs and dialogue occur?
- Legal: what categories existed in Sumerian and Akkadian law?
- Literary: how does the episode compare with Inanna and Šukaletuda or Enki and Ninhursag?
- Social: why is Enlil expelled, and why does Ninlil follow him?

A responsible reconstruction neither sanitizes the refusal nor pretends that modern and ancient legal taxonomies are identical.

### Function of the underworld births

Thorkild Jacobsen emphasized agricultural and cosmic symbolism. Jerrold Cooper stressed the etiological logic: one luminous son rises while three substitutes remain below. Later scholarship also examines boundaries, ritual pollution, succession, and sexual politics. The text itself explicitly supports substitution; more elaborate seasonal readings remain reconstructions.

### Mullissu and Ištar

The equation is real but variable. Problems arise when all of the following are treated as identical:

- Ninlil of Nippur.
- Mullissu, Aššur’s spouse.
- Šerua.
- Ištar of Nineveh.
- Ištar of Arbela.
- Mylitta/Aphrodite.

Royal lists sometimes distinguish them, while theological texts merge them. Identity in one cultic or lexical context does not guarantee identity in another.

### Mylitta and sacred prostitution

Herodotus’ account profoundly shaped modern images of Babylonian religion. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scholarship often treated it as confirmed fact and connected temple women, sacred marriage, fertility ritual, and commercial sex into one institution.

Recent scholarship distinguishes those phenomena and notes the absence of corroborating Babylonian documentation. Debate remains, but the burden of proof now rests on claims of institutional “sacred prostitution.” It cannot be entered into Ninlil’s dossier as established cult practice.

### Iconographic identifications

Early art historians frequently named anonymous horned goddesses from textual expectations. Henri Frankfort’s seal studies remain foundational for visual grammar but cannot make an unlabeled seated goddess into Ninlil. Modern museum catalogues increasingly distinguish secure inscriptional identification from stylistic probability.

### State of the evidence

Evidence survives across:

- Nippur tablets in the University of Pennsylvania Museum, Istanbul collections, Baghdad, Jena, Manchester, Yale, and other collections.
- Fara and Abu Ṣalabikh lexical and hymn tablets.
- CDLI digital photographs and transliterations.
- ETCSL literary composites.
- ORACC lexical, ritual, and royal corpora.
- RINAP Neo-Assyrian inscriptions.
- Excavated architecture at Nippur, Assur, Nineveh, and Dūr-Kurigalzu.

The corpus is incomplete because of ancient destruction, early excavation practices, illicit digging, unpublished fragments, uncertain provenance, and the ordinary survival bias of clay tablets.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Ninlil was certainly and originally “goddess of air.”  
  **Too confident; based partly on late exegesis.**

- Ninlil and Lilith are the same being.  
  **False; based on superficial spelling resemblance.**

- The Burney Relief securely depicts Ninlil.  
  **Unsupported modern proposal.**

- Every Babylonian woman served as a prostitute in Ninlil’s temple.  
  **Known from Herodotus’ Mylitta report, not corroborated by Babylonian records.**

- Ninlil was worshiped continuously in secret into the modern period.  
  **No evidence.**

- *Enlil and Ninlil* and *Enlil and Sud* are successive chapters of one canonical biography.  
  **False harmonization of distinct compositions.**

- Enūma eliš, *Atrahasis*, and *Gilgamesh* all narrate Ninlil’s deeds.  
  **They do not; at most they preserve the surrounding pantheon or related theology.**

|---|---|---|
| c. 3350–3000 BCE | Proto-cuneiform **E₂.NUN/NINLIL** sign appears. | Earliest probable attestation; meaning not narratively explicit. |
| c. 2900–2600 BCE | Ninlil and Sud emerge in Early Dynastic divine-name, lexical, and civic traditions. | Secure named-goddess horizon. |
| c. 2600–2500 BCE | Sud associated with Šuruppak in the Early Dynastic city-hymn tradition; Ninlil increasingly paired with Enlil. | Regional cult evidence. |
| c. 2500–2350 BCE | Nippur’s divine couple occupies high pantheon rank; offering and god-list evidence expands. | Early Dynastic III. |
| c. 2334–2154 BCE | Akkadian-period rulers maintain Nippur cult and invoke Enlil and Ninlil. | Royal and administrative texts. |
| c. 2112–2004 BCE | Ur III rulers heavily sponsor Nippur and Tummal; festivals, offerings, processions, and temple estates are extensively documented. | Institutional high point. |
| c. 2100–2000 BCE | Traditions later assembled in the *Tummal Chronicle* connect temple renewal and Ninlil’s ceremonial installation with early kings and Ur III restoration. | Historiographic compilation. |
| c. 2000–1800 BCE | *Enlil and Ninlil*, *Enlil and Sud*, Ninlil hymns, Nanna narratives, and royal praise poetry enter the surviving Old Babylonian manuscript record. | Major literary corpus. |
| c. 1800–1600 BCE | Copies of *Enlil and Sud* circulate at Nippur and elsewhere; later Susa witness preserves a variant. | Scribal transmission. |
| c. 1600–1155 BCE | Kassite rulers restore Nippur and build Ninlil’s Egašanantagal at Dūr-Kurigalzu. | Inscriptions and archaeology. |
| Second millennium BCE | *An = Anum* and related lists systematize Ninlil’s names, Sud equation, family, and court. | Learned theology. |
| c. eighteenth century BCE onward | Aššur increasingly assumes Enlil’s cosmic status; Ninlil/Mulliltu becomes relevant as Aššur’s spouse. | Assyrian theological development. |

| 668–c. 631 BCE | Ashurbanipal calls himself “creation of Aššur and Mullissu”; a Mullissu building inscription is placed at Nineveh. | RINAP. |
| c. fifth century BCE | Herodotus identifies Mylitta as Assyrian Aphrodite and reports the disputed Babylonian sexual custom. | Greek ethnography. |
| Hellenistic–Parthian periods | Ninlil/Mullissu persists in learned, astronomical, lexical, and local religious traditions. | Fragmentary continuity. |

| Late antiquity | Mandaic and Greek lexical forms may preserve the name; organized cult is no longer demonstrable. | Linguistic survival, not certain worship. |
| Nineteenth century | Excavations and cuneiform decipherment return Ninlil to modern scholarship. | Archaeological rediscovery. |
