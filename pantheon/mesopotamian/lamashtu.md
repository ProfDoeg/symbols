# Lamashtu: Research Dossier

**Class:** A named female supernatural aggressor variously classified in Mesopotamian texts as a dangerous **demoness**, a divine or quasi-divine being marked with the divine determinative, and the “Daughter of Anu.” She is not a normal beneficent goddess with a temple cult. Her exceptional individuality, divine parentage, autonomous agency, and use of the divine determinative distinguish her from many anonymous disease demons.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The written sign-name **DIM(.ME)** occurs in Sumerian incantations of the **late third millennium BCE**, but these early contexts are too fragmentary or nonspecific to reconstruct the fully individualized figure. Secure, unmistakable descriptions occur in **Old Babylonian and Old Assyrian incantations of the early second millennium BCE**. An Old Assyrian tablet from Anatolia, YPM BC 006647/NBC 3672, conventionally dated about 2000–1700 BCE and probably to the first half of the nineteenth century BCE, calls the attacker a daughter of Anu and says that Anu expelled her from heaven. The surviving text does not preserve her name, but its narrative and language identify her with the later Lamashtu tradition. The demoness is therefore securely attested as an individualized figure by approximately the nineteenth–eighteenth centuries BCE, with a less specific Sumerian antecedent in the late third millennium. [CDLI/Yale tablet record](https://cdli.earth/cdli-tablet/688); [Yale exhibition catalogue](https://babylonian-collection.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/Lassen_Wagensonner_Women_at_the_Dawn_of_History_2020%20reduced.pdf)

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **Early attested** means third- or early second-millennium evidence; **canonical** means the standardized first-millennium Akkadian incantation series; **regional variant** denotes a geographically restricted manuscript or iconographic form; **learned systematization** denotes scholarly lists and explanatory equations; **syncretistic identification** denotes an explicit or proposed equation with another being; **late accretion** denotes post-cuneiform development; **modern reconstruction** is a scholarly inference; and **modern invention** is a recent claim or creative reuse unsupported by ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Lamashtu is the highly individualized Mesopotamian female demon who invades houses and attacks pregnancy, birth, mothers, newborn children, infants, and sometimes adults and domestic animals. Incantations accuse her of strangling, poisoning, infecting, abducting, or devouring infants; drinking their blood; gnawing their bones; corrupting milk; inducing miscarriage; disturbing sleep; and spreading fever or inflammation. Her mythology is preserved primarily in texts and objects intended to **remove** her. Consequently, the dossier reconstructs an enemy imagined by ritual specialists, not the theology of a worshipping congregation.

### Names and spellings

- **Akkadian:** *Lamaštu*, conventionally anglicized **Lamashtu**. The spelling may carry the divine determinative: ^dLa-maš-tu. Older publications often print **Labartu**, owing to outdated readings of the relevant cuneiform signs; Labartu is not securely a separate ancient being.

- **Sumerian/logographic:** ^d**DIM.ME**, also transliterated in older literature as **Dimme**. The reading is not phonetically transparent. Modern Assyriological discussion sometimes favors **Kamadme/Kamad-me** for the Sumerian sign-group or for an associated category. A straightforward lexical translation of DIM.ME is therefore unsafe.

- Less frequent logographic writings include **LU.GAM.ME** and **LUGAL.ME** in learned or variant traditions. These should not automatically be treated as independent personal names.

- **“Daughter/Child of Anu”:** Akkadian *mārat Anu/Anim*; Sumerian *dumu An(-na)*. This is her most stable relational title from Old Assyrian evidence through the first-millennium series.

- **Pašittu**, “the Eradicator,” “Wiper-out,” or “Snatcher,” is used for an infant-killing female demon and can function as a designation of Lamashtu or a closely associated manifestation. Whether every occurrence denotes Lamashtu herself must be decided contextually.

### The seven names

A recurring apotropaic strategy enumerates seven names so that no manifestation can escape ritual identification. A representative sequence, with variations among manuscripts, is:

1. **“DIMME, Daughter of Anu”**
2. **“Sister of the gods of the streets”**
3. **“Dagger/knife that smashes or splits the head”**
4. **“She who kindles inflammation”**
5. **“The goddess whose face is frightful”**
6. **“The trusted one/caretaker”**—probably deliberately ironic or reflecting her perversion of nursing
7. A difficult name involving **Irnina**, translated in older scholarship as “the one seized/taken by Irnina”

The sequence is attested on incantation tablets and amulets; wording and interpretation vary. It is a **canonical or pre-canonical ritual taxonomy**, not evidence for seven separate daughters of Anu. [Penn Museum discussion](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/demons-and-population-control/); [Schøyen MS 2779](https://www.schoyencollection.com/magical-literature-introduction/babylonian-magic/ritual-against-demon-lamashtu-ms-2779)

### Etymology

No universally accepted derivation of *Lamaštu* exists. Proposed connections to Akkadian verbs of tasting, licking, or devouring are semantically tempting but not philologically secure. The name cannot responsibly be translated as “she who erases,” “the devourer,” or “the one who dries up” without qualification. **Finding:** its etymology remains disputed.

### Classification through time

- **Early and canonical texts:** demoness, female *utukku*, she-wolf-like attacker, and simultaneously “Daughter of Anu.” One canonical passage emphasizes the paradox: although “not a goddess,” she is terrifying; other texts use the divine determinative and call her *iltu*, “goddess.”

- **Learned systematization:** lexical and god-list traditions place DIM.ME forms among named demons, diseases, and divine beings. The explanatory list *An = Anum ša amēli* associates differentiated DIM.ME forms with such figures as *labāṣu*, *aḫḫāzu*, *bibītu*, and *lilītu*. This is scribal classification, not a narrative in which all are simply identical.

- **Modern scholarship:** normally “demoness,” sometimes “demonic goddess.” Calling her a “goddess of childbirth” is misleading: she threatens childbirth and receives coercive or deceptive offerings meant to make her leave.

- **Modern occultism and games:** “goddess of monsters,” “mother goddess,” or worshipped demon. These are modern reinterpretations unless explicitly identified as fictional settings.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Late third millennium BCE

Sumerian incantations contain DIMME in the late third millennium. Frans Wiggermann stresses that these contexts are too unspecific to recover her early history. They establish the antiquity of the name or category but do not yet document every feature of the later child-killing Daughter of Anu. [Wiggermann, “Lamaštu, Daughter of Anu: A Profile”](https://libraryoflilith.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Lama-WiggermannLamashtu.pdf)

### Old Babylonian and Old Assyrian evidence, ca. 2000–1600 BCE

This is the earliest secure stratum of the individualized tradition.

The Old Assyrian tablet YPM BC 006647/NBC 3672, from the Anatolian merchant milieu, describes a female *utukku*, “a parching force,” expelled from heaven by Anu because of her murderous nature. Its absence of the personal name is a caution: the identification rests on close continuity with later Lamashtu texts rather than a preserved spelling of *Lamaštu*.

Old Babylonian incantations name DIMME/Lamashtu and already contain the stable complex of:

- descent or intrusion from outside the household;
- monstrous female form;
- danger to mother and child;
- expulsion by divine authority;
- movement through animal tracks or marginal places;
- inducements or travel provisions for her departure.

The core figure is thus substantially formed early; the first-millennium series organizes rather than invents her.

### Middle Babylonian, Middle Assyrian, and western transmission

By the later second millennium, Lamashtu incantations circulated beyond Babylonia:

- A Middle Babylonian or very late Old Babylonian manuscript, BM 120022, probably from Sippar or Babylon but of uncertain provenance, preserves material later incorporated into the series.

- A multi-column Akkadian compilation from **Ugarit**, copied in the Late Bronze Age, preserves many of the same incantations. Its Babylonian shows signs of a learned local scribe. Whether Ugarit assembled the corpus or copied an eastern exemplar is unresolved.

- RS 25.513, also from Ugarit, has been proposed as an imported tablet from Hattusa; this remains disputed.

- Amulets and texts from Emar, Assur, Anatolia, and southern Mesopotamia document a wide geographical transmission.

The Ugaritic corpus already approaches a collection but lacks the full ritual directions of the later standard series. [Farber’s edition](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781575068824/html)

### The canonical Lamashtu Series

In its first-millennium Standard Babylonian form, the series comprises **three tablets** of incantations and ritual instructions. The best-preserved witnesses come from Ashurbanipal’s seventh-century BCE library at Nineveh, although the constituent spells are centuries older.

Principal contents include:

- identification and description of Lamashtu;
- her birth or generation by Anu and expulsion from heaven;
- catalogs of her crimes and monstrous features;
- appeals to great gods and divine couples;
- oaths binding her;
- creation and treatment of a Lamashtu figurine;
- substitute gifts and food;
- comb, spindle, pin, fibula, shoes, and other “women’s” objects;
- a donkey and boat for the outward journey;
- removal, burial, abandonment, or dispatch of the figurine;
- healing procedures for the patient.

Walter Farber’s 2014 *Lamaštu: An Edition of the Canonical Series of Lamaštu Incantations and Rituals and Related Texts from the Second and First Millennia B.C.* is the fundamental edition. It supplies scores, transliterations, translations, and related noncanonical texts. [Full searchable edition](https://dokumen.pub/lamatu-an-edition-of-the-canonical-series-of-lamatu-incantations-and-rituals-and-related-texts-from-the-second-and-first-millennia-bc-9781575068824.html)

### Other ritual corpora

Lamashtu materials also occur:

- in pregnancy and birth ritual collections, notably TCL 6, 49;
- in rituals for abnormally crying infants;
- in late Babylonian Uruk, especially SBTU 3, 84;
- in the *Ḫulbazizi* or “Evil is eradicated” tradition, whose formulas can be placed on Lamashtu amulets even when the Akkadian wording addresses generic evil grammatically as masculine;
- in incantation catalogs such as KAR 44, which places the series near texts concerning women in labor and crying babies;
- in medical-magical procedures where “fever and Lamashtu” form a compound diagnosis.

### Texts in which she is not securely present

Lamashtu does **not** play a documented narrative role in *Enūma eliš*, *Atrahasis*, the Gilgamesh epics, *Inanna’s Descent*, or *Enki and Ninmah*. These works are vital comparative evidence for Mesopotamian creation, mortality, childbirth, and the underworld, but inserting her into them produces a synthetic modern mythology. Claims that she appears “in passing” in *Atrahasis* require a tablet-and-line citation and are not supported by the standard editions.

The royal inscriptions collected in RINAP likewise do not establish a Lamashtu cult or royal mythology. Her documentary home is domestic magic, medicine, amulets, scholarly lists, and incantation libraries—not royal epic.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and characteristic actions

Her best-attested targets are:

- pregnant women;
- women in labor;
- women during the postpartum period;
- fetuses and newborns;
- nursing infants;
- sick children;
- occasionally adult men, livestock, and broader household welfare.

Texts say she touches the abdomen of a pregnant woman, interferes with delivery, seizes or strangles infants, suckles them with poisonous milk, drinks blood, eats flesh, gnaws bones, produces fever, and enters through doors or windows. These are ritual explanations of reproductive and infant mortality; they should not be converted into retrospective clinical diagnoses, although miscarriage, obstructed labor, puerperal infection, neonatal illness, dehydration, and sudden infant death supplied their lived context.

Unlike many demons who execute divine punishment, Lamashtu often acts on her own initiative. This autonomy helps explain her divine parentage and why she must be expelled rather than simply ordered to cease by a patron deity.

### Textual physique

The mature descriptions combine:

- a lioness or lion-griffin head;
- donkey teeth and ears in some descriptions;
- hairy body;
- naked or pendulous breasts;
- long fingers and claws;
- blood-stained hands;
- bird-of-prey or Anzû-like talons;
- wolf-like or dog-like qualities;
- mottled or leopard-like skin;
- an odor and presence associated with carrion, dung tracks, wilderness, and thresholds.

No single image must contain every textual feature. “Snake goddess” is not an adequate classification: snakes may be held in her hands, but her dominant bodily type is leonine-human-avian.

### The amulets and plaques

Lamashtu is unusually identifiable in Mesopotamian art because text and image repeatedly converge. The corpus includes small wearable pendants, stone and obsidian plaques, clay pieces, seals, and larger bronze suspension plaques. Wiggermann’s older catalog reached approximately eighty-five items; later work speaks of more than ninety published amulets. Some unprovenanced examples and possible modern forgeries complicate the count.

A common image shows her:

- standing or kneeling on a donkey;
- nursing a dog and piglet at her breasts;
- holding snakes;
- surrounded by a comb, spindle, pin, fibula, food, or other travel gifts;
- placed within or above a boat;
- confronted or overseen by Pazuzu.

The dog and piglet serve as substitute nurslings and emphasize the perversion of human motherhood. The comb and spindle are not evidence that she governed weaving; in ritual they belong to the social persona constructed for her departure.

The Metropolitan Museum’s obsidian plaque 1984.348, early first millennium BCE and probably from Mesopotamia or Iran, shows a lion-griffin head, clawed paws, bird talons, flanking dog and pig, and domestic gifts. Its Akkadian inscription praises her as an “exalted lady” while calling gods to expel her—ritual flattery rather than worship. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/326961)

British Museum 117759, provisionally Neo-Assyrian and dated approximately 800–550 BCE, shows her standing on an ass while suckling a jackal or dog and a wild pig. [British Museum 117759](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1925-0715-1)

British Museum 127371 is an obsidian Lamashtu amulet, 800–550 BCE, carrying a spell whose wording had apparently become imperfectly understood by the engraver. This is evidence both for continued use and for degraded scribal transmission. [British Museum 127371](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1983-0101-78)

### The “Plaque of the Underworld”

The Louvre bronze AO 22205, traditionally called the **Plaque des Enfers**, is Neo-Assyrian, broadly 911–604 BCE, reportedly found or acquired in Syria. It contains several registers:

1. ten divine symbols;
2. seven animal-headed protective beings;
3. a sick person on a bed, two fish-cloaked ritual figures, and a lamp;
4. Lamashtu kneeling on a donkey in a boat on a fish-filled river, holding snakes and nursing a dog and pig;
5. Pazuzu grasping or looming over the reverse.

The boat scene visualizes removal. Calling its destination unequivocally “hell” is anachronistic: the journey may lead to the netherworld, wilderness, mountains, or simply outside civilized and domestic space, depending on text and image. [Louvre AO 22205](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010120479)

### Colors, numbers, planets, and calendar

Seven is important because of the seven names and recurring groups of seven protective or demonic figures. There is no secure ancient evidence for a unique sacred color, weekday, planet, zodiac sign, or festival date belonging to Lamashtu. Modern occult correspondences assigning her Mars, Saturn, the Moon, red, black, particular crystals, or fixed calendar days are **modern inventions** unless independently sourced to a cuneiform text.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Her only stable parent is **Anu/An**, the sky god. No ancient source securely names a mother. Because incantations sometimes invoke **Antu** beside Anu, modern summaries occasionally make Antu her mother; the inference is understandable but not explicitly established by a birth narrative.

No secure Mesopotamian source gives Lamashtu a consort, siblings in an ordinary genealogical sense, or offspring. “Sister of the gods of the streets” belongs to her seven-name litany and should not be transformed into a literal family tree.

Pazuzu is not securely her husband. He is her ritual counterforce and iconographic expeller. Statements calling them spouses are modern harmonization.

### Documented divine interactions

The following list distinguishes an actual episode or ritual relation from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Anu/An — father, creator, judge, and expeller.** Early Old Assyrian and later canonical incantations call her his daughter and relate that he cast her from heaven or that the gods rejected her after her demand for human flesh. **Tier:** early attested; canonical elaboration.

- **Antu — divine authority paired with Anu in an oath.** Lamashtu is adjured by Anu and Antu in the canonical series. Antu is not explicitly called her mother. **Tier:** canonical ritual relation.

- **Enlil and Ninlil — oath authorities.** Their names bind and dismiss her; no independent narrative meeting survives. **Tier:** canonical ritual relation.

- **Ea/Enki and Damgalnuna — exorcistic authorities.** Their names and ritual wisdom authorize expulsion. Ea’s traditional mastery of incantation stands behind the procedure, but no separate myth narrates a combat between Ea and Lamashtu. **Tier:** canonical ritual relation.

- **Marduk/Asalluhi and Ṣarpānītu — ritual authorities.** They are invoked in oaths and exorcistic formulas. The familiar Ea–Marduk incantation structure helps frame the human exorcist’s authority. **Tier:** canonical ritual relation.

- **Šamaš/Utu — judge and illuminator.** Incantations appeal to Šamaš to exercise judgment and command Lamashtu’s removal. **Tier:** early-to-canonical ritual interaction.

- **Ištar/Inanna — invoked superior goddess.** A damaged canonical oath probably invokes Ištar as the powerful woman among the gods. There is no secure myth of a personal combat between Inanna and Lamashtu. **Tier:** canonical, text partly restored.

- **Bēlet-ilī/Mami — invoked mother-goddess.** She appears as an oath authority in material concerned with childbirth. No surviving narrative makes her Lamashtu’s mother. **Tier:** canonical ritual relation.

- **Ninkarrak/Gula — healing or incantation authority.** An Old Babylonian form attributes the spell to Ninkarrak, mistress of incantations; later healing traditions place Lamashtu among illnesses overcome through divine medicine. **Tier:** early regional/canonical medical relation.

- **Annunītum — hostile healing power.** In medical-incantation material Annunītum is asked to trample murderous bile, Lamashtu, bites, and related afflictions. **Tier:** regional ritual interaction.

- **Irnina — relation embedded in the seventh name.** The exact syntax and mythology are uncertain; older translations make Lamashtu “seized by Irnina.” It is not safe to reconstruct a lost combat. **Tier:** canonical name tradition, meaning disputed.

- **Nuska/Nusku — nocturnal guardian.** His lamp protects the bedroom against her. On the Louvre plaque a lamp symbol in the sickroom may represent Nusku. This is a protective opposition, not a narrated duel. **Tier:** first-millennium iconographic and ritual interaction. [ORACC AMGG: Nuska](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/nuska/)

- **Kūbu — invoked dead or stillborn beings.** An incantation from Nimrud with an Ugaritic parallel conjures Lamashtu by the Kūbu of high priestesses and legendary figures. The category’s precise meaning varies, but it provides coercive ritual authority. **Tier:** later second-/first-millennium ritual relation.

- **Pazuzu — adversarial protector and expeller.** First-millennium amulets place his head or body above or behind Lamashtu, directing his dangerous force against her and driving her away from mother and child. No surviving ancient narrative establishes a marriage or a long personal feud. **Tier:** first-millennium canonical/iconographic relation. [Metropolitan Museum Pazuzu pendant](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327485); [British Museum Pazuzu head](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1962-0514-1)

- **Lilītu — learned equation of a manifestation, not a universal identity.** The explanatory list *An = Anum ša amēli* appears to equate “DIMME/Lamashtu of the night” with *lilītu*. This is a specific scribal equation among differentiated demonic types; it does not prove that every Lamashtu was Lilītu or that the later Jewish Lilith was simply Lamashtu under another name. **Tier:** learned systematization/syncretistic identification.

No securely attested interaction with Gilgamesh, Enkidu, Ereshkigal, Tiamat, or Inanna in the descent myth is known. Their frequent conjunction with Lamashtu on modern mythology sites is associative, not documentary.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Lamashtu has no epic cycle comparable to those of Gilgamesh or Inanna. Her “myths” are embedded in incantations whose narratives make ritual expulsion possible.

### The Daughter of Anu and expulsion from heaven

**Early form:** A female attacker is born or brought forth in heaven. She is destructive by nature, demands or consumes human flesh and blood, and is expelled by Anu. The Old Assyrian tradition already contains the expulsion; canonical texts expand her crimes.

**Canonical elaboration:** She declares her intention to attack human pregnancies and children. The gods reject her. Her divine birth explains both her power and the difficulty of destroying her: ritual can bind, divert, shame, bribe, or deport her, but does not narrate her permanent death.

**Function:** The story locates otherwise inexplicable infant mortality in a named, external agent while giving the exorcist a precedent—Anu himself already rejected her.

There is no evidence that this was once a benign childbirth goddess “demonized” by patriarchy or conquest. That hypothesis is a **modern reconstruction without supporting cultic documentation**.

### Intrusion into the household

Incantations describe her approaching from wilderness, street, river, mountain, animal tracks, or roof and entering the domestic space. She watches pregnancy and birth, approaches the bed, masquerades as a nurse or midwife, and substitutes lethal nursing for care.

The exact plot varies because the tradition is oral-formulaic and ritual rather than epic. In some texts she is wolf-like; in others a female *utukku*, a foreign woman, a wet nurse, or a goddess of frightful appearance.

### The counterfeit nurse

Lamashtu asks to hold or nurse the infant. Once admitted, she poisons milk, strangles, drinks blood, or devours flesh. Ritual speech exposes her disguise: she is “not a nurse,” “not a midwife,” and has no legitimate place in the household.

The dog and piglet on amulets offer substitute recipients for her milk. This motif is securely ancient; interpretations of it as a critique of socially marginal wet nurses are possible but remain scholarly readings rather than statements made explicitly by the texts.

### The donkey and boat journey

This is the principal expulsion narrative.

A figurine or image of Lamashtu is provisioned with bread, drink, shoes, comb, spindle, pin, fibula, clothing, ointment, and other goods. A donkey is assigned to her. A boat, paddle, rudder, bitumen, and river imagery transfer her away from the patient. The ritual may include tying, placing, carrying, burying, or abandoning the figure beyond the inhabited zone.

The logic is simultaneously coercive and seductive: she is commanded to leave but also treated as a traveler, bride, or displaced woman receiving a deliberately defective “dowry.” Calling this a genuine Lamashtu cult mistakes **mock hospitality directed toward expulsion** for devotional worship.

The destination varies. Some texts send her to mountain or steppe, some toward river or netherworld. The image of “the boat to the underworld” is well grounded in first-millennium iconography, especially AO 22205, but “underworld” should not erase alternative destinations.

### Substitution rites

Late Babylonian Uruk rituals can replace a threatened infant with another object or animal. One explicit procedure uses a strangled lamb dressed as a child. The substitute absorbs the attack and is removed with the demoness. This is animal killing within an apotropaic ritual; it is not evidence of human sacrifice to Lamashtu.

### Pazuzu’s intervention

First-millennium amulets place Pazuzu’s head above the scene or his full body on the reverse. His terrifying gaze and wind-demonic power are turned outward against Lamashtu. The ancient material does not preserve a continuous prose myth in which they battle. The “battle” is performed by the object’s juxtaposition.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrated temple cult

No temple dedicated to Lamashtu, priesthood of Lamashtu, festival calendar, regular sacrificial economy, royal patronage, or secure theophoric naming tradition has been identified. She receives neither the ordinary food service of a city god nor evidence of congregational worship.

This absence is important. Calling the ritual corpus her “cult” is acceptable only in the loose sense of practices concerning her. Institutionally, it is an **anti-Lamashtu cultus** conducted for patients.

### Practitioners

The procedures belonged chiefly to the expertise of the **āšipu**, the exorcist or ritual specialist, working alongside or overlapping with medical practitioners. Fish-cloaked figures on the Louvre plaque probably represent apkallu-like ritual specialists. Family members may have worn or suspended amulets, but the technical texts were copied in scholarly and temple libraries.

### Ritual sequence

A typical reconstruction from the canonical series is:

1. diagnose the affliction as Lamashtu or include her among possible agents;
2. recite identification formulas and her names;
3. create a clay, wax, wood, or other figurine;
4. provide substitute nurslings or food;
5. furnish the figure with travel objects;
6. invoke and swear her by major gods;
7. attach or suspend an inscribed amulet near mother, infant, bed, clothing, or doorway;
8. use Pazuzu’s image as a counter-demon;
9. transfer impurity or illness to the image;
10. send, bury, abandon, or float the figure away;
11. wash, anoint, fumigate, or otherwise treat the patient.

These actions can be repeated over several days. The object is an active participant: inscription identifies and binds, image contains or confronts, and removal relocates the danger.

### Offerings

Bread, beer, water, flour, fats, aromatic plants, meat, combs, pins, spindles, shoes, garments, and jewelry-like objects appear in the procedures. They are better understood as bait, ransom, substitute care, mock bridal provisions, or travel supplies than as ordinary devotional offerings.

### Human sacrifice and temple prostitution

There is **no evidence of human sacrifice to Lamashtu**. Texts accuse her of killing and eating humans; this is mythic predation, not human ritual performance.

There is likewise no evidence of “temple prostitution” in her service. Popular claims combining Mesopotamian demonesses, sacred sexuality, infant sacrifice, and Lilith are unsupported composites of separate and frequently disputed subjects.

### Geography

Texts and amulets occur across:

- southern Babylonia;
- Assur and the Neo-Assyrian heartland;
- Anatolian Old Assyrian sites;
- Ugarit;
- Emar and northern Syria;
- Mesopotamia or western Iran;
- late Babylonian Uruk.

The especially large Neo-Assyrian amulet record indicates intense first-millennium use but not necessarily the moment of greatest belief; survival and excavation biases matter.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Late third millennium BCE: category before biography

DIMME appears in Sumerian magical material. The context supports an old demonic designation but not yet the entire later biography.

### Early second millennium BCE: the individualized Daughter of Anu

Old Assyrian and Old Babylonian texts establish the recognizable demoness. Her parentage, expulsion, child-killing, and apotropaic treatment are already present. The many small textual variations suggest a vigorous oral and local incantation tradition.

### Later second millennium BCE: international scholarly transmission

The texts travel through Mesopotamian scholarly networks to Ugarit, Emar, and Anatolia. Collections begin to assemble multiple Lamashtu spells. Amulets acquire more regular imagery. The Middle Babylonian Ugarit corpus demonstrates that the later canon was not simply invented in Assyria.

### Early first millennium BCE: canonization and iconographic standardization

The three-tablet series becomes standardized in scholarly libraries. Neo-Assyrian amulets multiply. Pazuzu becomes her most visible counterforce, while elaborate plaques combine divine symbols, protective spirits, ritual professionals, patient, Lamashtu, donkey, river, boat, and Pazuzu.

### Seventh century BCE: Nineveh library witnesses

Copies in Ashurbanipal’s library preserve the canonical series. Their library context places domestic danger within the state-supported system of learned exorcistic knowledge.

### Late Babylonian and Hellenistic periods

Uruk manuscripts preserve alternative rituals rather than merely copying the Nineveh recension. A tablet from a Hellenistic scholarly library shows that Lamashtu material remained part of learned cuneiform medicine and magic centuries after the Neo-Assyrian peak.

### Late antiquity

Aramaic incantation bowls and amulets preserve female child-killing demons, Liliths, and sometimes names or formulas comparable to the older tradition. This demonstrates continuity of **protective technology and motifs**, not an uninterrupted temple cult or a provable one-to-one survival of Lamashtu.

### Medieval and early-modern periods

No continuous community devoted to Lamashtu is documented. Jewish Lilith, Greek Lamia/Gello, and other child-killing females develop in their own textual histories. Their resemblance encouraged comparison, but calling each “Lamashtu under another name” erases distinct languages, sources, and chronologies.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries: rediscovery

Cuneiform decipherment and museum acquisition restored Lamashtu to modern knowledge. Important landmarks include:

- publication of relevant British Museum cuneiform copies by T. G. Pinches in 1891;
- D. W. Myhrman’s *Die Labartu-Texte* in 1902;
- Thureau-Dangin’s 1921 identification of the depicted demoness through correspondence between texts and images;
- later catalogs by Klengel, Farber, Green, and Wiggermann;
- Wiggermann’s 2000 synthetic profile;
- Farber’s 2014 critical edition.

### Present

Modern practitioners sometimes venerate or “work with” Lamashtu in demonolatry, chaos magic, or eclectic Mesopotamian paganism. These groups are decentralized, mostly online, and not demonstrably continuous with ancient practice. Their transformation of expulsion gifts into offerings of welcome is a consciously or unconsciously reversed ritual logic: a **modern religious invention**, even when informed by accurate translations.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### The Lilith comparison

Lamashtu resembles later Lilith traditions in being female, nocturnal or marginal, sexually and maternally inverted, dangerous to pregnancy and babies, and repelled with written amulets. Mesopotamian *lilītu* demons provide the closer linguistic parallel to Hebrew *lilit*; Lamashtu provides a strong functional and iconographic parallel.

The defensible formulation is:

- direct identity: **not established**;
- shared ancient Near Eastern demonic vocabulary and protective practice: **well supported**;
- learned equation of a nighttime DIMME form with *lilītu*: **attested but limited**;
- “Lilith evolved directly from Lamashtu”: **possible influence, not demonstrable as a single genealogical line**;
- “Lamashtu is the Sumerian Lilith” or “Lilith’s original name”: **modern simplification**.

The alleged Lilith in “Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld” and readings of the Arslan Tash amulets remain disputed and cannot independently prove descent.

### Greek Lamia, Gello, and Gorgons

Scholars have compared Lamashtu with Greek Lamia and Gello, both child-killing females, and with Gorgons as apotropaic monstrous faces. Contact and transmission are historically plausible, especially during the Orientalizing period, but resemblance alone does not demonstrate that Lamia’s name derives from Lamashtu. These remain comparativist hypotheses.

### Occultism

Lamashtu does not have a substantial documented role in Renaissance magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or classical ceremonial grimoires. Claims that she possessed an ancient sigil, planetary seal, enn, standardized invocation, or initiatory grade are modern.

Contemporary demonolatry sometimes presents her as:

- a dark mother;
- patron of abortion, infertility, rage, predators, or social outsiders;
- an aspect of Lilith;
- a sovereign goddess opposed to patriarchal maternity;
- a healer through confrontation with trauma.

These are legitimate descriptions of present religious creativity but are **not translations of ancient Lamashtu theology**.

### Literature, screen, and games

- **Dungeons & Dragons** reused Lamashtu’s name within modern demon-lord lore and associated her with Pazuzu. This is fictional adaptation.

- **Pathfinder/Golarion** makes Lamashtu a major demon goddess, “Mother of Monsters,” with priests, temples, a church, a portfolio, and extensive mythology. Paizo explicitly classed Lamashtu and Pazuzu among names borrowed from real-world mythology, while its theology is proprietary fantasy. [Paizo](https://paizo.com/blog/demon-lords-of-golarion)

- NBC’s **Constantine**, “The Saint of Last Resorts” (2014), used Lamashtu as a demonic antagonist.

- **Venus** (2022) and **The Exorcist: Believer** (2023) contributed to a broader cinematic reuse of Mesopotamian demon imagery, although publicity and fan summaries often conflate Lamashtu, Pazuzu, and generic possession demons.

The most influential modern visual distortion is the transfer of Pazuzu’s *The Exorcist* notoriety back onto Lamashtu. In the ancient record Pazuzu is dangerous but apotropaically deployed **against** her; neither ancient figure corresponds exactly to the Christian Devil.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No substantial, organized nationalist or extremist Lamashtu appropriation is presently documented in the sources reviewed. Isolated online satanic or anti-natalist uses should not be inflated into a movement. No reliable demographic scale exists for modern worship.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major scholarly landmarks

- **Pinches, 1891:** published substantial cuneiform portions of the series.
- **Myhrman, 1902:** first major edition, using the then-current reading Labartu.
- **Thureau-Dangin, 1921:** connected textual descriptions with the distinctive amulet figure.
- **Klengel, Farber, Green, and others:** expanded the amulet and tablet corpus.
- **Wiggermann, 2000:** reconstructed the figure’s historical development and classified the amulets.
- **Farber, 2014:** comprehensive critical edition of canonical and related texts.
- **Miriam Said, 2020:** reexamined the amulet as a “portrait of the caregiver as a demoness,” emphasizing the manipulation of nursing and feminine domestic objects. [Said, Metropolitan Museum Journal](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Said_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_55_2020.pdf)

### Origin dispute: Sumerian or Elamite?

An Elamite origin was proposed from supposedly Lamashtu-like lion imagery extending back toward the Proto-Elamite period. Wiggermann judged the iconographic connection extremely tenuous and rejected the theory because the proposed origin depended on that identification. The surviving evidence therefore supports an early Mesopotamian DIMME tradition; an Elamite origin is **unproven and currently weak**.

### Goddess or demon?

Her divine determinative, parentage, individual name, autonomous action, and occasional designation as *iltu* favor “demonic goddess” or “divine demoness.” Her absence from normal cult, hostile role, and expulsion rituals favor “demoness.” Ancient classifications themselves are inconsistent; choosing one English category as exclusively correct obscures the evidence.

### Individual or category?

DIMME could function both as an individual name and a productive classificatory element. Learned lists differentiate several DIMME-type entities. The canonical tradition nevertheless treats “Daughter of Anu” as a coherent personality. Scholarship need not choose between an originally broad demonic category and its later individualization; the historical development may include both.

### Dating and formation of the canon

The first-millennium recension is standardized, but most components are older. The Ugarit compilation demonstrates a substantial late-second-millennium collection. Whether the Ugaritic scribe compiled it locally or copied an earlier eastern compilation is unresolved.

### Amulet chronology and authenticity

Provenance, paleography, iconography, material, and inscription type divide amulets into chronological groups. Many entered collections through the art market. Pseudo-cuneiform may be ancient, representing an owner or engraver unable to reproduce the learned spell; it is not automatically evidence of modern forgery. Conversely, some pieces in older catalogs may be modern fakes, and unprovenanced plaques cannot bear the same historical weight as excavated objects.

### Medical interpretation

Modern historians reasonably connect Lamashtu with miscarriage, maternal mortality, puerperal fever, neonatal infection, malnutrition, and sudden infant death. These are **modern clinical correlations**, not ancient diagnoses. The texts conceptualize the attack through agency, impurity, boundary violation, and divine or demonic power.

### Discredited or unsupported popular theories

- Lamashtu was originally a universally benevolent mother goddess: unsupported.
- Her cult practiced infant or human sacrifice: unsupported.
- She employed sacred prostitutes: unsupported.
- Pazuzu was her husband: unsupported.
- She is identical to Lilith in all periods: false as a documentary claim.
- She appears in *Enūma eliš*, *Atrahasis*, or the Gilgamesh epic as a named character: unsupported by the standard texts.
- Every lion-headed female image is Lamashtu: methodologically invalid.
- Modern seals, enns, planetary correspondences, and devotional festivals are ancient: generally modern inventions.
- The Louvre plaque depicts a Christian-style Hell: anachronistic.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Late third millennium BCE | DIMME occurs in Sumerian incantations; contexts do not yet permit a complete reconstruction of the individualized figure. |
| Ca. nineteenth–eighteenth centuries BCE | Old Assyrian and Old Babylonian incantations securely attest the child-threatening Daughter of Anu; an Anatolian Old Assyrian tablet says Anu expelled the unnamed but recognizable demoness. |
| Ca. eighteenth–sixteenth centuries BCE | Old Babylonian formulas establish her monstrous body, intrusion into households, attacks on children, and ritual expulsion. |
| Middle Bronze Age | Earliest likely Lamashtu amulets develop in southern Mesopotamia, although individual datings remain debated. |
| Ca. fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE | Middle Babylonian Lamashtu incantations circulate at Ugarit; related evidence appears at Emar and elsewhere in the international cuneiform scholarly network. |
| Late second millennium BCE | Collections approach the later series; amulet imagery and expulsion narratives become increasingly standardized. |
| Early first millennium BCE | The three-tablet Standard Babylonian series takes canonical form. Pazuzu becomes a prominent apotropaic counterforce. |
| 911–604 BCE | Conventional Neo-Assyrian date range of the Louvre’s bronze “Plaque of the Underworld,” AO 22205. |
| Eighth–seventh centuries BCE | Major expansion of inscribed stone, bronze, and obsidian Lamashtu amulets across Assyria and neighboring regions. |
| Seventh century BCE | Canonical copies enter Ashurbanipal’s library at Nineveh. |
| 800–550 BCE | Approximate museum dates for several British Museum Lamashtu amulets. |
| Late Babylonian period | Uruk preserves alternative anti-Lamashtu rituals, including substitution procedures. |
| Hellenistic period | Lamashtu incantations remain in cuneiform scholarly libraries, including the library associated with Tanittu-Bēl. |
| Late antiquity | Aramaic bowls preserve related defenses against Liliths and child-killing female demons; continuity is thematic and ritual, not proof of simple identity. |
| Medieval period | Jewish Lilith, Greek Gello/Lamia, and other child-threatening females develop in distinct literary and folkloric traditions. No continuous Lamashtu cult is known. |
| 1879 | The Louvre plaque later numbered AO 22205 is acquired in Syria by Péretié, according to museum provenance. |
| 1891 | Pinches publishes large portions of the cuneiform series. |
| 1902 | Myhrman publishes *Die Labartu-Texte*. |
| 1921 | Thureau-Dangin’s work connects textual and iconographic evidence and identifies Lamashtu on the plaque corpus. |
| Mid–late twentieth century | Klengel, Farber, Green, Wiggermann, and others enlarge and reassess the text and amulet corpora. |
| 1973 onward | Popular awareness of Pazuzu through *The Exorcist* indirectly reshapes modern reception of Lamashtu. |
| 2000 | Wiggermann publishes “Lamaštu, Daughter of Anu: A Profile.” |
| 2007 | Farber publishes a focused study of Lamashtu and dogs. |
| 2008 onward | Pathfinder transforms the name into a fictional goddess of monsters with a developed church and mythology. |
| 2014 | Farber publishes the comprehensive modern edition of the canonical series and related texts. |
| 2014 | Lamashtu appears in NBC’s *Constantine*. |
| 2020 | Miriam Said publishes a major reinterpretation of the amulet’s caregiver and domestic imagery. |
| 2020s | Online demonolatry and eclectic pagan communities begin openly venerating or “working with” Lamashtu; these are modern revivals with no demonstrated institutional continuity from antiquity. |
| 2026 | Lamashtu remains a subject of Assyriology, museum interpretation, fantasy media, horror, and small decentralized modern religious experiments. |
