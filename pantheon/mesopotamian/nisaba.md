# Nisaba: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*diĝir*, Akkadian *iltu*) in Sumerian and later Mesopotamian tradition: originally and persistently associated with grain, harvest accounting, and storage; from the Early Dynastic period especially the divine patron of writing, scribes, calculation, surveying, record-keeping, and learned wisdom.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the divine name written **𒀭𒉀, ^dNAGA**, in the Archaic Texts from Ur, conventionally dated to the late fourth or very early third millennium BCE. The archaic spelling establishes a recognized deity but does not by itself document all the later mythology or prove that every later scribal function was already present. The developed literary figure is securely visible in Early Dynastic and especially late-third- and early-second-millennium sources. The principal modern synthesis is Johanna Tudeau’s ORACC/AMGG entry, based chiefly on Piotr Michalowski’s *Reallexikon der Assyriologie* article and the primary corpora ([ORACC/AMGG](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/nidaba/)).

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Nisaba is the clearest Mesopotamian example of a divine portfolio joining agricultural production to information management. Grain had to be measured, allotted, stored, taxed, and recorded; writing itself emerged largely in administrative settings concerned with commodities. Ancient texts consequently make her at once grain’s abundance and the intelligence that counts it. This historical connection is strong, but the frequent modern formula that “writing evolved from grain accounting and therefore Nisaba became writing goddess” remains a scholarly reconstruction rather than an ancient narrative explicitly describing her promotion.

### Name and spellings

- **𒀭𒉀, ^dNAGA**: the archaic written form at Ur. NAGA may represent a plant; precisely which plant and whether the sign’s graphic origin determined the goddess’s nature remain disputed.
- **𒀭𒊺𒉀, ^dŠE.NAGA**: later expanded spelling incorporating ŠE, “barley/grain.”
- **Nisaba / Nissaba**: the now generally preferred conventional normalization, supported by syllabic spellings such as *ni-is-sa-ba* and *ni-sa-ba*.
- **Nidaba**: Miguel Civil’s influential alternative reading, based on forms such as *ni-da-ba*. ORACC retained “Nidaba” in its page title, while listing Nisaba and Nissaba as normalized alternatives. Later scholarship generally considers the evidence for /d/ less persuasive than that for /s(s)/. The ancient written logogram itself often does not settle pronunciation.
- **Nisaba(k)**: a scholarly notation reflecting a possible final genitive element, not a securely recoverable everyday pronunciation.
- **Nanibgal**, written early **^dAN.NAGA**, later **^dAN.ŠE.NAGA**: sometimes a praising name or epithet of Nisaba, but in most god lists a separate goddess. It must therefore not be treated as an unconditional synonym.
- **Nunbaršegunu / Nun-bar-še-gunu**, approximately “Princess/Lady whose body is flecked barley”: an agricultural name used for Nisaba, especially as Sud/Ninlil’s mother.
- **Nisaba-ursag**, “Nisaba the hero/warrior”: a learned form linked with Nanibgal and Geme-Dukuga in theological lists.
- **Ezina-Kusu / Ašnan**: grain-goddess names occasionally identified or merged with Nisaba in literary theology. Offering and god lists also enumerate these figures separately, proving that equivalence in one learned text did not erase cultic distinction elsewhere.
- **“Lady of Ereš”**: city title, found for example in the fragmentary Old Babylonian praise poem *Abī-Ešuḫ B* ([ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.8.5.a)).
- **“Chief scribe of An”** and **“record-keeper of Enlil”**: *Nisaba A*, Old Babylonian recension, lines 7–13.
- **“Wise sage of the gods”**: same passage.
- **“Supreme nursemaid”** and **“keeper of the seal in the treasury”**: *Nisaba A*, lines 36–55.
- **“She of the tablets”**: *Nisaba B*, describing her destroyed house.
- **“Creator of god, king, and humankind”** and apparently the “net of the fierce Anunnakū”: a markedly late Neo-Assyrian praise formula in CTN 4, 168, from Nabû’s temple at Kalḫu. This is a genuine ancient enlargement of her titles, not evidence that early Sumerian creation myths gave Nisaba sole authorship of humanity.

### Etymology

No etymology commands consensus.

1. **Unknown/nontransparent name — current cautious position.** The archaic spelling is older than the syllabic explanations and may preserve a name whose morphology was no longer understood.
2. **\*nin-še-ba-ak, “lady of grain rations.”** This fits her grain-allocation and accounting functions and was defended by G. J. Selz as “Herrin der Getreidezuteilungen.” It is semantically attractive but reconstructive.
3. **\*nin-saba-ak, “Lady of Saba.”** Wilfred Lambert proposed a toponymic derivation, but no securely attested Sumerian place Saba supplies the required anchor.
4. **Plant-sign interpretation.** NAGA may have depicted vegetation and was later augmented by ŠE, “barley.” This explains scribal perception of the written name but is not a demonstrated spoken etymology.
5. **Mari’s Nišpa.** A single Amorite personal name, Ḫabdu-Nišpa, has been connected with Nisaba. Other scholars identify Nišpa/Nišba as a deified mountain in the Zagros region; identification with Nisaba is therefore insecure.

No secure Greek or Roman *interpretatio* of Nisaba is attested. Modern comparisons with Demeter/Ceres, Athena, Seshat, or Thoth are functional comparisons, not ancient equations.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaic and Early Dynastic evidence

The Archaic Ur spelling ^dNAGA establishes Nisaba among the earliest written divine names. Because archaic administrative texts are terse, they do not yield a connected mythology. Early Dynastic evidence from southern Mesopotamia establishes her as a city goddess and as a deity of grain, accounting, and learned textual production. Her city, **Ereš**, was written with the same principal sign as her name but a place determinative. Ereš’s archaeological location remains unproved; Tell Abu Ṣalābīkh and Jarin have been proposed.

Early scribal colophons and literary conventions associate composition with her authority. The recurring **^dNisaba za₃-mí**, literally “Nisaba—praise!” or “Praise be to Nisaba,” is not simply an opening blessing. It most characteristically closes Sumerian literary compositions, acknowledging the patroness of the written and learned product. Individual witnesses vary: the formula can close works addressed to other gods and kings, showing that it functions as a scribal doxology rather than declaring Nisaba the narrative subject.

### Principal primary corpus

#### *Nisaba A* / “The Blessing of Nisaba by Enki” — ETCSL 4.16.1

The fullest self-contained hymn survives principally in Old Babylonian school manuscripts, with an earlier stone-tablet version from Lagash possibly dating to the Ur III period. It calls Nisaba a star-colored lady with a lapis-lazuli tablet, daughter of Uraš, “chief scribe of An,” “record-keeper of Enlil,” agricultural provider, seal keeper, steward, and heap-maker of grain. Enki opens her house of learning, places the celestial tablet upon her knees, addresses and blesses her.

The Old Babylonian composite contains manuscript-level variants: “holding” versus “perfectly endowed with” a lapis tablet; “born in” versus “born by” the mountains; and different readings of damaged agricultural and ritual phrases. ETCSL lists twelve cuneiform witnesses, including tablets from Nippur, Ur, and Yale ([text and translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.16.1); [manuscripts and bibliography](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/b4161.htm)).

**Tier:** securely attested late-third-millennium core; Old Babylonian canonical-school elaboration.

#### *Nisaba B*, a *šir-namšub* — ETCSL 4.16.2

A short, damaged lament known from manuscripts from Kiš and an unprovenanced/VAT witness. Nisaba’s house “of the tablets,” Nunbaršegunu’s house, and other sanctuaries have been destroyed. Nisaba lies alone in moonlight and weeps; the conclusion calls her “Mother Ezina-Kusu.” The devastation is attributed generally to the angry words or hearts of An and Enlil, but the damaged text supplies no recoverable historical destruction event.

**Tier:** securely attested Old Babylonian lament; historical referent unknown ([ETCSL text](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.16.2); [bibliography](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section4/b4162.htm)).

#### Gudea Cylinders A and B

Cylinder A, composed in Sumerian under Gudea of Lagash, approximately 2120–2110 BCE, survives on a monumental terracotta cylinder found at Tello/Girsu in 1877 and now in the Louvre, MNB 1512 ([Louvre catalogue](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark%3A/53355/cl010124631)). In Gudea’s dream, a young woman with vegetal/sheaf-like features holds a refined-silver stylus and consults a tablet bearing favorable stars. Nanše identifies her as “my sister Nisaba.” She assists the planning of Ningirsu’s E-ninnu temple.

This passage securely establishes the stylus, tablet, celestial calculation, temple planning, and divine-family language by the twenty-second century BCE. The object is textual evidence about a dream figure, not a surviving image of Nisaba herself ([ETCSRI, Cylinder A](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/etcsri/Q000377/html); [ETCSL line 135](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslgloss.php?charencgcirc=&lookup=c217.135&sn=ON)).

**Tier:** securely attested Neo-Sumerian royal theology.

#### *Enlil and Sud* — ETCSL 1.2.2

Old Babylonian school manuscripts tell how Enlil comes to Ereš, propositions Sud, and initially offends her. Through Nuska he negotiates with Sud’s mother, Nisaba/Nanibgal/Nunbaršegunu, bringing marriage gifts. Nisaba consents; Enlil marries Sud and renames/exalts her as Ninlil. The text directly makes Nisaba Enlil’s mother-in-law, while other traditions call her Enlil’s daughter—an ancient contradiction, not a genealogy to harmonize ([ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.1.2.2)).

**Tier:** Old Babylonian canonical literary theology centered on Ereš and Nippur.

#### *Enlil and Ninlil* — ETCSL 1.2.1

Nunbaršegunu, here Ninlil’s mother, advises her daughter before the river encounter with Enlil. If Nunbaršegunu is Nisaba, as the name normally indicates in this context, the composition supplies another mother-daughter relation. The poem’s sexual narrative differs fundamentally from *Enlil and Sud*: Enlil impregnates Ninlil and is expelled from the city, after which further underworld conceptions occur. The two poems preserve incompatible narrative constructions of the divine marriage.

**Tier:** Old Babylonian canonical variant; identification of every occurrence of Nunbaršegunu with Nisaba is traditional but should be stated rather than assumed.

#### *Keš Temple Hymn* — ETCSL 4.80.2

The composition has an Early Dynastic antecedent and numerous Old Babylonian school copies. Enlil praises Keš; Nisaba is its “decision-maker” or textual organizer and holds its description written on tablets. The poem represents her as the agent who fixes divine architectural praise in writing ([ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.80.2)).

**Tier:** securely attested Early Dynastic literary tradition with Old Babylonian transmission.

#### *Song of the Hoe* — ETCSL 5.5.4

After Enki praises Enlil’s hoe and humanity is organized to serve the gods, “the maiden Nisaba was made responsible for keeping records of the decisions.” This is one of the clearest mythical statements joining labor organization and written administration ([ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.5.5.4)).

**Tier:** Old Babylonian school text, likely drawing on older institutional theology.

#### *Nanše A*

At New Year personnel are examined. Nisaba, Nanše’s chief scribe, places precious tablets on her knees and takes a golden stylus; Haya registers a faithful servant and deletes an unfaithful one. This is a divine image of temple personnel administration and judicial accounting ([ETCSL glossed passage](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslgloss.php?charenc=gcirc&lookup=c4141.98)).

**Tier:** Old Babylonian canonical literary administration.

#### *The Herds of Nanna* / Nanna F

After enormous, symbolically inflated totals for Nanna’s cattle, Nisaba takes the grand total, counts the herds, and writes the result on clay. She is not Nanna’s antagonist or consort; she is the divine accountant of his property ([ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?charenc=j&text=t.4.13.06)).

**Tier:** Old Babylonian literary elaboration.

#### Edubba literature

*The Advice of a Supervisor to a Younger Scribe* says that Nisaba placed the honor of teaching in the learned man’s hand, can bless him, and closes by praising her as the unrivaled lady who orders districts and boundaries ([ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.5.1.3)). Other school dialogues, royal praises, hymns, and disputations close with **Nisaba za₃-mí**.

**Tier:** direct evidence for Old Babylonian scribal ideology; not proof that all instruction occurred in institutional temple “schools.” Archaeological evidence increasingly favors small household-based instruction in many cities.

#### *Curse of Agade*

Nisaba appears among the great gods whose ordered abundance is overturned. Her withdrawal or disruption signifies failed grain production and administration.

**Tier:** Old Babylonian literary copy tradition describing the fall of Akkad.

#### *Enmerkar and Ensuhgirana*

Sagburu, a wise woman from Ereš, mocks the notion that a foreign sorcerer can surpass her in Nisaba’s own city. Nisaba does not personally enter the magical contest, but Ereš’s identity as her city authorizes Sagburu’s wisdom.

**Tier:** Old Babylonian literary association; indirect divine participation.

#### Akkadian “Nisaba and Wheat/Grain”

A first-millennium Akkadian disputation is attested at Assur and Ḫuzirina/Sultantepe. Nisaba and personified Wheat debate or are juxtaposed. The poem gives Nisaba underworld language, including “Mistress of the Underworld,” producing a learned rapprochement with Ereškigal. Because ordinary god and offering lists continue to distinguish Nisaba from grain goddesses and Ereškigal, this is best classified as late scholarly syncretism, not an original identity.

**Tier:** Neo-Assyrian learned systematization.

#### CTN 4, 168

A Neo-Assyrian Akkadian prayer/ritual tablet—ND 5493, Iraq Museum IM 67630, CDLI P363582—was excavated in the Nabû temple at Kalḫu. Its extraordinary titles for Nisaba show that she could still receive serious theological attention inside an institution dominated by her successor Nabû. The tablet calls her a creator of divine, royal, and human order and associates her with the Anunnakū ([object and edition audit](https://thecode.wiki/2026/09/05/p363582-nisaba-humankind-fierce-anunnaku/)).

**Tier:** securely attested Neo-Assyrian survival and elaboration.

### Named corpora in which she is absent or peripheral

- **Enūma eliš:** Nisaba has no substantive narrative role in the standard Babylonian creation epic. Marduk and Nabû’s theology belongs to the environment in which her scribal primacy diminished, but she should not be inserted into the poem.
- **Atrahasis:** no named narrative role for Nisaba in the standard Old Babylonian epic. A damaged famine phrase conventionally restored as the “breast of Nisaba” uses her name metonymically for grain abundance.
- **Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh:** she is not a character. A famine image involving Nisaba’s “breast” similarly personifies depleted grain production; this is an agricultural metaphor, not an encounter with Gilgamesh. George’s critical edition remains the controlling textual resource; a digitized older edition preserves the phrase ([Chicago digitization](https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/misc_gilgamesh.pdf)).
- **Inanna’s Descent:** Nisaba does not appear as an acting character.
- **Enki and Ninmah:** Nisaba does not appear as an acting creator in the standard composition. CTN 4, 168’s much later creator title must not be back-projected into it.
- **Assyrian royal inscriptions/RINAP:** occurrences are sparse and mostly epithets or divine invocations rather than myths. The RINAP indices nevertheless confirm her persistence in first-millennium royal corpora ([RINAP 5/2 index](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/rinap/downloads/0RINAP5_2_final.pdf)).
- **An = Anum:** this first-millennium canonical god list organizes her in Enlil’s circle, associates Haya and attendant figures with her, and preserves learned equations. It is evidence for theological classification, not necessarily for active popular cult.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

1. **Grain and agricultural abundance — securely early.** She causes barley and flax to rise, heaps grain in stores, and metaphorically possesses nourishing breasts. Grain, rations, storehouses, fields, and harvest are persistent features, not a discarded primitive phase.
2. **Accounting and allocation — Early Dynastic onward.** She totals herds, fixes rations, records decisions, manages seals, and oversees treasuries.
3. **Writing and scribes — Early Dynastic through Old Babylonian peak.** Literary colophons praise her; edubba compositions assign her authority over teachers and learned speech.
4. **Calculation, surveying, and boundaries.** The school text praising her for fixing districts and boundaries, together with architectural and astronomical tablet imagery, supports these functions.
5. **Celestial knowledge.** Her “tablet with stars” or “holy tablet of the heavenly stars” is consulted in *Nisaba A* and Gudea Cylinder A. It can denote celestial writing, omens, or divinely ordered plans; calling her simply an “astrology goddess” is a modern overcompression.
6. **Temple planning and canonical formulation.** She registers Ningirsu’s building plan and fixes the Keš hymn on tablets.
7. **Wisdom and learned order.** This grows from, but exceeds, practical accounting.
8. **Creation:** explicit only in the late CTN 4, 168 title. It is a first-millennium enlargement, not her ordinary early portfolio.
9. **Underworld sovereignty:** confined to the late “Nisaba and Grain” disputation’s learned equation with Ereškigal.

### Tools and recognizable attributes

- **Tablet:** lapis lazuli in *Nisaba A*; a celestial or star-marked tablet in Gudea Cylinder A; clay in *Herds of Nanna*.
- **Stylus:** refined silver in Gudea Cylinder A; gold in *Nanše A*.
- **Seal:** *Nisaba A* calls her keeper of the treasury seal.
- **Grain sheaves or vegetal growth:** the Gudea dream description probably gives the woman sheaf-like vegetation; NAGA and ŠE.NAGA spellings encouraged the association.
- **Grain heap/storehouse:** textual rather than a securely identified artistic emblem.
- **Stars:** written or depicted on her tablet, linking writing, calculation, and heavenly signs.
- **Reeds:** *Nisaba A* gives the damaged phrase “opening the mouth for seven … reeds”; its exact meaning is uncertain.
- **Lapis lazuli:** marks precious divine tablets and celestial appearance.
- **Seven and fifty:** *Nisaba A* speaks of seven reeds/thrones and fifty great *me*. These literary numbers should not be transformed into an exclusive sacred-number system.

### Iconography

A crucial negative finding is that **no depiction is universally and securely identified as Nisaba**. Literary descriptions are far clearer than surviving images. Proposed identifications include:

- Early Dynastic or Akkadian “vegetation goddesses” with plants or grain rising from the body.
- A fragmentary stone vessel from Girsu showing a goddess with vegetation in her headdress, held in Berlin and catalogued as Nisaba ([Berlin museum record](https://search.smb.museum/object/obj-1743571?doc_types=Rundbild+%28Figur-zoomorph%29&sammlungen=Vorderasiatisches+Museum)).
- Female deities on scribes’ cylinder seals.
- Van Buren’s proposal that some Early Dynastic seals show Nisaba supervising construction.

All remain partly inferential because inscriptions naming the figure are lacking. No known figural image securely shows Nisaba specifically “as goddess of writing.” Modern pictures of a winged goddess, a woman holding a modern book, or a figure with fixed zodiacal insignia are reconstructions or inventions.

The **stylus-on-tablet emblem** later associated with Nabû is often said to have been “inherited” from Nisaba. Functional inheritance is plausible; direct transmission of one standardized emblem is harder to prove because her own emblem is not securely attested iconographically.

No securely attested exclusive animal, color, weekday, planet, or fixed sacred plant belongs to her. Claims assigning Mercury, the color blue, an owl, snake, or particular modern festival date are modern extrapolations unless tied to a specified ancient text.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

- **Daughter of Uraš:** explicit in *Nisaba A*, both the Ur III-period stone version and the Old Babylonian recension.
- **Engendered by Enlil/“Great Mountain”:** also in *Nisaba A*. Whether this is literal parentage or hymnic creation/promotion is uncertain.
- **Daughter of An/Anu and Uraš:** preserved in genealogical tradition and later god-list systematization.
- **Daughter of Ea:** a later learned tradition, probably encouraged by the relation between wisdom, writing, Enki/Ea, and Nisaba.
- **Mother of Sud/Ninlil:** explicit in *Enlil and Sud* through the names Nisaba, Nanibgal, and Nunbaršegunu.
- **Enlil’s mother-in-law:** consequence of the Sud marriage narrative.
- **Enlil’s daughter:** a competing tradition. Thus she can be both daughter and mother-in-law of Enlil in different theological constructions.
- **Spouse of Haya/Ḫaia:** standard from the Old Babylonian period; Haya also has scribal and registration functions. The pair is surprisingly rare together in private seal inscriptions.
- **Sister of Nanše:** implied by Nanše’s interpretation of Gudea’s dream—“my sister Nisaba.”
- **Sister of Ninsumun:** preserved in learned genealogical tradition, which makes Nisaba maternal aunt of Gilgamesh, but no Gilgamesh narrative stages an encounter between them.
- **Attendants:** An = Anum assigns her the *sukkals* Ungasaga and Ḫamun-ana.
- **Nanibgal and Ennugi:** when Nanibgal is treated as Nisaba, she can be Ennugi’s wife and Ninlil’s throne-bearer. Since many god lists distinguish Nanibgal, this relationship is not universally transferable to Nisaba.
- **Ašnan/Ezina/Kusu:** occasional equation or compounded identity, against sources that list them separately.
- **Ereškigal:** late learned equation in “Nisaba and Grain.”
- **Nabû:** successor and occasional co-patron, not securely her husband. Claims that she became Nabû’s consort confuse her with Tashmetu or overread later association.

### Documented divine interactions

Each line below records an actual textual relation or episode rather than mere membership in one pantheon.

- **Uraš — birth:** Nisaba is “the great wild cow born by Uraš”; *Nisaba A*, Ur III fragment/Old Babylonian recension. **Tier: securely attested early genealogy.**
- **Enki — blessing and institutional empowerment:** Enki opens her house of learning, places the lapis celestial tablet on her knees, addresses her, and cherishes her; *Nisaba A*, chiefly Old Babylonian copies. **Tier: canonical elaboration with possible Ur III core.**
- **Enlil — service as record-keeper:** she records Enlil’s decisions and supplies his festival and offerings; *Nisaba A*. **Tier: canonical Old Babylonian theology.**
- **Enlil — possible engendering:** “engendered in wisdom by the Great Mountain”; *Nisaba A*. **Tier: ambiguous hymnic genealogy.**
- **Enlil — marriage negotiation:** Nisaba receives Enlil’s emissary and marriage gifts and gives Sud in marriage; *Enlil and Sud*. **Tier: canonical Old Babylonian narrative.**
- **Enlil — destructive anger:** Nisaba’s sanctuary is ruined under the malign word/heart of Enlil; *Nisaba B*. **Tier: Old Babylonian lament, historical setting unknown.**
- **An — chief scribe:** she is An’s chief scribe; *Nisaba A*. **Tier: canonical functional relation.**
- **An — destructive anger:** An’s angry word accompanies destruction of her house; *Nisaba B*. **Tier: Old Babylonian lament.**
- **Haya — spouse and co-administrator:** Haya impregnates Nunbaršegunu in *Enlil and Sud* Version A and elsewhere registers temple personnel while Nisaba handles tablets; *Enlil and Sud*, *Nanše A*, Old Babylonian. **Tier: canonical family/administrative relation.**
- **Sud/Ninlil — mother and daughter:** Nisaba/Nunbaršegunu bears, nurses, raises, and arranges the marriage of Sud; *Enlil and Sud*. **Tier: canonical Old Babylonian genealogy.**
- **Nuska — emissary:** Enlil sends Nuska to Nisaba/Nanibgal to repair the insult to Sud and negotiate marriage; *Enlil and Sud*. **Tier: canonical episode.**
- **Nanše — chief scribe and sister:** Nisaba administers tablets and personnel for Nanše; Nanše identifies her as “my sister” in Gudea’s dream; *Nanše A* and Gudea Cylinder A. **Tier: securely attested Neo-Sumerian/Old Babylonian relation.**
- **Ningirsu — temple planning:** Nisaba’s celestial-tablet work assists Gudea in planning Ningirsu’s E-ninnu; Gudea Cylinder A, c. 2120–2110 BCE. **Tier: securely attested royal narrative.**
- **Nanna/Suen — accountant:** she totals and records his cattle on clay; *Herds of Nanna*, Old Babylonian. **Tier: canonical literary interaction.**
- **Kusu — cultic provision:** Nisaba establishes offerings to appease Kusu; *Nisaba A*. **Tier: canonical ritual relation.**
- **Ezina — cultic provision and identity overlap:** Nisaba establishes offerings for Ezina; later in the same theological field she can be called Ezina-Kusu; *Nisaba A* and *Nisaba B*. **Tier: canonical interaction plus syncretistic identification.**
- **Ašnan/Wheat — debate and contested identity:** Nisaba confronts or is contrasted with personified grain in the Neo-Assyrian disputation; elsewhere they are equated, while lists distinguish them. **Tier: late learned systematization.**
- **Ereškigal — equation rather than episode:** the same late disputation calls Nisaba “Mistress of the Underworld.” No narrative meeting is preserved. **Tier: late scholarly syncretism.**
- **Nabû — co-presence and succession:** Neo-Assyrian texts can invoke both; CTN 4, 168 addresses Nisaba within Nabû’s Kalḫu temple. No surviving myth narrates Nabû defeating or formally taking office from her. **Tier: historical cultic succession and coexistence, not narrative combat.**
- **Anunnakū — dominion-title:** CTN 4, 168 calls her their “net” or controller in a difficult phrase. It does not narrate a meeting and does not call the Anunnakū humanity’s creators. **Tier: Neo-Assyrian theological elaboration.**
- **Nanibgal — identity and differentiation:** some literary passages use Nanibgal as Nisaba’s name; most god lists place them separately. **Tier: variable identity, not a recoverable interaction.**
- **Ninsumun — sisterhood:** learned genealogical tradition calls them sisters; no shared episode is presently known. **Tier: learned genealogy.**
- **Gilgamesh — no documented interaction:** the inferred aunt-nephew relationship produces no extant meeting.
- **Inanna, Ereshkigal in *Inanna’s Descent*, Ninmah in *Enki and Ninmah*, Marduk in *Enūma eliš* — no documented interaction in those narratives.** Their inclusion in the same pantheon is not evidence of an encounter.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The blessing of Nisaba

In *Nisaba A*, the goddess appears simultaneously as vegetation, cosmic intelligence, and administrator. Her body and dress are described in fertile and stellar language. She makes grain and flax grow, organizes Enlil’s festival, supplies offerings, manages treasure and storehouses, and holds the divine tablet. Enki approaches, opens the house of learning, and installs or confirms her authority. The climax is not combat but successful ordering: field, temple, treasury, palace, and tablet are one system.

The surviving Lagash stone version shows that part of the hymn predates the Old Babylonian school canon. The fuller Old Babylonian text expands the Enki setting and the institutional detail. It is sometimes called “The Blessing of Nisaba by Enki,” accurately reflecting its conclusion but potentially obscuring Nisaba’s own prominence.

### The marriage of Sud

In *Enlil and Sud*, Sud stands at Ereš near Nisaba’s E-zagin. Enlil approaches her in the street, but his manner offends her. Sud reports him to her mother. Enlil, advised or served by Nuska, sends gifts and a formal proposal. Nisaba receives the delegation, negotiates the marriage, and her daughter becomes Ninlil, queen of the E-kur.

The tale integrates Ereš’s goddess-family into Nippur’s dominant theology. It probably served, among other things, to explain how Sud of Šuruppak/Ereš-related tradition became Ninlil, Enlil’s wife. It differs from *Enlil and Ninlil*, where the sexual encounter, expulsion, and underworld substitutions dominate. These are variant theological narratives, not consecutive chapters.

### Gudea’s temple dream

Ningirsu commands Gudea to build the E-ninnu but communicates through a symbolically dense dream. A gigantic divine figure is Ningirsu; the woman with stylus and star-tablet is Nisaba; another figure and divine interpreters complete the revelation. Nisaba’s action turns divine will into calculable architectural order. Gudea then gathers materials and constructs the temple.

This is royal commemorative literature inscribed on a monumental object, not an independent “myth of Nisaba.” Nevertheless, it is her earliest securely datable extended narrative appearance and the best evidence for the conjunction of stylus, astronomical tablet, and construction planning.

### The Keš textualization

Enlil praises the Keš temple; Nisaba fixes or “weaves” its words like a net and holds them on tablets. Her role makes divine praise durable and authoritative. The image is an ancient theory of literature: inspiration belongs to the gods, while Nisaba gives ordered textual form.

### The destruction lament

*Nisaba B* begins amid destroyed sanctuaries and overgrown walls. Nisaba, isolated from “her man,” lies in moonlight and laments. Its end moves into the name Ezina-Kusu. Because the text is fragmentary, it cannot safely be assigned to the historical fall of Ereš, Ur, or any particular invasion. Modern retellings that make it a complete story of Nabû’s violent overthrow of Nisaba have no textual basis.

### Nisaba and Grain

The Neo-Assyrian disputation represents learned reflection on whether Nisaba is grain, rules grain, or is superior to personified grain. Its underworld epithet further aggregates divine identities. It belongs to first-millennium scholarly culture rather than early Sumerian myth and is important precisely because it shows late scholars reconfiguring an old goddess after Nabû had become chief scribal patron.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ereš

Ereš was Nisaba’s principal and probably original city. Its name is securely textual; its physical site is not. Tell Abu Ṣalābīkh and Jarin are candidates, but neither identification is proven. The city had sufficient Early Dynastic standing to be represented by a tutelary deity and possibly a ruler.

*Nisaba A* names the **E-zagin**, “Lapis-Lazuli House,” as her residence in Ereš or, in one difficult passage, as placed at her disposal in Aratta. *Enlil and Sud* likewise locates Sud at the gate of E-zagin. Temple-list evidence records Nisaba sanctuaries in multiple cities, surveyed in Andrew George’s *House Most High* ([book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/House_most_high.html?hl=en&id=3LkNAAAAYAAJ)).

No independently excavated building has been securely identified as Nisaba’s exclusive main temple. This absence should not be converted into “she had no temples”: textual temple names and cultic references prove sanctuaries, while archaeology has not yet matched one conclusively.

### Nippur

When Ereš disappears from the documentation after the Ur III period, Nisaba’s cultic center of gravity moves into the orbit of **Nippur**. She belongs to Enlil and Ninlil’s court and seems to have been worshipped especially in connection with Ninlil’s temple and Tummal. God lists classify her within the Enlil circle. This transfer helps explain contradictory genealogies: local theology redefined a formerly independent city goddess through the Nippur family.

### Other locations

Textual sanctuaries or offerings are attested at Lagash/Girsu, Umma, Ur, Kiš, and other southern cities. First Sealand sources continued her cult. In later Assyria, the clearest material context is Nabû’s Ezida at Kalḫu, where CTN 4, 168 was found.

### Personnel and ritual

The evidence does not permit a detailed, continuous reconstruction of a separate Nisaba priesthood. Terms such as *en* priest occur in her hymn, but the passage describes her institution of cultic service more than a surviving administrative roster from her temple.

Attested or inferable practices include:

- grain, bread, beer, oil, and animal offerings;
- festival provision for Enlil’s court;
- recitation of hymns and laments;
- invocation by scribes and teachers;
- closing literary doxologies;
- temple-accounting rituals involving seals, tablets, totals, and allocations;
- consultation of divine or celestial tablets in literary representation;
- offerings in the sanctuaries of related deities, especially Ninlil.

The closing **“Nisaba be praised!”** is the most extensive evidence for her routine presence in scribal culture. Its frequency does not mean every tablet was a votive offering to her; it shows that learned literary work could be placed under her patronage.

### Politics and economics

Nisaba’s mythology reflects temple and palace economies dependent on cereal land, ration systems, surveying, taxation, storage, seals, and documentary control. Her divinity authorizes both abundance and the records that distribute it. Royal compositions such as Gudea Cylinder A present building knowledge and imported materials as divinely sanctioned.

No reliable evidence specifically connects Nisaba’s cult with human sacrifice, cult prostitution, coercive sexual rites, or organized cult violence. General claims about “Sumerian temple prostitution” are controversial and cannot be assigned to Nisaba without direct evidence. Her destroyed sanctuary in *Nisaba B* is literary testimony to devastation, not documentation of a sacrifice or massacre.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Late fourth–early third millennium BCE: emergence

^dNAGA appears at archaic Ur. Nisaba is one of several tutelary goddesses already embedded in the earliest urban written culture. The evidence is primarily lexical and administrative; a developed myth cannot be recovered.

### Early Dynastic period, approximately 2900–2350 BCE

Her cult is associated with Ereš, while literary and lexical traditions increasingly associate her with grain allocation and scribal production. Early compositions and colophonic habits establish her learned authority. The exact point at which “grain goddess” became “writing goddess” cannot be dated to a single reform.

### Akkadian and Lagash II periods, approximately 2350–2112 BCE

She remains an agricultural and administrative goddess. Gudea’s Cylinder A gives the first securely dated extended portrait: stylus, star-tablet, calculation, and temple planning. Proposed contemporary images remain uncertain.

### Ur III period, 2112–2004 BCE

Nisaba is prominent in royal and learned Sumerian culture and receives offerings within state cult networks. The Ur III stone version of *Nisaba A* shows the hymn’s pre-Old-Babylonian core. Ereš is still remembered, but its later disappearance encourages transfer of her functions and cult to Nippur.

### Isin-Larsa and Old Babylonian periods, approximately 2000–1595 BCE: literary peak, institutional transition

Most surviving Sumerian literary manuscripts concerning Nisaba come from this period: *Nisaba A*, *Nisaba B*, *Enlil and Sud*, school dialogues, debates, temple hymns, and royal praise poetry. This is the high point of her visibility as patron of the Sumerian learned curriculum.

At the same time, Nabû’s cult expands in central Babylonia. The common statement that he “replaced” Nisaba is broadly correct but too abrupt if treated as a decree or mythic event. The process was gradual and uneven. Nisaba remained important in Sumerian-language schooling while Nabû became increasingly central to Akkadian scribal identity and state scholarship. A UCL study dates scribes’ clear patronage of Nabû from the later Old Babylonian period and stresses that extant myths do not give Nabû an elaborate writing role comparable to Nisaba’s ([Bywater thesis](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1387202/3/Bywater%20Thesis%20No%20Pictures.pdf)).

### Kassite and Middle Babylonian periods, approximately 1595–1155 BCE

Nabû’s standing becomes increasingly secure. Nisaba’s independent cult is less visible, but she survives in god lists, lexical tradition, inherited Sumerian compositions, agricultural language, and regional cult. This is diminution, not extinction.

### Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian periods, 911–539 BCE

Nabû is now the dominant patron of scribes and scholarship, with major temples and royal sponsorship. Yet Nisaba appears beside him and can still receive elaborate prayer. CTN 4, 168 from Kalḫu’s Nabû temple shows coexistence within the very institution often taken as proof of her disappearance. Late debate literature rethinks her relation to grain and Ereškigal. Royal inscriptions can invoke Nabû and Nisaba together as givers of wisdom or royal protection.

### Achaemenid and Seleucid periods, 539 BCE onward

Her cultic visibility is small but not zero. A Seleucid scholarly text, TCL 6, 38, invokes her blessing; SpTU 2, 29 associates her with a temple. These notices demonstrate survival in learned temple culture into the Hellenistic period. They do not demonstrate an uninterrupted mass cult.

### Late antiquity

As cuneiform temple institutions ended during the late first millennium BCE and early first millennium CE, Nisaba disappears from living documentary religion. No securely traceable Nisaba cult survives in Christian, Jewish, Mandaean, or Islamic Mesopotamian folklore under another name. There is likewise no documented chain connecting her to a medieval grimoire spirit.

### Nineteenth century to present: rediscovery and revival

Excavations at Tello beginning in the 1870s recovered Gudea’s cylinders; publication of Sumerian literary tablets gradually reconstructed Nisaba’s profile. François Thureau-Dangin published an early modern treatment of the goddess in 1909. Twentieth-century editions by Hallo, Civil, Cohen, Lambert, and others established the textual corpus. ETCSL, CDLI, ORACC, and electronic dictionaries made it widely accessible.

Modern Mesopotamian polytheists and reconstructionists sometimes worship Nisaba as patroness of writers, students, archivists, programmers, librarians, and academics. Online devotional calendars adapt Nippur or Babylonian festivals for present practice. This is a genuine modern religious revival but not an uninterrupted survival, and its scale cannot presently be quantified with confidence.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Nisaba never acquired the large Western occult afterlife of Ishtar, Lilith, or Pazuzu. No substantial Nisaba tradition is documented in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, classical ceremonial magic, or the standard early-modern grimoires. Lists claiming an ancient astrological seal, planetary hour, Tarot correspondence, or angelic/demonic counterpart for her are modern constructions unless they cite a specific cuneiform source.

Modern reinventions include:

- **Writers’ and students’ patroness:** a reasonable extension of ancient scribal patronage.
- **Libraries, coding, data, and digital archives:** modern functional analogies, not ancient domains.
- **Feminist recovery of female wisdom:** a modern interpretive program grounded in her genuinely female scribal role but shaped by contemporary concerns.
- **Comparison with Egyptian Seshat:** useful comparativism; no evidence of an ancient equation or borrowing.
- **“Goddess of astrology”:** an expansion from her star-tablet imagery. Ancient texts support celestial calculation, but not a fully defined exclusive astrology cult.
- **Fixed modern iconography:** images giving her a horned crown, sheaves from the shoulders, lapis tablet, and stylus synthesize literary data and uncertain ancient artworks. They should be labeled reconstructions.
- **New Age personality/archetype readings:** modern invention when they assign psychological traits, chakras, crystals, zodiac signs, or manifestation rituals.
- **Ancient-astronaut claims:** CTN 4, 168’s grammar makes Nisaba—not “the Anunnaki”—the bearer of its creator title. Using the tablet as proof that extraterrestrial Anunnaki engineered humanity reverses or ignores the syntax.
- **Commercial and organizational names:** “Nisaba” is used by educational, charitable, publishing, data, and cultural projects. Name use alone is not cultic continuity.
- **Contemporary worship:** publicly visible in online Mesopotamian-polytheist communities. These practices selectively reconstruct offerings, prayers, and calendars from scholarship and then add modern household rites. They are properly classified as modern reconstruction or revival.

No documented nationalist or violent extremist appropriation of Nisaba is prominent in the evidence reviewed. Absence here is a finding, not proof that no isolated use exists.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Reading the name

Older literature commonly printed Nisaba or Nissaba; Civil promoted Nidaba from syllabic variants. Modern discussion has generally returned to Nisaba/Nissaba. ORACC’s retention of “Nidaba” demonstrates that nomenclature can lag or legitimately preserve scholarly convention. Searches should use all forms.

### Grain first, writing later?

The dominant model says Nisaba began as grain goddess and acquired writing through agricultural accounting. It fits archaic administrative history and her combined functions. The caution is that the earliest terse attestations do not furnish a dated narrative of transformation. “Originally only grain, then writing” is a reconstruction, not a documented ancient doctrinal sequence.

### Etymology

Selz’s “lady of grain allocations” gives an elegant institutional explanation; Lambert’s “Lady of Saba” requires an unattested place; the archaic plant-sign theory explains graphic associations but not necessarily phonology. The responsible conclusion is that the etymology remains unsettled.

### Nanibgal

Literature sometimes treats Nanibgal as Nisaba’s praise-name. Most god lists distinguish them, and Nanibgal develops relations—especially to Ennugi and Ninlil’s court—that cannot automatically be assigned to Nisaba. This is a classic case in which learned equation and cultic individuality coexist.

### Ašnan, Ezina, and Kusu

Syncretistic texts merge grain goddesses; offering lists from Lagash enumerate them separately. The source genre matters: a hymn can collapse functions poetically while an account must distinguish recipients materially.

### Ereškigal

Nisaba’s underworld title in the late disputation is real but isolated. It does not make her an early underworld goddess, nor does it establish that Ereškigal was always a form of Nisaba.

### Iconography

Henri Frankfort and later seal specialists classified vegetation deities and divine construction scenes, but without identifying inscriptions most proposed Nisaba figures remain uncertain. Braun-Holzinger’s RlA treatment emphasizes the lack of secure iconography. The Berlin vessel fragment is plausible, not decisive.

### “Displacement” by Nabû

The older narrative of a male god simply replacing a female goddess can imply a single religious reform for which there is no evidence. The documented process is:

1. Nisaba dominates Sumerian literary and scribal ideology.
2. Nabû’s cult expands in central Babylonia during the Old Babylonian period.
3. Later Old Babylonian scribes increasingly identify Nabû as patron.
4. Kassite and first-millennium institutions make Nabû the major god of scholarship.
5. Nisaba nevertheless remains in lists, prayers, agricultural theology, and occasional joint invocations.

Broad arguments that the change reflects a general “decline of goddesses” may identify a real longue durée tendency but do not alone explain each local cultic development.

### State of the evidence

Primary witnesses are dispersed across the Iraq Museum, Penn Museum, British Museum, Louvre, Vorderasiatisches Museum, Istanbul collections, Yale Babylonian Collection, and other tablet holdings. Important monuments include Gudea Cylinder A in the Louvre and CTN 4, 168 from Kalḫu. CDLI provides object-level records; ETCSL supplies composite Sumerian editions; ORACC provides lemmatized corpora, royal inscriptions, dictionaries, and deity dossiers.

ETCSL is foundational but represents editorial work largely completed by 2006; it is not always the latest critical edition. Composite translations can conceal manuscript differences, so object-level editions, handcopies, and newer studies remain necessary. The *Reallexikon der Assyriologie* is now digitally accessible through the Bavarian Academy ([RlA access portal](https://rla.badw.de/digitaler-zugriff.html)).

### Discredited or unestablished popular claims

- Nisaba was not securely the inventor of cuneiform in a surviving ancient creation story.
- She was not demonstrably a wife of Nabû.
- No evidence makes her a member of an extraterrestrial “Anunnaki race.”
- No securely identified cult statue supplies the standardized modern image.
- She has no established ancient Tarot, chakra, crystal, zodiac, or planetary correspondence.
- No evidence supports human sacrifice or “sacred prostitution” specifically in her cult.
- A genealogical relation to Ninsumun does not create a documented encounter with Gilgamesh.
- The loss of scribal primacy to Nabû was historical and gradual; no ancient myth describes a battle or expulsion.
