# Sarpanit (Zarpanitu): Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*ištaru*; divine determinative **𒀭 / d**), specifically the tutelary queen and divine consort of Marduk in Babylonian religion.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The twenty-fourth year-name of Sumu-la-El, king of Babylon (Middle Chronology c. 1880–1845 BCE; year 24 approximately 1857 BCE), commemorating the manufacture of Zarpanitu’s cult statue. This proves that she possessed an official image and royal cult by the early nineteenth century BCE. It does **not** establish that her later identities as Erua, “creatress of seed,” mother of Nabû, or queen of all goddesses were already current.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary evidence, principally Old Babylonian.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** first-millennium or otherwise late texts presenting an established Babylonian theological or ritual system.
- **[RV] Regional or local variant:** a city-specific, Assyrian, Elamite, Dilmunite, or other geographically restricted formulation.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** lexical lists, god lists, explanatory theology, or scholarly wordplay.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically datable equation or fusion with another deity.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or non-institutional survival.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**
- **[U] Uncertain:** evidence is fragmentary or permits more than one interpretation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Zarpanitu was the goddess who occupied the female side of Babylon’s supreme divine household. Her rise followed—but should not be reduced entirely to—the political and theological rise of Marduk. In the earliest evidence she is already an independently named recipient of royal patronage. By the first millennium BCE she is Marduk’s wife, the principal goddess of Esagila, “Lady” or “Queen of Babylon,” and, in learned theology, a female counterpart to Marduk. Under the name or aspect Erua she is associated with pregnancy and birth. Her cult image participated in Babylon’s processional and New Year ritual system.

The evidence does not support treating her as a richly narrated “mother goddess” comparable to Ninhursag or as an interchangeable form of Ishtar. Her surviving mythology is limited; much more is known about her cultic position than about independent adventures.

### The principal name

The normal Akkadian form is:

- **Zarpānītu(m)** or **Zarpanītu(m)**, commonly written **ᵈzar-pa-ni-tum**, with later spelling variants.
- Older and still frequent modern romanizations include **Ṣarpānītu**, **Sarpanit**, **Sarpanitu**, **Zarpanit**, **Zerpanitum**, and **Zirbanit**.

Cuneiform ordinarily does not distinguish initial *z* from emphatic *ṣ* decisively in this name. Wilfred G. Lambert, Andrew George, Joan Goodnick Westenholz, Paul-Alain Beaulieu, and Takayoshi Oshima prefer **Zarpanitu**, in part because alphabetic Aramaic writes the name **zrpnt**, not *ṣrpnt*. The Sefire treaty inscriptions, conventionally dated to the mid-eighth century BCE, supply the important alphabetic evidence. **Zarpanitu** is therefore used here, while **Sarpanit/Ṣarpānītu** remains a legitimate bibliographic variant. [EA/LC; scholarly interpretation]

ORACC’s ePSD2 currently indexes forty-three occurrences distributed across Old Babylonian, Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, and Hellenistic material, with seven spellings and three normalized transcriptions. That count reflects the corpus indexed, not the total historical corpus.

### Etymologies

1. **“She of Zarpan”** — the currently preferred explanation.  
   The name is understood as a nisbe derived from **Zarpan**, a settlement near Babylon: “the woman/goddess belonging to Zarpan.” A fragmentary first-millennium myth from Ashurbanipal’s library reverses the relation by saying that Enlil and Ea gave the goddess a city and named it Zarpan after her. That is best classified as theological etiology, not reliable evidence for the word’s historical derivation. [EA name; LC etiological myth; MSR linguistic conclusion]

2. **Zēr-bānītu, “creatress of seed/offspring.”**  
   Babylonian scholars analyzed the name through *zēru*, “seed,” and *bānītu*, “creatress.” Spellings such as **ᵈNUMUN-DÙ-tu**, literally “seed-creating,” document the interpretation. This is an ancient learned etymology that helped connect Zarpanitu with conception and birth. It need not be the name’s original linguistic derivation. [LS]

3. **Erua and pregnancy.**  
   **Erua** was associated with Akkadian *erû*, “to be pregnant.” This again supports a birth-goddess interpretation in later theology. Erua’s history is more complicated, however: she and Papnunanki originally belonged to the circle of Asalluhi, whose equation with Marduk allowed their transfer to Zarpanitu. [SI/LS]

4. **“Silvery” from *ṣarpu*, “refined silver.”**  
   This appears in older and popular literature but lacks convincing textual or linguistic support. [MSR, largely rejected]

5. **Poplar-tree derivations.**  
   Proposed connections with Akkadian *ṣarbatu*, a poplar, are similarly speculative. [MSR/U]

### Other names and learned equivalents

- **Erua** (spellings include ᵈE₄-ru₆ and variants): originally an independent or Asalluhi-associated goddess; later an epithet, name, or logographic writing for Zarpanitu. In late texts she embodies pregnancy and maternal creation. [SI/LS]
- **Papnunanki / Panunanki**: originally the spouse of Asalluhi. Once Marduk and Asalluhi were equated, Papnunanki could replace Zarpanitu’s ordinary name. A formula of Samsu-ditana provides an Old Babylonian-era example of this transferred usage. [EA/SI]
- **Bēltīya**, “My Lady”: the feminine counterpart of Marduk’s title **Bēl**, “Lord.” It frequently denotes Zarpanitu, especially in first-millennium Babylonian cult, but context matters because *bēltīya* can function as an ordinary title and was sometimes applied to Ishtar. [LC/U]
- **Bēltu**, “Lady,” and **Šarratu**, “Queen.” [LC]
- **Bēlet Bābili / Šarrat Bābili**, “Lady/Queen of Babylon.” [LC]
- **Bēlet Esagil / Šarrat Esagil**, “Lady/Queen of Esagila.” [LC]
- **Bēlet-ilāti**, “Mistress of the Goddesses,” in the learned “Archive of Mystic Heptads.” [LS]
- **Gašan-abzu / Bēlet apsî**, “Lady of the Apsû,” and related names **Nin-abzu**, **Nin-zu-ab**, and **Me-abzu** in laments, vocabularies, and god lists. These reflect her incorporation into the Ea–Asalluhi–Marduk theological family. [LS/SI]
- **Elagu**, described by a Mesopotamian god list as “Zarpanitu of Elam.” No independent Elamite cult of Elagu has been demonstrated; the equation is Mesopotamian learned geography. [LS, not evidence of Elamite worship]
- **Laḫamun/Lahamu**, described as “Zarpanitu of Dilmun.” Again, this occurs in Mesopotamian lists and does not establish a historical Dilmun cult under that name. [LS]
- **Damkianna:** Takayoshi Oshima has suggested that a Neo-Assyrian myth may use this normally Damkina-related name for Zarpanitu. The reading depends on context and comparison with a late prayer; it remains uncertain. [U/MSR]
- **Šerua:** in Assyria, Zarpanitu could be identified with Šerua when Marduk was assimilated to or placed alongside Aššur. Phonetic resemblance between Šerua and Erua facilitated the equation. It is first securely visible in the Sennacherib-era theological environment. [SI/RV]

No secure Greek or Roman interpretatio identifying her consistently with Hera, Aphrodite, Demeter, or another classical goddess is known. Generic comparisons are modern, not ancient equations.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest record

The earliest securely datable testimony is Sumu-la-El’s year 24: a Sumerian-form year-name commemorating the making of Zarpanitu’s statue. Its importance is material as well as nominal: a statue was the installed cultic body through which the deity received dress, food, processions, and royal service. [EA]

Samsu-iluna’s year 19 records:

> “Year in which Samsu-iluna the king made two throne-daises in gold, both of them, for Marduk and Zarpanitum.”

This establishes the pair’s joint cult by the eighteenth century BCE. A related inscription, surviving in a copy from Nippur and datable to Hammurabi or Samsu-iluna, associates Marduk, Zarpanitu, and Esagila with the preparation of cult equipment. [EA]

Old Babylonian letters also invoke Zarpanitu; she is among the more frequently named goddesses in that epistolary corpus, although far behind Ishtar and not furnished with an extensive mythology. [EA]

### Principal textual witnesses

#### Old Babylonian administrative and royal texts

- Sumu-la-El year 24: cult statue. [EA]
- Samsu-iluna year 19: paired golden throne-daises for Marduk and Zarpanitu. [EA]
- Hammurabi/Samsu-iluna cult-object inscription copied at Nippur. [EA/U as to precise reign]
- Samsu-ditana’s use of Papnunanki for the goddess. [EA/SI]
- Letters and personal names invoking her. [EA]

#### God lists and lexical theology

The Old Babylonian forerunner of **An = Anum** treats Erua and Papnunanki as names of one goddess but does not yet explicitly make that goddess Zarpanitu. The later list tradition places Zarpanitu and her names after Marduk and before Nabû, turning familial order into taxonomic order. Babylonian and Assyrian recensions differ, and the Assyrian recension assigns more names. Elagu and Laḫamun are expressly geographical equivalents. [LS]

This evidence documents scribal classification; it cannot automatically be projected backward as popular belief.

#### The Zarpan fragment

A small fragment from Ashurbanipal’s library at Nineveh says, insofar as its damaged text permits reconstruction, that Enlil and Ea gave Zarpanitu a city and named it Zarpan. Lambert published the piece in *Babylonian Creation Myths* under “The Toil of Babylon.” Its date as a surviving manuscript is seventh century BCE; its composition might be older, but cannot be securely dated. [LC/U]

#### Ashurbanipal’s acrostic hymn

SAA 3 no. 2, **“Ashurbanipal’s Acrostic Hymn to Marduk and Zarpanitu”** (ABRT 1, 29–30), survives on a Neo-Assyrian tablet, c. 668–631 BCE. Much of the preserved praise addresses Marduk, but the composition invokes the divine couple and is direct evidence for royal Assyrian appropriation of Babylon’s gods. [RV/LC]

A separate fragmentary dedicatory inscription, RINAP 5/3 no. 1024, probably Ashurbanipal, addresses Zarpanitu with epithets including “goddess of pleasure” and may concern the manufacture of a “pleasure bed”; the object itself is lost in the break. [RV/U]

#### Letters and ritual reports

A Neo-Assyrian letter published as SAA 19 records one ram offered before Bēl and one before Zarpanitu and closes with a request that Marduk and Zarpanitu bless the palace scribe. It supplies unusually concrete evidence for sacrifice rather than only theological naming. [RV/LC]

#### *Love Lyrics*

The title denotes a corpus of first-millennium Assyrian and Babylonian ritual and verbal texts involving Marduk, Zarpanitu, and Ishtar of Babylon. The tablets are fragmentary and were formerly interpreted too readily as ordinary “sacred-marriage songs.” Recent editions emphasize ritual performance, processions, sexual rivalry, and comic or carnivalesque elements.

The central configuration is a triangle: Marduk pursues or receives Ishtar as lover; Zarpanitu responds as jealous wife; Ishtar and Zarpanitu exchange hostile or competitive words. The corpus is the closest thing to a sustained narrative in which Zarpanitu acts rather than merely receives titles. [LC]

#### Babylonian New Year ritual

Late ritual tablets for Nisannu prescribe the *šēšgallu* or chief priest’s movements before Marduk and Zarpanitu. On the fourth day he enters, performs purification, prays to Marduk, turns toward Zarpanitu—addressed as **Bēltīya**—and recites a penitential prayer. Her cult image is consequently an active ritual addressee within Esagila. [LC]

Claims that the king physically had intercourse with an Esagila priestess impersonating Zarpanitu are **not securely supported** by these texts. A divine bed and ceremonies of divine conjugal union are documented; an enacted human sexual rite is a modern reconstruction frequently presented as fact. [MSR/MI]

#### Neo-Babylonian royal inscriptions

Nabopolassar calls himself the shepherd who pleases the heart of **Pa(p)nunanki (Zarpanitu)**. Nebuchadnezzar II repeatedly records work on **Kaḫilisu**, Zarpanitu’s chapel or gate within Esagila, and on the furnishings and architectural setting shared by Marduk, Zarpanitu, and Nabû. These inscriptions place devotion to her within the king’s duty to restore Babylon’s divine household. [LC]

#### Magical and apotropaic texts

A first-millennium ritual in the Corpus of Mesopotamian Anti-witchcraft Rituals places an operation “before Marduk and Zarpanitu.” An incantation found at Ugarit against Lamashtu invokes paired divine couples—Anu and Antu, Enlil and Ninlil, Ea and Damkina, Marduk and Zarpanitu, and Papsukkal and Amasagnudi. These are ritual deployments of divine authority, not narrative myths. [RV/LC]

#### Hellenistic cultic texts

Seleucid-period ritual texts from Babylon continue to mention **Bēl and Bēltīya**, normally Marduk and Zarpanitu. Records connected with royal participation in Babylonian cult include offerings for Bēl, Bēltīya, Ishtar, and the life of the Seleucid king. This demonstrates institutional survival into the third and second centuries BCE, not a simple disappearance in 539 BCE. [LC]

### Texts named in the brief in which she does not appear

Absence matters:

- **Enūma eliš:** Zarpanitu has no meaningful narrative role in the surviving standard text. Marduk’s genealogy and victory are central, but his wife is not made a participant. [Negative finding]
- **Atrahasis:** no securely identified appearance.
- **Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh:** no securely identified appearance; the generic word *ištaru* or titles translated “goddess” should not be converted into Zarpanitu.
- **Inanna’s Descent:** no appearance.
- **Enki and Ninmah:** no appearance.
- **The ETCSL Sumerian literary corpus generally:** no secure narrative about Zarpanitu under her ordinary name.

These absences reinforce that her recoverable identity rests primarily on royal records, god lists, ritual texts, prayers, and temple documentation rather than the famous Mesopotamian epics.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

1. **Divine queenship and Babylon’s welfare.**  
   Her most securely documented first-millennium function is institutional: queen beside Marduk, resident of Esagila, and divine protectress of Babylon. [LC]

2. **Intercession and mercy.**  
   Penitential prayers ask her to forgive guilt or use her favor within the divine court. This resembles the intercessory role of other Mesopotamian goddesses married to powerful male gods. [LC]

3. **Pregnancy, generation, and birth.**  
   Strongly attested under the Erua and zēr-bānītu interpretations. The function belongs especially to later learned theology; it should not automatically be projected into the earliest cult. [LS/LC]

4. **Divination and cosmic authority.**  
   Some late hymnic and scholarly texts give her powers analogous to Marduk’s, including knowledge and judgment. [LC/LS]

5. **Erotic and marital identity.**  
   The *Love Lyrics* portray her as wife, sexual partner, and jealous rival of Ishtar of Babylon. Ashurbanipal’s epithet “goddess of pleasure” and the possible pleasure-bed inscription reinforce an erotic-cultic dimension. [LC/RV]

### Celestial associations

*MUL.APIN* I i 11 identifies a stellar object as:

> “The date-palm branch of Erua, Zarpanitu.”

A New Year ritual calls her “the most brilliant of the stars,” while another prayer describes her as goddess of morning and evening and ruler of the distant heavens. These passages have encouraged an identification with Venus. That identification is plausible in particular learned contexts but is not equivalent to saying that Zarpanitu was universally “the Venus goddess”; Ishtar remained the overwhelmingly established Mesopotamian Venus deity. [LS/U]

Zarpanitu must not be confused with **Zibānītu**, “the Scales,” the constellation Libra. The similar-looking names are distinct in ORACC’s astronomical glossaries. [Corrective finding]

### Iconography

No surviving unlabeled figure can presently be identified as Zarpanitu with confidence solely from distinctive attributes. Her cult statue certainly existed—the Sumu-la-El year-name proves its manufacture—and later texts describe its placement, dress, movement, and furniture. But the statue itself is lost.

Claims that a generic standing Mesopotamian goddess, a star-bearing woman, or a mother-and-child figurine “is Sarpanit” usually rest on modern guesswork. Unlike Ishtar’s lion and weapons, Gula’s dog, or Marduk’s spade and mušḫuššu, Zarpanitu has no securely exclusive surviving emblem. [Negative finding]

The “tablet with a hymn to Marduk and Zarpanitu” sometimes captioned as her image is an inscribed clay tablet, not a portrait of the goddess.

### Associated objects and places

- Cult statue. [EA]
- Golden throne-dais. [EA]
- Divine bed or “pleasure bed.” [LC/U]
- Chapel/cella and its gate, Kaḫilisu. [LC]
- Aromatics, textiles, livestock, prepared food, jewelry, and cult furniture supplied through Esagila’s economy. [LC]
- The stellar date-palm branch of Erua. [LS]

No securely exclusive sacred animal, color, weekday, number, metal, or plant is documented. Modern lists assigning her lions, doves, pomegranates, silver, green, Friday, or the number eight generally borrow Ishtar’s attributes or extrapolate from generic fertility symbolism. [MI]

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

**Spouse:** Marduk. Secure by Samsu-iluna’s reign and foundational to all later evidence. [EA]

**Son:** Nabû. This genealogy becomes standard no earlier than the Kassite or post-Kassite period. Earlier sources can treat Nabû as Marduk’s minister rather than son. Late Babylonian theology makes Marduk, Zarpanitu, and Nabû a family triad; Nabû’s own spouse is usually Tašmetu or Nanaya according to local theology. [LC/developmental]

**Children Ṣilluš-ṭāb and Katunna:** learned god-list traditions can place minor deities in the household, but their status and precise parentage are not nearly as secure as Nabû’s. [LS/U]

**Parents:** no stable parentage exists. The fragment in which Enlil and Ea each grant her Zarpan is not necessarily a statement that either is her biological father, although its wording can sound paternal. Her incorporation into the Ea–Asalluhi–Marduk circle makes her Ea’s daughter-in-law in systematized genealogy. [LC/LS/U]

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

Because much of the evidence is ritual rather than narrative, “joint appearance” is specified where no action between the gods is described.

- **Marduk — spouse and co-resident:** paired throne-daises; joint Esagila cult; invoked together in prayers, sacrifices, magic, royal inscriptions, and processions. Samsu-iluna year 19 and later ritual corpus. Eighteenth century BCE onward. **[EA → LC]**
- **Marduk — erotic/marital interaction:** the *Love Lyrics* stage Zarpanitu as the jealous wife confronting Marduk’s pursuit of Ishtar of Babylon. First-millennium tablets. **[LC]**
- **Marduk — cultic reunion/restoration:** Agum-kakrime’s later-copied inscription claims to recover and reinstall the statues of Marduk and Zarpanitu after their removal from Babylon. The text’s historical reliability and the precise king are debated. **[LC/U]**
- **Nabû — mother and son:** late family theology calls Nabû her offspring; prayers address him as “offspring of Zarpanitu.” Kassite/post-Kassite development, standard in first millennium. **[LC]**
- **Nabû — joint ritual presence:** Marduk, Zarpanitu, and Nabû share cultic space and appear together in Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian inscriptions and festival contexts. **[LC]**
- **Ishtar of Babylon — erotic rival:** Ishtar is Marduk’s lover or pursued partner; Zarpanitu reacts with jealousy and verbal hostility in the *Love Lyrics*. First millennium BCE. **[LC]**
- **Ishtar of Babylon — attempted theological assimilation:** Bēltiya could sometimes be applied to Ishtar, and eighth–seventh-century Babylonian political theology may have sought to subordinate or assimilate local Ishtar forms to Zarpanitu. The two remain distinct in the *Love Lyrics* and in later offering lists. **[SI/U]**
- **Ishtar of Uruk — proposed political assimilation:** Beaulieu reconstructs an effort by Chaldean rulers to align Uruk’s Eanna cult with Babylonian supremacy, possibly involving Bēltiya/Zarpanitu. Direct identification is incomplete and was resisted or reversed. **[MSR/SI/U]**
- **Asalluhi — indirect transferred relationship:** no independent narrative interaction is documented. Because Asalluhi was equated with Marduk, his spouses Erua/Papnunanki were transferred to Zarpanitu. **[SI/LS]**
- **Erua — identity equation rather than encounter:** late theology makes Erua a name or aspect of Zarpanitu. **[SI/LS]**
- **Papnunanki — identity equation rather than encounter:** transferred from Asalluhi’s spouse to a name of Zarpanitu. **[SI]**
- **Enlil — grants her Zarpan:** fragment from Ashurbanipal’s library says Enlil gives her the city and names it. **[LC/U]**
- **Ea — likewise grants her Zarpan:** the same fragment attributes a parallel grant and naming to Ea, producing a conspicuous duplication rather than a smooth story. **[LC/U]**
- **Ea — affine genealogy:** through Marduk/Asalluhi, Zarpanitu becomes Ea’s daughter-in-law in learned ordering; the relation is classificatory, not narrated. **[LS]**
- **Aššur — no direct narrated interaction:** Assyrian theology aligns or assimilates Marduk with Aššur, enabling the corresponding equation of Zarpanitu with Šerua. **[SI/RV]**
- **Šerua — Assyrian equation:** first clearly documented in the Sennacherib-era syncretistic system; supported by analogy of spouses and similarity to Erua. **[SI/RV]**
- **Antu, Ninlil, Damkina, and Amasagnudi — joint incantation:** an Ugarit tablet invokes Zarpanitu and Marduk among a sequence of divine couples alongside these goddesses and their spouses. No narrative interaction occurs. **[RV]**
- **Tašmetu and Nanaya — joint divine household/festival appearances:** offering lists and Assyrian royal catalogues can place them with Zarpanitu, Marduk, and Nabû. No specific personal encounter is narrated. **[LC/RV]**
- **Elagu and Laḫamun — learned equivalents:** god lists call them the Elamite and Dilmunite Zarpanitu respectively; no encounter or independent fusion event is narrated. **[LS]**
- **Ṣilluš-ṭāb and Katunna — possible children or household deities:** documented chiefly through learned lists; no surviving episode of interaction. **[LS/U]**

No securely attested interaction exists between Zarpanitu and Tiamat, Kingu, Enki in *Enki and Ninmah*, Inanna in *Inanna’s Descent*, Gilgamesh, Enkidu, Atrahasis, or the flood hero Utnapishtim.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The granting and naming of Zarpan

A damaged seventh-century BCE library tablet preserves a myth in which Zarpanitu receives Zarpan. Enlil gives her the place and names it for her; a parallel portion apparently attributes the same deed to Ea. The duplication may combine variant traditions, employ ceremonial repetition, or reflect the fragment’s misunderstood structure.

This is not evidence that the historical town was literally named after the goddess. Linguistically, the reverse—Zarpanitu as “the goddess of Zarpan”—is more economical. The myth is a late etiological defense of an established divine name and cult place. [LC/LS/U]

### The marital triangle of Marduk, Zarpanitu, and Ishtar

The first-millennium *Love Lyrics* present the fullest surviving dramatic material concerning Zarpanitu:

1. Marduk moves through Babylon in erotic pursuit of Ishtar of Babylon or receives her as lover.
2. Ishtar occupies the role of seductive outsider or concubine.
3. Zarpanitu reacts as threatened wife.
4. The goddesses exchange songs, accusations, or challenges.
5. Ritual directions connect the words with movement through Babylonian sacred space.

Because the tablets are fragmentary, no single continuous plot or definitive ending can be reconstructed. Older descriptions as a straightforward fertility “sacred marriage” oversimplify a corpus that also appears comic, processional, competitive, and politically charged. [LC/U]

### Removal and return of the divine couple

A later-copied inscription attributed to the Kassite king Agum-kakrime claims that he sought the displaced statues of Marduk and Zarpanitu in Ḫana, purified them, clothed them, and returned them to Esagila. It has traditionally been connected to the Hittite sack of Babylon around 1595 BCE.

The inscription is ideologically useful—it presents a foreign-origin ruler as restorer chosen by Babylon’s gods—but it is not contemporary historical reporting. The king’s identity, date, and the exact history of the statues remain debated. [LC/U]

### The New Year cycle

Zarpanitu is not a protagonist in *Enūma eliš*, even though that composition was recited in the Babylonian New Year setting. Her role belongs to the ritual architecture surrounding Marduk’s festival:

- she occupies her chapel in Esagila;
- the chief priest prays to her as Bēltiya;
- she receives gifts and offerings;
- her image participates in divine assembly and procession;
- conjugal symbolism links her with Marduk.

Later descriptions sometimes turn these facts into an elaborate story of annual sexual union enacted by king and priestess. The cultic bed and divine pairing are genuine; the human sexual reenactment is not demonstrated. [LC versus MSR/MI]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Zarpan

Zarpan, probably near Babylon, was remembered as her original or eponymous cult center. It is known through the divine name, the Zarpan myth, and a Neo-Babylonian or later topographical fragment from Sippar, BM 66534. Its exact archaeological location remains unidentified. [EA name; LC geography; U location]

### Esagila at Babylon

Zarpanitu’s principal historical cult operated inside **Esagila**, Marduk’s great temple complex. Her sanctuary was second only to Marduk’s within the complex:

- **Kaḫilisu**: in royal inscriptions, her chapel or the gate of her cella.
- **Edaraʾana**: called her cella in a gate list and her temple in the Canonical Temple List.
- **Eḫalanki**: called her “seat” in *Tintir = Babylon* II.

These are not necessarily three independent temples; they are partly different classifications of the spaces assigned to her. The relevant northwest sector of Esagila remains incompletely excavated.

Nebuchadnezzar II states that he decorated Kaḫilisu and restored adjacent sacred spaces. Royal building work was an act of service to the divine couple and a claim to legitimate Babylonian kingship. [LC]

### Personnel and economy

Esagila possessed a large institutional establishment: senior lamentation and purification specialists, singers, brewers, cooks, bakers, craftsmen, administrators, scribes, and prebend holders. Zarpanitu’s offerings and wardrobe were supported within this common economy rather than by a separately documented independent priesthood.

A “Hymn to Babylon” refers to people sweeping or serving the courtyard of the great queen Zarpanitu. Administrative evidence supplies livestock, grain, aromatics, oil, textiles, and crafted furnishings for the divine household. [LC]

No securely documented office of “high priestess of Zarpanitu” who ritually married the human king can be identified. [Negative finding]

### Offerings and sacrifice

Attested offerings include sheep and rams, food, drink, aromatics, precious materials, clothing, furniture, and jewelry. A Neo-Assyrian letter explicitly states that one ram was presented before Bēl and another before Zarpanitu. Royal inscriptions mention valuable woods and stones in work for her sanctuary. [LC/RV]

There is **no evidence that human sacrifice formed part of Zarpanitu’s cult**. [Negative finding]

### Temple prostitution

There is **no primary evidence for institutional “temple prostitution” dedicated to Zarpanitu**. The older model of Mesopotamian sacred prostitution has been substantially criticized because it merged distinct female cult offices, sexual vocabulary, classical polemic, and modern assumptions. The *Love Lyrics*, divine bed, and erotic ritual do not prove commercial sex or obligatory intercourse by priestesses. [Negative finding; contested-material result]

### Akītu

At the Nisannu New Year festival:

- Zarpanitu received prayer and offerings in Esagila.
- She was addressed as Bēltīya.
- Her cult image stood within the sequence of divine visits and processions.
- Her marriage to Marduk expressed the ordered divine household.
- Kings could provide gifts and participate in the festival, as Sargon II did in Babylon.

Her part was important but subordinate to the festival’s central drama of Marduk, Nabû, divine assembly, and kingship. [LC]

### Personal devotion and names

Neo-Babylonian theophoric names invoke Zarpanitu, mostly for women. Two noted masculine exceptions are **Ardi-Zarpanitu**, “Servant of Zarpanitu,” and **Ardi-Erua**, “Servant of Erua.” The gender distribution may reflect her association with women, childbirth, or household piety, but the explanation remains inferential. [LC/MSR]

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### c. 1857–1600 BCE: emergence as Babylon’s goddess

The cult statue under Sumu-la-El and golden paired thrones under Samsu-iluna show that Zarpanitu became prominent during the political consolidation of Babylon. She is already Marduk’s cultic partner, but the elaborate late titles and pregnancy theology are not yet demonstrable. [EA]

As Marduk absorbed Asalluhi, Zarpanitu absorbed or inherited Asalluhi’s spouse traditions, especially Papnunanki and Erua. This is the decisive mechanism by which she acquired a fuller learned identity. [SI]

### Late second millennium BCE: construction of the divine family

Nabû’s transformation from Marduk’s minister into his son reorganized the Babylonian pantheon as a family: Marduk, Zarpanitu, and Nabû. Marduk’s elevation toward pantheon leadership likewise elevated his spouse. [LC/developmental]

The return-of-the-statues story associated with Agum makes Marduk and Zarpanitu jointly embody Babylon’s displaced and restored sovereignty. [LC/U]

### First millennium BCE: queen of Esagila

Zarpanitu’s best-documented flourishing belongs to the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian periods. She receives exalted titles, cult furniture, prayers, animal offerings, processional participation, and royal building work. Learned texts give her birth, astral, divinatory, and cosmic dimensions. [LC]

### Assyrian transformation

Assyrian rulers alternately honored Babylonian tradition and reworked it. Sargon II offered gifts to Zarpanitu at Babylon’s *akītu*. Sennacherib’s theological program aligned Marduk with Aššur and facilitated Zarpanitu’s equation with Šerua. Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal restored Babylonian cults while using them to express Assyrian imperial legitimacy. Ashurbanipal commissioned or preserved hymns and possibly a pleasure-bed dedication for her. [RV/SI]

### Eighth–sixth centuries BCE: Zarpanitu, Bēltiya, and Ishtar

In Uruk, the name Bēltiya appears in contexts that can point to Ishtar rather than straightforwardly to Zarpanitu. Beaulieu interprets this as part of a Babylon-centered policy seeking to subordinate Uruk theology. Chronicles accuse Nabû-šuma-iškun’s regime of replacing Ishtar’s proper image with an unsuitable goddess, while Nebuchadnezzar II later restored the image.

The evidence supports rivalry and partial assimilation, not a completed identity. The *Love Lyrics* explicitly require Zarpanitu and Ishtar of Babylon to be separate goddesses. [SI/U]

### Neo-Babylonian peak, 626–539 BCE

Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar II repeatedly invoke or build for the divine household. Kaḫilisu’s restoration, Esagila’s wealth, Babylon’s imperial status, and the royal ideology of Bēl make this the clearest institutional peak in Zarpanitu’s documented cult. [LC]

### Achaemenid and Seleucid survival

Persian conquest in 539 BCE did not immediately terminate Esagila’s cult. Babylonian temple practice continued under Achaemenid administration, although episodes of political conflict and changes under Xerxes remain debated.

Seleucid ritual texts still address Bēl and Bēltīya. Antiochus III participated in Babylon’s New Year rites in 205 and 187 BCE, and offerings could be made for Bēl, Bēltīya, Ishtar, and the king’s life. Hellenistic rule therefore marks transformation and bilingual political negotiation, not instantaneous “suppression.” [LC]

### Parthian decline and disappearance

Cuneiform temple scholarship at Babylon persisted into the Parthian period, but the institutional base contracted. Zarpanitu has no clearly traceable continuous cult in late antique Aramaic, Mandaean, Christian, Jewish, or Islamic practice. Precise final attestation depends on whether ambiguous references to Bēltiya are counted; a sharp last date cannot be established.

There is no documented episode in which Christians or Muslims specifically destroyed a temple of Zarpanitu, forcibly converted her priesthood, or transformed her into a named demon. The cult disappeared through the broader decline of Babylon’s temples, cuneiform expertise, and civic institutions. [Negative finding/U]

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Zarpanitu had little independent reception before the decipherment of cuneiform. Greek and Latin authors knew Babylonian “Bel” more readily than his wife, and no demonstrable medieval magical tradition transmitted her name. [Negative finding]

### Assyriological recovery

Nineteenth-century publication of cuneiform tablets restored the name in forms such as Zerbanit, Zirbanit, and Sarpanit. Early comparativists sometimes reconstructed her as a primordial “fish goddess,” “mother of Marduk,” silver goddess, or generalized fertility power. Some of these claims depended on obsolete readings of lexical lists.

Modern editions have shifted attention to:

- her origin in or connection with Zarpan;
- the Marduk–Asalluhi equation;
- the transferred Erua/Papnunanki identities;
- her role in Esagila and the *Love Lyrics*;
- the political use of Bēltiya in Uruk and Assyria.

### Occultism and neopaganism

No important Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or classical grimoire tradition centered on Zarpanitu has been located. She does not have a transmission comparable to Ishtar, Astarte, Lilith, or Tiamat. [Negative finding]

Individual contemporary Mesopotamian reconstructionists and eclectic pagans may honor her, often as Marduk’s wife, a pregnancy goddess, or “Lady of Esagila.” Such practice is a modern religious revival based on translated scholarly material; it is not demonstrably continuous from antiquity. Its scale cannot presently be quantified. [MI as reconstructed continuity; modern religion as documented phenomenon]

Recent online mythology pages commonly claim that she:

- ruled all fertility;
- protected every pregnancy;
- possessed a star as her fixed emblem;
- renewed agriculture through annual sacred sex;
- was represented by an identifiable generic goddess statue.

These claims combine late Erua theology, Ishtar’s iconography, older sacred-marriage theory, and modern synthesis. They should not be cited as ancient tradition without the underlying text. [MI]

No substantial extremist, nationalist, or state appropriation specifically focused on Zarpanitu was found. Modern Iraqi heritage discourse concentrates more often on Babylon, Ishtar, Marduk, and ancient kingship. [Negative finding]

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Z or Ṣ?

Older reference works favored **Ṣarpānītu**. Lambert argued for **Zarpānītu**, citing alphabetic **zrpnt** and the likely Zarpan place-name. Most recent specialists favor Z, though Ṣ remains common. The issue is orthographic and linguistic, not evidence for two goddesses.

### Place-name or “seed-creatress”?

The leading historical etymology is “she of Zarpan.” The “seed-creatress” explanation is nevertheless ancient and historically significant because Babylonian scholars themselves used it to develop her theology. The correct distinction is:

- probable linguistic origin: Zarpan;
- ancient theological reinterpretation: zēr-bānītu;
- later function encouraged by that reinterpretation: pregnancy and generation.

### Was Erua always Zarpanitu?

No. The Old Babylonian forerunner to *An = Anum* can identify Erua with Papnunanki without explicitly identifying either with Zarpanitu. Their merger follows the equation of Marduk and Asalluhi. Projecting the completed late system into the early second millennium erases the development the lists preserve.

### Was she Ishtar?

Sometimes the titles and political projects overlap, but the goddesses remain distinguishable. The strongest contrary evidence is the *Love Lyrics*, which require both to appear separately. Bēltiya is not a mechanically unique proper name.

### Was she a universal mother goddess?

This is an overstatement. Pregnancy and birth are real late functions under Erua and zēr-bānītu, but Zarpanitu’s earliest and most persistent documented identity is the local goddess and spouse of Marduk. “Mother goddess” may describe one aspect; it should not become a totalizing prehistoric origin story.

### Was the *akītu* a sacred-sex rite?

A divine couple, bed, erotic language, and conjugal symbolism are attested. Sexual intercourse between the king and a human priestess representing Zarpanitu is not. Earlier twentieth-century ritual theories often filled textual gaps with a generalized “sacred marriage” pattern derived partly from Sumerian compositions. Current practice should distinguish divine-image ritual from conjectured human enactment.

### Did her cult practice human sacrifice or temple prostitution?

No evidence establishes either. Their inclusion in modern accounts generally reflects generalized claims about “Babylonian religion,” classical stereotypes, or outdated comparativism—not documentation specific to Zarpanitu.

### State of the evidence

Her record is dispersed across:

- year-name catalogues;
- Old Babylonian letters;
- the *An = Anum* tradition and explanatory lists;
- Nineveh library fragments now principally in the British Museum;
- Neo-Assyrian letters and royal inscriptions;
- Neo-Babylonian royal inscriptions;
- Babylonian ritual tablets;
- magical texts;
- Seleucid temple and astronomical records.

Key modern resources include ORACC’s RINAP, RIBo, SAA, ePSD2, and anti-witchcraft corpora; CDLI’s tablet and year-name databases; Lambert’s *Babylonian Creation Myths*; Oshima’s *Reallexikon* entry; and recent editions of the *Love Lyrics*. Jeremy Black and Anthony Green remain useful for orientation but retain the Ṣ-transcription and necessarily compress disputes.

The often-requested image evidence is the greatest lacuna: texts attest multiple statues, but no securely identified statue survives.
