# Bali (Mahabali): Research Dossier

**Class:** In Sanskrit Brahmanical sources Bali is a **Daitya/Asura king**, not ordinarily an independent god. Later Vaiṣṇava texts make him an exemplary devotee, future Indra, ruler of Sutala, and one of the long-lived *cirañjīvins*. In Kerala he is also the returning ancestral king Māveli, ritually welcomed at Onam. Modern anti-caste and Dravidian readings treat “Bali Chakravarti” as an indigenous egalitarian sovereign displaced by Brahmanical power.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The *Ṛgveda*—usually dated approximately 1500–1200 BCE—attests Viṣṇu’s three cosmic strides, especially RV 1.22, 1.154, 6.49, and 7.100, **but neither names Bali nor tells the dwarf’s deception-and-gift story**. Middle-Vedic prose, most explicitly *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.2.5 (roughly eighth–sixth centuries BCE in its received form), makes Viṣṇu a dwarf who wins the earth from the Asuras through sacrificial stratagem, but still does not name an Asura king Bali. The first securely identifiable Bali–Vāmana conjunction belongs to the Sanskrit epics, notably *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa* 1.29 and Mahābhārata/Harivaṃśa traditions, whose relevant strata were composed and redacted during the last centuries BCE and first centuries CE.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Epic and later canonical elaboration]** Bali is the son of Virocana, grandson of Prahlāda, and descendant of the Daitya matriarch Diti through Kaśyapa. He conquers Indra and the three worlds, becomes celebrated for generosity and fidelity to his word, and is dispossessed when Viṣṇu appears as the dwarf Brahmin Vāmana. Asked for three paces of land, Bali agrees; Vāmana expands into Trivikrama, measures the cosmos, and leaves no place for the third step. Developed Purāṇic versions have Bali offer his own head. Viṣṇu binds or subdues him, then rewards rather than annihilates him: Bali receives Sutala, divine protection, and eventually the office of Indra.

That synthesis is late. The evidentiary sequence is:

1. Vedic Viṣṇu takes three strides, without Bali.
2. Brahmanical Viṣṇu becomes a dwarf and wins land from the collective Asuras, without Bali.
3. Epic tradition names Bali as the dispossessed universal ruler.
4. Purāṇic texts elaborate his sacrifice, Śukra’s warning, cosmic transformation, binding, surrender, wife, grandfather, Sutala, future Indrahood, and devotional status.
5. Kerala folklore makes him the former ruler of Kerala who returns annually at Onam.

### Names and scripts

- **बलि — Bali**, IAST *Bali*. The usual Sanskrit personal name. It must not be confused with **बलि, bali**, “tribute, offering, oblation,” although later storytelling can exploit the resonance between the generous king and giving.
- **महाबलि — Mahābali**, Malayalam **മഹാബലി**: “Great Bali,” or, if read adjectivally, “greatly powerful.” In compounds, *mahā-* means “great”; *balin*/*balī* can mean “strong, powerful.” Popular translations such as “great sacrificer” are possible wordplay, not a demonstrated historical etymology of the character.
- **मावेलि / മാവേലി — Māveli or Māvēli**, the dominant Malayalam vernacular form. It is conventionally understood as a regional development of *Mahābali*, not proof of a separately attested pre-Sanskritic deity.
- **बलिचक्रवर्ती / ബലിചക്രവർത്തി — Bali Cakravartī**, “Bali the universal emperor.”
- **वैरोचन — Vairocana**, “son/descendant of Virocana,” frequent in Sanskrit accounts.
- **Daityendra, Daityarāja, Asurendra**, “lord/king of the Daityas/Asuras.”
- **Indrasena** is sometimes listed in later lexicographical or popular material, but its standing as a major early name for this Bali is weak.
- **Balirāja/Bali Rājā**, “King Bali,” important in western Indian folk and anti-caste discourse.
- **Māvelimannan**, Malayalam/Tamilized “King Māveli.”
- **Future Indra:** *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.13 and 8.22 promise Bali Indrahood in the future Sāvarṇi Manvantara.

The Monier-Williams lexicon distinguishes the personal name Bali and identifies the Daitya as Virocana’s son who lost heaven and earth to the dwarf Viṣṇu. It does not establish that the king originated as a personification of the ritual *bali* offering.

### Classifications through time

- **[Vedic background]** No named Bali; the antagonists are collective Asuras.
- **[Epic]** Daitya king and former ruler of the three worlds; also a philosophical speaker in the *Mahābhārata*’s Indra–Bali dialogues.
- **[Purāṇic Vaiṣṇava]** Asura king, righteous donor, devotee, beneficiary of Viṣṇu’s grace, ruler of Sutala, future Indra.
- **[Medieval philosophical]** In the *Mokṣopāya/Yogavāsiṣṭha*, Bali becomes a contemplative king whose disenchantment and self-knowledge illustrate liberation.
- **[Kerala regional tradition]** An ideal ancestral king who visits his people annually.
- **[Modern political interpretation]** Symbol of egalitarian indigenous or non-Brahmin sovereignty. This is an interpretation of the myth, not independent evidence that a historical “Dravidian King Mahabali” once ruled Kerala.

There is no sound evidence identifying Bali with a Greek, Roman, Iranian, Mesopotamian, or Norse deity. Similarities to worldwide tales of tricked rulers or land measured by a hide are comparative motifs, not historically demonstrated equations.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 The Vedic prehistory of the narrative

**Ṛgveda. [Securely attested early material]** RV 1.154 praises Viṣṇu, “within whose three wide-extended paces all living creatures have their habitation,” and says that he measured the terrestrial spaces in three strides. RV 1.22.17–21, 6.49.13, 7.100, and 8.29 likewise associate Viṣṇu with wide stepping and the “highest step” or station.

What this evidence establishes:

- Viṣṇu’s three strides were an important early Vedic image.
- They articulate cosmic extent, divine support, and a supreme station.
- Viṣṇu is associated with Indra in the wider Vedic corpus.

What it does **not** establish:

- no dwarf;
- no gift of three paces;
- no king Bali;
- no Kerala;
- no annual return;
- no foot placed on Bali’s head.

Solar explanations—sunrise, zenith, sunset—or earth, atmosphere, and heaven are old scholarly interpretations, but the hymns do not reduce the strides to a single explicit astronomical code. Macdonell, Gonda, and later scholars differ on whether the image is primarily solar, spatial-cosmological, ritual, or deliberately multivalent.

**Atharvaveda. [Securely attested early material]** AV 7.26 continues praise of wide-striding Viṣṇu. It adds no named Bali narrative.

**Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā and related Yajurvedic prose. [Securely attested middle-Vedic development]** The Vāmana or dwarf form begins to be associated with Viṣṇu. Dating individual layers is difficult, but the development precedes the completed epics.

**Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.2.5. [Securely attested middle-Vedic development]** The gods and Asuras dispute the earth. The Asuras agree to give the gods as much ground as Viṣṇu can lie upon; “Viṣṇu was a dwarf” (*vāmano ha viṣṇur āsa*). The gods enclose Viṣṇu with sacrificial metres and thereby gain the earth. This is the clearest precursor to Vāmana’s acquisition of territory.

The differences from the later legend are decisive: the opposing Asuras are collective, Bali is absent, the grant is based on Viṣṇu’s bodily extent rather than three requested footfalls, and sacrificial knowledge—not Bali’s personal generosity—is the narrative engine.

### 2.2 Epic corpus

**Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa 1.29. [Epic canonical elaboration]** Viśvāmitra tells Rāma that Bali, son of Virocana, defeated Indra, the Maruts, and the gods and ruled the three worlds. At Siddhāśrama, Kaśyapa and Aditi ask Viṣṇu to be born as their son. Viṣṇu becomes Vāmana, asks Bali for three paces, strides over the worlds, restrains Bali, and restores sovereignty to Indra. The passage explicitly praises Bali’s generosity.

The *Bālakāṇḍa* is generally regarded as later than the epic’s oldest narrative core; accordingly this is evidence for an early epic Vāmana–Bali formulation, not evidence from the Vedic age.

**Mahābhārata. [Epic, multiple strands]** Bali appears beyond the avatāra story:

- Genealogical passages make him Virocana’s son and Prahlāda’s descendant.
- The Śāntiparvan preserves an **Indra–Bali dialogue** in which Indra visits the fallen and bound king. Bali responds to lost sovereignty with philosophical equanimity.
- A following exchange concerns Śrī/Fortune departing Bali and distributing herself elsewhere. Here Bali exemplifies the impermanence of power rather than merely serving as Vāmana’s victim.
- References to Viṣṇu’s conquest of Bali occur throughout epic and didactic material, but recensions vary. The BORI Critical Edition must be distinguished from Ganguli’s translation of the longer vulgate.

**Harivaṃśa. [Epic supplement/later epic elaboration]** The *Harivaṃśa* develops the Daitya genealogy and Vāmana cycle, including Bali’s descent from Prahlāda and the divine response to his sovereignty. Critical-edition and vulgate passages differ; long narrative forms circulated outside or in appendices to the critical text.

### 2.3 Purāṇic corpus

The Purāṇas are layered texts, normally impossible to date by a single year. The dates below refer to broad periods of formation, not the age of every verse.

**Matsya Purāṇa 244–246. [Later canonical elaboration; approximately first millennium CE]** Gives an extended Vāmana cycle, including the gods’ distress, Vāmana’s birth, Bali’s sacrifice, the gift, cosmic strides, and Bali’s removal to the netherworld.

**Vishṇu Purāṇa, principally Book I. [Later canonical elaboration; core commonly placed around the mid-first millennium CE]** Integrates Bali into the dynastic history of the Daityas and the avatāra system. Bali’s conquest is corrected by Viṣṇu, but the king remains a distinguished figure rather than an embodiment of absolute evil.

**Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.11–23. [Later canonical Vaiṣṇava elaboration; commonly dated c. ninth–tenth centuries CE, with debate]** This is the fullest influential narrative:

- Indra defeats and kills Bali; Śukrācārya and the Bhṛgus restore him.
- Bali performs the Viśvajit and a hundred horse sacrifices.
- He conquers heaven; the gods withdraw.
- Aditi undertakes the *payovrata*; Viṣṇu is born to her and Kaśyapa as Vāmana.
- At Bali’s sacrifice at Bhṛgukaccha on the Narmadā, Vāmana requests three paces.
- Śukra recognizes Viṣṇu and warns Bali that the gift will dispossess him.
- Bali refuses to retract his promise, citing truth and his family’s generosity.
- Vindhyāvalī participates in receiving Vāmana.
- Vāmana expands into the cosmic body and covers all domains in two strides.
- Bali’s followers attack against his wish and are repelled by Viṣṇu’s attendants.
- Garuḍa binds Bali with Varuṇa’s noose.
- Bali offers his head for the remaining step.
- Prahlāda and Vindhyāvalī intercede; Brahmā addresses Viṣṇu.
- Viṣṇu grants Bali Sutala, promises personal protection, and assigns him future Indrahood.

The familiar devotional image of Bali as the ideal surrendering devotee derives especially from this recension.

**Vāmana Purāṇa. [Learned Purāṇic systematization]** Despite its title, the extant text is not simply a monograph on Vāmana and contains strong Śaiva materials. It preserves Vāmana-related material but should not automatically be treated as the earliest or normative version.

**Kūrma, Agni, Brahma, Padma, Brahmāṇḍa, Bhaviṣya, Liṅga and Skanda Purāṇas. [Later canonical and regional elaborations]** These offer differing details of Vāmana’s initiation, teachers and gifts; the location and form of Bali’s sacrifice; Śukra’s intervention; the measurement of the worlds; and Bali’s destination. The *Skandapurāṇa*’s early recension also adapts the Vāmana motif to a Śaiva narrative environment. The *Padma Purāṇa* explicitly makes Bali king over netherworld beings.

**Śiva and Liṅga Purāṇa traditions. [Sectarian reuse]** Vāmana and Bali enter broader Śaiva cosmology and lists of Viṣṇu’s manifestations. They do not make Bali a Śaiva god. Claims that a Purāṇa straightforwardly identifies Bali with Śiva require passage-specific proof.

**Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa and Devīmāhātmya. [Absence finding]** Bali is not a principal actor in the *Devīmāhātmya*. Generic Asura kings in that work must not be silently identified with Mahābali.

### 2.4 Philosophical and regional literature

**Mokṣopāya/Yogavāsiṣṭha, Upaśama section. [Medieval philosophical accretion; Kashmirian *Mokṣopāya*, probably tenth century, expanded later]** Bali rules Pātāla, becomes weary of repetitive pleasures, questions his father Virocana’s teaching, consults Śukra, and achieves contemplative insight. This story presupposes the Purāṇic netherworld king but turns him into an exemplar of nondual knowledge. It is not part of the earliest Vāmana cycle.

**Tamil corpus.** *Maturaikkāñci*, conventionally assigned to the late Sangam period, mentions Ōṇam as the natal festival of Māyōn, the Tamil Viṣṇu, amid martial urban festivities. It does **not securely narrate Mahābali’s annual return**. Āḻvār hymns, especially those celebrating *Ulagāḷanda Perumāḷ* (“the Lord who measured the worlds”), make Trivikrama central to Tamil Vaiṣṇava devotion; Bali commonly remains the donor/opponent within the remembered episode.

**Agamas and Tantras. [Learned ritual systematization]** Vaiṣṇava iconographic and temple traditions prescribe forms of Vāmana and Trivikrama, sometimes including Bali, Śukra, Brahmā, attendants, and the donation scene. Bali normally functions as a subsidiary narrative figure, not the central installed deity. The ritual term *bali*—a food offering to attendant beings—is ordinarily lexical and ritual, not worship of King Bali.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic qualities

Bali has no stable Vedic “domain” comparable to Agni’s fire or Varuṇa’s sovereignty. His functions accumulate narratively:

- **Universal kingship:** conqueror of the three worlds.
- **Generosity and truth:** giver who will not retract a vow even when warned.
- **Reversal of fortune:** the great king rendered powerless, especially in the *Mahābhārata*.
- **Devotional surrender:** most explicit in the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*.
- **Netherworld sovereignty:** ruler of Sutala/Pātāla in Purāṇic and medieval literature.
- **Future cosmic office:** future Indra of the Sāvarṇi Manvantara.
- **Egalitarian golden age and annual return:** Kerala folklore and modern civic culture.
- **Counter-sovereignty:** modern anti-caste image of a just indigenous ruler displaced through priestly deception.

### Iconography

Bali usually lacks an independent standardized icon. He is recognized through the Vāmana–Trivikrama narrative:

1. **Donation scene:** a crowned king, often beside Queen Vindhyāvalī, pours water to ratify the gift; dwarf Vāmana carries umbrella, water pot, staff, sacred thread, and sometimes palm-leaf manuscript.
2. **Śukra episode:** the preceptor may be shown attempting to prevent the donation.
3. **Cosmic stride:** gigantic Trivikrama raises one leg vertically or diagonally; Bali appears below, kneeling, seated, restrained, or offering his head.
4. **Submission:** Bali’s hands are in *añjali*; later images emphasize humility and devotion rather than combat.
5. **Kerala festival figure:** modern Māveli is often a smiling, pot-bellied, mustached crowned king carrying a parasol. This popular form is largely modern commercial and festival imagery, not the fixed iconography of early Sanskrit art.

Important survivals include the sixth-century Chalukya Trivikrama panel at Badami, Pallava-period representations at Mamallapuram, the early medieval Namakkal panels, Pāla-period images, and later bronzes and paintings. A seventh-century Pallava description records Vāmana receiving the water of donation from Bali below the enormous Trivikrama. A National Museum sculpture similarly places Bali and Śukra below Viṣṇu’s raised leg. The late nineteenth-/early twentieth-century Ravi Varma Press image helped popularize the scene in which Viṣṇu’s foot presses Bali’s head.

The scene is frequently labeled “Vāmana” or “Trivikrama,” so catalog searches under “Mahabali” alone miss much of Bali’s visual record.

### Symbols and claimed correspondences

Bali’s consistent regalia are royal crown, throne, parasol, sacrificial implements, and water of donation. There is no ancient evidence for a unique sacred animal, planet, color, number, or weapon belonging exclusively to him. Associations with gold, harvest abundance, floral carpets, boats, and the Onam feast belong chiefly to the Kerala festival complex. They should not be retrojected into Vedic religion.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical traditions

The dominant Purāṇic line is:

**Kaśyapa + Diti → Hiraṇyakaśipu → Prahlāda → Virocana → Bali → Bāṇa and numerous other sons.**

Texts vary over the number and names of Bali’s sons; “one hundred sons, Bāṇa the eldest” is common in Purāṇic and Harivaṃśa traditions. Vindhyāvalī is his best-attested wife in the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*. Some later sources give additional wives or descendants, but these are not uniform.

Bali belongs to the Daityas because he descends from Diti. “Asura” in these texts is a theological-political class, not an ethnic label equivalent to “Dravidian.” The conversion of Daitya/Asura into a modern racial category is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century historical interpretation, not a statement made by the early texts.

### Every securely located named divine interaction

- **Viṣṇu/Vāmana/Trivikrama — dispossession, testing, binding, surrender, and reward.** *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* supplies the unnamed-Asura prototype; *Rāmāyaṇa* 1.29 supplies an early named Bali encounter; Purāṇas, especially *Bhāgavata* 8.18–23, elaborate it. **Tier:** epic canonical elaboration with Purāṇic devotional expansion.
- **Indra/Śakra — Bali defeats and replaces him; Viṣṇu restores Indra.** *Rāmāyaṇa* 1.29; *Harivaṃśa*; several Purāṇas. **Tier:** epic and later canonical.
- **Indra — philosophical encounter with the fallen Bali.** *Mahābhārata*, Śāntiparvan, Indra–Bali dialogue: Indra questions the bound former sovereign about his equanimity. **Tier:** epic didactic tradition.
- **Śrī/Lakṣmī — Fortune leaves Bali after his fall and explains the conditions of her residence.** *Mahābhārata*, Śāntiparvan, following the Indra–Bali discourse. **Tier:** epic didactic personification.
- **Aditi — her austerity and request cause Viṣṇu’s Vāmana birth and Bali’s loss of heaven.** *Rāmāyaṇa* 1.29 and Purāṇas; no direct meeting with Bali is required. **Tier:** epic/Purāṇic causal interaction.
- **Kaśyapa — joins Aditi’s request for Viṣṇu’s incarnation against Bali.** Same sources. **Tier:** epic/Purāṇic causal interaction.
- **Śukrācārya/Uśanas — revives, advises, and warns Bali.** *Bhāgavata* 8.15–20 and other Purāṇas. Bali rejects the instruction to revoke his gift; Śukra curses him in some versions but later completes ritual deficiencies. **Tier:** Purāṇic canonical elaboration.
- **Prahlāda — grandfather, devotional model, intercessor.** *Bhāgavata* 8.22; Harivaṃśa/Purāṇic genealogies. **Tier:** Purāṇic elaboration.
- **Brahmā — witnesses Trivikrama and asks Viṣṇu to release or reward Bali.** *Bhāgavata* 8.21–22 and other Purāṇas. **Tier:** Purāṇic elaboration.
- **Garuḍa — binds Bali with Varuṇa’s noose at Viṣṇu’s intention.** *Bhāgavata* 8.21. **Tier:** later canonical elaboration.
- **Varuṇa — his noose is the instrument of Bali’s binding.** *Bhāgavata* 8.21; the *Mahābhārata* also depicts Bali bound in Varuṇa’s cords. **Tier:** epic/Purāṇic motif; usually indirect.
- **The Bhṛgus — restore Bali to life, consecrate him, conduct sacrifices, and repair ritual faults.** *Bhāgavata* 8.15 and 8.23. **Tier:** later canonical elaboration.
- **Bṛhaspati — advises Indra that Bali’s Brahmanically acquired power cannot yet be defeated directly.** *Bhāgavata* 8.15. **Tier:** later canonical.
- **Nārada — appears in wider Daitya and avatāra traditions but has no indispensable direct encounter with Bali in the earliest narrative.** **Tier:** later and text-dependent.
- **Viṣṇu’s attendants—Nanda, Sunanda, Jaya, Vijaya, Viśvaksena, Kumuda, Kumudākṣa, Prabala, Bala, Puṣpadanta, Sātvata and others — repel Bali’s Asura army.** *Bhāgavata* 8.21. **Tier:** later canonical elaboration.
- **Rāhu, Nemi and Vipracitti — Bali orders named Asura commanders to stop fighting because time favors the gods.** *Bhāgavata* 8.21. **Tier:** later canonical; these are divine/demonic beings rather than human officers.
- **Bāṇa — son of Bali; later receives Śiva’s favor and fights Kṛṣṇa in the Aniruddha–Uṣā cycle.** Harivaṃśa and Purāṇas. Bali does not necessarily participate directly in that battle. **Tier:** genealogical relation; no interaction should be inferred beyond texts that explicitly stage one.
- **Śiva — present among the gods who honor Vāmana in *Bhāgavata* 8.23.** This is a joint scene, not a personal Bali–Śiva encounter. **Tier:** later canonical.
- **Kārttikeya — likewise present when Vāmana is installed in authority.** *Bhāgavata* 8.23. **Tier:** later canonical joint appearance.
- **Sūrya, Soma, Earth, Sarasvatī, Kubera, Umā and the Seven Sages — supply Vāmana’s initiation implements before his meeting with Bali.** *Bhāgavata* 8.18. Their relation to Bali is indirect. **Tier:** later liturgical elaboration.

No early text records Bali interacting with Kālī, Durgā, Gaṇeśa, Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, or a non-Indian god as an independent episode. Their membership in the same expanded pantheon is not evidence of an encounter.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 From Viṣṇu’s strides to the dwarf’s land acquisition

**[Securely attested early core]** The *Ṛgveda*’s Viṣṇu strides through and stabilizes the cosmos. The image concerns divine reach and the supreme station.

**[Middle-Vedic transformation]** In the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*, gods and Asuras negotiate over the earth. The Asuras grant the dwarf Viṣṇu only as much as his body occupies, but the gods use sacrificial metres to encompass the earth. The story legitimates the gods’ victory through ritual intelligence.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The epic Vāmana legend plausibly combines these two inherited components: Viṣṇu’s cosmic strides and the dwarf’s deceptively modest acquisition. The insertion of a named, generous king Bali personalizes what was previously a collective gods-versus-Asuras contest. This developmental model is strong; the exact date and mechanism of combination cannot be recovered.

### 5.2 Bali’s conquest and the three paces

**[Epic canonical form]** Bali conquers Indra and the gods. Aditi and Kaśyapa appeal to Viṣṇu, who becomes their dwarf son. During Bali’s sacrifice, Vāmana requests three steps of ground. Bali grants them. Vāmana spans the worlds and restores them to Indra.

The *Rāmāyaṇa* version is brief and politically clear: Bali’s universal sovereignty is reversed, and Indra’s order is restored. It does not yet dwell on Bali’s annual return or Kerala.

### 5.3 The full Bhāgavata cycle

**[Later canonical devotional elaboration]** The *Bhāgavata* reframes defeat as grace. Bali’s power derives from service to the Bhṛgu Brahmins and sacrifice, not sheer demonic violence. Śukra correctly identifies the dwarf as Viṣṇu and advises strategic refusal. Bali replies that falsehood would stain him more than poverty or death. The king therefore defeats the moral test even while losing his empire.

When Vāmana becomes the cosmic person, Bali recognizes that all apparently owned things were already contained in Viṣṇu. His soldiers attack, but Bali restrains them: fate now favors the gods. Bound and accused of failing his promise, he offers his head. Prahlāda praises the removal of wealth as grace; Vindhyāvalī observes that human ownership is illusory; Brahmā asks that the faithful donor be released. Viṣṇu says that those specially favored may first be deprived of pride-producing wealth. He grants Bali Sutala, protection, and future Indrahood.

The third step’s placement varies. Some versions expressly put it on Bali’s head; others emphasize that Bali offers his head but move directly to his reward. The popular image must therefore be cited to particular texts or art rather than treated as identical in every recension.

### 5.4 Bali in the nooses: impermanence rather than devotion

**[Epic didactic variant]** In the *Mahābhārata*, Indra finds Bali fallen, deprived, and bound in Varuṇa’s nooses. Bali refuses grief and explains the passage of time and fortune. Śrī departs his body. The episode is not primarily about Vāmana or loving surrender; it uses Bali as a sage of political reversal. Its theology is less sectarian than the *Bhāgavata*’s and should not be harmonized into it.

### 5.5 Bali as philosopher

**[Medieval accretion]** In the *Mokṣopāya/Yogavāsiṣṭha*, Bali grows weary of cyclic pleasures in Pātāla. Guided by Virocana’s teaching and Śukra, he recognizes consciousness as the basis of experience. His “binding” becomes psychological ignorance; liberation is attained through knowledge. This is a philosophical reuse of an already famous mythic king.

### 5.6 The annual return at Onam

**[Kerala regional tradition/folklore]** Māveli once ruled a realm without deceit, inequality, danger, or want. His prosperity or popularity provoked the gods; Vāmana obtained the kingdom through three paces and sent him below. Because Māveli loved his people—or because the people loved him—he was permitted to return once a year. Kerala households welcome him with flower carpets, new clothes, feast, games, and song.

The well-known Malayalam Onam song beginning *Māvēli nāḍu vāṇīḍum kālam* describes a remembered age when people were equal and free from falsehood. Its exact date and authorship are uncertain; claims that it is an unchanged Vedic or Sangam hymn are false.

The annual-return motif is not securely present in the Vedic, epic, or principal Sanskrit Purāṇic versions. Those sources grant Sutala and future Indrahood. Kerala tradition creatively joins that continuing life below with a periodic homecoming. The date at which this precise form crystallized is not securely established.

### 5.7 Balipratipadā

**[Medieval and early-modern ritual systematization]** On the first lunar day of the bright half of Kārttika, immediately following the principal new-moon night of Dīpāvalī in many calendars, Bali is remembered as returning or briefly reigning. Ritual manuals prescribe a figure or image of Bali, lamps, gifts, festive play, cattle observances, and wishes for prosperity. Regional names include Bali Pāḍyamī, Bali Pratipadā, and Pāḍvā.

The festival is related to the same king but is not simply “northern Onam.” Its calendrical setting, ritual system, and historical development are distinct. Western Indian sayings wishing for the return of *Balīcā rājya* preserve Bali as a figure of hoped-for abundance.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Status of a “cult of Bali”

There is little evidence for an ancient, autonomous institutional cult in which Bali was the principal pan-Indian temple god. The surviving material overwhelmingly embeds him in Viṣṇu/Vāmana/Trivikrama worship or seasonal rites. Calling every Bali image evidence for a separate “Bali cult” would overstate the record.

### Thrikkakara

The Vāmana temple at Thrikkakara near Kochi is the principal Kerala center connecting Vāmana, Mahābali, and Onam. Its main deity is Vāmana; the complex also contains Śiva and a Mahābali-associated installation. Onam is its major festival.

The historical claims require separation:

- **[Epigraphically secure regional evidence]** Early-medieval Kerala inscriptions attest Ōṇam-related endowments, feasts, and temple obligations. An 861 CE copper-plate record from the reign of Sthāṇu Ravi, associated in scholarship with a Thiruvalla/Thiruvattuvāy context, concerns observances and Brahmin feeding. It is often inaccurately presented online as a Thrikkakara inscription.
- **[Epigraphically secure]** An eleventh-century Thrikkakara inscription records offerings around Thiruvonam.
- **[Later temple tradition]** Chera Perumals supposedly assembled subordinate chiefs at Thrikkakara and ordered the festival’s diffusion to households. Elements may reflect the temple’s political centrality, but numerical claims such as exactly 56 kings and 64 chieftains need stronger contemporaneous documentation.
- **[Living practice]** The ten-day festival begins at Atham and ends with Thiruvonam rites, procession, feast, floral decoration, and ceremonial welcoming of Mahābali. Kerala Tourism reports that Vāmana and Mahābali are both honored and that large public *sadyas* are served.

### Household Onam

Documented modern practices include:

- *pūkkalam*, successive floral designs;
- clay or wooden conical figures called *Thrikkakara Appan/Mathevar*, whose identification varies locally among Vāmana, Trivikrama, Śiva, and associated figures;
- *onasadya*, the communal vegetarian feast;
- new clothes, *onakkodi*;
- songs, dances, games, swings, boat races, and local processions;
- ritual or narrative welcoming of Māveli.

Not every Onam custom is specifically directed to Bali. Harvest, royal, temple, domestic, and modern civic components coexist.

### Balipratipadā practice

Regional observances honor Bali through lamps, diagrams or images, offerings, gambling or games, husband–wife rites, cattle decoration, and the symbolic renewal of sovereignty or accounting years. Prescriptions vary among Dharmaśāstra and *nibandha* texts such as the *Dharmasindhu*. No single rite can be declared universally ancient.

### Sacrifice and the word *bali*

Sanskrit *bali* can mean tribute or an offering deposited for gods, spirits, guardians, animals, or beings at ritual boundaries. Tantric and temple *bali* can include vegetarian food and, in some Śākta contexts, animal sacrifice. These practices are not thereby sacrifices **to King Bali**.

No reliable evidence connects Mahābali’s personal worship with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or a priesthood conducting violence in his name. The homonym is a recurring source of false claims. There is likewise no documented historical campaign of forced conversion specifically aimed at a Mahābali cult.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### c. 1500–600 BCE: Vedic components without Bali

The three strides of Viṣṇu and the dwarf who wins territory from Asuras emerge separately. Bali is absent. This is the secure early core behind, but not yet identical with, his legend.

### c. 300 BCE–300 CE: Epic personalization

The developing epics introduce Bali, son of Virocana, as the ruler whose conquest of the gods necessitates Vāmana. The king’s generosity is already important. The *Mahābhārata* separately turns fallen Bali into a philosophical authority on time, power, and fortune.

### c. 300–900 CE: Purāṇic and visual expansion

Purāṇic redaction provides elaborate genealogies, sacrifice, Śukra’s warning, the binding, Pātāla/Sutala, future Indrahood, and the head offered for the third step. Vāmana becomes securely placed in avatāra lists.

Rock-cut and structural temple art at Badami, Mamallapuram, Namakkal, Ellora, and elsewhere gives the story a stable visual syntax: diminutive Brahmin, water gift, gigantic stride, king below.

### c. third–twelfth centuries: South Indian festival and temple formations

The Sangam-era *Maturaikkāñci* knows Ōṇam as Māyōn’s natal day, not demonstrably yet as Mahābali’s return. Tamil Vaiṣṇava poetry celebrates the god who measured the worlds. Kerala inscriptions later document temple-centered Onam observance and economic redistribution through offerings, feeding, dues, and gifts. The exact point when Māveli’s annual visit became central remains unknown.

### c. tenth–fourteenth centuries: Philosophical reinterpretation

The *Mokṣopāya/Yogavāsiṣṭha* makes Bali a liberated philosopher. Ritual compilations increasingly systematize Balipratipadā. Regional Purāṇas and temple traditions continue to multiply variants.

### Early modern Kerala

Onam becomes a broad agrarian, household, royal, and temple festival. Landlord–tenant relations could be enacted through produce, clothing, and feasting. This social order was not necessarily egalitarian even while festival songs praised Māveli’s equality. The contrast between remembered golden age and historical caste society later became politically potent.

### Nineteenth century: anti-caste reinterpretation

Jotirao Phule’s *Gulamgiri* (1873) and related writings transformed Bali Rājā into a central symbol of the indigenous productive majority defeated by Aryan Brahmins. Vāmana’s request became political fraud; Bali’s expected return represented liberation from caste domination. Phule’s reading drew on existing western Indian reverence for Bali but placed it in a modern racial-historical theory of Aryan conquest.

This was a major modern intellectual tradition, but it is not archaeological proof that Mahābali was a historical non-Aryan emperor. “Aryan” and “Dravidian” in nineteenth-century polemic do not map neatly onto present genetic, linguistic, or archaeological categories.

### Twentieth century: Kerala civic icon

Onam became a primary public emblem of Kerala, celebrated across religious communities as well as within Hindu ritual frameworks. State institutions promoted it as a cultural or “national” festival of Kerala. Schoolbooks, advertisements, parades, and cartoons standardized the smiling crowned Māveli.

This civic universalization did not erase disagreement. Vaiṣṇava interpretations celebrate Vāmana and Bali as mutually devoted; anti-caste interpretations mourn Bali’s deception; secular presentations foreground harvest, equality, and homecoming.

### Twenty-first century

Māveli continues simultaneously as:

- a living figure of Hindu festival belief;
- a cultural ancestor for Malayalis;
- a brand mascot and comic figure;
- an anti-caste symbol;
- a subject of disputes about whether Onam “belongs” to Vāmana, Mahābali, harvest culture, Buddhism, or Kerala civic identity.

The evidence supports a historically layered festival. Exclusive-origin claims—“purely Vedic,” “originally Buddhist,” or “proof of one historical Dravidian monarch”—exceed the documentation.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Anti-caste and Dravidian readings

**[Documented modern reinterpretation]** Phule’s Bali Rājā is the just ruler of the oppressed cultivators. Later non-Brahmin, Dalit, Bahujan, and Dravidian writers have read Vāmana as priestly appropriation and Bali as a displaced indigenous sovereign. In Kerala, dark-skinned or plainly dressed depictions of Māveli consciously contest the fair, corpulent, sacred-thread-wearing commercial king.

These readings are historically important political uses of the myth. Their ethical critique of hierarchy does not depend on Mahābali being recoverable as a historical person. Presenting him as a securely documented “Dravidian emperor of Kerala” is modern historical literalization.

### Nationalist counter-readings

Some Hindu nationalist writers answer that Bali was himself part of Sanskritic Daitya genealogy, a grandson of the Vaiṣṇava Prahlāda, and that Vāmana rewarded rather than destroyed him. This accurately reflects major Purāṇic texts, but the claim that those texts exhaust all legitimate Onam meaning ignores the festival’s regional transformations.

Online disputes often create a false binary:

- “Mahābali good, Vāmana evil,” versus
- “Vāmana alone is worshipped and Mahābali is merely a demon.”

The textual record permits neither simplification. Bali can be a conqueror of the gods, righteous donor, defeated rival, exemplary devotee, future Indra, and beloved returning king, depending on source and period.

### Literature, visual culture, and performance

Raja Ravi Varma and Ravi Varma Press imagery helped stabilize the visual moment of Vāmana’s foot on Bali. Malayalam Onam songs and school narratives foreground the annual return and egalitarian reign. Kathā, dance-drama, calendar art, television mythologicals, children’s books, advertising, and festival installations have made Māveli more independently visible than he usually was in early temple art.

The jolly, rotund, umbrella-bearing “Onam mascot” is a modern vernacular-commercial synthesis. It should not be described as an ancient canonical icon.

### Esotericism, occultism, and neopaganism

A dedicated search finds no substantial historical role for Mahābali in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophical ritual, European grimoires, or established Western neopagan reconstructionism. Modern occult websites may recast him as an abundance spirit, underworld king, or archetype of ego-surrender, but these are recent eclectic inventions.

Modern yoga and Advaita presentations legitimately draw on the medieval *Yogavāsiṣṭha*’s philosophical Bali, though they often omit the text’s complicated redaction history.

### Commercial use

Māveli appears in retail campaigns, tourism promotions, greeting cards, inflatable figures, cartoons, and corporate Onam events. Commercial imagery commonly neutralizes the conflict with Vāmana and depicts the visitor as cheerful, harmless, and consumable. That image is socially influential but not a survival of early iconography.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Bali already in the Vedas?

No. Viṣṇu’s strides are Vedic, and the dwarf’s victory over Asuras is Brahmanical; the named king Bali is not present in those passages. Statements that “the complete Mahabali story occurs in the Rigveda” conflate motif ancestry with narrative attestation.

A defensible formulation is: **the Bali–Vāmana story was built from Vedic materials but is first securely recognizable as the named king’s story in the epics.**

### Was Bali a historical Dravidian ruler?

No inscription, coin, contemporary chronicle, or archaeological horizon identifies a historical Mahābali who ruled Kerala. The claim is a modern historicizing interpretation. Its political importance, particularly after Phule and in anti-caste thought, is documentary fact; the proposed ancient biography is not.

“Asura” is a changing Indo-Iranian and Sanskrit religious category. It cannot by itself be translated into a race, language family, caste, or archaeological population.

### Was Onam originally Buddhist?

Buddhist-origin theories point to Kerala’s lost Buddhist institutions, the festival’s ethical and agrarian character, and speculative readings of seasonal chronology. No early Buddhist text or inscription presently demonstrates that Onam began as a Buddhist festival for Mahābali. Buddhist participation or influence in medieval Kerala is plausible in general; exclusive Buddhist origin remains unproven.

### Was Onam originally Vāmana’s birthday rather than Bali’s return?

The strongest early Tamil evidence, *Maturaikkāñci*, connects Ōṇam with Māyōn/Viṣṇu. Early medieval inscriptions document temple observance. The explicit annual-return narrative is harder to date and appears comparatively late in recoverable evidence. Thus a Viṣṇu-centered festival layer is earlier in surviving documents, but that does not make the now-central Māveli tradition fraudulent or trivial; it marks historical development.

### Did Vāmana “murder” Bali?

Major texts do not normally kill him in the final encounter. They restrain, bind, dispossess, or send him to Sutala/Pātāla. The *Bhāgavata* explicitly grants protection and future Indrahood. “Murder” is therefore a modern polemical description, not a neutral summary of that text.

### Was the gift a deception?

The *Śatapatha* prototype unquestionably depends on a small grant expanded through sacrificial power. Epic and Purāṇic narrators differ in moral emphasis. Śukra calls the request a threat and advises refusal; Bali knows the risk yet insists on truth. Vaiṣṇava readings treat the episode as divine testing and grace. Anti-caste readings call it fraudulent dispossession. The narrative itself preserves the tension rather than resolving it historically.

### Etymological disputes

The proper name **Bali** and common noun **bali**, “tribute/offering,” are homonymous and may have encouraged literary association. The record does not prove that King Bali began as the anthropomorphized sacrificial offering. *Mahābali* is transparently a Sanskrit compound or honorific, while Malayalam *Māveli* is its regional phonological form. Attempts to derive Māveli from an undocumented aboriginal royal title remain speculative.

### Textual state and editions

- The Vedas survive in highly controlled oral recensions, but translation and interpretation remain debated.
- The *Mahābhārata* and *Harivaṃśa* exist in numerous recensions; the BORI Critical Edition reconstructs the most widely supported transmissional archetype, not an authorial original.
- Ganguli translates a longer vulgate and contains passages absent from the critical text.
- The *Rāmāyaṇa* also has northern, southern, and northwestern recensions; Goldman’s translation follows the critical edition.
- Purāṇas are open, layered textual traditions with large manuscript variation. Precise dates assigned to whole Purāṇas should be treated cautiously.
- Kerala’s epigraphic evidence is stronger for festival observance than for the full annual-return legend.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Bali appears by name in RV 1.154: **false**.
- The *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* already contains the entire three-footstep Mahabali story: **false**.
- Mahābali is a securely historical Chera emperor: **unestablished**.
- Mahabalipuram was named after this Bali: **generally rejected; the place-name is associated with the Pallava title Māmallan**.
- *Bali* animal sacrifice is worship of King Bali: **false conflation**.
- Onam proves either an exclusively Brahmanical or exclusively anti-Brahmanical origin: **not supported by the layered evidence**.
- A continuous secret Mahabali cult survived from the Indus civilization: **no evidence**.
- Western occult orders preserved an ancient Mahabali rite: **no evidence**.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The reconstructable Bali is not a god with a continuous biography but a figure produced through successive acts of reinterpretation. Vedic poetry supplied the cosmic stride; Brahmanical ritual prose supplied the dwarf’s apparently small land claim; the epics supplied King Bali; the Purāṇas supplied his moral grandeur, binding, Sutala, and future Indrahood; medieval philosophy made him liberated; Kerala made him the returning king; and modern anti-caste thought made his dispossession a history of social power.

The most important negative finding is equally clear: no surviving early source identifies him as a historical ruler of Kerala, and no Vedic source names him in the three-strides story. The most important positive finding is that his rehabilitation is not purely modern. Epic and Purāṇic texts already respect his composure, truthfulness, generosity, and spiritual rank. Kerala’s Māveli and modern Bali Rājā readings magnify genuine possibilities within that older, internally conflicted archive.
