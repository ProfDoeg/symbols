# Vayu: Research Dossier

**Class:** *deva* (male god) in Vedic and later Hindu classification; specifically the divine wind, atmospheric motion, and—through later identifications—the chief life-breath (*mukhya-prāṇa*). In Purāṇic and architectural systems he is also a *dikpāla/lokapāla*, guardian of the northwest. In Iranian tradition the cognate Vayu is a *yazata* whose domain includes wind, atmosphere, the intermediate space, victory, and death.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the Sanskrit name **Vāyú** is securely attested in the orally transmitted *Ṛgveda*, especially hymns 1.134–135 and 7.90–92, within the text’s principal compositional horizon conventionally placed around **1500–1200 BCE** in northwest South Asia. No physical manuscript from that period survives: the evidence is a conservatively transmitted oral text preserved in much later manuscripts. The underlying deity and name are already Indo-Iranian, as shown by Avestan **Vayu**, but neither an archaeological image nor an inscription securely predates the Vedic hymns.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early]** securely attested Vedic or cognate early-Iranian material.
- **[Later Vedic]** Brāhmaṇa, Āraṇyaka, early Upaniṣadic, or related ritual-philosophical elaboration.
- **[Epic]** Sanskrit epic tradition, composed and redacted over several centuries.
- **[Later canonical]** Purāṇic, Āgamic, Tantric, or mature scholastic material.
- **[Regional]** geographically localized tradition or vernacular reception.
- **[Systematization]** learned organization of older material into cosmology, iconography, yoga, or sectarian hierarchy.
- **[Syncretistic]** historically contingent equation with another being or religious concept.
- **[Folklore]** orally or locally transmitted material whose date cannot be fixed securely.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly inference from linguistic, textual, or comparative evidence.
- **[Modern invention/reinterpretation]** demonstrably modern occult, commercial, literary, or popular construction.

Dates below are dates of composition or textual strata, not dates assigned to mythical events.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### 1.1 Identity

In the oldest hymns, Vāyu is not an abstract philosophical “element of air.” He is an anthropomorphic sacrificial guest who speeds to the rite in a noisy chariot drawn by tens, hundreds, or thousands of horses and receives the first Soma draught. The moving wind remains perceptible behind the imagery: his horses express speed, his roar the audible wind, and the Maruts are said to arise from his belly in *Ṛgveda* 1.134.4. He often arrives jointly with Indra as the dual deity **Indrāvāyū**. Jamison and Brereton accordingly distinguish Vāyu from **Vāta**, the wind as a more immediately observable natural force addressed separately in *Ṛgveda* 10.168 and 10.186. [Early] [Jamison–Brereton introduction and translation](https://wiswo.org/books/_resources/book-reference-pdfs/Jamison-Brereton-2014-The%20Rigveda-The%20Earliest%20Poetry%20of%20India%20all%203%20Volume%20Sets.pdf)

Later sources progressively connect or identify:

1. atmospheric Vāyu;
2. the wind called Vāta, Pavana, or Marut;
3. breath in a living organism;
4. cosmic *prāṇa*;
5. one of five bodily “winds”;
6. the northwest directional guardian;
7. the divine father or animating power of Hanumān and Bhīma;
8. in Madhva theology, the highest created soul and indispensable mediator between Viṣṇu and lesser beings.

Those identifications are developments, not all meanings present in the earliest hymns.

### 1.2 Names and scripts

- **Vāyú**, Sanskrit वायु, Vedic accent **vāyú-**: “wind,” “air,” and the god. Conventionally derived from the verbal root **√vā**, “blow,” ultimately Indo-European *h₂weh₁-*. The suffixal formation has a close Iranian cognate. [Early; etymology substantially secure]
- **Vāyu-deva**, वायुदेव: “god Vāyu,” a transparent later devotional designation.
- **Vāta**, वात, Vedic **vā́ta-**: literally “wind,” probably a participial derivative, “that which blows.” Vāta has separate Ṛgvedic hymns; treating “Vāyu-Vāta” as one timeless compound deity obscures this distinction. [Early]
- **Pávana**, पवन: “purifier” or “that which blows”; a common epic and later Sanskrit name for wind and Vāyu.
- **Pavamāna**, पवमान: “self-purifying/purifying”; primarily an epithet of Soma in Vedic usage, but subsequently usable of purifying wind. It is unsafe to equate every Pavamāna occurrence with Vāyu.
- **Anila**, अनिल: “wind/air”; a later common synonym, sometimes personified. Etymological explanations vary, including derivation from √an, “breathe.”
- **Marut**, मरुत्: “wind/storm”; usually the name of the plural storm-gods, not simply an interchangeable personal name for Vāyu.
- **Gandhavaha / Gandhavāha**, गन्धवह: “fragrance-bearer,” describing wind’s carriage of scent. [Later poetic epithet]
- **Śvasana**, श्वसन: “breathing/blowing.”
- **Samīra / Samīraṇa**, समीर/समीरण: “wind, breeze,” later poetic names.
- **Pavana-tanaya, Pavana-putra, Vāyu-putra, Vātātmaja, Māruti**: “son of Pavana/Vāyu/Marut,” titles of Hanumān rather than strict alternative names of Vāyu.
- **Mukhya-prāṇa**, मुख्यप्राण, “chief breath”: Upaniṣadic language given an exceptionally personal and theological interpretation in the Madhva tradition. [Later Vedic term; medieval sectarian systematization]
- **Prāṇa-deva**: “god of vital breath,” especially prominent in Dvaita.
- **Śrī Vāyu Jīvottama**: “Vāyu, highest of souls,” summarizing Madhva hierarchy: *Hari sarvottama, Vāyu jīvottama*—Hari is supreme; Vāyu is supreme among finite souls. [Medieval/modern Dvaita]
- **Avestan Vayu**, 𐬬𐬀𐬌𐬌𐬎; Middle Persian **Wāy**: the Iranian cognate, not a borrowing from Sanskrit.
- **Vayu uparō.kairyō**, “Vayu who operates above” or “of the upper region”: an Avestan title important in Iranian cosmology.
- **Wāy ī weh / Wāy ī wattar**: Middle Persian “Good Wāy” and “Bad Wāy,” a dualized development of Vayu’s beneficent and death-dealing aspects. [Iranian systematization]
- **Viśvaparikara / Wēšparkar**: an eastern-Iranian development possibly connected with Avestan *vayuš uparō.kairyō*. The interpretation of the Kushan coin-name **Oēšo** as Iranian Vayu rather than Śiva remains disputed. [Syncretistic; scholarly debate]

### 1.3 Comparative identification

Vedic and Iranian Vayu are best treated as descendants of a **Proto-Indo-Iranian wind/atmosphere deity**, not as historically identical cults. The shared name, root, and Vayu/Vāta distinction support common inheritance. Iranian Vayu subsequently develops much more explicit associations with the neutral intermediate space and with death. *Yašt* 15 portrays a martial giver of victory; the *Vendīdād*, *Nyāyišn*, *Aogəmadaēčā*, and Middle Persian works expose a feared or harmful aspect. [Early Iranian / later Iranian] [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/vayu/)

Equations with Greek Aeolus, Roman Ventus, Chinese Fēngtiān, Japanese Fūten, or a generic Western “air elemental” are comparative, translational, or iconographic identifications—not evidence of a single ancient international cult. Buddhist Vāyu/Fūten belongs to the transmission of Indian directional and elemental divinities into Central and East Asian Buddhism.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 The earliest evidence

The *Ṛgveda* was orally composed and transmitted. The oldest relevant evidence is therefore textual-linguistic, not an object datable by excavation. Manuscripts are medieval or later, though recitational techniques preserve accent and wording with remarkable precision. A broad scholarly range for the collection is approximately 1500–1100 BCE, with the family books generally earlier than Books 1 and 10. Consequently, 7.90–92 may represent an older stratum than 1.134–135, although neither hymn can be assigned an exact year.

The earliest evidence says that Vāyu:

- comes in a horse-drawn chariot;
- is invited to drink Soma;
- is entitled to its first draught;
- accepts offerings alone and jointly with Indra;
- has an intimate but incompletely defined relation to the Maruts;
- grants prosperity, offspring, cattle, horses, and protection.

It does **not** yet say that he is guardian of the northwest, father of Hanumān or Bhīma, one of five yogic winds, Madhva’s highest finite soul, or an antelope-riding four-armed icon.

### 2.2 Ṛgvedic dossier

Principal passages include:

- **1.2.1–3:** Vāyu is the first deity invited; the prepared Soma awaits him. [Early]
- **1.14.10, 1.23.2–3, 1.139.1–2:** sacrificial invitations and joint drinking.
- **1.134:** the fullest sole hymn. Vāyu is summoned to “drink first”; his chariot has immense teams; he brings wealth; the Maruts are born from his belly. [Early] [Text and Griffith translation](https://rigveda-online.github.io/1/134.html)
- **1.135:** a second extended hymn, alternating Vāyu and Indra-Vāyu; Soma is his divinely allotted portion. [Early] [Text](https://rigveda-online.github.io/1/135.html)
- **4.47–48:** Vāyu and Indra-Vāyu are invoked for the first Soma portion.
- **5.41.3; 5.51.4:** appearances in divine lists.
- **7.90–92:** Vasiṣṭha-family hymns to Vāyu and Indra-Vāyu. [Early]
- **8.26:** Vāyu and Indra-Vāyu.
- **9.108.15:** pressed Soma is acceptable to Mitra, Vāyu, and Varuṇa.
- **10.168:** a hymn to **Vāta**, whose chariot roars and who moves where he wills; his birthplace is unknown. [Early, probably later Ṛgvedic stratum]
- **10.186:** Vāta is asked to bring healing and the stored “immortality” from his dwelling. [Early] [Griffith translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Hymns_of_the_Rigveda/Book_10/Hymn_186)
- **10.90.13, Puruṣa hymn:** wind/Vāyu comes from the cosmic Person’s breath. This is a late Ṛgvedic cosmogonic placement, not an account found in Vāyu’s older hymns.

The expression “first drinker of Soma” describes ritual precedence, not necessarily supreme rank over the pantheon. Vāyu’s priority is embedded in the ordering of Soma cups and repeatedly shared with Indra.

### 2.3 Other Vedic Saṃhitās and Brāhmaṇas

The *Yajurvedic* Saṃhitās and Brāhmaṇas preserve detailed explanations of the **Vāyavya** and **Aindra-vāyava** cups. Some narratives explain why Indra obtained a share in Vāyu’s formerly exclusive cup: Indra performs a needed service—one explanation has him making undifferentiated speech intelligible—and chooses participation in Vāyu’s graha as his fee. [Later Vedic; versions vary by school]

The *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* repeatedly correlates fire, wind, sun, breaths, ritual layers, and cosmic regions. Vāyu can function as the atmosphere or as the breath into which other powers are gathered. The Brāhmaṇas thus provide the bridge from mobile sacrificial god to cosmological breath, but they do not erase the older deity.

The *Atharvaveda* contains invocations of wind and an important independent **Prāṇa hymn**, Śaunaka 11.4. It salutes Prāṇa as lord of all, says beings rest in it, connects it with rain, plants, life, death, and the vital processes, and has the gods worship it. The hymn is crucial to the history of ideas later applied to Vāyu, but its deity is explicitly **Prāṇa**; identifying every verse directly with the personal god Vāyu is an interpretive step. [Later Vedic] [Sanskrit text of AV 11.4.1](https://www.vedicscriptures.in/bhashya/62/atharvaveda/11/4/0/1)

### 2.4 Upaniṣads and the prāṇa doctrine

In the early prose Upaniṣads, the vital breath becomes a central cosmological and psychophysical principle.

- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.3:** the gods employ the senses against the demons; each faculty is corrupted until the breath in the mouth defeats the attackers. [Later Vedic]
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.5.21–23:** the faculties dispute; breath’s departure causes them to fail, demonstrating its supremacy.
- **Chāndogya Upaniṣad 1.2:** the *mukhya-prāṇa* remains unpierced by evil when speech, smell, sight, hearing, and mind are compromised.
- **Chāndogya 4.3:** fire, sun, moon, and water resolve into Vāyu; speech, sight, hearing, and mind resolve into Prāṇa. Vāyu is the cosmic “absorber,” Prāṇa the bodily counterpart.
- **Chāndogya 5.1:** the organs dispute superiority; when Prāṇa prepares to depart, all are uprooted and acknowledge it.
- **Praśna Upaniṣad 2–3:** Prāṇa presents itself as sustaining the organism, divides into subsidiary breaths, and is linked to the sun and cosmic order.
- **Kauṣītaki Upaniṣad 2:** Prāṇa is treated as the primary life-power and object of worship.

These texts supply the foundation for later Vedāntic interpretation. Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, and Dvaita do not construe their language identically. Madhva’s identification of *mukhya-prāṇa* with a particular exalted conscious soul named Vāyu is therefore a sectarian exegesis, not the only transparent meaning of the Upaniṣads.

### 2.5 The epics

The Sanskrit *Mahābhārata* accumulated between roughly the final centuries BCE and early centuries CE, with later additions. Its principal Vāyu traditions are:

- Kuntī invokes Vāyu through Durvāsas’s mantra; Bhīma is conceived, and a heavenly voice predicts unmatched strength.
- Bhīma repeatedly bears the patronymics Vāyu-putra, Vātātmaja, or Mārutātmaja.
- Hanumān identifies Bhīma as his brother because both derive from the wind-god.
- Vāyu appears in assemblies and divine lists and participates in cosmogonic explanations.
- Stories of his conflict with mountains or with Vāsuki explain broken peaks or geographical features in some strata.

The *Rāmāyaṇa*, likewise layered, contains the decisive Hanumān material. In *Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa* 4.66, Vāyu approaches Añjanā without violating her physically and promises a son equal to himself in speed and power; Hanumān is simultaneously Kesarin’s socially recognized son and Vāyu’s direct or spiritual son. When Indra strikes the child Hanumān with the thunderbolt, Vāyu withdraws the air and suffocates the worlds until the gods revive and bless the child. [Epic]

“Hanumān is an incarnation of Vāyu” is stronger than the epic’s ordinary language of paternity or transmitted power. It becomes explicit and systematic in Madhva tradition. Other Purāṇic and regional traditions instead make Hanumān an emanation or incarnation of Śiva/Rudra, or combine Rudra and Vāyu elements. Those accounts are variants, not mutually reducible formulations.

### 2.6 Purāṇas

The Purāṇas are composite works whose chapters may span approximately the fourth through twelfth centuries CE or later. A work’s traditional title does not guarantee that every passage shares one date.

- **Vāyu Purāṇa:** a Śaiva-oriented cosmological, genealogical, geographical, ritual, and dynastic compilation. Its title associates it with Vāyu as narrator or transmitter; it is not predominantly a collection of Vāyu myths. The relation between the *Vāyu Purāṇa* and portions of the *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa* is a major textual problem. Claims for a core as early as the late first millennium BCE are disputed; much of the received text belongs to the early medieval Purāṇic formation. [Later canonical]
- **Viṣṇu Purāṇa:** includes elemental cosmogony, directional organization, and epic genealogies; Vāyu is subordinated within a Viṣṇu-centered cosmos.
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa:** retells Bhīma’s divine parentage, Hanumān-related traditions, and Kṛṣṇa myths involving winds such as Tṛṇāvarta. Tṛṇāvarta is a distinct wind-demon, not Vāyu.
- **Śiva Purāṇa:** preserves Rudra-centered accounts of Hanumān’s origin, creating a genuine conflict with simple “Vāyu alone” parentage.
- **Liṅga Purāṇa:** incorporates Vāyu in Śaiva cosmology, elemental dissolution, and directional systems.
- **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa / Devī Māhātmya:** Vāyu appears among the gods and cosmic powers, but is not a major independent protagonist.
- **Skanda Purāṇa:** a vast accretional corpus containing localized sacred geography, tīrtha narratives, directional guardians, and Vāyu-linked places; passages must be dated by recension rather than by the whole title.
- **Agni, Matsya, and Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇas:** important for developed *dikpāla* and iconographic prescriptions. The dates of individual iconographic chapters are uncertain and often later than the notional date of their host work.

### 2.7 Āgamas, Tantras, yoga, and medicine

Āgamic and Tantric ritual installs Vāyu or the Vāyu principle in the northwest, in protective circuits, *maṇḍalas*, temple walls, and *vāstu-puruṣa-maṇḍalas*. In *bhūtaśuddhi*, the air element is dissolved into ether; the seed syllable **yaṃ**, touch, the skin, smoky or blue-grey color, and a hexagonal or circular emblem are variously assigned to air. These systems differ by text and lineage.

Classical Āyurveda’s **vāta-doṣa** is not simply the god Vāyu. It is a physiological regulatory principle characterized by motion; later writers frequently use divine and cosmic analogies. Yoga and Tantric works classify five principal bodily winds:

- *prāṇa*: inward/upward vital intake, often centered in chest or head;
- *apāna*: downward elimination;
- *samāna*: assimilation and digestion;
- *udāna*: upward movement, speech, and departure at death;
- *vyāna*: pervasive circulation.

Lists of five subsidiary winds are later systematizations. Popular charts assigning every function, chakra, color, mantra, and disease to an immutable ancient scheme often combine sources of very different dates.

### 2.8 Tamil evidence

Early Tamil Caṅkam poetry knows wind primarily through Tamil terms such as **kāṟṟu** and as a feature of landscape poetics. Vāyu is not a comparably prominent independent cult-god in the early corpus. In medieval Tamil Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava bhakti, Sanskritic cosmology is well known; wind, breath, and directional guardians appear, while Hanumān develops a much stronger devotional presence than Vāyu in his own icon. Claims for a major independent “Sangam Vāyu cult” cannot presently be established.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### 3.1 Domains

**[Early]** Wind, rapid atmospheric movement, arrival at sacrifice, carriage, vitality, purification, martial assistance, wealth, horses, cattle, and procreation.

**[Later Vedic]** Cosmic atmosphere; the breath that outlasts or gathers other faculties; the medium into which fire and celestial lights are absorbed.

**[Epic]** Physical force, speed, appetite, restlessness, heroic vigor, and fatherhood of extraordinarily strong sons.

**[Later canonical]** Northwest direction, one of the elemental principles, temple protection, bodily winds, and cosmic dissolution.

**[Madhva systematization]** Chief life-breath, highest finite soul, perfect devotee and servant of Viṣṇu, epistemic and salvific mediator.

### 3.2 Vedic appearance

The hymns offer no stable body type. Vāyu is imagined as:

- arriving in a chariot;
- accompanied by ruddy, tawny, or swift horses;
- possessing enormous teams;
- making a great noise;
- beautiful or desirable;
- drinking Soma before or with Indra.

The golden chariot and vast horse team are earlier than the antelope mount. Statements that the “original Vedic icon” was a four-armed man on an antelope are anachronistic.

### 3.3 Developed iconography

Medieval iconographic texts and surviving temple programs generally identify Vāyu as the northwest guardian. Features vary:

- male, crowned, sometimes bearded;
- two or four arms;
- flowing garments, scarf, or hair suggesting wind;
- a banner or billowing standard;
- goad (*aṅkuśa*), staff, water-pot, sword, or gesture of gift/protection, depending on text;
- vehicle: antelope or deer; in other representations, a chariot with horses;
- color: smoky, grey, blue-grey, white, or coppery, depending on the prescriptive source.

A documented modern museum example from West Bengal shows Vāyu beneath a banana tree holding fruit and a goad, with an antelope before him. This is useful evidence for regional iconography, not a universal canonical form. [National Mission on Monuments and Antiquities](https://nmma.nic.in/nmma/antiqDetail.do?refId=23889&state=WB)

Directional-guardian cycles survive at major early-medieval temples, including Śaiva temple programs at Ellora, Odisha, Khajuraho, and elsewhere. Identification must follow position and attributes: weathered figures can be misidentified, and local cycles do not always contain the canonical eight in textbook order. The eightfold scheme itself developed historically; Vāyu’s northwest position was not a feature of the early Ṛgvedic hymns. [Study of directional gods](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/catalog/view/884/1485/93486)

### 3.4 Associations requiring caution

- **Animal:** antelope/deer is medieval iconographic convention, not clearly Vedic.
- **Color:** no single pan-Hindu canonical color; “green” in modern charts is frequently derived from modern chakra systems.
- **Day or planet:** Vāyu has no universally established weekday or planet comparable to the Navagrahas.
- **Number:** five belongs to the five winds/elements; eight to directional guardians. Neither is an exclusive primordial number of Vāyu.
- **Mantra:** *oṃ vāyave namaḥ* is a straightforward later invocation. The seed *yaṃ* belongs to developed elemental and Tantric systems.
- **Plant:** no securely established plant is exclusively sacred to Vāyu across periods.
- **Weapon:** the banner and goad are better documented than modern claims that a mace is Vāyu’s universal weapon. The mace chiefly reflects Bhīma and Hanumān/Mukhyaprāṇa iconography.

## 4. Genealogy and Divine Relations

### 4.1 Origin accounts

| Version | Source and date | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Vāyu appears as an already existing divine sacrificial guest; no stable parents supplied | *Ṛgveda*, second millennium BCE | [Early] |
| Vāyu arises from the cosmic Person’s breath | *Ṛgveda* 10.90.13, late Ṛgvedic stratum | [Early cosmogonic elaboration] |
| Wind arises in a sequence from ether and generates fire | *Taittirīya Upaniṣad* 2.1, first millennium BCE | [Later Vedic] |
| Vāyu is incorporated into creation by Brahmā or another supreme deity | multiple Purāṇas, first millennium CE | [Later canonical; versions differ] |
| Vāyu is a permanent, exalted finite soul subordinate to Viṣṇu, not a material product in the ordinary sense | Madhva commentarial tradition, thirteenth century onward | [Sectarian systematization] |

Vāyu has no consistently attested wife or single Purāṇic family comparable to Śiva–Pārvatī or Viṣṇu–Lakṣmī. Lists occasionally supply consorts or personifications, but they do not form a stable pan-Hindu tradition.

### 4.2 Offspring

- **Bhīma:** Kuntī invokes Vāyu; the epic represents him as Bhīma’s divine father. [Epic]
- **Hanumān:** son of Añjanā and socially of Kesarin, but conceived through Vāyu’s divine agency or empowered by him. [Epic]
- **Maruts:** *Ṛgveda* 1.134.4 describes the Maruts as born from Vāyu’s belly. This isolated poetic genealogy coexists with much fuller accounts making the Maruts sons of Rudra and Pṛśni. [Early competing genealogies]
- Claims that Vāyu fathered all atmospheric deities are learned generalization, not a stable textual genealogy.

### 4.3 Every recoverable named divine interaction

The following inventory covers specific interactions central to Vāyu’s documented textual history. Mere membership in a list is separated from action.

- **Indra — shared Soma and chariot:** Vāyu and Indra come together as Indrāvāyū and receive the first Soma pressing; *Ṛgveda* 1.135, 4.47–48, 7.90–92. **[Early]**
- **Indra — negotiated ritual share:** later Vedic prose explains how Indra obtained a share in Vāyu’s cup, in some versions for articulating speech; Yajurvedic Saṃhitā/Brāhmaṇa traditions, roughly 1000–700 BCE. **[Later Vedic variant]**
- **Soma — first recipient/drinker:** Soma is purified or poured expressly for Vāyu’s first draught; *Ṛgveda* 1.2, 1.134–135. **[Early]**
- **Maruts — generated from Vāyu’s belly:** *Ṛgveda* 1.134.4. **[Early poetic genealogy]**
- **Maruts — co-invocation:** Vāyu and storm-gods appear in shared sacrificial and atmospheric contexts; scattered Ṛgvedic hymns. **[Early]**
- **Agni — cosmic counterpart:** fire and wind are coordinated as terrestrial and atmospheric powers; later, fire resolves into Vāyu; Brāhmaṇas and *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* 4.3. **[Later Vedic]**
- **Sūrya/Āditya — cosmic counterpart:** sun and wind form celestial/atmospheric correspondences; sun is related to cosmic Prāṇa in the *Praśna Upaniṣad*. **[Later Vedic systematization]**
- **Speech/Vāc — Vāyu or Prāṇa proves indispensable:** speech and other faculties fail without chief breath; *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* 1.3, 1.5 and *Chāndogya* 1.2, 5.1. **[Later Vedic]**
- **Eye, ear, and mind as personified faculties — supremacy contest:** each faculty departs without killing the body; Prāṇa’s attempted departure uproots all; early Upaniṣads. **[Later Vedic]**
- **Puruṣa — cosmogonic generation:** Vāyu/wind emerges from Puruṣa’s breath; *Ṛgveda* 10.90.13. **[Early, late-stratum hymn]**
- **Indra and child Hanumān — crisis mediated by Vāyu:** Indra strikes Hanumān; Vāyu withdraws breath from the cosmos; the gods grant boons and restore order; *Rāmāyaṇa* and later recensions. **[Epic, later elaborated]**
- **Brahmā — appeases Vāyu:** Brahmā leads or joins the gods in reviving and blessing Hanumān after Vāyu’s withdrawal; epic/Purāṇic Hanumān cycle. **[Epic/later canonical]**
- **Sūrya — relation through Hanumān:** Vāyu’s son seeks or receives instruction from Sūrya in later Hanumān narratives; direct Vāyu–Sūrya action is slight. **[Later canonical]**
- **Viṣṇu as Rāma — service through Hanumān:** Madhva theology interprets Hanumān as Vāyu’s first descent accompanying Viṣṇu’s Rāma incarnation. **[Medieval sectarian systematization]**
- **Viṣṇu as Kṛṣṇa — service through Bhīma:** Bhīma becomes Vāyu’s second descent, executing Kṛṣṇa’s purpose; Madhva readings of the *Mahābhārata*. **[Medieval sectarian systematization]**
- **Vyāsa — disciple/teacher relation through Madhva:** Madhva hagiography represents Madhva, third Vāyu incarnation, meeting Vyāsa and receiving authoritative instruction. **[Medieval hagiography]**
- **Śiva/Rudra — competing relation through Hanumān:** Śaiva Purāṇic accounts make Hanumān Rudra’s manifestation, while Vāyu carries or transmits the power; other traditions maintain Vāyu alone as divine father. **[Later canonical competing variants]**
- **Rudra and the Maruts — conflicting paternity:** Vāyu’s “belly” genealogy in *Ṛgveda* 1.134 competes with the widespread Rudra–Pṛśni genealogy. No early text reconciles them fully. **[Early variants]**
- **Vāsuki — contest of strength:** later epic/Purāṇic tradition describes Vāyu and the serpent-king struggling around a mountain; broken peaks are blown away and become islands or landmarks, with details varying. **[Later canonical/regional variants]**
- **Mount Meru or mountains — assault:** in traditions connected with the older motif that mountains once had wings, wind’s force breaks or moves peaks; texts and outcomes vary. **[Epic/Purāṇic accretion]**
- **Ahura Mazdā — petitioner to Iranian Vayu:** in *Yašt* 15, Ahura Mazdā sacrifices to or petitions Vayu for victory. **[Early Iranian]**
- **Spənta Mainyu and Aŋra Mainyu — between both creations:** Iranian Vayu operates between or pursues both creations; *Yašt* 15 and later exegesis. **[Early Iranian/later systematization]**
- **Aži Dahāka and other Iranian heroes or monsters — grants/refusals:** *Yašt* 15 structures episodes around beings sacrificing to Vayu and seeking victory; he grants legitimate petitioners and refuses destructive ones. **[Early Iranian]**

No secure early narrative records Vāyu marrying a named goddess, fighting Varuṇa, or participating personally in the *samudra-manthana* as a principal actor. Modern pantheon charts sometimes infer such interactions from proximity alone.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Vāyu’s first Soma

**Earliest source:** *Ṛgveda* 1.2, 1.134–135, 4.47–48, 7.90–92.

The sacrificers press and filter Soma and summon Vāyu’s immense, rapid horse team. He is told that the first draught is his. In some hymns he comes alone; in others Indra joins him, producing the dual Indrāvāyū. The poetry praises the drink, the god’s speed, and the material benefits expected in return.

Later ritual prose turns the poetic priority into an etiological problem: why does Indra share the cup if Vāyu originally possessed it? School-specific narratives explain an exchange of service for a share. Thus the historical development runs from poetic invitation, to fixed Soma-cup order, to Brāhmaṇic explanation. It is not evidence that Vāyu was once historically “king of all gods” and later overthrown.

### 5.2 Vāta, the mysterious wind

In *Ṛgveda* 10.168, Vāta’s roaring chariot raises dust, precedes dawn, moves between heaven and earth, and follows his own law; the poet does not know where he was born. In 10.186 he is a healer carrying immortality from his home. These hymns preserve an encounter with wind less domesticated by Soma ritual than Vāyu’s chariot hymns.

Later lexicons and mythography merge Vāta and Vāyu. The shared root and domain justify association, but the distinct hymn addressees remain historical evidence of differentiation.

### 5.3 The supremacy of breath

**Earliest full cycle:** Brāhmaṇa and early Upaniṣadic prose.

The faculties dispute which is greatest. Speech, sight, hearing, and mind depart in turn; the organism survives in an impaired state. When Prāṇa prepares to leave, every faculty is dragged out like horses attached to a central stake. They acknowledge Prāṇa’s supremacy and identify their own powers as dependent manifestations.

The story’s function is doctrinal and contemplative: it establishes vital breath as the unity behind differentiated bodily powers. Madhva theology subsequently reads the chief breath as the conscious deity Vāyu. That interpretation is important and historically influential, but later than the Upaniṣadic narrative.

### 5.4 Birth and crisis of Hanumān

In the *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Añjanā is wife of the vāṇara Kesarin. Vāyu approaches her invisibly or mentally and assures her that her chastity is not violated; she will bear a son equal to him in force and speed. Hanumān is consequently both Kesarin’s son in social genealogy and Vāyu’s son in divine genealogy.

In the expanded childhood cycle, Hanumān leaps toward the sun, mistaking it for fruit. Indra strikes him with the thunderbolt, breaking or marking his jaw—popular etymology connects *Hanumān* with *hanu*, “jaw.” Vāyu, enraged, withdraws into a cave and removes air from the worlds. Beings suffocate; the gods placate him by healing Hanumān and granting protections. The episode explains Hanumān’s powers and the cosmos’s dependence on breath.

Variants make Śiva/Rudra the ultimate source of the child, Agni or Vāyu the carrier of divine seed, or connect Hanumān’s conception to Daśaratha’s sacrificial pudding. Those are later Purāṇic and vernacular developments. The familiar synthetic claim “Hanumān is simultaneously Śiva’s incarnation and Vāyu’s son” is a harmonization, not the single wording of the earliest epic account.

### 5.5 Birth of Bhīma

Pāṇḍu cannot father children because of a curse. Kuntī possesses a mantra enabling her to invoke a god. At Pāṇḍu’s request she calls Vāyu, and Bhīma is conceived. A supernatural announcement predicts extraordinary strength. The infant’s fall shatters rock rather than injuring him, establishing the bodily force inherited from his divine father.

The epic normally calls Bhīma Vāyu’s **son**. Madhva tradition interprets him as Vāyu’s second full manifestation. Modern summaries that state this as an uncontested belief of every Hindu tradition erase the distinction.

### 5.6 Bhīma meets Hanumān

While seeking the fragrant Saugandhika flower, Bhīma encounters an old monkey whose tail blocks the path. Unable to move it despite his strength, he recognizes a superior being. The monkey reveals himself as Hanumān, his elder brother through Vāyu, teaches humility, displays a vast form, and promises aid through his presence on Arjuna’s banner and his roar in battle.

Vāyu does not appear bodily in the scene, but shared paternity structures it. Madhva interpreters make the meeting one Vāyu incarnation confronting another.

### 5.7 Vāyu and the mountain/serpent

Later sources tell of a strength contest between Vāyu and Vāsuki. The serpent coils around a mountain; Vāyu blows against it. When Vāsuki relaxes or exposes part of the mountain, a peak is torn away and cast into the sea. Some recensions connect the fallen mass with Laṅkā; others with different islands or mountains.

The narrative’s geography and participants are unstable. It should be treated as a cluster of etiological traditions, not one securely reconstructable early myth. It likely interacts with the widespread motif that mountains once possessed wings until their mobility was stopped.

### 5.8 The three incarnations in Madhva tradition

From the thirteenth century onward, Madhva and his successors interpret Vāyu’s history as three salvific services:

1. **Hanumān**, servant of Rāma;
2. **Bhīma**, servant and instrument of Kṛṣṇa;
3. **Madhva/Ānandatīrtha**, servant of Vyāsa and teacher of correct Vedānta.

The *Bālitthā Sūkta*—*Ṛgveda* 1.141—is reread as cryptically predicting these forms, although the hymn’s immediate Vedic subject is Agni and mainstream historical philology does not accept it as a literal thirteenth-century prophecy. Trivikrama Paṇḍitācārya’s *Vāyu-stuti* and Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍitācārya’s *Sumadhva-vijaya* establish the devotional and hagiographic form of the doctrine. Appayya Dīkṣita and modern scholars have challenged some of the scriptural citations attributed to Madhva. Within Dvaita, however, the doctrine is canonical. [Scholarly discussion](https://academic.oup.com/jhs/article/14/3/279/6321182)

### 5.9 Iranian Vayu

*Yašt* 15 presents Vayu as a formidable armed deity petitioned for victory by Ahura Mazdā and heroic figures. He moves through the interval between good and evil creations and can grant or refuse military success. Elsewhere he receives the souls of the dead or embodies the lethal wind no one escapes. Middle Persian theology makes the ambivalence explicit as Good Wāy and Bad Wāy.

The Iranian parallel shows that the frightening and martial potential of wind is inherited or very old, but individual Avestan episodes cannot simply be imported into Vedic Vāyu’s biography.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### 6.1 Vedic worship

The clearest cult of Vāyu is the Soma sacrifice itself. Priests:

- press and filter Soma;
- invite Vāyu by reciting ṛc verses;
- draw the Vāyavya and Aindra-vāyava cups;
- seat him on ritual grass;
- request wealth, horses, offspring, food, victory, and protection.

This is strong textual ritual evidence. Archaeology cannot normally identify a particular fire altar as “Vāyu’s,” and no securely excavated second-millennium-BCE Vāyu temple is known.

Offerings in this setting include Soma, milk mixtures, clarified butter, grain preparations, and the wider sacrificial complex. Animal sacrifice existed within Vedic religion, but the surviving evidence does not establish a distinctive Vāyu cult centered on human sacrifice. The dedicated contested-material search found no credible primary or archaeological evidence for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or systematic cult violence specifically in Vāyu’s worship. Absence of evidence for a Vāyu-specific institution must not be expanded into a claim about all Hindu traditions.

### 6.2 Independent temples

Vāyu did not develop the extensive independent temple network known for Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī, Gaṇeśa, or Skanda. His most common temple presence is structural:

- northwest niche in an eight-directional guardian cycle;
- northwest station in ritual enclosure and temple-consecration diagrams;
- inclusion in *vāstu* rites;
- subsidiary image in Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and Smārta complexes.

“Vāyu temples” in modern lists often prove to be one of four different things:

1. a temple whose principal deity is locally called Vāyu;
2. a Śiva temple associated with the air element, especially **Śrīkālahasti**, the *vāyu-liṅga* of the South Indian five-element temple scheme;
3. a Hanumān/Mukhyaprāṇa temple;
4. a shrine containing a northwest directional image.

Śrīkālahasti’s primary icon is Śiva as the air-element liṅga, not an independent anthropomorphic Vāyu. Calling it simply “the main temple of Vāyu” confuses elemental identification with divine identity.

### 6.3 Madhva worship

In Karnataka and Madhva communities elsewhere, Vāyu is worshipped chiefly as **Mukhyaprāṇa** through Hanumān, Bhīma, and Madhva. Practices include:

- recitation of the *Vāyu-stuti*;
- worship of Hanumān/Mukhyaprāṇa images;
- celebration of Madhva’s life and teachings;
- theological invocation of Vāyu as the soul’s superior guide to Hari;
- monastic transmission through the Uḍupi institutions and related maṭhas.

This is genuine continuous living worship, though not identical to the Ṛgvedic Soma cult. The institutional center is usually Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa, with Vāyu as chief servant and mediator.

### 6.4 Priesthood, festivals, and economy

No durable hereditary “priesthood of Vāyu” comparable to major temple priesthoods is securely documented. Vedic officiants served the whole sacrificial system; temple priests serve the presiding deity and attendant pantheon. Hanumān festivals, Madhva observances, and local air-element celebrations may include Vāyu, but no single pan-Hindu annual “Vāyu festival” is textually or ethnographically universal.

The scarcity of independent temples limits evidence for a separate Vāyu cult economy, land endowments, temple-servant corporations, or pilgrimage network. Claims about such institutions require local inscriptions; generalized statements cannot be supported.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### 7.1 Indo-Iranian background, before c. 1500 BCE

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Linguistic correspondence between Sanskrit Vāyu/Vāta and Avestan Vayu/Vāta indicates inherited Indo-Iranian terminology and probably a personified atmospheric power. Reconstruction cannot recover a complete Proto-Indo-Iranian myth or icon.

### 7.2 Early Vedic period, c. 1500–1200 BCE

Vāyu is a visible sacrificial god: swift charioteer, Soma’s first guest, partner of Indra, and power behind atmospheric motion. His independent hymn count is modest, but his fixed ritual precedence indicates importance not captured by counting hymns alone.

### 7.3 Later Vedic period, c. 1200–500 BCE

Ritual schools formalize Vāyu’s Soma cups and explain Indra’s share. Cosmological speculation correlates wind with the intermediate region and breath. The Atharvavedic Prāṇa hymn and early Upaniṣadic faculty contests elevate breath from one function to the organism’s sustaining unity.

This is the decisive transition from **wind-god** to **cosmic and bodily vitality**, though Vāyu and Prāṇa remain distinguishable in some texts.

### 7.4 Epic formation, c. 400 BCE–400 CE

Vāyu’s narrative significance shifts into his sons. Bhīma embodies violent physical force, appetite, loyalty, and protection; Hanumān embodies speed, flight, service, and controlled power. The father becomes less prominent as an independent protagonist even while his attributes flourish in heroic descendants.

### 7.5 Purāṇic and classical temple period, c. 300–900 CE

Vāyu enters standardized pantheon architecture as northwest guardian. Purāṇic cosmologies place him within elemental creation and dissolution; iconographic manuals assign form, vehicle, implements, and color. His cult becomes spatially omnipresent in temple diagrams but institutionally subordinate.

The *Vāyu Purāṇa* preserves his name as textual authority, although its content is predominantly broader Śaiva cosmology, genealogy, and sacred history.

### 7.6 Early-medieval to medieval bhakti, c. 600–1300

Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava bhakti magnify the supreme deity while retaining Vāyu among dependent cosmic powers. Hanumān devotion increasingly overshadows direct Vāyu worship. Tamil evidence supports integration into Sanskritic temple cosmology, not a comparably strong independent Vāyu cult.

### 7.7 Madhva transformation, thirteenth century onward

Madhva gives Vāyu an unusually exalted position: beneath Viṣṇu and Lakṣmī but above ordinary finite souls and many gods. He is Mukhyaprāṇa, the paradigmatic devotee, and the mediator whose three descents accompany divine action across three ages.

The doctrine consolidates older epic paternity, Upaniṣadic breath supremacy, and sectarian autobiography into a new historical theology. It constitutes Vāyu’s most important post-Vedic promotion.

### 7.8 Early modern period

Madhva maṭhas, commentators, hagiographies, and South Indian Hanumān traditions sustain Mukhyaprāṇa worship. Elsewhere Vāyu remains an attendant directional deity and linguistic name for wind, while Hanumān becomes one of South Asia’s most popular gods.

There is no evidence of a general suppression campaign directed specifically at Vāyu worship. Temples containing directional images shared the fortunes of their larger institutions during warfare, iconoclasm, royal patronage changes, and colonial reorganization, but that is different from a targeted eradication of a Vāyu cult.

### 7.9 Colonial and modern era

European Indology translated the Vedas and distinguished Vāyu from Vāta through comparative philology. Printed editions made the Soma hymns newly accessible outside hereditary recitation. Hindu reform, yoga globalization, Theosophy, and occultism increasingly rendered Vāyu as “air,” “vital force,” or an impersonal cosmic vibration.

Living Hindu worship continues, particularly through Hanumān and Madhva’s Mukhyaprāṇa. This is continuity by reinterpretation and incorporation, not an unchanged institutional cult extending directly from the Soma sacrifice.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### 8.1 Indology

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century translators such as H. H. Wilson, Ralph T. H. Griffith, Karl Geldner, and Arthur Anthony Macdonell established the familiar scholarly portrait: Vāyu as a relatively transparent personification of wind, more anthropomorphic and ritually specialized than Vāta. Macdonell emphasized the small number of independent hymns, the horses, chariot, Soma priority, and partnership with Indra. Modern work, including Jamison and Brereton, pays closer attention to ritual pragmatics, poetic ambiguity, and hymn structure.

### 8.2 Theosophy and Western occultism

Theosophical literature reinterpreted Vāyu as cosmic motion, intelligence, or a vehicle of “Fohat.” This is a late nineteenth- and twentieth-century esoteric synthesis, not a recoverable Vedic doctrine.

Rāma Prasād’s English presentation of tattva theory, *Nature’s Finer Forces* (1890), influenced the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. Golden Dawn practice represented **Vāyu-tattva** as a blue circle for meditation and “tattva vision.” Here “Vayu” means the element air within an occult correspondence system, not ordinarily the personal Soma-drinking deity. Modern occult sources that describe the blue circle, particular tarot suit, archangel, compass point, and Vāyu as one seamless ancient system are combining Indian and European materials. [Golden Dawn adaptation](https://www.divinityatlas.com/tattvas/air) [Golden Dawn manual](https://s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com/luminist/EB/R/Regardie%20-%20The%20Complete%20Golden%20Dawn.pdf)

### 8.3 Yoga and New Age usage

Modern yoga frequently uses “the five vayus” as a practical physiology. This has premodern textual roots, but contemporary charts often standardize:

- fixed chakra locations;
- precise colors;
- psychological affirmations;
- therapeutic promises;
- breathwork sequences;
- commercial teacher-training diagrams.

The system’s ancient vocabulary should not conceal modern selection and synthesis. “Vayu” in such contexts usually means a current or functional movement of *prāṇa*, not worship of the god.

### 8.4 Literature, film, comics, and games

Vāyu usually enters modern narrative indirectly:

- adaptations of the *Rāmāyaṇa* identify Hanumān as “son of the wind”;
- *Mahābhārata* adaptations use Bhīma’s wind-god parentage to explain strength;
- comics and television anthropomorphize Vāyu in the Hanumān childhood cycle;
- fantasy games often translate him into an “air god,” “wind elemental,” or spell-name.

These media strengthen an elemental four- or five-power image and frequently give Vāyu control of weather generally. The Vedas do not make him an omnipotent controller of every meteorological phenomenon; rain and storm also belong to Parjanya, Indra, and the Maruts.

### 8.5 Political and nationalist reception

Vāyu himself has not become a major independent emblem of modern Hindu nationalism. Relevant uses are usually indirect:

- Hanumān as a public political and devotional symbol;
- Madhva identity in regional religious history;
- claims that modern aerodynamics, atmospheric science, or aviation are completely encoded in Vedic Vāyu narratives;
- yoga promoted as an exclusively unchanged Vedic inheritance.

Such scientific claims are modern apologetic readings unless supported by actual technical content. Scholarly discussion of yoga’s commodification and nationalist framing does not establish a coordinated political “Vāyu movement.” [Modern yoga and politics](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2018.00047/full)

### 8.6 Neopaganism

No large, historically documented Vāyu reconstructionist religion comparable to some modern European pagan movements is established. Individual eclectic practitioners may invoke Vāyu as an air deity, but Hinduism is a living set of traditions rather than an extinct religion requiring archaeological reconstruction. Claims that a Wiccan east/air correspondence is “the ancient Vāyu rite” are modern syncretism. Traditional Hindu directional systems ordinarily place Vāyu in the **northwest**, while Western ceremonial magic normally places air in the **east**—a clear sign that the systems are not identical.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 9.1 Vāyu and Vāta: one god or two?

Older summaries often call Vāta merely another name of Vāyu. The Ṛgvedic distribution argues for distinction: Vāyu is a divine Soma guest with a great horse team; Vāta is the phenomenological wind in 10.168 and 10.186. Their names share a root, and later tradition merges them. The best historical formulation is **early differentiation within a common wind complex, followed by extensive later identification**.

### 9.2 Was Vāyu originally supreme?

His first Soma draught, cosmic associations, and Upaniṣadic supremacy of Prāṇa have encouraged claims that Vāyu was once the supreme Vedic god. The sources establish ritual priority in a specific libation and later philosophical elevation of breath. They do not establish a lost historical monotheistic “Church of Vāyu.” That larger hypothesis remains unsupported.

### 9.3 Is Prāṇa identical to Vāyu?

Evidence supports several levels:

- breath and wind are analogized very early;
- some texts correlate cosmic Vāyu and bodily Prāṇa;
- later systems treat *vāyu* as the generic category of bodily winds;
- Madhva makes Mukhyaprāṇa a particular conscious divine being.

The terms are therefore related but not mechanically interchangeable in every passage. Translating every *prāṇa* as “the god Vayu” prejudges a sectarian question.

### 9.4 Hanumān: son, incarnation, or Rudra?

The *Rāmāyaṇa* prominently supports divine sonship through Vāyu. Madhva tradition supports incarnation. Śaiva Purāṇas and later regional works support Rudra/Śiva manifestation, sometimes with Vāyu as carrier. Combined Rudra–Vāyu accounts are harmonizations. No single description is historically universal.

### 9.5 Bhīma: son or incarnation?

The *Mahābhārata* speaks principally in terms of fatherhood. Dvaita reads Bhīma as Vāyu’s second incarnation. Describing “Bhīma was Vāyu incarnate” without a Madhva label retrojects medieval theology into the epic.

### 9.6 The *Bālitthā Sūkta*

Madhva exegesis reads *Ṛgveda* 1.141 as encoding Hanumān, Bhīma, and Madhva. Historical-philological interpretation treats the hymn in its Vedic sacrificial context, chiefly as an Agni hymn. Appayya Dīkṣita’s polemic and modern debate over Madhva’s untraceable citations make this a central contested issue. The proper documentary conclusion is dual: it is not an accepted historical meaning of the Ṛgvedic composition, but it is authoritative reception history within Dvaita.

### 9.7 Date and identity of the *Vāyu Purāṇa*

The text is composite. Proposed dates for its earliest core range widely, while most scholarship recognizes substantial early-medieval formation and later revision. Its overlap with the *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa*, its designation as *Vāyavīya*, and its Śaiva content generated disputes over whether catalogues refer to one text, two recensions, or a displaced Śiva Purāṇa. Very early datings of the complete received text are not secure.

### 9.8 Iranian Vayu and Kushan Oēšo

The Indo-Iranian cognacy of Vedic and Avestan Vayu is secure. More controversial is the identification of **Oēšo** on Kushan coins. The image has strong Śiva-like traits, while a linguistic argument derives the name through eastern Iranian forms of Vayu. Recent scholarship treats the visual and linguistic evidence as evidence of a flexible syncretic environment rather than a conclusively single identity. [Iranica on Vima Kadphises](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kadphises-vima/)

### 9.9 Contested harmful practices

A dedicated search for Vāyu combined with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, suppression, extremist appropriation, and forced conversion produced no credible evidence of a Vāyu-specific institution in those categories. Relevant cautions:

- Vedic animal sacrifice belongs to a wider ritual system and cannot automatically be assigned as Vāyu’s special cult.
- Narratives in which Vāyu withholds breath are cosmic myths, not evidence of ritual suffocation.
- Bhīma’s brutality is an epic characterization and, in some comparative scholarship, an echo of wind’s ambivalence; it is not evidence for a violent priesthood.
- Destruction or conversion of temples containing a Vāyu niche does not by itself demonstrate targeted suppression of Vāyu worship.
- Modern political uses of Hanumān should not automatically be attributed to Vāyu as a separate god.

### 9.10 State of the evidence

The primary Ṛgvedic evidence survives through oral recensions and medieval manuscripts rather than Bronze Age documents. Critical resources include the Śākala text, the *padapāṭha*, traditional commentaries—especially Sāyaṇa—and modern editions and translations. Epic evidence must be checked against the Baroda critical editions because Ganguli translates a vulgate containing passages excluded or relocated by critical editors. Purāṇic evidence remains especially difficult because manuscripts vary substantially and many works lack complete critical editions.

Material evidence is strongest from early-medieval directional-guardian sculpture onward. Museum catalogues and Archaeological Survey reports document individual images, but an image’s northwest position may be as important for identification as its surviving attributes.
