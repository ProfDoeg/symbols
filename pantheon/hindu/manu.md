# Manu: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest Vedic evidence, Manu is a primordial human ancestor, exemplary sacrificer, culture-founder, and semi-divine *ṛṣi*—not an independently worshipped “god” in the ordinary temple-cult sense. Later texts distinguish numerous Manus, each a divinely appointed progenitor and ruler of a cosmic age; Vaivasvata Manu, survivor of the flood, is the seventh and present Manu. The *Mānava-Dharmaśāstra* further makes “Manu” an archetypal lawgiver and textual speaker.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The name and figure are securely attested in the orally transmitted Vedic Sanskrit hymns of the *Ṛgveda*, conventionally dated in their principal layers to approximately 1500–1000 BCE. No contemporary inscription or image accompanies these hymns. The earliest surviving full flood narrative is considerably later: *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.8.1, commonly placed in the later Brāhmaṇa period, approximately eighth–sixth centuries BCE.

**Evidence warning:** “Manu” is not always one continuously imagined individual. Vedic *Manu/Manus* can be the primordial sacrificer, “Father Manu,” an eponym for humanity, or occasionally nearly an appellative “man.” Epic and Purāṇic sources distinguish Vaivasvata, Svāyambhuva, Sāvarṇi, and other Manus. Dharma literature uses Manu as the speaker and legitimating authority of rules. The dossier therefore does not collapse all occurrences into one biography.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core name

**Manu — मनु, Manu.** The usual etymology connects the name with Sanskrit √*man*, “think,” and with *manas*, “mind”: Manu is the “thinking one” or, historically, “man.” It belongs to the same Indo-European lexical family as English *man* and German *Mann*. The associated Sanskrit terms *manuṣya* and *mānava* mean “human,” “human being,” or “descendant of Manu.”

- **Secure linguistic reconstruction:** A Proto-Indo-Iranian form conventionally reconstructed as something like \**Manu-* is plausible from Indo-Aryan and Iranian evidence.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** Comparison with Germanic Mannus, reported by Tacitus as an ancestral progenitor, has encouraged reconstruction of a Proto-Indo-European ancestral “Man.” The linguistic comparison is strong; a detailed common myth cannot be reconstructed with equal certainty.
- **Disputed extension:** Georges Dumézil’s and Bruce Lincoln’s reconstructed “Manu and Yemo” myth interprets Manu as a primordial priest and *Yemo* (“Twin,” Vedic Yama) as a sacrificed primordial victim. This is comparative reconstruction, not a narrative preserved in an Indian text about Manu killing Yama.

### Major designations

- **Manu / Manus:** Vedic nominative forms referring to the ancestral sacrificer or, in some contexts, humanity’s representative.
- **Manuṣ pitā / “Father Manu”:** Vedic ancestral title. *Ṛgveda* 2.33.13 calls him “Father Manu” in connection with prosperity and longevity obtained through sacrifice.
- **Vaivasvata Manu — वैवस्वत मनु, “Manu descended from Vivasvat”:** The solar Manu and flood survivor, made the seventh Manu in the developed system.
- **Manu Vivasvat / Manu Vivasvant:** Archaic Vedic phrasing connecting Manu with Vivasvat; *Ṛgveda* 8.52.1 recalls Indra drinking Soma with Manu Vivasvat.
- **Śrāddhadeva — श्राद्धदेव:** A later name of Vaivasvata Manu, especially in Purāṇic genealogy.
- **Satyavrata — सत्यव्रत, “true to his vow”:** In *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.24 the pre-flood king Satyavrata becomes Vaivasvata Manu. This identification is a later Vaiṣṇava elaboration, not present in the Vedic flood account.
- **Svāyambhuva Manu — स्वायम्भुव मनु, “Manu of the Self-existent”:** The first Manu in later cosmology, born from or produced by Brahmā/Svayambhū.
- **Prācetasa Manu:** “Manu descended from Pracetas”; used in learned traditions about Manu the lawgiver and sometimes distinguished from Vaivasvata.
- **Sāvarṇi Manu — सावर्णि:** Usually the eighth and next Manu. Genealogies derive the epithet from Savarṇā/Chāyā, the Sun’s substitute wife.
- **Āpsava and Sāṃvaraṇi:** Vedic patronymic or lineage forms associated with seers named Manu. Their precise relationship to the primordial Manu is uncertain.

### Classification through time

| Period | Classification |
|---|---|
| *Ṛgveda* | Primordial father, ancestral sacrificer, seer, exemplar of ritual humanity |
| Brāhmaṇas | First or sole post-flood human, ritual progenitor, quasi-Prajāpati |
| Epics | Ancient king, ascetic, flood survivor, creator/progenitor |
| Dharmaśāstra | Archetypal sage and lawgiver; purported original speaker of dharma |
| Purāṇas | A recurring cosmic office: fourteen progenitor-kings, one per *manvantara* |
| Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas | Vaivasvata/Satyavrata as devotee rescued by Viṣṇu’s Matsya incarnation |
| Modern public culture | “First lawgiver,” symbol of Brahmanical law, or symbol of caste oppression, depending on the community invoking him |
| Theosophy | A superhuman racial/cosmic administrator—an explicitly modern occult reinvention |

No Greek or Roman source securely equates Manu with a classical deity through *interpretatio graeca* or *romana*. Modern comparisons with Noah, Deucalion, Utnapishtim, Xisuthros, Yima, and Mannus are comparisons, not ancient equations.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Ṛgvedic Manu

The *Ṛgveda* is preserved as an exceptionally controlled oral text, but its extant manuscripts are medieval or later. Dating pertains to composition and oral fixation, not to surviving physical copies.

**Securely attested early material:**

- *Ṛgveda* 1.31.17 places Manu among ancient ritual authorities.
- 1.36.19 says Manu established Agni as a light for the peoples.
- 1.63.7 recalls the first offering made by Manu with seven priests.
- 1.76.5 makes Manu’s sacrifice a prototype for the present rite.
- 1.80.16 associates “Father Manu,” Atharvan, and Dadhyañc with the extension of sacred insight.
- 2.33.13 asks Rudra to grant the well-being and lifetime Father Manu gained through sacrifice.
- 4.26.4 says the bird brought Soma to Manu.
- 4.37.1, 4.37.3 and related passages call worshippers the people of Manu and compare their Soma offering to his.
- 5.29.7 associates Manu’s Soma with Indra’s empowerment for combat against Vṛtra.
- 7.2.3 asks worshippers to invoke Agni as Manu did.
- 8.43.13 associates Manu with Bhṛgu and Aṅgiras.
- 8.52.1 recalls Indra’s drinking with Manu Vivasvat.
- 9.96.12 asks Soma to flow as it did for Manu.
- 10.63.7 speaks of Manu’s primordial sacrifice.
- 10.130.6 calls ancient ritual arrangers “sons of Manu.”

These passages show Manu’s priority in sacrifice and ancestry. They do **not** contain the flood, enumerate fourteen Manus, present a legal code, or describe a fixed anthropomorphic appearance.

Arthur A. Macdonell counted roughly twenty occurrences of *Manu* and almost as many of *Manus*, with Manu called a father five times. His 1897 synthesis remains useful as an index, though Jamison and Brereton’s modern translation provides better philological control.

### Other Vedic texts

- **Atharvaveda, later Vedic Saṃhitās, and Yajurvedic ritual texts:** Manu remains an ancestral authority and exemplar. Some ritual statements explain practices by saying “Manu established it,” making him the paradigmatic first performer.
- **Aitareya Brāhmaṇa:** The story of Manu’s division of property among his sons and the exclusion or delayed inheritance of Nābhānediṣṭha makes Manu a precedent for inheritance and ritual entitlement.
- **Taittirīya Saṃhitā and related prose:** Ritual acts are repeatedly traced to Manu as institutor; exact identities sometimes remain uncertain because *manu* approaches the meaning “human.”
- **Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā 11.66:** Later exegetical tradition reads Manu as father of humans and closely identifies him with Prajāpati.
- **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.8.1:** Earliest complete flood narrative; discussed below.
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya Upaniṣads:** Manu is not a principal speculative interlocutor comparable with Yājñavalkya or Uddālaka. Upaniṣadic genealogies and cosmology inherit the wider Vedic world but add no comparably important independent Manu cycle.

### Epic corpus

**Mahābhārata**, composed and redacted over centuries, broadly about 400 BCE–400 CE:

- *Vana Parvan*, Critical Edition 3.185, preserves the “Legend of the Fish.” Vaivasvata Manu protects a small fish; the fish grows, warns him, and orders him to gather seeds and board a ship. Manu, seven seers, and the fish alone remain visible during the deluge. At the Himalayan peak Naubandhana, the fish identifies itself as Brahmā and commissions Manu to recreate beings.
- The received Ganguli numbering is often 3.186; chapter numbering must therefore be stated with the edition.
- Elsewhere the epic invokes Manu as ancient king, progenitor, authority on kingship and dharma, and names descendants or dynasties after him.
- *Bhagavad Gītā* 4.1 says Kṛṣṇa taught the imperishable yoga to Vivasvat, Vivasvat taught it to Manu, and Manu taught Ikṣvāku. This is an important transmission genealogy, not a flood episode.

**Rāmāyaṇa**, developed approximately fifth century BCE–early centuries CE:

- The royal genealogies make Manu the ancestor of Ikṣvāku and hence of the Solar Dynasty culminating in Rāma.
- Manu functions chiefly as dynastic and normative antiquity, not as an active god.
- The critical translation begun by Robert Goldman is preferable for textual history; later devotional recensions enlarge the sacred genealogy but do not create a major Manu cult.

### Dharma corpus

The **Mānava-Dharmaśāstra**, commonly called *Manusmṛti* or “Laws of Manu,” is usually dated around the second century BCE to second or third century CE; Patrick Olivelle argues for a relatively unified authored/redacted work rather than a verbatim survival of a primordial lawbook.

Its narrative frame is crucial:

1. Great seers approach Manu and ask him to teach dharma.
2. Manu says Brahmā taught him.
3. Manu transmits the material to Bhṛgu.
4. Bhṛgu delivers most of the text to the assembled sages.

Thus even the extant work does not present itself simply as Manu personally writing 2,685 verses. “Manu” functions as primordial authority legitimating a human Dharmaśāstra composition.

The work covers creation, life-stage and class duties, marriage, inheritance, kingship, judicial procedure, penance, karma, and liberation. Its rules are internally layered and sometimes conflicting. Its unequal norms for varṇas and sexes—including graded punishment, restrictions on Śūdras, women’s legal dependence, and the protection of Brahmanical status—are genuine features of the transmitted text. Conversely, verses praising women or prescribing protection also exist. Quoting one group to erase the other is not sound textual history.

The manuscript tradition is plural. Pre-modern commentators include Medhātithi, Govindarāja, Nārāyaṇa, Rāghavānanda, Nandana, and Kullūka Bhaṭṭa. Kullūka’s *Manvarthamuktāvalī* became the basis of the early modern and colonial *textus receptus*, but it is not identical with every manuscript. Olivelle’s 2004/2005 critical edition collates significant variants and supersedes reliance on a single Kullūka manuscript.

### Purāṇic corpus

Purāṇas are layered, open textual traditions whose received forms commonly contain material spanning much of the first millennium CE and later. Assigning a single composition year is misleading.

- **Matsya Purāṇa 1–2:** Manu performs austerities; the fish warns him, orders preservation of seeds or living beings, and becomes the revealer of Purāṇic teaching. The fish is explicitly integrated with Viṣṇu/Matsya.
- **Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1 and 3.1–2:** Systematizes creation, the fourteen Manus, their gods, Indras, seven seers, sons, and the Vaiṣṇava power presiding in each age.
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.24 and book 9:** The Dravidian king Satyavrata becomes Vaivasvata Manu. Matsya directs him to board a divinely sent vessel with the seven sages, seeds, herbs, and living beings; Vāsuki serves as the rope; Matsya teaches him and recovers the Vedas from Hayagrīva.
- **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa:** Particularly important for the Sāvarṇika *manvantara*. The embedded *Devī Māhātmya* is narrated within a frame explaining how King Suratha will become Sāvarṇi Manu through the Goddess’s boon.
- **Liṅga, Śiva, Vāyu, Brahmāṇḍa, Brahma, Kūrma, Skanda, and other Purāṇas:** Give overlapping but non-identical lists of Manus, seers, gods, sons, and future cosmic administrations.
- **Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa:** Recasts the Manus and their attainments within a Goddess-centered cosmology.
- **Agni Purāṇa and Dharma-oriented compendia:** Cite Manu as a juridical and ritual authority more often than as a cult deity.

### Āgamas, Tantras, and Tamil literature

A dedicated search found no substantial evidence that Manu became an independently installed deity with a standard Āgamic image, *mūlamantra*, priesthood, or temple liturgy. Āgamic and Tantric sources may mention Manus in cosmological lists, lineage schemes, or the succession of ages, but Manu is not a major ritual deity comparable with Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī, Gaṇeśa, or the planetary gods.

The early Tamil Sangam corpus does not preserve an independent Manu myth-cycle of comparable importance. Tamil bhakti literature inherits Sanskritic cosmic and dynastic language, especially through Vaiṣṇava Matsya traditions, but supplies no demonstrable ancient Tamil Manu cult. Later Tamil political references to “Manu’s justice,” especially the Chola legend of Manu Nīti Chōḻaṉ—the king who executes his own son to give justice to a cow—concern an ideal of justice associated with Manu, not a securely attested episode in Manu’s own ancient mythology.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Early Vedic core:**

- Origin and continuity of the human community
- Establishment of sacrifice
- Proper ritual precedent
- Ancestral prosperity and longevity
- Transmission of fire and Soma rites

**Brāhmaṇic and epic development:**

- Survival through cosmic destruction
- Regeneration of creatures
- Kingship and ordered society
- Ancestral and dynastic legitimacy

**Dharmaśāstric development:**

- Dharma, social classification, penance, kingship, adjudication, family law
- Authority attributed to primordial antiquity

**Purāṇic development:**

- Government of a *manvantara*
- Production of human lineages
- Coordination of each age’s Indra, gods, seven seers, and royal descendants

Manu is therefore less a god of a bounded natural phenomenon than a figure through whom texts imagine the institution of human order.

### Powers and limitations

Manu sacrifices, performs austerities, receives revelation, preserves seed-life, fathers or generates lineages, and transmits teaching. He is not normally omnipotent. In the flood accounts he requires rescue by the fish; in the *Mahābhārata* he must perform asceticism before recreating beings; in Purāṇic systems each Manu holds a temporary cosmic office within Brahmā’s day.

### Iconography

No ancient canonical iconographic type of Manu is securely established. There is no equivalent of Viṣṇu’s conch-and-disc formula or Śiva’s trident and matted hair.

Later manuscript paintings commonly show:

- A bearded royal or ascetic Manu
- Joined hands before Matsya
- A boat containing sages, animals, or seed vessels
- Matsya with a horn, often anthropomorphic or crowned as Viṣṇu
- Vāsuki used as a rope in the *Bhāgavata* version

These images illustrate the Matsya narrative; they do not prove an autonomous Manu cult.

The best-known modern statue is the eleven-foot Manu holding the *Manusmṛti* on the Rajasthan High Court grounds in Jaipur, installed in 1989. Its lawbook-bearing appearance is modern public iconography, not an ancient prescription.

### Animals, plants, colors, planets, days

No ancient source securely assigns Manu a personal sacred animal, plant, color, weekday, or planet. The fish belongs to the rescuer, later Matsya-Viṣṇu, rather than functioning as Manu’s cult animal. The Sun connection derives from Vaivasvata’s genealogy. Claims circulating online that Manu has a fixed color, gemstone, planetary seal, or Tantric mantra should be classified as modern invention unless tied to a named primary ritual text.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Major genealogical systems

#### Vaivasvata Manu

The dominant later genealogy makes Vaivasvata:

- Son of Vivasvat/Sūrya and Saraṇyū/Saṃjñā
- Brother or half-brother of Yama, Yamī, the Aśvins, and Sāvarṇi, depending on source and handling of Saraṇyū and Chāyā
- Husband or ritual partner of Śraddhā in several later accounts
- Father of Ikṣvāku and numerous other dynastic founders
- Father of Ilā/Sudyumna, whose union with Budha produces Purūravas and connects solar and lunar dynasties

Lists of his sons vary. A common Purāṇic list includes Ikṣvāku, Nabhāga, Dhṛṣṭa, Śaryāti, Nariṣyanta, Nābhāganediṣṭha/Diṣṭa, Karūṣa, Pṛṣadhra, and Prāṃśu or Vasumat. Some traditions count Ilā as a daughter; others make Sudyumna a son whose sex changes.

#### Svāyambhuva Manu

Later cosmology makes the first Manu emerge from Brahmā or the self-existent creator. His consort is Śatarūpā. Children commonly include Priyavrata, Uttānapāda, Ākūti, Devahūti, and Prasūti. Through their descendants he becomes the ancestor of extensive Purāṇic creation genealogies.

This is not the genealogy of the Vedic flood survivor, although later summaries sometimes conflate “first Manu” with Vaivasvata.

### The fourteen Manus

A widely received Purāṇic sequence is:

1. Svāyambhuva  
2. Svārociṣa  
3. Uttama or Auttami  
4. Tāmasa  
5. Raivata  
6. Cākṣuṣa  
7. Vaivasvata  
8. Sāvarṇi  
9. Dakṣa-Sāvarṇi  
10. Brahma-Sāvarṇi  
11. Dharma-Sāvarṇi  
12. Rudra-Sāvarṇi  
13. Deva-Sāvarṇi or Raucya  
14. Indra-Sāvarṇi or Bhautya  

The final names, genealogies, Indras, and seven seers vary among the *Viṣṇu*, *Mārkaṇḍeya*, *Vāyu*, *Brahmāṇḍa*, *Bhāgavata*, and other Purāṇas. These differences are evidence of multiple priestly systematizations, not copying errors to be harmonized away.

One *manvantara* conventionally contains seventy-one *mahāyugas*. Fourteen manvantaras, with transitional intervals, occupy a day of Brahmā. This arithmetic is later cosmological systematization; it is absent from the earliest Ṛgvedic Manu passages.

### Documented divine interactions

The following list separates actual narrated or stated interactions from mere pantheon membership.

- **Agni — Manu establishes or kindles Agni as humanity’s ritual light.** *Ṛgveda* 1.36.19, 1.44.11, 7.2.3; Vedic period. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Agni — Agni dwells among Manu’s descendants and performs the sacrifice modeled by Manu.** *Ṛgveda* 1.68.4, 1.76.5. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Mātariśvan — gives or conveys Agni to Manu.** *Ṛgveda* 1.128.2 and 10.46.9 in Macdonell’s indexing. **Tier: securely attested early; exact interpretation debated.**
- **Indra — drinks pressed Soma with Manu Vivasvat/Manu Sāṃvaraṇi.** *Ṛgveda* 8.52.1 and Vālakhilya hymns. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Indra — drinks Manu’s Soma in preparation for the Vṛtra battle.** *Ṛgveda* 5.29.7. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Soma — flows for Manu or is offered by him.** *Ṛgveda* 4.37.3, 9.96.12. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Śyena, the divine/fabulous bird — brings Soma to Manu.** *Ṛgveda* 4.26.4. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Rudra — is asked to guide present worshippers to the good fortune Father Manu obtained.** *Ṛgveda* 2.33.13. **Tier: securely attested early; invocation rather than narrative meeting.**
- **Ādityas — are asked to grant the safety associated with Manu’s first sacrifice.** *Ṛgveda* 10.63.7. **Tier: securely attested early.**
- **Mitra and Varuṇa — meet Idā, ask her to say that she belongs to them, and seek a share in her.** *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.8.1.7–8. Their interaction is with Manu’s generated daughter and affects Manu’s regenerative sacrifice. **Tier: later Vedic canonical elaboration.**
- **The unnamed Fish — asks Manu for protection, predicts the flood, and saves him.** *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.8.1.1–6. The text does not call the fish Viṣṇu. **Tier: earliest attested flood version.**
- **Idā/Ilā — generated from Manu’s water-offering; identifies herself as his daughter and enables him to generate “the race of Manu.”** *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.8.1.7–11. **Tier: later Vedic canonical elaboration.**
- **Brahmā — the rescuing fish reveals himself as Brahmā and commands Manu to recreate beings.** *Mahābhārata*, *Vana Parvan* 3.185 CE. **Tier: epic canonical variant.**
- **Viṣṇu/Matsya — saves and instructs Manu; in later versions restores the Vedas.** *Matsya Purāṇa* 1–2; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.24. **Tier: later Vaiṣṇava canonical elaboration.**
- **Vāsuki — serves as the rope fastening Manu’s boat to Matsya’s horn.** *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.24.36. **Tier: later Vaiṣṇava elaboration.**
- **Hayagrīva — does not directly combat Manu; Matsya kills the Veda-stealing being while securing the new age over which Manu will preside.** *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.24. **Tier: later Vaiṣṇava elaboration; indirect relation only.**
- **Vivasvat/Sūrya — fathers Vaivasvata Manu and transmits Kṛṣṇa’s yoga to him.** *Bhagavad Gītā* 4.1; Purāṇic genealogies. **Tier: epic canonical and later genealogical elaboration.**
- **Kṛṣṇa — teaches Vivasvat, whose teaching descends through Manu; no direct Kṛṣṇa–Manu meeting is narrated in 4.1.** *Bhagavad Gītā* 4.1. **Tier: epic canonical; indirect interaction.**
- **Brahmā — creates or emanates Svāyambhuva Manu and teaches primordial dharma to Manu in the *Manusmṛti* frame.** *Mānava-Dharmaśāstra* 1; Purāṇic creation accounts. **Tier: learned systematization.**
- **The Goddess/Devī — grants King Suratha the future status of Sāvarṇi Manu.** *Devī Māhātmya*, *Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 81–93, particularly the conclusion. **Tier: later Śākta canonical elaboration.**
- **Budha — unites with Manu’s child Ilā/Sudyumna and fathers Purūravas.** Epic-Purāṇic genealogies. **Tier: later canonical genealogy; the interaction is chiefly Budha–Ilā, not Budha–Manu.**
- **Yama — later genealogies make him Manu’s brother.** Vedic and Purāṇic solar genealogies. No early surviving text narrates a substantial meeting or combat between them. **Tier: genealogical relation; absence of narrated interaction.**
- **Aśvins — sometimes genealogical brothers through Saraṇyū.** No securely attested Manu–Aśvin episode was found. **Tier: later genealogical relation; absence of interaction.**
- **Śiva/Rudra as father or patron of a future Rudra-Sāvarṇi Manu:** Purāṇic lists vary. **Tier: sectarian Purāṇic systematization, not a single stable narrative.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Manu as first sacrificer

**Earliest layer:** The *Ṛgveda* repeatedly treats Manu’s rite as the prototype of present sacrifice. He kindles Agni, offers with seven priests, obtains well-being, and establishes patterns later humans repeat *manuṣvat*, “as Manu did.”

The narrative is fragmentary because hymns presuppose rather than tell the story. The core claim is not “Manu invented religion” in a modern sense, but that correct Vedic offering reproduces primordial precedent.

**Function:** Ritual legitimation. A present act is valid because it repeats Manu’s first human performance.

### The flood in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa

In *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.8.1:

1. While Manu washes, a small fish enters his hands.
2. It asks to be reared and promises to save him.
3. Manu protects it in progressively larger containers and finally the sea.
4. The fish predicts a flood and tells him to build a ship.
5. At the appointed time Manu boards; he ties the vessel to the fish’s horn.
6. The fish tows him to a northern mountain.
7. Manu secures the ship to a tree and descends as the waters subside.
8. Every creature has been swept away; Manu alone remains.
9. Desiring offspring, he pours clarified butter, sour milk, whey, and curds into the waters.
10. After a year a woman arises. She calls herself Manu’s daughter; Mitra and Varuṇa seek her allegiance.
11. She returns to Manu as Idā, the blessing of sacrifice.
12. Through sacrifice and austerity “with her,” Manu generates the race of Manu.

**What it does not say:** The fish is not named Matsya or identified as Viṣṇu; Manu does not carry paired animals; seven sages are absent; no demon steals the Vedas; no exact Mesopotamian-style ark dimensions are given.

The “with her” language has produced disagreement over whether the passage implies sexual generation, ritual co-performance, or deliberate ambiguity between woman and sacrificial *iḍā*. The text’s overriding concern is the ritual origin and efficacy of the Iḍā portion.

### Epic Legend of the Fish

The *Mahābhārata* expands the story:

- Manu is explicitly Vaivasvata, a powerful ascetic king.
- He transfers the growing fish from vessel to tank, Ganges, and ocean.
- The fish commands him to gather every kind of seed.
- The seven seers join him.
- The boat is tied to the fish’s horn.
- The landing place becomes Naubandhana, “Boat-binding.”
- The fish declares itself Brahmā.
- Manu recreates gods, Asuras, humans, and immobile and mobile beings through ascetic power.

This version moves from a ritual etiology toward cosmic preservation and recreation.

### Vaiṣṇava Matsya cycle

*Matsya Purāṇa* and *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* identify the fish with Viṣṇu. In the *Bhāgavata*:

- Satyavrata is king of Draviḍa.
- He protects the fish during ablutions.
- The fish reveals the coming dissolution.
- A divine boat arrives.
- The seven seers, herbs, seeds, and living beings embark.
- Their light comes from the sages.
- Vāsuki binds the boat to Matsya’s horn.
- Matsya instructs Satyavrata in supreme knowledge throughout Brahmā’s night.
- Matsya kills Hayagrīva and recovers the Vedas.
- Satyavrata becomes Vaivasvata Manu of the new age.

The identification Satyavrata = Vaivasvata and the Veda-recovery episode are later canonical elaborations, not recoverable from *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*.

### Ilā/Sudyumna cycle

Traditions about Manu’s child diverge sharply:

- *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*: Idā arises from Manu’s offering and is his daughter and ritual blessing.
- Later genealogies: Ilā is Manu’s child and mother of Purūravas by Budha.
- Other Purāṇic accounts: Manu performs a rite for a son; through a ritual error a daughter, Ilā, is born.
- In the Sudyumna cycle, Ilā becomes male, later alternates sex, or changes upon entering Śiva and Pārvatī’s forbidden grove.

These are related but not identical narrative constructions. Later genealogists use Ilā to join the solar and lunar dynasties.

### The fourteen-Manu cycle

The office of Manu becomes cyclical. Each age has:

- A Manu
- An Indra
- Classes of gods
- Seven seers
- Sons or royal founders
- A form or intervention of Viṣṇu in Vaiṣṇava accounts

The system solves a cosmological problem: humanity can repeatedly end and begin while dharma and divine administration continue. It also absorbs disparate figures called Manu into a numbered sequence.

### Manu as lawgiver

The *Manusmṛti* frames Manu as recipient of Brahmā’s total knowledge and transmitter of dharma. The work’s subject is normative order rather than events in Manu’s life. Its attribution belongs to the widespread ancient practice of attaching teachings to primordial sages.

No evidence establishes that one historical ruler named Manu personally wrote the extant text. Conversely, calling the entire work a nineteenth-century fabrication is also false: quotations, commentaries, manuscripts, and pre-colonial legal-literary reception long predate European translation.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Independent cult

No securely documented ancient temple dedicated principally to Manu, standard Manu priesthood, annual pan-Indian Manu festival, oracle, pilgrimage, or sacrificial economy has been identified.

This absence is significant. Manu is religiously consequential through:

- Ritual precedent
- Recitation and citation of texts
- Cosmic genealogy
- Matsya iconography
- Legal and political symbolism

He is not generally a focus of *pūjā* in the way Viṣṇu, Śiva, Devī, or local guardian deities are.

### Ritual presence

Manu’s effective “cultic” presence is indirect:

- Vedic rites are performed “as Manu did.”
- Dharma authors cite “Manu says.”
- Ancestor and creation narratives locate humans as Manu’s descendants.
- Matsya festivals, stories, and images include Manu as the rescued devotee.
- Purāṇic recitation reproduces the succession of Manus and manvantaras.

### Temples and material remains

No archaeologically established “Temple of Manu” from the Vedic or early historic period can be confidently assigned to this figure. Modern sites bearing Manu’s name, including local shrines promoted as “Manu temples,” require individual epigraphic and architectural investigation; present dedication does not prove ancient continuity.

The modern Rajasthan High Court statue is securely documented material reception. Installed in 1989 at Jaipur, it depicts Manu holding the lawbook. A full-court resolution called for removal, a legal challenge stayed that action, and protests by Dalit and anti-caste groups have continued. In 2023 the Supreme Court declined to entertain a removal petition because related proceedings were before the High Court.

### Sacrifice, sexual institutions, and violence

- **Human sacrifice:** No primary source reviewed here documents a historical cult in which humans were sacrificed to Manu. Comparative theories about primordial sacrifice must not be converted into evidence of such a cult.
- **Animal sacrifice:** The *Manusmṛti* accepts Vedic animal sacrifice in prescribed contexts while also praising abstention from meat. This tension belongs to Brahmanical dietary and ritual debate, not to offerings made to Manu as a deity.
- **Temple prostitution:** No evidence connects Manu worship with an institution accurately described by that term. The category itself is contested in South Asian historiography.
- **Legal violence:** The *Manusmṛti* prescribes corporal and capital punishments and makes status-sensitive distinctions. These are textual prescriptions; whether and where they were enforced must be demonstrated from independent legal or epigraphic evidence.
- **Suppression:** There is no evidence of an ancient Manu cult forcibly suppressed as an institution. Modern rejection has principally targeted the *Manusmṛti* as an ideological text.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Approximately 1500–1000 BCE: ancestral ritual figure

The *Ṛgveda* knows Manu as father and first sacrificer. The figure’s authority is ritual and genealogical. No flood narrative or fourteen-Manu system appears.

### Approximately 1000–600 BCE: ritual prose and flood

Brāhmaṇa literature turns allusive memory into explanatory narrative. *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.8.1 joins the fish rescue to the origin of Iḍā and post-flood humanity. Other Brāhmaṇas make Manu a precedent for inheritance and ritual organization.

### Approximately 400 BCE–400 CE: epic and juridical expansion

The *Mahābhārata* makes Vaivasvata Manu an ascetic king, flood survivor, and re-creator, with Brahmā as fish. Solar dynasties claim descent through him. The *Mānava-Dharmaśāstra* makes Manu the primordial authority behind an ambitious Brahmanical account of social and political order.

### First millennium CE: Purāṇic systematization

The Manus become fourteen office-holders. Vaivasvata is fixed as seventh and present. Vaiṣṇava texts identify the fish with Matsya-Viṣṇu; Śākta texts explain the future Sāvarṇi Manu through Devī’s boon. Competing lists remain.

### Medieval period: commentary and regionalization

Dharma scholars write extensive commentaries on “Manu.” The lawgiver’s textual authority becomes more prominent than the flood survivor in juridical discourse. Regional literatures adapt Manu as a type of primordial king or justice, while Matsya art illustrates the rescue.

No evidence shows that this prominence produced a major temple-centered cult.

### 1772–1794: colonial juridical elevation

Warren Hastings’s 1772 judicial plan sought to administer “Hindu law” through Sanskrit learned traditions. William Jones’s 1794 English translation, *Institutes of Hindu Law: or, the Ordinances of Menu*, elevated the *Manusmṛti* within colonial knowledge.

This did not invent the text, but it transformed its function. A scholastic Dharmaśāstra, mediated by selected paṇḍits and Kullūka’s recension, was treated more like a positive legal code than it had uniformly been in precolonial practice. Custom, rival Smṛtis, commentaries, and regional law were thereby compressed.

### Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries: reform, nationalism, and occultism

Orientalists compared Manu with Moses, Noah, Minos, Menes, and other culture-founders. Some Indian reformers defended an allegedly original rational Manu while attributing objectionable verses to interpolation. Critics treated the work as a central document of caste and patriarchal power.

Theosophy radically reinterpreted Vaivasvata Manu as an adept supervising “root races,” Atlantis, planetary rounds, and human evolution. Helena Blavatsky’s *Secret Doctrine* and Annie Besant and C. W. Leadbeater’s *Man: Whence, How and Whither* are the key sources. These claims are modern occult inventions, not secret meanings demonstrable in Vedic or Purāṇic manuscripts.

### 25 December 1927: burning of the Manusmṛti

At the Mahad conference, held in the context of B. R. Ambedkar’s struggle against untouchability and for equal civil access, a copy of the *Manusmṛti* was publicly burned. The action symbolically rejected the Brahmanical social order associated with the text. Accounts differ over whose hands physically ignited it, but Ambedkar’s leadership, approval, and the event’s deliberate symbolism are securely documented. The date is now commemorated by many Ambedkarite groups as *Manusmṛti Dahan Din*.

This was not the destruction of a unique manuscript and did not suppress textual study; it was political ritual directed against the work’s authority.

### 1947–present: constitutional and political symbol

Independent India’s Constitution rejects legal disability based on untouchability and guarantees equality before the law. The *Manusmṛti* has no status as India’s constitution. It remains studied as Sanskrit literature and legal history, invoked by some Hindu traditionalists, and rejected by anti-caste and feminist movements.

The 1989 Rajasthan High Court statue crystallized the conflict. Supporters call Manu the world’s first lawgiver; opponents regard the statue at a constitutional court as honoring caste and gender hierarchy. The dispute concerns modern public memory, not survival of an ancient Manu cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Comparative mythology

Manu has repeatedly been compared with:

- Noah
- Utnapishtim/Atraḥasīs
- Greek Deucalion
- Iranian Yima
- Germanic Mannus

Structural similarity alone does not establish borrowing. The Indian flood’s earliest preserved form differs markedly from Genesis and Mesopotamian accounts: a fish warns Manu; ritual reciprocity is central; the post-flood woman is generated from offering; and no moral judgment on sinful humanity is stated.

Direct borrowing from Mesopotamia, common Indo-European inheritance, independent development, and later literary influence have all been proposed. The available texts do not permit a single secure transmission history.

### Theosophy and racial occultism

Blavatsky transformed Purāṇic Manus into “Root” and “Seed Manus” overseeing planetary rounds. Vaivasvata becomes progenitor or director of a fifth “Aryan root race,” sometimes linked with escape from Atlantis. Besant and Leadbeater expanded this through purported clairvoyant history.

**Classification: modern invention.** The terminology selectively borrows Sanskrit names, but the planetary-chain structure, Atlantean chronology, Venusian adepts, and racial hierarchy do not occur in the ancient Manu corpus. Because “Aryan” becomes racialized, this reception must be distinguished from Vedic *ārya*, a culturally and ethically marked self-designation rather than the nineteenth-century biological race category.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

No numerically significant, well-documented contemporary reconstructionist religion centered on worship of Manu was found. Modern Hindu communities continue to narrate Matsya and recognize the current Vaivasvata *manvantara*, but that is not the same as a separate Manu cult.

Internet rituals, invocations, “Manu mantras,” sigils, or claims that Manu is an Atlantean ascended master require source-by-source examination. Where they derive from Theosophy or recent occult sites rather than Sanskrit ritual manuals, they are modern inventions.

### Literature, art, and popular media

Manu appears chiefly in:

- Illustrated Matsya manuscripts and calendar art
- Retellings of the flood in children’s mythology and television
- Discussions of ancient law
- Political cartoons and protest art surrounding caste
- Comparative “world flood” anthologies

Modern retellings often import Noah-like cargo—families or animal pairs—into the story. Those details should not be projected backward into *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*.

## 9. Historiography and contested questions

### Is Manu a god or a man?

The answer changes by corpus. The *Ṛgveda* treats him as primordial father and ritual hero, sometimes close to the divine sphere. Later cosmology grants Manus superhuman longevity and creative function. Yet Manu generally remains a progenitor-king or sage under higher gods rather than an autonomous supreme deity.

### One Manu or many?

The Vedic evidence does not present the finished fourteen-Manu system. Later theologians preserve multiple old names by turning *Manu* into a recurring office. “Manu” can consequently mean:

- Primordial humanity
- A particular ancestral seer
- Vaivasvata, the flood survivor
- Svāyambhuva, first of the cosmic series
- Any ruler of a manvantara
- The literary speaker of the Dharmaśāstra

### Etymology and Indo-European origin

The √*man* connection is philologically strong. A shared Indo-European ancestral figure is plausible from Indic Manu and Germanic Mannus, but detailed reconstructions of a first priest sacrificing a twin are models produced through comparison. They are not direct attestations.

### Iranian parallels

The strongest Iranian evidence is onomastic, not a surviving Iranian Manu flood story.

Avestan *Manuš.čiθra* in *Yašt* 13.131 means “of the lineage of Manuš/*Manu” and designates a high-ranking priestly figure or lineage. Middle and New Persian Manūčihr/Manūchehr continues the compound as a personal and royal name. Encyclopaedia Iranica notes the interpretation “descended from the mythical first priest.”

Iranian Yima shares features with Vedic Yama, not straightforwardly with Manu: Ahura Mazdā warns Yima of a deadly winter and commands construction of a protected enclosure, the *vara*. Comparative scholars have proposed that the Iranian winter catastrophe transformed an older flood pattern, but this is disputed reconstruction. It is inaccurate simply to call Yima “the Persian Manu.”

### Did the Manusmṛti govern all Hindus?

No. It was influential and widely cited, but Dharmaśāstra was a plural learned tradition interacting with royal commands, guild and caste norms, local custom, regional commentaries, and other Smṛtis. Colonial governance gave selected Sanskrit texts a novel quasi-code status. Neither “Manu was irrelevant before the British” nor “Manu was India’s universally enforced constitution” fits the evidence.

### Is the received text wholly interpolated?

Manuscripts contain significant variants, and redaction is real. But claims that every objectionable verse is a recent interpolation usually proceed selectively and require manuscript evidence verse by verse. Olivelle’s critical edition finds a reconstructable work with variants; it does not vindicate the theory that the caste and gender hierarchy was inserted wholesale by British translators.

### Flood historicity

No archaeological event has been securely identified as “Manu’s flood.” Geological floods, river changes, and common human experience can inform myth, but correlating a particular catastrophe with *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* exceeds the evidence. The historical facts are the composition, transmission, and reinterpretation of the flood story.

### Contested-material audit

- **Etymology:** √*man* is strong; elaborate “mind-created master race” explanations are modern.
- **Origin:** Vedic ancestral sacrificer is secure; Proto-Indo-European narrative reconstruction remains comparative.
- **Syncretism:** Fish = Brahmā in the *Mahābhārata* and fish = Viṣṇu/Matsya in later Purāṇas are datable internal identifications.
- **Human sacrifice:** No Manu cult evidence.
- **Temple prostitution:** No Manu connection established.
- **Suppression:** No ancient institutional Manu cult is known to have been suppressed; modern symbolic rejection targets the law text.
- **Occultism:** Root-Manu and Atlantean racial administrator derive from nineteenth- and twentieth-century Theosophy.
- **Neopaganism:** No established ancient-continuity Manu reconstructionist movement was identified.
- **Political appropriation:** Manu is invoked both as primordial jurist and as emblem of caste oppression.
- **Extremism:** Racialized “Aryan root-race” teaching belongs to modern esotericism. It must not be attributed to Vedic authors.
- **Modern fabricated antiquity:** Fixed Manu yantras, planets, gemstones, “lost temples,” and literal multimillion-year historical dates are unestablished unless supported by named primary sources. Purāṇic cosmic numbers are textual cosmology, not independently verified chronology.

### State of the evidence

The primary evidence is overwhelmingly textual. Vedic works survive through oral transmission and late manuscripts; epic and Purāṇic works survive in divergent regional manuscript recensions. The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute’s *Mahābhārata* Critical Edition reconstructs the earliest recoverable common text rather than reproducing a single ancient manuscript. GRETIL and the Sanskrit Library offer searchable electronic texts, while reliable interpretation still requires printed critical apparatuses.

For the *Manusmṛti*, Olivelle’s critical edition is the essential modern basis. Early translations by Jones, Bühler, and others remain historiographically important but should not be mistaken for the critical Sanskrit text.
