# Vithoba: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male Hindu deity; in the dominant Vārkarī and temple traditions, a regional manifestation of Kṛṣṇa-Viṣṇu, though historical scholarship also investigates pastoral, heroic, Śaiva, Jain, and Buddhist components in the formation of his cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** An inscription at Pandharpur dated Śaka 1111/1189 CE, conventionally read as recording the establishment of a small shrine to Viṭṭhala. The first extensive, securely dated evidence is the Sanskrit–Kannada inscription of Śaka 1159/1237 CE in the temple, naming Viṭṭhala, Pandharpur as *Paṇḍarage*, a Hoysala royal donor, worship and food offerings, and Puṇḍarīka. Claims for a Vedic, sixth-century, or otherwise ancient Vithoba are theories or retrospective identifications, not secure attestations of this named god. The Maharashtra Gazetteer summarizes the 1189 and 1237 evidence, although individual readings and historical conclusions remain debated. [Maharashtra Gazetteer](https://gazetteers.maharashtra.gov.in/cultural.maharashtra.gov.in/english/gazetteer/Solapur/places_Pandharpur.html)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Vithoba is the dark stone god of Pandharpur in Maharashtra, standing erect upon a brick with his hands planted on his hips. To Vārkarīs he is simultaneously Kṛṣṇa, Viṣṇu, Hari, the supreme *brahman*, an intimate companion, a child or mother, and the lord who made himself accessible to devotees. His principal consort is Rukmini under the regional names Rakhumāī or Rakhumābāī. The central image and Rukmini’s image occupy separate shrines, a material arrangement explained by several incompatible legends.

Principal documented names include:

- **Viṭṭhala / Vitthal** — Marathi विठ्ठल; Kannada ವಿಠ್ಠಲ; Telugu విఠ్ఠల. This is the principal inscriptional and liturgical name.
- **Viṭhobā / Vithoba** — Marathi विठोबा. The ending *-bā* is an affectionate or honorific Marathi formation, comparable to “father” or “dear lord.”
- **Viṭhū, Viṭhūrāyā, Viṭhāī** — affectionate Marathi forms. *Viṭhāī*, grammatically maternal, is especially important in sant poetry, where the male god may be addressed as mother.
- **Pāṇḍuraṅga / Pandurang** — Marathi पांडुरंग; Sanskrit पाण्डुरङ्ग. Literally it could mean “white/pale-colored,” paradoxical for the black image. A better historical explanation may connect it retrospectively to the place-name Paṇḍarage/Pandharpur. Hemacandra’s twelfth-century *Deśīnāmamālā* uses *Pāṇḍuraṅga* as a name of Rudra, supporting—but not proving—a Śaiva stratum.
- **Pāṇḍharīnātha, Pāṇḍharīrāyā, Pāṇḍharīcā rāṇā** — lord or king of Pandhari, the devotional name of Pandharpur.
- **Hari, Nārāyaṇa, Keśava, Mādhava, Govinda, Gopāla, Kṛṣṇa** — Vaiṣṇava identifications common in Marathi sant poetry.
- **Kānaḍā Viṭṭhala** — “Kannada/Karnataka Vitthal,” an epithet in the Marathi tradition that preserves memory of southern associations but cannot by itself prove a Karnataka origin.
- **Bāuddha / Buddha** — some medieval Marathi compositions call the silent, standing deity Buddha or equate him with Viṣṇu’s ninth avatāra. This is a sectarian identification, not evidence that the Pandharpur image began as a historical Buddha image.
- **Viṭṭhaleśvara** — a name found at several southern sites. Because *īśvara* names frequently designate Śiva, scholars disagree whether such monuments demonstrate Viṭṭhala worship, Śaiva appropriation, or merely unrelated names.

Proposed etymologies remain unresolved:

1. **Kannada derivation:** From *viṭṭu* or related Kannada forms, sometimes explained as “standing” or connected to a local title. No consensus linguistic derivation has been demonstrated.
2. **Viṣṇu derivation:** Viṭṭhala as a vernacular transformation of *Viṣṇu*. Phonologically possible intermediate explanations have been proposed but are not securely documented.
3. **Royal-name theory:** M. S. Mate associated the name with Hoysala Biṭṭideva/Viṣṇuvardhana. This is a modern reconstruction without direct inscriptional confirmation.
4. **Tukaram’s devotional etymology:** *vittha* “ignorant” plus *la* “one who accepts”: the god who accepts the ignorant. This is a seventeenth-century theological wordplay, not historical linguistics.
5. **Brick etymology:** Popular accounts derive the name from Marathi *vīṭ*, “brick.” It elegantly matches the Pundalik legend but is linguistically secondary and unattested in the earliest inscription.

R. C. Dhere’s influential synthesis treats Vitthal as a pastoral or folk god progressively Vaiṣṇavized, Sanskritized, and embraced by Brahmanical and vernacular traditions. It remains a historical model, not an established genealogy. [Dhere, *Rise of a Folk God*](https://academic.oup.com/book/27754)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Evidence before 1189

**Absence of evidence:** Vithoba, Viṭṭhala, Pundalik of Pandharpur, the brick episode, and Pandharpur do not occur as such in the Ṛgveda, Atharvaveda, Brāhmaṇas, principal early Upaniṣads, Mahābhārata, Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, early Bhagavad Gītā, or the securely ancient strata of the Purāṇas. Those works are relevant because later worship identifies Vithoba with their Viṣṇu and Kṛṣṇa, but they are not primary attestations of Vithoba.

The frequently cited *Pāṇḍuraṅgāṣṭaka*, attributed to Śaṅkara, is textually undated. Attribution to the eighth-century philosopher cannot be treated as secure proof that the Pandharpur cult existed then.

### Epigraphic sequence

- **1189 CE — securely attested but interpretively disputed:** A Pandharpur inscription, found and read by S. G. Tulpule, records a small shrine associated with Viṭṭhala. Some descriptions overstate it as proof that worship “began” in 1189; it proves a shrine by that date, not the cult’s precise origin.
- **1237 CE — securely attested:** A bilingual Sanskrit–Kannada inscription of Hoysala Vīra Someśvara refers to *Paṇḍarage*, the renowned god Viṭṭhala, provision for *aṅga-raṅga-bhoga* or ritual service and food offerings, and Puṇḍarīkamuni. This is the earliest firm conjunction of deity, place, institutional cult, and Pundalik.
- **1249 CE — securely attested:** A Yadava copper plate refers to Pauṇḍarīkakṣetra on the Bhīmarathī and invokes Viṣṇu. Its relationship to the developed Vithoba cult is probable but the plate does not narrate the later Pundalik story.
- **1273–1277 CE — securely attested:** The “Stone of Eighty-Four” and related inscriptions record donations and temple construction. They show broad patronage and an institutionalized pilgrimage economy.
- **Late thirteenth century:** Royal, ministerial, and sant associations place Pandharpur within the religious geography of the Yadava realm.

### Sanskrit *māhātmyas*

The chief Sanskrit narrative corpus consists of locally transmitted *Pāṇḍuraṅga-māhātmyas* claiming affiliation with the Skanda, Padma, and Viṣṇu Purāṇas, plus a *Bhīmā-māhātmya*. Their attribution to famous Purāṇas is a literary framing device; these are regional, fluid, comparatively late textual strata, not necessarily passages belonging to the earliest recensions of those Purāṇas.

The Skanda-attributed versions describe Pandharpur’s sacred geography, Puṇḍarīka, the Bhīmā/Candrabhāgā, and the appearance of Kṛṣṇa. Variants imply that more than one local sacred image or shrine was drawn into the developing complex. Their manuscripts and recensions require individual dating; “the Skanda Purāṇa says” is therefore insufficiently precise.

### Marathi corpus

- **Jñāneśvarī/Bhāvārthadīpikā, 1290 CE:** Jñāneśvar’s Marathi commentary on the Bhagavad Gītā. Its colophon supplies the date. It is not chiefly a Vithoba narrative, and its silence or sparseness concerning the named god cautions against making Jñāneśvar the cult’s sole founder.
- **Jñāneśvar’s attributed abhangas:** Vitthal is Kṛṣṇa and cosmic brahman, but the corpus is textually less secure than the *Jñāneśvarī*.
- **Namdev corpus, late thirteenth–fourteenth centuries in traditional chronology:** Abhangas stage unusually intimate exchanges with Vithoba. The corpus is composite: poems circulated in Marathi, Hindi, Punjabi, and Rajasthani traditions; sixty-one hymns under Namdev’s name entered the *Ādi Granth* in 1604, while surviving Marathi collections are substantially later. Christian Lee Novetzke calls later composition under Namdev’s name “anamnetic authorship.”
- **Janabai:** About 340 abhangas are attributed to her in the Namdev tradition. Vithoba helps grind grain, wash clothes, and perform other domestic labor. Attribution to one historical author cannot always be guaranteed because the collections are posthumous and variable. [New Historia on Janabai](https://thenewhistoria.org/schema/janabai/)
- **Chokhamela and family:** Abhangas address Vithoba across the barrier of untouchability and accuse society—and sometimes the god—of tolerating exclusion. Later tradition locates Chokhamela’s memorial at the temple gate.
- **Eknath, sixteenth century:** Abhangas and bhāruḍs consolidate Vārkarī theology, Kṛṣṇa-Vithoba devotion, public performance, and social criticism.
- **Tukaram, c. 1608–1649/50:** Thousands of attributed abhangas speak to Vitthal as lord, parent, friend, creditor, and sometimes an exasperatingly absent protector. The received *Tukaram Gāthā* contains later textual growth; not every poem bearing the signature *Tukā mhaṇe* can automatically be authenticated.
- **Bahinabai, seventeenth century:** The *Puṇḍalīka-māhātmya* supplies an important full version of Pundalik’s filial-piety legend.
- **Śrīdhar Svāmī Nāzarekar, c. 1658–1729:** His Marathi *Pāṇḍuraṅga-māhātmya* gave the legends an accessible and influential narrative form. J. P. Raeside dated Śrīdhar’s principal literary activity approximately 1690–1720. [Raeside, “The Pāṇḍuraṅga-māhātmya of Śrīdhar”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/abs/panurangamahatmya-of-sridhar/CB08C59D998572D1A9CC44D03C3DE52F)
- **Mahipati, eighteenth century:** *Bhaktavijaya* and *Bhaktalīlāmṛta* organize the saints’ miracle traditions into a connected sacred history. They are indispensable evidence for eighteenth-century memory, but are not contemporary biographies of thirteenth-century saints.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

Vithoba’s primary historical domain is not a Vedic natural force but devotional accessibility: pilgrimage, salvation through loving remembrance, protection of devotees, and the sanctification of ordinary labor.

The Pandharpur image is a dark, approximately human-sized stone figure:

- standing frontally on a brick;
- feet together;
- arms akimbo, hands at the waist;
- high, cylindrical or conical headgear;
- prominent earrings;
- necklaces and other ornaments;
- conventionally identified miniature lotus and conch attributes near the hands, though the worn sculpture and ritual coverings complicate inspection;
- a chest mark interpreted in learned tradition as the *śrīvatsa* or a mantra;
- a head form interpreted variously as a crown, a Śiva-liṅga, or an ordinary headdress.

The image’s date is unresolved. Stylistic comparisons have produced proposals from the sixth century onward, while the secure architectural and epigraphic setting begins in the late twelfth century. The sculpture may predate the surviving temple, but claims of a specific early century exceed the evidence.

The hands-on-hips pose has been compared with:

- pastoral Kṛṣṇa figures;
- Deccan and Karnataka hero stones;
- the Bihar cattle god Bīr Kuār;
- Vīrabhadra;
- southern Viṭṭhala images.

These comparisons support hypotheses of a pastoral/heroic substrate but do not establish direct descent.

The **brick** is now indispensable iconographically, yet it derives its meaning from the Pundalik cycle. Its Marathi name, *vīṭ*, encouraged popular etymological connection with Vitthal.

Rukmini/Rakhumai’s separate shrine is defining. Learned Vaiṣṇava interpretation identifies her with Lakṣmī; local legends explain the separation through anger, jealousy, or her withdrawal to Dindi/Dindiravana.

No ancient source establishes a canonical sacred color, planet, weapon, occult seal, or number peculiar to Vithoba. Modern charts assigning these should be treated as recent systematization.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

Vithoba has no independent, securely early genealogy. Once identified with Kṛṣṇa, he inherits Kṛṣṇa’s Purāṇic genealogy—Vasudeva and Devakī as parents, Balarāma as brother, Rukmini and other wives, and Pradyumna among his sons. That is a **syncretistic incorporation**, not evidence that early Pandharpur traditions originally supplied those relations.

### Documented interactions with named divine beings

- **Rukmini/Rakhumai — consort, quarrel, separation, and reunion.** In regional *Pāṇḍuraṅga-māhātmya* and later Marathi legend, Rukmini withdraws in anger; Kṛṣṇa-Vithoba follows her toward Dindiravana/Pandharpur. **Tier:** medieval regional elaboration.
- **Kṛṣṇa — identity rather than meeting.** The dominant Vārkarī claim is that Vithoba is Kṛṣṇa standing for Pundalik. No independent episode has two separate beings meet. **Tier:** thirteenth-century onward Vaiṣṇava identification.
- **Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa — identity and theological elevation.** Sant poetry and Sanskrit māhātmyas identify Vithoba with supreme Viṣṇu. **Tier:** medieval learned systematization and devotional canon.
- **Lakṣmī — equation with Rukmini/Rakhumai.** **Tier:** Vaiṣṇava theological systematization; no separate early Pandharpur encounter is securely attested.
- **Śiva — coexistence and disputed assimilation.** Pandharpur’s sacred landscape includes Puṇḍarīkeśvara and other Śaiva shrines; the headgear has been read as a liṅga. Dhere argues that Śaiva material was absorbed during Vaiṣṇavization. No secure early narrative records Vithoba meeting Śiva. **Tier:** modern scholarly reconstruction from medieval cultic evidence.
- **Vīrabhadra — proposed affinity or transformation.** Dhere compares local Viṭṭhaleśvara/Vīrabhadra traditions. No canonical combat or meeting is documented. **Tier:** modern comparative reconstruction.
- **Buddha — avatāric equation.** Marathi poets and nineteenth-century observers report identification of the silent Vithoba with Buddha, Viṣṇu’s ninth avatāra. No Buddhist scripture contains a Vithoba–Buddha encounter. **Tier:** medieval/early-modern syncretistic identification.
- **Brahmā and other gods — attendance upon Vithoba or his devotees.** Later abhangas and saint hagiographies show divine assemblies witnessing kīrtan. **Tier:** devotional hagiography; individual poem attribution requires verification.
- **Garuda — servant or vehicle by inherited Vaiṣṇava identity.** Present in temple and liturgical symbolism; no distinctive early Vithoba–Garuda narrative is established. **Tier:** learned Vaiṣṇava systematization.
- **Hanuman — associated temple deity.** Joint presence in the temple complex, not a documented Vithoba-specific interaction. **Tier:** cultic association.
- **Gaṇeśa, Dattātreya, Narasiṃha, Sūrya, Kālabhairava, Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa, Rādhā and Satyabhāmā — subordinate or neighboring cult images in the Pandharpur complex.** Their presence documents an inclusive sacred complex, not narrative interaction with Vithoba. **Tier:** accumulated medieval and early-modern temple system.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Pundalik and the brick

**Early core:** The 1237 inscription associates Puṇḍarīkamuni with the cult. Early sant poetry says that the god came or stood at Pandharpur “for Pundalik’s sake.” It does not necessarily contain the now-standard filial-piety story.

**Later canonical narrative:** Pundalik initially neglects or mistreats his parents. Through an encounter with exemplary women, sages, or sacred rivers—variants differ—he realizes that serving his parents is the highest duty. Kṛṣṇa, sometimes accompanied by Rukmini, comes to see him. Pundalik refuses to interrupt his service and throws a brick outside, asking the visitor to stand on it. Pleased rather than insulted, the god waits with hands on hips. Pundalik asks him to remain permanently for all devotees; the god becomes Vithoba.

The full filial-piety form is securely visible only in seventeenth-century and later Marathi sources such as Bahinabai and Śrīdhar. It is therefore a **late-medieval canonical elaboration**, not a securely recoverable twelfth-century story.

Dhere and others connect Pundalik with **Puṇḍarīkeśvara**, a Śaiva shrine, proposing that a local Śaiva sacred figure or liṅga was transformed into the ideal Vaiṣṇava devotee Pundalik. This is a powerful explanatory hypothesis, not a documented conversion event.

### Rukmini’s anger and Dindiravana

In regional legend, Rukmini sees Kṛṣṇa with Rādhā or resents his intimacy with another woman and leaves Dvārakā. She settles at Dindiravana near Pandharpur. Kṛṣṇa follows, then remains for Pundalik. The cycle explains:

- why Vithoba is Kṛṣṇa;
- why he appears in Pandharpur rather than Dvārakā;
- why Rukmini has a separate shrine;
- why the landscape contains Dindiravana.

Dhere reads the story as an overlay upon older pastoral cults, including Dhangar traditions of Viṭṭhal and Padubāī. The Rādhā element is not uniform across versions.

### Vithoba and the saints

Hagiography repeatedly brings the god into ordinary life:

- For **Namdev**, Vithoba drinks milk offered by the child, speaks directly, accepts intimate complaint, and eventually sends him to a human guru.
- For **Janabai**, Vithoba grinds grain, sweeps, carries water, and washes clothes. The inversion makes the god servant of a low-status woman devotee.
- For **Chokhamela**, Vithoba validates devotion despite caste exclusion, though the continued exclusion in the stories exposes a conflict between devotional equality and temple society.
- For **Gora Kumbhar**, **Savata Mali**, **Sena Nhavi**, and other occupational saints, the god performs or sanctifies labor.
- For **Eknath**, later narratives emphasize hospitality and recognition of God in socially marginalized persons.
- For **Tukaram**, Vithoba restores abhang manuscripts thrown into the Indrayani and ultimately summons Tukaram bodily to Vaikuṇṭha. These are seventeenth- and eighteenth-century hagiographic traditions, not independently documented events.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Pandharpur

The principal temple stands on the Bhīmā, locally Candrabhāgā because of the river’s crescent bend. The existing complex is cumulative rather than the product of one foundation. The 1189, 1237, and 1270s inscriptions document successive patronage. The Namdev/Mahādvāra gate and later additions materialize the sant tradition.

Devotees traditionally enter through the Namdev gate, honor memorials associated with Namdev and Chokhamela, and receive *darśan* of Vithoba. A distinctive practice permits touching the deity’s feet during ordinary access, though crowd management can alter this. Daily services include *kākad āratī*, dressing, *mahāpūjā*, food offering, incense rite, foot worship, and *śejāratī*. [Solapur district administration](https://www.zpsolapur.gov.in/en/places-centres/pandharpur/)

### Vārkarī practice

*Vārkarī* means one who performs the recurring *vārī*, especially pilgrimage to Pandharpur. Core disciplines include:

- devotion to Vitthal;
- repetition of divine names, especially “Rām Kṛṣṇa Hari” and “Jñānbā Tukārām”;
- singing abhangas and performing *kīrtan*;
- pilgrimage on Āṣāḍhī and Kārtikī Ekādaśī;
- vegetarianism and avoidance of alcohol in normative codes;
- reverence for the sant lineage;
- carrying tulsi beads and sometimes a banner;
- group organization in *diṇḍīs*.

Pilgrimage is medieval, but the now-familiar **pālkhī system** carrying the saints’ sandals in regimented processions was substantially organized in the 1820s by Haibatravbaba Arphalkar and others. Claims that its complete modern form is unchanged from Jñāneśvar’s lifetime are not supported. A contemporary scholarly account describes the approximately 250-kilometer, twenty-one-day event involving hundreds of thousands. [Heidelberg, “Pandharpur Wari”](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/dasta/article/view/27346)

### Priesthood, caste, and government control

Hereditary Badve Brahmans historically controlled important ritual and economic privileges. The Maharashtra **Pandharpur Temples Act, 1973** abolished hereditary managerial, priestly, and offering-receipt rights and vested administration in a statutory committee. The Supreme Court upheld the relevant scheme in 2014 after litigation lasting decades. The Act is unusually detailed about hereditary service groups, revenues, jewels, daily worship, and pilgrims’ access. [Pandharpur Temples Act](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/117949677/) [Supreme Court judgment](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/62292138/?type=print)

In 2014 the committee began appointing women and non-Brahmans as priests. This was a modern institutional reform, not proof that historic temple access had always embodied the casteless ideals voiced in sant literature.

No reliable evidence associates Vithoba’s historical cult with human sacrifice or temple prostitution. The 1270 inscription’s reference to a *yajña* or sacrifice cannot, without further specification, be converted into evidence of human sacrifice.

## 7. Historical evolution

- **Before the twelfth century:** No secure named attestation. Pastoral, hero-stone, Śaiva, Kannada, and local-god antecedents are scholarly reconstructions.
- **1189–late thirteenth century:** Formation of the documented shrine, royal patronage, donations, and consolidation of Pundalik and Pandharpur’s sacred geography.
- **Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries:** Jñāneśvar, Namdev, Janabai, Chokhamela, and associated traditions turn Vitthal into the focus of Marathi vernacular devotion. The god is firmly identified with Kṛṣṇa-Viṣṇu.
- **Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries:** Expansion through abhang performance, Eknath, Tukaram, Bahinabai, and regional networks. Sanskrit māhātmyas and Marathi retellings systematize Pundalik, Rukmini, and pilgrimage myths.
- **Eighteenth century:** Mahipati’s hagiographies canonize a transhistorical fellowship of saints around Vithoba.
- **Maratha and Peshwa periods:** Patronage, pilgrimage, monasteries, and hereditary temple offices expand; records require institution-by-institution study.
- **Mughal conflict:** Reports that Vithoba’s image was removed or hidden and that Aurangzeb ordered action against the Pandharpur temple circulate in later documentary collections. The precise extent of destruction and continuity at the site remains less securely reconstructed than popular polemic suggests. It should not be stated that the cult was extinguished.
- **British period:** Colonial administrators regulated pilgrimage, revenue, sanitation, epidemics, and crowd movement. The organized pālkhī format developed during this period; plague policy in 1908–1916 treated pilgrimage as a public-health threat.
- **Twentieth century:** Print editions of sant corpora, devotional recording, mass political culture, reform movements, and cinema nationalized the regional image.
- **1973–2014:** Statutory takeover and litigation ended hereditary Badve control.
- **Present:** Vithoba remains one of Maharashtra’s most widely worshipped deities. The annual Wari, temple webcasts, mass media, state sponsorship, and diaspora temples extend the cult beyond its earlier geography. The official temple committee continues to administer worship and pilgrimage. [Official temple site](https://vitthalrukminimandir.org/)

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

The 1936 Prabhat film **Sant Tukaram**, directed by V. G. Damle and Sheikh Fattelal, gave cinematic form to Vithoba’s interventions in Tukaram’s life and was screened at the 1937 Venice festival. Its music and miracle imagery helped establish a mass-media template for Vārkarī devotion. [Bengaluru International Film Festival catalogue](https://filmfestival.biffes.org/frontend/images/widget/12th-Biffes-Festival-Catalogue-2020.pdf)

Vithoba also appears in Marathi devotional theatre, kīrtan, radio, gramophone recording, television saint serials, calendar art, Amar Chitra Katha-style narrative, and films in Marathi, Kannada, Telugu, and Hindi. Modern images often regularize the weathered sculpture into a youthful blue or black Kṛṣṇa, add a clearly visible conch and lotus, or place Rakhumai symmetrically beside him, even though the Pandharpur shrines are separate.

Political uses include presentation of the Wari as:

- a symbol of Marathi cultural unity;
- proof of an inherently egalitarian Hindu past;
- a model for social reform;
- a vehicle of state cultural policy;
- part of Hindu nationalist sacred geography.

The sant corpus genuinely criticizes caste pride, but the claim that historical Vārkarī institutions eliminated caste is contradicted by Chokhamela traditions, segregated access, hereditary priesthood, and modern temple-entry struggles.

No historically established Vithoba-specific tradition exists in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Wicca, or early modern European ceremonial magic. Internet claims assigning him occult correspondences or presenting an ancient global “Vitthal mystery school” are modern inventions unless accompanied by independent documentation. Contemporary Hindu worship outside India is continuation or adaptation, not usually “neopagan reconstruction.”

## 9. Historiography and contested questions

### Origin

- **G. A. Deleury:** emphasized the historical cult, inscriptions, Vārkarī organization, and a possible southern background. His *The Cult of Viṭhobā* remains foundational. [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Cult_of_Vi%E1%B9%ADhob%C4%81.html?id=iv4XAAAAIAAJ)
- **R. C. Dhere:** proposed a “great synthesis” in which a pastoral or heroic deity associated with cowherds, Dhangars, hero stones, Vīrabhadra, and regional cults became Kṛṣṇa-Viṣṇu while absorbing Śaiva, Jain, and Buddhist associations.
- **Günther-Dietz Sontheimer and Anne Feldhaus:** situated the god in Deccan pastoral religion and regional sacred geography.
- **Christian Lee Novetzke:** studies public memory, authorship, performance, and Namdev, warning against treating late hagiographic corpora as transparent medieval biography.
- **Richard Eaton:** places Pandharpur and Vārkarī devotion in the social history of the Deccan, including state formation, vernacularization, and religious networks. [Eaton, *A Social History of the Deccan*](https://the-deccan.in/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/cambridge_history_eaton_deccan_social_history.pdf)

### Śaiva hypothesis

Evidence cited includes the name Pāṇḍuraṅga for Rudra, surrounding Śiva shrines, Puṇḍarīkeśvara, a possible liṅga-like headdress, and Viṭṭhaleśvara place-names. Against a simple Śiva-to-Viṣṇu conversion stands the unequivocally Vaiṣṇava character of thirteenth-century evidence and the possibility that a multi-sectarian landscape accumulated around an already Vaiṣṇava deity. The hypothesis is plausible in complex form, unproved in a simple one.

### Buddhist hypothesis

Nineteenth-century missionary scholar John Stevenson called the devotees “Bauddho-Vaishnavas,” relying on Vithoba’s equation with the Buddha avatāra and perceived ethical resemblance. His article is important evidence for nineteenth-century classification, not proof of Buddhist institutional origin. [Stevenson, 1843](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/art-vian-account-of-the-bauddhovaishnavas-or-vitthalbhaktas-of-the-dakhan/73724FF6382B4E04265BF859ACF15D03)

Dhere treats Buddhist language as one component of synthesis. No Buddhist inscription, monastery record, canonical text, or securely Buddhist image identifies the Pandharpur figure as Vithoba. Therefore “Vithoba was originally Buddha” is unestablished.

### Jain connections

Jain evidence includes Hemacandra’s vocabulary and regional interactions, while some scholars perceive similarities between the standing image and Jina iconography. Yet Vithoba’s arms-akimbo posture is unlike standard Jina meditation, and no early Jain text identifies the Pandharpur deity. Jain origin remains speculative.

### Image displacement

Traditions say the image was removed to Vijayanagara or hidden at Māḍhe during military danger and later returned. Vādirājatīrtha’s pilgrimage literature and later regional traditions preserve versions, but inscriptions at Hampi do not demonstrate that its famous Vitthala image was transported from Pandharpur. The identity of the “original” image remains contested.

### Textual limitations

There is no single critical edition of the entire Vithoba corpus. The materials span inscriptions, unstable Sanskrit māhātmyas, orally transmitted abhangas, late manuscripts, sectarian collections, hagiographies, legal records, and living performance. A poet’s signature line is not sufficient proof of authorship. In particular, later Namdev, Janabai, Chokhamela, and Tukaram collections must be used composition by composition.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the evidentiary record |
|---|---|
| Vedic through early epic period | No secure Vithoba, Pandharpur, or brick-standing Pundalik attestation. |
| 1089–1172 | Hemacandra records *Pāṇḍuraṅga* as a Rudra name; relevant to etymological debate, not an attestation of Pandharpur’s Vithoba. |
| 1189 | Inscription records a small Viṭṭhala shrine at Pandharpur. |
| 1237 | Sanskrit–Kannada inscription names Viṭṭhala, Paṇḍarage, royal patronage, ritual provision, and Puṇḍarīka. |
| 1249 | Yadava copper plate names Pauṇḍarīkakṣetra on the Bhīmarathī in a Vaiṣṇava context. |
| 1273–1277 | “Stone of Eighty-Four” and related records document temple building and donations. |
| 1290 | Composition/colophon date of Jñāneśvar’s *Bhāvārthadīpikā*. |
| Late 13th–14th c. | Namdev, Janabai, Chokhamela, and other sant traditions establish Vithoba as the intimate god of Marathi bhakti. |
| 16th c. | Eknath expands and regularizes Vārkarī literary and performative culture. |
| c. 1604 | Namdev hymns are included in the *Ādi Granth*, providing an early fixed witness to part of the transregional Namdev tradition. |
| c. 1608–1649/50 | Tukaram’s period; Vithoba-centered abhang devotion reaches a major literary culmination. |
| 17th c. | Bahinabai’s *Puṇḍalīka-māhātmya* provides a developed filial-piety narrative. |
| c. 1690–1720 | Śrīdhar’s Marathi *Pāṇḍuraṅga-māhātmya* consolidates popular narratives. |
| 18th c. | Mahipati composes major saint hagiographies. |
| 1820s | Haibatravbaba and associates organize major features of the modern pālkhī procession. |
| 1843 | Stevenson publishes his “Bauddho-Vaishnavas” interpretation. |
| 1908–1916 | Colonial plague and sanitation administration targets the Pandharpur pilgrimage. |
| 1936–1937 | *Sant Tukaram* enters mass cinema and receives international exhibition at Venice. |
| 1960 | Deleury publishes *The Cult of Viṭhobā*. |
| 1973/1974 | Maharashtra enacts the Pandharpur Temples Act, abolishing hereditary service rights and transferring management to a committee. |
| 1984; English translation 2011 | Dhere’s *Śrī Viṭṭhal: Ek Mahāsamanvay* / *Rise of a Folk God* transforms modern debate over origins. |
| 2014 | Supreme Court resolves the Badve litigation; women and non-Brahman priests enter temple service under committee administration. |
| 2020 | COVID-19 restrictions sharply reduce the physical Wari, demonstrating state capacity to interrupt its normal mass form. |
| Present | Large-scale pilgrimage, digital darśan, abhang performance, state administration, media, and diaspora worship continue. |
