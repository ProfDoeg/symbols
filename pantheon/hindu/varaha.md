# Varaha: Research Dossier

**Class:** In developed Vaiṣṇava traditions, Varāha is a male god and avatāra (“descent,” manifestation) of Viṣṇu, appearing either as a complete boar or as a boar-headed man. In earlier Vedic cosmogony, however, the earth-raising boar is a form of the creator Prajāpati rather than securely an avatāra of Viṣṇu. In still earlier Ṛgvedic passages, a boar named Emuṣa is an adversary struck or overcome by Indra; those verses do not narrate the rescue of Earth and cannot simply be treated as the Purāṇic Varāha myth.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The noun *varāha*, “boar,” occurs in the Ṛgveda, notably 1.61.7 and 8.77.10, conventionally dated within the late second millennium BCE. The earliest secure earth-raising divine boar is the Prajāpati narrative of the Black Yajurvedic tradition—Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.1.5 and its expanded form in Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 1.1.3.5–6—probably compiled during the first millennium BCE. Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.1.2.11 calls the boar Emūṣa and identifies him as Prajāpati. None of these passages yet gives the complete later configuration “Viṣṇu-Varāha kills Hiraṇyākṣa and raises Bhūdevī.”

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** Vedic or other early evidence whose wording is directly preserved.
- **[CE] Canonical elaboration:** Epic or Purāṇic development that became widely authoritative.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** Local, sectarian, or geographically delimited version.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Theology, ritual manuals, iconographic prescriptions, or scholastic interpretation.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification:** A historically identifiable fusion or equation.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or temple legend:** Reported as tradition, not converted into archaeological history.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An inference or interpretation, not a statement of the ancient source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or demonstrably modern popular claim.**

Dates assigned to Vedic, epic, and Purāṇic works refer to broad compositional strata, not the ages of extant manuscripts. Purāṇas are layered works and normally cannot be dated as single compositions.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Varāha

Sanskrit **वराह**, IAST *Varāha*, means “boar” or “wild boar.” Monier-Williams marked its derivation as doubtful; confidence in the translation “boar” should not be confused with confidence in a specific etymology. Yāska’s *Nirukta* offers an indigenous exegetical derivation based on the animal’s rooting or tearing action, while the *Nighaṇṭu* also lists *varāha* among words for cloud. Attempts to derive the deity directly from thundercloud, agricultural, Dravidian, “tribal,” or Indo-European boar cults remain comparative hypotheses, not facts demonstrated by the Sanskrit name itself. [Sanskrit dictionary entry](https://sanskritdictionary.in/shabdkosh.php?search=%E0%A4%B5%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B9)

Relevant modern forms include Hindi **वराह**, Bengali **বরাহ**, Odia **ବରାହ**, Telugu **వరాహ**, Kannada **ವರಾಹ**, Malayalam **വരാഹം**, and Tamil **வராகம்** (*Varākam*) or **வராஹர்** (*Varāhar*). Tamil Vaiṣṇava usage also employs **Jñānappirān** or **Ñāṉappirāṉ**, “the Lord of knowledge,” especially in traditions associated with Tirumala.

### Principal titles

- **Varāhadeva / Śrī Varāha:** “Lord Varāha.” General devotional forms. **[CE]**
- **Varāhāvatāra:** “the boar descent/incarnation.” This presupposes developed avatāra doctrine and is not the classification used in the early Yajurvedic accounts. **[CE/LS]**
- **Ādivarāha:** “primeval” or “first Varāha”; associated particularly with cosmic creation and sacred places such as Tirumala. **[MA/RV]**
- **Bhūvarāha:** “Varāha of the Earth,” emphasizing Bhūdevī’s rescue and frequently their paired icon. **[CE/LS]**
- **Nṛvarāha:** “man-boar,” an art-historical or iconographic designation for the human-bodied, boar-headed form. **[LS]**
- **Yajñavarāha:** “Sacrificial Varāha,” whose body is identified with the sacrifice, its implements, priests, metres, and offerings. The identification grows from Brāhmaṇa ritual thought but is elaborated in the *Harivaṃśa*, Purāṇas, and iconographic literature. **[CE/LS]**
- **Pralayavarāha:** the Varāha of cosmic dissolution, raising Earth after *pralaya*. **[LS]**
- **Mahāvarāha:** “Great Boar.” **[CE/LS]**
- **Śvetavarāha:** “White Boar,” associated with the current *Śvetavarāha-kalpa* in Purāṇic cosmology. **[CE]**
- **Kroḍa/Kroḍākāra:** “boar” or “boar-formed,” a poetic and theological synonym. **[CE]**
- **Mahībhartṛ/Mahībhartā:** “bearer” or “husband of Earth.” **[LS]**
- **Dharanīdhara/Dharādhara:** “supporter of Earth.” Not exclusive to Varāha. **[LS]**
- **Yajñāṅga:** “whose limbs are the sacrifice.” **[LS]**
- **Vaikhāna:** interpreted by commentators as “the digger,” from the boar’s excavation of Earth; it also resembles the name of the Vaikhānasa ritual tradition but should not automatically be treated as its etymological source. **[LS]**
- **Ekaśṛṅga/Ekadaṃṣṭra:** “one-horned” or “single-tusked,” occurring in epic praise and later iconography. **[CE]**
- **Emuṣa/Emūṣa:** the named Ṛgvedic boar and the boar of Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.1.2.11. Its meaning is uncertain. A traditional or older scholarly gloss “fierce” is not securely established; Jamison and Brereton emphasize that the name appears only once in the Ṛgveda. **[EA]**

Varāha’s classification consequently changes:

1. an ordinary or mythic boar and Indra’s opponent in the Ṛgveda;
2. Prajāpati’s creative animal form in Black Yajurvedic and Brāhmaṇa cosmogony;
3. Brahmā’s creative form in some epic recensions;
4. Nārāyaṇa-Viṣṇu’s form in the *Mahābhārata* and early Vaiṣṇava mythology;
5. one member of standardized avatāra lists;
6. an independently worshipped form of Viṣṇu, often with Bhūdevī;
7. a royal protector and visual analogy for the sovereign who “raises” or restores the realm.

No secure ancient Greek, Roman, Iranian, Islamic, or Christian *interpretatio* equating Varāha with a foreign deity is documented. Comparisons with the Calydonian boar, Freyr’s boar, Verethragna, or worldwide flood myths are modern comparative constructions, not ancient identifications.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Ṛgvedic boars: related evidence, not yet the rescue myth

**Ṛgveda 1.61.7** describes a powerful figure—normally Indra—piercing a boar through or across a mountain. **Ṛgveda 8.77.10** names *varāham … emuṣam*, the boar Emuṣa, in a compressed episode involving Indra, Viṣṇu, a hundred buffaloes, and cooked rice. Jamison and Brereton observe that 8.77.10 may join or harmonize otherwise distinct versions of an Emuṣa story. The verse does not say that the boar is Viṣṇu, that Earth is submerged, or that a demon named Hiraṇyākṣa is killed. **[EA]** [Jamison–Brereton translation and commentary](https://wiswo.org/books/_resources/book-reference-pdfs/Jamison-Brereton-2014-The%20Rigveda-The%20Earliest%20Poetry%20of%20India%20all%203%20Volume%20Sets.pdf)

Griffith’s older translation can misleadingly suggest a smooth narrative because of ambiguous syntax. The underlying Sanskrit of 8.77.10 is:

> viśvettā viṣṇur ābharad urukramas tveṣitaḥ  
> śataṃ mahiṣān kṣīrapākam odanaṃ varāham indra emuṣam

Jamison and Brereton construe its close as an allusion to “when Indra pierced the boar Emuṣa.” The Ṛgvedic Emuṣa is therefore at most a possible precursor or inverted relative of the later savior-boar. Equating him without qualification with Viṣṇu-Varāha is a retrospective harmonization.

The Atharvaveda contains boars in charms and animal imagery but supplies no securely independent early account of Viṣṇu-Varāha rescuing Earth. Absence here matters: the developed avatāra is not pan-Vedic.

### Taittirīya tradition: Prajāpati’s cosmogonic boar

In **Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.1.5**, the world is initially water. Prajāpati moves or toils in the waters, sees a lotus leaf, concludes that something supports it, assumes a boar form, dives, brings up earth from below, spreads it on the lotus leaf, and stabilizes or extends it. **Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 1.1.3.5–6** gives an expanded version. **[EA]**

This is the earliest securely attested nucleus of the later rescue: primeval waters, a divine boar’s descent, recovery of earth from below, and cosmic establishment. It lacks Hiraṇyākṣa, combat, Bhūdevī as a speaking consort, and explicit identification with Viṣṇu. The agent is Prajāpati.

### Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa

In **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.1.2.11**, within the Pravargya ritual’s explanation of clay collection, Earth was originally only a span in extent; “a boar called Emūṣa raised her up,” and that boar was her lord, Prajāpati. The ritualist takes clay from earth while re-enacting the cosmogony. **[EA]** [Eggeling’s complete translation](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbr/index.htm)

The passage uses a feminine pronoun for Earth and calls Prajāpati her husband or lord, foreshadowing the later divine couple; it still does not describe a maiden hanging from a tusk.

### Taittirīya Āraṇyaka

A passage usually cited as **Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 10.1.8** describes Earth raised by a black or dark boar with a hundred arms. Text numbering varies by recension. This represents further Vedic ritual-cosmic elaboration but still does not automatically prove the full Purāṇic avatāra narrative. **[EA/LS]**

### Upaniṣads

The principal early Upaniṣads do not develop Varāha as a personal deity. The **Varāha Upaniṣad**, a Yoga Upaniṣad framed as instruction from Varāha to the sage Ṛbhu, is late—probably medieval, with uncertain date and transmission. It teaches yoga, nondual knowledge, and liberation rather than preserving early Vedic evidence. **[MA/LS]** [Overview of the Varāha Upaniṣad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varaha_Upanishad)

### Rāmāyaṇa

In **Ayodhyākāṇḍa 2.102.3**, a cosmogony says that the self-existent creator, emerging when all was water, became a boar and raised Earth. The immediately preceding verse identifies the creator as Brahmā; some recensions or later readings more explicitly assimilate him to imperishable Viṣṇu. **[CE with textual variation]** [Sanskrit verse and translation](https://enjoylearningsanskrit.com/scriptures/ramayana/book-2/chapter-102/verse-3/)

In **Yuddhakāṇḍa**, Rāma is praised with imagery of the single-tusked boar. This shows that the boar had entered the Vaiṣṇava repertoire, but the epic’s layers and recension differences preclude treating every verse as belonging to a single date. The *Rāmāyaṇa* grew approximately between the last centuries BCE and early centuries CE; Goldman’s critical translation is preferable to treating a late vulgate as uniform.

### Mahābhārata and Harivaṃśa

The *Mahābhārata* contains multiple cosmogonies. In its Nārāyaṇīya and other Vaiṣṇava strata, Nārāyaṇa assumes the great boar form, raises Earth, and becomes explicitly identifiable with Viṣṇu. The epic’s surviving form was produced over centuries, broadly c. 400 BCE–400 CE, and individual Vaiṣṇava passages may be comparatively late within it. **[CE]** [BORI Critical Edition electronic text](https://bombay.indology.info/mahabharata/welcome.html)

The *Harivaṃśa*, an appendix to the epic compiled and expanded during the early centuries CE, gives the influential **Yajñavarāha** description: parts of the boar’s body correspond to Vedic metres, fires, priests, altar, Soma, clarified butter, and other sacrificial elements. This is not zoological description but a learned transformation of the Vedic creative boar into the embodied Vedic sacrifice. **[CE/LS]**

### Principal Purāṇic versions

#### Viṣṇu Purāṇa

In **Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.4**, Nārāyaṇa, identified with Brahmā in his creative capacity, becomes the boar, enters the waters, raises Earth on his tusk, and is praised by the sages. Earth addresses him as her lord. The surviving *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* is layered; substantial portions may belong to the Gupta or early post-Gupta centuries, but precise chapter dates cannot be secured. **[CE]** [Wilson translation](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/vp/vp000.htm)

Notably, the chapter’s principal emphasis is creation and praise. Popular summaries often import the much fuller Hiraṇyākṣa duel from the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* into it.

#### Bhāgavata Purāṇa

**Skandha 3, chapters 13 and 17–19** provide the best-known full narrative. A tiny boar emerges from Brahmā’s nostril, rapidly grows to cosmic size, roars, dives into the Garbhodaka ocean, locates Earth, and raises her on his tusks. Hiraṇyākṣa challenges him. After a mace duel, insults, and magical attacks, Varāha kills the demon with a blow near the ear and restores Earth. Brahmā and assembled sages praise him. **[CE]** [Canto 3 contents and chapters](https://www.srimadbhagavatam.org/canto3/c3-contents.html), [battle text](https://bhagavata.org/canto3/chapter18.html)

The *Bhāgavata* connects Hiraṇyākṣa and Hiraṇyakaśipu to Viṣṇu’s gatekeepers Jaya and Vijaya, cursed by the Four Kumāras to be born as enemies of the deity. This creates a serial avatāra theology linking Varāha and Narasiṃha. The text is generally placed around the ninth or tenth century CE, although proposed dates vary.

#### Other Purāṇas

- **Matsya, Vāyu, Brahmā, Brahmāṇḍa, Padma, and Garuḍa Purāṇas:** preserve variant creation, combat, and sacrificial-body accounts. Some make Brahmā the immediate agent while identifying him with Viṣṇu; some place the fight on Mount Sumana rather than under the cosmic waters. **[CE]**
- **Linga, Śiva, and Skanda Purāṇas:** incorporate Varāha into Śaiva narratives. In the *liṅgodbhava* cycle Viṣṇu becomes a boar and digs downward seeking the bottom of Śiva’s infinite fiery liṅga, while Brahmā flies upward as a goose. Viṣṇu admits failure; Brahmā in many versions lies and is punished. Here Varāha is subordinate within a Śaiva supremacy narrative. **[SY/CE]**
- **Kālikā Purāṇa 25–31:** gives a markedly Śākta-Śaiva cycle in which Varāha, after accomplishing the rescue, becomes dangerously attached to his boar embodiment; Earth may assume a sow form and bear sons. Śiva as Śarabha destroys the Varāha form, and sacrificial institutions emerge from its dismembered body. This is a late, regional text, probably composed in Kāmarūpa around the tenth–eleventh century. It must not be substituted for pan-Hindu “canonical” narrative. **[RV/MA]**
- **Varāha Purāṇa:** despite its title, the extant work is principally a Vaiṣṇava pilgrimage and ritual compendium framed partly as Varāha’s discourse to Earth. Its manuscripts are composite; Hazra distinguished several sections, and a broad c. eighth–fifteenth-century formation is safer than a single date. [Rocher’s survey of Purāṇic textual history](https://wuecampus.uni-wuerzburg.de/moodle/pluginfile.php/3220375/mod_resource/content/1/A%20history%20of%20Indian%20literature.%20Vol.%20II%2C%20fasc.3.%20Puranas.%20L.Rocher.%20Wiesbaden%2C1986%20%28400dpi_lossy%29.pdf)

### Āgamas, Tantras, and image-making texts

Vaikhānasa and Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās regularize Varāha’s image, mantras, installation, and worship. The Vaikhānasa corpus distinguishes standing Ādi/Bhūvarāha from seated Yajña- and Pralayavarāha forms. Texts such as the *Atri*, *Bhṛgu*, and *Marīci Saṃhitās*, the *Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa*, *Matsya Purāṇa*, *Agni Purāṇa*, and later *śilpaśāstras* specify body, arms, weapons, companions, and proportions. These prescriptions were compiled over centuries and should not be projected onto Kuṣāṇa or Gupta images automatically. **[LS]**

### Tamil corpus

Early Tamil poems praise Māyōṉ/Tirumāl and know his cosmic deeds. The *Paripāṭal*, conventionally dated within the early centuries CE though its exact chronology is disputed, alludes to the dark god’s rescue of Earth in boar form. Later Āḻvār hymns—approximately sixth to ninth centuries—make the image devotional: the supreme Lord becomes a mud-covered boar for Earth’s sake. Periyāḻvār, Nammāḻvār, Tirumaṅkai Āḻvār, and Āṇṭāḷ employ Varāha allusions in praising Viṣṇu’s accessibility and salvific condescension. **[RV/CE]**

A celebrated Śrīvaiṣṇava theological tradition connects Varāha’s speech to Earth with the **Varāha Carama Śloka**, in which the Lord promises to remember and save a devotee who remembers him while healthy even if that person is helpless at death. Its authority is medieval and sectarian rather than Vedic. **[MA/LS]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and characteristic action

Varāha’s defining domain is not “boars” but **cosmic restoration**: descent into undifferentiated waters or the underworld, recovery of habitable Earth, defeat of obstructive chaos, and re-establishment of spatial and moral order. Secondary domains developed from this:

- creation and stabilization of land;
- royal protection and recovery of territory;
- embodied sacrifice;
- protection of devotees and sages;
- sovereignty over land and kingship;
- in Śrīvaiṣṇava theology, assurance of salvation at death;
- in temple geography, lordship over a sacred tract granted to another manifestation of Viṣṇu.

### Principal visual types

1. **Theriomorphic Varāha:** a full boar, often colossal. Rows of gods, sages, planets, worlds, or sacrificial beings cover its body. Bhūdevī may hang from a tusk. The Eran and Khajuraho boars are the chief monuments. **[Gupta and medieval]**

2. **Nṛvarāha/Bhūvarāha:** human body with boar’s head, normally monumental and heroic. One leg may rest upon a Nāga; Bhūdevī stands or hangs near the raised snout or sits on the arm, thigh, or elbow. The Udayagiri relief is the paradigmatic early example. **[Gupta onward]**

3. **Seated Yajñavarāha:** Varāha enthroned, often with Bhūdevī and Lakṣmī; the image represents sacrificial sovereignty rather than the moment of underwater struggle. **[LS/medieval]**

4. **Pralayavarāha:** seated or composed with Bhū alone, emphasizing rescue after dissolution. **[LS]**

5. **Vaikuṇṭha Viṣṇu:** a composite form in which boar and lion heads accompany the central human face, associating Varāha and Narasiṃha with the transcendent Viṣṇu. Especially important in Kashmir and related northern traditions. **[SY/LS]**

### Attributes

Standard arms vary from two to four. Four-armed forms may carry Viṣṇu’s **cakra** (discus), **śaṅkha** (conch), **gadā** (mace), and **padma** (lotus); other hands support or embrace Bhūdevī. Early monumental reliefs do not always follow later manuals.

A Vaikhānasa prescription gives a four-armed, boar-headed figure: conch and discus in the upper hands; one lower hand supporting Bhūdevī and the other encircling her waist; a foot placed upon the jeweled hood of Ādiśeṣa. [Summary of canonical forms](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/52987.pdf)

No single sacred color is universal. Texts and images describe black, dark-blue, red, white, or golden Varāhas according to cosmological or ritual setting. “Black boar with a hundred arms” belongs to a specific Vedic formulation; “white Varāha” belongs to the *Śvetavarāha-kalpa* system. Modern charts assigning fixed colors, weekdays, planets, crystals, or numerological correspondences to Varāha are generally **[MI]** unless traceable to a named ritual text.

### Bhūdevī and the tusk

The familiar woman suspended from or touching the tusk is a personification of Earth, not evidence that ancient Indians represented the planet as a modern astronomical globe. Early and medieval images normally render Earth anthropomorphically. Modern internet claims that a spherical object on a Varāha image proves knowledge of the modern globe require object-by-object examination; they cannot be inferred from the myth.

### Earliest images

A fragmentary Kuṣāṇa-period anthropomorphic Varāha from Mathura is commonly dated around the second or third century CE. Its interpretation established that Varāha imagery predates Gupta imperial monuments, though precise dating remains stylistic. [Ashmolean discussion of the Mathura-to-Gupta development](https://jameelcentre.ashmolean.org/collection/7/10230/10260/all/per_page/25/offset/0/sort_by/seqn./object/11153)

### Udayagiri Cave 5

The colossal relief near Vidisha, Madhya Pradesh, belongs to the Gupta complex developed under Candragupta II around 400 CE. An inscription elsewhere in the complex names the king and his minister Vīrasena in Gupta year 82, corresponding approximately to 401–402 CE; this dates the royal phase of the site, though some scholars place completion of the Varāha panel slightly later, around 410–412.

Varāha stands as a gigantic boar-headed man. Bhūdevī rises by his tusk. His foot dominates a serpent/Nāga figure; gods, sages, rivers, and cosmic beings fill the scene. **[Materially attested]**

Michael Willis reads the reworked Udayagiri landscape as an “astro-political” ritual center where Viṣṇu’s mastery of cosmic time and Varāha’s restoration of Earth articulated Candragupta’s paramount kingship. The proposal that the small royal-looking figure represents Candragupta himself, and that the subdued Nāga also alludes to conquered Nāga rulers, is influential but remains interpretation rather than an inscriptional caption. **[MSR]** [Willis, *The Archaeology of Hindu Ritual* excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805215/18741/excerpt/9780521518741_excerpt.pdf)

### Eran

The colossal red-sandstone boar at Eran, Madhya Pradesh, is a completely theriomorphic cosmic Varāha, approximately 3.4 metres high and 4 metres long. Its surface is covered with sages and divine beings; Bhūdevī hangs from a tusk. A Sanskrit inscription on its neck records the first regnal year of the Hūṇa ruler Toramāṇa, early sixth century CE, and dedicatory activity by Dhanyaviṣṇu, brother of the deceased Mātr̥viṣṇu. It opens with victory to Viṣṇu in boar form, whose snout’s blows shook the mountains as he raised Earth.

The inscription establishes Toramāṇa’s sovereignty at Eran and the monument’s Vaiṣṇava use at that date. Whether the entire sculpture was newly carved under Toramāṇa or an existing late-Gupta image was appropriated or completed under Hūṇa rule remains debated. [Siddham inscription record and publication history](https://siddham.network/inscription/in00099/?section=concordance)

### Later major monuments

- **Badami Cave 3, Karnataka, sixth century:** Chalukya Nṛvarāha with Bhūdevī, demonstrating southern diffusion.
- **Aihole, sixth–seventh centuries:** several Chalukya variants.
- **Mahabalipuram Varāha Cave, seventh century:** Pallava relief; Varāha delicately raises Bhū while divine spectators attend. Its courtly aesthetic differs markedly from Udayagiri’s colossal imperial idiom.
- **Ellora, seventh–eighth centuries:** Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava contexts place Varāha within larger divine programs.
- **Khajuraho Varāha shrine, c. 900–925:** monolithic cosmic boar facing the Lakṣmaṇa temple; hundreds of divine figures cover its body. **[Materially attested]**
- **Bago-Pathari and other central Indian boars:** show a durable regional preference for monumental theriomorphic images.
- **Hoysala temples, twelfth–thirteenth centuries:** highly ornamented Nṛvarāhas conform partly, but never mechanically, to textual prescriptions.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Ontological “genealogy”

Varāha normally has no biological parents. His origin changes with theology:

- **Prajāpati becomes the boar:** Taittirīya Saṃhitā/Brāhmaṇa and Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa. **[EA]**
- **Brahmā becomes the boar:** one epic and Purāṇic line. **[CE]**
- **Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa assumes the form:** *Mahābhārata*, *Harivaṃśa*, and mature Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas. **[CE]**
- **A tiny boar emerges from Brahmā’s nostril but is revealed as Viṣṇu:** *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.13. This makes Brahmā the site of manifestation, not Varāha’s biological father. **[CE]**

### Consorts and descendants

**Bhūdevī/Pṛthivī/Dharanī** is the primary consort. In early Brāhmaṇa language Earth is Prajāpati-boar’s mate or domain; in Purāṇic and temple theology she is the rescued goddess and often Varāha’s spouse. **[EA → CE]**

**Lakṣmī/Śrī** sometimes accompanies Yajñavarāha or appears in Lakṣmī-Varāha icons. She is not present in the oldest earth-raising account. **[LS/MA]**

**Narakāsura/Bhauma** is in some later traditions a son of Bhūdevī by Varāha; other texts give different origins. The genealogy is therefore regional and unstable. **[RV/MA]**

The *Kālikā Purāṇa* gives Earth a sow form and offspring from Varāha, whose unruliness contributes to Śiva’s intervention. This cannot be generalized beyond that late regional cycle. **[RV/MA]**

### Every principal documented god–god interaction

The following list covers interactions in the principal Vedic, epic, Purāṇic, Āgamic, and major temple traditions surveyed here. Mere co-membership in an avatāra list is not counted.

- **Prajāpati — identity:** Prajāpati assumes the boar form, dives, and raises Earth; Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.1.5, Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 1.1.3.5–6, first millennium BCE. **[EA]**
- **Prajāpati — identity and marital lordship:** Emūṣa, who raises Earth, is declared her lord Prajāpati; Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.1.2.11, mid-to-late first millennium BCE. **[EA]**
- **Indra — hostile encounter with a boar:** Indra pierces or overcomes the boar Emuṣa; Ṛgveda 1.61.7 and 8.77.10, late second millennium BCE. Whether this boar belongs genealogically to later Varāha is unproved. **[EA]**
- **Viṣṇu — cooperation in the Emuṣa episode:** far-striding Viṣṇu brings the buffaloes, food, or spoils at Indra’s urging; Ṛgveda 8.77.10. **[EA]**
- **Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa — identity:** the creator’s boar becomes an avatāra or manifestation of Viṣṇu; *Mahābhārata*, *Harivaṃśa*, and Purāṇas, approximately early centuries BCE onward. **[CE]**
- **Brahmā — manifestation:** a minute boar comes from Brahmā’s nostril and reveals cosmic size; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.13, c. ninth–tenth century. **[CE]**
- **Brahmā — praise and tactical counsel:** Brahmā recognizes Varāha as Nārāyaṇa and urges him to kill Hiraṇyākṣa before an astrologically dangerous hour; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.18. **[CE]**
- **Bhūdevī/Pṛthivī — rescue:** Varāha finds and raises Earth from the primeval waters; Taittirīya and Śatapatha traditions, later epics and Purāṇas. **[EA → CE]**
- **Bhūdevī — marriage/dialogue:** Earth praises Varāha and becomes his consort or interlocutor; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 1.4 and *Varāha Purāṇa*. **[CE/MA]**
- **Bhūdevī — erotic union and offspring:** Earth takes sow form and bears offspring in the *Kālikā Purāṇa* cycle, c. tenth–eleventh century. **[RV/MA]**
- **Hiraṇyākṣa — combat:** the Daitya challenges Varāha and is killed after mace combat and magical attacks; fully developed in *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.17–19, with variants in several Purāṇas. **[CE]**
- **Jaya and Vijaya — causal relation:** their curse and births as Hiraṇyākṣa and Hiraṇyakaśipu cause Viṣṇu’s Varāha and Narasiṃha confrontations; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.15–16. Varāha does not meet them under their gatekeeper identities in this episode. **[CE]**
- **Four Kumāras — indirect causal interaction:** their curse on Jaya and Vijaya prepares Hiraṇyākṣa’s birth; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.15–16. They do not battle Varāha. **[CE]**
- **Varuṇa — indirect direction:** Hiraṇyākṣa challenges the sea-god; Varuṇa refuses and directs him toward Viṣṇu, leading to the Varāha battle; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.17. **[CE]**
- **Nārada — prediction and narration:** in Purāṇic frames Nārada communicates divine destinies and episodes connected with the Daityas; the exact role varies by text. **[CE]**
- **Ādiśeṣa/Ananta — bodily support or attendant:** canonical images place Varāha’s foot on Śeṣa’s hood; Padma-Purāṇic and Āgamic systems associate Śeṣa with supporting Earth. **[LS]**
- **Lakṣmī — paired enthronement:** Lakṣmī accompanies Yajñavarāha or Lakṣmī-Varāha alongside or instead of Bhū; medieval iconographic texts and sculptures. **[LS/MA]**
- **Varāhī — emanation:** Varāha produces or corresponds to the boar-faced Mother Varāhī, who joins the Mātṛkās in the Goddess’s battles; *Devī Māhātmya* and later Purāṇic/Āgamic elaboration. Varāhī becomes an independent goddess with her own tantric history. **[SY/CE]**
- **Devī/Caṇḍikā — martial alliance through Varāhī:** Varāha’s power participates in the Goddess’s army against demonic enemies such as Raktabīja; *Devī Māhātmya* 8, approximately sixth century. Varāha himself is not the central combatant. **[CE/SY]**
- **Śiva as infinite liṅga — failed quest:** Viṣṇu becomes a boar and digs downward but cannot find Śiva’s base; *Linga*, *Śiva*, and Śaiva portions of the *Skanda Purāṇa*, early medieval forms. **[SY/CE]**
- **Śiva/Śarabha — destruction of the boar body:** Śarabha kills or dismembers a dangerously persistent Varāha manifestation; *Kālikā Purāṇa* 29–31. **[RV/MA]**
- **Brahmā and Śiva — joint sacrificial reconstitution:** in the *Kālikā Purāṇa*, Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Maheśvara appropriate aspects arising from the destroyed Yajña-Varāha body. **[RV/MA]**
- **Garuda — service:** temple legends make Garuda transport a divine hill or garden for Varāha; Tirumala *sthāla-purāṇa*. **[F/MA]**
- **Veṅkaṭeśvara/Śrīnivāsa — land grant:** Varāha grants land at Tirumala to Śrīnivāsa, who promises Varāha first worship and first food offering; temple tradition, continuously enacted today but not early historical evidence. **[F/MA; living ritual]** [Official TTD account](https://tirumala.org/TTDTempleHistory.aspx)
- **Narasiṃha — fused identity:** at Simhachalam, Varāha and Narasiṃha form the single deity Varāha-Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha; inscriptions refer to Yajñavarāha and Simhādrinātha. **[SY/RV]**

No early source records a direct meeting between Varāha and Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Vāmana, Matsya, or Kūrma merely because they later share Daśāvatāra lists. Their grouping is a systematized relation, not a narrated encounter.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The cosmogonic dive

**Early core:** water exists before differentiated Earth. Prajāpati sees a lotus leaf, infers support below, becomes a boar, dives, breaks or gathers earth, rises, spreads it, and establishes the world. **Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.1.5; Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 1.1.3.5–6. [EA]**

The story is etiological: Earth is *pṛthivī*, the “broad” or extended one, because the creator spreads her. The boar’s natural rooting makes it an apt mythic excavator, but claims that the story originated directly in observation of real boars remain **[MSR]**.

Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa compresses the narrative: Earth was a span in size; Emūṣa raised her, and he was her lord Prajāpati. Its immediate ritual function is to authorize clay-taking in the Pravargya. **[EA]**

### B. Transfer from Prajāpati/Brahmā to Viṣṇu

The *Rāmāyaṇa* preserves Brahmā as the boar-creator. The *Mahābhārata* and *Harivaṃśa* increasingly identify the cosmic agent with Nārāyaṇa-Viṣṇu. Purāṇic theology then makes the boar one deliberate avatāra among Viṣṇu’s descents. **[CE]**

This is not necessarily a single act of “appropriation.” Prajāpati, Brahmā, Nārāyaṇa, and Viṣṇu overlap in developing creator theology. Still, the textual sequence documents a genuine change in naming and sectarian emphasis. The early passage should not be rewritten as though it already said “Viṣṇu’s third avatāra.”

### C. Rescue of Bhūdevī

In the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*, Varāha dives through the waters as a cosmic body; sages in higher worlds praise him, drops shaken from him sanctify them, and Earth recognizes her lord. He raises her on his tusk and arranges the continents. The rescue is both cosmogony and divine marriage. **[CE]**

Visual traditions turn the abstract Earth into a woman: she clings to the tusk, stands near the snout, or sits upon Varāha’s arm. Udayagiri makes the cosmic rescue an ordered assembly of all beings. Mahabalipuram makes it a more intimate royal epiphany. The images are interpretations, not illustrations mechanically copied from one surviving text.

### D. Hiraṇyākṣa cycle

The fullest version is *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.13–19:

1. Jaya and Vijaya are cursed by the Kumāras.
2. They are born to Diti and Kaśyapa as Hiraṇyākṣa and Hiraṇyakaśipu.
3. Hiraṇyākṣa grows enormously powerful and challenges the gods.
4. Varuṇa refuses combat and tells him to seek Viṣṇu.
5. Meanwhile, the boar has emerged from Brahmā’s nostril, expanded cosmically, and entered the waters.
6. Varāha finds Earth and raises her.
7. Hiraṇyākṣa mocks him as a forest animal and accuses him of acting through illusion.
8. Varāha places Earth safely, then fights.
9. The combat includes maces, a trident, demonic illusions, and the Sudarśana discus.
10. Varāha finally strikes Hiraṇyākṣa below the ear, killing him.
11. Gods and sages praise the victor.

The often-repeated claim that Hiraṇyākṣa personally “rolled Earth into a ball and carried it under the ocean” is not uniform across sources. Some texts simply find Earth submerged after dissolution; others make the demon seize or conceal her; others narrate the fight separately from the lifting. **[Variant warning]**

Likewise, images of Varāha raising Bhūdevī do not always depict Hiraṇyākṣa. Udayagiri’s serpent figure should not automatically be labeled Hiraṇyākṣa.

### E. Yajñavarāha

The *Harivaṃśa* and Purāṇic texts map sacrifice onto the cosmic boar: Vedas are his feet or limbs, sacrificial posts his tusks, fire his tongue, darbha grass his hair, metres his movement, day and night his eyes, and ritual gifts or clarified butter other bodily parts. Details vary. **[CE/LS]**

The myth turns Varāha into more than an avatāra who once acted: every properly ordered sacrifice becomes participation in the body that restored the world. Monumental boars covered with gods and sages visually express a related cosmic-body idea, though direct dependence on one textual passage is rarely demonstrable.

### F. Liṅgodbhava

Brahmā and Viṣṇu dispute supremacy. Śiva appears as an endless fiery pillar. Viṣṇu becomes Varāha and burrows downward; Brahmā becomes a goose and flies upward. Neither finds an end. Viṣṇu admits failure; Brahmā falsely claims success in many versions and is punished. **[Śaiva canonical elaboration]**

This myth reuses the boar’s downward-diving competence but reverses its triumph: the animal who could reach submerged Earth cannot reach Śiva’s foundation. It is a sectarian ordering of gods, not part of the earliest Varāha cycle.

### G. Śarabha and the death of Varāha’s animal body

The *Kālikā Purāṇa* gives an uncommon sequel. Varāha remains attached to the boar form and generates destructive progeny. Śiva intervenes as Śarabha; the Varāha body is destroyed, and aspects of sacrifice emerge from it. **[RV/MA]**

Modern summaries often blend this with better-known Śarabha-versus-Narasiṃha stories. They are related sectarian patterns but not the same episode. No evidence makes Śiva’s killing of Varāha a universal Hindu conclusion to the rescue myth.

### H. Tirumala land-grant cycle

According to Tirumala tradition, Varāha occupied Veṅkaṭa Hill before Śrīnivāsa. He granted Śrīnivāsa land; in exchange he received the rights of first sight, first worship, and first food offering. **[F/MA]**

The historical fact is that the Bhūvarāhasvāmi shrine stands by the Svāmipuṣkariṇī and that offerings and recommended pilgrimage order continue to privilege Varāha. The divine land deed belongs to sacred narrative, not the surviving epigraphic archive.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Early cult evidence

The Vedic passages belong to ritual explanation, but they do not establish a separate Varāha priesthood or temple cult. Independent image worship becomes materially visible in the Kuṣāṇa period. Its great expansion in central India during the fourth–sixth centuries is demonstrated by sculptures, caves, temples, and inscriptions, not merely inferred from Purāṇic literature.

### Udayagiri

Udayagiri was an imperial sacred landscape rather than a free-standing “Varāha temple” in the later sense. Cave 5’s relief stood within a coordinated program containing Viṣṇu, Śiva, Devī, royal inscriptions, astronomical orientation, and ritual architecture. It is the clearest evidence that Varāha’s cosmic rescue had become usable as Gupta royal theology by c. 400 CE.

### Eran

The Toramāṇa inscription documents continuing institutional Vaiṣṇavism across a regime change. Its donor genealogy and regnal dating are stronger historical evidence than later assumptions that either “the Guptas” or “the Huns” alone created the sculpture.

### Khajuraho

The small Varāha shrine opposite the Lakṣmaṇa temple, normally dated around the early tenth century, houses a monolithic cosmic boar. The shrine’s relationship to the Chandella royal-Vaiṣṇava complex demonstrates continued elite use of the deity. Worship there is no longer comparable in scale to major living pilgrimage centers.

### Śrīmuṣṇam

The Bhūvarāhasvāmi temple at Śrīmuṣṇam, Tamil Nadu, is one of the principal southern Varāha centers. Medieval Chola and Vijayanagara inscriptions record donations; a record associated with Virarājendra’s period and another under Kulottuṅga I are frequently cited, though individual readings should be checked in the epigraphic corpus. The deity is a dark stone, human-bodied, boar-headed standing image. Local practice is notable for participation extending beyond narrowly sectarian Vaiṣṇava boundaries; claims of timeless interreligious custom require historical caution. **[Material record plus living tradition]**

### Tirumala

The east-facing Bhūvarāhasvāmi temple stands by the Svāmipuṣkariṇī. In current TTD practice, Varāha receives offerings before Veṅkaṭeśvara, and pilgrims are traditionally instructed to worship him first. This is one of the strongest cases of a living Varāha cult embedded within a much larger Vaiṣṇava pilgrimage economy. [Official temple legend](https://www.tirumala.org/Varaha_TempleLegend.aspx)

### Simhachalam

Simhachalam’s deity is the fused Varāha-Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha. The earliest surviving temple inscription is dated 1087 CE and records a private gift of a garden. Nearly five hundred inscriptions, in Sanskrit, Telugu, Odia, and Tamil, record donations and construction. Eastern Ganga patronage transformed the complex in the thirteenth century; a 1293 record mentions subsidiary forms including Yajñavarāha. Vijayanagara ruler Kṛṣṇadevarāya and his queens donated ornaments in the sixteenth century. [Documented temple history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varaha_Lakshmi_Narasimha_temple%2C_Simhachalam)

The icon is ordinarily covered in sandalwood paste and revealed during the annual *Candanotsava*. This is a living ritual of the fused deity, not evidence for an unchanged Vedic ceremony.

### Other centers

Important Varāha or Varāha-associated sites include:

- Tiruvaḍandai and other Śrīvaiṣṇava *Divya Deśas*;
- Jajpur’s Varāhanātha temple in Odisha;
- Soron/Sūkarakṣetra in Uttar Pradesh, whose Varāha antiquity is asserted in regional *māhātmyas*;
- Varāha Cave at Mahabalipuram;
- Badami and Aihole;
- Eran and Udayagiri;
- Pushkar, where a Varāha temple has a complicated history of construction and rebuilding;
- numerous subsidiary shrines in Vaiṣṇava temple complexes.

### Rituals and offerings

Varāha worship follows Vaiṣṇava temple norms: bathing and dressing the icon, flowers, incense, lamps, food offerings, recitation of Vaiṣṇava mantras, processions, and annual festival observances. **Varāha Jayantī** is commonly observed on the third lunar day of the bright half of Bhādrapada, though calendars and local observances vary.

The *Nārada Purāṇa* transmits a Varāha mantra seeking lordship over land, reflecting the deity’s association with sovereignty. Vaikhānasa and Pāñcarātra manuals govern consecration and daily service in their respective institutions. No evidence establishes a universal Varāha-specific caste of priests separate from the ritual communities serving Vaiṣṇava temples.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

The term Yajñavarāha makes the deity the metaphysical body of Vedic sacrifice; it does not by itself prove that animals or humans were sacrificed to Varāha images. Searches for Varāha-specific human sacrifice produced no credible epigraphic, archaeological, or ethnographic documentation. Texts such as the *Kālikā Purāṇa* discuss human sacrifice in Śākta royal contexts, especially Kamakhya, but that cannot be reassigned to Varāha merely because the same work contains a Varāha cycle.

No reliable evidence links Varāha cults specifically with institutional “temple prostitution.” Colonial-era generalizations about *devadāsīs* must not be attached to this deity without temple-specific records.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late second millennium BCE: unrelated or precursor boars

The Ṛgveda knows the boar Emuṣa as Indra’s opponent. Viṣṇu participates in the compressed episode as far-strider and retriever of spoils. There is no rescued Earth. **[EA]**

### Early to mid-first millennium BCE: cosmogonic Prajāpati

Black Yajurvedic prose supplies the decisive mythic innovation: the creator becomes a boar, dives, and raises or spreads Earth. Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa names the boar Emūṣa and declares him Prajāpati. The narrative is embedded in sacrificial ritual rather than an independent temple cult. **[EA]**

### Last centuries BCE–early centuries CE: Vaiṣṇava assimilation

Epic composition brings the boar into Brahmā-Nārāyaṇa-Viṣṇu theology. Avatāra concepts remain fluid; lists differ, and Varāha’s numerical position is not eternally fixed. Early Tamil sources show knowledge outside Sanskrit scholastic circles. **[CE/RV]**

### Second–third centuries CE: first surviving icons

Kuṣāṇa-period Mathura produces boar-headed anthropomorphic images. This is the first material evidence for recognizable Varāha devotion, though the scale of organized cult remains uncertain.

### Fourth–sixth centuries: Gupta monumentalization and royal theology

Varāha becomes one of the most politically charged images of early Hindu kingship. Udayagiri equates cosmic restoration with imperial order. Eran translates the myth into a self-contained cosmic boar and survives a transfer from Gupta to Hūṇa authority. The material concentration in central India justifies speaking of a Gupta-era peak in monumental Varāha imagery there, though not necessarily of a numerically dominant popular cult.

### Sixth–eighth centuries: pan-Indian diffusion

Chalukya Badami and Aihole, Pallava Mahabalipuram, and Ellora incorporate Varāha into temple programs. The *Devī Māhātmya* generates Varāhī from his power. Tamil Āḻvārs interpret his descent as divine humility and grace. Iconographic manuals increasingly classify his forms.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: dynastic emblems and systematization

The Gurjara-Pratihāra emperor Mihira Bhoja adopted **Ādivarāha** as a royal title and placed a boar on coins. Chalukya houses used the boar as a dynastic emblem on seals and coinage. These are materially documented uses; claims about the exact design of lost “flags” are less secure.

The *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* gives the Hiraṇyākṣa battle its most influential narrative form. Khajuraho’s cosmic boar monumentalizes the deity in Chandella territory.

### Tenth–thirteenth centuries: regional syntheses

The *Kālikā Purāṇa* subjects Varāha to Śaiva-Śākta supremacy. Simhachalam fuses Varāha and Narasiṃha. Śrīmuṣṇam, Tirumala, and other southern institutions receive royal endowments. Śrīvaiṣṇava teachers embed the Varāha promise of salvation in systematic theology.

### Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: Vijayanagara appropriation

Vijayanagara seals and monuments combine the boar with other symbols, drawing on older Chalukya idioms. The gold denomination called *varāha*—“pagoda” in European usage—became widespread. The emblem expressed protection and legitimate sovereignty, but one should distinguish the coin-name from an assertion that every coin bore a detailed image of the god.

### Islamic-period conflict and survival

Some Varāha temples, like many north Indian temples, underwent destruction, abandonment, relocation, or rebuilding during warfare and regime change. The Pushkar Varāha temple is associated in historical and local accounts with destruction under Mughal authority and later Rajput rebuilding. Evidence must be handled monument by monument: there was no single documented empire-wide campaign specifically against Varāha.

Conversely, the Eran image, Udayagiri relief, and many cave sculptures survived partly because they were immovable, remote, buried, or incorporated into changing sacred landscapes. Survival does not prove uninterrupted worship.

### Colonial period

Antiquarians such as James Prinsep, Alexander Cunningham, FitzEdward Hall, and John Fleet documented Eran and its inscription during the nineteenth century. Their publications brought Varāha into modern histories of Indian art and political chronology. They also sometimes treated Purāṇic myth as a single stable narrative and used evolutionary categories no longer accepted without qualification.

### Present

Varāha remains ritually active at Tirumala, Śrīmuṣṇam, Simhachalam, and other temples. More commonly he is encountered as one member of the Daśāvatāra, in household images, calendrical art, dance, television, and online devotional culture. Direct, exclusive Varāha worship is smaller than devotion to Kṛṣṇa, Rāma, Veṅkaṭeśvara, or Narasiṃha, but it is neither extinct nor merely reconstructed.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Art, literature, television, and games

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century print culture standardized a dramatic image: a muscular, boar-headed Viṣṇu holding a visibly spherical Earth above an ocean while fighting a horned demon. This combines several older elements but often differs from early sculpture, where Earth is a woman and Hiraṇyākṣa is absent. **[MI as standardized composite, based on older materials]**

Indian television serials—especially Purāṇic and Viṣṇu-centered series—have popularized the *Bhāgavata* version as though it were the single original story. Amar Chitra Katha, devotional posters, animation, and video games likewise compress the textual variants.

### Environmental interpretation

Modern writers frequently treat Varāha as an ecological symbol: the deity rescues “planet Earth” from environmental degradation. This is a meaningful contemporary interpretation, but the modern concept of a planet threatened by anthropogenic climate change is not an ancient textual doctrine. **[MI/reinterpretation]**

### National and political use

Varāha had political uses long before modern nationalism: Gupta, Pratihāra, Chalukya, and Vijayanagara rulers associated the boar with territorial restoration and sovereignty. Modern presentations sometimes turn that history into a timeless claim that Hindu kings uniformly used Varāha as a national emblem. The evidence instead shows several distinct dynastic uses.

Udayagiri has also entered modern narratives of an ancient unified Hindu nation. The monument unquestionably joins religion and Gupta kingship; mapping modern nation-state ideology directly onto it is anachronistic. Conversely, denying its political character because it is religious would ignore its royal setting.

Claims that Varāha sculpture proves that ancient Hindu texts uniquely discovered the spherical Earth, plate tectonics, submarine geology, or evolutionary biology are **[MI]**. The images and texts document cosmology and myth, not experimental demonstrations of those sciences.

### Occultism and neopaganism

Unlike Kālī, Gaṇeśa, or Hanumān, Varāha did not become a major autonomous figure in the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or European ceremonial magic. Modern occult websites sometimes assign him chakras, planets, tarot correspondences, “frequencies,” crystals, or manifestation rituals. Unless tied to a named Sanskrit ritual manual, these are modern syntheses.

There is little evidence for a statistically significant Western reconstructionist or neopagan Varāha movement. Contemporary worship is overwhelmingly continuous Hindu practice or individualized global Hindu devotion, not a recovered pre-Hindu boar cult.

### Commercial use

“Varaha” is now used for businesses, software, military or engineering projects, and scientific algorithms. These uses normally invoke strength, excavation, rescue, or an Indian cultural identity; they are not cult evidence. The name of the VARAHA gravitational-wave sampler is a technical acronym and should not be treated as a modern religious tradition.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Was the Ṛgvedic Emuṣa already Varāha?

Older handbooks often drew a straight line from Indra’s boar adversary to Viṣṇu’s saving boar. Macdonell connected the passages, and later popular summaries routinely call every Vedic boar “Varāha avatāra.”

The objection is textual: in Ṛgveda 8.77.10 Emuṣa is the object of Indra’s attack, while Viṣṇu is a cooperating but separate figure. No Earth is raised. In the Brāhmaṇas, Emūṣa/Prajāpati is the raiser. Scholars therefore variously propose transformation, reversal, conflation of two boar traditions, or merely shared animal imagery. The secure conclusion is sequence, not derivation: hostile boar and cosmogonic boar are both attested; the precise mechanism linking them is unrecoverable.

### Indigenous versus “non-Aryan” origin

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century comparativists sometimes labeled animal-shaped gods survivals of “primitive,” “Dravidian,” “tribal,” or pre-Aryan religion. Others derived Varāha from Indo-European storm or boar symbolism. Neither position has decisive evidence. The myth is already integrated into Vedic ritual prose; this proves early Brahmanical use but not ultimate invention. Boar reverence among later communities cannot, by itself, reconstruct second-millennium BCE origins.

### Which text first makes the boar Viṣṇu?

The Taittirīya and Śatapatha accounts say Prajāpati. The *Rāmāyaṇa* passage primarily names Brahmā. Explicit Nārāyaṇa-Viṣṇu identification becomes clear in epic and Purāṇic strata. Attempts to make the Ṛgvedic presence of Viṣṇu in 8.77.10 proof that Emuṣa himself was already Viṣṇu are grammatically and narratively disputed.

### When did Hiraṇyākṣa enter the story?

The demon is absent from the early earth-diving accounts. Epic and Purāṇic material gradually combine cosmogony with divine combat, while the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* supplies the most developed narrative. It is therefore a later canonical elaboration, not part of the demonstrable Vedic core.

### Dating the Purāṇas

Rocher emphasized that a Purāṇa’s chapters may derive from different periods and that assigning one date to an entire work is methodologically unsafe. The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* has relatively early materials but surviving redaction; the *Bhāgavata* is commonly dated c. ninth–tenth century; the *Varāha Purāṇa* is particularly composite. Manuscripts are usually centuries later than the putative compositions.

### Text versus image

Early images sometimes precede surviving iconographic prescriptions. A sculpture that does not conform to a medieval manual is not necessarily “incorrect.” Conversely, a late manual’s taxonomy—Ādi-, Bhū-, Yajña-, Pralaya-Varāha—cannot automatically identify every earlier image. Visual and literary traditions influenced one another without simple one-way dependence. [Study of regional variation in temple imagery](https://dokumen.pub/temple-imagery-from-early-mediaeval-peninsular-india-1409430294-9781409430292.html)

### Udayagiri’s political meaning

Three broad positions recur:

1. the relief is primarily a religious representation of Viṣṇu’s cosmic deed;
2. it analogically celebrates Candragupta II’s conquests and restoration of order;
3. it belongs to a larger astronomical-ritual complex producing sacred kingship.

These are compatible to a degree. What remains uncertain is the exact identity of minor figures and whether particular Nāgas represent specific defeated rulers. The nearby inscription dates royal activity but does not caption the panel as a conquest allegory.

### Dating and patronage of the Eran boar

The Toramāṇa inscription securely fixes use in his first regnal year. It does not unambiguously settle whether the statue was carved at that moment, slightly earlier under Gupta authority, or completed and reinscribed. Treating it simply as either a “Gupta” or “Hūṇa” sculpture suppresses the contested evidence.

### Colonial discovery and editions

- Eran was reported to scholarly circles by T. S. Burt in 1838.
- James Prinsep published an early reading.
- FitzEdward Hall revised it in 1861.
- John Fleet re-edited it in *Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum* in 1888.
- Archaeological Survey reports documented Udayagiri, Eran, Khajuraho, and related sites.
- The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute’s critical *Mahābhārata* supplies the indispensable basis for separating epic recension material.
- Reliable modern work must combine Sanskrit philology, inscriptional editions, stratigraphic or architectural study, and regional-language devotional corpora.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Varāha is explicitly Viṣṇu’s third avatāra in the Ṛgveda”: false.
- “The Vedas already contain Hiraṇyākṣa kidnapping Earth”: false.
- “All Purāṇas tell the same Varāha story”: false.
- “Every woman or sphere on a Varāha tusk proves ancient scientific knowledge of a globe”: unsupported.
- “Varāha was borrowed demonstrably from one named tribal god”: unproved.
- “The Udayagiri Nāga is certainly Hiraṇyākṣa”: not established.
- “The Eran statue was certainly commissioned personally by Toramāṇa”: possible but not proved by the inscription alone.
- “Human sacrifice was a documented regular Varāha rite”: no credible evidence found.
- “Varāha has a major ancient Western occult lineage”: no evidence found.

## 11. Sources

### Primary texts and editions

The essential textual witnesses are the Ṛgveda—especially 1.61.7 and 8.77.10—in Jamison and Brereton’s translation; the Taittirīya Saṃhitā and Brāhmaṇa; Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.1.2.11 in Eggeling; the critical *Mahābhārata*; the critical and regional *Rāmāyaṇa* traditions; the *Harivaṃśa*; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 1.4; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 3.13 and 3.17–19; the relevant *Linga*, *Śiva*, *Skanda*, *Matsya*, *Vāyu*, *Padma*, *Brahmāṇḍa*, *Kālikā*, and *Varāha Purāṇas*; the *Devī Māhātmya*; Vaikhānasa and Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās; and the Tamil *Paripāṭal* and Āḻvār corpus.

The chief physical documents are the Kuṣāṇa Mathura fragments, Udayagiri Cave 5 and its neighboring Gupta inscriptions, the Eran boar and Toramāṇa inscription, Badami and Aihole reliefs, Mahabalipuram’s Varāha Cave, the Khajuraho monolith, and the inscriptional corpora of Śrīmuṣṇam, Tirumala, and Simhachalam.

### Selected modern scholarship

Especially important are A. A. Macdonell’s *Vedic Mythology*; Jan Gonda’s studies of Viṣṇu and Vedic religion; J. L. Brockington on the Sanskrit epics; Ludo Rocher on the Purāṇas; Michael Willis on Udayagiri and early Hindu ritual institutions; Stella Kramrisch and T. A. Gopinatha Rao on iconography; and studies of Gupta, Chalukya, Pallava, Pratihāra, Chandella, and Vijayanagara political art. Older works remain useful as records of editions and discoveries but require correction by critical philology and archaeology.
