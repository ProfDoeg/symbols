# Hanuman: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest recoverable tradition Hanuman is a male *vānara*—a superhuman, shape-changing forest/monkey being—not yet an independently worshipped supreme god. Later Hindu traditions classify him variously as a deified epic hero, god, immortal (*cirañjīvin*), son or manifestation of Vāyu, manifestation of Rudra–Śiva, exemplary servant of Rāma, tantric guardian, and village or martial deity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Sanskrit *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, whose surviving text developed over several centuries, conventionally c. fourth/third century BCE–third century CE. Hanuman belongs to its old epic narrative, but individual passages—especially parts of the *Bāla-* and *Uttara-kāṇḍas*—cannot all be assigned the same date. No secure reference to Hanuman as Rāma’s monkey ally occurs in the *Ṛgveda*, *Atharvaveda*, Brāhmaṇas, or early Upaniṣads. Attempts to extract him from Vedic words or wind mythology are modern reconstruction, not attestation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Hanuman is the eloquent minister of Sugrīva who discovers Sītā in Laṅkā and becomes Rāma’s indispensable scout, messenger, warrior, and devotee. His later religious identity combines three complementary paradigms: limitless strength, disciplined celibacy, and total devotional service.

### Names

- **हनुमत्, Hanumat; nominative हनुमान्, Hanumān** — the normal Sanskrit forms. The traditional grammatical analysis is *hanu* (“jaw”) plus possessive *-mat*: “having a [prominent or injured] jaw.” The childhood story explaining the name through Indra’s blow is explicit in later epic material.  
  **Tier:** securely attested epic name; the explanatory myth may be secondary etiology.
- **Añjaneya / Āñjaneya** (“son of Añjanā”), especially southern India; Telugu ఆంజనేయుడు, Kannada ಆಂಜನೇಯ.  
  **Tier:** later canonical and regional cult title.
- **Anjaniputra, Anjanisuta** — “son of Añjanā.”
- **Vāyuputra, Mārutātmaja, Māruti, Pavanasuta, Pavanaputra** — “son of Vāyu/Wind.”  
  **Tier:** epic and later canonical.
- **Kesari-nandana / Kesarīsuta** — “son or delight of Kesarī,” his social father.
- **Mahāvīra** — “great hero”; prominent in northern worship.
- **Bajraṅgbalī / Bajrang Bali** — conventionally “the powerful one whose body is like *vajra*,” the thunderbolt or adamant. The vernacular form is associated with *vajrāṅga*, “adamant-bodied.”  
  **Tier:** medieval/early-modern vernacular title.
- **Saṅkaṭamocana** — “releaser from distress.”
- **Rāmadūta** — “Rāma’s messenger”; **Rāmadāsa** — “Rāma’s servant.”
- **Kapiśa/Kapīśvara** — “lord of monkeys,” although Sugrīva remains the political king in the epic.
- **Cirañjīvin** — an immortal or extraordinarily long-lived being.  
  **Tier:** later canonical systematization.
- **Pañcamukhī Hanumān** — five-faced Hanuman.  
  **Tier:** tantric and late-medieval/early-modern development, subsequently popularized through printed ritual literature.
- Regional forms include Tamil **Aṉumaṉ/Anuman** (அனுமன்), Kannada **Hanumanta**, Telugu **Hanumantudu**, Thai **Hanuman**, Khmer **Hanuman**, Indonesian/Javanese **Hanoman/Anoman**.

A proposed derivation from non-Indo-Aryan or Dravidian names has periodically appeared in scholarship, usually alongside hypotheses of a pre-Sanskritic monkey, yakṣa, or tribal hero cult. No pre-*Rāmāyaṇa* inscription, image, or vernacular text securely demonstrates such a prototype. It remains **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not established genealogy.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Vedic absence

The *Ṛgveda*, *Atharvaveda*, Brāhmaṇas, and early Upaniṣads know Vāyu, Maruts, Rudra, Indra, and numerous semi-divine beings, but not Hanuman as a named Rāma-associated figure. Similarity between Hanuman’s wind parentage and Vedic wind deities is evidence for later mythic construction, not evidence that Hanuman himself is Vedic.

The common designation “Hindu and Vedic pantheon” therefore requires qualification: Hanuman belongs to Hindu epic and post-epic religion; he is not a securely attested Vedic god.

### Sanskrit epics

1. **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa**, Sanskrit, layered c. fourth/third century BCE–third century CE; surviving manuscripts much later. The Baroda critical edition reconstructs an archetype from northern and southern recensions.

   - *Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa*: Hanuman, Sugrīva’s minister, meets Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa in an ascetic disguise. Rāma praises his flawless grammar and speech. Jāmbavān reminds him of his forgotten powers; he expands his body for the ocean crossing.
   - *Sundarakāṇḍa*: the fullest early Hanuman cycle—leap to Laṅkā; encounters with Maināka, Surasā, and Siṃhikā; defeat of Laṅkinī; discovery of Sītā; delivery of Rāma’s ring; destruction of the grove; capture; audience with Rāvaṇa; burning of Laṅkā; return with Sītā’s token and message. The online Sanskrit-text tradition preserves 68 *sargas* and 2,772 verses.
   - *Yuddhakāṇḍa*: bridge and war episodes; rescue missions and medicinal mountain. In the received text Suṣeṇa names four herbs—Viśalyakaraṇī, Sāvarṇyakaraṇī, Saṃdhānī, and Mṛtasañjīvanī/Sañjīvakaraṇī. Unable to identify them, Hanuman carries the mountain peak itself.
   - *Bāla-* and *Uttarakāṇḍa*: divine arrangements for the births of the *vānaras*; Hanuman’s childhood leap at the sun, Indra’s thunderbolt, Vāyu’s withdrawal, divine boons, and the curse causing him to forget his strength.  
     **Tier:** later strata of the received epic; the exact textual history remains disputed.

2. **Mahābhārata**, Sanskrit, approximately fourth century BCE–fourth century CE. In *Vana Parvan* Hanuman meets Bhīma. Both are sons of the wind-god; Hanuman humbles Bhīma by making him unable to lift his tail, reveals his expanded form, recounts the Rāma story, and promises to strengthen the Pāṇḍavas’ battle cries. Hanuman later appears on Arjuna’s banner.  
   **Tier:** securely attested epic cross-assimilation, though its relation to successive redactions is debated.

### Purāṇic and sectarian development

The *Bhāgavata*, *Viṣṇu*, *Brahma*, *Nārada/Nāradīya*, *Śiva*, *Skanda*, *Liṅga*, and *Bhaviṣya Purāṇas* contain scattered references or retellings of the Rāma cycle and Hanuman. Their relevant passages are neither uniform nor necessarily as old as each Purāṇa’s earliest core.

- Vaiṣṇava presentations stress service to Rāma/Vishnu.
- The *Śiva Purāṇa’s Śatarudrasaṃhitā* makes Hanuman a manifestation of Śiva/Rudra: Śiva’s generative power, aroused through Vishnu’s Mohinī form, is transmitted through Vāyu to Añjanā.
- A *Bhaviṣya Purāṇa* birth account has Kesarī undertake Śiva worship; Śiva and Vāyu enter him before his union with Añjanā.
- Later sources can describe Hanuman as jointly embodying Śiva, Vāyu, and even Vishnu.  
  **Tier:** sectarian systematization, not the genealogy of the oldest epic.

The *Devīmāhātmya* does not supply a major early Hanuman dossier. Claims that it establishes a detailed Hanuman theology are unsupported.

### Vernacular Rāmāyaṇas

Hanuman changes substantially as the Rāma story enters regional languages:

- Kampaṉ’s Tamil *Irāmāvatāram*, conventionally twelfth century: develops the emotional and theological dimensions of his service.
- Kṛttivāsa’s Bengali *Rāmāyaṇa*, late medieval: incorporates regional expansions.
- Eknāth’s Marathi *Bhāvārtha Rāmāyaṇa*, sixteenth century.
- Tulsidas’s Awadhi *Rāmacaritamānasa*, begun traditionally in 1574/1575: makes Hanuman an exemplary bhakta whose strength is entirely oriented to Rāma.
- Thai *Ramakien*: Hanuman is a brilliant, frequently erotic warrior; this sharply differs from dominant north-Indian celibate Hanuman.
- Khmer, Lao, Burmese, Malay, Balinese, and Javanese tellings produce distinct courtly, theatrical, and local forms.  
  **Tier:** regional canonical traditions, not corruptions of a single original.

### Hanuman Cālīsā

The **हनुमान चालीसा, Hanumān Cālīsā**, in Awadhi, is traditionally attributed to Tulsidas (sixteenth–early seventeenth century). Its forty *caupāīs*, framed by opening and closing couplets, praise Hanuman as learned, powerful, Rāma’s messenger, son of Añjanā and Wind, remover of distress, and granter of success.

Attribution to Tulsidas is traditional and highly influential, but popular stories assigning a precise prison setting or miraculous first performance generally lack contemporary documentation. No authorial autograph is publicly established.  
**Tier:** early-modern devotional text; some biographical framing is later hagiography.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

In the epic Hanuman is a diplomat, grammarian, spy, messenger, shape-shifter, leaper, fighter, and healer’s courier. Later worship expands these roles:

- protector from danger, spirits, nightmares, disease, and planetary affliction;
- giver of courage, strength, fertility, discipline, eloquence, and success;
- guardian of thresholds, forts, villages, roads, temples, and wrestling grounds;
- paradigm of *dāsya-bhakti*, devotion through service;
- model of *brahmacarya*, disciplined celibacy.

Celibacy is dominant in north-Indian theology but is not universal. Southeast Asian traditions give Hanuman lovers; some Indian regional stories supply wives, including Suvarcalā. These are **regional or late traditions**, not a single pan-Hindu biography.

### Iconography

Identifying features are simian face, tail, muscular anthropomorphic body, and frequently a mace. Principal forms include:

1. Kneeling beside Rāma and Sītā with joined palms—the servant-devotee.
2. Standing with mace—the protective warrior.
3. Flying while carrying the medicinal mountain.
4. Tearing open his chest to reveal Rāma and Sītā in his heart—a popular devotional image whose monumental visual prominence is relatively late.
5. Trampling demons in tantric guardian form.
6. **Pañcamukhī Hanuman**, combining Hanuman with heads conventionally identified as Narasiṃha, Garuḍa, Varāha, and Hayagrīva.

Narrative *Rāmāyaṇa* reliefs are attested on temples by the sixth–eighth centuries. An independent late-ninth-century Chola image has been proposed. The earliest securely dated inscribed independent Hanuman image is the colossal Khajuraho figure dated 922 CE. This is crucial: literary Hanuman is ancient, but securely dated independent cult images are much later.

The gada is now nearly indispensable but is not the essence of every early depiction. Tuesday and Saturday observance, red or orange sindūr, oil, sweets, gram, and garlands are prominent in living north-Indian cult; they should not be projected backward into the epic period.

## 4. Genealogy and Divine Relations

### Competing genealogies

| Tradition | Parentage or nature | Evidentiary tier |
|---|---|---|
| Epic mainstream | Mother Añjanā; Kesarī as husband/social father; Vāyu as divine father | Early/later epic |
| Divine-*vānara* creation | Gods generate heroic allies for Rāma | Epic theological framing |
| Śaiva | Hanuman is Rudra/Śiva’s manifestation, power, or son | Purāṇic sectarian systematization |
| Composite | Śiva’s energy conveyed by Vāyu into Añjanā | Later Purāṇic synthesis |
| Vaiṣṇava, especially Madhva traditions | Hanuman is Vāyu’s first major manifestation, followed by Bhīma and Madhva | Medieval learned theology |
| Regional marriage traditions | Suvarcalā or another woman as spouse | Regional/late; conflicts with dominant celibate theology |

### Documented interactions with named divine or superhuman beings

The following covers the substantive named interactions in the principal epic and major later cycles; innumerable local dramas and temple legends make literal exhaustiveness impossible.

- **Rāma:** recognizes Hanuman’s eloquence; commissions the search; gives him the ring; embraces and rewards him after the mission. *Rāmāyaṇa*, *Kiṣkindhā–Yuddha* books; epic/canonical.
- **Sītā:** Hanuman finds her, proves his identity through Rāma’s ring and private memories, offers rescue, receives her jewel and message. *Sundarakāṇḍa*; epic/canonical.
- **Lakṣmaṇa:** Hanuman carries the medicinal mountain needed to revive him. *Yuddhakāṇḍa*; epic/canonical.
- **Vāyu:** divine father; withdraws the wind after Indra injures the child Hanuman. *Uttarakāṇḍa* childhood narrative; later epic.
- **Indra:** strikes the child with the thunderbolt, injuring his jaw, then grants protection or boons. *Uttarakāṇḍa*; later epic.
- **Sūrya:** mistaken for fruit in childhood; in later accounts becomes Hanuman’s teacher while moving through the sky. Later epic/Purāṇic elaboration.
- **Brahmā:** releases or protects Hanuman and grants boons; Hanuman strategically submits to Brahmā’s weapon in Laṅkā. Epic/canonical.
- **Agni:** fire does not injure Hanuman’s burning tail, attributed to Sītā’s virtue, Rāma, and Vāyu’s friendship. *Sundarakāṇḍa* 53–55; epic/canonical.
- **Varuṇa, Yama, Kubera, Śiva and other gods:** supply childhood boons in expanded accounts. *Uttarakāṇḍa* and later retellings; later canonical.
- **Sugrīva:** Hanuman serves as minister, introduces him to Rāma, and supports his alliance and kingship. *Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa*; epic/canonical.
- **Jāmbavān:** reminds Hanuman of his forgotten strength and prompts the ocean leap. *Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa* 65–67; epic/canonical.
- **Maināka:** the mountain rises from the ocean and offers rest in gratitude to Vāyu; Hanuman politely touches it and continues. *Sundarakāṇḍa* 1; epic/canonical.
- **Surasā:** tests Hanuman by demanding he enter her mouth; he outwits her by changing size. *Sundarakāṇḍa* 1; epic/canonical.
- **Siṃhikā:** seizes Hanuman’s shadow; he enters and kills her. *Sundarakāṇḍa* 1; epic/canonical.
- **Laṅkinī:** guardian of Laṅkā whom Hanuman defeats; she recognizes a prophecy of the city’s destruction. *Sundarakāṇḍa* 3; epic/canonical.
- **Rāvaṇa:** Hanuman confronts and warns him, suffers the sentence of the burning tail, then burns much of Laṅkā. *Sundarakāṇḍa* 49–54; epic/canonical.
- **Indrajit:** captures Hanuman with the Brahmāstra; later wounds Lakṣmaṇa, prompting Hanuman’s herb mission. *Sundara-* and *Yuddhakāṇḍas*; epic/canonical.
- **Vibhīṣaṇa:** argues that an envoy may not lawfully be killed and later fights beside Hanuman’s allies. *Sundarakāṇḍa*; epic/canonical.
- **Bhīma:** half-brother through Vāyu; Hanuman humbles, instructs, blesses, and embraces him. *Mahābhārata*, *Vana Parvan* 145–150; epic cross-tradition.
- **Arjuna:** Hanuman occupies his banner in the Kurukṣetra war. *Mahābhārata* tradition; epic/canonical. Later tales of a bridge-building contest with Arjuna and Krishna are post-epic elaborations.
- **Kṛṣṇa:** implicated through Hanuman’s presence on Arjuna’s banner; later stories make Krishna arbitrate Hanuman’s contest with Arjuna. Epic association/later accretion.
- **Śiva:** later texts make Hanuman his son, portion, or incarnation rather than merely an interacting deity. *Śiva Purāṇa* and allied texts; sectarian systematization.
- **Vishnu/Mohinī:** in Śaiva birth narratives, Mohinī’s beauty occasions Śiva’s emission, eventually producing Hanuman. Later Purāṇic syncretism.
- **Narasiṃha, Garuḍa, Varāha, Hayagrīva:** fused iconographically into Pañcamukhī Hanuman. Tantric/late systematization, not an epic meeting.
- **Śani:** popular legends say Hanuman rescues or subdues Saturn, who promises to spare his devotees. Late folklore; no early epic basis.
- **Ahirāvaṇa/Mahirāvaṇa:** underworld adversary slain during the rescue of Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa. Regional and late *Rāmāyaṇa* cycles; absent from the critical *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The childhood leap at the sun

The infant sees the rising sun as fruit and leaps toward it. Indra strikes him with the thunderbolt; he falls and injures his jaw. Vāyu, enraged, withdraws air from the cosmos. The gods placate him and grant the child protections and powers. Sages later curse Hanuman to forget his strength until reminded, explaining why he initially fails to volunteer for the Laṅkā leap.

**Development:** The story combines name etiology, divine legitimation, and a narrative explanation for latent power. Its familiar full form belongs to later epic and Purāṇic development, not to the oldest recoverable narrative core.

### Meeting Rāma

Disguised as an ascetic, Hanuman approaches Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa for Sugrīva. His polished Sanskrit so impresses Rāma that he remarks that no one ignorant of the Veda and grammar could speak so faultlessly. Hanuman mediates the alliance between Rāma and Sugrīva.

**Function:** It establishes that the “monkey” is also an ideal diplomat and master of disciplined speech.

### Sundarakāṇḍa: leap, discovery, and burning of Laṅkā

Jāmbavān restores Hanuman’s awareness of his power. Hanuman enlarges himself and leaps across the sea. He respectfully declines Maināka’s hospitality, passes Surasā’s test by alternately expanding and becoming minute, and kills Siṃhikā. Entering Laṅkā in a small form, he defeats its guardian Laṅkinī.

After searching Rāvaṇa’s palaces he finds Sītā in the aśoka grove. He first recites Rāma’s story from concealment, then presents Rāma’s ring. Sītā refuses his offer to carry her away: the rescue must vindicate Rāma, and she doubts that the army could follow. She gives Hanuman a token and private message.

Hanuman destroys the grove to test the enemy. Indrajit employs the Brahmāstra; Hanuman allows himself to be bound so he can see Rāvaṇa. Vibhīṣaṇa prevents his execution as an envoy. Rāvaṇa orders his tail burned; Hanuman escapes, uses the fire to burn Laṅkā, then fears he has killed Sītā. Reassured that she survives, he returns.

This self-reproach is significant: the epic does not portray him as mechanically infallible but as a powerful agent capable of anger, reflection, and correction.

### The mountain of herbs

When Lakṣmaṇa and other warriors are gravely wounded, Suṣeṇa directs Hanuman to a Himalayan herb mountain. Unable to distinguish the required luminous plants—or because the herbs conceal themselves—Hanuman uproots and carries the peak. The medicines restore the warriors.

Popular retellings reduce the four herbs to a single “Sañjīvanī” plant and often conflate separate healing journeys. The mountain-bearing image is canonical, but many precise modern geographic identifications are folklore or pilgrimage claims.

### Bhīma and Hanuman

Bhīma encounters an aged monkey whose tail blocks his route. Unable to move it, he recognizes a superior power. Hanuman reveals their common father Vāyu, teaches humility, shows a vast form, and promises aid in the coming war.

**Function:** The episode integrates the two epics and subordinates raw strength to disciplined, older strength.

### Pañcamukhī Hanuman and Mahirāvaṇa

Late and regional narratives send Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa into an underworld controlled by Mahirāvaṇa/Ahirāvaṇa. Hanuman assumes five faces to extinguish lamps in different directions and kill the demon.

The episode is frequently mislabeled online as part of the *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa* or *Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*. Its textual distribution is late and variable; the five-headed form also has an independent tantric ritual history.  
**Tier:** late accretion and regional cycle.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

Independent Hanuman worship appears materially much later than the epic. Sixth–eighth-century temple programs contain narrative *Rāmāyaṇa* scenes; by the ninth–tenth centuries independent images appear. The inscribed Khajuraho image of 922 CE is a firm landmark.

Major later cult centers include:

- **Sankat Mochan, Varanasi:** traditionally associated with Tulsidas; present institutional history is early-modern and later.
- **Hanuman Garhi, Ayodhya:** a major fortress-temple and monastic establishment; its modern political importance should not be projected into antiquity.
- **Salasar Balaji, Rajasthan:** an eighteenth-century regional pilgrimage center.
- **Mehandipur Balaji, Rajasthan:** prominent for healing and spirit-affliction rites; ethnographically living, not ancient epic practice.
- **Jakhoo, Shimla; Namakkal Anjaneyar, Tamil Nadu; Hampi and Karnataka sites; Nepalese royal gateways and shrines:** historically distinct regional cults.

Ritual commonly includes recitation of the *Hanumān Cālīsā*, *Sundarakāṇḍa*, mantras, oil and sindūr offerings, sweets, gram, coconuts, flags, and circumambulation. Tuesdays and Saturdays are especially important in northern practice. Protective *kavacas* and tantric manuals invoke multiheaded forms and install mantra “armor” upon the body.

There is no sound evidence that human sacrifice or institutional temple prostitution formed a characteristic Hanuman cult. Exorcistic rites and physical restraint have been documented at some modern healing shrines, but they must not be generalized to all Hanuman worship.

### Wrestlers and akhāṛās

Joseph Alter’s ethnography documents a Hanuman shrine in virtually every north-Indian wrestling *akhāṛā*. Wrestlers model themselves on his:

- *śakti/bala*—energy and strength;
- *brahmacarya*—celibacy and conservation of semen;
- *bhakti*—submission to deity and guru.

The association is historically powerful but its institutional form is chiefly medieval, early-modern, colonial, and modern; it is not documented in Vedic athletics. Wrestlers themselves normally conduct daily pūjā, while specialists may officiate at larger rites.

## 7. Historical Evolution

1. **Before the Sanskrit epics:** no secure Hanuman attestation. Hypothetical tribal, yakṣa, Dravidian, or animal-cult prototypes remain reconstructions.
2. **Epic formation, late first millennium BCE–early first millennium CE:** Hanuman emerges as an extraordinary *vānara*, Vāyu’s son, Sugrīva’s minister, and Rāma’s decisive helper.
3. **Early classical period:** the *Mahābhārata* incorporates him as Bhīma’s brother and Arjuna’s banner emblem; divine childhood material expands.
4. **Sixth–tenth centuries:** visual evidence becomes substantial; *Rāmāyaṇa* reliefs precede clearly independent cult images. The 922 Khajuraho statue anchors autonomous worship.
5. **Medieval period:** Purāṇic and sectarian texts identify him more explicitly with Rudra/Śiva or Vāyu; regional-language *Rāmāyaṇas* diversify him.
6. **Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries:** Tulsidas’s *Rāmacaritamānasa* and attributed Hanuman hymns elevate him as the paradigmatic north-Indian servant-devotee and protector.
7. **Early-modern to colonial period:** monastic, village, royal, and martial cults expand; Hanuman becomes central to *akhāṛā* masculinity and public physical culture.
8. **Nineteenth–twentieth centuries:** inexpensive prints, hymn booklets, calendars, theater, and eventually cinema and television standardize the mace-bearing, mountain-carrying, celibate devotee.
9. **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** global diaspora worship expands alongside nationalist and commercial appropriations; monumental statues and digitally circulated “ancient” claims proliferate.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Ramanand Sagar’s television *Ramayan* (1987–1988) strongly standardized modern visual and vocal expectations. Animation, comics, devotional cinema, televised serials, video games, bodybuilding culture, and mass-produced dashboard images have further globalized Hanuman.

The “chest-opening” Hanuman and enormous roadside statues now appear timeless but owe much of their saturation to modern print and monumental culture. Tantric Pañcamukhī images have likewise moved from specialized ritual settings into calendars, online shops, and household devotion.

### Political appropriation

The **Bajrang Dal**, founded in 1984 as a youth mobilization associated with the Vishva Hindu Parishad and the Ayodhya movement, takes its name from Bajrang Bali. Its slogan adapts Hanuman’s service to Rāma: “How can I rest before completing Rāma’s work?” This is a datable modern political deployment, not an ancient organization or inevitable expression of Hanuman devotion.

The orange “Angry Hanuman” graphic, created by artist Karan Acharya in 2015, replaced the familiar humble devotee with a glaring, combative head. Sangh Parivar organizations and commercial users widely appropriated it. Its visual idiom is **modern invention**, even though it draws on Hanuman’s older warrior aspect.

Hanuman’s names and slogans have appeared both in ordinary worship and in communal intimidation and violence. The documented political use must not be transferred indiscriminately to all devotees, nor may devotional antiquity be used to erase the appropriation.

### Sun Wukong comparison

Hanuman and the Chinese Sun Wukong share simian form, superhuman leaps, transformations, combat power, celestial confrontation, and service on a great mission. Ramayana materials demonstrably reached parts of China, and a thirteenth-century Quanzhou temple relief provides material evidence of such transmission.

Nevertheless, direct descent of Sun Wukong from Hanuman is **contested scholarly reconstruction**. Hu Shih and later comparativists argued for Indian influence; other scholars emphasize indigenous Chinese monkey lore, the water-monkey Wuzhiqi, Buddhist pilgrimage literature, and pre-Ming Monkey Pilgrim traditions. Similarity and cultural contact make influence plausible, but the evidence does not establish that the two are “the same god” or that Hanuman was Sun Wukong’s sole prototype.

## 9. Historiography and Contested Material

Important studies include Catherine Ludvik’s comparison of the Sanskrit epic and Tulsidas, Philip Lutgendorf’s cultural history *Hanuman’s Tale*, and Joseph Alter’s ethnography of wrestling. The Baroda critical edition and the Goldman translation remain essential for distinguishing the reconstructed epic text from later recensions.

Major disputes include:

- **Dating:** the *Rāmāyaṇa* is layered; dates assigned to the whole work cannot automatically date every Hanuman passage.
- **Origin:** tribal-totemic, Dravidian, yakṣa, and Vedic-wind hypotheses lack direct pre-epic evidence.
- **Species:** *vānara* is used for Hanuman’s forest people and became conventionally understood as monkey. Claims that it “really” meant only human forest tribes usually overstate the linguistic evidence.
- **Jaw etymology:** Sanskrit *hanu* + *-mat* is grammatically straightforward; the childhood injury story may be an etiological narrative developed from the name.
- **Śiva identity:** canonical for particular later Śaiva traditions, but absent as the controlling identity of the earliest epic Hanuman.
- **Celibacy:** dominant but not universal; Thai and other regional traditions preserve erotic Hanumans, while Indian regional traditions sometimes give him a spouse.
- **Pañcamukhī antiquity:** often advertised as primordial; surviving ritual texts and images indicate later tantric systematization.
- **Sañjīvanī geography:** modern claims identifying one botanical species or exact mountain are not established by the epic.
- **Sun Wukong:** possible influence, not proven identity.
- **Suppression:** individual shrines have suffered warfare, political conflict, colonial regulation, and communal violence, but evidence does not support a single continuous historical campaign specifically to eradicate Hanuman worship.
- **Occult/neopagan claims:** modern Western occult appropriations exist, but Hanuman has no historically significant Renaissance Hermetic or Golden Dawn dossier comparable to Greco-Egyptian gods. Assertions of an ancient Western “Hanuman current” are modern invention.

| Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries | Traditional period of the *Hanumān Cālīsā* and other Tulsidas-attributed Hanuman works. |
| Early-modern period | Growth of independent temples, protective rites, *akhāṛā* devotion, and tantric multiheaded forms. |
| Nineteenth century | Print culture disseminates Cālīsā, *Sundarakāṇḍa*, ritual manuals, and standardized images. |
| Colonial–postcolonial period | Hanuman becomes a central model in organized wrestling and nationalist physical culture. |
| 1984 | Bajrang Dal founded; Hanuman’s Bajrang Bali identity adopted for political mobilization. |
| 1987–1988 | Sagar’s televised *Ramayan* profoundly shapes mass-media representation. |
| 2015 onward | Karan Acharya’s “Angry Hanuman” circulates commercially and politically. |
| Present | Extensive worship across South and Southeast Asia and the diaspora; continued vernacular performance, pilgrimage, political appropriation, and digital reinvention. |
