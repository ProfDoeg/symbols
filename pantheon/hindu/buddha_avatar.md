# The Buddha as avatar: Research Dossier

**Class:** A male divine descent (*avatāra*, अवतार) of Viṣṇu in Purāṇic and later Vaiṣṇava traditions; alternatively a divinely generated deluder (*māyāmoha*), a partial or empowered incarnation in later theological classifications, or no avatar at all in Buddhist, Ambedkarite, and some Hindu traditions.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest surviving textual layer that identifies “Buddha” with a manifestation of Viṣṇu is Purāṇic, not Vedic. *Matsya Purāṇa* 47.246 and 54.19 may preserve the earliest formulation, within a textual core conventionally dated approximately 200–500 CE, but the Buddha verses are suspected interpolations because parallel passages in the *Vāyu* and *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇas* name Kṛṣṇa instead. The earliest comparatively secure, extensive witness is therefore *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.17–18, whose principal contents are generally placed between the late third and early sixth centuries CE. Before that period there is no securely attested Buddha-avatar in the Ṛgveda, Atharvaveda, Brāhmaṇas, early Upaniṣads, the critical core of the *Mahābhārata*, or Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa*. [Bradley S. Clough, “The Ambivalence of the Hindus”](https://academic.oup.com/jhs/article/14/2/177/6219682)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[Syncretistic identification; approximately third–sixth centuries CE onward]** The Hindu Buddha-avatar is the result of identifying a Buddha—usually, but not always unambiguously, Śākyamuni/Gautama—with a descent or emanation of Viṣṇu. This identification is not part of the early Buddhist record of Gautama Buddha and should not be retrojected into his lifetime. Nor is every Purāṇic “Buddha” a biographically coherent representation of Siddhārtha Gautama. The sources variously make him:

- a benign ascetic who establishes *dharma;
- the destroyer of demons;
- Viṣṇu’s embodied power of delusion;
- a teacher sent to detach demons from Vedic authority so that the gods may defeat them;
- a compassionate opponent of animal sacrifice;
- a Kali-age teacher of an inferior or deceptive doctrine;
- a full divine form of Viṣṇu;
- an *aṃśāvatāra*, partial descent;
- a *śaktyāveśāvatāra*, a pre-existing being temporarily invested with divine power;
- or, in Buddhist and Ambedkarite classifications, an awakened human teacher who is explicitly **not** Viṣṇu.

These are competing historical constructions, not parts of a single uncontested doctrine.

### Names

**Buddha — बुद्ध.** Sanskrit *buddha*, “awakened,” “understood,” or “enlightened,” is the past passive participle of √*budh*, “to wake, notice, understand.” It was a title before it functioned as a quasi-personal name. The corresponding Pāli form is *Buddha*. The Purāṇic etymological play in *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.18 derives the followers’ designation from the teacher’s commands *budhyadhvam*, “understand/awaken,” and their response *budhyate*, “it is understood.” This is sectarian wordplay, not historical linguistics. [*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.18](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/vp/vp092.htm)

**Buddhāvatāra — बुद्धावतार.** “The Buddha descent/incarnation.” This is a descriptive compound used in later discourse; early Purāṇic passages more commonly narrate the manifestation rather than consistently employ a fixed proper name.

**Māyāmoha — मायामोह.** “Delusion through māyā,” “one whose supernatural power is delusion,” or the personified Great Delusion. In *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.17–18 this being arises from Viṣṇu’s body and assumes successive ascetic forms. Later theology sometimes distinguishes Māyāmoha from a full incarnation, but the Purāṇa itself says that he issues bodily from Viṣṇu; the later ontological demotion must not be read back into the passage.

**Śākyamuni — शाक्यमुनि.** “Sage of the Śākyas,” the normal Buddhist designation of Gautama. Purāṇic accounts do not consistently use it, and some later interpreters distinguish “the Purāṇic Buddha” from Śākyamuni because their parents and narratives differ.

**Gautama/Gotama.** A clan or lineage name of the historical Buddha. Its appearance in later Hindu sources helps identify the avatar with the Buddhist founder, but “Buddha” alone does not always prove that identification.

**Sugata — सुगत.** “Well-gone” or “gone to the good,” a major Buddhist epithet later incorporated into Sanskrit lexica and Hindu iconographic descriptions. Its presence demonstrates shared nomenclature, not two historically proven Buddhas.

**Tathāgata — तथागत.** A central Buddhist epithet, conventionally interpreted as “thus-gone” or “thus-come.” It is not a defining early name of the Purāṇic avatar, though later Hindu treatments may appropriate Buddhist titles.

**Jina — जिन**, “conqueror,” and **Arhat — अर्हत्**, “worthy one,” are titles shared with or associated with Buddhist and Jain ascetic traditions. *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.18 polemically derives *Arhata* from Māyāmoha’s statement “you are worthy.” The story deliberately conflates and differentiates Jain and Buddhist opponents through two costumes: naked/peacock-feathered and red-robed.

**Añjana-suta — अञ्जनसुत**, “son of Añjanā.” The received reading of *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 1.3.24 describes the Buddha as Añjana’s son in the Kīkaṭa country. This conflicts with Buddhist traditions in which Siddhārtha is the son of Śuddhodana and Māyā/Mahāmāyā. The verse reads:

> ततः कलौ संप्रवृत्ते सम्मोहाय सुरद्विषाम् ।  
> बुद्धो नाम्नाञ्जनसुतः कीकटेषु भविष्यति ॥

“Then, when the Kali age has begun, one named Buddha, son of Añjana, will arise among the Kīkaṭas to delude the enemies of the gods.” [Sanskrit text, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 1.3.24](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/bhagavata-purana-sanskrit/d/doc1240239.html)

Some editions or faulty digital segmentations produce *jana-suta*, “son of a person/people”; the familiar traditional reading is *añjana-sutaḥ*.

**Kīkaṭa.** A region already named in Ṛgvedic geography but not securely identical in every period with Gayā. Later Vaiṣṇava exegesis identifies the *Bhāgavata’s* Kīkaṭa with Gayā/Bihar. That geographical equation is traditional, not established merely by the verse.

### Numerical title: “ninth avatar”

**[Later learned systematization]** “Viṣṇu’s ninth avatar” presupposes a stabilized Daśāvatāra sequence. Earlier lists vary in number, order, and membership. Purāṇic lists did not become substantially standardized around ten principal descents—including Buddha—until approximately 800 CE. “Ninth” is consequently not the earliest identity of this figure.

Common later order:

1. Matsya  
2. Kūrma  
3. Varāha  
4. Narasiṃha  
5. Vāmana  
6. Paraśurāma  
7. Rāma  
8. Kṛṣṇa  
9. Buddha  
10. Kalki

Alternatives replace Buddha with Balarāma, treat Kṛṣṇa as the source of incarnations rather than one numbered avatar, or include both Balarāma and Buddha in longer lists.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Vedic and epic negative evidence

**[Secure negative finding]** No Buddha-avatar appears in the Saṃhitās, Brāhmaṇas, or early Upaniṣads. The word forms derived from √*budh* occur in Vedic Sanskrit in their ordinary senses of waking or perceiving, but they do not designate Gautama Buddha or a Viṣṇu incarnation.

Neither the *Ṛgveda* nor Atharvaveda supplies a prophetic reference to the Buddhist founder. Claims that a Vedic occurrence of *buddha* proves the avatar’s antiquity confuse a common verbal form with a proper name.

**[Secure negative finding with textual qualification]** The older epic strata know Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa’s manifestations but do not securely contain the familiar Buddha-as-ninth-avatar system. The *Harivaṃśa*, a supplement transmitted with the *Mahābhārata*, contains later avatar-list material, including Buddha in some received recensions. Such passages belong to the developing epic-Purāṇic tradition and cannot be assigned automatically to the earliest epic.

The Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* does not document Buddha as Viṣṇu’s avatar. A verse in *Ayodhyākāṇḍa* comparing a *nāstika* with a “Buddha” has long been disputed textually and semantically and, even if retained, does not furnish a Daśāvatāra doctrine.

### *Matsya Purāṇa*

**[Probable earliest Purāṇic layer; interpolation disputed]** *Matsya Purāṇa* 47.246 describes Buddha as a divinely splendid, lotus-eyed ascetic associated with establishing *dharma* and destroying demons; 54.19 salutes him as peaceful. Its core is often dated 200–500 CE. However, because parallel verses in the *Vāyu* and *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇas* describe Kṛṣṇa in the same language, scholars have proposed that “Buddha” replaced Kṛṣṇa in a later redaction. The passage is evidence for the received *Matsya*, but not an uncontested terminus for the idea’s origin. [Clough, pp. 3–4](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350808629_The_Ambivalence_of_the_Hindus_The_Buddha_as_Avatara_of_Visnu_in_the_Mahapuranas_and_Beyond)

### *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.17–18

**[Secure early Purāṇic elaboration; late third–early sixth centuries CE for the principal text]** After the Daityas defeat the gods, the gods appeal to Viṣṇu. A being called Māyāmoha arises from Viṣṇu’s body and approaches Daityas performing austerities on the Narmadā. First appearing naked, shaven, and carrying peacock feathers, he persuades one body of demons into an Arhata-like rejection of the Vedas. He then assumes red garments and a gentle manner, condemns animal slaughter, teaches that the world is unsupported and bound through mistaken knowledge, and creates Bauddhas through commands built on √*budh*. Once deprived of Vedic “armor,” the demons can be defeated by the gods.

The narrative preserves recognizable Jain and Buddhist markers but polemically blends them. It also accurately notices Buddhist objections to sacrificial killing while representing Buddhist teaching as divinely designed deception. [*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.18](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/vp/vp092.htm)

### *Bhaviṣya Purāṇa*

**[Later canonical elaboration; approximately sixth–eighth centuries, with much later strata]** The *Pratisargaparvan* assigns the Buddha to the Kali age and develops confused genealogies in which Gautama, Śākyamuni, Śuddhodana, and Śākyasiṃha become successive figures or kings. This is not reliable biography; it is evidence of Purāṇic efforts to domesticate Buddhist names within dynastic chronology.

### *Agni Purāṇa* 16 and 49

**[Later canonical elaboration; broadly seventh–eleventh centuries]** Chapter 16 repeats the gods’ defeat and Viṣṇu’s manifestation as Buddha/Māyāmoha to delude the Daityas. The passage presents the resulting Buddhists as *arhats* whose abandonment of Vedic practice makes defeat possible.

Chapter 49 is iconographic. In the received translation, Buddha/Sugata belongs among Viṣṇu’s forms and is represented in the visual vocabulary of a calm ascetic or Buddha image. Because the *Agni Purāṇa* is layered, its narrative and iconographic chapters need not be contemporary. [*Agni Purāṇa* 49](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-agni-purana/d/doc1083137.html)

### *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*

**[Later canonical elaboration; commonly eighth–tenth centuries]**

- **1.3.24:** Buddha, son of Añjana, will appear among the Kīkaṭas in Kali to delude the enemies of the gods.
- **2.7.37:** Buddha confounds those unfit for Vedic sacrifice through *upadharma/aupadharmya*, a subordinate, counterfeit, or non-normative religion.
- **6.18.19:** In a prayer to the incarnations, Buddha is asked to protect against the negligence or error of heretical multitudes.
- **11.4.22–23:** Buddha appears in the sequence of divine manifestations associated with Kali-age delusion.

This text became especially influential in Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism, but it does not present a biography corresponding neatly to Buddhist scripture.

### Other Purāṇas and related texts

**[Later canonical and regional elaborations]**

- *Garuḍa Purāṇa* 1.1, 2.30.37, 3.15.26: brief list references and Kali-age delusion.
- *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71: Buddha in an avatar sequence.
- *Nārada/Nāradīya Purāṇa* 2.72: Buddha in the ten-avatar scheme.
- *Varāha Purāṇa*: standard Daśāvatāra lists in its later medieval form.
- *Padma Purāṇa* 1.75.91, 2.32.43, 6.71, 6.197, 6.235–236, 7.6.96, 7.11.93: an exceptionally ambivalent Buddha—deluder, preserver of heretical texts, preserver of Vedic meaning, compassionate opponent of killing, cosmic deity, master of *prajñāpāramitā*, wish-granting savior.
- *Skanda Purāṇa*, especially later Vaiṣṇava māhātmya strata: Buddha deludes asuras through specious reasoning.
- *Bṛhat Parāśara Horāśāstra* 2: in later astrological avatar theory, Buddha is connected with Mercury (*Budha*). The near-homophony Buddha/Budha must not be mistaken for an original Vedic identity.
- Pāñcarātra texts of approximately 600–850 CE include Buddha among Viṣṇu’s manifestations, although lists and classifications vary.

### Jayadeva’s *Gītagovinda*

**[Medieval devotional reinterpretation; late twelfth century]** The opening Daśāvatāra hymn praises Keśava in Buddha form:

> निन्दसि यज्ञविधेरहह श्रुतिजातं  
> सदयहृदयदर्शितपशुघातम् ।  
> केशव धृतबुद्धशरीर जय जगदीश हरे ॥

“You condemn—alas—the body of revelation prescribing sacrifice, your compassionate heart having beheld the slaughter of animals. O Keśava who assumed the body of Buddha, victory to you, Lord of the world, Hari.”

Here deception recedes. Compassion and opposition to animal killing become the avatar’s defining action. Jayadeva’s poem dates to the courtly culture of Bengal around the turn of the twelfth to thirteenth century and exerted enormous influence in Odisha and eastern Indian devotion. [GRETIL Sanskrit text](https://tylergneill.github.io/gretil-mirror/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_jayadeva-gItagovinda.htm); [Oxford Bibliographies](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62357/reference-article-abstract/554514874)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and actions

**[Early Purāṇic]**

- divine delusion;
- withdrawal of Vedic protection from the gods’ enemies;
- strategic restoration of divine supremacy;
- criticism or abolition of animal sacrifice;
- production or explanation of non-Vedic religions;
- destruction of demons, directly or through their loss of ritual merit.

**[Later devotional]**

- compassion (*dayā*, *karuṇā*);
- nonviolence (*ahiṃsā*);
- contemplative power;
- peacefulness;
- protection of devotees;
- preservation or re-establishment of *dharma*.

These domains remain contradictory. A compassionate savior and a teacher who intentionally leads hearers toward ruin are both textually attested.

### Form

The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa’s* Māyāmoha has two costumes:

1. naked, shaven, carrying peacock feathers—coded as Jain or broadly śramaṇa;
2. red/ochre-robed, gentle, and soft-spoken—coded as Buddhist.

Later Daśāvatāra art usually adopts Buddhist iconography rather than depicting the grotesque deceiver:

- shaven head or stylized snail-shell curls;
- *uṣṇīṣa*, cranial protuberance;
- elongated earlobes;
- monastic robe;
- lotus seat;
- meditation posture;
- *dhyāna*, *abhaya*, teaching, or earth-touching gesture;
- calm, downcast expression.

In some Hindu avatar panels Buddha is standing and may hold a staff. An 1872–73 Archaeological Survey report describes a Buddha-avatar figure “holding a staff diagonally,” evidence that regional Hindu iconography did not always reproduce standard monastic Buddha types. [ASI report](https://www.discoveringbuddha.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/ASI-Vol-8-Report-for-1872-73-twelve-Bengal-Provinces-Beglar-Cunningham-1878.pdf)

### Earliest images and identification problems

The earliest surviving anthropomorphic Buddha images securely dated by inscription belong to Mathura from 129 CE onward; contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous production occurred in Gandhāra and the Swat region. These are Buddhist images, not evidence that Buddha was already worshipped as Viṣṇu. [British Museum guide](https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/ancient-india-living-traditions/ancient-india-living-traditions-large-print-guide)

A Buddha figure in a Vaiṣṇava avatar series is materially evidence for the Hindu identification; an isolated Buddha statue ordinarily is not. Context—neighboring avatars, inscription, temple program, or inventory—is indispensable.

### Associations not securely attested

No stable early Hindu source assigns the Buddha-avatar a distinctive weapon, mount, consort, sacred animal, plant, color, weekday, or independent planet. Later astrology’s association with Mercury arises largely from *Budha* rather than a secure early Buddha-avatar cult. Claims of a special “Buddha-avatar mantra,” gemstone, occult seal, or tantric weapon should therefore be treated as later ritual invention unless tied to a datable text.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to divine beings

### Parentage and origin

- **[Early Purāṇic]** *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.17: Māyāmoha/Buddha issues from Viṣṇu’s body. This is emanation, not human birth.
- **[Later canonical]** *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 1.3.24: Buddha is Añjana’s son.
- **[Buddhist canonical tradition]** Siddhārtha is the son of King Śuddhodana and Queen Māyā/Mahāmāyā; Prajāpatī Gautamī raises him after Māyā’s death.
- **[Later Hindu identification]** Some Purāṇas, including received *Agni Purāṇa* traditions, identify the avatar more directly with Śuddhodana’s son.
- **[Modern sectarian harmonization]** Assertions that Añjana’s son and Māyā’s son were two entirely different historical Buddhas attempt to eliminate the contradiction. No early independent evidence establishes such a second Buddha.

The avatar has no Purāṇically stable consort or children. Buddhist accounts of Yaśodharā and Rāhula belong to Gautama’s human biography; Hindu avatar lists rarely incorporate them.

### Every documented god–god interaction

The narrow corpus contains fewer individually named divine interactions than other avatar cycles. Collective interactions should not be inflated into named meetings.

- **Viṣṇu — emanation/identity.** Māyāmoha arises from Viṣṇu’s body and carries out his strategy against the Daityas. *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.17–18, principal text late third–early sixth century. **Tier: securely attested early Purāṇic formulation.**
- **Viṣṇu — incarnation.** Keśava assumes Buddha’s body to oppose sacrificial slaughter. Jayadeva, *Gītagovinda* 1, late twelfth century. **Tier: medieval devotional elaboration.**
- **The Devas collectively — strategic alliance.** After defeat, the gods seek Viṣṇu’s help; Buddha/Māyāmoha causes their enemies to abandon Vedic rites, enabling the gods’ victory. *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.17–18; *Agni Purāṇa* 16. **Tier: early and later canonical.** No individual god is consistently the avatar’s scene partner.
- **The Daityas/Asuras collectively — deception and instruction.** The avatar teaches them anti-Vedic or śramaṇa doctrines; after loss of ritual protection they are defeated. *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.18; *Agni Purāṇa* 16; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 1.3.24 and 2.7.37. **Tier: canonical Purāṇic cycle.**
- **Śiva/Rudra — indirect textual relation, not a meeting.** In *Padma Purāṇa* 6.235–236, Rudra answers Pārvatī’s question about *tāmasa* scriptures and includes Buddhist works. Buddha does not personally meet him in the scene. **Tier: late sectarian systematization.**
- **Pārvatī — indirect textual relation, not a meeting.** She asks Rudra to classify false or dark scriptures; the Buddha is only discussed. *Padma Purāṇa* 6.235–236, approximately twelfth–thirteenth-century received text. **Tier: late canonical polemic.**
- **Kalki — sequential, not interpersonal.** Buddha and Kalki occupy consecutive places in the Kali-age schema: Buddha helps bring about or marks religious confusion; Kalki ends the age. No early source narrates a meeting between them. **Tier: learned systematization.**
- **Kṛṣṇa/Keśava — identity through source theology, not a narrated meeting.** Jayadeva addresses Keśava as the one who “put on Buddha’s body.” **Tier: medieval devotional identification.**
- **Balarāma — list rivalry only.** Buddha and Balarāma replace one another in competing ten-avatar schemes; no myth narrates combat, conversation, kinship, or meeting between them. **Tier: learned classificatory alternative.**

**Absence finding:** No securely attested Purāṇic episode gives the Buddha-avatar a divine spouse, offspring, mount, servant, named demon opponent, or sustained personal encounter with Brahmā, Indra, Lakṣmī, Sarasvatī, Gaṇeśa, Skanda, or another individual deity.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The delusion of the Daityas

**[Early Purāṇic; fullest in *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.17–18]** The Daityas defeat the gods despite divine efforts because their Vedic observances furnish spiritual protection. Viṣṇu produces Māyāmoha. On the Narmadā, the latter finds the Daityas engaged in austerities for heaven and liberation. He offers a superior secret path.

As a naked ascetic he advances deliberately reversible propositions—an act may be duty or non-duty, conducive or not conducive to release, real or unreal—and creates Arhatas. As a red-robed ascetic he condemns sacrificial killing, describes existence as revolving through error and passion, and creates Bauddhas. The converts reject the Vedas, gods, Brahmins, sacrifice, ancestral rites, and householder duties. Their protection gone, the devas defeat them.

The story has at least three functions:

1. explaining the undeniable existence of Jain and Buddhist teaching without conceding an independent revelation;
2. subordinating rival religions by making them instruments of Viṣṇu;
3. defending Vedic social and sacrificial order while acknowledging the ethical force of nonviolence.

It is not a neutral description of either religion.

### The compassionate opponent of sacrifice

**[Medieval devotional reinterpretation]** In Jayadeva, Buddha condemns the sacrificial injunctions because his compassionate heart sees animal slaughter. The deception of demons disappears. The avatar becomes the means by which Viṣṇu corrects violent abuse of revelation.

Later Vaiṣṇava commentators split:

- Buddha rejected only a demonic or corrupt interpretation of the Vedas;
- Buddha temporarily rejected even genuine Vedic authority because stopping mass killing required it.

The latter view makes divine deception medicinal rather than punitive.

### Kali-age prophecy

**[Later canonical]** *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 1.3.24 casts the Buddha in future tense: he “will arise” in Kīkaṭa to delude the enemies of the gods. Within the narrative setting this is prophecy; historically it is a post-Buddhist text projecting a known figure into sacred chronology. Treating the verse as independently predictive reverses its textual dating.

### Cosmic and salvific Buddha

**[Late Purāṇic accretion]** *Padma Purāṇa* 6.71.275–278 calls Buddha eternal, dear to the world, Śrīdhara, god of gods, victorious through meditation, and lord of the Perfection of Wisdom. He grants happiness and appropriate desires. The passage borrows recognizably Buddhist *prajñāpāramitā* terminology while giving it a Vaiṣṇava theological frame. This is more than mere demon-delusion and demonstrates that no single linear path—from hostility to acceptance or acceptance to hostility—explains the corpus.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Independent cult

**[Material negative finding]** The Buddha-avatar developed no major, clearly separable pan-Indian Hindu cult comparable to those of Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Narasiṃha, or Vāmana. There is no securely documented ancient network of temples whose presiding deity was specifically “Viṣṇu as Buddha,” no distinct hereditary priesthood, and no securely attested sacrifice or oracle belonging exclusively to this avatar.

Most material presence occurs through:

- Daśāvatāra panels on Vaiṣṇava or ecumenical temples;
- manuscript painting;
- ivory or metal avatar sets;
- introductory hymns;
- recitation of Jayadeva;
- modern festival discourse around Buddha Pūrṇimā.

A Buddha image in a Hindu space is not automatically an avatar image, and Buddhist worship at Bodh Gayā is not evidence of a Hindu Buddha-avatar cult.

### Temple and image contexts

The early sixth-century Viṣṇu temple at Deogarh is commonly called the “Daśāvatāra Temple,” but this modern designation should not be used to prove that its original program displayed the now-standard ten, much less Buddha in ninth position. Its major surviving reliefs concern Viṣṇu, Nara-Nārāyaṇa, Gajendramokṣa, and Anantaśayana. [Smarthistory, Deogarh](https://smarthistory.org/dashavatara-temple-deogarh/)

Medieval and early-modern avatar friezes more securely include Buddha recognizable by monastic dress, seated meditation, or staff. Regional sequences may instead show Balarāma, Jagannātha, or another local form.

At Puri, Jayadeva’s *Gītagovinda* became part of Jagannātha’s ritual culture. This gives the Buddha verse a liturgical life, but the recipient of the temple service is Jagannātha, not an independently enthroned Buddha-avatar. [IGNCA, *Gīta Govinda*](https://ignca.gov.in/divisionss/cultural-informatics/gita-govinda/)

### Bodh Gayā and contested custody

The late nineteenth-century Mahābodhi dispute illustrates the political consequences of overlapping claims. Anagārika Dharmapāla and the Mahā Bodhi Society sought Buddhist control of the shrine then managed by a Śaiva mahant. Court testimony debated whether Hindu scriptures called Buddha an avatar and whether they prescribed his worship. The record contains the significant admission that the *Bhāgavata* named the Buddha-avatar but did not thereby enjoin a distinct Buddha cult. [*The Budh-Gaya Temple Case*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/84/The_Budh-Gaya_temple_case._H._Dharmapala_versus_Jaipal_Gir_and_others._%28Prosecution_under_sections_295%2C_296%2C_297%2C_143_%26_506_of_the_Indian_penal_code%29_%28IA_budhgayatempleca00dharrich%29.pdf)

### Sacrifice and disputed abuses

- **Animal sacrifice:** central to the myth as something Buddha condemns. The sources discuss its rejection, not sacrifice offered to Buddha.
- **Human sacrifice:** the dedicated search found no credible evidence for human sacrifice to the Buddha-avatar.
- **Temple prostitution:** no credible evidence connects it specifically with this figure.
- **Cult violence:** no independent Buddha-avatar cultic institution is documented as conducting violence. Polemical literary violence—the prediction of hellish or animal rebirths for Buddhists, and their defeat by the devas—is securely attested.
- **Forced conversion:** the myth narrates supernatural deception, not a documented human conversion campaign conducted by a Buddha-avatar priesthood.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before approximately 300 CE: absence

The Vedic and early epic record contains no Buddha-avatar. Buddhist communities venerated Gautama through relics, stupas, texts, symbols, and eventually anthropomorphic images without classifying him as Viṣṇu.

### Approximately 200–500 CE: first Purāṇic incorporation

The received *Matsya Purāṇa* offers a peaceful, dharma-establishing Buddha who also destroys demons, though interpolation remains possible. This is the earliest recoverable phase of explicit incorporation.

### Late third–sixth centuries: developed ambivalent theology

The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* provides the first extensive narrative. Incorporation is simultaneously elevation and subordination: Buddha comes from Viṣṇu, but Buddhist doctrine becomes a strategic deception. The narrative preserves both condemnation of animal killing and severe rejection of Buddhist social-religious practice.

### Sixth–eighth centuries: Kali-age placement

The *Bhaviṣya Purāṇa* and related strata place Buddha within Kali chronology. Lists remain unstable. The category *avatāra* itself becomes increasingly systematic; its noun-form terminology is not prominent in earlier Purāṇic strata.

### Approximately 800–1000: stabilization

The ten-avatar sequence becomes much more regular. The *Agni*, *Garuḍa*, and *Bhāgavata Purāṇas* establish Buddha as a Kali-age manifestation, usually a deluder of enemies of the gods. The *Bhāgavata* becomes especially influential in later Vaiṣṇava theology.

This consolidation overlaps with Buddhism’s institutional decline in much of India, but simple causal claims—“Hindus invented the avatar solely to destroy Buddhism” or “they adopted him solely through tolerance”—exceed the evidence. Scholarship has proposed both accommodation and appropriation. The texts themselves show praise and hostility in every broad period.

### Tenth–thirteenth centuries: standardization and devotional rehabilitation

As institutional Buddhism weakened across much of the subcontinent, later Purāṇas could treat Buddha as a familiar item in the avatar list while also granting him cosmic qualities. Jayadeva’s compassionate Buddha became one of the most memorable medieval formulations.

Buddhism’s Indian decline had multiple causes—changing patronage, institutional and economic transformations, competition and assimilation, regional political change, and destruction of monasteries during warfare. The Buddha-avatar doctrine was one part of Hindu-Buddhist boundary-making; it cannot by itself explain the decline.

### Early modern period

Daśāvatāra series circulated in manuscripts, performance, painting, ivory, and temple sculpture. Regional lists continued to vary. Buddha often functioned iconographically as “the seated ascetic between Kṛṣṇa and Kalki,” with little independent narrative cult.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Colonial philology, Buddhist revival, and disputes over Bodh Gayā made the identification politically charged. Hindu universalists presented it as proof of religious inclusiveness; Buddhist reformers often interpreted it as absorption or appropriation.

### 1956 to the present: Ambedkarite rejection

At the Nagpur conversion of 14 October 1956, B. R. Ambedkar’s twenty-two vows made rejection explicit:

- vow 4 rejects belief in divine incarnation;
- vow 5 rejects the claim that Buddha was Viṣṇu’s incarnation and calls it false propaganda.

This is not a marginal objection but a constitutive boundary of Navayāna/Ambedkarite identity. [Twenty-two vows in Hindi](https://www.baiae.org/22-vows-of-dr-ambedkar/)

Contemporary Ambedkarite Buddhists frequently interpret promotion of the ninth-avatar claim as Hinduization intended to reincorporate former Dalits into a Hindu social order. Ethnographic work records this concern and the modern political promotion of the avatar identification in Hindu nationalist settings. [“Negotiating Boundaries,” *Journal of Global Buddhism*](https://www.globalbuddhism.org/article/download/3840/4152)

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Hindu universalism and nationalism

**[Modern reinterpretation]** Modern Hindu presentations often detach Buddha from the Purāṇic demon-delusion narrative and portray him as Viṣṇu’s revelation of compassion, nonviolence, meditation, or reform. This owes more to Jayadeva, modern interreligious universalism, and selective reading than to *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 3.18 alone.

Hindu nationalist organizations have also invoked Buddha as part of a unified Hindu civilization. Ambedkarite critics contest that move as political appropriation. Neither “inclusion” nor “appropriation” is a neutral description; each identifies a historically situated interpretation.

### Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism and ISKCON

Modern Gauḍīya teaching commonly follows *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 1.3.24 and Jayadeva: Buddha is Kṛṣṇa/Viṣṇu appearing to stop animal killing and mislead atheists who misuse Vedic sacrifice. Some teachers distinguish an eternal divine Buddha-avatar from the historical Śākyamuni; others identify them. The distinction is a later harmonization, not a consensus of the Purāṇas.

### Buddhist and Ambedkarite response

Theravāda, Mahāyāna, and Vajrayāna sources classify Śākyamuni according to their own Buddhologies—human awakened teacher, transcendent Buddha, emanation body, or cosmic manifestation—but not as a subordinate incarnation of Viṣṇu. Some Buddhist cultures incorporate Viṣṇu differently: in Sri Lanka, Viṣṇu can be a guardian of Buddhism or a future Buddha. That reciprocal inclusion does not amount to Buddhist acceptance of the Hindu avatar claim.

### Occultism, Theosophy, and New Age material

Theosophical and New Age systems frequently speak of Buddha as an “avatar,” “world teacher,” “initiate,” or member of a hierarchy of masters. These uses generalize the Sanskrit word and are modern esoteric constructions, not continuations of the Purāṇic Buddha cycle.

The dedicated search found no historically substantial “Buddha-avatar” current in the Golden Dawn, European grimoire magic, chaos magic, or modern Western neopaganism. Modern occult charts that give him a planet, ray, chakra, sigil, or evolutionary office should be labeled modern invention unless a specific source history is supplied.

### Popular culture

Calendars, posters, comics, websites, television mythologies, games, and temple statuary usually present Buddha as the serene ninth figure in a fixed evolutionary-looking sequence. The once-popular modern claim that the ten avatars “predict biological evolution”—fish, amphibian/tortoise, mammal, half-man, dwarf, civilized human, enlightened human—is a nineteenth- or twentieth-century concordist reinterpretation, not an ancient explanation of the list.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### When was Buddha absorbed?

Older scholarship frequently placed the incorporation around the Gupta age, approximately 450–600 CE. More recent source-sensitive work emphasizes:

- the possible early *Matsya* witness;
- interpolation risk;
- the comparatively secure *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* narrative;
- continued variation before approximately 800;
- and ambivalence rather than a single movement from hostility to tolerance or the reverse.

### Accommodation versus subordination

Two major explanatory families have dominated:

1. **Accommodation/syncretism:** Brahmanical authors incorporated a powerful rival figure, acknowledging his sanctity and creating peaceful religious inclusion.
2. **Appropriation/subordination:** by making Buddha an instrument or fragment of Viṣṇu, authors denied Buddhism independent authority and explained its teachings as divine deception.

The primary sources support elements of both. The same corpus praises Buddha’s peace, compassion, meditation, and wisdom while calling his doctrine counterfeit and his followers doomed. Clough’s central historiographical contribution is to argue that the Purāṇic evidence is too internally diverse for a single-stage theory. [Clough](https://academic.oup.com/jhs/article/14/2/177/6219682)

### Is the avatar Gautama Buddha?

Evidence for identification includes the name Buddha, Buddhist dress, renunciation, nonviolence, critique of sacrifice, Buddhist philosophical language, and later references to Śuddhodana or Śākyamuni.

Evidence complicating it includes Añjana rather than Māyā as parent, Kīkaṭa rather than Lumbinī, conflation with Jain ascetics, supernatural production from Viṣṇu’s body, and invented dynastic genealogies.

The historically economical conclusion is that Purāṇic authors were appropriating and transforming the cultural figure of the Buddha and Buddhist/Jain institutions, not preserving an independent biography of a second verifiable “Buddha avatar.” The claim of two entirely separate Buddhas is a sectarian harmonization unsupported by independent early evidence.

### Buddha or Balarāma?

The alternatives arise from unstable avatar enumeration:

- Where Kṛṣṇa is counted as the eighth avatar, Buddha readily becomes ninth.
- Where Kṛṣṇa is treated as *svayaṃ bhagavān*, the source rather than an avatar, Balarāma may enter the numbered list.
- Śrīvaiṣṇava and some South Indian enumerations prefer Balarāma and omit Buddha.
- Other schemes contain both by using lists longer than ten.
- Jayadeva includes Buddha while treating Keśava/Kṛṣṇa as the bearer of all ten forms.

Neither candidate is universally “the original ninth,” because early sources do not attest one fixed, primordial list of ten.

### Suppression and violence

Purāṇic literary hostility toward Buddhists is documented. Historical claims that Brahmins collectively exterminated Indian Buddhism, or conversely that only Muslim conquest caused its disappearance, are overly monocausal. Local conflict, seizure or conversion of sites, loss of patronage, assimilation, warfare, and monastic-economic decline must be studied case by case. The avatar doctrine provided an ideological mechanism of incorporation; evidence that it functioned as a centrally coordinated program of forced conversion is lacking.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

- The Buddha-avatar occurs in the Ṛgveda as a named prophecy: **unsupported**.
- The standard ten-avatar list is Vedic and primordial: **unsupported**.
- *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 1.3.24 independently predicts Gautama before his birth: **text-historically untenable**.
- Añjana proves a second historical Buddha distinct from Gautama: **not independently established**.
- Buddha replaced Balarāma everywhere through one identifiable conspiracy: **unsupported**.
- The ten avatars were devised to encode Darwinian evolution: **modern concordism**.
- Every Buddha statue in a Hindu-controlled or shared site represents Viṣṇu: **iconographically invalid**.
- The Buddha-avatar had a major ancient independent temple cult: **not established**.
- Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or an ancient occult cult belonged to him: **no credible evidence found**.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Attestation or historical development |
|---|---|
| c. 1500–500 BCE | Vedic corpus composed and transmitted. No Buddha-avatar is attested. |
| c. fifth–fourth centuries BCE | Historical Gautama Buddha’s conventional scholarly period; early Buddhist community forms. No contemporary evidence identifies him with Viṣṇu. |
| third century BCE | Aśokan inscriptions and monuments establish imperial Buddhist patronage; still no Buddha-avatar identification. |
| first–second centuries CE | Anthropomorphic Buddha imagery develops in Mathura, Gandhāra, and related centers. These are Buddhist, not yet demonstrably Vaiṣṇava avatar images. |
| c. 200–500 CE | Core of the *Matsya Purāṇa*; received verses 47.246 and 54.19 include a peaceful, lotus-eyed Buddha who establishes dharma and destroys demons. Interpolation disputed. |
| late third–early sixth centuries | Main contents of the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*; 3.17–18 gives the first extensive, comparatively secure Māyāmoha narrative. |
| fourth–sixth centuries | Gupta-period religious interaction provides a broad historical setting for increasingly systematized Vaiṣṇava incarnation theology; it does not by itself date individual verses. |
| sixth–eighth centuries | *Bhaviṣya Purāṇa* strata associate Buddha with Kali and construct confused Buddha genealogies. |
| c. 600–850 | Pāñcarātra literature incorporates expanding manifestation lists. |
| seventh–eleventh centuries | *Agni Purāṇa* strata narrate Buddha’s delusion of the Daityas and prescribe iconographic forms. |
| c. 800 CE onward | Lists of ten principal avatars become substantially more standardized, usually including Buddha. |
| eighth–tenth centuries | *Garuḍa* and *Bhāgavata Purāṇas* consolidate Buddha as Kali-age deluder; *Bhāgavata* 1.3.24 names Añjana and Kīkaṭa. |
| tenth–twelfth centuries | *Varāha* and other later Purāṇas reproduce increasingly standard lists amid the decline of institutional Buddhism in much of India. |
| twelfth–thirteenth centuries | Received *Padma Purāṇa* combines severe sectarian polemic with exalted descriptions of Buddha as eternal, meditative, compassionate, and linked with *prajñāpāramitā*. |
| late twelfth century | Jayadeva’s *Gītagovinda* praises Keśava’s Buddha body for compassionately condemning animal slaughter. |
| medieval–early modern | Buddha becomes common in Daśāvatāra hymnody, paintings, ivory sets, sculpture, and performance, but rarely as an independent Hindu temple deity. |
| nineteenth century | European Indology compares Purāṇic and Buddhist sources; Hindu and Buddhist reformers debate whether avatar status is inclusion or appropriation. |
| 1890s | The Mahābodhi/Bodh Gayā custody dispute makes the Hindu avatar claim legally and politically salient. |
| 14 October 1956 | Ambedkar’s mass Buddhist conversion at Nagpur; vows 4–5 reject incarnation doctrine and specifically deny that Buddha is Viṣṇu’s avatar. |
| late twentieth century | Gauḍīya/ISKCON publications globalize the animal-sacrifice and delusion interpretations; Hindu universalists emphasize compassion. |
| twenty-first century | The identification remains common in Hindu Daśāvatāra discourse and is actively rejected in Buddhist and especially Ambedkarite discourse; political uses continue. |

## 11. Sources

### Primary and critical witnesses

The decisive primary materials are the Sanskrit *Matsya*, *Viṣṇu*, *Bhaviṣya*, *Agni*, *Bhāgavata*, *Garuḍa*, *Liṅga*, *Padma*, *Nārada*, *Varāha*, and *Skanda Purāṇas*; relevant Pāñcarātra lists; Jayadeva’s *Gītagovinda*; and the modern documentary record of the Mahābodhi dispute and Ambedkar’s vows.

The Ṛgveda, Atharvaveda, Brāhmaṇas, early Upaniṣads, critical *Mahābhārata*, and Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* are essential chiefly as controls: they do not establish a Vedic Buddha-avatar. Buddhist Nikāya/Āgama, Vinaya, biographical, and art-historical sources establish what Buddhist communities said about Gautama and thereby expose the transformations made by Purāṇic authors.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The Buddha-avatar is neither an unchanged Vedic god nor simply the historical Buddha under another name. He is a changing Hindu theological construction built around the cultural fact of Buddha and Buddhism. His earliest recoverable forms emerge in layered Purāṇic literature of the first millennium CE. Those forms are persistently ambivalent: divine yet deceptive, anti-Vedic yet produced by Viṣṇu, destructive of demons yet compassionate toward animals, marginal in independent cult yet indispensable to the standard avatar list. Medieval devotion elevated his compassion; modern Hindu universalism often softened his polemical role; Buddhist and Ambedkarite movements rejected the entire identification as a denial of Buddhist independence. The contradictions are the history of the figure.
