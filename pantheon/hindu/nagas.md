# The Nagas: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A collective class of serpent beings—variously gods, demigods, territorial and water spirits, chthonic guardians, dangerous monsters, royal lineages, and, in Buddhist and Jain classifications, powerful nonhuman rebirth orders or attendant deities. Individual Nāgas may be male (*nāga*) or female (*nāgī/nāginī*), animal, anthropomorphic, or shape-shifting.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Serpents called *áhi* occur throughout the Ṛgveda, probably composed c. 1500–1200 BCE, most famously Vṛtra/Ahi; these are antecedents, not yet the developed Nāga people of the epics. Serpent-propitiation and anti-venom rites occur in the Atharvaveda, including AV 6.56, commonly dated around the early first millennium BCE. The word *nāga* in the sense “serpent/serpent-being” is securely attested in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, broadly c. 800–500 BCE. A collective divine society of Nāgas—with kings, genealogy, subterranean cities, marriages, and conflicts—is fully visible only in the Sanskrit epics, whose extant forms accumulated approximately between the last centuries BCE and the early centuries CE. [“Cobra Deities and Divine Cobras”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/10/8/454); [Atharvaveda translation](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/av.htm)

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Definition

**[Securely attested, later Vedic onward]** Sanskrit **नाग**, *nāga*, denotes a snake—especially a cobra—and a supernatural serpent being; it can also mean an elephant and, in some lexical contexts, a mountain or cloud. These homonyms generated later symbolic associations but do not prove that all referents originated in one cult.

**[Epic and Purāṇic systematization]** Nāgas constitute a populous race descended principally from the sage Kaśyapa and Kadru. They inhabit subterranean or aquatic domains—*Pātāla*, *Nāgaloka*, *Rasātala*, rivers, lakes, springs, wells, and palaces beneath the earth. They possess jewels, poisons, medicines, treasures, rain-making and shape-changing powers. They may marry humans, protect kingdoms and sacred places, or cause venom, drought, infertility, and disease.

The category remains deliberately unstable. A text may use *nāga*, *sarpa*, *ahi*, *uraga*, *pannaga*, or *bhujaga* almost interchangeably, yet elsewhere distinguish ordinary snakes from divine Nāgas. By the fourth or fifth century CE at the latest, some sources explicitly distinguished supernatural Nāgas from their terrestrial snake manifestations. [“Cobra Deities and Divine Cobras”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/10/8/454)

### Names and linguistic forms

- **Sanskrit:** नाग, *nāga*; feminine नागी, *nāgī*, and later नागिनी, *nāginī*.
- **Pāli:** *nāga*. It can mean elephant, great or excellent person, cobra, or supernatural serpent; context determines the sense.
- **Prakrits:** *nāga*, *ṇāga*; Jain texts also employ *paṇṇaga/pannaga*, “crawler/serpent.”
- **Tamil:** நாகம், *nākam*, and நாகர், *nākar*; also native snake terms such as *pāmpu* and *aravu*. Tamil place and personal names containing *nāka-* cannot automatically be treated as evidence for a single ethnic “Nāga people.”
- **Kannada:** ನಾಗ, *nāga*; serpent stones are commonly *nāgakallu/nāgakal*.
- **Malayalam:** നാഗം, *nāgam*; sacred serpent groves are *sarppakkāvu*.
- **Hindi and related languages:** नाग, *nāg*; नागिन, *nāgin*.
- **Tibetan Buddhist translation:** *klu*, an indigenous water-spirit category used to translate Sanskrit *nāga*.
- **Chinese Buddhist translation:** 龍, *lóng*, “dragon”; this is a translation-equation, not evidence that Indian Nāgas and Chinese dragons were originally identical.
- **Khmer:** *neak*; Thai: *nāga/nāk*; Lao: *nak*. These represent Southeast Asian receptions in which imported Buddhist-Hindu narratives fused with local aquatic-serpent traditions.

### Etymology

**[Philologically disputed]** Manfred Mayrhofer treated the derivation as uncertain while discussing a possible Indo-European relationship to words for “naked,” perhaps “the hairless animal,” which might also explain the application to elephants. A proposed cognacy with Germanic “snake” has likewise been argued but is not settled. The Indo-European Cognate Relationships database marks the “naked/hairless” derivation as possible, not certain. Folk derivations such as “one who is wise” are modern esoteric explanations, not Sanskrit historical etymology. [IE-CoR cognate set](https://iecor.clld.org/cognatesets/1818)

### Principal collective titles and classes

- **Nāgarāja / Nāgendra / Nāgādhipa:** “Nāga king,” “lord of Nāgas”; applied to Śeṣa/Ananta, Vāsuki, Takṣaka, Karkoṭaka, Mucalinda, Dharaṇendra, Nīla, and others in different traditions.
- **Mahānāga:** “great Nāga”; in Buddhism it may also praise an awakened or spiritually eminent person.
- **Aṣṭanāga:** “Eight Nāgas.” Lists vary, commonly including Ananta, Vāsuki, Takṣaka, Karkoṭaka, Padma, Mahāpadma, Śaṅkhapāla, and Kulika.
- **Navanāga:** “Nine Nāgas”; a later ritual grouping with unstable membership.
- **Nāgakumāras:** In Jain cosmology, a class of *bhavanavāsin* or mansion-dwelling gods; not simply biological snakes.
- **Uraga:** “Breast-goer,” serpent.
- **Pannaga:** “Creeping/crawling one,” serpent; common in Sanskrit and Pāli-related registers.
- **Bhujaga/bhujaṅga:** “Going in curves,” serpent.
- **Sarpa:** Snake/serpent, from “creeping”; often an earthly snake but not consistently distinguished from Nāga.
- **Ahi:** The principal Vedic serpent term; Vṛtra is *ahi*, not securely a member of the later epic Nāga genealogy.

### The “Nāga tribes” question

**[Modern scholarly dispute]** The heterogeneous Tibeto-Burman-speaking peoples now collectively called the Naga peoples of northeast India and Myanmar must not be identified with mythological Nāgas without evidence. “Naga” was largely an outsider’s regional classification, not an original common endonym. Proposed derivations include Sanskrit *nāga*, “snake”; *nagna/nanga*, “naked”; a Kachari word allegedly meaning “young man/warrior”; and Burmese or regional forms interpreted as “people with pierced ears.” Nineteenth-century observers already judged these derivations inconclusive. Modern Naga political and cultural movements have adopted and transformed the once-external name into a shared identity. [Cambridge Naga database](https://wwwe.lib.cam.ac.uk/nagas/record/r87301.html); [University of Hull study](https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/output/4213949/nagas-in-the-museum-an-anthropological-study-of-the-material-culture-of-the-hill-peoples-of-the-assam-burma-border); [Longkumer](https://digitalcommons.macalester.edu/himalaya/vol35/iss1/10/)

No secure evidence demonstrates that the epic serpent race was a disguised historical account of today’s Naga peoples. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century attempts to turn every Nāga narrative into a memory of a conquered aboriginal “snake tribe” are reconstructions, not statements made by the earliest sources.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Vedic background

**Ṛgveda, Vedic Sanskrit, c. 1500–1200 BCE.** The hymns repeatedly call Vṛtra an *ahi*, a serpent whom Indra kills to release obstructed waters, especially RV 1.32. Ahi Budhnya, the “Serpent of the Deep,” appears as a more enigmatic divine being. Neither supplies the epic genealogy from Kadru, the subterranean Nāga kingdom, or Nāga kings such as Takṣaka and Vāsuki. Calling them “Nāgas” is a retrospective comparison.

**Atharvaveda, Vedic Sanskrit, substantial strata c. 1200–900 BCE, with later material.** AV 6.56 is a charm against snake poison; other passages name types of serpents, invoke plants and divine powers against bites, and treat serpents as dangerous beings that can be addressed ritually. This is secure evidence for serpent ritual but not yet for the whole epic Nāga nation. [Atharvaveda translation](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/av.htm)

**Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, Vedic prose, c. 800–500 BCE.** This is the earliest secure occurrence commonly cited for *nāga* as serpent or serpent-demon. Its evidence prevents a confident claim that *nāga* in that sense belongs to the oldest Ṛgvedic stratum.

**Gṛhya and ritual literature, later first millennium BCE onward.** Domestic rites increasingly include offerings to serpents, often at holes, thresholds, fields, or water sources. They document accommodation and propitiation rather than a single centralized “Nāga religion.”

### The Sanskrit epics

**Mahābhārata, Sanskrit; oral and textual growth approximately 400 BCE–400 CE; surviving manuscripts much later.** This is the foundational Nāga archive.

Especially important are:

- **Ādi Parvan, Pauṣya and Āstīka sections:** Kadru and Vinatā; the color of Uccaiḥśravas’s tail; Kadru’s curse; Garuḍa’s enslavement and acquisition of amṛta; the feud between Garuḍa and the snakes; Vāsuki’s sister Jaratkāru; Āstīka’s birth; Takṣaka’s killing of Parīkṣit; Janamejaya’s snake sacrifice; Āstīka’s intervention.
- **Ādi Parvan, Ulūpī episode:** Arjuna is taken into the Nāga realm and marries the Nāga princess Ulūpī.
- **Ādi and later books:** Bhīma’s encounter with Nāgas and receipt of strength-giving elixir; Khaṇḍava’s burning and Takṣaka’s association with the forest; Nāgas in cosmological catalogues.
- **Udyoga Parvan:** Mātali’s visit to Bhogavatī and the proposed marriage involving the Nāga prince Sumukha.
- **Vana Parvan:** Karkoṭaka bites Nala, changing his appearance while enabling a future cure.
- **Mausala Parvan:** Balarāma’s departing life-force appears as a great serpent, connecting him with Śeṣa.

The epic is internally plural. Some Nāgas are condemned as venomous killers; some are “virtuous” and explicitly spared; some are royal allies and marriage partners. The Āstīka narrative itself rejects indiscriminate extermination. [Mahābhārata, Āstīka narrative](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m01/m01039.htm); [Takṣaka and Parīkṣit](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m01/m01051.htm); [end of the sacrifice](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m01/m01057.htm); [victims by lineage](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m01/m01058.htm)

**Rāmāyaṇa, Sanskrit; core commonly placed in the final centuries BCE, expanded into the early centuries CE.** Nāgas appear in cosmology, divine genealogies, subterranean realms, and the ocean-churning story. Śeṣa/Ananta and Vāsuki belong to the epic’s shared mythic universe, but the Rāmāyaṇa does not contain a Nāga cycle comparable in extent to the Mahābhārata’s Āstīka material.

### Purāṇic corpus

Purāṇas are layered compilations; dates below refer to major formative periods, not single acts of authorship.

- **Viṣṇu Purāṇa, chiefly c. fourth–fifth centuries CE with later transmission:** creation, underworlds, Nāga genealogies, Śeṣa supporting the earth, Viṣṇu reclining upon Ananta, Vāsuki at the ocean churning. [Viṣṇu Purāṇa](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/vishnu-purana-wilson)
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa, probably c. ninth–tenth centuries CE:** Kṛṣṇa’s subjugation and expulsion of Kāliya; Ananta/Śeṣa theology; Takṣaka, Parīkṣit, and Janamejaya retold. [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-bhagavata-purana); [Kāliya episode](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-bhagavata-purana/d/doc1128882.html)
- **Matsya, Vāyu, Brahmāṇḍa, Mārkaṇḍeya, Śiva, Liṅga, and Skanda Purāṇas, broadly first millennium CE with medieval additions:** variant genealogies, cosmological lists, pilgrimage sites, serpent rites, Śiva’s serpent ornaments, and localized Nāga sacred geographies.
- **Bhaviṣya Purāṇa, highly composite and substantially late:** contains festival and vrata prescriptions used in later Nāgapañcamī literature. Its present ritual chapters cannot safely be cited as Vedic-age evidence.
- **Nīlamata Purāṇa, Kashmir, probably c. sixth–eighth centuries CE:** Kashmir is represented as a former lake, Satīsaras, allotted to the Nāgas; Kaśyapa drains the valley; Nīla Nāga becomes a principal territorial authority; numerous springs and places are attached to named Nāgas. The text ritualizes Kashmir’s landscape and incorporates Nāga, Piśāca, Brahmanical, Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and local elements. Its self-attribution to Nīla is literary, not proof that a historical Nāga chief authored it. [Ved Kumari Ghai’s study](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_N%C4%ABlamata_Pur%C4%81%E1%B9%87a_A_cultural_liter.html?id=0sJHAAAAMAAJ); [Mahesh Sharma](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2386078)

### Agamic and Tantric material

**[Medieval learned systematization]** Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Śākta, Buddhist, and Jain ritual manuals prescribe serpent mantras, protective diagrams, installation of Nāga images, poison remedies, fertility rites, and expiation for *sarpa-doṣa*. Lists of eight or nine Nāgas become formulaic. This corpus is vast and recension-dependent; a standardized pan-Indian “Nāga Tantra” cannot be reconstructed from it.

Kuṇḍalinī is portrayed in tantric and haṭhayogic literature as a coiled power, often serpent-like, but she is not thereby one of Kadru’s Nāga children. Equating all Nāgas with “kundalini energy” is a modern homogenization.

### Tamil evidence

**[Regional, early historic through medieval]** Sangam and post-Sangam literature uses *nāka/nākar* in several registers: serpents, supernatural beings, place-names, royal or maritime peoples, and inhabitants of *Nāka-nāṭu*. The Buddhist Tamil epic *Maṇimēkalai*, probably c. fifth–sixth centuries CE, locates part of its story in a Nāga-associated island and maritime world. Such passages do not establish that all southern groups called Nākar were one cobra-worshipping ethnicity.

Medieval Tamil Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava hymns regularly invoke Śiva adorned with snakes and Viṣṇu reclining on the serpent bed. Murukaṉ/Subrahmaṇya’s association with serpent shrines became particularly important in Karnataka and Kerala, but it is a regional historical fusion rather than a universal feature of the earliest Tamil Murukaṉ corpus.

### Buddhist corpus

- **Pāli Vinaya, Udāna 2.1, and parallel biographies; canonical compilation beginning c. third century BCE but preserved in later manuscripts:** Mucalinda, a Nāga king, coils around the newly awakened Buddha during a storm and spreads his hood over him; afterward he assumes the form of a young man. [Pāli Mucalinda text](https://buddhistcanon.ru/en/pali_canon/kn/ud/vagga2/ud2.1/pli/ms.html); [translation](https://quangduc.com/a52861/muccalinda-sutta)
- **Vinaya narratives:** A Nāga assumes human appearance and seeks ordination. When his nature is exposed, animal/nonhuman birth is declared ineligible for full monastic ordination, but the Nāga remains devoted; in some traditions this explains calling an ordination candidate a *nāga*.
- **Jātakas and avadānas:** Nāga kings guard waters and treasures, receive instruction, retaliate against injury, and shelter the Buddha or bodhisattvas.
- **Mahāyāna literature, early centuries CE onward:** Nāgas become guardians of sūtras. Later tradition says Nāgārjuna recovered Prajñāpāramitā teachings from the Nāga realm; this is a sectarian hagiographic claim, not a historical account of manuscript discovery.
- **Buddhist relic traditions:** Nāgas guard the Buddha’s relics, notably those of the Rāmagrāma stūpa, and contest or participate in relic distribution.

### Jain corpus

**[Canonical and medieval elaboration]** Jain cosmology classifies Nāgakumāras among the mansion-dwelling gods. The central narrative is Pārśvanātha’s relationship with the serpent pair later reborn as Dharaṇendra and Padmāvatī: Pārśva saves snakes trapped in a burning log; in a later life Dharaṇendra and Padmāvatī protect him from the storm raised by his enemy Kamatha/Meghamālin. The full protective-deity system is medieval, though Pārśva’s serpent canopy is already present in early Mathura art. A Pārśva homage tablet from Kaṅkālī Ṭīlā, associated with the reign of the Northern Satrap Śoḍāsa around the turn of the era, is among the earliest relevant images. [Jain iconographic study](https://www.heritageuniversityofkerala.com/Publications/P1/39_Ete_PPJ_20230201.pdf); [Jaina Iconography](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/57452.pdf)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Epic-Purāṇic and regional]**

Nāgas govern or mediate:

- subterranean waters, rivers, springs, tanks, wells, lakes, and rain;
- fertility of land, humans, and cattle;
- poison, snakebite, fever, and their cures;
- underground jewels, metals, treasure, and secret knowledge;
- boundaries, thresholds, trees, anthills, fields, and family estates;
- dynastic legitimacy and territorial sovereignty;
- protection of scriptures, relics, temples, and contemplatives;
- destructive flooding, drought, venom, and retaliatory illness when offended.

Their ambivalence is fundamental. They protect water but may withhold it; cure poison but embody it; sustain fertility yet punish violations of groves, burrows, springs, or kin obligations.

### Forms

1. **Entirely serpentine:** Often a giant cobra, sometimes many-headed.
2. **Human above, serpent below:** Especially common in sculpture.
3. **Anthropomorphic with cobra canopy:** A human figure identified by one, three, five, seven, or more cobra hoods.
4. **Nāga couple:** A male and female beneath hoods, often linked with fertility.
5. **Shape-shifter:** Epic and Buddhist Nāgas assume human form for marriage, diplomacy, instruction, or attempted ordination.
6. **Cosmic serpent:** Ananta/Śeṣa bears worlds or becomes Viṣṇu’s couch.
7. **Architectural serpent:** Multiheaded bodies form balustrades, arches, thresholds, and bridges.

Hood number is not a universally reliable identifier. Seven hoods often signal Mucalinda, Pārśvanātha, or a major king, but local conventions differ.

### Major surviving images and sites

- **Bharhut and Sanchi, c. second–first centuries BCE:** Early Buddhist railings depict serpent beings or anthropomorphic Nāgas with hoods, establishing Nāga imagery before the mature Hindu temple canon.
- **Sonkh near Mathura, approximately first century BCE–first century CE:** Excavations uncovered an apsidal Nāga shrine and cult sculpture, material evidence for an institutional local cult rather than merely literary symbolism.
- **Mathura, Kushan period:** Monumental hooded Nāga images and inscriptions attest donors and installed cult images. An image inscription in year 8 of Kaniṣka is described in *Epigraphia Indica* as Mathura’s earliest dated Nāga image. [Epigraphia Indica](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Epigraphia_Indica_Vol_17_Romanized/032571); [NGV survey](https://www.ngv.vic.gov.au/essay/a-kushan-bodhisattva-and-early-indian-sculpture/)
- **Chhargaon/Mathura, reign of Huviṣka:** An inscription records installation of a Nāga image beside a tank and asks that “Lord Nāga” be pleased—direct epigraphic evidence connecting cult, donors, and water.
- **Karnataka Nāga stones:** The official antiquities register documents, for example, a Badami sandstone *nāga-mithuna* of approximately the twelfth–thirteenth century: an entwined crowned male and female serpent pair with attendant serpents. [National Mission on Monuments and Antiquities](https://nmma.nic.in/nmma/antiqDetail.do?refId=77880&state=KR)
- **Pārśvanātha images:** From early Mathura onward, the Jina’s serpent canopy becomes his clearest iconographic identifier; Dharaṇendra and Padmāvatī appear more fully in later compositions.
- **Viṣṇu Anantaśayana:** Gupta and post-Gupta images show Viṣṇu reclining upon Ananta over the cosmic ocean, with creation emerging around him.
- **Mucalinda Buddha:** Especially prominent in Southeast Asia. A late twelfth–early thirteenth-century Cambodian example associated with Jayavarman VII shows the meditating Buddha seated on coils beneath seven hoods. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/38451)
- **Angkorian bridges:** Nāga balustrades monumentalize the serpent as a boundary between ordinary and sacred or royal space. [Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art](https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/collections-areas/southeast-asian/sacred-sites-in-southeast-asia/naga-bridge-spean-ta-ong/)

### Symbols and substances

- Cobra hood and coils.
- Water vessel, lotus, jewel (*nāgamaṇi*), garland, and sometimes sword or club.
- Anthill, spring, well, lake, sacred tree, grove, and stone slab.
- Milk, turmeric, vermilion, flowers, lamps, uncooked or sweet foods.
- Gem-bearing crown, representing both kingship and subterranean wealth.
- The rainy season, especially Śrāvaṇa.
- Astrological nodes Rāhu and Ketu become associated with *sarpa-doṣa* in later astrology, but they are not simply Nāga kings in the earliest sources.

**[Folklore/modern popular religion]** The *nāgamaṇi*, imagined as a luminous jewel in a cobra’s head, is pervasive in story and film. Although Nāga jewels are old, modern claims that biological cobras produce a recoverable magical gemstone are unsupported.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

### Genealogical systems

**Epic dominant version:** Prajāpati Dakṣa’s daughters Kadru and Vinatā marry Kaśyapa. Kadru becomes mother of the snakes; Vinatā bears Aruṇa and Garuḍa. Kadru wins a wager through deception, enslaving Vinatā. Garuḍa obtains amṛta to ransom his mother, creating the enduring Garuḍa–Nāga hostility.

Prominent sons or descendants of Kadru include Śeṣa/Ananta, Vāsuki, Takṣaka, Karkoṭaka, Airāvata, Kāliya, Padma, Mahāpadma, Kulika, Śaṅkhapāla, and innumerable others. Lists vary among the Mahābhārata and Purāṇas.

**Variant:** Some cosmologies derive serpents from Brahmā’s anger, from Kaśyapa through other maternal lines, or treat particular local Nāgas as autochthonous beings with no interest in Kadru’s genealogy.

**Kashmir regional system:** Nīla Nāga is chief of the Nāgas of Kashmir and participates in the territory’s ordering. The Nīlamata integrates him into Brahmanical cosmology while preserving the logic of local spring deities.

**Vaiṣṇava systematization:** Śeṣa/Ananta becomes both a Nāga king and an expansion, servant, couch, or cosmic manifestation of Viṣṇu. Balarāma is identified with Śeṣa.

**Jain system:** Dharaṇendra is a Nāga king and Pārśvanātha’s yakṣa or protective attendant; Padmāvatī is the corresponding yakṣiṇī or goddess and, in developed traditions, Dharaṇendra’s consort.

### Named divine interactions

The following are discrete source-attested interactions rather than inferred associations. “Date” refers to the text’s broad formative period.

- **Indra — Vṛtra/Ahi:** Indra kills the water-obstructing serpent and releases the waters; Ṛgveda 1.32, c. 1500–1200 BCE. **[Securely attested early; antecedent, not securely a later Nāga]**
- **Brahmā — Kadru’s sons:** Brahmā permits Kadru’s curse to operate against destructive snakes while foretelling that virtuous ones will survive; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan, final centuries BCE–early centuries CE. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Kadru — the Nāgas:** Kadru orders her sons to darken Uccaiḥśravas’s tail and curses disobedient sons to Janamejaya’s sacrifice; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Vinatā — Nāgas:** Through Kadru’s wager, Vinatā becomes enslaved to Kadru and her sons; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Garuḍa — Nāgas:** Garuḍa obtains amṛta as their demanded ransom, frees Vinatā, and becomes their predator; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Indra — Garuḍa and Nāgas:** Indra recovers the amṛta after Garuḍa deposits it for the Nāgas; the snakes lick kuśa grass and acquire split tongues; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Vāsuki — Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devas, and Asuras:** Vāsuki serves as the churning rope around Mount Mandara; Mahābhārata, Rāmāyaṇa, and Purāṇic accounts, epic onward. **[Epic core; later canonical elaboration]**
- **Śeṣa — Brahmā:** After Śeṣa practices austerities to escape his violent brothers, Brahmā appoints him to support the earth; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Ananta/Śeṣa — Viṣṇu:** Ananta becomes Viṣṇu’s couch upon the cosmic ocean and participates in periodic creation; Viṣṇu Purāṇa and other Purāṇas, chiefly first millennium CE. **[Purāṇic canonical]**
- **Śeṣa — Balarāma:** Balarāma is interpreted as Śeṣa’s embodiment; at his departure a great white serpent issues from him and enters the sea; Mahābhārata, Mausala Parvan, followed by Purāṇic theology. **[Epic/Purāṇic canonical]**
- **Vāsuki — Āstīka:** Vāsuki arranges his sister Jaratkāru’s marriage so that her son Āstīka will save the Nāgas; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Takṣaka — Indra:** Takṣaka seeks refuge with Indra during Janamejaya’s sacrifice; the priests attempt to draw both into the fire until Āstīka intervenes; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Kṛṣṇa — Kāliya:** Kṛṣṇa leaps into Kāliya’s poisoned Yamunā pool, dances upon his hoods, and orders him to depart after the Nāginīs supplicate; Harivaṃśa and later Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.16–17. **[Early-classical supplement; later Vaiṣṇava canonical]**
- **Garuḍa — Kāliya:** Kāliya hides in the Yamunā because Garuḍa cannot enter a place protected by a sage’s curse; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.16–17. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Nāga wives — Kṛṣṇa:** Kāliya’s wives praise Kṛṣṇa and obtain clemency for Kāliya; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.16. **[Later canonical]**
- **Vāsuki — Śiva:** Vāsuki appears as Śiva’s ornament and, in the Tripura narrative, as part of Śiva’s cosmic weapon, including the bowstring in some versions; Mahābhārata and Purāṇic sources. **[Epic/Purāṇic, version-dependent]**
- **Nāgas — Skanda/Subrahmaṇya:** Vāsuki and serpent cults become associated with Skanda, especially in South India; textual and epigraphic evidence becomes prominent in the early-medieval period. **[Regional syncretism]**
- **Mucalinda — Buddha:** Mucalinda shelters the Buddha from a storm, then assumes human form and salutes him; Udāna 2.1 and Vinaya parallels. **[Early Buddhist canonical]**
- **Nāga ordination-candidate — Buddha:** A Nāga disguises himself as a human and enters the Saṅgha; after discovery, the Buddha excludes nonhumans from ordination while recognizing his devotion; Vinaya traditions. **[Buddhist canonical]**
- **Nāga kings — Buddha:** Various Nāgas receive teaching, offer protection, or surrender after conflict in Jātaka, Vinaya, and avadāna literature. **[Canonical and later Buddhist]**
- **Nāgas — Nāgārjuna:** Nāgārjuna is said to receive Prajñāpāramitā scriptures preserved in the Nāga realm; medieval Buddhist biographies. **[Late hagiographic accretion]**
- **Dharaṇendra and Padmāvatī — Pārśvanātha:** Reborn serpents shelter Pārśva from Meghamālin’s storm; medieval Jain lives and iconography. **[Jain canonical/hagiographic elaboration]**
- **Dharaṇendra — Meghamālin/Kamatha:** Dharaṇendra counters the enemy’s storm while Pārśva remains unmoved; medieval Jain narrative. **[Later canonical]**
- **Nīla Nāga — Viṣṇu:** In the Nīlamata’s sacred geography, Nāga claims to Kashmir are integrated into a Vaiṣṇava-Brahmanical ordering of the valley. **[Kashmiri regional synthesis, c. sixth–eighth centuries]**
- **Garuḍa — Sumukha:** Garuḍa threatens the Nāga prince Sumukha; Viṣṇu and Indra participate in resolving his protection and marriage prospects; Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**
- **Śeṣa and Vāsuki — other Nāgas:** Śeṣa separates himself from violent brothers; Vāsuki remains their political king and plans collective survival; Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan. **[Epic canonical]**

No early primary source documents a meeting between “the Nāgas” collectively and every member of the Hindu pantheon. Mere appearance in the same cosmological list is not an interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Kadru, Vinatā, Garuḍa, and the amṛta

**[Epic canonical]** Kadru and Vinatā compete over the color of the divine horse Uccaiḥśravas. Kadru says its tail is black; Vinatā says white. Kadru commands her serpent sons to cling to the tail as black hairs. Some refuse; she curses them to perish in Janamejaya’s future sacrifice. Through the deceit Vinatā loses and becomes enslaved.

Vinatā’s son Garuḍa bargains for her release. The snakes demand the gods’ amṛta. Garuḍa defeats its guardians and carries it away but reaches an accommodation with Indra: he will set the vessel down without drinking; Indra will retrieve it. The Nāgas go to purify themselves before drinking. Indra removes the amṛta; the snakes lick the sharp kuśa grass where it stood, splitting their tongues.

The cycle explains hostility between raptors and snakes, snakes’ forked tongues, the sanctity of kuśa, and the impending snake sacrifice. It also refuses a simple good/evil division: Kadru deceives, some sons reject deception, Garuḍa attacks the gods but keeps his word, and only guilty snakes are destined to die.

### Parīkṣit, Takṣaka, Janamejaya, and Āstīka

**[Epic canonical]** King Parīkṣit insults the silent sage Śamīka by placing a dead snake on him. Śamīka’s son Śṛṅgin curses the king to die from Takṣaka’s bite within seven days. Takṣaka, sometimes defeating or bribing away the healer Kaśyapa, reaches the protected king in disguise and kills him with venomous fire.

Parīkṣit’s son Janamejaya orders a *sarpa-sattra*, a sacrificial session designed to draw every serpent into the fire by mantra. Snakes of multiple lineages fall into the flames. Takṣaka shelters with Indra, but the officiants intensify the rite to pull down “Takṣaka together with Indra.”

The rescue has been prepared generations earlier. Kadru’s curse and Brahmā’s response foretold that Jaratkāru’s son would save virtuous snakes. Vāsuki therefore gives his sister—also called Jaratkāru—to the ascetic Jaratkāru. Their son Āstīka enters Janamejaya’s rite, praises it, wins a boon, and asks that it stop. Takṣaka hangs suspended rather than falling. Janamejaya reluctantly honors the boon.

This is not securely a memory of a historical genocide or Aryan conquest. Such readings became influential in colonial and nationalist scholarship but depend on converting vividly supernatural serpents into a human ethnicity. The episode’s explicit literary concern is the limitation of vengeance and the differentiation of harmful from harmless beings.

### Śeṣa’s withdrawal and cosmic service

**[Epic core; Purāṇic expansion]** Repelled by his brothers’ conduct, Śeṣa performs austerities. Brahmā directs him to stabilize the earth. Later Vaiṣṇava theology develops him as Ananta, “Endless,” the cosmic serpent on whom Nārāyaṇa rests between manifestations of the universe. His many hoods bear worlds; his name links him to the “remainder” left after cosmic dissolution.

This theology absorbs a Nāga king into the highest Vaiṣṇava cosmology without eliminating his serpent identity. Balarāma and, in some traditions, Lakṣmaṇa are manifestations of Śeṣa, extending his defining quality of service to Viṣṇu’s incarnations.

### The churning of the ocean

**[Epic and Purāṇic canonical]** Gods and Asuras seek amṛta. Mount Mandara becomes the churning rod and Vāsuki the rope; Viṣṇu as the tortoise supports the mountain. Versions differ over which party holds Vāsuki’s head. Poison, heat, or smoke from the serpent afflicts those near his mouths; the churning releases treasures, goddesses, the moon, poison, and finally amṛta.

Vāsuki is simultaneously a tool, indispensable collaborator, and source of danger. Later images monumentalize his stretched body, most famously at Angkor.

### Kṛṣṇa and Kāliya

**[Harivaṃśa/classical; Bhāgavata canonical elaboration]** Kāliya occupies a Yamunā pool and poisons its water and surroundings. Kṛṣṇa enters the water, is coiled by the serpent, escapes, and dances upon his hoods. Kāliya’s wives plead for mercy. Kṛṣṇa spares him and orders him to return to the sea or Ramaṇaka island; the marks of Kṛṣṇa’s feet will protect him from Garuḍa.

The episode makes Kṛṣṇa purifier and master of a dangerous local water-being, but not his exterminator. Regional festivals and performance traditions emphasize different elements: heroic victory, environmental purification, divine grace, or the Nāginīs’ intercession.

### Karkoṭaka and Nala

**[Epic canonical]** Nala rescues Karkoṭaka from a forest fire. The Nāga then bites him—not as simple treachery, but to transform and protect him. Nala becomes the misshapen Bāhuka; the venom torments the possessing Kali and will later enable restoration. The story exemplifies Nāga poison as pharmakon: injury, disguise, discipline, and cure at once.

### Kashmir’s lake and Nīla Nāga

**[Regional, c. sixth–eighth centuries]** The Nīlamata Purāṇa portrays Kashmir as Satīsaras, a primordial lake inhabited by Nāgas. Kaśyapa drains it and defeats the water demon Jalodbhava with divine assistance. Nīla prescribes Kashmir’s festivals and ritual order. Named Nāgas inhabit springs and water sites throughout the valley.

The text may preserve and Brahmanize local water-spirit cults, but its author cannot be securely identified as an indigenous Nāga chief. Its mapping of hundreds of Nāgas onto springs is stronger evidence for a regional sacred geography than for a census of a historical tribe.

### Mucalinda

**[Early Buddhist canonical]** During a storm following the Buddha’s awakening, Mucalinda emerges, wraps his coils seven times around the Buddha, and spreads his hood above him. When the storm passes he assumes a youth’s form and pays homage. Later art enlarges a brief canonical episode into one of Buddhism’s most recognizable serpent images.

### Pārśvanātha and Dharaṇendra

**[Jain narrative with early iconographic roots and medieval elaboration]** Pārśva sees that an ascetic’s burning log contains serpents and has it split, saving them or enabling their peaceful rebirth. Reborn as Dharaṇendra and Padmāvatī, they later protect Pārśva against a supernatural storm created by the same hostile soul, now Meghamālin. Pārśva remains meditating and spiritually invulnerable while the serpent king raises a hood and the goddess provides further shelter.

### Nāgārjuna and hidden scriptures

**[Medieval Buddhist hagiography]** Nāgārjuna visits the Nāga realm and retrieves Prajñāpāramitā scriptures entrusted to them. The legend authorizes texts as ancient teachings providentially preserved until humanity was ready. It is not independently verifiable history and should not be used to date the scriptures.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Material and institutional evidence

Nāga worship rarely formed one centrally administered sect. It appears instead through:

- independent local shrines;
- images at tanks, wells, springs, groves, and field boundaries;
- subordinate shrines inside Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Śākta, Buddhist, or Jain complexes;
- domestic lineage cults;
- seasonal festivals and vows;
- healing, fertility, rain, and poison rites.

The apsidal shrine at Sonkh and Kushan-period Mathura inscriptions demonstrate that some Nāgas received formal images, buildings, donors, and public cult by the early centuries CE. Mathura’s large anthropomorphic Nāgas show that they were not merely decorative motifs. [NGV](https://www.ngv.vic.gov.au/essay/a-kushan-bodhisattva-and-early-indian-sculpture/)

### Nāgapañcamī

**[Medieval textual codification; living regional festival]** Nāgapañcamī is observed on a fifth lunar day, most widely Śrāvaṇa bright fifth in July–August, though regional calendars differ. Worship may address live snakes, painted serpents, clay or metal images, stones, anthills, door images, or established temple icons. Offerings include milk, turmeric, vermilion, flowers, incense, lamps, grain, sweets, and water. Women often perform vows for children, brothers, household welfare, or protection from snakebite.

Later vrata literature connects the date variously with:

- Āstīka stopping Janamejaya’s sacrifice;
- Kṛṣṇa’s victory over Kāliya;
- Kadru and the Nāga genealogy;
- protection from death by snakebite.

These are overlapping explanations, not a single recoverable “original” myth. The Bhaviṣya and other Purāṇic ritual passages are medieval or later evidence, not proof of a Vedic Nāgapañcamī. A nineteenth- and early twentieth-century record already identifies it as India’s chief snake festival in the rainy season. [Snake Worship](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdclcn/26/01/38/33/v2/26013833v2/26013833v2.pdf)

Offering milk to captive snakes is a living custom but is neither biologically natural nourishment for snakes nor mandated by the earliest sources. Modern wildlife authorities and conservationists frequently discourage capture, defanging, and forced feeding.

### Karnataka and the southwest coast

**[Regional cult, materially attested]** Karnataka preserves large numbers of *nāgakallu*: relief stones showing single cobras, interlaced pairs, multihooded serpents, or anthropomorphic figures. Stones are installed beneath trees, beside water, in temple compounds, and in dedicated *nāgabanas* or serpent groves. Their functions include lineage protection, fertility, expiation, and commemoration.

The coastal Karnataka *Nāgamaṇḍala* creates an elaborate multi-colored serpent design on the ground; ritual specialists sing and dance around it, and possession or divine presence may be enacted. The cult has become closely associated with Subrahmaṇya, notably at Kukke, but local Nāga rites are not reducible to orthodox Skanda worship. Epigraphic evidence cited in regional historical work reaches at least the ninth century, while surviving stones range widely in date. [Karnataka Itihasa Academy](https://karnatakaitihasaacademy.org/naga-pantha-and-nagamandala-a-historical-survey/)

In parts of Karnataka and Kerala, *sarpa-doṣa* is diagnosed as a cause of infertility or misfortune; remedies include *nāga-pratiṣṭhā*, *āśleṣā-bali*, feeding Brahmins, restoring groves, or installing stones. Contemporary South Indian ethnography records snake stones, anthills, silver-serpent offerings, children’s rites, and rapidly changing urban temples. [Emory dissertation](https://etd.library.emory.edu/concern/etds/7w62f8854?locale=zh)

### Kerala

Sacred serpent groves attached to houses or matrilineal estates preserve localized cults of Nāga kings and serpent goddesses. Ritual specialists conduct songs, drawings, possession rites, and offerings. Modern land pressure, migration, and family partition have led both to destruction of groves and to ritual transfers or elaborate propitiations before removal.

### Kashmir

Springs called *nāg*—including sites associated with Nīla, Ananta, and other beings—maintain the old link between Nāga and water. The Nīlamata supplies an extensive ritual calendar in which local Nāga observance participates in a larger Brahmanical civic order.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

**[Negative finding]** No reliable evidence located ties an institutional cult of the mythological Nāgas to a regular requirement for human sacrifice. Janamejaya’s *sarpa-sattra* sacrifices serpents, not humans. General Vedic debates about *puruṣamedha*, documented human sacrifice to other deities, and colonial reports concerning some northeast hill communities cannot be transferred to Nāga worship merely because the same word “Naga” appears.

Colonial records do discuss head-taking, warfare, slavery, and alleged human sacrifice among particular northeast communities. Those reports concern historically distinct peoples, were produced within colonial campaigns, and require case-by-case criticism. They are not evidence about Vāsuki, Takṣaka, Nāgapañcamī, or serpent stones. A 1931 *Nature* notice, for example, reports colonial suppression of slavery and “human sacrifice” among communities on the Burma frontier but does not establish a serpent-deity cult behind those practices. [Nature archival notice](https://www.nature.com/articles/128762a0.pdf)

No evidence supports “temple prostitution” as a defining Nāga institution.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### c. 1500–900 BCE: serpent adversaries and propitiation

The oldest Vedic strata know dangerous and cosmic serpents under *ahi*, especially Vṛtra. Atharvavedic charms demonstrate practical and ritual engagement with venomous snakes. There is not yet a documented collective Nāga people descended from Kadru.

### c. 800–300 BCE: the name and ritual category emerge

The noun *nāga* acquires a serpent/supernatural sense in later Vedic prose. Domestic and protective rites increasingly accommodate serpent powers. Buddhist and Jain communities inherit and transform an already recognizable category of water and territorial beings.

### c. 300 BCE–300 CE: narrative and cultic consolidation

The Mahābhārata assembles the genealogy, Garuḍa feud, Āstīka cycle, royal Nāga houses, subterranean cities, and human-Nāga marriages. Buddhist monuments and texts make Nāgas guardians of Buddha, relics, waters, and sacred sites. Jain art adopts the serpent canopy for Pārśvanātha.

Bharhut, Sanchi, Sonkh, and Mathura provide the crucial material record. Independent Nāga images, tank-side dedications, and shrines show public cult. This is the best-attested period for formal freestanding Nāga worship in north-central India.

### c. 300–700 CE: incorporation into sectarian cosmologies

Vaiṣṇavism elevates Ananta/Śeṣa as cosmic support and Viṣṇu’s couch; Balarāma becomes his manifestation. Śaivism places serpents on Śiva’s body and within his cosmic weaponry. Purāṇic geography multiplies Nāga realms and kings. Regional sacred geographies such as Kashmir’s are assimilated into Brahmanical frameworks.

This was not simple suppression. Local powers were retained while subordinated to, identified with, or made attendants of higher sectarian gods.

### c. 600–1200 CE: regional florescence and trans-Asian expansion

Nāga imagery becomes ubiquitous in Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain temples. Kashmir’s Nīlamata maps Nāgas onto springs. Southern serpent stones and grove cults proliferate. Jain Dharaṇendra–Padmāvatī iconography develops.

In Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, Indian Nāga forms merge with local serpent, water, ancestry, and kingship traditions. A fifth-century Cham inscription praised a “divine serpent of the king”; Khmer art later made Nāga balustrades and Mucalinda Buddhas central royal forms. [Metropolitan Museum, *Lost Kingdoms*](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2014/lost-kingdoms)

### c. 1200–1800 CE: continuity, localization, and ritual codification

Festival manuals, pilgrimage texts, astrology, temple practice, and family cult maintain Nāga worship. Some independent shrines become attached to Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī, or Subrahmaṇya. The category remains productive in vernacular literature and oral traditions.

Islamic rule did not produce a documented empire-wide campaign specifically against Nāga worship. Temples and images were damaged in particular wars and political transformations, while local spring, stone, and grove rites often continued without centralized institutions. Any claim of uniform “suppression of the Nāga religion” exceeds the evidence.

### c. 1800–1947: colonial classification and archaeological recovery

British administrators, antiquarians, and ethnologists often merged three subjects: mythological serpent beings, serpent worship, and northeast peoples labeled “Nagas.” Racial and evolutionist theories described Nāgas as remnants of a conquered “aboriginal snake race.” Archaeology at Mathura and the publication of inscriptions provided firmer evidence for early cult images, while colonial ethnography preserved observations distorted by categories such as “animism,” “primitive religion,” and “tribe.”

J. Ph. Vogel’s work on Indian serpent lore and Nāga iconography remains foundational as a collection of textual and material evidence, though parts of its cultural-evolutionary framework are dated.

### 1947–present: living cult, heritage, and reinvention

Nāgapañcamī, grove worship, serpent stones, temple rites, astrology, and fertility observances remain active. Urbanization has simultaneously endangered sacred groves and encouraged new, professionalized temple rituals. Museum, heritage, and archaeological agencies catalogue Nāga stones and sculptures.

The Naga peoples’ modern political identity developed through colonial administration, Christian missions, anti-colonial mobilization, nationalism, and negotiations with India and Myanmar. Their name’s resemblance to Sanskrit *nāga* remains etymologically unresolved and does not collapse their distinct histories into mythology.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Theosophy and occultism

**[Modern reinterpretation]** Helena Blavatsky and later Theosophists treated Nāgas as initiates, adepts, or universal “serpents of wisdom,” comparing them to dragons and serpent figures from Egypt, Mesoamerica, Chaldea, and elsewhere. These comparisons are nineteenth-century esoteric constructions, not an ancient globally unified Nāga doctrine. Theosophical claims that Nāgas were universally enlightened teachers or technologically advanced beings have no support in the Sanskrit epic record. [Theosophy Wiki](https://www.theosophy.wiki/en/Naga)

### Kuṇḍalinī

Premodern tantric sources do describe coiled *kuṇḍalinī*, but the modern, standardized image of “serpent power” rising through a fixed seven-chakra system owes much to transnational occultism, Theosophy, Indian reformers, and Arthur Avalon/John Woodroffe’s *The Serpent Power* (1919). It should not be projected back as the hidden meaning of every Nāga myth. Modern scholarship describes the popular system as a selective synthesis rather than a reproduction of one ancient map. [Harvard CSWR](https://cswr.hds.harvard.edu/news/2024/09/kundalini-and-magic-comparison); [University of Vienna research](https://rw-ktf.univie.ac.at/fileadmin/user_upload/i_religionswiss/The_History_of_Modern_Kundalini_Research._Gopi_Krishna_and_the_Transformation_of_a_South_Asian_Goddess_in_the_Late_Twentieth_Century__Marleen_Thaler_2024_.pdf)

### New Age and pseudo-history

The following are **modern inventions or unsupported claims** when presented as ancient fact:

- Nāgas were extraterrestrial “reptilians.”
- They built a worldwide prehistoric technological civilization.
- All dragon traditions descend from a single Nāga priesthood.
- *Nāga* etymologically means “wise one.”
- The Naga peoples are biological descendants of supernatural serpents.
- Every serpent image encodes kuṇḍalinī or DNA.
- Nāgamaṇi is a scientifically verified gemstone produced by cobras.
- The Mahābhārata’s serpent sacrifice is a literal record of a datable racial genocide.

These ideas mix comparative symbolism, Theosophy, speculative archaeology, internet occultism, and science-fiction motifs.

### Literature, film, comics, and games

Modern Indian cinema and television frequently portray Nāgas and *icchādhārī nāg/nāgin* as immortal shape-shifters, lovers, avengers, and jewel-guardians. The shape-shifting capacity is traditional, but the standardized revenge-romance plot, sexualized cobra woman, hereditary jewel, and modern domestic melodrama are largely products of twentieth- and twenty-first-century popular media.

Fantasy games and global fiction use “naga” for snake-bodied monster species, often detached from water, kinship, ritual reciprocity, or South Asian religious context. This gaming taxonomy is modern commercial reception, not a traditional classification.

### Political use

Interpretations of the snake sacrifice as “Aryan invaders exterminating indigenous Nāga tribes” have served anti-caste, Dravidianist, indigenous-rights, Hindu-nationalist, and regional identity narratives in different ways. The epic permits reflection on mass violence, but it does not identify its Nāgas with a securely recoverable historical ethnicity. Conversely, claims that archaeology proves an unchanged “Vedic Nāga cult” from the Indus civilization to today also exceed the evidence: Indus signs remain undeciphered, and serpent-like images cannot by themselves establish the later Sanskrit category.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Serpent cult versus textual construction

Older scholarship often posited an autonomous, pre-Aryan serpent religion later absorbed by Brahmanism. Archaeology confirms early and widespread serpent cults, but it cannot assign them a single ethnicity or language. The category probably grew through repeated interaction among real snakes, water cults, territorial spirits, royal symbols, and expanding Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical systems.

### Indus claims

Serpent figures and possible hooded forms on Indus objects have been interpreted as “proto-Nāga” worship. Because the script remains undeciphered and there is a substantial chronological gap, continuity is possible but unproven. Claims to read inscriptions naming Śiva or Nāgas are not scholarly consensus.

### “Aryans versus Nāgas”

The theory that Indra’s killing of Vṛtra, Garuḍa’s hostility, Khaṇḍava’s burning, and Janamejaya’s sacrifice collectively encode an Aryan war against indigenous Nāga tribes was prominent in older historical reconstruction. Problems include:

- the sources portray literal serpentine and shape-shifting bodies;
- narratives differ in period and function;
- Nāgas intermarry with epic heroes and participate in the same cosmology;
- the texts do not identify a single historical people behind them;
- archaeology reveals serpent cult without ethnic labels.

An historical memory of conflicts may have entered some narratives, but no particular conquest can presently be extracted securely.

### *Nāga* and *sarpa*

Some writers posit originally separate “Nāga” and “Sarpa” cults. In surviving literature the terms overlap too extensively to reconstruct a clean primordial distinction. Contextual distinctions—supernatural lord versus ordinary snake—are real but inconsistent.

### Water, fertility, and ecology

The strongest cross-regional pattern is association with water, rainfall, soil, fertility, and territorial boundaries. This is supported simultaneously by texts, tank-side inscriptions, spring cults, grove practice, and iconography. Yet “fertility god” is not exhaustive: Nāgas also embody poison, sovereignty, treasure, scripture, ascetic protection, and cosmic stability.

### State of the evidence

- Vedic texts survive through oral recensional transmission rather than Bronze Age manuscripts.
- Epic manuscripts are medieval and later; critical editions reconstruct earlier textual states comparatively.
- Purāṇas are fluid compilations with major regional recensions.
- Many early sculptures survive without inscriptions, making exact identification uncertain.
- Inscribed Mathura images are especially valuable because they connect an image, donor, ruler’s era, and cultic setting.
- Local wooden, earthen, painted, and grove installations decay or are ritually replaced, biasing the archaeological record toward stone.
- Colonial catalogues preserve lost provenances but sometimes impose speculative ethnic theories.

### Major scholarship

A. A. Macdonell’s *Vedic Mythology* remains useful for Vedic serpent figures but predates much later philology. J. Ph. Vogel’s *Indian Serpent-Lore* assembled an unmatched range of literary and iconographic data, though its comparative assumptions require revision. Herbert Härtel’s Sonkh excavations transformed discussion by demonstrating a built Nāga sanctuary. More recent work emphasizes animality, water, social reciprocity, regional practice, and the instability between deity, animal, and human manifestation. [“Cobra Deities and Divine Cobras”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/10/8/454)

## 11. Sources and evidentiary conclusions

The Nāgas cannot be reduced to a single god, zoological species, tribe, or symbol. The record instead shows a long historical aggregation:

1. Vedic serpent powers and anti-venom rites;
2. later Vedic use of *nāga*;
3. epic construction of an internally differentiated serpent society;
4. Buddhist and Jain adoption of Nāgas as protectors and divine rebirth classes;
5. early historic independent cults documented by shrines, images, tanks, and inscriptions;
6. Purāṇic incorporation into Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva, and regional cosmologies;
7. local spring, grove, fertility, lineage, and healing traditions;
8. trans-Asian fusion with other aquatic-serpent traditions;
9. colonial racialization and tribal nomenclature;
10. modern occult, media, political, and commercial reinvention.

The earliest record does not support treating the Ṛgvedic *ahi*, Kadru’s epic children, Kashmir’s spring deities, Buddhist Mucalinda, Jain Dharaṇendra, Karnataka serpent stones, and the modern Naga peoples as one unchanged entity. Their intersections—rather than an invented primordial unity—constitute the documented history.
