# Kuaray: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Mbyá-Guaraní sacred tradition, **Pa’i Rete Kuaray** is a primordial man-god, culture hero, ancestor or “father” of the Mbyá, and ultimately the Sun. In Apapokúva-Guaraní tradition, the nearest corresponding figure is **Ñanderyke’y**, “our elder brother,” a creator’s son and culture hero who becomes or bears the Sun. In Paraguayan and Argentine vernacular folklore, *kuarahy/kuaray* usually means the physical sun, while **Kuarahy Jára**, “Owner/Master of the Sun,” is a distinct forest or siesta spirit frequently identified with Pombero. These figures must not be collapsed into one deity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639) securely attests the Old Guaraní solar vocabulary, conventionally printed in forms such as *quaracy* under seventeenth-century missionary orthography. It does **not** furnish the later Mbyá narrative biography of Pa’i Rete Kuaray. The earliest extensive, securely published divine narrative in the relevant Guaraní complex is Curt Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva corpus, recorded principally during 1905–1912 and published in German in 1914; its elder-brother hero is Ñanderyke’y, not simply the Pa’i Rete Kuaray of Cadogan’s Mbyá recension. Pa’i Rete Kuaray’s full Mbyá cycle was recorded by León Cadogan in the 1940s–1950s, partially published before its definitive appearance as chapter VIII of *Ayvu Rapyta* in 1959.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY LEXICAL]** Colonial lexical or documentary evidence securely dated, but not necessarily evidence for a divine personality.
- **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC]** The oldest controlled ethnographic recording of a living oral tradition.
- **[MBYÁ SACRED]** Material dictated or sung by Mbyá religious authorities and published by Cadogan.
- **[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva recension.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A documented version belonging to another Guaraní community or regional folklore.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Paraguayan or Argentine vernacular material, not automatically equivalent to Indigenous sacred teaching.
- **[MISSIONARY/SYNCRETIC]** Material shaped by Christian translation, mission institutions, or documented religious interchange.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** An analytical comparison proposed by a modern scholar.
- **[LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]** An authored literary arrangement subsequently mistaken for an ancient canon.
- **[MODERN RETELLING/INVENTION]** Recent synthesis, adaptation, commercial presentation, or unsupported attribution.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Kuaray and Kuarahy

**[EARLY LEXICAL]** Modern Mbyá **kuaray** and Paraguayan Guaraní **kuarahy** mean “sun.” Older sources and modern Portuguese or Spanish texts variously print *Cuaray, Cuarahy, Quaracy, Guaracy, Kuarasy,* and *Kuarasi*. The differences partly reflect dialect and partly successive orthographies:

- Mbyá: **kuaray**
- Paraguayan Guaraní: **kuarahy**
- Old Tupi and historical Brazilian spellings: **kûarasy, Guaracy**
- Cadogan’s Spanish-era typography: often **Cuaray**
- Apapokúva: Nimuendajú normally identifies the solar hero primarily by the relational name **Ñanderyke’y**, not by making *Kuaray* his sole personal name.

The spelling **Guaraci**, common in Brazilian popular mythology, is a Portuguese-mediated Old Tupi form. It should not be retrojected mechanically into every Guaraní-speaking community.

### Etymology

**[SECURE LINGUISTIC CORE]** Comparative Tupi-Guaraní evidence reconstructs a word approximately \**kʷaracɨ* or \**kʷaratʃɨ* for “sun.” Mbyá *kuaray*, Paraguayan *kuarahy*, and Old Tupi *kûarasy* are cognates.

**[DISPUTED ETYMOLOGY]** Popular segmentation as “source/origin of the day,” “manifestation of creative power,” or “sun-mother” is not uniformly accepted. A comparative proposal analyzes the second element as a historical word for “mother,” but present-day speakers do not necessarily perceive the word through that ancient segmentation. Cadogan himself discussed the religious meaning of *Kuaray* in ways richer than a dictionary gloss, but theological interpretation is not the same thing as demonstrated historical morphology. Claims that the name transparently means “creator of all light,” “lord of the universe,” or similar phrases are modern expansions unless tied to a particular speaker and text.

### Pa’i Rete Kuaray

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Cadogan translates **Pa’i Rete Kuaray** approximately as “the lord/priest whose body is like the Sun” or “the man-god of the solar body.” Its elements are significant:

- **pa’i**: in Paraguayan usage now often “priest,” especially a Catholic priest; in Indigenous religious language it may denote a sacred male person, religious specialist, or venerable divine figure.
- **rete**: “body,” with an incorporated relational element.
- **kuaray**: “sun.”

Cadogan calls him a **hombre-dios**, “man-god.” This is a translation choice, not proof of a Guaraní category identical to the Greco-Roman “demigod.”

### Other titles and relational names

- **Ñanderyke’y / Ñande Ryke’y** — “our elder brother.” **[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** The principal culture hero in Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva text. He is a son of Ñanderuvuçú and the senior of the two mythic brothers.
- **Tyvyry / Tyvyra’i** — “younger brother.” **[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** The junior twin or half-brother; later associated with the Moon.
- **Jachyrã / Jasyrã** — “the future Moon” or “he who is to become Moon.” **[MBYÁ SACRED]** Cadogan’s younger companion of Pa’i Rete Kuaray. The suffix *-rã* marks prospective or future status.
- **Kuaray Ru Ete / Ñande Ru Kuaray** — “true father Sun” or “our father Sun.” **[REGIONAL/TERMINOLOGICAL VARIANT]** Such titles occur in Guaraní religious discourse, but their referent varies: the celestial Sun, a divine father identified with it, or Pa’i Rete Kuaray after ascent.
- **Kuarahy Jára** — “owner/master of the Sun.” **[CORRIENTES FOLKLORE]** A forest or siesta guardian recorded in relation to Pombero traditions. Despite the name, he is not demonstrably the same being as Mbyá Pa’i Rete Kuaray.
- **Kuarahy Ra’y** — literally “son of the Sun.” **[LATE FOLKLORE/MUSEUM SYSTEMATIZATION]** Modern Paraguayan presentations depict him as a small blond child who colors fruit, clay, and flowers. This is not the Pa’i Rete Kuaray cycle as published by Cadogan and lacks comparable early documentation.

### False equivalences

**[MISSIONARY/SYNCRETIC]** Christian missionaries regularly used Indigenous divine vocabulary to translate Christian concepts. *Tupã* became the standard word for the Christian God in much mission literature, while *pa’i* came to denote priests. This makes Christian influence a real historical layer in recorded Guaraní religious language, but it does not establish that Pa’i Rete Kuaray was originally Jesus, God the Son, or a displaced Christian saint.

**[MODERN INVENTION]** Internet “pantheons” often make Kuaray one member of a single pan-Guaraní family headed by Tupã and Arasy, then attach Narciso R. Colmán’s seven monsters to it. No early source supplies that integrated canon.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial documentation

#### Montoya, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639)

**[EARLY LEXICAL]** Printed at Madrid by Juan Sánchez, the *Tesoro* is a Guaraní–Spanish lexicon created in the Jesuit mission environment. It is physical evidence that solar terminology cognate with modern *kuarahy/kuaray* existed in seventeenth-century Guaraní. It provides extensive linguistic and cultural examples but no securely identifiable full Pa’i Rete Kuaray biography.

What it establishes:

- the colonial-era word for the Sun;
- associated idioms and a large body of Classical or Mission Guaraní;
- the missionary orthographic and semantic environment through which later texts were transmitted.

What it does not establish:

- that all modern Kuaray myths existed in their recorded form by 1639;
- that “Kuaray” was worshipped through a centralized temple cult;
- the Colmán pantheon;
- equivalence with Kuarahy Jára or Kuarahy Ra’y.

#### Montoya, *Conquista espiritual* (1639)

**[MISSIONARY DOCUMENT]** Montoya’s history of Jesuit work in Paraguay, Paraná, Uruguay, Guairá, and Tape documents evangelization, Indigenous resistance, mission settlements, the destruction caused by slave-raiding bandeirantes, and Christian judgments about Indigenous ritual authorities. It is essential for the historical setting but is not a neutral catalogue of Guaraní theology.

Missionary descriptions frequently call non-Christian religious specialists “sorcerers,” “false prophets,” or servants of the devil. Such labels document the missionary encounter, not an Indigenous classification of Kuaray.

### Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva corpus

#### *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* (1914)

**[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC; APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** Published in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46, pp. 284–403. The evidence came from Nimuendajú’s years among Apapokúva/Ñandeva communities in southern Brazil, especially through named Indigenous authorities such as Joguyroquy. A Portuguese translation appeared much later as *As lendas da criação e destruição do mundo*.

The work records:

- Ñanderuvuçú, “our great father,” as creator;
- Ñandecy, “our mother”;
- two brothers associated with different fathers;
- the mother’s journey and death among jaguars;
- the brothers’ survival, discovery of the murder, and vengeance;
- numerous animal-transforming and culture-establishing adventures;
- Ñanderyke’y’s communication with his absent father through ritual song and dance;
- the brothers’ celestial destiny;
- Apapokúva teachings on cataclysm, the Land without Evil, souls, ritual, and migration.

This is a close structural cognate of the Mbyá Kuaray cycle, but its names, genealogy, theology, and episode order are not identical.

### Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus

#### Early articles, 1940s–1950s

Cadogan published portions or related versions under titles including “Jasy ra’y ojovahéi,” “Parakáo Ñe’ẽngatu,” and “Pa’i Rete Kuaray,” followed by comparative discussion in “Mitología en la zona guaraní” (1951). **[MBYÁ SACRED/FOLKLORE INTERFACE]** These show that he was documenting both esoteric Mbyá teaching and survivals or transformations in Paraguayan folklore.

#### *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959)

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Full title: *Ayvu Rapyta: textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá*. The principal sacred texts were communicated by Mbyá authorities including Pablo Vera, Kachirito, Che’iro, Mayor Francisco, Tomás, Cirilo, Higinio, and others. Cadogan emphasized that the speakers, not he, were the true authors.

The Pa’i Rete Kuaray cycle is chapter VIII. Cadogan calls it the beginning of the second part of the Mbyá sacred annals. It contains the earthly appearance of Pa’i Rete Kuaray, the creation or acquisition of Jachyrã as younger brother, the journey among fruit trees and jaguars, the revelation of the mother’s death, vengeance, teaching activities, and final celestial ordering.

The surviving “manuscript gap” differs from ancient textual traditions: Cadogan’s written and printed text is twentieth-century, while the tradition claims primordial events and was orally maintained. One may date the recording and publication securely; one cannot assign an archaeological date to each oral episode.

#### *Ywyra Ñe’ery* and later Cadogan collections

**[MBYÁ ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Cadogan’s later publications preserve prayers, botanical and spiritual vocabulary, ritual speech, and further Mbyá traditions. They provide the semantic world in which Kuaray operates but do not turn all Mbyá sacred figures into one fixed hierarchy.

### Comparative works

- Alfred Métraux, *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus Tupi-Guarani* (1928): **[SCHOLARLY COMPARISON]** Important for older Tupinambá and comparative Tupi-Guaraní mythology. Tupinambá material cannot be quoted as direct evidence for Mbyá belief without labeling the comparison.
- Egon Schaden, *Aspectos fundamentais da cultura guarani* (first edition 1954; revised editions): compares Guaraní communities while stressing their differences.
- Hélène Clastres, *La Terre sans Mal* (1975): historical and theoretical study of Tupi-Guaraní prophetism and migration.
- Pierre Clastres, *Le Grand Parler* (1974): French translations and philosophical presentation of Guaraní sacred speech.
- Bartomeu Melià: extensive linguistic, historical, and theological work on Guaraní religion, colonial evangelization, and the “word-soul.”
- Branislava Sušnik: ethnohistorical studies indispensable for Paraguay, though her broad cultural reconstructions require checking against the primary corpus for each individual claim.
- Juan B. Ambrosetti, *Supersticiones y leyendas*: early regional folklore evidence for Misiones and Corrientes, including Kuarahy Jára/Pombero.
- Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini: twentieth-century Argentine folktale collections, valuable for regional transmission rather than primordial theology.
- Narciso R. Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929): **[LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]**, not a transcript of a single ancient oral canon.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Pa’i Rete Kuaray is simultaneously:

- a primordial culture hero walking on the first earth;
- a revealer of the edible or dangerous qualities of plants and fruits;
- the senior and wiser companion of Jachyrã;
- avenger of the First Mother;
- organizer of features of the present world;
- teacher of human conduct and ritual;
- progenitor or father of the Mbyá;
- a solar being whose final celestial station accounts for the Sun.

His solar identity is therefore not merely “a sun god who controls weather.” The narrative presents an earthly, bodily culture hero who becomes or manifests the Sun.

**[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** Ñanderyke’y likewise organizes the world, overcomes jaguars, communicates ritually with his divine father, and assumes a solar or celestial role. Apapokúva eschatology places greater explicit emphasis on successive destructions of the world and attaining the Land without Evil.

### Character and limitations

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Kuaray possesses extraordinary knowledge and creative power, but his story includes discovery, grief, testing, and imperfect restoration. The parrot’s revelation of his mother’s fate causes bitter weeping and awakening to his origin. This emotional sequence is central: he does not traverse the world with omniscience displayed at every moment.

He is regularly the competent elder to Jachyrã’s impulsive, sexual, comic, or mistaken younger brother. Yet the contrast is relational rather than a metaphysical dualism of absolute good and evil.

### Symbols and material attributes

Securely associated elements include:

- **the Sun and solar radiance**;
- **the human body**, expressed in *Pa’i Rete Kuaray*;
- **fruit trees**, especially guavira and pindó, whose names and properties occur in the journey;
- **the kurupika’y branch**, from which Jachyrã is created in Cadogan’s account;
- **ritual song, dance, and sacred speech**;
- **traps, river crossings, and stratagems** in the jaguar vengeance cycle;
- **the pindo palm**, a cosmic or imperishable tree in broader Mbyá tradition.

### Iconography

**[NEGATIVE FINDING]** No pre-Columbian image can presently be identified securely as “Kuaray” by inscription or continuous iconographic attributes. Guaraní religion left no known Kuaray temple-statue corpus comparable to labeled Mesopotamian or Classical divine art.

**[MODERN RECEPTION]** Modern illustrations commonly show:

- a golden-skinned or radiant Indigenous youth;
- paired boys representing Sun and Moon;
- jaguars and a forest background;
- European solar disks or haloes.

These are interpretive illustrations, not recoveries of a securely attested ancient iconographic type.

The Ramón Elías Mythological Museum’s small blond **Kuarahy Ra’y** belongs to a modern Paraguayan folklore display. Its blondness and picturesque function of coloring fruit and flowers should not be transferred to Pa’i Rete Kuaray.

### Sacred correspondences not established

No early Kuaray-specific evidence securely establishes a canonical color, number, weekday, metal, zodiacal sign, planet beyond the Sun, temple direction, weapon, or sacrificial animal. Lists assigning him gold, Sunday, the number one, lions, or solar chakras are modern comparative invention.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to divine beings

### Mbyá genealogy

**[MBYÁ SACRED]**

- **First Mother / Ñande Chy Ete:** mortal or earth-associated mother of Pa’i Rete Kuaray, impregnated by a divine father without ordinary sexual union in Cadogan’s explanation.
- **Divine father:** versions and explanatory summaries differ over the precise divine name and level of the Mbyá hierarchy. It is unsafe to substitute the internet-standard “Ñamandú and Ñande Sy had twins” for Cadogan’s actual episode.
- **Jachyrã:** not a uterine twin in the decisive Mbyá version. Kuaray creates him from a branch as a companion and calls or treats him as younger brother.
- **The Mbyá:** Kuaray is described as father or divine ancestor of the people.

### Apapokúva genealogy

**[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]**

- **Ñanderuvuçú:** father of Ñanderyke’y.
- **Ñandecy:** mother of the brothers.
- **Ñanderu Mbaecuaá:** in Nimuendajú’s account, associated with the second conception.
- **Tyvyry:** younger brother or half-brother, gestated by the same woman but attributed to a different father.

Thus “Kuaray and Jasy were twins, sons of one father” is not a universal Guaraní datum. The Apapokúva version gives womb-sharing brothers of different paternity; the Mbyá sacred account makes Kuaray the creator of his future lunar brother.

### Every documented named divine interaction

The list below is confined to interactions for which the consulted principal sources provide a discrete episode. Collective jaguars and unnamed beings are included afterward because they are indispensable but are not single named gods.

- **Ñamandu Ru Ete / the First Father — Kuaray belongs within the divine order ultimately established by the primordial father; later summaries say the celestial offices of Sun and Moon are assigned within that order.** Source: Mbyá materials in Cadogan, culminating in *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959). **[MBYÁ SACRED; wording varies by recension]**

- **The divine father of Pa’i Rete Kuaray — generates Kuaray in the First Mother without ordinary intercourse and withdraws or resides beyond the hero’s immediate earthly reach.** Source: Cadogan, “Mitología en la zona guaraní” (1951) and *Ayvu Rapyta* VIII (1959). **[MBYÁ SACRED]**

- **Ñande Chy Ete / the First Mother — Kuaray guides her while still unborn; after she strikes or rebukes the child in her womb, guidance ceases; jaguars kill her; Kuaray later learns her fate, mourns her, attempts restoration in some versions, and avenges her.** Source: Cadogan’s Pa’i Rete Kuaray corpus, definitive edition 1959. **[MBYÁ SACRED]**

- **Jachyrã / future Jasy — Kuaray creates the future Moon from a kurupika’y branch, makes him his younger brother and traveling companion, teaches him fruit names and correct conduct, repeatedly repairs his mistakes, and assigns or enables his lunar destiny.** Source: Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* VIII (1959), with earlier published fragments. **[MBYÁ SACRED]**

- **Jasy — after women complain of a nocturnal sexual intruder, Kuaray arranges for them to mark his face with resin and burnt takuapi; the culprit is Jasy, whose permanent facial marks explain the Moon’s appearance; Kuaray orders him to his nocturnal station.** Source: Cadogan’s Paraguayan folklore discussion, collected and published during the mid-twentieth century. **[FOLKLORE DERIVATIVE OF MBYÁ CYCLE]**

- **Ñanderuvuçú — Ñanderyke’y is his elder son; the son performs shamanic song and dance to communicate with his departed father, receives power or direction, and later occupies the solar zenith.** Source: Nimuendajú, *Die Sagen* (1914). **[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT; EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC]**

- **Ñandecy — Ñanderyke’y is carried in her womb, attempts unsuccessfully to restore her body after the jaguars kill her, and is separated from her when Ñanderuvuçú takes and restores her soul in the Land without Evil.** Source: Nimuendajú (1914). **[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]**

- **Tyvyry — Ñanderyke’y travels with his younger brother, instructs him, joins him in vengeance against the jaguars, and shares numerous culture-creating adventures; their final differentiation supports the Sun/Moon opposition.** Source: Nimuendajú (1914). **[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]**

- **Ñanderu Mbaecuaá — he is the attributed father of the younger brother and thus stands in a relationship of differentiated paternity to Ñanderyke’y’s family. No extended face-to-face encounter with the solar hero is securely prominent in the published summary.** Source: Nimuendajú (1914). **[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT; ABSENCE OF DIRECT INTERACTION NOTED]**

- **Charía — Kuaray/Ñanderyke’y and his brother encounter this trickster or adversarial figure in comic contests and deceptions, including fishing episodes in which the brothers interfere with his catch.** Source: Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva cycle (1914), with cognate Guaraní recensions. **[APAPOKÚVA/REGIONAL VARIANT]**

- **Tupã — no secure episode in Cadogan’s Pa’i Rete Kuaray cycle establishes the internet-common relationship “Tupã is Kuaray’s father and king.” Tupã Ru Ete occupies a particular position in Mbyá theology, but the Christian-era prominence of *Tupã* must be separated from Kuaray’s recorded genealogy.** **[NEGATIVE FINDING; MISSIONARY/SYNCRETIC CAUTION]**

- **Tau, Keraná, and the seven monsters — no interaction with Kuaray is attested in the principal Nimuendajú or Cadogan sacred corpora. Their incorporation into a single Kuaray-centered pantheon derives principally from modern readings of Colmán’s literary construction.** **[NEGATIVE FINDING; LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Collective supernatural interaction

- **The jaguars:** kill the First Mother; their old mother or grandmother shelters or raises the surviving child or children; Kuaray and Jasy/Ñanderyke’y and Tyvyry later lure jaguars into traps or across treacherous water and destroy most of them. One pregnant jaguar may survive, explaining the continued existence of jaguars. **[MBYÁ SACRED and APAPOKÚVA VARIANTS]**

- **The good-speaking parrot, Parakáo Ñe’ẽngatu:** reveals that the jaguars killed Kuaray’s mother, precipitating his grief and revenge. Although not necessarily a “god,” it is a named mythic agent and indispensable interlocutor. **[MBYÁ SACRED]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The unborn guide and the mother’s death

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** The First Mother sets out through the primordial landscape in search of the absent divine father. Kuaray, already alive and articulate within her womb, directs her path and asks her to pluck flowers. When an insect stings her, she angrily strikes or rebukes her belly. The unborn guide then refuses further directions. She takes the wrong path and reaches the settlement of the jaguars.

A jaguar grandmother attempts to protect her, but returning jaguars discover and kill her. The unborn divine child survives. Details of extraction, concealment, failed eating, and nurture vary between narrations.

The episode defines Kuaray before birth as knowledgeable but responsive to reciprocal respect. The mother’s momentary anger has cosmic consequences, but the text does not reduce her to a moral caricature.

### Were Kuaray and Jasy twins?

This is the dossier’s principal textual dispute.

**[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** Nimuendajú records two boys carried by the same woman and attributed to different divine fathers. They are appropriately discussed as twins or uterine brothers, though their paternity distinguishes them.

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Cadogan’s “Beautiful Words” do not make Kuaray and the future Moon identical uterine twins. Kuaray survives first and later creates Jachyrã from vegetal material as companion and younger brother. Carlos Fausto emphasizes this transformation: in the Mbyá recension the Sun produces the Moon from himself or by his own creative action, denying perfect twin identity.

**[MODERN RETELLING]** “Kuaray and Jasy, twin sons of Ñamandú and Ñande Sy” is therefore a flattened synthesis. It preserves the broad Sun/Moon brother pattern but erases the decisive difference between the Apapokúva and Mbyá accounts.

### Creation of Jachyrã

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Lonely after his mother’s death, Kuaray takes a branch of *kurupika’y* and awakens its latent divine capacity, producing Jachyrã, the future Moon. He establishes him as younger brother.

This episode carries an important Mbyá conception of creation: *ombojera*, often translated “to create,” can mean causing a latent form or capacity to unfold or emerge. Kuaray is thus less a craftsman making inert matter than a revealer who brings a potential being into manifest existence.

### The fruit journey

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** The brothers travel beneath or among fruit trees. Kuaray knows the names, properties, and appropriate consumption of fruits; Jachyrã experiments, disobeys, or misunderstands. Guavira, pindó, and other species structure the sequence.

The cycle is etiological and pedagogical:

- it maps edible resources;
- distinguishes mature knowledge from impulsive appetite;
- presents ecological knowledge as sacred inheritance;
- establishes the elder/younger asymmetry.

The younger brother is not merely “evil.” His mistakes generate differentiation and narrative movement.

### Revelation by the parrot

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Parakáo Ñe’ẽngatu, “Parrot of the Discerning/Beautiful Words,” tells Kuaray that the beings among whom he has lived killed his mother. Kuaray leans for support and weeps bitterly; his understanding opens, and he recognizes both his origin and destiny.

The episode makes speech revelatory. Knowledge of kinship and history comes through rightly uttered words, a theme consonant with the wider Mbyá identification of sacred speech, personhood, and soul.

### Vengeance on the jaguars

**[MBYÁ SACRED; APAPOKÚVA PARALLEL]** The brothers devise traps and deceptions. Jaguars are lured to fruit, into a *mundéu* deadfall, or toward water with unstable or withdrawing banks. Many are killed or cast away. A pregnant female commonly escapes or refuses the fatal invitation, accounting for surviving jaguars.

Variants differ over:

- which traps are used;
- which brother devises them;
- whether the jaguar grandmother is spared;
- how the brothers cross the water;
- how many jaguars survive.

The episode is both filial vengeance and cosmological differentiation: primordial jaguars are reduced from a society of human-eating masters to dangerous animals inhabiting the later world.

### Attempted restoration of the mother

**[APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** Ñanderyke’y tries to reconstruct his mother from her remains but cannot complete the act. Ñanderuvuçú has taken her soul and restores her in the Land without Evil.

**[MBYÁ VARIANTS]** Some Mbyá-associated summaries describe Kuaray reviving or reconstituting his mother in animal form, including a paca; the exact sequence must be cited to the particular version. It should not be blended silently with Nimuendajú’s account.

The common structural point is that even the solar culture hero does not simply reverse death and restore the former earthly condition. Mortality has entered the world.

### Moon’s misconduct and facial markings

**[PARAGUAYAN FOLKLORE WITH MBYÁ ROOTS]** Women complain to Pa’i Rete Kuaray that someone enters at night and lies beside or pursues them without consent. Kuaray instructs them to coat their hands with sticky resin and burnt takuapi leaves and mark the intruder. The culprit is Jasy. He washes repeatedly but cannot remove the marks, explaining the visible stains on the Moon. Kuaray assigns him the night and an association with women.

The episode must be reported plainly: Jasy’s conduct is sexual intrusion, and the marking is punitive exposure. It is not securely present in exactly this form in all Guaraní communities.

### Ascent and celestial differentiation

**[MBYÁ SACRED/APAPOKÚVA VARIANT]** After completing earthly transformations, the brothers assume differentiated celestial positions. The elder becomes or manifests the Sun; the younger becomes the Moon. Their inequality—wisdom and error, elder and younger, day and night—is preserved in the sky.

Modern summaries sometimes say Ñamandú simply “appoints” the brothers governors of day and night. That is a concise theological retelling, not an adequate replacement for the journey, vengeance, instruction, and transformation cycles.

### The Land without Evil

**[APAPOKÚVA ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Nimuendajú connected mythology, ritual dance, migration, and the quest for **Yvy Marã e’ỹ**, the “land without evil” or imperishable land. Ñanderyke’y’s solar station and the creator’s celestial domain belong to this eschatological geography.

**[HISTORIOGRAPHIC DISPUTE]** Hélène Clastres treated the Land without Evil and prophetic migration as a major key to Tupi-Guaraní religion. Later scholarship has warned against universalizing the Apapokúva and certain Tupinambá evidence to every Guaraní group or interpreting every migration as a messianic movement. Kuaray participates in the cosmological setting; he is not securely documented as commanding every historically observed migration.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No monumental Kuaray cult

**[NEGATIVE ARCHAEOLOGICAL FINDING]** No securely identified Kuaray temple, priestly corporation, cult statue, dedicatory inscription, or temple treasury is known. The absence reflects both Guaraní material-religious forms and colonial destruction; it does not imply an absence of religion.

### Opy and sacred performance

**[MBYÁ ETHNOGRAPHIC]** The central Mbyá ritual setting is the **opy**, often translated “prayer house.” Sacred speech, singing, dancing, smoke, ritual instruments, naming, healing, and communication with divine fathers take place there. Kuaray belongs to an enacted oral theology rather than an image-centered temple cult.

Relevant practices include:

- collective singing and dance;
- the use of **mbaraka** rattles and **takuapu** rhythm staffs;
- tobacco smoke and ritual attention;
- sacred personal names understood as having divine provenance;
- instruction by religious leaders commonly called *karaí*, *opygua*, or related regional titles;
- seasonal rites connected with maize, naming, and renewal.

A modern report of an Mbyá ceremony describes a portion dedicated to Kuaray under the expression **Ñande Ru Tenonde’i**. This supports continued ritual vitality, but one observation cannot define all contemporary Mbyá practice.

### Solar observation

The everyday Sun obviously regulates travel, labor, planting, warmth, and time. **[CAUTION]** These facts do not by themselves prove a separate institutional “sun cult.” Ethnographic evidence must show when solar address is directed to Pa’i Rete Kuaray, to a divine father represented by the Sun, or simply to the celestial body.

### Offerings and sacrifice

**[NEGATIVE FINDING]** The reviewed primary Kuaray corpus gives no evidence of human sacrifice offered to Kuaray. No Kuaray-specific sacrificial altar or victim class is documented.

Early colonial sources describe warfare, captive execution, and anthropophagy among some coastal Tupinambá and related populations. Carlos Fausto analyzes the changing relationship between cannibalism and Christianity across Tupi-Guaraní history. These practices cannot be reassigned to a Kuaray cult without evidence.

Likewise, no Kuaray-associated “temple prostitution” is documented. Any such claim would be an unsupported import from Old World categories.

### The Jesuit reductions

**[MISSIONARY HISTORY]** From the early seventeenth century, Jesuit reductions reorganized Guaraní settlement, labor, worship, education, music, and language. Missionaries displaced Indigenous ritual specialists, translated Christianity into Guaraní, destroyed or condemned practices they classified as idolatry, and promoted the Mass, saints, Christian marriage, and sacramental life.

Montoya’s works arose from this setting. Bandeirante slave raids devastated the Guairá reductions, forcing major migrations. The Jesuits were expelled from Spanish territories in 1767–1768. None of these events provides a dated “destruction of the Temple of Kuaray,” because no such structure is documented; their impact was instead on speakers, communities, ritual authority, language, and transmission.

### Politics and suppression

Religious suppression was inseparable from colonial control. Indigenous prophets and ritual leaders could organize resistance to mission residence, labor extraction, and clerical authority. Mission sources’ charges of devil worship must therefore be read both as theology and colonial politics.

In later centuries, land loss, forced relocation, boarding education, settler violence, and missionary activity continued to disrupt Mbyá and Ñandeva ritual transmission. Conversely, Indigenous communities selectively adopted, translated, refused, or reinterpreted Christian elements. “Syncretism” was never a one-way replacement.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1639: reconstructable linguistic antiquity

**[LINGUISTIC RECONSTRUCTION]** The cognate solar word across Tupi-Guaraní languages demonstrates considerable antiquity. The widespread “twins and jaguars” structure likewise precedes its twentieth-century transcription, but no exact date can be assigned to the Mbyá Pa’i Rete Kuaray recension.

Comparisons with Tupinambá, Tembé, Guarayu, Chiriguano/Guaraní, and other Amazonian twin cycles show deep circulation of motifs:

- a pregnant wandering mother;
- jaguars who kill her;
- surviving miraculous children;
- vengeance;
- culture-making adventures;
- eventual Sun/Moon differentiation.

Structural breadth does not prove that every episode derives unchanged from a single Proto-Tupi-Guaraní myth.

### 1609–1768: mission transformation

**[MISSIONARY/SYNCRETIC]** Jesuit reductions produced the first large written Guaraní corpus. Solar vocabulary survived in dictionaries and Christianized texts, while Indigenous sacred narratives were rarely transcribed sympathetically. Christian terms entered Guaraní, and Guaraní categories reshaped Indigenous understandings of Christian teaching.

The later resemblance between Pa’i Rete Kuaray and Christ—a divinely generated man-god, teacher, model, and mediator—has prompted comparison. Direct derivation is unproved. The jaguar cycle and twin structure have broad Indigenous comparanda, while some theological language in twentieth-century texts may reflect centuries of Christian contact.

### Nineteenth century: regional folklore differentiation

**[FOLKLORE]** In Paraguay, Misiones, and Corrientes, forest-owner, siesta, child-warning, and bird-protector traditions circulated under names including Pombero, Jasy Jatere, and Kuarahy Jára. Ambrosetti reported that in parts of Corrientes Kuarahy Jára or Pombero was imagined as a tall, thin man with a very large straw hat and cane who guarded birds and carried off children hunting at siesta.

This being is functionally closer to a forest owner or disciplinary spirit than to Cadogan’s primordial solar ancestor. The City of Corrientes now presents Kuarahy Jára in a public mythological park, an example of folklore becoming civic heritage.

### 1905–1914: Nimuendajú and the Apapokúva record

**[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Nimuendajú learned the language, lived among Guaraní communities, documented named informants, and published the first detailed controlled account of this religious complex. His work made Ñanderuvuçú, Ñanderyke’y, world destruction, ritual migration, and the Land without Evil central subjects of scholarship.

### 1940s–1959: Cadogan and the Mbyá sacred texts

**[MBYÁ SACRED]** Cadogan’s relations with Mbyá leaders gave him access to sacred discourse not ordinarily communicated to outsiders. Chapters of *Ayvu Rapyta* appeared from 1953 onward; the full work followed in 1959. This transformed scholarship by demonstrating that Mbyá theology centered not on the missionary stereotype of Tupã as a universal supreme god but on a more differentiated order of divine fathers, sacred speech, and primordial creative acts.

Pa’i Rete Kuaray emerged in print as a distinctly Mbyá culture hero and ancestor, not merely the astronomical Sun.

### 1929 onward: the Colmán effect

**[LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]** Narciso R. Colmán, writing as Rosicrán, published *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* in 1929. His poetic work assembled a grand Guaraní origin cycle and helped popularize Tau, Keraná, and the seven monstrous offspring as a coherent pantheon.

Individual beings such as Kurupi, Jasy Jatere, Pombero, and Luisõ predate Colmán in regional folklore, but the seven-sibling genealogy and its polished narrative architecture are literary systematization. Kuaray’s insertion into that pantheon on websites, games, and illustrated charts is later still.

### Late twentieth century to present

**[CONTINUING INDIGENOUS TRADITION]** Mbyá communities in Paraguay, Argentina, and Brazil continue sacred singing, naming, opy ritual, and transmission of cosmological knowledge. The tradition is not merely an extinct mythology preserved by Cadogan.

**[CULTURAL REVIVAL]** Guaraní-language education, Indigenous publishing, political organization, museums, public sculpture, theater, and digital media have made Kuaray more visible. These activities range from community-based renewal to outsider commercialization and should be distinguished case by case.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and translation

Pierre Clastres’s French rendering of Guaraní sacred discourse gave *Ayvu Rapyta* a wide philosophical readership. Later poetic adaptations often emphasize creation through word, music, light, and hummingbird imagery. Some are valuable literary responses; they are not substitutes for Cadogan’s Guaraní text.

Kaká Werá Jecupé’s *Tupã Tenondé* and related Indigenous literary work present creative contemporary engagements. Their authority is modern Indigenous authorship, not a claim that every formulation is verbatim pre-contact doctrine.

### Paraguayan national folklore

Schoolbooks, museums, tourism, and civic sculpture often present “Guaraní mythology” as a national pantheon. Kuaray may appear alongside Colmán’s seven monsters, Pombero, and Jasy Jatere. This creates an accessible national mythology but collapses:

- Mbyá sacred narrative;
- Apapokúva religion;
- colonial mission Guaraní;
- Paraguayan mestizo folklore;
- Corrientes and Misiones legends;
- literary inventions.

The blond child Kuarahy Ra’y who colors fruit and clay is a conspicuous example of modern folkloric iconography.

### Argentine reception

Misiones and Corrientes maintain living Guaraní and mestizo traditions. Kuarahy Jára/Pombero belongs to the region’s siesta and forest-warning lore. Public attractions such as Corrientes’s Paseo Mitológico turn these figures into civic visual culture.

A recent Corrientes retelling that gives the twins the names Erekeí and Erevú, introduces Añá and his daughter, and has Tupã assign them solar and lunar offices is a composite popular narrative. Its value is as twenty-first-century folklore, not as the earliest textual form.

### Esotericism and neopaganism

**[NEGATIVE FINDING]** The dedicated search found no well-documented Kuaray-specific Renaissance occult, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, ceremonial-magic, or historical grimoire tradition.

Modern “solar deity” lists sometimes assimilate Kuaray/Guaraci to Apollo, Ra, Inti, or a generic masculine solar archetype. These are comparativist or neopagan constructions, not historical Guaraní equations.

Individual contemporary practitioners may honor Kuaray, but no reliable evidence located establishes a large, organized, numerically documented Kuaray neopagan movement. Indigenous Mbyá ritual must not be relabeled “neopaganism.”

### Psychology and New Age use

Universal “solar hero” or Jungian readings are modern interpretive overlays. They may analyze the story but cannot serve as evidence that Mbyá narrators understood Kuaray as an archetype of ego-consciousness.

Claims that Kuaray represents chakras, extraterrestrials, Lemuria, “quantum solar frequency,” or an ancient global sun religion are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless a specific modern author is being documented.

### Games and popular media

Kuaray appears sporadically in mythology databases, role-playing material, proposed game pantheons, illustrations, and educational projects. These frequently:

- use the Brazilian spelling Guaraci;
- treat him as a generic “god of the Sun”;
- make Tupã his king or father;
- call Jasy his literal twin;
- join him to Colmán’s monsters.

That composite is modern database mythology.

### Political meanings

Kuaray and the twin story can serve Indigenous assertions of prior presence and territorial continuity. Contemporary Guaraní narrators in Argentina use origin traditions to contest histories that portray them as recent migrants. Such political use does not make the myth inauthentic; it is part of the history of how living narrative functions.

No substantial Kuaray-specific appropriation by a documented extremist movement was located. Generic nationalist appropriation of an undifferentiated “Guaraní pantheon” is better attested than extremist cult use of Kuaray himself.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is the recorded mythology pre-Christian?

Two extreme positions are untenable:

1. that Cadogan transcribed a completely untouched pre-contact scripture;
2. that Christian resemblance proves wholesale missionary invention.

Nimuendajú and Cadogan both found Indigenous structures incompatible with the standard missionary pantheon: multiple divine fathers, the limited rank of Tupã in some systems, word-soul theology, jaguar transformations, and culture-hero cycles. Yet the texts were recorded after centuries of Christian presence and use terms transformed by contact.

Carlos Fausto’s study of cannibalism and Christianity treats Guaraní religious history as creative appropriation and transformation rather than pure survival or passive conversion.

### Twins or created brothers?

This dispute is resolved textually, not by harmonization:

- Apapokúva: same mother, differentiated fathers, twin-like brothers.
- Mbyá: Kuaray precedes and creates Jachyrã as younger companion.
- Popular syntheses: usually restore literal twinship and one father.

The variants represent different theological solutions to the Amerindian problem of similarity and difference. Lévi-Straussian comparison interprets twin myths as denying perfect identity; Fausto notes that the Mbyá version intensifies this by having the elder produce the younger.

### What does *ombojera* mean?

Cadogan’s Spanish “crear” can suggest creation from nothing, while Mbyá usage more often evokes unfolding, causing to arise, or making manifest what exists potentially. This matters for Kuaray’s creation of Jachyrã: he activates or reveals divine capacity in vegetal material.

### Was Tupã the supreme Guaraní god?

**Discredited generalization:** missionaries and popular writers made Tupã the single creator of “Guaraní mythology.” Nimuendajú and Cadogan showed that, in the communities they studied, Tupã was one divine power within a wider order and had been disproportionately elevated through Christian translation.

The corrective must also avoid a new universalism: different Guaraní communities developed different hierarchies under different histories of contact.

### How ancient is the Land without Evil?

Nimuendajú treated Apapokúva religion and migration toward Yvy Marã e’ỹ as deeply connected. Hélène Clastres expanded the theme into a major interpretation of Tupi-Guaraní prophetism. Later critics argue that “Land without Evil” has sometimes been generalized too broadly or made to explain migrations whose causes included warfare, enslavement, land pressure, and colonial flight.

### Cadogan’s mediation

*Ayvu Rapyta* is not an anonymous timeless scripture. It is a collaborative twentieth-century textual event:

- Mbyá authorities selected and dictated material;
- Cadogan transcribed, translated, ordered, annotated, and published it;
- secrecy and unequal access shaped what could be recorded;
- Cadogan’s Christian and comparative vocabulary affected translation;
- the Indigenous narrators retained authorship of the oral texts.

This mediation does not invalidate the corpus; it defines its evidentiary status.

### Colmán’s seven monsters

The proposition that seven monsters were an ancient, universally recognized Guaraní divine family is not supported by Montoya, Nimuendajú, Cadogan, Métraux, or the principal community-specific ethnographies. Colmán assembled and genealogized heterogeneous folklore in 1929. Later popular culture naturalized the arrangement.

This matters for Kuaray because websites routinely place him inside Colmán’s system and present the resulting chart as pre-Columbian canon.

### Human sacrifice and cannibalism

No Kuaray-specific human sacrifice is documented. Colonial reports of Tupi-Guaraní captive killing and ritual anthropophagy concern particular peoples and contexts. Scholarship debates how these practices related to warfare, vengeance, personhood, incorporation, and Christianity. Calling them “sacrifices to the sun god Kuaray” is unsupported.

### State of the evidence

The principal evidence survives as:

- Montoya’s seventeenth-century printed books in major libraries and digital facsimiles;
- Nimuendajú’s 1914 German publication and later Portuguese and Spanish translations;
- Cadogan’s articles and *Ayvu Rapyta*, with reprints and digital scans;
- folklore collections in Argentine and Paraguayan archives and publications;
- living oral and ritual knowledge maintained by Guaraní communities.

Losses include unrecorded oral repertoires, traditions disrupted by epidemic disease, enslavement, forced movement, mission discipline, deforestation, and elder deaths. There is no complete recoverable “original pantheon.”

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary and near-primary sources

- **Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639).** Earliest secure lexical anchor; mission orthography and semantic evidence.
- **Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Conquista espiritual* (1639).** Essential colonial setting, read critically as missionary advocacy.
- **Curt Nimuendajú, *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* (1914).** Earliest extensive ethnographic narrative corpus relevant to the solar elder brother.
- **León Cadogan, “Mitología en la zona guaraní” (1951).** Comparative discussion and bridge between Mbyá sacred tradition and Paraguayan folklore.
- **León Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959), especially chapter VIII.** Principal source for Pa’i Rete Kuaray.
- **León Cadogan, later collections including *Ywyra Ñe’ery* and *Tradiciones guaraníes en el folklore paraguayo*.** Supplementary ritual, linguistic, and folkloric evidence.
- **Juan B. Ambrosetti, regional folklore collections.** Evidence for Kuarahy Jára/Pombero in Corrientes and adjoining regions.
- **Narciso R. Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929).** Evidence for twentieth-century literary systematization, not an undifferentiated ancient canon.

### Major critical scholarship

- Métraux provides the broad Tupinambá and Tupi-Guaraní comparison.
- Schaden compares Guaraní communities without presuming complete uniformity.
- Hélène Clastres develops the Land-without-Evil and prophetism thesis.
- Pierre Clastres emphasizes the philosophical and poetic character of sacred speech.
- Melià grounds religious interpretation in language, colonial history, and Indigenous concepts of the word.
- Sušnik supplies a wide Paraguayan ethnohistorical framework.
- Fausto analyzes the transformation of cannibalism, divinity, and Christianity and directly addresses the Mbyá departure from perfect twinship.
- Modern Indigenous scholarship and testimony increasingly challenge the treatment of these accounts as inert folklore divorced from women’s knowledge, territory, and continuing ritual life.

### Conclusions established with the highest confidence

1. *Kuaray/kuarahy* is first securely documented as a Guaraní word for the Sun, not as a fully narrated “god biography.”
2. The first extensive relevant divine cycle in print is Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva corpus of 1914.
3. The definitive Mbyá Pa’i Rete Kuaray text is Cadogan’s 1959 *Ayvu Rapyta*, based on Indigenous testimony recorded in the mid-twentieth century.
4. Mbyá Kuaray and Jachyrã are not straightforward uterine twins in Cadogan’s principal recension; Kuaray creates the future Moon.
5. Apapokúva Ñanderyke’y and Tyvyry are same-mother brothers of differentiated paternity.
6. Pa’i Rete Kuaray, Kuarahy Jára/Pombero, and Kuarahy Ra’y are historically distinguishable figures.
7. No Kuaray temple cult, cult statue tradition, human sacrifice, temple prostitution, occult grimoire, or ancient seven-monster family is securely documented.
8. Colmán’s 1929 pantheon is a literary systematization that powerfully shaped modern popular mythology.
9. The tradition remains living; its twentieth-century publication date is not the date of its cultural origin.
10. The variants—not a harmonized master story—constitute Kuaray’s documented history.
