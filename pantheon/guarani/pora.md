# Pora (Póra): Research Dossier

> **Class:** A generic or collective class of ghost, apparition, or haunting presence—not, in the securely documented record, a named god with a stable personality, genealogy, cult, or canonical iconography. In Paraguayan Guaraní and regional Spanish, *póra* can denote “ghost, spirit, apparition”; particular stories individualize a *póra* as the unquiet soul of a dead person or as the uncanny inhabitant of a place.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The underlying Guaraní/Tupí-Guaraní element meaning “inhabitant; that which is contained in or belongs to a place” is colonial: compare Anchieta’s Tupí *-bóra* (1595) and Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s Classical Guaraní evidence in *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639). These do **not securely attest an incorporeal ghost called Póra**. León Cadogan explicitly observed in 1959 that neither Mbyá usage nor Montoya’s *Tesoro* employed the older expression for incorporeal beings; according to him, the specialized “ghost” sense belonged to contemporary Paraguayan vernacular. The earliest secure narrative documentation located here is therefore Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s late-nineteenth-century Misiones field material, subsequently collected in *Supersticiones y leyendas* (1917), where *Porá* means an apparition and appears beside, or as a reduced form of, *Caá-Porá*. [Montoya facsimile record](https://digital.bbm.usp.br/handle/bbm/4922); [Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta*](https://es.scribd.com/doc/311155333/AYVU-RAPYTA-Textos-miticos-de-los-Mbya-guarani-de-Leon-Cadogan-publicado-por-la-Universidad-de-Sao-Paulo-ano-1959); [Ambrosetti digitization](https://api.naturalis.fcnym.unlp.edu.ar/server/api/core/bitstreams/e2c403b0-0fd0-4f53-a98f-aba45caf75ff/content).

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early linguistic attestation]** Colonial evidence for the word or formative, not necessarily for the later folkloric being.
- **[Secure folklore attestation]** A dated collection or explicitly identified oral report.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally recorded form not safely generalizable to every Guaraní-speaking community.
- **[Christian-syncretic folklore]** Material framed through sin, salvation, the rosary, priests, crosses, or the repose of souls.
- **[Learned systematization]** A literary or folkloristic ordering that should not be mistaken for a precolonial canon.
- **[Modern retelling]** Recent popular, educational, journalistic, or commercial presentation.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An interpretation proposed by researchers rather than an explicit traditional statement.
- **[Unestablished]** A claim for which no adequate primary or scholarly documentation was found.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

### Póra as a category rather than an individual

**[Secure folklore attestation]** *Póra* is best understood as a common noun and a category of manifestation. Modern dictionaries define it as *fantasma, duende, espíritu*, while the same form in compounds can mean “inhabitant,” “dweller,” “content,” or something that abounds in a place. The modern Proyecto Montoya gives the pronunciation **[ˈpo.ɾa]** and precisely these two semantic fields. The *Diccionario de americanismos* records Argentine/Paraguayan Spanish **pora**, “fantasma o aparición,” demonstrating lexical transfer into regional Spanish. [Proyecto Montoya](https://www.proyectomontoya.org.py/nthg/p%C3%B3ra); [ASALE](https://www.asale.org/damer/pora).

This distinction matters. “Póra” is sometimes presented in tourist and popular lists as though it were one member of a bounded “Guaraní pantheon.” The documentary record instead supports:

1. an old relational or locative formative, “inhabitant/that which is in”;
2. compounds naming the inhabitant or numinous owner of a particular domain;
3. a later vernacular specialization, “ghost/apparition”;
4. individual ghost stories in which a particular dead person’s manifestation is called *un/una póra*.

There is consequently no single Póra biography to reconstruct. Its history is the history of a word, a class of apparitions, and a changing body of local stories.

### Forms and orthography

- **Póra** — modern Guaraní spelling, with stress on the first syllable: [ˈpo.ɾa].
- **Pora / Porá** — Spanish-language spellings in Paraguayan and Argentine folklore collections. Older collectors were inconsistent about both Guaraní orthography and accent marks.
- **Péra** — recorded or printed in some older works and summaries; it may reflect dialectal pronunciation, collector orthography, or faulty transcription. It must not automatically be made a separate being.
- **-póra / -bóra / -vóra** — related formative variants meaning an inhabitant or content of a place.
- **yvy-póra / yvypóra** — “earth-dweller,” human being; important evidence that the formative does not intrinsically mean “ghost.”
- **ka’a-póra / caá-porá** — “forest inhabitant,” conventionally a forest apparition or owner; historically and regionally variable, and not simply identical with every *póra*.
- **y-póra / ypóra** — “water inhabitant,” a water being or water apparition in some regional classifications.

**[Early linguistic attestation]** A modern study tracing the formative cites José de Anchieta’s 1595 Tupí grammar for *-bóra*, connected with being inside or inhabiting something. Because Anchieta described coastal Tupí, this is comparative Tupí-Guaraní evidence, not direct proof of a Paraguayan ghost belief in 1595. [Linguistic study](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S2448-62212022000200007&script=sci_arttext).

**[Early linguistic attestation]** Montoya’s *Tesoro* was printed in Madrid in 1639 and contains 407 folios. It is indispensable evidence for Classical Guaraní, but a missionary dictionary is neither a transparent transcript of precontact religion nor proof that every later meaning already existed. [Biblioteca Brasiliana catalogue and facsimile](https://digital.bbm.usp.br/handle/bbm/4922).

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Cadogan’s 1959 philological note is decisive: *yvy-póra* and related forms retained “inhabitant/that contained in,” whereas in Paraguayan vernacular *póra* had come to be used almost exclusively for ghosts. He adds that neither Mbyá usage nor Montoya’s *Tesoro* used the expression for incorporeal beings. Thus an etymology that simply translates *póra* as “ghost” and projects that sense intact into the sixteenth or seventeenth century is historically unsound. [Cadogan, searchable text](https://es.scribd.com/doc/311155333/AYVU-RAPYTA-Textos-miticos-de-los-Mbya-guarani-de-Leon-Cadogan-publicado-por-La-Universidad-de-Sao-Paulo-ano-1959).

### Distinctions from similar-looking words

- **porã**, “good, beautiful,” is a different word, containing the nasal vowel /ã/.
- **ã / ánga / angue** belong to Guaraní vocabularies of soul, shadow, or the spirit of a deceased person. Modern narrators may explain a *póra* as an *angue*, but the words are not etymological synonyms.
- **Ka’a Póra/Caipora** has an extensive Brazilian and Argentine history of its own. It may be called a forest ghost, owner, animal guardian, monstrous human, or shape-shifter. The bare *Póra* overlaps with it in some Misiones testimony but cannot universally be reduced to it.
- **Pombero, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Luisõ**, and the seven monsters popularized by Narciso R. Colmán are discrete figures. Póra is not securely one of Colmán’s seven offspring of Tau and Keraná.

### No stable epithets

No ancient cult titles, divine epithets, sacerdotal names, or royal invocations are documented. Expressions such as “the Póra of the abandoned house,” “the Póra of the treasure,” or “the Póra of the dead man” are descriptive labels for local manifestations, not a canonical titulary.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial sources: what they establish—and what they do not

**[Early linguistic attestation]** Anchieta’s *Arte de gramática da língua mais usada na costa do Brasil* (1595) supplies comparative evidence for the inhabitant/content formative. It is not a Paraguayan Póra narrative.

**[Early linguistic attestation]** Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639) documents Classical Guaraní morphology and vocabulary. Its physical witness is a printed book, issued at Madrid by Juan Sánchez. The work establishes the historical depth of related *-pora* expressions but, following Cadogan’s explicit warning, does not securely establish *Póra* as an incorporeal ghost.

**[Unestablished]** Montoya’s *Conquista espiritual* (1639) records Jesuit activity, conversions, conflicts, missions, and missionary interpretations of Indigenous religion. No securely identified passage in the material examined names Póra as a distinct supernatural personality. It is therefore contextual evidence for the colonial transformation of Guaraní religious life, not a primary Póra myth.

### Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century regional collections

**[Secure folklore attestation; regional variant]** Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s fieldwork in Misiones provides the earliest clearly located narrative evidence found in this investigation. His writings circulated in late-nineteenth-century scientific and folkloric venues and were collected posthumously as *Supersticiones y leyendas: región misionera, valles calchaquíes, las Pampas* in 1917.

Ambrosetti reports several forms:

- In some testimony, **Caá-Porá** is a hairy forest man who smokes through a pipe made from a human skull and tibia and devours people while leaving their intestines.
- “In other people,” Caá-Porá becomes a simple **Porá**, a forest apparition taking the form of a pig or dog and emitting flames from its mouth.
- In another mode it is invisible: it hobbles or arrests a mule while a stampede-like sound is heard in the forest.
- His material also says certain plants—fig trees and banana plants in later summaries—were kept away from houses because they had *porá*, an “alma fantasma” whose night lament frightened residents.

These reports do not describe one consistent body or personality. They show *porá* functioning as “haunting presence” and demonstrate overlap between a generalized apparition and the more domain-specific Caá-Porá. [Ambrosetti, full digitization](https://api.naturalis.fcnym.unlp.edu.ar/server/api/core/bitstreams/e2c403b0-0fd0-4f53-a98f-aba45caf75ff/content); [bibliographical overview](https://editoriales.conabip.gob.ar/node/11191).

### Indigenous cosmological collections

**[Negative finding]** Curt Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* (1914), León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959), and later Mbyá materials such as *Ywyra Ñe’ery* document elaborate creation traditions, divine speech, human souls, death, afterlife, sacred names, and relations with divine beings. They should not be mined indiscriminately to construct a universal “Guaraní pantheon.”

For Póra specifically, Cadogan supplies a linguistic-historical correction rather than a mythic role: the older “inhabitant” term is not used by his Mbyá interlocutors for incorporeal beings. Póra consequently has no demonstrated role in the Mbyá creation sequence printed in *Ayvu Rapyta*.

**[Negative finding]** Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva creation-and-destruction cycle and Land without Evil material do not securely make Póra a creator, child of a creator, cosmic adversary, or participant in world destruction.

### Colmán and the “seven monsters”

**[Learned systematization]** Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní literary epic *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929) systematized the familiar genealogy of Tau, Keraná, and seven monstrous offspring. Its enormous influence on popular “Guaraní mythology” should not conceal its character as a modern literary construction using and rearranging oral materials. Póra is not securely established as an eighth member, collective parent, servant, or opponent in that scheme. Claims that every Paraguayan supernatural figure belongs to a single Colmán-style family are modern harmonization.

### Later oral and popular corpora

**[Secure folklore attestation]** Berta Elena Vidal de Battini’s extensive *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina* preserves twentieth-century oral narratives collected by locality and informant; Corrientes material appears across the regional volumes. The corpus is valuable because it records variant tellings rather than imposing one theology. The digital catalogue identifies the later ten-volume publication and its source edition. [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes catalogue](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/promocion_lectura_y_letras_en_espanyol/obra/cuentos-y-leyendas-populares-de-la-argentina-tomo-10--0/).

**[Modern retelling; Christian-syncretic folklore]** Domingo Aguilera’s *Folklore paraguayo* (2007) prints a bilingual Spanish–Guaraní account, “Mito del Pora / Póra rehegua.” It calls Póra an unsaved dead person’s *angue* that returns among the living, either seeking salvation or vengeance. This is a documented modern folkloric text, not evidence for precolonial doctrine. [Aguilera text](https://www.portalguarani.com/detalles_museos_otras_obras.php?id=27&id_obras=2372&id_otras=369).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

Póra’s documented domain is not a divine portfolio but a group of haunted circumstances:

- **The dead and their former places:** an apparition wandering where the person lived.
- **Violent or spiritually troubled deaths:** murder sites, suicides, crosses, graves, or places where death occurred.
- **Abandoned or old houses:** noises, displaced objects, lamentation, silhouettes, or an unseen inhabitant.
- **Forest paths in Misiones:** animal shapes, fire, invisible obstruction, or stampede noises.
- **Buried wealth:** a guardian, revealer, or dangerous mediator of *plata yvyguy*.
- **Night travel:** cries, strange bundles, moving shadows, troops, riders, or disembodied sounds.

### Forms

**[Regional variant]** No standard body exists. Attested and reported forms include:

- an invisible presence;
- a vague “bulto,” on the ground or elevated;
- a pig or dog breathing fire;
- a white-clad woman, though sometimes a man;
- a skull or skeleton beneath a black mantle;
- a headless white dog associated with treasure;
- voices, cries, marching soldiers, drovers, or hoofbeats;
- a newly appearing tree or an object displaced without visible agency.

The 2021 Fundación Tape Aviru survey found participants describing Póra as a white apparition, frequently female but also male, manifesting at night near the place of death and frightening witnesses. That is evidence for present-day public representation, not an ancient iconographic canon. [Survey report](https://fundaciontapeaviru.org.py/wp-content/uploads/INFORME-GENERAL-PERSONAJES-MITOLO%CC%81GICOS.pdf).

### Powers and effects

**[Secure folklore attestation]** Aguilera’s version attributes to the encounter:

- fainting from fright;
- temporary stammering or muteness;
- paralysis;
- fever or delirium;
- lasting mental disturbance.

The apparition may move or imitate natural objects, produce human or military sounds, appear in transformed shapes, or communicate a request. Ambrosetti’s Misiones Porá can stop a mule without appearing.

These are narrated effects. No medical or historical evidence establishes supernatural causation.

### Defenses and release

**[Christian-syncretic folklore]** Aguilera prescribes retreating seven steps without turning around, praying a complete fifteen-mystery rosary, and reciting three Creeds backward. One may also ask the apparition what it needs, then consult a priest and have the required offices performed so that the soul reaches eternal rest.

This complex combines Catholic soteriology, clerical intercession, the rosary, and the widespread magical practice of reversing a sacred formula. It should not be labeled an unchanged precolonial Guaraní rite.

### Iconography

**[Negative finding]** No securely identified precolonial sculpture, ceramic image, petroglyph, colonial church image, cult statue, or canonical painted type represents Póra. Modern white specters, skulls, hooded figures, and headless dogs are illustrations of particular folklore motifs. Because Póra is a class of apparition, visual instability is expected.

There are no securely attested sacred colors, astronomical bodies, days, numbers, animals, plants, weapons, regalia, or emblems. Seven steps and nocturnal manifestations belong to individual later prescriptions, not a universal sacred numerology.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

### Genealogy

**[Negative finding]** No source securely gives Póra parents, spouse, children, siblings, or a place in a divine hierarchy. In the Christianized soul-in-pain model, each *póra* was formerly an individual human being; its “origin” is death without repose, unresolved obligation, sin, murder, suicide, or attachment to place.

Póra is therefore not securely:

- a child of Tau and Keraná;
- a creation of Tupã;
- a sibling of Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao Ao, or Luisõ;
- a servant of Añá;
- a universal enemy of Pombero;
- an Indigenous equivalent of the Christian Devil.

Those equations belong to modern charts unless an individual storyteller explicitly makes one.

### Every documented named being–being interaction

No securely documented narrative was found in which a particular Póra meets, fights, seduces, judges, deceives, rescues, or allies with another named divine being.

The complete checkable list is consequently:

- **Caá-Porá — transformation or terminological reduction:** Ambrosetti states that for some people Caá-Porá “transforms into simple Porá,” appearing as a pig or dog that emits fire. This is a relationship between two folkloric classifications, not an episode in which two independently named beings meet. **Source:** Ambrosetti’s Misiones field material, late nineteenth century; collected 1917. **Tier:** [Secure folklore attestation; regional variant].
- **God/Ñandejára — indirect soteriological relation:** in Aguilera’s 2007 text, religious aid may enable the wandering soul to enter God’s presence. God does not appear in a narrated encounter with Póra. **Tier:** [Christian-syncretic folklore; modern retelling].
- **All other named Guaraní beings:** no interaction established. Shared inclusion in popular pantheon lists is not interaction evidence.

## 5. Major legends and narrative cycles

Póra has no epic cycle comparable to the Mbyá creation hymns or the literary Tau–Keraná genealogy. Its narratives are short legends organized by place, death, encounter, and remedy.

### A. The wandering soul seeking salvation or vengeance

**[Christian-syncretic folklore]** In Aguilera’s 2007 formulation, Póra is the *angue* of someone who died without salvation. It returns to places inhabited in life, either seeking help toward salvation or taking revenge. A traveler must not answer distant cries, approach strange shapes, or curse noises near crosses and death sites.

The witness may question the apparition. If it states its need, the living person takes the matter to a priest; prayers or offices release the soul, after which it ceases troubling the living.

This legend’s theological grammar is Catholic: salvation, eternal rest, priests, rosaries, Creeds, and efficacious offices. The Guaraní language and older ideas about soul, place, speech, and dangerous dead may shape its local expression, but no evidence permits separation into a pure “Indigenous core” plus detachable European additions.

### B. The forest Póra of Misiones

**[Secure folklore attestation; regional variant]** Ambrosetti’s Porá appears in the monte as pig or dog, sometimes with fire issuing from its mouth. It frightens animals and travelers. In an invisible manifestation it arrests a mule while a tremendous sound passes through the forest.

This story belongs to a continuum with Caá-Porá, forest-owner traditions, dangerous road apparitions, and animal transformation. Ambrosetti himself presents “simple Porá” as a form into which Caá-Porá is reduced by some narrators. That is collector testimony about variation, not proof that one figure historically descended from the other.

### C. Haunted vegetation and the inhabited place

**[Secure folklore attestation; regional variant]** Ambrosetti records beliefs that particular plants should not stand close to houses because they possess *porá*: an unseen ghost-soul lamenting or frightening people at night. Here the oldest locative sense—an inhabitant or something present within a place—remains semantically visible.

The significant feature is not a universal sacred plant. It is the proposition that something can be “inhabited” by an uncanny presence.

### D. Póra of abandoned houses

**[Folklore]** Paraguayan and Argentine regional speech applies *póra* to the unseen resident of an empty house: footsteps, doors, voices, shadow figures, moved utensils, or a presence attached to a former resident. These narratives seldom preserve a fixed plot. Often an unexplained disturbance prompts neighbors to reconstruct a death, crime, burial, or former owner.

The abandoned-house Póra should be treated as a productive legend pattern, not one ancient myth. Its Christian “soul in pain” interpretation can coexist with the more linguistically basic idea of a place having an uncanny inhabitant.

### E. Póra and buried treasure

**[Late folklore; historical core after 1864–1870]** Paraguayan *plata yvyguy* (“money/silver under the earth”) legends grew especially around valuables allegedly hidden during the War of the Triple Alliance. Stories also project treasures back onto Jesuit expulsion in 1767. The folklore distinguishes real practices of hiding property during war from supernatural claims about guardians and omens.

Reported signs include lights or flames above the ground, sounds, dreams, unusual odors, and a headless white dog. A Póra may guard the deposit, reveal it to a chosen person, demand masses or fulfillment of an obligation, punish greed, or tempt the finder into ruin. A 2024 historical-news examination notes that the tradition was strongly nourished by the Triple Alliance War but that some supposed maps and treasure claims have disputed authenticity. [Historical review](https://www.lanacion.com.py/estilodevida/2024/08/13/mito-o-realidad-los-tesoros-enterrados-de-la-guerra-contra-la-triple-alianza/).

**[Modern literary reception]** Paraguayan literary history includes stories about *porá* guarding wartime treasure. This is evidence for twentieth-century literary elaboration, not for a precolonial treasure deity. [Literary study](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/s3/BVMC_OBRAS/ff6/4b8/508/2b1/11d/fac/c70/021/85c/e60/64/mimes/ff64b850-82b1-11df-acc7-002185ce6064.pdf).

### F. Corrientes apparition stories

**[Regional variant]** In Corrientes, *la Pora* or *el pora* denotes an apparition or soul in pain. The vocabulary has entered local Spanish, sometimes taking the Spanish article and ordinary Spanish pluralization. Narratives associate it with familiar places, moonlit nights, death sites, cries, fear, or revelation of a burial.

A Corrientes popular account presents the dead person’s Póra as indicating the exact location of its burial or hidden wealth. The same account advances the broad theory that Jesuits imported European ghost motifs to discipline Indigenous communities. That theory is not demonstrated by the story itself and should be treated as popular historical interpretation, not established fact. [Corrientes account](https://archivo.corrientesaldia.info/la-pora/).

### G. Misiones variants

**[Secure folklore attestation; regional variant]** Misiones material is more strongly tied to forest travel, dangerous vegetation, animal forms, Caá-Porá, and invisible interference with pack animals. Modern provincial tourism simplifies Póra as “the classic ghost with a Guaraní name,” a useful indicator of contemporary reception but not a source for early history. [Misiones tourism presentation](https://misiones.tur.ar/mitos-y-leyendas-misioneras/).

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No institutional cult

**[Negative finding]** There is no documented Póra temple, shrine network, priesthood, festival calendar, cult statue, sacrificial tariff, theophoric personal-name series, pilgrimage center, oracle, or political economy. Calling Póra a “god” therefore misdescribes the available evidence.

### Practices directed toward an apparition

The absence of institutional cult does not mean absence of ritual.

**[Christian-syncretic folklore]**

- reciting the rosary;
- saying the Creed, sometimes backward;
- requesting masses or offices for the dead;
- consulting a priest;
- asking what obligation remains unfulfilled;
- praying for repose;
- avoiding insults or curses at death sites;
- retreating without turning around.

These are apotropaic or funerary responses, not worship.

### Treasure practice

**[Folklore]** Treasure seekers may interpret lights, dreams, spectral dogs, noises, and household disturbances as guidance from a Póra. Actual digging for *plata yvyguy* is documented as continuing popular behavior, sometimes causing property damage or legal controversy. The supernatural interpretation remains folklore; individual buried hoards may nevertheless have historical origins.

### Sacrifice, violence, and sexual cult claims

**[Contested-material pass: negative finding]** No reliable evidence links Póra to human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, temple prostitution, sexual rites, cult violence, divination offices, or organized magical fraternities. Ambrosetti’s monstrous Caá-Porá may kill or consume humans in narrative, but that is not evidence of humans being sacrificed to Póra.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before European documentation

**[Unestablished]** The age of the locative “inhabitant” concept cannot be equated with the age of every modern ghost story. Oral traditions may be older than their documentation, but no securely dated precontact text or image names Póra as a ghost.

### 1595–1639: colonial linguistic evidence

Anchieta’s Tupí grammar and Montoya’s Guaraní lexicography establish an old Tupí-Guaraní formative concerned with inhabitants, contents, and beings associated with places. They do not establish a named deity or the complete modern “soul in pain” complex.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: missionary transformation

Jesuit missions reorganized Indigenous settlement, ritual authority, language, and teaching. Christian doctrines about judgment, salvation, purgatorial suffering, masses, demons, and the repose of the dead entered Guaraní-language discourse.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The later Póra as a soul requiring priestly offices is plainly Christian in formulation. Whether missionaries created the figure wholesale is not demonstrable. A more economical reading is semantic and ritual convergence: older concepts of soul, death, place, and nonhuman inhabitants were expressed alongside Catholic categories.

The expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767 later became one chronological anchor for treasure legends. Claims that Jesuits buried vast supernatural hoards are folklore unless independently documented.

### Late nineteenth century: first secure regional narratives

Ambrosetti documented Misiones beliefs in Caá-Porá, simple Porá, animal apparitions, invisible forest interference, and haunted vegetation. This is the first secure Póra narrative horizon located in the present research.

### 1864–1870 and its aftermath: war ghosts and treasure

The War of the Triple Alliance produced mass death, displacement, ruined settlements, hidden possessions, and unresolved burials. It supplied a powerful historical field for Póra stories: spectral soldiers, marching troops, wandering dead, guardian dogs, and *plata yvyguy*.

Not every treasure legend dates directly from the war, but stories that explicitly mention López, retreat routes, military caches, or refugees cannot predate those events in their present form.

### 1914–1959: ethnographic differentiation

Nimuendajú, Schaden, Cadogan, Métraux, and later Hélène and Pierre Clastres documented distinct Guaraní-speaking communities and religious systems. Their work undermines the notion of one timeless “Guaraní pantheon.” Cadogan’s 1959 note shows that Póra’s vernacular ghost sense cannot simply be imposed on Mbyá sacred language or Montoya’s seventeenth-century lexicon.

### 1929 onward: literary pantheon-making

Colmán’s *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* helped organize Paraguayan supernatural figures into an accessible literary mythology. Later schoolbooks, illustrated encyclopedias, tourist sites, and internet lists often flatten Colmán, Mbyá texts, Apapocúva narratives, Brazilian Caipora lore, and Paraguayan mestizo folklore into one system. Póra’s placement in such lists is a modern classificatory event, not proof of an ancient pantheon.

### Late twentieth century to present

Póra survives vigorously as:

- an ordinary Guaraní and regional-Spanish word for a ghost;
- a label for haunted houses and death sites;
- a Misiones and Corrientes forest or road apparition;
- a treasure guardian or revealer;
- a literary and educational folklore character;
- a conversational explanation for unexplained noises, silhouettes, and frightening places.

This is continuity of vocabulary and legend-making, not evidence for an uninterrupted institutional cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Language and everyday speech

**[Secure modern usage]** The word crossed into Argentine regional Spanish as **pora**, “ghost or apparition,” recognized by the Association of Academies of the Spanish Language. Speakers can use it generically—roughly, “there is a ghost/haunting there”—without invoking a named mythological personality.

### Education, tourism, and media

**[Modern reinvention]** School materials and tourism pages commonly capitalize Póra and place it beside Pombero, Jasy Jatere, and the seven monsters. Capitalization turns a class noun into something resembling a proper name. This makes the lore easy to present but creates a misleadingly tidy pantheon.

Modern visual culture tends to depict Póra as a white-sheet ghost, translucent woman, hooded skeleton, or headless dog. None is an exclusive traditional icon.

### Literature

Treasure-guardian stories, haunted-house tales, and war apparitions occur in Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine prose and oral-history writing. The literary Póra often becomes more individualized than the older common noun: it acquires a particular death, grievance, hidden cache, and chosen witness.

### Occultism and neopaganism

**[Contested-material pass: negative finding]** No historically significant Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, ceremonial-magical, spiritist, or grimoire tradition centered on Póra was found.

**[Unestablished]** Contemporary individuals may incorporate Póra into eclectic neopagan or occult practice, but no documented movement, lineage, organization, membership estimate, or standardized ritual was located. Internet “Guaraní pantheon” pages should not be treated as evidence of Indigenous religious continuity.

### Political and extremist appropriation

**[Contested-material pass: negative finding]** No documented extremist, nationalist, racist, or party-political movement appears to have adopted Póra as a central emblem. Broader romantic nationalism has used a homogenized “Guaraní mythology,” but a specific Póra appropriation was not established.

### Commercial use

Póra appears in tourism, entertainment, folklore books, school projects, ghost tours, and branded cultural presentations. Commercial representation generally favors a singular monster or character, even though the source record favors a category of apparition.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Póra ancient?

Two questions are often conflated:

1. Is the linguistic element old? **Yes:** comparative Tupí and Classical Guaraní evidence is colonial.
2. Is the fully developed wandering Catholic soul called Póra demonstrably precolonial? **No.**

Cadogan’s evidence weighs against projecting the modern ghost sense unchanged into Montoya or Mbyá religious language.

### Indigenous being, European ghost, or syncretism?

- **European-origin thesis:** Some modern Corrientes presentations argue that European and Jesuit ghost stories were used to frighten or discipline Indigenous populations.
- **Indigenous-continuity thesis:** The old locative “inhabitant” formative, Indigenous soul vocabularies, place-masters, and forest/water beings show that local conceptual materials did not begin with the Jesuits.
- **Best-supported reconstruction:** Póra folklore is historically layered. The word’s old structure, Indigenous concepts of inhabited places and postmortem presence, Catholic souls in need of salvation, European apparition motifs, war trauma, and regional storytelling converged. A single-origin claim exceeds the evidence.

### Póra versus Caá-Porá

Ambrosetti is the pivotal witness. He distinguishes Caá-Porá yet says that among some people it becomes a simple Porá. This may reflect:

- semantic reduction of a compound;
- confusion between a forest owner and a generic apparition;
- genuine regional transformation;
- Ambrosetti’s own attempt to systematize fluid testimony.

The evidence supports overlap, not universal identity.

### “God,” “demon,” or “ghost”?

“God” is poorly supported. “Demon” reflects Christian or popular classification when the apparition is malignant. “Ghost/apparition” is the broadest documented category. Some instances are human dead; others are animal-shaped or unidentified presences. “Collective folkloric class” is therefore the most accurate dossier classification.

### The problem of the “Guaraní pantheon”

Mbyá, Apapocúva/Nhandeva, Kaiowá, Paraguayan vernacular, Corrientes, Misiones, and Brazilian traditions are not interchangeable. The popular unified pantheon largely results from literary systematization, national folklore publishing, school texts, and internet repetition.

Colmán’s seven monsters are especially prone to anachronistic treatment as a prehistoric canon. Póra has no securely documented genealogical place within that literary system.

### State of the evidence

The record is unusually uneven:

- strong for modern lexical meaning;
- strong for late-nineteenth- and twentieth-century regional folklore;
- moderate for semantic history;
- weak for exact first use in the ghost sense;
- absent for ancient temples, cult images, priesthood, genealogy, or epic narrative;
- absent for a continuous organized Póra cult.

The chief archives are printed missionary dictionaries, ethnographic monographs, folklore collections, regional periodicals, oral-history surveys, and modern digital repositories—not temple inscriptions or archaeological cult deposits.

## 11. Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not of a Guaraní god but of a semantic and folkloric transformation.

An old Tupí-Guaraní formative denoted an inhabitant or that which exists within a place. In Paraguayan vernacular it developed a specialized sense, “ghost/apparition,” sufficiently established by the twentieth century for Cadogan to contrast it explicitly with both Mbyá religious usage and Montoya’s Classical Guaraní. Late-nineteenth-century Misiones testimony already records Porá as an animal-shaped or invisible forest apparition and shows its overlap with Caá-Porá.

Later Paraguayan and Argentine traditions made Póra the unquiet human dead: a soul tied to its home, death site, grievance, or buried wealth. Catholic salvation, priests, rosaries, crosses, and masses became integral to many versions. The Triple Alliance War supplied dead soldiers, ruined houses, displaced families, hidden valuables, and unmarked graves; *plata yvyguy* lore consequently gave Póra a prominent role as treasure guardian or revealer.

No evidence supports a temple cult, priesthood, sacrifice, stable divine genealogy, canonical image, or membership in Colmán’s seven-monster family. No documented mythic encounter with another named deity was located. Póra’s instability is not a deficiency in the evidence: it is the central fact of the tradition. It is the name of whatever inhabits the feared place.
