# Angatupyry: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest securely recoverable narrative, Angatupyry is an invisible **spirit**, specifically *“Espíritu del bien”* (“Spirit of Good”), not a god with an independent cult, body, temple, or priesthood. Later popular sources sometimes call the figure a god, a son of Tupã, or Taû’s brother, but those classifications are not stated in the foundational text.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní-language literary poem *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (“Our Ancestors”), published in Asunción in **1929**. A manuscript of the poem received recognition at the Twentieth International Congress of Americanists in 1922, but the available notice does not establish that Angatupyry was already present in that manuscript. Accordingly, 1929 is the earliest secure public attestation of the named personification. Colmán’s Spanish version, *Nuestros antepasados*, appeared in 1936/1937.

**Principal historical finding:** Angatupyry cannot presently be documented as an independently worshipped pre-Columbian Guaraní divinity. The recoverable character belongs first to Colmán’s twentieth-century national-literary cosmogony. Its name is ordinary and meaningful Guaraní vocabulary, but its transformation into Tupã’s invisible champion of moral good—and its placement opposite Taû in a universal good-versus-evil system—is a **learned literary systematization strongly shaped by Christian and European moral cosmology**. It subsequently entered Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine school folklore as if it were an ancient, pan-Guaraní god.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Lexically attested:** the word or its components belong to Guaraní vocabulary, without proving the existence of a divine individual.
- **Secure literary attestation:** directly stated in Colmán’s recoverable text.
- **Learned systematization:** Colmán’s arrangement of heterogeneous names, tales, comparative speculations, and moral concepts into one genealogy.
- **Folkloric reception:** a later oral, educational, touristic, or popular retelling derived substantially from that system.
- **Modern accretion:** a claim absent from Colmán and unsupported by an identified earlier source.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an analytical conclusion based on comparison of texts rather than a traditional claim.
- **Not established:** no adequate source has been found.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

In *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, Tupã leaves Taû and Angatupyry as invisible companions of the first human pair, Rupavê and Sypavê. Taû impels them toward evil; Angatupyry sustains them in good. Illness initiates a struggle between the two spirits, and recovery signifies Angatupyry’s victory. Later, Angatupyry physically intervenes when Taû attempts to abduct Kerana. After seven days and nights of combat, Taû is almost defeated but obtains the aid of Pytajovái, who knocks Angatupyry to the ground. Taû then carries Kerana away.

This is virtually the whole primary narrative dossier. There is no separate birth myth, shrine, prayer, festival, priesthood, sacred animal, image type, or cycle of adventures.

### Forms of the name

- **Angatupyry** — Colmán’s form and the standard modern spelling.
- **Angatupry** — a common modern misspelling or contraction, especially in English- and Portuguese-language internet mythology.
- **Ãngatupyry** — a spelling sometimes used to mark the initial vowel as nasal, especially in modern dictionaries.
- **Angatupyrý / Angatupirí** — occasional accentual or phonetic adaptations; none has been established as an early cult name.
- Colmán’s Spanish edition writes **Taû** with a circumflex but normally **Angatupyry** without additional diacritics.

### Meaning and etymology

Modern Guaraní lexicography distinguishes:

- *ánga / ãnga*: soul, spirit;
- *angatu / ãngatu*: good or noble soul, moral strength, goodness, perseverance in good;
- *angatupyry / ãngatupyry*: good or powerful soul, spirit of good, nobility, generosity, dignity, conscience, or aspiration toward improvement.

One searchable Guaraní dictionary gives *angatu* as “good soul” and *angatupyry* as “spirit of good.” Another gives the broader semantic range “nobility,” “dignity,” “act of conscience,” and “spirit of self-improvement.” Thus the name is transparent as an ethical-spiritual expression, not an otherwise inexplicable proper name. Colmán himself simply glosses it, in explanatory note 20, as *“Espíritu del bien.”*

The exact historical segmentation should not be overconfidently reconstructed from modern dictionary entries. The similarity of the ending to *katupyry* (“capable, skilful, excellent”) may contribute to senses such as “powerful” or “excellent,” but no early source has been found giving a traditional divine etymology.

### Titles and epithets

Only a narrow set is documented:

- **Espíritu del bien** — “Spirit of Good”; Colmán’s explicit gloss. **Secure literary attestation.**
- **Mba’e porã rerekua** — approximately “possessor/keeper of good things” or “guardian of the good,” in a modern Guaraní prose adaptation of the Colmán narrative. **Folkloric reception.**
- “Protector,” “guardian,” “guide,” “inspirer of virtue,” and “personification of goodness” are useful modern summaries. They are not independently attested cult titles.
- “God of all good,” “god of goodness,” and “most powerful benevolent son of Tupã” occur on modern websites. **Modern accretions.**

No equivalent in Greek, Roman, Catholic, Afro-American, or other Indigenous pantheons is historically attested. Calling Angatupyry the Guaraní equivalent of an angel, conscience, guardian angel, or Ahura Mazda is comparison, not an ancient identification.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The negative early record

The precise name **Angatupyry** has not been located as a divine individual in the standard earlier corpora named in the brief:

- Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639);
- Montoya, *Conquista espiritual* (Madrid, 1639);
- Jesuit reduction chronicles examined through modern editions and indexes;
- Curt Nimuendajú, *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* (1914);
- León Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959);
- Cadogan’s other Mbyá materials;
- Egon Schaden’s Guaraní ethnography;
- Alfred Métraux’s Tupinambá synthesis;
- Pierre and Hélène Clastres’s collections and analyses.

This is a finding about the presently searchable and published record, not proof that no person anywhere used the word religiously before 1929. It does, however, preclude treating Angatupyry as a securely documented ancient pan-Guaraní deity.

The absence is especially significant because these sources preserve elaborate but differing cosmologies. Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva materials concern Ñanderuvusu/Ñande Ru, world destruction, ritual migration, and the Land Without Evil. Cadogan’s Mbyá hymns center on Ñamandu Ru Ete, the divine fathers, sacred speech, and the unfolding of the first earth. Neither supplies Colmán’s first couple plus opposing moral spirits.

### *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*

Narciso R. Colmán, writing as Rosicrán, published *Ñande Ypy Kuéra: poema etnogenético y mitológico* in Guaraní in 1929. It was printed in Asunción by Imprenta El Arte and associated with the Sociedad Científica del Paraguay. Bibliographic notices describe it as approximately 278 pages and about 2,800 verses. Colmán subsequently produced the Spanish *Nuestros antepasados*, printed at San Lorenzo in 1936 or dated 1937 in the commonly digitized edition.

The work calls itself a mythological and ethnogenetic poem. Its scientific-looking footnotes, etymologies, cross-cultural comparisons, and references to traditions give it the appearance of an ethnographic compilation. Modern literary research, however, identifies it as an authored fictional construction that reorganized selected folkloric beings into a national epic. Rodrigo Villalba Rojas states particularly clearly that the poem was “entirely fictional” and that its fame displaced Indigenous Guaraní mythologies in the social imagination.

### What Colmán actually says

In the creation episode, Tupã tells Rupavê and Sypavê that Taû and Angatupyry will be their companions. They cannot be seen; one will sustain humanity in good, the other push it toward evil. Taû’s presence is declared necessary because fear, illness, labor, pain, and death give value or intelligibility to their opposites. When a person falls ill, Taû and Angatupyry struggle; recovery means Angatupyry has prevailed. Colmán calls this *“la eterna lucha del bien con el mal.”*

In the Kerana episode, Taû assumes the form of a handsome young man and attempts to abduct her. Angatupyry appears to defend her. They fight for seven days and nights. Taû weakens and calls his “old grandfather” Pytajovái, god of courage and destructive fire. Pytajovái knocks Angatupyry down, allowing Taû to seize Kerana.

The primary text then says that **Arasy**, responding to the people’s prayers, curses Taû. It does not say that Angatupyry pronounces the curse. Modern summaries that make Angatupyry curse Taû and Kerana contradict Colmán.

### Comparison with the ethnographic corpus

*Ayvu Rapyta* was published in 1959 from Mbyá-Guaraní religious discourse recorded by Cadogan, with preliminary installments appearing in the early 1950s. Its creator is Ñamandu Ru Ete, who unfolds himself and establishes sacred speech before creating the earth and other divine fathers. This differs structurally from Colmán’s anthropomorphic Tupã and Arasy creating a first couple out of clay.

Nimuendajú’s 1914 Apapocúva account likewise does not provide Colmán’s Angatupyry–Taû dyad. Pierre Clastres’s *Le grand parler* (1974) draws on Nimuendajú, Cadogan, and field materials; its central opposition is not a personified cosmic Good and Evil but the contrast between an imperfect earth and divine or imperishable modes of existence.

These are not “variants” of the Angatupyry story. They are different Guaraní religious systems from different communities. Treating them as chapters of one pantheon is the very flattening the evidence forbids.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**Secure literary attestation:**

- sustaining human beings in good;
- opposing Taû’s impulse toward evil;
- combating the agency responsible for illness;
- defending Kerana against abduction;
- functioning as an invisible moral companion of humanity.

“Guardian and inspirer of good” is therefore a defensible synopsis, provided it is attributed to Colmán’s system rather than projected into pre-Columbian religion.

### Form and visibility

Colmán explicitly makes Angatupyry normally **invisible**. During the battle with Taû the spirit is sufficiently embodied to fight and be knocked down, but Colmán gives no anatomy, sex, clothing, color, weapon, or physiognomy. Masculine Spanish grammar and later illustrations encourage a male presentation, but no explicit sex statement or Indigenous iconographic convention has been established.

### Iconography

No securely identified pre-Columbian, colonial, or nineteenth-century representation exists. There is no diagnostic emblem by which an archaeological image can be recognized as Angatupyry.

Modern educational pages sometimes envision a luminous or radiant humanoid, associating it with white light and purity. These are **modern visual conventions**, probably generated from Christian angelic and generalized fantasy imagery. They should not be labeled traditional iconography.

No sacred animal, plant, color, number, weekday, planet, weapon, or regalia is securely attached to Angatupyry. The number seven belongs to the Taû–Kerana narrative structure, not specifically to Angatupyry.

### Powers and limitations

Angatupyry can:

- affect moral disposition;
- oppose Taû invisibly;
- defeat Taû when unaided;
- manifest for physical combat;
- support recovery from disease.

Angatupyry cannot, in Colmán’s narrative, overcome Taû once Pytajovái intervenes. The spirit is neither omnipotent nor the final judge. Arasy, rather than Angatupyry, imposes the consequential curse.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

Colmán says Tupã **leaves** Taû and Angatupyry with humanity; modern retellings commonly paraphrase this as Tupã “created” them. Colmán does not call Angatupyry Tupã’s biological son. Nor does he call Taû Angatupyry’s brother.

Accordingly:

- **Creator or commissioning deity:** Tupã, by inference from the creation scene. **Secure literary attestation, though not biological parentage.**
- **Mother:** none stated.
- **Sibling:** none stated.
- **Consort:** none stated.
- **Children:** none stated.
- **Temple retinue, servants, or mount:** none stated.

“Son of Tupã,” “brother of Taû,” and “uncle of the seven monsters” are **modern genealogical literalizations**.

### Every documented divine or supernatural interaction

- **Tupã — commissions or leaves Angatupyry with Rupavê and Sypavê to sustain them in good; explains the spirit’s role in illness and moral struggle.** *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. **Secure literary attestation / learned systematization.**
- **Taû — paired with Angatupyry as the contrary invisible influence on humanity; the two fight whenever evil or illness assails a person.** *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. **Secure literary attestation.**
- **Taû — Angatupyry confronts him to prevent Kerana’s abduction and fights him for seven days and nights.** *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. **Secure literary attestation.**
- **Pytajovái — intervenes on Taû’s side and knocks Angatupyry down, deciding the combat.** *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. **Secure literary attestation / Colmán’s systematization.**
- **Arasy — both occur in the same narrative complex: after Angatupyry’s defeat, the people appeal to Arasy and she curses Taû. No direct Angatupyry–Arasy exchange is narrated.** *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. **Joint narrative presence, not a direct interaction.**
- **Rupavê and Sypavê — Angatupyry is assigned as their invisible companion and moral support.** *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. They are primordial humans, not gods.
- **Kerana — Angatupyry attempts to defend her from Taû; no conversation or continuing relationship is narrated.** *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. Kerana is human in Colmán, not a “goddess of sleep.”
- **Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’î, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao-Ao, and Huicho/Luisõ — no direct interaction with Angatupyry is narrated in Colmán.** Later summaries sometimes say Angatupyry cursed their parents or the children, but Colmán assigns the curse to Arasy. **Absence of evidence / later misattribution.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The invisible companions of humanity

**Secure literary version, 1929.** Tupã and Arasy create Rupavê and Sypavê and instruct them about work, procreation, land, animals, plants, death, and moral conduct. Tupã then leaves two unseen companions: Angatupyry sustains people in good; Taû pushes them toward evil. Human affliction is interpreted as their contest, and healing as Angatupyry’s victory.

The narrative is not absolute Manichaean dualism. Taû is subordinate to Tupã, and Tupã declares adversity necessary for human valuation, effort, and experience. The two spirits are opposing moral agencies inside a creator’s ordered world, not coequal uncreated principles.

**Developmental assessment:** The clay creation of a first man and woman, creator’s ethical commandments, invisible good and evil advisers, temptation, universalized moral struggle, and reward after death are conspicuously compatible with Christian catechetical cosmology. This does not mean every word or motif was invented from nothing. It means that the assembled structure is not independently attested as pre-Christian Guaraní theology.

### Angatupyry, Taû, and Kerana

**Secure literary version, 1929.** Taû desires Kerana, daughter of Marangatu and granddaughter of the primordial couple. He assumes a handsome human form, visits her, and on the seventh day tries to abduct her. Angatupyry intervenes. After seven days and nights of battle, Taû is near defeat. He calls Pytajovái, described as his aged grandfather, god of courage and bearer of destructive fire. Pytajovái fells Angatupyry. Taû then abducts Kerana.

The people pray to Arasy, who curses Taû. Kerana thereafter bears seven monstrous children at seven-month intervals: Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’î, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao-Ao, and Huicho/Luisõ.

**Later contradictory versions:**

- Some retellings say Angatupyry wins and Taû is exiled; Colmán says Taû wins only through Pytajovái’s intervention.
- Some say Angatupyry curses the couple; Colmán says Arasy does.
- Some describe Kerana as a goddess; Colmán makes her a human descendant of the first couple.
- Some call Taû Angatupyry’s brother; Colmán does not.
- Some make the seven beings Angatupyry’s nephews; that depends on the unattested brother relationship.
- Modern accounts sometimes omit Pytajovái entirely, converting the encounter into a simple Good-versus-Evil duel.

These are not interchangeable details. They show how the authored poem became oralized and simplified after publication.

### The “seven monsters” as a system

Kurupi, Jasy Jatere, Luisõ/Lobizón, Pombero-like beings, and other regional figures have histories independent of Colmán. What Colmán systematized was their incorporation into one numbered sibling group born to Taû and Kerana, within a Genesis-like genealogy. Scholarly and recent literary analysis treats that family structure as Colmán’s creation or radical reorganization, not a securely recovered pre-Columbian canon.

Angatupyry’s role belongs to this literary architecture: the defeat of Good enables the abduction that produces the monstrous lineage.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No cult of Angatupyry is documented.

Specifically, research found no evidence for:

- a temple, shrine, sacred grove, altar, or archaeological dedication;
- priests or hereditary religious specialists serving Angatupyry;
- a feast day or ritual calendar;
- sacrifice, including human sacrifice;
- food, drink, tobacco, animal, or first-fruit offerings;
- invocations, prayers, hymns, healing formulas, or divination addressed to Angatupyry;
- votive objects or inscriptions;
- theophoric names securely demonstrating a historic cult;
- pilgrimage or oracle sites;
- temple prostitution, cult violence, or cult economics.

Modern use of **Angatupyry as a personal name** reflects linguistic and cultural revival rather than proof of ancient theophoric naming.

The absence is consistent with the character’s textual nature. Colmán presents a universal invisible influence, not a recipient of worship. General Guaraní prayer, shamanism, sacred song, dance, tobacco practice, and relations with divine fathers cannot be reassigned to Angatupyry without evidence.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1639: no securely reconstructable Angatupyry figure

Pre-contact Guaraní-speaking peoples had diverse religious systems, ritual specialists, culture heroes, masters of species, divine fathers, and accounts of world creation and destruction. No evidence securely connects a named Angatupyry personification to this period.

### 1639–1767: Jesuit translation and reduction

Montoya’s dictionaries and mission history document Christian evangelization and the translation of Christian categories into Guaraní. Missionaries used Indigenous words to express “God,” “soul,” “sin,” “devil,” holiness, and salvation. This linguistic history matters because *angatupyry* is an ethical-spiritual Guaraní expression readily available for Christianized moral discourse.

It does **not** follow that Jesuits invented the named character Angatupyry: no colonial text located here contains the character. The more defensible conclusion is that centuries of Christian Guaraní vocabulary and catechesis created part of the semantic environment in which Colmán could personify “the spirit of good.”

### Nineteenth century: folklore without the complete Colmán pantheon

Collectors in Paraguay, Corrientes, and Misiones documented numerous beings and local legends. The evidence reviewed does not establish Angatupyry as an independently circulating divine personality or the full seven-sibling genealogy before Colmán.

### 1914: Nimuendajú’s counterexample

Nimuendajú published Apapocúva-Guaraní creation and destruction narratives. These provide a detailed Indigenous religious comparison but not Angatupyry. His account also complicates simple claims that contemporary Guaraní religion is either wholly pre-Christian or wholly Christian: he recognized limited Christian borrowings within a continuing Indigenous system.

### 1922–1929: composition and publication

Colmán’s poem was recognized in manuscript form in 1922 and published in 1929. Its moment was one of Paraguayan linguistic nationalism, indigenismo, and the elevation of Guaraní as a national literary medium. Angatupyry enters the documented record inside this project.

### 1936/1937: Spanish dissemination

The Spanish version made the cosmogony accessible beyond Guaraní readers. Its paratexts presented etymologies and comparative claims with quasi-ethnographic authority, contributing to later treatment of the poem as a repository of ancient belief.

### 1950s–1970s: ethnography reveals different cosmologies

Schaden, Cadogan, Métraux, and the Clastres published community-specific Guaraní and Tupí-Guaraní religious materials. These demonstrated the importance of sacred speech, divine plurality, shamanic practice, world renewal, and the Land Without Evil. They do not confirm Colmán’s Angatupyry–Taû dualism.

### Late twentieth century: folklorization and schooling

Colmán’s system entered folklore books, school lessons, tourism, children’s illustration, and regional identity programs in Paraguay and northeastern Argentina. Once detached from its author, it was often described simply as “Guaraní mythology.” Argentine bilingual educational material still retells Tupã’s clay creation and the Angatupyry–Taû pairing while identifying it as Colmán’s conception in some editions.

### Twenty-first century: digital canonization

Wikipedia derivatives, mythology databases, educational sites, and fandom pages reproduce a compact profile: son of Tupã, brother and opposite of Taû, guardian of virtue, defender of Kerana. Repetition has increased visibility but not antiquity. Some sites add radiant iconography, powers, genealogy, and male sex without citing an earlier source.

### Present

Angatupyry is a real component of **modern Paraguayan folklore and cultural memory**, regardless of its literary origin. What is not demonstrated is a continuous Indigenous cult from pre-contact times. Contemporary Indigenous Mbyá, Ava/Chiripá, Pai Tavyterã, Kaiowá, and other communities should not be presumed to share Colmán’s character merely because they speak Guaraní languages.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Education and public folklore

The character now appears in school material as a moral guide paired with Taû. This is the strongest modern reception channel. Educational retellings often collapse three distinct things:

1. Indigenous Guaraní religious traditions;
2. colonial Christianized Guaraní vocabulary;
3. Colmán’s national literary mythology.

The resulting story is culturally established but historically layered.

### Digital mythology

Modern webpages promote Angatupyry from “spirit” to “god,” supply a masculine body, and make Taû a sibling. A 2023 Paraguayan educational page calls the figure one of Tupã’s sons and the most powerful benevolent sibling, associates him with courage, wisdom, justice, and luminous imagery, and presents these expansions without a primary citation. These should be labeled **modern elaboration**, not recovered antiquity.

### “Angatupyry – Un viaje primigenio”

In November 2024, Frank Mareco’s multidisciplinary work *Angatupyry – Un viaje primigenio*, directed by Wal Mayans, was staged at the Centro Cultural del Puerto in Asunción with support from Paraguay’s Secretaría Nacional de Cultura. It combined performance, dance-theatre, music, film, painting, photography, engraving, and sculpture.

Its Angatupyry is an explicit contemporary symbol: the “spirit of good” and “wise spirit” becomes a figure for human aspiration, identity, freedom, environmental consciousness, and recovery from aculturation after a global catastrophe. This is not a traditional myth episode but a documented modern philosophical reinvention.

### Occultism, neopaganism, and New Age claims

The dedicated search found no historically documented Angatupyry tradition in:

- Renaissance Hermetism;
- ceremonial magic or grimoires;
- Theosophy;
- the Golden Dawn;
- Spiritism;
- Umbanda;
- chaos magic;
- established neopagan or reconstructionist organizations.

No substantial contemporary worshipping community or ritual lineage could be established. Individual devotional experiments may exist online, but they cannot be generalized into an organized revival.

### Political and nationalist use

The decisive political context is cultural nationalism rather than extremist appropriation. Colmán helped make written Guaraní a vehicle for Paraguayan national literature. His cosmogony supplied a dignified ancestral epic, but it also converted diverse Indigenous traditions into a single genealogy of “the Guaraní race.” No specific far-right, paramilitary, or extremist appropriation of Angatupyry was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Angatupyry ancient?

The popular position treats the character as an ancient Guaraní spirit remembered by Colmán. The critical position observes that:

- the name as a being is absent from the principal earlier textual record examined;
- the complete mythology first appears in an authored poem;
- Colmán’s creator, first couple, moral commandments, and good/evil spirits closely resemble Genesis and catechetical moral theology;
- the poem merges beings with separate folkloric histories into one genealogy;
- Colmán’s explanatory notes include speculative etymology, diffusionism, Atlantean material, and comparisons that do not meet modern ethnographic standards.

The evidence favors classifying the individualized Angatupyry as **Colmán’s learned literary systematization**, perhaps built from existing Guaraní moral vocabulary and unrecorded oral motifs, but not demonstrably an ancient god.

### Christian shaping versus total Christian invention

Two overstatements should be avoided.

- It is too strong to say that Christian missionaries demonstrably invented Angatupyry: no Jesuit source naming the character has been produced.
- It is also too strong to say that the Colmán narrative is an untouched pre-Christian survival: its architecture and documentary history contradict that claim.

A historically careful formulation is that Colmán personified an intelligible Guaraní ethical term inside a literary cosmogony deeply shaped by Christianized Guaraní language, biblical narrative patterns, and early twentieth-century nationalism.

Judith Shapiro’s study of the Christianization of Tupí-Guaraní cosmology demonstrates the broader missionary process: Christian concepts were repeatedly translated through Indigenous terms, while later interpreters also recast Indigenous concepts for changing Christian projects. Angatupyry is not discussed there as an early deity, but the process supplies the appropriate historical context.

### The “moral dualism” question

Colmán explicitly names an eternal conflict between good and evil. Nevertheless:

- Angatupyry and Taû are not equal creators;
- both operate within Tupã’s order;
- Tupã regards Taû’s presence as instrumentally necessary;
- another deity can defeat Angatupyry;
- Arasy exercises punitive authority.

The system is therefore moral dualism in narrative function but not a fully dualist theology like classic Manichaeism.

Indigenous Guaraní traditions certainly distinguish beneficent and dangerous beings, flourishing and destructive states, and the imperfect earth from imperishable divine realms. That does not establish a native binary personification matching Angatupyry and Taû.

### Problems in modern summaries

Recurring unsupported or erroneous claims include:

- **“Angatupyry was worshipped throughout the Tupí-Guaraní world.”** Not established.
- **“He was the son of Tupã.”** Modern literalization.
- **“Taû was his brother.”** Not in Colmán.
- **“Angatupyry defeated Taû and banished him.”** Contradicts Colmán’s battle.
- **“Angatupyry cursed Taû and Kerana.”** Colmán assigns the curse to Arasy.
- **“Kerana was a sleep goddess.”** In Colmán she is a human woman nicknamed for sleeping.
- **“The seven monsters formed an ancient canonical family.”** Their organization into this family is first securely documented in Colmán.
- **“Radiance, white robes, wings, or a specific emblem are traditional.”** No early source supports them.

### State of the evidence

The essential primary object is the printed poem, preserved in library records and digital reproductions. The original 1922 manuscript history deserves archival investigation, especially in records of the Twentieth International Congress of Americanists, the Sociedad Científica del Paraguay, and Paraguayan national collections. Finding an earlier version containing Angatupyry could move the first attestation back several years, but not into the colonial or pre-contact era.

A definitive negative corpus study would require full-text collation of all editions of Montoya, Jesuit manuscripts, Ambrosetti, early Corrientes and Misiones collections, Colmán’s papers, and regional newspapers. The present result is strong but properly provisional: **no pre-1929 attestation of the character was found.**

| 2000s–2020s | Encyclopedias and mythology sites increasingly describe Angatupyry as an ancient god, Tupã’s son, and Taû’s brother; these are modern accretions. |
| 2023 | A Paraguayan educational website supplies luminous imagery, expanded virtues, and a divine sibling genealogy without early documentation. |
| 15–17 November 2024 | *Angatupyry – Un viaje primigenio* is staged in Asunción, explicitly transforming the figure into a contemporary symbol of identity, freedom, wisdom, and environmental consciousness. |
| 2026 | Angatupyry remains well established in Paraguayan national folklore, but no ancient cult, continuous worship, or pre-Colmán textual attestation has been demonstrated. |

## 11. Sources and source assessment

### Primary and near-primary sources

- Narciso R. Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929); Spanish version *Nuestros antepasados* (1936/1937). The digitized Spanish text contains the creation commission, illness contest, Kerana battle, Pytajovái’s intervention, and Colmán’s glossary.
- Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639). Colonial lexical control source; important for what it can and cannot establish.
- Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Conquista espiritual* (1639). Mission history, not evidence for an Angatupyry cult.
- Curt Nimuendajú, *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt* (1914). Comparative Apapocúva primary ethnography.
- León Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959). Primary Mbyá sacred discourse with Spanish translation.
- The 2024 Radio Nacional notice for *Angatupyry – Un viaje primigenio*. Primary evidence for contemporary artistic reinterpretation.

### Critical scholarship

- Rodrigo N. Villalba Rojas, “Rosicrán y su poema *Ñande Ĭpĭ Cuéra* (1929).” The clearest published challenge to reading Colmán as transparent ethnography.
- Villalba Rojas’s work on Guaraní poetry and Paraguayan nationalism places Colmán in the formation of national Guaraní literature.
- Judith Shapiro, “From Tupã to the Land Without Evil: The Christianization of Tupi-Guarani Cosmology” (1987), supplies the comparative history of missionary translation.
- Schaden, Cadogan, Métraux, Melià, and Pierre and Hélène Clastres remain essential for preventing Colmán’s literary pantheon from being projected indiscriminately onto all Guaraní-speaking peoples.

The resulting conclusion is narrow but firm: **Angatupyry is securely documented from Colmán’s twentieth-century literary cosmogony onward. The word has genuine Guaraní ethical and spiritual meaning; the individualized divine actor, binary moral office, genealogy, and narrative career are not securely attested before Colmán.**
