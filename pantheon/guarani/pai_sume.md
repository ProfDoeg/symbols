# Pa'i Sumé (Sumé): Research Dossier

**Class:** culture hero, wandering teacher, prophet, and landscape-marking spirit in colonial and modern Tupi–Guaraní traditions; subsequently identified with Saint Thomas the Apostle. He is not securely attested as a “god” with an organized cult, priesthood, temple, or fixed place in a Guaraní pantheon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Manuel da Nóbrega’s Portuguese Jesuit letters from Brazil in **1549**, reporting that coastal Tupinambá knew a figure he rendered **Zomé/Sumé**, associated with Saint Thomas and footprints in rock. A **1543** document says Guaraní-speaking Indigenous companions called a Franciscan **Payçumé**, but this is evidence for the expression as an honorific applied to a missionary, not an unambiguous attestation of the culture hero. A German news-sheet of about **1515**, the *Newen Zeytung auss Presillg Landt*, is sometimes treated as a precursor to the American Saint Thomas legend, but it does not securely document Pa'i Sumé under that name.

**Evidentiary conclusion:** “Pa'i Sumé” is best treated not as one recoverable pre-Columbian deity with a stable biography, but as a layered historical complex:

1. a sixteenth-century Tupinambá/Southern Tupi wandering culture hero called Sumé or a similar form;
2. Indigenous traditions concerning footprints, paths, useful knowledge, and departed teachers;
3. a colonial Christian identification with **São Tomé/Santo Tomás**;
4. a Paraguayan and regional folklore figure whose agricultural, yerba-mate, ethical, and apostolic attributes accumulated in later retellings;
5. the literary **Tumé Arandú** created or systematized by Narciso R. Colmán in 1929;
6. modern attempts to equate him with unrelated Mbyá beings, especially **Pa'i Rete Kuaray**, or to make him evidence of pre-Columbian Christian, Phoenician, Hebrew, or other transoceanic visitors.

These layers must not be collapsed.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

In the earliest documentation, Sumé is a remembered visitor or teacher whose departure left physical traces. The sources do not consistently classify him as a god. Colonial authors variously make him an Indigenous culture hero, a prophet, an apostle, a holy man, a wonder-worker, or the distorted memory of Saint Thomas. Modern Paraguayan folklore commonly calls him **Pa'i Sumé**, describing a wise traveler who taught cultivation, food preparation, social precepts, and—especially in late Paraguayan versions—the properties of yerba mate.

The divine classification is therefore historically variable:

- **[Securely attested early material]** Tupinambá remembered visitor/culture hero, in Nóbrega’s 1549 report.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** Saint Thomas the Apostle, beginning in the earliest missionary documentation.
- **[Early-modern missionary elaboration]** apostolic precursor who preached Christianity, carried a cross, prophesied the Jesuits, and opened a transcontinental road.
- **[Folklore]** white-bearded teacher of agriculture, cassava, maize, yerba mate, crafts, morality, and religion.
- **[Learned systematization]** Tumé Arandú, son of Rupavê and Sypavê and prophet of Colmán’s literary pantheon.
- **[Modern reconstruction/invention]** a single pan-Guaraní high god, a securely pre-Columbian Christian missionary, or an extraterrestrial/transoceanic civilizer.

### Names and spellings

- **Sumé** — the most common modern Portuguese form.
- **Zomé / Zumé** — colonial and later Portuguese spellings.
- **Somé** — occasional orthographic variant.
- **Tumé / Thomé / Tomé** — forms reflecting, or interpreted through, Portuguese *Tomé*, “Thomas.”
- **Pay Zumé / Pai Sumé / Pa'i Sumé** — Spanish, Portuguese, and modern Guaraní spellings.
- **Payçumé / Payçume / Paycume** — early-modern spellings in Spanish sources.
- **Pay Tumé** — form attributed by Montoya to Peru.
- **Avaré Sumé / Avaré Sumé marangatú** — later Paraguayan formulations: *avaré* is an old Guaraní expression used for a religious specialist and subsequently for a priest; *marangatu* means sacred, privileged, virtuous, or holy according to context.
- **Tumé Arandú** — literally or approximately “Tumé the wise/knowing,” the name used in Colmán’s 1929 poem.
- **Karaíba Sumé** — “Sumé the karaíba,” where *karaíba* in early Tupi sources can designate an extraordinary religious specialist or prophet; it should not automatically be translated “god.”

### What *pa'i* means

Modern Guaraní **pa'i** is the ordinary term for a Catholic priest, but historically it belongs to a more complicated vocabulary of religious authority. Montoya glossed related forms as applying to something divine, powerful, wise, or to Indigenous ritual specialists. His *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* records such ecclesiastical uses as *pa'i/abaré* and *payguasu* for prelate or bishop. His etymological explanation of **Payçumé** as “powerful or wise Thomas” is, however, part of his apostolic argument, not a modern comparative-linguistic demonstration.

Isabelle Combès draws attention to a 1543 report by Pedro de Orantes: Indigenous companions in the upper Paraguay called a Franciscan **Payçumé** and held him in great veneration. This shows the expression could function as an Indigenous designation for a missionary very early. It does not prove that an independently named god Pa'i Sumé already possessed all the later legends.

### The etymological dispute

Three principal explanations appear in the literature:

1. **Indigenous-name-first hypothesis.** *Sumé* was the name of a pre-existing Tupi culture hero; missionaries equated it with *Tomé* because of phonetic resemblance and shared footprint traditions. This was the standard interpretation of Métraux, Schaden, Sérgio Buarque de Holanda, and much subsequent scholarship.

2. **Missionary-translation hypothesis.** The recorded form may already be an Indigenous adaptation of Portuguese or Spanish *Tomé/Tomás*. The sources were written after contact by Christians actively looking for apostolic traces, so the documentation cannot recover a pristine pre-contact name without uncertainty.

3. **Circular formation.** Indigenous stories about teachers, prophets, paths, and marked rocks and the Christian legend of the traveling apostle became mutually translated. “Sumé” and “Tomé” then reinforced one another. Cavalcante’s work on cultural appropriation and resignification favors this historically interactive model.

No surviving pre-contact inscription, image, or Indigenous manuscript settles the question.

### Identifications that must be kept separate

- **Sumé = Saint Thomas:** a sixteenth-century colonial Christian identification, not a timeless Indigenous equivalence.
- **Sumé = Pa'i Rete Kuaray:** a modern comparison, not an identification made in *Ayvu Rapyta*.
- **Sumé = Tumé Arandú:** largely a literary-national formulation deriving from Colmán’s *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*.
- **Sumé = Viracocha, Quetzalcoatl, Bochica, Manco Cápac, or the “White God”:** comparativist or diffusionist constructions, generally dependent on the broad “bearded civilizer” motif rather than historical evidence of identity.
- **Sumé = Paititi’s “White King”:** Isabelle Combès’s explicitly proposed historical-linguistic hypothesis, not an attested Indigenous equation.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Possible precursor: *Newen Zeytung* (c. 1515)

The German *Newen Zeytung auss Presillg Landt* is repeatedly cited in the scholarship on Saint Thomas in America. It belongs to the earliest European circulation of reports about Brazil, but it is not secure evidence for a named Pa'i Sumé. Claims that it proves a fully formed pre-contact Sumé myth commonly depend on later interpretation.

**Tier:** precursor to the colonial Saint Thomas discourse; not a secure named attestation.

### Pedro de Orantes, 1543

A factor’s report quoted and analyzed by Combès says that Indigenous companions in the upper Paraguay called a Franciscan **Payçumé** and greatly revered him.

What it establishes:

- a *Payçumé*-like expression was in use among Guaraní-speaking people by 1543;
- it could be applied to a Christian friar.

What it does not establish:

- a divine person named Pa'i Sumé;
- the footprint legend;
- agricultural teaching;
- yerba mate;
- a genealogy or cult.

**Tier:** securely attested early linguistic/religious-contact evidence; ambiguous as evidence for the being.

### Manuel da Nóbrega, 1549

Nóbrega’s letters are the earliest secure reports of the named culture-hero complex. Writing from Portuguese Brazil, he says the Tupinambá had knowledge of Saint Thomas and a companion and showed vestiges in rock. In another formulation he reports personally seeing four footprints, complete with toes, on a rock periodically covered by a river. He also transmits the claim that the visitor gave the people the roots and herbs on which they lived.

These statements are evidence that Nóbrega heard such explanations in 1549. They are not independent proof that the figure was Saint Thomas or that the marks were artificial footprints.

Nóbrega’s interpretive environment matters. His letters elsewhere initially deny that Indigenous people possess stable gods or religious vocabulary. His discovery of an apostolic tradition therefore served a Christian apologetic function: the inhabitants of America could be fitted into universal salvation history, while their failure to preserve Christianity could be blamed on forgetting or rejecting an earlier revelation.

**Tier:** securely attested early report, already filtered through missionary identification.

### André Thevet, 1557/1575

Thevet’s writings about Brazil give a more developed Sumé as **héros civilisateur**, a civilizing hero who taught useful practices. His evidence derives from the French colonial encounter with coastal Tupinambá, not from Paraguayan Guaraní communities. The relevant accounts appeared in *Les Singularitez de la France antarctique* (1557) and were elaborated in the *Cosmographie universelle* (1575).

**Tier:** early colonial Tupinambá elaboration.

### Martín González, 1588, and Alonso de Barzana, 1594

Combès identifies further notices in Paraguayan documentation: Martín González in 1588 and the Jesuit Alonso de Barzana among the Guaraní-speaking Cario of Asunción in 1594. These strengthen the case that a Pa'i Sumé/Saint Thomas complex circulated widely before Montoya, but each source must still be read within colonial missionary discourse.

**Tier:** securely attested late-sixteenth-century regional transmission.

### Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Conquista espiritual* (Madrid, 1639)

Chapters XXI–XXVI are the fullest early-modern construction of Saint Thomas in the Paraguay–Paraná–Guayrá mission world. Montoya says:

- the apostle came through Brazil, Paraguay, and Peru;
- he was called **Pay Zumé** in Paraguay and **Pay Tumé** in Peru;
- a path attributed to him extended inland from the São Vicente region;
- the road was about eight palms wide and bore a distinctive small herb even when surrounding country was burned;
- footprints were shown near Asunción, at São Vicente, and elsewhere;
- Indigenous people said he had preached from rocks;
- he foretold successors who would carry crosses;
- Indigenous reception of Jesuits could consequently be represented as fulfillment of Sumé’s prophecy.

Montoya’s account combines personal observation of roads, reports by Indigenous and European informants, older apostolic lore, etymological speculation, and missionary theology. Its physical descriptions of a trail do not demonstrate apostolic authorship. Its prophecy of future cross-bearing missionaries is especially well suited to Jesuit legitimation and cannot be assumed to be pre-contact.

The *Conquista* is an apologetic history of the missions and a plea concerning Indigenous suffering and bandeirante slave raids, not a neutral ethnographic transcript. Its 81 chapters mix history, ethnography, edification, prodigy, and institutional advocacy.

**Tier:** early-modern missionary systematization and syncretistic identification.

### Montoya, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639)

The *Tesoro* is indispensable for seventeenth-century Guaraní religious vocabulary. It clarifies the semantic field of *pa'i*, *abaré*, religious specialists, and ecclesiastical titles. It does not supply an independent pre-contact narrative corpus for Sumé. Montoya’s lexical analysis of **Payçumé** must be read alongside his effort in the *Conquista* to prove Saint Thomas’s American mission.

**Tier:** primary linguistic evidence; missionary lexical interpretation.

### Later colonial chronicles

Frei Vicente do Salvador, Simão de Vasconcellos, Antonio de la Calancha, and later ecclesiastical historians extended the American Saint Thomas itinerary. They connected rock marks, springs, crosses, roads, and local traditions from Brazil through Paraguay and into the Andes. By this stage the story had become a continental apostolic geography.

Calancha’s seventeenth-century argument is particularly expansive: he interprets *Payçumé* as “powerful and wise Thomas,” describes a footprint near Asunción, and treats tonsured or long-haired peoples and alleged descendants of the apostle’s hearers as surviving testimony.

**Tier:** early-modern Catholic accretion and learned harmonization.

### Nimuendajú, 1914

Curt Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* records Apapocúva creation and destruction traditions and the search for the Land Without Evil. It is a major primary ethnographic corpus for Guaraní religion but does **not** establish Pa'i Sumé as a central Apapocúva divinity. Its importance here is comparative and negative: the detailed recorded cosmology does not reproduce Montoya’s apostolic Sumé narrative as a canonical divine biography.

**Tier:** modern ethnography; absence of a central Sumé is significant.

### Métraux, 1928

Alfred Métraux’s *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus Tupi-Guarani* assembled the colonial Tupinambá sources and treated Sumé within the culture-hero and civilizer complex. Métraux remains foundational because he compared Nóbrega, Thevet, Anchieta, Léry, and related chroniclers rather than accepting the Saint Thomas identification as history.

**Tier:** modern scholarly reconstruction.

### Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929)

Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní poem makes **Tumé Arandú** the eldest son of the primordial couple **Rupavê** and **Sypavê**, brother of Marangatu, Japeusá, Porãsy, Guarasyáva, Tupinambá, and Yrasẽ. Tumé becomes prophet, teacher, organizer, and opponent of the seven monsters born to Taû and Kerana.

This is not an ancient scripture or a verbatim collection of Indigenous oral texts. It is a 2,800-line literary, nationalist, and etnogenetic construction. Rodrigo Villalba Rojas characterizes it as an “entirely fictional” poem that came to displace diverse Indigenous myths in schools and popular consciousness. Its closed genealogy of Taû, Kerana, and seven monster-children is therefore a **twentieth-century literary systematization**, however deeply it has entered Paraguayan folklore.

The author’s Spanish prose translation appeared in 1937.

**Tier:** learned literary systematization/modern invention based on selected folkloric materials.

### Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959), and *Ywyra Ñe'ery* (1971)

León Cadogan recorded sacred Mbyá texts, cosmology, hymns, and the cycle of **Pa'i Rete Kuaray** and Jachy. These works are frequently recruited into synthetic “Guaraní mythology,” but their Pa'i Rete Kuaray is not simply Pa'i Sumé under another name.

Pa'i Rete Kuaray is a luminous solar-bodied ancestral figure, culture hero, and father of the Mbyá people. His myths belong to the widespread Tupi–Guaraní Twins cycle: the mother’s death, the brothers’ encounters with jaguars and other beings, restoration and transformation, and their celestial destinations. Neither the name nor the narrative warrants replacing him with Saint Thomas/Sumé.

**Tier:** Indigenous Mbyá corpus; comparative analogue, not an attested identity.

### Pierre and Hélène Clastres

Pierre Clastres’s *Le Grand Parler* (1974) anthologizes Guaraní myths and sacred speech drawn from Cadogan, Nimuendajú, and his own fieldwork. Hélène Clastres’s *La Terre sans Mal* (1975) interprets Tupi–Guaraní prophecy and migration. They are central to the study of Guaraní religious speech and the Land Without Evil, but neither supplies evidence for making Pa'i Sumé the master deity of a unified Guaraní pantheon.

**Tier:** modern anthropological interpretation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Secure and early attributes

- **Wandering visitor:** Nóbrega and Thevet.
- **Teacher of useful subsistence knowledge:** sixteenth-century Tupinambá reports, especially roots, herbs, cassava, and food preparation.
- **Footprint maker:** Nóbrega, followed by numerous colonial chroniclers.
- **Departed or rejected teacher:** colonial variants often say the people refused, mistreated, or drove him away.
- **Road opener:** Montoya’s seventeenth-century association with the route later called the Caminho de São Tomé or linked to Peabiru.

### Later or regional attributes

- **Teacher of maize cultivation:** later Paraguayan and regional folklore.
- **Teacher of agriculture as a complete system:** an expansion of the early food-giver theme.
- **Teacher of yerba mate:** strongly represented in modern Paraguayan folklore and accounts dependent on the Jesuit Saint Thomas complex; it is not securely present in Nóbrega’s 1549 notice.
- **Teacher of crafts, utensils, ethics, monogamy, and social organization:** colonial and modern harmonization.
- **White, tall, long-haired, and bearded:** familiar from later “white civilizer” narratives. The early documentary core is less iconographically fixed.
- **Long robe, staff, or cross:** Christianized iconography.
- **Walking over water or returning across the sea:** late colonial and folkloric elaboration.

### Footprints and sacred geography

Reported footprint sites stretch far beyond one culture or region: Bahia, Pernambuco, Paraíba, Cabo Frio, São Vicente/Santos, the Paraguay River region near Asunción, Paraná, and Andean locations. Their proliferation demonstrates the geographical success of the legend, not one traveler’s itinerary.

Some footprint stones became Catholic contact relics. They were visited, scraped for powder, incorporated into shrines, or destroyed. At Embaré, eighteenth-century tradition said footprint-bearing stones were broken for rebuilding the São Vicente church. Ambrosetti later recorded “hands and feet of the saint” traditions at Santo Pipó in Misiones.

No securely identified pre-contact image of Sumé survives. Petroglyphs, natural depressions, and fossil impressions attributed to him cannot be dated by the accompanying legend alone. Claims that the Pedra do Ingá or other rock-art complexes were made by Sumé are modern interpretations, not archaeological identifications.

### Symbols and correspondences

Securely attested:

- footprints;
- a path or cleared road;
- rocks used as preaching places;
- roots and cultivated foods.

Christianized:

- cross;
- priestly garment;
- apostolic staff;
- holy springs and relic stones.

Not securely attested:

- a fixed sacred animal;
- a cult color;
- a planet, star, weekday, or number;
- weapons or royal regalia;
- temples or canonical statues.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The early sources give Sumé **no parentage, spouse, children, or divine siblings**. This absence is important. The figure’s earliest profile is functional and itinerant, not genealogical.

Colmán’s 1929 *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* supplies a genealogy:

- parents: **Rupavê** and **Sypavê**;
- brothers: **Marangatu** and **Japeusá**;
- sisters: **Porãsy**, **Guarasyáva**, **Tupinambá**, and **Yrasẽ**;
- adversaries: **Taû**, **Kerana**, and their seven monstrous offspring;
- superior divine authority: **Tupã**.

This is a twentieth-century literary genealogy. It must not be retrojected into the sixteenth century.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

Because the early Sumé corpus is sparse, most named-being interactions occur only in Colmán’s modern literary system.

- **Saint Thomas the Apostle — identity/fusion:** missionaries identify Sumé with Thomas; Nóbrega, 1549, followed by Montoya, 1639. **Tier: syncretistic identification.**
- **God/Christian Deus — apostolic agent:** Montoya makes Sumé/Thomas a missionary sent under the Christian God’s providence. *Conquista espiritual*, 1639. **Tier: early-modern Christian elaboration.**
- **Tupã — Tumé acts under Tupã’s authority and seeks divine aid against evil:** Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Taû — ideological and practical opposition between wise Tumé and the embodiment of evil:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Kerana — indirect adversarial relation through the monster cycle:** Colmán, 1929. No separate early myth of an encounter is securely attested. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Teju Jagua — Tumé participates in the campaign against the monstrous children:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Mbói Tu'ĩ — same monster-destruction cycle:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Moñái — Tumé and his people oppose and destroy the monsters gathered at Moñái’s cave:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Jasy Jateré — included among the seven adversarial monsters:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Kurupí — included among the seven adversarial monsters:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Ao Ao — included among the seven adversarial monsters:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Luisõ — included among the seven adversarial monsters:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Porãsy — Tumé’s sister sacrifices herself as part of the plan to destroy the monsters:** Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Pa'i Rete Kuaray — no documented meeting or interaction:** the two are compared by modern writers because both are culture heroes called *Pa'i*, but Cadogan does not narrate them meeting or identify them. **Tier: absence of evidence.**
- **Ñamandu Ru Ete — no documented interaction with Sumé:** central in Mbyá cosmology, but absent from the secure Sumé narratives. **Tier: absence of evidence.**
- **Jachy — no documented interaction with Sumé:** Jachy is Pa'i Rete Kuaray’s companion/brother in the Mbyá cycle, not Sumé’s securely attested brother. **Tier: absence of evidence.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The visiting teacher

**Earliest form:** Nóbrega, 1549.

A mysterious visitor taught the inhabitants useful foods or the roots and herbs by which they lived. The people retained notice of him and showed marks associated with his presence. In later Tupinambá accounts he becomes a fuller civilizing hero who teaches agriculture, cassava preparation, fishing techniques, fire, and social rules.

**Development:** the earliest documentary witnesses already write after European contact and already identify him with Thomas. A wholly pre-contact version cannot be reconstructed word for word.

**Function:** explanation of cultural knowledge and, in missionary hands, evidence that Christianity had once reached America.

### The rejected prophet

In several colonial variants the teacher urges moral or religious reform, but the people resist, shoot arrows at him, or drive him away. He departs over the sea or toward another country, leaving marks behind.

This story may preserve an Indigenous departure motif, a missionary allegory of rejected revelation, or both. Versions that have him condemn polygyny, warfare, ritual drinking, or anthropophagy are especially compatible with colonial Christian polemic.

**Tier:** colonial syncretistic narrative with uncertain Indigenous core.

### The footprints

Sumé/Thomas steps on rock and leaves prints. Nóbrega says he personally saw four marked footprints in a river rock. Later accounts multiply them across eastern South America. The prints validate local memory, sacralize landscapes, and—in Christian use—operate as relics of contact.

No archaeological method can identify a footprint-shaped depression as belonging to a mythical or apostolic traveler. The historical datum is the attribution and its recorded date.

**Tier:** securely attested early legend; expanding colonial and folkloric geography.

### The road of Sumé/Saint Thomas

Montoya describes a conspicuous route from the São Vicente area into the interior, known as a path of Saint Thomas or Pa'i Zumé. Later writers merge it with the **Peabiru**, the Indigenous trail network linking the Atlantic, Paraná basin, Paraguay, and routes toward the Andes.

The trail system was real and predated the Jesuits. Attribution of its construction to one supernatural teacher is legendary. Treating every segment called Peabiru as a single engineered “Sumé road” is a modern simplification.

**Tier:** real Indigenous infrastructure plus early-modern apostolic attribution.

### The prophecy of cross-bearing successors

In Montoya’s account, Pa'i Zumé foretells that priests carrying crosses will come after him. Guaraní then recognize the Jesuits as the promised successors, listen to the Gospel, and form a reduction at Encarnación.

This is the clearest example of the legend’s political and institutional utility. It retrospectively authorizes the mission and frames conversion as fulfillment rather than rupture.

**Tier:** early-modern Jesuit legitimating narrative.

### The yerba-mate teacher

Modern Paraguayan versions say Sumé taught the virtues and preparation of **ka'a**, yerba mate. Some scholarship traces this attribution to Jesuit efforts to place Indigenous botanical knowledge inside an apostolic history. The link is later than the securely documented Tupinambá food-giver motif.

The legend should be reported in full, but not dated to pre-contact antiquity without evidence.

**Tier:** Paraguayan folklore and colonial/postcolonial elaboration.

### Tumé Arandú and the seven monsters

In Colmán’s poem Tumé Arandú belongs to the first Guaraní family, becomes the wise prophet, opposes the disorders caused by Taû and Kerana’s offspring, and helps organize their destruction. Porãsy offers herself as bait; the monsters gather in a cave and are burned, with Porãsy dying in the conflagration.

This is sometimes presented today as the canonical ancient Guaraní pantheon. It is instead a modern literary synthesis drawing on disparate names and traditions, biblical structures, nationalist etnogenesis, and the author’s own invention.

**Tier:** modern literary systematization, not early canonical myth.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There is no securely documented institutional cult of Pa'i Sumé comparable to a temple-centered deity.

### What is attested

- showing and visiting footprint rocks;
- Christian veneration of supposed Saint Thomas traces;
- removal or scraping of footprint stone as relic material;
- place-names and routes associated with São Tomé/Sumé;
- oral narration in Paraguay, Misiones, Corrientes, and Brazil;
- Catholic reinterpretation through crosses, chapels, processions, and apostolic memory.

### What is not established

- dedicated pre-contact temples;
- an exclusive priesthood;
- calendrical festivals specifically for Sumé;
- animal or human sacrifice to Sumé;
- temple prostitution;
- divine images receiving offerings;
- theophoric names proving a widespread cult;
- an uninterrupted Indigenous liturgy.

The lack of such evidence is not proof that no ritual remembrance ever occurred. It means the surviving record documents a legend and sacred geography much more clearly than a cult.

### Jesuit reductions

The reductions did not preserve an autonomous Sumé priesthood. Jesuit authors used the Saint Thomas identification to legitimate Christian mission, translate religious authority, and interpret Indigenous paths and memories. Their records arose in conditions of epidemic disease, forced relocation, colonial labor demands, bandeirante slave raids, military conflict, and campaigns against Indigenous ritual specialists.

Missionary appropriation could protect certain Indigenous communities from enslavement while simultaneously suppressing religious autonomy. Both are part of the record.

### Misiones and Corrientes

Ambrosetti’s late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century collections show a regional landscape dense with saints’ marks, animated stones, caves, spirits, and Guaraní-Portuguese-Spanish traditions. His material includes the “hands and feet of the saint” at Santo Pipó. This supports continued footprint folklore, but not a surviving temple cult of Sumé.

The large collections of Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini are essential controls for Argentine folklore. They demonstrate how narrators localize and continually refashion legendary motifs. They should not be mined as if every twentieth-century story were an unchanged pre-Columbian myth.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before European documentation

No securely datable pre-contact text, inscription, or image names Sumé. A pre-existing Indigenous culture-hero or landscape-marking tradition is plausible and supported indirectly by its early breadth, but its precise content cannot be reconstructed independently of colonial mediation.

### 1515–1549: formation of the documentary problem

Early European reports begin placing America within apostolic universal history. By 1543 *Payçumé* is recorded as an honorific applied to a Franciscan in the upper Paraguay. In 1549 Nóbrega explicitly records Zomé/Sumé traditions among coastal Tupinambá and identifies them with Thomas.

### 1550s–1590s: expansion across Tupi–Guaraní contact zones

Thevet develops Sumé as a Tupinambá civilizer. Reports in Paraguay associate a related figure with missionaries and apostolic memory. Footprints and useful arts become the chief evidentiary motifs.

### 1600–1650: Jesuit synthesis

Montoya transforms scattered reports into a connected apostolic geography extending from Brazil through Paraguay toward Peru. Sumé becomes the apostle who opened roads, preached, left footprints, and promised Jesuit successors.

This was the figure’s greatest political utility: it naturalized the reductions as completion of an ancient prophecy.

### Later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

Catholic chroniclers elaborate the relics, paths, crosses, and continental itinerary. Rock marks are absorbed into Catholic sacred geography. The story circulates alongside other American “apostle before conquest” legends.

### Nineteenth century

Regional antiquarians, naturalists, clergy, and folklorists record footprint sites and local variants. Romantic nationalism increasingly treats Sumé as an Indigenous civilizer, sometimes while retaining the Saint Thomas equation.

### 1914–1975: ethnographic differentiation

Nimuendajú, Métraux, Cadogan, Schaden, Susnik, Melià, and the Clastres provide much richer accounts of specific Tupi–Guaraní peoples. Their work makes clear that there is no single, uniform “Guaraní pantheon.” Sumé belongs strongly to the colonial-contact archive, while Mbyá and Apapocúva cosmologies center other named beings and narrative structures.

### 1929 onward: Colmán’s national mythology

*Ñande Ypy Kuéra* gives Paraguay a coherent literary genealogy and an organized seven-monster system. Schoolbooks, tourism, popular compilations, and internet summaries subsequently turn this literary synthesis into supposed ancient canon. Tumé Arandú is often retrospectively equated with Pa'i Sumé.

### Late twentieth century to present

Pa'i Sumé persists in folklore, regional history, popular music, tourism, Peabiru promotion, alternative archaeology, and online mythology. The legend remains culturally alive, but there is no well-documented, numerically assessable reconstructionist cult centered on him.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### National and educational mythology

The largest modern transformation is Colmán’s. By organizing Guaraní figures into a single family and moral cosmic history, he produced a teachable pantheon suited to national literature. Its descendants appear in Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine school materials, tourism, festivals, sculpture, and commercial illustration.

### Peabiru tourism

Modern heritage projects promote the Caminho de Peabiru as Sumé’s road. The connection has colonial precedent in Montoya, but contemporary presentations often overstate certainty, treating a network of Indigenous paths as a single apostolic highway.

### Pedra do Ingá and alternative archaeology

Brazilian popular literature sometimes attributes the Pedra do Ingá carvings to Sumé or links them to the biblical Flood, Phoenicians, Hebrews, Atlantis, or extraterrestrials. Archaeology has not identified the inscriptions as writing by Sumé or evidence of a Christian apostle.

### Music

Zé Ramalho’s “Segredos de Sumé” helped keep the name visible in Brazilian popular culture and connects Sumé with the mystery of northeastern rock inscriptions. This is modern artistic reception, not ancient testimony.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found scattered esoteric and New Age uses but no historically continuous occult order, grimoire tradition, Golden Dawn lineage, or established premodern magical cult of Sumé. Internet claims that present him as an Atlantean master, extraterrestrial, white initiate, or member of a universal brotherhood are modern inventions.

Likewise, individual neo-Indigenous or neopagan invocations may occur, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes a substantial organized Sumé reconstructionist religion.

### Political uses

The Saint Thomas identification historically served colonial Christian claims: America had already been touched by apostolic revelation, so missionary conversion restored a forgotten truth. Later nationalism reversed the emphasis, celebrating Sumé as an Indigenous or Paraguayan civilizer. Both uses select different layers of the same composite.

No well-documented modern extremist movement centered specifically on Pa'i Sumé emerged from the searched record. “White god” versions can nevertheless reproduce racial and diffusionist assumptions by denying Indigenous societies authorship of agriculture, roads, or rock art.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was there a pre-contact Sumé?

The early geographical spread and the existence of Indigenous teacher and footprint motifs make a pre-existing tradition plausible. But every surviving written witness is post-contact, and the earliest observers were already seeking Saint Thomas. Scholarship therefore ranges from “Indigenous culture hero appropriated by missionaries” to a more cautious model of mutual translation and colonial co-production.

### Was Sumé Saint Thomas?

No historical evidence places the first-century apostle in South America. The identification rests on:

- phonetic resemblance between *Sumé* and *Tomé*;
- apostolic universality traditions;
- the Christian cult of Saint Thomas in India;
- footprint relics;
- missionary interpretation of Indigenous narratives.

These materials document belief in the identification, not the apostle’s journey.

### Did Sumé teach agriculture and yerba mate?

Agricultural/food teaching is early in the Tupinambá record. Specific claims about maize, all social institutions, or yerba mate belong to later regional elaboration. The safest reconstruction is that a general culture-giver motif expanded as it moved between regions and missionary genres.

### Is Pa'i Rete Kuaray another name for Sumé?

No primary Mbyá source establishes this. The comparison arises from functional resemblance: both can be ancestral teachers and culture heroes, and both bear *Pa'i*. Their names, relations, and narratives differ. Equating them erases Mbyá specificity and imports the colonial Thomas complex into a separate sacred corpus.

### Is Tumé Arandú ancient?

The name may reuse Sumé/Tumé and the widespread *arandu* “wisdom” vocabulary, but the genealogy familiar today is fixed in Colmán’s 1929 poem. It is literary systematization, not securely ancient canon.

### Sumé and the “White King” or Paititi

Combès proposes that the “Rey Blanco” sought by early explorers may reflect **Pai ti**, “white pai,” and connect with Pai Sumé and Paititi. She labels this a hypothesis built from routes, terminology, and colonial reports. It is stimulating but not a directly attested etymology.

### Human sacrifice, violence, and suppression

No source documents sacrifice to Sumé. Porãsy’s self-sacrifice belongs to Colmán’s literary monster cycle. Tupinambá warfare and ritual anthropophagy are documented in the broader colonial corpus, but they are not a Sumé cult ritual.

Missionary violence appears more structurally: Indigenous religions were classified as error or demonic practice; ritual specialists were displaced; communities were reorganized in reductions; and Christian readings appropriated Indigenous places and memories. Simultaneously, Jesuits opposed settler enslavement and suffered attacks from bandeirantes. The history is not adequately described as either simple preservation or simple destruction.

### State of the evidence

The primary record survives chiefly in:

- printed Jesuit letters and chronicles;
- Montoya’s 1639–1640 publications;
- European travel narratives;
- ecclesiastical chronicles;
- archives such as the Archivo General de Indias;
- nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore collections;
- ethnographic recordings and texts.

There is no Sumé inscription, Indigenous manuscript, securely identified ancient statue, or excavated temple archive.

## 10. Chronology

- **Pre-contact period:** possible Indigenous teacher, prophet, and footprint traditions; no securely datable named evidence.
- **c. 1515:** *Newen Zeytung auss Presillg Landt* contributes to early European reports later associated with American Saint Thomas traditions; not a secure named Sumé attestation.
- **1543:** Pedro de Orantes reports Indigenous companions calling a Franciscan **Payçumé**; earliest relevant expression, but not unambiguously the mythical being.
- **15 April and August 1549:** Nóbrega’s letters from Brazil record Zomé/Sumé, Saint Thomas, rock footprints, and useful foods among coastal Tupinambá.
- **1557:** Thevet’s *Singularitez* develops Brazilian Indigenous religious and culture-hero material.
- **1575:** Thevet’s *Cosmographie universelle* gives a fuller Sumé civilizer narrative.
- **1588:** Martín González records relevant tradition in Paraguay.
- **1594:** Alonso de Barzana records it among Cario/Guaraní speakers near Asunción.
- **Early seventeenth century:** Jesuit annual letters circulate accounts of Pay Zumé, footprints, prophecy, and missionary reception.
- **1639:** Montoya publishes *Conquista espiritual* and *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* in Madrid, producing the principal Paraguay–Brazil–Peru Saint Thomas synthesis.
- **Seventeenth century:** Calancha and other Catholic chroniclers extend the apostolic itinerary and etymological argument.
- **Eighteenth century:** footprint stones and associated springs remain objects of Catholic-local memory; some stones are removed or reused.
- **Late nineteenth century:** regional antiquarians and folklorists record saintly footprints and related legends in Brazil, Paraguay, Misiones, and Corrientes.
- **1914:** Nimuendajú publishes the Apapocúva creation/destruction corpus; it does not establish Sumé as their central god.
- **1928:** Métraux publishes *La religion des Tupinamba*, critically assembling the colonial corpus.
- **1929:** Colmán publishes *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* in Guaraní, systematizing Tumé Arandú and the seven-monster genealogy.
- **1937:** Colmán publishes his Spanish prose version.
- **1944:** Egon Schaden publishes “O mito do Sumé.”
- **1959:** Cadogan publishes *Ayvu Rapyta*, documenting Mbyá cosmology and Pa'i Rete Kuaray without securely identifying him as Sumé.
- **1971:** Cadogan publishes *Ywyra Ñe'ery*.
- **1974:** Pierre Clastres publishes *Le Grand Parler*.
- **1975:** Hélène Clastres publishes *La Terre sans Mal*.
- **1980–1995:** Vidal de Battini’s ten-volume Argentine folklore corpus preserves thousands of regional oral narratives and provides comparative context.
- **2008:** Cavalcante publishes a major study of cultural circulation and the Sumé–Saint Thomas identification.
- **2011:** Combès publishes “Pai Sumé, el Rey Blanco y el Paititi,” reopening questions about early Paraguayan terminology, routes, and the White King.
- **2020s:** Sumé circulates through Peabiru heritage projects, Paraguayan mythology sites, music, alternative archaeology, and online neopagan/esoteric retellings; no substantial continuous cult is documented.

### Principal findings

The most defensible reconstruction is deliberately untidy. An early Tupinambá Sumé tradition is documented, but only through post-contact Christian writers. A related *Payçumé* expression circulated among Guaraní speakers, sometimes as a title for missionaries. Jesuits converted these materials into a continental Saint Thomas history. Paraguayan folklore later amplified Sumé’s instruction in agriculture and yerba mate. Colmán then supplied a genealogy and mythic system that many popular sources mistakenly project backward. Cadogan’s Pa'i Rete Kuaray belongs to a distinct Mbyá corpus and should remain distinct unless an Indigenous source explicitly makes the equation.
