# Ñamandu (Ñande Ru Pa-pa Tenonde): Research Dossier

**Class:** primordial divine father, self-manifesting creator, source of sacred speech and principal deity in the recorded theology of the Mbyá-Guaraní of Guairá. “God” is a serviceable English classification, but Mbyá titles more precisely make him a *ru ete*, “true father,” rather than a member of a universally shared “Guaraní pantheon.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** the name and developed theology of **Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua** are securely documented only in twentieth-century Mbyá oral texts recorded by León Cadogan in Paraguay. Chapters I–II appeared in 1953, chapter III in 1954, and the complete bilingual corpus as *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá* in 1959. Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s seventeenth-century Guaraní dictionary and mission chronicle are earlier evidence for Guaraní language and religion but do **not securely attest this named Mbyá creator**. The distinction is fundamental.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Securely attested Mbyá]** Recorded in Mbyá Guaraní from identified Mbyá authorities and printed by Cadogan.
- **[Colonial evidence]** Seventeenth- or eighteenth-century missionary documentation; valuable but produced within evangelization.
- **[Regional variant]** Material belonging to another Guaraní-speaking people—Apapocúva/Nhandéva, Kaiowá/Paĩ-Tavyterã, Chiripá, or colonial Guaraní—and not automatically transferable to Mbyá theology.
- **[Ethnographic elaboration]** Later interpretation or synthesis by an ethnographer.
- **[Syncretistic]** Indigenous-Christian convergence, borrowing, translation, or contested identification.
- **[Literary systematization]** An authored arrangement presented in literary form rather than a direct transcription of one Indigenous oral canon.
- **[Folklore]** Paraguayan, Correntine, or Misionero popular tradition, often culturally Guaraní but not necessarily Mbyá sacred teaching.
- **[Modern adaptation]** Recent literature, theater, art, spirituality, or popular mythology.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

In Cadogan’s principal Mbyá creation text, Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua manifests his own divine body amid primordial darkness. Before sun, earth, or ordinary time exist, his heart’s wisdom illuminates him. He produces the foundations of sacred speech, love or reciprocity, creative wisdom, and the hymnic language through which human beings will participate in divinity. He then manifests other divine fathers and their feminine counterparts and assigns them differentiated cosmological responsibilities. **[Securely attested Mbyá: *Ayvu Rapyta* I–III, recorded c. 1940s–1950s; published 1953–1959.]**

This is not demonstrably the single prehistoric religion of every Guaraní-speaking people. It is a particularly rich Mbyá theology whose family resemblance to other Guaraní traditions must not erase differences between them.

### Names and titles

### The Pa-pa problem

Cadogan’s earlier abbreviated version presented **Ñande Ru Pa-pa Tenonde** as creator of Ñamandu Ru Ete. His fuller version, dictated by Cacique Pablo Vera and Mayor Francisco, did not: it made the primordial Ñamandu create **Ñamandu Py’a Guachu**. When Cadogan asked about the discrepancy, consultants reportedly told him that Pa-pa Tenonde was a Christian designation. Che’iro said, in substance, that *Pa-pa* was good for the *jurua*—non-Indigenous Christians—but not for Mbyá, “for whom Ñamandu is the first.” Cristino used the formula “Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua, whom you call Pa-pa Tenonde.” Cadogan therefore concluded that the two titles were used interchangeably in his later material but that *Pa-pa*’s genuinely Mbyá origin could not be asserted. **[Securely documented indigenous disagreement; *Ayvu Rapyta*, Cadogan’s note to chapter II.]**

This prevents three common but unsupported harmonizations:

1. Pa-pa Tenonde cannot safely be made an eternally separate super-god above Ñamandu.
2. Nor can the title’s pre-Christian antiquity be assumed.
3. Its use by twentieth-century Mbyá was nevertheless real and must not be dismissed merely because Christian influence is possible.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial record: what it establishes and what it does not

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* was printed in Madrid in 1639. His *Conquista espiritual*, also printed in 1639, recounts Jesuit expansion among Guaraní communities principally between 1610 and 1637. These are indispensable records of classical missionary Guaraní, Indigenous religious specialists, Christian translation, colonial violence, bandeirante enslavement, and the reductions. They do not provide secure evidence for a seventeenth-century being named Ñamandu with the full *Ayvu Rapyta* theology. **[Colonial evidence; significant absence.]**

Montoya wrote as an evangelist defending the mission enterprise. His categories—“sorcerers,” “magicians,” “devil,” “superstition”—cannot be treated as neutral transcriptions of Indigenous classifications. The mission record shows attempts to displace or subordinate Indigenous ritual authorities, together with Indigenous opposition, negotiation, selective conversion, and survival.

The absence of Ñamandu from Montoya does not prove that the name or conception did not then exist. It establishes only that no secure named attestation has been demonstrated in this major surviving colonial corpus.

### Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva corpus

Curt Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní*, published in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914), pp. 284–403, inaugurated modern documentary study of Guaraní religion. It records the Apapocúva creator **Ñanderuvuçú**, his brother **Ñanderýke’y**, creation and destruction of the world, sacred names, souls, migration, and expectations concerning the Land Without Evil. **[Securely attested Apapocúva regional variant.]**

Ñanderuvuçú creates the earth upon crossed wooden supports. Ñanderýke’y later pulls the eastern end, initiating the earth’s collapse; fire, flood, and a celestial blue jaguar belong to the expected catastrophe. These are not episodes in which Cadogan’s Ñamandu may simply be substituted for Ñanderuvuçú. Comparison is warranted; identity is not.

Nimuendajú’s work was based on residence with Apapocúva communities and appeared in German in 1914; Charlotte Emmerich and Eduardo Viveiros de Castro’s Portuguese translation appeared in 1987.

### *Ayvu Rapyta*

Cadogan described the collection as literal transcriptions of Mbyá dictation with Guaraní text and Spanish translation. He named principal contributors:

- Cacique Pablo Vera of Yro’ysã/Potrero Blanco;
- Kachirito of Paso Jovái;
- Cacique Che’iro of the Upper Monday;
- Mayor Francisco (“Chico’i”) of Tava’i;
- Tomás and Cirilo of Yvytuko;
- Higinio and Mario Higinio;
- other named and unnamed Mbyá authorities.

The collection is therefore neither an anonymous ancient manuscript nor simply Cadogan’s invented scripture. It is a twentieth-century textualization of living, guarded oral knowledge, mediated by selection, dictation, translation, orthography, explanation, and editorial arrangement.

Its principal Ñamandu materials are:

- **Chapter I, *Maino’i reko ypykue*, “The primordial customs of the hummingbird.”** Ñamandu manifests his body in primordial darkness; the hummingbird nourishes or refreshes him; his own heart’s wisdom is his light. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**
- **Chapter II, *Ayvu Rapyta*, “The foundation of human speech.”** Before the earth, Ñamandu generates flames and fine mist, creates the foundation of *ayvu*, love/reciprocity, and hymnic language, and manifests the divine fathers and mothers. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**
- **Chapter III, commonly titled “The First Earth.”** Ñamandu and the associated divine fathers establish the first earth, its supports, plants, animals, divisions, and divine responsibilities. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**
- Later chapters contain prayers, moral precepts, accounts of sacred knowledge, healers, animals, death, and the relation between human word-souls and divine regions. Ñamandu often appears liturgically rather than as the protagonist of a continuous biography.

The complete 1959 edition appeared in São Paulo as volume 5 of the University of São Paulo’s *Boletim da Faculdade de Filosofia, Ciências e Letras*, Anthropology series. Later Paraguayan editions altered typography and accessibility but did not convert the oral corpus into an ancient codex.

### *Ywyra Ñe’ery*

Cadogan’s *Ywyra Ñe’ery: fluye del árbol la palabra* appeared in 1971. It supplements *Ayvu Rapyta* with Mbyá narratives, ritual vocabulary, ethnographic interpretation, and further evidence for a world in which word, plant growth, breath, smoke, and personhood interpenetrate. **[Ethnographic continuation.]**

### Later translations and presentations

- Pierre Clastres, *Le Grand Parler: mythes et chants sacrés des Indiens Guarani* (1974), offered a French literary-philosophical presentation of Guaraní sacred speech. Its influence has sometimes encouraged readers to treat “Guaraní thought” as more uniform than the ethnographic record permits.
- Hélène Clastres, *La Terre sans mal: le prophétisme tupi-guarani* (1975), interpreted Tupi-Guaraní migration and the Land Without Evil as a major historical-religious formation. Her synthesis is influential but debated, especially where widely separated periods and peoples are drawn into one theory.
- Bartomeu Melià emphasized that Guaraní religion is centrally enacted as word: *ayvu* as word-soul, prayer and song as religious action, and history as the history of Indigenous speech.
- Kaká Werá Jecupé’s *Tupã Tenondé* (2001) is an Indigenous-authored modern literary and spiritual presentation in Portuguese and Guaraní. Its title and synthesis belong to its own modern context and should not be retrojected as the original title or hierarchy of Cadogan’s corpus.
- A new annotated Portuguese translation, *Ayvu Rapyta: cosmopoética guarani mbyá*, was reported in preparation for publication in 2026. Recent philological work re-examines Cadogan’s segmentation, word choices, and the assumption that his Spanish is a transparent equivalent of the Guaraní.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

**Self-manifestation.** Ñamandu does not have an ordinary birth. His feet, seat, sensory faculties, hands, staff, flowering fingers, crown, and consciousness unfold in primordial darkness. Cadogan connects the verb forms to opening or developing, as a flower opens. Describing this as “creation from nothing” imports a foreign philosophical category; “self-unfolding” or “self-manifestation” better follows the verbal imagery. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**

**Primordial light.** Before the sun exists, he is illuminated by the reflected wisdom within his own heart. The text does not say merely that he owns the sun; wisdom functions as primordial luminosity. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**

**Flame and mist.** From a portion of his divinity and creative knowledge he causes **tataendy**, flames or fiery radiance, and **tatachina**, fine or sacred mist, to arise. These precede the ordered earth. They are generative conditions of sacred speech, not a scientifically described material chaos. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**

**Speech and personhood.** Ñamandu conceives *ayvu rapyta*, the foundation of human language or the word-soul, before creating the earth. The first divine fathers later distribute word-souls to human beings. *Ayvu* cannot adequately be reduced either to “language” or to a separable Western “soul”: in the corpus it marks animated, speaking personhood. **[Securely attested Mbyá; translation debated.]**

**Love and sociality.** He establishes *mborayu rapyta*, the foundation of love, reciprocity, or proper mutual relation. Christian “agape” is possible as a later comparative gloss but not a secure original definition. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**

**Creative wisdom.** *Kuaa-ra-ra* or related forms denote generative knowledge, illumination, and the capacity to unfold beings. Cadogan’s “sabiduría creadora” remains an interpretation of difficult sacred vocabulary.

**Delegated order.** Ñamandu assigns sacred fire to Karai, life-giving mist to Jakaira, and water or cosmic refreshment to Tupã. His sovereignty is therefore not the monopolization of every force but the generation and differentiation of divine agencies.

**Creation and possible destruction.** Contemporary Mbyá accounts describe the earth as supported by a cross or crossed framework of divine wisdom; when irreparably disordered, Ñamandu may withdraw its support and the earth collapse. This resembles, without simply duplicating, Apapocúva cataclysmology. **[Living Mbyá teaching/regional comparison.]**

### Form and insignia

Cadogan’s first chapter gives a verbal image:

- divine soles or roots of the feet;
- a small round seat, *apyka apua’i*;
- all-seeing wisdom and all-hearing faculty;
- divine palms;
- a **yvyra’i**, sacred staff or insignia;
- flowering branches as fingers and nails;
- a lofty crown or feather ornament, wet with dew;
- radiant heart-wisdom.

This is embodied imagery, but no ancient carved, painted, or archaeological representation can securely be identified as Ñamandu. **There is no established ancient Ñamandu iconographic canon.**

Modern illustrations depicting an elderly bearded creator, a solar king, or a figure resembling the Christian God are **modern adaptations** unless tied to a specific Indigenous artist’s declared representation. The hummingbird, staff, feather crown, flame, mist, round seat, palms, and heart-light are textually grounded motifs; a fixed human physiognomy is not.

### Animals, plants, substances, and directions

- **Primordial hummingbird, Maino’i:** refreshes Ñamandu’s mouth and supplies paradisal nourishment while the first father manifests himself. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**
- **Owl, Urukure’a:** in one passage makes or maintains darkness/rest around the primordial father. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**
- **Sacred mist/smoke:** associated especially with Jakaira, inspired words, ritual breath, and tobacco smoke.
- **Fire/radiance:** generated primordially by Ñamandu but entrusted to Karai.
- **Water, rain, thunder, cooling:** entrusted to Tupã.
- **Eternal palms:** establish and support the first earth at its center and margins in the creation cycle.
- **Cedar water, maize, yerba mate, tobacco, maraca and ritual staff:** important in living Mbyá practice, although not all are exclusive attributes of Ñamandu.

No securely attested exclusive sacred color, weekday, planet, numerical cult, weapon, or sacrificial animal belongs to Ñamandu.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Origin and hierarchy

Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua has no securely attested parents. He manifests himself from primordial darkness through his own divine wisdom. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**

He is not merely one equal member of a four-god pantheon. The fuller *Ayvu Rapyta* sequence makes him the prior source who manifests the other divine fathers. The resulting order nevertheless has multiple divine houses and agencies rather than an exclusively acting creator.

### The four divine pairs

After establishing speech, love, and wisdom, the first father produces beings who will be companions in divinity and true fathers and mothers of future word-souls:

1. **Ñamandu Ru Ete / Ñamandu Py’a Guachu** and **Ñamandu Chy Ete**;
2. **Karai Ru Ete** and **Karai Chy Ete**;
3. **Jakaira Ru Ete** and **Jakaira Chy Ete**;
4. **Tupã Ru Ete** and **Tupã Chy Ete**.

The source’s grammar and repetitions have generated ambiguity over whether the first member is the creator himself in paired form, a distinct emanated Ñamandu, or a collectivity of “Ñamandus of great heart.” Cadogan’s fuller version says the primordial father created Ñamandu Py’a Guachu; popular summaries often erase this distinction. **[Securely attested ambiguity.]**

The feminine beings are not developed as narrative characters to the same degree as the fathers. The corpus nevertheless explicitly includes them as **true mothers of the word-souls**. Omitting them creates a misleadingly all-male pantheon.

### Consort

**Ñamandu Chy Ete**, the “true mother Ñamandu,” is the corresponding feminine source in the divine pairing. She is best understood from the paired cosmological structure. The corpus does not supply an independent biography comparable to later European goddess narratives. **[Securely attested Mbyá.]**

Popular internet narratives sometimes combine this figure with a wife in the Sun-and-Moon twin cycle, whose pregnancy or supposed infidelity causes a divine husband to depart. The identity of the husband varies in Guaraní narratives; the episode should not automatically be assigned to the self-created Ñamandu of chapters I–III.

### Offspring

Humans are not presented simply as biological children. Divine fathers and mothers send or distribute *ñe’ẽ/ayvu*, word-souls, which take human form. Persons are thus “children” through sacred speech and origin from a divine region. A child’s sacred name reveals the provenance and character of its *ñe’ẽ*. **[Securely attested Mbyá and continuing practice.]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Self-manifestation in primordial darkness

**Earliest source:** *Ayvu Rapyta* chapter I, recorded in the mid-twentieth century and published completely in 1959.

The first father begins in *pytũ yma*, primordial darkness. He unfolds the divine soles of his feet, his round seat, seeing-wisdom, hearing, hands, staff, flowering fingers, and feather crown. Dew rests on the crown’s flowers; Maino’i moves among them. Before a future heaven or earth exists, the hummingbird refreshes his mouth.

Although the sun does not yet exist, Ñamandu does not experience darkness: the wisdom reflected from his own heart serves as his sun. The episode is best read as a song of progressive manifestation, not a chronicle of anatomical assembly.

**Variants and development:** Modern summaries often say Ñamandu “created himself from chaos.” That is broadly faithful but less precise than the Guaraní imagery of unfolding. Comparisons to a growing tree derive from feet as roots, arms and fingers as flowering branches, and vertical emergence; they are illuminating interpretations, not a separate sentence in the myth declaring him a tree.

### B. Creation of flame, mist, and the word-soul

**Earliest source:** *Ayvu Rapyta* chapter II, partially published 1953 and in full in 1959.

From a small part of his divinity and its indwelling wisdom, Ñamandu causes primordial flames and fine mist to arise. He then creates:

- the foundation of human language, *ayvu rapyta*;
- its placement within his own divinity;
- the foundation of love or reciprocity, *mborayu rapyta*;
- the source of hymnic or inspired words;
- the creative wisdom by which future beings will receive consciousness.

All this precedes earth. The sequence reverses any assumption that language is a late human tool: human speaking-personhood exists first as a divine possibility.

The divine fathers and mothers will send their word-soul children to earth. This remains ritually consequential in Mbyá naming: the religious specialist discerns a child’s sacred name and divine provenance.

### C. Manifestation of the divine companions

Ñamandu reflects within himself and manifests Ñamandu Py’a Guachu, Karai Ru Ete, Jakaira Ru Ete, and Tupã Ru Ete, together with their true mothers. He gives them consciousness of divinity and names them fathers and mothers of future word-souls.

This is sometimes rendered “Ñamandu created four gods and their wives.” That communicates the arithmetic but loses the relational language of true fathers, true mothers, companions, divine portions, and word-souls.

### D. Ordering the first earth

In the third creation movement the first earth, **Yvy Tenonde**, unfolds. Eternal palms and a central support hold it. Ñamandu creates or orders vegetation and animals and allocates cosmic maintenance among the divine fathers:

- Karai’s line bears sacred flame and heat;
- Jakaira’s line bears living mist, smoke, medicine, and inspired speech;
- Tupã’s line bears waters, rain, thunder, cooling, and moderation;
- Ñamandu’s line bears radiant wisdom and sacred speech.

The world is not finished once and for all. Its continued existence depends on correct relations among divine powers, humans, speech, ritual, and the nonhuman environment.

### E. The first earth’s destruction and later earth

Guaraní traditions widely contain destruction cycles, but the versions differ. Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva account includes collapsing earth supports, subterranean fire, flood, and a celestial blue jaguar. Mbyá accounts include destruction or removal of an earlier earth and the precarious support of the present one.

A composite story in which “Ñamandu creates earth, his brother pulls out its cross, Tupã floods it, and a blue jaguar finishes humanity” is not one primary text. It is a fusion of related but distinct Guaraní corpora. **[Regional variants must remain separate.]**

### F. Land Without Evil

The search for **Yvy Marã E’ỹ**, conventionally “Land Without Evil,” is central to scholarship on Guaraní religion. Its meanings include an imperishable land, a place without decay, a divine destination, and a condition approached through ritual discipline. Nimuendajú documented Apapocúva migrations and eschatology; Hélène Clastres interpreted earlier Tupi-Guaraní prophetic movements through this framework.

Ñamandu is relevant to Mbyá divine geography and imperishable abodes, but no evidence supports making him the sole originator of every historical “Land Without Evil” movement from coastal sixteenth-century Tupinambá through twentieth-century Mbyá.

## 6. Cult, prayer houses, and ritual practice

### No temple-state cult

There is no evidence of monumental stone temples, centralized priestly estates, royal patronage, temple prostitution, dedicated armies, coinage, or a state cult of Ñamandu. Archaeological remains cannot presently be assigned to a named Ñamandu cult. Claims modeled on Mediterranean or Mesoamerican temple systems are unsupported.

### The *opy*

Living Mbyá religion centers on the **opy** or **opy’i**, the prayer house. It is a communal ritual building rather than a sculptural temple housing a fixed idol. Daily or periodic *ñembo’e jeroky* combines prayer, chant, dance, maracas, ritual staffs, tobacco smoke, and instruction.

The *opygua*, *karaí*, or other recognized ritual leader directs prayer, healing, naming, and the interpretation of sacred words. Terminology and authority vary by community. “Shaman” is a comparative scholarly label, not a Guaraní office-name.

### Prayer

Ñamandu is directly invoked in morning and communal prayers as **Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua**. Prayer is not merely petition. It renews the “beautiful words,” aligns human speech with divine speech, and helps maintain the world’s order.

### *Nhemongarai*

The *nhemongarai/ñemongarai* is a consecration and naming complex associated, according to community and occasion, with children, maize, yerba mate, or cedar water. A child’s sacred name reveals the *ñe’ẽ*—name-spirit or word-soul—which has come from one of the divine regions. The rite remains practiced in Mbyá communities in Brazil, Paraguay, and Argentina. **[Living Indigenous tradition.]**

It should not be translated simply as Christian baptism, despite historical comparison and mutual influence. Its theory of personhood is tied to the divine provenance of speech.

### Offerings and ritual substances

Documented practice emphasizes:

- sung prayer and dance;
- tobacco smoke and breath;
- maize and maize beer in some communities;
- yerba mate;
- cedar water;
- feathers and bodily adornment;
- maracas and ritual staffs;
- communal discipline and ethical conduct.

No reliable evidence establishes animal or human sacrifice **to Ñamandu**. Colonial sources discuss warfare, captive killing, cannibalism, and missionaries’ allegations about “sorcery” among different Tupi-Guaraní peoples. None may be converted into evidence for a sacrificial cult of this named Mbyá deity.

### Images

Ñamandu is principally addressed through word, chant, smoke, gesture, and divine orientation. No cult statue is required by the recorded ritual system. Consequently, searching for “temples and images of Ñamandu” using Old World expectations produces a false absence; the material record is the prayer house, ritual instrument, cultivated plant, body, voice, and landscape.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before European documentation

A pre-contact history of the specific name Ñamandu cannot presently be reconstructed. Tupi-Guaraní linguistic history, archaeology, and early chronicles establish long Indigenous occupation, migration, cultivation, ritual leadership, and diverse creator traditions. They do not securely date the full *Ayvu Rapyta* theology.

### Sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries

European chroniclers encountered Tupi-Guaraní-speaking peoples with prophets, ritual specialists, powerful speech traditions, ancestor and spirit concepts, migrations, and differentiated cosmologies. Observers frequently claimed that Indigenous people lacked “religion” because they saw no idols or monumental temples. Hélène Clastres showed that these reports reveal European expectations more clearly than Indigenous absence of religious life. **[Colonial evidence and modern reinterpretation.]**

### Jesuit reductions, c. 1609–1768

Jesuits established reductions across Paraguay, Paraná, Guairá, Uruguay, and Tape. Missionaries translated Christian concepts into Guaraní, promoted **Tupã** and **Ñandejára** as terms for God, attacked or demonized rival religious specialists, regulated marriage and ritual, destroyed or prohibited practices they judged incompatible with Christianity, and reorganized settlement and labor.

Indigenous leaders resisted, negotiated, appropriated Christian language, or maintained teachings outside mission control. The reductions also offered partial protection from encomienda and enslavement, while imposing extensive religious and social discipline. Portuguese bandeirante raids devastated Guairá; Montoya’s account was written partly to secure royal protection.

Nothing in this history permits a simple conclusion that Jesuits “invented Ñamandu.” What can be demonstrated is that creator terminology and Guaraní sacred vocabulary existed in a multilingual field profoundly altered by Christian translation.

### Nineteenth century

After Jesuit expulsion and the collapse or secularization of the reductions, Mbyá and other Guaraní peoples continued religious life in dispersed communities. National territorial expansion, logging, land seizure, war, and missionary activity constrained transmission. Written documentation remained sparse and externally controlled.

### 1905–1914: Nimuendajú and the Apapocúva

Nimuendajú’s residence among the Apapocúva and his 1914 publication established that Guaraní religion possessed complex creation, soul, naming, eschatological, and poetic systems independent of missionary summaries. It also provided the principal comparison for Cadogan’s later work.

### 1929: Colmán’s literary pantheon

Narciso R. Colmán published the Guaraní poem *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (“Our Ancestors”) in 1929. Its Tau-and-Kerana genealogy and seven monstrous children became enormously influential in Paraguayan popular mythology.

That scheme is **not the Mbyá divine family of *Ayvu Rapyta***. It is an authored literary systematization using Guaraní folklore, language, and creative arrangement. Treating Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’i, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupí, Ao Ao, and Luisón as Ñamandu’s canonical pantheon is a modern conflation.

### 1940s–1959: Cadogan’s documentation

Cadogan gained unusual access to guarded Mbyá teachings, in part through long personal relationships and advocacy for Indigenous communities. He recorded the creation chants and printed them first in sections and then in the 1959 volume. This is the first secure documentary appearance of the developed Ñamandu theology.

The publication was simultaneously preservation and transformation: restricted oral speech became a movable printed text available to scholars, writers, schools, and non-Indigenous spiritual readers.

### 1960s–1990s: comparative and philosophical reception

Egon Schaden compared Mbyá, Ñandeva, and Kaiowá culture and religion, emphasizing both continuities and acculturation. Cadogan’s *Ywyra Ñe’ery* appeared in 1971. Pierre and Hélène Clastres made Guaraní sacred speech and the Land Without Evil central to Francophone anthropology. Melià re-centered Guaraní language, *teko*, *tekoha*, and word-soul as historical and religious realities.

The risk of this period’s scholarship is abstraction: “the Guaraní” could become a timeless philosophical subject detached from named communities, land loss, and variant traditions.

### Twenty-first century

Mbyá communities continue *opy* worship, sacred naming, sung prayer, cultivation rites, and oral teaching across Paraguay, Argentina, and Brazil. Contemporary accounts still invoke Ñamandu. This is continuity within active social change, not a neopagan reconstruction of an extinct religion.

Indigenous writers, educators, performers, filmmakers, and organizations increasingly mediate their own traditions. At the same time, popular mythology websites often fuse Mbyá creation theology, Colmán’s seven monsters, Apapocúva cataclysm, Catholic vocabulary, and New Age language into a single invented “Guaraní pantheon.”

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and translation

Cadogan’s Spanish translation made Ñamandu legible to Latin American literature as a metaphysical creator whose first act is language. Pierre Clastres’ French presentation strengthened this philosophical reception. Josely Vianna Baptista’s *Roça Barroca* and recent “cosmopoetic” translations foreground the Guaraní verbal texture and question smooth Western equivalents.

Kaká Werá Jecupé’s *Tupã Tenondé* (2001) is a consequential Indigenous literary-spiritual re-presentation. It should be read as a modern authored work in dialogue with oral tradition, Cadogan, and contemporary Indigenous politics—not as a verbatim pre-Columbian scripture.

### Theater and visual art

The Paraguayan stage production **Ñamandu: Soplo divino**, performed in Asunción in 2023, placed the creator in contemporary performing arts. Such productions materially shape public imagery because no ancient standardized portrait exists. **[Modern adaptation.]**

Recent illustrated and graphic adaptations depict Ñamandu, the animals, and his staff. Their iconography is authored visual interpretation rather than recovery of an archaeological canon.

### National and intercultural use

Ñamandu increasingly appears in Paraguayan and regional cultural writing as a symbol of Guaraní language, ecological balance, sacred speech, and resistance to cultural disappearance. This can support Indigenous recognition, but it can also nationalize a specifically Mbyá teaching while marginalizing living Mbyá ownership and diversity.

### New Age, occult, and neopagan claims

The dedicated search found no documented Ñamandu current in Renaissance Hermeticism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, established grimoire traditions, or a historically organized pre-twentieth-century occult order. Claims assigning him planetary seals, elemental correspondences, tarot cards, chakras, angelic equivalents, or ritual sigils are **modern inventions** unless accompanied by a traceable contemporary author.

No large, documented reconstructionist “Ñamandu cult” distinct from living Guaraní religion was found. Individual internet spirituality, ecological readings, and eclectic pagan invocation may exist, but scale and continuity cannot be established. Living Mbyá practice must not be relabeled “neopaganism.”

### Political and extremist appropriation

No substantial evidence was found for organized extremist appropriation of Ñamandu. Broader nationalist appropriation of “Guaraní mythology” occurs through homogenized pantheons and cultural branding, but a specific violent or exclusionary Ñamandu movement cannot be established.

### Commercial use

The name appears in cultural projects, businesses, artistic titles, tourism, and personal names. Such use testifies to public recognition, not necessarily worship. No commercial emblem has become a historically authoritative icon of the deity.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Ñamandu ancient?

The correct answer is evidentially divided:

- The theology was recorded from Mbyá authorities as inherited sacred knowledge, not openly composed by Cadogan. **[Secure twentieth-century Indigenous testimony.]**
- No surviving pre-Columbian text exists.
- Montoya does not securely document the named being in 1639.
- Therefore the tradition’s pre-contact depth is plausible but not directly datable.

“First written down in the twentieth century” does not mean “invented in the twentieth century.” Conversely, oral antiquity cannot be converted into an exact ancient date.

### Christian influence

Christian influence is demonstrable in Guaraní religious vocabulary after four centuries of missions. The disputed **Pa-pa Tenonde** title is the clearest internal case. Cadogan’s consultants themselves differentiated Mbyá usage from Christian outsider usage.

Possible Christian analogies include a supreme father, creation before the world, love, sacred word, and paired parental language. None proves wholesale borrowing: creator figures, sacred speech, naming, breath, and layered worlds have independent and comparative Indigenous contexts. The responsible conclusion is historical entanglement, with particular elements assessed separately.

### Monotheism or polytheism?

Calling the system “monotheistic” privileges Ñamandu’s priority but understates multiple divine fathers, mothers, abodes, descendants, and agencies. Calling it simply “polytheistic” can miss the emanational structure in which divine beings arise from the first father’s wisdom.

“Plural Mbyá theology ordered around a primordial first father” is less misleading. The classification remains analytic, not an Indigenous creedal label.

### What does *ayvu* mean?

Translations include “language,” “speech,” “word,” “soul,” “word-soul,” and even “being.” Melià’s “palabra-alma; alma-palabra” captures the refusal of the Mbyá category to separate language from animate personhood. No single English word is adequate.

Recent philology also questions inherited punctuation and chapter divisions, which are editorial devices applied to performed verbal art.

### Cadogan’s authority and mediation

Cadogan provided names of contributors and recorded original-language texts, a major evidentiary strength. Nevertheless:

- access depended on personal relationships and judgments about “orthodox” authorities;
- sacred texts were selected and arranged;
- Spanish translation inevitably interpreted difficult theological language;
- secrecy may have limited what was disclosed;
- community variation existed;
- print detached words from performance, music, gesture, and ritual setting.

The “authors” of *Ayvu Rapyta* were the Mbyá authorities Cadogan named and others whose words he recorded; describing Cadogan alone as author obscures this history.

### Land Without Evil debate

Nimuendajú and Hélène Clastres made prophetic migration toward the Land Without Evil a master theme of Guaraní religion. Later scholarship has cautioned against turning every migration into a religious pilgrimage or treating all Tupi-Guaraní peoples as expressions of one timeless prophetic system. Land pressure, warfare, kinship, ecology, colonial violence, and religious expectation interacted.

### Colmán and the “seven monsters”

Colmán’s 1929 poem is often presented online as ancient Guaraní scripture. It is instead a named modern literary work. Individual beings such as Pombero, Kurupí, Jasy Jatere, and Luisón have wider folklore histories; their organization as siblings born to Tau and Kerana is Colmán’s influential systematization. Ñamandu does not thereby become their canonical grandfather, sovereign, or adversary.

### Corrientes and Misiones folklore

Ambrosetti, Susana Chertudi, Berta Vidal de Battini, and regional collectors documented Pombero, Jasy Jatere, Kurupí, Luisón, Póra, animal owners, apparitions, and Catholic-Guaraní narratives. Vidal de Battini’s ten-volume collection preserves more than 3,000 oral narratives gathered over decades.

These collections establish a broad Guaraní-influenced folklore zone. They do not, without an explicit narrator’s statement, supply missing episodes in the Mbyá Ñamandu corpus. The relative absence of Ñamandu from popular Correntine and Misionero monster tales is itself a finding: he belongs most securely to esoteric Mbyá cosmology rather than the common nocturnal folklore cycle.

### Unsupported or discredited popular claims

The following cannot presently be established:

- that Ñamandu was worshipped in monumental temples;
- that he had an ancient cult image;
- that human beings or animals were sacrificed to him;
- that he fathered Tau, Kerana, or Colmán’s seven monsters;
- that he is simply the Christian God copied by Cadogan;
- that he is a primordial pan-Guaraní deity securely recorded in 1639;
- that “Ñamandu” has the settled dictionary meaning “the origin”;
- that the entire *Ayvu Rapyta* is a pre-Columbian manuscript;
- that an organized ancient priesthood used tarot-like symbols, planetary correspondences, or occult seals;
- that Ñamandu and the Apapocúva Ñanderuvuçú are textually identical persons rather than comparable creator figures.

|---|---|
| **Pre-contact period** | No datable inscription, manuscript, or securely identified image of Ñamandu survives. Pre-contact depth of the Mbyá oral tradition remains undetermined. |

| **1767–1768** | Spanish Crown expels the Jesuits; the reduction system enters secular administration and decline. |
