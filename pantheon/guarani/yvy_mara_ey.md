# Yvy Marã Ey (the Land Without Evil): Research Dossier

**Class:** Sacred place, eschatological destination, condition of perfected existence, and—under some modern political readings—an ethical-territorial ideal. **It is not securely classified as a god, goddess, spirit, demon, monster, or collective being.** Treating Yvy Marã Ey as a personified deity is unsupported by the early lexical and ethnographic evidence.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639) records **yby marãne’ỹ** lexically in the sense of “intact soil [not built upon].” This is secure evidence for the expression, but **not** secure evidence that seventeenth-century Guaraní used it for an immortal paradise or organized migrations around it. The earliest unambiguous published documentation of **Yvy marãey as “Land without Evil”**, a place without death sought by living people, is Curt Unkel Nimuendajú’s fieldwork among the Apapokúva-Guaraní, undertaken principally in 1905–1912 and published in German in 1914.

**Central evidentiary finding:** Two histories must be kept separate. First is the living and internally diverse Guaraní history of good, intact, indestructible, or perfected lands and of movement, ritual maturation, catastrophe, and relations with divine ancestors. Second is the scholarly history by which Nimuendajú’s specifically Apapokúva material was expanded by Alfred Métraux and especially Hélène and Pierre Clastres into a general theory of “Tupí-Guaraní prophetism.” Francisco Noelli, Cristina Pompa, Branislava Susnik, Diego Villar, Isabelle Combès, and others have shown that this synthesis often outran the colonial evidence. That criticism does not make the Apapokúva or Mbyá traditions unreal; it limits claims of timeless pan-Tupí-Guaraní uniformity.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY LEXICAL]** Secure colonial-period Guaraní language evidence, without assuming the full later religious meaning.
- **[COLONIAL TESTIMONY]** European descriptions of Indigenous prophets, movements, rituals, or promised abundance; mediated by missionary and colonial agendas.
- **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Directly collected Indigenous testimony from the late nineteenth or early twentieth century.
- **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Directly collected Indigenous testimony after Nimuendajú.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A belief securely documented for a particular Guaraní-speaking community but not transferable automatically to all others.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** An interpretation connecting otherwise separate sources.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** A literary or intellectual synthesis presented more cohesively than the underlying traditions warrant.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC]** Material formed in sustained Indigenous-Christian interaction or translated through Christian vocabulary.
- **[MODERN REINVENTION]** A recent literary, political, ecological, esoteric, or popular-cultural use not demonstrably ancient.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Yvy Marã Ey is not a divine personality with a body, genealogy, temple, or independent will. In the most securely documented Apapokúva and Mbyá materials it is a **place or state of existence**: indestructible or incorruptible land; the abode or destination of perfected beings; a place without sickness, painful labor, or death; and, in some traditions, a place reachable alive after ritual maturation. In modern Indigenous political speech it may also designate land on which a Guaraní mode of life can flourish.

The frequently encountered English “Guaraní paradise” is only an approximation. “Paradise” can incorrectly imply a postmortem heaven, divine judgment, or a fixed Christian otherworld. Some versions locate the destination beyond the sea or in the sky; others connect it to an earthly center or to actual sacred landscapes. Some can be reached without dying.

### Names and orthographies

- **Yvy marã e’ỹ / Yvy marã’ỹ / Yvy marane’ỹ / Yvy marãey / Ywy marã e’ỹ / Yby marãne’ỹ.** Orthographic variation reflects dialect, time, and different Guaraní writing systems.
- **Portuguese:** *Terra sem Mal*, commonly pluralized as *Terra sem Males*.
- **Spanish:** *Tierra sin Mal* or *Tierra sin Males*.
- **French:** *Terre sans Mal*.
- **German:** *Land ohne Übel*.
- **English:** *Land without Evil* or *Land without Ills*.
- **Yva / Yvá:** “heaven/sky,” sometimes equated with or closely connected to Yvy Marã Ey in Mbyá exposition, but not universally synonymous.
- **Yvyju / Ywy djú:** “golden/resplendent earth,” recorded in some Mbyá descriptions for an abode of immortals; the exact relation to Yvy Marã Ey varies.
- **Para Guachu Rapyta:** “origin/end-foundation of the Great Sea,” the last terrestrial stage in one Mbyá journey recorded by Cadogan.
- **Kandire:** A colonial-era term associated with western lands, the Incas, immortality, or bodily preservation in different sources. Its equation with Yvy Marã Ey is a contested scholarly construction, not a timeless synonym.
- **Guajupiá:** A Tupinambá destination of the dead named by Claude d’Abbeville in the early seventeenth century. It should not be silently renamed Yvy Marã Ey; the two belong to related but different documentary histories.

### Etymology

**[EARLY LEXICAL]** Montoya’s 1639 *Tesoro* gives **yby** as earth, soil, or world and records **marãne’ỹ** in the semantic field “good, whole, uncorrupted,” with the phrase glossed as intact or virgin soil, particularly soil that has not been built upon. Melià emphasized this ecological and territorial meaning.

**[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC]** Nimuendajú explained the Apapokúva form analytically: *yvy*, “earth/land”; *marã*, an older word he associated with sickness, evil, injury, mourning, or affliction; and *e’ỹ*, privation or “without.” Hence “Land without Evil/Ill.” His own note acknowledges that *marã* was no longer ordinarily used in that dialect.

These analyses need not be mutually exclusive. “Unspoiled,” “not subject to corruption,” “without affliction,” and “indestructible” occupy overlapping semantic territory. What is disputed is whether the fully eschatological sense existed in 1639 or emerged, crystallized, or became dominant later. Melià proposed a historical semantic development from intact cultivable land toward a religious-political ideal under colonial dispossession. A recent linguistic summary describes the trajectory as one from “virgin soil” to “Land without Evil,” not as proof that Montoya documented the complete later myth.

The capitalization of **Land Without Evil** makes an abstraction appear like a proper name. It is useful editorially but should not imply personification.

### Classification shifts

- **1639:** Description of a quality of soil or land. **[EARLY LEXICAL]**
- **1914:** Named eschatological refuge of the Apapokúva, sought in response to impending cosmic destruction. **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC]**
- **1950s:** Mbyá destination of those who attain *aguyje*, sometimes identified with heaven and reachable without bodily death. **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **1927–1975:** Organizing concept of a generalized Tupí-Guaraní religion and prophetic history. **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Late twentieth century onward:** Indigenous territorial, ecological, decolonial, and constitutional principle. **[MODERN REINVENTION grounded in living tradition]**
- **Popular mythology:** Sometimes misrepresented as a deity, a single island, or the residence of a standardized “Guaraní pantheon.” **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Montoya, 1639

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya published both the *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual* in Madrid in 1639. The *Tesoro* is the earliest secure textual support for the expression. It is a missionary dictionary composed amid the Jesuit reductions, encomienda, slave-raiding, epidemic disease, and forced territorial concentration.

What it establishes:

- The relevant expression existed in colonial Guaraní.
- It referred to land characterized as intact, whole, or unspoiled.

What it does not establish:

- A named paradise where maize grows by itself.
- Immortality or bodily ascent.
- A migration directed by a *karai* toward that destination.
- A uniform pan-Guaraní eschatology.

*Conquista espiritual* documents Guaraní resistance, prophetic or shamanic authority, reduction life, missionization, and violence. It is indispensable historical context, but it does not supply an uncontested seventeenth-century version of Nimuendajú’s complete Land-without-Evil complex.

### Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Tupí testimony

André Thevet, Hans Staden, Jean de Léry, Pero de Magalhães Gândavo, José de Anchieta, Manuel da Nóbrega, Fernão Cardim, Claude d’Abbeville, and Yves d’Évreux describe Tupinambá ritual specialists, *caraíbas*, dances, traveling prophets, ancestral destinations, promised abundance, war, captivity, cannibalism, and flight from colonial domination.

Yves d’Évreux reports a *caraíba* inviting followers to a beautiful country where things would be available without trouble or work. Gândavo describes a movement whose religious interpretation remains debatable. D’Abbeville records Tupinambá relocation to Maranhão, but Portuguese agents also manipulated Indigenous prophetic idioms for colonial expeditions.

**Finding:** These descriptions provide plausible analogues for later traditions of effortless abundance and prophet-led movement, but they do not themselves use the securely identified Apapokúva name *Yvy marãey*. The equation is **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**, not verbatim colonial testimony.

### Nimuendajú, 1914

Curt Unkel Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní*, published in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914), pp. 284–403, is the foundational document.

He reported that:

- Inspired by dreams and visions, shamans announced the imminent destruction of the world.
- They gathered followers and traveled with ritual dancing and magical songs toward the Land without Evil.
- Some located it at the earth’s center, but most placed it in the east beyond the sea.
- Its inhabitants did not die or suffer pain.
- Apapokúva movements toward the Atlantic occurred in waves during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
- A final expedition known to him had been attempted in 1905 but abandoned.
- Brazilian authorities discouraged or obstructed such movement.
- Epidemics and dispossession had reduced the communities among whom he worked.

Nimuendajú also recorded the myth of Guyraypotý: warned of destruction, the shaman led his people east, constructed a wooden house, survived flood and cosmic collapse, and ultimately passed through the celestial opening.

The publication date is 1914, although later translations sometimes misleadingly cite 1912, the closing phase of the underlying fieldwork. The surviving support is a printed ethnographic article containing Guaraní texts, German translations, and commentary—not an Indigenous precolonial manuscript.

### Métraux

Alfred Métraux’s 1927 “Migrations historiques des Tupi-Guarani” assembled more than fifteen documented movements from the sixteenth through twentieth centuries. He used Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva evidence as the interpretive key for colonial movements. His *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus Tupi-Guarani* further connected Tupinambá religion to later Guaraní material.

Métraux’s collection of evidence remains valuable. His conclusion—that the Land without Evil supplied a common religious cause for widely separated migrations—is **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**, now substantially contested.

### Cadogan, 1959

León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá*, published by the University of São Paulo in 1959, presents Mbyá-language texts with Spanish translation and commentary. Cadogan worked with recognized Mbyá authorities; the work preserves oral-liturgical material but is still a mediated publication organized and translated by an outsider.

Relevant material includes:

- Ñamandu Ru Ete’s self-creation and the foundation of sacred speech.
- The sending of word-souls to earthly existence.
- The divine fathers Ñamandu, Karai, Jakaira, and Tupã.
- Human pursuit of *aguyje*, maturity or perfection.
- The journey through *Para Guachu* toward Yvy Marã Ey/Yva.
- Parakáo Ñe’ẽngatu, the “Parrot of Discreet Speech,” who guards the rope or passage at the Great Sea.
- Pa’i, Chikú, Chapa, and other remembered leaders who crossed, ascended, or became immortal.

Cadogan’s Mbyá account differs materially from Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva catastrophe narrative. It emphasizes sacred words, disciplined maturation, bodily lightness, humility, dance, fasting, and divine assistance.

### Other major corpora

- **Egon Schaden**, especially *Aspectos fundamentais da cultura guarani* and related studies: comparative ethnography of Ñandeva, Mbyá, and Kaiowá; useful but sometimes influenced by the Nimuendajú-Métraux synthesis.
- **Branislava Susnik:** ethnohistory, migration, economy, and colonial transformation. She generally resisted making Yvy Marã Ey the master explanation of all movement and emphasized practical, political, and extractive causes, including western movements connected with Andean silver.
- **León Cadogan, *Ywyra Ñe’ery*:** later Mbyá ethnographic and linguistic materials, including forest, sacred word, and ritual knowledge.
- **Hélène Clastres, *La Terre sans Mal: le prophétisme tupi-guarani* (1975):** the most influential philosophical-historical synthesis.
- **Pierre Clastres, *Le Grand Parler* and *La Société contre l’État*:** interprets Guaraní prophetic thought as resistance to political unification and coercive power.
- **Bartomeu Melià:** relocates the concept in Guaraní economy, reciprocity, cultivated land, feast, and *teko*, while warning that colonial documents do not justify making every Guaraní religious practice revolve around it.
- **Graciela Chamorro:** studies sacred speech and Guaraní religious experience while reviewing the construction of the “Land without Evil” field.
- **Villar and Combès (2013):** trace how an Apapokúva narrative became first pan-Guaraní, then pan-Amerindian, and finally a constitutional-political value.
- **Francisco Noelli (1999):** calls the generalized migration thesis an academic invention produced when Métraux converted Nimuendajú’s late hypothesis into an explanatory system.
- **Cristina Pompa (2004):** demonstrates that theories of Tupí-Guaraní prophetism reflect anthropological schools and forced comparisons between colonial chronicles and modern ethnography at least as much as they reflect direct documentary continuity.

### Corrientes, Misiones, and Paraguayan folklore compilations

Ambrosetti, the Argentine folklore collections of Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini, and later investigations in Corrientes and Misiones are important for Pombero, Jasy Jateré, Kurupí, Luisón, and regional supernatural lore. They do **not** provide evidence that Yvy Marã Ey is a personified member of that folklore cast.

Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní poem *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, presented in the 1920s and published in 1929, arranged Tau, Keraná, their seven monstrous children, and other figures into a literary cosmogony. It is a **twentieth-century learned-literary systematization**, incorporating oral motifs but also Colmán’s acknowledged reconstruction and imagination. It supplies no documented interaction between Yvy Marã Ey and the seven monsters.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

Across sources, Yvy Marã Ey can denote:

- **Unspoiled cultivable land.** **[EARLY LEXICAL]**
- **Indestructible land beyond the reach of cosmic fire, collapse, flood, or predatory beings.** **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC: Apapokúva]**
- **A place without sickness, pain, death, or exhausting labor.**
- **A place of spontaneous abundance:** crops—especially maize—grow without cultivation; arrows or weapons may hunt independently; feast and dance replace labor. The famous “maize grows by itself” formulation is securely part of the twentieth-century ethnographic and later Clastrian synthesis, not a quotation from Montoya.
- **The destination of people who attain *aguyje*.** **[Mbyá regional variant]**
- **The residence of divine ancestors or immortals.**
- **A sacred orientation rather than a cartographically fixed place.**
- **Good land capable of supporting *teko porã*, a good or beautiful mode of life.**
- **A political image for territorial recovery and Indigenous autonomy.** **[MODERN]**

### Location variants

- **East, beyond the Atlantic:** dominant in Nimuendajú’s account of nineteenth-century Apapokúva prophets.
- **Center of the earth:** reported as a minority Apapokúva opinion.
- **Across the Great Sea:** Cadogan’s Mbyá texts.
- **Heaven or sky:** Yvy Marã Ey and Yva overlap in some Mbyá explanations.
- **West, beyond the mountains/near Peru or the Inca realm:** proposed for some colonial movements and the contested term *Kandire*.
- **Sacred terrestrial sites:** the Atlantic coast, Serra do Mar, Paraná River, Sete Quedas, Itaipu-area stones and caves, and particular *tekoha* may serve as thresholds, traces, or routes rather than being universally identified as the final land.

A recent Brazilian land-demarcation report records Avá-Guaraní testimony that Sete Quedas and an *ita ipy pe* near Foz do Iguaçu were places of sacred sound—song, women’s *takwá*, and *mbaraka*—and contact with Ñande Ru. One witness explicitly says that Yvy Marã Ey is not physical, although terrestrial places provide a route to it. This is **[CONTEMPORARY INDIGENOUS TESTIMONY]**, not evidence for a single ancient map.

### Sensory and material attributes

There is no canonical anthropomorphic image. Relevant material forms are instead:

- The **road or eastward path**.
- The **ocean** and the crossing rope.
- The **ritual house**, *opy* or *jeroky aty*.
- The **mbaraka**, ritual rattle.
- The **takwá**, women’s bamboo percussion instrument.
- The **dance**, *jeroky*.
- Sacred or “beautiful” speech, *ñe’ẽ porã*.
- Maize, gardens, forest, fruit, and shared feast.
- A body made light or incorruptible through *aguyje*.
- The wooden house or vessel that survives the flood in the Guyraypotý cycle.

There are no securely ancient statues, painted icons, cult emblems, sacred colors, numbers, weekdays, zodiacal correspondences, or weapons belonging to Yvy Marã Ey. Modern illustrations of a paradisal island, celestial village, or green utopia are interpretive images.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Divine Beings

Yvy Marã Ey has no parents, consort, children, siblings, or independent genealogy in the secure sources. It is a destination within several cosmologies, not a genealogical person.

### Documented divine or supernatural relations

Each line below records a relationship actually supported by a source. “Interaction” is necessarily spatial or causal because the Land does not ordinarily speak or act.

- **Ñanderuvuçú / Ñande Ru Vusú —** Creator of the present earth in the Apapokúva account; Yvy Marã Ey is the safe destination sought before the destruction of his created world. *Nimuendajú, 1914.* **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC; APAPOKÚVA]**
- **Ñanderýke’y / Nanderzqueý —** Powerful brother of Ñanderuvuçú who pulls out the eastern support of the earth and initiates its collapse; the Land without Evil is refuge from that destruction. *Nimuendajú, 1914.* **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC; APAPOKÚVA]**
- **Jaguarový, the Blue Jaguar —** Descends during the terminal catastrophe to devour humanity; inhabitants or successful seekers of Yvy Marã Ey escape its reach. *Nimuendajú, 1914.* **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC; APAPOKÚVA]**
- **Guyraypotý —** Prophet-shaman and culture hero who reenacts the successful path of deliverance, leading followers east and through the flood into the celestial realm. *Nimuendajú, 1914.* **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC; APAPOKÚVA]**
- **Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenonde —** Primordial Mbyá father, source of sacred wisdom and word-souls; the perfected divine realm toward which human beings aspire belongs to the cosmological order he establishes. *Cadogan, 1959.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**
- **Karai Ru Ete —** One of the Mbyá divine fathers, associated with sacred fire and the word-souls sent to earth; participates in the divine order from which guidance for perfection comes. *Cadogan, 1959.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**
- **Jakaira Ru Ete —** Divine father associated with mist, vivifying power, and sacred tobacco smoke; part of the order sustaining the ritual maturation that may lead to Yvy Marã Ey. *Cadogan, 1959.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**
- **Tupã Ru Ete —** Divine father associated with waters, rain, thunder, and the sea; in some Mbyá texts he participates in destruction and renewal of the imperfect earth. *Cadogan, 1959.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**
- **Parakáo Ñe’ẽngatu —** The Parrot of Discreet Speech guards the rope at the origin of the Great Sea and decides or reveals whether a humble, perfected traveler may cross into the country of the blessed. *Cadogan, 1959, chapter VIII.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**
- **Pa’i —** Culture hero or divinized leader who crosses the Great Sea and leaves Parakáo to guide later travelers. *Cadogan, 1959.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**
- **Karai Jeupie —** After an incest transgression and the resulting deluge, attains *aguyje* and reaches indestructibility; Cadogan records his transformation into Father Taparí. *Cadogan, 1959.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**
- **Captain Chikú and his wife —** Remembered human or divinized ancestral couple who attain perfection and cross without dying. *Cadogan, 1959.* **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC; MBYÁ]**

### Explicit absences

No secure source documents Yvy Marã Ey interacting with Tau, Keraná, Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jateré, Kurupí, Ao Ao, Luisón, Pombero, Pora, Mala Visión, or other Paraguayan-Correntine folklore figures. Their incorporation into one unified “Guaraní pantheon” is largely a modern encyclopedic and literary practice.

No source supports a marriage, battle, conversation, command, or judgment performed by Yvy Marã Ey as an acting deity.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Apapokúva destruction and eastward escape

**Earliest full source:** Nimuendajú, 1914.

Ñanderuvuçú makes the earth upon crossed wooden supports. His brother will eventually disturb the eastern support. The western side will fall, fire will spread beneath the earth, waters will overwhelm it, and the Blue Jaguar will descend. Dreams and visions disclose this coming catastrophe to shamans. They gather followers, perform prolonged dances and chants, and travel east toward a land that cannot be destroyed.

The destination is not merely a postmortem reward. The urgent goal is to enter alive before the world ends. Success depends on prophetic knowledge, correct ritual, endurance, and bodily transformation.

**Variants:** Some Apapokúva located it at the world’s center; most of Nimuendajú’s informants placed it beyond the eastern sea. Some journeys failed or were abandoned without invalidating the belief. **[EARLY ETHNOGRAPHIC]**

### Guyraypotý’s successful passage

Guyraypotý learns of the approaching end, leads his people east, and remains for years near the coast. He has a strong wooden house built. When the flood arrives, ritual song and dance—or the house’s supernatural elevation—carry the survivors upward through the celestial opening. His success becomes the precedent for later prophets.

This is a mythic model for the documented movements, but the historical expeditions should not be narrated as literal repetitions unless the travelers themselves said so.

### The Mbyá path of *aguyje*

In Cadogan’s corpus, human beings contain or embody divine word-souls. Earthly life is imperfect and heavy; disciplined ritual practice, moral comportment, song, fasting, dance, and the reception of divine words may bring *aguyje*: ripeness, maturation, perfection.

As imperfection is shed, the body becomes light. The perfected traveler reaches the Great Sea and, with Parakáo’s help, crosses into Yvy Marã Ey or Yva without undergoing ordinary death. Some remembered leaders—including Chikú and his wife—are said to have succeeded.

This is not identical to the Apapokúva flight from immediate catastrophe. It is **[REGIONAL MBYÁ VARIANT]** centered on sacred speech and maturation.

### The land where food produces itself

Hélène Clastres’s famous synthesis describes a place where maize grows without human work, arrows hunt by themselves, marriage rules disappear, and feast replaces social constraint. Some components derive from colonial accounts of *caraíba* promises; others from later ethnography.

The formulation is historically important but is **[SCHOLARLY SYNTHESIS]**. It should not be attributed wholesale to Montoya or to every Guaraní community.

Clastres interprets “evil” as society’s coercive order: work, kinship law, generational difference, and political power. Pierre Clastres subsequently reads Guaraní prophetism as a refusal of the One and, metaphorically, of the state. These are powerful philosophical interpretations, not transparent translations of every Indigenous statement.

### Colonial movements to Peru

A widely repeated narrative says that approximately 10,000 or 12,000 Tupinambá left Brazil around 1539–1540 and that only about 300 reached the Chachapoyas region after roughly ten years.

The movement itself is documented in colonial histories. Its interpretation as a search for Yvy Marã Ey is not directly established by the participants’ recorded words. Other motives include flight from Portuguese enslavement, alliance, war, trade, attraction to Inca or Spanish wealth, and search for viable territory. Métraux and Hélène Clastres made it part of the Land-without-Evil sequence; Noelli, Pompa, Susnik, Villar, and Combès warn against that certainty.

**Classification:** movement **[COLONIAL FACT]**; Land-without-Evil motive **[CONTESTED SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**.

### Coastal migrations, 1820–1912

Nimuendajú reconstructed successive movements of Apapokúva, Tañyguá, and Oguauíva/related Guaraní groups from the upper Paraná toward São Paulo and the Atlantic. He argued that fear of world destruction—not enemy pressure, material improvement, or attraction to Brazilian society—was the exclusive cause.

His testimony is strong for prophets among the communities he knew. The claim of exclusive religious causation for the whole century is less secure. Warfare, epidemic, enslavement, settler advance, forest economy, and state pressure also formed the historical setting.

## 6. Cult, Sacred Places, and Ritual Practice

Yvy Marã Ey has no securely identified temple or specialized priesthood devoted to it alone.

### Religious specialists

- **Karai / karaíba:** Depending on language, period, and source, inspired speaker, shaman, healer, traveling prophet, or powerful outsider. Colonial missionaries often translated such figures as sorcerers or agents of the devil.
- **Paje / pajé:** Ritual and healing specialist in Portuguese-mediated terminology.
- **Ñanderu, ñandesy, opygua:** Male or female elders and ritual leaders in different contemporary Guaraní communities.
- **Mburuvicha:** Political or community leadership, not automatically equivalent to a prophet.

The claim that a single pan-Tupí-Guaraní class of anti-state prophets existed unchanged from the sixteenth century is disputed.

### Ritual means

- Long communal dances and song cycles.
- Use of the *mbaraka* rattle and women’s *takwá*.
- Reception of sacred words in dream, trance, or inspired speech.
- Fasting or dietary discipline.
- Sexual or social rules varying by community and moment.
- Collective travel or *oguata*.
- Ritual maturation and bodily lightening.
- Prayer and nightly ceremonial life in the *opy*.

Nimuendajú also observed the use of firearms within ritual cosmology: in one episode an Apapokúva specialist instructed him to fire in the direction indicated by the rattle, interpreting the shot as cosmically efficacious. This demonstrates religious adaptation, not an ancient “weapon of Yvy Marã Ey.”

### Sacred geography

The Atlantic coast and Serra do Mar became major destinations in nineteenth- and twentieth-century movement. Mbyá occupation along southern and southeastern Brazil’s coast cannot, however, be reduced to a single mythic cause.

Contemporary Avá-Guaraní identify Sete Quedas, the Paraná River, and former Itaipu-area ritual sites as places where sacred sound and divine communication were available. The flooding of Sete Quedas by the Itaipu reservoir in 1982 is therefore remembered not merely as environmental loss but as removal of access to a spiritual route.

### Sacrifice and violence

No secure source associates human sacrifice with attaining Yvy Marã Ey.

Colonial Tupinambá captive killing and ritual anthropophagy are documented, but it is methodologically improper to make them rites “to the Land without Evil” without a source. Likewise, neither temple prostitution nor a formal sacrificial economy is documented for this concept.

Documented violence belongs instead to its historical setting:

- Encomienda and coerced labor.
- Slave-raiding and bandeirante attacks.
- Mission concentration and suppression of Indigenous ritual specialists.
- Epidemic disease.
- Territorial expulsion and agricultural colonization.
- State restriction of movement.
- Twentieth- and twenty-first-century land invasion and violence against Guaraní communities.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1500

Archaeology demonstrates extensive Tupí-Guaraní settlement and migration, but archaeology cannot by itself reveal that a particular movement sought Yvy Marã Ey. Claims of a prehistoric pan-Guaraní quest are **[SCHOLARLY INFERENCE]**.

### Sixteenth century

European sources encounter mobile Tupí and Guaraní societies, powerful ritual speakers, warfare, feasts, ritual anthropophagy among some coastal Tupinambá, and promises of abundance. Colonial enslavement and epidemics produce enormous population movements.

Métraux’s and Clastres’s identification of these movements as Land-without-Evil pilgrimages is possible in selected cases but not securely generalizable.

### Seventeenth century

Montoya records *yby marãne’ỹ* as intact soil. Jesuit reductions institutionalize Christian worship in Guaraní and translate Christian terms through Indigenous language. Missionaries suppress or demonize non-Christian ritual leadership while also preserving extensive linguistic evidence.

Christian heaven, resurrection, apocalypse, saint cult, and the “promised land” offered possible points of translation. The evidence does not permit either extreme conclusion: neither “the Jesuits invented Yvy Marã Ey” nor “Christianity had no effect on its later articulation” is demonstrable.

### Eighteenth century

After the Jesuit expulsion in 1767–1768, reduction communities fragmented, adapted, or entered new labor and settlement arrangements. Documentation is thinner. No continuous centralized “cult of the Land without Evil” is attested.

### Nineteenth century

Guaraní-speaking groups moved east from regions around the upper Paraná toward Brazil’s coast. Later oral testimony connects important parts of these journeys with prophetic warnings and the search for an indestructible land. Settler expansion, disease, conflict, and labor exploitation were simultaneous causes and conditions.

### 1905–1914

Nimuendajú joined and documented Apapokúva communities. The reported 1905 attempt was among the last major expeditions in his immediate account. His 1914 publication introduced *Yvy marãey* to international Americanist scholarship.

### 1927–1959

Métraux generalized the concept across historical migrations. Schaden and others expanded comparative Guaraní ethnography. Cadogan’s 1959 *Ayvu Rapyta* provided a distinct Mbyá sacred-text corpus, shifting attention toward divine speech, *aguyje*, and bodily transcendence.

### 1960s–1970s

Hélène and Pierre Clastres transformed the material into a philosophical anthropology of prophetism, freedom, and refusal of political power. Hélène Clastres’s 1975 book sharply distinguished the Land without Evil from a merely Christian heaven: it was a terrestrial or bodily attainable counter-order.

### 1980s–1990s

Melià linked land, ecology, reciprocity, feast, and *teko*, stressing that “good land” is the material ground of the Guaraní way of being. Noelli attacked the generalized migration theory as an academic myth. Indigenous land movements increasingly mobilized the concept in territorial claims.

### 2000s–present

Yvy Marã Ey operates simultaneously as:

- A living religious orientation.
- A name for territorial dignity and good living.
- A scholarly controversy.
- A Paraguayan cultural emblem.
- An ecological and decolonial metaphor.
- A constitutional principle in Bolivia.

The Bolivian Constitution of 2009, article 8, includes **ivi maraei**, glossed “land without evil,” among the ethical-moral principles of the plural state. This is a historic state appropriation and reformulation of a Guaraní concept, not evidence that every Bolivian Guaraní historically used it identically.

Contemporary Avá-Guaraní *retomadas*—re-entry into lands claimed as ancestral—may be described as *oguata porã*, sacred movement toward Yvy Marã Ey. This is neither merely metaphorical nor a simple survival of a frozen ancient myth: cosmology, law, memory, and political action are intertwined.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Indigenous and political use

The most consequential modern reception is Indigenous territorial politics. In Paraguay, Brazil, Bolivia, and Argentina, “Land without Evil” can name the continuing demand for land capable of sustaining language, ritual, kinship, agriculture, autonomy, and reciprocity.

The phrase has also been used by non-Indigenous writers as shorthand for a generalized social utopia. Such use can support Indigenous causes, but it can also erase differences between Mbyá, Ñandeva, Kaiowá, Paĩ Tavyterã, Avá, Chiriguano/Guaraní, and historical Tupinambá.

The Fundación Yvy Marãe’ỹ, established in Paraguay on 23 April 2005 and granted legal personality in 2007, promotes Guaraní language rights, multilingualism, and intercultural citizenship. Its use of the name is a documented modern institutional application.

### Christianity

Catholic pastoral movements have adopted the image, notably Brazil’s 2002 *Campanha da Fraternidade*, “Por uma terra sem males.” The campaign linked Indigenous rights, Christian hope, and social justice. This is **[MODERN SYNCRETISTIC RECEPTION]**, not an ancient Christian-Guaraní identity.

### Philosophy and anthropology

Pierre Clastres’s equation of metaphysical “One” with the political state has strongly shaped anarchist and anti-statist reception. It remains an authorial interpretation. It should not be quoted as if it were an untranslated Guaraní doctrine.

### Literature and visual arts

Modern writers have employed the image as Indigenous memory, anti-colonial future, ecological ideal, or existential destination. Documented examples include:

- Sophia de Mello Breyner Andresen’s poetic “country without evil.”
- Josely Vianna Baptista’s poem “Yvy Marã’ey.”
- Waldo Motta’s related poetic reworking.
- Raquel Chaves’s *Yvy maraê’y*.
- Sibran and Emmanuel Lepage’s graphic novel *La Terre sans Mal*.
- Later novels and speculative fiction that move the concept to imagined planets, lost islands, or psychological interiors.

These are **[MODERN LITERARY REINVENTIONS]**.

### Popular pantheon construction

Websites, school summaries, games, and illustrated mythologies often combine:

1. Cadogan’s Mbyá divine fathers;
2. Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva catastrophe cycle;
3. Tupinambá heroes from French chronicles;
4. Pombero, Jasy Jateré, Kurupí, and other Paraguayan-Argentine folklore;
5. Colmán’s seven-monster genealogy.

The resulting system is not found in any single Indigenous source. In particular, no evidence makes Yvy Marã Ey the kingdom of Tau’s seven children or a deity interacting with them.

### Occult, neopagan, and extremist appropriation

The dedicated search found no securely documented historical occult order, Golden Dawn derivative, ceremonial-magic current, or substantial reconstructionist “cult of Yvy Marã Ey.” New Age webpages sometimes redescribe it as an astral plane, energetic island, fifth-dimensional Earth, or generic shamanic ascension. These are **[MODERN INVENTIONS]** unless tied to identified contemporary Indigenous testimony.

No substantial, organized extremist appropriation comparable to modern racialized uses of some European gods was found. Nationalist folklorization does occur: Paraguayan cultural discourse may present a homogenized Guaraní mythology as the timeless soul of the nation while marginalizing living Indigenous peoples and their territorial claims.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was it ancient and pan-Tupí-Guaraní?

**Continuity thesis:** Nimuendajú suggested that modern Apapokúva movements explained older Guaraní and Tupí migrations. Métraux assembled colonial cases in support. Hélène Clastres treated prophetic migration as a central, long-duration religious pattern.

**Critical thesis:** Noelli argues that Nimuendajú offered a hypothesis that Métraux transformed into an academic certainty. Pompa shows that authors joined heterogeneous colonial and ethnographic materials under categories such as “prophetism” and “messianism.” Villar and Combès follow the idea’s enlargement from a specific Apapokúva narrative to a continental myth and constitutional slogan.

**Balanced conclusion:** Prophet-led movements, promised abundance, and sacred travel are genuinely documented across multiple periods. The proposition that all or most Tupí-Guaraní migration was motivated by one unchanged doctrine called Yvy Marã Ey is not demonstrated.

### Was it created by Christianity?

Some features—apocalypse, flood, paradise, immortality, prophecy, and a promised destination—invite Christian comparison. Centuries of missionary translation make complete isolation implausible.

Yet the relevant traditions also contain structures not reducible to Christianity: attainment without death, bodily lightening through dance, divine word-souls, recurrently destructible worlds, sacred eastward geography, and land as the condition of *teko*.

The safest classification is **historically interactive and partly syncretistic**, not simply borrowed or pristine.

### What does *marã e’ỹ* mean?

- Nimuendajú: absence of sickness, evil, mourning, or affliction.
- Montoya/Melià line: intact, whole, uncorrupted, undeveloped soil.
- Later Mbyá interpretation: indestructibility or incorruptibility.
- Modern translations: “without evil,” “without ills,” “unspoiled,” “undamaged,” or “imperishable.”

“Without evil” is now conventional but encourages a moral dualism not always present in the Guaraní semantic field.

### What caused migration?

Possible and often simultaneous causes include:

- Prophetic warning and eschatology.
- Search for fertile forest and gardens.
- Agricultural rotation and ecological exhaustion.
- Kinship and marriage.
- War and enemy pressure.
- Flight from slavers, encomenderos, missionaries, or colonists.
- Epidemic disease.
- Trade and attraction to mineral or political centers.
- Territorial reorganization after dispossession.

Nimuendajú’s claim of exclusively religious causation is strongest for statements by particular Apapokúva informants and weakest as a universal historical law.

### State of the evidence

The primary record is asymmetrical:

- Colonial evidence was written mainly by missionaries, soldiers, and administrators.
- Indigenous sacred knowledge was predominantly oral and sometimes deliberately withheld.
- Modern ethnographers selected, translated, edited, and systematized performances.
- Different Guaraní peoples should not be treated as interchangeable witnesses.
- Much relevant ritual knowledge remains community-held and need not be publicly disclosed.
- The principal published corpora survive in major libraries and digital repositories, including the Biblioteca Digital Curt Nimuendajú and institutional journal archives.

### Final assessment

Yvy Marã Ey is securely real as a documented Guaraní expression, a specifically attested Apapokúva eschatological destination, a Mbyá horizon of perfected existence, and a continuing Indigenous political-religious idea. It is not securely a deity. The seventeenth-century phrase and the twentieth-century religious complex cannot be collapsed into one unchanged doctrine. Nor can the concept be dismissed merely because anthropologists generalized it: Nimuendajú’s informants, Cadogan’s Mbyá interlocutors, and contemporary Guaraní witnesses supply independent Indigenous testimony.

The fullest defensible reconstruction is therefore plural. There are intact lands, indestructible lands, celestial countries, destinations beyond the sea, sacred terrestrial thresholds, paths opened by inspired speech, and modern territories of autonomy. “The Land Without Evil” is the conventional name for this connected but non-uniform history.
