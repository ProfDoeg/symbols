# Ao Ao: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A supernatural monster or dangerous forest being in Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine folklore; in twentieth-century Paraguayan systematizations, also a male “genius,” “fate,” or minor divinity of fertility and lord/protector of hills. Ao Ao is not securely documented as a god with an organized cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s collected legend “El Ahó-Ahó,” published in *Supersticiones y leyendas* in 1917 from folklore recorded in the Guaraní-speaking Alto Paraná/Misiones region. Ambrosetti had gathered regional material during expeditions in the 1890s, but the precise collection date and informant for this item have not been established here. His 1917 publication predates Narciso R. Colmán’s *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929), which first securely places Ao Ao inside the now-familiar genealogy of the seven children of Taú and Keraná. [The University of La Plata’s bibliographic record describes Ambrosetti’s geographical corpus](https://naturalis.fcnym.unlp.edu.ar/entities/publication/b3b6e64f-e44c-48c0-a516-57eb18a9e38a); [the 1917 volume’s contents explicitly include “La leyenda del Ahó-Ahó”](https://editoriales.conabip.gob.ar/node/11191).
>
> **Central evidentiary conclusion:** The man-eating, sheep-like monster and the palm-tree escape belong to documented regional folklore by 1917 and probably derive from oral material collected earlier. Ao Ao’s status as the *sixth son of Taú and Keraná*, its position among exactly seven monsters, and its destruction with all six siblings are securely documented only in Colmán’s 1929 literary mythography. Those two layers must not be conflated.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

In the earliest securely published account, Ao Ao is a terrifying forest animal: quadrupedal, woolly or sheep-like, equipped with enormous claws, anthropophagous, and defeated only when its quarry climbs a palm. It belongs to the supernatural geography of the Guaraní-speaking Alto Paraná—Misiones, Corrientes, southern Paraguay, and adjoining Brazilian territory—rather than to a recoverable pan-Guaraní theological system.

In Colmán’s 1929 *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, Ao Ao becomes a genealogically fixed mythological person: sixth of the seven prematurely born monsters fathered by the evil spirit Taú on Keraná, “hado de la fecundidad,” extraordinarily prolific, “dominator” of hills and mountains, and one of the beings burned at Moñái Kuare. This is a **learned literary systematization**, although it incorporates a demonstrably older regional legend.

Later Paraguayan compilers commonly call it a *dios de la fecundidad*, *señor de la procreación*, *duende*, *ente*, *monstruo*, or *animal fabuloso*. These are not interchangeable indigenous classifications. “Monster” or “dangerous supernatural animal” is the best evidence-led general classification; “fertility god” is a twentieth-century interpretive title derived from Colmán’s statement that it produced numerous offspring.

### Recorded forms

- **Ahó-Ahó:** Ambrosetti’s 1917 Spanish orthography.
- **Ao-Ao / Ao Ao / Aó-Aó:** standard Paraguayan and Argentine Spanish spellings in twentieth- and twenty-first-century folklore literature.
- **Aho Aho:** Portuguese and international popular spelling.
- **Ovecha ka’aguy**, approximately “forest sheep”: reported at Mbuyapey to sculptor and folkloric artist Ramón Elías and published posthumously in Pedro Encina Ramos’s *Huellas de metal y madera* (2005). Because *ovecha* is a Hispanism from Spanish *oveja*, this name cannot establish a precolonial ovine iconography. [The Ramón Elías museum account preserves this local name](https://www.portalguarani.com/detalles_museos_otras_obras.php?id=27&id_obras=2406&id_otras=369).

### Etymology

Three interpretations circulate:

1. **Onomatopoeia — securely attested as a folkloric explanation.** The pack calls *ao-ao, ao-ao* while pursuing prey. Ambrosetti’s legend and Colmán’s account associate the repeated name with the monster’s cries; modern retellings compare it to barking or howling.

2. **Guaraní *ao*, “clothing” — modern folk etymology.** In modern Guaraní, *ao* means clothing; reduplication can therefore be glossed “clothes-clothes” or “much clothing.” Writers connect this with the animal’s woolly covering. Fernando Achucarro presents this cautiously as one possibility, alongside the onomatopoeia. [His discussion explicitly labels the alternatives as possibilities](https://www.portalguarani.com/1076_fernando_javier_achucarro/9798_ao_ao__serie_mitologia_guarani.html).

3. **Montoya’s *aú* — modern scholarly conjecture.** A 2020 survey by Fundación Tapé Avirú proposes a relationship to a root written *aú* in Ruiz de Montoya’s 1639 *Tesoro*. This does not amount to a 1639 attestation of the named monster: lexical resemblance must not be converted into evidence that Montoya knew Ao Ao.

No secure historical etymology has been demonstrated. The onomatopoeic explanation is integral to the recorded story but is not thereby a proven linguistic origin.

### Titles and domains

- **“Hado de la fecundidad”** — Colmán, 1929; **learned systematization**.
- **“Dominador de los cerros y de las montañas”** — Colmán, 1929; **learned systematization**, subsequently folklorized.
- **“Dios de la fecundidad” / “señor de la procreación”** — later twentieth-century expansion of Colmán; **later folklore and museum interpretation**.
- **Protector or lord of hills:** common modern paraphrase, but Colmán says *dominador*, not clearly “benevolent protector.” Treat “protector” as a later interpretive gloss.
- **Forest sheep:** twentieth-century local or antiquarian nomenclature; **regional folklore**.

There is no documented Greek, Roman, Christian, Afro-American, or other formal interpretatio identifying Ao Ao with a foreign deity. Comparisons to werewolves, boars, peccaries, the Mapuche Guallipén, or cryptozoological animals are modern analogies, not historical syncretisms.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Early sources that do not securely attest Ao Ao

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual*, both published at Madrid in 1639, are fundamental witnesses to colonial Guaraní language and the Jesuit missions. The *Tesoro* was composed from Montoya’s linguistic work between approximately 1612 and 1638. Neither work has been shown to contain Ao Ao as the named sheep-like anthropophage, nor the Taú–Keraná genealogy.

That absence matters, but it is not proof that no comparable oral being existed. Missionary documents were selective, polemical, and designed for evangelization or linguistic instruction. They cannot be treated as exhaustive catalogues of Guaraní belief.

The same qualification applies to:

- Curt Nimuendajú, *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* (1914);
- León Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959), recording Mbyá sacred speech and creation traditions;
- Cadogan’s other Mbyá collections, including material later assembled under *Ywyra Ñe’ery*;
- Egon Schaden’s Guaraní ethnographies;
- Alfred Métraux’s Tupinambá synthesis;
- Pierre and Hélène Clastres on Guaraní sacred speech and the Land Without Evil.

These corpora document distinct Guaraní peoples and theological vocabularies. They do not securely place Ao Ao in the Mbyá or Apapocúva creation cycles, nor do they attest the seven children of Taú and Keraná as a shared ancient Guaraní pantheon. Claims that Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta* contains that genealogy are erroneous or at least unsupported by the cited text.

### Ambrosetti: the earliest secure legend

Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s *Supersticiones y leyendas: región misionera, valles calchaquíes, las Pampas* appeared in Buenos Aires in 1917. Its regional material covers Corrientes, Misiones, southern Paraguay, the Alto Uruguay, and neighboring Brazilian influences. The book includes a discrete chapter, “La leyenda del Ahó-Ahó.”

Its Ao Ao is:

- a terrible animal resembling a sheep;
- armed with large claws;
- a devourer of people encountered in the forest;
- capable of digging around the roots of an ordinary tree until it falls;
- unable to take a victim who climbs a palm, understood as a sacred tree associated with Calvary.

This is **securely attested early regional folklore**. It already contains the defining pursuit, tree-felling, anthropophagy, ovine appearance, claws, and sacred-palm escape. It does **not**, on present evidence, make Ao Ao Taú’s sixth son or one of seven monsters.

Ambrosetti’s book was published in 1917, but it drew on field material gathered substantially earlier. His *Materiales para el estudio del folk-lore misionero* appeared in 1894, while his later compilation says that part of the material had remained unpublished. The safe dating is therefore: **recorded in the late nineteenth or early twentieth century; securely published by 1917**. [The 1894 bibliographic record establishes his earlier Misiones collection](https://books.google.com/books/about/Materiales_para_el_estudio_del_folk_lore.html?id=llHnF5c6uy8C).

### Colmán’s literary system

Narciso Ramón Colmán, writing as Rosicrán, published the Guaraní poem *Ñande Ypy Kuéra: génesis de la raza guaraní* in 1929; his Spanish version appeared in 1937. It is a literary ethnogenetic epic, not a verbatim indigenous scripture. Colmán drew on folklore, Christianized motifs, antiquarian speculation, place legends, and his own composition.

His glossary gives the decisive canonical formulation:

> “Ao-Ao – Sexto hijo del espíritu maléfico Taû y de Kerana, que era el Hado de la fecundidad.”

It continues that Ao Ao generated innumerable descendants, ruled hills and mountains, lived in packs like wild boars, pursued people, cried “¡Ao-Ao Ao-Ao!,” dug out trees, and was powerless against a palm with a secret virtue. [The complete online transcription preserves Colmán’s entry](https://www.portalguarani.com/376_narciso_ramon_colman_rosicran__/10966_nuestros_antepasados_nande_ypy_kuera__obra_de_narciso_r_colman.html).

Colmán’s larger poem makes the seven monsters part of an elaborate cosmogony involving Tupã, Arasy, Taú, Keraná, Rupavẽ, Sypavẽ, Porãsy, and Tumê Arandu. Modern literary scholarship identifies the poem’s biblical architecture, Jesuit recoding of Tupã, Western numerology, and national-foundational ambitions. It also notes that some monsters came from regional indigenous folklore, while Colmán assembled all seven into one system. [A modern CONICET study analyzes precisely that construction](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/187924/CONICET_Digital_Nro.2ec7a1e2-a607-4e69-b460-81d8d7ee9be9_L.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=5).

### Later textual and oral record

- **Paulo de Carvalho Neto, *Folklore del Paraguay* (1961):** a systematic collection based substantially on Paraguayan research in 1950–51. It cites Ambrosetti’s Ao Ao and helped establish the legend in academic folklore classification. Métraux praised the work’s breadth and organization. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com.py/books?hl=es&id=xgrgAAAAMAAJ); [Métraux’s review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hom_0439-4216_1962_num_2_1_366465).
- **Yvytyrusu, April 1963:** a Guairá version was reported, affirming continuing local circulation.
- **Mauricio Cardozo Ocampo, *Mundo folklórico paraguayo*, part II (1989):** presents the sheep-like quadruped, group pursuit, onomatopoeic cries, and protective pindó. Wolf Lustig’s Guaraní studies page reproduces this version and explicitly interprets the palm motif as Jesuit or Christian intrusion. [Text and attribution](https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/lustig/guarani/ao_ao.htm).
- **Tomás L. Micó, *Leyendas del Paraguay: mitología guaraní* (1997):** adds a bear-like head, barking, the name *ovecha ka’aguy*, and cryptozoological claims that Ao Ao is a real unclassified animal known to Mbyá and Chulupí communities. These claims are **late antiquarian/cryptozoological speculation**, not zoological evidence. [Micó’s account](https://www.portalguarani.com/detalles_museos_otras_obras.php?PqwoiflUYTeslk=MjQ0Ng%3D%3D).
- **Francisco Pérez-Maricevich, ed., *Mitos y leyendas del Paraguay* (1998):** distinguishes the basic sheep-monster legend from “la versión de Rosicrán,” implicitly recognizing Colmán’s genealogical layer. [Dionisio González Torres’s version](https://www.portalguarani.com/detalles_museos_otras_obras.php?id=103&id_obras=2315&id_otras=369).
- **Pedro Encina Ramos, *Huellas de metal y madera* (2005):** documents the Ramón Elías museum sculpture, local names, and a further Christianized explanation of the palm.
- **Fundación Tapé Avirú survey (2020):** a contemporary online survey of Paraguayan knowledge. Its importance is evidence of present cultural recognition, not proof of antiquity.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Form

**Early regional folklore:** a quadruped like a sheep but larger and ferocious, with claws capable of uprooting trees.

**Colmán, 1929:** compared behaviorally to *jabalíes* living in large packs. Colmán’s wording need not mean a literal boar’s head.

**Later iconography:** commonly combines:

- sheep-like or densely woolly body;
- boar, pig, bear, or vaguely predatory head;
- tusks or long teeth;
- very large claws;
- short ears and hoof-like hind feet;
- massive size.

The sharply specified “boar head,” knife-like claws, exact anatomy, and ability to imitate an ordinary sheep are largely **modern illustrative elaborations**. They should not be retrojected into Ambrosetti’s text.

### Actions and powers

- Hunts people in forests and hills: **early regional folklore**.
- Operates singly or in packs: a collective plurality is already implicit in later versions and explicit in Colmán.
- Calls “ao-ao” to its fellows or howls during pursuit: **early/Colmán-era folklore**.
- Circles the refuge tree, excavates its roots, and topples it: **early regional folklore**.
- Cannot overcome the sacred palm: **early recorded but Christianized folklore**.
- Produces numerous offspring: **Colmán systematization**.
- Represents fertility: **Colmán and later interpretation**.
- Protects hills and mountains: “dominates” them in Colmán; benevolent protection is **later reinterpretation**.
- Reproduces without a mate, by self-division, or “mitosis”: **modern invention or unsupported popular expansion**. Colmán says only that it procreated abundantly.
- Is immortal or the sole surviving monster: **modern invention**, contradicted by Colmán’s narrative, where all seven are burned.
- Eats clothing because *ao* means clothing: **modern wordplay**, absent from the early legend.

### The pindó problem

The generic term *pindó* can denote native palms, often *Syagrus romanzoffiana*. Recorded versions vary between *palmera*, *pindó*, *cocotero*, and occasionally other improperly substituted trees.

Three historical layers must be distinguished:

1. **Legendary rule:** climbing the palm saves the pursued person.
2. **Christian explanation:** the palm is sacred because it adorns Calvaries or is used during Holy Week/Palm Sunday.
3. **Expanded sacred history:** Tupã blessed it because it fed the infant Jesus on the flight into Egypt, or gave it to Rupavẽ to nourish the Guaraní people.

The first two are present in early recorded folklore; the third is a later syncretistic elaboration. Wolf Lustig and Cardozo Ocampo reasonably infer Jesuit or wider Catholic influence from the explicit Calvary/Palm Sunday symbolism, but no located Jesuit reduction document names Ao Ao or demonstrates that missionaries invented the creature. The evidence establishes a **Christianized legend**, not a documented Jesuit fabrication.

### Iconographic record

No precolonial image, mission-period carving, archaeological figure, or securely identified indigenous pictograph of Ao Ao is known.

Its iconography is modern and text-dependent:

- Ramón Elías (1929–1981) created a sculptural representation now associated with the Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías. The surviving published description presents a woolly, clawed monster and calls it a fertility god.
- José Escobar produced a popular carved representation reproduced with Tomás Micó’s account.
- Fernando Javier Achucarro’s graphite-and-pastel image is explicitly described as arising “desde la imaginación del artista,” an important acknowledgment that it is modern artistic reception rather than traditional iconographic evidence.

There are no secure traditional colors, numbers, days, celestial bodies, ritual implements, or sacred offerings belonging specifically to Ao Ao. Seven belongs to Colmán’s narrative architecture, not to a separately documented Ao Ao cult.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Genealogies

#### Independent regional-legend layer

Ambrosetti’s Ao Ao has no recorded parents, consort, siblings, or named offspring. It is an autonomous monster or species. This is the earliest secure configuration.

#### Colmán’s system, 1929

- **Father:** Taú, malignant spirit.
- **Mother:** Keraná, daughter of Marangatu.
- **Maternal grandfather:** Marangatu.
- **Siblings, in order:** Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupí, Ao Ao, Huisõ/Luisón.
- **Offspring:** innumerable Ao Aos, unnamed.
- **Curser of the line:** Arasy.
- **Destroyers:** Porãsy, who lures the seven into a cave, and Tumê Arandu, who orders or effects their incineration.
- **Ultimate transformation:** in Colmán’s numerological gloss, the purified souls of the seven become the Pleiades or “seven little goats.”

This family tree is **not securely attested before Colmán**. It became dominant through print, schools, museums, folklore anthologies, public sculpture, and mass media.

### Every documented named supernatural interaction

- **Taú — fathering:** Ao Ao is conceived as Taú’s sixth son by Keraná. Source: Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Keraná — motherhood:** Keraná bears Ao Ao prematurely as one of seven malformed children. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Arasy — curse:** Arasy’s curse causes Taú and Keraná’s offspring, including Ao Ao, to be monstrous. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization with Christian/biblical narrative architecture.**
- **Teju Jagua — sibling and joint death:** appears with Ao Ao among the seven summoned to Moñái’s wedding and burned in the cave. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Mbói Tu’ĩ — sibling and joint death:** same collective episode. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Moñái — sibling and summoner:** Moñái arranges the supposed wedding with Porãsy and assembles his six siblings, thereby bringing Ao Ao into the fatal cave. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Jasy Jatere — sibling and provider of victims:** Colmán’s glossary says Jasy Jatere misleads people and takes them to his cannibal brother Ao Ao. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.** Later versions specify disobedient children, a **later folkloric elaboration**.
- **Kurupí — sibling and joint death:** present among the seven cave victims. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Huisõ/Luisón — sibling and joint death:** seventh sibling, burned with Ao Ao. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Porãsy — deceiver and sacrificial adversary:** Porãsy pretends to accept Moñái, requires that all his brothers attend, and becomes trapped with them when the cave is sealed. Ao Ao has no individualized dialogue with her, but they share the episode. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Tumê Arandu — destroyer:** after the cave is sealed, Tumê brings or applies the fire that kills Ao Ao and its siblings. Source: Colmán (1929). **Learned systematization.**
- **Tupã — no direct encounter securely attested:** later retellings say Tupã created, gave, or blessed the protective palm, but no located text has Ao Ao directly meet or contend with Tupã. **Later syncretistic elaboration.**

No direct interaction with Angatupyry is documented: Angatupyry fights Taú before Ao Ao’s birth, so listing an Ao Ao–Angatupyry encounter would be artificial.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The pursuit and the sacred palm

**Earliest secure source:** Ambrosetti, 1917, from regional folklore.

A traveler, hunter, or person in the forest meets the sheep-like Ahó-Ahó. The monster pursues and attempts to devour the victim. If the person climbs an ordinary tree, Ahó-Ahó uses its enormous claws to dig around the roots until the tree collapses. If the quarry reaches a palm, however, the monster cannot take them.

Later accounts intensify the episode: a pack gathers, circles the trunk, howls its name in chorus, and excavates collectively. Once the person reaches the pindó, the monsters lose the scent, become confused, howl in defeat, or depart.

**Development:**

- Sheep-like anthropophage, claws, tree-felling, palm refuge: **early regional folklore**.
- Pack coordination and numerous descendants: explicit in Colmán, 1929.
- Palm sacred through Calvary: already embedded in the early recorded form; **Christianized folklore**.
- Palm feeding the infant Jesus: **later syncretistic elaboration**.
- Palm created by Tupã and associated with Kuarahy: **modern educational synthesis**, combining separate Guaraní and Christian sacred-tree motifs.
- Ao Ao is destroyed by touching palm wood or can be killed with a pindó stake: **modern invention**, not the traditional rule.

### Birth of the seven monsters

**Source:** Colmán, 1929.

Taú, the personification or spirit of evil, desires and abducts Keraná after defeating Angatupyry. Arasy curses the resulting line. After seven-month gestations Keraná bears seven malformed beings, culminating in Luisón; Ao Ao is sixth. Ao Ao’s assigned function is fertility, explained by its prodigious descendants, while its territory is the hills and mountains.

The recurrent seven—seven children, seven-month gestation, seven days of combat or burning, and eventual celestial seven—is Colmán’s organizing device. Modern scholarship observes that this numerology is characteristic of Western traditional narration and contradicts Colmán’s unsupported assertion that seven was intrinsically ominous to the Guaraní. Therefore the cycle is **literary mythography using folkloric beings**, not a securely recovered precolonial scripture.

### The cave, Porãsy, and destruction of the monsters

**Source:** Colmán, 1929.

The monsters’ predations, especially those of Moñái and Kurupí, lead the people of Atyrá to seek Tumê Arandu’s help. Porãsy volunteers to entice them. She feigns love for Moñái but requires all six of his brothers to attend their wedding in a cave at Yaguarón. The monsters assemble, drink, and become incapacitated. Porãsy tries to escape, is discovered, and orders the entrance sealed although she remains inside. Tumê applies fire. Porãsy and all seven monsters, Ao Ao included, perish.

Colmán’s glossary adds that after seven days and nights of burning, the monsters’ purified souls rise as the Pleiades. Taú then curses humanity; Tupã responds to mounting violence with a flood. The whole sequence closely participates in Colmán’s national-biblical epic design.

Ao Ao plays no individualized role in the cave beyond being summoned, trapped, and burned. Stories in which it escapes, survives, or reproduces after the fire are later attempts to reconcile Colmán’s ending with continuing belief in packs of Ao Aos.

### Ao Ao and Jasy Jatere

Colmán’s glossary says Jasy Jatere “had the habit of leading people astray in order to take them to his brother Ao-Ao, who was a cannibal.” Later children’s lore makes the victims specifically disobedient children. This episode links two originally separable regional warning beings into Colmán’s sibling system.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is no evidence of an organized cult of Ao Ao.

Specifically, no securely identified evidence exists for:

- temples, shrines, altars, or priesthoods;
- sacrifices or offerings;
- hymns, formal prayers, invocations, or liturgical calendars;
- oracles or divination;
- theophoric personal names;
- votive inscriptions;
- initiation societies;
- human sacrifice to Ao Ao;
- temple prostitution;
- a precolonial sacred image.

Calling Ao Ao a “fertility god” does not establish worship. The title originates in literary and lexicographic classification, and fertility is inferred from abundant offspring.

The palm refuge is better described as legendary prophylaxis than cult ritual. Later popular testimony about carrying palm wood, making a stake, or invoking the palm’s holiness belongs to continuing folklore and cannot be projected backward without documentation.

Ao Ao does perform a social function in oral tradition: it warns against wandering alone through forest and mountain terrain and is reportedly invoked to frighten children. That is a use of the legend, not evidence of devotional practice.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before European contact

No evidence securely establishes the named Ao Ao, its ovine form, or its seven-monster genealogy before contact. Sheep were introduced animals, so a literal sheep-bodied precolonial image is chronologically problematic. A pre-existing dangerous forest animal or peccary-associated being may underlie the legend, but this is **modern reconstruction**, not demonstrated continuity.

### Jesuit and colonial period, seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

Montoya and the reduction corpus document Guaraní language, religious categories, evangelization, forced settlement, warfare, enslavement pressures, and Christian recoding. They do not securely attest Ao Ao by name.

The palm’s Calvary/Palm Sunday immunity indicates Catholic influence. Whether missionaries deliberately attached the motif to an older being, whether Guaraní-speaking communities produced the synthesis themselves, or whether the whole surviving form arose in colonial folk culture cannot presently be decided.

Claims that Jesuits invented Ao Ao to prevent people leaving the reductions are **modern speculation without located documentary support**.

### Regional oral folklore and first collection, nineteenth century–1917

Ao Ao circulated in the mixed Guaraní-speaking culture of Misiones, Corrientes, southern Paraguay, and the Alto Paraná. Ambrosetti’s work converts this oral being into a documented folkloric subject. His account belongs to a regional population including indigenous communities, Paraguayan and Correntino mestizos, Brazilian migrants, laborers, and settlers; it should not automatically be attributed to every Guaraní people.

### National literary systematization, 1929–1937

Colmán transforms independent beings and place traditions into a unified national “Guaraní genesis.” Ao Ao acquires:

- numbered birth order;
- named parents and siblings;
- a fertility office;
- a cosmic curse;
- a collective death;
- a celestial afterlife.

This was the decisive change in the figure’s documentary history.

### Folklore scholarship and popular canon, 1950s–1990s

Carvalho Neto, Guasch, Jover Peralta and Osuna, Cadogan’s reviewers and contemporaries, Cardozo Ocampo, González Torres, Micó, and others repeated, classified, questioned, or embellished the legend. Colmán’s genealogy gradually became what schools and popular media call “Guaraní mythology.”

A competing interpretation recorded in later Paraguayan antiquarian writing proposes that *aó-ao* was originally the name of an edible animal known among Guaraní communities in Amambay, subsequently transformed into a man-eating monster in national folklore. The proposed identification is unclear and not zoologically established. A peccary model is plausible because peccaries travel in groups and can be dangerous, but remains **modern naturalistic reconstruction**.

### Museum and educational canon, late twentieth century–present

The Ramón Elías museum sculptures and other popular carvings gave Ao Ao a stable visible form. Textbooks and bilingual education materials in Corrientes and Paraguay now teach it alongside the other six “monsters,” often merging:

- Ambrosetti’s regional pursuit legend;
- Colmán’s genealogy;
- Christian palm lore;
- later boar and sheep iconography;
- the modern title “protector of hills.”

This synthesis is culturally real today even though it is historically composite.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Visual art and heritage

Ramón Elías’s sculpture is among the most influential modern representations. The museum description calls Ao Ao a fertility god and preserves testimony from Mbuyapey that it was locally called *ovecha ka’aguy*. José Escobar and Fernando Achucarro provide further modern artistic forms.

These images have materially standardized features that the earliest text left vague, especially the boar-like head and exaggerated claws.

### Education and public folklore

Argentine intercultural educational material presents Ao Ao as:

- sixth child of Taú and Keraná;
- anthropophagous;
- prolific and fertile;
- ruler of hills;
- vulnerable to the pindó.

Such material is evidence of current regional transmission, not independent corroboration of precolonial antiquity. [The Corrientes educational booklet gives this complete modern synthesis](https://mec.gob.ar/descargas/Bibliograf%C3%ADa/Educaci%C3%B3n%20Intercultural%20Biling%C3%BCe/GUARANI/avane.pdf).

### Literature, games, and online culture

Ao Ao now appears in fantasy art, online bestiaries, horror videos, role-playing discussions, games, and “cryptid” lists. These media commonly add:

- a boar’s head and tusks;
- exclusive consumption of human flesh;
- a curse of eternal hunger;
- asexual reproduction;
- immortality;
- weakness to palm-wood weapons;
- equivalence with the Mapuche Guallipén;
- survival in named contemporary mountain ranges.

These are **modern retellings or inventions** unless individually sourced to an oral informant.

### Occultism and revival

The dedicated search found no documented Ao Ao tradition in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, modern grimoires, chaos magic, Jungian literature, or an organized reconstructionist religion. There may be individual internet-era devotional or artistic uses, but no evidence located supports a continuous cult or a measurable neopagan movement.

### Political appropriation

Colmán’s work participates in twentieth-century Paraguayan nation-building: it constructs a deep Guaraní antiquity while combining indigenous vocabulary, regional folklore, biblical structures, and nationalist protohistory. Ao Ao’s modern pantheon position owes much to this project.

No significant extremist appropriation, state cult, organized political movement, or commercial brand centered on Ao Ao was located. Absence from the searched record is not proof of absolute absence, but such a history cannot responsibly be manufactured.

## 9. Historiography and contested questions

### Was Ao Ao invented by Colmán?

**No, not in its entirety.** Ambrosetti published the independent Ahó-Ahó legend in 1917, before *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*. Its principal pursuit motifs were already present.

**But Colmán did construct its canonical pantheon position.** No earlier source located gives Ao Ao the complete Taú–Keraná genealogy or securely numbers it as sixth of seven. Colmán’s system is therefore not a simple transcription of an intact ancient pantheon.

### Is it indigenous Guaraní religion or mestizo folklore?

The safest answer is historically layered:

- The legend was recorded in a Guaraní-speaking regional environment containing indigenous, Paraguayan, Correntino, and Brazilian elements.
- Its native-language name and forest geography support substantial local formation.
- Its sheep form presupposes the post-contact world.
- Its sacred-Calvary palm is explicitly Christian.
- Its seven-monster genealogy is Colmán’s twentieth-century literary synthesis.

“Guaraní folklore” is appropriate as a regional-cultural label. “Unchanged precolonial Guaraní god” is not supported.

### Was it originally a peccary?

This is plausible but unproved. Packs, ferocity, forest habitat, digging behavior, and Colmán’s comparison to wild boars support the hypothesis. Later writers sometimes call Ao Ao a giant carnivorous peccary. Against treating this as settled fact:

- the earliest published description explicitly compares it to a sheep;
- no early source identifies a zoological species;
- “wild boar” language may describe pack behavior rather than anatomy;
- cryptozoological claims lack specimens or reliable documentation.

### Is the pindó motif Jesuit?

The Christian element is clear; the institutional origin is not. Palm Sunday, Calvary decoration, the flight into Egypt, and Tupã recoded as the Christian creator all show syncretism. Yet no Jesuit text has been found ordering or narrating the Ao Ao story. “Christianized during or after the mission period” is justified; “invented by Jesuits” is not.

### Does *Ayvu Rapyta* contain Ao Ao?

No secure evidence located supports that statement. Modern webpages sometimes cite Cadogan and Colmán together as if both record the same genealogy. Cadogan’s Mbyá creation corpus is structurally different. This is a **modern citation error** caused by treating all Guaraní-language traditions as a single canon.

### Fertility god or prolific monster?

Colmán calls Ao Ao a *hado de la fecundidad* because it produced numerous descendants. Later authors promote this to *dios de la fecundidad*. No cultic evidence confirms a fertility deity receiving worship. “Prolific monster interpreted as a fertility being” best preserves the evidentiary distinction.

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

Porãsy’s death is a literary self-sacrifice in Colmán’s cave narrative, not evidence of historical human sacrifice to Ao Ao. The seven monsters themselves are collectively killed by enclosure and burning. No archaeological, epigraphic, colonial, or ethnographic evidence documents people being sacrificed to Ao Ao.

### State of the evidence

The primary evidence is small:

1. Ambrosetti’s regional folklore text;
2. Colmán’s poem and glossary;
3. later oral collections, dictionaries, anthologies, educational texts, and artworks.

There are no inscriptions, manuscripts in an indigenous precolonial script, excavated sanctuaries, ancient images, or contemporary records of worship. The documentary history is therefore the history of a legend’s collection, literary incorporation, national canonization, and modern visualization.

### Evidentiary assessment

Ao Ao has a genuine documented folklore history, but not the deep, textually continuous divine biography often claimed online. Its firm historical sequence is:

**regional monster legend → Ambrosetti’s folklore record → Colmán’s seven-monster genealogy → folklore scholarship and national canonization → museum, school, and digital reinvention.**

The sheep-like hunter, terrible cry, human pursuit, tree-felling, and saving palm belong to the oldest recoverable narrative layer. “Sixth son,” fertility divinity, named siblings, cave incineration, and Pleiades transformation belong to Colmán’s 1929 literary system. Benevolent mountain protection, asexual reproduction, immortality, palm-wood weapons, and many anatomical particulars are later additions or modern inventions.
