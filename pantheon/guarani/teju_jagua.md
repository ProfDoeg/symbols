# Teju Jagua: Research Dossier

**Class:** A monstrous nonhuman being or nature-monster in early twentieth-century Apapocuva-Guaraní documentation; a dragon-like monster in Mbyá narrative; and, in Narciso R. Colmán’s Paraguayan literary system, the first of seven monstrous siblings born to Taû and Keraná. “God of caves” is a loose modern label, not the classification used by the earliest evidence.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Curt Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* (1914). Nimuendajú briefly lists *teju-jaguá*, glossed “Eidechsen-Jaguar” or “lizard-jaguar,” among nature-demons or fabulous animal beings in Apapocuva tradition. He does **not** give it seven canine heads, the Taû–Keraná genealogy, fruit guardianship, fiery eyes, or a cave at Yaguarón. The familiar seven-headed Paraguayan figure is securely documented in Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní mythological poem *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* (1929; reportedly presented in an earlier form in 1922), with Colmán’s Spanish translation and explanatory apparatus published as *Nuestros antepasados* in 1937.

## Evidentiary key

- **Secure early attestation:** Directly documented in an early linguistic or ethnographic source.
- **Indigenous regional variant:** Recorded from a specified Guaraní-speaking community, but not necessarily identical to Colmán’s character.
- **Learned/literary systematization:** A named author’s synthesis, elaboration, or mythopoetic composition.
- **Later folklore:** A field-collected or published popular narrative later than the foundational records.
- **Modern institutional reception:** Museums, public art, education, tourism, science, or contemporary cultural presentation.
- **Modern invention/unverified:** A recent assertion for which no supporting ethnographic, textual, archaeological, or historical evidence could be located.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

### Teju Jagua / Teju-jaguá / Tejú Jaguá / Teyú Yaguá

The name is written with considerable orthographic variation: **Teju Jagua**, **Tejú-Jaguá**, **Teju-jaguá**, **Teyú Yaguá**, Portuguese **Teiú-iaguá**, and older spellings reflecting differing Guaraní orthographies. It is composed of *teju* or *teyú*, “lizard,” “iguana,” or a large lizard, and *jagua*.

The second element requires historical caution:

- In modern Paraguayan Guaraní, *jagua* ordinarily means “dog.”
- Across the wider Tupí-Guaraní family, cognate forms commonly designate the jaguar or a fierce predatory animal. The modern scholarly survey by D. A. Aguilera Jiménez therefore gives the alternatives “iguana/lizard” plus “tiger [jaguar]/dog.”
- Nimuendajú translated the Apapocuva name as **“lizard-jaguar,”** not “lizard-dog.”
- Cadogan noted that *jagua*, when attached to animal names, could signify a monstrous, fierce, or transformed exemplar rather than literally adding a domestic dog to the creature. Comparable formations include *tatu-jaguá*, “armadillo-jaguar,” and other enhanced or monstrous animal names.

Consequently, “dog-lizard” is appropriate for the modern Paraguayan and Colmán-derived image, but it should not be retrojected automatically into the 1914 Apapocuva record. The paleontological paper naming *Teyujagua paradoxa* translated the name as “fierce lizard,” illustrating this broader semantic interpretation.

### Classification by source

- **Nimuendajú, 1914 — secure early attestation:** One of the fabulous animal beings associated with Apapocuva nature-demon narratives. Nimuendajú mentions it in a survey rather than preserving a complete story.
- **Cadogan, material published in *Ayvu Rapyta*, 1959 — indigenous regional variant:** A *tejujagua* is the dragon-like adversary in a Mbyá folktale. Cadogan explicitly glosses it as a monster comparable to a dragon.
- **Colmán, 1929/1937 — learned/literary systematization:** A “major myth,” monstrous son of Taû and Keraná, with the body of a colossal iguana and seven dog heads.
- **Later Paraguayan folklore:** A forest, yerba-grove, water, or cave monster, sometimes with one canine or jaguar head, sometimes seven heads; it can be a devourer rather than Colmán’s nearly immobile fruit-eater.
- **Modern tourism and popular reference:** Frequently called a god, spirit, guardian, or “lord” of caves, fruit, and treasure. “God” overstates the source evidence: no Teju Jagua priesthood, temple cult, liturgy, or divine office is securely documented.

### Jaguaru and Yaguarón

Colmán’s Spanish apparatus equates or closely associates Teju Jagua with **Jaguaru**, and places its residence at **Jaguaru/Yaguarón**. Later folklore sometimes treats Jaguaru as a separate aquatic or earth-undermining monster. The two names therefore should not be collapsed universally. Their equation is a feature of Colmán’s system and some later Paraguayan retellings, not a demonstrated pan-Guaraní identity.

No securely attested ancient epithet, cult title, sacred script form, or interpretatio-style equation with a foreign deity exists. Comparisons with Cerberus, the Hydra, Ladon, or European dragons are modern comparative analogies, not documented historical syncretisms.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Seventeenth-century evidence: absence

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639) is a Guaraní–Spanish lexicon drawn from the Jesuit mission world. It is indispensable evidence for historical Guaraní vocabulary, but the present search found no entry documenting **Teju Jagua as Colmán’s named seven-headed being**. Montoya’s *Conquista espiritual* of the same year likewise supplies essential evidence about Jesuit evangelization and religious conflict but no securely identifiable Taû–Keraná–Teju Jagua cycle.

This is a significant negative result. It does not prove that no comparable oral monster existed in the seventeenth century; it does establish that claims that Montoya documented the seven-headed firstborn, cave guardian, or fruit protector require evidence that has not been produced.

The same caution applies to Jesuit reduction records generally. Missionaries described indigenous religious specialists and translated Christian categories using Guaraní terms, but no colonial temple, festival, sacrifice, priesthood, or image of Teju Jagua has been identified.

### Curt Nimuendajú, 1914

In *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní*, Nimuendajú says that his recorded creation and destruction narratives did not exhaust Apapocuva mythology. Volumes, he observes, could be filled with stories of nature-demons, animals, and animal-humans. Among them appear:

- *teju-jaguá*, “lizard-jaguar”;
- *tatú-jaguá*, “armadillo-jaguar”;
- a long-clawed humanoid monster;
- cannibal ogres;
- dwarfs and other fabulous peoples.

This is the earliest secure occurrence located. It establishes the name and a broad monstrous-animal class. It establishes **none** of the following: seven heads, dog heads, descent from Taû and Keraná, brotherhood with six named monsters, cave guardianship, fruit, honey, fire-emitting eyes, buried gold, Yaguarón, or death with Porãsy.

### Narciso R. Colmán, 1922–1937

Colmán, writing under the name Rosicrán, reportedly presented the work that became *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* at the 1922 International Congress of Americanists in Rio de Janeiro. The Guaraní poem was printed in 1929 by Imprenta “El Arte,” in the series of the Sociedad Científica del Paraguay. A Spanish version with extensive explanations appeared as *Nuestros antepasados* in 1937.

Colmán’s work is the decisive source for the now-standard pantheon. It calls itself an ethnogenetic and mythological poem and belongs to modern Paraguayan Guaraní literature as well as to folklore history. It must not be treated as an anonymous pre-Columbian scripture.

In Colmán’s narrative:

1. Taû, the malevolent spirit, desires Keraná, daughter of Marangatu.
2. Taû struggles with Angatupyry, the good spirit, and obtains Keraná with Pytãjovái’s intervention.
3. Arasy curses the union.
4. After seven lunar months Keraná bears Teju Jagua, an iguana-dog with seven heads.
5. Six additional malformed sons follow at seven-month intervals.
6. Teju Jagua embodies enforced inaction: its many heads and gigantic body prevent effective movement.
7. Its eyes emit flames, but Tupã does not grant it the ability to exercise its potential ferocity.
8. It resides at Yaguarón and cannot travel to the final gathering.
9. Moñái brings Porãsy to Teju Jagua’s cave and later gathers the siblings there.
10. The monsters become drunk on chicha; Porãsy’s escape fails; Tumê Arandú and his people seal and burn the cavern.
11. Teju Jagua barks in desperation during the conflagration.
12. Colmán’s notes say the purified souls of the seven become the Pleiades or “Seven Little Goats.”

The recurrent seven—seven children, seven-month births, seven heads, seven-day events—is conspicuously programmatic. Later criticism appropriately treats it as part of Colmán’s literary architecture, not automatically as proof of an archaic Guaraní numerical theology.

### León Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959)

Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá* records a tale in which a Mbyá hero, aided by extraordinary dogs, encounters a *tejujagua*. The text describes combat: the dogs fight it repeatedly until it tires, and the human owner kills it. Cadogan glosses *teju-jagua* as a “monster comparable to the dragon.”

This is valuable independent evidence that the compound name denoted a dragon-like monster outside Colmán’s exact genealogy. It is not evidence that Cadogan’s Mbyá narrators recognized the seven sons of Taû and Keraná. Nor is *Ayvu Rapyta* authority for attributing Colmán’s whole pantheon to Mbyá creation theology.

### Other major Guaraní corpora

Cadogan’s creation hymns, his later *Ywyra Ñe’ery*, Nimuendajú’s Land Without Evil materials, Métraux’s Tupinambá synthesis, Schaden’s Guaraní ethnography, the works of Hélène and Pierre Clastres, Melià, and Sušnik provide the indispensable comparative setting: plural Guaraní cosmologies, creator beings, owners or masters of species and places, eschatology, sacred speech, shamanism, and colonial transformation.

They do not, however, furnish a securely shared canonical biography of Colmán’s seven-headed firstborn. Treating their detailed Indigenous cosmologies as chapters of a single Taû–Keraná pantheon would erase differences between Apapocuva, Mbyá, Paĩ-Tavyterã/Kaiowá, Ñandeva, and Paraguayan mestizo traditions.

A notable Kaiowá parallel does survive in modern ethnohistorical documentation: **Morro Teju Jagua** in the Ñande Ru Marangatu Indigenous Territory is described as representing a jaguar-lizard hybrid and as one guardian of the celestial dwelling of Ñande Ru Vusu. This is a living regional sacred-geographical complex, but it is not simply Colmán’s cave-bound son of Taû.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Form

- **1914 Apapocuva:** Only the compound “lizard-jaguar” and its placement among fabulous nature beings are secure.
- **Cadogan’s Mbyá tale:** A dragon-comparable monster that fights magical or heavenly dogs.
- **Colmán:** A colossal iguana or lizard with seven dog heads. Its bodily deformity makes movement difficult; its eyes cast flames; it barks.
- **Alto Paraná later folklore:** A gigantic lizard with a single dog or jaguar head and phosphorescent eyes. It dwells in deep forest, yerba groves, or water and devours victims.
- **Guairá later folklore:** A cave monster that gives terrifying nocturnal howls and devours people.
- **Modern standard illustration:** A long, low reptilian body bearing seven canine necks or heads. This is substantially Colmán-derived.

Claims that each head has a different rainbow color, that a carbuncle is embedded in its head, or that its scales shine because it rolls in gold occur in later popular retellings. They have not been established as early elements.

### Domains

**Caves and immobility — Colmán/later folklore:** The cave is both residence and narrative necessity: Teju Jagua’s inability to travel causes the fatal gathering to take place where it lives.

**Fruit and honey — later Colmán-derived folklore:** Dionisio González Torres distinguishes Colmán’s harmless, seven-headed fruit protector from field variants that are predatory. In the Colmán-derived account, Teju Jagua eats fruit and honey brought by Jasy Jatere. “Protector of fruit” is widely repeated in educational and museum texts but is not in the 1914 notice.

**Treasure and subterranean wealth — later folklore:** Museums and Yaguarón tourism call it protector of Guaraní treasure or hidden riches. This likely intersects the widespread Río de la Plata and Paraguayan *plata yvyguy*—buried-treasure—legend complex. No archaeological hoard, votive deposit, or colonial testimony proves a Teju Jagua treasure cult.

**Forest, yerba groves, and deep water — regional folklore:** González Torres’s Alto Paraná version belongs to dangerous-landscape folklore rather than Colmán’s domesticated fruit guardian.

### Limitations and powers

Colmán’s central paradox is fearsome form without effective agency. Teju Jagua’s eyes blaze and it is potentially ferocious, yet its heads and heavy reptilian body hinder it, and Tupã withholds full ferocity. Later regional accounts reverse this limitation and make it an active man-eater.

No reliable evidence assigns Teju Jagua a sacred color, weekday, planet, ritual number other than Colmán’s pervasive seven, dedicated plant, priestly emblem, oracle, or astronomical identity before Colmán’s transformation of all seven siblings into the Pleiades.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Colmán’s genealogy

**Learned/literary systematization, 1929/1937:**

- **Father:** Taû, a malevolent spirit.
- **Mother:** Keraná, daughter of Marangatu.
- **Maternal grandfather:** Marangatu.
- **Siblings:** Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao Ao, and Huichõ/Luisón.
- **Curser:** Arasy.
- **Restraining higher power:** Tupã.
- **Opponents:** Tumê Arandú, Porãsy, and the human community that destroys the monsters.

No consort, offspring, servants, priesthood, or mount is documented. The Apapocuva and Mbyá attestations do not supply this genealogy.

### Every documented named-being interaction

- **Taû — parentage:** Teju Jagua is Taû’s first monstrous son. *Colmán, Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* (1929; Spanish 1937). **Learned systematization.**
- **Keraná — parentage:** Keraná bears Teju Jagua after seven lunar months. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Arasy — curse:** Arasy’s curse on the union of Taû and Keraná determines the children’s monstrous form. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Tupã — limitation:** Tupã withholds Teju Jagua’s ability to exercise ferocity, leaving it comparatively harmless. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Moñái — brother and intermediary:** Moñái explains that the gathering must occur at Teju Jagua’s home because Teju Jagua cannot travel; he brings Porãsy there and summons the other siblings. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Porãsy — meeting and joint death:** Teju Jagua encounters Porãsy at Yaguarón, feels humiliated by its own seven-headed deformity before her beauty, joins the gathering around her, and dies when she orders the cave burned despite being trapped inside. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Tumê Arandú — destruction:** Tumê organizes the plan, seals the cavern, and burns Teju Jagua with its siblings and Porãsy. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Mbói Tu’ĩ — sibling and joint appearance:** Present with Teju Jagua at the drunken gathering and destruction. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Jasy Jatere — sibling, joint appearance, and feeder:** Present at the final gathering; later Colmán-derived folklore says Jasy Jatere brings Teju Jagua honey. Colmán, 1929/1937 for siblinghood and gathering; González Torres/Pérez-Maricevich, 1998, for the feeding formulation. **Learned systematization/later folklore.**
- **Kurupi — sibling and joint appearance:** Present at the gathering and destruction. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Ao Ao — sibling and joint appearance:** Present at the gathering and destruction. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **Luisón/Huichõ — sibling and joint appearance:** Youngest sibling, present at the gathering and destruction. Colmán, 1929/1937. **Learned systematization.**
- **The unnamed Tupã-associated dogs — combat:** In Cadogan’s Mbyá story, extraordinary dogs fight the *tejujagua*, tire it, and enable their human master to kill it. *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959). **Indigenous regional variant.**

There is no securely recorded interaction between Teju Jagua and Pombero, Ka’a Jarýi, Ñande Ru, Ñamandu, or other frequently listed Guaraní beings merely because modern charts place them in the same “pantheon.”

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The Apapocuva lizard-jaguar

**Secure early attestation, 1914.** Nimuendajú supplies only a catalogue notice. The evidentiary core is a fabulous *teju-jaguá* belonging to a larger body of animal and nature-demon stories he did not record fully. Any complete narrative reconstructed around it would be conjectural.

### B. The Mbyá dragon combat

**Indigenous regional variant, published 1959.** A Mbyá adventurer confronts a monster designated *tejujagua*. His remarkable dogs attack it; after repeated calls and renewed attacks, the dragon-like monster becomes exhausted and its owner kills it. Cadogan associates the dogs with the defenders of Tupã and cautiously compares them with Nimuendajú’s mythical Blue Jaguar/Dog.

This story shares a name and monstrous reptile-like conception with other Teju Jagua records. It lacks Colmán’s genealogy, seven heads, fruit, treasure, Yaguarón, and Porãsy.

### C. The birth of the seven monsters

**Learned systematization, Colmán, 1929/1937.** Taû desires and abducts or obtains the sleeping Keraná after conflict with Angatupyry. Arasy curses their offspring. Keraná bears seven malformed sons, one every seven months. Teju Jagua is first: a colossal lizard with seven dog heads.

Versions that say Angatupyry, rather than Arasy, pronounces the curse are later variant retellings and should not be silently combined. Likewise, modern summaries alternate between abduction, rape, seduction, and marriage. Colmán’s actual literary narrative must be cited for whichever formulation is used.

### D. The inactive guardian

**Colmán-derived elaboration.** Teju Jagua’s seven heads hinder movement. Its eyes blaze, but it cannot deploy its strength. It becomes the relatively harmless, fruit-eating lord of caves. Jasy Jatere supplies honey in later tellings.

The active, cannibalistic forest monster documented by González Torres is a genuine counter-version. It is not necessary to choose between them: the contradiction records the development of the figure.

### E. Porãsy and the burning cave

**Learned systematization, Colmán, 1929/1937.** Human society suffers under the seven monsters. Tumê Arandú devises a plan requiring a woman to lure them together. Porãsy volunteers and pretends to accept Moñái. Because Teju Jagua cannot move, the meeting is arranged at its cavern in Yaguarón. The siblings drink chicha and become intoxicated. When Porãsy attempts to leave, Moñái stops her. She calls to Tumê’s people to seal and burn the cave even though she remains inside. Teju Jagua barks, the siblings roar, and the earth trembles as they die.

This is a literary sacrificial death, not evidence that historical Guaraní communities sacrificed people to Teju Jagua. No human sacrifice **to** Teju Jagua is documented.

### F. Celestial transformation

Colmán’s explanatory material says the souls of the seven, purified by the fire, rise as the **Pleiades**, while Porãsy becomes the Morning Star in common retellings. This astral closure belongs to Colmán’s systematic poem. Recent online attempts to map each sibling onto a lunar phase lack identified primary or scholarly support and should be classified as **modern speculation**.

### G. Villa Rica and Luís Home

A later story collected or reproduced by Cardozo, preserved in material associated with Ramón Elías’s museum, places Teju Jagua in nineteenth-century Villa Rica. A wealthy and reclusive Portuguese, Luís Home, is rumored to keep a man-eating Teju Jagua in his castle, feeding it human flesh for an unknown future design. The rumor becomes so feared that people avoid the house at night.

This is **later local folklore**, joining the named monster to social suspicion surrounding an outsider and an unusual house. It is not part of Colmán’s primordial genealogy, though later compilers present both.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, prayer, hymn, oracle, dedicated offering, theophoric personal name, cult economy, or excavated votive image of Teju Jagua has been securely documented.

This absence matters because modern encyclopedic language often calls it a “god of caves.” The record instead consists of:

- ethnographic mention;
- oral narrative;
- literary mythography;
- sacred or storied geography;
- museum sculpture;
- public murals and monuments;
- educational folklore;
- contemporary cultural memory.

Claims circulating online about clay Teju Jagua amulets carried by fishermen, maternal offerings, transition ceremonies, ancient chants, or formal water-respect rituals are unsupported by the cited ethnographic corpus. They appear in recent synthetic storytelling sites and should be treated as **modern inventions unless independent documentation emerges**.

### Sacred geography

- **Yaguarón, Paraguay:** Colmán locates the cave and fiery death there. Later local tradition attaches Teju Jagua to the hill, caves, treasure, and landscape. Today the association is sustained by municipal tourism, murals, and sculpture.
- **Alto Paraná and Guairá:** González Torres records predatory forest, water, and cave variants.
- **Ñande Ru Marangatu, Mato Grosso do Sul:** Kaiowá testimony identifies Morro Teju Jagua as a jaguar-lizard entity and guardian associated with the celestial dwelling of Ñande Ru Vusu. The hill forms part of living Indigenous religious geography and land relations. This evidence should be allowed to stand in its own Kaiowá context rather than absorbed into Paraguay’s seven-sibling scheme.

### Christianity and suppression

Jesuit missions imposed Christian religious institutions, condemned Indigenous ritual authorities, reorganized settlement, and translated Christian concepts through Guaraní. That broad colonial transformation is documented. No source located records a discrete campaign against a Teju Jagua cult, destruction of its temple, or conversion of its sanctuary. Such statements would exceed the evidence.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1639: unrecoverable prehistory

Large predatory or hybrid animal beings probably belonged to a broad Guaraní narrative world, but Teju Jagua’s specific antiquity cannot be dated. There is no archaeological image securely identified as this being.

### 1639–1900: colonial documentary silence

Montoya’s lexicon and missionary history preserve extensive linguistic and religious evidence but not the recognizable seven-headed character. Absence is not proof of nonexistence; it prevents claims of demonstrated continuity.

### 1914: Apapocuva documentation

Nimuendajú records *teju-jaguá*, “lizard-jaguar,” among fabulous animal/nature beings. This is the earliest secure named attestation found.

### 1922–1937: national literary systematization

Colmán integrates Teju Jagua into an ambitious Guaraní ethnogenetic poem. The work gives the being its familiar genealogy, seven dog heads, fiery eyes, disability, Yaguarón home, and death with the other six monsters. Published first in Guaraní in 1929 and in Spanish in 1937, it profoundly shapes later Paraguayan education and popular mythology.

This does not mean Colmán invented every name or motif. Nimuendajú proves that *teju-jaguá* existed independently by 1914. It means the evidence for the **complete canonical family system** begins with Colmán.

### 1950s onward: ethnography and divergent folklore

Cadogan publishes a distinct Mbyá dragon-combat story. Later folklorists record canine-headed, jaguar-headed, cave-dwelling, aquatic, harmless, and cannibalistic forms. These variants demonstrate continuing oral reshaping rather than one stable canonical creature.

### Late twentieth century: museum canon

Artist and collector Ramón Elías represented Teju Jagua among Paraguay’s mythic beings at the Museo Mitológico in Capiatá. The museum, inaugurated in 1979, helped convert literary and oral descriptions into durable national iconography. Its Teju Jagua is a lizard with a dog head, first child of Taû and Keraná and protector of treasure.

### Twenty-first century: education, tourism, land testimony, and science

Educational materials in Argentina and Paraguay standardize the seven-headed dog-lizard and cave/fruit guardianship. Yaguarón’s Paseo de los Mitos, begun in 2016, uses murals, multilingual interpretation, and a monumental Teju Jagua near the hill.

In 2016 paleontologists named the Early Triassic archosauromorph **_*Teyujagua paradoxa*_** after the being. The taxon is represented by a well-preserved skull from Brazil’s Sanga do Cabral Formation. The naming is modern scientific commemoration, not evidence that the fossil inspired the myth.

Kaiowá testimony concerning Morro Teju Jagua also shows that the name remains embedded in living Indigenous sacred geography outside the Paraguayan nationalized monster cycle.

No reliable demographic evidence establishes organized contemporary worship of Teju Jagua as a deity. Cultural celebration, storytelling, sacred-land testimony, and heritage presentation should not automatically be called “neopagan worship.”

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Art and public heritage

- Ramón Elías’s sculptural museum representation helped define a visual canon.
- Yaguarón’s municipal monument by Luis Delgado and Elvio Jara and the Paseo de los Mitos transform Teju Jagua into a place-branding and heritage figure.
- Contemporary Paraguayan artist Julia Isídrez created a ceramic work titled *Teju Jagua* (2023), now catalogued by the Syracuse University Art Museum.
- Schoolbooks present it bilingually as part of regional cultural education.

### Literature and entertainment

Teju Jagua increasingly appears in fantasy art, bestiaries, online stories, role-playing contexts, and mythology compilations. These treatments frequently add psychology, invented rites, colored heads, amulets, moral lessons, or ecology symbolism absent from the historical record.

The most consequential modern reinvention remains Colmán’s own: not a fraudulent antiquarian forgery, but an openly authored mythopoetic and ethnogenetic synthesis that later popular culture often mistakes for an untouched pre-contact canon.

### Esotericism, occultism, and neopaganism

The dedicated searches located contemporary demonological and occult web profiles, but no historical Teju Jagua current in Renaissance hermetism, ceremonial magic, theosophy, the Golden Dawn, traditional grimoires, or documented twentieth-century occult orders.

No established reconstructionist Teju Jagua liturgy, initiatory lineage, or sizable organized cult was found. Modern personal invocations may exist, but they cannot be projected backward as ancient practice.

### Political uses

Colmán’s work belongs to the twentieth-century elevation of Guaraní as a foundation of Paraguayan national identity. This is cultural nationalism in the broad historical sense, not evidence of extremist appropriation. No documented fascist, racial-supremacist, terrorist, or comparable extremist adoption of Teju Jagua was found.

### Commercialization

Tourism, craft production, illustrated books, websites, and merchandise use the image. The heritage economy privileges the spectacular seven-headed form because it is visually recognizable, further marginalizing the simpler lizard-jaguar and one-headed predatory variants.

## 9. Contested material and historiography

### Was Teju Jagua invented by Colmán?

**No, not as a name or general monster:** Nimuendajú documented *teju-jaguá* in 1914, before Colmán’s publication, and Cadogan later recorded an independent Mbyá narrative.

**Possibly yes in much of its canonical biography:** The first securely identified source for the Taû–Keraná genealogy, status as first of seven, seven dog heads, enforced immobility, fiery eyes, Yaguarón residence, and collective death is Colmán’s authored poem. Until earlier documentation is produced, that configuration should be called **Colmán’s literary systematization**, not ancient pan-Guaraní canon.

### Dog or jaguar?

Both readings are historically defensible in different contexts. “Dog” fits modern Paraguayan Guaraní and Colmán’s explicit *siete cabezas de perro*. “Jaguar,” “fierce animal,” or a monstrous intensifier better fits Nimuendajú’s translation and broader Tupí-Guaraní semantics. The name’s history embodies the shift.

### One head or seven?

- Seven dog heads: Colmán and Colmán-derived national mythology.
- One dog or jaguar head: later Alto Paraná and museum descriptions.
- No recorded head count: Nimuendajú’s brief notice and Cadogan’s dragon story.

The single-headed form should not automatically be dismissed as degeneration; it may preserve a narrative stream independent of Colmán’s numerological construction.

### Guardian or devourer?

- Harmless, fruit-eating cave lord: Colmán-derived tradition.
- Cannibalistic forest/water/cave monster: González Torres’s regional reports and the Villa Rica legend.
- Combat adversary: Cadogan’s Mbyá story.

These are genuine incompatible roles. A composite “benevolent guardian who sometimes eats trespassers” conceals rather than explains the sources.

### Human sacrifice

Porãsy’s voluntary death in Colmán is a literary human sacrifice for communal deliverance. It is not sacrifice offered in worship of Teju Jagua. No archaeological or historical evidence for human sacrifice to the being was located.

### Syncretism

The monster resembles dragons, Cerberus, the Hydra, and Ladon, while the number seven has strong European and Christian literary associations. Resemblance alone does not establish borrowing. No source documents a Jesuit deliberately translating a European dragon into Teju Jagua or equating it with a named Christian demon. Comparative influence is possible in Paraguay’s long colonial contact zone but remains a **scholarly hypothesis**, not demonstrated transmission.

### Suppression

Christianization profoundly transformed Guaraní religious life. A specific Teju Jagua cult suppressed by Jesuits is unproven because no such cult is independently attested.

### Unsubstantiated recent claims

The following should be rejected provisionally as modern inventions or unsupported embellishments:

- ancient Teju Jagua temples or priesthoods;
- fishermen’s clay amulets;
- formal offerings by mothers;
- initiation ceremonies centered on surviving its gaze;
- an ancient ritual song;
- a traditional correspondence to one lunar phase;
- a continuous occult order;
- fixed rainbow-colored heads as an early attribute;
- identification of archaeological images as Teju Jagua without provenance or iconographic argument.

## 11. Sources and assessment

The most important primary or near-primary witnesses are Nimuendajú’s 1914 ethnography, Colmán’s 1929 Guaraní poem and 1937 Spanish edition, and Cadogan’s 1959 Mbyá texts. Montoya is crucial negative and linguistic evidence. González Torres preserves explicit regional contrast; the Kaiowá land report records a living but distinct sacred-geographical form.

The central historiographical conclusion is therefore:

> Teju Jagua is neither simply an invention of 1929 nor securely an unchanged pre-Columbian seven-headed deity. A lizard-jaguar or dragon-like Guaraní monster is independently documented; the now-dominant genealogy and iconography are chiefly the product of Colmán’s twentieth-century mythopoetic system, subsequently naturalized through folklore books, schools, museums, tourism, and the internet.
