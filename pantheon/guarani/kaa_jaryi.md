# Ka'a Jarýi: Research Dossier

**Class:** Female tutelary spirit or *jára/jarýi* (“owner,” “mistress,” “grandmother,” “protectress”) of yerba mate and, in some later Paraguayan variants, of the forest generally. “Goddess of the yerbales” is a common literary and tourist translation, but no priesthood, temple cult, fixed pantheon, or pre-Columbian divine genealogy is documented.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Juan Bautista Ambrosetti, “La Leyenda de la Caá-Yarí (Abuela de la Yerba),” published in Buenos Aires in 1898. Ambrosetti said he reconstructed it from information supplied by Eloy Rodríguez, a yerba producer at Tacurú-Pucú on the Paraguayan bank of the Upper Paraná, supplemented by fragments elicited directly from *mineros*, the workers who cut wild yerba. The record therefore securely establishes a workers’ belief in the Upper Paraná yerbales in the late nineteenth century. It does **not** establish the antiquity Ambrosetti attributed to an unidentified “Indian” core.

---

## Evidentiary labels used

- **Securely attested:** Directly documented in a dated text, ethnographic record, object, or named testimony.
- **Early folklore attestation:** Part of the earliest recoverable oral-folklore record, without proof of pre-contact antiquity.
- **Christianized folklore:** A traditional or folkloric account using God, saints, churches, Holy Week, or other Christian materials.
- **Regional variant:** A separately recorded local form, not automatically combinable with other versions.
- **Later literary or folkloric elaboration:** A twentieth- or twenty-first-century reshaping of older material.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An interpretation offered by a scholar, rather than something stated by an informant or primary text.
- **Modern institutional retelling:** A version circulated by schools, tourist agencies, governments, museums, or the yerba industry.
- **Modern invention or unverified claim:** A proposition that cannot be traced to the older sources cited for it.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

**[Early folklore attestation, 1898]** Ambrosetti’s Caá-Yarí is a beautiful, young, blond woman who lives invisibly in the wild yerbales. A male yerba worker can contract a secret, exclusive relationship with her. She tests his courage, assists his harvest, increases the apparent weight of his load and therefore his wages, and kills him if he breaks his promise by having relations with another woman. She is simultaneously the “owner” of the plant, an invisible supernatural work-partner, a forest bride, and an explanation for both extraordinary productivity and otherwise unexplained deaths in the yerbales.

She is not one of Narciso R. Colmán’s seven children of Taú and Keraná. That group—Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao Ao, and Luisõ—was consolidated as a literary pantheon in *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929). Folding Ka'a Jarýi into that genealogy would be a modern systematization without primary support.

### Name forms

Documented spellings include:

- **Ka'a Jarýi** — modern Paraguayan Guaraní orthography.
- **Ka’a Jaryi / Ka'a Jaryi** — common digital spellings without full diacritics.
- **Caá-Yarí / Caa-Yarí / Caá Yarí / Caa Yari** — Spanish-era and Argentine spellings.
- **Ka'a Yary / Ka'a Yaryi / Kaa yarihi / Caá Yaryhi** — variants reported in later folklore inventories.
- **Ka'a Jára / Ka’ajára** — “owner/mistress of the yerba/forest,” sometimes treated as a synonym.
- **Ka'a Póra / Caá-Porá** — sometimes equated with her, but historically also the name of a broader forest apparition or game-owner; the equation must not be treated as universal.

### Etymology

**Ka'a.** Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639) documents *caá* with the semantic territory of plant growth, herb and forest. In modern Paraguayan Guaraní, *ka'a* normally denotes yerba mate, while *ka'aguy* specifies forest. The older semantic breadth helps explain why Ka'a Jarýi can oscillate between “mistress of yerba mate” and “mistress of the forest.” Montoya’s lexical evidence establishes the word, not the supernatural figure.

**Jarýi.** In modern Guaraní, *jarýi* means “grandmother.” In names for nonhuman custodians it can also carry the relational sense “female owner,” “mistress,” “ancestress,” or “protectress.” Thus:

- literal domestic gloss: “grandmother of the yerba”;
- functional gloss: “mistress/owner of the yerba”;
- literary glosses: “goddess of the yerbales,” “fairy of the yerbales.”

“Fairy” is an analogy imported from European vocabulary; it is not a Guaraní classification. “Goddess” is defensible as loose English or Spanish shorthand but implies a more formal pantheon than the evidence warrants.

A modern Guaraní dictionary confirms *jarýi* as “grandmother” and *ka'ay* as mate. The compound’s supernatural sense depends on the wider Guaraní category of owners or guardians, not on the ordinary dictionary meanings alone.

### Epithets and titles

- **Abuela de la Yerba** — Ambrosetti’s 1898 subtitle; securely attested.
- **Dueña de la yerba / de los yerbales** — the best functional translation of her earliest documented role.
- **Diosa de los yerbales** — later literary and public-culture title.
- **Protectora de la yerba mate** — later explanatory formulation.
- **Hada de los yerbales** — Europeanizing modern folkloric gloss.
- **Protectora del monte / de la naturaleza** — prominent in later Paraguayan ecological retellings, where she guards trees, springs and forest animals rather than yerba workers alone.

There is no securely attested sacred script, sigil, ritual epithet, liturgical name, theophoric personal-name series, or ancient title.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The 1898 text

Ambrosetti’s “Fantasmas del bosque” states that the legend was exclusive to the Paraguayan yerbales and that its protagonists were *mineros*. He identified Eloy Rodríguez of Tacurú-Pucú as a principal source and said he assembled the remainder from fragments told by workers who regarded the subject with “great superstitious respect.” This is unusually useful provenance for nineteenth-century folklore, although Ambrosetti did not name the individual workers, record the Guaraní wording, or preserve performance contexts.

The account distinguishes two propositions:

1. God turned an innocent young woman into the yerba-mate plant, which therefore sprouts again after cutting.
2. The workers instead said God made her **owner of the yerba**, and that she still existed in the yerbales.

That internal correction is crucial: even within the earliest record, transformation into the plant and appointment as its owner were competing versions.

Ambrosetti called the legend “Indian in origin” but modified under Jesuit domination. He offered no pre-Jesuit text demonstrating that conclusion. He further judged the Christian opening to be secondary. These are **1898 scholarly inferences**, not separately documented chronological layers.

The piece was later incorporated into Ambrosetti’s posthumous *Supersticiones y leyendas: Región Misionera, Valles Calchaquíes, Las Pampas* (1917), under “Fantasmas de la selva misionera.”

### Earlier sources and the problem of silence

**Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, 1639.** *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* supplies indispensable vocabulary for *ka'a* and other Guaraní categories. *Conquista espiritual* documents Jesuit encounters, conversion, reductions and indigenous religion. Neither work has been shown here to contain an identifiable Ka'a Jarýi narrative. Consequently, Montoya is evidence for language, yerba, mission history and the possibilities of later Christianization—not an attestation of this named being.

**Jesuit reduction records.** Yerba mate became economically important in the reductions, and Jesuit records document its collection, cultivation, trade and labor organization. They do not, in the presently located evidence, document a Caá-Yarí cult, shrine, priesthood or myth. The absence is not proof that no oral plant-owner belief existed, but it blocks claims that the named goddess is securely recorded in the seventeenth-century missions.

**Curt Nimuendajú, 1914.** *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* documents Apapocúva creation, destruction and Land Without Evil traditions. No securely identified Ka'a Jarýi episode has been located in it. Its importance is comparative: it shows that named Guaraní-speaking communities possessed complex cosmologies that should not be collapsed into the later mestizo Paraguayan folklore roster.

**León Cadogan, 1950.** In “El culto al árbol y a los animales entre los Guaraní,” Cadogan explicitly described Ka'a Yary as the spirit of the yerba-mate tree, surviving in Paraguayan mixed-population folklore. He summarized the same moral economy as Ambrosetti: she helps a faithful worker but punishes infidelity with death. This is the first located scholarly interpretation connecting the figure to a broader Guaraní complex of plant and animal owners.

**Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959).** This fundamental Mbyá corpus contains sacred narratives, ritual language and ethnobotanical materials, including medicinal use of yerba mate. It should not be cited as though it contains Ambrosetti’s blond forest-bride narrative unless an exact passage is supplied. Modern adaptations sometimes combine Cadogan’s Mbyá creation material with a separate yerba-origin episode; one published adaptation expressly says the yerba episode came instead from *Ayvu Rendy Vera* (1984). That admission demonstrates how composite “Guaraní myths” can be manufactured from distinct corpora.

**Cadogan, *Ywyra Ñe'ery* (1971).** This corpus contains more everyday Mbyá discourse and practical knowledge than *Ayvu Rapyta*. Again, no verified passage has been located that reproduces the Caá-Yarí pact narrative. It is valuable for the surrounding concepts of trees, owners, ritual and Mbyá life, not automatic proof of this particular legend.

**Later collections.** Relevant twentieth-century repositories include Susana Chertudi’s Argentine folktale work, Berta Vidal de Battini’s national oral-narrative collections, Félix Coluccio’s supernatural-being inventories, Mauricio Cardozo Ocampo, Ramón Indalecio Cardozo, González Torres, Domingo Aguilera and Pedro Encina Ramos. The later texts broaden Ka'a Jarýi from yerba-owner to environmental guardian, change her hair and appearance, and sometimes detach her from the mensú pact.

### Corpus limitations

No inscription, manuscript in Guaraní from the colonial period, archaeological image, pre-contact object, hymn, prayer book, sacred recitation, or early theophoric personal name securely identifies Ka'a Jarýi. Her documentary corpus begins as collected oral folklore in print.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Early folklore attestation]**

- Wild yerba-mate stands of the Upper Paraná.
- Male workers who cut and carry yerba.
- Harvest productivity and the weight of the *raído* or *rairo*.
- Sexual fidelity within an exclusive supernatural pact.
- Punishment by death for betrayal.

**[Later regional folklore]**

- Trees, springs, streams and forest animals.
- Restraint in hunting and gathering.
- Punishment of hunters who kill wastefully or foul water.
- Protection of orphaned animals.
- The forest as an ecological totality.

The ecological guardian is consistent with a wider Guaraní owner-spirit framework, but the detailed conservation narrative is documented in later collections rather than Ambrosetti’s 1898 text.

### Form and appearance

**[1898]** Young, beautiful and blond. Visible only to her contracted worker; invisible to others.

**[Later Paraguayan variant]** A beautiful maiden with golden, curly hair falling to her waist, surrounded by floral fragrance. She can walk above the forest, cover great distances quickly and transform into many things. Hanging vines, fruits and epiphytes on a fallen tree may actually be her hair and clothing.

**[Modern Misiones version]** Copper-colored hair.

**[Other modern summaries]** Silver hair.

These colors are mutually inconsistent. No one hair color is canonical. Blond or blue-eyed depictions may reflect the racial aesthetics of regional mestizo and immigrant society; no early indigenous-language description establishes those traits as pre-contact.

### Powers

- Conjures snakes, toads, wild beasts and other forest terrors to test a prospective partner.
- Induces sleep while completing the worker’s harvest.
- Helps lift and carry loads.
- Makes the load weigh more at the scale by sitting invisibly on it.
- Kills an unfaithful partner.
- In later versions, shapeshifts and creates phantom animals.
- Makes bullets and knives ineffective against a phantom boar.
- Protects springs, young animals, trees and restrained hunters.
- Leaves no footprints and cannot be pierced by thorns or bitten by animals.

### Emblems and objects

Her primary emblem is the yerba-mate plant, *Ilex paraguariensis*. Associated objects in the earliest narrative are:

- a yerba bush bearing the worker’s written name and appointment time;
- the *raído/rairo*, a large bundle of cut yerba;
- the church where the initial Holy Week vow is made;
- the balance on which the load is weighed.

There are no securely traditional colors, numbers, planets, feast calendars, weapons, statues, ritual costumes or sacred animals. Friday or Holy Week derives from the pact ritual; it should not be expanded into an ancient holy day.

### Iconographic record

No pre-Columbian or colonial image can securely be identified as Ka'a Jarýi. Her visual record consists of modern book illustrations, educational art, theater, museum sculpture projects and commercial or tourist representations. A beautiful woman holding a yerba branch is a modern illustrative convention, not an ancient diagnostic iconography.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The earliest documented version gives no divine parents, consort, children or siblings. She begins as the unnamed daughter of a poor old man and his wife. God renders her immortal by transformation or appointment.

Later versions name the daughter **Yarí** and sometimes the father **Yar**. This is probably narrative wordplay or an etiological back-formation from the title, not an independently documented ancient genealogy.

Another later cycle substitutes Tupã or his messenger for the Christian God. Yet another makes the Moon and Cloud the givers of yerba and appoints the hunter’s daughter its guardian. These are variants, not pieces of a single family tree.

She is not securely a relative of Pombero, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Luisõ, Taú, Keraná, Angatupyry or Colmán’s seven monsters.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **God / Ñandejára —** Rewards the poor old man’s hospitality by making his daughter immortal: in one wording he transforms her into yerba mate; in the workers’ correction he makes her owner of the yerba. Source: Ambrosetti, “La Leyenda de la Caá-Yarí,” 1898. **[Early attestation; Christianized folklore]**

- **Saint Peter —** Travels with God and Saint John; when asked how the hospitable old man should be treated, agrees that he should be generously rewarded. Source: Ambrosetti, 1898. **[Early attestation; Christianized folklore]**

- **Saint John —** Travels with God and Saint Peter and likewise endorses rewarding the host. Source: Ambrosetti, 1898. **[Early attestation; Christianized folklore]**

- **Tupã —** In later Argentine and Paraguayan origin narratives, visits or sends a traveler to the old man and daughter, produces the yerba plant, teaches its preparation, and appoints Yarí its divine protectress. The Argentine Ministry of Tourism version preserves this form. **[Later folkloric elaboration / regional variant]**

- **Jasy/Yasí, the Moon —** In a widespread twentieth-century school and industry version, descends to the forest as a woman, is rescued from a jaguar by a hunter, gives yerba mate in gratitude, and in some versions appoints the hunter’s daughter Caá-Yarí. A traceable printed version is Neli Garrido de Rodríguez’s *Leyendas Argentinas* (1981). **[Later literary/educational variant]**

- **Araí, the Cloud —** Accompanies Jasy to earth and participates in rewarding the hunter. Same modern moon-and-cloud cycle. **[Later literary/educational variant]**

- **Tume Arandu —** Pedro Encina Ramos’s 2005 presentation places Ka'a Jarýi in an ancient era when Tume Arandu, represented as Tupã’s envoy, taught crops. It does not provide a specific direct encounter between them. **[Modern folkloric systematization; no documented interaction]**

- **Pombero —** Encina Ramos compares their behavior: both help friends and retaliate against disloyalty. Comparison is not an episode. **[No documented interaction]**

- **Ka'a Póra/Caá-Porá —** Ambrosetti proposes a regional relationship or transformation of names between Caá-Yarí and Brazilian Caá-Porá. He does not record the two meeting. **[Scholarly comparison; no interaction]**

No securely attested interaction with Taú, Keraná, Angatupyry, the seven monsters, Ñande Ru, Ñamandú, or other beings in the Mbyá and Apapocúva creation corpora has been established.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The hospitable old man: God and the saints

**[Earliest attested form, 1898; Christianized folklore]**

God travels on earth with Saint Peter and Saint John. After a difficult day they reach the home of a very poor old man, his wife and their young daughter. The father has withdrawn with the girl into deep forest so that she may remain innocent. Although he owns almost nothing, he kills his only chicken to feed the strangers.

God asks the saints how such generosity should be rewarded. They answer that the old man deserves a great reward. God announces that the daughter will be made immortal.

Ambrosetti then gives two outcomes:

- God transforms her into the yerba-mate plant, whose immortality is demonstrated by its resprouting after it is cut.
- According to the workers, God makes her the owner of yerba mate; she continues living invisibly in the yerbales.

The myth explains vegetative regeneration, rewards hospitality and attaches a Christian moral drama to an already economically and culturally central plant.

Ambrosetti considered the Christian opening a later addition, but he did not possess an earlier recorded version against which to prove that stratigraphy.

### B. The pact with the yerba worker

**[Earliest attested form, 1898]**

A *minero* seeking her help waits for Holy Week. If near a settlement, he enters its church and promises:

- to live permanently in the forest;
- to “become friends” or enter a relationship with Caá-Yarí;
- to have no dealings with any other woman.

He then leaves a paper bearing his name and the hour of a future meeting in a yerba bush. At the appointment, she sends snakes, toads and dangerous forest animals against him. These are a courage test. If he remains composed, she appears as a beautiful blond young woman and receives his renewed oath.

Thereafter the worker falls into a sweet sleep while cutting yerba. She prepares an enormous *raído*, helps hold it from behind during the carry, and invisibly sits upon it at the scales. Because workers are paid by weight, his earnings increase.

Absolute secrecy surrounds the pact. If he has relations with another woman, Caá-Yarí kills him. When a strong, taciturn worker dies in the forest, companions whisper that he betrayed her and she took revenge.

This cycle contains no named god besides the Christian setting of the church vow. Its social world is the late nineteenth-century extractive frontier: isolated male labor, payment by weight, punishing loads, debt, danger, illness and unexplained death.

### C. Tupã, Yarí and her aged father

**[Later Argentine-Paraguayan regional variant]**

An aged Guaraní man can no longer accompany his migrating community. His daughter Yarí remains with him. A strange traveler arrives; despite their poverty they serve him forest food, often roast agouti and sometimes other delicacies. The traveler proves to be Tupã or an envoy of Tupã.

The father wishes for renewed strength or for a means to continue receiving guests. The visitor causes a new plant to grow, teaches the drying and infusion of its leaves, and appoints Yarí the plant’s eternal guardian, Caá-Yarí. Some tellings appoint the father her custodian.

This form removes Saint Peter and Saint John, replaces the Christian God with Tupã, names the daughter and embeds mate in hospitality, reciprocal generosity and relief of fatigue. Although regularly marketed as an “ancient Guaraní legend,” its secure printed history is later than Ambrosetti’s Christian form.

### D. Jasy and Araí: the Moon’s gift

**[Later literary and educational variant]**

Jasy/Yasí, the Moon, wishes to see the forest described by her companion Araí, the evening cloud. They descend as young women. A jaguar stalks or attacks them, but an old Guaraní hunter shoots it or drives it away. In fuller versions he takes them home, where his family shares its last maize cakes.

The visitors return to the sky and reveal themselves in the hunter’s dream. In gratitude they create yerba mate, instruct him to roast and prepare its leaves, and declare that the drink will restore the weary, comfort the lonely and strengthen friendship. Some versions make the hunter’s daughter the plant’s protectress and call her Caá-Yarí; others never introduce Ka'a Jarýi and concern only the origin of mate.

A traceable source for a full Spanish version is Neli Garrido de Rodríguez, *Leyendas Argentinas* (1981). Modern school sheets, tourism sites and the yerba industry have made it one of the best-known versions. Its popularity does not prove pre-contact age.

### E. The guardian of animals and waters

**[Later Paraguayan folklore]**

A beautiful golden-haired maiden protects trees, springs and small forest animals. She dislikes cruel hunters, water-polluters and those who kill without need. She may create the illusion of a herd of wild pigs, drawing a wasteful hunter deep into the forest. The final boar cannot be harmed by bullets or knife; it turns, with fiery eyes and flame from its nostrils, leaving the hunter half-mad and his dogs permanently terrified.

She helps restrained hunters and gatherers, buries abandoned carcasses and tends orphaned animals. Some informants say she eats the abandoned dead; others explicitly deny it and say she only buries them. The published Spanish-Guaraní collection preserves the disagreement rather than harmonizing it.

This figure is closer to a generalized forest-owner than Ambrosetti’s erotic yerba-worker partner. It may reflect convergence with Ka'a Póra, broader *jára* traditions, or later environmental reframing.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult status

There is no evidence of a formal cult in the institutional sense:

- no temple;
- no permanent shrine;
- no priesthood;
- no public festival exclusively hers;
- no sacrificial calendar;
- no documented idol or ancient cult image;
- no temple economy;
- no oracle;
- no theophoric dynasty.

Calling her worship a “cult” is therefore potentially misleading. The documented practice is a private workers’ pact within vernacular religion.

### Pact ritual

The 1898 sequence is the only detailed ritual securely attached to her:

1. Wait for Holy Week.
2. Enter a church if one is accessible.
3. Promise forest residence and sexual exclusivity.
4. Place one’s name and return time in a yerba bush.
5. Keep the appointment.
6. Withstand terrifying apparitions.
7. Renew the oath before the manifested spirit.
8. Maintain secrecy and fidelity.

This is syncretic in form: church, Holy Week and Christian oath coexist with a personalized nonhuman forest owner. Ambrosetti’s evidence places the ritual among workers, not in Jesuit liturgy.

### Offerings and sacrifice

No food, animal or human sacrifice to Ka'a Jarýi is documented in the earliest account. The only animal killed there is the old man’s chicken, served hospitably to God and the saints—not sacrificed to her.

No evidence was located for:

- human sacrifice;
- temple prostitution;
- institutional sacred marriage;
- blood offerings;
- a required sexual act during the pact;
- possession rites;
- divination in her name.

A modern website mentions a “pact of blood,” but this detail is absent from Ambrosetti and should be classified as **unverified modern elaboration** unless an earlier field record is produced.

### Sexual relationship

The terms *amigarse*, fidelity, jealousy and forest marriage imply an intimate or erotic supernatural partnership. Later accounts make the sexual dimension more explicit. It should not be redescribed as “temple prostitution”: there is neither temple nor evidence of organized sexual service. The closest comparative category is the supernatural spouse or forest mistress.

### Material and economic setting

Ambrosetti defines the *minero* as the worker who directly stripped branches from wild yerba. His *rairo* was a square leather-net bundle carried on the back. Ordinary loads weighed roughly eight to ten arrobas—approximately 80–100 kilograms—while Ka'a Jarýi supposedly prepared 18–20 arrobas and increased its apparent weight further.

This economic mechanism is not incidental. Her miracle directly affects piece-rate income.

The mensú system flourished in the Upper Paraná extractive frontier from the late nineteenth into the early twentieth century. Workers were recruited by advances, transported to remote camps, indebted to company stores and frequently constrained by violence. Historical research describes weak state enforcement, employer complicity and repeated denunciations of abuse. The legend’s lethal forest bride cannot be reduced to a labor protest, but it belongs to that world of isolation, masculine camps, extreme physical burden and deaths that employers or medicine did not necessarily explain.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1639: possible conceptual antecedents, no named attestation

Guaraní-speaking peoples possessed complex relations with plants, animals, ancestors and nonhuman owners. Yerba mate had indigenous cultural and medicinal importance. That setting makes a plant-owner tradition plausible, but **Ka'a Jarýi as a named individual is not securely documented before European contact**.

### 1639–1767: Jesuit lexicography, conversion and yerba production

Montoya documents the language and early mission environment. Reductions made yerba mate a major economic resource, and Christianity transformed the religious vocabulary of the region. Yet no located Jesuit text names Ka'a Jarýi. The later story of God and the saints is evidence of Christianized folklore, not proof that Jesuits deliberately created it.

### After the Jesuit expulsion to the late nineteenth century

Yerba collection continued through changing colonial and national regimes. Wild yerbales of Paraguay, Misiones and adjoining Brazil supported mobile, dangerous extraction. The social type later called the *mensú* or *minero* developed in this frontier.

### 1898: first secure textual emergence

Ambrosetti publishes the hospitable-old-man story and the workers’ pact. This is the documentary beginning of Ka'a Jarýi’s recoverable history.

### 1917: canonization in an Argentine folklore volume

The narrative enters *Supersticiones y leyendas*, becoming available to folklorists, teachers and writers beyond the Upper Paraná camps.

### 1920s–1930s: pantheon-building around her

Colmán’s 1929 *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* created an influential literary system around Taú, Keraná and seven monstrous children. Ka'a Jarýi remained outside it, but later popular “Guaraní pantheon” lists often flatten Colmán’s characters, indigenous sacred narratives, mestizo saints and regional spirits into a single timeless mythology. That composite is historically inaccurate.

### 1950: Cadogan’s survival interpretation

Cadogan classifies Ka'a Yary as the spirit of the yerba-mate tree and interprets her as a survival in Paraguayan mestizo folklore of older Guaraní tree and animal religion. His formulation is important scholarship, but “survival” is an interpretive model; it does not itself supply the missing pre-contact text.

### Mid-twentieth century: literature, theater and regional identity

Alejandro Magrassi’s *La Caá Yarí: Novela de los yerbales misioneros* (1945) placed the figure inside a fictionalized mensú world of recruitment, contracts and exploitation. A 1956 dramatic treatment described the story as an “indio-Jesuit” mixture and turned the supernatural marriage into theater. Ballet, poetry and regional prose further fixed the image of a seductive yerbal goddess.

### Late twentieth century: divergence of two public figures

Two partially separate Ka'a Jarýis became common:

- the jealous supernatural bride of the mensú or tarefero;
- the benevolent ecological protector and origin-goddess of mate.

The Jasy-and-Araí story spread through children’s books and schools. It became associated with Ka'a Jarýi even where its basic plot originally explained only the plant’s origin.

### Twenty-first century

Provincial tourism in Misiones presents her as the owner and grandmother of yerba mate, preserving the pact but often replacing the dangerous *minero* with the modern *tarefero*. Paraguayan compilations present a forest-wide guardian. Government, conservation and commercial sources invoke her as an emblem of mate, regional hospitality and environmental stewardship.

A municipality and a provincial protected area in Misiones bear the name Caa Yarí/Caá Yarí. These are civic and conservation commemorations, not cult centers.

A Paraguayan survey project has continued to ask respondents whether they have heard of, believe in, seen or sensed Ka'a Jarýi. This documents active folklore awareness, though published survey forms alone do not establish prevalence or continuous ritual practice.

No reliable evidence was found for an organized contemporary reconstructionist religion devoted to her.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

- **1898:** Ambrosetti’s literary-ethnographic publication.
- **1945:** Alejandro Magrassi, *La Caá Yarí: Novela de los yerbales misioneros*, links the name to mensú exploitation.
- **Mid-century poetry and drama:** The forest bride becomes a symbol of desire, loneliness, labor and the devouring jungle.
- **Later regional poetry:** Olga Zamboni and others use Caá-Yarí as a Misionero cultural emblem.
- **Modern theater:** Raúl Novau’s *El manjar del jaguar* explicitly identifies a character called “La Diosa” as Caá-Yarí, protector of yerba mate.

### Music and dance

Regional poetry and song include works titled “Ka'a Jarýi.” A 2014 album by Miguel “Cholo” Cabral contains a track under that name. Carmen Larumbe’s Ballet Misiones staged a *Leyenda de la Caá Yarí* with music by Lucas Braulio Areco. These are artistic receptions, not ritual hymns.

### Tourism and education

Tourist and school texts now supply standardized plots, moral vocabulary and imagery. They commonly:

- identify Tupã as a monotheistic “God of Good”;
- make mate a timeless symbol of friendship;
- join the Jasy/Araí jaguar rescue to the Yarí guardian story;
- call Ka'a Jarýi an ancient goddess;
- present blond, silver-haired or copper-haired imagery as canonical.

These are useful records of modern reception but poor evidence for antiquity.

### Commercial appropriation

Yerba producers and mate-promotion bodies use origin legends to give a commodity sacred and indigenous depth. The National Yerba Mate Institute’s website publishes the Tupã-and-hospitality version. Commercial association does not make the account false as folklore, but it changes its function: an explanation once tied to precarious workers becomes a national product narrative.

### Ecological reinvention

Modern institutional accounts emphasize sustainable hunting, clean water, protection of orphaned animals and defense of biodiversity. These themes have roots in owner-spirit ethics, but their detailed articulation also fits contemporary conservation discourse. Naming the Parque Provincial Caá Yarí makes her an environmental emblem.

### Esotericism, occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated searches did not locate a historically significant Renaissance, Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, grimoire, ceremonial-magic or established neopagan tradition concerning Ka'a Jarýi. Generic deity websites and social-media pantheon lists sometimes recast her as a nature goddess available for personal devotion. Without traceable communities, dates and texts, this is **modern individual reinvention**, not an old occult lineage.

No documented extremist, racial-nationalist or violent political appropriation was found. Regionalist and national-cultural appropriation exists, chiefly around Guaraní heritage, Misiones identity and mate.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is she pre-Columbian?

The strongest affirmative case is comparative:

- *ka'a* is old Guaraní vocabulary;
- owner-spirits of species and natural places are well documented;
- yerba mate was culturally important before the nineteenth century;
- Cadogan interpreted her as a survival of Guaraní tree religion.

The limiting evidence is documentary:

- the named figure is absent from the currently identified colonial sources;
- the first full narrative is from 1898;
- that narrative is saturated with Christian figures and Holy Week;
- Ambrosetti himself supplied the “Indian origin” and Jesuit-layer interpretation without an earlier text.

The responsible conclusion is therefore: **a Guaraní conceptual substrate is plausible; the antiquity of the named, blond, sexually exclusive mistress of mensú workers is unproven.**

### Ka'a Jarýi and Ka'a Póra

Ambrosetti suggested that Brazilian Caá-Porá represented a regional transformation of Caá-Yarí. Later dictionaries and folklore inventories sometimes treat Ka'a Póra, Ka'a Jára and Ka'a Jarýi as synonyms.

Against a complete equation:

- *Caipora/Ka'a Póra* has a much wider Brazilian and Tupi distribution;
- its sex, appearance, mount and behavior vary drastically;
- it commonly protects game animals and punishes hunters rather than supervising yerba harvest;
- Ambrosetti published Caá-Yarí and Caá-Porá as separate entries.

They belong to an overlapping owner-spirit complex, but are not demonstrably one timeless being.

### Goddess, spirit, owner or demon?

“Goddess” stresses her superhuman power and plant domain. “Spirit” or “female owner” better fits the vernacular category and the lack of institutional worship. “Demon” reflects hostile Christian or sensationalist description and is misleading as a neutral classification. “Monster” is inappropriate: beauty rather than deformity defines her earliest manifestation.

### Origin myth versus worker belief

Ambrosetti’s own text reveals a split:

- an etiological myth explaining the plant;
- a living workers’ belief explaining success, secrecy and death.

Later retellings merge the two. Historically they should remain distinguishable.

### Moon-gift versus old-man hospitality

The moon-and-cloud story and the God/Tupã hospitality story share reward, hospitality and creation of yerba but differ in divine agents and dramatic structure. No early source establishes that one descended directly from the other. Their modern fusion is a literary process, not recovered ancient canon.

### Mensú interpretation

A social-historical reading sees the pact as a symbolic response to an overwhelmingly male, isolated and violent extractive economy. The invisible helper makes impossible quotas achievable; her jealousy polices separation from ordinary domestic life; her revenge narrates unexplained death.

That reading is compelling but remains interpretation. The workers themselves, as reported by Ambrosetti, treated her as an agent, not merely an allegory of capitalism or loneliness.

### Colmán’s “pantheon”

*Ñande Ypy Kuéra* is culturally influential but must be read as a 1929 literary systematization. Its seven-monster genealogy should not be projected backward or extended to Ka'a Jarýi. Modern charts that call every folkloric character a child, sibling or servant of Tupã reproduce this flattening.

### State of the evidence

The foundational physical text is Ambrosetti’s printed 1898 piece, later collected in 1917. The folklore it reports was oral and survives only through his mediation. No recordings from those informants exist in the cited record.

Cadogan’s published ethnography provides the strongest twentieth-century comparative framework. His Mbyá works must nevertheless be handled separately from Paraguayan mestizo and Upper Paraná labor folklore. Modern digital collections greatly increase access while also encouraging unattributed copying and hybridization.

## 10. Contested-Material Findings

### Etymology

Secure: *ka'a* denotes yerba mate and historically had wider plant/forest senses; *jarýi* means grandmother and, in this title, female owner/protectress.

Disputed or oversimplified: translating the name exclusively as “forest grandmother,” exclusively as “yerba goddess,” or treating ordinary “grandmother” as a complete explanation of the supernatural category.

### Syncretism

Secure: the 1898 story contains God, Saint Peter, Saint John, Holy Week and a church vow.

Probable but unproven: an older Guaraní plant-owner belief was Christianized during the Jesuit period.

Unsupported: a known Jesuit author deliberately composed the legend, or a particular reduction formally celebrated her.

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

No evidence found for human sacrifice, institutional sacrifice, sacred killing or cult violence in her name. Her killing of unfaithful workers belongs to legend. Historical violence in the yerbales was real, but was labor and frontier violence, not documented worship of Ka'a Jarýi.

### Sexuality

Secure: the pact demands exclusivity and uses marital or lover vocabulary.

Not established: temple prostitution, obligatory intercourse, priestly sexual rites or an ancient fertility cult.

### Suppression

Christian conversion and missionization transformed Guaraní religions generally, but no specific campaign against Ka'a Jarýi is documented. Indeed, her first recorded story incorporates Christian sacred time and space.

### Occultism and neopaganism

No significant historical occult lineage or organized modern cult located. Contemporary personal invocations, deity lists or “work with this goddess” pages should be labeled modern eclectic spirituality.

### Pre-Columbian claims

Claims that ancient Guaraní universally worshipped her, that she was already known under precisely this name before European contact, or that her blond/blue-eyed form is indigenous antiquity remain unverified.

## 11. Chronology

- **Before European contact:** Indigenous use of yerba mate and broader Guaraní relations with plant, animal and place owners are plausible or independently documented; Ka'a Jarýi herself is not securely attested.

- **1639:** Ruiz de Montoya publishes *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual*. These establish relevant language and mission context, not the named figure.

- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** Yerba production becomes economically important in Jesuit reductions. No Ka'a Jarýi shrine, priesthood or narrative is securely recorded.

- **1767–1768:** Jesuits are expelled from Spanish America; reduction economies are reorganized and decline unevenly. Later folklore attributes some Christianization of the legend to the mission era, but without a contemporaneous text.

- **1876:** Corrientes issues regulations intended to control exploitation of fiscal yerbales; the measure belongs to the extractive history from which the mensú world emerged.

- **Late nineteenth century:** Wild-yerba extraction expands in the Upper Paraná amid severe labor conditions.

- **1898:** Ambrosetti publishes “La Leyenda de la Caá-Yarí,” based on testimony from Tacurú-Pucú and yerba workers. First secure attestation.

- **1908:** Rafael Barrett publicly denounces conditions in Paraguayan yerbales. This does not mention a Ka'a Jarýi cult but documents the violent labor environment surrounding the legend.

- **1914:** Nimuendajú publishes the Apapocúva creation and world-destruction corpus. It does not securely attest Ka'a Jarýi.

- **1917:** Ambrosetti’s material appears in *Supersticiones y leyendas*, giving the story durable folkloric circulation.

- **1929:** Colmán publishes *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, systematizing the seven monsters. Ka'a Jarýi is not one of them.

- **1945:** Alejandro Magrassi publishes *La Caá Yarí: Novela de los yerbales misioneros*.

- **1950:** Cadogan publishes “El culto al árbol y a los animales entre los Guaraní,” identifying Ka'a Yary as the yerba-mate-tree spirit surviving in Paraguayan folklore.

- **1956:** A dramatic treatment of the mensú-Caá-Yarí relationship is described as an Indigenous-Christian or “indio-Jesuit” legend.

- **1959:** Cadogan publishes *Ayvu Rapyta*. It becomes foundational for Mbyá studies but should not be treated as the source of Ambrosetti’s pact narrative.

- **1971:** Cadogan publishes *Ywyra Ñe'ery*, a separate Mbyá corpus with more everyday and recorded discourse.

- **1981:** Neli Garrido de Rodríguez publishes a traceable modern version of the Jasy-and-Araí yerba-origin legend.

- **1984:** *Ayvu Rendy Vera* publishes Mbyá prayers; a later adaptation acknowledges using it, rather than *Ayvu Rapyta*, for a yerba-creation episode.

- **2003:** Domingo Aguilera’s *Folklore paraguayo* presents a Ka'a Jarýi myth separately from the legend of *ka'a*, illustrating continued genre distinction.

- **2004:** Pau Navajas publishes *Caá Porã'í: el espíritu de la yerba mate*, a commercially connected historical and cultural treatment that further associates mate with a guardian spirit.

- **2005:** Pedro Encina Ramos publishes a modern synthesis portraying Ka'a Jarýi as a blue-eyed blond woman of the Upper Paraná and asserting pre-European antiquity without presenting an early text.

- **2006 onward:** Misiones employs Caá Yarí’s name for conservation initiatives and a provincial park.

- **2014:** Miguel “Cholo” Cabral releases a song titled “Ka'a Jaryi.”

- **Twenty-first century:** Provincial tourism, schools, mate institutions and environmental projects circulate the Tupã, Jasy/Araí and ecological-guardian versions. A Paraguayan foundation surveys continued awareness and claimed experience of the figure.

- **Present:** Ka'a Jarýi persists primarily as regional folklore, literary heritage, mate-origin heroine, labor-memory figure and conservation emblem. Organized continuous worship remains unestablished.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

Ambrosetti’s 1898 text is the indispensable primary record. Its later 1917 collection should be consulted for the full regional arrangement, including the separate Caá-Porá entry.

Montoya is primary evidence for seventeenth-century Guaraní vocabulary and the Jesuit mission environment, but not presently an attestation of Ka'a Jarýi.

Cadogan’s 1950 article is primary ethnographic scholarship for the mid-twentieth-century survival interpretation. *Ayvu Rapyta* and *Ywyra Ñe'ery* are primary Mbyá textual corpora, but they must not be mined selectively to authenticate a different mestizo or workers’ narrative.

The bilingual Paraguayan collection published online under Aguilera, Acosta and Villagra Marsal supplies a later Guaraní-language ecological-guardian account and preserves an explicit disagreement over what she does with abandoned animal carcasses.

### Essential critical distinctions

1. **The plant is old; the documentary record of the named being is late.**
2. **The owner-spirit category may be old; the blond mensú bride is first securely recorded in 1898.**
3. **Christian material is present in the earliest recorded story, not merely in a visibly late appendix.**
4. **Ka'a Jarýi and Ka'a Póra overlap but remain separately documented figures.**
5. **The Jasy/Araí story is a later traceable literary-educational cycle, not the earliest attestation.**
6. **Colmán’s seven-monster genealogy does not include her.**
7. **Modern conservation and hospitality meanings are historically important receptions, not proof of ancient doctrine.**
