# Ñande Sy (the First Mother): Research Dossier

> **Class:** Female ancestral divine being / mythic mother in several Guaraní traditions; in modern Kaiowá and Paĩ-Tavyterã usage, *ñandesy* also designates a living female ritual leader, healer, or prayer specialist. She is not securely attested as a universally sovereign “mother goddess.”
>
> **Earliest secure attestation of the specific mythic figure:** Curt Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva-Guaraní materials, collected principally during his residence among Apapokúva communities in Brazil before 1914 and published in 1914 as *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní*. Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s seventeenth-century works document the constituent Guaraní vocabulary and the colonial religious setting, but no securely identified occurrence of the complete named Ñande Sy twin-cycle figure has been established in them. [Nimuendajú’s 1914 publication](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Animuendaju-1914-apapocuva); [Montoya’s 1639 *Conquista espiritual*](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Amontoya-1639-conquista)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identification

Ñande Sy is literally “our mother”:

- *ñande*: inclusive “we/us/our,” embracing speaker and addressee.
- *sy*: “mother.”
- The spelling *Ñande Chy* or *Ñande Cy* occurs in older Portuguese-, Spanish-, and French-influenced orthographies.
- Joined forms include *Ñandesy*, *Ñandesý*, *Nhandesy*, and *Ñandecy*.
- *Ñande Sy Ete* or *Ñande Chy Ete* means “our true/genuine mother.”
- *Ñande Sy Tenonde* means “our first/primordial mother.”
- *Ñande Sy Guasu* means “our great mother,” but this is not demonstrably a universal ancient title.
- *Ñande Jari/Jaryi*, “our grandmother,” can overlap functionally with female ancestral beings, but it is not automatically a synonym for Ñande Sy.

A comparative catalogue correctly reduces the etymology to *ñande* plus *sy* and records *Ñandesy, Ñandesý, Ñandecy, Ñande Sy, Ñande Cy,* and *Ñande Sy Eté*. It also identifies her as mother of the astral twins, while noting the two-father version of their conception. [Comparative name record](https://www.mathieu-colas.fr/michel/Classes/Dieux_d%27Afrique_et_d%27Amerique.pdf)

The phrase is not a uniquely identifying proper name in every context. It may mean:

1. one’s or the community’s mother;
2. the mythic mother of the twins;
3. an ancestral or celestial “True Mother”;
4. a female religious leader or *rezadora*;
5. Mary in an Indigenous Christian discourse;
6. metaphorically, the mother or female owner of a crop or class of beings.

Accordingly, an occurrence of *ñande sy* cannot be assigned to this mythic individual without context.

### Classification by tradition

| Tradition or setting | Classification |
|---|---|
| Apapokúva/early Ñandeva documentation | Mythic first woman and mother of culture-hero twins |
| Mbyá narrative tradition | Primordial or “true” mother within the cycle of Pa’i Rete Kuaray and Jasy; a resident of the celestial/imperishable realm after death or restoration |
| Paĩ-Tavyterã | *Ñande Sy Ete*, wife of Ñande Ru and mother of Pa’i Kuara and Jasy; ancestral divine woman |
| Kaiowá | Mythic wife or counterpart of Ñande Ru in some accounts; in others, Ñande Ru and Ñande Sy are primordial paired beings rather than husband and wife |
| Contemporary Kaiowá and Guaraní social-religious usage | *Ñandesy*: female prayer leader, healer, elder, or head of an extended ritual family |
| Paraguayan popular Catholic usage | Sometimes used for Mary, though the standard missionary compound is *Tupãsy*, “mother of Tupã/God” |
| Modern popular mythology | Often flattened into “the Guaraní mother goddess” or “First Mother,” a broader and more systematic identity than the evidence warrants |

### Etymology and disputed identity

The etymology itself is secure; the dispute concerns reference. “Our Mother” may be a relational title rather than an individual personal name. Nimuendajú, Cadogan, Melià, Schaden, and later ethnographers found multiple celestial fathers, mothers, grandfathers, and grandmothers whose titles shift with community, narrator, ritual register, and generation. A glossary of Paĩ-Tavyterã vocabulary defines *ñande sy* as “our mother,” “mother of the lineage,” wife of the shaman, and mythic mother, also called *ñande sy ete* and sometimes *ñande jaryi*. [Paĩ-Tavyterã glossary](https://www.pueblos-originarios.ucb.edu.bo/digital/106001185.pdf)

The safest formulation is therefore:

> Ñande Sy is a recurrent Guaraní relational title that becomes the identifying name of the mother in several versions of the twin myth. It does not prove a single pan-Guaraní goddess with a uniform theology.

### Mary, Tupãsy, and Christian translation

Montoya’s missionary lexicon helped establish *Tupãsy* as the Christian Guaraní name for Mary: literally “mother of Tupã,” with *Tupã* appropriated as the name of the Christian God. Other Christian narratives call Mary *Ñandesy*, “our mother.” These are historically datable translations and accommodations, not proof that the mythic twins’ mother and Mary were always one being.

A study of mission-era religious language notes both the Montoyan *Tupãn Sy* and the later association of Mary with *Ñande Sy*. [Study of mission religious vocabulary](https://tede2.pucrs.br/tede2/bitstream/tede/2444/1/444645.pdf) Nimuendajú also recorded an Apapokúva Christian story in which Mary was called Ñande Sy and Jesus *Ñandejára*, “our lord.” That is a syncretic reuse of Indigenous kinship language, not an early Indigenous genealogy of Mary. [Study of Ñandesy and Tupãsy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/355108319_RIBLA_RIBLA_REVISTA_DE_INTERPRETACION_BIBLICA_LATINOAMERICANA_N_46)

**Evidentiary label:** colonial and modern syncretistic identification.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### What Montoya does—and does not—establish

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual*, both printed in Madrid in 1639, are indispensable witnesses to seventeenth-century Guaraní language and Jesuit encounters with Guaraní societies. The *Tesoro* is a contextual Guaraní–Spanish lexicon; the *Conquista* is a missionary history and apologetic account of the reductions. [Bibliographic record for the *Tesoro*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL16500412W/Tesoro_de_la_lengua_guarani); [digital *Conquista espiritual*](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Amontoya-1639-conquista)

They establish:

- the antiquity of *sy* as ordinary Guaraní “mother” vocabulary;
- the seventeenth-century missionary translation environment;
- missionary transformation of Guaraní divine terminology;
- conflict with Indigenous ritual authorities and the institutional setting in which native narratives could be suppressed, reframed, or left unrecorded.

They do **not**, on presently verifiable evidence, give the complete named Ñande Sy narrative: two divine fathers, pregnancy, the journey directed by an unborn son, killing by primordial jaguars, preservation of the twins, reconstruction from bones, and celestial restoration.

Thus Montoya is the earliest lexical and colonial contextual witness, but not the earliest secure attestation of this divine biography.

### Nimuendajú, 1914: earliest secure dossier

Curt Nimuendajú lived among the Apapokúva and received his Indigenous name in 1906. His German-language study appeared in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914), pp. 284–403. The work records creation and destruction cycles, religious language, the Land Without Evil, migrations, and the twins’ cycle. [Original 1914 publication and facsimile](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Animuendaju-1914-apapocuva)

In the Apapokúva version as transmitted through Nimuendajú and later translated or retold:

- Ñanderuvusu or Ñamandu and Ñande Ru Mba’ekuaa make or obtain a woman, Ñande Sy.
- She conceives children associated with the two fathers.
- The senior creator withdraws.
- Pregnant Ñande Sy follows him, guided for a time by the elder child speaking from her womb.
- A quarrel over flowers or fruit interrupts that guidance.
- She takes the path to the jaguars’ dwelling.
- The primordial jaguars kill and eat her.
- Her unborn children survive.
- Subsequent episodes concern the twins’ upbringing, discovery of their mother’s fate, attempted restoration, revenge, and ascent.

**Evidentiary label:** securely attested early ethnographic record of an oral tradition. “Early” here means early documentation, not proof of a 1914 origin.

### Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus

León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta: textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá* was published by the University of São Paulo in 1959. It presents Guaraní texts with Spanish translations and commentary. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ayvu_Rapyta.html?id=wLgPAQAAMAAJ); [digital text and commentary](https://www.portalguarani.com/793_leon_cadogan/16228_ayvu_rapyta_textos_miticos_de_los_mbya_guarani_del_guaira__por_leon_cadogan.html)

Cadogan’s relevant corpus includes:

- creation hymns centered on Ñamandu Ru Ete;
- accounts of the First Earth;
- narratives of Pa’i Rete Kuaray, Jasy, the primordial jaguars, and the murder of their mother;
- explanations of birds, animals, ritual speech, death, and celestial geography;
- sayings attributed to Ñande Sy Tenonde concerning imperishable honey and celestial baskets prepared for her descendants.

Cadogan’s *Ywyra Ñe’ery: fluye del árbol la palabra* assembled additional Mbyá texts and interpretations. The title’s publication history is later than *Ayvu Rapyta*, and the contents include previously published articles, among them material on the “miraculous baskets” of Ñande Sy Tenonde.

A digitized Cadogan text preserves the saying:

> *Kóma, ajaká’i ju ano’ĩ che memby’i kuéry oñevangá haguã che yvarokáre*  
> “Look, I have gathered imperishable little baskets so that my little daughters may play with them around my celestial dwelling.”

This connects the First Mother with women, female descendants, basketry, honey, and the imperishable celestial country. [Cadogan on Mbyá acculturation and Ñande Sy’s baskets](https://etnolinguistica.wdfiles.com/local--files/biblio%3Acadogan-1960-aculturacion/Cadogan_1960_EnTornoMbyaGuaraniGuaira_OCR.pdf)

**Evidentiary label:** mid-twentieth-century Mbyá ritual and narrative corpus; not interchangeable with the Apapokúva or Paĩ versions.

### Paĩ-Tavyterã corpus

Bartomeu Melià, Georg Grünberg, and Friedl Grünberg’s *Los Paĩ-Tavyterã: etnografía guaraní del Paraguay contemporáneo* appeared in 1976. It gives a detailed Paĩ account of Ñande Ru, Ñande Sy Ete, Pa’i Kuara, Jasy, and the primordial jaguars. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books?id=0YZsAAAAMAAJ)

Its version distinguishes two divine generations:

1. Ñane Ramõi Jusu Papa and Ñande Jari;
2. their son Ñande Ru Pavẽ and his wife Ñande Sy Ete.

But the authors warn that narrators sometimes blur Ñane Ramõi with Ñande Ru and Ñande Jari with Ñande Sy. They explicitly ask whether the distinction represents separate beings or different aspects. In the collected story, Ñande Ru dies or leaves after a quarrel, and the pregnant Ñande Sy sets out to find him. The account then proceeds through the jaguars, the twins’ survival, failed restoration of the mother, and revenge. [Accessible text of the ethnography](https://es.scribd.com/document/500762907/Los-Pa%C4%A9-Tavytera-Etnografia-Guarani-Del-Paraguay-Contemporaneo)

**Evidentiary label:** regional Paĩ-Tavyterã variant documented in the twentieth century.

### Pierre and Hélène Clastres

Pierre Clastres translated and philosophically interpreted Guaraní ritual speech in *Le Grand Parler: mythes et chants sacrés des Indiens Guarani* (1974). Hélène Clastres’s *La Terre sans mal: le prophétisme tupi-guarani* (1975) examined the Land Without Evil and prophetic migration. These works made the Nimuendajú–Cadogan corpus influential in French anthropology.

They should be treated as translations and interpretations of Indigenous and ethnographic materials, not as independent ancient witnesses. Pierre Clastres’s elegant French sometimes regularizes disparate narratives into a literary sequence.

### Métraux and the Tupinambá comparison

Alfred Métraux’s *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus Tupi-Guarani* (1928) assembled sixteenth- and seventeenth-century evidence for coastal Tupinambá religion and compared it with other Tupi-Guaraní traditions. The widespread Tupi-Guaraní twin complex is relevant to the antiquity and distribution of the story-pattern. It does not prove that every element of the documented Ñande Sy cycle, or the name Ñande Sy itself, existed unchanged among sixteenth-century Tupinambá.

**Evidentiary label:** modern comparative reconstruction.

### Argentine folklore collections

Juan Bautista Ambrosetti documented Misiones folklore and jaguar traditions in the late nineteenth century, including *La leyenda del Yaguareté-Abá* (1895). [Ambrosetti bibliographic record](https://naturalis.fcnym.unlp.edu.ar/entities/publication/beb25929-1e94-4064-ab65-92ff07d883a9)

Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini later collected Argentine folktales. Vidal de Battini’s ten-volume *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina* was published from 1980 to 1995 from field materials gathered over decades. Her method preserves local narrator, language, and variants rather than manufacturing a single national mythology. [Collection record](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/promocion_lectura_y_letras_en_espanyol/obra/cuentos-y-leyendas-populares-de-la-argentina-tomo-10--0/)

These collections are important for Corrientes and Misiones reception, but the widely printed Argentine “Guaraní mythology” of Tupã, the first human couple, and the seven monsters frequently reflects Paraguayan literary folklore rather than an unchanged Indigenous Ñande Sy corpus.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains securely or regionally attested

Ñande Sy’s clearest domains are relational rather than administrative:

- motherhood and pregnancy;
- ancestry and female descent;
- the origin and fate of the twins;
- mortality, bones, and failed or completed regeneration;
- celestial hospitality and the imperishable realm;
- in some Mbyá material, women’s baskets and honey;
- in some Kaiowá speech, crop motherhood or the female spiritual ownership of maize;
- by extension, the ritual authority of female elders called *ñandesy*.

She is not securely documented as a general goddess of “all fertility,” “the earth,” or “the moon.” Those broader labels are modern simplifications unless attached to a particular narrator or community.

### Physical form

The narratives assume an adult human female who can become pregnant, walk, speak with her unborn child, be hidden under a vessel or basket, killed, cooked or eaten, and reduced to bones. No stable nonhuman anatomy is supplied.

Her mythic importance lies precisely in bodily transformations:

1. made or revealed from a covered clay vessel in one Apapokúva recension;
2. pregnant with twins;
3. killed and consumed;
4. surviving materially as bones;
5. incompletely reconstructed, or restored in heaven, depending on the version.

### Iconography

No securely identified precolonial image of Ñande Sy is known. There is no established canonical combination of crown, weapon, sacred animal, color, or celestial sign by which a museum object can safely be identified as her.

Relevant but indirect visual evidence includes:

- Mbyá and Avá-Chiripá drawings of Kuaray, Jasy, jaguars, birds, and beings generated during the attempted restoration of their mother;
- basket designs connected by Cadogan and later authors with Ñande Sy Tenonde;
- mission images of Mary interpreted through Guaraní categories;
- contemporary illustrations of a pregnant woman, jaguars, the twins, bones, or maize.

Avá-Chiripá drawings collected by José A. Perasso and Olga Blinder associate the *ynambú* and *jaichá* with Kuarahy’s effort to revive Ñande Sy Ete, and depict the sun, moon, jaguar, rainbow, and messenger birds. These are twentieth-century Indigenous drawings of the narrative world, not surviving ancient cult icons. [Perasso and Blinder collection](https://www.portalguarani.com/951_jose_antonio_perasso/18783_dibujos_ava_kue_chiripa__por_jose_a_perasso__olga_blinder.html)

### Symbols and associated beings

| Motif | Status |
|---|---|
| Clay vessel or covered container | Apapokúva creation/origin variant |
| Flower | Causes the quarrel between mother and unborn elder twin in several versions |
| Wasp or bee sting | Regional narrative mechanism precipitating that quarrel |
| Wrong fork or path | Leads to the primordial jaguars |
| Basket or pot | Used to hide her, preserve remains, or signify celestial female craft, depending on the account |
| Bones | Central to attempted resurrection and the origin of mortality |
| Maize meal | Used in some retellings in reconstructing a body from bones |
| Jaguar | Killer of the mother; primordial antagonist |
| Parrot, *paraka/parakáu ñe’ẽngatu* | Reveals the murder to the twins |
| Honey and imperishable bees | Mbyá celestial provision attributed to Ñande Sy |
| Baskets | Mbyá female descent and celestial play |
| Maize | Kaiowá ritual complex, but the exact equation “Ñande Sy = mother of maize” requires grammatical caution |

No secure traditional evidence assigns her a universal color, planet, weekday, number, weapon, or sacrificial animal.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogical variants

#### Apapokúva version

- Senior father: Ñanderuvusu/Ñamandu.
- Second father: Ñande Ru Mba’ekuaa, “our knowing/wise father.”
- Mother: Ñande Sy.
- Elder son: Ñande Ryke’y, later associated with Kuaray/Sun.
- Younger son: Tyvyry’i, later associated with Jasy/Moon.
- Each child may have a different father.

**Label:** earliest securely documented version of this genealogy, 1914.

#### Mbyá versions

The supreme creator is normally Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua. The twin-cycle names and paternities vary. The chief hero is Pa’i Rete Kuaray, “the lord/priest whose body shines like the sun,” with Jasy as younger brother. The twins’ mother may be called Ñande Sy, Ñande Chy Ete, or by an expanded title.

Cadogan’s corpus should not be forced into the same divine family tree as the Apapokúva account merely because cognate roles occur.

**Label:** regional Mbyá variants, documented principally in the mid-twentieth century.

#### Paĩ-Tavyterã version

- Grandparents or first pair: Ñane Ramõi Jusu Papa and Ñande Jari.
- Their son: Ñande Ru Pavẽ.
- Ñande Ru’s wife: Ñande Sy Ete.
- Her twins: Pa’i Kuara and Jasy.

The ethnographers themselves record uncertainty over whether the two generations are consistently differentiated. [Paĩ-Tavyterã account](https://es.scribd.com/document/500762907/Los-Pa%C4%A9-Tavytera-Etnografia-Guarani-Del-Paraguay-Contemporaneo)

#### Kaiowá variants

Some Kaiowá narrators call Ñande Sy the divine wife of Ñande Ru. Others reject the idea that they were spouses and state that the pair “were born together,” after which human and animal marriage became possible. This is direct evidence against treating one husband-wife genealogy as universal. [Kaiowá field study](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/server/api/core/bitstreams/22e01336-762a-4d25-9a59-f0ea63bd2433/content)

### Absences worth stating

No securely attested narrative interaction was located between the twins’ mother and the seven monsters Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jateré, Kurupí, Ao Ao, or Luisõ. Nor is she securely part of the Taú–Keraná genealogy. Those beings belong to a Paraguayan literary-folkloric system most famously organized by Narciso R. Colmán, not to the documented Ñande Sy twin genealogy.

No interaction with Pombero is securely documented in the primary Ñande Sy corpus.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The making of the First Mother

In the Apapokúva version known from Nimuendajú, Ñanderuvusu encounters Ñande Ru Mba’ekuaa and proposes that they obtain a woman. They form a clay vessel or pot, cover it, and on opening it find Ñande Sy. She has relations with both divine men and conceives a son by each.

This is not identical to a creation of all humanity. She is created or manifested as the mother required for the twin cycle.

**Earliest source:** Nimuendajú, 1914.  
**Label:** securely attested early ethnographic material.

Later summaries sometimes replace the two fathers with Ñamandu alone or moralize the event as “adultery.” The moral term may reproduce a narrator’s own judgment, missionary vocabulary, or the translator’s framing. The source history does not justify turning one version into a universal Guaraní doctrine of marital sin.

### The journey and the fatal flower

After the senior father withdraws to his celestial dwelling, pregnant Ñande Sy sets out after him. Her elder unborn son speaks from inside her and gives directions. He asks her to pick flowers. In several recensions a bee or wasp stings her finger. She strikes or reproaches her abdomen and refuses another request. The offended child stops guiding her or directs her at a fork onto the wrong path.

The path leads to the house of primordial jaguars.

The episode distributes responsibility ambiguously:

- the father withdrew;
- the mother rebuked the unborn child;
- the child withheld correct guidance;
- the jaguars performed the killing.

No responsible edition should reduce this to “the disobedient woman was punished” or “the malicious sun murdered his mother.” The narrative itself supports neither simple moral.

**Sources:** Nimuendajú 1914; Cadogan’s twentieth-century Mbyá/Chiripá corpus; Paĩ-Tavyterã materials published in 1976.  
**Label:** widespread twin-cycle with significant regional variation.

### The mother eaten by the primordial jaguars

At the jaguars’ dwelling, an elderly female—variously a jaguar grandmother or Mba’e Ypy—tries to conceal Ñande Sy beneath a basket, pot, or vessel. The hunting jaguars return without game, smell or discover her, kill her, and consume her. In some versions they cut open her body before cooking it; in others the unborn twins emerge alive from her corpse or cooking vessel.

The grandmother saves the children, sometimes compassionately and sometimes as future food. Repeated efforts to kill or cook the divine children fail.

The killers are not ordinary zoological jaguars. They are primordial *jaguarete*, *jaguarete ypy*, or Añag, ancestral beings from whom present jaguars may descend. A Paĩ account says one pregnant female escapes the twins’ later massacre, explaining why jaguars remain in the world.

**Sources:** Nimuendajú 1914; Cadogan 1959 and related publications; Paĩ-Tavyterã ethnography 1976.  
**Label:** securely documented central myth, with regional variants.

### Discovery and the speaking parrot

The twins grow while unknowingly supplying birds or other game to the beings who ate their mother. A parrot—*Paraka* or *parakáu ñe’ẽngatu*, the “parrot of good speech”—tells them, in Cadogan’s translation, that they are feeding those who devoured their mother.

Pa’i Rete Kuaray understands his origin, weeps, and releases or revives the captured birds, naming them as he does so. Cadogan relates the Guaraní comparison for extreme anger: one is furious “as he who looks upon the killer of his own mother.” [Cadogan’s narrative](https://www.portalguarani.com/793_leon_cadogan/6594_tradiciones_guaranies_en_el_folklore_paraguayo__fragmentos_de_etnografia_mbya_guarani__edicion_preparada_por_bartomeu_melia_sj_.html)

The episode simultaneously explains:

- the twins’ recognition of their ancestry;
- their turn against the jaguars;
- the names or restoration of birds;
- an ethic against taking more game than can be used, in some Indigenous or anthropological interpretations.

**Label:** Mbyá/Chiripá regional form; cognate revelation episodes occur elsewhere.

### Her bones and failed return

The twins gather their mother’s bones. The elder arranges them and performs song, prayer, blowing, or another creative rite. In some accounts the bones are ground or mixed with maize meal; in others reconstruction occurs directly through divine speech.

A female body begins to form. The younger brother, Jasy, cannot wait and cries “Mother!” or rushes toward her before the rite is complete. The body collapses back into bones. A second attempt may fail in the same manner. The failure explains why the dead do not return bodily and why humans are mortal.

There are at least three distinguishable outcomes:

1. **Failed earthly resurrection:** Ñande Sy cannot be restored because Jasy interrupts. Her death institutes human mortality.
2. **Transformation:** the attempted reconstitution produces animals or birds, or her substance enters other forms.
3. **Celestial restoration:** when the twins eventually reach their father’s dwelling, they find their mother alive there, restored by the father or existing in an imperishable mode.

These should not be harmonized. The first makes her irrecoverable on earth; the third makes her available in heaven. Both can coexist within a theology distinguishing mortal embodiment from perfected celestial life, but the surviving texts do not require that solution.

**Sources:** Nimuendajú-derived Apapokúva materials; Cadogan’s Mbyá and Chiripá texts; Paĩ-Tavyterã ethnography.  
**Label:** widespread narrative complex with incompatible endings.

### Revenge on the jaguars

The twins create a crossing or bridge over a river, weaken it, and lure the primordial jaguars across with promises of abundant game. They cause the bridge to break and kill the jaguars in the water. In several Paĩ and Ñandeva retellings, one pregnant female survives, accounting for contemporary jaguars.

The mother does not personally participate after her death. The revenge is carried out in her name.

**Label:** securely documented twin-cycle; etiological regional variants.

### Ascent and reunion

After numerous terrestrial adventures, the twins ascend by prayer, dance, fasting, or a ladder of arrows. The elder receives or assumes the Sun and the younger the Moon. Some versions say their mother awaits them in the celestial realm; others leave her restoration unresolved.

This story is often compressed into “Ñande Sy gave birth to Sun and Moon.” The older documented cycle is more complex: in some accounts both are already present in her womb; in others the younger is made from her bones by the elder; and only later are they assigned or transformed into the celestial luminaries.

### Ñande Sy and maize

The requested proposition—“the Ñande Sy of the Kaiowá as mother of maize”—requires close qualification.

Kaiowá accounts identify white maize, *avati morotĩ* or *saboró*, as sacred. Its cultivation requires prayer at planting, flowering, harvest, and ritual blessing. Jakaira is frequently called the owner or creator of white maize. In one recent field account, Crescência of Guaiviry says:

> *o dono do avati morotĩ é o Jakaira, trata-se da Ñande Sy (a mãe) do milho*  
> “the owner of white maize is Jakaira; this is its Ñande Sy, the mother of the maize.”

The same fieldwork also records other origins: maize arising from the buried Ñande Ru Paitambeju or from a chigoe flea on his body; another narrator says land, Indigenous people, maize, and cultivation appeared together. [Kaiowá maize fieldwork](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/bitstream/1843/50260/1/Disserta%C3%A7%C3%A3o%20de%20antropologia%20Jana%C3%ADna%20pdf.pdf)

Two readings remain possible:

1. Jakaira is here a female or maternally classified maize divinity called its *Ñande Sy*.
2. *Ñande Sy* is a relational or ritual title—“our mother/the mother”—not the personal identity of the jaguar-slain twins’ mother.

The evidence does not authorize silently merging them. What is securely documented is that Kaiowá religious language can call the spiritual owner of maize its mother, while maize-origin accounts conflict even within one contemporary field site.

**Label:** regional Kaiowá testimony; identification with the twin-mother unresolved.

## 6. Cult, sacred places, and ritual practice

### No demonstrated exclusive cult

No temple, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, or oracle exclusively dedicated to the mythic mother of the twins has been securely documented. She belongs to a living narrative and ritual cosmos, but that does not amount to a distinct “Cult of Ñande Sy.”

There are:

- *opy* or *oga pysy*, communal prayer houses;
- male *ñanderu* and female *ñandesy* ritual specialists;
- songs, prayers, dances, naming rites, maize rites, healing, smoking, and blessing;
- recitation or restricted transmission of sacred narratives;
- ritual relations with celestial fathers, mothers, owners, and word-souls.

These institutions serve the wider Guaraní religious world rather than one exclusive deity.

### Female ritual leadership

In contemporary Kaiowá and Guaraní communities, *ñandesy* can mean a living female religious leader. An academic glossary distinguishes:

- Ñande Sy: divine female being or wife of Ñande Ru;
- *ñandesy*: female shaman or prayer specialist.

[Contemporary Kaiowá glossary](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/bitstream/1843/50260/1/Disserta%C3%A7%C3%A3o%20de%20antropologia%20Jana%C3%ADna%20pdf.pdf)

Such women lead prayers, healing, blessings, maize ceremonies, community deliberations, and territorial mobilization. Their title resonates with “our mother” but does not mean that the individual woman impersonates the mythic twins’ mother.

### Maize ritual

Kaiowá *jerosý* or *avatikyry* rites accompany the sacred white maize. Prayer is required to protect it, make it safe, and bring it into social circulation. Female and male ritual leaders may jointly officiate. The maize drink *jakairary* may be described as the crop’s own bodily broth.

The rite supplies the strongest living ritual context for “mother of maize,” but the principal named maize owner in the cited material is Jakaira.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Documented practices include food, maize drink, tobacco smoke, song, dance, prayer, and ritual blessing. No reliable evidence was located for human sacrifice to Ñande Sy, temple prostitution, an exclusive animal sacrifice, or cult violence performed in her name.

That negative finding is important. General descriptions of warfare, anthropophagy among some historical Tupi-speaking peoples, or missionary accusations against shamans cannot be transferred to this being without a source.

### Sacred structures and objects

- *Opy/oga pysy*: prayer house, not a temple exclusively belonging to Ñande Sy.
- *Mbaraka*: rattle or, in some Mbyá usage, guitar; generally associated with male ritual performance.
- *Takuapu*: bamboo stamping tube associated particularly with women.
- Baskets: practical and symbolic female craft, linked in Mbyá sayings to Ñande Sy Tenonde.
- Tobacco and smoke: media of prayer and healing in the wider religious system.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1600: reconstructable background

Comparative distribution suggests that a hero-twins complex is old within Tupi-Guaraní-speaking societies. Sixteenth-century coastal accounts later assembled by Métraux contain cognate twin figures and jaguar or culture-hero motifs.

What cannot be established is that a single being named Ñande Sy possessed the complete later dossier before European contact. “Our Mother” is too productive a title, and the early colonial record is too selective.

**Label:** modern comparative reconstruction.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: mission transformation

Franciscan and Jesuit missionaries reduced Guaraní to writing, translated Christian doctrine, formed reductions, and opposed Indigenous ritual authorities. Montoya’s works are simultaneously extraordinary linguistic documents and products of evangelization.

Missionary language:

- elevated *Tupã* into a principal translation for the Christian God;
- coined or stabilized *Tupãsy* for Mary;
- used kinship terms such as *ñandejára*, “our lord/owner”;
- redirected song, dance, sacred speech, calendrical ritual, and images into Christian forms.

The reductions also offered protection from encomienda labor and Paulista slave raids, making the history more complex than a simple tale of either benevolent preservation or total cultural destruction. Melià emphasizes that missions arose amid the damage already caused by the encomienda system. [Melià on the reductions](https://ihu.unisinos.br/37603)

The mythic Ñande Sy is not securely visible by name in the extant seventeenth-century published corpus. That silence may reflect regional difference, missionary selection, secrecy, loss, or later development; it cannot decide among them.

### Nineteenth century: dispersal and fragmentary folklore records

After the Jesuits’ expulsion in 1767 and the political and military crises of the nineteenth century, mission settlements were broken up, populations moved, and Guaraní traditions continued in Indigenous communities and regional folklore. Ambrosetti’s late nineteenth-century work in Argentina documented jaguar and transformation beliefs but did not establish a unified Ñande Sy cult.

### 1906–1914: Nimuendajú and the first secure publication

Nimuendajú’s residence among the Apapokúva produced the first securely documented extended account. His 1914 article transformed a restricted oral corpus into a printed ethnological source. This is the decisive attestation date for the dossier.

### 1928–1929: comparative ethnology and literary systematization

Métraux’s 1928 study compared historical Tupinambá religion with other Tupi-Guaraní traditions.

In 1929 Narciso R. Colmán published the Guaraní poem *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, later translated by him as *Nuestros antepasados*. Its Tupã-centered creation, human genealogies, and seven monstrous children of Taú and Keraná became enormously influential in Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine schools, tourism, illustration, and popular reference works.

It is not an ancient scripture. Recent literary scholarship describes it as an extensive fictional mythological poem that displaced Indigenous narratives in the national imagination. [CONICET literary study](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/187924/CONICET_Digital_Nro.2ec7a1e2-a607-4e69-b460-81d8d7ee9be9_L.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=5) Earlier criticism likewise observes that Colmán’s creation is closer to Genesis and gives Tupã a status unlike that found in Cadogan’s Mbyá theology. [Critical history of Paraguayan Guaraní literature](https://biblioteca.umem.mx/books/Colectivo/Literatura%20guarani%20del%20Paraguay%20%281199%29/Literatura%20guarani%20del%20Paraguay%20-%20Colectivo.pdf)

**Label:** learned literary systematization and modern invention. This judgment applies to the organized Colmán system, not necessarily to every folkloric being whose name he used.

### 1940s–1970s: Cadogan, Schaden, Susnik, Melià, and the Clastres

Cadogan documented Mbyá ritual language and narratives in collaboration with Mbyá interlocutors. His *Ayvu Rapyta* made it impossible to sustain a simple Tupã-centered “Guaraní pantheon.”

Egon Schaden compared Mbyá, Ñandeva/Chiripá, and Kaiowá religion and acculturation. Branislava Susnik reconstructed Guaraní ethnohistory and social categories. Melià joined linguistic, historical, theological, and ethnographic approaches. Pierre and Hélène Clastres brought sacred speech and the Land Without Evil into French anthropological debates.

The period created the modern scholarly Ñande Sy: not by inventing the oral figure, but by selecting, transcribing, translating, arranging, and comparing locally distinct accounts.

### Late twentieth century to the present

The myth continues in Indigenous oral instruction, ritual thought, bilingual education, published collections, art, and political-cultural affirmation. Simultaneously:

- school and tourism materials often combine Mbyá creation hymns, Paĩ genealogies, Colmán’s seven monsters, Catholic Tupã, and Argentine folklore into a synthetic pantheon;
- *ñandesy* remains a living title for female spiritual leaders;
- Guaraní and Kaiowá women use ritual authority in territorial recovery and political assemblies;
- Protestant and Pentecostal expansion has sometimes labeled traditional prayer “demonic.”

A 2022 Indigenous report documents threats, assaults, torture, attempted homicide, and burned prayer houses, including violence directed at *ñandesy* prayer women. [Instituto Socioambiental record](https://acervo.socioambiental.org/index.php/acervo/documentos/intolerancia-religiosa-racismo-religioso-e-casa-de-rezas-kaiowa-e-guarani) These attacks concern living religious leaders and traditional practice—not a documented campaign specifically against the mythic figure—but they are part of the present history of the title and its religious world.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Paraguayan national folklore

Ñande Sy is often inserted into an apparently fixed “Guaraní pantheon” headed by Tupã and populated by the seven monsters. This structure owes much to twentieth-century nationalism, schoolbooks, folklore writing, and Colmán’s literary poem.

The seven-monster genealogy should therefore not be back-projected into Nimuendajú’s or Cadogan’s twin cycle. Ñande Sy is not their grandmother, mother, or adversary in the securely documented sources.

### Argentine regional folklore

Corrientes and Misiones retellings frequently reduce the cycle to:

- a woman killed by a jaguar;
- surviving twins who become Sun and Moon;
- the elder creating the younger from their mother’s bones;
- a Christianized Tupã granting the twins their astral roles.

A 2022 Corrientes retelling, for example, combines Tupã’s first couple, the jaguar killing, differently named twins, Añá, and an explanation of lunar phases. [Modern Corrientes retelling](https://buendiacorrientes.wixsite.com/noticias/post/la-leyenda-del-sol-kuarahy-y-la-luna-jasy) It is valuable evidence for current folklore but not for precolonial doctrine.

### Mary and Marian devotion

Modern devotional writing sometimes claims that Tupãsy or the Virgin of Caacupé simply “replaced” Ñande Sy. The documented history supports contact, translation, accommodation, and possible structural resonance, but not a complete one-to-one succession.

A more defensible formulation is:

- Christian Guaraní applied maternal Indigenous vocabulary to Mary.
- Missionaries distinguished or confused *Tupãsy* and *Ñandesy* in different settings.
- Indigenous narrators incorporated Mary and Jesus into their own relational vocabulary.
- Later interpreters read Marian devotion through the mythic First Mother.

### Esotericism, occultism, and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no documented Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, traditional grimoire, or historical ceremonial-magic cult of Ñande Sy.

Modern websites may list her as a “Guaraní mother goddess,” and individuals may incorporate her into eclectic neopagan practice, but no organized reconstructionist body, continuous initiatory lineage, membership scale, or foundational ritual text was established.

Claims of an ancient universal earth-goddess cult, a secret temple priesthood, planetary correspondences, tarot correspondences, or inherited occult sigils should be labeled **modern invention unless a specific source is produced**.

### Literature, visual art, and education

The largest modern transformations come from:

- Colmán’s mythological poetry;
- Cadogan-inspired literary translations;
- Indigenous and non-Indigenous illustrations;
- school curricula in Paraguay and northeastern Argentina;
- tourist sculptures and “seven myths” routes;
- online pantheon databases that merge local variants.

Unlike the seven monsters, Ñande Sy has not developed one overwhelmingly standardized commercial image. She is generally represented narratively—as a pregnant traveler, jaguar victim, skeletal mother under reconstruction, celestial woman, or maize mother.

### Political meaning

The mythic mother herself is not widely documented as a nationalist emblem. The term *ñandesy*, however, has strong contemporary political life because female ritual leaders participate in assemblies, land-recovery movements, healing, and protection of prayer houses. Scholarship on Guaraní and Kaiowá women emphasizes that *ñandesy* speech carries divine authorization in territorial and community politics. [Study of Guaraní and Kaiowá women’s speech](https://revistas.usp.br/ra/es/article/view/211401/212820)

## 9. Historiography and scholarly disputes

### Was Ñande Sy an ancient pan-Guaraní goddess?

**For continuity:** cognate mother-and-twins narratives are distributed widely in Tupi-Guaraní-speaking South America; the structure is unlikely to have been invented independently by Nimuendajú or Cadogan.

**Against a unitary goddess:** the name is an ordinary relational phrase; genealogy, husband, sons, mode of creation, death, restoration, and divine rank all vary. There is no exclusive pan-Guaraní cult or iconography.

**Best-supported conclusion:** the twin-mother role belongs to an old, widely distributed narrative complex, while “Ñande Sy” as one sharply bounded, uniform goddess is a scholarly and popular abstraction.

### Christian influence

Parallels proposed by scholars or commentators include:

- a primordial mother and heavenly father;
- miraculous pregnancy;
- resurrection;
- a celestial paradise;
- sacred word theology;
- Mary as “our mother.”

Cadogan directly considered possible Christian influence but argued that Indigenous linguistic, ritual, and conceptual structures could not be reduced to catechesis. The evidence favors interaction rather than either absolute purity or total missionary fabrication.

Christian vocabulary clearly affected recorded forms after four centuries of contact. Conversely, Indigenous categories transformed Christian narratives: Mary could become *Ñandesy*, and Jesus *Ñandejára*.

### The two fathers and “adultery”

Some versions make each twin the son of a different divine father. Others insist on Ñande Sy’s innocence, make both twins one father’s sons, or shift the marital quarrel to an older divine couple. The Paĩ ethnographers explicitly record these conflicts.

Calling the story “the adultery of Ñande Sy” is therefore a version-specific interpretation, not a neutral title.

### Did her sons resurrect her?

The sources preserve both failure and heavenly restoration. A modern composite telling often states both without explanation: resurrection fails on earth, but the father later restores her in heaven. That reconciliation may be theologically reasonable, but unless supplied by the narrator it is editorial harmonization.

### Who is mother of maize?

The Kaiowá evidence identifies Jakaira as owner or creator of white maize and, in one testimony, calls Jakaira the maize’s *Ñande Sy*. This does not securely prove that the jaguar-slain mother of the twins is also Jakaira. The phrase may classify the crop’s spiritual owner maternally.

### Colmán’s authority

Colmán’s *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* is primary evidence for Paraguayan literary nationalism beginning in 1929. It is not primary evidence for precolonial Guaraní religion. The distinction is especially important because popular encyclopedias reproduce his Tupã-centered genealogy and seven monsters as an undifferentiated ancient pantheon.

### Evidence lost or inaccessible

Most pre-twentieth-century Guaraní religious discourse was oral. Missionaries selectively recorded what served linguistic or evangelical purposes. Ritual specialists could also restrict sacred knowledge. Epidemic disease, enslavement, displacement, warfare, mission regimentation, land loss, and language discrimination destroyed speakers and contexts.

Consequently, the absence of Ñande Sy from a seventeenth-century printed text is meaningful but not dispositive: it proves lack of attestation, not nonexistence.

### Source ethics

Cadogan and Nimuendajú preserved material unavailable elsewhere, but the printed works remain mediated:

- narrators were selected;
- restricted discourse was translated;
- oral performance became linear print;
- names were normalized;
- editors compared variants and sometimes created sequence;
- Indigenous intellectual ownership was rarely credited in modern terms.

Current scholarship increasingly treats Indigenous narrators, ritual leaders, teachers, and writers as knowledge producers rather than anonymous “informants.”

## 11. Source assessment and conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not a single biography but a history of variants.

The earliest securely documented Ñande Sy is the Apapokúva “Our Mother” published by Nimuendajú in 1914: a woman made or revealed by divine fathers, pregnant with differently fathered twins, abandoned or required to seek the senior father, misdirected after quarreling with the elder unborn child, and eaten by primordial jaguars. Her sons survive, learn what happened, attempt to restore her bones, avenge her, and become associated with Sun and Moon.

Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus supplies related but nonidentical sacred names, the Pa’i Rete Kuaray cycle, sayings of Ñande Sy Tenonde, celestial baskets and honey, and important bird and resurrection episodes. Paĩ-Tavyterã narrators make her Ñande Sy Ete, wife of Ñande Ru Pavẽ and mother of Pa’i Kuara and Jasy, while acknowledging instability between divine generations. Kaiowá accounts preserve still other relationships: in one, Ñande Ru and Ñande Sy were not spouses but primordial coevals; in crop discourse, Jakaira can be called the maize’s “mother.”

Three conclusions follow:

1. **The twin-mother complex is genuinely and widely Indigenous, not a creation of Colmán or modern internet mythology.**
2. **The neat, unitary “Guaraní goddess Ñande Sy” is a secondary abstraction.** The name is relational, and no single genealogy, theology, iconography, or cult applies everywhere.
3. **Modern layers are part of the record but must be dated.** Marian translation is colonial and postcolonial syncretism; Colmán’s seven-monster pantheon is a 1929 literary systematization; current “mother goddess,” occult, and neopagan profiles are modern receptions unless independently documented.

No evidence was found for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, a dedicated temple network, a hereditary exclusive priesthood, occult planetary correspondences, or documented interaction with Colmán’s seven monsters. Their absence is not grounds for filling the gaps with comparative mythology.
