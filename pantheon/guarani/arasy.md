# Arasy: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Goddess in Narciso R. Colmán’s Paraguayan Guaraní literary cosmogony; subsequently a goddess of Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine popular mythology. She is **not securely attested as a divine being in the earlier colonial Guaraní record or in the independently collected Apapokúva and Mbyá sacred corpora**.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Colmán’s *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* (*Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, “Our Ancestors”), presented in manuscript at the Twentieth International Congress of Americanists in Rio de Janeiro in 1922 and first published in Guaraní at Asunción in 1929. The surviving published text calls her Tupã’s wife and “Mother of the Sky.” No earlier occurrence of Arasy as this goddess has been established.
>
> **Central evidentiary conclusion:** The familiar Arasy–Tupã creation story is **[learned/literary systematization, 1920s]**, not a securely documented precolonial or pan-Guaraní theology. Its immense later diffusion has converted a character in Colmán’s literary construction into a seemingly timeless “Guaraní goddess.” Rodrigo Villalba Rojas’s textual study describes *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* as an explicitly fictional, hybrid montage that reorganized heterogeneous Indigenous, Paraguayan-folkloric, Christian, and scholarly materials into a single genealogy. [Villalba Rojas, “Rosicrán y su poema,” 2022](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/187924/CONICET_Digital_Nro.2ec7a1e2-a607-4e69-b460-81d8d7ee9be9_L.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=5)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[Learned/literary systematization, 1922–1929]** Arasy is the divine female partner of Tupã in Narciso R. Colmán’s *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra*. Tupã celebrates his marriage with her, consecrates her “Mother of the Sky,” and assigns the Moon as her dwelling. She accompanies him to Arigua/Areguá for the creation of the terrestrial world and participates in making and instructing the first human couple. In a later episode she curses Taû after he abducts Kerana; their seven children consequently appear as monsters.

**[Modern folklore derived chiefly from Colmán]** Popular summaries now call her the Guaraní goddess of the Moon, creation, fertility, agriculture, abundance, or motherhood. Of these, lunar residence, maternity, creation, and concern with fertility or reproduction can be derived broadly from Colmán; a formal agricultural cult, fixed ritual calendar, and specialized office as “goddess of crops” cannot.

**[Negative finding]** Arasy is not one of the creator beings named in León Cadogan’s Mbyá creation hymns. Those begin with the self-unfolding of Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua and subsequently name divine fathers such as Karai Ru Ete, Jakaira Ru Ete, and Tupã Ru Ete. Calling Cadogan’s Mbyá theology an “Arasy creation myth” is therefore incorrect. [Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta*, 1959](https://pueblosoriginarios.com/textos/ayvu/ayvu/ayvu%20raptya.pdf)

### Name and orthography

- **Arasy** — modern Paraguayan Guaraní spelling.
- **Arasý / Arasŷ** — occasional representations intended to emphasize the final element *sy*, “mother”; these are not separate beings.
- **Aracy / Araci** — Portuguese or Brazilian spellings frequently encountered online. They are potentially misleading because Brazilian Tupi *Araci/Jaci* traditions have their own literary and folkloric histories and should not automatically be equated with Colmán’s Arasy.
- **“Mother of the Sky,” “Mother of the Heavens,” “Mother of the Universe”** — translations or expansions of the name and Colmán’s title.
- **“Moon goddess”** — later classificatory shorthand. Colmán says that the Moon is her residence; he does not simply state that she is identical with the Moon.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic analysis of a transparent modern compound; divine application is later]** The usual segmentation is:

- *ára*: day, time, weather, sky, world, or cosmic space, depending on context;
- *sy*: mother.

Thus *Arasy* can mean “mother of the sky/world/universe.” A recent Paraguayan linguistic catalogue explicitly gives “mother of the universe, according to Narciso R. Colmán,” thereby correctly identifying Colmán as the authority for the mythological interpretation. It also reports modern personal-name usage. [Secretaría de Políticas Lingüísticas, “Nombres propios en guaraní,” 2024](https://spl.gov.py/files/DGIL/Nombre_propio_%20guarani_ESP.pdf)

**[Textual caution]** “Mother of the sky” is not demonstrated as an ancient inherited divine name merely because the compound is grammatical Guaraní. A transparent name can be coined or poetically repurposed at any date.

**[Negative lexicographical finding]** Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s 1639 *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* supplies extensive evidence for seventeenth-century Classical Guaraní but has not yielded a divine Arasy. Modern governmental lexicographical tables likewise associate *Arasy* with twentieth-century dictionaries rather than Montoya, although older component words are attested. [Montoya, *Tesoro*, 1639 facsimile catalogue](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-8657.html)

### Titles securely present in Colmán

- **Madre del Cielo** — “Mother of the Sky”; bestowed by Tupã immediately after their marriage.
- **Diosa** — narrator’s classification in the Taû–Kerana episode.
- **Wife/consort of Tupã** — *esposa* in the narrative.
- **Lunar resident** — the Moon is her *morada*, not expressly her body.
- **Mother or model of Sypavê** — Arasy tells Sypavê that the woman was born from her and made in her likeness. This makes Arasy the female divine prototype of humanity in Colmán, but not the biological ancestress of all later humans; Sypavê fills that role.

No secure ancient epigraphic, cultic, priestly, calendrical, or temple title is known.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Before Colmán

#### Montoya, 1639

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní*, printed in Madrid by Juan Sánchez in 1639, is a Guaraní–Spanish lexicon compiled in the Jesuit mission world. His *Conquista espiritual*, printed in Madrid the same year, narrates Jesuit activity in Paraguay, Paraná, Uruguay, Guayrá, and Tape. Both are indispensable but mission-conditioned sources.

**[Secure early evidence]** Montoya documents Guaraní vocabulary, religious translation, missionaries’ encounters with ritual specialists, conversion, reductions, violence, and displacement.

**[Negative finding]** Neither work securely identifies a goddess Arasy, a marriage of Tupã and Arasy, or an Arasy cult. The omission cannot prove that no local oral form ever existed, but it prevents assigning the later figure to the securely documented seventeenth-century corpus. [*Conquista espiritual*, digital edition](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Amontoya-1639-conquista)

Montoya’s glossing of *Tupã* as a name applied to the Christian God belongs to the history of missionary translation. Later scholarship commonly distinguishes earlier associations of *tupã* with thunder and weather from the monotheistic Christian sense produced or consolidated through catechesis. This history is directly relevant because Colmán’s Tupã behaves much more like the sovereign creator of Genesis than like one divine power among several.

#### Ambrosetti, 1893 and later collections

Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s 1893 “Materiales para el estudio del Folk-lore misionero” defined its regional field broadly—Misiones, Corrientes, Paraguay, Rio Grande do Sul, and Paraná—and recorded figures including Caá-Yarí, Caá-Porá, Ahó-Ahó, Yasí-Yateré, Pombero, Curupí, lobisón, and water spirits.

**[Negative finding]** Arasy is absent from Ambrosetti’s published table of regional beings. This is significant because several beings later incorporated into Colmán’s unified genealogy were already independently recorded, while Arasy was not. [Ambrosetti, “Materiales…,” 1893](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Revista_del_Jard%C3%ADn_Zool%C3%B3gico_de_Buenos_Ayres/Tomo_I/Materiales_para_el_estudio_del_Folk-lore_misionero)

This does not establish absolute nonexistence in every household or locality; it establishes that a major pre-Colmán survey does not document her.

### Colmán’s corpus

#### Manuscript and congress presentation, 1922

**[Earliest securely documented public attestation]** Colmán presented or entered the work at the Twentieth International Congress of Americanists in Rio de Janeiro in 1922, receiving a diploma and bronze medal. The text remained unpublished for seven years.

#### Guaraní edition, 1929

**[Primary source; learned/literary systematization]** *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra: poema guaraní etnogenético y mitológico; protohistoria de la raza guaraní seguida de un estudio etimológico…* appeared at Asunción through Imprenta “El Arte” and the Sociedad Científica del Paraguay. The bibliographic record describes a 96-page Guaraní volume. [Google Books bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/%C3%91ande_%C4%ADp%C4%AD_cu%C3%A9ra.html?id=9iA9AQAAIAAJ)

The work is not an anonymous sacred text or field transcription. Its framing story says that enigmatic stones or “hieroglyphs” found in a cave were intuitively interpreted into the poem. Villalba Rojas observes that this device itself signals fiction rather than authenticated archaeology.

#### Spanish version, 1937

Colmán’s own Spanish version widened circulation. The currently accessible digital text contains the full Arasy narrative, explanatory notes, and later historical-fantastic material. [Full digital text](https://www.portalguarani.com/376_narciso_ramon_colman_rosicran_/10966_nuestros_antepasados_nande_ypy_kuera__obra_de_narciso_r_colman_.html)

### The independent Apapokúva and Mbyá corpora

#### Nimuendajú, 1914

Curt Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní*, published in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914), pp. 284–403, records Apapokúva creation, destruction, eschatology, migration, and the Land Without Evil.

**[Negative finding]** It does not provide the Colmánian Arasy–Tupã marriage and Areguá anthropogony. Its absence is especially important because Nimuendajú predates Colmán’s publication and documents a named Guaraní community rather than constructing a Paraguayan national pantheon. [Nimuendajú, 1914 digital edition](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Animuendaju-1914-apapocuva)

#### Cadogan, 1959

*Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá*, published by the University of São Paulo in 1959, contains Mbyá sacred discourse in Guaraní with Spanish translation. In its opening creation sequence Ñamandu Ru Ete Tenondegua creates or unfolds his own body amid the primordial darkness; the hummingbird sustains him; divine wisdom, the foundations of speech, the earth, and other divine fathers follow.

**[Secure Mbyá tradition; explicit contrast]** Arasy is absent. Tupã Ru Ete is a subordinate or subsequently generated divine father within this plural theology, not the solitary supreme creator married to Arasy. [*Ayvu Rapyta*, complete 1959 scan](https://pueblosoriginarios.com/textos/ayvu/ayvu/ayvu%20raptya.pdf)

#### Cadogan, 1971

*Ywyra Ñe’ery: fluye del árbol la palabra*, prepared with a prologue and illustrations by Bartomeu Melià, assembles further Mbyá ethnographic and verbal material.

**[Negative finding]** Its catalogue and searchable textual record do not establish Arasy as a Mbyá goddess. [Cadogan, *Ywyra Ñe’ery*, 1971](https://portalguarani.com/793_leon_cadogan/12984_ywyra_ne_700ery_fluye_del_arbol_lapalabra__por_leon_cadogan.html)

### Comparative works

Alfred Métraux’s *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus Tupi-Guarani* (1928) is comparative and primarily Tupinambá, not evidence for a homogeneous “Tupi-Guaraní pantheon.” It is useful precisely because its assembled early chronicles do not authorize treating every Brazilian Tupi lunar or dawn figure called Araci/Jaci as Colmán’s Paraguayan Arasy. [Bibliographic record](https://pesquisa.museudoindio.gov.br/index.php/la-religion-des-tupinamba-et-ses-rapports-avec-celle-des-autres-tribus-tupi-guarani-par-a-metraux)

Pierre Clastres’s *Le Grand Parler* (1974) translates and rearranges material from Cadogan, Nimuendajú, and Clastres’s own collecting. Its creation sections concern Ñamandu and the divine fathers, not a recovery of Colmán’s Arasy. Scholarship on Clastres’s translation practice identifies exactly which *Ayvu Rapyta* chapters underlie his sections. [Study of the retranslations](https://revues.u-bordeaux-montaigne.fr/conceptos/article/download/134/289/606)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains securely attributable to Colmán

**[Learned/literary systematization]**

- celestial motherhood;
- divine femininity and the prototype of woman;
- lunar residence;
- participation in cosmogony and anthropogony;
- moral instruction concerning work, reciprocity, marriage, children, theft, killing, and care for living things;
- judgment and cursing of a transgressor;
- care of righteous humans after death;
- ability to withdraw or receive life-breath;
- responsiveness to prayer;
- protection of travelers or the dead invoked by descendants.

### Powers and characteristic actions

In Colmán, Arasy:

1. marries Tupã;
2. descends with him to Arigua/Areguá;
3. participates in creation;
4. recognizes Sypavê as made in her image and names her;
5. challenges Tupã’s promise of effortless abundance, insisting that meaningful life requires work;
6. gives Sypavê the guava, *arasa*, through wordplay with her own name;
7. promises care to the morally good after death;
8. curses Taû after the abduction of Kerana;
9. causes or sanctions the monstrous condition of the seven offspring;
10. is said to remove Yrasêma’s breath;
11. grieves at humanity’s impending destruction;
12. receives later prayers from Guarasyáva and Tupina.

### Moon, fertility, and agriculture

**[Textually secure in Colmán]** The Moon is Arasy’s dwelling. She is concerned with the first woman, reproduction, marriage, cultivation, and food.

**[Later folklore/modern elaboration]** Websites and popular art often convert this cluster into the fixed portfolios “Moon goddess,” “fertility goddess,” and “goddess of agriculture and abundance.” No independent early ritual corpus has been found for these offices. Assertions that farmers historically invoked her at lunar festivals require evidence not presently available.

**[Incorrect conflation]** Arasy is not simply a variant spelling of *Jasy*, the ordinary Guaraní word for Moon, nor has an identity with the Brazilian Tupi Jaci been historically demonstrated. Similar spelling, lunar association, and broad Tupi-Guaraní linguistic kinship are insufficient.

### Form and iconography

**[Primary text]** Colmán supplies no stable physical description, emblem, weapon, animal, garment, color, or anthropomorphic cult image for Arasy.

**[Negative archaeological finding]** No securely proven pre-Columbian statue, relief, ceramic image, petroglyph, temple image, or mission-period representation has been identified as Arasy.

**[Modern invention]** Contemporary illustrations of a youthful Indigenous woman with moon disk, feathers, white dress, stars, crops, or forest animals are artist-specific visualizations. They cannot be used retroactively to identify archaeological objects.

### Sacred objects, numbers, animals, and plants

- **Moon:** residence, securely Colmánian.
- **Guava/*arasa*:** gift to Sypavê, based on poetic similarity to *Arasy*.
- **Seven:** central to the curse narrative—seven days of conflict, seven lunar months, seven sons—but belongs to Colmán’s elaborate numerical organization, not a proven Arasy cult number.
- **No securely attested sacred animal, color, weekday, priestly implement, or offering substance.**

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogical position

**[Learned/literary systematization]**

- **Origin/parents:** unstated. Colmán does not give Arasy parents or a birth narrative.
- **Consort:** Tupã.
- **Divine children:** none expressly identified as children of Tupã and Arasy.
- **Human analogue/daughter-like creation:** Sypavê is born from or made after Arasy’s likeness; the language is archetypal rather than an ordinary genealogy.
- **Descendants:** humanity descends biologically from Rupavê and Sypavê, not directly from Arasy.
- **Possible celestial daughter:** one song calls the morning star or celestial “pearl” *Arasy memby*, “child/daughter of Arasy.” The referent is poetic and should not be inflated into a fully developed goddess without further evidence.
- **Hierarchy:** she stands beside Tupã but does not share all his prerogatives. Tupã appoints her title and dwelling and often speaks as chief creator; Arasy nevertheless corrects him, instructs humans independently, judges, curses, and receives prayers.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The celestial marriage

**[Earliest version: Colmán]** In primordial darkness Tupã observes the universe from the Sun. He celebrates his marriage to Arasy, consecrates her Mother of the Sky, and gives her the Moon as her dwelling.

The wording makes Tupã the initiating and title-conferring party. Nothing in the scene records courtship, Arasy’s origin, a wedding ritual, or independent witnesses.

**Development:** Later summaries routinely turn this short literary scene into an inherited Guaraní divine marriage and treat Sun husband plus Moon wife as an ancient binary. The proposed antiquity is unestablished.

### Descent to Areguá and creation

Tupã invites Arasy to descend to the hill of Arigua, identified by Colmán with Areguá. From there they produce seas, rivers, forests, stars, and living things. Tupã makes human statues from clay mixed with *ka’a ruvicha*, night-bird blood, sensitive-plant leaves, a millipede, and spring water. The figures dry in the Sun and become alive.

Arasy names the woman Sypavê, “mother of all,” while Tupã names the man Rupavê, “father of all.” Arasy’s speech makes woman specifically in her likeness; Tupã’s naming parallels this for man.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The clay anthropology, primordial couple, moral commandments, benevolent sovereign creator, fall-prone humanity, and later purgative flood show conspicuous Genesis-like organization. Villalba Rojas argues that Colmán’s Tupã is the Jesuit-recoded deity homologized to the God of Genesis.

### The first teaching

Arasy’s most individualized scene is her correction of Tupã. When he promises the first couple freedom from anxiety and abundant provision, she warns that a life without labor could become unhappiness or “slow death.” She instructs humans to cultivate, share food between spouses, care for children, respect descendants, avoid murder and theft, preserve animals and plants, and learn medicinal plants through effort.

**Interpretive caution:** These teachings are Colmán’s literary ethics. They cannot be quoted as a verbatim ancient Guaraní catechism.

### Taû, Kerana, and the seven monsters

Taû, “spirit of evil,” desires and abducts Kerana after defeating Angatupyry with assistance from Pytajovái. The people pray to Arasy for exemplary punishment. She responds with a curse against Taû. At intervals of seven lunar months, Kerana bears seven extraordinary sons: Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao-Ao, and Huisõ/Luisón.

**[Learned/literary systematization]** The unified genealogy is Colmán’s. Several component beings were documented independently in regional folklore, but they did not originally constitute a securely attested set of seven brothers with one pair of parents. Villalba Rojas notes that Colmán joined beings from differing oral and cultural provenances; Huisõ/Luisón reflects European werewolf tradition, while Cadogan regarded Ao-Ao as possibly borrowed into Mbyá speech from Paraguayan Guaraní.

**[Later variant]** Some summaries transfer the curse from Arasy to Angatupyry or leave the curser unnamed. These are later retellings, not the wording of Colmán’s principal narrative.

### Death and afterlife

At Yrasêma’s death, Tumê says Arasy has removed her breath. Elsewhere Arasy promises to comfort the good when they reach her dwelling after death. Later, Guarasyáva and Tupina lift their arms toward the Moon and ask Arasy to protect their missing husbands, believed to have died in pursuit of an ideal and gone to the ancestors’ celestial kingdom.

This gives Arasy a funerary and protective aspect inside the poem, but no independent mortuary cult is documented.

### Flood

Arasy grieves over the impending extermination of humankind, while Tupã orders a flood because of human cruelty encouraged by Taû. She does not initiate the destruction or rescue episode.

**[Literary development]** Colmán’s flood functions like the punitive flood of Judeo-Christian universal history. It should not be substituted for the distinct flood or world-destruction narratives recorded from Apapokúva and Mbyá communities.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult centers and temples

**[Negative finding]** No archaeological structure is securely identifiable as a temple of Arasy. No colonial inventory, Jesuit annual letter, parish record, or excavation report presently establishes an Arasy shrine, idol, altar, priesthood, treasury, or pilgrimage center.

Areguá is the setting of Colmán’s literary creation scene, not thereby an archaeologically demonstrated ancient cult center.

### Priests and religious offices

No *avare*, shaman, priestess, hereditary officiant, or sodality of Arasy is recorded. Colmán mentions *avare* elsewhere in his system, but does not institute an Arasy priesthood.

### Rituals and offerings

**[Within the literary narrative]**

- prayers are directed toward the Moon;
- petitioners ask Arasy for punishment of Taû;
- Guarasyáva and Tupina ask her to protect their absent husbands;
- moral living and reciprocal labor are presented as pleasing to the divine order.

**[Unattested claims]** No secure evidence was found for sacrifices, food offerings, lunar dances, fertility rites, temple prostitution, oracles, human sacrifice, or ritual killing performed for Arasy.

The sacrifice of Porãsy in the monster cycle is a literary self-sacrifice aimed at destroying the monsters, not a human offering to Arasy.

### Human sacrifice and violence

The requested contested-material search produced no credible source connecting Arasy to human sacrifice. Tupi and Tupinambá warfare, captive execution, and anthropophagy belong to other historical and cultural discussions and cannot be assigned to Arasy merely through the broad label “Tupi-Guaraní.”

### Ordinary worship

No securely dated votive object, dedicatory inscription, theophoric colonial name, oath formula, or household altar to Arasy is known. Modern use of **Arasy as a female personal name** is documented, but naming alone is not proof of worship.

## 7. Historical evolution of the figure

### Seventeenth century: mission records without Arasy

The Jesuit reductions sought conversion to Christianity and created a large Guaraní textual culture. Missionaries translated Christian categories through Guaraní terms, most consequentially *Tupã* for God. Montoya’s books document this environment but not Arasy.

The reductions unquestionably transformed Guaraní religion, language, settlement, and political life. It would nevertheless be methodologically invalid to say that Jesuits “suppressed the cult of Arasy”: no pre-mission Arasy cult is first demonstrated.

### 1890s–1910s: regional folklore and Indigenous ethnography

Ambrosetti recorded numerous named beings in the Paraná–Misiones–Corrientes–Paraguay cultural zone without recording Arasy. Nimuendajú documented Apapokúva theology and the Land Without Evil without Colmán’s divine couple.

These absences show that the later popular pantheon was not yet the standard organizing form of the available record.

### 1922–1929: Arasy’s documented emergence

Colmán constructed an ethnogenetic poem intended to give Paraguay a continuous cosmogonic, genealogical, territorial, and national prehistory. Its initial recognition at the 1922 Americanists’ congress lent it quasi-scholarly prestige; publication in Guaraní in 1929 gave Arasy her first securely datable textual life.

### 1937 onward: Spanish circulation

The Spanish version made the story accessible to readers who could not read literary Guaraní. Colmán’s explanatory notes and confident use of “tradition” encouraged readers to treat his synthesis as ethnographic recovery.

### 1950s–1970s: field ethnography supplies a counter-corpus

Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta* and subsequent Mbyá work revealed a markedly different religious language: Ñamandu, word-souls, divine fathers and mothers, primordial mist, ritual speech, and community-specific sacred knowledge. Schaden, Susnik, Melià, and the Clastres likewise demonstrated that “Guaraní religion” must be treated through historically and socially distinct peoples.

Arasy did not become a missing Mbyá goddess; instead, comparison exposed the specifically Paraguayan literary-national character of Colmán’s scheme.

### Late twentieth century: folklorization

Schoolbooks, dictionaries of myths, tourism, children’s literature, and regional cultural policy reproduced the family tree of Tupã, Arasy, Rupavê, Sypavê, Taû, Kerana, and the seven monsters. Characters with older independent folklore were absorbed into the Colmán genealogy; the genealogy in turn was projected backward as primordial tradition.

In northeastern Argentina the seven-monster system circulated beside older Corrientes and Misiones traditions of Pombero, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Caá-Yarí, lobisón, and animal transformations. Arasy’s presence there is therefore substantially a **twentieth-century transnational diffusion of Paraguayan literary folklore**, not evidence that Ambrosetti overlooked a fully formed ancient pantheon.

### Twenty-first century

Arasy now circulates in educational pages, fantasy illustration, folklore catalogues, personal naming, heritage events, and tourism. The figure is culturally real as modern folklore even though claims of ancient documentation are weak.

The distinction is not “real goddess versus fake goddess.” It is:

1. a securely documented literary goddess created or systematized in the 1920s;
2. adopted into popular mythology;
3. increasingly presented online as an ancient, pan-Guaraní deity;
4. absent from the best-known independently collected Mbyá creation corpus.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and popular reference works

Colmán’s poem remains the decisive modern source. Encyclopedic retellings regularly omit his authorship, turning statements from the poem into unattributed “Guaraní belief.” This is the chief mechanism by which modern invention acquires an appearance of antiquity.

### Music and ballet

**[Modern artistic reception]** Paraguayan composer Florentín Giménez wrote *Arasy*, a suite or ballet based on a mythological beauty bearing the name. It was initially performed in concert form and continues to be programmed by Paraguayan orchestras. The work is creative national art, not evidence of precolonial ritual music. [Report on the work](https://letrasparaguayas.blogspot.com/2011/12/musica-sinfonica-paraguaya-en-el-decimo.html)

### Personal names

Arasy has become a documented female personal name in Paraguay. The national linguistic catalogue records dozens of civil-registry occurrences and explains it through Colmán. This is a measurable modern reception independent of devotional worship.

### Visual culture

Modern artists portray Arasy as an Indigenous moon woman, often using blue or white clothing, feathers, silver jewelry, stars, crops, or a crescent. These images provide an iconography for books, websites, festivals, and fantasy media, but there is no continuous ancient image tradition behind them.

### Astronomy

The International Astronomical Union’s 2019 Paraguayan NameExoWorlds campaign named star HD 108147 **Tupã** and its planet **Tumearandu**, both names drawn from the Colmán-derived Paraguayan mythological system. Arasy herself was not selected. The episode nonetheless shows the system’s modern national authority: a literary genealogy could be presented internationally as Paraguayan folklore. [IAU exoplanet naming list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_exoplanets/)

### Occultism and neopaganism

**[Negative finding]** No historically documented Arasy current comparable to Wicca, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, Afro-Atlantic initiatory religions, or a published reconstructionist organization was found.

Individual devotional pages may call Arasy a lunar, mother, fertility, or Earth goddess and propose meditation or moon rituals. Without an identified community, dated liturgy, membership record, or founder’s publication, these should be labeled **[modern personal spirituality/invention]**, not “surviving Guaraní worship.”

### Political and nationalist use

Colmán’s poem participates in early twentieth-century Paraguayan cultural nationalism. It makes Paraguayan geography—Areguá, Ypacaraí, Yaguarón, Atyrá—the terrain of universal beginnings and supplies a continuous ancestry for a Guaraní-speaking nation. Villalba Rojas emphasizes its mixture of national ideology, the “race” discourse of its period, Christian moral structure, and speculative ethnology.

No specific extremist appropriation centered on Arasy was established in the reviewed material.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Arasy an ancient Guaraní goddess?

Three positions must be distinguished:

1. **Popular traditionalist position:** Arasy is an ancient Guaraní Moon and mother goddess whose story Colmán preserved.
2. **Qualified folklorist position:** Colmán organized genuine names, motifs, beings, local stories, and linguistic materials into a new synthesis; some components may predate him, but the whole genealogy does not.
3. **Literary-critical position:** Arasy as Tupã’s wife and the governing genealogy is first securely Colmánian and should be treated as literary fiction subsequently naturalized as folklore.

The surviving evidence strongly supports positions 2–3. No independent pre-1920 text presently verifies the Arasy–Tupã marriage.

### Colmán as collector or creator

Colmán claimed inspiration from sayings, legends, etymologies, place-names, and writers such as Moisés Bertoni. Yet his frame—the intuitive decipherment of fictional “hieroglyphs”—and the work’s genre, plot architecture, Christian moral dualism, Atlantis speculation, and unified family tree demonstrate creative authorship.

Villalba Rojas calls the work “entirely fictional” and a “simulacrum of ethnological-ethnographic record,” while also recognizing Colmán’s sophisticated transformation of disparate sources. The proper conclusion is not that every name was invented ex nihilo, but that the integrated pantheon and narrative cannot be treated as a field transcription.

### Mbyá absence

The absence of Arasy from *Ayvu Rapyta* is not a defect or an accidental omission to be repaired by inserting her. It is positive evidence of a different theology and textual tradition. Mbyá creation begins with Ñamandu; Tupã Ru Ete belongs to a plural company of divine fathers and has a corresponding divine mother, but that mother is not identified as Colmán’s Arasy.

### Etymological debate

The compound *ára + sy* is linguistically plausible. What remains disputed is historical, not grammatical:

- Was *Arasy* an inherited divine name?
- Was it a preexisting personal or poetic expression repurposed by Colmán?
- Did Colmán coin it from productive Guaraní elements?
- Was he influenced by Brazilian literary *Araci* traditions?

The presently accessible record cannot decide among the last three with certainty. It can say that “ancient goddess” is unproven.

### Arasy and Jasy/Araci

Modern sources often merge:

- Paraguayan Guaraní **Arasy**;
- Guaraní **Jasy**, Moon;
- Brazilian Tupi **Jaci/Iacy**, a lunar being;
- Brazilian literary **Araci**, frequently “mother of the day” or “dawn.”

These equations are **[modern comparativist conflation]** unless a dated source explicitly makes them. Sound resemblance is not historical identity.

### State of the evidence

The principal Arasy witness is a printed twentieth-century literary text, not an archaeological or epigraphic corpus. The best controls are:

- Montoya’s seventeenth-century lexicon and mission narrative;
- Ambrosetti’s pre-Colmán regional folklore;
- Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva corpus;
- Cadogan’s Mbyá texts;
- later linguistic, ethnographic, and literary-critical scholarship.

The imbalance is itself decisive: extensive narrative detail about Arasy comes almost entirely from one authorial source.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Event in the documentary history |
|---|---|
| **1609 onward** | Jesuit reductions expand among Guaraní communities; Christian translation and social transformation intensify. No Arasy cult is securely documented. |
| **1639** | Montoya publishes *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual* in Madrid. Neither securely attests Arasy as Tupã’s wife or Mother of the Sky. |
| **1767–1768** | Jesuits are expelled from Spanish dominions and the Paraguayan reductions. This is important to Guaraní religious history but cannot be described as suppression of an evidenced Arasy cult. |
| **1893** | Ambrosetti publishes “Materiales para el estudio del Folk-lore misionero,” recording numerous regional beings but not Arasy. |
| **1914** | Nimuendajú publishes the Apapokúva creation and destruction traditions. The Colmánian Arasy–Tupã cosmogony is absent. |
| **Early 1920s** | Colmán composes his Guaraní cosmogonic and ethnogenetic poem. |
| **1922** | The work is presented at the Twentieth International Congress of Americanists in Rio de Janeiro and receives recognition. This is the earliest securely documented public appearance of Colmán’s system and therefore of Arasy as reconstructable here. |
| **1929** | *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* is printed at Asunción. Arasy appears as Tupã’s wife, Mother of the Sky, lunar resident, co-creator, model of Sypavê, moral instructor, and curser of Taû. |
| **1937** | Colmán publishes or circulates his Spanish version, materially expanding access. |
| **1950s** | Arasy begins appearing in modern Paraguayan dictionaries and name lists; this is lexical reception after Colmán, not independent ancient evidence. |
| **1959** | Cadogan publishes *Ayvu Rapyta*. Its Mbyá creation theology centers Ñamandu and does not contain Arasy. |
| **1971** | Cadogan’s *Ywyra Ñe’ery*, with Bartomeu Melià, extends the Mbyá ethnographic corpus without validating Arasy as a Mbyá deity. |
| **1974** | Pierre Clastres publishes *Le Grand Parler*, drawing on Cadogan, Nimuendajú, and his field materials; it reinforces the distinctiveness of community-specific Guaraní sacred traditions. |
| **Late twentieth century** | Schoolbooks, folklore dictionaries, tourism, illustrated literature, and Argentine-Paraguayan regional media normalize Colmán’s family tree as “Guaraní mythology.” |
| **2010s** | Digital encyclopedias and myth websites spread Arasy internationally, often stripping away Colmán’s authorship and adding generalized fertility and nature-goddess attributes. |
| **2019** | Paraguay’s IAU NameExoWorlds choices, Tupã and Tumearandu, publicly internationalize the Colmán-derived mythic system, though not Arasy’s name itself. |
| **2022** | Villalba Rojas publishes a sustained literary-critical analysis identifying *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* as a fictional hybrid montage rather than direct ethnographic record. |
| **2024** | Paraguayan linguistic research documents Arasy as a modern personal name and explicitly attributes the meaning “mother of the universe” to Colmán. |
| **2026** | Arasy remains active in digital folklore, visual art, education, personal naming, and occasional individualized spirituality. No continuous institutional cult or securely excavated ancient cult has been demonstrated. |

## 11. Conclusions by evidentiary tier

### Securely attested early material

- The component Guaraní words *ára* and *sy* are old linguistic material.
- Seventeenth-century sources document Guaraní religious vocabulary and missionary recoding of *Tupã*.
- They do **not** securely document Arasy as a goddess.

### Earliest secure Arasy material

- Colmán’s work, publicly known by 1922 and printed in 1929.
- Tupã’s wife; Mother of the Sky; resident in the Moon.
- Co-creator and female prototype of humanity.
- Instructor of Sypavê and judge of Taû.

### Learned systematization

- Tupã and Arasy as a primordial married couple.
- Rupavê and Sypavê as the first humans.
- Taû and Kerana as parents of exactly seven monster brothers.
- Arasy’s curse as the organizing cause of their forms.
- The integration of these stories into one linear Paraguayan universal history.

### Regional folklore

- Several of the monsters and spirits individually predate or exist independently of Colmán’s genealogy.
- Misiones, Corrientes, and Paraguay sustain overlapping but nonidentical traditions.
- Arasy’s regional familiarity today largely reflects later circulation of Colmán’s synthesis.

### Syncretistic and Christianized features

- Sovereign creator Tupã;
- clay-made first couple;
- divine commandments;
- personified struggle of good and evil;
- punitive universal flood;
- moralized afterlife;
- national history organized on a Genesis-like chronological line.

### Modern invention or unsupported elaboration

- an ancient temple cult of Arasy;
- priestesses, fixed lunar festivals, or offerings to her;
- human sacrifice in her worship;
- canonical sacred animals, colors, weapons, or emblems;
- identity with every Tupi-Guaraní lunar goddess;
- membership in the Mbyá creation theology recorded by Cadogan;
- an unbroken Arasy neopagan or occult lineage;
- archaeological images securely identified as her.
