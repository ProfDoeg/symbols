# Tau and Kerana: Research Dossier

**Class.** **Tau:** malevolent spirit and literary personification of evil; sometimes loosely called a “god” or “demon” in later popular retellings. **Kerana:** human or semi-divinized woman, subsequently called a “goddess/personification of sleep” in Colmán’s notes and derivative folklore. **Together:** a mythic couple and parents of the “seven monsters” of Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine folklore.

**Earliest secure attestation.** The earliest presently verifiable source for **Tau and Kerana as a couple, their abduction story, Arasy’s curse, and their seven named children is Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní-language ethnogenetic poem *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* (*Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, “Our Ancestors”)**. Colmán presented the work at the Twentieth International Congress of Americanists in Rio de Janeiro in **1922**; the securely catalogued first book edition was printed by El Arte in Asunción in **1929**. His expanded or associated Spanish version appeared in **1937**. The surviving publication calls itself a mythological and ethnogenetic poem, not a verbatim ethnographic transcription. No pre-1922 text located in the colonial, linguistic, or early ethnographic corpus records Tau and Kerana’s joint narrative or the seven beings as one genealogical family. [WorldCat bibliographic record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Nande-p-cuera-%28nuestros-antepasados%29-poema-etnogenetico-y-mitologico.-Protohistoria-de-la-raza-guarani-seguida-de-un-estudio-etimologico-de-los-mitos-nombres-y-voces-empleados/oclc/23929819); [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/%C3%91ande_%C4%ADp%C4%AD_cu%C3%A9ra.html?id=9iA9AQAAIAAJ); [digitized Spanish text](https://portalguarani.com/376_narciso_ramon_colman_rosicran___/10966_nuestros_antepasados_nande_ypy_kuera__obra_de_narciso_r_colman_.html).

## Evidentiary labels

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[EARLY DOCUMENTARY]** Securely attested before Colmán, whether as language, belief, or an individual folkloric figure.
- **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]** First securely documented in Colmán’s 1922/1929 literary construction.
- **[OLDER ORAL COMPONENT POSSIBLE]** Colmán says that he drew on popular traditions, but supplies no named narrator, collection date, transcript, or pre-publication witness for the particular claim.
- **[REGIONAL FOLKLORE]** Subsequently recorded or publicly transmitted in Paraguay, Corrientes, Misiones, or neighboring areas.
- **[MODERN RETELLING]** Dependent on Colmán or on later summaries, school texts, tourism, art, or popular media.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/UNSUPPORTED]** Presented as ancient although no supporting early evidence has been located.
- **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]** A targeted corpus or search did not yield the claim; this is not proof that no oral antecedent ever existed.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Tau

**Forms:** *Taû* in Colmán’s historical spelling; modern *Tau*, *Taú*, and occasionally *Ta’u*. In the couple’s Guaraní title, *Tau ha Kerana*, *ha* means “and.”

**Colmán’s definition:** “*Taû = Espíritu del mal*.” In the poem Tau is the invisible adverse companion placed beside humanity in opposition to **Angatupyry**, the spirit of good. Illness, temptation, discord, and destructive impulses are attributed to Tau’s influence. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

Colmán compares Tau with the Araucanian/Mapuche *Huekuvu* and speculates that *Huekuvu/Hakuvú/takuvú* could be analyzed as Guaraní for something hot, stifling, or oppressive. This is not accepted historical etymology; it is one of Colmán’s free comparativist constructions. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION; DISCREDITED ETYMOLOGICAL SPECULATION]**

The form *ta’u* does occur in Montoya’s seventeenth-century lexicographic material, but as a verbal or particle-related linguistic form associated in later analysis with such semantic fields as fiction, imagining, divination, or “as if.” It is not there securely identified as the proper name of Tau, evil spirit and father of seven monsters. A lexical resemblance therefore cannot be converted into a seventeenth-century attestation of the deity. **[EARLY DOCUMENTARY LANGUAGE; ABSENCE OF MYTHIC IDENTIFICATION]**

Later epithets include:

- “spirit of evil,” “malevolent spirit” — directly dependent on Colmán.
- “chief of the evil spirits” — later narrative expansion in retellings such as “La cueva de los monstruos.”
- “personification of evil” — modern interpretive description.
- “demon” or “devil” — popular Christianizing glosses, not Colmán’s stable technical classification and not proof of a precolonial identification with Satan.
- “god of evil” — common tourism and internet terminology but textually imprecise.

There is no secure evidence of an Indigenous cult title, priesthood, litany, temple epithet, or theophoric personal name for Tau.

### Kerana

**Forms:** *Kerana*, often accented *Keraná* in Spanish. Modern Standard Paraguayan Guaraní dictionaries give *kéra* “sleep” and *kerana* “sleepy person, dormouse; somnolence”; some now add the mythological character as a secondary dictionary sense. [Paraguayan Guaraní–Spanish dictionary](https://www.mec.gob.ar/descargas/Bibliograf%C3%ADa/Educaci%C3%B3n%20Intercultural%20Biling%C3%BCe/GUARANI/avane-Diccionario-Guarani-Esp-Esp-Guarani.pdf).

Colmán explains the name simply as “*dormilona*,” because the girl spends her days asleep. He calls her Marangatu’s only daughter and, in an explanatory note, “*Diosa del sueño*.” Within the story itself, however, she begins as a beautiful woman of the first Guaraní community, not as a separately functioning cosmic deity. Her elevation to “goddess of sleep” is therefore best read as Colmán’s authorial classification or a poetic personification, not evidence of a documented cult. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

Later titles include “sleeping maiden,” “mother of the seven monsters,” and, in Sonia Cabrera’s twenty-first-century art, “matron of sleep.” The last is deliberately modern and feminist, not an inherited ancient epithet. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**

### The pair

*Tau ha Kerana* (“Tau and Kerana”) and Spanish *Tau y Kerana* designate the couple and the origin cycle of the seven folkloric monsters. No evidence supports treating the pair as primordial husband-and-wife gods worshipped together before European contact. In Colmán, Tau abducts Kerana and she later appears as his *consorte*; the text does not narrate an ordinary consensual courtship or a formal marriage. Later versions variously soften the episode into seduction, courtship, marriage, or “union.” **[LATER VARIANTS]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The pre-Colmán control corpus

The principal methodological error in popular treatments is to combine religious records from different Guaraní-speaking peoples and periods as if they described a single fixed pantheon.

#### Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, 1639

Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní*, printed in Madrid by Juan Sánchez in 1639, is an extensive Classical Guaraní–Spanish dictionary produced in the Jesuit mission environment. It is indispensable linguistic and ethnographic evidence, but it contains no presently located Tau-and-Kerana narrative and no closed genealogy of seven monstrous children. [Facsimile and catalogue](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Amontoya-1639-tesoro); [Biblioteca Nacional de Chile record](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-8657.html).

Montoya’s *Conquista espiritual*, also published in 1639, documents Jesuit activity, Indigenous resistance, prophetic leaders, mission destruction, conversion, and colonial violence. It likewise supplies no secure witness to Tau and Kerana as Colmán presents them. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**

The omission is meaningful because Montoya recorded a vast vocabulary and numerous religious concepts. It cannot prove that no related oral motif existed, but it prevents the story from being called securely seventeenth-century.

#### Curt Nimuendajú, 1914

*Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní*, published in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914), pp. 284–403, documents Apapocúva/Ñandeva creation, destruction, religious migration, and the Land Without Evil. It is an ethnographic corpus from a particular Guaraní-speaking community, not a general Paraguayan canon. The Tau–Kerana couple and their seven-child genealogy are absent. [Complete bibliographic record and scan](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Animuendaju-1914-apapocuva).

#### León Cadogan, 1959 and 1971

Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá* was published by the University of São Paulo in 1959, after preliminary chapters appeared in 1953–1954. It records Mbyá sacred speech and creation teachings, including Ñamandu, Jakaira, Karaí, Tupã Ru Ete, the origin of language, and ritual life. It is not a textual witness to Colmán’s Tau–Kerana genealogy. [Digital edition](https://acervo.socioambiental.org/acervo/livros/ayvu-rapyta-textos-miticos-de-los-mbya-guarani-del-guaira); [1959 PDF](https://biblioteca.trabalhoindigenista.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/AYVU-RAPYTA-Textos-Misticos-de-los.pdf).

Cadogan’s *Ywyra Ñe’ery: fluye del árbol la palabra* (1971) records further language, ritual, women’s roles, agriculture, dancing, curing, and everyday Mbyá culture. It likewise does not establish Tau and Kerana as Mbyá divinities. [Fundación León Cadogan digital library](https://www.fundacionleoncadogan.org/biblioteca-digital).

Consequently, citations that say Cadogan or Nimuendajú “recorded the seven children of Tau and Kerana” are usually circular modern attributions. Those authors recorded Guaraní religious traditions and, in some contexts, figures or motifs that later popular writers associate with the seven; they did not thereby attest Colmán’s complete family system.

### *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*

The 1929 book is catalogued as:

> Narciso R. Colmán, *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra (nuestros antepasados): poema etnogenético y mitológico. Protohistoria de la raza guaraní, seguida de un estudio etimológico de los mitos, nombres y voces empleados*, Asunción: Imprenta El Arte, 1929.

The original catalogue describes a 96-page Guaraní edition; later Spanish or expanded presentations differ in pagination. The online Spanish text identifies itself as Colmán’s version and carries a 1937 San Lorenzo imprint.

Colmán says that his deductions draw upon popular sayings, native myths and legends, etymologies, place-names, and works by Moisés Bertoni, Manuel Domínguez, Diógenes Decoud, Fulgencio R. Moreno, and Eloy Fariña Núñez. He calls the result an “argument” and “hypothesis.” He does not give named Indigenous narrators, dates or places of collection, parallel oral texts, or a source-by-source apparatus for the Tau–Kerana episode. **[OLDER ORAL COMPONENT POSSIBLE, BUT UNTRACEABLE]**

A 2023 academic survey of Paraguayan mythological figures accordingly characterizes Colmán’s project as an attempt to create a genealogy for familiar traditional beings through literary fiction, combining stories he had heard with his own creation. [“Personajes mitológicos conocidos en Paraguay”](https://revistas.unc.edu.ar/index.php/antropologia/article/download/34505/41172/154407).

### Later corpus

- **1937:** Colmán’s Spanish version makes the complete plot readily available beyond Guaraní-language readers.
- **1980:** Tomás L. Micó’s popular collections retell the cycle.
- **1986:** Adolfo Colombres’s *Seres sobrenaturales de la cultura popular argentina* helps circulate the beings within Argentine folklore studies.
- **1995:** Dionisio M. González Torres, *Folklore del Paraguay*, further consolidates the Paraguayan canon.
- **1996:** Elsa Leonor Pasteknik, *Misiones y sus leyendas*, represents Argentine provincial transmission.
- **2005:** Pedro Encina Ramos’s *Huellas de metal y madera* documents the Ramón Elías museum sculptures.
- **Twenty-first century:** government tourism pages, school materials, museums, digital encyclopedias, videos, and art exhibitions reproduce or revise Colmán’s genealogy.

These witnesses demonstrate the cycle’s modern cultural reality. They do not retroactively make it pre-Columbian.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Tau

In Colmán, Tau embodies or promotes evil, temptation, illness, discord, theft, vengeance, warfare, and social disintegration. He can remain invisible, assume the appearance of a handsome young man, travel rapidly, and influence human conduct. His power is not absolute: Angatupyry can initially defeat him; Tau requires Pytajovái’s intervention; Tumê later overcomes him with a gaze and triangular sign; Tupã remains sovereign.

Colmán does not give Tau a stable monstrous body, color, weapon, sacred animal, plant, day, planet, festival, or cult image. The handsome youth is a disguise, not necessarily his natural form. Descriptions of Tau as horned, red, Satanic, or a fixed zoological monster are later artistic choices. **[MODERN ICONOGRAPHY]**

### Kerana

Kerana’s defining Colmánian traits are extraordinary beauty and sleepiness. She has no independent weapon, emblem, animal, plant, celestial body, priesthood, or ritual. Her principal actions are passive or reactive: being visited, defended, abducted, impregnated, informed of her children’s deaths, mourning, and dying.

Modern art has identified that narrative passivity as a subject in its own right. Sonia Cabrera’s 2025 *Kerana, Hipnagogia* deliberately reimagined her as an autonomous figure whose sleep becomes refusal, self-possession, and a space of possibility. The exhibition was explicitly presented as a revision of Colmán, not recovery of an ancient tradition. [Contemporary exhibition report](https://www.hoy.com.py/espectaculos/arte/2025/09/08/kerana-hipnagogia-muestra-de-sonia-cabrera-en-espacio-k).

### Joint iconography

The most identifiable joint scene is **Tau abducting Kerana**. Ramón Elías sculpted the episode for his mythological museum at Capiatá. The surviving museum description calls Tau the spirit of evil, narrates his transformation, battle, abduction, and monstrous offspring, but changes the curser from Arasy to Angatupyry—an instructive example of modern variation. [Museum sculpture record](https://portalguarani.com/967_ramon_elias__/8883_tau_y_kerana__escultura_del_museo_mitologico_ramon_elias.html).

There is no identified pre-Columbian ceramic, petroglyph, colonial church image, or archaeological object securely depicting Tau, Kerana, or the couple.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogies

#### Colmán’s genealogy

Kerana is the only daughter of **Marangatu**, whose name Colmán glosses as “virtuous, good.” Marangatu is a son of the primordial human pair **Rupavê** and **Sypavê**. Kerana is consequently human within the poem’s primeval genealogy, although Colmán later calls her “goddess of sleep.”

Tau’s parentage is not supplied. **Pytajovái** is called his “old grandfather” or *tamõi*. Tau is placed on earth by **Tupã**, alongside the opposed spirit **Angatupyry**, but “placed” or commissioned is not the same as biologically begotten.

Tau and Kerana become parents of seven successive seven-month children:

1. **Teju Jagua** — first child; colossal lizard with seven dog heads; cave and fruit protector.
2. **Mbói Tu’ĩ** — second; enormous serpent with a parrot head; wetlands, amphibians, moisture, dew, and flowers.
3. **Moñái** — third; lord of fields, air, and birds; patron of theft and mischief.
4. **Jasy Jatere** — fourth; golden-haired siesta spirit with a magical wand; associated with bees and magical plants.
5. **Kurupi** — fifth; hypersexual forest being with an enormously elongated penis; abductor of women and children.
6. **Ao Ao** — sixth; cannibal and lord of fertility, hills, and mountains.
7. **Huicho/Juicho/Luisõ/Luisón** — seventh; lord of night, companion of death, cemetery-haunter, and eater of corpses.

**[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

Individual beings—especially Kurupi, Jasy Jatere, Pombero-type figures, and the European-derived lobisón—possess wider and partly independent oral histories. Their ordering as seven brothers born to Tau and Kerana is the systematizing innovation.

### Every documented interaction with another named supernatural being

- **Tupã — Tau:** Tupã leaves Tau and Angatupyry among the first humans as opposed influences, one toward evil and one toward good. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, chs. II and notes, 1922/1929. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tupã — Kerana:** no direct conversation or individual meeting is recorded. Tupã later limits the destructive reproduction of her monstrous descendants and ultimately orders a flood after Tau-inspired violence. **[INDIRECT COLMÁN RELATION; ABSENCE OF DIRECT INTERACTION]**
- **Angatupyry — Tau:** Angatupyry fights Tau for seven days and nights to prevent Kerana’s abduction; he initially has the advantage before Pytajovái intervenes. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, ch. VII. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Angatupyry — Kerana:** he appears specifically as her defender, but no dialogue between them is recorded. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Pytajovái — Tau:** Tau calls upon his grandfather for help; Pytajovái defeats Angatupyry, permitting Tau to seize Kerana. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Pytajovái — Kerana:** no independent contact is narrated. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **Arasy — Tau:** after the community prays for punishment, Arasy curses Tau; the curse takes effect in the monstrous births. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Arasy — Kerana:** Kerana’s pregnancies are the physical medium of the curse, but Colmán directs the malediction explicitly against Tau. Modern sources often say the couple was cursed, or incorrectly make Kerana Arasy’s daughter. Same source and later retellings. **[COLMÁN CORE; LATER VARIANT]**
- **Marangatu — Kerana:** father and only daughter. No substantial dialogue between them survives in the published narrative. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Marangatu — Tau:** Tau abducts Marangatu’s daughter; no direct encounter is narrated. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION; INDIRECT]**
- **Teju Jagua — Tau and Kerana:** first son. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, ch. VII and notes. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Mbói Tu’ĩ — Tau and Kerana:** second son. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Moñái — Tau and Kerana:** third son. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Jasy Jatere — Tau and Kerana:** fourth son. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Kurupi — Tau and Kerana:** fifth son. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Ao Ao — Tau and Kerana:** sixth son. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Luisõ/Luisón — Tau and Kerana:** seventh son. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tau — the seven children collectively:** while Tau is absent in his supposed overseas domain of Ruapehû near “Tauranga, New Zealand,” the children are burned; on returning he mourns them, rages, and seeks vengeance against Tumê. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, ch. XIII. The New Zealand geography reflects Colmán’s diffusionist literary fantasy, not Indigenous historical geography. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION/MODERN INVENTION]**
- **Kerana — the seven children collectively:** she gives birth at seven-month intervals; after their deaths she weeps on Cerro Jaguaru/Yaguarón and dies from grief. The spring there is said to be her continuing tears. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION WITH CLAIMED LOCAL LEGEND]**
- **Tumê Arandu — Tau:** after the monsters’ destruction Tau attempts revenge; Tumê defeats or “magnetizes” him through his gaze and a triangular symbol. Same source. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tumê Arandu — Kerana:** no direct interaction is recorded; Tumê engineers the death of her children. **[INDIRECT]**
- **Porãsy — Tau:** no meeting is recorded. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **Porãsy — Kerana:** no meeting is recorded. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **Porãsy — their seven sons:** she lures the brothers into a wedding gathering and dies with them when the cave is sealed and burned. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, ch. XI. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Gua’a — Tau:** the prophetic macaw warns Tumê of Tau’s approach. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, ch. XIII. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

No securely documented interaction connects Tau or Kerana with Ñamandu, Jakaira, Karaí Ru Ete, or the other central Mbyá figures in *Ayvu Rapyta*. Combining those pantheons produces a modern composite.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The abduction and curse

Kerana, Marangatu’s beautiful and habitually sleeping daughter, lives among the first community. Tau has long desired her. He takes the appearance of a handsome young man and visits for seven days; on the seventh he attempts to abduct her.

Angatupyry intervenes. The opposed spirits fight for seven days and nights. When Tau is near defeat, he appeals to his grandfather Pytajovái, god of courage and war, whose breath carries destructive fire. Pytajovái knocks Angatupyry down. Tau then abducts Kerana.

The community petitions Arasy. She responds with a curse against Tau. After seven lunar months Kerana bears Teju Jagua; six additional births follow at seven-month intervals.

**Earliest source:** Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, ch. VII, 1922/1929. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

#### Variants

- Colmán: Arasy curses Tau.
- Ramón Elías museum text: Angatupyry curses Tau.
- Misiones tourism text: Angatupyry curses both Tau and Kerana.
- Some summaries: Tau defeats Angatupyry by trickery, omitting Pytajovái.
- Some: Pytajovái “exiles” Tau, which contradicts Colmán’s plot, where Pytajovái actively rescues him.
- Some: Kerana is Arasy’s daughter as well as Marangatu’s; Colmán does not give that genealogy.
- Some: Tau woos and marries Kerana; Colmán narrates attempted and completed abduction.
- Modern discussions sometimes call the resulting relationship rape. Given captivity and the absence of consent in the source, sexual coercion is a reasonable interpretation, but Colmán does not narrate the sexual act. It should not be disguised as an uncomplicated romance.

### The seven-year reign of the monsters

The seven mature by age seven. Their powers and offenses spread fear, theft, abduction, sexual violence, cemetery violation, cannibalism, and communal conflict. Tau’s influence causes humans themselves to murder, burn settlements, and turn against one another.

Colmán’s numerology is highly constructed: seven visits, seven days of combat, seven pregnancies of seven months, seven children, maturity at seven years, seven years of depredation, and seven days of burning. This literary regularity is one reason to treat the closed cycle as authorial systematization rather than an untouched oral transcript.

### Porãsy’s sacrifice and the burning cave

Tumê convenes priests and leaders to end the monsters’ domination. His sister Porãsy volunteers to serve as bait. She approaches Moñái and pretends to desire him but insists on meeting all his brothers before marriage.

The seven gather in the cave at Yaguarón and drink chicha. Porãsy attempts to escape so that Tumê’s people can seal the entrance, but Moñái catches her. She orders her companions to close the cave anyway. Tumê piles wood and stone against it and sets it on fire.

Porãsy dies first; her spirit becomes the morning star. The seven brothers take seven days and nights to perish; their purified spirits rise as the “seven little goats,” the Pleiades. **[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

This episode is a literary self-sacrifice, not evidence of an actual Guaraní institution of human sacrifice. No archaeological or Jesuit source confirms a cultic rite in which a woman was burned for Tau, Kerana, or their children.

### Kerana’s death and Tau’s vengeance

Tau returns from Colmán’s fanciful overseas “domain” after the burning. Kerana tells him what occurred. He kicks a rock in rage, leaving an alleged footprint, and sets out to kill Tumê. Kerana remains on Cerro Jaguaru, cries until her tears are exhausted, and dies of grief. A spring is interpreted as her tears.

Tumê, forewarned by Gua’a, defeats Tau with a gaze and triangular sign. Tau breathes upon a mirror-like rock, leaves an ostrich-foot mark, spreads warfare, and causes a devastating fire. Tupã ultimately decides to destroy the corrupt generation with a flood.

**[COLMÁN SYSTEMATIZATION, incorporating local place-legend claims]**

The alleged footprints, cave, spring, and named hills constitute **etiological landscape traditions**. Their existence as natural or cultural sites is real; identifying them as physical evidence of mythic events is folklore and tourism, not archaeology.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No historical cult of Tau and Kerana has been demonstrated.

There are:

- no securely identified precolonial temples;
- no inscriptions or votive deposits;
- no priesthood devoted to either figure;
- no liturgical calendar;
- no sacrifice or offering formula;
- no oracle;
- no pilgrimage documented before the literary story;
- no theophoric personal-name series establishing worship;
- no colonial prosecution specifically of Tau or Kerana worship;
- no archaeological iconography securely naming them.

Tau functions in Colmán as a moral-cosmic agent, not the recipient of devotion. Kerana functions principally as a narrative woman and mother.

### Material and public sites

- **Moñái Kuare/Itá Potrero, Yaguarón:** identified in Colmán and municipal tradition as the monsters’ death cave.
- **Cerro Jaguaru/Yaguarón:** associated with Kerana’s mourning and death.
- **Paseo de los Mitos, Yaguarón:** a modern municipal heritage and tourism installation presenting Colmán’s story as local mythology. [Municipal account](https://www.yaguaron.gov.py/descubre-el-paseo-de-los-mitos/).
- **Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías, Capiatá:** modern sculptures including Tau’s abduction of Kerana.
- **Paseo Mitológico Correntino:** modern public sculpture and municipal folklore interpretation in Corrientes; its Lobizón presentation explicitly identifies him as the seventh child of Tau and Kerana. [Corrientes municipal tourism page](https://visitcorrientes.tur.ar/quehacer/paseomitologicosangeronimo/).
- **Misiones provincial tourism:** official presentation of Teju Jagua as their first child demonstrates regional adoption in northeastern Argentina. [Misiones tourism page](https://misiones.tur.ar/mitos-y-leyendas-misioneras/).

These are acts of commemoration, heritage-making, education, and tourism—not evidence of ancient cult continuity.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1639: unrecorded possibilities

Guaraní-speaking peoples possessed rich, locally differentiated oral religions before conquest. Specific antecedents to individual “seven monsters” may be old. For Tau and Kerana as a couple, however, there is no datable precolonial witness. **[UNESTABLISHED]**

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: missions and colonial recording

Jesuits documented Classical Guaraní, Indigenous leaders, ritual specialists, religious resistance, and many concepts while translating Christianity into Guaraní. Their accounts are mediated by missionary aims and cannot be treated as neutral inventories. Nevertheless, they do not record the Tau–Kerana family cycle.

The Christian-looking polarity between Tau and Angatupyry, Colmán’s use of “infernal fire,” and his moralized cosmic opposition have encouraged theories of Jesuit influence. Syncretism is plausible at the broad level of Paraguayan folk culture, but no document presently establishes that Jesuit missionaries invented Tau and Kerana. That specific claim is **unsupported**.

### Nineteenth century: regional folklore components

Paraguayan, Brazilian, and northeastern Argentine folklore contained figures later placed among the seven. Kurupi/Curupira-type beings, Jasy Jatere, and the Iberian-derived lobisón have histories not reducible to Colmán. The **lobisón/Luisón** especially reveals European–regional mixture: its werewolf and seventh-son associations belong to an Atlantic Iberian and Rioplatense complex.

What remains undemonstrated is the parents-and-seven-siblings framework before Colmán.

### 1922–1929: literary creation and systematization

Colmán presented an ambitious Guaraní ethnogenetic epic at the 1922 Congress of Americanists and published it in 1929. It sought a comprehensive national antiquity, binding language, folklore beings, etymologies, geography, Atlantic diffusion theories, Christian material, and a moral history into one continuous “protohistory.”

This is the decisive historical moment in Tau and Kerana’s documented record. Colmán did not necessarily invent every name or motif, but he supplied the surviving canonical genealogy, sequence, cosmology, and interlocking plot.

### 1937–late twentieth century: canonization

The Spanish version broadened access. Folklore books, classrooms, public recitation, museums, and illustrated collections detached the story from its literary provenance. The seven became widely known as *los siete mitos del Paraguay*. In northeastern Argentina, especially Corrientes and Misiones, regional collections and cultural institutions adopted the genealogy alongside locally transmitted forms of Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Pombero, and Lobizón.

Thus “folkloric canon” is accurate if it means a twentieth- and twenty-first-century shared popular canon. “Ancient pan-Guaraní pantheon” is not.

### Twenty-first century: public heritage and critical reassessment

Tourism, museums, school exercises, government procurement, and online summaries keep the cycle highly visible. At the same time, scholars and artists increasingly identify *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* as a literary construction assembled from folklore and authorial invention.

The tradition is therefore neither simply “fake” nor securely ancient. It is a genuine modern Paraguayan tradition whose component beings have uneven histories and whose canonical genealogy is documentably literary.

---

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### National and regional folklore

The couple now operates primarily as an origin device: audiences often know Tau and Kerana because they explain why seven otherwise heterogeneous beings belong together. The family structure has become a mnemonic map of Paraguayan folklore.

Corrientes and Misiones reproduce the cycle in public sculpture, provincial tourism, school materials, and popular collections. This spread is historical evidence of reception, not evidence that every Guaraní community shared the genealogy before 1929.

### Museums and tourism

Ramón Elías’s sculpture made the abduction visually concrete. Yaguarón’s Paseo de los Mitos localizes the story in an explorable landscape. Corrientes’s mythological walks place descendants such as Lobizón within provincial identity. These displays often omit Colmán’s authorship or present the narrative simply as “Guaraní mythology,” accelerating its naturalization as anonymous tradition.

### Literature and education

The poem itself is a landmark of written Guaraní literature. Its characters have entered children’s books, bilingual exercises, school plays, illustrated myth collections, and digital quizzes. A Paraguayan government transparency record from 2024 lists acquisition or circulation of material titled *7 mitos guaraníes: los hijos de Tau y Kerana*, demonstrating continuing institutional educational use. [Government record](https://informacionpublica.paraguay.gov.py/public/2024/1730372156_1_87.3141339-2024-RAMIREZ.pdf).

### Contemporary feminist reinterpretation

Sonia Cabrera’s 2025 exhibition *Kerana, Hipnagogia* directly addresses Kerana’s near-total lack of agency in Colmán. The artist reconceives sleeping not as laziness but as autonomy and opposition to the Paraguayan ideal of the endlessly laboring *kuña guapa*. This is an explicit, documented modern reinvention. [Pinta Asunción exhibition statement](https://www.pinta.art/Pinta/Past-Editions/Pinta-Asuncion-Art-Week/2025/Participaiting-spaces/Galleries/K-Arte-y-Naturaleza).

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no well-documented continuous Tau–Kerana cult, organized reconstructionist movement, Golden Dawn lineage, Theosophical doctrine, chaos-magical current, or historically significant modern grimoire tradition devoted to the pair. Generic online “mythology” and spirituality pages should not be mistaken for organized worship. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**

### Political and nationalist use

Colmán’s epic participates in the larger twentieth-century construction of a national Paraguayan antiquity centered on Guaraní language and imagined ancestry. That does not mean the government commissioned the myth or that Tau and Kerana became official political symbols. Their more demonstrable political role is diffuse: they serve as emblems of a mestizo Guaraní-speaking national culture.

No documented extremist appropriation specific to Tau and Kerana was found. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**

### Commercial use

Images of the pair and the seven appear in souvenirs, illustrated books, museums, tourism branding, craft objects, and educational products. This commercial circulation largely reproduces Colmán’s canon.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is this “authentic Guaraní mythology”?

The question requires disaggregation.

1. **Yes, in modern ethnographic usage:** the story is genuinely told, taught, represented, and believed or enjoyed within Paraguayan and regional popular culture.
2. **No, if “authentic” means securely documented before contact or shared by all Guaraní peoples:** the evidence does not support that.
3. **Partly, at the component level:** several children are older or independent folkloric beings.
4. **Colmán’s family structure:** securely documentary only from his twentieth-century epic.

The strongest formulation supported by current evidence is:

> Tau and Kerana are the parental couple of a modern Paraguayan folkloric canon fixed by Narciso R. Colmán from heterogeneous oral, linguistic, local, literary, Christian, and authorial materials.

### Did Colmán merely record an older oral legend?

Colmán says he drew on native legends and popular memory. That deserves to be reported, not dismissed. Yet he supplies no collection transcript or provenance for this episode. His highly patterned numerology, universal genealogy, New Zealand excursion, Atlantis-like diffusionism, Christian moral dualism, explanatory etymologies, and placement of already diverse beings into one family reveal extensive authorial construction.

Accordingly:

- an older oral seed is possible;
- the precise plot is not independently verified;
- the complete family canon is demonstrably Colmánian in its earliest surviving form.

### Christian influence

Tau versus Angatupyry resembles a moral dualism of Evil against Good. Colmán’s vocabulary includes “diabolical,” “infernal,” sin, purification by fire, prophetic figures, and a flood. These features may reflect centuries of colonial Christianity and Colmán’s own literary environment. But the pair is not simply Adam and Eve: Tau is an abducting spirit, Kerana descends from the first humans, and their children are folkloric monsters.

Claims that missionaries deliberately invented the entire cycle to frighten Indigenous converts remain speculative unless tied to a specific mission manuscript.

### Comparative and diffusionist excesses

Colmán linked Guaraní words and peoples to Araucanian terminology, the Caribbean, Oceania, New Zealand, Africa, and an imagined ancient global migration. Such theories belong to early twentieth-century diffusionism and national-romantic protohistory; they are not accepted linguistic or archaeological reconstructions.

### Gender and violence

Older retellings frequently romanticize the story as Tau “falling in love” and the pair “uniting.” The primary narrative says he disguises himself, attempts abduction, defeats her defender through supernatural assistance, and carries her away. Kerana never gives consent in the surviving episode. Later labeling of the event as rape is interpretive but consistent with the coercive narrative; it should be identified as interpretation rather than quoted as an explicit Colmánian scene.

Arasy’s punishment also operates through Kerana’s body. Modern retellings that say Kerana herself deserved or shared the curse add a victim-blaming element not required by Colmán’s wording.

### Sacrifice

Porãsy’s death is a voluntary literary self-sacrifice in the myth. There is no evidence that the scene records an institutional Guaraní practice of human sacrifice. Confusing narrative sacrifice with cultic archaeology is methodologically invalid.

### State of the evidence

The most important surviving items are:

- Montoya’s 1639 dictionary and mission history;
- Nimuendajú’s 1914 Apapocúva corpus;
- the 1929 Guaraní printing of *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra*;
- Colmán’s 1937 Spanish version;
- Cadogan’s recordings and manuscripts, including *Ayvu Rapyta*;
- later folklore collections, museum objects, and public installations.

A definitive study would require collation of every edition of Colmán, archival investigation of his manuscripts and correspondence, and systematic comparison with pre-1922 regional folklore collections by Ambrosetti and others. No such evidence currently cited overturns the 1922/1929 documentary terminus.

## 11. Conclusions

Tau and Kerana’s “life” in the evidence begins not in a datable pre-Columbian inscription or colonial testimony but in a remarkable modern literary act. Colmán took names, folk beings, Guaraní vocabulary, regional landscapes, Christianized moral categories, speculative etymologies, and claimed oral materials and made them kin.

That system became extraordinarily successful. The couple’s seven children now form a genuine folkloric canon in Paraguay and have substantial public currency in Corrientes and Misiones. Museums sculpt them, schools teach them, municipalities attach them to caves and hills, and artists revise their gender politics.

The evidentiary boundary nevertheless remains firm:

- **Tau and Kerana as parents of a closed sequence of seven monsters:** first securely attested in Colmán.
- **The complete abduction–battle–curse–birth–immolation–vengeance cycle:** Colmánian literary systematization.
- **Individual child-beings and some associated motifs:** often older, regional, syncretic, or independently folkloric.
- **A pre-Columbian pan-Guaraní cult, temple system, sacred iconography, or universal pantheon containing the couple:** not established.
- **Human sacrifice to the pair, temple prostitution, organized occultism, extremist appropriation, or continuous modern worship:** no evidence found.
- **The tradition’s importance today:** securely and abundantly documented.
