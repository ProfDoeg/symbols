# Mbói Tu'i: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest secure source, a male **monstrous supernatural being** (*mito*, *fenómeno maléfico*, later often “deity,” “spirit,” or wetland guardian), not a documented precolonial god with an institutional cult.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní-language literary poem *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* (*Nuestros antepasados*), presented in an early form at the International Congress of Americanists in 1922 and published at Asunción in 1929. No earlier occurrence of Mbói Tu'i as a named being has been securely located in the principal seventeenth- to early-twentieth-century Guaraní linguistic, missionary, or ethnographic corpora examined here.  
> **Evidentiary conclusion:** The familiar serpent-parrot, its position as the second child of Taû and Kerana, its wetland domain, and its participation in the death of the seven monsters are first securely documented as elements of Colmán’s modern literary synthesis. Some components may draw on oral motifs, but their existence as a coherent pre-Colmán indigenous tradition cannot presently be demonstrated.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

### Name

The normalized modern spelling is **Mbói Tu'ĩ**, although **Mbói Tu'i**, **Mbói Tu'î**, **Mboi Tu'i**, **Mboí-Tuí**, and Colmán’s older orthographic **Mbói Tú'i** all occur.

The name is transparently analyzable in Paraguayan Guaraní:

- **mbói**: “snake,” “serpent,” or “viper.” The Proyecto Montoya dictionary gives *serpiente, víbora*.
- **tu'ĩ**: a parakeet, small parrot, or cotorra; popular Spanish renderings often simplify it to *loro*.
- Consequently, **Mbói Tu'ĩ** means approximately “snake-parakeet,” conventionally “serpent-parrot” or “viper-parrot.”

The modern Argentine Guaraní–Spanish educational dictionary also treats **mboitu'i** as a lexicalized name for an “enormous mythological animal with a reptile’s body and parrot’s head.” That entry documents the name’s present educational standardization, not its antiquity.

### Classifications through time

- **1929 — learned literary systematization:** Colmán calls Mbói Tu'i one of the seven *mitos* and one of the malformed, malevolent beings born to Taû and Kerana. His accompanying gloss calls it a *hado protector*—a guardian or tutelary fate—of amphibians and aquatic animals.
- **Later Paraguayan folklore:** “monster,” *ser mitológico*, *mito mayor*, guardian, or protector.
- **Touristic and pedagogical reception:** sometimes elevated to “god” or “deity of waterways.” Yaguarón’s official Paseo de los Mitos says that it is “respected as a god and protector of amphibians and flowers.” This is a modern classificatory choice, not evidence for a temple-based precolonial divinity.
- **Modern fantasy and cryptid media:** monster, cryptid, boss creature, or environmental guardian.

No primary source establishes a priesthood, prayer, sacrifice, temple, divine image, or independent worship directed specifically to Mbói Tu'i.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The negative prehistory

Absence from a source is not proof that no oral narrative existed, but it limits what can responsibly be reconstructed.

#### Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, 1639

Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní*, printed at Madrid in 1639 and preserved in several complete digital facsimiles, is the major early lexicographic witness for mission-era Guaraní. It documents Guaraní vocabulary and expressions in extensive context. No securely identifiable entry for **Mbói Tu'i as a named supernatural being** has been found in it. Individual snake and bird vocabulary does not attest the compound character.

Montoya’s *Conquista espiritual* likewise supplies evidence for Jesuit activity, indigenous religion as missionaries perceived it, forced displacement, enslavement raids, and mission history, but no verified Mbói Tu'i episode has been located. Claims that Jesuits specifically demonized, suppressed, or renamed Mbói Tu'i therefore remain unsubstantiated.

**Tier:** securely attested early corpus; **Mbói Tu'i absent as far as presently verifiable**.

#### Curt Nimuendajú, 1914

Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní*, published in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914), records Apapocúva creation, destruction, migration, and Land-without-Evil traditions. It does not furnish the familiar Taû–Kerana genealogy or an identifiable serpent-parrot named Mbói Tu'i.

This matters because Nimuendajú’s material predates Colmán and derives from a Guaraní-speaking indigenous community rather than from Paraguayan nationalist literary synthesis.

**Tier:** securely attested indigenous ethnographic corpus; **no verified Mbói Tu'i**.

#### Other tradition-specific corpora

No verified Mbói Tu'i occurrence has been located in the relevant parts of:

- Ambrosetti’s late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century folklore and ethnography of Misiones;
- the indigenous cosmological corpora later analyzed by Alfred Métraux;
- Cadogan’s Mbyá texts;
- Pierre or Hélène Clastres’s editions and interpretations of Guaraní sacred speech and the Land without Evil;
- the better-known collections of Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini;
- published descriptions of Apapocúva, Mbyá, and related Guaraní religious systems by Nimuendajú, Schaden, Melià, and Sušnik.

This dossier does not claim that every manuscript or oral archive has been exhaustively checked. The finding is narrower: **no citable passage has been established that places Mbói Tu'i in those corpora before Colmán**.

### Narciso R. Colmán, *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra*

Colmán composed his long Guaraní cosmogonic poem in the early 1920s. An initial version received recognition at the 1922 International Congress of Americanists; the complete book appeared at Asunción in 1929 as *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra (Nuestros antepasados): poema etnogenético y mitológico; protohistoria de la raza guaraní seguida de un estudio etimológico de los mitos, nombres y voces empleados*. The first edition contained approximately three thousand verses; Colmán later issued a Spanish version, including a 1937 edition widely reproduced online.

The first edition is bibliographically documented as a 96-page Guaraní work printed by Imprenta El Arte in 1929. The accessible Spanish text says:

> “Mbói Tu'î (víbora-loro) – Serpiente de formas colosales con cabeza de loro, fue el segundo hijo del connubio maléfico. Sus dominios se extendían por los esteros. Protector de los anfibios, del rocío, de la humedad y de las flores.”

Its explanatory note expands the aquatic remit to “the amphibians [and] aquatic animals.”

This establishes, no later than 1929:

- the compound name;
- enormous serpent form;
- parrot head or beak;
- male gender and second birth-order;
- parents Taû and Kerana;
- membership in a set of seven monsters;
- dominion over marshes;
- guardianship of amphibians, aquatic animals, dew, moisture, and flowers;
- participation in the cave-and-fire episode;
- a loud parrot-like vocalization.

### The status of Colmán’s account

Rodrigo Villalba Rojas’s textual study describes *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* as an “entirely fictional” literary construction and an ethnographic-looking *simulacro*: Colmán fitted heterogeneous indigenous motifs, Paraguayan-Creole folklore, etiological tales, nationalist discourse, Christian structures, and Moisés Bertoni’s speculative ethnology into one genealogy.

This does not make Mbói Tu'i culturally unreal. Colmán’s figure became genuine Paraguayan folklore through publication, school circulation, oral retelling, museums, public sculpture, tourism, and digital media. It does mean that “recorded in 1929” cannot be silently converted into “ancient pan-Guaraní god.”

### Cadogan and *Ayvu Rapyta*

León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá* was issued in preliminary installments in 1953–1954 and in complete form by the University of São Paulo in 1959. It records Mbyá sacred speech, including Ñamandu, Karai, Jakaira, Tupã, divine spouses, creation, souls, ritual vocabulary, and the perfected land.

Modern websites sometimes claim that Cadogan collected the “seven children of Taû and Kerana” and Mbói Tu'i. The available bibliographic descriptions and searchable text do not support that assertion. The claim appears to be a **modern source-misattribution**, probably produced by combining Colmán’s nationally familiar cycle with Cadogan’s authority as an ethnographer.

**Tier:** modern misinformation unless an exact Cadogan passage can be produced.

## 3. Domain, form, attributes, and iconography

### Earliest securely documented form

Colmán gives a **colossal serpent with a parrot’s head or beak**. He does not supply a complete anatomical catalogue.

Secure Colmán-era attributes are:

- immense serpentine body;
- parrot head or parrot beak;
- marsh or estero domain;
- amphibian and aquatic-animal guardianship;
- associations with dew, moisture, and flowers;
- a conspicuous parrot-like cry.

### The cry that terrifies

Two layers must be separated.

- **1929 literary core:** During the destruction of the seven monsters, Colmán writes that Teju Jagua roars while “Mbói-tu'i … *okororõ*”; Villalba translates this as Mbói Tu'i “cacareaba”—cackled or clucked. The Spanish version calls it *gárrulo* and says that it emitted wild cries. Together with its siblings, the noise makes the earth tremble.
- **Later folklore elaboration:** Websites and popular summaries turn this scene-bound cry into a permanent supernatural power: a terrible squawk audible for miles that instills terror in everyone who hears it. No earlier independent oral testimony for that enlarged attribute has been identified.

Thus “the cry that terrifies” is culturally widespread today, but only its noisy bird-like basis is securely present in Colmán; distance, paralysis, bodily injury, or supernatural sonic force are later embellishments.

### Later physical additions

Modern descriptions commonly add:

- a blood-red forked tongue;
- striped or mottled scales;
- plumage over the head, spine, or back;
- two legs near the waist;
- a malicious or hypnotic gaze;
- green, yellow, red, black, or white feathers;
- exclusive consumption of fruit or bitter oranges;
- the power to drown people who harm wetland animals.

These features are **later folklore or modern visual invention** unless a dated pre-Colmán source can be supplied. They should not be retrojected into the 1929 description.

A Portuguese Wikipedia-derived narrative has accumulated manifest fantasy additions—mechanical tail components, rubellite, venom-altering enzymes, false memories, prophetic visions, and cries displacing internal organs. These are modern collaborative-fiction contamination, not Guaraní ethnography.

### Iconographic history

No securely attributed pre-1929 image is known. The iconographic template proceeds directly from the name and Colmán’s prose: snake body plus parrot head.

Important modern embodiments include:

- illustrations associated with later editions and adaptations of Colmán;
- the figure at the Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías in Capiatá, displayed among Paraguayan folkloric beings;
- the municipal Paseo de los Mitos at Yaguarón;
- contemporary drawings, online fantasy art, school illustrations, and tabletop-game designs.

The frequent wings, legs, crests, bright macaw coloration, and dragon-like anatomy vary by artist and do not constitute a stable traditional canon.

No historically documented sacred color, number, weekday, planet, weapon, ritual implement, plant, or offering belongs specifically to Mbói Tu'i. “Seven” structures Colmán’s entire monster genealogy, not an independently attested cultic number of Mbói Tu'i.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

### Colmán’s genealogy

In Colmán’s literary system:

- **Father:** **Taû**, malevolent spirit.
- **Mother:** **Kerana**, daughter of Marangatu and granddaughter of the first human pair Rupavê and Sypavê.
- **Birth order:** second.
- **Siblings:** Teju Jagua, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao-Ao, and Huicho/Juicho/Luisón.
- **Curse:** Arasy condemns Taû’s offspring to monstrous form.
- **Death:** Mbói Tu'i and the other six are trapped and burned in a cave through a plan executed by Porãsy and Tumê Arandu.

This is Colmán’s constructed family tree. Earlier Guaraní sources do not establish it as a common or pan-Guaraní genealogy.

### Every documented named interaction

- **Taû — fatherhood:** Taû fathers Mbói Tu'i with Kerana; after the seven monsters are killed, Taû curses the Guaraní collectively. *Source:* Colmán, *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra*, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Kerana — motherhood:** Kerana bears Mbói Tu'i as the second of seven premature monstrous sons, at seven-month intervals. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Arasy — prenatal curse:** Arasy curses Taû’s descendants, determining their monstrous birth. No separate encounter between her and Mbói Tu'i is narrated. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Teju Jagua — sibling and joint death:** First-born brother; both assemble in the Yaguarón cave, cry out, and die in the fire. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Moñái — sibling and convener:** Third brother; in later prose adaptations Moñái brings the siblings to the cave for his supposed marriage to Porãsy. All die together. *Source:* Colmán, 1929; later adaptations make Moñái’s coordinating action more explicit. **Tier: learned systematization / later retelling.**
- **Jasy Jatere — sibling and joint death:** No individual conversation or action with Mbói Tu'i is specified; both attend and perish in the cave. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Kurupi — sibling and joint death:** Same collective interaction; no independent Mbói Tu'i–Kurupi episode is attested. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Ao-Ao — sibling and joint death:** Same collective interaction; no separate two-character myth is attested. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Luisón/Huicho/Juicho — sibling and joint death:** Same collective interaction. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Porãsy — deception and shared death:** Porãsy draws the monsters into the wedding trap. Once escape fails, she orders her people to seal or burn the cave, sacrificing herself with Mbói Tu'i and his brothers. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Tumê Arandu — execution of the plan:** Tumê and his followers accumulate wood and stone outside the cave; he sets the fire that kills Mbói Tu'i, the six brothers, and Porãsy. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **Tier: learned literary systematization.**
- **Tupã — indirect relation only:** Tupã belongs to Colmán’s constructed divine order and sanctions the campaign against evil, but the text supplies no discrete face-to-face Mbói Tu'i–Tupã scene. **Tier: learned literary systematization; direct interaction absent.**
- **Rupavê — no interaction in Colmán’s monster cycle:** He is an ancestor of Kerana, not an actor in Mbói Tu'i’s documented 1929 episodes. A direct confrontation belongs to the later “secret road” story discussed below. **Tier: absence in the earliest source; later accretion.**

No secure interaction with Ñamandu, Karai, Jakaira, the Mbyá divine fathers, Pombero, or any non-Colmán deity has been established. Merely appearing in modern lists of “Guaraní mythology” is not an interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Birth of the seven monsters

**Earliest source:** Colmán, 1929.  
**Tier:** learned literary systematization.

Taû, Colmán’s embodiment of evil, becomes joined to Kerana. Arasy curses their descendants. After seven months Kerana bears the first malformed child, Teju Jagua; subsequent births occur at seven-month intervals until there are seven sons. Mbói Tu'i is second.

Each child is assigned a body, moral character, natural territory, and tutelary function. Mbói Tu'i receives the shape of a colossal serpent-parrot and the esteros, amphibians, aquatic animals, moisture, dew, and flowers.

The repeated sevens and the sharply ordered moral genealogy are integral to Colmán’s architecture. They cannot presently be demonstrated in pre-1929 indigenous testimony.

### B. Porãsy and the cave of the monsters

**Earliest source:** Colmán, 1929.  
**Tier:** learned literary systematization, subsequently canonical in Paraguayan national folklore.

The seven brothers bring terror and disorder. Porãsy volunteers for a plan to end them and offers herself as a bride or object of courtship, conventionally to Moñái. The monsters gather in a cave at Yaguarón for the marriage celebration. Porãsy attempts to escape or gives the waiting people their signal, but she cannot get clear. She tells them to proceed despite her presence.

Tumê Arandu and his companions close the cave entrance with wood and stone and set it on fire. Inside, Teju Jagua roars and Mbói Tu'i emits parrot-like cries; the monsters’ combined noise shakes the earth. All seven and Porãsy die.

This episode provides the only securely early narrative action particular to Mbói Tu'i: he gathers with his brothers, participates in their cries, and is burned.

The story contains death through collective incineration and Porãsy’s deliberate self-sacrifice. Calling this “human sacrifice” without qualification would be misleading: it is a literary heroic self-sacrifice inside Colmán’s mythic narrative, not evidence of an actual Guaraní sacrificial institution.

### C. “Mbói-Tuí and the secret path”

**Secure documentation:** Argentine governmental educational publication, circulating digitally by the 2010s–2021.  
**Tier:** late folklore retelling or modern literary invention; origin presently unverified.

In this quite different origin story, Mbói Tu'i was originally a beautiful parrot living in the **Land without Evil**, whose guarded entrance was known only to Rupavê. The parrot knew a secret path. Malicious outsiders intoxicated it with *lechiguana* honey, causing it to chatter and reveal the route. Rupavê discovered the betrayal and cursed the bird: it lost flight, its wings became legs, and it became a serpent with a parrot’s beak, thereafter eating bitter oranges.

This story is absent from the located Mbói Tu'i passages of Colmán. It mixes the nationally standardized Rupavê genealogy with the ethnographically important but historically complex Land-without-Evil tradition. Its precise collector, narrator, locality, and date are not supplied by the educational retelling.

It must therefore not be cited as a precolonial Mbyá, Apapocúva, or pan-Guaraní myth. It is valuable evidence for modern Argentine Mesopotamian folklore and pedagogy.

### D. Environmental guardian narrative

**Documentation:** late twentieth- and twenty-first-century school, tourism, web, and popular-cultural texts.  
**Tier:** later folklore with substantial modern ecological elaboration.

Mbói Tu'i is now commonly said to patrol wetlands, protect amphibians and aquatic animals, and punish overfishing, hunting, pollution, or damage to marsh habitats. Colmán supports the guardian role but not the developed conservationist morality. The latter translates the figure into current environmental language.

## 6. Cult, ritual, temples, and material practice

There is **no secure evidence of an independent cult of Mbói Tu'i**.

Specifically, the research located no documented:

- temple or shrine;
- priesthood or hereditary religious office;
- festival date;
- prayer, hymn, invocation, or ritual formula;
- sacrifice or food offering;
- oracle or divination practice;
- pilgrimage;
- theophoric personal name;
- votive object;
- colonial prosecution of Mbói Tu'i worship;
- archaeological image securely identified as Mbói Tu'i.

The being’s association with flowers, dew, fruit, amphibians, and water is descriptive and tutelary in Colmán; it does not itself prove offerings involving those things.

### Modern public heritage sites

- **Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías, Capiatá:** houses modern sculptural embodiments of Paraguayan mythological beings, including Mbói Tu'i. This is a folklore museum, not an excavated sanctuary.
- **Paseo de los Mitos, Yaguarón:** a municipal heritage-tourism presentation locates the familiar figures in the landscape and calls Mbói Tu'i a god and protector.
- **Educational installations and tourism planning:** Paraguayan state tourism material uses Mbói Tu'i among figures suitable for themed interpretive trails and multimedia attractions.

These sites are evidence of modern commemoration and cultural canonization.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1639: unreconstructable

Serpent, bird, water-spirit, and hybrid-animal motifs certainly existed widely in lowland South America, but no evidence presently connects a specifically named Mbói Tu'i to a precontact date.

### 1639–early twentieth century: documented Guaraní traditions, no secure Mbói Tu'i

Montoya’s linguistic and missionary sources, later Jesuit records, and early ethnographic collections preserve extensive material about Guaraní language and religion. None yet supplies a secure occurrence of Mbói Tu'i as the serpent-parrot son of Taû and Kerana.

Christian missionization, slave raids, relocation, war, and the destruction of indigenous communities undeniably transformed the transmission of Guaraní religion. Those general historical processes cannot, without a source, be turned into a specific claim that missionaries suppressed Mbói Tu'i.

### 1914: comparative baseline

Nimuendajú publishes his Apapocúva creation and destruction narratives. Their divine personnel and cosmological structure differ substantially from Colmán’s later national pantheon. Mbói Tu'i is not verified in this corpus.

### 1922–1929: creation or first textual crystallization

Colmán organizes numerous figures into an ordered national genealogy. Mbói Tu'i becomes the second of seven sons and receives a stable morphology and domain.

Whether Colmán invented the name outright, adapted an unrecorded oral expression, or combined existing snake and bird motifs cannot be decided from the surviving evidence. “First documented by Colmán” is secure; “invented entirely by Colmán” is a plausible but stronger conclusion.

### 1930s–late twentieth century: canonization as Paraguayan folklore

Colmán’s Spanish version and later adaptations made the cycle accessible beyond readers of literary Guaraní. Folklore handbooks, schools, illustrated retellings, and public art detached the characters from the poem and presented them as traditional “Guaraní mythology.”

During this transmission:

- *hado protector* became “god,” “deity,” or “guardian”;
- the cry became a long-range terror power;
- artists supplied legs, wings, elaborate feathers, colors, and a forked tongue;
- Mbói Tu'i’s aquatic guardianship expanded from amphibians and marshes to all wetlands and aquatic creatures.

### Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries: transnational regional folklore

The figure now circulates in Paraguay and in Spanish-language educational and folklore material from Corrientes, Misiones, and the broader Argentine Mesopotamia. That circulation is real contemporary regional tradition, but it does not establish ancient distribution across all Guaraní peoples.

The Argentine “secret path to the Land without Evil” story illustrates further narrative growth: Mbói Tu'i acquires a former parrot existence, intoxication, betrayal, punishment, and transformation.

### Present

Mbói Tu'i is institutionalized in dictionaries, bilingual teaching materials, museums, municipal tourism, environmental retellings, fantasy art, and games. This is a robust modern afterlife, although no continuous historical worship has been demonstrated.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Education and language policy

Argentine bilingual educational material describes Mbói Tu'i in Guaraní and Spanish as an enormous ugly being with a snake-like body, parrot head, control over lagoons and wetlands, a fruit diet, and responsibility for terrestrial and aquatic animals, dew, green fruit, and flowers.

The accompanying dictionary’s inclusion of *mboitu'i* demonstrates that the name has become part of standardized regional cultural vocabulary.

### Tourism and museums

The Yaguarón municipal presentation closely follows Colmán: second child of Taû and Kerana, snake body, parrot beak, marsh domain, and protector of amphibians and flowers. The Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías similarly turns the literary-folkloric figure into a durable three-dimensional image.

### Literature

Dirma Pardo de Carugati’s modern micro-story renders Mbói Tu'i as a glittering, feather-headed reptile-bird and “Lord of the marshes,” sleeping between stones when it is not crawling or flying. Flight is a modern variant inconsistent with the wingless serpentine emphasis of most versions.

### Games and popular fantasy

A commercial third-party supplement produced for a MÖRK BORG folklore game jam presents Mbói Tu'i as a monster “ready to be slain.” It repeats the huge serpent, parrot head, forked red tongue, scales, feathers, terrifying squawk, and wetland guardianship. This is explicitly a modern game adaptation.

Claims that the *Monster Hunter* creature Najarala was directly inspired by Mbói Tu'i circulate among fans, but a reliable Capcom statement was not located; the connection should be treated as fan inference.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated searches found no substantial historical role for Mbói Tu'i in:

- Renaissance hermeticism;
- Theosophy;
- the Golden Dawn;
- ceremonial magic;
- established modern grimoires;
- documented Guaraní reconstructionist religious organizations.

A Brazilian tabletop role-playing text assigns Mbói Tu'i the power to teach a shamanic “walk on water” ability. That is game mechanics, not evidence of occult transmission.

No reliable data establish organized neopagan worship, numbers of adherents, or a continuous cult. Individual devotional or imaginative uses may exist online but cannot presently be characterized as a documented movement.

### Political and commercial appropriation

Mbói Tu'i participates broadly in Paraguayan national and regional identity-making. Colmán’s work itself belongs to an early twentieth-century project of constructing a heroic Guaraní ancestry for the Paraguayan nation. Current municipal tourism and merchandise continue that nationalization.

No documented extremist appropriation, racial-supremacist cult, or organized political movement centered on Mbói Tu'i was found.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### Indigenous deity or literary creation?

Two positions must be separated.

1. **Traditionalist/popular position:** Mbói Tu'i is an ancestral Guaraní being, transmitted orally and merely written down by Colmán.
2. **Critical literary-historical position:** The coherent seven-monster genealogy is Colmán’s construction from diverse folklore, indigenous motifs, Christian structures, nationalism, and speculative ethnology.

The evidence favors the second position for the **system as a system**. No presently identified pre-1929 document names Mbói Tu'i or assigns it this genealogy and domain. Oral antecedents remain possible but unproven.

### “Absence proves invention”

That conclusion is too strong. Colonial archives are incomplete and hostile recording conditions destroyed or distorted indigenous knowledge. A lack of early record cannot demonstrate that nobody ever told a serpent-parrot story.

The defensible formulation is:

> Mbói Tu'i’s name, genealogy, morphology, and function are first securely attested in Colmán; no independent earlier witness has yet been established.

### Misattribution to Cadogan

Modern web pages repeatedly cite *Ayvu Rapyta* for the seven monsters. That attribution conflicts with the known contents and religious vocabulary of Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus. It should be rejected unless accompanied by volume, chapter, page, Guaraní text, and edition.

### Confusion with other serpents

Mbói Tu'i must not be merged automatically with:

- **Mbói Tata/Boitatá**, the fiery serpent;
- **Mbói Jagua**, a serpent-dog or other hybrid in regional nomenclature;
- **Ñakyrãmbói/Jekyranamboia**, another fabulous creature used by Colmán;
- river-serpent figures in the Iguaçu Falls legend;
- Mesoamerican feathered serpents such as Quetzalcoatl.

Visual resemblance does not prove historical syncretism. No evidence of an indigenous equation between Mbói Tu'i and Quetzalcoatl was found.

### Sacrifice, violence, and suppression

- **Human sacrifice:** Porãsy’s death is a voluntary literary self-sacrifice in Colmán’s story, not archaeological or ethnographic evidence of ritual killing.
- **Cult violence:** none documented.
- **Missionary suppression:** the Jesuit mission system transformed and often condemned indigenous religion generally, but no Mbói Tu'i-specific suppression is attested.
- **Forced conversion:** historically relevant to Guaraní religious change, but not a documented episode in this figure’s individual history.
- **Syncretism:** Colmán’s poem contains Christian and European narrative structures, including a moralized cosmic opposition and a flood comparable to Genesis. That is literary-cultural synthesis, not a documented formal identification of Mbói Tu'i with a Christian demon or saint.

| 2010s–2021 | Argentine state educational material circulates “Mbói-Tuí and the secret path,” a different transformation legend with no identified early source. |
| 2020s | Yaguarón tourism, the Ramón Elías museum, digital folklore sites, fantasy art, and tabletop games continue the figure’s modern cultural life. |
| 2026 | Mbói Tu'i remains a recognized Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine folkloric being; no independent pre-Colmán attestation or historical institutional cult has been demonstrated. |

## 11. Sources and assessment

The core evidentiary chain is unusually short. Colmán’s text is the indispensable primary witness; Villalba Rojas supplies the most important critical contextualization. Montoya, Nimuendajú, and Cadogan are principally **control corpora**: they show what early missionary and indigenous ethnographic records contain and prevent Colmán’s national pantheon from being projected indiscriminately onto all Guaraní-speaking peoples.

Modern government, museum, tourism, and game sources document reception rather than antiquity. Anonymous mythology websites are useful only for tracing recent accretions and misattributions.
