# Moñái: Research Dossier

**Class:** Monstrous supernatural being or *monstruo* in Paraguayan and regional Guaraní-language folklore; in modern popular and educational classifications also “god,” “spirit,” “demon,” guardian, or tutelary being. The evidence does not establish an ancient institutional cult of Moñái as a god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Narciso R. Colmán’s Guaraní-language literary epic *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* (*Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, “Our Ancestors”), written in the early 1920s, presented to the XX International Congress of Americanists in Rio de Janeiro in 1922, and first published in Paraguay in 1929. This is the earliest securely datable source presently located that makes Moñái the third of the seven monstrous children of Taú and Keraná and incorporates him into the Porãsy episode. The full Spanish version appeared in 1937. The individual being may draw on older oral material: León Cadogan later recorded a separate Mbyá Moñái associated with other solitary animal-monsters. That evidence does not, however, establish the entire seven-sibling genealogy before Colmán.

---

## Evidentiary key

- **[Secure early attestation]** Datable primary documentation rather than an inference about prehistoric tradition.
- **[Indigenous ethnographic attestation]** Material collected from identified or described Indigenous communities, though recorded after European contact.
- **[Literary systematization]** A named author has joined, altered, or organized disparate folklore into a designed literary system.
- **[Folklore]** A belief or narrative presented as orally circulating, with the collector, place, and date supplied where recoverable.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally different form or narrative.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An argument made by a modern researcher rather than an explicit statement in a primary tradition.
- **[Modern popular elaboration]** Schoolbooks, websites, museums, fiction, art, or other recent retellings.
- **[Modern invention/unverified attribution]** A recent claim that cannot be traced to the source to which it is attributed.

The central source problem is fundamental: **the Colmán cycle, Mbyá religious texts, Paraguayan mestizo folklore, and Argentine regional legends are related but not interchangeable corpora.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[Literary systematization, 1922–1929]** In Colmán’s *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra*, Moñái is the third of seven prematurely born monsters produced by Taú’s sexual seizure of Keraná and the curse pronounced over the resulting offspring. Colmán calls him a hideous, deformed lord of theft. He and Kurupí afflict the inhabitants of Atyrá through robbery and sexual violence respectively. Porãsy pretends to love the aged Moñái, persuades him to summon his brothers to a wedding in a cave near Yaguarón, and enables their destruction by fire at the cost of her own life. Rodrigo Villalba Rojas’s textual study emphasizes that Colmán assembled diverse Indigenous, Paraguayan-creole, Christian, literary, and contemporary ethnological materials into a linear genealogy; the work is therefore a literary “montage,” not a transparent transcript of a single precolonial theology. [Villalba Rojas, “Rosicrán y su poema,” 2022](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/187924/CONICET_Digital_Nro.2ec7a1e2-a607-4e69-b460-81d8d7ee9be9_L.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=5)

**[Indigenous ethnographic attestation, collected mid-twentieth century]** Cadogan’s Mbyá evidence places a being called Moñái among animal-associated solitary monsters rather than in Colmán’s seven-child family. A note in *Ayvu Rapyta* says that the *Ka’i jagua*, a monkey-monster, lives in association with “Moñái or Ta’ytetu Jagua,” the latter glossed as a small wild-pig monster. In the same discussion, Cadogan says that such monsters are extraordinary solitary exemplars of animal species, usually inhabiting isolated places and not reproducing. They are powerful but do not equal the gods or divine emissaries. This is positive evidence for a Mbyá being named Moñái, but not for the horned serpent, thief, third son, or Porãsy cycle. [Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta*, searchable edition](https://pueblosoriginarios.com/textos/ayvu/ayvu/ayvu%20raptya.pdf)

### Name and spelling

Documented modern spellings include **Moñái**, **Moñai**, **Moñáy**, and unaccented **Monai**. Colmán’s older orthography commonly appears as *Moñái* or *Moñáy*. The spelling with **ñ** represents the Guaraní palatal nasal; the accent marks final stress in Spanish-oriented transcription.

Antonio Guasch’s Guaraní–Spanish dictionary records lowercase **moñái** as “(ratero), astuto, pillo, tunante”: approximately “petty thief, crafty person, rogue, rascal.” [Guasch dictionary, letter M](https://portalguarani.com/1688_antonio_guasch/13583_diccionario_guarani__castellano_letra_m__por_antonio_guasch.html) This lexical sense fits Colmán’s characterization extremely well, but it does not by itself prove that the common noun produced the supernatural name or vice versa. A secure historical etymology was not found.

Claims that the name means “lord of the air,” “horned serpent,” or “spirit of the fields” are descriptions, not demonstrated etymologies.

### Titles and epithets

- **“Lord of theft” / protector of thieves and rogues.** **[Literary systematization; later folklore]** Colmán’s characterization supports the association with theft; Guasch’s lexical entry strengthens the semantic connection. Modern educational and museum accounts call him *protector de los ladrones y los pícaros*.
- **“Lord of the fields,” “of the earth,” “of the winds,” or “of the air.”** **[Modern folklore/popular elaboration]** These titles occur in museum, educational, and folkloric retellings. Their variation shows that no stable liturgical title is attested.
- **Protector of birds or wild animals.** **[Later folklore/modern interpretation]** The Ramón Elías Museum tradition explicitly preserves a dispute: Moñái is described both as a frightening serpent and as a protector of wild animals, winds, and birds. [Ramón Elías Museum account](https://www.portalguarani.com/967_ramon_elias__/8886_monai__escultura_del_museo_mitologico_ramon_elias.html)
- **“God of the fields, air, and birds.”** **[Modern educational classification]** Argentine bilingual teaching material uses *dios*, while other sources prefer monster or protector. “God” should not be retrojected into the securely attested Mbyá classification, where Cadogan distinguishes animal-monsters from gods.

### Claimed equations

The equation **Moñái = Karai Yvyra’ija**, sometimes found online, is not established by the accessible primary evidence. Cadogan describes *Karugua yvyra’ija* as a duende whom some confuse with another being, but this is not an explicit identification with Moñái. Likewise, equations with **Mboi Tatá**, the Brazilian fire-serpent, or with the biblical serpent are modern comparisons, not documented traditional identities.

No interpretatio graeca/romana-type ancient equation is applicable.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial documentation

**[Absence of evidence]** Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual*, both published in Madrid in 1639, are essential witnesses for colonial Guaraní vocabulary, missionary encounters, Indigenous ritual specialists, and Jesuit suppression of non-Christian religion. No verified Moñái narrative or seven-monster genealogy has been located in them. Their silence cannot prove that no related oral being existed, but it prevents dating the Colmán form to the seventeenth century. [Digital catalogue and facsimile of Montoya’s *Tesoro*](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Amontoya-1639-tesoro)

The surviving Jesuit-reduction literature documents the destruction or displacement of Indigenous religious authority in general, but no reduction record located here establishes a temple, priesthood, rite, exorcism, or suppression campaign specifically directed at Moñái.

### Colmán corpus

**[Secure early attestation / literary systematization]**

- **Early 1920s:** Colmán composes a Guaraní poem of more than three thousand verses.
- **1922:** The work is presented at the XX International Congress of Americanists in Rio de Janeiro and receives a diploma and bronze medal.
- **1929:** *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra (Nuestros antepasados): poema guaraní etnogenético y mitológico* is published by Imprenta El Arte and the Sociedad Científica del Paraguay. This is the first secure printed narrative source for Moñái’s position in the seven-monster cycle.
- **1937:** Colmán issues his own expanded Spanish version, *Génesis de la raza guaraní: nuestros antepasados*, including an etymological study. The catalogue describes it explicitly as a poem, not an anonymous scripture or oral transcript. [1937 edition catalogue](https://books.google.com/books/about/G%C3%A9nesis_de_la_raza_guaran%C3%AD.html?hl=en&id=ttJVAAAAMAAJ&output=html_text)

Colmán describes Moñái in narrative and explanatory bestiary-like notes: his genealogy, deformity, criminal behavior, and habitat are components of an authorially constructed epic.

### Cadogan corpus

**[Indigenous ethnographic attestation]**

- **1959:** León Cadogan publishes *Ayvu Rapyta: textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá*, based chiefly on sacred and mythic material communicated by Mbyá specialists, prominently Pablo Vera. Moñái occurs only marginally in the accessible text, associated with *Ka’i jagua* or *Ta’ytetu Jagua*. The elaborate Taú–Keraná cycle widely attributed online to *Ayvu Rapyta* is not supported by the relevant passage.
- **1971:** *Ywyra Ñe’ery* further documents Mbyá language and thought. No securely verified Moñái narrative from that work was located.
- **1992:** Cadogan’s posthumous *Diccionario mbyá-guaraní–castellano*, prepared under Bartomeu Melià’s direction, consolidates vocabulary and cultural notes accumulated over decades. Villalba Rojas cites its page 27 as evidence that Cadogan recorded Moñái and Teju-jagua as Mbyá monsters. [Digital bibliographic record](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Acadogan-1992-diccionario)

Cadogan’s evidence makes a categorical correction possible: **Moñái was not simply invented ex nihilo by Colmán**, but the evidence still does not make Colmán’s genealogy an ancient Mbyá canon.

### Nimuendajú and comparative Guaraní corpora

Curt Nimuendajú’s 1914 *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* records Apapokúva creation, destruction, eschatological, and Land-Without-Evil traditions. No Moñái entry was located. That absence is important because the work predates Colmán and is an extensive account of another Guaraní-speaking community’s religion. It shows that no single undifferentiated “Guaraní pantheon” can be assumed. Nimuendajú’s work appeared in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46, pp. 284–403. [Bibliographic discussion of Nimuendajú’s 1914 work](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/printer--friendly/text%3Aarnaud-1983-curt)

The same caution applies to Métraux’s Tupinambá synthesis, Hélène Clastres’s work on the Land Without Evil, Pierre Clastres’s translations of Guaraní sacred speech, and Melià’s linguistic and ethnohistorical studies. They supply comparative history of Tupí-Guaraní religions; they do not independently attest the Colmán Moñái cycle.

### Argentine regional collections

Later Argentine compilations locate **Moñay** in Corrientes, Misiones, and adjacent Brazilian territory. A reported regional description gives him a huge body with very short legs, moving or rolling with a noise like a partly filled barrel. This differs radically from the elegant, tree-climbing serpent of popular Paraguayan representations. Modern summaries attribute this form to Ambrosetti, Perkins Hidalgo, and Pasternak, but the original passages were not recovered here; the attribution should therefore remain provisional. [Modern discussion preserving the attribution and variant](https://ferenikeart.wordpress.com/2024/09/12/monai/)

Searches of online descriptions of Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini’s Argentine folklore collections did not yield an independently checkable Moñái text. They should not be cited as witnesses without volume, tale number, informant, and locality.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Colmán-era core

**[Literary systematization]** The firmest early attributes are deformity, age, theft, menace to Atyrá, association with a cave, and susceptibility to Porãsy’s deception. Villalba Rojas summarizes Colmán’s Moñái as *horrendo y deforme señor del hurto*, “hideous and deformed lord of theft.”

### Horned-serpent form

**[Later canonical popular form; exact first appearance unresolved]** The now-standard Moñái is:

- an enormous or thick-bodied serpent;
- sometimes only about a metre long but as thick as a tree trunk;
- armed with pointed teeth;
- bearing two straight, colored, iridescent, or mobile hornlike projections;
- able to climb trees;
- capable of mesmerizing birds through the horns, treated as “antennae”;
- resident in fields, wetlands, deep streams, hills, or caves;
- a predator of birds in some versions, their protector in others;
- a thief of crops, household food, and possessions, which he hides as trophies.

These details are repeatedly reproduced in twentieth- and twenty-first-century folklore, museum, and educational presentations. The Ramón Elías Museum sculpture and catalogue present the extraordinary serpent with two spikes and pointed teeth while also acknowledging the protector/predator dispute.

The horns are frequently described as hypnotic antennae, but an exact quotation establishing this feature in the 1929 Guaraní text has not been located. It is safest to classify it as a later canonical feature rather than securely as Colmán’s wording.

### Domain contradictions

The traditions divide into at least four overlapping conceptions:

1. **Thief and patron of thieves** — morally negative and socially disruptive.
2. **Predator or devourer** — dangerous to animals and sometimes human travelers.
3. **Guardian of birds, wild animals, wind, fields, and wetlands** — tutelary rather than predatory.
4. **Solitary animal-monster** — Cadogan’s Mbyá category, not necessarily a moral criminal.

These must not be harmonized. “Protecting birds” and “hypnotizing birds in order to eat them” are competing explanations of the avian association.

### Sacred correspondences

No reliable early source establishes sacred colors, days, numbers, festivals, planets, offerings, or celestial rulership belonging specifically to Moñái.

The number **three** derives from Colmán’s ordering of Moñái as third child. The larger emphasis on **seven** is central to Colmán’s cycle, but Villalba Rojas argues that Colmán’s claim that seven was ill-omened among the Guaraní is false and that his numerology belongs substantially to Western narrative and Judeo-Christian influence.

## 4. Genealogy and interactions with named beings

### Genealogy

**[Literary systematization, Colmán]**

- **Father:** **Taú**, personification or spirit of evil in Colmán.
- **Mother:** **Keraná**, daughter of Marangatu; her name is explained through sleep or sleepiness. Colmán’s narrative implies that Taú abducts and rapes her.
- **Curser of the offspring:** **Arasy**, whose curse causes Keraná’s seven-month pregnancies to issue in seven monsters.
- **Siblings:** **Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Jasy Jatere, Kurupí, Ao-Ao,** and **Huisõ/Luisõ/Luisón**.
- **Proposed bride and killer:** **Porãsy**.
- **Human/divine opponent behind the plan:** **Tume-Arandu**, Porãsy’s brother and priestly wise man.
- **No consort or offspring:** None securely attested.

This family tree is not present in the verified Mbyá Moñái notice. It is Colmán’s unifying mechanism.

### Every documented interaction

- **Taú — father of Moñái.** Taú rapes or forcibly takes Keraná; Moñái is the third monstrous result of the cursed union. *Source:* Colmán, *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra*, chapters VII–VIII, written early 1920s, published 1929. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Keraná — mother of Moñái.** She bears the seven monsters after seven-month gestations. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Arasy — pronounces the curse governing Moñái’s monstrous birth.** *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **[Literary systematization with Judeo-Christian moral structure]**

- **Teju Jagua — brother and fellow participant in the cave gathering.** Modern fuller retellings say Moñái leaves Porãsy in Teju Jagua’s care while summoning the others; all are trapped at the wedding. *Source:* Colmán cycle, 1929; elaborated in later retellings. **[Literary systematization / later canonical elaboration]**

- **Mbói Tu’ĩ — brother summoned by Moñái and trapped at the wedding.** *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Jasy Jatere — brother summoned by Moñái and trapped at the wedding.** *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Kurupí — brother and parallel afflicter of Atyrá.** Their robberies and sexual violence prompt the inhabitants to seek Tume-Arandu’s help; Kurupí joins the wedding gathering. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Ao-Ao — brother summoned by Moñái and trapped at the wedding.** *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Huisõ/Luisón — brother summoned by Moñái and trapped at the wedding.** Huisõ is itself a Guaraní phonological adaptation of Spanish/Portuguese *lobizón/lobisomem*, demonstrating the cycle’s colonial and transatlantic mixture. *Source:* Colmán, 1929; analysis by Villalba Rojas, 2022. **[Literary systematization / syncretistic inclusion]**

- **Porãsy — seducer, proposed bride, captor, and co-victim.** She feigns love, requires all Moñái’s brothers to attend their wedding, attempts to escape after they become intoxicated, is caught by Moñái, and commands that the cave be sealed and burned despite her presence. *Source:* Colmán, 1929, especially the episode culminating around verse 1054. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Tume-Arandu — strategic opponent.** The people of Atyrá seek his help; his sister Porãsy undertakes the fatal plan. *Source:* Colmán, 1929. **[Literary systematization]**

- **Ka’i jagua — associated animal-monster.** Cadogan says this monkey-monster lives associated with Moñái or Ta’ytetu Jagua. No combat, kinship, or hierarchy is specified. *Source:* Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta*, 1959. **[Indigenous ethnographic attestation]**

- **Ta’ytetu Jagua — possible alternate designation or associated wild-pig monster.** Cadogan’s syntax permits ambiguity over whether this is another name for Moñái or a second possible companion of Ka’i jagua. *Source:* Cadogan, 1959. **[Indigenous ethnographic attestation; ambiguous relation]**

- **Tupã — no early direct interaction securely documented.** Modern accounts sometimes say Tupã ordered or rewarded the monsters’ destruction, but Colmán’s plot centers on Tume-Arandu and Porãsy. **[Modern elaboration unless a precise textual citation is supplied]**

- **Angatupyry — no direct Moñái encounter located.** Angatupyry opposes Taú in Colmán’s cosmic moral scheme but is not securely shown interacting personally with Moñái. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The birth of the seven monsters

**[Literary systematization, 1929]** Colmán sets his first-world history around a conflict between Angatupyry, the good principle, and Taú, the evil principle. Taú distracts or evades Keraná’s protector, seizes her, and fathers seven children. Arasy curses the offspring. After gestations of seven months, Keraná bears the monsters in sequence, Moñái third.

This story’s structure contains unmistakably colonial and literary elements: sharply polarized good and evil, sin and divine punishment, the malediction of offspring for the father’s act, repeated sacred numerology, and an ordered genealogy. Colmán presents these through Guaraní language and regional beings but does not preserve an uncontaminated pre-contact scripture.

### Moñái’s thefts

**[Colmán core with later elaboration]** Moñái steals from the communities around Atyrá and conceals his plunder. Later accounts amplify the social consequence: because no human thief is discovered, villagers accuse one another, and communal trust collapses. This makes Moñái’s injury both material and social.

The designation “protector of thieves and rogues” may be a folkloric extension from his own thieving behavior and from the ordinary adjective *moñái*, “rogue/thief.” No documented thieves’ guild, offering, prayer, or invocation to Moñái has been found.

### Porãsy’s sacrifice and the burning cave

**[Literary systematization, subsequently canonical Paraguayan folklore]** The people appeal to Tume-Arandu. Porãsy, his beautiful, chaste young sister, volunteers to entrap the monsters. She approaches the aged Moñái, professes love, and promises marriage on condition that all his brothers attend.

Moñái gathers Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Jasy Jatere, Kurupí, Ao-Ao, and Huisõ in a cave on a hill near Yaguarón. At the wedding the brothers become drunk and quarrel over the remaining drink. Porãsy attempts to escape, but Moñái discovers the deception and prevents her flight. Knowing that she cannot survive, she commands her people outside to seal the cave completely—Colmán gives the order *hatã katu pemboty*, “close it firmly/completely”—and burn those within. Porãsy dies with the monsters.

Later versions add that Porãsy becomes the morning star, **Mbyja Ko’ẽ**, while the monsters become the Pleiades or “Seven Little Goats.” The celestial transformations should be cited to specific retellings rather than assumed to be universal. Argentine teaching material reproduces this version and locates the resulting cave, *Moñaikuare*, near Yaguarón. [Argentine bilingual educational text](https://www.mec.gob.ar/descargas/Documentos/Educacion%20Intercultural%20Bilingue/GUARANI/avane.pdf)

### Regional rolling monster

**[Regional variant, documentation incomplete]** The Corrientes–Misiones form attributed to earlier folklorists has an immense, short-legged animal body and rolls noisily like a barrel. It is not obviously a horned snake and may represent a distinct regional creature brought under the same name. Until the Ambrosetti, Perkins Hidalgo, or Pasternak passage is produced, its date and relationship to Colmán remain uncertain.

### Mbyá animal-monster complex

**[Indigenous ethnographic attestation, 1959]** Cadogan’s Moñái belongs to a world of solitary monstrous counterparts of animal species. These beings inhabit remote places, may announce themselves through thunder, wind, or earth tremors, and remain inferior to gods and divine emissaries. The short notice does not describe theft, Porãsy, hypnotic horns, or seven siblings. It may preserve an Indigenous substrate from which Colmán borrowed a name or being, but that developmental connection is a scholarly inference, not a narrated Mbyá genealogy.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival, sacrificial calendar, hymn, prayer, theophoric personal name, oracle, pilgrimage, or institutional cult devoted specifically to Moñái is securely documented.

No archaeological image has been identified as Moñái. Serpent imagery or unusual animal figures cannot be assigned to him merely by resemblance.

No evidence was found for:

- human sacrifice offered **to** Moñái;
- temple prostitution;
- animal sacrifice to Moñái;
- a thieves’ ritual invocation;
- an organized initiatory or magical society;
- a precolonial or colonial priesthood bearing his name.

Porãsy’s death is a **literary self-sacrifice during an extermination plot**, not documented cultic human sacrifice.

Cadogan mentions tying a chicken at the mouth of **Kurupí’s** cave as an initial emissary when seeking contact with a group of monsters. That prescription must not be reassigned to Moñái without evidence.

Toponyms such as **Moñaikuare**—“Moñái’s hole/lair”—and localized claims about dens near Yaguarón, Lake Ypoá, Atyrá, or Cerro Kavaju constitute evidence of geographic folklore, not proof that an ancient sanctuary stood there.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1639: unreconstructable prehistory

Guaraní-speaking peoples possessed extensive religious, shamanic, owner-spirit, animal-master, and monster traditions before European invasion. The evidence presently available cannot isolate a pre-contact Moñái or date the name archaeologically.

### 1639–eighteenth century: missionary documentation and suppression

Montoya and the Jesuit archive record Guaraní language and the missionary campaign against Indigenous religious specialists and practice. They do not securely attest Moñái. Christianity profoundly altered regional vocabulary and cosmology; nevertheless, the popular claim that Jesuits specifically invented Moñái as a Guaraní Satan is unsupported.

### Nineteenth to early twentieth century: regional folklore collection

Collectors in Paraguay, Corrientes, and Misiones documented numerous beings—Pombero, Jasy Jatere, Kurupí, Ao-Ao, lobizón, animal-monsters, and local serpents. A distinct Moñay is attributed to Ambrosetti and other collectors, but its primary citation remains to be verified.

### 1922–1929: Colmán’s synthesis

This is the decisive historical turning point. Colmán organizes heterogeneous figures into one family and embeds them in an ethnogenetic epic of the Paraguayan “race.” Moñái becomes the third child of Taú and Keraná, lord of theft, suitor of Porãsy, and agent through whom the seven brothers are assembled and destroyed.

### 1937–1960: Spanish transmission and official canonization

Colmán’s 1937 Spanish version makes the work accessible beyond elite Guaraní-literate circles. In 1960 the Paraguayan Ministry of National Defence’s Institute of History and Military Museum republishes it in the series *Paraguay Histórico*, during Alfredo Stroessner’s dictatorship. Villalba Rojas interprets this as official canonization: a literary national mythology was endorsed as the origin-story of the Paraguayan people.

### 1950s–1990s: ethnographic counter-record

Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta* and later dictionary, along with Nimuendajú’s belatedly translated work and studies by Melià, Schaden, Susnik, Hélène and Pierre Clastres, showed the depth and diversity of actual Guaraní religious systems. Their evidence undermines any claim that Colmán’s system is a universal Guaraní canon.

In 1978 Augusto Roa Bastos’s *Las culturas condenadas* foregrounded the suppression of Indigenous peoples and honored Cadogan’s work. Around 1980 Rubén Bareiro Saguier criticized nationalist folklorization for obscuring Indigenous cultures. Villalba Rojas identifies these works as a direct intellectual countercurrent to treating *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra* as ethnographic scripture.

### Late twentieth century to the present: folklore, school canon, and art

Moñái now circulates through school curricula, museum sculpture, comics, regional tales, tourism, paintings, online bestiaries, fantasy art, and games. The seven-monster family has consequently become genuine modern Paraguayan folklore even where its supposedly ancient genealogy is Colmán’s construction. A literary system can become tradition through repeated performance and teaching; its later folkloric reality does not retroactively make every element precolonial.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Museums and visual culture

Ramón Elías created a sculptural representation now presented through the Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías. The figure is catalogued as an extraordinary serpent with two spikes and pointed teeth. Pedro Encina Ramos’s 2005 *Huellas de metal y madera* accompanies the sculpture with an explicitly Christianizing poem comparing Moñái to the serpent of Eden. The comparison is a modern interpretive act, not an ancient Guaraní equation.

### Education and nationalism

Educational texts commonly present the Colmán family as “the seven Guaraní myths,” flattening distinctions between Paraguayan national folklore and Mbyá, Apapokúva, Ava, Paĩ-Tavyterã, or Tupinambá traditions. Some call Moñái a god; others a monster. These materials document the modern canon but cannot prove its antiquity.

Colmán’s wider epic also included national and racial themes, Christian moral structure, and speculative connections with Atlantis. Modern retellings usually omit the Atlantean material while preserving the monsters, demonstrating selective canon formation.

### Literature, comics, and digital culture

Villalba Rojas documents continuing reproduction in schoolbooks, comics, animated shorts, regional stories, poetry, plastic arts, and social media. Modern horror sites and mythology encyclopedias expand Moñái into a “cryptid,” dragon, occult entity, or game monster. Traits such as treasure guardianship, universal hypnosis of humans, feathered horns, weather control, exact threat levels, and elaborate magical weaknesses generally belong to this recent expansion unless tied to a named earlier collector.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated contested-material search found no historical evidence of Moñái in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, traditional grimoires, Afro-American ritual systems, or an established modern Pagan denomination.

Recent occult websites classify Moñái as an “entity” or primordial monster but acknowledge no historically transmitted rite. Claims about treasure-seeking rituals or magical invocation are modern inventions unless independently documented. There is likewise no reliable demographic evidence for organized contemporary worship.

### Commercial and political appropriation

The being participates in the commercial imagery of Paraguayan folklore through prints, books, tourism, crafts, museum display, and entertainment. The seven-monster system also served twentieth-century national identity, particularly after the state-sponsored 1960 edition. No documented extremist appropriation specific to Moñái was found.

## 9. Historiography and disputed questions

### Was Moñái invented by Colmán?

Two extreme answers both exceed the evidence.

- **“The entire being is ancient and Colmán merely transcribed it.”** Unsupported. His genealogy and epic are demonstrably constructed from heterogeneous sources.
- **“Colmán invented Moñái completely.”** Too strong. Cadogan recorded an Mbyá monster bearing the name and associated with other animal-monsters; regional collectors are also said to have recorded a Moñay outside Colmán’s exact serpent form.

The defensible conclusion is:

> **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A Moñái name or monster-complex probably circulated independently of Colmán, but the seven-sibling genealogy, ordered position as third son, cosmic good-versus-evil framework, and integrated Porãsy extermination cycle are securely documented first as Colmán’s literary systematization.

### Does *Ayvu Rapyta* contain the standard seven-monster cycle?

No supporting passage was located. The accessible Moñái notice instead concerns a monster associated with *Ka’i jagua* or *Ta’ytetu Jagua*. Websites naming *Ayvu Rapyta* as the source for Taú, Keraná, the horned serpent, and the seven children appear to conflate Cadogan with the later national-folklore canon.

### Is Moñái a serpent?

- Colmán’s modern reception and museum tradition: predominantly yes.
- Cadogan’s notice: not clearly; “Moñái or Ta’ytetu Jagua” may suggest a wild-pig monster or an ambiguous association.
- Corrientes–Misiones variant: an immense short-legged, rolling animal.

The serpent is canonical today but not the sole documented morphology.

### Is he a god?

Colmán and Cadogan more securely support “monster.” Modern school and popular materials sometimes elevate him to “god of fields, air, and birds.” Cadogan explicitly says the class of terrestrial monsters cannot equal gods or their emissaries. “God” is therefore a later classificatory choice, not a secure common Guaraní category.

### Predator or protector?

Both versions circulate. The predator hypnotizes and consumes birds; the guardian protects birds, wind, and wild creatures. The Ramón Elías Museum account states the disagreement rather than resolving it. It may reflect divergent local traditions or modern attempts to reinterpret a harmful monster as an ecological guardian.

### Human sacrifice

No cultic human sacrifice is attested. Porãsy’s voluntary death is narrative martyrdom. Calling it evidence that Guaraní communities sacrificed humans to Moñái would be a categorical error.

### Christian influence

Colmán’s poem contains good-versus-evil dualism, sin, punishment, sanctity, celibacy, divine speech, and Judeo-Christian numerological or narrative elements. Modern art explicitly compares Moñái with Eden’s serpent. That supports Christian influence on the received literary system; it does not prove that Jesuits invented each component or the name Moñái.

### Surviving evidence

The record is primarily:

- printed literature;
- ethnographic transcriptions and dictionaries;
- later folklore collections;
- schoolbooks;
- museum sculpture and modern art;
- toponymic and oral claims.

There is no identified ancient inscription, manuscript, temple image, or archaeological cult-object.

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary and near-primary sources

- Narciso R. Colmán, *Ñande ĭpĭ cuéra (Nuestros antepasados)*, Asunción, 1929: first secure printed form of the integrated cycle.
- Colmán, *Génesis de la raza guaraní: nuestros antepasados*, 1937: author’s Spanish version and etymological apparatus.
- León Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta*, 1959: Indigenous Mbyá corpus containing a short, materially different Moñái notice.
- Cadogan, *Diccionario mbyá-guaraní–castellano*, 1992: posthumous linguistic and cultural record.
- Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual*, 1639: essential colonial controls, but no verified Moñái cycle.
- Curt Nimuendajú, *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt…*, 1914: important negative comparative control.
- Ramón Elías and Pedro Encina Ramos: modern museum and artistic reception rather than evidence of ancient cult.

### Principal modern analysis

Rodrigo N. Villalba Rojas’s 2022 “Rosicrán y su poema” is the most important accessible source for separating Colmán’s literary construction from Indigenous ethnography. Its conclusions are supported by the internal structure of Colmán’s work and by comparison with Cadogan and Nimuendajú.

### Final evidentiary conclusion

The fullest reconstruction does not yield an ancient serpent-god with an uninterrupted cult. It yields a layered figure:

1. a possibly older regional or Mbyá monster-name;
2. transformed by Colmán into the third of a designed seven-monster genealogy;
3. attached to an elaborate national epic of crime, seduction, sacrifice, and cosmic punishment;
4. officially and educationally canonized during the twentieth century;
5. reabsorbed into genuine living folklore, where Moñái now exists simultaneously as thief, serpent, bird-hypnotist, guardian, demon, national mythic character, and artistic symbol.

The horned serpent of the fields, the third son defeated through Porãsy’s sacrifice, and the patron of thieves are therefore all documented traditions—but their secure documentary center is **Colmán and the post-Colmán national-folklore complex**, not an independently recoverable precolonial pantheon.
