# The Guaraní Twins (Pa'i Kuaray and Jasy): Research Dossier

**Class:** A paired cycle of male culture heroes and celestial beings—usually an elder solar brother and a younger lunar brother—not a single uniformly defined pair of “gods.” In Mbyá testimony they are explicitly *not* biological twins: Pa’i Rete Kuaray creates the future Jasy as his companion. Apapocúva, Paĩ-Tavyterã/Kaiowá, Ava/Chiripá, Mbyá, Tupinambá, and Argentine-Bolivian Guaraní traditions preserve materially different pairs and genealogies.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The astral words *kuarahy* “sun” and *jasy* “moon/month” are documented in Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s seventeenth-century Guaraní lexicography, especially the *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639). Montoya does **not**, however, securely record the complete twin narrative under the later names Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy. The earliest securely published, extended Guaraní twin cycle is Curt Nimuendajú’s bilingual Apapocúva text in “Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní,” *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914), pp. 284–403. Its protagonists are principally **Ñanderyke’y** (“Our Elder Brother”) and **Tyvýry/Tyvyry’i** (“Younger Brother”), not straightforwardly Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy. The first major published Mbyá corpus explicitly centered on **Pa’i Rete Kuaray** and the future **Jachy/Jasy** is León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959), especially chapter VIII. [Nimuendajú 1914](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Animuendaju-1914-apapocuva); [Cadogan 1959](https://pueblosoriginarios.com/textos/ayvu/ayvu/ayvu%20raptya.pdf)

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early lexical attestation]** Seventeenth-century missionary documentation of a word or concept, not necessarily of the complete narrative.
- **[Secure ethnographic attestation]** A narrative or practice transcribed from identified Guaraní communities by an ethnographer.
- **[Regional variant]** A version belonging to a particular Guaraní people, locality, or narrator.
- **[Comparative reconstruction]** A scholarly reconstruction from related Tupí-Guaraní or wider South American narratives.
- **[Syncretistic or colonial interpretation]** A form affected by missionary language, Christianity, or colonial folklore.
- **[Literary systematization]** An authored synthesis subsequently mistaken for a timeless indigenous canon.
- **[Modern retelling/invention]** A recent literary, educational, touristic, psychological, occult, or Internet construction not demonstrated in the older record.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The pair

“The Guaraní Twins” is an analytical label for a widespread narrative complex. It ordinarily joins:

1. an elder, more competent, solar or solarizing culture hero;
2. a younger, fallible, experimental, comic, transgressive, or mortalizing companion;
3. their murdered or lost mother;
4. jaguars or proto-jaguars who kill her;
5. the brothers’ revenge and world-ordering journeys;
6. an eventual relation to Sun and Moon.

The category must not be allowed to erase three fundamental differences:

- In the **Apapocúva text**, the brothers are sons of different fathers and are called by kin terms, Ñanderyke’y and Tyvýry.
- In an important **Mbyá text**, Pa’i Rete Kuaray creates Jachyrã, “the future Moon,” from a *kurupika’y* leaf. They therefore cannot be literal twins.
- In **Paĩ-Tavyterã/Kaiowá and other regional versions**, Pa’i Kuara/Kuarahy and Jasy may be treated as brothers born from their murdered mother, sometimes with distinct paternity.

Cadogan himself warned that the conventional phrase “myth of the twins” was inaccurate for his Mbyá material. He wrote that Pa’i Rete Kuaray created his younger companion Jachyrã and connected the denial of literal twinship with Mbyá beliefs about human twin births. [Cadogan’s explicit qualification](https://biblioteca.trabalhoindigenista.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/AYVU-RAPYTA-Textos-Misticos-de-los.pdf)

### Kuaray / Kuarahy

Attested spellings include **Kuaray, Kuarahy, Cuaray, Kuarasy, Kuarasy**, and, in older Portuguese and comparative literature, **Guaraci/Coaraci**. Orthographic differences reflect dialect, national spelling systems, and older missionary conventions; they do not establish separate beings.

The ordinary lexical meaning is “sun.” The deeper historical segmentation and Proto-Tupí-Guaraní derivation are not securely settled enough to warrant the popular etymologies often circulated online. Montoya’s *Tesoro* is secure evidence that the solar lexeme belonged to seventeenth-century Guaraní; it is not evidence for every modern myth attached to it. [Montoya bibliographic record and facsimile links](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL25199063M/Tesoro_de_la_lengua_guarani)

Documented titles include:

- **Pa’i Rete Kuaray** — Cadogan glosses this as “the Lord of the body [resplendent] like the Sun.” *Pa’i* is an honorific whose semantic history includes “father,” religious elder, shaman/sacred person, and, after missionization, priest. It should not automatically be translated “Catholic priest.”
- **Kuaray/Kuarahy** — Sun, and by extension the solar person or office.
- **Ñanderu Kuaray / Ñande Ru Kuaray** — “Our Father Sun,” in some later or regional formulations.
- **Pa’i Kuara/Pa’i Kuará** — Paĩ-Tavyterã/Kaiowá and Paraguayan spellings.
- **Ñanderyke’y** — “Our Elder Brother,” the Apapocúva hero whom comparative writers often equate functionally with Kuaray. That equation is scholarly and cross-traditional, not the literal name used throughout Nimuendajú’s text.

**[Secure Mbyá attestation]** Cadogan calls Pa’i Rete Kuaray the “man-god of the race” and begins the second portion of Mbyá religious annals with his appearance. [*Ayvu Rapyta*, chapter VIII](https://pueblosoriginarios.com/textos/ayvu/ayvu/ayvu%20raptya.pdf)

### Jasy / Jaxy / Jachy

Spellings include **Jasy, Jaxy, Jachy, Djatchy, Yasy, Yacy, Jaci**. The common meaning is “moon,” also “month” or a lunar period. The being is usually male in the Guaraní brother cycle. Modern pan-Brazilian presentations of “Jaci” as a universal female moon goddess should not be imported into the Mbyá or Apapocúva brother cycle without a source.

Documented designations include:

- **Jachyrã/Jasyrã** — “future Moon,” literally Moon-in-prospect.
- **Jasy/Jaxy/Jachy** — Moon or the lunar person.
- **Tyvýry, Tyvyry’i, Chyvy’i** — “younger brother/little younger brother” in the Apapocúva material.
- **Ñanderu Jasy** — “Our Father Moon,” in some systematized retellings.

Jasy the lunar brother is **not Jasy Jatere**. The latter is a distinct Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine siesta/forest figure. Narciso R. Colmán made Jasy Jatere one of Taú and Keraná’s seven offspring in his 1929 literary poem; that genealogy does not belong to Cadogan’s Pa’i Rete Kuaray cycle.

### Classification

The sources variously treat the brothers as:

- **culture heroes** who institute hunting, fire, plant and animal distinctions, ritual, social conduct, and mortality;
- **divine or imperishable persons**, especially Kuaray;
- **ancestral brothers** of particular Guaraní peoples;
- **Sun and Moon themselves**, or persons who carry the solar and lunar lights;
- **models of unequal brotherhood**—the elder approximating divine perfection, the younger the erring and perishable human condition.

They are not known to have had a centralized temple cult, divine statues, a hereditary priesthood devoted exclusively to them, or a uniform pan-Guaraní theology.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Seventeenth-century evidence

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* was printed in Madrid in 1639: a 407-leaf Guaraní–Spanish lexical archive created within the Jesuit mission enterprise. It securely documents the historical language in which later solar and lunar traditions were recorded. The companion Spanish–Guaraní *Vocabulario* and *Arte* followed in 1640. [Edition record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL25199063M/Tesoro_de_la_lengua_guarani)

Montoya’s *Conquista espiritual*—also printed in Madrid in 1639 from his experience in the Guayrá and Paraná missions—documents the coercive and negotiated colonial environment: reductions, conversion, indigenous religious specialists, warfare, enslavement, mission discipline, and Jesuit interpretations of Guaraní religion. It does not supply a secure full Pa’i Kuaray–Jasy narrative. Its silence is significant. The twin cycle may have been unrecorded, restricted, transformed, or simply uninteresting to Montoya’s apologetic purpose; silence cannot determine which. [Historical context of the *Conquista espiritual*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S221413241600056X)

**Finding:** The frequent assertion that the complete Kuaray-and-Jasy cycle is “recorded by Montoya in 1639” is not established by the located evidence. Montoya securely attests language and colonial setting, not the later complete cycle.

### Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva corpus, 1914

Curt Unkel Nimuendajú lived among Guaraní groups in Brazil and published Guaraní texts with German translations in 1914. The principal creation narrative, *Inypyrũ*, gives:

- Ñanderuvuçú/Ñanderuvusu, the great creator;
- Ñanderu Mbaekuaa, another primordial father;
- Ñandecy/Ñandesy, “Our Mother”;
- the brothers Ñanderyke’y and Tyvýry;
- the mother’s journey, death among jaguars, and the brothers’ survival;
- their discovery of the murder;
- revenge on the jaguars;
- their culture-hero adventures;
- attempts to reach or communicate with the celestial father;
- the religious setting of creation, destruction, naming, dance, and the future end of the world.

Nimuendajú’s work appeared in *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46, pp. 284–403. It is the earliest secure, extensive published Guaraní version located here. The surviving support is a printed ethnographic article, not a pre-Columbian manuscript or inscription. [Digital bibliographic record](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Animuendaju-1914-apapocuva)

The text’s great strength is its Guaraní transcription. Its limitations are those of early-twentieth-century ethnography: editorial ordering, translation choices, incomplete identification of individual narrators, and Nimuendajú’s comparative and historical hypotheses. Later writers sometimes replace his actual names with Kuaray and Jasy, producing the false impression that he recorded Cadogan’s exact Mbyá pair.

### Métraux and the comparative Tupí-Guaraní corpus

Alfred Métraux’s *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus tupi-guarani* was published in Paris in 1928. A Portuguese edition, *A religião dos Tupinambás e suas relações com a das demais tribus Tupi-Guaranis*, appeared in 1950. Métraux assembled sixteenth- and seventeenth-century coastal Tupinambá testimony and compared it with later Guaraní ethnography. [1928 archive record](https://pesquisa.museudoindio.gov.br/index.php/la-religion-des-tupinamba-et-ses-rapports-avec-celle-des-autres-tribus-tupi-guarani-par-a-metraux); [1950 Portuguese edition](https://bdor.sibi.ufrj.br/handle/doc/40)

Métraux helped establish the “divine twins” as a comparative Tupí-Guaraní complex. This was productive but encouraged later summaries to treat correspondences as identity. Coastal Tupinambá creator-hero sequences, Apapocúva brothers, Mbyá Kuaray and Jaxy, and Chiriguano twins share transformations and narrative structures; they are not manuscript variants of a single canonical scripture.

### Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus

León Cadogan recorded Mbyá sacred speech in eastern Paraguay from religious authorities who had accepted him into trusted relations. *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá* appeared in 1959, in Mbyá Guaraní with Spanish translations and linguistic notes. Chapter VIII, “Pa’i Rete Kuaray,” is the principal witness for the Mbyá form. [Foundation description](https://www.fundacionleoncadogan.org/biblioteca-digital); [full digital text](https://pueblosoriginarios.com/textos/ayvu/ayvu/ayvu%20raptya.pdf)

Its sequence differs radically from simplified “twin” summaries:

- the first father departs;
- Kuaray’s mother follows while the unborn Kuaray advises her;
- after the separation and maternal catastrophe, Kuaray undergoes his earthly career;
- Kuaray creates his younger companion, the future Moon, from a *kurupika’y* leaf;
- the elder and younger travel, hunt, acquire or regulate cultural goods, encounter enemies, and display unequal powers;
- Kuaray is a model of mature sacred conduct;
- Jaxy is associated with error, appetite, sexuality, periodicity, death, and the imperfect human condition.

Cadogan’s *Ywyra Ñe’ery* (1971) adds Mbyá narratives and ethnographic observations but is not an earlier witness. It remains valuable for language, ritual practice, and the continuing place of sacred speech. [Publication description](https://www.fundacionleoncadogan.org/biblioteca-digital)

### Paĩ-Tavyterã, Kaiowá, Ava/Chiripá, and Argentine-Bolivian witnesses

Paĩ-Tavyterã ethnography records a more recognizably twin-like story: Ñande Sy reaches the jaguar household; the jaguars kill and eat her; the grandmother jaguar preserves or raises the unborn children; a bird reveals their mother’s fate; Pa’i Kuara tries to restore her bones; Jasy interrupts prematurely; the brothers destroy most jaguars by a sabotaged crossing. These texts were published in later-twentieth-century ethnography and must not be retrojected into seventeenth-century Paraguay.

Recent Kaiowá work records tellings in which women shift responsibility for the primordial separation away from the mother, revising the moral burden found in some classic male-recorded versions. That is living mythic argument, not corruption of a frozen original.

Chiriguano/Ava Guaraní versions were recorded in Bolivia by Métraux in 1929. The twin complex ranges from the Atlantic to the Bolivian cordillera, but its names, parentage, social applications, and final astral transformations vary. A modern edition of a Bolivian Guaraní version describes twins who establish the natural and social order and seek their absent father. [Bolivian Guaraní synopsis](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780822371618_A35683243/preview-9780822371618_A35683243.pdf)

Modern Guaraní accounts from Calilegua, Jujuy, make the twins founders of historical movement and indigenous presence, sometimes treating them simultaneously as ancestors, culture heroes, and celestial beings. This is a documented contemporary regional use rather than evidence for one prehistoric “original.” [Calilegua study](https://scielo.pt/scielo.php?pid=S0873-65612020000200010&script=sci_arttext)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Kuaray

**[Secure Mbyá and comparative ethnographic material]**

Kuaray’s principal domains are:

- sunlight, daytime, regularity, heat, and celestial order;
- exemplary religious and social conduct;
- shamanic knowledge, resurrection, healing, and powerful speech;
- naming or differentiating animals and plants;
- hunting and the acquisition of fire;
- protection or establishment of Guaraní human life after the jaguar age;
- the contrast between divine imperishability and earthly perishability.

Modern Mbyá interlocutors have described Sun and Moon as persons carrying luminous “apparatuses”: Kuaray performs his task consistently, while Jaxy’s irregularity explains the Moon’s changeable light. Such testimony is a contemporary cosmological formulation, not an ancient iconographic description.

### Jasy

Jasy’s domains include:

- Moon and lunar periodicity;
- night and monthly recurrence;
- experimentation, appetite, comic failure, or disobedience;
- sexuality, seduction, menstruation, and incest in some Mbyá narratives;
- death and resurrection, because he is repeatedly killed, dismembered, or revived;
- the human, imperfect, *tekoaxy* condition in contrast to Kuaray’s more perfect state.

Isabelle Combès notes that the southern Tupí-Guaraní corpus makes the two celestial siblings male, unlike widespread American Sun-brother/Moon-sister patterns. In one Apapocúva analysis the lunar brother directs an aborted same-sex advance toward his brother; in Mbyá versions Moon’s sexual transgression is redirected toward a paternal aunt. The aunt marks him, explaining the Moon’s spots. This is a specific comparative result, not a universal Guaraní doctrine. [Combès, “Yaci”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hom_0439-4216_1991_num_31_120_369441?pageId=T1_8)

### Powers and limitations

Kuaray can:

- receive or exercise knowledge while still in the womb;
- frustrate the jaguars’ attempts to cook or kill him;
- restore life from remains;
- heal animals or his brother;
- make game, traps, crossings, or cultural techniques;
- create Jaxy in the Mbyá version;
- ascend or assume the solar function.

Jasy can:

- assist in revenge;
- revive Kuaray in some Apapocúva episodes;
- participate in hunting and culture-making;
- fail through haste, hunger, desire, or disobedience;
- be killed and reconstituted.

Neither brother is omnipotent in narrative. Kuaray’s attempt to restore the mother fails because the younger brother interrupts or attempts to nurse before completion. The lost mother cannot then simply be returned to ordinary human life.

### Material iconography

No securely identified pre-Columbian statue, temple relief, vessel painting, or inscribed image of “Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy” has been established. Guaraní archaeology documents settlements, ceramics, burials, subsistence, and movement, but it presently does not provide an iconographic key allowing two figures to be securely named as these brothers.

Likewise, there is no ancient canonical color scheme, costume, weapon set, sacred number, or heraldic emblem. Bows, arrows, traps, fire, feathers, ritual implements, birds, jaguars, bones, the *kurupika’y* leaf, river crossings, and the solar and lunar discs are narrative attributes. Contemporary paintings and educational illustrations are modern interpretations.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogical variants

| Tradition | Elder | Younger | Mother | Father(s) | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apapocúva, Nimuendajú 1914 | Ñanderyke’y | Tyvýry/Tyvyry’i | Ñandecy | Ñanderuvuçú and Ñanderu Mbaekuaa in the recorded dual-paternity complex | Brothers, conventionally called twins; not named Kuaray and Jasy throughout |
| Mbyá, Cadogan 1959 | Pa’i Rete Kuaray | Jachyrã/Jaxy | Kuaray has an earthly mother | Divine paternity in the Pa-pa/Ñamandu complex | Not biological twins: Kuaray creates Jaxy from a leaf |
| Paĩ-Tavyterã/Kaiowá | Pa’i Kuara/Kuarahy | Jasy | Ñande Sy | Version-dependent Ñanderu figure or figures | Frequently treated as brothers born from the mother |
| Comparative Tupinambá | Older and younger creator/culture heroes under other names | — | Version-dependent | Version-dependent | Structural comparanda, not identical names |
| Modern Paraguayan popular synthesis | Kuarahy | Jasy | Often Ñande Sy | Frequently Ñamandu, Tupã, or a composite father | Frequently harmonized; often dependent on ethnographic or literary popularization |

### Every documented named divine-being interaction

The following lines distinguish named supernatural persons from collective jaguars, animals, and unnamed enemies.

- **Pa’i Rete Kuaray — Jachyrã/Jaxy:** Kuaray creates the future Moon from a *kurupika’y* leaf to be his younger companion; they hunt and travel together. **Source:** Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta*, ch. VIII, published 1959. **Tier:** secure Mbyá regional attestation.
- **Kuaray — Jaxy:** Kuaray repeatedly instructs the younger brother, whose haste or disobedience produces failure, periodicity, sexuality, or death. **Source:** Cadogan 1959 and later Mbyá ethnography. **Tier:** secure Mbyá complex with local variants.
- **Kuaray — Jaxy:** Kuaray resurrects or reconstructs the dismembered younger brother. **Source:** Mbyá and Chiripá cycle, documented in twentieth-century ethnography. **Tier:** regional variant.
- **Ñanderyke’y — Tyvýry:** The elder leads; the younger assists, tests cures, and in some episodes revives the elder by blowing life into him. **Source:** Nimuendajú 1914. **Tier:** earliest secure Apapocúva cycle.
- **Ñanderyke’y — Ñanderuvuçú:** The elder seeks or communicates with the creator-father and ultimately joins or approaches his celestial sphere. **Source:** Nimuendajú 1914. **Tier:** secure Apapocúva attestation.
- **Tyvýry — Ñanderu Mbaekuaa:** The younger belongs to the dual-paternity genealogy involving Mbaekuaa. **Source:** Nimuendajú 1914. **Tier:** secure Apapocúva attestation; the exact allocation of sons varies in subsequent summaries.
- **The unborn elder brother — Ñandecy/Ñande Sy:** He speaks from the womb and directs his mother along the father’s path; after being angered he ceases reliable guidance, contributing to her arrival at the jaguars. **Source:** Nimuendajú 1914; parallel forms in Cadogan and later Paĩ/Kaiowá records. **Tier:** secure early ethnographic core with regional variants.
- **Both brothers — Ñandecy/Ñande Sy:** They attempt to restore their mother from her bones; the younger cries out, embraces her, or attempts to nurse too soon, and the reconstruction collapses. **Source:** Paĩ-Tavyterã/Kaiowá and related Guaraní versions, twentieth-century documentation. **Tier:** regional variant.
- **Pa’i Rete Kuaray — Ñande Ru Pa-pa/Ñamandu-related father:** Kuaray’s conception and divine status are embedded in the Mbyá first-father complex; the father departs for the celestial abode. **Source:** Cadogan 1959. **Tier:** secure Mbyá attestation, with contested nomenclature and possible colonial lexical influence.
- **Jaxy — paternal aunt:** Moon has sexual relations with an aunt; she marks the unidentified visitor with wax or another substance, producing the lunar spots. **Source:** Mbyá variants analyzed by Combès from Cadogan and later collections. **Tier:** regional variant.
- **Jaxy — Anhã/Aña:** The younger brother is killed, cooked, beheaded, or dismembered after disobedient fishing or food-taking; Kuaray subsequently restores him. **Source:** Mbyá/Chiripá variants documented in twentieth-century ethnography. **Tier:** regional variant.
- **The brothers — Charia or the Celestial Jaguar:** Later Mbyá materials associate a celestial jaguar with attacks on or consumption of Sun and Moon during eclipses. Direct participation of both brothers varies by telling. **Tier:** regional cosmological complex; not securely the same episode as the mother’s murder.
- **Kuaray/Jasy — Tupã:** No early secure text establishes the popular modern genealogy in which a Christianized supreme Tupã simply fathers the two brothers. **Finding:** absent from Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva genealogy and Cadogan’s specific Mbyá account as a uniform formula.
- **Kuaray/Jasy — Taú, Keraná, and the seven monsters:** No interaction is securely attested in the foundational Apapocúva or Mbyá corpora. The integrated “Paraguayan pantheon” joining these families is largely a modern literary/popular construction.
- **Kuaray/Jasy — Jasy Jatere:** No secure genealogical identity or direct interaction in the foundational twin texts. Similar lunar vocabulary does not make them the same being.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The mother follows the departed father

**[Earliest secure extended witness: Nimuendajú 1914]**

After a quarrel or separation, the divine father departs. The pregnant mother follows. Her unborn elder child speaks, requests flowers or fruit, and indicates the path. A thorn, bee, or other irritation causes the mother to strike or rebuke her belly. Offended, the unborn guide falls silent or misdirects her. At a fork she takes the path leading not to the father’s celestial home but to the jaguars.

The episode assigns causation differently among tellings:

- Some emphasize the mother’s anger or adultery.
- Some make the unborn child’s touchiness decisive.
- Some contemporary Kaiowá women reject the older blame placed on Ñande Sy.
- The Mbyá narrative is not genealogically identical to the Apapocúva version.

It is therefore incorrect to state simply that “the mother disobeyed and was punished.”

### B. The mother is eaten and the children survive

At the jaguars’ dwelling, an old jaguar woman may try to conceal or warn the pregnant visitor. Returning jaguars smell her, discover her, kill her, and consume her. The unborn children survive extraction from the womb.

The jaguars try to kill them by boiling, roasting, crushing, or eating them, but their bodies resist. The grandmother raises them, whether from affection, caution, or a plan to fatten them. This combination—affinal proximity to jaguars, cannibal danger, adoption, and eventual retaliation—is central across many South American versions.

“Jaguars” here are not merely zoological cats. They are myth-age persons and dangerous affines, capable of domestic life and speech. Later writers have interpreted them as images of enemy peoples; Nimuendajú proposed a relation to Apapocúva views of Kaingang enemies, but that historical identification is an ethnographic hypothesis, not the story’s literal universal meaning.

### C. Revelation and revenge

A jacu/curassow, parrot, or another bird reveals that the jaguars killed the brothers’ mother. The brothers may first wound the informant, then heal it and obtain the truth.

They arrange a collective jaguar hunt or journey. A bridge, log, or crossing over deep water is weakened or controlled by a vine. When the jaguars crowd onto it, the brothers break or overturn it. The jaguars drown or are killed with arrows and clubs. One pregnant female commonly escapes, explaining why jaguars still exist.

The episode is both revenge and an etiological transformation: the myth-age jaguar people become or produce the jaguars of the present world. It also reverses the initial relation of helpless children to predatory fosterers.

### D. Recovery of the mother’s bones

In Paĩ-Tavyterã/Kaiowá and related versions, the brothers find their mother’s bones. Kuaray arranges them and performs a prayer, chant, breath, or other restorative operation. Flesh and bodily form return. Before the process is complete, Jasy cries to her, embraces her, or tries to suckle. She collapses again into bones.

This narrative explains the irreversibility of human death and contrasts Kuaray’s controlled knowledge with Jasy’s premature desire. It should not be projected indiscriminately into every Apapocúva or Mbyá telling; its clearest accessible attestation here is in later Paĩ material. A published ethnographic summary describes exactly this failed restoration. [Paĩ-Tavyterã version](https://es.scribd.com/document/500762907/Los-Pa%C4%A9-Tavytera-Etnografia-Guarani-del-Paraguay-Contemporaneo)

### E. Culture-making journeys

After revenge, the brothers travel and transform the world. Episode order varies, but actions include:

- inventing or regulating the hunting of coatis, birds, and other game;
- establishing animal behavior and bodily characteristics;
- encountering snakes and discovering remedies;
- dying and reviving one another;
- obtaining fire from nonhuman possessors;
- distinguishing edible from dangerous foods;
- instituting shamanic song, dance, or communication;
- creating geographical features;
- separating human life from the mythic jaguar order.

In one Apapocúva sequence, Ñanderyke’y deliberately exposes himself to snakes while Tyvýry tests medicines; when a treatment fails, the younger restores the elder with breath. This reverses the simplistic idea that only the elder possesses power.

### F. Creation of Jaxy in the Mbyá version

**[Secure Mbyá regional variant]**

Pa’i Rete Kuaray, lonely and needing a companion, creates Jaxy from a leaf of the soft-wooded *kurupika’y*. Cadogan’s text says that Kuaray produces him from his own *marã e’ỹ* condition—translated by Cadogan in terms of divinity and by later analysts as imperishability.

This episode makes the pair an unequal duplication rather than twins. A modern Mbyá religious authority objected to white researchers’ “twin” label because Jaxy was Kuaray’s image or derivative, not his simultaneous womb-mate. [Ethnographic discussion](https://acervo.socioambiental.org/sites/default/files/documents/GMT00009.pdf)

### G. Jaxy’s errors, death, and restoration

Jaxy repeatedly disregards Kuaray’s instructions. In different Mbyá and Chiripá versions he fishes at a prohibited place, approaches dangerous beings, eats or desires at the wrong time, is decapitated, or is cooked by Anhã. Kuaray gathers and restores him.

These events express the asymmetry between an elder associated with controlled sacred knowledge and a younger associated with ordinary human vulnerability. Jaxy is not therefore simply an “evil moon god.” His failures make culture, periodicity, and present human existence intelligible.

### H. Lunar sexuality and the Moon’s spots

In a documented Mbyá form, Jaxy secretly has intercourse with his aunt. She marks the unknown visitor with forest-bee wax; his stained face becomes the spotted Moon. Comparative analysis shows that other South American traditions employ brother–sister incest, while the Guaraní versions may use two male brothers and redirect Moon’s heterosexual desire toward an aunt. [Combès](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hom_0439-4216_1991_num_31_120_369441?pageId=T1_8)

This is not a license to merge every South American lunar-incest story into the Guaraní cycle.

### I. Ascent and becoming Sun and Moon

Some versions have the brothers ascend and become Sun and Moon after reaching the father; others treat their solar and lunar identities as already present in their names and powers; still others distinguish the divine persons from the lights they carry.

The simple schoolbook conclusion “the twins turned into the Sun and Moon” is thus only one formulation. In the Apapocúva source, the brothers’ primary names remain kin terms; in Mbyá thought, Kuaray may be both a divine person and the bearer or operator of the solar light.

### J. Tupí and wider South American comparanda

Métraux demonstrated extensive affinities with Tupinambá and other Tupí-Guaraní hero cycles: unequal brothers, jaguar relations, a murdered or lost mother, revenge, fire, and world-ordering transformations. The comparison supports an old Tupí-Guaraní narrative family, but no surviving pre-Columbian text lets scholarship reconstruct one exact Proto-Tupí-Guaraní script.

The motif extends beyond Tupí-Guaraní into Carib, Arawak, Kichwa, Panoan, and other Amazonian traditions. Amazonian Kichwa versions also feature a pregnant mother entering the jaguars’ house, posthumous children, revelation, and revenge. These parallels may reflect ancient diffusion, shared regional structures, repeated borrowing, or combinations of all three. They do not prove descent from the Maya Hero Twins or any single Old World model. [Comparative Kichwa–Mbyá study](https://www.flacsoandes.edu.ec/sites/default/files/%25f/agora/files/FA-AGORA-2014-Uzendoski_0.pdf)

“Andean” comparison must likewise be divided:

- **Lowland eastern Andean/Chaco Guaraní:** directly relevant, since Chiriguano/Ava Guaraní carried related narratives into Bolivia.
- **Amazonian Kichwa of the eastern Andean foothills:** close narrative parallels.
- **Highland Inca solar religion:** only broad comparison unless a specific transmission argument is demonstrated.
- **Mesoamerican Maya twins:** resemblance does not itself establish contact or derivation.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No temple cult securely documented

There is no evidence for dedicated temples of Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy, cult statues, temple estates, sacrificial priesthoods, or civic festivals comparable to Mediterranean or Mesoamerican state religions. Claims assigning them ancient temples, fixed feast days, seven sacred colors, planetary sigils, or a formal “solar order” are modern inventions unless separately documented.

Guaraní religious practice historically centered on settlements, houses of prayer, communal song and dance, tobacco smoke, rattles, name-giving, healing, visions, ritual speech, and the authority of religious leaders variously called *karaí, pa’i, opygua, ñanderu,* or related titles. These offices and their meanings differ among peoples.

### Sacred speech and performance

For the Mbyá, the text is not merely a folktale detached from worship. Cadogan presented the *Ayvu Porã Tenonde*, “first beautiful words,” as restricted or sacred speech transmitted by knowledgeable leaders. Narration, chanting, prayer, and explanation can themselves be religious acts.

Kuaray functions as exemplary elder, shamanic predecessor, and regulator. Narratives about him can teach controlled conduct, relations with animals, proper speech, and the difference between the divine *marã e’ỹ* condition and earthly imperfection. Jaxy provides the counter-example through which mortality, sexual disorder, and error are explained.

### Offerings and sacrifice

No securely documented human sacrifice to Kuaray or Jasy was found. Tupinambá ritual killing and anthropophagy are historically documented in early colonial sources, but it is incorrect to convert those practices into a temple sacrifice “to the Sun and Moon.”

A much more specific and disturbing item appears in Cadogan’s discussion of Mbyá twin births. He reported a belief that human twins could embody malign spirits and stated that newborn twins were killed; he cited Franz Müller’s 1935 report and information from a young Mbyá man. Cadogan used this as an argument that the Mbyá could not consistently worship literal divine twins. [Cadogan’s discussion](https://biblioteca.trabalhoindigenista.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/AYVU-RAPYTA-Textos-Misticos-de-los.pdf)

This is an ethnographic report of infanticide associated with human twin births, not “human sacrifice to Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy.” Its frequency, chronology, and continuation cannot be inferred from Cadogan’s short report. Sensational modern campaigns about indigenous infanticide must not be used as retrospective corroboration without independent field evidence.

### Jesuit reductions, conversion, and suppression

From the early seventeenth century, Jesuit reductions reorganized settlement, labor, ritual time, kin relations, naming, burial, and sacred authority. Missionaries replaced or translated indigenous terms through Christian categories, attacked shamans they saw as rivals or sorcerers, and promoted baptism, Mass, saints, Marian devotion, confession, and Christian burial. Reductions also offered varying degrees of refuge from enslavement and colonial raiding; their history cannot be reduced to either voluntary syncretism or simple total coercion.

Montoya’s books are products of this environment. Their lexical richness preserves Guaraní language, but their theological framing is missionary. The later survival of complex non-Christian sacred narratives among Apapocúva and Mbyá communities demonstrates that conversion did not erase indigenous cosmology.

There is no documented single decree suppressing “the cult of the Twins.” Suppression operated through the broader campaign against non-Christian ritual specialists, speech, mobility, and communal institutions.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before European documentation

**[Comparative reconstruction]** The broad distribution of unequal-brother and jaguar-mother narratives among Tupí-Guaraní-speaking peoples makes a precolonial ancestry highly probable. The precise names Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy, the exact sequence of episodes, and the final astral assignments cannot be reconstructed word-for-word.

Archaeology establishes deep Guaraní movement and settlement across the Paraná–Paraguay basin and Atlantic regions but has not identified the brothers iconographically.

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Coastal Brazilian chroniclers recorded Tupinambá creator and culture-hero traditions later compared with the Guaraní twins. In the Río de la Plata and Guayrá, Jesuit sources recorded Guaraní vocabulary and religious conflict but did not preserve a complete securely identifiable Kuaray–Jasy cycle.

Montoya’s 1639–1640 works preserve the relevant solar and lunar vocabulary. The documentary asymmetry—excellent language records, poor recording of this narrative—may reflect missionary selectivity and restricted indigenous transmission.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

The Jesuit order was expelled from Spanish America in 1767–1768; the missions fragmented under secular and colonial administration. Guaraní-speaking communities continued under warfare, land loss, state expansion, labor exploitation, and national reclassification.

In Paraguay, Corrientes, and Misiones, indigenous stories entered a bilingual rural folklore field. Figures such as Pombero/Kuarahy Jára and Jasy Jatere acquired regional configurations that must not be assumed to descend directly from the celestial brothers.

Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century work on Misiones and Corrientes documented this mixed regional folklore. His “Kuarahy Jára,” a bird-protecting siesta figure associated with Pombero, is not automatically Pa’i Kuaray the Mbyá culture hero.

### 1905–1914: Nimuendajú and the Apapocúva record

Nimuendajú’s fieldwork transformed the documentary situation. His 1914 publication supplied an extensive indigenous-language narrative and connected it to Apapocúva cosmology, naming, migration, the Land without Evil, and expectations of world destruction. Later scholarship often treated this first detailed publication as the universal Guaraní template; it is instead one extraordinarily important regional witness.

### 1920s–1950s: comparison and literary nationalization

Métraux’s 1928 synthesis positioned the twins within a broad Tupí-Guaraní historical religion.

Narciso R. Colmán’s *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, first published in 1929, created an ambitious Guaraní-language ethnogenetic and mythological poem. Its Taú–Keraná genealogy and seven monstrous sons became enormously influential in Paraguayan education and popular culture. It is a **literary systematization**, not an untouched precolonial canon. The work’s own developed narrative and publication history are accessible in a digital edition. [Colmán text](https://www.portalguarani.com/376_narciso_ramon_colman_rosicran__/10966_nuestros_antepasados_nande_ypy_kuera__obra_de_narciso_r_colman.html)

Egon Schaden’s *Aspectos fundamentais da cultura Guaraní* appeared in 1954 and compared Ñandeva, Mbyá, and Kaiowá religious life without reducing them to one theology. [USP bibliographic record](https://obrasraras.usp.br/xmlui/handle/123456789/8692?show=full)

### 1959–1970s: Cadogan and the Mbyá corpus

Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959) placed Mbyá-language religious texts at the center of scholarship. His material demonstrated that Pa’i Rete Kuaray and Jaxy were not simply the Apapocúva twins under new names. *Ywyra Ñe’ery* (1971) extended his record.

Pierre Clastres’s *Le Grand Parler* (1974) translated and arranged Guaraní myths and sacred songs, drawing partly on Nimuendajú and Cadogan. Hélène Clastres’s *La Terre sans Mal* (1975; Portuguese translation 1978) made Guaraní prophetism and the Land without Evil central to political anthropology. [Hélène Clastres edition record](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Aclastres-1978-terra)

Their work was influential but interpretive. It sometimes presented Guaraní sacred language as a unified metaphysics of anti-state prophetism. Bartomeu Melià and later scholars emphasized linguistic, economic, territorial, and colonial histories and questioned timeless generalization of *yvy marã e’ỹ* as a single millennia-old migration doctrine.

### Late twentieth century to present

Paĩ-Tavyterã, Kaiowá, Ava/Chiripá, and Mbyá narrators continue to transmit and reinterpret the brothers’ cycle. The narrative remains connected to land, kinship, food, ritual conduct, indigenous education, and relations with non-indigenous society.

Contemporary variants in Brazil, Paraguay, Argentina, and Bolivia are evidence of living traditions, not degraded residues. At the same time, national folklore, schoolbooks, tourism, Internet mythology lists, and fantasy media increasingly merge:

- Cadogan’s Ñamandu-centered Mbyá creation;
- Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva brothers;
- Colmán’s seven monsters;
- Christianized Tupã;
- Pombero and Jasy Jatere;
- pan-Brazilian “Jaci” traditions.

That composite pantheon is modern.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and national folklore

Colmán’s *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* is the decisive Paraguayan literary systematization. Its importance is genuine: it is a major Guaraní literary work and helped create a shared national mythology. Its authored status is equally genuine. Taú, Keraná, Angatupyry, and the seven monsters should not be silently inserted into the twin cycle as though Cadogan or Nimuendajú recorded one seamless family tree.

Argentine educational and folklore collections preserve solar and lunar tales, but some belong to international tale types—children bearing solar and lunar marks, jealous sisters, enchanted princesses—rather than the indigenous jaguar-mother cycle. Berta Vidal de Battini’s *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina* therefore supplies comparative Argentine folklore, not automatic evidence for Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy.

Modern stories naming the twins **Erekeí and Erevuí**, or making Tupã directly create a first couple whose twins become Sun and Moon, require attribution to the particular adapter. They are not securely attested ancient names in the foundational corpora.

### Visual art and education

Modern artists commonly show two indigenous youths bearing solar and lunar discs, bows, feather ornaments, or gold and silver coloring. These are useful contemporary interpretations but not recoveries of ancient iconography.

A modern comparative art project explicitly depicted Kuaray and Jaxy in reconstructed clothing and described Jaxy as Kuaray’s created companion. The project is evidence of twenty-first-century reception, not an early cult image.

### Psychological interpretation

A 2024 Jungian study reads the jaguar and the Guaraní twins as material for psychological “amplification.” This is a modern analytical tradition, openly dependent on Jungian categories, not an indigenous ancient interpretation. [Jungian study](https://ajb.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/18-edicao-Cadernos-Junguianos-versao-final-2024_AJB-EN.pdf)

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search found no evidence for a historically continuous Western occult, Hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, grimoire, or chaos-magic tradition centered on Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy. Contemporary invocations on eclectic spirituality sites may exist, but no demonstrable lineage connects them to Guaraní ritual authorities.

Likewise, no reliable evidence was located for a large organized “Guaraní reconstructionist” religion worshipping the pair independently of living Guaraní communities. Indigenous ritual continuity should not be relabeled neopaganism.

### Games and commercial mythology

Online “pantheon registries,” game proposals, fantasy bestiaries, and AI-generated mythology pages frequently give Kuaray fixed RPG-style “domains,” “powers,” parents, symbols, and enemies. They often cite Nimuendajú while substituting Cadogan’s names, or attach the Land without Evil to the brothers as though it were their jointly governed celestial kingdom. These are modern taxonomic inventions.

### Political uses

Contemporary Guaraní tellings may place the brothers’ travels in arguments about ancestral territorial presence. A Calilegua account studied in 2020 uses the myth to pluralize national history and affirm that Guaraní people were present before modern borders and settler accounts. That is a documented modern political use grounded in living narration, not a falsification of myth. [Calilegua study](https://scielo.pt/scielo.php?pid=S0873-65612020000200010&script=sci_arttext)

No specific extremist appropriation of Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy was established in the sources reviewed.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Are they actually twins?

This is the central dispute.

- **Apapocúva:** two brothers gestated together in the maternal cycle, associated with two fathers; “twins” is defensible as a narrative classification.
- **Mbyá:** Pa’i Rete Kuaray creates Jaxy later from a leaf; literal twinship is explicitly denied.
- **Comparative mythology:** “Divine Twins” names a structural pair—elder/younger, competent/incompetent, Sun/Moon—even when biological twinship is absent.
- **Popular reception:** usually erases the distinction.

The safest title is therefore “twin/brother cycle,” with local genealogies stated separately.

### Did the brothers originally represent Sun and Moon?

Possibly at an old stage, but the evidence does not justify a single answer. The solar and lunar identifications are explicit in several documented traditions. Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva narrative, however, primarily names the figures through kinship, and the relationship between divine persons and physical luminaries is not always simple identity.

Scholars must distinguish:

- a person named “Sun”;
- a culture hero who later takes the solar office;
- an ancestor associated with Sun;
- a person who carries a solar apparatus;
- the visible celestial body.

### Dual paternity and “twinship”

The Apapocúva brothers’ different fathers exemplify a South American idea sometimes called dual or multiple paternity, in which more than one man can contribute to a pregnancy. Older scholarship sometimes treated this as irrational folklore; contemporary anthropology treats it as a coherent local theory of bodily formation and kinship.

The Mbyá solution is different: the younger is not conceived at all but made by the elder.

### The jaguars as enemies or affines

Nimuendajú linked Apapocúva jaguar imagery to relations with Kaingang enemies. Later structural and perspectivist readings emphasize the jaguars as dangerous affines, cannibal subjects, or mythic persons whose household mirrors human sociality. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive, but none should be presented as the narrator’s explicit universal gloss without evidence.

### The Land without Evil

Nimuendajú connected Apapocúva migration, shamanism, and *yvy marã e’ỹ*. Métraux and the Clastres expanded the theme across Tupí-Guaraní history. Later scholarship, particularly Melià’s, challenged its transformation into a timeless, singular motive explaining every Guaraní migration.

Melià emphasized the semantic and material dimension of good, intact, cultivable land and the colonial history through which *yvy marã e’ỹ* came to be translated “Land without Evil.” One study summarizes the problem succinctly: the ethnological theory of an all-Guaraní search originates largely with Nimuendajú and was subsequently generalized by Métraux and the Clastres. [Critical overview](https://diposit.ub.edu/bitstreams/08fddea0-6d8c-472f-843f-306907e8bb8b/download)

The twins should therefore not automatically be described as “rulers of the Land without Evil.”

### Christian influence

Terms such as *Pa-pa*, *Tupã*, cross-shaped world supports, flood narratives, heavenly fathers, and paradisal destinations prompted claims of Christian borrowing. Cadogan initially suspected that *Pa-pa* derived from the Catholic “Pope,” then reconsidered because other Guaraní groups used the term in indigenous theological contexts.

The available evidence supports centuries of interaction, translation, resistance, and selective adoption. It does not support either extreme claim—that Guaraní cosmology is untouched by Christianity, or that missionaries invented the entire twin cycle.

### Comparative diffusion

Métraux’s wide comparisons and Lévi-Strauss’s structural analyses demonstrate recurring transformations across South America. They do not yield a single point of origin. Claims deriving the Guaraní pair directly from Castor and Pollux, Gemini, Cain and Abel, the Maya Hero Twins, or Inca solar religion remain unestablished without historical transmission evidence.

### Textual state and archives

The principal evidence survives as:

- Montoya’s printed seventeenth-century books in rare-book collections and digital facsimiles;
- Nimuendajú’s 1914 printed German–Guaraní article;
- Métraux’s 1928 French synthesis and archival holdings;
- Cadogan’s notebooks, publications, bilingual texts, and later editions;
- recordings and field notes dispersed among researchers, indigenous communities, museums, universities, and foundations;
- living oral and ritual transmission.

These are mediated records. The printed page is not the original “scripture”; the original event was narrated, sung, explained, or performed speech.

## 10. Dedicated contested-material findings

### Etymology

- *Kuaray/kuarahy* = “sun” and *jasy/jaxy* = “moon/month” are secure lexical meanings.
- Elaborate Internet decompositions into multiple morphemes are not secure unless supported by historical linguistics.
- *Pa’i Rete Kuaray* is a ritual title, not simply “the Sun priest.”
- *Jasy* must not be etymologically conflated with Jasy Jatere.

### Origin

- A precolonial Tupí-Guaraní ancestry for the broader brother complex is a strong comparative reconstruction.
- The exact Pa’i Kuaray–Jasy form is first securely documented in modern ethnography, not pre-Columbian writing.
- Nimuendajú’s 1914 text is the earliest full Guaraní record located, but its heroes bear kinship names.
- Cadogan’s Mbyá form is not a duplicate of Nimuendajú’s.

### Syncretism

- Christian influence is demonstrable in the colonial setting and in shifting uses of *Tupã*, *pa’i*, baptismal categories, paradise language, and missionary translation.
- No evidence establishes that the Jesuits simply invented Kuaray and Jasy.
- The modern universal “Tupã as supreme creator and father of all Guaraní gods” is often a Christianized or literary synthesis.

### Human sacrifice and violence

- No human sacrifice offered to Kuaray or Jasy is documented.
- Cadogan reports killing of human twins in a specific Mbyá belief context; it is not sacrificial worship of the mythical brothers.
- The myth itself includes cannibalism, maternal killing, attempted child-killing, mass revenge, drowning, dismemberment, incest, and failed resurrection. These are integral narrative elements.
- Historical colonial violence includes enslavement, mission coercion, attacks on religious leaders, relocation, epidemic loss, and territorial dispossession.

### Occultism and neopaganism

No documented pre-2026 occult lineage, ceremonial-magic cult, or substantial reconstructionist movement centered on the pair was found. Modern magical correspondences should be labeled inventions.

### Literary invention

Colmán’s Taú–Keraná/seven-monsters system is a 1929 authored literary systematization. It is part of Paraguayan cultural history, but it must not be used to supply the twins with an “ancient canonical pantheon.”

### Modern misinformation

Common unsupported composites include:

- Nimuendajú recording a pair explicitly named Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy;
- Kuaray and Jasy being biological twins in all traditions;
- Jasy being universally female;
- Jasy being identical to Jasy Jatere;
- the brothers being sons of Colmán’s Tupã–Arasy pair;
- the twins ruling the Land without Evil;
- the seven monsters interacting with the twins in an ancient epic;
- fixed temples, sigils, sacred colors, or feast days;
- any single smooth “Guaraní creation myth” representing every Guaraní people.

## 12. Sources and assessment

### Primary and near-primary corpora

- Ruiz de Montoya, Antonio. *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní*. Madrid: Juan Sánchez, 1639. Essential for early lexicon; not a full twin narrative.
- Ruiz de Montoya, Antonio. *Conquista espiritual*. Madrid, 1639. Essential for mission history and colonial religious conflict.
- Nimuendajú Unkel, Curt. “Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní.” *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie* 46 (1914): 284–403. Earliest secure extended Guaraní brother-cycle publication.
- Métraux, Alfred. *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus tupi-guarani*. Paris, 1928. Comparative historical synthesis using early coastal sources.
- Colmán, Narciso R. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*. 1929. Literary primary source for modern Paraguayan myth-making, not transparent precolonial ethnography.
- Cadogan, León. *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá*. São Paulo, 1959. Principal Mbyá bilingual corpus.
- Cadogan, León. *Ywyra Ñe’ery*. Asunción, 1971. Additional Mbyá narrative and ethnographic material.
- Paĩ-Tavyterã, Kaiowá, Ava/Chiripá, and Bolivian Guaraní ethnographic texts. Necessary for regional variant comparison.

### Major analytical scholarship

- Schaden, Egon. *Aspectos fundamentais da cultura Guaraní*. 1954.
- Pierre Clastres. *Le Grand Parler*. 1974.
- Hélène Clastres. *La Terre sans Mal*. 1975.
- Bartomeu Melià. Studies of Guaraní language, *teko*, territory, mission history, and the semantic history of *yvy marã e’ỹ*.
- Isabelle Combès. “Yaci: mythes et représentations tupi-guarani de la lune.” 1991.
- Later work by Guaraní scholars and collaborators correcting the flattening tendencies of older comparative anthropology.

### Overall evidentiary conclusion

A deep, widespread Tupí-Guaraní brother-hero complex is securely documented. What cannot be sustained is a single canonical biography of “Pa’i Kuaray and Jasy.” The historically responsible reconstruction is plural:

- Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva brothers are not simply Cadogan’s named pair.
- Cadogan’s Kuaray creates Jaxy and is not his biological twin.
- Paĩ/Kaiowá tellings preserve the maternal bones and jaguar-revenge sequence in distinctive forms.
- Tupinambá and Andean-foothill narratives are comparanda, not interchangeable witnesses.
- Colmán’s national pantheon is a twentieth-century literary achievement.
- Living Guaraní narration remains the latest stage of the documentary history, not an appendix to an “ancient” religion frozen in 1914.
