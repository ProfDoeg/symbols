# Jasy: Research Dossier

**Class:** A male lunar culture hero/divine ancestor in the best-documented Mbyá, Apapokúva/Ñandeva, and Kaiowá–Paĩ Tavyterã traditions; the personified Moon and younger brother—or, in Mbyá versions, created companion-brother—of the solar hero Kuaray/Paĩ Kuara. In Paraguayan and Argentine popular discourse, *Jasy* may instead mean simply “the Moon,” grammatically unmarked for natural gender, and modern summaries frequently recast Jasy/Jaci as a female “moon goddess.” That female pan–Tupi-Guaraní goddess is not the classification supported by the principal Mbyá and Kaiowá texts.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (Madrid, 1639), under the missionary spelling *jachy*, “Luna, y mes”—“moon and month.” This securely attests the word and its calendrical extension, but not a named god, genealogy, temple, cult image, or developed Jasy narrative. The earliest systematic surviving divine-person narratives relevant to this dossier are Curt Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva texts, collected in the early twentieth century and published in German in 1914. The earliest extensive Mbyá text is León Cadogan’s “Paĩ Rete Kuaray” cycle, first published in abbreviated form in 1946 and fully in *Ayvu Rapyta* in 1959.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY LEXICAL]** Seventeenth-century missionary documentation of language or custom.
- **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]** An orally transmitted account recorded from an identified or locally situated Indigenous community.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** Material attested only, or differently, among a particular Guaraní-speaking people or locality.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** A historical, linguistic, or structural inference rather than an Indigenous narrator’s explicit statement.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Paraguayan, Correntino, or Misionero popular tradition, often transmitted among Guaraní-speaking but non-Indigenous rural populations.
- **[LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]** A modern author’s ordering or expansion of traditional motifs.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A claim for which no corresponding early or community-specific evidence has been located and whose documented circulation is modern.
- **[NOT ESTABLISHED]** Unsupported by the examined record.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Jasy and its spellings

**Jasy**, modern Paraguayan Guaraní pronunciation approximately /jaˈsɨ/, means “Moon,” “month,” and in some modern dictionaries “menstruation.” Montoya printed **jachy** in 1639. Spanish-language sources commonly use **Yasy**; Portuguese and Old Tupi orthographies use **Jaci**, **Yaci**, or **Iacy**. Mbyá sources normally write **Jaxy** or Cadogan’s older **Jachy**. Reconstruction gives Proto–Tupi-Guaraní *jatʃɨ and, more remotely, Proto-Tupian *watɨ, but those forms are comparative-linguistic reconstructions, not historically written divine names. **[EARLY LEXICAL / SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**

The double meaning “moon/month” is secure and ordinary: lunar cycles supplied the calendrical unit. Modern Guaraní month names such as *jasyapy* incorporate *jasy*, although some standardized month formations are modern lexical planning rather than ancient cult titles.

### Documented personal and titular forms

- **Jasy / Jaxy / Jachy:** Moon; in myths, the lunar younger brother. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**
- **Jasyrã / Jachyrã:** “Future Moon,” from *jasy* plus prospective *-rã*. Cadogan applies it to the companion created by Paĩ Rete Kuaray before he takes his celestial station. **[Mbyá REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Ñanderu Jasy:** “Our Father Moon.” A reverential title in some Guaraní recensions. It expresses divine kinship rather than biological paternity in the European sense. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Tyvyry / Tyvýra / Tyvyra’i:** “Younger brother,” the relational name of the junior hero in the Twins cycle. Not every source explicitly equates Tyvyry with the visible Moon. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION; IDENTIFICATION DISPUTED]**
- **Jasy, owner/guardian of night:** Contemporary Kaiowá testimony calls him the *jára*, owner or guardian, of night, paired with Paĩ Kuara as owner of daylight. **[CONTINUING ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**
- **Ava jasuka ei ñepyrũ:** A Paĩ Tavyterã religious expression reported in later lexicography for Jasy, approximately “primordial man of indestructible jasuka/honey.” The semantics of *jasuka* belong to Paĩ theology and should not be reduced to ordinary honey. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**

### Gender

In Cadogan’s Mbyá material and the Kaiowá–Paĩ corpus, Jasy is male: “the Moon” is a younger brother and male divine ancestor. Isabelle Combès’s comparative study finds the personified Moon generally male across the relevant Tupi-Guaraní evidence, while recording exceptional or doubled male/female moons among some Chiripá. Claude d’Abbeville reported that early seventeenth-century Tupinambá addressed the eclipsed Moon as *tamõi*, “grandfather.” **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION / COMPARATIVE SCHOLARSHIP]**

The widespread internet formula “Jaci, Guaraní goddess of the Moon, wife or sister of Guaraci, mother of Rudá” improperly merges Brazilian literary folklore, several Indigenous traditions, and late national mythology. It is not a safe description of Mbyá or Kaiowá Jasy. **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Jasy is not Jasy Jatere

Jasy Jatere is a distinct forest/siesta being. Juan Bautista Ambrosetti documented **Yasy-Yateré** in the Alto Paraná–Misiones region in 1894 as a small, child-stealing or child-enticing being associated with a whistle and golden staff. Narciso R. Colmán later made him the fourth of Tau and Kerana’s seven monstrous sons in *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929). Neither fact makes Jasy Jatere the Moon himself, Jasy’s son, or a member of the Mbyá Twins cycle. The popular etymology “fragment of the Moon” is disputed; proposals include an elided *Jasy apytere*, “from the middle/interior of the Moon,” but these are retrospective explanations, not securely established historical derivations. **[FOLKLORE / LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Seventeenth-century evidence

Montoya’s *Tesoro* is a Guaraní–Spanish dictionary printed in Madrid in 1639 after his missionary work in Guayrá and the Paraná reductions. Its *jachy* entry establishes “moon/month.” Its lunar phrases document observation and ordinary speech, but Montoya does not supply the later Mbyá biography of Jasy. A modern study of Montoya’s ethnographic vocabulary accordingly observes that nothing in the *Tesoro* demonstrates his knowledge of the myth of Jasy as Kuaray’s younger brother. **[EARLY LEXICAL; NEGATIVE FINDING]**

Montoya’s *Conquista espiritual*—also printed in Madrid in 1639 and narrating Jesuit activity from 1610 to 1637—is an important colonial record of conversion, Indigenous resistance, missions, and bandeirante violence. It is not an early witness to the complete Jasy cycle. Its silence cannot prove that no such stories existed, only that the chronicle does not document them.

The Jesuit evidence is structurally biased. Dictionaries preserved Guaraní words while catechisms translated Christian concepts through them; missionary chronicles privileged matters relevant to conversion. A missing myth may reflect suppression, secrecy, selection, or simple irrelevance to the author. Conversely, later sacred narratives cannot automatically be projected unchanged behind 1639.

### Nimuendajú and the Apapokúva

Curt Nimuendajú collected Apapokúva-Guaraní religion in southern Brazil and published *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* in 1914. A Portuguese translation, *As lendas da criação e destruição do mundo*, appeared in 1987.

Nimuendajú recorded:

- Ñande Ru Vusu/Ñanderuvuçú, “Our Great Father,” the first-earth creator;
- Ñande Sy, mother of the great brothers;
- Ñanderykey, “Our Elder Brother,” and Tyvyry, “Younger Brother”;
- an additional astral Sun-and-Moon brother narrative;
- the Twins’ terrestrial adventures, separation of humans from jaguar beings, and eventual celestial order;
- eschatology and the Land Without Evil.

A crucial qualification is often lost: Nimuendajú did not simply write “Ñanderykey is the Sun and Tyvyry is the Moon” as an uncontested Apapokúva identity. He distinguished the great culture-hero pair from an astral pair, while suspecting that they were variants of the same complex. Later comparativists—Métraux and Combès among them—argued for the solar/lunar identification because parallel Tupi-Guaraní versions explicitly make the elder Sun and the younger Moon. Graciela Chamorro therefore notes both facts: Nimuendajú’s categorical caution and the widespread Indigenous identification in later testimony. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION versus SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus

León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta: Textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá* was published by the University of São Paulo in 1959. The collection preserves Mbyá-language texts with Spanish translation, including sacred material disclosed after years of relationship with Mbyá leaders. Chapter VIII, “Paĩ Rete Kuaray,” is explicitly the “myth of the Twins,” the genesis of Sun and Moon, and their deeds.

The principal narrators named in Cadogan’s apparatus include Cacique Pablo Vera, Mayor Francisco of Tava’i/San Juan Nepomuceno, Patricio Escobar, and Laureano Escobar. Cadogan also recorded disagreement over Christian-sounding names: his consultants reportedly identified *Papa Tenonde* as a Christian name for the creator. This makes *Ayvu Rapyta* evidence of a living, internally discussed twentieth-century tradition—not a stenographic transcript of a timeless precontact scripture.

Cadogan’s earlier publications are chronologically important:

- “Jasy ra’y ojovahéi,” a Guairá/Mbyá account explaining new-moon rains and lunar spots, published in *Cultura* in October 1945.
- “Paĩ Rete Kuaray,” abbreviated, in the *Revista de la Sociedad Científica del Paraguay*, 1946.
- *Ayvu Rapyta*, full corpus, 1959.
- *Ywyra Ñe’ery*, a later lexical-ethnographic “treasure” of Mbyá language and culture, now preserved by the Fundación León Cadogan.

Pierre Clastres later translated and reorganized related Guaraní sacred speech in *Le Grand Parler: Mythes et chants sacrés des Indiens Guarani* (1974). His French prose is an influential scholarly-literary presentation, not an earlier primary attestation than Cadogan.

### Kaiowá–Paĩ Tavyterã corpus

Egon Schaden, Bartomeu Melià and colleagues, Branislava Sušnik, Graciela Chamorro, Antônio Brand, Levi Marques Pereira, and Indigenous Kaiowá researchers document a related but nonidentical cycle. Paĩ Kuara and Jasy are the elder and younger sons of Ñande Ru/Ñane Ramõi and Ñande Sy/Ñande Jari. Their family is described in current Kaiowá scholarship as the most important ancestral-divine family.

Modern Indigenous testimony continues to call Paĩ Kuara owner of daylight and Jasy owner of night. These sources demonstrate continuity into the twenty-first century; they do not license replacing the different Mbyá genealogy with the Kaiowá one.

### Argentine folklore corpora

Ambrosetti’s *Materiales para el estudio del folk-lore misionero* (1894), later incorporated into *Supersticiones y leyendas* (1917), is early evidence for regional Yasy-Yateré belief but provides little for Jasy as the lunar brother. Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini’s twentieth-century Argentine collections preserve solar, lunar, Christian, and Guaraní-derived tales from Corrientes and Misiones. Their records are valuable as folklore with named places and narrators, not automatically as Indigenous sacred tradition.

No secure evidence located in these corpora establishes a separate temple cult of Jasy in Corrientes or Misiones.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Jasy’s secure primary domain is the Moon, hence night, lunar phases, and calendrical months. Among contemporary Kaiowá he may be called the *jára* of night. In Mbyá accounts, Kuaray assigns him the nocturnal celestial station while taking day for himself. Their motion sustains alternating day and night, lunar phases, and seasonal cycles. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**

The Moon regulates practical time. Guaraní agricultural evidence links lunar phases with planting, cutting wood, hunting, fishing, and harvesting. Such prescriptions vary: one community plants particular manioc cultivars during *jasy mbarete*, the strong/full moon; other popular prescriptions prefer new or waning phases. There is no single pan-Guaraní lunar agricultural code. **[LOCAL PRACTICE]**

### Character

Jasy is repeatedly the impulsive, impatient, imitative, or disobedient junior to prudent Kuaray. This is not merely comic inferiority. Their contrast produces an imperfect but habitable world:

- Jasy pulls the jaguar-trap vine too soon, allowing one pregnant jaguar to escape.
- He interrupts or spoils the attempt to restore their mother, making death permanent.
- He visits dangerous beings after warning and is killed; Kuaray revives him.
- His unsuccessful or incomplete acts contrast with Kuaray’s controlled creative power.

The younger brother’s mistakes explain mortality, dangerous animals, and imperfections while displaying the elder brother’s continuing protection.

### Physical form and symbols

In the terrestrial cycle Jasy is anthropomorphic, a boy or young man. In the celestial phase he is the visible lunar body. His chief visual marker is the spotted face. He may carry a bow and arrows; an arrow-chain or arrow-ladder enables celestial ascent in several recensions.

The Mbyá creation of Jasyrã from a branch of **kurupika’y** (*Sapium* species) associates him with a resinous tree used for material and ritual purposes. Other versions create the younger brother from their mother’s bones. These are genuine contradictory variants, not two stages of one canonical birth.

No precolonial cult statue, securely identified archaeological image, fixed color, sacred number, or standard regalia of Jasy has been established. Museum statues and illustrated “Guaraní pantheons” are modern representations.

### Moon spots

Cadogan’s Guairá account says Jasyrã entered women’s sleeping places at night. Acting on Paĩ Rete Kuaray’s instruction, the women marked the intruder’s face with indelible resin, identifying him. Kuaray then assigned him to the sky, where the stains remain. At new moon he washes his face; the proverbial *Jasy ra’y ojovahéi hína*, “the new moon is washing its face,” explains torrential rain around the new moon. **[Mbyá/GUAIRÁ REGIONAL VARIANT, recorded 1945]**

Comparative Tupi-Guaraní versions use genipap rather than tree resin and frame the act as incest: Moon secretly visits a sister, aunt, or—in the Apapokúva astral story—his elder brother, who marks his face. Combès’s comparison correctly treats “lunar incest plus facial pigment” as a widespread complex while preserving the differences in partner and gender. **[COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION BASED ON DISTINCT REGIONAL TEXTS]**

### Moon and manioc

Two claims must be separated:

1. Lunar phases guide manioc planting and the cutting of manioc stems. This is directly documented agricultural knowledge. **[LOCAL PRACTICE]**
2. Jasy personally created manioc, wept upon a manioc field, or is a universal “goddess of manioc.” This circulates in modern popular mythology but was not found in Montoya, Nimuendajú, or the relevant portion of Cadogan. **[NOT ESTABLISHED / MODERN RETELLING]**

The famous girl Mani whose body produces manioc belongs to Brazilian Tupi literary folklore and should not be inserted into Jasy’s Mbyá biography without a local source.

## 4. Genealogy and Divine Relations

### Competing genealogies

**Mbyá, Cadogan:** Paĩ Rete Kuaray creates Jasyrã from a kurupika’y branch because he desires a companion. They become elder and younger brothers by created kinship, not uterine twins. **[Mbyá REGIONAL VARIANT]**

**Mbyá variants:** Jaxy is formed from the scattered bones of the dead mother. **[Mbyá REGIONAL VARIANT]**

**Apapokúva:** Ñanderykey and Tyvyry have complicated paternity in the Twins cycle; Nanderu Mba’ekuaa is associated with the conception of the junior in some readings. Nimuendajú keeps the great brothers analytically distinct from a separate Sun-and-Moon pair, though later comparison joins them. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION / DISPUTED IDENTIFICATION]**

**Kaiowá–Paĩ Tavyterã:** Paĩ Kuara and Jasy are the sons of the primordial father and Ñande Sy/Ñande Jari, called twins but hierarchically elder and younger. Some accounts give them the same divine father; comparative sources preserve dual-paternity structures. **[REGIONAL VARIANTS]**

**Modern Paraguayan national mythology:** Lists that make a female Jasy wife of Kuarahy, or place Jasy within Colmán’s Tupã–Arasy genealogy, are literary syntheses and must not replace the ethnographic versions. **[LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Documented interactions with named divine or supernatural beings

- **Kuaray / Paĩ Rete Kuaray / Paĩ Kuara:** Creator-companion and elder brother in Mbyá texts; co-born elder brother in Kaiowá; together they avenge their mother, transform the world, combat hostile beings, and ascend. Cadogan, “Paĩ Rete Kuaray,” 1946/1959; Kaiowá ethnographies, twentieth–twenty-first centuries. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION; REGIONAL VARIANTS]**
- **Ñande Sy / Ñande Chy (“Our Mother”):** Mother in the Kaiowá and wider Twins cycle. Jasy’s impatience helps defeat Kuaray’s attempt to restore her, fixing death in the world. Nimuendajú 1914; later Ñandeva/Kaiowá and comparative versions. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**
- **Ñande Ru Vusu / Ñanderuvusu / Ñane Ramõi Jusu Papa:** Father or senior divine ancestor sought in heaven. Nimuendajú 1914; Paĩ Tavyterã and Kaiowá ethnographies. **[REGIONAL VARIANTS]**
- **Ñanderu Mba’ekuaa (“Our Father Who Knows Things”):** Father of the astral brothers in the Apapokúva material and, in some readings, genitor of the junior hero. Nimuendajú 1914. **[Apapokúva REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Jaguarete / primordial jaguar beings:** Kill the pregnant mother and rear or retain the brothers; Jasy and Paĩ Kuara later kill them in a bridge or water trap. One pregnant female survives because Jasy acts prematurely, explaining surviving jaguars. Nimuendajú 1914; Cadogan 1959; Kaiowá versions. **[WIDESPREAD CYCLE WITH VARIANTS]**
- **Parakáo Ñe’ẽngatu, “Parrot of the Discreet/Good Words”:** Reveals to the brothers that the jaguars killed their mother, precipitating revenge. Cadogan 1945/1959; Kaiowá versions. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**
- **Añay / Añá:** Jasy disobeys warnings, attends the hostile beings’ feast, is killed or trapped, and is restored by Paĩ Kuara. Kaiowá ethnographies and later recensions. *Añay* is often translated “devil,” but Christian equivalence may obscure a specifically Guaraní category of dangerous other-being. **[REGIONAL VARIANT / POSSIBLE CHRISTIAN TRANSLATIONAL OVERLAY]**
- **Women who mark Moon:** They identify the nocturnal sexual intruder with resin or genipap; their marks become lunar spots. Cadogan 1945 and 1959. These women are not named goddesses. **[Mbyá REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Jasy Jatere:** A Kaiowá study reports Jasy Jatere as Paĩ Kuara’s son, after which uncle and nephew join in frustrating Paĩ Kuara. This is a specific modern Kaiowá recension, not the Colmán genealogy and not universal. **[LOCAL KAIowá VARIANT]**
- **Tau, Kerana, Arasy, and the seven monsters:** No interaction with Moon-Jasy is established in early Mbyá or Apapokúva sources. Jasy Jatere’s placement among Tau and Kerana’s sons belongs to Colmán’s 1929 system. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE / LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Tupã:** No secure early episode of a personal Jasy–Tupã encounter was located. Colmán and popular pantheon charts connect them inside a synthesized genealogy. **[NOT ESTABLISHED before modern systematization]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The journey of the pregnant mother

The primordial father quarrels with or leaves his pregnant wife and ascends. She follows, guided by children who speak from the womb. The unborn elder asks for flowers; after she refuses or injures him, he misdirects her. She reaches the dwelling of primordial jaguar beings. An old female attempts to hide or protect her, but the returning jaguars kill and eat her, sparing the unborn children. **[Apapokúva/Kaiowá cycle; details vary]**

In Mbyá versions, the elder hero may precede and create the junior from a branch or bones; this resists the label “biological twins.” “Twins cycle” is therefore a comparative genre name, not a universally correct genealogy.

### Revenge on the jaguars

A parrot tells the growing brothers what happened. They devise a crossing or bridge trap. As the jaguars cross, the brothers release the support and drown or destroy them. Jasy pulls too early; one pregnant jaguar escapes and becomes ancestress of present jaguars. The story explains why jaguars exist and why relations between humans and predatory alterity remain dangerous. **[ETHNOGRAPHIC ATTESTATION]**

### Failed resurrection and the origin of death

Kuaray gathers or works upon his mother’s bones and nearly restores her. Jasy looks, laughs, cries out, attempts to suckle, or otherwise interrupts too soon. Complete resurrection fails. Depending on the recension, the remains produce animals such as paca or birds; the mother becomes another celestial being or remains dead. Jasy’s impatience accounts for irreversible human death. **[REGIONAL VARIANTS]**

### Creation and correction

The brothers travel, naming, making, or transforming animals, plants, paths, and human conditions. Kuaray’s deliberate work is repeatedly compromised by Jasy’s haste. The world is completed but retains predators, death, bodily limitations, and other imperfections. In Kaiowá interpretation, the elder’s repeated rescue of his erring brother models protective relations between divine elder siblings and living Kaiowá descendants.

### The Moon’s marked face

Jasyrã sexually approaches women under darkness. They mark him with resin at Kuaray’s direction. Recognized and rebuked, he is sent to govern night. His stains become lunar markings, and his attempts to wash them explain new-moon rain. Other Tupi-Guaraní versions change the partner to sister, paternal aunt, or elder brother and the pigment to genipap. **[Mbyá account plus comparative variants]**

### Celestial ascent

The brothers fast, pray, dance, or shoot arrows into one another to form a ladder. They ascend to the divine realm. Kuaray reaches the higher blue sky and takes the day; Jasy’s arrows reach a nearer white-cloud level, and he takes night. The narrative establishes their unequal but complementary celestial positions. **[Mbyá and Kaiowá variants]**

### Lunar phases and eclipses

Some regional stories explain lunar phases through Jasy’s encounters with an *añá* and bodily dismemberment or destruction followed by restoration. Eclipses may be understood as a celestial jaguar or great bat attacking the luminary; some Kaiowá accounts treat eclipse, storm, and cosmic predators as warnings of possible catastrophe. These explanations belong to particular communities and are not uniform throughout Guaraní-speaking America.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No securely identified precolonial temple, permanent priesthood devoted solely to Jasy, cult statue, treasury, or state festival has been documented. Guaraní religious life historically centered on settlement, kin group, sacred speech, dance, song, tobacco, maize rites, and specialist leaders rather than a Mediterranean-style lunar temple institution. Calling every celestial being’s social importance a “cult” would misdescribe the evidence.

Jasy is present ritually and practically through:

- reckoning months and seasons by the Moon;
- choosing times for planting manioc and maize, harvesting, hunting, fishing, and cutting timber;
- reading eclipses and abnormal celestial events as danger;
- sacred recitation of the brothers’ cycle;
- dances, songs, fasting, and bodily lightening associated with the heroes’ celestial ascent;
- contemporary Kaiowá prayer and song addressed to or mediated through the *jára* and divine elder siblings.

The *opy*, or Mbyá prayer house, is the institutional setting for broader Mbyá sacred life, not a temple dedicated exclusively to Jasy. No documented human sacrifice to Jasy, temple prostitution, lunar oracle, or Jasy-specific sacrificial priesthood was found. Claims of those practices should be rejected unless primary evidence is produced.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1639: reconstructable background

Comparative linguistics securely reconstructs a Tupi-Guaraní lunar word ancestral to *jasy/jaxy/jaci*. Comparative mythology reveals a widely distributed elder/younger culture-hero complex associated in many communities with Sun and Moon. Neither method dates a particular surviving narrative to a precise precolonial century. **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**

### 1639–1767: mission period

Montoya records *jachy* as moon/month amid the Jesuit program of reducing, catechizing, and defending Guaraní communities. Missionaries translated Christian doctrine into Guaraní and condemned non-Christian religious specialists and practices. Reduction, epidemic disease, enslavement, warfare, and bandeirante raids radically altered the social setting in which oral traditions circulated.

The Jesuit records do not document a Jasy temple destroyed or a specific campaign against lunar worship. Suppression of Indigenous religion was general and institutional; a Jasy-specific persecution is **[NOT ESTABLISHED]**. Jesuit expulsion in 1767 ended their administration but did not restore premission social conditions.

### Nineteenth century: regional folklore

Guaraní lunar vocabulary remained active in Paraguay and northeast Argentina. Rural folklore increasingly mixed Indigenous motifs, Catholic figures, Iberian beings, plantation life, and yerbatero experience. Ambrosetti’s 1894 Yasy-Yateré record is evidence of this mixed regional world, not evidence that Colmán’s later seven-monster genealogy was already canonical.

### 1914–1959: ethnographic disclosure

Nimuendajú’s 1914 monograph made the Apapokúva creation/destruction cycle and Land Without Evil central to international scholarship. Cadogan’s work from the 1940s culminated in *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959), documenting Mbyá sacred language and a clearly lunar male younger brother. These publications transformed a living but locally guarded tradition into a printed scholarly corpus.

### 1929 onward: national literary pantheon

Narciso R. Colmán, writing as Rosicrán, published *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* in 1929. The long Guaraní poem assembled a grand national “Genesis of the Guaraní race,” linking Tupã, Arasy, Tau, Kerana, seven monstrous children, Porãsy, and named places.

Modern scholarship describes the work as fictional or literary despite its use of genuine folkloric beings. Colmán did not invent every being—Yasy-Yateré, Pombero, Kurupí, and Luisón circulated independently—but he fixed them into a closed genealogy. The resulting schoolbook “Guaraní pantheon” often displaced the far less tidy Mbyá, Apapokúva, and Kaiowá evidence. **[LITERARY SYSTEMATIZATION]**

### Late twentieth century to present

Mbyá and Kaiowá communities continue religious practice and narration under conditions of land loss, missionization, discrimination, and cultural revitalization. Contemporary territorial reports describe Kuaray and Jaxy as the Guaraní brothers whose path serves as a “book” for human conduct. Jasy survives not merely as an antiquarian god-name but within living Indigenous cosmology.

At the same time, commercial and internet culture has produced a female “Jaci goddess,” pantheon charts, fantasy genealogies, statues, games, webcomics, and neopagan devotion. These may be meaningful new religious or artistic practices, but they are modern receptions, not evidence for an ancient uniform goddess cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, education, and national identity

Colmán’s 1929 poem had the greatest systematizing effect. Later school materials and tourist museums turned fluid beings into a roster comparable to Greek mythology. Jasy Jatere’s membership in the seven-monster family is the clearest example. The Moon-Jasy dossier must not import that family merely because the two names share *jasy*.

Modern Guaraní poetry uses Jasy as moon, beloved, beauty, time, and national-language symbol. Communities and people bear names such as Jasy Porã, “beautiful moon.” Such names attest continuing cultural productivity, not necessarily worship of a deity.

### “Goddess Jaci”

Brazilian children’s literature, folklore dictionaries, New Age pages, and neopagan posts increasingly present Jaci as:

- a female moon goddess;
- wife or sister of a male sun god Guaraci;
- mother of Rudá;
- protector of lovers, women, animals, and plants;
- maker of stars or mermaids.

The examined Mbyá and Kaiowá sources instead make Moon male. Some Tupi-Guaraní groups do possess female or dual lunar conceptions, so “female Moon” is not intrinsically impossible; the error is treating the modern composite as the single ancient Guaraní doctrine. **[MODERN SYNCRETIC COMPOSITE]**

### Occultism and neopaganism

Searches for Jasy with “occult,” “neopagan,” “Wicca,” and related terms found recent devotional posts but no documented Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, medieval grimoire, or early-modern European cult of Jasy. A modern practitioner may adopt Jaci/Jasy, but claims of an unbroken esoteric lineage are **[MODERN INVENTION]**.

### Political appropriation

Guaraní names and mythic beings are widely used in Paraguayan national identity, tourism, monuments, school curricula, and branding. This nationalization can celebrate the Guaraní language while obscuring the authority and distinct traditions of living Indigenous peoples. No substantial extremist appropriation of Jasy specifically was located.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is the younger hero actually the Moon?

Nimuendajú distinguished the great Apapokúva brothers from the astral brothers and cautioned against an automatic equation. Métraux argued comparatively that the elder and junior culture heroes are solar and lunar because neighboring Tupi-Guaraní traditions say so explicitly. Combès refined the comparison, treating the pairs as transformations rather than simply declaring them identical. Later Indigenous Kaiowá and Mbyá testimony often makes the identification explicit. The responsible conclusion is therefore regional:

- explicit Moon in Cadogan’s Mbyá and modern Kaiowá records;
- structurally probable but textually qualified in Nimuendajú’s Apapokúva account.

### Twins or creator and companion?

“Divine Twins” is useful comparatively but inaccurate for Cadogan’s Mbyá version, in which Kuaray creates Jasyrã. Mbyá variants form him from a branch or bones. Kaiowá versions may call them twins while maintaining elder/junior hierarchy. The contradiction is part of the corpus.

### Male or female?

The Mbyá and Kaiowá Moon is male. Some Chiripá reports distinguish male and female moons. Brazilian national folklore often feminizes Jaci, perhaps under Portuguese grammatical gender (*a lua*) and literary comparison with European moon goddesses. No single gender statement covers every Tupi-Guaraní people.

### Christian influence

Mission influence is undeniable at the level of colonial history and vocabulary, but particular borrowings must be demonstrated. “Devil” for *añay*, “paradise” for a divine celestial land, and *Papa Tenonde* for the creator invite Christian comparison. Cadogan’s own consultants reportedly called *Papa Tenonde* a Christian name. This does not make the entire cycle a Jesuit invention; its structural parallels across distant Tupi-Guaraní peoples argue otherwise.

### “Land Without Evil”

Nimuendajú made *yvy marã e’ỹ* famous as “Land Without Evil.” Hélène Clastres emphasized prophetic migration and resistance; later scholars, especially Melià, criticized overly abstract or universalized readings. Jasy participates in the celestial journey complex, but he is not independently the god of the Land Without Evil.

### State of the evidence

There is no ancient manuscript written by precontact Guaraní authors and no excavated inscription naming Jasy as a god. Evidence consists of:

- missionary dictionaries, catechisms, chronicles, and reduction records;
- orally transmitted texts recorded by outsiders;
- Indigenous testimony within later ethnography;
- folklore collections;
- comparative linguistics;
- contemporary Indigenous scholarship and practice.

This archive preserves invaluable material but reflects translation, editorial selection, secrecy, and colonial power.

## 10. Chronology

- **Precolonial, undated:** Proto–Tupi-Guaraní lunar vocabulary and a widespread culture-hero complex are reconstructable, but no precise “origin date” for Jasy can be assigned. **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **1575:** André Thevet publishes early coastal Tupi twin and lunar materials; they are comparative Tupi evidence, not direct Paraguayan Mbyá testimony.
- **1614:** Claude d’Abbeville reports Tupinambá addressing the eclipsed Moon as *tamõi*, “grandfather.”
- **1639:** Montoya’s *Tesoro* records *jachy*, “moon and month.” Earliest secure Guaraní attestation used here.
- **1639:** Montoya publishes *Conquista espiritual*, a major mission chronicle but not a developed Jasy dossier.
- **1767–1768:** Jesuits are expelled from Spanish America; the reduction system is dismantled and reorganized.
- **1894:** Ambrosetti publishes an early Misionero account of Yasy-Yateré, showing that figure’s independent folklore existence before Colmán.
- **1914:** Nimuendajú publishes the Apapokúva creation/destruction corpus in German.
- **1928:** Métraux publishes *La religion des Tupinamba et ses rapports avec celle des autres tribus tupi-guarani*, establishing a major comparative framework.
- **1929:** Colmán publishes *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, fixing a literary national pantheon and the seven-monster genealogy.
- **1945:** Cadogan publishes “Jasy ra’y ojovahéi,” documenting the face-washing/new-moon-rain tradition and the origin of the lunar spots.
- **1946:** Cadogan publishes an abbreviated “Paĩ Rete Kuaray.”
- **1959:** Complete *Ayvu Rapyta* appears; chapter VIII preserves the Mbyá genesis and adventures of Sun and Moon.
- **1960s–1970s:** Schaden, Melià, Cadogan, Sušnik, and others expand comparative Guaraní ethnography.
- **1974:** Pierre Clastres publishes *Le Grand Parler*.
- **1978:** Hélène Clastres’s *La Terre sans Mal* gives the prophetic-migratory complex broad scholarly influence.
- **1987:** Portuguese translation of Nimuendajú’s monograph appears; Melià and collaborators publish major Paĩ Tavyterã ethnography.
- **1991:** Isabelle Combès publishes “Yaci: mythes et représentations tupi-guarani de la lune,” directly addressing gender, incest, spots, and the relation between the astral and culture-hero pairs.
- **1992:** A major expanded edition of *Ayvu Rapyta* appears under the Fundación León Cadogan.
- **2003:** Cadogan’s Guaraní-folklore studies are republished under Bartomeu Melià’s editorship.
- **Twenty-first century:** Mbyá and Kaiowá accounts continue in Indigenous education, territorial documentation, ethnography, ritual life, and scholarship; simultaneously, internet and popular culture expand the unsupported universal “Jaci moon goddess” composite.

## 11. Conclusions of the contested-material audit

- **Etymology:** *Jasy* “moon/month” is old and securely attested; its deeper Proto-Tupian ancestry is reconstructed. No secure derivation from a divine personal name is required.
- **Origin:** The date and community of the personified hero’s first creation cannot be established.
- **Gender:** Male in the principal Mbyá and Kaiowá records; variable elsewhere; a universal female goddess is unsupported.
- **Twins:** Accurate for many comparative and Kaiowá versions, but Cadogan’s Mbyá Jasy is made by Kuaray.
- **Manioc:** Lunar agricultural regulation is documented; a canonical Jasy-creates-manioc myth is not.
- **Moon spots:** Strongly attested as pigment/resin placed on a nocturnal sexual visitor; the identity of the partner varies sharply.
- **Jasy Jatere:** A separate folklore being; Colmán’s seven-brother placement is a 1929 literary system.
- **Syncretism:** Christian vocabulary and mission history influenced presentation; no simple Jasy–Virgin Mary, Jasy–Diana, or Jasy–Selene equation is historically documented.
- **Human sacrifice:** No evidence of sacrifice to Jasy located.
- **Temple cult:** No Jasy-specific temple, idol, priesthood, or state cult securely documented.
- **Suppression:** General colonial Christian suppression and restructuring of Guaraní religion are documented; a targeted destruction of a Jasy cult is not.
- **Occult/neopagan lineage:** Recent devotion exists online; no premodern Western occult lineage was found.
- **Continuous existence:** Jasy remains a living religious-cosmological figure among Guaraní communities, but “continuity” does not mean that every modern statement reproduces an unchanged precontact doctrine.
