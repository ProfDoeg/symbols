# Tupã: Research Dossier

**Class:** The classification changes with corpus and period. In the earliest colonial Tupinambá reports, *Toupan/Toupan* denotes thunder or a sky-associated rain-and-thunder power, not securely a supreme creator. Jesuit usage made *Tupã* the Guaraní and Tupi name for the Christian God. In twentieth-century Mbyá testimony, **Tupã Ru Ete** is one of several divine “true fathers,” especially associated with rain, thunder, lightning, waters, and cosmic refreshment. In Paraguayan literary mythology after Narciso R. Colmán, Tupã becomes an anthropomorphic supreme creator and culture-god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** André Thevet’s *Les Singularitez de la France antarctique*, printed in Paris in **1557**, chapter 28, concerning coastal Tupinambá near Guanabara Bay. Thevet writes that they speak of “a great lord,” called **Toupan**, who is above and makes rain and thunder, but whom—according to Thevet—they neither pray to nor honor. This is a European-mediated ethnographic report, not a surviving pre-contact Indigenous text or inscription. [Thevet’s work and passage](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Singularitez_de_la_France_antarctique)

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Documentary identity

“Tupã” is best understood as a historically changing complex of related words and beings rather than a single god with one recoverable biography.

- **Securely attested early material:** Sixteenth-century coastal Tupi/Tupinambá *Toupan, Toupan/Tupan(a)*, associated with thunder and rain.
- **Early-modern missionary translation:** *Tupã* as “God,” especially the Christian creator, Trinity, and God the Father in catechetical Guaraní and Tupi.
- **Regional Mbyá tradition:** **Tupã Ru Ete**, “Tupã, the true father,” a divine father within a plural cosmogony.
- **Paraguayan vernacular Christianity:** *Tupã* remains a name for God, alongside **Ñandejára**, “our lord/owner.”
- **Modern literary systematization:** Tupã as supreme creator, husband of Arasy, maker of the first human pair, and creator or overseer of good and evil.
- **Modern popular mythology:** A standardized “Guaraní pantheon” assembled from Mbyá ethnography, Jesuit vocabulary, Colmán’s poem, regional folklore, and later retellings.

These identities overlap historically but should not be collapsed.

### Forms

| Form | Language or context | Attestation and meaning |

### Etymology

**Secure linguistic finding:** Proto-Tupi-Guaraní lexicography reconstructs a form approximately ***tupã*** with the field “thunder” and, in daughter languages under missionary influence, “deity/God.” The Hunter-Gatherer Language Database summarizes it as an inherited word for an old thunder-causing spirit or power later selected for the Christian God. [Comparative lexical entry](https://huntergatherer.la.utexas.edu/languages/language/152)

**Disputed analyses:**

- Derivations such as *tu* “exclamation” plus *pã* “question/interrogation,” or “the sound that strikes,” are popular etymologies rather than secure historical morphology.
- The old proposal that the name meant “exalted father,” from alleged elements comparable to *tuba* “father” and *ama* “standing/exalted,” was rejected by Geraldo Lapenda because the required semantic and phonological developments are not attested.
- Lapenda regarded **Tupana** as the older Tupi form and **Tupã** as a Guaraní-influenced contraction, but his further claim that it naturally developed from thunder into an Indigenous “Supreme Being” partly preserves the Christian evolutionary assumptions of older comparative religion. [Lapenda, “Etimologia da Palavra Tupã”](https://pt.wikisource.org/wiki/Etimologia_da_Palavra_%E2%80%9CTup%C3%A3%E2%80%9D)

“Thunder” is therefore the safest early gloss. “Supreme creator” is not a linguistically recoverable original meaning.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

There are no known pre-contact inscriptions, manuscripts, temples, or securely identified images of Tupã. All early evidence is post-contact and was recorded by Europeans in alphabetic writing.

### Thevet, 1557

In *Les Singularitez de la France antarctique*, chapter 28, André Thevet says the Tupinambá speak of a “grand Seigneur,” **Toupan**, situated above, who makes rain and thunder. He immediately adds that they have no manner of praying to or honoring him. The information was at least partly mediated by an interpreter who had lived among the people.

What this establishes:

- The name and weather association existed by the mid-sixteenth century.
- Some informants permitted a personal or agentive description of the power.
- Thevet perceived neither an organized cult nor routine prayer.

What it does not establish:

- That Toupan was the supreme creator.
- That all Tupi-Guaraní peoples held one theology.
- That Toupan possessed temples, priests, an anthropomorphic image, spouse, or genealogy.

### Jean de Léry, 1578

Léry’s *Histoire d’un voyage faict en la terre du Brésil*, based on his 1557–1558 stay near Guanabara but printed in 1578, treats **Toupan** primarily through thunder. His account is filtered through Reformed Christian polemic: thunder becomes an occasion for discussion of God, fear, conscience, and humanity’s knowledge of divinity. It confirms the lexical association but is not independent evidence for a developed “thunder-god cult.” [Bibliographic account](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histoire_d%27un_voyage)

### Anchieta and Brazilian missionary usage

José de Anchieta wrote in 1584 that the people worshipped no creature as God, except that they thought the thunder was God, while giving it no honor and ordinarily possessing neither idols nor divinatory lots. This closely parallels Thevet’s observation. The apparent contradiction—“think thunder is God” but do not worship it—warns against translating every early use as a European-style god.

Brazilian Jesuits nevertheless adopted **Tupã/Tupana** for the Christian God. The Christian translation is therefore already established in the sixteenth century, before Montoya’s Paraguayan publications.

### Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, 1639–1640

Montoya’s four major works—*Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639), *Conquista espiritual* (1639), *Arte* and *Vocabulario* (1640), and the associated catechetical corpus—are fundamental evidence for mission Guaraní. The original *Tesoro* comprises 407 folios. [Biblioteca Brasiliana record and scan](https://digital.bbm.usp.br/handle/bbm/4922)

Montoya used **Tupã** for the Christian God and generated or normalized compounds required by Catholic doctrine. In *Conquista espiritual*, he explains that the Guaraní knew Tupã as a feared, destructive or awe-inspiring power and that missionaries judged the name suitable because hearers already understood it as something great. He also speculated—within a colonial Christian framework—about Saint Thomas having preached in America. That speculation is not evidence about pre-contact Tupã.

Montoya’s translation produced a profound semantic expansion:

- a weather or thunder power became the lexical bearer of monotheistic omnipotence;
- *Tupã* entered statements about creation, incarnation, crucifixion, judgment, sacraments, and Trinity;
- derivatives such as *tupao* “church” and *Tupãsy* “Mother of God” entered Christian Guaraní;
- later speakers could receive Indigenous, Christian, and hybrid meanings simultaneously.

Bartomeu Melià accordingly calls words such as *Tupã*, *tupao*, *Tupãsy*, *angaipa*, and *pa’i* Guaraní words with transformed Christian meanings. [Melià on the “third language”](https://portalguarani.com/807_bartomeu_melia_lliteres__/22413_la_tercera_lengua_del_paraguay_2013__por_bartomeu_melia.html)

### Nimuendajú, 1914

Curt Nimuendajú’s *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* records Apapocúva/Ñandéva narratives and religious practice. It is the first major modern ethnographic corpus to challenge the missionary-created picture of Tupã as the single ancient Guaraní God.

Nimuendajú explicitly approached Tupã with caution because missionaries had spread the name for the Christian God through Brazil, Paraguay, Argentina, and Bolivia, including among peoples not closely related to Guaraní. His Apapocúva material instead places **Ñanderuvusú/Ñande Ru Vusu**, “Our Great Father,” at the center. Tupã is not the universal supreme creator of later schoolbook mythology. [Digital bibliographic record](https://www.etnolinguistica.org/biblio%3Animuendaju-1914-apapocuva)

### Cadogan, 1959

León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta: textos míticos de los Mbyá-Guaraní del Guairá*, published by the University of São Paulo in 1959, records sacred speech supplied principally by Mbyá religious specialists, including Pablo Verá. It is an oral-text corpus transcribed in Mbyá and translated into Spanish, not an ancient manuscript. [Publication record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ayvu_Rapyta.html?id=wLgPAQAAMAAJ)

The creation sequence centers on **Ñamandu Ru Ete/Ñande Ru Tenondé**, the first or true father, who unfolds himself and establishes the foundations of sacred speech. He brings forth other divine fathers and their female counterparts:

- Ñamandu Ru Ete and Ñamandu Chy Ete;
- Karai Ru Ete and Karai Chy Ete;
- Jakaira Ru Ete and Jakaira Chy Ete;
- Tupã Ru Ete and Tupã Chy Ete.

Tupã is therefore important but neither solitary nor first. Cadogan calls him the god or divine owner of waters, rains, thunder, lightning, and moderation or “refreshment.” He observed that Tupã’s relatively late place in this Mbyá theogony supports Nimuendajú’s criticism of missionary “abuse” of the name. [Cadogan text scan](https://pueblosoriginarios.com/textos/ayvu/ayvu/ayvu%20raptya.pdf)

Cadogan’s later *Ywyra Ñe’ery* and related studies add Mbyá prayers, divine names, ritual vocabulary, and testimony but do not reverse the basic result: Mbyá cosmology is plural and Tupã is not simply the Christian-style creator.

### Colmán, 1929

Narciso R. Colmán—“Rosicrán”—published *Ñande Ypy Kuéra (Nuestros antepasados)* in 1929. It is an **ethnogenetic and mythological poem**, not a verbatim Indigenous oral scripture. Its famous synthesis includes:

- Tupã as supreme creator;
- Arasy as his celestial wife;
- Rupavẽ and Sypavẽ as the first man and woman;
- Tau and Angatupyry as evil and good principles;
- Kerana and the seven monstrous offspring;
- an integrated national Guaraní genealogy.

Elements of the work draw on Guaraní words and folklore, but the integrated system is Colmán’s literary construction. The seven-monster family should consequently be labeled **modern literary systematization**, not securely pre-contact mythology. [Text and metadata](https://ro.scribd.com/document/455540335/Andeypykuranuestrosantepassados-Narcizor-Colmn-110118063918-Phpapp02-pdf)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains by stratum

**Early coastal attestation:** thunder, rain, the upper sky, fear or awe at storms.

**Missionary-Christian layer:** universal creation, providence, moral judgment, salvation, and every other attribute of the Christian God. These are functions carried by a translated name, not proof of an earlier Tupã theology.

**Mbyá layer:** waters, rain, thunder, lightning, storm, coolness, moderation, and renewal. Cadogan’s “refreshment” is significant: Tupã’s weather is not merely violent but restores or moderates the world.

**Colmán layer:** creation of cosmos and humanity, allocation of lands and natural resources, institution of moral opposites, blessing and punishment.

### Form

No securely pre-contact anthropomorphic form is known. Early observers report no cult statue of Toupan. Thunder itself, or the power manifested through thunder, is the most defensible early “form.”

Mbyá discourse personifies Tupã as **Ru Ete**, a true father dwelling in a divine *amba* or celestial domain, with a true mother counterpart. This does not establish a standard physical portrait.

Modern images showing a muscular Indigenous man with feather headdress, lightning, hammer, staff, or solar disk are artistic inventions. A Wikimedia drawing explicitly described as an “interpretation” dates only to 24 January 2026. [Modern drawing record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ATupadrawing.png)

The Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías and Paraguayan popular artists have likewise produced modern sculptures of Tupã. They document reception, not ancient iconography. [Modern museum sculpture](https://portalguarani.com/967_ramon_elias__/8882_tupa__escultura_del_museo_mitologico_ramon_elias.html)

### Symbols and associations

Secure or reasonably grounded:

- thunder;
- lightning;
- rain and storm clouds;
- water;
- the upper sky;
- in Mbyá religion, a celestial dwelling and divine lineage.

Late or unestablished:

- the Sun as Tupã’s exclusive residence: mainly Colmán-derived or popular synthesis;
- hammer or thunderbolt weapon: modern comparative iconography;
- fixed sacred animal, color, weekday, number, or temple emblem: **not established**;
- identification with a hummingbird: properly belongs in Cadogan’s text to the self-unfolding First Father, not securely to Tupã;
- ownership of all medicinal plants or invention of shamanism: popular modern accretion unless tied to a specific local testimony.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical systems

#### Mbyá system

Ñande Ru Tenondé/Ñamandu Ru Ete brings forth Tupã Ru Ete and Tupã Chy Ete among the divine fathers and mothers. Tupã is therefore generated or unfolded within the First Father’s ordered cosmos. His spiritual children transmit names or word-souls to humans affiliated with the Tupã lineage.

#### Christian system

As the translated Christian God, Tupã is uncreated and is identified with the Trinitarian God. *Tupãsy* means Mary as “Mother of God,” while Jesus is *Tupã Ra’y*, “Son of God.” These are mission-Christian relations, not Indigenous genealogies.

#### Colmán’s system

Tupã is Arasy’s husband and co-creator; he creates Rupavẽ and Sypavẽ and establishes Tau and Angatupyry. This is the source of much modern Paraguayan mythology.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Thunder above the Tupinambá

The earliest recoverable narrative is minimal: Toupan is above and causes rain and thunder. Thunder inspires fear; Europeans ask whether it is a god or the voice of God. Neither Thevet nor Anchieta found temples, images, formal sacrifice, or sustained worship devoted to it.

This is the early core. Elaborate stories in which Tupã personally rides clouds, hurls thunderbolts, fights demons, or rules a celestial court are not present in these reports.

### The Mbyá creation order

In *Ayvu Rapyta*, the primordial figure is Ñande Ru Tenondé/Ñamandu, who unfolds himself amid the original winds and establishes sacred speech, wisdom, love, and the foundations of the earth. He brings into being the other divine fathers and mothers. Tupã Ru Ete receives his place in this cosmos and participates in sustaining later creation, especially through waters and weather.

The narrative is neither monotheistic nor a Greek-style pantheon. Divine names may also name classes, lineages, modes of sacred agency, and sources of human word-souls. Calling Tupã merely “the thunder god” understates this Mbyá theological setting; calling him “the supreme creator” reverses its hierarchy.

### Colmán’s genesis of the Guaraní race

In *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, Tupã and Arasy descend to a chosen place, combine earth or clay with natural substances and water, form Rupavẽ and Sypavẽ, animate them, and assign them the world’s resources. Tupã speaks as an omnipresent creator, identifying himself with light, darkness, water, plants, thought, disease, health, construction, and destruction.

This rhetoric resembles Christian omnipotence, Romantic nature religion, and literary pantheism. It should not be backdated before 1929.

Tupã also leaves goodness and evil—Angatupyry and Tau—in the human world. Tau desires and abducts Kerana after conflict with Angatupyry. Their seven monstrous children become the familiar Paraguayan beings. Porãsy ultimately sacrifices herself in the struggle against them and is rewarded celestially by Tupã.

This cycle became enormously influential in Paraguayan schools, tourism, sculpture, comics, and internet mythology. Its cultural importance is real; its claimed antiquity is not demonstrated.

### Land Without Evil

The **Yvy marã e’ỹ**, “land without evil,” is central to Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva materials and later scholarship by Hélène Clastres. Tupã is not the exclusive author or ruler of this tradition. Migration, ritual song, prophetic leadership, lightness of the body, catastrophe, and access to an imperishable land belong to several Guaraní religious systems. Later popular retellings that make Tupã the monarchical gatekeeper of a single Guaraní heaven impose Christian and Colmán-derived structure.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult institutions

No securely documented pre-contact temple, priesthood, permanent sanctuary, idol, festival calendar, or sacrificial cult dedicated specifically to Tupã is known.

Early writers’ insistence that Toupan received no honor must be treated cautiously: Europeans often failed to recognize non-temple religious practice. Yet no contrary material record supports a large institutional Tupã cult.

Mbyá religious life is centered on the **opy**, sacred house; prayer-song, dance, tobacco smoke, ritual speech, dream and inspiration; the leadership of *opygua*, *karaí*, or other religious specialists; and the reception of sacred personal names. Tupã’s divine lineage participates in this living system, but the opy is not simply a “Temple of Tupã.”

### Offerings and prayer

Cadogan records prayers and hymnic material directed to or invoking divine fathers, including Tupã-related beings. These belong to Mbyá communal ritual, not to a reconstructed pan-Guaraní ancient liturgy.

The Jesuit reductions institutionalized prayer to **Tupã** as the Christian God through:

- Mass and the sacraments;
- catechism;
- confession and penance;
- Marian devotion to Tupãsy;
- church music and processions;
- Christian feast days and Sunday observance.

The surviving carved saints, Madonnas, churches, music manuscripts, and mission ruins are evidence for Christian Tupã-language, not images or temples of a pre-Christian thunder god. A seventeenth-century **Imaculada Tupãsy** sculpture, for example, represents the Immaculate Virgin under a Guaraní Christian title. [Museum documentation](https://sites.google.com/view/imagemepreservacao/escultura-texto/imaculada-tup%C3%A2nsy-museu-j%C3%BAlio-de-castilhos-rs)

### Sacrifice and violence

No source securely connects Tupã with human sacrifice. Tupinambá ritual execution and anthropophagy are amply debated in sixteenth-century sources, but those rites concerned warfare, vengeance, the prisoner, communal identity, and relations with enemies; they are not documented as sacrifices offered to Toupan. Calling them “human sacrifice to Tupã” is unsupported.

There is likewise no evidence for temple prostitution or a Tupã priestly economy.

The relevant violence is colonial:

- missionary suppression or recoding of Indigenous religious specialists;
- population concentration in reductions;
- epidemics, enslavement, military attack, and forced displacement;
- the replacement of Indigenous divine vocabulary by Christian semantic structures.

Mission settlements could also provide refuge against slave raiders and became sites of Guaraní political agency. The record cannot be reduced to either pure protection or pure cultural erasure.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1557

The comparative linguistic distribution makes a pre-contact association between ***tupã*** and thunder probable. Nothing securely reconstructs a universal supreme god, genealogy, anthropomorphic image, or institutional cult.

### 1557–1584: coastal attestations

Thevet, Léry, and Anchieta record Toupan/Tupana in relation to thunder, rain, fear, and an upper power. Their descriptions are already shaped by Christian questions about whether Indigenous people “have God,” idols, worship, or natural religion.

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: Christian translation

Jesuits selected Tupã/Tupana for God. The semantic choice was not wholly arbitrary—the word already carried atmospheric power and awe—but it was transformative. Christian predicates were attached to the name, while *Añá* was increasingly aligned with the Devil and *Tupãsy* with Mary.

### 1609–1767: reductions

Within the Paraguay mission system, Guaraní became a major written and liturgical language. Montoya’s works stabilized missionary vocabulary. Tupã was heard in sermons, catechisms, prayers, theater, hymns, confession, and sacramental instruction. The 1767 expulsion of the Jesuits ended their administration but not Christian Guaraní.

### Nineteenth century: national Romanticism

Brazilian Indianism transformed Tupã into the national-literary “God of the Indians.” Gonçalves Dias invokes Tupã repeatedly in *I-Juca-Pirama* (1851) and especially *Os Timbiras* (1857). The latter opens by calling ritual specialists “**Cultores de Tupã**” and gives Tupã providential, martial, dream-sending, and paternal functions. [Full *Os Timbiras* text](https://pt.wikisource.org/wiki/Anexo%3AImprimir/Os_Timbiras)

Dias’s Tupã is neither a neutral transcription of Tupinambá religion nor simply the Christian God. It is a Romantic national symbol constructed from missionary vocabulary, colonial ethnography, epic convention, and nineteenth-century nationalism.

### 1914–1959: ethnographic correction

Nimuendajú’s Apapocúva research and Cadogan’s Mbyá corpus demonstrated the inadequacy of the “Tupã was the one supreme god of all Guaraní” formula. Tupã appeared instead in locally specific divine orders.

### 1929 onward: Paraguayan mythographic synthesis

Colmán produced a coherent national mythology around Tupã, Arasy, the first couple, moral dualism, and the seven monsters. The poem circulated through folklorists, schoolbooks, museums, tourist routes, children’s literature, and digital encyclopedias. Once detached from its bibliographic origin, it was repeatedly presented as anonymous ancient belief.

### Present

Three major uses coexist:

1. **Christian Guaraní:** Tupã means God.
2. **Living Indigenous religion:** Mbyá and other communities preserve distinct theologies in which Tupã may be a divine father, collectivity, weather power, or lineage.
3. **National/popular mythology:** Tupã is the supreme creator of a standardized Paraguayan-Brazilian pantheon.

These uses are related historically but not interchangeable.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

Gonçalves Dias made Tupã a central emblem of Brazilian Romantic Indianism. In *Os Timbiras*, Tupã watches, sends dreams, permits glory, owns lightning, and stands as the divine patron of Indigenous peoples. This literary Tupã helped naturalize the idea of a single “Indian God.”

Colmán’s 1929 poem had still greater effects in Paraguay. Its characters and genealogies are now routinely reproduced without attribution.

### Folklore in Corrientes and Misiones

Juan Bautista Ambrosetti’s *Supersticiones y leyendas*—materials collected in the Alto Paraná, Corrientes, Misiones, southern Paraguay, and adjacent Brazil and published in collected form in 1917—documents a regional world of Pombero, Kurupí, animal transformations, ghosts, saints, devils, and Guaraní-Catholic language. [Ambrosetti bibliographic record](https://naturalis.fcnym.unlp.edu.ar/entities/publication/b3b6e64f-e44c-48c0-a516-57eb18a9e38a)

Berta Vidal de Battini’s later *Cuentos y leyendas populares de la Argentina* preserves more than 3,000 oral narratives collected over several decades. [INAPL description](https://inapl.cultura.gob.ar/noticia/mitos-y-leyendas/) In this regional folklore, **Tupá** frequently functions simply as “God,” while Pombero, Curupí, Lobizón and other beings carry independent narrative action. Their coexistence does not prove membership in one pre-contact pantheon.

Modern Corrientes tourist installations now combine Colmán’s monsters, vernacular tales, and Tupá as a benevolent creator. This is an active regional tradition but also a public, curated synthesis. [Corrientes mythological walk](https://nuevodesavisit.ciudaddecorrientes.gov.ar/quehacer/paseomitologicosangeronimo/)

### Neopaganism and occultism

Brazilian neopagan groups sometimes incorporate Indigenous names and entities into Wiccan, ecological, shamanic, or nationalist frameworks. Scholarship documents this general appropriation, but no evidence located establishes a large, continuous, separately organized modern “Tupã cult.” [Study of Brazilian neopaganism](https://trilhasdahistoria.ufms.br/index.php/RevTH/article/view/24141)

Claims that Tupã had a Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Renaissance Hermetic, grimoire, or medieval European occult tradition are unsupported. Online correspondences assigning Tupã planets, tarot cards, candles, colors, runes, or magical seals are modern inventions.

### Games and popular media

Internet pantheon lists commonly present Tupã as a Guaraní Zeus: an anthropomorphic thunder king married to a moon goddess and ruling a roster of nature gods. This combines Thevet’s thunder, Christian supremacy, Colmán’s Arasy narrative, Mbyá divine names, and modern fantasy conventions. It is a recognizable twenty-first-century retelling, not one recovered primary tradition.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Tupã originally “the Guaraní God”?

Three positions must be distinguished.

1. **Older missionary and Romantic position:** Tupã was already the Indigenous supreme being; missionaries merely translated correctly.
2. **Nimuendajú–Métraux–Cadogan critique:** missionaries inflated a comparatively secondary thunder being into the Christian God.
3. **More recent translation-centered position:** the choice rested on real Indigenous semantics, but translation recursively altered both Indigenous and Christian discourse. Tupã after missionization cannot be divided cleanly into a pure “original” and a wholly foreign overlay.

The second position remains a necessary correction to the supreme-god myth; the third best explains how Indigenous speakers could appropriate and transform the missionary term rather than merely receive it passively. Judith Shapiro’s “From Tupã to the Land Without Evil” is central to this discussion.

### Can Tupinambá and Guaraní evidence be combined?

Only cautiously. Tupinambá, Apapocúva/Ñandéva, Mbyá, Paĩ-Tavyterã, Kaiowá and Paraguayan Guaraní are related but historically distinct. Sixteenth-century Guanabara evidence cannot automatically fill gaps in twentieth-century Mbyá cosmology, and Mbyá theology cannot simply be projected backward onto Thevet’s Tupinambá.

### Is Colmán folklore or invention?

It is both literature and a formative source of later folklore. Colmán used living language and inherited motifs, but the integrated genealogy of Tupã, Arasy, Rupavẽ, Sypavẽ, Tau, Kerana and seven monsters is first securely documented in his authored poem. “Modern literary systematization” is more accurate than either “fraud” or “unaltered ancient myth.”

### State of the evidence

Surviving evidence is uneven:

- no pre-contact Tupã text or inscription;
- sixteenth-century travel and missionary books in European archives and modern facsimiles;
- seventeenth-century Guaraní dictionaries and catechisms;
- Jesuit-reduction architecture, sculpture, music and administrative records;
- twentieth-century field notebooks, recordings and publications;
- living oral testimony, some of it restricted or context-dependent.

The greatest historiographic error has been to take lexical continuity—one word recurring for five centuries—as proof of theological continuity.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Pre-contact | Proto-Tupi-Guaraní evidence supports an inherited word associated with thunder; no securely reconstructable universal Tupã cult. |
| **1557** | Thevet prints the earliest secure description of **Toupan**, above and responsible for rain and thunder. |
| **1557–1558 / 1578** | Léry observes the Tupinambá and later publishes his thunder-related discussion of Toupan. |

| **1914** | Nimuendajú publishes the Apapocúva creation-and-destruction corpus and criticizes missionary distortion of Tupã. |
| **1917** | Ambrosetti’s collected *Supersticiones y leyendas* documents Misiones–Corrientes–Paraguay regional folklore. |
| **1928** | Alfred Métraux publishes *La religion des Tupinamba*, synthesizing early colonial sources and arguing against a simple supreme-god reading. |
| **1929** | Colmán publishes *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, creating the influential integrated Tupã–Arasy–seven-monsters cycle. |
| **1959** | Cadogan publishes *Ayvu Rapyta*, locating Tupã Ru Ete within the plural Mbyá divine order. |
| **1970s–1990s** | Melià, Hélène Clastres, Pierre Clastres, Schaden, Susnik and others deepen linguistic, historical and political interpretation of Guaraní religion. |
| Late twentieth century | Colmán-derived mythology spreads through schools, folklore collections, monuments and tourist culture in Paraguay and northeastern Argentina. |
| Twenty-first century | Digital mythology sites, games and neopagan media increasingly portray Tupã as an anthropomorphic thunder king; Indigenous writers and researchers simultaneously reassert community-specific cosmologies. |
| **2026** | Newly circulated artistic depictions continue to demonstrate that Tupã’s standardized visual iconography is modern and still being created. |

## 11. Sources and Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not “Tupã was the ancient supreme thunder god of every Tupi-Guaraní people.” It is this:

1. An inherited Tupi-Guaraní term associated with thunder and storm-power existed before sustained missionary translation.
2. Sixteenth-century Tupinambá reports personified that power to varying degrees but found no formal cult recognizable to Europeans.
3. Missionaries chose the word for the Christian God, enormously enlarging its semantic field.
4. Distinct Guaraní communities retained or developed different Tupã beings; the Mbyá Tupã Ru Ete is an important divine father but not the primordial solitary creator.
5. Brazilian Romanticism made Tupã the providential national “God of the Indians.”
6. Colmán’s 1929 poem created the coherent supreme-creator genealogy now commonly mistaken for undifferentiated ancient Guaraní mythology.
7. Modern religious, folkloric and artistic uses are genuine parts of Tupã’s history, but they must be dated as modern reception rather than projected backward.
