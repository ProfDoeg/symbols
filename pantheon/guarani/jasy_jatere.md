# Jasy Jateré: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest secure description, a supernatural dwarf or forest being; in later Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine folklore, a *duende*, child-spirit, “lord of the siesta,” or frightening nursery figure. He is not securely attested as a pre-Columbian “god.” His status as the fourth of seven monstrous sons of Taú and Keraná belongs to Narciso R. Colmán’s twentieth-century literary systematization.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Juan Bautista Ambrosetti, “Materiales para el estudio del folk-lore misionero,” published in the *Revista del Jardín Zoológico de Buenos Ayres*, vol. I, in the early 1890s. Ambrosetti recorded the belief near the Itaquirí stream in the Tacurú-Pucú yerba district and reported it as widespread in Paraguay and Corrientes. His Yasy-Yateré is an invisible or intermittently visible blond dwarf with a straw hat and golden staff who whistles, abducts infants, licks and plays with them, and leaves them entangled in vines. The identification is explicit and considerably predates Colmán’s seven-monster genealogy.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

Jasy Jateré is a supernatural figure of Guaraní-speaking and bilingual rural folklore, especially in Paraguay and the Argentine provinces of Misiones and Corrientes. Its stable narrative complex comprises an arresting whistle, the forest or its cultivated margins, children who wander when adults are resting, abduction or enchantment, and some lasting disorder in the returned child. The conspicuous “small blond boy with a golden staff who comes at siesta” is not the form of the earliest record in every detail: Ambrosetti’s dwarf is blond, handsome, straw-hatted, staff-bearing and principally nocturnal in the encounter he reports, while twentieth-century Paraguayan tellings increasingly fix the being at the hot midday siesta.

Calling Jasy Jateré a “Guaraní god” is therefore too definite. The record supports, depending on narrator and period:

- **[Secure early folklore]** a mysterious whistling being associated with a bird-call, abduction and the forest;
- **[Regional folklore]** a dwarf, *duende*, homunculus, child-spirit or owner of the siesta;
- **[Learned systematization]** the fourth son of Taú and Keraná and one of seven “myths” or monsters;
- **[Modern popular taxonomy]** a member of “the Guaraní pantheon,” sometimes loosely called a god;
- **[Living belief]** an explanation or warning invoked in accounts of children lost in the bush, unexplained whistles and dangerous midday wandering.

### Names and spellings

Documented forms include:

- **Jasy Jateré / Jasy Jatere** — modern Paraguayan Guaraní orthography, with *j* representing the sound conventionally written *y* in older Spanish texts.
- **Yasy-Yateré / Yasy Yateré / Yasí Yateré** — common Argentine and older Hispanophone spellings.
- **Yaciyateré / Yaciyaterê** — literary Spanish spelling used by Horacio Quiroga and related editions.
- **Jasy Atere / Jasy Tere** — alternatives printed by Colmán.
- **Jaci Jaterê** — Portuguese/Brazilian normalization.
- **Jaty** rather than *jasy* occurs for “moon” in some Mbyá varieties, but a corresponding historical cult-name “Jaty Jateré” has not been established.

The first component is secure: *jasy* means “moon” and, by extension, lunar month. Antonio Ruiz de Montoya records the word in the 1639 *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* but does **not**, in the material located, record Jasy Jateré as a supernatural being. Montoya’s entry includes lunar phases and phenomena, demonstrating the antiquity of *jasy* as a word, not of the compound supernatural name.

The second component is disputed. Montoya’s *ateré* has been cited with senses including “piece of wood,” “small or stunted thing,” or something prematurely aged or diminished. Accordingly:

- **“Fragment/piece of the moon”** is Colmán’s explicit 1929/1937 gloss for *Jasy atere*. It is now the standard popular translation.
- **“Little/stunted moon-being”** is linguistically possible under the diminutive or stunted-object interpretation.
- **“Owner of the moon”** occasionally appears in modern literature but is not supported by the normal Guaraní owner construction *jára*.
- A literal myth in which a fragment of the moon becomes the being is a **modern explanatory expansion**, not something attested in Montoya or Ambrosetti.
- The entity’s blond or golden hair cannot safely be derived from this etymology: golden hair is already present in Ambrosetti, but no early informant is recorded explaining it as moonlight.

The accent is commonly written **Jateré** in Spanish. Standard Guaraní normally marks stress only where needed; variation in popular publication is extensive.

### Titles and descriptive labels

- **“Señor de las siestas” — “lord of the siestas”:** fixed prominently by Colmán, 1929/1937; widespread thereafter.
- **“Protector of the bees” and of *ka’a ruvicha*, the magical or sovereign herb:** Colmán.
- **“Protector of birds, wild fruits and forest animals”:** later folklore compilations and museum literature; related functions overlap with Pombero/Kuarahy Jára.
- **Kuarahy Jára, “Owner of the Sun”:** recorded by Ambrosetti as a name for Pombero in Corrientes, not securely as an original name of Jasy Jateré. Later conflations should not be projected backward.
- **“Cupid of the Guaraní” or fertility-bringer:** a late folkloristic interpretation, based principally on sexual-abduction variants. It is not an early cult title.

There is no ancient script tradition distinct from the Latin alphabet, no inscriptional corpus, and no securely documented interpretatio graeca or romana. Comparisons to European dwarfs, gnomes, fairies, Pan or Cupid are classificatory analogies, not historical identifications made by an indigenous cult.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial sources: what they do and do not establish

Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* and *Conquista espiritual*, both printed at Madrid in 1639, are indispensable witnesses to seventeenth-century mission Guaraní language and Jesuit activity. The *Tesoro* records *jasy* and *ateré* independently. Neither work, in the searchable and cited material examined, securely attests a being called Jasy Jateré, the golden staff, a blond child-dwarf, the siesta abduction, or the Taú–Keraná genealogy.

That negative finding matters. It does not prove that no cognate oral belief existed in the seventeenth century; missionary texts were selective and often recoded indigenous religious terminology. It does mean that statements such as “Montoya recorded the god Jasy Jateré in 1639” confuse lexical components with attestation of the named being.

Jesuit-reduction records likewise document campaigns against *paje*, non-Christian ritual authority and practices classified as superstition or idolatry, but no securely located reduction document names Jasy Jateré. Claims of a specific Jesuit suppression, demonization or Christian equation of Jasy Jateré therefore remain unestablished.

### Ambrosetti: earliest secure record

Ambrosetti’s “La leyenda del Yasy-Yateré” is the first secure, datable narrative witness located. He says that at a yerba-workers’ shed near the Itaquirí stream, women had been unable to sleep because they heard Yasy-Yateré whistling. Informants disagreed over the supposed bird’s size and plumage. The legend, he wrote, was widespread in Paraguay and Corrientes.

His account contains several distinct variants:

1. **[Secure early folklore]** The whistle belongs not to a bird but to a handsome blond dwarf in the forest, wearing a straw hat and carrying a golden staff.
2. He steals nursing infants, takes them into the forest, licks and plays with them, and abandons them wrapped in *ysypó*, vines.
3. On the anniversary of the abduction, the returned child suffers epileptic attacks.
4. **[Recorded variant]** He abducts children to teach them his own profession as an abductor.
5. **[Recorded sexual variant]** He takes attractive young women; a child born from such a union becomes another Yasy-Yateré.
6. Whoever takes his staff supposedly acquires his sexual or seductive powers.

Ambrosetti interpreted the belief through the racial and evolutionist assumptions of his era. He speculated that memories of children and women being abducted by Aché people—whom he called Guayaquí—generated the legend. His conjecture is not demonstrated by comparative evidence and should be treated as **nineteenth-century scholarly speculation**, not the tradition’s own explanation.

The account also complicates the now-standard “siesta spirit.” Ambrosetti’s field incident and concluding scene occur at night. His adjacent description of Pombero/Kuarahy Jára, by contrast, expressly places that being in the forest at siesta, guarding birds and taking boys who hunt them.

### Horacio Quiroga

Horacio Quiroga’s “El Yaciyateré,” composed from his Misiones experience and published in the early twentieth century, is a literary—not ethnographic—witness. Its narrator distinguishes southern opinion, where the yaciyateré is a strange nocturnal bird, from northern Misiones belief, where the call accompanies a dangerous supernatural presence. A child develops an extreme fever and deranged laughter while the call circles outside. Quiroga’s tale helped carry the name into national Argentine literature and consolidated the association between bird-call, childhood illness and the Misiones forest.

The story must not be read as a transcript of a single oral version. It is authored fiction using a locally recognized belief.

### Colmán’s *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*

Narciso R. Colmán, writing as Rosicrán, published the Guaraní poem *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* in 1929; his own Spanish version, *Nuestros antepasados*, followed in 1937. The work calls itself an ethnogenetic and mythological poem. Colmán constructed an expansive national prehistory incorporating oral motifs, local place-names, Christianity, classical mythology, speculative etymology and his own imagination.

Colmán explicitly gives the now-familiar schema:

- Jasy Jateré/Jasy Atere is the **fourth** child of Taú and Keraná.
- He is a small golden-haired man.
- He is lord of the siestas.
- His golden wand makes him invisible.
- He protects bees and *ka’a ruvicha*.
- He supplies honey to Teju Jagua.
- He causes people to lose their way and sends or brings them to the cannibal Ao-Ao.
- Together with his six siblings he is trapped in a cave through Porãsy’s stratagem, burned, purified and translated with them into the Pleiades.

This is the most important source for the organized seven-monster pantheon, but it is **learned literary systematization**, not a verbatim recovery of a unitary pre-contact canon. Colmán’s text itself includes conspicuous classical, biblical and nationalist scaffolding: Tupã and Arasy as a creator couple, an ethical opposition between Taú and Angatupyry, a flood, Atlantis-derived migrations, and direct comparison of a rain deity to Neptune. Modern educational texts frequently detach the seven beings from that authored framework and present them as an immemorial canonical Guaraní family.

### Cadogan, Nimuendajú and indigenous religious corpora

Curt Nimuendajú’s 1914 study of Apapocúva-Guaraní creation and destruction narratives and León Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959), based on Mbyá sacred discourse, are foundational sources for Guaraní cosmologies, speech-souls, divine figures and the Land Without Evil. Neither should be cited casually as proof of Colmán’s exact Taú–Keraná seven-child genealogy. Search results and modern popular articles often claim that *Ayvu Rapyta* contains or validates that pantheon; the accessible text and bibliographical evidence do not substantiate that attribution.

Cadogan did, however, publish **“Guasu-ja-ete. Tradiciones guaraníes”** in *Cultura* 4, no. 37 (October 1946), whose bibliographical description identifies it as an “Origin of the duende Jasy jatere.” This is a relevant indigenous-tradition witness distinct from *Ayvu Rapyta*. Its existence shows that Cadogan treated a Jasy-Jateré-related tradition, but it does not justify transferring the figure into every Mbyá creation cycle.

Nimuendajú, Cadogan, Alfred Métraux, Hélène and Pierre Clastres, Egon Schaden, Branislava Susnik and Bartomeu Melià supply essential comparative controls. Their work demonstrates that “Guaraní religion” is not one homogeneous pantheon: Apapocúva, Mbyá, Ñandeva/Chiripá, Kaiowá/Paĩ Tavyterã, Ava and Paraguayan mestizo traditions preserve different theological vocabularies and narrative structures. Jasy Jateré’s strongest documentation is in regional folklore rather than the central sacred creation recitations recorded in *Ayvu Rapyta*.

### Argentine folklore collections

Susana Chertudi and Berta Vidal de Battini’s collections of Argentine folktales and legends place the being in a wider northeastern Argentine oral field. Spellings such as Yasy Yateré and Yasí Yateré predominate. These records preserve local variation—bird or invisible whistler, child-sized being, reversed feet in some recent tellings, nighttime versus siesta activity—and should not be standardized automatically to the Paraguayan museum image.

## 3. Domain, attributes and iconography

### Core attributes by evidentiary layer

**[Secure early folklore: Ambrosetti]**

- Forest presence.
- Whistle identified by some as a bird-call.
- Invisible or elusive manifestation.
- Handsome blond dwarf.
- Straw hat.
- Golden staff.
- Abduction of nursing infants.
- Licking, playing and entanglement in vines.
- Anniversary convulsions or epilepsy.
- Occasional sexual abduction of young women.

**[Early-twentieth-century literary folklore: Quiroga]**

- Nocturnal call.
- Regional uncertainty over whether it is a bird or supernatural being.
- Association with a child’s fever and delirium.
- Misiones forest setting.

**[Learned systematization: Colmán]**

- Fourth son of Taú and Keraná.
- Lord of the siesta.
- Golden hair.
- Golden wand causing invisibility.
- Protector of bees and magical vegetation.
- Misleader of people.
- Auxiliary of Ao-Ao.
- Participant in the destruction and astral transformation of the seven siblings.

**[Later Paraguayan popular folklore and museum presentation]**

- Beautiful naked boy or child-sized *duende*.
- Long blond or golden curls, often blue eyes.
- Golden staff containing all his power.
- A whistle sounding “Jasy Jatere… tere…,” sometimes paradoxically loud when distant and faint when near.
- Midday appearance, especially in summer or during *avatiky*, the season of tender maize.
- Attraction of children with whistles, fruit, tender maize and wild honey.
- Games with abducted children in forest clearings.
- Riding deer, tapirs or jaguars and swinging on vines in elaborated versions.
- A kiss or licking that leaves a child mute, stunned, deaf, feeble-minded or unable to remember.
- Loss of magical power if deprived of the staff; he then becomes a weeping, helpless child.
- Occasional bird metamorphosis, especially into a *chochĩ*-type bird.

The magical golden staff is present already in Ambrosetti, so it is not Colmán’s invention. The staff’s sharply defined functions—hypnosis, invisibility, animation of eggs, or containing all supernatural power—are elaborated differently in later sources.

### Honey and forest

Honey joins several layers of the tradition. Later oral-literary tellings make it one of the treats offered to abducted children. Colmán makes Jasy Jateré protector of bees and honey-provider to Teju Jagua. The Ramón Elías Museum tradition expands the motif into playful visits to bees’ nests with children. These are related but separate attestations; one should not assume that the earliest abduction tale already contained the honey feast.

The landscape is neither an abstract wilderness nor a temple precinct. It is the inhabited forest edge: yerba camps, small farms, maize fields, paths, vines, fruiting trees and the boundary beyond adult supervision. The temporal boundary—the adults’ midday rest—became as important as the spatial one.

### Iconography

No pre-Columbian, colonial or nineteenth-century artifact has been securely identified as Jasy Jateré. The tradition’s iconography is modern and largely illustrative.

The canonical visual type is a naked or minimally clothed blond boy holding a gold-colored staff. It derives from folklore descriptions and Colmán-era systematization, not archaeological attribution. Ramón Elías’s sculptural series, displayed at the Museo Mitológico Ramón Elías at Capiatá, is a major modern visualization. The museum opened in 1979 and includes Jasy Jateré among modeled Paraguayan supernatural beings. Later painters, textbook illustrators and craft carvers have repeated the pale-haired child form.

Blue eyes, reversed feet and a pronounced phallus occur in some modern representations or conflated descriptions. None is universal. The phallic trait belongs more characteristically to Kurupí; transferring it to Jasy Jateré may reflect overlap among sexual-abduction traditions.

No sacred color, number, weekday, celestial cult calendar, sacrificial animal or historically attested emblem beyond the staff has been securely established. The number four is genealogical only in Colmán’s ordering, while the recurrent seven belongs to his entire literary system.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogical versions

**Independent folklore version:** Ambrosetti supplies no parents, siblings or place in a pantheon. His sexual variant makes children born from abducted women into further Yasy-Yateré beings, implying reproduction by repetition rather than descent from Taú and Keraná.

**Colmán’s version:** Taú, the malevolent spirit, abducts Keraná after overcoming Angatupyry with Pytajovái’s aid. Arasy curses the couple, and Keraná produces seven monstrous children at seven-month intervals: Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu’ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jateré, Kurupí, Ao-Ao and Luisón. Jasy Jateré is fourth.

This family is now dominant in Paraguayan school and popular culture because Colmán’s work supplied a memorable ordered national mythology. It should be cited to Colmán, not retroactively labeled the uniform religion of all pre-contact Guaraní peoples.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or supernatural being

- **Taú — parentage:** Jasy Jateré is Taú’s fourth child by Keraná. *Ñande Ypy Kuéra*, 1929; Spanish version 1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Keraná — parentage:** Keraná bears him as the fourth of seven cursed children at a seven-month interval. Colmán, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Arasy — curse affecting his birth:** Arasy curses Taú’s union with Keraná; the curse produces the monstrous offspring. Colmán, 1929/1937. Jasy does not converse with her individually. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Teju Jagua — provisioning:** Jasy Jateré supplies his disabled elder brother with bee honey. Colmán, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Mbói Tu’ĩ — joint appearance:** They assemble with the other siblings around Porãsy and are trapped and burned together. Colmán, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Moñái — joint conspiracy and death:** Moñái summons Jasy Jateré and the other brothers to meet Porãsy; all participate in the cave gathering and die in the fire. Colmán, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Kurupí — joint appearance:** Present together at Porãsy’s cave gathering and destruction. No separate dialogue or contest between them is recorded. Colmán, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Ao-Ao — delivery of victims:** Jasy Jateré misleads people so that Ao-Ao may consume them. Colmán’s note 46, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Luisón/Huicho — joint appearance:** Present together at the cave gathering, death and transformation. No independent encounter is narrated. Colmán, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Porãsy — adoration, entrapment and shared death:** Jasy Jateré and his brothers gather around and “adore” the decorated Porãsy; her ruse enables Tumê’s people to seal and burn the cave. Colmán, 1929/1937. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Tumê Arandú — destruction:** Tumê plans the deception, seals the cave and orders the fire in which Jasy Jateré and his siblings die. Colmán, 1929/1937. There is no one-to-one combat. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Tupã — posthumous cosmic order:** The brothers’ spirits, purified by fire, rise and become the Pleiades within Colmán’s Tupã-governed cosmos. Direct individual action by Tupã upon Jasy Jateré is not narrated. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Pombero/Kuarahy Jára — comparative relation only:** Ambrosetti calls the Corrientes Pombero/Kuarahy Jára a modification of Yasy-Yateré. They do not meet in a story. **[Early scholarly comparison, 1890s]**
- **Saci-Pererê — comparative/syncretistic relation only:** Brazilian folklorists and later writers compare or historically connect Saci with Guaraní Jasy Jateré-like beings. No primary narrative records an encounter. **[Modern comparative reconstruction]**

No securely attested interaction with Ñamandú, the Mbyá creator figures of *Ayvu Rapyta*, the Apapocúva divine twins, or the Tupinambá divine figures studied by Métraux has been located. Shared membership in a broad “Tupí-Guaraní mythology” is not an interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The whistling abductor

The earliest reconstructable cycle begins with an ambiguous sound. A bird seems to whistle the name *Yasy-Yateré*, but informants cannot agree on the bird’s appearance. The supernatural explanation is a blond forest dwarf with a hat and gold staff. He steals a baby, carries it into the forest, licks or plays with it, and leaves it tangled in vines. The child may suffer recurring seizures.

This is not a birth-to-death divine epic. It is an encounter legend: its force lies in claimed proximity and repetition. The being may be invisible, while the sound and the missing child function as evidence within the story.

Variants alter motive:

- affection and play;
- initiation of the child into abduction;
- sexual seizure of young women;
- creation of further Yasy-Yateré offspring;
- delivery of victims to Ao-Ao;
- benign entertainment followed by an accidentally destructive kiss;
- predatory enchantment ending in illness or lasting disability.

These versions should not be harmonized into the idea that he “always means well” or “always wants to kill.” The moral character is unstable.

### The siesta warning

In twentieth-century Paraguayan and Corrientes folklore, the legend becomes strongly synchronized with the hot midday interval. A child who refuses to sleep or leaves the house hears a repetitive whistle, follows a bird or lovely boy into fields or forest, eats honey or fruit, plays and becomes lost. Adults later find the child mute, dazed or caught in vines.

The warning “do not go out at siesta or Jasy Jateré will take you” is socially functional: it keeps children away from heat, snakes, water, forest and agricultural hazards while adults rest. That practical explanation is plausible and commonly offered, but it is **modern functional interpretation**, not necessarily the narrators’ own exhaustive meaning.

The siesta feature may have grown partly through interchange with Pombero/Kuarahy Jára. Ambrosetti explicitly assigns the midday bird-guardian role to Pombero immediately after narrating nocturnal Yasy-Yateré accounts.

### Colmán’s seven-monster cycle

Colmán embeds Jasy Jateré in a continuous national cosmogony:

1. Tupã and Arasy create the world and first people.
2. Taú and Angatupyry represent opposing moral forces.
3. Taú desires and abducts Keraná.
4. Arasy curses the union.
5. Seven monstrous children are born; Jasy Jateré is fourth.
6. Their depredations destabilize the community.
7. Tumê Arandú devises a plan using his sister Porãsy.
8. Porãsy persuades Moñái to assemble all his brothers.
9. They gather in a cave, drink chicha and surround Porãsy.
10. The plot is discovered; Porãsy remains inside while the entrance is sealed.
11. Tumê’s people burn the cave.
12. Porãsy becomes the morning star; the purified brothers become the Pleiades.

This narrative is internally canonical to Colmán’s poem and profoundly influential, but it cannot be treated as the earliest recoverable Jasy Jateré myth. Ambrosetti’s independent being predates it by decades and lacks the genealogy.

### Bird, child or dwarf

The “bird” is not simply a disguise in all versions. Ambrosetti records genuine uncertainty: people associate a recognizable call with a poorly agreed bird, while the legend denies that a bird is responsible. Quiroga preserves a north/south disagreement. Later versions solve the ambiguity by making Jasy Jateré transform into, command or imitate the bird. That is a narrative resolution of older uncertainty, not necessarily the original form.

## 6. Cult, temples and ritual practice

No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival calendar, sacrificial institution, inscription, votive deposit or theophoric personal-name tradition dedicated to Jasy Jateré has been securely documented.

Consequently, “cult” is largely the wrong institutional category. The evidence concerns:

- oral warning and storytelling;
- interpretation of whistles and disappearances;
- household supervision of children;
- reported encounters;
- literary publication;
- visual representation;
- heritage and educational display.

No documentary evidence connects Jasy Jateré with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, organized cult violence or a political priesthood. Colmán’s Porãsy episode contains voluntary self-sacrifice and collective burning within an authored myth; it is not evidence that historical Guaraní communities performed a ritual human sacrifice to or for Jasy Jateré.

Claims of offerings specifically made to Jasy Jateré are poorly documented. Offerings of tobacco, honey or liquor are much more securely associated in Paraguayan folklore with Pombero. Their transfer to Jasy Jateré in undated internet lists should be treated as conflation unless accompanied by locality, informant and collection date.

Traditional healers, parents or community authorities may be called upon after a child’s frightening disappearance or illness, but a standardized Jasy Jateré exorcism has not been established from the principal early sources. Modern claims that a Mbyá *opygua* treats Jasy-Jateré enchantment with a fixed protocol require better ethnographic citation.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1639: possible antecedents, no direct attestation

The compound being cannot presently be documented before European contact. Widespread Guaraní traditions of forest owners, dangerous nonhuman persons, animal calls, wandering souls and child vulnerability offer a plausible indigenous conceptual environment. That is comparative reconstruction, not proof that the fully formed blond siesta-child existed pre-contact.

### 1639–eighteenth century: lexical and colonial context

Montoya securely records the language from which the name is built. Jesuit records document indigenous ritual worlds and their attempted Christian transformation, but the named figure remains absent in the materials located. The blond appearance, straw hat, fixed siesta and golden staff therefore cannot be assigned securely to this period.

### Nineteenth century: first documentation

Ambrosetti’s record demonstrates a mature legend circulating across Misiones, Paraguay and Corrientes. It already includes blond dwarf, hat, staff, whistle, invisibility, abduction, vines, illness and sexual variants. This wide distribution makes a purely individual literary invention impossible; the tradition existed orally before publication.

Ambrosetti’s juxtaposition with Pombero reveals active overlap among regional forest beings. His attempt to derive the story from Aché raids reflects contemporary settler prejudice and is not accepted as established origin.

### 1900–1928: literary regionalization

Quiroga uses the yaciyateré in Misiones fiction, preserving the haunting bird-call and child illness while moving the being into modern Latin American literary culture. Darío Gómez Serrato reportedly titled a 1919 Guaraní poetry collection *Jasy Jatere*, evidence that the name was already usable as a Paraguayan literary-cultural emblem.

### 1929–1937: national mythological system

Colmán gives the figure a genealogy, ordinal position, domain, helpers and cosmic death. This marks the decisive shift from locally variable legend to textbook pantheon. Colmán did not invent the underlying being, which Ambrosetti had already documented, but he systematized and substantially expanded its relations.

### 1940s–1960s: ethnography and folklore scholarship

Cadogan published a 1946 tradition concerning the origin of the *duende* Jasy Jateré. His 1959 *Ayvu Rapyta* transformed the study of Mbyá sacred discourse but should not be conflated with Colmán’s pantheon. Nimuendajú, Schaden, Susnik and others increasingly showed the diversity of Guaraní peoples and the inadequacy of a single composite “Guaraní mythology.”

Nicolás Yampey’s 1969 “Análisis de dos Mitos Sudamericanos: Kurupí y Yasy-yateré” represents a psychoanalytic/comparative phase of scholarship. Such readings illuminate twentieth-century scholarly interests but do not replace source-critical history.

### Late twentieth century: museum and school canon

Folklore dictionaries, children’s books and school materials standardized the blond, naked child with staff and the seven-sibling genealogy. Ramón Elías’s Capiatá museum, inaugurated in 1979, made these beings materially visible through sculpture. The museum form is culturally important but modern.

Argentine educational material likewise presents Yasy Yateré as the being “heard but not seen,” with a gold staff and sometimes reversed feet, who takes children who evade siesta.

### Twenty-first century: living folklore and cultural heritage

The being remains widely recognizable in Paraguay and northeastern Argentina. It appears in local journalism, schools, tourism, painting, carving, bilingual storybooks and online horror narration. Contemporary first-person accounts of whistles continue the old encounter-legend form.

Paraguay’s heritage law protects oral traditions and collective memory in general, but no separate national legal declaration specifically consecrating Jasy Jateré was located. Cultural recognition must not be overstated as statutory individual designation.

There is no reliable evidence of a large, organized modern Jasy Jateré religion or reconstructionist cult. Contemporary reverence for Guaraní cosmology, indigenous cultural revitalization and artistic celebration are real, but they do not by themselves constitute continuous worship of this being.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature

Quiroga’s “El Yaciyateré” is the most influential literary transformation outside Paraguay. Rather than supplying the later seven-monster biography, it preserves uncertainty and dread: bird versus being, southern versus northern account, empirical narrator versus embodied fear.

Colmán’s greater transformation is structural. He turned an already circulating figure into one member of an ordered supernatural family and supplied relationships that now pass as anonymous antiquity.

Later children’s literature commonly softens the being into a lonely playmate whose dangerous kiss or departure harms the child unintentionally. Horror retellings emphasize predation, sexual violence or delivery to Ao-Ao. Both select genuine earlier motifs, but neither is the single canonical version.

### Visual culture and museums

The Ramón Elías sculpture and later Paraguayan illustrations established a recognizable national icon: pale or gold-haired, naked, beautiful and carrying a short gold staff. The image combines early verbal traits with modern figurative art. It is not based on an ancient image.

### Music and performance

Darío Gómez Serrato’s poetry and Mauricio Cardozo Ocampo’s song “Yasy Yateré” brought the being into modern Guaraní and Paraguayan musical culture. School plays, dance and folk pageants often present the seven monsters together, thereby reproducing Colmán’s system rather than the looser nineteenth-century field.

### Comics, web fiction and popular media

Contemporary webcomics and fantasy retellings reimagine Jasy as an attractive supernatural youth, demigod or romantic figure. These works belong to modern fantasy reception. Romantic kinship plots, fixed divine ranks and cosmological powers not documented before Colmán should be labeled adaptations.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated searches located no evidence that Jasy Jateré held a documented place in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, established grimoire traditions or early modern occultism. Internet pages occasionally classify him as an elemental, fertility spirit or “Guaraní Cupid,” but these are recent comparative labels.

Nor was a historically traceable organized neopagan denomination centered on Jasy Jateré located. Personal invocation or eclectic practice may exist, but its extent and lineage cannot be established from public documentation.

### Politics and identity

The figure is used as an emblem of Paraguayan and Guaraní regional identity, particularly in school and heritage settings. This nationalization can conceal two facts: the tradition is transborder, extending through Corrientes and Misiones, and “Guaraní” designates multiple peoples whose religious corpora cannot be reduced to Colmán’s national poem.

No sustained extremist appropriation specific to Jasy Jateré was located.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is the being pre-Columbian?

The safest conclusion is layered:

- The name is Guaraní, and indigenous forest-being concepts are older than the surviving record.
- The complete familiar portrait is first securely documented only in the late nineteenth century.
- Montoya proves the age of component words, not the entity.
- The blond hair, straw hat and gold staff may reflect colonial or postcolonial cultural interchange, but no source proves a European origin for them.
- The seven-monster genealogy is securely twentieth-century in its extant literary form.

Thus “pre-Columbian god, unchanged for centuries” is unproved; “pure modern invention by Colmán” is also false because Ambrosetti predates him.

### Jasy Jateré and Pombero

Ambrosetti explicitly regarded the Corrientes Pombero/Kuarahy Jára as a modification of Yasy-Yateré. His Pombero is tall and thin, wears a very large straw hat, carries a cane, guards birds and abducts boys hunting during siesta. The overlap is substantial.

Yet distinctions remain:

- Ambrosetti’s Yasy-Yateré is a blond dwarf and infant abductor associated with a nocturnal whistle.
- His Pombero is tall, a bird protector and explicitly active at siesta.
- Later Paraguayan tradition usually makes Pombero dark, hairy, nocturnal and receptive to offerings, while Jasy Jateré becomes a golden-haired child of midday.

The likely history is reciprocal differentiation and borrowing, not timeless identity or complete separation.

### The etymology

“Fragment of the moon” is culturally dominant and explicitly used by Colmán. Montoya’s lexical evidence makes a compound of *jasy* and *ateré* possible, but the exact morphology and semantic development are not settled. Translations such as “moon owner,” or elaborate lunar-phase schemes assigning each of the seven siblings a phase, lack early documentation and are best treated as modern speculation.

### Cadogan misattribution

A recurrent modern claim says that Cadogan recorded the seven children of Taú and Keraná in *Ayvu Rapyta*. This conflates distinct corpora. Cadogan did publish on a Jasy-Jateré origin tradition in 1946, but *Ayvu Rapyta* is principally a corpus of Mbyá sacred texts and does not thereby authenticate Colmán’s exact national pantheon. The distinction is central to responsible use of indigenous sources.

### Indigenous religion versus Paraguayan folklore

The phrase “Guaraní mythology” often collapses Mbyá sacred speech, Apapocúva theology, colonial mission vocabulary, Paraguayan bilingual peasant folklore, Argentine regional legend and authored nationalist poetry. Jasy Jateré’s documentary history cuts across these fields, but its densest evidence lies in the last three. Scholars should specify community, collector, language, date and genre.

### Lost and surviving evidence

The tradition is mainly oral; individual performances and local variants were inevitably lost before systematic recording. Surviving evidence is dispersed among periodicals, folklore collections, literary editions, Cadogan’s papers and later recordings. Ambrosetti’s printed article and Colmán’s bilingual literary corpus are accessible digitally. Museum sculpture preserves twentieth-century visualization rather than ancient cult art.

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Principal primary and near-primary sources

- **Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, *Tesoro de la lengua guaraní* (1639):** primary linguistic evidence for the name’s components, not secure evidence of the being.
- **Juan Bautista Ambrosetti, “Materiales para el estudio del folk-lore misionero” (early 1890s):** earliest secure descriptive record and indispensable evidence for variation before Colmán.
- **Horacio Quiroga, “El Yaciyateré” (early twentieth century):** literary reception grounded in Misiones belief.
- **Narciso R. Colmán, *Ñande Ypy Kuéra* (1929; Spanish version 1937):** primary source for the ordered seven-monster genealogy and Jasy Jateré’s “fourth son” identity.
- **León Cadogan, “Guasu-ja-ete. Tradiciones guaraníes” (1946):** ethnographic tradition concerning the being’s origin; distinct from *Ayvu Rapyta*.
- **Cadogan, *Ayvu Rapyta* (1959):** comparative Mbyá sacred corpus and a control against homogenizing all Guaraní traditions.
- **Nimuendajú, *Die Sagen von der Erschaffung und Vernichtung der Welt als Grundlagen der Religion der Apapocúva-Guaraní* (1914):** comparative Apapocúva religious corpus; not direct evidence for Colmán’s genealogy.
- **Ramón Elías Museum materials:** primary evidence for modern iconographic and heritage canonization.

### Bottom-line reconstruction

The fullest defensible history begins not with an ancient temple or scripture but with a nineteenth-century encounter legend already circulating across a transborder Guaraní-speaking region. That legend knew a whistling, elusive blond dwarf with a golden staff who abducted children and caused illness. The now-defining midday office was sharpened through regional storytelling and possible interchange with Pombero. Colmán then incorporated the being into an authored national cosmogony as the fourth child of Taú and Keraná, protector of bees and magical plants, helper of Teju Jagua and Ao-Ao, and one of seven beings burned and transformed into the Pleiades. Schools, museums, music and illustration made that literary synthesis look timeless.

The evidence therefore supports both antiquity of oral roots and substantial historical change. What cannot presently be established is a pre-Columbian cult, an ancient seven-monster canon, temples or sacrifices, continuous worship, or the attribution of Colmán’s genealogy to Cadogan’s *Ayvu Rapyta*.
