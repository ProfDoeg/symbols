# Sulis (Sulis Minerva): Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess; more precisely, a locally rooted Romano-British goddess of the thermal spring at Bath, worshipped independently as *Sulis* and syncretistically as *Sulis Minerva*. She is not securely attested as a member of a unified “Irish, Welsh, Gaulish and British pantheon.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** The sanctuary and probable cult statue belong archaeologically to the later first or early second century CE, but these remains do not preserve Sulis’s name. The earliest closely datable written attestation is **RIB 143**, an altar erected at Bath after CE 122 to *dea Sulis* by Aufidius Eutuches for the welfare of Marcus Aufidius Maximus, centurion of Legio VI Victrix. Other undated Roman-period Bath inscriptions might be earlier, but cannot securely be made so.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[EA]** securely attested ancient evidence; **[RV]** regional variant; **[SI]** syncretistic identification; **[MA]** medieval or early-modern accretion; **[F]** folklore; **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction; **[MI]** modern invention or modern devotional elaboration; **[U]** unestablished.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[EA]** Sulis is known overwhelmingly from **Aquae Sulis**, modern Bath in Somerset. The defensible minimum is that she was the female divine owner or guardian of its natural thermal spring. Roman-period worshippers addressed her for personal welfare, recovery of stolen property, punishment of offenders, and enforcement of oaths. Her sphere therefore included the spring, bodily well-being, protection, divine retribution, and quasi-judicial discovery of wrongdoing.

**[MSR]** “Healing goddess” is probable but should not be overstated. The hot water, bathing installation, prayers *pro salute et incolumitate* (“for welfare and safety”), and comparison with healing sanctuaries support the classification. No surviving ancient text, however, supplies a theology of Sulis as healer, narrates a cure by her, or calls her *medica*. Likewise, “mother goddess,” “sun goddess,” “wisdom goddess,” “war goddess,” and “fertility goddess” go beyond the direct evidence unless carefully qualified.

### Attested forms

- **Sulis** — Latin-script nominative, securely preserved on a Bath tablet as *dea Sulis*, “the goddess Sulis.” This discovery settled the older uncertainty over whether the nominative was *Sul*, *Sulis*, or another form. **[EA]**
- **Suli** — dative, “to/for Sulis,” the common form in Bath dedications: *deae Suli*. **[EA]**
- **Sulis** — genitive, “of Sulis,” in *sacerdos deae Sulis*, “priest of the goddess Sulis,” and in the place-name *Aquae Sulis*, “the waters of Sulis.” **[EA]**
- **Sul** — traditional modern headword and a possible reading of the Alzey inscription, but not now the preferred nominative for the Bath goddess. **[MSR/U]**
- **Sulis Minerva / Suli Minervae** — double divine name in monumental inscriptions, portable vessels, and tablets. The order normally places the local name first. **[SI]**
- **Minerva Sulis** — the reversed order occurs exceptionally in the Bath tablets; *Tabellae Sulis* 65 is the principal example. **[SI/RV]**
- **Aquae Sulis; Aquis Sulis** — “the waters/at the waters of Sulis,” the Roman place-name preserved in the Antonine Itinerary. Ptolemy’s Greek *Ὕδατα Θερμά*, “Hot Waters,” describes the place without naming Sulis. **[EA]**

The Alzey altar, CIL XIII 6266, was found in 1872 at ancient *Altiaia* in Upper Germany. Its damaged first line reads **DEA SVL**. The Celtic Divine Names database restores *Dea(e) Sul(i)*, “to the goddess Sulis,” but records the competing expansion *Dea(bus) Sul(eviis)*, “to the goddesses Suleviae.” It is therefore a probable, not completely uncontroversial, extra-British attestation. **[RV/U]**

### Titles and forms of address

The curse tablets supply titles but no elaborate litany:

- *dea Sulis* — “goddess Sulis.” **[EA]**
- *dea Sulis Minerva* — “the goddess Sulis Minerva.” **[SI]**
- *domina* — “Lady/Mistress,” *Tab. Sulis* 98. **[EA]**
- *sanctissima* — “most holy,” *Tab. Sulis* 10. **[EA]**
- *numen* — her divine will or power, tablets 10, 32, and 34. **[EA]**
- *maiestas* — “majesty,” tablets 32 and 35. **[EA]**
- *praesul* — “guardian/presiding power,” used by Solinus not as a cult title written beside her name but in saying that Minerva’s divine power presides over the hot springs. **[EA]**

Modern titles such as “Lady of the Sacred Spring,” “Goddess of the Gap,” “Eye of the Sun,” “Keeper of the Flame,” or “Goddess of Boiling Truth” are intelligible devotional poetry but are not ancient epithets. **[MI]**

### Etymology

No etymology is universally established.

1. **Sight/eye.** Delamarre connects the theonym with Celtic *sūli-* and Old Irish *súil*, “eye, sight.” Pierre-Yves Lambert instead proposed *su-wli-*, approximately “good-seeing” or “she who sees well,” from *su-* plus a root *wel-*, “see.” This family of explanations fits later associations with sight only at the level of word history; the Bath inscriptions never call Sulis an eye goddess. **[MSR]**

2. **Sun.** A connection with the Indo-European solar word represented by Greek *hēlios*, Latin *sol*, Sanskrit *sūrya-* and Celtic reflexes has repeatedly led to “sun goddess” reconstructions. The linguistic path is disputed: the Brittonic words for sun, including Old Welsh *heul* and Old Breton *houl*, do not straightforwardly reproduce the form *Sulis*. Matasović has argued that Old Irish *súil* may ultimately reflect the inherited solar word through the widespread “sun as eye of the sky” metaphor. Even if the names are cognate, etymology alone does not demonstrate an ancient solar cult at Bath. **[MSR/U]**

3. **Beneficial flow.** A proposed reconstruction *Su-lījīs*, “good flooding one” or “beneficial flow,” has circulated in reference works and online discussions. It would suit a spring goddess semantically, but it has not displaced the “sight/eye” analysis in current linguistic reference literature. **[MSR/U]**

### Sulis and the Suleviae

The **Suleviae** were plural goddesses attested at Bath and on the Continent. RIB 151 at Bath was dedicated to the *Suleviae* by Sulinus, a sculptor and son of Brucetus. Similar names occur in Gaul and the Rhineland.

**[U]** Linguistic resemblance does not establish that the Suleviae were “three forms of Sulis,” her sisters, daughters, attendants, or continental manifestations. No inscription gives any such relationship. The uncertain Alzey reading is especially important: if the altar names plural Suleviae rather than singular Sulis, it ceases to be evidence that Sulis’s own cult travelled to Germany.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Pre-Roman evidence

**[EA]** Eighteen Late Iron Age coins were recovered from low levels in the spring reservoir. Their unworn state has been used to argue for pre-conquest deposition. This makes pre-Roman reverence for the spring plausible.

**[U]** No pre-Roman object from Bath names Sulis. Roman reconstruction disturbed or removed much of the earlier spring context. Consequently, “Sulis was certainly worshipped here by the Dobunni before CE 43” is a reasonable hypothesis, not a documented fact. The name of the goddess, her functions, and her supposed pre-Roman iconography remain unknown before Roman rule.

### Monumental epigraphy

The main dossier is RIB 141–155 and associated small finds:

- **RIB 143, after CE 122:** *Deae Suli pro salute et incolumitate Marci Aufidi Maximi…* Aufidius Eutuches, a freedman, fulfilled a vow to Sulis for the welfare and safety of his patron, a centurion of Legio VI Victrix. This is the earliest narrowly datable named evidence. **[EA]**
- **RIB 144, Roman period:** altar to Sulis by Lucius Marcius Memor, apparently a *haruspex*, who restored or rededicated something after a divine vision or command. Its interpretation supplies evidence for professional divination at the sanctuary but not a narrative revelation by Sulis in surviving words. **[EA]**
- **RIB 146, probably CE 161–169 or a later joint reign:** *Deae Suli Minervae et Numinibus Augustorum*. A centurion of Legio II Augusta fulfilled a vow to Sulis Minerva and the numina of two emperors for himself and his family. This explicitly embeds the cult in imperial religion. **[EA/SI]**
- **RIB 147–149:** further dedications to Sulis, demonstrating repeated individual votive practice but generally lacking precise dates. **[EA]**
- **RIB 150:** *Deae Suli Minervae Sulinus Maturi filius…* Sulinus son of Maturus fulfilled his vow to Sulis Minerva. **[EA/SI]**
- **RIB 155:** funerary inscription of Gaius Calpurnius Receptus, *sacerdos deae Sulis*, “priest of the goddess Sulis,” who died aged seventy-five. His freedwoman and wife Calpurnia Trifosa erected it. This is the direct evidence for an institutional priesthood; it does not reveal its recruitment, hierarchy, dress, income, or rites. **[EA]**
- **RIB 2417.5–8:** inscribed pewter vessels from the spring, including *Deae Suli Minervae*. These show that the double name appeared on deposited or cult-associated tableware as well as stone monuments. **[EA/SI]**

### The Bath tablets

Approximately **130 sheets and fragments**, mostly pewter or lead alloy rather than pure lead, were recovered from the Sacred Spring during the 1979–1980 excavations. UNESCO describes them as personal prayers dating from the second to fourth centuries CE. Tomlin’s 1988 *Tabellae Sulis* remains their critical publication.

Of the traditional 130 catalogue entries, 111 contain literate text, four pseudo-writing, six no inscription, and seven were folded or illegible. Numbering an archaeological corpus of fragments does not necessarily mean 130 complete texts or exactly 130 identifiable individual petitioners.

Most are in Latin, written in capitals, Old Roman Cursive, or New Roman Cursive. Some are reversed, scrambled, or boustrophedon. Two or three texts have been proposed as British Celtic written in Roman letters, but readings and even linguistic classification remain disputed. Their value lies partly in showing uneven, everyday provincial literacy rather than a standardized priestly script.

The tablets mostly concern theft. Petitioners “give” the stolen object, culprit, or culprit’s body to the goddess and ask her to discover or punish the offender until restitution is made. Stolen things include cloaks, bathing garments, gloves, rings, bracelets, coins, vessels, a frying pan, and a ploughshare. Common requested penalties include loss of sleep, health, sanity, eyes, blood, or bodily functions.

Representative evidence includes:

- **Tab. Sulis 5:** Docimedis reports two lost gloves and asks that the thief lose mind and eyes in the goddess’s temple. **[EA]**
- **Tab. Sulis 10:** addresses Sulis as *sanctissima* and asks vengeance and forced return of a stolen cloak. It also preserves the nominative *Sulis*. **[EA]**
- **Tab. Sulis 35:** asks Sulis Minerva’s “most holy majesty” to avenge fraud. **[EA/SI]**
- **Tab. Sulis 65:** places Minerva’s name before Sulis and seeks the thief’s exposure. **[EA/SI/RV]**
- **Tab. Sulis 94:** records persons who swore *ad fontem deae Sulis*, “at the spring of the goddess Sulis,” and threatens future perjury. This is the clearest evidence for oath enforcement at the spring. **[EA]**
- **Tab. Sulis 97:** Basilia petitions concerning a silver ring and distinguishes the thief from anyone knowingly concealing the theft. **[EA]**
- **Tab. Sulis 98:** asks punishment whether the culprit is *paganus* or *Christianus* and addresses the goddess as *domina*. It is important evidence for a late religiously mixed environment, not proof that a Christian authored the tablet. **[EA]**

“Curse tablet” is conventional but incomplete. Tomlin describes many as **quasi-legal prayers for justice**: complainants identify property, set out wrongdoing, name suspects or categories of suspect, and seek divine judgment. Their logic resembles petitions to a magistrate while invoking a supernatural patron where ordinary policing was inadequate.

### Literary and geographical texts

- **Ptolemy, *Geography* 2.3.28, mid-second century CE:** mentions the Belgic *Ὕδατα Θερμά*, “Hot Waters,” probably Bath. Sulis is not named. **[EA/U as an attestation of Sulis]**
- **Antonine Itinerary, compiled in its received form in the later Roman Empire:** *Aquis Sulis*. This is a place-name attestation, not a myth or description of the goddess. **[EA]**
- **Solinus, *Collectanea rerum memorabilium* 22.10, probably third or early fourth century:** describes Britain’s splendidly equipped hot springs: *quibus fontibus praesul est Minervae numen, in cuius aede perpetui ignes numquam canescunt in favillas, sed ubi ignis tabuit vertit in globos saxeos*—“over these springs the divine power of Minerva presides; in her temple perpetual fires never whiten into ash, but when the fire subsides it turns into stony balls.” Sulis is not named; identification with Bath rests on the hot springs, Minerva, and later Bath tradition. The “stony balls” probably reflect burning mineral fuel, commonly interpreted as coal, rather than an otherwise attested mystery of Sulis. **[EA/MSR]**
- **Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* 6.17, 50s BCE:** says the Gauls’ “Minerva” taught beginnings of crafts. This is Roman interpretatio of unnamed Gallic divinity or divinities. It predates the Bath evidence but neither names Sulis nor concerns Britain. It cannot establish Sulis’s original domain. **[EA/U]**
- **Geoffrey of Monmouth, *Historia regum Britanniae* 2.10, c. 1136:** King Bladud builds Bath, places the baths under Minerva’s guardianship, and establishes unquenchable fires that turn into stone. Geoffrey has adapted Solinus and attached the marvel to his legendary British king. He never calls the goddess Sulis. **[MA]**
- **Old English “The Ruin,” perhaps eighth or ninth century, surviving in the tenth-century Exeter Book:** describes ruined stone buildings, bath-houses, and a hot spring. Bath is a convincing but not textually named identification. Neither Sulis nor Minerva appears. **[MA/U]**

### The named Celtic corpora: a negative finding

**[EA as corpus history; U as Sulis evidence]** Sulis does **not** appear by name in the *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, *Cath Maige Tuired*, the *Dindshenchas*, *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, *Tochmarc Étaíne*, *Acallam na Senórach*, the Four Branches of the *Mabinogi*, *Culhwch ac Olwen*, or the *Book of Taliesin*. Nor does she occur in Lucan’s list of Esus, Taranis, and Teutates, the Gundestrup Cauldron, the Pillar of the Boatmen, or the Coligny Calendar.

Those sources are indispensable for their own regional traditions, but using them to construct Sulis’s genealogy or myths would merge distinct societies separated by geography, language, and centuries. Resemblance between Sulis and Irish or Welsh divine figures is comparative reconstruction, not a lost Sulis narrative.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Secure domains

- **The thermal spring:** Tablet 94 explicitly names “the spring of the goddess Sulis”; the place-name makes the waters hers. **[EA]**
- **Welfare and bodily safety:** RIB 143 petitions her *pro salute et incolumitate*. **[EA]**
- **Discovery and punishment of thieves:** the dominant function in the tablet archive. **[EA]**
- **Oaths and perjury:** Tablet 94. **[EA]**
- **Vows:** numerous altars end *votum solvit libens merito*, “willingly and deservedly fulfilled the vow.” **[EA]**
- **Divination:** RIB 144’s *haruspex* and *ex visu* language suggest a divinatory or visionary dimension. Details are lost. **[EA]**
- **Public and imperial welfare:** RIB 146 links her with the emperors’ numina. **[EA/SI]**

### Probable or reconstructed domains

- **Healing:** strongly probable from the spring, baths, welfare dedications, and pilgrimage context, but direct cure narratives and anatomical cure inscriptions are absent. **[MSR]**
- **Craft, wisdom, warfare, civic order:** possible consequences of identification with Minerva, but not independently assigned to Sulis in surviving texts. **[SI/MSR]**
- **Solar power and sight:** based on disputed etymology, the temple’s fiery or luminous setting, and modern comparison—not a stated ancient doctrine. **[MSR/U]**
- **Fertility, maternity, childbirth:** sometimes inferred from female anatomical votives or generalized “Celtic mother goddess” models. The surviving evidence does not establish these as Sulis’s defining functions. **[U]**

### Cult statue and head

The best-known image is the slightly over-life-sized **gilt-bronze female head**, found in 1727 and now in the Roman Baths Museum. It is probably the head of the temple cult statue. Rivet holes hidden in the hair once held a tall Corinthian helmet, identifying the figure iconographically as Minerva. Technical examination found six gilding campaigns: two fire-gilded layers followed by four applications of gold leaf. The original statue may date to the sanctuary’s later first-century foundation and may have remained in use for three centuries.

**[SI]** Because the sanctuary’s epigraphy calls the deity Sulis Minerva, the statue is conventionally identified as that fused goddess. Its surviving iconography is nevertheless classical Minervan; nothing on the head isolates a visibly pre-Roman “Sulis” component.

Claims that the complete statue certainly copied Athena Parthenos, held a spear and shield, or displayed an owl remain plausible reconstructions, not surviving facts.

### Temple pediment

Fragments reconstruct a classical triangular pediment centered on a powerful frontal, male or bearded mask commonly called a **Gorgon’s head**. Wings, snakes or locks, Triton-like marine figures, victories, an owl, helmet, shield, and celestial motifs have been identified in the ensemble.

Interpretations compete:

- a masculine provincial Gorgon or Medusa protecting Minerva’s temple;
- a water deity or Oceanus-like head appropriate to the spring;
- a deliberately hybrid image joining classical apotropaic, aquatic, military, and imperial symbols.

The face is **not securely an image of Sulis herself**. Describing it as “the face of Sulis” erases its conspicuously masculine and Gorgonic form and the scholarly debate. **[U/MSR]**

### Symbols and non-symbols

Securely associated through the physical sanctuary are hot water, the spring reservoir, votive altars, deposited coins, inscribed metal sheets, the helmeted gilt head, and Minervan imagery.

No ancient source assigns Sulis a sacred color, number, weekday, star, planet, tree, herb, pig, snake, raven, dog, or solar wheel. Modern lists making the Sun her planet, water and fire her elements, Sunday her day, or pigs her animals are modern systems. The pig belongs chiefly to the post-medieval Bladud folklore, not the Roman cult. **[MI]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

There is none. No source names Sulis’s parents, birthplace, siblings, spouse, lover, child, servant, rival, enemy, or animal companion. She is not fitted into a preserved native British family tree.

Minerva’s classical genealogy—daughter of Jupiter, born from his head—must not be silently transferred to Sulis. No Bath text does so. Modern stories in which Sulis is born from Taranis, marries a Celtic god, or belongs to a pan-Celtic divine family are modern constructions. **[MI]**

### Every documented divine interaction or conjunction

Because no narrative myth survives, the list is short:

- **Minerva — identity/fusion:** Sulis is explicitly named with Minerva in RIB 146, RIB 150, inscribed vessels, and several curse tablets, second–fourth centuries CE. This is a cultic identification, not a story in which two goddesses meet. **[SI/EA]**
- **Numina Augustorum — joint dedication:** RIB 146 dedicates one vow to Sulis Minerva and the divine powers of two emperors, probably Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus or a later pair. This is political-cultic conjunction, not mythology. **[EA/SI]**
- **Mars — separate petitions in Sulis’s spring:** Bath tablets 33 and 97 invoke or “give” property to Mars despite deposition in Sulis’s spring. Sulis and Mars do not interact in the text. **[EA; absence of interaction noted]**
- **Mercury — separate petition in Sulis’s spring:** Tablet 53 is intended for Mercury. Again, no encounter with Sulis is described. **[EA; absence of interaction noted]**
- **Suleviae — same sanctuary, no stated relation:** RIB 151 names the Suleviae at Bath. No text makes them Sulis’s kin, companions, or forms. **[EA/U]**
- **Other sanctuary gods:** Mars Loucetius and Nemetona appear together on RIB 140; images of Mercury, Diana, Jupiter, Luna/Selene, Cupid, Bacchus and other classical figures have been catalogued at Bath. Shared sacred topography does not constitute interaction with Sulis. **[EA; absence of interaction noted]**

There are no securely documented meetings, combats, marriages, seductions, rescues, contests, judgments between gods, or divine councils involving Sulis.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Ancient myths

None survives. No Roman or Celtic narrative tells Sulis’s birth, deeds, travels, battles, love affairs, or foundation of the spring. Her ancient record is cultic and documentary rather than mythographic.

### The perpetual fire

**[EA]** Solinus describes Minerva’s temple at Britain’s hot springs and its perpetual fires, whose exhausted fuel becomes stony globes rather than ash. If the site is Bath—and the identification is persuasive—the passage records a late Roman marvel attached to Sulis Minerva’s sanctuary.

**[MSR]** Coal or another mineral fuel provides the most economical explanation. Solinus’s wording does not state that Sulis created the fire, that it was imported from Troy, or that priestesses maintained it as a revealed mystery.

**[MA]** Geoffrey of Monmouth reproduces the marvel in the twelfth century and credits Bladud with establishing the baths, dedicating them to Minerva, and arranging the perpetual fires. This is a literary foundation legend made from Roman antiquarian material.

### Bladud and the pigs

The familiar story in which the leprous Prince Bladud is cured after observing pigs wallowing in Bath’s hot mud is not present in Geoffrey’s earliest Bladud account. It developed in later local historiography and folklore. In some modern versions Bladud becomes Sulis’s priest or founder of her temple, although Geoffrey names only Minerva. **[F/MI]**

### Troy and the sacred fire

Stories that Bath’s eternal fire came from Troy, sometimes carried by Brutus or Aeneas, belong to medieval and early-modern attempts to connect Britain with Trojan origin legends. They are not Roman-period traditions of Sulis. **[MA]**

### “Sulis and Minerva” as a myth

Modern retellings sometimes narrate the Roman and Celtic goddesses meeting, merging, or agreeing to share the spring. Ancient inscriptions instead present a double name and sometimes use either name independently. They reveal religious translation and hybridization, not an attested dramatic episode. **[MI]**

## 6. Cult, Temple, and Ritual Practice

### Sanctuary

The Roman sanctuary stood beside and partly organized the natural thermal spring. Its principal components included:

- a lead-lined sacred reservoir around the spring;
- a classical podium temple;
- a monumental courtyard and precinct;
- a great outdoor altar;
- access to bathing suites;
- votive and administrative spaces;
- later enlargements, repairs, and increasingly dense building around the complex.

The temple’s exact date remains debated. Stratigraphy and architectural ornament favor the later first or early second century, with a possible Flavian or Domitianic foundation. Proposals that the client king Togidubnus sponsored it are attractive but unproven. There is no foundation inscription naming patron, architect, or goddess.

The shrine differed from a purely curative bathing establishment: the spring belonged to the deity, while public bathing and treatment occupied connected but functionally distinguishable spaces.

### Worshippers

Names and status markers show a socially and geographically mixed body:

- Roman citizens and persons with Celtic names;
- soldiers and centurions of Legiones II Augusta and VI Victrix;
- freedpeople;
- craftsmen, including the sculptor Sulinus;
- ordinary bathers whose modest belongings were stolen;
- people capable of fluent composition and others with limited literacy.

RIB 143 is socially revealing: a freedman made a vow for his military patron. RIB 146 joins family welfare, military identity, local goddess, Roman Minerva, and emperor cult in a single act.

### Priesthood and divination

Gaius Calpurnius Receptus is the sole securely named *sacerdos deae Sulis*. A seventy-five-year lifespan and marriage to his freedwoman can be recovered from his epitaph; no source says how he became priest or what he did.

Lucius Marcius Memor, associated with RIB 144, was probably a *haruspex*. This supports specialist divination and visionary authorization within the sanctuary’s Roman ritual environment. It does not prove a native Druidic priesthood. No Druid is attested at Bath.

There is likewise no evidence for an all-female priesthood, hereditary “priestesses of Sulis,” sacred prostitution, or temple sexual rites.

### Offerings

**Coins.** More than twelve thousand Roman coins and eighteen Iron Age coins were recovered from the spring. Coins were deposited over centuries, although not every coin entering water must be a formally consecrated offering.

**Metal vessels.** Pewter cups, bowls, and a *trulla* were inscribed to Sulis Minerva and deposited or lost in the spring.

**Altars and vows.** Monumental stone altars publicly commemorated reciprocal vows.

**Tablets.** The complainant wrote or commissioned a text, sometimes reversed or folded it, and cast it into the spring. The water transferred it to the goddess’s domain.

**Animal sacrifice.** The great altar and faunal remains make ordinary Roman animal sacrifice probable. Which species were offered specifically to Sulis and on what calendar cannot be reconstructed securely.

**Human sacrifice.** No human sacrifice is documented at Bath. Caesar’s report of human sacrifice in Gaul, *Bellum Gallicum* 6.16, is geographically and politically distant and cannot be transferred to Sulis. Tablet language that “gives” a thief’s body or blood to the goddess is ritualized verbal surrender and threatened supernatural punishment, not archaeological evidence that victims were killed at the temple. **[EA for violent language; U for actual killing]**

### Curses, justice, and violence

The tablets should not be sanitized. Petitioners requested blindness, madness, sleeplessness, sickness, loss of blood, and death-like bodily wasting, sometimes against all possible offenders of either sex or status. The practice directed intense private violence through divine agency.

At the same time, the tablets were normally responses to alleged theft or fraud, not random aggressive magic. Their accusations are one-sided: no evidence establishes that named suspects were guilty or that punishments occurred. “Prayer for justice” describes the petitioner’s framing, not proof of justice.

### Pilgrimage, festivals, and economics

The scale of the baths, deposits, and mixed population supports regional pilgrimage and a substantial sanctuary economy. Visitors required lodging, food, bathing services, metal sheets or writing help, offerings, and ritual mediation.

No named annual festival of Sulis survives. No ancient calendar assigns her a feast day. The Coligny Calendar says nothing about her. Dates offered in contemporary pagan calendars are modern choices. **[U/MI]**

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before CE 43: sacred spring, unnamed deity

Late Iron Age coin deposition makes ritual use plausible. Sulis’s name and identity are not yet documented. **[MSR]**

### Later first–early second century: monumental Roman sanctuary

A classical temple, reservoir, altar, and cult image transformed the spring into a major Romano-British sanctuary. Naming the goddess Sulis Minerva retained a local divine name within Roman architecture, Latin epigraphy, anthropomorphic statuary, sacrifice, and civic-imperial religion. This was neither simple Roman replacement nor proof of an unchanged indigenous religion.

### Second century: institutional consolidation

RIB 143 after 122 establishes named worship. Military worshippers, a priest, a diviner, and large-scale monumental dedications indicate a mature institution. The town’s name, Aquae Sulis, publicly inscribed the goddess into imperial geography.

### Second–fourth centuries: the tablet archive

Private petitioning flourished. Changes from Old to New Roman Cursive help spread the archive across several generations. The formula “whether pagan or Christian” shows that petitioners in the later phase imagined Christians within the sanctuary’s social world, whether as visitors, suspects, or rhetorical categories.

### Later fourth century: contraction and desecration

The great altar was dismantled and covered during a late fourth-century reorganization, approximately CE 370–380. The cult statue was probably toppled and decapitated during the sanctuary’s decline, but the responsible agents are not securely known.

The Roman Baths’ interpretation associates closure with Theodosius I’s anti-sacrificial and anti-temple legislation in the 390s. That supplies a historical environment, not documentary proof that a named Christian mob destroyed Sulis’s cult on a particular day. Possible explanations include official suppression, local Christian action, withdrawal of resources, deliberate desacralization, political disorder, or some combination.

### Fifth century: demolition

Radiocarbon and stratigraphic analysis place major demolition of the temple around CE 450–500, later than older narratives of immediate abandonment in 410. Late activity therefore continued around the precinct after formal Roman government ended, although continuity of Sulis worship is not proved.

### Early medieval Bath

“The Ruin” may preserve memory of Bath’s monumental hot-water complex but does not preserve the goddess’s cult. A Christian ecclesiastical center developed nearby. There is no evidence of continuous, disguised worship of Sulis in medieval wells, saints, or folk customs.

### Twelfth century onward: Minerva, Bladud, and antiquarian memory

Geoffrey of Monmouth retained Minerva and Solinus’s fire while inventing a royal founder. Later chroniclers, antiquaries, and local folklore enlarged the Bladud story. Sulis’s own name returned primarily through inscriptions and archaeological scholarship, not continuous oral tradition.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: rediscovery

Roman remains emerged during rebuilding of fashionable spa Bath. The gilt head was found in 1727; altars came to light in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; the temple remains were recognized in 1790. Samuel Lysons’s 1802 reconstruction made the classical temple visually intelligible. Victorian scholarship commonly used “Sul” and increasingly treated the sanctuary as Romano-Celtic.

### Twentieth century: archaeological reconstruction

Systematic excavation by Barry Cunliffe, Peter Davenport, and collaborators produced the modern phasing. The 1979–1980 spring excavation recovered the tablet archive. Tomlin’s 1988 edition transformed Sulis from a goddess known chiefly from monumental dedications into one documented through the requests of ordinary worshippers.

### Twenty-first century

The Roman Baths Museum presents Sulis Minerva as the sanctuary’s presiding deity. The tablets entered the UNESCO UK Memory of the World Register in 2014. Sulis is also worshipped by some modern pagans and independent devotees, but no reliable census isolates her following, and revival should not be called institutional continuity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Antiquarian and Romantic reception

Sulis did not occupy the central place in Celtic Revival literature held by Brigid, the Morrígan, Lugh, or figures from the *Mabinogi*. Yeats, Lady Gregory, and the principal literary revivalists worked mainly with medieval Irish material in which Sulis is absent. Modern Druid orders use Bath and Sulis Minerva as examples of sacred landscape and Romano-Celtic religious blending, but this is modern reception rather than recovery of an unbroken Druid liturgy.

### Pagan and reconstructionist worship

Modern practitioners invoke Sulis for healing, purification, justice, water rites, solar rites, divination, empowerment, and pilgrimage to Bath. Documented examples include Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids educational material, private devotional sites, “priestess of Sulis” ceremonies, and Rachel Patterson’s *Pagan Portals: Sulis*.

These practices are real contemporary religion. Their historicity must nevertheless be differentiated:

- prayer at a spring, vows, gifts, and justice petitions have ancient analogues; **[modern revival based on EA]**
- Sunday observance, solar water, crystal correspondences, tarot associations, elemental systems, sacred colors, and generalized “divine feminine” theology are modern; **[MI]**
- modern priestesses are self-defined or authorized by modern communities, not demonstrable successors of Gaius Calpurnius Receptus; **[MI]**
- throwing metal, coins, oils, candles, or non-biodegradable offerings into springs can imitate an ancient act without reproducing its social or environmental context. **[MI]**

“Goddess of the Gap,” maintained as a modern devotional identity, explicitly combines sun, water, liminality, and personal revelation. It is valuable evidence for present religion but not for Romano-British theology.

### Occult and esoteric systems

No significant Sulis tradition is documented in Renaissance Hermetism, the classical grimoires, Theosophy, or the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. Modern occult encyclopedias and websites have retrospectively inserted her into correspondence systems.

Judika Illes’s *Encyclopedia of Spirits* and derivative sites describe Sulis through Sun, water, pigs, justice magic, cleansing, and bodily ex-votos. Some elements derive loosely from Bath archaeology; the assembled correspondence system is modern. Claims that breasts were her characteristic ex-votos, pigs her sacred animals, or the spring a formal portal to spirits require evidence not supplied by the Bath texts. **[MI/U]**

### Popular culture and commerce

Sulis supplies names and imagery for wellness businesses, bath products, fantasy characters, games, fiction, art, and local branding. These uses usually emphasize healing water, fierce justice, or a Celtic sun goddess. The museum’s gilt head and reconstructed Gorgon pediment have materially shaped the modern visual identity.

No single novel, film, comic, or game has yet created a universally dominant popular version comparable to modern portrayals of Thor or Hades.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No substantial, well-documented nationalist or extremist cult of Sulis emerged from the dedicated search. Generic Celtic indigeneity, Roman-colonial encounter, and “native goddess versus invader” rhetoric occur in popular discussion, but evidence does not support naming Sulis as a major symbol of organized ethnonationalism.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal foundations

- The **RIB** corpus supplies authoritative editions of the monumental inscriptions and now the Bath tablets online.
- Barry Cunliffe and Peter Davenport’s *The Temple of Sulis Minerva at Bath*, especially the 1985 site volume and 1988 finds volume, remains foundational.
- R. S. O. Tomlin’s *Tabellae Sulis* established the corpus, readings, palaeography, language, and prayer-for-justice interpretation.
- T. F. C. Blagg examined the architectural dating.
- James Gerrard reassessed late use and dated temple demolition to CE 450–500.
- Eleri Cousins’s *The Sanctuary at Bath in the Roman Empire* recontextualizes the site within empire, mobility, local knowledge, and provincial religion.
- Delamarre, Lambert, Zair, and Matasović frame the linguistic debate.
- Green, Maier, MacKillop, Koch and related Celtic reference works are useful syntheses but necessarily reconstruct beyond Bath’s sparse direct record.

### Major controversies

**Was there a pre-Roman cult of Sulis?**  
Ritual activity at the spring is plausible from Iron Age coins. Her name and attributes are not pre-Romanly attested. The careful formulation is “pre-Roman sacred use of the spring, possibly involving the predecessor of Roman-period Sulis.”

**Was Sulis a sun goddess?**  
Possible etymological relationship among “sun,” “eye,” and *Sulis* has encouraged the theory. Neither inscriptions nor imagery calls her the sun. Solinus’s perpetual fire and pedimental celestial motifs are insufficient to prove an original solar cult.

**Was she principally a healing goddess?**  
The hot spring and welfare inscriptions favor healing; the largest textual corpus instead shows retribution and property recovery. “Spring goddess with healing and punitive/judicial functions” fits the evidence better than a single-domain label.

**Does Minerva conceal Sulis?**  
Older “Roman veneer over unchanged Celtic goddess” models underplay genuine transformation. Conversely, treating the double name as merely Latin Minerva erases the persistence of *Sulis*, her spring, and locally distinctive petitioning. Hybridization, translation, and negotiated local practice are more accurate than either total resistance or total replacement.

**Was the pediment face Sulis?**  
It has been read as Gorgon, male Medusa, water god, Oceanus, solar figure, or hybrid provincial emblem. It should not be captioned as a secure portrait of Sulis.

**Are the tablets magical curses or legal prayers?**  
They combine both categories. Their bodily penalties and manipulated scripts belong to curse practice; their grievances, named property, conditional punishment, restitution, and appeal to divine majesty resemble petitions for justice.

**Was Vilbia a kidnapped woman?**  
RIB 154/*Tab. Sulis* 4 was long popularized as a curse concerning the theft of a woman named Vilbia. More recent linguistic discussion allows *vilbia* to be an object term, perhaps a pointed implement. The romantic kidnapping story is not secure.

**Was Sulis widely worshipped?**  
Almost every secure attestation comes from Bath. Alzey is singular and textually ambiguous. Equating every Suleviae inscription with Sulis manufactures a broad cult that the evidence does not independently show.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- Sulis has an ancient Irish or Welsh narrative cycle. **Unsupported.**
- She is named in the *Mabinogion* or Irish Mythological Cycle. **False.**
- She was certainly the universal Celtic sun goddess. **Unsupported systematization.**
- Minerva was simply “the Roman name” for an otherwise unchanged Sulis. **Over-simplification.**
- The Gorgon pediment is a secure portrait of Sulis. **Disputed.**
- Bladud’s pigs preserve an Iron Age Sulis myth. **Late folklore.**
- A continuous line of Sulis priestesses survived from Rome to modern paganism. **No evidence.**
- Human sacrifice occurred at her Bath temple. **No evidence.**
- The eternal flame is archaeologically attested. **It is a literary report, plausibly related to mineral fuel.**

### State and location of the evidence

Most principal objects—including the gilt head, altars, architectural sculpture, vessels, coins, and tablets—are held by the Roman Baths Museum/Bath and North East Somerset Council. Many original structures remain beneath the modern city. RIB provides digital editions; Tomlin’s 1988 publication remains essential for the tablets. The Alzey altar is recorded as formerly or presently associated with the Landesmuseum Mainz, although its preservation history has at times been uncertain.

## 10. Chronology

- **Late Iron Age, before CE 43:** eighteen unworn Celtic coins deposited in or near the spring; ritual interpretation plausible, Sulis unnamed. **[EA/MSR]**
- **CE 43 onward:** Roman conquest creates the political setting for the sanctuary’s monumental transformation.
- **c. CE 70–110:** likely construction range for the classical temple and early baths; cult statue possibly installed. Exact date and patron disputed. **[EA/MSR]**
- **After CE 122:** RIB 143 dedicates an altar to *dea Sulis* for a centurion of Legio VI Victrix—the earliest closely dated named attestation. **[EA]**
- **Mid-second century:** Ptolemy lists “Hot Waters,” probably Bath, without Sulis’s name. **[EA/U]**
- **CE 161–169 or later joint reign:** probable earliest setting for RIB 146, dedicated to Sulis Minerva and two emperors’ numina. **[EA/SI]**
- **Second–fourth centuries:** principal period of the Bath justice-prayer tablets. **[EA]**
- **Third or early fourth century:** Solinus records British hot springs presided over by Minerva and a perpetual temple fire. **[EA]**
- **Later Roman period:** Antonine Itinerary records *Aquis Sulis*. **[EA]**
- **c. CE 370–380:** great altar deliberately dismantled and buried; cult precinct reorganized. **[EA/MSR]**
- **CE 391–392:** Theodosian prohibitions on sacrifice and pagan cult provide the imperial background for closure; no surviving order names Bath. **[EA/U locally]**
- **c. CE 450–500:** archaeological and radiocarbon evidence places major temple demolition in this period. **[EA]**
- **Eighth–ninth century:** probable composition of “The Ruin,” possibly describing Bath, but not Sulis. **[MA/U]**
- **Tenth century:** “The Ruin” copied into the Exeter Book.
- **c. 1136:** Geoffrey of Monmouth credits Bladud with building Bath and placing it under Minerva, adapting Solinus’s eternal fire. **[MA]**
- **Later medieval–early modern period:** Bladud legend expands; pig-and-leprosy cure and Trojan associations enter local tradition. **[F/MA]**
- **1727:** gilt-bronze cult-statue head discovered.
- **1753–1809:** important inscribed altars discovered during work in Bath.
- **1790:** major temple remains uncovered.
- **1802:** Samuel Lysons publishes an influential temple reconstruction.
- **1872:** DEA SVL altar discovered at Alzey; its identification with Sulis remains qualified.
- **1950s–1980s:** increasingly systematic excavation and architectural study.
- **1978–1983:** Cunliffe and Davenport excavate the temple precinct.
- **1979–1980:** approximately 130 tablet entries recovered from the Sacred Spring.
- **1985:** Cunliffe and Davenport publish the principal site report.
- **1988:** Tomlin publishes *Tabellae Sulis* in the finds volume.
- **2008:** Gerrard publishes the case for CE 450–500 demolition.
- **2014:** Bath tablets entered on the UNESCO UK Memory of the World Register.
- **Twenty-first century:** continuing museum interpretation, scholarship, tourism, and small-scale pagan/reconstructionist worship; no demonstrated institutional continuity from antiquity.
