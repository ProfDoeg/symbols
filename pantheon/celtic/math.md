# Math fab Mathonwy: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the surviving medieval Welsh tradition, Math fab Mathonwy is a **supernaturally powerful human-shaped ruler and master enchanter**, lord of Gwynedd—not explicitly called a *duw* (“god”) in the primary text. Modern mythographers and neopagans frequently classify him as a Welsh god of magic, sovereignty, or wisdom, but that divine classification is a reconstruction or modern religious interpretation rather than the wording of the medieval witnesses.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Math’s earliest presently secure physical attestation is in the Middle Welsh Fourth Branch of the *Mabinogi* as copied in **Llyfr Gwyn Rhydderch**, the White Book of Rhydderch, National Library of Wales Peniarth MS 4, in the **mid-fourteenth century, approximately 1350**. The narrative itself is generally assigned to the late eleventh or twelfth century, although its exact composition date remains disputed and individual motifs may be older. Math is not securely attested in Iron Age inscriptions, Romano-British dedications, classical ethnography, or datable pre-Christian iconography.

**Evidentiary labels used below:**

- **[SA] Securely attested:** directly present in a surviving medieval text or material witness.
- **[LE] Literary elaboration:** medieval material outside, or secondary to, the principal narrative.
- **[RV] Regional/textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, mythographic, or scholarly arrangement.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an explicit primary-source statement.
- **[MA] Modern adaptation or invention.**
- **[MR] Modern religious reception.**
- **[NE] Negative evidence:** sought in the relevant corpus but not found.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Math is the stationary but far-seeing sovereign at the center of the Fourth Branch of the *Mabinogi*. The opening identifies him as lord of Gwynedd and says that, except during war, he cannot remain alive unless his feet are enclosed in the lap of a virgin. His permanent court is Caer Dathyl in Arfon. He can hear even a whisper once the wind encounters it; wields a wand that changes human beings into animals and animals into human foster-sons; tests Arianrhod’s claim to virginity by making her step over a magical rod; names and baptizes Dylan; helps create Blodeuwedd from flowers; provides physicians for the wounded Lleu; and acts as sovereign judge over his household. All of this is **[SA]**, carried by the Fourth Branch, whose Middle Welsh opening is:

> *Math uab Mathonwy oed arglwyd ar Wyned*  
> “Math son of Mathonwy was lord over Gwynedd.”

The same text calls him *arglwyd*—“lord”—not *duw*, “god.” “King” is an appropriate functional paraphrase, but “lord” is the primary text’s exact title.

### Names

- **Math fab Mathonwy**: standard Modern Welsh form, “Math son of Mathonwy.” **[SA]**
- **Math uab Mathonwy**: Middle Welsh manuscript spelling; initial *m* in *mab* regularly lenites to *v/f* after a personal name. **[SA]**
- **Math ap Mathonwy / Math ab Mathonwy**: later orthographic variants of the patronymic. **[LE]**
- **Math Hen**, “Old Math” or “Math the Ancient”: a poetic designation usually identified with this Math, but its identification depends on context rather than a full patronymic. A late medieval Taliesin/Gwion poem places “Math Hen” among *gwŷr cywrein*—skilled or artful men—beside Gofannon. **[LE]**
- **Mathonwy**: the Fourth Branch supplies no deeds, sex description, office, ancestry, or cult for this figure; Mathonwy exists securely only in Math’s patronymic. **[SA]**

### Etymology

The name’s etymology is unresolved.

1. **Celtic \**matu-**, “good, favorable,” and/or “bear.”** Earlier Celtic scholarship connected *Math* with a Celtic stem represented by Gaulish personal names in *Matu-* and Old Irish *math*/*maith*, “good.” Because “bear” terms were sometimes euphemistically replaced by “the good one,” the senses “good” and “bear” have frequently been joined. This is a plausible comparison, not a demonstrated interpretation of the literary character. **[SR]**

2. **Mathonwy as an Irish borrowing.** Heinrich Zimmer proposed derivation from an Irish name represented by *Mathgamain/Mathgamnai*, later Mahony. Joseph Loth objected that Welsh names in *-onwy* are independently common and considered the proposed Irish derivation improbable. The issue cannot be settled by the Fourth Branch itself. **[SR/disputed]**

3. **Mathonwy as a matronymic.** Rachel Bromwich and others have entertained the possibility that *Mathonwy* functions like a maternal formation, comparable to *Gwydion fab Dôn*, particularly because the Fourth Branch’s kinship language emphasizes Math’s sister and her sons. The grammatical form still looks like an ordinary patronymic, and no independent figure named Mathonwy clarifies it. **[SR/disputed]**

4. Popular expansions such as “Bear of the Moving Waters,” derived by separating *Math-on-wy*, have no secure medieval linguistic support. **[MA]**

Math is unrelated to the English word *mathematics*. That resemblance is accidental.

### Epithets and classifications

No extensive ancient or medieval cult-title system survives.

- **“Lord of Gwynedd”**—explicit in the Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Math Hen, “Old Math”**—poetic, if the identification is correct. **[LE]**
- **“One of the three chief/great enchantments”**—the Welsh Triads credit a chief enchantment to Math and say that he taught it to Gwydion. This is a classification of his magic, not a cult epithet. **[LE]**
- **“Chief Enchanter of the Gods,” “god of magic,” “god of sovereignty,” “ruler god,” and “Druid god”** are modern mythographic, occult, or devotional titles. **[MA/MR]**

### Proposed equations with other beings

No ancient *interpretatio Romana* or *interpretatio Graeca* identifies Math with any classical deity. **[NE]**

Antiquarians proposed several equations:

- John Rhŷs compared Math with a Welsh Pluto/Plutus and elsewhere treated him as a Brythonic Zeus-like figure. **[LS, nineteenth century]**
- W. Y. Evans-Wentz reported speculative comparisons with the Irish magician Math mac Umair and even Aengus. These rest on analogical mythography, not a medieval identification. **[LS, 1911]**
- Math has sometimes been set beside Nuadu, the Dagda, Manannán, or Merlin because of shared motifs of kingship, magic, teaching, or bodily restrictions. No primary Welsh or Irish text equates them. **[SR/MA]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Physical witnesses

#### White Book of Rhydderch

The earliest complete witness is **NLW Peniarth MS 4**, part of the White Book of Rhydderch. The National Library of Wales dates it to the **mid-fourteenth century**, probably produced for Rhydderch ab Ieuan Llwyd and probably copied near Strata Florida by several scribes. It is the earliest major compendium of Welsh prose and contains the earliest surviving complete copy of the Four Branches.

What it establishes:

- Math’s name and patronymic;
- his lordship of Gwynedd;
- the foot-in-lap condition;
- his household and relations;
- the entire Fourth Branch narrative.

What it does **not** establish:

- worship of Math;
- pre-Christian dating;
- historical kingship;
- an ancient temple, priesthood, image, festival, or sacrifice;
- identity with an inscribed Celtic god.

#### Red Book of Hergest

Oxford, Jesus College MS 111, the Red Book of Hergest, was copied approximately **1382–1400**. It preserves another complete recension. The White and Red books probably derive from a lost earlier textual tradition rather than one simply copying the other. **[SA]**

No surviving thirteenth-century fragment of the Four Branches contains the Fourth Branch: Peniarth 6 preserves fragments of *Branwen* and *Manawydan*, not Math. Consequently, the material existence of Math’s particular narrative cannot presently be pushed earlier than the fourteenth-century complete manuscripts, even if linguistic and literary arguments place composition earlier.

### Date of composition

The proposed dates include:

- before 1100, argued by Ifor Williams and others from language and historical context;
- approximately 1050–1120, compatible with Thomas Charles-Edwards’s linguistic discussion;
- approximately 1060–1200, Patrick Sims-Williams’s deliberately broad range;
- 1170–1190, associated with Saunders Lewis;
- the 1120s or early 1130s, Andrew Breeze’s more precise but contested proposal.

The prudent finding is that the Four Branches reached something close to their surviving literary form in the **late eleventh or twelfth century**, certainly before the surviving fourteenth-century codices. An “Iron Age text,” “Druid scripture,” or exact transcription of pagan oral liturgy is not established. **[SR]**

### Primary corpus actually naming Math

#### 1. The Fourth Branch, *Math fab Mathonwy*

- **Language:** Middle Welsh.
- **Composition:** probably late eleventh–twelfth century; disputed.
- **Surviving complete manuscripts:** mid-fourteenth-century White Book and late-fourteenth-century Red Book.
- **Role:** lord, magician, judge, teacher, creator, foster-father, and dynastic predecessor.
- **Status:** principal and overwhelmingly fullest source. **[SA]**

#### 2. *Trioedd Ynys Prydein*, Welsh Triads

A triad of the “Three Great Enchantments of the Island of Britain” names the enchantment of Math fab Mathonwy and says that he taught it to Gwydion fab Dôn. The triadic corpus is multilayered: individual triads circulated in different manuscript groupings, and the wording cannot automatically be treated as older than the Fourth Branch. It independently confirms Math’s medieval reputation as an exemplary magician and teacher. **[LE]**

#### 3. Medieval Welsh poetry

A poem associated with the Taliesin/Gwion tradition says:

> “I have been with artful men:  
> with Math Hen, with Gofannon …”

If *Math Hen* is Math fab Mathonwy, the poem preserves a separate allusion to his magical or artisanal company. Dating the underlying verse is difficult; the surviving *Book of Taliesin* is a fourteenth-century manuscript, and many poems are composite or later than the historical Taliesin. **[LE]**

Antiquarian notes also cite a “wand of Mathonwy” in *Cerdd Daronwy*. Since that expression names Mathonwy rather than securely naming Math, it cannot by itself prove an independent story about Math’s father. **[LE/uncertain]**

#### 4. Later literary and antiquarian references

Dafydd ap Gwilym and other poets enumerate famous magicians, though variants such as *Maeth* cannot always safely be corrected to Math. Early-modern manuscript transcripts and dictionaries demonstrate continued textual knowledge, not continuous cult. John Davies quoted the Fourth Branch in his 1632 Welsh-Latin dictionary.

### Relevant corpora that do not attest Math

The following sources are indispensable for Celtic religion generally but do not securely name Math fab Mathonwy:

- Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* VI;
- Lucan, *Pharsalia* I and its scholia;
- the *Lebor Gabála Érenn*;
- *Cath Maige Tuired*;
- the *Dindshenchas*;
- *Táin Bó Cúailnge*;
- *Tochmarc Étaíne*;
- *Acallam na Senórach*;
- Gaulish inscriptions catalogued in the *Recueil des inscriptions gauloises*;
- Romano-British inscriptions in *Roman Inscriptions of Britain*;
- the Gundestrup cauldron;
- the Pillar of the Boatmen;
- the Coligny calendar.

The absence matters. These corpora cannot be combined to manufacture an ancient “pan-Celtic Math.” Similar names, animals, magical transformations, or sovereign figures do not constitute identity. **[NE]**

*Culhwch ac Olwen* and *Cad Goddeu* contain members of the family of Dôn—notably Gwydion, Amaethon, and Gofannon—but do not supply another developed account of Math comparable to the Fourth Branch. **[NE/related tradition]**

### Editions and translations

Important stages include:

- Lady Charlotte Guest’s bilingual publication and English translation, issued in the nineteenth century and later collected as *The Mabinogion*. It popularized the English collective title but sometimes employs Victorian euphemism and antiquarian interpretation.
- Ifor Williams, *Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi* (1930), long a foundational critical Welsh edition.
- Gwyn Jones and Thomas Jones, *The Mabinogion*.
- Patrick K. Ford, *The Mabinogi and Other Medieval Welsh Tales* and a separate edition of *Math uab Mathonwy*.
- Sioned Davies, *The Mabinogion* (Oxford, 2007), a major modern English translation.
- Ian Hughes, ed., *Math Uab Mathonwy: The Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi* (Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 2013), with Welsh text, introduction, and critical apparatus.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The primary text gives Math no abstract portfolio in the manner of an inscribed Roman or Near Eastern deity. His demonstrated spheres are:

- **sovereignty over Gwynedd;**
- **judgment within the royal household;**
- **transformative enchantment;**
- **knowledge carried by the wind;**
- **teaching magic to Gwydion, according to the Triads;**
- **fertility, sex, and generative transformation**, though not as a formally stated “fertility-god” portfolio;
- **name-giving, baptism, fostering, healing, land grants, and dynastic succession.**

“God of magic and sovereignty” is therefore a defensible modern summary of narrative functions but not an attested medieval title. **[SR/MR]**

### The foot-holder condition

The Middle Welsh is unusually forceful: Math could not be alive unless his two feet were *ymlyc croth morwyn*, “in the fold/lap of a maiden,” except when the tumult of war prevented it. **[SA]**

Interpretations include:

- a unexplained magical taboo or *geis*-like condition; **[SR]**
- symbolic dependence of kingship upon female sovereignty; **[SR]**
- a distorted memory of court ritual; **[SR]**
- sexualized royal idleness or bodily incapacity used to make the plot possible; **[literary interpretation]**
- an initiation or sacred-marriage rite; **[speculative]**.

No independent ritual text, law code, sculpture, or description of Welsh coronation confirms that historical rulers rested their feet in virgins’ laps.

### Powers and instruments

- **Wind-borne hearing:** even the smallest conversation becomes known to him if the wind meets it. **[SA]**
- **Magic wand:** changes Gwydion and Gilfaethwy into animal pairs and later restores them; transforms their animal young into boys. **[SA]**
- **Virginity test:** a wand or magical implement is placed so that Arianrhod must step over it; she immediately gives birth. The text reports the event but does not explain the mechanism. **[SA]**
- **Creation from flowers:** Math and Gwydion make Blodeuedd/Blodeuwedd from the flowers of oak, broom, and meadowsweet. **[SA]**
- **Naming and baptism:** Math names Arianrhod’s first child Dylan following the child’s emergence and movement toward the sea. **[SA]**
- **Healing patronage:** he receives the wounded Lleu at Caer Dathyl and summons Gwynedd’s best physicians; the text does not say Math personally performs the cure. **[SA]**
- **Limitations:** he cannot normally leave his foot-holder; he can be deceived into war; he neither prevents Pryderi’s death nor initially knows Lleu’s location. His knowledge and magic are extensive but not omniscient or omnipotent. **[SA]**

### Animals

The deer, wild boar, and wolf belong to the punishment of Gwydion and Gilfaethwy. Their alternating male and female forms are imposed instruments of sentence; the text never calls these animals sacred to Math. Modern lists of “Math’s sacred animals” convert narrative material into a devotional system. **[MA/MR]**

The pigs at the beginning of the story belong to Pryderi and derive from Arawn of Annwfn; they are not Math’s animals. **[SA]**

### Plants

Oak, broom, and meadowsweet are the substances used by Math and Gwydion to form Blodeuwedd. They are not explicitly called sacred plants of Math. **[SA versus MA]**

### Iconography

There is no securely identified pre-modern image of Math. **[NE]**

- The White and Red books are textual manuscripts; decorated initials are not portraits of Math.
- No Gaulish or Romano-British statue, relief, coin, or votive object bears his name.
- Figures on the Gundestrup cauldron cannot securely be identified as Math.
- Modern paintings, book illustrations, tarot-like images, gaming art, and devotional portraits commonly show an elderly white-haired magician, antlered king, Druid, or wand-bearing monarch. These are modern visual conventions. **[MA]**

No ancient color, number, weekday, celestial body, or fixed emblem is documented for him. Lists assigning such correspondences are modern occult constructions.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

### Genealogy

- **Father or parent named:** Mathonwy. Nothing further is securely said. **[SA]**
- **Sister:** unnamed in the Fourth Branch’s immediate kinship statement; because Gwydion and Gilfaethwy are “sons of Dôn” and Math’s nephews, Dôn is normally inferred to be Math’s sister. **[SA plus straightforward inference]**
- **Nephews:** Gwydion fab Dôn and Gilfaethwy fab Dôn. **[SA]**
- **Other children of Dôn:** Arianrhod, Gofannon, and Amaethon belong to the larger medieval Welsh family complex, but Math does not interact with all of them in surviving narrative.
- **Wife:** Goewin, whom Math marries after Gilfaethwy’s rape makes her unable to continue as his virgin foot-holder. The marriage is presented as elevating her and compensating for injury; her consent is not narrated. **[SA]**
- **Biological children:** none recorded. **[NE]**
- **Foster-sons:** Hyddwn, Hychdwn, and Bleiddwn, the three offspring born during Gwydion and Gilfaethwy’s animal transformations, whom Math converts into human boys and has baptized. **[SA]**
- **Dynastic successor:** Lleu Llaw Gyffes becomes lord of Gwynedd after the story’s final conflict; Math’s death or abdication is not narrated. **[SA]**

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or mythological being

The designation “divine” below does not mean the Fourth Branch explicitly calls every participant a god. It identifies figures treated as supernatural or pantheon-level beings in later Celtic mythography.

- **Mathonwy — parent-child relation only.** Math is named “son of Mathonwy”; no scene between them survives. Fourth Branch, composition probably eleventh–twelfth century, manuscripts c. 1350 and 1382–1400. **[SA]**
- **Dôn — sibling relation inferred, no meeting narrated.** Gwydion and Gilfaethwy are sons of Dôn and Math’s sister’s sons. Fourth Branch. **[SA/inference]**
- **Gwydion fab Dôn — ruler and deceptive nephew.** Gwydion hides Gilfaethwy’s desire, engineers the theft of Pryderi’s pigs and the war, and enables the assault on Goewin. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Gwydion — judge and condemned offender.** Math hears Goewin’s testimony, condemns Gwydion, transforms him successively into stag, sow, and wolf, takes his offspring, and eventually restores him. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Gwydion — magical teacher.** Math teaches Gwydion one of Britain’s three great enchantments. *Trioedd Ynys Prydein*, manuscript tradition medieval and multilayered. **[LE]**
- **Gwydion — collaborator.** Math and Gwydion test Arianrhod and jointly create Blodeuwedd. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Gilfaethwy fab Dôn — absent uncle and betrayed lord.** Gilfaethwy rapes Goewin on Math’s bed while Math is at war. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Gilfaethwy — judge and condemned offender.** Math transforms him successively into hind, boar, and she-wolf—opposite Gwydion’s sex in each year—then restores him. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Goewin ferch Pebin — lord and foot-holder.** She holds Math’s feet as the virgin upon whom his life-condition depends. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Goewin — recipient of testimony and subsequent husband.** She reports that Math’s nephews raped her and that they came upon her in Math’s chamber and bed; Math marries her and grants her authority over his dominions. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Pryderi fab Pwyll — opposing sovereign.** Math accepts Gwydion’s proposal to obtain Pryderi’s pigs and later leads Gwynedd’s army when Pryderi seeks restitution. He permits the dispute to be settled by combat between Gwydion and Pryderi. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Arawn — no direct interaction.** Arawn gave the otherworldly pigs to Pryderi’s father Pwyll; Math only hears Gwydion’s report about them. Fourth Branch. **[SA/absence of meeting]**
- **Arianrhod ferch Dôn — uncle and examiner.** Math considers her as a new foot-holder, asks whether she is a virgin, and makes her step over his magical implement; she gives birth. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Dylan ail Dôn — great-uncle, namer, and baptizer.** Math has the newborn baptized and names him Dylan after observing his sea-nature. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Lleu Llaw Gyffes — great-uncle and patron.** Math assists Gwydion in making Lleu a wife and grants Lleu the cantrefs of Dinoding and Eifionydd/Ardudwy territory according to the text’s geography. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Lleu — healer’s patron and sovereign judge.** After Gwydion restores the wounded eagle-Lleu to human form, Math provides physicians and supports Lleu’s demand for justice against Gronw. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Blodeuwedd/Blodeuedd — co-creator.** Math and Gwydion form her from flowers as Lleu’s wife after Arianrhod’s prohibition. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Blodeuwedd — no later direct confrontation.** Math is distressed by the attack on Lleu, but Gwydion—not Math—finds and transforms Blodeuwedd into an owl. Fourth Branch. **[SA/absence]**
- **Gronw Pebr — no face-to-face scene securely narrated.** Math learns of Gronw’s seizure of Lleu’s land and recognizes Lleu’s claim to justice; Gwydion and Lleu conduct the pursuit and vengeance. Fourth Branch. **[SA/absence]**
- **Hyddwn — foster-father.** Math converts the deer-born fawn into a human boy, names him Hyddwn, and has him fostered. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Hychdwn — foster-father.** Math converts the boar-born offspring into a human boy and names him Hychdwn. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Bleiddwn — foster-father.** Math converts the wolf-born offspring into a human boy and names him Bleiddwn. Fourth Branch. **[SA]**
- **Gofannon — poetic co-presence only.** “Math Hen” and Gofannon appear together in a list of skilled men; no action between them is recorded. Late medieval Taliesin/Gwion poetic tradition. **[LE]**
- **Amaethon — no documented interaction.** Both belong to the family complex surrounding the children of Dôn, but no surviving scene joins Math and Amaethon. **[NE]**
- **Manawydan, Brân, Branwen, Rhiannon, Pwyll, Efnisien, Nisien — no direct interactions.** They occupy other Branches; structural membership in the same work is not a meeting. **[NE]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycle

## The Fourth Branch

### I. The foot-holder and the theft of the pigs

**[SA]** Math rules Gwynedd from Caer Dathyl. His feet must remain in Goewin’s lap except in wartime. Because this prevents a normal royal circuit, his sister’s sons conduct it for him.

Gilfaethwy desires Goewin. Knowing Math can hear wind-touched whispers, he is initially afraid to speak; Gwydion nevertheless divines his condition. Gwydion promises to create a military mobilization that will remove Math.

Gwydion tells Math that a new creature—pigs—has entered Wales. Pryderi possesses them, having received them through Pwyll from Arawn, king of Annwfn. Gwydion goes south disguised as a bard. Since Pryderi has promised not to part with the pigs until they have bred twice, Gwydion manufactures twelve horses, twelve hunting dogs, shields, saddles, and bridles from mushrooms, illusion, and enchantment. Pryderi exchanges the pigs for these objects; the glamours subsequently collapse.

The geographic passages explain place-names through the pigs’ journey. These are medieval literary onomastics, not archaeological proof that the events occurred. **[SA as narrative; not history]**

### II. War, rape, and punishment

Pryderi pursues the thieves. Math mobilizes Gwynedd, thereby leaving Goewin. Gwydion and Gilfaethwy return to Caer Dathyl. Goewin later tells Math that they came into his chamber, forced her, and that the act occurred in his bed. Modern summaries sometimes attribute the rape only to Gilfaethwy because he desired her; the Welsh account’s plural accusation implicates both brothers in the assault, although Gilfaethwy is the intended sexual beneficiary. **[SA/textual nuance]**

Math and Pryderi’s forces meet. To prevent broader slaughter, Pryderi asks to fight Gwydion. Gwydion kills him through strength, valor, magic, and enchantment. Math did not order Pryderi’s death, but he consented to the single combat after joining the war produced by Gwydion’s deception.

When Math returns, Goewin says she can no longer be his foot-holder. Math declares that he will make reparation so far as he can and marries her, giving her authority over his realm. The tale does not record whether she wanted the marriage. It also does not restore her virginity or erase the injury.

Math then sentences Gwydion and Gilfaethwy to three years as breeding animal couples:

1. Gwydion as stag and Gilfaethwy as hind;
2. Gilfaethwy as boar and Gwydion as sow;
3. Gwydion as wolf and Gilfaethwy as she-wolf.

Each year they produce offspring. Math strikes each young animal with his wand, gives it human shape and an animal-derived name—Hyddwn, Hychdwn, Bleiddwn—and arranges baptism and fostering. The punishment imposes pregnancy, birth, sexual-role reversal, and animality upon the offenders. It is not evidence for a historical rite, though scholars have read it through initiation, shamanic transformation, gender reversal, and poetic metamorphosis. **[SA plus SR]**

### III. Arianrhod’s test and Dylan’s birth

Math needs another virgin. Gwydion proposes Arianrhod. Asked whether she is a virgin, she answers that she knows no reason she is not. Math makes her step over his wand or magical implement. She immediately drops a large, fully formed boy; as she flees, something smaller also falls, which Gwydion hides.

The first child goes toward the sea and takes its nature. Math says he will have him baptized and names him Dylan. The text’s Christian baptismal language is important evidence that the surviving tale is a medieval Christian composition or redaction, however old individual mythic components may be.

The second child is raised secretly by Gwydion. Math is not identified as its father. Older scholarship sometimes construed the test as a sexual union, fertility ritual, or proof of Math’s paternity, but the narrative does not say this. **[SR rejected as unproven]**

### IV. Lleu’s three maternal prohibitions

Arianrhod places three destinies or prohibitions upon the second child:

1. he shall have no name unless she gives it;
2. he shall bear no arms unless she gives them;
3. he shall never have a wife of the race living upon the earth.

Gwydion circumvents the first two by disguise. Arianrhod unknowingly names the boy Lleu Llaw Gyffes after admiring his skillful shot and unknowingly arms him when Gwydion creates the illusion of an attack.

Math’s direct role is greatest in overcoming the third prohibition. He tells Gwydion they must make a woman from flowers. From oak blossom, broom, and meadowsweet they fashion the woman Blodeuedd, “Flower-face/form,” and give her to Lleu.

No text says Math creates a soul, presides over a pagan marriage sacrament, or uses a historically attested Druid flower ritual. Such elements in later adaptations are additions. **[NE/MA]**

### V. Blodeuwedd, Gronw, and Lleu’s return

Lleu receives lands from Math and establishes a court. While Lleu is away, Blodeuwedd encounters Gronw Pebr. They become lovers and plan Lleu’s death. Because Lleu cannot easily be killed, Blodeuwedd coaxes him into explaining the unique circumstances:

- neither indoors nor outdoors;
- neither clothed nor naked;
- neither on horseback nor on foot;
- while standing with one foot on a bath’s rim and one on a buck;
- struck with a spear worked on only during Sunday Mass or sacrifice over the course of a year.

Gronw attacks under these conditions. Lleu becomes an eagle and disappears. News reaches Math, who is grieved, but Gwydion conducts the search. Following a sow, Gwydion finds the eagle above rotting flesh, sings three *englynion*, and restores Lleu with his wand. At Caer Dathyl, Math obtains physicians, and Lleu recovers over a year.

Gwydion transforms Blodeuwedd into an owl. Lleu kills Gronw by returning the spear-cast; the missile passes through a stone and breaks Gronw’s back. The tale concludes that Lleu thereafter rules Gwynedd.

The sometimes-repeated description of Lleu’s episode as a literal death and resurrection is interpretive. The text depicts catastrophic wounding, avian transformation, restoration, and healing; it never clearly says that he dies. **[SA versus SR]**

### Narrative development

Scholars have proposed that the Fourth Branch combines previously separate tales:

- the war over Pryderi’s pigs;
- Goewin’s rape and the animal punishment;
- Arianrhod and the births;
- Lleu’s naming and arming;
- the making of Blodeuwedd;
- Lleu’s wounding, transformation, and vengeance.

W. J. Gruffydd’s *Math vab Mathonwy* (1928) attempted to reconstruct older and Irish-derived mythic strata. Such source-dissection was influential but is not equivalent to recovering a lost original. Later literary scholarship gives more weight to the surviving Branch as an intentionally constructed whole. **[Historiographic dispute]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ancient cult

No securely documented cult of Math exists. **[NE]**

Specifically, no evidence presently identifies:

- a temple or shrine dedicated to Math;
- an altar carrying his name;
- a votive inscription;
- an image labeled as Math;
- a priest or priesthood of Math;
- a festival or calendar day;
- a sacrificial prescription;
- a prayer, hymn, oracle, or curse tablet addressed to him;
- an ancient theophoric personal name demonstrably formed from this divine Math;
- an ancient sanctuary at Caer Dathyl;
- human sacrifice offered to Math;
- temple prostitution;
- cult violence or political suppression carried out in his name.

The Fourth Branch describes magic, baptism, royal justice, war, rape, and manufactured life. It does not describe the worship of Math by characters within the story.

### Caer Dathyl and landscape

Caer Dathyl is Math’s court in Arfon, but its exact location is unknown. Sites proposed include hillforts or defended sites in the Nantlle/Arfon landscape. Craig y Dinas has been investigated as one candidate. A Gwynedd Archaeological Trust survey and excavation found little surviving occupation evidence beyond a small post-hole and later features; it did not establish that the site was Math’s historical court or sanctuary. **[archaeology plus NE]**

The story’s landscape is nevertheless geographically dense: Arfon, Ardudwy, Eifionydd, Nantlle, Traeth Mawr, the Cynfael, and other named places make the narrative a literary map of north and west Wales. Stones and place-names later associated with Gronw and Lleu are reception and landscape folklore, not inscriptions naming Math.

### Foot-holding as ritual

The lap condition has often been treated as a fossil of sacred kingship. No external evidence proves that it was practiced. Its ritual interpretation remains **[SR]**.

### Modern practice

Modern Druids and polytheists sometimes invoke Math in ritual. Ár nDraíocht Féin has published a “General Welsh Liturgy” containing the invocation “Math ap Mathonwy.” The Druid Network publishes the Fourth Branch in a religiously accessible setting. Contemporary teachers sell courses devoted to “working with” Math as a guide to sovereignty, magic, and self-mastery.

These are documented modern forms of reception and worship. They do not establish continuous transmission from pre-Christian Wales. **[MR]**

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Possible pre-literary background: before c. 1050

Names, transformation motifs, otherworldly livestock, magical prohibitions, and members of the family of Dôn may descend from earlier Brittonic traditions. Lleu is widely compared with Gaulish Lugus and Irish Lug, but this does not automatically make Math an ancient god or identify his functions. No securely datable pre-Christian evidence names Math. **[SR/NE]**

### Literary formation: c. 1050–1200

The Fourth Branch probably reached a substantial written form during this period. Its society mixes archaizing legendary geography with medieval courts, legal compensation, baptism, Sunday Mass, Christian oaths, and contemporary literary technique. The author or redactor was not simply transcribing a pagan scripture. **[SR grounded in text]**

### Manuscript attestation: fourteenth century

The White Book and Red Book give Math his first secure physical documentary record. Triads and poetry reinforce his identity as an archetypal enchanter. **[SA]**

### Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries

The manuscript tradition remained in Welsh learned and antiquarian custody. Copies were made as leaves deteriorated or disappeared. John Davies’s 1632 dictionary quotes from the Fourth Branch. This is textual preservation in Christian Welsh culture—not evidence of suppressed underground worship. **[SA]**

### Romantic and Victorian rediscovery: 1795–1900

William Owen Pughe helped introduce the collective term *Mabinogion*. Lady Charlotte Guest’s edition and translation made the tales accessible to a broad Anglophone readership. Comparative mythologists then classified Math as an ancient Celtic god, culture hero, Pluto, Zeus, or ritual sovereign. These theories shaped popular mythology but frequently outran the documentation. **[LS]**

### Comparative reconstruction: 1900–1960

John Rhŷs, W. J. Gruffydd, W. Y. Evans-Wentz, and the Reeses sought underlying Indo-European, Irish, seasonal, initiatory, or ritual structures. Gruffydd’s reconstruction of separate source tales was especially influential. Some comparisons remain suggestive; none supplies a missing inscription or cult record. **[SR/LS]**

### Literary and philological correction: later twentieth century onward

Critical editions, linguistic analysis, narrative study, gender criticism, manuscript work, and landscape archaeology increasingly distinguished:

- the surviving medieval composition;
- possible older motifs;
- nineteenth-century comparative hypotheses;
- contemporary creative reuse.

Math consequently appears more cautiously in current scholarship as a powerful legendary ruler whose divinity is possible but unproved.

### Present

Math survives through:

- editions and translations;
- Welsh-language education and cultural performance;
- fiction and visual art;
- Druid, pagan, and polytheist devotion;
- internet mythography, games, and commercial spiritual courses.

There is no reliable census of Math’s worshippers and no evidence of an unbroken hereditary cult. **[MR/NE]**

### Suppression and conversion

Christian vocabulary is embedded in the Fourth Branch itself: baptism, God-oaths, and the making of the fatal spear during Sunday religious observance. The manuscript was preserved by Christian scribes and institutions. No record describes a shrine of Math being destroyed, his priests being persecuted, or his worship being forcibly converted. General histories of Britain’s Christianization cannot be converted into a specific biography of Math’s cult. **[NE]**

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Publication and the Celtic Revival

Lady Charlotte Guest’s nineteenth-century *Mabinogion* transformed Math from a figure principally available to Welsh manuscript readers into part of an international “Celtic mythology.” Subsequent dictionaries by MacKillop, Green, Maier, Koch, and others systematized his family, powers, and presumed pantheon position. Their designation “god” is a modern taxonomic choice.

Yeats and Lady Gregory drew chiefly on Irish rather than Welsh materials; neither created a major Math-centered canonical work. Their wider Celtic Revival helped create the cultural market in which figures such as Math could be treated as members of one transnational Celtic pantheon. **[reception context]**

### Occultism and theosophy

Kenneth Morris’s “The Making of Blodeuwedd,” published in a Theosophical setting, calls Math the “Chief Enchanter of the Gods” and adds systematic divine offices and spiritual interpretation. These are twentieth-century elaborations.

Later British esoteric writing treated Math, Arianrhod, and other Welsh beings as “god-forms” representing fixed energies. Such correspondences are not medieval.

No significant Renaissance grimoire, Hermetic corpus, Golden Dawn initiation paper, or early modern magical manuscript is known to invoke Math. Claims that he belonged to a continuous Western mystery tradition are **[MA]**.

### Literature

- **Evangeline Walton’s Mabinogion tetralogy**, especially *The Island of the Mighty* and *The Song of Rhiannon*, reworks the Four Branches through twentieth-century fantasy, psychology, and feminist interpretation. **[MA]**
- **Alan Garner, *The Owl Service* (1967)** transposes the Blodeuwedd–Lleu–Gronw triangle into a recurring modern pattern. Math is background to the source myth rather than the novel’s principal active character. The book materially shaped modern reception of the Fourth Branch. **[MA]**
- Numerous Welsh plays, poems, children’s adaptations, fantasy novels, and visual works retell Blodeuwedd more frequently than Math himself.

### Neopagan and Druid reception

Modern practitioners variously understand Math as:

- god of magic;
- guardian of esoteric knowledge;
- sovereign or lawgiver;
- teacher of Gwydion;
- deity of transformation;
- ancestral Welsh power.

These roles extrapolate from the Fourth Branch. Published invocations demonstrate real contemporary religion, but “ancient correspondences” involving planets, ritual quarters, tarot cards, crystals, colors, or feast days are not historically attested unless a modern author explicitly devised them.

The claim that Math’s animal transformations make deer, boar, and wolf his sacred animals is a particularly clear modern inference. The source presents them as penal bodies, not cult symbols.

### Gender and sexuality

Recent readers have examined:

- Goewin’s rape and the failure of the narrative to provide her subjectivity after marriage;
- Math’s test of Arianrhod;
- enforced pregnancy and alternating sex in the animal punishment;
- the manufactured woman Blodeuwedd and her punishment;
- the control of names, weapons, marriage, and reproduction by male magicians.

Queer, transgender, feminist, psychoanalytic, and trauma-centered readings are legitimate modern interpretations. They should not be passed off as a recovered ancient doctrine of gender.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No prominent, specifically documented extremist cult of Math emerged from the searched evidence. Broader Celtic imagery has been used by nationalist, racialist, and esoteric movements, but attributing those movements specifically to Math without evidence would be misleading. **[NE]**

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Math a god?

**Argument for:** supernatural abilities, kinship with the children of Dôn, his teacher-creator role, his extraordinary bodily condition, and the likely mythic character of other Fourth Branch figures suggest a divine or formerly divine background. **[SR]**

**Argument against certainty:** the surviving text calls him lord, not god; no pre-Christian inscription or image names him; no worship is depicted; medieval Welsh narrative freely attributes magic to kings and heroes. **[SA/NE]**

**Finding:** “Probable mythological being, possibly derived from a pre-Christian deity” is defensible. “Securely attested ancient Celtic god with a known cult” is not.

### Dating

The linguistic and historical debate spans dates before 1100 through the late twelfth century. Andrew Breeze’s 1120s–1130s proposal is a contribution, not settled consensus. The surviving manuscript date must always be distinguished from the hypothesized composition date.

### Unity versus compilation

- Gruffydd treated the Fourth Branch as an assembly of older narrative units and searched especially for Irish parallels.
- Later scholars have emphasized deliberate composition, thematic linkage, legal language, geography, repetition, and the relation of all four Branches.
- Both can be partly true: inherited tales may have been shaped into a sophisticated medieval work. What cannot be recovered with certainty is the form of any hypothetical pagan original.

### Irish parallels

The animal transformations, otherworld pigs, divine family patterns, Lleu/Lug comparison, and proposed name cognates encouraged Irish-source theories. Resemblance does not prove direct borrowing, and Math himself has no uncontested Irish counterpart.

### The foot-holder

Proposals range from ritual kingship and sexual sovereignty to narrative grotesque and bodily taboo. There is no external ritual evidence capable of choosing decisively among them.

### Ethics and law

Math’s response to Goewin combines recognition of grave injury, marriage, transfer of authority, and spectacular punishment of offenders. Whether this represents medieval Welsh legal compensation, a literary ideal, or mythic sovereignty remains debated. The text’s violence should not be euphemized: Goewin describes coercive sexual violation, and Math’s sentence imposes animalization, compelled mating, pregnancy, and separation from the offspring.

### Primary evidence and repositories

- **White Book:** National Library of Wales, Peniarth MS 4.
- **Red Book:** Oxford, Jesus College MS 111, Bodleian-associated college collection.
- **Book of Taliesin:** National Library of Wales, Peniarth MS 2.
- **Later transcripts and Lady Charlotte Guest papers:** National Library of Wales.

The loss of earlier exemplars prevents direct examination of the narrative before the surviving medieval copies.

### Discredited or unproved theories still circulating

- Math was definitely worshipped at a particular surviving hillfort. **Unproved.**
- The Gundestrup cauldron depicts Math. **Unproved.**
- Math is securely the Welsh Zeus, Pluto, Dagda, Nuadu, Odin, or Merlin. **Comparative speculation.**
- The foot-holder was a known historical Druid ritual. **Unproved.**
- Mathonwy means “Bear of the Moving Waters.” **Modern folk etymology.**
- Math’s deer, boar, and wolf are ancient sacred animals. **Modern extrapolation.**
- The Fourth Branch is an unchanged Iron Age scripture. **Contradicted by its manuscript history and Christian-medieval vocabulary.**
- Math is Arianrhod’s children’s biological father. **Not stated.**
- Blodeuwedd is made from nine flowers. The surviving Fourth Branch names three kinds—oak, broom, and meadowsweet; expanded lists are later. **[MA]**
- Lleu unambiguously dies and resurrects. The text more precisely describes wounding, eagle-transformation, restoration, and healing. **[interpretive overstatement]**

### Evidentiary conclusion

Math’s complete reconstructable record is unusually concentrated. A sophisticated medieval Welsh tale and several learned allusions securely establish a lord of Gwynedd renowned for magic, judgment, bodily taboo, transformation, creation, and dynastic authority. They do not securely establish an ancient temple god, a pan-Celtic identity, a formal cult, or an unbroken devotional tradition. The modern god of magic and sovereignty is not wholly invented—he is built from real medieval narrative functions—but his divine portfolio, symbols, correspondences, ritual calendar, and contemporary worship are modern reconstructions.
