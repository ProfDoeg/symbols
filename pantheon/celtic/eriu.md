# Ériu: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess/divine woman and eponymous personification of Ireland in medieval Irish learned literature; more cautiously, an otherworldly female figure identified with the land and its sovereignty.  
**Earliest secure attestation as a divine person:** Early medieval Irish literary material, probably attested from the ninth century onward, although the principal surviving witnesses are considerably later.  
**Earliest evidence for the name of Ireland:** Classical geographical forms related to *Ierne/Iverna/Hibernia* precede the medieval goddess narratives, but do not attest a goddess named Ériu.  
**Cultic status:** No inscription, temple, priesthood, votive deposit, festival, or securely identified pre-Christian image can presently be assigned to Ériu.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Securely attested in the earliest recoverable stratum of an Irish text, normally medieval rather than pre-Christian.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** Part of the developed medieval mythographic tradition, especially *Lebor Gabála Érenn*.
- **[Regional/textual variant]** A version peculiar to a recension, manuscript, locality, or related text.
- **[Learned systematization]** Genealogy or pantheon-building imposed by medieval scholars.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An analytical category or proposed prehistoric interpretation, not an indigenous statement preserved verbatim.
- **[Modern reception]** Literary, political, artistic, or religious reuse after the medieval period.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim presented as ancient for which no ancient or medieval source has been identified.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Ériu is simultaneously the Old and Middle Irish name of Ireland and, in medieval narrative, a named woman of the Túatha Dé Danann who embodies or represents the island. In *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (“The Book of the Taking of Ireland”), she and her sisters Banba and Fódla confront the incoming sons of Míl. Each asks that the island bear her name; the poet and judge Amergin promises the honor, but Ériu receives permanent primacy. In *Cath Maige Tuired* (“The Battle of Mag Tuired”), Ériu is instead principally the daughter of Delbáeth who meets the Fomorian Elatha and conceives Bres. These are related but not completely harmonized personae.

### Name forms

- **Old Irish:** *Ériu*, nominative; *Érenn*, genitive; *Érinn* or *Érind*, accusative/dative. The word is a feminine n-stem.
- **Middle Irish:** *Ériu*, with spelling variants including *Eriu*, *Eri*, and *Hériu*.
- **Modern Irish:** *Éire*, “Ireland.”
- **Scottish Gaelic:** *Èirinn*.
- **Manx:** *Nerin*, probably incorporating a fossilized preceding nasal from “in Ireland.”
- **English poetic form:** *Erin*, derived from an oblique Irish form rather than from the old nominative *Ériu*.
- **Related Brittonic name:** Welsh *Iwerddon*, “Ireland.” This is a linguistic relative of the island-name, not evidence for a Welsh goddess corresponding to Ériu.
- **Classical forms:** Greek *Iernē/Iouernia* and Latin *Iverna/Hibernia* belong to the history of the island-name. Classical authors do not describe a goddess Ériu.

The relevant Old Irish paradigm and its modern descendants are summarized in the linguistic entry for [Ériu](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%C3%89riu), which cites Karin Stüber’s study of Celtic n-stems. The online Dictionary of the Irish Language covers Irish materials from approximately 700–1700; CELT describes that lexicographical scope [here](https://celt.ucc.ie/digineen.html).

### Etymology

Two questions must be separated:

1. What is the history of the island-name?
2. Was the goddess originally the source of the name, or was a personified goddess created from an already existing place-name?

T. F. O’Rahilly’s “On the Origin of the Names Érainn and Ériu” remains a major treatment of the relationship between *Ériu*, the ethnonym *Érainn*, Ptolemy’s *Iverni*, and related forms. The article appeared in *Ériu* 14 (1946), pp. 7–28; its bibliographic record is [available through JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/30007646). O’Rahilly reconstructed an earlier *Iveriu*-type form and associated the country-name with the Érainn.

Later comparative accounts commonly reconstruct something like Proto-Celtic *\*Īwerjū* or *\*Φīwerjū*, ultimately connected by some linguists with an Indo-European root signifying “fat” or “fertile.” The comparison often includes Greek *píeira*, “fertile, rich.” The phonological details—especially the initial vowel of *Ériu*—remain disputed. Therefore “Ériu means fertile land” should be labeled **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**, not a translation supplied by any medieval Irish text.

The proposition that *Éire* derives from Sanskrit *ārya*, “noble,” or that Ireland and Iran share their names through an “Aryan” identity is **[modern invention/false etymology]**. It is incompatible with the securely attested Celtic and classical sequence of the name.

### Ériu and the Érainn

The Érainn were a historically important early Irish population-group, particularly associated in genealogical and saga traditions with Munster and with Clanna Dedad. An older scholarly model derived both *Érainn* and *Ériu* from *Ivéri*-type forms. The CELT edition of Eoin MacNeill’s population-group study gives the proposed development “older *Ivéri*, whence *Ivériu*, Ériu, Iwerddon” [in its discussion of the Érainn](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/E900000-003/text001.html).

This linguistic relationship does not establish that the Érainn worshipped a goddess Ériu, nor that Ériu was originally their tribal deity. No inscription or cult record supplies that missing link.

### Titles and classification

No ancient cult epithet securely belonging to Ériu survives. Descriptions such as “goddess of Ireland,” “land goddess,” “mother goddess,” “matron goddess,” and “sovereignty goddess” are predominantly modern scholarly classifications.

“**Sovereignty goddess**” is useful because Ériu personifies the territory, confronts conquerors, predicts possession of the land, and secures the perpetuation of her name. Nevertheless, it is not an epithet used of her in the surviving narrative. Recent scholarship warns that the category can collapse distinct roles—land, queen, kingmaker, mother of a future king—into one hypothetical prehistoric figure. Thomas Charles-Edwards and Gregory Toner’s broader discussion emphasizes that a literary sovereignty goddess cannot automatically be treated as a transparent survival of a pre-Christian cult figure; see [Manifestations of Sovereignty in Medieval Ireland](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/publications/Chadwick/HMC%20Vol%2029%202018%20Toner.pdf).

### Alleged equivalents

There is no documented ancient interpretatio identifying Ériu with:

- a Gaulish goddess;
- Britannia;
- a Welsh goddess such as Rhiannon;
- Danu, Anu, the Morrígan, Macha, Brigid, or Tailtiu;
- a Greek or Roman earth goddess.

Comparisons with Gaia, Demeter, sovereignty figures, or Mother Ireland are typological or modern, not historically attested syncretisms.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The decisive chronological distinction

The island-name is older than the surviving goddess narratives. Classical geographers knew forms related to *Iverna/Ierne*, and Ptolemy recorded the *Iverni*. Those forms establish the antiquity of the geographical and ethnonymic word-family; they do not show that people worshipped an eponymous goddess.

Síle Ní Mhurchú’s modern reference study states that the eponymous goddess appears in Irish literature “from the ninth century onward” and that *Lebor Gabála* gives primacy to Ériu among the three land-goddesses; see [“Ériu” in the Wiley-Blackwell Encyclopedia of Literature](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118396957.wbemlb568). Because the earliest relevant texts survive in later manuscripts and underwent revision, “ninth century” normally describes a recoverable textual stratum or estimated composition, not a ninth-century autograph.

### *Cath Maige Tuired*

**Language:** predominantly Middle Irish as transmitted, incorporating older linguistic material.  
**Probable formation:** composite; often assigned in something like its surviving form to the eleventh or twelfth century, with portions traceable to ninth-century material.  
**Sole substantial witness:** British Library, Harley MS 5280, fols. 63r–70v, copied in the early sixteenth century.

The manuscript catalogue confirms the location of *Cath Maige Tuired* in [Harley MS 5280](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002051125). Elizabeth A. Gray’s critical edition and translation, *Cath Maige Tuired: The Second Battle of Mag Tuired* (Irish Texts Society, 1982), is reproduced by [CELT](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/T300010/). CELT also preserves Whitley Stokes’s older translation, [The Second Battle of Moytura](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/T300011/).

Ériu’s episode occurs near the beginning:

- Ériu, daughter of Delbáeth and a woman of the Túath Dé, looks out from the house of Máeth Scéni.
- A splendid man arrives over a supernaturally calm sea in a silver vessel.
- He identifies himself as Elatha son of Delbáeth, a Fomorian king.
- They unite sexually.
- Before departing he gives her a ring and foretells the birth of a son.
- She bears Bres, who later becomes king of the Túatha Dé Danann.

This is Ériu’s most substantial independent narrative role. The episode makes her the mother through whom the half-Túath Dé, half-Fomorian Bres can be installed as a compromise king. Her connection with sovereignty is therefore dynastic and maternal, not merely territorial.

### *Lebor Gabála Érenn*

**Language:** Middle Irish, with verse of varying dates and extensive Latin-Christian learned material.  
**Formation:** a developing pseudo-history rather than a single composition; its major structure probably took shape between the ninth and eleventh centuries.  
**Surviving witnesses:** five principal recension families in more than a dozen medieval and early-modern manuscripts. The first recension survives in the Book of Leinster, c. 1150, and the later Book of Fermoy; other versions occur in the Book of Ballymote and Great Book of Lecan.

The multiplicity of recensions matters: there is no single invariant *Lebor Gabála*. The textual situation is summarized [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebor_Gab%C3%A1la_%C3%89renn), while the Book of Leinster text is available in the [CELT diplomatic edition](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/G800011C/). R. A. S. Macalister’s five-volume Irish Texts Society edition remains indispensable but has been strongly criticized for conflating readings and for unreliable translations in places. It should not be treated as a pristine “Celtic Bible.”

Ériu appears in three principal capacities:

1. **Genealogical figure:** one of a triad with Banba and Fódla.
2. **Queen:** wife of Mac Gréine, one of the three last Túatha Dé kings.
3. **Land-personification:** she meets the sons of Míl and asks that Ireland be named for her.
4. **Casualty of conquest:** in at least one developed version, Suirge kills Ériu in the final battle.

The naming encounter is **[later canonical elaboration]**:

- Banba meets the invaders and asks that the island bear her name.
- Fódla makes the same request.
- Ériu meets them at Uisneach and likewise requests the honor.
- Amergin promises it and judges that Ériu’s name will be the island’s principal name, while Banba and Fódla remain poetic alternatives.
- Ériu favors or prophesies the invaders’ future possession, though recensions differ in wording and detail.

An online transcription derived from Macalister’s edition preserves the relevant narrative and the concluding deaths of the three kings and queens in [The Lebor Gabala Erren](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/celtic/irish/lebor.html). It is useful for consultation but is not a substitute for the printed critical apparatus.

### Other Irish corpora

The *Dindshenchas*, *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, *Tochmarc Étaíne*, and *Acallam na Senórach* are crucial for reconstructing Irish concepts of land, sovereignty, divine women, and the Túatha Dé. They do not, however, supply a comparably substantial independent Ériu cycle.

The Book of Leinster preserves a catalogue of prominent Túatha Dé women naming “Eriu, Fotla, and Banba” and associating them with the sons of Cermait; see the [CELT Book of Leinster text](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/G800011C/). Such catalogues are evidence for medieval mythographic systematization, not for a documented temple pantheon.

### Welsh, Gaulish, and Romano-British material

No Ériu appears in:

- the Four Branches of the *Mabinogi*;
- *Culhwch ac Olwen*;
- the *Book of Taliesin*;
- Caesar’s *Bellum Gallicum* VI;
- Lucan’s *Pharsalia* I or its scholia;
- the Gaulish epigraphic corpus *Recueil des inscriptions gauloises*;
- the *Roman Inscriptions of Britain*;
- the Gundestrup cauldron;
- the Pillar of the Boatmen;
- the Coligny calendar.

These sources establish broader Celtic religious contexts but cannot be entered as evidence for Ériu herself. Connecting a nameless female image on the Gundestrup cauldron to Ériu would be **[modern invention]**.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Securely textual domains

- **Ireland itself:** Ériu’s name and the country-name are identical in Old Irish.
- **Territorial legitimacy:** she negotiates with incoming rulers and secures her name in the new political order.
- **Sovereignty and succession:** her son Bres becomes king; her encounter with the Milesians frames conquest as requiring acknowledgment by the land.
- **Prophecy:** in *Lebor Gabála*, she speaks about the invaders’ future possession and her name’s endurance.
- **Dynastic mediation:** in *Cath Maige Tuired*, she connects Túath Dé and Fomorian lineages through Bres.

### Form

The texts present Ériu as an anthropomorphic woman. *Cath Maige Tuired* does not describe her as a hag, giantess, animal, triple-bodied figure, or armed warrior. The famous “loathly lady” who becomes beautiful after recognizing the rightful king belongs to other Irish sovereignty narratives. Applying that physical transformation to Ériu is **[modern scholarly conflation]** unless a particular retelling explicitly does so.

### Symbols and correspondences

No early source assigns Ériu:

- a sacred animal;
- a sacred plant;
- a color;
- a number;
- a weekday;
- a planet or constellation;
- a weapon;
- a festival;
- a standardized emblem.

Modern associations with green, shamrock, harp, horse, cow, raven, sovereignty cup, sun, or earth are either national symbols, comparisons with other figures, or contemporary devotional choices. None is an attested personal attribute of Ériu.

### Iconography

There is no securely identified ancient or medieval image of Ériu. She has no diagnostic premodern iconographic type. A female figure labeled “Ériu,” “Éire,” “Hibernia,” or “Erin” in modern art is an allegorical reception of Ireland, not proof of ancient iconography.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Competing genealogies

#### Version A: *Cath Maige Tuired*

**[Early attested literary tradition]**

- Father: Delbáeth.
- People: Túath Dé.
- Lover: Elatha son of Delbáeth, a Fomorian king.
- Son: Bres.

The recurrence of Delbáeth in the genealogies is unstable and may reflect homonymous figures or mythographic compression.

#### Version B: *Lebor Gabála Érenn*

**[Learned systematization/later canonical elaboration]**

- Sisters: Banba and Fódla.
- Father: Fiachna son of Delbáeth in an important recension.
- Mother: Ernmas.
- Husband: Mac Gréine, also called Cethor, one of three sons of Cermait.
- Brothers-in-law: Mac Cuill/Sethor, husband of Banba; Mac Cecht/Tethor, husband of Fódla.

Other passages simplify the women as daughters of Delbáeth. The contradiction should not be silently repaired: *Cath Maige Tuired* directly calls Ériu “Delbáeth’s daughter,” while developed *Lebor Gabála* genealogies may insert Fiachna between her and Delbáeth.

The widely repeated statement “Ériu was the daughter of Delbáeth and Ernmas” is therefore a harmonized modern family tree assembled from inconsistent medieval notices.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The conception and recognition of Bres

**Earliest source:** *Cath Maige Tuired*.

Ériu watches the sea from Máeth Scéni’s house. The water becomes unnaturally level, and a silver vessel approaches. Its occupant is richly equipped and extraordinarily beautiful. He and Ériu become lovers. When he prepares to leave, she weeps because she does not know his identity; he tells her he is Elatha son of Delbáeth, gives her a ring, and predicts that their son must not be named until the ring fits him.

Ériu bears the boy Eochaid Bres—usually simply Bres—whose rapid growth and beauty are emphasized. He later becomes king because Nuada’s mutilation disqualifies Nuada from sovereignty, and Bres’s mixed descent appears capable of reconciling the Túatha Dé and Fomorians. His oppressive reign and eventual downfall drive the main political action of the battle tale.

The episode’s function is dynastic: Ériu is the maternal hinge between antagonistic peoples. Calling the meeting a formal sacred marriage rite is **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**; the text presents a supernatural sexual encounter and succession narrative, not a documented public ritual.

### The three goddesses and the naming of Ireland

**Principal source:** *Lebor Gabála Érenn*.

As the Milesians travel toward Tara, they encounter Banba, Fódla, and Ériu in turn, each associated with a mountain or central place and each married to one of the three reigning sons of Cermait. Each asks that Ireland be named after her. Amergin grants all three requests in some sense, but judges Ériu’s claim superior. Consequently:

- *Ériu/Éire* becomes the normal name;
- *Banba* and *Fódla* survive as poetic names.

The narrative converts conquest into a negotiated transfer of territorial legitimacy. The invaders cannot simply possess an anonymous tract of land: they must meet its female embodiments, accept their claims, and preserve their names.

The story does not prove a historical coronation ritual in which Irish kings married a priestess representing Ériu. The relation between narrative and ritual remains conjectural.

### Ériu’s death

In a developed *Lebor Gabála* battle list, the Milesians kill the three Túatha Dé kings and their wives:

- Mac Cecht by Érimón;
- Mac Cuill by Éber Finn;
- Mac Gréine by Amergin;
- Ériu by Suirge;
- Banba by Caicher;
- Fódla by Étan.

This is a striking counterweight to modern portrayals of Ériu as eternally enthroned. The same mythographic tradition can both kill the divine eponym and preserve her perpetually as the island’s name. The account is available in the online [Macalister-derived transcription](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/celtic/irish/lebor.html).

### What is not an Ériu myth

There is no early text in which Ériu:

- creates Ireland;
- rules it alone from the beginning of time;
- fights Roman invaders;
- carries a magical harp;
- transforms into a white mare;
- establishes a festival at Uisneach;
- marries every high king;
- forms a fixed triple goddess with the Morrígan and Brigid;
- is identical with Danu;
- is solar, lunar, or planetary;
- survives Christian persecution as a named saint.

These are modern expansions, confusions with other figures, or unsupported extrapolations.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Negative material record

No securely attested evidence identifies:

- a temple of Ériu;
- an altar or dedicatory inscription to her;
- a priestess or priest of Ériu;
- a sacrificial victim offered to Ériu;
- a festival bearing her name;
- a sacred spring, grove, or mountain explicitly dedicated to her in pre-Christian evidence;
- a theophoric personal name demonstrably formed from the goddess rather than the country;
- a Romano-Celtic equation;
- a votive image.

Uisneach is important in Irish political and mythological geography, and *Lebor Gabála* situates Ériu’s meeting with the Milesians there in some versions. That literary localization does not establish an archaeologically documented cult of Ériu at the site.

### Kingship ritual

Medieval Irish literature contains many unions between rulers and female embodiments of territory or sovereignty. Scholars have inferred rituals of inauguration, sacred marriage, drink-giving, or symbolic union with the land. For Ériu specifically, the evidence is literary and indirect. No ritual manual says that an Irish king married or copulated with Ériu.

The category remains debated. The study [“The law of succession established by Eochaid Fedlech and its implications for the theme of the Irish sovereignty goddess”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ecelt_0373-1928_2016_num_42_1_2473) argues that scholarship may need to distinguish the king’s wife from the mother of a succeeding king rather than treating all female sovereignty roles as one goddess-pattern.

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

Classical authors describe or allege human sacrifice among some continental Celtic peoples. Nothing in those passages names Ériu. No Irish archaeological victim can be securely identified as a sacrifice to her. Claims that Ériu demanded royal blood, human sacrifice, or the ritual death of kings are **[unsupported modern generalization]**.

There is likewise no evidence for temple prostitution, an organized warrior cult, or coercive conversion carried out in Ériu’s name.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the medieval texts

The place-name underlying *Ériu* is old and appears in forms known to classical geography. A corresponding prehistoric goddess is possible but not directly demonstrable. The evidence cannot decide whether:

1. a goddess’s name became the island-name;
2. the island-name was personified as a woman;
3. both arose from an older territorial term;
4. medieval authors systematized several inherited personifications into the triad Ériu–Banba–Fódla.

### c. ninth–eleventh centuries

Ériu becomes textually visible in Irish learned literature. The Bres episode and invasion traditions probably contain material of different dates. Christian scholars preserve, reshape, euhemerize, and synchronize native narratives with biblical and universal history.

It is misleading to describe this simply as “Christian suppression.” Ériu survives because Christian manuscript-makers copied and reorganized her stories. At the same time, their works do not give access to an untouched pagan canon.

### Twelfth–sixteenth centuries

The Book of Leinster, Book of Ballymote, Great Book of Lecan, Harley 5280, and related manuscripts transmit increasingly elaborate genealogies. Ériu becomes fixed within a learned Túatha Dé pantheon as:

- sister of Banba and Fódla;
- wife of Mac Gréine;
- ancestral mother of Bres in the separate battle tradition;
- eponym of Ireland.

The contradictory genealogies demonstrate active mythographic construction rather than a stable canonical family tree.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

Micheál Ó Cléirigh and other antiquarian scholars compile later recensions from medieval sources. Geoffrey Keating further organizes Irish pseudo-history. English-language antiquarianism increasingly treats Ériu as an identifiable “Celtic goddess,” often smoothing away recension differences.

### Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries

Nationalist and Revival-era writers transform medieval territorial personifications into modern Mother Ireland. Ériu’s specific narratives are less prominent than related figures such as Cathleen Ní Houlihan, the Shan Van Vocht, Hibernia, and Erin, but all participate in the same gendered political symbolism.

The Irish Literary Revival, led by figures including W. B. Yeats and Lady Gregory, deliberately employed myth and folklore in constructing a national literature; an overview is provided by [Ask About Ireland](https://askaboutireland.ie/reading-room/history-heritage/folklore-of-ireland/Folklore-of-ireland/anglo-irish-literary-rena/). Yeats and Gregory’s *Cathleen ni Houlihan* presents Ireland as an old woman who becomes young through nationalist sacrifice. That character is not Ériu under an ancient alternate name, but a modern nationalist relation of the broader land-woman tradition. RTÉ summarizes the play’s political reception [here](https://www.rte.ie/centuryireland/articles/literature-and-1916).

### 1937 to present

Article 4 of the 1937 Constitution declares: “The name of the State is Éire, or, in the English language, Ireland.” The official constitutional wording is preserved [here](https://theconstitution.ie/articles/4). This is a legal use of the Irish country-name, not establishment of a state cult or official recognition of the goddess.

Modern Gaelic polytheists, reconstructionists, Druids, Wiccans, and independent practitioners sometimes honor Ériu as:

- the living spirit of Ireland;
- a land or sovereignty goddess;
- one member of the Ériu–Banba–Fódla triad;
- an ancestral or tutelary being;
- an ecological symbol.

These practices are real contemporary religion but not demonstrably continuous from pre-Christian worship. Their rites—offerings of water, whiskey, grain, poetry, soil, or environmental service—are modern devotional constructions unless practitioners explicitly adapt a documented medieval episode.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### National allegory

“Erin,” “Hibernia,” “Mother Ireland,” and “Cathleen Ní Houlihan” became female national personifications. They inherit Ériu’s fundamental equivalence between woman and territory but possess distinct modern histories.

The Revival’s use of myth helped create cultural memory and national literature. Lady Gregory’s retellings made mythological material accessible in English, while Yeats joined Irish folklore to occult and Symbolist interests. Neither provides evidence for Ériu’s ancient cult.

### Occultism and modern paganism

Ériu is not a significant named figure in the foundational ritual systems of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the major early modern grimoires. Claims that an ancient “sigil of Ériu,” planetary seal, ceremonial color scale, or invocation survived through such orders are **[modern invention]** unless accompanied by a demonstrable manuscript source.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century Pagan literature often expands her into a universal earth mother or assigns fixed correspondences. These are legitimate modern religious interpretations but should be labeled as such.

### Political appropriation

Ériu’s name can serve inclusive cultural nationalism, linguistic revival, territorial nationalism, environmental activism, or romantic ethnic identity. No evidence supports an inherently racial doctrine attached to the medieval goddess. Attempts to connect Ériu etymologically to “Aryan” identity or to use her as proof of an unbroken ethnically exclusive prehistoric religion are pseudo-historical.

### Popular culture

Modern fantasy, role-playing games, spiritual websites, artwork, and commercial products often depict Ériu as:

- a green-clad red-haired queen;
- a warrior with spear or sword;
- a triple goddess;
- an earth mother accompanied by deer, ravens, horses, or cattle;
- a literal ruler of the Túatha Dé.

These visual and narrative conventions are modern. The medieval sources supply womanhood, kinship, prophecy, territorial identity, motherhood of Bres, and marriage to Mac Gréine—but no standardized costume, weapon, animal, or physiognomy.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Ériu an ancient goddess or a medieval personification?

The most economical textual observation is that *Ériu* means Ireland and the narratives make Ireland into a woman. Whether that woman preserves an independently worshipped pre-Christian deity remains unresolved.

- **Continuity-oriented reading:** the stories preserve an indigenous land or sovereignty goddess later euhemerized by Christian scribes.
- **Literary-personification reading:** medieval writers anthropomorphized an ancient country-name and incorporated the resulting woman into learned genealogy.
- **Intermediate reading:** inherited divine or mythic material was substantially rewritten through medieval personification, pseudo-history, and dynastic ideology.

The first possibility is plausible but cannot be confirmed by cult evidence.

### “Sovereignty goddess” debate

Older comparativist scholarship often reconstructed a single goddess who personified the land, offered drink or sex to the rightful king, became young or beautiful upon his acceptance, and caused prosperity. This model remains illuminating but can become circular: every powerful woman is classified as a sovereignty goddess, and then their differences are used as evidence of a single underlying goddess.

Ériu unquestionably has territorial and succession functions. She does not, in her own surviving episodes, enact every element of the reconstructed complex.

### Genealogical contradiction

*Cath Maige Tuired* calls Ériu daughter of Delbáeth. *Lebor Gabála* can call her a daughter of Fiachna son of Delbáeth, with Ernmas as mother, while popular summaries frequently combine all notices. This contradiction is evidence of separate textual traditions and later systematization.

### Érainn and Ériu

O’Rahilly’s analysis linked the ethnonym and island-name and embedded the question in his larger prehistoric migration model. The linguistic relationship remains important; the sweeping historical population schemes associated with O’Rahilly have not all retained scholarly acceptance. Language history does not by itself establish tribal worship.

### Macalister’s edition

R. A. S. Macalister’s *Lebor Gabála Érenn* edition and translation opened the text to modern readers but combines variant materials and contains disputed readings. CELT’s bibliographical discussion records severe scholarly criticism, including Myles Dillon’s judgment that the edition failed to provide a fully reliable text and translation; see [CELT’s Lebor Gabála guide](https://celt.ucc.ie/LGA.pdf). Claims depending on a single English sentence in Macalister should therefore be checked against the Irish and recension apparatus.

### State of the evidence

Surviving evidence is manuscript-centered:

- **Book of Leinster**, Trinity College Dublin MS 1339, c. 1160;
- **Book of Ballymote**, Royal Irish Academy MS 23 P 12, late fourteenth century;
- **Great Book of Lecan**, Royal Irish Academy MS 23 P 2, early fifteenth century;
- **Harley MS 5280**, British Library, early sixteenth century;
- other recension witnesses and fragments in Irish collections.

The Book of the Dun Cow is the oldest surviving manuscript written wholly in Irish; the Royal Irish Academy’s digitization describes its eleventh–twelfth-century construction and later restoration [here](https://www.isos.dias.ie/RIA/RIA_MS_23_E_25.html). Its importance for Irish literary transmission should not be mistaken for direct evidence of Ériu’s cult.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Antiquity, before the Common Era | Greek geographical forms related to *Ierne* attest an old name for Ireland; no goddess is described. |
| Second century CE | Ptolemaic geography records *Iouernia* and the *Iverni*, relevant to the later Ériu/Érainn debate. |
| Fifth–seventh centuries | Christian literacy and manuscript culture develop in Ireland. No contemporary inscription or cult record names Ériu as a goddess. |
| Ninth century onward | Earliest recoverable literary presence of an eponymous female Ériu, according to modern textual scholarship. |
| Ninth–eleventh centuries | Materials incorporated into *Cath Maige Tuired* and *Lebor Gabála Érenn* develop in learned Irish circles. |
| Eleventh–twelfth centuries | *Lebor Gabála* reaches major composite forms; *Cath Maige Tuired* is probably compiled from older materials. |
| c. 1160 | Book of Leinster preserves a major first-recension *Lebor Gabála* text and learned lists of Túatha Dé women. |
| Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries | Book of Ballymote and Great Book of Lecan transmit later recension families and expanded genealogies. |
| Early sixteenth century | Harley MS 5280 supplies the sole substantial witness of the second *Cath Maige Tuired*, including Ériu and Elatha’s conception of Bres. |
| 1631 | Micheál Ó Cléirigh and collaborators compile a late *Lebor Gabála* recension from multiple manuscripts. |
| Nineteenth century | Antiquarian editions and nationalist uses popularize “Erin” and Ireland-as-woman. |
| 1891 | Whitley Stokes publishes *The Second Battle of Moytura* in *Revue Celtique*. |
| 1890s–1920s | Irish Revival writers reshape medieval myth and female national personification for modern cultural nationalism. |
| 1937 | Article 4 of the Constitution makes *Éire* the Irish-language name of the state. |
| 1938–1956 | Macalister publishes his five-part edition and translation of *Lebor Gabála Érenn*. |
| 1946 | T. F. O’Rahilly publishes “On the Origin of the Names Érainn and Ériu.” |
| 1982 | Elizabeth A. Gray publishes the modern critical edition and translation of *Cath Maige Tuired*. |
| Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries | Ériu enters reconstructionist Paganism, Druidry, fantasy art, ecological spirituality, and online devotional culture; no institutional scale or continuous ancient lineage can be established. |
| Present | Éire remains the constitutional Irish name of the state; Ériu remains a medieval literary goddess/personification and an actively reinterpreted modern religious and cultural figure. |

## 11. Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction yields three overlapping histories:

1. **The name:** A very old Celtic name for Ireland, reflected in classical geography and related to *Érainn* and Welsh *Iwerddon*.
2. **The medieval divine woman:** Ériu as mother of Bres in *Cath Maige Tuired* and as sister of Banba and Fódla, wife of Mac Gréine, territorial negotiator, eponym, and battle casualty in *Lebor Gabála Érenn*.
3. **The modern symbol and deity:** Éire/Erin as national personification and Ériu as a revived land or sovereignty goddess in contemporary Pagan practice.

What cannot currently be reconstructed is an ancient institutional cult. There is no known temple, inscription, priesthood, festival calendar, image, or sacrifice securely belonging to Ériu. The medieval evidence supports calling her divine, otherworldly, territorial, maternal, and concerned with sovereignty. It does not justify supplying her with a universal “Celtic” iconography or importing evidence from Welsh, Gaulish, or Romano-British religion.

The variants are therefore central rather than accidental. The mother of Bres, the wife of Mac Gréine, the sister-eponym among three land-women, the slain Túath Dé queen, the country-name, Mother Ireland, and the modern Pagan land-goddess are related historical constructions—but they are not one timeless, uniformly attested identity.
