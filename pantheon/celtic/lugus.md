# Lugus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Continental Celtic god or divine collective (*Lugus/Lugoves*); in medieval Irish tradition, **Lug/Lugh** is one of the *Túatha Dé Danann*; in medieval Welsh literature, **Lleu Llaw Gyffes** is a supernatural heroic figure whose identification as the Brittonic continuation of Lugus is linguistic and comparative, not explicitly stated by the manuscripts.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **[Secure early attestation]** The Celtiberian rock inscription from Peñalba de Villastar, Teruel, conventionally dated around the first century BCE–first century CE, contains the dative **LVGVEI**, normally interpreted “to/for Lugus” or, in one syntactic analysis, “toward Lugus.” Because its reading and syntax remain disputed, the safest unambiguous cult evidence is the Roman-period plural **Lugovibus** dedication from Uxama Argaela (Osma, Soria), probably first–second century CE. A monumental inscription reading **Lugoves** at Aventicum (Avenches, Switzerland) is dated approximately 200–250 CE.

## Evidentiary key

- **[Secure early]** Contemporary inscription, archaeological object, or classical text.
- **[Later canonical]** Medieval text central to an Irish or Welsh literary tradition, but not contemporary evidence for Iron Age belief.
- **[Regional variant]** Locally attested form or story.
- **[Learned systematization]** Medieval or later organization of diverse material into genealogy, pantheon, or history.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** Equation inferred or asserted between gods of different traditions.
- **[Late accretion]** Later medieval or early-modern expansion.
- **[Folklore]** Post-medieval oral or customary evidence.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A reasoned modern hypothesis rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** Modern religious, literary, political, or commercial development.

A central conclusion governs the entire dossier: **the epigraphic Lugus/Lugoves, Irish Lug, and Welsh Lleu are not documented in one continuous ancient narrative tradition**. Their relationship is supported principally by historical linguistics and selected structural correspondences. The continental evidence is mostly names without myths; the insular myths survive in Christian manuscripts centuries after conversion.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Continental forms

**[Secure early]** *Lugus* is the conventional Latinized nominative reconstructed from Celtiberian **LVGVEI**, generally treated as a singular dative, and from plural forms:

- **LVGVEI** — Peñalba de Villastar, in a Celtiberian text written in Latin letters.
- **LUGOVIBUS** — dative plural on the Uxama altar: “sacred to the Lugoves.”
- **LUGOVES** — nominative plural, preserved in metal-letter sockets on a Corinthian capital from Aventicum.
- **LUCUBO, LUCOVBO, LVGVBO, LVGVNIS** and related northwestern Iberian forms have been interpreted as members of the same plural theonymic family, but individual readings and linguistic affiliations vary.
- Alleged singular or related attestations in Gaulish graffiti and the Chamalières tablet remain disputed.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The conventional Gaulish form *Lugus* and reconstructed Common Celtic **\*Lugus** are therefore partly grammatical back-formations. The singular is not abundantly written in clear Latin dedications, whereas plural formations are conspicuous.

### Irish forms and titles

**[Later canonical]** Old and Middle Irish normally have **Lug**; modern Irish spelling is **Lugh**. Major titles include:

- **Lug Lámfhota/Lámfhada**, “Lug of the long arm/long hand.”
- **Samildánach**, “possessing many arts” or “many-skilled.”
- **Ildánach**, “skilled in many arts.”
- **Macnia**, “young warrior/hero.”
- **Lug mac Ethlenn/Ethnenn**, “Lug son of Ethliu/Ethniu.”
- **Lug mac Céin**, “Lug son of Cian.”
- **Lug Scimaig**, an obscure epithet or localized designation in some genealogical material.
- **Rí Érenn**, king of Ireland, in the synthetic reign lists of *Lebor Gabála Érenn*.

“Long hand” can denote physical reach, martial efficacy, or skill; the texts do not establish one exclusive interpretation. “Many-skilled” is secure within *Cath Maige Tuired*, but projecting it directly onto continental Lugus is comparative reconstruction.

### Welsh forms

**[Later canonical/regional variant]** The Fourth Branch names **Lleu Llaw Gyffes**. The epithet is difficult: “Lleu of the skilful/deft hand,” “of the sure hand,” or “whose hand is well fitted” are defensible translations. Early Welsh poetic and Arthurian texts also preserve forms such as **Lluch Llawynnauc**, **Lluch Lleawc**, and **Llwch Llawwynnyawc**. Whether all refer to the same inherited figure is disputed.

The common modern spelling “Llew,” Welsh “lion,” can mislead. The name in the medieval tale is **Lleu**, and the traditional identification with Irish *Lug* rests on historical phonology, not on the word for lion.

### Etymology

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction—disputed]**

1. **“Light/brightness.”** Older scholarship connected *Lugus* with Indo-European **\*leuk-**, “shine/light,” producing the popular “sun god” interpretation. The expected Celtic sound development of PIE *-k-* does not straightforwardly yield the *-g-* of *Lugus*. Most recent Celtic linguists therefore reject or heavily qualify this derivation.

2. **“Oath/binding.”** A comparison with Old Irish *luige*, “oath, act of swearing,” and Welsh *llw*, “oath,” has generated a theory of Lugus as a god of binding agreements or sovereignty. The semantic proposal is attractive but the precise derivational relationship remains debated.

3. **“Lynx,” hence “warrior/hero.”** Rudolf Thurneysen, A. G. van Hamel, Bernhard Maier, and others discussed a Celtic *lugus* “lynx,” possibly extended to “warrior.” John Carey challenged whether the required Common Celtic noun is actually established. Mark Williams notes that even if a homonymous “warrior” word existed, it complicates the evidentiary use of *Lugu-* names: not every one need be theophoric.

4. **“Raven.”** Pseudo-Plutarch’s *De fluviis* reports a Galatian/Celtic word *lougos* for “raven” in an aetiology of Lugdunum. The gloss is late, textually suspect, and not independently confirmed as the correct etymology.

5. **Loki comparison.** Comparisons between Lugus and Norse Loki are old but phonologically and historically unproved. Similar narrative ingenuity does not establish common identity.

**Finding:** No etymology commands consensus. “Light god,” “raven god,” “oath god,” and “lynx god” should not be presented as established translations of the name.

### Lugdunum and related place-names

**[Secure early names; interpretation disputed]** Gaulish *dūnon* means a fortified place, fort, or elevated stronghold. The first element in **Lugudunum/Lugdunum** may be the divine name, a homonymous personal or common noun, or another *lugu-* formation. Consequently “Fort of Lugus” is plausible but not automatically proven for each site.

Places commonly discussed include:

- **Lugudunum/Lugdunum**, Lyon.
- **Lugdunum Clavatum**, usually associated with Laon.
- **Lugdunum Batavorum**, a learned Roman/early-modern name associated with the Leiden region, although the exact ancient referent and its relationship to Leiden are problematic.
- **Lugdunum Convenarum**, Saint-Bertrand-de-Comminges; the compound form with *Convenarum* is itself partly modern scholarly usage.
- **Loudun** and several lesser *Lugdunum* sites.
- **Lugo**, Galicia: *Lucus Augusti* may mean “sacred grove of Augustus” in ordinary Latin rather than preserve Lugus.
- Claims for Carlisle, Louth, Legnica, Lucca, or other distant names vary greatly in linguistic probability and cannot collectively demonstrate a pan-European cult.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The wide distribution of *lugu-* names may indicate a widespread divine name, but place-name evidence cannot by itself supply myths, iconography, or cult organization.

### Interpretatio Romana

**[Secure early testimony; syncretistic identification disputed]** Caesar writes in *Bellum Gallicum* 6.17, circa 52 BCE, that the Gauls especially worship “Mercury,” possess many images of him, call him inventor of all arts and guide of roads and journeys, and credit him with power over profit and commerce. Caesar never writes *Lugus*.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Lug’s designation *samildánach* and the Uxama shoemakers’ dedication make Lugus a plausible contributor to Caesar’s “Mercury.” This has become the standard comparative theory. Against it:

- No known inscription says **Mercurius Lugus**.
- Gaulish Mercury bears numerous other indigenous epithets, including Visucius, Cissonius, and Gebrinius.
- Caesar may have compressed several local gods into one Roman category.
- Medieval Irish literature is separated from Caesar by roughly a millennium.

Thus “Lugus was the Mercury of the Gauls” is a useful hypothesis, not an ancient equation.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Peñalba de Villastar

**[Secure early, interpretation disputed]** The open-air sanctuary at Peñalba de Villastar in Aragon bears Celtiberian and Latin graffiti. The long Celtiberian inscription, discovered in the early twentieth century and usually dated near the turn of the era, includes **TO LVGVEI** and a later **LVGVEI**. Blanca María Prósper translates part approximately as a collective dedication of fields “to Lugu”; Patrizia de Bernardo Stempel argues that *to* marks direction, “toward Lugus,” rather than a simple dedicatory dative. Large portions remain uncertain.

What it securely establishes:

- A form *Luguei* existed in Celtiberian religious language.
- The text belonged to a ritualized sanctuary landscape.
- The divine name was meaningful enough to recur.

What it does not establish:

- Lugus’s genealogy, appearance, myths, or exact domain.
- A relation to Mercury.
- A pan-Celtic festival.
- That a lost bicéphalic figure once copied at the site depicts Lugus. That iconographic attribution is conjectural.

### Uxama Argaela

**[Secure early]** CIL II 2818, found in the forum zone of Uxama near modern Osma, reads approximately:

> **LUGOVIBUS / SACRUM / L. L. URCI/CO(M) COLLE/GIO SUTORU/M D.D.**

“Sacred to the Lugoves. Lucius Licinius, of the Urcici, gave/dedicated [this] on behalf of the guild of shoemakers.”

The exact expansion of the donor’s name is uncertain, but the dedication, plural recipients, and *collegium sutorum* are clear. Its Roman-imperial date is normally placed in the first or second century CE.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The shoemakers suggest patronage of craft. John Rhys famously compared the Fourth Branch, in which Gwydion and Lleu disguise themselves as shoemakers. The comparison is striking but cannot show that the Uxama guild knew that Welsh episode.

### Aventicum

**[Secure early]** CIL XIII 5078 consists simply of **LUGOVES**, represented by sockets for applied metal letters on the abacus of a large Corinthian capital. It was found in 1844 near the Cigognier sanctuary at Aventicum and is now in the Roman Museum at Avenches. The archaeological catalogue dates it approximately **200–250 CE**.

Its monumental scale implies a public cultic or architectural setting. The missing lower structure prevents reconstruction of the complete monument or ritual.

### Other inscriptional evidence

**[Secure early/regional; readings variable]** Plural *Lucubo/Lucoubus/Lugubus* dedications in northwestern Hispania have been gathered into corpora of plural indigenous deities. Some carry epithets such as **Arquienobo**. Their grouping with Lugoves is linguistically plausible but not certain in every case. Proposals that three altar depressions or plural grammar prove a trinity are speculative; plurality does not inherently mean “three.”

A possible **LVG[...]** dedication from Fuensabiñán and other damaged stones are too fragmentary for firm conclusions. A purported Lugus in the Chamalières curse tablet depends on contested segmentation. Britain has no secure inscription naming Lugus.

### Classical authors

**[Secure early]**

- **Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* 6.16–18**, composed in the 50s BCE: describes human sacrifice and the Roman-named gods Mercury, Apollo, Mars, Jupiter, and Minerva. It contains no name Lugus.
- **Lucan, *Pharsalia* 1.444–446**, circa 61–65 CE: names Teutates, Esus, and Taranis. It does not name Lugus.
- **Lucan scholia**, surviving substantially in medieval manuscripts but containing earlier commentary: assign sacrificial modes and Roman equations to those three gods. Lugus is absent.
- **Pseudo-Plutarch, *De fluviis* 6**, probably imperial-era pseudepigraphy: gives a raven etymology for Lugdunum. Its linguistic authority is weak.

Accordingly, Lucan and his scholia are evidence for the construction of “Celtic religion,” but not for a Lugus myth or Lugus-specific human sacrifice.

### Irish corpus

**[Later canonical/learned systematization]**

- **Lebor Gabála Érenn**, compiled through the eleventh and twelfth centuries in several recensions, in manuscripts including the Book of Leinster: makes Lug a king of the Túatha Dé Danann; supplies genealogy and a reign of forty years in some versions. It is Christian synthetic history, not a pagan scripture.
- **Cath Maige Tuired**, substantially a twelfth-century Middle Irish redaction surviving uniquely in sixteenth-century British Library Harley MS 5280: fullest account of Lug as many-skilled war leader and killer of Balor.
- **The Metrical Dindshenchas**, chiefly eleventh–twelfth century: connects Lug with Tailtiu, Lugnasad, assembly sites, and numerous place legends.
- **Táin Bó Cúailnge**, composed and redacted across the Old and Middle Irish periods, surviving principally in *Lebor na hUidre* and the Book of Leinster: Lug appears to Cú Chulainn, declares himself his father, heals him, and temporarily fights in his place in Recension I.
- **Tochmarc Emire**, with an Old Irish core and expanded Middle Irish recension: explains seasonal assemblies and Lugnasad in learned dialogue.
- **Oidheadh Chloinne Tuireann/The Fate of the Children of Tuireann**, surviving chiefly in early-modern manuscripts though based on earlier matter: greatly expands Lug’s prosecution of his father’s killers.
- **Acallam na Senórach**, approximately late twelfth or early thirteenth century: preserves retrospective traditions in which pagan figures and landscapes are interpreted in dialogue with Saint Patrick.
- **Baile in Scáil** and related sovereignty literature: Lug or a Lug-associated otherworldly ruler validates royal succession in some readings.
- **Poems attributed to or concerning Lug**, including difficult *roscada* in *Cath Maige Tuired*: philologically important but not transparent ritual hymns.

*Tochmarc Étaíne* is vital for the Túatha Dé world but is not a principal Lug narrative. Its inclusion in a Lug corpus should not imply that he is central to its surviving plot.

### Welsh corpus

**[Later canonical/regional variant]**

- **Math fab Mathonwy**, Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi: composed perhaps in the eleventh–twelfth century; survives in the White Book of Rhydderch, circa 1350, and Red Book of Hergest, circa 1400. It gives Lleu’s conception, naming, arming, marriage, attempted killing, transformation, restoration, and vengeance.
- **Culhwch ac Olwen**, probably eleventh century in substantial form: includes a figure **Llwch Llawwynnyawc** in Arthur’s court-list. The allusion supplies little narrative.
- **Pa gur yv y porthaur?**, probably an early Welsh Arthurian poem preserved in a thirteenth-century manuscript: names **Lluch Llawynnauc**, but identification with Lleu is uncertain.
- **Preiddeu Annwfn**, preserved in the Book of Taliesin: a **Lluch Lleawc** occurs in a difficult passage. The poem does not narrate the Fourth Branch.
- The broader **Book of Taliesin** is a thirteenth-century manuscript containing poems of varied dates. Similar names should not be automatically merged into one deity.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Continental Lugus

**[Secure early]** The direct evidence supports only:

- Cult in Celtiberian and Romanized urban settings.
- A plural form or divine collective.
- Association with a shoemakers’ guild at Uxama.
- Monumental recognition at Aventicum.
- Possible sanctuary rites at Peñalba.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Craft, trade, travel, eloquence, kingship, warfare, oath, and sovereignty have all been proposed. Craft has the strongest direct support through the Uxama dedicators. The remainder depends chiefly on identifying Lugus with Caesar’s Mercury and medieval Lug.

### Irish Lug

**[Later canonical]** *Cath Maige Tuired* presents a deliberately comprehensive figure. At Tara’s door Lug offers sequentially to serve as:

- wright or carpenter,
- smith,
- champion,
- harper,
- warrior,
- poet and historian,
- sorcerer,
- physician,
- cupbearer,
- brazier.

The porter says that Tara already has each specialist. Lug gains admission because no one person possesses all the arts. His defining attribute is therefore not one trade but coordinated mastery.

He also:

- directs the Túatha Dé’s preparations;
- questions every specialist about the contribution each will make;
- uses incantation, strategic counsel, and martial skill;
- kills Balor with a sling-stone cast through the destructive eye;
- becomes king in synthetic histories;
- is associated with assemblies, games, harvest-season gatherings, and legitimate order.

### Weapons and objects

**[Later canonical]**

- **Sling and sling-stone:** explicit in *Cath Maige Tuired* as the weapon against Balor.
- **Spear:** later texts and antiquarian syntheses associate Lug with an irresistible spear, frequently identified as one of the “Four Treasures.” The treasure lists often call it the spear of Lug, but descriptions of its bloodlust or need for cooling may derive from separate heroic spear traditions and later harmonization.
- **Sword:** Lug uses martial weapons but has no single universally canonical sword comparable to Nuada’s.
- **Harp:** he demonstrates musicianship at Tara, playing sleep, sorrow, and joy.
- **Board game:** in some versions he defeats Nuada at *fidchell*, signifying intelligence and eligibility for leadership.
- **Magic cow, hound, horse, boat, or solar wheel:** many such associations appear in modern summaries, but no stable ancient Lugus iconographic ensemble supports them.

### Appearance

**[Later canonical]** Irish narratives depict Lug as a young, beautiful, radiant, accomplished warrior. Radiance in literary description does not prove that his original domain was the physical sun.

Welsh Lleu becomes an eagle after Gronw’s attack and perches wounded in an oak. This metamorphosis belongs securely to the Fourth Branch; projecting an eagle or oak cult onto continental Lugus is unjustified.

### Iconography

**[Secure early absence]** No surviving ancient image bears an inscription securely identifying it as singular Lugus. Consequently there is no certain Lugus iconographic type.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Proposed images include:

- a lost copied bicéphalic figure at Peñalba;
- Gallo-Roman Mercuries with purse, caduceus, cock, tortoise, ram, or multiple faces;
- a three-faced or triple Mercury;
- the large-eyed or wheel-bearing figures on the Gundestrup cauldron;
- figures on third-century Gallic coins.

None is securely labeled Lugus. The **Pillar of the Boatmen** names or depicts Jupiter, Vulcan, Esus, Tarvos Trigaranus, Cernunnos, Smertrios, and others, but not Lugus. The **Gundestrup cauldron** is uninscribed; its manufacture, cultural setting, and every divine identification are debated. The **Coligny calendar** is also uninscribed with Lugus’s name and cannot serve as direct evidence for his festival.

### Animals, colors, planets, and days

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction/modern invention]**

- Raven: derived from Pseudo-Plutarch’s disputed gloss and modern symbolism.
- Cock and tortoise: borrowed from Roman Mercury iconography through the Lugus–Mercury hypothesis.
- Eagle: extrapolated from Welsh Lleu’s transformation.
- Lynx: etymological theory, not cult evidence.
- Sun, gold, yellow, Sunday: mostly modern solar interpretation.
- Mercury, Wednesday: modern consequence of the interpretatio Romana theory.
- Number three: inferred from plural Lugoves and Celtic triple imagery, but no Lugus inscription specifies three.
- August 1: firmly associated with Irish Lugnasad in medieval tradition; its continental application is disputed.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Irish genealogies

**[Later canonical; conflicting versions]**

The dominant *Cath Maige Tuired* genealogy makes Lug the son of **Cian**, son of Dían Cécht, and **Ethniu/Ethliu**, daughter of the Fomorian Balor. This places him across both opposing populations: paternal Túatha Dé and maternal Fomorian.

Other genealogical notices vary the names, spellings, and number of Ethniu’s brothers or Lug’s paternal ancestry. *Lebor Gabála* systematizes these relationships more rigidly than narrative tales do.

**Foster relations:**

- **Tailtiu**, wife of the last Fir Bolg king Eochaid mac Eirc, becomes Lug’s foster-mother in the dominant Lugnasad tradition.
- **Manannán mac Lir** is Lug’s foster-father or instructor in later and variant traditions, especially material surrounding the sons of Tuireann.
- Some texts distribute fosterage differently, reflecting the social importance of multiple foster ties.

**Consorts and children:**

- A stable ancient consort of Lugus is not epigraphically attested.
- Irish texts variously associate Lug with **Buach**, **Nás**, or **Bói**, but the genealogies are inconsistent and sometimes etiological.
- **Cú Chulainn** is explicitly called Lug’s son in *Táin Bó Cúailnge* Recension I, though other birth traditions foreground the mortal Sualtaim and Deichtine’s complicated conception.
- **Cnú Deireóil** and other obscure offspring occur in genealogical or place-lore traditions but never form one stable family.

### Welsh genealogy

**[Later canonical/regional variant]** In *Math fab Mathonwy*, Lleu is the son of **Arianrhod**. Gwydion’s magical testing of Arianrhod causes the birth of Dylan and the hidden infant later called Lleu. The text does not explicitly name a biological father; modern summaries calling Gwydion his father go beyond the narrative, although Gwydion acts as guardian, creator, and social father.

**Blodeuedd/Blodeuwedd** is made from flowers by Math and Gwydion as Lleu’s wife because Arianrhod has forbidden him to marry a human woman. Her adultery and attempted murder of Lleu lead Gwydion to transform her into an owl. No continental inscription pairs Lugus with her.

### Every documented divine interaction

The following entries separate discrete attested scenes. Figures classed as Túatha Dé, Fomorians, or supernatural Welsh beings are included; purely mortal interactions are omitted unless divine classification is ambiguous.

#### Irish interactions

- **Nuada — admission, testing, and transfer of command.** Lug seeks entrance to Tara, demonstrates unique mastery of all arts, and in some versions defeats Nuada at *fidchell*; Nuada yields the king’s seat or command for thirteen days. *Cath Maige Tuired*, twelfth-century redaction in sixteenth-century manuscript. **[Later canonical]**

- **The Dagda — strategic collaboration.** Lug asks what power the Dagda will contribute; the Dagda promises destruction and magical provisioning. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Ogma — contest and alliance.** Lug proves strength by returning a great flagstone thrown from Tara and later assigns Ogma a martial role; both fight the Fomorians. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Goibniu — mobilization of divine craft.** Lug asks the smith how he will arm the host; Goibniu promises rapidly made, unfailing weapons. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Credne — mobilization of divine craft.** Lug questions the brazier, who promises rivets, shield-rims, and spear fittings. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Luchta — mobilization of divine craft.** Lug questions the carpenter, who promises shields and spear-shafts. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Dían Cécht — mobilization of healing.** Lug asks what medical power he possesses; Dían Cécht promises to restore the wounded, subject to lethal limits. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Miach — indirect relation through healing tradition.** Some recensions or associated traditions place Miach’s superior healing within the divine medical background, but no major surviving Lug–Miach dialogue is secure. **[Absence finding]**

- **Airmed — indirect relation through healing tradition.** She belongs to the healing family and herb lore, but no independent narrative interaction with Lug is securely recorded. **[Absence finding]**

- **Cairbre mac Étaín — mobilization of satire and poetry.** Lug asks what the poet will contribute against the Fomorians; Cairbre promises satire and magical verbal action. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **The Morrígan — strategic alliance.** Lug questions her or receives her promise to pursue and destroy the enemy; both serve the Túatha Dé war effort. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Badb and Macha — joint battle presence.** War-goddess figures support or proclaim the Túatha Dé victory; manuscript variation makes their differentiation unstable. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Bres — political and military opposition.** Lug helps organize the coalition that ends Bres’s Fomorian-supported rule; Bres is spared after supplying agricultural counsel in the battle’s aftermath. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Balor — grandson kills grandfather.** Balor’s destructive eye is opened during battle; Lug casts a sling-stone through it, turning its force on the Fomorian army. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Later canonical]**

- **Ethniu/Ethliu — mother and son.** Lug’s maternal descent from Balor’s daughter crosses the two hostile peoples. The surviving principal battle text states genealogy rather than narrating a sustained reunion. *Cath Maige Tuired* and genealogical literature. **[Later canonical]**

- **Cian — son avenges father.** After Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharba kill Cian, Lug discovers or is informed of the crime and imposes an impossible-seeming *éraic*, or compensation. *Oidheadh Chloinne Tuireann*, chiefly early-modern recension of older material. **[Late accretion based on medieval tradition]**

- **Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharba — punitive quest.** Lug demands magical objects from across the world; after the brothers return mortally wounded, he refuses the healing pigskin they had acquired, allowing them to die. *Fate of the Children of Tuireann*. **[Late canonical/accretion; moral tone varies by retelling]**

- **Manannán mac Lir — fosterage and equipment.** Later traditions make Manannán Lug’s fosterer and supplier of weapons, horse, boat, or magical equipment. Details vary among texts and modern compilations. **[Regional/late accretion]**

- **Tailtiu — foster-son founds funeral assembly.** Lug establishes games or an assembly at Tailtiu after her death from clearing the land. *Lebor Gabála* and Dindshenchas. **[Learned systematization/later canonical]**

- **Cú Chulainn — divine father heals and substitutes for son.** During the Táin, Lug identifies himself as Cú Chulainn’s father, puts him to sleep for three days, heals his wounds, and fights in his stead. *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, Recension I, eleventh-century manuscript witness. **[Later canonical]**

- **Donn Cúailnge — no secure direct interaction.** Lug appears in the Táin but does not have a discrete encounter with the divine or supernatural bull. **[Absence finding]**

- **Medb and the Morrígan in the Táin — no direct Lug scene securely preserved.** Sharing the narrative does not constitute interaction. **[Absence finding]**

#### Welsh interactions

- **Arianrhod — maternal repudiation and three prohibitions.** Arianrhod denies Lleu a name, arms, and a human wife; Gwydion defeats each prohibition by deception or magic. *Math fab Mathonwy*. **[Later canonical/regional]**

- **Gwydion — guardian, magician, and rescuer.** Gwydion raises Lleu, engineers his naming and arming, helps create Blodeuedd, finds the wounded eagle, restores Lleu to human form, and enables vengeance. *Math fab Mathonwy*. **[Later canonical/regional]**

- **Math fab Mathonwy — dynastic patron and co-creator.** Math participates in testing Arianrhod, recognizes the child’s claims, and with Gwydion creates Blodeuedd as Lleu’s wife. *Math fab Mathonwy*. **[Later canonical/regional]**

- **Dylan ail Don — twin or co-born sibling.** Dylan and the hidden infant Lleu emerge during Arianrhod’s magical virginity test, but the tale records no later meeting between them. *Math fab Mathonwy*. **[Later canonical; absence of subsequent interaction]**

- **Blodeuedd/Blodeuwedd — created wife, conspirator, and betrayer.** She extracts the unique conditions under which Lleu can be killed and arranges Gronw’s spear attack; after Lleu’s recovery, Gwydion transforms her into an owl. *Math fab Mathonwy*. **[Later canonical/regional]**

- **Gronw Pebr — rival and attempted killer.** Gronw attacks Lleu under the contrived liminal conditions; restored Lleu later kills Gronw with a spear cast through or around the stone Gronw uses as a shield. *Math fab Mathonwy*. Gronw is a supernatural or heroic ruler, not explicitly called a god. **[Later canonical/regional]**

- **Dôn — genealogical relation only.** Lleu belongs to the Children of Dôn through Arianrhod; no direct Lleu–Dôn scene survives. **[Absence finding]**

#### Continental divine interactions

**[Secure early absence]** No inscription narrates Lugus meeting, fighting, marrying, or collaborating with another named god. No monument securely labels Lugus beside Mercury or any other deity. The absence is important: the continental “family” and myths are entirely comparative reconstruction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The many-skilled stranger at Tara

**[Later canonical]** Lug arrives at Tara while Nuada’s court prepares for conflict with the Fomorians. The doorkeeper refuses entry because every craft Lug names already has a practitioner. Lug asks whether the court has one person who possesses all of them. It does not, and he is admitted.

He proves himself through feats of strength, music, poetry, gaming, or intellectual skill depending on recension and retelling. Nuada recognizes that this comprehensive ability is precisely what the crisis requires and gives him precedence.

**Development:** This episode is the main basis for calling Lug a god “of all arts.” Caesar’s Mercury as *omnium inventorem artium* supplies a striking earlier parallel, but the texts are separated by many centuries and no transmission can be demonstrated.

### The Second Battle of Mag Tuired

**[Later canonical]** Bres’s unjust kingship and Fomorian exactions provoke the Túatha Dé. Lug organizes resistance. Rather than simply charging into battle, he inventories the differentiated capacities of smith, brazier, carpenter, healer, poet, sorcerer, champion, Dagda, and war goddess. The narrative’s central idea is coordinated social and technical completeness.

The Túatha Dé attempt to keep Lug away from the fighting because his combined knowledge is too valuable to risk. He escapes supervision and leads or inspires the host. Balor’s eye destroys whatever it faces when attendants raise its lid. Lug strikes the eye with a sling-stone so that it passes through the back of Balor’s head and turns its destructive force against the Fomorians.

Bres is captured. He offers successive forms of prosperity in exchange for life; the settlement that survives concerns correct agricultural timing. The tale therefore ends not merely with slaughter but with wresting useful knowledge from the defeated power.

**Variants:** Later retellings increasingly make Lug the single heroic champion and supply more elaborate speeches, weapons, and family prophecy. The surviving manuscript’s difficult verse passages resist confident translation.

### Conception and Balor prophecy

**[Later/variant accretion]** Modern narratives often say Balor was warned that his grandson would kill him, imprisoned Ethniu in a tower, and was defeated after Cian entered magically. Elements occur in Irish and later folklore, but the seamless “Celtic Perseus” story commonly printed today combines sources of different dates. The principal *Cath Maige Tuired* does not narrate the complete popular conception tale in that familiar form.

### The sons of Tuireann

**[Late canonical/accretion]** Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharba kill Cian after he tries to escape them through transformation. Lug discovers the murder and demands an *éraic* consisting of marvellous objects and feats: healing pigskin, an invincible spear, magical horses and chariot, pigs, a hound, a cooking-spit, and shouts upon a dangerous hill, with lists varying by version.

The brothers obtain nearly everything but suffer fatal wounds. Tuireann begs Lug to lend the healing skin; Lug refuses, and the brothers die. The story depicts Lug as legal avenger, strategist, and implacable enforcer, not merely benevolent craftsman. Lady Gregory’s 1904 retelling made this cycle widely accessible in English and helped stabilize one composite version.

### Lug and Cú Chulainn

**[Later canonical]** In the Táin’s Recension I, Cú Chulainn is exhausted from defending Ulster alone. A radiant warrior appears and identifies himself as Lug mac Ethnenn, his father. Lug takes over the fighting while Cú Chulainn sleeps and heals. The episode overlays divine paternity upon other accounts in which Sualtaim is Cú Chulainn’s social father and Deichtine’s pregnancy has variant causes.

The scene does not demonstrate that Cú Chulainn is simply a mortal form of Lug; that is a comparativist theory.

### Tailtiu and Lugnasad

**[Later canonical/learned systematization]** Tailtiu dies after clearing the plain for agriculture. Lug orders funeral games or an assembly at her grave. The Metrical Dindshenchas says she died around the Calends of August and that the principal assembly of Ireland was held there.

The festival name **Lugnasad/Lughnasa** is transparently associated by medieval Irish tradition with Lug. It involved assemblies, legal business, games, horse-racing, courtship, trade, and movement to prominent places in surviving literary and folkloric evidence. Not every later hilltop fair is demonstrably a continuous pagan rite.

### Lleu’s birth, naming, and arms

**[Later canonical/regional]** Math can survive only while his feet rest in a virgin’s lap, except during war. Arianrhod undergoes a magical virginity test and gives birth to Dylan; another small object is hidden by Gwydion and develops into a child.

Arianrhod refuses to name him. Gwydion creates an illusion in which she sees the boy strike a bird and exclaims that the fair-haired one has a skilful hand; her words inadvertently name him **Lleu Llaw Gyffes**. She then forbids him arms. Gwydion disguises them and creates an apparent attack, causing Arianrhod to arm Lleu herself. Her third prohibition denies him a human wife.

### Blodeuedd, death conditions, and restoration

**[Later canonical/regional]** Math and Gwydion make a woman from oak, broom, and meadowsweet and call her Blodeuedd, “flower-face.” She marries Lleu but falls in love with Gronw Pebr.

Lleu can be killed neither indoors nor outdoors, neither riding nor walking, neither clothed nor naked, and only by a spear made during specified periods. Blodeuedd coaxes the secret from him. He demonstrates the necessary position while bathing beside a river, with one foot on a goat and one on the rim of a bath beneath a thatched shelter. Gronw casts the spear. Lleu transforms into an eagle and flies away.

Gwydion follows a sow that eats maggots and flesh dropping from an eagle in an oak. He sings successive *englynion*, brings Lleu down, and restores him. Lleu later grants Gronw the same chance at a spear-cast. Gronw shelters behind a stone, but Lleu’s spear pierces it and kills him. Gwydion turns Blodeuedd into Blodeuwedd, an owl.

**Comparative status:** The invulnerable hero betrayed through contrived liminal conditions is a widespread narrative pattern. It may preserve pre-Christian myth, but its exact relationship to continental Lugus cannot be documented.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Continental cult sites

#### Peñalba de Villastar

**[Secure early]** An open-air rock sanctuary with inscriptions in Celtiberian and Latin, used around the transition to Roman rule. The long *Luguei* text implies collective ritual activity. Because no intact temple, altar program, or liturgy survives, descriptions of a formal Lugus priesthood or annual Lugus pilgrimage there are conjectural.

#### Uxama Argaela

**[Secure early]** The Lugoves altar came from the forum area. The dedicating *collegium sutorum* provides rare institutional evidence: an occupational association publicly honored the plural gods. Whether Lugoves were the guild’s permanent patrons or recipients of a single vow cannot be determined.

#### Aventicum

**[Secure early]** The monumental *Lugoves* capital was found near the Cigognier sanctuary complex. It may have belonged to a shrine or architectural dedication, but its original position and missing text prevent reconstruction. It shows that the collective name operated within the monumental religious language of a Roman provincial capital.

### Sacrifice and offerings

**[Secure early absence]** No surviving Lugus inscription specifies an animal, human victim, food, incense, or ritual formula beyond dedication.

Caesar’s account of human sacrifice in Gaul and Lucan’s verses on Teutates, Esus, and Taranis cannot be transferred to Lugus. No source says that Lugus received human sacrifice. Conversely, the absence of such evidence cannot prove that no local Lugus cult ever participated in wider sacrificial institutions.

Claims about “temple prostitution” have no basis in the Lugus evidence.

### Priests and economics

**[Secure early absence]** No priest of Lugus is named. No Lugus temple treasury, estate, tariff, oracle, or pilgrimage administration is documented. The shoemakers’ association is economic and cultic evidence, but not a priesthood.

### Lugnasad practice

**[Later canonical and folklore]** Medieval and later evidence connects the season around August 1 with:

- assemblies at Tailtiu, Carman, and other locations;
- athletic and equestrian contests;
- legal proclamations and dispute settlement;
- trade;
- courtship or temporary unions;
- first-fruit customs;
- climbing hills and visiting wells;
- communal meals.

Máire MacNeill’s *The Festival of Lughnasa* mapped a large body of Irish and British folklore. Her work remains foundational, but similarity between a modern fair and medieval Lugnasad does not establish uninterrupted worship of Lug.

### Lyon’s August 1 imperial festival

**[Secure Roman festival; continuity disputed]** In 12 BCE Drusus established the federal sanctuary of the Three Gauls at Condate beside Lugdunum. Provincial delegates met around the altar of Rome and Augustus. Literary and epigraphic evidence places the concilium and associated games on **August 1**.

The proposed chain is:

> Lugus → Lugdunum → a pre-Roman August festival → August 1 imperial assembly → Irish Lugnasad.

Arguments for continuity cite the city-name, calendrical match, centrality of the gathering, and Lug’s Irish festival. Arguments against it note:

- Augustus’s victory at Alexandria was commemorated on August 1, providing a Roman political reason.
- No Lyon inscription names Lugus or Lugnasad.
- Caesar’s lunar/seasonal Gaulish calendars need not have corresponded permanently to Julian August 1.
- The Coligny calendar does not name Lugus or establish this equivalence.
- *Lugdunum* itself may not mean “fort of Lugus.”

The federal festival may have deliberately overlaid an indigenous gathering, but this remains a historical hypothesis, not a documented succession.

### Suppression and conversion

**[Historical reconstruction]** No edict specifically suppressing Lugus survives. Continental cult practice disappeared amid the broader Christianization of the western Roman Empire and the decline of public sacrifice and collegial pagan cults in late antiquity.

In Ireland and Wales, there is no documentation of missionaries closing temples of Lug or Lleu. Instead, divine figures survived as ancestors, kings, heroes, *síd* beings, and literary characters in manuscripts produced by Christians. The texts sometimes rationalize gods into mortal pre-Christian populations without erasing their supernatural powers.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the Roman conquest

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Linguistic comparison suggests that a divine or heroic name *Lugus* may predate the separation of the Goidelic and Brittonic languages. The first direct evidence, however, is late Iron Age or early Roman Celtiberian, not a Proto-Celtic hymn or image.

### First century BCE–third century CE

**[Secure early]** The name appears at Peñalba and in Roman-period plural dedications at Uxama, Aventicum, and perhaps northwestern Iberia. These records reveal geographically separated cults but no unified clergy or mythology.

Caesar’s Mercury description may preserve comparable functions. Romanization encouraged indigenous gods to be expressed through Latin dedications, occupational associations, monumental architecture, and Roman divine categories.

### Fourth–seventh centuries

**[Historical reconstruction]** Public pagan institutions declined under Christian imperial and post-imperial regimes. No securely dated late-antique Lugus cult document marks a last worshipper, temple destruction, or formal conversion.

Christianity in Ireland developed without Roman conquest. Native learned traditions were written in monasteries and secular ecclesiastical networks. Their preservation was also transformation: oral or learned myth became Christian-era literature.

### Eighth–twelfth centuries

**[Later canonical]** Lug emerges clearly in Old and Middle Irish literature as divine father, many-skilled champion, royal ancestor, and festival founder. The texts are not neutral transcripts of paganism: scribes organize pre-Christian peoples through biblical history, euhemerism, dynastic politics, and literary craft.

Welsh Lleu enters surviving prose as a dynastic, magical hero. The phonological correspondence with Lug is strong enough for comparison, but the plots are markedly different.

### Thirteenth–sixteenth centuries

**[Late manuscript transmission]** The principal Welsh manuscripts and the unique surviving *Cath Maige Tuired* witness are copied. Early-modern Irish versions, particularly the sons of Tuireann narrative, expand Lug’s ethical and dramatic characterization.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Antiquarian systematization]** Scholars increasingly reconstructed a “Celtic pantheon” from classical testimony, inscriptions, Irish manuscripts, and Welsh literature. Similar names and motifs were often harmonized more confidently than present evidence permits.

John Rhys connected the Uxama shoemakers with Gwydion and Lleu’s shoemaker disguise. Henri d’Arbois de Jubainville argued explicitly from Irish Lug to Gaulish *Lugu-*.

### Twentieth century

**[Modern scholarship]** Georges Dumézil-style functional comparison, Jan de Vries’s Celtic religion, and broad pan-Celtic synthesis made Lug/Lugus a major “Celtic god.” Dictionaries by MacKillop, Green, Maier, and Koch disseminated the reconstruction while differing in caution.

Linguistic criticism weakened simple solar etymologies. Textual scholars emphasized the medieval composition and Christian transmission of Irish material. Archaeology and epigraphy simultaneously expanded the plural Lugoves dossier.

### Twenty-first century

**[Modern scholarship and revival]** Current work is generally more cautious about treating Lugus, Lug, and Lleu as a single timeless personality. Contemporary polytheists nevertheless worship Lug, Lugh, Lleu, or Lugus, sometimes distinctly and sometimes as cultural forms of one god. These are genuine modern religious practices, but no reliable census isolates Lugus worshippers.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Celtic Revival

**[Modern reception]** Lady Augusta Gregory’s *Gods and Fighting Men* (1904), with a preface by W. B. Yeats, retold Irish material in accessible English under the section “Lugh of the Long Hand.” It merged manuscript traditions into literary narrative and strongly shaped twentieth-century popular mythology.

Yeats praised Gregory’s volume and participated in a broader Irish Revival that recast native mythology as national literature. This was cultural reception, not a restoration of a continuous Lug priesthood.

### Druid revivals

**[Modern invention/reception]** Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Druid orders grew from antiquarianism, fraternalism, Romanticism, and figures such as Iolo Morganwg, whose claims of unbroken bardic transmission included forged or invented material. Later Druid groups adopted Lughnasadh as a seasonal festival.

The eightfold “Wheel of the Year,” combining solstices, equinoxes, and four Gaelic festivals, is a twentieth-century neopagan system, not an attested unified ancient Celtic calendar.

### Wicca and neopaganism

**[Modern invention/reception]** In Wiccan and eclectic pagan literature, Lughnasadh/Lammas commonly becomes:

- a harvest sabbat;
- a festival of a solar or sacrificial “corn king” Lugh;
- a time of bread magic;
- a marriage of sun god and earth goddess;
- a celebration with fixed colors, crystals, tarot cards, and astrological correspondences.

Bread, harvest, and seasonal themes can draw on folklore and Lammas, but the complete “dying corn king Lugh” theology is modern synthesis. No medieval Irish text says that Lug is ritually killed each Lugnasad as grain.

Celtic reconstructionist polytheists generally distinguish attested material from innovation more explicitly, while still creating rituals because no complete liturgy survives.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

**[Modern reception/absence finding]** Lugus has no substantial role in the Renaissance grimoires, Hermetic planetary magic, the canonical Golden Dawn system, or traditional Solomonic demonology. Modern claims of an ancient “Lugus sigil,” fixed planetary seal, crystal correspondence, or ceremonial invocation are modern constructions unless a publication history is supplied.

Some twentieth- and twenty-first-century occultists align Lug with Mercury, the Sun, Odin, or Loki. These are comparative or experiential systems, not recovered ancient doctrine.

### Literature, drama, and popular culture

**[Modern reception]**

- Gregory’s *Gods and Fighting Men* fixed an English-language heroic Lugh.
- Yeats and the Irish Revival made the Túatha Dé part of cultural nationalism.
- Brian Friel’s *Dancing at Lughnasa* (1990) uses the festival season as a dramatic frame; it is not a mythological retelling of Lug.
- Fantasy fiction, role-playing games, anime, comics, and video games frequently make Lugh a sun god, spear god, master craftsman, or magical warrior. These images usually combine Irish narrative, Roman Mercury, later spear lore, and genre conventions.
- The name has commercial uses in festivals, craft businesses, games, and music; such use documents modern recognition, not cult continuity.

### Political appropriation

**[Modern reception]** Lug and Celtic deities have appeared in Irish, Welsh, Breton, Galician, and pan-Celtic cultural nationalism. Most uses are literary or heritage-oriented. Evidence does not support labeling Lugus intrinsically nationalist or extremist.

Occasional ethnic-nationalist or racialized neopagan appropriations should be documented case by case. “Celtic” identity has sometimes been falsely presented as a biologically homogeneous ancient race, but Lugus’s actual attestations span culturally and linguistically varied regions.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Is there one pan-Celtic Lugus?

**For:** The names *Lugus*, Irish *Lug*, and Welsh *Lleu* can be related through Celtic historical phonology; all three traditions contain associations with skill, hand/arm, high status, and ingenious action. Widespread *lugu-* place- and personal names support antiquity.

**Against:** Continental inscriptions provide almost no mythology; Britain supplies no Lugus dedication; Lleu’s surviving story differs sharply from Lug’s; and *lugu-* can belong to personal names or common words. “Pan-Celtic” can conceal local religious diversity.

**Assessment:** A shared inherited name or figure is probable, but reconstructing a single detailed Proto-Celtic biography is not possible.

### Singular versus plural

The plural **Lugoves** may represent:

- multiple gods sharing one name;
- local manifestations;
- attendant spirits;
- an intensive dyad or triad;
- a grammatically plural collective;
- a cult title whose relation to singular Lugus was no longer transparent.

The inscriptions do not choose among these. A “Celtic divine trinity” is not proved.

### Mercury identification

The case rests on Caesar’s craftsman-traveller-trader god, Lug’s many skills, and the Uxama guild. The lack of **Mercurius Lugus** remains a significant negative fact. It is safest to say that medieval Lug resembles some functions attributed to Gallo-Roman Mercury and may descend from a related divine complex.

### Solar theory

The theory relies on the old “light” etymology, literary radiance, Lugnasad’s summer date, and occasional eye imagery. Against it are the phonological problem, absence of explicit sun governance, and lack of secure solar iconography. “Sun god” is therefore not an established ancient classification.

### Lugdunum

The divine etymology remains plausible, especially because *dūnon* compounds often contain personal or divine names. But “raven fort,” “shining fort,” “fort of the warrior,” and non-theophoric alternatives have histories in scholarship. The name alone does not prove a Lugus temple at Lyon.

### August 1 continuity

The date match with Lugnasad is genuine. The inference of direct continuity is not. A Roman Augustan anniversary and an indigenous calendrical accommodation are both possible; deliberate dual resonance is also possible.

### Iconographic overreach

Identifications of Lugus on Gundestrup, the Pillar of the Boatmen, coins, triple-headed Mercuries, or Peñalba’s lost figure are hypotheses. The absence of an inscribed image is the controlling fact.

### Human sacrifice

Caesar alleges that some Gauls used human victims, including large wicker constructions; archaeological evidence confirms ritualized killing in some Iron Age contexts but does not validate every ethnographic detail. Lucan and scholia associate sacrifice with other named gods. There is **no Lugus-specific human-sacrifice record**.

### Primary evidence and repositories

- Peñalba fragments: several were removed; some survive in the Archaeology Museum of Catalonia and other collections, while others are lost and known only from drawings.
- Uxama altar: historically reused as a holy-water basin at San Roque in El Burgo de Osma; its text is registered as CIL II 2818.
- Aventicum capital: Roman Museum, Avenches, inventory 7160.
- *Cath Maige Tuired*: British Library Harley MS 5280, fols. 63r–70v.
- *Lebor na hUidre*: Royal Irish Academy MS 23 E 25.
- Book of Leinster: Trinity College Dublin MS 1339.
- Welsh Four Branches: chiefly White Book of Rhydderch and Red Book of Hergest.
- Gundestrup cauldron: National Museum of Denmark.
- Pillar of the Boatmen: Musée de Cluny, Paris.
- Coligny calendar: Lugdunum–Musée et théâtres romains, Lyon.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Lugus unquestionably means light.”
- “Lugh was the Celtic sun god.”
- “All towns called Lugdunum had Lug temples.”
- “The Lyon festival is proven to be Lughnasadh.”
- “The three Lugoves were a documented Celtic trinity.”
- “Gundestrup depicts Lugus.”
- “Caesar explicitly called Lugus Mercury.”
- “The Pillar of the Boatmen shows Lugus.”
- “Lug had a fixed ancient wife, color, crystal, planet, tarot card, and sigil.”
- “Modern Druid Lughnasadh rites reproduce an unbroken Iron Age liturgy.”

## 11. Sources and conclusions

The recoverable Lugus is not one neatly bounded god. The evidence instead preserves three unequal dossiers:

1. **Continental Lugus/Lugoves:** a sparsely documented divine name, conspicuously plural in Roman-period inscriptions, associated at least once with shoemakers and probably invoked at a Celtiberian sanctuary.
2. **Irish Lug:** an extraordinarily developed medieval literary god—many-skilled strategist, killer of Balor, king, legal avenger, father of Cú Chulainn, and founder of Tailtiu’s assembly.
3. **Welsh Lleu:** a magical dynastic hero whose birth, prohibitions, flower-wife, liminal wounding, eagle transformation, restoration, and vengeance form a coherent Welsh tale largely absent from the Irish record.

Historical linguistics makes common inheritance probable. It does not license transferring every Irish or Welsh attribute to every continental inscription. The plural Lugoves cannot yet be explained with confidence; Lugus has no securely identified ancient image, temple plan, priesthood, sacrifice, or narrated continental myth. Caesar’s Mercury, Lyon’s August festival, Lugdunum place-names, and the Gundestrup cauldron remain comparative problems rather than direct attestations.

The fullest responsible reconstruction is therefore deliberately asymmetrical: **a continental cult-name with minimal theology; medieval insular figures with abundant but late narrative; and a modern Lugus assembled through scholarship, revival, and creative reception.**
