# Danu (Anu): Research Dossier

> **Class:**  
> **Anu/Ana** is explicitly classified in medieval Irish learned tradition as a **goddess**, “mother of the gods of Ireland,” and later as a **goddess of prosperity** associated with Munster.  
> **Danu** is a **modernly reconstructed goddess-name**, inferred chiefly from *Danann/Donann* in *Tuatha Dé Danann*. No medieval Irish text securely contains the nominative **Danu** or gives such a figure an independent narrative biography.  
> **Danand/Donand** is a named female genealogical figure in recensions of *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, but whether she is Anu, the hypothetical Danu, the Morrígan, or a separate literary figure varies by recension and interpretation.  
> **Welsh Dôn** is the ancestral mother implied by *Plant Dôn*, “Children of Dôn,” but her equation with Irish Danu or Anu is a comparative hypothesis, not an identification made in medieval Welsh or Irish sources.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest secure, individualized evidence is the entry **“Anu .i. mater deorum hibernensium”**—“Anu, that is, mother of the gods of Ireland”—in the early Irish glossary tradition represented by *Sanas Cormaic* and the related *Dúil Dromma Cetta*. Its materials are conventionally dated to approximately the ninth–tenth centuries, although the surviving manuscripts are later. The entry also explains **Dá Chích Anann**, the Paps of Anu in Kerry. The form **Danu** is not securely attested in any medieval source.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** present in an early or securely transmitted primary source.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** medieval literary synthesis that became influential.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** localized Irish, Welsh, or other tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** etymological, genealogical, or antiquarian organization by scribes.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation of originally distinguishable figures.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** orally collected or locally transmitted material whose antiquity cannot automatically be inferred.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or creative elaboration.**

The central finding is negative but decisive: **there is no recoverable ancient narrative cycle, temple cult, inscription, or securely identified image of a Celtic goddess named Danu**. Anu is genuinely attested, but sparsely. Most familiar descriptions of “Danu” combine Anu’s glossary entry, Danand’s genealogy, Welsh Dôn, Indo-European river etymologies, generalized Celtic mother-goddess imagery, and modern Pagan theology.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Anu / Ana

**[EA]** The glossary entry appears as:

> *Anu .i. mater deorum hibernensium. robo maith di nobiathad-si deos … ⁊ de cuius nomine da cigh anainne iar luachair nominatur, ut fabula ferunt.*

Approximately: “Anu, that is, mother of the gods of Ireland. Well did she nourish the gods … and from her name the Two Paps of Anu beyond Luachair are named, as the tales relate.”

The most useful searchable diplomatic witness is the [Early Irish Glossaries Database, D¹ 33](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/irishglossaries/texts.php?versionID=2). “As the tales relate” (*ut fabula ferunt*) is important: the glossator presents a received story or learned tradition, not eyewitness cult testimony.

Documented forms include:

- **Anu, Ana, Ánu, Ána** — nominative forms in editorial normalization.
- **Anann, Anainne** — oblique/genitive forms.
- **Dá Chích Anann / Dá Chích Anaine** — “the Two Paps/Breasts of Anu,” the Kerry mountains.
- **Iath nAnann** — “land of Anu,” a poetic expression for Ireland in later learned literature.
- **bandía in tṡónusa** — “goddess of prosperity,” the description associated with *Cóir Anmann*.

**[LS]** The glossary derives *anae*, “abundance,” from Anu. This is medieval etymology, not necessarily historical linguistics. The interpretation nevertheless proves that medieval scholars associated Anu with nourishment or abundance.

### Danu / Dana

**[MSR]** *Danu* and *Dana* are supplied nominatives reconstructed from *Danann*, traditionally understood as a genitive in *Tuatha Dé Danann*. They should conventionally be printed with an asterisk—***Danu, *Dana**—when the distinction between reconstruction and attested spelling matters.

No source supplies an ancient Irish spelling in a separate nominative sentence such as “Danu did X.” Even in passages where grammar might lead one to expect a nominative, manuscripts can retain *Danand/Donand*. That irregularity is one reason John Carey questioned the conventional explanation in his 1981 article, [“The Name Tuatha Dé Danann”](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/the-name-tuatha-de-danann/).

### Danann / Danand / Donand

**[LC/LS]** These forms occur:

- in the collective name **Tuath(a) Dé Danann/Donann**;
- as the name or additional name of a female genealogical figure in *Lebor Gabála Érenn*;
- in variant equations involving **Anand** and the **Morrígan**.

The usual translation “people/tribes of the goddess Danu” is traditional and possible, but not a settled grammatical fact. Carey emphasized that the shorter **Tuath Dé / Túatha Dé** is earlier and that *Danann* may be a secondary expansion.

### Tuath Dé and Tuatha Dé Danann

*Tuath* means a people, polity, or tribe; *dé* is an oblique/plural form of *día*, “god/divine being.” Early sources use **Tuath Dé**, plausibly “the divine people” or “people of the gods.” Medieval Christian Irish also used comparable wording for the biblical Israelites. One scholarly explanation is therefore:

1. **Tuath Dé** was the original supernatural collective;
2. medieval ambiguity with the biblical “people of God” became inconvenient;
3. **Danann/Donann** was added, perhaps through learned manipulation of **dán**, “skill/art,” and **Anann**, Anu’s genitive.

This is a reconstruction, not an explicit medieval explanation, but the textual distribution supports it: the ninth-century version of *De Gabáil in tSída* uses *Túatha Déa/Fir Déa*, while its Middle Irish reworking prefers *Túatha Dé Danann*. A recent discussion of that chronological evidence appears in this [Cambridge repository study](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/7362b36c-c18f-44e0-bf02-177a47463d76/content).

### Dôn

**[EA/RV]** Middle Welsh **Dôn** occurs principally through patronymic or family expressions such as **Plant Dôn**, “Children of Dôn.” The Fourth Branch names figures belonging to this kindred—Gwydion, Gilfaethwy, Arianrhod, Lleu and others—but does not narrate Dôn’s life, cult, or deeds.

The Welsh circumflex is modern orthographic marking of the long vowel; manuscript spellings vary. Her gender is inferred from genealogy and later interpretation rather than from a surviving story centered upon her.

### Proposed etymologies

1. **Anu and abundance.**  
   **[LS]** Medieval glossators connect Anu with *anae*, plenty or abundance. This establishes medieval interpretation, not necessarily the name’s original derivation.

2. **Danu and Indo-European “flow/river.”**  
   **[MSR, disputed]** Older comparative scholarship connected *Danu* with Vedic **Dānu**, Iranian/Scythian river words, and river names including Latin **Dānuvius/Danubius**, the Danube. The ancient hydronym is real; the Oxford Classical Dictionary notes that *Danuvius* denoted the Danube’s upper course and may be Celtic or Thracian in origin ([OCD, “Danuvius”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548752949)). What is not established is that the Irish name *Danann/Donann* derives from this hydronymic family or that Irish people worshipped a Danube-like river goddess.

3. **Donann/Dôn and “earth, ground.”**  
   **[MSR, disputed]** Carey proposed comparison with Old Irish *don*, “place/ground/earth,” and Indo-European words represented by Greek *khthōn*, “earth.” On this reading, Welsh *Plant Dôn* could mean something like “Children of Earth.” It competes with, rather than confirms, the river theory.

4. **Danann and dán, “skill, craft.”**  
   **[LS/MSR]** Medieval phrases such as *trí dé dána*, “three gods of craft,” may have helped create or reshape *Danann*. This fits the Tuath Dé’s literary characterization as masters of arts and occult skills, but it does not prove that *Danann* simply means “of skill.”

5. **Danu as d[ia] Anu or [ban]d[ia] Anu.**  
   **[MSR]** Suggested contraction from “god/goddess Anu.” Possible as scribal wordplay; unattested as an ancient derivation.

### Claimed epithets that are not anciently documented

“Great Mother,” “Mother Earth,” “Lady of Waters,” “Lady of the Danube,” “Queen of the Fairies,” “Triple Goddess,” “Mother of All,” and “consort of Bile” are not securely attested ancient or medieval titles of Danu. They are **[MI]** modern descriptive or devotional formulations unless a modern author is explicitly being quoted.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest individualized witness: the glossary tradition

**[EA]** *Sanas Cormaic* is traditionally associated with Cormac mac Cuilennáin, king-bishop of Cashel, who died in 908, but it is a composite glossary transmitted through multiple recensions. The Anu entry also belongs to the related early glossary tradition. Paul Russell’s study demonstrates the complicated textual relations between *Dúil Dromma Cetta* and Cormac’s Glossary ([Russell, “Dúil Dromma Cetta and Cormac’s Glossary”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ecelt_0373-1928_1996_num_32_1_2095)).

What this evidence establishes:

- Anu was known to early medieval Irish learned tradition as a female divine figure.
- She was called mother and nourisher of the Irish gods.
- the Paps in the Luachair/Kerry region were associated with her.
- her name was connected with abundance.

What it does **not** establish:

- a prehistoric date for her worship;
- that she was named Danu;
- that she was a river goddess;
- that she mothered every named Tuath Dé member biologically;
- a priesthood, temple, festival, sacrifice, image, or continuous cult.

### *Cóir Anmann*

**[MA/LS]** *Cóir Anmann*, “Fitness/Appropriateness of Names,” is a Middle to Early Modern Irish onomastic compilation preserved in late manuscripts. Its Munster entry offers competing explanations of *Mumu* and says that in Munster “was worshipped the goddess of prosperity whose name was Ána,” after whom the Two Paps were called. The text and edition metadata are available through [CELT’s *Cóir Anmann* record](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/G503002/).

This is the clearest medieval statement of **worship**, but it is centuries later than any recoverable pagan practice and belongs to learned etymology. It is evidence for medieval memory or systematization, not direct proof of a functioning pre-Christian sanctuary.

### *Lebor Gabála Érenn*

**[LC/LS]** The *Book of the Taking of Ireland* is not one text composed at one moment but a Christian synthetic history assembled and repeatedly revised between roughly the eleventh and twelfth centuries, surviving in recensions and manuscripts including the Book of Leinster and later witnesses.

Relevant variants include:

- **Danand/Donand**, daughter of Delbáeth, is mother of Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba.
- One passage makes Delbáeth both her father and the father of her sons, producing an incestuous genealogy.
- Other genealogical arrangements give Danand a different parentage, including association with Flidais.
- The Morrígan is called **Anand** in some passages.
- A recension or gloss can make **Danann** an additional name of the Morrígan and connect her both with the Paps and with the collective name.

These variants should not be harmonized. They show scribes attempting to reconcile similar names and competing genealogies. Macalister’s edition and translation are available in the [CELT-hosted *Lebor Gabála Érenn* PDF](https://www.ucc.ie/celt/LGTZ.pdf).

### *Cath Maige Tuired*

**[LC]** The Second Battle of Mag Tuired survives principally in British Library Harley MS 5280, a sixteenth-century manuscript containing a text with older linguistic and narrative strata. It presents the Túatha Dé Danann collectively—their northern cities, magical learning, treasures, kingship struggle and war with the Fomoire—but gives Danu no independent role. The text is available in [Elizabeth Gray’s CELT translation](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/T300010.html).

This absence matters. A text capable of naming many divine specialists does not summon Danu as their mother, leader, patron, or participant.

### Other Irish corpora named in the brief

- **Dindshenchas:** The place-name collections contain numerous divine women and river-origin narratives, but no securely identifiable Danu myth comparable to the stories of Bóann or Sinann. The Paps association comes most clearly through the glossary and onomastic traditions.
- **Táin Bó Cúailnge:** Its recensions contain divine interventions, especially those of the Morrígan, but no Danu. The earliest surviving recension is preserved in *Lebor na hUidre* and the Yellow Book of Lecan; the Book of Leinster preserves the second. See [CELT’s Recension I record](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/G301012/).
- **Tochmarc Étaíne:** Linguistically often assigned to the eighth–ninth centuries, partly preserved in *Lebor na hUidre* and completely in the late-fourteenth-century Yellow Book of Lecan. It gives no Danu episode.
- **Acallam na Senórach:** A substantial Finn-cycle prosimetrum, probably composed around the late twelfth or early thirteenth century. It preserves Christian encounters with Ireland’s supernatural past but no independent Danu dossier.
- **Cath Maige Tuired, the Táin, Tochmarc Étaíne and the Acallam** therefore document the larger divine and otherworldly world in which modern retellings place Danu; they do not document her supposed adventures.

### Welsh corpus

- **Fourth Branch, *Math fab Mathonwy*:** preserved in the White Book of Rhydderch and Red Book of Hergest, with composition usually placed earlier than those fourteenth-century manuscripts. Members of *Plant Dôn* dominate the narrative, but Dôn herself does not act.
- **Culhwch ac Olwen:** probably composed by about 1100, preserved in the same principal manuscript tradition. It contains vast catalogues of legendary figures but no Dôn/Danu biography.
- **Book of Taliesin:** National Library of Wales Peniarth MS 2, copied in the first half of the fourteenth century, preserving poems of varied and sometimes much earlier date. References to Dôn and her kindred occur in learned mythological allusion, not a continuous narrative. The manuscript’s date and contents are summarized by the [National Library of Wales](https://www.library.wales/fileadmin/docs_gwefan/ymweld_a_ni/arddangosfeydd/ardd_pan_4llyfr_s.pdf).
- **Welsh Triads:** later manuscript compilations preserve genealogical and heroic classifications. They help reconstruct Dôn’s family but do not identify her as Irish Danu.

### Classical, Gaulish and Romano-British corpus

A dedicated search of the relevant evidence produces no Danu or Anu:

- Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* VI.17, names Roman equivalents Mercury, Apollo, Mars, Jupiter and Minerva; VI.16 alleges human sacrifice. It does not mention Danu/Anu ([Latin and translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/6B%2A.html)).
- Lucan, *Pharsalia* I, names Teutates, Esus and Taranis; the scholia debate their sacrifices. Neither poem nor scholia names Danu.
- No accepted inscription in **RIG**, **CIL**, or **RIB** securely dedicates an altar to Danu or Anu.
- The Gundestrup Cauldron, probably made between approximately 150 BCE and the beginning of the Common Era in a Celtic–Thracian contact zone, has no identifying inscriptions. No figure can securely be called Danu ([National Museum of Denmark](https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-early-iron-age/the-gundestrup-cauldron/the-dating-and-origin-of-the-silver-cauldron/)).
- The early first-century CE Pillar of the Boatmen names Jupiter, Esus, Cernunnos, Smertrios and others, but not Danu ([French Ministry of Culture](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/paris/en/boatmens-pillar)).
- The second-century CE Coligny Calendar is a Gaulish calendrical document; it does not name Danu ([Lugdunum Museum catalogue](https://lugdunum.grandlyon.com/Oeuvre/2381-Calendrier-gaulois-de-Coligny)).

Consequently, there is no documentary basis for calling Danu a demonstrably “pan-Celtic” goddess.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Attested domains

**Anu [EA/LS]:**

- motherhood or maternal precedence among the gods;
- nourishment of the gods;
- abundance or plenty, through medieval etymology;
- the Munster landscape, particularly the Paps;
- prosperity, explicitly in later *Cóir Anmann*.

**Danand [LC]:**

- genealogical maternity of Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba;
- possibly an eponymous relationship with the Tuatha Dé Danann.

No text gives Danand a natural domain, weapon, animal, celestial body, festival, or ritual specialty.

**Dôn [RV]:**

- ancestry of the Children of Dôn;
- no explicitly stated cosmic domain.

### Modernly assigned domains

The following are **[MSR/MI]**, not securely medieval:

- primordial waters;
- rivers, rain and wells;
- earth and agricultural fertility;
- sovereignty;
- wisdom, magic or druidic knowledge;
- the moon;
- wind;
- death and regeneration;
- cattle;
- faeries;
- a maiden–mother–crone triad.

Some assignments are reasonable devotional extrapolations from “nourisher,” “prosperity,” the breast-shaped hills, or the river etymology. They remain extrapolations.

### Iconography

There is **no securely identified ancient or medieval image of Anu, Danu, Danand, or Dôn**.

The Paps themselves constitute a named sacred or mythologized landscape, not an anthropomorphic portrait. Their breast-like form made the association natural to medieval etymologists or storytellers. Summit cairns are prehistoric, but neither cairn carries an inscription naming Anu; their construction cannot be attributed to her cult merely because a medieval name later connected the hills with her.

Modern art commonly shows Danu as:

- a mature woman crowned with leaves;
- a pregnant or large-breasted earth mother;
- a woman pouring river water;
- a triple goddess;
- a green-clad sovereign;
- a figure accompanied by oak trees, cauldrons, spirals or swans.

All are **[MI]** unless described explicitly as contemporary sacred art.

### Symbols and correspondences

No ancient source assigns Danu/Anu a color, number, weekday, planet, animal, plant, weapon, or seasonal feast. Modern lists assigning green, silver, the moon, the oak, horses, fish, rivers, Beltane or Lughnasadh are modern ritual correspondences.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

| Version | Genealogy | Source/status |
|---|---|---|
| Anu as mother of the gods | No father, consort, or individual children named | *Sanas Cormaic* glossary tradition; **[EA]** |
| Danand as daughter of Delbáeth | Delbáeth fathers Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba upon her | *Lebor Gabála* recension; **[LC/LS]** |
| Danand associated with Flidais | Alternative parentage separates or rearranges the Delbáeth line | *Lebor Gabála* variants; **[RV/LS]** |
| Anand as Morrígan | Anand is a name of the Morrígan, daughter of Ernmas | *Lebor Gabála*; **[LC/LS/SI]** |
| Danann as Morrígan’s additional name | Connects the Morrígan, Paps and Tuatha Dé name | Recensional/glossarial formulation; **[SI/LS]** |
| Dôn as ancestral mother | Mother or ancestress of the Welsh divine kindred | Welsh genealogical language; **[RV]** |
| Danu as wife of Bile and mother of the Dagda | No supporting medieval Irish passage | **[MI]** |
| Danu as mother of Brigid, Lugh, Nuadu and every Tuath Dé member | Expansion of “mother of the gods” into a literal family tree | **[MI]** |

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

Because the evidence is exceptionally thin, the complete defensible list is short.

- **Delbáeth — sexual union and disputed father–daughter relationship.** Danand is made daughter of Delbáeth and mother by him of Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba. *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, medieval recensional genealogy, approximately eleventh–twelfth centuries. **[LC/LS]**
- **Brian — mother–son relation.** Danand is named as Brian’s mother. *Lebor Gabála Érenn*. **[LC/LS]**
- **Iuchar — mother–son relation.** Danand is named as Iuchar’s mother. *Lebor Gabála Érenn*. **[LC/LS]**
- **Iucharba — mother–son relation.** Danand is named as Iucharba’s mother. *Lebor Gabála Érenn*. **[LC/LS]**
- **Flidais — parent–child relation in a variant genealogy.** Some genealogical arrangements make Danand a daughter or descendant of Flidais rather than using the Delbáeth scheme consistently. *Lebor Gabála* textual variants. **[RV/LS]**
- **The Morrígan — identification, not a narrated meeting.** Anand and sometimes Danann are treated as names or additional names of the Morrígan. *Lebor Gabála* recensions and glosses. **[SI/LS]**
- **Ernmas — relation only through the Anand–Morrígan identification.** When Anand is the Morrígan, she is a daughter of Ernmas; this does not securely establish Anu or Danand as Ernmas’s daughter. *Lebor Gabála*. **[SI/LS]**
- **Badb and Macha — sisterhood only through the Anand–Morrígan equation.** The daughters of Ernmas are listed as Badb, Macha and the Morrígan/Anand. *Lebor Gabála*. **[SI/LS]**
- **The Irish gods collectively — maternity/nourishment.** Anu is called their mother and feeder, but no individual encounter is narrated. Glossary tradition, ninth–tenth-century material. **[EA]**

### Explicit absences

- No text records Danu meeting Brigid, the Dagda, Lugh, Nuadu, Boann, Manannán, Ériu or the other famous Tuatha Dé.
- No ancient or medieval text records Danu marrying Bile.
- No Irish text records a meeting between Anu/Danand and Welsh Dôn.
- No Continental inscription equates her with Matrona, Dea Matres, Sequana or another named goddess.
- The adventures of Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba in *Oidheadh Chloinne Tuireann* do not give their mother an active role equivalent to modern Danu narratives.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### There is no surviving Danu myth

This must be stated without qualification: **no medieval Irish narrative has Danu as an acting protagonist**. There is no surviving creation story, courtship, battle, river-origin tale, death tale, voyage, judgment, or festival myth about her.

### The nourishment of the gods

**[EA]** The glossary entry’s statement that Anu “nourished” or “fed” the gods is the nearest thing to a mythic action. It may preserve a fragment of a lost story, a cult title, or merely an etymological explanation of abundance. No fuller form survives.

### Naming of the Paps

**[EA/LS]** The same entry says that the Two Paps west of Luachair were named after Anu, “as the tales relate.” This implies a narrative tradition, but the glossary does not tell it. Later writers interpret the hills as the goddess’s breasts and as signs of fertility. The physical resemblance is real; the supposed prehistoric theology is unrecoverable.

### Mother of Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba

**[LC]** *Lebor Gabála* gives Danand the only substantial genealogical episode attached to the name-complex: conception of three sons by Delbáeth. It is not a creation myth and does not make her mother of the entire pantheon. The three brothers later belong to the Tuireann cycle, but their mother does not act in its surviving narrative.

### The coming of the Tuatha Dé Danann

**[LC]** Modern retellings frequently place Danu at the center of the Tuath Dé’s return to Ireland, teaching them magic or leading them through mist. *Cath Maige Tuired* instead says that the collective studied occult knowledge in four northern cities and names four male teachers. It never assigns this education to Danu. The Danu-centered version is **[MI]**.

### Dôn and the Fourth Branch

**[RV]** The Fourth Branch tells how Math and Gwydion manipulate Goewin, Gilfaethwy, Arianrhod, Lleu and Blodeuwedd. Dôn is genealogically present through her children but never enters the action. Treating the entire Fourth Branch as “Dôn’s myth” is a modern structural reading, not the narrative’s presentation.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers

The only plausible named center is the landscape of **Dá Chích Anann**, the Paps of Anu, in the Derrynasaggart Mountains of County Kerry.

What is documented:

- the medieval glossary associates the hills with Anu;
- *Cóir Anmann* says Ána was worshipped in Munster;
- the hills bear prehistoric cairns;
- the surrounding landscape accumulated later pilgrimage and folklore.

What is not documented:

- a temple dedicated to Anu;
- an altar or inscription naming her;
- a securely dated pagan ritual deposit made to her;
- a priesthood;
- a festival calendar;
- continuity between the cairn builders and the medieval name.

Nearby **Cathair Crobh Dearg** became the focus of Christian-era ritual rounds and local traditions. These may show the continuing sacralization of the Paps landscape, but identifying Crobh Dearg as Danu or treating Christian rounds as an unchanged Anu cult is conjectural. A useful archaeological-folkloric study is [“Cathair Crobh Dearg: From Ancient Beliefs to the Rounds”](https://www.estudiosirlandeses.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Art%C3%ADculo-1.pdf).

### Offerings, sacrifice and priesthood

None is securely attested for Anu/Danu.

Caesar’s allegations of Gallic human sacrifice cannot be transferred to an Irish goddess whom Caesar does not name. Lucan’s sacrificial notices concern other named Continental gods. There is no evidence for:

- human sacrifice to Danu or Anu;
- temple prostitution;
- sexual rites;
- druidesses serving her;
- animal sacrifice specifically to her;
- cauldron rites;
- river drowning or votive deposition in her name.

Claims of such practices are normally **[MI]** extrapolations from generalized accounts of Celtic religion.

### Theophoric names

No securely identified ancient Irish, Gaulish or Romano-British personal name demonstrates devotion to Danu/Anu. Similar-looking names require individual linguistic analysis and cannot simply be treated as theophoric.

### Suppression and conversion

There is no documented event in which a shrine of Anu was closed, a statue destroyed, or her priesthood converted. Ireland’s Christianization transformed the recording and classification of indigenous supernatural beings, but a specific “suppression of Danu” cannot be reconstructed.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before the ninth century

**Evidence:** none securely naming Anu or Danu.

The prehistoric cairns on the Paps establish ancient activity at the hills, not the name or cult of their divinity. No Iron Age inscription or image closes the gap.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: Anu enters the written record

**[EA]** The glossary tradition calls Anu mother and nourisher of the Irish gods and associates her with the Paps. This is the historical foundation of the dossier.

### Ninth–eleventh centuries: Tuath Dé becomes a literary collective

Early Irish narrative describes the supernatural race as **Tuath Dé/Túatha Dé**. Their members are euhemerized as earlier settlers, kings, craftsmen and magicians within Christian universal history.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: Danann/Donann and genealogical synthesis

**[LC/LS]** *Lebor Gabála* expands the collective name and creates or records Danand genealogies. Similar names—Anu, Anand, Danand, Donand—are variously separated or conflated. This is the period in which the later “Danu” problem principally takes shape.

### High and late Middle Ages: localized prosperity goddess

**[MA/LS]** *Cóir Anmann* explicitly describes Ána as a goddess of prosperity worshipped in Munster. Welsh manuscripts preserve Dôn’s kindred without equating her with Irish Anu.

### Early modern antiquarianism

Genealogical and place-name traditions continue to be copied. Seathrún Céitinn/Geoffrey Keating’s seventeenth-century historical synthesis helped transmit the Tuatha Dé Danann as an ordered national pantheon. Learned harmonization increasingly encouraged readers to treat variant names as one goddess.

### Nineteenth-century comparative mythology

Scholars including John Rhys and others connected Irish Danu, Welsh Dôn, Vedic Dānu and river names such as Danube and Don. Comparative Indo-European study made such proposals intellectually plausible, but often converted hypotheses into family trees more confidently than the Irish evidence allowed.

### 1904 and the Literary Revival

Lady Gregory’s *Gods and Fighting Men*, with a preface by W. B. Yeats, popularized “the people of the gods of Dana” for English-language readers. Gregory did not provide an independent ancient Danu cycle, but her smooth narrative presentation made the divine collective feel like a coherent national pantheon. The full text is available from [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14465/pg14465-images.html).

### Twentieth century: dictionary canonization

Reference works by MacCulloch, Squire, Rees and Rees, Anne Ross, Miranda Green, Dáithí Ó hÓgáin, James MacKillop, Bernhard Maier and John T. Koch repeated, refined or challenged the maternal and comparative reconstruction. Popular summaries frequently retained the maximalist version after specialists had become more cautious.

### Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries: revived worship

**[MI as historical origin; living religion as present fact]** Modern Druids, Celtic Pagans, Wiccans and eclectic practitioners worship Danu as a mother, earth, river or sovereignty goddess. These are genuine contemporary religious practices but are not demonstrably continuous with an ancient Danu cult.

A revealing example of internal correction comes from the New Order of Druids, which states that older course material called Danu a “primal mother goddess” but that this presentation was being revised in light of historical criticism ([course update](https://www.druidcircle.org/nod/index.php/en/home/nod-news/302-updates-on-the-bardic-ovate-courses)).

No reliable census isolates the number of Danu devotees. Individual testimony demonstrates practice, not scale or antiquity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Celtic Revival

Yeats, Lady Gregory and their contemporaries transformed difficult manuscript traditions into a usable national mythology. Danu herself remained mostly offstage, but translating the collective as “people of the gods/goddess of Dana” helped normalize Dana/Danu as their maternal source.

### Occult and esoteric reception

Danu has no established role in Renaissance grimoires, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn’s core grade rituals, or premodern ceremonial magic. Claims that she was an ancient initiatory patroness, Atlantean goddess or secret name of a universal Great Mother are **[MI]**.

Twentieth-century Goddess spirituality, Jungian archetypal writing, Wicca and modern Druidry supplied the missing personality:

- earth mother;
- cosmic womb;
- primordial water;
- divine feminine;
- triple goddess;
- mother of a divine race;
- ecological guardian.

These themes often reflect the modern movements’ concerns more than medieval Irish evidence.

### The Bile narrative

The claim that Danu married **Bile**, represented by a sacred oak, and bore the Dagda is widely circulated online. No cited medieval Irish text supplies this story. Bile in *Lebor Gabála* belongs to Milesian genealogy, not securely to a divine marriage with Danu. The marriage is **[MI]**.

A 2019 RTÉ/Herstory feature repeats the Bile union and a Danu-led return from exile ([“Herstory: Danu”](https://www.rte.ie/culture/herstory/2019/0903/1073499-herstory-danu/)). It is valuable evidence for twenty-first-century reception, not for early Irish religion.

### Literature, comics, music and games

- Pat Mills and collaborators’ *Sláine* comics use Danu as a powerful tribal mother goddess. **[MI]**
- The Japanese role-playing game *Shin Megami Tensei V* includes Danu as an individualized mother-goddess character. **[MI]**
- The Irish traditional band **Danú**, formed in the 1990s, uses the name in a modern cultural setting.
- Fantasy novels and spiritual manuals frequently give Danu dialogue, consorts, children, colors and ritual preferences absent from medieval sources.

These works have materially shaped the popular image of Danu as a fully narrated deity.

### Political and nationalist uses

The Tuatha Dé Danann became part of Irish cultural nationalism through the Literary Revival. Danu can consequently symbolize ancestral Ireland, indigeneity, feminine sovereignty or ecological nationhood. There is no major, well-documented extremist cult centered upon her comparable to modern appropriations of some Germanic symbols. Assertions of such a movement require case-specific evidence.

### Commercial use

Danu appears on statues, oracle decks, jewelry, candles and “Celtic goddess” merchandise. Common commercial imagery—triple spirals, pregnant earth mother, cauldron, flowing river—is modern composite iconography.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The identity problem

There are four distinguishable evidentiary units:

1. **Anu**, explicitly named and described in the glossary tradition;
2. **Danann/Donann**, in the divine collective’s name;
3. **Danand**, a genealogical woman in *Lebor Gabála*;
4. **Dôn**, ancestral figure of Welsh *Plant Dôn*.

Older scholarship frequently treated all four as reflexes of one pan-Celtic mother goddess. Current caution asks whether the similarities arose through common inheritance, scribal conflation, phonetic resemblance, modern comparison—or some combination.

### Carey’s intervention

John Carey’s 1981 article remains central. He emphasized:

- earlier **Tuath Dé** versus later **Tuatha Dé Danann**;
- the form **Donann** and its grammatical problems;
- possible influence from *dán*, “craft,” and Anu/Anann;
- the danger of assuming the collective was always named after a goddess.

His theory is influential, not universally conclusive.

### River-goddess theory

The comparison with Vedic Dānu and the Danube is attractive because:

- Indo-European *dānu-* is genuinely associated with water or rivers in several linguistic traditions;
- Celtic religion includes named river goddesses such as Sequana, Bóann and Sinann;
- divine collectives named through an ancestress have comparanda.

Against it:

- Irish **Danu** is unattested;
- early forms include **Donann**, creating phonological difficulties;
- Anu has no surviving water association;
- no Irish river bears her name in a primary source;
- similarity does not prove identity or transmission.

Verdict: **possible remote comparison, not an established Irish cult or domain**.

### Anu and Danu

Arguments for identity:

- Anu is mother of the gods; *Danann* appears to name the divine people;
- *Anann* and *Danann* are graphically and phonetically close;
- medieval scribes themselves sometimes connect Danann/Anand, the Morrígan and the Paps.

Arguments against:

- the initial *d-* needs explanation;
- Danand has particular genealogies absent from Anu’s glossary notice;
- *Lebor Gabála* variants expose active scribal harmonization;
- similar function and name do not prove original identity.

Verdict: **a medieval or modern equation is documented; an original identity is unproved**.

### Dôn and Danu

Arguments for:

- both appear as ancestral names of divine families;
- the forms resemble one another;
- Irish and Welsh mythological traditions share genuine cognate figures elsewhere.

Arguments against:

- Dôn may have a different etymology, possibly “earth”;
- no medieval source equates them;
- Dôn’s children and Danand’s sons do not correspond closely;
- the Irish nominative used in the comparison is itself reconstructed.

Verdict: **historically important comparative hypothesis, not a secure cognate equation**.

### State of the evidence

Primary manuscripts are held principally in:

- Trinity College Dublin;
- the Royal Irish Academy;
- the National Library of Ireland;
- the British Library;
- the Bodleian Library;
- the National Library of Wales.

Digital access through [CELT](https://www.ucc.ie/en/research-sites/celt/), the Early Irish Glossaries Database, Irish Script on Screen and National Library of Wales resources has made recensional comparison easier. The greatest evidentiary loss is not a single known destroyed “Book of Danu,” but the general absence of pre-Christian Irish writing and the survival of indigenous traditions only through medieval Christian manuscripts.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Danu is securely the oldest Celtic goddess.
- She was worshipped throughout the Celtic world.
- the Danube was certainly named for her.
- she married Bile and bore the Dagda.
- Brigid was her daughter.
- she led the Tuatha Dé from Atlantis or the Danube valley.
- Gundestrup depicts her.
- the Paps cairns were proven temples to her.
- Christian scribes systematically deleted a once-extensive Danu scripture.
- she had an ancient maiden–mother–crone cult.
- Danu, Welsh Dôn, Vedic Dānu, Greek Danaë and Hebrew Dinah are one goddess.

Each is either **[MSR]** at best or **[MI]** in its strong form.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Prehistory | Cairns are built on the Paps in Kerry. No inscription or contemporary evidence connects them with Anu. |
| c. 150–1 BCE | Gundestrup Cauldron produced in a Celtic–Thracian artistic environment; no named Danu image. |
| 50s BCE | Caesar describes Gallic gods and sacrifice in *Bellum Gallicum* VI; no Danu/Anu. |
| c. 14–37 CE | Pillar of the Boatmen erected in Lutetia under Tiberius; several Gallic gods named, not Danu. |
| c. 60 CE | Lucan names Teutates, Esus and Taranis; no Danu. |
| Second century CE | Coligny Calendar produced; no Danu theonym. |
| Early medieval period | The supernatural collective appears as *Tuath Dé/Túatha Dé* in Irish literary tradition. |
| Ninth–tenth centuries | Glossary tradition records Anu as *mater deorum hibernensium*, nourisher of the gods, and eponym of the Paps. Earliest secure individualized evidence. |
| Eleventh century | Expansion from *Tuath Dé* toward *Tuatha Dé Danann/Donann* becomes prominent in Middle Irish literature. |
| Eleventh–twelfth centuries | *Lebor Gabála Érenn* recensions develop Danand’s genealogy and competing Anand/Danann/Morrígan equations. |
| Twelfth century | Book of Leinster preserves major Irish mythological and historical synthesis. |
| Late twelfth–early thirteenth century | *Acallam na Senórach* reworks pagan legendary geography within a Christian frame; no independent Danu narrative. |
| Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries | Welsh manuscript tradition preserves the Children of Dôn and the Fourth Branch; Dôn remains non-narrative. |
| Fourteenth century | Book of Taliesin copied, preserving varied mythological allusions involving Dôn’s kindred. |
| Late medieval/early modern period | *Cóir Anmann* states that Ána, goddess of prosperity, was worshipped in Munster. |
| Seventeenth century | Keating and other learned historians transmit an organized Tuatha Dé Danann pseudohistory. |
| Nineteenth century | Comparative Celtic and Indo-European scholarship links Danu with Dôn, Vedic Dānu and river names. |
| 1904 | Lady Gregory publishes *Gods and Fighting Men*, popularizing “the gods of Dana” in Revival English. |
| 1981 | John Carey publishes “The Name Tuatha Dé Danann,” challenging the assumed ancient goddess-name. |
| Late twentieth century | Danu becomes established in neopagan, Goddess-spirituality, fantasy and commercial iconography. |
| 2019 | RTÉ/Herstory presents a modern synthesized Danu story, including Bile and leadership of the returning Tuatha Dé. |
| Twenty-first century | Druids, reconstructionists and eclectic Pagans continue to worship Danu, while historically oriented groups increasingly label her biography as reconstruction. |
| 2026 | Scholarly position remains cautious: Anu is attested; Danu is reconstructed; Danand and Dôn are comparable but not securely identical. |

### Final assessment

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not the biography of a lost, richly documented pan-Celtic mother goddess. It is the history of a **small early medieval notice about Anu**, a **problematic divine collective-name**, a set of **contradictory literary genealogies**, a **Welsh ancestral name**, and the enormous comparative and devotional structure later built upon them.

Anu is securely present in medieval Irish learned tradition. Danand is securely present in medieval genealogy. Dôn is securely present in Welsh kinship terminology. **Danu as a primordial earth-and-river mother with a husband, children, symbols, myths and ancient international cult is chiefly a modern reconstruction.** That reconstruction has become religiously and culturally real for modern communities, but its documentary thinness must remain visible.
