# Dôn and Beli Mawr: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Dôn is a medieval Welsh ancestral parent, conventionally reconstructed in modern scholarship as a goddess; the surviving medieval texts never explicitly call Dôn a deity. Beli Mawr is a legendary ancestor-king and dynastic progenitor, not securely an ancient god. Their presentation as a primordial divine couple is a modern reconstruction.
>
> **Earliest secure attestations:** Beli Mawr appears at the head of Welsh royal pedigrees whose earliest recoverable strata belong to the ninth–tenth centuries, although the principal manuscript, British Library Harley MS 3859, was copied around 1100. Dôn is securely named as the parent of Gwydion, Gilfaethwy, and Aranrhod in the Fourth Branch of the *Mabinogi*, probably composed in the eleventh or twelfth century and preserved in manuscripts from approximately 1350 onward. No pre-Roman or Romano-British inscription securely names either figure.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Dôn

**Dôn**—Middle Welsh *Don*, modern spelling *Dôn*—is the parent from whom several figures in medieval Welsh literature take the patronymic *mab Dôn* “son of Dôn” or *ferch Dôn* “daughter of Dôn.” The Fourth Branch identifies Gwydion, Gilfaethwy, and Aranrhod as children of Dôn and simultaneously describes them as the nephews and niece of Math, “children of his sister.” Dôn is therefore inferred to be Math’s sister; the text does not state this in the form of an independent genealogy and does not name Mathonwy as Dôn’s parent.

Dôn has no surviving epithet, prayer, temple title, narrated birth, speech, action, physical description, or cult. *Plant Dôn*, “the Children of Dôn,” is useful modern shorthand for the family, but it should not be mistaken for the self-designation of an attested ancient pantheon.

The figure’s gender is not grammatically demonstrated by the name alone. The kinship wording of the Fourth Branch strongly favors a female Dôn: Gwydion and Gilfaethwy are Math’s nephews and “sons of his sister,” while Aranrhod is his niece. Early-modern antiquaries nevertheless sometimes treated Dôn as male. Iolo Morganwg made Dôn a male king of Scandinavia and Dublin and an invader of Anglesey and Arfon. That material belongs to Iolo’s late eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century synthetic antiquarian tradition, not to the medieval narrative record. Bartrum’s documentary survey identifies this explicitly as Iolo’s account and distinguishes it from the medieval evidence ([Welsh Classical Dictionary, D–F](https://www.library.wales/fileadmin/docs_gwefan/new_structure/discover/digital_exhibitions/printed_material/welsh_classical_dictionary/05_D-E-F.pdf)).

#### Etymology and the Danu problem

Three positions must be distinguished:

1. **Older comparativist reconstruction:** John Rhŷs and many twentieth-century mythographers equated Dôn with an Irish *Danu/Donu*, understood as the mother of the *Tuatha Dé Danann*. Similarity of name and apparent status as ancestral mother supplied the argument. This became the familiar “Welsh Dôn = Irish Danu” equation.

2. **Textual limitation:** Old Irish sources chiefly supply the oblique form *Danann/Donann*. The nominative **Danu** is reconstructed, not securely present as the name of a narratively developed Irish goddess. Medieval Irish writers themselves offer different explanations for *Tuatha Dé Danann*. The Irish corpus therefore does not furnish a detailed goddess whose biography can simply be transferred to Dôn.

3. **Alternative linguistic reconstruction:** John Carey and scholars following this line have considered connection with a Celtic word for “earth/ground,” ultimately compared with Indo-European *\*dʰéǵʰōm-* “earth.” This would fit an ancestral female figure but remains an etymological reconstruction, not a medieval gloss. The older association with river names such as the Danube and with Vedic Dānu is contested on phonological grounds.

Accordingly, “earth mother,” “river goddess,” “mother of all gods,” and “Welsh Danu” are **modern scholarly or religious reconstructions**, not titles borne by Dôn in a medieval source.

### Beli Mawr

**Beli Mawr** means “Beli the Great.” Medieval spellings include *Beli*, while Latin and English chronicles offer forms such as *Belinus*, *Bellinus*, and *Heli*. These forms do not necessarily designate one original person.

Beli’s securely attested medieval class is **ancestral king/progenitor**. Royal pedigrees locate him at the legendary opening of British dynasties; poems and prose make famous Britons his sons. Calling him a “sun god,” “god of light,” “death god,” or universal father belongs to scholarly speculation or modern pagan systematization.

#### Beli, Bellinus son of Minocannus, and Beli son of Manogan

A major source of confusion is the corrupted textual afterlife of the historical Cunobelinus and his son Adminius:

- Suetonius wrote of *Adminio, Cunobellini Britannorum regis filio*.
- Orosius transmitted a corrupted form resembling *Minocynobellinum*.
- The ninth-century *Historia Brittonum* separated the components into *Bellinus filius Minocanni*, “Bellinus son of Minocannus.”
- Welsh tradition consequently knew **Beli ap Manogan/Mynogan**.

This chain explains one medieval Beli construction but does not exhaust the independent Welsh tradition of Beli Mawr. Rachel Bromwich argued that Beli was too firmly embedded in Welsh genealogical and poetic tradition to be wholly explained as a corruption of this Latin passage.

#### Beli and Belenus

The equation **Beli = Belenus** is old and influential but unproved.

Belenus or Belinus is an independently attested Gallo-Roman deity, especially in Aquileia, Cisalpine Gaul, and Noricum. A second-century altar from Aquileia reads *[Apo]llini Belen(o)*, “to Apollo Belenus,” and is catalogued as CIL V 8212; it was found at Beligna in 1861 and is now in the Museo Archeologico at Aquileia ([University of Graz epigraphic record](https://gams.uni-graz.at/o%3Aepsg.796/sdef%3ATEI/get?context=context%3Aepsg.corpus%21context%3Aepsg.corpus.cil)). Herodian, describing the siege of Aquileia in 238 CE, says that Belenus was intensely worshipped there and identified with Apollo ([Herodian VIII.3](https://ancienttexts.org/library/celtic/ctexts/classical_herodian.html)). Modern epigraphic reviews locate the strongest concentration at Aquileia and in the eastern Alpine region, not Wales ([Haeussler, “Belenus”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/9781444338386.wbeah17070)).

The linguistic objection is that Brittonic *Belinos/Belenos* should normally yield Welsh **Belyn**, not **Beli**; names such as Cunobelinus > Cynfelyn illustrate the expected development. John Koch instead connected Beli with *Belgius/Bolgios*, potentially associating the ancestor with the Belgae. Neither derivation proves Beli’s historical nature.

Thus:

- Belenus’s cult is securely attested.
- Beli Mawr’s medieval literary and genealogical record is securely attested.
- Their identity is a **contested scholarly reconstruction**, not an ancient equation recorded in an inscription or medieval text.
- Belenus’s iconography, healing or solar associations, sanctuaries, and rites cannot automatically be assigned to Beli.

### Dôn and Beli as a couple

No surviving medieval narrative describes a marriage, sexual union, household, cult, or conversation between Dôn and Beli Mawr. A version of Welsh Triad 35 calls Arianrhod *merch Beli eu mam*, literally “daughter of Beli, their mother,” while the Fourth Branch makes her daughter of Dôn. The triadic wording is textually difficult: Beli is otherwise male, and *eu mam* may reflect corruption. Later interpreters harmonized the two traditions by making Dôn the mother and Beli the father of Arianrhod, then extended that pairing to all of Dôn’s children. That harmonized “divine couple” is not stated in the Fourth Branch.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Manuscript foundations

The Four Branches survive principally in:

- **The White Book of Rhydderch**, National Library of Wales, Peniarth MS 4, copied in the second and third quarters of the fourteenth century. Its contents include the Four Branches and *Lludd and Llefelys* ([NLW manuscript catalogue](https://archives.library.wales/index.php/llyfr-gwyn-rhydderch%3Bisad?sf_culture=en)).
- **The Red Book of Hergest**, Jesus College MS 111, copied approximately 1382–1410.
- Older fragments of the Second and Third Branches survive, but none supplies a pre-Christian witness to Dôn.

The stories’ linguistic and literary formation predates these manuscripts, generally being placed in the eleventh or twelfth century. Claims that their plots reproduce prehistoric myth may be plausible in individual cases, but they do not turn the surviving texts into Iron Age documents.

### Dôn’s corpus

#### The Fourth Branch, *Math fab Mathonwy*

**Tier: medieval Welsh narrative; securely attested manuscript evidence, not direct evidence of ancient cult.**

The text says:

- Gilfaethwy son of Dôn and Gwydion son of Dôn are Math’s nephews, “sons of his sister.”
- Aranrhod daughter of Dôn is Math’s niece.
- Dôn does not enter the scene or act.

A complete modern translation preserves these relationships directly ([*Math fab Mathonwy*](https://mabinogi.net/math.htm)). The narrative concerns the acts of Dôn’s descendants—Gwydion’s deception of Pryderi, Gilfaethwy’s assault on Goewin, Math’s punishment of the brothers, and the births of Dylan and Lleu—not Dôn’s deeds.

#### *Culhwch ac Olwen*

**Tier: medieval Arthurian narrative, probably c. 1100, surviving in fourteenth-century manuscripts.**

Ysbaddaden’s impossible tasks require:

- **Amaethon son of Dôn** to till a field;
- **Gofannon son of Dôn** to prepare or tend the plough-irons.

The passage makes Amaethon an incomparable agricultural expert and Gofannon a smith but says nothing about Dôn beyond parentage ([text and scholarly excerpt](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9f/The_Mabinogion_Volume_I.pdf)).

#### *Cad Goddeu*, “The Battle of the Trees”

**Tier: difficult medieval praise/legendary poem, Book of Taliesin; interpretation disputed.**

The poem associates Amaethon and Gwydion with a battle precipitated by something taken from Annwfn. Later prose and commentary connect the battle with a dispute between the sons of Dôn. It does not narrate Dôn’s participation.

#### Other names

Medieval and later genealogical compilations attach numerous additional children to Dôn, including Amaethon, Gofannon, and several obscure figures. Only Gwydion, Gilfaethwy, and Aranrhod are explicitly children of Dôn in the Fourth Branch; Amaethon and Gofannon have separate medieval patronymic attestations. Expansive lists including Nudd, Penarddun, or every northern Welsh supernatural figure are learned harmonizations.

### Beli’s corpus

#### Early Welsh royal genealogies

**Tier: early-medieval genealogical construction, copied c. 1100.**

The Harleian genealogies in British Library Harley MS 3859 trace the Merfynion dynasty through Cunedda to Beli Mawr. David Thornton and Ben Guy’s work shows that early Welsh pedigrees grew by textual layering: short historically plausible lines were extended backward to prestigious primordial ancestors. Beli’s position therefore documents early-medieval dynastic memory and legitimation, not a datable Iron Age monarch. The genealogical collections contain material formed in the ninth and tenth centuries, while Harley 3859 is the surviving later copy ([Ben Guy, “The earliest Welsh genealogies”](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/9730b98e-0a95-43f2-8179-f17840cc4bfe/content)).

#### The Second Branch, *Branwen ferch Llŷr*

**Tier: medieval canonical Welsh narrative.**

Penarddun is named as “daughter of Beli son of Manogan.” She is mother of Brân, Branwen, and Manawydan by Llŷr and of Nisien and Efnisien by Euroswydd. Beli himself takes no action. The source therefore attests a genealogical relation, not Beli’s godhood or marriage to Dôn ([Second Branch passage](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/the-celts-and-celtic-life/celtic-religion/celtic-deities-and-heroes/penarddun/)).

#### *Lludd and Llefelys*

**Tier: medieval learned legendary history.**

The tale opens by saying that Beli Mawr son of Manogan had three sons—Lludd, Caswallawn, and Nynniaw—and adds Llefelys as another son. After Beli’s death, Lludd inherits Britain. The narrative then concerns Lludd and Llefelys’s response to three plagues; Beli is an ancestral point of departure, not an acting deity.

#### Book of Taliesin prophetic verse

A poem speaks of the **“seven sons of Beli”** and names Caswallawn, Lludd, Cestuddyn, Diwed, Plo, Coll, and Iago. Its context is political prophecy and legendary British history, not a divine cosmogony ([Book of Taliesin XLVII](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-four-ancient-books-of-wales/shell/lx-book-of-taliessin-xlvii)).

#### Welsh Triads

The Triads repeatedly call Caswallawn “son of Beli”; they also connect Arianrhod with Beli in the difficult Triad 35 and call Afarwy the son of Lludd son of Beli. These are discrete mnemonic traditions preserved in several recensions, not one stable genealogy. The Hergest recension’s wording can be consulted here: [Hergest Triads](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/celtic/ctexts/triads1.html).

#### Geoffrey of Monmouth

Two figures require separation:

1. **Heli**, father of Lud, Cassibellaunus, and Nennius, is Geoffrey’s counterpart to Beli in the line of British kings.
2. **Belinus**, son of Dunvallo Molmutius and brother of Brennius, is an entirely different legendary king. Geoffrey narrates their civil war, reconciliation, continental campaign, and confrontation with Rome.

Geoffrey’s *Historia regum Britanniae*, completed around 1136 and revised thereafter, profoundly reorganized British legendary history. His Belinus must not be merged with Beli Mawr merely because Latin spelling makes them look similar. Geoffrey’s Latin calls the rival brothers *Belinus* and *Brennius* ([Latin Book III](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Historia_Regum_Britanniae/Liber_III)); the narrative is available in translation here: [*Historia Regum Britanniae*](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/classical-references/historia-regum-britanniae/).

### Corpora that do not attest them

A dedicated check finds **no Dôn or Beli Mawr** in:

- *Lebor Gabála Érenn*;
- *Cath Maige Tuired*;
- the *Dindshenchas*;
- *Táin Bó Cúailnge*;
- *Tochmarc Étaíne*;
- *Acallam na Senórach*;
- Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* VI;
- Lucan, *Pharsalia* I or its scholia;
- the Gaulish *Recueil des inscriptions gauloises*;
- the Romano-British *RIB* corpus;
- the Gundestrup Cauldron;
- the Pillar of the Boatmen;
- the Coligny Calendar.

Those sources are indispensable for Celtic religions generally but do not constitute evidence for Dôn or Beli. The Irish texts attest the *Tuatha Dé Danann* and later explanations of that name; they do not independently narrate the Welsh Dôn. Caesar’s unnamed “Dis Pater” cannot be identified with Beli without circular reconstruction.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Dôn

The securely attested attributes are genealogical:

- parent of Gwydion, Gilfaethwy, and Aranrhod;
- inferred sister of Math;
- eponymous ancestress of a family whose members possess magic, agriculture, smithcraft, and celestial associations.

Dôn is not assigned those descendants’ functions. No medieval text calls Dôn goddess of earth, rivers, fertility, motherhood, magic, the moon, or the stars.

**Iconography:** none securely identified. No Romano-British sculpture, coin, altar, or manuscript image bears Dôn’s name. Modern images of Dôn as an earth mother, crowned matriarch, or star goddess are creative representations.

**Caer Dôn:** Modern Welsh astronomical lists sometimes give *Caer Dôn*, “Dôn’s fortress,” for Cassiopeia; *Llys Dôn*, “Dôn’s court,” also circulates. The antiquity and exact manuscript pedigree of these constellation names are poorly documented. They should be classified as **Welsh astronomical folklore or learned nomenclature**, not evidence that ancient Britons worshipped Dôn as Cassiopeia. The better-attested celestial place-name in medieval tradition is *Caer Arianrhod*, associated with Arianrhod rather than Dôn.

### Beli Mawr

The securely attested attributes are:

- remote royal ancestor;
- father or predecessor of notable British kings;
- marker of dynastic prestige;
- possessor, in poetic allusion, of a bloody or battle-associated spear.

There is no securely identified image of Beli. Solar wheels, chariots, thunderbolts, healing springs, Beltane fires, and Apollo’s attributes belong either to Belenus or to modern syncretism. Assigning them to Beli depends entirely on first accepting the disputed Beli–Belenus equation.

No sacred animal, plant, color, number, festival, or celestial body is securely associated with Beli Mawr in medieval Welsh evidence.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or supernatural being

Because neither figure receives an extant active myth, the list is short. Kinship statements are included; invented interactions are not.

#### Dôn

- **Gwydion — parent/child:** Gwydion is called *mab Dôn*. Fourth Branch, probably eleventh–twelfth century; **medieval canonical attestation**.
- **Gilfaethwy — parent/child:** Gilfaethwy is called *mab Dôn*. Fourth Branch; **medieval canonical attestation**.
- **Aranrhod — parent/child:** Aranrhod is called *ferch Dôn*. Fourth Branch; **medieval canonical attestation**.
- **Amaethon — parent/child:** Amaethon is called son of Dôn in *Culhwch ac Olwen*. **Medieval canonical attestation**.
- **Gofannon — parent/child:** Gofannon is called son of Dôn in *Culhwch ac Olwen*. **Medieval canonical attestation**.
- **Math — inferred siblingship:** Dôn’s children are Math’s sister’s children. **Secure implication, not an explicit “Dôn was Math’s sister” sentence**.
- **Beli Mawr — alleged consort:** no direct medieval interaction. **Learned harmonization/modern reconstruction**.
- **Danu/Donu — alleged equivalence:** no textual meeting or ancient equation. **Modern comparative reconstruction**.

Dôn performs no securely preserved meeting, combat, seduction, judgment, theft, rescue, punishment, or joint action with another named being.

#### Beli Mawr

- **Penarddun — father/daughter:** Second Branch. **Medieval canonical genealogy**.
- **Lludd — father/son and succession:** *Lludd and Llefelys* says Lludd receives Britain after Beli’s death. **Medieval legendary history**.
- **Llefelys — father/son:** *Lludd and Llefelys*. **Medieval legendary history**.
- **Caswallawn — father/son:** Triads, prophetic poetry, and prose. **Medieval canonical genealogy**.
- **Nynniaw — father/son:** *Lludd and Llefelys*. **Medieval legendary history**.
- **Arianrhod — parent/child in Triad 35:** textually problematic and inconsistent with the Fourth Branch. **Regional/manuscript variant or scribal corruption**.
- **Dôn — alleged spouse:** no direct medieval encounter. **Learned/modern systematization**.
- **Belenus — alleged identity:** no ancient or medieval source equates them. **Contested modern scholarly reconstruction**.
- **Geoffrey’s Belinus — frequent modern conflation:** distinct genealogy and narrative. **Medieval pseudo-history, secondarily conflated**.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Dôn: an absent matriarch in the Fourth Branch

There is no surviving “myth of Dôn.” Her narrative importance lies in organizing a family whose actions dominate the Fourth Branch.

Gwydion engineers a war with Pryderi so Math will leave his court, allowing Gilfaethwy to assault Goewin. Math punishes the brothers by transforming them successively into deer, boar, and wolves of alternating sex, requiring them to mate and bear offspring. Later Aranrhod fails Math’s virginity test and gives birth to Dylan and Lleu. Gwydion raises Lleu and overcomes Aranrhod’s prohibitions against the child’s name, weapons, and human wife. Math and Gwydion create Blodeuedd from flowers; she conspires with Gronw to kill Lleu; Gwydion restores Lleu, transforms Blodeuedd into an owl, and arranges Gronw’s death.

All of this is tradition about **Dôn’s family**, not about Dôn acting. Making Dôn the commanding mother of a “tribe of gods” fills a genuine gap but goes beyond the evidence.

### Beli: ancestor at the threshold of stories

Beli likewise functions principally before the action:

- In *Lludd and Llefelys*, his death initiates Lludd’s reign. Lludd and Llefelys defeat three plagues: the Coraniaid, a debilitating scream caused by battling dragons, and the theft of royal provisions.
- The Second Branch uses Beli to locate Penarddun within a prestigious genealogy.
- Triadic and prophetic sources make Caswallawn and other heroes sons of Beli.
- Genealogies use Beli to anchor later dynasties in Britain’s legendary past.

### Geoffrey’s Belinus and Brennius

**Medieval accretion; not safely a Beli Mawr myth.**

After Dunvallo Molmutius dies, his sons Belinus and Brennius dispute the succession. Belinus, as elder, receives the chief crown; Brennius receives the north. Their agreement collapses, Brennius seeks Scandinavian support, and Belinus defeats him. Their mother reconciles them. The brothers campaign in Gaul, Germany, and Italy and confront Rome. Belinus returns to Britain, confirms his father’s laws, builds roads and cities, and enjoys an idealized reign.

This is Geoffrey’s synthetic British imperial pseudo-history, incorporating classical Brennus traditions and medieval political ideas. It should not be retold as an ancient myth of the Welsh god Beli.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Dôn

No temple, altar, inscription, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, votive object, theophoric name, oracle, sacred site, or ritual addressed to Dôn is known from ancient Britain.

### Beli Mawr

The same negative finding applies to Beli Mawr. No *RIB* dedication invokes him. There is no archaeologically established shrine or priesthood.

The evidence for Belenus must remain separate. Belenus possessed:

- Latin votive inscriptions;
- a major concentration of worship at Aquileia;
- identification with Apollo;
- literary testimony from Tertullian and Herodian;
- probable official and imperial dedications.

None names Beli Mawr, Dôn, or Wales. Transferring this cult to Beli is possible only through the disputed name equation.

### Human sacrifice and other contested cult claims

No source associates Dôn or Beli Mawr with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, or an organized sacrificial priesthood. Classical descriptions of Gallic sacrifice and Lucan’s sensationalized gods cannot be reassigned to them. Conversely, the Fourth Branch does contain rape, coercion, magical punishment, war, and killing—but these are narrative acts of Dôn’s descendants, not documented rites.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Roman Britain: no secure evidence

Romano-British inscriptions document many native deities, but not Dôn or Beli Mawr. The absence is important. Neither the Gundestrup Cauldron nor the Pillar of the Boatmen provides an image identifiable as either figure.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: Beli as dynastic terminus

Beli becomes visible in the recoverable Welsh genealogical tradition as a remote ancestor. His principal social function is political and genealogical: he supplies venerable common ancestry for ruling houses.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: literary formation

The Four Branches and *Culhwch ac Olwen* give Dôn’s children their surviving literary setting. *Lludd and Llefelys*, Triadic lore, prophetic verse, and genealogies expand Beli’s descendant network.

### c. 1136 onward: Geoffrey’s restructuring

Geoffrey of Monmouth converts and rearranges Welsh and Latin legendary matter into a continuous British royal chronicle. Heli and the separate Belinus become internationally influential through Latin and vernacular adaptations. Subsequent readers repeatedly conflate them with Beli Mawr.

### Fourteenth century: surviving manuscript canon

The White and Red Books preserve the chief prose witnesses. These manuscripts are the physical evidence; conjectured pagan originals are not extant.

### Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries: antiquarian harmonization

Genealogists and antiquaries expanded pedigrees and synchronized them with biblical, classical, and British history. Dôn’s gender and ancestry remained unstable. Beli accrued identifications with Heli, Belinus, Belenus, and sometimes biblical or Trojan figures.

### Late eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: Celtic Revival

Iolo Morganwg’s forged and synthetic bardic materials gave Dôn a male royal biography absent from medieval texts. Comparative philology and Romantic Celtic studies then increasingly interpreted Dôn as a mother goddess and Beli as a reflex of Belenus. Lady Charlotte Guest’s English *Mabinogion* translations, beginning in 1838, made the family widely accessible. Later Victorian scholarship treated the medieval tales as windows into a reconstructed pagan mythology.

### Twentieth century: mythological system-building and correction

Comparativists such as Rhŷs, the Reeses, and other Celtic mythographers popularized an Irish–Welsh divine structure: Dôn/Danu as ancestral mother, Beli/Belenus as great father or solar god, and the Children of Dôn as a pantheon. Bromwich, Mac Cana, Bartrum, Carey, Koch, Maier, and more textually cautious scholarship emphasized manuscript variants, linguistic problems, and the difference between medieval characters and epigraphically attested ancient gods.

### Present

Dôn and Beli are honored in some Druid, Celtic reconstructionist, and eclectic pagan communities. Ár nDraíocht Féin material, for example, explicitly presents Beli as father of Dôn’s artisanal children ([ADF dedicant manual](https://ng.adf.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/dedicant-manual.pdf)). The Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids describes Beli/Belinus/Belenos as an ancestral and solar “all father,” while also drawing on *Lludd and Llefelys* ([OBOD, “The Honey Isle”](https://druidry.org/resources/the-honey-isle)). These are documented modern religious interpretations, not continuous survivals of a demonstrable ancient cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

The dominant modern image is a structured divine family:

- Dôn as earth mother or Welsh Danu;
- Beli as sky father, sun god, or dark ancestral father;
- their children as a functional pantheon of magic, agriculture, smithcraft, moon, and sovereignty.

This synthesis draws on real medieval patronymics but supplies the missing marriage, cosmology, domains, and cult.

Common modern claims requiring correction include:

- **“Dôn and Beli are explicitly married in the Mabinogion.”** They are not.
- **“Dôn is named daughter of Mathonwy.”** This is inferred, not stated.
- **“Beli is certainly Belenus.”** Disputed and linguistically problematic.
- **“Beli’s feast is Beltane.”** No medieval Welsh source says this.
- **“Beli was worshipped as a British sun god.”** No British inscription establishes it.
- **“Caer Dôn proves an ancient constellation cult.”** The name’s antiquity and ritual significance are unestablished.
- **“The Welsh and Irish corpora preserve the same goddess Danu/Dôn.”** This is a comparative hypothesis.
- **“Their worship survived continuously through Welsh folklore.”** No continuous ritual lineage is documented.

Literary retellings of the Fourth Branch—from Evangeline Walton to Alan Garner’s *The Owl Service* and later fantasy—have strengthened the impression of a coherent pre-Christian Welsh pantheon even when Dôn remains offstage. Modern Druid organizations have converted antiquarian reconstruction into living religious symbolism; that is historically important as reception but must be dated to the revival.

No significant extremist appropriation specific to Dôn or Beli Mawr is securely documented in the reviewed material. Broad Celtic nationalist or racialized uses of “Aryan” and Druidic imagery should not be imputed to these figures without specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Central disputes

1. **Was Dôn a goddess?**  
   Probably, in the limited sense that her children preserve divine or supernatural characteristics and the Fourth Branch makes her an ancestral female. But no surviving source applies a word meaning “goddess” to her, and no cult evidence exists.

2. **Is Dôn identical with Danu?**  
   The resemblance and parallel ancestral function support comparison. The weakness is that Irish *Danu* is itself reconstructed from oblique forms and later glosses. Direct identity is not demonstrated.

3. **Does Dôn mean “river” or “earth”?**  
   Both are scholarly etymological proposals. Neither is a medieval interpretation.

4. **Was Beli a god or king?**  
   The documents classify him as king and ancestor. “Ancestor deity” is an interpretation of his position at the head of pedigrees.

5. **Is Beli Belenus?**  
   Earlier scholars commonly said yes; later linguistic criticism notes that *Belenos* should yield Belyn. The question remains open, but identity cannot be treated as fact.

6. **Is Beli son of Manogan merely a scribal accident?**  
   The Latin transmission from Adminius/Cunobelinus to Bellinus/Minocannus explains the patronymic. Yet Beli Mawr’s broader Welsh tradition may have attracted this corrupted material rather than been created wholly by it.

7. **Are Heli, Belinus, and Beli one figure?**  
   No. Geoffrey’s narrative distinguishes Heli from Belinus. Later harmonizers blurred them.

8. **Do the Four Branches preserve pagan scripture?**  
   They preserve medieval Welsh literature containing mythic, legendary, social, and Christian-era elements. Recovering pre-Christian structures from them is possible only by comparative argument, not by stripping away an easily identifiable Christian layer.

### State of the evidence

The chief artifacts are manuscripts, not cult objects:

- Peniarth MS 4, White Book of Rhydderch, National Library of Wales;
- Jesus College MS 111, Red Book of Hergest, Bodleian Library;
- Peniarth MS 2, Book of Taliesin, National Library of Wales;
- Harley MS 3859, British Library;
- later Triad and genealogical manuscripts.

Modern foundational tools include Rachel Bromwich’s *Trioedd Ynys Prydein*, Bartrum’s *Early Welsh Genealogical Tracts* and *Welsh Classical Dictionary*, Patrick Sims-Williams and John Koch on Brittonic language and tradition, John Carey on Celtic mythological comparison, Bernhard Maier’s critical reference works, and the dictionaries of James MacKillop and Miranda Green. Their entries are starting points; the medieval witnesses must control the conclusions.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Attestation or development |
|---|---|
| First–third centuries CE | Belenus/Belinus is epigraphically and literarily attested in Aquileia, Noricum, and neighboring regions. No source equates him with Beli Mawr. |
| c. 800–830 | *Historia Brittonum* transmits *Bellinus filius Minocanni*, produced through corruption of classical Cunobelinus/Adminius material. |
| Ninth–tenth centuries | Earliest recoverable strata of Welsh royal genealogies extend dynasties toward Beli Mawr. |
| c. 1100 | Harley MS 3859 copies the Harleian genealogies. |
| c. 1100 | Approximate literary horizon often assigned to *Culhwch ac Olwen*; Amaethon and Gofannon are sons of Dôn. |
| Eleventh–twelfth century | Probable formation of the Four Branches; Dôn is parent of Gwydion, Gilfaethwy, and Aranrhod. |
| 1129 onward | Henry of Huntingdon transmits and later revises related Bellinus material. |
| c. 1136–1147 | Geoffrey of Monmouth composes and revises the *Historia regum Britanniae*, distinguishing Heli from Belinus son of Dunvallo. |
| Thirteenth century | The Book of Taliesin manuscript preserves poems including the seven sons of Beli and material concerning Dôn’s descendants. |
| c. 1350 | White Book of Rhydderch copies the earliest complete surviving Four Branches and *Lludd and Llefelys*. |
| c. 1382–1410 | Red Book of Hergest copies another major recension. |
| Fifteenth–eighteenth centuries | Pedigrees proliferate and are harmonized with Geoffrey, biblical history, and antiquarian schemes. |
| Late eighteenth–early nineteenth century | Iolo Morganwg constructs a male King Dôn tradition in neo-bardic material. |
| 1838–1849 | Charlotte Guest publishes her English *Mabinogion*, greatly expanding reception. |
| 1880s–early twentieth century | Rhŷs and comparativist scholarship systematize Dôn/Danu and Beli/Belenus theories. |
| 1928 | W. J. Gruffydd’s *Math vab Mathonwy* develops influential comparative interpretations. |
| 1961/1978 and later editions | Bromwich’s *Trioedd Ynys Prydein* establishes a critical foundation for separating triadic recensions and variants. |
| Late twentieth century | Linguistic and textual scholarship increasingly challenges simple Dôn–Danu and Beli–Belenus equations. |
| Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries | Druidic, reconstructionist, and eclectic pagan groups worship or reinterpret Dôn and Beli as a divine couple; this is a modern revival rather than demonstrable cult continuity. |
| 2026 | Dôn and Beli remain culturally active in scholarship, fiction, online polytheism, and Druid organizations, while their ancient divinity and mutual relationship remain unproved. |

## 11. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction is deliberately asymmetrical. Dôn is the almost silent parent of a conspicuously supernatural medieval Welsh family. Beli Mawr is the almost silent ancestor of legendary kings and politically useful dynasties. Neither has a preserved active divine biography or demonstrable ancient British cult. Their later union into an earth-mother/sky- or sun-father pair arose through the combination of medieval genealogies, comparative philology, Romantic Celtic studies, and modern pagan creativity.

The negative findings are therefore substantive:

- no ancient inscription for Dôn or Beli Mawr;
- no securely identified image;
- no temple, priesthood, festival, or offering;
- no narrative meeting between them;
- no medieval statement that they were married;
- no primary-source equation of Dôn with Danu;
- no primary-source equation of Beli with Belenus;
- no warrant for transferring Belenus’s Apollo association or continental cult to Beli;
- no warrant for transferring every action or attribute of Dôn’s children to Dôn herself.

What is secure is still significant: medieval Wales remembered supernatural and heroic lineages through named ancestral parents, used Beli to organize dynastic time, used Dôn to organize a family of magical and artisanal figures, and transmitted both through manuscripts that became raw material for centuries of increasingly elaborate reconstruction.
