# The Cailleach: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest securely datable evidence, **Caillech Bérri** is an aged female literary persona—possibly a nun, widow, legendary ancestress, or supernatural woman, but not explicitly called a goddess. In later Gaelic folklore, *cailleach* may designate an old woman, hag, witch, supernatural giantess, landscape-maker, weather-being, animal guardian, harvest figure, or local tutelary spirit. Her classification as a single pan-Celtic “winter goddess” is principally a modern scholarly, literary, and Pagan synthesis.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Old Irish poem conventionally called *Caillech Bérri* or *The Lament of the Old Woman of Beare*, probably composed around **c. 900 CE**, though scholarly datings vary. It survives only in later medieval manuscripts. The poem is the earliest evidence for the named figure; it does not call her a deity, give her control of winter, or describe her making mountains. [Katja Ritari’s textual study](https://journal.fi/scf/article/download/7419/5771) emphasizes both the poem’s uncertain interpretation and the danger of reading nineteenth-century folklore backward into it.

## Evidentiary key

- **Securely attested early material:** medieval evidence datable approximately to the ninth–twelfth centuries.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** later manuscript framing or a text that became authoritative in scholarship without necessarily representing an ancient canon.
- **Regional variant:** localized Irish, Scottish, or Manx tradition.
- **Learned systematization:** antiquarian, folkloristic, comparative, or literary organization of originally separate evidence.
- **Syncretistic identification:** an explicit or inferred equation with another supernatural figure.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** material later than the primary Old Irish poem but predating systematic folklore collection.
- **Folklore:** orally transmitted material recorded mainly in the eighteenth–twentieth centuries.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a proposed historical interpretation not directly stated by a primary source.
- **Modern invention or retelling:** a substantially new narrative, ritual system, genealogy, or characterization presented in modern literature or religion.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

“The Cailleach” is not demonstrably one continuous divine individual worshipped throughout Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Britain, and Gaul. The evidence instead consists of:

1. an early Irish poetic speaker called **Caillech Bérri**;
2. several medieval Irish women or hags described by the common noun *caillech*;
3. localized Gaelic supernatural “old women” attached to mountains, islands, storms, deer, wells, megaliths, and territorial ancestry;
4. harvest figures named for an old woman;
5. Scottish traditions reorganized as **Cailleach Bheur/Beira, Queen of Winter**;
6. modern literary, folkloristic, occult, and Pagan constructions that combine these materials.

The dossier therefore uses “Cailleach complex” when referring collectively to this family of traditions.

### Names and forms

- **Old Irish:** *caillech*, genitive *caillige*—“veiled woman,” “nun,” “old woman,” or “hag.” It is derived from *caille*, “veil,” itself an early borrowing ultimately from Latin *pallium*, “cloak.” This Christian-era loanword is powerful evidence against treating *Cailleach* as the unchanged proper name of a prehistoric goddess. The semantic range was already broad in Old and Middle Irish. [Ritari](https://journal.fi/scf/article/download/7419/5771) summarizes Máirín Ní Dhonnchadha’s lexical work; Alexander MacBain’s older [Gaelic etymological dictionary](https://deriv.nls.uk/dcn23/1064/5717/106457176.23.pdf) gives the same basic derivation.
- **Modern Irish:** *cailleach*, “old woman, hag,” with extended senses such as witch or wise woman in compounds.
- **Scottish Gaelic:** *cailleach*, “old woman, hag, nun,” also regionally the last standing corn or final sheaf. The [Dictionary of the Scots Language](https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/cailleach) documents borrowed Scots forms including *cailliach*, *caillach*, and *cal(l)ach*.
- **Caillech Bérri / Cailleach Bhéarra:** “Old Woman/Hag of Beare,” referring to the Beara Peninsula in southwest Ireland.
- **Sentainne Béirri:** “Old Woman/Ancestress of Beare,” the designation in the prose heading to one recension of the poem.
- **Buí:** The poem has *Is mé Caillech Bérri, Buí*. It has been translated either “I am the Caillech of Beare, Buí” or “the Caillech of Beare of Buí.” Ritari notes that **Buí is also the name of Dursey Island** at the end of the Beara Peninsula; it is consequently uncertain whether Buí is her personal name, a place qualification, or both.
- **Cailleach Bheur / Bheurr:** Scottish Gaelic form. *Bheur* has been interpreted through *beur*, “sharp, cutting, shrill,” fitting wintry weather, but it may also represent the transfer or reinterpretation of Irish *Bérri/Bhéarra*. A derivation from “horned” has also been proposed. None is certain.
- **Beira:** Anglicized literary name prominently used by Donald Alexander Mackenzie in 1917.
- **Cailleach nan Cruachan:** “Old Woman of Cruachan/the stacks,” associated especially with Ben Cruachan.
- **Cailleach Beinne Bric / Cailleach Beinn a’ Bhric:** localized “Old Woman of the Speckled Mountain.”
- **Cailleach Mhòr nam Fiadh:** “Great Old Woman of the Deer,” a Scottish animal-mistress designation.
- **Cailleach an Dùdain:** “Hag of the mill-dust,” appearing in a Scottish seasonal dance-drama tradition.
- **Cailleach Bhuana / A’ Chailleach Bhuain:** “Harvest Old Woman,” the final sheaf or a figure made from it.
- **Cailleach Bealltainn:** “Beltane old woman,” a term reported in connection with Scottish seasonal lots or mock victims. Its existence does not establish sacrifice to a goddess of that name.

### Etymological and comparative claims rejected or unproved

- **Kālī:** The proposed equation of Cailleach with Sanskrit *Kālī* is a modern sound-association, not accepted historical linguistics. *Caillech* has a well-documented development from *caille* and Latin *pallium*.
- **Callaeci/Gallaecia:** No demonstrated linguistic or cultic connection.
- **Gaulish goddess:** No Gaulish inscription names *Cailleach*. She is not found on the Pillar of the Boatmen, the Coligny calendar, or securely identified on the Gundestrup cauldron.
- **Welsh goddess:** Welsh literature contains old women, witches, giants, seasonal figures, and sovereignty motifs, but no named Cailleach. Neither the Four Branches, *Culhwch ac Olwen*, nor the *Book of Taliesin* supplies a secure occurrence.
- **Roman or Greek equation:** There is no ancient *interpretatio Romana* equating her with Ceres, Demeter, Diana, Hecate, or another classical goddess.

Accordingly, the brief’s phrase “Celtic (Irish, Welsh, Gaulish and British) pantheon” describes a modern comparative category, not the reach of the primary evidence. The securely documented complex is **Gaelic: Irish, Scottish, and to a lesser extent Manx**.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### *Caillech Bérri* / *Aithbe dam-sa bés mora*

**Tier: securely attested early material, with later manuscript transmission.**

The poem is usually dated linguistically to approximately the ninth or early tenth century, often “around 900.” Its opening is conventionally titled from its first words, *Aithbe dam-sa bés mora*, approximately “Ebb-tide to me, as to the sea.” The speaker laments physical decline, vanished lovers and kings, poverty, loneliness, and approaching death. The complete Old Irish text in Gerard Murphy’s edition is available through [CELT](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/G400034); CELT identifies its printed source as Murphy’s *Early Irish Lyrics, Eighth to Twelfth Century* (1956), pp. 74–82, in the accompanying [bibliographic record](https://celt.ucc.ie/document/G400034/G400034.bib).

Representative lines include:

> *Is mé Caillech Bérri, Buí*  
> “I am the Old Woman of Beare, [of] Buí.”

The exact construction and meaning of *Buí* remain disputed.

The poem juxtaposes the ebbing sea with aging, recalls richly attended former life, mentions kings, and ends within an explicitly Christian conceptual world: God, Christ, and “the Son of Mary” occur in several stanzas. These references are not merely in the modern translation. The speaker expects the body’s return when God claims his deposit.

The poem does **not** explicitly state that she:

- rules winter;
- creates mountains;
- carries a hammer;
- alternates annually with Brigid;
- becomes a maiden each spring;
- is mother of all gods;
- was worshipped in a temple;
- is identical with Ireland or sovereignty.

Those readings arise from later prose framing, comparative mythology, and folklore.

### Manuscript transmission

Ritari reports **five manuscript witnesses**, falling into two closely related but differently ordered textual traditions. The poem’s composition is therefore centuries earlier than its surviving codices. Editors have rejected various verses as interpolation, although Ritari argues for taking the medieval transmitted forms seriously rather than reconstructing too confidently.

One important manuscript is the **Great Book of Lecan**, Royal Irish Academy MS 23 P 2, produced c. 1397–1418. Its catalogue explicitly lists an account of “Seantond Beirri,” otherwise “Cailleach Berri”; images and manuscript description are available through [Irish Script on Screen](https://www.isos.dias.ie/RIA/RIA_MS_23_P_2.html).

The prose preface found in only one branch is not securely part of the original poem. It states that the woman passed through seven periods of youth, outlived seven husbands, and became ancestress of peoples. That framing is the chief medieval basis for cyclic rejuvenation, multiple royal husbands, and genealogical interpretations. It must not be silently inserted into the poem itself.

### *Aislinge Meic Con Glinne*

**Tier: later medieval literary attestation.**

This Middle Irish comic vision was probably composed in the late eleventh century. It lists Caillech Bérri among famous figures known at Armagh; it does not supply a developed divine biography. The principal version survives in the early fifteenth-century *Leabhar Breac* and another in later Trinity College Dublin MS H.3.18. The [Irish Sagas project](https://iso.ucc.ie/Aislinge-meic/Aislinge-meic-sources.html) identifies the manuscripts and critical editions; its [textual introduction](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/iriol/100) dates composition most probably to the last quarter of the eleventh century.

### Dindshenchas and Buí of Cnogba

**Tier: later canonical elaboration; disputed identification.**

In the metrical place-lore of Cnogba, modern Knowth, **Buí** is associated with the place and with Lug. T. F. O’Rahilly and Tomás Ó Cathasaigh connected this Buí with the Buí/Caillech Bérri of the lament. The identification supplies a possible named divine husband and a sovereignty-landscape dimension, but it depends on treating *Buí* in separate texts as one individual. It is not stated in the lament.

Ó Cathasaigh consequently argued that Caillech Bérri was the eponym or sovereign female of Cnogba and that her “promiscuity” represented serial union with kings. Ritari observes that the relevant verses can also mean simply that an old woman remembers courtly lovers. The evidence supports the identification as a serious scholarly reconstruction, not an established textual fact.

### Sovereignty-hag narratives

**Tier: medieval parallel, not a named Cailleach myth.**

In *Echtra mac nEchach Muigmedóin* (“Adventures of the Sons of Eochaid Mugmedón”), a hideous woman guarding a well demands intimacy from Eochaid’s sons. Niall alone fully embraces her; she becomes beautiful and, in the prose version, identifies herself as *in Flaithius*, “the Sovereignty.” The tale legitimates Niall and the Uí Néill.

She is a *caillech* in the generic sense, but she is not called Caillech Bérri. Moreover, the accompanying poem calls her a female seer or poet and a specter rather than Sovereignty. Gregory Toner’s study warns that the sovereignty interpretation may be later or less integral than often claimed. [The published analysis](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/publications/Chadwick/HMC%20Vol%2029%202018%20Toner.pdf) shows that the prose and verse versions cannot simply be conflated.

Comparable loathly-lady transformations surround Lugaid Loígde and other kings. They establish a medieval Irish narrative type, not direct proof that the Old Woman of Beare herself regularly changed into a maiden.

### Texts that do not contain a securely identifiable Cailleach

The following are essential for Celtic religion generally but yield no secure named occurrence:

- *Lebor Gabála Érenn*;
- *Cath Maige Tuired*;
- *Táin Bó Cúailnge*;
- *Tochmarc Étaíne*;
- *Acallam na Senórach*, apart from verses or motifs shared with wider place-lore;
- the Four Branches of the *Mabinogi*;
- *Culhwch ac Olwen*;
- the *Book of Taliesin*;
- Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* VI;
- Lucan, *Pharsalia* I and its scholia;
- the Gaulish inscriptional corpus;
- the Romano-British *RIB* corpus.

None may responsibly be used to manufacture an ancient “Cailleach cult.”

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains by evidentiary period

**Around 900:** old age, memory, bodily decline, former sexuality and status, the passing of kings, the sea’s ebb, Christian mortality.

**Later medieval framing:** exceptional longevity, successive husbands, repeated youth, ancestry, and perhaps sovereignty.

**Irish folklore:** landscape creation, immense age, territorial memory, rocks and cairns, wells, floods, famine, healing, death, midwifery, keening, kinship, and the supernatural “wise woman.”

**Scottish folklore:** mountains, storms, snow, the dangerous end of winter, deer and other wild animals, herding or milking deer, local topography, harvest fortune, and hostile old women.

**Twentieth-century systematization onward:** a generalized “Goddess of Winter,” Earth Mother, Dark Goddess, death-and-rebirth initiatrix, and one pole of a Cailleach/Brigid seasonal duality.

### Physical forms

No ancient or medieval image is securely identified as the Cailleach. Literary and oral descriptions vary:

- an ordinary aged woman;
- withered, poor, and physically diminished;
- an immense giantess striding between mountains;
- a blue, dark, gray, or weather-beaten hag;
- one-eyed or sharp-sighted in some localized stories;
- guardian or milker of deer;
- a beautiful young woman after transformation;
- stone or mountain features anthropomorphized as her body.

“Blue skin, one eye, red teeth, matted hair, and a magic hammer” is now a common composite portrait, but no single early source supplies that iconographic set.

### Landscape creation

**Tier: chiefly nineteenth–twentieth-century folklore.**

Cailleach figures drop stones from aprons, form cairns, carry boulders, create islands, scoop valleys, or leap between hills. These are widespread giant-legend motifs, often secondarily attached to local names. A named rock, mountain, tomb, or chair is evidence of recorded place-lore, not proof that its prehistoric builders worshipped her.

Alasdair Whyte’s peer-reviewed survey, [“The Cailleach in Place-Names and Place-Lore”](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/226524/), documents the geographical spread and variability of the Scottish material. It also supports treating *cailleach* as a productive category in Gaelic naming rather than automatically as one divine proper name.

### Weather and winter

The Scottish Cailleach’s relationship to bad weather is genuine folklore, but her precise six-month reign, her annual enthronement at Samhain, and her ritual defeat by Brigid at Imbolc or Beltane are not securely medieval doctrines.

Donald Mackenzie’s 1917 *Wonder Tales from Scottish Myth and Legend* gives the decisive literary synthesis. His Beira is “mother of all the gods and goddesses in Scotland,” Queen of Winter, cyclically young in summer and old in winter, and enemy of Bride. The [complete text](https://www.globalgreyebooks.com/online-ebooks/donald-alexander-mackenzie_wonder-tales-from-scottish-myth-and-legend_complete-text.html) shows both the detailed cosmology and Mackenzie’s own explanatory voice. This is a modern retelling using folklore, not a transcript of an ancient Scottish scripture.

### Deer and wild animals

**Tier: regional Scottish folklore.**

Several local Cailleacha own, herd, protect, or milk deer. Hunters may require their leave or must spare a favored hind. Such material supports a regional “mistress of animals” function, but the reconstruction of an ancient Caledonian deer-goddess cult or priesthood is speculative.

### Harvest last sheaf

**Tier: documented agrarian custom; identification with the winter goddess unproved.**

*Cailleach* could mean the last sheaf, the last patch of uncut grain, or a straw figure made from it. It might be passed to the farmer who finished last, hung in a house, kept over winter, or fed to workhorses before spring ploughing. Other names included “maiden” and *clyack*, showing that the figure was not uniformly an old woman.

The [Carmichael Watson Project](https://carmichaelwatson.blogspot.com/2011/01/cailleach-or-corn-dolly.html) summarizes Alexander Carmichael’s Scottish collections and notes that the practice survived in memory into the twentieth century. A surviving modern Tiree reconstruction, *A’ Chailleach Bhuain*, is documented by [An Iodhlann](https://www.aniodhlann.org.uk/object/2003-71-1/).

The custom is firm evidence for the word’s ritualized agricultural use. It is not evidence that farmers were knowingly sacrificing to the same personal goddess who speaks in the Old Irish poem.

### Objects, colors, plants, days, and celestial bodies

- **Stones, cairns, mountains, and aprons:** folklore.
- **Hammer or staff producing frost:** best known through modern Scottish literary synthesis.
- **Deer and occasionally goats or cattle:** regional folklore.
- **Last sheaf/straw effigy:** documented harvest custom.
- **Blue, gray, white, and black:** variable modern and folklore descriptors, not canonical sacred colors.
- **Samhain, Imbolc, Là Fhèill Brìghde, Beltane:** modern seasonal systematization built around scattered sayings and customs.
- **Moon:** lunar-goddess theories advanced by D. Fitzgerald and Robert Briffault were criticized even by A. H. Krappe; no early source associates her with a celestial body.
- **Number seven:** appears in the late prose account of seven periods of youth and seven husbands, not as a generally attested cult number.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogies

There is no single early genealogy.

- **The poem:** gives no parents, divine siblings, or named divine children.
- **Late prose preface:** makes her a serially rejuvenated ancestress who outlives seven husbands and leaves numerous descendants.
- **Buí identification:** if Caillech Bérri is the Buí of the Cnogba dindshenchas, she is associated with **Lug**. This is disputed.
- **Scottish local tradition:** individual Cailleacha may have a Bodach, sons, daughters, or herds; these relationships are regional.
- **Mackenzie, 1917:** Beira is mother of Angus and “all the gods and goddesses in Scotland.” This is learned literary construction, not a medieval genealogy.

### Every securely or specifically documented god–god interaction

Because the evidence is thin, absence must be stated plainly.

- **Lug — possible marriage/territorial association.** Buí is Lug’s wife in Cnogba place-lore; identifying that Buí with Caillech Bérri was argued by O’Rahilly and Ó Cathasaigh. **Source:** medieval dindshenchas, interpreted in twentieth-century scholarship. **Tier:** disputed learned identification.
- **Bride/Brigid — captivity and seasonal rivalry.** Beira imprisons Bride; Bride’s release marks returning spring. **Source:** Donald A. Mackenzie, *Wonder Tales from Scottish Myth and Legend* (1917). **Tier:** modern literary synthesis drawing on Scottish seasonal folklore.
- **Angus — mother and opponent.** In Mackenzie, Angus is Beira’s son, seeks Bride, and is opposed by Beira’s storms. **Source:** Mackenzie, 1917. **Tier:** modern retelling.
- **The King of the Green Isle — counsel concerning Bride.** He explains Bride’s imprisonment and advises Angus. **Source:** Mackenzie, 1917. **Tier:** modern retelling.
- **Brigid as the Cailleach’s younger self — transformation/equation.** Common in modern Pagan presentations, but no medieval narrative establishes that Brigid and Cailleach are annually alternating forms of one being. **Tier:** modern syncretistic identification.
- **Manannán mac Lir — alleged marriage.** Frequently repeated online, but no secure medieval source establishing the named Caillech Bérri as Manannán’s wife has been identified. **Tier:** unestablished modern claim.

No interaction between a named Cailleach and the Dagda, Morrígan, Nuadu, Goibniu, Ériu, Macha, Badb, Arawn, Cernunnos, Epona, or a Romano-Gallic deity is securely recorded.

### Human interactions relevant to sovereignty

- **Former kings/lovers:** the poem remembers close relations with men of wealth and kings, but leaves them unnamed.
- **Niall Noígiallach and the well-hag:** a generic *caillech* transforms after embracing Niall and, in prose, identifies herself as Sovereignty. This is a parallel, not a demonstrated meeting with Caillech Bérri.
- **Lugaid Loígde and related royal heroes:** comparable old-woman/maiden sovereignty tests; again, typological parallels rather than appearances of the named being.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Lament of the Old Woman of Beare

**Tier: securely attested early literary material.**

The speaker compares herself to an ebbing tide. Once she wore fine clothing, rode in chariots, enjoyed lovers, feasts, and royal company; now she is poor and physically diminished. Young women flourish while she decays. Her arms are thin, beauty gone, and former companions dead. Nature renews itself, but the human body moves toward death. She accepts that God will reclaim what was entrusted to her.

Interpretations divide into four principal groups:

1. **Literal elegy of aging:** Ritari argues that nothing in the poem requires supernatural biography.
2. **Sovereignty goddess displaced by political change:** Mac Cana, Ó Cathasaigh, Simms, and Ó Crualaoich see former royal unions as the land’s unions with kings.
3. **Christianization of a pagan immortal:** the “veil” becomes a nun’s veil and linear Christian death replaces cyclic renewal.
4. **Flood and immortality tradition:** John Carey reads the ebbing landscape against Irish inundation stories and lost otherworldly realms.

All illuminate features of the poem; none is explicitly announced by its anonymous author.

### Seven youths and seven husbands

**Tier: later manuscript accretion.**

The prose introduction says she experienced seven successive periods of youth and that each husband died of old age while she remained alive. She became progenitor of peoples. This supplies a mythic explanation of her age and serial kingship associations, but it appears in only one manuscript tradition and cannot automatically be dated to the poem’s composition.

### The hag who becomes a maiden

**Tier: medieval narrative type; association with the Cailleach disputed.**

A hideous woman demands a kiss or sexual union from a candidate king, then reveals her beauty and sovereignty. The motif is securely medieval in the Niall and Lugaid cycles. The Old Woman of Beare’s own poem, however, never transforms her. Indeed, its emotional force depends on her inability to recover youth.

Calling this “the Cailleach’s annual transformation” therefore merges three things:

- the generic Irish sovereignty hag;
- the prose account of repeated youth;
- Mackenzie’s annually rejuvenated Beira.

The synthesis is modern, even though each component has earlier roots.

### Landscape-making hag

**Tier: regional folklore.**

She carries stones in an apron; the apron-string breaks, forming hills, cairns, or islands. She strides between mountains, uses peaks as stepping stones, or alters coasts and lochs. Individual stories attach her to Loughcrew, Slieve Gullion, Beara, Ben Cruachan, Glen Lyon, Skye, Mull, and numerous other places.

The motif is not confined to the Cailleach: giants, saints, the Devil, and other supernatural builders receive analogous stories. Consequently, prehistoric cairns attributed to her are not “temples of the Cailleach” without independent evidence.

### Beira, Angus, and Bride

**Tier: modern literary synthesis, 1917.**

In Mackenzie’s developed cycle, Beira rules winter and imprisons Bride so that her own reign will continue. Angus, Beira’s son and a golden youthful god, dreams of Bride and seeks her. He borrows three days from August to cross the sea in February. Bride’s tears create violets; Angus finds and rescues her; Beira raises tempests but loses power as spring returns.

Mackenzie also makes Beira annually pass from aged winter hag to young summer woman and back again. The story now shapes popular accounts of the Cailleach more than the medieval poem does, but it must be cited to **1917**, not to pagan antiquity.

### Harvest Cailleach

**Tier: folklore and customary practice.**

The final patch of standing grain concentrates the anxiety of being last to harvest. Its cutter “gets the Cailleach,” constructs a straw figure, or sends it to a neighbor who has not finished. Elsewhere it is the “maiden,” showing a flexible old/young opposition. Keeping the sheaf over winter and feeding it to horses carries fertility forward into the next agricultural cycle.

Sir James Frazer and later goddess theorists interpreted such customs as survivals of a prehistoric corn spirit. The practice is documented; its descent from Neolithic religion is not.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrated ancient cult

No inscription, altar, votive dedication, priestly title, temple account, theophoric personal name, or ancient image securely names the Cailleach. Specifically:

- no Gaulish inscription in *RIG*;
- no Romano-British dedication in *RIB*;
- no identification on the Gundestrup cauldron;
- no reference on the Pillar of the Boatmen;
- no entry in the Coligny calendar;
- no classical testimony from Caesar or Lucan.

Calling her “one of the oldest Celtic goddesses” is therefore a hypothesis about the ancestry of folklore, not an epigraphically established fact.

### Glen Cailleach and Tigh nam Bodach

At Glen Lyon, Perthshire, stones representing an old woman, old man, and daughter are housed in a small structure and traditionally brought outside for summer and returned under cover for winter. This is an important surviving localized custom. Its antiquity is not securely fixed, and the stone shelter is not demonstrably an Iron Age temple. It is best classified as a historic regional folk observance subsequently adopted into modern Pagan interpretation.

### Harvest practice

The last-sheaf customs are the strongest material ritual record connected to the word *cailleach*. Their practitioners need not have understood the sheaf as a goddess, and the terminology alternated between hag and maiden.

### Offerings and animal guardianship

Stories about leaving food, respecting a hut or stone, obtaining permission to hunt, or sparing a white hind belong to regional folklore. They indicate customary negotiation with local supernatural ownership, not a unified institutional cult.

### Alleged human sacrifice

Late eighteenth-century Scottish accounts describe a Beltane cake divided into lots, with the recipient of a blackened piece treated as a mock victim and sometimes called *cailleach Bealltainn*. Antiquarian witnesses conjectured that this preserved actual human sacrifice.

There is no archaeological or documentary evidence that humans were historically sacrificed to a deity called Cailleach. The inference proceeds from:

1. a mock-victim custom;
2. antiquarian assumptions about “Druidic” paganism;
3. later survivalist theory.

The custom is attested; the sacrifice is conjectural. No temple prostitution, organized cult violence, or Cailleach-specific priesthood is documented.

### Christianization and suppression

The early poem is itself Christian in surviving form. It may Christianize an older legendary figure, but this is interpretation. No edict suppressing a “cult of the Cailleach,” no destruction of her temple, and no forced conversion directed specifically against her are recorded.

Broader Gaelic supernatural traditions were reclassified as fairy belief, superstition, witchcraft, local legend, or harmless seasonal custom under Christianity. That cultural process is real, but claims that Roman missionaries deliberately eradicated a named Cailleach religion are modern inventions.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### c. 800–950: Old Irish literary formation

A poet adopts the voice of Caillech Bérri. The audience may already have recognized her extreme age or territorial associations, but the poem is the earliest surviving evidence. Its worldview is Christian, while its memories of kings and its ambiguous title permit older legendary readings.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: literary recognition and related motifs

*Aislinge Meic Con Glinne* treats her as a famous figure. Dindshenchas traditions concerning Buí, Cnogba, and Lug offer possible connections. Sovereignty-hag narratives flourish, but do not name Caillech Bérri.

### Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: manuscript preservation and framing

Late medieval scribes preserve the poem, reorder stanzas, and in one tradition add a prose biography of seven youths, seven husbands, and descent groups. These manuscripts are the physical evidence through which the earlier composition survives.

### Early-modern period: localized Gaelic hags

Cailleacha appear in place-lore and story as old women, witches, ancestors, giants, and owners of animals. The surviving record is uneven because much remained oral and was written down only later.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: collection and antiquarian comparison

Scottish and Irish collectors record weather sayings, harvest customs, mountain hags, deer guardians, and landscape legends. Antiquarians increasingly interpret them as survivals of pagan religion. John Gregorson Campbell’s *Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland*, published in 1900 from nineteenth-century collecting, is a major Scottish repository; a digitized copy is available through [Digital Pitt](https://digital.library.pitt.edu/islandora/object/pitt%3A31735054856707).

### 1910s–1930s: Celtic Revival and comparative “goddess” construction

Lady Gregory translated or adapted the lament, while the broader Literary Revival elevated Gaelic folklore as national literature. This revival should not be treated as simple preservation: translation, selection, archaism, and nationalist symbolism created new meanings.

Mackenzie’s 1917 *Wonder Tales* crystallized Beira as Queen of Winter, mother of the gods, mother of Angus, and captor of Bride. Eleanor Hull’s 1927 article assembled Irish traditions around a divine Hag of Beare. A. H. Krappe’s 1936 [French study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ecelt_0373-1928_1936_num_1_2_1123) compared her with Indo-European myth and rejected some particularly loose lunar arguments, while still working within a comparative mythological framework.

### Mid-to-late twentieth century: sovereignty and mother-goddess models

Mac Cana emphasized a pagan land or mother goddess behind the poem. Ó Cathasaigh connected Buí, Cnogba, Lug, and sovereignty. Ó Crualaoich developed the most ambitious synthesis of literary hag, healer, midwife, keener, landscape woman, and sovereignty figure. These studies recovered structural relationships overlooked by purely literary readings, but they necessarily bridge large chronological gaps.

### Twenty-first century: source-critical reassessment and religious revival

Ritari cautions that the poem itself may be an extraordinarily human meditation on age. Ronald Hutton argues that the primeval pan-Gaelic Great Goddess is not demonstrated, even though the localized Cailleach is a major folklore figure. A review of Hutton’s *Queens of the Wild* summarizes the tension between nineteenth-century prominence and claimed prehistoric antiquity in [The Medieval Review](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/35677).

At the same time, Celtic Reconstructionist, Druid, Wiccan, witchcraft, Goddess-spirituality, and eclectic Pagan practitioners now worship or honor the Cailleach as a real deity. This is a documented living religion, though not proof of continuous institutional worship from antiquity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literary Revival

The old woman as embodiment of land, dispossession, and renewal resonated with Irish nationalist personifications such as Cathleen Ní Houlihan and the *spéirbhean* of aisling poetry. This does not make every old-woman national allegory the Cailleach. Yeats and Lady Gregory participated in a movement that transformed orally and textually inherited material into deliberately national literature; an institutional account of Gregory’s collecting is provided by [Ask About Ireland](https://askaboutireland.ie/reading-room/history-heritage/folklore-of-ireland/Folklore-of-ireland/anglo-irish-literary-rena/).

### Mackenzie and the modern winter-goddess image

Mackenzie’s Beira cycle is the single most consequential modern reinvention. His book gave readers:

- a personal name, Beira;
- a Scottish divine monarchy;
- motherhood of the gods;
- seasonal rejuvenation;
- enmity with Bride;
- motherhood of Angus;
- a coherent combat between winter and spring.

These elements now circulate detached from their 1917 source and are frequently advertised as ancient Celtic doctrine.

### Occult and Pagan literature

- Sorita d’Este and David Rankine, *Visions of the Cailleach* (2009), combines folklore, historical discussion, and contemporary spiritual practice; the [publisher’s bibliographic listing](https://www.theportobellobookshop.com/9781905297245) dates it to 28 February 2009.
- Rachel Patterson, *Pagan Portals: The Cailleach* (2016), explicitly combines legends with the author’s personal magical experiences. The [publisher’s description](https://www.simonandschuster.net/books/Pagan-Portals-The-Cailleach/Rachel-Patterson/9781785353222) is valuable evidence for modern devotional construction.
- Contemporary observances may honor her at Samhain, ask her help through winter, offer whisky, bread, stones, or water, or release her authority at Imbolc or Beltane. These are legitimate modern religious practices but cannot be backdated without evidence.
- The triple sequence maiden–mother–crone is heavily influenced by twentieth-century Goddess religion and modern witchcraft. It is not a documented medieval Cailleach theology.
- Equations with Hecate, Kali, Baba Yaga, the Morrígan, Cerridwen, Hel, or “the Dark Mother” are modern comparativism or personal theology.

### Popular culture

Modern appearances include:

- *Doctor Who: The Stones of Blood* (1978), where a supposed Cailleach is revealed as an alien;
- the BBC series *Merlin*, where “the Cailleach” guards a spirit-world gateway;
- fantasy fiction, games, music, and role-playing settings using her as an ice queen, death hag, or primordial witch;
- Megan Barnard’s *The Winter Goddess* (2025);
- the film *Hokum* (2026), which uses the Cailleach as a horror figure.

These works reinforce the modern image of one named supernatural individual far more consistently than the premodern sources do.

### Political appropriation

The Cailleach has been used as:

- a personification of colonized or ancient land;
- an emblem of Gaelic cultural survival;
- a feminist reclamation of aging women;
- an ecological symbol of wild terrain and winter;
- occasionally, a “native European” or pre-Christian identity marker.

No substantial, organized extremist cult centered on her is documented in the sources examined. Claims about a pure prehistoric matriarchal nation displaced by outsiders belong to modern ideological mythmaking unless supported independently.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is she a goddess?

**Pro-goddess reconstruction:** Hull, Mac Cana, Ó Cathasaigh, Ó Crualaoich, and others see enough continuity among the aged poetic woman, royal consort, land, ancestry, landscape, and folklore giantess to posit a degraded or Christianized goddess.

**Source-critical objection:** the earliest poem never calls her divine; its prose supernatural biography is later; localized folklore uses a common noun; and there is no cultic inscription or image. Hutton therefore treats the primeval Great Goddess as unproved.

A cautious conclusion is that some Cailleach traditions may preserve pre-Christian supernatural conceptions, but the precise nature, unity, and cultic status of any precursor cannot be reconstructed securely.

### Sovereignty

Ó Cathasaigh reads her former royal partners politically. Simms linked the poem to the decline of the Corcu Duibne after conflict with the Eóganachta around 793–803. Ó Crualaoich treats sovereignty as one layer of a broader otherworld-female tradition.

Ritari counters that the poem’s “kings” can be literal memories of courtly life and that it contains no transformation. Toner shows that even the Niall well-hag’s status as personified Sovereignty differs between prose and verse.

### Christian lament or pagan elegy?

The surviving poem explicitly invokes Christ. Scholars differ over whether this is:

- the genuine devotional horizon of its composition;
- monastic appropriation of a pagan figure;
- a deliberate tension between two eternities;
- or a layered work whose old-age lament matters more than religious allegory.

Authorship is unknown. A female author has been proposed because of the poem’s embodied perspective, but male ecclesiastical authorship or a male poet adopting a female voice remains possible.

### One being or many?

Ó Crualaoich’s synthesis treats literary, folkloric, healing, keening, territorial, and landscape manifestations as an adaptable tradition. Hutton stresses the risk of constructing one ancient goddess from unrelated figures all called “old woman.”

The linguistic fact that *cailleach* is a common noun is decisive: identical terminology does not by itself establish identical personality.

### Antiquity of place-lore

A prehistoric tomb called “the Hag’s” may have acquired that story long after construction. Folklore can preserve old structures of thought, but neither a name nor a giant-building legend dates itself. Nineteenth-century collection is the first secure date for much material.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- she is linguistically identical with Kali;
- she is securely depicted on the Gundestrup cauldron;
- she was a pan-Celtic goddess known in Gaul and Wales;
- she led an organized prehistoric matriarchal priesthood;
- every “hag” place-name marks a former temple;
- she and Brigid formed an ancient canonical two-season theology;
- she was historically offered human sacrifices;
- Beira’s complete genealogy is ancient;
- Christian missionaries are documented destroying her specific cult;
- the last sheaf proves continuous Neolithic worship.

### State of the evidence

The major medieval witnesses are held in Irish manuscript collections, including the Royal Irish Academy and Trinity College Dublin. The [Irish Script on Screen record for the Yellow Book of Lecan](https://www.isos.dias.ie/TCD/TCD_MS_1318.html) illustrates the physical and scribal context of related medieval literature. Important oral materials are dispersed among Scottish collections, the Irish National Folklore Collection, printed folklore journals, and local archives.

The central modern critical works include:

- Gerard Murphy, ed., *Early Irish Lyrics* (1956);
- Donncha Ó hAodha, “The Lament of the Old Woman of Beare” (1989);
- Máirín Ní Dhonnchadha, “Caillech and Other Terms for Veiled Women” (1994–95);
- Katja Ritari, “Images of Ageing in the Early Irish Poem *Caillech Bérri*” (2006);
- Tomás Ó Cathasaigh, “The Eponym Cnogba” (1989);
- Gearóid Ó Crualaoich, “Continuity and Adaptation in the Legends of Cailleach Bhéarra” (1988), “Non-Sovereignty Queen Aspects…” (1994–95), and *The Book of the Cailleach* (2003);
- Alasdair Whyte, “The Cailleach in Place-Names and Place-Lore” (2020);
- Ronald Hutton, *Queens of the Wild* (2022).

## 11. Sources and documentary conclusions

The most defensible reconstruction is not a biography of one ancient goddess. It is the history of a title and a changing family of figures.

At the center stands a profound Old Irish lament whose aged speaker may carry legendary associations but is not textually declared divine. Around it accumulated manuscript biography, dynastic interpretation, sovereignty comparison, localized landscape and weather traditions, harvest customs, and animal-guardian stories. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century collectors then placed these scattered traditions into comparative frameworks; Mackenzie supplied the influential Scottish myth of Beira, Angus, and Bride; scholars constructed competing sovereignty, mother-goddess, Christianization, and folklore-continuity models; modern Pagans turned the resulting figure into a living object of devotion.

Three conclusions are secure:

1. **The Old Woman of Beare is medieval and real as a documented literary figure.**
2. **Cailleach traditions are widespread and important in Gaelic folklore, especially in Ireland and Scotland.**
3. **The unified pan-Celtic winter goddess familiar today is a modern synthesis, not an identity stated by the earliest sources.**

That conclusion does not make the later traditions spurious. Their changes—old woman to ancestress, sovereignty figure, mountain giant, harvest sheaf, Queen of Winter, national symbol, and modern goddess—are the documented history of the Cailleach.
