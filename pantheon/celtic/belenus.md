# Belenus: Research Dossier

**Class:** God; more precisely, an indigenous Continental Celtic deity absorbed into Roman provincial religion and repeatedly identified with Apollo. No ancient Irish or Welsh text securely names him.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **[Secure early evidence]** Late Republican Aquileia, probably second–first century BCE. A limestone altar reads *A(ulus) Barbius A(uli) l(ibertus) Parnax / B(elino) v(otum) s(olvit) l(ibens) m(erito)*—“Aulus Barbius Parnax, freedman of Aulus, willingly and deservedly discharged his vow to B(elinus)” (CIL I² 3411; InscrAqu I.3). Because the divine name is abbreviated to **B**, its expansion is contextual rather than self-proving. A separate truncated-pyramidal cippus bearing Belenus’s name has been dated by Claudio Zaccaria to the second century BCE, but the exact dating remains debated. The safest formulation is therefore: **Belenus is securely present in the late-Republican cult of Aquileia, with a possible second-century-BCE monument.** The evidence is inscriptional, not narrative.

**Central finding:** **[Secure ancient evidence]** The documented Belenus is above all the god of Aquileia, northeastern Italy, the Carnian–eastern Alpine zone, and parts of Noricum and southern Gaul. He received votive altars, temples, imperial dedications, healing-water associations, oracular consultation, and—in 238 CE—a civic-defense legend. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Calling him a pan-Celtic “sun god,” an Irish god, the deity of Beltane, the consort of Belisama, or the Welsh Beli Mawr goes beyond what the ancient evidence securely establishes.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[Secure ancient evidence]** Ancient dedications treat Belenus as a male *deus*. At Aquileia he could be invoked independently as *Belenus/Belinus*, or under the compound divine name *Apollo Belenus*. Herodian calls him the city’s *epichōrios theos*, its indigenous or local god, and says the Aquileians identified him with Apollo. Tertullian, writing around 197 CE, lists Belenus as the characteristic god of the Noricans: *ut Noricis Belenus*.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** His best-supported functions are civic protection, healing or health-giving power, sacred water, and divination. “Solar god” is possible but not a securely stated indigenous theology: it rests largely on the Apollo equation, disputed etymology, and uncertain or generic radiate imagery.

### Documented forms

- **[Secure ancient evidence]** **Belenus**, Latin nominative; **Beleno**, dative. This is the familiar Roman spelling.
- **[Secure ancient evidence]** **Belinus**, with dative **Belino**, is especially important in early northeastern Italian material and may be the earlier local form.
- **[Secure ancient evidence]** **Belanus**, attested regionally in the western Alpine zone.
- **[Secure ancient evidence]** **Bellinus**, a geminated spelling in southern Gaul.
- **[Secure ancient evidence]** **Βέλεν / Belen**, the form transmitted in Herodian’s Greek manuscripts.
- **[Secure ancient evidence]** **ΒΕΛΕΝΙ**, a Greek-letter dative on an Aquileian monument, followed by **ΧΑΙΡΕ**, “hail” or “farewell.”
- **[Probable linguistic reconstruction]** Gaulish **Belenos/Belinos**, conventionally reconstructed behind the Latin forms. No lengthy indigenous-language text explains the name.

### Titles and epigraphic combinations

- **[Secure syncretistic identification]** *Apollo Belenus* or *Deus Apollo Belenus*: explicit Roman-period fusion or identification with Apollo.
- **[Secure Roman-period cult title]** *Belenus Augustus* and *Apollo Belenus Augustus*: “august” Belenus, placing the cult within imperial religious vocabulary.
- **[Secure Roman-period cult title]** *Belenus defensor* or a restored equivalent: “Belenus the defender,” probably reflecting his protective civic function; association with the siege of 238 is plausible but not demonstrable for every such inscription.
- **[Secure but abbreviated evidence]** *Fons B(eleni)* or *Fontes B(eleni)*: the spring or springs of Belenus. The expansion of **B** is accepted from the Aquileian context but remains epigraphically abbreviated.
- **[Secure ritual formula]** *sacrum*, “consecrated”; *votum solvit libens merito*, “discharged the vow willingly and deservedly.” These are dedication formulas, not distinctive theological epithets.

### Etymology

- **[Traditional scholarly reconstruction; disputed]** The name has long been connected with an Indo-European root conventionally represented as *bhelH-*—“white, bright, shining”—producing “the Bright One” or “Shining One.” This interpretation helped create the standard description “sun god.”
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed]** Xavier Delamarre instead relates Gaulish *belo-/bello-* to “strong, powerful,” analyzing *Belenos* approximately as “lord/master of power.”
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed]** Patrizia de Bernardo Stempel has proposed a connection with a root signifying a spring or source, attractive in light of the *Fons Beleni* dedications.
- **[Rejected or unestablished]** Derivations from Semitic Baal/Bel, numerological claims that the letters of *Belenus* encode the 365-day solar year, and confident translations such as “Celtic Apollo-Sun” are antiquarian constructions, not established Celtic linguistics.
- **[Contested]** Pliny’s plant-name *belenuntia* for henbane has encouraged a Belenus–henbane theory. The lexical history is complicated, the plant-name does not itself document ritual use, and archaeological evidence for henbane elsewhere in the Roman northwest does not connect it specifically to Belenus.

### Apollo and interpretatio Romana

**[Secure syncretistic identification]** The Apollo equation is not merely a modern comparison. Several inscriptions say *Apollini Beleno*, and Herodian explicitly says Aquileians identified the local god with Apollo. Caesar’s *Bellum Gallicum* 6.17 says that the Gauls’ “Apollo” drives away disease, but Caesar supplies no indigenous name. Consequently, using Caesar as direct testimony about Belenus is **[Modern scholarly inference]**, not an attestation.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Apollo supplied an intelligible Roman religious vocabulary—healing, prophecy, radiance, defense—but it cannot be assumed that every Apollo trait belonged to pre-Roman Belenus.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The epigraphic beginning

**[Secure early evidence]** CIL I² 3411 from Aquileia’s Beligna area is a limestone votive altar erected by Aulus Barbius Parnax. Its Republican lettering and context make it one of the oldest Belenus-related objects. The abbreviated *B(elino)* prevents treating it in isolation as an unequivocal full-name attestation.

**[Probable early evidence]** A cippus catalogued by Brusin has been assigned to the second century BCE. Its form has parallels at Lanuvium, Praeneste, and Pisaurum. The date is typological, not supplied by an inscribed year.

**[Secure late-Republican evidence]** At Iulium Carnicum, an inscription records refurbishment of a temple:

> *aedem Belini / [su]a pecunia refecere et / [clu]pea inaurata in fastigio V / et signa duo dedere …*

“They restored the temple of Belinus at their own expense and gave five gilded shields on the pediment and two statues …” (CIL V 1829).

This establishes an organized sanctuary, elite funding, architectural ornament, and images, but does not preserve a native myth.

### Inscriptional corpus

**[Secure ancient evidence]** Modern counts vary with restorations, doubtful readings, duplicates, and what constitutes a Belenus form. Approximately fifty inscriptions are commonly cited; newer studies describe roughly sixty or more from Aquileia alone when fragments and contested examples are included. The corpus includes:

- **Aquileia/Beligna:** the largest concentration; Republican through Tetrarchic dedications, temple evidence, *Apollo Belenus*, *Belenus Augustus*, springs, civic and imperial patrons, freedmen, magistrates, and professional associations.
- **Iulium Carnicum/Zuglio:** temple restoration and later dedications.
- **Altinum, Iulia Concordia, Venice, Rimini, Rome and Tibur:** votive or literary-epigraphic evidence, sometimes transported from original contexts.
- **Bardonecchia/Beaulard:** Lucius Erax Bardus’s dedication to *Deus Apollo Belenus*, reportedly made *ex responso* and involving a temple with ornaments.
- **Noricum:** comparatively few inscriptions despite Tertullian’s characterization; examples include Celeia and the Magdalensberg/Carnian orbit.
- **Southern Gaul:** Narbonne, the Huveaune valley, Clermont-Ferrand, Nîmes, Saint-Chamas, Marseille, and Glanum, with varying degrees of certainty.
- **Sainte-Sabine, Burgundy:** sanctuary and spring material conventionally assigned to Apollo Belenus, including votive figurines.
- **Britain:** **[Negative finding]** RIB contains many inscriptions to Apollo and indigenous Apollo-equivalents, but no securely identified Romano-British dedication to *Belenus* comparable to Aquileia’s corpus. Claims of a widespread British epigraphic cult are not supported by RIB.

### Literary witnesses

#### Tertullian, *Apologeticum* 24.8, ca. 197 CE

**[Secure ancient literary evidence]** Tertullian writes:

> *Unicuique etiam provinciae et civitati suus deus est … ut Noricis Belenus …*

“Every province and city also has its own god … Belenus for the Noricans …”

This establishes late-second-century recognition of Belenus as a Norican regional god. It supplies no mythology, genealogy, ritual, or iconography.

#### Herodian, *History of the Empire* 8.3.8–9, written after 238 CE

**[Secure ancient literary evidence]** During Maximinus Thrax’s siege of Aquileia in 238, Herodian says the inhabitants trusted their local god, whom they called **Belen** and identified with Apollo. Some of Maximinus’s soldiers claimed repeatedly to see the god’s image in the sky fighting for Aquileia. This is the sole substantial ancient Belenus narrative.

#### *Historia Augusta*, *Two Maximini* 22.1–3, late fourth century CE

**[Late ancient elaboration]** The notoriously unreliable *Historia Augusta* says Belenus, speaking through soothsayers, foretold Maximinus’s defeat. It converts Herodian’s protective apparition into an explicit oracle. This is evidence for the story’s late-antique development, not independent proof of what occurred.

#### Ausonius, *Commemoratio professorum Burdigalensium* 4 and 10, late fourth century CE

**[Secure late-antique literary evidence; interpretation disputed]** Ausonius addresses Attius Patera:

> *tu Baiocassi stirpe Druidarum satus … Beleni sacratum ducis e templo genus*

“You, sprung from the Druid stock of the Baiocasses … trace your consecrated lineage from the temple of Belenus.”

He also calls Phoebicius *Beleni aedituus*, “temple-keeper of Belenus,” and says he came from an Armorican Druid family. The name *Phoebicius* invites association with Phoebus Apollo.

**[Historiographical dispute]** Mommsen and Wissowa suspected learned poetic play rather than evidence for a living Gallic Belenus cult. The explicit temple vocabulary, Gallic inscriptions, and personal geography make total dismissal difficult. Nevertheless, Ausonius does not prove uninterrupted pre-Roman Druidic priesthood into the fourth century; “Druid descent” may be family prestige, antiquarian memory, or poetic genealogy.

### Sources named in the tradition-specific brief that do not attest Belenus

**[Negative textual finding]** Belenus does not appear under that name in the *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, *Cath Maige Tuired*, the *Dindshenchas*, *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, *Tochmarc Étaíne*, or *Acallam na Senórach*. These texts cannot serve as a Belenus myth-cycle.

**[Negative textual finding]** Neither the Four Branches of the *Mabinogi*, *Culhwch ac Olwen*, nor the *Book of Taliesin* names Belenus. They attest Beli and his descendants in Welsh tradition, but the equation Beli = Belenus is a linguistic-historical hypothesis.

**[Negative textual finding]** Caesar, Lucan’s *Pharsalia* and its scholia do not name Belenus. Lucan names Teutates, Esus, and Taranis. Belenus should not be inserted into that triad.

**[Negative material finding]** The Gundestrup Cauldron, Pillar of the Boatmen, and Coligny Calendar contain no labeled representation or entry securely identifiable as Belenus. Any attribution is comparative speculation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Functions

- **[Secure ancient evidence] Civic defender:** Herodian’s god fights from the sky for Aquileia; *defensor* language reinforces the protective function.
- **[Secure ancient evidence] Oracle-giver:** the *Historia Augusta* attributes a prophecy to Belenus through diviners. Because the source is late and unreliable, the general existence of divination is more credible than its exact speech.
- **[Probable ancient evidence] Healing and sacred water:** *Fons/Fontes Beleni*, spring sanctuaries, and the Apollo equation support healing. No surviving Belenus hymn explicitly calls him “healer.”
- **[Secure ancient evidence] Civic and imperial benefactor:** emperors, civic officials, freedmen, and associations dedicated monuments to him.
- **[Modern reconstruction] Sun or light:** possible, but neither an indigenous hymn nor an unambiguous inscription calls Belenus *Sol*. Diocletian and Maximian dedicated separately to Apollo Belenus and Sol, which may actually caution against simply collapsing the two.
- **[Modern invention or unsupported extension] Fire, livestock fertility, wheel of the year, love, creativity, purification, and a horse-drawn solar chariot are common modern portfolio items but are not securely stated in the ancient Belenus corpus.

### Iconography

**[Negative finding]** There is no universally accepted, labeled sculptural type of Belenus comparable to Jupiter’s thunderbolt-bearing type. Most claimed images are recognized as Apollo-like figures because inscriptions or sanctuary context supply the name.

**[Secure material evidence]** The Iulium Carnicum temple inscription mentions two *signa*, “statues,” but their forms do not survive in the text.

**[Regional archaeological evidence]** Votive statuettes from the Sainte-Sabine sanctuary include conventional Apollo-like and animal forms. Horses and wheels found in such contexts may concern healing, mobility, solar imagery, or ordinary votive symbolism; they do not prove a distinctive Belenus mythology.

**[Contested evidence]** A Nîmes gem inscribed **ΒΗΛΗΝΟΣ** reportedly depicts an elderly bearded figure in a mitre-like headdress with stars. Its authenticity and relevance have long been questioned.

**[Modern reconstruction]** The radiant male head sometimes presented as Belenus may be Apollo or Sol iconography read through the cult context. A radiate crown is not by itself an identifying Belenus emblem.

### Alleged sacred correspondences

**[Unestablished]** No ancient source assigns Belenus a sacred color, weekday, number, month, metal, crystal, tarot card, or zodiacal sign.

**[Contested]** Horse figurines and wheels may have cultic relevance regionally but cannot be promoted into universal attributes.

**[Rejected as evidence]** Henbane’s possible linguistic association with *belenuntia* does not establish it as Belenus’s sacred plant or an ingredient in his rites.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

**[Negative finding]** No ancient inscription or literary source names Belenus’s parents, birth, siblings, spouse, or children. No securely preserved Continental Celtic genealogy exists.

**[Modern scholarly conjecture]** Belisama has sometimes been made his female counterpart or consort because her name appears linguistically similar and both were interpreted through bright/powerful etymologies. No ancient narrative joins them, and no certain paired dedication establishes marriage.

**[Modern scholarly conjecture]** The Norican or Carinthian goddess Belestis/Beléna/Belinca has likewise been proposed as a feminine counterpart. Similar names and geographical proximity do not demonstrate genealogy or marriage.

### Beli Mawr

**[Medieval Welsh evidence]** Beli Mawr is an ancestor figure in Welsh genealogical and narrative tradition, connected with figures such as Lludd, Caswallawn, Arianrhod, and other dynastic ancestors. Those relations belong to **Beli’s** dossier, not automatically to Belenus.

**[Older scholarly reconstruction]** Ifor Williams and others connected Beli with Belenus, treating medieval Beli as an insular survival or euhemerized form of the god.

**[Competing modern reconstruction]** John T. Koch noted that *Belenos* should normally yield Middle Welsh **Belyn**, as related compound names suggest, not **Beli**. He proposed that *Beli* is better derived from *Belgius/Bolgios*, perhaps reflecting Belgic ancestral tradition. This phonological objection makes “Belenus = Beli Mawr” possible only as a contested historical hypothesis, not a secure identity.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Apollo — identity/fusion.** **[Secure syncretistic identification]** In inscriptions, Belenus is named jointly as *Apollo Belenus*; Herodian states that Aquileians identify their local god with Apollo. This is the only fully secure god-to-god relationship, though it is an equation rather than a narrated meeting.
- **Fortuna Conservatrix — joint cultic appearance.** **[Secure regional cult association]** An inscription dedicates an offering *Apollini Beleno et Fortunae Conservatrici*, “to Apollo Belenus and Fortuna the Preserver.” It documents co-recipiency, not a mythic episode.
- **The Nymphs — shared water-cult context.** **[Secure regional association]** An altar associates Belenus with the Nymphs, fitting the spring evidence. No narrative interaction survives.
- **Antinous — poetic comparison/equation.** **[Late Roman literary-epigraphic evidence]** The Tibur funerary epigram CIL XIV 3535 includes *Antinous sit quoque qui Belenus …*, apparently aligning the deified Antinous with Belenus/Apollo. The damaged text does not preserve a mythic encounter.
- **Sol — adjacent but distinct imperial dedication.** **[Secure cultic juxtaposition]** Diocletianic Aquileia produced imperial dedications to Apollo Belenus and to Sol. Their coordinated political setting is documented; identity between them is not.
- **Belisama — no documented interaction.** **[Negative finding]** No ancient text records marriage, conversation, joint action, or combat.
- **Belestis — no documented interaction.** **[Negative finding]** Proposed linguistic or regional association only.
- **Any Irish or Welsh deity — no documented interaction.** **[Negative finding]** Belenus never appears in the Irish or Welsh narrative corpora and therefore has no attested meeting with Lugh, Brigid, Beli Mawr, Dôn, Arianrhod, Lleu, or any other named insular being.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The defense of Aquileia

**[Secure Roman-period legend]** In 238 CE Maximinus Thrax besieged Aquileia after the city supported the senatorial emperors. According to Herodian, the defenders trusted the local god Belen, identified with Apollo. Maximinus’s soldiers claimed to see his form repeatedly in the sky fighting for Aquileia. The siege failed; Maximinus and his son were killed by their own troops.

**[Historical function]** The story made civic resistance divinely authorized and converted military survival into proof of Aquileia’s patron god. Because Herodian attributes the vision to enemy soldiers, the narrative gives Belenus’s intervention unusually strong rhetorical force.

**[Late ancient elaboration]** The *Historia Augusta* says Belenus explicitly answered through soothsayers that Maximinus would be defeated. This is a different claim: prophecy precedes victory, while Herodian emphasizes the celestial combat apparition.

**[Modern comparative interpretation]** Scholars have compared the episode with Apollo defending Delphi from invading Gauls. The parallel may have shaped literary presentation, but dependence is not demonstrable.

### No surviving indigenous cycle

**[Negative finding]** No creation story, birth myth, courtship, divine combat, seasonal death and return, cattle myth, healing legend, or journey of Belenus survives from pre-Roman Celtic tradition.

**[Modern invention]** Stories in which Belenus marries Belisama, fathers Welsh dynasties, drives the sun in a chariot, presides over Beltane, battles a darkness god, or dies and returns annually are modern syntheses unless explicitly presented as new devotional myth.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Aquileia and Beligna

**[Secure archaeological and epigraphic evidence]** Aquileia was the principal known cult center. The concentration of dedications, Republican beginnings, repeated *Apollo Belenus* formulas, water dedications, elite offerings, and the siege narrative justify calling Belenus one of the city’s principal divine patrons.

**[Contested topography]** Beligna, south of Aquileia, preserves a name traditionally related to Belenus. Numerous monuments were recovered in or near this zone, but the exact number and location of separate Belenus sanctuaries remain debated. A later Christian basilica at Fondo Tullio/Beligna does not by itself prove that it physically replaced the principal pagan temple.

### Iulium Carnicum

**[Secure ancient evidence]** The temple-restoration inscription proves a substantial sanctuary by the late first century BCE. Local officials funded repair, five gilded pedimental shields, and two statues. This is the clearest evidence for the material splendor and public financing of a Belenus temple.

### Bardonecchia/Beaulard

**[Secure regional evidence]** Lucius Erax Bardus dedicated to *Deus Apollo Belenus* *ex responso*, “in accordance with a response,” and reportedly supplied a temple and ornaments. The phrase supports an oracular setting or divine instruction.

### Sainte-Sabine

**[Regional archaeological reconstruction]** A spring sanctuary in Burgundy is assigned to Apollo Belenus through inscriptions and finds. Pilgrims left votives, including small figurines. Healing is a reasonable interpretation, but individual cures are not recorded in surviving case histories.

### Personnel

- **[Secure late-antique evidence]** Ausonius calls Phoebicius an *aedituus*, temple attendant or custodian of Belenus.
- **[Secure late-antique evidence]** Attius Patera’s family is described as deriving from Belenus’s temple and Druid stock.
- **[Secure epigraphic evidence]** Civic magistrates, *seviri*, freedmen, merchants, imperial officials, and private vow-makers appear among dedicants.
- **[Unestablished]** No complete priestly hierarchy, initiation system, theological school, or hereditary “Order of Belenus” can be reconstructed.

### Ritual

**[Secure ancient evidence]** Vow-making and fulfillment were central: altars repeatedly carry *votum solvit libens merito*. Temple repair, statues, gilded shields, money, and architectural ornament were offerings.

**[Probable ancient evidence]** Divination is indicated by *ex responso* and by the *Historia Augusta*’s *haruspices*. The precise technique and personnel are uncertain.

**[Secure/likely ancient evidence]** Water cult is represented by *Fons/Fontes Beleni* and Nymph associations.

**[Unestablished]** No ancient calendar dates a Belenus festival. No source assigns his rites to 1 May.

### Sacrifice and allegations

**[Negative finding]** No source documents human sacrifice to Belenus. Caesar’s and later authors’ generalized allegations about Gallic sacrifice cannot be transferred to a specific god without evidence.

**[Negative finding]** No evidence connects Belenus with temple prostitution, cultic sexual coercion, or ritual violence.

**[Likely but nonspecific]** Animal sacrifice was ordinary in Roman provincial cult, but the currently accessible Belenus evidence does not establish a distinctive animal victim or sacrificial prescription.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Possible indigenous formation, before the second century BCE

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The Celtic morphology of the name, concentration among the Carni/eastern Alpine populations, and early Republican evidence indicate a pre-Roman or locally indigenous origin. Whether the cult began in Noricum, Carnia, Aquileia’s hinterland, or southern Gaul remains disputed.

### Late Republic, second–first centuries BCE

**[Secure emerging record]** Altars at Aquileia and the Iulium Carnicum temple show the cult entering Latin epigraphy and civic architecture. The spelling *Belinus* is prominent.

### Early Empire, first–second centuries CE

**[Secure expansion and Romanization]** Dedications multiply through northeastern Italy and the Alpine network. Apollo identification becomes explicit. Dedicants participate in ordinary Roman civic, commercial, and imperial religious structures.

**[Regional spread]** Southern Gaulish and other Italian attestations demonstrate that the name was not confined to Aquileia, although distribution is much less dense elsewhere.

### Around 197 CE

**[Secure literary recognition]** Tertullian can cite Belenus as Noricum’s characteristic provincial god without explanation, implying broad contemporary recognizability.

### 238 CE

**[Secure historical turning point]** Aquileia’s successful resistance turns Belenus into a narrated defender and oracle. This is the high point of his surviving literary profile.

### Later third–early fourth centuries

**[Secure imperial integration]** Diocletian and Maximian sponsored an Aquileian dedication to Apollo Belenus. The cult was therefore compatible with Tetrarchic state religion and civic loyalism.

### Late fourth century

**[Secure late survival or memory]** Ausonius knew traditions linking Gallic educated families and temple personnel to Belenus. Whether he describes functioning pagan institutions, family memory, or learned antiquarian self-fashioning remains disputed.

### Christianization

**[Secure general history; locally incomplete]** Aquileia became an important Christian center in late antiquity. Christian churches and cemeteries transformed its sacred landscape. Belenus dedications cease, but no surviving decree records a targeted suppression of his cult by name.

**[Medieval accretion]** Claims that a Benedictine church or monastery was erected directly from Belenus’s ruined temple near Beligna occur in later historical traditions. They may preserve topographical memory but require archaeological corroboration.

### Medieval survival

**[Negative finding]** There is no demonstrated continuous medieval cult of Belenus. Similar personal names, place-names, saints’ sites, Beli traditions, and folklore are not evidence of secret survival without an explicit chain of transmission.

### Modern revival

**[Modern reinvention]** Belenus returned through antiquarian publications, comparative mythology, Celtic revival literature, dictionaries, and modern Paganism. Contemporary devotion is real as modern religion but is reconstructive: ancient liturgy, theology, and festival calendars have not survived.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Antiquarian Belenus

**[Early-modern accretion]** Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century writers connected Belenus with British kings, Billingsgate, Cunobelinus, Baal, Apollo, the sun, and numerological 365-day symbolism. These arguments predate modern Celtic historical linguistics and frequently merge homonymous figures.

### Celtic revival

**[Documented modern context]** The eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Druid revival—Stukeley, Iolo Morganwg, later Gorsedd and Druid orders—created new ritual and literary frameworks from classical testimony, Welsh manuscripts, Romantic nationalism, Freemasonry, folklore, and invention.

**[Negative finding]** Yeats and Lady Gregory materially shaped the modern reception of Irish myth, but neither supplies ancient evidence about Belenus. Their Irish revival cannot validate his placement in the Mythological Cycle.

### Beltane

**[Medieval evidence]** *Sanas Cormaic*, conventionally dated to around the ninth–tenth century, explains *Belltaine* as *bil-tene*, “lucky/good fire,” and describes two fires made with incantations between which cattle were driven against yearly disease.

**[Contested learned derivation]** Later writers reinterpreted the first element as the name of a god Bel/Belenus, producing “Bel’s fire.” The gloss itself does not name Belenus, and no Irish narrative worships him.

**[Modern scholarly assessment]** The phonology and textual history make a direct Belenus derivation insecure. Peter Schrijver and other linguists have proposed alternative analyses; popular etymology may already have reshaped the medieval spelling. The most defensible conclusion is: **Beltane is not demonstrably named after Belenus.**

**[Modern invention]** Presenting Beltane as Belenus’s birthday, marriage, chief feast, or solar coronation is modern ritual mythology.

### Esotericism and modern Paganism

**[Negative finding]** Belenus has no clearly documented major role in the original Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn corpus, classical Theosophy, or a standard Renaissance grimoire. Generic assertions that he was a Golden Dawn, Hermetic, or ancient occult deity are unsupported.

**[Modern reinvention]** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century Druid, Celtic reconstructionist, eclectic Pagan, Wiccan, and New Age practitioners invoke Belenus as a solar, healing, fire, pastoral, or Beltane god. The “Order of Belenos” in Reformed Druid material and comparable ritual titles are modern creations.

**[Modern invention]** Correspondences involving quartz, gold candles, Sunday, midsummer, May Day, tarot cards, chakras, or “masculine divine energy” belong to modern occult systems.

### Popular culture

**[Documented modern reception]** Fantasy role-playing settings—notably Ravenloft—use Belenus as the name of a fictional institutional sun god. Modern games and proposed games likewise borrow the name.

**[Modern transformation]** These versions often construct churches, moral codes, clerical hierarchies, holy wars, and elaborate myths absent from the ancient record. They have significantly influenced internet descriptions of Belenus but are fictional receptions.

### Political appropriation

**[Negative finding]** No major, distinctive extremist cult of Belenus emerged in the reviewed evidence. Celtic and “Aryan sun god” language can occur in racialist or nationalist esotericism, but a Belenus-specific movement should not be asserted without organizational documents.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### From universal sun god to local cult

**[Historiographical development]** Older comparativists commonly began with “bright” etymology and Apollo, then reconstructed a pan-Celtic sun god surviving as Beli Mawr and Beltane. More recent work begins from the distribution, chronology, dedicants, spellings, and local sanctuaries. The result is a more securely documented but less uniform Belenus.

### Origin and direction of spread

- **[Theory] Carnian/eastern Alpine origin:** supported by early northeastern Italian evidence and Iulium Carnicum.
- **[Theory] Norican origin:** supported principally by Tertullian and regional linguistic arguments.
- **[Theory] Aquileian development:** supported by the exceptionally dense and early urban corpus.
- **[Theory] Pan-Celtic deity:** supported by the Celtic name and broad distribution, but weakened by the absence of secure Irish, Welsh, and British cult inscriptions.

No direction of diffusion commands universal agreement.

### The Apollo problem

**[Secure evidence]** Ancient worshippers sometimes explicitly called him Apollo Belenus.

**[Debate]** Scholars disagree whether this reflects close functional equivalence, Roman translation of an indigenous healing deity, deliberate civic Romanization, or the emergence of a new composite cult. “Belenus was simply the Celtic name for Apollo” erases the historical act of identification.

### Solar versus healing interpretation

**[Traditional theory]** Bright-name etymology, Apollo, radiate imagery, and the sky apparition support a solar reading.

**[Critical response]** Apollo was not exclusively solar; water and therapeutic evidence is stronger locally; separate dedications to Sol complicate identity; and the etymology itself is disputed.

### Ausonius and the Druids

**[Debate]** One reading treats Attius Patera and Phoebicius as evidence for late Gallic families attached to Belenus cult and proud of Druid ancestry. A skeptical reading sees literary archaism and name-play. What is secure is Ausonius’s fourth-century representation, not institutional continuity from the Iron Age.

### Beli Mawr

**[Debate]** Similarity and older comparative tradition favor identification; regular Brittonic sound development favors Koch’s objection that *Belenos* should yield **Belyn**, not **Beli**. The identification remains possible as cultural association or later contamination but is not philologically secure.

### Condition of the evidence

**[Secure assessment]** The surviving record is lopsided: dozens of short votive inscriptions, a handful of literary notices, uncertain sculptures, and almost no indigenous-language exposition. Most stones tell us who dedicated, the divine name, and that a vow was fulfilled—not what worshippers believed about the god’s origins.

**[Research infrastructure]** Principal corpora and studies include CIL I, V, VI, XI, XII and XIV; *Inscriptiones Aquileiae*; RIG I; ILLPRON; Jufer and Luginbühl’s *Répertoire des dieux gaulois*; Bauchhenss’s LIMC entry “Apollo Belenus”; Claudio Zaccaria’s *Cultores Beleni*; Marjeta Šašel Kos’s studies of eastern Alpine cult; and Ralph Haeussler’s *Encyclopedia of Ancient History* entry.

## 10. Chronology

- **Second century BCE? — [Probable early evidence]** An Aquileian cippus naming Belenus is assigned typologically to this century; dating remains debated.
- **Late second–first century BCE — [Secure early horizon]** Aulus Barbius Parnax dedicates an altar to *B(elinus)* at Aquileia, CIL I² 3411.
- **Second half of first century BCE — [Secure cult institution]** Officials restore the temple of Belinus at Iulium Carnicum and provide gilded shields and two statues, CIL V 1829.
- **Late Republic/early Empire — [Regional expansion]** Dedications appear along Alpine and northern Italian routes.
- **First–second centuries CE — [Secure flourishing]** Aquileia produces a dense body of private, civic, and professional dedications; *Apollo Belenus* becomes explicit.
- **Second century CE — [Secure Romanized cult]** Monumental dedications combine Apollo, Belenus, and imperial titles.
- **Ca. 197 CE — [Secure literary attestation]** Tertullian identifies Belenus as the Noricans’ god.
- **238 CE — [Secure historical legend]** During Maximinus Thrax’s siege, Belenus is said to appear in the sky defending Aquileia.
- **After 238 CE — [Late elaboration]** The siege story develops an oracular form.
- **Late third century CE — [Secure imperial patronage]** Diocletian and Maximian dedicate to Apollo Belenus at Aquileia.
- **Late fourth century CE — [Secure reception]** Ausonius associates Belenus temples and temple service with Druid-descended families of Gaul.
- **Fourth–sixth centuries CE — [Historical displacement]** Christian Aquileia expands; Belenus inscriptions disappear. No named suppression decree survives.
- **Middle Ages — [No demonstrated continuity]** Beli genealogies and Beltane traditions continue independently, but no document identifies them with Belenus.
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries — [Early-modern reinvention]** Antiquarians link Belenus with British kings, Billingsgate, solar numerology, Baal, and Beltane.
- **Nineteenth century — [Comparativist systematization]** Celtic mythographers popularize the pan-Celtic solar-god model.
- **1890s–twentieth century — [Archaeological consolidation]** Aquileian and Beligna discoveries are catalogued; CIL and *Inscriptiones Aquileiae* establish the epigraphic corpus.
- **Late twentieth century — [Scholarly revision]** Linguistic and regional-cult studies question automatic equations with Beli, Beltane, Belisama, and the sun.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries — [Modern revival]** Pagan and Druid groups adopt Belenus into reconstructed or newly composed ritual systems.
- **2026 — [Current evidentiary position]** Belenus remains securely an important eastern Alpine and Aquileian god with Apollo, healing-water, oracular, and civic-defense associations; the Irish, Welsh, Beltane, universal-solar, and elaborate genealogical identities remain contested or modern.

### Evidentiary conclusion

**[Secure]** Belenus was a real object of ancient worship, documented most densely through late-Republican and imperial inscriptions at Aquileia and in the eastern Alpine world. Ancient people identified him with Apollo, invoked him through vows and springs, consulted him or represented him as oracular, and credited him with defending Aquileia.

**[Not established]** No surviving evidence gives him an indigenous genealogy, a complete myth-cycle, a fixed solar iconography, an Irish cult, a Welsh narrative identity, a Beltane festival, a marriage to Belisama, or a continuous medieval priesthood.

**[Historically important modern reception]** Those absent features were supplied by antiquarian comparison, Romantic Celticism, comparative mythology, modern Druidry, Pagan reconstruction, and fantasy literature. They belong to Belenus’s reception history, but not to the securely recoverable ancient core.
