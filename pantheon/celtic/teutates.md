# Teutates: Research Dossier

**Class:** male divine being—a Gaulish and Romano-British god or divine title, commonly interpreted as “the god/belonging to the *toutā* (‘people, tribe, polity’).” Whether *Teutates/Toutatis* originally named one pan-Gaulish god or functioned as a title applied to different communities’ protective gods remains disputed.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Lucan, *Bellum civile*/*Pharsalia* 1.444–446, composed about 60–65 CE, names *Teutates* among three Gaulish gods propitiated with human blood. This is the earliest presently datable explicit occurrence of the name, although its surviving manuscripts are medieval. Romano-British and Continental inscriptions provide independent material attestation principally from the Roman imperial period, approximately the second and third centuries CE.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** ancient literary, epigraphic, or archaeological evidence.
- **[LE] Later elaboration:** an ancient or medieval source expanding earlier evidence.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally confined epigraphic or cultic evidence.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** ancient, medieval, or modern attempts to arrange fragmentary evidence into a coherent theology.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation or conjunction with another god.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reception:** a demonstrably modern representation, ritual, identification, or story.
- **[NE] Not established:** a claim for which no direct evidence has been identified.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

**[EA]** Teutates is directly documented in Roman-period Gaulish religion through Lucan, Lactantius, inscriptions, and later commentary on Lucan. The evidence portrays—or was interpreted as portraying—a god connected with a community, warfare, human blood, and either Mars or Mercury. It does not preserve a biography, genealogy, native mythological narrative, canonical image, named temple, or indigenous theological exposition.

**[MSR]** “God of the tribe” is a reasonable etymological shorthand, not an ancient definition. Proto-Celtic \**towtā* or \**toutā* means “people, tribe, political community,” reflected in Old Irish *túath*, Middle Welsh *tut*, Breton *tud*, Celtiberian *toutam*, and related Continental forms. The suffix of *Toutatis* can be understood as relational—approximately “he of the people”—but older expansions such as “Father of the People,” from a supposed *Touto-tatis*, are debated and should not be treated as translations established by an ancient gloss. Matasović reconstructs Proto-Celtic \**towtā*; the University of Vienna’s Lexicon Leponticum traces the family to Indo-European \**tewtéh₂*. [S1, S2]

### Documented forms

- **Teutates**: Lucan’s Latin literary form, nominative; Lactantius has accusative *Teutaten*. **[EA]**
- **Toutatis**: the form conventionally reconstructed from epigraphic oblique cases and adopted in French reception. **[EA/MSR]**
- **Toutati**: dative in the Barkway dedication *Marti Toutati*. **[EA/RV/SI]**
- **Totati/Tota(ti)**: Romano-British spelling or abbreviation, including the Hockliffe ring *deo To(u)ta(ti)*. **[EA/RV]**
- **Totates**: dative-like form in pottery graffiti from Beauclair, in Arvernian territory. **[EA/RV]**
- **Tutatis/Teutatis**: spelling variants reported in the epigraphic and antiquarian literature. **[EA/RV]**
- **TOT** and **TOT TOT**: abbreviated inscriptions on Romano-British rings. Their expansion was formerly uncertain, but the Hockliffe ring’s *DEO TOTA* provides strong evidence that at least this ring series invoked Toutatis. **[EA/RV]**

### Titles and associated names

- **Mars Toutatis**—“Mars Toutatis”: unequivocally attested at Barkway, Hertfordshire, and in a compound dedication at Seckau in Noricum. **[EA/SI]**
- **Toutatis Medurinus**: the Roman inscription CIL VI 31182 reads approximately *Petiganus Placidus Toutati Medurini votum solvet anniversarium*. The meaning of *Medurinus* is uncertain; proposals connecting it with Celtic *medu-* (“mead, intoxication”) remain etymological reconstruction. **[EA/RV; interpretation MSR]**
- **Toutatis with Riocalatis and Mars Cocidius**: the damaged Cumbrian altar RIB 1017 is now read as addressing Jupiter Optimus Maximus, Riocalatis, Toutatis, and Mars Cocidius. Earlier restoration as a single *Mars Tutatis Cocidius* is rejected by the current RIB editors. **[EA/RV]**
- **Mars Latobius Marmogius Toutatis Sinatis Mogetius**: CIL III 5320/11721 from Seckau, Austria. *Toutati* appears to have been inserted in smaller lettering, possibly secondarily, among several indigenous epithets of Mars. **[EA/RV/SI]**
- **Teutanus, Toutenus, Toutiorix, Toutanicus**: linguistically related “people/tribe” formations occur as epithets of Jupiter, Mercury, and Apollo. They demonstrate the productivity of the root but are not automatically attestations of Teutates. In particular, Apollo Toutiorix (“king of the people”) at Wiesbaden is not evidence that Teutates was Apollo. **[EA; distinction MSR]**
- **Crougintoudadigoe** in a Celtiberian inscription probably denotes a “hill/mound of Toutatis” or another compound containing the same root. Its segmentation and religious significance remain disputed; it cannot securely establish a Spanish cult of the same individual god. **[EA/MSR]**

### One god or a title?

Two principal models remain viable:

1. **Pan-regional deity model [LS/MSR].** Lucan’s use of an unqualified proper name and its recurrence across Britain, Gaul, Rome, and Noricum may indicate a recognizable deity distributed across several Celtic-speaking populations.
2. **Community-title model [MSR].** *Toutatis* may mean “the one of the tribe,” so different communities could apply it to their own protector. Its attachment to Mars and its relationship to other *toutā*-derived epithets support this possibility.

The evidence does not decide whether every inscription refers to one transregional personality. Nor does Lucan establish that Teutates, Esus, and Taranis formed a canonical “Celtic triad.” That influential arrangement is a modern scholarly systematization of three names juxtaposed for poetic effect.

### The alleged Irish and Welsh identity

**[NE]** Teutates does not appear by name in *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, *Cath Maige Tuired*, the *Táin*, *Tochmarc Étaíne*, the *Dindshenchas*, the *Acallam na Senórach*, the Four Branches of the *Mabinogi*, *Culhwch ac Olwen*, or the *Book of Taliesin*. Old Irish *túath* and Middle Welsh *tut* are linguistic cognates of the root in *Toutatis*, but cognate vocabulary is not evidence that the literary characters of Ireland or Wales are Teutates.

Equations with the Dagda, Nuadu/Nodens, Lugus/Lug, or a generic “tribal father” are comparative reconstructions. No medieval Irish or Welsh text identifies any of those beings as Teutates.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Lucan

**[EA]** Lucan composed the *Bellum civile* under Nero and died in 65 CE. At 1.444–446 he describes Gaulish peoples who pacify:

> *et quibus immitis placatur sanguine diro  
> Teutates horrensque feris altaribus Esus  
> et Taranis Scythicae non mitior ara Dianae*

In sense: “those by whom pitiless Teutates is appeased with dreadful blood, and Esus of the savage altars, and Taranis whose altar is no gentler than that of Scythian Diana.” [S3]

Lucan supplies no genealogy, appearance, myth, festival, priesthood, victim type, or precise locality for Teutates. He does assert—or poetically deploys the claim—that the god received bloody human sacrifice. The passage forms part of an ethnographic catalogue and draws on Roman literary conventions about northern “barbarian” religion. That context requires caution, but it does not justify deleting the testimony.

### Caesar

**[EA, indirect only]** Caesar’s *Bellum Gallicum* 6.16–17, written in the 50s BCE, predates Lucan but never names Teutates. Caesar says that some Gauls offered humans, sometimes burned in immense wicker structures, and that druids administered sacrifices. He separately gives Roman names to principal Gaulish gods: Mercury, Apollo, Mars, Jupiter, and Minerva. Mars governed war and received vows of booty. [S4, S5]

**[MSR]** Because later scholia identify Teutates as Mercury or Mars, Caesar has often been used to reconstruct Teutates’ functions. This is indirect reasoning: Caesar never says “Teutates is Mars/Mercury,” and his burning wicker victims cannot be assigned specifically to Teutates.

### Lactantius

**[LE]** About 303–311 CE, the Christian apologist Lactantius wrote in *Divinae institutiones* 1.21.3: *Galli Esum atque Teutaten humano cruore placabant*—“The Gauls appeased Esus and Teutates with human blood.” [S6] This confirms continued literary transmission of the charge but adds neither drowning nor independent cult detail. Lactantius’ polemical purpose was to condemn pagan sacrifice.

### The Lucan scholia

**[LE/LS]** The *Commenta Bernensia* and *Adnotationes super Lucanum* survive in manuscripts of approximately the ninth through eleventh centuries, although they contain older scholastic material, some potentially late antique. Paul-Marie Duval emphasizes their complicated transmission and the appearance of related glosses in manuscripts of the tenth–twelfth centuries. [S7]

The *Commenta* gives two incompatible explanations:

- *Mercurius lingua Gallorum Teutates dicitur… Teutates Mercurius sic apud Gallos placatur: in plenum semicupium homo in caput demittitur, ut ibi suffocetur*—“In the language of the Gauls Mercury is called Teutates … Teutates-Mercury is appeased thus: a man is lowered headfirst into a filled small vat so that he suffocates.” **[LE/SI]**
- *Item aliter exinde in aliis invenimus. Teutates Mars sanguine diro placatur…*—“We also find it differently in other [authorities]. Teutates-Mars is appeased with dreadful blood,” either because he incites battles or because the Gauls sacrificed humans to him as to other gods. **[LE/SI]**

The *Adnotationes* calls Teutates Mercury and says Gauls appeased him by killing people. The scholia are the sole source for the headfirst vat-drowning. They may preserve lost ancient learning, medieval inference, or both; they are not contemporary eyewitness records. Claims that the rite occurred specifically at Samhain or on 1 November are not in Lucan and should be classified as **[MI]** unless an independent source is produced.

### Epigraphic corpus

The securely relevant corpus is small:

- **RIB 219, Barkway, Hertfordshire:** silver leaf-shaped votive plaque, found in 1744; Roman-period but more precisely undated: *MARTI TOVTATI … V S L M*, “To Mars Toutatis … [the dedicator] willingly and deservedly fulfilled his vow.” **[EA/RV/SI]** [S8]
- **RIB 1017, probably Cumbria:** damaged sandstone altar, Roman-period, found before 1875; current reconstruction addresses Jupiter Optimus Maximus, Riocalatis, Toutatis, and Mars Cocidius. **[EA/RV]** [S9]
- **Romano-British TOT rings:** a concentrated British class, especially in Lincolnshire and eastern Britain. A Hockliffe silver fragment reads *DEO TOTA* and *[UTERE] FELIX*: “To the god Toutatis” and “Use [this] happily.” This turns the previously conjectural expansion of many *TOT* rings into a strong interpretation, though individual abbreviated specimens remain context-dependent. **[EA/RV]** [S10, S11]
- **Beauclair, Voingt/Giat, Puy-de-Dôme:** several ceramic graffiti addressed to *Totates*, recovered at a Roman road settlement in Arvernian territory. These are important because they demonstrate local Continental invocation outside Lucan’s literary tradition. **[EA/RV]** [S12]
- **CIL VI 31182, Rome:** a vow to *Toutati Medurini*, apparently to be renewed annually. **[EA/RV]**
- **CIL III 5320 = 11721, Seckau:** a dedication to Mars with several indigenous epithets, including secondarily inserted *Toutati*. **[EA/RV/SI]**
- **Jort, Calvados:** a bronze stylus reportedly carrying a votive inscription to Toutatis; published discussion dates from the twenty-first century. Its precise archaeological context and reading should be checked in the definitive edition before using it as a chronological anchor. **[EA claimed, publication-dependent]**

There is no surviving indigenous Gaulish hymn, myth, prayer book, priestly commentary, or ritual calendar naming Teutates.

### Corpora that do not attest him

**[NE]** The Coligny calendar contains no secure occurrence of Teutates. The Pillar of the Boatmen names and depicts several gods—including Esus and Cernunnos—but not Teutates. No inscription on the Gundestrup cauldron identifies any figure as Teutates. None of these objects may be treated as his image or calendar without explicit argument, and no such identification commands consensus.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

- **Community protection:** inferred from the name’s relationship to “people/tribe.” **[MSR]**
- **Warfare and collective defense:** supported by the epigraphic *Mars Toutatis* and the scholion’s Teutates-Mars. **[EA/SI; function partly MSR]**
- **Commerce, travel, invention, or prosperity:** arises only if the Teutates-Mercury equation is accepted and Caesar’s description of Gaulish Mercury is transferred to him. **[LE/SI/MSR]**
- **Human sacrifice/bloody propitiation:** directly alleged by Lucan and Lactantius; drowning in a vat appears only in the medieval scholia. **[EA allegation; LE method]**

It is therefore excessive to define Teutates simply as “the Celtic war god,” “chief god,” “father god,” or “Mercury.” Each formulation promotes one contested strand into an exclusive identity.

### Iconography

**[NE]** No ancient image bears an inscription securely identifying the depicted figure as Teutates. Accordingly, Teutates has no demonstrable canonical beard, helmet, spear, cauldron, animal, color, number, day, planet, tree, or emblem.

The bronze Mars found with or near the Barkway votive assemblage provides cultic context but does not prove that every generic Mars image represents Teutates. Assignments of figures on the Gundestrup cauldron, the Pillar of the Boatmen, or other anonymous Gallo-Roman sculpture to Teutates are **[MSR]** or popular speculation. The cauldron itself, found dismantled in Denmark in 1891, combines disputed Celtic and southeastern European/Thracian features; its figures are unnamed. [S13, S14]

### Powers and limitations

No primary source describes Teutates performing a miracle or mythic action. His inferred capacities are receiving vows, accepting sacrifice, protecting a community, and—in the Mars scholion—impelling battle. No source states a limitation or defeat.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

**[NE]** No source supplies Teutates with parents, birth, siblings, consort, children, servants, mount, rival, or successor. He is not textually a father of the Gauls, an ancestor of the Tuatha Dé Danann, the spouse of Rosmerta, or a relative of Esus and Taranis.

### Every documented named divine interaction or conjunction

Because no surviving narrative gives Teutates an episode with another god, the complete record consists of joint appearances and identifications:

- **Esus — joint literary appearance:** Teutates and Esus are named consecutively among blood-propitiated Gaulish gods. Source: Lucan, *Bellum civile* 1.444–446, c. 60–65 CE; repeated by Lactantius, c. 303–311. **[EA]**
- **Taranis — joint literary appearance:** Teutates and Taranis occur in the same Lucanian catalogue; no action between them is described. Source and date as above. **[EA]**
- **Mercury — identity asserted:** the *Commenta Bernensia* and *Adnotationes* identify Teutates with Mercury. Surviving witnesses are early medieval; underlying material may be older. **[LE/LS/SI]**
- **Mars — identity asserted and epigraphically enacted:** the scholia offer Teutates-Mars as an alternative; RIB 219 directly dedicates to *Mars Toutatis*, and Seckau includes *Toutati* among epithets of Mars. **[EA/SI plus LE]**
- **Jupiter Optimus Maximus — joint recipient:** named with Riocalatis, Toutatis, and Mars Cocidius on RIB 1017. No narrative interaction occurs. Roman-period, more closely undated. **[EA/RV]**
- **Riocalatis — joint recipient:** same altar and qualification. Whether Riocalatis is a separate god, epithet, or damaged name remains epigraphically difficult. **[EA/RV]**
- **Mars Cocidius — joint recipient:** same altar; the current RIB punctuation treats Mars Cocidius separately from Toutatis. **[EA/RV/SI]**
- **Diana of Scythia — comparison, not interaction:** Lucan says Taranis’ altar was no gentler than hers. Diana does not interact with Teutates. **[EA; explicitly not a relation]**
- **Apollo Toutiorix — no documented interaction:** related name formation only. **[NE]**
- **Dagda, Nuadu/Nodens, Lugus/Lug, Belenos, Rosmerta, Cernunnos — no documented interaction:** proposed associations belong to comparative scholarship or modern religion. **[NE/MSR]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Lucanian sacrificial tableau

**[EA]** The nearest thing to a Teutates myth is Lucan’s brief catalogue: after Roman troops leave Gaul during the civil war, several peoples resume their practices; Teutates, Esus, and Taranis are introduced as recipients of dreadful blood. There is no plot involving the gods themselves.

**[LE]** Medieval commentators transform this allusion into differentiated ritual lore: Teutates receives a victim drowned headfirst in a water-filled vat; Esus and Taranis receive other forms of death. This is a scholastic explanatory scheme, not a surviving Gaulish narrative cycle.

### Human sacrifice: evidence and limits

The relevant layers must remain separate:

1. Caesar, before Teutates is named, reports human sacrifice among Gauls, including burning people in wicker structures. **[EA, general]**
2. Lucan attributes bloody propitiation specifically to Teutates and associates comparable cruelty with Esus and Taranis. **[EA, literary]**
3. Lactantius repeats that Esus and Teutates received human blood. **[LE, polemical]**
4. The *Commenta Bernensia* supplies vat-drowning and conflicting Roman identities. **[LE]**
5. Archaeological finds from Iron Age Europe sometimes show violent death or deposition, but none identifies a victim as sacrificed to Teutates. **[NE as to this god]**

Consequently, “Teutates’ worshippers drowned victims in vats” is a faithful report of what a medieval scholium says, not an archaeologically demonstrated cult procedure. The frequent association of the rite with Samhain is a modern harmonization with Irish calendrical tradition.

### Alleged Irish parallels

Comparisons between the drowned Teutates victim and medieval Irish stories about triple death, regicide, cauldrons, or sacrificial kings are **[MSR]**. They may illuminate comparative themes but do not constitute variants of a Teutates myth. No Irish narrative names him.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult geography

The material distribution is scattered but real:

- eastern and northern Roman Britain: Barkway, the Cumbrian altar, and the *TOT* ring distribution;
- central Gaul: Beauclair in Arvernian territory;
- Rome: Toutatis Medurinus;
- Noricum: Seckau’s Mars dedication;
- possibly Normandy: Jort.

This pattern is compatible with mobility under the Roman Empire—soldiers, freedmen, travelers, artisans, and local devotees—but cannot establish a centrally organized cult.

### Vows and portable devotion

**[EA]** RIB 219 uses the standard votive formula *votum solvit libens merito*. RIB 1017 also records fulfillment of a vow. The Rome inscription apparently specifies an annual vow. These documents show ordinary petition-and-fulfillment religion rather than a recoverable mythology.

**[EA/RV]** The rings imply portable or personal devotion. Their concentration in Britain and formulas such as *utere felix* suggest objects worn, exchanged, or dedicated for well-being and divine protection. Whether all abbreviated *TOT* rings were cult objects, identity markers, or both remains debated.

**[EA/RV]** Beauclair’s ceramic graffiti point to repeated invocation at an unmonumental roadside settlement rather than only at grand temples.

### Priesthood and temples

**[NE]** No priest of Teutates is named. Caesar’s statement that druids administered some Gaulish sacrifices is not evidence for a specialized Teutates priesthood.

**[NE]** No excavated sanctuary can securely be called a Temple of Teutates from an identifying inscription. Barkway produced a votive hoard rather than a securely reconstructed temple. The locations of the Beauclair graffiti and other inscriptions demonstrate cult activity but not an architectural program.

### Festivals and calendar

**[NE]** No festival, feast day, pilgrimage, oracle, initiation, sacred marriage, or place in the Coligny calendar is securely attached to Teutates. Samhain/1 November is not documented as his feast.

### Cult economics and politics

**[MSR]** A communal protector called “of the people” could readily support civic or military identity. The Mars conjunction and British ring concentration are consistent with that role. Nevertheless, no inscription names a sponsoring *civitas*, council, king, or military unit, so claims that Teutates served as the national god of all Gaul exceed the evidence.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the first century BCE

**[NE]** The divine name is not securely attested. The linguistic root *toutā* is older than the Roman conquest, but that does not date the formation of the god or title.

### Caesar’s Gaul, 50s BCE

**[EA, contextual]** Caesar describes Gaulish human sacrifice and Romanizes major gods as Mercury, Apollo, Mars, Jupiter, and Minerva. He does not name Teutates.

### Lucan, c. 60–65 CE

**[EA]** Teutates first enters the datable written record as a fierce recipient of blood. Lucan places him with Esus and Taranis but does not call them a formal triad.

### Roman imperial period, chiefly second–third centuries CE

**[EA]** Vows and graffiti document the name across Britain and parts of the Continent. The pairing *Mars Toutatis* is materially secure. The evidence reflects Gallo-Roman and Romano-British religious practice, in which indigenous names could qualify Roman gods without necessarily disappearing into them.

The cult’s “peak” cannot be quantified. The survival of several personal objects and a handful of stone or metal dedications suggests geographical reach but not mass membership.

### Late antiquity

**[LE]** Lactantius repeats the human-sacrifice charge in Christian apologetic. There is no record of a temple’s closure, a law naming Teutates, forced conversion of a specific Teutates community, or continuity beneath a saint’s cult. General imperial restrictions on sacrifice affected traditional religion, but applying them specifically to this god is inference.

### Early and high Middle Ages

**[LE/LS]** Teutates survives in the manuscript classroom through Lucan and his scholia, not in demonstrable continuous worship. Papias and other glossators reproduce Roman identifications. The vat-drowning becomes the most vivid feature of his later learned profile.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

**[LS]** Classical texts, antiquarian epigraphy, and developing Celtic philology reintroduced Teutates to European scholarship. Some antiquarians interpreted him as a national father, a Gaulish Mercury, a Mars, or a member of a grand triad. These reconstructions often turned a sparse corpus into a unified “Celtic pantheon.”

Salomon Reinach challenged the idea that Lucan’s three names proved a formal national triad, arguing for a more local or poetic reading. Later scholarship has generally become more cautious about pan-Celtic equations. [S15]

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

**[MSR/MI]** Epigraphic discoveries changed the balance of evidence. Toutatis is no longer only Lucan’s literary god: Barkway, Beauclair, and the British ring corpus show actual invocation. The 2005 Hockliffe discovery was particularly important because *DEO TOTA* supports expansion of abbreviated *TOT* rings as dedications to Toutatis.

Modern polytheists and reconstructionists sometimes worship Toutatis as a tribal protector, war god, community guardian, or localized title. Such practice is a modern religious revival drawing creatively on ancient fragments. There is no demonstrated institutional continuity from Roman Gaul.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Celtic Revival and occultism

**[MI]** Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Celtic Revival authors and druid organizations popularized a generalized Celtic sacred past. Yet Teutates was not a central figure in the best-documented Irish literary revival of Yeats and Lady Gregory, whose primary mythic material was medieval Irish rather than Gaulish epigraphy.

No reliable evidence places Teutates in a central Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or canonical modern grimoire role. Modern websites sometimes attach planetary, elemental, calendrical, or magical correspondences to him, but these are modern constructions unless a dated ritual text is cited. The absence is significant: Teutates’ chief modern vehicle was popular Gaulish nationalism and entertainment, not the major Hermetic orders.

### *Astérix*

**[MI]** René Goscinny and Albert Uderzo launched *Astérix* in *Pilote* in 1959. Its Gauls repeatedly exclaim *Par Toutatis!*—“By Toutatis!” The oath transformed an obscure classical name into a familiar marker of cartoon Gaulish identity. The expression is modern comic dialogue, not a documented ancient formula. BnF and official *Astérix* materials document the series’ late-1950s genesis and its enormous cultural afterlife. [S16, S17]

The comics also encourage the modern impression that Toutatis was the single national god of all Gauls. Ancient evidence supports neither the oath’s wording nor that exclusive status.

### Astronomical and commercial reception

**[MI]** Near-Earth asteroid 4179 Toutatis, formerly 1989 AC, was named after the deity as invoked in *Astérix*. JPL explicitly records the comic connection. The object was later visited by China’s Chang’e-2 spacecraft in December 2012. [S18, S19]

The name has also been used for games, music, products, and a major Parc Astérix roller coaster. These uses descend primarily from the comic’s celebrity.

### Neopagan reception

**[MI]** Contemporary Gaulish-polytheist communities invoke Toutatis in reconstructed prayers and communal rites. Practices differ: some treat him as a distinct god; others as a title for each *toutā*’s protector. Neither model can claim unbroken ancient transmission. Sacred plants, colors, feast days, or detailed personalities assigned in such settings are modern ritual choices unless separately grounded in an inscription or text.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

“God of the people” language can be adapted to ethnic nationalism, but the present source search did not establish a major, organized extremist cult specifically centered on Teutates. Claims of such appropriation require case-specific documentation. Romantic nationalist portrayals of him as the unitary god of an ethnically homogeneous “Celtic race” are modern ideological constructions, not conclusions compelled by the ancient record.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### Etymology

The secure element is the relationship to Celtic “people/tribe.” Dispute concerns the formation:

- “the one belonging to the tribe” from *toutā* plus a relational suffix;
- “father of the tribe,” an older but less secure segmentation;
- a fixed proper name versus a repeatedly formed communal title.

The name’s spelling also reflects linguistic development and Latin transmission. Lucan’s *Teutates* need not reproduce the contemporary Gaulish pronunciation represented by *Toutati/Totates*.

### Mars versus Mercury

The evidence is asymmetrical:

- **Mars:** direct epigraphic conjunction at Barkway and Seckau, plus one strand of scholia.
- **Mercury:** medieval scholia and glosses, but no currently secure *Mercurius Toutatis* dedication.
- **Conclusion:** Mars has stronger material support, but the Mercury tradition is genuinely attested and should not be erased. It may preserve a regional equation, confused commentary, or a title capable of attachment to different functional gods.

### One universal god versus many tribal protectors

The distribution of the name supports recognition beyond one locality, while the etymology favors community specificity. Related epithets of several Roman gods show that “people/tribe” terminology was not monopolized by one divine personality. The best reconstruction leaves both possibilities open.

### Human sacrifice

The dossier contains three different evidentiary questions:

1. Did Roman authors say Gauls practiced human sacrifice? Yes.
2. Did Lucan and Lactantius associate Teutates with human blood? Yes.
3. Has archaeology independently demonstrated the scholia’s headfirst vat ritual for Teutates? No.

Roman conquest rhetoric and Christian polemic could exaggerate enemy cruelty; conversely, rhetorical interest does not by itself prove invention. The appropriate conclusion is attestation of the claim, not certainty about frequency or procedure.

### The “Teutates–Esus–Taranis triad”

Lucan names three gods together. He does not call them a triad, assign cosmic functions, give family relations, or describe joint worship. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century arrangements of them as a sovereign-war-fertility system are **[LS]**. Reinach and Duval’s more cautious readings better fit the limited evidence.

### Insular Celtic comparisons

The root shared with Old Irish *túath* is linguistically important. It does not make Teutates a member of the Tuatha Dé Danann, identify him with the Dagda, or make Welsh *tud* a divine name. Comparative identifications with Nuadu/Nodens or Lugus may generate hypotheses about communal sovereignty but remain unconfirmed.

### Iconographic overreach

No named Teutates image survives. Identifying an anonymous warrior, a cauldron figure, a horned god, a three-headed figure, or a generic Mars statue as Teutates is hypothesis. The Gundestrup cauldron and Pillar of the Boatmen belong in the broader study of Iron Age/Gallo-Roman religious imagery, not in a secure Teutates iconographic corpus.

### State of the evidence

The primary evidence survives in:

- literary manuscripts and critical editions of Lucan, Lactantius, Caesar, and the Lucan scholia;
- RIB and CIL epigraphic corpora;
- museum-held plaques, altars, pottery, and rings;
- modern archaeological publications such as *Gallia* and *Britannia*.

Portable-antiquities discoveries continue to enlarge the British corpus, but loss of find context is a major limitation. Many objects are only broadly datable within Roman Britain, 43–410 CE.

## 10. Chronology

- **Pre-Roman period:** Proto-Celtic *toutā*, “people/tribe,” develops as a social-political term. No securely named Teutates cult object is dated to this period. **[linguistic reconstruction]**
- **58–50 BCE:** Caesar’s Gallic campaigns and composition of *Bellum Gallicum*. Book 6 reports human sacrifice and describes Gaulish Mercury and Mars, but does not name Teutates. **[EA, contextual]**
- **c. 60–65 CE:** Lucan composes *Bellum civile*; 1.444–446 is the earliest securely datable explicit attestation of Teutates. **[EA]**
- **c. second–third centuries CE:** most relevant Romano-British and Continental dedications probably belong broadly to this period: Barkway’s *Marti Toutati*, British rings, Beauclair graffiti, Roman and Norican dedications. Many cannot be dated more narrowly. **[EA]**
- **c. 303–311 CE:** Lactantius states that Gauls appeased Esus and Teutates with human blood. **[LE]**
- **Fourth–fifth centuries CE:** traditional public cult declines under Christian imperial rule. No suppression decree or converted temple specifically naming Teutates survives. **[general context; NE specifically]**
- **Ninth–eleventh centuries:** principal surviving manuscript witnesses of early Lucan commentary transmit Teutates-Mercury, Teutates-Mars, and the drowning-in-a-vat account. **[LE/LS]**
- **Eleventh–twelfth centuries:** further Lucan glosses continue the learned identifications. **[LE]**
- **1744:** Barkway votive treasure, including the silver *Marti Toutati* plaque, is discovered. **[modern rediscovery]**
- **Before 1875:** the damaged Cumbrian altar later catalogued as RIB 1017 is recorded in a museum collection. **[modern rediscovery]**
- **1891:** Gundestrup cauldron discovered in Denmark; subsequent writers sometimes attempt to associate unnamed figures with Celtic gods, including Teutates, without identifying inscriptions. **[modern scholarly reception]**
- **1897–early twentieth century:** Reinach challenges national-triad constructions; Celtic religion becomes a major comparativist field. **[historiography]**
- **1959:** *Astérix* begins publication; *Par Toutatis!* becomes the god’s most influential modern representation. **[MI]**
- **1989:** asteroid 4179 Toutatis is discovered as 1989 AC and later receives its permanent name, explicitly inspired by the deity’s *Astérix* fame. **[MI]**
- **2005:** Hockliffe silver ring fragment discovered; *DEO TOTA* materially strengthens the Toutatis interpretation of British *TOT* rings. **[EA rediscovered]**
- **2009:** Clémençon and Ganne publish the Beauclair *Totates* graffiti in *Gallia*. **[scholarly publication]**
- **2010:** Adam Daubney publishes a catalogue and interpretation of the British *TOT* rings as evidence for cult and tribal identity. **[historiography]**
- **13 December 2012:** Chang’e-2 flies past asteroid Toutatis. **[modern reception]**
- **Present:** Toutatis remains prominent through *Astérix*, museums, scholarship, astronomy, commercial entertainment, and small-scale reconstructed polytheist worship. No continuous cult from antiquity has been demonstrated. **[MI]**

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary evidence

The indispensable primary witnesses are Lucan, *Bellum civile* 1.444–446; Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* 6.16–17; Lactantius, *Divinae institutiones* 1.21; the *Commenta Bernensia* and *Adnotationes super Lucanum*; RIB 219 and 1017; the British ring corpus; the Beauclair graffiti; and CIL III 5320/11721 and VI 31182.

### Modern scholarship

Particularly important are Paul-Marie Duval’s analysis of Lucan and the scholia; Clémençon and Ganne’s publication of the Beauclair graffiti; Adam Daubney’s work on *TOT* rings; Ranko Matasović’s Proto-Celtic etymological dictionary; and the RIB editors’ revised readings. Dictionaries by Miranda Green, Garrett Olmsted, Bernhard Maier, James MacKillop, Dáithí Ó hÓgáin, and John Koch provide orientation but cannot replace the primary corpus.

The Irish and Welsh works named in the research brief remain essential for determining what is—and is not—present in Insular Celtic literature. Their silence about Teutates prevents importing him into those mythological cycles solely through the cognate word *túath*.
