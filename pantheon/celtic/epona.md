# Epona: Research Dossier

**Class:** goddess (*dea* in Latin dedications), specifically a Gaulish-speaking Celtic horse divinity incorporated into Gallo-Roman and imperial Roman religion. She is not securely attested as an Irish or Welsh goddess, although later comparativists have associated her with Macha and Rhiannon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the named dedication from Entrains-sur-Nohain, ancient Intaranum in Burgundy, *Aug(usto) sac(rum) deae Epona(e)*, dated palaeographically to approximately 75–125 CE, probably the late first century. First-century figurines may be earlier, but their identification or archaeological dating is less secure. No pre-Roman inscription naming Epona is generally accepted. The frequently repeated claim that her cult is demonstrably prehistoric therefore exceeds the evidence. [Boucher on CIL XIII 2903](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ista_0000-0000_1984_ant_294_1_3341); [datable-evidence catalogue](https://www.epona.net/timeline.html)

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Epona was the protectress of horses and the people and institutions dependent upon them: grooms, muleteers, carters, couriers, cavalrymen, remount personnel, and, by extension, military mobility. Her material record also connects her with foals, nourishment, fertility, prosperity, springs, and occasionally funerary contexts. The last two functions are scholarly deductions from find contexts and iconography, not preserved Epona myths.

Her cult began in the Gaulish cultural region as far as the surviving distribution permits us to judge, then spread through Roman military and transport networks from Gaul and the Rhine to Britain, Italy, the Danube, Dacia, Moesia, Dalmatia, and more isolated sites elsewhere. Oxford’s summary identifies northeastern Gaul—especially Aeduan Burgundy, the Treveran Moselle, and the Rhine—as the probable original heartland. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.2465)

Calling her a deity of the “Irish, Welsh, Gaulish and British pantheon” requires qualification:

- **Securely attested:** Gaulish name; Gallo-Roman cult; Roman-period worship in Britain.
- **Not attested:** Epona does not appear by name in medieval Irish or Welsh narrative.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** comparison with Irish Macha, Étaín Echraide, Medb, and Welsh Rhiannon.
- **Modern invention when stated as identity:** “Epona is the Irish Macha” or “Epona’s Welsh name is Rhiannon.” No primary text establishes either equation.

### Name and etymology

**Epona**, Latin dative commonly **Eponae**, Greek **Ἐπόνα**, derives securely from Gaulish *epos*, “horse,” cognate with Old Irish *ech*, Latin *equus*, and Greek *hippos*, ultimately from Proto-Indo-European *h₁éḱwos*. The formation is normally analyzed as *epo-* plus the name-forming or individuating suffix *-on-* and feminine *-ā*. “Horse-goddess,” “Mare-goddess,” or “She of Horses” is safer than the popular “Great Mare”: an augmentative meaning for *-on-* is possible but not assured. [Linguistic survey citing Delamarre and Lambert](https://www.epona.net/introduction.html)

Attested spellings include:

- **Epona / Eponae** — overwhelmingly usual Latin nominative/dative.
- **Epone** — vulgar or regional dative spelling in some inscriptions.
- **Eponabus** — pluralized form under the influence of associated plural deities such as the *Campestres*; it need not prove an original plurality of Eponas.
- **Hippona / Ypona** — late learned spellings or glosses influenced by Greek *hippos*.
- **Epana / Epane:** Iberian forms have been associated with Epona, but each inscription must be judged separately; phonetic resemblance does not by itself establish identity.
- **Eponina:** a diminutive or derivative sometimes claimed from the controversial Rom tablet; not a secure independent epithet.

### Secure cult titles

The following are genuinely epigraphic:

- **dea Epona** — “the goddess Epona”; Entrains and elsewhere.
- **Epona Augusta** — “August/Imperial Epona”; an incorporation into imperial religious language, not evidence that she was literally the emperor’s relative.
- **Epona Regina** — “Queen Epona,” attested at Doclea.
- **Epona Regina Sancta** — “Holy Queen Epona,” at Apulum in Dacia, CIL III 7750.
- **Epona Sancta** — “Holy Epona.”
- Possible **Mater/Magna/Matrona** supplements are sometimes proposed for damaged texts but cannot be treated as certain.

The titles **Atanta, Potia, Catona, Dibonia, Vovesia, Epotia**, and a sacrificial liturgy to an “Eponina” come from Garrett Olmsted’s highly disputed interpretation of a lead tablet from Rom, Deux-Sèvres. Wolfgang Meid instead read the document as a late, vulgar-Greek magical or erotic text invoking Dibona, not as an Epona hymn. These names must therefore be labeled **contested modern readings**, not an established ancient litany. The accompanying claim that the tablet documents a filly sacrifice to Epona is equally insecure.

### Roman adoption

Rome did not normally translate Epona into a familiar Roman goddess. Her Gaulish name was retained, an unusually clear instance of adoption rather than simple *interpretatio Romana*. Comparisons with Ops, Ceres, Cybele, Isis, Fortuna, or the *Matres* arise from iconographic or functional resemblance; no ancient source states that any of these “is Epona.”

Her designation as “the only Celtic deity with a Roman festival” should be narrowed. A Roman-style calendar from Guidizzolo in Cisalpine Italy records **XV Kalendas Ianuarias Eponae**, “the fifteenth day before the Kalends of January, for Epona,” meaning December 18. That proves a calendrical observance, perhaps local. It does not demonstrate that every Roman celebrated it or establish a legal category in which no other deity of Celtic origin possessed any Roman observance. [Guidizzolo text and Entrains inscriptions](https://www.epona.net/inscriptions.html); [Gregori, De Megille, and Melmeluzzi](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1608806)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest archaeological and epigraphic evidence

Possible first-century objects include:

- A pipe-clay figurine from Baux-Sainte-Croix, approximately 50–100 CE.
- A fragment from a sacrificial area at Nuits-Saint-Georges, broadly mid-first to early second century.
- Pompeian paintings made before 79 CE have been identified as Epona, but one may instead be Isis with Horus or a Nereid. They are not secure named attestations.
- **CIL XIII 2903, Entrains-sur-Nohain:** approximately 75–125 CE. Its surviving text consecrates a gift or vow to *dea Epona*. Letter forms led Hirschfeld to a first-century date, while Thévenot allowed the late first or early second century. This is the earliest secure named evidence.
- **CIL XIII 2902, Entrains:** early second century. Connonius son of Icotasgus gave Epona a temple “with all its ornaments,” paid from his own resources.

The evidence does not establish what Epona’s pre-conquest rites were, whether her cult had a single birthplace, or whether the image preceded the name.

### Classical literary corpus

#### Juvenal, *Satires* 8.155–157

Composed approximately 113–121 CE, this is the earliest securely datable literary appearance. Juvenal derides Lateranus, who associates with muleteers and “swears by Epona alone and the painted faces at the reeking manger”:

> *iurat / solam Eponam et facies olida ad praesepia pictas.*

**Secure implication:** Epona images were familiar in stable or manger settings.  
**Not implied:** a biography, genealogy, or exclusively military cult.

#### Pseudo-Plutarch, *Parallela minora* 29B

Probably composed in the Roman imperial period and transmitted among Plutarch’s *Moralia*, but stylistically not by Plutarch. It cites the otherwise lost *Italian History* of Agesilaus:

> Fulvius Stellus, hating women, coupled with a mare; in time the mare bore a beautiful girl named Epona, “the goddess who takes care of horses.”

This is the only surviving ancient narrative resembling an Epona birth myth. Its cited authority may be fictitious or distorted, a recurring problem throughout *Parallela minora*. It is best classified as **imperial-period learned or comic aetiology**, not secure Gaulish tradition. [Greek and Loeb translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Parallela_Minora%2A.html)

#### Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* 3.27

Written approximately 155–180 CE. Lucius, transformed into an ass, sees:

> *Eponae deae simulacrum residens aediculae*,

an image of the goddess Epona seated in a small shrine on the central stable pillar and decorated with fresh rose garlands. Because roses could restore Lucius, the shrine briefly seems a means of salvation; he is prevented from taking them.

**Secure evidence:** stable shrine, image, rose garlanding, the shrine’s sanctity.  
**Literary interpretation:** modern scholars have examined whether Epona anticipates Isis, who ultimately restores Lucius, but Apuleius never equates the two. [Ancient-literature dossier](https://www.epona.net/literature.html)

#### Minucius Felix, *Octavius* 28.7

Usually dated within the later second or early third century. The Christian Octavius retorts that pagans consecrate whole asses in stables “with their Epona” and adorn or devote them with Isis. The manuscript wording is problematic.

**Secure evidence:** Christians regarded Epona as a recognizable stable deity.  
**Caution:** polemical exaggeration is not a neutral ritual description.

#### Tertullian, *Ad nationes* 1.11.6 and *Apologeticum* 16.5

About 197 CE. Tertullian similarly answers accusations of Christian ass-worship by saying pagans worship whole beasts “with their Epona” and consecrate animals with their mangers.

**Secure evidence:** continued public recognizability of Epona’s association with draft animals and stables.  
**No secure evidence:** actual worship of the animals themselves rather than the deity protecting them.

#### Prudentius, *Apotheosis* 197–199

Approximately 405–413 CE. Prudentius says no one gives Cloacina or Epona a throne above the stars, even though incense and sacrificial meal and entrails are offered.

This late Christian polemic indicates remembered or continuing ritual language but deliberately ranks Epona as a low deity.

#### Anonymous homily attributed to Valerian of Cimiez

A fifth-century sermon for the dedication of a basilica of Saint Dalmatius near Pedona in Cisalpine Gaul denounces local paganism in language that may refer to Epona’s cult. Patrice Lajoye argues that it adds evidence for her late survival in northern Italy. The identification is plausible but not as explicit as the named literary passages. [Lajoye’s study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ecelt_0373-1928_2016_num_42_1_2469)

#### Fulgentius, *Expositio sermonum antiquorum* 11

Approximately 475–525 CE. Epona is classed with Priapus and Vertumnus among the *Semones*, beings supposedly neither worthy of heaven nor merely terrestrial. This is **late-antique learned classification**, not demonstrably an old Gaulish pantheon.

#### *Anecdotum medicum* 189

A fifth- or sixth-century charm for recovering fugitives includes the sequence *irrifa epona nupsit illegy*. Whether *epona* here is the goddess, a corrupt word, or magical gibberish is uncertain.

### Negative corpus audit

The sources specified in the brief require an explicit finding:

- Caesar’s *Bellum Gallicum* VI discusses Gallic religion but **does not name Epona**.
- Lucan’s *Pharsalia* I and its scholia name or discuss other Gaulish gods but provide **no secure Epona narrative**.
- The Gundestrup Cauldron, Pillar of the Boatmen, and Coligny Calendar contain **no named, securely identifiable Epona**.
- *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, *Cath Maige Tuired*, the *Dindshenchas*, *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, *Tochmarc Étaíne*, and *Acallam na Senórach* do **not contain Epona**.
- The Four Branches of the *Mabinogi*, *Culhwch ac Olwen*, and the *Book of Taliesin* do **not name Epona**.

These corpora are evidence for other Celtic-speaking societies and later comparative motifs, not primary Epona documents.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Secure central domain

The name, stable texts, dedicators, and images converge on horses. Epona protects horses, foals, stables, riders, and horse-dependent occupations. Military dedications make her especially visible among auxiliary cavalry and the imperial horse guard, but she was not exclusively a war goddess: domestic statuettes, rural shrines, muleteer references, and civilian vows broaden the constituency.

### Principal image types

Two broad Roman-period types dominate:

1. **Side-saddle or riding type:** Epona sits upon a mare, usually in the conventionally feminine side-saddle posture. This is concentrated in Gaul and the Germanies. In Burgundy, a foal frequently rests or suckles beneath the mare.
2. **“Imperial” or stable-mistress type:** Epona sits frontally on a throne or stool between two or more horses, sometimes feeding or touching them. This form predominates farther east, especially along the Danube, and fits standard Greco-Roman divine composition. [Jovičić and Bogdanović](https://doi.fil.bg.ac.rs/pdf/journals/arhe_apn/2017/arhe_apn-2017-13-3.pdf)

Other representations show her:

- standing between horses or foals;
- holding a patera;
- carrying fruit, grain, a basket, or cornucopia;
- with a dog, keys, or birds in a small minority of monuments;
- alone as a female bust or figure where identification depends on an inscription.

The mare-and-foal reliefs of Burgundy—including the often reproduced Brazey-en-Plaine relief—are particularly important. They support protection and nourishment of horse stock. They do not by themselves prove human motherhood, a cosmic Mother Goddess, sovereignty, or reincarnation.

### Attributes and disputed domains

- **Patera:** offering, feeding, or ritual participation; secure visual attribute.
- **Fruit, grain, cornucopia:** prosperity and abundance in Roman visual language.
- **Foal:** reproduction and protection of young horses.
- **Rose garland:** secure in Apuleius, but not necessarily an exclusive sacred plant.
- **Keys, dogs, ravens, funerary placement:** have encouraged a “chthonic Epona” or psychopomp interpretation. This remains **modern scholarly reconstruction**; no ancient text says that Epona guides the dead.
- **Springs and wells:** some monuments occur near water, including the Entrains sanctuary by the Nohain. A healing-water function is possible but not explicitly stated.
- **White horse:** **not securely Eponian**. It is commonly imported from Rhiannon’s story.
- **Moon, December solstice, number three, apple, turquoise, myrrh:** common in modern devotional literature but not securely ancient Epona attributes.
- **Sovereignty:** inferred by analogy with later Irish and Welsh stories; not an explicit function in Epona’s surviving cult record.

No evidence establishes that Epona had a weapon, a celestial body, a fixed sacred color, or a weekly sacred day.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Only Pseudo-Plutarch supplies parents:

| Tradition | Parentage | Source and status |
|---|---|---|
| Imperial learned aetiology | Fulvius Stellus and an unnamed mare | *Parallela minora* 29B; **late/learned, source reliability doubtful** |
| Gaulish cult | No parentage recorded | **absence of evidence** |
| Modern comparative religion | Sometimes linked or identified with Macha, Rhiannon, the *Matres*, or a hypothetical Indo-European mare goddess | **modern reconstruction, not genealogy in an ancient source** |

No ancient source gives Epona a consort, child, sibling, rival, servant, mount with an individual name, or place in a narrated Celtic divine family.

### Every documented interaction or joint appearance with another named deity

Because Epona has no preserved indigenous myth cycle, “interaction” mostly means epigraphic association rather than narrative action.

- **Isis — juxtaposed in a polemical discussion of animal and stable cults.** Minucius Felix, *Octavius* 28.7, late second/early third century. **Attested joint literary passage; not an identification or narrated meeting.**
- **Jupiter Optimus Maximus — jointly dedicated with Epona and Celeia.** Celeia/Celje inscription, Roman imperial period. **Secure epigraphic association.**
- **Celeia — jointly dedicated as *sancta* with Jupiter and Epona.** Celeia inscription. **Secure regional association.**
- **Mars Segomo — a monument at Lyon reportedly dedicated by a priest of Rome and Augustus to Epona and Mars Segomo.** Roman imperial inscription. **Secure cultic juxtaposition; no mythic interaction.**
- **Hercules — shared or restored shrine at Rome with Epona and Silvanus.** Roman imperial inscription. **Secure cultic juxtaposition.**
- **Silvanus — shared or restored shrine with Hercules and Epona.** Roman imperial inscription. **Secure cultic juxtaposition.**
- **Campestres — Epona appears with the parade-ground goddesses in cavalry dedications.** Roman military inscriptions. **Secure institutional association.**
- **Genius loci — jointly honored at Naix and in other dedications.** Third-century epigraphy. **Secure local-cult association.**
- **Moirai/Parcae or *deae Mairae* — included with Epona and the local Genius in a damaged inscription dated under Decius.** **Secure joint dedication, exact divine reading debated.**
- **Cloacina — rhetorically paired as an allegedly humble goddess.** Prudentius, *Apotheosis* 197–199, early fifth century. **Late Christian literary juxtaposition, no cultic interaction.**
- **Priapus and Vertumnus — jointly classified as *Semones*.** Fulgentius, approximately 475–525. **Late learned systematization.**
- **Rhiannon — no ancient or medieval interaction.** Their identification is **modern comparative reconstruction**.
- **Macha — no ancient or medieval interaction.** Their identification is **modern comparative reconstruction**.
- **Rosmerta, Cybele, Demeter, Ceres, Fortuna, and the *Matres* — no surviving mythic interaction and no universal ancient equation.** Similar attributes or joint regional religious environments do not create a documented relationship.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The birth of Epona

The sole ancient narrative says Fulvius Stellus hated women, mated with a mare, and fathered a beautiful girl whom the mare bore and who was named Epona; she became protector of horses.

Its human-animal union gives a comic or paradoxographical explanation of a foreign goddess’s horse domain. The alleged source, Agesilaus’s *Italian History* III, is lost and may be one of the pseudo-sources characteristic of *Parallela minora*. Nothing identifies the story as a translation of Gaulish oral tradition.

**Classification:** imperial-period learned aetiology; later than the probable emergence of the cult; not safely recoverable as an early Gaulish myth.

### Epona and Lucius’s roses

Apuleius supplies a literary episode rather than a cult myth. The ass-Lucius sees roses garlanding Epona’s stable shrine, the very substance that can restore his humanity, but his attempt to reach them fails. Isis later accomplishes the transformation.

Some modern interpretation makes Epona an incomplete “salvatrix” foreshadowing Isis. This is a productive literary reading, especially because both goddesses appear in the same novelistic economy of animals, roses, and salvation. It is not an ancient myth of Epona and Isis.

### The supposed Epona–Rhiannon cycle

Rhiannon first appears in the medieval Welsh First and Third Branches. She rides a horse that cannot seemingly be overtaken, endures accusation over her missing child, and is punished by carrying visitors. These equine motifs prompted comparison with Epona, as did the reconstructed Brittonic name *Rīgantonā*, “Great/Divine Queen,” beside Epona’s title *Regina*.

Against identity:

- the names are unrelated;
- Rhiannon’s earliest manuscripts are medieval, many centuries after Roman Epona;
- Epona is not securely a white-horse rider;
- Epona’s images and inscriptions contain none of Rhiannon’s marriage, child-loss, punishment, or enchanted-bird narratives;
- *regina* was a widespread Roman divine title.

The careful conclusion is a possible inheritance of broad Brittonic horse-divinity or sovereignty motifs, not proof that Rhiannon is Epona under another name. The modern source itself warns that the white horse has been retrojected from Rhiannon to Epona. [Comparative assessment](https://www.epona.net/later.html)

### The supposed Epona–Macha cycle

One Irish Macha, wife of Cruinniuc, is forced to race the king’s horses while pregnant, wins, bears twins, and curses the Ulstermen with labor pains. Other Machas are associated with sovereignty, battle, Emain Macha, or the Morrígan complex.

The horse race, female sovereignty, fertility, and suffering invite comparison. But:

- no Irish text names Epona;
- Macha’s name is not cognate with Epona;
- Epona has no recorded race, curse, twins, or Ulster connection;
- the “pan-Celtic horse goddess” is a comparative model, not a transmitted doctrine.

### No recoverable indigenous narrative cycle

Unlike Rhiannon or Macha, Epona survives principally as a cult figure without a securely transmitted Gaulish mythology. Filling this absence with Irish or Welsh stories produces a modern composite rather than the documented Epona.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers

#### Entrains-sur-Nohain/Intaranum

The strongest evidence for a dedicated temple comes from CIL XIII 2902:

> *Augusto sacrum deae Eponae … templum cum suis ornamentis omnibus de suo donavit.*

Connonius son of Icotasgus gave the temple and all its equipment. A second early dedication was made by Marcellus son of Maturus. This combination of sanctuary, inscriptions, indigenous personal names, and an early date makes the Aeduan/Burgundian zone central to Epona’s history.

#### Alesia and Burgundy

Alise-Sainte-Reine, Brazey-en-Plaine, Nuits-Saint-Georges, and neighboring sites have produced conspicuous concentrations of riding Eponas, especially mare-and-foal reliefs. Alesia is therefore important to the cult’s regional iconography, though it was not demonstrably a universal Epona headquarters.

Claims that Saint Reine replaced Epona are **post-antique continuity theories**. No medieval hagiographic text equates the saint with Epona or gives Saint Reine a defining horse connection.

#### Rhine and Moselle

The Treveran and Rhine military regions yield many dedications. These show interaction between regional Gallic traditions and the army. The dedicators include cavalrymen, legionary clerks, beneficiaries, and people with varied provincial origins.

#### Rome

Bases from the barracks of the *equites singulares Augusti*, the emperor’s mounted guard, document collective dedications involving Epona between 132 and 141 CE. This is unusually strong evidence for institutional military reception in Rome itself. The guard recruited heavily in Rhine and Danubian provinces; its religious assemblages traveled with personnel. [Study of Epona’s movement to Rome](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1608806)

#### Britain

RIB records nine textual attestations or possible references. The clearest monumental example is **RIB 1777**, an undated sandstone altar from Carvoran/Magnis:

> *Deae Eponae P(…) So(…)*  
> “To the goddess Epona, P(…) So(…) [set this up].”

The altar bears sacrificial implements: knife, axe, and jug. It is now lost. Britain’s record is Roman-provincial and particularly military; it does not prove an independently attested pre-conquest British Epona. [RIB 1777](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1777); [RIB Epona index](https://www.romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/person/3050)

### Worshippers

Documented constituencies include:

- cavalry *alae* and imperial horse guards;
- grooms and stable workers;
- muleteers and carters;
- dispatch riders and military support personnel;
- beneficiarii and legionary clerks;
- governors or dedications made for governors’ welfare;
- civilians who commissioned inexpensive pipe-clay images or private votives.

The army spread and standardized the cult but did not necessarily invent it. The distribution is denser in horse-using military zones partly because military communities erected more durable Latin inscriptions.

### Rituals and offerings

Secure or reasonably supported practices include:

- vows fulfilled by altars, statuettes, or temple construction;
- libation and sacrifice at altars;
- rose garlanding of a stable image;
- installation of images in stable niches and on central pillars;
- military collective dedications;
- offerings of temple equipment;
- December 18 observance at Guidizzolo;
- possible incense, meal, and animal entrails in Prudentius’s late polemic.

### Festival

The Guidizzolo calendar reads:

> *XV Kalendas Ianuarias Eponae.*

By Roman inclusive counting, this is December 18. The calendar’s date and institutional reach are uncertain; it was probably a local or private “rustic” calendar in the Mantuan/Brescian region. “Eponalia” is a convenient modern name, but the surviving line gives Epona’s dative name, not a festival title *Eponalia*.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and cult violence

- **Human sacrifice:** no evidence associates it with Epona.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence.
- **Cult violence:** no evidence.
- **Horse or filly sacrifice:** plausible within ancient ritual generally, but the supposed explicit Epona filly sacrifice depends on Olmsted’s disputed reading of the Rom tablet. It is not secure.
- **Bestiality:** appears only in Pseudo-Plutarch’s origin tale and cannot be converted into an Epona ritual.
- **Political use:** secure where *Augusta*, imperial-welfare formulas, and military corporate dedications place Epona within imperial loyalty.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before approximately 75 CE: hypothesized antecedents

The Gaulish name and early Burgundian concentration make a pre-Roman Gaulish cult highly probable. Yet no securely identified pre-conquest object bears her name. Bronze- and Iron-Age horse symbolism is background, not automatically Epona.

### Approximately 75–125: first secure documentation

Entrains supplies the earliest named inscription and an early temple. Commercial pipe-clay figures indicate that recognizable image types were already circulating.

### Approximately 100–160: military expansion

Epona appears in Bulgaria, Dacia, Britain, Rome, and other military zones. Juvenal can treat her stable imagery as familiar. The emperor’s horse guard at Rome honors her in dated collective inscriptions from 132–141.

### Approximately 150–250: greatest surviving density

Most datable sculpture and epigraphy belongs to the second and third centuries. Regional iconographies diverge:

- Burgundy/Gaul: side-saddle rider, mare and foal.
- Rhine and Germanies: both riding and multiple-horse types.
- Danube/Dacia/Moesia: frontal goddess between horses, with stronger Roman formal vocabulary.

This is the defensible “peak”: not necessarily maximum inner devotion, but the maximum surviving monument production.

### Third century: imperial titles and broad constituencies

*Augusta*, *Regina*, *Sancta*, and official dedications show that Epona could be integrated into imperial provincial religion without losing her name. A dedication dated under Decius demonstrates survival into the mid-third century.

### Fourth–sixth centuries: displacement and polemical memory

Public pagan dedications diminish as Christian institutions expand and imperial support changes. Prudentius, the Cisalpine homily, Fulgentius, and perhaps the medical charm show that Epona remained culturally intelligible after monumental worship declined.

No document records a single empire-wide decree specifically suppressing Epona. Her decline belongs to the wider transformation and suppression of public polytheist sacrifice and temples. Claims of a continuous secret Epona religion through the Middle Ages are unsupported.

### Medieval period

There is no securely continuous Epona cult. Horse customs, saints, hobby-horse rites, the Mari Lwyd, and medieval Rhiannon or Macha narratives may preserve older equine symbolism, but direct descent from Epona is unproved.

### Nineteenth century onward

Archaeological discovery, publication in the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*, Reinach’s 1895 synthesis, and Magnen and Thévenot’s 1953 corpus reconstructed Epona as a historical deity. Modern Pagan worship later reactivated her as a living religious figure, but this is revival rather than demonstrable institutional continuity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Antiquarian and Celtic Revival reception

Nineteenth-century Celtic studies encouraged scholars and artists to seek a unified “Celtic mythology.” Epona consequently became linked to Rhiannon, Macha, sovereignty goddesses, and an assumed prehistoric Great Mother.

Yeats, Lady Gregory, and the Irish Literary Revival transformed medieval Irish myth’s cultural status, while neo-Druidic orders drew on medieval Welsh and Irish texts. They did not recover an otherwise lost Epona liturgy. Assertions that their horse-goddess material reproduces ancient Gaulish practice are **modern construction**.

### Occultism

No substantial Epona cult is documented in the classical Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or canonical Renaissance grimoires. Modern lists that automatically place every ancient goddess inside “Golden Dawn tradition,” astrology, chakra systems, or ceremonial correspondences are usually recent eclectic systems.

Modern occult associations—moon, north, earth, turquoise, myrrh, apples, dream travel, feminine independence—should be labeled **twentieth- or twenty-first-century correspondences**, not recovered antiquity.

### Modern Paganism and Druidry

Contemporary Druids, Celtic reconstructionists, Wiccans, eclectic Pagans, and horse-centered devotional groups worship Epona. The Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids presents both historical material and openly devotional experience. Its page also repeats Olmsted’s controversial Rom-tablet translation without adequately foregrounding Meid’s rival interpretation. [OBOD devotional page](https://druidry.org/resources/epona)

Common modern practices include:

- December 18 observances called “Eponalia”;
- offerings of apples, oats, carrots, roses, horsehair, or stable work;
- horse rescue and welfare as devotional service;
- combining Epona, Macha, and Rhiannon in a “triple horse goddess.”

The date has ancient documentary support; the standardized name, offerings, ethical program, and triadic theology are largely modern.

### Popular culture

The most consequential modern appearance is Nintendo’s **Epona**, Link’s mare in *The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time* (1998) and later games. Designer Yoshiaki Koizumi stated explicitly that he chose the name from the Celtic goddess of horses and fertility. The franchise has probably made “Epona” more widely recognizable as a horse’s name than any modern religious publication. [Nintendo developer interview](https://www.nintendo.co.jp/3ds/interview/aqej/vol2/index5.html)

Other reception includes Enya’s instrumental “Epona” on *The Celts*, fantasy novels, role-playing games, equestrian businesses, horse registries, and the Giotto spacecraft’s EPONA particle instrument. These uses transmit the name, not necessarily ancient theology.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Epona occurs occasionally in generalized Celtic-national or ethnic-revival symbolism, but the evidence consulted does not show a major, organized extremist cult centered on her. It would be misleading to infer such a movement from the broader appropriation of “Celtic” identity. No documented ancient ethnic exclusivity exists: her Roman-period worshippers were provincially and linguistically diverse, including a Syrian dedicator at Mainz.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Foundational studies

- Salomon Reinach, “Épona,” *Revue archéologique* (1895), assembled literary, epigraphic, and iconographic evidence and remains historiographically important.
- René Magnen and Émile Thévenot, *Épona, déesse gauloise des chevaux, protectrice des cavaliers* (1953), established the classic corpus, including hundreds of monuments and inscriptions.
- Thévenot’s later work emphasized Burgundy and eastern Gaul.
- Miranda Green integrated Epona into studies of Celtic animal symbolism and Romano-Celtic iconography.
- Euskirchen’s corpus and later regional studies refined image typologies.
- Jufer and Luginbühl catalogued Gaulish divine names.
- Delamarre supplied the modern linguistic account of *epos*.

An online annotated bibliography usefully maps the French, German, Romanian, and English specialist literature, though it is not a substitute for the editions. [Annotated bibliography](https://www.epona.net/bibliography.html)

### Principal controversies

#### Gallic or Danubian origin?

Fernand Benoît once argued for a Danubian starting point and westward transmission. The Gaulish name, early Entrains evidence, and Burgundian concentration favor Gaul; later Danubian monuments are better explained through military movement and local adaptation. A Celtic/Gaulish origin is the present mainstream position.

#### What does *Epona* mean?

“Horse-goddess” is secure; “Great Mare” depends on interpreting *-on-* as augmentative. Popular works often present the stronger translation as fact.

#### Was she a fertility or mother goddess?

Foals, fruit, grain, paterae, and cornucopia justify prosperity and reproductive associations. “Universal Mother Goddess” is a broader comparativist model, not an ancient title consistently attached to her.

#### Was she a psychopomp?

Funerary contexts, keys, dogs, and later horse-otherworld comparisons make the proposal possible. No literary source explicitly assigns her the transportation of souls.

#### Is she Rhiannon or Macha?

Similarity exists at the level of female horse symbolism. Direct identity, genealogy, or transmission is unproved. Ronald Hutton’s objection is especially pertinent: Rhiannon rides a horse, but her narrative and attributes otherwise differ markedly from Epona’s surviving evidence.

#### The Rom tablet

Olmsted’s “Epona sacrificial hymn” created an alluring native liturgy with many epithets. Meid’s incompatible reading makes it unsafe as a foundation for ritual reconstruction. Websites frequently repeat Olmsted as settled fact; it is not.

#### Was December 18 an empire-wide Roman festival?

The Guidizzolo line is real. Its uncertain date and local/private format mean the safest formulation is “a December 18 observance is attested in a Roman-style Cisalpine calendar,” not “the universal Roman state celebrated Eponalia.”

### State of the evidence

The evidence is unusually rich for a Gaulish deity but structurally limited:

- hundreds of inscriptions and images, many lacking modern excavation context;
- few securely dated first-century monuments;
- no indigenous narrative manuscript;
- hostile or humorous Roman literary references;
- regional corpora scattered across museums and archaeological publications;
- lost monuments, including RIB 1777;
- many uninscribed female-and-horse images whose identification remains probabilistic.

The central historiographical danger is compensating for the absence of Gaulish narrative by importing medieval Irish and Welsh narrative wholesale.
